The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with \(m\) item kinds each supplied at \(1/m\) per capita, does there exist a uniformly specified family of finite-round distributed protocols for every compatible replication \(q\), with \(q\mu_t\) applicants of type \(t\) and \(q/m\) copies of each item, whose honest outcome is ordinally efficient and satisfies strong equal treatment, and under which every honest applicant’s expected item lottery weakly dominates its honest-execution lottery against every fail-stop coalition of at most \(q/2\) applicants?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(m\geq1\) item kinds, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length. Each kind has supply \(1/m\) per capita.
A compatible replication is a positive integer \(q\) with \(q\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\) for every \(t\) and \(q/m\in\mathbb Z\). It has labeled applicants, \(q\mu_t\) of each type, and \(q/m\) interchangeable copies of each kind; every execution must assign exactly one copy to every applicant, including applicants who abort.
Preferences are public and fixed. Protocols involve only the applicants, using independent private randomness, synchronous public broadcast, and ideal perfectly hiding and binding commitments; a publicly agreed deterministic function of the transcript determines the assignment.
A uniformly specified family means one algorithm, allowed to depend on the given society, generates the protocol and a finite worst-case round bound for each compatible \(q\); that bound applies to every permitted execution. No polynomial resource bound or consistency of lotteries across replications is imposed.
Write \(O_i(k)\) for applicant \(i\)'s \(k\)-th favorite kind. Strong equal treatment requires that applicants sharing their first \(\ell\) ranked kinds have identical honest-execution probabilities for each of those kinds, for every \(1\leq\ell\leq m\). Ordinal efficiency is stochastic-dominance Pareto efficiency of the honest-execution marginal lotteries among all feasible random assignments with these copy counts.
For every applicant \(i\), a rushing adversary may observe corrupted applicants' internal states and adaptively corrupt at most \(\lfloor q/2\rfloor\) other applicants. Its only deviation is to stop a corrupted applicant's broadcasts permanently, possibly after seeing honest messages in the current round. Maximin security requires \(i\)'s resulting unconditional item lottery to weakly stochastically dominate its all-honest lottery under every such strategy.
Call the input feasible if a uniformly specified family exists whose protocol, for every compatible replication, has ordinal efficiency, strong equal treatment, and maximin security as defined above. Measure decision running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and protocol resources additionally in \(q\).

Is this protocol-family existence problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete societies, some instance with four applicants and four distinct items admits no bounded-round protocol combining ordinal efficiency, strong equal treatment, and maximin security against coalitions of at most two applicants. With ideal commitments, strong equal treatment and ex-post Pareto efficiency can instead coexist with maximin security against up to \(n-1\) corrupted applicants. [Chan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.13338)
No result settling this exact support-input, all-replications decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Complete rankings are strict rankings of item kinds; copies of the same kind are interchangeable, and no ranking of copies is required.
  • Compatible replications are all positive integers making every type count and every kind's copy count integral.
  • Every execution returns a complete feasible assignment, including assignments to aborted applicants, matching the discrete formal output domain.
  • Preferences are public and fixed; security concerns protocol deviations rather than preference misreports.
  • The distributed model uses synchronous public broadcast, independent private randomness, ideal commitments, and deterministic transcript-based output.
  • Fail-stop security includes rushing, internal-state observation, and adaptive corruption of at most floor(q/2) applicants; each protected applicant remains uncorrupted.
  • Uniform specification means a single computable generator for the given society, with a worst-case finite round bound for each replication; no polynomial protocol bound or common lottery across replications is required.
  • Ordinal efficiency and strong equal treatment apply to honest-execution applicant marginals, with capacities determined by the replication.
  • The domain includes every positive number of item kinds; the anchor's four-item instance is an existential obstruction, not a minimum-domain condition.

Partial result: an exact replication criterion for a four-type subclass

The full decision problem remains unresolved. I prove an exact criterion for a nontrivial subclass: for the four rankings below, a compatible replication admits the required protocol precisely when every type has an even number of applicants. Consequently, protocol-family existence for this subclass has a polynomial-time arithmetic test.

The proof also establishes a general obstruction: a bounded-round protocol cannot securely generate a nonconstant public numerical outcome whose expectation must remain unchanged under either of two complementary coalitions.

1. The subclass and the result

Consider four kinds and the following support, where $0<\alpha<1/2$:

| Type | Ranking | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $a\succ c\succ b\succ d$ | $\alpha$ |
| $2$ | $a\succ d\succ b\succ c$ | $1/2-\alpha$ |
| $3$ | $b\succ c\succ a\succ d$ | $1/2-\alpha$ |
| $4$ | $b\succ d\succ a\succ c$ | $\alpha$ |

The case $\alpha=1/4$ is the profile in [Chan et al., Example 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.13338). The proof below is self-contained and treats all compatible replications of the displayed society.

Write $\alpha=u/v$ in lowest terms.

Theorem. For this subclass:

  • A compatible replication $q$ admits a protocol satisfying ordinal efficiency, strong equal treatment, and the required maximin security if and only if $q\alpha$ is even.
  • The society admits a uniformly specified family for every compatible replication if and only if

$$ \operatorname{lcm}(4,v)\alpha $$
is even.

  • Equivalently, the family exists if and only if

$$ 4\nmid v. $$

The arithmetic criterion applies to whichever rational masses the input convention permits. In particular, if the permitted masses are dyadic, every society in this four-type subclass is infeasible: $0<\alpha<1/2$ then forces its reduced dyadic denominator to be divisible by $4$.

I prove the theorem in Sections 2–6.

2. Honest marginals are uniquely determined

Fix a compatible replication. Set

$$ r=\frac q4,\qquad n=q\alpha,\qquad h=2r-n. $$

There are respectively $n,h,h,n$ applicants of the four types and $r$ copies of each kind. Positivity of the masses gives

$$ 1\leq n,h\leq 2r-1. $$

Define the applicant marginal lottery $P^*$ by

$$ \begin{array}{c|cccc} &a&b&c&d\\ \hline 1&1/2&0&1/2&0\\ 2&1/2&0&0&1/2\\ 3&0&1/2&1/2&0\\ 4&0&1/2&0&1/2 \end{array} $$

Thus every applicant receives its first and second choices with probabilities $1/2$ each.

2.1. Feasibility of $P^*$

Choose an integer-valued random variable $K$ with mean $n/2$, supported on

$$ \left\{\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor, \left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil\right\}. $$

If these values differ, give each probability $1/2$.

For each realization of $K$, assign the following numbers of applicants their first choices:

$$ \begin{array}{c|cccc} \text{Type}&1&2&3&4\\ \hline \text{Number receiving first choice}&K&r-K&r-K&K. \end{array} $$

Assign every remaining applicant its second choice. Within each type, choose the first-choice recipients uniformly among subsets of the required size.

These counts are valid. Indeed, the permissible interval for $K$ is

$$ \max(0,n-r)\leq K\leq \min(n,r). $$

Its endpoints are integers, and it contains $n/2$, because $0<n<2r$. It therefore contains both the floor and ceiling of $n/2$.

The numbers of assigned copies are

$$ \begin{aligned} a &: K+(r-K)=r,\\ b &: (r-K)+K=r,\\ c &: (n-K)+(h-r+K)=r,\\ d &: (h-r+K)+(n-K)=r. \end{aligned} $$

Furthermore,

$$ \mathbb E[K]=\frac n2,\qquad \mathbb E[r-K]=\frac h2. $$

Uniform selection within each type therefore gives every applicant first-choice probability $1/2$. This proves feasibility of $P^*$ as a random assignment; no distributed implementation is asserted here.

2.2. Ordinal efficiency of $P^*$

Suppose a feasible marginal assignment weakly stochastically dominates $P^*$.

Every type-$1$ or type-$2$ applicant must receive $a$ with probability at least $1/2$. There are $2r$ such applicants, whereas there are only $r$ copies of $a$. Hence all these probabilities must equal $1/2$.

The same argument forces first-choice probability exactly $1/2$ for every type-$3$ or type-$4$ applicant.

At the second-choice cutoff, $P^*$ has cumulative probability $1$. Dominance therefore requires each applicant to receive only its first two choices. Its second-choice probability is consequently also $1/2$.

Thus every dominating marginal assignment equals $P^*$, proving ordinal efficiency.

2.3. Uniqueness under strong equal treatment and ordinal efficiency

Let $P$ be any feasible marginal assignment satisfying strong equal treatment.

All type-$1$ and type-$2$ applicants share first choice $a$, so their probabilities of receiving $a$ are identical. Capacity bounds that common probability by $1/2$. Likewise, every type-$3$ or type-$4$ applicant has first-choice probability at most $1/2$.

For every applicant, $P^*$ therefore has at least as much cumulative probability at the first-choice cutoff. At every later cutoff, its cumulative probability is $1$, and hence at least that of $P$.

Consequently, $P^*$ weakly stochastically dominates $P$. If $P$ is ordinally efficient, it follows that

$$ P=P^*. $$

This conclusion holds separately at each compatible replication, without assuming consistency between replications.

3. A two-party expectation obstruction

I first prove the protocol lemma used for necessity.

Lemma. Consider a two-party protocol with independent private randomness, public broadcast, ideal commitments, and a finite worst-case round bound. Its numerical output $Y$ is bounded and is a deterministic function of its public transcript.

Suppose there is a constant $c$ such that

$$ \mathbb E[Y]=c $$

under every strategy in which either one party alone may stop its broadcasts permanently, including strategies that condition the stopping decision on its internal state and the available public messages.

Then its all-honest output satisfies

$$ Y=c $$

almost surely.

Here ideal commitments store a sender’s hidden value and verify later openings; they supply no additional shared random source.

Proof

Fix the private random tapes of both parties. Honest computation is then deterministic.

For analysis, serialize each synchronous round into the first party’s message vector followed by the second party’s message vector. Honest messages still use only the preceding rounds, as specified by the original protocol. A second-party stopping decision after observing the first vector is permitted by rushing.

Conditional independence. At any public prefix $H$, the two private tapes remain conditionally independent.

To verify this, fix a prefix $H$ of positive probability. With all preceding public messages fixed, consistency of the first party’s messages with $H$ imposes conditions only on its own tape. Consistency of the second party’s messages imposes conditions only on its tape. Thus the set of tape pairs producing $H$ is a Cartesian product. Conditioning the independent tapes on this product preserves independence.

Commitment creation and opening add sender-local consistency conditions, so they preserve this argument.

Now consider a message position following prefix $H$. Let the sender’s local view $V$ include the private randomness used to prepare its outgoing message $M$.

On the same private tapes, define $D$ to be the eventual output if this sender stops permanently at this position and the other party continues honestly. Because the sender sends nothing further, $D$ depends only on $H$ and the other party’s tape. Therefore

$$ \mathbb E[D\mid H,V]=\mathbb E[D\mid H]=d(H) $$

for some function $d$.

Let $E$ be any event determined by the sender’s available view at this position, restricted to a chosen public prefix. Consider the adversary that stops exactly on $E$ and otherwise follows the honest protocol. Coupling executions by their private tapes, its output is $D$ on $E$ and $Y$ outside $E$.

The assumed equality of expectations implies

$$ \mathbb E\!\left[\mathbf 1_E(D-Y)\right]=0. $$

Since this holds for every such view event,

$$ \mathbb E[D-Y\mid H,V]=0. $$

Hence

$$ \mathbb E[Y\mid H,V]=d(H). $$

Averaging over $V$ gives

$$ d(H)=\mathbb E[Y\mid H]. $$

Because $M$ is determined by $H$ and $V$, another application of conditional expectation yields

$$ \mathbb E[Y\mid H,M]=\mathbb E[Y\mid H]. $$

Thus revealing each successive honest message leaves the conditional expectation of $Y$ unchanged.

There are finitely many message positions. Starting with $\mathbb E[Y]=c$ and applying this equality through the final public transcript gives

$$ \mathbb E[Y\mid \text{final transcript}]=c. $$

The final transcript determines $Y$, so its left-hand side is $Y$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

Consequence. If such an output is integer-valued, its invariant expectation must be an integer. More strongly, its honest output must be constant.

4. Necessity: odd type counts are impossible

Suppose a protocol exists for the fixed replication. Section 2 forces its honest marginals to equal $P^*$.

Partition the applicants into two blocks:

$$ A=\{\text{types }1,2\},\qquad B=\{\text{types }3,4\}. $$

Each block has $2r=q/2$ applicants. Corrupting either entire block is therefore allowed.

For any output assignment, define these public validity conditions:

  • $V_A$: every applicant in $A$ receives its first or second choice, and exactly $r$ applicants in $A$ receive $a$.
  • $V_B$: every applicant in $B$ receives its first or second choice, and exactly $r$ applicants in $B$ receive $b$.

When $V_A$ holds, let $K_A$ be the number of type-$1$ applicants receiving $a$.

When $V_B$ holds, let $X_3$ be the number of type-$3$ applicants receiving $b$, and put

$$ K_B=r-X_3. $$

4.1. The two statistics agree whenever both blocks are valid

If both conditions hold, the number of type-$2$ applicants receiving $a$ is $r-K_A$, and the number of type-$4$ applicants receiving $b$ is $r-X_3$.

All remaining applicants receive their own second choices. The total number receiving $c$ is therefore

$$ (n-K_A)+(h-X_3)=2r-K_A-X_3. $$

It must equal the supply $r$. Hence

$$ K_A+X_3=r, $$

which gives $K_A=K_B$.

Define a bounded integer-valued public statistic on every assignment by

$$ F= \begin{cases} K_A,&\text{if }V_A,\\ K_B,&\text{if }V_A\text{ fails and }V_B,\\ 0,&\text{if both conditions fail}. \end{cases} $$

4.2. Security pins its expectation when $A$ is honest

Under any allowed strategy against block $B$, maximin security gives each applicant in $A$ probability at least $1/2$ of receiving $a$, and probability $1$ of receiving one of its first two choices.

Let $Z_A$ count the copies of $a$ assigned to $A$. Then

$$ Z_A\leq r,\qquad \mathbb E[Z_A]\geq 2r\cdot\frac12=r. $$

Consequently $Z_A=r$ almost surely. Since the individual first-choice probabilities are all at least $1/2$ and sum to $r$, each equals $1/2$.

Thus $V_A$ holds almost surely, and

$$ \mathbb E[F]=\mathbb E[K_A]=\frac n2. $$

4.3. Security pins the same expectation when $B$ is honest

The symmetric argument shows that $V_B$ holds almost surely and that every applicant in $B$ has first-choice probability exactly $1/2$. Therefore

$$ \mathbb E[X_3]=\frac h2. $$

If $V_A$ also holds, Section 4.1 gives $F=K_B$; otherwise the definition gives the same equality. Hence

$$ \mathbb E[F] =r-\frac h2 =\frac n2. $$

4.4. Applying the two-party lemma

Let each of two parties simulate one block, retaining the independent private randomness of all applicants in that block.

A party that stops all its simulated applicants at a chosen point produces a permitted coalition fail-stop strategy. Each coalition has exactly $q/2$ applicants. The public assignment, and hence $F$, is determined by the public transcript.

Sections 4.2–4.3 show that under every such stopping strategy by either party,

$$ \mathbb E[F]=\frac n2. $$

The lemma forces the honest output $F$ to be constant and equal to $n/2$. But $F$ is integer-valued. Therefore

$$ n\text{ is even}. $$

This proves necessity.

5. Sufficiency: secure allocation when type counts are even

Suppose $n$ is even. Then $h=2r-n$ is also even.

For each type, reserve half as many copies of its first choice as it has applicants, and the same number of its second choice. These four fixed pools partition supply:

$$ \begin{aligned} a &: \frac n2+\frac h2=r,\\ b &: \frac h2+\frac n2=r,\\ c &: \frac n2+\frac h2=r,\\ d &: \frac h2+\frac n2=r. \end{aligned} $$

It remains to implement each pool securely.

5.1. A secure two-kind pool

Consider $N$ applicants sharing a preference for premium kind $x$ over kind $y$, with $N$ even and $N/2$ copies of each.

Allocate the premium copies successively using the following tournament among currently active, unassigned applicants.

Choose a deterministic binary tree on its $s$ candidates. Each subtree’s weight is its number of leaves. At a duel between representatives of child weights $w_1,w_2$:

  • Each representative samples a fresh uniform element of $\mathbb Z_{w_1+w_2}$ and commits to it.
  • Both open. Their sum modulo $w_1+w_2$ selects the first representative on $w_1$ residues and the second on the remaining $w_2$ residues.
  • If exactly one representative fails to commit or open, the other wins. If both fail, the representative with the smaller public label wins. Record all detected aborts.

Assign the premium copy to the tournament winner, even if that winner has aborted. Remove the winner and every detected abort from future tournaments.

If no active applicants remain, complete the pool deterministically. After all premium copies are assigned, give the remaining applicants the other copies. Every applicant, including every aborted applicant, receives exactly one copy.

Tournament guarantee. A protected honest candidate wins an $s$-candidate tournament with probability at least $1/s$.

At a duel on its path, its fresh committed value is uniform and hidden. If the modular outcome favors it, the opponent either opens, giving it the win, or aborts, also giving it the win. Thus its conditional winning probability is at least the ratio of its child weight to the parent weight. Multiplying these ratios along its path gives $1/s$.

Under honest execution, every duel has exactly its specified probabilities, so every candidate wins with probability exactly $1/s$.

Premium-copy guarantee. At every reachable history containing an unassigned protected applicant, suppose $s$ active candidates and $K$ premium copies remain. Its probability of receiving a premium copy is at least

$$ \min\left(1,\frac Ks\right). $$

Here $K$ is a nonnegative integer no larger than the number of unassigned pool copies.

For $K=0$, the assertion is immediate. For $K\geq s$, each tournament removes at least one active candidate, while the protected applicant cannot be removed through abort detection. It therefore wins within those $K$ allocations.

For $1\leq K<s$, let $p\geq 1/s$ be its probability of winning the next tournament. Conditional on losing, at most $s-1$ active candidates remain. Induction on $K$ gives a subsequent winning probability at least $(K-1)/(s-1)$. Therefore its total probability is at least

$$ \begin{aligned} p+(1-p)\frac{K-1}{s-1} &\geq \frac1s+\left(1-\frac1s\right)\frac{K-1}{s-1}\\ &=\frac Ks. \end{aligned} $$

Initially $K=N/2$ and $s=N$, so the protected applicant receives $x$ with probability at least $1/2$.

Under honest execution, successive tournament winners form a uniformly random ordering. Its premium-copy probability is exactly $1/2$.

Since every execution assigns only $x$ or $y$ to each pool member, increasing premium-copy probability proves stochastic dominance whenever $x$ and $y$ are that member’s first two choices. The argument permits adaptive rushing corruption of all other pool members.

There are at most two rounds per duel. The active population decreases after every tournament allocation, giving a finite bound of at most $2N^2$ rounds.

5.2. Applying the pool protocol

Run this construction independently in each reserved type pool.

Every applicant’s honest marginal equals its row of $P^*$. Section 2 proves ordinal efficiency. Strong equal treatment holds because applicants sharing first choice have first-choice probability $1/2$, and applicants of the same type have identical lotteries.

Each pool construction protects an honest applicant even against corruption of all other members of that pool. It therefore satisfies the required bound against corruption of at most $q/2$ other applicants across the society.

The deterministic transcript rules assign all copies, including copies to aborted applicants. The combined round bound is at most $2q^2$.

This proves sufficiency for the fixed replication.

6. All replications and the arithmetic criterion

Write $\alpha=u/v$ in lowest terms and set

$$ q_0=\operatorname{lcm}(4,v). $$

Every compatible replication must be divisible by both $4$ and $v$, hence by $q_0$.

Conversely, $q_0/4$ and $q_0\alpha$ are integers, and

$$ q_0\left(\frac12-\alpha\right) =\frac{q_0}{2}-q_0\alpha $$

is an integer. Thus the compatible replications are exactly

$$ q=kq_0,\qquad k\geq1. $$

By the fixed-replication result, the family exists precisely when $q_0\alpha$ is even. If it is odd, the replication $q=q_0$ is impossible. If it is even, every compatible replication has even type counts, and one algorithm generates the reserved pools and tournaments described above.

To simplify the criterion:

  • If $v$ is odd, then $q_0=4v$ and $q_0\alpha=4u$ is even.
  • If $v$ is divisible by $2$ but not by $4$, then $q_0=2v$ and $q_0\alpha=2u$ is even.
  • If $4\mid v$, then $q_0=v$. Coprimality forces $u$ odd, so $q_0\alpha=u$ is odd.

Therefore the criterion is exactly

$$ 4\nmid v. $$

Reduction of rational numbers and the required divisibility tests take polynomial time in their binary bit lengths.

For the equal-mass case $\alpha=1/4$, this also gives the exact fixed-replication distinction:

$$ \begin{aligned} q\equiv0\pmod8 &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{a secure protocol exists},\\ q\equiv4\pmod8 &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{no such protocol exists}. \end{aligned} $$

Thus arbitrarily large feasible replications coexist with arbitrarily large infeasible replications of the same society. Its protocol family is impossible because it must include the infeasible replications.

7. What remains open

The result supplies a reusable expectation obstruction and completely resolves the displayed four-type subclass. It does not characterize general supports.

In particular, the integrality condition proved here depends on the special capacity equalities and the public statistic constructed in Section 4. No argument above establishes a necessary-and-sufficient condition for arbitrary societies, a polynomial-time decision procedure for the full problem, or an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

The argument applies separately to each replication and does not assume consistency across replications.

3. The two-party expectation lemma

The lemma’s substantive argument is valid under the stated interpretation of ideal commitments as sender-local storage and verification, without an additional shared random source.

The important steps check out:

  • Serialization. Honest messages remain functions of preceding synchronous rounds. The serialized second party’s additional opportunity to observe the first party’s current message is permitted by rushing. The stopping opportunities used for the serialized first party are also permitted.
  • Conditional independence. For a fixed public prefix of positive probability, consistency conditions factor between the two private tapes. Sender-local commitments and openings preserve this factorization.
  • Abort continuation. Once the sender stops, its remaining hidden state cannot affect subsequent honest messages or the transcript-determined output. Thus the claimed conditional expectation of $D$ is justified.
  • Selective stopping. The strategy that stops on a sender-view event $E$ and otherwise behaves honestly is within the lemma’s hypothesis. Coupling on the private tapes gives

$$ \mathbb E[\mathbf 1_E(D-Y)]=0. $$
Allowing every such event justifies the conditional-expectation identity.

  • Message revelation. Since the outgoing message is determined by the sender’s view, conditioning on that message cannot change the conditional expectation established in the preceding step.
  • Termination. A fixed finite sequence of message positions, followed by transcript determinacy of $Y$, gives $Y=c$ almost surely.

Minor issue — transcript formalization. The proof expressly treats prefixes of positive probability and then speaks of a fixed finite sequence of message positions. It should state that messages and commitment handles have countable encodings and that terminated executions are padded to the common round bound. These are routine conventions for an algorithmic protocol, but they should be explicit in a lemma stated this generally. If arbitrary non-atomic public message spaces are intended instead, the positive-probability-prefix argument alone does not cover that formulation.

Minimum clarification: State the countable transcript convention and padding convention used by the proof.

4. Necessity of even type counts

The reduction to the expectation lemma is correct.

Both blocks have exactly $q/2$ applicants. Corrupting one entire block leaves every applicant in the other block protected and respects the adversarial bound.

When $A$ is honest, security implies:

  • every applicant in $A$ receives one of its first two choices almost surely;
  • every applicant in $A$ receives $a$ with probability at least $1/2$.

Since $Z_A\leq r$ and $\mathbb E[Z_A]\geq r$, the count is $r$ almost surely. The individual lower bounds then all equal $1/2$. Therefore $V_A$ holds and
