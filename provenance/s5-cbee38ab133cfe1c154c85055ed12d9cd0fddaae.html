Theorem 5.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) chores of supply one and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\lambda_t>0\), \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), disutilities \(d_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^m\), and budgets \(b_t>0\), can an SGR-type algorithm return prices \(p_j>0\) and per-capita allocations \(x_{tj}\ge0\) in ~\(O(m^3(τ+m)/ε^3)\) arithmetic operations so that \((1-ε)b_t\le p^\top x_t\le b_t/(1-ε)\), \((1-ε)d_t^\top x_t\le d_t^\top y\) for every \(y\ge0\) with \(p^\top y\ge p^\top x_t\), and \(1-ε\le\sum_t\lambda_t x_{tj}\le1/(1-ε)\) for each chore \(j\)?

There are \(m\geq2\) divisible chores, each of supply one. The unrestricted complexity question remains unresolved here. I prove three results that advance it:

  • The support-and-mass formulation is exactly equivalent to a finite market with $\tau$ agents and aggregate earning requirements $B_t=\lambda_t b_t$.
  • For every fixed number $m$ of chores, an exact rational equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time. For unrestricted $m$, an exact equilibrium still exists with polynomial encoding length.
  • If the aggregate-income ratio $\rho=\max_t B_t/\min_t B_t$ is polynomially bounded, a rational LP algorithm computes the requested approximation in polynomial time, with explicit control of encoding lengths.

I also reprove the exponential-update example for the specified SGR execution. Thus its stated operation bound cannot be used as a uniform bound with polynomial dependence on $L$. This does not exclude a different algorithm achieving the requested bound.

1. Aggregation and a finite optimality test

Define
$$ B_t=\lambda_t b_t,\qquad z_t=\lambda_t x_t,\qquad S=\sum_t B_t. $$
Consider the finite market with one agent per supported type, earning requirement $B_t$, and unchanged disutility vector $d_t$.

Multiplying the society’s earning inequalities by $\lambda_t>0$ gives
$$ (1-\epsilon)B_t\leq p^\top z_t\leq \frac{B_t}{1-\epsilon}. $$
For individual optimality, the substitution $w=\lambda_t y$ is a bijection of the nonnegative comparison bundles, and
$$ p^\top y\geq p^\top x_t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^\top w\geq p^\top z_t. $$
Multiplying the disutility inequality by $\lambda_t$ therefore gives precisely the finite-market optimality condition. Finally,
$$ \sum_t z_{tj}=\sum_t\lambda_t x_{tj}. $$
Thus all three approximation conditions—and their exact versions—are preserved in both directions. The inverse is $x_t=z_t/\lambda_t$.

Products and quotients of binary rationals have polynomial encoding length. In particular, this transformation does not replicate agents according to mass denominators. Conversely, a finite market with $n$ agents and requirements $B_i$ becomes a society by taking
$$ \lambda_i=\frac1n,\qquad b_i=nB_i. $$
Consequently, the two search problems are polynomially equivalent.

We will use the following elementary characterization. For $p>0$, put
$$ a_t(p)=\min_j\frac{d_{tj}}{p_j}. $$
For any $c\geq0$,
$$ \min_{\substack{y\geq0\\p^\top y\geq c}}d_t^\top y =a_t(p)c. $$
Indeed, every feasible $y$ satisfies
$$ d_t^\top y\geq a_t(p)p^\top y\geq a_t(p)c. $$
Equality is achieved by placing $c/p_j$ units on a chore attaining the minimum.

Hence approximate individual optimality is equivalent to the single rational inequality
$$ (1-\epsilon)d_t^\top z_t \leq a_t(p)\,p^\top z_t. $$
Exact optimality holds whenever every positively allocated chore maximizes $p_j/d_{tj}$.

2. Exact existence from a price minimization problem

This section establishes existence without importing an equilibrium-existence theorem.

Let
$$ \Delta_S=\left\{p\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^m:\sum_jp_j=S\right\}, $$
and define
$$ \beta_t(p)=\max_j\frac{p_j}{d_{tj}}, \qquad H(p)=\sum_t B_t\log\beta_t(p). $$
Write $d_{\max}=\max_{t,j}d_{tj}$. Every $p\in\Delta_S$ has some coordinate at least $S/m$, so
$$ \beta_t(p)\geq\frac{S}{m d_{\max}}>0. $$
Therefore $H$ is continuous on the compact set $\Delta_S$ and attains a minimum.

2.1 Every minimizer has strictly positive prices

Suppose $p_j=0$. For $0<h<1$, set
$$ p'_j=hS,\qquad p'_k=(1-h)p_k\quad(k\neq j). $$
For sufficiently small $h>0$, simultaneously for all types,
$$ \frac{hS}{d_{tj}}<(1-h)\beta_t(p). $$
The maximum over the other chores equals $(1-h)\beta_t(p)$, because the zero coordinate was not a maximizer. Thus
$$ \beta_t(p')=(1-h)\beta_t(p) $$
for every $t$, and
$$ H(p')=H(p)+S\log(1-h)<H(p). $$
A boundary point cannot minimize $H$.

2.2 Every minimizer supports an exact equilibrium

Let $p$ minimize $H$, and define the nonempty winning sets
$$ J_t(p)=\operatorname*{argmax}_j\frac{p_j}{d_{tj}}. $$
All coordinates of $p$ are positive by the preceding argument.

For any direction $h$, the one-sided directional derivative is
$$ H'(p;h)= \sum_t B_t\max_{j\in J_t(p)}\frac{h_j}{p_j}. $$
To justify this formula, nonwinning coordinates remain nonwinning for sufficiently small positive steps. Among the winning coordinates, the largest derivative determines the derivative of their maximum; applying the derivative of $\log$ gives $h_j/p_j$.

Define the compact convex set
$$ K= \left\{ \sum_t B_t\sum_{j\in J_t(p)} \theta_{tj}\frac{e_j}{p_j}: \theta_{tj}\geq0,\ \sum_{j\in J_t(p)}\theta_{tj}=1 \right\}. $$
Then
$$ H'(p;h)=\max_{a\in K}a^\top h. $$

Let
$$ V=\left\{h:\sum_jh_j=0\right\}. $$
I claim that $K$ intersects the line spanned by the all-ones vector.

Otherwise, the orthogonal projection of $K$ onto $V$ is a compact convex set not containing zero. Let $w_0$ be its closest point to zero. For every point $w$ in that projection, minimality of $w_0$ implies
$$ w_0^\top w\geq\|w_0\|^2. $$
This follows by differentiating the squared norm along the segment from $w_0$ to $w$ at its initial endpoint.

Taking $h=-w_0\in V$ would give
$$ H'(p;h)\leq-\|w_0\|^2<0. $$
Since $p>0$, sufficiently small positive steps in this direction remain in $\Delta_S$, contradicting minimality.

Thus some $a\in K$ equals $\alpha\mathbf1$. Every $a\in K$ satisfies
$$ p^\top a =\sum_t B_t\sum_{j\in J_t(p)}\theta_{tj} =S. $$
Since $\sum_jp_j=S$, it follows that $\alpha=1$.

Choose corresponding coefficients $\theta_{tj}$, and set
$$ v_{tj}=B_t\theta_{tj},\qquad z_{tj}=\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j}, $$
with zero entries outside $J_t(p)$. We obtain
$$ \sum_jv_{tj}=B_t,\qquad \sum_tv_{tj}=p_j. $$
Hence every agent earns exactly $B_t$, and every chore clears exactly. All positive allocations use winning chores, so Section 1 proves exact individual optimality.

3. An exact algorithm for every fixed number of chores

The minimization argument above also identifies finitely many candidate prices. The algorithm below does not evaluate logarithms.

3.1 A minimizing price is a vertex of a price region

Choose, for each type, one chore $a_t\in J_t(p)$ at a minimizing price $p$. Define
$$ P_a= \left\{ q\in\Delta_S: \frac{q_{a_t}}{d_{t,a_t}}\geq \frac{q_j}{d_{tj}} \text{ for all }t,j \right\}. $$
On this polytope,
$$ H(q)=\sum_tB_t\log\frac{q_{a_t}}{d_{t,a_t}}. $$
Each $q_{a_t}$ is strictly positive throughout $P_a$: if it were zero, the defining inequalities would force every coordinate of $q$ to be zero. Therefore this expression is a well-defined concave function on $P_a$.

Express $p$ as a convex combination of vertices of $P_a$. Concavity implies that at least one such vertex $q$ satisfies $H(q)\leq H(p)$. Since $p$ is a global minimizer, that vertex is also a global minimizer. Section 2.1 consequently implies $q>0$.

At a positive vertex, nonnegativity constraints are inactive. Besides $\sum_jq_j=S$, the active constraints are tie equations of the form
$$ d_{tk}q_j-d_{tj}q_k=0. $$
There must be $m-1$ such equations that, together with the price-sum equation, are linearly independent. Otherwise there would be a nonzero direction preserving all active equations; sufficiently small steps of either sign would remain feasible, contradicting the vertex property.

3.2 Enumeration and flow verification

There are at most
$$ M=\tau\binom m2 $$
tie hyperplanes. Enumerate every subset of $m-1$ hyperplanes, solve it together with
$$ \sum_jp_j=S, $$
and discard singular systems and solutions with a nonpositive coordinate.

For each remaining candidate:

  • Compute every winning set $J_t(p)$ using exact rational comparisons.
  • Construct a flow network with source-to-type capacity $B_t$, type-to-chore capacity $S$ for $j\in J_t(p)$, and chore-to-sink capacity $p_j$.
  • Accept if its maximum flow has value $S$.

A flow of value $S$ saturates every source-to-type and chore-to-sink arc, because both sets of capacities sum to $S$. Its type-to-chore flows $v_{tj}$ therefore satisfy
$$ \sum_jv_{tj}=B_t,\qquad \sum_tv_{tj}=p_j. $$
Returning
$$ z_{tj}=\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j},\qquad x_{tj}=\frac{z_{tj}}{\lambda_t} $$
gives an exact equilibrium by Section 1.

Sections 2 and 3.1 guarantee that at least one enumerated candidate passes this test.

Using Gaussian elimination and the Edmonds–Karp maximum-flow algorithm, an arithmetic-operation bound is
$$ O\!\left( \binom{M}{m-1} \left[ m^3+\tau m+ (\tau+m)(\tau m+\tau+m)^2 \right] \right). $$
Here the maximum-flow bound uses $O(\tau+m)$ vertices and $O(\tau m+\tau+m)$ arcs.

This is polynomial in $\tau$ for every fixed $m$. In particular, it resolves all fixed-$m$ cases exactly, with arbitrary positive binary masses and requirements.

3.3 Encoding lengths and exact witnesses

After clearing denominators in an enumerated system, each integer coefficient has $O(L)$ bits. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound
$$ |\det A|\leq m!\left(\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|\right)^m $$
show that every candidate price coordinate has
$$ O\!\left(m(L+\log m)\right) $$
bits. Moreover, all price coordinates can use the common denominator $|\det A|$.

The total encoding length of the aggregate requirements $B_t$ is $O(L)$. Multiply all flow capacities by the product of their budget denominators and $|\det A|$. The resulting capacities are integers with
$$ O\!\left(m(L+\log m)\right) $$
bits. Starting from zero, augmenting-path maximum flow preserves integral flows, whose magnitudes are bounded by the total capacity.

Dividing back, and then dividing by $p_j$ and $\lambda_t$, preserves a polynomial bit bound. Thus:

  • The fixed-$m$ algorithm has polynomial bit complexity.
  • Even when $m$ varies, every valid input admits an exact rational equilibrium with polynomial total encoding length.

Section 1 also supplies a polynomial-time rational verifier for the requested approximate equilibrium. These witness and verification results do not themselves provide a polynomial-time search algorithm.

4. A rational algorithm when aggregate-income ratios are bounded

Here I give a direct proof, including the rational implementation, rather than relying on an unspecified rational-output provision in the earlier FPTAS.

The LP iteration is the greedy Frank–Wolfe iteration from [Chaudhury–Kroer–Mehta–Nan, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10439#S4); the proof below states the normalization and approximation bounds needed here explicitly.

Normalize budgets to
$$ \widehat B_t=\frac{B_t}{S},\qquad \sum_t\widehat B_t=1. $$
Also divide all disutility coefficients by their common maximum. Write the resulting coefficients as $\widehat d_{tj}$, and let
$$ D=\frac{\max_{t,j}d_{tj}}{\min_{t,j}d_{tj}}. $$
Then
$$ \frac1D\leq\widehat d_{tj}\leq1. $$
Common positive scaling of disutilities preserves optimality.

4.1 The iteration and its rational implementation

Initialize
$$ p_j^0=\frac1m,\qquad \beta_t^0=\max_j\frac{p_j^0}{\widehat d_{tj}}. $$
Given $\beta^k$, choose an optimal vertex of the rational LP
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad& \sum_t\frac{\widehat B_t}{\beta_t^k}\beta_t,\\ \text{subject to}\quad& p_j\leq\widehat d_{tj}\beta_t \quad\text{for all }t,j,\\ &\sum_jp_j=1,\\ &p\geq0,\qquad 0\leq\beta_t\leq2D. \end{aligned} $$
Denote its solution by $(p^{k+1},\beta^{k+1})$, and put
$$ r_t=\frac{\beta_t^{k+1}}{\beta_t^k}. $$
Stop when
$$ 1-\alpha\leq r_t\leq1+\alpha \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \alpha=\frac{\epsilon}{4}. $$

The feasible polytope is fixed throughout the iteration. Its coefficients have $O(L)$ bits, and it has $\tau+m$ variables. Every vertex therefore has polynomial encoding length, independent of the iteration count. The objective coefficients $\widehat B_t/\beta_t^k$ likewise have polynomial encoding length.

The standard rational linear-programming theorem gives an exact optimal vertex in time polynomial in the LP’s encoding length. Applying that theorem is justified here because the polytope is nonempty and compact and all data are rational. Consequently, every iteration and its rational stopping test have bit complexity polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

4.2 Positivity and dual reconstruction

The objective coefficients are strictly positive. Thus at any optimum,
$$ \beta_t=\max_j\frac{p_j}{\widehat d_{tj}}. $$
In particular,
$$ \frac1m\leq\beta_t\leq D<2D, $$
so the artificial upper bounds are inactive.

Also every optimal price is strictly positive. If $p_j=0$, the perturbation used in Section 2.1 makes every $\beta_t$ smaller by the same factor, strictly decreasing this LP’s objective.

Let $z_{tj}\geq0$ be optimal dual multipliers for the constraints
$$ p_j-\widehat d_{tj}\beta_t\leq0. $$
Since $p$ and $\beta$ are positive and the upper bounds on $\beta$ are inactive, LP stationarity and complementary slackness give some $\kappa$ with
$$ \sum_tz_{tj}=\kappa,\qquad \sum_j\widehat d_{tj}z_{tj} =\frac{\widehat B_t}{\beta_t^k}, $$
and
$$ z_{tj}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_j=\widehat d_{tj}\beta_t^{k+1}. $$
Therefore
$$ p^\top z_t =\beta_t^{k+1}\sum_j\widehat d_{tj}z_{tj} =\widehat B_t r_t. $$
Summing these earnings gives
$$ \kappa=\sum_t\widehat B_t r_t>0. $$
This explicitly establishes the positivity needed for normalization.

Set
$$ \bar z_{tj}=\frac{z_{tj}}{\kappa}. $$
Every chore now clears exactly. Every positive allocation is on a winning chore, so individual optimality holds exactly. At termination,
$$ 1-\alpha\leq\kappa\leq1+\alpha, $$
and hence
$$ \frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha} \leq \frac{p^\top\bar z_t}{\widehat B_t} \leq \frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}. $$
Because $\alpha=\epsilon/4$,
$$ \frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\geq1-\epsilon. $$
Taking reciprocals supplies the required upper bound.

Optimal rational dual multipliers can also be obtained with polynomial encoding length by rational LP. Thus $(Sp,\bar z)$ is a rational $\epsilon$-equilibrium for the aggregate market, and division by $\lambda_t$ returns the society’s allocation.

4.3 Number of iterations

Use the potential
$$ \Phi(\beta)=-\sum_t\widehat B_t\log\beta_t. $$
The previous iterate is feasible for the next LP, so
$$ \sum_t\widehat B_t r_t\leq1. $$
For
$$ \varphi(r)=r-1-\log r, $$
we consequently have
$$ \begin{aligned} \Phi(\beta^{k+1})-\Phi(\beta^k) &=-\sum_t\widehat B_t\log r_t\\ &=\sum_t\widehat B_t\varphi(r_t) +1-\sum_t\widehat B_t r_t\\ &\geq\sum_t\widehat B_t\varphi(r_t). \end{aligned} $$

The function $\varphi$ decreases up to $1$ and increases thereafter. For $0<\alpha<1/2$,
$$ \varphi(1+\alpha) =\int_0^\alpha\frac{s}{1+s}\,ds \geq\frac{\alpha^2}{2(1+\alpha)} \geq\frac{\alpha^2}{3}, $$
and
$$ \varphi(1-\alpha) =\int_0^\alpha\frac{s}{1-s}\,ds \geq\frac{\alpha^2}{2}. $$
Every nonterminating iteration therefore increases the potential by at least
$$ \frac{\alpha^2}{3}\min_t\widehat B_t. $$

For every feasible point, $\beta_t\geq1/m$, giving $\Phi(\beta)\leq\log m$. At initialization,
$$ \beta_t^0\leq\frac Dm, \qquad \Phi(\beta^0)\geq\log m-\log D. $$
Thus the total available increase is at most $\log D$. The number of LP solves is at most
$$ 1+\frac{48\log D}{\epsilon^2\min_t\widehat B_t}. $$

Now define
$$ \rho=\frac{\max_t B_t}{\min_t B_t}. $$
Since $S\leq\tau\max_t B_t$,
$$ \min_t\widehat B_t\geq\frac1{\tau\rho}. $$
Binary encoding also gives $\log D=O(L)$. The LP count is therefore
$$ O\!\left(1+\frac{\tau\rho L}{\epsilon^2}\right), $$
and each solve has polynomial bit complexity in $m,\tau,L$.

This proves polynomial-time computability whenever $\rho$ is polynomially bounded. It does not settle unrestricted binary masses: even with $b_1=b_2=1$,
$$ \lambda_1=2^{-k},\qquad \lambda_2=1-2^{-k} $$
gives $\rho=2^k-1$ with input length $O(k)$.

5. The specified SGR execution can require exponentially many updates

This lower bound uses the update, rounding threshold, stopping test, and reconstruction in [Chen–Jiang–So, Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04036v1#S5). It concerns that execution, not equilibrium computation by arbitrary algorithms.

5.1 Instance and scalar dynamics

Take
$$ \lambda_1=\lambda_2=\frac12,\qquad b_1=b_2=1,\qquad d_1=(1,R),\qquad d_2=(2,2R), \qquad R=2^k. $$
The aggregate requirements are $B_1=B_2=1/2$.

Choose the permitted parameters
$$ \epsilon=\frac14,\qquad \delta=\frac5{26},\qquad \gamma=\frac12,\qquad \eta=\frac5{62}, $$
and initialize $\mu^0=(0,0)$.

Write $r=\mu_1-\mu_2$, and define
$$ q(r)=\frac1{1+e^{-r}}, \qquad s(r)=\frac1{1+e^{-(r+\log R)/\delta}}. $$
The two disutility rows differ by a common positive factor, which cancels from their smoothed choice probabilities. Thus the prices are $(q,1-q)$, and the gradient is
$$ (g(r),-g(r)),\qquad g(r)=s(r)-q(r). $$
When rounding is inactive, the update is
$$ r^{\mathrm{new}}=r-2\eta g(r). $$

5.2 Rounding is inactive on the relevant interval

Put
$$ c=1+\frac{3\delta}{4},\qquad r_b=-c\log R,\qquad h=\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}. $$
The prescribed price floor is
$$ A=\frac14(2R)^{-h}8^{-\delta}. $$
For $r\in[r_b,0]$,
$$ q(r)\geq\frac1{1+R^c}\geq\frac12R^{-c}, \qquad 1-q(r)\geq\frac12. $$
Moreover,
$$ h-c =\delta\left(\frac2{1-\delta}-\frac34\right)>0. $$
Both prices are therefore strictly above $A$, so rounding leaves these iterates unchanged.

Also,
$$ c<\frac1{1-\delta}. $$
Consequently, throughout this interval,
$$ \frac{r+\log R}{\delta}>r, $$
and hence $g(r)>0$. Iterates move downward, with each step having magnitude at most $2\eta$.

5.3 Every reconstruction in this interval violates clearance

The reconstructed aggregate allocation of chore $1$ is $s(r)/q(r)$.

For $R\geq256$, divide $[r_b,0]$ into three intervals.

If $-\frac12\log R\leq r\leq0$, then
$$ \frac{s(r)}{q(r)} \geq\frac2{1+R^{-1/(2\delta)}} \geq\frac{32}{17}>\frac43. $$
Here $q(r)\leq1/2$, and $R^{-1/(2\delta)}\leq1/16$.

If $-\log R\leq r\leq-\frac12\log R$, then $s(r)\geq1/2$ and $q(r)\leq R^{-1/2}$, so
$$ \frac{s(r)}{q(r)}\geq\frac12R^{1/2}>\frac43. $$

Finally, write $r=-u\log R$ for $1\leq u\leq c$. Then
$$ s(r)\geq\frac12R^{-(u-1)/\delta}, \qquad q(r)\leq R^{-u}, $$
and therefore
$$ \frac{s(r)}{q(r)} \geq\frac12R^{u-(u-1)/\delta} \geq\frac12R^{41/104}>\frac43. $$
The exponent decreases with $u$, and its value at $u=c$ is
$$ c-\frac{c-1}{\delta} =\frac14+\frac{3\delta}{4} =\frac{41}{104}. $$
At $R=256$, the last lower bound already exceeds $4$.

Thus every reconstruction in $[r_b,0]$ violates the upper clearance bound $1/(1-\epsilon)=4/3$. The stopping test also fails because
$$ \frac{g(r)}{q(r)} =\frac{s(r)}{q(r)}-1 >\frac13>\epsilon. $$

5.4 Traversing the interval takes exponential time

Set
$$ r_a=-\left(1+\frac\delta2\right)\log R. $$
For $r\in[r_b,r_a]$,
$$ 0<g(r)\leq s(r) \leq e^{(r+\log R)/\delta} \leq R^{-1/2}. $$
Each update starting in this interval decreases $r$ by at most $2\eta R^{-1/2}$.

Let $t$ be the first iterate with $r_t\leq r_a$, and let $u$ be the first with $r_u<r_b$. An execution returning a valid reconstruction must reach both thresholds. If it never does, it cannot return a valid reconstruction.

The global step bound implies
$$ r_t\geq r_a-2\eta. $$
For sufficiently large $R$, this remains above $r_b$. Every update from $t$ through $u-1$ therefore starts inside the interval where the small-step bound applies. Telescoping gives
$$ (u-t)\,2\eta R^{-1/2} \geq r_t-r_u > \frac\delta4\log R-2\eta. $$
For sufficiently large $R$,
$$ u-t\geq\frac{\delta}{16\eta}R^{1/2}\log R. $$
With $R=2^k$, this is $\Omega(2^{k/2}k)$. The input length is $L=\Theta(k)$ while $m,\tau,\epsilon$ are fixed.

Therefore this permitted SGR execution requires $2^{\Omega(L)}$ gradient updates, even under unit-cost exact real arithmetic. Stopping it earlier by an iteration cap returns an invalid reconstruction.

The family itself has the easy exact equilibrium
$$ p=\left(\frac1{1+R},\frac R{1+R}\right), \qquad x_1=x_2=(1,1). $$
Each type earns one, both chores clear, and both chores have equal disutility per unit of earnings for each type. The lower bound isolates an algorithmic limitation.

6. What remains open in this argument

The support representation causes no additional complexity obstacle: aggregation produces only $\tau$ finite agents, with polynomial encoding length.

The fixed-$m$ enumeration settles a broader exact subcase than the two-chore construction in the notes. The LP proof also establishes the bounded aggregate-income subcase with positive earnings and rational outputs explicitly justified.

Neither argument settles polynomial-time computability versus hardness when both $m$ and unrestricted binary aggregate requirements vary. The SGR example prevents transferring the stated operation count as a uniform polynomial-in-$L$ guarantee for that execution, but does not rule out an alternative algorithm with the requested arithmetic bound.
