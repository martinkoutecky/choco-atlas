CLAIM 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a fixed event hierarchy, a finite support of trader-portfolio types with rational masses μ_t summing to one, rational per-capita liquidity β, and two legal queried securities E_1 and E_2, determine which has the higher LMSR quote, where q_μ(ω)=∑_t μ_t h_t(ω) and p_μ(E)=∑_{ω∈E} exp(q_μ(ω)/β) / ∑_{ω∈Ω} exp(q_μ(ω)/β).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T\) be an explicitly given rooted tree with \(m\geq1\) labeled leaves, fixed throughout trading, and let \(N\geq1\) be an integer. The outcome space is \(\Omega=\{0,\ldots,N\}^{m}\); each node \(v\) determines the nonempty set \(S_v\) of its descendant leaves.
A legal sum-with-varying-weights (SVW) security is the event \(a\leq\sum_{i\in S_v}c_i x_i\leq d\), where \(a,d\) are nonnegative integers with \(a\leq d\), and every \(c_i\) is a positive integer chosen separately for that security. Either bound may be omitted; equality securities are allowed. A share pays one on the event and zero otherwise.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) trader-portfolio types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Type \(t\) explicitly lists legal securities \(F_{tj}\) and signed rational share quantities \(s_{tj}\), with negative quantities representing short positions; its payoff is \(h_t(\omega)=\sum_j s_{tj}\mathbf{1}_{F_{tj}}(\omega)\).
Starting from zero outstanding shares, define \(q_\mu(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_t h_t(\omega)\). For positive rational per-capita liquidity \(\beta\), the LMSR quote is \(p_\mu(E)=\frac{\sum_{\omega\in E}\exp(q_\mu(\omega)/\beta)}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\exp(q_\mu(\omega)/\beta)}\).
Given two legal securities \(E_1,E_2\), compare their quotes exactly, reporting either strict ordering or equality. All numerical inputs are binary encoded. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,N,|T|,K\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length and \(K\) is the total number of listed portfolio positions; polynomial time means polynomial in the encoded input size.

Is this exact LMSR quote-comparison problem for a society given by rational masses in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete SVW LMSR pricing is reported to be \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for any event hierarchy; the hardness argument compares two legal securities. [Guo–Pennock](https://cs.adelaide.edu.au/~mingyu/publication/aamas2009.pdf). No result specifically settling the rational-mass formulation above was located.

  • Specified legal securities using SVW: positive integer weights chosen separately per security, nonnegative integer bounds, and optional one-sided bounds.
  • Encoded each portfolio explicitly as signed rational quantities of legal securities, retaining the ability to purchase and short securities.
  • Required positive liquidity and zero initial outstanding shares, as in the discrete market model.

Result

The exact quote-comparison problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds even when \(\tau=1\), \(K=1\), \(N=1\), and \(\beta=1\), with an integer share quantity and two nonempty comparison events. The reduction produces strictly opposite orderings in its yes and no cases.

Hardness concerns instances with growing \(m\). The construction works with any rooted-tree shape on the required labeled leaves.

Reduction from positive SUBSET SUM

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem positive SUBSET SUM: given positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) and a positive integer \(B\), all binary encoded, decide whether some subset sums to \(B\). Assume \(n\geq1\).

Construct a tree with \(m=n+1\) leaves, labeled by coordinates \(x_1,\ldots,x_n,y\), and set \(N=1\). Thus

\(\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n+1}\).

A star suffices, although only the root and the leaf labeled \(y\) will be used.

The society has one type, with mass \(\mu_1=1\). Its portfolio consists of one long share of the leaf security

\(F=\{y=0\}\).

This is legal: its coefficient is \(1\), and both bounds are \(0\). Set \(\beta=1\). Starting from zero outstanding shares, the resulting aggregate payoff is

\(q_\mu(x_1,\ldots,x_n,y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{y=0\}}\).

Ask to compare the two root securities

\(E_1=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i x_i+B y=B\right\}\)

and

\(E_2=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i x_i+B y=0\right\}\).

Both are legal equality securities: every root coefficient is a positive integer, and their bounds are nonnegative integers.

Both events are nonempty. The outcome \((0,\ldots,0,1)\) belongs to \(E_1\), while \((0,\ldots,0,0)\) belongs to \(E_2\). Since \(B>0\), they are disjoint.

Exact computation of the ordering

Define the number of SUBSET SUM witnesses by

\(s=\left|\left\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i x_i=B\right\}\right|\).

There are two cases for outcomes in \(E_1\).

  • If \(y=0\), the defining equality becomes \(\sum_i w_i x_i=B\). There are exactly \(s\) such outcomes, each having \(q_\mu=1\) and hence exponential weight \(e\).
  • If \(y=1\), the equality becomes \(\sum_i w_i x_i=0\). Positivity of every \(w_i\), together with \(x_i\geq0\), forces every \(x_i=0\). This contributes exactly one outcome, having \(q_\mu=0\) and exponential weight \(1\).

Consequently,

\(\sum_{\omega\in E_1}\exp(q_\mu(\omega)/\beta)=se+1\).

Similarly, positivity of every coefficient implies that \(E_2\) consists only of the all-zero outcome. That outcome has \(y=0\), so

\(\sum_{\omega\in E_2}\exp(q_\mu(\omega)/\beta)=e\).

For each fixed \(y\), there are \(2^n\) choices of \(x\). Therefore the common, positive denominator is

\(Z=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\exp(q_\mu(\omega)/\beta)=2^n(e+1)\).

It follows exactly that

\(p_\mu(E_1)-p_\mu(E_2)=\dfrac{1+(s-1)e}{2^n(e+1)}\).

If the SUBSET SUM instance is a no-instance, then \(s=0\), and the numerator is \(1-e<0\).

If it is a yes-instance, then \(s\geq1\), and the numerator is at least \(1>0\).

Thus

\(\text{SUBSET SUM is satisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_\mu(E_1)>p_\mu(E_2)\).

No constructed instance has equal quotes. Hence the decision problem asking whether \(p_\mu(E_1)>p_\mu(E_2)\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and the requested three-way comparison is at least as hard.

Encoding size and running time

Let \(\ell\) be the source SUBSET SUM input bit length. For a star, the constructed parameters satisfy

\(m=n+1,\qquad |T|=m+1,\qquad \tau=K=N=1\).

The only potentially large numerical values are the copied integers \(w_i\) and \(B\). The output bit length satisfies

\(L=O\!\left(\ell+n\log(n+1)\right)\).

The reduction can be written in \(O\!\left(\ell+n\log(n+1)\right)\) bit operations: it copies the input integers and writes the tree, labels, constant-size society, and two coefficient lists.

A comparator running in time polynomial in the encoded input size would therefore solve SUBSET SUM in polynomial time. Indeed, even a running-time bound polynomial in all six parameters \(m,\tau,L,N,|T|,K\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), because \(N,\tau,K\) are constant in this reduction.

Why rational masses introduce no obstruction

More generally, any society in the question can be replaced by a single type of mass \(1\). Retain every listed security \(F_{tj}\), and replace its quantity by the rational number

\(r_{tj}=\mu_t s_{tj}\).

The resulting payoff is exactly

\(\sum_{t,j}r_{tj}\mathbf{1}_{F_{tj}}=\sum_t\mu_t h_t=q_\mu\),

so every quote is preserved. Products of binary-encoded rationals have polynomial encoding length; retaining duplicate positions avoids any need to combine denominators. Using ordinary integer multiplication, this transformation takes \(O(KL^2+L)\) bit operations.

[Guo–Pennock’s Claim 2](https://cs.adelaide.edu.au/~mingyu/publication/aamas2009.pdf) also reduces SUBSET SUM using root and leaf securities. The explicit reduction above settles the stated rational-mass formulation using only integer quantities.

For \(y=0\), membership is precisely the source subset-sum condition. Therefore there are exactly \(s\) outcomes, each with exponential weight \(e\).

\[ Z=2^n e+2^n=2^n(e+1)>0. \]
The resulting difference
\[ p_\mu(E_1)-p_\mu(E_2) =\frac{1+(s-1)e}{2^n(e+1)} \]

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the strict-comparison decision problem. A three-way comparator immediately decides that problem, establishing the requested hardness. No computation or finite encoding of \(e\) is required by the reduction.
