The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given firm types \(I\) and worker types \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), capacities, pair costs \(c_{ij}\), and succinct path-independent choice rules on integral vectors of partner-type counts, minimize total pair cost over stable mass matchings. A feasible plan assigns each type’s mass to integral bundle states, with compatible pair-incidence masses realizing those bundles; it is stable when no positively represented firm and worker states both demand an additional partner of the other type.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be nonempty sets of firm and worker types, with \(m=\max\{|I|,|J|\}\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses need not sum to one or balance across sides.
Each type \(a\) has a binary nonnegative integer capacity \(q_a\) and a deterministic succinct choice algorithm \(C_a\) on nonnegative integral vectors of opposite-side type counts. It evaluates in time polynomial in its description and argument bit lengths, and satisfies \(0\le C_a(u)\le u\) and \(\|C_a(u)\|_1\le q_a\). Here path independence on counts means substitutability \(u-C_a(u)\le v-C_a(v)\) whenever \(u\le v\), and consistency \(C_a(u)\le v\le u\Rightarrow C_a(v)=C_a(u)\); inequalities are coordinatewise.
Firm bundle states are \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\) with \(\|b\|_1\le q_i\) and \(C_i(b)=b\); worker states are \(d\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{I}\) with \(\|d\|_1\le q_j\) and \(C_j(d)=d\). Empty states are allowed.
A mass plan consists of nonnegative real state masses \(x_{ib},y_{jd}\) and pair-incidence masses \(z_{ib,jd}\), satisfying \(\sum_bx_{ib}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_dy_{jd}=\nu_j\), \(\sum_dz_{ib,jd}=b_jx_{ib}\), and \(\sum_bz_{ib,jd}=d_iy_{jd}\).
Writing \(e_k\) for the appropriate unit vector, firm state \(b\) demands an additional worker of type \(j\) when \(C_i(b+e_j)_j=b_j+1\); worker demand is defined symmetrically. Choice may discard existing partners, including at full capacity. A plan is stable if no \(x_{ib}>0,y_{jd}>0\) simultaneously satisfy \(C_i(b+e_j)_j=b_j+1\) and \(C_j(d+e_i)_i=d_i+1\).
Given signed binary rational costs \(c_{ij}\), the total cost is \(\sum_{i,j,b,d}c_{ij}z_{ib,jd}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including choice descriptions, capacities, masses, costs, and a rational threshold \(B\).

Is deciding whether a stable mass plan of cost at most \(B\) exists in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents with succinct polynomial-time path-independent choice functions and signed integer pair costs, minimum-cost stable matching is NP-hard ([En–Faenza](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17916v3)).
For a different continuous model, stable allocation in polymatroid intersection admits a strongly polynomial existence algorithm ([Iwata–Yokoi](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf)); no complexity result for the bundle-state mass optimization problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Masses are arbitrary positive binary rationals, without normalization or equal side totals, reflecting the unrestricted sizes of the two discrete populations.
  • Capacities are nonnegative integers encoded in binary and bound chosen bundles, while choice remains defined on offers exceeding capacity so that blocking can involve replacement.
  • The undefined count-vector version of path independence is fixed as coordinatewise monotonicity of rejected counts together with consistency; these specialize to the discrete assumptions on binary vectors.
  • Succinct choice rules are deterministic input algorithms promised to evaluate in polynomial time in description and argument bit lengths; their descriptions are included in L.
  • Individual rationality is restored by allowing only bundle states fixed by their type's choice function; empty bundles remain available.
  • Compatible incidences are defined by state-level degree balance equations with nonnegative real masses. Stability applies to every pair of positively represented opposite-side states, regardless of their existing incidence.
  • Additional-partner demand means accepting the increased count after offering one more partner, with replacements permitted.
  • Pair costs may have either sign, as in the discrete result; rational costs and a rational threshold define the decision version of minimum-cost optimization.
  • For this two-sided model, m is the maximum number of opposite-side type coordinates and tau is the total support size.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already when every type has mass \(1\), every cost belongs to \(\{0,1\}\), and the threshold is \(B=0\).

The main issue is mass splitting. The reduction below includes a gadget that forces every variable type to choose a Boolean state without splitting.

1. Preliminary facts

Eliminating the incidence variables

For state masses \(x,y\), define the firm-side and worker-side incidences by
\(p^F_{ij}=\sum_b b_jx_{ib}\) and \(p^W_{ij}=\sum_d d_iy_{jd}\).

Incidence variables \(z\) exist exactly when \(p^F_{ij}=p^W_{ij}\) for every pair. Necessity follows by summing the prescribed marginal equations.

For sufficiency, write their common value as \(p_{ij}\). If \(p_{ij}>0\), set
\(z_{ib,jd}=(b_jx_{ib})(d_iy_{jd})/p_{ij}\).
If \(p_{ij}=0\), set all these incidences to zero. Summing this formula over either state index gives the required marginal. Thus the cost is \(\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}p_{ij}\).

Extending set choices to count choices

Our construction first specifies a choice \(F\) on sets. Its count version is
\(C(u)=\mathbf 1_{F(S(u))}\), where \(S(u)=\{a:u_a>0\}\) and \(\mathbf 1\) denotes the indicator vector.

Suppose \(F\) is substitutable and consistent on sets. Then this extension satisfies the task’s count axioms.

Indeed, let \(u\le v\). For a coordinate with \(u_a=0\), the rejection inequality follows from \(v_a-C(v)_a\ge0\). If \(u_a>0\), set substitutability gives \(C(v)_a\le C(u)_a\), hence
\(u_a-C(u)_a\le v_a-C(v)_a\).

Also, \(C(u)\le v\le u\) implies
\(F(S(u))\subseteq S(v)\subseteq S(u)\).
Set consistency therefore gives \(C(v)=C(u)\).

Every fixed-point state of such a count choice is binary: \(C(u)=u\) implies \(u_a\in\{0,1\}\).

2. Choice rules used in the reduction

We use two classes of set choices. Unlisted types are always rejected.

Ranked primary types with conditional auxiliaries. There is a strictly ranked list of primary types, a designated initial segment of that list, and a set of auxiliary types. Choose the best available primary type, if any. Additionally choose every available auxiliary type exactly when no primary type in the designated initial segment is available.

This rule is substitutable: a rejected primary remains rejected when the menu grows, because a better primary remains available; a rejected auxiliary remains rejected because a primary in the designated initial segment remains available.

It is consistent as well. Restricting a menu while retaining all chosen types retains its best primary, if one exists. If auxiliaries were suppressed, that retained primary lies in the designated initial segment, so suppression persists. Otherwise every available auxiliary was chosen and must be retained.

Gate rule. Given an input set \(A\) and a distinct special type \(s\), choose every available input. Choose \(s\) exactly when it is available and no input is available.

Inputs are never rejected. A rejected special type remains rejected when the menu grows, since an input remains available. For consistency, if an input is available, every available input is chosen and must be retained; if no input is available, restricting the menu cannot introduce one. Thus this rule also is substitutable and consistent.

We use the count extensions from Section 1. These algorithms inspect supports and fixed lists, so they evaluate in polynomial time in their descriptions and argument bit lengths.

3. Construction from \(3\)-SAT

Let the formula have variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) and clauses indexed by \(c\). We may take \(n\ge1\); this restriction does not affect the hardness of \(3\)-SAT.

For each variable \(X_v\), introduce three firm types \(f_{v,1},f_{v,2},f_{v,3}\) and three worker types \(w_{v,1},w_{v,2},w_{v,3}\). For each clause, introduce a firm type \(g_c\) and a worker type \(h_c\). Every type has mass \(1\).

Variable choices

Within each variable gadget, the primary rankings are:

| Firm | Primary ranking | Worker | Primary ranking |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(f_{v,1}\) | \(w_{v,1}\succ w_{v,3}\succ w_{v,2}\) | \(w_{v,1}\) | \(f_{v,3}\succ f_{v,1}\) |
| \(f_{v,2}\) | \(w_{v,2}\succ w_{v,3}\) | \(w_{v,2}\) | \(f_{v,1}\succ f_{v,2}\) |
| \(f_{v,3}\) | \(w_{v,3}\succ w_{v,1}\) | \(w_{v,3}\) | \(f_{v,2}\succ f_{v,1}\succ f_{v,3}\) |

All these types choose the best available primary type.

There are two auxiliary rules:

  • The auxiliaries of \(f_{v,1}\) are \(H_v=\{h_c:X_v\text{ occurs positively in clause }c\}\). It chooses all available auxiliaries exactly when neither \(w_{v,1}\) nor \(w_{v,3}\) is available.
  • The auxiliaries of \(w_{v,3}\) are \(G_v=\{g_c:\neg X_v\text{ occurs in clause }c\}\). It chooses all available auxiliaries exactly when neither \(f_{v,2}\) nor \(f_{v,1}\) is available.

These are the ranked rules proved path-independent above. Set capacities \(q_{f_{v,1}}=1+|H_v|\) and \(q_{w_{v,3}}=1+|G_v|\); the other four variable types have capacity \(1\).

Clause choices

Define the input sets
\(N_c=\{w_{v,3}:\neg X_v\text{ occurs in clause }c\}\) and
\(P_c=\{f_{v,1}:X_v\text{ occurs positively in clause }c\}\).

Firm \(g_c\) uses the gate rule with inputs \(N_c\) and special worker \(h_c\). Worker \(h_c\) uses the gate rule with inputs \(P_c\) and special firm \(g_c\).

Set \(q_{g_c}=1+|N_c|\) and \(q_{h_c}=1+|P_c|\). These capacities bound their outputs.

Costs

Set \(c_{f_{v,1},w_{v,3}}=1\) for every variable, and \(c_{g_c,h_c}=1\) for every clause. Every other cost is zero, and \(B=0\).

Call \((f_{v,1},w_{v,3})\) the probe pair.

Because costs and incidences are nonnegative, any plan of cost at most zero has zero incidence on every probe pair and every clause special pair.

4. Zero cost forces Boolean variable states

Fix a variable and suppress its index \(v\). Write \(p_{kl}=p_{f_k,w_l}\).

Every admissible state contains at most one primary partner. We first prove that, in any stable plan, every variable type has a primary partner in every positive-mass state.

Every worker has a primary partner

Suppose worker \(w_k\) has positive mass \(\delta\) in states with no primary firm. Then \(p_{kk}\le1-\delta<1\), so firm \(f_k\) has positive mass in states lacking \(w_k\).

Worker \(w_k\), when it has no primary partner, demands \(f_k\). This remains true for \(w_3\) even if it has auxiliaries: adding its lowest primary \(f_3\) retains those auxiliaries and selects \(f_3\).

Firm \(f_k\) ranks \(w_k\) first, so every state lacking \(w_k\) demands it. These two positive-mass states block, a contradiction.

Thus every worker has a primary partner.

Every firm has a primary partner

The top firms of workers \(w_2,w_3,w_1\) are respectively \(f_1,f_2,f_3\).

Suppose \(f_k\) has positive mass \(\delta\) without a primary partner. Its incidence with the worker that ranks it first is at most \(1-\delta\). That worker consequently has positive mass in states lacking \(f_k\), and those states demand \(f_k\).

The primary-empty states of \(f_k\) demand that worker, which is an acceptable primary. Again there is a block.

Therefore every firm also has a primary partner.

The probe excludes splitting

Now assume cost zero. The probe incidence is \(p_{13}=0\). All unlisted primary incidences vanish as well.

Every primary row and column sums to one, so, for some \(t\in[0,1]\), the incidence matrix is

\( (p_{kl})=\begin{pmatrix}1-t&t&0\\0&1-t&t\\t&0&1-t\end{pmatrix}. \)

If \(0<t<1\), firm \(f_1\) has positive mass in states with primary \(w_2\). Every such state demands \(w_3\), since \(w_3\) ranks above \(w_2\).

Worker \(w_3\) has positive mass in states with primary \(f_3\). Every such state demands \(f_1\), since \(f_1\) ranks above \(f_3\).

These states block through the probe pair. Hence \(t\in\{0,1\}\).

We interpret \(t=0\) as \(X_v\) false and \(t=1\) as \(X_v\) true. Thus every zero-cost stable plan determines an unsplit Boolean assignment.

5. Clause inputs encode literal truth

Consider a positive occurrence of \(X_v\) in clause \(c\), corresponding to pair \((f_{v,1},h_c)\).

If \(X_v\) is false, every supported state of \(f_{v,1}\) has primary \(w_{v,1}\), which suppresses its auxiliaries. The pair incidence is therefore zero.

If \(X_v\) is true, every supported state of \(f_{v,1}\) has primary \(w_{v,2}\). A state lacking \(h_c\) demands it. On the other side, \(h_c\) always chooses the input \(f_{v,1}\) when available.

Let this pair’s incidence be \(p\). Since both types have mass one and every state coordinate is binary, each endpoint has mass \(1-p\) in states lacking the other endpoint. If \(p<1\), positive-mass states at both endpoints demand each other, contradicting stability. Therefore \(p=1\).

The negative occurrence pair \((g_c,w_{v,3})\) behaves symmetrically:

  • If \(X_v\) is true, the primary \(f_{v,2}\) suppresses the auxiliaries of \(w_{v,3}\), so its incidence is zero.
  • If \(X_v\) is false, its primary is \(f_{v,3}\), and stability forces incidence one.

Consequently, every clause input has incidence one exactly when its literal is true.

Suppose a clause is unsatisfied. All inputs at both \(g_c\) and \(h_c\) then have incidence zero. Their special pair also has incidence zero because the plan has cost zero. Since these are their only acceptable partners, both types have only the empty state in their positive-mass supports.

Both empty states demand the special pair \((g_c,h_c)\), contradicting stability. Thus the assignment extracted from a zero-cost stable plan satisfies every clause.

6. A satisfying assignment gives a zero-cost stable plan

Conversely, fix a satisfying assignment.

For a false variable, use the primary pairs
\((f_{v,1},w_{v,1})\), \((f_{v,2},w_{v,2})\), and \((f_{v,3},w_{v,3})\).

For a true variable, use
\((f_{v,1},w_{v,2})\), \((f_{v,2},w_{v,3})\), and \((f_{v,3},w_{v,1})\).

Also include \((f_{v,1},h_c)\) for every true positive literal, and \((g_c,w_{v,3})\) for every true negative literal. Include no probe pair and no clause special pair.

Give each type mass one at its resulting bundle state, and give each included pair incidence one. The marginal equations follow immediately.

Every bundle is a fixed point of its choice:

  • A false variable’s \(f_{v,1}\) chooses its top primary and has no auxiliaries; its \(w_{v,3}\) chooses its lowest primary together with all its negative-literal auxiliaries.
  • A true variable’s \(f_{v,1}\) chooses its lowest primary together with all its positive-literal auxiliaries; its \(w_{v,3}\) chooses its top primary and has no auxiliaries.
  • Clause types choose exactly their present input sets, including the empty set where appropriate.

The plan is stable:

  • For a false variable, every firm has its top primary, so every absent primary pair is rejected by its firm.
  • For a true variable, every worker has its top primary, so every absent primary pair is rejected by its worker.
  • Every absent signal pair corresponds to a false literal and is rejected by its variable endpoint.
  • Every clause has a true literal. At least one of its gate types therefore has an input and rejects the special pair.
  • An included pair cannot demand an additional partner of the same type, because every output coordinate is at most one.
  • All remaining pairs are unlisted and rejected.

The cost is zero. We have proved the equivalence:
the formula is satisfiable if and only if the constructed society has a stable plan of cost at most \(0\).

With \(n\) variables and \(r\) clauses, the construction has \(m=3n+r\) and \(\tau=6n+2r\). Its choice descriptions, capacities, and cost matrix have polynomial encoding length. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under the requested succinct representation.

7. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

We finally justify membership for the full promised input class, including binary capacities and real state masses.

Suppose a stable plan of cost at most \(B\) exists. Retain its positive-mass firm and worker states as allowed states. There are finitely many such states because each capacity is finite.

Consider the polytope of nonnegative masses on these allowed states satisfying:

  • the \(\tau\) type-mass equations;
  • the \(|I||J|\) pair-incidence balance equations from Section 1;
  • the cost inequality.

This polytope is nonempty and bounded: every state mass is bounded by its type’s prescribed mass. Every feasible point remains stable, since its positive-mass support is contained in the original stable support.

Choose a vertex. It has at most \(K=\tau+|I||J|+1\) positive state masses. Otherwise, on its positive coordinates there would be a nonzero direction annihilating all mass, balance, and cost rows. Sufficiently small perturbations in both directions would preserve nonnegativity and every constraint, contradicting extremality.

The positive masses at this vertex are rational with polynomial bit length. To see this, select a square nonsingular subsystem of the tight constraints determining those masses. Its coefficients are rational input masses and costs, integral state coordinates, and sums of products of costs with state coordinates. Each coefficient has polynomial bit length. After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule bounds the solution bit lengths polynomially in \(K\) and the coefficient bit lengths.

Each listed state also has polynomial encoding length: its coordinates are at most its binary capacity.

A certificate therefore consists of at most \(K\) states and their rational masses. A polynomial-time verifier checks their capacities and fixed-point conditions, the mass and incidence-balance equations, the cost, and every possible blocking combination of listed positive-mass states. The coupling formula in Section 1 supplies the required incidences.

Thus the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with the reduction, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
