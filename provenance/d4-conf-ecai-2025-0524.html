9. Hardness consequences

9.1. Universal-real completeness at degree nine

We use the following external theorem: deciding whether an integer-coefficient biquadratic form is nonnegative everywhere is $\forall\mathbb R$-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions. A biquadratic form is homogeneous of total degree four, with degree two in each of two variable groups. [Bläser–Dutta–Jindal, Theorem 2.3](https://goravjindal.github.io/assets/pdf/soda26-hn.pdf).

Let $h$ be such a form in $N$ total variables. Homogeneity gives
$$ h\geq0\text{ on }\mathbb R^N \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h\geq0\text{ on }[-1,1]^N. $$
Indeed, any negative value can be scaled into the centered cube by multiplying its argument by a positive scalar; its value is multiplied by the fourth power of that scalar.

Define
$$ f(w)=h(2w-\mathbf1). $$
The affine map sends the unit cube onto the centered cube, so
$$ f\geq0\text{ on }[0,1]^N \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h\geq0\text{ on }\mathbb R^N. $$
The polynomial $f$ has degree at most four. Expanding a degree-four monomial under this substitution produces at most
$$ \prod_j(\alpha_j+1)\leq2^{\sum_j\alpha_j}\leq16 $$
terms, so the sparse expansion has polynomial length and coefficient bit size.

Section 8 now supplies a polynomial-time, promise-preserving reduction with $D=9$. Combined with Section 6, this proves $\mathrm{co}\exists\mathbb R$-completeness.

9.2. Additional hardness result: degree five suffices for coNP-hardness

Use the standard NP-completeness theorem for weighted MAX-CUT: given a finite undirected graph with positive integer edge weights and an integer threshold $K$, deciding whether a cut has weight at least $K$ is NP-complete. [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let the edge weights be $a_{jk}$, and define
$$ f(w)= K-\frac12- \sum_{\{j,k\}\in E} a_{jk}\bigl(w_j+w_k-2w_jw_k\bigr). $$
This is a rational, multi-affine polynomial of degree at most two. At a Boolean vertex, the expression in parentheses equals one exactly when the edge crosses the cut. Thus
$$ f(w)=K-\frac12-\text{cut weight} $$
at every Boolean vertex.

If a cut has weight at least $K$, the corresponding vertex satisfies $f\leq-1/2$.

If no such cut exists, integrality of cut weights gives $f\geq1/2$ at every Boolean vertex. For a multi-affine polynomial, repeated affine interpolation in each coordinate gives
$$ f(w)= \sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^n} f(v) \prod_{j:v_j=1}w_j \prod_{j:v_j=0}(1-w_j). $$
The coefficients in this sum are nonnegative and sum to one on the unit cube. Therefore $f\geq1/2$ throughout that cube.

We have proved
$$ \text{no cut has weight at least }K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\geq0\text{ on }[0,1]^n. $$
Applying Section 8 to this degree-two polynomial gives a promise-preserving reduction from the complement of weighted MAX-CUT to uniqueness with $D=5$. Hence uniqueness is many-one $\mathrm{coNP}$-hard already at degree five.

Negating the answer to one uniqueness-oracle call decides weighted MAX-CUT. This also proves polynomial-time Turing $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

10. Precisely what remains open

The exact-output problem under the uniqueness promise is resolved: the tuple is rational and polynomial-time computable.

For decision, the proved $\mathrm{co}\exists\mathbb R$-completeness characterizes its complexity, but does not itself establish the many-one $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness branch of the requested dichotomy.

More precisely,
$$ \text{uniqueness is many-one }\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{NP}\subseteq\forall\mathbb R \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{coNP}\subseteq\exists\mathbb R. $$

For necessity, compose any promise-preserving many-one reduction from an NP language with the agreeing $\forall\mathbb R$ language from Section 6. Closure under polynomial-time preimages places that NP language in $\forall\mathbb R$.

For sufficiency, if $\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\forall\mathbb R$, every NP language reduces to biquadratic nonnegativity by its completeness theorem, and then to promised uniqueness by Section 9.1.

The required inclusion is not proved here. Accordingly, the overall answer remains partial under the conventional many-one interpretation.
