The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite adjacency graph \(G=(B,E)\), rational masses \(\mu_{b,p}\ge 0\) over location-party types \((b,p)\) with \(\sum_{b,p}\mu_{b,p}=1\), \(K\) connected districts, seat counts \(N_1,\ldots,N_K\) summing to \(N\), and a population-balance tolerance, choose a partition of \(B\) into connected districts satisfying the balance constraints to maximize the total number of \(R\) seats. For district \(D_k\), let \(y_{R,k}=\frac{\sum_{b\in D_k}\mu_{b,R}}{\sum_{b\in D_k,p}\mu_{b,p}}\), and let \(n_{R,k}\) be the tie-broken PAV maximizer of \(y_{R,k}H_\ell+(1-y_{R,k})H_{N_k-\ell}\) over \(0\le \ell\le N_k\), where \(H_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}1/i\); output the optimal value \(N^{-1}\sum_k n_{R,k}\) and a maximizing map.

1. Result

The exact optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even under all the following restrictions:

  • \(G\) is a complete graph.
  • \(K=2\), \(N_1=N_2=3\), and \(\epsilon=0\).
  • Every block has positive mass for both parties, so \(\tau=2m\).
  • All masses are dyadic rationals.
  • A feasible map is supplied with the instance.

Thus, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), there is no algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof below establishes hardness of optimizing the election outcome on feasible instances.

2. Exact evaluation of a district

Consider a district with \(s\geq1\) seats and Republican mass share \(y\in[0,1]\). Define \(F(\ell)=yH_\ell+(1-y)H_{s-\ell}\).

For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}\),

\[ F(j)-F(j-1) =\frac{y}{j}-\frac{1-y}{s-j+1} =\frac{(s+1)y-j}{j(s-j+1)}. \]

The denominator is positive. Consequently, the score increases whenever \(j<(s+1)y\), remains equal across a step whenever \(j=(s+1)y\), and decreases thereafter. Since ties select the smaller Republican seat count, the selected count is exactly the number of strictly positive increments. Therefore,

\[ n_R(y,s)= \max\!\left\{0,\, \min\!\left\{s,\left\lceil(s+1)y\right\rceil-1\right\}\right\}. \]

This agrees, for positive \(y\), with [Lemma C.1 of the supplied PAV source](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.07083v6); the derivation also covers \(y=0\).

In particular, if \(r_k=\sum_{b\in D_k}\mu_{b,R}\), we can compute the district outcome directly from

\[ n_{R,k}= \max\!\left\{0,\, \min\!\left\{N_k, \left\lceil\frac{(N_k+1)r_k}{a_k}\right\rceil-1 \right\}\right\}. \]

No harmonic numbers or explicit candidate lists need to be constructed.

3. Preparing a dyadic PARTITION instance

We reduce from PARTITION on positive binary-encoded integers, whose \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is a classical result of [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may assume the given integers \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\) have sum \(2T\): multiplying every integer by two preserves the existence of an equal partition and makes the total even.

Choose a power of two \(Q>2T\), and append two integers \(Q-T\). Denote the resulting list by \(w_1,\ldots,w_t\), where \(t=n+2\). Its total is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{t}w_i=2T+2(Q-T)=2Q. \]

We claim that some subset of the \(w_i\) sums to \(Q\) precisely when some subset of the original \(u_i\) sums to \(T\).

Indeed, a subset summing to \(Q\) must contain exactly one appended integer:

  • Without either appended integer, its sum is at most \(2T<Q\).
  • With both appended integers, its sum is at least \(2(Q-T)>Q\).

After including exactly one appended integer, the original integers selected must sum to \(Q-(Q-T)=T\). Conversely, any original subset summing to \(T\), together with one appended integer, sums to \(Q\).

Choosing the least suitable power of two gives \(Q\leq4T\). Hence this transformation has polynomial encoding length.

Create a complete graph on the blocks \(x,z,v_1,\ldots,v_t\). Set \(K=2\), \(N_1=N_2=3\), \(N=6\), and \(\epsilon=0\).

Assign masses as follows:

| Block | Republican mass | Democratic mass | Total mass |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(x\) | \(3/32\) | \(5/32\) | \(1/4\) |
| \(z\) | \(1/32\) | \(7/32\) | \(1/4\) |
| \(v_i\) | \(w_i/(16Q)\) | \(3w_i/(16Q)\) | \(w_i/(4Q)\) |

Because \(\sum_iw_i=2Q\), the item blocks have total mass \(1/2\), and all blocks together have total mass one. Every listed mass is positive and dyadic.

The graph has \(m=t+2\) vertices and \(\tau=2m\) supported types. Its encoding, including all masses, has polynomial length in the original PARTITION input.

Every nonempty induced subgraph of a complete graph is connected. Exact balance requires each district to have mass \(3/6=1/2\).

A feasible map always exists: place \(\{x,z\}\) in one district and all item blocks in the other.

5. Classifying every feasible map

There are exactly two cases, according to whether \(x\) and \(z\) belong to the same district.

*Case A: the anchor blocks are together.*

Their combined mass is \(1/2\). Since every item block has positive mass, their district contains no item block. All item blocks therefore constitute the other district.

The Republican mass in each district is \(1/8\). Thus each district has Republican share \(y=1/4\), and Section 2 gives

\[ n_R(1/4,3)=\lceil1\rceil-1=0. \]

Every feasible map in this case awards zero Republican seats.

*Case B: the anchor blocks are separated.*

Let \(I\) index the item blocks accompanying \(x\). Exact balance requires

\[ \frac14+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{w_i}{4Q}=\frac12, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \sum_{i\in I}w_i=Q. \]

Thus this case is possible precisely when the prepared PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

The item mass in each district is \(1/4\), and one quarter of every item block’s mass is Republican. Hence each district receives Republican item mass \(1/16\).

The Republican shares in the districts containing \(x\) and \(z\), respectively, are

\[ y_x=\frac{3/32+1/16}{1/2}=\frac5{16}, \qquad y_z=\frac{1/32+1/16}{1/2}=\frac3{16}. \]

Applying the exact seat rule,

\[ n_R(5/16,3)=\left\lceil\frac54\right\rceil-1=1, \qquad n_R(3/16,3)=\left\lceil\frac34\right\rceil-1=0. \]

Every feasible map in this case awards exactly one Republican seat.

Together, the two cases prove

\[ \mathrm{OPT}= \begin{cases} 1/6,&\text{if the original PARTITION instance is a yes-instance},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Therefore exact optimization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, with all the restrictions stated in Section 1.

6. Verification and exact running times

For a supplied map, feasibility and the objective are computable in polynomial time, including when seat counts are binary encoded.

To justify the arithmetic bound, take the product of the denominators of all supported masses as a common denominator. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Since the masses sum to one, every district mass and party mass has an integer numerator with \(O(L)\) bits over this denominator. Balance comparisons and the ceiling quotient from Section 2 consequently involve only \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Standard integer arithmetic performs each such calculation in \(O(L^2)\) bit operations.

Connectivity is checked by traversing the graph while retaining only edges whose endpoints receive the same district label. The resulting verification time is bounded by

\[ O\!\left((m+|E|+\tau+K)L^2\right). \]

Thus the decision problem asking whether a feasible map awards at least a specified Republican seat share belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Section 5 proves that it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even for the fixed threshold \(1/6\) and fixed seat counts \(N_1=N_2=3\).

An unconditional exact algorithm enumerates all \(K^m\) assignments of blocks to district labels. For each assignment, it checks nonemptiness, connectivity, positive mass, and balance, then computes its seat total. It retains an assignment with the largest total. If no assignment passes the checks, it reports that no feasible map exists.

This returns both the exact optimum and a maximizing map in time

\[ O\!\left(K^m(m+|E|+\tau+K)L^2\right) \subseteq O\!\left(K^m(m^2+\tau+K)L^2\right). \]

The bound depends on seat counts through their binary encoding lengths, rather than their numerical magnitudes. For \(K=1\), checking the sole map and evaluating its outcome is polynomial. The two-district reduction establishes the general hardness result.
