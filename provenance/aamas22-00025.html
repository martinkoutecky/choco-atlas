The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite resident and position type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\ge0\) summing to \(1\), and strict orders of each \(a\in A\) over \(B\) and each \(b\in B\) over \(A\), let \(x_{ab}\ge0\) satisfy \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b\). Define stability by requiring that no pair \((a,b)\) has both \(P_{ab}(x)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}>0\) and \(Q_{ab}(x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}>0\). With type ranks \(\rho_A(a,b)\) and \(\rho_B(b,a)\), define \(S_A(x)=\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\rho_A(a,b)\) and \(S_B(x)=\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\rho_B(b,a)\). Promised that the \(A\)- or \(B\)-proposing batch-deferred-acceptance extreme matching satisfies \(S_A(x^A)\ge S_B(x^A)\) or \(S_A(x^B)\le S_B(x^B)\), output a stable mass matching minimizing \(\lvert S_A(x)-S_B(x)\rvert\).

Set \(\tau=|A|+|B|\), the total support size, not the number of possible preference types, and \(m=\max\{|A|,|B|\}\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Each type has a strict complete order over the opposite support, with ranks \(\rho_A(a,b),\rho_B(b,a)\) starting at \(1\).
A mass matching is a real matrix \(x\ge0\) with \(\sum_bx_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_ax_{ab}=\nu_b\). It is stable when, for every \((a,b)\), at least one of \(P_{ab}(x)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(Q_{ab}(x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\) is zero.

Define \(x^A,x^B\) by mass deferred acceptance with the indicated side proposing: initially all mass is free; each round, each proposer offers its free mass to its favorite receiver that has not previously rejected positive mass from it; each receiver retains its most preferred offered and previously held mass up to its capacity, allowing partial retention, and rejects the remainder. Repeat until all mass is held.
Restrict inputs to the promise \(S_A(x^A)\ge S_B(x^A)\) or \(S_A(x^B)\le S_B(x^B)\).

Result

The promised problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). More precisely:

  • If \(S_A(x^A)\ge S_B(x^A)\), then \(x^A\) minimizes the absolute rank gap.
  • If \(S_A(x^B)\le S_B(x^B)\), then \(x^B\) minimizes the absolute rank gap.

The essential point is to establish rank-score bounds for every real stable mass matching, and then compute the endpoints using a polynomial-time stable-allocation algorithm.

1. Correspondence with stable allocation

Use the complete bipartite graph with vertex sets \(A,B\), vertex quotas \(\mu_a,\nu_b\), and edge capacities

\[ u_{ab}=\min\{\mu_a,\nu_b\}. \]

These capacities impose no additional restriction on a mass matching: its marginal equations already imply \(x_{ab}\le u_{ab}\).

In the capacitated stable-allocation model, an edge blocks precisely when

\[ x_{ab}<u_{ab},\qquad P_{ab}(x)>0,\qquad Q_{ab}(x)>0. \]

This agrees with the task’s stability condition. Indeed, if \(x_{ab}=u_{ab}\), then either \(x_{ab}=\mu_a\), making \(P_{ab}(x)=0\), or \(x_{ab}=\nu_b\), making \(Q_{ab}(x)=0\). Thus the additional capacity test cannot exclude any blocking pair under the task’s definition.

The cited algorithm uses dummy vertices to represent unassigned mass. To check that this convention causes no discrepancy, add a dummy resident and a dummy position, each of quota \(1\), and rank a dummy last at every original vertex.

For any feasible augmented allocation, write

\[ r_a=\mu_a-\sum_{b\in B}x_{ab}, \qquad s_b=\nu_b-\sum_{a\in A}x_{ab}. \]

These quantities are the allocations to the opposite dummy. They satisfy

\[ \sum_a r_a=\sum_b s_b. \]

If this common value were positive, some \(a,b\) would satisfy \(r_a>0\) and \(s_b>0\). Consequently,

\[ x_{ab}<\mu_a,\qquad x_{ab}<\nu_b, \]

and both vertices would have positive allocation to a dummy, which they strictly prefer each other to. Hence \((a,b)\) would block. Therefore every stable augmented allocation assigns all original mass to original vertices.

Conversely, a stable mass matching extends to a stable augmented allocation by assigning mass \(1\) between the two dummies and zero between original and dummy vertices. Edges incident to exactly one dummy cannot block because that dummy is last in the original vertex’s list; the dummy–dummy edge is saturated.

Thus restriction and extension identify the two sets of stable solutions exactly.

2. The structural theorem being used

We use these established results for bipartite stable allocation with strict preferences and real quotas and capacities:

  • Deferred acceptance has a unique proposing-side optimal endpoint, independent of proposal order.
  • From any stable allocation, a finite sequence of stability-preserving rotations reaches the receiving-side optimal endpoint. Each rotation transfers equal mass from a better to a worse partner at each participating proposer, and from a worse to a better partner at each participating receiver.
  • The endpoints can be computed in \(O(EN)\) arithmetic operations, where \(E,N\) are the graph’s edge and vertex counts.

These are provided by Lemma 1, Lemmas 6–8 and the augmenting-path algorithm in Sections 3–4 of [Dean and Munshi, *Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

All hypotheses hold here: the graph is finite and bipartite, preferences are strict, and the quotas and capacities are positive rationals.

The task’s batch deferred acceptance produces these same endpoints. To see the correspondence, process a round’s offers sequentially, postponing newly freed mass until the next round. At each receiver, repeated retention of its best mass gives exactly the same result as retaining its best mass from all offers together. Offers to an already saturated edge, or to a receiver that has previously rejected that type, cannot increase the retained amount of that type; omitting these redundant offers gives the standard stable-allocation procedure. Hence its endpoints restrict to the task’s \(x^A,x^B\).

3. Rank-score bounds for every stable mass matching

Claim. For every stable mass matching \(x\),

\[ S_A(x^A)\le S_A(x)\le S_A(x^B), \]

and

\[ S_B(x^B)\le S_B(x)\le S_B(x^A). \]

Proof. First orient the structural theorem with \(A\) proposing. Starting from \(x\), follow its stable rotations to the \(B\)-optimal endpoint \(x^B\).

Suppose one rotation transfers mass \(\delta>0\) at resident \(a\) from position \(b_{\mathrm{old}}\) to the less preferred position \(b_{\mathrm{new}}\). Its contribution to the change in \(S_A\) is

\[ \delta\bigl(\rho_A(a,b_{\mathrm{new}}) -\rho_A(a,b_{\mathrm{old}})\bigr)>0. \]

At a participating position \(b\), the corresponding transfer replaces resident \(a_{\mathrm{old}}\) by a more preferred resident \(a_{\mathrm{new}}\). Its contribution to the change in \(S_B\) is

\[ \delta\bigl(\rho_B(b,a_{\mathrm{new}}) -\rho_B(b,a_{\mathrm{old}})\bigr)<0. \]

Nonparticipating vertices contribute zero. Summing over each rotation and then over the finite sequence gives

\[ S_A(x)\le S_A(x^B), \qquad S_B(x)\ge S_B(x^B). \]

Now interchange the sides. Starting from \(x\), the analogous rotation sequence reaches \(x^A\); throughout it, \(S_B\) increases and \(S_A\) decreases. Therefore

\[ S_B(x)\le S_B(x^A), \qquad S_A(x)\ge S_A(x^A). \]

Together these prove the claim. The theorem applies to real allocations, so this argument includes irrational stable mass matchings. \(\square\)

4. Applying the endpoint-sign promise

Define the signed gap

\[ D(x)=S_A(x)-S_B(x). \]

The bounds just proved imply, for every stable \(x\),

\[ D(x^A)\le D(x)\le D(x^B). \]

We now treat the two promised cases.

Case 1: \(D(x^A)\ge0\). Every stable \(x\) satisfies \(D(x)\ge D(x^A)\ge0\). Hence

\[ |D(x)|=D(x)\ge D(x^A)=|D(x^A)|. \]

Thus \(x^A\) is optimal.

Case 2: \(D(x^B)\le0\). Every stable \(x\) satisfies \(D(x)\le D(x^B)\le0\). Hence

\[ |D(x)|=-D(x)\ge-D(x^B)=|D(x^B)|. \]

Thus \(x^B\) is optimal.

The promise guarantees that at least one case applies. If both apply, the endpoint bounds force every stable matching to have gap zero.

5. Algorithm and bit complexity

Compute both endpoints using the polynomial-time stable-allocation algorithm. Return \(x^A\) if \(D(x^A)\ge0\); otherwise return \(x^B\). The preceding proof establishes correctness on every promised input.

For completeness, the arithmetic-operation bound also yields polynomial bit complexity here. Let \(H\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Scale all quotas and capacities by \(H\), making them integers.

The elementary augmenting-path implementation uses comparisons, minima, additions and subtractions of allocation quantities. Consequently, all allocations remain integers in the scaled instance. Each edge allocation and residual capacity is bounded by \(H\), and total augmented mass is \(2H\); thus the numerical quantities have \(O(L)\) bits.

The augmented graph has

\[ N=\tau+2, \qquad E=|A||B|+O(\tau)\le m^2+O(\tau). \]

The \(O(EN)\) arithmetic-operation bound therefore gives polynomial bit complexity in \(m,\tau,L\). Dividing the output by \(H\) produces an exact rational mass matching. Its rank scores and their signs can likewise be computed exactly in polynomial time.

Ordinary batch deferred acceptance need not itself run in polynomial time; its endpoints are computed by the faster algorithm cited above.
