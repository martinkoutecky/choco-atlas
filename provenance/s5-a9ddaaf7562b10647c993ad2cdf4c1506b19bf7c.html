Proposition A.1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational item values \(v_j>0\) and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\) with \(\sum_j q_j\ge1\), let \(x_S\) be the mass receiving the nonempty subset \(S\subseteq[d]\), with \(\sum_S x_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni j}x_S=q_j\). Is it NP-hard to return a feasible rational distribution with \(W(x)>\mathrm{OPT}-1\), where \(W(x)=\exp(\sum_S x_S\ln(\sum_{j\in S}v_j))\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x W(x)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge1\) item categories, with binary rational values \(v_j>0\) and per-capita supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_{j=1}^m q_j\ge1\). Every agent has the same additive valuation; a bundle contains at most one item of each category.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here the society has total mass \(1\) and a single valuation type, so \(\tau=1\); \(\tau\) denotes input support size, not the number of possible bundles.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_S\ge0\), indexed by nonempty \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), satisfying \(\sum_S x_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni j}x_S=q_j\) for every \(j\).
Define \(W(x)=\exp\bigl(\sum_S x_S\ln(\sum_{j\in S}v_j)\bigr)\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x W(x)\), over feasible allocations. The computational problem is to return a feasible rational allocation, encoded as a list of its positive-mass bundles and their binary rational masses, with omitted masses zero.
Does returning such an allocation with \(W(x)>\mathrm{OPT}-1\) admit an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length, or is this approximation problem NP-hard?
For finite agents and indivisible goods with identical additive valuations, achieving Nash social welfare strictly greater than the optimum minus \(1\) is NP-hard, even with two agents; an additive PTAS achieves error \(\varepsilon v_{\max}\), where \(v_{\max}=\max_j v_j\). [Inoue–Kobayashi](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jorsj/68/4/68_133/_pdf)
No result settling the stated mass-allocation approximation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Renamed the number of item categories from d to m to match the required running-time convention.
  • Interpreted the identical valuation society as one input type of mass 1, so tau is 1 and is distinct from allocation support size.
  • Made nonnegativity of allocation masses explicit and defined OPT over real feasible masses.
  • Specified sparse output encoding by positive-mass bundles with binary rational masses, rather than requiring an exponentially long vector.
  • Made explicit that receiving a subset means receiving at most one item from each category.

Result

The approximation problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\), even when \(\tau=1\) and every supply is \(q_j=\tfrac12\). Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm with the stated guarantee would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof uses the required explicit output: on a suitably scaled yes-instance of PARTITION, every valid approximate allocation must list a bundle witnessing an exact partition. No numerical evaluation of logarithms is needed.

1. A quantitative bound for the geometric mean

Lemma. Let \(r>d>0\). Suppose positive numbers \(u_i\), with nonnegative weights \(p_i\), satisfy

\(\sum_i p_i=1,\qquad \sum_i p_i u_i=r,\qquad |u_i-r|\ge d\ \text{whenever }p_i>0.\)

Then

\(\exp\!\left(\sum_i p_i\ln u_i\right)\le\sqrt{r^2-d^2}.\)

Proof. Set \(a=r-d\), \(b=r+d\), and let \(\ell\) be the affine function through \((a,\ln a)\) and \((b,\ln b)\). Its slope is

\(s=\dfrac{\ln b-\ln a}{b-a}=\dfrac1{b-a}\int_a^b\dfrac{dt}{t},\)

so \(1/b\le s\le1/a\).

For \(0<u\le a\),

\(\ln a-\ln u=\int_u^a\dfrac{dt}{t}\ge\dfrac{a-u}{a}\ge s(a-u),\)

and hence \(\ln u\le\ell(u)\). For \(u\ge b\),

\(\ln u-\ln b=\int_b^u\dfrac{dt}{t}\le\dfrac{u-b}{b}\le s(u-b),\)

which again gives \(\ln u\le\ell(u)\).

Every positive-weight \(u_i\) lies in one of these two ranges. Since \(\ell\) is affine,

\(\sum_i p_i\ln u_i\le\sum_i p_i\ell(u_i)=\ell(r)=\tfrac12\ln(ab).\)

Exponentiating proves the lemma.

2. Exact structure when every supply is one half

This special case explains why arbitrary real allocation masses do not eliminate the partition obstruction.

Proposition. Suppose \(m\ge2\), all \(v_j>0\), and \(q_j=\tfrac12\). Define

\(V(S)=\sum_{j\in S}v_j,\qquad \mu=\tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^m v_j,\qquad \delta=\min_{\varnothing\ne S\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,m\}}|V(S)-\mu|.\)

Then

\(\mathrm{OPT}=\sqrt{\mu^2-\delta^2}.\)

Moreover, an optimum assigns mass \(\tfrac12\) to a minimizing bundle \(S\) and mass \(\tfrac12\) to its complement.

Proof. Every feasible allocation has mean utility

\(\sum_S x_SV(S)=\sum_j v_j\sum_{S\ni j}x_S=\tfrac12\sum_jv_j=\mu.\)

Every nonempty proper bundle has utility strictly between \(0\) and \(2\mu\), so \(0\le\delta<\mu\).

If \(\delta=0\), Jensen’s inequality gives \(W(x)\le\mu\).

If \(\delta>0\), every nonempty proper bundle satisfies \(|V(S)-\mu|\ge\delta\). The full bundle also satisfies this inequality because its utility is \(2\mu\). The lemma therefore gives \(W(x)\le\sqrt{\mu^2-\delta^2}\).

For a minimizing proper bundle \(S\), its complement is nonempty and has utility \(2\mu-V(S)\). Assigning each of these two bundles mass \(\tfrac12\) gives total mass \(1\) and every category marginal \(\tfrac12\). Its welfare is

\(\sqrt{V(S)(2\mu-V(S))}=\sqrt{\mu^2-\delta^2}.\)

This attains the upper bound in both cases.

3. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete PARTITION problem: given positive binary integers \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\), determine whether a subset sums to half their total. Its completeness is established in [Karp’s *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf). We may restrict to \(m\ge2\), since a singleton positive-integer instance is always a no-instance.

Put

\(B=\sum_{j=1}^m b_j,\qquad a_j=2b_j.\)

Thus \(B\ge2\) and \(\sum_j a_j=2B\). Construct the mass-allocation instance

\(q_j=\tfrac12,\qquad v_j=2B\,a_j=4B\,b_j.\)

The society consists of one valuation type of mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\). All values are positive integers, and \(\sum_jq_j=m/2\ge1\).

For each bundle define its unscaled utility

\(u(S)=\sum_{j\in S}a_j,\)

so its actual utility is \(2B\,u(S)\). Every feasible allocation satisfies

\(\sum_Sx_Su(S)=\sum_ja_jq_j=B.\)

Consequently, Jensen’s inequality gives the universal upper bound

\(W(x)=2B\exp\!\left(\sum_Sx_S\ln u(S)\right)\le2B^2.\)

Yes-instances attain the upper bound

Suppose the PARTITION instance has a subset \(S\) with \(\sum_{j\in S}b_j=B/2\). Then

\(u(S)=u(S^c)=B.\)

Positivity of the integers ensures that both bundles are nonempty. Giving each mass \(\tfrac12\) is feasible and has welfare \(2B^2\). Hence

\(\mathrm{OPT}=2B^2.\)

An allocation omitting every partition witness loses more than one

Suppose a feasible allocation contains no positive-mass bundle with \(u(S)=B\).

All \(u(S)\) and \(B\) are integers. Therefore every positive-mass bundle satisfies \(|u(S)-B|\ge1\). Apply the lemma with \(r=B\) and \(d=1\), whose hypotheses hold because \(B\ge2\). It gives

\(W(x)\le2B\sqrt{B^2-1}.\)

The gap from the upper bound is strictly greater than \(1\):

\(2B^2-2B\sqrt{B^2-1} =\dfrac{2B}{B+\sqrt{B^2-1}}>1,\)

where the last inequality follows from \(B+\sqrt{B^2-1}<2B\).

On a yes-instance, such an allocation therefore satisfies

\(W(x)<\mathrm{OPT}-1.\)

Thus every allocation meeting the requested guarantee on a yes-instance contains a positive-mass bundle with \(u(S)=B\). That bundle witnesses the original partition because

\(u(S)=B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j\in S}b_j=B/2.\)

4. Polynomial encoding and witness recovery

Assume an algorithm returns the required allocation in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). To decide PARTITION, construct the instance above, run the algorithm, and inspect each listed bundle. Accept precisely when one satisfies \(\sum_{j\in S}a_j=B\).

On a yes-instance, the preceding argument forces such a bundle into the output. On a no-instance, no such bundle exists.

The construction has polynomial bit length: \(B\) has at most the maximum input integer bit length plus \(\lceil\log_2m\rceil+1\) bits, and the products \(4Bb_j\) also have polynomial bit length. A polynomial-time algorithm can emit only polynomially many output bits, so scanning its bundle list and computing the integer sums takes polynomial time.

This is a polynomial-time reduction using one call to the allocation algorithm and polynomial-time decoding of any valid answer. It proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of the stated search problem. The argument allows arbitrary real feasible masses; it uses the required rational list encoding only to recover the witness efficiently.
