The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an excludable project, finite valuation types \(0\le v_1<\dots<v_\tau\le1\), rational masses \(\mu_j\) summing to \(1\), and per-capita cost \(\kappa>0\), maximize served mass \(\sum_j\mu_jx_j\) over service fractions \(x_j\in[0,1]\) and average payments \(p_j\ge0\), subject to \(v_jx_j-p_j\ge v_jx_k-p_k\) for all \(j,k\), \(v_jx_j-p_j\ge0\), and \(\sum_j\mu_jp_j=\kappa\) (report infeasibility if necessary). Characterize when the threshold rule serving all types above \(t\), none below \(t\), and a fraction at \(v_j=t\) when needed, with served agents paying \(t\), attains the optimum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_j\), where \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There is one excludable, nonrivalrous project, so \(m=1\). Valuations \(0\le v_1<\cdots<v_\tau\le1\) and the fixed per-capita construction cost \(\kappa>0\) are binary rationals. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A menu assigns each reported support type a service fraction \(x_j\in[0,1]\) and an average payment \(p_j\ge0\). Fractions are admission probabilities, and utility is evaluated in expectation; reports range over the support, with truthful reporting required only to be weakly optimal.
Maximize \(\sum_j\mu_jx_j\), subject to \(v_jx_j-p_j\ge v_jx_k-p_k\) for every \(j,k\), \(v_jx_j-p_j\ge0\) for every \(j\), and \(\sum_j\mu_jp_j=\kappa\). Report infeasibility if no construction-financing menu exists.
A threshold menu has \(t\in[0,1]\), assigns \(x_j=1\) when \(v_j>t\), \(x_j=0\) when \(v_j<t\), permits \(x_j\in[0,1]\) when \(v_j=t\), and sets \(p_j=tx_j\); it must satisfy the same financing equality.

Is exact served-mass maximization polynomial-time solvable, or is its decision version with a binary-rational served-mass target \(q\in[0,1]\) NP-hard?
For which feasible inputs does some threshold menu attain the optimum?

For finite agents with i.i.d. valuations and a differentiable density on \([0,1]\), equal sharing by the largest willing coalition maximizes expected participation for two agents under log-concavity, and for three agents under log-concavity and a nonincreasing density. The comparison class assigns each coalition nonnegative shares summing to construction cost, weakly decreasing as the coalition expands, and selects its largest willing coalition. Result

Exact served-mass maximization is polynomial-time solvable. For \(m=1\), an exact algorithm uses \(O(\tau^2L^2)\) bit operations and either reports infeasibility or returns an optimal rational menu.

Threshold menus are not always optimal. Below is a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for threshold optimality, expressed entirely in the input data.

1. Tail masses and maximal revenue

Define the tail masses and tail revenues by

\(S_i=\sum_{j=i}^{\tau}\mu_j,\qquad a_i=v_iS_i,\qquad A=\max_{1\le i\le\tau}a_i.\)

Thus \(S_1=1\), and the strict positivity of the masses gives \(S_1>S_2>\cdots>S_\tau>0\).

Lemma 1. Every incentive-compatible menu has a monotone allocation:

\(0\le x_1\le\cdots\le x_\tau\le1.\)

For such an allocation, put \(x_0=0\) and \(y_i=x_i-x_{i-1}\). The componentwise largest payments compatible with incentive compatibility and individual rationality are

\(\overline p_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}v_i y_i.\)

These payments are nonnegative, and their total revenue is

\(R(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\overline p_j=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}a_i y_i.\)

The served mass is

\(s(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_jx_j=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}S_i y_i.\)

Proof. Adding the incentive constraints for types \(j\) and \(k\) gives

\((v_j-v_k)(x_j-x_k)\ge0.\)

Because valuations are strictly ordered, allocations are monotone.

Individual rationality gives \(p_1\le v_1x_1\). The incentive constraint preventing type \(j\) from reporting \(j-1\) gives

\(p_j-p_{j-1}\le v_j(x_j-x_{j-1}).\)

Telescoping proves \(p_j\le\overline p_j\).

Conversely, the proposed payments satisfy individual rationality because

\(\overline p_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}v_i y_i\le v_j\sum_{i=1}^{j}y_i=v_jx_j.\)

For \(j>k\),

\(v_k(x_j-x_k)\le\overline p_j-\overline p_k=\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}v_i y_i\le v_j(x_j-x_k).\)

The right inequality prevents type \(j\) from reporting \(k\), and the left inequality prevents type \(k\) from reporting \(j\). Thus all incentive constraints hold. Nonnegativity follows from \(v_i,y_i\ge0\). Finally, interchanging finite sums gives the revenue and served-mass identities. \(\square\)

2. Feasibility and exact optimization

Proposition 2. A construction-financing menu exists if and only if \(\kappa\le A\).

Proof. Every feasible menu satisfies

\(\kappa=\sum_j\mu_jp_j\le R(y)=\sum_i a_i y_i\le A,\)

where the last inequality uses \(\sum_i y_i=x_\tau\le1\).

Conversely, suppose \(\kappa\le A\), and choose \(h\) with \(a_h=A\). Since \(\kappa>0\), necessarily \(A>0\). Set \(y_h=\kappa/A\) and all other increments to zero. Lemma 1 supplies an admissible menu with revenue exactly \(\kappa\). \(\square\)

If \(\kappa\le v_1\), the optimal served mass is \(1\): take \(x_j=1\) and \(p_j=\kappa\) for every type. Incentive compatibility holds because all reports have the same outcome; individual rationality follows from \(v_j\ge v_1\ge\kappa\).

It remains to consider

\(v_1<\kappa\le A.\)

Consider the following auxiliary optimization problem:

maximize \(s(y)=\sum_i S_i y_i\), subject to

\(y_i\ge0,\qquad \sum_i y_i\le1,\qquad R(y)=\sum_i a_i y_i\ge\kappa.\)

Every financing menu supplies a feasible point of this problem, so its optimum is initially only an upper bound.

Lemma 3. Every optimum of the auxiliary problem satisfies

\(R(y)=\kappa,\qquad \sum_i y_i=1.\)

Consequently, its optimum equals the optimal served mass of the original menu problem.

Proof. The feasible region is nonempty and compact, so an optimum exists. Let \(e_1\) denote the vector whose first coordinate is \(1\) and whose other coordinates are zero. It has revenue \(v_1\) and served mass \(1\).

Suppose a feasible \(y\) has \(R(y)>\kappa\). Define

\(\theta=\frac{\kappa-v_1}{R(y)-v_1},\qquad y'=\theta y+(1-\theta)e_1.\)

Then \(0<\theta<1\), and \(R(y')=\kappa\). Moreover, \(s(y)<1\): equality would force every \(x_j=1\), since every mass is positive, and hence would force \(y=e_1\) and \(R(y)=v_1<\kappa\). Therefore

\(s(y')=\theta s(y)+(1-\theta)>s(y).\)

Thus an optimum cannot have revenue strictly above \(\kappa\).

Now suppose \(c=\sum_i y_i<1\). Replacing \(y\) by \(y+(1-c)e_1\) preserves all auxiliary constraints: revenue increases by the nonnegative quantity \((1-c)v_1\). Served mass increases by \(1-c>0\). Thus an optimum must have \(c=1\).

At an auxiliary optimum, Lemma 1 supplies admissible payments with revenue \(R(y)=\kappa\). This attains the auxiliary upper bound in the original problem. \(\square\)

This establishes a useful stronger statement: when \(\kappa>v_1\), every optimal financing menu has

\(x_\tau=1,\qquad R(y)=\kappa,\qquad p_j=\overline p_j\text{ for every }j.\)

Indeed, its allocation must also optimize the auxiliary problem. Furthermore, \(p_j\le\overline p_j\), and equality of their weighted sums, together with \(\mu_j>0\), forces equality coordinatewise.

An explicit formula for the optimum

There is an optimal increment vector with at most two positive coordinates.

To prove this without invoking linear-programming algorithms, choose an optimum with the smallest number of positive coordinates. If it has at least three, there is a nonzero vector \(d\), supported on those coordinates, satisfying

\(\sum_i d_i=0,\qquad \sum_i a_i d_i=0.\)

Small perturbations in either direction preserve nonnegativity and both equalities. If \(\sum_i S_i d_i\ne0\), one direction improves the objective, a contradiction. Otherwise, move along \(d\) until a positive coordinate becomes zero. This preserves optimality and contradicts minimality of the support.

If two positive coordinates have equal revenues, retaining only the one with larger \(S_i\) weakly improves the objective and reduces the support. Hence a two-coordinate optimum can be chosen with distinct revenues.

It follows that, for \(v_1<\kappa\le A\), the optimum \(V(\kappa)\) is the maximum of the following candidates:

  • \(S_i\), for every \(i\) satisfying \(a_i=\kappa\);
  • \(\displaystyle \frac{(a_k-\kappa)S_i+(\kappa-a_i)S_k}{a_k-a_i}\), for every pair satisfying \(a_i<\kappa<a_k\).

This candidate set is nonempty: \(a_1=v_1<\kappa\), while some \(a_k\ge\kappa\).

For a maximizing pair, take

\(y_i=\frac{a_k-\kappa}{a_k-a_i},\qquad y_k=\frac{\kappa-a_i}{a_k-a_i},\)

with all other coordinates zero. Recover the optimal menu using

\(x_j=\sum_{\ell\le j}y_\ell,\qquad p_j=\sum_{\ell\le j}v_\ell y_\ell.\)

Geometrically, \(V(\kappa)\) is the greatest second coordinate among convex combinations of the points \((a_i,S_i)\) whose first coordinate is \(\kappa\).

3. Exact characterization of threshold optimality

Assume the input is feasible.

If \(\kappa\le v_1\), a threshold menu is optimal: choose \(t=\kappa\) and serve everyone, including the whole atom at \(v_1\) if \(t=v_1\).

For the remaining case \(\kappa>v_1\), define the following test for an index \(i\ge2\).

First require

\(a_i=\kappa,\qquad a_j<a_i\text{ for every }j<i.\)

When this holds, define

\(\displaystyle B_i=\max_{j<i}\frac{S_j-S_i}{a_i-a_j},\)

and

\(\displaystyle U_i=\min_{\substack{j>i\\a_j>a_i}}\frac{S_i-S_j}{a_j-a_i},\)

where \(U_i=+\infty\) if its indexing set is empty.

Theorem 4. Some threshold menu attains the optimum if and only if either:

  • \(\kappa\le v_1\); or
  • an index \(i\ge2\) satisfies the requirements above and \(B_i\le U_i\).

In the second case, an optimal threshold is \(t=v_i\), serving every type \(j\ge i\) fully and excluding every type \(j<i\).

Why an optimal threshold must serve a whole tail

Suppose \(\kappa>v_1\) and a threshold menu is optimal. The stronger statement following Lemma 3 applies.

If the threshold equals no support valuation, its nonempty served set is a full tail beginning at some \(i\), with \(t<v_i\). Its actual revenue is \(tS_i\), while its maximal revenue is \(v_iS_i>tS_i\). This contradicts \(R(y)=\kappa\).

Now suppose \(t=v_i\), and let \(\alpha=x_i\).

If \(i<\tau\), the nonzero allocation increments are \(y_i=\alpha\) and \(y_{i+1}=1-\alpha\). Its actual and maximal revenues differ by

\(\displaystyle R(y)-\kappa=(1-\alpha)(v_{i+1}-v_i)S_{i+1}.\)

Strictly increasing valuations and positive tail masses imply that this difference vanishes only when \(\alpha=1\).

If \(i=\tau\), optimality requires \(x_\tau=1\), again giving \(\alpha=1\).

Thus an optimal threshold must serve a whole tail at price \(v_i\), and consequently must satisfy \(\kappa=a_i\).

Necessity of the slope test

Suppose the full tail beginning at \(i\) is optimal.

If some \(j<i\) has \(a_j\ge a_i=\kappa\), then \(e_j\) is feasible for the auxiliary problem and serves \(S_j>S_i\), contradicting optimality. Hence every earlier revenue is strictly smaller.

Suppose now that \(B_i>U_i\). Then \(U_i\) is finite, and there are indices \(j<i<k\), with \(a_j<a_i<a_k\), such that

\(\displaystyle \frac{S_j-S_i}{a_i-a_j}>\frac{S_i-S_k}{a_k-a_i}.\)

Put \(D_j=a_i-a_j>0\) and \(D_k=a_k-a_i>0\). The convex combination assigning weights \(D_k/(D_j+D_k)\) and \(D_j/(D_j+D_k)\) to \(e_j\) and \(e_k\), respectively, has revenue \(a_i\). Its served mass exceeds \(S_i\) by

\(\displaystyle \frac{D_jD_k}{D_j+D_k}\left(\frac{S_j-S_i}{D_j}-\frac{S_i-S_k}{D_k}\right)>0.\)

This contradicts optimality. Therefore \(B_i\le U_i\).

Sufficiency of the slope test

Suppose the stated requirements hold. Choose a finite \(\lambda\) satisfying \(B_i\le\lambda\le U_i\); choosing \(\lambda=B_i\) suffices. Since \(i\ge2\), \(B_i>0\).

For every \(j\),

\(S_j+\lambda a_j\le S_i+\lambda a_i.\)

For \(j<i\), this is precisely the lower-bound condition defining \(B_i\). For \(j>i\) with \(a_j>a_i\), it follows from the upper-bound condition defining \(U_i\). For \(j>i\) with \(a_j\le a_i\), it follows from \(S_j<S_i\) and \(\lambda\ge0\).

Write \(C=S_i+\lambda a_i>0\). Every feasible auxiliary allocation satisfies

\(\displaystyle s(y)+\lambda R(y)=\sum_j y_j(S_j+\lambda a_j)\le C\sum_jy_j\le C.\)

Since \(R(y)\ge\kappa=a_i\), this gives

\(s(y)\le C-\lambda R(y)\le C-\lambda a_i=S_i.\)

The full tail beginning at \(i\) attains \(S_i\) and finances construction exactly. It is therefore optimal. \(\square\)

4. A strict counterexample to universal threshold optimality

Take

\(\displaystyle (v_1,v_2,v_3)=\left(0,\frac12,1\right),\qquad (\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3)=\left(\frac12,\frac14,\frac14\right),\qquad \kappa=\frac18.\)

Then

\(\displaystyle (S_1,S_2,S_3)=\left(1,\frac12,\frac14\right),\qquad (a_1,a_2,a_3)=\left(0,\frac14,\frac14\right).\)

The menu

\(\displaystyle (x_1,x_2,x_3)=\left(\frac12,1,1\right),\qquad (p_1,p_2,p_3)=\left(0,\frac14,\frac14\right)\)

is admissible by Lemma 1, raises \(\kappa=1/8\), and serves mass \(3/4\).

It is optimal because \(S_i+2a_i\le1\) for every \(i\). Hence every feasible menu satisfies

\(s(y)+2R(y)\le1,\qquad s(y)\le1-2\kappa=\frac34.\)

The best threshold serves only mass \(1/2\). Specifically:

  • For \(0<t<1/2\), it serves mass \(1/2\), and financing requires \(t=1/4\).
  • At \(t=1/2\), financing requires excluding the entire atom at \(1/2\), leaving served mass \(1/4\).
  • For \(1/2<t<1\), revenue is \(t/4>1/8\), so financing equality fails.
  • At \(t=1\), financing serves mass \(1/8\).
  • At \(t=0\), revenue is zero.

Thus the optimum is strictly larger than every threshold menu’s served mass.

5. Running time and relation to the cited result

Compute the tail masses and revenues, test \(\kappa\le A\), and then enumerate the singleton and pair candidates. This uses \(O(\tau^2)\) rational arithmetic operations. The threshold criterion requires at most another \(O(\tau^2)\) operations.

All intermediate numbers can be represented with \(O(L)\) bits. For example, tail masses can use the product of the input mass denominators as a common denominator; its bit length is at most \(O(L)\). Each candidate and comparison subsequently involves only a constant number of arithmetic operations on such numbers. Using elementary integer arithmetic gives \(O(\tau^2L^2)\) bit operations for the stipulated \(m=1\). The output menu also has polynomial encoding length.

For the decision problem, compare the computed optimum with \(q\) by exact rational arithmetic. Therefore the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\); NP-hardness under polynomial-time many-one reductions would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The finite-agent model in [Wang et al.](https://ifmas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2021/pdfs/p1380.pdf) imposes financing at each profile where construction occurs. Here financing is an equality of support-weighted average payments. The proof above addresses the stated mass-menu model directly and requires no density or log-concavity hypothesis.
