The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support rational society \(\mu\) over location types \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\), define \(\mathrm{SC}_p(\mu,y)=\left(\sum_t\mu_t\|x_t-y\|_2^p\right)^{1/p}\) for finite \(p\), and \(\mathrm{SC}_{+\infty}(\mu,y)=\max_{t:\mu_t>0}\|x_t-y\|_2\). Let mass-weighted CRD return \(\bar{x}_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t x_t\) with probability \(\frac12\), and return \(x_t\) with probability \(\frac{\mu_t}{2}\). For each \(p\in[1,+\infty]\), determine the sharp supremum over societies with positive optimum of \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SC}_p(\mu,Y)]/\min_y\mathrm{SC}_p(\mu,y)\), and characterize societies that attain or approach it.

Partial solution and new results for even exponents

The sharp unrestricted values proved here are
$$ A_1=2,\qquad A_2=\frac{1+\sqrt2}{2},\qquad A_{+\infty}=2. $$
The endpoint values are not attained by any admissible finite society. At $p=2$, equality holds exactly when all support points lie on a positive-radius circle centered at the mass-weighted centroid.

The full sharp values for intermediate finite exponents remain unresolved. The main new progress is the following finite-support theorem.

Theorem. For every positive integer $k$, the supremum $A_{2k}$ is unchanged if societies are restricted to
$$ \tau\le (k+1)^2. $$
Moreover, allowing positive real masses, this supremum is attained by a society with at most $(k+1)^2$ support points. The value $A_{2k}$ is algebraic, and an attaining real-mass society can be chosen with algebraic masses and coordinates. Consequently, rational-mass societies with rational coordinates and at most $(k+1)^2$ support points form an extremizing sequence.

In particular, nine support points suffice at $p=4$. I also prove a strict version of the previous fourth-moment upper bound.

1. Definitions, continuity, and encoding

Let $X$ have distribution $\mu$, and write
$$ b=\mathbb EX,\qquad F_p(y)=\mathrm{SC}_p(\mu,y). $$
The mechanism numerator is
$$ N_p(\mu)=\frac12F_p(b)+\frac12\mathbb E F_p(X). $$
This formula already aggregates coincident outcomes.

For every exponent in the question, $F_p$ is continuous and coercive. Indeed, if $M=\max_t\|x_t\|_2$, then
$$ F_p(y)\ge \|y\|_2-M $$
whenever the right side is nonnegative. Hence an optimal location exists.

For $1<p<+\infty$, the function
$$ G_p(y)=F_p(y)^p=\mathbb E\|X-y\|_2^p $$
is strictly convex and differentiable. Strict convexity follows from strict convexity of the Euclidean norm raised to a power greater than one. Its unique minimizer $o$ therefore satisfies
$$ \mathbb E\bigl[\|X-o\|_2^{p-2}(X-o)\bigr]=0, $$
where the vector contribution at $X=o$ is defined to be zero. Conversely, this equation implies optimality by convexity.

Real masses and rational approximation

Temporarily allowing positive real masses does not change the supremum.

For fixed finite support, suppose the masses converge within the probability simplex. On every bounded set of facility locations, the corresponding costs converge uniformly: the sums defining their powers converge uniformly, and taking the $p$th root preserves uniform convergence on bounded intervals. The same assertion holds for the maximum objective.

The optimal locations can be restricted to a common bounded set. For example, if all support points have norm at most $M>0$, then $F_p(0)\le M$, whereas $\|y\|_2>2M$ implies $F_p(y)>M$. Thus the optima converge. The centroid and both numerator contributions also converge. A positive limiting optimum therefore implies convergence of the ratios.

Every positive real mass vector can be approximated by positive rational mass vectors whose entries sum to one. This proves equality of the rational-mass and real-mass suprema.

Locations can likewise be approximated by rational coordinates. If corresponding points move by at most $\delta$, the weighted reverse triangle inequality gives
$$ |F_p(y)-F'_p(y)|\le\delta $$
uniformly in $y$, including for $p=+\infty$. The centroids move by at most $\delta$, so costs at corresponding mechanism outcomes change by at most $2\delta$. Distinctness is preserved by sufficiently close approximations of a fixed finite support.

No running-time bound is asserted below. For a computational encoding, locations may be given by binary rational coordinates of total bit length $B$, alongside the mass encoding of total length $L$, with $m=2$ and $\tau$ listed types.

Replication

For rational masses, choose a common denominator $n$ and replace type $t$ by $n\mu_t$ equally weighted agents. The centroid and dictatorship outcome probabilities agree. Finite-exponent social costs and their optima are both multiplied by $n^{1/p}$; maximum costs agree directly. Thus replication preserves the ratio.

Conversely, grouping coincident equally weighted agents produces a rational-mass society. Consequently,
$$ A_p=\sup_{n\ge2}\alpha_p(n). $$
Replication is a mathematical correspondence; the number $n$ can be exponential in $L$.

2. The sharp endpoint values

Theorem: $A_1=2$, without finite attainment

Let $o$ be optimal, put $r_t=\|x_t-o\|_2$, and define
$$ D=\sum_{t,s}\mu_t\mu_s\|x_t-x_s\|_2. $$
The triangle inequality gives
$$ F_1(b) =\sum_t\mu_t\left\|\sum_s\mu_s(x_t-x_s)\right\|_2 \le D. $$
The expected dictatorship cost equals $D$. If $a=\min_t\mu_t>0$, then
$$ \begin{aligned} D &\le \sum_{t\ne s}\mu_t\mu_s(r_t+r_s)\\ &=2\sum_t\mu_t(1-\mu_t)r_t\\ &\le 2(1-a)\mathrm{OPT}_1(\mu). \end{aligned} $$
Hence
$$ R_1(\mu)\le2(1-a)<2. $$

For sharpness, place mass $1-\varepsilon$ at $0$ and mass $\varepsilon$ at $e=(1,0)$, with rational $0<\varepsilon\le1/2$. For every $y$,
$$ (1-\varepsilon)\|y\|_2+\varepsilon\|e-y\|_2 \ge \varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)\|y\|_2 \ge\varepsilon. $$
Thus $\mathrm{OPT}_1=\varepsilon$. Both the centroid cost and the expected dictatorship cost are $2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)$, so
$$ R_1=2(1-\varepsilon)\longrightarrow2. $$

Theorem: $A_{+\infty}=2$, without finite attainment

Let
$$ d=\max_{t,s}\|x_t-x_s\|_2>0. $$
Every enclosing ball has radius at least $d/2$. Also,
$$ \|x_t-b\|_2 \le\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s\|x_t-x_s\|_2 \le(1-\mu_t)d. $$
The centroid cost is therefore at most $(1-a)d$, and every dictatorship cost is at most $d$. Hence
$$ R_{+\infty}(\mu)\le2-a<2. $$

For the preceding two-location construction,
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{+\infty}=\frac12,\qquad F_{+\infty}(b)=1-\varepsilon,\qquad \mathbb EF_{+\infty}(X)=1. $$
Consequently,
$$ R_{+\infty}=2-\varepsilon\longrightarrow2. $$

Both endpoint extremizing sequences use only two listed types.

3. The complete result at $p=2$

Set
$$ \sigma^2=\mathbb E\|X-b\|_2^2>0. $$
Expansion gives
$$ F_2(y)^2=\sigma^2+\|y-b\|_2^2. $$
Thus $\mathrm{OPT}_2=\sigma$, and
$$ R_2(\mu) =\frac12\left( 1+\sum_t\mu_t\sqrt{1+\frac{\|x_t-b\|_2^2}{\sigma^2}} \right). $$
The expressions inside the square roots have weighted mean $2$. Concavity therefore gives
$$ R_2(\mu)\le\frac{1+\sqrt2}{2}. $$
Because the square root is strictly concave and every mass is positive, equality holds precisely when
$$ \|x_t-b\|_2^2=\sigma^2 \qquad\text{for every }t. $$
Equal masses at two distinct points attain this bound. This proves the value and the full finite-attainment characterization.

There is also an exact criterion for extremizing sequences. For each society define
$$ Z=\frac{\|X-b\|_2^2}{\sigma^2},\qquad \mathbb EZ=1. $$
Then
$$ R_2(\mu_j)\longrightarrow A_2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbb E|Z_j-1|\longrightarrow0. $$
Indeed,
$$ \mathbb E\bigl(\sqrt{1+Z}-\sqrt2\bigr)^2 =4-2\sqrt2\,\mathbb E\sqrt{1+Z}. $$
Convergence of the ratios makes this quantity tend to zero. Cauchy–Schwarz applied to
$$ |Z-1| =|\sqrt{1+Z}-\sqrt2|(\sqrt{1+Z}+\sqrt2) $$
proves necessity, because the second factor has uniformly bounded second moment. Sufficiency follows from
$$ |\sqrt{1+Z}-\sqrt2|\le|Z-1|. $$

4. Explicit bounds and constructions for intermediate exponents

The supplied equal-agent theorem applies after replication, in Euclidean dimension $m=2$. Since its powers of $q_n$ are nonnegative, it implies
$$ A_p\le \begin{cases} \frac12\left(2^{2/p-1}+2^{1/p}\right),&1\le p\le2,\\[4pt] \frac12\left(2^{1-2/p}+2^{1-1/p}\right),&2\le p\le+\infty. \end{cases} $$
This application uses the verified bound in [Theorem 3 of the supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08621v2).

Exact two-location formula

Fix $1<p<+\infty$, and place mass $t$ at $0$ and mass $1-t$ at $e=(1,0)$. Projection onto the segment cannot increase either distance. On the segment, differentiation gives the optimal coordinate
$$ y_*= \frac{(1-t)^{1/(p-1)}} {t^{1/(p-1)}+(1-t)^{1/(p-1)}}. $$
Writing
$$ S=t^{1/(p-1)}+(1-t)^{1/(p-1)}, $$
substitution gives
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_p=\frac{[t(1-t)]^{1/p}}{S^{1-1/p}}. $$
The centroid is $(1-t)e$, with cost
$$ F_p(b)^p=t(1-t)\bigl(t^{p-1}+(1-t)^{p-1}\bigr). $$
The expected dictatorship cost is
$$ t(1-t)^{1/p}+(1-t)t^{1/p}. $$
Thus the exact ratio is
$$ H_p(t)=\frac12S^{1-1/p} \left[ \bigl(t^{p-1}+(1-t)^{p-1}\bigr)^{1/p} +t^{1-1/p}+(1-t)^{1-1/p} \right]. $$

It extends continuously to $H_p(0)=H_p(1)=1$, while
$$ H_p(1/2)=\frac12\left(1+2^{1-1/p}\right)>1. $$
Therefore
$$ B_p=\max_{0\le t\le1}H_p(t) $$
is attained at an interior real value of $t$, and rational approximations give
$$ A_p\ge B_p. $$
This is the exact supremum restricted to two location types. It does not establish unrestricted sharpness.

Regular-polygon sequences

For every finite $p\ge1$, define
$$ J_p= \left[ \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (2|\sin(\theta/2)|)^p\,d\theta \right]^{1/p}. $$
Take the equally weighted vertices of a regular $h$-gon on the unit circle. Its centroid is zero. The convex objective has gradient zero there, because all support points have unit norm and their sum is zero. Hence its optimum and centroid cost both equal one.

Symmetry makes all dictatorship costs equal, so its ratio is
$$ \frac12\left[ 1+ \left( \frac1h\sum_{j=0}^{h-1} (2|\sin(\pi j/h)|)^p \right)^{1/p} \right]. $$
The Riemann sums converge, proving
$$ A_p\ge\frac{1+J_p}{2}. $$
Here $J_p$ has explicitly been defined for every finite $p$, so this lower bound has no domain ambiguity.

5. New theorem: bounded support for every even exponent

Fix $p=2k$, where $k$ is a positive integer, and identify $\mathbb R^2$ with $\mathbb C$. Define
$$ M=(k+1)^2. $$

The finite moment space

The polynomial identity
$$ |z-w|^{2k}=(z-w)^k(\overline z-\overline w)^k $$
shows that the entire function $F_{2k}(w)^{2k}$ is determined by the moments
$$ \mathbb E[Z^a\overline Z^{\,b}], \qquad 0\le a,b\le k. $$

These moments can be represented by $M$ real quantities:

  • the $k+1$ real diagonal moments with $a=b$;
  • the real and imaginary parts for $a<b$, giving $k(k+1)$ further quantities.

Let $f(z)\in\mathbb R^M$ be this real moment vector. Its components include the constant function and the two coordinates of $z$.

Eliminating support points without decreasing the ratio

Start with any finite society, allowing real masses. Set
$$ g_t=F_{2k}(x_t). $$
If its support size exceeds $M$, the vectors $f(x_t)$ are linearly dependent. Thus there is a nonzero real vector $(h_t)$ such that
$$ \sum_t h_t f(x_t)=0. $$
The constant component implies $\sum_t h_t=0$, so $(h_t)$ has both positive and negative entries. Reverse its sign if necessary to arrange
$$ \sum_t h_tg_t\ge0. $$
Set
$$ \lambda=\min_{h_t<0}\frac{\mu_t}{-h_t}>0, \qquad \mu'_t=\mu_t+\lambda h_t. $$
The new masses are nonnegative, sum to one, and at least one becomes zero. All $M$ moments are preserved.

Consequently, for every facility location $y$,
$$ F'_{2k}(y)=F_{2k}(y). $$
The centroid is also preserved, because its coordinates are among the moments. Thus the optimum and centroid contribution remain unchanged, while
$$ \sum_t\mu'_tF'_{2k}(x_t) =\sum_t\mu_tg_t+\lambda\sum_t h_tg_t \ge\sum_t\mu_tg_t. $$
Deleting zero masses and repeating produces a society with at most $M$ support points and no smaller ratio.

The resulting masses need not be rational. However, the continuity argument in Section 1 supplies rational approximations with the same support and convergent ratios. Therefore
$$ A_{2k} = \sup_{\substack{\mu\text{ has rational masses}\\ \tau\le(k+1)^2}} R_{2k}(\mu). $$

For $p=4$, the nine moment functions can be written explicitly as
$$ 1,\ x,\ y,\ x^2-y^2,\ 2xy,\ x^2+y^2,\ x(x^2+y^2),\ y(x^2+y^2),\ (x^2+y^2)^2. $$

6. New theorem: existence of an even-exponent maximizer with real masses

The bounded-support reduction alone does not give compactness, because some locations might diverge while their masses vanish. The following argument handles that possibility.

Fix $p=2k$ and $M=(k+1)^2$. Choose a maximizing sequence with at most $M$ support points. Translate each optimum to zero and scale to obtain
$$ F_{p,j}(0)=\mathrm{OPT}_p(\mu_j)=1. $$
Pad the profiles with zero-mass slots if necessary. Write their masses and locations as $\lambda_{i,j}$ and $x_{i,j}$, for $1\le i\le M$.

Define
$$ e_{i,j}=\lambda_{i,j}\|x_{i,j}\|_2^p. $$
Since $\sum_i e_{i,j}=1$, a subsequence satisfies
$$ \lambda_{i,j}\longrightarrow a_i,\qquad e_{i,j}\longrightarrow e_i. $$
Let $I=\{i:a_i>0\}$. For $i\in I$, the locations are bounded, so a further subsequence has
$$ x_{i,j}\longrightarrow x_i. $$
The finite measure
$$ \nu=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\delta_{x_i} $$
is a probability measure, because $\sum_{i\in I}a_i=1$.

Set
$$ h=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\|x_i\|_2^p,\qquad c=\sum_{i\notin I}e_i. $$
Then
$$ h+c=1. $$

Vanishing masses do not affect the centroid or stationarity

For $i\notin I$,
$$ \lambda_{i,j}\|x_{i,j}\|_2 =\lambda_{i,j}^{1-1/p}e_{i,j}^{1/p}\longrightarrow0. $$
Thus the centroids converge to $b=\mathbb E_\nu X$.

Likewise,
$$ \lambda_{i,j}\|x_{i,j}\|_2^{p-1} =\lambda_{i,j}^{1/p}e_{i,j}^{1-1/p}\longrightarrow0. $$
Passing to the limit in the normalized optimality equations gives
$$ \sum_{i\in I}a_i\|x_i\|_2^{p-2}x_i=0. $$
Therefore zero minimizes the objective of $\nu$, and its optimal $p$th-power cost is $h$.

The exact limiting numerator

If $y_j\to y$, then for $i\notin I$,
$$ \left| \lambda_{i,j}^{1/p}\|x_{i,j}-y_j\|_2 -\lambda_{i,j}^{1/p}\|x_{i,j}\|_2 \right| \le\lambda_{i,j}^{1/p}\|y_j\|_2\longrightarrow0. $$
It follows that
$$ F_{p,j}(y_j)^p\longrightarrow F_{p,\nu}(y)^p+c. $$

The outer dictatorship contribution of a vanishing-mass slot tends to zero, since Minkowski gives
$$ \lambda_{i,j}F_{p,j}(x_{i,j}) \le\lambda_{i,j}\bigl(1+\|x_{i,j}\|_2\bigr) \longrightarrow0. $$
Hence the limiting ratio is
$$ \frac12\left[ \bigl(F_{p,\nu}(b)^p+c\bigr)^{1/p} +\sum_{i\in I}a_i \bigl(F_{p,\nu}(x_i)^p+c\bigr)^{1/p} \right]. $$
Its denominator is one by normalization.

If $h=0$, every retained point is zero and $c=1$, so this limit equals one. That is impossible for a maximizing sequence, because balanced two-location societies have ratio greater than one.

Thus $h>0$. Every evaluated cost satisfies $F_{p,\nu}(y)^p\ge h$. For any $g\ge h$, using $h+c=1$ gives
$$ g+c\le \frac gh, $$
with strict inequality when $c>0$ and $g>h$.

Since the objective of $\nu$ is strictly convex, its unique optimum is zero. Moreover, $h>0$ implies that some positive-mass support point is nonzero, and its cost is strictly greater than $h^{1/p}$. Therefore, if $c>0$, the displayed limiting ratio is strictly smaller than $R_p(\nu)$.

But $R_p(\nu)\le A_p$, by rational approximation. This contradicts convergence of the sequence to $A_p$. Hence $c=0$, and
$$ R_p(\nu)=A_p. $$
After deleting zero masses and grouping coincident locations, $\nu$ is an attaining real-mass society with at most $M$ support points.

7. An explicit polynomial formulation and algebraicity

Fix $p=2k$ and $M=(k+1)^2$. Introduce variables
$$ w_i\ge0,\quad x_i=(u_i,v_i),\quad b\in\mathbb R^2,\quad C\ge0,\quad D_i\ge0,\quad r\in\mathbb R. $$
Consider the following polynomial constraints:
$$ \sum_{i=1}^M w_i=1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^M w_i(u_i^2+v_i^2)^k=1, $$
$$ \sum_{i=1}^M w_i(u_i^2+v_i^2)^{k-1}u_i=0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^M w_i(u_i^2+v_i^2)^{k-1}v_i=0, $$
$$ b=\sum_{i=1}^M w_ix_i, $$
$$ C^{2k}=\sum_{i=1}^M w_i\|x_i-b\|_2^{2k}, $$
$$ D_i^{2k}=\sum_{j=1}^M w_j\|x_j-x_i\|_2^{2k}, \qquad 1\le i\le M, $$
$$ 2r=C+\sum_{i=1}^M w_iD_i. $$
All norm powers here are polynomials in the coordinates.

The stationarity equations and convexity make zero optimal. The normalization makes its cost one. Thus every feasible point represents the ratio of a real-mass society, after deleting zero masses and grouping coincident locations. Conversely, the maximizer from Section 6 can be normalized to satisfy these constraints. Therefore
$$ A_{2k}=\max r $$
over this explicit polynomial system.

To deduce algebraicity, use the following standard form of real quantifier elimination: a first-order formula built from polynomial equalities and inequalities over a real closed field is equivalent to a quantifier-free formula over the same coefficient field. In particular, projections of sets defined over $\mathbb Q$ are semialgebraic over $\mathbb Q$. This is the [Tarski–Seidenberg theorem, stated in Basu’s survey](https://arxiv.org/html/1409.1534).

The set of achievable $r$ values consequently has a quantifier-free description involving finitely many rational polynomials in $r$. If its maximum $A_{2k}$ were not a root of any nonzero polynomial in that description, all polynomial signs would be constant in a neighborhood of $A_{2k}$. Membership would then also be constant there, contradicting maximality. Thus $A_{2k}$ is algebraic.

The real algebraic numbers form a real closed subfield of $\mathbb R$. Quantifier elimination also implies that a nonempty system over algebraic coefficients has a solution in that subfield: its existential statement has the same truth value in both fields. Apply this to the system above with $r=A_{2k}$. An attaining profile therefore exists with algebraic masses and coordinates.

Approximating that fixed profile by positive rational masses and rational coordinates yields an extremizing sequence with
$$ \tau\le(k+1)^2. $$

This formulation is exact, but it has not been solved here for $k\ge2$. No computational running-time claim is being made.

8. A strict upper bound at $p=4$

Define
$$ f(u)=\frac12\left[ (1+3u-3u^3)^{1/4}+(2+6u)^{1/4} \right], \qquad 0\le u\le1. $$
I prove
$$ A_4<\max_{0\le u\le1}f(u). $$

Derivation of the non-strict moment bound

Translate an optimum to zero and normalize
$$ \mathbb E\|X\|_2^4=1. $$
Write
$$ b=\mathbb EX,\qquad s=\mathbb E\|X\|_2^2,\qquad z=\|b\|_2^2,\qquad Q=\mathbb E[XX^{\mathsf T}]. $$
Then $0<s\le1$, and optimality gives
$$ \mathbb E[\|X\|_2^2X]=0. $$
Consequently,
$$ sb=\mathbb E[(s-\|X\|_2^2)(X-b)]. $$
Cauchy–Schwarz yields
$$ s^2z\le(1-s^2)(s-z), $$
and hence
$$ z\le s(1-s^2). $$

Expanding the centroid fourth moment gives
$$ \mathbb E\|X-b\|_2^4 =1+2sz+4b^{\mathsf T}Qb-3z^2. $$
Since $Q$ is positive semidefinite with trace $s$,
$$ b^{\mathsf T}Qb\le sz. $$
The function $1+6sz-3z^2$ is increasing for $0\le z\le s$. Therefore
$$ \mathbb E\|X-b\|_2^4 \le1+3s^2-3s^6. $$

For independent copies $X,X'$, expansion gives
$$ \mathbb E\|X-X'\|_2^4 =2+2s^2+4\operatorname{tr}(Q^2) \le2+6s^2. $$
Here the terms involving $\mathbb E[\|X\|_2^2X]$ vanish, and
$$ \operatorname{tr}(Q^2)\le(\operatorname{tr}Q)^2=s^2. $$
Finally, concavity of the fourth root gives
$$ \mathbb EF_4(X) \le\bigl(\mathbb E\|X-X'\|_2^4\bigr)^{1/4}. $$
Putting $u=s^2$ proves
$$ R_4(\mu)\le f(u)\le\max_{0\le u\le1}f(u). $$

Equality is impossible

The function $f$ is strictly concave on $[0,1]$: its first term is concave because $1+3u-3u^3$ is positive and concave, and its second term is strictly concave. Furthermore,
$$ f'(0)>0,\qquad f'(1)=\frac34\left(8^{-3/4}-1\right)<0. $$
Thus its unique maximizing point $u_*$ lies in $(0,1)$.

Suppose a society attained $\max f$. All inequalities in the preceding derivation would be equalities, with $0<s<1$. In particular,
$$ z=s(1-s^2)>0, $$
and equality would hold in the Cauchy–Schwarz estimate.

Set $U=s-\|X\|_2^2$. Equality implies
$$ X-b=\frac{sb}{1-s^2}U $$
almost surely. To verify this implication directly, expand the expected squared norm of the difference; the Cauchy–Schwarz equality makes it zero.

Thus every support point lies on the line spanned by $b$. Writing $X=\xi e$ and $b=\beta e$, where $\beta>0$, gives
$$ \xi-\beta=\frac{s\beta}{1-s^2}(s-\xi^2). $$
This is a nonconstant quadratic equation in $\xi$, so the support contains at most two distinct points. It contains exactly two, since a singleton cannot have normalized optimum one.

Let their masses be $a$ and $1-a$, and their distance be $d>0$. Their dictatorship costs satisfy
$$ F_4(x_1)^4=(1-a)d^4,\qquad F_4(x_2)^4=ad^4. $$
Equality in the strictly concave fourth-root Jensen inequality forces these two costs to agree, so $a=1/2$. For two equally weighted points, the unique optimum is their midpoint, which is also their centroid. Since the optimum was translated to zero, this gives $b=0$, contradicting $z>0$.

No finite real-mass society therefore attains $\max f$. Section 6 proves that $A_4$ itself has a finite real-mass maximizer. Consequently,
$$ A_4<f(u_*). $$

The number $u_*$ is characterized uniquely by
$$ u_*\in(1/\sqrt3,1), $$
$$ (3u_*^2-1)^4(2+6u_*)^3 =16(1+3u_*-3u_*^3)^3. $$
Indeed, this equation follows from $f'(u_*)=0$, and strict concavity makes the relevant root unique.

The two-location formula has the expansion
