Theorem 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given socioeconomic categories \(1,\ldots,k\), explicit mass-preserving transfer vectors \(T=\{t^1,\ldots,t^m\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^k\) satisfying Theorem 1’s Hilbert-basis condition, and rational population distributions \(\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{Q}_+^k\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_i\nu_i=1\), decide whether \(\mu-\nu\in\operatorname{cone}(T)\), equivalently whether \(T\lambda=\mu-\nu\) for some \(\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(k\ge 2\) ordered socioeconomic categories. Each society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. For two societies, use \(\tau\) for the larger support size.
The input distributions \(\mu,\nu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{k}\) satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_i\nu_i=1\); unlisted categories have zero mass.
Also given are \(m\ge1\) nonzero transfer directions \(\mathcal T=\{t^1,\ldots,t^m\}\subseteq\mathbb Z^k\), encoded in binary, with \(\sum_i t_i^j=0\). Let \(A=[t^1\ \cdots\ t^m]\) and \(C=\operatorname{cone}(\mathcal T)\).
Promise that \(C\) is pointed and \(\mathcal T\) contains a Hilbert basis of \(C\cap\mathbb Z^k\): every integer vector in \(C\) is a nonnegative integer combination of these transfers. These promises need not be checked.
Decide whether \(\mu-\nu\in C\), equivalently whether \(A\lambda=\mu-\nu\) for some \(\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{m}\). Membership imposes no intermediate-population constraints.
Is this promised continuous cone-membership problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, with running time measured in \(k,m,\tau\) and total input bit length \(L\)?
For integer count distributions of equal total, the Hilbert-basis assumption makes discrete dominance equivalent to membership in a cone generated by linearly independent transfers and to an appropriate generalized-inverse criterion. [Abul Naga](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-025-01620-y)
The published treatment permits arbitrary transfer-matrix rank and discusses a pivoting algorithm whose worst-case iteration count may be exponential; no separate complexity classification for precisely this normalized support-encoded promise problem was verified. 1. Classification

The promised problem is in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(k,m,\tau,L\). This follows from exact rational linear-programming feasibility. Neither pointedness nor the Hilbert-basis promise is needed for this classification.

Here, cone membership means precisely the existence of nonnegative transfer coefficients, without constraints on intermediate populations.

2. Constructing a polynomial-size feasibility instance

Set \(b=\mu-\nu\). For each category, read its mass from the support list, assigning zero to an unlisted category. The union of the two supports contains at most \(2\tau\) categories, so \(b\) has at most \(2\tau\) nonzero coordinates.

This subtraction can be performed exactly with polynomial bit complexity. Indeed, for two listed masses \(p/q\) and \(r/s\), with positive denominators,

\[ \]
\(b_i=(ps-rq)/(qs).\)

The products and subtraction have bit lengths bounded by a constant multiple of the input bit length. Reducing the resulting fraction is optional.

Construct the following rational linear-inequality system in the \(m\) variables \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\):

\(A\lambda\le b,\qquad -A\lambda\le-b,\qquad -I_m\lambda\le0.\)

All inequalities are coordinatewise. The first two groups together are equivalent to \(A\lambda=b\); the third is equivalent to \(\lambda\ge0\). Thus the system has \(m\) variables and \(2k+m\) inequalities.

Its coefficient matrix consists of copies of \(A\), its negative, and an identity matrix. Its right-hand side consists of copies of \(b\), its negative, and zeros. Consequently, its total binary encoding length is polynomial in \(k,m,\tau,L\). Writing any required zero entries also takes only polynomial time in these parameters.

Sparse-support caveat. An equation cannot be discarded merely because its category is absent from both population supports: a zero coordinate of \(b\) can still impose a nontrivial equation on the transfers. Only rows satisfying both \(A_{i,*}=0\) and \(b_i=0\) may be discarded automatically.

If transfers themselves are encoded sparsely, one can retain precisely the categories appearing in either population support or in a nonzero transfer coordinate. Every omitted row is then identically zero on both sides. This also avoids expanding a potentially large ambient category set.

3. Real and rational feasibility coincide

For completeness, the rational coefficients required in the question introduce no additional restriction.

Lemma. If \(A\) and \(b\) are rational and \(A\lambda=b\) has a solution \(\lambda\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m}\), then it has a solution in \(\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{m}\). Moreover, some such solution uses at most \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\) positive coefficients.

Proof. If \(b=0\), choose \(\lambda=0\). Otherwise, among all nonnegative real solutions choose one whose support \(J=\{j:\lambda_j>0\}\) has minimum cardinality. Such a minimum exists because support cardinalities belong to the finite set \(\{0,\ldots,m\}\).

Suppose the columns indexed by \(J\) are linearly dependent. There is a nonzero vector \(d\), supported on \(J\), with \(Ad=0\). Replacing \(d\) by its negative if necessary, assume at least one coordinate of \(d\) is positive. Define

\(\varepsilon=\min_{j\in J:\,d_j>0}\lambda_j/d_j.\)

This is positive. The vector \(\lambda'=\lambda-\varepsilon d\) is nonnegative: coordinates with \(d_j>0\) remain nonnegative by the definition of \(\varepsilon\), and coordinates with \(d_j\le0\) do not decrease. At least one previously positive coordinate becomes zero, and no coordinate outside \(J\) changes. Also,

\(A\lambda'=A\lambda-\varepsilon Ad=b.\)

This contradicts minimality of the support. Therefore the columns of \(A_J\) are linearly independent.

Writing \(s=|J|\), select \(s\) rows on which \(A_J\) forms an invertible square matrix \(B\). On these rows, the coefficients satisfy \(B\lambda_J=b_R\), so

\(\lambda_J=B^{-1}b_R\in\mathbb Q^{s}.\)

The inverse is rational because \(B\) is an invertible rational matrix. These coefficients are the original nonnegative coefficients, and extending them by zeros proves the claim. Column independence gives \(s\le\operatorname{rank}(A)\). \(\square\)

In the present problem, every column lies in the hyperplane \(\{x:\sum_i x_i=0\}\), so \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\le k-1\). Hence a positive instance has a rational representation using at most \(\min\{m,k-1\}\) transfers. This is an existence statement; the algorithm need not enumerate subsets.

4. Applying polynomial-time linear programming

Use the following standard theorem.

Exact rational linear-feasibility theorem. Given a rational matrix \(M\) and rational vector \(h\), feasibility of \(Mx\le h\) can be decided by a deterministic algorithm in time polynomial in the numbers of rows and columns and the total binary encoding length. This theorem allows arbitrary rank, unbounded feasible regions, and feasible regions with empty interior. It is the polynomial-time linear-programming result originating with [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

All hypotheses hold for the system constructed in Section 2: its data are explicitly rational, its dimensions are polynomial in the specified parameters, and its encoding length is polynomial in the original input size.

Run that algorithm and accept exactly when the system is feasible. Correctness follows from

\(b\in C\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists\lambda\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m}:A\lambda=b\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{m}:A\lambda=b,\)

where the first equivalence is the definition of the generated cone and the second is the lemma. The constructed inequalities express exactly the final condition.

The support preprocessing, system construction, and feasibility decision therefore take total time \(\operatorname{poly}(k,m,\tau,L)\).

The Hilbert-basis promise connects integer cone membership with nonnegative integer combinations, but the continuous decision problem already admits this polynomial-time algorithm. Likewise, an exponential worst-case iteration count for a particular pivoting algorithm does not establish hardness of the underlying decision problem.

Thus the target classification is polynomial-time solvable. Under polynomial-time reductions that respect the promise, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
