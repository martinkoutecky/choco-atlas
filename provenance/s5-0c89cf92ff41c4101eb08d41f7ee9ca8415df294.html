The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(\alpha,\beta,\rho,\varepsilon\) with \(0<\alpha<1\), \(\beta>0\), \(0<\rho<1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), let a unit-mass society have valuations distributed as \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), and let \(v_\rho\) satisfy \(F^+(v_\rho)=\rho\). Find \(\hat p\in[0,v_\rho]\) such that \(U(\hat p)\geq\max_{p\in[0,v_\rho]}U(p)-\varepsilon\), where \(U(p)=\frac{\rho}{F^+(p)}\int_p^1(v-p)f(v)\,dv\), and report the resulting lottery probability.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) denotes support size, not the number of possible types. Running times use \(m\), \(\tau\), total input bit length \(L\), and other input parameters.
The prescribed Beta society is a parametric exception to this convention: its support is continuous, so there is no finite \(\tau\); there is one good, \(m=1\), and complexity is measured in \(L\).
Input consists of binary rationals \(\alpha,\beta,\rho,\varepsilon\) satisfying \(0<\alpha<1\), \(\beta>0\), \(0<\rho<1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). A unit-mass society has unit demand and valuations distributed as \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) on \([0,1]\); supply is \(\rho\).
Let \(f\) be its density, \(F^+(p)=\Pr[v\ge p]\), and \(v_\rho\in(0,1)\) the unique price satisfying \(F^+(v_\rho)=\rho\).
At a posted price \(p\in[0,v_\rho]\), eligible individuals have \(v\ge p\), each receives a unit with probability \(\pi(p)=\rho/F^+(p)\), and recipients pay \(p\). Expected aggregate consumer surplus is \(U(p)=\frac{\rho}{F^+(p)}\int_p^1(v-p)f(v)\,dv\).
The computational problem is to find rational \(\hat p\in[0,v_\rho]\) satisfying \(U(\hat p)\ge\max_{p\in[0,v_\rho]}U(p)-\varepsilon\), together with rational \(\hat\pi\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(|\hat\pi-\pi(\hat p)|\le\varepsilon\). All numerical computation is charged in the Turing bit model.

Is this additive \(\varepsilon\)-approximation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite populations with independent identically distributed valuations, [Scrogin (2017)](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=76136) establishes sufficient hazard-rate conditions for market assignment or a positive-price lottery to maximize expected consumer surplus; this is a structural result, not a discrete complexity classification.
The same source analyzes Beta-distributed valuations and gives numerical allocation comparisons, without a bit-complexity bound. No result settling the stated computational problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The required finite-support representation is incompatible with the prescribed Beta distribution. Preserved the Beta question and explicitly identified its parametric representation as an exception; no finite support-size parameter applies.
  • Interpreted rho as supply divided by population size, giving supply rho after normalizing population mass to one.
  • Specified unit demand, eligibility at valuations at least the posted price, uniform lottery probability among eligible individuals, and payment only by recipients.
  • Required a rational feasible price and a rational lottery probability with absolute error at most epsilon, since an exact probability need not have a finite rational representation.
  • Used the Turing bit model and total binary input length L, including epsilon, rather than unit-cost access to special functions.

Partial result: an output-length obstruction and a single-peak theorem

Under the ordinary encoding of a rational output by its explicit binary numerator and denominator, the problem cannot be solved in time polynomial in $L$: some inputs require every valid output price to have exponentially many bits.

This does not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. Beyond that obstruction, I prove two structural and algorithmic results:

  • For every allowed $\alpha,\beta$, the surplus objective has exactly one unconstrained peak.
  • When $\beta$ is a positive integer and $\alpha=a/q$, the stated approximation problem has an algorithm polynomial in $L$ and the numerical values of $q,\beta$.

The connection with mean residual value and hazard rates is also used in [Scrogin (2017)](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=76136). All properties and algorithmic guarantees needed here are proved below.

1. Definitions and existence of an optimum

Write
$$ C=\left(\int_0^1 v^{\alpha-1}(1-v)^{\beta-1}\,dv\right)^{-1}, \qquad f(v)=Cv^{\alpha-1}(1-v)^{\beta-1}. $$
Because $\alpha,\beta>0$, the normalization integral is finite and positive. Define
$$ S(p)=F^+(p)=\int_p^1 f(v)\,dv, \qquad I(p)=\int_p^1(v-p)f(v)\,dv. $$
The density is positive on $(0,1)$, so $S$ is continuous and strictly decreasing from $1$ to $0$. Thus the prescribed $v_\rho\in(0,1)$ exists and is unique.

Changing the order of integration in the nonnegative integral gives
$$ I(p)=\int_p^1 S(t)\,dt. $$
Define the mean residual value
$$ e(p)=\frac{I(p)}{S(p)},\qquad 0\le p<1. $$
Then
$$ U(p)=\rho e(p). $$
On the feasible interval, $S(p)\ge\rho>0$. Consequently, $U$ is continuous on $[0,v_\rho]$ and attains its maximum there.

2. Exponential output length rules out polynomial time

This section reproduces the verified obstruction from the notes, including its proof.

2.1. Exact formulas when $\beta=1$

In this case,
$$ f(v)=\alpha v^{\alpha-1}, \qquad S(p)=1-p^\alpha, \qquad v_\rho=(1-\rho)^{1/\alpha}. $$
Direct integration yields
$$ \begin{aligned} I(p) &=\alpha\int_p^1 v^\alpha\,dv -p\alpha\int_p^1v^{\alpha-1}\,dv\\ &=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}-p+\frac{p^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}. \end{aligned} $$
Therefore,
$$ U(p)=\frac{\rho}{1-p^\alpha} \left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}-p+\frac{p^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}\right). $$

2.2. A family on which every valid price is long

For each integer $n\ge3$, choose
$$ \alpha=2^{-n}, \qquad \beta=1, \qquad \rho=\frac12, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\alpha}{8}. $$
The total input length is $L=\Theta(n)$, while
$$ v_\rho=2^{-2^n}. $$

At price zero,
$$ U(0)=\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+1)}. $$
At the feasible endpoint, $S(v_\rho)=\rho$, so
$$ U(v_\rho)= \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1} -\frac{\alpha+1/2}{\alpha+1}v_\rho. $$
It follows that
$$ U(v_\rho)-U(0) = \frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+1)} -\frac{\alpha+1/2}{\alpha+1}v_\rho. $$
Since $0<\alpha<1$,
$$ \frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+1)}>\frac{\alpha}{4}, \qquad \frac{\alpha+1/2}{\alpha+1}<1. $$
Also, $2^n\ge n+3$ for $n\ge3$, by induction. Hence
$$ v_\rho=2^{-2^n}\le2^{-(n+3)}=\frac{\alpha}{8}. $$
Combining these bounds gives
$$ U(v_\rho)-U(0)> \frac{\alpha}{4}-v_\rho \ge\frac{\alpha}{8} =\varepsilon. $$
Thus price zero is not a valid approximate answer.

Every valid output must therefore have
$$ 0<\hat p\le2^{-2^n}. $$
Write $\hat p=r/d$ with positive integers $r,d$. Then
$$ \frac1d\le\frac rd\le2^{-2^n}, $$
so
$$ d\ge2^{2^n}. $$
The denominator alone requires at least $2^n+1$ binary digits. Reduction of the fraction is unnecessary for this argument.

Proved conclusion. Every valid output on this family has length $2^{\Omega(L)}$. A Turing machine needs at least that much time to write it. Therefore the full problem, with explicit rational output, is not polynomial-time solvable in $L$.

This is an output-representation obstruction; it supplies no $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

3. A single-peak theorem for the full Beta family

Theorem. For every $0<\alpha<1$ and $\beta>0$, there is a unique $p_0\in(0,1)$ such that $e$ is strictly increasing on $[0,p_0]$ and strictly decreasing on $[p_0,1)$. Consequently, the unique feasible maximizer is
$$ p_*=\min\{p_0,v_\rho\}. $$

The proof first establishes the shape of the hazard rate.

3.1. The hazard rate decreases and then increases

For $0<p<1$, define
$$ h(p)=\frac{f(p)}{S(p)}, \qquad \ell(p)=\log f(p). $$
Differentiation gives
$$ \ell'(p)=\frac{\alpha-1}{p}-\frac{\beta-1}{1-p}, \qquad \ell''(p)=\frac{1-\alpha}{p^2}-\frac{\beta-1}{(1-p)^2}. $$
Introduce
$$ J(p)=f(p)+\ell'(p)S(p). $$
Using $f'=\ell'f$ and $S'=-f$, we obtain
$$ J'(p)=\ell''(p)S(p), \qquad h'(p)=\frac{f(p)}{S(p)^2}J(p). $$
Thus the sign of $h'$ is the sign of $J$.

At the left endpoint,
$$ \lim_{p\downarrow0}pJ(p)=\alpha-1<0. $$
Indeed, $pf(p)\to0$, $S(p)\to1$, and $p\ell'(p)\to\alpha-1$.

At the right endpoint, put $x=1-p$. Since the remaining density factor is smooth there,
$$ f(p)=Cx^{\beta-1}(1+O(x)), \qquad S(p)=\frac{C}{\beta}x^\beta(1+O(x)), $$
and
$$ \ell'(p)=-\frac{\beta-1}{x}+O(1). $$
Substitution gives
$$ J(p)=\frac{C}{\beta}x^{\beta-1}(1+O(x)), $$
so $J$ is positive sufficiently close to $1$.

If $0<\beta\le1$, then $\ell''>0$ throughout $(0,1)$. Hence $J$ is strictly increasing. Its endpoint signs imply that it has exactly one zero.

If $\beta>1$, define
$$ d=\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}} {\sqrt{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{\beta-1}}. $$
Then $\ell''$ is positive on $(0,d)$ and negative on $(d,1)$. Thus $J$ increases before $d$ and decreases afterward. Moreover,
$$ \lim_{p\uparrow1}J(p)=0. $$
A strictly decreasing function with this limit is strictly positive on $[d,1)$. On $(0,d)$, $J$ strictly increases from negative values to $J(d)>0$, so it has exactly one zero there.

In both cases there is a unique $r\in(0,1)$ such that
$$ h'(p)<0\quad(0<p<r), \qquad h'(p)>0\quad(r<p<1). $$

3.2. The mean residual value has exactly one peak

Define
$$ W(p)=I(p)-\frac{S(p)}{h(p)},\qquad 0<p<1. $$
Since $I'=-S$ and $S'=-hS$,
$$ W'(p)=\frac{S(p)h'(p)}{h(p)^2}. $$
Therefore $W$ is strictly decreasing on $(0,r)$.

Because $\alpha<1$, we have $f(p)\to+\infty$ as $p\downarrow0$. Consequently,
$$ \lim_{p\downarrow0}\frac{S(p)}{h(p)} = \lim_{p\downarrow0}\frac{S(p)^2}{f(p)} =0, $$
and hence
$$ \lim_{p\downarrow0}W(p)=I(0)>0. $$

We next show that $W(p)<0$ for every $p\ge r$. For $p\le t<1$,
$$ \frac{S(t)}{S(p)} =\exp\left(-\int_p^t h(u)\,du\right). $$
When $p\ge r$, the hazard is nondecreasing on $[p,1)$, giving
$$ \frac{S(t)}{S(p)}\le e^{-h(p)(t-p)}. $$
Therefore,
$$ \begin{aligned} e(p) &=\int_p^1\frac{S(t)}{S(p)}\,dt\\ &\le\frac{1-e^{-h(p)(1-p)}}{h(p)} <\frac1{h(p)}. \end{aligned} $$
This proves $W(p)=S(p)(e(p)-1/h(p))<0$.

By continuity and strict decrease, $W$ has exactly one zero $p_0$ in $(0,r)$. Finally,
$$ e'(p)=\frac{f(p)I(p)-S(p)^2}{S(p)^2} =\frac{h(p)}{S(p)}W(p). $$
Thus $e'$ is positive before $p_0$ and negative afterward, proving the theorem. In particular, $p_0$ is the unique solution of
$$ f(p_0)I(p_0)=S(p_0)^2. $$

For example, with $\alpha=1/2$, $\beta=1$, and $\rho=1/2$, setting $t=\sqrt p$ gives
$$ U(t^2)=\frac{1+t-2t^2}{6}, \qquad 0\le t\le\frac12. $$
Its unique maximizer is
$$ p_*=\frac1{16}, \qquad \pi(p_*)=\frac23, \qquad U(p_*)=\frac3{16}. $$
Thus a feasible interior peak really occurs.

The single-peak theorem is a structural result. It does not itself give a Turing bit-complexity bound for evaluating or locating the peak.

4. An exact-arithmetic algorithm for integer $\beta$

Theorem. Suppose
$$ \alpha=\frac aq,\qquad 1\le a<q, \qquad \beta=b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}, $$
where $a/q$ is in lowest terms. Then the approximation problem is solvable in time polynomial in $L,q,b$.

Here $q,b$ denote their numerical values, so this is not a general polynomial bound in their binary encoding lengths.

4.1. Polynomial formulas after changing variables

For integer $b$, the normalization constant is rational:
$$ C=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+b-1)}{(b-1)!}. $$
To verify this formula, let
$$ Z_b(\alpha)=\int_0^1v^{\alpha-1}(1-v)^{b-1}\,dv. $$
The base case is $Z_1(\alpha)=1/\alpha$. For $b>1$, integration by parts gives
$$ Z_b(\alpha)=\frac{b-1}{\alpha}Z_{b-1}(\alpha+1). $$
The boundary terms vanish because $\alpha>0$ and $b-1>0$. Iteration yields the displayed formula for $C$.

Also,
$$ C=\alpha\prod_{i=1}^{b-1}\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{i}\right) \le\prod_{i=1}^{b-1}\left(1+\frac1i\right)=b. $$

Set $p=t^q$, and define
$$ S_q(t)=S(t^q), \qquad M_q(t)=\int_{t^q}^1vf(v)\,dv. $$
Expanding the integer power $(1-v)^{b-1}$ and integrating term by term gives
$$ S_q(t)= C\sum_{j=0}^{b-1} (-1)^j\binom{b-1}{j} \frac{1-t^{a+qj}}{\alpha+j}, $$
and
$$ M_q(t)= C\sum_{j=0}^{b-1} (-1)^j\binom{b-1}{j} \frac{1-t^{a+q(j+1)}}{\alpha+j+1}. $$
Both are polynomials with rational coefficients. Put
$$ A_q(t)=M_q(t)-t^qS_q(t)=I(t^q). $$
Then the transformed objective is
$$ g(t)=U(t^q)=\rho\frac{A_q(t)}{S_q(t)}. $$

For $0<t<1$, define the polynomial
$$ D_q(t)= C(1-t^q)^{b-1}A_q(t) -t^{q-a}S_q(t)^2. $$
Because
$$ f(t^q)=Ct^{a-q}(1-t^q)^{b-1}, $$
we have
$$ D_q(t)=t^{q-a}\bigl(f(t^q)I(t^q)-S(t^q)^2\bigr). $$
The multiplier is positive. Hence the sign of $D_q(t)$ is exactly the sign of $e'(t^q)$.

All feasibility and derivative-sign tests at rational $t$ can therefore be performed by exact rational arithmetic.

4.2. Bisection without an explicitly computed feasible endpoint

Let
$$ t_0=p_0^{1/q}, \qquad t_\rho=v_\rho^{1/q}, \qquad t_*=\min\{t_0,t_\rho\}. $$
Initialize $\ell=0$ and $r=1$. Maintain
$$ \ell\le t_*\le r, \qquad S_q(\ell)\ge\rho. $$

At a midpoint $t=(\ell+r)/2$:

  • If $S_q(t)<\rho$, replace $r$ by $t$.
  • If $S_q(t)\ge\rho$ and $D_q(t)<0$, replace $r$ by $t$.
  • If $S_q(t)\ge\rho$ and $D_q(t)>0$, replace $\ell$ by $t$.
  • If $S_q(t)\ge\rho$ and $D_q(t)=0$, return

$$ \hat p=t^q,\qquad \hat\pi=\frac{\rho}{S_q(t)}. $$

For the first case, strict decrease of $S_q$ implies $t>t_\rho\ge t_*$. For the second case, the single-peak theorem implies $t>t_0\ge t_*$. For the third case, $t<t_0$ and $t\le t_\rho$, so $t\le t_*$. The fourth case gives the feasible unconstrained optimum. Thus every branch is justified.

Absent early termination, after $N$ iterations,
$$ r-\ell=2^{-N}. $$

4.3. A proved error bound

On the feasible transformed interval,
$$ S_q(t)\ge\rho, \qquad 0\le A_q(t)\le1. $$
Differentiation gives
$$ S_q'(t)=-qCt^{a-1}(1-t^q)^{b-1}, \qquad A_q'(t)=-qt^{q-1}S_q(t). $$
Because $a\ge1$, $b\ge1$, and $C\le b$,
$$ |S_q'(t)|\le qb. $$
Therefore,
$$ \begin{aligned} |g'(t)| &=\rho\left| -qt^{q-1}-\frac{A_q(t)S_q'(t)}{S_q(t)^2} \right|\\ &\le q+\frac{qb}{\rho}. \end{aligned} $$
Set
$$ K=q+\frac{qb}{\rho}, $$
and choose a nonnegative integer $N$ satisfying
$$ K2^{-N}\le\varepsilon. $$
Return
$$ \hat p=\ell^q, \qquad \hat\pi=\frac{\rho}{S_q(\ell)}. $$
The maintained invariant proves feasibility and $\hat\pi\in[0,1]$. Moreover,
$$ U(p_*)-U(\hat p) =g(t_*)-g(\ell) \le K(t_*-\ell) \le K2^{-N} \le\varepsilon. $$
The reported allocation probability is exact, so its error is zero.

4.4. Turing bit complexity

The required iteration count satisfies
$$ N=O(L+\log q+\log b). $$
It can be chosen using exact rational comparisons with powers of two.

The polynomials above have degree $O(qb)$. Their coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in $L,b$: the binomial coefficients have $O(b)$ bits, and all products and denominators in the displayed formulas involve polynomially many rational factors of polynomial bit length.

Every midpoint has $O(N)$ bits. Exact polynomial evaluations therefore use integers of bit length polynomial in $L,q,b,N$ and polynomially many arithmetic operations. The returned price $\ell^q$ has $O(qN+1)$ bits, and the exact returned probability also has polynomial bit length in these parameters.

Using ordinary integer arithmetic, the entire algorithm consequently runs in time polynomial in $L,q,b$.

This extends the restricted algorithm in the notes from $\alpha=1/k,\beta=1$ to arbitrary rational $\alpha$ and positive integer $\beta$. It remains consistent with the lower-bound family, where $q=2^n$.

5. What remains unresolved

The explicit-rational-output problem is unconditionally outside polynomial time in $L$. The full Beta objective nevertheless has a unique peak, and the integer-$\beta$ subfamily admits the proved algorithm above.

Neither fact establishes $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. Settling that alternative requires a hardness reduction under a specified reduction notion for this search problem, whose outputs need not be polynomially bounded. No such reduction is proved here.
