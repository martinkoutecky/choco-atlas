The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m,\alpha,k\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over moderate-up-to-\(k\) ranking-with-intensity ballots, can one compute an alternative \(a\) such that, for every unit-mass electorate \(X\) whose ballot map has pushforward \(\mu\), and every compatible metric \(d\), \(\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x) \le [2+\max(\alpha,t_k)]\min_{c\in A}\int_X d(x,c)\,d\lambda(x)\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)? Here \(t_k\) is the bound defined in Theorem 2.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m\ge2\) alternatives, let \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) be a binary rational, and let \(1\le k\le m-1\). A ballot consists of a strict ranking \(\pi\) and a normal or strong mark between each consecutive pair.
A ballot is moderate-up-to-\(k\) when its first \(k\) marks are normal and its next mark is strong; for \(k=m-1\), all marks are normal. Later marks are unrestricted.
A society \(\mu\) is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass ballot types, each mass a binary rational, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible ballots; \(L\) is the total input bit length. Every supported ballot is moderate-up-to-\(k\).
An electorate is any probability space \((X,\lambda)\) with a measurable ballot map having pushforward \(\mu\). Voters and alternatives occupy a metric space, with coincident locations allowed; their induced distances are measurable and have finite social costs.
Compatibility requires, almost everywhere, for consecutive alternatives \(u=\pi(j)\), \(v=\pi(j+1)\): a normal mark means \(\alpha d(x,v)<d(x,u)\le d(x,v)\), and a strong mark means \(d(x,u)\le\alpha d(x,v)\). Equal distances may be ordered arbitrarily subject to these conditions.
Define \(w_1=(1+\alpha)/(1+3\alpha)\), \(t_1=(1-\alpha)/(1+3\alpha)\), and, for \(2\le j\le k\), \(w_j=1-2\alpha/((1-\alpha)t_{j-1}+2w_{j-1}+2\alpha)\), \(t_j=w_j+(1-w_j)t_{j-1}\).
Can a deterministic algorithm, given only \((A,\alpha,k,\mu)\), compute an alternative \(a\) satisfying \(\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x)\le[2+\max\{\alpha,t_k\}]\min_{c\in A}\int_X d(x,c)\,d\lambda(x)\) for every such electorate and compatible metric, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is obtaining this same guarantee NP-hard?
For finite electorates, a polynomial-time deterministic rule achieves this distortion bound ([Abbaszadeh et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02095v1)). No result addressing exactly the distribution-input problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified binary-rational alpha in [0,1], m at least two, and integer k between 1 and m-1; retained the endpoints.
  • Used the formal definition of moderate-up-to-k: k normal marks followed by a strong mark, except that k=m-1 means no strong mark; later marks remain unrestricted.
  • Specified mandatory elicitation, including the strict normal-mark boundary and weak strong-mark boundary; strict ballot rankings may order equal distances.
  • Made masses positive and normalized to one, and defined L as the total input bit length.
  • Interpreted arbitrary unit-mass electorates as probability spaces with measurable ballot maps and distances, requiring finite social costs.
  • Allowed coincident metric-space locations, consistent with the zero-distance configurations used in the discrete results.
  • Specified deterministic selection using only the reported distribution.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

1. Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. An algorithm can obtain the exact stated guarantee using at most \(m^2+1\) rational linear programs, each of polynomial size.

The proof uses the finite-electorate existence guarantee supplied in the question, namely [Abbaszadeh et al., Theorem 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02095v1#S3.Thmtheorem3.2). It does not require running their algorithm on an exponentially expanded electorate.

Write the supported ballot types as \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_\tau\), with masses \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau>0\). Set \(R=2+\max\{\alpha,t_k\}\).

2. Exact representation by conditional mean distances

Introduce variables \(D_{ab}\) for distances between alternatives, and \(h_{ia}\) for the distance from a representative of ballot type \(i\) to alternative \(a\). Use the conventions \(D_{aa}=0\) and \(D_{ab}=D_{ba}\).

Impose nonnegativity and the following inequalities:

  • For all alternatives \(a,b,c\), \(D_{ab}\le D_{ac}+D_{cb}\).
  • For every type \(i\) and alternatives \(a,b\), \(h_{ia}-h_{ib}\le D_{ab}\), \(h_{ib}-h_{ia}\le D_{ab}\), and \(D_{ab}\le h_{ia}+h_{ib}\).

For each consecutive pair \(u,v\) in ballot \(\sigma_i\), impose:

  • If its mark is normal, \(\alpha h_{iv}<h_{iu}\le h_{iv}\).
  • If its mark is strong, \(h_{iu}\le\alpha h_{iv}\).

Define \(C_a(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i h_{ia}\).

We prove that these variables exactly represent the social-cost vectors attainable by compatible electorates.

From an arbitrary electorate to these variables.

Let \(X_i\) be the measurable set of voters with ballot \(\sigma_i\). Its mass is \(p_i\). Define

\(\displaystyle h_{ia}=\frac{1}{p_i}\int_{X_i}d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x),\qquad D_{ab}=d(a,b).\)

Every \(h_{ia}\) is finite by the assumed finite social costs. The metric inequalities follow by integrating the pointwise triangle inequalities. For example,

\(\displaystyle D_{ab}\le d(a,x)+d(x,b)\)

gives \(D_{ab}\le h_{ia}+h_{ib}\).

The ballot inequalities also survive averaging, including strict normal inequalities. Indeed, for a normal pair, the integrable function \(d(x,u)-\alpha d(x,v)\) is positive almost everywhere on \(X_i\), so its integral is positive. Consequently, \(h_{iu}>\alpha h_{iv}\).

Finally,

\(\displaystyle C_a(h)=\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x).\)

From these variables to a finite metric representation.

Suppose the listed inequalities hold. Construct an undirected weighted graph with alternative vertices and one additional vertex \(v_i\) per ballot type:

  • The edge between alternatives \(a,b\) has length \(D_{ab}\).
  • The edge between \(v_i\) and \(a\) has length \(h_{ia}\).

Give the vertices their shortest-path distances.

These distances preserve every specified alternative distance. To see this, an internal segment \(a,v_i,b\) has length \(h_{ia}+h_{ib}\ge D_{ab}\), so replacing that segment by the alternative edge cannot increase the path length. After all such replacements, the alternative triangle inequalities show that any path from \(a\) to \(b\) has length at least \(D_{ab}\). The direct edge attains this length.

Likewise, a shortest path from \(v_i\) to \(a\) can be chosen without repeated vertices, since edge lengths are nonnegative. If its first alternative is \(b\), replacing its remaining internal type vertices as above shows that its length is at least \(h_{ib}+D_{ba}\). The inequality \(h_{ia}-h_{ib}\le D_{ab}\) then gives

\(\displaystyle h_{ib}+D_{ba}\ge h_{ia}.\)

The direct edge attains \(h_{ia}\).

Thus the resulting distances are exactly \(D_{ab}\) and \(h_{ia}\). Zero distances produce a pseudometric; identifying vertices at zero distance gives a metric space with coincident labeled locations, as permitted in the question.

Put mass \(p_i\) at \(v_i\), with ballot \(\sigma_i\). All compatibility inequalities hold, and the social costs are \(C_a(h)\).

This proves the exact representation.

3. Handling strict inequalities by linear programming

Let \(P\) be the closed polytope obtained from the constraints above by:

  • Replacing every strict normal inequality by \(h_{iu}\ge\alpha h_{iv}\).
  • Adding the bounds \(h_{ia}\le1\) and \(D_{ab}\le1\).

Let \(S\subseteq P\) consist of the points where every normal lower inequality is strict.

The upper bounds lose no relevant information: all metric and ballot constraints are homogeneous, so any feasible finite coordinate vector can be scaled into the unit cube. Social-cost comparisons scale by the same positive factor.

We can determine whether \(S\) is empty with one linear program. Add a variable \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\), require

\(\displaystyle h_{iu}-\alpha h_{iv}\ge\varepsilon\)

for every normal pair, and maximize \(\varepsilon\) over \(P\).

Its optimum is positive exactly when \(S\ne\varnothing\). One direction is immediate. For the other, a point of \(S\) has finitely many positive normal gaps; their minimum is positive and supplies a feasible positive \(\varepsilon\).

If the optimum is zero, no compatible electorate exists, by the representation proved above. The algorithm may then return any alternative.

Assume henceforth that \(S\ne\varnothing\), and fix \(z^0\in S\). For any \(z\in P\) and \(0<\delta\le1\), define

\(\displaystyle z^\delta=(1-\delta)z+\delta z^0.\)

Convexity gives \(z^\delta\in P\). Every normal gap at \(z^\delta\) is a convex combination of a nonnegative gap and a strictly positive gap, hence is positive. Therefore \(z^\delta\in S\), and \(z^\delta\to z\).

In particular, every point of \(P\) is a limit of compatible finite metric representations.

4. A uniformly good alternative exists on the whole polytope

Because the masses are rational, choose an integer \(N\) such that every \(Np_i\) is an integer. Form a fixed finite profile containing \(Np_i\) copies of ballot \(\sigma_i\).

This profile satisfies the moderate-up-to-\(k\) hypothesis: every copied ballot is one of the supported ballots, and the all-normal exception is unchanged.

The supplied finite-electorate theorem gives an alternative \(a^\star\), depending only on this fixed profile, whose guarantee holds for every compatible metric.

For each \(z\in S\), realize its coordinates using the metric construction in Section 2 and place all copies of type \(i\) at \(v_i\). This is a compatible finite electorate. Its total cost for alternative \(a\) is \(N C_a(z)\). Hence, for every \(b\in A\),

\(\displaystyle C_{a^\star}(z)\le R C_b(z)\qquad(z\in S).\)

The alternative \(a^\star\) is the same for every \(z\), because the finite rule sees the same profile throughout.

Now fix any \(z\in P\). Apply this inequality to \(z^\delta\in S\) and let \(\delta\) tend to zero. Linearity and continuity of the costs yield

\(\displaystyle C_{a^\star}(z)\le R C_b(z)\qquad(z\in P,\ b\in A).\)

Thus a single alternative satisfies all required comparisons throughout the closed polytope.

The potentially enormous integer \(N\) is used only in this existence proof.

5. The deterministic algorithm and its correctness

Fix a deterministic order of the alternatives. After the strict-feasibility test above, compute, for each ordered pair \(a,b\),

\(\displaystyle M_{ab}=\max_{z\in P}\bigl(C_a(z)-R C_b(z)\bigr).\)

These are bounded rational linear programs. The origin belongs to \(P\), so every \(M_{ab}\) is nonnegative.

Return the first alternative \(a\) for which \(M_{ab}=0\) for every \(b\in A\). Section 4 proves that at least one such alternative exists.

To verify the guarantee, take any compatible measurable electorate. Section 2 supplies coordinates representing its social costs. Scale those coordinates into \(P\). The selected alternative satisfies \(C_a\le R C_b\) there for every \(b\), by the definition of \(M_{ab}\). Scaling back gives

\(\displaystyle \int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x)\le R\int_X d(x,b)\,d\lambda(x)\qquad(b\in A).\)

Taking the minimum over \(b\) proves precisely the requested guarantee. This argument also covers zero optimal social cost.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

It remains to verify that computing \(R\) exactly does not produce exponentially long rational numbers.

Write \(\alpha=p/q\), with integers \(0\le p\le q\) and \(q\ge1\). Define integer triples by

\(\displaystyle W_1=q+p,\qquad T_1=q-p,\qquad Q_1=q+3p,\)

and, for \(j\ge2\), by

\(\displaystyle W_j=(q-p)T_{j-1}+2qW_{j-1},\)

\(\displaystyle T_j=(q+p)T_{j-1}+2qW_{j-1},\)

\(\displaystyle Q_j=(q-p)T_{j-1}+2qW_{j-1}+2pQ_{j-1}.\)

These satisfy \(w_j=W_j/Q_j\) and \(t_j=T_j/Q_j\). For verification, assuming the previous identities, the denominator in the defining recurrence for \(w_j\) is

\(\displaystyle (1-\alpha)t_{j-1}+2w_{j-1}+2\alpha=\frac{Q_j}{qQ_{j-1}}.\)

Therefore

\(\displaystyle w_j=1-\frac{2pQ_{j-1}}{Q_j}=\frac{W_j}{Q_j},\)

and

\(\displaystyle t_j=\frac{W_j+2pT_{j-1}}{Q_j}=\frac{T_j}{Q_j}.\)

All denominators are positive: initially \(W_1,Q_1>0\), and subsequently \(W_j\ge2qW_{j-1}>0\) and \(Q_j\ge W_j\).

If \(B_j=\max\{W_j,T_j,Q_j\}\), the displayed recurrences give \(B_j\le5qB_{j-1}\), while \(B_1\le4q\). Hence these integers have \(O(k(1+\log q))\) bits. Computing \(R\) and comparing \(\alpha\) with \(t_k\) therefore takes polynomial time.

Each linear program has \(O(\tau m+m^2)\) variables and \(O(\tau m^2+m^3)\) constraints. Its rational coefficients have polynomial bit length in \(k,L\). There are at most \(m^2+1\) programs.

We use the standard theorem that rational linear programming admits deterministic exact optimization in time polynomial in its binary encoding length; exactness permits comparison of the optimum with zero. See [Trevisan’s linear-programming notes](https://lucatrevisan.github.io/cs261/lecture05.pdf). All programs here are nonempty and bounded, so their optima exist.

Consequently, the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), with the exact distortion bound \(2+\max\{\alpha,t_k\}\).
