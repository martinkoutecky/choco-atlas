The continuous mirror question, as graded

For each side \(t\in\{0,1,2\}\), let \(T_t\) be a finite set of types, each with a strict ranking of the types in \(T_{t+1\bmod 3}\), and let \(\mu_t(a)\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) be their masses, summing to \(1\) on each side. Does there exist a rational allocation \(x_{a_0,a_1,a_2}\ge 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_t(a)\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{\mathbf a:a_t=a}x_{\mathbf a}+u_t(a)=\mu_t(a)\), such that no triple \((a_0,a_1,a_2)\) has positive willing mass on all three sides? Here the willing mass of type \(a\in T_t\) for next-side type \(b\) is \(u_t(a)+\sum_{\mathbf c:c_t=a,\ b\succ_a c_{t+1}}x_{\mathbf c}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=3\) sides, indexed by \(t\in\{0,1,2\}\), with indices interpreted modulo \(3\). Each side has a nonempty finite support \(T_t\). Every type \(a\in T_t\) has a strict complete ranking \(\succ_a\) of \(T_{t+1}\), cares only about its next-side partner, and prefers every partner to being unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=\sum_t|T_t|\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses satisfy \(\sum_{a\in T_t}\mu_t(a)=1\) on each side. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including rankings and masses; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(m=3\) fixed.
An allocation consists of \(x_{\mathbf a}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) for every \(\mathbf a\in T_0\times T_1\times T_2\), and unmatched masses \(u_t(a)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), satisfying \(\sum_{\mathbf a:a_t=a}x_{\mathbf a}+u_t(a)=\mu_t(a)\) for every \(t,a\).
Define willing mass by \(W_t(a,b)=u_t(a)+\sum_{\mathbf c:c_t=a,\ b\succ_a c_{t+1}}x_{\mathbf c}\). An allocation is weakly stable if every triple \(\mathbf a\) has some side \(t\) with \(W_t(a_t,a_{t+1})=0\).

Is deciding whether a rational weakly stable allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with equally many agents on each side and strict complete cyclic preferences, weakly stable matching existence is NP-complete ([Lam–Plaxton](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02844)). No result settling the exact mass-allocation decision problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made completeness explicit: each ranking includes every supported next-side type, and every such partner is preferable to being unmatched.
  • Required unmatched masses to be rational, consistently with the rational allocation and input masses.
  • Defined tau as the total support size across the three sides, L as the total encoded input length, and m as the fixed number of sides.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable. Every valid instance admits a rational weakly stable allocation. This follows from Scarf’s lemma for fractional hypergraph matchings. The proof applies to the exact willing-mass definition in the question.

1. The theorem used

We use the following form of Scarf’s lemma.

Let \(A\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{n\times N}\) have a nonzero entry in every column, and let \(b\in\mathbb R_{>0}^{n}\). For each row \(i\), specify a strict total order \(\widehat{\succ}_i\) on the columns \(j\) with \(A_{ij}>0\). Then the polytope

\(P=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{N}:Ax\le b\}\)

has an extreme point \(x\) such that every column \(j\) is dominated at some row \(i\): namely,

\(A_{ij}>0,\qquad (Ax)_i=b_i,\qquad k\widehat{\succeq}_i j\text{ whenever }A_{ik}x_k>0.\)

Here \(\widehat{\succeq}_i\) is the reflexive closure of \(\widehat{\succ}_i\). This statement, including arbitrary nonnegative real capacities, appears as Lemma 1 in [Biró–Csáji–Schlotter, *Stable Hypergraph Matching in Unimodular Hypergraphs*](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31.html).

Thus domination means that the row is saturated and every positive allocation incident to that row is at least as preferred as the proposed column.

2. Constructing the hypergraph preference system

Treat types on different sides as distinct vertices, writing

\(V=\{(t,a):t\in\{0,1,2\},\ a\in T_t\}.\)

For every triple \(\mathbf a=(a_0,a_1,a_2)\), introduce the hyperedge

\(e_{\mathbf a}=\{(0,a_0),(1,a_1),(2,a_2)\}.\)

Let \(E\) be this collection of hyperedges. Its size is

\(N=|T_0||T_1||T_2|\le\tau^3.\)

Let \(A\) be the vertex–hyperedge incidence matrix: \(A_{v,e}=1\) if \(v\in e\), and \(A_{v,e}=0\) otherwise. Give vertex \(v=(t,a)\) capacity \(b_v=\mu_t(a)\).

All capacities are positive, and every column of \(A\) contains exactly three ones. Therefore the matrix and capacity hypotheses of Scarf’s lemma hold. The polytope \(P\) is nonempty because it contains zero, and bounded because each coordinate satisfies \(x_e\le b_v\) for every \(v\in e\).

It remains to define strict preferences over incident hyperedges. At vertex \(v=(t,a)\), first compare hyperedges according to their next-side partners:

\(c_{t+1}\succ_a d_{t+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e_{\mathbf c}\widehat{\succ}_v e_{\mathbf d}.\)

Break ties between hyperedges with the same next-side partner arbitrarily. This produces a strict total order. Crucially,

\(e_{\mathbf c}\widehat{\succeq}_v e_{\mathbf a}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{t+1}=a_{t+1}\text{ or }c_{t+1}\succ_a a_{t+1}.\)

Thus a hyperedge that is at least as good in the refined order never supplies a strictly worse next-side partner.

3. Domination implies the required stability

Apply Scarf’s lemma to obtain an extreme point \(x\in P\) dominating every hyperedge. Define unmatched masses by

\(u_t(a)=\mu_t(a)-\sum_{\mathbf c:c_t=a}x_{e_{\mathbf c}}.\)

These are nonnegative because \(Ax\le b\), and the allocation equations hold by definition.

Fix any triple \(\mathbf a\). Its hyperedge is dominated at some vertex \(v=(t,a_t)\). Saturation gives

\(\sum_{\mathbf c:c_t=a_t}x_{e_{\mathbf c}}=\mu_t(a_t),\)

so \(u_t(a_t)=0\).

Moreover, every incident hyperedge with positive mass is at least as good as \(e_{\mathbf a}\) in the refined order. Consequently,

\(x_{e_{\mathbf c}}>0,\ c_t=a_t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \neg(a_{t+1}\succ_{a_t}c_{t+1}).\)

Every summand in the willing-mass expression is therefore zero, yielding

\(W_t(a_t,a_{t+1})=u_t(a_t)+\sum_{\mathbf c:c_t=a_t,\ a_{t+1}\succ_{a_t}c_{t+1}}x_{e_{\mathbf c}}=0.\)

Since the triple was arbitrary, the allocation is weakly stable exactly as defined in the question.

4. Rationality

The point supplied by Scarf’s lemma is an extreme point of \(P\), whose defining matrix and right-hand side are rational.

For completeness, the active constraint normals at an extreme point must span the ambient space. Otherwise, a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would allow sufficiently small perturbations in both directions: active constraints would remain equalities, and the finitely many inactive constraints would remain satisfied. That would contradict extremality.

Select a full-rank subsystem of active constraints. Its coefficients and right-hand side are rational, and \(x\) is its unique solution. Cramer’s rule therefore gives \(x\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{N}\). The unmatched masses, being differences of rational quantities, are rational as well.

This proves rational existence, rather than merely real existence.

5. All mass is matched

In fact, every weakly stable allocation under the stated normalization has zero unmatched mass.

Let \(X=\sum_{\mathbf a}x_{\mathbf a}\). Summing the allocation equations on each side gives

\(\sum_{a\in T_t}u_t(a)=1-X\qquad\text{for every }t.\)

If \(X<1\), each side contains a type \(a_t\) with \(u_t(a_t)>0\). For the resulting triple,

\(W_t(a_t,a_{t+1})\ge u_t(a_t)>0\qquad\text{for all }t,\)

contradicting weak stability. Hence \(X=1\), and nonnegativity forces every unmatched mass to vanish.

6. Decision complexity and the finite-matching result

On inputs satisfying the premises, the decision algorithm simply returns YES, taking \(O(1)\) time. Including input reading and validation, a straightforward implementation using elementary integer arithmetic takes \(O(L^3)\) bit operations, with \(m=3\) fixed and \(\tau\le L\).

The [Lam–Plaxton hardness result](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02844) concerns integral matchings. It does not contradict the existence proved here: divisible triple masses admit a stable fractional solution even when no integral stable matching exists.

The proof establishes existence and the decision complexity. It does not assert a polynomial-time algorithm for constructing a stable allocation.
