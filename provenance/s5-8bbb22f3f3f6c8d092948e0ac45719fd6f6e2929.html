Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite item set \(B\), a supported set \(T\) of strict preference types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and item prices \(c_b\), choose supplies \(z_b\ge 0\) and a complete assignment \(\pi_{tb}\) minimizing \(\sum_{b\in B}c_bz_b\). Require \(\sum_b\pi_{tb}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi_{tb}\le z_b\), and \(\pi_{tb}=0\) unless type \(t\) accepts item \(b\). The assignment must be popular: there is no feasible reassignment of the same atomless population, represented by a typewise coupling \(\eta_{tbb'}\) from current item \(b\) to new item \(b'\), whose mass preferring the new item exceeds its mass preferring the current item.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(B\) be a finite item universe, \(m=|B|\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each type strictly ranks a nonempty acceptable subset \(A_t\subseteq B\). Item prices \(c_b\) and a budget \(K\) are nonnegative binary rationals. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Choose real supplies \(z_b\geq0\) and assignment masses \(\pi_{tb}\geq0\), with \(\sum_{b\in B}\pi_{tb}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\pi_{tb}\leq z_b\), and \(\pi_{tb}=0\) for \(b\notin A_t\). All supplies are purchased, at cost \(\sum_b c_bz_b\); there is no initial free supply. Copies of the same item are indifferent.
Comparison assignments use these same supplies and may leave people unmatched. Write \(\bot\) for being unmatched, ranked below every acceptable item. A comparison is any real coupling \(\eta_{tbd}\geq0\), indexed by \(t\in T\), \(b\in B\), \(d\in B\cup\{\bot\}\), satisfying \(\sum_d\eta_{tbd}=\pi_{tb}\), \(\sum_{t,b}\eta_{tbd}\leq z_d\) for \(d\in B\), and \(\eta_{tbd}=0\) unless \(d\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}\).
The complete assignment is popular if every such coupling satisfies \(\sum_{t,b,d:\,d\succ_t b}\eta_{tbd}\leq\sum_{t,b,d:\,b\succ_t d}\eta_{tbd}\); equal votes do not defeat it. Decide whether a popular complete assignment can be purchased for cost at most \(K\).

Is this continuous budget decision problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

The discrete min-cost popular instance problem is NP-hard even for strict lists of length at most two drawn from a common total order. With fixed integer supplies, discrete minimum-cost popular matching is polynomial-time solvable. [Kavitha, Nasre, and Nimbhorkar](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.2591)
No result settling the continuous problem with the coupling comparison defined above was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Comparison reassignments may leave mass unmatched, as discrete popularity compares against all matchings, including incomplete ones; unmatched is worse than every acceptable item.
  • Comparison reassignments retain the chosen supplies, may leave supplies unused, and cannot purchase additional supplies.
  • Prices are nonnegative binary rationals, allowing zero prices as in the discrete construction.
  • Each supported type has a nonempty acceptable subset; an empty list would make complete assignment impossible.
  • Supplies, assignment masses, and comparison couplings are arbitrary nonnegative real quantities, with no integrality restriction.
  • The computational question uses a nonnegative binary rational budget threshold to state the decision version of minimum cost.

Result

The continuous budget decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when every acceptable list has length at most two and all lists are drawn from a common total order.

The key fact is that splitting a type’s mass among several items never improves the minimum achievable cost: every popular complete assignment can be replaced by a popular assignment that gives each type a single item, without increasing cost.

1. Removing unused supply

For any feasible assignment, define its used supply by \(s_b=\sum_t\pi_{tb}\).

If \((z,\pi)\) is popular, then \((s,\pi)\) is also popular. Indeed, \(s_b\leq z_b\), so every comparison feasible under \(s\) was feasible under \(z\). Moreover, nonnegative prices imply \(\sum_b c_bs_b\leq\sum_b c_bz_b\).

Consequently, throughout the structural argument we may assume \(z_b=\sum_t\pi_{tb}\) for every item. Let \(U=\{b:z_b>0\}\).

2. An exact characterization of popularity

Define a directed graph \(D_\pi\) on \(U\). There is an arc \(b\to d\) precisely when some type \(t\) satisfies \(\pi_{tb}>0\) and \(d\succ_t b\).

Lemma. Under the assumption \(z_b=\sum_t\pi_{tb}\), the assignment is popular if and only if \(D_\pi\) contains no directed walk of length two. Here a walk \(x\to y\to x\) also counts.

Necessity

Suppose \(x\to y\to w\). Choose witnesses \(t,s\) with \(\pi_{tx}>0\), \(y\succ_t x\), \(\pi_{sy}>0\), and \(w\succ_s y\).

Strict improvement implies \(x\neq y\) and \(y\neq w\). There are two cases.

  • If \(w=x\), choose \(0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{\pi_{tx},\pi_{sy}\}\). Starting from the identity comparison, move \(\varepsilon\) mass from the row \((t,x)\) to \(y\), and from \((s,y)\) to \(x\). Item loads remain unchanged. Both moved groups improve, giving a positive vote margin of \(2\varepsilon\).
  • If \(x,y,w\) are distinct, choose a type \(r\) with \(\pi_{rw}>0\), which exists because \(w\in U\). Choose \(0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{\pi_{tx},\pi_{sy},\pi_{rw}\}\). Starting from the identity comparison, move \(\varepsilon\) mass from \((t,x)\) to \(y\), from \((s,y)\) to \(w\), and from \((r,w)\) to \(\bot\). The load on \(x\) decreases by \(\varepsilon\); all other item loads remain unchanged. Two groups improve and one becomes unmatched, giving a positive margin of \(\varepsilon\).

The modified rows are distinct because their original items are distinct. Thus both constructions are feasible couplings and contradict popularity.

Sufficiency

Suppose \(D_\pi\) has no directed walk of length two. Assign each supplied item a potential \(q_b\in\{0,1\}\): set \(q_b=1\) if \(b\) has an incoming arc, and \(q_b=0\) otherwise. Set \(q_\bot=0\).

No vertex has both an incoming and an outgoing arc. Consequently, every improvement arc \(b\to d\) satisfies \(q_b=0\) and \(q_d=1\).

For an original item \(b\) and comparison outcome \(d\), let \(v_t(b,d)\) equal \(1\) for an improvement, \(0\) for equality, and \(-1\) for a worsening, including becoming unmatched.

For every row with \(\pi_{tb}>0\) and every possible comparison destination \(d\in U\cup\{\bot\}\), we have

\(v_t(b,d)\leq q_d-q_b.\)

For improvements this follows from the preceding observation. For equality both sides are zero. For a worse supplied item, the right side is at least \(-1\). For becoming unmatched, it equals \(-q_b\), again at least \(-1\).

Now take any feasible coupling \(\eta\), and write \(\widehat z_d=\sum_{t,b}\eta_{tbd}\) for its load on item \(d\). Destinations outside \(U\) have zero comparison mass. Therefore its vote margin satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,b,d}\eta_{tbd}v_t(b,d)\leq\sum_{d\in U}q_d\widehat z_d-\sum_{b\in U}q_bz_b\leq0.\)

The first inequality uses the pointwise potential bound and the coupling’s row sums. The second uses \(\widehat z_d\leq z_d\) and \(q_d\geq0\). This proves popularity.

3. Rounding each supported type to one item

Proposition. Every popular complete assignment can be replaced by a popular complete assignment of no greater cost that assigns each supported type entirely to one item.

First remove unused supply. For every type \(t\), choose an item \(b_t\) minimizing price among those with \(\pi_{tb}>0\). This set is nonempty because \(\mu_t>0\).

Define \(\pi'_{tb_t}=\mu_t\), set all other entries of that row to zero, and buy exactly \(z'_b=\sum_t\pi'_{tb}\).

Every item used by \(\pi'\) was used by \(\pi\). Moreover, every arc in \(D_{\pi'}\) already belonged to \(D_\pi\): its witnessing assigned pair had positive mass in the original assignment. Thus \(D_{\pi'}\) is a subgraph of \(D_\pi\), so it contains no directed walk of length two. The lemma proves that \(\pi'\) is popular.

For each type,

\(\displaystyle \mu_tc_{b_t}\leq\sum_b c_b\pi_{tb}.\)

Summing over types proves the cost claim.

This proposition applies to arbitrary real assignment masses; no rationality or integrality assumption on the original solution is needed.

4. Polynomial reduction from the discrete problem

Use the discrete hardness result stated in the question: buying integer item supplies supporting a popular complete matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even for lists of length at most two drawn from a common order. The cited reduction uses integer prices and an integer budget threshold. [Kavitha, Nasre, and Nimbhorkar, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.2591)

Let such a discrete instance have \(N\geq1\) explicitly listed people and budget \(C\). Group identical preference lists into distinct supported types, with multiplicities \(n_t\).

To make even the masses dyadic, let \(R\) be the smallest power of two with \(R\geq N\). Give each original type mass \(\mu_t=n_t/R\). If \(R>N\), introduce one additional type of mass \((R-N)/R\), accepting only a new zero-price item \(h\). Set the continuous budget to \(K=C/R\), and retain all original item prices and rankings.

There are at most \(N+1\) supported types. All new rational values have polynomial encoding length, so this construction is polynomial.

Discrete yes implies continuous yes

Take a discrete popular complete matching costing at most \(C\), and delete unused purchased copies.

Its improvement graph contains no directed walk of length two. Indeed, either construction in the necessity proof above, with \(\varepsilon=1\), would otherwise give a defeating discrete comparison. The people selected there are distinct because they originally receive distinct items.

For each original type and item, let \(\pi_{tb}\) be the number of people of that type receiving that item, divided by \(R\). Buy exactly these scaled item loads. Assign the additional type, if present, entirely to \(h\).

Aggregation and scaling preserve the original improvement graph; \(h\) is isolated. The sufficiency lemma therefore proves popularity against all real couplings, not merely discrete comparisons. The cost is at most \(C/R=K\).

Continuous yes implies discrete yes

Take a continuous popular complete assignment costing at most \(K\). Apply the rounding proposition, obtaining one assigned item \(b_t\) per supported type.

In the original discrete instance, assign all \(n_t\) people of type \(t\) to \(b_t\), and buy exactly the resulting integer item loads. Its cost is

\(\displaystyle \sum_t n_tc_{b_t}=R\sum_t\mu_tc_{b_t}\leq RK=C,\)

where the sums concern original types; the additional type contributes zero cost.

The discrete assignment’s improvement graph is a subgraph of the rounded continuous assignment’s graph, so it has no directed walk of length two. The potential proof in Section 2 applies equally to unit-mass people and shows that every discrete comparison has nonpositive vote margin. Hence this discrete matching is popular.

The reduction preserves list length and the common-order restriction; the isolated item \(h\) can be placed anywhere in the common order. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

By the rounding proposition, every yes-instance has a certificate specifying one acceptable item \(b_t\) for each supported type.

A verifier buys exactly \(z_b=\sum_{t:b_t=b}\mu_t\), checks the rational cost against \(K\), and constructs the improvement graph. The graph has a directed walk of length two exactly when some vertex has both positive indegree and positive outdegree. Thus popularity is verifiable in polynomial time.

The certificate has polynomial length, and exact rational arithmetic takes time polynomial in the input bit length. Therefore the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
