Theorem 3.1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of voting-rights types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive integer weights \(a_t\), and a rational quota per capita \(q\), form each admissible replicated weighted voting game with \(N\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\) and quota \(Nq\). Determine whether the type aggregates \(b_t(N)=\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}PBI_i^{(N)}\) converge as \(N\) grows through the specified admissible values and, if so, compute their exact limits.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a binary-encoded positive integer weight \(a_t\). Let \(m=\max_t a_t\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The binary-rational quota per capita satisfies \(0<q\leq\sum_t\mu_t a_t\). An integer \(N>0\) is admissible exactly when every \(N\mu_t\) is integral; the replicated game has \(N\mu_t\) distinct voters of type \(t\), each of weight \(a_t\), and a coalition wins exactly when its total weight is at least \(Nq\).
For voter \(i\), let \(BS_i^{(N)}\) count winning coalitions containing \(i\) that become losing when \(i\) is removed, and set \(PBI_i^{(N)}=BS_i^{(N)}/\sum_j BS_j^{(N)}\). Define \(b_t(N)=\sum_{i\text{ of type }t}PBI_i^{(N)}\). Convergence means convergence of the entire vector along all admissible \(N\to\infty\). Exact limits use standard real-algebraic-number encodings by integer polynomials and rational isolating intervals.

Is deciding convergence of \((b_t(N))_{t\in T}\), and computing its exact limits whenever it converges, polynomial-time solvable in the binary input size, or NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).

For finite simple voting games, Banzhaf scores admit an exact inclusion–exclusion formula over minimal winning coalitions; this formula supplies no polynomial-time guarantee.
On the asymptotic side, [Kurz, *Approximating Power by Weights*](https://epub.uni-bayreuth.de/3568/1/approximating_power.pdf) establishes existence of a limiting distance between normalized Banzhaf power and relative weights for equal-sized groups of weight \(1\) and weight \(2\), with any fixed relative quota in \((0,1)\). This special-case result does not classify the general convergence-and-exact-computation problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the quota to be positive and at most the mean weight, preserving the discrete requirements that the empty coalition loses and the grand coalition wins; retained the upper endpoint.
  • Defined admissibility solely by integral type counts and used all such positive replication sizes. A rational threshold need not be integral under the discrete definition.
  • Specified that equality with the quota wins and that replicated voters remain distinct coalition members.
  • Specified joint convergence along the full admissible sequence rather than a selected subsequence.
  • Fixed standard polynomial-and-isolating-interval real algebraic encodings for exact limit computation, since the graded question did not specify an exact-output representation.
  • Defined m as the maximum individual weight and L as total binary input length; polynomial time refers to binary input size, not polynomial dependence on the numerical weight bound.

Proven partial results

Every valid society has a convergent type-power vector. Convergence decision is therefore polynomial-time solvable in $L$. The exact limits can be computed in
$$ (\tau m+L)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations.

Under the conventional dense encoding of the defining integer polynomials, exact output can require $2^{\Omega(L)}$ bits, already for $\tau=2$. Thus exact computation in polynomial total time in $L$ is impossible under that encoding.

I do not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. The output-size lower bound does not establish it, so the requested hardness classification remains incomplete.

In addition to proving these statements and repairing the quantitative gaps identified in the notes, I prove that the limits admit additive $2^{-k}$ approximations in time
$$ (\tau+L+k)^{O(1)}. $$

1. The limiting vector

Set
$$ A=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t. $$
For $0<q<A$, define $x$ to be the unique positive solution of
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t\frac{x^{a_t}}{1+x^{a_t}}=q. $$
This solution exists and is unique: the left-hand side is continuous and strictly increasing on the positive real axis, with endpoint limits $0$ and $A$.

Define
$$ h_t(x)=\frac{x^{a_t}-1}{x^{a_t}+1}. $$

Theorem. Along all admissible replication sizes,
$$ \lim_{N\to\infty}b_t(N)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle \frac{\mu_th_t(x)}{\sum_s\mu_sh_s(x)}, &0<q<A,\quad q\ne A/2,\\[8pt] \displaystyle\frac{\mu_ta_t}{A}, &q=A/2,\\[8pt] \mu_t, &q=A. \end{cases} $$

The parameter satisfies $x=1$ exactly when $q=A/2$. If $x\ne1$, all $h_t(x)$ have the same nonzero sign. Consequently, the denominator in the first formula is nonzero, and every limit coordinate is positive.

Admissible replication sizes form an infinite sequence: every positive multiple of a common denominator of the masses is admissible. The proof below applies to that entire sequence.

1.1. A lattice local estimate

Fix $0<q<A$ and its parameter $x$. Put
$$ p_s=\frac{x^{a_s}}{1+x^{a_s}},\qquad d=\gcd_{s\in T}a_s,\qquad v=\sum_s\mu_sa_s^2p_s(1-p_s)>0. $$

For admissible $N$, include each voter of type $s$ independently with probability $p_s$. Let $S_{N,t}$ be the coalition weight after one specified voter of type $t$ has been deleted.

For every fixed constant $M$, uniformly over $t\in T$ and integers $k\in d\mathbb Z$ satisfying $|k-Nq|\le M$,
$$ \Pr(S_{N,t}=k) = \frac{d}{\sqrt{2\pi Nv}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr). $$

Here and below, the society is fixed while $N$ tends to infinity; constants may depend on that society.

To prove the estimate, write $c_s=a_s/d$. The characteristic function of $S_{N,t}/d$ is
$$ \phi_{N,t}(u) = \prod_s (1-p_s+p_se^{ic_su})^{N\mu_s-\mathbf1_{\{s=t\}}}. $$
Its mean is
$$ M_{N,t}=Nq/d-c_tp_t, $$
and its variance is
$$ Nv/d^2-c_t^2p_t(1-p_t). $$

For $0<p_s<1$,
$$ |1-p_s+p_se^{ic_su}|=1 $$
holds exactly when $c_su\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. Since $\gcd_s c_s=1$, all factors have modulus one simultaneously on $[-\pi,\pi]$ only at $u=0$.

For sufficiently large $N$,
$$ N\mu_s-\mathbf1_{\{s=t\}}\ge N\mu_s/2. $$
Thus, outside any fixed neighborhood of zero, compactness gives a bound
$$ |\phi_{N,t}(u)|\le\rho^N $$
for some $\rho<1$, uniformly in $t$.

Near zero, Taylor expansion of the centered characteristic function gives
$$ \log\!\left(e^{-iuM_{N,t}}\phi_{N,t}(u)\right) = -\frac{Nv}{2d^2}u^2+O(N|u|^3+u^2). $$
The bounded $u^2$ term accounts for the deleted voter. On a sufficiently small fixed neighborhood of zero, the modulus is bounded by $e^{-cNu^2}$ for some $c>0$.

Fourier inversion gives
$$ \Pr(S_{N,t}=k) = \frac1{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{-iuk/d}\phi_{N,t}(u)\,du. $$
After substituting $u=y/\sqrt N$, the centered characteristic function tends to
$$ e^{-vy^2/(2d^2)}. $$
The additional phase tends uniformly to one because $k/d-M_{N,t}$ is bounded. Gaussian domination applies near zero, and the remaining integral is exponentially small. Hence
$$ \Pr(S_{N,t}=k) = \frac1{2\pi\sqrt N} \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-vy^2/(2d^2)}\,dy +o(N^{-1/2}) = \frac{d}{\sqrt{2\pi Nv}}+o(N^{-1/2}). $$
The same bounds establish the stated uniformity.

1.2. Swing counts and quota rounding

All voters of type $t$ have the same Banzhaf score; write it as $s_t(N)$. The generating polynomial for coalition weights before deletion is
$$ F_N(X)=\prod_s(1+X^{a_s})^{N\mu_s}. $$

A voter of type $t$ is critical precisely when the weight $k$ of the other coalition members satisfies
$$ Nq-a_t\le k<Nq. $$
Exponential tilting gives the exact identity
$$ [X^k]\frac{F_N(X)}{1+X^{a_t}} = \frac{F_N(x)}{1+x^{a_t}}x^{-k}\Pr(S_{N,t}=k). $$

Let
$$ H_N=d\left\lceil\frac{Nq}{d}\right\rceil, \qquad r_t=a_t/d. $$
The lattice points in the criticality interval are exactly
$$ H_N-jd,\qquad 1\le j\le r_t. $$
This remains true when $Nq/d$ is integral, because the interval excludes its upper endpoint.

The local estimate therefore gives
$$ s_t(N) = \frac{F_N(x)d\,x^{-H_N}}{\sqrt{2\pi Nv}} \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r_t}x^{jd}}{1+x^{a_t}} \bigl(1+o(1)\bigr). $$
The relative error is justified because there are finitely many positive summands and the local estimate is uniform over them.

For $x\ne1$,
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{r_t}x^{jd} = \frac{x^d(x^{a_t}-1)}{x^d-1}. $$
Consequently,
$$ s_t(N)=K_N|h_t(x)|\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr), $$
where the positive common factor is
$$ K_N= \frac{F_N(x)d\,x^{-H_N}x^d} {\sqrt{2\pi Nv}\,|x^d-1|}. $$
All dependence on quota rounding occurs in this common factor.

There are $N\mu_t$ voters of type $t$, so
$$ b_t(N)= \frac{\mu_ts_t(N)}{\sum_s\mu_ss_s(N)}. $$
Cancelling $K_N$ proves the first limiting formula.

When $x=1$, the swing estimate instead becomes
$$ s_t(N) = \frac{2^Na_t}{2\sqrt{2\pi Nv}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr), $$
which proves the midpoint formula.

Finally, if $q=A$, positivity of the weights makes the grand coalition the unique winning coalition. Every voter has exactly one swing. Thus $b_t(N)=\mu_t$ for every admissible $N$.

The normalization is always defined: the empty coalition loses, the grand coalition wins, and an inclusion-minimal winning coalition has at least one critical voter. This completes the proof of universal convergence.

2. Exact computation in pseudopolynomial time

The endpoint and midpoint limits are rational and computable in $L^{O(1)}$ time. Suppose henceforth that
$$ 0<q<A,\qquad q\ne A/2. $$

Let $E$ be a common denominator of the masses and quota. Its bit length is $O(L)$. Define
$$ S=\sum_ta_t\le\tau m,\qquad D(X)=\prod_s(1+X^{a_s}), $$
and
$$ P(X)= EqD(X) - \sum_tE\mu_ta_tX^{a_t} \prod_{s\ne t}(1+X^{a_s}). $$
This is an integer polynomial of degree at most $S$. Its coefficients have magnitude at most $2^H$, where one can choose
$$ H=O\!\left(L+\tau+\log(m+1)+\log(\tau+1)\right). $$
Indeed, the product coefficients are at most $2^\tau$, the scalar factors have polynomial bit length in $L$, and there are at most $\tau+1$ summands.

For positive $X$, $D(X)>0$, so $P(X)=0$ is equivalent to the parameter equation. In particular, $x$ is its unique positive root. It is simple: writing
$$ f(X)=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\frac{X^{a_t}}{1+X^{a_t}}, $$
we have
$$ P'(x)=-ED(x)f'(x)\ne0. $$

Define
$$ B_t(X)= E\mu_t(X^{a_t}-1)\prod_{s\ne t}(1+X^{a_s}), \qquad C(X)=\sum_sB_s(X). $$
These integer polynomials have degree at most $S$ and coefficient height bounded by the same form of $H$. The required coordinate is
$$ \beta_t=\frac{B_t(x)}{C(x)}, $$
and $C(x)\ne0$ by the common-sign argument in Section 1.

Take the primitive square-free part of $P$, remove its greatest common divisor with $C$, and normalize the result to a primitive integer polynomial $P_0$ with positive leading coefficient. The root $x$ is preserved, and
$$ \gcd(P_0,C)=1. $$

2.1. Coefficient growth after factor removal

The height of $P_0$ need not equal that of $P$. The following elementary bound accounts for this growth.

Every complex root of $P$ has modulus at most
$$ R_P=1+2^H\le2^{H+1} $$
by the Cauchy root bound. Since $P_0$ is a primitive integer divisor of the primitive part of $P$, its leading coefficient divides that of the latter and has magnitude at most $2^H$.

If $n=\deg P_0\le S$, expansion in terms of its roots bounds every coefficient by
$$ 2^H\,2^nR_P^n \le 2^{H+S+S(H+1)}. $$
Thus a valid coefficient-height bound for $P_0$ is
$$ H_0=(S+1)H+2S+2. $$
In particular, factor removal increases the height by only a polynomial amount in $S$ and $H$.

Now form
$$ R_t(Y)= \operatorname{Res}_X(P_0(X),\,C(X)Y-B_t(X)). $$
Because $P_0$ and $C$ are coprime, every factor in the root-product expression for this resultant has nonzero coefficient of $Y$. Hence $R_t$ is nonzero, has degree at most $S$, and vanishes at $\beta_t$.

The Sylvester matrix has dimension at most $2S$. Each entry has degree at most one in $Y$ and coefficients bounded by $2^{H_0}$, after enlarging $H_0$ if necessary. Bounding the determinant expansion gives coefficient bit lengths
$$ K=O\!\left(S(H_0+\log(S+1))\right) = O\!\left(S^2(H+\log(S+1))\right). $$
This bound explicitly includes the height growth of $P_0$.

2.2. A quantitative algebraic evaluation bound

The following bound will identify the correct root of $R_t$.

Lemma. Let $Q\in\mathbb Z[X]$ be primitive, of degree $n$ and coefficient magnitudes at most $2^h$. Let $\alpha$ be a root of $Q$. If $G\in\mathbb Z[X]$ has degree $g$, coefficient magnitudes at most $2^j$, and $G(\alpha)\ne0$, then
$$ |G(\alpha)|\ge2^{-\kappa}, $$
where
$$ \kappa= gh+(n-1)\left( j+\left\lceil\log_2(g+1)\right\rceil+g(h+1) \right). $$

Proof. Let $F$ be the primitive irreducible polynomial of $\alpha$. By Gauss's lemma, $F$ divides $Q$ in $\mathbb Z[X]$. Its leading coefficient has magnitude at most $2^h$, and all its roots have modulus at most $2^{h+1}$.

Since $G(\alpha)\ne0$ and $F$ is irreducible,
$$ \operatorname{Res}(F,G) $$
is a nonzero integer. Its absolute value is therefore at least one. At every conjugate $\alpha_i$,
$$ |G(\alpha_i)| \le (g+1)2^j2^{g(h+1)}. $$
Using the resultant product formula and bounding the other at most $n-1$ factors gives the displayed lower bound. $\square$

Apply the lemma with $Q=P_0$ and $G=C$. It supplies an explicit lower bound
$$ |C(x)|\ge2^{-\operatorname{poly}(S,H_0,H)}. $$

More generally, let $u/v$, with $v>0$, be a rational number that is not a root of $R_t$. Then
$$ G_{t,u/v}(X)=vB_t(X)-uC(X) $$
does not vanish at $x$. Its degree is at most $S$, and its coefficient height is at most
$$ H+O\!\left(\operatorname{bits}(u)+\operatorname{bits}(v)+1\right). $$
The lemma therefore provides a polynomial-exponent lower bound for its nonzero value at $x$.

These value bounds give a polynomial precision bound for sign determination. Indeed, if $G$ has degree $g$ and coefficient magnitudes at most $2^j$, then on
$$ |z|\le2^{H_0+1}+1 $$
its derivative is bounded by
$$ |G'(z)| \le (g+1)^2\,2^j(2^{H_0+1}+1)^g. $$
The logarithm of this bound is polynomial in the parameters. Approximating $x$ sufficiently accurately that the resulting evaluation error is less than half the lemma's lower bound determines the sign of $G(x)$. The required number of precision bits is polynomial in the degree and coefficient-height parameters.

2.3. Producing the isolating intervals

Use the standard real-root isolation theorem: all distinct real roots of an integer polynomial of degree $n$ and coefficient bit length $h$ can be isolated by rational intervals in $(n+h)^{O(1)}$ bit operations, including for polynomials with repeated roots. This gives polynomial-length endpoints. Such algorithms are established in [Emiris, Mourrain, and Tsigaridas, *Real Algebraic Numbers: Complexity Analysis and Experimentation*](https://who.paris.inria.fr/Elias.Tsigaridas/b2hd-emt-lncs-2006.html).

Isolate the unique positive root $x$ of $P_0$, and isolate all distinct real roots of each $R_t$. Choose open isolating intervals $(\ell,u)$ whose rational endpoints are not roots of $R_t$. All input degrees and heights for these applications are bounded by $S$, $H_0$, and $K$, so their hypotheses and polynomial bounds apply.

For each candidate interval, determine the signs of
$$ C(x),\qquad B_t(x)-\ell C(x),\qquad B_t(x)-uC(x), $$
clearing rational denominators first. The quantitative lemma proves that polynomial precision suffices. Refinement of the isolating interval for $x$ to that precision also takes polynomial time: its root is simple, and bisection with exact polynomial evaluations either finds a rational root or maintains a sign-changing isolating interval.

Exactly one candidate interval satisfies
$$ \frac{B_t(x)}{C(x)}>\ell, \qquad \frac{B_t(x)}{C(x)}<u. $$
Return its interval with $R_t$, or with the primitive square-free part of $R_t$.

Dense polynomial construction, polynomial gcds via subresultant algorithms, and resultants all have polynomial bit complexity in these degree and height bounds. Consequently, the total running time is
$$ (S+L)^{O(1)} \subseteq (\tau m+L)^{O(1)}. $$
Since $\tau\le L$ and $m\le2^{O(L)}$, this also gives a $2^{O(L)}$ worst-case upper bound.

3. Polynomial-time finite-precision computation

The algebraic output can be large, but approximating its value does not require expanding the defining polynomials.

Theorem. For every integer $k\ge1$, rational approximations $\widehat\beta_t$ satisfying
$$ |\widehat\beta_t-\beta_t|\le2^{-k} \qquad(t\in T) $$
can be computed in $(\tau+L+k)^{O(1)}$ bit operations.

The endpoint and midpoint cases follow directly from their rational formulas. Consider the remaining case, and put
$$ c=|2q-A|,\qquad \rho=c/A,\qquad w_t=\frac{\mu_ta_t}{A}. $$
Then $0<\rho<1$ and $\sum_tw_t=1$.

Set
$$ H_t(u)=\tanh(a_tu/2),\qquad g(u)=\sum_tw_tH_t(u). $$
The parameter equation implies that
$$ u_* = |\log x| $$
is the unique nonnegative solution of $g(u_*)=\rho$. The limit formula becomes
$$ \beta_t= \frac{\mu_tH_t(u_*)}{D(u_*)}, \qquad D(u)=\sum_s\mu_sH_s(u). $$

3.1. Bounds for inversion and normalization

For $u\ge0$,
$$ g'(u) = \sum_tw_t\frac{a_t}{2}(1-H_t(u)^2) \ge \frac{1-g(u)}2, $$
because $a_t\ge1$ and $1-H_t(u)^2\ge1-H_t(u)$.

Also,
$$ g(u)\le mu/2, \qquad g(u)\ge\tanh(u/2)\ge1-2e^{-u}. $$
Therefore,
$$ \frac{2\rho}{m}\le u_* \le \log\frac{2}{1-\rho}. $$

Using the common denominator $E$ from Section 2,
$$ c\ge1/E, \qquad 1-\rho=\frac{2\min(q,A-q)}A\ge\frac{2}{Em}. $$
Thus both the lower bounds and the upper search bound have polynomial bit length in $L$. An integer $B=O(L)$ can be chosen with $g(B)\ge\rho$.

At the solution,
$$ \rho = \frac1A\sum_t\mu_ta_tH_t(u_*) \le \frac mA D(u_*), $$
so
$$ D(u_*)\ge c/m. $$

Each $H_t$ is increasing and concave on $[0,\infty)$, with $H_t(0)=0$. Hence, for $u\ge u_*/2$,
$$ D(u)\ge D(u_*)/2\ge c/(2m). $$

Writing $n_t(u)=\mu_tH_t(u)$, we have
$$ 0\le n_t(u)\le D(u),\qquad 0\le n_t'(u)\le m/2,\qquad 0\le D'(u)\le m/2. $$
Consequently, throughout $u\ge u_*/2$,
$$ \left|\frac{d}{du}\frac{n_t(u)}{D(u)}\right| \le \frac m{D(u)} \le \frac{2m^2}{c}. $$

3.2. Certified approximate bisection

Choose the positive rational tolerances
$$ \delta= \min\left\{ \frac14,\, \frac{\rho}{2m},\, \frac{c\,2^{-k}}{16m^2} \right\}, \qquad \zeta=\frac{(1-\rho)\delta}{16}. $$
Their bit lengths are $O(L+k)$.

Bisect $[0,B]$, evaluating $g(u)$ to absolute error at most $\zeta$. If the approximate value exceeds $\rho+2\zeta$ or is below $\rho-2\zeta$, its side of $\rho$ is certain, and the corresponding half-interval can be discarded.

If neither comparison holds, then
$$ |g(u)-\rho|\le3\zeta. $$
Throughout the interval between $u$ and $u_*$,
$$ g\le\rho+3\zeta\le(1+\rho)/2. $$
The derivative bound therefore gives
$$ |u-u_*| \le \frac{12\zeta}{1-\rho} = \frac{3\delta}{4}. $$
One can then stop. Otherwise, stop once the maintained interval has width at most $2\delta$, and use its midpoint.

This needs $O(\log(B/\delta))=O(L+k)$ iterations and returns a rational $v$ with
$$ |v-u_*|\le\delta. $$
Since $\delta\le\rho/(2m)\le u_*/4$, the interval between $v$ and $u_*$ lies above $u_*/2$. Thus
$$ \left| \frac{\mu_tH_t(v)}{D(v)}-\beta_t \right| \le \frac{2m^2}{c}\delta \le2^{-k}/8. $$

Now approximate every $H_t(v)$ to absolute error at most
$$ \eta=\frac{c\,2^{-k}}{64m}. $$
The errors in the numerator and denominator of the resulting quotient are each at most $\eta$. Since $D(v)\ge c/(2m)$, the approximate denominator is positive, and elementary quotient estimation gives an additional error at most
$$ \frac{8m\eta}{c}=2^{-k}/8. $$
Final rational rounding to error at most $2^{-k}/4$ keeps the total below $2^{-k}$.

3.3. Polynomial-time evaluation of the hyperbolic tangents

It remains to justify the evaluations without a dependence polynomial in the numerical value of $m$.

For a nonnegative rational $z$,
$$ \tanh(z/2)=\frac{1-e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}. $$
To obtain error at most $\varepsilon$, choose an integer
$$ h=\left\lceil\log_2(4/\varepsilon)\right\rceil. $$
If $z\ge h+2$, return $1$. Its error is at most
$$ 2e^{-z}\le2^{1-z}\le2^{-h-1}<\varepsilon. $$

Otherwise, $z=O(h)$. Evaluate the Taylor polynomial of $e^{-z}$ through degree
$$ n=\left\lceil16(h+z+1)\right\rceil. $$
Taylor's remainder is bounded by
$$ \frac{z^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \le \left(\frac{ez}{n+1}\right)^{n+1} \le (3/16)^{n+1} < 2^{-h}. $$
Clamp the rational approximation to $[0,1]$, which cannot increase its error. The map
$$ r\longmapsto\frac{1-r}{1+r} $$
has derivative of absolute value at most $2$ on that interval, so the resulting hyperbolic-tangent error is below $\varepsilon$.

The Taylor sum can be evaluated exactly using rational arithmetic with polynomial-length operands: a common denominator divides the denominator of $z$ raised to the power $n$, multiplied by $n!$. All precisions used above have $h=O(L+k)$, and all rational arguments have $O(L+k)$ bits. Thus each evaluation has polynomial bit complexity in $L+k$, regardless of the numerical magnitude of a binary-encoded weight.

Together with the bisection bound, this proves the theorem.

4. Exponential dense-output size with two types

Take an odd integer $m>3$ not divisible by $3$, and specify
$$ (\mu_1,a_1)=(3/4,1),\qquad (\mu_2,a_2)=(1/4,m),\qquad q=3/4. $$
Then
$$ A=(m+3)/4>q, \qquad q\ne A/2. $$

The parameter equation is
$$ \frac{3x}{1+x}+\frac{mx^m}{1+x^m}=3, $$
which rearranges to
$$ mx^m(1+x)=3(1+x^m). $$
For odd $m$,
$$ 1+X^m=(1+X)\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^jX^j. $$
Since $x>0$, division by $1+x$ is legitimate, and $x$ is a root of
$$ G_m(X)=mX^m-3\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^jX^j. $$

Eisenstein's criterion at $3$ applies: the leading coefficient is not divisible by $3$, every lower coefficient is divisible by $3$, and the constant coefficient is $-3$, not divisible by $9$. Hence
$$ [\mathbb Q(x):\mathbb Q]=m. $$

We must also check that normalization preserves this degree. Put
$$ h=\frac{x-1}{x+1}, \qquad h_m=\frac{x^m-1}{x^m+1}, $$
and let $b$ be the first limit coordinate. The parameter and limit equations give
$$ 3h+mh_m=3-m, \qquad b=\frac{3h}{3h+h_m} = \frac{3mh}{(3m-3)h+3-m}. $$
Solving for $h$,
$$ h=\frac{b(3-m)}{3m-(3m-3)b}. $$
Because $0<b<1$, this denominator is positive. Also,
$$ x=\frac{1+h}{1-h}, $$
whose denominator is nonzero for positive finite $x$. Therefore,
$$ \mathbb Q(b)=\mathbb Q(x), \qquad [\mathbb Q(b):\mathbb Q]=m. $$

Choose
$$ m=2^{2r}+1,\qquad r\ge1. $$
These integers satisfy the required parity and congruence conditions. The input length is $L=\Theta(r)$, while the first output coordinate has degree $m$.

Every nonzero integer polynomial vanishing at that coordinate must have degree at least $m$. A dense coefficient-list encoding therefore requires at least $m+1$ coefficient positions. Exact output has worst-case length
$$ 2^{\Omega(L)}, $$
already for two supported types.

This argument depends on dense encoding. Algebraic degree alone would not establish the same lower bound for sparse or circuit representations.

5. Established complexity bounds and the remaining question

The proved bounds are:

| Task | Bit complexity |
|---|---:|
| Decide convergence on valid inputs | $L^{O(1)}$ |
| Compute endpoint or midpoint limits exactly | $L^{O(1)}$ |
| Compute general limits exactly in polynomial-and-interval form | $(\tau m+L)^{O(1)}$ |
| Approximate all limits to additive error $2^{-k}$ | $(\tau+L+k)^{O(1)}$ |

For dense exact output, the worst-case total-time upper bound is $2^{O(L)}$, and some inputs require $2^{\Omega(L)}$ output bits.

What remains open in this write-up is an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness result for exact-limit computation under a specified standard polynomial-time reduction. Universal convergence settles the decision component, and exponential output length rules out polynomial total-time exact computation under dense encoding, but neither statement supplies that hardness reduction.
