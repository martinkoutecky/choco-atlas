Partial solution: the exact one-win threshold for every $j$

The one-win case can be resolved for every number of districts, extending the even-$j$ result in the notes:

$$ \boxed{\theta_j(1)=\frac1j\qquad\text{for every }j\ge2.} $$

The proof gives explicit strategies on both sides of this threshold. More precisely, writing $T_{j,K}(1)$ for the minimum winning brick inventory,

$$ T_{j,K}(1)=K\quad\text{if }j\text{ is even}, $$

and

$$ K-1\le T_{j,K}(1)\le K\quad\text{if }j\text{ is odd}. $$

Thus deciding $\beta\ge\theta_j(1)$ is polynomial-time solvable, including when $j$ is supplied as input.

The general questions for $q\ge2$ remain unresolved here. Below, I also prove inventory monotonicity, explain why convergence would imply all the requested strict-threshold sequence guarantees, and give the general bounds and finite computation procedure.

1. Definitions and finite-game determinacy

Fix admissible integers $j,q$ and an odd capacity

$$ K=2m+1. $$

Put

$$ h=m+1,\qquad N=jK. $$

A district is won by $B$ exactly when its terminal brick count is at least $h$. Define

$$ T_{j,K}(q)=\min\{n:B\text{ can force at least }q\text{ district wins}\}. $$

This minimum exists because $n=N$ makes every district a brick district. By definition,

$$ \theta_{j,K}(q)=\frac{T_{j,K}(q)}{N}. $$

Every play lasts exactly $N$ moves: each move decreases both remaining inventory and vacant capacity by one. The game tree is finite. At a terminal node, either $B$ has at least $q$ wins or does not. Backward induction, using maximization on $B$'s turns and minimization on $A$'s turns, proves that exactly one player can force its corresponding outcome.

These strategies need not depend only on the current counts; finite-game determinacy holds even under the permitted dependence on the entire history.

2. Monotonicity in the brick inventory

Proposition

For fixed $j,K,q$, increasing $n$ cannot destroy a forcing strategy for $B$. Consequently,

$$ B\text{ can force at least }q\text{ wins} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\ge T_{j,K}(q). $$

Proof

Suppose $B$ has a forcing strategy with $n<N$ bricks. Consider an actual game with $n+1$ bricks and a virtual game with $n$ bricks. In the virtual game, $B$ follows the given strategy.

Until an exceptional placement described below occurs, the boards coincide and the remaining inventories satisfy

$$ r_{\mathrm{actual}}=r_{\mathrm{virtual}}+1,\qquad u_{\mathrm{actual}}=u_{\mathrm{virtual}}-1, $$

where $r$ counts bricks and $u$ counts apples.

On an actual move by $A$, copy the district and type virtually whenever possible. An actual apple is always available virtually. An actual brick can fail to be available virtually only when the virtual brick inventory is zero. In that case, copy the move as a virtual apple in the same district. This is legal: the virtual apple inventory exceeds the actual apple inventory by one.

On a virtual move prescribed for $B$, copy the district and type actually whenever possible. A virtual brick is always available actually. A virtual apple can fail to be available actually only when the actual apple inventory is zero. In that case, place an actual brick in the same district. This is legal because the actual brick inventory exceeds the virtual brick inventory by one.

Either exceptional placement makes the remaining inventories identical. The corresponding district occupancies remain identical, and the actual board has one extra brick and one fewer apple in one district. All later moves can therefore be copied with identical type and district.

The resulting virtual play is legal and follows $B$'s forcing strategy. At termination, every actual district has at least as many bricks as its virtual counterpart. Hence every virtual win is an actual win.

This proves monotonicity from $n$ to $n+1$, and iteration proves the proposition. Determinacy supplies an $A$ strategy whenever $n<T_{j,K}(q)$. $\square$

3. A slot-pairing strategy for $A$

The new ingredient is to pair individual slots across districts. For odd $j$, one unpaired slot is allowed to move during play.

Slots are bookkeeping devices. A legal move specifies a district, not a slot within it. Thus a deterministic strategy can record a voter placed by $B$ in any vacant artificial slot of the selected district.

3.1. Constructing the initial matching

We first prove an elementary matching fact.

Lemma. Suppose groups of slots have even total size $2s$, and every group has size at most $s$. Then the slots can be partitioned into pairs whose endpoints belong to different groups.

Proof. If $s=0$, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, select one slot from each of the two largest groups and pair them. There are at least two nonempty groups, since one group alone cannot contain $2s$ slots while having size at most $s$.

After the selection, the total is $2(s-1)$. Each selected group has size at most $s-1$. No unselected group can have size $s$: otherwise the two selected groups also had size at least $s$, giving total size at least $3s>2s$. Thus every remaining group has size at most $s-1$.

Induction completes the construction. $\square$

Apply the lemma with districts as groups.

If $N$ is even, each group has size $K\le N/2$, so all slots can be matched across distinct districts.

If $N$ is odd, then $j$ is odd and $j\ge3$. Remove one slot and designate it as unpaired. The remaining total is $N-1$, and every remaining district group has size at most $K$. Moreover,

$$ 2K\le jK-1=N-1, $$

because $(j-2)K\ge1$. The lemma therefore matches all remaining slots across distinct districts.

3.2. Maintaining the vacant-slot matching

Immediately before each turn by $B$, maintain the following invariant:

  • Every vacant slot belongs to a pair with a vacant slot in a different district.
  • If $N$ is odd, there is additionally exactly one unpaired vacant slot.

If $B$ plays in a paired slot $u$, let $v$ be its mate. Player $A$ responds in $v$. Removing this pair preserves the invariant.

Suppose instead that $B$ plays in the unpaired slot $u$.

If $u$ was the only vacant slot, the game has ended and there is no response.

Otherwise, before this move there were at least three vacant slots, so at least one vacant pair existed. Its endpoints were in different districts; consequently, at least one vacant paired slot $v$ lies in a district different from that of $u$. Player $A$ responds in such a slot $v$. If $w$ was the mate of $v$, remove the pair $\{v,w\}$ and designate $w$ as the new unpaired slot.

The invariant is again restored. In particular, every completed round places its two voters in distinct districts.

For the response type, $A$ uses the type just placed by $B$ if any voter of that type remains. Otherwise, $A$ uses the other type. A response always has an available voter because at least one slot remains vacant.

There can be at most one completed round whose two voters have different types. Such a round occurs only when $B$ exhausts a type, after which all remaining voters have the other type.

All choices can be made deterministic by fixing orders on districts and slots.

3.3. Bounding every district's brick count

Let $r$ be the number of completed rounds containing two bricks. Let $e$ be the number of bricks in rounds containing different types. Then

$$ e\in\{0,1\}. $$

When $N$ is odd, let $z\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the final unpaired voter is a brick. When $N$ is even, put $z=0$.

Every completed round uses distinct districts. Thus a given district receives at most one brick from each two-brick round, at most $e$ bricks from the exceptional round, and at most $z$ bricks from the final voter. Its terminal brick count $b_i$ satisfies

$$ b_i\le r+e+z. $$

The total brick inventory is

$$ n=2r+e+z. $$

For even $N$, this gives

$$ \boxed{b_i\le\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil.} $$

For odd $N$, write $s=e+z$, so $s\in\{0,1,2\}$. Then

$$ r+s =\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor +\left\lceil\frac s2\right\rceil \le\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor+1. $$

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{b_i\le\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor+1.} $$

These bounds hold simultaneously for every district and against every legal strategy of $B$.

4. Exact limiting threshold for one win

4.1. A forcing strategy for $B$

For every $j\ge2$, the inventory $n\ge K$ suffices to force one win.

Fix a district. On each of $B$'s first $h$ turns, place a brick there, stopping this prescription if the district has already reached $h$ bricks.

Immediately before the $r$-th turn by $B$, for $r\le h$, exactly $2r-2$ moves have occurred. Hence the selected district has occupancy at most

$$ 2r-2\le2h-2=K-1, $$

and is nonfull. At most $2r-2$ bricks have been used globally, so the remaining brick inventory is at least

$$ n-(2r-2)\ge K-(K-1)=1. $$

Thus every prescribed move is legal. If the district was not won earlier, these $h$ placements give it at least $h$ bricks. Later placements cannot undo a win.

Consequently,

$$ T_{j,K}(1)\le K. $$

4.2. Preventing strategies and finite bounds

If $j$ is even, then $N$ is even. For $n<K=2m+1$, the slot-pairing strategy gives

$$ b_i\le\left\lceil\frac n2\right\rceil\le m $$

in every district. Hence $A$ prevents every win. Combining this with the forcing strategy proves

$$ \boxed{T_{j,K}(1)=K\qquad\text{for even }j.} $$

If $j$ is odd and $K\ge3$, then for $n\le K-2=2m-1$ the moving-unpaired-slot strategy gives

$$ b_i\le\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor+1\le m. $$

Again, $A$ prevents every win. Therefore,

$$ K-1\le T_{j,K}(1)\le K. $$

For $K=1$, the same displayed bounds hold because $T_{j,1}(1)=1$: zero bricks cannot win a district, and one brick lets $B$ win immediately.

4.3. Convergence

For every $j\ge2$ and every odd $K$,

$$ \frac{K-1}{jK}\le\theta_{j,K}(1)\le\frac1j. $$

Both outer expressions tend to $1/j$. Therefore the odd-capacity limit exists and

$$ \boxed{\theta_j(1)=\frac1j.} $$

For even $j$, the finite thresholds already equal this limit. For odd $j$, their distance from it is at most $1/(jK)$.

4.4. Guarantees along every inventory sequence

Let $n_K$ be any sequence of legal inventories with

$$ \frac{n_K}{jK}\longrightarrow\beta. $$

If $\beta>1/j$, then $n_K/K\to j\beta>1$. Eventually $n_K\ge K$, so the fixed-district strategy forces a win.

If $\beta<1/j$, choose $\delta>0$ with $j\beta<1-\delta$. Eventually,

$$ n_K\le(1-\delta)K. $$

For sufficiently large $K$, this implies $n_K\le K-2$. The slot-pairing strategy then prevents every win, for either parity of $j$.

This proves both requested sequence guarantees for $q=1$.

5. Running times and comparison for the one-win case

The slot matching and strategy admit a direct implementation.

Store the vacant slots in a doubly linked list for each district, together with mate pointers and the possible unpaired slot. Construct the initial matching by repeatedly scanning the $j$ district sizes and pairing slots from the two largest groups.

During play, paired responses and list updates take constant many operations. When $B$ uses the unpaired slot, scanning the districts finds a vacant slot in a different district in at most $O(j)$ operations.

Since there are $N=jK$ placements, construction and complete play require

$$ O(jN)=O\bigl(j^2(m+1)\bigr) $$

operations on integers and pointers of $O(\log(N+1))$ bits. Storage is

$$ O\bigl(N\log(N+1)\bigr) $$

bits. Thus the strategy is polynomial-time in the requested finite-game parameters. Its guarantee does not rely on exhaustive game-tree computation.

For the comparison problem with $q=1$, write the input rational as

$$ \beta=\frac uv,\qquad v>0. $$

The exact test is

$$ \beta\ge\theta_j(1) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ju\ge v. $$

Multiplication and comparison take polynomial time in $L+\log(j+1)$. Under the support representation, $\tau=1$ when $\beta=0$ and $\tau=2$ otherwise, because apples always have positive mass.

For $j=2$, the promise $\beta<1/2$ makes every comparison answer negative. The result remains nontrivial for $j\ge3$.

6. What follows for general $q$

6.1. Convergence would imply the sequence guarantees

Suppose, for a fixed admissible pair $j,q$, that

$$ \theta_{j,K}(q)\longrightarrow c $$

along odd capacities.

If $n_K/(jK)\to\beta>c$, set $\varepsilon=(\beta-c)/3$. Eventually,

$$ \frac{n_K}{jK}>\beta-\varepsilon >c+\varepsilon>\theta_{j,K}(q). $$

Thus $n_K>T_{j,K}(q)$, and inventory monotonicity gives a forcing strategy for $B$.

If $\beta<c$, use $\varepsilon=(c-\beta)/3$. Eventually,

$$ \frac{n_K}{jK}<\beta+\varepsilon <c-\varepsilon<\theta_{j,K}(q). $$

Hence $n_K<T_{j,K}(q)$, and determinacy gives a preventing strategy for $A$.

Thus, once convergence is proved, the requested strict-threshold sequence guarantees follow automatically. This argument supplies existence of finite strategies, without asserting efficient construction for variable $j$.

6.2. General bounds from the supplied discrete results

Use as premises the two discrete strategy guarantees supplied in the task, also stated in [Liang and Amenta, Theorems 8 and 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.07440v2):

$$ n\ge2q(m+1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\text{ can force }q\text{ wins}, $$

and

$$ n<\frac{2jq(m+1)}{j+q}-1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A\text{ can prevent }q\text{ wins}. $$

They imply

$$ \left\lceil\frac{jq(K+1)}{j+q}-1\right\rceil \le T_{j,K}(q)\le q(K+1). $$

After division by $jK$,

$$ \frac{q}{j+q} +\frac1K\left(\frac{q}{j+q}-\frac1j\right) \le\theta_{j,K}(q) \le\frac qj+\frac{q}{jK}. $$

Consequently,

$$ \boxed{ \frac{q}{j+q} \le\liminf_{\substack{K\to\infty\\K\text{ odd}}}\theta_{j,K}(q) \le\limsup_{\substack{K\to\infty\\K\text{ odd}}}\theta_{j,K}(q) \le\frac qj. } $$

The same premises give unconditional sequence guarantees when $\beta<q/(j+q)$ or $\beta>q/j$, respectively, because the corresponding normalized sufficient thresholds converge to those endpoints.

For $q\ge2$, these bounds leave a gap of width

$$ \frac qj-\frac{q}{j+q} =\frac{q^2}{j(j+q)}. $$

Neither convergence nor an exact value follows from them.

7. Exact finite computation and strategy extraction

For a fixed inventory $n$, represent a state by the labeled district counts

$$ \mathbf{s}=((a_1,b_1),\ldots,(a_j,b_j)), \qquad a_i+b_i\le K. $$

The remaining inventories are

$$ r=n-\sum_i b_i,\qquad u=N-n-\sum_i a_i. $$

Retain only states with nonnegative remaining inventories. If

$$ t=\sum_i(a_i+b_i), $$

then $B$ moves when $t$ is even and $A$ moves when $t$ is odd.

At a terminal state, define

$$ W(\mathbf{s})= \begin{cases} 1,&|\{i:b_i\ge h\}|\ge q,\\ 0,&|\{i:b_i\ge h\}|<q. \end{cases} $$

At a nonterminal state, take the maximum successor value on $B$'s turn and the minimum on $A$'s turn. Successors are precisely placements of an available type in a nonfull district.

Every transition increases $t$. Backward induction therefore proves that this recurrence computes the forcing outcome exactly. Recording an optimal successor supplies a deterministic strategy. The state counts suffice because they determine legal moves, inventories, turn, and terminal outcome.

One district has

$$ H=\frac{(K+1)(K+2)}2 $$

possible count pairs. Thus there are at most $H^j$ labeled states and at most $2j$ successors per state. Inventory monotonicity allows binary search for $T_{j,K}(q)$ using $O(\log(N+1))$ outcome evaluations.

A conservative bit-complexity bound is

$$ H^j\operatorname{poly}\bigl(j,q,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\bigr). $$

For fixed $j$, this is polynomial in $m$ and the remaining parameters. Its exponential dependence on variable $j$, and its dependence on finite $K$, prevent it from classifying the general continuous comparison problem.

8. Proven conclusions and remaining questions

The moving-unpaired-slot strategy resolves the complete one-win family:

$$ \theta_j(1)=\frac1j $$

for every $j\ge2$, with explicit polynomial-time finite strategies certifying both strict sides along every convergent inventory sequence. Comparison with this threshold is in $\mathrm P$.

For $q\ge2$, the present argument does not compare different capacities sufficiently to prove convergence. The exact limiting threshold and the complexity of its comparison problem therefore remain open in this solution.
