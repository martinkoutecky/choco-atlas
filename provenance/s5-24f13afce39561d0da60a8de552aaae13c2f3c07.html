The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant-type and position-type sets \(I,J\), rational applicant masses \(\mu_i\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), rational position masses \(\nu_j\), mutual acceptability \(E\subseteq I\times J\), and strict preference orders over types, maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) over rational allocations \(x_{ij}\ge 0\). Require \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\mu_i\), \(\ell_j\nu_j\le\sum_ix_{ij}\le u_j\nu_j\), and no positive-mass envy: there are no \(i,k,j,r\), with \(r\in J\cup\{\bot\}\), such that \(x_{ir}>0\), \(x_{kj}>0\), \(j\succ_i r\), and \(i\succ_j k\). For the ONE-ONE-LQ case, \((\ell_j,u_j)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}\); report the maximum or that no feasible envy-free allocation exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be finite applicant-type and position-type supports, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), where \(\sum_{i\in I}\mu_i=1\); position masses need not sum to one.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; write \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Mutual acceptability is \(E\subseteq I\times J\). Each type strictly ranks its acceptable opposite-side types; every acceptable position is preferred to \(\bot\). Individuals of the same type have identical preferences and equal priority.
For each \(j\), let \((\ell_j,u_j)\in\{(0,1),(1,1)\}\), and define \(q=|\{j\in J:\ell_j=1\}|\).
An allocation has \(x_{ij}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\mu_i\), and \(\ell_j\nu_j\le\sum_ix_{ij}\le u_j\nu_j\). Define unmatched mass by \(x_{i\bot}=\mu_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\). Assume these capacity and quota constraints admit an allocation.
An allocation is envy-free if there are no \(i,k\in I\), \(j\in J\), and \(r\in J\cup\{\bot\}\) with \((i,j)\in E\), \(x_{ir}>0\), \(x_{kj}>0\), \(j\succ_i r\), and \(i\succ_j k\).
The problem is to compute the maximum \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) among feasible envy-free allocations, or report that none exists. Measure running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(q\).

Is this continuous optimization problem in \(\mathrm P\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, is it \(\mathrm{FPT}\) parameterized by \(q\)?

Discrete maximum-size feasible envy-free matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with unit upper quotas ([Krishnaa et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.07159v3)); it is \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by the number \(q\) of positions with positive lower quotas ([Limaye](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09917)).
No result settling this exact mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined unmatched applicant mass as the applicant mass minus its total assigned mass, and placed unmatched status below every acceptable position.
  • Restricted preference orders to mutually acceptable types, explicitly required acceptability for an envy comparison, and set allocations outside the acceptability relation to zero.
  • Required positive masses for listed support types; retained applicant normalization and imposed no normalization on position masses.
  • Carried over the assumption that an allocation satisfying capacities and lower quotas exists, without promising an envy-free allocation.
  • Made identical preferences and equal priority within each type explicit; no tie-breaking distinguishes individuals within a type.
  • Selected the anchor's parameter q, extended as the number of position support types with positive lower quotas, rather than their total mass.
  • Defined m as the number of acceptable type pairs, tau as the combined support size, and L as the total input bit length.

Result

The continuous optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Its threshold decision version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Parameterized by \(q\), it is \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard, so it admits no \(\mathrm{FPT}\) algorithm unless \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[1]\).

These hardness results hold even when every mass is dyadic. The crucial point is that fractional allocations can be rounded while preserving envy-freeness if unmatched mass is included in the support being preserved.

1. Envy-freeness is preserved by reducing the extended support

Write \(\widehat E=E\cup\{(i,\bot):i\in I\}\), and define the extended support of an allocation by

\(\operatorname{supp}^{+}(x)=\{(i,r)\in\widehat E:x_{ir}>0\}\).

Lemma 1. Suppose \(x\) is envy-free and \(y\) satisfies the capacity and quota constraints. If \(\operatorname{supp}^{+}(y)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}^{+}(x)\), then \(y\) is envy-free.

Proof. If \(y\) were not envy-free, there would be \(i,k,j,r\) satisfying

\((i,j)\in E,\quad y_{ir}>0,\quad y_{kj}>0,\quad j\succ_i r,\quad i\succ_j k\).

Support containment implies \(x_{ir}>0\) and \(x_{kj}>0\). The same preferences would therefore witness envy in \(x\), a contradiction. \(\square\)

The inclusion of \((i,\bot)\) matters: preserving only acceptable-edge support could turn a fully assigned applicant type into a partially unmatched type, creating envy.

2. A support-preserving rounding lemma

Lemma 2. Suppose there is a positive rational \(c\) such that every \(\mu_i/c\) and \(\nu_j/c\) is an integer. For every feasible envy-free allocation \(x\), there is a feasible envy-free allocation \(y\) such that:

  • every \(y_{ir}/c\) is an integer;
  • \(\operatorname{supp}^{+}(y)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}^{+}(x)\);
  • \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}y_{ij}\geq\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\).

Proof. Set \(a_i=\mu_i/c\), \(b_j=\nu_j/c\), and \(A=\sum_i a_i=1/c\). These are integers.

Construct a directed network with applicant nodes \(i\), position nodes \(j\), an unmatched node \(d\), and a sink \(t\). Give applicant \(i\) supply \(a_i\), give \(t\) demand \(A\), and give every other node zero net supply. Include these arcs:

  • \(i\to j\), with bounds \([0,a_i]\), exactly when \(x_{ij}>0\);
  • \(i\to d\), with bounds \([0,a_i]\), exactly when \(x_{i\bot}>0\);
  • \(j\to t\), with bounds \([\ell_jb_j,b_j]\);
  • \(d\to t\), with bounds \([0,A]\).

The scaled allocation \(x/c\), together with its column sums, gives a feasible flow. Maximize the sum of flows on the applicant-to-position arcs.

We verify integrality directly. All arc bounds and node supplies are integers. Suppose a feasible-flow vertex had a nonintegral arc. Form the undirected graph consisting of its nonintegral arcs. No node can have degree exactly one in this graph: its balance equation would then equate an integer to an integer plus or minus one noninteger. Consequently, this finite graph contains a cycle.

Perturb the flow around that cycle, adding a small amount on arcs agreeing with a chosen traversal and subtracting it on oppositely directed arcs. This preserves every node balance. Since every arc on the cycle has nonintegral flow and integral bounds, it lies strictly between its bounds. Thus sufficiently small perturbations in both directions remain feasible. The original flow is their midpoint, contradicting that it is a vertex.

Hence every feasible-flow vertex is integral. The flow polytope is nonempty and bounded, so the linear objective has an optimal vertex; choose such an integral flow \(f\).

Define \(y_{ij}=cf(i,j)\) and \(y_{i\bot}=cf(i,d)\), taking missing arcs to have zero flow. Conservation and the arc bounds give precisely the required allocation constraints. Optimality gives an objective at least that of \(x\). Finally, all positive coordinates of \(y\) belong to the extended support of \(x\), so Lemma 1 proves envy-freeness. \(\square\)

3. Embedding a discrete instance using dyadic masses

Consider any discrete instance with \(n\geq1\) applicants, unit position upper quotas, position lower quotas in \(\{0,1\}\), and strict preferences.

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Construct a mass instance as follows:

  • each original applicant has mass \(1/N\);
  • each position has mass \(1/N\), with its original lower-quota indicator;
  • if \(N>n\), add one isolated applicant type of mass \((N-n)/N\).

Keep all original acceptable pairs and rankings. The isolated type has no acceptable positions.

All masses are positive dyadic rationals, applicant masses sum to one, and the number \(q\) of required positions is unchanged.

A discrete feasible envy-free matching \(M\) induces a mass allocation of value \(|M|/N\), by assigning mass \(1/N\) on every matching edge.

Conversely, apply Lemma 2 to any feasible envy-free mass allocation, with \(c=1/N\). Each original applicant has scaled supply one, and each position has scaled capacity one. Therefore the resulting integral flow assigns each original applicant either to exactly one position or to the unmatched node. Required positions receive exactly one applicant. The isolated type remains unmatched.

The resulting edges constitute a discrete feasible envy-free matching. Its envy-freeness follows because a discrete envy witness would also be an envy witness in the rounded allocation.

Consequently, existence is equivalent in the two instances, and, whenever feasible envy-free solutions exist,

\(\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}/N\).

In particular, for every integer threshold \(K\),

\(\exists x\text{ feasible and envy-free with }\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\geq K/N\)

if and only if the discrete instance has a feasible envy-free matching of size at least \(K\).

This conversion introduces at most one applicant type, leaves \(m\) unchanged, and adds only \(O(\tau\log n)\) bits for masses. It neither expands the binary masses into individuals nor changes \(q\).

4. Transferring classical and parameterized hardness

The needed discrete results have exactly the restrictions used above:

  • Maximum-size feasible envy-free matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard with unit upper quotas and lower quotas in \(\{0,1\}\). See Theorem 4(I)(b) of [Krishnaa et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.07159v3).
  • The corresponding threshold problem is \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by the number \(q\) of required positions, even with those same quota restrictions. See Theorem 1 and Lemma 9 of [Limaye](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09917).

The ordinary-feasibility promise also holds in these reductions. Their Independent Set constructions have \(k\) required positions, each accepting all \(n\) vertex applicants. Restricting to \(k\leq n\), assign distinct vertex applicants to those positions and leave everyone else unmatched. This satisfies the capacity and quota constraints regardless of whether the Independent Set instance is a yes-instance. These constructions appear in [Krishnaa et al., Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.07159v3) and [Limaye, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09917).

Applying Section 3 therefore gives polynomial-time reductions into the promised mass problem. The exact correspondence of thresholds proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Because \(q\) is preserved, it also proves \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hardness parameterized by \(q\).

Thus an algorithm with running time \(f(q)(m+\tau+L)^C\), for a computable function \(f\) and a constant \(C\) independent of \(q\), would imply \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[1]\).

5. Exact attainment, certificates, and an upper bound

For completeness, the rational formulation does not introduce an issue with unattained optima or excessively long certificates.

Write all masses as fractions with binary-encoded numerators and denominators. Let \(D\) be the product of their denominators. Then \(\log D=O(L)\), and every \(D\mu_i\) and \(D\nu_j\) is integral.

For a subset \(S\subseteq\widehat E\), consider the allocation polytope obtained by forcing all coordinates outside \(S\) to zero. Discard \(S\) if it contains both \((i,r)\) and \((k,j)\) for any preference configuration satisfying

\((i,j)\in E,\quad j\succ_i r,\quad i\succ_j k\).

Every allocation in a remaining polytope is envy-free. Conversely, the extended support of every envy-free allocation is a remaining subset. Thus the envy-free feasible set is a finite union of these closed, bounded polytopes.

Each nonempty polytope has an objective maximizer. Applying the network argument of Lemma 2 with \(c=1/D\) shows that it has a maximizer whose coordinates are integer multiples of \(1/D\). Taking the best polytope proves that, whenever feasible envy-free allocations exist, a rational optimum is attained.

Every allocation coordinate is at most one, so its numerator over denominator \(D\) needs only \(O(L)\) bits. A threshold yes-instance therefore has a certificate of polynomial length. Capacity constraints, quotas, objective value, and all possible envy witnesses can be checked using polynomially many exact rational operations. The threshold problem, including the threshold in its input length, belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\); combined with the reduction above, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Enumerating the subsets \(S\) and solving the associated rational flow problems also gives an exact algorithm running in

\(2^{m+|I|}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

It returns an optimal allocation or reports nonexistence. The hardness in \(q\) rules out replacing this by an \(\mathrm{FPT}\) running time in \(q\), subject to the standard parameterized-complexity assumption.
