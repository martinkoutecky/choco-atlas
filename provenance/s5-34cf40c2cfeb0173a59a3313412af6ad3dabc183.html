The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(N\) candidates and \(L\) equal-sized client populations, each described by a finite-support distribution \(\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\) over complete rankings with binary-rational masses summing to one, compute the Borda consensus permutation \(\pi\) that orders candidates by increasing pooled mean position \(B(a)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{\sigma}\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\sigma(a)\). Under the promise that these means have strict adjacent gaps in the order of a common consensus ranking \(\sigma_0\), does this computation recover \(\sigma_0\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\geq2\) candidates and \(k\geq2\) equal-sized client populations be given. Rankings are complete and strict; \(\sigma(a)\) denotes candidate \(a\)'s position, with position \(1\) highest.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses. Here types are client–ranking pairs \((\ell,\sigma)\), with mass \(\mu_{\ell,\sigma}/k\); each client's masses satisfy \(\sum_\sigma\mu_{\ell,\sigma}=1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the total number of supported client–ranking pairs, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Define \(B(a)=\frac1k\sum_{\ell=1}^k\sum_\sigma\mu_{\ell,\sigma}\sigma(a)\). The Borda consensus is the position map \(\pi\) satisfying \(\pi(a)<\pi(b)\) exactly when \(B(a)<B(b)\).
An unknown ranking \(\sigma_0\) is promised to satisfy \(B(\sigma_0^{-1}(j+1))>B(\sigma_0^{-1}(j))\) for every \(1\leq j<m\). No minimum gap is supplied; the promise excludes ties. The distributions need not be Mallows distributions.

Is computing this promised Borda consensus polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is it NP-hard, and does its output always equal \(\sigma_0\)?

Discrete: Federated quantized Borda recovers a common Mallows centroid with probability at least \(1-\delta\), for fixed \(0<\phi<1\), using per-client sample size \(\max\{C_1(\phi),C_2(\phi)k^{-1}\log(m/\delta)\}\) and communication scaling as \(km\log m\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00848).
This finite-sample guarantee concerns quantized local averages, whereas the stated mass problem supplies exact rational distributions and uses pooled mean positions.

Result

The promised Borda consensus is polynomial-time computable, and its output always equals \(\sigma_0\). Both conclusions hold for arbitrary distributions satisfying the stated promise; no Mallows assumption is needed.

Exact computation and bit complexity

Index the supported client–ranking pairs by \(i\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Write their conditional masses as \(\mu_i=p_i/q_i\), where \(p_i,q_i\) are positive integers represented in binary, and denote their rankings by \(\sigma_i\). Thus
\[ B(a)=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\frac{p_i}{q_i}\sigma_i(a). \]

Define the positive integer
\[ Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i \]
and, for each candidate \(a\), compute
\[ S(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\frac{Q}{q_i}\sigma_i(a). \]
Every division \(Q/q_i\) is exact, and
\[ B(a)=\frac{S(a)}{kQ}. \]
Consequently, \(B(a)<B(b)\) if and only if \(S(a)<S(b)\). The algorithm computes these integer scores, sorts candidates by increasing score, and assigns positions according to that order.

Here is why this is polynomial-time in the binary input model. The denominator encodings are part of the input, so
\[ \log_2 Q=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\log_2 q_i\leq L. \]
Hence \(Q\) has at most \(L+1\) bits. Client normalization gives \(\sum_i\mu_i=k\). Since every position belongs to \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\), we have
\[ 0<S(a)\leq mkQ. \]
All partial score sums satisfy the same upper bound because their summands are positive. Thus the scores and arithmetic intermediates have \(O(L+\log k+\log m)\) bits.

Forming \(Q\), computing the \(\tau\) weights \(p_i(Q/q_i)\), and accumulating the scores requires \(O(\tau+m\tau)\) integer arithmetic operations. Sorting requires \(O(m\log m)\) integer comparisons. Binary integer multiplication and division take polynomial time in their operand lengths, so the entire algorithm takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,k\). It processes only the supplied support, without enumerating the possible rankings.

The absence of a supplied minimum gap causes no difficulty: comparisons are exact. Indeed, any two distinct scores satisfy
\[ |B(a)-B(b)|=\frac{|S(a)-S(b)|}{kQ}\geq\frac1{kQ}. \]
This bound may be numerically small, but its denominator has polynomial bit length.

Proof that the output equals the promised ranking

Let \(a_j=\sigma_0^{-1}(j)\) for \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). The promise states that
\[ B(a_1)<B(a_2)<\cdots<B(a_m). \]
By transitivity, \(B(a_i)<B(a_j)\) whenever \(i<j\). Since the \(a_j\) enumerate all candidates, all scores are distinct, and their unique increasing order is \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\).

The algorithm therefore assigns \(\pi(a_j)=j=\sigma_0(a_j)\) for every \(j\). Hence \(\pi=\sigma_0\).

The promise itself identifies \(\sigma_0\) as the unique order of the pooled means. The computational argument above establishes that this order can be recovered efficiently from the explicitly supplied rational support.

Relation to the finite-sample statement

The cited result concerns recovery under a Mallows model using samples and quantized client scores, with a probabilistic guarantee. ([Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00848).) Here the distributions are supplied exactly, so neither estimation nor quantization is required. The conclusion follows deterministically from exact arithmetic and the strict-order promise.

Thus the requested classification is polynomial-time solvable; an NP-hardness classification under standard polynomial-time reductions would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
