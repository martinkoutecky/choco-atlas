The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given districts \(D\), parties with named candidate sets, a fixed tie-breaking order, and for each district either an empty electorate or a finite-support rational distribution over complete rankings of the candidates with total mass \(1\), does there exist a nomination profile in which each candidate is nominated in at most one district and no party can increase its number of elected candidates by unilaterally changing its nominations? Each nonempty district elects the plurality winner under its ranking distribution and the fixed tie-break.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(D\) be a nonempty set of \(d\) districts and \(C\) a set of \(m\ge d\) named candidates, partitioned into nonempty parties. A fixed strict order on \(C\) breaks all electoral ties; party sizes are unrestricted.
Each district has either an empty electorate or a distribution of total mass \(1\) over strict complete rankings of \(C\). A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Write \(\tau_D\) for a district's support size and \(\tau=\sum_{D}\tau_D\); these count listed types, not possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A party may nominate any subset of its candidates in each district, including several candidates or none, provided each candidate is nominated in at most one district; candidates may remain unnominated. A unilateral deviation may replace the party's entire nomination strategy.
Each district with nominees elects one plurality winner using the ranking masses and the fixed tie-break. An empty electorate gives every nominee score \(0\), so the tie-break selects its winner; a district without nominees elects nobody. Each party's utility is its number of elected candidates.

Is deciding whether such a game admits a pure Nash equilibrium in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,d\), and the number of parties.

For explicitly listed finite electorates, equilibrium existence is \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete with unrestricted party sizes and \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete with any fixed bound on party size, including singleton parties ([Harrenstein and Turrini, 2022](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/162392/1/WRAP-computing-Nash-equilibria-district-based-nominations-Turrini-2022.pdf)). No result specifically treating the rational-distribution formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the discrete model's candidate partition into nonempty parties and its condition that the number of candidates is at least the number of districts; took the district set to be nonempty.
  • Interpreted complete rankings as strict total orders and the fixed tie-breaking order as a strict total order.
  • Specified that parties may nominate multiple candidates in one district, leave candidates unnominated, and replace their entire nomination strategy in a deviation.
  • Resolved empty electorates by assigning all nominees zero plurality scores and applying the fixed tie-break; districts without nominees elect nobody.
  • Defined tau as the total number of district-specific support entries and L as total input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Equilibrium existence is \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. If every party has at most \(\,k\) candidates for any fixed positive constant \(\,k\), it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, including when every party is a singleton.

Here, binary rationals mean fractions whose numerators and denominators are encoded in binary. The proof transfers the stated finite-electorate hardness results and establishes polynomial-time exact outcome computation for the rational formulation.

The boundary case \(\,m=d\) always admits an equilibrium: nominate each candidate in a different district. Every candidate is elected, so each party attains its maximum possible utility. The hardness concerns the general case permitting \(\,m>d\).

1. Strategies and exact outcome computation

Let \(\,p\) be the number of parties, let their sizes be \(\,s_1,\ldots,s_p\), and put \(\,k=\max_i s_i\).

Label the districts by \(\,1,\ldots,d\). A nomination profile is exactly a function
\(x:C\longrightarrow\{0,1,\ldots,d\}\),
where \(\,x(c)=0\) means that candidate \(\,c\) remains unnominated. This representation allows several candidates from one party in a district and ensures that each candidate is nominated in at most one district.

Consequently, there are exactly \(\,(d+1)^m\) profiles, and party \(\,i\) has exactly \(\,(d+1)^{s_i}\) strategies.

We first show that a profile’s outcome can be computed exactly in polynomial time.

For a nonempty district \(\,D\), write its listed masses as \(\,a_{D,j}/b_{D,j}\), and define
\(Q_D=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau_D}b_{D,j}\)
and
\(w_{D,j}=a_{D,j}(Q_D/b_{D,j})\).
These are integers, and the total-mass assumption gives
\(\sum_j w_{D,j}=Q_D\).

The bit length of \(\,Q_D\) is at most the sum of the bit lengths of its input denominators, hence is \(\,O(L)\). Every weight and every accumulated score also has \(\,O(L)\) bits. Using ordinary integer multiplication and division, all these weights can be precomputed in \(\,O(L^3)\) bit operations.

Given a profile, scan each listed ranking until reaching its highest-ranked nominee. Add that type’s integer weight to the nominee’s score. Integer scores are precisely the rational scores multiplied by the positive constant \(\,Q_D\), so they preserve all comparisons and equalities. Apply the given tie-break among maximum-score nominees.

For an empty electorate, all nominee scores are zero, as required. For a district without nominees, record no winner.

A conservative bound for evaluating a profile, including all party utilities, is
\(T=O\bigl((m\tau+dm+m+p+1)L^2\bigr)\)
bit operations after preprocessing. Here \(\,m\tau\) counts scanned ranking entries; no enumeration of unlisted rankings is involved.

2. An outcome-preserving polynomial reduction

We use the following stated finite-electorate result: with one plurality winner per district, a fixed candidate tie-break, the specified nomination strategies, and utilities counting elected party members, equilibrium existence is \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete; its singleton-party restriction is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. These are Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 of [Harrenstein and Turrini (2022)](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/162392/1/WRAP-computing-Nash-equilibria-district-based-nominations-Turrini-2022.pdf).

Take an instance of that finite-electorate problem. Keep its candidates, parties, districts, and tie-break unchanged. In a district containing \(\,n_D>0\) voters, group identical rankings. If ranking \(\,\rho\) occurs \(\,n_{D,\rho}>0\) times, list it with mass
\(\mu_D(\rho)=n_{D,\rho}/n_D\).
Represent an empty electorate by an empty support.

These masses are positive binary-encoded rationals summing to one. If the original instance has \(\,n\) voters altogether, the output has \(\,\tau\leq n\) listed types, and each numerator and denominator uses \(\,O(\log(n+1))\) bits. Grouping explicitly listed rankings and computing these fractions therefore takes polynomial time and produces polynomial output length.

Fix any nomination profile \(\,x\), any nonempty district \(\,D\), and any nominee \(\,c\). Its new score is exactly
\(\operatorname{score}_{\mathrm{new},D}(c;x)=\operatorname{score}_{\mathrm{old},D}(c;x)/n_D\).

Division by the same positive number preserves every plurality comparison and every tie. Because the tie-break is unchanged, the elected candidate is unchanged. Empty electorates and districts without nominees also behave identically.

Thus every profile has exactly the same party utilities in both games. The strategy spaces are identical, so this equality also holds after every unilateral deviation. A profile is therefore an equilibrium in the finite instance if and only if it is an equilibrium in the rational instance.

The reduction preserves party sizes and the condition \(\,m\geq d\). It proves \(\Sigma_2^P\)-hardness in general and \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for singleton parties. Singleton instances also satisfy every fixed positive upper bound on party size.

3. Matching upper bounds

A profile requires \(\,O(m\log(d+1))\) bits. A deviation consists of a party index and a replacement assignment for its candidates, also requiring polynomially many bits.

By Section 1, whether that deviation strictly increases the party’s utility is decidable in polynomial time. Equilibrium existence consequently has the form
\(\exists x\;\forall(i,y)\;[u_i(x)\geq u_i(x_{-i},y)]\),
with polynomial-length quantified strings and a polynomial-time predicate. Invalid universal encodings can simply satisfy the predicate automatically. This proves membership in \(\Sigma_2^P\), and therefore \(\Sigma_2^P\)-completeness.

If \(\,s_i\leq k\) for a fixed constant \(\,k\), a proposed profile can instead be checked by enumerating every party’s replacement strategies. There are at most
\(\sum_{i=1}^p(d+1)^{s_i}\leq p(d+1)^k\)
such checks. Since \(\,d\leq m\) and the candidates are explicitly listed, this is polynomial in input length for fixed \(\,k\). Guessing and checking a profile proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with singleton hardness, this establishes the bounded-size completeness claim.

4. Explicit running-time bounds

Let
\(S=\sum_{i=1}^p(d+1)^{s_i}\),
and use the evaluation bound \(\,T\) from Section 1.

After the one-time \(\,O(L^3)\) preprocessing:

| Task | Running time in bit operations |
|---|---|
| Evaluate one profile | \(\,O(T)\) |
| Verify one proposed equilibrium by enumerating deviations | \(\,O((1+S)T)\) |
| Decide existence by enumerating profiles and deviations | \(\,O((d+1)^m(1+S)T)\) |

These algorithms are exhaustive: the verifier checks every legal replacement strategy, and the existence algorithm checks every legal nomination profile.

Using \(\,S\leq p(d+1)^k\), the total deterministic existence bound simplifies to
\(O\bigl(L^3+p(d+1)^{m+k}T\bigr)\).
For unrestricted party sizes, \(\,k\leq m\), giving
\(O\bigl(L^3+p(d+1)^{2m}T\bigr)\).

For fixed \(\,k\), certificate verification takes
\(O\bigl(L^3+p(d+1)^kT\bigr)\),
which is polynomial. This is a verification bound, not a polynomial-time existence algorithm.

Thus the rational support encoding preserves the hardness while allowing polynomial-time exact utility evaluation. A polynomial-time existence algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
