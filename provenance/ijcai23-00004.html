1. Parameters and the birth–death reduction

I use the stated universal quantifier over all admissible initial societies.

Set
$$ n=h^{-1},\qquad k=\eta/h,\qquad N=n-k. $$
The assumptions imply that these are integers and
$$ 1\le k<n. $$
Here $n$ counts all mass quanta, $k$ counts source quanta, and $N$ counts ordinary quanta.

For a fixed correct opinion $b$, let $J_t$ count incorrect ordinary quanta. Every admissible initial society corresponds to an initial value in $\{0,\ldots,N\}$. Define
$$ q=\min\{N,\lfloor n\varepsilon\rfloor\},\qquad K=N-q. $$
Then
$$ H_b=\inf\{t:J_t\le q\}. $$
If $K=0$, feasibility holds because every initial society already meets the target. Henceforth assume $K\ge1$.

Every admissible society supports one source type and at most two ordinary types. Thus $\tau\le3$, although $n$ can be exponentially large in the binary input length.

Flip-probability coordinates

It is convenient to express the rule through probabilities of adopting the opinion opposite to the current opinion. Put
$$ r_{0,s}=1-g_1(\ell-s),\qquad r_{1,s}=g_0(s) \quad(0\le s\le\ell). $$
These coordinates satisfy
$$ r_{b,0}=0,\qquad r_{b,\ell}=1,\qquad 0\le r_{b,s}\le1. $$
Conversely, every pair of arrays satisfying these conditions determines an admissible common rule.

Define
$$ R_b(p)=\sum_{s=0}^{\ell} \binom{\ell}{s}p^s(1-p)^{\ell-s}r_{b,s}. $$
For an incorrect quantum when the correct opinion is $b$, $R_b(p)$ is its probability of correction when the fraction holding the correct opinion is $p$.

At state $j$, the probabilities of a downward and upward move are therefore
$$ d_{b,j}=\frac jn R_b\left(1-\frac jn\right), \qquad u_{b,j}=\frac{N-j}{n}R_{1-b}\left(\frac jn\right). $$
The remaining probability is a self-loop, including source selections.

Finite expectations and the worst initial state

For $j>0$, selecting an incorrect quantum and sampling only correct opinions forces correction. Consequently,
$$ d_{b,j}\ge \frac jn\left(1-\frac jn\right)^\ell \ge\frac{\eta^\ell}{n}>0. $$
Write $\delta=\eta^\ell/n$. From any state above the target, a sequence of at most $N$ downward moves reaches the target with probability at least $\delta^N$. Conditioning successively on blocks of $N$ rounds gives
$$ \Pr(H_b>rN)\le(1-\delta^N)^r. $$
In particular, every admissible rule has finite expected hitting times.

Let
$$ T_b(j)=\mathbb E_j[H_b],\qquad \Delta_{b,j}=T_b(j)-T_b(j-1) \quad(j>q). $$
The first-step equations give
$$ d_{b,j}\Delta_{b,j}-u_{b,j}\Delta_{b,j+1}=1. $$
At the upper boundary $u_{b,N}=0$, so backward substitution yields
$$ \Delta_{b,N}=\frac1{d_{b,N}}, \qquad \Delta_{b,j} =\frac1{d_{b,j}}+ \frac{u_{b,j}}{d_{b,j}}\Delta_{b,j+1}. $$
All increments are positive. Thus the largest initial state $N$ maximizes the expected hitting time, without any monotonicity assumption on the rule.

Feasibility is consequently equivalent to
$$ T_0(N)\le B,\qquad T_1(N)\le B. $$
Expanding the recurrence gives
$$ T_b(N)= \sum_{i=q+1}^{N}\sum_{r=i}^{N} \frac{\prod_{t=i}^{r-1}u_{b,t}} {\prod_{t=i}^{r}d_{b,t}}, $$
with empty products equal to $1$.

2. Exact symmetrization for $\ell=2$

Theorem. When $\ell=2$, a feasible rule exists if and only if a feasible opinion-exchange-symmetric rule exists.

Proof

The two free flip parameters are
$$ a=r_{0,1}=1-g_1(1),\qquad c=r_{1,1}=g_0(1). $$
Define
$$ R_z(p)=p^2+2zp(1-p). $$
Then $R_0$ in the preceding notation is $R_a$, and $R_1$ is $R_c$.

For $p\in(0,1)$, $R_z(p)$ increases with $z$. Suppose first that $a\le c$, and choose any $z\in[a,c]$. Consider the symmetric rule
$$ g_0(1)=z,\qquad g_1(1)=1-z. $$
For correct opinion $b=0$, the original rule has
$$ d_{0,j}=\frac jnR_a(1-j/n), \qquad u_{0,j}=\frac{N-j}{n}R_c(j/n). $$
The symmetric rule has downward probabilities at least as large and upward probabilities at most as large:
$$ d^{(z)}_j\ge d_{0,j}, \qquad u^{(z)}_j\le u_{0,j}. $$

The increment recurrence proves that these inequalities decrease every expected hitting time. Indeed, the inequality holds at $N$ because
$$ \frac1{d^{(z)}_N}\le\frac1{d_{0,N}}. $$
If it holds for the increment at $j+1$, then
$$ \frac1{d^{(z)}_j} +\frac{u^{(z)}_j}{d^{(z)}_j}\Delta^{(z)}_{j+1} \le \frac1{d_{0,j}} +\frac{u_{0,j}}{d_{0,j}}\Delta_{0,j+1}. $$
Backward induction and summation give
$$ T^{(z)}(N)\le T_0(N). $$
The symmetric rule has identical error-count chains for the two correct opinions. Hence its worst-case expectation is at most that of the original rule.

If $c\le a$, the same argument uses $b=1$. This proves the theorem. $\square$

The remaining one-parameter objective

For the symmetric parameter $z\in[0,1]$, direct substitution gives
$$ d_j(z)= \frac{j(n-j)}{n^3}(n-j+2zj), $$
$$ u_j(z)= \frac{j(N-j)}{n^3}(j+2z(n-j)). $$
Define $T(z)$ by the increment recurrence in Section 1. The exact $\ell=2$ feasibility condition is
$$ \min_{0\le z\le1}T(z)\le B. $$
Every downward probability is positive on this interval, so $T$ is continuous and the minimum is attained.

This proof depends on the ordering of the single free parameter. It does not establish symmetrization for larger sample sizes. For example, at $\ell=3$, the two interior arrays $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ give flip polynomials whose difference is
$$ 3p(1-p)(1-2p). $$
Their ordering reverses at $p=1/2$, so the comparison used above does not apply.

3. An optimal rule for a tolerance regime when $\ell=2$

Proposition. Suppose
$$ 2(q+1)\ge n. $$
For $\ell=2$, the symmetric rule $z=1$ minimizes the worst-case expected hitting time.

This rule switches precisely when at least one sampled opinion differs from the current opinion.

Proof

By Section 2, it suffices to optimize symmetric rules.

Every active state satisfies $j\ge n/2$. The downward probability $d_j(z)$ strictly increases with $z$. Moreover,
$$ \frac{u_j(z)}{d_j(z)} = \frac{N-j}{n-j} \frac{j+2z(n-j)}{n-j+2zj}. $$
Differentiating gives
$$ \frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{u_j(z)}{d_j(z)}\right) = \frac{N-j}{n-j} \frac{2n(n-2j)}{(n-j+2zj)^2} \le0. $$
Thus both $1/d_j(z)$ and $u_j(z)/d_j(z)$ are nonincreasing, with the first strictly decreasing.

Starting at $\Delta_N(z)=1/d_N(z)$, backward induction in
$$ \Delta_j(z)=\frac1{d_j(z)} +\frac{u_j(z)}{d_j(z)}\Delta_{j+1}(z) $$
shows that every increment strictly decreases with $z$. Therefore $T(z)$ strictly decreases, and its minimum occurs at $z=1$. $\square$

This identifies the optimal rule in this regime. It does not, by itself, provide a binary-polynomial algorithm for comparing its expectation with $B$ when $K$ is large.

5. An explicit lower bound for every sample size

Theorem. For every admissible rule and every $\ell\ge1$, if
$$ n\ge32k,\qquad \varepsilon<\frac14, $$
then
$$ \max\{T_0(N),T_1(N)\}\ge\frac{n^2}{512k}. $$
In particular, feasibility under these hypotheses requires
$$ B\ge\frac{n^2}{512k}. $$

Proof

Write
$$ F(p)=\sum_{s=0}^{\ell} \binom{\ell}{s}p^s(1-p)^{\ell-s}g_0(s), $$
$$ Q(p)=1-\sum_{s=0}^{\ell} \binom{\ell}{s}p^s(1-p)^{\ell-s}g_1(s). $$
Thus $F(p)$ is the probability that a currently zero ordinary quantum adopts one, and $Q(p)$ is the probability that a currently one ordinary quantum adopts zero.

The endpoint constraints imply
$$ F(p)\ge p^\ell>0,\qquad Q(p)\ge(1-p)^\ell>0 \quad(0<p<1). $$
Also $F(p),Q(p)\le1$.

Let $F_w=F(w/n)$ and $Q_w=Q(w/n)$. For $b=0$, the incorrect count equals the total number of ones, and
$$ d_{0,w}=\frac wnQ_w,\qquad u_{0,w}=\frac{N-w}{n}F_w. $$
For $b=1$, an incorrect count of $n-w$ corresponds to a total of $w$ ones, and
$$ d_{1,n-w}=\frac{n-w}{n}F_w,\qquad u_{1,n-w}=\frac{w-k}{n}Q_w. $$

For valid indices, denote the summands in the hitting-time expansion by
$$ f_b(i,r)= \frac{\prod_{t=i}^{r-1}u_{b,t}} {\prod_{t=i}^{r}d_{b,t}}. $$
Pair $f_0(i,r)$ with $f_1(n-r,n-i)$. Substitution gives
$$ f_0(i,r)= n\, \frac{\prod_{w=i}^{r-1}(n-k-w)F_w} {\prod_{w=i}^{r}wQ_w}, $$
$$ f_1(n-r,n-i)= n\, \frac{\prod_{w=i+1}^{r}(w-k)Q_w} {\prod_{w=i}^{r}(n-w)F_w}. $$
Cancelling the common factors proves the identity
$$ f_0(i,r)f_1(n-r,n-i) = \frac{n^2}{i(n-r)Q_iF_r} \prod_{w=i}^{r-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n-w}\right) \prod_{w=i+1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{k}{w}\right). $$

Now put
$$ D=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{16k}\right\rfloor $$
and select pairs satisfying
$$ \left\lceil\frac n4\right\rceil \le i\le\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor, \qquad r=i+s,\qquad 0\le s\le D. $$
Because $q<n/4$ and $n\ge32k$, both $(i,r)$ and $(n-r,n-i)$ lie in the required index range from $q+1$ to $N$. Indeed,
$$ r\le\frac n2+\frac{n}{16k}\le\frac{9n}{16}, $$
while $N\ge31n/32$, and the reflected indices lie between $7n/16$ and $3n/4$.

All $w$ appearing in the products satisfy $w\ge n/4$ and $n-w\ge n/4$. Each product factor is therefore at least $1-4k/n$. There are $2s$ factors, so Bernoulli’s inequality gives
$$ \prod_{w=i}^{r-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n-w}\right) \prod_{w=i+1}^{r}\left(1-\frac{k}{w}\right) \ge \left(1-\frac{4k}{n}\right)^{2s} \ge1-\frac{8ks}{n} \ge\frac12. $$
Since $Q_iF_r\le1$ and $i(n-r)\le n^2$, the paired-product identity implies
$$ f_0(i,r)f_1(n-r,n-i)\ge\frac12. $$
The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality consequently gives
$$ f_0(i,r)+f_1(n-r,n-i)\ge\sqrt2. $$

There are at least $n/8$ choices of $i$. Also, since $n/(16k)\ge2$,
$$ D\ge\frac{n}{32k}. $$
Thus at least $n^2/(256k)$ distinct pairs have been selected. Reflection is injective, so no summand is counted twice within either hitting-time expansion. Summing the paired inequalities yields
$$ T_0(N)+T_1(N)\ge \frac{\sqrt2\,n^2}{256k}. $$
Hence
$$ \max\{T_0(N),T_1(N)\} \ge\frac{n^2}{256\sqrt2\,k} \ge\frac{n^2}{512k}. $$
This proves the theorem. $\square$

The bound is independent of $\ell$. For fixed positive $k$, it gives a quadratic lower bound even for the nonzero tolerances $\varepsilon<1/4$.

6. Exact algorithms and their running times

Arbitrary $\ell$

There are
$$ v=2(\ell-1) $$
free flip coordinates, all in $[0,1]$.

Set $M=n^{\ell+1}$ and define integer-coefficient affine polynomials
$$ a_{b,j}=Md_{b,j} = j\sum_{s=1}^{\ell} \binom{\ell}{s}(n-j)^sj^{\ell-s}r_{b,s}, $$
$$ c_{b,j}=Mu_{b,j} = (N-j)\sum_{s=1}^{\ell} \binom{\ell}{s}j^s(n-j)^{\ell-s}r_{1-b,s}, $$
where $r_{b,\ell}=1$ is fixed.

Put
$$ D_b=\prod_{j=q+1}^{N}a_{b,j}, $$
$$ P_b=M\sum_{i=q+1}^{N}\sum_{r=i}^{N} \left(\prod_{t=q+1}^{i-1}a_{b,t}\right) \left(\prod_{t=i}^{r-1}c_{b,t}\right) \left(\prod_{t=r+1}^{N}a_{b,t}\right). $$
Multiplication of the hitting-time expansion by $D_b$ proves
$$ T_b(N)=\frac{P_b}{D_b}. $$
There are $K$ factors in $D_b$ and $K-1$ affine factors in each summand of $P_b$, so
$$ \deg D_b\le K,\qquad \deg P_b\le K-1. $$
Furthermore, $D_b>0$ throughout the admissible cube.

Write $B=\beta/\gamma$, with integers $\beta\ge0$ and $\gamma>0$. Feasibility is exactly the existence of flip coordinates satisfying their cube constraints and
$$ \beta D_0-\gamma P_0\ge0,\qquad \beta D_1-\gamma P_1\ge0. $$

For coefficient-size control, let $\|A\|_1$ denote the sum of the absolute values of a polynomial’s coefficients. All coefficients in each affine factor are nonnegative, and their sum is at most $M$. Submultiplicativity therefore gives
$$ \|D_b\|_1\le M^K,\qquad \|P_b\|_1\le K^2M^K. $$
After incorporating the budget, coefficient bit lengths are bounded by
$$ A=O\!\left( L+K(\ell+1)\log(n+1)+\log(K+1) \right). $$
A polynomial of degree at most $K$ in $v$ variables has at most $\binom{K+v}{v}$ monomials, so explicit construction has polynomial cost for fixed $\ell$.

The one-block existential real-algebraic decision bound is $(sD)^{O(v+1)}(A+1)^{O(1)}$ bit operations for $s$ integer-polynomial conditions of degree at most $D$ and coefficient bit length at most $A$. This is the no-free-variable specialization of [Basu’s survey, Theorem 2.27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.1534). Here there is one existential block, $v=2(\ell-1)$, $s=O(\ell)$, and maximum degree at most $\max\{1,K\}$, so the hypotheses apply.

Including construction, an exact running-time bound is
$$ (1+\ell K)^{O(\ell)} \left( L+K(\ell+1)\log(n+1) \right)^{O(1)}. $$
A simpler bound is
$$ (n+\ell)^{O(\ell)}(L+1)^{O(1)}. $$
For $\ell=1$, there are no free coordinates and exact rational evaluation suffices.

The sharper formulation for $\ell=2$

By symmetrization, substitute the single parameter $z$ into the preceding polynomials. Feasibility becomes
$$ \exists z\in[0,1]: \quad A(z)=\beta D(z)-\gamma P(z)\ge0, $$
where $A$ is an integer polynomial of degree at most $K$.

This can also be decided by endpoint evaluation and exact real-root counting. If either endpoint value is nonnegative, the answer is yes. If both are negative, the answer is yes precisely when $A$ has a root in $(0,1)$:

  • A root itself satisfies the non-strict feasibility inequality.
  • If a nonnegative interior value exists, continuity gives a root between it and an endpoint.

Thus the $\ell=2$ problem admits an exact algorithm with running time
$$ (K+1)^{O(1)} \left(L+K\log(n+1)\right)^{O(1)}. $$

7. What remains open

These algorithms are polynomial in $h^{-1}$ and $L$ for fixed $\ell$. They are polynomial in $L$ when both $\ell$ and $K$ are fixed. Neither conclusion establishes polynomial time in the unrestricted binary input length: $K$ and $h^{-1}$ may be exponentially large.

The new $\ell=2$ symmetrization reduces the number of real variables to one, but the resulting polynomial can still have exponentially large degree. The lower bound and the example requiring randomization establish structural facts, not computational hardness.

The target therefore remains to remove the population-size dependence from exact decision, or to establish hardness for a fixed sample size while preserving both correctness quantifiers and the common-rule requirement.
