The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), target \(c\), and rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings, compute an optimal allocation \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,k}\) is mass of type \(t\) moving \(c\) upward by \(k\) adjacent swaps and \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\). Require, for every \(d\ne c\), that its resulting pairwise margin satisfy \(\mathrm{mar}_\mu(c,d)+2\sum_{t,k:\,d\text{ is crossed}}x_{t,k}\ge 0\); minimize the per-capita swap cost \(\sum_{t,k}kx_{t,k}\), and report the optimal allocation and edited society.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(c\in C\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(>_t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For each type \(t\), let \(a_t\) be the number of candidates above \(c\). For \(k\in\{0,\ldots,a_t\}\), let \(R_{t,k}\) be its ranking after moving \(c\) upward by \(k\) adjacent swaps, and let \(B_{t,k}\) be the set of candidates crossed. An allocation has real masses \(x_{t,k}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{k=0}^{a_t}x_{t,k}=\mu_t\); its edited society assigns each ranking \(R\) mass \(\sum_{t,k:R_{t,k}=R}x_{t,k}\).
Define \(\operatorname{mar}_\mu(c,d)=\sum_{t:c>_t d}\mu_t-\sum_{t:d>_t c}\mu_t\). An allocation is feasible when \(\operatorname{mar}_\mu(c,d)+2\sum_{t,k:d\in B_{t,k}}x_{t,k}\ge0\) for every \(d\ne c\); pairwise ties are accepted. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the minimum per-capita cost \(\sum_{t,k}kx_{t,k}\) over feasible allocations.

Does this problem admit a deterministic algorithm, polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), returning a feasible allocation and edited society with cost at most \((\ln m+1)\mathrm{OPT}\), or is obtaining this approximation guarantee \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite electorates, Marginal-Cost-Greedy-Dodgson is a deterministic polynomial-time \((\ln m+1)\)-approximation of Dodgson score. [Covey–Homan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0804.1421)
For explicitly listed electorates, the homogeneous Dodgson score defined by the replication limit is polynomial-time computable; its published fractional formulation uses strict pairwise victories rather than the weak inequalities specified here. [Rothe–Spakowski–Vogel](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0112021)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one, making the stated objective a per-capita cost.
  • Specified strict complete rankings, a nonempty candidate set, and a target belonging to that set.
  • Restricted k to the integers from zero through the number of candidates initially above c; zero permits leaving mass unchanged.
  • Defined the pairwise margin and the crossed-candidate sets, and specified aggregation of identical output rankings.
  • Retained the graded question's explicit weak pairwise inequalities, accepting ties rather than requiring strict Condorcet victories.

Conclusion

The problem is exactly solvable by a deterministic algorithm polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. In particular, the algorithm returns a feasible allocation and its edited society with cost $\mathrm{OPT}$, which satisfies the requested approximation guarantee.

The proof uses the real-valued allocation model specified in the question, including its weak pairwise inequalities.

1. An exact linear program

Let
$$ I=\{(t,k):1\le t\le\tau,\ 0\le k\le a_t\}, \qquad N=|I|=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}(a_t+1)\le\tau m. $$
For each $d\ne c$, define the input coefficients
$$ h_d=\operatorname{mar}_\mu(c,d), \qquad e_{d,t,k}= \begin{cases} 1,&d\in B_{t,k},\\ 0,&d\notin B_{t,k}. \end{cases} $$
Consider the rational linear program
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad& \sum_{(t,k)\in I}kx_{t,k}\\ \text{subject to}\quad& \sum_{k=0}^{a_t}x_{t,k}=\mu_t && (1\le t\le\tau),\\ & 2\sum_{(t,k)\in I}e_{d,t,k}x_{t,k}\ge-h_d && (d\in C\setminus\{c\}),\\ & x_{t,k}\ge0 && ((t,k)\in I). \end{aligned} $$

Its feasible solutions are exactly the feasible allocations in the question: the first and third families impose the allocation conditions, and the second family is precisely the stated pairwise feasibility condition. Its objective is precisely the allocation cost. Consequently, its optimum, if attained, equals $\mathrm{OPT}$.

2. Feasibility and attainment

For every type $t$, assign all its mass to moving $c$ to the top:
$$ x_{t,a_t}=\mu_t, \qquad x_{t,k}=0\quad(k\ne a_t). $$
To check feasibility, fix $d\ne c$ and write
$$ q_d=\sum_{t:d>_t c}\mu_t. $$
Because rankings are strict and complete and total mass is one,
$$ h_d=(1-q_d)-q_d=1-2q_d. $$
The proposed allocation crosses $d$ in exactly those types originally ranking $d$ above $c$. Thus its resulting margin is
$$ h_d+2q_d=1. $$
The allocation is therefore feasible.

Moreover, every allocation satisfies
$$ 0\le x_{t,k}\le\mu_t. $$
The feasible region is closed, since all its constraints are weak inequalities or equalities, and bounded by these coordinate bounds. It is therefore a nonempty compact polytope. The linear objective attains its minimum, with
$$ 0\le\mathrm{OPT}\le\sum_ta_t\mu_t\le m-1. $$

For $m=1$, there are no pairwise constraints and every available move has cost zero. The same formulation handles this case.

3. Polynomial input and output sizes

There are $N\le\tau m$ variables, $\tau$ equalities, $m-1$ pairwise inequalities, and $N$ nonnegativity inequalities. The sets $B_{t,k}$ and their incidence coefficients can be constructed directly from the listed rankings in polynomial time.

The constraint matrix has integer entries of absolute value at most two. Objective coefficients are integers between zero and $m-1$. It remains to check the bit lengths of the rational right-hand sides.

Write each input mass as $\mu_t=p_t/q_t$, with positive integer denominator, and let
$$ D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t. $$
The binary length of $D$ is $O(L)$, because the sum of the binary lengths of the input denominators is at most $L$. Each $\mu_t$ and each margin $h_d$ has denominator dividing $D$. Since $\mu_t\le1$ and $|h_d|\le1$, these right-hand sides have binary length $O(L)$. Hence the whole linear program has encoding length polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

For completeness, an exact optimum also has a polynomial-size rational representation. A linear objective on a nonempty compact polytope has an optimal vertex. At such a vertex, choose $N$ linearly independent active constraints and write their equations as
$$ Bx=b. $$
Here $B$ is an integer matrix with entries of absolute value at most two, and $Db$ is an integer vector with entries of absolute value at most $D$. Let $B_j$ be obtained by replacing column $j$ of $B$ with $Db$. Cramer’s rule gives
$$ x_j=\frac{\det B_j}{D\det B}. $$
Expanding determinants by permutations yields
$$ |\det B|\le N!\,2^N, \qquad |\det B_j|\le N!\,D\,2^{N-1}. $$
Since $\det B$ is a nonzero integer, each coordinate can therefore be represented with
$$ O\!\left(L+N\log(N+1)\right) $$
bits.

We now apply the standard exact rational linear-programming theorem: a deterministic algorithm solves a rational linear program in time polynomial in its binary encoding length, returning an exact rational optimal solution when a finite optimum exists. This is the polynomial-time linear-programming result originating with [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). All its hypotheses have been verified above.

Thus we can compute an optimal allocation $x^*$ in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. No expansion into individual voters is required.

4. Constructing the edited society

For every $(t,k)\in I$ with $x^*_{t,k}>0$, construct $R_{t,k}$. Group identical rankings and give each resulting ranking $R$ mass
$$ \nu(R)=\sum_{(t,k):R_{t,k}=R}x^*_{t,k}. $$
There are at most $N\le\tau m$ rankings before grouping, each containing $m$ candidates. Comparing rankings and summing their rational masses takes polynomial time. The resulting masses are positive after zero entries are omitted, and
$$ \sum_R\nu(R)=\sum_t\sum_kx^*_{t,k}=\sum_t\mu_t=1. $$

To verify the edited society’s margins explicitly, define
$$ s_t(d)= \begin{cases} 1,&c>_t d,\\ -1,&d>_t c. \end{cases} $$
Moving $c$ upward changes its comparison with $d$ exactly when it crosses $d$. The comparison contribution in $R_{t,k}$ is consequently $s_t(d)+2e_{d,t,k}$. Therefore
$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{mar}_{\nu}(c,d) &=\sum_{t,k}x^*_{t,k}\bigl(s_t(d)+2e_{d,t,k}\bigr)\\ &=\sum_ts_t(d)\mu_t+ 2\sum_{t,k}e_{d,t,k}x^*_{t,k}\\ &=h_d+2\sum_{t,k}e_{d,t,k}x^*_{t,k}\\ &\ge0. \end{aligned} $$
Grouping identical rankings preserves this sum. Thus the returned edited society satisfies every required weak pairwise inequality.

5. Approximation guarantee and the strict-inequality distinction

The returned allocation has cost exactly $\mathrm{OPT}$. Since $m\ge1$ and $\mathrm{OPT}\ge0$,
$$ \sum_{t,k}kx^*_{t,k} =\mathrm{OPT} \le(\ln m+1)\mathrm{OPT}. $$
This also covers $\mathrm{OPT}=0$ without an additive error.

The published homogeneous formulation uses strict pairwise inequalities; see [Rothe–Spakowski–Vogel, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0112021). Strict inequalities can prevent a minimum from being attained. For example, with two candidates and mass $1/2$ on each opposing ranking, the unedited society meets the weak condition at cost zero. Strict victory requires moving positive mass from the ranking placing $c$ second, so the strict problem has infimum zero but no minimum.

The proof above directly solves the stipulated weak-inequality problem, whose compact feasible region guarantees an attained exact optimum.
