The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(k,T,C,B\) and rational masses over persistent observable cohorts, with online current approval sets and no future revelation, construct or decide existence of an online rule choosing \(\theta_r \in [k]\) that guarantees \(\max_q \sum_r 1[\theta_r \notin A_q^r] \le B\) whenever every positive-mass \(k\)-tuple has empty approval intersection in at most \(C\) rounds.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) persistent, observable cohort types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_q\), where \(\sum_q\mu_q=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval sets. Cohorts retain their identities and masses; all members of a cohort share its current approvals.
The input also specifies \(m=k\geq1\) alternatives, a horizon \(T\geq1\), and integers \(0\leq C,B\leq T\). Alternatives are explicitly listed, \(T\) is encoded in unary, and \(C,B\) in binary; \(L\) denotes total input bit length.
In round \(r\), an adaptive adversary reveals arbitrary approval sets \(A_q^r\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\), possibly empty, for every cohort. A deterministic online rule then chooses one alternative \(\theta_r\), using only the input and the revealed history; no future approvals are supplied.
Adversary moves must maintain \(\sum_{s=1}^{r}\mathbf{1}\{\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}A_{q_i}^{s}=\varnothing\}\leq C\) for every ordered tuple \((q_1,\ldots,q_k)\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}^{k}\), with repetitions allowed. Thus the restriction also covers every nonempty subset of at most \(k\) cohorts.
A rule meets the threshold if every legal adversarial play satisfies \(\max_{1\leq q\leq\tau}\sum_{r=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{\theta_r\notin A_q^r\}\leq B\); dissatisfaction is measured per member, without mass weighting.

Is deciding whether such an online rule exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,T,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete societies with \(N\geq2\) agents and \(C\geq1\), an explicit exponential-weights online rule guarantees dissatisfaction \(O(T^{1-1/(k+1)}(Ck\ln N)^{1/(k+1)})\). This is a guaranteed bound, rather than an exact threshold-feasibility algorithm. [Kozachinskiy, Shen, and Steifer (2025)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/33529/35684)
No result settling this exact mass-cohort online existence problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational cohort masses to sum to one and interpreted cohorts as persistent identities whose members always share current approvals.
  • Allowed repeated cohort indices in k-tuples, preserving the discrete restriction on all nonempty subsets of at most k agents.
  • Allowed empty approval sets, as in the discrete game; their occurrences are constrained by the conflict budget.
  • Specified deterministic rules, adaptive adversaries, and a universal guarantee over every legal play; all choices, including tie-breaking, belong to the rule.
  • Selected existence decision rather than explicit strategy construction, avoiding an undefined representation for the output rule.
  • Set m=k, with explicitly listed alternatives; chose unary horizon encoding and binary conflict and dissatisfaction thresholds, both integers between zero and T.
  • Kept dissatisfaction as an unweighted count for each cohort member, matching the stated maximum over cohorts.

1. Definitions and elementary reductions

Write $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$, and let
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C) $$
be the smallest dissatisfaction threshold guaranteed by a deterministic online rule. The game has finitely many histories, and threshold $T$ always succeeds, so this value exists. The decision problem asks whether
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C)\le B. $$

The masses do not affect this value. Indeed, fix any two mass assignments with the same parameters. A strategy for one assignment can be copied to the other, using identical choices on identical approval histories. Legality and cohort dissatisfaction are unchanged because neither uses masses. This copying argument works in both directions.

Repetitions in ordered tuples imply that legality is equivalent to
$$ c_S(r):=\sum_{s=1}^{r} \mathbf 1\left\{\bigcap_{q\in S}A_q^s=\varnothing\right\}\le C $$
for every nonempty $S\subseteq[\tau]$ with $|S|\le k$. An ordered tuple determines such a subset, and every such subset can be represented by a length-$k$ tuple using repetitions.

We will repeatedly use the following elementary witness property.

Empty-intersection witness. If a collection of approval sets on $[k]$ has empty intersection, some nonempty subcollection of at most $k$ sets already has empty intersection.

Proof. For each alternative $a\in[k]$, choose one cohort rejecting $a$. The distinct chosen cohorts form the required subcollection. $\square$

The adversary can reveal all-empty approvals for $C$ rounds and full approvals thereafter. Every constrained subset conflicts exactly $C$ times, and every cohort incurs dissatisfaction $C$. Consequently,
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C)\ge C. $$

If $C=0$, the witness property shows that every legal round has a globally common alternative. Choosing one gives
$$ V(k,\tau,T,0)=0. $$

2. A general upper bound by assigning conflict occurrences

Put
$$ s=\min(k,\tau), \qquad p=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{\tau}{j}. $$

Theorem 1. For every instance,
$$ C\le V(k,\tau,T,C) \le \min\left\{ T,\ \left\lceil\frac{C}{\tau} \sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{\tau}{j}\right\rceil \right\}. $$

The upper bound has an explicit deterministic online strategy. Its construction and execution take time polynomial in $Cp,\tau,k,T,L$. This is not a uniform polynomial bound because $p$ can be exponential.

2.1. Assigning the available conflict occurrences

Assume $C\ge1$; the case $C=0$ was proved above. For every constrained subset $S$, create $C$ tokens
$$ (S,1),\ldots,(S,C). $$
We assign each token to one cohort belonging to $S$. Let
$$ d=\left\lceil\frac{Cp}{\tau}\right\rceil. $$
There is an assignment giving at most $d$ tokens to each cohort.

To prove this, make a bipartite graph whose left vertices are the tokens and whose right vertices are $d$ copies of each cohort. A token belonging to $S$ is adjacent to every copy of every cohort in $S$.

Hall’s marriage theorem states that a matching covering all left vertices exists if every family of left vertices has at least as many neighbors as members. We check this condition.

Let $\mathcal F$ be any family of tokens, and let $U$ be the union of their underlying cohort subsets. Write $u=|U|$. For a nonempty family, $u\ge1$, and
$$ |\mathcal F| \le C\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{u}{j}, $$
where binomial coefficients vanish when $j>u$.

For $u\ge1$,
$$ \frac1u\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{u}{j} = \sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac1j\binom{u-1}{j-1}. $$
Every summand is nondecreasing in $u$. Since $u\le\tau$,
$$ C\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{u}{j} \le \frac{Cp}{\tau}\,u \le du. $$
The family’s neighborhood consists of the $d$ copies of the cohorts in $U$, so it has size $du$. Hall’s condition follows. The empty family satisfies it trivially.

Thus the required assignment exists and can be found by bipartite matching.

2.2. The online rule

In a round where $S$ conflicts for the $j$th time, consume token $(S,j)$. Legality ensures $j\le C$.

Call a cohort protected in the current round if it owns no token consumed in that round. The protected cohorts have a common approved alternative.

Indeed, suppose their approval intersection were empty. The witness property would give a conflicting subset $H$ of at most $k$ protected cohorts. The token consumed for $H$ is assigned to a member of $H$. That member would be unprotected, a contradiction.

Choose the smallest alternative approved by all protected cohorts. If there are none, choose alternative $1$.

Every dissatisfied cohort is unprotected, so it owns at least one token consumed that round. Charge its dissatisfaction to one such token. A token is consumed in only one round and has only one owner. Hence each cohort’s dissatisfaction is at most its number of assigned tokens, which is at most $d$.

This proves the upper bound. The trivial bound $T$ completes the theorem. $\square$

3. A counting lower bound and matching leading terms

Theorem 2. If $\tau\ge k\ge2$, then
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C) \ge \left\lceil \frac{\min\left\{T,\ C\binom{\tau}{k}\right\}}{\tau} \right\rceil. $$

Proof. Choose a sequence of
$$ R=\min\left\{T,\ C\binom{\tau}{k}\right\} $$
size-$k$ cohort subsets, with each subset appearing at most $C$ times.

For a round assigned to subset $S$, choose a bijection $f:S\to[k]$ and reveal
$$ A_q=[k]\setminus\{f(q)\}\quad(q\in S), \qquad A_q=[k]\quad(q\notin S). $$

A constrained subset $H$ conflicts precisely when $H=S$. To see this, empty intersection requires rejecting all $k$ alternatives. The only rejecting cohorts are the members of $S$, each rejecting a different alternative. Thus $H$ must contain all of $S$; since $|H|\le k=|S|$, it must equal $S$.

Consequently, every constrained subset conflicts at most $C$ times, so all prefixes are legal.

Whichever alternative the rule chooses, exactly one cohort is dissatisfied in that round. Fill remaining rounds with full approvals. Total cohort dissatisfaction is $R$, so some cohort incurs at least $\lceil R/\tau\rceil$. $\square$

For horizons satisfying
$$ T\ge C\binom{\tau}{k}, $$
Theorems 1 and 2 give
$$ \left\lceil\frac{C}{\tau}\binom{\tau}{k}\right\rceil \le V(k,\tau,T,C) \le \left\lceil\frac{C}{\tau} \sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{\tau}{j}\right\rceil. $$

In particular,
$$ 0\le V(k,\tau,T,C)-\frac{C}{k}\binom{\tau-1}{k-1} \le C\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac1j\binom{\tau-1}{j-1}+1. $$
Here we used
$$ \frac1\tau\binom{\tau}{j} = \frac1j\binom{\tau-1}{j-1}. $$

For every fixed $k\ge2$, this proves the asymptotically sharp statement
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C) = \frac{C}{k!}\tau^{k-1} + O_k\!\left(C\tau^{k-2}+1\right) $$
as $\tau$ tends to infinity, provided $C\ge1$ and $T\ge C\binom{\tau}{k}$. The estimate follows by expanding the fixed-degree binomial polynomial and bounding the lower-degree summands above.

This determines the leading term, not the exact finite-parameter value.

4. Exact feasibility when the support has at most one extra cohort

Theorem 3. If $\tau\le k+1$, then
$$ V(k,\tau,T,C)=C. $$
Also, if $k=1$, the same conclusion holds for every $\tau$.

Proof for $\tau\le k$. The whole cohort set is constrained, so globally empty intersection occurs at most $C$ times. Choose a globally common alternative whenever one exists. Dissatisfaction can occur only in those at most $C$ rounds. The lower bound from Section 1 gives equality.

Proof for $\tau=k+1$. Arrange the cohorts in a directed cycle. Let $\operatorname{pred}(q)$ denote the predecessor of $q$, and define
$$ S_q=[\tau]\setminus\{\operatorname{pred}(q)\}. $$
Each $S_q$ has size $k$, contains $q$, and has conflict budget $C$.

In a round, call $q$ protected if
$$ \bigcap_{p\in S_q}A_p\ne\varnothing. $$
The protected cohorts have a common alternative.

For otherwise, the witness property supplies a nonempty subset $H$ of protected cohorts with empty intersection and
$$ |H|\le k=\tau-1. $$
Thus $H$ is a proper subset of the cycle. There is some $q\in H$ whose predecessor lies outside $H$: traverse the cycle from an outside vertex until first entering $H$. Therefore $H\subseteq S_q$, which implies that $S_q$ has empty intersection. This contradicts the protection of $q$.

Choose an alternative approved by all protected cohorts. Whenever $q$ is dissatisfied, $S_q$ conflicts. Hence
$$ D_q\le c_{S_q}(T)\le C. $$
The lower bound again gives equality.

Proof for $k=1$. The only alternative is forced. A cohort is dissatisfied exactly when its approval set is empty, and its singleton conflict budget bounds this count by $C$. $\square$

Thus these regimes admit the exact polynomial-time test $B\ge C$.

5. Two alternatives: an optimal bound for long horizons

For $k=2$, singleton and pair conflicts are the only constraints. We can improve Theorem 1 by using pair tokens alone.

Theorem 4. If $k=2$ and $\tau\ge3$, then
$$ V(2,\tau,T,C) \le \min\left\{ T,\ \left\lceil\frac{C(\tau-1)}2\right\rceil \right\}. $$
The strategy is computable in time polynomial in $\tau,T,L$.

Consequently, if
$$ T\ge C\binom{\tau}{2}, $$
then
$$ V(2,\tau,T,C) = \left\lceil\frac{C(\tau-1)}2\right\rceil. $$
For $\tau\le3$, Theorem 3 already gives $V=C$ for every horizon.

5.1. A balanced orientation of pair tokens

For every unordered pair, create $C$ parallel edges, representing its successive conflict occurrences. Orient each edge toward the cohort owning that token.

First fix a directed Hamilton cycle on the $\tau$ cohorts. Orient all $C$ copies of each cycle edge in its cycle direction. Every cohort receives exactly $C$ incoming cycle tokens.

The remaining multigraph has degree
$$ C(\tau-3) $$
at every vertex. It admits an orientation with indegree at most
$$ \left\lceil\frac{C(\tau-3)}2\right\rceil $$
at every vertex.

Here is a proof of the orientation fact. In any finite undirected multigraph, the number of odd-degree vertices is even by the degree-sum identity. Add an auxiliary vertex joined once to each odd-degree vertex. All degrees become even. Orient an Euler circuit in each nontrivial connected component. Incoming and outgoing degrees are then equal. Removing the auxiliary edges leaves indegree at most half the original degree rounded upward at every original vertex.

Combining the two orientations, every cohort owns at most
$$ C+\left\lceil\frac{C(\tau-3)}2\right\rceil = \left\lceil\frac{C(\tau-1)}2\right\rceil $$
pair tokens.

Label each pair’s copies by its occurrence numbers from $1$ to $C$. For cycle pairs, all these copies have the same owner.

5.2. Protection also handles empty approvals

In each round, consume the next token of every conflicting pair. Call a cohort protected if it owns no token consumed that round.

Every protected cohort has nonempty approvals. Indeed, if $q$ has empty approvals, its pair with its cycle predecessor conflicts. The token for that pair is always directed toward $q$, making $q$ unprotected.

Moreover, the protected cohorts have a common alternative. For nonempty subsets of $\{1,2\}$, empty common intersection requires a cohort approving only $1$ and another approving only $2$. Their pair conflicts, and its consumed token belongs to one of them, contradicting that cohort’s protection.

Choose an alternative approved by all protected cohorts. Charge each dissatisfied cohort to an incoming pair token consumed that round. Tokens are never consumed twice, so the orientation bound proves the claimed dissatisfaction guarantee.

The orientation construction uses at most $C\binom{\tau}{2}$ edges. Because $C\le T$ and $T$ is unary, construction and round-by-round processing are polynomial in the stated input parameters.

Finally, Theorem 2 for $k=2$ gives the matching lower bound when the horizon contains all $C\binom{\tau}{2}$ pair rounds. $\square$

In particular, for $C=1$, $\tau\ge2$, and $T\ge\binom{\tau}{2}$,
$$ V(2,\tau,T,1)=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau}{2}\right\rfloor. $$

6. Two alternatives and one conflict: exactly when every round can be lost

This section gives an exact finite-horizon criterion at threshold $B=T-1$.

Theorem 5. Suppose $k=2$, $C=1$, and $T\ge2$. Then
$$ V(2,\tau,T,1)=T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau\ge2^T. $$

6.1. Empty approvals cannot cause repeated dissatisfaction

Consider any rule that chooses a globally common alternative whenever one exists.

Lemma. Under this rule, a cohort that has empty approvals in some round incurs dissatisfaction exactly once in the entire play.

Proof. For $\tau=1$, every other round has nonempty approvals because the singleton conflict budget is one. The rule satisfies the cohort in those rounds.

Suppose $\tau\ge2$, and $q$ is empty in round $r$. Every pair containing $q$ conflicts in that round. Thus, by the pair budget, in every other round $s$,
$$ A_q^s\cap A_p^s\ne\varnothing \quad\text{for every }p\ne q. $$
The singleton budget also ensures $A_q^s\ne\varnothing$.

If $A_q^s=\{1,2\}$, the cohort is satisfied by any choice. If $A_q^s=\{a\}$, the displayed intersections imply that every cohort approves $a$. The rule therefore chooses $a$. Thus $q$ is satisfied in every round except $r$. $\square$

6.2. The upper direction by halving candidates

Maintain
$$ H_0=[\tau], $$
and let $H_r$ contain the cohorts that have had nonempty approvals and been dissatisfied in every one of the first $r$ rounds.

Choose a globally common alternative whenever one exists. Otherwise, among the nonempty-approval cohorts in $H_{r-1}$, choose the alternative approved by the larger number, breaking ties deterministically.

If there are $h$ such cohorts, their total number of approvals of the two alternatives is at least $h$. Hence one alternative is approved by at least $\lceil h/2\rceil$ of them. It follows that
$$ |H_r| \le \left\lfloor\frac{h}{2}\right\rfloor \le \left\lfloor\frac{|H_{r-1}|}{2}\right\rfloor. $$
A globally common choice instead makes $H_r$ empty. Inductively,
$$ |H_T|\le\left\lfloor\frac{\tau}{2^T}\right\rfloor. $$

If $\tau<2^T$, this set is empty. By the lemma, a cohort encountering empty approvals has total dissatisfaction one, which is less than $T$. Every cohort dissatisfied in all $T$ rounds would therefore belong to $H_T$, a contradiction. Thus
$$ V(2,\tau,T,1)\le T-1. $$

6.3. The lower direction by adaptive splitting

Suppose $\tau\ge2^T$. Select an active set $H_0$ of $2^T$ cohorts.

In round $r$, partition $H_{r-1}$ into equal groups $X_r,Y_r$. Give approvals $\{1\}$ to $X_r$, approvals $\{2\}$ to $Y_r$, and full approvals to all other cohorts. After the rule chooses, let $H_r$ be the dissatisfied group.

Then
$$ |H_r|=2^{T-r}, $$
and every member of $H_r$ has been dissatisfied in all first $r$ rounds. One cohort therefore loses all $T$ rounds.

Singletons never conflict. A pair conflicts only when both cohorts are active and placed in opposite groups. After such a split, at most one member remains active, so that pair cannot conflict again. Hence every pair conflicts at most once, and the adversary is legal.

This proves $V=T$ and completes the theorem. $\square$

For $T=1$, all-empty approvals give $V=1$ regardless of support size; this is why the theorem assumes $T\ge2$.

7. What the results imply about complexity

7.1. Ordinary many-one hardness would collapse $\mathrm{P}$ and $\mathrm{NP}$

Let $\mathcal E$ be the decision language, rejecting malformed inputs. Since masses and names are irrelevant, map each valid input to
$$ 1^k0\,1^\tau0\,1^T0\,1^C0\,1^B0. $$
Let $\mathcal S$ contain exactly those valid canonical strings whose parameters satisfy $V(k,\tau,T,C)\le B$.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. Input validity, including positivity and normalization of binary-rational masses, can be checked by polynomial-bit rational arithmetic. Explicit listing and unary $T$ imply that $k,\tau,T$ are bounded by input length $L$; also $C,B\le T$. Thus the canonical output has polynomial length. Invalid inputs can be mapped to a fixed invalid canonical string.

There are at most $(n+1)^5$ candidate canonical strings of length at most $n$, so $\mathcal S$ is sparse.

Mahaney’s theorem states that a sparse language that is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions implies $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$; membership of the sparse language in $\mathrm{NP}$ is not required. This precise statement and proof appear in [Grochow, Theorem 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1610.05825).

Therefore, if $\mathcal E$ were $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, composing reductions would make the sparse language $\mathcal S$ $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, forcing $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.

Conversely, under $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$, every $\mathrm{NP}$ language can be decided in polynomial time and reduced to any language with a fixed yes-instance and a fixed no-instance. Here one may use mass one and:

  • A yes-instance with $k=\tau=T=1$ and $C=B=0$.
  • A no-instance with $k=\tau=T=C=1$ and $B=0$.

Thus
$$ \mathcal E\text{ is }\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}. $$

7.2. Polynomial-space membership

An entire approval and choice history uses polynomial space because $k,\tau,T$ are bounded by $L$.

For a proposed next approval matrix, enumerate the ordered tuples in $[\tau]^k$ one at a time. For each tuple, scan the stored history and count its empty intersections. The enumeration can take exponential time, but the tuple, intersections, and counters require only polynomial space.

Evaluate the game recursively: a history is winning if every legal next approval matrix admits an alternative leading to a winning continuation. At depth $T$, test all cohort dissatisfaction counts against $B$. Depth-first evaluation enumerates matrices and alternatives sequentially and has polynomial depth and polynomial space per frame.

This proves
$$ \mathcal E\in\mathrm{PSPACE}. $$

8. Remaining open points

The new strategies decide exact feasibility in the stated special regimes and give asymptotically sharp values for fixed $k$ with sufficiently long horizons. They do not provide an exact feasibility algorithm for arbitrary $k,\tau,T,C,B$.

In particular, the general token construction may enumerate exponentially many subsets. Even for two alternatives, the results above do not characterize every finite-horizon threshold. Consequently, uniform membership in $\mathrm{P}$ remains unproved, while ordinary many-one $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness would settle $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
