Proposition 5 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_a>0\), rational incentives \(\alpha_a\), a common rational conformity parameter \(\omega>0\), a rational type-level peer matrix \(\bar{\mathbf G}\), and a specified long-run preference or action target, compute a typewise policy vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) that implements the target under the paper's dynamics. For action targets, apply Proposition 5's action formula with \(\mathbf G\) replaced by \(\bar{\mathbf G}\); for preference targets, use its preference formula.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_a\), normalized by \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has one scalar action and preference, so \(m=1\). Inputs are rational incentives \(\alpha_a\geq0\), parameters \(\omega>0\) and \(\gamma\in(0,1)\), and a nonnegative rational row-stochastic peer matrix \(\bar G\), diagonalizable over \(\mathbb C\). Its entry \(\bar g_{ab}\) is the total peer weight assigned to type \(b\), already incorporating mass; diagonal entries represent interactions with other individuals of the same type.
A policy is a time-independent rational vector \(\sigma\), chosen before dynamics begin, with effective incentives \(\beta=\alpha+\sigma\in[0,1]^\tau\). Taxes and subsidies are both allowed, with no budget or sign restriction on \(\sigma\).
For every initial preference vector \(s^0\in[0,1]^\tau\), simultaneous equilibrium actions and preference updates satisfy \((2+\omega)x^t=\beta+s^t+\omega\bar Gx^t\) and \(s^{t+1}=\gamma x^t+(1-\gamma)s^t\), with \(x^t,s^t\in[0,1]^\tau\).
The input specifies either an exact preference target \(q\in[0,1]^\tau\) or an exact action target \(q\in[0,1]^\tau\). A policy implements it if, respectively, \(\lim_{t\to\infty}s^t=q\) or \(\lim_{t\to\infty}x^t=q\), for every initial preference vector.
Is deciding whether an implementing policy exists in \(\mathrm P\), with an implementing rational policy computable in polynomial time, or is the existence problem NP-hard? State running times in \(m=1\), \(\tau\), and the total binary input length \(L\).
For finite agents, explicit tax/subsidy formulas implement homogeneous and heterogeneous long-run preference or action targets in the linear equilibrium regime; no complexity classification is asserted. [Olcina, Panebianco and Zenou (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2024.02.021)
No complexity classification for exactly this distribution-over-types formulation was verified.

Result

For \(m=1\), the existence problem is in \(\mathrm P\), and an implementing rational policy can be computed in polynomial time.

More precisely, either target type is implementable if and only if

\[ 0\le (1+\omega)q_a-\omega\sum_{b=1}^{\tau}\bar g_{ab}q_b\le 1 \qquad\text{for every }a. \]

When these inequalities hold, the unique implementing policy is

\[ \sigma_a=(1+\omega)q_a-\omega\sum_{b=1}^{\tau}\bar g_{ab}q_b-\alpha_a. \]

For complexity, I interpret the numerical input—including \(\omega\) and \(q\)—as binary-encoded rationals. Arbitrary real inputs would require a specified representation before a bit-complexity classification could be stated.

1. Equilibrium actions exist uniquely and remain feasible

Write \(G=\bar G\), and let \(\mathbf 1\) denote the all-ones vector. Nonnegativity and row-stochasticity give

\[ G\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1,\qquad \|G\|_\infty=1. \]

For any \(c>0\), define

\[ A_c=(c+\omega)I-\omega G,\qquad r_c=\frac{\omega}{c+\omega}<1. \]

The geometric series converges in the induced infinity norm and gives

\[ A_c^{-1}=\frac{1}{c+\omega}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_c^kG^k. \]

Indeed, multiplying its partial sum by \(A_c\) gives \(I-r_c^{n+1}G^{n+1}\), whose remainder has norm at most \(r_c^{n+1}\) and therefore tends to zero. Consequently, \(A_c^{-1}\) exists, is entrywise nonnegative, and satisfies

\[ A_c^{-1}\mathbf 1 =\frac{1}{c+\omega}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}r_c^k\mathbf 1 =\frac1c\mathbf 1. \]

Set \(C=A_2^{-1}\). The equilibrium equation therefore has the unique solution

\[ x^t=C(\beta+s^t),\qquad C\mathbf 1=\frac12\mathbf 1. \]

If \(\beta,s^t\in[0,1]^\tau\), then \(0\le\beta+s^t\le2\mathbf 1\). Nonnegativity of \(C\) implies

\[ 0\le x^t\le2C\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1. \]

The preference update is a convex combination of \(x^t\) and \(s^t\), so it also belongs to \([0,1]^\tau\). Induction proves feasibility at every time for every permitted initial preference vector.

2. Every feasible policy has a common action and preference limit

For a fixed \(\beta\in[0,1]^\tau\), define

\[ q^\beta=A_1^{-1}\beta. \]

Because \(A_1^{-1}\) is nonnegative and \(A_1^{-1}\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1\), we have \(q^\beta\in[0,1]^\tau\). Its defining equation gives

\[ A_1q^\beta=\beta,\qquad A_2q^\beta=\beta+q^\beta, \]

and hence \(C(\beta+q^\beta)=q^\beta\).

Substituting equilibrium actions into the preference update yields

\[ s^{t+1}=\gamma C\beta+Hs^t, \qquad H=(1-\gamma)I+\gamma C. \]

The matrix \(H\) is nonnegative, with every row sum equal to

\[ \rho=1-\gamma+\frac{\gamma}{2}=1-\frac{\gamma}{2}<1. \]

Thus \(\|H\|_\infty=\rho\). The preceding fixed-point identity implies

\[ s^{t+1}-q^\beta=H(s^t-q^\beta). \]

Iterating and taking norms gives

\[ \|s^t-q^\beta\|_\infty \le \rho^t\|s^0-q^\beta\|_\infty \longrightarrow0. \]

Similarly,

\[ x^t-q^\beta=C(s^t-q^\beta), \qquad \|x^t-q^\beta\|_\infty \le\frac12\rho^t\|s^0-q^\beta\|_\infty \longrightarrow0. \]

Therefore actions and preferences converge to the same vector \(q^\beta\), independently of the initial preferences.

This proof does not require diagonalizability, so it applies in particular to every matrix satisfying the problem’s promise.

3. Necessary and sufficient implementation condition

Suppose an admissible policy implements either target type. By the convergence result, its common limiting vector must equal \(q\). Therefore

\[ \beta=A_1q=(1+\omega)q-\omega Gq. \]

Since admissibility requires \(\beta\in[0,1]^\tau\), the inequalities stated above are necessary.

Conversely, suppose

\[ b=(1+\omega)q-\omega Gq\in[0,1]^\tau. \]

Choose \(\sigma=b-\alpha\). This is a rational policy, and its effective incentives are \(\beta=b\). Since \(A_1\) is invertible,

\[ q^\beta=A_1^{-1}b=q. \]

Section 2 then proves convergence of both actions and preferences to \(q\) for every initial preference vector. Thus the condition is sufficient.

The policy is unique because every implementing policy must have \(\beta=A_1q\), and \(\sigma=\beta-\alpha\). The unrestricted availability of taxes and subsidies makes this subtraction admissible.

4. Exact algorithm and bit complexity

The algorithm computes \(b=(1+\omega)q-\omega Gq\), checks each coordinate against zero and one, and outputs \(b-\alpha\) if every check passes.

For \(m=1\), a dense matrix representation requires \(O(\tau^2)\) exact rational arithmetic operations. Here is an explicit polynomial bit bound that accounts for intermediate sizes.

Let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators appearing in \(\omega\), \(G\), \(q\), and \(\alpha\), using one factor for each encoded scalar. Since their total encoding length is at most \(L\), the binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\).

Expand each coordinate as

\[ b_a=q_a+\omega q_a-\sum_{b=1}^{\tau}\omega g_{ab}q_b. \]

Each term’s denominator divides \(D\): a term uses at most one denominator from each of the distinct input fields \(\omega\), \(g_{ab}\), and \(q_b\). Thus exact integer arithmetic computes \(B_a=Db_a\). Feasibility is precisely the integer comparison

\[ 0\le B_a\le D. \]

On acceptance, let \(U_a=D\alpha_a\) and output \(\sigma_a=(B_a-U_a)/D\).

All intermediate integers have \(O(L)\) bits. To see this, \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits, every input numerator has at most \(L\) bits, and summing at most \(\tau\) terms adds at most \(O(\log\tau)\) bits. Explicit input encoding gives \(\log\tau=O(L)\).

Constructing these integers and performing the comparisons uses \(O(\tau^2)\) integer operations. Schoolbook multiplication and division on \(O(L)\)-bit integers therefore give the conservative bounds

\[ O(\tau^2L^2)\quad\text{bit operations}, \qquad O(\tau L)\quad\text{output bits}. \]

The algorithm computes no matrix inverse and simulates no dynamics. The masses do not enter the calculation because \(G\) already incorporates peer weights assigned to types.

5. Feasibility is a substantive restriction

Every homogeneous target \(q=c\mathbf 1\), with rational \(c\in[0,1]\), is implementable: row-stochasticity gives \(Gq=q\), so \(\beta=q\) and \(\sigma=c\mathbf 1-\alpha\).

Heterogeneous targets can fail. Take \(\tau=2\), masses \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\), and

\[ G=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad q=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}. \]

This matrix is nonnegative, row-stochastic, and diagonalizable, but its required incentive vector is

\[ \beta=\begin{pmatrix}-\omega\\1+\omega\end{pmatrix}, \]

which violates admissibility for every \(\omega>0\). Thus the algorithm distinguishes feasible and infeasible instances.

The cited [Olcina, Panebianco and Zenou paper](https://researchmgt.monash.edu/ws/files/584316177/577219042_oa.pdf) provides the finite-agent policy context. The complexity classification here follows directly from the specified equations and the exact algorithm above.
