The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and contract classes \(E\) between types, each with endpoint-type weak rankings, choose pair masses \(x_e\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\ni t}d_{te}x_e\le\mu_t\), where \(d_{te}=2\) for a same-type pair and \(1\) for an ordinary endpoint. Let \(A_t(e;x)\) be the mass of type \(t\) unmatched or assigned a contract it strictly ranks below \(e\); a distinct-type contract blocks when both endpoint types have positive willing mass, and a same-type contract blocks when \(A_t(e;x)>0\). Does an algorithm polynomial in the type and contract counts, preference input length, and mass bit length return a stable \(x\) whose matched mass \(2\sum_e x_e\) is at least \(2/3\) of the maximum stable matched mass?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The explicitly listed contract classes \(E\) join unordered pairs of types; parallel classes and same-type pairs are allowed. Each type weakly ranks its incident classes, with ties allowed, and strictly prefers every listed class to being unmatched. A same-type class has the same rank for both participants; it pairs distinct individuals of that type. There are no additional contract capacities.
A feasible allocation has real pair masses \(x_e\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\ni t}d_{te}x_e\leq\mu_t\), where \(d_{te}=2\) for a same-type class and \(d_{te}=1\) otherwise. Define \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_{f\ni t}d_{tf}x_f\) and \(A_t(e;x)=u_t+\sum_{f\ni t:\,e\succ_t f}d_{tf}x_f\).
A distinct-type class \(e=\{s,t\}\) blocks precisely when \(A_s(e;x)>0\) and \(A_t(e;x)>0\); a same-type class at \(t\) blocks precisely when \(A_t(e;x)>0\). An allocation is stable if no class blocks. Its matched mass is \(M(x)=2\sum_e x_e\); let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum of \(M(y)\) over stable feasible allocations, with \(\sup\varnothing=0\).

Does an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) always return a stable allocation \(x\) with \(M(x)\geq\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{OPT}\), or is obtaining this approximation NP-hard?

For finite individual agents of unit capacity, maximum-size weakly stable fractional matching on arbitrary graphs with ties and parallel contracts admits a polynomial-time \(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation returning a half-integral allocation ([Csáji](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16173v3)).
Continuum roommate existence results are available under rationality and continuity assumptions in a different distributional model ([Carmona and Laohakunakorn](https://klaohakunakorn.com/ocwp.pdf)); the search identified no approximation result for the precise mass problem above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Removed zero-mass types from the supplied list so that the society is encoded by its positive support; retained total mass one.
  • Required listed contracts to be strictly preferable to being unmatched, matching the intended blocking condition and approximation guarantee despite the source's literal allowance of indifference to unmatched status.
  • Specified unordered contract classes, allowed parallel classes, and imposed no additional contract capacities.
  • Specified that a same-type contract pairs distinct individuals with identical rankings for that class.
  • Defined willing mass explicitly, counting both participants in a same-type allocation.
  • Used a supremum to define the benchmark without presupposing attainment; set the empty-set benchmark to zero while retaining the requirement that the algorithm return a stable allocation.
  • Defined m as the contract-class count and L as total input bit length, including preferences and masses.

1. Result

There is a deterministic algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) that returns a stable allocation \(x\) satisfying \(M(x)\geq \frac23\mathrm{OPT}\).

The proof combines a capacitated stable-allocation algorithm with a modification of the three-copy construction: distinct-type classes receive three copies, while same-type classes receive only a middle copy. An expansion into individuals is used solely to prove the approximation ratio.

2. The stable-allocation primitive

We use the following known result.

Stable-allocation theorem. On a finite loopless undirected multigraph, give each vertex a nonnegative integer bound, each edge a positive integer capacity, and each vertex a strict ranking of its incident edges. A half-integral stable allocation can be found deterministically in time polynomial in the graph size and the binary encoding lengths of the bounds and capacities. Here stability means that every edge below capacity has an endpoint that is saturated and receives allocation only on edges ranked at least as high as that edge.

This is the weakly polynomial stable-allocation algorithm of [Biró and Fleiner (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disopt.2010.02.002); their [later account, Section 4.2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disopt.2015.02.002), explicitly states the half-integral output guarantee. Disconnected components can be processed separately.

We first explain how this theorem handles the same-type classes in the question, assuming strict class rankings.

For each same-type class \(\ell\) at \(t\), introduce a fresh dummy vertex \(a_\ell\) of mass \(2\), and replace \(\ell\) by the ordinary edge \(\{t,a_\ell\}\). Preserve its position in \(t\)'s ranking; it is the dummy's only incident edge. If \(z_\ell\) is the allocation on this replacement edge, interpret the original pair mass as \(h_\ell=z_\ell/2\). Thus its consumption at \(t\) is exactly \(2h_\ell=z_\ell\).

Every replacement edge has \(z_\ell\leq\mu_t\leq1\), so its dummy is always unsaturated. Consequently, stability of that edge forces \(t\) to be saturated and to receive only classes ranked at least as high as \(\ell\). This is exactly the same-type condition \(A_t(\ell;h)=0\). The conditions for all distinct-type classes are also preserved, because both vertex consumption and class rankings are preserved.

For binary-rational masses, choose an integer \(Q\) such that every \(Q\mu_t\) is integral. The product of the input denominators suffices, and \(\log Q=O(L)\). Scale all vertex bounds by \(Q\), and set every auxiliary edge capacity to \(3Q\). Every edge has an original endpoint with bound at most \(Q\), so these edge capacities can never bind.

The stable-allocation theorem therefore applies with integer data of polynomial encoding length. Dividing its output by \(Q\), and halving replacement-edge allocations, gives a rational stable allocation for the strict instance with same-type classes.

This establishes the primitive we need: strict class rankings, rational masses, parallel classes, and same-type classes can all be handled in polynomial time.

3. The construction and algorithm

We adapt the three-copy construction described in [Csáji, Section 3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16173v3), with the following treatment of same-type classes.

For each distinct-type class \(e=\{s,t\}\), create three classes:

  • \(B_s(e)\), its best copy at \(s\) and worst copy at \(t\);
  • \(C(e)\), its middle copy at both endpoints;
  • \(B_t(e)\), its best copy at \(t\) and worst copy at \(s\).

For each same-type class \(\ell\), create only \(C(\ell)\), still a same-type class.

Construct a strict ranking at each type \(t\) as follows. Process its original preference tiers from best to worst. Within a tier, list first all best copies \(B_t(e)\) of its distinct-type classes, then all middle copies \(C(e)\), including those of same-type classes. Break orders within these lists arbitrarily. After all tiers, append all copies that are worst at \(t\), in an arbitrary strict order. Every created class remains preferable to being unmatched.

Two properties of this ranking will be used:

  • Every worst copy is below every best copy.
  • For a distinct-type class \(e\) incident to \(t\) and any original class \(f\) incident to \(t\),

\(C(f)\succeq'_t B_t(e)\) holds if and only if \(f\succ_t e\).

The second property follows because a middle copy from a higher original tier precedes \(B_t(e)\), whereas middle copies from the same or a lower tier follow it.

Run the primitive from Section 2 on this strict expanded instance, obtaining a stable allocation \(h\). Return its projection:

\(x_e=h_{B_s(e)}+h_{C(e)}+h_{B_t(e)}\) for each distinct-type \(e=\{s,t\}\),

and \(x_\ell=h_{C(\ell)}\) for each same-type class \(\ell\).

There are at most \(3m\) expanded classes and at most \(m\) dummy vertices. Their encoding length is polynomial in the original encoding length. The algorithm therefore runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), with polynomial-length rational output.

4. Feasibility and stability of the projection

Each expanded copy consumes exactly the same original-type masses as its original class. Projection consequently preserves consumption at every original type, so \(x\) is feasible. It also preserves pair mass:

\(\sum_e x_e=\sum_k h_k\).

Suppose an original class \(e\) has \(A_t(e;x)>0\) at an incident type \(t\). By the definition of \(A_t\), either \(t\) has positive unmatched mass or some strictly worse original class \(f\) has positive allocation.

In the latter case, some expanded copy of \(f\) has positive allocation. Every such copy is below \(C(e)\): its best and middle copies belong to a lower original tier, and its worst copies were appended last. Hence

\(A'_t(C(e);h)>0\).

If a distinct-type class blocked \(x\), its middle copy would therefore block \(h\). If a same-type class blocked \(x\), its sole middle copy would block \(h\). Both contradict stability of \(h\).

Thus \(x\) is stable. In particular, stable allocations always exist in the specified model.

5. Rational optima and an individual-level representation

The optimum is attained, and a rational optimum exists.

Indeed, the feasible set is a bounded rational polytope. On this set every \(A_t(e;x)\) is nonnegative. Stability of a distinct-type class \(e=\{s,t\}\) is precisely the disjunction

\(A_s(e;x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad A_t(e;x)=0\),

and stability of a same-type class is one linear equality.

Choose one endpoint equality for each distinct-type class. Intersecting these equalities and all same-type equalities with the feasible polytope gives a rational polytope. The stable set is the finite union of these polytopes, so it is compact. It is nonempty by Section 4. A linear objective attains its maximum on this union, and on a maximizing constituent it has a rational maximizing vertex.

Fix such an optimum \(y\).

For the approximation proof only, choose an integer \(q\) making every \(q\mu_t\), every expanded allocation \(qh_k\), and every \(qy_e\) integral. Replace each type \(t\) by \(q\mu_t\) unit individuals. Every distinct-type class is available between every pair of individuals of its endpoint types; every same-type class is available between every pair of distinct individuals of that type.

The integer allocation \(qh\) can be represented by an ordinary matching \(X\) in this expanded individual graph. To see this, at each type partition its individuals into the required incidence slots. A distinct-type copy needs \(qh_k\) slots at each endpoint, which can be paired bijectively. A same-type copy needs \(2qh_k\) slots, which can be paired within that type. Feasibility supplies enough individuals.

Likewise, \(qy\) can be represented by a matching \(Y\) in the original individual graph, using the same individual vertex set.

Individuals rank classes as their type does, and are indifferent between partners within the same class-copy. These two matchings inherit stability:

  • For each expanded distinct-type class, stability of \(h\) supplies an endpoint type that is saturated and receives only class-copies ranked at least as high. Every individual of that type therefore rejects a strict improvement along that class-copy.
  • For each expanded same-type class, stability of \(h\) gives this property at its single type.
  • The identical argument applied to \(y\) proves stability of \(Y\) in the original individual graph.

Hence \(X\) is stable under the expanded preferences, and \(Y\) is stable under the original preferences. Their sizes satisfy

\(|X|=q\sum_k h_k=\frac q2M(x)\), and \(|Y|=q\sum_e y_e=\frac q2\mathrm{OPT}\).

No individual graph is constructed by the algorithm.

6. Excluding short augmenting paths

When comparing \(X\) and \(Y\), forget the copy labels on \(X\)'s edges but retain their original class labels. Remember the expanded label of each \(X\)-edge for preference comparisons.

Their symmetric difference consists of alternating paths and cycles. We prove that it contains no \(Y\)-augmenting path with zero or one \(X\)-edge.

A single \(Y\)-edge. Such an edge joins two individuals unmatched by \(X\). Both strictly prefer its original class to being unmatched. This contradicts stability of the individual matching induced by the stable projection \(x\).

A path with one \(X\)-edge. Suppose such a component is the path \(a,b,c,d\), with \(Y\)-edges \(e=\{a,b\}\) and \(g=\{c,d\}\), and \(X\)-edge \(f=\{b,c\}\). Its endpoints \(a,d\) are unmatched by \(X\).

Any type incident to a same-type class is saturated by \(h\): the stability condition for that class is \(A'_t(C(\ell);h)=0\), and this nonnegative expression includes the unmatched mass. All individuals of such a type are consequently matched by \(X\).

Thus neither \(e\) nor \(g\) is a same-type class, since each has an endpoint unmatched by \(X\).

Let \(t_b,t_c\) be the types of \(b,c\). Consider the best-at-\(b\) copy \(B_{t_b}(e)\) between \(a,b\). Individual \(a\) is unmatched and would strictly prefer it. Stability of \(X\) therefore implies that \(b\)'s current expanded copy of \(f\) is ranked at least as high as \(B_{t_b}(e)\). In particular, it cannot be a worst copy at \(b\). Applying the same argument to \(g\) shows that the current copy of \(f\) cannot be worst at \(c\).

If \(f\) is distinct-type, its two endpoint-specific copies are each worst at one endpoint. Its current copy must therefore be \(C(f)\). If \(f\) is same-type, \(C(f)\) is its only copy anyway.

Consequently,

\(C(f)\succeq'_{t_b}B_{t_b}(e)\), and \(C(f)\succeq'_{t_c}B_{t_c}(g)\).

Property 2 of the construction gives

\(f\succ_{t_b}e\), and \(f\succ_{t_c}g\).

In \(Y\), individual \(b\) receives \(e\), and individual \(c\) receives \(g\). Both therefore strictly prefer their class-\(f\) contract to their \(Y\)-assignments. Since \(b,c\) are distinct individuals, this is a blocking pair even when \(f\) is same-type. It contradicts stability of \(Y\).

Thus neither kind of short augmenting path exists.

7. Counting and conclusion

An alternating cycle has equally many \(X\)- and \(Y\)-edges. Any alternating path with more \(Y\)-edges has \(r\) \(X\)-edges and \(r+1\) \(Y\)-edges. Section 6 excludes \(r=0,1\), so \(r\geq2\), and

\(r+1\leq\frac32r\).

Every other alternating component has at most as many \(Y\)-edges as \(X\)-edges. Common edges contribute equally to both matchings. Summing therefore yields

\(|Y|\leq\frac32|X|\).

Using the size identities from Section 5 gives

\(\mathrm{OPT}\leq\frac32M(x)\),

or equivalently \(M(x)\geq\frac23\mathrm{OPT}\).

The constructed algorithm is deterministic, polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and always returns a stable allocation with the requested guarantee, including parallel classes and same-type contracts.
