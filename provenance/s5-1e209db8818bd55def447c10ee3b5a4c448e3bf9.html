Theorem 3.6 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) items, workforce scale \(N\), a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over per-comparison disutilities, \(\pi\), \(\delta\), the principal's utility and verification-cost parameters, and CrowdSort's fixed task allocation, choose a payment cutoff, verification count \(v\), and replication \(r\) to maximize expected principal utility subject to incentive compatibility and probability at least \(1-\delta\) of recovering the true ranking; output an optimal contract and its running time in \(n,\tau,L,\log N\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) nonnegative rational per-comparison disutilities \(\psi_j\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(w_j\), where \(\sum_j w_j=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Write \(F(c)=\sum_{\psi_j\leq c}w_j\).
There are \(n\geq2\) items with an unknown strict ground-truth order, a binary-encoded integer workforce scale \(N\geq1\), rational \(0<\pi<1\), \(0<\delta<1\), and rational utility and verification-cost rates \(\lambda,\bar\psi\geq0\).
For probabilistic execution, the \(N\) workforce positions receive independent disutilities drawn from this society; masses are not rounded to integer multiplicities. Effort choices precede task assignment. An effortful worker is good with probability \(\pi\); a worker without effort is bad. Good workers report the true order; each bad worker independently reports a uniformly random permutation of all items.
The fixed, type-independent allocation template is supplied succinctly as part of the input. For each admissible integer \(1\leq v\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), \(1\leq r\leq N\), it specifies a common set \(V\) of \(v\) item-disjoint verified pairs and comparison sets \(W_i\), with each unverified pair assigned to exactly \(r\) distinct workers. Its description specifies its assignment randomness, admissible pairs \((v,r)\), and the common exact rational expected comparison workload \(d(v,r)>0\); assignment sets are accessible by worker index without listing all \(N\) positions.
Workers know the workload distribution and verification count before choosing effort, but not the verification identities. A contract chooses a nonnegative rational cutoff \(c\) and pays \(p=d(v,r)c/[\pi(1-2^{-v})]\) to every worker who answers all verified pairs correctly, and zero otherwise. Workers maximize expected payment minus \(\psi_j\) times their expected comparison workload, have outside utility zero, and choose effort when indifferent; thus precisely the types with \(\psi_j\leq c\) exert effort.
Discard every failed worker's reports. Among the remaining reports, retain each distinct pair once, discarding that pair entirely if opposite answers occur; this does not alter payments. Let \(K\) be the number of retained pairs and \(A\) the number of paid workers. Recovery means the retained comparisons have the true order as their unique consistent total order.
Maximize the exact expected utility \(U=\lambda\mathbb E[K]-\bar\psi v-p\mathbb E[A]\), subject to recovery probability at least \(1-\delta\) and principal participation \(U\geq\lambda\binom n2-\bar\psi Q_n\), where \(Q_n=2(n+1)\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k-4n\) is the expected comparison count of randomized quicksort. Require these conditions for every ground-truth order. Expectations include workforce, quality, permutation, and allocation randomness.
Is this distribution-input contract optimization problem solvable in polynomial time, or is its rational-threshold decision version NP-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can an optimal contract, or infeasibility, be computed in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,\log N\), where \(L\) includes all rational data and the succinct allocation description?
For a finite workforce, a linear-time scan over the desired number of incentivized agents is claimed to maximize expected principal utility; the displayed optimization actually maximizes a utility lower bound under sufficient recovery conditions. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09974v2#S3.SS4).
No result settling the exact distribution-input optimization above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive support masses to one and allowed nonnegative rational disutilities; the distribution is known exactly, so no CDF estimation error is included.
  • Interpreted workforce scale as a binary-encoded positive integer and distribution masses as probabilities for independent workforce positions. This supplies a probabilistic execution without rounding masses to agent multiplicities.
  • Kept verification count and replication integral, bounded verification by the number of disjoint item pairs, and bounded replication by the workforce size.
  • Required a succinct allocation description, including its randomness, admissible parameter pairs, and common exact expected workload, because an explicit workforce-sized allocation would undermine the requested dependence on log N.
  • Defined the cutoff through the payment formula and resolved effort indifference in favor of effort, including atoms at zero disutility.
  • Used the specified conflict-discarding postprocessing to define distinct returned comparisons, exact expected principal utility, and successful recovery through a unique consistent total order.
  • Carried over principal participation relative to sorting independently, using the exact rational expected randomized-quicksort comparison count instead of its asymptotic approximation.
  • Required correctness and participation for every unknown strict ground-truth order rather than introducing an unspecified prior over rankings.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result and scope

The rational-threshold decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds already for \(\tau=1\), \(n=3\), \(N=2\), and a single admissible pair \((v,r)=(1,1)\), with positive disutility and positive utility and verification-cost rates.

The reduction uses a succinct randomized allocation description: a Boolean circuit evaluated on independent fair bits, followed by constant-size assignment operations. Such a description satisfies the stated access and workload requirements. The result concerns the general supplied-template problem; it does not establish hardness for a separately prescribed allocation algorithm.

1. Cutoff discretization does not settle the problem

For any fixed \((v,r)\), an optimal cutoff, if feasible, can be chosen from \(\{0,\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_\tau\}\).

Indeed, given a cutoff \(c\), replace it by the largest support value at most \(c\), or by zero if no support value is at most \(c\). This preserves the set of effortful types, hence preserves the distribution of reports, recovery probability, \(\mathbb E[K]\), and \(\mathbb E[A]\). It weakly decreases payment, so it weakly increases utility for every ground-truth order.

Thus there are few relevant cutoffs. The difficulty below is evaluating exact recovery for a succinct randomized template.

2. Two allocation modes with different recovery probabilities

Use three labeled items, two workers, and \((v,r)=(1,1)\). Every worker receives the verified pair. Each of the other two pairs is assigned to exactly one worker.

Choose the verified pair independently of everything else, with probabilities

\(\displaystyle \Pr[V=\{\{1,2\}\}]=\frac12,\qquad \Pr[V=\{\{1,3\}\}]=\Pr[V=\{\{2,3\}\}]=\frac14. \)

Two fair bits implement this choice exactly. Consider two allocation modes:

  • Mode A: Give both unverified pairs to one uniformly chosen worker.
  • Mode B: Give one unverified pair to each worker, randomly exchanging their assignments.

In Mode A, each worker receives either one or three comparisons, with equal probability. In Mode B, each receives two. Therefore both modes have the common exact expected workload \(d(1,1)=2\).

Suppose both workers exert effort and \(\pi=\frac12\). Fix an arbitrary ground-truth order, written \(a<b<c\). Conditional on the allocation, the workers’ outcomes are independent.

A worker passes verification with probability

\(\displaystyle \frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac34.\)

For a bad worker, among the six permutations:

  • exactly one satisfies two comparisons forming a chain;
  • exactly two satisfy two comparisons having a common minimum or common maximum.

Consequently, the unconditional probabilities of satisfying those two comparisons are respectively

\(\displaystyle \frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac16=\frac7{12}, \qquad \frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac13=\frac23. \)

A worker fails verification with probability \(\frac14\).

For three items, recovery occurs precisely when all retained comparisons are true and both adjacent comparisons, \(a<b\) and \(b<c\), are retained. Necessity follows because an erroneous comparison excludes the true order, while omission of an adjacent comparison permits swapping those adjacent items. Sufficiency follows because the two adjacent comparisons force the entire true order.

Mode A. If the worker assigned both unverified pairs fails verification, at most the verified pair remains, so recovery is impossible. If that worker passes, all three comparisons are present and agree with that worker’s permutation. Recovery therefore occurs exactly when that worker reports the complete true order. Hence, for every ground truth,

\(\displaystyle R_A=\frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac16=\frac7{12}.\)

Mode B, nonadjacent verification. If the verified pair is \(\{a,c\}\), both unverified adjacent pairs must be reported correctly by their respective workers. Each worker must satisfy two comparisons with a common minimum or maximum. Therefore

\(\displaystyle R_{B,\mathrm{nonadjacent}}=\left(\frac23\right)^2=\frac49.\)

Mode B, adjacent verification. Suppose the verified pair is \(\{a,b\}\). The worker assigned \(\{b,c\}\) must pass and report correctly, which requires satisfying the chain \(a<b<c\), with probability \(\frac7{12}\).

The worker assigned \(\{a,c\}\) must either fail verification or pass and report that pair correctly. These disjoint possibilities have total probability

\(\displaystyle \frac14+\frac23=\frac{11}{12}.\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle R_{B,\mathrm{adjacent}}=\frac7{12}\cdot\frac{11}{12}=\frac{77}{144}.\)

The other adjacent verified pair gives the same calculation.

Since \(\frac49<\frac{77}{144}\), the worst ground-truth orders are those whose nonadjacent pair is \(\{1,2\}\), the pair verified with probability \(\frac12\). Their recovery probability in Mode B is

\(\displaystyle R_B^{\min} =\frac12\cdot\frac49+\frac12\cdot\frac{77}{144} =\frac{47}{96}. \)

This minimum is attained, for example, by the order \(1<3<2\). The other orders give at least this probability. The exact gap is

\(\displaystyle R_A-R_B^{\min}=\frac7{12}-\frac{47}{96}=\frac3{32}.\)

3. Reduction from Boolean satisfiability

Let \(\varphi\) be a Boolean formula with \(t\geq1\) variables, and define

\(\displaystyle \alpha=2^{-t}\bigl|\{x\in\{0,1\}^t:\varphi(x)=1\}\bigr|. \)

Construct the allocation template as follows:

  • Draw \(t\) independent fair bits \(x\).
  • Evaluate \(\varphi(x)\).
  • Use Mode A if \(\varphi(x)=1\), and Mode B otherwise.
  • Independently choose the verified pair and worker exchange as above.

This is a polynomial-size, polynomial-time executable description. It evaluates the formula on one assignment; it does not compute satisfiability or count satisfying assignments. Every realization satisfies the assignment requirements, and every worker’s expected workload is exactly two, independently of \(\alpha\).

Use the economic parameters

\(\displaystyle \tau=1,\quad w_1=1,\quad \psi_1=\frac1{100},\quad \pi=\frac12,\quad \lambda=\bar\psi=1. \)

The only admissible pair is \((v,r)=(1,1)\). Set the decision utility threshold to \(T=1\), and choose

\(\displaystyle 1-\delta=\frac{47}{96}+\frac3{64}\,2^{-t}. \)

This specifies a rational \(\delta\) strictly between zero and one, with encoding length polynomial in \(t\).

If \(c\geq\frac1{100}\), both workers exert effort. For each ground truth, recovery is the corresponding mixture of the two modes. Because Mode A has the same recovery probability for every order, the minimum over ground-truth orders is exactly

\(\displaystyle R^{\min}(c) =\alpha\frac7{12}+(1-\alpha)\frac{47}{96} =\frac{47}{96}+\frac3{32}\alpha. \)

Therefore:

  • If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, then \(\alpha=0\), so \(R^{\min}(c)<1-\delta\).
  • If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, then \(\alpha\geq2^{-t}\), so \(R^{\min}(c)>1-\delta\).

This calculation explicitly handles the requirement for every ground-truth order.

We must also exclude cutoffs \(c<\frac1{100}\), which induce no effort. In that case both workers are bad. Mode A recovers with probability \(\frac16\). Repeating the preceding Mode B calculations without the good-worker component gives

\(\displaystyle R_{B,\mathrm{nonadjacent}}=\left(\frac13\right)^2=\frac19, \qquad R_{B,\mathrm{adjacent}}=\frac16\left(\frac12+\frac13\right)=\frac5{36}. \)

Both are at most \(\frac16\). Thus every mixture has recovery probability at most \(\frac16\) for every order, whereas \(1-\delta>\frac{47}{96}>\frac16\). No such cutoff is feasible.

4. Exact utility and participation

For \(c\geq\frac1{100}\), each worker is paid with probability \(\frac34\), so

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[A]=2\cdot\frac34=\frac32.\)

Each unverified pair has one assigned worker. It is retained exactly when that worker passes verification; an erroneous answer still counts toward \(K\). Each therefore contributes \(\frac34\) to \(\mathbb E[K]\).

The verified pair is retained when at least one worker passes. All its surviving answers are correct, so there is no conflict. Its retention probability is

\(\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac14\right)^2=\frac{15}{16}.\)

Consequently, independently of allocation mode and ground truth,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[K]=\frac{15}{16}+2\cdot\frac34=\frac{39}{16}.\)

The payment is

\(\displaystyle p=\frac{2c}{(\frac12)(1-\frac12)}=8c, \)

and exact expected utility is

\(\displaystyle U(c)=\frac{39}{16}-1-8c\cdot\frac32=\frac{23}{16}-12c.\)

At the cheapest effort-inducing cutoff \(c=\frac1{100}\), indifference induces effort by the stipulated tie rule, and

\(\displaystyle U\left(\frac1{100}\right)=\frac{527}{400}>1=T.\)

For three items,

\(\displaystyle Q_3=2(4)\left(1+\frac12+\frac13\right)-12=\frac83. \)

The participation benchmark is therefore

\(\displaystyle \lambda\binom32-\bar\psi Q_3=3-\frac83=\frac13,\)

which is strictly below \(\frac{527}{400}\).

Thus, whenever \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, this cutoff satisfies recovery, participation, and the decision utility threshold for every ground-truth order. Whenever \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, every cutoff fails recovery.

5. Complexity consequence

We have proved the equivalence

\(\displaystyle \varphi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed instance admits a feasible contract with }U\geq T. \)

The construction has polynomial encoding length. Boolean satisfiability is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, so this is a polynomial-time many-one proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Moreover, satisfiable instances have optimal cutoff \(c=\frac1{100}\): every smaller cutoff fails recovery, and utility strictly decreases among larger cutoffs. Unsatisfiable instances are infeasible. An algorithm computing an optimal contract or infeasibility in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,\log N\) would therefore decide Boolean satisfiability in polynomial time. Such an algorithm cannot exist unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

This does not contradict the cited scan: the proof of Theorem 3.6 explicitly optimizes a utility lower bound under sufficient correctness conditions. It does not provide exact recovery evaluation for general succinct randomized templates. [Source, proof of Theorem 3.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09974v2#S3.SS4).
