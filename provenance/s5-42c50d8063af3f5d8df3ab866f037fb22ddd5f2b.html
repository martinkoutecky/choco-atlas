The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of agent types on the two sides, binary-rational type masses, strict rankings over partner types with being unmatched last, compatible type pairs, and nonnegative type-pair weights, find a maximum-weight popular allocation. An allocation matches individuals and may leave them unmatched; it is popular when, against every feasible challenger on the same population, the mass of agents who strictly prefer it is at least the mass who strictly prefer the challenger.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,B\) be nonempty disjoint sets of types, with a connected compatibility graph \(E\subseteq A\times B\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), without requiring equal side masses. Each type represents an atomless population.
Each type \(t\) strictly ranks its compatible partner types, followed by the unmatched option \(\bot\); partners of the same type are indifferent. Edge weights \(w_{ab}\) and a threshold \(K\) are nonnegative binary rationals. Let \(m=|E|\) and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{ab}\geq0\), with \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\leq\mu_t\); it matches individuals one-to-one. Write \(P_t=\Gamma(t)\cup\{\bot\}\), \(d_{t,p}=x_{tp}\) for compatible partners (using the same edge mass at both endpoints), and \(d_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\). Its weight is \(\sum_{ab\in E}w_{ab}x_{ab}\).
A challenger may reassign any individuals, including previously unmatched ones. Precisely, its joint reassignment masses are arbitrary real \(z^t_{pq}\geq0\), for \(p,q\in P_t\), satisfying \(\sum_q z^t_{pq}=d_{t,p}\) and \(\sum_{p\in P_a}z^a_{pb}=\sum_{p\in P_b}z^b_{pa}\) for every \(ab\in E\); \(z^t_{pq}\) counts individuals whose old option is \(p\) and new option is \(q\).
Let \(v_t(p,q)\) equal \(1\) if \(t\) prefers \(p\) to \(q\), \(-1\) if it prefers \(q\) to \(p\), and \(0\) if \(p=q\). An allocation is popular if \(\sum_t\sum_{p,q\in P_t}v_t(p,q)z^t_{pq}\geq0\) for every feasible challenger.

Is deciding whether a popular allocation of weight at least \(K\) exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite individual populations, maximum-weight popular matching with nonnegative edge weights is NP-hard; a polynomial-time \(\tfrac12\)-approximation exists, and a better ratio is impossible unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01478)
Maximum-weight popular mixed matchings are computable in polynomial time, but their voting compares independently sampled lotteries over finite matchings, rather than arbitrary reassignments of the same individuals. [Source](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass across both sides to one, preserving potentially unequal side masses.
  • Made each supported type an atomless population, allowing arbitrary real subdivisions while retaining one partner per individual.
  • Carried over the connected bipartite compatibility graph and required both sides to be nonempty.
  • Specified indifference between individuals of the same partner type and strict preferences over compatible types, with unmatched last.
  • Specified binary-rational nonnegative weights and threshold, and used the threshold decision version of maximum-weight optimization.
  • Made comparisons on the same individuals explicit through joint reassignment masses, allowing every feasible coupling of old and new assignments.
  • Defined m as the number of compatible type pairs and L as total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness already holds when all supported types have equal mass and every edge weight belongs to \(\{0,1\}\).

The key property of the reassignment model is that popularity passes to any allocation using only old options that occur in a given popular allocation. This yields a rounding theorem for equal masses.

1. Popularity passes to smaller supports

For an allocation \(x\), write \(d^x_{t,p}\) for its old-option masses, including the unmatched option.

Lemma 1. Suppose \(x\) and \(y\) are feasible allocations for the same society, and
\[ \]
\(d^y_{t,p}>0\Longrightarrow d^x_{t,p}>0\)
for every \(t,p\). If \(x\) is popular, then \(y\) is popular.

Proof. Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) sufficiently small that
\(\varepsilon d^y_{t,p}\leq d^x_{t,p}\) for every \(t,p\). Such a choice exists because there are finitely many options and the assumed support inclusion holds.

Let \(z^y\) be any feasible challenger to \(y\). Define a challenger to \(x\) by

\(z^{x,t}_{pq}=\varepsilon z^{y,t}_{pq}+\mathbf{1}_{\{p=q\}}\bigl(d^x_{t,p}-\varepsilon d^y_{t,p}\bigr).\)

All entries are nonnegative. Its row sums are

\(\sum_q z^{x,t}_{pq}=\varepsilon d^y_{t,p}+d^x_{t,p}-\varepsilon d^y_{t,p}=d^x_{t,p}.\)

For an edge \(ab\), the new mass assigned to \(b\) at endpoint \(a\) is

\(\sum_p z^{x,a}_{pb}=\varepsilon\sum_p z^{y,a}_{pb}+x_{ab}-\varepsilon y_{ab}.\)

The corresponding expression at endpoint \(b\) is identical, because \(z^y\) satisfies edge balance. Thus \(z^x\) is feasible.

The additional diagonal terms contribute zero votes. Consequently,

\(\sum_{t,p,q}v_t(p,q)z^{x,t}_{pq}=\varepsilon\sum_{t,p,q}v_t(p,q)z^{y,t}_{pq}.\)

Popularity of \(x\), together with \(\varepsilon>0\), implies that the right-hand challenger vote against \(y\) is nonnegative. Since \(z^y\) was arbitrary, \(y\) is popular. \(\square\)

2. A popular allocation can be replaced by a vertex

Let the feasible allocation polytope be

\(\mathcal P(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}:\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\leq\mu_t\text{ for every }t\}.\)

For \(x\in\mathcal P(\mu)\), define \(F(x)\) by imposing every coordinate and capacity constraint that is tight at \(x\):

\(F(x)=\{y\in\mathcal P(\mu):y_e=0\text{ whenever }x_e=0,\ \sum_{e\ni t}y_e=\mu_t\text{ whenever }\sum_{e\ni t}x_e=\mu_t\}.\)

This is a nonempty face of \(\mathcal P(\mu)\), since it is obtained by making some of its valid inequalities equalities.

For every \(y\in F(x)\), a positive partner-option mass in \(y\) also occurs in \(x\). Moreover, if \(d^x_{t,\bot}=0\), the defining capacity equality forces \(d^y_{t,\bot}=0\). Hence the support hypothesis of Lemma 1 holds.

Corollary 2. If \(x\) is popular, every allocation in \(F(x)\) is popular. For any edge weights, some vertex \(y\) of \(\mathcal P(\mu)\) is popular and has weight at least that of \(x\).

Proof. The first assertion follows from Lemma 1. The polytope is bounded because every edge mass is bounded by both endpoint masses. Thus the linear weight function attains its maximum over \(F(x)\) at a vertex of that face. Every vertex of a face is a vertex of the original polytope. \(\square\)

3. Equal masses recover finite popular matchings

Consider a finite bipartite preference instance on \(n\) vertices. Make each vertex a supported type of mass \(1/n\), retaining its preferences.

We use the standard bipartite matching-polytope theorem: for a bipartite graph, the vertices of

\(\{r\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}:\sum_{e\ni t}r_e\leq1\text{ for every }t\}\)

are precisely the incidence vectors of matchings. Its hypotheses hold here: the graph is bipartite, and the constraints are nonnegativity and unit vertex capacities. Scaling by \(n\) therefore shows that the vertices of \(\mathcal P(\mu)\) are precisely allocations

\(x^M_e=\mathbf{1}_{\{e\in M\}}/n\)

associated with finite matchings \(M\).

We must also check that popularity of these allocations agrees with finite popularity.

For a finite matching \(M\), let \(p_M(t)\) be its partner at \(t\), or \(\bot\) if unmatched. Define the finite vote margin

\(V(M,N)=\sum_t v_t(p_M(t),p_N(t)).\)

Thus \(M\) is popular exactly when \(V(M,N)\geq0\) for every finite matching \(N\).

Lemma 3. The allocation \(x^M\) is popular under arbitrary reassignment if and only if \(M\) is a finite popular matching.

Proof. Any finite matching \(N\) gives a feasible challenger to \(x^M\) by setting

\(z^t_{p_M(t),p_N(t)}=1/n\)

and all other entries to zero. Its vote margin is \(V(M,N)/n\). Therefore popularity of \(x^M\) implies popularity of \(M\).

Conversely, suppose \(M\) is popular and take any challenger \(z\) to \(x^M\). Only the row indexed by \(p_M(t)\) can be nonzero at type \(t\). Define its scaled new edge masses by

\(r_{ab}=n\sum_p z^a_{pb}=n\sum_p z^b_{pa}.\)

Edge balance gives the equality, and the row constraints give \(\sum_{e\ni t}r_e\leq1\). The matching-polytope theorem consequently gives a decomposition

\(r=\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf{1}_{N_i},\qquad \lambda_i\geq0,\qquad\sum_i\lambda_i=1.\)

The scaled new unmatched mass at \(t\) is \(1-\sum_{e\ni t}r_e\). It therefore equals the same convex combination of the unmatched indicators of the \(N_i\). Since the old option is fixed at \(p_M(t)\), linearity gives

\(\sum_{t,p,q}v_t(p,q)z^t_{pq}=\frac1n\sum_i\lambda_iV(M,N_i)\geq0.\)

Thus every challenger has nonnegative vote margin, proving popularity of \(x^M\). \(\square\)

Combining Corollary 2 and Lemma 3 yields the required rounding theorem:

Theorem 4. With masses \(1/n\), every popular allocation can be replaced, without decreasing weight, by an allocation \(x^M\) associated with a finite popular matching.

4. Polynomial reduction proving hardness

We use the following established hardness result: given a connected bipartite graph with strict preferences and two specified edges, deciding whether a popular matching contains both edges is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is the two-forced-edge case of Theorem 1 in [Faenza, Powers, and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.01478); Section 2 specifies connected input graphs.

Given such an instance with \(n\) vertices and distinguished edges \(e_1,e_2\), construct the society as follows:

  • Keep the graph and all preference lists.
  • Give each vertex/type mass \(\mu_t=1/n\).
  • Set \(w_{e_1}=w_{e_2}=1\), and all other weights to zero.
  • Set \(K=2/n\).

The graph remains connected, every listed type has positive mass, and the total mass is one. The masses and threshold are binary-encoded rationals of bit length polynomial in the source input size.

If a finite popular matching contains both specified edges, Lemma 3 gives a popular allocation of weight \(2/n\).

Conversely, suppose a popular allocation has weight at least \(2/n\). Theorem 4 provides a finite popular matching \(M\) whose associated allocation has at least that weight. But

\(\operatorname{weight}(x^M)=|M\cap\{e_1,e_2\}|/n.\)

Therefore \(M\) contains both specified edges.

This is a polynomial many-one reduction, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under the stated restrictions.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For completeness, arbitrary binary rational masses also admit polynomial-size certificates.

If a popular allocation reaches the threshold, Corollary 2 provides such an allocation at a vertex of \(\mathcal P(\mu)\). Every vertex has polynomial rational encoding length. Indeed, its coordinates are determined by an invertible subsystem of \(m\) tight constraints, whose coefficient matrix has entries in \(\{0,1\}\). Clearing the mass denominators, Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound give coordinate bit lengths \(O(L+m\log m)\).

Given this rational allocation, feasibility and its weight are directly checkable. Popularity is checked by the linear program that minimizes

\(\sum_{t,p,q}v_t(p,q)z^t_{pq}\)

subject to exactly the challenger constraints in the question. It has

\(\sum_t(\deg(t)+1)^2\leq(2m+\tau)^2\)

variables and polynomially many constraints. Its input has polynomial bit length. The identity challenger is feasible and has objective zero; all variables are bounded by their row masses. Hence the allocation is popular exactly when the minimum is zero. Rational linear programming decides this in polynomial time.

The problem is therefore in \(\mathrm{NP}\), and the reduction proves that it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.
