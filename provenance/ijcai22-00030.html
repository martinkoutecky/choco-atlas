Corollary 8 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), ranking types \(T\subseteq L(C)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a piecewise-constant circular witness \(\varphi:\mathbb S^1\to T\) whose pairwise preference sets are circular intervals, and a target \(c^\star\), decide whether some minimizer of \(K_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,t)\) ranks \(c^\star\) first.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. Let \(L(C)\) be the linear orders on \(C\). The input gives rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) for \(t\in T\subseteq L(C)\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a piecewise-constant map \(\varphi:\mathbb S^1\to T\). Target. Is deciding whether some minimizer of \(K_\mu\) ranks a designated candidate \(c^\star\) first NP-hard, or solvable in polynomial time?

Result

Under the natural encoding in which \(\varphi\) is represented by a cyclic list of constant pieces, the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. In fact, hardness holds even when all pieces have equal length and each mass equals the total arc length occupied by its type.

1. Finite two-crossing hardness

For a finite profile \(P=(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\), write \(N_P(a,b)\) for the number of voters ranking \(a\) above \(b\), and define the majority margin \(M_P(a,b)=N_P(a,b)-N_P(b,a)\).

If a ranking \(\pi\) places \(a\) above \(b\), then the pair \(\{a,b\}\) contributes \(N_P(b,a)=(n-M_P(a,b))/2\) to its total Kendall–tau score. Consequently, two profiles on the same candidate set having identical majority margins have Kemeny scores differing by a constant independent of \(\pi\). They therefore have exactly the same Kemeny-optimal rankings and the same Kemeny winners.

The ordinary finite Kemeny-Winner problem, with the existential convention that \(c^\star\) is a winner when it is first in some optimal ranking, is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; indeed, a stronger complexity classification is known ([Hemaspaandra, Spakowski, and Vogel](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcs.2005.08.031)).

Given an \(n\)-voter profile, all its majority margins have the same parity, since \(M_P(a,b)\equiv n\pmod 2\), and their absolute values are at most \(n\). The constructive two-crossing Debord–McGarvey theorem produces, in polynomial time, a two-crossing profile with exactly these margins and \(O(|C|^2(n+1))\) voters. Thus finite Kemeny-Winner remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard on two-crossing profiles. This is also stated explicitly by Constantinescu and Wattenhofer ([Voting in Two-Crossing Elections](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2022/30)).

It remains to show that these finite hard instances embed in the stated mass formulation.

2. From a two-crossing order to circular intervals

Let \(Q=(q_1,\ldots,q_N)\) be a finite two-crossing profile in a witnessing voter order. Thus, for every distinct \(a,b\in C\), the binary sequence

\[ \mathbf 1[a\succ_{q_1}b],\ldots,\mathbf 1[a\succ_{q_N}b] \]

changes value at most twice between consecutive positions.

Let \(I_{a,b}=\{i:a\succ_{q_i}b\}\). We verify that \(I_{a,b}\) is a circular interval.

  • With no change, \(I_{a,b}\) is either empty or all of \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\).
  • With one change, \(I_{a,b}\) is a prefix or a suffix.
  • With two changes, the first and last bits agree. If they are \(0\), the \(1\)-indices form one ordinary interval. If they are \(1\), the \(1\)-indices form a suffix together with a prefix, hence one wrapping circular interval.

Therefore every \(I_{a,b}\) is a circular interval. This is precisely the finite equivalence between two-crossing profiles and single-interval-on-a-circle profiles proved in the cited paper.

3. Construction of the mass instance

Partition \(\mathbb S^1=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\) into the \(N\) equal half-open arcs

\[ A_i=\left[(i-1)/N,i/N\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N. \]

Define \(\varphi(x)=q_i\) for \(x\in A_i\). Let \(T\) be the set of distinct rankings occurring among the \(q_i\), and, for \(t\in T\), put

\[ n_t=\bigl|\{i:q_i=t\}\bigr|,\qquad \mu_t=n_t/N. \]

These masses are rational, positive, and sum to \(1\). Moreover,

\[ \mu_t=\lambda\bigl(\varphi^{-1}(t)\bigr), \]

where \(\lambda\) denotes normalized arc length. Thus the reduction remains valid even under the stronger interpretation that masses must equal the lengths of their type regions.

For distinct \(a,b\), the required preimage is

\[ \{x:a\succ_{\varphi(x)}b\}=\bigcup_{i\in I_{a,b}}A_i. \]

Because \(I_{a,b}\) is a circular interval of indices and the arcs occur in that cyclic order, this union is a circular interval in \(\mathbb S^1\). Hence the constructed instance satisfies the promised geometric restriction.

4. Exact preservation of the objective

For every \(\pi\in L(C)\),

\[ K_\mu(\pi) =\sum_{t\in T}\frac{n_t}{N}d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,t) =\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,q_i). \]

The right-hand side is exactly \(1/N\) times the finite Kemeny score of \(\pi\) in \(Q\). Multiplication by the positive constant \(1/N\) does not change the set of minimizers. Therefore,

\[ c^\star\text{ is first in some finite Kemeny optimum of }Q \]

if and only if

\[ c^\star\text{ is first in some minimizer of }K_\mu. \]

The construction uses \(N\) rational breakpoints with \(O(\log N)\)-bit denominators and is polynomial in the finite profile size.

Conclusion

The mass-distribution problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Independence between the masses and the arc lengths does not obstruct the reduction: the hard subclass already has \(\mu_t=\lambda(\varphi^{-1}(t))\). Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Overall assessment

The prover correctly establishes NP-hardness under the stated cyclic-list encoding. The reduction preserves the complete Kemeny objective up to positive scaling, and the constructed map satisfies the circular-interval restriction. No substantive gap was found.

Step-by-step audit

1. Dependence of Kemeny scores on majority margins

For each unordered pair \(\{a,b\}\), if \(\pi\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\), exactly \(N_P(b,a)\) voters disagree with \(\pi\) on that pair. The identity

\[ N_P(b,a)=\frac{n-M_P(a,b)}{2} \]

is correct.

Thus profiles with identical signed majority margins have Kemeny scores differing only by the baseline contribution caused by their possibly different numbers of voters. That difference is independent of \(\pi\), so their minimizing rankings and existential Kemeny winners coincide. This part is correct.

2. Hardness source and two-crossing realization

The cited finite Kemeny-Winner problem uses the relevant existential convention: a candidate wins if it is first in some Kemeny-optimal ranking. Its known stronger complexity classification implies NP-hardness.

For every pair \(a,b\),

\[ M_P(a,b)=2N_P(a,b)-n, \]

so all margins have parity \(n\), and \(\lvert M_P(a,b)\rvert\leq n\). These are exactly the parity and magnitude conditions checked by the prover.

The cited two-crossing Debord–McGarvey result states that every weighted majority tournament whose weights have a common parity is realizable by a two-crossing profile, with \(O(m^2W)\) voters when \(W\) is the maximum weight. It explicitly treats absent edges as having weight \(0\). Hence zero margins in even-voter profiles are covered. The paper also explicitly derives NP-hardness of Kemeny winner determination on two-crossing profiles. See [Theorem 14 and Corollary 15 of Constantinescu–Wattenhofer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00474).

Since \(W\leq n\), the claimed \(O(\lvert C\rvert^2(n+1))\) output bound is polynomial. The theorem’s construction also supplies the witnessing voter order. The application is valid.

3. Circular-interval property

A binary sequence with at most two changes has one of the forms correctly enumerated by the prover:

  • constant;
  • one prefix followed by one suffix;
  • three blocks with the first and last bits equal.

In the last case, the positions containing \(1\) are either an ordinary interval or a prefix together with a suffix, which is a wrapping circular interval. Therefore the set of voters preferring \(a\) to \(b\) is circularly consecutive.

This argument correctly translates the standard linear definition of two-crossingness into the required circular-interval property.

4. Construction of \(\varphi\) and the masses

The arcs \(A_i=[(i-1)/N,i/N)\) form a disjoint partition of \(\mathbb S^1\). Assigning \(q_i\) to \(A_i\) therefore defines a piecewise-constant map unambiguously.

For every type \(t\),

\[ \mu_t=\frac{n_t}{N} \]

is rational and positive because \(T\) contains exactly the rankings that occur. The masses sum to \(1\), and each equals the normalized total arc length of its preimage.

For each ordered candidate pair, the relevant preimage is the union of arcs indexed by a circular interval. Because the arcs occur in the same cyclic order, their union is a circular interval in \(\mathbb S^1\). Endpoint choices are consistent through the use of half-open arcs.

5. Preservation of the objective

The computation

\[ K_\mu(\pi) =\sum_{t\in T}\frac{n_t}{N}d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,t) =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,q_i) \]

is exact, including when a ranking type occurs multiple times. Scaling by \(1/N>0\) preserves the complete set of minimizers. Consequently, the existential condition that \(c^\star\) be first in some optimum is preserved exactly.

6. Encoding size

There are \(N\) explicitly represented arcs, with breakpoints having denominator \(N\) and therefore \(O(\log N)\) bits. Since \(N=O(\lvert C\rvert^2(n+1))\), both the number of pieces and their rational descriptions are polynomial in the explicit finite-profile input size.

The prover appropriately qualifies the result by specifying the cyclic-list encoding; without some finite representation of \(\varphi\), the original complexity question would not be formally defined.
