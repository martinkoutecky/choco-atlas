The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \((x,b)\), with \(b\in[0,B]\), can one compute a weighted median \(y\) of the public location mass such that \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_{(x,b)}\mu_{x,b}\bigl||x-y|-b\bigr|\le \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+2B\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list of \(\tau\) types \((x,b)\), each with positive mass \(\mu_{x,b}\) encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_{(x,b)}\mu_{x,b}=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Locations are public, \(x\in\mathbb{Q}\); preferred distances satisfy \(b\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,B]\), where \(B\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\) is given. One facility may be placed anywhere in \(\mathbb{R}\).
Define \(SC_\mu(z)=\sum_{(x,b)}\mu_{x,b}\bigl||x-z|-b\bigr|\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}SC_\mu(z)\). Use the lower weighted median of the location marginal, namely \(y=\min\{a\in\{x:(x,b)\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\}:\sum_{x\leq a}\mu_{x,b}\geq\tfrac12\}\); its selection uses only locations and masses.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length. State running times in \(\tau\) and \(L\); the spatial dimension is fixed at \(m=1\).

Can the lower weighted median \(y\) be computed in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\) with the guarantee \(SC_\mu(y)\leq\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+2B\) for every such society, or does this additive approximation guarantee fail?

For finite societies of \(n\) agents, the public-location median achieves additive error at most \(2nB\); a stronger \(nB\) bound is also established in one dimension. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18114v1)

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The guarantee holds. In fact, for every society in the question,
\(SC_\mu(y)\leq\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+B\).
The lower weighted median is computable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\). We prove this directly for weighted supports.

1. Median properties and continuity

Index the support by \((x_i,b_i)\), with masses \(\mu_i>0\), for \(i\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Define
\(M_-=\sum_{i:x_i\leq y}\mu_i\) and
\(M_+=\sum_{i:x_i\geq y}\mu_i\).

The definition of the lower weighted median gives \(M_-\geq\tfrac12\). It also gives \(\sum_{i:x_i<y}\mu_i<\tfrac12\): otherwise an earlier support location would meet the median threshold. Consequently, \(M_+>\tfrac12\).

Write \(c_i(z)=\bigl||x_i-z|-b_i\bigr|\). The reverse triangle inequality, applied twice, gives
\[ \]
For all real \(u,v\),
\(\lvert c_i(u)-c_i(v)\rvert\leq\bigl||x_i-u|-|x_i-v|\bigr|\leq|u-v|\).
Since the masses sum to one, it follows that
\(\lvert SC_\mu(u)-SC_\mu(v)\rvert\leq|u-v|\).
In particular, \(SC_\mu\) is continuous.

2. An optimum lies within distance \(B\) of the median

Set \(\ell=y-B\) and \(r=y+B\). We show that projecting any facility location into \([\ell,r]\) cannot increase social cost.

Suppose first that \(z>r\). For every type with \(x_i\leq y\), we have
\(r-x_i\geq B\geq b_i\).
Thus both facility locations lie beyond that type’s right preferred point, and
\(c_i(z)-c_i(r)=z-r\).

For every remaining type, the Lipschitz inequality gives
\(c_i(z)-c_i(r)\geq-(z-r)\).
Summing with the masses yields
\(SC_\mu(z)-SC_\mu(r)\geq(2M_--1)(z-r)\geq0\).

Now suppose that \(z<\ell\). For every type with \(x_i\geq y\), we have
\(x_i-\ell\geq B\geq b_i\).
Both facility locations therefore lie to the left of that type’s left preferred point, and
\(c_i(z)-c_i(\ell)=\ell-z\).

For every remaining type,
\(c_i(z)-c_i(\ell)\geq-(\ell-z)\).
Hence
\(SC_\mu(z)-SC_\mu(\ell)\geq(2M_+-1)(\ell-z)\geq0\).

Continuity ensures that \(SC_\mu\) attains a minimum on the compact interval \([\ell,r]\). The two inequalities above show that this minimum is also global. Let \(z^\star\in[\ell,r]\) be such a minimizer. Then \(|y-z^\star|\leq B\), so the Lipschitz inequality proves
\(SC_\mu(y)\leq SC_\mu(z^\star)+|y-z^\star|\leq\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+B\).

Since \(B\geq0\), this implies the requested bound with \(2B\). The stronger bound is the weighted, normalized analogue of the finite-society bound stated in the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18114v1).

3. Exact computation and running time

Write each mass as \(\mu_i=a_i/d_i\), with positive integers \(a_i,d_i\). Compute
\(D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}d_i\) and integer weights
\(A_i=a_i(D/d_i)\).
Normalization gives \(\sum_i A_i=D\).

Sort the support entries by their rational locations. Scan them in increasing order, grouping equal locations and accumulating their integer weights. Return the first location whose cumulative weight \(S\) satisfies \(2S\geq D\). This returns exactly the specified lower weighted median and uses only locations and masses.

The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because it is a product of denominators whose total encoding length is at most \(L\). Moreover, \(A_i\leq D\) and every cumulative sum is at most \(D\). Thus all integer arithmetic uses \(O(L)\)-bit operands. Rational location comparisons use cross multiplication, also with \(O(L)\)-bit operands.

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, constructing the weights takes \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations; sorting takes \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2)\); and scanning takes \(O(\tau L)\). A valid overall bound is therefore
\(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2)\) bit operations.

4. Sharpness of the stronger bound

For any rational \(B>0\), take two types \((0,B)\) and \((2B,B)\), each of mass \(\tfrac12\). The lower weighted median is \(y=0\), and \(SC_\mu(0)=B\). At \(z=B\), both types have zero cost, so \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=0\).

Thus the stronger additive bound \(B\) is attained even with two distinct support locations.
