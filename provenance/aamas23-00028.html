The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite \(G\), rational per-capita seat capacities \(q_g\), finite complete student types \(T\) with matroid-rank valuation oracles \(v_t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\), compute finite-support configuration masses \(\lambda_{t,B}\ge0\) over integral independent bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}\lambda_{t,B}\le q_g\), such that the increasing utility-quantile curve induced by \(v_t(B)\), with an optional fixed finite type-priority perturbation, Lorenz-dominates that of every feasible allocation \(\lambda'\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a finite set of \(m\) goods. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) complete types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not all possible types. Each type specifies a matroid-rank valuation oracle \(v_t\) on \(G\). Capacities \(q_g\ge0\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total explicit input bit length, and each oracle query costs at most \(c\).
Use either unperturbed utilities, with \(\delta_t=0\), or a supplied priority permutation \(\pi:T\to\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), with lower numbers indicating higher priority and \(\delta_t=\pi(t)/\tau^2\).
A feasible allocation consists of finitely many nonzero rational configuration masses \(\lambda_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(B\subseteq G\) is independent for type \(t\), satisfying \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}\lambda_{t,B}\le q_g\). Empty bundles and unused capacity are allowed; each student receives at most one seat of each good.
Let \(Q_\lambda\) be the increasing quantile function of the utility distribution assigning mass \(\lambda_{t,B}\) to \(v_t(B)+\delta_t\), and set \(C_\lambda(a)=\int_0^a Q_\lambda(s)\,ds\). An allocation is Lorenz dominating if \(C_\lambda(a)\ge C_{\lambda'}(a)\) for every \(a\in[0,1]\) and every feasible \(\lambda'\).

Is computing a Lorenz-dominating allocation polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L,c\), or is it NP-hard, for the unperturbed and fixed-priority versions?

For finitely many individual agents, Yankee Swap computes a non-redundant prioritized Lorenz-dominating allocation in \(O(m^2(n+c)(m+n))\) time, where \(n\) is the number of agents ([Viswanathan–Zick](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.08495)).
No result settling this exact configuration-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so capacities are measured per capita; required positive masses on the listed support and nonnegative capacities.
  • Made the optional perturbation precise: either zero offsets or the discrete priority-offset formula with support size replacing the number of agents, namely δ_t=π(t)/τ².
  • Interpreted Lorenz dominance as dominance of integrated increasing quantiles, including the total-utility endpoint, rather than pointwise dominance of quantiles.
  • Required an exact finite rational representation of the configuration masses and separated oracle-query cost from support size.
  • Retained empty bundles and unused capacity, and excluded unused capacity from the utility distribution.

Result

Both the unperturbed and fixed-priority versions are polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L,c\). A Lorenz-dominating allocation always exists, and one can compute a rational allocation having at most \(m+\tau\) positive configuration masses.

The proof has two separate parts: existence follows from a finite-population matroid argument; computation uses a configuration linear program with polynomial-time dual separation. The algorithm does not expand the rational masses into individual agents.

1. Lorenz dominance and capped utilities

Let \(\mathcal I_t\) denote the independent sets of type \(t\)'s matroid. Because configurations are independent, \(v_t(B)=|B|\) for \(B\in\mathcal I_t\).

For an allocation \(\lambda\), let \(U_\lambda\) be its utility random variable. For a real threshold \(z\), define

\(F_z(\lambda)=\mathbb E[\min\{U_\lambda,z\}]=\sum_{t,B\in\mathcal I_t}\lambda_{t,B}\min\{|B|+\delta_t,z\}.\)

These are linear functions of the configuration masses.

The following identities connect them to the Lorenz curve:

\(C_\lambda(a)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb R}\bigl(F_z(\lambda)-(1-a)z\bigr),\)

\(F_z(\lambda)=\inf_{a\in[0,1]}\bigl(C_\lambda(a)+(1-a)z\bigr).\)

To verify them, write \(Q=Q_\lambda\). For every \(a,z\),

\(az-C_\lambda(a)=\int_0^a(z-Q(s))\,ds\le\int_0^1(z-Q(s))_+\,ds=z-F_z(\lambda).\)

Equality holds when \(z\) is an \(a\)-quantile. At the endpoints, choose \(z\) below every utility when \(a=0\), and above every utility when \(a=1\). Conversely, for fixed \(z\), equality holds upon choosing \(a=\Pr(U_\lambda<z)\). This proves both identities.

Consequently, Lorenz dominance is equivalent to simultaneous maximization of every \(F_z\).

Only polynomially many thresholds are needed. Define

\(H=\{k+\delta_t:t\in T,\ k\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\}.\)

Every attainable utility belongs to \(H\), and \(|H|\le\tau(m+1)\). As a function of \(z\), each \(F_z(\lambda)\) is linear between consecutive elements of \(H\). Below its smallest element it equals \(z\); above its largest element it equals \(\mathbb E[U_\lambda]\). Therefore,

\(F_h(\lambda)\ge F_h(\lambda')\text{ for every }h\in H\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_\lambda(a)\ge C_{\lambda'}(a)\text{ for every }a\in[0,1].\)

We next prove that the capped objectives have a common maximizer.

2. A finite-population lemma

Lemma. Suppose finitely many agents have matroids on a common finite set \(E\). Agent \(i\)'s utility from an independent bundle \(B_i\) is \(|B_i|+b_i\), where the offsets \(b_i\) are fixed real numbers. Bundles must be pairwise disjoint; empty bundles and unassigned goods are allowed.

There is an allocation simultaneously maximizing

\(\sum_i\min\{|B_i|+b_i,z\}\)

for every real \(z\).

In particular, offsets may coincide. This will allow all copies of a type to retain exactly the supplied type offset.

2.1. Characterizing feasible bundle sizes

Let \(N\) be the finite agent set, and let \(r_i\) be agent \(i\)'s matroid rank function. For \(S\subseteq N\), define

\(f(S)=\min_{A\subseteq E}\left(|E\setminus A|+\sum_{i\in S}r_i(A)\right).\)

We claim that a nonnegative integer vector \(x=(x_i)_{i\in N}\) is realizable as the sizes of pairwise disjoint independent bundles precisely when

\(x(S):=\sum_{i\in S}x_i\le f(S)\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq N.\)

Necessity. Given such bundles, for every \(S,A\),

\(\sum_{i\in S}|B_i|\le |E\setminus A|+\sum_{i\in S}r_i(A).\)

Indeed, at most \(|E\setminus A|\) assigned goods lie outside \(A\), while \(|B_i\cap A|\le r_i(A)\). Taking the minimum over \(A\) proves necessity.

Sufficiency. Truncate agent \(i\)'s matroid at cardinality \(x_i\); its rank on \(A\) becomes \(\min\{r_i(A),x_i\}\). The matroid-union theorem states that the largest union of independent sets of these truncated matroids has cardinality

\(\min_{A\subseteq E}\left(|E\setminus A|+\sum_i\min\{r_i(A),x_i\}\right).\)

The theorem applies because all truncations are matroids on the same finite ground set. See [Oxley, *Matroid Theory*, Theorem 11.3.1 and formula 11.3.3](https://www.math.ens.psl.eu/~benoist/refs/Oxley.pdf#page=440).

For a fixed \(A\), put \(S_A=\{i:x_i>r_i(A)\}\). The assumed inequalities give

\(\sum_{i\in S_A}x_i\le f(S_A)\le |E\setminus A|+\sum_{i\in S_A}r_i(A).\)

Equivalently,

\(|E\setminus A|+\sum_i\min\{r_i(A),x_i\}\ge\sum_i x_i.\)

Thus the union has cardinality \(\sum_i x_i\). Its constituent independent sets can be made disjoint by deleting overlaps. Since each constituent has size at most \(x_i\), a union of cardinality \(\sum_i x_i\) forces their sizes to be exactly \(x_i\). This establishes sufficiency.

2.2. Submodularity of the size constraints

The function \(f\) is nonnegative, integer-valued, monotone, and satisfies \(f(\varnothing)=0\). These facts follow directly from its definition.

It is also submodular. Write

\(\Phi(S,A)=|E\setminus A|+\sum_{i\in S}r_i(A).\)

For \(S_1,S_2\subseteq N\), choose minimizing sets \(A_1,A_2\). Rank submodularity and monotonicity imply

\(\Phi(S_1,A_1)+\Phi(S_2,A_2)\ge \Phi(S_1\cup S_2,A_1\cap A_2)+ \Phi(S_1\cap S_2,A_1\cup A_2).\)

Here agents in \(S_1\cap S_2\) contribute the rank-submodularity inequality. An agent belonging only to \(S_1\) contributes \(r_i(A_1)\ge r_i(A_1\cap A_2)\), and similarly for \(S_2\). The two complement-cardinality terms satisfy equality.

Taking the respective minima on the right yields

\(f(S_1)+f(S_2)\ge f(S_1\cup S_2)+f(S_1\cap S_2).\)

2.3. A matroid on utility increments

Introduce slots

\(V=\{(i,k):i\in N,\ 1\le k\le r_i(E)\}.\)

For \(I\subseteq V\), let \(x_i(I)\) be the number of slots of agent \(i\) contained in \(I\). Declare \(I\) independent when

\(x(I)(S)\le f(S)\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq N.\)

We verify that this defines a matroid. The empty set is independent, and independence is hereditary. It remains to prove augmentation.

Let \(I,J\) be independent with \(|I|<|J|\). Suppose no slot in \(J\setminus I\) can be added to \(I\). For each such slot belonging to agent \(i\), some set \(S_i\) containing \(i\) must be tight:

\(x(I)(S_i)=f(S_i).\)

This follows because both sides are integers, and adding that slot increases the left side by one.

Tight sets are closed under union. Indeed, if \(A,B\) are tight, then feasibility and submodularity give

\(f(A)+f(B)=x(I)(A\cup B)+x(I)(A\cap B)\le f(A\cup B)+f(A\cap B)\le f(A)+f(B).\)

Thus both \(A\cup B\) and \(A\cap B\) are tight.

Let \(S\) be the union of the selected tight sets. Every slot of \(J\setminus I\) belongs to an agent in \(S\). Therefore, outside \(S\), the slots of \(J\) are a subset of those of \(I\). Inside \(S\),

\(x(J)(S)\le f(S)=x(I)(S).\)

Adding these inequalities gives \(|J|\le|I|\), a contradiction. Hence augmentation holds.

By Section 2.1, an independent slot set corresponds exactly to a feasible bundle-size vector.

2.4. One greedy order optimizes every cap

Order the slots by increasing

\(b_i+k-1,\)

breaking ties arbitrarily, and run the ordinary matroid greedy algorithm in this order: accept a slot whenever independence is preserved. Let \(I^\star\) be its output.

For a threshold \(z\), assign slot \((i,k)\) the weight

\(w_z(i,k)=\min\{b_i+k,z\}-\min\{b_i+k-1,z\} =\min\{1,\max\{0,z-(b_i+k-1)\}\}.\)

These weights are nonnegative and nonincreasing in the chosen slot order, for every \(z\).

For completeness, greedy maximizes any such weight sequence. Let the ordered slots be \(e_1,\ldots,e_s\), and let \(R_j=\{e_1,\ldots,e_j\}\). Greedy's accepted slots in \(R_j\) form a maximal independent subset of \(R_j\), hence have maximum possible cardinality by augmentation. Thus, for any independent \(J\),

\(|I^\star\cap R_j|\ge |J\cap R_j|.\)

For nonincreasing nonnegative weights \(w_1,\ldots,w_s\), with \(w_{s+1}=0\),

\(\sum_{e_j\in I^\star}w_j =\sum_{j=1}^s(w_j-w_{j+1})|I^\star\cap R_j| \ge\sum_{j=1}^s(w_j-w_{j+1})|J\cap R_j| =\sum_{e_j\in J}w_j.\)

Moreover, greedy accepts a prefix of each agent's slots. If one slot of an agent is rejected, its corresponding count increment is infeasible. Subsequent accepted slots only increase counts, so downward closure prevents any later slot of that agent from becoming feasible.

Let \(x_i^\star\) be the resulting prefix lengths. They are realizable by disjoint independent bundles. For any feasible size vector \(x\), its prefix slot set is independent, and telescoping gives

\(\sum_i\min\{b_i+x_i,z\} =\sum_i\min\{b_i,z\}+\sum_i\sum_{k=1}^{x_i}w_z(i,k).\)

Greedy's optimality therefore proves that \(x^\star\) maximizes this expression for every \(z\). This proves the lemma.

3. Existence for rational configuration masses

Let \(\mathcal P\) be the configuration polytope, temporarily allowing real masses. It has finitely many variables and rational constraints. It is nonempty because every type can receive the empty bundle, and bounded because \(\lambda_{t,B}\le\mu_t\).

For each \(h\in H\), choose a rational maximizer \(\lambda^h\) of \(F_h\) over \(\mathcal P\). Such a maximizer exists because this is a bounded rational linear program with a rational objective.

Choose an integer \(D\) clearing the denominators of:

  • every \(\mu_t\) and \(q_g\);
  • every configuration mass appearing in the finitely many allocations \(\lambda^h\).

Construct a finite instance with \(D\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each retaining offset \(\delta_t\), and \(Dq_g\) copies of good \(g\). There are exactly \(D\) agents.

Each agent's matroid on copied goods is the parallel extension of its original matroid: a set of copies is independent precisely when it contains at most one copy of each original good and its projection is originally independent. This is a matroid, including when some goods have no copies.

Every \(\lambda^h\) is realizable in this instance. Assign \(D\lambda^h_{t,B}\) agents bundle \(B\), and give its occurrences distinct good copies. The capacity inequalities ensure that enough copies exist.

Apply the finite-population lemma. Its allocation maximizes every capped-utility sum and hence performs at least as well as every \(\lambda^h\). Aggregate its bundles by type and divide their counts by \(D\), obtaining a feasible rational configuration allocation \(\lambda^\star\). For every \(h\in H\),

\(F_h(\lambda^\star)\ge F_h(\lambda^h)=\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal P}F_h(\lambda).\)

Thus \(\lambda^\star\) simultaneously maximizes all the capped objectives. Section 1 implies that it Lorenz dominates every feasible allocation.

This replication establishes existence; it is not part of the algorithm.

4. A single polynomial-time optimization problem

Define the rational coefficients

\(A_t(k)=\sum_{h\in H}\min\{k+\delta_t,h\},\qquad 0\le k\le m.\)

Consider the configuration linear program with objective

\(\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal P}\sum_{t,B\in\mathcal I_t}A_t(|B|)\lambda_{t,B} =\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal P}\sum_{h\in H}F_h(\lambda).\)

Every optimum is Lorenz dominating. To see this, let \(M_h=\max_{\lambda\in\mathcal P}F_h(\lambda)\). Section 3 supplies a feasible allocation attaining every \(M_h\), so the optimum of the summed objective is \(\sum_hM_h\). At any optimum \(\widehat\lambda\), all deficits \(M_h-F_h(\widehat\lambda)\) are nonnegative and sum to zero. Each deficit is therefore zero. Section 1 then gives Lorenz dominance.

It remains to compute an optimum efficiently.

4.1. Dual separation by weighted matroid greedy

The dual has variables \(\alpha_t\in\mathbb R\) and \(p_g\ge0\), objective

\(\min\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t+\sum_gq_gp_g,\)

and constraints

\(\alpha_t+\sum_{g\in B}p_g\ge A_t(|B|)\qquad(t\in T,\ B\in\mathcal I_t).\)

Given a candidate dual vector, first check \(p_g\ge0\). For each type, separation requires computing

\(\max_{B\in\mathcal I_t}\left(A_t(|B|)-\sum_{g\in B}p_g\right).\)

Sort the goods by increasing \(p_g\), and greedily accept a good whenever it preserves independence in type \(t\)'s matroid. Let the accepted sequence have prefixes \(B_{t,0},\ldots,B_{t,r_t}\), where \(r_t=v_t(G)\).

For every \(k\le r_t\), \(B_{t,k}\) is a minimum-weight independent set of cardinality \(k\). This is weighted matroid greedy applied to the rank-\(k\) truncation. More explicitly, use nonnegative weights \(K-p_g\), where \(K=\max_g p_g\): maximizing their sum over cardinality-\(k\) independent sets is equivalent to minimizing \(\sum_gp_g\).

Consequently, the required maximum equals

\(\max_{0\le k\le r_t}\left(A_t(k)-\sum_{g\in B_{t,k}}p_g\right).\)

If this exceeds \(\alpha_t\), the maximizing prefix supplies an actual violated dual constraint. Otherwise, every configuration constraint for that type is satisfied.

Independence is tested by one rank query: for an independent current set \(B\), the good \(g\) can be accepted precisely when \(v_t(B\cup\{g\})=|B|+1\). A separation call therefore uses at most \(\tau m\) rank queries, together with polynomially many rational operations.

4.2. Exact recovery and output size

We use the constructive rational-LP theorem for column access: a feasible linear program with bounded optimum, polynomially many rows, uniformly bounded coefficient encoding lengths, and a polynomial-time dual separation oracle returning violated original column constraints admits polynomial-time exact optimization and sparse primal recovery. The ellipsoid method identifies polynomially many relevant columns, after which a polynomial-size restricted primal is solved. This is the standard configuration-LP recovery described in [Roughgarden, Section 8.3.2, including footnote 9](https://www.nowpublishers.com/article/DownloadSummary/TCS-085#page=51).

All hypotheses hold here:

  • There are \(m+\tau\) primal rows.
  • The primal is feasible and bounded.
  • Configuration columns have incidence coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\).
  • Each column is represented by a type and an \(m\)-bit bundle.
  • The preceding separation routine returns an actual violated column constraint.

The objective coefficients also have polynomial encoding length. In both versions, \(0\le\delta_t\le1\), and \(|H|\le\tau(m+1)\). In the priority version all utility levels, and hence all \(A_t(k)\), have denominator dividing \(\tau^2\); in the unperturbed version the coefficients are integers. Their magnitudes are at most \(\tau(m+1)^2\).

Thus exact LP optimization takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,c\), including oracle costs.

Finally, choose an optimal basic feasible solution of the recovered restricted primal. Adding capacity slacks gives \(m+\tau\) equality rows, so this solution has at most \(m+\tau\) positive configuration variables. Their rational encoding lengths are polynomial: basic solutions solve systems of order at most \(m+\tau\) with integer incidence coefficients and the supplied rational right-hand side.

5. Conclusion

The algorithm computes the possible utility levels, forms the summed capped-utility objective, and solves its configuration LP using weighted matroid greedy for dual separation. Every optimal allocation Lorenz dominates all feasible allocations.

The proof covers both \(\delta_t=0\) and the exact supplied offsets \(\delta_t=\pi(t)/\tau^2\), including repeated offsets among replicated agents and arbitrary positive rational type masses.
