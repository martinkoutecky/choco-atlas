The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite bundle set \(B\), voter or student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita course supplies \(q_j\), and integral bundles for each individual, let \(\Omega\) be the feasible mass plans \(x_{t,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}a_j(b)x_{t,b}\le q_j\). Given a sampler for a fair prior \(F\) over \(\Omega\), a mechanism returning \(A\in\Omega\) with \(W(A)\ge\lambda\max_{x\in\Omega}W(x)\), and \(\alpha,\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), construct a sampler for a lottery \(Q_\varepsilon\) over plans such that \(\operatorname{TV}(Q_\varepsilon,F)\le\alpha\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W(Q_\varepsilon)]\ge\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\), where \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha=\sup_{Q:\operatorname{TV}(Q,F)\le\alpha}\mathbb{E}[W(Q)]\). Theorem 3.1's algorithm uses \(s=\left\lceil 8((1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)^{-1}\log(2/\varepsilon)\right\rceil\) samples from \(F\), in addition to the supplied welfare mechanism.

Assume \(\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\tau\times|B|}:\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t,\ \sum_{t,b}a_j(b)x_{t,b}\le q_j\}\ne\varnothing\).
Welfare \(W:\Omega\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) is bounded and Borel measurable, with an attained maximum; lottery welfare is \(\int_\Omega W\,dQ\). No additivity is assumed.
Access consists of independent exact samples from a designated fair Borel prior \(F\), exact welfare comparisons, and a supplied mechanism returning \(A\in\Omega\) with \(W(A)\ge\lambda\max_{x\in\Omega}W(x)\). Plans may be opaque exact handles; oracle costs are charged separately.

Resolution

The guarantee is achievable in the stated oracle model. In fact, it suffices to use \(n=\left\lceil((1-\alpha)\varepsilon^2)^{-1}\right\rceil\) prior samples, which is at most \(s\).

The sampler uses the fractional trimming construction from Algorithm 1 of the [cited discrete interpolation paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.03071). The proof below establishes its validity for Borel probability measures directly, including nonatomic priors and welfare ties.

1. Definitions and sampler

Write \(r=1-\alpha\), and let \(M=\max_{x\in\Omega}W(x)\). This maximum exists by hypothesis. The feasible set \(\Omega\) is a closed, bounded subset of the finite-dimensional space \(\mathbb R^{\tau|B|}\), equipped with its Borel sets.

Define total variation by \(\operatorname{TV}(P,F)=\sup_{E\text{ Borel}}|P(E)-F(E)|\).

Set \(n=\lceil(r\varepsilon^2)^{-1}\rceil\). Since \(8\log(2/\varepsilon)>1\), we have \(n\le s\).

For each draw, run the following algorithm:

  • With probability \(\alpha\), call the supplied welfare mechanism and return its output.
  • Otherwise, draw independent \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) from \(F\).
  • Sort these samples in decreasing welfare order, obtaining \(X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}\). Break welfare ties by original sample index.
  • Put \(k=\lfloor rn\rfloor\) and \(\theta=rn-k\). Assign weights \(w_i=1\) for \(i\le k\), \(w_{k+1}=\theta\), and \(w_i=0\) for \(i\ge k+2\).
  • Return \(X_{(i)}\) with probability \(w_i/(rn)\).

Here \(k<n\), because \(r<1\). The weights satisfy \(0\le w_i\le1\) and \(\sum_iw_i=rn\).

Only comparisons of sampled welfare values are needed. Because \(W\) is Borel, sorting with the prescribed tie rule is a Borel operation. Thus the sampling branch defines a Borel output law.

Let \(G\) denote the welfare mechanism’s output law. For a deterministic mechanism, \(G=\delta_A\). In either case, its guarantee gives \(\int_\Omega W\,dG\ge\lambda M\).

2. Exact total-variation guarantee

Define a finite Borel measure \(R\) by

\[ \text{[Equation]} \]

\(R(E)=\mathbb E\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\mathbf1_{\{X_{(i)}\in E\}}\right]\).

Its total mass is \(R(\Omega)=r\). Moreover, for every Borel set \(E\),

\(R(E)\le\mathbb E\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{X_{(i)}\in E\}}\right]=\mathbb E\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{X_i\in E\}}\right]=F(E)\).

Consequently, \(D=F-R\) is a nonnegative measure of mass \(\alpha\).

The unconditional output law is \(Q_\varepsilon=R+\alpha G\): the sampling branch has conditional law \(R/r\) and is used with probability \(r\). Therefore, for every Borel \(E\),

\(|Q_\varepsilon(E)-F(E)|=|\alpha G(E)-D(E)|\le\alpha\),

because both \(\alpha G(E)\) and \(D(E)\) belong to \([0,\alpha]\). Taking the supremum proves

\(\operatorname{TV}(Q_\varepsilon,F)\le\alpha\).

This argument concerns the unconditional law and requires no discreteness assumption.

3. Characterization of the optimal welfare

For finite nonnegative Borel measures, write \(\nu\le F\) when \(\nu(E)\le F(E)\) for every Borel \(E\). Define

\(H_r=\sup_{\nu\le F,\ \nu(\Omega)=r}\int_\Omega W\,d\nu\).

This supremum is finite, since \(W\le M\), and its feasible set is nonempty, since \(rF\) is feasible. We prove the identity

\(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha=\alpha M+H_r\).

Lower bound. Choose a maximizer \(x^\star\) of \(W\). For any feasible \(\nu\), the measure \(P_\nu=\nu+\alpha\delta_{x^\star}\) is a probability measure. Since \(F-\nu\) has mass \(\alpha\), the argument from Section 2 gives \(\operatorname{TV}(P_\nu,F)\le\alpha\). Its welfare is \(\int W\,d\nu+\alpha M\). Taking the supremum over \(\nu\) proves the lower bound.

Upper bound. Let \(P\) be any probability measure with \(\beta=\operatorname{TV}(P,F)\le\alpha\). Take the dominating finite measure \(\rho=P+F\), and Radon–Nikodym densities \(p=dP/d\rho\) and \(f=dF/d\rho\).

The measure \(C\) with density \(\min(p,f)\) satisfies \(C\le P,F\). Because \(\int(p-f)\,d\rho=0\), the positive and negative parts of \(p-f\) have equal integrals, and

\(\beta=\frac12\int|p-f|\,d\rho,\qquad C(\Omega)=1-\beta\).

Since \(1-\beta\ge r>0\), define \(\nu=\frac{r}{1-\beta}C\). Then \(\nu\le P,F\) and \(\nu(\Omega)=r\). Hence \(P-\nu\) is nonnegative and has mass \(\alpha\). Using \(W\le M\), we obtain

\(\int W\,dP=\int W\,d\nu+\int W\,d(P-\nu)\le H_r+\alpha M\).

Taking the supremum over feasible \(P\) proves the identity. No attainment of \(H_r\) is needed.

4. Expected welfare of empirical trimming

Define the retained empirical welfare

\(T=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n w_iW(X_{(i)})\).

We will prove \(\mathbb ET\ge(1-\varepsilon)H_r\).

A deterministic comparison

For any nonnegative values \(y_1,\ldots,y_n\), the prescribed weights on their decreasing ordering maximize \(\sum_i v_i y_{(i)}\) among weights satisfying \(0\le v_i\le1\) and \(\sum_i v_i=rn\).

To verify this, put \(c=y_{(k+1)}\). For any feasible \(v\),

\(\sum_i(w_i-v_i)y_{(i)}=\sum_i(w_i-v_i)(y_{(i)}-c)\ge0\).

Indeed, for \(i\le k\), both factors are nonnegative; for \(i=k+1\), the second factor is zero; and for \(i\ge k+2\), both factors are nonpositive.

Now choose any subset of \(c_0\ge1\) sample indices. If \(c_0\le rn\), assign weight one to these indices and distribute the remaining weight among the other indices. This is feasible, and nonnegativity of the values gives

\(T\ge\frac1n\sum_{\text{chosen indices}}y_i\).

If \(c_0>rn\), assigning weight \(rn/c_0\) to each chosen index is feasible, giving

\(T\ge\frac{r}{c_0}\sum_{\text{chosen indices}}y_i\).

Together, these inequalities say

\(T\ge\min(r,c_0/n)\frac1{c_0}\sum_{\text{chosen indices}}y_i\).

Comparison with an arbitrary retained submeasure

Fix any \(\nu\le F\) with \(\nu(\Omega)=r\), and define probability measures \(P_+=\nu/r\) and \(P_-=(F-\nu)/\alpha\). Then \(F=rP_++\alpha P_-\).

For analysis only, generate each prior sample by independently choosing a label \(+\) with probability \(r\), and then sampling from \(P_+\) or \(P_-\) according to its label. The resulting samples are still independent with law \(F\).

Let \(C_n\) count the \(+\) labels. Thus \(C_n\) has binomial distribution with parameters \(n,r\). Conditional on \(C_n=c_0\ge1\), the expected average welfare of the \(+\)-labelled samples is

\(\int W\,dP_+=\frac1r\int W\,d\nu\).

Apply the deterministic comparison to those labelled samples. For \(c_0=0\), use \(T\ge0\). Averaging gives

\(\mathbb ET\ge\mathbb E[\min(r,C_n/n)]\,\frac1r\int W\,d\nu\).

These labels are solely a proof device; the algorithm does not construct \(P_+\), \(P_-\), or the labels.

Binomial estimate

Put \(Z=C_n/n\). Then \(\mathbb EZ=r\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Z)=r(1-r)/n\). Since \(Z-r\) has mean zero,

\(\mathbb E(r-Z)_+=\frac12\mathbb E|Z-r|\).

Therefore, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\(\mathbb E\min(r,Z)=r-\frac12\mathbb E|Z-r|\ge r-\frac12\sqrt{\frac{r(1-r)}n}\).

Our choice \(nr\ge\varepsilon^{-2}\) implies

\(\mathbb E\min(r,Z)\ge r\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\ge r(1-\varepsilon)\).

Consequently, \(\mathbb ET\ge(1-\varepsilon)\int W\,d\nu\). Since this holds for every feasible \(\nu\), taking the supremum proves

\(\mathbb ET\ge(1-\varepsilon)H_r\).

5. Welfare guarantee

The output law satisfies

\(\int_\Omega W\,dQ_\varepsilon=\alpha\int_\Omega W\,dG+\mathbb ET\ge\alpha\lambda M+(1-\varepsilon)H_r\).

Because \(M,H_r\ge0\) and \(0<\lambda\le1\),

\(\alpha\lambda M+(1-\varepsilon)H_r\ge\lambda(1-\varepsilon)(\alpha M+H_r)=\lambda(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha\).

This proves the requested approximation.

6. Oracle use and computational overhead

Each draw uses at most \(n\le s\) prior samples and at most one welfare-mechanism call. Sorting requires \(O(n\log n)\) exact welfare comparisons.

All selection probabilities are rational and have bit length polynomial in \(L+\log n\). They can be sampled exactly using fair random bits: generate a uniform integer below the relevant denominator by rejection from a power-of-two range. Each rejection procedure has expected fewer than two attempts.

The sampler stores and returns handles, so it need not inspect or approximate plan coordinates. Its additional work consists of sorting, handle operations, and rational arithmetic. Since \(n=O((1-\alpha)^{-1}\varepsilon^{-2})\), the expected overhead is polynomial in the parameters requested, with oracle costs charged separately.
