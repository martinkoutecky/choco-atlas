The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given one agent type, a finite set \(J\) of repeatable item classes, rational per-capita supply \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{J}\), binary weights \(w\in\{0,1\}^{J}\), and an explicitly represented finite downward-closed family \(\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{J}\) of integral feasible configurations, define \(v(B)=\sum_{j\in J}w_jB_j\) for \(B\in\mathcal{F}\) and \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)=\max\{z:\exists y_B\ge0,\ \sum_B y_B=1,\ \sum_B y_BB_j\le q_j,\ y_B>0\Rightarrow v(B)\ge z\}\). Compute, or decide the existence of, a finite-support mass allocation \(x_B\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_B=1\), \(\sum_Bx_BB_j\le q_j\), and \(x_B>0\Rightarrow v(B)\ge(2/3+\varepsilon)\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) denotes support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(\tau=1\), with total mass \(1\).
Let \(J\) contain \(m\ge1\) repeatable item classes, with per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{J}\) and weights \(w\in\{0,1\}^{J}\). The input explicitly lists every configuration in a nonempty finite family \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{J}\), with coordinates encoded in binary; downward closure means \(B\in\mathcal F\) and \(0\le C\le B\) coordinatewise imply \(C\in\mathcal F\).
For \(B\in\mathcal F\), let \(v(B)=\sum_{j\in J}w_jB_j\). Define \(\mathcal P(q)=\{y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\mathcal F}:\sum_{B\in\mathcal F}y_B=1,\ \sum_{B\in\mathcal F}y_BB_j\le q_j\text{ for every }j\in J\}\) and \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)=\max_{y\in\mathcal P(q)}\min_{B:y_B>0}v(B)\).
An allocation consists of rational masses \(x\in\mathcal P(q)\); each positive mass receives its integral configuration, and unused supply is permitted. Let \(K=|\mathcal F|\) and \(L\) be the total binary input length.

For every fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon\le1/3\), can an allocation satisfying \(x_B>0\Rightarrow v(B)\ge(2/3+\varepsilon)\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\) be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is achieving this approximation NP-hard?

For indivisible items, computing a \((2/3+\varepsilon)\)-approximate MMS allocation is strongly NP-hard even for identical agents, binary item weights, and explicitly listed hereditary independent sets. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.11582v1).
No result settling this exact mass-allocation formulation was located in the literature search.

Result

The mass-allocation problem is polynomial-time solvable, even exactly. One can compute both \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\) and a rational allocation \(x\) satisfying
\[ x_B>0\ \Longrightarrow\ v(B)\ge \operatorname{HMMS}(q). \]
Consequently, the requested approximation is computable for every rational \(0<\varepsilon\le 1/3\).

The proof uses the explicit configuration list and the permission to allocate arbitrary rational masses.

1. Threshold feasibility

For a threshold \(t\), define
\[ \mathcal F_t=\{B\in\mathcal F:v(B)\ge t\}. \]
Consider the following linear feasibility problem, denoted by \(\mathrm{LP}(t)\):
\[ \begin{aligned} y_B&\ge0 &&(B\in\mathcal F_t),\\ \sum_{B\in\mathcal F_t}y_B&=1,\\ \sum_{B\in\mathcal F_t}B_jy_B&\le q_j &&(j\in J). \end{aligned} \]

Lemma. The problem \(\mathrm{LP}(t)\) is feasible if and only if there exists \(y\in\mathcal P(q)\) such that every positive-mass configuration has value at least \(t\).

Proof. Extend any feasible solution of \(\mathrm{LP}(t)\) by setting \(y_B=0\) for \(B\notin\mathcal F_t\). Its normalization and supply constraints are exactly those defining \(\mathcal P(q)\), and its support is contained in \(\mathcal F_t\).

Conversely, if \(y\in\mathcal P(q)\) has the stated guarantee, then \(y_B=0\) whenever \(v(B)<t\). Restricting \(y\) to \(\mathcal F_t\) therefore gives a feasible solution of \(\mathrm{LP}(t)\). This proves both directions.

2. Only finitely many thresholds need testing

Let the distinct configuration values, in increasing order, be
\[ a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_r,\qquad r\le K. \]

Because \(\mathcal F\) is nonempty and downward closed, it contains the zero configuration. Giving that configuration mass \(1\) is feasible because \(q_j\ge0\) for every \(j\). Thus \(\mathcal P(q)\) is nonempty, and \(a_1=0\) is feasible.

For every \(y\in\mathcal P(q)\), its support is nonempty because its masses sum to \(1\). Its minimum support value is therefore one of the \(a_i\). By the lemma,
\[ \operatorname{HMMS}(q) =\max\{a_i:\mathrm{LP}(a_i)\text{ is feasible}\}. \]
This also proves that the maximum in the definition of \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\) is attained: the displayed set is finite and nonempty, and feasibility at its largest member supplies an attaining allocation.

3. Algorithm, rationality, and running time

Use the following algorithm:

  • Compute every \(v(B)\) and sort the distinct values.
  • Test \(\mathrm{LP}(a_i)\) for each candidate value.
  • Let \(t^\star\) be the largest feasible candidate.
  • Compute a rational feasible solution of \(\mathrm{LP}(t^\star)\), extend it by zero outside \(\mathcal F_{t^\star}\), and output the resulting \(x\).

The computational theorem used here is polynomial-time solvability of rational linear programming in the binary encoding model: feasibility can be decided, and a rational feasible point can be produced when one exists, in time polynomial in the encoded system size. This applies to exact feasibility, including systems whose feasible region has empty interior. See [MIT’s notes on linear programming and the ellipsoid method](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-854j-advanced-algorithms-fall-2008/908fe54d659623efadda2d033b895caf_notes_lp.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here. Each threshold problem has at most \(K\) variables, \(m\) supply inequalities, one normalization equality, and nonnegativity constraints. All coefficients are explicitly given integers or rationals from the input. Filtering configurations does not enlarge their encodings.

Computing \(v(B)\) requires adding at most \(m\) binary integers, so each value has bit length bounded by the largest coordinate bit length plus \(O(\log(m+1))\). Sorting consequently takes polynomial time. There are at most \(K\) LP calls, each of polynomial encoded size. Hence the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,K,L\), and therefore in \(m,\tau,K,L\) with \(\tau=1\).

For completeness, real feasibility cannot conceal a requirement for irrational or excessively long masses. In a feasible threshold LP, introduce nonnegative supply slacks. The resulting system has \(m+1\) equations. Choose a feasible solution having the fewest positive coordinates. Its corresponding columns must be linearly independent: otherwise a nonzero dependence permits a perturbation preserving the equations and nonnegativity until one positive coordinate becomes zero, contradicting minimality. Thus there are at most \(m+1\) positive coordinates.

After clearing the rational supply denominators row by row, select a square nonsingular subsystem on those columns. Cramer’s rule expresses each positive coordinate as a ratio of integer determinants. The matrix entries have \(O(L)\) bits, and Hadamard’s inequality bounds those determinants’ bit lengths by
\[ O\bigl((m+1)(L+\log(m+1))\bigr). \]
Thus a rational allocation of polynomial output length exists, consistently with the exact LP algorithm.

By construction and Section 2,
\[ x_B>0\ \Longrightarrow\ v(B)\ge t^\star=\operatorname{HMMS}(q). \]
Since \(\operatorname{HMMS}(q)\ge0\) and \(2/3+\varepsilon\le1\), this implies the requested guarantee.

4. Why the indivisible-item hardness does not transfer

The cited hardness result concerns allocations to finitely many agents, and its reduction requires an integral partition into admissible triples. See [Theorem 4.1 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.11582v1).

For example, representing \(n\) identical agents by mass \(1\) would force configuration masses to have the form \(c_B/n\), where each \(c_B\) is a nonnegative integer counting agents. The present formulation imposes no such denominator restriction. Its supply constraints instead allow arbitrary rational mixtures of configurations.

The integral partition requirement that drives the cited reduction is therefore absent. Every individual still receives an integral configuration; the masses assigned to those configurations are the variables solved by linear programming.
