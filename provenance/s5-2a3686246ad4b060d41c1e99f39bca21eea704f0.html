The continuous mirror question, as graded

A bracket is a permutation of the candidates paired in order over successive knockout rounds; each match is won by the candidate preferred by more than half the society. Describe the input and running time in \(m,\tau,L\).

For every \(n\geq3\), explicitly constructed strict majority graphs admit every candidate as a knockout winner under a suitable bracket.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The key is that every tournament can be represented by a polynomial-size support of distinct strict rankings with positive dyadic masses.

1. Source problem and bracket equivalence

We use the following established result: given a deterministic tournament on a power-of-two number of players and a designated player, deciding whether some balanced knockout bracket makes that player win is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is Theorem 1 of [Aziz et al., *Fixing balanced knockout and double elimination tournaments*](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~twalsh/agmmswaij2018.pdf). Their hardness construction uses at least five rounds, so its instances satisfy the restriction \(n\geq3\).

Their balanced binary-tree bracket is equivalent to the bracket in the question. For a permutation of the candidates, after round \(r\), each consecutive block of \(2^r\) original positions has exactly one survivor. This follows by induction: it holds after the first round, and adjacent survivors from consecutive blocks meet in the next round. Thus the survivor of each block is exactly the winner of the corresponding binary-tree subtree.

It therefore suffices to represent any source tournament as the majority graph of a society of the required form.

2. Explicit majority representation

Let \(T\) be any tournament on \(C=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m=2^n\) and \(n\geq3\). Write \(a\to b\) when \(a\) beats \(b\) in \(T\).

For each edge \(a\to b\), list the remaining candidates in increasing order as \(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-2}\), and introduce these two rankings:

  • \(a\succ b\succ r_1\succ\cdots\succ r_{m-2}\);
  • \(r_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ r_1\succ a\succ b\).

At this intermediate stage, rankings form a multiset and repetitions are allowed.

For distinct \(x,y\), define the integer margin \(M(x,y)\) of a multiset to be the number of rankings placing \(x\) above \(y\), minus the number placing \(y\) above \(x\).

Contribution of one edge gadget. The two rankings for \(a\to b\) contribute \(2\) to \(M(a,b)\) and zero to every other unordered pair:

  • Both rankings place \(a\) above \(b\).
  • Each of \(a,b\) is above every remaining candidate in the first ranking and below it in the second.
  • Every pair of remaining candidates appears in opposite orders in the two rankings.

These cases exhaust all candidate pairs.

There are \(m(m-1)/2\) edges, so the gadgets introduce \(m(m-1)\) rankings. Every unordered pair has its own gadget, while all other gadgets contribute zero to its margin. Consequently, \(M(a,b)=2\) whenever \(a\to b\).

Now let \(\rho\) be the increasing ranking of \(C\), and let \(\rho^{\mathrm{rev}}\) be its reverse. Add \(m/2\) copies of the pair \((\rho,\rho^{\mathrm{rev}})\). This is possible because \(m\) is even. Each such pair contributes zero to every margin.

The resulting multiset has exactly \(N=m(m-1)+m=m^2\) rankings, and its margins remain unchanged.

For every distinct ranking \(t\) occurring in this multiset, let \(k_t\) be its multiplicity and assign mass \(\mu_t=k_t/m^2\). Retain only one copy of each ranking.

This produces precisely the required support representation:

  • The listed rankings are distinct, strict, and complete.
  • Every mass is positive.
  • The masses sum to one because \(\sum_t k_t=m^2\).
  • Every mass is dyadic, since \(m^2=2^{2n}\).
  • The support size satisfies \(\tau\leq m^2\).

For an edge \(a\to b\), let \(W_{ab}\) be the number of rankings in the padded multiset placing \(a\) above \(b\). Completeness of the rankings and the margin calculation give \(2W_{ab}-N=2\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t=\frac{W_{ab}}{N}=\frac12+\frac1{m^2}.\)

The reverse comparison has mass \(\frac12-\frac1{m^2}\). Thus there are no majority ties, and the society’s majority graph is exactly \(T\).

3. Size and correctness of the reduction

Each ranking requires \(O(m\log m)\) bits, and each multiplicity and denominator requires \(O(\log m)\) bits. Since \(\tau\leq m^2\), the output bit length satisfies \(L=O(m^3\log m)\).

Constructing the multiset and merging duplicate rankings by comparison sorting takes \(O(m^3(\log m)^2)\) bit operations. Indeed, there are \(m^2\) ranking records, sorting uses \(O(m^2\log m)\) comparisons, and comparing two records costs at most \(O(m\log m)\) bit operations. Construction and multiplicity counting fit within the same bound.

Keep the designated candidate unchanged. For any permutation, every match has the same winner in \(T\) and in the constructed society. Induction over the rounds therefore gives identical survivor lists and the same final winner.

Thus some bracket makes the designated candidate win in the source instance if and only if some bracket makes it win in the constructed society. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and running times

A certificate is a permutation of \(C\), encoded using \(O(m\log m)\) bits.

First, compute the position of every candidate in every supported ranking. Then, for each candidate pair, sum the masses of rankings favoring either orientation and compare the result exactly with \(\frac12\). With standard binary encodings and classical integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for this preprocessing is \(O(m^2\tau L^2)\) bit operations. Intermediate rational sums have \(O(L)\)-bit numerators and denominators: a common denominator can be chosen as the product of the input denominators, whose total bit length is \(O(L)\).

After computing the majority table, check that the certificate is a permutation and simulate its \(m-1\) matches. This adds \(O(m\log m)\) bit operations in the standard random-access model. Certificate verification is therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

For comparison, exhaustive enumeration gives a deterministic decision algorithm with running time \(O(m^2\tau L^2+m!\,m\log m)\).

Combining membership and hardness proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness, even for societies with \(\tau\leq m^2\) and every pairwise winning mass equal to \(\frac12+\frac1{m^2}\). A polynomial-time algorithm for all such inputs exists if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
