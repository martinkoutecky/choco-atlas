The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite object kinds \(O\), a finite set of ranking types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_o\), and a finite family of integral bundles \(\mathcal{B}\), decide whether there exist masses \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}B_o x_{t,B}=\rho_o\) for every \(o\), such that whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\), no bundle \(C\) with positive aggregate mass is strictly SD-preferred to \(B\) by type \(t\); if so, output \(x\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. Each type \(t\in T\) has a complete ordinal ranking \(\succeq_t\) of object kinds \(O\). For bundles \(B,C\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{O}\), write \(C\succ_t^{SD}B\) if, for every rank cutoff of \(\succeq_t\), \(C\) contains at least as many objects above that cutoff as \(B\), and strictly more for some cutoff.

An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), supplies \(\rho_o\), and allowed integral bundles \(\mathcal{B}\). A feasible allocation is \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) with \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}B_o x_{t,B}=\rho_o\). Let \(y_C=\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,C}\). It is weakly SD envy-free if \(x_{t,B}>0\) implies that no \(C\in\mathcal{B}\) with \(y_C>0\) satisfies \(C\succ_t^{SD}B\).

Target. Is deciding whether such a weakly SD envy-free feasible allocation exists (and computing one when it does) polynomial-time solvable, or NP-hard?

Nearest known results. For finite-agent allocations of indivisible objects with ordinal preferences, deciding whether a weakly SD envy-free discrete allocation exists is NP-complete, including when ties are allowed.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is NP-hard, even with one type, a strict ranking, and an explicitly listed family of bundles. With explicitly listed bundles, the decision problem is NP-complete.

The proof below uses divisible allocation masses throughout.

1. Encoding stochastic dominance by coordinatewise order. Let \(V\subseteq\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}^{D}\), and let \(z\in[-2,2]^D\) be rational. Consider the following auxiliary problem: find weights \(\lambda_v\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v=1\), \(\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_vv=z\), and the positive-weight vectors form an antichain under coordinatewise order.

Here, an antichain means that no two distinct vectors \(v,w\) in the support satisfy \(v_k\leq w_k\) for every coordinate \(k\).

This problem can be encoded as the allocation problem with one type of mass \(1\). Introduce objects ranked strictly as \(o_1\succ o_2\succ\cdots\succ o_D\). For each \(v\in V\), define an integral bundle \(B(v)\) by

\(B(v)_{o_1}=4+v_1,\qquad B(v)_{o_k}=4+v_k-v_{k-1}\quad(2\leq k\leq D).\)

Every entry is nonnegative because the coordinates of \(v\) lie between \(-2\) and \(2\). Its cumulative counts are

\(\sum_{i=1}^{k}B(v)_{o_i}=4k+v_k.\)

Consequently, \(B(w)\succ^{SD}B(v)\) holds exactly when \(w\geq v\) coordinatewise and \(w\neq v\).

Set the supplies to

\(\rho_{o_1}=4+z_1,\qquad \rho_{o_k}=4+z_k-z_{k-1}\quad(2\leq k\leq D).\)

These supplies are rational and nonnegative. For weights summing to \(1\), the supply equations are equivalent to \(\sum_v\lambda_vv=z\): this follows by summing the supply equations over each initial segment of the ranking, and conversely by taking consecutive differences.

Because there is only one type, weak SD envy-freeness is exactly the requirement that the positive-weight vectors form an antichain. Thus it suffices to prove NP-hardness of the auxiliary problem.

2. Construction from satisfiability. Start with a 3-CNF formula with variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) and clauses \(C_1,\ldots,C_m\), where \(n,m\geq1\). Treat all three literal occurrences in each clause as distinct, including repeated literals.

Let \(U=\{u_{i,b}:1\leq i\leq n,\ b\in\{0,1\}\}\) contain the two assignment choices for each variable. Let \(W\) contain the \(3m\) literal occurrences. For an occurrence \(w\), let \(\operatorname{bad}(w)\in U\) denote the unique assignment choice that makes that occurrence false.

There are \(g=n+m\) groups: one for each variable and one for each clause. Write \(\delta_r\) for the \(r\)-th standard basis vector of \(\mathbb{R}^g\), and \(\mathbf{1}_g\) for its all-ones vector.

Use another coordinate block indexed by \(U\cup W\cup\{*\}\), of dimension \(d=2n+3m+1\). Denote its standard basis vectors by \(e_k\).

The complete vectors have five blocks:

\((h,G,E,s_1,s_2)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{g}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.\)

Define the following choice vectors:

  • For each assignment choice \(u_{i,b}\), set

\(v_{i,b}=(0,\delta_i,e_{u_{i,b}}-e_*,0,0)\).

  • For each occurrence \(w\) in clause \(C_j\), set

\(v_w=(1,\mathbf{1}_g+\delta_{n+j},e_{\operatorname{bad}(w)}+e_w,0,0)\).

Also introduce auxiliary vectors

\(f_0=(0,0_g,0_d,1,-1)\)

and, for every \(k\in U\cup W\),

\(f_k=(0,0_g,2e_*-2e_k,1,-1).\)

All coordinates belong to \(\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\). Let \(V\) consist of all these vectors.

Finally, put

\(a=\dfrac{1}{2g},\qquad H=ma,\qquad z=\left(H,(a+H)\mathbf{1}_g,2He_*,\dfrac12,-\dfrac12\right).\)

The construction has polynomial size, and \(z\in[-2,2]^{g+d+3}\).

3. Exact comparability relations. We now verify every possible kind of comparison between distinct vectors.

Two assignment vectors are incomparable: at their respective \(U\)-coordinates, each has a \(1\) where the other has a \(0\).

Two occurrence vectors are incomparable: at their respective \(W\)-coordinates, each has a \(1\) where the other has a \(0\).

An occurrence vector cannot be coordinatewise below an assignment vector, because its \(h\)-coordinate is \(1\), whereas an assignment vector has \(h=0\).

For an assignment vector \(v_{i,b}\) and an occurrence vector \(v_w\),

\(v_{i,b}\leq v_w\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_{i,b}=\operatorname{bad}(w).\)

Indeed, if the two assignment labels differ, the \(u_{i,b}\)-coordinate is \(1\) in \(v_{i,b}\) and \(0\) in \(v_w\), preventing the comparison. If the labels agree, the comparison holds in every block:

  • The \(h\)-coordinates satisfy \(0<1\).
  • Every coordinate of \(\delta_i\) is at most the corresponding coordinate of \(\mathbf{1}_g+\delta_{n+j}\).
  • The embedding blocks satisfy \(e_{u_{i,b}}-e_*\leq e_{u_{i,b}}+e_w\).
  • Both final coordinates agree.

Every auxiliary vector is incomparable with every choice vector. In the final two coordinates, auxiliary vectors have \((1,-1)\), while choice vectors have \((0,0)\), preventing either direction of comparison.

Finally, distinct auxiliary vectors are incomparable. Their embedding blocks are distinct and all have coordinate sum \(0\). If two distinct such blocks were coordinatewise ordered, their coordinate sums would be strictly ordered, a contradiction.

Therefore, the only comparabilities are between a clause occurrence and the assignment choice that makes it false.

4. A satisfying assignment gives a feasible antichain mixture. Suppose the formula is satisfiable. Select the assignment vector corresponding to each variable’s value, and select one true occurrence from each clause. Give each of these \(g\) vectors weight \(a\), for total choice-vector weight \(ga=1/2\).

By the comparison analysis, the selected choice vectors form an antichain.

Their weighted \(h\)-coordinate is \(ma=H\). Their weighted group block is

\(a\mathbf{1}_g+ma\mathbf{1}_g=(a+H)\mathbf{1}_g,\)

because precisely one selected vector belongs to each group, and each selected occurrence vector contributes an additional \(\mathbf{1}_g\).

Let \(q\) be their weighted embedding block. For every \(k\in U\cup W\), we have \(q_k\geq0\). Moreover,

\(\sum_{k\in U\cup W}q_k=(n+2m)a,\qquad q_*=-na.\)

The first equality holds because every selected assignment vector contributes one positive unit to these coordinates, while every selected occurrence vector contributes two.

Give auxiliary vector \(f_k\) weight \(q_k/2\), and give \(f_0\) the remaining weight

\(\dfrac12-\dfrac12\sum_{k\in U\cup W}q_k =\dfrac12-\dfrac{n+2m}{4(n+m)} =\dfrac{n}{4(n+m)}\geq0.\)

Thus the total auxiliary weight is \(1/2\). The resulting embedding block is

\(q+\sum_{k\in U\cup W}\dfrac{q_k}{2}(2e_*-2e_k) =\left(q_*+\sum_{k\in U\cup W}q_k\right)e_* =2mae_*=2He_*.\)

The auxiliary vectors leave the \(h\)- and group blocks unchanged and contribute \((1/2,-1/2)\) to the final two coordinates. The complete weighted mean is therefore exactly \(z\).

All auxiliary vectors are mutually incomparable and incomparable with all choice vectors. Hence the complete positive-weight support is an antichain.

5. A feasible antichain mixture gives a satisfying assignment. Conversely, suppose weights \(\lambda\) form an antichain mixture with mean \(z\).

The \(s_1\)-coordinate implies that the total auxiliary weight is \(1/2\). The \(h\)-coordinate implies that the total occurrence-vector weight is \(H\).

For each group \(r\), let \(R_r\) be the total weight of its choice vectors. The mean of group coordinate \(r\) is exactly \(R_r+H\): choice vectors in that group contribute their group indicator, every occurrence vector contributes the common additional \(1\), and auxiliary vectors contribute \(0\).

Since the target value is \(a+H\), we obtain \(R_r=a>0\) for every group.

Thus every variable has at least one positive-weight assignment choice, and every clause has at least one positive-weight occurrence. Choose any positive-weight assignment choice for each variable.

Consider a clause and any positive-weight occurrence \(w\) in it. Its false assignment choice \(\operatorname{bad}(w)\) cannot have positive weight, since its vector is comparable with \(v_w\). The variable appearing in \(w\) nevertheless has a positive-weight assignment choice. Therefore its chosen value makes \(w\) true.

Every clause consequently has a true occurrence, so the selected assignment satisfies the formula.

Combining this implication with the preceding construction proves the equivalence between satisfiability and existence of the required antichain mixture. Section 1 then gives the claimed polynomial reduction to weakly SD envy-free allocation.

6. Membership in NP and the computational conclusion. Assume that the allowed bundles are explicitly listed, with binary-encoded integer entries, and that masses and supplies are binary-encoded rationals.

Given an allocation certificate, feasibility is checked by rational arithmetic. Its envy condition is checked by examining every positive allocation entry \(x_{t,B}\) and every used bundle \(C\), and comparing cumulative counts at the rank cutoffs of type \(t\). There are polynomially many such comparisons.

It remains to justify a certificate of polynomial bit length. Suppose a feasible envy-free allocation exists, and restrict allocation variables to its positive support. The resulting feasibility system is \(Ax=b,\ x\geq0\), with rational input data.

Choose a feasible solution with the fewest positive entries. Its positive-support columns of \(A\) must be linearly independent. Otherwise, a nonzero linear dependence gives a direction preserving all equalities; choosing its sign and moving until the first positive coordinate becomes zero produces a feasible solution with smaller support.

For these independent columns, select an invertible square submatrix of rows. The positive entries are its unique solution. After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule and determinant bounds show that their bit lengths are polynomial in the input length.

This rational solution uses a subset of the original support. Deleting supported allocation entries cannot introduce envy: every remaining assigned bundle and every remaining used bundle was already present. Thus a polynomial-length certificate exists.

The explicitly listed problem is therefore NP-complete. A polynomial-time algorithm that decides existence and constructs an allocation when one exists would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). If the bundle family is represented implicitly, the reduction still establishes NP-hardness for representations that can encode these lists efficiently; NP membership would require specifying that representation.
