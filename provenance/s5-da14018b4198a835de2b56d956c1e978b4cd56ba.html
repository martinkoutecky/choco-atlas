The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), post categories \(\mathcal P\) with rational capacities \(\kappa_p\), and an acceptable-pair graph \(E\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_p\kappa_p=1\) and each type ties all its acceptable categories at rank \(1\), count the vertices of \(X=\{x\ge 0: x_{tp}=0\text{ for }(t,p)\notin E,\ \sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t,\ \sum_t x_{tp}=\kappa_p\}\) that are popular assignments.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=|\mathcal P|\) post categories with positive binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_p\), and an explicitly given acceptable-pair graph \(E\subseteq\mathcal T\times\mathcal P\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_p\kappa_p=1\).
Each type ties all its acceptable categories at rank \(1\), strictly prefers any acceptable assignment to being unmatched, and gives no preference between acceptable categories; posts do not vote.
A partial assignment is a nonnegative real matrix supported on \(E\), with row sums at most \(\mu_t\) and column sums at most \(\kappa_p\). The vote margin of a partial assignment \(y\) over \(z\) is \(\Delta(y,z)=\sum_{(t,p)\in E}(y_{tp}-z_{tp})\), measuring mass promoted from unmatched to matched minus mass demoted. An assignment \(x\) is popular if no feasible partial assignment \(y\) has \(\Delta(y,x)>0\).
Define \(X=\{x\geq0:x_{tp}=0\text{ for }(t,p)\notin E,\ \sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t,\ \sum_t x_{tp}=\kappa_p\}\). Count its distinct vertices that are popular, returning \(0\) when \(X\) is empty. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Can this exact count be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|E|,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

Discrete popular-matching counting with ties is \(\#\mathrm P\)-complete, even for cubic bipartite acceptable-pair graphs with every acceptable post tied first ([Nasre](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.0902v1)).
Counting vertices of an inequality-described \(0/1\) polytope is \(\#\mathrm P\)-complete ([Guo–Jerrum](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00454-022-00406-8)); this broader result does not itself classify the popularity-qualified problem above. No source located explicitly classifies that problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The continuous question did not define popularity: use mass voting with acceptable categories tied and matching strictly preferred to being unmatched.
  • Competitors may be partial assignments, carrying over the discrete definition's comparison against arbitrary matchings.
  • Discard zero-capacity categories; retain positive masses and capacities with the supplied normalization.
  • Count distinct vertices rather than representations or bases, and return zero for an empty feasible set.
  • Use polynomial-time Turing reductions for NP-hardness of this function problem.

Result

The exact count is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. In fact, it is \(\#\mathrm P\)-complete under these reductions, even when \(\tau=m\) is a power of two and every mass and capacity equals \(1/m\).

The proof uses a direct reduction from counting bipartite perfect matchings.

1. Every assignment in \(X\) is popular

For a feasible partial assignment \(y\), define its total matched mass by
\[ M(y)=\sum_{(t,p)\in E}y_{tp}. \]
The row constraints imply
\[ M(y)\leq\sum_t\mu_t=1. \]
Every \(x\in X\) satisfies \(M(x)=1\). Consequently,
\[ \Delta(y,x)=M(y)-M(x)\leq0 \]
for every feasible partial assignment \(y\).

Thus every point of \(X\) is popular, and the requested count is exactly the number of distinct vertices of \(X\).

2. A characterization of the vertices

For \(x\in X\), let \(H_x\) be the bipartite graph on \(\mathcal T\cup\mathcal P\) whose edges are precisely the pairs with \(x_{tp}>0\).

Lemma. A point \(x\in X\) is a vertex if and only if \(H_x\) is a forest.

Proof. Suppose first that \(H_x\) contains a cycle. Because the graph is bipartite, this cycle has even length. Define a matrix \(h\) by assigning alternating values \(+1\) and \(-1\) to its edges and zero elsewhere. Every row sum and column sum of \(h\) is zero.

Choose
\[ 0<\varepsilon<\min\{x_{tp}:(t,p)\text{ belongs to the cycle}\}. \]
Then \(x+\varepsilon h\) and \(x-\varepsilon h\) are distinct points of \(X\), and their midpoint is \(x\). Hence \(x\) is not a vertex.

Conversely, suppose \(H_x\) is a forest and
\[ x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z,\qquad y,z\in X,\quad 0<\lambda<1. \]
Whenever \(x_{tp}=0\), nonnegativity forces \(y_{tp}=z_{tp}=0\). Therefore \(h=y-z\) is supported on \(H_x\), and all its row and column sums are zero.

At a leaf of this forest, the zero-sum equation forces the value of \(h\) on its unique incident edge to be zero. Delete that edge and repeat. Induction on the number of edges gives \(h=0\). Thus \(y=z=x\), proving that \(x\) is a vertex. \(\square\)

This argument characterizes distinct vertices, rather than bases, which could represent a degenerate vertex multiple times.

3. Equal margins give exactly perfect matchings

Suppose \(\tau=m=N\) and every row and column margin equals \(a=1/N\).

If \(x\) is a vertex, its positive-support graph is a forest by the lemma. Every node has an incident positive edge, since its margin is positive.

Take a leaf \(v\) of any component, with neighbor \(w\). Its incident edge has value \(a\), because it alone supplies the margin at \(v\). That edge also exhausts the margin at \(w\), so \(w\) has no other positive incident edge. The component therefore consists of this single edge.

It follows that the positive-support graph of \(x\) is a perfect matching. Every matching edge has value \(1/N\).

Conversely, a perfect matching \(M\) defines the feasible assignment
\[ x^M_{tp}= \begin{cases} 1/N,&(t,p)\in M,\\ 0,&(t,p)\notin M. \end{cases} \]
Its support is a forest, so the lemma proves that it is a vertex. Different perfect matchings give different matrices.

Hence
\[ |\operatorname{vert}(X)|=\#\{\text{perfect matchings of }E\}. \]

4. A reduction satisfying all input restrictions

Counting perfect matchings of bipartite graphs is \(\#\mathrm P\)-complete under polynomial-time Turing reductions: it is the permanent of the graph’s square adjacency matrix. This is Valiant’s theorem. [Valiant, *The complexity of computing the permanent*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304397579900446)

Let \(G=(U,V,F)\) be such a graph, with
\[ U=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\},\qquad V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\},\qquad n\geq1. \]
If \(G\) has an isolated vertex, its perfect-matching count is zero and can be returned directly. Assume otherwise.

The following construction also ensures that the listed types have distinct acceptable-category sets.

For each \(i\), introduce a type \(d_i\) and a category \(q_i\). Add the edges
\[ (u_i,q_i),\qquad(d_i,q_i), \]
while retaining every original edge of \(F\). The type \(d_i\) accepts only \(q_i\).

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two with \(N\geq2n\). Add \(N-2n\) disjoint type-category pairs, each having its single connecting edge. There are now exactly \(N\) types and \(N\) categories. Give every one mass or capacity \(1/N\).

All required conditions hold:

  • The masses and capacities are positive and sum to \(1\) on each side.
  • Because \(N\) is a power of two, \(1/N\) is a dyadic rational with an exact binary representation.
  • Every type ties all its acceptable categories at rank \(1\).
  • Original types have distinct neighborhoods through their private categories \(q_i\); their original neighborhoods are nonempty, so none coincides with a singleton neighborhood.
  • Since \(N<4n\), the construction has polynomial size. Its edge count is \(|F|+N\), and each margin needs only \(O(\log n)\) bits.

In every perfect matching of the constructed graph, \(d_i\) must match \(q_i\). Thus \(u_i\) cannot use \(q_i\). The padding pairs are also forced. The remaining edges form precisely a perfect matching of \(G\).

Conversely, every perfect matching of \(G\) extends uniquely by these forced edges. Therefore the construction preserves the count exactly.

By Sections 1–3, the requested popular-vertex count on the constructed instance equals the perfect-matching count of \(G\). Composing this construction with Valiant’s reductions proves the claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Membership in \(\#\mathrm P\)

For completeness, the general problem also belongs to \(\#\mathrm P\).

A nondeterministic machine guesses a subset \(S\subseteq E\), using exactly one bit per edge. It rejects unless \(S\) is a forest. It then checks whether the margin equations, with all variables outside \(S\) fixed to zero, have a solution strictly positive on every edge of \(S\).

For a forest, these equations have at most one solution: the difference of two solutions vanishes by the leaf-elimination argument in Section 2. Existence and positivity can be checked by exact rational linear algebra in polynomial time. The matrix has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), and the right-hand side consists of the input rationals, so standard exact linear-system algorithms have polynomial bit complexity.

Every accepted subset is the exact positive support of one vertex, by the lemma. Every vertex yields exactly one accepted subset. Thus the number of accepting branches is the number of distinct vertices. If \(X\) is empty, no branch accepts. Section 1 shows that these are exactly the vertices requested in the task.

This establishes \(\#\mathrm P\)-completeness under polynomial-time Turing reductions. A polynomial-time exact algorithm in \(m,\tau,|E|,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Both directions of the lemma are valid.

A cycle prevents extremality. A cycle in the bipartite positive-support graph has even length. Alternating signs around it yield a nonzero perturbation with zero row and column sums. The chosen bound on \(\varepsilon\) preserves nonnegativity in both perturbation directions. Since only acceptable edges are changed, both perturbed matrices belong to \(X\). Their distinctness and midpoint relation correctly show that \(x\) is not a vertex.

A forest implies extremality. In any proposed nontrivial convex decomposition, nonnegativity forces both endpoint matrices to vanish wherever \(x\) vanishes. Their difference is therefore supported on the forest and has zero sum at every node. Leaf elimination correctly forces every edge value of that difference to vanish. Isolated nodes have no edge values to check.

The characterization applies to positive supports, including degenerate vertices; it does not count multiple bases representing the same vertex.

3. Equal margins and perfect matchings

The claimed bijection is correct.

Positive margins ensure that every node of the support graph has an incident positive edge. Consequently, every forest component has an edge and a leaf. At a leaf, the unique incident edge must carry the entire margin \(a\). Because its neighbor also has margin \(a\), that neighbor cannot have another positive incident edge. The component therefore contains just those two nodes and their edge.

Applying this reasoning to every component shows that every vertex support is a perfect matching. Its edge values are necessarily \(1/N\).

Conversely, the assignment defined by any perfect matching satisfies all margins and has forest support, so the lemma makes it a vertex. Distinct matchings have distinct positive supports and hence define distinct matrices. This correspondence also covers graphs with no perfect matching: neither side has a counted object.
