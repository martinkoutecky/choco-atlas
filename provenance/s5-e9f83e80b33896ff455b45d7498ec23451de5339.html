The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a compact rational polytope \(P\), and positive rational piecewise-linear concave utilities \(u_t\), compute a shared predictor maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(\theta)\). Is the resulting predictor core-stable against every measurable coalition of mass \(\alpha>0\) that prefers an alternative \(\theta'\) by the factor \(1/\alpha\), with strict improvement on positive mass?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq 1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The feasible predictors form a nonempty compact rational polytope \(P\subseteq\mathbb R^m\), given by explicit linear inequalities. Each utility is given explicitly as \(u_t(\theta)=\min_{1\leq j\leq k_t}(a_{tj}^{\mathsf T}\theta+b_{tj})\), with rational coefficients and \(u_t(\theta)>0\) throughout \(P\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including all inequalities and utility pieces.
The society is atomless: type \(t\) occupies a measurable set of mass \(\mu_t\). A measurable coalition is represented by arbitrary real masses \(0\leq s_t\leq\mu_t\), with \(\alpha=\sum_t s_t>0\). It blocks \(\theta\) if some shared alternative \(\theta'\in P\) satisfies \(\alpha u_t(\theta')\geq u_t(\theta)\) whenever \(s_t>0\), with strict inequality for types whose coalition masses sum to a positive number. A predictor is core-stable when no coalition blocks it.
Compute any exact maximizer of \(F(\theta)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(\theta)\); encode its coordinates as real algebraic numbers using dense integer polynomials and rational isolating intervals.

Is computing such an exact maximizer polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard? Is every maximizer core-stable against the coalitions defined above?

For finite agents with positive concave utilities on a compact convex domain, every maximizer of the unweighted sum of logarithms is core-stable; the corresponding weighted statement is also established. [Chaudhury et al. (2022)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/25e92e33ac8c35fd49f394c37f21b6da-Paper-Conference.pdf).
Those statements do not specify exact algebraic-output bit complexity or treat measurable coalitions taking arbitrary fractions of types; no result settling this exact formulation was located.

Status

The following statements are proved below.

  • Every maximizer is core-stable, including against coalitions taking arbitrary real fractions of types.
  • No algorithm can always compute a maximizer in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ in the required dense algebraic encoding. Some polynomial-size instances have a unique maximizer whose first coordinate has degree $2^m$.
  • For $\tau\leq 2$, exact computation is polynomial-time, and a rational maximizer can be returned.
  • For $\tau\geq 2$, every instance admits a maximizer whose coordinates belong to a common number field of degree at most $(\tau-1)^{\tau-1}$.

The general $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness classification remains unproved here. The output-size lower bound establishes failure of polynomial-time computation, but does not establish $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness.

1. Existence and core stability

Each utility is continuous and concave, being the minimum of finitely many affine functions. Positivity on the compact set $P$ makes
$$ F(\theta)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(\theta) $$
continuous. Therefore a maximizer exists.

Fix an arbitrary maximizer $\theta^*$ and an arbitrary alternative $\theta'\in P$. Define
$$ r_t=\frac{u_t(\theta')}{u_t(\theta^*)}>0. $$

We first prove the proportional-fairness inequality
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t r_t\leq 1. $$

For $0<\varepsilon<1$, the point
$$ \theta_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\theta^*+\varepsilon\theta' $$
belongs to $P$. Concavity gives
$$ u_t(\theta_\varepsilon) \geq u_t(\theta^*)\bigl(1+\varepsilon(r_t-1)\bigr). $$
The parenthesized factor is positive. By optimality of $\theta^*$ and monotonicity of the logarithm,
$$ 0\geq F(\theta_\varepsilon)-F(\theta^*) \geq \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \log\bigl(1+\varepsilon(r_t-1)\bigr). $$
Dividing by $\varepsilon$ and taking the limit as $\varepsilon$ decreases to zero yields
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(r_t-1)\leq 0. $$
This limit is legitimate because the support is finite and each scalar logarithm is differentiable at $\varepsilon=0$. Since the masses sum to one, the claimed inequality follows.

Now suppose a coalition with masses $s_t$ and total mass $\alpha>0$ blocks $\theta^*$ using $\theta'$. For every participating type,
$$ r_t\geq \frac1\alpha. $$
The strict-improvement condition says precisely that
$$ \sum_{\{t:\,s_t>0,\ r_t>1/\alpha\}}s_t>0. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_{t\in T}s_t r_t > \frac1\alpha\sum_{t\in T}s_t =1. $$
But $s_t\leq\mu_t$ and $r_t>0$, so
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t r_t \geq \sum_{t\in T}s_t r_t >1, $$
contradicting proportional fairness.

Thus every maximizer is core-stable under the stated measurable-coalition definition.

2. The optimal utility vector and an algebraic-degree bound

This section establishes that the requested algebraic output always exists. It also gives a bound depending only on support size.

2.1. A rational polytope of utility vectors

Every affine utility piece is positive throughout $P$: its value is at least the minimum defining its utility. Hence
$$ \eta= \min_{t,j}\ \min_{\theta\in P} \bigl(a_{tj}^{\mathsf T}\theta+b_{tj}\bigr) $$
is positive. It is rational and can be computed by finitely many rational linear programs.

Define the lifted rational polytope
$$ Q= \left\{ (\theta,y): \theta\in P,\quad \eta\leq y_t\leq a_{tj}^{\mathsf T}\theta+b_{tj} \text{ for every }t,j \right\}. $$
It is nonempty because $(\theta,(u_t(\theta))_t)\in Q$ for every $\theta\in P$. It is bounded because $P$ is bounded and all utility pieces are bounded on $P$.

Let its projection onto the utility coordinates be
$$ Y=\{y:\text{ some }(\theta,y)\in Q\}. $$
This is a nonempty compact rational polytope contained in the positive orthant. Define
$$ \Phi(y)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log y_t. $$
Its Hessian is
$$ \nabla^2\Phi(y) = -\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\mu_t}{y_t^2}\right)_{t\in T}, $$
which is negative definite. Therefore $\Phi$ has a unique maximizer $y^*$ on $Y$.

The lifted problem and the original problem have the same optimum. Indeed, every $\theta\in P$ gives the feasible vector $y=u(\theta)$, while every feasible $(\theta,y)$ satisfies $y_t\leq u_t(\theta)$ and hence
$$ \Phi(y)\leq F(\theta). $$

Moreover, if $(\theta,y^*)\in Q$, then $u_t(\theta)=y_t^*$ for every $t$. Otherwise at least one coordinate would be strictly larger, and positivity of every mass would imply
$$ F(\theta)>\Phi(y^*), $$
contradicting equality of the optimal values.

Thus all predictor maximizers have the same utility vector $y^*$, and their set is exactly
$$ R= \left\{ \theta\in P: a_{tj}^{\mathsf T}\theta+b_{tj}\geq y_t^* \text{ for every }t,j \right\}. $$

2.2. Polynomial equations on the optimal face

Let $G$ be the smallest face of $Y$ containing $y^*$. Then $y^*$ lies in the relative interior of $G$. Write $r=\dim G$.

Since $Y$ is a rational polytope, its face has a rational affine parametrization
$$ \operatorname{aff}G=\{c+Bx:x\in\mathbb R^r\}, $$
where $c\in\mathbb Q^\tau$ and $B\in\mathbb Q^{\tau\times r}$ has rank $r$. Write $y^*=c+Bx^*$.

Relative interior permits sufficiently small perturbations in both directions along every column of $B$. Optimality therefore implies
$$ B^{\mathsf T}\nabla\Phi(y^*)=0. $$
Since $\nabla\Phi(y^*)$ is nonzero, this also shows
$$ r\leq\tau-1. $$

If $r=0$, then $y^*=c$ is rational. Suppose $r>0$, and put
$$ v_t(x)=c_t+\sum_{j=1}^rB_{tj}x_j. $$
The stationarity equations are
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\frac{\mu_tB_{tj}}{v_t(x^*)}=0, \qquad 1\leq j\leq r. $$
Clearing denominators gives rational polynomial equations
$$ p_j(x)= \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tB_{tj} \prod_{\substack{q\in T\\q\ne t}}v_q(x)=0, \qquad 1\leq j\leq r. $$
Each has degree at most $\tau-1$.

These equations have a nonsingular zero at $x^*$. To verify this, define
$$ H(x)=\prod_{t\in T}v_t(x), \qquad g(x)=\nabla_x\Phi(c+Bx). $$
Locally around $x^*$, all denominators are nonzero and $p=Hg$. Since $g(x^*)=0$,
$$ Dp(x^*) = H(x^*)Dg(x^*) = -H(x^*)B^{\mathsf T} \operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\mu_t}{(y_t^*)^2}\right)B. $$
For every nonzero $h\in\mathbb R^r$,
$$ h^{\mathsf T}Dg(x^*)h = -\sum_{t\in T} \frac{\mu_t(Bh)_t^2}{(y_t^*)^2}<0, $$
because $B$ has rank $r$. Hence the Jacobian is invertible, and $x^*$ is an isolated complex zero.

2.3. Algebraicity and degree

We use the affine Bézout bound: $r$ polynomials in $r$ complex variables, of respective degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_r$, have at most $\prod_jd_j$ isolated common zeros, even if other components have positive dimension. [Affine Bézout theorem, Theorem 3.1](https://event.cwi.nl/semester-programs/2022/PolOpt/notes_Simon.pdf).

For completeness, nonsingularity also supplies algebraicity here. Set $K=\mathbb Q(x_1^*,\ldots,x_r^*)$. If $K$ had positive transcendence degree, choose a transcendence basis and differentiate with respect to one of its elements. This gives a nonzero $\mathbb Q$-derivation on the corresponding rational-function field. It extends to $K$, because the remaining finite algebraic extension is separable in characteristic zero: for an algebraic element with minimal polynomial $M$, the extension is determined by
$$ D\alpha=-\frac{(DM)(\alpha)}{M'(\alpha)}. $$
Applying this derivation to the equations $p_j(x^*)=0$ gives
$$ Dp(x^*)\,(Dx_1^*,\ldots,Dx_r^*)^{\mathsf T}=0. $$
Invertibility forces every $Dx_j^*=0$. The derivation would then vanish on all of $K$, contrary to its value on the chosen transcendence-basis element. Thus $K$ is a number field.

Every embedding of $K$ into $\mathbb C$ maps $x^*$ to a common zero of the same rational polynomial equations. Its Jacobian remains nonsingular. Distinct embeddings give distinct tuples because the coordinates generate $K$. Bézout therefore gives
$$ [K:\mathbb Q]\leq(\tau-1)^r\leq(\tau-1)^{\tau-1}. $$

Since $B$ has a rational left inverse,
$$ \mathbb Q(y^*)=\mathbb Q(x^*)=K. $$
The nonempty bounded polytope $R$ has a vertex. At any such vertex, $m$ linearly independent active constraints determine its coordinates. Their coefficient matrix is rational and their right-hand side belongs to $K$. Cramer’s rule consequently places all those coordinates in $K$.

We have proved:

Algebraic-output existence. For $\tau\geq2$, some predictor maximizer has all its coordinates in a common number field of degree at most $(\tau-1)^{\tau-1}$. For $\tau=1$, a rational maximizer exists.

This is a degree bound, not a polynomial-time algorithm for variable $\tau$.

3. Exact polynomial-time computation for at most two types

The degree bound implies rationality when $\tau=2$. Here we prove the stronger algorithmic statement without explicitly constructing the potentially large projected polytope $Y$.

We use the standard theorem that rational linear programs can be solved exactly in polynomial bit complexity, including returning rational optimal solutions. All linear programs below have explicit rational constraints, a nonempty compact feasible region, and polynomial-size data. [Polynomial-time linear programming](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.5.1.iv?journalCode=moor).

For $\tau=1$, maximize $y_1$ over $Q$. An exact rational LP solution gives the required predictor.

Assume henceforth that $\tau=2$, with types indexed by $1,2$.

3.1. A polynomial bound on the rational answer

Let $N=m+2$, and let $S$ be the original input length plus the encoding length of $Q$. Computing $\eta$ and constructing $Q$ take polynomial time, so $S$ is polynomial in the original parameters.

After clearing denominators separately in each constraint row, all integer entries, including right-hand sides, have absolute value at most $2^{2S}$. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound
$$ |\det A|\leq N!\,2^{2SN} $$
show that every vertex coordinate of $Q$ has numerator and denominator bounded in absolute value by $2^C$, where we may take
$$ C=2NS+N\left\lceil\log_2(N+1)\right\rceil+2. $$
Every vertex of $Y$ is the projection of a vertex of $Q$, so the same bound applies to its coordinates.

The smallest face containing $y^*$ has dimension at most one.

If it has dimension zero, $y^*$ is a rational vertex with the preceding bound. Otherwise, let $c,d$ be the endpoints of that face, put $b=d-c$, and write
$$ y^*=c+b x^*. $$
Stationarity gives
$$ \frac{\mu_1b_1}{c_1+b_1x^*} + \frac{\mu_2b_2}{c_2+b_2x^*}=0. $$
The denominators and masses are positive. Since $b\ne0$, this equality requires $b_1b_2<0$. Using $\mu_1+\mu_2=1$, clearing denominators yields
$$ x^* = -\frac{\mu_1b_1c_2+\mu_2b_2c_1}{b_1b_2}. $$
Thus $y^*$ is rational.

Here are explicit size bounds sufficient for the algorithm. Coordinates of $b$ have numerator and denominator bounded by $2^{2C+1}$. In the displayed formula, ordinary multiplication, addition, and division give numerator and denominator bounds
$$ 2^{2S+10C+5} $$
for $x^*$, and
$$ 2^{B_y}, \qquad B_y=2S+13C+8, $$
for each coordinate of $y^*$. These bounds also cover the vertex case.

Define the positive rational number
$$ \lambda^*=\frac{\mu_2y_1^*}{\mu_1y_2^*}. $$
Its numerator and denominator are bounded by
$$ H=2^B, \qquad B=2S+2B_y. $$
In particular,
$$ 0<\lambda^*\leq H, $$
and $B$ is polynomial in the original input parameters.

3.2. An exact test for a rational parameter

For a positive rational $\lambda$, maximize
$$ h_\lambda(y)=y_1+\lambda y_2 $$
over $Q$. Let $M_\lambda$ be the optimal value. On the face
$$ Q_\lambda= \{(\theta,y)\in Q:h_\lambda(y)=M_\lambda\}, $$
use two further linear programs to find the minimum and maximum of
$$ q_\lambda(y)=\mu_2y_1-\lambda\mu_1y_2. $$

If the range contains zero, a rational convex combination of the two returned feasible points gives $(\theta,y)\in Q_\lambda$ with $q_\lambda(y)=0$. Then
$$ \nabla\Phi(y)=\frac{\mu_1}{y_1}(1,\lambda). $$
For every $v\in Y$, the scalar inequality $\log a\leq a-1$ gives
$$ \Phi(v)-\Phi(y) \leq \nabla\Phi(y)^{\mathsf T}(v-y) = \frac{\mu_1}{y_1} \bigl(h_\lambda(v)-h_\lambda(y)\bigr) \leq0. $$
Therefore $y=y^*$, and the returned $\theta$ is a maximizer of the original objective.

We next prove that the other outcomes locate $\lambda^*$.

First-order optimality of $y^*$ gives
$$ \nabla\Phi(y^*)^{\mathsf T}(v-y^*)\leq0 \qquad(v\in Y). $$
Since
$$ \nabla\Phi(y^*)=\frac{\mu_1}{y_1^*}(1,\lambda^*), $$
the vector $y^*$ maximizes $h_{\lambda^*}$.

Let $s$ be any utility vector maximizing $h_\lambda$. The two supporting inequalities are
$$ s_1+\lambda s_2\geq y_1^*+\lambda y_2^*, $$
$$ y_1^*+\lambda^*y_2^*\geq s_1+\lambda^*s_2. $$
Adding them gives
$$ (\lambda-\lambda^*)(s_2-y_2^*)\geq0. $$

If $\lambda<\lambda^*$, then $s_2\leq y_2^*$; the first supporting inequality also gives $s_1\geq y_1^*$. Hence
$$ q_\lambda(s) \geq \mu_2y_1^*-\lambda\mu_1y_2^* = \mu_1y_2^*(\lambda^*-\lambda)>0. $$

If $\lambda>\lambda^*$, then $s_2\geq y_2^*$; the second supporting inequality gives $s_1\leq y_1^*$. Consequently,
$$ q_\lambda(s) \leq \mu_1y_2^*(\lambda^*-\lambda)<0. $$

At $\lambda=\lambda^*$, the supporting face contains $y^*$ and its $q_{\lambda^*}$-value is zero. Thus the LP test has the exact interpretation:

  • A strictly positive range means $\lambda<\lambda^*$.
  • A strictly negative range means $\lambda>\lambda^*$.
  • A range containing zero returns an exact maximizer.

3.3. Bisection and rational reconstruction

Start with the interval $[0,H]$. Query its midpoint and use the preceding test to update the appropriate endpoint, unless a maximizer has already been returned.

After $3B+2$ iterations, the remaining interval has width
$$ \frac{H}{2^{3B+2}}=\frac1{4H^2}. $$
It contains $\lambda^*$. Distinct reduced rationals with denominators at most $H$ differ by at least $1/H^2$, so $\lambda^*$ is the unique such rational in the interval.

It can be recovered in polynomial time using continued fractions. If $z$ is the interval midpoint and $\lambda^*=p/q$ in lowest terms, then
$$ \left|z-\frac pq\right| \leq\frac1{8H^2} < \frac1{2q^2}. $$
Legendre’s continued-fraction criterion makes $p/q$ a convergent of $z$; the case $z=p/q$ is immediate. The Euclidean algorithm lists the convergents of this polynomial-bit rational in polynomial time, and the interval identifies the required one. [Legendre’s criterion](https://crypto.stanford.edu/pbc/notes/contfrac/converge.html).

Apply the LP test once more at the recovered $\lambda^*$. It returns a rational maximizing predictor.

There are polynomially many LP calls, and every queried parameter and constraint has polynomial bit length. The final rational coordinates also have polynomial bit length. A coordinate $p/q$ is encoded by the dense integer polynomial
$$ qX-p $$
and, for example, the rational isolating interval
$$ \left(\frac pq-1,\frac pq+1\right). $$

Therefore the exact problem is polynomial-time solvable for $\tau\leq2$, even with arbitrary predictor dimension and piecewise-affine concave utilities.

3.4. Irrationality already occurs with three types

The rationality statement does not extend to three types.

Take
$$ P=[1/4,3/4], $$
utilities
$$ u_1(x)=x,\qquad u_2(x)=1+x,\qquad u_3(x)=1-x, $$
and dyadic masses
$$ \mu_1=\frac38,\qquad \mu_2=\mu_3=\frac5{16}. $$
All utilities are positive on $P$, and the masses sum to one. The derivative of the objective is
$$ F'(x)=\frac{3/8-x^2}{x(1-x^2)}. $$
The denominator is positive throughout $P$, so the unique maximizer is
$$ x^*=\sqrt{\frac38}=\frac{\sqrt6}{4}. $$
It lies strictly inside $P$ and has algebraic degree two.

4. An unconditional obstruction to polynomial-time dense output

We now construct polynomial-size instances with a unique maximizer having an exponentially large required coordinate encoding.

4.1. Primes and the domain

For each integer $n\geq1$, choose distinct primes satisfying
$$ p_1=5,\qquad p_i<p_{i+1}<2p_i. $$
Bertrand’s postulate supplies each successive prime, and consequently
$$ p_i\leq5\cdot2^{i-1}. $$
Thus every chosen prime has $O(n)$ bits. [Bertrand’s postulate and proof](https://math.stanford.edu/~ksound/Math155Spr12/Bertrand.pdf).

Let the predictor coordinates be
$$ \theta=(z,y_2,\ldots,y_n)\in\mathbb R^n, $$
and define
$$ \ell_1(\theta)=z-\sum_{i=2}^ny_i, \qquad \ell_i(\theta)=y_i\quad(2\leq i\leq n). $$
Set
$$ P_n= \left\{ \theta: \frac1{p_i}\leq\ell_i(\theta)\leq\frac12 \text{ for every }i \right\}. $$
The transformation $x_i=\ell_i(\theta)$ is invertible, with inverse
$$ z=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i,\qquad y_i=x_i\quad(2\leq i\leq n). $$
Therefore $P_n$ is an invertible linear image of the nonempty box
$$ \prod_{i=1}^n[1/p_i,1/2]. $$
It is a compact, full-dimensional rational polytope.

4.2. Utilities and dyadic masses

Put
$$ W=\sum_{i=1}^np_i, $$
and let $D$ be the smallest power of two strictly greater than $W$.

For each $i$, introduce three types:
$$ u_{i,0}(\theta)=\ell_i(\theta), \qquad \mu_{i,0}=\frac1D, $$
$$ u_{i,+}(\theta)=1+\ell_i(\theta), \qquad \mu_{i,+}=\frac{p_i-1}{2D}, $$
$$ u_{i,-}(\theta)=1-\ell_i(\theta), \qquad \mu_{i,-}=\frac{p_i-1}{2D}. $$
Add a constant-utility type with utility $1$ and mass
$$ \frac{D-W}{D}. $$

Every mass is positive and dyadic. For a fixed $i$, the three masses sum to $p_i/D$, so the total mass is one. Every utility is affine and strictly positive throughout $P_n$.

The instance has
$$ m=n,\qquad \tau=3n+1. $$
Since
$$ W\leq5(2^n-1), $$
the primes, masses, and bounds have $O(n)$ bits. There are $O(n^2)$ explicitly listed coefficients, yielding the sufficient bound
$$ L=O(n^3). $$

4.3. The unique maximizer

In the independent box coordinates $x_i=\ell_i(\theta)$, the objective satisfies
$$ DF(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^nf_i(x_i), $$
where
$$ f_i(x)=\log x+\frac{p_i-1}{2}\log(1-x^2). $$
For $0<x<1$,
$$ f_i'(x) = \frac1x-\frac{(p_i-1)x}{1-x^2} = \frac{1-p_ix^2}{x(1-x^2)}. $$
The denominator is positive. Thus $f_i$ increases below $1/\sqrt{p_i}$ and decreases above it.

Because $p_i\geq5$,
$$ \frac1{p_i}<\frac1{\sqrt{p_i}}<\frac12. $$
Each coordinate consequently has a unique maximizing value
$$ x_i^*=\frac1{\sqrt{p_i}}. $$
Independence of the box coordinates and invertibility of the transformation prove uniqueness of the predictor maximizer. Its first coordinate is
$$ z^*=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{\sqrt{p_i}}. $$

4.4. The first coordinate has degree $2^n$

Define
$$ K_r=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_r}). $$
We prove inductively that $K_r$ has degree $2^r$, has basis
$$ e_S=\prod_{i\in S}\sqrt{p_i}, \qquad S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}, $$
and admits every independent sign choice on its generating square roots as an automorphism.

The assertions hold for $r=0$. Suppose they hold for $r-1$. If $v^2=p_r$ for some $v\in K_{r-1}$, every sign automorphism $\sigma$ satisfies $\sigma(v)=v$ or $\sigma(v)=-v$.

Expand $v$ in the asserted basis. The automorphism flipping only $\sqrt{p_i}$ multiplies $e_S$ by $-1$ exactly when $i\in S$. Since $v$ is an eigenvector of each such flip, all basis terms with nonzero coefficients must have identical membership patterns. Hence
$$ v=c\,e_S $$
for some nonzero $c\in\mathbb Q$ and some subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r-1\}$. Squaring gives
$$ p_r=c^2\prod_{i\in S}p_i. $$
This is impossible: the valuation at the distinct prime $p_r$ is odd on the left and even on the right.

Thus $X^2-p_r$ is irreducible over $K_{r-1}$. Adjoining its root doubles the degree and supplies the stated basis. Every previous sign automorphism extends with either sign on the new root, because its defining relation is $\left(\sqrt{p_r}\right)^2=p_r$. This completes the induction.

The sign automorphisms of $K_n$ send $z^*$ to the $2^n$ values
$$ z_\varepsilon= \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\varepsilon_i}{\sqrt{p_i}}, \qquad \varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^n. $$
They are distinct. Equality of two would imply
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\varepsilon_i-\delta_i}{p_i}\sqrt{p_i}=0. $$
The singleton basis elements $\sqrt{p_i}$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$, so every coefficient vanishes and $\varepsilon=\delta$.

Every $z_\varepsilon$ is a root of the minimal polynomial of $z^*$, since rational coefficients are fixed by these automorphisms. Therefore that polynomial has degree at least $2^n$. The reverse bound follows from $z^*\in K_n$ and $[K_n:\mathbb Q]=2^n$. Consequently,
$$ [\mathbb Q(z^*):\mathbb Q]=2^n. $$

4.5. Required output length

Every nonzero integer polynomial vanishing at $z^*$ has degree at least $2^n$, because the minimal polynomial divides it over $\mathbb Q$.

A dense polynomial encoding must list every coefficient, including zero coefficients. Thus any valid polynomial encoding for this first coordinate contains at least $2^n+1$ coefficient entries. An isolating interval cannot reduce that requirement.

The predictor maximizer is unique, so another maximizer cannot be selected to avoid this coordinate. Yet
$$ m=n,\qquad \tau=3n+1,\qquad L=O(n^3). $$
Writing the required output therefore takes more than every polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

This proves, unconditionally, that the general exact-output task is not polynomial-time solvable in the specified encoding. Efficiently selecting the prime sequence is unnecessary for this conclusion: the lower bound uses the existence of small valid instances, rather than a reduction.

5. Remaining open point

The core-stability question is completely settled, and polynomial-time exact computation in the required general encoding is ruled out.

What remains unproved is an $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness result under a specified polynomial-time reduction for this exact-output search problem. The exponential output requirement alone supplies no such reduction.
