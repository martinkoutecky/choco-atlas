The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), mass distribution \(\mu\), monotone piecewise-\(C^1\) scalable characteristic function \(V\), focal type \(h\), accuracy parameters \(\epsilon,\delta\), and \(G\ge\Gamma_h^\infty\), where \(D_h(z)=\partial_hV(z)\), \(a_h(\mu)=\int_0^1D_h(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda\), and \(\Gamma_h^\infty=\sup_{z\le\mu}D_h(z)/a_h(\mu)\), construct a type-level random-order sampling protocol and stopping rule under the paper's rushing adversary and unknown detectable violation-rate model that returns \((\widehat a_h,\widehat\epsilon)\) satisfying \(\Pr[\widehat\epsilon\le\max\{\epsilon,4f\Gamma_h^\infty\}]=1\) and \(\Pr[\widehat a_h\ge(1-\widehat\epsilon)a_h(\mu)]\ge1-\delta\), while minimizing permutation-sample count independently of clone count.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support \(T\): the \(\tau\) types of positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is support size, not the number of possible types; let \(m\) be the maximum type-description length and \(L\) the total binary input length.
The characteristic function \(V:[0,\mu]\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) is continuous, coordinatewise monotone and piecewise-\(C^1\), with \(V(0)=0\). Scalability means that coalition value depends only on its type masses, independently of how those masses are divided into clones; no homogeneity is assumed.
Use an ideal real-oracle model with exact evaluations of \(V\) and its bounded, nonnegative partial derivatives \(D_t\), and exact continuous random draws. At nondifferentiable points, a fixed one-sided derivative convention is part of the oracle; all radial derivative functions are integrable.
For focal type \(h\in T\), define \(a_h(\mu)=\int_0^1D_h(\lambda\mu)\,d\lambda\) and \(\Gamma_h^\infty=\sup_{0\le z\le\mu}D_h(z)/a_h(\mu)\). Set this ratio to \(1\) when numerator and denominator vanish, and to \(+\infty\) when only the denominator vanishes. Inputs include binary rational \(0<\epsilon,\delta<1\) and finite \(G\ge\Gamma_h^\infty\); the actual ratio is not supplied.
A type-level random-order sample represents the predecessor masses of a tagged infinitesimal unit of type \(h\) in the limit of uniformly ordering equal-mass clones. Without violations its predecessor vector is \(U\mu\), with fresh independent \(U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]\); its observed reward is \(D_h(z)\) at its actual predecessor vector \(z\).
Types have communicating representatives with independent local randomness; the focal representative is honest. Communication uses synchronous public broadcast and ideal perfectly hiding, binding, non-malleable commitments. An unbounded rushing adversary controls the other representatives and sees honest messages before acting in each round.
Refusing a required commitment opening is publicly detected and counts as one violation; multiple refusals in one sample count separately. Violations may change the generated order but cannot change the characteristic function or the marginal-reward rule. For every integer \(r\ge1\), the first \(r\) samples contain at most \(fr\) violations, for an unknown \(f\in[0,1]\).
The returned \(\widehat a_h\) must average actual marginal rewards over a completed sample prefix, which may precede termination; the empty-prefix value is \(0\). The protocol must terminate almost surely and return \(\widehat\epsilon\in[0,1]\).
What is the optimal worst-case permutation-sample complexity of a distributed sampling protocol and stopping rule satisfying \(\Pr[\widehat\epsilon\le\max\{\epsilon,4f\Gamma_h^\infty\}]=1\) and \(\Pr[\widehat a_h\ge(1-\widehat\epsilon)a_h(\mu)]\ge1-\delta\) against every admissible adversary?
Can these guarantees be achieved with sample count independent of clone count? State sample, oracle and arithmetic complexity in \(m,\tau,L,G,\epsilon,\delta\), and any dependence on \(f\).
For finite monotone coalitional games, adaptive permutation sampling achieves the corresponding guarantees with the supplied discrete max-to-mean ratio \(\Gamma\), using \(O(\Gamma\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\epsilon\delta)))\) samples in the worst case. [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19192#S4.SS3)
No result settling this precise continuous distributed sampling model was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one and specified the mass-space domain and zero empty-coalition value.
  • Interpreted scalability as invariance under splitting masses into clones, without imposing homogeneity.
  • Specified an ideal real-oracle model because arbitrary piecewise-C1 functions have no supplied finite representation or numerical accuracy model.
  • Required bounded nonnegative derivative values, integrability along the radial path, and a fixed one-sided convention at nondifferentiable points.
  • Carried over the discrete zero-over-zero convention; a finite supplied G excludes zero mean with positive maximum derivative.
  • Interpreted random order as sampling a tagged infinitesimal unit in the equal-mass clone limit, rather than permuting indivisible type blocks.
  • Used one communicating representative per supported type, with an honest focal representative, and carried over the broadcast, commitment, rushing and individual-refusal counting conventions.
  • Required the focal estimate to average actual marginal rewards at a retained completed prefix, including the zero initial value, rather than allowing an arbitrary upper estimate.
  • Interpreted minimization as worst-case sample complexity and distinguished sample count from oracle and arithmetic costs.

Partial solution: optimal complexity for $G\ge 2$, and improved bounds for all $G$

The optimal worst-case sample complexity is determined below for the entire parameter range $G\ge 2$, $0<\epsilon,\delta<1$:

$$ \boxed{ \Theta\!\left( 1+\frac{G-1}{\epsilon^2} \log\!\left(1+\frac{\epsilon(1-\delta)}{\delta}\right) \right). } $$

The protocol is independent of clone count and of the unknown violation rate $f$. For $G=1$, exactly one sample is sufficient and necessary in the worst case.

For $1<G<2$, I prove improved upper bounds and additional lower bounds, but do not determine the optimal complexity throughout that range. In particular, the displayed formula cannot be extended uniformly to that range.

Throughout, “worst-case sample complexity” means a uniform cap on the number of completed samples, rather than expected sample count. The supplied [discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19192#S4.SS3) is not used in the proofs.

1. Focal-specific sampling immune to rushing

The construction uses the stipulated honest focal representative. It protects that focal type; it does not construct a common order that simultaneously protects every possible focal representative.

For each sample, use three synchronous rounds:

  • Every representative other than $h$ commits to a contribution in $[0,1)$.
  • Those representatives open their commitments. A refused required opening is recorded as a violation and contributes the default value $0$. Invalid contributions also receive the default value.
  • After the opening deadline, the honest focal representative draws fresh $R\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1)$ and broadcasts

$$ U=(R+C)\bmod 1, $$
where $C$ is the sum modulo $1$ of the accepted contributions.

The sample has actual predecessor vector $U\mu$ and actual reward

$$ X=D_h(U\mu). $$

All contributions and defaults are fixed before $R$ is drawn. Conditional on the entire history preceding that draw, addition modulo $1$ preserves the uniform distribution. Consequently, $U$ is conditionally uniform, whatever the adversary’s earlier actions.

Applying this conditional-distribution statement successively proves that the sample rewards are independent and identically distributed, with

$$ X_i=D_h(U_i\mu), \qquad \mathbb E[X_i]=a_h(\mu). $$

The expectation identity follows from the assumed integrability of the radial derivative. Later adversarial messages do not change the completed sample.

Thus refusals cannot bias this construction. Every refused required opening is still counted separately, but the violation budget is unnecessary for the proof.

Write $a=a_h(\mu)$. If $a=0$, finite $G$ forces $D_h(z)=0$ everywhere: any positive numerator would make the ratio infinite. All rewards then vanish, and the reward guarantee is automatic.

If $a>0$, define, only for analysis,

$$ Y_i=\frac{X_i}{a}. $$

Then

$$ 0\le Y_i\le G, \qquad \mathbb E[Y_i]=1. $$

Every feasible input has $G\ge 1$: for positive mean this follows by integrating the upper bound, and for zero mean the prescribed ratio is $1$.

2. A stronger prefix-hitting bound

Set

$$ A=G-1, \qquad B=A+\epsilon, \qquad W_i=Y_i-1+\epsilon. $$

Thus $\mathbb E[W_i]=\epsilon$, and $W_i\le B$. Define

$$ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i, \qquad H=\inf\{n\ge 1:S_n\ge 0\}. $$

The event $H\le n$ means that some completed prefix of length at most $n$ has average reward at least $(1-\epsilon)a$.

Let $q_n=\Pr[H>n]$. For every positive integer $n$,

$$ \boxed{ q_n\le \frac{A}{A+\epsilon n}. } $$

To prove this, observe that on $\{H\le n\}$ the crossing overshoot satisfies $S_H\le B$, while on $\{H>n\}$ we have $S_n<0$. Hence

$$ S_{H\wedge n}\le B\,\mathbf 1_{\{H\le n\}}. $$

Also,

$$ S_{H\wedge n} = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf 1_{\{H\ge i\}}W_i. $$

The indicator depends only on previous increments and is independent of $W_i$. Taking expectations therefore gives

$$ \epsilon\,\mathbb E[H\wedge n] = \mathbb E[S_{H\wedge n}] \le B(1-q_n). $$

Because $H\wedge n$ equals $n$ on $\{H>n\}$ and is at least $1$ otherwise,

$$ \mathbb E[H\wedge n]\ge 1+(n-1)q_n. $$

Combining the inequalities yields

$$ \epsilon\bigl(1+(n-1)q_n\bigr) \le B(1-q_n), $$

and rearrangement gives the claimed bound.

This strengthens the previous bound $q_n\le A/(\epsilon n)$ and handles $\delta$ close to $1$ correctly.

3. An exponential refinement

For $A>0$, a second bound is

$$ \boxed{ q_n \le \frac{3A}{\epsilon n} \exp\!\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^2}{8(A+\epsilon)}\right). } $$

Here is a complete change-of-measure proof.

First,

$$ \operatorname{Var}(W_i) = \operatorname{Var}(Y_i) \le A, $$

because $Y_i^2\le GY_i$, so $\mathbb E[Y_i^2]-1\le G-1$.

Put

$$ t=\frac{\epsilon}{4(A+\epsilon)}, \qquad M=\mathbb E[e^{-tW_i}]. $$

We have $t\le 1/4$. For $Z=Y_i-1$, the inequalities $Z\ge -1$, $\mathbb E[Z]=0$, and $\mathbb E[Z^2]\le A$ give

$$ \mathbb E[e^{-tZ}] \le 1+\frac{t^2e^t}{2}\mathbb E[Z^2]. $$

Indeed, Taylor’s formula with integral remainder bounds the second derivative along the segment between $0$ and $Z$ by $e^tZ^2$. Consequently,

$$ M \le \exp\!\left(-t\epsilon+\frac{t^2e^tA}{2}\right) \le \exp\!\left(-\frac{\epsilon^2}{8(A+\epsilon)}\right). $$

For the last inequality, substitution of $t$ gives

$$ -t\epsilon+\frac{t^2e^tA}{2} \le -\frac{\epsilon^2}{A+\epsilon} \left(\frac14-\frac{e^{1/4}}{32}\right) \le -\frac{\epsilon^2}{8(A+\epsilon)}. $$

Define the tilted increment distribution by

$$ \frac{d\mathbb P_t}{d\mathbb P}(w) = \frac{e^{-tw}}{M}, $$

and let $\nu=\mathbb E_t[W_i]$. We next prove

$$ \nu\ge \frac{\epsilon}{2}, \qquad B-\nu\le \frac{3A}{2}. $$

The function $w\mapsto e^{-tw}$ is decreasing and has Lipschitz constant at most $te^t$ on the support, since $w\ge -1$. For an independent copy $W_i'$, the covariance identity gives

$$ -\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,e^{-tW_i}) = -\frac12 \mathbb E\!\left[ (W_i-W_i') (e^{-tW_i}-e^{-tW_i'}) \right] \le te^tA. $$

Jensen’s inequality gives $M\ge e^{-t\epsilon}$. Therefore

$$ 0\le \epsilon-\nu = -\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,e^{-tW_i})}{M} \le te^{t(1+\epsilon)}A \le 2tA. $$

It follows that

$$ \nu\ge \epsilon-2tA\ge \frac{\epsilon}{2}, $$

and, using $t\le 1/4$,

$$ B-\nu=A+\epsilon-\nu\le A+2tA\le \frac{3A}{2}. $$

The stopped-sum proof from Section 2 applies to any independent increments with positive mean $\nu$ and upper bound $B$. Under the tilted law it yields

$$ \mathbb P_t(H>n) \le \frac{B-\nu}{B+\nu(n-1)} \le \frac{B-\nu}{\nu n} \le \frac{3A}{\epsilon n}. $$

Finally, the likelihood ratio for the first $n$ increments is

$$ \frac{d\mathbb P}{d\mathbb P_t} = M^n e^{tS_n}. $$

On $\{H>n\}$, $S_n<0$, so $e^{tS_n}\le 1$. Thus

$$ q_n = M^n\mathbb E_t[e^{tS_n}\mathbf 1_{\{H>n\}}] \le M^n\mathbb P_t(H>n), $$

which proves the exponential refinement.

4. A valid protocol and general upper bound

After a deterministic number $N$ of completed samples, return the largest completed-prefix average:

$$ \widehat a_h = \max_{1\le k\le N} \frac1k\sum_{i=1}^kX_i, \qquad \widehat\epsilon=\epsilon. $$

A fixed tie-breaking rule selects the returned prefix. This obeys the restriction that the output average actual rewards over a completed prefix.

For $A>0$, define

$$ Q_1=\frac{A(1-\delta)}{\epsilon\delta}, $$

and

$$ Q_2= \frac{16(A+\epsilon)}{\epsilon^2} \log\!\left( 1+\frac{A\epsilon}{(A+\epsilon)\delta} \right). $$

Either $N\ge Q_1$ or $N\ge Q_2$ is sufficient.

The first assertion follows immediately from Section 2. For the second, put

$$ x=\frac{A\epsilon}{(A+\epsilon)\delta}. $$

At $n\ge Q_2$, Section 3 gives

$$ q_n \le \delta\, \frac{3x}{16\log(1+x)(1+x)^2} \le \delta. $$

The last inequality uses $\log(1+x)\ge x/(1+x)$.

Hence a deterministic budget of order

$$ \boxed{ 1+\min\left\{ \frac{(G-1)(1-\delta)}{\epsilon\delta}, \; \frac{G-1+\epsilon}{\epsilon^2} \log\!\left( 1+\frac{(G-1)\epsilon}{(G-1+\epsilon)\delta} \right) \right\} } $$

suffices for every feasible $G>1$.

The reward guarantee follows because estimator failure is precisely $H>N$. Furthermore, pathwise,

$$ \widehat\epsilon=\epsilon \le \max\{\epsilon,4f\Gamma_h^\infty\}. $$

The protocol terminates deterministically and satisfies both required guarantees against every admissible adversary, without knowing $f$ or the actual ratio.

When $G=1$ and $a>0$, the normalized radial reward satisfies $Y\le 1$ and $\mathbb E[Y]=1$, so $Y=1$ almost surely. One sample therefore suffices. At least one is necessary in the worst case because an empty prefix fails on a positive constant-derivative instance.

5. Matching lower bounds for $G\ge 2$

Define

$$ \mathcal D = \frac{A}{\epsilon^2} \log\!\left(1+\frac{\epsilon(1-\delta)}{\delta}\right). $$

I prove that every protocol with a uniform sample cap requires $\Omega(1+\mathcal D)$ when $A\ge 1$.

All lower-bound instances have one type of mass $1$ and use a nonviolating adversary. Thus $f=0$, and the first required guarantee forces $\widehat\epsilon\le\epsilon$ almost surely.

For a distribution taking a high derivative value $v_+$ with probability $p$ and a low value $v_-$ otherwise, use

$$ V(x)=v_+\min\{x,p\}+v_-\max\{x-p,0\}. $$

This function is continuous, monotone, nonnegative, piecewise linear, and satisfies $V(0)=0$. Its sampled derivative has exactly the specified distribution; the breakpoint convention affects only a probability-zero event.

If its mean is $1$ and its maximum derivative is at most $G$, it satisfies the ratio promise.

5.1 Two elementary lower bounds

Take

$$ v_-=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}, \qquad v_+=G, \qquad p=\frac{1+\epsilon}{2A+1+\epsilon}. $$

Direct substitution gives mean $1$. If all first $N$ samples are low, every permitted output prefix fails. Therefore

$$ N \ge \frac{\log(1/\delta)} {\log\!\left(1+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2A}\right)} \ge A\log\frac1\delta. $$

For $0<\epsilon\le 1/4$, the choice

$$ v_-=1-2\epsilon, \qquad v_+=G, \qquad p=\frac{2\epsilon}{A+2\epsilon} $$

similarly yields

$$ N \ge \frac{\log(1/\delta)} {\log(1+2\epsilon/A)} \ge \frac{A}{2\epsilon}\log\frac1\delta. $$

These arguments remain valid for adaptive stopping: extend the independent sample sequence to the uniform cap $N$. Missing the high value throughout those $N$ draws makes every available prefix fail, regardless of oracle knowledge.

The remaining difficulty is simultaneous small $\epsilon$ and small $\delta$. It is resolved by the next lemma.

5.2 A lattice-walk survival lemma

There is a universal constant $a_0>0$ such that the following holds.

Let $k\ge 1$ be an integer and $0<\eta\le 1/2$. Let independent increments $J_i$ take values $-1$ and $k$, with

$$ \Pr[J_i=k]=p=\frac{1+\eta}{k+1}. $$

For

$$ q_n^{(k,\eta)} = \Pr\!\left[ \sum_{i=1}^jJ_i<0 \text{ for every }1\le j\le n \right], $$

we have

$$ \boxed{ q_n^{(k,\eta)} \ge a_0 \min\left\{1,\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\right\} \exp\!\left(-\frac{3n\eta^2}{k}\right). } $$

Proof of the exact cyclic identity. If $B_n$ counts the positive increments, then

$$ q_n^{(k,\eta)} = \frac1n \mathbb E\!\left[(n-(k+1)B_n)_+\right]. $$

To justify this identity, condition on $B_n=b$. If $r=n-(k+1)b$ is positive, negate the increments. The resulting integer increments are $1$ and $-k$, with total $r$.

Exactly $r$ of the $n$ cyclic shifts have all partial sums strictly positive. Here is a proof that also handles periodic sequences. Extend cumulative sums $s_j$ to all integer indices periodically, so that

$$ s_{j+n}=s_j+r. $$

For each integer level $\ell$, let $j(\ell)$ be the last index with $s_{j(\ell)}\le\ell$. Such an index exists because the cumulative sums tend to opposite infinities in the two directions. Since upward increments are at most $1$, necessarily $s_{j(\ell)}=\ell$. All subsequent sums are larger than $\ell$, so the shift starting there is strictly positive.

Conversely, a strictly positive cyclic shift remains positive through every future period, since each period adds $r>0$. Its starting index is therefore $j(\ell)$ for its starting level $\ell$. Finally,

$$ j(\ell+r)=j(\ell)+n. $$

Thus precisely $r$ such indices occur per period of length $n$. Conditional on $B_n=b$, cyclic invariance now gives survival probability $r/n$. If $r\le 0$, strict survival is impossible. This proves the identity.

Proof of the quantitative bound. First suppose $n\ge k$. Under the critical binomial law with

$$ p_0=\frac1{k+1}, \qquad Z=B_n-np_0, \qquad \sigma^2=np_0(1-p_0), $$

we have $\sigma^2\ge 1/4$. Independence of centered Bernoulli variables gives

$$ \mathbb E[Z^4] = 3\sigma^4+\sigma^2\bigl(1-6p_0(1-p_0)\bigr) \le 7\sigma^4. $$

Hölder’s inequality consequently gives

$$ \mathbb E|Z| \ge \frac{(\mathbb E[Z^2])^{3/2}} {(\mathbb E[Z^4])^{1/2}} \ge \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt7}. $$

Because $\mathbb E[Z]=0$,

$$ \mathbb E[(-Z)_+]\ge\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt7}. $$

Set $M_0=4\sqrt7$. The tail estimate

$$ \mathbb E[(-Z)_+;\,Z<-M_0\sigma] \le \frac{\mathbb E[Z^2]}{M_0\sigma} = \frac{\sigma}{M_0} $$

shows that

$$ \mathbb E[(-Z)_+;\,-M_0\sigma\le Z<0] \ge \frac{\sigma}{4\sqrt7}. $$

The likelihood ratio from the critical binomial law to the law with parameter $p$ has logarithm

$$ -nK(p_0,p)+Z\beta, $$

where

$$ K(p_0,p) = p_0\log\frac{p_0}{p} + (1-p_0)\log\frac{1-p_0}{1-p}, $$

and

$$ \beta=\log\frac{p(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p)}. $$

The elementary inequality $\log u\le u-1$ gives

$$ K(p_0,p) \le \frac{(p-p_0)^2}{p(1-p)} \le \frac{2\eta^2}{k}. $$

Also,

$$ \beta = \log(1+\eta)+\log\frac{k}{k-\eta} \le 3\eta. $$

On the truncated negative region, therefore, the likelihood ratio is at least

$$ \exp\!\left( -\frac{2n\eta^2}{k} -3M_0\eta\sqrt{\frac nk} \right) \ge \exp\!\left(-\frac{3n\eta^2}{k}-252\right). $$

The last inequality uses $3M_0\sqrt u\le u+9M_0^2/4$, with $u=n\eta^2/k$.

Applying the exact cyclic identity and observing that

$$ \frac{k+1}{n}\sigma=\sqrt{\frac kn} $$

proves the asserted bound for $n\ge k$, with

$$ a_0=\frac{e^{-252}}{4\sqrt7}. $$

For $n<k$, the event that every increment is $-1$ gives

$$ q_n^{(k,\eta)} \ge (1-p)^n \ge (1-p)^k \ge e^{-3}. $$

Indeed, $\log(1-p)\ge-p/(1-p)$ and

$$ \frac{kp}{1-p} = \frac{k(1+\eta)}{k-\eta} \le 3. $$

This also implies the claimed bound with the same $a_0$. The lemma is proved.

5.3 Realizing the walk as marginal rewards

Put $s=1-\epsilon$. Choose $0<d\le s$ and define

$$ k=\left\lfloor\frac{A+\epsilon}{d}\right\rfloor, \qquad v_-=s-d, \qquad v_+=s+kd, \qquad \eta=\frac{\epsilon}{d}. $$

Then $v_-\ge 0$ and $v_+\le G$. With

$$ p=\frac{1+\eta}{k+1}, $$

the mean reward is

$$ v_-+(v_+-v_-)p = s-d+(k+1)dp = s+\epsilon = 1. $$

Moreover, the increments determining prefix success satisfy

$$ \frac{Y_i-s}{d}\in\{-1,k\}, $$

with precisely the probabilities from the lemma. Thus its survival probability is a lower bound on the failure probability of every permitted output rule.

When $A\ge 1$, $\epsilon\le 1/4$, and $d\le s$,

$$ \frac{A}{2d}\le k\le\frac{2A}{d}. $$

This follows from $\lfloor x\rfloor\ge x/2$ for $x\ge1$, together with $A+\epsilon\le 2A$.

5.4 Matching dependence for small parameters

Choose universal constants

$$ \delta_*=\frac{a_0}{4}, \qquad c_*=\frac{a_0^2}{128}. $$

Suppose first that $\epsilon,\delta\le\delta_*$.

If $\delta\ge\epsilon$, choose

$$ d=\min\left\{1-\epsilon,\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\right\}. $$

Then

$$ \delta\le\eta\le\frac{4\delta}{3}\le\frac12. $$

For any positive integer

$$ n\le c_*\frac{A}{\epsilon\delta}, $$

the bounds on $k$ imply

$$ \frac nk\le\frac{2c_*}{\delta^2}, \qquad \frac{n\eta^2}{k}\le\frac{8c_*}{3}. $$

If $n\ge k$, the survival lemma gives

$$ q_n \ge \frac{a_0\delta}{\sqrt{2c_*}}e^{-8c_*} > \delta. $$

If $n<k$, its proof gives $q_n\ge e^{-3}>\delta$. Hence

$$ N=\Omega\!\left(\frac{A}{\epsilon\delta}\right) \qquad (\epsilon\le\delta\le\delta_*). $$

If $\delta<\epsilon$, choose $d=s$ and put $\ell=\log(\epsilon/\delta)$. For

$$ n\le c_*\frac{A}{\epsilon^2}(1+\ell), $$

we have

$$ \frac nk\le \frac{2c_*}{\epsilon^2}(1+\ell), \qquad \frac{n\eta^2}{k}\le \frac{8c_*}{3}(1+\ell). $$

When $n\ge k$, the lemma yields

$$ \frac{q_n}{\delta} \ge \frac{a_0e^{-8c_*}}{\sqrt{2c_*}}\, \frac{e^{(1-8c_*)\ell}}{\sqrt{1+\ell}} > 1. $$

To verify the last inequality, the first factor is $8e^{-8c_*}>1$, and the second is at least $1$: its logarithmic derivative is

$$ 1-8c_*-\frac{1}{2(1+\ell)}>0. $$

For $n<k$, again $q_n\ge e^{-3}>\delta$. Therefore

$$ N= \Omega\!\left( \frac{A}{\epsilon^2} \left(1+\log\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\right) \right) \qquad (\delta<\epsilon\le\delta_*). $$

These two bounds imply $N=\Omega(\mathcal D)$, since respectively

$$ \mathcal D\le\frac{A}{\epsilon\delta} $$

and

$$ \mathcal D \le \frac{A}{\epsilon^2} \left(1+\log\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\right). $$

5.5 Covering all remaining parameters

If $\epsilon\ge\delta_*$, Section 5.1 gives

$$ N\ge A\log\frac1\delta, $$

whereas

$$ \mathcal D \le \frac{A}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta \le \delta_*^{-2}A\log\frac1\delta. $$

If $\epsilon<\delta_*$ and $\delta\ge\delta_*$, the other elementary bound gives

$$ N\ge\frac{A}{2\epsilon}\log\frac1\delta. $$

Using $1-\delta\le\log(1/\delta)$,

$$ \mathcal D \le \frac{A(1-\delta)}{\epsilon\delta} \le \frac{A}{\epsilon\delta_*}\log\frac1\delta. $$

Thus $N=\Omega(\mathcal D)$ in every remaining case. At least one sample is necessary on a positive-mean instance, so the full lower bound is $\Omega(1+\mathcal D)$.

For the matching upper bound, when $\delta\ge1/4$, set

$$ x=\frac{\epsilon(1-\delta)}{\delta}\le3. $$

Then $\log(1+x)\ge x/4$, so $Q_1\le4\mathcal D$.

When $\delta<1/4$, use $A+\epsilon\le2A$ and concavity of the logarithm:

$$ Q_2 \le \frac{32A}{\epsilon^2}\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\delta}\right) \le \frac{128A}{3\epsilon^2} \log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon(1-\delta)}{\delta}\right). $$

This proves the stated optimal order for all $G\ge2$.

6. Why the remaining range requires a different analysis

Let $0<A<1$, so $G=1+A$, and define

$$ p=\frac{A}{1+A}, \qquad V(x)=(1+A)\max\{x-p,0\}. $$

Its sampled derivative is $0$ with probability $p$ and $1+A$ otherwise. Its mean is $1$, and its ratio is exactly $G$.

On the event that the first sample is zero, every prefix of length $k$ has average at most

$$ (1+A)\frac{k-1}{k}. $$

This is strictly below $1-\epsilon$ whenever

$$ k<\frac{1+A}{A+\epsilon}. $$

Consequently, if $\delta<p$, every valid uniform sample cap satisfies

$$ \boxed{ N\ge\frac{1+A}{A+\epsilon}. } $$

For example, take $\epsilon\to0$, $A=\epsilon^3$, and $\delta=A/2$. The lower bound is of order $1/\epsilon$, while

$$ 1+\frac{A}{\epsilon^2} \log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon(1-\delta)}{\delta}\right) $$

remains bounded. Thus the optimal formula proved for $G\ge2$ fails uniformly near $G=1$.

One additional regime is settled: if $0<A\le\epsilon$ and $\delta=A/2$, this lower bound and Section 4 give

$$ N_{\mathrm{optimal}}=\Theta\!\left(\frac1\epsilon\right). $$

Indeed, $A+\epsilon\le2\epsilon$, and the logarithm in $Q_2$ has argument between $2$ and $3$.
