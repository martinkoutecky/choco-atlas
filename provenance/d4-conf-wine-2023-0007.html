The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(\mathcal R\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_r\) summing to \(1\), and a finite sequence of items whose per-capita supplies \(q_t\) and type values \(v_{rt}\) are revealed on arrival, each policy must irrevocably choose per-capita allocations \(a_{rt}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{r\in\mathcal R}\nu_r a_{rt}\le q_t\). Type \(r\)'s utility is \(u_r=\sum_t v_{rt}a_{rt}\), and welfare is \(\exp(\sum_r\nu_r\log u_r)\). On instances where every offline welfare-maximizing allocation has utility ratio at most \(\kappa\), determine the best worst-case competitive ratio achievable by an online policy as a function of the type support, masses, and their encoding length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal R\) of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_r\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) divisible items. On arrival of item \(t\), its nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supply \(q_t\) and values \(v_{rt}\) are revealed. A policy immediately and irrevocably chooses real allocations \(a_{rt}\ge0\) with \(\sum_r\nu_ra_{rt}\le q_t\).
Utilities are \(u_r=\sum_t v_{rt}a_{rt}\), and welfare is \(W(a)=\prod_r u_r^{\nu_r}\), interpreted as zero if any utility is zero. Require \(\sum_tq_tv_{rt}>0\) for every type.
An instance is \(\kappa\)-impartial, for \(\kappa\ge1\), if every offline welfare-maximizing allocation satisfies \(\max_r u_r^*/\min_r u_r^*\le\kappa\). Policies know the support and masses initially, but receive no future information, horizon, or impartiality bound.
For a possibly randomized policy \(A\), its competitive ratio is \(\sup_I W^*(I)/\mathbb E[W(A(I))]\), over fixed adversarial sequences satisfying the promise. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any additional parameters.

What is the best achievable worst-case competitive ratio for this continuous online problem as a function of \(\kappa,\tau,(\nu_r)_r\), and \(L\)?

For finite societies of \(N\) equally weighted agents, the verified competitive lower bound is \(\min\{(\log\mu^*)^{1-o(1)},(N-1)/e\}\), where \(\mu^*\) is the actual offline optimal utility ratio; it holds even for binary values. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03077)
For general values, a finite-agent algorithm without an impartiality prediction achieves \(O((\log\mu^*)^2(\log\log\mu^*)^2)\) for large \(\mu^*\). No result settling the exact mass-weighted problem above was located. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03077)

1. Offline optimality and randomization

Write
$$ B_r=\sum_tq_tv_{rt}. $$
By hypothesis, $B_r>0$ for every type. The allocation $a_{rt}=q_t$ is feasible and gives utility $B_r$, so positive welfare is attainable.

Every feasible coordinate satisfies
$$ 0\le a_{rt}\le\frac{q_t}{\nu_r}. $$
The allocation set is therefore compact. Welfare is continuous, including at zero utilities, so an optimum exists and has positive utilities.

Unique optimal utilities

On positive utility vectors, welfare maximization is equivalent to maximizing
$$ \Phi(u)=\sum_r\nu_r\log u_r. $$
This function is strictly concave because every mass is positive. If two optimal allocations had different utility vectors, their midpoint would be feasible and would have strictly larger $\Phi$. Hence the optimal utility vector $u^*$ is unique.

Consequently, the promise about every maximizing allocation is equivalent to
$$ \mu:=\frac{\max_ru_r^*}{\min_ru_r^*}\le\kappa. $$

An optimality condition

Suppose $a_{rt}^*>0$. For sufficiently small $\delta>0$, transfer total item amount $\delta$ from type $r$ to type $s$. Their per-capita allocations change by $-\delta/\nu_r$ and $\delta/\nu_s$. The derivative of $\Phi$ is
$$ -\frac{v_{rt}}{u_r^*}+\frac{v_{st}}{u_s^*}. $$
Optimality implies that this derivative is nonpositive. Thus
$$ a_{rt}^*>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{v_{rt}}{u_r^*} = \max_s\frac{v_{st}}{u_s^*}. $$
In particular, with $v_t^{\max}=\max_sv_{st}>0$,
$$ a_{rt}^*>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{rt}\ge\frac{v_t^{\max}}{\mu}. $$

Whenever $q_tv_t^{\max}>0$, an optimum also uses the entire supply. Otherwise, allocating some unused supply to a positively valuing type would strictly increase welfare.

Randomization cannot improve the unrestricted optimum

The weighted geometric mean
$$ G(u)=\prod_ru_r^{\nu_r} $$
is concave. Indeed, for positive $u$ and any direction $z$,
$$ D^2G(u)[z,z] = G(u)\left[ \left(\sum_r\nu_r\frac{z_r}{u_r}\right)^2 - \sum_r\nu_r\left(\frac{z_r}{u_r}\right)^2 \right]\le0. $$
The inequality is weighted Cauchy–Schwarz. Continuity extends concavity to nonnegative vectors.

Given a randomized policy, define a deterministic policy whose allocation on each input prefix is the randomized policy’s expected allocation on that prefix. This is an online, feasible policy. On every fixed sequence, its utilities are the expected randomized utilities, and hence
$$ W(\text{mean policy})\ge\mathbb E[W(\text{randomized policy})]. $$
No efficient procedure for computing these expectations is asserted. The existence of the mean policy suffices for the lower bounds.

2. The entropy upper bound

The equal-per-capita policy allocates
$$ a_{rt}=q_t. $$
It is feasible because $\sum_r\nu_r=1$, and its utilities are $B_r$.

For every feasible allocation,
$$ u_r\le\frac{B_r}{\nu_r}. $$
Therefore
$$ \frac{W^*}{W_{\mathrm{eq}}} \le \prod_r\nu_r^{-\nu_r} = E(\nu). $$
Also,
$$ \log E(\nu) = \sum_r\nu_r\log\frac1{\nu_r} \le \log\left(\sum_r\nu_r\frac1{\nu_r}\right) = \log\tau, $$
by Jensen’s inequality. Thus $E(\nu)\le\tau$.

The policy simply copies each supply into $\tau$ output coordinates, requiring $O(m\tau)$ output entries and polynomial bit time.

3. A weighted water-filling lemma

Consider sequences in which, for each item $t$, values belong to $\{0,b_t\}$ for some common $b_t\ge0$.

Maintain current utilities $U_r$, initially zero. Ignore items with $q_tb_t=0$ or with no positively valuing type. Otherwise let
$$ T_t=\{r:v_{rt}=b_t\}. $$
Choose the unique water level $z$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{r\in T_t}\nu_r(z-U_r)_+=q_tb_t, $$
and allocate
$$ a_{rt}=\frac{(z-U_r)_+}{b_t} \quad(r\in T_t), $$
with zero allocations elsewhere. Existence and uniqueness follow because the left side is continuous, eventually strictly increasing, and unbounded.

Every positive recipient finishes at utility $z$, while every interested type finishes at or above $z$.

A supply inequality

Let $u_r$ be the final water-filling utilities, and let $w_r>0$ be the utilities of any feasible benchmark.

Fix $s\ge0$ and any set $S$ satisfying $u_r\le s$ for all $r\in S$. Let $\mathcal T(S)$ consist of items positively valued by at least one member of $S$. Benchmark feasibility gives
$$ \sum_{r\in S}\nu_rw_r \le \sum_{t\in\mathcal T(S)}q_tb_t. $$

For a fixed type $r$, its utility from these items is at most $\min\{s,u_r\}$. The bound by $u_r$ is immediate. To prove the bound by $s$, consider its last positive receipt from an item in $\mathcal T(S)$. An interested type in $S$ has utility after that item at most its final utility, hence at most $s$. The recipient finishes at the water level, which is no larger. All of its utility contributions from $\mathcal T(S)$ have accumulated by this last receipt.

Water filling distributes all positively valued supply. Summing these contribution bounds therefore yields
$$ \sum_{r\in S}\nu_rw_r \le \sum_r\nu_r\min\{s,u_r\} \le s. $$
Taking $S=\{r:u_r=0\}$ and $s=0$ shows that every final $u_r$ is positive.

Welfare bound

Define
$$ \eta=\frac{\max_rw_r}{\min_rw_r}, \qquad J=1+\lfloor\log_2\eta\rfloor. $$
I claim that
$$ \frac{\prod_rw_r^{\nu_r}}{\prod_ru_r^{\nu_r}} \le2eJ. $$

Put $h=\min_rw_r$. Partition the types into nonempty classes
$$ C_j=\{r:2^jh\le w_r<2^{j+1}h\}, \qquad p_j=\sum_{r\in C_j}\nu_r. $$
There are at most $J$ occupied classes.

Apply the supply inequality to
$$ S=\{r\in C_j:u_r\le s\}. $$
Since $w_r\ge2^jh$ on this set,
$$ \sum_{\substack{r\in C_j\\u_r\le s}}\nu_r \le\frac{s}{2^jh}. $$
Conditional on membership in $C_j$, use probabilities $\nu_r/p_j$. Then, for $x\ge0$,
$$ \Pr\!\left(u_r<2^jhp_je^{-x}\mid r\in C_j\right)\le e^{-x}. $$
Integrating this tail bound gives
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[\left(\log\frac{2^jhp_j}{u_r}\right)_+ \middle|r\in C_j\right]\le1. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_{r\in C_j}\nu_r\log u_r \ge p_j\log(2^jhp_j)-p_j. $$
Since $w_r<2^{j+1}h$,
$$ \sum_{r\in C_j}\nu_r\log\frac{w_r}{u_r} \le p_j(1+\log2-\log p_j). $$
The class masses sum to $1$, and their entropy is at most $\log J$. Summing and exponentiating proves the claim.

If all benchmark utilities equal $c$, there is no factor-of-two loss from class rounding. The same tail argument directly gives
$$ \prod_ru_r^{\nu_r}\ge\frac ce. $$

4. General values: a prediction-free polynomial-time policy

This section proves the stated general upper bound rather than assuming it from the notes.

A fixed rounding depth

Fix an integer $K\ge1$. For an item with $q_tv_t^{\max}>0$, define
$$ d_t= \max\left\{ 1, \left\lceil \log_2 \frac{v_t^{\max}}{\min_{r:v_{rt}>0}v_{rt}} \right\rceil \right\}. $$
For every ignored item, set $d_t=1$ by convention.

Replace a nonignored item by sub-items indexed by
$$ 1\le\ell\le\min\{K,d_t\}. $$
Each sub-item has supply $q_t/K$ and binary values
$$ \widehat v_{rt\ell} = \begin{cases} v_t^{\max}/2^\ell,&v_{rt}\ge v_t^{\max}/2^\ell,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
Run water filling on these sub-items and sum their allocations. This defines a feasible policy $A_K$.

Suppose
$$ K\ge K_0:=\max\{1,\lceil\log_2\mu\rceil\}. $$
For every positive optimal coordinate $a_{rt}^*$, there is a retained threshold satisfying
$$ \frac{v_{rt}}2 \le \frac{v_t^{\max}}{2^\ell} \le v_{rt}. $$
To check the depth, if $v_{rt}=v_t^{\max}$ choose $\ell=1$. Otherwise choose
$$ \ell=\left\lceil\log_2\frac{v_t^{\max}}{v_{rt}}\right\rceil. $$
The optimality condition gives $\ell\le K_0$, and the definition of $d_t$ gives $\ell\le d_t$.

Assign $a_{rt}^*/K$ to one such sub-item. Each sub-item receives a subset of the original optimal allocation divided by $K$, so its supply constraint holds. The resulting benchmark utilities satisfy
$$ \frac{u_r^*}{2K}\le w_r\le\frac{u_r^*}{K}. $$
Thus their ratio is at most $2\mu$, and their welfare is at least $W^*/(2K)$.

Applying the preceding lemma, and using that original values dominate rounded values, gives
$$ \frac{W^*}{W(A_K)} \le 4eK\bigl(2+\lfloor\log_2\mu\rfloor\bigr). $$

Removing the unknown depth

Use depths and weights
$$ K_j=2^j, \qquad p_j=\frac1{(j+1)(j+2)} \quad(j\ge0). $$
The weights telescope to $1$.

Normalize expert $A_{K_j}$ by multiplying its virtual utilities and allocations by $K_j$. Its normalized execution processes each retained sub-item with supply $q_t$.

At arrival $t$, put
$$ D_t=\max\{1,d_1,\ldots,d_t\}, \qquad h_t=\lceil\log_2D_t\rceil. $$
All experts with $j\ge h_t$ have retained every level encountered so far, so their normalized histories coincide.

Maintain separate normalized states for $j<h_t$ and one common state for $j\ge h_t$. When $h_t$ increases, copy the previous common state to initialize newly separated experts before processing the current item. This reproduces their histories exactly.

Let $\alpha_{rt}^{(j)}$ be expert $j$’s normalized allocation on the current item, summed over retained levels, and let $\alpha_{rt}^{(\infty)}$ be the common state’s allocation. Allocate
$$ a_{rt}^{\mathrm{mix}} = \sum_{j<h_t}\frac{p_j}{K_j}\alpha_{rt}^{(j)} + \frac{2p_{h_t}}{K_{h_t}}\alpha_{rt}^{(\infty)}. $$

Each separated expert, after division by $K_j$, consumes at most $q_t$. The common normalized execution consumes at most $d_tq_t\le K_{h_t}q_t$. Hence actual consumption is at most
$$ q_t\left(\sum_{j<h_t}p_j+2p_{h_t}\right) = q_t\left( 1-\frac1{h_t+1} +\frac2{(h_t+1)(h_t+2)} \right) \le q_t. $$

Fix any expert $j$. When separated, its allocation appears with coefficient $p_j$. When common, its normalized allocation is the common allocation, and
$$ \frac{p_j}{K_j} \le \frac{p_{h_t}}{K_{h_t}} \le \frac{2p_{h_t}}{K_{h_t}}. $$
Thus the mixture dominates $p_j$ times this expert’s allocation coordinatewise at every arrival. Welfare is monotone and homogeneous, so
$$ W(A^{\mathrm{mix}})\ge p_jW(A_{K_j}). $$

For analysis only, choose the smallest $j_0$ with $2^{j_0}\ge K_0$. Then $2^{j_0}\le2K_0$, and
$$ \frac{W^*}{W(A^{\mathrm{mix}})} \le 4e\,2^{j_0} \bigl(2+\lfloor\log_2\mu\rfloor\bigr) (j_0+1)(j_0+2) = O(F(\mu)). $$
The policy never computes $\mu$ or $\kappa$.

Finally, average this policy’s allocation with the equal-per-capita allocation. The resulting welfare is at least half that of either component. Therefore a single deterministic policy satisfies
$$ \frac{W^*}{W(A)} = O\!\left(\min\{E(\nu),F(\mu)\}\right) \le O\!\left(\min\{E(\nu),F(\kappa)\}\right). $$

Bit complexity

Let $D=\max_td_t$. Explicit binary encoding implies $D=O(L)$: ratios between positive input values have logarithms bounded by their encoding lengths.

At one arrival, the number of normalized water-filling operations is at most
$$ \sum_{j<h_t}2^j+d_t = 2^{h_t}-1+d_t = O(D). $$
A water-filling operation can be implemented by sorting current utilities, scanning candidate filled sets, and solving the resulting linear equation. It uses
$$ O(\tau\log(\tau+1)) $$
rational arithmetic operations.

Here is an explicit denominator bound. Give the masses a common denominator $M$, of bit length $O(L)$. If current utilities have common denominator $Q$, a water level for a filled set $S$ is
$$ z= \frac{q_tb+\sum_{r\in S}\nu_rU_r}{\sum_{r\in S}\nu_r}. $$
Writing $\nu_r=n_r/M$, a common denominator for this update divides
$$ Q\,C\sum_{r\in S}n_r, $$
where $C$ is a denominator of $q_tb$. Both $C$ and the positive integer $\sum_{r\in S}n_r$ have $O(L+D)$ bits. Thus each update increases a common denominator’s bit length by at most $O(L+D)$.

Apply this bound to a common denominator covering all virtual states, enlarging it after every update. There are $O(mD)$ updates. Their utility magnitudes have logarithms $O(L+\log(mD+2))$, since each update adds bounded input utility and every mass is at least $2^{-O(L)}$.

For the current item, division by its rounded values adds only a common input numerator factor and powers of two. The mixture coefficients add $O(D)$ denominator bits. Consequently all state and output rationals have bit length bounded by
$$ B=O\!\left(mD(L+D)+L+\log(mD+2)\right). $$
Using the conservative bound $O(B^3)$ for exact rational arithmetic gives total bit time
$$ O\!\left(mD\tau\log(\tau+1)B^3\right) = \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
The policy stores $O(\tau(1+\log(D+1)))$ virtual utility coordinates. Neither the final $L$ nor the horizon is required beforehand.

5. A lower bound for any partition of the support

Partition the actual types into $J$ nonempty blocks, with block masses
$$ p_g=\sum_{r\in g}\nu_r. $$
All constructed values will be identical within a block. Define
$$ p_{\min}=\min_gp_g, \qquad p_{\max}=\max_gp_g. $$

Fix $0<\varepsilon\le1$, and choose a power of two $B$ satisfying
$$ B\ge1+\frac1{\varepsilon p_{\min}}. $$

I prove that every policy has a binary-valued instance with $m=J$, impartiality at most
$$ B^{J-1}\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}, $$
and competitive ratio at least
$$ \frac{\prod_gp_g^{-p_g}}{e(1+\varepsilon)}. $$

Construction and online utility bounds

By Section 1, it suffices to construct the sequence against the deterministic mean policy.

Initially every block is active. At stage $i$, supply
$$ q_i=B^{i-1} $$
and give value $1$ to all types in active blocks, and value $0$ to all other types.

Let $S_i$ be the total active mass. Define the block’s average per-capita allocation on this item by
$$ A_{gi}=\frac1{p_g}\sum_{r\in g}\nu_ra_{ri}. $$
Feasibility implies that some active block $g_i$ satisfies
$$ A_{g_i i}\le\frac{q_i}{S_i}. $$
Remove that block after this item.

Its final average utility, denoted $\bar u_{g_i}$, satisfies
$$ \bar u_{g_i} \le \frac{q_i}{S_i} + \frac1{p_{g_i}}\sum_{t<i}q_t \le \frac{q_i}{S_i} + \frac{q_i}{p_{g_i}(B-1)}. $$

This procedure uses expected decisions on fixed prefixes, never realized random coins. Once all stages have been determined, the resulting sequence is a fixed adversarial sequence.

Offline certificate

Allocate item $i$ entirely to block $g_i$, equally per capita within that block. Every type in it receives utility
$$ u_r^*=\frac{q_i}{p_{g_i}}. $$
These utilities increase strictly in removal order because
$$ B>\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}. $$

For any feasible utility vector $w$,
$$ \sum_r\nu_r\frac{w_r}{u_r^*} = \sum_i\sum_r\frac{\nu_ra_{ri}v_{ri}}{u_r^*} \le \sum_i\frac{q_i}{q_i/p_{g_i}} = \sum_ip_{g_i} = 1. $$
The inequality holds because the designated block has the smallest proposed utility among blocks interested in item $i$.

If any $w_r=0$, its welfare is zero and it cannot improve the proposed allocation. Otherwise, $\log x\le x-1$ gives
$$ \sum_r\nu_r\log\frac{w_r}{u_r^*} \le \sum_r\nu_r\left(\frac{w_r}{u_r^*}-1\right) \le0. $$
This proves optimality. Uniqueness of optimal utilities establishes the promise for every maximizer, with
$$ \mu\le B^{J-1}\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}}. $$

Every type values the first item, so the required positivity condition also holds.

Welfare comparison

Within each block, concavity of $\log$ gives
$$ W(A)\le\prod_g\bar u_g^{p_g}. $$
If online welfare is zero, the lower bound is immediate. Otherwise,
$$ \frac{q_i/p_{g_i}}{\bar u_{g_i}} \ge \frac{S_i/p_{g_i}} {1+S_i/(p_{g_i}(B-1))} \ge \frac{S_i/p_{g_i}}{1+\varepsilon}. $$
Multiplication with block weights yields
$$ \frac{W^*}{W(A)} \ge \frac1{1+\varepsilon} \exp\left( -\sum_gp_g\log p_g + \sum_ip_{g_i}\log S_i \right). $$

Set $S_{J+1}=0$. The intervals $[S_{i+1},S_i]$ partition $[0,1]$ and have lengths $p_{g_i}$. Since $\log x$ is increasing,
$$ \sum_ip_{g_i}\log S_i \ge \int_0^1\log x\,dx = -1. $$
This proves the claimed lower bound, including against randomized policies.

If $L_\nu$ is the encoding length of the original support and masses, the constructed instance has length
$$ O\!\left(L_\nu+\tau J+J^2(1+\log B)\right). $$
Indeed, it has a binary $\tau$-by-$J$ valuation matrix, and supply $q_i$ has $O(1+i\log B)$ bits.

6. A mass-sensitive lower bound at intermediate impartiality

Define the truncated entropy quantity
$$ E_k(\nu) = \prod_r\min\left\{k,\frac1{\nu_r}\right\}^{\nu_r} \qquad(k\ge1). $$
It satisfies
$$ 1\le E_k(\nu)\le\min\{k,E(\nu)\}. $$

A partition lemma

For every integer $k\ge1$, the support can be partitioned into $J\le\min\{k,\tau\}$ blocks such that
$$ p_g\ge\frac1k \quad\text{for every block}, $$
and
$$ \prod_gp_g^{-p_g}\ge\frac{E_k(\nu)}3. $$

To construct the partition, make every type with mass at least $1/k$ a singleton block. Process the remaining types in any order, closing a block whenever its accumulated mass reaches $1/k$. Each such completed block has mass less than $2/k$.

A residual block, if present, has mass $\delta<1/k$. If a completed small-type block exists, merge the residual into it. Otherwise merge it into a heavy singleton. If no heavy singleton exists, a completed small-type block must exist because total mass is $1$.

All final block masses are at least $1/k$, so $J\le k$.

Put
$$ H_k=\sum_r\nu_r\log\frac1{\max\{\nu_r,1/k\}}, \qquad H(p)=-\sum_gp_g\log p_g. $$
Then
$$ H_k-H(p) = \sum_r\nu_r \log\frac{p_{g(r)}}{\max\{\nu_r,1/k\}}. $$

If a completed small-type block exists, its possible residual merge produces mass less than $3/k$. Every heavy type remains a singleton. Thus every summand’s logarithmic factor is at most $\log3$, and
$$ H_k-H(p)\le\log3. $$

Otherwise all light types have total mass $\delta<1/k$ and are merged with one heavy type of mass $h$. Only this merged block contributes to the difference:
$$ H_k-H(p) = h\log\frac{h+\delta}{h} + \delta\log\bigl(k(h+\delta)\bigr). $$
Using $\log(1+x)\le x$ and $h+\delta\le1$,
$$ H_k-H(p) \le \delta(1+\log k) \le \frac{1+\log k}{k} \le1<\log3. $$
Here the penultimate inequality follows from $\log k\le k-1$. Exponentiating proves the partition lemma.

Applying the lower-bound construction

Use this partition, set $\varepsilon=1$, and let $B$ be the smallest power of two at least $k+1$. Then
$$ B<2(k+1)\le4k, \qquad B\ge1+\frac1{p_{\min}}. $$
The construction in Section 5 has impartiality at most
$$ B^{J-1}\frac{p_{\max}}{p_{\min}} \le (4k)^{J-1}k \le (4k)^k. $$
Its competitive lower bound is at least
$$ \frac{E_k(\nu)}{6e}. $$

Therefore, whenever $\kappa\ge(4k)^k$, every policy has competitive ratio at least
$$ \boxed{ \max\left\{ 1,\, \frac1{6e} \prod_r\min\left\{k,\frac1{\nu_r}\right\}^{\nu_r} \right\}. } $$
The witness has
$$ m=J\le\min\{k,\tau\} $$
and encoding length
$$ O\!\left(L_\nu+\tau J+J^2\log(k+1)\right). $$
For a prescribed input-length cap, this lower bound applies when the cap permits this witness.

Consequences

For equal masses,
$$ E_k(\nu)=\min\{k,\tau\}. $$
For $\log\kappa\ge4$, the choice
$$ k= \left\lfloor \frac{\log\kappa}{\log(4\log\kappa)} \right\rfloor $$
is admissible: $k\le\log\kappa$, and hence
$$ k\log(4k) \le k\log(4\log\kappa) \le \log\kappa. $$
Thus the explicit equal-mass lower bound has order
$$ \min\left\{\tau,\frac{\log\kappa}{\log\log\kappa}\right\} $$
as $\kappa$ grows, subject to the stated encoding requirement.

For unequal masses, $E_k(\nu)$ records how much mass lies in types smaller than the scale $1/k$. Grouping improves on applying the original construction directly to every type, which would require supply growth controlled by $\nu_{\min}$.

Taking singleton blocks in Section 5 also recovers the unrestricted bounds
$$ \max\{1,E(\nu)/e\} \le \text{optimal unrestricted ratio} \le E(\nu). $$
The lower inequality follows by letting $\varepsilon$ approach zero; the necessary impartiality and input length are then allowed to grow. Thus the unrestricted ratio is $\Theta(E(\nu))$, with universal constants.

7. Perfect impartiality and an exact two-type result

A prediction-free $e$-competitive policy for $\kappa=1$

For each item, retain only the types attaining its maximum value:
$$ T_t=\{r:v_{rt}=v_t^{\max}\}. $$
Run weighted water filling on these types, using common value $b_t=v_t^{\max}$.

This policy receives no impartiality bound. Its guarantee here is restricted to perfectly impartial instances.

When $\mu=1$, write $u_r^*=c$ for every type. The optimality condition from Section 1 implies
$$ a_{rt}^*>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{rt}=v_t^{\max}. $$

Exact ratio for two equally weighted types

Assume $\tau=2$, $\nu_1=\nu_2=1/2$, and $\mu=1$.

For the maximum-value water-filling policy, let the final utilities be $u_1,u_2$. Both this policy and the optimum allocate all positive supply to maximum-valuing types. Therefore
$$ \frac{u_1+u_2}{2} = \sum_tq_tv_t^{\max} = c. $$

Let $s=\min\{u_1,u_2\}$. Apply the supply inequality from Section 3 to a singleton type attaining this minimum. Its benchmark utility is $c$, so
$$ \frac c2 \le \frac12\min\{s,u_1\}+\frac12\min\{s,u_2\} = s. $$
Also $s\le c$, and the other utility equals $2c-s$. Hence
$$ W(A)^2=s(2c-s). $$
This expression is increasing for $s\in[c/2,c]$, so
$$ W(A)^2\ge\frac c2\cdot\frac{3c}{2} =\frac{3c^2}{4}. $$
Since $W^*=c$, the competitive ratio is at most $2/\sqrt3$.

For the matching lower bound, give a first item with supply $1/2$ and value $1$ for both types. Against the deterministic mean policy, choose a type whose allocation on this item is $x\le1/2$. Give a second item with supply $1/2$ and value $1$ only for the other type.

The selected type’s final utility is $x$. Feasibility bounds the other type’s final utility by $2-x$. Thus
$$ W(A)\le\sqrt{x(2-x)}\le\frac{\sqrt3}{2}. $$
The last inequality holds because $x(2-x)$ is increasing on $[0,1/2]$.

Offline, allocate the first item entirely to the selected type and the second entirely to the other. Both utilities are $1$. Every feasible allocation has weighted arithmetic mean utility at most $1$, so weighted arithmetic–geometric mean proves optimality. The unique optimal utility vector is $(1,1)$, establishing perfect impartiality.

The mean-policy argument transfers the lower bound to randomized policies. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ \text{For }\tau=2,\ \nu_1=\nu_2=\tfrac12,\ \kappa=1, \text{ the exact optimal ratio is }\frac2{\sqrt3}. } $$

Maximum-value water filling requires $O(m\tau\log(\tau+1))$ rational arithmetic operations. The denominator argument from Section 4, with one update per item, gives polynomial bit time in $m,\tau,L$.

8. Finite-agent normalization and what remains open

There is a scaling error in the notes’ finite-agent transfer. For $N=\tau$ equally weighted types, the correct substitutions from finite-agent supplies $s_t$ and allocations $x_{rt}$ are
$$ q_t=\frac{s_t}{\tau}, \qquad a_{rt}=x_{rt}. $$
Indeed,
$$ \sum_r\nu_ra_{rt} = \frac1\tau\sum_rx_{rt} \le \frac{s_t}{\tau} = q_t. $$
Utilities, welfare, and impartiality ratios are unchanged. The converse mapping is also feasible, so offline optimality is preserved.

Consequently, the finite-agent lower-bound families supplied in the question transfer to this model. Theorem 20 and its binary-value extension appear in [Huang, Li, Shu, and Wei, *Online Nash Welfare Maximization Without Predictions*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.03077). Under a fixed input-length cap, their use still requires an admissible witness.

For arbitrary unequal masses, replacing types by equal-weight clones does not transfer unrestricted finite-agent hard instances: clones must have identical valuation histories. Section 6 instead groups actual types and explicitly respects this restriction.

The proved bounds do not determine the optimal ratio for general intermediate $\kappa$. They leave a gap between the truncated-entropy lower bound and the quadratic-logarithmic upper bound. They also do not settle sharp dependence on a prescribed input-length cap or exact constants for general masses.
