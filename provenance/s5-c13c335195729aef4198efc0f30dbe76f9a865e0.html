The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given weight types \(T\), positive integer weights \(w_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a population scale \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, and a quota fraction \(\rho\in(0,1)\), form the weighted majority game with \(N\mu_t\) players of type \(t\) and quota \(Q=\lceil \rho N\bar w\rceil\), where \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\). What randomized algorithm, operating on this type-mass input, estimates each type’s total Shapley–Shubik power \(\Phi_t\) so that \(\frac12\sum_t|\hat\Phi_t-\Phi_t|<\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and what is its running time in the input bit length, \(|T|\), \(N\), \(1/\varepsilon\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\)?

Result

The support-input problem admits the requested randomized polynomial-time additive approximation. Dependence on the population scale \(N\) can be polynomial in \(\log N\); a factor polynomial in \(N\) is unnecessary.

The key is to sample the pivot’s type without constructing a permutation of all players. We recursively divide the remaining players into random halves, represented only by their type counts.

The proof below includes a finite-bit implementation of the binomial sampling needed for these divisions.

1. Preliminaries

For probability distributions \(p,q\) on a finite set, define their total variation distance by

\(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_x|p_x-q_x|.\)

We use two elementary properties:

  • Applying a deterministic function cannot increase total variation distance.
  • If two adaptive processes have conditional transition distributions within distance \(\eta\) whenever their histories agree, they can be coupled so that the probability of disagreement during \(s\) transitions is at most \(s\eta\).

For the second property, use a maximal coupling at each transition until the first disagreement, and apply the union bound.

Compute the integer multiplicities and total weight:

\(n_t=N\mu_t,\qquad W=\sum_{t\in T}n_tw_t,\qquad Q=\lceil\rho W\rceil.\)

The input hypotheses imply \(\sum_tn_t=N\) and \(1\le Q\le W\). All these quantities have bit lengths polynomial in \(L\).

2. A finite-bit binomial sampling lemma

Lemma. Given a binary-encoded nonnegative integer \(n\) and a rational \(\eta\in(0,1)\), there is a randomized algorithm whose output distribution is within total variation distance \(\eta\) of \(\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/2)\). Its worst-case running time is polynomial in \(\log(n+2)\), \(1/\eta\), and the encoding length of \(\eta\).

2.1. Restricting to a central interval

The case \(n=0\) is immediate. Assume \(n\ge1\), and write

\(p_k=2^{-n}\binom nk,\qquad c=\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\qquad R=\lceil\sqrt{n/\eta}\rceil.\)

Let

\(I=\{k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}:|k-n/2|\le R\},\qquad D=|I|.\)

For \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/2)\), its variance is \(n/4\). Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\(\Pr[X\notin I]\le n/(4R^2)\le\eta/4.\)

Consequently, the conditional distribution \(P_I=\mathcal L(X\mid X\in I)\) is within distance \(\eta/4\) of \(\mathcal L(X)\).

The central probability satisfies

\(p_c\le 2/\sqrt n.\)

Indeed, for \(n=2m\),

\(p_m=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-1/(2j))\le\exp(-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{m}1/j)\le1/\sqrt{m+1}.\)

For \(n=2m+1\), the central probability is the preceding even central probability multiplied by \((2m+1)/(2m+2)\). These formulas also cover \(n=1\), using the empty product.

Consider ideal rejection sampling: propose \(k\) uniformly from \(I\), and accept with probability

\(r_k=p_k/p_c.\)

Since \(c\) is a mode, \(r_k\in[0,1]\). Conditional on acceptance, the output law is exactly \(P_I\).

Its acceptance probability \(a_0\) satisfies

\(a_0=\Pr[X\in I]/(Dp_c).\)

Because \(D\le2R+1\le5\sqrt{n/\eta}\), we obtain

\(a_0\ge\frac{3\sqrt\eta}{40}\ge\frac{\eta}{20}.\)

Thus rejection sampling needs only polynomially many trials in \(1/\eta\), independently of the numerical value of \(n\).

2.2. Computing acceptance probabilities efficiently

We next show that \(r_k\) can be approximated to additive error \(\alpha\) in time polynomial in \(\log(n+2)\) and \(1/\alpha\).

Define \(F(a)=\log(a!)\), with \(F(0)=0\). Then

\(\log r_k=F(c)+F(n-c)-F(k)-F(n-k).\)

We first establish an efficient additive approximation for \(F\).

Let

\(G(x)=(x+\tfrac12)\log x-x.\)

For integers \(a>B\ge2\), comparison with the trapezoidal rule gives

\(F(a)=F(B)+G(a)-G(B)-E_{B,a},\)

where

\(0\le E_{B,a}\le\frac1{12}\sum_{j=B}^{a-1}\frac1{j^2}\le\frac1{12(B-1)}.\)

Here is a direct verification of the error bound. On \([j,j+1]\), let \(\ell_j\) be the linear interpolant of \(\log x\) at the endpoints. Concavity gives \(\log x-\ell_j(x)\ge0\). Comparing second derivatives gives

\(\log x-\ell_j(x)\le (x-j)(j+1-x)/(2j^2).\)

Integrating yields an error at most \(1/(12j^2)\). Summing these trapezoidal identities gives the stated formula for \(F(a)\).

To approximate \(F(a)\) within an arbitrary \(\sigma\in(0,1)\), choose

\(B=\lceil2/\sigma\rceil+2.\)

If \(a\le B\), sum \(\log j\) directly. If \(a>B\), compute \(F(B)+G(a)-G(B)\). The omitted error is less than \(\sigma/24\), and the arithmetic errors can be made smaller than the remaining allowance.

These calculations have polynomial bit complexity. Explicitly, after writing a positive integer as \(2^b y\), with \(1\le y<2\), evaluate logarithms using

\(\log y=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1},\qquad z=(y-1)/(y+1).\)

Here \(0\le z<1/3\), so the tail decreases geometrically. The same series evaluates \(\log2\). Precision sufficient for the products in \(G(a)\) requires only polynomially many bits in \(\log(a+2)\) and \(\log(1/\sigma)\). There are only polynomially many summands in \(1/\sigma\).

Approximate each of the four factorial logarithms within \(\alpha/16\). This approximates \(\log r_k\) within \(\alpha/4\). Clamp the approximation to be nonpositive. Since the exponential function is \(1\)-Lipschitz on the nonpositive real axis, the corresponding exponential differs from \(r_k\) by at most \(\alpha/4\).

Exponentiation also has polynomial bit complexity here. If the argument is below \(-h\), where \(h=\lceil\log_2(8/\alpha)\rceil\), replace the exponential by zero. Otherwise, its argument lies in \([-h,0]\), where the Taylor polynomial approximates it efficiently: its remainder after degree \(m\) is at most \(h^{m+1}/(m+1)!\). Taking \(m\) polynomial in \(h+\log(1/\alpha)\), and then rounding to a dyadic rational, gives an acceptance probability \(\widetilde r_k\in[0,1]\) satisfying

\(|\widetilde r_k-r_k|\le\alpha.\)

A Bernoulli variable with dyadic success probability can be generated exactly using finitely many fair bits.

2.3. Bounded running time and total error

Set

\(H=\lceil\log_2(4/\eta)\rceil,\qquad K=\lceil20H/\eta\rceil,\qquad \alpha=\eta/(8K).\)

Run at most \(K\) rejection trials, returning any fixed value in \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\) if none accepts.

In each trial, approximate the uniform proposal on \(I\) within distance \(\alpha\). This requires only finitely many bits: generate \(J\) uniformly from \(\{0,\ldots,2^b-1\}\), and propose

\(k=\min I+\lfloor DJ/2^b\rfloor,\)

choosing \(b\) so that \(D/2^b\le\alpha\).

The joint proposal-and-acceptance law of each implemented trial differs from its ideal law by at most \(2\alpha\). Coupling the \(K\) trials therefore contributes at most

\(2K\alpha=\eta/4.\)

The probability that all \(K\) ideal trials reject is at most

\((1-a_0)^K\le\exp(-K\eta/20)\le\exp(-H)\le\eta/4.\)

Including the initial conditioning error, the implemented output distribution is within distance

\(\eta/4+\eta/4+\eta/4<\eta\)

of \(\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/2)\).

Every loop has a deterministic polynomial bound. This proves the lemma.

3. Sampling a pivot using type counts

Assign each distinct player an independent infinite sequence of fair bits, and order players lexicographically by their sequences.

Two sequences agree with probability zero. Exchangeability implies that the resulting order is a uniformly random permutation of the distinct players.

We locate its pivot without revealing the entire order. Maintain:

  • remaining type counts \(c_t\);
  • a residual quota \(q\), satisfying \(1\le q\le\sum_t c_tw_t\).

Initially, \(c_t=n_t\) and \(q=Q\).

At a division, let \(X_t\) count the remaining type-\(t\) players whose next bit is zero. Conditional on the previously revealed information,

\(X_t\sim\operatorname{Bin}(c_t,1/2)\)

independently across types. This holds because previous decisions depend only on already revealed bits and weights; all unrevealed bits remain independent and fair.

Let

\(A=\sum_tX_tw_t.\)

All players with next bit zero precede all players with next bit one.

  • If \(A\ge q\), the pivot lies in the zero group. Replace \(c_t\) by \(X_t\), leaving \(q\) unchanged.
  • If \(A<q\), the pivot lies in the one group. Replace \(c_t\) by \(c_t-X_t\), and replace \(q\) by \(q-A\).

In both cases, the invariant \(1\le q\le\sum_t c_tw_t\) remains true. In particular, the selected group is nonempty.

When \(\sum_t c_t=1\), return its sole player’s type. With exact binomial draws and unlimited depth, this is exactly the pivot type of the uniform permutation.

Controlling the depth

After \(d\) divisions, every remaining player has the same length-\(d\) bit prefix. If all players’ length-\(d\) prefixes are distinct, every such group has size at most one, so the procedure has terminated.

Each pair has equal prefixes with probability \(2^{-d}\). Therefore,

\(\Pr[\text{procedure has not terminated after }d\text{ divisions}]\le\binom N2\,2^{-d}.\)

Choose

\(d=\left\lceil\log_2(4N^2/\varepsilon)\right\rceil.\)

Then the probability above is at most \(\varepsilon/8\). Stop after \(d\) divisions, returning an arbitrary type if necessary. With exact binomial draws, this bounded procedure’s output law is within distance \(\varepsilon/8\) of \(\Phi\).

Replacing exact binomial draws

Use the lemma with accuracy

\(\eta=\varepsilon/(8\tau d)\)

for every binomial draw. There are at most \(\tau d\) draws. The adaptive coupling property shows that replacing all exact draws changes the output law by at most

\(\tau d\eta=\varepsilon/8.\)

Thus the implemented pivot sampler has an output distribution \(p\) satisfying

\(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,\Phi)\le\varepsilon/4.\)

It uses only polynomially many bit operations in \(L\), \(\tau\), and \(1/\varepsilon\). Its dependence on \(N\) is through \(\log N\).

4. Estimating the probability vector

Run the implemented pivot sampler independently \(M\) times, and output the empirical type frequencies \(\hat\Phi\). This is always a probability vector.

For probability vectors on \(T\),

\(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat\Phi,p)=\max_{B\subseteq T}\bigl(\hat\Phi(B)-p(B)\bigr).\)

Indeed, the maximizing subset consists of coordinates where \(\hat\Phi_t-p_t\) is positive.

For each fixed subset \(B\), its empirical frequency is an average of independent Bernoulli variables of mean \(p(B)\). Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\(\Pr[\hat\Phi(B)-p(B)\ge r]\le\exp(-2Mr^2).\)

For completeness, the needed bound follows by noting that the logarithm of a centered Bernoulli moment-generating function has value and derivative zero at zero, and second derivative at most \(1/4\). Hence its moment-generating function is at most \(\exp(\lambda^2/8)\); applying Markov’s inequality and optimizing \(\lambda\) gives the displayed bound.

A union bound over the \(2^\tau\) subsets yields

\(\Pr[d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat\Phi,p)\ge r]\le2^\tau\exp(-2Mr^2).\)

Take \(r=\varepsilon/2\) and, for example,

\(M=\left\lceil\frac{2\left(\tau+\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\rceil\right)}{\varepsilon^2}\right\rceil.\)

Then the failure probability is at most \(\delta\). On the complementary event, the triangle inequality gives

\(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat\Phi,\Phi)\le d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat\Phi,p)+d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,\Phi)<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/4<\varepsilon.\)

This proves the requested strict-error guarantee.

5. Running time and interpretation

The number of pivot samples is

\(M=O((\tau+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon^2).\)

Each sample makes at most \(\tau d\) binomial calls, where

\(d=O(\log(2N)+\log(1/\varepsilon)),\qquad 1/\eta=8\tau d/\varepsilon.\)

The binomial lemma and the exact integer arithmetic therefore give an overall worst-case bit running time polynomial in

\(L,\quad\tau,\quad1/\varepsilon,\quad\log(1/\delta).\)

In particular, the population dependence is polynomial in \(\log N\). The construction does not establish an optimal exponent for that dependence.

The explicitly enumerated Monte Carlo algorithm in the [supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02841) scans players to find a pivot. The recursive sampler above removes that population scan by retaining only type counts.

Thus the stated additive approximation is randomized polynomial-time solvable. An NP-hardness result under reductions that transfer this randomized guarantee to NP decision problems would imply \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}\).
