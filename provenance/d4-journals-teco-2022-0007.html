The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) items, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), nondecreasing symmetric submodular valuations \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), and nondecreasing rational piecewise-linear item cost curves \(c_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) with \(c_j(0)=0\) and a supplied \(\alpha\) satisfying \(\alpha c_j(x)/x\ge c_j(y)/y\) for all \(0<x\le y\le1\), can a polynomial-time direct mechanism return masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) of type \(t\) receiving whole bundle \(S\subseteq M\), and per-capita payments, such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), individual rationality and no positive transfers hold, no positive-mass coalition can make every member strictly better off by misreporting, total payments lie between \(\sum_j c_j(q_j)\) and \(\alpha\sum_j c_j(q_j)\), and social cost is at most \(2\alpha^3H_\tau\) times the minimum feasible social cost, where \(q_j=\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) contain \(m\ge1\) shareable items. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\ge1\) positive-mass valuation types, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Type \(t\) has a nonnegative, nondecreasing symmetric submodular valuation, encoded by rational values \(a_t(0),\ldots,a_t(m)\), with \(v_t(S)=a_t(|S|)\). Empty-bundle values need not vanish.
Public item costs \(c_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) are continuous nondecreasing piecewise-linear functions specified by rational breakpoints and values, with \(c_j(0)=0\). A supplied rational \(\alpha\ge1\) satisfies \(\alpha c_j(x)/x\ge c_j(y)/y\) whenever \(0<x\le y\le1\).
An anonymous direct mechanism takes any reported society in this domain and returns a sparse rational list of bundle masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) and per-capita payments \(p_{t,S}\), with \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\). Each member reporting \(t\) receives the bundle-payment lottery with probabilities \(x_{t,S}/\mu_t\); bundles themselves are whole subsets of \(M\).
Utilities are expected quasilinear utilities evaluated using true valuations. Coalitions may split rational positive mass from any types among finitely many rational valuation reports in the same domain; weak group-strategyproofness means no such deviation makes every participating member strictly better off, with outsiders reporting truthfully.
At every truthful profile require \(0\le p_{t,S}\le v_t(S)\) on positive-mass cells. Put \(q_j=\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\), \(C(x)=\sum_jc_j(q_j)\), and require \(C(x)\le\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}p_{t,S}\le\alpha C(x)\).
Social cost is \(\operatorname{SC}(x)=C(x)+\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}[v_t(M)-v_t(S)]\); \(\operatorname{OPT}\) minimizes this over all nonnegative real bundle masses satisfying the type-mass constraints, without payment or incentive constraints. Let \(H_\tau=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}1/k\), and let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Does there exist a direct mechanism running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) that satisfies these incentive, individual-rationality, and budget requirements and guarantees \(\operatorname{SC}(x)\le2\alpha^3H_\tau\operatorname{OPT}\) on every instance, or is this approximation guarantee unattainable by polynomial-time mechanisms with those requirements?
For finite societies of \(n\) agents, an iterative ascending mechanism is polynomial-time, individually rational, has no positive transfers, is weakly group-strategyproof, is \(\alpha\)-budget-balanced, and achieves social-cost ratio \(2\alpha^3H_n\) for symmetric submodular valuations and nondecreasing \(\alpha\)-average-decreasing costs ([source](https://doi.org/10.1145/3505586)). No result settling the continuous formulation above was located in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Symmetric valuations are supplied as rational cardinality-value tables, and L includes these tables, masses, cost descriptions, and alpha.
  • The cost curves have real nonnegative outputs and rational piecewise-linear descriptions: requiring rational outputs at every real argument would exclude ordinary nonconstant piecewise-linear curves. Continuity is the convention adopted for piecewise-linear curves.
  • Alpha is a binary rational at least one, as required by the discrete average-decreasing parameter.
  • The aggregate bundle masses do not specify individual assignments. They are interpreted as anonymous bundle-payment lotteries, with expected quasilinear utility and individual rationality imposed separately on every positive-mass bundle-payment cell.
  • Reports are valuations in the stated domain; coalitions may contain fractions of several true types and divide their mass among finitely many reports. Coalition masses and reported valuation tables are rational, keeping deviated inputs within the specified computational domain.
  • The feasible social-cost benchmark includes all real mass allocations satisfying the type-mass constraints, without budget or incentive restrictions, matching the discrete allocation-only benchmark.
  • No zero normalization is added for empty-bundle valuations, because the discrete valuation domain allows nonnegative empty-bundle values.
  • The output is a sparse list of rational masses and payments, so polynomial running time includes output length.

Impossibility, including mechanisms using lotteries

The requested guarantee is unattainable, even without a running-time restriction. A counterexample exists with $m=1$, $\alpha=1$, and $\tau=5$.

The proof applies to every anonymous mechanism satisfying the stated incentive, payment, and budget requirements. It does not assume deterministic bundles.

1. One-item notation and incentive inequalities

Restrict the domain to one item and normalized valuations
$$ a_t(0)=0,\qquad a_t(1)=b_t, $$
where $b_t$ is a nonnegative rational number. These valuations satisfy all the required valuation hypotheses.

For a type of mass $\mu_t$, write $s_t$ for its probability of receiving the item and $\pi_t$ for its expected per-capita payment. The payment requirements imply
$$ 0\le \pi_t\le b_t s_t. $$
Indeed, an empty bundle must have payment zero, and an item recipient pays at most $b_t$. Thus truthful expected utility is
$$ u_t=b_t s_t-\pi_t\ge0. $$

When $\alpha=1$, budget balance is exact:
$$ \sum_t\mu_t\pi_t=c(q), \qquad q=\sum_t\mu_t s_t. $$

We repeatedly use the following consequence of weak group-strategyproofness. If a rational positive mass of members with the same true value $b$ changes its report, anonymity gives all those members the same expected utility before and after the deviation. Consequently,
$$ b s-\pi\ge b s'-\pi', $$
where $(s,\pi)$ and $(s',\pi')$ are their truthful and deviating outcomes. Otherwise every participating member would strictly improve.

Assume, towards a contradiction, that a mechanism achieves the requested guarantee. In particular, for each support size $k$, it has a finite approximation bound independent of valuation magnitudes. The proof below needs only this weaker property.

2. A forced-service and forced-exclusion lemma

Let $c$ be continuous, satisfy $c(0)=0$, and have nonincreasing average cost:
$$ \frac{c(x)}x\ge\frac{c(y)}y \qquad (0<x\le y\le1). $$

Fix a rational mass $Q>0$ and a rational number $\ell\ge0$. Fix the remaining population, of mass $1-Q$, with values at most $\ell$.

Suppose the following property has already been established:

Whenever the population of mass $Q$ reports values strictly above $\ell$, every member of the fixed population receives the empty bundle with probability one.

Call the population of mass $Q$ active, and put
$$ R=\frac{c(Q)}Q. $$
Assume $\ell<R$.

We prove:

  • If every active value exceeds $R$, all active members receive the item with probability one and have expected per-capita payment $R$.
  • If one active type has value $b\in(\ell,R)$ and all other active values exceed $R$, that type receives the empty bundle with probability one.

2.1 Homogeneous active populations

First let every active member have the same rational value $h>\ell$. Write their common outcome as $(s(h),\pi(h))$.

The fixed population receives nothing and pays zero. Therefore
$$ q=Qs(h), \qquad \pi(h)=\frac{c(Qs(h))}{Q}. $$

Let
$$ D=\sum_{t\text{ fixed}}\mu_t b_t. $$
Serving all active members and none of the fixed population is a feasible allocation, so
$$ \operatorname{OPT}\le c(Q)+D. $$

The support size of these homogeneous profiles is bounded independently of $h$. Their finite approximation bound therefore implies, for some finite constant $A$,
$$ Qh(1-s(h)) \le \operatorname{SC} \le A\bigl(c(Q)+D\bigr). $$
It follows that, along rational $h$ tending to infinity,
$$ s(h)\longrightarrow1. $$
Continuity of $c$ then gives
$$ \pi(h)\longrightarrow R. $$

For any fixed rational $h>\ell$, the entire active population can jointly report another rational value $h'>\ell$. The incentive inequality gives
$$ h s(h)-\pi(h) \ge h s(h')-\pi(h'). $$
Taking $h'$ to infinity yields
$$ h s(h)-\pi(h)\ge h-R. $$

Average-decreasing costs give
$$ c(Qs(h))\ge s(h)c(Q), $$
including $s(h)=0$ because $c(0)=0$. Hence
$$ h s(h)-\pi(h)\le s(h)(h-R). $$

If $h>R$, these inequalities imply
$$ h-R\le s(h)(h-R), $$
so $s(h)=1$ and $\pi(h)=R$.

If $\ell<h<R$, individual rationality instead gives
$$ 0\le h s(h)-\pi(h)\le s(h)(h-R), $$
forcing $s(h)=0$.

Thus the homogeneous active population has a forced threshold at $R$, apart from the unused equality case.

2.2 Heterogeneous active values above the threshold

We now prove the first assertion by induction on the number of distinct active reports.

The homogeneous case was proved above. Suppose there are at least two active types, all with values exceeding $R$, and the assertion holds with fewer distinct active reports.

For any active type $t$, its entire mass can report the value of another active type. This reduces the number of distinct active reports, preserves $Q$, and leaves the fixed population unchanged.

By induction, after this deviation every active member receives the item and has expected payment $R$. Weak group-strategyproofness therefore requires
$$ b_t s_t-\pi_t\ge b_t-R, $$
or
$$ \pi_t\le R-b_t(1-s_t). $$

Because the fixed population is unserved, actual item usage is
$$ q=\sum_{t\text{ active}}\mu_t s_t\le Q. $$
Exact budget balance and the preceding inequalities give
$$ c(q) \le QR-\sum_{t\text{ active}}\mu_t b_t(1-s_t). $$

If any active type has $s_t<1$, then $b_t>R$ implies
$$ \sum_{t\text{ active}}\mu_t b_t(1-s_t) > R\sum_{t\text{ active}}\mu_t(1-s_t) =R(Q-q). $$
Consequently,
$$ c(q)<QR-R(Q-q)=Rq. $$
But average-decreasing costs imply
$$ c(q)\ge \frac qQ c(Q)=Rq, $$
a contradiction. Therefore every active type has $s_t=1$.

Now each $\pi_t\le R$, while exact budget balance requires
$$ \sum_{t\text{ active}}\mu_t\pi_t=c(Q)=QR. $$
All active masses are positive, so every $\pi_t=R$. This proves the first assertion.

2.3 A type below the threshold must be excluded

Consider an active type of value
$$ \ell<b<R, $$
while all other active values exceed $R$. Denote its outcome by $(s,\pi)$.

Replace this type’s value by a rational number $v>R$. At the resulting truthful profile, the first assertion forces full service and expected payment $R$ for that type.

Its members, now having true value $v$, could jointly report $b$, producing the original reported profile. Therefore
$$ v-R\ge vs-\pi, $$
which rearranges to
$$ \pi\ge R-v(1-s). $$
Taking rational $v$ down to $R$ gives
$$ \pi\ge Rs. $$
The sequence can avoid all other reported values, so these deviations can use the entire distinguished type without merging reports.

At the original truthful profile, the payment restriction gives $\pi\le bs$. Hence
$$ Rs\le\pi\le bs. $$
Since $b<R$, necessarily $s=0$. This proves the second assertion and completes the lemma.

3. A family forcing successive exclusions

Fix an integer $\tau\ge2$. Choose a dyadic rational
$$ 0<\delta\le\frac14, \qquad \rho=1-\delta. $$
Define
$$ Q_i=\delta^{\,i-1} \qquad (1\le i\le\tau), $$
and type masses
$$ \mu_i=Q_i-Q_{i+1}=\rho Q_i \qquad (i<\tau), \qquad \mu_\tau=Q_\tau. $$
These positive binary-rational masses sum to one.

Set
$$ b_i=\frac{\rho}{Q_i}, \qquad c(q)=\min\left\{\frac q{Q_\tau},1\right\}. $$

The cost is continuous, nonnegative, nondecreasing, and piecewise linear with rational data. Its average cost is constant up to $Q_\tau$ and decreases thereafter, so $\alpha=1$ is valid.

For every $i$, since $Q_i\ge Q_\tau$,
$$ c(Q_i)=1, \qquad R_i=\frac{c(Q_i)}{Q_i}=\frac1{Q_i}. $$
Put $R_0=0$. The values satisfy
$$ R_{i-1}<b_i<R_i. $$
For $i>1$, the first inequality follows from
$$ R_{i-1}=\frac{\delta}{Q_i} <\frac{\rho}{Q_i}=b_i, $$
because $\rho>\delta$. Also, every later value satisfies
$$ b_k>R_i \qquad (k>i). $$

We establish exclusions successively.

Initially there is no fixed population, so the hypothesis of the lemma holds with $Q=Q_1=1$ and $\ell=R_0=0$. Type $1$ has value in $(R_0,R_1)$, while all later values exceed $R_1$. The lemma forces type $1$ to receive nothing.

More precisely, it proves that type $1$ is unserved whenever the remaining population reports values above $R_1$.

Inductively, suppose types $1,\ldots,i-1$, at their specified masses and values, are all unserved whenever the remaining population reports values above $R_{i-1}$. That remaining population has mass $Q_i$.

Apply the lemma with
$$ Q=Q_i,\qquad \ell=R_{i-1},\qquad R=R_i. $$
Type $i$ has value in $(R_{i-1},R_i)$, while all subsequent active values exceed $R_i$. It must therefore be unserved.

The earlier types remain unserved because all reports in this application exceed $R_{i-1}$. Thus, after fixing type $i$ as well, the induction hypothesis holds for the residual population reporting values above $R_i$.

This argument also applies to the final type, when there are no other active types. Therefore every type is unserved on the constructed profile.

The resulting social cost is
$$ \operatorname{SC}_{\mathrm{mech}} =\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i b_i =(\tau-1)\rho^2+\rho. $$

4. The unrestricted optimum is exactly one

Write
$$ V=(\tau-1)\rho^2+\rho, \qquad B=\frac1{Q_\tau}. $$
Since $\tau\ge2$ and $\rho\ge3/4$,
$$ V\ge \frac9{16}+\frac34=\frac{21}{16}>1. $$

Consider any feasible allocation, including allocations with arbitrary real served masses $z_i\in[0,\mu_i]$. Its usage is $q=\sum_i z_i$, and
$$ \operatorname{SC} =c(q)+V-\sum_i z_i b_i. $$
Every value satisfies $b_i\le\rho B$.

If $q\le Q_\tau$, then
$$ \operatorname{SC} = Bq+V-\sum_i z_i b_i \ge V+(1-\rho)Bq \ge V>1. $$

If $q\ge Q_\tau$, then $c(q)=1$, and disutility is nonnegative, so
$$ \operatorname{SC}\ge1. $$

Serving everyone has cost one and zero disutility. Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{OPT}=1. $$

5. An explicit violation with five supported types

Take
$$ \tau=5,\qquad \delta=\frac1{32},\qquad \rho=\frac{31}{32}. $$
All instance data are rational; in particular, the cost breakpoint is
$$ Q_5=2^{-20}. $$

Every mechanism under consideration is forced to serve nobody. Its approximation ratio is therefore
$$ \frac{\operatorname{SC}_{\mathrm{mech}}}{\operatorname{OPT}} = 4\left(\frac{31}{32}\right)^2+\frac{31}{32} = \frac{1209}{256}. $$
However,
$$ 2H_5 =2\left(1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15\right) =\frac{137}{30}, $$
and
$$ \frac{1209}{256}-\frac{137}{30} =\frac{599}{3840}>0. $$
Because $\alpha=1$, this strictly violates the requested $2\alpha^3H_\tau$ guarantee.

In fact, letting $\delta$ tend to zero through dyadic rationals gives
$$ (\tau-1)(1-\delta)^2+(1-\delta)\longrightarrow\tau. $$
Thus any mechanism satisfying these requirements and finite uniform approximation bounds depending only on support size has worst-case ratio at least $\tau$ on this one-item subclass.

The [finite-society theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.06384) uses the number of agents in its harmonic factor. It is compatible with this impossibility: the five types above have sharply unequal masses, and replacing agent count by supported-type count demands a stronger guarantee.

  • Restriction to one item and incentive inequalities.

The cellwise payment constraints imply that empty-bundle payments vanish and that expected payments satisfy
$$ 0\le\pi_t\le b_ts_t. $$
Thus truthful expected utility is nonnegative. With $\alpha=1$, the two budget inequalities indeed force exact budget balance.

The consequence of weak group-strategyproofness is justified. A coalition consisting of a rational positive mass of one true type has a common truthful expected utility. If all its members make the same report, anonymity also gives them a common deviating expected utility, including when that report merges with an existing type. A strictly larger deviating utility would therefore make every participant strictly better off. This establishes the displayed incentive inequality.

  • Homogeneous active populations.

Under the lemma’s exclusion hypothesis, the fixed population receives nothing and pays zero. Consequently,
$$ q=Qs(h),\qquad \pi(h)=\frac{c(Qs(h))}{Q} $$
follows directly from exact budget balance.

Serving the entire active population is a feasible comparator, giving
$$ \operatorname{OPT}\le c(Q)+D. $$
The homogeneous profiles have a fixed support size: their active value exceeds every fixed-population value, so it cannot merge with a fixed report. The assumed support-dependent approximation guarantee therefore supplies a constant independent of $h$. Since
$$ Qh(1-s(h))\le A(c(Q)+D), $$
it follows that $s(h)\to1$ along rational $h\to\infty$. Continuity of the cost function then gives $\pi(h)\to R$.

Taking this limit in the incentive inequality is legitimate. No continuity of the mechanism is assumed or needed; convergence of the relevant outcomes was established separately.

Average-decreasing costs give
$$ c(Qs(h))\ge s(h)c(Q), $$
with the zero-service case covered by $c(0)=0$. The resulting utility bounds force full service when $h>R$ and zero service when $\ell<h<R$. The equality case $h=R$ is explicitly left unused and is unnecessary later.

Once all types are fully served, each expected payment is at most $R$ and their weighted average is exactly $R$. Positive masses therefore force every expected payment to equal $R$.
