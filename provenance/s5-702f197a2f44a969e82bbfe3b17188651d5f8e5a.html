The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 4 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given hospitals \(H\), possibly overlapping regions \(\mathcal E\) of size at most \(2\), and a finite support \(T\) of resident types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), determine whether there is a feasible allocation with no strong blocking pair. Each type has a strict ranking over at most \(2\) acceptable hospitals, and each hospital strictly ranks at most \(2\) acceptable types. An allocation assigns mass \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) and unmatched mass \(x_{t,\bot}\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\); hospital capacities \(q_h\) and regional caps \(c_E\) are rational per-capita limits. A pair \((t,h)\) blocks if positive mass of \(t\) is unmatched or assigned below \(h\), and either some positive amount of that mass can move to \(h\) while preserving feasibility, or \(h\) ranks \(t\) above a positive mass assigned there.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a set of \(m\) hospitals and \(\mathcal E\) an explicitly listed family of nonempty, possibly overlapping regions with \(1\le |E|\le2\). Capacities \(q_h\) and regional caps \(c_E\) are nonnegative binary rationals measured in units of total resident mass.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Acceptability is mutual. Each type strictly ranks at most two acceptable hospitals, each hospital strictly ranks at most two acceptable types, and every acceptable hospital is preferred to being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\).
An allocation consists of nonnegative real masses \(x_{t,h}\) on acceptable pairs and \(x_{t,\bot}\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\). It is feasible when \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h\) for every hospital and \(\sum_{h\in E}\sum_t x_{t,h}\le c_E\) for every region.
An acceptable pair \((t,h)\) strongly blocks if \(x_{t,s}>0\) for some \(s=\bot\) or acceptable hospital \(s\) ranked below \(h\), and either: (i) moving some \(0<\varepsilon\le x_{t,s}\) from \(s\) to \(h\), changing no other masses, preserves feasibility; or (ii) \(x_{u,h}>0\) for some type \(u\) ranked below \(t\) by \(h\). Condition (ii) does not require the move in (i) to be feasible.
An allocation is strongly stable if it is feasible and has no strong blocking pair.

Is deciding whether a strongly stable allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|\mathcal E|,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For indivisible residents, existence of a strongly stable matching is NP-complete when both preference-list lengths and region sizes are at most \(2\), allowing overlapping regions.
With disjoint regions and the same bounds, the discrete problem is solvable in \(O(|H|(|H|+|R|))\) time. Neither result settles the divisible-mass problem defined above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made acceptability mutual and restricted allocations and blocking pairs to acceptable pairs, as in the discrete definition.
  • Specified nonnegative rational hospital capacities and regional caps, allowing zero, and excluded empty regions, as in the discrete definition.
  • Specified real, divisible allocation masses and placed unmatched status below every acceptable hospital.
  • Made the feasible move a transfer from one positive-mass source assignment to the destination hospital, preserving hospital capacities and regional caps and changing no other masses.
  • Clarified that the hospital-priority branch of strong blocking is independent of feasibility of the transfer, as in the discrete definition.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Proven result. Deciding whether a strongly stable allocation exists is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even with dyadic masses and caps. The key is a clause gadget whose internal cap is half a type’s mass.

1. When an infinitesimal move is feasible

Write \(\ell_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\) for the load of hospital \(h\). Consider an acceptable pair \((t,h)\) and a less preferred destination \(s\) with \(x_{t,s}>0\).

Moving mass from \(s\) to \(h\) increases precisely these constrained quantities:

  • The load of \(h\).
  • The load of each region \(E\) containing \(h\) but not \(s\), interpreting \(\bot\) as belonging to no region.

Every other constrained load stays constant or decreases. Consequently, some positive move is feasible if and only if all the increasing constraints have strictly positive slack. Indeed, if they all have positive slack, choose \(\varepsilon>0\) no larger than \(x_{t,s}\) and their minimum slack.

Thus such a move is prevented exactly when \(h\) is full or some region containing \(h\) but not \(s\) is tight. In particular, a tight region \(\{h,s\}\) does not prevent a transfer from \(s\) to \(h\).

2. Reduction and input bounds

We reduce from positive exact-one satisfiability: given nonempty sets \(C_1,\ldots,C_k\) of at most three variables, find a Boolean assignment making exactly one variable in each set true. This is the NP-complete set formulation of one-in-three satisfiability. [Schaefer, p. 217](https://doi.org/10.1145/800133.804350)

Delete unused variables. We may restrict to instances with at least one clause. Let the remaining variables be \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\).

Set \(N=n+2k\), choose the smallest power of two \(K\) satisfying \(K\ge N\), and put \(w=1/K\).

For each variable \(v_i\), create a type \(r_i\) of mass \(w\) and a hospital \(X_i\), mutually acceptable only to each other.

For each clause \(C_j\), create two types \(a_j,b_j\), each of mass \(w\), and two hospitals \(A_j,B_j\), with preferences:

| Agent | Preference order |
|---|---|
| \(a_j\) | \(A_j\succ B_j\succ\bot\) |
| \(b_j\) | \(B_j\succ A_j\succ\bot\) |
| \(A_j\) | \(b_j\succ a_j\) |
| \(B_j\) | \(a_j\succ b_j\) |

All hospitals have capacity \(w\). This crossed-preference gadget also appears in the discrete reduction; the smaller internal cap below supplies the argument needed for divisible mass. [Hamada–Miyazaki, Theorem 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03123)

For each clause \(C_j\), introduce:

  • The region \(\{A_j,B_j\}\), with cap \(w/2\).
  • For every \(v_i\in C_j\), the regions \(\{A_j,X_i\}\) and \(\{B_j,X_i\}\), each with cap \(w\).
  • For every two distinct variables \(v_i,v_\ell\in C_j\), the region \(\{X_i,X_\ell\}\), with cap \(w\).

Repeated variable-pair regions are listed only once; their caps agree.

The specified types have total mass \(Nw\). If \(Nw<1\), add one type of mass \(1-Nw\) with no acceptable hospitals. Its mass must remain unmatched.

This is a valid instance: all masses are positive dyadic rationals, their sum is one, all preference lists have length at most two, and every region has size two. There are \(n+2k\) hospitals, at most \(n+2k+1\) types, and at most \(10k\) regions. Every numerical quantity has bit length \(O(\log N)\), so the construction is polynomial.

3. A satisfying assignment gives a stable allocation

Suppose exactly one variable in every clause is true. Assign all mass \(w\) of \(r_i\) to \(X_i\) when \(v_i\) is true; otherwise leave it unmatched. Leave all clause types unmatched.

This allocation is feasible. Clause hospitals are empty, each external region has load at most \(w\), and a variable-pair region contains at most one hospital of load \(w\).

We check every possible blocking pair.

A true variable type has all its mass at its only acceptable hospital, so it has no less preferred positive mass.

For a false variable \(v_i\), choose a clause containing it. That clause has a distinct true variable \(v_\ell\). The region \(\{X_i,X_\ell\}\) is tight, preventing any move from unmatched mass to \(X_i\). Hospital \(X_i\) is empty, so condition (ii) cannot hold.

For either clause type and either acceptable clause hospital, the region connecting that hospital to the clause’s true variable hospital is tight. This prevents a move from unmatched mass. Both clause hospitals are empty, so condition (ii) again cannot hold.

The added type, if present, has no acceptable pairs. Therefore the allocation is strongly stable.

4. Every stable allocation yields a satisfying assignment

Let \(x\) be strongly stable, and define \(z_i=\ell_{X_i}=x_{r_i,X_i}\). Hospital capacities give \(0\le z_i\le w\).

Fix a clause \(C_j\). Its internal cap gives \(\ell_{A_j}+\ell_{B_j}\le w/2\). Hence each clause type has at least \(w/2\) unmatched mass.

Because \(a_j\) has unmatched mass and \(B_j\) ranks it above \(b_j\), condition (ii) would make \((a_j,B_j)\) block whenever \(x_{b_j,B_j}>0\). Therefore \(x_{b_j,B_j}=0\). Symmetrically, \(x_{a_j,A_j}=0\).

Consequently, the only possible positive clause allocations are

\(p=x_{a_j,B_j}=\ell_{B_j}\), \(r=x_{b_j,A_j}=\ell_{A_j}\),

with \(p+r\le w/2\).

We prove that some variable in \(C_j\) satisfies \(z_i>w/2\). Suppose, to the contrary, that every variable in this clause satisfies \(z_i\le w/2\).

  • If \(p>0\): Then \(r<w/2\). Type \(a_j\) can transfer positive mass from \(B_j\) to its preferred hospital \(A_j\). The internal regional load stays constant. Hospital \(A_j\) has slack, and every external region containing it has load \(r+z_i<w\). All other constrained loads stay constant or decrease. Section 1 therefore gives a feasible positive transfer, contradicting stability.
  • If \(p=0\) and \(r>0\): Type \(b_j\) can transfer positive mass from \(A_j\) to \(B_j\). Hospital \(B_j\) is empty, every external region containing it has load \(z_i\le w/2<w\), and the internal regional load stays constant. This again gives a blocking move.
  • If \(p=r=0\): Move positive unmatched mass of \(a_j\) to \(A_j\). Its hospital capacity, internal regional cap, and every external regional cap all have strictly positive slack. This also blocks.

These cases exhaust all possibilities, proving that each clause contains a variable with \(z_i>w/2\).

For two distinct variables in the same clause, feasibility of their variable-pair region gives \(z_i+z_\ell\le w\). They therefore cannot both have load strictly greater than \(w/2\).

Declare \(v_i\) true exactly when \(z_i>w/2\). Every clause contains at least one true variable by the preceding argument, and at most one by its variable-pair constraints. This is a satisfying exact-one assignment.

The reduction proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness without requiring allocations to be integral.

5. Membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

Real-valued allocations require a certificate argument.

For every acceptable pair \((t,h)\) and every less preferred destination \(s\), stability is equivalent to the following disjunction:

  • \(x_{t,s}=0\); or
  • every allocation \(x_{u,h}\) to a type ranked below \(t\) is zero, and at least one constraint that increases under the move from \(s\) to \(h\) is tight.

Section 1 proves the assertion about tight constraints; the zero allocations exclude condition (ii).

A certificate chooses one branch for each such triple. For the second branch, it also names one increasing constraint to be made tight. There are at most \(3\tau\) triples, and each named constraint is selected from the explicitly listed hospital and regional constraints. Thus the certificate has polynomial length.

For a fixed certificate, impose its zero-allocation and tightness equalities together with the original feasibility constraints. This is a rational linear system of polynomial encoded size. Feasibility of rational linear systems is decidable in polynomial time.

Every stable allocation supplies a feasible certificate. Conversely, every allocation satisfying a certificate is stable: each positive less preferred source has both blocking conditions excluded. This proves membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\).

Combining both directions, the decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete.
