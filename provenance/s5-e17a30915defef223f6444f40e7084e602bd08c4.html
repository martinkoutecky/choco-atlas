The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given item categories \(V\), a finite support \(T\) of out-star preference graphs \(G_t=(V_t,A_t)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\ge 0\), and an integer threshold \(D\), does there exist \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) for integral bundles \(S\subseteq V_t\) such that \(\sum_{S\subseteq V_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni v}x_{t,S}\le q_v\) for each \(v\), and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(\delta_t(S)>D\)? The continuous min-max value is the least feasible \(D\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(V\) be a set of \(m\ge 1\) item categories. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a nonempty approved set \(V_t\subseteq V\) and an explicitly given out-star \(G_t=(V_t,A_t)\): a root \(r_t\) and exactly the arcs \((r_t,v)\) for \(v\in V_t\setminus\{r_t\}\). Singleton stars are allowed. Arcs express strict preference; leaves are incomparable.
For an integral bundle \(S\subseteq V_t\), define \(\delta_t(S)=|\{v\in V_t\setminus S:\text{ no }s\in S\text{ reaches }v\text{ by a directed path in }G_t\}|\). Empty bundles are allowed.
The input also gives binary rational supplies \(q_v\ge 0\) per unit of total population and a binary encoded integer \(D\). Each individual receives at most one item of each category, and unused supply is allowed.
Feasibility means that real masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), indexed by \(t\in T\) and \(S\subseteq V_t\), satisfy \(\sum_{S\subseteq V_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq V_t:\,v\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_v\), and \(x_{t,S}=0\) whenever \(\delta_t(S)>D\). Thus the bound applies to every bundle receiving positive mass.
Is this continuous out-star min-max dissatisfaction feasibility problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), including the encoding of \(D\).
For a finite list of agents and one copy of each item, the corresponding decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even when every preference graph is an out-star ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.04465)).
No result settling exactly the mass feasibility problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined the omitted dissatisfaction function by counting approved items neither received nor dominated by a received item.
  • Required positive masses in the listed support; zero-mass entries are omitted.
  • Made out-stars nonempty, allowed singleton stars, and specified their root-to-leaf arcs and strict preferences.
  • Interpreted bundles as containing at most one item per category, allowed empty bundles and unused supply, and measured supplies per unit of total population.
  • Kept the stated integer threshold domain and specified binary encoding; defined L as total input bit length.

Result

The continuous out-star feasibility problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). It admits an exact linear-programming formulation with at most \(m\tau\) variables and \(O(m\tau+m)\) constraints, solvable in \((m\tau+m+L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

The key is that, within each type, a suitable collection of leaf marginals can always be realized by a mixture of integral bundles satisfying the dissatisfaction bound.

1. Allowed bundles and their normalization

Write \(W_t=V_t\setminus\{r_t\}\), \(n_t=|V_t|\), and \(\ell_t=|W_t|=n_t-1\).

For every bundle \(S\subseteq V_t\), the out-star structure gives

\(\displaystyle \delta_t(S)= \begin{cases} 0,&r_t\in S,\\ n_t-|S|,&r_t\notin S. \end{cases}\)

Indeed, an allocated root reaches every unallocated leaf. Without the root, allocated leaves reach no other vertices, so every unallocated vertex contributes to dissatisfaction.

If \(D<0\), feasibility is impossible because dissatisfaction is nonnegative and every type has positive mass. Henceforth assume \(D\geq 0\), and define the integer

\(\displaystyle k_t=\max\{0,n_t-D\}.\)

Every feasible allocation can be replaced, without increasing consumption of any category, by one using only:

  • the root bundle \(\{r_t\}\);
  • bundles consisting of exactly \(k_t\) leaves.

To prove this, replace any root-containing bundle by \(\{r_t\}\). A root-free allowed bundle satisfies \(|S|\geq n_t-D\), hence \(|S|\geq k_t\); replace it by any \(k_t\)-element subset. When \(k_t=0\), this replacement is the empty bundle, which is allowed because \(D\geq n_t\).

If \(k_t>\ell_t\), there is no allowed root-free bundle. This occurs precisely when \(D=0\).

2. Exact realization of leaf marginals

Lemma. For integers \(\ell\geq 0\) and \(0\leq k\leq\ell\), every vector \(p\in[0,1]^\ell\) satisfying \(\sum_i p_i=k\) is a convex combination of incidence vectors of \(k\)-element subsets.

Proof. Induct on the number of coordinates strictly between \(0\) and \(1\).

If there are none, \(p\) itself is the required incidence vector. There cannot be exactly one fractional coordinate, since the coordinate sum is an integer.

Otherwise choose two fractional coordinates \(p_i,p_j\). Put

\(\displaystyle h_+=\min\{1-p_i,p_j\},\qquad h_-=\min\{p_i,1-p_j\}.\)

Both numbers are positive. Define

\(\displaystyle p^+=p+h_+(e_i-e_j),\qquad p^-=p-h_-(e_i-e_j).\)

Both vectors remain in \([0,1]^\ell\), retain coordinate sum \(k\), and have fewer fractional coordinates: in each vector, at least one of the chosen coordinates reaches \(0\) or \(1\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle p=\frac{h_-}{h_++h_-}p^+ +\frac{h_+}{h_++h_-}p^-.\)

Apply the induction hypothesis to both vectors and combine their convex combinations. This also covers \(\ell=0\), where the unique vector represents the empty subset. \(\square\)

3. A polynomial-size linear program

For each type introduce a variable \(a_t\), representing the mass receiving its root. For each \(v\in W_t\), introduce \(y_{t,v}\), representing the mass receiving that leaf.

Consider the following rational linear system:

\(\displaystyle 0\leq a_t\leq\mu_t\qquad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle 0\leq y_{t,v}\leq\mu_t-a_t \qquad(t\in T,\ v\in W_t),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v\in W_t}y_{t,v} =k_t(\mu_t-a_t)\qquad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t:r_t=v}a_t+ \sum_{t:v\in W_t}y_{t,v}\leq q_v \qquad(v\in V).\)

Proposition. For \(D\geq0\), this system is feasible if and only if the allocation problem is feasible.

Proof, forward direction. Normalize a feasible allocation as in Section 1. Let \(a_t\) be its root-bundle mass, and let \(y_{t,v}\) be its leaf marginals.

The remaining mass is \(\mu_t-a_t\). Each individual in that mass receives exactly \(k_t\) distinct leaves. Consequently, each leaf marginal lies between \(0\) and \(\mu_t-a_t\), and their sum equals \(k_t(\mu_t-a_t)\). The supply inequalities are exactly the category consumption bounds.

Proof, reverse direction. Suppose the system has a solution. Set \(b_t=\mu_t-a_t\).

If \(b_t=0\), the leaf inequalities force every \(y_{t,v}=0\); assign all mass \(\mu_t\) the root bundle.

If \(b_t>0\), summing the leaf upper bounds gives

\(\displaystyle k_tb_t=\sum_{v\in W_t}y_{t,v}\leq\ell_tb_t,\)

so \(k_t\leq\ell_t\). The normalized vector \(p_{t,v}=y_{t,v}/b_t\) satisfies the lemma’s hypotheses. Thus there exist nonnegative weights \(\lambda_{t,B}\), summing to \(1\), over \(k_t\)-element subsets \(B\subseteq W_t\), such that

\(\displaystyle p_{t,v}=\sum_{B:v\in B}\lambda_{t,B}.\)

Assign mass \(a_t\) to \(\{r_t\}\), and mass \(b_t\lambda_{t,B}\) to each \(B\).

These masses sum to \(\mu_t\), and their leaf marginals are exactly \(y_{t,v}\). Every root bundle has dissatisfaction \(0\). For a leaf bundle, if \(k_t>0\), its dissatisfaction is \(n_t-k_t=D\); if \(k_t=0\), it is \(n_t\leq D\). Hence every positive-mass bundle satisfies the threshold. The program’s supply inequalities ensure that supplies are respected. \(\square\)

4. Encoding and running time

Let \(N=\sum_t n_t\leq m\tau\). The program has exactly \(N\) variables and \(2N+\tau+m\) constraints, counting each equality as one constraint.

All coefficients are rational. Structural coefficients belong to \(\{0,1,-1,k_t\}\), and \(0\leq k_t\leq m\). Right-hand sides use the supplied rationals and products \(k_t\mu_t\).

A sparse binary encoding of the program has length

\(\displaystyle B=O\!\left(mL+(m\tau+m)\log(m\tau+m+1)\right).\)

This bound accounts for repetitions of each mass in at most \(m+1\) constraints, all supplies, coefficients, and variable indices.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming states that feasibility of an explicitly encoded finite system of rational linear equalities and weak inequalities is decidable deterministically in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here; no strict feasibility or integrality assumption is needed. Therefore construction and exact feasibility testing take

\(\displaystyle (m\tau+m+L)^{O(1)}\)

bit operations.

The binary encoding of \(D\) is included in \(L\). Computing \(k_t\) uses comparisons and binary arithmetic; there is no iteration up to \(D\) and no running-time dependence polynomial in its numerical magnitude.

5. Why discrete hardness does not transfer

The cited paper establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for the finite-agent out-star problem in [Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.04465). Continuous splitting changes feasibility, as the following example demonstrates.

Take two disjoint leaf triangles, with categories \(a,b,c\) and \(d,e,f\), and three root categories \(r_1,r_2,r_3\). Define six types by their roots and leaf pairs:

| Type | Root | Leaves |
|---|---|---|
| \(1\) | \(r_1\) | \(\{a,b\}\) |
| \(2\) | \(r_2\) | \(\{b,c\}\) |
| \(3\) | \(r_3\) | \(\{c,a\}\) |
| \(4\) | \(r_1\) | \(\{d,e\}\) |
| \(5\) | \(r_2\) | \(\{e,f\}\) |
| \(6\) | \(r_3\) | \(\{f,d\}\) |

Set \(D=1\). An agent receiving no root must receive both its leaves.

With one agent of each type and one copy of each category, at most three agents receive their roots. Within either triangle, at most one agent can receive its leaf pair, because every two pairs intersect. Thus at most five agents can be satisfied: the discrete instance is infeasible.

For the continuous instance, set every type mass and every category supply to \(1/6\). Give half of each type its root and half its leaf pair, assigning mass \(1/12\) to each bundle. Every root is consumed by two masses of \(1/12\), and every leaf likewise. All supply bounds hold, and every bundle has dissatisfaction at most \(1\).

Thus the continuous problem permits precisely the mixtures captured by the linear program, and its polynomial-time classification does not imply a polynomial-time algorithm for the discrete problem.
