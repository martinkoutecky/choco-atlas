The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(A,B,C\), rational masses \(\mu^A,\mu^B,\mu^C\) summing to one on each side, and complete strict cyclic rankings over counterpart types, with all \(C\)-types sharing one ranking of \(A\)-types, does there exist a strongly stable mass matching? A matching is a nonnegative tensor \(x_{abc}\) with the prescribed marginal masses; stability means there is no triple \((a,b,c)\) for which some \(\varepsilon>0\) mass from each type can leave current assignments it weakly prefers, with at least two types strictly preferring the proposed partner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,B,C\) be disjoint nonempty finite type sets. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|+|C|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; masses sum to \(1\) on each side. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types. Set \(m=\max\{|A|,|B|,|C|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each \(a\in A\) has a complete strict ranking of \(B\), each \(b\in B\) of \(C\), and each \(c\in C\) of \(A\); all \(C\)-types share the same ranking. Preferences over assignments depend only on this cyclic successor partner, with indifference when that partner type is unchanged.
A mass matching is a real tensor \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{A\times B\times C}\) satisfying \(\sum_{b,c}x_{abc}=\mu^A_a\), \(\sum_{a,c}x_{abc}=\mu^B_b\), and \(\sum_{a,b}x_{abc}=\mu^C_c\).
A triple \((a,b,c)\) blocks if, for some \(\varepsilon>0\), one can select \(\varepsilon\) of currently assigned mass from each of its three types such that every selected portion weakly prefers its proposed successor partner to its current one, and for at least two types every selected portion strictly prefers the proposed partner. Portions may be split arbitrarily; departure does not require consent from abandoned partners. A matching is strongly stable if no triple blocks.

Is deciding whether such an instance admits a strongly stable mass matching in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies with equally many individual agents on each side, complete strict cyclic rankings and a common ranking on one side guarantee a strongly stable matching, constructible in polynomial time. [Cseh et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10601-022-09335-y)
No result settling this precise mass-matching decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the listed types to positive mass and defined the total support size, the maximum side support size, and the total input bit length.
  • Allowed unequal support sizes across sides; equal total mass replaces the discrete requirement of equally many agents.
  • Specified real-valued matching entries and arbitrary splitting of assigned mass.
  • Interpreted the blocking condition as proposed partners being weakly preferred to current partners, with strict preference throughout the selected mass of at least two types.
  • Specified that a blocking departure does not require consent from abandoned partners or a complete rematching of the remaining society.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Polynomial-time solvability

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). In fact, a strongly stable mass matching, if one exists, is unique. A greedy procedure constructs the only possible candidate; checking that candidate for blocking triples decides existence.

The greedy choices follow the serial rule in [Cseh et al., Lemma 1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10601-022-09335-y). The proof below establishes why these choices are forced for divisible masses, while allowing the resulting candidate to fail stability.

1. Two preliminary facts

Write \(a_1,\ldots,a_{|A|}\) in the common preference order of the \(C\)-types, from best to worst.

During the algorithm, residual masses on each side have the same total \(T\), which may be less than \(1\). We use the same matching and blocking definitions for these residual instances. This is legitimate: multiplying all masses and tensor entries by a positive constant preserves stability, because a blocking selection of size \(\varepsilon\) scales by that constant.

Lemma 1: stability is inherited by subtensors. Suppose \(x\) is strongly stable and \(0\leq y\leq x\) coordinatewise. Give each type its marginal mass in \(y\), delete types with zero marginal mass, and restrict the rankings accordingly. Then \(y\) is strongly stable in this residual instance.

Proof. Any portions selected from \(y\) can also be selected from \(x\), since \(y\leq x\). Their current successor types are unchanged. Restricting rankings preserves comparisons between remaining types. Thus a blocking triple for \(y\), with its required weak and strict improvements, would also block \(x\). This contradicts stability. \(\square\)

The next lemma is the key forcing argument.

Lemma 2: a triple of mutual first choices receives the minimum mass. Consider types \(a,b,c\) such that \(b\) is \(a\)'s first choice, \(c\) is \(b\)'s first choice, and \(a\) is \(c\)'s first choice. Let their positive masses be \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\). Every strongly stable matching satisfies

\(x_{abc}=\min\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}.\)

Proof. Call a type dissatisfied if it has positive assigned mass whose successor differs from its proposed first choice.

If at least two of \(a,b,c\) are dissatisfied, their positive dissatisfied portions strictly prefer the proposed successors. Every portion of the third type weakly prefers its proposed first choice. Selecting a sufficiently small positive amount from each type therefore makes \((a,b,c)\) a blocking triple. The selections are independent, as permitted by the blocking definition.

Consequently, at least two of the three types have all their assigned mass going to their respective first choices. There are three possibilities:

  • If \(a\) receives only \(b\), and \(b\) receives only \(c\), every triple containing \(a\) is \((a,b,c)\). Hence \(x_{abc}=\alpha\).
  • If \(b\) receives only \(c\), and \(c\) receives only \(a\), every triple containing \(b\) is \((a,b,c)\). Hence \(x_{abc}=\beta\).
  • If \(c\) receives only \(a\), and \(a\) receives only \(b\), every triple containing \(c\) is \((a,b,c)\). Hence \(x_{abc}=\gamma\).

These implications use nonnegativity: a zero successor marginal forces every tensor entry contributing to it to be zero.

The marginal constraints also give \(x_{abc}\leq\min\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}\). Since \(x_{abc}\) equals one of these three masses, it must equal their minimum. \(\square\)

2. Constructing the candidate

Initialize residual masses \(\rho^A,\rho^B,\rho^C\) to the input masses, and initialize \(g=0\).

While positive residual mass remains:

  • Choose the highest-ranked remaining \(A\)-type \(a\) in the common \(C\)-ranking.
  • Choose \(a\)'s most preferred remaining \(B\)-type \(b\).
  • Choose \(b\)'s most preferred remaining \(C\)-type \(c\).
  • Set \(\delta=\min\{\rho^A_a,\rho^B_b,\rho^C_c\}\), add \(\delta\) to \(g_{abc}\), and subtract \(\delta\) from each of these three residual masses.
  • Delete every type whose residual mass becomes zero.

The three chosen types are mutual first choices in the residual instance: the first two choices hold by construction, and every remaining \(C\)-type ranks \(a\) first.

The residual total decreases by \(\delta\) on each side, so the three totals remain equal. If this total is positive, each side has a remaining type and the next step is defined. Every step exhausts at least one type. Thus the procedure terminates, and \(g\) has exactly the required marginals.

There are at most \(\tau-2\) steps: each step deletes at least one type, and the final step deletes at least three. In particular, \(g\) has at most \(\tau-2\) positive entries.

3. Every stable matching equals the candidate

Theorem. If a strongly stable mass matching exists, it equals \(g\).

Proof. Let \(x\) be any strongly stable matching.

At the first greedy step, the chosen triple consists of mutual first choices. Lemma 2 forces its entry in \(x\) to equal the greedy amount \(\delta\). Subtract this entry from \(x\). The resulting tensor is nonnegative and has precisely the residual marginals produced by the greedy step.

Every exhausted type has zero residual marginal and therefore occurs in no positive residual entry. Delete these types. By Lemma 1, the residual tensor remains strongly stable.

Repeat this argument. At every step, Lemma 2 forces the greedy entry, and Lemma 1 preserves stability of the remainder. When the procedure terminates, all residual marginals are zero; nonnegativity then makes every residual tensor entry zero.

Hence \(x=g\). \(\square\)

It follows that existence is equivalent to stability of \(g\). This proves uniqueness as well; it does not assert that the greedy candidate is always stable.

4. Checking stability exactly

For a type \(t\), let \(r_t(s)\) be the rank of successor \(s\), with rank \(1\) best. Define the worst successor ranks appearing with positive mass in \(g\):

\(W_A(a)=\max\{r_a(b):g_{abc}>0\text{ for some }c\},\)

\(W_B(b)=\max\{r_b(c):g_{abc}>0\text{ for some }a\},\)

\(W_C(c)=\max\{r_c(a):g_{abc}>0\text{ for some }b\}.\)

Each maximum is defined because every input type has positive mass.

For a proposed successor \(s\), a type has positive assigned mass weakly preferring \(s\) exactly when \(r_t(s)\leq W_t\). It has positive assigned mass strictly preferring \(s\) exactly when \(r_t(s)<W_t\). Indeed, the worst-ranked successor appears with positive mass, and no assigned successor has a larger rank.

Therefore \((a,b,c)\) blocks exactly when all three comparisons

\(r_a(b)\leq W_A(a),\qquad r_b(c)\leq W_B(b),\qquad r_c(a)\leq W_C(c)\)

hold, and at least two are strict.

For sufficiency, select positive eligible mass for each required comparison and choose \(\varepsilon\) no larger than the three eligible amounts. For necessity, the portions in any blocking selection witness these comparisons, including at least two strict ones.

The decision algorithm checks this condition for every triple. If some triple satisfies it, the candidate is unstable and the theorem excludes every other matching. Otherwise, the candidate is strongly stable.

5. Running time and binary masses

Inverse rank tables require \(O(m\tau)\) entries. There are \(O(\tau)\) greedy steps, each requiring at most \(O(m)\) work when the preference lists are scanned directly. Constructing the candidate therefore uses \(O(m\tau)\) operations.

The worst ranks are obtained from its \(O(\tau)\) positive entries. Checking all triples takes \(O(|A||B||C|)\), hence \(O(m^3)\), rank comparisons.

Exact mass arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity. Let \(D\) be the product of the input denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Express every input mass as an integer multiple of \(1/D\). All residual masses and greedy allocations then use this denominator, and their integer numerators lie between \(0\) and \(D\).

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for the entire algorithm is

\(O\bigl((m\tau+m^3+\tau)L^2\bigr)\)

bit operations. This includes denominator conversion, greedy construction, and stability verification. The algorithm operates on the listed types and binary masses directly.

6. Existence is not guaranteed

A small example shows why verification is necessary.

Take two types on each side, with masses

\((\mu^A_{a_1},\mu^A_{a_2})=(3/4,1/4),\)

\((\mu^B_{b_1},\mu^B_{b_2})=(1/2,1/2),\)

\((\mu^C_{c_1},\mu^C_{c_2})=(1/4,3/4).\)

Let every \(C\)-type rank \(a_1\) above \(a_2\), let \(a_1\) rank \(b_1\) above \(b_2\), and let \(b_1\) rank \(c_1\) above \(c_2\). Complete the other rankings arbitrarily and strictly.

In any mass matching, at least \(1/4\) of \(a_1\)'s mass receives \(b_2\), because \(b_1\)'s total mass is only \(1/2\). Likewise, at least \(1/4\) of \(b_1\)'s mass receives \(c_2\), because \(c_1\)'s total mass is only \(1/4\).

These portions strictly prefer \(b_1\) and \(c_1\), respectively. All of \(c_1\)'s mass weakly prefers its first choice \(a_1\). Thus \((a_1,b_1,c_1)\) blocks, with \(\varepsilon=1/4\), in every matching.

So some instances have no strongly stable matching, but the forced-candidate algorithm decides which instances do in polynomial time.
