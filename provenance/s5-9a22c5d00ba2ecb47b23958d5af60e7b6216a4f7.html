The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given institutes \(I\), voter or applicant types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), type preferences over institutes, institute rankings over types, and class families \(\mathcal C_i\) with rational upper quotas \(u_{i,K}\), where the class-inclusion posets belong to a fixed set \(P\), decide whether there exists a mass allocation \(x_{i,t}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_i x_{i,t}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in K}x_{i,t}\le u_{i,K}\), and no blocking type-institute pair. Construct such an allocation when one exists, especially when \(P\) contains a non-downward forest.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) applicant types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) institutes; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Preferences are strict, possibly incomplete, and mutually acceptable pairs form \(\Gamma\). Each type prefers every acceptable institute to being unmatched; each institute strictly ranks its acceptable types. Individuals within a type are interchangeable.
Institute \(i\) has a positive binary-rational capacity \(Q_i\) and an explicitly listed family \(\mathcal C_i\) of nonempty subsets of its acceptable types, with positive binary-rational upper quotas \(u_{i,K}\) and no lower quotas. Write \(c=\sum_i|\mathcal C_i|\).
Fix a finite set \(P\) of realizable poset isomorphism types containing a non-downward forest. The inclusion poset of the distinct nonempty intersections \(K\cap K'\), for \(K,K'\in\mathcal C_i\), including self-intersections, must belong to \(P\); every member of \(P\) must occur. A downward forest means that the strict supersets of each element form a chain. Capacity constraints are separate from these posets.
A feasible allocation has real entries \(x_{i,t}\ge0\), zero outside \(\Gamma\), satisfying \(\sum_i x_{i,t}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{i,t}\le Q_i\), and \(\sum_{t\in K}x_{i,t}\le u_{i,K}\).
For an acceptable pair define the mass willing to enter \(i\) by \(d_{i,t}=\mu_t-\sum_jx_{j,t}+\sum_{j:\,i\succ_t j}x_{j,t}\). A blocking deviation at \(i\) is a vector \(y\ne x_i\), supported on its acceptable types, satisfying its capacity and class quotas, \(0\le y_t\le x_{i,t}+d_{i,t}\), and \(\sum_{t\in H}y_t\ge\sum_{t\in H}x_{i,t}\) for every prefix \(H\) of its ranking, including the whole list. Thus incoming mass is willing, and the institute's ranked allocation improves without decreasing total mass. A type–institute pair blocks when such a deviation has \(y_t>x_{i,t}\); stability excludes all such pairs.

1. Universal existence

Put $E=\Gamma$ and $n=|E|$. There is one variable $x_{i,t}$ for each $(i,t)\in E$. Write feasibility, apart from nonnegativity, as
$$ Ax\le b. $$
The rows consist of the $\tau$ type constraints, $m$ capacity constraints, and $c$ class constraints. Every coefficient is either $0$ or $1$, every bound is positive, and every column occurs in both a type row and a capacity row.

Order the incident columns of each type row by that type’s institute preferences. Order the incident columns of each institute constraint by the institute’s ranking of types.

We use the following packing form of Scarf’s lemma: if $A$ is nonnegative, its columns are nonzero, $b$ is positive, and $\{z\ge0:Az\le b\}$ is bounded, then this polytope has an extreme point $x$ such that every column $e$ has an incident row $r$ satisfying
$$ A_rx=b_r, \qquad A_{r,f}x_f>0\ \Longrightarrow\ f\succeq_r e. $$
This is the domination condition and existence statement in [Nguyen, Nguyen, and Teytelboym, Definition 9 and Lemma 1](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~nguye161/complexconstraint.pdf).

The hypotheses hold. The origin is feasible, and the type constraints imply
$$ \sum_{(i,t)\in E}x_{i,t} \le \sum_t\mu_t=1. $$
Hence the feasible region is bounded. Zero rows, if any, can be discarded. If $E$ is empty, the zero allocation is stable directly.

For nonempty $E$, Scarf’s lemma provides a feasible allocation with the following certificate for every acceptable pair $(i,t)$:

  • Type certificate: type $t$ is fully allocated, and every institute receiving positive mass of $t$ is weakly preferred to $i$.
  • Institute certificate: a capacity or class constraint containing $t$ is tight, and every type receiving positive mass in that constraint ranks weakly above $t$ at $i$.

Certificates exclude the specified deviations

Suppose institute $i$ has a permitted deviation $y\ne x_i$. Write its acceptable types as
$$ t_1\succ_i t_2\succ_i\cdots\succ_i t_a. $$
Let $k$ be the first changed coordinate. The preceding coordinates agree, so the prefix inequality through $t_k$ gives
$$ y_{t_k}-x_{i,t_k}\ge0. $$
Since this coordinate changes, it strictly increases. The incoming-mass bound therefore implies
$$ d_{i,t_k}>0. $$

A type certificate for $(i,t_k)$ contradicts this: full allocation eliminates unmatched mass, and the certificate eliminates mass at institutes worse than $i$. Thus $d_{i,t_k}=0$.

Otherwise, let $S$ be the set in its institute certificate, with upper bound $q$. Then
$$ t_k\in S, \qquad \sum_{t\in S}x_{i,t}=q. $$
Within $S$, coordinates above $t_k$ are unchanged; $t_k$ strictly increases; and every coordinate below $t_k$ initially equals zero. Nonnegativity consequently gives
$$ \sum_{t\in S}y_t>\sum_{t\in S}x_{i,t}=q, $$
contradicting the quota.

Every nontrivial permitted deviation increases its first changed coordinate, so excluding these deviations excludes all blocking pairs. This proves universal existence, independently of the restriction on $P$.

Existence decision

On the promised domain, the decision algorithm always answers yes.

If validity must also be checked, this remains polynomial time. Let
$$ s_P=\max\{|V(F)|:F\in P\}, $$
the maximum number of elements of a poset in the fixed set $P$. Form and deduplicate the nonempty pairwise class intersections at each institute, compute their inclusion relations, and reject if their number exceeds $s_P$. Otherwise, poset isomorphism can be checked by at most $s_P!$ bijections. This is a constant depending only on $P$. Checking that every member of $P$ occurs, and checking the remaining input conditions, also takes polynomial time.

2. An exact characterization of stability

The certificates above suffice for existence, but they do not characterize all stable allocations. The following characterization is exact.

Fix a feasible allocation $x$ and an institute $i$ with acceptable types ranked as
$$ t_1\succ_i\cdots\succ_i t_a. $$
For this section, abbreviate $x_{i,t}$ to $x_t$ and $d_{i,t}$ to $d_t$.

Let $\mathcal R_i$ consist of its capacity constraint and class constraints. For $r\in\mathcal R_i$, write $S_r$ for its type set and $q_r$ for its bound. Define the tight constraints by
$$ T_i(x)= \left\{ r\in\mathcal R_i: \sum_{t\in S_r}x_t=q_r \right\}. $$

Theorem: exact weighted-quota certificate

Institute $i$ has no permitted nontrivial deviation if and only if there exist nonnegative weights
$$ \lambda_r\ge0\qquad(r\in T_i(x)) $$
and strictly decreasing positive scores
$$ \gamma_{t_1}>\gamma_{t_2}>\cdots>\gamma_{t_a}>0 $$
such that, on defining
$$ p_t=\sum_{\substack{r\in T_i(x)\\t\in S_r}}\lambda_r, $$
one has
$$ x_t>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \gamma_t\ge p_t, \qquad d_t>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \gamma_t\le p_t. $$

In particular, if $x_t>0$ and $d_t>0$, then $\gamma_t=p_t$. The theorem is vacuous for an institute with no acceptable types.

Step 1: characterize infinitesimal deviations

For $v\in\mathbb R^a$, define its prefix sums
$$ s_k(v)=\sum_{\ell=1}^k v_{t_\ell}. $$
Consider the polyhedral cone $C_i(x)$ defined by
$$ \begin{aligned} s_k(v)&\ge0 &&(1\le k\le a),\\ \sum_{t\in S_r}v_t&\le0 &&(r\in T_i(x)),\\ v_t&\ge0 &&(x_t=0),\\ v_t&\le0 &&(d_t=0). \end{aligned} $$

A permitted deviation $y$ gives a nonzero vector $v=y-x_i$ in this cone.

Conversely, suppose $v\ne0$ belongs to the cone. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$,
$$ y=x_i+\varepsilon v $$
is a permitted deviation. To verify this, impose the following bounds whenever their indicated denominators are positive:

  • for each slack quota whose left-hand side increases,

$$ \varepsilon\le \frac{q_r-\sum_{t\in S_r}x_t} {\sum_{t\in S_r}v_t}; $$

  • for $x_t>0$ and $v_t<0$,

$$ \varepsilon\le\frac{x_t}{-v_t}; $$

  • for $d_t>0$ and $v_t>0$,

$$ \varepsilon\le\frac{d_t}{v_t}. $$

There are finitely many such bounds and each is strictly positive. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ below all of them. Tight quotas and coordinates with $x_t=0$ or $d_t=0$ are already protected by the cone inequalities. Prefix inequalities also follow directly from those inequalities.

Therefore,
$$ i\text{ has no permitted nontrivial deviation} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_i(x)=\{0\}. $$

Step 2: necessity of the certificate

Let $S$ be the prefix-sum matrix, so $(Sv)_k=s_k(v)$, and put
$$ w=S^{\mathsf T}\mathbf1. $$
Explicitly,
$$ w_{t_k}=a-k+1. $$

Write the cone inequalities as $Bv\le0$, with rows consisting of:

  • the indicators of tight quota sets;
  • the coordinate rows $v_t\le0$ where $d_t=0$;
  • the coordinate rows $-v_t\le0$ where $x_t=0$;
  • the prefix rows $-Sv\le0$.

If the institute is stable, the cone is $\{0\}$. Hence the linear program
$$ \max\{w^{\mathsf T}v:Bv\le0,\ v\in\mathbb R^a\} $$
is feasible, attains its optimum, and has optimum zero.

The standard linear-programming duality theorem for free primal variables therefore provides nonnegative dual multipliers satisfying
$$ B^{\mathsf T}\eta=w. $$
Separate these multipliers into $\lambda$ for tight quotas, $\alpha$ for coordinates with $d_t=0$, $\beta$ for coordinates with $x_t=0$, and $\rho$ for prefix inequalities. Extend $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by zero to the other coordinates. The dual equality becomes
$$ w=p+\alpha-\beta-S^{\mathsf T}\rho. $$
Set
$$ \gamma=w+S^{\mathsf T}\rho=p+\alpha-\beta. $$
Its coordinates satisfy
$$ \gamma_{t_k}=a-k+1+\sum_{j=k}^a\rho_j, $$
so
$$ \gamma_{t_k}-\gamma_{t_{k+1}}=1+\rho_k>0, \qquad \gamma_{t_a}=1+\rho_a>0. $$

If $x_t>0$, then $\beta_t=0$, giving $\gamma_t=p_t+\alpha_t\ge p_t$. If $d_t>0$, then $\alpha_t=0$, giving $\gamma_t=p_t-\beta_t\le p_t$. These are precisely the required conditions.

Step 3: sufficiency of the certificate

Suppose the certificate exists and $y$ is a permitted nontrivial deviation. Put $v=y-x_i$.

If $v_t>0$, the incoming-mass bound implies $d_t>0$, so $\gamma_t\le p_t$. If $v_t<0$, nonnegativity of $y_t$ implies $x_t>0$, so $\gamma_t\ge p_t$. In either case,
$$ \gamma_tv_t\le p_tv_t. $$
Consequently,
$$ \gamma^{\mathsf T}v \le p^{\mathsf T}v =\sum_{r\in T_i(x)}\lambda_r\sum_{t\in S_r}v_t \le0. $$

On the other hand, summation by parts gives
$$ \gamma^{\mathsf T}v = \sum_{k=1}^{a-1} (\gamma_{t_k}-\gamma_{t_{k+1}})s_k(v) +\gamma_{t_a}s_a(v). $$
Every coefficient is strictly positive and every prefix sum is nonnegative. At least one prefix sum is positive: if all were zero, successive subtraction would give $v=0$. Thus $\gamma^{\mathsf T}v>0$, a contradiction.

This completes both directions of the theorem.

Polynomial-time stability verification

For a rational feasible candidate $x$, compute its tight quotas and the conditions $x_t=0$ and $d_{i,t}=0$ exactly. Stability at institute $i$ can then be checked by testing feasibility of
$$ v\in C_i(x), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^a s_k(v)=1. $$
Indeed, every nonzero vector in the cone has a strictly positive sum of prefix sums and can be scaled to satisfy this equality.

These linear programs have polynomial size. Thus stability verification takes polynomial time in the instance length and the candidate’s encoding length. Alternatively, the weighted-quota certificate itself can be sought by linear programming, replacing strict score inequalities by
$$ \gamma_{t_k}-\gamma_{t_{k+1}}\ge1, \qquad \gamma_{t_a}\ge1; $$
the necessity proof already supplies this normalization.

3. Construction by enumerating institute cutoffs

Here is a construction bound that uses the fixed-poset restriction.

For each institute constraint $r$, let $S_r$ be its set and $q_r$ its bound. A cutoff choice for this row is either:

  • unmarked, imposing no additional condition; or
  • marked at a type $h\in S_r$, imposing

$$ \sum_{t\in S_r}x_{i,t}=q_r, \qquad x_{i,t}=0 \quad\text{for }t\in S_r\text{ ranked below }h. $$

There are $|S_r|+1\le\tau+1$ choices. An empty capacity row has only the unmarked choice.

For any fixed set of choices, call $(i,t)$ institute-certified if some marked row at $i$ contains $t$ and its cutoff $h$ ranks weakly above $t$. The marked-row equations ensure that this is an institute certificate from Section 1.

For every pair $(i,t)$ that is not institute-certified, impose its type certificate as linear equations:
$$ \sum_jx_{j,t}=\mu_t, \qquad x_{j,t}=0 \quad\text{for every }j\text{ strictly worse than }i\text{ for }t. $$
Together with original feasibility and the marked-row equations, these conditions form a rational linear-programming feasibility problem.

Correctness

Every feasible solution of one of these programs has a certificate for every acceptable pair. Section 1 therefore proves that every such solution is stable.

Some program is feasible. To see this, take a dominating allocation provided by Scarf’s lemma. Mark every tight institute row at its worst-ranked type with positive allocation, and leave every other institute row unmarked. A tight row has a positive occupant because its bound is positive.

Any pair dominated by an institute row is institute-certified under these choices. Every remaining pair must be dominated by its type row, so it satisfies the imposed type equations. The dominating allocation is therefore feasible for this program.

The algorithm enumerates the choices, solves each feasibility program, and returns a feasible solution from the first feasible program.

Running time and the fixed-$m$ consequence

There are $m+c$ institute rows, so the number of programs is at most
$$ (\tau+1)^{m+c}. $$
Each program has polynomial size in $m,\tau,c,L$, including all imposed coordinate equations. Exact rational linear programming therefore gives the bound
$$ (\tau+1)^{m+c}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,c,L). $$

Now use the restriction on $P$. Every class $K$ appears as the self-intersection $K\cap K$, so each institute has at most $s_P$ distinct classes. Hence
$$ c\le s_Pm. $$
If repeated copies of a class appear in the encoding, retain only its smallest quota; this preserves feasibility and the permitted deviations.

Thus the construction bound becomes
$$ (\tau+1)^{(s_P+1)m}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,c,L). $$
For fixed $P$ and fixed $m$, this is polynomial time.

Limitation: $m$ varies in the full task. The exponent above depends on $m$, so this algorithm does not establish polynomial-time construction for the general promised domain.

4. Polynomial construction under a common institute ranking

A further special case permits unrestricted $m$ and arbitrary upper-quota families.

Proposition. Suppose all institute rankings are restrictions of one strict order on the support types. Then a stable allocation can be constructed in polynomial time.

Algorithm. Process types in the common order, from highest to lowest. For each type $t$, visit its acceptable institutes in its preference order. At institute $i$, assign the minimum of:

  • the remaining mass of $t$;
  • the residual capacity of $i$;
  • the residual quota of every class at $i$ containing $t$.

Continue until the type is exhausted or its acceptable list ends.

Proof of correctness. Feasibility follows from the minimum defining each assignment.

Fix an acceptable pair $(i,t)$. If $d_{i,t}=0$ in the final allocation, then the unmatched mass of $t$ and its mass at institutes worse than $i$ are both zero. Therefore the pair has a type certificate.

If $d_{i,t}>0$, positive mass of $t$ remained after the algorithm visited $i$. The minimum defining its assignment at that visit must consequently have exhausted a capacity or class constraint containing $t$. At that moment, the constraint contained only types ranked weakly above $t$: higher types had already been processed, and lower types had not yet been processed.

The constraint remains full. The algorithm never removes mass, and its zero residual quota prevents any subsequent assignment to a type in that constraint. It therefore supplies an institute certificate for $(i,t)$ in the final allocation.

All acceptable pairs have certificates, proving stability.

The algorithm performs at most $|E|$ visits and polynomially many quota checks. For exact arithmetic, let $D$ be the product of the input denominators. Its binary length is $O(L)$. Every quantity calculated by the algorithm remains an integer multiple of $1/D$, because the operations are addition, subtraction, and taking a minimum. Numerator lengths remain polynomial in the input length. The algorithm therefore runs in polynomial bit complexity.

This proposition allows non-downward-forest classifications; the additional hypothesis concerns preferences.

5. Stability does not require Scarf domination

The following example shows why constructing a dominating allocation is a stronger objective than constructing an allocation stable under the task’s definition.

There are four types $a,b,c,d$, each of mass $1/4$, and one institute $I$. Only $a,b,c$ are acceptable, and
$$ a\succ_I b\succ_I c. $$
Each acceptable type prefers $I$ to being unmatched. The capacity is $1/2$, and the two classes
$$ \{a,b\},\qquad \{a,c\} $$
each have upper quota $1/4$.

Their distinct nonempty pairwise intersections are
$$ \{a\},\qquad \{a,b\},\qquad \{a,c\}. $$
The intersection poset has $\{a\}$ below two incomparable strict supersets. Thus it is not a downward forest. Taking $P$ to consist of this poset alone satisfies the occurrence promise.

Consider
$$ x_{I,a}=0, \qquad x_{I,b}=x_{I,c}=\frac14. $$
Type $d$ remains unmatched. This allocation is feasible, with both class quotas and capacity tight.

Direct proof of stability

The willingness quantities are
$$ d_{I,a}=\frac14, \qquad d_{I,b}=d_{I,c}=0. $$
For a permitted deviation, write $h=y_a$. The two class constraints imply
$$ y_b\le\frac14-h, \qquad y_c\le\frac14-h. $$
Therefore
$$ y_a+y_b+y_c\le\frac12-h. $$
The whole-list prefix inequality requires total mass at least $1/2$, forcing $h=0$.

The incoming-mass bounds also give $y_b,y_c\le1/4$. With total mass at least $1/2$, both must equal $1/4$. Hence $y=x_I$, proving stability.

Failure of domination

The pair $(I,a)$ has no type certificate because $a$ is entirely unmatched. It also has no institute certificate: every constraint containing $a$ has positive allocation to a type ranked below $a$. Thus this stable allocation is not a dominating allocation for the matrix in Section 1.

The exact certificate of Section 2 nevertheless applies. Choose weights $2$ on $\{a,b\}$, $1$ on $\{a,c\}$, and $0$ on capacity. Then
$$ (p_a,p_b,p_c)=(3,2,1). $$
Taking $\gamma=(3,2,1)$ satisfies all its conditions.

This example does not prove construction hardness. It establishes that the sufficient domination condition excludes some stable allocations.

6. Encoding length and the remaining question

The existence proof also guarantees a stable allocation with polynomial encoding length. At the dominating vertex, choose $n$ independent active equations, including coordinate equations where necessary:
$$ Mx=r. $$
Here $M$ is an invertible integer matrix with entries in $\{0,1\}$, and $r$ consists of zeroes and input bounds.

Let $D$ be the product of the input-bound denominators. Cramer’s rule gives coordinate denominators dividing $D|\det M|$, while Hadamard’s inequality gives
$$ |\det M|\le n^{n/2}. $$
Since every coordinate lies in $[0,1]$, each has encoding length
$$ O\!\left(L+n\log(n+1)\right). $$
The empty-variable case is immediate. Because $n\le m\tau$, total output length is polynomial.

Accordingly, the existence-decision part is settled, and polynomial-size solutions and polynomial-time verification are available. The cutoff algorithm resolves construction for fixed $m$, and the common-ranking algorithm resolves another nontrivial family with unrestricted $m$.

2. Exact weighted-quota characterization

Infinitesimal deviation cone

Every permitted deviation satisfies the four families of cone inequalities:

  • Prefix sums are nonnegative.
  • Tight quota totals cannot increase.
  • A coordinate initially equal to zero cannot decrease.
  • A coordinate with zero willing mass cannot increase.

Conversely, the stated bounds on $\varepsilon$ protect every slack quota, every positive coordinate that decreases, and every coordinate with positive willingness that increases. Tight constraints and zero-coordinate restrictions are already protected by the cone.

All bounds included in the minimum are strictly positive. If there are none, any sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$ suffices. Thus the equivalence
$$ C_i(x)=\{0\} $$
with absence of permitted nontrivial deviations is correct.

Necessity via duality

The primal has free variables, constraints $Bv\le0$, and objective $w^{\mathsf T}v$. Under $C_i(x)=\{0\}$, it is feasible and attains finite optimum zero.

The applicable linear-programming duality statement is that a feasible primal with finite attained optimum has an optimal dual solution; for this free-variable primal, dual feasibility is
$$ \eta\ge0,\qquad B^{\mathsf T}\eta=w. $$
The prover uses this form correctly.

The multiplier decomposition has the correct signs:
$$ w=p+\alpha-\beta-S^{\mathsf T}\rho. $$
Therefore
$$ \gamma=w+S^{\mathsf T}\rho=p+\alpha-\beta. $$
The computations
$$ \gamma_{t_k}-\gamma_{t_{k+1}}=1+\rho_k>0, \qquad \gamma_{t_a}=1+\rho_a>0 $$
are correct. The restrictions on where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ may be nonzero give precisely the claimed implications involving $x_t>0$ and $d_t>0$.
