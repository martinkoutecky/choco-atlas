Theorem 5.6 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support of household types \(\Theta\), with population masses \(\mu_\theta\), group \(g(\theta)\), priority \(\beta_{g(\theta)}\), and integer demand \(d_\theta\), let each of \(T\) rounds produce an independent cohort-arrival vector \(A_r\) drawn from the same finite rational distribution, with \(0\le A_{r\theta}\le\mu_\theta\). The per-capita inventory is \(b\), corresponding to \(nb\) indivisible kits in a population of \(n\) households. A causal policy assigns arriving mass \(z_{r\theta k}\) to integral bundles \(k\in\{0,\ldots,d_\theta\}\), with \(\sum_k z_{r\theta k}=A_{r\theta}\) and total use at most \(b\). For group totals \(C_i\) allocated and \(D_i\) demanded, determine the optimal worst-case \(\alpha\) under \(\mathbb E[C_i]\ge\alpha\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\) for every group, as a function of \(\mathcal R_\beta=b^{-1}\sum_{r,\theta}\beta_{g(\theta)}d_\theta\mathbb E[A_{r\theta}]\). In particular, does the stationary frontier equal \([1-(1-\rho/T)^T]/\rho\) at load \(\mathcal R_\beta=\rho\), and can a causal policy attain it?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) household types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), normalized by \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a group \(g(\theta)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) and integer demand \(d_\theta\ge1\); group priorities are rational, with \(0<\beta_i\le1\) and \(\max_i\beta_i=1\).
The inventory is a positive rational \(b\). Over \(T\ge1\) rounds, arrival vectors \(A_r\) are independent draws from a known, explicitly listed distribution with \(s\) rational vectors and positive rational probabilities. Each vector satisfies \(0\le A_{r\theta}\le\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta d_\theta A_{r\theta}\le b\); every listed type has positive expected arriving mass. Arrivals represent fresh requests each round.
After observing the entire current vector, a possibly randomized causal policy irrevocably assigns nonnegative real masses \(z_{r\theta k}\) to integral bundles \(k\in\{0,\ldots,d_\theta\}\), with \(\sum_kz_{r\theta k}=A_{r\theta}\) and \(\sum_{r,\theta,k}kz_{r\theta k}\le b\) on every realization. Partial fulfillment is allowed.
Define \(C_i=\sum_{r,\theta:g(\theta)=i,k}kz_{r\theta k}\), \(D_i=\sum_{r,\theta:g(\theta)=i}d_\theta A_{r\theta}\), and \(\alpha(\pi,I)=\max\{a\in[0,1]:\mathbb E[C_i]\ge a\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\text{ for every }i\}\).
Fix \(T\), \(\beta\), and rational \(0<\rho<T\). Let \(\mathcal I(\rho,\beta,T)\) contain all such instances with \(\mathcal R_\beta=b^{-1}\sum_i\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]=\rho\), and define \(F_T(\rho,\beta)=\inf_{I\in\mathcal I(\rho,\beta,T)}\sup_\pi\alpha(\pi,I)\). Policies know their instance. State running times in \(m,\tau,T,s,d_{\max}\) and total input bit length \(L\).

What is the optimal stationary frontier \(F_T(\rho,\beta)\)? Does it equal \(\frac{1-(1-\rho/T)^T}{\rho}\), or differ, and can a causal policy attain the frontier uniformly over the class?

For independent stationary discrete arrivals, with at most one request per round, demand bounded by inventory, and positive probability for every group–demand pair, the optimal worst-case guarantee is \(\frac{1-(1-\rho/T)^T}{\rho}\), attained by Weighted Random Cyclic Blocks. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03436v1).
For divisible supply and sequential independent random demands, LP-based policies achieve competitive ratio \(1/2\) against an LP benchmark under expected individual filling ratios; this uses a different fairness objective and does not settle the cohort frontier above. [Source](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=1OsRSrkFWl).

Result

The frontier is
\[ F_T(\rho,\beta)=\frac{1-(1-\rho/T)^T}{\rho}. \]

A single deterministic causal policy attains this guarantee on every instance in the class. It uses the current arrival vector and remaining inventory, and requires no knowledge of future arrivals or their distribution.

Thus the frontier agrees with the discrete stationary result in the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03436v1). The proof below establishes it directly for the present model, including simultaneous arrivals and real allocation masses.

1. A uniformly feasible policy

Write \(B_r\) for the inventory remaining immediately before round \(r\), with \(B_1=b\). For each arriving type \(\theta\), define
\[ t_{r\theta}=\beta_{g(\theta)}\frac{B_r}{b}. \]
Assign
\[ z_{r\theta,d_\theta}=t_{r\theta}A_{r\theta}, \qquad z_{r\theta,0}=(1-t_{r\theta})A_{r\theta}, \]
and set all other bundle masses to zero.

This assigns full bundles to a fraction of the arriving mass and zero bundles to the rest. Both bundle sizes are integral; the model permits their assigned masses to be real.

Define the round’s weighted demand by
\[ W_r=\sum_{\theta}\beta_{g(\theta)}d_\theta A_{r\theta}. \]
Because \(\beta_i\leq1\), the instance assumptions give
\[ 0\leq W_r\leq\sum_\theta d_\theta A_{r\theta}\leq b. \]

Inductively, suppose \(0\leq B_r\leq b\). Then \(0\leq t_{r\theta}\leq1\), so the bundle masses are nonnegative and sum to \(A_{r\theta}\). The round consumes
\[ \sum_{\theta,k}kz_{r\theta k}=\frac{B_r}{b}W_r\leq B_r. \]
Consequently,
\[ B_{r+1}=B_r\left(1-\frac{W_r}{b}\right)\in[0,B_r]. \]
Starting from \(B_1=b\), this proves feasibility on every realization. The policy is causal because \(B_r\) depends only on previous arrivals.

2. Exact expected cohort allocations

For each group \(i\), let
\[ X_{ri}=\sum_{\theta:g(\theta)=i}d_\theta A_{r\theta}, \qquad \delta_i=\mathbb E[X_{ri}]. \]
Stationarity makes \(\delta_i\) independent of \(r\), and
\[ \mathbb E[D_i]=T\delta_i. \]
The load constraint therefore implies
\[ \frac{\mathbb E[W_r]}{b} =\frac{\sum_i\beta_i\delta_i}{b} =\frac{\rho}{T}. \]
Set \(q=1-\rho/T\), which lies strictly between zero and one.

The variable \(B_r\) is determined by arrivals before round \(r\). Independence across rounds therefore makes \(B_r\) independent of both \(W_r\) and \(X_{ri}\). Taking expectations in the inventory recurrence gives
\[ \mathbb E[B_{r+1}] =\mathbb E[B_r]\left(1-\frac{\mathbb E[W_r]}{b}\right) =q\,\mathbb E[B_r]. \]
Hence, by induction,
\[ \mathbb E[B_r]=bq^{r-1}. \]

The allocation to group \(i\) in round \(r\) is \(\beta_i B_rX_{ri}/b\). Using the same independence,
\[ \mathbb E[C_i] =\sum_{r=1}^{T}\frac{\beta_i}{b}\mathbb E[B_r]\delta_i =\beta_i\delta_i\sum_{r=1}^{T}q^{r-1}. \]
The finite geometric sum yields
\[ \mathbb E[C_i] =\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\, \frac{1-q^T}{T(1-q)} =\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\, \frac{1-(1-\rho/T)^T}{\rho}. \]

The proposed factor is admissible because it also equals
\[ \frac1T\sum_{r=1}^{T}q^{r-1}\in(0,1]. \]
Groups with zero expected demand impose no additional constraint. Thus every instance admits this policy, proving
\[ F_T(\rho,\beta)\geq\frac{1-(1-\rho/T)^T}{\rho}. \]

In particular, when \(T=1\), the guarantee is one.

3. A matching family with positive demand in every group

We construct admissible instances approaching the reverse bound. This construction gives every group positive expected demand, even though the literal definition only requires that property for listed types.

Fix a group \(j\) with \(\beta_j=1\), and put \(p=\rho/T\).

Use one type \(\theta_i\) for each group \(i\), with \(d_{\theta_i}=1\). Choose
\[ b=2^{-m},\qquad \mu_{\theta_1}=1-(m-1)2^{-m},\qquad \mu_{\theta_i}=2^{-m}\quad(i\geq2). \]
These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. Each is at least \(b\), since \(2^m\geq m\).

The arrival distribution contains the zero vector and, for each \(i\), a vector \(a^{(i)}\) whose only nonzero coordinate is
\[ a^{(i)}_{\theta_i}=b. \]
Every nonzero vector therefore demands exactly the entire inventory \(b\), and satisfies all coordinate bounds.

First suppose \(m\geq2\). Define
\[ S=\sum_{i\neq j}\beta_i, \qquad G=\sum_{i\neq j}(1-\beta_i). \]
Choose a positive rational \(\varepsilon\) sufficiently small that
\[ \varepsilon S<p,\qquad p+\varepsilon G<1. \]
Such choices exist because \(S>0\), \(G\geq0\), and \(0<p<1\).

Give vector \(a^{(i)}\) probability
\[ p_i=\varepsilon\quad(i\neq j), \qquad p_j=p-\varepsilon S. \]
The total nonzero-arrival probability is
\[ u_\varepsilon=\sum_i p_i=p+\varepsilon G. \]
Assign the zero vector probability \(1-u_\varepsilon\). Every listed probability is positive, and every type has positive expected arriving mass.

Moreover, the weighted load is exactly the required value:
\[ \mathcal R_\beta =T\sum_i\beta_i p_i =T\left(p-\varepsilon S+\varepsilon S\right) =\rho. \]

Consider any policy, including a randomized causal policy, and let \(a\) be any fairness level it achieves. Summing its group constraints gives
\[ a b\rho =a\sum_i\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i] \leq\sum_i\mathbb E[C_i]. \]

On every realization, total allocation is at most \(b\). It is zero if all rounds have zero arrivals. Therefore, independence gives
\[ \sum_i\mathbb E[C_i] \leq b\,\Pr(\text{at least one nonzero arrival}) =b\left[1-(1-u_\varepsilon)^T\right]. \]
Consequently every policy satisfies
\[ \alpha(\pi,I_\varepsilon) \leq\frac{1-(1-p-\varepsilon G)^T}{\rho}. \]
This bound holds uniformly over policies. Taking their supremum, then letting rational \(\varepsilon\) decrease to zero, proves
\[ F_T(\rho,\beta)\leq\frac{1-(1-p)^T}{\rho}. \]

When \(m=1\), simply give the nonzero vector probability \(p\) and the zero vector probability \(1-p\). Its load is \(\rho\), and the same argument gives the reverse bound directly.

Together with Section 2, this proves the asserted equality. The upper-bound family uses only \(\tau=m\), \(s=m+1\), and \(d_{\max}=1\).

4. Running time

The policy produces at most two nonzero bundle masses per type. It never enumerates all bundle sizes.

Reading the instance and computing its load takes \(O(m+s\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. Executing the policy takes \(O(\tau)\) operations per round, hence \(O(T\tau)\) overall. Evaluating the frontier by repeated multiplication takes \(O(T)\) additional operations.

For an explicit bit-complexity bound, let
\[ K=(T+1)\bigl(L+\log(d_{\max}+1)\bigr). \]
Every single-round weighted-demand fraction has \(O(L+\log(d_{\max}+1))\) bits: a common denominator can be formed from the input denominators and the numerator of \(b\). The normalized remaining inventory \(B_r/b\) is a product of at most \(T\) such fractions, so its bit length, and that of each output mass, is \(O(K)\).

Using reduced rational arithmetic with schoolbook integer algorithms gives the conservative total bound
\[ O\!\left((m+s\tau+T\tau)K^3\right) \]
bit operations. Thus execution is polynomial in the numerical horizon \(T\) and input length, with only logarithmic dependence on \(d_{\max}\).

Conclusion

The frontier is completely resolved. The deterministic policy above achieves it uniformly over the entire class; the matching family proves that no larger uniform guarantee is possible.
