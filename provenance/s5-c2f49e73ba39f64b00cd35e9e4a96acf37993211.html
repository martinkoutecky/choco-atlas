Theorem 5 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(N\) types on each side, each of mass \(1/N\), a bipartite compatibility graph of maximum degree \(3\), strict type rankings satisfying the theorem's single-peaked and single-crossing restrictions, and a stable matching of mass \(1-1/N\), decide whether there is a measurable perfect matching that is popular against every feasible measurable matching and has at most one blocking type pair. A pair \(ab\) is blocking when positive mass on each side is unmatched or prefers the other type to its current assignment; count each such type pair once.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Each side is an atomless Lebesgue society of total mass \(1\), partitioned into \(N\geq2\) labelled types of mass \(1/N\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=N\) positive-mass types, each mass encoded as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(m=2N\) be the total number of types and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Compatibility is a simple bipartite graph \(G=(A,B;E)\) of maximum degree at most \(3\). Each type strictly ranks its neighbors above being unmatched; individuals are indifferent between partners of the same type.
Preferences are promised single-peaked on separate axes for \(A\) and \(B\): at degree \(3\), the worst neighbor never lies between the other two on its axis. They are also promised extendible to complete strict rankings that are single-crossing on each side, with separate orders of the types casting preferences; these extensions only certify the restriction and do not add compatible pairs.
A feasible measurable matching is a measure-preserving measurable bijection, with measurable inverse, between measurable subsets of the two societies, respecting \(E\), modulo null sets. It may split a type's mass among different partner types. Its mass is the matched mass on either side; it is perfect if that mass is \(1\).
An edge \(ab\in E\) blocks a matching when a positive mass of type \(a\) is unmatched or strictly prefers \(b\) to its assigned partner type, and independently a positive mass of type \(b\) is unmatched or strictly prefers \(a\) to its assigned partner type. Count each blocking type pair once; stability means no blocking type pair.
In comparing two matchings on these same individuals, each individual votes for the strictly better assigned partner type, with unmatched worst and indifference giving no vote. A matching is popular if the total voting mass on both sides favoring it is at least the mass favoring its opponent, for every feasible measurable opponent, including partial matchings.
The input additionally supplies a stable matching of mass \(1-1/N\), encoded by its rational masses assigned to compatible type pairs; use any measurable realization of these masses.

Is deciding whether such an instance admits a perfect measurable matching that is popular and has at most one blocking type pair in \(\mathsf{P}\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and \(N\).

The discrete counterpart is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete under the same graph and preference restrictions, with a stable matching of size \(N-1\) and a required perfect popular matching having at most one blocking edge. [Schlotter–Cseh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02189)
Maximum-utility popular mixed matchings are polynomial-time computable under expected voting between random matchings, without a blocking-pair bound; that voting model differs from the measurable individual comparisons above. [Huang–Kavitha](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf)

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard. Moreover, on the promised instances, yes-instances have polynomially verifiable certificates consisting of discrete perfect matchings. Thus the promised problem has the usual \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness classification.

The key fact is an exact equivalence: allowing measurable mass splitting does not change whether a perfect popular matching with at most one blocking type pair exists.

1. Definitions and measurable realizations

Write \(\mu\) for population measure on either side. Each type has measure \(1/N\).

For a type \(v\), let \(\bot\) denote being unmatched, and define
\[ \]
\(s_v(p,q)=1\) if \(v\) strictly prefers \(p\) to \(q\), \(s_v(p,q)=-1\) if it strictly prefers \(q\) to \(p\), and \(s_v(p,q)=0\) otherwise.

For discrete matchings \(Q,M\), define the vote margin favoring \(Q\) by
\[ \]
\(\Delta(Q,M)=\sum_{v\in A\cup B}s_v(Q(v),M(v))\),
where an unmatched vertex has partner \(\bot\). Thus \(M\) is popular exactly when \(\Delta(Q,M)\leq 0\) for every discrete matching \(Q\), including partial matchings.

We use two standard properties of atomless Lebesgue spaces:

  • A measurable set can be partitioned into finitely many measurable subsets of prescribed nonnegative measures summing to its measure.
  • Two measurable sets of equal positive finite measure admit a measure-preserving measurable bijection with measurable inverse, modulo null sets.

The second property follows from the isomorphism theorem for standard atomless finite measure spaces. All sets used below are measurable subsets of the given Lebesgue societies, so its hypotheses hold.

2. Extracting a discrete solution from a measurable one

Lemma. Let \(F\) be a perfect measurable matching. Every discrete perfect matching contained in the positive type-pair support of \(F\):

  • is popular if \(F\) is popular;
  • has all its blocking edges among the blocking type pairs of \(F\).

Proof. Let
\[ \]
\(x_{ab}=\mu(\{u\text{ of type }a:F(u)\text{ has type }b\})\).
Perfection and measure preservation imply
\[ \]
\(\sum_b x_{ab}=1/N\) for every \(a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}=1/N\) for every \(b\).

First, the positive support contains a perfect matching. For any \(S\subseteq A\), let \(\Gamma(S)\) be its neighborhood in that support. Then
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \frac{|S|}{N} =\sum_{a\in S}\sum_{b\in\Gamma(S)}x_{ab} \leq\sum_{b\in\Gamma(S)}\sum_{a\in A}x_{ab} =\frac{|\Gamma(S)|}{N}.\)
Thus Hall’s condition holds, and Hall’s theorem supplies a discrete perfect matching in the support.

Now fix any supported discrete perfect matching \(M\), and put
\[ \]
\(\delta=\min_{ab\in M}x_{ab}>0.\)

For every \(ab\in M\), choose a measurable subset \(U_{ab}\) of measure \(\delta\) from the type-\(a\) individuals whom \(F\) matches to type \(b\). Include its image \(F(U_{ab})\) on the other side.

Because \(M\) is perfect, these selected sets contain exactly measure \(\delta\) of every type. They are closed under \(F\): selected individuals are matched only to selected individuals. Every selected individual of type \(v\) has baseline partner type \(M(v)\).

Suppose \(M\) were unpopular. There would be a discrete matching \(Q\), possibly partial, with \(\Delta(Q,M)>0\). Construct a measurable opponent \(H\) as follows:

  • retain \(F\) on all unselected individuals;
  • for each \(ab\in Q\), bijectively match the selected type-\(a\) set to the selected type-\(b\) set;
  • leave selected individuals whose types are unmatched by \(Q\) unmatched.

Each newly paired set has measure \(\delta\). The measurable realization properties above therefore make \(H\) feasible. Closure of the selected sets under \(F\) ensures that retaining the remaining pairs creates no conflict.

Unselected individuals abstain because their assignments are unchanged. Each selected type contributes \(\delta\,s_v(Q(v),M(v))\). Consequently, the voting mass favoring \(H\), minus that favoring \(F\), is exactly
\[ \]
\(\delta\,\Delta(Q,M)>0.\)
This contradicts popularity of \(F\). Hence \(M\) is popular.

Finally, suppose \(ab\) blocks \(M\). Since \(M\) is perfect, both endpoints strictly prefer each other to their partners in \(M\). In \(F\), a positive mass \(x_{a,M(a)}\) of type \(a\) receives partner type \(M(a)\), and independently a positive mass \(x_{M(b),b}\) of type \(b\) receives partner type \(M(b)\). These masses witness that \(ab\) blocks \(F\).

Therefore
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{bp}(M)\subseteq\operatorname{bp}(F).\)
This proves both assertions. \(\square\)

In particular, a perfect measurable popular matching with at most one blocking type pair yields a discrete perfect popular matching with at most one blocking edge. This argument also applies when the measurable matching’s masses are irrational.

3. Lifting a discrete solution: all partial opponents are covered

Lemma. Let \(M\) be a discrete perfect popular matching. Match every individual of type \(a\) to type \(M(a)\), using any measure-preserving measurable bijection for each edge of \(M\). The resulting matching \(F_M\) is popular against every feasible measurable opponent, including partial opponents. Its blocking type pairs are exactly the blocking edges of \(M\).

Proof. Let \(H\) be an arbitrary measurable opponent, and let \(y_{ab}\) be its type-pair masses. Define
\[ \]
\(z_{ab}=Ny_{ab}.\)
Then \(z\) is a nonnegative fractional bipartite matching:
\[ \]
\(\sum_b z_{ab}\leq 1,\qquad \sum_a z_{ab}\leq 1,\qquad z_{ab}=0\text{ outside }E.\)

We first justify that \(z\) is a convex combination of incidence vectors of discrete partial matchings.

Regard \(z\) as an \(N\)-by-\(N\) matrix \(Z\). Set \(r_a=1-\sum_b z_{ab}\) and \(c_b=1-\sum_a z_{ab}\), and form
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle T=\begin{pmatrix} Z&\operatorname{diag}(r)\\ \operatorname{diag}(c)&Z^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix}. \)
Every row and column of \(T\) sums to \(1\), and all entries are nonnegative.

For completeness, such a matrix decomposes into permutation matrices by repeated application of Hall’s theorem. If a nonzero residual has common row and column sum \(t>0\), its positive support satisfies \(t|S|\leq t|\Gamma(S)|\). Choose a supported permutation and subtract the minimum entry on that permutation. All residual row and column sums remain equal, and at least one positive entry disappears. This process terminates after finitely many steps. The subtracted coefficients sum to \(1\).

Restricting those permutations to the upper-left block gives discrete partial matchings \(Q_i\) satisfying
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle z=\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf 1_{Q_i},\qquad \lambda_i>0,\qquad\sum_i\lambda_i=1.\)
Every edge used by \(Q_i\) lies in the positive support of \(z\), hence in \(E\).

Now define
\[ \]
\(w_{ab}=s_a(b,M(a))+s_b(a,M(b)).\)

The individuals matched by \(H\) on edge \(ab\) contribute \(y_{ab}w_{ab}\) to its vote margin against \(F_M\). Because \(F_M\) is perfect, every individual unmatched by \(H\) contributes \(-1\). The unmatched mass on both sides together is \(2-2\sum_{ab}y_{ab}\). Thus
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Delta_\mu(H,F_M) =\frac1N\left(\sum_{ab\in E}z_{ab}(w_{ab}+2)-2N\right). \)

For any discrete partial matching \(Q\), the corresponding discrete identity is
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Delta(Q,M)=\sum_{ab\in Q}(w_{ab}+2)-2N. \)
Indeed, its unmatched vertices number \(2N-2|Q|\), and each votes against \(Q\).

Substituting the decomposition of \(z\) therefore gives
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Delta_\mu(H,F_M)=\frac1N\sum_i\lambda_i\Delta(Q_i,M)\leq 0. \)
The inequality follows from discrete popularity of \(M\), which includes comparison with partial matchings. Hence \(F_M\) is measurably popular.

Every individual of each type has its discrete partner type under \(F_M\). Thus a type pair blocks \(F_M\) exactly when its edge blocks \(M\). \(\square\)

4. Exact equivalence and hardness

Combining the two lemmas proves the following equivalence, without needing the degree or preference restrictions:

With equal type masses \(1/N\), a perfect measurable popular matching with at most one blocking type pair exists if and only if a discrete perfect popular matching with at most one blocking edge exists.

We now apply the discrete hardness theorem supplied in the question. Schlotter–Cseh’s Theorem 5 establishes hardness with maximum degree \(3\), single-peaked and single-crossing preferences, and target size one larger than a stable matching. Their construction explicitly supplies a stable matching leaving precisely two vertices unmatched, so it has size \(N-1\). [Schlotter–Cseh, Theorem 5 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02189)

Given such a discrete instance, keep its graph and preferences and replace each vertex by a Lebesgue type of measure \(1/N\). For its supplied stable matching \(M_s\), supply the rational type-pair masses
\[ \]
\(x^s_{ab}=1/N\) for \(ab\in M_s\), and \(x^s_{ab}=0\) otherwise.

Any measurable realization has mass \((N-1)/N=1-1/N\). Each type receives its discrete partner type, or is entirely unmatched. Therefore its blocking predicate is exactly the discrete one, and this supplied measurable matching is stable.

All remaining promises are preserved: the graph, degrees, restricted rankings, axes, and single-crossing extensions are unchanged. No compatible pairs are added.

By the proved equivalence, the discrete instance is a yes-instance exactly when the constructed measurable instance is. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction and proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Certificates and running times

A yes-certificate can specify a discrete perfect matching \(M\), using \(O(N\log N)\) bits. Perfection and its blocking-edge count are checked directly.

Popularity can be verified through one maximum-weight bipartite matching computation. Give each compatible edge weight
\[ \]
\(c_{ab}=w_{ab}+2.\)
The formula proved above shows that \(M\) is popular exactly when the maximum weight of a discrete partial matching is at most \(2N\).

Here \(c_{ab}\in\{0,2,4\}\): an edge of \(M\) has weight \(2\), and both endpoints of any other edge compare distinct partner types. Set every incompatible pair’s weight to \(0\) and solve a complete assignment problem. Its optimum equals the maximum partial-matching weight:

  • every partial matching extends to an assignment, and added weights are nonnegative;
  • deleting incompatible pairs from an assignment preserves its weight and gives a feasible partial matching.

A standard assignment algorithm uses \(O(N^3)\) arithmetic operations on integers of polynomially bounded magnitude. Consequently, certificate verification has polynomial bit complexity, and, under the usual indexed input encoding, can be bounded by \(\widetilde O(L+N^3)\), where the tilde suppresses logarithmic factors.

Since \(m=2N\) and \(\tau=N\), this is also \(\widetilde O(L+m^3)=\widetilde O(L+\tau^3)\).

A deterministic exact algorithm enumerates each type in \(A\)’s choice of a neighbor, checks whether the choices form a perfect matching, and performs the verification above. Degree at most \(3\) gives at most \(3^N\) choices. Its running time is
\[ \]
\(\widetilde O(L+3^N N^3) =\widetilde O(L+3^\tau\tau^3) =\widetilde O(L+3^{m/2}m^3).\)

The hardness reduction copies the discrete input and adds \(O(N\log N)\) bits for the society masses and supplied stable matching. It therefore runs in polynomial time and does not expand a binary-encoded population.

Thus a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or equivalently in \(N,L\), would imply \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\).

6. Why mixed-matchings tractability does not apply

The extraction proof uses an opponent that changes only a selected positive-mass subset of the actual individuals and retains all other assignments. This is permitted by the measurable individual-comparison model.

Expected voting between mixed matchings instead averages comparisons with a randomly drawn baseline matching. It does not require immunity to such a change conditional on a realized component; indeed, a popular mixed matching can contain unpopular matchings in its support. [Huang–Kavitha, Definition 2 and Figure 1](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf)

The proof here handles the specified measurable voting rule directly.
