The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), an integer generalized scoring rule \(GS(f,g)\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(T=L(C)\), and a target \(c\), find the minimum \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\) such that \(x_{t,t'}\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\), and \(g\!\left(\operatorname{Ord}\!\left(\sum_t\mu_t f(t)+\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}(f(t')-f(t))\right)\right)=c\), or report \(\infty\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) have \(m\ge1\) candidates, let \(T=L(C)\) be the strict complete rankings, and let \(c\in C\).
A society is given by its support \(S\subseteq T\): the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their binary rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|S|\), not \(m!\); unlisted types have zero mass.
An integer generalized scoring rule of dimension \(K\ge1\) consists of \(f:T\to\mathbb Z^K\) and \(g:\operatorname{Pre}(\{1,\ldots,K\})\to C\). For \(s\in\mathbb R^K\), \(\operatorname{Ord}(s)\) orders coordinates by their values, retaining exact equalities; the winner is \(g(\operatorname{Ord}(s))\), including all tie handling.
The input supplies Boolean circuits for \(f\), with signed binary integer outputs, and \(g\), whose input is a total preorder encoded by its pairwise comparison matrix; these circuits are promised to define the stated functions. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For each \(t\in S\) and \(t'\in T\setminus\{t\}\), a real amount \(x_{t,t'}\ge0\) may be changed from ranking \(t\) to ranking \(t'\), subject to \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\). Destinations need not belong to \(S\).
The cost is \(\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\), and success means \(g\!\left(\operatorname{Ord}\!\left(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t f(t)+\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}(f(t')-f(t))\right)\right)=c\).
The optimization value is the infimum of successful costs, with \(\infty\) for infeasibility; also distinguish whether a finite infimum is attained. Budget feasibility asks whether success is possible at cost at most a given binary rational \(b\ge0\).

Is this continuous constructive bribery problem solvable exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard via its budget-feasibility decision version?

For fixed candidate sets, integer generalized scoring rules, and operation sets, discrete minimum operation counts under independent identically distributed votes lie, with arbitrarily high asymptotic probability, in \(0,\Theta(\sqrt n),\Theta(n),\infty\); the corresponding classification also holds for discrete constructive bribery. This is an asymptotic classification, not a worst-case complexity classification. [Xia](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.1231)
No result settling this exact continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The rule representation was unspecified; supply Boolean circuits for both functions, with explicit signed binary score outputs and a pairwise comparison encoding of total preorders, and include their sizes in the input length.
  • Normalize society mass to one, as required by the stated distribution, and represent only positive-mass support types.
  • Permit changes to every complete ranking, including rankings outside the initial support, as in discrete constructive bribery.
  • Keep score equalities exact and let the supplied winner function determine all tie outcomes.
  • A minimum need not exist with exact tie handling; use the infimum together with its attainment status, reserving infinity for infeasibility.
  • Use a nonnegative binary rational budget for the associated decision problem, and allow the candidate count and rule dimension to vary for worst-case complexity.

Result

Budget feasibility is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete under the stated circuit representation. Hardness already holds with \(\tau=1\), \(K=2\), scores in \(\{0,1\}^2\), and budget \(b=\tfrac12\).

Consequently, an exact polynomial-time algorithm for the optimization problem would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). In the hardness construction, every feasible instance has minimum cost exactly \(\tfrac12\), and that minimum is attained.

The representation matters: the rule circuits are part of the input. This result does not assert hardness for every fixed, familiar voting rule.

1. Reduction from Boolean satisfiability

Let \(\varphi\) be a Boolean formula on variables \(z_1,\ldots,z_n\), where \(n\ge1\). We construct a continuous bribery instance in time polynomial in the encoding length of \(\varphi\).

Candidates and assignment encoding

Take the candidate set

\(C=\{c,d,u_1,v_1,\ldots,u_n,v_n\}\).

For each strict ranking \(t\), define the assignment \(a(t)\in\{0,1\}^n\) by

\(a_i(t)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_i\text{ precedes }v_i\text{ in }t.\)

Define

\(h(t)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c\text{ precedes }d\text{ in }t\ \text{and}\ \varphi(a(t))=1.\)

Otherwise, let \(h(t)=0\).

Every assignment \(a\in\{0,1\}^n\) has a corresponding ranking with \(c\) preceding \(d\): put \(c,d\) first, followed by the pairs \(\{u_i,v_i\}\), ordering each pair according to \(a_i\). Hence

\(\exists t\in T:\ h(t)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\varphi\text{ is satisfiable}.\)

Precedence tests are computable by polynomial-size Boolean circuits from an explicit ranking encoding. Combining those tests with a circuit for \(\varphi\) gives a polynomial-size circuit for \(h\).

Scoring and winner circuits

Set \(K=2\) and define

\(f(t)=(h(t),\,1-h(t)).\)

Thus every score vector belongs to \(\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\), and the scoring circuit has polynomial size.

Define the winner function on every total preorder of the two coordinates by

\(g(\operatorname{Ord}(s))= \begin{cases} c,&s_1\ge s_2,\\ d,&s_1<s_2. \end{cases}\)

In particular, an exact tie selects \(c\). This is a constant-size circuit reading the comparison between coordinates \(1\) and \(2\), and it defines a valid winner on all three possible total preorders.

Initial society and budget

Let the support contain only the ranking

\(t_0:\ d\succ c\succ u_1\succ v_1\succ\cdots\succ u_n\succ v_n,\)

with \(\mu_{t_0}=1\). Because \(d\) precedes \(c\),

\(h(t_0)=0,\qquad f(t_0)=(0,1).\)

Set the budget to \(b=\tfrac12\).

All supplied data, including the ranking and the circuits, have encoding length polynomial in the size of \(\varphi\).

2. Correctness, including arbitrary fractional mixing

Consider any legal bribery. Write \(x_t=x_{t_0,t}\) for \(t\ne t_0\), and define

\(X=\sum_{t\ne t_0}x_t,\qquad q=\sum_{t\ne t_0}x_t h(t).\)

Here \(X\) is the cost, and \(q\) is the total mass sent to destinations with \(h(t)=1\). Since \(h(t)\in\{0,1\}\),

\(0\le q\le X\le1.\)

The resulting score vector is exactly

\[ \text{[The score calculation is given below using the requested inline delimiters.]} \]

\(s=(0,1)+\sum_{t\ne t_0}x_t\bigl((h(t),1-h(t))-(0,1)\bigr)=(q,1-q).\)

Therefore success is equivalent to

\(q\ge1-q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\ge\tfrac12.\)

A satisfying assignment gives successful bribery

Suppose \(\varphi\) has a satisfying assignment \(a\). Choose a corresponding ranking \(t_a\) with \(c\) preceding \(d\). Then \(h(t_a)=1\), and \(t_a\ne t_0\).

Change mass \(\tfrac12\) from \(t_0\) to \(t_a\). The cost is \(\tfrac12\), the resulting score is \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), and the tie selects \(c\).

Successful bribery gives a satisfying assignment

Conversely, suppose a bribery succeeds. Then \(q\ge\tfrac12>0\). The sum defining \(q\) is finite and consists of nonnegative terms, so some destination \(t\) satisfies

\(x_t>0\quad\text{and}\quad h(t)=1.\)

By the definition of \(h\), the assignment \(a(t)\) satisfies \(\varphi\).

Thus fractional mixing cannot create success when every destination fails the formula test. We have proved

\(\varphi\text{ is satisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{success is possible at cost at most }\tfrac12.\)

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Exact optimization and attainment in the reduction

If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, then \(h(t)=0\) for every ranking. Every bribery leaves the score equal to \((0,1)\), so success is infeasible and the optimization value is \(\infty\).

If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, every successful bribery satisfies

\(X\ge q\ge\tfrac12.\)

The single-destination construction above attains cost \(\tfrac12\). Consequently,

\(\operatorname{OPT}= \begin{cases} \tfrac12,&\varphi\text{ is satisfiable, with the minimum attained},\\ \infty,&\varphi\text{ is unsatisfiable}. \end{cases}\)

Thus the hardness does not depend on confusing an infimum with an attainable budget. Even determining whether the optimization value is finite is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for this family.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

We now prove membership for the general promised input model, including arbitrary real bribery amounts and exact ties.

4.1. A successful bribery has a sparse equivalent

For every allowed operation \((t,t')\), define the vector

\(v_{t,t'}=(e_t,\ f(t')-f(t),\ 1)\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau+K+1},\)

where \(e_t\) is the unit vector indexed by the supported source type \(t\).

For any bribery, the sum

\(\sum_{t,t'}x_{t,t'}v_{t,t'}\)

records all source outflows, the score change, and the cost. We claim that the same sum can be represented using at most \(\tau+K+1\) positive amounts.

To prove this, suppose more than \(\tau+K+1\) amounts are positive. The corresponding vectors are linearly dependent, so there are coefficients \(\alpha_j\), not all zero, with

\(\sum_j\alpha_jv_j=0.\)

Every vector has last coordinate \(1\), hence \(\sum_j\alpha_j=0\). Therefore the coefficients include both a positive and a negative value. Set

\(\lambda=\min_{\alpha_j>0}\frac{x_j}{\alpha_j},\qquad x'_j=x_j-\lambda\alpha_j.\)

Every new amount is nonnegative, at least one positive amount becomes zero, and the recorded vector remains unchanged. Repeating this step proves the claim.

In particular, any successful bribery has a successful equivalent using at most \(\tau+K+1\) operations, with exactly the same source outflows, score, and cost.

4.2. Polynomial-size rational amounts suffice

Fix the operations in such a sparse successful bribery. Let their number be \(q\le\tau+K+1\), and let \(\preceq\) be its resulting coordinate preorder.

Using variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_q\), impose the source-capacity constraints, nonnegativity, and the budget bound. Write

\(s(x)=s^0+\sum_{j=1}^q x_j\Delta_j,\qquad s^0=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t f(t).\)

For every pair tied under \(\preceq\), impose \(s_i(x)=s_j(x)\). Introduce another variable \(\varepsilon\), impose \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\), and for every pair with \(s_i>s_j\) under \(\preceq\), impose

\(s_i(x)-s_j(x)\ge\varepsilon.\)

The original real bribery yields a feasible point with \(\varepsilon>0\): if there are strict comparisons, use the minimum of \(1\) and their finitely many positive gaps; otherwise use \(\varepsilon=1\).

This rational linear program is bounded. Each operation amount belongs to a source-capacity constraint and therefore lies in \([0,1]\); also \(\varepsilon\in[0,1]\). Its feasible set is closed. Maximizing \(\varepsilon\) consequently has an optimal vertex, and its optimal value is positive.

The program has polynomially many variables and constraints. Its coefficients have polynomial encoding length:

  • circuit outputs \(f(t)\) have bit length bounded by the input encoding;
  • score differences are differences of those integers;
  • \(s^0\) is a sum of \(\tau\) products of supplied rationals and circuit outputs;
  • masses and the budget are supplied binary rationals.

The optimal vertex also has polynomial encoding length. Indeed, in dimension \(r=q+1\), a vertex is determined by \(r\) linearly independent active constraints. After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule expresses its coordinates as ratios of integer determinants. If the resulting entries have magnitude at most \(H\), each determinant has magnitude at most \(r!H^r\), whose bit length is polynomial in the input size.

The resulting rational amounts preserve every equality and strict comparison of \(\preceq\), because \(\varepsilon>0\). They therefore give a successful bribery within budget.

4.3. Verification

A certificate lists at most \(\tau+K+1\) operations, their destination rankings, and their polynomial-size rational amounts.

A verifier checks that the destinations are valid rankings distinct from their sources, evaluates the scoring circuit, checks source capacities and budget, computes the resulting rational scores, constructs their exact comparison matrix, and evaluates \(g\).

All these steps take polynomial time. Thus budget feasibility belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness proof.

5. Unattained infima can also occur

For completeness, change only the winner function in the reduction: let \(c\) win exactly when \(s_1>s_2\), with \(d\) winning the tie.

If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, success now requires \(q>\tfrac12\). Consequently every successful cost is strictly greater than \(\tfrac12\). Nevertheless, sending mass \(\tfrac12+\delta\) to a satisfying destination succeeds for every \(0<\delta\le\tfrac12\).

The infimum is therefore \(\tfrac12\), and it is not attained. If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, success remains infeasible. This illustrates why exact budget feasibility cannot generally be inferred from the infimum alone.

6. Scope of the conclusion

The requested complexity alternative is settled: the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and its budget-feasibility version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The hardness already occurs with two score coordinates and one supported type; the exponentially large destination space is represented succinctly by the scoring circuit.

This does not conflict with [Xia’s asymptotic classification](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.1231), which concerns fixed alternatives and operations under independently sampled votes, rather than worst-case complexity with rule circuits supplied as input.
