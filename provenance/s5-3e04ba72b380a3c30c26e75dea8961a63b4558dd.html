The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many programs and a finite support \(T\) of applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies preferences, a grade vector, and an eligibility vector, plus rational per-capita capacities and reserve masses, compute a feasible assignment \(x_{tp}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_p x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\). The assignment must be stable under the paper's multidimensional-reserve priorities, meaning no positive mass can obtain a preferred program through a blocking contract, and fair under its grade-and-privilege criterion, with equal-grade mass allowed to split at cutoffs.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq1\) programs and \(k\geq1\) privileges; type \(t\) has strict preferences over programs and being unmatched, rational program-specific grades \(\theta_{tp}\), and eligibility vector \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^k\).
A contract specifies a program and a claimed vector \(v\leq a_t\), coordinatewise; applicants care only about their program. Assigned mass claims \(a_t\), but blocking offers may claim any \(v\leq a_t\).
Each program has nonnegative rational capacity \(\kappa_p\) and an explicitly ordered list of reserve blocks \((r_{pj},b_{pj})\), with \(r_{pj}\in\{0,1\}^k\), \(b_{pj}\geq0\), and \(\sum_j b_{pj}=\kappa_p\). Let \(R\) be the total number of blocks and \(L\) the total input bit length; unreserved capacity is a block with vector \(0\).
A block reserved for \(r\) first ranks all contracts claiming \(v\geq r\) together by decreasing grade; remaining contracts are ranked by decreasing binary value of \(v\), then decreasing grade. Every contract is acceptable to every block. Equal grades within a priority class may split arbitrarily.
The choice correspondence \(C_p\) processes blocks in their given order, filling each up to its mass from the highest-priority remaining offered mass and removing selected mass before processing the next block; it contains every outcome permitted by cutoff ties.
An assignment is rational \(x_{tp}\geq0\), with \(\sum_p x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\), and \(x_{tp}=0\) whenever being unmatched is preferred to \(p\); write \(x_{t\varnothing}=\mu_t-\sum_p x_{tp}\).
Stability requires that choosing from the incumbent contracts can retain all incumbents, and that no \(t,p,q,v,\varepsilon\) with \(q\in P\cup\{\varnothing\}\), \(p\succ_t q\), \(v\leq a_t\), and \(0<\varepsilon\leq x_{tq}\) blocks: blocking means that adding a separately labelled offer of \(\varepsilon\) type-\(t\) mass claiming \(v\) to the incumbents at \(p\) makes every outcome of \(C_p\) select positive mass from that offer.
Fairness requires that whenever \(x_{tq}>0\), \(x_{up}>0\), and \(p\succ_t q\), either \(\theta_{up}\geq\theta_{tp}\) or \(a_t\not\geq a_u\), including \(q=\varnothing\).

Is computing a stable, fair assignment under these definitions, or determining that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,R,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite applicants with distinct grades, the cumulative offer mechanism with these multidimensional priorities produces stable, fair allocations ([Aygün–Bó](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/163090/1/AygunBo2020.pdf)).
Fractional rationing algorithms respecting category priorities and non-wastefulness are known for identical resources without applicant preferences over distinct programs ([Aziz](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2023/pdfs/p949.pdf)). No result settling the precise computational problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the number of privileges variable and encoded eligibility and reserve vectors explicitly, following the general multidimensional model.
  • Added an input precedence order over rational reserve blocks; repeated reserve vectors are permitted to represent interleaved precedence orders.
  • Required reserve block masses to sum to program capacity, representing unreserved capacity by the zero vector; reserves confer priority rather than impose hard eligibility constraints.
  • Specified rational grades without a sign restriction, strict preferences including being unmatched, and individual rationality.
  • Assigned mass claims its full eligibility vector, while blocking offers retain the discrete model's option to claim any coordinatewise smaller vector.
  • Defined divisible choice by sequentially consuming offered mass and allowing arbitrary splits at equal-grade cutoffs.
  • Resolved blocking at ties by requiring positive acceptance of the new offer in every permitted choice outcome, so equal-priority displacement alone does not block.
  • Extended fairness to unmatched mass and allowed equal-grade splitting by excluding only strictly higher-grade justified envy.
  • Specified exact rational output and included reporting nonexistence in the computational problem.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,R,L\). Every instance admits a rational stable, fair assignment.

The construction reduces the problem to a bipartite stable-allocation problem with one vertex per supported type and one vertex per positive reserve block. Ordering a program’s blocks by their processing order on the applicant side ensures that the resulting allocation respects sequential choice.

The proof below uses the stipulated blocking definition: an offer blocks only when every permitted choice outcome selects positive mass from it.

1. Priority observations and automatic fairness

Write \((t,v)\succeq^0_{pj}(u,w)\) when the type-\(t\) contract claiming \(v\) has weakly higher priority than the type-\(u\) contract claiming \(w\) at block \(j\) of program \(p\). Labels do not affect priorities.

Two properties follow from the specified rankings.

Claim 1: Full claims weakly dominate reduced claims. For every \(v\leq a_t\),

\((t,a_t)\succeq^0_{pj}(t,v)\)

at every block.

Indeed, if \(v\geq r_{pj}\), then \(a_t\geq r_{pj}\), and both contracts belong to the reserved priority class with the same grade. If only \(a_t\) satisfies the reserve, its priority is strictly higher. If neither satisfies the reserve, the binary value of \(a_t\) is at least that of \(v\), and their grades coincide.

Claim 2: Eligibility dominance and a strictly higher grade imply strict priority at every block. If

\(a_t\geq a_u\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{tp}>\theta_{up},\)

then

\((t,a_t)\succ^0_{pj}(u,a_u)\)

for every block \(j\).

If \(u\) satisfies the reserve, so does \(t\), and their grades establish the comparison. If only \(t\) satisfies it, the comparison follows from reserved priority. If neither satisfies it, the binary value of \(a_t\) is strictly larger unless \(a_t=a_u\); in that remaining case, the grade comparison establishes strict priority.

Proposition 1. Every stable assignment under the question’s definitions is fair.

Proof. Suppose a feasible assignment violates fairness. Then there are \(t,u,p,q\) with

\(x_{tq}>0,\quad x_{up}>0,\quad p\succ_t q,\quad a_t\geq a_u,\quad\theta_{tp}>\theta_{up}.\)

Choose any positive rational \(\varepsilon\leq x_{tq}\), and offer that mass at \(p\), separately labelled and claiming \(a_t\).

Let \(S=\sum_s x_{sp}\) be the incumbent mass at \(p\). Since every contract is acceptable to every block, every choice outcome selects total mass

\(\min\{\kappa_p,S+\varepsilon\}.\)

This follows by processing the blocks in order: each block takes its capacity or all remaining offered mass, whichever is smaller.

Suppose an outcome selects none of the offer. Since \(S\leq\kappa_p\), its selected mass must then equal \(S\), and it selects all incumbents. In particular, it selects positive type-\(u\) mass at some block. The entire offer remains available at that block, where Claim 2 places it strictly above the type-\(u\) incumbents. Selecting those incumbents while rejecting the offer contradicts the block’s priority rule.

Thus every outcome selects positive offer mass, giving a block and contradicting stability. \(\square\)

Consequently, it suffices to construct a stable assignment.

2. The stable-allocation theorem used

A bipartite stable-allocation instance consists of a graph \(G=(A\cup B,E)\), vertex quotas \(Q_w\), edge capacities \(c_e\), and a strict ordering of incident edges at each vertex.

An allocation \(z\) satisfies

\(0\leq z_e\leq c_e,\qquad \sum_{e\ni w}z_e\leq Q_w.\)

An edge \(e\) blocks if \(z_e<c_e\) and each endpoint either has unused quota or has positive allocation on an incident edge strictly below \(e\) in its ordering.

Stable-allocation theorem. Such an instance has a stable allocation, computable in strongly polynomially many arithmetic operations. The inductive algorithm preserves integrality for integer quotas and capacities. These results appear in Sections 1–2 of the [corrected Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

We verify its hypotheses in the construction below and address binary arithmetic in Section 6.

3. Construction of the auxiliary instance

Discard zero-mass reserve blocks, preserving the order of all remaining blocks. This leaves the choice correspondence unchanged. Let \(J_p\) denote the remaining block indices at \(p\).

Construct a bipartite instance as follows.

  • Each supported type \(t\) is a left vertex with quota \(Q_t=\mu_t\).
  • Each positive block \((p,j)\) is a right vertex with quota \(Q_{pj}=b_{pj}\).
  • Include an edge \((t,p,j)\) exactly when \(p\succ_t\varnothing\). Give it capacity \(c_{tpj}=\mu_t\).

At a type vertex, order edges first by the type’s strict program preferences. Among edges belonging to the same program, rank earlier blocks strictly above later blocks.

At block \((p,j)\), rank types using the original priorities of their full-claim contracts \((t,a_t)\), and refine every tie by a fixed ordering of type indices. Denote this strict refinement by \(\widehat\succ_{pj}\), with weak version \(\widehat\succeq_{pj}\). In particular,

\(u\widehat\succeq_{pj}t\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(u,a_u)\succeq^0_{pj}(t,a_t).\)

Both sides now have strict preferences. Quotas and capacities are positive rationals, and the graph is bipartite. Isolated vertices simply receive zero allocation. Thus the stable-allocation theorem applies.

Compute a stable allocation \(z\), and define

\(x_{tp}=\sum_{j\in J_p}z_{tpj},\qquad x_{t\varnothing}=\mu_t-\sum_p x_{tp}.\)

Vertex feasibility gives

\(\sum_p x_{tp}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tp}\leq\sum_{j\in J_p}b_{pj}=\kappa_p.\)

Edges to unacceptable programs were omitted, so \(x\) also satisfies applicant individual rationality.

We next prove stability.

4. The auxiliary allocation respects sequential choice

Call type \(t\) willing to increase an edge if it has unused quota or positive allocation on an edge ranked strictly below that edge in its auxiliary ordering.

Lemma 1. If \(t\) is willing to increase \((t,p,j)\), then

\(\sum_u z_{upj}=b_{pj},\)

and every type \(u\) with \(z_{upj}>0\) satisfies \(u\widehat\succeq_{pj}t\).

Proof. Willingness implies \(z_{tpj}<\mu_t=c_{tpj}\): either some quota remains unused or positive mass is assigned to another edge.

If block \((p,j)\) had unused quota or positive allocation to a type ranked strictly below \(t\), both endpoints could improve along this unsaturated edge. That would block \(z\). Auxiliary stability therefore requires the block to be full and all its positive holders to be ranked at least as high as \(t\). \(\square\)

Lemma 2. For each program \(p\), sequential choice from its incumbents can select exactly the mass \(z_{tpj}\) at each block \(j\).

Proof. Proceed through \(J_p\) in its given order. After selecting the prescribed masses at earlier blocks, the remaining type-\(t\) incumbent mass is

\(s_t^j=\sum_{\ell\in J_p:\,\ell\geq j}z_{tp\ell}.\)

We prescribe selecting \(z_{tpj}\) at block \(j\).

If some type \(t\) has mass left after this prescribed selection, then \(z_{tp\ell}>0\) for some later block \(\ell>j\). Type \(t\) strictly prefers block \(j\) to block \(\ell\) in the auxiliary ordering. Lemma 1 therefore says that block \(j\) is full and that every prescribed selected type \(u\) satisfies

\((u,a_u)\succeq^0_{pj}(t,a_t).\)

This applies to every type with positive remaining mass after block \(j\). Hence no prescribed selected mass has lower priority than any mass left unselected.

If no mass remains after the prescribed selection, it selects all available incumbents, whose total is at most \(b_{pj}\).

In either case, the prescribed selection has the required total mass and respects the priority ordering. For a finite weak ordering, these conditions characterize a legal cutoff selection: all classes above the cutoff are selected, all classes below it are rejected, and the cutoff class may split arbitrarily. Thus the prescribed selection is permitted.

Induction proves the lemma. \(\square\)

After the last block, this outcome has selected all incumbents. The incumbent-retention requirement therefore holds.

5. Every proposed blocking offer can be rejected

Fix any candidate tuple \(t,p,q,v,\varepsilon\) from the blocking definition:

\(x_{tq}>0,\qquad p\succ_t q,\qquad v\leq a_t,\qquad 0<\varepsilon\leq x_{tq}.\)

Because every positively assigned program is acceptable, \(p\succ_t q\) implies \(p\succ_t\varnothing\). Thus type \(t\) has an auxiliary edge to every positive block of \(p\).

For every \(j\in J_p\), type \(t\) is willing to increase \((t,p,j)\):

  • If \(q=\varnothing\), then \(x_{t\varnothing}>0\), so its auxiliary vertex has unused quota.
  • If \(q\) is a program, then \(x_{tq}>0\) implies \(z_{tq\ell}>0\) for some block \(\ell\). Every block of \(p\) ranks above that edge in type \(t\)’s auxiliary ordering.

Lemma 1 consequently gives, at every positive block of \(p\),

\(\sum_u z_{upj}=b_{pj},\qquad z_{upj}>0\Longrightarrow(u,a_u)\succeq^0_{pj}(t,a_t).\)

By Claim 1 and transitivity,

\(z_{upj}>0\Longrightarrow(u,a_u)\succeq^0_{pj}(t,v).\)

Now add the separately labelled offer of mass \(\varepsilon\). Process the blocks using exactly the incumbent selections prescribed by \(z\), leaving the entire offer unselected.

These selections are legal. Lemma 2 establishes that they respect priorities relative to all remaining incumbents. The displayed comparison establishes that every selected incumbent weakly outranks the offer. Every block is full, so there is no requirement to select additional mass. Whenever equality occurs at a cutoff, the correspondence permits selecting the prescribed incumbents and rejecting the separately labelled offer.

This constructs an outcome of \(C_p\) selecting zero mass from the offer. Therefore the offer does not satisfy the universal selection condition required for blocking.

If \(J_p\) is empty, program \(p\) has zero capacity and selects no offer mass, so the same conclusion holds immediately.

Since the candidate tuple was arbitrary, no blocking offer exists. Hence \(x\) is stable. Proposition 1 then makes \(x\) fair.

6. Polynomial time and rational output

The auxiliary graph has at most \(\tau+R\) vertices and at most \(\tau R\) edges.

Its priority lists are constructible in polynomial time. Testing \(a_t\geq r_{pj}\) takes \(O(k)\) coordinate comparisons. Binary values can be compared as \(k\)-bit strings, and rational grades can be compared exactly in polynomial bit complexity. No reduced-claim vectors need to be enumerated.

For an explicit bit bound, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all masses \(\mu_t\) and positive block capacities \(b_{pj}\). Their encodings are part of the input, so

\(\log_2 D=O(L).\)

Scale all auxiliary quotas and capacities by \(D\). They become integers of polynomial bit length. Apply the strongly polynomial inductive stable-allocation algorithm. Its numerical updates use sums, differences, minima, and comparisons of allocations and residual capacities, so integer data produce an integer stable allocation \(y\). These quantities have polynomial bit length: allocations are bounded by their capacities, and residuals and sums involve only polynomially many input quantities.

The scaled computation therefore takes polynomial bit time. Dividing by \(D\) yields \(z=y/D\). Scaling preserves feasibility and blocking comparisons.

Moreover,

\(0\leq\sum_{j\in J_p}y_{tpj}\leq D\mu_t\leq D,\)

so each output entry \(x_{tp}\) has a rational representation with polynomial bit length.

Thus the complete construction runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,R,L\), produces a rational stable, fair assignment, and proves that a “none exists” outcome never occurs under the stated definitions.
