The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational per-capita chore supplies \(q_e\ge0\) and costs \(c_e\in[0,1]\), let \(b=\sum_e q_ec_e\). Choose a finite-support distribution \(y_z\ge0\) over integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), with \(\sum_z y_z=1\) and \(\sum_z y_zz_e=q_e\) for every chore type \(e\), minimizing \(\sum_z y_z[c\cdot z-b]_+\). Determine the worst-case optimum and whether an optimal mass assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\) and the binary input length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) agent types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) means support size, not the number of possible types. Here the society has total mass \(1\) and one identical additive-cost type, so \(\tau=1\).
There are \(m\ge1\) chore types, with binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_e\ge0\) and unit costs \(c_e\in[0,1]\); supplies need not sum to \(1\). Write \(b=\sum_{e=1}^m q_ec_e\).
An assignment is a finite-support distribution of masses \(y_z\ge0\) over integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), satisfying \(\sum_z y_z=1\) and \(\sum_z y_zz_e=q_e\) for every \(e\). Empty bundles and multiple copies of a chore type are allowed.
The subsidy for bundle \(z\) is \([c\cdot z-b]_+\), where \([a]_+=\max\{a,0\}\). Define \(S^*(q,c)=\min_y\sum_z y_z[c\cdot z-b]_+\). An optimal assignment is represented by listing its supported bundles and binary-rational masses; \(L\) is the total binary input length, including any threshold.

Is deciding \(S^*(q,c)\le K\), for binary-rational \(K\ge0\), in \(\mathrm P\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and can an exact optimal assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?
What is the sharp worst-case value \(\sup_{m\ge1,\;q\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m,\;c\in(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1])^m}S^*(q,c)\)?

For \(n\ge2\) identical discrete agents, a polynomial-time algorithm achieves total proportionality subsidy at most \(n/4\) for even \(n\), and \((n^2-1)/(4n)\) for odd \(n\); both worst-case bounds are tight. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04411)
No result settling exactly this finite-support mass-assignment problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted society types as agent cost types: the identical-cost society has one supported type of mass 1; assignment support is distinct from society support.
  • Made costs binary rational, as required for a binary-input complexity question, and specified explicit rational encoding of the output masses and bundles.
  • Interpreted worst case as the supremum over all admissible rational supplies, normalized costs, and positive numbers of chore types.
  • Introduced a nonnegative binary-rational subsidy threshold to state the two-sided decision complexity target precisely.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

1. Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already with \(\tau=1\), threshold \(K=0\), and every supply equal to \(1/2\). Consequently, computing an exact optimal assignment in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Nevertheless, every instance has an exact optimal assignment with at most \(m+1\) supported bundles and polynomial total binary encoding length.

The sharp worst-case value is

\(\displaystyle \sup_{m,q,c} S^*(q,c)=\frac14.\)

This value is attained.

2. A useful identity

Write an assignment as a finitely supported random integral bundle \(Z\), with \(\mathbb E Z=q\), and let \(X=c\cdot Z\). Then \(\mathbb E X=b\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[X-b]_+=\frac12\mathbb E|X-b|.\)

Indeed, the positive and negative parts of \(X-b\) have equal expectations because \(\mathbb E(X-b)=0\).

It follows that an assignment has zero subsidy exactly when every bundle receiving positive mass has cost \(b\). We will use this characterization in the reduction.

3. Attainment and polynomial-size optimal assignments

We first justify that the minimum is attained and that optimal assignments provide polynomial-size certificates.

Define the capped-cost function

\(\displaystyle h(z)=\min\{c\cdot z,b\}.\)

For any feasible assignment,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[c\cdot Z-b]_+=b-\mathbb E h(Z).\)

Consider the relaxed maximization problem over finite-support distributions satisfying

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E Z_e\le q_e\quad\text{for every }e,\)

with objective \(\mathbb E h(Z)\).

Reduction to finitely many bundles. For each positive-cost coordinate, set

\(\displaystyle B_e=\left\lfloor\frac{b+1}{c_e}\right\rfloor,\)

and set \(B_e=0\) when \(c_e=0\). Let

\(\displaystyle \mathcal T=\{z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m: z_e\le B_e\text{ for every }e\}.\)

Every integral bundle can be replaced by a coordinatewise smaller bundle in \(\mathcal T\), preserving its capped cost:

  • Delete all zero-cost chores.
  • If its cost is below \(b\), retain the remaining bundle.
  • If its cost is at least \(b>0\), retain chores only until their accumulated cost first reaches \(b\). The retained cost is less than \(b+1\), since every unit cost is at most \(1\).
  • If \(b=0\), replace the bundle by the empty bundle.

Thus every relaxed feasible distribution can be replaced by one supported on \(\mathcal T\), without increasing any coordinate mean or changing its objective.

Consequently, the relaxed optimum is the optimum \(H\) of the finite rational linear program

\(\displaystyle \max \sum_{z\in\mathcal T}p_z h(z), \quad \sum_{z\in\mathcal T}p_z=1, \quad \sum_{z\in\mathcal T}p_z z_e\le q_e, \quad p_z\ge0. \)

The feasible region is nonempty, since it contains the distribution concentrated on the empty bundle, and compact, since it lies in a finite-dimensional probability simplex. Hence an optimum exists. A basic optimal solution has at most \(m+1\) positive bundle masses.

Although \(\mathcal T\) may be very large, each coordinate \(B_e\) has polynomial binary length in the input. The masses of a basic solution also have polynomial binary length: they solve a rational linear system of order at most \(m+1\), with integer bundle coefficients of polynomial binary length and rational right-hand sides from the input. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule gives the required determinant bounds.

Restoring the missing supplies. Let \(Z\) have such a basic optimal relaxed distribution, and define

\(\displaystyle d=q-\mathbb E Z\ge0.\)

There is a rational distribution on at most \(m+1\) integral vectors \(W\) with \(\mathbb E W=d\). Explicitly, write

\(\displaystyle d_e=\lfloor d_e\rfloor+r_e,\qquad 0\le r_e<1,\)

and take a common uniform random variable \(U\) on \([0,1]\), setting

\(\displaystyle W_e=\lfloor d_e\rfloor+\mathbf 1_{\{U<r_e\}}.\)

The vector changes only at the at most \(m\) distinct breakpoints \(r_e\). Its induced finite distribution therefore has at most \(m+1\) outcomes, with rational masses.

Take \(Z\) and \(W\) independently. Then \(Z+W\) is a finite-support assignment with mean \(q\). Since \(h\) is coordinatewise nondecreasing,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E h(Z+W)\ge \mathbb E h(Z)=H.\)

Conversely, \(Z+W\) is feasible for the relaxed problem, whose optimum is \(H\). Thus equality holds, and

\(\displaystyle S^*(q,c)=b-H.\)

This proves attainment by a rational finite-support assignment. The construction uses at most \((m+1)^2\) bundles, all with polynomial encoding length. Optimizing the original subsidy objective over this already finite collection of bundles yields a basic optimal solution with at most \(m+1\) supported bundles, again with polynomial-length rational masses.

A proposed such assignment can be checked in polynomial time: verify nonnegative masses, total mass, all supply equalities, and the subsidy threshold using exact rational arithmetic. Therefore the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Hardness despite allowing repeated copies

We reduce from PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), decide whether some subset sums to half their total. This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; see [Karp’s original paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf). We may assume the total \(A=\sum_i a_i\) is even, because doubling every input integer preserves the answer and ensures this restriction.

The encoding below forces a cost-\(b\) integral bundle to contain exactly one chore from each pair. This is necessary because repeated copies are allowed.

Set

\(\displaystyle a_{\max}=\max_i a_i,\qquad B=n+1,\qquad M=na_{\max}+1, \)

\(\displaystyle D=MB^{n-1}+a_{\max},\qquad H=nD+1,\qquad P=\sum_{i=1}^n B^{i-1}. \)

For each \(i\), introduce two integer weights

\(\displaystyle w_{i,0}=H+MB^{i-1}, \qquad w_{i,1}=H+MB^{i-1}+a_i. \)

Define

\(\displaystyle T=nH+MP+\frac A2.\)

Choose a power of two \(C\ge H+D\). The society has \(2n\) chore types, with

\(\displaystyle q_{i,t}=\frac12,\qquad c_{i,t}=\frac{w_{i,t}}C,\qquad K=0.\)

All costs lie in \((0,1]\); indeed, the reduction uses dyadic costs and supplies. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle b=\frac1{2C}\sum_{i,t}w_{i,t}=\frac TC. \)

Every constructed number has polynomial binary length: the largest radix power has binary length bounded by a constant multiple of \(n\log(n+1)\). Thus this is a polynomial-time reduction.

Characterizing cost-\(b\) bundles. Suppose an integral bundle \(z\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,t}w_{i,t}z_{i,t}=T.\)

Let \(k=\sum_{i,t}z_{i,t}\) be its total number of chores.

The target remainder \(R=MP+A/2\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle 0\le R\le nMB^{n-1}+\frac{na_{\max}}2\le nD<H. \)

Since each weight is at least \(H\), and \(T=nH+R<(n+1)H\), we have \(k\le n\). Each weight’s remainder after subtracting \(H\) is at most \(D\). Hence the bundle’s total remainder is at most \(kD\le nD<H\).

Uniqueness of quotient and remainder upon division by \(H\) now gives

\(\displaystyle k=n\)

and

\(\displaystyle M\sum_{i=1}^n B^{i-1}(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}) +\sum_{i=1}^n a_i z_{i,1} =MP+\frac A2. \)

Because

\(\displaystyle 0\le\sum_i a_i z_{i,1}\le na_{\max}<M \quad\text{and}\quad 0\le A/2<M, \)

reduction modulo \(M\) gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_i a_i z_{i,1}=\frac A2.\)

Subtracting this equality leaves

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n B^{i-1}(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}) =\sum_{i=1}^n B^{i-1}. \)

Every coefficient \(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}\) is at most \(n<B\). Uniqueness of base-\(B\) representation therefore forces

\(\displaystyle z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}=1\quad\text{for every }i.\)

Thus any cost-\(b\) bundle selects exactly one chore from each pair, and its selected indices with \(z_{i,1}=1\) solve PARTITION.

Equivalence of the instances. If PARTITION has a solution, take the corresponding selecting bundle and its complementary selecting bundle. Both have cost \(b\). Giving each mass \(1/2\) supplies every chore type in quantity \(1/2\) and yields zero subsidy.

Conversely, a zero-subsidy assignment must consist entirely of cost-\(b\) bundles, by Section 2. Any one of these bundles yields a partition by the characterization above.

There is also an explicit separation in a no instance. Every bundle cost is an integer multiple of \(1/C\), and none equals \(b=T/C\). Therefore every bundle satisfies

\(\displaystyle |c\cdot z-b|\ge\frac1C.\)

For every feasible assignment, Section 2 then implies

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[c\cdot Z-b]_+\ge\frac1{2C}>0.\)

Hence deciding \(S^*(q,c)\le0\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Together with Section 3, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

5. The universal upper bound

Choose a positive integer \(N\) such that all \(Nq_e\) are integers. Make \(Nq_e\) individual chores of each type \(e\), and allocate this finite collection among \(N\) identical agents.

Starting from empty bundles, assign each chore to an agent of currently minimum load. All loads remain within an interval of length at most \(1\). To verify the invariant, suppose the current minimum is \(s\), and all loads lie in \([s,s+1]\). Assigning a chore of cost at most \(1\) to a minimum-load agent leaves every load in that same interval. The initial loads satisfy the invariant.

Give each resulting bundle mass \(1/N\), merging identical bundles if necessary. This is a feasible finite-support assignment with average load \(b\).

Let its minimum and maximum loads be \(\ell\) and \(u\). If \(u=\ell\), the subsidy is zero. Otherwise, \(\ell\le b\le u\) and \(u-\ell\le1\). Convexity of \(x\mapsto[x-b]_+\) gives, for \(x\in[\ell,u]\),

\(\displaystyle [x-b]_+\le\frac{x-\ell}{u-\ell}(u-b). \)

Taking expectations yields

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[X-b]_+ \le\frac{(b-\ell)(u-b)}{u-\ell} \le\frac{u-\ell}{4} \le\frac14. \)

The middle inequality follows from

\(\displaystyle (b-\ell)(u-b)\le\frac{((b-\ell)+(u-b))^2}{4}.\)

Thus \(S^*(q,c)\le1/4\) for every instance. This construction proves existence; its expansion into individual chores can be exponentially large in the binary input length.

6. Sharpness and the computational conclusion

Take one chore type with \(q_1=1/2\) and \(c_1=1\). Then \(b=1/2\). Every integral bundle has cost a nonnegative integer, so

\(\displaystyle |c\cdot z-b|\ge\frac12.\)

Section 2 implies that every feasible assignment has subsidy at least \(1/4\). The assignment giving mass \(1/2\) to the empty bundle and mass \(1/2\) to the one-chore bundle attains this value.

Finally, an exact optimal-assignment algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would decide the reduced PARTITION instances by checking whether its output subsidy is zero. Since these instances have \(\tau=1\), such an algorithm would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). Polynomial-size exact outputs exist, but finding them is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.
