The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types that is realizable on a line, find a set \(S\) of at most two candidates such that, for every line placement of the candidates and every finite-support voter-location measure inducing \(\mu\), \(S\) contains a candidate minimizing \(\int \min_{c\in S'} |x-c|\,d\nu(x)\) over all single candidates \(S'=\{c\}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\ge 2\) candidates. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge 1\) distinct strict ranking types \(r\) over \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_r\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A realization consists of distinct candidate coordinates \(a_c\in\mathbb R\) and, for each supported type \(r\), a nonnegative finite-support measure \(\nu_r\) on \(\mathbb R\) of total mass \(\mu_r\). Every \(x\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_r)\) must satisfy \(|x-a_c|\le |x-a_d|\) whenever \(r\) ranks \(c\) above \(d\); distance ties may be resolved arbitrarily in the reported ranking. Locations and atom masses in a realization may be real, and one ranking type may occupy several locations. The input is promised to admit a realization.
Write \(\nu=\sum_r\nu_r\) and \(F(c)=\int |x-a_c|\,d\nu(x)\). Using only the ranking support and its masses, output a nonempty set \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|\le 2\) such that \(S\cap\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c\in C}F(c)\ne\varnothing\) for every realization of the input.

Is this promised search problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For explicitly listed, equally weighted voters on a line, a deterministic polynomial-time ordinal algorithm returns at most two candidates containing a minimum-sum-distance candidate in every compatible realization. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10671v1)
No result addressing exactly the binary-rational support formulation above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the rational distribution to total mass one and required nonempty positive-mass support.
  • Required at least two candidates and distinct candidate coordinates, consistent with the intended line-metric candidate ordering.
  • Interpreted compatibility as a strict reported ranking respecting weak distance inequalities, allowing arbitrary resolution of equal distances; the displayed biconditional in the supplied definition cannot literally hold for strict rankings at distance ties.
  • Represented inducing voter measures by type-indexed measures so that mass at an equal-distance location can have different reported tie resolutions.
  • Allowed each ranking type to split across finitely many locations, with arbitrary real realization coordinates and atom masses.
  • Made realizability an input promise, defined the total encoding length, and required a nonempty output shortlist.

Result

The promised search problem is solvable in polynomial time in \((m,\tau,L)\).

The proof adapts the ordering and shortlist ideas in [Sections 3 and 4.1 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10671v1). Below, all required properties are proved directly for the stated model, including arbitrary distance ties, real atom masses, and ranking types occupying several locations.

Write \(c\succ_r d\) when type \(r\) ranks \(c\) above \(d\).

1. Recovering an ordered subset of candidates

We first construct a subset \(A\subseteq C\), containing at least two candidates, with two properties:

  • Every supported type ranks every member of \(A\) above every candidate outside \(A\).
  • The algorithm determines the coordinate order of \(A\), up to reflection, in every compatible realization.

A geometric observation

In any compatible realization, every prefix of any supported ranking is a consecutive block in the coordinate order of candidates.

Indeed, suppose a prefix contains candidates \(u,w\), with \(a_u<a_w\), but excludes a candidate \(v\) satisfying \(a_u<a_v<a_w\). Choose any location \(x\) occupied by this ranking type; such a location exists because its mass is positive.

If \(x\le a_v\), then \(|x-a_w|>|x-a_v|\). If \(x\ge a_v\), then \(|x-a_u|>|x-a_v|\). Either inequality contradicts the requirement that both included candidates are ranked above \(v\). This argument permits arbitrary distance ties.

The construction

Fix one supported ranking \(r_0\), and let \(p,q\) be its first two candidates. Initially set \(A=\{p,q\}\), assigning \(p\) to a left set and \(q\) to a right set.

For the proof, fix an arbitrary realization and reflect its coordinates if necessary so that \(a_p<a_q\). The geometric observation implies that \(p,q\) are consecutive candidates. Thus every other candidate lies either left of \(p\) or right of \(q\).

Repeat the following operation, using the old set \(A\) throughout each round:

  • For each supported type \(r\), let \(j_r\) be its least preferred member of \(A\), and let \(P_r(A)\) be its ranking prefix ending at \(j_r\).
  • Add every candidate in \(\bigcup_r P_r(A)\setminus A\).
  • To assign a newly added candidate \(k\) to a side, choose a witnessing type \(r\), so that \(k\succ_r j_r\).
  • If both \(p\succ_r k\) and \(q\succ_r k\), assign \(k\) to the side opposite \(j_r\).
  • Otherwise, assign \(k\) to the side of whichever of \(p,q\) is ranked higher by \(r\).

Stop when a round adds nothing.

We prove that these side assignments are correct and that \(A\) remains consecutive.

Initially, consecutiveness follows from the geometric observation. Suppose the old \(A\) is consecutive. Every newly added \(k\) lies outside its coordinate block.

In the first assignment case, the prefix ending at \(k\) contains both pivots and excludes \(j_r\). If \(k\) were on the same side as \(j_r\), then \(j_r\) would lie strictly between \(k\) and one of the pivots. This contradicts consecutiveness of that prefix.

In the second case, let \(b\) be the higher-ranked pivot and \(b'\) the other pivot. Since at least one pivot is below \(k\), we have \(k\succ_r b'\). Consequently, the prefix ending at the lower-ranked of \(b,k\) contains both \(b,k\) and excludes \(b'\). If \(k\) were on the side opposite \(b\), then \(b'\) would lie strictly between \(b\) and \(k\), again a contradiction.

Thus every assignment is correct. In particular, multiple witnesses give consistent assignments.

Moreover, every \(P_r(A)\) is consecutive and contains the old \(A\). Their union is therefore consecutive. This establishes the invariant after the round.

Every nonterminal round adds a candidate, so the construction terminates after at most \(m\) rounds. At termination, \(P_r(A)=A\) for every type \(r\). Hence every type ranks all of \(A\) above all of \(C\setminus A\).

Ordering the resulting subset

List the left-side candidates in reverse order of their appearance in \(r_0\), followed by the right-side candidates in their order of appearance in \(r_0\).

This is their coordinate order. To verify the left part, suppose \(a_u<a_v\le a_p\). If \(u\succ_{r_0}v\), the prefix ending at \(u\) contains \(p,u\) and excludes the intervening \(v\), unless \(v=p\), in which case the fact that \(p\) is first already gives a contradiction. The right part follows similarly, using \(q\), which is second in \(r_0\).

Denote the resulting order by \(c_1,\ldots,c_s\), where \(s=|A|\).

Because the realization was arbitrary, this order is correct, up to the fixed pivot reflection, in every compatible realization.

Finally, the dominance property gives, for every realization,

\[ \text{(written inline below)} \]

\(F(c)\le F(d)\qquad(c\in A,\ d\notin A).\)

This follows by integrating the pointwise distance inequalities over every type. Therefore, at least one global optimum belongs to \(A\). Candidates outside \(A\) may tie with members of \(A\); strict dominance is unnecessary.

2. Finding a block containing a population median

For each supported type, restrict its ranking to \(A\) and replace each candidate \(c_i\) by its index \(i\). Sort these sequences lexicographically, grouping identical sequences into blocks \(B_1,\ldots,B_h\).

Let \(w_j=\sum_{r\in B_j}\mu_r\), and choose the first block \(B_b\) satisfying

\(\sum_{j=1}^{b}w_j\ge \frac12.\)

Thus \(\sum_{j<b}w_j<\frac12\). Let \(t\) be the common first-ranked candidate of the types in \(B_b\).

We claim that, in every realization, some population median is occupied by a type in \(B_b\).

Different blocks have a fixed spatial order

Consider two different restricted rankings, with the first lexicographically smaller. At their first differing position, let their candidates be \(u,v\), respectively. Then \(a_u<a_v\), the first ranking places \(u\) above \(v\), and the second places \(v\) above \(u\).

For any locations \(x,y\) occupied by these respective types, compatibility implies

\(x\le \frac{a_u+a_v}{2}\le y.\)

Here we used the elementary equivalence

\(|x-a_u|\le |x-a_v|\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\le \frac{a_u+a_v}{2}\)

when \(a_u<a_v\), obtained by squaring both nonnegative distances.

Hence every location in an earlier block is weakly left of every location in a later block. This statement applies to all atoms, even when a type occupies several locations.

The median argument

For the proof only, list all labelled atoms block by block, and within each block by increasing coordinate. The preceding property makes this a globally nondecreasing coordinate list.

Choose the first atom whose cumulative mass reaches \(\frac12\), and denote its coordinate by \(y\). The defining inequalities for \(B_b\) imply that this atom belongs to \(B_b\). Moreover,

\(\nu((-\infty,y])\ge\frac12,\qquad \nu([y,\infty))\ge\frac12.\)

Thus \(y\) is a population median. Since its type belongs to \(B_b\), compatibility also gives

\(|y-a_t|\le |y-a_c|\qquad(c\in A).\)

The algorithm needs neither the atoms nor their masses individually: it finds \(B_b\) using only the input masses.

3. A shortlist of at most three candidates

Let \(l,r\) be the immediate predecessor and successor of \(t\) in the recovered order of \(A\), whenever they exist. Define

\(T=\{t\}\cup\{l:\ l\text{ exists}\}\cup\{r:\ r\text{ exists}\}.\)

We prove that \(T\) contains a global optimum.

For a real coordinate \(z\), write \(G(z)=\int |x-z|\,d\nu(x)\). If \(u<v\le y\), then all mass at coordinates at least \(y\) experiences a distance decrease of \(v-u\) when moving from \(u\) to \(v\); every other atom experiences an increase of at most \(v-u\). Therefore,

\(G(v)-G(u)\le (v-u)\bigl(1-2\nu([y,\infty))\bigr)\le0.\)

Similarly, if \(y\le u<v\), then \(G(u)\le G(v)\).

Consequently, among candidates in \(A\), a minimum occurs at one of the candidates immediately bracketing \(y\), with the evident one-sided interpretation outside the candidate interval.

The candidate \(t\) is nearest to \(y\). If \(a_t=y\), it minimizes \(G\). Otherwise, no candidate in \(A\) can lie strictly between \(a_t\) and \(y\), or at \(y\), because such a candidate would be strictly closer to \(y\). Thus the candidates bracketing \(y\), when present, belong to \(T\).

Hence \(T\) contains a minimum over \(A\). By the dominance property proved above, that minimum is also a global minimum.

If \(|T|\le2\), output \(T\).

4. Reducing three candidates to two

It remains to handle \(T=\{l,t,r\}\), ordered so that \(a_l<a_t<a_r\).

Partition supported types according to their highest-ranked candidate among these three. Let their total masses be \(\alpha_l,\alpha_t,\alpha_r\).

We prove the two inequalities

\(F(t)-F(r)\le(\alpha_r-\alpha_l)(a_r-a_t),\)

\(F(t)-F(l)\le(\alpha_l-\alpha_r)(a_t-a_l).\)

For the first inequality, examine the contribution of each group to \(|x-a_t|-|x-a_r|\).

  • In the \(l\)-group, compatibility gives \(x\le(a_l+a_t)/2<a_t\). Its contribution is exactly \(-(a_r-a_t)\).
  • In the \(t\)-group, its contribution is nonpositive.
  • In the \(r\)-group, its contribution is at most \(a_r-a_t\), by the triangle inequality.

Integrating proves the first inequality.

For the second inequality, the \(r\)-group satisfies \(x\ge(a_t+a_r)/2>a_t\), so its contribution to \(|x-a_t|-|x-a_l|\) is exactly \(-(a_t-a_l)\). The \(t\)-group contributes at most zero, and the \(l\)-group contributes at most \(a_t-a_l\). Integration gives the second inequality.

These arguments hold separately at every atom, regardless of how a type splits its mass.

The output rule is therefore:

  • If \(\alpha_l\le\alpha_r\), output \(S=\{t,r\}\). The second inequality gives \(F(t)\le F(l)\) in every realization.
  • If \(\alpha_l>\alpha_r\), output \(S=\{l,t\}\). The first inequality gives \(F(t)<F(r)\) in every realization.

In either case, removing the discarded candidate preserves the existence of an optimum in the shortlist.

5. Bit complexity and conclusion

Precompute the position of every candidate in every supported ranking.

A construction round requires \(\mathcal O(\tau m)\) ranking operations: find each \(j_r\), scan the associated prefix, and classify additions. There are at most \(m\) rounds. Ordering the restricted rankings takes \(\mathcal O(\tau m\log(\tau+1))\) further operations.

All mass operations are sums and exact comparisons of input rationals. For an explicit bit-complexity bound, write \(\mu_r=n_r/d_r\), and take \(D=\prod_r d_r\). Its bit length is \(\mathcal O(L)\). Compute integer weights \(N_r=n_r(D/d_r)\); their sum is \(D\).

Block masses and group masses can then be represented by integer sums. Median selection compares twice a cumulative weight with \(D\), and the elimination step compares two group weights. Every integer involved has polynomial bit length. No population replication is performed.

The algorithm is deterministic, runs in polynomial time in \((m,\tau,L)\), and outputs a nonempty set of at most two candidates containing an optimum in every compatible realization.

These imply both median inequalities stated in the proof. Coincident atoms do not invalidate either inequality.

Every type in \(B_b\) ranks \(t\) first within \(A\). Consequently, compatibility at the selected atom gives
\[ |y-a_t|\le |y-a_c| \qquad(c\in A). \]
