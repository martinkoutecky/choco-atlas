The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational mass distribution \(\mu\), complete values \(a_{tq}\) over a finite good catalogue \(Q\), exact MIV predictions \(p_t\), and a finite adaptive stream of per-capita batches \((q_r,\nu_r)\), compute or decide feasibility of a nonanticipating policy that visibly transfers mass \(y_{t,B}\) between integral bundle states \(B\) and \(B\cup\{q_r\}\). At the end of every allowed stream, every positive-mass state must satisfy \(a_t(B)+\max_{g\in G\setminus B}a_t(g)\ge\alpha V_t(G)\), where \(V_t(G)=\sum_r\nu_ra_{tq_r}\); maximize \(\alpha\) or determine whether a prescribed \(\alpha\) is achievable.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Initially the policy knows a catalogue \(Q\), with \(m=|Q|\ge1\), complete nonnegative rational values \(a_{tq}\), and rational predictions \(p_t\ge0\). Bundle values are additive: \(a_t(B)=\sum_{q\in B}a_{tq}\).
An adaptive adversary reveals batches \((q_r,\nu_r)\), with distinct \(q_r\in Q\) and binary rational \(0<\nu_r\le1\), and may terminate after any nonempty finite stream consistent with \(p_t=\max_r a_{tq_r}\) for every type. It observes all previous transfers; future batches and the stopping time are unknown.
Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) holding the integral bundle \(B\); initially \(x_{t,\varnothing}=\mu_t\). Upon a batch, a deterministic policy chooses rational transfers \(0\le y_{t,B}\le x_{t,B}\) satisfying \(\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}=\nu_r\), moving each such mass from \(B\) to \(B\cup\{q_r\}\) immediately and irrevocably.
For the realized set \(G=\{q_r\}_r\), define \(V_t(G)=\sum_r\nu_r a_{tq_r}\). The policy guarantees \(\alpha\)-PROP1 if, at every allowed termination, every state with \(x_{t,B}>0\) satisfies \(a_t(B)+\max_{g\in G\setminus B}a_{tg}\ge\alpha V_t(G)\); the empty maximum is zero.
What is the optimal worst-case factor \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) achievable by nonanticipating policies in this model? In particular, can \(19/30\)-PROP1 be guaranteed by a policy explicitly outputting its nonzero transfers in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is that guarantee impossible, where \(L\) is the total binary encoding length of the initial data and realized batches?
For discrete agents, exact MIV predictions permit deterministic \(19/30\)-PROP1, improved to \(\max\{19/30,n/(n+\kappa)\}\) when \(2\le\kappa\le n\) bounds the positive-valuing agents per good, without a known horizon ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05310v2)).
A separate discrete result bounds every deterministic exact-MIV guarantee strictly below one even for two agents ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13650v1)). No result settling this precise continuous mass-state model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so the batch-weighted total value is the per-capita proportional benchmark.
  • Made values and predictions nonnegative binary rationals; allowed zero predictions and defined the empty maximum as zero.
  • Interpreted catalogue entries as distinct good identities, each arriving at most once, preserving the stated set-valued bundle transitions; identical valuation vectors may have distinct identities.
  • Restricted each batch mass to the interval (0,1], so every individual can receive at most one good from that batch.
  • Defined allowed streams as all nonempty adaptive streams whose realized maxima equal the announced predictions, with no advance stream or adversary tree supplied.
  • Specified complete allocation through mass conservation, rational transfers, empty initial bundles, and explicit output of nonzero transfers.
  • Used the approximation target dictated by the anchor's factor guarantee rather than exact policy feasibility.

Result

The optimal factor is \(\alpha^*=1\). A deterministic nonanticipating policy guarantees exact PROP1 at every prefix and explicitly outputs its nonzero transfers in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Consequently, it guarantees \(19/30\)-PROP1.

The policy does not need the predictions. Its essential feature is that each batch can be split across every supported type.

Policy: allocate within each type to the lowest-value bundles

Fix deterministic orders on the types and on bundle bit vectors for tie-breaking.

When batch \((q_r,\nu_r)\) arrives, process each type \(t\) independently:

  • Set its remaining transfer quota to \(R_t=\mu_t\nu_r\).
  • Sort its currently supported bundles \(B\) by increasing \(a_t(B)\), using the fixed tie-breaking order.
  • In that order, set \(y_{t,B}=\min\{x_{t,B},R_t\}\) and decrease \(R_t\) by \(y_{t,B}\).
  • Transfer the selected mass from \(B\) to \(B\cup\{q_r\}\).

All quantities are rational. Since \(0<\nu_r\le1\) and the total mass of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t\), its quota can always be filled. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}=\sum_t\mu_t\nu_r=\nu_r.\)

Thus every batch is allocated feasibly. Distinctness of the arriving goods ensures that no source bundle already contains \(q_r\). The policy uses only the current state and current batch, so it is nonanticipating and immediately irrevocable.

The sorting rule has the following property:

Selection property. If a positive amount is selected from bundle \(C\), and a positive amount remains unselected in bundle \(B\), then \(a_t(C)\le a_t(B)\).

Indeed, the policy exhausts lower-valued states before selecting from higher-valued states. At most one state is partially selected, and its selected and unselected portions have equal initial utility.

Proven invariant

Let \(G_r=\{q_1,\ldots,q_r\}\). For a supported bundle \(B\), define

\(\displaystyle h_{t,r}(B)=\max_{g\in G_r\setminus B}a_{tg},\)

with the empty maximum equal to zero.

We prove that, after every prefix, every pair of supported bundles \(B,C\) of the same type satisfies

\(\displaystyle a_t(C)-a_t(B)\le h_{t,r}(B). \qquad (1)\)

Thus the value of any other bundle of that type is at most the value of \(B\), augmented by its most valuable missing good.

Base case

Initially the only supported bundle of each type is \(\varnothing\). Both sides of (1) are zero.

Inductive step

Assume (1) holds before the current batch \((q,\nu)\). Fix a type \(t\), and write \(a=a_{tq}\ge0\).

Every supported bundle after the transfer originates from a previously supported bundle, either as an unselected portion or as a selected portion receiving \(q\). Consider two such resulting bundles, originating from \(B\) and \(C\).

There are four possibilities.

Both portions receive \(q\). Their utility difference remains \(a_t(C)-a_t(B)\). Since \(q\) belongs to the new first bundle, its missing-good maximum remains \(h_{\mathrm{old}}(B)\). The induction hypothesis therefore proves (1).

Neither portion receives \(q\). Their utility difference again remains unchanged. The first bundle’s missing-good maximum becomes \(\max\{h_{\mathrm{old}}(B),a\}\), which is at least its previous maximum. Hence (1) continues to hold.

The portion originating from \(B\) receives \(q\), while the portion originating from \(C\) does not. Their new utility difference is

\(\displaystyle a_t(C)-a_t(B)-a\le a_t(C)-a_t(B)\le h_{\mathrm{old}}(B).\)

The first bundle’s missing-good maximum remains \(h_{\mathrm{old}}(B)\), so (1) holds.

The portion originating from \(B\) does not receive \(q\), while the portion originating from \(C\) does. The selection property gives \(a_t(C)\le a_t(B)\). Therefore their new utility difference satisfies

\(\displaystyle a_t(C)+a-a_t(B)\le a.\)

Because \(q\) is missing from the first resulting bundle, its missing-good maximum is at least \(a\). This proves (1) in the last case.

These cases exhaust all pairs of supported resulting bundles, completing the induction.

Exact PROP1 follows by averaging

Define the total utility held by type \(t\) after prefix \(r\) as

\(\displaystyle U_t^r=\sum_B x_{t,B}^r a_t(B).\)

Every batch preserves the total mass \(\mu_t\). In batch \(r\), precisely \(\mu_t\nu_r\) of that type’s mass receives \(q_r\). Additivity therefore gives

\(\displaystyle U_t^r-U_t^{r-1}=\mu_t\nu_r a_{tq_r}.\)

Since initial utility is zero,

\(\displaystyle U_t^r=\mu_t\sum_{j=1}^r\nu_j a_{tq_j}=\mu_t V_t(G_r). \qquad (2)\)

Now fix any supported bundle \(B\) of type \(t\). By invariant (1), every supported bundle \(C\) of that type satisfies

\(\displaystyle a_t(C)\le a_t(B)+h_{t,r}(B).\)

Multiply by \(x_{t,C}^r\) and sum over \(C\). Using mass conservation and (2), we obtain

\(\displaystyle \mu_t V_t(G_r)=U_t^r\le\mu_t\bigl(a_t(B)+h_{t,r}(B)\bigr).\)

Every supported type has \(\mu_t>0\), so division yields

\(\displaystyle a_t(B)+\max_{g\in G_r\setminus B}a_{tg}\ge V_t(G_r).\)

This is exact PROP1 for every positive-mass state.

The argument holds at every prefix, including prefixes satisfying the prediction-consistency condition at which the adversary may terminate. It also applies to adaptive adversaries: every induction step holds for every next batch, independently of how that batch was chosen.

Polynomial-time explicit transfers

Number of supported states

Within one type, a fully selected state is replaced by one state, and an unselected state remains one state. Only a partially selected state produces two states. There is at most one partially selected state per type per batch.

Consequently, after \(r\) batches each type has at most \(r+1\) supported states. Before batch \(r\), it has at most \(r\), so that batch outputs at most \(\tau r\) nonzero transfers. Over the entire stream, the number is at most

\(\displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^m\tau r=\frac{\tau m(m+1)}2.\)

Bundles can be represented explicitly by bit vectors of length \(m\).

Encoding lengths

Write each realized batch mass as \(\nu_j=c_j/d_j\), with integer numerator and positive denominator, and let \(D_r=\prod_{j=1}^r d_j\).

For a fixed type, normalize its state masses and transfers by dividing by \(\mu_t\). Initially the normalized mass is one. The algorithm uses only comparisons, minima, and subtraction, with normalized quota \(\nu_r\). Inductively, every normalized mass and transfer after batch \(r\) is an integer multiple of \(1/D_r\).

These quantities lie in \([0,1]\), so their numerators over denominator \(D_r\) are bounded by \(D_r\). Their encoding lengths are therefore polynomial in \(L\). Multiplying by \(\mu_t\) preserves that bound.

Likewise, bundle utilities are sums of at most \(m\) input rationals. A common denominator is the product of the corresponding input denominators, whose binary length is bounded by the input encoding length. Utility encodings are therefore polynomial in \(L+\log m\).

Running time

Store each supported bundle together with its exact utility and mass. Creating a receiving bundle adds the current value \(a_{tq_r}\) to its stored utility.

Sorting and processing the state lists requires \(O(\tau m^2\log(m+1))\) comparisons and rational arithmetic operations overall. Each operates on numbers of polynomial encoding length. Explicit bundle bookkeeping and transfer output also take polynomial time.

Hence the policy meets the requested polynomial-time bound in \(m,\tau,L\).

Optimality and the discrete comparison

The requested factor belongs to \([0,1]\), and the policy achieves its upper endpoint. Thus the optimal factor is exactly \(1\).

Even if larger factors were considered, a single type, a single good of value \(1\), and a batch of mass \(1\) would force \(\alpha\le1\): every individual receives that good, giving utility and benchmark both equal to \(1\).

The cited discrete impossibility concerns allocating each arriving good to one indivisible agent. Here the policy gives every type exactly a \(\nu_r\) fraction of its mass in each batch; this freedom makes the typewise averaging identity possible. The discrete upper bound therefore has different allocation hypotheses. [Discrete model and upper bound](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13650v1)

1. Feasibility and nonanticipation

For each supported type \(t\), the policy assigns transfer quota \(\mu_t\nu_r\). Since \(0<\nu_r\le1\), this quota lies between zero and the available type mass \(\mu_t\). Sequentially taking the minimum of the remaining quota and each available state mass therefore fills the quota exactly, while respecting every constraint \(0\le y_{t,B}\le x_{t,B}\).

Summing the quotas over types gives
\[ \sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}=\nu_r\sum_t\mu_t=\nu_r. \]
Thus the batch constraint is satisfied.

Distinct arrivals ensure that no current bundle contains the arriving good. Consequently, selected mass receives exactly one new good, and its utility increases by exactly that good’s value.

The fixed ordering resolves all ties deterministically. Decisions depend only on the current state and batch, so the policy is nonanticipating. Transfers are immediate and irreversible as required.

2. Selection property

The stated selection property follows from processing bundles in increasing utility order.

If positive mass is selected from \(C\) while positive mass remains unselected in \(B\), then \(C\) cannot have strictly higher utility than \(B\): the algorithm would have exhausted \(B\) before reaching \(C\). If both portions originate from the same partially selected state, their initial utilities agree.

The assertion that at most one state is partially selected is also correct. Once a state consumes the remaining quota without being exhausted, the remaining quota becomes zero.

3. Pairwise invariant

The invariant quantifies over every ordered pair of positive-mass states of the same type. This is the appropriate scope for the subsequent averaging argument.

The base case is valid: initially each type has only the empty bundle, and the missing-good maximum is zero.

For the induction, every resulting state has a previously supported source. Because the arriving good is absent from every source bundle, its effect on both utility and the missing-good maximum is exactly as described. All four cases are valid:

  • Both portions receive the good. Both utilities increase by the same amount. The first bundle’s missing-good maximum is unchanged.
  • Neither receives the good. The utility difference is unchanged. The first bundle’s missing-good maximum can only increase.
  • Only the first receives the good. The utility difference decreases by the nonnegative arriving value. Its missing-good maximum is unchanged.
  • Only the second receives the good. The selection property bounds the second source’s utility by the first source’s utility. Hence the resulting difference is at most the arriving value, which is included among the first bundle’s missing goods.

These cases cover every ordered pair, including the two portions of a partially selected state. Zero values, equal utilities, and empty missing-good sets introduce no exception.

4. Averaging establishes exact PROP1

The utility accounting identity is correct. Exactly \(\mu_t\nu_r\) mass of type \(t\) receives the arriving good, so additivity yields
\[ U_t^r-U_t^{r-1}=\mu_t\nu_r a_{tq_r}. \]
Summing these increments gives \(U_t^r=\mu_t V_t(G_r)\).

For a fixed supported bundle \(B\), the invariant bounds the utility of every supported \(C\) by the same quantity \(a_t(B)+h_{t,r}(B)\). Multiplication by the nonnegative masses and summation therefore preserve the inequality. Mass conservation supplies the factor \(\mu_t\) on the right.

Division by \(\mu_t\) is justified because the input lists only types with positive mass. The resulting inequality is precisely the required exact PROP1 condition for every positive-mass state.

The proof holds at every prefix, so it covers every permitted prediction-consistent termination. Its induction is valid for every next batch, which also covers adaptive choices and stopping times. Predictions need not be used.
