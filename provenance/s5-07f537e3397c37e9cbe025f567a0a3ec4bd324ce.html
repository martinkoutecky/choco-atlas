The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(q\) and an explicitly listed set of types \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \{-1,1\}^q\), decide whether every rational probability distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal{T}\) with \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{T}} \mu_x x_j \ne 0\) for every issue \(j\) has its strict issue-wise majority vector \(M(\mu)_j = \operatorname{sgn}(\sum_{x \in \mathcal{T}} \mu_x x_j)\) in \(\mathcal{T}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m=q\geq 1\) be the number of binary issues, and let \(\varnothing\ne\mathcal T\subseteq\{-1,1\}^{m}\) be an explicitly listed domain of \(s=|\mathcal T|\) distinct types; constant coordinates are allowed.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\).
For a rational probability distribution \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal T\), zero masses are allowed. It is admissible precisely when \(\sum_{x\in\mathcal T}\mu_x x_j\ne0\) for every issue \(j\); its majority vector is \(M(\mu)_j=\operatorname{sgn}(\sum_{x\in\mathcal T}\mu_x x_j)\). There is no tie-breaking.
The decision input is only \(m\) and \(\mathcal T\). Decide whether \(M(\mu)\in\mathcal T\) for every admissible rational distribution \(\mu\), with no bound on mass denominators. Let \(L\) denote the total binary encoding length of the relevant input; state running times in \(m,s,L\), and additionally in \(\tau\) when processing an individual society.

Is this universal majority-stability decision problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies of every odd size at least \(3\), majority stability is equivalent to closure under issue-wise majority of all triples of domain elements, allowing repetition. Consequently, the explicitly listed discrete-domain recognition problem has a polynomial-time algorithm.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required a nonempty domain and at least one issue, so probability distributions exist and the issue dimension follows the discrete model.
  • Allowed zero masses on listed domain types: the domain contains permitted types, while an individual society's support consists only of its positive-mass types.
  • Retained constant coordinates, which the discrete domain definition explicitly permits.
  • Distinguished domain size s from society support size tau; the decision input contains the domain but no particular distribution, and universally quantified mass denominators are unbounded.
  • Excluded every distribution with an issue tie and supplied no tie-breaking, as required by the graded strict-majority question.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. More precisely, the following conditions are equivalent:

  • Every admissible rational distribution on \(\mathcal T\) has its majority vector in \(\mathcal T\).
  • The domain \(\mathcal T\) is closed under coordinate-wise majority of triples, allowing repetition.

The equivalence holds even if arbitrary real probabilities are allowed. Zero masses, constant coordinates, and unbounded rational denominators do not change the characterization.

1. A structural lemma

For \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), define the projection
\[ \mathcal T|_I=\{x|_I:x\in\mathcal T\}. \]
Write \(\operatorname{maj}(x,y,z)\) for the vector whose coordinate \(j\) is the majority of \(x_j,y_j,z_j\).

Lemma. Suppose that \(\mathcal T\) is nonempty and closed under \(\operatorname{maj}\). For every \(v\notin\mathcal T\), there is a nonempty coordinate set \(I\), with \(|I|\leq 2\), such that \(v|_I\notin\mathcal T|_I\).

Proof. Since \(v\notin\mathcal T\), the full coordinate set has the required exclusion property. Choose an inclusion-minimal set \(I\) with that property. Nonemptiness of \(\mathcal T\) implies that \(I\neq\varnothing\).

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \(|I|\geq 3\). Choose distinct coordinates \(i_1,i_2,i_3\in I\). By minimality, for each \(r\in\{1,2,3\}\) there exists \(x^{(r)}\in\mathcal T\) satisfying
\[ x^{(r)}|_{I\setminus\{i_r\}}=v|_{I\setminus\{i_r\}}. \]
Let \(z=\operatorname{maj}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)},x^{(3)})\). Closure gives \(z\in\mathcal T\).

For every \(j\in I\), at most one of these three vectors can disagree with \(v\) at coordinate \(j\). Consequently, \(z_j=v_j\) for every \(j\in I\). This contradicts \(v|_I\notin\mathcal T|_I\). Hence \(|I|\leq 2\). \(\square\)

Thus, a triple-majority-closed domain is completely determined by its permitted patterns on single coordinates and pairs of coordinates.

2. Equivalence with weighted majority stability

Universal stability implies triple closure

Take any \(x,y,z\in\mathcal T\), allowing repetition. Define a rational distribution by assigning each type its number of occurrences in this triple divided by \(3\).

For every coordinate \(j\), its expectation is
\[ \frac{x_j+y_j+z_j}{3}. \]
The numerator belongs to \(\{-3,-1,1,3\}\), so this distribution is admissible. Its majority vector is exactly \(\operatorname{maj}(x,y,z)\).

Universal stability therefore implies triple closure.

Triple closure implies universal stability

Suppose that \(\mathcal T\) is triple-majority closed. Let \(\mu\) be any admissible distribution on \(\mathcal T\), and put \(v=M(\mu)\).

For any coordinate \(j\) and sign \(a\in\{-1,1\}\), define
\[ p_j(a)=\sum_{\substack{x\in\mathcal T\\x_j=a}}\mu_x. \]
Since
\[ a\sum_{x\in\mathcal T}\mu_xx_j=2p_j(a)-1, \]
the equality \(v_j=a\) implies \(p_j(a)>\tfrac12\).

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \(v\notin\mathcal T\). Apply the lemma.

If the excluded pattern is on one coordinate \(i\), no type in \(\mathcal T\) has \(x_i=v_i\). Hence \(p_i(v_i)=0\), contradicting \(p_i(v_i)>\tfrac12\).

Otherwise, the excluded pattern is on two distinct coordinates \(i,j\). The events
\[ A=\{x\in\mathcal T:x_i=v_i\}, \qquad B=\{x\in\mathcal T:x_j=v_j\} \]
are disjoint, because their intersection would realize the excluded pattern. Therefore,
\[ \mu(A)+\mu(B)=\mu(A\cup B)\leq 1. \]
But both probabilities exceed \(\tfrac12\), again a contradiction.

Thus \(M(\mu)\in\mathcal T\). This proof uses only nonnegative masses summing to \(1\), so it applies to real distributions as well as rational ones. \(\square\)

3. Recognition algorithm and complexity

The decision algorithm is:

  • Enumerate all ordered triples in \(\mathcal T^3\).
  • Compute their coordinate-wise majority vectors.
  • Reject if any resulting vector is absent from \(\mathcal T\); otherwise accept.

The preceding equivalence proves correctness.

There are \(s^3\) triples. Computing one majority vector costs \(O(m)\) time, and testing membership by scanning the explicitly listed domain costs \(O(ms)\). A straightforward implementation therefore runs in
\[ O(L+ms^4) \]
time, which is polynomial in the input length.

On a standard word RAM, storing the domain in a binary trie gives \(O(m)\) membership queries and yields
\[ O(L+ms^3) \]
word operations, with words of \(O(\log(L+2))\) bits.

If the algorithm rejects, the offending triple directly supplies an admissible rational counterexample with support size at most \(3\). In fact, its three types must be distinct: a triple with repetition has its repeated type as its majority. Thus every failure has a witness consisting of three distinct types, each with mass \(\tfrac13\).

4. Processing an individual society

Here let \(L\) denote the total encoding length of the domain and the supplied society. Write its positive masses as
\[ \mu_{x^{(r)}}=\frac{a_r}{b_r}, \qquad r\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}, \]
where the numerators and denominators are positive binary integers.

Set
\[ D=\prod_{r=1}^{\tau}b_r, \qquad w_r=a_r\frac{D}{b_r}. \]
The integer \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Since the masses sum to \(1\),
\[ \sum_{r=1}^{\tau}w_r=D. \]

For each issue, compute the exact signed integer
\[ S_j=\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}w_rx^{(r)}_j. \]
Its expectation is \(S_j/D\). Thus the society is admissible exactly when every \(S_j\neq 0\); when admissible, its majority coordinate is \(\operatorname{sgn}(S_j)\).

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, constructing the weights costs \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations. Computing all issue sums costs \(O(m\tau L)\), because every intermediate sum has \(O(L)\) bits. A linear membership check costs \(O(ms)\). Including input reading, the total bound is
\[ O\!\left(L+\tau L^2+m\tau L+ms\right). \]

These computations depend on denominator encoding lengths, not denominator magnitudes. No denominator bound is needed.

Conclusion

Triple-majority closure characterizes stability for the weighted societies in the question, just as it characterizes stability for all odd finite societies. Consequently, the explicitly listed universal majority-stability problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\).
