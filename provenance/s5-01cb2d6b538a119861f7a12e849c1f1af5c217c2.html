The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 8. — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite house set \(H\), acceptable ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(c_h\), a rational bound \(b\), and an increase-only or signed-change mode, decide whether there are rational changes \(\delta_h\) and an allocation \(x_{t,h}\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\), zero allocation to unacceptable houses, and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le c_h+\delta_h\), such that the allocation is perfect and popular. Require \(\delta_h\ge 0\) in increase-only mode, \(c_h+\delta_h\ge 0\), and \(|\delta_h|\le b\). Popularity means that every feasible alternative reallocation of the same type mass, possibly leaving some unmatched, has aggregate incumbent vote at least zero, with each unit of mass voting according to its type's ranking and unmatched ranked last.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a nonempty house set, \(m=|H|\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type strictly ranks a nonempty acceptable set \(A_t\subseteq H\); houses have no preferences and do not vote.
The input also contains binary rational initial capacities \(c_h>0\), a binary rational bound \(b>0\), and an increase-only or signed-change mode. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Capacities and changes are measured per unit of total society mass.
Seek rational changes \(\delta_h\) with \(|\delta_h|\le b\) and \(C_h=c_h+\delta_h\ge0\), requiring \(\delta_h\ge0\) in increase-only mode, and rational allocations \(x_{t,h}\ge0\) satisfying \(x_{t,h}=0\) for \(h\notin A_t\), \(\sum_hx_{t,h}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_tx_{t,h}\le C_h\). Such an allocation is perfect.
To define popularity, let \(\bot\) denote unmatched, ranked below every acceptable house, and put \(B_t=A_t\cup\{\bot\}\). An alternative reallocation is any nonnegative real array \(z_{t,h,k}\), indexed by \(h\in A_t\) and \(k\in B_t\), with \(\sum_{k\in B_t}z_{t,h,k}=x_{t,h}\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{h\in A_t}z_{t,h,k}\le C_k\) for every \(k\in H\); terms with \(k\notin B_t\) are zero, and unmatched mass has no capacity constraint.
Here \(z_{t,h,k}\) is the mass currently at \(h\) moved to \(k\). Define \(v_t(h,k)\) to be \(1\) if \(h\succ_t k\), \(-1\) if \(k\succ_t h\), and \(0\) if \(h=k\). The incumbent allocation is popular if \(\sum_t\sum_{h\in A_t}\sum_{k\in B_t}z_{t,h,k}v_t(h,k)\ge0\) for every alternative reallocation, under the same modified capacities.

For each mode, is deciding whether such capacity changes and a perfect popular allocation exist solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

The discrete problem with unit-capacity applicants and integer house capacities is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete even with maximum increase \(2\) in increase-only mode, or maximum absolute change \(1\) in signed-change mode. [Csáji](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00598v1)
For fixed integer capacities, popular mixed matchings can be computed in polynomial time using expected votes between independent lotteries over matchings; this uses a different comparison from the reallocations defined above. [Kavitha, Mestre and Nasre](https://www.columbia.edu/~cs2035/courses/ieor8100.F18/popular.pdf)
No result settling the specified continuous capacity-change problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified strict rankings, acceptable houses above unmatched, and no house votes, following the discrete model.
  • Required nonempty acceptable sets, consistent with the intended model's use of every applicant's first-choice house.
  • Required positive initial capacities and a positive bound, following the discrete positive-integer input domains; modified capacities may be zero as allowed by the graded question.
  • Specified nonnegative rational allocation entries and capacities measured relative to total mass one, without imposing an upper bound on capacities.
  • Resolved the ambiguity in comparing split types by tracking mass from each incumbent house to each alternative destination and quantifying over every such reallocation.
  • Specified that alternatives use the same modified capacities, may leave mass unmatched, and may use real masses.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. Both increase-only mode and signed-change mode are \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard. The reduction below allows arbitrary fractional allocations and capacity changes in its converse, so it does not rely on integrality.

1. A sufficient condition for popularity. Call a type’s most preferred acceptable house its *first choice*.

Suppose a perfect allocation has a set \(R\) of houses satisfying:

  • Every house in \(R\) is full and all its occupants regard it as their first choice.
  • Whenever a currently allocated type can improve by moving, its destination belongs to \(R\).

Then the allocation is popular under the reallocation comparison in the question.

To prove this, fix any feasible reallocation \(z\). For \(k\in R\), let \(G_k\) be the improving mass entering \(k\), and let \(D_k\) be the mass leaving \(k\) for a different destination, including \(\bot\). The capacity constraint gives

\(\displaystyle G_k\le C_k-\sum_t z_{t,k,k} =\sum_t x_{t,k}-\sum_t z_{t,k,k} =D_k. \)

The equality uses fullness. Every unit counted by \(D_k\) becomes strictly worse off because it leaves its first choice. By condition 2, all improving mass enters \(R\). Consequently, if \(G\) and \(D\) denote total improving and worsening mass, respectively,

\(\displaystyle G=\sum_{k\in R}G_k\le\sum_{k\in R}D_k\le D. \)

The incumbent’s vote margin is \(D-G\), which is nonnegative. This proves the condition for arbitrary real reallocations.

2. Reduction and normalization. We reduce from perfect three-dimensional matching. Its input consists of disjoint sets \(U,V,W\), each of cardinality \(n\ge1\), and triples \(S_1,\ldots,S_r\), each containing one element from each set. The question is whether \(n\) pairwise disjoint triples cover \(E=U\cup V\cup W\). This is a standard \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard problem; the restricted version recalled in [Csáji, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00598v1) already suffices to establish hardness of this unrestricted version.

The construction modifies the set and collector gadgets in [Csáji’s discrete reduction](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.00598v1). The essential change is to give each element type mass \(3\); the proof below shows why this prevents fractional choices from bypassing exact cover.

We first describe unnormalized masses and capacities. Write each triple as \(S_j=\{e_{j,1},e_{j,2},e_{j,3}\}\). Create an element house \(e\) for every \(e\in E\), three houses \(t_j,p_j,q_j\) for every triple, and a collector house \(w\).

The types are:

| Type | Unnormalized mass | Strict preference list |
|---|---:|---|
| \(d_e\), for each \(e\in E\) | \(1\) | \(e\) |
| \(s_{j,\ell}\), for each \(j\) and \(\ell\in\{1,2,3\}\) | \(3\) | \(e_{j,\ell}\succ p_j\succ t_j\) |
| \(a_j\), for each \(j\) | \(1\) | \(q_j\succ p_j\succ w\) |
| \(d^p_j\), for each \(j\) | \(1\) | \(p_j\) |
| \(d^q_j\), for each \(j\) | \(2\) | \(q_j\) |
| \(d^w\) | \(n+2\) | \(w\) |

The initial capacities are:

| House | Unnormalized initial capacity |
|---|---:|
| \(e\), for each \(e\in E\) | \(3\) |
| \(t_j\) | \(9\) |
| \(p_j\) | \(1\) |
| \(q_j\) | \(1\) |
| \(w\) | \(r+1\) |

Set the unnormalized change bound to \(1\).

The total type mass is

\(\displaystyle N=3n+13r+(n+2)=4n+13r+2.\)

Divide every mass, initial capacity, and the change bound by \(N\). This produces a valid input with total mass \(1\), strictly positive initial capacities, and \(b=1/N\). Every acceptable set is nonempty and has at most three houses.

Multiplication by \(N\) bijectively converts feasible allocations, changes, and reallocations between the normalized and unnormalized instances. It multiplies every vote margin by \(N>0\), preserving popularity. We therefore work in unnormalized units below.

3. A perfect matching yields a perfect popular allocation. Suppose \(J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\) indexes an exact cover, so \(|J|=n\).

Choose modified capacities as follows:

  • Every element house has capacity \(4\).
  • Every \(q_j\) has capacity \(2\).
  • For \(j\in J\), \(p_j\) has capacity \(2\); otherwise it retains capacity \(1\).
  • Every \(t_j\) retains capacity \(9\).
  • The collector \(w\) has capacity \(r+2\).

Every change is either \(0\) or \(1\), so these choices are permitted in both modes.

Assign every single-house type to its only house. For \(j\in J\), assign each \(s_{j,\ell}\), of mass \(3\), to \(e_{j,\ell}\), and assign \(a_j\) to \(p_j\). For \(j\notin J\), assign all three \(s_{j,\ell}\) to \(t_j\), and assign \(a_j\) to \(w\).

This allocation is perfect and feasible:

  • Each element house receives its compulsory mass \(1\) and mass \(3\) from its unique covering triple.
  • Each selected \(p_j\) receives its compulsory mass \(1\) and \(a_j\)’s mass \(1\).
  • Each unselected \(t_j\) receives mass \(9\).
  • Each \(q_j\) receives its compulsory mass \(2\).
  • The collector receives mass \((n+2)+(r-n)=r+2\).

For popularity, take \(R\) to consist of all element houses, all \(q_j\), and the unselected \(p_j\). Every house in \(R\) is full with first-choice occupants.

The only types that can improve are:

  • An unselected \(s_{j,\ell}\), currently at \(t_j\), which can improve to \(e_{j,\ell}\) or its unselected \(p_j\).
  • A selected \(a_j\), currently at \(p_j\), which can improve only to \(q_j\).
  • An unselected \(a_j\), currently at \(w\), which can improve to \(q_j\) or its unselected \(p_j\).

Every such destination belongs to \(R\). The sufficient condition in Section 1 proves popularity against every permitted real reallocation.

4. Any perfect popular allocation yields an exact cover. Now suppose arbitrary permitted capacity changes admit a perfect popular allocation. This argument applies to signed-change mode and therefore also to increase-only mode.

Let \(X_{t,h}\) denote its unnormalized allocated masses, and \(K_h\) its modified capacities. The bound on changes implies

\(\displaystyle K_e\le4,\qquad K_{p_j}\le2,\qquad K_{q_j}\le2,\qquad K_w\le r+2. \)

Perfectness forces every single-house type to remain entirely at its only acceptable house. In particular:

  • Each element house has compulsory mass \(1\).
  • Each \(p_j\) has compulsory mass \(1\).
  • Each \(q_j\) has compulsory mass \(2\).
  • The collector has compulsory mass \(n+2\).

Thus \(K_{q_j}=2\), and \(a_j\) has no mass at \(q_j\). Define

\(\displaystyle \alpha_j=X_{a_j,p_j},\qquad J=\{j:\alpha_j>0\}.\)

Since \(a_j\) has mass \(1\), we have \(0\le\alpha_j\le1\), and its remaining mass \(1-\alpha_j\) is at \(w\).

The collector’s capacity gives

\(\displaystyle n+2+\sum_{j=1}^r(1-\alpha_j)\le r+2, \)

hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^r\alpha_j\ge n.\)

Because each \(\alpha_j\le1\), it follows that \(|J|\ge n\).

We next prove that the triples indexed by \(J\) are pairwise disjoint.

Fix \(j\in J\). No mass of any \(s_{j,\ell}\) can be at \(t_j\). Otherwise choose a positive \(\varepsilon\) no greater than that mass and \(\alpha_j\), and small enough to take \(\varepsilon\) from \(d^q_j\)’s mass \(2\). Reallocate:

  • \(\varepsilon\) of \(s_{j,\ell}\) from \(t_j\) to \(p_j\).
  • \(\varepsilon\) of \(a_j\) from \(p_j\) to \(q_j\).
  • \(\varepsilon\) of \(d^q_j\) from \(q_j\) to \(\bot\).

Leave all other mass in place. Loads at \(p_j\) and \(q_j\) are unchanged; the load at \(t_j\) decreases. Thus this is a feasible reallocation. Its incumbent vote margin is

\(\displaystyle -\varepsilon-\varepsilon+\varepsilon=-\varepsilon<0,\)

contradicting popularity.

Therefore each \(s_{j,\ell}\) splits its entire mass \(3\) between \(e_{j,\ell}\) and \(p_j\). The compulsory mass \(1\) at \(p_j\), together with \(K_{p_j}\le2\), implies

\(\displaystyle \alpha_j+\sum_{\ell=1}^3X_{s_{j,\ell},p_j}\le1. \)

For every \(\ell\), consequently,

\(\displaystyle X_{s_{j,\ell},e_{j,\ell}} =3-X_{s_{j,\ell},p_j} \ge3-(1-\alpha_j) =2+\alpha_j>2. \)

If two distinct triples indexed by \(J\) shared an element \(e\), their corresponding element types would contribute more than \(4\) units to \(e\). Together with its compulsory mass \(1\), this would exceed \(K_e\le4\). Hence those triples are pairwise disjoint.

There are only \(3n\) elements, and each triple contains three elements, so \(|J|\le n\). Combining this with \(|J|\ge n\), we obtain exactly \(n\) pairwise disjoint triples. Their union is all of \(E\), giving a perfect three-dimensional matching.

5. Complexity conclusion. The normalized instance has

\(\displaystyle m=3n+3r+1,\qquad \tau=3n+6r+1.\)

All numerators and denominators have bit length \(O(\log(n+r))\), and the preference lists have length at most three. The construction is therefore polynomial in the source input size.

An exact cover provides rational witnesses using only increases. Conversely, even arbitrary real allocations and signed changes satisfying the bounds force an exact cover. Thus the same construction proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness in both modes. In particular, neither mode admits a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) unless \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).

The condition and its proof are correct.

For a house \(k\in R\), improving mass entering \(k\) is part of its total incoming mass other than mass staying at \(k\). Therefore feasibility implies
\[ G_k\le C_k-\sum_t z_{t,k,k}. \]
Fullness gives \(C_k=\sum_t x_{t,k}\), and the reallocation row constraints give
\[ \sum_t x_{t,k}-\sum_t z_{t,k,k}=D_k. \]
Sums here naturally omit nonexistent entries for types that do not accept \(k\).

Every unit leaving a house in \(R\) becomes strictly worse off: its origin is its first choice, and its destination is either another acceptable house or unmatched. Condition 2 ensures that every improving unit enters a house in \(R\). Thus
\[ G=\sum_{k\in R}G_k\le\sum_{k\in R}D_k\le D. \]

Outgoing masses from distinct houses are distinct portions of incumbent mass, so the last sum does not double-count worsening mass. Staying mass contributes zero, and strict preferences make every other move either improving or worsening. Hence the incumbent vote margin is exactly \(D-G\), as asserted.
