The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), distinct positive rational weights \(w_t\), a rational quota share \(q\) with \(0<q<\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), consider every positive integer \(K\) for which all \(K\mu_t\) are integral; form the weighted game with \(K\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\) and quota \(Kq\). Let \(B_t(K)\) and \(G_t(K)\) be that type's raw Banzhaf and Public Good scores per voter, and set \(p_t(K)=\lambda B_t(K)+(1-\lambda)G_t(K)\). Decide whether some \(K_0\) exists such that for every admissible \(K\ge K_0\) and every pair \(t,s\) with \(w_t>w_s\), \(p_t(K)\ge p_s(K)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and pairwise distinct positive binary rational weights \(w_t\). The support size \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types.
The remaining inputs are binary rationals \(q\) and \(\lambda\), with \(0<q<\sum_t\mu_t w_t\) and \(0\leq\lambda\leq1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; measure running times in \(\tau\) and \(L\), with no independent \(m\) parameter.
A positive integer \(K\) is admissible when every \(K\mu_t\) is integral. For each admissible \(K\), let \(N_K\) contain \(K\mu_t\) distinct voters of weight \(w_t\) for each type \(t\).
For \(S\subseteq N_K\), write \(W(S)\) for its total weight; it wins exactly when \(W(S)\geq Kq\), including equality. A winning coalition is minimal winning when deleting any member makes it losing.
For any fixed voter \(i\) of type \(t\), define \(B_t(K)=|\{S\subseteq N_K:i\in S,\ W(S)\geq Kq>W(S\setminus\{i\})\}|\) and \(G_t(K)=|\{S\subseteq N_K:i\in S,\ S\text{ is minimal winning}\}|\).
These scores count coalitions of distinct voters without normalization; symmetry makes them independent of the chosen voter within a type. Set \(p_t(K)=\lambda B_t(K)+(1-\lambda)G_t(K)\).
Call an input eventually locally monotonic when \(\exists K_0\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\ \forall K\geq K_0\) admissible, \(\forall t,s\), \(w_t>w_s\Rightarrow p_t(K)\geq p_s(K)\).

Is deciding eventual local monotonicity in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies, universal local monotonicity of a specified convex combination over all weighted games with a fixed number of voters is decidable using integer linear programming; no polynomial running-time bound is asserted ([Freixas–Kurz](https://arxiv.org/html/1411.0944)).
No result settling this exact eventual replication decision problem was located in the literature search.

Partial resolution: arithmetic restrictions and a higher-order critical test

I do not establish either membership in $\mathrm{P}$ or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness for the full decision problem.

The new results proved here are:

  • For a genuine mixture, a rational critical quota forces the exponential tilt parameter to be rational.
  • Consequently, critical equality has a necessary condition that can be checked in polynomial time in $\tau$ and $L$. In particular, the smallest normalized integer weight must have numerical value $O(L)$.
  • At a critical quota, the second-order test only needs the zero ceiling phase.
  • An explicit next-order coefficient resolves some cases where the second-order coefficient vanishes.
  • A fully dyadic input satisfies eventual local monotonicity despite this second-order cancellation.

All asymptotic statements below concern a fixed input. They do not imply uniform polynomial running-time bounds.

1. Normalization and exact counting identities

Choose a positive rational $c$ and positive integers
$$ a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_\tau, \qquad \gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_\tau)=1, $$
such that $w_t=ca_t$. Put
$$ r=\frac qc, \qquad A=\sum_t\mu_ta_t. $$
Then $0<r<A$.

Such a normalization can be computed in polynomial time: clear the denominators of the weights and divide the resulting integers by their greatest common divisor. All resulting numbers have bit lengths polynomial in $L$.

Normalized coalition weights are integers. Thus the winning condition is exactly
$$ W(S)\geq T_K, \qquad T_K=\lceil Kr\rceil. $$
Write
$$ \delta_K=T_K-Kr\in[0,1). $$

Fix types $i>j$ and distinguished voters of those types. Let $R$ be the society with these two voters removed, and define
$$ C_h(K)= \#\left\{ U\subseteq R: T_K-a_i+h\leq W(U)<T_K-a_j+h \right\}. $$

Banzhaf difference

Partitioning pivotal coalitions according to whether they contain the other distinguished voter gives
$$ \begin{aligned} B_i(K) ={}&\#\{U\subseteq R:T_K-a_i\leq W(U)<T_K\}\\ &+\#\{U\subseteq R:T_K-a_i-a_j\leq W(U)<T_K-a_j\}. \end{aligned} $$
The formula for $B_j(K)$ exchanges $i$ and $j$. Subtraction, with cancellation of overlapping intervals, yields
$$ B_i(K)-B_j(K)=2C_0(K). $$
In particular, $\lambda=1$ gives local monotonicity for every admissible replication. If $\tau=1$, the property is vacuous.

Minimal-winning difference

Every coalition satisfying
$$ T_K\leq W(S)<T_K+a_1 $$
is minimal winning: deleting a member reduces its weight by at least $a_1$.

Let $\widehat G_i(K)$ count coalitions in this interval containing the distinguished type-$i$ voter, and put
$$ E_i(K)=G_i(K)-\widehat G_i(K). $$
Then $E_i(K)\geq0$. Every coalition counted by $E_i(K)$ omits type $1$, because a minimal-winning coalition containing a smallest-weight voter has weight less than $T_K+a_1$. Also, every such coalition has weight less than $T_K+a_\tau$, by minimality.

Coalitions containing both distinguished voters cancel when comparing $\widehat G_i$ and $\widehat G_j$. The remaining difference is
$$ \widehat G_i(K)-\widehat G_j(K)=C_0(K)-C_{a_1}(K). $$
Indeed, for $U=T_K-a_i$ and $V=T_K-a_j$, the difference between the intervals $[U,U+a_1)$ and $[V,V+a_1)$ equals the difference between $[U,V)$ and $[U+a_1,V+a_1)$, as an identity between their counting functions.

Consequently,
$$ p_i(K)-p_j(K) =(1+\lambda)C_0(K)-(1-\lambda)C_{a_1}(K) +(1-\lambda)(E_i(K)-E_j(K)). $$
All interval endpoints here retain the stipulated winning-at-equality convention.

2. The strict threshold, with a self-contained proof

For the remainder, assume $\tau\geq2$ and $0<\lambda<1$.

Define
$$ f(z)=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\frac{z^{a_t}}{1+z^{a_t}}, \qquad z>0. $$
Each summand is strictly increasing, and the limits of $f$ are $0$ and $A$. Hence there is a unique $z>0$ satisfying
$$ f(z)=r. $$

Set
$$ x_t=\frac{z^{a_t}}{1+z^{a_t}}, \qquad V=\sum_t\mu_ta_t^2x_t(1-x_t)>0. $$
Under the tilted probability measure, each voter of type $t$ is independently selected with probability $x_t$. The full selected weight has mean $Kr$ and variance $KV$.

Let
$$ F_K(z)=\prod_t(1+z^{a_t})^{K\mu_t}. $$
For a remaining voter set $R$, write $F_R(z)$ for the analogous product. Its number of coalitions of weight $k$ is exactly
$$ F_R(z)z^{-k}\Pr_z(S_R=k). $$
This follows because every coalition of weight $k$ has tilted probability $z^k/F_R(z)$.

Leading local estimate

After deleting any fixed number of voters, uniformly over integer $k$ at bounded distance from the tilted mean,
$$ \Pr_z(S_R=k)=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi KV}}. $$

Here is the Fourier justification. A type-$t$ characteristic-function factor has modulus
$$ \left|1-x_t+x_te^{ia_t\theta}\right|. $$
Since $0<x_t<1$, it equals $1$ precisely when $a_t\theta$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$. The greatest-common-divisor assumption implies that all factors simultaneously have modulus $1$ only at $\theta=0$ in the Fourier integration interval. Positive masses and only finitely many deletions therefore give exponential decay outside any fixed neighborhood of zero.

Near zero, the centered characteristic function, at $\theta=y/\sqrt K$, converges to $e^{-Vy^2/2}$. Its modulus is bounded by $e^{-by^2}$ for some fixed $b>0$ throughout a sufficiently small neighborhood. Fourier inversion and dominated convergence give the displayed estimate. Bounded displacements from the mean do not change its leading term.

Applying this estimate to the finitely many weights in $C_0$ and $C_{a_1}$ gives
$$ \frac{C_{a_1}(K)}{C_0(K)} =z^{-a_1}(1+o(1)), $$
and $C_0(K)>0$ eventually.

The exceptional counts are exponentially smaller. Coalitions omitting type $1$ have generating function
$$ F_K(z)(1+z^{a_1})^{-K\mu_1}. $$
Bounding tilted probabilities by $1$ and summing over their fixed-width weight interval gives
$$ E_i(K) =O\!\left( F_K(z)z^{-T_K}(1+z^{a_1})^{-K\mu_1} \right). $$
Meanwhile,
$$ C_0(K)\asymp F_K(z)z^{-T_K}K^{-1/2}. $$
Therefore, for every fixed positive integer $M$,
$$ E_i(K)=o\!\left(C_0(K)K^{-M}\right). $$

It follows that
$$ p_i(K)-p_j(K) =C_0(K)\left[ (1+\lambda)-(1-\lambda)z^{-a_1}+o(1) \right]. $$

Define
$$ \rho=\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\in(0,1), \qquad \zeta=\rho^{1/a_1}, \qquad q_{\mathrm{crit}}=c f(\zeta). $$
Strict increase of $f$ proves:

  • If $q>q_{\mathrm{crit}}$, every required difference is eventually positive.
  • If $q<q_{\mathrm{crit}}$, every heavier–lighter difference is eventually negative.

Finitely many pairs permit a common eventual threshold in the first case. Thus only $q=q_{\mathrm{crit}}$ requires further asymptotic analysis.

3. New arithmetic restriction: critical equality forces a rational tilt

Theorem

Suppose $0<\lambda<1$ and $q=q_{\mathrm{crit}}$. Then
$$ \zeta=\rho^{1/a_1}\in\mathbb Q. $$

Proof

The critical parameter satisfies
$$ 0<\zeta<1, \qquad \zeta^{a_1}=\rho\in\mathbb Q, \qquad f(\zeta)=r\in\mathbb Q. $$

Let $\zeta'$ be any algebraic conjugate of $\zeta$. Because the minimal polynomial of $\zeta$ divides $X^{a_1}-\rho$, we have
$$ \zeta'=\zeta\eta $$
for some root of unity $\eta$ with $\eta^{a_1}=1$.

No denominator $1+(\zeta')^{a_t}$ vanishes, since $|(\zeta')^{a_t}|=\zeta^{a_t}<1$. The rational function $f$ has rational coefficients, so conjugating the equality $f(\zeta)=r$ gives
$$ f(\zeta')=r. $$

For $0<b<1$ and real $\theta$,
$$ \operatorname{Re}\frac{be^{i\theta}}{1+be^{i\theta}} =\frac{b\cos\theta+b^2}{1+2b\cos\theta+b^2}. $$
Moreover,
$$ \frac b{1+b} -\operatorname{Re}\frac{be^{i\theta}}{1+be^{i\theta}} = \frac{b(1-b)(1-\cos\theta)} {(1+b)(1+2b\cos\theta+b^2)}. $$
This difference is nonnegative, and vanishes exactly when $e^{i\theta}=1$.

Apply this identity to $b=\zeta^{a_t}$ and $e^{i\theta}=\eta^{a_t}$. All coefficients $\mu_ta_t$ are strictly positive. Since
$$ \operatorname{Re}f(\zeta')=r=f(\zeta), $$
every summand must attain equality. Hence
$$ \eta^{a_t}=1 \qquad\text{for every }t. $$
The greatest common divisor of the $a_t$ is $1$, so Bézout's identity implies $\eta=1$. Thus every conjugate of $\zeta$ equals $\zeta$.

The polynomial $X^{a_1}-\rho$ has distinct roots, because its derivative is nonzero at each root. Therefore the minimal polynomial of $\zeta$ has degree $1$, proving $\zeta\in\mathbb Q$. $\square$

Polynomial-time necessary test for critical equality

Write $\rho=R/S$ in lowest terms, with $0<R<S$. If $\zeta=u/v$ is in lowest terms, then
$$ R=u^{a_1}, \qquad S=v^{a_1}. $$
Conversely, these two perfect-power conditions imply that $\zeta$ is rational.

Since $v\geq2$, critical equality requires
$$ a_1\leq\lfloor\log_2 S\rfloor. $$
The bit length of $S$ is $O(L)$. Thus a genuine-mixture critical input necessarily has smallest normalized integer weight of numerical size $O(L)$.

This necessary condition is testable in polynomial time:

  • If $a_1>\lfloor\log_2 S\rfloor$, reject critical equality.
  • Otherwise, compute the integer $a_1$-th roots of $R$ and $S$ and test their powers.

In the second step, the exponent has numerical size $O(L)$. Binary search and integer powering, with values capped once they exceed the target, use polynomially many operations on numbers of polynomial bit length.

Passing this test does not establish $q=q_{\mathrm{crit}}$. The remaining equality $f(\zeta)=r$ still has to be checked.

4. Critical expansion and the zero ceiling phase

Assume henceforth that $q=q_{\mathrm{crit}}$, and write $z=\zeta$. Then
$$ (1-\lambda)z^{-a_1}=1+\lambda. $$

Define the per-replication third, fourth, and fifth cumulants
$$ \begin{aligned} C_3&=\sum_t\mu_ta_t^3x_t(1-x_t)(1-2x_t),\\ C_4&=\sum_t\mu_ta_t^4x_t(1-x_t) \bigl(1-6x_t(1-x_t)\bigr),\\ C_5&=\sum_t\mu_ta_t^5x_t(1-x_t)(1-2x_t) \bigl(1-12x_t(1-x_t)\bigr). \end{aligned} $$
These formulas follow by differentiating the Bernoulli cumulant-generating function $\log(1-x+xe^s)$. Equivalently, its cumulants obey the recurrence
$$ \kappa_{n+1}(x)=x(1-x)\frac{d}{dx}\kappa_n(x), $$
starting with $\kappa_1(x)=x$. Multiplication of the Bernoulli variable by $a_t$ multiplies its $n$-th cumulant by $a_t^n$.

Fix $i>j$, and put
$$ a=a_1,\qquad d=a_i-a_j, \qquad Z_d=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}z^{-\ell}, \qquad \pi_\ell=\frac{z^{-\ell}}{Z_d}. $$
For a phase $\delta$, define
$$ u_\ell=\delta-a_i(1-x_i)+a_jx_j+\ell $$
and
$$ M_d(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\pi_\ell\ell. $$
Since $z<1$, geometric summation gives
$$ M_d(z)=\frac{d}{1-z^d}-\frac1{1-z}. $$

Set
$$ \Gamma_{ij}(\delta) = \delta-a_i(1-x_i)+a_jx_j +M_d(z)+\frac a2+\frac{C_3}{2V}. $$

The next section proves, in particular,
$$ p_i(K)-p_j(K) =\mathcal A_{ij,K} \left[ \frac{a\,\Gamma_{ij}(\delta_K)}{KV} +O(K^{-2}) \right], $$
where $\mathcal A_{ij,K}>0$.

Why only phase zero matters for this test

Because $r$ and all masses are rational, every multiple of a common denominator of these numbers is admissible and satisfies $Kr\in\mathbb Z$. Thus $\delta_K=0$ occurs infinitely often.

Also,
$$ \Gamma_{ij}(\delta)=\Gamma_{ij}(0)+\delta. $$
Consequently:

  • If $\Gamma_{ij}(0)<0$, that pair violates monotonicity on all sufficiently large replications in an infinite zero-phase subsequence.
  • If $\Gamma_{ij}(0)>0$, that pair is eventually strictly ordered on every phase.
  • If $\Gamma_{ij}(0)=0$, every occurring nonzero phase is eventually strictly ordered; only phase zero remains undecided by this coefficient.

There are finitely many phases because $r$ is rational.

It suffices to examine adjacent pairs $(t,t-1)$. Indeed,
$$ p_i(K)-p_j(K)=\sum_{t=j+1}^{i}\bigl(p_t(K)-p_{t-1}(K)\bigr). $$

5. New next-order test when $\Gamma_{ij}(0)=0$

The following coefficient resolves additional critical cases.

For the distinguished pair, define the removed variance and removed third cumulant
$$ \begin{aligned} v_b&=a_i^2x_i(1-x_i)+a_j^2x_j(1-x_j),\\ c_b&=a_i^3x_i(1-x_i)(1-2x_i) +a_j^3x_j(1-x_j)(1-2x_j). \end{aligned} $$
At phase zero, define
$$ D_m=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\pi_\ell \bigl((u_\ell+a)^m-u_\ell^m\bigr), \qquad m=2,3,4. $$
Also put
$$ R_3=\frac{C_3}{V}, \qquad R_4=\frac{C_4}{V}, \qquad R_5=\frac{C_5}{V}. $$

Proposition

Suppose $\Gamma_{ij}(0)=0$. Define
$$ \Phi_{ij} = -\frac{D_4}{8} -\frac{a c_b}{2} -\frac{5R_3D_3}{12} -\frac{aR_5}{8} +\frac{aR_3v_b}{2} +\frac{5aR_3R_4}{12}. $$
Along admissible replications with $\delta_K=0$,
$$ p_i(K)-p_j(K) = \mathcal A_{ij,K} \left[ \frac{\Phi_{ij}}{K^2V^2} +o(K^{-2}) \right], $$
where
$$ \mathcal A_{ij,K} = \frac{(1+\lambda)F_R(z)z^{-T_K+a_i}Z_d} {\sqrt{2\pi KV}}>0. $$
Thus $\Phi_{ij}>0$ gives eventual strict ordering on this phase, whereas $\Phi_{ij}<0$ gives eventual strict reversal.

Proof

The tilted mean of the remaining weight is
$$ Kr-a_ix_i-a_jx_j. $$
Hence the weights in $C_0$ have displacements $u_\ell$, and the corresponding weights in $C_a$ have displacements $u_\ell+a$.

Let $\chi_K(\theta)$ be the centered characteristic function of this remaining weight. By the critical relation and Fourier inversion, the nonexceptional part of the score difference is
$$ \frac{(1+\lambda)F_R(z)z^{-T_K+a_i}Z_d}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\chi_K(\theta)H(\theta)\,d\theta, $$
where
$$ H(\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\pi_\ell \left(e^{-iu_\ell\theta}-e^{-i(u_\ell+a)\theta}\right). $$
In particular, $H(0)=0$.

At $\theta=y/\sqrt K$, expansion of the centered characteristic function gives
$$ \chi_K(y/\sqrt K) = e^{-Vy^2/2} \left[ 1+\frac{e_1(y)}{\sqrt K} +\frac{e_2(y)}K +\frac{e_3(y)}{K^{3/2}} +O(K^{-2}P(y)) \right], $$
with a fixed polynomial bound $P$, where
$$ \begin{aligned} e_1(y)&=-\frac{iC_3y^3}{6},\\ e_2(y)&=\frac{v_by^2}{2}+\frac{C_4y^4}{24} -\frac{C_3^2y^6}{72},\\ e_3(y)&=\frac{ic_by^3}{6} +\frac{iC_5y^5}{120} -\frac{iC_3v_by^5}{12} -\frac{iC_3C_4y^7}{144} +\frac{iC_3^3y^9}{1296}. \end{aligned} $$
For example, the centered logarithm has terms
$$ -\frac{Vy^2}{2} -\frac{iC_3y^3}{6\sqrt K} +\frac1K\left(\frac{v_by^2}{2}+\frac{C_4y^4}{24}\right) +\frac{i}{K^{3/2}} \left(\frac{c_by^3}{6}+\frac{C_5y^5}{120}\right). $$
Exponentiating gives the stated $e_1,e_2,e_3$.

The kernel expansion is
$$ H(y/\sqrt K) = \frac{h_1(y)}{\sqrt K} +\frac{h_2(y)}K +\frac{h_3(y)}{K^{3/2}} +\frac{h_4(y)}{K^2} +O(K^{-5/2}P_1(y)), $$
where
$$ h_1(y)=iay,\quad h_2(y)=\frac{D_2y^2}{2},\quad h_3(y)=-\frac{iD_3y^3}{6},\quad h_4(y)=-\frac{D_4y^4}{24}. $$

These expansions justify integration term by term. More explicitly, restrict first to $|y|\leq K^{1/100}$. Taylor remainders there have polynomial bounds multiplied by the Gaussian factor. In the rest of a small fixed neighborhood of zero, the modulus bound $e^{-bK\theta^2}$ makes the contribution smaller than every power of $K^{-1}$. Outside that neighborhood, the greatest-common-divisor argument from Section 2 gives exponential decay. Since $H(0)=0$, the integrated characteristic-function remainder contributes $o(K^{-2})$ relative to the leading Gaussian integral.

Odd integrands integrate to zero. The normalized Gaussian moments are
$$ \mathbb E[y^{2m}]=(2m-1)!!\,V^{-m}. $$
The coefficient of $K^{-1}$ comes from $h_2+h_1e_1$ and equals
$$ \frac{D_2}{2V}+\frac{aC_3}{2V^2} =\frac{a\Gamma_{ij}(\delta)}V. $$
Here
$$ D_2=2a\sum_\ell\pi_\ell u_\ell+a^2. $$
This proves the expansion used in Section 4.

The coefficient of $K^{-2}$ comes from
$$ h_4+h_3e_1+h_2e_2+h_1e_3. $$
Using the Gaussian moments, it is
$$ \begin{aligned} Q_2={}& \frac{-D_4/8+3D_2v_b/4-ac_b/2}{V^2}\\ &+\frac{-5D_3C_3/12+5D_2C_4/16-aC_5/8 +5aC_3v_b/4}{V^3}\\ &+\frac{-35D_2C_3^2/48+35aC_3C_4/48}{V^4} -\frac{35aC_3^3}{48V^5}. \end{aligned} $$

When $\Gamma_{ij}(0)=0$, the preceding $K^{-1}$ coefficient vanishes, so
$$ D_2=-\frac{aC_3}{V}=-aR_3. $$
Substituting this identity into $Q_2$ cancels the cubic terms in $C_3$ and gives
$$ Q_2=\frac{\Phi_{ij}}{V^2}. $$

Finally, the exceptional contribution is smaller than $\mathcal A_{ij,K}K^{-M}$ for every fixed $M$, by the exponential bound in Section 2. It therefore does not affect the displayed coefficient. $\square$

Resulting sufficient criteria

At a critical quota, let
$$ \Gamma_t=\Gamma_{t,t-1}(0). $$
Then:

  • If some $\Gamma_t<0$, eventual local monotonicity fails.
  • If every $\Gamma_t>0$, eventual local monotonicity holds.
  • If every $\Gamma_t\geq0$ and every pair with $\Gamma_t=0$ has $\Phi_{t,t-1}>0$, eventual local monotonicity holds.
  • If some pair has $\Gamma_t=0$ and $\Phi_{t,t-1}<0$, eventual local monotonicity fails.

The positive conclusions follow by combining finitely many adjacent pairs and phases. The negative conclusions use the infinitely recurring zero phase.

The case $\Gamma_t=\Phi_{t,t-1}=0$ remains undecided by these results.

6. A dyadic input resolved after second-order cancellation

Take weights
$$ (a_1,a_2,a_3)=(1,4,10) $$
and masses
$$ (\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3) = \frac1{2^{60}} \left( 42192619842384,\; 128665531136742,\; 1152750646455867850 \right). $$
Take
$$ \lambda=\frac12, \qquad q=\frac{106020275261045}{2^{59}}. $$
The three mass numerators are positive and sum to $2^{60}$. Thus every input number is dyadic, and the weights are distinct positive integers.

Here $\rho=1/3$, so the critical parameter is $z=1/3$. The selection probabilities are
$$ (x_1,x_2,x_3)=\left(\frac14,\frac1{82},\frac1{59050}\right). $$
The critical-quota equality follows exactly from
$$ \begin{aligned} f(1/3) &=\frac1{2^{60}} \left( 10548154960596+ 6276367372524+ 195216028188970 \right)\\ &=\frac{106020275261045}{2^{59}} =q. \end{aligned} $$
Since all $x_t$ lie strictly between $0$ and $1$, this also proves $0<q<\sum_t\mu_ta_t$.

For verification of the critical coefficients, direct rational calculation gives
$$ V=\frac{96107823554906315061} {55825612174568137424896}, \qquad \frac{C_3}{V}=\frac{435591021}{44063110}. $$
The adjacent second-order coefficients are
$$ \Gamma_{21}(0)=\frac{277521}{82670}>0, \qquad \Gamma_{32}(0)=0. $$

For the pair $(a_i,a_j)=(10,4)$, the quantities in the new test are
$$ \begin{aligned} u_0&=-\frac{2409199}{242105}, & M_6(1/3)&=\frac{1641}{364},\\ v_b&=\frac{11396825469}{58614831025}, & c_b&=\frac{10914247159665192}{14190943665307625},\\ D_3&=\frac{403489336289233}{5333949623275}, & D_4&=-\frac{1380060537841492388157}{2582751747085987750}, \end{aligned} $$
and
$$ R_4= \frac{6090932359633912078100598489437} {61819959811811780159187133800}, $$
$$ R_5= \frac{2103817929919670076770306086405267151} {2138131624319813005062857289807000}. $$
Substitution into the formula for $\Phi_{32}$ gives
$$ \Phi_{32} = \frac{4487336401095526612282010363} {116181333365169492461659800}>0. $$

For completeness, $\mu_2$ has reduced denominator $2^{59}$, and all other mass denominators divide $2^{59}$. Hence admissible replications are exactly the multiples of $2^{59}$. The quota has the same denominator, so every admissible replication has $\delta_K=0$.

Thus the pair $(4,1)$ is eventually strictly ordered by its positive $\Gamma$ coefficient, and the pair $(10,4)$ is eventually strictly ordered by its positive $\Phi$ coefficient. Transitivity proves that this input is eventually locally monotonic.

This example resolves a genuine-mixture critical case that the second-order criterion alone cannot decide.

7. What remains open
