The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of indivisible good categories \(G\), valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), and compactly represented normalized monotone submodular valuations \(v_t\), does there exist an allocation of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), each category's total assigned mass equals its supply, every pair of positively assigned bundles satisfies EF1 under the relevant type's valuation, and no feasible reassignment preserving type masses and supplies weakly benefits almost every recipient while strictly benefiting positive mass?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a set of \(m\geq1\) good categories. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each category has binary-rational supply \(b_g\in[0,1]\). Each recipient receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\), containing at most one unit of each category.
Each valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) is represented by a Boolean circuit taking a bundle's incidence vector and outputting a binary numerator and positive denominator; valuations are promised monotone and submodular, with \(v_t(\varnothing)=0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including circuits.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=b_g\). It is EF1 if, whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\) and \(x_{u,R}>0\), either \(R=\varnothing\) or some \(g\in R\) satisfies \(v_t(S)\geq v_t(R\setminus\{g\})\); this includes pairs within one type.
It is Pareto optimal if there are no real reassignment masses \(z_{t,S,R}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_R z_{t,S,R}=x_{t,S}\), \(\sum_t\sum_S\sum_{R\ni g}z_{t,S,R}=b_g\), and \(z_{t,S,R}>0\Rightarrow v_t(R)\geq v_t(S)\), with \(\sum_{t,S,R:\,v_t(R)>v_t(S)}z_{t,S,R}>0\). Reassignments need not satisfy EF1.

Is deciding whether an EF1 and Pareto-optimal allocation exists solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete agents, this existence problem is NP-hard even with three agents and explicitly represented weighted coverage valuations. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18220v1)
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified the undefined compact valuation encoding as Boolean circuits outputting binary-rational values, with normalized monotone submodularity promised.
  • Interpreted normalization as zero value for the empty bundle, without imposing unit value on the full bundle.
  • Restricted per-capita supplies to the feasible range [0,1], since each bundle contains at most one unit of a category; zero supplies are allowed.
  • Allowed real allocation and reassignment masses and formalized recipient-level Pareto improvements by coupling each type's old and new bundles.
  • Made EF1 apply to every ordered pair of positively assigned type-bundle cells, including pairs within the same type, with the empty envied bundle exempt.
  • Required Pareto comparisons to range over all feasible reassignments, without requiring those reassignments to satisfy EF1.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable: every promised instance admits an EF1 and Pareto-optimal allocation.

In fact, submodularity is unnecessary. The proof applies to arbitrary monotone valuations on the finite bundle space.

The argument uses artificial budgets lying in a narrow interval, a device related to [Budish’s approximate competitive equilibrium from equal incomes framework](https://ericbudish.org/research/combinatorial-assignment/). We prove the exact market-clearing statement needed for this continuous model below.

1. A sufficient condition for EF1 and Pareto optimality

Set
$$ B=1+\frac{1}{2m}. $$

For a nonnegative price vector $p\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^m$, write
$$ p(S)=\sum_{g\in S}p_g. $$

For type $t$ and an auxiliary budget $w\in[1,B]$, define $D_t(p,w)$ as follows:

  • Among bundles $S$ with $p(S)\leq w$, maximize $v_t(S)$.
  • Among those maximizers, minimize $p(S)$.

Thus $D_t(p,w)$ contains all bundles surviving both steps. It is nonempty because the bundle space is finite and $\varnothing$ is affordable.

Lemma 1. Suppose $x$ is feasible and there is a nonnegative price vector $p$ such that
$$ x_{t,S}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\in D_t(p,w) \text{ for some }w\in[1,B]. $$
Then $x$ is EF1 and Pareto optimal.

Proof of EF1

Consider any supported pairs $(t,S)$ and $(u,R)$, with $R\neq\varnothing$. Choose a budget $w\in[1,B]$ witnessing $S\in D_t(p,w)$. The corresponding condition for $R$ implies
$$ p(R)\leq B. $$

If $p(R)\leq1$, removing any $g\in R$ leaves a bundle costing at most $1$.

Otherwise, choose a category $g\in R$ of maximum price. Since $|R|\leq m$,
$$ p_g\geq\frac{p(R)}{|R|} \geq\frac{p(R)}m >\frac1m. $$
Consequently,
$$ p(R\setminus\{g\}) =p(R)-p_g <B-\frac1m =1-\frac{1}{2m} <1. $$

In either case, some removal $R\setminus\{g\}$ is affordable at budget $w\geq1$. Utility maximization therefore gives
$$ v_t(S)\geq v_t(R\setminus\{g\}). $$
This proves EF1, including comparisons within one type.

Proof of Pareto optimality

Fix a supported source $(t,S)$ and its witnessing budget $w$.

If $v_t(R)\geq v_t(S)$, then
$$ p(R)\geq p(S). $$
Indeed, if $p(R)<p(S)\leq w$, then $R$ is affordable. A strictly higher value would contradict utility maximization, while equal value would contradict minimum expenditure among utility maximizers.

Moreover,
$$ v_t(R)>v_t(S) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(R)>w\geq p(S), $$
because an affordable strictly better bundle would contradict utility maximization.

Now suppose $z$ were a Pareto-improving reassignment as defined in the question. Every positive transition has nonnegative price change, and any strictly improving positive transition has strictly positive price change. Since the bundle space is finite and the strictly improving mass is positive,
$$ \sum_{t,S,R}z_{t,S,R}\bigl(p(R)-p(S)\bigr)>0. $$

But the reassignment’s row equations and supply equations imply
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t,S,R}z_{t,S,R}\bigl(p(R)-p(S)\bigr) &= \sum_g p_g \left( \sum_{t,S,R:\,g\in R}z_{t,S,R} - \sum_{t,S:\,g\in S}x_{t,S} \right)\\ &=\sum_g p_g(b_g-b_g)=0. \end{aligned} $$
This contradiction proves Pareto optimality. $\square$

It remains to construct a feasible allocation satisfying Lemma 1.

2. Aggregate demand with continuously varying budgets

Set
$$ M=B+1, $$
and restrict prices temporarily to the compact cube $[0,M]^m$.

For each type, give its mass a uniform auxiliary budget distribution on $[1,B]$. These budgets are proof devices; they impose no additional requirement on the original allocation.

We now define the aggregate demand correspondence explicitly, using only finite-dimensional objects.

For a price vector $p$, form the list consisting of $1$, $B$, and the clipped bundle costs
$$ c_S(p)=\max\{1,\min\{B,p(S)\}\}, \qquad S\subseteq G. $$
Sort this list as
$$ 1=a_0(p)\leq a_1(p)\leq\cdots\leq a_N(p)=B, \qquad N=2^m+1. $$
Each sorted coordinate is continuous in $p$: if every entry of a finite list changes by at most $\delta$, every order statistic changes by at most $\delta$.

Define
$$ \omega_j(p)=\frac{a_j(p)-a_{j-1}(p)}{B-1}, \qquad j=1,\ldots,N. $$
These weights are nonnegative and sum to $1$.

Whenever $\omega_j(p)>0$, the affordable bundle set is constant throughout
$$ I_j(p)=(a_{j-1}(p),a_j(p)). $$
Indeed, no bundle cost lies inside this interval. Therefore $D_t(p,w)$ is also constant as $w$ ranges over $I_j(p)$. Let $w_j(p)$ be its midpoint.

For every $t,j$, choose a probability vector $\gamma_{t,j,S}$ over bundles. Require
$$ \gamma_{t,j,S}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\in D_t(p,w_j(p)) $$
whenever $\omega_j(p)>0$. For zero-weight intervals, impose no support restriction.

Let $A(p)$ be the set of aggregate category-demand vectors obtained as
$$ d= \sum_t\mu_t \sum_{j=1}^N\omega_j(p) \sum_{S\subseteq G}\gamma_{t,j,S}\chi(S), $$
where $\chi(S)$ is the incidence vector of $S$.

For every $p$, the set $A(p)$ is nonempty, compact, and convex: it is the linear image of a finite product of nonempty probability simplexes restricted to demand sets. Furthermore,
$$ A(p)\subseteq[0,1]^m, $$
because total recipient mass is $1$.

3. The aggregate demand correspondence has a closed graph

This continuity property is the central existence step.

Lemma 2. If $p^{(r)}\to p$, $d^{(r)}\to d$, and $d^{(r)}\in A(p^{(r)})$ for every $r$, then $d\in A(p)$.

Proof. Choose probability vectors $\gamma^{(r)}$ representing each $d^{(r)}$. They belong to a fixed finite product of probability simplexes. By compactness, after passing to a subsequence,
$$ \gamma^{(r)}\to\gamma. $$
Continuity of the sorted endpoints gives
$$ \omega_j(p^{(r)})\to\omega_j(p). $$

Fix an interval index $j$ with $\omega_j(p)>0$, and choose a point $w$ in its interior. For sufficiently large $r$,
$$ w\in I_j(p^{(r)}). $$
Also, $w$ differs from every bundle cost $p(T)$. Because there are finitely many bundles, for sufficiently large $r$ the affordable bundle family at $(p^{(r)},w)$ is exactly the affordable family at $(p,w)$.

Suppose $\gamma_{t,j,S}>0$. Then $\gamma^{(r)}_{t,j,S}>0$ for all sufficiently large $r$, so
$$ S\in D_t(p^{(r)},w). $$
Here we used the constancy of demand within $I_j(p^{(r)})$.

The affordable family eventually remains fixed, so its utility-maximizing family also remains fixed. Thus $S$ maximizes utility at $(p,w)$. For every bundle $T$ in that maximizing family, minimum expenditure at $p^{(r)}$ gives
$$ p^{(r)}(S)\leq p^{(r)}(T). $$
Taking limits yields $p(S)\leq p(T)$. Hence
$$ S\in D_t(p,w). $$

Therefore $\gamma$ obeys every support restriction associated with a positive-weight interval at $p$. Taking limits in the aggregate-demand formula shows that $\gamma$ represents $d$, proving $d\in A(p)$. $\square$

Because the range lies in the compact set $[0,1]^m$, this closed-graph property implies upper hemicontinuity. Explicitly, a failure of upper hemicontinuity would produce a convergent price sequence and demand vectors outside an open neighborhood of $A(p)$; a convergent demand subsequence would contradict Lemma 2.

4. Prices clear every positive-price category

We use the following standard form of Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem: an upper hemicontinuous correspondence from a nonempty compact convex subset of finite-dimensional Euclidean space to itself, with nonempty compact convex values, has a fixed point.

Let $C:\mathbb R^m\to[0,M]^m$ clip each coordinate to $[0,M]$. On
$$ K=[0,M]^m\times[0,1]^m, $$
define
$$ \Phi(p,d)=\{C(p+d-b)\}\times A(p). $$

All hypotheses of Kakutani’s theorem hold:

  • $K$ is nonempty, compact, and convex.
  • Each value of $\Phi$ is nonempty, compact, and convex.
  • Its graph is closed, by continuity of $C$ and Lemma 2.
  • Its range lies in the compact set $K$, so it is upper hemicontinuous.

We obtain a fixed point $(p,d)$ satisfying
$$ d\in A(p), \qquad p=C(p+d-b). $$

Consider a category $g$.

If $0<p_g<M$, the clipping equation gives $d_g=b_g$.

If $p_g=0$, the equation gives $d_g\leq b_g$.

If $p_g=M$, every bundle containing $g$ costs at least $M>B$, so no such bundle is affordable at any auxiliary budget. Thus $d_g=0$. The clipping equation at its upper boundary requires $d_g\geq b_g$, and consequently $b_g=d_g=0$.

We have proved
$$ d_g\leq b_g\quad\text{for every }g, \qquad p_g>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_g=b_g. $$

Choose probability vectors representing $d\in A(p)$, and define preliminary masses
$$ y_{t,S} = \mu_t\sum_{j=1}^N\omega_j(p)\gamma_{t,j,S}. $$
Their type totals are $\mu_t$, and their category totals are $d_g$. Moreover, every positive $y_{t,S}$ has a witnessing budget $w\in[1,B]$ with
$$ S\in D_t(p,w). $$
Indeed, a positive finite sum contains a positive term with positive interval weight.

5. Assign the remaining zero-price supply

Only zero-price categories can have unassigned supply. We can fill these shortages while preserving the demand condition.

First observe that if $p_g=0$ and $S\in D_t(p,w)$, then
$$ S\cup\{g\}\in D_t(p,w). $$
The new bundle has the same cost and remains affordable. Monotonicity gives
$$ v_t(S\cup\{g\})\geq v_t(S). $$
Strict inequality would contradict the utility optimality of $S$, so the values are equal. The expenditure is also unchanged, preserving minimum expenditure among utility maximizers.

Now process each zero-price category $g$. Let $d_g$ denote its current allocated mass. If $d_g=b_g$, make no change. Otherwise $d_g<b_g\leq1$, and define
$$ q=\frac{b_g-d_g}{1-d_g}\in(0,1]. $$
For every currently supported bundle missing $g$, move a fraction $q$ of its mass from $S$ to $S\cup\{g\}$.

This operation preserves each type’s total mass and every other category’s total. Since total recipient mass is $1$, the mass currently missing $g$ is $1-d_g$. Its new supply is therefore
$$ d_g+q(1-d_g)=b_g. $$
The preceding observation preserves the demand condition for every resulting positive type–bundle pair.

After processing all zero-price categories, we obtain an allocation $x$ satisfying every type-mass and supply equation exactly. Every supported pair still belongs to $D_t(p,w)$ for some $w\in[1,B]$.

Lemma 1 now proves that $x$ is EF1 and Pareto optimal.

6. Complexity conclusion

The construction establishes existence for every finite society in the question, including heterogeneous types, arbitrary supplies in $[0,1]$, and the required within-type EF1 comparisons. It uses only monotonicity of the valuations.

Consequently, on the promised input domain, the decision algorithm simply returns YES. Its running time is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$; reading the input, if required, takes $O(L)$ time.

The fixed-point proof concerns existence and does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for constructing an allocation. That distinction does not affect the requested decision problem.
