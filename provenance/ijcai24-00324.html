The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) indivisible items, \(g\) groups, a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(i,v_t)\) with additive \(v_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\), and \(p\), output an ordered partition \(A=(A_1,\ldots,A_g)\) such that every type with \(\mu_t>0\) satisfies \(v_t(A_i)\ge\theta_t(p)\), where \(\theta_t(p)=\max_{\{B_1,\ldots,B_p\}\in\Pi_p(M)}\min_j v_t(B_j)\); for \(r_i\) positive-mass types in group \(i\) and \(p=80\sum_i\lceil\log(r_i+1)\rceil\), find such an \(A\) in randomized polynomial time with success probability at least \(2/3\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) contain \(m\geq1\) indivisible items and let \(g\geq2\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct types \(t=(i,v_t)\), with \(i\in\{1,\ldots,g\}\), additive \(v_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^m\), and positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Every group has at least one supported type. Set \(r_i=|\{t:t=(i,v_t)\}|\) and \(p=80\sum_{i=1}^g\lceil\log_2(r_i+1)\rceil\); \(p\) is determined by the support.
Define \(\theta_t(p)=\max_{(B_1,\ldots,B_p)}\min_{1\leq j\leq p}v_t(B_j)\), where the maximum ranges over pairwise disjoint, possibly empty bundles whose union is \(M\). These thresholds are defined by the valuations, not supplied as additional input.
Find an ordered partition \(A=(A_1,\ldots,A_g)\) of \(M\), allowing empty bundles, such that \(v_t(A_i)\geq\theta_t(p)\) for every supported type \(t=(i,v_t)\).

Does this allocation search problem admit a deterministic algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,g,p\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete groups of sizes \(n_i\geq1\), such allocations always exist at \(p=80\sum_i\lceil\log_2(n_i+1)\rceil\), and a randomized polynomial-time algorithm finds one with probability at least \(2/3\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.11543v1)
The support formulation inherits that randomized guarantee; this is a consequence of the discrete result. The search did not verify a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm or an NP-hardness classification for this exact search problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Require at least two groups and positive support in every group, carrying over the discrete domain.
  • Normalize the distribution to total mass one; masses are positive binary rationals.
  • Use base-two logarithms and determine p from supported type counts rather than treating p as an independent input.
  • Allow empty bundles in both benchmark partitions and allocations, including when p exceeds the number of items.
  • Define L as the total binary input length and do not supply exact MMS thresholds as an oracle.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

1. Result

The problem admits a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm.

We give a complete construction. It uses the sampling probabilities from [Theorem 3.4 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.11543v1), but replaces sampling with exact rational conditional expectations. The normalization and completion steps are proved below. In particular, the algorithm never computes the exact thresholds \(\theta_t(p)\).

Write \(a_i=\lceil\log_2(r_i+1)\rceil\) and \(S=\sum_{i=1}^g a_i\), so \(p=80S\). Since every group has a supported type, \(a_i\geq1\), hence \(S\geq g\geq2\).

The masses \(\mu_t\) do not enter the construction: every supported type imposes its constraint regardless of its positive mass.

2. A computable upper bound on each threshold

If \(m<p\), every partition into \(p\) bundles has an empty bundle. Consequently, all thresholds are zero, and any allocation works. Henceforth assume \(m\geq p\).

For each type \(t\), sort its item values as \(w_{t,1}\geq\cdots\geq w_{t,m}\), and compute the rational number

\(b_t=\min_{0\leq k<p}\dfrac{\sum_{j=k+1}^m w_{t,j}}{p-k}\).

We establish two properties.

*First, \(\theta_t(p)\leq b_t\).* Fix any partition into \(p\) bundles, and let \(\lambda\) be its minimum bundle value. The \(k\) highest-valued items occur in at most \(k\) bundles. Thus at least \(p-k\) bundles contain none of these items. Their combined value is at least \((p-k)\lambda\) and at most \(\sum_{j=k+1}^m w_{t,j}\). Therefore

\(\lambda\leq\dfrac{\sum_{j=k+1}^m w_{t,j}}{p-k}\)

for every \(k<p\). Taking the maximum over partitions and then the minimum over \(k\) proves the claim.

Call \(t\) active if \(b_t>0\), and let \(T^+\) denote the active types. If \(b_t=0\), nonnegativity and the preceding upper bound imply \(\theta_t(p)=0\), so this type needs no further attention.

For an active type, define additive normalized values by

\(u_t(\ell)=\min\{v_t(\ell)/b_t,1\}\).

*Second, \(u_t(M)\geq p\).* Let \(k\) be the number of items whose original value exceeds \(b_t\). If \(k\geq p\), these items alone contribute at least \(p\) to \(u_t(M)\). If \(k<p\), the definition of \(b_t\) gives

\(\sum_{j=k+1}^m w_{t,j}\geq(p-k)b_t\),

and therefore

\(u_t(M)=k+\dfrac{\sum_{j=k+1}^m w_{t,j}}{b_t}\geq p\).

Thus every active type satisfies \(0\leq u_t(\ell)\leq1\) and \(u_t(M)\geq p\). Moreover, for every bundle \(B\),

\(u_t(B)\geq1\ \Longrightarrow\ v_t(B)\geq b_t\geq\theta_t(p)\).

It suffices to give every active type normalized value at least \(1\).

3. A rational potential

Temporarily consider assigning each item independently a label in \(\{0,1,\ldots,g\}\), with probabilities

\(q_0=\dfrac12,\qquad q_i=\dfrac{a_i}{2S}\quad(1\leq i\leq g)\).

These probabilities are positive and sum to \(1\). Label \(0\) denotes a reserve set \(R\); label \(i\) denotes a preliminary bundle \(P_i\).

For an active type \(t=(i,v_t)\), define

\(F_t=2\prod_{\ell\in P_i}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}2\right)\),

\(H_t=2^{2g}\prod_{\ell\in R}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}2\right)\).

Empty products equal \(1\). Define the nonnegative rational potential

\(\Phi=\dfrac1g\sum_{t\in T^+}F_t+\sum_{t\in T^+}H_t\).

We first prove that every assignment with \(\Phi<1\) has two useful properties:

  • Fewer than \(g\) active types have \(u_t(P_i)<1\).
  • Every active type has \(u_t(R)\geq2g\).

For \(x\in[0,1]\), convexity of \(2^{-x}\) places its graph below the chord joining its endpoint values, giving

\(2^{-x}\leq1-\dfrac{x}{2}\).

Consequently,

\(F_t\geq2^{\,1-u_t(P_i)},\qquad H_t\geq2^{\,2g-u_t(R)}\).

If \(u_t(P_i)<1\), then \(F_t>1\). If there were at least \(g\) such types, their contributions alone would imply \(\Phi>1\). This proves the first property.

If any active type had \(u_t(R)<2g\), then \(H_t>1\), again contradicting \(\Phi<1\). This proves the second property.

We next show that the initial expectation is less than \(1\). Independence across items gives, for \(t\) in group \(i\),

\(\mathbb{E}[F_t]=2\prod_{\ell\in M}\left(1-\dfrac{q_i u_t(\ell)}2\right)\).

Using \(1-y\leq\exp(-y)\), \(u_t(M)\geq p\), and \(\exp(-z)\leq2^{-z}\) for \(z\geq0\), we obtain

\(\mathbb{E}[F_t]\leq2\exp\left(-\dfrac{q_i u_t(M)}2\right)\leq2\cdot2^{-q_i p/2}=2\cdot2^{-20a_i}\).

Similarly,

\(\mathbb{E}[H_t]=2^{2g}\prod_{\ell\in M}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}4\right)\leq2^{2g}\cdot2^{-p/4}=2^{2g-20S}\).

To sum these bounds, observe that \(r_i\leq2^{a_i}\). Also,

\(\tau+1=1+\sum_i r_i\leq\prod_i(r_i+1)\leq2^S\),

where the first inequality follows by expanding the product and retaining its constant and linear terms. Hence \(\tau\leq2^S\).

There are at most \(r_i\) active types in group \(i\), so

\(\mathbb{E}[\Phi]\leq\dfrac2g\sum_i r_i2^{-20a_i}+\tau2^{2g-20S}\)

\(\leq\dfrac2g\sum_i2^{-19a_i}+2^{2g-19S}\)

\(\leq2^{-18}+2^{-17S}\leq2^{-18}+2^{-34}<1\).

The penultimate inequality uses \(a_i\geq1\) and \(g\leq S\).

4. Deterministically obtaining \(\Phi<1\)

Assign the labels one item at a time. At each step, compute the conditional expectation of \(\Phi\) for every possible next label and choose one minimizing it.

Here these conditional expectations are exactly computable rational numbers. To see this explicitly, suppose some labels have been fixed and \(U\) is the set of unassigned items. For a type \(t\) in group \(i\),

\(\mathbb{E}[F_t\mid\text{fixed labels}]=2\prod_{\substack{\ell\text{ fixed}\\\ell\text{ labeled }i}}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}2\right)\prod_{\ell\in U}\left(1-\dfrac{q_i u_t(\ell)}2\right)\),

and

\(\mathbb{E}[H_t\mid\text{fixed labels}]=2^{2g}\prod_{\substack{\ell\text{ fixed}\\\ell\text{ labeled }0}}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}2\right)\prod_{\ell\in U}\left(1-\dfrac{u_t(\ell)}4\right)\).

These formulas follow because the remaining item labels are independent.

If \(\Psi\) denotes the current conditional expectation and \(\Psi_c\) denotes the conditional expectation after fixing the next label to \(c\), then

\(\Psi=\sum_{c=0}^g q_c\Psi_c\).

Since this is a weighted average, at least one choice satisfies \(\Psi_c\leq\Psi\). Choosing a minimum therefore never increases the conditional expectation.

After all labels have been fixed, the conditional expectation equals the actual potential. Thus the resulting assignment satisfies

\(\Phi\leq\mathbb{E}[\Phi]<1\).

By Section 3, its preliminary bundles leave a set \(D\) of \(k<g\) unsatisfied active types, and every active type values the reserve at least \(2g\).

5. Completing the allocation by greedy bag filling

We prove directly that the reserve can satisfy all types in \(D\).

Maintain the remaining types and the unused reserve items. Construct a bag \(B\) by adding unused items in a fixed order until some remaining type has \(u_t(B)\geq1\). Assign this bag to that type’s group, remove that type from the remaining set, and repeat.

Two facts justify this procedure.

First, immediately before the last item is added to a bag, every remaining type values the bag below \(1\); otherwise the construction would already have stopped. Each item has normalized value at most \(1\). Hence the completed bag has value strictly below \(2\) for every type remaining at that step.

Second, consider a type still remaining after \(h\) bags have been allocated. It was present when each earlier bag was constructed, so those bags removed value less than \(2h\) from its reserve. Its unused reserve therefore has value at least

\(2g-2h\).

Whenever another bag is needed, \(h\leq k-1\), and \(k<g\). Consequently,

\(2g-2h\geq2g-2(k-1)\geq4\).

In particular, adding all unused items would give every remaining type value exceeding \(1\). Thus the stopping condition for the next bag must occur. The procedure allocates one bag per remaining type and terminates after \(k\) steps.

For each group \(i\), combine \(P_i\) with all repair bags assigned to that group. Assign any unused reserve items arbitrarily to complete an ordered partition of \(M\).

Every active type now has normalized value at least \(1\): it either already had that value in \(P_i\), or received a repair bag. Nonnegativity ensures that subsequent additions preserve this guarantee. Section 2 then implies \(v_t(A_i)\geq\theta_t(p)\). Inactive types have threshold zero and are also satisfied.

6. Polynomial running time and bit complexity

Sorting the rational values and computing all candidates defining \(b_t\) takes polynomial time. The numbers \(b_t\), \(u_t(\ell)\), and \(q_i\) have polynomial bit length.

Each conditional-expectation evaluation uses a sum of at most \(2\tau\) terms, each containing at most \(m\) rational factors. Products and sums of polynomially many rationals of polynomial bit length again have polynomial bit length: numerator and denominator lengths grow at most by the sum of the operand lengths, plus the bits required for additions. The multiplier \(2^{2g}\) has only \(2g+1\) bits.

There are \(m\) labeling steps and \(g+1\) candidate labels per step. Thus all conditional expectations and their comparisons can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Greedy completion uses fewer than \(g\) bags and polynomially many rational additions and comparisons.

The complete algorithm is therefore deterministic and polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,g,p\), proving the requested classification.
