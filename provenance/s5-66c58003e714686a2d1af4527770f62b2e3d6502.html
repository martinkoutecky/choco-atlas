The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of APs \(F\), user types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a population scale \(\rho\), and explicitly represented continuous cell-payoff functions derived from the WLAN throughput and control rules, compute an assignment \(x_{tf}\geq 0\) (including an outside option) with \(\sum_f x_{tf}=\mu_t\) that has no blocking reassignment. A block may involve a finite set of APs and portions of incumbent or unmatched type mass; all participating APs and user mass must weakly improve under the new cell payoffs, with at least one strict improvement.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) user types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge2\) access points (APs), a binary-rational population scale \(\rho\ge3\), and binary-rational quotas \(2\le q_f\le\rho-1\). Each type specifies its nonnegative rational physical rate to each AP; rate zero forbids association. APs have no interference or other cross-cell payoff externalities.
For each AP, the input explicitly supplies a nonnegative continuous controlled cell worth \(V_f(z)\), its payoff \(p_f(z)\), and per-user payoffs \(p_{tf}(z)\), on feasible cell vectors \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), \(\rho\sum_tz_t\le q_f\), with forbidden coordinates zero. Function graphs are given by finite quantifier-free formulas of rational polynomial equalities and inequalities; \(L\) is the total input bit length. Empty cells and unmatched users have payoff zero; nonempty feasible cells have positive worth.
The supplied payoffs are promised to arise from zero-threat Nash bargaining with fixed type and AP utilities \(u_i\), continuous on \([0,\infty)\), differentiable on \((0,\infty)\), strictly increasing and concave, with \(u_i(0)=0\): maximize \(\log u_f(s_f)+\rho\sum_{t:z_t>0}z_t\log u_t(s_t)\) subject to \(s_f+\rho\sum_tz_ts_t\le V_f(z)\) and nonnegative shares. Thus \(p_{tf}\) is an individual user's share, not the total share of type \(t\).
An assignment has \(x_{tg}\ge0\) for \(g\in F\cup\{0\}\), where \(0\) is the outside option, \(\sum_gx_{tg}=\mu_t\), forbidden associations are zero, and \(\rho\sum_tx_{tf}\le q_f\).
A blocking reassignment selects APs \(S\subseteq F\) and portions \(b_{tgh}\ge0\) of users currently at \(g\in F\cup\{0\}\), with destinations \(h\in S\cup\{0\}\) and \(\sum_hb_{tgh}\le x_{tg}\). Every selected AP's new cell consists exactly of selected portions sent to it, \(z_{tf}=\sum_gb_{tgf}\), and must be feasible; unselected incumbents may be expelled to the outside option without consent.
Every selected AP must weakly improve, and every positive selected portion must weakly improve relative to its own original cell payoff; at least one selected AP or positive portion must strictly improve. Selected users may become unmatched, and departing users need no consent from their former APs. Payoff ties remain indifferences. Stability means absence of such a block.
Is computing an exact stable assignment, or correctly reporting that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard? Exact coordinates are represented as real algebraic numbers; dependence on \(\rho\) and the quotas must be through their binary encodings.
For finite users, BDAA has an \(O(\max\{|F|,|W|\}^{5})\) proposal bound; with stability-inducing Nash-bargaining sharing and strict preferences, it returns a core-stable matching. This bound does not include constructing coalition preference lists. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.00931v2)

Result

The exact search problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\). This holds even with two APs, fixed population scale and quotas, linear utilities, polynomial payoff functions of degree at most two, and polynomial worth functions of degree at most three.

The reduction below produces instances with a unique stable assignment whose coordinates are rational. Thus the option of reporting nonexistence does not circumvent the hardness.

1. A polynomial encoding of maximum cut

We reduce from weighted MAX-CUT: given a loopless graph \(G=(\{1,\ldots,n\},E)\), positive integer edge weights \(w_{ij}\), and an integer threshold \(K\), decide whether some cut has weight at least \(K\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-complete}\), with weights encoded in binary. See [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may assume \(n\ge2\). For \(y\in[0,1]^n\), define

\(\displaystyle C(y)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}\bigl(y_i(1-y_j)+y_j(1-y_i)\bigr),\)

and

\(\displaystyle A(y)=C(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_i y_i^2,\qquad \varepsilon_i=2^{-(i+1)}.\)

At a Boolean vector \(v\in\{0,1\}^n\), \(C(v)\) is the weight of the cut specified by \(v\).

We establish four properties.

Nonnegativity and positivity. Each summand of \(C\) is nonnegative on the cube. Consequently \(A(y)\ge0\), \(A(0)=0\), and \(A(y)>0\) whenever \(y\ne0\), because every \(\varepsilon_i\) is positive.

Every maximizer is Boolean. The polynomial \(A\) is continuous on the compact cube, so it attains its maximum. Fix all coordinates except \(y_i=r\). Since the graph has no loops, \(C\) is affine in \(r\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle A(y_1,\ldots,r,\ldots,y_n)=\varepsilon_i r^2+\alpha r+\beta\)

for constants \(\alpha,\beta\). This is strictly convex. For \(0<r<1\), its value is strictly less than the convex combination of its endpoint values, hence strictly less than at least one endpoint value. A maximizer therefore cannot have an interior coordinate.

The Boolean maximizer is unique. Put

\(\displaystyle T(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_i v_i.\)

These values are distinct: multiplying by \(2^{n+1}\) gives the distinct integers

\(\displaystyle 2^{n+1}T(v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{n-i}v_i.\)

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle 0\le T(v)\le\sum_{i=1}^{n}2^{-(i+1)}=\frac12-2^{-(n+1)}<\frac12.\)

The cut weights \(C(v)\) are integers. Thus vertices with different cut weights have different \(A\)-values, and vertices with equal cut weights have different \(A\)-values because their \(T\)-values differ.

The unique maximizer specifies a maximum cut. If \(C(v)>C(u)\), then \(C(v)-C(u)\ge1\), whereas \(\lvert T(v)-T(u)\rvert<1/2\). Hence \(A(v)>A(u)\).

Let \(v^\star\) denote this unique maximizer and let \(M=A(v^\star)\). We also have \(M>0\), since \(A(e_1)\ge\varepsilon_1>0\).

2. Construction of the society

Set

\(\displaystyle k=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil,\qquad \eta=2^{-(k+1)}.\)

Use \(n+1\) types, labelled \(1,\ldots,n,d\), with masses

\(\displaystyle \mu_i=\eta\quad(1\le i\le n),\qquad \mu_d=1-n\eta.\)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. Since \(2^{k-1}<n\le2^k\),

\(\displaystyle \frac14<n\eta\le\frac12,\qquad \frac12\le\mu_d<\frac34.\)

There are two APs, with

\(\displaystyle \rho=4,\qquad q_1=2,\qquad q_2=3.\)

These satisfy all quota restrictions.

Type \(i\) has physical rate \(i\) to AP \(1\) and rate zero to AP \(2\). Type \(d\) has rate zero to AP \(1\) and rate one to AP \(2\). Thus the types are distinct, and every type has an allowed association.

For AP \(1\), the feasible cell vectors are exactly

\(\displaystyle 0\le z_i\le\eta\quad(1\le i\le n),\qquad z_d=0.\)

Indeed, every such vector satisfies

\(\displaystyle \rho\sum_i z_i\le4n\eta\le2=q_1.\)

For AP \(2\), the feasible cell vectors are exactly

\(\displaystyle z_i=0\quad(1\le i\le n),\qquad 0\le z_d\le\mu_d,\)

because \(4\mu_d<3=q_2\).

Define the common cell shares

\(\displaystyle a_1(z)=A(z_1/\eta,\ldots,z_n/\eta),\qquad a_2(z)=z_d.\)

Supply the worths and AP payoffs

\(\displaystyle V_f(z)=\left(1+4\sum_tz_t\right)a_f(z),\qquad p_f(z)=a_f(z).\)

For each allowed association, supply \(p_{tf}(z)=a_f(z)\). For forbidden associations, supply \(p_{tf}(z)=0\).

These functions are nonnegative and continuous. Empty-cell payoffs and worths are zero. Every nonempty feasible cell has positive worth: this follows from the positivity of \(A\) for AP \(1\), and from \(z_d>0\) for AP \(2\).

3. Verification of the Nash-bargaining promise

Choose the fixed utility of every type and both APs to be

\(\displaystyle u(s)=s.\)

This utility is continuous, differentiable on the positive half-line, strictly increasing, concave, and zero at zero.

Consider any nonempty feasible cell \(z\), with worth \(V>0\). Write

\(\displaystyle N=4\sum_tz_t.\)

The bargaining objective for its active participants is

\(\displaystyle \log s_f+4\sum_{t:z_t>0}z_t\log s_t,\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle s_f+4\sum_tz_ts_t\le V.\)

The objective is strictly concave in the active shares. Its maximizer uses the entire budget, since the objective increases in every active share.

For a positive Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\), the stationarity equations are

\(\displaystyle \frac1{s_f}=\lambda,\qquad \frac{4z_t}{s_t}=\lambda\,4z_t\quad(z_t>0).\)

Consequently every active participant receives \(1/\lambda\). The budget equation gives

\(\displaystyle s_f=s_t=\frac{V}{1+N}.\)

These positive shares satisfy the stationarity equations and the budget equality; strict concavity proves they are the unique active-share maximizer.

For the supplied worth \(V_f(z)=(1+N)a_f(z)\), this share is exactly \(a_f(z)\). Thus the supplied AP payoffs and individual-user payoffs satisfy the promised bargaining model. Values of per-user payoff functions on absent coordinates do not affect the bargaining objective or any positive selected portion.

Empty cells use the stipulated zero-payoff convention.

4. Every stable assignment reveals the maximum cut

Let \(x\) be an arbitrary feasible assignment and write

\(\displaystyle y_i=x_{i1}/\eta.\)

Because type \(i\) cannot associate with AP \(2\),

\(\displaystyle x_{i0}=\eta-x_{i1}.\)

The payoff of AP \(1\), and of each user currently associated with it, is \(R=A(y)\). Unmatched users receive zero.

Suppose \(R<M\). Select only AP \(1\), and give it the new cell

\(\displaystyle z_i^\star=\eta v_i^\star,\qquad z_d^\star=0.\)

This cell can be formed from the available users: for each type \(i\), select \(\min\{x_{i1},z_i^\star\}\) from AP \(1\), and select the remaining amount from the outside option. The latter amount is available because \(z_i^\star\le\eta=x_{i1}+x_{i0}\).

Every selected user previously received either \(R\) or zero and now receives \(M>R\ge0\). AP \(1\) also strictly improves from \(R\) to \(M\). Any incumbents omitted from the new cell may be expelled, as explicitly permitted by the blocking rule. Hence this is a block.

Therefore stability implies \(A(y)=M\). Uniqueness of the maximizer gives

\(\displaystyle x_{i1}=\eta v_i^\star\quad(1\le i\le n).\)

Stability also forces all type-\(d\) mass into AP \(2\). If \(x_{d2}<\mu_d\), select AP \(2\) and all type-\(d\) mass, taking the missing mass from outside. Its new share is \(\mu_d>x_{d2}\), so AP \(2\) strictly improves and every selected user weakly improves. This is another block.

Thus any stable assignment must be the following assignment \(x^\star\):

\(\displaystyle x_{i1}^\star=\eta v_i^\star,\qquad x_{i0}^\star=\eta(1-v_i^\star),\qquad x_{i2}^\star=0,\)

and

\(\displaystyle x_{d2}^\star=\mu_d,\qquad x_{d1}^\star=x_{d0}^\star=0.\)

5. The forced assignment is stable, including against tie blocks

We now verify stability under the full stated blocking rule, allowing either or both APs to participate and allowing selected portions to go outside.

At \(x^\star\), AP \(1\) receives \(M\). If selected in a block, its new cell must satisfy \(a_1(z)\ge M\). Since \(M\) is the global maximum and its maximizing cell is unique, necessarily

\(\displaystyle z_i=\eta v_i^\star.\)

Hence AP \(1\)'s payoff cannot strictly increase.

Moreover, every type appearing in this new cell has \(v_i^\star=1\). All of that type's mass was already associated with AP \(1\) at \(x^\star\). Therefore every selected portion sent to AP \(1\) previously received \(M\) and again receives \(M\).

At \(x^\star\), AP \(2\) receives \(\mu_d\). If selected, weak improvement requires \(z_d\ge\mu_d\), while feasibility requires \(z_d\le\mu_d\). Thus \(z_d=\mu_d\), and AP \(2\)'s payoff is unchanged. Every portion sent there was already at AP \(2\) and again receives \(\mu_d\).

Finally, a selected portion sent outside receives zero. To weakly improve, its original payoff must also have been zero, since all original payoffs are nonnegative. Such a portion is indifferent.

Thus every selected AP and every positive selected portion is indifferent in any proposed weakly improving reassignment. There can be no strict improvement, so there is no block.

Consequently \(x^\star\) is stable and is the unique stable assignment.

6. Encoding size and complexity consequence

The construction has \(m=2\) and support size \(\tau=n+1\). The population scale and quotas are fixed constants.

The masses have encoding length bounded by a polynomial in \(\log n\). The perturbation coefficients have encoding length bounded by a polynomial in \(n\). Substitution of \(z_i/\eta\) introduces only polynomially many additional coefficient bits.

The share functions have degree at most two, and the worth functions have degree at most three. Even after explicit polynomial expansion, all functions have polynomially many monomials. Repeating the per-user function graphs for the explicitly supplied payoffs also costs only polynomial space. Each graph is expressible by a rational polynomial equality, together with the feasible-domain inequalities. Therefore the complete society input has length polynomial in the weighted MAX-CUT input length.

Suppose an algorithm computes an exact stable assignment, or correctly reports nonexistence, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). On our constructed instances it must return \(x^\star\), because a stable assignment exists and is unique.

From its exact coordinates we recover

\(\displaystyle v_i^\star=x_{i1}^\star/\eta\in\{0,1\}.\)

Under the usual exact representation of real algebraic numbers, comparison with the rational threshold \(\eta/2\) takes polynomial time in the representation length. Thus the Boolean vector can be decoded in polynomial time, even if the algorithm returns its rational coordinates using algebraic-number representations.

We then compute \(C(v^\star)\) using binary integer arithmetic and compare it with \(K\). Section 1 proves that this decides weighted MAX-CUT.

Hence the requested exact search problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\). In particular, it has no polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The finite-user BDAA proposal bound does not contradict this conclusion. Its stated bound counts proposals, and its stability theorem assumes strict preferences; see Propositions 5–6 of the [supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.00931v2). The reduction above proves hardness directly for the supplied, compactly encoded mass model and its exact blocking rule.
