The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) of worker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), employer types \(J\) with rational per-capita capacities, and a rational initial assignment \(x'\) that is individually rational and worker quasi-stable, compute a stable mass assignment \(x^*\) that firms weakly prefer to \(x'\) and that is firm-worst among all such stable assignments. Each worker type ranks employer types, employer choices rank worker types and accept preferred mass up to capacity, and \(x_{tj}\) records assigned mass; each individual worker still receives at most one employer.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) worker types \(T\) with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) employer types \(J\), each representing a capacity pool with positive binary-rational capacity \(q_j\) per unit worker population. Each worker type strictly ranks \(J\cup\{\emptyset\}\); each employer strictly ranks \(T\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Ranking above \(\emptyset\) means acceptable.
An assignment \(x\) has \(x_{tj}\geq0\), \(\sum_jx_{tj}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\sum_tx_{tj}\leq q_j\); write \(x_{t\emptyset}=\mu_t-\sum_jx_{tj}\). Masses may be partitioned among employers, but each individual worker receives at most one employer. Individual rationality requires positive assignments only on mutually acceptable pairs.
For a nonnegative offered-mass vector \(z\), employer choice \(C_j(z)\) retains acceptable mass in decreasing priority order up to capacity \(q_j\), taking a partial amount of the last type if necessary. Define the mass analogue of Blair's order by \(x\succeq_F y\) precisely when \(C_j(\max\{x_{\cdot j},y_{\cdot j}\})=x_{\cdot j}\) for every \(j\), with the maximum taken coordinatewise.
A blocking move is a triple \((t,k,j)\), where \(k\in J\cup\{\emptyset\}\), \(x_{tk}>0\), worker type \(t\) strictly prefers \(j\) to \(k\), employer \(j\) finds \(t\) acceptable, and either \(\sum_sx_{sj}<q_j\) or \(x_{sj}>0\) for some type \(s\) ranked below \(t\) by \(j\). Stability means individual rationality and no blocking move; worker quasi-stability means individual rationality and that every blocking move has \(k=\emptyset\).
Given a rational worker quasi-stable assignment \(x'\), let \(\mathcal S(x')=\{x:x\text{ is stable and }x\succeq_F x'\}\). The required outcome is \(x^*\in\mathcal S(x')\) such that \(y\succeq_F x^*\) for every \(y\in\mathcal S(x')\).

Is computing such a firms-worst stable mass assignment \(x^*\) solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

Discrete: with substitutable, quota-separable firm preferences, acceptance–firing from a worker quasi-stable matching yields the firms-worst stable matching above it in Blair's order ([source](https://doi.org/10.4236/ojdm.2022.124007)).
Continuous: a strongly polynomial procedure reaches a stable allocation from any feasible allocation in the capacitated bipartite model; its stated guarantee does not identify the firms-worst outcome above a prescribed initial allocation ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.2169)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total worker mass to one, so the given per-capita employer capacities are total capacities in the same units; each employer type is one aggregate capacity pool.
  • Used strict rankings including unemployment and non-hiring as outside options, allowing unacceptable partners and requiring positive employer capacities.
  • Interpreted the graded question's priority-based employer choices as responsive capacity choices, a restriction of the anchor's substitutable, quota-separable domain; workers within a type are interchangeable.
  • Defined the aggregate mass analogue of Blair's set-union comparison using coordinatewise maximum followed by employer choice.
  • Defined blocking through positive mass that can move from unemployment or a less preferred employer, with vacancy or displacement of strictly lower-priority mass; worker quasi-stability excludes every such move originating from employment.
  • Allowed atomless partitions of each type's mass while retaining at most one employer per individual; defined firms-worst as a least element, rather than merely a minimal element.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. A reduction to an ordinary stable-allocation instance identifies exactly the stable assignments above \(x'\). The worker-proposing outcome of that instance is the required firms-worst assignment.

For \(m,\tau\geq 1\), one implementation uses

\(O\!\left(m\tau(m+\tau)\right)\)

arithmetic operations and, with conventional binary numerator–denominator encoding, has the conservative bit-complexity bound

\(O\!\left(L^3+m\tau(m+\tau)L\right)\).

The proof below establishes the reduction and the required extremality in the stated mass analogue of Blair’s order.

1. Employer rejection and Blair’s order

Restrict attention to mutually acceptable pairs. For an employer \(j\) and an acceptable type \(t\), define

\(P_{jt}(a)=\sum_{s:\,s\succeq_j t}a_{sj}\),

where \(s\succeq_j t\) means that \(s=t\) or \(j\) ranks \(s\) above \(t\).

A feasible column \(a_{\cdot j}\) cannot accept additional mass of \(t\) precisely when

\(P_{jt}(a)=q_j.\)

Indeed, this equality means that capacity is filled entirely with types ranked at least as highly as \(t\). If the equality fails, the employer either has spare capacity or holds positive mass of a lower-ranked type.

We need two elementary facts.

Lemma 1. For individually rational feasible assignments \(a,b\),

\(a\succeq_F b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigl(b_{tj}>a_{tj}\Longrightarrow P_{jt}(a)=q_j\bigr) \text{ for every mutually acceptable }(t,j).\)

Proof. Write \(z_{\cdot j}=\max\{a_{\cdot j},b_{\cdot j}\}\).

If \(C_j(z_{\cdot j})=a_{\cdot j}\) and \(z_{tj}>a_{tj}\), choice must exhaust capacity by the time it reaches \(t\). Consequently \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\).

Conversely, suppose the implication holds. If no coordinate of \(b_{\cdot j}\) exceeds the corresponding coordinate of \(a_{\cdot j}\), then \(z_{\cdot j}=a_{\cdot j}\), so choice returns \(a_{\cdot j}\).

Otherwise let \(t\) be the highest-ranked coordinate at which \(b_{tj}>a_{tj}\). Above \(t\), the offered vector equals \(a_{\cdot j}\). Since \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\), choice takes exactly \(a_{tj}\) at \(t\) and nothing below \(t\). This returns \(a_{\cdot j}\). Apply the argument to every employer. \(\square\)

Lemma 2. If \(b\succeq_F a\) and \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\), then

\(P_{jt}(b)=q_j \quad\text{and}\quad b_{tj}\leq a_{tj}.\)

Proof. Put \(z=\max\{a_{\cdot j},b_{\cdot j}\}\). Its offered mass through \(t\) is at least \(q_j\), so \(C_j(z)=b_{\cdot j}\) fills capacity through \(t\). Thus \(P_{jt}(b)=q_j\).

Moreover, the mass offered strictly above \(t\) is at least that in \(a\). The capacity remaining for \(t\) is therefore at most

\(q_j-\sum_{s\succ_j t}a_{sj}=a_{tj}.\)

Hence \(b_{tj}\leq a_{tj}\). \(\square\)

2. Constructing the auxiliary instance

For each type \(t\) initially assigned positive mass, let \(w_t\) be its least-preferred employer with \(x'_{tw_t}>0\).

Keep the original worker quotas \(\mu_t\), employer capacities \(q_j\), and restricted strict preference lists. Introduce edge capacities

\(c_{tj}= \begin{cases} x'_{tj},&\text{if }w_t\text{ exists and }j\succ_t w_t,\\ \mu_t,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\)

Delete edges with zero capacity.

Thus, assignments to employers strictly better than the type’s worst initial employer cannot exceed their initial masses. Types initially entirely unemployed receive no such restrictions.

An assignment is auxiliary-stable if it satisfies these edge capacities and has no blocking pair with \(x_{tj}<c_{tj}\), using the original worker-desire and employer-acceptance conditions.

The construction takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations. The initial assignment \(x'\) satisfies its capacities.

3. The auxiliary stable set is exactly \(\mathcal S(x')\)

We prove both directions.

3.1 Every auxiliary-stable assignment dominates \(x'\)

Let \(a\) be auxiliary-stable, and suppose

\(a_{tj}<x'_{tj}.\)

Because \(x'_{tj}>0\), the employer \(w_t\) exists. Every employer \(h\succ_t j\) is strictly better than \(w_t\), so

\(a_{th}\leq c_{th}=x'_{th}.\)

Consequently,

\(\sum_{h\succ_t j}a_{th}+a_{tj} < \sum_{h\succ_t j}x'_{th}+x'_{tj} \leq\mu_t.\)

Therefore, in \(a\), type \(t\) has positive unemployed mass or positive mass at an employer worse than \(j\). It desires additional mass at \(j\).

Also,

\(a_{tj}<x'_{tj}\leq c_{tj},\)

so the auxiliary edge is unsaturated. Auxiliary stability forces \(j\) to reject additional mass of \(t\), giving

\(P_{jt}(a)=q_j.\)

Lemma 1 now yields \(a\succeq_F x'\).

3.2 Every auxiliary-stable assignment is original-stable

Suppose an auxiliary-stable \(a\) has an original blocking move \((t,k,j)\).

If \(a_{tj}<c_{tj}\), this would also block the auxiliary instance. Hence

\(a_{tj}=c_{tj}.\)

An uncapped edge has \(c_{tj}=\mu_t\). Saturation would assign all of type \(t\) to \(j\), contradicting \(a_{tk}>0\) for a less-preferred \(k\). Thus the edge is capped:

\(j\succ_t w_t \quad\text{and}\quad a_{tj}=x'_{tj}.\)

Since \(x'_{tw_t}>0\), worker quasi-stability of \(x'\) excludes the blocking move \((t,w_t,j)\). Therefore \(j\) rejects additional \(t\)-mass at \(x'\):

\(P_{jt}(x')=q_j.\)

Section 3.1 gives \(a\succeq_F x'\). Lemma 2 therefore gives \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\), contradicting the employer-acceptance condition of the alleged blocking move.

Thus \(a\) is original-stable and belongs to \(\mathcal S(x')\).

3.3 Every member of \(\mathcal S(x')\) is auxiliary-stable

Let \(b\in\mathcal S(x')\). Consider a capped edge, so \(j\succ_t w_t\).

As above, quasi-stability of \(x'\) implies

\(P_{jt}(x')=q_j.\)

Because \(b\succeq_F x'\), Lemma 2 gives

\(b_{tj}\leq x'_{tj}=c_{tj}.\)

The uncapped capacities are automatic from worker feasibility. Hence \(b\) is auxiliary-feasible.

Any auxiliary blocking pair would also be an original blocking move, contradicting original stability of \(b\).

We have proved the exact identity

\(\{\text{auxiliary-stable assignments}\}=\mathcal S(x').\)

4. Worker-proposing extremality in Blair’s order

We next establish the relevant extremal property directly; lexicographic optimality alone would not suffice.

Consider worker-proposing deferred acceptance for a stable-allocation instance with edge capacities \(c_{tj}\), starting from the zero assignment.

At a current assignment \(a\), a type \(t\) with unemployed mass chooses its most-preferred employer \(j\) satisfying

\(a_{tj}<c_{tj} \quad\text{and}\quad P_{jt}(a)<q_j.\)

It offers an amount \(\delta>0\) satisfying

\(\delta\leq\mu_t-\sum_h a_{th},\qquad \delta\leq c_{tj}-a_{tj},\qquad \delta\leq q_j-P_{jt}(a).\)

Employer \(j\) retains the new mass and, if needed, fires its lowest-ranked mass. Thus \(a_{tj}\) increases by exactly \(\delta\), and only lower-ranked types at \(j\) can lose mass.

Lemma 3. Every stable assignment \(b\) of this instance satisfies \(b\succeq_F a\) throughout this process.

Proof. Initially \(a=0\), and \(C_j(b_{\cdot j})=b_{\cdot j}\), so the assertion holds.

Assume it holds before an offer by \(t\) to \(j\). For every employer \(h\succ_t j\), either its edge is saturated,

\(a_{th}=c_{th},\)

or it rejects additional \(t\)-mass,

\(P_{ht}(a)=q_h.\)

In the first case, feasibility gives \(b_{th}\leq a_{th}\). In the second case, the induction hypothesis and Lemma 2 give the same inequality. Therefore

\(b_{th}\leq a_{th}\quad\text{for all }h\succ_t j.\)

Let \(a^+\) be the assignment after the offer. Only coordinate \((t,j)\) increases.

If \(b_{tj}\geq a^+_{tj}\), Lemma 1 immediately preserves \(b\succeq_F a^+\).

Otherwise,

\(b_{tj}<a^+_{tj}=a_{tj}+\delta\leq c_{tj}.\)

Furthermore,

\(\sum_{h\succ_t j}b_{th}+b_{tj} < \sum_{h\succ_t j}a_{th}+a_{tj}+\delta \leq\mu_t.\)

Hence \(t\) desires \(j\) in \(b\), and its edge at \(j\) is unsaturated. Stability of \(b\) forces

\(P_{jt}(b)=q_j.\)

By Lemma 1, this permits the newly increased coordinate while preserving \(b\succeq_F a^+\). All other coordinates either stay fixed or decrease, so they preserve the same implication. This completes the induction. \(\square\)

Consequently, the stable worker-proposing outcome \(a^{\mathrm{GS}}\) satisfies

\(b\succeq_F a^{\mathrm{GS}} \quad\text{for every stable assignment }b\)

of the capacitated instance.

5. Computing the outcome efficiently

The external algorithmic result needed is the following: in a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences, vertex quotas, and edge capacities, the worker-proposing Gale–Shapley outcome exists and can be computed by an accelerated algorithm. Dean and Munshi give an \(O(E\log N)\) implementation; their elementary implementation without dynamic trees takes \(O(EN)\), where \(E\) and \(N\) denote edge and vertex counts. Their Lemmas 1 and 5 identify the accelerated output with the worker-proposing outcome. See [*Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*, Sections 2–3](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • The auxiliary graph is bipartite.
  • All preference lists remain strict.
  • Quotas and edge capacities are nonnegative rationals.
  • Unemployment and unused employer capacity are handled by the standard dummy vertices.

If the implementation requires edge capacities no larger than either endpoint quota, replace \(c_{tj}\) by \(\min\{c_{tj},\mu_t,q_j\}\). This preserves feasibility and stability: an edge saturated at either endpoint quota cannot be a blocking pair.

Run the worker-proposing accelerated algorithm on the auxiliary instance, and return its restriction \(x^*\) to the original pairs.

Section 3 gives \(x^*\in\mathcal S(x')\). For every \(y\in\mathcal S(x')\), the same section makes \(y\) auxiliary-stable, and Lemma 3 gives

\(y\succeq_F x^*.\)

This is exactly the required outcome. It is unique: if two outcomes dominated each other, applying choice to their coordinatewise maximum would return both, forcing equality.

6. Arithmetic and bit complexity

Including dummy vertices and edges, the auxiliary instance has

\(N\leq m+\tau+2,\qquad E\leq m\tau+m+\tau+1.\)

Thus the elementary accelerated implementation uses

\(O(EN)=O\!\left(m\tau(m+\tau)\right)\)

arithmetic operations. The dynamic-tree implementation alternatively gives

\(O\!\left(m\tau\log(m+\tau)\right)\)

arithmetic operations.

For an explicit bit bound, use the elementary implementation. Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the input masses, capacities, and entries of \(x'\). Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Scaling by \(D\) gives integral data of \(O(L)\) bits, including the dummy quotas.

The elementary accelerated algorithm uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of these integers. Feasibility bounds their magnitudes by the scaled quotas, so all numerical values have \(O(L)\) bits. Each such operation therefore costs \(O(L)\) bit operations.

Schoolbook arithmetic gives a conservative \(O(L^3)\) bound for preparing the scaled input. Returning the integer output divided by \(D\) requires no expansion into individual workers. The resulting total bound is

\(O\!\left(L^3+m\tau(m+\tau)L\right).\)

If there are no employers, the unique assignment is entirely unemployed and can be returned directly.

Step-by-step verification

1. Employer rejection and Blair’s order

The rejection criterion \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\) is correct for an individually rational feasible assignment and an acceptable type \(t\). Feasibility gives \(P_{jt}(a)\leq q_j\). If the inequality is strict, the employer either has unused capacity or has positive mass below \(t\).

Lemma 1 is correct in both directions.

  • In the forward direction, if the offered amount at \(t\) exceeds the chosen amount, priority choice must exhaust capacity at or before \(t\). Since its output is \(a_{\cdot j}\), this gives \(P_{jt}(a)=q_j\).
  • In the reverse direction, the highest-priority coordinate where \(b\) exceeds \(a\) has unchanged offers above it. The asserted prefix equality makes choice retain exactly \(a_{tj}\) there. Feasibility then forces every coordinate of \(a\) below it to be zero.

Restricting the condition to mutually acceptable pairs is valid because individual rationality makes both assignments zero on other pairs.

Lemma 2 is correct. The coordinatewise maximum offers at least \(q_j\) through \(t\), so its chosen vector \(b_{\cdot j}\) fills capacity through \(t\). Its offers above \(t\) leave at most \(a_{tj}\) capacity for \(t\), giving the second conclusion.

2. Auxiliary instance

The definition of \(w_t\) is well-defined whenever type \(t\) initially has positive employment: the lists are finite and strict.

The capacities are nonnegative rational numbers. On an initially positive edge, the assigned amount satisfies the new capacity: capped edges retain their initial amount, and uncapped edges have capacity \(\mu_t\).

Deleting zero-capacity edges is valid. Such edges can never carry positive auxiliary mass or be auxiliary blocking pairs. When discussing them in the subsequent comparison with original stability, their assignment coordinates should be understood as zero.

3. Identification of the stable set

Section 3.1 is correct. If \(a_{tj}<x'_{tj}\), then \(j\) was initially assigned, so \(j\) is weakly preferred to \(w_t\). Every employer strictly above \(j\) is therefore capped at its initial amount. The displayed strict inequality follows.

That inequality establishes worker desire: the remaining positive mass must be unemployed or assigned below \(j\). The edge is unsaturated, so auxiliary stability forces employer rejection. Lemma 1 then gives \(a\succeq_F x'\).

Section 3.2 is correct.

  • An original blocking move on an unsaturated auxiliary edge would also be an auxiliary blocking pair.
  • Saturating an uncapped edge assigns all \(\mu_t\) mass to \(j\), leaving no positive mass at a worse destination.
  • On a capped edge, \(j\succ_t w_t\), and \(x'_{tw_t}>0\). Quasi-stability therefore rules out employer acceptance of additional \(t\)-mass at \(j\).
  • Section 3.1 and Lemma 2 preserve that rejection condition at \(a\), contradicting the alleged original blocking move.

This argument also covers deleted capped edges, using their zero coordinates.

Section 3.3 is correct. For every capped edge, quasi-stability supplies the prefix equality at \(x'\), and dominance plus Lemma 2 supplies the capacity inequality at \(b\). Uncapped capacities follow from worker feasibility. An auxiliary blocking pair would be an original blocking move.

Thus the asserted identity between auxiliary-stable assignments and \(\mathcal S(x')\) is established.

4. Extremality invariant

The proposed update is feasible. Its three bounds prevent worker-quota violation, edge-capacity violation, and rejection of any newly offered \(t\)-mass. Any necessary firing affects only lower-priority types.

Lemma 3 is correct.

For every employer above the selected employer \(j\), the selection rule gives either edge saturation or employer rejection. Feasibility or Lemma 2, respectively, yields \(b_{th}\leq a_{th}\).

The two cases at the increased coordinate are exhaustive:

  • If \(b_{tj}\geq a^+_{tj}\), no new coordinate excess over \(b\) arises.
  • Otherwise, the displayed inequality establishes worker desire in \(b\), while \(b_{tj}<c_{tj}\) establishes edge slack. Stability of \(b\) supplies \(P_{jt}(b)=q_j\).

All other coordinates remain fixed or decrease, preserving the implications in Lemma 1. The induction therefore proves the stated Blair-order extremality for any stable endpoint of this process.
