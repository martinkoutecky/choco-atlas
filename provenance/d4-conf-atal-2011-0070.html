The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix a pure scoring rule whose vector for \(|C|+1\) candidates is \((\alpha_1,\alpha_2,1,0,\ldots,0)\), with \(\alpha_1>\alpha_2>1\). Given an initial candidate set \(C\), a new candidate \(a\), a target \(c^\star\in C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings of \(C\), is there a mass assignment \(q_{t,p}\ge 0\), for \(t\in T\) and \(p\in\{0,\ldots,|C|\}\), with \(\sum_p q_{t,p}=\mu_t\), such that inserting \(a\) after position \(p\) for mass \(q_{t,p}\) makes \(c^\star\) a co-winner?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a pure positional scoring rule whose vector for every candidate count \(n\geq4\) is \((\alpha_1,\alpha_2,1,0,\ldots,0)\), where \(\alpha_1>\alpha_2>1\) are fixed integers.
An instance consists of an initial candidate set \(C\), with \(m=|C|\geq3\), one new candidate \(a\notin C\), and a target \(c^\star\in C\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings of \(C\), each with positive mass \(\mu_t\) encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An admissible extension assigns real masses \(q_{t,p}\geq0\), for \(t\in T\) and \(p\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\), satisfying \(\sum_{p=0}^{m}q_{t,p}=\mu_t\). This mass ranks \(a\) immediately after the first \(p\) candidates of \(t\), preserving their order; \(p=0\) puts \(a\) first.
Scores are the mass-weighted positional scores in the extended society. The target is a co-winner exactly when its score is at least that of every candidate in \(C\cup\{a\}\); ties are accepted without tie-breaking.

Is deciding whether an admissible extension makes \(c^\star\) a co-winner in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For indivisible unweighted voters, adding exactly one new candidate under these scoring rules is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete (Baumeister, Roos, and Rothe, 2011). No result settling this exact divisible-mass version was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The scoring coefficients' domain was unspecified; they are fixed integers, as in the discrete scoring-rule definition.
  • The candidate-count domain was unspecified; require at least three initial candidates so the extended vector contains the three positive entries and a trailing zero.
  • Interpret distribution as positive support masses summing to one.
  • Allow real, divisible extension masses, with no integrality or common-denominator restriction.
  • Evaluate co-winner status against every extended candidate, including the new candidate, accepting ties.
  • Define L as the total binary input bit length; the fixed scoring coefficients are not input parameters.

Result

The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). We prove this by constructing a rational linear feasibility problem with \(4\tau\) variables and proving its exact equivalence to the required extension.

1. Four insertion types suffice

Fix a ranking \(t\in T\), and denote its first three candidates by \(b_{t,1},b_{t,2},b_{t,3}\). These exist because \(m\geq3\).

The extended election has \(m+1\geq4\) candidates, so its scoring vector is the specified vector. The scores contributed per unit mass are:

| Insertion position | \(a\) | \(b_{t,1}\) | \(b_{t,2}\) | \(b_{t,3}\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| \(p=0\) | \(\alpha_1\) | \(\alpha_2\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |
| \(p=1\) | \(\alpha_2\) | \(\alpha_1\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |
| \(p=2\) | \(1\) | \(\alpha_1\) | \(\alpha_2\) | \(0\) |
| \(p\geq3\) | \(0\) | \(\alpha_1\) | \(\alpha_2\) | \(1\) |

Every other initial candidate contributes zero in every row: its original rank is at least four, and inserting \(a\) cannot improve that rank.

For \(p\geq3\), the original first three candidates retain their positions, while \(a\) occupies a position at least four. Thus all such insertion positions have identical score contributions.

Let \(J=\{0,1,2,3\}\), where type \(3\) represents all positions \(p\geq3\). For each \(t\in T\), \(j\in J\), and \(d\in C\cup\{a\}\), define \(w_{t,j}(d)\) as the corresponding entry in the table, or zero if \(d\) is an initial candidate outside its first three positions.

2. The rational linear system

Introduce real variables \(x_{t,j}\), representing the mass of ranking \(t\) assigned insertion type \(j\). Impose nonnegativity and preservation of each ranking’s mass:

\[ \]
\(x_{t,j}\geq0\qquad(t\in T,\ j\in J),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j\in J}x_{t,j}=\mu_t\qquad(t\in T).\)

For every candidate \(d\in C\cup\{a\}\), define its score as the linear expression

\(\displaystyle S_d(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in J}w_{t,j}(d)x_{t,j}.\)

Finally, impose one inequality for each competitor:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in J}\bigl(w_{t,j}(c^\star)-w_{t,j}(d)\bigr)x_{t,j}\geq0 \qquad\bigl(d\in(C\cup\{a\})\setminus\{c^\star\}\bigr).\)

These inequalities are exactly \(S_{c^\star}(x)\geq S_d(x)\). Their weak form implements the stipulated acceptance of ties.

3. Exact equivalence

From an admissible winning extension to feasibility.
Given masses \(q_{t,p}\), set

\(x_{t,0}=q_{t,0},\quad x_{t,1}=q_{t,1},\quad x_{t,2}=q_{t,2},\quad \displaystyle x_{t,3}=\sum_{p=3}^{m}q_{t,p}.\)

Nonnegativity and mass preservation follow directly from admissibility. By the table, aggregating positions \(p\geq3\) preserves every candidate’s score. Consequently, if the extension makes \(c^\star\) a co-winner, all competitor inequalities hold.

From feasibility to an admissible winning extension.
Given a feasible vector \(x\), set

\(q_{t,p}=x_{t,p}\quad\text{for }p\in\{0,1,2,3\},\)

and set \(q_{t,p}=0\) for \(4\leq p\leq m\).

Position \(3\) is available because \(m\geq3\). The resulting masses are nonnegative and sum to \(\mu_t\) for each ranking. Again, the table shows that their actual scores equal \(S_d(x)\). The competitor inequalities therefore make \(c^\star\) a co-winner.

This proves equivalence in both directions.

4. Polynomial bit complexity

The system has \(4\tau\) variables, \(4\tau\) nonnegativity inequalities, \(\tau\) mass equalities, and \(m\) competitor inequalities. Replacing each equality by two inequalities gives \(6\tau+m\) inequalities.

All matrix coefficients are integers of absolute value at most \(\alpha_1\). Because the scoring rule is fixed, these coefficients have constant bit length. The only nonzero right-hand sides are copies of the input rationals \(\mu_t\), whose total encoding length is bounded by \(L\). The rankings and table coefficients can be processed in polynomial time, and even a dense encoding of the resulting system has length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No rankings outside the supplied support are enumerated.

We apply the standard polynomial-time rational linear feasibility theorem: given a rational matrix \(A\) and rational vector \(b\) in binary encoding, deciding whether there exists a real vector \(x\) satisfying \(Ax\leq b\) takes time polynomial in the total encoding length. This is the exact feasibility theorem underlying Khachiyan’s polynomial-time algorithm for linear programming. [Khachiyan, 1979](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

Every hypothesis holds here: the system is finite, all its data are rational, and its encoding has polynomial length. The theorem decides exact feasibility, including feasible sets confined to equality boundaries; no positive winning margin is required.

Together with the equivalence proved above, this establishes the claimed polynomial-time algorithm. The indivisible-voter hardness result does not obstruct it: indivisibility restricts how each voter may be assigned, whereas this problem permits arbitrary splitting of mass, exactly as represented by the continuous variables.
