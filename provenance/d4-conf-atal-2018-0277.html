The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), its ranking types \(\mathcal T\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(S\subseteq\mathcal T\), rational conversion costs \(\gamma(t,u)\), and a target condition \(\Psi\) given as a disjunction of systems of linear inequalities on type masses, find nonnegative transfers \(x_{t,u}\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in S,u\in\mathcal T}\gamma(t,u)x_{t,u}\), subject to \(\sum_u x_{t,u}\leq\mu_t\) for each \(t\in S\) and \(\Psi(\mu')\). Here \(\mu'_u=\mu_u+\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,u}-\mathbf{1}_{u\in S}\sum_v x_{u,v}\); return an optimal transfer or report infeasibility.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) candidates, and let \(\mathcal T\) be all strict rankings of \(C\). A society is given by its support: the list \(S\) of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau=|S|\) is the support size, not \(m!\). Require \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\), and set \(\mu_u=0\) outside \(S\).
Conversion costs are an explicit table \(\gamma:\mathcal T^2\to\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\), with \(\gamma(t,t)=0\) and \(\gamma(t,v)\leq\gamma(t,u)+\gamma(u,v)\). Costs need not be symmetric.
The target condition is explicitly given as \(\Psi(y)=\bigvee_{i=1}^{d}(A_i y\leq b_i)\), with binary-encoded integer coefficients, \(d\geq1\), at most \(r\) rows per system, and \(\alpha=\max(1,\max_i\|A_i\|_\infty)\). Inequalities are non-strict; \(\Psi\) alone determines acceptance.
A transfer consists of real masses \(x_{t,u}\geq0\) for \(t\in S,u\in\mathcal T\), satisfying \(\sum_u x_{t,u}\leq\mu_t\). Its resulting society is \(\mu'_u=\mu_u+\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,u}-\mathbf1_{u\in S}\sum_{v\in\mathcal T}x_{u,v}\), with the last sum interpreted as zero outside \(S\).
Continuous Minimum Move asks for a transfer minimizing \(\sum_{t\in S,u\in\mathcal T}\gamma(t,u)x_{t,u}\) subject to \(\Psi(\mu')\), or a report of infeasibility. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the cost table and all systems; state running times in \(m,\tau,d,r,\alpha,L\).

Is Continuous Minimum Move solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? If it is NP-hard, is it nevertheless fixed-parameter tractable with parameter \(r+\alpha\)?

Discrete Minimum Move admits an algorithm whose dependence on \(r,\alpha\) is unrestricted but whose dependence on the total type universe and number of disjuncts is polynomial ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/1801.09584)); discrete Borda shift bribery, a covered specialization, is NP-complete ([source](https://www.math.auckland.ac.nz/~slinko/Research/swap.pdf)).
For continuous moves, a polynomial-time result is known when winner regions have polynomially many systems and inequalities in \(m\), with running time polynomial also in the allowed destination count; this does not explicitly cover arbitrary input conditions \(\Psi\) ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444v1)).

Result

Continuous Minimum Move is solvable exactly in polynomial time in the total binary input length \(L\). Consequently, it is fixed-parameter tractable for parameter \(r+\alpha\), with no parameter-dependent factor required. NP-hardness would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

This conclusion applies to the full stated problem, including arbitrary explicitly listed systems, binary rational masses and costs, and asymmetric costs.

1. An equivalent allocation formulation

Let \(N=|\mathcal T|=m!\). Introduce a variable \(z_{t,u}\) for every \(t\in S\) and \(u\in\mathcal T\). Interpret it as the mass originating at \(t\) that ends at \(u\), including mass that stays at its original type. Require

\[ \]
\(z_{t,u}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{u\in\mathcal T}z_{t,u}=\mu_t\quad(t\in S).\)

The resulting mass at \(u\) is then

\(y_u=\sum_{t\in S}z_{t,u}.\)

In particular, \(y_u\geq0\) and \(\sum_{u\in\mathcal T}y_u=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\).

We prove that this formulation preserves both resulting societies and costs.

From transfers to allocations. Given a legal transfer \(x\), define

\(z_{t,u}=x_{t,u}\quad(u\ne t),\qquad z_{t,t}=\mu_t-\sum_{v\ne t}x_{t,v}.\)

The capacity inequality \(\sum_vx_{t,v}\leq\mu_t\) implies \(z_{t,t}\geq0\), and each row of \(z\) sums to \(\mu_t\).

If \(u\in S\), diagonal transfers cancel in the original definition of \(\mu'_u\), giving

\(\mu'_u=\mu_u-\sum_{v\ne u}x_{u,v}+\sum_{\substack{t\in S\\t\ne u}}x_{t,u}=\sum_{t\in S}z_{t,u}.\)

If \(u\notin S\), then \(\mu_u=0\) and

\(\mu'_u=\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,u}=\sum_{t\in S}z_{t,u}.\)

Because \(\gamma(t,t)=0\), the costs agree:

\(\sum_{t\in S,u\in\mathcal T}\gamma(t,u)z_{t,u}=\sum_{t\in S,u\in\mathcal T}\gamma(t,u)x_{t,u}.\)

From allocations to transfers. Conversely, given such an allocation \(z\), set \(x_{t,u}=z_{t,u}\) for \(u\ne t\), and set \(x_{t,t}=0\). Then

\(\sum_ux_{t,u}=\mu_t-z_{t,t}\leq\mu_t.\)

The same calculations show that its resulting society is \(y\) and its cost equals the allocation cost. Thus the two formulations are exactly equivalent.

2. One rational linear program per disjunct

Let \(r_i\leq r\) be the number of rows of \(A_i\), and write its entries as \(a^{(i)}_{j,u}\). For each \(i\), solve the following linear program:

\( \displaystyle \min_z\ \sum_{t\in S}\sum_{u\in\mathcal T}\gamma(t,u)z_{t,u}, \)

subject to

\(z_{t,u}\geq0\quad(t\in S,\ u\in\mathcal T),\)

\(\sum_{u\in\mathcal T}z_{t,u}=\mu_t\quad(t\in S),\)

\(\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{u\in\mathcal T}a^{(i)}_{j,u}z_{t,u}\leq b^{(i)}_j\quad(j\in\{1,\ldots,r_i\}).\)

The last constraints are precisely \(A_i y\leq b_i\), after substituting \(y_u=\sum_tz_{t,u}\).

All coefficients are rational. Moreover, every variable satisfies \(0\leq z_{t,u}\leq\mu_t\leq1\). The feasible region is therefore a closed, bounded polyhedron. Whenever it is nonempty, the continuous linear objective attains a finite minimum.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: an explicitly encoded rational LP can be tested for feasibility and, when its optimum is finite and attained, an exact rational optimal solution and value can be computed in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. The rationality and finite-attainment hypotheses have just been checked.

3. Correctness of the algorithm

Solve all \(d\) linear programs. If every one is infeasible, report infeasibility. Otherwise, choose a feasible program with the smallest optimal value and convert its allocation into a transfer as above.

To justify this procedure, every accepted transfer satisfies at least one system \(A_i\mu'\leq b_i\), by the definition of \(\Psi\). Its corresponding allocation is feasible for that system’s LP with exactly the same cost. Hence its cost is at least the smallest optimum among the feasible programs.

Conversely, an optimal allocation from any feasible program yields a legal transfer whose resulting society satisfies that program’s system, and hence satisfies \(\Psi\), with the same cost.

These two implications prove both the infeasibility report and global optimality of the returned transfer.

4. Running time and parameter dependence

Each program has \(n=\tau m!\) variables, \(\tau\) equality constraints, at most \(r\) target inequalities, and \(n\) nonnegativity constraints.

Its coefficients are copied from the input or are zero or one. Repeating coefficients across source types increases encoding length only polynomially. For example, a dense encoding of program \(i\) has length at most

\(O\bigl((\tau+r_i+1)(\tau m!+1)(L+1)\bigr).\)

Consequently, for a universal constant exponent, the running time is bounded by

\(d\bigl(m+\tau m!+r+L+\log_2(1+\alpha)\bigr)^{O(1)}.\)

Crucially, this is polynomial in the actual input length. The explicit cost table contains \(N^2\) rational entries, so \(N^2=O(L)\). Since \(\tau\leq N\), we obtain

\(\tau m!=\tau N\leq N^2=O(L).\)

Also, \(d\), the actual number of rows, \(m\), and \(\log_2(1+\alpha)\) are all \(O(L)\). If \(r\) is merely a loose upper bound, use the actual maximum row count in the construction. The total running time is therefore \(L^{O(1)}\), including exact comparison of optimal values and production of the transfer.

Thus the parameterized running time has the form \(f(r+\alpha)L^c\) with \(f\equiv1\) and a universal constant \(c\).

The triangle inequality and symmetry play no role in this proof. The allocation equivalence uses the zero diagonal; LP solvability uses the explicit rational data.

5. Relation to the cited results

The cited discrete Borda shift-bribery hardness concerns indivisible votes. It does not establish hardness for real-valued transfers; the paper states that discrete result as Theorem 7. [Elkind, Faliszewski, and Slinko](https://www.math.auckland.ac.nz/~slinko/Research/swap.pdf).

The cited continuous paper’s Theorem 1 likewise uses linear programs when destination counts and winner-region descriptions permit them. Here, the explicitly supplied disjunction can be enumerated in time polynomial in \(L\), and the full cost table makes the destination universe polynomially bounded in \(L\). Thus no bound on the number of systems purely in terms of \(m\) is needed for this input model. [Continuous Computational Social Choice](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444v1).
