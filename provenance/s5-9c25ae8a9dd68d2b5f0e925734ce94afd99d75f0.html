Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\) of partner types, and rational costs \(c_t(u)\), find a minimum-cost stable matching or report that none exists. A matching is a symmetric nonnegative matrix \(q\) with \(\sum_u q_{tu}=\mu_t\); define \(W_t(u;q)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}q_{tv}\), and require \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) for every pair \(t,u\). Determine the exact computational complexity in the support size \(\tau=|T|\) and input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; the number of partner types is \(m=\tau\), and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\) has a strict complete ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(T\), including its own type, and costs \(c_t(u)\in\mathbb{Q}\), possibly negative, satisfying \(c_t(u)<c_t(v)\) exactly when \(u\succ_t v\).
A matching is a symmetric matrix \(q\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{T\times T}\) with \(\sum_{u\in T}q_{tu}=\mu_t\). The entry \(q_{tt}\) measures the mass of people matched to other people of type \(t\); such matches are allowed, and nobody remains unmatched.
Define \(W_t(u;q)=\sum_{v\in T:\,u\succ_t v}q_{tv}\). Stability requires \(W_t(u;q)=0\) or \(W_u(t;q)=0\) for every \(t,u\in T\), including \(t=u\).
The objective is to find a stable matching minimizing \(C(q)=\sum_{t,u\in T}c_t(u)q_{tu}\), or report that none exists.

Is this exact optimization problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard? If NP-hard, is it FPT parameterized by \(\tau\)?

Discrete minimum-cost stable roommates admits a \(2^{O(k)}n^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm parameterized by minimum crossing distance \(k\). [Cheng and Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.26943v1).
Discrete egalitarian stable roommates is NP-hard with strict incomplete lists of length at most three, even on solvable instances. [Cseh, Irving, and Manlove](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/120965/2/120965.pdf).
For unit-capacity graphs, maximum-welfare ordinally stable fractional roommates matching is NP-complete with strict preferences; that model permits missing pairs and unmatched agents. No result settling the complete, arbitrary-mass problem above was verified. [Chen, Roy, and Sorge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12259).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the rational population distribution to total mass one and retained only positive-mass types.
  • Made rankings complete over all supported partner types, including the person's own type; diagonal entries represent matches between distinct people of the same type, and diagonal stability constraints apply.
  • Restored the discrete definition's strict agreement between costs and preferences; allowed signed rational costs because the definition imposes no sign restriction.
  • Specified total cost as the sum of individual costs weighted by matched population mass, including both endpoints of each match.
  • Allowed real matching masses and required full matching, preserving the stated row-sum equalities.
  • Used support size as the parameter for the conditional FPT question because no continuous crossing-distance parameter was supplied; set m equal to the number of partner types, hence to tau.

Result

The exact optimization problem is NP-hard, even with equal dyadic masses. It is nevertheless FPT parameterized by \(\tau\): an exact algorithm runs in time \(\tau^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\).

The reduction below uses the diagonal stability constraints to enforce missing pairs, and a sufficiently small positive self cost to handle unmatched mass.

1. A finite union of linear programs

Write \(u\succeq_t v\) when either \(u=v\) or \(u\succ_t v\).

Because \(\mu_t>0\), every feasible matching \(q\) has a uniquely defined worst assigned partner \(r_t\): the least preferred \(v\) satisfying \(q_{tv}>0\). Nonnegativity and strict preferences imply
\[ \]
\(W_t(u;q)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\succ_t r_t.\)

Indeed, if \(u\succ_t r_t\), the positive entry \(q_{t r_t}\) contributes to \(W_t(u;q)\). Conversely, a positive contribution identifies an assigned partner strictly below \(u\), so the worst assigned partner is also strictly below \(u\).

Consequently, stability is equivalent to
\( \neg\bigl(u\succ_t r_t\ \text{and}\ t\succ_u r_u\bigr)\)
for every \(t,u\in T\), including \(t=u\).

Call a vector \(r\in T^T\) admissible if it satisfies these conditions. For each admissible vector, define the polytope
\(P_r=\{q:q_{tu}=q_{ut}\geq0,\ \sum_u q_{tu}=\mu_t,\ q_{tv}=0\text{ whenever }r_t\succ_t v\}.\)

Every point of \(P_r\) is stable. To verify this, fix \(t,u\). Admissibility guarantees that at least one of \(u\succ_t r_t\) and \(t\succ_u r_u\) fails. If the first fails, then \(r_t\succeq_t u\). Every partner strictly below \(u\) is therefore strictly below \(r_t\), and its assigned mass is zero. Thus \(W_t(u;q)=0\). The other case is symmetric.

Conversely, every stable matching belongs to \(P_r\) for its actual worst assigned partners, and that vector is admissible. Hence the stable set is exactly
\(\bigcup_{r\text{ admissible}}P_r.\)

Each \(P_r\) is closed and bounded: its entries are nonnegative and bounded by the prescribed row sums. Therefore, whenever stable matchings exist, the minimum cost is attained.

There are at most \(\tau^\tau\) vectors to inspect. For each admissible vector, solve the rational linear program minimizing \(C(q)\) over \(P_r\). Return the best feasible solution, or report nonexistence if every program is infeasible.

Each program has at most \(\tau(\tau+1)/2\) independent variables and polynomial encoding length. Polynomial-time exact rational linear programming therefore gives running time
\(\tau^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L).\)

This proves the claimed FPT result for arbitrary input masses and costs.

2. The source problem for hardness

We use Theorem 5.2 of [Chen, Roy, and Sorge, *Fractional Matchings under Preferences: Stability and Optimality*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12259), which proves NP-hardness of maximum-welfare ordinally stable fractional roommates matching with strict preferences.

A source instance consists of a graph \(G=(V,E)\), nonnegative rational utilities \(s_t(u)\) on directed acceptable pairs, distinct within each agent’s acceptable list, and a rational threshold \(B\). Put \(n=|V|\).

A fractional matching assigns \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\geq0\) to acceptable edges, with
\(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\leq1.\)

It is ordinally stable if, for every acceptable edge \(\{t,u\}\), at least one endpoint satisfies
\(\sum_{v:\{t,v\}\in E,\ v\succeq_t u}x_{tv}=1.\)

Its welfare is
\(F(x)=\sum_{t\in V}\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}s_t(u)x_{tu}.\)

The NP-hard decision question asks whether an ordinally stable matching has \(F(x)\geq B\). The cited reduction uses rational, in fact integer, numerical data.

We may restrict to source instances with \(d=|E|\geq1\).

3. A rational gap bound

We first prove a bound needed to preserve the decision answer when assigning a positive cost to diagonal mass.

Let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators of all source utilities and of \(B\), in their given rational representations. Thus all those denominators divide \(D\). Set \(H=d!\).

Gap lemma. If source stable matchings exist and their maximum welfare \(F^*\) satisfies \(F^*<B\), then
\(F^*\leq B-\frac{1}{DH}.\)

Proof. For each acceptable edge, choose which endpoint satisfies the saturation equality in the definition of ordinal stability. Fixing all these choices gives a polytope defined by:

  • nonnegativity;
  • capacity inequalities with right-hand side \(1\);
  • the selected saturation equalities with right-hand side \(1\).

The source stable set is the union of these finitely many polytopes. Every polytope is bounded, so maximum welfare is attained at a vertex of one of them.

There are \(d\) edge variables. At a vertex, select \(d\) linearly independent active constraints. Their coefficient matrix \(A\) has entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and their right-hand side is integral. By the determinant expansion,
\(1\leq|\det A|\leq d!=H.\)

Cramer’s rule shows that all coordinates have a common denominator \(h=|\det A|\). Since the utilities have denominators dividing \(D\), the welfare at that vertex has the form \(a/(Dh)\) for an integer \(a\). Also \(B=b/D\) for an integer \(b\). Therefore, if the difference is positive,
\(B-F^*=\frac{bh-a}{Dh}\geq\frac{1}{Dh}\geq\frac{1}{DH}.\)
This proves the lemma.

The binary lengths of \(D\) and \(H\) are polynomial in the source input length: \(\log D\) is bounded by the sum of the denominator encoding lengths, and \(\log(d!)=O(d\log d)\).

4. Completing the lists with diagonal mass

Choose the smallest power of two \(K\geq n\). Introduce one type for each source agent and \(K-n\) additional dummy types. Give every type mass \(1/K\). These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to \(1\).

For each source type \(t\), its complete ranking is:

  • all acceptable source partners, in their original order;
  • its own type \(t\);
  • every remaining type, in an arbitrary strict order.

Each dummy type ranks itself first and all other types in an arbitrary strict order.

4.1 Forbidden pairs receive zero mass

For a source type \(t\), diagonal stability requires
\(W_t(t;q)=0.\)

Every partner below self contributes to this sum. Nonnegativity therefore forces every such entry to be zero. In particular, unacceptable source pairs and pairs involving dummy types receive zero mass.

For a dummy type \(a\), self is first. Thus diagonal stability forces all its off-diagonal entries to zero, and its row equation forces \(q_{aa}=1/K\).

4.2 Stability is preserved exactly

Given any source fractional matching \(x\), define, for acceptable pairs,
\(q_{tu}=x_{tu}/K,\)
and set
\(q_{tt}=\frac{1-\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}}{K}.\)

Set all forbidden off-diagonal entries to zero and set \(q_{aa}=1/K\) for each dummy. This is a feasible society matching.

For an acceptable pair \(\{t,u\}\), the row equation gives
\(K\,W_t(u;q)=1-\sum_{v:\{t,v\}\in E,\ v\succeq_t u}x_{tv}.\)

Thus \(W_t(u;q)=0\) exactly when the source saturation equality holds at \(t\). Stability on acceptable pairs is consequently equivalent in the two models.

All other stability conditions hold automatically: source types assign no mass below self, and dummy types assign mass only to self.

Conversely, any stable society matching has the forbidden entries proved zero above. Defining \(x_{tu}=Kq_{tu}\) on acceptable edges yields a source fractional matching, and the same identity proves its ordinal stability.

We have therefore established a bijection between the source stable matchings and the society’s stable matchings.

5. Costs and the reduction threshold

Set
\(\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2nDH}.\)

For a source type \(t\), assign costs as follows:

  • \(c_t(u)=-s_t(u)\) for acceptable partners;
  • \(c_t(t)=\varepsilon\);
  • costs \(\varepsilon+1,\varepsilon+2,\ldots\) to the remaining partners, following their ranking.

These costs induce exactly the required strict complete ranking. Acceptable costs are distinct and nonpositive, so they all lie strictly below the positive self cost. This also handles acceptable partners having zero utility.

For each dummy, give self cost zero and give the remaining partners distinct increasing positive integer costs.

For a corresponding pair \(x,q\), define total unmatched source mass
\(R(x)=\sum_{t\in V}\left(1-\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\right).\)

Then \(0\leq R(x)\leq n\). Forbidden entries contribute nothing, and dummy diagonal costs are zero. Hence
\(C(q)=\frac{-F(x)+\varepsilon R(x)}{K}.\)

Use the society cost threshold
\(\beta=\frac{-B+\varepsilon n}{K}.\)

Forward implication. If a source stable matching satisfies \(F(x)\geq B\), its corresponding society matching satisfies
\(C(q)\leq\frac{-B+\varepsilon n}{K}=\beta.\)

Reverse implication. Suppose the source has no stable matching with welfare at least \(B\). If it has no stable matching at all, the bijection gives no stable society matching. Otherwise the gap lemma implies that every source stable matching satisfies
\(F(x)\leq B-\frac{1}{DH}.\)

Since the diagonal penalty is nonnegative, every corresponding society matching satisfies
\(C(q)\geq\frac{-B+1/(DH)}{K}>\frac{-B+\varepsilon n}{K}=\beta,\)
where the strict inequality follows from \(\varepsilon n=1/(2DH)\).

Thus the source decision answer is yes exactly when the constructed society has a stable matching of cost at most \(\beta\).

All constructed numbers have polynomial binary length, and \(K<2n\), so this is a polynomial-time many-to-one reduction. The exact society optimization problem is therefore NP-hard.

6. Decision complexity and conclusion

The associated decision problem—whether a stable matching has cost at most a given rational threshold—is also in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Indeed, a yes instance has a witness at a minimum-cost vertex of one of the polytopes \(P_r\). After clearing the mass denominators, Cramer’s rule bounds that vertex’s rational encoding length polynomially in \(\tau,L\). A verifier checks nonnegativity, symmetry, row sums, every stability condition, and the cost bound using exact rational arithmetic.

Consequently, the decision version is NP-complete. The exact optimization problem is NP-hard, so a polynomial-time algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). Its parameterized complexity is settled positively by the \(\tau^\tau\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\)-time algorithm above.
