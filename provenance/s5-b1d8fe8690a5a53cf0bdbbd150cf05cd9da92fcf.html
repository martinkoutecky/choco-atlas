The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite firms \(F\), finite worker types \(T=S\cup U\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), type rankings over \(F\cup\{\varnothing\}\), and explicitly represented rational piecewise-affine mass-choice maps \(C_f:\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) satisfying \(C_f(x)\le x\) and the mass analogue of USC, does a stable allocation always exist, and can one be computed exactly in time polynomial in the input length? An allocation \(y\) splits each type’s mass among firms and the outside option, preserves \(\sum_f y_{t,f}=\mu_t\), and is stable when assignments are individually rational and \(C_f(a_f(y))=y_{\cdot,f}\) for every firm, where \(a_f(y)\) is the mass of types that weakly prefer \(f\) to their assignment.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(F\) be a finite set of \(m\) firms and \(T=S\sqcup U\) a common partition of worker types; either group may be empty. Each type has a strict total ranking \(\succ_t\) over \(\widetilde F=F\cup\{\varnothing\}\), with no monetary transfers.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each firm has a single-valued rational piecewise-affine map \(C_f:\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\), explicitly encoded by a finite partition into rational polyhedral cells and an affine formula on each cell; boundary values are specified and continuity is not required. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
The maps are promised to satisfy componentwise feasibility \(C_f(x)\le x\) and contraction consistency: \(C_f(x)\le z\le x\) implies \(C_f(z)=C_f(x)\).
Define \(R_f(x)=x-C_f(x)\). Mass USC means that, for each \(G\in\{S,U\}\), \(x\le x'\) and \(x_{T\setminus G}=x'_{T\setminus G}\) imply \(R_f(x)_G\le R_f(x')_G\); additionally, \(x_S=x'_S\) implies \(C_f(x)_S=C_f(x')_S\). These conditions hold for all nonnegative real inputs.
An allocation has real entries \(y_{t,g}\ge0\) for \(g\in\widetilde F\), with \(\sum_{g\in\widetilde F}y_{t,g}=\mu_t\). Write \(y_f=(y_{t,f})_{t\in T}\) and \(a_f(y)_t=\sum_{g\in\widetilde F:\,f\succeq_t g}y_{t,g}\).
It is individually rational if \(y_{t,f}=0\) whenever \(\varnothing\succ_t f\), and \(C_f(y_f)=y_f\) for every firm; it is stable if it is individually rational and \(C_f(a_f(y))=y_f\) for every firm.

Does every such instance admit a stable allocation? Is deciding existence in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard, and can a stable allocation, whenever one exists, be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For finite individual workers, USC guarantees a stable matching produced by two-stage worker-proposing deferred acceptance ([Huang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.12572)).
For continuum workers, existence is known under convexity and continuity of firms’ choices ([Che–Kim–Kojima](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/ECTA13547)); that result does not settle the stated class allowing discontinuous maps or its exact computational complexity.

  • Specified strict total worker rankings and individual rationality for both workers and firms, following the discrete definitions.
  • Corrected mass conservation to include the outside option, and defined availability by summing precisely the portions assigned to weakly worse options.
  • Specified an explicit rational polyhedral partition with affine formulas and boundary values; allowed discontinuities because continuity was not stipulated.

Partial solution: existence and a lower bound for exact computation

Every promised instance admits a stable allocation. Hence the promised existence decision problem is in $\mathrm P$: its answer is always yes.

Exact computation is more substantial. I prove that a stable allocation of polynomial binary length always exists, give an exact algorithm with potentially exponential running time, and establish a new computational reduction:

Computing a stable allocation is at least as hard as computing the exact values of stopping simple stochastic games—even with one worker group, continuous choices, a constant number of affine cells per firm, and a unique stable allocation.

This reduction does not establish NP-hardness or exclude a polynomial-time algorithm. General polynomial-time exact computation remains unresolved here.

1. A fixed-point lemma without continuity

Throughout, vector inequalities mean componentwise inequalities.

Lemma 1. Let
$$ B=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[0,M_i], $$
and let $\Phi:B\to B$ preserve order. Then $\Phi$ has a fixed point. No continuity assumption is needed.

Proof. Define
$$ E=\{q\in B:\Phi(q)\leq q\}. $$
Since $\Phi(M)\leq M$, this set is nonempty. Its coordinatewise infimum $q^*=\inf E$ belongs to $B$.

For every $q\in E$, monotonicity gives
$$ \Phi(q^*)\leq\Phi(q)\leq q. $$
Taking coordinatewise infima yields $\Phi(q^*)\leq q^*$. Consequently,
$$ \Phi(\Phi(q^*))\leq\Phi(q^*), $$
so $\Phi(q^*)\in E$. By the defining property of the infimum,
$$ q^*\leq\Phi(q^*). $$
Thus $\Phi(q^*)=q^*$. $\square$

2. Stable existence with one worker group

Consider a finite type set $G$, with positive masses $\mu_t$ whose sum need not be one. Suppose each firm has a choice map
$$ D_f:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{G}\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{G} $$
satisfying feasibility, contraction consistency, and monotone rejection:
$$ x\leq x' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x-D_f(x)\leq x'-D_f(x'). $$

Lemma 2. This market admits a stable allocation.

2.1 Worker choices

Use coordinates $(t,f)\in G\times F$, and define
$$ M_{t,f}=\mu_t, \qquad B=\prod_{(t,f)\in G\times F}[0,\mu_t]. $$

Given offers $p\in B$, allocate each type’s mass among acceptable firms in preference order. For an acceptable firm $f$, put
$$ H_{t,f}(p)=\sum_{g\in F:\,g\succ_t f}p_{t,g}, $$
and define
$$ W(p)_{t,f} = \min\{p_{t,f},(\mu_t-H_{t,f}(p))_+\}, \qquad r_+=\max\{r,0\}. $$
For an unacceptable firm, set $W(p)_{t,f}=0$.

To verify the formula, list the acceptable firms in preference order. After processing the first $j$ offers, remaining mass equals
$$ \left(\mu_t-\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{t,f_i}\right)_+. $$
Indeed, processing the next offer subtracts the smaller of that offer and the remaining mass. Induction proves the displayed identity.

Therefore $W(p)$ is nonnegative, assigns at most $\mu_t$ in each row, and assigns nothing to unacceptable firms.

Define worker rejection by $R_W(p)=p-W(p)$. At an acceptable coordinate,
$$ R_W(p)_{t,f} = \bigl(p_{t,f}-(\mu_t-H_{t,f}(p))_+\bigr)_+. $$
This expression cannot decrease when its own offer increases or when any better firm’s offer increases. Other offers do not affect it. At an unacceptable coordinate, rejection is simply $p_{t,f}$. Hence $R_W$ preserves order.

2.2 Coupled firm and worker choices

Combine firm choices into
$$ D(q)_f=D_f(q_f), \qquad R_D(q)=q-D(q). $$
Feasibility ensures that $R_D$ and $R_W$ map $B$ into $B$. Both preserve order.

Define
$$ \Phi(q)=M-R_W\bigl(M-R_D(q)\bigr). $$
The two maps obtained by subtracting a monotone rejection map from $M$ reverse order. Their composition $\Phi$ therefore preserves order.

By Lemma 1, choose $q^*\in B$ with $\Phi(q^*)=q^*$. Set
$$ p^*=M-R_D(q^*). $$
Then
$$ q^*=M-R_W(p^*). $$
Rearranging gives
$$ D(q^*)=q^*+p^*-M=W(p^*). $$

Define firm assignments by this common vector:
$$ y_{t,f}=D(q^*)_{t,f}=W(p^*)_{t,f}. $$
Assign remaining mass to $\varnothing$. Worker feasibility and worker individual rationality follow from the construction of $W$.

2.3 Stability

For an acceptable firm $f$, conservation and the greedy worker calculation give
$$ a_f(y)_t = \mu_t-\sum_{g\succ_t f}y_{t,g} = (\mu_t-H_{t,f}(p^*))_+. $$
Thus
$$ y_{t,f}=\min\{p^*_{t,f},a_f(y)_t\}, \qquad q^*_{t,f}=\mu_t-p^*_{t,f}+y_{t,f}. $$
In particular,
$$ q^*_{t,f}-a_f(y)_t = \mu_t-\max\{p^*_{t,f},a_f(y)_t\} \geq0, $$
because both arguments of the maximum lie in $[0,\mu_t]$.

For an unacceptable firm, all destinations receiving positive mass lie above that firm, so $a_f(y)_t=0$. Consequently, for every firm,
$$ y_f\leq a_f(y)\leq q^*_f. $$
Since $D_f(q^*_f)=y_f$, contraction consistency implies
$$ D_f(a_f(y))=y_f. $$
Applying consistency also at the intermediate input $y_f$ yields
$$ D_f(y_f)=y_f. $$
Hence $y$ is stable. $\square$

This proof uses neither continuity nor piecewise-affineness.

3. Two-stage existence under mass USC

We now apply Lemma 2 to the two groups in the question.

3.1 Allocate $S$

Define
$$ A_f(s)=C_f(s,0_U)_S. $$
Feasibility forces $C_f(s,0_U)_U=0_U$, and makes $A_f$ feasible.

If $A_f(s)\leq z\leq s$, then
$$ C_f(s,0_U)=(A_f(s),0_U) \leq(z,0_U)\leq(s,0_U). $$
Contraction consistency of $C_f$ gives $A_f(z)=A_f(s)$.

Monotone rejection for $A_f$ follows from mass USC for $S$, holding the $U$-input at zero. Thus Lemma 2 gives a stable allocation $y^S$. Write
$$ \alpha_f=a_f(y^S)_S. $$
Its stability means
$$ A_f(\alpha_f)=y_f^S. $$

3.2 Allocate $U$

Hold $\alpha_f$ fixed and define
$$ B_f(u)=C_f(\alpha_f,u)_U. $$
The independence clause of mass USC gives, for every $u\geq0$,
$$ C_f(\alpha_f,u)_S = C_f(\alpha_f,0_U)_S = y_f^S. $$
Therefore
$$ C_f(\alpha_f,u)=(y_f^S,B_f(u)). $$

Feasibility and monotone rejection for $B_f$ follow directly from the corresponding promises. To check consistency, suppose
$$ B_f(u)\leq v\leq u. $$
Because $y_f^S\leq\alpha_f$,
$$ C_f(\alpha_f,u) = (y_f^S,B_f(u)) \leq(\alpha_f,v)\leq(\alpha_f,u). $$
Consistency of $C_f$ therefore gives $B_f(v)=B_f(u)$.

Lemma 2 yields a stable allocation $y^U$. With
$$ \beta_f=a_f(y^U)_U, $$
we have $B_f(\beta_f)=y_f^U$.

3.3 Combine the allocations

Combine the two allocations, including their outside assignments. Availability is calculated separately for each type, so
$$ a_f(y)=(\alpha_f,\beta_f). $$
It follows that
$$ C_f(a_f(y)) = (y_f^S,B_f(\beta_f)) = y_f. $$
Worker individual rationality holds in both stages. Finally, $y_f\leq a_f(y)$ and consistency imply $C_f(y_f)=y_f$.

If either group is empty, omit its stage. If there are no firms, assign all mass to $\varnothing$.

Theorem 3. Every promised instance admits a stable allocation.

Decision consequence. On the promised input class, existence is decidable in constant time by answering yes. This does not include verifying the global promises.

4. Polynomial-size rational solutions and an exact finite algorithm

Let $K_f$ count firm $f$’s explicitly encoded affine pieces, including its boundary cases.

4.1 Fixing cells gives linear constraints

Choose one piece for each firm. Write its affine formula as
$$ C_f(x)=P_fx+b_f. $$
Impose the following constraints on allocation variables:

  • nonnegativity and $\sum_g y_{t,g}=\mu_t$;
  • $y_{t,f}=0$ for unacceptable firms;
  • membership of $a_f(y)$ in the selected piece;
  • the equations

$$ P_fa_f(y)+b_f=y_f. $$

Since $a_f(y)$ is linear in $y$, these are rational linear constraints, with any prescribed strict boundary inequalities retained.

Every solution is stable: the choice equations hold at the actual availabilities, and consistency supplies firm individual rationality because $y_f\leq a_f(y)$. Conversely, every stable allocation satisfies the system for its actual cell combination.

4.2 Strict inequalities

Introduce $\delta\in[0,1]$. Replace each strict inequality
$$ \ell_i(y)<b_i $$
by
$$ \ell_i(y)+\delta\leq b_i, $$
and maximize $\delta$.

The original system is feasible exactly when this auxiliary program is feasible with positive optimum. Indeed, finitely many positive slacks admit a common positive lower bound, while $\delta>0$ implies all original strict inequalities. With no strict inequalities, $\delta=1$ is available.

All allocation coordinates lie in $[0,1]$. Thus the auxiliary feasible set is closed and bounded, and any feasible optimization problem attains its optimum.

4.3 Binary length

Each selected system has polynomially many variables and polynomial encoding length in $m,\tau,L$.

An optimum can be chosen at a vertex. One way to obtain such a vertex is to successively minimize every coordinate over the optimal face: the final singleton is an extreme point. At an extreme point, active constraint normals span the variable space; otherwise a sufficiently small displacement in both directions along their common nullspace would preserve feasibility.

Consequently, a nonsingular square subsystem of active rational constraints determines the vertex. Clear denominators. If the resulting integer entries and right-hand sides have absolute value at most $2^B$, then determinants have absolute value at most
$$ n!\,2^{nB}. $$
Cramer’s rule therefore bounds every coordinate’s binary length by a polynomial in the system’s encoding length.

Theorem 3 guarantees a feasible cell combination. Its auxiliary optimum is positive, so an optimal rational vertex gives a stable allocation of polynomial binary length.

Exact rational linear programming is polynomial-time solvable; this standard algorithmic theorem applies to the explicitly constructed finite rational systems. See [Karger’s linear-programming algorithm notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

Proposition 4. Every promised instance has a rational stable allocation of polynomial binary length. Such an allocation can be verified in polynomial time.

Enumerating cell combinations gives an exact algorithm with running time
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\prod_{f\in F}K_f. $$
For fixed $m$, this is polynomial. For variable $m$, the product can be exponential.

5. New result: stopping stochastic games reduce to stable allocation

The reduction below establishes a computational lower bound for the outstanding search problem.

5.1 The source problem and its fixed-point equations

A stopping simple stochastic game has two terminal vertices, valued $0$ and $1$. Every other vertex has two successors and is one of:

  • a maximizing vertex;
  • a minimizing vertex;
  • a random vertex, choosing either successor with probability $1/2$.

The stopping promise says that, from every starting vertex, play reaches a terminal almost surely under every pair of strategies.

For successors $j,k$, define the Bellman map $H:[0,1]^n\to[0,1]^n$ by
$$ H_i(v)= \begin{cases} 0,&i\text{ is the zero terminal},\\ 1,&i\text{ is the one terminal},\\ \max\{v_j,v_k\},&i\text{ is maximizing},\\ \min\{v_j,v_k\},&i\text{ is minimizing},\\ (v_j+v_k)/2,&i\text{ is random}. \end{cases} $$
This is a continuous, order-preserving map. Lemma 1 gives a fixed point.

Its fixed point is unique. Suppose $v,w$ are fixed points and
$$ d=\max_i(v_i-w_i)>0, \qquad A=\{i:v_i-w_i=d\}. $$
No terminal belongs to $A$.

At a maximizing vertex $i\in A$, choose a successor $j$ attaining the maximum defining $v_i$. Then
$$ d=v_i-w_i \leq v_j-w_j \leq d, $$
so $j\in A$.

At a minimizing vertex $i\in A$, choose a successor $j$ attaining the minimum defining $w_i$. Again,
$$ d=v_i-w_i \leq v_j-w_j \leq d, $$
so $j\in A$.

At a random vertex in $A$, the average of the two successor differences equals $d$, while each difference is at most $d$. Hence both successors belong to $A$.

These selections define stationary strategies keeping play inside $A$ forever. This contradicts stopping. Thus $\max_i(v_i-w_i)\leq0$. Interchanging $v,w$ proves equality.

The fixed point gives the game values. Let the maximizing player always choose a successor attaining the maximum in $v$. Against any opposing strategy, conditional expected next-state value is at least current-state value: equality holds at maximizing and random vertices, and the inequality holds at minimizing vertices. Therefore expected state value after any finite number of steps is at least the starting value.

Make terminals absorbing. Almost-sure stopping implies that the difference between expected state value at time $k$ and the probability of eventual absorption at the one terminal has absolute value at most the probability of not having stopped by time $k$. This tends to zero. Thus the maximizing strategy guarantees winning probability at least $v_i$.

Choosing minimizing successors attaining the minimum gives the converse inequality. Hence $v_i$ is exactly the game value from vertex $i$.

This is the standard stopping-game model discussed in [Etessami–Papadimitriou–Rubinstein–Yannakakis, Section 6.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.03210). The argument above proves the fixed-point properties needed here.

5.2 A bounded-degree network of gates

Represent the Bellman equations by gates with operations
$$ 0,\quad 1,\quad \operatorname{id},\quad \min,\quad\max,\quad\operatorname{avg}, $$
where $\operatorname{avg}(a,b)=(a+b)/2$.

We require each gate to have at most two input wires and at most two output wires, with no wire connecting a gate to itself.

To achieve this, give every original game vertex one output wire leading to a new identity gate. Distribute its value through a binary tree of identity gates to every occurrence where it is used as an argument. Add one private readout consumer for each original vertex. Internal identity gates have one input and at most two outputs; readout consumers have no outputs.

There are only two argument occurrences per nonterminal game vertex. Thus the resulting network has polynomial size. All original gates have an output, all identity gates have an output, and no wire is a self-loop.

Identity equations propagate each original value through its distribution tree. Therefore fixed points of the enlarged network correspond bijectively to fixed points of the original Bellman equations.

5.3 Workers, masses, and rankings

Create one firm $f_v$ for every network vertex, including readout consumers.

Create one worker type $t_e$ for each wire $e:v\to w$. Let $q$ be the number of wires, and let $N$ be the smallest power of two with $N\geq q$. Give every wire type mass
$$ \lambda=\frac1N. $$
Add $N-q$ padding types, also of mass $\lambda$. The total mass is one, all masses are dyadic, and the support size is at most $2q$.

For a wire type $t_e$, prescribe
$$ f_v\succ_{t_e}f_w\succ_{t_e}\varnothing \succ_{t_e}\text{ every other firm}, $$
with the remaining firms strictly ordered arbitrarily. Padding types rank $\varnothing$ above every firm.

Put every type in $S$, and take $U=\varnothing$.

5.4 Firm choices

For a gate vertex $v$, let $I_v$ and $O_v$ be its incoming and outgoing wire sets. They are disjoint because there are no self-loops.

Let $H_v$ be its local gate operation. For each incoming wire, define
$$ b_e(x)=\left(1-\frac{x_{t_e}}{\lambda}\right)_+. $$
For nonnegative inputs, these quantities lie in $[0,1]$. Set
$$ \theta_v(x) = \lambda H_v\bigl((b_e(x))_{e\in I_v}\bigr). $$

The firm accepts incoming wire types completely and applies this common ceiling to its outgoing wire types:
$$ C_{f_v}(x)_{t_e} = \begin{cases} x_{t_e},&e\in I_v,\\ \min\{x_{t_e},\theta_v(x)\},&e\in O_v,\\ 0,&e\notin I_v\cup O_v. \end{cases} $$
It chooses zero padding mass.

A readout consumer accepts its incoming wire type completely and rejects all other types.

We now verify every choice promise globally.

Feasibility. Each chosen coordinate is nonnegative and at most its input.

Contraction consistency. Suppose
$$ C_{f_v}(x)\leq z\leq x. $$
Every incoming coordinate is completely accepted, so $z_{I_v}=x_{I_v}$. Consequently,
$$ \theta_v(z)=\theta_v(x). $$
For an outgoing coordinate, if $x_{t_e}\leq\theta_v(x)$, its chosen amount is $x_{t_e}$ and the inequalities force $z_{t_e}=x_{t_e}$. If $x_{t_e}>\theta_v(x)$, they force $z_{t_e}\geq\theta_v(x)$, so its chosen amount remains the ceiling. All other chosen coordinates remain zero.

Thus $C_{f_v}(z)=C_{f_v}(x)$. Readout consumers satisfy the same property immediately.

Monotone rejection. Every gate operation preserves order, while each $b_e$ reverses order. Hence $\theta_v$ reverses order. Rejection is
$$ R_{f_v}(x)_{t_e} = \begin{cases} 0,&e\in I_v,\\ (x_{t_e}-\theta_v(x))_+,&e\in O_v,\\ x_{t_e},&e\notin I_v\cup O_v. \end{cases} $$
If $x\leq x'$, the outgoing expression cannot decrease: its input coordinate increases and its ceiling cannot increase. Therefore rejection preserves order.

Since all types are in $S$, this is precisely the relevant mass USC condition. The independence clause is automatic when $U=\varnothing$.

Encoding and continuity. The formulas use only affine operations, minima, maxima, and positive parts, so they are continuous and rational piecewise-affine.

For each firm, split at incoming thresholds $x_{t_e}=\lambda$, at the comparison for a $\min$ or $\max$ gate, and at outgoing thresholds $x_{t_e}=\theta_v(x)$. On every preceding branch, each new comparison is affine. There are at most two incoming coordinates and two outgoing coordinates, so the number of cells, including separate boundary cells if used, is bounded by an absolute constant.

All other coordinates have a single fixed formula. Consequently, even encoding the full affine matrices explicitly gives polynomial total input length.

5.5 Stable allocations give gate fixed points

Let $y$ be a stable allocation in the constructed market.

For a wire type $t_e$ with $e:v\to w$, worker individual rationality permits assignments only to $f_v,f_w,\varnothing$. At its second-choice firm,
$$ a_{f_w}(y)_{t_e} = y_{t_e,f_w}+y_{t_e,\varnothing}. $$
That firm accepts this incoming coordinate completely. Stability therefore forces
$$ y_{t_e,\varnothing}=0. $$
Hence
$$ y_{t_e,f_w}=\lambda-y_{t_e,f_v}. $$

At its first-choice firm, availability is $\lambda$. Since $\theta_v\in[0,\lambda]$, stability gives
$$ y_{t_e,f_v} = \min\{\lambda,\theta_v(a_{f_v}(y))\} = \theta_v(a_{f_v}(y)). $$
All outgoing wires of $v$ therefore have the same first-choice assignment. Since every gate has an outgoing wire, define
$$ h_v=\frac{y_{t_e,f_v}}{\lambda} \qquad(e\in O_v). $$

For an incoming wire $e:u\to v$, its availability at $f_v$ is
$$ a_{f_v}(y)_{t_e} = \lambda(1-h_u). $$
Thus
$$ b_e(a_{f_v}(y))=h_u, $$
and the outgoing choice equation becomes
$$ h_v=H_v\bigl((h_u)_{e:u\to v}\bigr). $$
Therefore $h$ is a fixed point of the gate network. Restricting to original game vertices gives the game’s unique value vector.

5.6 Gate fixed points give stable allocations

Conversely, let $h$ be a gate-network fixed point. For every wire $e:v\to w$, assign
$$ y_{t_e,f_v}=\lambda h_v, \qquad y_{t_e,f_w}=\lambda(1-h_v), \qquad y_{t_e,\varnothing}=0. $$
Assign every padding type entirely to $\varnothing$.

These assignments conserve mass and satisfy worker individual rationality.

For an incoming wire $e:u\to v$, availability at $f_v$ is $\lambda(1-h_u)$, which the firm accepts completely. For an outgoing wire, availability is $\lambda$, and the fixed-point equation gives
$$ \theta_v(a_{f_v}(y)) = \lambda H_v((h_u)_{e:u\to v}) = \lambda h_v. $$
Thus its chosen amount is exactly its assignment. At unrelated firms, availability is zero because both assigned destinations lie above those firms. Padding availability is likewise zero everywhere.

Hence $C_f(a_f(y))=y_f$ for every firm. Consistency supplies firm individual rationality, so the allocation is stable.

5.7 Computational consequence

The two constructions are inverse. The stopping game has a unique fixed point; identity gates have uniquely determined values; and every wire allocation is determined by its source value. Therefore the constructed market has a unique stable allocation.

The construction has polynomial size. From its exact stable allocation, each original game value is recovered by dividing a first-choice wire assignment by $\lambda$.

Theorem 5. Exact value computation for stopping simple stochastic games polynomial-time reduces to exact stable-allocation computation in the promised class. The reduction uses:

  • $U=\varnothing$;
  • continuous choices;
  • an absolute constant number of affine cells per firm;
  • a unique stable allocation.

This establishes a lower bound for search. It does not establish NP-hardness, and it does not prove that polynomial-time search is impossible.

6. Another polynomial-time case: a common ranking

Suppose all supported types have the same complete ranking. List its acceptable firms as
$$ f_1\succ\cdots\succ f_k\succ\varnothing. $$
Initialize $r^0=\mu$. Sequentially set
$$ y_{f_i}=C_{f_i}(r^{i-1}), \qquad r^i=r^{i-1}-y_{f_i}. $$
Assign $r^k$ to $\varnothing$ and zero to unacceptable firms.

Feasibility keeps every residual nonnegative. At $f_i$, availability consists exactly of mass not assigned to earlier firms, so
$$ a_{f_i}(y)=r^{i-1}. $$
Thus its stability equation holds by construction. Unacceptable firms have zero availability and choose zero. Consistency supplies firm individual rationality.

There is also polynomial control of exact arithmetic. Start with a common denominator for all masses. At each step, multiply it by the denominators of the coefficients in the affine formula actually evaluated. Each firm is evaluated once, so the cumulative denominator has polynomial binary length bounded by the input coefficient lengths. Residuals and assignments lie in $[0,1]$, so their numerators have polynomial length as well.

Therefore this algorithm runs in polynomial time. In particular, it covers $\tau=1$ with arbitrary $m$.

7. What is settled and what remains open

The existence question is completely settled: every promised instance has a stable allocation, and promised existence is in $\mathrm P$.

Exact stable allocations have polynomial binary length and admit the finite cell-enumeration algorithm. Exact computation is polynomial for fixed $m$, and for a common ranking.

The new reduction shows that general exact computation already contains stopping stochastic-game value computation within a continuous, one-group subclass with unique allocations. A general polynomial-time algorithm or a stronger search-hardness classification is not proved here.
