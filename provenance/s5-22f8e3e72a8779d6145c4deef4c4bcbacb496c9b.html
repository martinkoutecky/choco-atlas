The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(r\) good kinds, two complete additive valuation types \(v^1,v^2\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^r\) of mass \(1/2\) each, and per-capita supply \(1/2\) of every kind, decide whether there is a finitely supported rational lottery over feasible profiles satisfying the following conditions, and construct one when it exists. A profile consists of masses \(x_{a,B}\ge 0\) on integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^r\), with \(\sum_B x_{a,B}=1/2\) for each type \(a\) and \(\sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_g=1/2\) for each good kind \(g\). Define \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a=\sup_{\pi:\,\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\pi}[B_g]=1/2\ \forall g}\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{B\sim\pi}v^a(B)\). In every profile in the lottery's support, each occupied type-\(a\) bundle \(B\) must satisfy \(v^a(B)\ge(4/5)\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a\), and every pair of occupied bundles \(B,C\) of types \(a,b\) must satisfy \(v^a(B)\ge v^a(C\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in C\) with \(v^a_g>0\). The lottery must also be ex-ante envy-free: for its expected masses \(\bar x\), \(\sum_B\bar x_{a,B}v^a(B)/(1/2)\ge\sum_B\bar x_{b,B}v^a(B)/(1/2)\) for all types \(a,b\). What is the complexity of deciding feasibility and constructing such a lottery, in \(r\), the valuation bit length, and the output size?

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here there are two indexed valuation types \(v^1,v^2\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{r}\), each of mass \(1/2\), so total mass is \(1\); there are \(m=r\ge1\) good kinds, each with per-capita supply \(1/2\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A profile is a finitely supported family \(x_{a,B}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), indexed by \(a\in\{1,2\}\) and integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{r}\), satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{a,B}=1/2\) and \(\sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_g=1/2\) for every kind \(g\). Bundle multiplicities have no prescribed upper bound; write \(v^a(B)=\sum_gv^a_gB_g\).
Define \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a=\sup_{\pi}\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{B\sim\pi}v^a(B)\), where \(\pi\) ranges over all probability distributions on \(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{r}\) satisfying \(\mathbb E_\pi[B_g]=1/2\) for every \(g\).
An admissible lottery has finitely many profiles \(x^{(k)}\) with positive rational probabilities \(p_k\) summing to \(1\). In every profile, each occupied bundle satisfies \(v^a(B)\ge(4/5)\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a\).
In every profile, every pair \((a,B),(b,C)\) with positive mass, including pairs within one type, satisfies \(v^a(B)\ge v^a(C-e_g)\) for every \(g\) with \(C_g\ge1\) and \(v^a_g>0\), where \(e_g\) is the unit vector for kind \(g\).

Is deciding whether an admissible lottery exists in P or NP-hard? What is the complexity of constructing one when it exists, with running times stated in \(m=r\), \(\tau=2\), \(L\), and the total binary output length \(S\)?

Result

Every instance in the question admits an admissible lottery. Thus the decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\).

For \(m=r\) and \(\tau=2\), one can construct a lottery with at most two profiles in \(O(m\log(m+1))\) arithmetic operations. A conservative bound using exact binary arithmetic is

\(O\!\left(mL^2+m\log(m+1)L+S\right)\)

bit operations. The constructed profiles use only bundles in \(\{0,1\}^m\), and their total output length is \(S=O(m)\) when bundles are encoded as incidence vectors.

The essential point is to prove a stronger guarantee for the ordered allocation procedure: its output satisfies \((4/5)\)-MMS relative to the continuous benchmark in this question.

1. Bounds on the continuous benchmark

Fix a valuation vector \(v\in\mathbb Q_{\geq 0}^m\). Write

\(T=\sum_{g=1}^m v_g\), and \(\mu(v)=\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}(v)\).

Consider any distribution allowed in the definition of \(\mu(v)\), and any almost-sure guaranteed value \(t\), so that \(v(B)\geq t\) almost surely. All expectations below are finite because every coordinate has expectation \(1/2\).

Expectation bound. Additivity gives

\(t\leq\mathbb E[v(B)]=T/2\).

Taking the supremum over all guaranteed values yields \(\mu(v)\leq T/2\).

Single-kind bound. For any kind \(g\), integrality and nonnegativity imply

\(\Pr(B_g\geq 1)\leq\mathbb E[B_g]=1/2\).

On the event \(B_g=0\), the remaining kinds must provide value at least \(t\). Consequently,

\((T-v_g)/2=\mathbb E[\sum_{h\neq g}v_hB_h]\geq t\Pr(B_g=0)\geq t/2\).

Thus

\(\mu(v)\leq T-v_g\).

Three-kind bound. Suppose three distinct kinds have values at most \(d>0\), and the sum of the values of all remaining kinds is \(R<d\). Then

\(\mu(v)\leq d+R\).

To prove this, let \(K\) count copies of those three kinds in a random bundle, and let \(W\) be the value contributed by the remaining kinds. Then

\(\mathbb E[K]=3/2\), \(\mathbb E[W]=R/2\), and \(v(B)\leq dK+W\).

The expectation bound gives \(t\leq T/2\leq(3d+R)/2<2d\). If \(t\leq d\), the desired bound already holds. Otherwise \(d<t<2d\), and pointwise

\(W\geq(t-d)(2-K)\).

Indeed, for \(K=0\), we have \(W\geq t\geq2(t-d)\); for \(K=1\), we have \(W\geq t-d\); and for \(K\geq2\), the right-hand side is nonpositive.

Taking expectations gives

\(R/2\geq(t-d)(2-\mathbb E[K])=(t-d)/2\),

so \(t\leq d+R\). Taking the supremum proves the claim.

These bounds apply to arbitrary distributions and arbitrary integral multiplicities; finite support is not assumed.

2. Ordered envy-cycle elimination meets the continuous benchmark

An instance is ordered if both agents value the indexed goods in nonincreasing order.

Consider the following allocation procedure on an ordered instance. Process goods in index order, give each good to an unenvied agent, and swap the two bundles whenever both agents envy. Here an agent is unenvied if the other agent values her own bundle at least as much as that agent’s bundle.

We prove that every complete allocation produced by this procedure is EFX and gives each agent value at least \((4/5)\mu(v)\).

EFX

Initially EFX holds.

Suppose good \(g\) is added to an unenvied agent’s bundle \(C\), while the other agent holds \(D\) and has valuation \(v\). Before the addition,

\(v(D)\geq v(C)\).

Every good \(h\) already in \(C\) was processed earlier, so \(v_h\geq v_g\). Hence, for every positively valued good \(h\) in the enlarged bundle,

\(v(C+e_g-e_h)\leq v(C)\leq v(D)\).

Thus the addition preserves EFX. The recipient’s own value increases, so her EFX comparisons also remain valid.

After a mutual-envy swap, each agent prefers her new bundle to the other bundle. The resulting allocation is therefore envy-free and hence EFX. This proves EFX throughout the procedure.

The continuous MMS guarantee

Fix one agent, with final bundle value \(u\), and let \(w\) be her value for the opposing bundle. Thus \(T=u+w\). Let \(k\) be the number of goods in the opposing bundle that she values positively.

If \(u\geq w\), then \(u\geq T/2\geq\mu(v)\). Hence assume \(u<w\).

Case \(k=0\). This cannot occur under \(u<w\), because then \(w=0\).

Case \(k=1\). If the opposing bundle’s sole positively valued kind is \(g\), then \(w=v_g\). The single-kind bound gives

\(\mu(v)\leq T-v_g=u\).

Case \(k\geq3\). Let \(\delta\) be the smallest positive value in the opposing bundle. EFX gives

\(u\geq w-\delta\).

Since \(\delta\leq w/k\), we obtain \(u\geq2w/3\). Therefore

\(T=u+w\leq5u/2\),

and consequently

\(u\geq2T/5\geq(4/5)\mu(v)\).

Case \(k=2\). Write the two positive values as \(h\geq b>0\), where \(b\) is the value of the later processed good. EFX, applied by removing the good of value \(b\), gives \(u\geq h\).

If \(b\leq u/2\), then

\(T=u+h+b\leq5u/2\),

which again proves \(u\geq(4/5)\mu(v)\).

It remains to treat \(b>u/2\). Immediately after the last mutual-envy swap, the fixed agent was envy-free. If there was no swap, use the initial empty allocation instead. Since she is envious in the final allocation, a positively valued good must have been added to the opposing bundle after that point. Thus the opposing bundle’s last positively valued addition—the good of value \(b\)—occurred after the last swap.

Immediately before that addition:

  • The opposing bundle had positive value \(h\).
  • The fixed agent held the bundle that subsequently became her final bundle.
  • Because the recipient was unenvied, the fixed agent’s current bundle had value at least \(h\).
  • Every processed good had value at least \(b\), by orderedness.

Her current bundle cannot contain two goods: their combined value would be at least \(2b>u\), whereas her value can only increase after the last swap. It therefore contains exactly one good, of some value \(d\). We have \(d\geq h\) and \(d\geq b\).

At that moment the processed goods are precisely these three goods of values \(d,h,b\). Every remaining positively valued good ultimately belongs to the fixed agent, since the opposing final bundle has only two positively valued goods. Their total value is

\(R=u-d<d\),

where the strict inequality follows from \(u<2b\leq2d\).

The three-kind bound now gives

\(\mu(v)\leq d+R=u\).

This completes every case and proves the stronger continuous MMS guarantee.

3. Computing two suitable ordered allocations

We use the ordered-stage guarantee of Garg–Sharma: their Algorithm 3 computes two complete allocations \(P,Q\), each generated by the ordered procedure above, such that for each agent \(a\),

\(\widehat v^a(P_a)+\widehat v^a(Q_a)\geq\sum_{j=1}^m\widehat v^a_j\).

The ordered stage takes \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations. This is Algorithm 3 and Lemmas 5–6 of [Garg–Sharma](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08986v2).

The hypotheses hold here: create two labeled discrete agents, one for each indexed type, and one discrete good for each kind. Sort each agent’s valuation row separately, obtaining the ordered vectors \(\widehat v^1,\widehat v^2\). Both valuations are nonnegative and additive. Identical valuation rows and zero values are allowed.

By Section 2, both ordered allocations are EFX and satisfy

\(\widehat v^a(P_a),\widehat v^a(Q_a)\geq(4/5)\mu(\widehat v^a)\).

A permutation of coordinates preserves \(\mu\): permuting a random bundle’s coordinates gives a bijection between the admissible distributions, because every coordinate has the same required expectation \(1/2\). Therefore

\(\mu(\widehat v^a)=\mu(v^a)\).

4. Returning to the original goods

For each ordered allocation, process its positions in order. At position \(j\), the designated recipient chooses her favorite remaining original good. Let \(A\) be the allocation obtained from \(P\), and \(C\) the allocation obtained from \(Q\).

We verify the properties needed from this transformation.

Own values increase

Fix agent \(a\). Suppose her ordered positions are \(i_1<\cdots<i_\ell\). When she picks at position \(i_s\), only \(i_s-1\) goods have been removed. Hence her favorite remaining good has value at least \(\widehat v^a_{i_s}\).

Her successive chosen values are nonincreasing, since the remaining set only shrinks. Thus her sorted chosen-value vector dominates, coordinate by coordinate, her sorted ordered-bundle value vector. In particular,

\(v^a(A_a)\geq\widehat v^a(P_a)\).

The same argument applies to \(C,Q\). Consequently, both mapped allocations retain the continuous MMS guarantee.

EFX is preserved for two agents

Continue to fix agent \(a\). Coordinatewise dominance of her own sorted value vectors implies that, for every threshold \(z\), her mapped bundle contains at least as many goods of value at least \(z\) as her ordered bundle does.

The total multiset of values is unchanged. Because there are exactly two agents, the opposing mapped bundle therefore contains at most as many goods above each threshold as the opposing ordered bundle. Its sorted value vector is consequently dominated coordinate by coordinate by the opposing ordered vector.

Let the mapped opposing bundle contain \(q\) positively valued goods, and the ordered opposing bundle contain \(\widehat q\) such goods. Then \(q\leq\widehat q\). If \(q>0\), removing any positively valued good from the mapped opposing bundle leaves value at most the sum of its \(q-1\) largest values. This is at most the sum of the ordered opposing bundle’s \(\widehat q-1\) largest positive values.

Ordered EFX bounds the latter sum by \(\widehat v^a(P_a)\), which is at most \(v^a(A_a)\). Thus \(A\) is EFX. The proof for \(C\) is identical.

Ex-ante envy-freeness

Own-value improvement and the ordered-stage inequality give

\(v^a(A_a)+v^a(C_a)\geq T_a\),

where \(T_a=\sum_gv^a_g\).

Since both allocations are complete, for \(b\neq a\),

\(v^a(A_b)+v^a(C_b)=2T_a-v^a(A_a)-v^a(C_a)\).

Therefore

\(v^a(A_a)+v^a(C_a)\geq v^a(A_b)+v^a(C_b)\).

The uniform lottery over \(A,C\) is ex-ante envy-free.

5. Constructing the mass profiles

For each allocation \(D\in\{A,C\}\), let \(\mathbf1_{D_a}\in\{0,1\}^m\) be the incidence vector of agent \(a\)’s bundle. Define the corresponding profile by

\(x^D_{a,\mathbf1_{D_a}}=1/2\),

with all other entries zero.

Each type has mass \(1/2\). Because \(D\) is a complete allocation, every kind belongs to exactly one bundle, so its total consumption is exactly \(1/2\).

Each occupied bundle satisfies the continuous MMS requirement proved above. Cross-type EFX follows from the discrete allocation’s EFX property. Each type occupies only one bundle; its within-type comparisons hold because removing a positively valued good decreases that bundle’s value.

Give the two profiles probability \(1/2\) each, merging them if they coincide. These probabilities and profile masses are rational.

Finally,

\(2\sum_B\bar x_{a,B}v^a(B)=\bigl(v^a(A_a)+v^a(C_a)\bigr)/2\).

The inequality established in Section 4 is therefore exactly the required ex-ante envy-freeness inequality for the mass formulation. All admissibility requirements are satisfied.

6. Running time and output size

Decision is always affirmative on the promised input class; reading the input takes \(O(L)\) time.

Construction uses sorting, the linear-time ordered stage, and two sequences of favorite choices. After sorting each agent’s preferences, favorite choices can be implemented with availability marks and monotonically advancing pointers. The total arithmetic-operation count is \(O(m\log(m+1))\).

For a binary-arithmetic bound, multiply valuations by the product of their input denominators. This product and every scaled valuation have \(O(L)\) bits; bundle-value sums also have \(O(L)\) bits. Schoolbook multiplication and division give an \(O(mL^2)\) preprocessing bound. Sorting and allocation then take \(O(m\log(m+1)L)\) bit operations. Writing the output takes \(O(S)\).

Thus, for \(m=r\) and \(\tau=2\), construction takes

\(O\!\left(mL^2+m\log(m+1)L+S\right)\)

bit operations. There are at most two profiles and four occupied bundle entries overall, each with a binary incidence vector and constant-size rational mass. Hence dense incidence-vector encoding gives \(S=O(m)\).
