The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(K\) rankers, a finite set of behavioral types \(\mathcal T\), rational population masses \(\mu_t\), and unknown type-specific response laws \(q_t(i,j)\), design an adaptive policy over horizon \(T\). Each comparison samples a fresh hidden type according to \(\mu\), then returns a preference drawn from that type's law, giving aggregate preference probability \(p^\mu_{ij}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_tq_t(i,j)\). Assuming \(p^\mu_{ij}\ne \tfrac12\) for distinct rankers, bound the policy's expected cumulative regret relative to an aggregate Copeland winner, using the paper's regret definition.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m:=K\ge 2\) be the number of rankers and \(T\ge 2\) an integer horizon. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) behavioral types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the bit length of the supplied input; express running times in \(m,\tau,L,T\) and relevant gap parameters.
Each type has an unknown stationary response law \(q_t(i,j)\in[0,1]\), with \(q_t(j,i)=1-q_t(i,j)\) and \(q_t(i,i)=\tfrac12\). In each round, an adaptive policy chooses a pair \((i,j)\), allowing \(i=j\); a fresh type is sampled independently according to \(\mu\), and an independent response conditional on that type and pair selects the first ranker with probability \(q_t(i,j)\). Only the response is observed.
Write \(p^\mu_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(i,j)\), and assume \(p^\mu_{ij}\ne\tfrac12\) whenever \(i\ne j\). Define normalized Copeland scores \(c_i\), their maximum \(c^*\), and \(L_C=(K-1)(1-c^*)\); any maximizer is an acceptable winner.
For the selected pairs \((I_s,J_s)\), cumulative regret is \(R_T=\sum_{s=1}^T(2c^*-c_{I_s}-c_{J_s})\). Define \(\Delta_{ij}=|p^\mu_{ij}-\tfrac12|\), \(\Delta_{\min}=\min_{i\ne j}\Delta_{ij}\), \(H_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\Delta_{ij}^{-2}\), and \(\Delta_i=\max\{c^*-c_i,1/(K-1)\}\).

Can an adaptive policy, without knowing the response laws or gaps, guarantee \(\mathbb E[R_T]=O\!\left(\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K\frac{H_i(1-c_i)}{\Delta_i^2}\log T\right)\) on every such instance, or is this guarantee unattainable?

Known results: SCB has this expected-regret guarantee for arbitrary stationary, tie-free finite preference matrices, also bounded by \(O\!\left(K(L_C+\log K)\Delta_{\min}^{-2}\log T\right)\). [Copeland Dueling Bandits](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.00312).
The stated hidden-type observation model is itself covered by that stationary-matrix model; consequently, the target above is already settled affirmatively, rather than open.

Conclusion

The guarantee is attainable. Run Scalable Copeland Bandits (SCB) on the observed comparisons. The hidden-type model induces exactly the stationary preference-matrix experiment covered by the stated SCB theorem.

The proof below uses that theorem as the supplied known result and verifies all hypotheses needed to apply it.

1. Definitions and induced preferences

Set \(K=m\), and define
\[ c_i=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf 1\!\left\{p^\mu_{ij}>\tfrac12\right\}, \qquad c^*=\max_i c_i. \]
Thus any index attaining \(c^*\) is an acceptable winner, and
\[ L_C=(m-1)(1-c^*) \]
is the number of losses of each winner.

The mixture matrix \(P^\mu=(p^\mu_{ij})\) satisfies
\[ 0\le p^\mu_{ij}\le1,\qquad p^\mu_{ji} =\sum_t\mu_t(1-q_t(i,j)) =1-p^\mu_{ij}, \qquad p^\mu_{ii}=\tfrac12. \]
Here the second equality uses \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). By assumption, its off-diagonal entries differ from \(\tfrac12\). Because there are finitely many such entries,
\[ \Delta_{\min}>0. \]
Consequently, every \(H_i\) is finite, and every \(\Delta_i\) is positive.

2. Equality of the adaptive observation experiments

Let \(\mathcal F_{s-1}\) contain the actions and observations before round \(s\), together with the policy’s randomness used to choose its next action. Condition on this information and the selected distinct pair \((I_s,J_s)=(i,j)\). Let \(X_s\) indicate that the response selects \(i\).

The new type is independent of this conditioning and has distribution \(\mu\). Conditional on that type and the pair, the response is an independent draw from the specified stationary response law. Therefore,
\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr(X_s=1\mid\mathcal F_{s-1},I_s=i,J_s=j) &=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau} \Pr(\text{type }t\mid\mathcal F_{s-1},I_s=i,J_s=j)\,q_t(i,j)\\ &=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_tq_t(i,j)\\ &=p^\mu_{ij}. \end{aligned} \]
A binary observation with this success probability has distribution \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p^\mu_{ij})\). For self-comparisons, no information is needed or used.

This conditional identity is sufficient under adaptivity. Indeed, compare two experiments using the same policy:

  • the hidden-type experiment;
  • an ordinary stationary dueling-bandit experiment with matrix \(P^\mu\).

They start with the same history distribution. If their history distributions agree through round \(s-1\), the policy gives the same conditional distribution of the next action in both experiments. The calculation above gives the same conditional observation distribution. Hence their history distributions agree through round \(s\). Induction proves equality for every finite horizon.

Their regret distributions also agree: regret is the same deterministic function of the selected pairs and the Copeland scores of \(P^\mu\).

3. Application of the SCB theorem

Theorem 13 of [*Copeland Dueling Bandits*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.00312) gives, for SCB on a stationary, tie-free matrix,
\[ \mathbb E[R_T] = O\!\left( \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{H_i(1-c_i)}{\Delta_i^2}\log T \right). \]
Its score, gap, and regret definitions coincide with those in this task.

Every hypothesis has now been checked:

  • \(P^\mu\) is a fixed finite preference matrix with complementary entries.
  • Its off-diagonal comparisons are tie-free.
  • Each new observation has the required conditional response law.
  • Self-comparisons are permitted.
  • Multiple Copeland winners are acceptable.

SCB uses observed comparisons and confidence calculations; it does not require the unknown response probabilities or gaps. By the equality of experiments proved above, its expected-regret guarantee transfers unchanged to the hidden-type model.

The same theorem also supplies
\[ \mathbb E[R_T] = O\!\left( m(L_C+\log m)\Delta_{\min}^{-2}\log T \right). \]
Moreover, \(0\le R_T\le2T\) deterministically, because every normalized score lies in \([0,1]\).

4. Observation and computational costs

At horizon \(T\), the policy uses \(T\) comparisons. It need not enumerate additional types, estimate individual response laws, or compute the entries of \(P^\mu\). Thus \(\tau\) introduces no multiplicative observation cost.

For clarity, a conservative computational operation count for the published SCB procedure is:

  • \(O(L)\) time to read the supplied input;
  • \(O(mT)\) bookkeeping operations and \(O(mT)\) scalar confidence calculations through horizon \(T\).

To justify the latter count, each completed simulated score observation consumes at least one actual comparison, so there are at most \(T\) such observations. A sweep updating confidence intervals and eliminating candidates can scan at most \(m\) arms; unfinished comparison tests also perform at most one update per actual observation. Epoch initialization is absorbed by the same bound.

This gives \(O(L+mT)\) runtime when scalar confidence calculations are counted as primitives. It is an operation-count bound, not a bit-complexity claim for evaluating transcendental confidence expressions. The observation and regret guarantees do not depend on that numerical implementation convention. The gap parameters enter the displayed regret bounds; they need not be supplied to the policy.

5. Resolution

The required adaptive policy exists: SCB, applied directly to the observed comparisons, achieves the requested bound on every instance satisfying the stated assumptions.
