Theorem 3 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings over acceptable types on the opposite side, find a rational flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_i\) and column sums \(\nu_j\). For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(P_{ij}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned to types it ranks below \(j\), and \(Q_{ij}\) the mass of type \(j\) assigned to types it ranks below \(i\); require \(P_{ij}Q_{ij}=0\). Among such stable flows, minimize \(d_A(x)+d_B(x)\), where \(d_A(x)=\max\{\operatorname{rank}_i(j):x_{ij}>0\}\) and \(d_B(x)\) is defined symmetrically.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,B\) be nonempty finite type supports. A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational; write \(\tau=|A|+|B|\), the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1\). Let \(m\) be the total preference-list length and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Each type strictly ranks a subset of opposite-side types. A pair is acceptable precisely when both types list each other; denote these pairs by \(E\). Ranks \(r_i(j),s_j(i)\) are positions starting at \(1\) in the supplied lists.
A flow has rational entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\), vanishes outside \(E\), and satisfies \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\nu_j\).
For \((i,j)\in E\), define \(P_{ij}=\sum_{k:(i,k)\in E,\ r_i(k)>r_i(j)}x_{ik}\) and \(Q_{ij}=\sum_{h:(h,j)\in E,\ s_j(h)>s_j(i)}x_{hj}\). The flow is stable when \(P_{ij}Q_{ij}=0\) for every acceptable pair. Promise that a stable flow exists.
Define \(d_A(x)=\max_{x_{ij}>0}r_i(j)\) and \(d_B(x)=\max_{x_{ij}>0}s_j(i)\); the optimization problem finds a stable flow minimizing \(d_A(x)+d_B(x)\).

Is this optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete stable marriage with strict incomplete lists, minimizing the sum of the two sides’ worst assigned ranks is polynomial-time solvable ([Cooper–Manlove](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10875)).
For real-valued stable allocation with edge capacities and vertex quotas, a polynomial-time algorithm finds a stable allocation ([Cseh–Skutella](https://arxiv.org/html/1211.2169)); this establishes a nearby feasibility result, not this objective’s complexity. No result settling this precise optimization problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made acceptability mutual and required zero flow on unacceptable pairs, as in the discrete matching definition.
  • Fixed ranks as positions starting at one in the supplied preference lists, retaining any entries that are not mutually acceptable.
  • Promised existence of a fully assigned stable flow, carrying over the discrete definition’s restriction to agents assigned in stable matchings.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size, m as total preference-list length, and L as total input bit length.

Result

The optimization problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The algorithm enumerates the two rank bounds. For each pair of bounds, it finds a stable allocation on a suitably truncated graph and checks whether every quota is filled. The crucial fact, proved below, is that if a complete stable allocation exists on that graph, then every stable allocation on it is complete.

1. Auxiliary allocations and stability

Let \(F\subseteq E\). On the bipartite graph with edge set \(F\), give each vertex its original mass as its quota:
\(q(i)=\mu_i\) and \(q(j)=\nu_j\).
Give every edge capacity \(2\), and restrict each preference order to the remaining edges.

An auxiliary allocation \(z\) satisfies nonnegativity and the quota inequalities
\(\sum_j z_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i z_{ij}\leq\nu_j\).
Call it complete when every quota inequality is an equality.

Write \(z(v)\) for the total allocation incident to \(v\). Say that \(v\) desires an incident edge \(e\) under \(z\) when either:

  • \(z(v)<q(v)\); or
  • some edge strictly worse than \(e\) at \(v\) has positive allocation.

Every auxiliary allocation has total mass at most \(1\), so every edge allocation is at most \(1\), strictly below its capacity \(2\). Consequently, standard stable-allocation stability says precisely that no edge is desired by both endpoints. This agrees with the blocking-edge definition in [Cseh–Skutella, Definition 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1211.2169).

For a complete allocation on \(E\), the endpoints of \((i,j)\) desire that edge exactly when \(P_{ij}>0\) and \(Q_{ij}>0\), respectively. Thus auxiliary stability agrees with the task’s stability condition for complete allocations.

2. Completeness is invariant when a complete stable allocation exists

Lemma. Fix \(F\subseteq E\). If this auxiliary instance has a complete stable allocation \(x\), then every stable auxiliary allocation \(y\) is complete.

Proof. Suppose \(y\) is incomplete. Its total allocated mass is then less than \(1\): if its total were \(1\), the quota inequalities and the quota sums \(1\) on both sides would force every quota to be filled. In particular, there is a vertex \(i_0\in A\) with \(y(i_0)<\mu_{i_0}\).

Construct a weighted directed graph on \(A\cup B\). For every edge \((i,j)\in F\):

  • if \(x_{ij}>y_{ij}\), put an arc \(i\to j\) of weight \(x_{ij}-y_{ij}\);
  • if \(y_{ij}>x_{ij}\), put an arc \(j\to i\) of weight \(y_{ij}-x_{ij}\);
  • otherwise put no arc.

We establish the following properties for vertices reachable from \(i_0\):

  • At every reachable \(i\in A\), \(y\) desires every edge corresponding to an outgoing arc \(i\to j\).
  • Every reachable \(j\in B\) has \(y(j)=\nu_j\).

The first property holds at \(i_0\), because its quota is unfilled.

Now suppose the first property holds at a reached vertex \(i\), and consider an outgoing arc \(i\to j\), corresponding to edge \(e=(i,j)\). Stability of \(y\) implies that \(j\) does not desire \(e\). Therefore \(y(j)=\nu_j\), and every edge with positive \(y\)-allocation at \(j\) is at least as good as \(e\). This establishes the second property at \(j\).

Consider any outgoing arc \(j\to h\), corresponding to \(f=(h,j)\). We have \(y_f>x_f\), so \(y_f>0\). Also \(f\neq e\), since their difference signs are opposite. Strict preferences therefore imply that \(j\) prefers \(f\) to \(e\). Since \(x_e>y_e\geq0\), we have \(x_e>0\), so \(j\) desires \(f\) under \(x\).

Stability of \(x\) implies that \(h\) does not desire \(f\). Hence every edge with positive \(x\)-allocation at \(h\) is at least as good as \(f\). For any outgoing arc \(h\to k\), its edge \(g=(h,k)\) satisfies \(x_g>0\) and \(g\neq f\). Thus \(h\) strictly prefers \(g\) to \(f\). Because \(y_f>0\), \(h\) desires \(g\) under \(y\). This establishes the first property at \(h\).

Induction along directed paths proves both properties.

Let \(S\) be the set of vertices reachable from \(i_0\). No arc leaves \(S\). Define the divergence of a vertex as its total outgoing arc weight minus its total incoming arc weight.

For \(i\in A\), completeness of \(x\) gives
\(\operatorname{div}(i)=\sum_j(x_{ij}-y_{ij})=\mu_i-y(i)\geq0\).
At \(i_0\), this inequality is strict.

For every \(j\in S\cap B\), the second property gives
\(\operatorname{div}(j)=\sum_i(y_{ij}-x_{ij})=y(j)-\nu_j=0\).

It follows that
\(\sum_{v\in S}\operatorname{div}(v)>0\).

On the other hand, internal arcs cancel in this sum. Since no arc leaves \(S\), the sum equals the negative of the total weight of arcs entering \(S\), and is therefore nonpositive. This is a contradiction.

Thus \(y\) is complete. \(\square\)

3. Fixed rank bounds reduce to completeness

For positive integers \(a,b\), define
\(F_{a,b}=\{(i,j)\in E:r_i(j)\leq a,\ s_j(i)\leq b\}\).
Rank comparisons and objective values always refer to the original supplied lists.

Proposition. There exists a stable flow \(x\) in the original instance with \(d_A(x)\leq a\) and \(d_B(x)\leq b\) if and only if the auxiliary instance on \(F_{a,b}\) has a complete stable allocation.

Proof.

Suppose first that \(x\) is such an original stable flow. Its positive entries lie in \(F_{a,b}\), so it is a complete allocation there. For any retained edge, the worse-edge sums are unchanged, because every deleted edge has allocation zero. Hence \(x\) is stable on the restricted graph.

Conversely, let \(x\) be a complete stable allocation on \(F_{a,b}\), extended by zero to \(E\). It satisfies the original flow equalities and the desired rank bounds.

For a retained edge, auxiliary stability gives \(P_{ij}Q_{ij}=0\). For a deleted edge \((i,j)\), at least one of the following holds:

  • \(r_i(j)>a\). Every positive assignment of \(i\) has rank at most \(a\), so \(P_{ij}=0\).
  • \(s_j(i)>b\). Every positive assignment of \(j\) has rank at most \(b\), so \(Q_{ij}=0\).

Thus every original acceptable pair satisfies the stability condition. \(\square\)

To test the bounds, compute any stable auxiliary allocation \(y\) on \(F_{a,b}\). If \(y\) is complete, the proposition supplies a bounded stable flow. If \(y\) is incomplete, the lemma rules out every complete stable allocation on that graph, and the proposition rules out every bounded stable flow.

4. Algorithm and optimality

Let \(R_A\) and \(R_B\) be the maximum supplied list lengths on the two sides. The promise implies that both are positive. Since lists contain distinct opposite-side types,
\(R_A\leq|B|\) and \(R_B\leq|A|\).

Use the following algorithm:

  • Enumerate every \(a\in\{1,\ldots,R_A\}\) and \(b\in\{1,\ldots,R_B\}\).
  • Construct the auxiliary instance on \(F_{a,b}\).
  • Compute a stable allocation, starting from the zero allocation.
  • Retain the pair and its allocation exactly when every quota is filled.
  • Return an allocation retained for a pair minimizing \(a+b\).

The stable-allocation computation is polynomial: [Cseh–Skutella, Theorem 5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1211.2169) gives an algorithm taking \(O(|V|^2|F|)\) time in the arithmetic model, for every real-valued stable-allocation instance and every feasible initial allocation. Here the graph is bipartite, quotas and capacities are nonnegative, incident preferences are strict, and the zero allocation is feasible. Thus its hypotheses hold. Empty edge sets can be rejected directly.

To prove optimality, let \(C\) be the minimum original objective. Such a minimum exists because the promised feasible set is nonempty and the objective takes values in a finite set of integers.

An optimal flow makes the pair \((d_A(x),d_B(x))\) feasible, so the minimum retained bound sum \(M\) satisfies \(M\leq C\). The allocation \(y\) returned for that pair is an original stable flow and satisfies \(d_A(y)+d_B(y)\leq M\). By the definition of \(C\),
\(C\leq d_A(y)+d_B(y)\leq M\leq C\).
All inequalities are therefore equalities, proving that the returned flow is optimal.

5. Binary encoding and polynomial bit complexity

It remains to justify polynomial time in the binary input length, without expanding masses into individual agents.

Let \(D\) be the product of all denominators in the input masses. Its binary length is at most the sum of the binary lengths of those denominators, hence \(O(L)\). Scale all quotas and edge capacities by \(D\). The scaled quotas are integers, and every edge capacity is \(2D\). Scaling allocations back by \(D\) preserves completeness, positive support, and stability.

The accelerated algorithm in [Cseh–Skutella, Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1211.2169) uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of allocation and residual quantities. Its auxiliary dummy quotas and capacities are also formed by sums and maxima. Consequently, with scaled integer input, all allocation quantities remain integers. Quotas, capacities, allocations, and the sums used in its operations have magnitudes bounded by a polynomial in \(\tau\) times \(D\), so they have \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits.

There are at most \(R_AR_B\leq|A||B|\leq\tau^2\) bound pairs. Each instance has \(\tau\) original vertices and at most \(|E|\leq m\) original edges. Allowing for the algorithm’s auxiliary vertices and edges gives a conservative total bound of \(O(\tau^4(m+\tau))\) arithmetic and comparison operations, on numbers of polynomial bit length.

Therefore the entire optimization algorithm, including construction of its rational output, runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).
