The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant and position type sets \(A,B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to one on each side, mutually acceptable pairs, strict preference orders over acceptable types, and a target matched mass \(\rho\), choose a rational transport matrix \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with row sums at most \(\mu_a\), column sums at most \(\nu_b\), zero flow on unacceptable pairs, and total flow at least \(\rho\). For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(W_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) that is unmatched or assigned to a type ranked below \(b\), and define \(W_{ba}(x)\) symmetrically; minimize \(\sum_{(a,b)} W_{ab}(x)W_{ba}(x)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,B\) be disjoint, nonempty applicant and position type sets, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\) and strict preferences over acceptable types; every acceptable assignment is preferred to being unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational. Masses satisfy \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Put \(m=|B|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Given rational \(\rho\in[0,1]\), feasible transports are rational matrices \(x\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), \(\sum_ax_{ab}\leq\nu_b\), \(x_{ab}=0\) outside \(E\), and \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\geq\rho\); restrict attention to instances admitting a feasible transport.
For \((a,b)\in E\), define \(W_{ab}(x)=\mu_a-\sum_{c:(a,c)\in E}x_{ac}+\sum_{c:(a,c)\in E,\ b\succ_a c}x_{ac}\) and \(W_{ba}(x)=\nu_b-\sum_{d:(d,b)\in E}x_{db}+\sum_{d:(d,b)\in E,\ a\succ_b d}x_{db}\).
The objective is to minimize \(Q(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}W_{ab}(x)W_{ba}(x)\).
Let \(D\) be the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of all masses, and use scaled cost \(C(x)=D^2Q(x)\). Given an integer budget \(\beta\geq1\) and a computable \(F:\mathbb N_{>0}\to\mathbb N_{>0}\) with \(F\geq1\), the gap problem distinguishes existence of a feasible \(x\) with \(C(x)\leq\beta\) from every feasible \(x\) having \(C(x)>F(\beta)\beta\).

Does this gap problem admit an \(f(\beta)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\)-time algorithm for some computable \(F\), or is it \(\mathsf{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by \(\beta\) for every such \(F\)?

For discrete strict incomplete stable marriage, minimizing blocking pairs subject to a matching-size lower bound has \(\mathsf{W}[1]\)-hard gaps for every computable factor depending on the blocking-pair budget, even when a perfect matching is required ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10129v1)).
For unit-capacity fractional marriage without ties, maximizing welfare or the number of fully matched agents under ordinal stability is polynomial-time solvable; these are different objectives ([source](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)). No result settling the product-cost gap problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made explicit that every acceptable assignment is preferred to being unmatched, as in the discrete definition.
  • Specified the target matched mass as a binary rational in the interval from zero to one and restricted approximation instances to those with a feasible transport.
  • Defined m as the position support size, tau as the combined support size, and L as the total input bit length.
  • The continuous question omitted the approximation parameter. Used the least common denominator D of the masses to scale the objective by D squared, and introduced a positive integer budget beta; equal masses of 1/N then recover blocking-pair counts on integral transports.
  • Used a strict upper gap boundary so the promise cases are disjoint, including for factor one; the anchor's construction establishes this stronger boundary.

Result

For every computable \(F:\mathbb N_{>0}\to\mathbb N_{>0}\) with \(F\geq1\), the gap problem is \(\mathsf{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by \(\beta\).

This holds even when \(\rho=1\) and all supported types have the same dyadic mass. The proof combines a rounding bound with increased repetition in the cited discrete reduction.

1. A rounding bound for fractional perfect matchings

Let \(G=(U,V,E)\) be a bipartite acceptability graph with strict preferences and \(|U|=|V|\). A fractional perfect matching is a nonnegative matrix \(y\), supported on \(E\), whose row and column sums are all one.

For each acceptable pair, define

\(w_{uv}(y)=\sum_{v':\,v\succ_u v'}y_{uv'}\),
\(w_{vu}(y)=\sum_{u':\,u\succ_v u'}y_{u'v}\),

and put \(c(y)=\sum_{uv\in E}w_{uv}(y)w_{vu}(y)\).

Lemma. There is an integral perfect matching \(M\) with at most \(\sqrt{|E|c(y)}\) blocking pairs.

Proof. First, \(y\) is a convex combination of integral perfect matchings supported on \(E\). Here is a direct justification.

For any \(S\subseteq U\), the positive support of \(y\) satisfies

\(|S|=\sum_{u\in S,v}y_{uv}\leq\sum_{v\in N(S),u}y_{uv}=|N(S)|\).

Thus Hall’s theorem supplies a perfect matching \(M_0\) in that support. Set \(\lambda=\min_{uv\in M_0}y_{uv}>0\). If \(\lambda=1\), then \(y\) is its incidence matrix. Otherwise,

\(y'=(y-\lambda\mathbf1_{M_0})/(1-\lambda)\)

is again a fractional perfect matching, with strictly fewer positive entries. Induction gives the required finite convex decomposition.

Choose a random perfect matching \(\boldsymbol M\) according to this decomposition. Its edge marginals are \(y\). For an acceptable pair \(uv\), let \(A_{uv}\) be the event that \(u\) receives a partner strictly worse than \(v\), and let \(B_{uv}\) be the analogous event for \(v\). Then

\(\Pr(A_{uv})=w_{uv}(y)\), and \(\Pr(B_{uv})=w_{vu}(y)\).

The pair blocks precisely when both events occur. No independence assumption is needed: for nonnegative \(s,t\), \(\min(s,t)\leq\sqrt{st}\), so

\(\Pr(uv\text{ blocks }\boldsymbol M)\leq\min(w_{uv}(y),w_{vu}(y))\leq\sqrt{w_{uv}(y)w_{vu}(y)}\).

Writing \(b(M)\) for the number of blocking pairs and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\(\mathbb E[b(\boldsymbol M)]\leq\sum_{uv\in E}\sqrt{w_{uv}(y)w_{vu}(y)}\leq\sqrt{|E|c(y)}\).

Some matching in the decomposition has at most this expectation. This proves the lemma.

Consequently, if every integral perfect matching has at least \(R\) blocking pairs, then every fractional perfect matching satisfies

\(c(y)\geq R^2/|E|\).

2. The discrete construction with variable repetition

We use these properties of the construction in [Chen, Roy and Simola, Section 3, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10129v1#S3).

For a simple graph with \(n\) vertices and \(q\) edges, let \(H=\binom h2\) and \(\beta=h+H\). With any positive integer repetition count \(R\), their strict marriage instance has equal side sizes

\(N=h+H+2n+q+4qR\),

and exactly

\(s=2hn+3n+(2H+1)q+12qR\)

acceptable pairs. If the graph has an \(h\)-clique, some perfect matching has at most \(\beta\) blocking pairs; otherwise every perfect matching has at least \(R\). Claims 1–3 establish these statements: the prescribed numerical choice of repetition count enters only the final gap comparison. The edge count follows by summing the applicant preference-list lengths.

When \(n\geq h\) and \(q\geq H\), a perfect matching exists independently of clique existence: select arbitrary \(h\) vertex gadgets and \(H\) edge gadgets for the selectors, and use Claim 1’s internal matchings according to edge selection. Incidence affects blocking pairs, not whether these assignments form a perfect matching.

3. Choosing the amplification

Fix the function \(F\). Reduce from \(\mathsf{Clique}\), parameterized by \(h\), restricted to \(h\geq3\).

We may ensure \(n\geq h\) and \(q\geq H\): append a disjoint complete bipartite graph with \(H\) vertices in each part. This adds no clique of size \(h\), because \(h\geq3\), and it ensures both inequalities. Its size depends polynomially on \(h\), so this preprocessing preserves parameterized hardness.

Define

\(T=F(\beta)\beta\),
\(c_0=h+H+2n+q\),
\(b_0=2hn+3n+(2H+1)q\),
\(a=16q\), and \(b=b_0+c_0\).

Choose the repetition count

\(R=T(a+b)+1\).

Apply the discrete construction with this \(R\). It has \(N=c_0+4qR\) agents per side and \(s=b_0+12qR\) acceptable pairs.

For dyadic normalization, let \(P\) be the smallest power of two with \(P\geq N\). Add \(P-N\) isolated acceptable pairs, each consisting of one new applicant and one new position. Each new type accepts only its paired partner. Let \(E'\) denote the resulting acceptability relation.

Every perfect matching necessarily matches these new pairs together. Therefore padding preserves the blocking-pair count of every original perfect matching. Since \(P<2N\),

\(|E'|=s+(P-N)\leq s+N=aR+b\leq(a+b)R\).

Our choice of \(R\) consequently gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{R^2}{|E'|}\geq\frac{R}{a+b}>T.\)

This is the numerical inequality needed to withstand fractional rounding.

4. Constructing the society and identifying its cost

Use the padded applicants and positions as distinct supported types. Give every type mass \(1/P\), preserve its preference list, and set \(\rho=1\).

These masses are positive dyadic rationals, and each side has total mass one. Their reduced denominators are all \(P\), so the problem’s scaling denominator is exactly \(D=P\).

A perfect matching exists by Section 2 and remains available after padding. Its normalized incidence matrix is a rational feasible transport, so the required feasibility promise holds.

Now consider any feasible transport \(x\). Its total assigned mass is at least one and at most one, hence exactly one. Every row must attain its capacity: the nonnegative row deficits sum to zero. The same reasoning applies to columns. Thus

\(y=Px\)

is a fractional perfect matching on \(E'\).

There is no unmatched mass. Consequently the willingness terms obey

\(P W_{uv}(x)=w_{uv}(y)\), and \(P W_{vu}(x)=w_{vu}(y)\).

Therefore the scaled objective is exactly the unit-capacity product objective:

\(C(x)=P^2Q(x)=\sum_{uv\in E'}w_{uv}(y)w_{vu}(y)=c(y)\).

For an integral perfect matching, each willingness term is either zero or one. Its product for \(uv\) equals one precisely when both endpoints prefer one another to their assigned partners. Hence \(c(\mathbf1_M)=b(M)\).

5. Completeness and soundness

Completeness. Suppose the graph has an \(h\)-clique. The discrete construction has a perfect matching \(M\) with \(b(M)\leq\beta\). Extend it across the isolated padding pairs and take \(x=\mathbf1_M/P\). Then \(x\) is rational and feasible, and

\(C(x)=b(M)\leq\beta\).

Soundness. Suppose the graph has no \(h\)-clique. Every original integral perfect matching has at least \(R\) blocking pairs. Since padding contributes no blocking pairs, every padded integral perfect matching also has at least \(R\).

For any feasible transport \(x\), apply the rounding lemma to \(y=Px\). It supplies an integral perfect matching \(M\) satisfying

\(R\leq b(M)\leq\sqrt{|E'|C(x)}\).

It follows that

\(\displaystyle C(x)\geq\frac{R^2}{|E'|}>T=F(\beta)\beta.\)

Thus the reduction produces the required strict NO gap for every feasible rational transport. In fact, the argument also covers real transports.

6. Parameter and running time

The output budget is \(\beta=h+\binom h2\), depending only on the clique parameter.

Evaluating \(F(\beta)\) takes computable time depending only on \(h\). The quantities \(a+b\) are polynomial in the graph size and \(h\), and \(R=F(\beta)\beta(a+b)+1\). Therefore the number of output types and preference entries is bounded by a computable function of \(h\) times a polynomial in the original input size. Each mass \(1/P\) has a binary encoding of length \(O(\log P)\).

The entire construction is consequently a parameterized gap reduction. This proves \(\mathsf{W}[1]\)-hardness for every specified computable \(F\). An algorithm with the proposed running time for any such \(F\) would imply \(\mathsf{FPT}=\mathsf{W}[1]\).
