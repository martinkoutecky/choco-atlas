The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite schools \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of student types, rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings \(P_t\), weak school priorities over types, and rational capacity shares \(q_c\) with \(\sum_c q_c\ge 1\), compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_c x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,c}\le q_c\). Require stability, meaning there are no \(t,u,c,d\) with \(x_{t,d}>0\), \(x_{u,c}>0\), \(cP_t d\), and \(t\succ_c u\), and require that no other stable mass assignment gives every type a weakly first-order stochastically better school distribution and some positive-mass type a strict improvement. Realize \(x\) as a type-symmetric lottery with per-person probabilities \(p_{t,c}=x_{t,c}/\mu_t\); determine whether this lottery is ex ante stable and is not ordinally dominated by any ex ante-stable lottery, including lotteries asymmetric within types.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) listed types \(t\in T\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a strict ranking \(P_t\) of all schools; each school has a weak priority order over types, whose strict part is \(\succ_c\). Students within a type are tied at every school. Capacities \(q_c\geq0\) are binary rationals with \(\sum_cq_c\geq1\); \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A feasible mass assignment satisfies \(x_{t,c}\geq0\), \(\sum_cx_{t,c}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_tx_{t,c}\leq q_c\). It is stable if no \(t,u,c,d\) satisfy \(x_{t,d}>0\), \(x_{u,c}>0\), \(cP_td\), and \(t\succ_cu\). Stability imposes no additional condition concerning vacant seats.
Write \(p_{t,c}=x_{t,c}/\mu_t\). Call \(x\) constrained efficient if no other feasible stable mass assignment weakly first-order stochastically improves every \(p_t\) and strictly improves at least one.
Represent each type by an atomless interval \(I_t\) of measure \(\mu_t\). A lottery is a probability distribution over measurable assignments of every student to a school, respecting capacities almost surely; its measurable marginals \(r_{i,c}\) may vary within types. It is ex ante stable if, whenever \(cP_td\) and \(t\succ_cu\), the sets \(\{i\in I_t:r_{i,d}>0\}\) and \(\{j\in I_u:r_{j,c}>0\}\) cannot both have positive measure. Ordinal dominance means weak first-order stochastic improvement almost everywhere and strict improvement on a set of positive measure.
The type-symmetric lottery associated with \(x\) has marginals \(r_{i,c}=p_{t,c}\) for almost every \(i\in I_t\); equal treatment is required within each listed type.
Can a rational constrained-efficient stable mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this search problem NP-hard? Does every such assignment admit a type-symmetric lottery realization that is ex ante stable and ordinally undominated by all ex ante-stable lotteries, including those asymmetric within types?
In the discrete model, averaging a constrained-efficient stable matching uniformly within groups of equals produces an ex ante-stable, ordinally undominated lottery satisfying equal treatment of equals; a polynomial-time construction is also stated ([Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06721v2)).
A related continuum model has an ordinally dominant outcome within the stronger stability class that also excludes discrimination among equal-priority students, under full preference support and balanced capacities ([Kesten–Ünver](https://lies.mat.uc.cl/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/A-theory-of-theory-of-school-choice-lotteries.pdf)). Result

A rational constrained-efficient stable mass assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Every such assignment admits the lottery realization described in the question, including undominatedness against asymmetric ex ante-stable lotteries.

The construction uses a polynomial-time stable-allocation algorithm for initialization, followed by at most \(\tau(m-1)+1\) linear programs. The efficiency argument and the bound on the number of linear programs are proved below.

For lotteries, I use the standard measurable interpretation: the assignment indicator is jointly measurable in the student and the lottery draw, so Tonelli’s theorem applies.

1. Cumulative masses and admissible pairs

Let \(\rho_t(c)\) be the position of school \(c\) in type \(t\)’s ranking, with the best school having position \(1\). Define

\(\displaystyle F_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{\rho_t(c)\leq k}x_{t,c},\qquad 1\leq k\leq m.\)

Because each \(\mu_t\) is fixed and positive, stochastic improvement of the normalized probabilities is equivalent to improvement of these cumulative masses. Write \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\) when

\(\displaystyle F_{t,k}(y)\geq F_{t,k}(x)\quad\text{for every }t,k.\)

Strict improvement means that at least one of these inequalities is strict.

Define the set of types demanding school \(c\) at \(x\) by

\(\displaystyle D_c(x)=\{t:\text{there is a school }d\text{ with }cP_td\text{ and }x_{t,d}>0\}.\)

Define the admissible type-school pairs by

\(\displaystyle A(x)=\{(t,c):\text{there is no }v\in D_c(x)\text{ with }v\succ_c t\}.\)

Two elementary facts will be used repeatedly.

Demand monotonicity. If \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), then \(D_c(y)\subseteq D_c(x)\) for every school \(c\).

Indeed, \(t\notin D_c(x)\) means that all of type \(t\)’s mass is assigned to schools weakly preferred to \(c\). The corresponding cumulative mass is already \(\mu_t\), so stochastic improvement leaves no mass below \(c\).

Stability under admissible support. If \(x\) is stable, \(y\) is feasible, \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), and \(y_{t,c}=0\) outside \(A(x)\), then \(y\) is stable.

To prove this, suppose \(y_{v,d}>0\), \(cP_vd\), \(y_{t,c}>0\), and \(v\succ_ct\). Demand monotonicity gives \(v\in D_c(x)\), contradicting \((t,c)\in A(x)\).

Also, every stable \(x\) is itself supported on \(A(x)\), directly by the definition of stability.

Use the linear objective

\(\displaystyle W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}F_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{t,c}(m-\rho_t(c))x_{t,c}.\)

Every strict stochastic improvement strictly increases \(W\).

2. A polynomial-time initialization

Call a feasible assignment nonwasteful if every demanded school is full:

\(\displaystyle D_c(x)\neq\varnothing\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_t x_{t,c}=q_c.\)

This is an auxiliary property of the construction. The problem’s definition of stability continues to impose no condition concerning vacancies.

We first compute a rational assignment \(x^0\) that is stable and nonwasteful. The external algorithmic input is the following standard stable-allocation result: with strict preferences on both sides of a bipartite graph, positive vertex quotas and nonnegative edge capacities, a stable allocation can be computed in strongly polynomial time. Stability excludes an unsaturated edge on which both endpoints could increase their allocation by using unused quota or replacing a less preferred allocation. The corrected Baïou–Balinski paper supplies this algorithm; its updates preserve integrality. [Baïou–Balinski, corrected paper, Theorem 2 and Corollary 1](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Apply this result as follows:

  • Omit zero-capacity schools during initialization.
  • Refine each remaining school’s weak priority order to a strict order over the listed types.
  • Give type \(t\) quota \(\mu_t\), school \(c\) quota \(q_c\), and every type-school edge capacity \(1\).
  • Use the given strict school rankings as the types’ preferences.

All hypotheses hold. There are at most \(\tau+m\) vertices and \(\tau m\) edges.

The resulting allocation assigns every type its full mass. Otherwise, some type would have unused quota. Total assigned mass would then be less than \(1\), and since total school capacity is at least \(1\), some school would have unused quota. Their edge would be unsaturated because its allocation is less than \(\mu_t\leq1\). Both endpoints could increase that edge, contradicting stable allocation.

The resulting \(x^0\) is stable under the original weak priorities: any violation involving a strict original priority comparison would remain a blocking edge under the refinement. Similarly, a demanded school with unused capacity would form a blocking edge with a demanding type. Thus \(x^0\) is nonwasteful. Restoring zero-capacity schools preserves both properties.

Let

\(\displaystyle b_c=\sum_t x^0_{t,c}.\)

These school loads will remain fixed during the subsequent construction.

Why fixing these loads loses no stochastic improvement

Lemma. If \(x\) is nonwasteful and \(y\) is any feasible assignment with \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), then \(x\) and \(y\) have identical school loads.

Proof. Suppose some school \(c\) has a larger load under \(y\). Then \(y_{t,c}>x_{t,c}\) for some type \(t\). Put \(k=\rho_t(c)\). Stochastic improvement gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\rho_t(d)<k}(y_{t,d}-x_{t,d})\geq0.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\rho_t(d)\leq k}(y_{t,d}-x_{t,d})>0.\)

The row totals are equal, so some worse school \(d\) satisfies \(x_{t,d}>y_{t,d}\). In particular, \(t\in D_c(x)\). Nonwastefulness makes \(c\) full under \(x\), contradicting feasibility of its increased load under \(y\).

Thus no school’s load increases. Since total assigned mass is \(1\) under both assignments, every load is unchanged. \(\square\)

Together with demand monotonicity, this also shows that every feasible stochastic improvement of a nonwasteful assignment remains nonwasteful.

3. The key improvement lemma

Lemma. Suppose \(x\) and \(y\) are stable assignments with identical row totals and identical school loads. If \(y\) strictly stochastically improves \(x\), then there exists an assignment \(z\) satisfying:

  • the same row totals and school loads;
  • \(z\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), with strict improvement;
  • support contained in \(A(x)\).

In particular, \(z\) is stable by Section 1.

Proof. Define

\(\displaystyle H=\{c:\text{there exists }t\text{ with }y_{t,c}>x_{t,c}\}.\)

Because \(y\neq x\) and row totals agree, \(H\) is nonempty. If \(d\notin H\), then every coordinate at \(d\) weakly decreases. Equality of school loads therefore implies

\[\displaystyle y_{t,d}=x_{t,d}\quad\text{for every }t,\quad d\notin H. \tag{1}\]

Consider the finite set of supported pairs

\(\displaystyle V=\{(t,d):d\in H,\ x_{t,d}>0\}.\)

For each \(c\in H\), let

\(\displaystyle G_c=\{(t,d)\in V:cP_td\}.\)

We establish two properties.

First, \(G_c\) is nonempty. Choose a type \(k\) gaining mass at \(c\). The cumulative-mass calculation in the preceding lemma shows that \(k\) loses mass at some worse school \(d\). Equation \((1)\) implies \(d\in H\), so \((k,d)\in G_c\).

Second, a highest-priority type represented in \(G_c\) is a highest-priority demander of \(c\) among all types. Choose \((t_c,d_c)\in G_c\) with highest priority at \(c\) among the types represented in \(G_c\).

Suppose some \(v\in D_c(x)\) satisfies \(v\succ_c t_c\). Every supported school of \(v\) worse than \(c\) must lie outside \(H\); otherwise it would give a pair in \(G_c\) with higher priority than \(t_c\). Choose one such school \(a\). Equation \((1)\) gives

\(\displaystyle y_{v,a}=x_{v,a}>0.\)

The gaining type \(k\) used above is represented in \(G_c\), so \(t_c\) has weakly higher priority than \(k\). Transitivity gives \(v\succ_ck\). Since \(y_{k,c}>0\), \(v\) and \(k\) violate stability of \(y\). This contradiction proves the second property.

Now construct a directed graph on \(V\). For every \(c\in H\), draw an edge from \((t_c,d_c)\) to every \((u,c)\in V\). Every vertex has an incoming edge. There are no self-loops because \(cP_{t_c}d_c\).

A finite directed graph in which every vertex has an incoming edge contains a directed cycle. Take one with distinct vertices, written

\(\displaystyle (t_1,c_1),\ldots,(t_s,c_s),\)

with cyclic subscripts. Each edge satisfies

\(\displaystyle c_{j+1}P_{t_j}c_j,\qquad (t_j,c_{j+1})\in A(x).\)

Choose

\(\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon\leq\min_j x_{t_j,c_j}.\)

For every \(j\), move mass \(\varepsilon\) of type \(t_j\) from \(c_j\) to \(c_{j+1}\), adding all changes if coordinates coincide.

Nonnegativity holds because the outgoing supported pairs are distinct. Every transfer preserves its type’s row total. The cycle preserves school loads. Each transfer goes to a strictly preferred school, so every cumulative mass weakly increases. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle W(z)-W(x)=\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{s}\bigl(\rho_{t_j}(c_j)-\rho_{t_j}(c_{j+1})\bigr)>0.\)

Every newly occupied pair belongs to \(A(x)\), and the original support already belonged to \(A(x)\). This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

4. The algorithm and its correctness

Start with \(x=x^0\). At each iteration, choose an optimal vertex \(y\) of the following linear program, maximizing \(W(y)\):

  • \(y_{t,c}\geq0\);
  • \(\sum_c y_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\);
  • \(\sum_t y_{t,c}=b_c\) for every school \(c\);
  • \(y_{t,c}=0\) whenever \((t,c)\notin A(x)\);
  • \(F_{t,k}(y)\geq F_{t,k}(x)\) for every type \(t\) and rank \(k\).

The program is feasible because \(x\) satisfies its constraints. It is bounded, and every feasible solution is stable by Section 1. Its solutions are also nonwasteful by Section 2.

If \(D_c(y)=D_c(x)\) for every school, return \(y\). Otherwise, replace \(x\) by \(y\) and repeat.

Termination

Demand sets can only shrink. Whenever the algorithm continues, at least one type-school demand incidence disappears permanently.

Each type demands at most \(m-1\) schools. Hence there are initially at most \(\tau(m-1)\) incidences, and the algorithm solves at most

\(\displaystyle \tau(m-1)+1\)

linear programs.

Constrained efficiency

Let \(y\) be the returned assignment, obtained from the final program with base assignment \(x\). At termination,

\(\displaystyle A(y)=A(x).\)

Suppose a feasible stable assignment \(u\) strictly stochastically improves \(y\). Since \(y\) is nonwasteful, Section 2 implies that \(u\) has the same school loads as \(y\).

The improvement lemma supplies an assignment \(z\) strictly stochastically improving \(y\), with those same loads and support contained in \(A(y)=A(x)\). Thus \(z\) is feasible for the final linear program: its cumulative masses dominate those of \(y\), which dominate those of \(x\).

But \(W(z)>W(y)\), contradicting optimality of \(y\) in that program. Therefore the returned assignment is constrained efficient.

5. Binary-input running time

The number of iterations alone does not establish polynomial bit complexity. We therefore bound all denominators explicitly.

Put \(n=\tau m\). Let \(D_0\) be a common denominator of the input masses and capacities. Taking the product of their denominators gives

\(\displaystyle \log_2 D_0=O(L).\)

The initialization algorithm uses additions, subtractions and minima, so its output can be taken with denominator dividing \(D_0\). Consequently, the fixed loads \(b_c\) have this same property.

Suppose an iterate has all coordinates with denominators dividing \(D_j\), where \(D_0\) divides \(D_j\). Every right-hand side in its linear program then has denominator dividing \(D_j\).

The constraint matrix has entries in \(\{0,1,-1\}\). At a vertex, select \(n\) linearly independent active constraints, with coefficient matrix \(B\). Cramer’s rule shows that all coordinates of the next iterate have denominators dividing

\(\displaystyle D_j|\det B|.\)

Hadamard’s inequality gives

\(\displaystyle |\det B|\leq n^{n/2}.\)

Thus we may maintain a common denominator satisfying

\(\displaystyle \log_2 D_{j+1}\leq\log_2 D_j+\frac n2\log_2 n.\)

There are at most \(n+1\) iterations. All common denominators therefore have bit length

\(\displaystyle O\bigl(L+n^2\log(2n)\bigr).\)

Every assignment coordinate lies in \([0,1]\), so its numerator has the same polynomial bit bound. Each linear program has polynomially many variables and constraints and can be solved exactly, returning an optimal vertex, in polynomial time.

The complete construction consequently runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), without expanding the masses into individual students.

6. A type-symmetric lottery realization

This construction works for every feasible mass assignment \(x\), even with irrational coordinates.

Identify each interval \(I_t\), after rescaling, with \([0,1)\). Partition \([0,1)\) into half-open intervals \(J_{t,c}\) of lengths

\(\displaystyle |J_{t,c}|=p_{t,c}=\frac{x_{t,c}}{\mu_t}.\)

Draw a single uniform random variable \(U\) on \([0,1)\). Assign the student with rescaled coordinate \(s\in I_t\) to school \(c\) exactly when

\(\displaystyle (s+U)\bmod1\in J_{t,c}.\)

This assignment map is jointly measurable. For every realization, rotation preserves Lebesgue measure, so the mass assigned from type \(t\) to school \(c\) is exactly

\(\displaystyle \mu_t|J_{t,c}|=x_{t,c}.\)

Hence capacities hold in every realization. For every student coordinate, its rotation is uniformly distributed, giving the required marginal

\(\displaystyle r_{i,c}=p_{t,c}.\)

If \(x\) is stable, this lottery is ex ante stable: the relevant positive-marginal sets have positive measure precisely when the corresponding coordinates \(x_{t,d}\) and \(x_{u,c}\) are positive.

7. Undominatedness against asymmetric lotteries

Let \(x\) be any constrained-efficient stable mass assignment, and take its type-symmetric realization from Section 6.

Suppose an ex ante-stable lottery with possibly asymmetric marginals \(r_{i,c}\) ordinally dominates this realization. Aggregate its marginals by type:

\(\displaystyle y_{t,c}=\int_{I_t}r_{i,c}\,di.\)

This is a feasible mass assignment. Row totals equal \(\mu_t\), and Tonelli’s theorem gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_t y_{t,c}=\mathbb E\!\left[\text{mass assigned to }c\right]\leq q_c.\)

For a nonnegative measurable function, its integral is positive exactly when its positive set has positive measure. Therefore, if \(y_{t,d}>0\) and \(y_{u,c}>0\), both corresponding positive-marginal sets have positive measure. Ex ante stability of the lottery consequently implies stability of \(y\).

Integrating the almost-everywhere stochastic-dominance inequalities gives

\(\displaystyle F_{t,k}(y)\geq F_{t,k}(x)\quad\text{for every }t,k.\)

Finally, strict stochastic improvement holds on a set of positive measure. There are only finitely many type-rank pairs, so some pair \((t,k)\) has a positive-measure set on which its cumulative inequality is strict. The cumulative difference is nonnegative almost everywhere; its integral is therefore strictly positive. Hence

\(\displaystyle F_{t,k}(y)>F_{t,k}(x)\)

for that pair.

Thus \(y\) is a feasible stable mass assignment strictly stochastically improving \(x\), contradicting constrained efficiency.

This proves the lottery guarantee for every constrained-efficient stable mass assignment, with equal treatment within each listed type and undominatedness against all measurable ex ante-stable competitors.
