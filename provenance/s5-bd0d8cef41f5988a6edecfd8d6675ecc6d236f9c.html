Theorem 6 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of item categories \(B\) and supported types \(T\), where each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), a strict ranking of \(B\), and a currently assigned category \(b(t)\), decide whether there are values \(x_{tu}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_t x_{tu}=\mu_u\) for every \(u\), \(x_{tu}>0\) only if type \(t\) strictly prefers \(b(u)\) to \(b(t)\), and the directed support graph with arc \(t\to u\) when \(x_{tu}>0\) is strongly connected on \(T\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(B\) be a finite set of item categories, with \(m=|B|\geq 2\). A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of its \(\tau\geq 2\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type consists of a strict total ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(B\) and a currently assigned category \(b(t)\in B\); identical pairs are merged. Categories may be assigned to multiple types. The available mass of category \(c\) is exactly \(\sum_{t:b(t)=c}\mu_t\); every individual initially holds one unit of its assigned category.
A feasible exchange is a real matrix \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T\times T}\) satisfying \(\sum_{u\in T}x_{tu}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{tu}=\mu_u\) for every \(u\), and \(x_{tu}>0\Rightarrow b(u)\succ_t b(t)\). Thus \(x_{tu}\) is mass of type \(t\) receiving goods initially held by type \(u\).
Require the directed graph on all of \(T\), with arc \(t\to u\) exactly when \(x_{tu}>0\), to be strongly connected. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether such a feasible exchange exists solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents holding distinct indivisible items, existence of one strictly improving trading cycle containing every agent is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard; equivalently, verifying its absence is \(\mathsf{coNP}\)-hard, even with one preference layer and preference lists of length at most three. [Steindl and Zehavi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.10434)
No result settling exactly the continuous decision problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one, interpreting the society as a distribution over types.
  • Required at least two supported types and two categories, matching the nontrivial trading-cycle setting.
  • Specified that a type includes both its ranking and its current category, and merged identical pairs.
  • Interpreted categories as goods with multiple units: their supplies are exactly the initially assigned masses, with no additional unassigned supply.
  • Specified real-valued exchange variables and total binary input length L.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The decision problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). One maximum-flow computation and two strongly connected component computations suffice.

The essential point is that feasible exchanges form a convex set: individually realizable positive entries can all be made positive simultaneously by averaging feasible exchanges.

1. Feasibility without the connectivity requirement

Define the allowed arcs by
\(E=\{(t,u)\in T\times T:b(u)\succ_t b(t)\}\).

Let \(\mathcal F\) be the set of nonnegative matrices supported on \(E\) with row sums and column sums both equal to the prescribed masses. These are precisely the feasible exchanges before imposing strong connectivity.

Construct a network with source \(s\), sink \(z\), and two distinct copies \(r_t,c_t\) of every type \(t\). Its arcs and capacities are:

  • \(s\to r_t\), with capacity \(\mu_t\);
  • \(r_t\to c_u\), with capacity \(1\), for every \((t,u)\in E\);
  • \(c_u\to z\), with capacity \(\mu_u\).

Claim. \(\mathcal F\) is nonempty if and only if this network has a flow of value \(1\).

Proof. Given \(x\in\mathcal F\), put flow \(x_{tu}\) on \(r_t\to c_u\), and flow \(\mu_t\) on the corresponding source and sink arcs. The prescribed margins give flow conservation.

Conversely, the total capacity leaving \(s\) and the total capacity entering \(z\) both equal \(1\). A flow of value \(1\) therefore saturates every source and sink arc. Its middle-arc flows consequently have exactly the required row and column sums. \(\square\)

If the maximum-flow value is less than \(1\), reject. Otherwise, fix the resulting feasible matrix \(x\).

2. Identify every entry that can be positive

Define
\(E^*=\{(t,u)\in E:\text{some }y\in\mathcal F\text{ satisfies }y_{tu}>0\}\).

We next compute \(E^*\) from the single matrix \(x\).

Because every mass is positive and \(\tau\geq 2\), we have \(\mu_t<1\). Thus \(x_{tu}\leq\mu_t<1\) for every entry.

Construct a directed bipartite graph \(D_x\) on the vertices \(r_t,c_u\), with:

  • a forward arc \(r_t\to c_u\) for every \((t,u)\in E\);
  • a backward arc \(c_u\to r_t\) whenever \(x_{tu}>0\).

These are exactly the residual middle arcs of the network: every allowed forward arc has positive remaining capacity.

Lemma. For every \((t,u)\in E\),
\((t,u)\in E^*\) if and only if \(r_t\) and \(c_u\) lie in the same strongly connected component of \(D_x\).

Proof.

If \(x_{tu}>0\), then \((t,u)\in E^*\), and both directed arcs between \(r_t\) and \(c_u\) occur in \(D_x\). Hence the claimed equivalence holds for these entries.

Now suppose \(x_{tu}=0\).

First assume that \(r_t\) and \(c_u\) lie in the same strongly connected component. There is a simple directed path from \(c_u\) to \(r_t\). Together with \(r_t\to c_u\), it forms a directed cycle.

Along this cycle, increase the entries corresponding to forward arcs by a common amount \(\varepsilon>0\), and decrease those corresponding to backward arcs by \(\varepsilon\). Choose
\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\frac12\min\bigl(\{1-x_{av}:r_a\to c_v\text{ is on the cycle}\}\cup\{x_{av}:c_v\to r_a\text{ is on the cycle}\}\bigr)\).

Every quantity in this minimum is positive. The resulting matrix is nonnegative and supported on \(E\). At each row vertex, one entry increases and one decreases; the same holds at each column vertex. All margins therefore remain unchanged. Since the cycle includes the forward arc \(r_t\to c_u\), its previously zero entry becomes positive. Thus \((t,u)\in E^*\).

Conversely, suppose \(y\in\mathcal F\) satisfies \(y_{tu}>0\). Write \(d_{av}=y_{av}-x_{av}\). Construct a nonnegative flow on \(D_x\) by sending:

  • \(d_{av}\) on \(r_a\to c_v\) when \(d_{av}>0\);
  • \(-d_{av}\) on \(c_v\to r_a\) when \(d_{av}<0\).

Every required backward arc exists, because \(d_{av}<0\) implies \(x_{av}>0\). Equality of the row and column margins of \(x\) and \(y\) shows that this flow conserves mass at every vertex. It is therefore a circulation. Moreover, \(r_t\to c_u\) carries positive flow.

Every positive arc of a finite nonnegative circulation has a return path using positive-flow arcs. Indeed, let \(U\) be the vertices reachable from its head. If its tail were outside \(U\), there would be positive flow entering \(U\), while no positive flow could leave \(U\). This contradicts conservation summed over \(U\).

Consequently, there is a directed path from \(c_u\) to \(r_t\) in \(D_x\). The forward arc \(r_t\to c_u\) completes mutual reachability. \(\square\)

Thus one strongly connected component computation on \(D_x\) determines all of \(E^*\).

3. Strong connectivity of the realizable arcs is sufficient

Let \(G^*=(T,E^*)\).

Proposition. A feasible exchange with strongly connected positive support exists if and only if \(G^*\) is strongly connected.

Proof. If \(y\in\mathcal F\) has strongly connected support, every arc in its support belongs to \(E^*\). Adding arcs preserves strong connectivity, so \(G^*\) is strongly connected.

For the converse, suppose \(G^*\) is strongly connected. For each \(e\in E^*\), choose a feasible matrix \(y^{(e)}\) whose entry at \(e\) is positive. Define
\(\displaystyle \bar y=\frac{1}{|E^*|}\sum_{e\in E^*}y^{(e)}\).

The set \(E^*\) is nonempty because \(\mathcal F\) is nonempty and every row has positive prescribed mass. Averaging preserves nonnegativity, the allowed-entry restrictions, and all row and column sums. Hence \(\bar y\in\mathcal F\).

For every \(e\in E^*\), its own witness contributes positively to \(\bar y_e\), and all other contributions are nonnegative. Thus every arc of \(E^*\) is positive in \(\bar y\). No entry outside \(E^*\) can be positive in any feasible matrix, by definition. Therefore the support of \(\bar y\) is exactly \(G^*\), which is strongly connected. \(\square\)

4. Algorithm and exact bit complexity

The algorithm is:

  • Construct \(E\) from the rankings.
  • Compute a maximum flow in the network above. Reject if its value is less than \(1\).
  • From a resulting feasible matrix \(x\), construct \(D_x\) and compute its strongly connected components.
  • Include \((t,u)\) in \(E^*\) precisely when \(r_t,c_u\) belong to the same component.
  • Accept precisely when \((T,E^*)\) is strongly connected.

To justify exact polynomial-time computation, write \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\) and take \(Q=\prod_{t\in T}q_t\). The binary length of \(Q\) is at most the sum of the denominator lengths, hence is bounded by \(O(L)\).

Multiply all network capacities by \(Q\). They become integers: the outer capacities are \(Q\mu_t\), and the middle capacities are \(Q\). Feasibility now means maximum-flow value exactly \(Q\).

The network has \(2\tau+2\) vertices and at most \(\tau^2+2\tau\) arcs. The Edmonds–Karp algorithm uses \(O(\tau^5)\) arithmetic operations on this network, independently of the numerical capacities. Starting from zero, its flows and residual capacities remain integers with polynomial binary length. Dividing the resulting middle flows by \(Q\) recovers \(x\), and positivity is tested exactly.

Constructing the ranking tables and allowed arcs takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Both strongly connected component computations use graphs of polynomial size. The entire algorithm therefore has polynomial bit complexity.

The averaging argument is the reason the cited [indivisible-assignment hardness result](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.10434) does not transfer: continuous feasibility permits combining different feasible exchanges, and their combined support need not be a single trading cycle.

Already-positive entries. If \(x_{tu}>0\), the matrix \(x\) itself witnesses realizability. Both directions between \(r_t\) and \(c_u\) exist, so these vertices belong to the same strongly connected component.

Zero entries with mutually reachable endpoints. A simple path from \(c_u\) to \(r_t\), together with the forward arc \(r_t\to c_u\), forms a directed cycle. The specified minimum defining \(\varepsilon\) is over a finite, nonempty collection of strictly positive quantities. It ensures that every decreased entry remains nonnegative and every increased entry stays within its middle-arc capacity. The bipartite cycle alternates forward and backward arcs, so each participating row and column has one increase and one decrease. The margins remain unchanged, and the target entry becomes positive.

Both directions of the lemma are established, and one strongly connected component computation determines \(E^*\).

Necessity is immediate and correctly justified: every positive entry of a feasible exchange belongs to \(E^*\), and adding arcs preserves strong connectivity on the same vertex set.

Sufficiency follows from the stated finite averaging argument. Each arc in \(E^*\) has a feasible witness. Their average preserves nonnegativity, allowed support, and both margins. Each realizable entry remains strictly positive because its own witness contributes positively and all other contributions are nonnegative.

No entry outside \(E^*\) can be positive in this average, since the average is itself feasible. Its support is therefore exactly \(E^*\).

The denominator \(|E^*|\) is nonzero: feasibility and positive row sums ensure that some entry is positive. This handles the potential empty-set edge case.

The proof consequently establishes the required equivalence on all of \(T\), including types sharing assigned categories.
