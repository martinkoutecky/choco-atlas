The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a finite set of ranking types \(T\subseteq S_C\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), an integer \(k\), and rational threshold \(B\), decide whether there exists \(\Lambda\subseteq T\) with \(|\Lambda|\le k\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda)\le B\); the optimization version minimizes this quantity.

Definitions

Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set and \(S\) a finite set of linear orders over \(C\). Let \(\mu_t>0\) be binary-encoded rationals with \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\), and let \(\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda)\) denote Kendall's \( au\) distance between rankings \(t\) and \(\lambda\).

Given a positive integer \(k\) and rational \(B\), decide whether some \(\Lambda\subseteq S\) with \(|\Lambda|\le k\) satisfies \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda)\le B\); on yes-instances, output such a \(\Lambda\).

Target

For finite elections with unit-weight voters and centers restricted to rankings appearing among the votes, the decision problem is NP-complete and \(W[2]\)-hard when parameterized by \(k\).

Complexity of Rational-Mass \(k\)-Kemeny Among Votes

Theorem

The decision version of rational-mass \(k\)-Kemeny Among Votes is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This holds even when every mass is obtained by normalizing an integer voter multiplicity. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm that also outputs a feasible center set on yes-instances exists if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Moreover, the stated \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness parameterized by \(k\) transfers to the rational-mass formulation.

1. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Let \(m=|C|\). A certificate is a subset \(\Lambda\subseteq S\), represented by indices of rankings in \(S\), satisfying \(|\Lambda|\leq k\).

For each \(t\in S\) and \(\lambda\in\Lambda\), the Kendall distance \(\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda)\) can be computed in polynomial time, for example by counting inversions after expressing \(\lambda\) in the order induced by \(t\). Every such distance is an integer between \(0\) and \(\binom{m}{2}\).

Thus one can compute

\[ d_t=\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda) \]

for every \(t\in S\), and then compare exactly

\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t d_t \]

with \(B\).

Write \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\) and \(B=a/b\), with positive denominators. Clearing denominators produces an integer comparison. The product of all denominators has bit length at most the sum of their input bit lengths, so this exact comparison requires only polynomially many bit operations. Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. Reduction from the unit-weight problem

Use the stated known \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem:

  • the candidates are \(C\);
  • the unit-weight election is a list \(V=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\) of linear orders;
  • centers must be rankings occurring in \(V\);
  • the question is whether some set of at most \(k\) centers has total Kendall cost at most an integer threshold \(D\).

Let

\[ S=\{t:t\text{ occurs among }v_1,\ldots,v_n\}. \]

For every \(t\in S\), let

\[ a_t=\bigl|\{i:v_i=t\}\bigr| \]

be its multiplicity. Construct the rational-mass instance by setting

\[ \mu_t=\frac{a_t}{n} \qquad\text{and}\qquad B=\frac{D}{n}. \]

These masses are positive and satisfy

\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in S}a_t = 1. \]

The construction is polynomial-time: each \(a_t\leq n\), so every mass has encoding length \(O(\log n)\), before or after reducing the fraction.

3. Preservation of feasible center sets and costs

The allowable center rankings in both instances are exactly the distinct rankings occurring in \(V\), namely \(S\).

If the unit-weight formulation treats centers as voter occurrences, choosing two occurrences with the same ranking never improves the objective. Duplicate centers can therefore be deleted without increasing cost or cardinality. Hence it is equivalent to choose a subset of \(S\).

For every \(\Lambda\subseteq S\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda) &= \sum_{t\in S}\frac{a_t}{n} \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda)\\ &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(v_i,\lambda). \end{aligned} \]

Consequently,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(v_i,\lambda) \leq D \]

holds if and only if

\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda) \leq B. \]

The reduction therefore preserves yes-instances and no-instances exactly. It also leaves \(k\) unchanged.

4. Consequences

The reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, while membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

The requirement to output \(\Lambda\) on yes-instances does not weaken this conclusion: any polynomial-time algorithm satisfying that requirement would, in particular, decide the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete decision problem. Conversely, if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), standard polynomial-witness search using the polynomial-time verifier yields such a center set.

Because the reduction preserves \(k\), the stated \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness parameterized by \(k\) transfers as well.

Step-by-step audit

1. Membership in NP

The proposed certificate, a subset \(\Lambda\subseteq S\) represented by indices, has polynomial encoding length because it contains at most \(|S|\) indices, regardless of the binary encoding of \(k\).

Computing Kendall distance by inversion counting is polynomial in \(|C|\). Taking the minimum over the proposed centers and evaluating the weighted sum are therefore polynomial-time operations.

The exact-arithmetic argument is valid. If all rational inputs are placed over the product of their denominators, that product has bit length equal to the sum of the denominator bit lengths, hence polynomial in the input size. The resulting integer arithmetic also involves only polynomial-bit-length integers.

There is one minor definitional omission: the prover does not specify how \(\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\) is interpreted when \(\Lambda=\varnothing\). Since the task permits \(|\Lambda|\leq k\), the empty subset is syntactically allowed even though its objective is undefined. The verifier should either require \(1\leq|\Lambda|\leq k\) or explicitly adopt the convention that the minimum over the empty set is \(+\infty\). This does not affect the reduction or complexity conclusion.

2. Construction of the reduction

For each distinct ranking \(t\), the multiplicity \(a_t\) is a positive integer and

\[ \sum_{t\in S}a_t=n. \]

Thus \(\mu_t=a_t/n\) is positive and the masses sum to \(1\), exactly satisfying the rational-mass input conditions.

The threshold transformation \(B=D/n\) is correct. All generated rational numbers have polynomial encoding length, and grouping identical rankings can be performed in polynomial time by comparing their finite encodings.

3. Admissible center sets

Both formulations permit exactly rankings that occur among the original votes. Replacing occurrences by their distinct rankings does not change the available ranking values.

The observation about duplicate centers is correct: selecting the same ranking more than once does not change any minimum distance. Removing duplicate occurrences cannot increase either the cost or the number of centers.

4. Objective preservation

For every nonempty \(\Lambda\subseteq S\), grouping voter occurrences according to their ranking gives

\[ \sum_{t\in S}\frac{a_t}{n} \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(t,\lambda) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\operatorname{swap}(v_i,\lambda). \]

Therefore comparison with \(B=D/n\) is exactly equivalent to comparison of the original total cost with \(D\). This proves both directions of the reduction without approximation or rounding.

5. Complexity consequences

The combination of NP-hardness and membership in NP establishes NP-completeness.

The claim concerning witness output is also correct. A polynomial-time algorithm that decides the problem and outputs a center set on yes-instances would decide an NP-complete language. Conversely, under \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), the associated polynomially balanced NP witness relation can be searched in polynomial time by the standard prefix-extension method.

Because the reduction is polynomial-time and maps \(k\) to the same value \(k\), it is also a valid parameterized reduction. Hence the stated \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness transfers.
