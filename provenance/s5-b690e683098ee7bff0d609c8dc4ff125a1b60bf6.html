The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda\), and a finite-support normalized prior \(\pi\) over disutility profiles \(x(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}_+^T\) with \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X_t]=1\), determine the worst-case competitive ratio of weighted Bottom-Heavy cap-and-fill allocation against the optimal prior-dependent Fair-Share allocation, where \(\sum_t\lambda_t a_{t,\omega}=1\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[a_t(X)X_t]\le1\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\ge2\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\lambda_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\). Types describe an atomless population; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
A prior has \(m\) distinct profiles \(x(\omega)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{T}\), positive binary-rational probabilities \(\pi_\omega\) summing to \(1\), and \(\sum_\omega\pi_\omega x_t(\omega)=1\) for every type. Correlations are unrestricted. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state any running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and additional parameters.
An allocation density \(a_{t,\omega}\ge0\) gives type \(t\) the aggregate share \(\lambda_t a_{t,\omega}\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_t\lambda_t a_{t,\omega}=1\); Fair Share requires \(\sum_\omega\pi_\omega a_{t,\omega}x_t(\omega)\le1\). Define \(C_\pi(a)=\sum_\omega\pi_\omega\sum_t\lambda_t a_{t,\omega}x_t(\omega)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\pi=\min_{a\text{ feasible and fair}}C_\pi(a)\).
Define weighted Bottom-Heavy \(b(x)\) using only \(\lambda,x\). For strictly positive \(x\), set \(\bar x=\sum_t\lambda_t x_t\) and density caps \(c_t(x)=\bar x/x_t\). Process disutility levels increasingly, assigning each level its full caps until the next level would exhaust the remaining share; at that level give every tied type the same density so that total assigned share is \(1\), and give higher levels zero.
If \(Z=\{t:x_t=0\}\ne\varnothing\), set \(b_t(x)=1/\sum_{s\in Z}\lambda_s\) on \(Z\) and zero elsewhere. Define \(R(\lambda)=\sup_\pi C_\pi(b)/\operatorname{OPT}_\pi\), over all finite normalized rational priors and all finite \(m\); use \(0/0=1\) and \(u/0=+\infty\) for \(u>0\).

What is the sharp worst-case approximation factor \(R(\lambda)\) of weighted Bottom-Heavy relative to the optimal prior-dependent Fair-Share allocation, as a function of the mass vector \(\lambda\)?

For \(n\ge2\) discrete agents, Bottom-Heavy has competitive ratio between \(n/4+1/2+1/(4n)\) and \(n/4+5/4\). [Bogomolnaia–Moulin–Sandomirskiy](https://arxiv.org/html/1903.10361v4)

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Sharp approximation factor

Let \(p=\min_{t\in T}\lambda_t\). Then the sharp factor is

\[ R(\lambda)=\frac{1}{4p}+\frac34. \]

The factor is attained by a normalized rational prior with exactly three distinct profiles. Thus the supremum over arbitrarily large prior supports is unnecessary.

1. Feasibility and Fair Share

For a strictly positive profile \(x\), write \(s=\sum_t\lambda_t x_t\). In aggregate-share coordinates \(q_t=\lambda_t a_t\), the density caps become

\[ 0\le q_t\le u_t:=\frac{\lambda_t s}{x_t}. \]

Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ s\sum_t\frac{\lambda_t}{x_t}\ge \left(\sum_t\lambda_t\right)^2=1. \]

Consequently, the caps have total capacity at least \(1\), so the Bottom-Heavy construction is feasible. Its treatment of a partially filled tied level also respects the caps: the remaining share is at most that level’s total capped capacity.

Moreover, for every type and profile,

\[ b_t(x)x_t\le s. \]

For profiles with a zero coordinate, every assigned type has zero disutility, so the same inequality holds. Under any normalized prior,

\[ \sum_\omega\pi_\omega b_t(x(\omega))x_t(\omega) \le \sum_\omega\pi_\omega\sum_s\lambda_s x_s(\omega) =1. \]

Thus weighted Bottom-Heavy always satisfies Fair Share.

Define its cost at one profile by \(B(x)=\sum_t\lambda_t b_t(x)x_t\). We next prove the sharp pointwise bound

\[ B(x)\le \left(\frac{1}{4p}+\frac34\right)\min_t x_t. \]

2. A threshold formula for the profile cost

Fix a strictly positive profile. Bottom-Heavy minimizes the linear cost over the capped aggregate shares:

\[ B(x)=\min\left\{\sum_t q_t x_t: \sum_tq_t=1,\quad 0\le q_t\le u_t\right\}. \]

Here is a direct verification that also provides the formula needed below. For any real threshold \(h\) and feasible \(q\),

\[ \sum_tq_tx_t =h+\sum_tq_t(x_t-h) \ge h-\sum_tu_t(h-x_t)_+, \]

where \((z)_+=\max\{z,0\}\). The inequality follows by using \(q_t\le u_t\) when \(x_t<h\), and \(q_t\ge0\) when \(x_t>h\).

Choose \(h\) to be a level at which Bottom-Heavy exhausts the remaining share. Every type below \(h\) receives its full cap, and every type above \(h\) receives zero. Therefore equality holds for Bottom-Heavy. It follows that

\[ B(x)= \max_{\min_t x_t\le h\le\max_t x_t} \left[ h-s\sum_t\lambda_t\left(\frac{h}{x_t}-1\right)_+ \right]. \]

This argument includes ties and the case in which a level’s full caps exactly exhaust the share.

3. Maximizing the threshold expression

Scaling a positive profile by a common positive number does not change its Bottom-Heavy allocation. We may therefore normalize \(\min_t x_t=1\).

Because \(\tau\ge2\), we have \(0<p\le1/2\). Set \(K=p^{-2}\).

If \(s\ge1/p\), the total mass of minimum-disutility types is at least \(p\), and their total capped capacity is at least \(ps\ge1\). Bottom-Heavy then allocates everything at disutility \(1\), giving \(B(x)=1\).

Otherwise \(s<1/p\). Since \(\lambda_t\ge p\),

\[ px_t\le\lambda_tx_t\le s<\frac1p, \]

so every coordinate lies in \([1,K]\).

For \(x\in[1,K]^\tau\) with minimum coordinate \(1\), and \(h\in[1,K]\), define

\[ F(x,h)=h-s\sum_t\lambda_t\left(\frac{h}{x_t}-1\right)_+. \]

Replacing every coordinate above \(h\) by \(h\) leaves the sum involving positive parts unchanged and weakly decreases \(s\). Hence it weakly increases \(F\). Thus it suffices to maximize \(F\) over the compact domain

\[ \mathcal D= \{(x,h):1\le x_t\le h\le K,\ \min_t x_t=1\}. \]

On this domain, put \(H=\sum_t\lambda_t/x_t\) and \(D=hH-1\). Then \(D\ge0\), and

\[ F(x,h)=h-sD=h+s-hsH. \]

We prove that every maximizer on \(\mathcal D\) has one coordinate equal to \(1\), and all other coordinates equal to \(h\).

3.1. The maximum exceeds one

Choose a type of mass \(p\), give it disutility \(1\), and give every other type disutility \(2\), with \(h=2\). This pair belongs to \(\mathcal D\), since \(K\ge4\). Direct substitution gives

\[ F=2-p(2-p)=1+(1-p)^2>1. \]

In particular, a maximizing pair has \(h>1\) and a nonconstant profile.

3.2. Exactly one coordinate is minimal

Fix a maximizing pair and a type \(i\) with \(x_i=1\). Holding \(h\) fixed gives

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_j} =\lambda_j\left(\frac{hs}{x_j^2}-D\right). \]

If another type \(j\ne i\) had \(x_j=1\), this derivative would equal

\[ \lambda_j\bigl(h(s-H)+1\bigr)>0, \]

because \(s\ge1\ge H\). Increasing \(x_j\) slightly would preserve membership in \(\mathcal D\) and increase \(F\), a contradiction. Thus \(i\) is the unique minimum type.

3.3. Some coordinate equals the threshold

Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(sH\ge1\), with equality only when all coordinates are equal. Our maximizing profile is nonconstant, so

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial h}=1-sH<0. \]

If every coordinate were strictly below \(h\), decreasing \(h\) slightly would remain feasible and increase \(F\). Therefore some type \(j\ne i\) has \(x_j=h\).

At this upper-bound coordinate, decreasing \(x_j\) must not improve the objective. The necessary one-sided derivative condition is therefore

\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_j} =\lambda_j\left(\frac{s}{h}-D\right)\ge0, \]

which implies \(D\le s/h\).

3.4. No intermediate coordinate is possible

Suppose \(1<x_k<h\). Both small increases and decreases of \(x_k\) are feasible, so its derivative must vanish:

\[ D=\frac{hs}{x_k^2}. \]

Together with \(D\le s/h\), this gives \(x_k^2\ge h^2\), contradicting \(x_k<h\).

Thus a maximizing profile has exactly two levels: \(x_i=1\) and \(x_t=h\) for every \(t\ne i\).

3.5. Evaluating the two-level maximum

Write \(q=\lambda_i\) and \(d=h-1\). Then \(s=1+(1-q)d\), \(D=qd\), and

\[ F=1+(1-q)d-q(1-q)d^2 =1+\frac{1-q}{4q} -q(1-q)\left(d-\frac{1}{2q}\right)^2. \]

Consequently,

\[ F\le\frac34+\frac{1}{4q} \le\frac34+\frac{1}{4p}. \]

This bounds the maximum over \(\mathcal D\). Combining it with the case \(B(x)=1\), and undoing the normalization, proves the pointwise bound.

When a profile has a zero coordinate, both \(B(x)\) and its minimum coordinate are zero, so the bound holds there as well.

4. Upper bound for every normalized prior

Let \(U_\pi=\sum_\omega\pi_\omega\min_t x_t(\omega)\). Every feasible allocation has cost at least \(U_\pi\), since the aggregate shares at each profile form a probability vector. In particular, \(\operatorname{OPT}_\pi\ge U_\pi\).

The pointwise bound gives

\[ C_\pi(b) \le\left(\frac{1}{4p}+\frac34\right)U_\pi \le\left(\frac{1}{4p}+\frac34\right)\operatorname{OPT}_\pi. \]

If \(\operatorname{OPT}_\pi=0\), then \(U_\pi=0\). All profile probabilities are positive, so every profile has a zero coordinate, and \(C_\pi(b)=0\). The prescribed ratio is then \(1\); no positive-over-zero case occurs.

Therefore \(R(\lambda)\le1/(4p)+3/4\).

5. A three-profile prior attaining the bound

Choose a type \(i\) with \(\lambda_i=p\), and set

\[ h=1+\frac{1}{2p}, \qquad \delta=\frac p4, \qquad q=\frac{1-\delta}{2}, \qquad r=\frac{2(1-\delta h)}{1-\delta}. \]

Here \(r>0\), because \(\delta h=(2p+1)/8\le1/4\).

Use the following three profiles and probabilities:

| Profile | Probability | Disutility of type \(i\) | Disutility of every type \(t\ne i\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(x^*\) | \(\delta\) | \(1\) | \(h\) |
| \(z\) | \(q\) | \(2\) | \(0\) |
| \(y\) | \(q\) | \(0\) | \(r\) |

The profiles are distinct, and all probabilities are positive and sum to \(1\). All entries are rational. If “binary rational” is interpreted as dyadic, the probabilities are also dyadic whenever the masses are dyadic.

Normalization holds type by type:

\[ \delta+2q=1 \]

for type \(i\), and

\[ \delta h+qr=1 \]

for every other type.

At \(x^*\), the mean disutility is

\[ s^*=p+(1-p)h=\frac{1+p}{2p}. \]

Bottom-Heavy gives type \(i\) its full cap, with aggregate share \(ps^*=(1+p)/2\). The remaining share is \((1-p)/2\), so every other type receives density \(1/2\). This respects their caps because \(s^*/h=(1+p)/(1+2p)>1/2\).

Its profile cost is therefore

\[ B(x^*)= \frac{1+p}{2}+\frac{1-p}{2}h =\frac34+\frac{1}{4p}. \]

At \(z\) and \(y\), Bottom-Heavy incurs zero cost. Hence

\[ C_\pi(b)=\delta\left(\frac34+\frac{1}{4p}\right). \]

To attain the optimal fair cost, allocate:

  • all of \(x^*\) to type \(i\);
  • all of \(z\) to any type different from \(i\);
  • all of \(y\) to type \(i\).

This allocation is feasible. Type \(i\)’s expected density-weighted disutility is \(\delta/p=1/4\), and every other type’s is zero. Thus it satisfies Fair Share.

Its social cost is \(\delta\). Conversely, every feasible allocation costs at least \(\delta\), because the minimum disutility at \(x^*\) is \(1\). Therefore

\[ \operatorname{OPT}_\pi=\delta, \qquad \frac{C_\pi(b)}{\operatorname{OPT}_\pi} =\frac34+\frac{1}{4p}. \]

This proves sharpness and attainment.

6. Consequences and computation

For equal masses, the formula becomes \(R(\lambda)=\tau/4+3/4\). For two equal-mass types, it gives \(5/4\). Even with \(\tau=2\), the factor is unbounded as the smaller mass tends to zero.

The rule specified here differs from the cited discrete Bottom-Heavy rule. For equal masses, its aggregate cap is \((\sum_jx_j)/(\tau^2x_t)\); the discrete rule’s cap is \((\sum_{j\ne t}x_j)/(\tau(\tau-1)x_t)\). Thus the discrete ratio bounds do not directly apply. See [Bogomolnaia–Moulin–Sandomirskiy, Definition 15](https://arxiv.org/html/1903.10361v4).

The sharp factor requires only finding the smallest mass and evaluating the formula. With schoolbook rational arithmetic, this takes \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations, with no additional dependence on \(m\).
