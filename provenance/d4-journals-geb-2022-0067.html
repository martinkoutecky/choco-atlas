The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite object types \(O\), applicant types \(\Theta\), rational applicant mass \( \mu \), per-capita object supplies \(q\), and type data specifying preferences and priorities, compute a feasible mass allocation \(x_{\theta,o}\) with \( \sum_o x_{\theta,o}=\mu_\theta \), \( \sum_\theta x_{\theta,o}=q_o \), and \(p_{\theta,o}=x_{\theta,o}/\mu_\theta\) satisfying \( \sum_{o'\succeq_\theta o,\;o'\ne o}p_{\theta,o'}+\frac{1}{q_o}\sum_{\theta':\,\theta'\succeq_o\theta}x_{\theta',o}\ge 1 \); return the resulting \( \mathrm{FS\text{-}VER} \), equivalently the constrained ordinally egalitarian allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(O\) be a finite set of object types and \(\Theta\) a finite set of applicant types. Each type has a weak preference over \(O\) and a positive rational mass \(\mu_\theta\); each object has a weak priority over \(\Theta\) and a positive rational supply \(q_o\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=\sum_o q_o\).
An allocation has \(x_{\theta,o}\geq0\), \(\sum_o x_{\theta,o}=\mu_\theta\), and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta,o}=q_o\). Write \(p_{\theta,o}=x_{\theta,o}/\mu_\theta\). It is fractionally stable when, for every \(\theta,o\), \(\sum_{o'\succeq_\theta o,\,o'\neq o}p_{\theta,o'}+\frac{1}{q_o}\sum_{\theta':\,\theta'\succeq_o\theta}x_{\theta',o}\geq1\).
For each type \(\theta\) and each initial segment of its preference indifference classes, record the total \(p_{\theta,o}\) in that segment. A constrained ordinally egalitarian allocation lexicographically maximizes the nondecreasing sort of all these recorded values over fractionally stable allocations; call any such allocation \(\mathrm{FS\text{-}VER}\).

Is computing \(\mathrm{FS\text{-}VER}\) for these finite-type mass instances in P, or is it NP-hard?

For finite-agent probabilistic allocation, \(\mathrm{FS\text{-}VER}\) is computable in polynomial time, including with weak preferences and priorities.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable whenever a fractionally stable allocation exists. More generally, a polynomial-time algorithm can either compute an \(\mathrm{FS\text{-}VER}\) allocation or report that no fractionally stable allocation exists.

The existence qualification is necessary: the stated assumptions do not guarantee feasibility.

1. Fractional stability defines a polynomial-size rational polytope

Let \(n=|\Theta|\) and \(m=|O|\). Write \(P\) for the set of allocations satisfying the mass constraints, supply constraints, nonnegativity, and the stated fractional-stability inequalities.

Substituting \(p_{\theta,o}=x_{\theta,o}/\mu_\theta\), each stability inequality becomes

\( \displaystyle \frac{1}{\mu_\theta} \sum_{\substack{o'\succeq_\theta o\\o'\neq o}}x_{\theta,o'} + \frac{1}{q_o} \sum_{\theta'\succeq_o\theta}x_{\theta',o} \geq 1. \)

The masses and supplies are given positive rational constants. Consequently, these are linear inequalities with rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. There are \(nm\) variables, \(nm\) stability inequalities, and polynomially many allocation constraints.

Moreover, nonnegativity and the row sums imply \(0\leq x_{\theta,o}\leq\mu_\theta\). Thus \(P\) is a bounded rational polytope, possibly empty.

We use the standard theorem that rational linear programming admits exact feasibility testing and optimization in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. See [MIT’s notes on polynomial-time linear programming](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

Hence emptiness of \(P\) can be decided in polynomial time. Assume henceforth that \(P\neq\varnothing\).

2. Replace the sorted objective by cumulative sums

Index all recorded preference-prefix values by \(i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\). Each is a rational linear function

\( \displaystyle f_i(x)=\frac{1}{\mu_{\theta(i)}}\sum_{o\in A_i}x_{\theta(i),o}, \)

where \(A_i\) is the corresponding initial segment. There are at most \(K\leq nm\) such values, and each belongs to \([0,1]\).

Let their nondecreasing ordering be

\( f_{(1)}(x)\leq\cdots\leq f_{(K)}(x), \)

and define

\( \displaystyle S_k(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{(j)}(x) \quad\text{for }k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}. \)

Lexicographically maximizing the sorted vector is equivalent to lexicographically maximizing \((S_1(x),\ldots,S_K(x))\). Indeed, if two sorted vectors first differ at coordinate \(r\), their cumulative sums agree before \(r\), and their cumulative sums at \(r\) differ by exactly the difference of their \(r\)-th coordinates.

We next represent upper bounds on these cumulative sums using linear constraints.

3. A polynomial-size extended formulation

For any \(v_1,\ldots,v_K\in[0,1]\), with nondecreasing ordering \(v_{(1)},\ldots,v_{(K)}\), we have

\( \displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{(j)} = \max_{0\leq t\leq1} \left(kt-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\max\{t-v_i,0\}\right). \)

To verify the identity, for every \(t\),

\( \displaystyle kt-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\max\{t-v_i,0\} \leq kt-\sum_{j=1}^{k}(t-v_{(j)}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{(j)}. \)

Here the inequality follows because the sum of positive parts over all indices is at least the sum of \(t-v_{(j)}\) over the first \(k\) indices. Equality holds at \(t=v_{(k)}\): terms after the first \(k\) contribute zero, while terms among the first \(k\) equal \(t-v_{(j)}\). This also covers ties.

Construct a polytope \(Q\) with variables \(x\), \(y_k\), \(t_k\), and \(u_{ki}\), subject to \(x\in P\) and, for every \(k,i\),

\( 0\leq t_k\leq1,\qquad 0\leq u_{ki}\leq1,\qquad u_{ki}\geq t_k-f_i(x), \)

together with, for every \(k\),

\( \displaystyle 0\leq y_k\leq k,\qquad y_k\leq kt_k-\sum_{i=1}^{K}u_{ki}. \)

The identity above proves both of the following:

  • Every point of \(Q\) satisfies \(y_k\leq S_k(x)\) for every \(k\).
  • Every \(x\in P\) extends to a point of \(Q\) satisfying \(y_k=S_k(x)\) simultaneously for every \(k\). Choose \(t_k=f_{(k)}(x)\) and \(u_{ki}=\max\{t_k-f_i(x),0\}\).

The polytope \(Q\) is nonempty and bounded. Its numbers of variables and constraints are \(O(nm+K^2)\), and its rational encoding length is polynomial in the original input length.

4. Exact lexicographic optimization takes polynomial time

Set \(Q_0=Q\). For \(k=1,\ldots,K\), solve the linear program

\( \displaystyle \alpha_k=\max\{y_k:z\in Q_{k-1}\}, \)

where \(z\) denotes all variables, and then set

\( Q_k=\{z\in Q_{k-1}:y_k=\alpha_k\}. \)

Each set \(Q_k\) is a nonempty face of \(Q_{k-1}\), because it is the set of maximizers of a linear functional on a nonempty compact polytope. Inductively, it is a face of \(Q\). Any point of \(Q_K\) therefore lexicographically maximizes \((y_1,\ldots,y_K)\) over \(Q\).

For completeness, the encoding lengths remain polynomial throughout this procedure. Every vertex of a face of \(Q\) is a vertex of \(Q\), so each \(\alpha_k\) is a coordinate of some vertex of the original polytope \(Q\). Vertex coordinates have polynomial bit length: after clearing denominators, a vertex solves a nonsingular subsystem of tight constraints, and Cramer’s rule bounds its numerator and denominator by determinants of polynomial-size integer matrices. Thus every added equality has polynomial encoding length. The algorithm uses \(K\) polynomial-size rational linear programs.

Let \(z^*\in Q_K\), with allocation component \(x^*\) and coordinates \(y^*\). Necessarily,

\( y_k^*=S_k(x^*)\quad\text{for every }k. \)

Otherwise, extending \(x^*\) with all coordinates equal to \(S_k(x^*)\), as permitted by Section 3, would weakly increase every coordinate and strictly increase at least one. That would contradict lexicographic optimality of \(y^*\).

If another allocation \(x'\in P\) had a lexicographically larger cumulative-sum vector, its extension to \(Q\) would likewise contradict optimality of \(y^*\). Therefore \(x^*\) lexicographically maximizes the cumulative sums and, by Section 2, the nondecreasing sorted recorded values. It is an \(\mathrm{FS\text{-}VER}\) allocation.

5. Feasibility is not guaranteed

Take one object type \(o\) with supply \(q_o=2\), and two applicant types \(a,b\), each of mass \(1\). Both have only \(o\) in their preferences, and the object ranks \(a\) strictly above \(b\).

The allocation constraints force \(x_{a,o}=x_{b,o}=1\). For the pair \((a,o)\), the preference sum is empty and only \(a\) has priority weakly above \(a\). Its stability inequality therefore requires

\( \displaystyle 0+\frac{x_{a,o}}{q_o}=\frac12\geq1, \)

which is false.

Thus the literal task of returning an allocation on every stated input is not well-defined. With the necessary convention that infeasibility may be reported—or under a feasibility promise—the exact computation is in \(\mathrm{P}\), including weak preferences and priorities.
