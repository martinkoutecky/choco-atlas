The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given bases \(B\), a fleet size \(M\), a horizon \(H\), a relocation limit \(r\), a coverage threshold \(f\), and supported zone types \(t=(A_t,\zeta_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there is a sequence of integer placements \(x^s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{B}\), \(s=1,\ldots,H\), with \(\sum_{b\in B}x_b^s\le M\), such that each round covers mass at least \(f\), consecutive placements satisfy \(\sum_{b\in B}|x_b^{s+1}-x_b^s|\le 2r\), and the spread of average type coverage is at most a given \(\delta\). Type \(t\) is covered in round \(s\) when \(\sum_{b\in A_t}x_b^s\ge\zeta_t\); its average coverage is \(y_t=H^{-1}\sum_s\mathbf{1}[\sum_{b\in A_t}x_b^s\ge\zeta_t]\), and the spread is \(\max_t y_t-\min_t y_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(B\) be a nonempty set of bases and \(m=|B|\). Inputs are integers \(M\ge1\), \(H\ge2\), \(r\ge0\), and rationals \(f,\delta\in[0,1]\). All numbers are binary encoded; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) distinct types \(t=(A_t,\zeta_t)\), where \(A_t\subseteq B\) is explicitly listed and \(\zeta_t\) is a positive integer, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
A policy consists of integer placements \(x^s\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{B}\), for \(s\in\{1,\ldots,H\}\), satisfying \(\sum_{b\in B}x_b^s\le M\). Define \(c_t^s=\mathbf1[\sum_{b\in A_t}x_b^s\ge\zeta_t]\) and \(y_t=H^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{H}c_t^s\); all members of a type receive this same coverage.
A policy is feasible when \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t^s\ge f\) in every round, \(\sum_{b\in B}|x_b^{s+1}-x_b^s|\le2r\) for every \(s<H\), and \(\max_t y_t-\min_t y_t\le\delta\). There is no prescribed initial placement or final-to-initial transition.

Is deciding whether a feasible policy exists in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,M,H,r\), distinguishing numerical dependence from dependence on binary encoding lengths.

For discrete zones, ordering a prescribed multiset of individually admissible placements subject to the same transition bound is equivalent to Hamiltonian Path in the graph having one vertex per occurrence and edges between placements at distance at most \(2r\) ([Lodi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03716v3)); this is a structural equivalence, not a hardness classification of the full decision problem.
No result settling this exact mass-based decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified binary encoding for every numerical input and defined L as total input bit length, including the horizon.
  • Specified a nonempty base set, positive integer fleet size and demands, nonnegative integer relocation limit, horizon at least two, and rational coverage and spread thresholds in [0,1].
  • Allowed empty accessibility sets and demands exceeding the fleet size: under the intended threshold definition, such types are never covered.
  • Allowed support size one because multiple stakeholders can share one type; the discrete requirement of at least two stakeholders does not require distinct types.
  • Specified that coverage is common to all members of a type, with no splitting of type masses into separately treated groups.
  • Specified free initial placement and no cyclic transition, following the constraints only between consecutive rounds.

Classification

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already when \(\ H=2\), \(\ r=1\), \(\ f=1\), \(\delta=0\), every threshold is \(1\), every acceptance set has two bases, and all types have equal mass.

Hardness also holds with the interior values \(f=\delta=1/2\), when \(r=0\). Thus, unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), there is no polynomial-time algorithm for the general binary-encoded problem.

We prove these claims and give an exact algorithm with explicit numerical dependence on \(M\) and \(H\).

1. Polynomial reduction from Vertex Cover

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete decision problem Vertex Cover: given a finite simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether some \(C\subseteq V\) satisfies \(|C|\le k\) and meets every edge. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is established, for example, in [CMU’s complexity lecture notes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/academic/class/15451-f14/www/lectures/lec17.pdf).

We may restrict to instances with \(q=|E|\ge1\) and \(1\le k\le |V|\): instances outside these restrictions are directly decidable and can be replaced by fixed yes- or no-instances satisfying the restrictions.

Given such an instance, construct a society as follows:

  • Set \(B=V\), so \(m=|V|\).
  • For every edge \(e=\{u,v\}\in E\), create the type \(t_e=(\{u,v\},1)\).
  • Give every type mass \(\mu_{t_e}=1/q\).
  • Set \(M=k\), \(H=2\), \(r=1\), \(f=1\), and \(\delta=0\).

The types are distinct because the graph is simple. Their masses are positive and sum to \(1\). The construction has polynomial bit length: there are exactly \(\tau=q\) types, and each mass has denominator \(q\).

A vertex cover gives a feasible policy

Suppose \(C\subseteq V\) is a vertex cover with \(|C|\le k\). In both rounds, place one unit at every vertex of \(C\) and zero elsewhere:

\(x_v^1=x_v^2=\mathbf1[v\in C]\).

The budget holds because \(\sum_v x_v^s=|C|\le M\). Every edge has a selected endpoint, so every type is covered in both rounds. Consequently, the covered mass is \(1=f\), and every type has \(y_t=1\).

The transition distance and fairness range are both zero. Hence the policy is feasible.

A feasible policy gives a vertex cover

Conversely, suppose the constructed instance has a feasible policy. In its first round,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{e\in E}\frac1q\,c_{t_e}^1\ge1.\)

There are \(q\) summands, each at most \(1/q\). Therefore every \(c_{t_e}^1\) equals \(1\), so

\(\displaystyle x_u^1+x_v^1\ge1\qquad\text{for every }\{u,v\}\in E.\)

Define \(C=\{v\in V:x_v^1\ge1\}\). Every edge meets \(C\), and integrality gives

\(\displaystyle |C|\le\sum_{v\in V}x_v^1\le M=k.\)

Thus \(C\) is a vertex cover of the required size.

This proves the equivalence and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. The restricted problem is also in \(\mathrm{NP}\): two placement vectors form a certificate of length \(O(m\log(M+1))\), and all constraints can be checked using exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time. Hence this restriction is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

2. Hardness with interior mass and fairness bounds

The preceding reduction uses \(f=1\). The following observation gives hardness with \(f=\delta=1/2\).

Lemma. If \(r=0\), \(f>0\), and \(\delta<1\), a feasible policy exists if and only if a budget-feasible placement covers every type.

Proof. The transition constraints force \(x^{s+1}=x^s\) for every \(s\). Therefore each type has constant coverage, and \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\).

If some type is covered and another is uncovered, the fairness range is \(1>\delta\). Thus either all types are covered or none are covered. The latter possibility violates the positive mass threshold \(f>0\).

Conversely, repeating a placement that covers every type satisfies the mass, transition, and fairness constraints. \(\square\)

Apply the same graph construction with \(H=2\), \(r=0\), and \(f=\delta=1/2\). By the lemma and the preceding vertex-cover equivalence, this restriction is also \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

All numerical values in these reductions are polynomially bounded in the graph size, including \(M\) and the mass denominators. Therefore hardness does not depend on exponentially large binary-encoded numbers. In particular, an algorithm polynomial jointly in \(m,\tau,L,M,H,r\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

3. An exact dynamic program

Let

\(\displaystyle \mathcal X=\left\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{B}:\sum_{b\in B}x_b\le M\right\},\qquad N=|\mathcal X|=\binom{M+m}{m}.\)

The formula follows by introducing the nonnegative slack coordinate \(M-\sum_bx_b\) and counting compositions of \(M\) into \(m+1\) nonnegative parts.

For each placement \(x\), compute its coverage vector \(c(x)\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}\). Retain precisely the individually admissible placements

\(\displaystyle \mathcal V=\left\{x\in\mathcal X:\sum_t\mu_t c_t(x)\ge f\right\}.\)

If \(\mathcal V\) is empty, reject. Otherwise, form the directed transition relation

\(\displaystyle \mathcal E=\{(x,z)\in\mathcal V^2:\|z-x\|_1\le2r\}.\)

Write \(e=|\mathcal E|\le N^2\). This relation is symmetric and contains every self-loop, allowing repeated placements.

State and recurrence

Number the types \(1,\ldots,\tau\). After \(s\) rounds, let

\(\displaystyle n_t(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_t(x^j).\)

Store the last placement \(x^s\) and only the differences

\(\displaystyle d_t(s)=n_t(s)-n_1(s),\qquad t=2,\ldots,\tau.\)

Every difference lies in \([-H,H]\), so there are at most

\(\displaystyle K=(2H+1)^{\tau-1}\)

difference vectors. When \(\tau=1\), the vector is empty and \(K=1\).

Initialize a state for every \(x\in\mathcal V\), with

\(\displaystyle d_t(1)=c_t(x)-c_1(x).\)

From a reachable state \((x,d)\) at round \(s\), for every \((x,z)\in\mathcal E\), mark \((z,d')\) reachable at round \(s+1\), where

\(\displaystyle d'_t=d_t+c_t(z)-c_1(z).\)

Induction on \(s\) proves that these states represent exactly the length-\(s\) policies satisfying all budgets, per-round mass constraints, and transitions. The initialization allows every admissible first placement, as required by the absence of a prescribed initial placement.

Terminal fairness test

Compute the integer \(Q=\lfloor H\delta\rfloor\) exactly. Since coverage counts are integers, final fairness is equivalent to

\(\displaystyle \max_t n_t(H)-\min_t n_t(H)\le Q.\)

Subtracting \(n_1(H)\) from all counts preserves their range. Hence accept precisely when some reachable terminal state satisfies

\(\displaystyle \max\{0,d_2,\ldots,d_\tau\}-\min\{0,d_2,\ldots,d_\tau\}\le Q.\)

No final-to-initial transition is tested.

4. Running time and encoding dependence

All arithmetic can be exact. For example, multiplying the denominators of the masses gives a common denominator whose bit length is at most their total input bit length. Mass comparisons therefore use integers of polynomial bit length. The same holds for computing \(Q\), transition comparisons, and count updates.

Enumerating coverage vectors costs \(O(Nm\tau)\) arithmetic operations. Constructing transitions by checking every placement pair costs \(O(N^2m)\). The dynamic program costs \(O(He\tau K)\). Consequently, a bit-operation bound is

\(\displaystyle T=O\!\left(Nm\tau+N^2m+He\tau(2H+1)^{\tau-1}\right)\operatorname{poly}(L+m+\tau),\qquad N=\binom{M+m}{m}.\)

In particular, using \(e\le N^2\),

\(\displaystyle T=O\!\left(Nm\tau+N^2m+HN^2\tau(2H+1)^{\tau-1}\right)\operatorname{poly}(L+m+\tau).\)

Two consecutive layers suffice. Including stored transitions and coverage vectors, space is bounded by

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(N^2+N\tau+N(2H+1)^{\tau-1}\right)\operatorname{poly}(L+m+\tau).\)

These bounds have the following interpretation:

  • For fixed \(m\) and \(\tau\), the algorithm is polynomial in the numerical values \(M,H\) and in \(L\): use \(N\le(M+1)^m\). This is a pseudopolynomial bound, not a polynomial bound in binary input length.
  • With variable \(m,\tau\), the exponents depend on these dimensions.
  • This implementation has no numerical dependence on \(r\) beyond constructing the transition relation; arithmetic depends on its binary encoding length. Moreover, \(\|z-x\|_1\le\sum_bz_b+\sum_bx_b\le2M\), so every transition is allowed when \(r\ge M\).

For the hardness restrictions above, it suffices to enumerate placements and test whether one covers every type, taking \(O(Nm\tau)\operatorname{poly}(L+m+\tau)\) time. Their hardness is therefore already present in choosing a single placement, independently of any prescribed-multiset ordering problem.

5. Binary horizons and complexity upper bounds

When \(H\) is fixed, or more generally polynomially bounded in \(L\), an explicit policy is a polynomial-size certificate. Thus the general problem restricted to polynomially bounded horizons is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

For unrestricted binary \(H\), listing every round need not give a polynomial-size certificate. Nevertheless, the full problem belongs to \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\).

To verify this upper bound, a nondeterministic algorithm guesses successive placement vectors and retains only the previous and current vectors, the round index, and the \(\tau\) coverage counts. It checks each budget, mass requirement, and transition immediately, then checks fairness after round \(H\). Every branch terminates after exactly \(H\) rounds. Its workspace is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(m\log(M+1)+\tau\log(H+1)\right)+\operatorname{poly}(L).\)

Bases appearing in no acceptance set can be discarded: setting their coordinates to zero preserves coverage and cannot increase budgets or transition distances. Thus the required dimension is bounded by the explicitly listed input. The workspace is polynomial in \(L\). Applying the standard identity \(\mathrm{NPSPACE}=\mathrm{PSPACE}\), a consequence of [Savitch’s theorem](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~eblais/cs365/w26/space), proves the claim.

The requested classification is therefore resolved: the full decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The arguments additionally establish \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\) membership, without asserting \(\mathrm{NP}\) membership for unrestricted binary horizons.
