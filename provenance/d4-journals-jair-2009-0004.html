The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given disjoint candidate sets \(C,D\), a target \(p\in C\), an integer \(k\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\cup D\), and rational \(0\le\alpha\le1\), does there exist \(E\subseteq D\) with \(|E|\le k\) such that, after restricting every ranking to \(C\cup E\), \(p\) is a Copeland\(_\alpha\) winner under \(\mu\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

The input explicitly lists a rational probability distribution \(\mu\) of total mass \(1\) over strict complete rankings of \(C\cup D\), where \(C\cap D=\varnothing\), \(p\in C\), and \(k\in\mathbb N\).
For candidates \(x,y\), \(x\) defeats \(y\) when \(\mu[x\succ y]>\tfrac12\); otherwise they tie. In Copeland\(_\alpha\), a victory gives \(1\) point, a loss \(0\), and a tie \(\alpha\); winners have maximum score.
For \(E\subseteq D\), restrict every ranking to \(C\cup E\) before computing the winner set.

Target

Is deciding whether some \(E\subseteq D\) with \(|E|\le k\) makes \(p\) a Copeland\(_\alpha\) winner under \(\mu\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Nearest known results

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result and conventions

For every fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), the stated problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The finite-voter hardness result transfers by a polynomial-time normalization of voter multiplicities into probabilities.

I use the standard Copeland convention: \(x\) defeats \(y\) when \(\mu[x\succ y]>\tfrac12\), loses when \(\mu[x\succ y]<\tfrac12\), and ties when equality holds. Thus “otherwise they tie” means neither candidate defeats the other. Reading it as declaring a tie whenever \(x\) fails to defeat \(y\) would conflict with the stated loss rule.

The winner condition is nonunique: \(p\) need only attain the maximum score.

Finite-voter hardness theorem

We use the following established result: for every fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), constructive control by adding at most \(k\) candidates for Copeland\(_\alpha\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for explicitly listed voters with strict complete rankings, in the nonunique-winner model. This is Theorem 4.9 of Faliszewski, Hemaspaandra, Hemaspaandra, and Rothe, [*Llull and Copeland Voting Computationally Resist Bribery and Control*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.4484#page=44).

Its input consists of disjoint candidate sets \(C,D\), rankings over \(C\cup D\), a designated candidate \(p\in C\), and a budget \(k\). These are precisely the combinatorial data required here.

Polynomial-time reduction

Fix such an \(\alpha\). Let a finite-voter instance have voter list \((R_1,\ldots,R_n)\), with \(n>0\). For each distinct ranking \(R\) appearing in the list, let \(m_R\) be its multiplicity. Construct the explicitly listed distribution

\(\mu(R)=m_R/n.\)

Keep \(C,D,p,k\) unchanged.

This is a valid rational probability distribution because all multiplicities are positive and

\(\sum_R\mu(R)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_Rm_R=1.\)

There are at most \(n\) support rankings. Each numerator and denominator has \(O(\log(n+1))\) bits. Grouping identical rankings and writing their probabilities therefore takes polynomial time and produces polynomial output length.

If the source formulation permits an empty voter list, handle it separately: replace it by two mutually reversed rankings, each with probability \(\tfrac12\). Every pair then ties, just as with zero voters, including after any candidate restriction.

Preservation of every candidate restriction

Let \(E\subseteq D\) be arbitrary and put \(S=C\cup E\). For distinct \(x,y\in S\), deleting other candidates from a strict ranking preserves the relative order of \(x,y\). Consequently,

\(\mu[x\succ y]=\sum_{R:\,x\succ_R y}\frac{m_R}{n}=\frac{|\{i:x\succ_{R_i}y\}|}{n}.\)

Writing \(N_{xy}=|\{i:x\succ_{R_i}y\}|\), strict completeness gives \(N_{yx}=n-N_{xy}\). Hence

\(\mu[x\succ y]>\tfrac12\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N_{xy}>N_{yx},\)

and

\(\mu[x\succ y]=\tfrac12\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N_{xy}=N_{yx}.\)

Thus every pairwise victory, loss, and tie in \(S\) agrees between the source election and the constructed distribution. Each candidate receives exactly the same Copeland\(_\alpha\) score in both instances, so their winner sets coincide.

Because the candidate sets and budget are unchanged, this proves

\(\exists E\subseteq D,\ |E|\le k:\ p\text{ wins in the source instance}\)

if and only if

\(\exists E\subseteq D,\ |E|\le k:\ p\text{ wins under }\mu.\)

This polynomial-time many-one reduction establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a subset \(E\subseteq D\), represented by a bit vector of length \(|D|\). Check \(|E|\le k\), then compute scores on \(S=C\cup E\).

For completeness, exact rational arithmetic remains polynomial even when probabilities have large binary denominators. Write the listed probabilities as \(a_i/b_i\), with \(b_i>0\), and let

\(B=\prod_i b_i.\)

The bit length of \(B\) is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths, up to an additive constant, and is therefore polynomial in the input length. For each distinct \(x,y\in S\), compute the integer

\(A_{xy}=\sum_{i:\,x\succ_{R_i}y}a_i(B/b_i).\)

Then \(\mu[x\succ y]=A_{xy}/B\). Comparing \(2A_{xy}\) with \(B\) determines the pairwise outcome exactly. There are at most \(|C\cup D|^2\) ordered pairs, and all these computations use integers of polynomial bit length.

Finally, write \(\alpha=u/v\), where \(u,v\) are integers and \(v>0\). If candidate \(x\) has \(w_x\) victories and \(t_x\) ties, its score is

\(s_\alpha(x)=w_x+\frac{u}{v}t_x.\)

Compare the integer scores \(vw_x+ut_x\). Accept exactly when \(p\)'s score is at least every other candidate's score. This is a polynomial-time verifier.

Therefore the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for every fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). The same conclusion holds if rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is supplied as part of the input: the verifier remains polynomial, and hardness already holds for any fixed allowed value.
