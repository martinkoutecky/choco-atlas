Theorem 5.2 (NPhardness) — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(O,E)\), rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_o\ge 0\), and finitely many types \(a\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\) and nonnegative additive valuations \(v_a\), decide whether masses \(x_{a,S}\ge 0\) exist for independent schedules \(S\subseteq O\). Require \(\sum_S x_{a,S}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_{a,S:o\in S}x_{a,S}\le\beta_o\), and, for each type \(a\), every pair of schedules \(S,T\) with positive mass to satisfy either \(T=\emptyset\) or \(v_a(S)\ge v_a(T\setminus\{o\})\) for some \(o\in T\). Require mass maximality as well: if capacity for \(o\) is slack, every positive-mass schedule contains \(o\) or a neighbor of \(o\). The anchor-aligned restriction has one type.

The input consists of a finite simple undirected conflict graph \(G=(O,E)\), where \(m=|O|\), binary rational capacities \(\beta_o\geq0\), and binary rational nonnegative additive item valuations \(v(o)\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Let \(\mathcal I(G)\) contain all independent schedules, including \(\emptyset\). An allocation assigns real masses \(x_S\geq0\) to schedules \(S\in\mathcal I(G)\), with \(\sum_S x_S=1\) and usage \(u_o:=\sum_{S:o\in S}x_S\leq\beta_o\).
Require EF1 across every ordered pair of positive-mass schedules: if \(x_S,x_T>0\), then either \(T=\emptyset\) or \(v(S)\geq v(T\setminus\{o\})\) for some \(o\in T\).

Is deciding whether such an EF1 and mass-maximal allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with \(\tau=1\)?

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). Every valid instance with \(\tau=1\) admits an EF1 and mass-maximal allocation.

The proof uses the ability to split schedule masses. It applies to arbitrary conflict graphs, nonnegative additive valuations, and nonnegative capacities, including zero capacities.

1. Choose an allocation by finite lexicographic optimization

Let \(\mathcal I=\mathcal I(G)\). For each schedule \(S\), write \(v(S)=\sum_{o\in S}v(o)\).

Consider the polytope \(\mathcal P\) of allocations satisfying normalization and capacities:

\[ \mathcal P=\left\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\mathcal I}: \sum_{S\in\mathcal I}x_S=1,\quad \sum_{S\ni o}x_S\leq\beta_o\text{ for every }o\in O \right\}. \]

This polytope is nonempty: assigning mass \(1\) to \(\emptyset\) is feasible. It is compact because it is a closed subset of the finite-dimensional probability simplex.

List the distinct schedule values in increasing order as \(a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_q\). Define

\[ p_i(x)=\sum_{\substack{S\in\mathcal I\\v(S)=a_i}}x_S. \]

Choose an allocation that lexicographically minimizes \((p_0(x),p_1(x),\ldots,p_q(x))\). Thus we first minimize mass at the lowest utility, then minimize mass at the next utility subject to the first minimum, and continue.

Such allocations exist: each successive minimization is of a continuous linear function over a nonempty compact set.

Among these lexicographically optimal allocations, choose \(x^\star\) maximizing the expected schedule cardinality

\[ C(x)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal I}|S|x_S. \]

Again, a maximum exists by compactness. We prove that \(x^\star\) has both required properties.

2. EF1 follows from splitting a richer schedule

Suppose EF1 fails. Then there are schedules \(S,T\) with \(x^\star_S,x^\star_T>0\), where \(T\neq\emptyset\), such that

\[ v(T\setminus\{o\})>v(S)\qquad\text{for every }o\in T. \]

Put \(k=|T|\). Because valuations are nonnegative, \(v(S)\geq0\). Consequently \(k\geq2\): if \(T\) were a singleton, deleting its item would leave value \(0\).

Nonnegativity also gives \(v(T)>v(S)\), so \(S\neq T\).

Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) sufficiently small that

\[ \varepsilon\leq x^\star_S,\qquad (k-1)\varepsilon\leq x^\star_T. \]

Construct a new allocation \(y\) by:

  • removing mass \(\varepsilon\) from \(S\);
  • removing mass \((k-1)\varepsilon\) from \(T\);
  • adding mass \(\varepsilon\) to each of the \(k\) schedules \(T\setminus\{o\}\), for \(o\in T\).

All added schedules are independent because they are subsets of \(T\). The chosen bounds ensure nonnegative masses. None of the deletion schedules equals \(S\), since each has value strictly greater than \(v(S)\).

The total mass change is

\[ -\varepsilon-(k-1)\varepsilon+k\varepsilon=0. \]

For each item in \(T\), exactly \(k-1\) of the deletion schedules contain it. Thus replacing the removed mass of \(T\) preserves every item’s usage. Removing mass from \(S\) only decreases usages. Explicitly,

\[ u_o(y)=u_o(x^\star)-\varepsilon\,\mathbf 1_{\{o\in S\}} \qquad\text{for every }o\in O. \]

Therefore \(y\in\mathcal P\).

Every schedule affected other than \(S\) has value strictly greater than \(v(S)\). Hence the utility-level masses below \(v(S)\) remain unchanged, while the mass at \(v(S)\) decreases by \(\varepsilon\). This strictly improves the lexicographic objective, contradicting the choice of \(x^\star\).

Therefore \(x^\star\) satisfies EF1 across every ordered pair of positive-mass schedules.

3. Mass maximality follows from the cardinality tie-break

Suppose mass maximality fails. Then some item \(o\) has \(u_o(x^\star)<\beta_o\), and some schedule \(S\) with \(x^\star_S>0\) contains neither \(o\) nor any neighbor of \(o\).

Therefore \(S'=S\cup\{o\}\) is independent. Choose

\[ 0<\delta\leq\min\{x^\star_S,\ \beta_o-u_o(x^\star)\}. \]

Transfer mass \(\delta\) from \(S\) to \(S'\). This preserves total mass, increases only the usage of \(o\), and respects its capacity. Thus the resulting allocation \(y\) belongs to \(\mathcal P\).

There are two cases.

If \(v(o)>0\): additivity gives \(v(S')=v(S)+v(o)>v(S)\). The transfer leaves all lower utility-level masses unchanged and decreases the mass at \(v(S)\). This contradicts lexicographic optimality.

If \(v(o)=0\): additivity gives \(v(S')=v(S)\). Every utility-level mass remains unchanged, so \(y\) is also lexicographically optimal. But

\[ C(y)=C(x^\star)+\delta, \]

contradicting the cardinality tie-break.

Both cases are impossible. Hence every supported schedule contains or neighbors every item whose capacity is unsaturated, exactly as required.

4. Complexity and the discrete comparison

The proof establishes existence for every valid input under the restriction \(\tau=1\). The decision algorithm therefore returns YES.

Its running time is \(O(L)\) if it reads the entire input; under the valid-input promise, the answer itself requires constant time. Thus the problem is in \(\mathrm P\), with no additional dependence on \(m\) beyond its contribution to \(L\), and with \(\tau=1\).

The [cited discrete hardness theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09930v1#S5.Thmtheorem2) concerns allocations to indivisible agents. The crucial perturbation above replaces one schedule’s mass by masses on all its single-item deletions and uses the extra mass to reduce a poorer schedule’s mass. That operation is available in this continuous model.
