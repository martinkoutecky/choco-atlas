The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to \(1\), rational piecewise-linear nondecreasing update rules \(F_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) with \(F_t(0)=0\) and \(F_t(1)=1\), and a rational initial yes fraction \(q_0\), consider each population size \(N\) for which all \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq_0\) are integers. Start with \(Nq_0\) yes opinions; when the current yes count is \(k\), each type-\(t\) agent independently says yes at the next step with probability \(F_t(k/N)\). Let \(\alpha_N\) be the probability of eventually reaching unanimous yes. Does \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\alpha_N\) exist along these population sizes, and, if so, what is its exact value?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(T\) lists the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a continuous, nondecreasing piecewise-linear rule \(F_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), specified by rational breakpoints and values, with \(F_t(0)=0\) and \(F_t(1)=1\). The initial fraction is rational \(q_0\in[0,1]\).
For each integer \(N\ge2\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\) for every type and \(Nq_0\in\mathbb Z\), there are \(N\mu_t\) agents of fixed type \(t\). Initially \(Nq_0\) agents say yes.
At each synchronous step, conditional on the current yes count \(k\), all agents draw fresh independent opinions; a type-\(t\) agent says yes with probability \(F_t(k/N)\), including her own opinion in the count.
Let \(\alpha_N\) be the probability of ever reaching \(N\) yes opinions. Other closed classes are allowed; reaching one that excludes unanimous yes contributes zero.
Let \(m\) be the total number of linear pieces and \(L\) the total binary input length, including a comparison threshold \(r\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb Q\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Does \(\alpha=\lim_{N\to\infty}\alpha_N\) always exist along admissible population sizes, and, whenever it exists, what is its exact value?
Is deciding whether this limit exists and satisfies \(\alpha\ge r\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite anonymous societies, absorption probabilities are computable using a count chain with \(N+1\) states, yielding polynomial-time computation in explicitly represented population size and rational input length ([finite result](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/151381/files/NDL2013-051.pdf)).
A related frequency-dependent Wright–Fisher model has exact large-population fixation limits for a fixed initial number of advantageous individuals under strict dominance; it does not cover arbitrary fixed heterogeneous piecewise-linear rules ([related result](https://people.tamu.edu/~roiterst/papers/JMB_2017r.pdf)). No result settling the stated general limit or its decision complexity was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted piecewise-linear rules as continuous functions encoded by rational breakpoints and values.
  • Specified synchronous updating with fresh conditional independence at every step, fixed agent types, and inclusion of each agent's own opinion in the population count.
  • Restricted admissible population sizes to integers at least two, matching the finite model.
  • Retained all rational initial fractions in the unit interval, including terminal initial states, whose hitting probabilities are defined directly; no promise of eventual consensus is imposed.
  • Introduced a rational threshold and a total decision problem that accepts exactly when the limit exists and is at least the threshold, so the polynomial-time versus NP-hard question does not require an unspecified representation of exact real outputs.
  • Defined the otherwise unspecified parameter m as the total number of linear pieces and L as the complete binary input length.

Status: rigorously proved partial results

I do not settle universal existence of the limit or the complexity of the general decision problem.

I establish two additional classes beyond the verified results in NOTES:

  • An interval of fixed points with outward drift: if the aggregate mean equals the identity on $[a,b]$, has negative drift below $a$, positive drift above $b$, and has positive conditional variance throughout $[a,b]$, then the limiting fixation probability is linear across that interval.
  • An isolated repelling fixed point: the limit exists, including when the initial fraction is exactly the fixed point. At that initial fraction, the limit is determined by a population-independent Gaussian recursion involving the two one-sided slopes. Equal slopes give exactly $1/2$. Unequal slopes generally give a biased limit.

All ingredients taken from NOTES are proved below. The unresolved points are stated at the end.

1. The count chain and elementary estimates

Let $K_n$ be the number of yes opinions after step $n$, and write $X_n=K_n/N$. Define
$$ G(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tF_t(x), \qquad V(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tF_t(x)\bigl(1-F_t(x)\bigr). $$
Both functions are continuous. The function $G$ is nondecreasing and piecewise linear.

Conditional on $X_n=x$, the next count is
$$ K_{n+1}=\sum_{t\in T}B_t, \qquad B_t\sim\operatorname{Bin}\bigl(N\mu_t,F_t(x)\bigr), $$
with independent summands. Hence
$$ \mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid X_n=x]=G(x), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_{n+1}\mid X_n=x)=\frac{V(x)}{N}. $$

Let $h_N(x)$ denote the probability of ever reaching $1$, starting from a lattice state $x\in\{0,1/N,\ldots,1\}$. Thus $\alpha_N=h_N(q_0)$.

The endpoints are absorbing, so
$$ h_N(0)=0,\qquad h_N(1)=1. $$
In particular, the initial conditions $q_0=0$ and $q_0=1$ are completely resolved. Below, the interesting initial fractions are interior.

Admissible population sizes are infinite in number: multiples of a common denominator of the masses and $q_0$ are admissible.

1.1 Monotonicity of fixation probabilities

For every admissible $N$, the function $h_N$ is nondecreasing on the lattice.

To prove this, couple two chains starting at $x\le y$. For each agent at each step, use the same independent uniform random variable in both chains. Because every $F_t$ is nondecreasing, an agent who says yes in the lower chain also says yes in the upper chain. Thus the ordering of the counts persists at every step.

If the lower chain reaches $1$, the upper chain is also at $1$ at that step. Therefore
$$ x\le y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_N(x)\le h_N(y). $$

The Markov property also gives
$$ h_N(x)=\mathbb E_x[h_N(X_1)]. $$
Consequently, $h_N(X_n)$ is a bounded martingale. For every almost surely finite stopping time $S$, bounded convergence applied to $S\wedge n$ gives
$$ h_N(x)=\mathbb E_x[h_N(X_S)]. $$
This identity does not require endpoint absorption to occur almost surely.

1.2 Concentration

For a Bernoulli random variable $B$, the logarithm of its centered moment-generating function has second derivative at most $1/4$: that derivative is a Bernoulli variance under exponential tilting. Its value and first derivative at zero are both zero. Integrating this bound twice gives
$$ \mathbb E[e^{s(B-\mathbb EB)}]\le e^{s^2/8} \qquad(s\in\mathbb R). $$
Independence of the $N$ opinions therefore yields
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[e^{s(X_{n+1}-G(x))}\mid X_n=x\right] \le e^{s^2/(8N)}. $$
Applying Markov's inequality with $s=4N\eta$, and then with $s=-4N\eta$, proves
$$ \mathbb P\!\left(X_{n+1}-G(x)\ge\eta\mid X_n=x\right) \le e^{-2N\eta^2}, $$
$$ \mathbb P\!\left(X_{n+1}-G(x)\le-\eta\mid X_n=x\right) \le e^{-2N\eta^2}. $$

1.3 Deterministic fixed points

Because every mass is positive,
$$ V(c)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_t(c)\in\{0,1\}\text{ for every }t. $$
If also $G(c)=c$, then
$$ c=\sum_{\{t:F_t(c)=1\}}\mu_t. $$
Thus $Nc$ is integral for every admissible population size, and the next fraction from $c$ is deterministically $c$.

Such a point is also a barrier. If $x\le c$, the types with $F_t(c)=0$ necessarily say no at $x$, so the next fraction is at most $c$. If $x\ge c$, the types with $F_t(c)=1$ necessarily say yes at $x$, so the next fraction is at least $c$.

In particular, an interior deterministic fixed point excludes fixation at $1$ from every initial fraction at or below it.

2. Endpoint drift, with stopping justified

The following lemma will connect the local calculations to eventual fixation.

Lemma 1. Let $c\in[0,1]$.

  • If $G(x)>x$ for every $x\in(c,1)$, then for any lattice initial states $x_N\to q\in(c,1)$,

$$ h_N(x_N)\longrightarrow1. $$

  • If $G(x)<x$ for every $x\in(0,c)$, then for any lattice initial states $x_N\to q\in(0,c)$,

$$ h_N(x_N)\longrightarrow0. $$

All limits are along admissible population sizes.

Proof. Consider positive drift. Choose
$$ c<a<q<b<1. $$
On the compact interval $[a,b]$, there are constants $\delta,\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$ G(x)-x\ge\delta, \qquad G(x)\ge(1+\varepsilon)x. $$
Choose $\theta>0$ sufficiently small that
$$ (1-e^{-\theta})(1+\varepsilon)\ge\theta. $$
This is possible because $(1-e^{-\theta})/\theta\to1$ as $\theta\downarrow0$.

Independence and $1-u\le e^{-u}$ imply
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[e^{-\theta K_{n+1}}\mid X_n=x] &=\prod_t\left[1-F_t(x)(1-e^{-\theta})\right]^{N\mu_t}\\ &\le e^{-N(1-e^{-\theta})G(x)} \le e^{-\theta Nx} \end{aligned} $$
for $x\in[a,b]$.

For sufficiently large $N$, start at $x_N\in(a,b)$ and let $T$ be the first exit from $(a,b)$. The stopped exponential process is a supermartingale. Moreover,
$$ \mathbb E[X_{n\wedge T}] \ge x_N+\delta\,\mathbb E[n\wedge T]. $$
The left side is at most $1$, so $T$ has finite expectation. Since the stopped exponential process is bounded for fixed $N$, stopping gives
$$ \mathbb P_{x_N}(X_T\le a) \le e^{-\theta N(x_N-a)}. $$

Next start from any lattice state $y\ge b$, and stop at the first state at most $a$, or at $1$. Every intervening lattice state has strictly positive drift. Its finite minimum proves, by the same stopped-expectation argument, that this stopping time has finite expectation.

If $p$ is the probability of reaching $1$ at this stopping time, the stopped submartingale gives
$$ y\le \mathbb E[X_{\mathrm{stop}}]\le a(1-p)+p. $$
Therefore
$$ h_N(y)\ge p\ge\frac{b-a}{1-a}. $$
Combining the two stopping arguments,
$$ h_N(x_N)\ge \left(1-e^{-\theta N(x_N-a)}\right)\frac{b-a}{1-a}. $$
For each fixed $b$, take the lower limit in $N$. Then let $b\uparrow1$, keeping $a$ fixed. This proves convergence to $1$.

For negative drift, replace each rule by
$$ \widetilde F_t(x)=1-F_t(1-x). $$
The complemented process has aggregate mean
$$ \widetilde G(x)=1-G(1-x)>x \qquad(1-c<x<1). $$
The positive-drift result shows that the original process reaches $0$ with probability tending to $1$. The absorbing endpoint events are mutually exclusive, so its probability of reaching $1$ tends to zero. $\square$

3. A fixed interval with outward drift

Theorem 2. Suppose there are rational numbers $0<a<b<1$ such that
$$ G(x)<x\quad(0<x<a), $$
$$ G(x)=x\quad(a\le x\le b), $$
$$ G(x)>x\quad(b<x<1), $$
and
$$ V(x)>0\quad(a\le x\le b). $$
Then the limit exists for every rational initial fraction and equals
$$ \alpha= \begin{cases} 0,&0\le q_0\le a,\\[2mm] \dfrac{q_0-a}{b-a},&a<q_0<b,\\[3mm] 1,&b\le q_0\le1. \end{cases} $$

The positive-variance assumption excludes deterministic barriers within the fixed interval.

3.1 Upper bound inside the fixed interval

Fix $q=q_0\in[a,b)$. Choose $\ell\in(0,a)$ sufficiently close to $a$ that $G(\ell)>0$. Let $T$ be the first exit from $(\ell,b)$.

For $x\in[\ell,b]$, we have $G(x)\le x$. With $f(x)=(b-x)^2$,
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[f(X_{n+1})-f(x)\mid X_n=x] &=2(b-x)(x-G(x))+(x-G(x))^2+\frac{V(x)}N\\ &\ge \frac{(x-G(x))^2+V(x)}N. \end{aligned} $$
The continuous function
$$ H(x)=(x-G(x))^2+V(x) $$
is strictly positive throughout $[\ell,b]$: below $a$ the first term is positive, and on $[a,b]$ the second term is positive. Hence
$$ h_*=\min_{x\in[\ell,b]}H(x)>0. $$
Stopping the displayed increment inequality and using $0\le f\le1$ gives
$$ \mathbb E[T]\le \frac N{h_*}. $$

Fix $0<\eta<G(\ell)$. Before exit, monotonicity gives $G(X_n)\ge G(\ell)$. The concentration bound and a union bound over the expected number of steps imply
$$ \mathbb P\bigl(X_T<G(\ell)-\eta\bigr) \le \mathbb E[T]e^{-2N\eta^2} \le \frac N{h_*}e^{-2N\eta^2}. $$
Write the last bound as $\varepsilon_N$, so $\varepsilon_N\to0$.

Let
$$ p_N=\mathbb P(X_T\ge b). $$
The stopped fraction process is a supermartingale, so $\mathbb E[X_T]\le q$. On upper exit, $X_T\ge b$. Except on an event of probability at most $\varepsilon_N$, a lower exit satisfies $X_T\ge G(\ell)-\eta$. Consequently,
$$ q\ge p_Nb+(1-p_N)(G(\ell)-\eta)-\varepsilon_N, $$
and therefore
$$ p_N\le \frac{q-G(\ell)+\eta+\varepsilon_N}{b-G(\ell)+\eta}. $$

On lower exit, monotonicity of $h_N$ bounds the remaining fixation probability by
$$ h_N\!\left(\frac{\lfloor N\ell\rfloor}{N}\right), $$
which tends to zero by Lemma 1. On upper exit it is at most $1$. Thus
$$ \limsup_N h_N(q) \le \frac{q-G(\ell)+\eta}{b-G(\ell)+\eta}. $$
First let $\eta\downarrow0$, and then let $\ell\uparrow a$. Continuity gives $G(\ell)\to a$, proving
$$ \limsup_N h_N(q)\le\frac{q-a}{b-a}. $$
This includes the boundary conclusion $h_N(a)\to0$.

3.2 Lower bound inside the fixed interval

Fix $q\in(a,b]$, choose $u\in(b,1)$, and stop at the first exit $S$ from $(a,u)$.

Here $G(x)\ge x$. For $f(x)=(x-a)^2$,
$$ \mathbb E[f(X_{n+1})-f(x)\mid X_n=x] = 2(x-a)(G(x)-x)+(G(x)-x)^2+\frac{V(x)}N. $$
As before, $(G(x)-x)^2+V(x)$ is strictly positive on $[a,u]$. Hence $\mathbb E[S]\le C_uN$ for a constant independent of $N$.

Because $G(X_n)\le G(u)$ before exit, concentration implies
$$ \mathbb P(X_S>G(u)+\eta)\le C_uN e^{-2N\eta^2}=o(1) $$
for each fixed $\eta>0$.

Let $p_N=\mathbb P(X_S\ge u)$. The stopped fraction process is a submartingale. The lower exit is at most $a$, while the upper exit is at most $G(u)+\eta$ except on the exceptional event. Therefore
$$ q\le \mathbb E[X_S] \le a(1-p_N)+(G(u)+\eta)p_N+o(1), $$
so
$$ \liminf_Np_N\ge\frac{q-a}{G(u)+\eta-a}. $$
On upper exit, the remaining fixation probability is at least
$$ h_N\!\left(\frac{\lceil Nu\rceil}{N}\right)\longrightarrow1 $$
by Lemma 1. Hence, letting $\eta\downarrow0$ and then $u\downarrow b$,
$$ \liminf_Nh_N(q)\ge\frac{q-a}{b-a}. $$
This includes $h_N(b)\to1$.

Together with the upper bound and Lemma 1 outside $[a,b]$, this proves Theorem 2.

4. An isolated repelling fixed point

Suppose $c\in(0,1)$ satisfies
$$ G(c)=c, $$
$$ G(x)<x\quad(0<x<c), \qquad G(x)>x\quad(c<x<1). $$
Because $G$ is piecewise linear, it has one-sided affine representations near $c$:
$$ G(c+z)= \begin{cases} c+\lambda_-z,&-\delta\le z\le0,\\ c+\lambda_+z,&0\le z\le\delta \end{cases} $$
for some $\delta>0$.

Both slopes satisfy
$$ \lambda_->1,\qquad \lambda_+>1. $$
Indeed, on the left the difference $G(c+z)-(c+z)=(\lambda_--1)z$ must be negative, while on the right the analogous difference must be positive.

Theorem 3. Under these hypotheses:

  • If $q_0<c$, then $\alpha=0$.
  • If $q_0>c$, then $\alpha=1$.
  • If $q_0=c$ and $V(c)=0$, then $\alpha_N=0$ for every admissible $N$.
  • If $q_0=c$ and $V(c)>0$, then the limit exists and equals

$$ \alpha=p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+), $$
where $p$ is defined by the Gaussian recursion below.

Moreover,
$$ 0<p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)<1, $$
and
$$ p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)= \begin{cases} \dfrac12,&\lambda_-=\lambda_+,\\[2mm] >\dfrac12,&\lambda_+>\lambda_-,\\[2mm] <\dfrac12,&\lambda_+<\lambda_-. \end{cases} $$

The first two conclusions follow from Lemma 1, including the absorbing endpoint initial conditions. The third follows from Section 1.3. The rest of this section proves the fourth conclusion and the assertions about $p$.

4.1 The Gaussian recursion and its escape probability

Define
$$ H(y)= \begin{cases} \lambda_-y,&y\le0,\\ \lambda_+y,&y\ge0. \end{cases} $$
Let $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$ be independent standard normal variables, and define
$$ Y_0=y,\qquad Y_{n+1}=H(Y_n)+Z_{n+1}. $$
Write
$$ d=\min(\lambda_--1,\lambda_+-1)>0. $$

We first prove that, from every initial $y$, this recursion almost surely tends either to $+\infty$ or to $-\infty$.

Fix $M>0$. At a state $y>M$, set $\theta=dM$. Since the active slope is $\lambda_+$,
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[e^{-\theta Y_{n+1}}\mid Y_n=y] &=e^{-\theta\lambda_+y+\theta^2/2}\\ &\le e^{-\theta y}. \end{aligned} $$
The last inequality follows from
$$ \frac{\theta^2}{2}\le\theta(\lambda_+-1)y. $$
Stopping at the first state at most $M$, and first stopping also at a deterministic time $n$, gives
$$ \mathbb P_y(\text{ever reach a state at most }M) \le e^{-dM(y-M)} \qquad(y>M). $$
Only finite-time supermartingale inequalities are needed; letting $n\to\infty$ uses monotonicity of the hitting events. Reflection gives
$$ \mathbb P_y(\text{ever reach a state at least }-M) \le e^{-dM(|y|-M)} \qquad(y<-M). $$

For any $R>|y|$, let $\tau_R$ be the first time $|Y_n|\ge R$. This time is almost surely finite. In fact, while $|Y_n|<R$, the probability of leaving $[-R,R]$ on the next step is at least
$$ \mathbb P\!\left(Z_1\ge R+\max(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)R\right)>0. $$
Thus the probability of remaining inside for $n$ steps decreases geometrically.

For $R>M$, apply the preceding return bounds at $\tau_R$. With probability at least
$$ 1-e^{-dM(R-M)}, $$
all subsequent states remain on the exit side and have magnitude greater than $M$. Therefore
$$ \mathbb P_y\bigl(\text{not eventually confined to one side beyond }M\bigr) \le e^{-dM(R-M)}. $$
Letting $R\to\infty$ makes this probability zero. Applying the conclusion for all positive integers $M$, and observing that their eventual sides must agree, proves
$$ Y_n\longrightarrow+\infty \quad\text{or}\quad Y_n\longrightarrow-\infty \qquad\text{almost surely}. $$

Define
$$ w(y)=\mathbb P_y(Y_n\to+\infty), \qquad p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)=w(0). $$
The return bounds show that
$$ \lim_{y\to-\infty}w(y)=0, \qquad \lim_{y\to+\infty}w(y)=1. $$

This gives a population-independent exact characterization. With
$$ \varphi(z)=\frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}, $$
the function $w$ satisfies
$$ w(y)=\int_{\mathbb R}w(H(y)+z)\varphi(z)\,dz. $$
It is the unique bounded measurable function satisfying this equation and the two boundary limits. Indeed, if $u$ is another such function, iteration gives
$$ u(y)=\mathbb E_y[u(Y_n)]. $$
The almost sure escape result and bounded convergence imply $u(y)=w(y)$.

Thus the characterization is not merely another population-size limit: it is a fixed one-dimensional Gaussian integral equation depending only on two slopes.

4.2 Finite-time Gaussian scaling of the society

Assume now $V(c)>0$, and set
$$ \sigma=\sqrt{V(c)}. $$
For the society started at $c$, define the scaled fractions
$$ Y_n^{(N)}=\frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(X_n-c). $$

For every fixed $n$,
$$ Y_n^{(N)}\ \Longrightarrow\ Y_n, $$
where the limiting Gaussian recursion starts at $Y_0=0$.

Here are the details of the transition limit. For lattice states $x_N$ satisfying
$$ \frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(x_N-c)\longrightarrow y, $$
the local affine formulas give
$$ \frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(G(x_N)-c)\longrightarrow H(y). $$
For the centered innovation
$$ W_N=\frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(X_{n+1}-G(x_N)), $$
Taylor expansion of the logarithms of the independent centered Bernoulli characteristic functions gives, for fixed real $t$,
$$ \log\mathbb E[e^{itW_N}\mid X_n=x_N] = -\frac{t^2V(x_N)}{2\sigma^2}+O(N^{-1/2}). $$
The remainder is uniform in $x_N$: each centered Bernoulli contribution is bounded in absolute value by $1/(\sigma\sqrt N)$, so the sum of the third-order remainder bounds is $O(N^{-1/2})$.

Continuity gives $V(x_N)\to\sigma^2$. Thus the characteristic functions tend to $e^{-t^2/2}$, the characteristic function of a standard normal variable. The continuity theorem for characteristic functions applies because this limiting characteristic function is continuous at zero.

The same argument is uniform when the scaled starting state ranges over a compact set. Since $H$ is continuous, the resulting convergence of transition kernels, together with tightness, proves the fixed-time assertion by induction. More explicitly, for any bounded continuous test function, conditional expectations converge uniformly on compact sets of scaled starting states; outside such sets, tightness bounds the remaining contribution. This supplies the induction step from time $n$ to time $n+1$.

Finite-time convergence alone is insufficient for fixation. The next estimate supplies the missing infinite-horizon control.

4.3 Uniform escape estimates for the finite societies

Write
$$ d_+=\lambda_+-1,\qquad d_-=\lambda_--1. $$
Fix $R>0$. Consider a lattice state with scaled coordinate
$$ y=\frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(x-c)\ge R $$
and $x<c+\delta$. For sufficiently large $N$, stop when either
$$ \frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(X_n-c)\le\frac R2 \quad\text{or}\quad X_n\ge c+\delta. $$

At every state before stopping, the mean of the next scaled state is $\lambda_+y$. The centered moment-generating estimate from Section 1.2 gives
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[ e^{-\theta(Y_{n+1}^{(N)}-\lambda_+y)} \mid Y_n^{(N)}=y \right] \le e^{\theta^2/(8\sigma^2)}. $$
Choose
$$ \theta=4\sigma^2d_+R. $$
For $y>R/2$,
$$ \frac{\theta^2}{8\sigma^2}\le\theta d_+y. $$
Therefore $e^{-\theta Y_n^{(N)}}$, stopped as specified, is a supermartingale.

The stopping time has finite expectation. Before stopping,
$$ G(X_n)-X_n =d_+(X_n-c)> \frac{d_+\sigma R}{2\sqrt N}, $$
so the stopped first-moment argument used in Lemma 1 applies.

At a lower exit, the stopped exponential is at least $e^{-\theta R/2}$. Its initial value is at most $e^{-\theta R}$. Hence
$$ \mathbb P_x\!\left(\text{lower exit before reaching }c+\delta\right) \le e^{-2\sigma^2d_+R^2}. $$

On upper exit, monotonicity bounds the remaining fixation probability below by
$$ b_N=h_N\!\left(\frac{\lceil N(c+\delta)\rceil}{N}\right)\longrightarrow1, $$
where the limit follows from Lemma 1. States already at least $c+\delta$ satisfy the same lower bound directly.

Reflection supplies the corresponding left-side bound, with
$$ a_N=h_N\!\left(\frac{\lfloor N(c-\delta)\rfloor}{N}\right)\longrightarrow0. $$
Consequently, for
$$ \kappa=2\sigma^2\min(d_+,d_-)>0, $$
we have, for all sufficiently large $N$,
$$ h_N(x)\ge(1-e^{-\kappa R^2})b_N \quad\text{if}\quad \frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(x-c)\ge R, $$
and
$$ h_N(x)\le a_N+(1-a_N)e^{-\kappa R^2} \quad\text{if}\quad \frac{\sqrt N}{\sigma}(x-c)\le-R. $$

4.4 Passage to the fixation limit

For each fixed $n\ge1$,
$$ h_N(c)=\mathbb E_c[h_N(X_n)]. $$
Use the escape estimates on the events $Y_n^{(N)}\ge R$ and $Y_n^{(N)}\le-R$.

The limiting variable $Y_n$ has no atoms, because its last innovation is an independent Gaussian. Fixed-time convergence therefore gives
$$ \liminf_Nh_N(c) \ge(1-e^{-\kappa R^2})\mathbb P(Y_n\ge R), $$
$$ \limsup_Nh_N(c) \le1-(1-e^{-\kappa R^2})\mathbb P(Y_n\le-R). $$
The Gaussian escape result implies
$$ \mathbb P(Y_n\ge R)\longrightarrow p, \qquad \mathbb P(Y_n\le-R)\longrightarrow1-p $$
as $n\to\infty$. Hence
$$ (1-e^{-\kappa R^2})p \le\liminf_Nh_N(c) \le\limsup_Nh_N(c) \le p+e^{-\kappa R^2}(1-p). $$
Letting $R\to\infty$ proves
$$ h_N(c)\longrightarrow p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+). $$

4.5 Equal slopes and strict bias at a kink

First, $0<p<1$. A sufficiently large positive first Gaussian innovation has positive probability, and the return estimate gives a positive conditional probability of never returning below $1$. Almost sure escape then forces positive escape. A sufficiently negative first innovation similarly gives negative escape with positive probability.

If $\lambda_-=\lambda_+=\lambda>1$, the recursion is linear:
$$ \frac{Y_n}{\lambda^n} =\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda^{-j}Z_j. $$
The series converges almost surely: its independent centered terms have summable variances. Its limit is a centered Gaussian variable with variance
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2j} =\frac1{\lambda^2-1}. $$
It is nonzero almost surely, and its sign determines the escape direction. Therefore
$$ p(\lambda,\lambda)=\frac12. $$

Now suppose $\lambda_+>\lambda_-=\lambda$. Couple $Y_n$ to
$$ S_0=0,\qquad S_{n+1}=\lambda S_n+Z_{n+1} $$
using the same innovations. Since $H(y)\ge\lambda y$ for every $y$, induction gives $Y_n\ge S_n$. Thus $p\ge1/2$.

For strictness, write
$$ W_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda^{-j}Z_j, \qquad W=\lim_nW_n. $$
Expanding the nonlinear recursion gives
$$ \frac{Y_n}{\lambda^n} = W_n+ \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{(\lambda_+-\lambda)(Y_{j-1})_+}{\lambda^j}. $$
For $n\ge2$, this is at least $W_n+D$, where
$$ D=\frac{(\lambda_+-\lambda)(Z_1)_+}{\lambda^2}. $$
Conditional on $Z_1=z>0$, the variable $W$ is Gaussian with positive variance and full support on $\mathbb R$. Therefore
$$ \mathbb P(Z_1>0,\ -D<W<0)>0. $$
On this event, $S_n\to-\infty$, but $W+D>0$ forces $Y_n\to+\infty$. This supplies positive probability of positive escape in addition to the event $W>0$, proving
$$ p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)>\frac12. $$
Reflection swaps the two slopes and the two escape directions. It proves the strict reverse inequality when $\lambda_+<\lambda_-$.

This completes Theorem 3.

5. A concrete asymmetric repeller

Take one type of mass $1$ and let its rule interpolate
$$ (0,0),\quad \left(\frac14,\frac18\right),\quad \left(\frac12,\frac12\right),\quad \left(\frac34,\frac{15}{16}\right),\quad (1,1). $$
The four slopes are
$$ \frac12,\quad\frac32,\quad\frac74,\quad\frac14. $$
Direct evaluation on the four pieces gives
$$ F(x)<x\quad(0<x<1/2), \qquad F(x)>x\quad(1/2<x<1). $$
At $c=1/2$,
$$ V(c)=\frac14,\qquad \lambda_-=\frac32,\qquad \lambda_+=\frac74. $$
Therefore, from $q_0=1/2$,
$$ \alpha=p\!\left(\frac32,\frac74\right), \qquad \frac12<\alpha<1. $$
Thus a nonabsorbing repelling fixed initial fraction need not have limiting fixation probability $1/2$.

The variance hypothesis also matters. Keep this aggregate $G=F$, but use two types of mass $1/2$ with rules
$$ F_1(x)=\min(2F(x),1), \qquad F_2(x)=\max(2F(x)-1,0). $$
These are admissible continuous nondecreasing rational piecewise-linear rules, and
$$ \frac{F_1(x)+F_2(x)}2=F(x). $$
At $x=1/2$, their probabilities are respectively $1$ and $0$. The fraction $1/2$ is therefore absorbing, and
$$ \alpha_N=0 $$
for every admissible $N$. The same aggregate mean and initial fraction can consequently give different limits.

6. Recognition and complexity of the proved classes

These results provide polynomial-time decision procedures for additional restricted classes, not for the full problem.

Form the common partition of all supplied breakpoints. It has $O(m)$ intervals, and $G(x)-x$ is affine on each interval. Exact rational calculations determine its zeros, signs, and identity intervals.

For Theorem 2, positivity of $V$ on the fixed interval can also be checked finitely. If $V(c)=0$ at an interior point of a common affine piece, every affine rule takes the value $0$ or $1$ there. An affine function bounded in $[0,1]$ cannot attain either endpoint at an interior point unless it is constant. Thus all rules would be locally constant, contradicting $G(x)=x$. Therefore any zero of $V$ in $[a,b]$ must be at a common breakpoint or at $a$ or $b$. Checking those candidates suffices.

For Theorem 3, the one-sided slopes and $V(c)$ are exactly computable. In particular:

  • Theorem 2 gives a rational exact answer and polynomial-time comparison with every rational threshold $r$.
  • A repeller with equal slopes gives the rational answer $1/2$ at its nonabsorbing fixed point.
  • A deterministic fixed initial fraction gives the exact answer zero.
  • At an asymmetric repeller, comparison with $r=1/2$ is determined exactly by the ordering of the slopes.

A conservative implementation uses
$$ O\bigl(m\tau+m\log(m+1)\bigr) $$
rational operations. Sorting the common breakpoints and evaluating each active affine rule achieves this bound.

Computing each aggregate independently keeps intermediate rational numerators and denominators to $O(L^2)$ bits: each evaluation uses input rationals and a rational partition point or affine root, and a sum involves at most $\tau\le L$ terms. With elementary arithmetic and fraction reduction, a conservative bit-time bound is
$$ O\!\left(\bigl(m\tau+m\log(m+1)\bigr)L^6\right). $$
This bound covers recognition and the rational comparisons just listed.

The Gaussian integral characterization does not establish polynomial-time comparison of $p(\lambda_-,\lambda_+)$ with an arbitrary rational threshold.

7. Explicitly unresolved questions

The arguments above do not cover arbitrary nonsymmetric attracting fixed points, multiple attracting regions, or all configurations of fixed intervals and deterministic barriers. They therefore do not establish universal convergence.

They also provide neither a general polynomial-time algorithm nor an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction. In particular, a complete complexity classification must address eventual escape from attracting regions and exact threshold comparisons that are not resolved by rational formulas or symmetry.
