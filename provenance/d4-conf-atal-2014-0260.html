Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\) for the fixed number \(m=5\) of alternatives.

The proof uses the manipulation requirement exactly as stated: it applies at the supplied society \(\mu\). The rule’s dependence on majority tournaments, neutrality, bipartisan-set containment, and Condorcet-winner selection apply throughout \(D\).

Two reductions make the decision finite and tractable:

  • Each possible destination tournament of a real-valued deviation can be recognized by exact one-dimensional inequalities.
  • Once the rule’s choice at \(T_\mu\) is fixed, its choices on the other tournament orbits can be tested independently.

1. Representing a rule by a finite tournament table

Write \(\mathcal T_m\) for the labelled tournaments on \(A\). Its cardinality is \(N=2^{\binom m2}\), so \(N=1024\) when \(m=5\).

Every tournament belongs to the majority domain. To prove this, fix a tournament \(T\). For each edge \(aTb\), list the remaining alternatives as \(c_1,\ldots,c_{m-2}\), and use the two rankings

\(a\succ b\succ c_1\succ\cdots\succ c_{m-2}\)

and

\(c_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ c_1\succ a\succ b\).

Their combined majority margin is \(2\) for \(a\) against \(b\), and zero for every other pair: comparisons involving a remaining alternative cancel between the two rankings, as do comparisons between remaining alternatives. Combining these two rankings for every edge of \(T\), and assigning equal mass to all occurrences, therefore realizes exactly \(T\), with no majority ties.

Consequently, a rule depending only on the majority tournament is equivalent to a table

\(f:\mathcal T_m\longrightarrow 2^A\setminus\{\varnothing\}\),

with \(F(\nu)=f(T_\nu)\).

Let \(G\) be the permutation group of \(A\). Neutrality means \(f(\pi T)=\pi f(T)\) for every \(T\in\mathcal T_m\) and \(\pi\in G\).

For a tournament \(R\), define its automorphism group by

\(\operatorname{Aut}(R)=\{\pi\in G:\pi R=R\}\).

A neutral table must choose a set \(Y=f(R)\) invariant under this group: \(\pi Y=Y\) for every \(\pi\in\operatorname{Aut}(R)\).

Conversely, any such invariant choice determines a neutral table on the entire orbit of \(R\), by setting \(f(\pi R)=\pi Y\). This is well-defined: if \(\pi R=\sigma R\), then \(\sigma^{-1}\pi\in\operatorname{Aut}(R)\), and hence \(\pi Y=\sigma Y\).

Computing and transporting the bipartisan set

Let \(M_T\) be the payoff matrix of \(T\). Its equilibrium strategy is the unique vector in

\(\mathcal E_T=\{p\in\mathbb R^m:p\geq 0,\ \sum_a p_a=1,\ M_Tp\leq 0\}\).

Indeed, skew-symmetry gives \(p^{\mathsf T}M_Tp=0\). The inequality \(M_Tp\leq 0\) says every pure strategy earns at most zero against \(p\). Also, for every mixed strategy \(q\),

\(p^{\mathsf T}M_Tq=-(M_Tp)^{\mathsf T}q\geq 0\).

Thus \(p\) guarantees zero and prevents its opponent from earning more than zero, so it is an equilibrium strategy. The converse follows from the same equilibrium inequalities.

The stated uniqueness allows exact computation by finite rational linear algebra. There are \(2m\) inequalities, besides the normalization equality. At the unique feasible point, their active equations, together with normalization, have rank \(m\). Otherwise a nonzero direction preserving all active equations would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving the inactive inequalities, contradicting uniqueness. Therefore, enumerating choices of \(m-1\) active inequalities, solving the resulting systems, and checking feasibility finds the equilibrium exactly. Its positive coordinates determine \(BP(T)\).

Relabelling the payoff matrix relabels its unique equilibrium strategy, so

\(BP(\pi T)=\pi BP(T)\).

If \(c\) is a Condorcet winner, its unit vector satisfies the equilibrium inequalities: the coordinate for \(c\) is zero and every other coordinate of \(M_Te_c\) is \(-1\). Uniqueness therefore gives \(BP(T)=\{c\}\). Thus nonempty bipartisan-set containment already enforces the required Condorcet-winner selection.

For each tournament representative \(R\), define its candidate sets by

\(\mathcal C(R)=\{Y:\varnothing\neq Y\subseteq BP(R),\ \pi Y=Y\text{ for every }\pi\in\operatorname{Aut}(R)\}\).

There are at most \(2^m-1\) candidates. The preceding arguments show that the globally admissible neutral tables are precisely the independent choices of one candidate for each tournament orbit, transported by permutations.

2. Exact reachability under real-valued deviations

We now determine, for each supported true type \(t\) and destination tournament \(T\), whether some permitted report and transfer reach \(T\).

Fix an enumeration of \(A\), and consider pairs \(a<b\). Define \(s_t(a,b)=1\) if \(a\succ_t b\), and \(s_t(a,b)=-1\) otherwise.

Write each supplied mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\). Let \(Q\) be the product of the supplied denominators, and put \(w_t=Q\mu_t\). These are positive integers, and \(\sum_t w_t=Q\). The integer majority margin for a pair is

\(g_{ab}=\sum_{t:\mu_t>0}w_t s_t(a,b)\).

Hence its normalized margin is \(g_{ab}/Q\).

Fix a true type \(t\), a report \(t'\), and a transfer \(\delta\). Put

\(k_{ab}=s_{t'}(a,b)-s_t(a,b)\in\{-2,0,2\}\).

After the transfer, the margin becomes \(g_{ab}/Q+k_{ab}\delta\).

For a proposed destination tournament \(T\), let \(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)=1\) if \(aTb\), and \(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)=-1\) otherwise. Set \(z=2Q\delta\). The transfer reaches exactly \(T\), with no ties, precisely when

\(0<z\leq 2w_t\)

and, for every pair,

\(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)(2g_{ab}+k_{ab}z)>0\).

These conditions also ensure that the resulting society belongs to \(D\): masses remain nonnegative because \(\delta\leq\mu_t\), their sum remains one, and all pairwise margins are nonzero.

The inequalities admit a particularly simple exact test:

  • If \(k_{ab}=0\), require \(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)g_{ab}>0\). Otherwise the system is infeasible.
  • If \(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)k_{ab}>0\), require \(z>h_{ab}\), where \(h_{ab}=-2g_{ab}/k_{ab}\).
  • If \(\varepsilon_{ab}(T)k_{ab}<0\), require \(z<h_{ab}\).

Every \(h_{ab}\) is an integer because nonzero \(k_{ab}\) equals \(2\) or \(-2\).

Let \(\ell\) be the maximum of zero and all strict lower bounds. Let \(u\) be the minimum of \(2w_t\) and all strict upper bounds. Provided the zero-slope conditions hold, feasibility is equivalent to \(\ell<u\).

For necessity, every feasible \(z\) satisfies \(z>\ell\) and \(z\leq u\). For sufficiency, \(z=(\ell+u)/2\) satisfies every bound, including the possibly weak bound \(z\leq 2w_t\).

Thus the test handles the real quantifier exactly. In fact, whenever a real transfer exists, a rational transfer exists.

Define the Boolean relation

\(E(t,T)\Longleftrightarrow\text{some report }t'\text{ and some permitted transfer reach }T\).

Compute it by applying the interval test to all \(m!\) reports and all \(N\) destination tournaments.

For any tournament table \(f\), the required manipulation condition is now exactly

\(\neg\bigl(f(T)P_t^K f(T_\mu)\bigr)\)

for every supported \(t\) and every \(T\) with \(E(t,T)\).

This equivalence holds because the preference comparison uses the true order \(t\), while the rule’s outcome depends only on the destination tournament. Consequently, report and transfer details can be discarded after reachability is established.

We use the specified asymmetric Kelly relation literally: \(YP_t^KX\) holds exactly when \(YR_t^KX\) holds and \(XR_t^KY\) does not. Both relations can be checked with at most \(m^2\) comparisons of alternatives.

3. The decision algorithm

Let \(T_0=T_\mu\). Enumerate tournament orbits, choosing \(R_0=T_0\) as the representative of its orbit and representatives \(R_1,\ldots,R_{r-1}\) for the others.

For each tournament \(T\), store its orbit index \(j(T)\) and a permutation \(\pi_T\) satisfying \(T=\pi_TR_{j(T)}\).

Compute all candidate lists \(\mathcal C(R_j)\), and the reachability relation \(E\).

For each \(X\in\mathcal C(R_0)\), perform the following checks.

Source-orbit check. Require

\(\neg\bigl((\pi_TX)P_t^KX\bigr)\)

for every supported \(t\) and every reachable \(T\) in the orbit of \(T_0\).

Other-orbit checks. For each \(j\neq 0\), seek a candidate \(Y_j\in\mathcal C(R_j)\) such that

\(\neg\bigl((\pi_TY_j)P_t^KX\bigr)\)

for every supported \(t\) and every reachable \(T\) in that orbit.

Accept if some \(X\) passes the source-orbit check and every other orbit has a passing candidate. Otherwise reject.

The other-orbit checks are independent because every manipulation constraint compares its destination outcome with the single source outcome \(X=f(T_0)\). No such constraint compares outcomes from two different destination orbits.

4. Proof of correctness

Acceptance constructs a valid rule

Suppose the algorithm accepts \(X\) and candidates \(Y_j\). Set \(Y_0=X\), and define

\(f(T)=\pi_TY_{j(T)}\), \(F(\nu)=f(T_\nu)\).

Automorphism invariance makes this definition well-defined and neutral. Each chosen set is nonempty and contained in its representative’s bipartisan set. Bipartisan-set equivariance therefore gives \(f(T)\subseteq BP(T)\) for every tournament. Condorcet winners are selected uniquely by the singleton property proved above.

Now consider any deviation quantified in the task, with true type \(t\) and destination tournament \(T\). Its existence implies \(E(t,T)\). The applicable orbit check gives

\(\neg\bigl(f(T)P_t^KX\bigr)\).

Because \(f(T_0)=X\), this is exactly the required absence of a profitable deviation. Thus the constructed rule satisfies every condition.

Every valid rule causes acceptance

Conversely, suppose a required rule exists, and let \(f\) be its tournament table.

Neutrality and bipartisan-set containment imply \(f(R_j)\in\mathcal C(R_j)\) for every representative. Take \(X=f(T_0)\) and \(Y_j=f(R_j)\).

Whenever \(E(t,T)\) holds, there is an admissible deviation reaching \(T\). The assumed manipulation condition implies

\(\neg\bigl(f(T)P_t^KX\bigr)\).

Neutrality gives \(f(T)=\pi_TY_{j(T)}\). Therefore \(X\) passes the source-orbit check, and each \(Y_j\) passes its other-orbit check. The algorithm accepts.

This proves both directions.

5. Running time and bit complexity

Let \(N=2^{\binom m2}\), \(H=m!\), and \(S=2^m-1\).

The common denominator \(Q\), the integers \(w_t\), and the margins \(g_{ab}\) have bit length \(O(L)\): the sum of the supplied denominator bit lengths is bounded by \(L\), and the masses sum to one.

The reachability computation uses \(O(\tau HNm^2)\) exact integer operations on numbers of \(O(L)\) bits. Computing the scaled masses and margins also takes polynomial time without expanding masses into voters.

Tournament orbit construction uses \(O(HNm^2)\) elementary operations. Candidate construction enumerates at most \(S\) subsets per representative. Across all candidate source choices, the manipulation checks require \(O(S^2\tau Nm^2)\) preference comparisons: summing destination counts across orbits gives \(N\).

The bipartisan-set computation enumerates at most \(2^{2m}\) systems per tournament. Their coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), so exact linear-system computation and feasibility checking have bit complexity polynomial in \(m\), independent of \(L\).

A conservative bound for the complete algorithm is

\(O\!\left(2^{\binom m2+2m}\,m!\,m^{10}\,(\tau+1)(L+1)^2\right)\)

bit operations. The polynomial factor in \(m\) accommodates exact linear algebra and the remaining finite-table operations.

With \(m=5\) fixed, this becomes \(O((\tau+1)(L+1)^2)\), hence is polynomial in the support-encoded input length. Also, \(\tau\leq 5!=120\).

The continuous rule-existence problem is therefore polynomial-time solvable. This classification follows directly from the specified model and does not require extending the finite-electorate existence theorem.
