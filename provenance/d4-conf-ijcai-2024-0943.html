The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite menu of alternatives in \([0,1]^n\), a fixed local aggregator \(\phi\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over report types \(t=(x_t,y_t,r_t)\), where \(r_t\) records strict preference or indifference, find a normalized Möbius vector \(m\) minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\ell_t(m)+\lambda\|m\|_1\), subject to monotonicity of \(v(S)=\sum_{R\subseteq S}m(R)\), optionally with supermodularity. Here \(\ell_t(m)=[\delta-\langle m,\phi(x_t)-\phi(y_t)\rangle]_+\) for strict preference and \(\ell_t(m)=[|\langle m,\phi(x_t)-\phi(y_t)\rangle|-\delta]_+\) for indifference.

Here \(x_t,y_t\in X\) are distinct, and \(r_t\) records either \(x_t\succ y_t\) or \(x_t\sim y_t\). The parameter \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.

A conditional obstruction to the requested dichotomy

The stated assumptions do not support a universal $\mathrm P$/NP-hard dichotomy unless $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$.

I prove the following stronger limitation:

There is a fixed aggregator family satisfying all the stated requirements such that, if $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$, its decision problem is neither in $\mathrm P$ nor NP-hard, with either setting of the supermodularity flag.

Here NP-hardness means hardness under polynomial-time many-one reductions. No membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ is claimed for this constructed family.

Consequently, the assertion that every permitted fixed aggregator family yields a problem in $\mathrm P$ or an NP-hard problem is equivalent to $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. This addresses the missing universal scope in the notes, although the unconditional truth of that assertion remains open.

The unconditional classifications from the notes also hold:

| Fixed family for nonempty $S$ | Without supermodularity | With supermodularity |
|---|---|---|
| $\phi_S(z_S)=|S|^{-1}\sum_{i\in S}z_i$ | $\mathrm P$ | $\mathrm P$ |
| $\phi_S(z_S)=z_{\max S}$ | $\mathrm P$ | $\mathrm P$ |
| $\phi_S(z_S)=\min_{i\in S}z_i$ | NP-hard | NP-hard |
| $\phi_S(z_S)=\prod_{i\in S}z_i$ | NP-hard | NP-hard |

Every permitted fixed family admits an exact algorithm in
$$ 2^{O(n)}\operatorname{poly}_{\phi}(n,m,\tau,L) $$
bit operations. The two polynomial cases above run in
$$ \operatorname{poly}(n,m,\tau,L). $$

All these statements are proved below. For $n=1$, normalization forces $m(\{1\})=1$, so the decision is immediately computable; the complexity questions concern unbounded $n$.

1. An exact exponential-time algorithm for every family

For each report and subset, define
$$ b_t(S)=\phi_S((x_t)_S)-\phi_S((y_t)_S). $$

Introduce rational LP variables $m(S),v(S),z(S)$ for every subset, and $h_t$ for every report. Require
$$ m(\varnothing)=v(\varnothing)=0,\qquad v(S)=\sum_{R\subseteq S}m(R),\qquad v(N)=1, $$
and
$$ z(S)\geq m(S),\qquad z(S)\geq-m(S). $$

Monotonicity is imposed through the cover inequalities
$$ v(A\cup\{i\})\geq v(A) \qquad(i\notin A). $$
They imply every required inclusion inequality by adding the missing elements successively.

When enabled, supermodularity is imposed through
$$ v(A\cup\{i,j\})+v(A) \geq v(A\cup\{i\})+v(A\cup\{j\}) \qquad(i\ne j,\ i,j\notin A). $$
These inequalities are equivalent to full supermodularity. Indeed, they state that
$$ \Delta_i(A)=v(A\cup\{i\})-v(A) $$
cannot decrease when another element is added to $A$. Iteration gives
$$ \Delta_i(A)\leq\Delta_i(B) \qquad(A\subseteq B,\ i\notin B). $$
For arbitrary $C,D$, add the elements of $C\setminus D$ in the same order, first starting from $C\cap D$ and then starting from $D$. At every addition the former current set is contained in the latter. Summing the marginal comparisons gives
$$ v(C)-v(C\cap D)\leq v(C\cup D)-v(D). $$
Necessity follows by using $C=A\cup\{i\}$ and $D=A\cup\{j\}$.

For a strict report, impose
$$ h_t\geq0,\qquad h_t\geq\delta-\sum_S b_t(S)m(S). $$
For an indifference report, impose
$$ h_t\geq0,\qquad h_t\geq\sum_S b_t(S)m(S)-\delta,\qquad h_t\geq-\sum_S b_t(S)m(S)-\delta. $$
Finally, impose
$$ \sum_t\mu_t h_t+\lambda\sum_S z(S)\leq q. $$

This LP is feasible exactly when the original instance is a yes-instance. Its inequalities imply $h_t\geq\ell_t$ and $z(S)\geq|m(S)|$; conversely, an original feasible vector extends by assigning these quantities their exact values.

There are $O(2^n+\tau)$ variables and $O(n^2 2^n+\tau)$ constraints. The subset-sum equations contain
$$ \sum_{S\subseteq N}2^{|S|}=3^n $$
coefficient occurrences. Uniform polynomial-time exact evaluation bounds both evaluation time and rational output length by a fixed polynomial in the input size. The explicit LP therefore has encoding length
$$ 2^{O(n)}\operatorname{poly}_{\phi}(n,m,\tau,L). $$
Polynomial-time feasibility of explicitly represented rational LPs proves the claimed running time.

This also supplies a terminating exact decision procedure needed later in the diagonalization.

2. Arithmetic-mean aggregators are polynomial-time solvable

Suppose
$$ \phi_S(z_S)=\frac1{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}z_i \qquad(S\ne\varnothing). $$
For an admissible vector, define
$$ a_i=\sum_{S\ni i}\frac{m(S)}{|S|}. $$
Interchanging finite sums gives
$$ F_{\boldsymbol m}(x) =\sum_Sm(S)\phi_S(x_S) =\sum_i a_i x_i. $$

To prove $a_i\geq0$, choose a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ of $N$, and let $P_i^\pi$ be the predecessors of $i$. Expanding a capacity marginal gives
$$ v(P_i^\pi\cup\{i\})-v(P_i^\pi) = \sum_{\substack{S\ni i\\S\setminus\{i\}\subseteq P_i^\pi}}m(S). $$
For fixed $S\ni i$, the condition in this sum holds precisely when $i$ is last among the elements of $S$, an event of probability $1/|S|$. Thus
$$ a_i = \mathbb E_\pi\bigl[v(P_i^\pi\cup\{i\})-v(P_i^\pi)\bigr] \geq0. $$
The inequality uses monotonicity; linearity of expectation remains valid for signed coefficients. Also,
$$ \sum_i a_i=\sum_{S\ne\varnothing}m(S)=1. $$

Replace the original vector by
$$ m'(\{i\})=a_i,\qquad m'(S)=0\quad(|S|\ne1). $$
Its capacity is additive, hence monotone and supermodular. It preserves every score, and
$$ \|\boldsymbol m'\|_1=1 \leq\|\boldsymbol m\|_1, $$
where the inequality follows from normalization.

The optimum therefore equals
$$ \lambda+ \min_{\substack{a_i\geq0\\\sum_i a_i=1}} \sum_t\mu_t\, \ell_t\!\left(\sum_i a_i((x_t)_i-(y_t)_i)\right). $$
The loss epigraph inequalities from Section 1 give an LP with $n+\tau$ variables and $O(n+\tau)$ constraints, of polynomial encoding length. This proves polynomial-time solvability under either flag, for arbitrary allowed losses and parameters.

3. A family that switches between minimum and coordinate projection

Let
$$ g:\mathbb N_{\geq1}\longrightarrow\{0,1\} $$
be a fixed function computable in polynomial time from the binary representation of its argument. Define
$$ \phi^g_\varnothing=0, $$
and, for nonempty $S$,
$$ \phi^g_S(z_S)= \begin{cases} \min_{i\in S}z_i,&g(\max S)=1,\\ z_{\max S},&g(\max S)=0. \end{cases} $$

These are local aggregators: they use only the coordinates indexed by $S$. Dependence on the labels in $S$ is permitted by the task, which imposes no symmetry requirement. Both branches agree on singletons, have rational outputs, and are exactly evaluable in polynomial time.

Write $D_{g,b}$ for the decision language with this family and flag fixed to $b\in\{0,1\}$. Fix an ordinary explicit binary encoding, and reject malformed inputs. Validity of the input format is polynomial-time checkable.

The next two lemmas establish the contrasting behavior of the two possible constant tails of $g$.

3.1. An eventual projection tail gives polynomial-time solvability

Lemma. If $g(r)=0$ for all $r>K$, where $K$ is a fixed constant, then both $D_{g,0}$ and $D_{g,1}$ are in $\mathrm P$. More explicitly, they are solvable in
$$ 2^{O(K)}\operatorname{poly}_g(n,m,\tau,L) $$
bit operations.

Proof. Put
$$ H=\{1,\ldots,\min(K,n)\}. $$
For every $i>K$, define
$$ a_i=\sum_{\max R=i}m(R). $$
The sets counted by this sum are exactly the subsets of $\{1,\ldots,i\}$ that are not subsets of $\{1,\ldots,i-1\}$. Therefore
$$ a_i=v(\{1,\ldots,i\})-v(\{1,\ldots,i-1\})\geq0. $$

Retain every coefficient indexed by a subset of $H$, replace all coefficients having maximum $i>K$ by the single coefficient $m'(\{i\})=a_i$, and set the other coefficients to zero. The new capacity is
$$ v'(A)=v(A\cap H)+\sum_{\substack{i\in A\\i>K}}a_i. $$
It is monotone. If the original capacity is supermodular, its restriction to $H$ is supermodular, and the displayed additive extension is supermodular as well.

Telescoping gives
$$ v(H)+\sum_{i>K}a_i=v(N)=1, $$
so normalization is preserved.

For every $R$ with $\max R=i>K$, the aggregator is $x_i$. Hence the original score is
$$ F_{\boldsymbol m}(x) = \sum_{\varnothing\ne R\subseteq H}m(R)\phi^g_R(x_R) +\sum_{i>K}a_i x_i, $$
which is exactly the replacement score. Finally,
$$ \|\boldsymbol m'\|_1 = \sum_{R\subseteq H}|m(R)|+\sum_{i>K}a_i \leq \sum_{R\subseteq H}|m(R)| +\sum_{i>K}\sum_{\max R=i}|m(R)| = \|\boldsymbol m\|_1. $$
Thus the replacement cannot increase the objective.

Conversely, every monotone capacity on $H$, with nonnegative outside weights satisfying
$$ v(H)+\sum_{i>K}a_i=1, $$
defines an admissible capacity through the displayed extension. The same equivalence holds with supermodularity required.

Consequently, it suffices to use the subset variables on $H$, the $n-|H|$ outside weights, and the report epigraph variables. The LP construction of Section 1 now has encoding length
$$ 2^{O(K)}\operatorname{poly}_g(n,m,\tau,L). $$
Rational LP feasibility proves the lemma. $\square$

Taking $g\equiv0$ gives the coordinate-projection classification in the opening table.

3.2. A signed-coefficient replacement lemma

Lemma. Suppose all aggregator values on the menu belong to $[0,1]$, and $\lambda\geq1$. Every admissible signed vector can be replaced, without increasing its objective, by a nonnegative normalized Möbius vector. The replacement satisfies both monotonicity and supermodularity.

Proof. Let
$$ \eta=\sum_S[-m(S)]_+. $$
Normalization implies that the positive mass is $1+\eta$ and
$$ \|\boldsymbol m\|_1=1+2\eta. $$
Define
$$ p(S)=\frac{[m(S)]_+}{1+\eta}. $$
Then $p(\varnothing)=0$, all its coefficients are nonnegative, and their sum is $1$. Removing negative mass changes the vector by $\eta$ in absolute sum, and renormalizing its positive part changes it by another $\eta$. Thus
$$ \|\boldsymbol p-\boldsymbol m\|_1=2\eta. $$

The range assumption gives $|b_t(S)|\leq1$, so
$$ |d_t(\boldsymbol p)-d_t(\boldsymbol m)|\leq2\eta. $$
Both specified loss functions are $1$-Lipschitz. Since the masses sum to $1$, the total weighted loss increases by at most $2\eta$. The regularizer decreases by $2\lambda\eta$, giving
$$ J(\boldsymbol p)\leq J(\boldsymbol m)+2\eta(1-\lambda) \leq J(\boldsymbol m). $$

For a nonempty $R$, define the unanimity capacity
$$ u_R(A)=\mathbf1\{R\subseteq A\}. $$
It is monotone and supermodular. For the latter property, if $R$ is contained in both arguments, both sides of the supermodular inequality equal $2$; if in exactly one, both sides equal $1$; if in neither, the right side is zero and the left side is nonnegative.

The replacement capacity is
$$ v_{\boldsymbol p}(A)=\sum_Rp(R)u_R(A). $$
A nonnegative combination preserves both properties, and the required normalization follows from $\sum_Rp(R)=1$. $\square$

3.3. An eventual minimum tail gives NP-hardness

Lemma. If $g(r)=1$ for all $r>K$, where $K$ is fixed, then $D_{g,b}$ is NP-hard for each $b$.

Proof. Reduce from Independent Set on a simple graph with $p$ vertices and threshold $k$, restricted to $p\geq2$ and $1\leq k\leq p$. This restricted problem is NP-hard: the usual reduction from $3$-SAT uses one vertex for each literal occurrence, clause cliques, and edges between complementary occurrences. An independent set of size equal to the number of clauses exists precisely when the formula is satisfiable.

Add $h=K+1$ isolated vertices, placing them after the original vertices in the labeling. Call the resulting graph $G=(N,E)$ and set $n=p+h$. Then
$$ \alpha(G)=\alpha(G_{\mathrm{original}})+h, $$
and vertex $n$ is isolated. Every maximum independent set includes $n$, since otherwise it could be enlarged.

Let $\boldsymbol1$ be the all-ones alternative. For each vertex $i$, let $c_i$ have its only zero at $i$. For each edge $\{i,j\}$, let $c_{ij}$ have zeros at $i,j$. Use the menu
$$ X=\{\boldsymbol1\}\cup\{c_i:i\in N\} \cup\{c_{ij}:\{i,j\}\in E\}. $$
All alternatives are distinct.

Put
$$ C=n+1,\qquad W=n+2C|E|, $$
and let $D$ be the smallest power of two strictly larger than $W$. Use these strict reports:

| Report | Mass |
|---|---|
| $\boldsymbol1\succ c_i$, for each vertex $i$ | $1/D$ |
| $c_i\succ\boldsymbol1$, for each edge $\{i,j\}$ with $i<j$ | $C/D$ |
| $c_j\succ c_{ij}$, for the same edge | $C/D$ |

Add $\boldsymbol1\succ c_1$ and $c_1\succ\boldsymbol1$, each with mass
$$ \frac{D-W}{2D}. $$
Merge repeated types by adding their masses. All resulting masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to $1$.

Choose
$$ \delta=2,\qquad\lambda=1,\qquad q=3-\frac{k+h}{D}. $$

For a nonempty subset $S$, let $H(S)/D$ be the mass-weighted sum of report differences for the vector with coefficient $1$ on $S$ and zero elsewhere. The added opposite reports cancel.

If the aggregator on $S$ is minimum, put
$$ a_i=\mathbf1\{i\notin S\}. $$
Its values on $\boldsymbol1,c_i,c_{ij}$ are respectively $1,a_i,a_i a_j$. Therefore
$$ \begin{aligned} H(S) &=\sum_i(1-a_i) +C\sum_{\substack{\{i,j\}\in E\\i<j}} (a_i-1+a_j-a_i a_j)\\ &=|S|-C\,e_G(S), \end{aligned} $$
where $e_G(S)$ counts edges internal to $S$. An independent $S$ has $H(S)=|S|$, while a nonindependent $S$ has
$$ H(S)\leq n-C=-1. $$

If the aggregator on $S$ is coordinate projection, write $r=\max S$. Its vertex contributions sum to $1$. Every edge contribution vanishes because
$$ (c_i)_r-1+(c_j)_r-(c_{ij})_r=0. $$
Thus $H(S)=1$ for a projection column.

It follows that every column satisfies $H(S)\leq\alpha(G)$. A maximum independent set contains $n>K$, so its aggregator is minimum and its column attains $\alpha(G)$. Hence
$$ \max_{\varnothing\ne S\subseteq N}H(S)=\alpha(G). $$

The signed replacement lemma applies. For nonnegative normalized coefficients, scores lie in $[0,1]$, so every strict loss is exactly $2-d_t$ and the regularizer is $1$. Consequently,
$$ J(\boldsymbol p) = 3-\frac1D\sum_{S\ne\varnothing}p(S)H(S). $$
The sum is a convex combination of column values. Concentration on a maximum independent set attains its maximum; the corresponding unanimity capacity satisfies either flag. Therefore
$$ \min J=3-\frac{\alpha(G)}D. $$
The threshold condition is equivalent to
$$ \alpha(G)\geq k+h \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha(G_{\mathrm{original}})\geq k. $$

The reduction has
$$ m=1+n+|E|=O(n^2),\qquad \tau\leq n+2|E|+2=O(n^2), $$
and $D\leq2W=O(n^3)$. Its total encoding length is $O(n^3)$ under the usual explicit encoding, and construction time is polynomial. The constant $K$ depends only on the fixed family. $\square$

With $g\equiv1$, no isolated-vertex padding is needed, and this proves hardness for minimum aggregators. Product aggregators have the same values on the binary menu and remain in $[0,1]$ on every allowed alternative, so exactly the same reduction and signed replacement prove their hardness.

4. Constructing a family outside both proposed classes

I now construct a single fixed, polynomial-time computable gate $g$. The construction is unconditional; its complexity consequences use $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$.

It uses delayed diagonalization, the technique underlying Ladner’s theorem. The version needed here is proved directly; it does not require assuming that these decision languages belong to $\mathrm{NP}$. See [Two Proofs of Ladner’s Theorem](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~rogaway/classes/220/winter06/ladner-theorem.pdf) for the classical technique.

4.1. Requirements

Effectively enumerate:

  • all polynomial-time clocked decision machines $M_0,M_1,\ldots$;
  • all polynomial-time clocked string functions $R_0,R_1,\ldots$.

For example, enumerate pairs consisting of a machine program and a polynomial clock, truncating execution when the clock expires. Every polynomial-time decider or string function occurs in the appropriate enumeration.

For each $i$, impose four requirements:

| Phase | Requirement | Gate value while waiting |
|---|---|---|
| $4i$ | $M_i$ fails to decide $D_{g,0}$ | $1$ |
| $4i+1$ | $R_i$ fails to reduce SAT to $D_{g,0}$ | $0$ |
| $4i+2$ | $M_i$ fails to decide $D_{g,1}$ | $1$ |
| $4i+3$ | $R_i$ fails to reduce SAT to $D_{g,1}$ | $0$ |

A phase ends only after finding a finite witness to its requirement.

For a decision-machine requirement, enumerate strings $z$ and test whether
$$ M_i(z)\ne\mathbf1\{z\in D_{g,b}\}. $$
For a reduction requirement, enumerate strings $z$ and test whether
$$ \mathbf1\{z\in\mathrm{SAT}\} \ne \mathbf1\{R_i(z)\in D_{g,b}\}. $$
SAT can be decided by exhaustive search. Section 1 supplies an exact terminating procedure for the target decision.

The remaining issue is to make $g$ polynomial-time computable while these searches can take arbitrarily long.

4.2. Assigning the gate without circularity

Define bits $a_0,a_1,\ldots$, and then set
$$ g(r)=a_{\lfloor\log_2 r\rfloor}. $$

The construction proceeds in calendar ticks $j=0,1,\ldots$. At tick $j$:

  • Permanently assign $a_j$ the waiting value of the current phase.
  • Advance the current witness-search computation by one simulated machine transition.
  • If a witness has been completed, move to the next phase and restart the search for that requirement.

A search deciding a target instance evaluates minimum or projection using the table of assigned bits. If it requests an unassigned bit $a_s$, it pauses until calendar tick $s$ assigns that bit. This pause does not prevent later calendar ticks from assigning bits.

For a target instance of dimension $d$, all its aggregator columns use only
$$ a_0,\ldots,a_{\lfloor\log_2 d\rfloor}. $$
Thus every target decision eventually has all the gate bits it needs. Once available, its explicit LP computation terminates.

This defines an ordinary effective construction: its background computation uses a universal machine simulator, a growing bit table, and the exact finite algorithms described above. It does not call the as-yet-undefined gate as an oracle. It reads already assigned table entries and waits for future entries when necessary.

To evaluate $g(r)$, simulate the construction only through tick
$$ j=\lfloor\log_2 r\rfloor. $$
There are $j+1$ ticks and at most $j+1$ background transitions. The table, simulator configuration, and phase counter have polynomial size in $j+1$. Straightforward universal simulation and table access therefore take
$$ O((j+1)^c) $$
time for some fixed constant $c$. Hence $g(r)$ is computable in polynomial time in the binary length of $r$.

It follows that $\phi^g$ is a valid uniformly polynomial-time evaluable fixed aggregator family.

Every discovered witness remains valid permanently: its target decision used only gate bits that had already been assigned, and none of those bits is subsequently changed.

4.3. No phase can remain forever if $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$

Assume $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$.

Suppose a decision-machine phase remains forever. From some calendar tick onward, all assigned bits equal $1$, so $g(r)=1$ for all sufficiently large $r$. Section 3.3 then makes the relevant $D_{g,b}$ NP-hard.

During a permanent phase, every finite candidate string is eventually examined. Its clocked machine computation terminates, every required gate bit eventually becomes available, and its exact target decision terminates. If no witness exists, $M_i$ decides $D_{g,b}$ correctly on every string. This would put an NP-hard language in $\mathrm P$, contradicting the assumption.

Now suppose a reduction phase remains forever. Its tail gate equals $0$, so Section 3.1 puts the relevant $D_{g,b}$ in $\mathrm P$. Again every finite candidate is eventually examined. If no witness exists, $R_i$ is a valid polynomial-time many-one reduction from SAT to this polynomial-time language. This would put SAT in $\mathrm P$, another contradiction.

Therefore every phase ends.

Every polynomial-time decider is consequently defeated for each flag, and every polynomial-time reduction from SAT is defeated for each flag. We obtain
$$ D_{g,0}\notin\mathrm P,\qquad D_{g,1}\notin\mathrm P, $$
and
$$ \mathrm{SAT}\not\leq_m^p D_{g,0},\qquad \mathrm{SAT}\not\leq_m^p D_{g,1}. $$
Neither language is NP-hard.

This proves the promised conditional obstruction for the full decision problems, including signed coefficients, arbitrary positive rational parameters, and both kinds of reports.

5. The universal dichotomy is equivalent to $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$

Let $\mathcal D$ denote the assertion:

For every permitted fixed aggregator family, and for each flag setting, the decision problem is in $\mathrm P$ or is NP-hard.

Section 4 proves
$$ \mathcal D\Longrightarrow\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}, $$
because otherwise its constructed family violates both alternatives for both flags.

For completeness, the converse also holds, even without proving that the decision problems belong to $\mathrm{NP}$.

Every permitted family has a fixed yes-instance and a fixed no-instance. Take $n=1$, menu $X=\{0,1\}$, one strict report $1\succ0$ of mass $1$, and
$$ \delta=\lambda=1. $$
Normalization forces $m(\{1\})=1$. The loss is zero and the objective is $1$, so threshold $q=1$ gives a yes-instance and threshold $q=0$ gives a no-instance. The capacity is modular, so these instances work for either flag and every permitted family.

If $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$, any language $A\in\mathrm{NP}$ has a polynomial-time decider. Decide whether the source input belongs to $A$, and output the fixed yes-instance or fixed no-instance accordingly. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from $A$ to the decision problem. Thus every permitted family’s problem is NP-hard.

Consequently,
$$ \boxed{\mathcal D\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}.} $$

The weak assumptions therefore permit a third complexity possibility if $\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}$. Additional structural hypotheses would be needed for a meaningful universal binary classification.

6. Running times and remaining scope

The proved algorithmic bounds are:

| Setting | Exact running time |
|---|---|
| Any permitted fixed family | $2^{O(n)}\operatorname{poly}_{\phi}(n,m,\tau,L)$ |
| Arithmetic mean | $\operatorname{poly}(n,m,\tau,L)$ |
| Coordinate projection | $\operatorname{poly}(n,m,\tau,L)$ |
