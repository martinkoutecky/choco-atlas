The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a network hypergraph \(H=(T,E)\) with singleton recipes \(e_t=\{t\}\), a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of each type's incident recipes, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), find a rational allocation \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{E}\) such that \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\), and for every recipe \(e\in E\) some \(t\in e\) satisfies \(\sum_{\substack{f\in\delta(t)\f\succeq_t e}}x_f=\mu_t\). Can such a stable allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau=|T|\) types, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The explicitly listed recipes form \(H=(T,E)\), with \(m=|E|\); every recipe is a nonempty subset of \(T\). The network-hypergraph promise means that some oriented tree has its arcs identified with \(T\), and each recipe is exactly the arc set of a directed path in that tree.
For every \(t\), the recipe \(e_t=\{t\}\) belongs to \(E\) and is ranked last in a given strict total order \(\succ_t\) on \(\delta(t)=\{e\in E:t\in e\}\). Write \(f\succeq_t e\) when \(f=e\) or \(f\succ_t e\).
A stable allocation is \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{E}\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\in\delta(t)}x_e=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and, for every \(e\in E\), admitting some \(t\in e\) with \(\sum_{f\in\delta(t):\,f\succeq_t e}x_f=\mu_t\). Recipes have no additional capacity bounds. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Can a stable allocation always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this search problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For the discrete version with unit capacities and integral allocations, a stable matching on a network hypergraph can be computed in polynomial time.
[Biró–Fleiner](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf) establish existence of fractional stable solutions with arbitrary positive vertex capacities. [Ishizuka–Kamiyama](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11) prove \(\mathrm{PPAD}\)-completeness for fractional stable matching on general hypergraphs, even with maximum degree three; this does not settle the network-hypergraph restriction with unequal masses.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the requirement that each singleton outside-option recipe is ranked last.
  • Excluded empty recipes, which cannot satisfy the required stability witness condition.
  • Specified the network-hypergraph promise through directed paths in an oriented tree; the input remains the explicit hypergraph and need not include a tree representation.
  • Set m to the number of recipes, tau to the number of supported types, and L to the total input bit length.
  • Made explicit that recipes have no additional capacity bounds, as in the stated allocation equations.

The full complexity question remains unresolved here. I prove two additional results beyond the supplied notes:

  • For laminar recipe families, a stable allocation with arbitrary positive rational masses can be computed in polynomial time.
  • Recipe capacities can be encoded by private tree arcs, preserving the network-hypergraph promise. Consequently, the problem in the question includes the search for ordinary stable network-hypergraph $b$-matchings with unequal vertex capacities.

I also give an explicit polynomial-time sparsification procedure, replacing the unspecified LP vertex-selection step in the notes.

1. Stability depends only on which recipes are positive

Let $A$ be the type–recipe incidence matrix, and define
$$ P(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq 0}^{E}:Ax=\mu\}. $$
For $x\in P(\mu)$, let
$$ \operatorname{supp}(x)=\{e\in E:x_e>0\}. $$

Because $\mu_t>0$, every type has at least one positive incident recipe. Write $w_t(x)$ for the worst such recipe according to $\succ_t$.

For $e\in\delta(t)$, conservation gives
$$ \sum_{\substack{f\in\delta(t)\\f\succeq_t e}}x_f=\mu_t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_f=0\ \text{for every }f\in\delta(t)\text{ with }e\succ_t f. $$
The equivalence follows because the complementary sum consists of nonnegative terms. Therefore,
$$ x\text{ is stable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall e\in E\ \exists t\in e: w_t(x)\succeq_t e. $$

This also proves the following support lemma.

Lemma 1. If $x,y\in P(\mu)$, $x$ is stable, and
$$ \operatorname{supp}(y)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(x), $$
then $y$ is stable.

Proof. Fix $e$ and a witness $t$ for its stability under $x$. Every incident recipe worse than $e$ has zero allocation under $x$, hence also under $y$. Conservation for $y$ supplies the required prefix equality. This holds for every $e$. $\square$

2. A polynomial algorithm for laminar recipe families

A family $E$ is laminar if, for every $e,f\in E$, either they are disjoint, or one contains the other.

The following theorem allows both unequal masses and variable support size.

Theorem 2. If $E$ is laminar, a stable allocation can be computed in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

The proof does not require a tree representation.

2.1 Algorithm

Initialize
$$ x_{\{t\}}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T), \qquad x_e=0\quad(|e|>1). $$
Thus conservation holds initially.

Process the nonsingleton recipes in nondecreasing order of cardinality. When processing $f$, perform the following operation repeatedly while $f$ has no stability witness.

Since $f$ has no witness, for every $t\in f$,
$$ f\succ_t w_t(x). $$
Form the family
$$ G_f=\{w_t(x):t\in f\}, $$
and let $M_f$ consist of its inclusion-maximal members. Set
$$ \Delta=\min_{g\in M_f}x_g. $$
Update
$$ x_f\leftarrow x_f+\Delta, \qquad x_g\leftarrow x_g-\Delta\quad(g\in M_f). $$
When $f$ has a witness, proceed to the next recipe.

2.2 The removed recipes partition the current recipe

Every positive recipe other than the current $f$ has already been processed. If such a recipe intersects $f$, laminarity makes the two comparable by inclusion. It cannot properly contain $f$, because larger recipes are processed later. Hence it is a proper subset of $f$.

During an update, $w_t(x)\ne f$, since $f\succ_t w_t(x)$. Consequently, every member of $G_f$ is a proper subset of $f$.

Distinct inclusion-maximal members of a laminar family are disjoint. Moreover, every $t\in f$ belongs to its selected recipe $w_t(x)$, which is contained in some maximal member. Therefore,
$$ \bigcup_{g\in M_f}g=f, $$
and this union is disjoint.

Also, every selected recipe has positive allocation, so $\Delta>0$.

2.3 Every update preserves feasibility

For each $t\in f$, exactly one recipe in $M_f$ contains $t$. The update increases its load through $f$ by $\Delta$ and decreases its load through that selected recipe by $\Delta$.

Types outside $f$ are unaffected. Thus
$$ Ax=\mu $$
is preserved.

The definition of $\Delta$ ensures that every decreased coordinate remains nonnegative. Hence every intermediate allocation belongs to $P(\mu)$.

2.4 Every type’s worst positive recipe can only improve

Consider an update while processing $f$.

For $t\notin f$, the positive incident recipes do not change. For $t\in f$, every newly positive incident recipe is either an old positive recipe or $f$. Every old positive recipe is weakly preferred to $w_t(x)$, while
$$ f\succ_t w_t(x). $$
Removing some old recipes therefore cannot introduce a recipe worse than the old worst recipe. If $x'$ denotes the updated allocation,
$$ w_t(x')\succeq_t w_t(x) \qquad(t\in T). $$

In particular, once a recipe has a stability witness, that witness remains valid throughout all subsequent updates.

Initially every singleton has a witness, because it is ranked last and conservation holds. When processing of $f$ finishes, $f$ has a witness by the stopping condition. Thus, once all recipes have been processed, every recipe has a witness.

2.5 Termination and bit complexity

Every update exhausts at least one recipe in $M_f$.

Each such recipe is a proper subset of $f$, so its own processing has already finished. The algorithm increases a recipe only during its own processing. An exhausted selected recipe therefore remains zero forever.

Consequently, different updates exhaust different previously processed recipes. There are at most $m$ updates in total. Computing worst positive recipes, testing inclusion, and performing each update require polynomially many operations.

For exact arithmetic, write $\mu_t=p_t/q_t$ and put
$$ Q=\operatorname{lcm}_{t\in T}q_t. $$
Initially all coordinates are multiples of $1/Q$. Each update chooses $\Delta$ equal to an existing coordinate and uses only addition and subtraction. Hence all intermediate coordinates remain multiples of $1/Q$.

Every intermediate allocation is feasible, and every recipe is nonempty, so
$$ 0\leq x_e\leq 1. $$
Thus each coordinate has a representation with denominator $Q$ and numerator between $0$ and $Q$. Finally,
$$ \log_2 Q\leq\sum_{t\in T}\log_2q_t\leq L. $$
The arithmetic therefore has polynomial bit complexity. This completes the proof. $\square$

2.6 Why this argument does not cover all network hypergraphs

Laminarity supplies the disjoint partition in Section 2.2. Directed paths need not have that property.

For example, on a forward-oriented three-arc path, the recipes
$$ g=\{1,2\},\qquad h=\{2,3\},\qquad f=\{1,2,3\} $$
are all directed paths. The inclusion-maximal members of $\{g,h\}$ overlap at type $2$. Increasing $f$ by $\Delta$ and decreasing both $g$ and $h$ by $\Delta$ decreases the load at type $2$ by $\Delta$.

Thus the conservation argument above requires laminarity; it does not establish an algorithm for general network instances.

3. Encoding recipe capacities by private tree arcs

Consider the following auxiliary problem on a network hypergraph. Types have positive rational capacities $b_t$, recipes have positive rational capacities $c_e$, and preferences are strict. An allocation $z$ is feasible if
$$ z_e\geq 0,\qquad z_e\leq c_e,\qquad \sum_{e\in\delta(t)}z_e\leq b_t. $$
It is stable if every unsaturated recipe $e$, meaning $z_e<c_e$, has some $t\in e$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{\substack{f\in\delta(t)\\f\succeq_t e}}z_f=b_t. $$
Singleton recipes are not required in this auxiliary problem.

Theorem 3. This capped fractional search problem and the search problem in the question are polynomial-time equivalent on network hypergraphs.

3.1 Construction

For every original recipe $e$, introduce a private type $d_e$. Define
$$ T'=T\cup\{d_e:e\in E\}, \qquad \widehat e=e\cup\{d_e\}. $$
The new recipes are all $\widehat e$, together with every singleton $\{v\}$ for $v\in T'$.

Give the new types capacities
$$ B_t=b_t\quad(t\in T), \qquad B_{d_e}=c_e. $$
At an original type $t$, preserve the original order of incident recipes under the correspondence $e\mapsto\widehat e$, and place $\{t\}$ last. At $d_e$, use
$$ \widehat e\succ_{d_e}\{d_e\}. $$

Normalize by
$$ C=\sum_{t\in T}b_t+\sum_{e\in E}c_e, \qquad \mu'_v=\frac{B_v}{C}. $$
All new masses are positive and sum to $1$.

3.2 The network promise is preserved

Choose any representing oriented tree for the original instance. For each recipe $e$, let its directed path start at $u_e$.

Attach a fresh leaf $\ell_e$ to $u_e$ by a new arc
$$ d_e=(\ell_e,u_e). $$
Adding these pendant arcs preserves the underlying tree. The path for $\widehat e$ starts at $\ell_e$, traverses $d_e$, and then follows the original path for $e$.

Every new singleton is itself a one-arc directed path. Hence the constructed instance satisfies precisely the network promise.

The reduction does not need to find the representing tree: the explicitly listed set $\widehat e=e\cup\{d_e\}$ is computable directly, and the preceding argument proves that a representation exists.

3.3 Exact correspondence of solutions

Suppose first that $z$ is a stable capped allocation. Before normalization, define
$$ y_{\widehat e}=z_e, $$
$$ y_{\{t\}}=b_t-\sum_{e\in\delta(t)}z_e, \qquad y_{\{d_e\}}=c_e-z_e. $$
Feasibility makes these coordinates nonnegative, and they satisfy conservation with right-hand side $B$.

Every singleton is stable because it is last-ranked.

If $z_e=c_e$, the private type $d_e$ witnesses stability of $\widehat e$, since
$$ y_{\widehat e}=B_{d_e}. $$
If $z_e<c_e$, take an original stability witness $t$ for $e$. The new singleton at $t$ is below $\widehat e$, and the other preferences are unchanged. Therefore,
$$ \sum_{\substack{\widehat f\ni t\\ \widehat f\succeq_t\widehat e}}y_{\widehat f} = \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\succeq_t e}}z_f =b_t. $$
Thus $y/C$ is a stable allocation for the constructed instance.

Conversely, suppose $x$ solves the constructed normalized instance. Set
$$ y=Cx,\qquad z_e=y_{\widehat e}. $$
Conservation at $d_e$ gives
$$ z_e+y_{\{d_e\}}=c_e, $$
and conservation at each original type gives the original vertex-capacity inequality.

If $z_e<c_e$, then $d_e$ cannot witness stability of $\widehat e$: its weak-preference prefix consists only of $\widehat e$ and has allocation $z_e<c_e$. A witness must therefore be an original type. Its prefix excludes its last-ranked singleton, yielding exactly the original capped stability condition.

These maps are inverse to each other. The constructed instance has
$$ |T'|=\tau+m,\qquad |E'|=\tau+2m, $$
and polynomial encoding length.

3.4 Reduction in the other direction

Given an instance from the question, retain its types, recipes, preferences, and vertex capacities $\mu_t$, and assign every recipe capacity $2$.

Every feasible allocation satisfies $z_e\leq\mu_t\leq 1$ for any $t\in e$. Thus every recipe is unsaturated. Stability of the last-ranked singleton at $t$ forces its total incident load to equal $\mu_t$. The capped stability conditions consequently become exactly those in the question.

This proves both reductions. $\square$

4. Exact sparse solutions and an explicit sparsification procedure

The next results justify extracting integral solutions from the capacity gadget.

4.1 Total unimodularity

Lemma 4. The incidence matrix $A$ of an instance in the question is totally unimodular.

Proof. A representing tree has $\tau$ arcs and $\tau+1$ vertices. Delete one row from its vertex–arc incidence matrix, using $-1$ at a tail and $1$ at a head, and call the resulting square matrix $D$.

Leaf-row expansion proves
$$ \det D\in\{-1,1\}. $$
For each recipe $e$, let $N_e$ be the reduced incidence column of an arc joining the start of its directed path to its end. Incidence columns telescope along a directed path, so
$$ DA=N. $$

Choose $\tau$ columns $J$. If $N_J$ is nonsingular, their endpoint multigraph is connected: otherwise the indicator of a component not containing the deleted vertex is a nonzero left null vector.

A connected multigraph with $\tau+1$ vertices and $\tau$ edges is a tree. Its reduced incidence determinant is therefore $1$ or $-1$, again by leaf-row expansion. Singular choices have determinant zero. Hence
$$ \det A_J=\frac{\det N_J}{\det D}\in\{-1,0,1\}. $$

Now consider any square minor $A_{R,F}$. If it is nonzero, none of its selected columns is the singleton of a type outside $R$, because that would give a zero column in the minor. Append all singleton columns for $T\setminus R$. Up to row and column permutations, the resulting full-size matrix is
$$ \begin{pmatrix} A_{R,F}&0\\ A_{T\setminus R,F}&I \end{pmatrix}. $$
Its determinant equals the smaller determinant up to sign. The full-size result therefore covers every nonzero minor; zero minors already satisfy the requirement. $\square$

4.2 Existence and denominator bounds

The relevant existence theorem of Biró–Fleiner states that every finite hypergraph with strict incident-recipe preferences, nonnegative vertex capacities, and nonnegative recipe capacities admits a real feasible allocation in which every unsaturated recipe has a vertex whose weak-preference prefix fills its capacity. See [Theorem 12](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf).

Apply it with vertex capacities $\mu_t$ and recipe capacities $2$. As in Section 3.4, every recipe is unsaturated, and last-ranked singleton stability forces conservation. Thus a real stable allocation $x$ exists.

Let $S=\operatorname{supp}(x)$. The set
$$ F_S=\{z\in P(\mu):z_e=0\text{ for }e\notin S\} $$
is a nonempty bounded polytope. It is bounded because each coordinate is bounded by the mass of any type in its nonempty recipe.

Choose a vertex $y$ of $F_S$. Lemma 1 makes it stable. Moreover, $F_S$ is a face of $P(\mu)$: in a convex combination of nonnegative feasible points, a zero coordinate forces that coordinate to be zero in both points. Thus $y$ is also a vertex of $P(\mu)$.

Its positive-support columns are independent. Otherwise a nonzero dependence $Ah=0$ supported on positive coordinates would permit both $y+\varepsilon h$ and $y-\varepsilon h$ for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, contradicting extremality. Consequently,
$$ |\operatorname{supp}(y)|\leq\tau. $$

Let this support have size $k$, and select $k$ rows making its column submatrix nonsingular. Its determinant is $1$ or $-1$ by Lemma 4, so its inverse is integral. For
$$ Q=\operatorname{lcm}_{t\in T}q_t, \qquad \mu_t=\frac{p_t}{q_t}, $$
it follows that
$$ Qy\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{E}. $$
Since $\log_2Q\leq L$ and $0\leq y_e\leq1$, this stable allocation has polynomial encoding length.

4.3 Polynomial-time sparsification of a supplied rational allocation

Lemma 5. Given a rational stable allocation $x$, one can compute, in polynomial time in the input and the encoding length of $x$, a stable vertex whose support is contained in $\operatorname{supp}(x)$.

Proof. Maintain a feasible allocation $z$, initially $x$.

If its positive-support columns are dependent, choose a maximal independent subfamily $B$ and another positive-support column $j$. Write
$$ A_j=A_B\alpha. $$
Choose $|B|$ rows on which $A_B$ is nonsingular. Cramer’s rule expresses every coordinate of $\alpha$ as a ratio of square minors of $A$. Total unimodularity therefore gives
$$ \alpha_i\in\{-1,0,1\}. $$
Define a null vector by
$$ h_j=1,\qquad h_i=-\alpha_i\quad(i\in B), $$
with all other coordinates zero. Then
$$ Ah=0,\qquad h\in\{-1,0,1\}^{E}. $$

The vector $h$ has both signs. It has a positive coordinate at $j$. If it had no negative coordinate, the nonnegativity of $A$ and the nonempty column $A_j$ would imply $Ah\ne0$.

Set
$$ \lambda=\min_{e:h_e=1}z_e, \qquad z'=z-\lambda h. $$
All coordinates used in this minimum are positive. Thus $z'$ is nonnegative, satisfies $Az'=Az$, introduces no new support coordinate, and eliminates at least one positive coordinate. Lemma 1 preserves stability.

After at most $m$ iterations, the support columns are independent. The resulting point is a vertex: any convex decomposition into feasible points forces both points to vanish outside that support, and independence then forces their remaining coordinates to coincide.

For bit complexity, let $R$ be the least common multiple of the denominators of the supplied coordinates. Each step uses only addition and subtraction of a coordinate, so all intermediate coordinates remain multiples of $1/R$. Feasibility bounds them by $1$, and
$$ \log_2R $$
is at most the total denominator encoding length of the supplied allocation. All linear-algebra operations and arithmetic therefore have polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

The same argument applies to any positive rational right-hand side $b$. If $b$ is integral, the final independent-support solution is integral, by the nonsingular-minor argument above.

5. Consequences for the original search question

5.1 Ordinary stable network $b$-matching reduces to this problem

Consider positive integer vertex capacities $b_t$ and recipe capacities
$$ c_e=1. $$
An integral capped allocation is then the characteristic vector of a subset of recipes, with at most $b_t$ selected recipes incident to each type. Stability is required for every unselected recipe.

Apply Theorem 3 and use a solver for the problem in the question. Here the normalization constant
$$ C=\sum_tb_t+m $$
is integral. Multiply the solver’s allocation by $C$, then apply Lemma 5 with the integral right-hand side $B$.

The resulting allocation is integral and stable. At each private type,
$$ y_{\widehat e}+y_{\{d_e\}}=1, $$
so
$$ y_{\widehat e}\in\{0,1\}. $$
Projecting these coordinates gives an ordinary stable network-hypergraph $b$-matching.

Therefore, a polynomial algorithm for the full task would also give a polynomial algorithm for that unequal-capacity discrete search problem. This is a reduction, not a hardness classification.

5.2 Exact computation for fixed support size

The stable vertex established in Section 4 has at most $\tau$ positive coordinates. Since singleton columns give $A$ full row rank, its support extends to a nonsingular set $J$ of $\tau$ columns.

One can therefore enumerate all such column sets, solve
$$ A_Jy_J=\mu,\qquad y_{E\setminus J}=0, $$
and test nonnegativity and every stability condition exactly. Some candidate succeeds. The running time is
$$ \binom{m}{\tau}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
This is polynomial for fixed $\tau$, but not a polynomial bound when $\tau$ varies.

6. Remaining open point

The laminar algorithm handles unequal masses without dependence on their numerical magnitudes. The private-arc construction shows that the absence of explicit recipe capacities does not remove the difficulty of capacitated network allocation.

What remains missing is either a polynomial algorithm for arbitrary directed-path families in an oriented tree, or a hardness reduction preserving that exact promise. Neither is established by the arguments above.

These arguments are correct.

Conservation and nonnegativity imply that a preference prefix fills $\mu_t$ exactly when all incident recipes below that prefix have zero allocation. Positivity of $\mu_t$ ensures that the worst positive incident recipe $w_t(x)$ exists.

Consequently, the stated characterization

2. Polynomial algorithm for laminar families

The algorithm is sound in substance.

Partition argument. During an update for $f$, every selected worst recipe is positive and differs from $f$. Other positive recipes were either initialized as singletons or increased during an earlier processing stage. Laminarity and the cardinality order therefore ensure that every selected recipe is a proper subset of $f$.

The inclusion-maximal selected recipes are disjoint. Every $t\in f$ belongs to its selected recipe, which belongs to a maximal selected recipe. Thus $M_f$ partitions $f$.

Feasibility. Each type in $f$ receives an increase of $\Delta$ through $f$ and exactly one decrease of $\Delta$ through the partition. Types outside $f$ are unaffected. The minimum defining $\Delta$ prevents negative coordinates.

Preservation of witnesses. While $f$ has no witness, strict total preferences imply
$$ f\succ_t w_t(x) \qquad(t\in f). $$
The update adds only $f$ and removes positive recipes. Hence no type acquires a worse worst positive recipe. Existing witnesses remain valid.

Termination. Every update exhausts at least one proper subset of $f$. Such a recipe cannot subsequently be increased: nonsingletons increase only during their own processing, and singletons never increase after initialization. Distinct updates therefore exhaust distinct recipes, giving at most $m$ updates.

Bit complexity. All coordinates remain multiples of $1/Q$, where
$$ Q=\operatorname{lcm}_{t\in T}q_t. $$
Feasibility and nonempty recipes bound each coordinate by $1$. The claimed bound $\log_2Q\leq L$ therefore supplies polynomial arithmetic complexity.

The cases $\tau=1$ and an instance containing only singletons are covered: no update is necessary.

Minor issue. Sections 2.2 and 2.5 describe positive singletons as already processed, although the algorithm processes only nonsingletons. This is a wording inconsistency, not a mathematical obstruction.

Minimum clarification: Say that initialized singletons are treated as completed recipes, or distinguish initialization from earlier nonsingleton processing in those statements.

The example is correct.

$$ \Delta-\Delta-\Delta=-\Delta. $$
Thus the laminar feasibility argument does not extend to arbitrary network instances.

This establishes a limitation of the proposed algorithm; it establishes no hardness result.

4. Private-arc capacity encoding and Theorem 3

Both reductions are correct.

Network promise. Attaching a fresh incoming pendant arc at the start of each original directed path preserves the underlying tree. The augmented recipe is precisely the arc set of the extended directed path. Singleton recipes also satisfy the promise.

The reduction needs only to output the augmented subsets. Its proof of representability does not require computing the original representing tree.

Forward solution map. The singleton coordinates are exactly the unused original vertex capacities and unused recipe capacities. They are nonnegative and enforce conservation. A saturated recipe is witnessed by its private type; an unsaturated recipe retains its original witness.

Reverse solution map. Conservation at $d_e$ enforces $z_e\leq c_e$. If $z_e<c_e$, the private type cannot witness the augmented recipe, so a witness must be an original type. Its prefix excludes the last-ranked singleton and reproduces the auxiliary stability condition.

The maps are inverse after normalization. The stated instance sizes and polynomial encoding bound are correct.

Other direction. Assigning recipe capacity $2$ makes every feasible recipe unsaturated because its allocation is bounded by an incident mass, which is at most $1$. Stability of each last-ranked singleton then forces conservation.

Theorem 3 proves an equivalence with the defined capped fractional problem. It does not classify either problem’s complexity.

5. Total unimodularity: Lemma 4

The proof is correct.

The reduced tree incidence matrix $D$ is square and has determinant $\pm1$. Directed-path incidence columns telescope, giving
$$ DA=N. $$

For a nonsingular selection $N_J$, a disconnected endpoint multigraph would provide the stated nonzero left null vector. Therefore that multigraph is connected. It has $\tau+1$ vertices and $\tau$ edges, so it is a tree and its reduced incidence determinant is $\pm1$.

This verifies every full-row-size minor of $A$. Appending the available singleton columns outside a selected row set correctly embeds every nonzero smaller square minor into a full-size block triangular minor. Hence all square minors belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$.

The singleton assumption is explicitly available in USER_TASK and is correctly used here.
