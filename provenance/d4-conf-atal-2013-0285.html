The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) alternatives, committee size \(K\), a positional scoring vector \(\alpha\), and \(\tau\) complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute a \(K\)-alternative committee \(W\) and a feasible assignment \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in W}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}=1/K\) for every \(a\in W\), whose score \(\sum_t\sum_{a\in W}x_{t,a}\alpha(\operatorname{pos}_t(a))\) is at least \((1-1/e)\) times the optimum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m\geq 1\) alternatives, and let \(1\leq K\leq m\) be an integer. The input scoring vector consists of binary integers \(\alpha_1\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_m=0\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,K,L\).
Write \(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)\) for the position of \(a\) in ranking \(t\), with position \(1\) best. A feasible solution consists of a set \(W\subseteq A\), \(|W|=K\), and divisible assignments \(x_{t,a}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in W}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}=1/K\) for every selected alternative.
Its score is \(S(W,x)=\sum_t\sum_{a\in W}x_{t,a}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_t(a)}\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the maximum score over all feasible committees and real assignments. Any committee meeting the requested guarantee is acceptable, without tie-breaking.

Does this problem admit an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\) that returns a feasible committee and rational assignment with \(S(W,x)\geq(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

Discrete Monroe has a polynomial-time \((1-1/e)\)-approximation for arbitrary positional scoring vectors ([Skowron, Faliszewski, and Slinko](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2013/docs/p399.pdf)); the extended treatment assumes the committee size divides the number of voters ([extended version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.4026)).
No result settling this guarantee for the exact model above with binary rational masses was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the unspecified scoring-vector domain to the original definition: explicitly encoded nonnegative integers, weakly decreasing, with the final entry zero.
  • Specified the committee-size domain as integers from 1 through m and rankings as strict complete orders.
  • Specified total input bit length L and binary encoding of scoring entries, so polynomial time includes numerical input size.
  • Required rational output assignments while defining the continuous optimum over real assignments.
  • Allowed any qualifying committee without tie-breaking, following the discrete definition.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem admits a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm. In fact, it returns a feasible rational assignment with score at least

\(\displaystyle \left[1-\left(1-\frac1K\right)^K\right]\mathrm{OPT}\ \geq\ (1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}.\)

The algorithm uses the greedy selection idea of Algorithm GM in the [extended treatment](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.4026), but computes assignments directly on the listed types. The proof below establishes both the guarantee and polynomial running time for binary rational masses.

1. Partial-assignment value.

Write \(w_{t,a}=\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_t(a)}\). The scoring-vector assumptions imply \(w_{t,a}\geq0\).

For every \(S\subseteq A\), define

\(\displaystyle f(S)=\max_y\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{a\in S}w_{t,a}y_{t,a},\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle y_{t,a}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{a\in S}y_{t,a}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}y_{t,a}\leq\frac1K.\)

Thus \(f\) permits unassigned mass and unused candidate capacity. This definition applies to all subsets, including those larger than \(K\). Its feasible region is nonempty and compact, so its maximum exists. Also, \(f(\varnothing)=0\), and enlarging \(S\) preserves every previously feasible assignment. Hence \(f\) is monotone.

For \(|S|=K\), we claim that \(f(S)\) equals the optimum under the required equality constraints.

To prove this, take an optimal partial assignment \(y\) and define residuals

\(\displaystyle r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{a\in S}y_{t,a},\qquad d_a=\frac1K-\sum_t y_{t,a}.\)

They are nonnegative, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_t r_t=1-\sum_{t,a}y_{t,a}=\sum_{a\in S}d_a.\)

Whenever this common residual total is positive, choose a type and candidate with positive residuals and increase their assignment by \(\min\{r_t,d_a\}\). Each step eliminates at least one positive residual. After at most \(\tau+K\) steps, all equalities hold.

Because all scores are nonnegative, completion cannot decrease the score. Conversely, every complete assignment is feasible for the partial problem. The completed assignment therefore has score exactly \(f(S)\). In particular,

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}=\max_{|S|=K}f(S).\)

2. Exact computation on the compressed input.

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/d_t\), with positive integers \(p_t,d_t\), and set

\(\displaystyle N=K\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}d_t,\qquad b_t=N\mu_t,\qquad q=N/K.\)

All \(b_t\) and \(q\) are integers, and \(\sum_t b_t=N\). Crucially,

\(\displaystyle \log N=O(L+\log(K+1)).\)

The integer \(N\) may be exponentially large, but its binary representation has polynomial length.

For a given \(S\), construct a directed network with source \(s\), sink \(h\), one vertex per type, and one vertex per candidate in \(S\). Its arcs, capacities, and costs are:

| Arc | Capacity | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(s\to t\) | \(b_t\) | \(0\) |
| \(t\to a\), for \(a\in S\) | \(b_t\) | \(-w_{t,a}\) |
| \(a\to h\) | \(q\) | \(0\) |
| \(h\to s\) | \(N\) | \(0\) |

A feasible circulation induces the partial assignment \(y_{t,a}=z_{t,a}/N\), where \(z_{t,a}\) is its flow on \(t\to a\). Conversely, a feasible partial assignment induces a circulation by setting these flows to \(Ny_{t,a}\), setting the other forward flows to the corresponding sums, and returning the total flow on \(h\to s\). The return-arc capacity is respected because total assigned mass is at most one.

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \min\{\text{circulation cost}\}=-Nf(S).\)

We use the standard minimum-cost circulation theorem: a finite network with integer capacities and costs can be optimized exactly in time polynomial in its network encoding length, and an integral optimal circulation can be returned. Polynomial-time optimization is provided, for example, by [Orlin’s minimum-cost flow algorithm](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/opre.41.2.338). Here all capacities and costs are integers, the zero circulation is feasible, and finite capacities make the feasible region bounded.

For completeness, the integrality assertion also follows directly. In a nonintegral feasible circulation with integer capacities, consider the arcs whose flows are noninteger. No vertex can be incident to exactly one such arc: conservation would then equate a noninteger quantity with an integer. Thus, if any such arcs exist, their underlying undirected multigraph contains a cycle. Perturb flow around that cycle, using signs that preserve conservation. Choose one of the two directions whose cost change is nonpositive, and continue until a cycle arc reaches its next integer value. All perturbed flows remain within their adjacent integer bounds, so feasibility is preserved, no integral arc becomes nonintegral, and at least one additional arc becomes integral. Starting from an optimum, the cost cannot strictly improve, so this procedure preserves optimality and eventually yields an integral optimum.

Thus the oracle returns both \(f(S)\) exactly and an optimal rational partial assignment whose coordinates have denominator \(N\).

3. Submodularity: a matching lemma.

We first prove a finite matching statement.

Let a weighted bipartite graph have fixed left vertex set and right vertex set \(R\). For \(B\subseteq R\), let \(H(B)\) be the maximum weight of a matching using only right vertices in \(B\); unmatched vertices are allowed. Then

\(\displaystyle H(P)+H(Q)\geq H(P\cup Q)+H(P\cap Q)\)

for all \(P,Q\subseteq R\).

Proof. Choose optimal matchings \(M_U\) for \(P\cup Q\) and \(M_I\) for \(P\cap Q\). Superimpose them, retaining two copies of any common edge.

Every vertex has degree at most two, and at every degree-two vertex one incident edge comes from each matching. Hence each nontrivial component is a path or an even cycle, with the two original matching labels alternating.

A right vertex in \(P\setminus Q\) or \(Q\setminus P\), if incident to an edge, is a path endpoint whose edge belongs to \(M_U\). A component cannot contain two such endpoints. Indeed, they would both be right vertices, forcing an even path length; but an alternating path whose first and last edges both belong to \(M_U\) has odd length.

Now recolor edges alternately red and blue in every component. If a component contains an endpoint in \(P\setminus Q\), require its incident edge to be red. If it contains an endpoint in \(Q\setminus P\), require its incident edge to be blue. There is at most one such requirement per component, so the coloring exists.

The red edges form a matching using only right vertices in \(P\), and the blue edges form one using only right vertices in \(Q\). Together they retain every edge, with multiplicity, of \(M_U\) and \(M_I\). Their combined weight is therefore \(H(P\cup Q)+H(P\cap Q)\), proving the inequality. \(\square\)

4. Submodularity for rational masses.

Use \(N,b_t,q\) from Section 2 to define a conceptual bipartite graph:

  • Type \(t\) has \(b_t\) left copies.
  • Candidate \(a\) has \(q\) right copies.
  • Every type-\(t\) copy is joined to every candidate-\(a\) copy by an edge of weight \(w_{t,a}\).

Let \(C(S)\) contain all right copies of candidates in \(S\). We claim

\(\displaystyle H(C(S))=Nf(S).\)

A matching gives integer assignment counts satisfying row bounds \(b_t\) and column bounds \(q\), so dividing these counts by \(N\) gives a feasible partial assignment.

Conversely, Section 2 supplies an optimal partial assignment whose scaled counts are integers. These counts can be realized by a matching: allocate distinct left copies within each type and distinct right copies within each candidate according to the counts. The row and column bounds ensure enough copies exist. The resulting matching has weight \(Nf(S)\). This proves the claim.

The candidate-copy groups are disjoint, so

\(\displaystyle C(S\cup T)=C(S)\cup C(T),\qquad C(S\cap T)=C(S)\cap C(T).\)

Applying the matching lemma and dividing by \(N\) yields

\(\displaystyle f(S)+f(T)\geq f(S\cup T)+f(S\cap T).\)

Thus \(f\) is submodular. In particular, for \(S\subseteq T\) and \(a\notin T\), applying this inequality to \(S\cup\{a\}\) and \(T\) gives diminishing returns:

\(\displaystyle f(S\cup\{a\})-f(S)\geq f(T\cup\{a\})-f(T).\)

The expanded graph is used only for this proof; the algorithm never constructs it.

5. Greedy algorithm and approximation proof.

Start with \(S_0=\varnothing\). For \(i=0,\ldots,K-1\), choose any

\(\displaystyle a_i\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in A\setminus S_i}f(S_i\cup\{a\}),\)

and set \(S_{i+1}=S_i\cup\{a_i\}\). Every comparison uses the exact oracle from Section 2.

Let \(O\) be an optimal size-\(K\) committee and let

\(\displaystyle \Delta_i=f(S_{i+1})-f(S_i).\)

Monotonicity gives \(f(O)\leq f(S_i\cup O)\). Add the candidates of \(O\setminus S_i\) one at a time. Diminishing returns bounds each successive gain by its gain when added directly to \(S_i\). Greedy selection bounds that gain by \(\Delta_i\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\leq f(S_i\cup O)\leq f(S_i)+\sum_{a\in O\setminus S_i}\bigl(f(S_i\cup\{a\})-f(S_i)\bigr)\leq f(S_i)+K\Delta_i.\)

Rearranging,

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}-f(S_{i+1})\leq\left(1-\frac1K\right)\bigl(\mathrm{OPT}-f(S_i)\bigr).\)

Starting from \(f(S_0)=0\) and applying this recurrence \(K\) times gives

\(\displaystyle f(S_K)\geq\left[1-\left(1-\frac1K\right)^K\right]\mathrm{OPT}.\)

For \(K=1\), this is exact optimization. For \(K>1\), the inequality \(\log(1-u)\leq-u\), applied at \(u=1/K\), gives

\(\displaystyle \left(1-\frac1K\right)^K\leq e^{-1}.\)

Finally, return \(W=S_K\), compute its optimal partial assignment, and complete it as in Section 1. Its score equals \(f(S_K)\), so it satisfies the requested guarantee.

6. Running time and output size.

There are at most \(Km\) oracle calls, plus at most one final assignment computation. Every queried set has size at most \(K\). Its flow network has at most

\(\displaystyle V=\tau+K+2,\qquad E=\tau K+\tau+K+1\)

vertices and arcs, respectively. Each capacity and cost has \(O(L+\log(K+1))\) bits. Hence an exact oracle call takes

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{poly}\!\left(\tau K,L+\log(K+1)\right)\)

bit operations, with a fixed polynomial independent of the input.

Including preprocessing, exact comparisons, and output construction, the total running time is

\(\displaystyle O(m\tau L)+\operatorname{poly}(L)+O(Km+1)\operatorname{poly}\!\left(\tau K,L+\log(K+1)\right),\)

which is polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\). There is no dependence on the numerical magnitude of \(N\).

The final completion can be performed with integer residual counts before division by \(N\). Consequently, every output coordinate remains a multiple of \(1/N\), with numerator at most \(N\). The rational assignment therefore has total encoding length \(O(\tau K(L+\log(K+1)))\).
