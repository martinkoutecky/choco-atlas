The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), and an explicitly listed support of \(\tau\) strict rankings \(t\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, promise that all induced pairwise margins \(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t\) are nonzero and have distinct absolute values; decide whether \(c^\star\) is the Simple Stable Voting winner of the tournament defined by \(M_\mu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(c^\star\in C\). A society is given by its support: an explicit list of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings of \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length, and state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Define \(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:b\succ_t a}\mu_t\). Promise that every margin between distinct candidates is nonzero and that absolute margins for different unordered candidate pairs are distinct.
For each nonempty \(S\subseteq C\), define Simple Stable Voting recursively. If \(S=\{a\}\), set \(W(S)=a\). Otherwise scan all ordered pairs \((a,b)\in S^2\) with \(a\ne b\) in decreasing order of the signed margin \(M_\mu(a,b)\), including negative margins; the first pair satisfying \(W(S\setminus\{b\})=a\) determines \(W(S)=a\). Candidate deletion retains the original margins between remaining candidates. The promise makes every scan order and winner unique.

Is deciding whether \(W(C)=c^\star\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete winner determination for Simple Stable Voting is \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-complete, even on uniquely weighted tournaments and under a promise that the winner is one of two designated candidates. Hardness also holds for explicitly listed finite electorates with strict rankings.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpret distinct absolute margins as distinct across unordered candidate pairs, since reversed ordered pairs necessarily have equal absolute margins.
  • Use the full recursive definition: scan signed margins over all ordered pairs, including negative margins, rather than requiring a positive pairwise victory.
  • Require a nonempty candidate set and a target belonging to it; allow the singleton base case.
  • Candidate deletion restricts the candidate set while retaining the original pairwise margins.
  • Define L as the total input bit length.

Result.

The problem is \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-complete, and therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, using the discrete hardness theorem supplied in the question. This holds even when every mass is dyadic and the support size satisfies \(\tau\leq m(m-1)+2\).

Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) exists if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{PSPACE}\). For \(m=1\), the answer is immediate; the hardness concerns unbounded \(m\).

1. The winner depends only on the signed-margin order.

Consider two antisymmetric margin functions on the same candidate set. Suppose their margins are nonzero, their absolute margins are pairwise distinct, and they induce the same ordering of all ordered pairs by signed margin.

We claim that they produce the same winner on every nonempty subset \(S\).

We prove this by induction on \(|S|\). Singleton winners agree by definition. For a larger subset, the induction hypothesis gives the same winner of \(S\setminus\{b\}\) for every \(b\in S\). Thus each ordered pair \((a,b)\) satisfies the recursive eligibility condition under one margin function exactly when it satisfies it under the other. The scan orders also agree, being restrictions of the common global order. Hence the first eligible pair, and its winning candidate, agree.

The scan always has an eligible pair: choose any \(b\in S\) and put \(a=W(S\setminus\{b\})\). Then \(a\neq b\), and \((a,b)\) is eligible.

2. A small dyadic support realizes any uniquely weighted tournament.

Let a source instance of the supplied discrete hardness theorem have candidate set \(C\), with \(m\geq2\), and let \(p=\binom m2\). Its underlying margin function is antisymmetric, with nonzero margins and pairwise distinct absolute margins.

Sort the unordered pairs by increasing absolute margin. Replace the absolute margin of the pair in position \(r\) by \(r\), for \(r\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\), retaining its orientation. Write \(A(a,b)\) for this new margin function.

This replacement preserves the entire signed-margin order:

  • Positive margins precede negative margins.
  • Among positive margins, larger absolute margins come first.
  • Among negative margins, smaller absolute margins come first.

Therefore Section 1 shows that the replacement preserves every recursive winner.

We now construct rankings whose unnormalized margins are \(2A(a,b)\).

For each oriented pair with \(A(a,b)=r>0\), choose an ordering \(R\) of \(C\setminus\{a,b\}\). Introduce the rankings

\(a\succ b\succ R\)

and

\(R^{\mathrm{rev}}\succ a\succ b\),

each with unnormalized weight \(r\).

Their contributions are as follows.

  • Both place \(a\) above \(b\), giving margin \(2r\) for \((a,b)\).
  • For any \(x\in C\setminus\{a,b\}\), the first places both \(a,b\) above \(x\), and the second places \(x\) above both. These contributions cancel.
  • For any two candidates in \(R\), their relative orders in the two rankings are opposite. These contributions also cancel.

Thus this gadget contributes only the desired margin for its designated unordered pair. Summing over all \(p\) pairs gives exactly \(2A\).

The total unnormalized weight is

\(T=2\sum_{r=1}^{p}r=p(p+1)\).

Let \(Q\) be the least power of two strictly larger than \(T\). Assign mass \(r/Q\) to each ranking in the gadget of weight \(r\). Their total mass is \(T/Q\).

Finally, choose any strict ranking \(\rho\) and add both \(\rho\) and its reversal, each with mass

\(\dfrac{Q-T}{2Q}\).

These masses are strictly positive. Their sum is \(1-T/Q\), so the complete society has total mass one. Their margin contributions cancel for every pair.

Consequently, the resulting society satisfies

\(M_\mu(a,b)=\dfrac{2A(a,b)}{Q}\)

for every pair of distinct candidates. In particular, its absolute margins are precisely

\(\dfrac{2}{Q},\dfrac{4}{Q},\ldots,\dfrac{2p}{Q}\).

They are nonzero and pairwise distinct, as required.

If rankings repeat, merge them by adding their masses. This preserves every margin and yields the required list of distinct rankings with positive masses. Because \(Q\) is a power of two, all masses remain dyadic, including after merging.

3. Encoding size and hardness transfer.

Before merging, the construction uses \(2p+2\) rankings. Thus

\(\tau\leq2p+2=m(m-1)+2\).

Since \(T=p(p+1)\) and \(T<Q\leq2T\), each mass has an encoding of length \(\mathcal O(\log m)\). Each ranking takes \(\mathcal O(m\log m)\) bits. Therefore the output length is

\(L=\mathcal O(m^3\log m)\).

Sorting the source weights takes polynomial time in their explicit input length. Generating and merging the rankings also takes polynomial time: there are \(\mathcal O(m^2)\) rankings, each of length \(m\), with polynomially bounded mass encodings. The construction never expands binary weights into an explicit list of individual voters.

Section 1 proves that the source instance and the constructed society have identical winners on every candidate subset. Keeping the designated candidate \(c^\star\) therefore gives a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

The supplied \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-hardness theorem applies to exactly the source class used here: uniquely weighted tournaments with the Simple Stable Voting recursion. Its hypotheses are preserved by the construction. Hence the support-based problem is \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-hard. The supplied additional promise that the winner belongs to two designated candidates is preserved as well.

4. Polynomial-space upper bound and running times.

First compute all margins exactly and sort the ordered pairs.

For general binary-encoded rational masses, take the product of their denominators as a common denominator. Its bit length is at most the sum of their denominator bit lengths, hence \(\mathcal O(L)\). The converted numerators and accumulated margin numerators also have \(\mathcal O(L)\) bits. Using elementary integer arithmetic, a valid loose preprocessing bound is

\(\mathcal O\!\left((\tau m^2+m^2\log m)L^2\right)\)

bit operations, with polynomial space.

Now evaluate \(W(S)\) recursively as follows. For a singleton, return its candidate. Otherwise, compute \(W(S\setminus\{b\})\) for every \(b\in S\), retaining these child winners in the current recursion frame. Then scan the precomputed global pair order, restricting it to \(S\), and return the first eligible candidate.

This is exactly the defining recursion. Its depth is at most \(m\). Each frame stores a subset, at most \(m\) child winners, and polynomially many bookkeeping bits. Including the margin table and input, total space is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

For completeness, the number of calls in this evaluation is at most

\(\sum_{d=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{m!}{(m-d)!}<e\,m!\).

Thus a valid running-time bound is

\(\mathcal O\!\left((\tau m^2+m^2\log m)L^2\right)+m!\operatorname{poly}(m)\),

while using polynomial space. Alternatively, memoizing one winner per nonempty subset gives time

\(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)+2^m\operatorname{poly}(m)\),

with exponential space.

Therefore winner determination belongs to \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\). Together with Section 3, this proves \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-completeness and settles the requested hardness alternative.
