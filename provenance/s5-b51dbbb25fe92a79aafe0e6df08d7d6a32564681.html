The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many chain classes with finite state sets, rational rewards and transition probabilities, a common rational discount factor, rational initial masses over class-state pairs, and a capacity κ ∈ (0,1], compute an ε-optimal allocation schedule for infinite-horizon discounted reward. At each period choose activated masses u_{j,s} with 0 ≤ u_{j,s} ≤ μ_{j,s} and ∑_{j,s} u_{j,s} ≤ κ; unactivated mass stays in place and activated mass transitions according to its class's Markov chain. Maximize the total discounted reward across the resulting mass flows.

1. An exact two-state control lemma

Consider two groups, $H$ and $L$, with total mass one. Let $x$ be the mass in $H$. Their activation rewards are $r_H\geq r_L\geq 0$.

Suppose activation moves a fraction $\alpha$ of activated $H$ mass into $L$, and a fraction $\beta$ of activated $L$ mass into $H$, where $\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]$.

An aggregate action consists of masses $p,q$ satisfying

$$ 0\leq p\leq x,\qquad 0\leq q\leq 1-x,\qquad p+q\leq\kappa. $$

Its reward and next state are

$$ r_Hp+r_Lq,\qquad x'=x-\alpha p+\beta q. $$

Lemma. For every $0<\gamma<1$ and $0<\kappa\leq 1$, the policy

$$ p=\min(x,\kappa),\qquad q=\kappa-p $$

is optimal for every initial $x\in[0,1]$.

1.1. Define the greedy policy’s value

The displayed action is feasible: when $x<\kappa$, its low-group activation is $\kappa-x\leq 1-x$.

Write

$$ a=1-\gamma,\qquad \Delta=r_H-r_L,\qquad c=\alpha+\beta,\qquad d=1-c. $$

The greedy state map and reward are

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} \beta\kappa+dx,&0\leq x\leq\kappa,\\ x-\alpha\kappa,&\kappa<x\leq 1, \end{cases} $$

and

$$ g(x)=\kappa r_L+\Delta\min(x,\kappa). $$

Define its value without assuming optimality:

$$ F(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t g(f^t(x)). $$

This converges, and

$$ 0\leq F(x)\leq\frac{\kappa r_H}{a}. $$

We will prove the global bound

$$ 0\leq F(z)-F(y)\leq D(z-y) \qquad(0\leq y\leq z\leq 1), $$

where

$$ D=\frac{\Delta}{1-\gamma d} =\frac{\Delta}{a+\gamma c}. $$

1.2. The value is affine on $[0,\kappa]$

The interval $[0,\kappa]$ is invariant under $f$. Indeed, its endpoint images are

$$ f(0)=\beta\kappa,\qquad f(\kappa)=(1-\alpha)\kappa, $$

both belonging to that interval, and $f$ is affine there.

For $x,y\in[0,\kappa]$, induction gives

$$ f^t(x)-f^t(y)=d^t(x-y). $$

Since $|d|\leq 1$ and $\gamma<1$, the resulting geometric series converges absolutely. Consequently,

$$ F(x)-F(y) =\Delta(x-y)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(\gamma d)^t =D(x-y). $$

Thus $F(x)=B+Dx$ on $[0,\kappa]$. Using the greedy recurrence at zero gives

$$ B=\kappa r_L+\gamma F(\beta\kappa) =\kappa r_L+\gamma(B+D\beta\kappa), $$

so

$$ B=\frac{\kappa r_L+\gamma D\beta\kappa}{a}. $$

1.3. The global monotonicity and Lipschitz bound

First suppose $\alpha=0$. For $x>\kappa$, greedy activation leaves $x$ unchanged and earns $\kappa r_H$ every period. Hence

$$ F(x)=\frac{\kappa r_H}{a}. $$

The same formula holds at $x=\kappa$, since $f(\kappa)=\kappa$. Therefore $F$ has slope $D$ below $\kappa$ and slope zero above it, proving the required bound.

Now suppose $\alpha>0$, and put $h=\alpha\kappa$. For $x>\kappa$, let

$$ n=\left\lceil\frac{x-\kappa}{h}\right\rceil. $$

Then greedy activation spends exactly $n$ periods above $\kappa$, after which its state is $x-nh\in[0,\kappa]$. Therefore

$$ F(x) =\frac{\kappa r_H(1-\gamma^n)}{a} +\gamma^n\bigl(B+D(x-nh)\bigr). $$

On each interval where $n$ is constant, this expression has slope $\gamma^nD$.

The adjacent expressions agree at their boundaries. To check this, the greedy recurrence at $\kappa$ is

$$ F(\kappa)=\kappa r_H+\gamma F(\kappa-h). $$

At a boundary $x=\kappa+nh$, this identity equates the expression using $n$ initial periods with the expression using $n+1$ initial periods.

There are finitely many such intervals on $[0,1]$, although their number can be large. Every slope lies in $[0,D]$, and the expressions join continuously. Summing the increments across the intervals proves

$$ 0\leq F(z)-F(y)\leq D(z-y). $$

1.4. Verify the Bellman inequality for every action

For any feasible $p,q$, define

$$ Q_x(p,q)=r_Hp+r_Lq+\gamma F(x-\alpha p+\beta q). $$

For fixed $p$, increasing $q$ cannot decrease $Q_x$: it increases the immediate reward by a nonnegative amount and increases the next state, while $F$ is nondecreasing. Hence it suffices to consider

$$ q(p)=\min(1-x,\kappa-p), \qquad 0\leq p\leq\min(x,\kappa). $$

We show that $Q_x(p,q(p))$ is nondecreasing in $p$.

When $q(p)=1-x$, increasing $p$ by a feasible amount $\delta$ decreases the next state by $\alpha\delta$. The Lipschitz bound gives

$$ Q_x(p+\delta,q(p+\delta))-Q_x(p,q(p)) \geq (r_H-\gamma\alpha D)\delta. $$

The coefficient is nonnegative because

$$ \gamma\alpha D \leq\gamma cD =\Delta\frac{\gamma c}{a+\gamma c} \leq\Delta \leq r_H. $$

When $q(p)=\kappa-p$, increasing $p$ by $\delta$ replaces $\delta$ units of low-group activation with high-group activation. The next state decreases by $c\delta$, so

$$ Q_x(p+\delta,q(p+\delta))-Q_x(p,q(p)) \geq(\Delta-\gamma cD)\delta =aD\delta \geq 0. $$

The two regimes join continuously. Thus the maximum occurs at

$$ p=\min(x,\kappa),\qquad q=\kappa-p. $$

By the defining recurrence of $F$, this proves, for every feasible action,

$$ r_Hp+r_Lq+\gamma F(x-\alpha p+\beta q)\leq F(x). $$

Apply this inequality along any feasible schedule, multiply its time-$t$ instance by $\gamma^t$, and sum through time $T-1$. The intermediate potentials cancel:

$$ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t(r_Hp_t+r_Lq_t) +\gamma^T F(x_T) \leq F(x_0). $$

The terminal term tends to zero because $F$ is bounded. Every feasible infinite schedule therefore has value at most $F(x_0)$. Greedy attains $F(x_0)$ by its definition. This proves the lemma.

2. A sufficient condition for an exact greedy circuit

Flatten the class-state types into $i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$, with block-diagonal transition matrix $P$.

Let the distinct reward levels be

$$ v_1>v_2>\cdots>v_K\geq 0. $$

For each $k<K$, define the upper reward group

$$ T_k=\{i:r_i\geq v_k\}. $$

Assume that, for each $k<K$, there are numbers $\alpha_k,\beta_k\in[0,1]$ such that

$$ \sum_{j\notin T_k}P(i,j)=\alpha_k \qquad\text{for every }i\in T_k, $$

and

$$ \sum_{j\in T_k}P(i,j)=\beta_k \qquad\text{for every }i\notin T_k. $$

Thus every activated state inside $T_k$ sends the same fraction outside it, and every activated state outside $T_k$ sends the same fraction inside it. Transitions within either group remain unrestricted.

Theorem. Under these assumptions, activating available mass in descending reward order until capacity $\kappa$ is filled is exactly optimal. Its circuit can be constructed in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, for unrestricted binary $\gamma,\kappa,\varepsilon$.

2.1. Each upper reward group is a two-state control problem

For any schedule, set

$$ x_k^t=\sum_{i\in T_k}\mu_i^t,\qquad p_k^t=\sum_{i\in T_k}u_i^t,\qquad q_k^t=\sum_{i\notin T_k}u_i^t. $$

Summing the original dynamics over $T_k$ and using the assumed transition sums gives

$$ x_k^{t+1} =x_k^t-\alpha_kp_k^t+\beta_kq_k^t. $$

The original activation constraints imply

$$ 0\leq p_k^t\leq x_k^t,\qquad 0\leq q_k^t\leq 1-x_k^t,\qquad p_k^t+q_k^t\leq\kappa. $$

The two-state lemma, applied with rewards $1$ and $0$, therefore bounds every schedule by

$$ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t p_k^t\leq F_k(x_k^0), $$

where $F_k$ is the corresponding greedy value from Section 1.

Because $T_k$ contains a complete prefix of the reward ordering, the original greedy policy simultaneously satisfies

$$ p_k^t=\min(x_k^t,\kappa),\qquad q_k^t=\kappa-p_k^t $$

for every $k<K$. It therefore attains every displayed upper bound simultaneously.

2.2. Decompose the original reward

For every type $i$,

$$ r_i=v_K+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(v_k-v_{k+1})\mathbf 1_{\{i\in T_k\}}. $$

Consequently, every schedule satisfies

$$ V(u) \leq \frac{v_K\kappa}{1-\gamma} +\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(v_k-v_{k+1})F_k(x_k^0). $$

Here the first term uses total activation at most $\kappa$ per period, and the other terms use the two-state bounds. All coefficients are nonnegative.

The greedy policy fills capacity every period and attains every upper-group bound. Hence it attains the displayed bound and is exactly optimal.

When $K=1$, all rewards are equal; filling capacity each period directly proves the same conclusion.

2.3. Construct the circuit

Sort the types as $i_1,\ldots,i_m$ in nonincreasing reward order. The circuit computes

$$ c_0=\kappa,\qquad u_{i_\ell}=\min(\mu_{i_\ell},c_{\ell-1}),\qquad c_\ell=c_{\ell-1}-u_{i_\ell} \quad(1\leq\ell\leq m). $$

A comparison and conditional selection implement each minimum. The circuit has $O(m)$ gates and uses binary rational constants. It ignores $t$.

For every nonnegative mass vector of total mass one, induction gives

$$ c_\ell= \max\left(0,\kappa-\sum_{h=1}^{\ell}\mu_{i_h}\right). $$

Thus all activations are feasible, and $c_m=0$ because $\kappa\leq 1$.

Sorting rewards and checking the transition conditions require only polynomially many exact rational operations with polynomial bit lengths. No value-function enumeration or horizon expansion is required.

2.4. Two consequences

Every instance with $m\leq 2$ is exactly solvable by this circuit. With two distinct rewards and two types, each reward group contains one type, so its transition-uniformity conditions hold automatically. Equal rewards and the one-type case were already covered.

A second consequence allows arbitrarily many states and arbitrary rewards. For one class, suppose

$$ P=(1-\rho)I+\rho\mathbf 1 p^{\mathsf T}, $$

where $\rho\in[0,1]$ and $p$ is a probability vector. For any upper group $T_k$, writing $p(T_k)=\sum_{i\in T_k}p_i$ gives

$$ \alpha_k=\rho(1-p(T_k)),\qquad \beta_k=\rho p(T_k). $$

The theorem therefore applies to every such transition matrix.

3. A three-state counterexample outside the condition

The sufficient condition does not hold for arbitrary transitions.

Consider one class with deterministic transitions:

| State | Initial mass | Reward | Activated successor |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| $a$ | $1/2$ | $10$ | $z$ |
| $b$ | $1/2$ | $9$ | $b$ |
| $z$ | $0$ | $0$ | $z$ |

Set $\gamma=1/2$ and $\kappa=3/4$. Thus $m=3$ and $\tau=2$.

Reward priority activates $1/2$ at $a$ and $1/4$ at $b$ at time zero. Subsequently, only the $1/2$ mass at $b$ earns rewards. Its value is

$$ V_{\mathrm{priority}} =\frac{29}{4} +\frac{\gamma(9/2)}{1-\gamma} =\frac{47}{4}. $$

Ordinary Gittins indices give the same ordering: the indices at $a,b,z$ are respectively $10,9,0$.

Instead, activate $1/4$ at $a$ and $1/2$ at $b$ at each of times zero and one, then activate $1/2$ at $b$ forever. This is feasible and has value

$$ 7+\gamma\,7+\frac{\gamma^2(9/2)}{1-\gamma} =\frac{51}{4}. $$

We now prove optimality among all divisible schedules.

Write $p_t=u_a^t$ and $q_t=u_b^t$. Since activation at $a$ permanently removes mass from $a$,

$$ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}p_t\leq\frac12. $$

Also,

$$ q_t\leq\min\left(\frac12,\frac34-p_t\right). $$

With $y_+=\max(y,0)$, the period reward is therefore bounded by

$$ 10p_t+9q_t \leq \frac92+10p_t-9\left(p_t-\frac14\right)_+. $$

Put $p=p_0$. For $t\geq 1$, use $\gamma^t\leq 1/2$ and discard the nonpositive correction term. This gives

$$ \begin{aligned} V(u) &\leq 9+10p-9\left(p-\frac14\right)_+ +5\left(\frac12-p\right)\\ &= \frac{23}{2}+5p-9\left(p-\frac14\right)_+. \end{aligned} $$

If $p\leq 1/4$, the last expression is at most $51/4$. If $p\geq 1/4$, it equals $55/4-4p$, again at most $51/4$. Therefore

$$ V^*=\frac{51}{4}. $$

The priority gap is exactly $1$, so that policy fails every tolerance permitted by the task, namely $0<\varepsilon<1$. This is a policy counterexample, not a hardness reduction.

4. Exponentially many value-function pieces can coexist with a small circuit

Consider two states $H,L$, with deterministic transitions $H\to L$ and $L\to L$, rewards $1,0$, discount $\gamma=1/2$, and

$$ \kappa=2^{-n}. $$

Let $x$ denote the initial mass in $H$. The preceding lemma proves that activating $\min(x,\kappa)$ high-state mass greedily is optimal.

For each integer $k\in\{0,\ldots,2^n-1\}$ and

$$ k\kappa\leq x\leq(k+1)\kappa, $$

the value is

$$ F(x) =\frac{\kappa(1-\gamma^k)}{1-\gamma} +\gamma^k(x-k\kappa). $$

Indeed, the schedule activates $\kappa$ high-state mass for $k$ periods, then the remaining $x-k\kappa$, after which high-state mass is zero.

These $2^n$ intervals have pairwise distinct slopes $\gamma^k$. Thus the value function has exactly $2^n$ affine pieces, while the transition, reward, capacity, and discount data have encoding length $O(n)$.

Nevertheless, its optimal circuit is simply

$$ u_H=\min(\mu_H,\kappa),\qquad u_L=\kappa-u_H. $$

Thus an approach requiring explicit enumeration of all exact value-function pieces can be exponential even on an exactly tractable two-state family. This establishes no circuit-size lower bound.

5. General upper bound and the remaining gap

For completeness, the general truncation bound can be established directly.

Let $R=\max_i r_i$ and $a=1-\gamma$. Mass conservation and feasibility imply

$$ 0\leq r^{\mathsf T}u^t\leq\kappa R. $$

For an integer horizon $H$, maximize

$$ \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^t r^{\mathsf T}u^t $$

subject to the supplied initial masses and the original constraints

$$ 0\leq u^t\leq\mu^t,\qquad \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}u^t\leq\kappa,\qquad \mu^{t+1}=\mu^t+(P^{\mathsf T}-I)u^t. $$

This is an exact finite-horizon rational LP. Its feasible set is nonempty because zero activation is feasible. Nonnegativity and mass conservation bound every coordinate by one. The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore applies.

Writing its optimum as $V_H$, zero activation after time $H$ gives a feasible infinite extension. Conversely, every infinite schedule has tail at most $\kappa R\gamma^H/a$. Hence

$$ V_H\leq V^*\leq V_H+\frac{\kappa R\gamma^H}{a}. $$

If $R=0$, the zero circuit is optimal. Otherwise, let $k$ be the least nonnegative integer satisfying

$$ 2^k\geq\frac{\kappa R}{\varepsilon a}, $$

and choose $H=\lceil(k+1)/a\rceil$. Since $\log(1-a)\leq-a$,

$$ \gamma^H\leq e^{-aH}\leq e^{-(k+1)}\leq 2^{-k}. $$

Thus the finite-horizon optimum followed by zero activation is $\varepsilon$-optimal.

The integer $k$ is $O(L)$, and

$$ H=O\left(\frac{L+1}{1-\gamma}\right). $$

The LP has $O(mH)$ variables and constraints, with coefficient bit lengths polynomial in $H,L$. Its optimal activations can be stored in a circuit that selects the appropriate activation vector for $t<H$ and returns zero afterward. This proves polynomial construction time and output length in

$$ m,\tau,L,(1-\gamma)^{-1}. $$

Because $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$ can be exponential in $L$, this does not establish the requested general polynomial-time bound.

The exact circuit theorem above eliminates that dependence for a substantial structural family, including every instance with at most two types. For arbitrary transitions, a polynomial-time construction or a hardness reduction that respects both divisibility and circuit output is still missing.
