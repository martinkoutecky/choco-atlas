1. A succinct family of utilities

Fix $n\geq1$ and put $N=2^n$. There is one type, with mass and budget both equal to $1$. Supplies are
$$ \bar s=(1,1), $$
and there are no additional constraints.

Define
$$ f_N(t)=t+\frac{t^N}{2},\qquad t\geq0. $$
This function is continuous, strictly increasing, and maps $[0,\infty)$ onto itself. Let $h_N$ denote its inverse. Define
$$ u_N(a,b)= \begin{cases} a\,h_N(b/a),&a>0,\\ 0,&a=0, \end{cases} \qquad a,b\geq0. $$

The inverse notation is only a mathematical description. The actual input graph formula is
$$ a\geq0\ \land\ b\geq0\ \land\ z\geq0\ \land\ \left[ (a=0\land z=0) \ \lor\ \left(a>0\land z^N+2a^{N-1}z-2a^{N-1}b=0\right) \right]. $$

For $a>0$, the polynomial expression on the second branch is strictly increasing as a function of $z\geq0$: its derivative is
$$ Nz^{N-1}+2a^{N-1}>0. $$
Its value at $z=0$ is nonpositive, and it tends to infinity. Thus it has exactly one nonnegative root. Substituting $z=a\,h_N(b/a)$ verifies that this root is the stated utility.

The graph polynomial has three monomials, coefficients in $\{1,2,-2\}$, and exponents with $O(n)$ binary digits. All other market data have constant length. Consequently, the total input length satisfies
$$ L_n=\Theta(n). $$

2. Verification of all utility promises

Nonnegativity, positivity, and continuity

Nonnegativity follows from the definition. If $a,b>0$, then $b/a>0$, so $h_N(b/a)>0$ and $u_N(a,b)>0$. In particular, $(1,1)$ witnesses the required positivity on a feasible bundle.

Writing $z=u_N(a,b)$, its graph equation gives
$$ z^N+2a^{N-1}z=2a^{N-1}b. $$
For $a>0$, both terms on the left are nonnegative. Hence
$$ 0\leq z\leq b, \qquad z\leq 2^{1/N}a^{(N-1)/N}b^{1/N}. $$
The second bound tends to zero as $a$ tends to zero while $b$ remains bounded. It proves continuity at every point with $a=0$. Elsewhere, continuity follows from continuity of $h_N$.

Degree-one homogeneity

For $a>0$ and $\lambda>0$,
$$ u_N(\lambda a,\lambda b) =\lambda a\,h_N(b/a) =\lambda u_N(a,b). $$
The same identity holds when $a=0$, and when $\lambda=0$, by the zero boundary values.

Concavity

First, $h_N$ is concave. Indeed, $f_N$ is increasing and convex. For $v,w\geq0$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$, convexity gives
$$ f_N\!\left(\theta h_N(v)+(1-\theta)h_N(w)\right) \leq \theta v+(1-\theta)w. $$
Applying the increasing inverse $h_N$ yields
$$ \theta h_N(v)+(1-\theta)h_N(w) \leq h_N\!\left(\theta v+(1-\theta)w\right). $$

Now take $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ with $a,c>0$. For $0<\theta<1$, put
$$ e=\theta a+(1-\theta)c, \qquad \eta=\frac{\theta a}{e}. $$
Then
$$ \frac{\theta b+(1-\theta)d}{e} =\eta\frac ba+(1-\eta)\frac dc. $$
Concavity of $h_N$, multiplied by $e$, gives
$$ u_N\!\left(\theta(a,b)+(1-\theta)(c,d)\right) \geq \theta u_N(a,b)+(1-\theta)u_N(c,d). $$
The cases $\theta=0,1$ are equalities.

For arbitrary nonnegative endpoints, add $\varepsilon>0$ to both first coordinates, apply the preceding inequality, and let $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero. Continuity proves concavity on the entire nonnegative orthant.

Thus every promised utility property holds.

3. Existence and uniqueness of equilibrium

Let
$$ s=(1,1),\qquad r=u_N(s)=h_N(1). $$
By definition,
$$ r^N+2r-2=0. $$
Since $f_N(0)=0$ and $f_N(1)=3/2$, we have
$$ 0<r<1. $$

Because there is one type of mass $1$, market clearing forces its bundle to be $s$.

Suppose $p$ is an equilibrium price vector. Full expenditure requires
$$ p^\top s=1, $$
so $p\neq0$. For every direction $v$ with $p^\top v=0$, sufficiently small perturbations $s+\varepsilon v$ of either sign remain strictly positive and spend exactly the same budget. Optimality of $s$ and differentiability there imply
$$ \nabla u_N(s)^\top v=0. $$
Therefore $\nabla u_N(s)$ is proportional to $p$:
$$ \nabla u_N(s)=\lambda p. $$
Differentiating homogeneity along the ray through $s$ gives
$$ \nabla u_N(s)^\top s=u_N(s)=r. $$
Taking the scalar product of the proportionality with $s$ shows that $\lambda=r$. Thus every equilibrium necessarily has
$$ p=\frac{\nabla u_N(s)}r. $$
This argument also excludes alternative signed equilibrium prices.

Inverse differentiation gives
$$ h_N'(1)=\frac{1}{1+\frac N2r^{N-1}}. $$
Since $u_N(a,b)=a\,h_N(b/a)$,
$$ \frac{\partial u_N}{\partial b}(s)=h_N'(1), \qquad \frac{\partial u_N}{\partial a}(s)=r-h_N'(1). $$
Using $r^N=2(1-r)$, the forced prices simplify to
$$ p_2=\frac{1}{N-(N-1)r}, \qquad p_1=1-p_2. $$
Because $0<r<1$, both prices are strictly positive and their sum is $1$.

It remains to verify optimality at these prices. Concavity and differentiability at $s$ imply the supporting inequality
$$ u_N(x)\leq u_N(s)+\nabla u_N(s)^\top(x-s) $$
for every $x\geq0$. Explicitly, concavity gives, for $0<t<1$,
$$ u_N(x)-u_N(s) \leq \frac{u_N(s+t(x-s))-u_N(s)}t. $$
Letting $t$ decrease to zero proves the supporting inequality, including for boundary bundles.

Substituting $\nabla u_N(s)=rp$ and $p^\top s=1$ gives
$$ u_N(x)\leq r\,p^\top x. $$
Every affordable bundle therefore satisfies $u_N(x)\leq r=u_N(s)$. Hence $s$ maximizes utility, spends its full budget, and clears the market.

The equilibrium exists and all its coordinates are unique.

4. The forced price has exponential algebraic degree

Set
$$ \alpha=p_2=\frac{1}{N-(N-1)r}. $$

We first prove that
$$ F_N(T)=T^N+2T-2 $$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

An integer polynomial is called *primitive* if its coefficients have greatest common divisor $1$. The product of two primitive integer polynomials is primitive: otherwise, reduction modulo a prime dividing every product coefficient would give a zero product of two nonzero polynomials over a field, which is impossible.

This observation also shows that a primitive integer polynomial admitting a factorization over $\mathbb Q$ admits one over $\mathbb Z$. To see this, scale the rational factors to primitive integer polynomials. Their product is primitive, so the remaining rational scalar must be an integer, because its denominator divides every product coefficient. Primitivity of the original polynomial forces that scalar to be $1$ or $-1$.

If the monic polynomial $F_N$ were reducible, it would therefore factor into monic integer polynomials of positive degree. Modulo $2$, their product would be $T^N$. Each monic factor must consequently reduce to a positive-degree power of $T$, so both constant coefficients would be even. Their product would be divisible by $4$, contradicting the constant coefficient $-2$ of $F_N$. This proves irreducibility.

It follows that
$$ [\mathbb Q(r):\mathbb Q]=N. $$
Moreover,
$$ r=\frac{N\alpha-1}{(N-1)\alpha}. $$
Thus $\mathbb Q(r)=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$, and
$$ [\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=N=2^n. $$

where
$$ P_N(T) =(NT-1)^N+ 2(N-1)^{N-1}T^{N-1}(T-1). $$
Its degree is $N$, and its leading coefficient is
$$ D_N=N^N+2(N-1)^{N-1}. $$
Because $N$ is even, its constant coefficient is $1$. Hence $P_N$ is primitive.

The algebraic degree of $\alpha$ is $N$, so $P_N$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ and is a rational scalar multiple of its minimal polynomial.

Let $Q\in\mathbb Z[T]$ be any nonzero polynomial satisfying $Q(\alpha)=0$. Minimal-polynomial divisibility gives
$$ Q=P_NH,\qquad H\in\mathbb Q[T]. $$
In fact, $H$ has integer coefficients. Write
$$ H=\frac abH_0, $$
where $H_0$ is primitive, $a,b$ are coprime integers, and $b>0$. Since $P_NH_0$ is primitive and $Q$ has integer coefficients, $b$ divides every coefficient of $P_NH_0$. Therefore $b=1$.

Consequently, the leading coefficient of $Q$ is a nonzero integer multiple of $D_N$. In particular,
$$ \left|\operatorname{lc}(Q)\right| \geq D_N >N^N =2^{n2^n}. $$
Writing this coefficient explicitly in binary requires at least
$$ n2^n+1 $$
magnitude bits.

This conclusion permits arbitrary degree, sparse polynomials, reducible polynomials, and nonminimal representations. It does not depend on the choice of isolating interval.

For completeness, a short isolating interval is available: $P_N$ has exactly one root in $(1/N,1)$. On this interval, the substitution
$$ r=\frac{NT-1}{(N-1)T} $$
is a strictly increasing bijection onto $(0,1)$, and $F_N$ has exactly one root there. Thus the coefficient requirement persists even though an isolating interval with $O(n)$ bits exists.
