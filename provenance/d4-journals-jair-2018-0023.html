Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m\ge 3\) alternatives. A trichotomy is an ordered partition \(T\succ M\succ B\) of \(A\) into exactly three nonempty indifference classes; strict preference holds precisely between classes in that order.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge 1\) distinct trichotomous weak-order types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total bit length of the masses.
Define \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr)\) and \(S_\mu(P)=\sum_{a\succ_P b}\Delta_\mu(a,b)\). Compute any trichotomy \(P\) maximizing \(S_\mu(P)\); no tie-breaking among maximizers is required.

Is computing a maximizing trichotomy polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite voter profiles, computing a maximizing trichotomy from trichotomous ballots is NP-hard. For integer-weighted tournaments, the corresponding threshold problem over ordered partitions into three nonempty classes is NP-complete, even for purely cyclic weights or transitive positive-weight comparisons. These results do not separately classify the rational-mass formulation.

  • Allowed any maximizing trichotomy as output, following the discrete rule's allowance of tied optimal orderings.
  • Defined L as the total binary encoding length of all masses.

Result

Computing any maximizing trichotomy is NP-hard, using the finite-profile hardness premise supplied in the question. The reduction has polynomial output size and works even when all masses must be dyadic rationals.

1. Exact normalization of a finite profile

Let a finite profile contain \(N\ge 1\) trichotomous ballots. Merge identical ballots, obtaining distinct types \(R_1,\ldots,R_\tau\) with positive integer multiplicities \(c_1,\ldots,c_\tau\), where \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}c_t=N\).

For each feasible output trichotomy \(P\), define its score against type \(R_t\) by

\( g_t(P)=\sum_{a\succ_P b}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{R_t}b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_{R_t}a]\bigr). \)

The unnormalized finite-profile objective is therefore

\( F(P)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}c_tg_t(P). \)

This is an integer. Set \(\mu_t=c_t/N\). These masses are positive and sum to one. Interchanging the two finite sums in the definition of \(S_\mu\) gives

\( S_\mu(P)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_tg_t(P)=F(P)/N. \)

Thus \(S_\mu\) and \(F\) have exactly the same maximizers, including all ties. The feasible outputs also coincide: both require exactly three nonempty classes.

The transformation is polynomial-time. Each ballot can be represented by its class assignment to the \(m\) alternatives, so identical ballots can be grouped in polynomial time. Moreover, \(\tau\le N\), and each numerator and denominator has \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits. Consequently, the total mass encoding length is \(O(\tau\log(N+1))\), polynomial in the explicit finite-profile input size.

A polynomial-time algorithm for the rational-mass problem would therefore compute an optimum for the finite-profile problem: transform the input, call the algorithm once, and return its output unchanged. By the supplied finite-profile hardness premise, this proves NP-hardness when arbitrary rationals are encoded in binary.

2. Hardness even for dyadic masses

If “binary-rational” specifically requires terminating binary expansions, the fractions \(c_t/N\) need not qualify. The following construction closes that possible encoding gap.

Put \(p_t=c_t/N\) and \(C=\binom{m}{2}\). Choose a power of two \(D\) satisfying

\( D>8NC\tau. \)

For \(t<\tau\), define

\( q_t=\frac{\lfloor Dp_t\rfloor}{D}, \)

and define

\( q_\tau=1-\sum_{t<\tau}q_t. \)

These are dyadic masses summing to one. They are strictly positive: since \(D>N\) and \(c_t\ge 1\), each \(\lfloor Dp_t\rfloor\) is at least one; also,

\( q_\tau=p_\tau+\sum_{t<\tau}(p_t-q_t)\ge p_\tau>0. \)

The total rounding error satisfies

\( \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}|q_t-p_t| =2\sum_{t<\tau}(p_t-q_t) \le \frac{2(\tau-1)}{D} <\frac{2\tau}{D}. \)

For every feasible \(P\), at most one orientation of each unordered pair occurs in the sum defining \(g_t(P)\), and every summand belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Hence \(|g_t(P)|\le C\). It follows uniformly over all feasible outputs that

\( \left|S_q(P)-\frac{F(P)}{N}\right| =\left|\sum_t(q_t-p_t)g_t(P)\right| \le C\sum_t|q_t-p_t| <\frac{1}{4N}. \)

Now suppose \(F(P)>F(Q)\). Since both scores are integers, \(F(P)-F(Q)\ge 1\), and therefore

\( S_q(P)-S_q(Q) >\frac{F(P)-F(Q)}{N}-\frac{1}{2N} \ge\frac{1}{2N}>0. \)

Thus every strict finite-profile score comparison is preserved. In particular, every maximizer of \(S_q\) maximizes \(F\): otherwise, a trichotomy with larger \(F\)-score would also have larger \(S_q\)-score. Rounding may distinguish original ties, which is harmless because no tie-breaking among original maximizers is required.

Choosing the smallest power of two above \(8NC\tau\) gives

\( \log D=O(\log(N+1)+\log m+\log(\tau+1)). \)

Each mass has an \(O(\log D)\)-bit numerator and denominator. The construction consequently has polynomial running time and output size. This proves NP-hardness even under the stricter dyadic interpretation.

3. Conclusion

The supplied finite-profile hardness result transfers to the support-based rational-mass formulation. Distinct support types, positive masses, normalization, and polynomial bit length are all preserved by the reduction. The integer-weighted tournament hardness results are unnecessary for this conclusion.

Hence computing a maximizing trichotomy is NP-hard; a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

2. Construction of dyadic masses

Here \(m\ge3\), so \(C=\binom{m}{2}\ge3\). Together with \(N,\tau\ge1\), the condition \(D>8NC\tau\) implies \(D>N\).

3. Rounding-error bound

For \(t<\tau\), rounding downward gives
\[ 0\le p_t-q_t<1/D. \]
The final coordinate absorbs exactly the accumulated deficit. Therefore the stated identity
\[ \sum_t|q_t-p_t|=2\sum_{t<\tau}(p_t-q_t) \]
is correct, including when \(\tau=1\). Its subsequent upper bounds are valid.

For any feasible \(P\), each unordered pair contributes at most one summand to \(g_t(P)\), and each summand has absolute value at most one. Hence \(|g_t(P)|\le C\). The triangle inequality then yields the claimed uniform perturbation bound:
\[ \left|S_q(P)-F(P)/N\right| <2C\tau/D<1/(4N). \]
The constants and strict inequalities are correct.

Thus every strict original comparison is preserved.

The conclusion about maximizers follows: an output with suboptimal \(F\)-score has a competitor with strictly larger \(F\)-score, hence strictly larger \(S_q\)-score. It cannot maximize \(S_q\).

Original ties may be broken, but this does not invalidate the reduction because the task permits any original maximizer.
