Result

The construction problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time for every fixed parameter choice in the question. In fact, every valid input admits the required output.

The proof uses a newer result: exact stable \(k\)-lotteries have distortion strictly below \(3\) whenever \(k>1\). The remaining issue under binary rational encoding is constructive: approximate the stable lottery while guaranteeing containment in its exact support. We give an explicit polynomial-time algorithm for that step.

The multisets constructed below have polynomial size in \(m\). Their size need not be constant.

1. Distortion results used

We use the following established results.

First, an exact stable \(k\)-lottery has distortion at most

\[ B_k=1+2\lambda_k, \qquad \lambda_k=\frac{1+(k-1)(k+1)^{-1/(k-1)}}{k}. \]

Since \(k>1\), we have \(\lambda_k<1\), hence \(B_k<3\). The theorem uses independent sampling and uniform tie-breaking among candidate copies, exactly as in the question. See [Cai–Charikar–Hastings–Ramakrishnan–Wang–Ye, Theorem 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08259v1).

Second, we use biased-metric reduction and two maximal-lottery bounds. Exact maximal lotteries have distortion at most \(3\). On a biased metric that is not strongly \((\alpha,\theta)\)-consistent, their distortion is at most \(3-2\alpha(\theta-1/2)\). The latter follows by applying the representative-approximation bound to an exact lottery and taking the approximation parameter to zero. These statements follow from [Cai–Gao–Ramakrishnan–Wang, §2.3, Theorem 5.1, and Lemma 7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08871v2).

We spell out the consistency notion when applying these bounds.

These finite-electorate results apply to the present model without an algorithmic expansion. Indeed, choose a common denominator \(N\) of the masses and conceptually replace type \(t\) by \(N\mu_t\) voters. All game payoffs remain identical. Any pseudometric on \(T\sqcup C\) lifts to those voters by placing copies of each type at the same point, preserving social costs. Thus the finite-electorate inequalities imply the required weighted inequalities. The algorithm below never constructs these copies.

2. Constructing the pruning set

The required independent set \(S\) can be constructed directly in polynomial time.

Here is a recursive algorithm for an arbitrary nonempty directed graph.

Choose a vertex \(v\), delete \(v\) and its out-neighbors, and recursively obtain an independent set \(Q\) reaching every remaining vertex within two edges. If the remaining graph is empty, set \(Q=\varnothing\).

If some \(q\in Q\) has an edge \(q\to v\), return \(Q\). Otherwise return \(Q\cup\{v\}\).

To verify the algorithm:

  • In the first case, every deleted out-neighbor \(u\) is reached by \(q\to v\to u\), and \(v\) is reached directly.
  • In the second case, there are no edges between \(v\) and \(Q\): outgoing edges were deleted, and the case assumption excludes incoming edges. Every deleted out-neighbor is reached directly from \(v\).
  • Vertices remaining in the recursive graph retain their previously established paths.

The returned set is nonempty, independent, and has the required reachability property. Applying this algorithm to the threshold graph gives \(S\) using polynomially many operations.

We also need a bound on the cost of pruning. If \(s_{uv}=p>0\), consistency gives

\[ p\,d(u,v) \leq 2\sum_{t:u\succ_t v}\mu_t d(t,v) \leq 2\operatorname{SC}(v). \]

For types preferring \(u\), the cost of \(u\) is at most that of \(v\); for other types, triangle inequality adds at most \(d(u,v)\). Consequently,

\[ \operatorname{SC}(u) \leq \operatorname{SC}(v)+(1-p)d(u,v) \leq \left(\frac2p-1\right)\operatorname{SC}(v). \]

Write \(K=2/\theta-1\). Following a path of at most two threshold edges from \(S\) to an optimal candidate therefore proves

\[ \min_{a\in S}\operatorname{SC}(a) \leq K^2\min_{b\in C}\operatorname{SC}(b). \tag{1} \]

This inequality also holds when the optimal cost is zero.

3. A convex formulation of exact stable lotteries

Fix any nonempty candidate set \(V\), and put \(c=1/(k+1)\). For \(P\in\Delta(V)\), define

\[ q_{ta}(P)=\sum_{b\in V:a\succ_t b}P_b, \qquad f_a(P)=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbb E_{A\sim P^k}[w_t(a,A)]. \]

Conditioning on the number of copies of \(a\) gives the polynomial formula

\[ f_a(P) = \sum_t\mu_t \sum_{j=0}^k \binom kj \frac{P_a^j q_{ta}(P)^{k-j}}{j+1} = \sum_t\mu_t\int_0^1 \bigl(q_{ta}(P)+uP_a\bigr)^k\,du. \tag{2} \]

Every \(f_a\) is convex on the nonnegative orthant: its integrand is a convex power of a nonnegative linear function. Its partial derivatives on the simplex lie in \([0,k]\). In particular,

\[ |f_a(P)-f_a(P')|\leq k\|P-P'\|_1. \tag{3} \]

A telescoping calculation along each ranking proves, throughout the nonnegative orthant,

\[ \sum_a P_a f_a(P) = \frac{(\sum_a P_a)^{k+1}}{k+1}. \tag{4} \]

Thus, on the simplex,

\[ \sum_aP_af_a(P)=c. \tag{5} \]

Existence and equilibrium characterization

Let \(r_a(P)=(f_a(P)-c)_+\), let \(r(P)=\sum_a r_a(P)\), and consider the continuous map

\[ \Phi_a(P)=\frac{P_a+r_a(P)}{1+r(P)}. \]

It maps the compact convex simplex into itself, so Brouwer’s theorem supplies a fixed point \(Q\).

If \(r(Q)>0\), the fixed-point equations imply, for every \(a\) with \(Q_a>0\),

\[ f_a(Q)=c+r(Q)Q_a. \]

This contradicts (5). Hence

\[ f_a(Q)\leq c\quad\text{for all }a. \tag{6} \]

Moreover, such a \(Q\) is Alice’s strategy in a pure-strategy equilibrium, as required by the question.

To see this, differentiate (4) and evaluate at \(Q\):

\[ \sum_aQ_a\,\partial_b f_a(Q)=1-f_b(Q). \]

The convex function \(g(P)=\sum_aQ_af_a(P)\) satisfies \(g(Q)=c\). Its supporting-hyperplane inequality gives, for every \(P\in\Delta(V)\),

\[ g(P) \geq c+\sum_b(1-f_b(Q))(P_b-Q_b) = c+c-\sum_bP_bf_b(Q) \geq c. \]

Meanwhile, (6) implies that Bob’s payoff against \(Q\) is at most \(c\), for every Bob strategy. Thus \((Q,Q)\) is a saddle point.

Conversely, any equilibrium has value \(c\), by comparison with this saddle point. Bob’s best response is a pure candidate, so Alice’s equilibrium strategy necessarily satisfies (6).

4. Quantitative approximation and exact support containment

The following estimate is the key to avoiding exact algebraic equilibrium computation.

Define

\[ a_k=\frac{1}{4^k(k+1)}. \]

For nonnegative \(x,y\) and integer \(k\geq2\),

\[ \frac{x^k+y^k}{2} - \left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^k \geq 2^{-k}|x-y|^k. \tag{7} \]

For completeness, with \(y=x+d\), the left side is nondecreasing in \(x\), by convexity of the \((k-1)\)-st power. At \(x=0\), it equals \((1/2-2^{-k})d^k\), which is at least \(2^{-k}d^k\).

Also,

\[ \int_0^1|z+u d|^k\,du \geq \frac{|d|^k}{2^k(k+1)}; \]

the minimum over \(z\) occurs when the interval is centered at zero. Applying these two inequalities to (2) yields

\[ f_a\!\left(\frac{P+P'}2\right) \leq \frac{f_a(P)+f_a(P')}{2} -a_k|P_a-P'_a|^k. \tag{8} \]

Let \(Q\) be exact stable, and suppose \(X\in\Delta(V)\) satisfies

\[ \max_a f_a(X)\leq c+\delta. \]

Multiply (8) by \((X_a+Q_a)/2\), sum over \(a\), and use (5). Since \(X_a+Q_a\geq|X_a-Q_a|\), we obtain

\[ \sum_a|X_a-Q_a|^{k+1}\leq\frac{\delta}{a_k}. \]

In particular,

\[ \|X-Q\|_\infty\leq \left(\frac{\delta}{a_k}\right)^{1/(k+1)}. \tag{9} \]

Taking \(\delta=0\) also proves uniqueness of the exact stable lottery for \(k\geq2\).

Support-preserving rounding

Let \(0<h\leq1/2\), and suppose \(|V|\leq m\). Set

\[ e=\frac{h^2}{8m}, \qquad \delta=a_ke^{k+1}. \tag{10} \]

Find a rational \(X\) satisfying the preceding approximate inequalities. By (9), \(\|X-Q\|_\infty\leq e\).

Retain precisely the coordinates satisfying

\[ X_a>e(1+1/h), \]

and normalize their masses. Call the resulting lottery \(H\), and let \(Z\) be the retained mass.

Every retained coordinate satisfies

\[ Q_a\geq X_a-e>e/h>0. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{supp}(H)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(Q). \tag{11} \]

Furthermore,

\[ Z\geq1-me(1+1/h)\geq1-h/4>0. \]

For retained coordinates, \(X_a\leq(1+h)Q_a\), and hence

\[ H_a \leq \frac{1+h}{1-h/4}Q_a \leq (1+2h)Q_a. \tag{12} \]

Finally,

\[ \|H-Q\|_1 \leq \|X-Q\|_1+\|H-X\|_1 \leq me+2(1-Z) \leq \frac{5h}{8} <h. \tag{13} \]

Equations (3), (11), and (13) show that \(H\) is a \(kh\)-RepApx stable lottery. Equation (12) gives the additional distortion control we need.

5. Computing the approximation in polynomial time

Here is an explicit rational algorithm for the \(X\) used above.

Let

\[ F(P)=\max_a f_a(P), \qquad G^2=k^2m. \]

The function \(F\) is convex, its minimum is \(c\), and every gradient of an active polynomial \(f_a\) has squared Euclidean norm at most \(G^2\).

Use a rational step size and integers

\[ \zeta=\frac{\delta}{4G^2}, \qquad J=\left\lceil\frac8{\zeta\delta}\right\rceil, \qquad B=\left\lceil\frac{16m}{\zeta\delta}\right\rceil. \tag{14} \]

Start at a simplex vertex. At each iteration:

  • Choose an index maximizing \(f_a(X)\), and use \(\nabla f_a(X)\) as a subgradient of \(F\).
  • Take a step of length \(\zeta\) and project onto the simplex.
  • Round the projection to the simplex grid with denominator \(B\).

The last operation is implemented by flooring all \(B\)-scaled coordinates and distributing the remaining units among distinct coordinates. Its Euclidean error is at most \(\xi=m/B\).

For the exact optimizer \(Q\), projection and convexity give

\[ \|X^{j+1}-Q\|_2^2 \leq \|X^j-Q\|_2^2 -2\zeta(F(X^j)-c) +\zeta^2G^2+4\xi. \]

The rounding term is valid because the simplex diameter is \(\sqrt2\) and \(\xi\leq1\). Summing over \(J\) iterations gives

\[ \frac1J\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}(F(X^j)-c) \leq \frac1{\zeta J}+\frac{\zeta G^2}{2}+\frac{2\xi}{\zeta} \leq \frac{3\delta}{8}. \]

Therefore the best iterate satisfies \(F(X)\leq c+\delta\).

All steps use rational arithmetic. Simplex projection is computed by sorting and solving the normalization equation for coordinates of the form \(\max\{z_a-\lambda,0\}\). Each iterate has denominator \(B\); consequently, intermediate bit lengths stay polynomial in \(L\), \(k\), and \(\log B\).

After support-preserving rounding, write \(X_a=n_a/B\). The resulting \(H\) is uniform on the multiset containing \(n_a\) copies of each retained candidate. Its size is at most \(B\).

For fixed \(k\) and \(h\), (10)–(14) give

\[ J=O(k^2m\delta^{-2}), \qquad B=O(k^2m^2\delta^{-2}), \]

where

\[ \delta^{-2} = 16^k(k+1)^2 \left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{2k+2}. \tag{15} \]

Thus both the computation and the explicit multiset output have polynomial size.

6. An exact mixture with a uniform distortion margin

Choose a rational constant \(\overline B\) satisfying

\[ B_k<\overline B<3, \qquad g=3-\overline B>0. \]

Such a constant can be precomputed for fixed \(k\), since \(B_k\) is algebraic.

Let \(Q_1\) be an exact maximal lottery on \(C\), and let \(Q_2\) be the exact stable \(k\)-lottery on the constructed \(S\).

By the stable-lottery distortion theorem and (1), for every consistent metric,

\[ \operatorname{SC}(Q_2) \leq G_0\min_{b\in C}\operatorname{SC}(b), \qquad G_0=\overline B K^2. \tag{16} \]

The case \(\theta<1\)

Set

\[ \alpha=\frac{g}{16\overline B}, \qquad \beta=\theta-\frac12, \qquad d_0=2\alpha\beta. \]

In the biased-metric reduction, choose an optimal candidate \(b\) and nonnegative parameters \(x_a\), with \(x_b=0\). Write \(R=2\operatorname{SC}(b)\). Strong \((\alpha,\theta)\)-consistency means

\[ s_{uv}\geq\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_u-x_v\leq\alpha R. \]

A path of at most two threshold edges from some \(j\in S\) to \(b\) then gives \(x_j\leq2\alpha R\). The biased-metric definition gives \(\operatorname{SC}(j)\leq\operatorname{SC}(b)+x_j\). Therefore,

\[ \min_{j\in S}\operatorname{SC}(j) \leq (1+4\alpha)\operatorname{SC}(b). \]

Applying the stable-lottery bound on \(S\), the cost ratio of \(Q_2\) is at most

\[ \overline B(1+4\alpha) = 3-\frac{3g}{4}. \tag{17} \]

On biased metrics failing this consistency condition, the maximal-lottery bound instead gives cost ratio at most \(3-d_0\) for \(Q_1\).

Choose rational constants

\[ q=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac{d_0}{2(G_0+3)}\right\}, \qquad \eta=1-q, \qquad \gamma=\min\left\{\frac{d_0}{2},\frac{3qg}{4}\right\}>0. \tag{18} \]

For \(D_0=\eta Q_1+(1-\eta)Q_2\), strongly consistent biased metrics give

\[ \frac{\operatorname{SC}(D_0)}{\operatorname{SC}(b)} \leq (1-q)3+q\left(3-\frac{3g}{4}\right) \leq 3-\gamma. \]

For the remaining biased metrics, (16) gives

\[ \frac{\operatorname{SC}(D_0)}{\operatorname{SC}(b)} \leq (1-q)(3-d_0)+qG_0 \leq 3-d_0+q(G_0+3) \leq 3-\gamma. \]

Biased-metric reduction therefore proves this bound for every consistent metric with positive optimal cost.

The case \(\theta=1\)

Here \(K=1\), so (16) bounds \(Q_2\) globally by \(\overline B\). Choose

\[ \eta=\frac12, \qquad \gamma=\frac g2. \]

Then \(D_0\) again has distortion at most \(3-\gamma\).

Thus, in both cases, we have rational constants \(\eta\in(0,1)\) and \(\gamma>0\) depending only on the fixed parameters.

7. Constructing the two required multisets

Choose the positive rational constant

\[ h=\min\left\{ \frac12,\frac{\varepsilon_1^2}{2}, \frac{\varepsilon_2}{k},\frac{\gamma}{12} \right\}. \tag{19} \]

The maximal-lottery multiset

Compute a rational exact maximal lottery \(Q_1\) by linear programming. Its constraints have \(m\) variables and rational coefficients of polynomial input bit length. Existence is the standard maximal-lottery existence theorem; it also follows from the argument in Section 3 with \(k=1\).

Set \(B_1=\lceil m/h\rceil\), and put \(\lfloor B_1Q_{1a}\rfloor\) copies of \(a\) in \(M_1\). Let \(H_1=U(M_1)\).

The unnormalized retained mass \(Z_1\) satisfies \(Z_1\geq1-h>0\). Therefore \(M_1\) is nonempty and

\[ \operatorname{supp}(H_1)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(Q_1), \qquad H_{1a}\leq\frac{Q_{1a}}{1-h}\leq(1+2h)Q_{1a}. \]

Moreover, \(\|H_1-Q_1\|_1\leq2h\). Since all \(s_{ab}\) belong to \([0,1]\),

\[ \sum_b s_{ab}H_{1b} \leq \frac12+2h \leq \frac12+\varepsilon_1^2. \]

Thus \(H_1\) is the required representative approximate maximal lottery.

The stable-lottery multiset

Apply Sections 4–5 to \(V=S\), using (19), and obtain \(M_2\) and \(H_2=U(M_2)\).

Those sections prove

\[ \operatorname{supp}(H_2)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(Q_2), \qquad H_{2a}\leq(1+2h)Q_{2a}, \]

and

\[ f_a(H_2)\leq\frac1{k+1}+kh \leq\frac1{k+1}+\varepsilon_2. \]

Hence \(H_2\) satisfies both representative stable-lottery requirements.

Distortion of the returned mixture

Coordinatewise,

\[ \eta H_1+(1-\eta)H_2 \leq (1+2h)\bigl(\eta Q_1+(1-\eta)Q_2\bigr). \]

Social costs are nonnegative. Consequently, on every consistent metric with positive optimal cost, the returned mixture has cost ratio at most

\[ (1+2h)(3-\gamma) \leq 3-\gamma+6h \leq 3-\frac{\gamma}{2} <3. \tag{20} \]

The zero-denominator convention causes no exception. Add \(\xi\) times the discrete metric to any given pseudometric. This preserves ranking consistency and makes every candidate’s social cost positive. Applying (20) and taking \(\xi\) to zero shows that zero optimal cost forces zero expected cost. The prescribed ratio is then \(1\).

Thus the requested strict inequality holds.

8. Complexity and parameter dependence

Pairwise masses, the threshold graph, and \(S\) are computed directly from the support list. The maximal lottery is obtained by rational linear programming. The stable approximation uses (14), with explicit output size bounded by (15).

For fixed \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,k,\theta\), the resulting bit complexity is bounded by

\[ m^{\,2k+O(1)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). \]

The hidden constants depend on those fixed parameters through \(\overline B,\gamma,h\). Explicitly,

\[ |M_1|\leq\left\lceil\frac m h\right\rceil, \qquad |M_2| = O\!\left( k^2m^2\,16^k(k+1)^2 \left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{2k+2} \right). \]

No step depends on the common-denominator electorate size.

This establishes deterministic polynomial-time solvability under binary rational support encoding. The proof does not establish fixed-parameter tractability when \(k\) varies.
