The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational parameters \(\mu,p_1,p_2\) with \(p_1<1/2<p_2\), derive \(a,b\) as in the paper. An atomless unit population has signal types \(L,H\). For each state \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\), an adversary chooses a probability law \(\rho_\omega\) over the mass \(\nu\in[0,1]\) of type \(H\), subject to \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_0}[\nu]=a\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_1}[\nu]=b\). The decision maker observes \(\nu\) and chooses a measurable rule \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) to minimize \(\sup_{\rho_0,\rho_1}\bigl(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)-\mu\int f\,d\rho_1-(1-\mu)\int(1-f)\,d\rho_0\bigr)\), where \(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)\) is the accuracy of a decision maker who knows both laws. Find an optimal rule and the minimum regret.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

An atomless population has total mass \(1\), with truthful signal types \(L,H\), and the state \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\) has prior \(\Pr(\omega=1)=\mu\). Input parameters are binary rationals satisfying \(0<p_1<\tfrac12<p_2<1\) and \(p_1<\mu<p_2\); these are the posterior probabilities of state \(1\) after signals \(L,H\), respectively.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) denotes support size, not the number of possible types. Here the input marginal society has masses \(1-q,q\), where \(q=(\mu-p_1)/(p_2-p_1)\), and \(\tau=2\); random realized societies may have arbitrary real masses.
Define \(a=(1-p_2)(\mu-p_1)/((1-\mu)(p_2-p_1))\) and \(b=p_2(\mu-p_1)/(\mu(p_2-p_1))\), so \(0<a<b<1\). Conditional on each state, the adversary may choose any Borel probability law \(\rho_\omega\) on the observed high-signal mass \(\nu\in[0,1]\), subject only to \(\int\nu\,d\rho_0=a\) and \(\int\nu\,d\rho_1=b\). These laws may have atoms.
A rule is any Borel measurable \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(f(\nu)\) is the probability of guessing state \(1\); accuracy is \(P(f;\rho_0,\rho_1)=\mu\int f\,d\rho_1+(1-\mu)\int(1-f)\,d\rho_0\).
The informed benchmark observes the same \(\nu\) and knows both laws: \(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)=\sup_g P(g;\rho_0,\rho_1)\), over the same class of rules. Define \(R(f)=\sup_{\rho_0,\rho_1}[P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)-P(f;\rho_0,\rho_1)]\) over admissible laws and \(R_* =\inf_f R(f)\). Ties in the benchmark may be resolved arbitrarily.

Is deciding \(R_*\le r\), for a binary rational threshold \(r\in[0,1]\), in P or NP-hard? What is \(R_*\), and which rules attain it? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(m=2\) is the number of states and \(L\) is the total input bit length, including \(r\).

For a finite population of \(n\) agents, when \(1/n\le a<b\le(n-1)/n\), the unique optimal rule on \(\{0,1/n,\ldots,1\}\) is \(f(\nu)=\nu\), with minimum regret \(1-(1-\mu)(1-a)-\mu b\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.03667v2).
The verified extension to continuously many possible private signals still uses finitely many agents; no result settling the exact population-mass problem above was located. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.03667v2).

  • Required 0 < p1 < 1/2 < p2 < 1 and p1 < μ < p2: posterior consistency requires the prior between the posteriors, and the anchor's interior-mean condition excludes fully revealing signals and zero-probability signal types.
  • Specified Borel probability laws and Borel measurable rules, allowing atomic laws despite the atomless population.
  • Defined the informed benchmark over rules observing exactly the same aggregate mass, with arbitrary tie-breaking.
  • Applied the rational-support convention to the input marginal society, whose masses are determined by the parameters; retained arbitrary real masses in adversarial realizations as required by the continuous question.
  • Used an infimum for the optimal value without assuming attainment, and added a rational regret threshold to make the two-sided complexity question precise.
  • Defined m as the number of states, fixed at two, and L as the total binary input length.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Its value is
\[ R_*=(1-\mu)a+\mu(1-b) =p_1(1-q)+(1-p_2)q =\frac{p_1(p_2-\mu)+(1-p_2)(\mu-p_1)}{p_2-p_1}. \]
The unique optimal Borel rule is \(f(\nu)=\nu\) at every \(\nu\in[0,1]\).

The proof below uses the finite-population theorem supplied in the question. The extension to arbitrary real population masses requires no continuity assumption on the rule.

1. The identity rule attains the claimed value

Write \(C=(1-\mu)a+\mu(1-b)\), and let \(f_{\mathrm{id}}(\nu)=\nu\). For every admissible pair of laws, the moment constraints give
\[ P(f_{\mathrm{id}};\rho_0,\rho_1) =\mu b+(1-\mu)(1-a)=1-C. \]
Every benchmark accuracy is at most \(1\), so \(R(f_{\mathrm{id}})\le C\).

For the reverse inequality, take \(\rho_0=\delta_a\) and \(\rho_1=\delta_b\), where \(\delta_x\) denotes the probability measure concentrated at \(x\). These laws satisfy the moment constraints. Because \(a<b\), their supports are disjoint, and the Borel rule \(g(\nu)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\nu=b\}}\) achieves accuracy \(1\). Consequently,
\[ R(f_{\mathrm{id}})=C,\qquad R_*\le C. \]

2. A finite restriction proves the matching lower bound

For a positive integer \(n\), define \(G_n=\{0,1/n,\ldots,1\}\). Let \(R_n(h)\) denote the regret of a rule \(h:G_n\to[0,1]\) when the adversary is restricted to laws supported on \(G_n\), with means \(a,b\).

For any such laws, the benchmark over Borel rules on \([0,1]\) equals the benchmark over rules on \(G_n\): restriction preserves accuracy, and any rule on the finite set extends to a Borel rule by setting it to zero outside that set. Since every grid-supported admissible law is also admissible in the population-mass problem, every Borel \(f\) satisfies
\[ R(f)\ge R_n(f|_{G_n}). \]

The supplied finite-population theorem states that, whenever \(1/n\le a<b\le1-1/n\), the minimum of \(R_n\) is \(C\), attained uniquely by \(h(\nu)=\nu\). This is [Theorem 4.3 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.03667v2#S4.Thm3).

Because \(a>0\) and \(b<1\), an integer satisfying \(n\ge\max\{3,1/a,1/(1-b)\}\) exists. Its grid therefore gives \(R(f)\ge C\) for every Borel rule. Together with Section 1, this proves \(R_*=C\).

3. Uniqueness holds pointwise, including at irrational masses

Suppose \(f\) is optimal, so \(R(f)=C\).

Rational masses

Fix a rational \(x\in[0,1]\), and express it with positive integer denominator \(d\). Choose a multiple \(n\) of \(d\) large enough that \(1/n\le a<b\le1-1/n\). Then \(x\in G_n\), and
\[ C\le R_n(f|_{G_n})\le R(f)=C. \]
Thus \(f|_{G_n}\) is optimal for the finite problem. Its stated uniqueness implies \(f(x)=x\). This holds for every rational \(x\in[0,1]\).

Irrational masses

Fix an irrational \(x\in[0,1]\). We show that either strict deviation from \(f(x)=x\) gives regret exceeding \(C\).

Suppose first that \(f(x)>x\). Choose a rational \(y\in[0,1]\) strictly on the opposite side of \(a\) from \(x\). Define
\[ \lambda=\frac{a-y}{x-y},\qquad \rho_0=\lambda\delta_x+(1-\lambda)\delta_y. \]
The choice of \(y\) ensures \(0<\lambda<1\), and this law has mean \(a\).

Choose rational points \(u,v\), both different from \(y\), satisfying \(0<u<b<v<1\), and put
\[ \rho_1=\frac{v-b}{v-u}\delta_u+\frac{b-u}{v-u}\delta_v. \]
This law has mean \(b\). Its support is disjoint from that of \(\rho_0\), because \(x\) is irrational and \(u,v\ne y\). Hence the benchmark achieves accuracy \(1\).

We already know that \(f\) equals the identity at \(y,u,v\). Its error under these laws is therefore
\[ 1-P(f;\rho_0,\rho_1) =C+(1-\mu)\lambda\bigl(f(x)-x\bigr)>C. \]
This contradicts optimality.

If instead \(f(x)<x\), choose rational \(y\) strictly on the opposite side of \(b\) from \(x\), and define
\[ \lambda=\frac{b-y}{x-y},\qquad \rho_1=\lambda\delta_x+(1-\lambda)\delta_y. \]
Choose rational \(u,v\ne y\) with \(0<u<a<v<1\), and define
\[ \rho_0=\frac{v-a}{v-u}\delta_u+\frac{a-u}{v-u}\delta_v. \]
Again the laws have the required means and disjoint supports. Since \(f\) equals the identity at their rational support points,
\[ 1-P(f;\rho_0,\rho_1) =C+\mu\lambda\bigl(x-f(x)\bigr)>C, \]
another contradiction.

Thus \(f(x)=x\) also at every irrational mass. The optimal rule is unique pointwise; allowing atoms makes even a deviation at one point consequential.

4. Exact decision algorithm and running time

Using the definitions of \(a,b,q\),
\[ C=\mu+(1-2p_2)q =p_1(1-q)+(1-p_2)q =\frac{p_1(p_2-\mu)+(1-p_2)(\mu-p_1)}{p_2-p_1}. \]
Because \(p_2-p_1>0\), decide \(R_*\le r\) by the exact rational comparison
\[ p_1(p_2-\mu)+(1-p_2)(\mu-p_1)\le r(p_2-p_1). \]

This takes a constant number of rational arithmetic operations. Keeping unreduced fractions, every intermediate integer has \(O(L)\) bits. Schoolbook integer arithmetic therefore gives \(O(L^2)\) bit time and \(O(L)\) space. Computing the value has the same bounds; the optimal rule has the constant-size description \(f(\nu)=\nu\).

In the requested parameters, the bound is \(T(m=2,\tau=2,L)=O(L^2)\). The finite grids serve only in the proof and are never enumerated by the algorithm. The result concerns the stated two-state, two-type problem.
