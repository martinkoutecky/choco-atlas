The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(T_M,T_W,T_D\) with rational masses summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict cyclic rankings of the next side’s types, decide whether there is a nonnegative mass assignment \(x_{ijk}\) to triples whose three marginals equal the given type masses and has no blocking type triple. A triple \((i,j,k)\) blocks when positive mass of type \(i\) is assigned to women-types it ranks below \(j\), positive mass of type \(j\) is assigned to dog-types it ranks below \(k\), and positive mass of type \(k\) is assigned to men-types it ranks below \(i\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J,K\) be nonempty, disjoint finite sets of types in three roles. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|+|K|\) positive-mass types, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible preference orders. Masses satisfy \(\sum_i p_i=\sum_j q_j=\sum_k r_k=1\).
Each type has a strict complete ranking of the next role cyclically: \(\succ_i\) over \(J\), \(\succ_j\) over \(K\), and \(\succ_k\) over \(I\). Preferences depend only on that next-role partner.
A family allocation is a real array \(x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{I\times J\times K}\) satisfying \(\sum_{j,k}x_{ijk}=p_i\), \(\sum_{i,k}x_{ijk}=q_j\), and \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ijk}=r_k\); all mass must be assigned.
Define \(A_i(j)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}\sum_{k'}x_{ij'k'}\), \(A_j(k)=\sum_{k':\,k\succ_j k'}\sum_{i'}x_{i'jk'}\), and \(A_k(i)=\sum_{i':\,i\succ_k i'}\sum_{j'}x_{i'j'k}\).
An allocation is stable if every \((i,j,k)\in I\times J\times K\) satisfies \(A_i(j)=0\) or \(A_j(k)=0\) or \(A_k(i)=0\). The decision problem asks whether such an allocation exists.
Let \(m=\max\{|I|,|J|,|K|\}\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including rankings and masses; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous stable-family existence problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete complete cyclic instances with \(20\) agents per role can lack a weakly stable matching ([Lerner](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.10102)); deciding discrete existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete ([Lam–Plaxton](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~plaxton/pubs/2019/arxiv_1905.02844.pdf)).
For general hypergraphic preference systems, finding a fractional stable matching is \(\mathrm{PPAD}\)-complete ([Kintali et al.](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2009/041/download/)); this concerns a different search problem. No classification of the exact decision problem above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Excluded zero-mass types because the input convention lists only the positive-mass support.
  • Made rankings complete and preferences dependent only on the next-role partner, following the discrete cyclic model.
  • Specified real allocation entries and full assignment of all mass.
  • Allowed unequal role support sizes while retaining equal total role masses, as stipulated by the continuous question.
  • Defined m as the largest role support size, tau as the combined support size, and L as the total encoded input length.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). In fact, every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses admits a stable allocation.

The key ingredients are Scarf’s fractional domination theorem, completeness of the set of possible families, and equality of the three roles’ total masses.

1. The existence theorem used

We use the following form of Scarf’s lemma.

Let \(B\in\mathbb R_{\geq 0}^{n\times N}\) have no zero column, and let \(b\in\mathbb R_{>0}^{n}\). For each row \(v\), prescribe a strict ordering \(\triangleright_v\) of the columns \(e\) satisfying \(B_{ve}>0\). There exists \(z\in\mathbb R_{\geq 0}^{N}\) with \(Bz\leq b\) such that every column \(e\) is dominated at some row \(v\), meaning:

  • \(B_{ve}>0\);
  • \((Bz)_v=b_v\);
  • every column \(f\) with \(B_{vf}z_f>0\) satisfies \(f\unrhd_v e\), where \(\unrhd_v\) means equality or preference under \(\triangleright_v\).

This statement, including arbitrary positive real capacities \(b\), appears as Lemma 1 of [Biró–Csáji–Schlotter, *Stable Hypergraph Matching in Unimodular Hypergraphs*](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31.html).

2. Apply Scarf’s lemma to the family hypergraph

Set \(V=I\cup J\cup K\) and \(E=I\times J\times K\). Regard each \(e=(i,j,k)\) as a hyperedge containing its three types.

Let \(B\) be the incidence matrix: \(B_{ve}=1\) when \(v\in e\), and \(B_{ve}=0\) otherwise. Define the capacities by \(b_i=p_i\), \(b_j=q_j\), and \(b_k=r_k\).

For each type, refine its next-partner preferences to a strict ordering of all incident families:

  • At \(i\), place every family with next partner \(j\) above every family with next partner \(j'\) whenever \(j\succ_i j'\).
  • At \(j\), order families primarily by their \(K\)-partner.
  • At \(k\), order families primarily by their \(I\)-partner.

Break ties between families having the same next-role partner arbitrarily. These refinements exist because all sets are finite. They preserve every strict comparison in the input.

All hypotheses of Scarf’s lemma hold: \(B\) is nonnegative, each column has exactly three nonzero entries, every capacity is positive, and each row has a strict ordering of its incident columns. Moreover, the feasible set is nonempty, since it contains zero, and bounded, since every coordinate is bounded by the capacities of its incident types.

Consequently, there is a nonnegative family array \(z\) satisfying the marginal inequalities, such that every family is dominated at one of its types.

3. Every marginal inequality is an equality

Write \(t=\sum_{e\in E}z_e\) and \(d_v=b_v-\sum_{e\ni v}z_e\). Feasibility gives \(d_v\geq 0\).

Every family contains exactly one type from each role. Therefore, using the stipulated mass totals,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in I}d_i=\sum_{j\in J}d_j=\sum_{k\in K}d_k=1-t.\)

Suppose \(t<1\). Each of these three sums is positive, so there exist \(i\in I\), \(j\in J\), and \(k\in K\) with \(d_i>0\), \(d_j>0\), and \(d_k>0\).

The family \(e=(i,j,k)\) belongs to \(E\), because every triple is available. Scarf’s domination property requires this family to be dominated at one of its three types. But domination requires that type’s capacity constraint to hold with equality, whereas all three selected types have positive slack. This is a contradiction.

Thus \(t=1\). The displayed slack sums are now zero. Since every slack is nonnegative, every slack is zero. Hence \(z\) satisfies all the required marginal equalities.

4. The resulting allocation is stable in the stated sense

Set \(x=z\). Fix any triple \(e=(i,j,k)\). Scarf’s lemma supplies a type in this triple at which \(e\) is dominated.

If that type is \(i\), every family \(f\) containing \(i\) with \(x_f>0\) is ranked at least as highly as \(e\) in the refined ordering. By construction of that ordering, such a family cannot have a next-role partner strictly worse than \(j\) under \(\succ_i\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle A_i(j)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}\sum_{k'}x_{ij'k'}=0.\)

If the dominating type is \(j\), the same argument gives \(A_j(k)=0\). If it is \(k\), it gives \(A_k(i)=0\).

Every triple therefore satisfies exactly the required disjunction:

\(\displaystyle A_i(j)=0\quad\text{or}\quad A_j(k)=0\quad\text{or}\quad A_k(i)=0.\)

This proves existence of a stable family allocation.

5. Decision complexity and the discrete comparison

On the promised input domain, the decision algorithm simply returns YES. Its running time is \(O(L)\) if it reads the input, or \(O(1)\) if reading a promised-valid input is unnecessary. There is no additional dependence on \(m\) or \(\tau\). The proof uses a hypergraph with \(\tau\) vertices and \(|I||J||K|\leq m^3\) families.

The discrete results require an integral matching; our argument permits types to split their masses between families. The fractional search hardness cited in the question also remains compatible with this result: the decision algorithm needs no allocation as output. The proof establishes universal existence without establishing a polynomial-time construction algorithm.

That family cannot be dominated: domination must occur at an incident type whose capacity is saturated, whereas all three selected types have positive slack. This contradicts Scarf’s conclusion.

Therefore \(t=1\). Each role’s finite collection of nonnegative slacks has sum zero, so every individual slack vanishes.
