Result

The search problem is deterministically solvable in polynomial time in the binary input length. In particular, its running time can be polynomial in \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), rather than in \(1/\varepsilon\).

For multiplicative weights, a controlled rational implementation gives the following conservative bit-complexity bound. Define

\(\displaystyle e=\min\{\varepsilon,1/2\},\qquad \ell=\left\lceil\log_2(d_{\max}+1)\right\rceil,\qquad h=\left\lceil\log_2 m\right\rceil,\)

and

\(\displaystyle B=1+L+\ell+\left\lceil\log_2\left(\frac{\tau m s}{e}\right)\right\rceil.\)

Then weighted exponential multiplicative weights can produce the required lottery using

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\frac{mh}{e^2}\left[\tau s\ell B^2+B^3\right]\right)\)

bit operations with schoolbook multiplication and division. This is an upper bound, not a claim of optimality.

The distinction matters: this multiplicative-weights bound is polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\), while the polynomial-time classification for binary-encoded \(\varepsilon\) follows from a separate ellipsoid algorithm.

1. The comparison functions

Write \(\rho_d=\Pr[D=d]\), and put

\(\displaystyle \alpha=\mathbb E\!\left[\frac1{D+1}\right]=\sum_{d\in\operatorname{supp}(D)}\frac{\rho_d}{d+1}.\)

For a lottery \(p\), candidate \(c\), and type \(t\), define

\(\displaystyle b_{t,c}(p)=\sum_{j:\,c\succ_t j}p_j,\qquad z_c(p)=p_c.\)

Conditional on the challenger’s label being \(u\), one draw from \(p\) loses to the challenger with probability \(b_{t,c}(p)+up_c\). Independence therefore gives

\(\displaystyle f_c(p):=\sum_t\mu_t\Pr[c\succ_t p^D] =\sum_t\sum_d\mu_t\rho_d\int_0^1\bigl(b_{t,c}(p)+up_c\bigr)^d\,du.\)

This includes zero candidate probabilities and exactly implements the prescribed labels.

We need \(f_c(p)\leq\alpha+\varepsilon\) for every \(c\).

Convexity and a Lipschitz bound

For every positive integer \(d\), the function \(x\mapsto x^d\) is convex on \([0,1]\). Its argument in the integral is affine in \(p\) and belongs to \([0,1]\) on the simplex. Positive weighted sums and integration preserve convexity. Thus every \(f_c\) is continuous, differentiable, and convex on the simplex.

For an ambient coordinate \(p_j\), differentiating the displayed polynomial gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial f_c}{\partial p_j}(p) =\sum_{t,d}\mu_t\rho_d\,d\int_0^1 \bigl(b_{t,c}(p)+up_c\bigr)^{d-1}a_{t,c,j}(u)\,du, \)

where \(a_{t,c,j}(u)\) equals \(1\) if \(c\succ_t j\), equals \(u\) if \(j=c\), and equals \(0\) otherwise. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\partial f_c}{\partial p_j}(p)\leq d_{\max}.\)

Integrating this derivative bound along the segment between two lotteries proves

\(\displaystyle |f_c(p)-f_c(q)|\leq d_{\max}\|p-q\|_1.\)

The identity that fixes the benchmark

For every lottery \(p\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_c p_c f_c(p)=\alpha.\)

Indeed, conditional on a type and \(D=d\), sample the challenger from \(p\) as well. The challenger and the other \(d\) occurrences are then \(d+1\) independent, identically distributed labeled draws. Almost surely there is a unique best occurrence: ranking decides between different candidates, and labels decide between copies of the same candidate. Exchangeability makes the challenger best with probability \(1/(d+1)\). Averaging proves the identity.

In particular, \(\max_c f_c(p)\geq\alpha\) for every \(p\).

Existence of an exactly feasible lottery

For lotteries \(p,q\), let

\(\displaystyle F(p,q)=\sum_c q_c f_c(p).\)

Both domains are compact convex simplices. The function is continuous, convex in \(p\), and linear, hence concave, in \(q\). The convex minimax theorem applies:

\(\displaystyle \min_p\max_q F(p,q)=\max_q\min_p F(p,q).\)

For each \(q\), choosing \(p=q\) gives \(F(q,q)=\alpha\), so the right side is at most \(\alpha\). Conversely, for every \(p\),

\(\displaystyle \max_qF(p,q)\geq F(p,p)=\alpha.\)

Therefore the minimax value is exactly \(\alpha\). Continuity and compactness ensure that the minimum is attained. There exists a lottery \(p^\star\) with

\(\displaystyle f_c(p^\star)\leq\alpha\quad\text{for every }c.\)

These arguments also establish the weighted-society version of the structural facts used in [Shah’s existence proof and multiplicative-weights algorithm](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.29308v2#A2.SS2).

2. Values and gradients have polynomial bit complexity

Exact rational exponentiation is unsuitable: its output length can grow proportionally to the numerical value of \(d\). We instead evaluate a bounded arithmetic circuit with rounding.

For \(n\geq1\), define

\(\displaystyle H_n(a,b)=\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a^{n-1-j}b^j.\)

The integral in the comparison function is

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1(b+uz)^d\,du=H_{d+1}(b+z,b).\)

For \(z>0\), this follows by integrating and factoring the difference of powers. For \(z=0\), both sides equal \(b^d\). Thus the formula avoids division by \(z\).

A circuit of logarithmic size

Simultaneously compute

\(\displaystyle A_n=a^n,\qquad B_n=b^n,\qquad H_n(a,b).\)

The base case is

\(\displaystyle A_1=a,\qquad B_1=b,\qquad H_1=1.\)

From the triple for \(k\), compute

\(\displaystyle A_{2k}=A_k^2,\qquad B_{2k}=B_k^2,\qquad H_{2k}=\frac{A_k+B_k}{2}H_k.\)

For an odd index, also compute

\(\displaystyle A_{2k+1}=aA_{2k},\qquad B_{2k+1}=bB_{2k},\)

\(\displaystyle H_{2k+1} =\frac{2k}{2k+1}\,aH_{2k} +\frac1{2k+1}\,B_{2k}.\)

These identities follow directly by splitting the geometric sums. Recursing through the binary representation of \(n\) uses \(O(\log n)\) operations.

Every primal circuit value belongs to \([0,1]\) when \(a,b\in[0,1]\): the operations are products and convex combinations.

Gradients without large intermediate derivatives

Each primal circuit polynomial is homogeneous, with nonnegative coefficients summing to one. For such a polynomial \(G\) of positive degree \(r\), define its normalized partial derivatives by

\(\displaystyle E_a(G)=\frac1r\frac{\partial G}{\partial a},\qquad E_b(G)=\frac1r\frac{\partial G}{\partial b}.\)

They belong to \([0,1]\) on \([0,1]^2\): each monomial contributes its exponent divided by \(r\), multiplied by a monomial bounded by one. For constants, define the normalized derivatives to be zero.

For a product \(G=UV\), where the degrees are \(r\) and \(s\),

\(\displaystyle E_a(G)=\frac r{r+s}E_a(U)V+\frac s{r+s}UE_a(V), \)

and likewise for \(b\). For a convex combination of equal-degree polynomials, normalized derivatives combine with the same coefficients.

Thus automatic differentiation adds only a constant number of bounded operations per primal operation. The ordinary derivatives of \(H_{d+1}\) are recovered by multiplying its normalized derivatives by \(d\).

For \(H_{d+1}(a,b)\) with \(a=b_{t,c}(p)+p_c\) and \(b=b_{t,c}(p)\), its derivative with respect to a probability coordinate is:

  • \(\partial_aH_{d+1}\) for \(p_c\);
  • \(\partial_aH_{d+1}+\partial_bH_{d+1}\) for a candidate below \(c\);
  • zero for a candidate above \(c\).

This gives the required gradients.

Rounding precision

Round every bounded circuit value and rational coefficient downward to a dyadic grid with spacing \(u=2^{-b}\).

Because the circuit uses only a fixed collection of bounded products and convex combinations, there is an absolute constant \(C\) such that each gate’s error is at most \(C\) times the preceding maximum error plus \(Cu\). There are \(O(\ell)\) gates, including normalized differentiation. Hence final bounded-value errors are at most

\(\displaystyle C^{O(\ell)}u.\)

Recovering ordinary derivatives introduces a factor at most \(d_{\max}\). Rounding input probabilities introduces at most \(mu\) error in a ranking prefix. Finally, rounding and summing the \(\tau s\) weighted terms contributes only polynomially many further errors, with factors at most \(d_{\max}\).

It therefore suffices to use

\(\displaystyle b=O\!\left(\ell+\log\frac{\tau ms}{\delta}\right)\)

fractional bits to approximate every comparison value to absolute error \(\delta\), and any specified comparison gradient to coordinatewise error \(\delta\).

The integer degrees and circuit coefficients have \(O(\ell)\) bits. Input rational masses have at most \(L\) bits. Conversion of a rational query to this grid is polynomial in its encoding length and the requested precision.

All ranking prefixes are computed by scans. Computing all comparison values, and a gradient for one selected candidate, uses

\(\displaystyle O(\tau ms\ell)\)

fixed-precision arithmetic operations.

Thus values and gradients are computable in polynomial time in the input and query encoding lengths, \(\ell\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\). The construction remains valid when some candidate probabilities vanish.

3. Polynomial-time search via rational ellipsoids

We now prove polynomial time for binary-encoded accuracy.

Use \(n=m-1\) coordinates:

\(\displaystyle p(x)=\left(x_1,\ldots,x_n,1-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j\right).\)

The simplex becomes

\(\displaystyle \mathcal S=\left\{x:x_j\geq0,\ \sum_jx_j\leq1\right\}.\)

Define the convex inner feasible body

\(\displaystyle K=\left\{x\in\mathcal S: f_c(p(x))\leq\alpha+\frac e4\text{ for every }c\right\}.\)

We will preserve \(K\) with every cut, while accepting points with slightly greater slack.

A guaranteed interior ball

Let \(u\) be the uniform lottery and set

\(\displaystyle q=(1-\theta)p^\star+\theta u,\qquad \theta=e/16.\)

Every coordinate of \(q\) is at least \(e/(16m)\). Convexity and \(f_c(u)\leq1\) give

\(\displaystyle f_c(q)\leq(1-\theta)\alpha+\theta\leq\alpha+e/16.\)

Let \(x_q\) denote its first \(m-1\) coordinates, and put

\(\displaystyle r=\frac e{128m^2d_{\max}}.\)

For a perturbation \(v\) with \(\|v\|_2\leq r\), each of the first coordinates changes by at most \(r\), and the last probability changes by at most

\(\displaystyle \left|\sum_jv_j\right|\leq\sqrt n\,r\leq mr.\)

These bounds are smaller than \(e/(16m)\), so \(p(x_q+v)\) remains a lottery. Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle \|p(x_q+v)-q\|_1\leq2mr.\)

The Lipschitz bound implies

\(\displaystyle f_c(p(x_q+v)) \leq\alpha+\frac e{16}+2d_{\max}mr =\alpha+\frac e{16}+\frac e{64m} <\alpha+\frac e4.\)

Thus \(K\) contains a Euclidean ball of radius \(r\). Its center need not be known to the algorithm.

Also, \(\mathcal S\), hence \(K\), lies in the radius-two ball about the origin. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \log(2/r)=O\!\left(\log m+\ell+\log(1/e)\right).\)

A success-or-separation oracle

Given a rational query \(x\), first test membership in \(\mathcal S\) exactly. A violated simplex inequality supplies a rational central cut containing \(K\).

Otherwise, approximate every \(f_c(p(x))\) within \(e/32\), obtaining \(\widehat f_c(x)\).

If all approximations satisfy

\(\displaystyle \widehat f_c(x)\leq\alpha+e/2,\)

accept \(x\). Every true comparison value then satisfies

\(\displaystyle f_c(p(x))\leq\alpha+17e/32.\)

If the test fails, select a violating candidate \(c\). Its true value satisfies

\(\displaystyle f_c(p(x))>\alpha+15e/32.\)

Approximate the gradient of \(x\mapsto f_c(p(x))\) by a rational vector \(a\), with coordinatewise error at most \(e/(32m)\). Section 2 supplies this gradient: its \(j\)-th coordinate is the difference between the ambient derivatives for \(p_j\) and \(p_m\).

For any \(y\in K\), convexity gives

\(\displaystyle \nabla(f_c\circ p)(x)\cdot(y-x) \leq f_c(p(y))-f_c(p(x)) <-7e/32. \)

Because \(x,y\in\mathcal S\), we have \(\|y-x\|_1\leq n\). The gradient approximation changes this inner product by less than \(e/32\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle a\cdot(y-x)<-6e/32.\)

In particular, \(a\neq0\), and the rational central cut

\(\displaystyle a\cdot(y-x)\leq0\)

contains every point of \(K\).

This is an exact, valid cut obtained from approximate function evaluations.

Ellipsoid theorem and hypothesis checks

Use the standard rounded rational central-cut ellipsoid algorithm. Its relevant guarantee is:

If a convex body in dimension \(n\) lies in a known radius-\(R\) ball and contains a radius-\(r\) ball, and each query returns either success or a rational central cut containing the body, then success occurs after \(O(n^2(1+\log(R/r)))\) cuts. The rational implementation has polynomial bit complexity in the dimension, geometric encoding lengths, and cut encoding lengths.

The usual volume argument applies even when “success” permits points in a larger feasible set: before success, every cut preserves the inner body. Rounded rational ellipsoids preserve containment and retain a volume reduction of \(\exp(-\Omega(1/n))\) per cut. See [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver’s ellipsoid treatment](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4); a polynomial-time binary-oracle formulation is also stated in [Coester–Cosson, Appendix A](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol374-icalp2026/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2026.65/LIPIcs.ICALP.2026.65.html#A1).

Here:

  • \(K\) is closed and convex.
  • Its outer radius is at most \(2\).
  • Its inner radius is the explicitly encoded \(r\) above.
  • Every unsuccessful query produces a valid rational central cut.
  • Section 2 bounds the oracle’s precision and cut lengths polynomially.

The value \(\alpha\) can also be computed exactly with polynomial encoding length: a common denominator is a product of input probability denominators and the listed integers \(d+1\), whose total binary length is \(O(L)\).

The resulting algorithm has bit complexity

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,s,L,\ell,\log(1/e)\right).\)

For binary rational input, both \(\ell\) and \(\log(1/e)\) are \(O(L)\). This proves the polynomial-time classification.

Binary output probabilities

We can ensure even dyadic output. Let the accepted lottery be \(p\), and choose

\(\displaystyle b_{\mathrm{out}}=\left\lceil\log_2\frac{16md_{\max}}e\right\rceil.\)

Round its first \(m-1\) coordinates downward to multiples of \(2^{-b_{\mathrm{out}}}\), and assign the remaining mass to the last coordinate. The result \(\widetilde p\) is a lottery and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \|\widetilde p-p\|_1\leq2m\,2^{-b_{\mathrm{out}}}\leq\frac e{8d_{\max}}.\)

Therefore

\(\displaystyle f_c(\widetilde p) \leq\alpha+\frac{17e}{32}+\frac e8 =\alpha+\frac{21e}{32} <\alpha+\varepsilon. \)

The total output length is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(m[\log m+\ell+\log(1/e)]\right).\)

4. A rational multiplicative-weights implementation

A bit bound requires specifying how the real exponential updates are implemented. We give an implementation using cumulative approximate gains; this avoids accumulating errors in repeatedly rounded weights.

Set

\(\displaystyle \eta=e/4,\qquad N=\left\lceil\frac{32h}{e^2}\right\rceil,\qquad \delta=\xi=e/32.\)

At round \(r\), maintain dyadic cumulative gains

\(\displaystyle S_{r,c}=\sum_{j<r}\widehat g_{j,c}.\)

Define the mathematical reference distribution

\(\displaystyle q_r(c)=\frac{\exp(\eta S_{r,c})}{\sum_b\exp(\eta S_{r,b})}.\)

Compute a rational lottery \(p_r\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \|p_r-q_r\|_1\leq\xi.\)

Evaluate the actual comparison functions at this rational lottery, obtaining dyadic gains in \([0,1]\) with

\(\displaystyle |\widehat g_{r,c}-f_c(p_r)|\leq\delta.\)

Clipping an approximation to \([0,1]\) does not increase this error.

Regret calculation with the two approximation errors

For \(0\leq x\leq1\), the exponential series gives

\(\displaystyle e^x\leq1+x+x^2,\)

because the sum of its coefficients from degree two onward is less than one. Since \(\eta\leq1\), the standard exponential potential calculation therefore yields

\(\displaystyle \max_c\frac1N\sum_{r=1}^N\widehat g_{r,c} \leq \frac1N\sum_{r=1}^N\sum_c q_r(c)\widehat g_{r,c} +\frac{\ln m}{\eta N}+\eta. \)

For completeness, if \(W_r=\sum_c\exp(\eta S_{r,c})\), then

\(\displaystyle \log\frac{W_{r+1}}{W_r} =\log\sum_cq_r(c)e^{\eta\widehat g_{r,c}} \leq\eta\sum_cq_r(c)\widehat g_{r,c}+\eta^2. \)

Sum over rounds and use \(W_1=m\) and
\(W_{N+1}\geq\exp(\eta\sum_r\widehat g_{r,c})\) for each candidate.

The exact identity holds at \(p_r\), and hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_cq_r(c)\widehat g_{r,c} \leq\sum_cp_r(c)f_c(p_r)+\xi+\delta =\alpha+\xi+\delta. \)

The first error uses \(0\leq f_c(p_r)\leq1\) and the \(\ell_1\) bound between \(q_r\) and \(p_r\); the second uses the gain approximation bound.

Replacing approximate gains by true gains incurs another \(\delta\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \max_c\frac1N\sum_r f_c(p_r) \leq\alpha+\xi+2\delta+\frac{\ln m}{\eta N}+\eta. \)

Our choices give

\(\displaystyle \frac{\ln m}{\eta N}\leq e/8,\qquad \eta=e/4,\qquad \xi+2\delta=3e/32. \)

Therefore, for the exact rational average \(\overline p=N^{-1}\sum_rp_r\), convexity implies

\(\displaystyle \max_cf_c(\overline p) \leq\alpha+\frac{15e}{32}. \)

Applying the output rounding from Section 3 increases this by at most \(e/8\). The final dyadic lottery satisfies

\(\displaystyle \max_cf_c(\widetilde p)\leq\alpha+19e/32<\alpha+\varepsilon.\)

5. Multiplicative-weights bit accounting

The comparison evaluations require

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\ell+\log\frac{\tau ms}{e}\right)\)

fractional bits. Cumulative gains require only another \(O(\log N)\) integer bits. Multiplication by the rational learning rate adds at most \(O(L)\) bits. All these lengths are \(O(B)\).

It remains to justify the exponential evaluations without dependence on the numerical size of the cumulative exponents.

Computing the reference probabilities

Subtract the largest exponent. With

\(\displaystyle y_c=\eta\left(\max_bS_{r,b}-S_{r,c}\right),\)

the unnormalized weights are \(w_c=e^{-y_c}\), where \(y_c\geq0\) and at least one weight equals one.

Take

\(\displaystyle \zeta=\frac{\xi}{16m},\qquad T=\left\lceil\log_2(1/\zeta)\right\rceil. \)

If \(y_c\geq T\), replace its weight by zero. This introduces error at most \(\zeta\), since

\(\displaystyle e^{-y_c}\leq e^{-T}\leq2^{-T}\leq\zeta.\)

Otherwise \(0\leq y_c<T\). Compute \(e^{y_c}\) by a Taylor sum and then take its reciprocal. A sum of \(J=O(T+\log(1/\zeta))=O(B)\) terms suffices: the remainder is bounded by

\(\displaystyle e^T\frac{T^{J+1}}{(J+1)!},\)

which is at most a prescribed constant multiple of \(\zeta\) for sufficiently large absolute constants in \(J\).

This calculation needs only \(O(B)\) bits of precision. Indeed, Taylor terms are bounded by \(e^T\), and a rounding error in one term propagates to a later term by a factor at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{T^k}{k!}\leq e^T.\)

Consequently, rounding each operation at spacing \(u\) contributes at most \(O(J^2e^Tu)\) total error. Choosing \(O(\log(1/\zeta)+T+\log J)=O(B)\) fractional bits controls this error. The Taylor sum is at least one, so taking its reciprocal does not amplify absolute error. Input rounding is controlled by the same derivative bound \(e^T\).

Thus each weight is approximated within \(\zeta\) using \(O(B)\) arithmetic operations on \(O(B)\)-bit numbers. Preserve a maximal weight as exactly one.

If \(\widetilde w\) denotes the resulting weights, normalization changes the probability vector by at most

\(\displaystyle \left\|\frac{\widetilde w}{\sum_c\widetilde w_c} -\frac{w}{\sum_cw_c}\right\|_1 \leq 2m\zeta=\xi/8. \)

To verify this, write the difference using denominator \(\sum_cw_c\); the numerator error and denominator error each contribute at most \(m\zeta/(\sum_cw_c)\), and the denominator is at least one.

Finally, rounding the normalized vector downward in its first \(m-1\) coordinates and assigning the residual to the last coordinate gives dyadic \(p_r\). Using \(O(\log(m/\xi))\) fractional bits makes the additional \(\ell_1\) error at most \(\xi/4\). Hence \(\|p_r-q_r\|_1\leq\xi\), as required.

Total cost

Using schoolbook arithmetic, multiplication and fixed-precision division of \(O(B)\)-bit numbers cost \(O(B^2)\) bit operations.

Each round costs:

  • \(O(\tau ms\ell B^2)\) for comparison evaluations;
  • \(O(mB^3)\) for exponential approximations, normalization, and rounding;
  • \(O(mB)\) for cumulative-gain updates, absorbed by the preceding terms.

Averaging and final output rounding are also absorbed. With \(N=O(h/e^2)\), the total is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left( \frac{mh}{e^2} \left[\tau s\ell B^2+B^3\right] \right). \)

No expansion into a finite electorate is used. The running time depends on the listed types and their binary masses, and on the binary lengths of the draw counts.
