The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 3.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(k\), finitely many location types \(T\) with positions \(z_t\), a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and a common nondecreasing concave cost function \(c\) with \(c(0)=0\), construct a randomized \(k\)-facility mechanism \(\mathcal M(\mu,c)\) that is group-strategyproof against feasible mass transfers \(\delta_{tu}\ge 0\), and guarantees \(\mathbb E[\max_{t:\mu_t>0} c(\min_j|z_t-Y_j|)]\le 2\,\mathrm{MC}^*_{\mu}\) and \(U_t(\mu)\le\mathrm{MC}^*_{\mu}\) for every supported type \(t\), while characterising its weighted social-cost guarantee.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) distinct location types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each mass a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Locations \(z_t\) are binary rationals on the real line; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible reports.
Fix \(k\geq1\). The public common cost function \(c:\mathbb R_{\geq0}\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) is continuous, nondecreasing and concave, with \(c(0)=0\). A mechanism without payments maps every finitely supported reported society to a distribution over \(Y\in\mathbb R^k\); coincident facilities are allowed.
Write \(d(z,Y)=\min_{1\leq j\leq k}|z-Y_j|\), \(U_t(\nu)=\mathbb E_{Y\sim\mathcal M(\nu,c)}[c(d(z_t,Y))]\), and \(\mathrm{MC}^*_{\mu}=\min_{Y\in\mathbb R^k}\max_{t\in T}c(d(z_t,Y))\).
Define \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tU_t(\mu)\) and \(\mathrm{SC}^*_{\mu}=\min_{Y\in\mathbb R^k}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tc(d(z_t,Y))\).
A deviation chooses a finite set of rational report locations, possibly outside the original support, and rational transfers \(\delta_{tu}\geq0\) to locations \(u\neq z_t\), with \(r_t=\sum_u\delta_{tu}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tr_t>0\). The reported society is \(\nu=\mu-\sum_tr_t\mathbf e_{z_t}+\sum_{t,u}\delta_{tu}\mathbf e_u\), where \(\mathbf e_z\) is unit mass at \(z\); zero masses are omitted.
Weak group-strategyproofness means that every such deviation has some \(t\) with \(r_t>0\) satisfying \(U_t(\nu)\geq U_t(\mu)\); costs are evaluated at true locations, per unit mass, in expectation.
For computational claims, use rational piecewise-linear costs with \(m\) explicitly listed pieces, including the final unbounded piece; let \(L\) be the total input bit length and state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\). The existence question covers all continuous costs specified above.

Does a weakly group-strategyproof randomized mechanism exist for every such society and cost function with \(\mathbb E[\max_{t\in T}c(d(z_t,Y))]\leq2\mathrm{MC}^*_{\mu}\) and \(U_t(\mu)\leq\mathrm{MC}^*_{\mu}\) for every \(t\in T\), or are these requirements incompatible?
If such a mechanism exists, what worst-case weighted social-cost approximation guarantee does its construction provide relative to \(\mathrm{SC}^*_{\mu}\)?

For finite societies of \(n\geq k+1\) agents, Equal Cost is weakly group-strategyproof, gives maximum-cost factor \(2\), social-cost factor \(n\), and expected individual cost at most the optimal maximum cost. [Verified theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/1305.3333).
No result settling exactly this rational mass-transfer model was located in the literature search.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The requirements are compatible. A version of Equal Cost that depends only on the reported support is weakly group-strategyproof under the stated rational mass transfers and satisfies both requested maximum-cost guarantees.

Its weighted social-cost guarantee is
\( \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M) \leq \frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}\mathrm{SC}^{*}_{\mu}, \qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_{t\in T}\mu_t. \)
This factor is asymptotically sharp for this construction. In particular, there is no bound depending only on \(\tau\) and \(k\).

The proof below uses the finite-agent Equal Cost theorem supplied in the question. The extension to rational mass transfers is proved explicitly.

1. A support-based construction

Fix the common cost function \(c\). Equal Cost has the following support-based form:

  • Find the minimum length \(\ell\) for which the reported locations can be covered by at most \(k\) intervals of length \(\ell\).
  • Choose a symmetric random variable \(X_{\ell,c}\) supported on \([0,\ell]\) whose expected connection cost is constant throughout that interval.
  • Place facilities in successive covering intervals using alternating reflections of the same sample of \(X_{\ell,c}\).

These are the ingredients of the [Equal Cost construction and finite-agent theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/1305.3333).

Here are precise conventions that make the construction independent of multiplicities. Sort the distinct reported locations. For a candidate length, greedily start an interval at the leftmost uncovered location. At the minimum feasible length, this produces disjoint intervals
\( I_i=[\alpha_i,\alpha_i+\ell],\quad 1\leq i\leq q,\quad q\leq k. \)
If necessary, append intervals to the right, setting
\( \alpha_{q+s}=\alpha_q+s(\ell+1), \quad 1\leq s\leq k-q. \)
This gives an optimal covering by exactly \(k\) disjoint intervals. The finite-agent proof applies to this choice: it uses disjointness and optimality of the covering.

Write \(C_c(\ell)\) for the constant satisfying
\( \mathbb E[c(|x-X_{\ell,c}|)]=C_c(\ell) \quad\text{for every }x\in[0,\ell]. \)
Use the facility locations
\( Y_i= \begin{cases} \alpha_i+X_{\ell,c},&i\text{ odd},\\ \alpha_i+\ell-X_{\ell,c},&i\text{ even}. \end{cases} \)

All choices depend on the support and \(c\), and are fixed consistently across inputs. In particular, replicating agents at existing locations does not change the output distribution.

For completeness, the assigned facility is always a closest facility for every point in its interval. Indeed, the midpoint between two consecutive facilities is
\( \frac{Y_i+Y_{i+1}}2 =\frac{\alpha_i+\alpha_{i+1}+\ell}{2}, \)
independently of the sample. Since \(\alpha_{i+1}\geq\alpha_i+\ell\), this midpoint lies in the gap between the intervals. Consequently, every reported type has expected cost exactly \(C_c(\ell)\).

If \(\ell=0\), take \(X_{\ell,c}=0\); all supported locations receive a facility and have zero cost.

2. Rational transfers are finite-agent coalition deviations

Consider any society \(\mu\) and any permitted deviation \((\delta_{tu})\), with reported society \(\nu\).

Because the masses and transfers are rational and only finitely many transfers are nonzero, there is an integer \(N\geq k+1\) such that all quantities
\( N\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad N\delta_{tu} \)
are integers.

Construct a finite society of \(N\) labelled agents, placing exactly \(N\mu_t\) agents at \(z_t\). For each transfer \(\delta_{tu}\), select \(N\delta_{tu}\) of those agents to report \(u\).

These selections can be disjoint for each true type, because
\( \sum_u N\delta_{tu}=Nr_t\leq N\mu_t. \)
The coalition is nonempty because \(\sum_t r_t>0\). Every selected agent changes its report, since transfers to \(u=z_t\) are excluded.

The truthful agent profile has support exactly \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), and the deviated profile has support exactly \(\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\). Support invariance therefore gives exactly the same outcome distributions as the mass mechanism at \(\mu\) and \(\nu\).

Apply the supplied finite-agent weak group-strategyproofness theorem. Its hypotheses hold:

  • there are \(N\geq k+1\) agents;
  • they have the same public admissible cost function;
  • their reports form a finite coalition deviation;
  • coincident true locations are allowed.

Some selected agent does not strictly improve. If its true location is \(z_t\), then \(r_t>0\), and its expected costs are precisely \(U_t(\mu)\) and \(U_t(\nu)\). Thus
\( U_t(\nu)\geq U_t(\mu). \)

This proves weak group-strategyproofness for the stated mass-transfer model. The common denominator is used only in the proof; the mechanism does not enumerate these agents.

3. Maximum cost and individual cost

Let \(\ell\) be the minimum covering length for the truthful support. Then
\( \mathrm{MC}^{*}_{\mu}=c(\ell/2). \)

To justify this identity, interval midpoints achieve maximum distance at most \(\ell/2\). Conversely, facilities achieving maximum distance \(r\) cover the support by \(k\) intervals of length \(2r\), so \(\ell\leq 2r\). Greedy covering gives the same minimum feasible length whether overlapping intervals are initially permitted or not. Monotonicity of \(c\) now gives the identity.

Symmetry implies \(\mathbb E[X_{\ell,c}]=\ell/2\). Evaluating the constant expected cost at the left endpoint and applying concavity gives
\( U_t(\mu) =C_c(\ell) =\mathbb E[c(X_{\ell,c})] \leq c(\mathbb E[X_{\ell,c}]) =c(\ell/2) =\mathrm{MC}^{*}_{\mu}. \)

For every realization, each supported point has its assigned facility at distance at most \(\ell\). Hence
\( \max_{t\in T}c(d(z_t,Y)) \leq c(\ell) \leq 2c(\ell/2) =2\mathrm{MC}^{*}_{\mu}. \)
The second inequality follows from concavity and \(c(0)=0\). Taking expectations proves the requested maximum-cost bound.

4. The weighted social-cost bound

Because the masses sum to one, the individual-cost bound gives
\( \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M) =\sum_t\mu_tU_t(\mu) \leq\mathrm{MC}^{*}_{\mu}. \)

For every facility configuration \(Y\), choose a type attaining its maximum cost. Its mass is at least \(\mu_{\min}\), so
\( \sum_t\mu_t c(d(z_t,Y)) \geq \mu_{\min}\max_t c(d(z_t,Y)). \)
Taking minima over \(Y\) yields
\( \mathrm{SC}^{*}_{\mu} \geq\mu_{\min}\mathrm{MC}^{*}_{\mu}. \)

Combining these inequalities proves
\( \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M) \leq\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}\mathrm{SC}^{*}_{\mu}. \)
This remains valid when the optimum is zero; no division by the optimum is needed.

Sharpness and absence of a support-count bound

Fix \(k\), an integer \(R\geq2\), and a positive rational \(\varepsilon<1/(k+1)\). Use the cost
\( c(d)=\min\{d,1\} \)
and the \(k+1\) locations
\( 0,\ R,\ 3R,\ 6R,\ldots,3(k-1)R, \)
where the last list is empty when \(k=1\). Assign mass \(1-k\varepsilon\) to \(0\), and mass \(\varepsilon\) to every other location. Thus \(\mu_{\min}=\varepsilon\).

The minimum covering length is \(R\): a shorter interval covers at most one location, whereas length \(R\) covers \(0,R\) together and the remaining locations separately.

For this cost and length, the Equal Cost law is uniform on
\( \{0,1,\ldots,R\}. \)
Indeed, for \(x\in[j,j+1]\), the costs to the two adjacent integers sum to one, and each of the other \(R-1\) integers contributes one. Therefore
\( \frac1{R+1}\sum_{i=0}^{R}\min\{|x-i|,1\} =\frac{R}{R+1}. \)
The uniqueness argument in the next section identifies this equalizing law with the construction. Consequently,
\( \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M)=\frac{R}{R+1}. \)

Meanwhile,
\( \mathrm{SC}^{*}_{\mu}=\varepsilon. \)
For the upper bound, put facilities at every location except \(R\). For the lower bound, all distinct locations are separated by at least two. One facility can therefore give cost strictly below one to at most one supported type. With \(k\) facilities and \(k+1\) types, some type incurs cost one, contributing at least \(\varepsilon\).

Thus the approximation ratio is
\( \frac{R}{(R+1)\varepsilon}, \)
which approaches \(1/\varepsilon=1/\mu_{\min}\) as \(R\) increases. Letting \(\varepsilon\) decrease also proves unboundedness with fixed \(\tau=k+1\).

5. Exact implementation for rational piecewise-linear costs

An exact grid implementation gives the verified running-time bound
\( 2^{O(L)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). \)
This is an exponential-time bound in the binary input length, not a polynomial-time claim.

The covering stage uses \(O(\tau^2\log\tau+k)\) rational arithmetic operations: enumerate and sort the location differences, binary-search feasibility, and perform greedy covering.

For the distribution stage, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all locations and all finite cost breakpoints. Set
\( h=1/Q,\qquad B=\ell Q. \)
Then \(B\) is an integer, all breakpoints are grid points, and
\( B\leq2^{O(L)}. \)

The cases \(\ell=0\) and \(c\equiv0\) are immediate. Otherwise, write \(a_r\) for the slope of \(c\) on \((rh,(r+1)h)\), for \(0\leq r<B\). Concavity gives
\( a_0>0,\qquad a_0\geq a_1\geq\cdots\geq a_{B-1}\geq0. \)
Define
\( w_s=a_{s-1}-a_s\geq0,\qquad 1\leq s<B. \)

Set unnormalized endpoint weights \(p_0=p_B=1\), and solve
\( 2a_0p_i -\sum_{\substack{0\leq j\leq B\\j\ne i}} w_{|i-j|}p_j =0, \qquad 1\leq i<B. \)

Existence, nonnegativity, and equalization

Let \(A\) be the matrix on the interior variables. It has diagonal \(2a_0\) and off-diagonal entries \(-w_{|i-j|}\).

The following maximum principle proves both nonsingularity and nonnegativity of its inverse. Extend an interior vector \(v\) by zero outside the interior indices, and suppose \(Av\geq0\). If \(v_i=M<0\) is a minimum, then
\( 2a_0M \geq\sum_{s=1}^{B-1}w_s(v_{i-s}+v_{i+s}) \geq2M\sum_{s=1}^{B-1}w_s \geq2a_0M. \)
Here \(\sum_s w_s=a_0-a_{B-1}\leq a_0\).

If that sum is strictly below \(a_0\), the last inequality is strict, a contradiction. Otherwise, equality forces every neighbor reached by a positive \(w_s\) to have the same negative minimum. Repeated jumps eventually reach a zero boundary or exterior value, again a contradiction. Thus \(v\geq0\).

Applying this principle to both signs of a null vector proves that \(A\) is nonsingular. The endpoint contributions give a nonnegative right-hand side, so the solved interior weights are nonnegative. Reflection symmetry gives \(p_i=p_{B-i}\). Normalize the weights to sum to one.

For the resulting potential
\( F(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{B}p_jc(|x-jh|), \)
its derivative on grid cell \(r\) is
\( D_r=\sum_{j=0}^{r}a_{r-j}p_j -\sum_{j=r+1}^{B}a_{j-r-1}p_j. \)
The defining equations give \(D_i-D_{i-1}=0\). Reflection symmetry gives \(D_{B-1-r}=-D_r\). Therefore every \(D_r=0\), and continuity makes \(F\) constant throughout \([0,\ell]\).

Uniqueness

For a nonzero admissible piecewise-linear cost, write
\( c(d)=a d+\sum_j\gamma_j\min\{d,b_j\}, \)
where \(a,\gamma_j\geq0\), each \(b_j>0\), and at least one coefficient is positive.

Suppose two probability measures equalize cost on \([0,\ell]\). Let their difference be \(\sigma\), and put
\( H(s)=\sigma((-\infty,s]). \)
Their potential difference is constant, and \(\sigma\) has total mass zero. Hence its energy is zero:
\( \iint c(|x-y|)\,d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y)=0. \)

Direct indicator-function integration gives
\( \iint |x-y|\,d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y) =-2\int H(s)^2\,ds \)
and
\( \iint\min\{|x-y|,b\}\,d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y) =-\int[H(s)-H(s-b)]^2\,ds. \)
For the second identity, use that \((b-|x-y|)_+\) is the overlap length of \([x,x+b]\) and \([y,y+b]\).

Every term is nonpositive. A positive linear coefficient forces \(H=0\) almost everywhere. A positive capped-term coefficient forces \(H\) to be periodic almost everywhere with a positive period; because \(H\) vanishes outside a bounded interval, this also forces \(H=0\) almost everywhere. Right continuity then implies \(\sigma=0\).

Thus the computed law is unique and agrees with the Equal Cost law, independently of grid refinement.

The slope table, rational linear system, and explicit outcome distribution can all be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L,B\). Since \(B\leq2^{O(L)}\), the stated exponential bound follows. No expansion of the rational masses into agents is required.
