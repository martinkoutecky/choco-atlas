The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite proposer- and acceptor-type sets \(\mathcal P,\mathcal A\), rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_a\), strict rankings over counterpart types, and reward values, let \(x^\star\) be the proposer-optimal stable mass flow, with each individual receiving at most one partner. Does there exist a completely uncoupled rule, using only each proposer's own past actions, rewards, and action set, such that for every \(\eta>0\) and \(p<1\) there are \(T,N_0\) for which \(\Pr(\lVert x^{t,N}-x^\star\rVert_1\le\eta)\ge p\) for every \(t\ge T\) and every compatible \(N\)-agent realization with \(N\ge N_0\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal P,\mathcal A\) be nonempty finite type sets. The society is given by its support: the \(\tau=|\mathcal P|+|\mathcal A|\) positive-mass types, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_q,\nu_a\) satisfying \(\sum_q\mu_q+\sum_a\nu_a=1\). Set \(m=|\mathcal A|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Each type strictly and completely ranks counterpart types, all above remaining unmatched. Proposer rewards \(r_{qa}\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1]\) strictly represent these rankings; being unmatched yields zero. Rankings and rewards are input data but are initially unknown to proposers.
A feasible mass flow satisfies \(x_{qa}\ge0\), \(\sum_a x_{qa}\le\mu_q\), and \(\sum_q x_{qa}\le\nu_a\). It is stable if no pair \((q,a)\) has both \(\mu_q-\sum_{b\succeq_q a}x_{qb}>0\) and \(\nu_a-\sum_{s\succeq_a q}x_{sa}>0\). Let \(x^\star\) be the proposer-optimal stable flow: each row, ordered from best to worst acceptor type, is lexicographically maximal among stable flows.
A compatible \(N\)-agent realization has exactly \(N\mu_q\) proposers of type \(q\) and \(N\nu_a\) acceptors of type \(a\), all integers. Each agent has capacity one. Rewards depend only on the two types; each acceptor uses its type ranking and an arbitrary fixed strict priority among proposers of the same type.
In every synchronous round, each proposer chooses an individual acceptor or remains single; each acceptor accepts its highest-priority proposal and rejects the rest. Matches last one round. Define \(x^{t,N}_{qa}\) as the number of matched type-\((q,a)\) pairs divided by \(N\).
All proposers start with empty histories and use the same completely uncoupled rule \(\Gamma\): their action distribution depends only on their own past actions and realized rewards and their action set of opaque acceptor identifiers plus the single action. Randomness is private and independent; no communication, type information, or population information beyond the action set is supplied.

Does one such rule \(\Gamma\) exist such that, for every society, every \(\eta>0\), and every \(0\le p<1\), there are \(T,N_0\) with \(\Pr(\|x^{t,N}-x^\star\|_1\le\eta)\ge p\) for every \(t\ge T\) and every compatible realization with \(N\ge N_0\), or is this guarantee impossible? For each fixed finite market with strict individual preferences and each \(p<1\), a completely uncoupled rule achieves the proposer-optimal stable match with probability at least \(p\) at every sufficiently late round ([Shah–Ferguson–Marden](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04669v4)).

The general existence-or-impossibility question remains open here. I prove two further results:

1. A population-uniform theorem with several acceptor types

For each acceptor type $a$, let $f(a)$ denote its most-preferred proposer type. Enumerate the distinct types appearing among these favorites as $q_1,\ldots,q_s$. Define
$$ F_i=\{a\in\mathcal A:f(a)=q_i\},\qquad v_i=\sum_{a\in F_i}\nu_a,\qquad V_i=\sum_{j=i}^s v_j. $$
Thus every $F_i$ is nonempty, these sets partition $\mathcal A$, and
$$ V:=V_1=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\nu_a. $$

Hypothesis. There is an ordering of the favorite proposer types such that
$$ \mu_{q_i}\ge V_i \qquad\text{for every }i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}. $$
No condition is imposed on proposer preferences or on the masses of proposer types outside this list.

Theorem. Under this hypothesis, the unique stable flow is
$$ \widehat x_{qa}= \begin{cases} \nu_a,&q=f(a),\\ 0,&q\ne f(a). \end{cases} $$
Moreover, the following single rule satisfies the population-uniform guarantee for every society meeting the hypothesis:

  • Initially, choose an acceptor uniformly.
  • After a positive reward, repeat the preceding action.
  • After zero reward, choose an acceptor uniformly, using fresh independent randomness.

This rule uses only the permitted information. Positivity of every matched reward distinguishes acceptance from rejection; the numerical reward and the proposer’s type are unnecessary.

1.1. Uniqueness of the stable flow

The displayed flow is feasible because
$$ \sum_a\widehat x_{q_i a}=v_i\le V_i\le\mu_{q_i}. $$
Every acceptor column is filled with its favorite proposer type. Consequently, for every pair $(q,a)$, its acceptor-side blocking slack is zero. Hence $\widehat x$ is stable.

Let $x$ be any stable flow. I show inductively that every column in $F_i$ is filled with $q_i$.

Suppose the assertion has been proved for $F_1,\ldots,F_{i-1}$. Those columns are full of their respective favorite types, so $q_i$ has zero allocation there.

Fix $a\in F_i$ and write
$$ d=\nu_a-x_{q_i a}. $$
If $d>0$, the total allocation of $q_i$ is at most
$$ x_{q_i a}+\sum_{\substack{b\in F_i\cup\cdots\cup F_s\\b\ne a}}\nu_b =V_i-d. $$
Therefore its unmatched mass is at least
$$ \mu_{q_i}-\sum_bx_{q_i b} \ge\mu_{q_i}-V_i+d \ge d>0. $$
Its proposer-side blocking slack at $a$ is at least this unmatched mass. Since $q_i=f(a)$, the acceptor-side blocking slack is exactly
$$ \nu_a-x_{q_i a}=d>0. $$
Thus $(q_i,a)$ blocks, a contradiction.

It follows that $x_{q_i a}=\nu_a$ for every $a\in F_i$. Induction proves $x=\widehat x$, so $\widehat x=x^\star$.

1.2. Conditional progress under the rule

Fix an arbitrary compatible realization. Let
$$ M=NV,\qquad M_i=Nv_i,\qquad n_i=N\mu_{q_i}. $$
Let $K_i(t)$ count acceptors in $F_i$ matched to $q_i$ in round $t$, and define
$$ D_i(t)=M_i-K_i(t). $$
Round zero denotes the initial state, with $K_i(0)=0$.

Each $K_i(t)$ is nondecreasing. Indeed, a proposer matched at an acceptor in $F_i$ repeats its proposal. Another proposer of type $q_i$ may replace it, but a different type cannot. This holds for every allowed within-type priority ordering.

Let $H_i(t)$ count all proposers of type $q_i$ matched in round $t$. The number rejected in that round is
$$ U_i(t)=n_i-H_i(t). $$
Conditional on the histories through round $t$, these $U_i(t)$ proposers sample acceptors independently and uniformly in round $t+1$.

A deficient acceptor in $F_i$ becomes matched to $q_i$ exactly when at least one of these proposers selects it. Consequently,
$$ \mathbb E[D_i(t+1)\mid\mathcal F_t] = D_i(t)\left(1-\frac1M\right)^{U_i(t)}. $$

To bound $U_i(t)$, observe that $q_i$ cannot occupy either:

  • the $\sum_{j<i}K_j(t)$ acceptors currently matched to earlier favorite types;
  • the $D_i(t)$ acceptors in $F_i$ currently not matched to $q_i$.

These sets are disjoint. Thus
$$ H_i(t)\le M-\sum_{j<i}K_j(t)-D_i(t). $$
The mass hypothesis gives
$$ n_i\ge NV_i=M-\sum_{j<i}M_j. $$
Combining these inequalities yields
$$ U_i(t)\ge D_i(t)-\sum_{j<i}D_j(t). $$

Put $R_i(t)=\sum_{j<i}D_j(t)$ and write $z_+=\max(z,0)$. Because
$$ 0\le\frac{(D_i(t)-R_i(t))_+}{M}\le1, $$
the inequalities
$$ \left(1-\frac1M\right)^u\le e^{-u/M}, \qquad 1-e^{-z}\ge\frac z2\quad(0\le z\le1) $$
imply
$$ \mathbb E[D_i(t)-D_i(t+1)\mid\mathcal F_t] \ge \frac{D_i(t)(D_i(t)-R_i(t))_+}{2M}. $$
Moreover,
$$ D_i(D_i-R_i)_+\ge D_i^2-D_iR_i\ge D_i^2-MR_i, $$
where the last inequality uses $D_i\le M$. Therefore
$$ \mathbb E[D_i(t+1)\mid\mathcal F_t] \le D_i(t)-\frac{D_i(t)^2}{2M}+\frac{R_i(t)}2. $$

1.3. Uniform decay estimates

Define
$$ e_i(t)=\frac{\mathbb E D_i(t)}M. $$
These quantities lie in $[0,1]$ and are nonincreasing. Jensen’s inequality applied to the preceding estimate gives
$$ e_i(t+1) \le e_i(t)-\frac{e_i(t)^2}{2} +\frac12\sum_{j<i}e_j(t). $$

For $i=1$, this implies
$$ e_1(t)\le\frac2{t+2}. $$
For completeness, the initial bound follows from $e_1(0)\le1$. The function $z-z^2/2$ is increasing on $[0,1]$, and
$$ \frac2{t+2}-\frac12\left(\frac2{t+2}\right)^2 = \frac{2(t+1)}{(t+2)^2} \le\frac2{t+3}, $$
which proves the bound by induction.

For $i\ge2$, sum the recurrence from $t$ through $2t-1$, where $t\ge1$. Monotonicity gives
$$ e_i(t)-e_i(2t) \ge \frac t2 e_i(2t)^2-\frac t2\sum_{j<i}e_j(t). $$
Since $e_i(t)\le1$, it follows that
$$ e_i(2t)^2\le\frac2t+\sum_{j<i}e_j(t). $$

Here are explicit bounds obtained from this inequality. Define
$$ \alpha_i=2^{1-i},\qquad C_1=2, $$
and, for $i\ge2$,
$$ C_i= \left(6+\sum_{j<i}3^{\alpha_j}C_j\right)^{1/2}. $$
Then
$$ e_i(t)\le C_i t^{-\alpha_i} \qquad(t\ge1). $$

To verify this, induct on $i$. The case $i=1$ follows above. For $i\ge2$, the assertion at $t=1$ follows from $e_i(1)\le1<C_i$. For $t\ge2$, put $u=\lfloor t/2\rfloor$. Since $2u\le t$ and $u\ge t/3$,
$$ e_i(t)^2 \le e_i(2u)^2 \le\frac2u+\sum_{j<i}e_j(u) \le\frac6t+\sum_{j<i}3^{\alpha_j}C_jt^{-\alpha_j}. $$
For every $j<i$, one has $\alpha_j\ge\alpha_{i-1}=2\alpha_i$, and $\alpha_{i-1}\le1$. Hence the last expression is at most
$$ \left(6+\sum_{j<i}3^{\alpha_j}C_j\right)t^{-2\alpha_i} =C_i^2t^{-2\alpha_i}. $$
This completes the induction.

1.4. The required probability bound

At an acceptor type $a$, let $D_a(t)$ be the number of its acceptors not matched to $f(a)$. Its favorite entry differs from the target by $D_a(t)/N$, while the sum of all other entries is at most $D_a(t)/N$. Consequently,
$$ \|x^{t,N}-x^\star\|_1 \le\frac2N\sum_{i=1}^sD_i(t). $$
Thus
$$ \mathbb E\|x^{t,N}-x^\star\|_1 \le 2V\sum_{i=1}^s C_it^{-\alpha_i}. $$

For a bound using only the supported type counts, put
$$ d=\min(m,\tau-m),\qquad B_d=\sum_{i=1}^dC_i. $$
Since $s\le d$, $V\le1$, and $\alpha_i\ge\alpha_d$ for $i\le d$,
$$ \mathbb E\|x^{t,N}-x^\star\|_1 \le2B_dt^{-2^{1-d}}. $$
Markov’s inequality therefore gives
$$ \Pr\!\left(\|x^{t,N}-x^\star\|_1>\eta\right) \le \frac{2B_d}{\eta\,t^{2^{1-d}}}. $$

For every $\eta>0$ and $p<1$, it suffices to take
$$ T= \max\left\{ 1,\ \left\lceil \left(\frac{2B_d}{\eta(1-p)}\right)^{2^{d-1}} \right\rceil \right\}, \qquad N_0=1. $$
This bound is independent of $N$ and of the realization. It uses only $m,\tau,\eta,p$.

1.5. A genuinely multiple-type example

Take
$$ \nu_a=\nu_b=\frac18,\qquad \mu_{q_1}=\frac14,\qquad \mu_{q_2}=\frac18,\qquad \mu_{q_0}=\frac38, $$
with acceptor rankings
$$ q_1\succ_a q_2\succ_a q_0,\qquad q_2\succ_b q_1\succ_b q_0. $$
The ordering $q_1,q_2$ satisfies the hypothesis:
$$ \mu_{q_1}=\nu_a+\nu_b,\qquad \mu_{q_2}=\nu_b. $$
The theorem applies to any strict proposer rankings and representing rewards, including opposite rankings for $q_1$ and $q_2$. Its unique stable flow fills $a$ with $q_1$ and $b$ with $q_2$.

Thus neither a common acceptor ranking nor a common proposer ranking is required.

2. A cycle involving only a bounded number of types

The following structural result applies without the mass hypothesis of Section 1.

Lemma. Let $x$ be stable and let $y$ be feasible. Suppose every proposer row of $y$, listed in preference order, is lexicographically at least the corresponding row of $x$. If $y\ne x$, there are distinct proposer types $q_1,\ldots,q_k$ and distinct acceptor types $a_1,\ldots,a_k$, with
$$ 2\le k\le\min(|\mathcal P|,m), $$
such that, with indices interpreted cyclically,
$$ y_{q_i a_i}>x_{q_i a_i}, \qquad x_{q_{i+1}a_i}>y_{q_{i+1}a_i}, $$
and
$$ a_i\succ_{q_i}a_{i-1}, \qquad q_{i+1}\succ_{a_i}q_i. $$
Moving a sufficiently small positive amount around this cycle preserves feasibility and improves every participating proposer row.

2.1. Constructing the cycle

Let $Q$ be the set of proposer types whose rows differ between $x$ and $y$. For $q\in Q$, let $a(q)$ be the most-preferred acceptor type where they differ. By lexicographic dominance,
$$ y_{q,a(q)}>x_{q,a(q)}. $$
All better coordinates agree, so feasibility of $y$ gives
$$ \mu_q-\sum_{b\succeq_q a(q)}x_{qb} = \mu_q-\sum_{b\succeq_q a(q)}y_{qb} +y_{q,a(q)}-x_{q,a(q)} >0. $$
Stability of $x$ therefore forces
$$ \sum_{s\succeq_{a(q)}q}x_{s,a(q)}=\nu_{a(q)}. $$
In particular, this column of $x$ is full and contains no positive allocation to types ranked below $q$.

Since $y$ is feasible and increases its $q$ entry, that column must have some decreased entry:
$$ x_{s,a(q)}>y_{s,a(q)}. $$
Every such $s$ is strictly preferred to $q$ by $a(q)$: it has positive allocation in $x$, cannot be below $q$, and cannot equal $q$.

Form a directed bipartite graph with:

  • an edge $q\to a(q)$ for every $q\in Q$;
  • an edge $a\to s$ whenever $a$ belongs to the image of $a(\cdot)$ and $x_{sa}>y_{sa}$.

Every vertex has an outgoing edge. A decreased row belongs to $Q$, and every acceptor vertex has a decreased entry by the preceding argument. Following outgoing edges in this finite graph yields a directed simple cycle
$$ q_1\to a_1\to q_2\to a_2\to\cdots \to q_k\to a_k\to q_1. $$
A cycle with $k=1$ would require the same entry to increase and decrease, so $k\ge2$.

At $q_i$, the new edge $a_i=a(q_i)$ is its best differing coordinate. Its old edge $a_{i-1}$ decreases, so
$$ a_i\succ_{q_i}a_{i-1}. $$
At $a_i$, every decreased entry is ranked above its incoming proposer $q_i$, giving
$$ q_{i+1}\succ_{a_i}q_i. $$

2.2. A feasible improving rotation

Choose
$$ 0<\delta\le \min_i\left\{ y_{q_i a_i}-x_{q_i a_i},\ x_{q_{i+1}a_i}-y_{q_{i+1}a_i} \right\}. $$
Starting from $x$, increase every entry $(q_i,a_i)$ by $\delta$ and decrease every entry $(q_{i+1},a_i)$ by $\delta$. Call the resulting flow $z$.

Each row and column has one increase and one decrease, so all row and column totals remain unchanged. Decreased entries stay nonnegative by the choice of $\delta$. Thus $z$ is feasible.

Each participating proposer transfers mass from $a_{i-1}$ to the strictly preferred $a_i$. Its cumulative allocations through every preference cutoff weakly increase, and its row strictly improves lexicographically. Its reward increases as well:
$$ \sum_{q,a}r_{qa}(z_{qa}-x_{qa}) = \delta\sum_{i=1}^k \left(r_{q_i a_i}-r_{q_i a_{i-1}}\right)>0. $$

Taking $y=x^\star$ proves that every stable $x\ne x^\star$ has such a cycle. Its length depends on supported types, rather than the number of individual agents.

Limitation: this lemma asserts feasibility and proposer improvement. It does not assert stability of $z$.

3. Why the cycle lemma does not yet produce a learning rule

Here is an explicit example where the direct rotation between two stable flows has no stable intermediate point.

Take three acceptor types $a,b,c$, each of mass
$$ w=\frac18, $$
and proposer types $q_1,q_2,q_3$ of mass $w$ each, together with $q_0$ of mass $1/4$. The total mass is
$$ 6w+\frac14=1. $$
All masses are positive and dyadic.

The proposer rankings are
$$ q_1:\ b\succ a\succ c,\qquad q_2:\ c\succ a\succ b,\qquad q_3:\ a\succ c\succ b. $$
Give $q_0$ any strict ranking. Rewards $1,2/3,1/3$, in rank order, strictly represent each ranking.

The acceptor rankings are
$$ a:\ q_1\succ q_2\succ q_3\succ q_0, $$
$$ b:\ q_2\succ q_1\succ q_3\succ q_0, $$
$$ c:\ q_3\succ q_2\succ q_1\succ q_0. $$

Define $x$ by
$$ x_{q_1a}=x_{q_2b}=x_{q_3c}=w, $$
and $y$ by
$$ y_{q_1b}=y_{q_2c}=y_{q_3a}=w, $$
with all other entries zero.

The flow $x$ is stable. The potentially improving pairs for the three matched types are
$$ (q_1,b),\quad(q_2,a),\quad(q_2,c),\quad(q_3,a). $$
Each relevant acceptor is full of a type it ranks above the requesting type. Type $q_0$ likewise faces full columns containing better-ranked types.

The flow $y$ is stable because every main proposer type receives its entire mass at its first choice, while $q_0$ faces full columns of better-ranked types.

Moreover, $q_0$ receives zero in every stable flow. For example, positive allocation of $q_0$ to $a$ would give positive acceptor-side slack for $(q_3,a)$. Since $a$ is $q_3$’s first choice, stability would force
$$ x_{q_3a}=\mu_{q_3}=w, $$
contradicting the capacity of $a$. The same argument uses $(q_1,b)$ and $(q_2,c)$ for the other columns. Consequently, $y$ is proposer-optimal: its other rows receive their full masses at their first choices, and $q_0$ cannot receive anything.

Now consider
$$ z^\lambda=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y, \qquad 0<\lambda<1. $$
For the pair $(q_2,a)$, the proposer-side blocking slack is
$$ \mu_{q_2}-z^\lambda_{q_2c}-z^\lambda_{q_2a} =w(1-\lambda)>0. $$
The acceptor-side blocking slack is
$$ \nu_a-z^\lambda_{q_1a}-z^\lambda_{q_2a} =w\lambda>0. $$
Thus every such $z^\lambda$ is unstable.

The cycle
$$ q_1:a\to b,\qquad q_2:b\to c,\qquad q_3:c\to a $$
strictly improves all three proposer types, but every nontrivial partial execution of this particular cycle creates a blocking pair. This rules out a proof based solely on interpolating along the improving cycles from Section 2.

4. What remains open

The theorem in Section 1 supplies a population-uniform rule for a class with multiple types and arbitrary proposer rankings. The cycle lemma supplies a structural consequence of nonoptimality whose size is independent of population.

Neither result settles arbitrary societies. In particular, the cycle lemma does not show how private action-and-reward histories can guide agents out of a nonoptimal stable flow at a population-uniform rate. The cited [finite-market learning theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04669v4) does not provide that uniform rate either.

$$ d=\nu_a-x_{q_i a}>0, $$

$$ \mathbb E[D_i(t+1)\mid\mathcal F_t] = D_i(t)\left(1-\frac1M\right)^{U_i(t)}. $$
