The continuous mirror question, as graded

Partial result: equivalence to succinct product comparison

I do not settle the polynomial-time versus NP-hardness classification. I prove a stronger characterization of the remaining difficulty:

The requested horizon-and-transcript function is polynomial-time computable if and only if comparison of binary-encoded products of integer powers is polynomial-time decidable.

The reverse reduction already uses societies with only $3$ cohorts whose least horizon is either $1$ or $2$. Thus the product comparison difficulty can actually be realized by the protocol; it is not merely an artifact of an upper-bound calculation.

1. The auxiliary comparison problem

Define $\mathsf{PoE}_{\le}$ as follows. The input consists of positive integers $b_j,e_j,c_k,f_k$, all encoded in binary. Decide whether

$$ P=\prod_j b_j^{e_j}\leq \prod_k c_k^{f_k}=Q. $$

This is the product-of-exponentials inequality problem studied by [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.5429).

I prove two unconditional reductions:

  • The society function is computable in polynomial time with an oracle for $\mathsf{PoE}_{\le}$.
  • $\mathsf{PoE}_{\le}$ has a polynomial-time many-one reduction to deciding whether the society’s least horizon is $1$, even for the restricted societies described above.

Neither reduction establishes a polynomial-time algorithm or NP-hardness by itself.

2. Exact simulation and a polynomial horizon bound

Index the listed prior atoms by $\Omega=\{1,\ldots,m\}$. Atom $i$ has probability $p_i$, outcome $w_i$, and signals $x_{i,t}$.

Multiply the denominators of the probabilities to obtain a common denominator $D$, and put

$$ a_i=Dp_i. $$

Then the $a_i$ are positive integers satisfying $\sum_i a_i=D$. Their binary lengths, and that of $D$, are $O(L)$.

At any point, equality of public transcripts partitions $\Omega$ into nonempty blocks. If $C$ is such a block and type $t$ speaks, its announcement on signal $x$ is

$$ q_{C,t,x} = \frac{\sum_{i\in C:\,x_{i,t}=x,\ w_i=1}a_i} {\sum_{i\in C:\,x_{i,t}=x}a_i}. $$

The denominator is positive for every realized signal. Both numerator and denominator are at most $D$.

The turn replaces each block $C$ by its subsets having equal announcement values. Rational equality is tested by cross multiplication. Updating the partition after every turn incorporates announcements earlier in the same round.

Thus every turn can be simulated in polynomial time using integers of polynomial binary length. Recording the announcement on each atom gives the requested transcript representation. The cohort masses do not enter this calculation or the stopping criterion.

A horizon bound

The least requested horizon satisfies

$$ r\leq \max\{1,m-1\}. $$

To prove this, observe that public partitions only refine. Each strict refinement increases the number of blocks, so at most $m-1$ strict refinements can occur.

Suppose an entire round leaves the partition unchanged. No turn in that round can have split a block, because refinements cannot subsequently merge blocks. Hence, for every block $C$ and every type $t$, the posterior

$$ \Pr(W=1\mid X_t=x,C) $$

is constant over realized signals $x$. Averaging over $x$ identifies that constant with $\Pr(W=1\mid C)$. Since $W$ is binary, this establishes conditional independence of $X_t$ and $W$ within $C$. Consequently, every residual conditional mutual information is zero.

For $m\geq2$, either an unchanged round occurs among the first $m-1$ rounds, or each of those rounds increases the block count. In the latter case there are $m$ blocks by the end of round $m-1$. They are singletons, so the transcript determines $W$, and every residual information is again zero.

For $m=1$, the least permitted horizon is $1$.

It follows that all potentially needed transcripts have polynomial total size and can be generated in polynomial time.

3. Upper reduction: exact stopping tests are product comparisons

Fix a candidate round and a type $t$. Let $\mathcal C$ be its end-of-round transcript partition. Define

$$ n_{Cwx}=\sum_{i\in C:\,w_i=w,\ x_{i,t}=x}a_i, $$

and define $n_{Cx}$, $n_{Cw}$, and $n_C$ by summing over the omitted indices.

From the definition of conditional mutual information,

$$ I(X_t;W\mid H_r) = \frac1D \sum_{\substack{C,w,x\\n_{Cwx}>0}} n_{Cwx} \log_2\frac{n_{Cwx}n_C}{n_{Cx}n_{Cw}}. $$

Indeed, conditional on $C$, the joint probability is $n_{Cwx}/n_C$, its marginals are $n_{Cx}/n_C$ and $n_{Cw}/n_C$, and $\Pr(C)=n_C/D$. All denominators in the displayed sum are positive.

Write $\epsilon=u/v$ with positive integers $u,v$. Form the succinct products

$$ P_t= \prod_{\substack{C,w,x\\n_{Cwx}>0}} (n_{Cwx}n_C)^{v n_{Cwx}}, $$

$$ Q_t= 2^{uD} \prod_{\substack{C,w,x\\n_{Cwx}>0}} (n_{Cx}n_{Cw})^{v n_{Cwx}}. $$

Then

$$ \log_2(P_t/Q_t) = vD\bigl(I(X_t;W\mid H_r)-\epsilon\bigr). $$

Therefore, including equality,

$$ I(X_t;W\mid H_r)\leq\epsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P_t\leq Q_t. $$

There are at most $m$ positive cells, because they are disjoint and each contains an atom. All bases and exponents have polynomial binary length.

Simulate successive rounds through $\max\{1,m-1\}$, test every type using the oracle, and return the first passing round and its transcript. This is a polynomial-time oracle algorithm. It does not require monotonicity of the stopping condition.

4. A short entropy representation of an integer logarithm

The reverse reduction uses the following identity.

Lemma. Given a positive integer $a$ in binary, one can compute in polynomial time a rational $c_a$ and at most $O(\log a)$ pairs of positive rationals $(\alpha_j,p_j)$ such that

$$ \log_2 a=c_a+\sum_j\alpha_j h(p_j), $$

where

$$ h(p)=-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p), \qquad \frac12<p_j<1. $$

All output rationals have polynomial binary length.

Proof. Write the binary expansion of $a$ as

$$ a=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}2^{k_j}, \qquad k_1>k_2>\cdots>k_\ell\geq0. $$

Set

$$ r_j=\sum_{q=j}^{\ell}2^{k_q}. $$

For $j<\ell$, define

$$ p_j=\frac{2^{k_j}}{r_j}, \qquad \alpha_j=\frac{r_j}{a}, $$

and put

$$ c_a=\frac1a\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_j2^{k_j}. $$

The lower binary digits sum to strictly less than $2^{k_j}$, so

$$ \frac12<p_j<1. $$

Since $r_{j+1}=r_j-2^{k_j}$, expanding the binary entropy gives

$$ r_jh(p_j) = r_j\log_2r_j - 2^{k_j}k_j - r_{j+1}\log_2r_{j+1}. $$

Summing over $j<\ell$ telescopes. The remaining terminal term is

$$ r_\ell\log_2r_\ell=2^{k_\ell}k_\ell. $$

Consequently,

$$ a\log_2a = \sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_j2^{k_j} + \sum_{j<\ell}r_jh(p_j). $$

Division by $a$ proves the identity.

There are at most as many terms as binary digits of $a$. The numerators and denominators displayed above have polynomial binary length. If $a$ is a power of $2$, the entropy sum is empty. $\square$

5. From product comparison to averaged binary mutual information

Given an instance of $\mathsf{PoE}_{\le}$, define

$$ \Delta = \log_2(P/Q) = \sum_j e_j\log_2b_j-\sum_k f_k\log_2c_k. $$

Apply the preceding lemma separately to every base. This produces, in polynomial time,

$$ \Delta=C+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_i h(p_i), $$

where $C$ and the nonzero coefficients $\eta_i$ are rational, and every $p_i$ satisfies $1/2<p_i<1$. We retain individual entropy terms rather than cancelling repeated ones.

If $n=0$, deciding $\Delta\leq0$ is a rational comparison. Assume henceforth that $n>0$, and define

$$ A=\sum_i|\eta_i|, \qquad B=\sum_{\eta_i<0}|\eta_i|, \qquad \beta_i=\frac{|\eta_i|}{A}. $$

Thus $A>0$, and the $\beta_i$ are positive rational weights summing to $1$.

For each $i$, construct a rational joint distribution $\mathcal P_i$ of binary variables $(X,R)$.

If $\eta_i>0$, let $R=X$ and let $\Pr(X=1)=p_i$. Then

$$ I_i:=I_{\mathcal P_i}(X;R)=h(p_i). $$

If $\eta_i<0$, let $X$ be fair and set

$$ \Pr(R=1\mid X=0)=p_i, \qquad \Pr(R=1\mid X=1)=1-p_i. $$

Here $R$ is fair, while $H(R\mid X)=h(p_i)$. Therefore,

$$ I_i=1-h(p_i). $$

Let

$$ J=\sum_i\beta_iI_i. $$

By the definitions,

$$ AJ=B+\sum_i\eta_i h(p_i), $$

so

$$ \Delta=AJ+C-B. $$

Consequently,

$$ P\leq Q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J\leq\theta, \qquad \theta=\frac{B-C}{A}. $$

The parameter ranges permit trivial cases to be handled exactly. For $p\in(1/2,1)$, both terms defining $h(p)$ are positive. Also,

$$ h'(p)=\log_2\frac{1-p}{p}<0, \qquad h(1/2)=1. $$

Hence $0<h(p)<1$, and therefore $0<I_i<1$ and $0<J<1$.

It follows that:

  • If $\theta\leq0$, the product comparison is false.
  • If $\theta\geq1$, the product comparison is true.
  • The remaining case is $0<\theta<1$.

The next construction realizes that remaining comparison as a protocol stopping test.

6. A three-cohort realization gadget

For each distribution $\mathcal P_i$, write

$$ s_i=\Pr_{\mathcal P_i}(R=1), \qquad \gamma_i(x)=\Pr_{\mathcal P_i}(X=x). $$

Our component distributions satisfy

$$ \frac12\leq s_i<1, \qquad \gamma_i(0)>0, \qquad \gamma_i(1)>0. $$

Specifically, $s_i=p_i$ for a positive coefficient and $s_i=1/2$ for a negative coefficient.

Set

$$ \lambda=\frac14, \qquad q_i=\frac13+\frac{i}{12(n+1)}, \qquad d_i=\frac{4q_i-s_i}{3}. $$

The $q_i$ are distinct and lie in $(1/3,5/12)$. Moreover,

$$ \frac19<d_i<\frac7{18}<\frac12\leq s_i. $$

The lower bound follows from $4q_i>4/3$ and $s_i<1$; the upper bound follows from $4q_i<5/3$ and $s_i\geq1/2$. In particular, $d_i\neq s_i$, and all dummy Bernoulli probabilities are positive.

The prior

Introduce a selector $S\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, a branch indicator $E\in\{0,1\}$, and a fair bit $V$.

The branch $E=0$ uses $\mathcal P_i$; the branch $E=1$ uses a dummy distribution in which $X$ has marginal $\gamma_i$ and $R$ is independently Bernoulli with parameter $d_i$.

Explicitly, for $v,x,b\in\{0,1\}$, set

$$ \Pr(S=i,E=0,V=v,X=x,R=b) = \frac{\lambda\beta_i}{2}\mathcal P_i(x,b), $$

$$ \Pr(S=i,E=1,V=v,X=x,R=b) = \frac{(1-\lambda)\beta_i}{2} \gamma_i(x)d_i^b(1-d_i)^{1-b}. $$

Define the actual state by

$$ W= \begin{cases} R,&V=0,\\ 1-R,&V=1. \end{cases} $$

List only positive-probability atoms. The probabilities are rational and sum to $1$: the total real-branch mass for selector $i$ is $\lambda\beta_i$, and the dummy-branch mass is $(1-\lambda)\beta_i$.

Use $3$ cohorts, in this order, with signals

$$ U=(S,X), \qquad Z=(S,V), \qquad E. $$

For example, assign them masses $1/4,1/4,1/2$.

First-round announcements

The first cohort announces $1/2$ everywhere. Indeed, $V$ is fair and independent of $(S,E,X,R)$, so

$$ \Pr(W=1\mid U)=\frac12. $$

The second cohort announces

$$ \Pr(W=1\mid S=i,V=0) = \lambda s_i+(1-\lambda)d_i = q_i, $$

or $1-q_i$ when $V=1$.

All these values are distinct: the $q_i$ are distinct and below $1/2$, while the $1-q_i$ are distinct and above $1/2$. Thus the second announcement reveals $(S,V)$.

The third cohort’s posterior, now conditioning on the publicly revealed $(S,V)$, is

$$ \begin{cases} s_i,&E=0,\ V=0,\\ 1-s_i,&E=0,\ V=1,\\ d_i,&E=1,\ V=0,\\ 1-d_i,&E=1,\ V=1. \end{cases} $$

For each fixed $(i,V)$, the two branch values differ because $s_i\neq d_i$. Therefore this announcement reveals $E$.

Conversely, all three announcements are determined by $(S,V,E)$. Hence $H_1$ and $(S,V,E)$ determine each other on the listed atoms.

Residual information after round one

The signals $Z$ and $E$ are determined by $H_1$, so

$$ I(Z;W\mid H_1)=I(E;W\mid H_1)=0. $$

For the first cohort, conditioning on $H_1$ fixes $S,V,E$. In the real branch, its residual information is $I_i$: complementing a binary outcome is a bijection and preserves mutual information. In the dummy branch, $R$ is independent of $X$, so the residual information is zero.

Averaging over selectors, branches, and $V$ therefore gives

$$ I(U;W\mid H_1) = \lambda\sum_i\beta_iI_i = \lambda J. $$

Round two gives zero residual information

Let $K$ denote the first cohort’s round-two announcement:

$$ K=\Pr(W=1\mid U,H_1). $$

Because $K$ is a function of $(U,H_1)$,

$$ \Pr(W=1\mid U,H_1,K)=K. $$

Averaging over compatible $U$ gives

$$ \Pr(W=1\mid H_1,K)=K. $$

Since $W$ is binary, these equalities establish conditional independence of $U$ and $W$ given $(H_1,K)$.

The other cohorts’ signals are already determined by $H_1$. Each therefore announces the public posterior $K$, adding no information. Thus every cohort has zero residual information at the end of round two.

For every positive threshold $\epsilon$, this gadget has least horizon

$$ r= \begin{cases} 1,&\lambda J\leq\epsilon,\\ 2,&\lambda J>\epsilon. \end{cases} $$

This statement includes equality exactly.

7. Completing the reverse reduction
