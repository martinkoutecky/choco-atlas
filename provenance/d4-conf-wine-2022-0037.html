The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite rational type distributions \(\mu_B\) over buyer values \(v\) and \(\mu_S\) over seller costs \(c\), with unit mass on each side and independent matching so pair \((v,c)\) has mass \(\mu_B(v)\mu_S(c)\), apply the paper’s random-offerer mechanism to each pair using the marginal distributions. Is aggregate first-best \(\mathsf{FB}_\mu=\sum_{v,c}\mu_B(v)\mu_S(c)(v-c)_+\) always at most \(3.15\) times aggregate random-offerer gains from trade?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There is one item, initially held by a seller. Buyer values and seller costs lie in \(\mathbb{Q}\), with independent distributions \(\mu_B\) and \(\mu_S\), each of total mass \(1\).
A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Let \(\tau\) be the combined support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) the total input bit length; here \(m=1\) item. State any running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
For each cost \(c\), choose the smallest maximizer \(p_c\in\arg\max_{p\in\mathbb{R},\,p\ge c}(p-c)\sum_{v\ge p}\mu_B(v)\). For each value \(v\), choose the largest maximizer \(q_v\in\arg\max_{q\in\mathbb{R},\,q\le v}(v-q)\sum_{c\le q}\mu_S(c)\).
The random-offerer mechanism flips a fair coin: the seller offers \(p_c\), accepted iff \(v\ge p_c\), or the buyer offers \(q_v\), accepted iff \(c\le q_v\); the buyer pays the seller the offered price upon trade.
Define \(\mathsf{RandOff}_\mu=\frac12\sum_{v,c}\mu_B(v)\mu_S(c)(v-c)(\mathbf{1}\{v\ge p_c\}+\mathbf{1}\{c\le q_v\})\) and \(\mathsf{FB}_\mu=\sum_{v,c}\mu_B(v)\mu_S(c)\max\{v-c,0\}\).

Does \(\mathsf{FB}_\mu\le 3.15\,\mathsf{RandOff}_\mu\) hold for every such pair of distributions?

Answer and theorem used

Yes. The inequality follows from Theorem 1.1 of Yumou Fei’s *Improved Approximation to First-Best Gains-from-Trade*: for independent buyer values and seller costs with finite first moments, the random-offerer mechanism satisfies \(\mathsf{FB}\le 3.15\,\mathsf{RandOff}\). The paper defines offers by maximizing the same expected-payoff objectives used here, with acceptance at equality. [Original source, Sections 1.1–1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00140)

We verify that the specified distributions and price selections satisfy these hypotheses.

Existence and validity of the specified offers

Write \(\mathcal V=\operatorname{supp}(\mu_B)\) and \(\mathcal C=\operatorname{supp}(\mu_S)\). Both sets are finite and nonempty.

Fix a seller cost \(c\). If \(\max\mathcal V\le c\), every feasible seller offer earns zero, and the smallest maximizer is \(p_c=c\).

Otherwise, offering \(\max\mathcal V\) earns strictly positive profit. Any maximizing offer therefore has positive demand and exceeds \(c\). For any such offer \(p\), let \(b\) be the smallest buyer support point satisfying \(b\ge p\). Raising the offer from \(p\) to \(b\) preserves demand and increases profit strictly unless \(p=b\). Thus every maximizer belongs to the finite set \(\{b\in\mathcal V:b>c\}\), so a smallest maximizer exists.

The buyer argument is symmetric. If \(\min\mathcal C\ge v\), every feasible buyer offer earns zero, and the largest maximizer is \(q_v=v\). Otherwise the maximum is positive. For an offer \(q\) with positive acceptance probability, lowering it to the largest seller support point at most \(q\) preserves acceptance probability and increases buyer utility unless it already equals that point. Consequently a largest maximizer exists among \(\{s\in\mathcal C:s<v\}\).

These constrained maximizers also maximize the paper’s unrestricted objectives: a seller price below \(c\), or a buyer price above \(v\), yields nonpositive expected payoff, whereas the constrained maximum is nonnegative.

Moreover, the prescribed tie-breaking favors gains from trade. Among optimal feasible seller prices, the smallest accepts the largest set of buyers; every added trade satisfies \(v\ge p_c\ge c\). Among optimal feasible buyer prices, the largest accepts the largest set of sellers; every added trade satisfies \(c\le q_v\le v\). Hence these selections yield at least as much gains from trade as any other feasible maximizing selections.

Application to the finite distributions

Let \(F\) and \(G\) be the cumulative distribution functions of \(\mu_B\) and \(\mu_S\). Their finite first moments follow from

\(\mathbb E|V|=\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}\mu_B(v)|v|<\infty\), and \(\mathbb E|C|=\sum_{c\in\mathcal C}\mu_S(c)|c|<\infty\).

Independence is an explicit premise. Furthermore,

\(1-F(p^-)=\sum_{v\ge p}\mu_B(v)\), and \(G(q)=\sum_{c\le q}\mu_S(c)\).

Thus the paper’s pricing objectives agree exactly with those in the question. Its first-best expectation equals \(\mathsf{FB}_\mu\), since \((v-c)\mathbf 1\{v\ge c\}=\max\{v-c,0\}\). Its equal mixture of seller-pricing and buyer-pricing gains equals the stated \(\mathsf{RandOff}_\mu\).

All hypotheses are therefore satisfied, giving

\(\boxed{\mathsf{FB}_\mu\le 3.15\,\mathsf{RandOff}_\mu}\).

This includes \(\mathsf{FB}_\mu=0\), because every accepted trade has nonnegative gains. No computational assumption concerning the support encoding is needed.
