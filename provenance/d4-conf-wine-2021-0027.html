The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(d_a\ge d_b>0\), take the population limit of bipartite markets with arrival rates \(Nd_a,Nd_b\), compatibility probability \(1/N\), and unit-rate exponential departures. Under Patient2, determine or bound the limiting unmatched-mass fractions \(L_a,L_b\), including whether the bounds of Theorem 3.2 hold.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses; \(\tau\) denotes support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(m=\tau=2\), with types \(a,b\) and unnormalized arrival masses \(d_a\ge d_b>0\); let \(L\) be their total encoding length.
For each integer \(N\ge2\), start with an empty bipartite market. Independent Poisson arrivals have rates \(Nd_a,Nd_b\); each arrival independently forms an edge to each currently present opposite-type agent with probability \(1/N\). Edges persist until an endpoint leaves.
Each agent has an independent exponential lifetime of rate one. Patient2 observes impending expiration on either side and then matches that agent to a uniformly chosen compatible agent, if available; both immediately leave. Otherwise the expiring agent leaves unmatched. No other matches occur.
Let \(D_i^N(T)\) count type-\(i\) agents who expire unmatched by time \(T\), excluding agents still present. Define \(\ell_i^N=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb E[D_i^N(T)]/(Nd_iT)\) and \(L_i=\lim_{N\to\infty}\ell_i^N\), whenever these limits exist.

Do \(L_a,L_b\) exist for every permitted input, and is computing both to additive error \(2^{-k}\) possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is this numerical evaluation problem NP-hard?

Known results: For the finite-agent stochastic markets, asymptotic performance bounds give \(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\ell_a^N\le(d_a-d_b+\log(d_b+3))/d_a\) and \(\limsup_{N\to\infty}\ell_b^N\le\exp(-\max\{d_a-d_b,d_a/(1+d_b)\})\); for \(d_a=d_b=d\), total loss is at most \(2e^{-Cd}\) for an absolute \(C>0\). These already cover the specified steady-state population scaling. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.10824v1).
No resolution of the numerical evaluation question was found in the literature searched.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted the two positive masses as unnormalized arrival intensities, rather than imposing unit total mass.
  • Specified the empty initial market, independent compatibility edges, persistent edges, independent lifetimes, and removal of both matched endpoints.
  • Resolved arbitrary compatible-partner selection by uniform random selection using only the expiring agent's neighbors.
  • Took the long-time limit before the population limit, matching the steady-state interpretation; excluded agents still present from unmatched loss.
  • Specified rational density inputs and additive precision indexed by a positive integer k to make numerical evaluation complexity well-defined; m and tau both equal two.

Result. Both limits exist for every permitted input. For the specified domain \(m=\tau=2\), both losses can be computed to additive error \(2^{-k}\) in deterministic time polynomial in \(L+k\).

More precisely, let \((x_*,y_*)\) be the unique positive solution of

\[ d_a=x_*+y_*(1-e^{-x_*}),\qquad d_b=y_*+x_*(1-e^{-y_*}). \]

Then

\[ L_a=\frac{x_*e^{-y_*}}{d_a},\qquad L_b=\frac{y_*e^{-x_*}}{d_b}. \]

The proof requires both a stationary-limit argument and a numerical algorithm whose running time remains polynomial when the masses are exponentially large in their encoding length.

1. The exact finite-market chain. Write \(A^N(t),B^N(t)\) for the pool sizes and put \(q_N=1-1/N\).

One can defer revealing each edge until an endpoint expires. At such an event, reveal the expiring agent’s incident edges and, if its neighborhood is nonempty, use independent randomness to select a uniformly random neighbor. All revealed edges have an endpoint that immediately leaves. The choice of the departing partner therefore uses no edges between surviving agents.

Inductively, conditional on this revealed history, all edges between surviving agents remain independent Bernoulli variables with parameter \(1/N\). Exponential memorylessness also leaves the surviving agents with independent rate-one expiration clocks. Thus the pool sizes themselves form a continuous-time Markov chain.

Its transitions from \((A,B)\) are:

| Destination | Rate |
|---|---|
| \((A+1,B)\) | \(Nd_a\) |
| \((A,B+1)\) | \(Nd_b\) |
| \((A-1,B)\) | \(Aq_N^B\) |
| \((A,B-1)\) | \(Bq_N^A\) |
| \((A-1,B-1)\) | \(A(1-q_N^B)+B(1-q_N^A)\) |

Transitions leaving the nonnegative quadrant have rate zero. These rates agree with the Patient2 chain in [Kakimura–Zhu, Section 6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.10824v1#S6.SS3).

Let \(Q_N\) denote its generator and \(V(A,B)=A+B\). Direct calculation gives

\[ Q_NV(A,B) =N(d_a+d_b)-A-B-A(1-q_N^B)-B(1-q_N^A) \le N(d_a+d_b)-V(A,B). \]

The chain is nonexplosive: on a bounded time interval there are finitely many arrivals, and every expiration event decreases the population. It is irreducible because arrivals and single-agent departures have positive rates whenever applicable.

We use the standard continuous-time Foster recurrence criterion: a nonexplosive irreducible countable-state chain is positive recurrent if a nonnegative function with finite sublevel sets has generator drift at most a negative constant outside a finite set. Here \(V\) has finite sublevel sets, and the displayed bound gives drift at most \(-1\) outside a finite set. Consequently there is a unique stationary law \(\pi_N\), and the chain converges to it from the empty state.

For moment control, couple the actual market with a market that retains every agent until its natural expiration, using the same arrivals and lifetimes. Each actual pool is always a subset of the corresponding comparison pool. Starting empty, the latter pool of type \(i\) has distribution

\[ \operatorname{Poisson}\!\bigl(Nd_i(1-e^{-t})\bigr). \]

Passing to stationarity gives stochastic domination by \(\operatorname{Poisson}(Nd_i)\). In particular,

\[ \mathbb E_{\pi_N}[A^2]\le (Nd_a)^2+Nd_a,\qquad \mathbb E_{\pi_N}[B^2]\le (Nd_b)^2+Nd_b. \]

The same bounds hold uniformly in time when starting empty.

The unmatched-expiration intensities are \(Aq_N^B\) and \(Bq_N^A\). Hence

\[ \mathbb E[D_a^N(T)] =\int_0^T\mathbb E[A^N(t)q_N^{B^N(t)}]\,dt, \]

and likewise for type \(b\). Convergence to stationarity, together with the second-moment bounds and \(Aq_N^B\le A\), implies convergence of these expected intensities. Taking their Cesàro averages proves that every finite-\(N\) limit exists and that

\[ \ell_a^N=\frac{\mathbb E_{\pi_N}[Aq_N^B]}{Nd_a}, \qquad \ell_b^N=\frac{\mathbb E_{\pi_N}[Bq_N^A]}{Nd_b}. \]

2. The limiting differential equation and its equilibrium. Define

\[ f_1(x,y)=d_a-x-y(1-e^{-x}),\qquad f_2(x,y)=d_b-y-x(1-e^{-y}). \]

The limiting population equation is \(\dot z=f(z)\).

For every \(x\ge0\), define \(Y(x)\) as the unique solution in \((0,d_b]\) of

\[ Y(x)+x(1-e^{-Y(x)})=d_b. \]

Existence and uniqueness follow because the left side, as a function of \(y\), is zero at \(y=0\), at least \(d_b\) at \(y=d_b\), and has derivative \(1+xe^{-y}\ge1\).

Implicit differentiation gives

\[ Y'(x)=-\frac{1-e^{-Y(x)}}{1+xe^{-Y(x)}}, \qquad -1\le Y'(x)\le0. \]

Now put

\[ H(x)=x+Y(x)(1-e^{-x}). \]

Then

\[ H'(x) =1+Y(x)e^{-x}+(1-e^{-x})Y'(x) \ge (1+Y(x))e^{-x} \ge e^{-x}>0. \]

Furthermore, \(H(0)=0\) and \(H(d_a)>d_a\). Thus there is exactly one \(x_*\in(0,d_a)\) satisfying \(H(x_*)=d_a\). Setting \(y_*=Y(x_*)\) gives exactly one positive equilibrium.

We next prove attraction, which will justify the stationary limit. The rectangle

\[ K=[0,d_a]\times[0,d_b] \]

is invariant: on each lower boundary the corresponding drift is positive, and on each upper boundary it is nonpositive. More generally, every nonnegative solution exists globally, since \(\dot x\le d_a-x\) and \(\dot y\le d_b-y\).

The derivatives satisfy

\[ \partial_xf_1=-1-ye^{-x},\qquad \partial_yf_1=-(1-e^{-x}), \]

\[ \partial_xf_2=-(1-e^{-y}),\qquad \partial_yf_2=-1-xe^{-y}. \]

Consequently, throughout \(K\),

\[ \partial_xf_1+|\partial_yf_1|=-(1+y)e^{-x}\le-e^{-d_a}, \]

\[ \partial_yf_2+|\partial_xf_2|=-(1+x)e^{-y}\le-e^{-d_a}, \]

where the second inequality uses \(d_b\le d_a\).

For two trajectories in \(K\), their difference satisfies a linear differential equation whose coefficient matrix is the Jacobian averaged along the segment joining them. Its off-diagonal entries remain nonpositive, and both row diagonal-dominance margins are at least \(c=e^{-d_a}>0\). At a coordinate attaining the maximum absolute difference, these inequalities give

\[ D^+\|z(t)-\widetilde z(t)\|_\infty \le-c\|z(t)-\widetilde z(t)\|_\infty. \]

Therefore the flow \(\phi_t\) satisfies

\[ \|\phi_t(z)-\phi_t(\widetilde z)\|_\infty \le e^{-ct}\|z-\widetilde z\|_\infty \quad\text{for }z,\widetilde z\in K. \]

In particular, every trajectory in \(K\) converges to \((x_*,y_*)\).

3. Convergence of the stationary populations. Let \(\nu_N\) be the stationary distribution of the scaled populations \((A/N,B/N)\).

The domination already proved gives

\[ \mathbb E_{\nu_N}[x^2]\le d_a^2+\frac{d_a}{N}, \qquad \mathbb E_{\nu_N}[y^2]\le d_b^2+\frac{d_b}{N}. \]

Thus the laws \(\nu_N\) are tight and their coordinates are uniformly integrable. Moreover, Poisson concentration implies that every weak subsequential limit is supported on \(K\). For example, for every \(\eta>0\),

\[ \nu_N\{x>d_a+\eta\} \le \mathbb P\{\operatorname{Poisson}(Nd_a)>N(d_a+\eta)\} \le \frac{d_a}{N\eta^2}. \]

We verify that such subsequential limits are invariant under \(\phi_t\).

Start the scaled chain \(X^N(t)\) in stationarity. Put \(\alpha_N=-N\log q_N\); then \(\alpha_N\to1\). Its drift, extended to the nonnegative quadrant, is

\[ f_N(x,y) = \bigl(d_a-x-y(1-e^{-\alpha_Nx}), \ d_b-y-x(1-e^{-\alpha_Ny})\bigr). \]

On every fixed compact rectangle, \(f_N\to f\) uniformly, and the \(f_N\) have a common Lipschitz bound.

The compensated-jump representation is

\[ X^N(t) =X^N(0)+\int_0^t f_N(X^N(s))\,ds+M^N(t). \]

Each coordinate jump has magnitude \(1/N\). The total event rate is \(N(d_a+d_b)+A+B\), and stationarity gives \(\mathbb E[A+B]\le N(d_a+d_b)\). Doob’s inequality therefore yields, for each coordinate \(i\) and each fixed \(T\),

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\sup_{0\le t\le T}|M_i^N(t)|^2\right] \le \frac{8T(d_a+d_b)}{N}. \]

Localization on a fixed compact rectangle is justified directly: a pool can increase only through arrivals, so

\[ \sup_{0\le t\le T}X_i^N(t) \le X_i^N(0)+\frac{P_i^N(T)}{N}, \]

where \(P_i^N(T)\) is Poisson with mean \(Nd_iT\). The initial populations and these arrival counts concentrate below fixed bounds. The corresponding deterministic trajectories are also bounded, because \(\dot z_i\le d_i-z_i\).

Subtracting the deterministic integral equation and applying Gronwall’s inequality on this compact rectangle now proves

\[ \sup_{0\le t\le T} \|X^N(t)-\phi_t(X^N(0))\|_\infty \longrightarrow0 \quad\text{in probability}. \]

If \(\nu_{N_j}\) converges weakly to \(\nu\), stationarity and this approximation imply, for every bounded continuous \(g\),

\[ \int g(z)\,\nu(dz) =\int g(\phi_t(z))\,\nu(dz). \]

Thus \(\nu\) is invariant under the deterministic flow. It is supported on \(K\), where contraction gives

\[ \int\|z-(x_*,y_*)\|_\infty\,\nu(dz) \le e^{-ct}\int\|z-(x_*,y_*)\|_\infty\,\nu(dz). \]

The integral is finite, and \(e^{-ct}<1\) for \(t>0\); hence it is zero. Therefore \(\nu=\delta_{(x_*,y_*)}\). Since every subsequential limit is this point mass,

\[ \nu_N\Longrightarrow\delta_{(x_*,y_*)}. \]

Finally,

\[ \ell_a^N=\frac1{d_a}\mathbb E_{\nu_N}[xe^{-\alpha_Ny}], \qquad \ell_b^N=\frac1{d_b}\mathbb E_{\nu_N}[ye^{-\alpha_Nx}]. \]

The integrands converge uniformly on compact sets and are bounded respectively by \(x\) and \(y\). Uniform integrability therefore proves the stated formulas for \(L_a,L_b\), and proves existence of both limits.

Subtracting the equilibrium equations also gives the useful exact identity

\[ d_a-d_b=x_*e^{-y_*}-y_*e^{-x_*}, \]

or equivalently,

\[ L_a=1-\frac{d_b}{d_a}+\frac{d_b}{d_a}L_b. \]

Thus it suffices numerically to approximate \(L_b\).

4. A certified polynomial-time algorithm. Assume \(k\ge0\), and write \(\varepsilon=2^{-k}\). Under the usual binary encoding of rational numerators and denominators, the total encoding length \(L\) permits the bounds

\[ B^{-1}\le d_b\le d_a\le B,\qquad B=2^{L+1}. \]

Define

\[ R=k+3,\qquad h=2^{-2R},\qquad \delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{32B},\qquad \theta=\frac{h\delta}{16}. \]

All these rationals have encoding length polynomial in \(L+k\). Let \(S(x)=H(x)-d_a\). On \([0,R]\), since \(e<4\),

\[ S'(x)\ge e^{-R}\ge h. \]

The algorithm evaluates \(S\) to absolute error at most \(\theta\), using the subroutine justified below.

First compute such an approximation \(\widetilde S(R)\).

  • If \(\widetilde S(R)\le\theta\), use the candidate losses \(0\) for type \(b\) and \(1-d_b/d_a\) for type \(a\).
  • Otherwise, bisect \(S\) on \([0,R]\), using approximate values with certified error \(\theta\).

We justify both branches.

In the first branch, \(S(R)\le2\theta\). If \(x_*\le R\), the derivative bound implies

\[ R-x_*\le\frac{2\theta}{h}=\frac{\delta}{8}<1. \]

If \(x_*>R\), the conclusion \(x_*>R-1\) is immediate. Since \(Y(x_*)\le d_b\), in either case

\[ L_b=\frac{Y(x_*)}{d_b}e^{-x_*} \le e^{-(R-1)} =e^{-(k+2)} <\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. \]

The exact balance identity shows that the type-\(a\) candidate has error at most \(\varepsilon/4\) as well.

In the second branch, \(S(R)>0\), whereas \(S(0)=-d_a<0\), so the root lies in \((0,R)\). At a bisection midpoint \(u\):

  • If \(\widetilde S(u)>\theta\), then \(S(u)>0\).
  • If \(\widetilde S(u)<-\theta\), then \(S(u)<0\).
  • Otherwise, \(|S(u)|\le2\theta\), so \(|u-x_*|\le\delta/8\).

Thus every interval reduction uses a certified sign. Stop at the third case, or when the interval width is at most \(\delta\). The resulting \(\widehat x\) satisfies \(|\widehat x-x_*|\le\delta\).

Define \(b(x)=Y(x)e^{-x}/d_b\). Using \(|Y'|\le1\), \(Y\le d_b\), and \(d_b^{-1}\le B\), we obtain

\[ |b'(x)| =\frac{e^{-x}}{d_b}|Y'(x)-Y(x)| \le B+1\le2B. \]

Hence

\[ |b(\widehat x)-L_b|\le2B\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{16}. \]

Compute \(Y(\widehat x)\) to absolute error \(\delta\), and \(e^{-\widehat x}\) to absolute error \(\varepsilon/32\). Clip these approximations to \([0,d_b]\) and \([0,1]\), respectively. Their product divided by \(d_b\) approximates \(b(\widehat x)\) with error at most

\[ B\delta+\frac{\varepsilon}{32}=\frac{\varepsilon}{16}. \]

The resulting approximation to \(L_b\) therefore has error at most \(\varepsilon/8\). Recover \(L_a\) from the balance identity; because \(d_b/d_a\le1\), its error is no larger.

Finally, round each candidate to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-(k+2)}\). This adds at most \(\varepsilon/8\), leaving both errors strictly below \(\varepsilon\) in either branch.

5. Bit complexity of the evaluation subroutines. It remains to verify that the preceding certified evaluations require only polynomial time.

For \(0\le x\le R\), evaluate \(Y(x)\) by bisection on \([0,d_b]\) for

\[ G_x(y)=y+x(1-e^{-y})-d_b. \]

Its derivative satisfies

\[ G_x'(y)=1+xe^{-y}\ge1. \]

To obtain absolute root error at most \(\zeta\), evaluate each midpoint residual to error at most \(\zeta/4\). If the approximate residual has absolute value at most \(\zeta/4\), the true residual has absolute value at most \(\zeta/2\), and the derivative bound certifies root error at most \(\zeta/2\). Otherwise its sign is certified, so bisection continues. An interval width at most \(\zeta\) also suffices.

There are at most \(O(\log(B/\zeta)+1)\) iterations. Evaluating the exponential to error at most \(\zeta/[4(R+1)]\) gives the required residual accuracy.

To evaluate \(S(x)\) to error \(\theta\), compute \(Y(x)\) to error \(\theta/4\) and \(e^{-x}\) to error \(\theta/(4B)\). After clipping the approximations to their valid ranges, substitution into \(S\) has error at most

\[ \frac{\theta}{4}+B\frac{\theta}{4B} =\frac{\theta}{2}\le\theta. \]

Because

\[ \log_2(1/\theta)=O(L+k), \]

the inner bisections and the outer bisection both have polynomially many iterations, using rational numbers with polynomially many bits.

For completeness, exponentials themselves admit the necessary polynomial-time approximation even when their arguments are large. Given a nonnegative rational \(t\) and requested absolute error \(2^{-P}\):

  • If \(t\ge P\), return zero; \(e^{-t}\le2^{-P}\).
  • If \(t<P\), use the Taylor polynomial of degree \(n=8(P+1)\) for \(e^{-t}\).

In the second case, Taylor’s theorem and \((n+1)!\ge((n+1)/e)^{n+1}\) give remainder at most

\[ \frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \le \left(\frac{eP}{n+1}\right)^{n+1} <2^{-P}. \]

The polynomial can be evaluated with exact rational arithmetic. If the argument has bit length \(s\), a common denominator has \(O(ns+n\log n)\) bits, so this computation is polynomial in \(s+P\). Clipping its value to \([0,1]\) cannot increase the error.

All requested precision parameters satisfy \(P=O(L+k+\log(R+1))\). Consequently every subroutine, and the whole algorithm, runs in deterministic time polynomial in \(L+k\).

The argument establishes the polynomial-time alternative for the specified two-type problem.
