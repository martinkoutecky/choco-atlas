The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 19 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-state arena with a finite support of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), discrete action sets, integer type rewards, and a finitely described rational polyhedral transition rule over aggregate action masses, does there exist a finite-memory population policy profile whose payoff vector admits no beneficial deviation by any positive mass coalition? A deviation is beneficial when, against every policy of the complementary mass, each deviating type receives strictly greater mean payoff.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support \(T\): the list of \(\tau\geq 1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also gives a nonempty finite state set \(S\), an initial state \(s_0\), nonempty finite action sets \(A_t\), and binary-encoded rewards \(w_t:S\to\mathbb Z\), allowing either sign. Put \(n=|S|\) and \(m=\sum_t|A_t|\).
At each state, an ordered list of rational polyhedral guards on the type-action mass vector \(x=(x_{t,a})\), each labelled by a successor state, specifies a deterministic transition: use the first satisfied guard. Guards use weak linear inequalities and cover every feasible vector \(x\geq0\) with \(\sum_{a\in A_t}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\).
For any fixed subpopulation \(b\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), a policy consists of one deterministic finite-state controller per type, observing only the public state history and outputting a real vector \(x_t\geq0\) with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=b_t\). Memory is finite but unbounded; mass allocations are deterministic splits among discrete actions.
Combined policies add their type-action mass vectors before applying the transition rule. All members of type \(t\) receive the same state reward, so their payoff is \(p_t=\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}w_t(s_k)\), without transfers or multiplication by mass.
A coalition is a fixed real vector \(c\) with \(0\leq c_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t c_t>0\), including the grand coalition. It blocks a baseline profile \(\sigma\) if it has a policy \(\alpha\) such that every complementary policy \(\beta\), of masses \(\mu-c\), yields \(p_t(\alpha,\beta)>p_t(\sigma)\) for every \(t\) with \(c_t>0\). All policies use the same finite-memory convention and all plays start at \(s_0\).

Is deciding whether some population policy profile is blocked by no positive-mass coalition in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,n,g,L\), where \(g\) is the total number of guards and \(L\) is the total binary input length.

For finite lists of players with deterministic finite-memory strategies, core non-emptiness is \(\Sigma_3^{\mathrm P}\)-complete. [Source](https://valvestate.github.io/files/csl24a.pdf)
No result settling the exact mass-based problem defined above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational input masses to total mass one.
  • Specified aggregate coordinates by type and action, and encoded the polyhedral transition rule by ordered, covering guards with weak rational inequalities; the first satisfied guard resolves overlaps and boundaries.
  • Interpreted population policies as deterministic finite-state controllers that split each type's mass among discrete actions, with arbitrary real allocation values and finite but unbounded memory.
  • Allowed coalitions to take arbitrary real portions of types, fixed throughout a play, including the grand coalition.
  • Applied the deterministic finite-memory, public-state-history observation convention to baseline, deviating, and complementary policies alike; deviations restart from the initial state.
  • Specified signed integer state rewards, common per-capita payoffs within each type, liminf mean-payoff semantics, and no utility transfers or mass weighting.
  • Defined m as the total number of type-action coordinates and included state count, guard count, and total binary input length in the running-time parameters.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(\tau=3\), \(n=7\), all masses are fixed dyadic rationals, and all rewards belong to \(\{0,1,2,3\}\). The proof is a direct polynomial-time reduction from \(3\)-SAT.

1. Fractional coalitions can be expanded to whole types. Define the support of a coalition \(c\) by \(I(c)=\{t:c_t>0\}\).

Suppose \(c\) blocks a profile \(\sigma\), witnessed by a policy \(\alpha\). Let \(d_t=\mu_t\) for \(t\in I(c)\), and let \(d_t=0\) otherwise. Then \(d\) also blocks \(\sigma\).

To prove this, choose any fixed policy \(\rho\) for masses \(d-c\), and give coalition \(d\) the policy \(\alpha+\rho\). Adding allocations preserves nonnegativity and the required mass sums. A product of the two finite controllers implements this policy, so it has finite memory.

For every complementary policy \(\beta\) of masses \(\mu-d\), the policy \(\rho+\beta\) is a valid finite-memory complementary policy of masses \(\mu-c\). The combined allocation vectors, and hence the resulting plays, satisfy
\[ \]
\(p_t(\alpha+\rho,\beta)=p_t(\alpha,\rho+\beta)>p_t(\sigma)\) for every \(t\in I(c)\).

Because \(I(d)=I(c)\), this proves the claim. Thus allowing arbitrary fractional coalitions does not introduce blocking possibilities beyond those of coalitions containing whole types.

2. The reduction. Let \(\varphi\) be a \(3\)-CNF formula with variables \(z_1,\ldots,z_v\) and clauses \(K_1,\ldots,K_k\), where \(v,k\geq1\).

Construct three types with masses
\[ \]
\((\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3)=(1/4,1/4,1/2)\).

Their action sets are:

  • \(A_1=\{a_1,\ldots,a_v,b_{12},b_{31}\}\);
  • \(A_2=\{b_{12},b_{23}\}\);
  • \(A_3=\{b_{23},b_{31}\}\).

An action name shared by two types still denotes a separate coordinate for each type.

The seven states are \(s_0,q,H,D,P_{12},P_{23},P_{31}\). The initial state is \(s_0\). The states \(H,D,P_{12},P_{23},P_{31}\) are absorbing, each implemented by one tautological guard.

Rewards at \(s_0\) and \(q\) are zero. Terminal rewards are:

| State | \(w_1\) | \(w_2\) | \(w_3\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(H\) | \(3\) | \(3\) | \(3\) |
| \(D\) | \(0\) | \(0\) | \(0\) |
| \(P_{12}\) | \(1\) | \(2\) | \(0\) |
| \(P_{23}\) | \(0\) | \(1\) | \(2\) |
| \(P_{31}\) | \(2\) | \(0\) | \(1\) |

It remains to specify the guards at \(s_0\) and \(q\).

3. Clause guards encode satisfiability, including boundary allocations. Put \(h=1/(8v)\) and write \(y_j=x_{1,a_j}\).

For each clause \(K_i\), create a guard \(G_i\) containing one inequality for each literal:

  • a positive literal \(z_j\) contributes \(y_j\leq h\);
  • a negative literal \(\neg z_j\) contributes \(y_j\geq h\).

Thus \(G_i\) is the conjunction of three weak rational linear inequalities. At \(s_0\), list \(G_1,\ldots,G_k\), all labelled by \(q\), followed by a tautological guard labelled by \(H\).

We claim that some feasible allocation reaches \(H\) exactly when \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

First, suppose an allocation reaches \(H\). No clause guard is satisfied. Define a Boolean assignment by declaring \(z_j\) true exactly when \(y_j>h\). If this assignment falsified a clause, every positive literal in that clause would have \(y_j\leq h\), and every negative literal would have \(y_j>h\), hence \(y_j\geq h\). All inequalities of its guard would hold, a contradiction. Therefore the assignment satisfies \(\varphi\). This reasoning includes allocations with \(y_j=h\).

Conversely, take a satisfying assignment and allocate type \(1\)'s mass by setting \(y_j=1/(4v)=2h\) for true variables and \(y_j=0\) for false variables. Their total is at most \(1/4=\mu_1\). Put the remaining type \(1\) mass on \(b_{12}\), with zero on \(b_{31}\).

For these allocations, each literal inequality holds exactly when its literal is false. Every clause has a true literal, so every clause guard fails. Hence the transition goes to \(H\). The other types may allocate their masses arbitrarily.

4. The failure branch has an empty core. At \(q\), use the following ordered guards:

| Guard | Successor |
|---|---|
| \(x_{1,b_{12}}\geq\mu_1\) and \(x_{2,b_{12}}\geq\mu_2\) | \(P_{12}\) |
| \(x_{2,b_{23}}\geq\mu_2\) and \(x_{3,b_{23}}\geq\mu_3\) | \(P_{23}\) |
| \(x_{3,b_{31}}\geq\mu_3\) and \(x_{1,b_{31}}\geq\mu_1\) | \(P_{31}\) |
| Tautological guard | \(D\) |

The first three guards are pairwise incompatible on feasible allocations. Any two require their shared type to put its entire positive mass on two different actions, contradicting that type’s mass constraint.

Let \(C_{ij}\) denote the coalition containing the full masses of types \(i\) and \(j\). Coalition \(C_{12}\) can force \(P_{12}\) from \(q\) by placing both members’ entire masses on \(b_{12}\). Similarly, \(C_{23}\) can force \(P_{23}\), and \(C_{13}\) can force \(P_{31}\). Each guarantee holds against every complementary policy because the designated guard holds and the competing guards cannot hold.

Every terminal outcome of this branch has a blocking coalition:

| Baseline terminal | Coalition | Forced terminal | Members’ old payoffs | Members’ new payoffs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \(D\) | \(C_{12}\) | \(P_{12}\) | \((0,0)\) | \((1,2)\) |
| \(P_{12}\) | \(C_{13}\) | \(P_{31}\) | \((1,0)\) | \((2,1)\) |
| \(P_{23}\) | \(C_{12}\) | \(P_{12}\) | \((0,1)\) | \((1,2)\) |
| \(P_{31}\) | \(C_{23}\) | \(P_{23}\) | \((0,1)\) | \((1,2)\) |

The member coordinates are ordered by type number. Every listed member strictly improves.

5. Correctness for plays starting at \(s_0\). Suppose first that \(\varphi\) is satisfiable. By Section 3, a population profile reaches \(H\). Its payoff is \(3\) for every type because \(H\) is absorbing. Every state reward is at most \(3\), so no policy combination can give any type a payoff strictly exceeding \(3\). Consequently, no positive-mass coalition blocks this profile.

Now suppose that \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable. Section 3 shows that every feasible combined allocation at \(s_0\) leads to \(q\). Every population profile therefore reaches one of \(D,P_{12},P_{23},P_{31}\) after the next transition. Its payoff equals that terminal state’s reward vector: the finite prefix contributes zero in the limiting average.

Choose the blocking coalition from the corresponding row of the table. Its members can use constant policies allocating their entire masses to the indicated pair action. Against every complementary policy, the first transition still goes to \(q\), since the formula is unsatisfiable. The second transition then reaches their designated terminal, yielding the stated strict improvements.

These blocking policies have finite memory. The guarantees hold against all feasible complementary allocations, so in particular they hold against every finite-memory complementary policy.

Thus the constructed society has an unblocked profile if and only if \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

6. Sizes, running time, and complexity conclusion. Every guard uses weak rational inequalities, and every state’s list ends with a tautological guard, ensuring coverage. The construction therefore satisfies all input requirements.

Its parameters are \(\tau=3\), \(n=7\), \(m=v+6\), and \(g=k+10\). Every guard has at most three inequalities. The only numerical quantities requiring a growing encoding are thresholds with denominator \(8v\), whose bit lengths are \(O(\log(v+2))\).

With sparse coefficient encoding, construction time and output length \(L\) are \(O((v+k)\log(v+k+2))\). With ordinary dense coefficient encoding, both are bounded by \(O((v+k)(v+1)\log(v+k+2))\), equivalently \(O((m+g)m\log(m+g+2))\). In either encoding the reduction is polynomial-time.

Since \(3\)-SAT is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, the requested decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), it has no general algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,n,g,L\), even with \(\tau=3\) and \(n=7\).
