The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and for each type a rational piecewise-constant density \(f_t\) with at most two positive segments, let \(F_t=\int_0^1 f_t(x)\,dx\). Choose measurable shares \(q_t(x)\in[0,1]\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}q_t(x)=1\) almost everywhere, and set \(W_t=\int_0^1q_t(x)f_t(x)\,dx\). Compute a welfare-maximizing allocation subject to \(W_t\geq\mu_tF_t\) for every type, with the type mass apportioned among its cake intervals so each infinitesimal resident receives one connected slice and at least \(F_t\) value per unit mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Each type has a nonnegative density \(f_t\) on \([0,1]\), specified by at most two positive constant segments with rational endpoints and heights, and zero elsewhere; \(F_t=\int_0^1 f_t(x)\,dx>0\). Valuations need not be normalized.
An allocation partitions the entire cake, up to endpoints, into finitely many connected intervals \(I_j\), each labelled by a type \(t_j\) and assigned positive mass \(w_j\), with \(\sum_{j:t_j=t}w_j=\mu_t\). Types may label several intervals; there is no prescribed order or bound on the number of intervals.
The atomless connected-slice convention is that mass within \(I_j\) is ordered by cumulative \(f_{t_j}\)-value: every consecutive subpopulation of mass \(\delta\) receives one connected subinterval worth \(\delta v_j/w_j\), where \(v_j=\int_{I_j}f_{t_j}(x)\,dx\). Require \(v_j\geq w_jF_{t_j}\) for every interval. Zero-value stretches may be attached to adjacent slices.
Set \(q_t=\mathbf{1}_{\bigcup_{j:t_j=t}I_j}\), \(W_t=\int_0^1q_t(x)f_t(x)\,dx\), and maximize welfare \(\sum_tW_t\); in particular, \(W_t\geq\mu_tF_t\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum welfare over these feasible allocations.
Let \(m\) count the intervals in the common refinement of all density breakpoints, \(L\) be the total input bit length, and \(N=1/\min_t\mu_t\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with any dependence on \(N\) explicit.

Does this continuous problem admit a polynomial-time approximation with welfare at least \(c\,\mathrm{OPT}/\sqrt{N}\), for some universal constant \(c>0\), while preserving all fairness and connected-slice requirements, or is obtaining such a guarantee NP-hard?

For finite societies of \(n\) agents, proportional welfare maximization with one connected piece per agent has reported \(\Omega(n^{-1/2})\) approximation hardness even with at most two positive segments per density ([Bei et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/8243/8102)).
Without connected-piece restrictions, optimal envy-free welfare for finite piecewise-constant societies is computable in polynomial time ([Cohler et al.](https://econcs.seas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum6346/files/econcs/files/cohler_aaai11.pdf)); this concerns a different feasibility condition. No result settling the specified atomless problem was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Excluded zero-total-value types and required nonnegative densities, following the discrete valuation domain; retained unnormalized valuations.
  • Replaced the undefined infinitesimal-resident requirement with an explicit cumulative-value ordering of atomless mass within each connected interval.
  • Required a finite partition of the entire cake into type-labelled intervals, making physical connected slices precise; measurable shares are indicators of the resulting type allocations.
  • Allowed each type's mass to be apportioned among arbitrarily many intervals, without imposing one interval per type or a population-based cut bound.
  • Used N=1/minimum supported type mass as the approximation parameter replacing the undefined finite population size; it equals n for n equal-mass supported types.
  • Defined m as the common density-refinement size and L as total binary input length, and used a supremum benchmark to avoid assuming attainment.

1. Result

The specified problem is exactly solvable in polynomial time. Consequently, the requested approximation holds with the universal constant \(c=1\).

The proof uses the stated freedom to repeat type labels and choose arbitrary positive interval masses. It constructs an optimal allocation with at most \(m\tau\) intervals, preserves every fairness requirement, and verifies the connected-slice convention.

2. A finite linear program

Let the common refinement have endpoints \(0=a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_m=1\). Write \(C_k=(a_{k-1},a_k)\), \(\ell_k=a_k-a_{k-1}\), and let \(d_{tk}\) be the constant value of \(f_t\) on \(C_k\). Endpoint values do not affect any integral. Thus

\(F_t=\sum_{k=1}^m d_{tk}\ell_k\).

Introduce a variable \(x_{tk}\) representing the length of cell \(C_k\) assigned to type \(t\). Consider the linear program

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{maximize}\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{tk}x_{tk}\),

subject to

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{tk}=\ell_k\quad\text{for every }k,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{tk}x_{tk}\geq\mu_tF_t\quad\text{for every }t,\)

\(x_{tk}\geq0\quad\text{for every }t,k.\)

Denote its optimal value by \(P\).

Feasibility and attainment. Setting \(x_{tk}=\mu_t\ell_k\) satisfies the cell constraints because \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_kd_{tk}x_{tk}=\mu_tF_t.\)

The feasible region is closed and bounded: its constraints imply \(0\leq x_{tk}\leq\ell_k\). Therefore the linear objective attains its maximum.

Upper bound on allocation welfare. Given any feasible allocation, define

\(\displaystyle x_{tk}=\sum_{j:t_j=t}|I_j\cap C_k|.\)

Because the intervals partition the cake up to endpoints, the cell constraints hold. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_kd_{tk}x_{tk}=W_t =\sum_{j:t_j=t}v_j \geq F_t\sum_{j:t_j=t}w_j =\mu_tF_t.\)

Its welfare therefore equals the objective of a feasible LP solution. Taking the supremum over allocations proves

\(\mathrm{OPT}\leq P.\)

3. Realizing an optimal LP solution by intervals

Let \(x^*\) be an optimal LP solution.

Call a cell *active* if at least one type has positive density there. We first prove that an active cell assigns no positive length to a type whose density there is zero.

Suppose otherwise that \(x^*_{tk}>0\), \(d_{tk}=0\), and \(d_{sk}>0\) for some type \(s\). Transfer the entire amount \(x^*_{tk}\) from variable \(x^*_{tk}\) to \(x^*_{sk}\). The cell constraint remains satisfied. Type \(t\)'s value does not decrease, because its density in that cell is zero; type \(s\)'s value increases. All fairness constraints remain satisfied, while welfare increases by

\(d_{sk}x^*_{tk}>0.\)

This contradicts optimality. Hence

\(x^*_{tk}>0\text{ in an active cell implies }d_{tk}>0.\)

In each active cell, list the types with \(x^*_{tk}>0\) in any fixed order and partition the cell into consecutive intervals of these lengths. Label each interval by its corresponding type. Every resulting interval has strictly positive value for its label.

The remaining cells have zero density for every type. Attach each maximal run of such cells to an adjacent constructed interval. For an initial or terminal run, use the first or last constructed interval; for an internal run, use the interval immediately to its left. At least one constructed interval exists because every \(F_t\) is positive.

These attachments preserve connectedness and change no type's value. We have obtained a partition of the entire cake into at most \(m\tau\) intervals, each having positive value for its label, with

\(\displaystyle W_t=\sum_kd_{tk}x^*_{tk}\geq\mu_tF_t>0.\)

In particular, every supported type labels at least one interval.

4. Assigning masses and verifying connected slices

For each constructed interval \(I_j\), let \(t=t_j\) and \(v_j=\int_{I_j}f_t(x)\,dx>0\). Assign it mass

\(\displaystyle w_j=\mu_t\frac{v_j}{W_t}.\)

These masses are strictly positive, and for every type,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j:t_j=t}w_j =\frac{\mu_t}{W_t}\sum_{j:t_j=t}v_j =\mu_t.\)

Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle \frac{v_j}{w_j}=\frac{W_t}{\mu_t}\geq F_t,\)

so \(v_j\geq w_jF_t\) holds on every interval.

We now verify the connected-slice convention, including zero-value stretches. Fix an interval \(I_j=[a,b]\), with mass \(w=w_j\), value \(v=v_j>0\), and label \(t\). Define

\(\displaystyle H(x)=\int_a^x f_t(z)\,dz.\)

The function \(H\) is continuous and nondecreasing, with \(H(a)=0\) and \(H(b)=v\). For \(0<u<w\), define the cut

\(\displaystyle c(u)=\min\{x\in[a,b]:H(x)\geq uv/w\},\)

and set \(c(0)=a\), \(c(w)=b\).

Continuity gives \(H(c(u))=uv/w\), including the two endpoint cases. The map \(c\) is nondecreasing. Thus a consecutive subpopulation indexed by \(u_1<u_2\) receives the connected subinterval between \(c(u_1)\) and \(c(u_2)\), whose value is exactly

\(\displaystyle H(c(u_2))-H(c(u_1)) =(u_2-u_1)\frac{v}{w}.\)

For any finite subdivision of the population, these intervals partition \(I_j\) up to endpoints. Flat portions of \(H\) are attached to adjacent slices through the same cut convention. This verifies precisely the required atomless connected-slice rule.

The constructed allocation consequently is feasible and has welfare \(P\). Together with the upper bound,

\(\mathrm{OPT}=P.\)

In particular, the supremum is attained.

5. Running time and output size

The LP has \(m\tau\) variables, \(m\) cell equalities, and \(\tau\) fairness inequalities, besides nonnegativity constraints.

All coefficients are rational. Under the specified two-segment representation, each \(d_{tk}\), \(\ell_k\), and \(\mu_tF_t\) has bit length \(O(L)\). A dense encoding of this LP therefore has length

\(B=O\!\left(m\tau(m+\tau+L)\right).\)

We use the standard theorem that a rational LP can be solved exactly in time polynomial in its binary encoding length, with a rational optimal solution of polynomial encoding length. Exact polynomial-time rational LP algorithms and rational recovery are discussed in [Gleixner and Steffy, Section 1.3](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/7507.pdf). Its hypotheses hold here: the data are rational, and the feasible region is nonempty and bounded.

Constructing the refinement, solving the LP, forming the interval endpoints, and computing the masses therefore take

\(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\)

bit operations. The output has at most \(m\tau\) intervals and polynomial total encoding length. The slice rule above specifies the internal atomless allocation without enumerating subpopulations.

There is no numerical dependence on \(N\); small masses matter only through their input bit lengths. The two-segment restriction also implies \(m\leq4\tau+1\), although the proof extends to arbitrary finitely represented rational piecewise-constant densities.

Finally, \(N\geq1\), so the exact solution satisfies

\(\displaystyle \text{welfare}=\mathrm{OPT}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{\sqrt N}.\)

6. Why finite-agent connected-piece hardness does not transfer

The finite-agent problem studied by [Bei et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/8243/8102) requires each agent to receive one connected piece. Its hardness construction explicitly uses that restriction to limit an agent to one of several separated available regions.

Here, a type may label several intervals, and its mass can be divided among them. The formula \(w_j=\mu_{t_j}v_j/W_{t_j}\) makes every such interval fair while preserving connected slices within it. This permitted division is the reason the LP realization works; substituting \(N\) for the finite agent count does not preserve the finite problem's restriction.
