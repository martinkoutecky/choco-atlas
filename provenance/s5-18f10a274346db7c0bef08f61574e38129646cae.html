1. Decomposition and the exact selection rule

Throughout, $\log$ denotes the natural logarithm. Let $J_z(S)$ be task $z$’s contribution to the objective.

Because $0\leq l\leq m$, there is a subset of size $l$. Its unconditioned prior probability is positive, since every $t_a$ lies strictly between $0$ and $1$. Consequently, $\beta>0$.

Define
$$ s_{za} = \log\frac{t_a}{1-t_a} + N\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r \left[ x_{rza}\log\frac{p_r}{q_r} + (1-x_{rza})\log\frac{1-p_r}{1-q_r} \right]. $$
Expanding the objective gives
$$ J_z(S)=C_z+\sum_{a\in S}s_{za}, $$
where
$$ C_z= -\log\beta+\sum_{a\in A}\log(1-t_a) + N\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{a\in A} \left[ x_{rza}\log q_r+(1-x_{rza})\log(1-q_r) \right]. $$
Every logarithm has a positive argument, and $C_z$ is independent of $S$.

Feasibility is a Cartesian product across tasks. Therefore, a tuple is optimal exactly when each component maximizes its own task contribution: replacing a nonoptimal component improves the total, whereas a tuple of componentwise optima dominates every feasible tuple. Computing $\beta$ is unnecessary.

For one task, order the alternatives so that
$$ s_{za_1}\geq s_{za_2}\geq\cdots\geq s_{za_m}. $$
For each cardinality $k$, the prefix of length $k$ maximizes the score sum. Indeed, if another $k$-element set differs from this prefix, replace a selected alternative outside the prefix by an unselected alternative inside it. The score cannot decrease, and repeated replacements produce the prefix.

Let
$$ h_z=\left|\{a:s_{za}>0\}\right|, \qquad k_z=\min\{u,\max\{l,h_z\}\}. $$
The prefix sums increase strictly through the positive scores and decrease weakly afterward. Thus the prefix of length $k_z$ is optimal among feasible cardinalities. Equal scores may be ordered arbitrarily; zero scores can yield additional optimal cardinalities.

This proves the selection rule, including ties. The remaining issue is the complexity of exact score comparisons.

2. Product-of-powers comparison and polynomial-time equality testing

Define product-of-powers comparison as follows: given positive integers $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ and signed integers $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, all encoded in binary, decide whether
$$ R=\prod_{i=1}^{n}b_i^{e_i}\geq1. $$
Equivalently, decide whether
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i\log b_i\geq0, $$
because $R>0$ and $\log$ is strictly increasing.

Negative exponents can be moved to the other side, so this is comparison between two products having nonnegative exponents.

Lemma: equality is decidable in polynomial time

Discard bases equal to $1$ and exponents equal to $0$. Whenever two remaining terms $(a,e)$ and $(b,f)$ have $g=\gcd(a,b)>1$, replace them by
$$ (a/g,e),\qquad (b/g,f),\qquad (g,e+f), $$
and discard any resulting trivial terms.

This preserves the represented rational number, since
$$ a^e b^f=(a/g)^e(b/g)^f g^{e+f}. $$

To bound the number of replacements, define
$$ \Phi=\sum_{\text{current terms }(b,e)}\log_2 b. $$
Before discarding trivial terms, a replacement decreases $\Phi$ by exactly $\log_2 g\geq1$. Discarding terms can only decrease it further.

Let $B_0$ be the total initial bit length of the bases. Initially $\Phi\leq B_0$, so there are at most $B_0$ replacements. At every stage there are at most $B_0$ retained terms, since each retained base contributes at least $1$ to $\Phi$.

Bases never exceed the largest original base. Each replacement increases the maximum exponent bit length by at most one, because its only new exponent is a sum of two existing exponents. Thus all intermediate integers have polynomial bit length. Searching pairs, computing greatest common divisors, and performing the replacements take polynomial time.

At termination, the remaining bases are pairwise coprime. If a term $(b,e)$ remains, choose a prime divisor of $b$. That prime divides no other remaining base, and its valuation in $R$ is $e$ times its positive valuation in $b$, hence is nonzero. Therefore $R\neq1$.

If no terms remain, the represented product is $1$. This proves the lemma without integer factorization.

3. Exact optimization reduces to product-of-powers comparison

Set
$$ T_a=\frac{t_a}{1-t_a}, \qquad D_r=\frac{1-p_r}{1-q_r}, \qquad W_r=\frac{p_r(1-q_r)}{q_r(1-p_r)}. $$
These are positive rationals, and
$$ s_{za} = \log T_a+ N\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\log D_r+ N\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r x_{rza}\log W_r. $$
Moreover,
$$ s_{za}-s_{zb} = \log\frac{T_a}{T_b} + N\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r(x_{rza}-x_{rzb})\log W_r. $$

Thus every comparison required by the selection rule has the form
$$ E=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_i\log v_i, $$
with rational coefficients $c_i$ and positive rational bases $v_i$. All these rationals have polynomial bit length: they are obtained using a polynomial number of rational arithmetic operations, including multiplication by the binary-encoded integer $N$.

Write $c_i=h_i/k_i$ with $k_i>0$, and let
$$ D=\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_i. $$
The bit length of $D$ is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths. Consequently, $D$ and the integers $f_i=Dc_i$ have polynomial bit length. Since $D>0$, multiplication by $D$ preserves the sign.

Writing $v_i=a_i/b_i$ gives
$$ DE=\sum_{i=1}^{d} \left(f_i\log a_i-f_i\log b_i\right). $$
The sign of $E$ is therefore a product-of-powers comparison. Two non-strict comparison queries, for the product and its reciprocal, distinguish positive, negative, and zero signs.

Sorting each task’s alternatives and testing their positivity require polynomially many such queries. Hence exact optimization has a polynomial-time Turing reduction to product-of-powers comparison.

4. The converse holds even when every type has $p_r>q_r$

This strengthens the lower reduction in the notes. It also shows that the arithmetic difficulty does not depend on allowing winning alternatives to have lower approval probabilities.

Start with
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i\log b_i. $$
Discard bases equal to $1$, combine repeated bases by adding their exponents, and discard zero exponents. If the resulting list is empty, the comparison is equality and is already decided. Otherwise, every $b_i>1$ and every $e_i\neq0$.

Let
$$ E_0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|e_i|, $$
and choose a power of two $H>E_0$. Its bit length is polynomial in the input length.

Construct an instance with
$$ m=K=N=1,\qquad l=0,\qquad u=1,\qquad t_a=\frac12. $$
For each remaining term, introduce a type of mass
$$ \mu_i=\frac{|e_i|}{H}. $$

Let $\ell_i$ be the bit length of $b_i$, and put
$$ h_i=\ell_i+1. $$
Then $2^{h_i}>2b_i$.

If $e_i>0$, choose
$$ p_i=\frac{b_i}{2^{h_i}}, \qquad q_i=\frac{1}{2^{h_i}}, \qquad x_{i1a}=1. $$
These probabilities satisfy $0<q_i<p_i<1$, and the type’s likelihood score is
$$ \log\frac{p_i}{q_i}=\log b_i. $$

If $e_i<0$, choose
$$ p_i=1-\frac{1}{2^{h_i}}, \qquad q_i=1-\frac{b_i}{2^{h_i}}, \qquad x_{i1a}=0. $$
Again $0<q_i<p_i<1$. This time the likelihood score is
$$ \log\frac{1-p_i}{1-q_i} = \log\frac{2^{-h_i}}{b_i2^{-h_i}} = -\log b_i. $$

Finally, introduce a dummy type with
$$ \mu_0=1-\frac{E_0}{H}, \qquad p_0=\frac34, \qquad q_0=\frac14, \qquad x_{01a}=\frac12. $$
Its mass is strictly positive, and its likelihood score is zero:
$$ \frac12\log\frac{p_0}{q_0} + \frac12\log\frac{1-p_0}{1-q_0} = \frac12\log3+\frac12\log\frac13=0. $$

All masses are positive and sum to $1$. All input rationals are dyadic, and every type satisfies $p_r>q_r$. Approval fractions belong to $\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$.

The support genuinely has polynomial size. In fact, its parameter pairs are distinct. Positive-exponent types have both probabilities below $\tfrac12$; negative-exponent types have both above $\tfrac12$; the dummy type has probabilities on opposite sides. Within either exponent sign, equality of parameter pairs determines both $h_i$ and $b_i$, and repeated bases have already been combined.

There are exactly two feasible sets. Their objective difference is
$$ J_1(\{a\})-J_1(\varnothing) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|e_i|}{H} \operatorname{sgn}(e_i)\log b_i = \frac{\Lambda}{H}. $$
The prior contribution vanishes because $t_a=\tfrac12$, and the dummy contribution vanishes by construction.

Therefore $\{a\}$ is uniquely optimal when $\Lambda>0$, and $\varnothing$ is uniquely optimal when $\Lambda<0$. When $\Lambda=0$, both are optimal.

First perform the polynomial-time equality test from Section 2. If equality holds, answer yes to the non-strict comparison. Otherwise, one call to any correct optimizer distinguishes the two strict cases. Arbitrary tie-breaking causes no difficulty.

Together with Section 3, this proves polynomial-time Turing equivalence, even for the restricted class just constructed.

5. An unconditional polynomial-space exact algorithm

Here is a direct proof that product-of-powers comparison, and hence the optimization problem, can be solved in polynomial space.

Write
$$ U=\prod_{e_i>0}b_i^{e_i}, \qquad V=\prod_{e_i<0}b_i^{-e_i}. $$
The required comparison is $U\geq V$. Let $I$ be the comparison instance’s total bit length, let $\ell(b)$ denote an integer’s bit length, and define
$$ B=1+\sum_i |e_i|\ell(b_i). $$
Both $U$ and $V$ are strictly below $2^B$. Although $B$ may be exponentially large, its binary encoding has polynomial length in $I$.

Repeated squaring constructs multiplication circuits for $U$ and $V$ with polynomially many gates and polynomial depth. Each constant is an input base or $1$. Intermediate powers can be restricted to powers no larger than the requested power; partial products are no larger than the final corresponding product.

It remains to compute individual output bits without storing the full integers.

Computing one multiplication bit

Suppose a gate multiplies positive integers $X$ and $Y$, with binary digits $x_h$ and $y_h$. The coefficient of $2^j$ before carrying is
$$ a_j=\sum_{h=0}^{j}x_hy_{j-h}. $$
Starting with $c_0=0$, define successively
$$ S_j=c_j+a_j, \qquad v_j=S_j\bmod2, \qquad c_{j+1}=\left\lfloor\frac{S_j}{2}\right\rfloor. $$
The digit $v_j$ is bit $j$ of $XY$. Indeed,
$$ XY=\sum_{j\geq0}a_j2^j, $$
and the recurrence replaces each column’s coefficient by its binary digit and the carry into the next column. Columns above $j$ cannot affect bit $j$.

The carry remains small relative to the column index. By induction, $c_j\leq j$: if this holds, then
$$ S_j\leq j+(j+1)=2j+1, \qquad c_{j+1}\leq j\leq j+1. $$
Consequently, all column counters, partial sums, and carries use $O(\log(k+2))$ bits when computing bit $k$.

To compute bit $k$ of a multiplication gate, process columns $0$ through $k$. Obtain every required child bit recursively, recomputing it whenever necessary. At a constant gate, read the requested bit directly, returning zero beyond the constant’s bit length.

If the circuit depth is $d$ and its size is $s$, this recursion uses
$$ O\!\left(d\bigl(\log(B+2)+\log(s+2)\bigr)\right) $$
working bits, apart from polynomial-size storage of the input and circuit. Each recursive call moves to a child gate, so the recursion terminates. Since $d$, $s$, and $\log B$ are polynomial in $I$, the space usage is polynomial.

Finally, scan bit positions from $B-1$ down to $0$. At the first unequal pair of bits of $U$ and $V$, the integer with bit $1$ is larger. If every bit agrees, the integers are equal.

This proves polynomial-space comparison. Applying it to the polynomially many comparisons in Section 3 gives a polynomial-space exact optimizer. The method permits extensive recomputation and does not establish polynomial time.

6. Polynomial-time additive approximation for unrestricted support

Let $j\geq0$ be a requested number of accuracy bits. There is an algorithm running in time polynomial in $m,\tau,K,L,j$ that returns a feasible tuple whose objective is within $2^{-j}$ of the optimum.

Polynomial-time approximation of rational logarithms

Let $v>0$ be a binary-encoded rational. By exact comparisons with powers of two, find an integer $h$ such that
$$ v=2^h y,\qquad 1\leq y<2. $$
The magnitude of $h$ is bounded by the total bit length of the numerator and denominator of $v$.

Put
$$ w=\frac{y-1}{y+1}, $$
so $0\leq w<\tfrac13$. Integrating the convergent geometric series for $\frac{2}{1-w^2}$ gives
$$ \log y = 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2k+1}}{2k+1}. $$
After retaining $M$ terms, the error is bounded by
$$ 2\sum_{k=M}^{\infty}w^{2k+1} = \frac{2w^{2M+1}}{1-w^2} \leq \frac94\,3^{-(2M+1)}. $$
The same formula with $w=\tfrac13$ approximates $\log2$. Since
$$ \log v=h\log2+\log y, $$
choosing $M$ proportional to the requested accuracy bits plus $\log_2(|h|+1)$ suffices.

Every truncated term is rational. Powers and sums can be computed exactly with polynomial bit length: the power denominators have bit length linear in $M$ and the input length, and even using products of all term denominators gives a polynomial bound. Thus $\log v$ can be approximated to error $2^{-b}$ in time polynomial in its input length and $b$.

Approximate scores and optimize them

Set
$$ \delta=\frac{2^{-j}}{2Km}, \qquad \eta=\frac{\delta}{1+N}. $$
Approximate each logarithm in
$$ s_{za} = \log\frac{t_a}{1-t_a} + N\sum_r\mu_r \left[ x_{rza}\log\frac{p_r}{q_r} + (1-x_{rza})\log\frac{1-p_r}{1-q_r} \right] $$
to absolute error at most $\eta$, and form the resulting rational score $\widehat s_{za}$ exactly.

All coefficients in this expression are nonnegative, and their sum is
$$ 1+N\sum_r\mu_r\bigl(x_{rza}+1-x_{rza}\bigr)=1+N. $$
It follows that
$$ |\widehat s_{za}-s_{za}|\leq(1+N)\eta=\delta. $$
The required number of logarithm accuracy bits is bounded by
$$ j+O\!\left(\log(Km)+\log(N+1)+1\right), $$
which is polynomial in $L$ and $j$.

Use the selection rule from Section 1 on the rational scores $\widehat s_{za}$. Let $\widehat{\mathbf S}$ be the returned tuple and $\mathbf S^*$ a true optimum. Define
$$ F(\mathbf S)=\sum_z\sum_{a\in S_z}s_{za}, \qquad \widehat F(\mathbf S)=\sum_z\sum_{a\in S_z}\widehat s_{za}. $$
Every tuple contains at most $Km$ selected occurrences, so
$$ |F(\mathbf S)-\widehat F(\mathbf S)|\leq Km\delta. $$
Because $\widehat{\mathbf S}$ maximizes $\widehat F$,
$$ \begin{aligned} F(\mathbf S^*)-F(\widehat{\mathbf S}) &= F(\mathbf S^*)-\widehat F(\mathbf S^*) +\widehat F(\mathbf S^*)-\widehat F(\widehat{\mathbf S}) +\widehat F(\widehat{\mathbf S})-F(\widehat{\mathbf S})\\ &\leq 2Km\delta\\ &=2^{-j}. \end{aligned} $$
The constants $C_z$ cancel, so this is also the difference in the original objective.

This proves the approximation guarantee. It does not establish exact optimization: distinct feasible objective values can be closer than a prescribed approximation tolerance.

7. Fixed support size and the remaining classification

The following established theorem supplies the fixed-support result:

Fixed-length comparison theorem. For every fixed integer $d$, the sign of
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{d}e_i\log b_i $$
is decidable in deterministic polynomial time when the positive integer bases and signed integer exponents are binary encoded. Separating positive and negative exponents puts this directly within Proposition 3.7(2) of [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.5429).

The hypotheses hold here. Before splitting rational bases into numerator and denominator, a score positivity test has at most $1+2\tau$ logarithmic terms, and a comparison between two alternatives has at most $1+\tau$. After splitting, the respective bounds are
$$ 2+4\tau \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2+2\tau. $$
Their integer encodings have polynomial length by Section 3. For fixed $\tau$, the number of terms is fixed, so the theorem makes every required comparison polynomial-time computable. The selection rule then yields an exact polynomial-time optimizer. Its polynomial may depend on the fixed value of $\tau$.

For unrestricted $\tau$, the proved equivalence identifies the precise remaining arithmetic problem. A polynomial-time product-of-powers comparison algorithm would give a polynomial-time exact optimizer. Conversely, a polynomial-time optimizer would solve product-of-powers comparison, even with dyadic inputs and $p_r>q_r$ throughout. An appropriate NP-hardness reduction for comparison would transfer to optimization through Section 4.

Neither the polynomial-space bound nor the approximation algorithm supplies either missing conclusion. Thus the unrestricted target remains unresolved in this write-up.
