The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) resources, \(\tau\) valuation types with public rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supplies \(h_j\), and normalized, monotone, subadditive valuations \(v_t:[0,1]^m\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) privately held by the type parties, determine the nondeterministic communication complexity of producing a feasible \((2-\varepsilon)\)-approximation to \(\mathrm{OPT}\). A feasible plan consists of a probability distribution \(\pi_t\) over bundles for each type, with welfare \(\sum_t\mu_t\int v_t(x)\,d\pi_t(x)\) and resource use \(\sum_t\mu_t\int x_j\,d\pi_t(x)\le h_j\) for every resource \(j\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) resources with public binary-rational per-capita supplies \(h_j\in(0,1]\); \(L\) is the total public input bit length.
Each type is represented by one party privately holding a Borel-measurable valuation \(v_t:[0,1]^m\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), with finite values, \(v_t(0)=0\), and coordinatewise monotonicity. Subadditivity means \(v_t(x+y)\le v_t(x)+v_t(y)\) whenever \(x,y,x+y\in[0,1]^m\); continuity is not assumed.
A plan assigns each type a Borel probability measure \(\pi_t\) on bundles. Its welfare is \(W(\pi)=\sum_t\mu_t\int v_t(x)\,d\pi_t(x)\), and feasibility means \(\sum_t\mu_t\int x_j\,d\pi_t(x)\le h_j\) for every \(j\). Define \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sup_{\pi\text{ feasible}}W(\pi)\).
Use nondeterministic bit communication on a shared blackboard, with unrestricted local computation and free access to each party's own valuation. Certificates and the output count toward communication; an output explicitly lists finitely supported lotteries with binary-rational probabilities and bundle coordinates. Complexity parameters are \(m,\tau,L\), and \(\varepsilon\).

For each fixed \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), what is the worst-case nondeterministic communication complexity of producing a feasible plan with \(W(\pi)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/(2-\varepsilon)\)? Does communication polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) suffice, or is communication exponential in \(m\) necessary?

For finitely many agents receiving deterministic bundles under unit aggregate supplies, a \((2-\varepsilon)\)-approximation requires exponential communication, even nondeterministically. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.07011). The [published version](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306925842_On_the_Efficiency_of_the_Proportional_Allocation_Mechanism_for_Divisible_Resources) retains this result. No result settling the mass-and-lottery problem was found in the searches conducted.

  • Required Borel-measurable valuations and Borel probability measures so that welfare integrals are defined, without imposing continuity.
  • Used a supremum for OPT because the stated valuation assumptions do not guarantee attainment.
  • Specified one communicating party per supported type, shared-blackboard nondeterministic bit communication, and unrestricted free local valuation access.

Conclusion

As stated, the worst-case communication is unbounded even when \(m=1\), \(\tau=2\), and \(L\) is constant. Thus neither a polynomial bound nor an exponential bound in the listed parameters suffices.

This follows from the absence of a parameter controlling private valuation scales. To address the substantive mass-and-lottery question beyond that obstruction, I also prove that polynomial nondeterministic communication suffices under the additional normalization \(v_t(\mathbf 1)=1\) for every supported type. That result accommodates discontinuities and even does not require subadditivity.

1. An unbounded lower bound for the stated model

Write \(\alpha=2-\varepsilon\), so \(1<\alpha<2\).

Use the fixed public instance
\[ \]
\(m=1,\qquad \tau=2,\qquad \mu_1=\mu_2=\tfrac12,\qquad h_1=\tfrac12.\)

For positive numbers \(a,b\), give the parties the valuations
\(v_1(x)=ax\) and \(v_2(x)=bx\). These valuations are finite, continuous, monotone, normalized at zero, and additive, hence subadditive.

For an output plan, define \(z_t=\int x\,d\pi_t(x)\). Feasibility is equivalent to
\(z_1+z_2\le 1\), and its welfare is
\(W=\tfrac12(az_1+bz_2)\).
Consequently,
\(\mathrm{OPT}=\tfrac12\max\{a,b\}\): the upper bound follows from feasibility, and assigning bundle \(1\) to a type with the larger slope achieves it.

The transcript property

A sound nondeterministic protocol must satisfy two conditions:

  • On every input, some certificate leads to acceptance and a valid approximate output.
  • Every accepting execution produces a valid approximate output.

Fix a complete accepting transcript, including its certificate and output. The pairs of private inputs compatible with it form a rectangle. Indeed, once the transcript is fixed, each party’s messages and acceptance decisions depend only on that party’s private input. Therefore, if the same transcript accepts on \((a,a)\) and \((b,b)\), it also accepts on \((a,b)\) and \((b,a)\), with the same output.

This remains true with arbitrary local computation and additional local nondeterministic choices: compatibility with the fixed transcript is still a separate condition on each party.

An infinite family requiring distinct transcripts

Choose an integer \(r>0\) such that
\(2^r>\alpha/(2-\alpha)\),
and put
\(a_n=2^{-rn}\) for \(n=1,2,\ldots\).

These slopes are positive dyadic rationals. In particular, this argument even restricts every valuation to take values in \([0,1]\).

Suppose a protocol has a finite worst-case communication bound \(C\). There are only finitely many complete transcripts of at most \(C\) bits. Choose one accepting transcript for every diagonal input \((a_n,a_n)\). Two distinct diagonal inputs must share a transcript.

Let their slopes be \(A>b\). The shared transcript also accepts on both \((A,b)\) and \((b,A)\). Let \(z_1,z_2\) be the means of its common output. Approximation on the two crossed inputs requires
\(Az_1+bz_2\ge A/\alpha\)
and
\(bz_1+Az_2\ge A/\alpha\).

Adding these inequalities and using feasibility gives
\((A+b)(z_1+z_2)\ge 2A/\alpha\),
and hence
\(1+b/A\ge 2/\alpha\).

But the construction gives
\(A/b\ge 2^r>\alpha/(2-\alpha)\).
Equivalently,
\(1+b/A<1+(2-\alpha)/\alpha=2/\alpha\),
a contradiction.

Thus no finite \(C\) exists. The public input is fixed throughout, so no finite function of \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon\) bounds the worst-case communication.

This is not caused by discontinuities or by irrational output requirements. The example uses linear valuations with dyadic slopes. The missing parameter is private valuation scale or representation length.

2. The substantive lottery case under public normalization

The preceding obstruction should not be mistaken for an exponential lower bound arising from the number of resources. Here is a positive result for a natural, explicitly stronger model.

Proven claim. Suppose additionally that \(v_t(\mathbf 1)=1\) for every type. For each fixed \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), a feasible \((2-\varepsilon)\)-approximation has a nondeterministic certificate and explicit output of polynomial length in \(m,\tau,L\).

Individual normalization changes relative welfare weights, so this is an additional hypothesis, not a without-loss-of-generality transformation.

2.1. A finite grid approximates arbitrary lotteries

Fix a rational number \(a\) with
\(1<a<2-\varepsilon\).
Choose a positive dyadic number \(\rho\) satisfying
\(\rho\le\min\{1/16,(a-1)/8\}\).

These choices ensure
\((1+\rho)^2/(1-2\rho)\le a\).
To verify this, put \(d=a-1\in(0,1)\). The difference between the right and left sides after clearing the positive denominator is
\(a(1-2\rho)-(1+\rho)^2 =d-4\rho-2d\rho-\rho^2\).
Since \(\rho\le d/8\), this is at least
\(d-6\rho-\rho^2 \ge d/4-d^2/64>0\).

Let \(H=\min_j h_j>0\). Choose a dyadic grid spacing \(\delta=2^{-k}\le\rho H\), and define
\(G=\{0,\delta,2\delta,\ldots,1\}^m\).

For a bundle \(x\), let \(R(x)\) round every coordinate upward to the next grid point. This is a Borel map, and
\(R(x)\ge x\) and \(R(x)_j\le x_j+\delta\).

Given any feasible plan, replace each lottery by its image under \(R\), then retain that lottery with probability \(1/(1+\rho)\) and otherwise assign zero. Its aggregate consumption of resource \(j\) is at most
\((h_j+\delta)/(1+\rho)\le h_j\).
Monotonicity and \(v_t(0)=0\) show that its welfare is at least the original welfare divided by \(1+\rho\).

Let \(P\) be the optimal welfare among feasible lotteries supported on \(G\). Taking the supremum over the original feasible plans gives
\(\mathrm{OPT}\le(1+\rho)P\).

No continuity or attainment of the original supremum was used. Also,
\(H\le P\le1\).
For the lower bound, give every type the full bundle with probability \(H\), and zero otherwise. For the upper bound, monotonicity and normalization give \(v_t(x)\le1\).

2.2. The finite linear program and its dual

Use variables \(\lambda_{t,g}\), denoting the probability that type \(t\) receives grid bundle \(g\). The finite linear program maximizes
\(\sum_{t,g}\mu_t\lambda_{t,g}v_t(g)\)
subject to
\(\sum_g\lambda_{t,g}=1\),
\(\sum_{t,g}\mu_t\lambda_{t,g}g_j\le h_j\),
and \(\lambda_{t,g}\ge0\).

Its feasible set is nonempty and compact, so it has an optimum at a vertex.

Such a vertex has at most
\(K=\tau+m\)
positive variables. Indeed, if there were more, the corresponding columns in the matrix of the \(\tau\) probability equations and \(m\) resource expressions would be linearly dependent. A sufficiently small perturbation in both signs along that dependence would preserve all those expressions and nonnegativity, contradicting extremality.

The dual has variables \(p_j\ge0\) and \(u_t\), with constraints
\(u_t+p\cdot g\ge v_t(g)\)
for every \(t,g\), and objective
\(D=\sum_t\mu_tu_t+\sum_jh_jp_j\).

The constraint for \(g=0\) implies \(u_t\ge0\). The choice \(u_t=1\), \(p_j=0\) is dual feasible. Finite-dimensional linear-programming duality therefore supplies optimal dual variables with \(D=P\le1\). Their nonnegativity implies
\(u_t\le1/\mu_t\) and \(p_j\le1/h_j\).

These bounds are crucial: their logarithms are controlled by the public input length.

2.3. Short dyadic output and certificate

Choose public dyadic numbers \(\eta,e>0\) such that
\(K\eta\le\rho H\)
and
\((m+1)e\le\rho H\).

Start with an optimal sparse primal solution. Round every positive probability downward to a multiple of \(\eta\), then assign each type’s missing probability to zero.

This preserves feasibility because every resource consumption weakly decreases. The resulting output has at most \(K+\tau=m+2\tau\) atoms, with dyadic coordinates and probabilities. Since \(v_t(g)\le1\), its welfare \(W\) satisfies
\(W\ge P-K\eta\ge P-\rho H\).

For each type, let \(M_t\) be its expected value in this output, and include the dyadic number
\(b_t=e\lfloor M_t/e\rfloor\)
in the certificate. Define the publicly computable lower bound
\(B=\sum_t\mu_tb_t\).
Then
\(B\ge W-e\ge P-2\rho H\ge(1-2\rho)P\).

Finally, round the optimal dual variables upward to multiples of \(e\). The rounded variables remain dual feasible, and their objective satisfies
\(D\le P+(m+1)e\le P+\rho H\le(1+\rho)P\).

2.4. Verification and soundness

The certificate contains the output lotteries, \(p,u\), and the numbers \(b_t\).

Each party checks:

  • Its claimed bound satisfies \(b_t\le\int v_t\,d\pi_t\).
  • For every grid bundle \(g\), it satisfies \(v_t(g)\le u_t+p\cdot g\).

The grid may be enormous, but these checks are local and finite; local computation is unrestricted.

The parties also check the rational probabilities, public feasibility, nonnegative dual variables, and the public inequality
\((1+\rho)D\le aB\).

For any accepting certificate, the local dual inequalities bound every feasible grid plan by \(D\). Thus
\(\mathrm{OPT}\le(1+\rho)P\le(1+\rho)D\le aB\le aW\).
Therefore the accepted output meets the requested approximation.

For the constructed certificate, acceptance follows from
\((1+\rho)D/B\le(1+\rho)^2/(1-2\rho)\le a\).
This establishes completeness as well as soundness.

2.5. Communication length

Positive public masses and supplies have logarithmic reciprocals bounded by \(O(L)\). Consequently:

  • Grid coordinates have \(O(L+\log(1/\rho))\) bits.
  • Output probabilities have \(O(L+\log(m+\tau)+\log(1/\rho))\) bits.
  • Rounded prices, dual constants, and value bounds have polynomial bit length.
  • There are at most \(m+2\tau\) output atoms and \(m+2\tau\) additional numerical certificate entries.

For fixed \(\varepsilon\), the total communication is therefore
\(O_\varepsilon((m+\tau)(m+1)(L+\log(m+\tau)))\).

3. Relation to the supplied lower bound

The cited paper’s Lemma 10 concerns deterministic allocations to finitely many agents under aggregate supply constraints. Its reduction uses the fact that two agents cannot both receive more than half of the same resource. That restriction does not hold for the separate lotteries constrained only by aggregate expectations here. [Christodoulou, Sgouritsa, and Tang, Lemma 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.07011)

The normalized theorem above establishes a polynomial certificate for the mass-and-lottery problem directly. For the exact stated model, however, the unrestricted private scales already make worst-case communication unbounded.
