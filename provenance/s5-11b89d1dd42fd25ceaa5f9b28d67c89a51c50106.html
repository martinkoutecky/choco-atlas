Partial resolution

I do not settle the unrestricted classification. The decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, and exact optimization has running time
$$ (m^2+1)^\tau(m^3+\tau+L)^{O(1)}. $$

The following additional cases and structural results can be proved:

  • For $\lambda\geq \tfrac12$, exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable.
  • If two sides are singletons, exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable for every $\lambda$.
  • If one side is a singleton and all masses are integers, an integral optimal flow exists whenever the instance is feasible.
  • A forced-allocation rule gives polynomial preprocessing for sufficiently small $\lambda$. Under common rankings, this rule completely determines the feasible flow or proves infeasibility.

All these statements concern the precise scores, strict inequalities, and fractional stability condition in the question. I assume that $K$ is a binary-encoded rational included in $L$.

1. Stability through minimum realized satisfaction

Let
$$ E=T_A\times T_B\times T_C, $$
and write $p_t$ for the mass of type $t$. For a triple $e$ containing $t$, abbreviate its score by $\mu_t(e)$.

Every score belongs to $[0,1]$: when the denominator is positive, the weighted rank numerator lies between zero and that denominator; when it is zero, the score is defined to be one.

For a capacity-feasible flow $x$, define
$$ w_t(x)= \min\left( \{\mu_t(f):f\ni t,\ x_f>0\} \cup \{0:u_t>0\} \right). $$
The second set is $\{0\}$ when $u_t>0$, and is empty otherwise. The set being minimized is nonempty because $p_t>0$.

Lemma 1. For every candidate triple $e\ni t$,
$$ D_t(e)>0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu_t(e)>w_t(x)+\lambda. $$

Proof. Put $h=\mu_t(e)-\lambda$. All summands defining $D_t(e)$ are nonnegative. Thus $D_t(e)>0$ exactly when either positive unmatched mass has satisfaction zero strictly below $h$, or some positive incident allocation has satisfaction strictly below $h$.

These alternatives say precisely that a realized satisfaction value is strictly below $h$. The finite, nonempty set of realized values has minimum $w_t(x)$, so this is equivalent to $w_t(x)<h$. $\square$

Consequently, stability is equivalent to
$$ \forall e\in E,\qquad \exists t\in e:\ \mu_t(e)\leq w_t(x)+\lambda. $$

In particular, if another capacity-feasible flow $y$ satisfies
$$ w_t(y)\geq w_t(x)\qquad\text{for every }t, $$
then stability of $x$ implies stability of $y$. This observation will be used in Section 6.

2. A finite union of rational polytopes

For every type, define
$$ H_t=\{0\}\cup\{\mu_t(e):e\ni t\}. $$
There are at most $m^2+1$ elements in $H_t$.

Call $s\in\prod_t H_t$ valid when
$$ \forall e\in E,\qquad \exists t\in e:\ \mu_t(e)\leq s_t+\lambda. $$

For each valid $s$, let $P(s)$ be the polytope given by:

  • Nonnegativity, all capacity constraints, and total flow $q$.
  • $x_e=0$ if $\mu_t(e)\leq\lambda$ at any endpoint.
  • $x_e=0$ if $\mu_t(e)<s_t$ at any endpoint.
  • $\sum_{e\ni t}x_e=p_t$ whenever $s_t>0$.

Proposition 2. The feasible stable rational flows are exactly the rational points in
$$ \bigcup_{\text{valid }s}P(s). $$

Proof. Given a feasible stable flow, choose $s_t=w_t(x)$. These thresholds belong to $H_t$, and Lemma 1 makes the vector valid. Every positive incident allocation has score at least $s_t$. Also, $s_t>0$ excludes unmatched mass. Thus $x\in P(s)$.

Conversely, suppose $x\in P(s)$. Every positive incident allocation has score at least $s_t$. If $u_t>0$, the saturation constraint implies $s_t=0$. Therefore $w_t(x)\geq s_t$.

For each candidate triple, validity provides an endpoint with
$$ \mu_t(e)\leq s_t+\lambda\leq w_t(x)+\lambda. $$
Lemma 1 gives $D_t(e)=0$, establishing stability. $\square$

Each $P(s)$ is bounded because every entry is bounded by its endpoints’ masses. Its constraints and objective are rational. Thus every nonempty $P(s)$ has an optimal rational vertex, and the finite union has an attained rational optimum whenever it is nonempty.

The linear-programming theorem used here is the following: a linear program with binary-encoded rational coefficients can be tested for feasibility and optimized in time polynomial in its encoding length; when feasible and bounded, its exact rational optimal value and a rational optimal point can be returned.

There are at most $(m^2+1)^\tau$ threshold vectors and at most $m^3$ variables per program. Scores, side totals, thresholds, and objective coefficients have polynomial encoding length in $m,\tau,L$. This proves the exact running-time bound
$$ (m^2+1)^\tau(m^3+\tau+L)^{O(1)}. $$
Since $m\leq\tau$ and $L$ includes the supported types, this is also
$$ 2^{O(\tau\log\tau)}L^{O(1)}. $$

3. Sparse optima and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$

Proposition 3. Every feasible instance has an optimal stable flow with at most $\tau-2$ positive entries.

Proof. Choose an optimal vertex of a threshold polytope attaining the global optimum. Let $R$ be its positive support, and let $M_R$ be the type–triple incidence matrix restricted to $R$.

Every column contains one endpoint from each side. Hence the rows satisfy the two independent dependencies
$$ \sum_{t\in T_A}(M_R)_{t,*} - \sum_{t\in T_B}(M_R)_{t,*}=0, $$
$$ \sum_{t\in T_A}(M_R)_{t,*} - \sum_{t\in T_C}(M_R)_{t,*}=0. $$
Their coefficient vectors are independent because the sides are disjoint and nonempty. Thus
$$ \operatorname{rank}(M_R)\leq\tau-2. $$

If $|R|>\tau-2$, there is a nonzero direction $d$ supported on $R$ with $M_Rd=0$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both $x+\varepsilon d$ and $x-\varepsilon d$ are nonnegative. They preserve all marginals, total flow, and prescribed zero entries, so both belong to the same threshold polytope. This contradicts extremality. $\square$

We also need a polynomial bit bound, rather than sparsity alone.

Let $r=|R|\leq\tau-2$. The active marginal constraints and total-flow equation have rank $r$ on these coordinates. Otherwise a nonzero direction preserving those equations would admit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions: positivity would persist, and inactive inequalities would retain their positive slack. This would again contradict extremality.

Select $r$ independent equations, written
$$ Fx=b. $$
The square matrix $F$ has entries in $\{0,1\}$, and every column has at most four ones: three marginal entries and one total-flow entry. Hadamard’s inequality gives
$$ |\det F|\leq 2^r. $$

Let $d$ be the product of the input masses’ denominators. Its bit length is $O(L)$. The entries of $db$ are integers of bit length $O(L+\log\tau)$; this includes $dq$, because $q$ is one side’s total mass.

By Cramer’s rule,
$$ x_\ell=\frac{\det F^{(\ell)}}{d\det F}, $$
where $F^{(\ell)}$ replaces column $\ell$ by $db$. Hadamard’s inequality also gives
$$ |\det F^{(\ell)}| \leq 2^{r-1}\sqrt r\,\max_j|(db)_j|. $$
Thus every positive allocation has $O(L+\tau)$ bits.

A yes-instance therefore has a certificate of
$$ O\bigl(\tau(L+\tau+\log m)\bigr) $$
bits: list the positive triples and their rational allocations. Exact rational arithmetic verifies capacities, total flow, admissibility, and cost. Computing the $w_t$ values and applying Lemma 1 over all candidate triples verifies stability.

Verification takes
$$ (m^3+\tau+L)^{O(1)} $$
bit time. Hence the stated decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

4. Polynomial optimization for $\lambda\geq\tfrac12$

Theorem 4. If $\lambda\geq\tfrac12$, every admissible capacity-feasible flow of total mass $q$ is stable.

Proof. Choose a side $S$ with $N_S=q$. Every type on that side is saturated, since its nonnegative unmatched masses sum to
$$ N_S-q=0. $$

For a candidate triple $e$, let $t$ be its endpoint on $S$. Every positive incident allocation $f$ satisfies $\mu_t(f)>\lambda$, whereas
$$ \mu_t(e)-\lambda \leq 1-\lambda \leq\lambda <\mu_t(f). $$
No positive allocation contributes to $D_t(e)$, and $u_t=0$. Therefore $D_t(e)=0$. $\square$

Optimization consequently requires one rational linear program: capacity and total-flow constraints, with inadmissible triples fixed to zero. Its bit running time is
$$ (m^3+\tau+L)^{O(1)}. $$

5. Polynomial optimization when two sides are singletons

This case permits an exact algorithm throughout the threshold range, including $\lambda<\tfrac12$.

Suppose, after renaming sides if necessary, that
$$ T_A=\{A\},\qquad T_B=\{B\},\qquad |T_C|=m. $$
Write their masses as $a,b,c_k$, and put
$$ z_k=x_{ABk},\qquad \alpha_k=\mu_A(B,k),\qquad \beta_k=\mu_B(A,k). $$
The type on side $C$ has two singleton other sides, so
$$ \mu_k(A,B)=1. $$

Every flow of total mass $q$ has
$$ u_A=a-q,\qquad u_B=b-q. $$
Moreover,
$$ D_k(A,B)=c_k-z_k, $$
because its allocated satisfaction is one and its unmatched satisfaction is zero.

Enumerate
$$ s_A\in\{0\}\cup\{\alpha_k:k\in T_C\}, \qquad s_B\in\{0\}\cup\{\beta_k:k\in T_C\}. $$
If $a>q$, retain only $s_A=0$; if $b>q$, retain only $s_B=0$.

For a retained pair of thresholds, permit coordinate $k$ only if
$$ \alpha_k>\lambda,\qquad \beta_k>\lambda, \qquad \alpha_k\geq s_A,\qquad \beta_k\geq s_B. $$
Set its upper bound to $c_k$ when permitted, and to zero otherwise.

Set its lower bound to $c_k$ when
$$ \alpha_k>s_A+\lambda \quad\text{and}\quad \beta_k>s_B+\lambda, $$
and to zero otherwise. Require
$$ \sum_k z_k=q. $$

Proposition 5. The union of these bounded linear systems is exactly the stable feasible set.

Proof. Given a stable flow, choose its actual thresholds $s_A=w_A(x)$ and $s_B=w_B(x)$. They occur in the enumerated sets; positive unmatched mass forces the corresponding threshold to zero.

Every positive coordinate satisfies the permission conditions. If both displayed strict inequalities hold for $k$, Lemma 1 gives $D_A(B,k)>0$ and $D_B(A,k)>0$. Stability then requires $D_k(A,B)=0$, so $z_k=c_k$.

Conversely, take a point in one enumerated system. The permission conditions and the treatment of unmatched singleton types imply
$$ w_A(x)\geq s_A,\qquad w_B(x)\geq s_B. $$
For any candidate $k$, either its lower bound forces $z_k=c_k$, giving $D_k(A,B)=0$, or one of
$$ \alpha_k\leq s_A+\lambda,\qquad \beta_k\leq s_B+\lambda $$
holds. Lemma 1 then gives zero dissatisfaction at that singleton endpoint. $\square$

The cost coefficient of $z_k$ is
$$ h_k=1-\frac{\alpha_k+\beta_k+1}{3}. $$
Sort coordinates once by increasing $h_k$. For each threshold pair, feasibility is equivalent to
$$ \sum_k \ell_k\leq q\leq\sum_k v_k, $$
where $\ell_k,v_k$ are its lower and upper bounds. Starting from the lower bounds, allocate the remaining mass in the sorted order, up to each upper bound.

This minimizes cost: moving any available positive amount from a higher-cost coordinate to a lower-cost coordinate with spare capacity cannot increase cost. Repeatedly making these exchanges yields the greedy allocation.

There are at most $(m+1)^2$ threshold pairs and $O(m)$ arithmetic operations per pair after sorting. Hence exact optimization takes
$$ O\bigl(m^3L^{O(1)}\bigr) $$
bit time, with $\tau=m+2$.

6. Integrality when one side is a singleton

Theorem 6. Suppose one side is a singleton and all masses are integers. For every feasible stable rational flow $x$, there is an integral feasible stable flow $y$ with
$$ C(y)\leq C(x). $$

Proof. Suppose $T_A=\{A\}$. Regard $x_{Ajk}$ as a bipartite transportation flow between $T_B$ and $T_C$.

Construct a bounded transportation polytope by:

  • allowing only coordinates positive in $x$;
  • retaining all capacities and total flow $q$;
  • requiring full saturation of every type in $T_B\cup T_C$ that is saturated in $x$.

The point $x$ belongs to this polytope.

Its vertices are integral. To verify this directly, represent it as a circulation network with arcs from a source to $T_B$, from $T_B$ to $T_C$, from $T_C$ to a sink, and an arc from sink to source fixed at $q$. All lower and upper bounds are integers. Saturated types have their corresponding source or sink arc fixed at their integer mass.

If a vertex circulation had a nonintegral arc, consider the undirected subgraph consisting of its nonintegral arcs. No incident vertex can have degree one in this subgraph: conservation would express that single nonintegral arc value as an integer combination of integral arc values. Thus this nonempty finite subgraph contains a cycle.

All its arc values lie strictly between their integer bounds. A sufficiently small circulation perturbation around the cycle is therefore possible in both directions, contradicting extremality. Hence every vertex is integral.

Minimize the original linear cost over this polytope and choose an integral optimal vertex $y$. Then $C(y)\leq C(x)$.

For any type saturated in $x$, saturation is preserved in $y$, and every positive incident coordinate of $y$ was positive in $x$. Its minimum realized satisfaction cannot decrease. A type unmatched in $x$ has $w_t(x)=0$, so its minimum also cannot decrease.

For the singleton $A$, unmatched mass is always $a-q$, independent of the allocation. If it is positive, both minima are zero; otherwise support containment again prevents a decrease. Thus
$$ w_t(y)\geq w_t(x)\qquad\text{for every }t. $$
Lemma 1 preserves stability, and support containment preserves admissibility. $\square$

Applying this theorem to an attained optimum proves the existence of an integral optimum.

More generally, if $d$ clears all mass denominators, scaling masses and allocations by $d$ preserves stability and scales cost by $d$. Consequently, with one singleton side, a feasible instance has an optimum whose allocations are integer multiples of $1/d$.

This integrality result does not yet classify the optimization problem with exactly one singleton side: selecting a stable support remains unresolved here.

7. Forced allocations for small $\lambda$

The next result provides a reduction for arbitrary rankings and a complete algorithm under common rankings.

For a side $S$, let its alphabetically ordered other sides be $U,V$, and define
$$ d_S=\gamma_1(|U|-1)+\gamma_2(|V|-1). $$
Define
$$ \delta= \min\left( \{1\} \cup \left\{ \frac{\gamma_1}{d_S}:d_S>0,\ |U|>1 \right\} \cup \left\{ \frac{\gamma_2}{d_S}:d_S>0,\ |V|>1 \right\} \right), $$
where both indexed sets range over the three sides. Thus $\delta>0$.

For $d_S>0$, the score can be written
$$ \mu_t(u,v)= \frac{ \gamma_1(|U|-1-r_t(u)) + \gamma_2(|V|-1-r_t(v)) }{d_S}. $$
It follows that every positive score is at least $\delta$.

Also, if one partner pair is componentwise no worse than another and is strictly better in at least one ranking, its score exceeds the other score by at least $\delta$. This statement concerns componentwise comparison; it does not assert a minimum gap between arbitrary scores.

7.1. A forced-allocation rule

Assume
$$ 0\leq\lambda<\delta. $$

Maintain residual masses by subtracting allocations already forced, and discard types whose residual mass is zero. Scores and ranks retain their original definitions.

A remaining triple is mutually first if each endpoint ranks its two partners first within their respective remaining sides.

Proposition 7. Suppose earlier allocations were obtained by repeatedly applying this rule. If a mutually first remaining triple $e$ has positive scores at all endpoints, every stable extension must allocate
$$ x_e=\min_{t\in e}p'_t, $$
where $p'_t$ is the residual mass.

Proof. Fix an endpoint $t$ of $e$.

Every earlier forced allocation incident to $t$ used its first available partner on each other side. The present partners were still available then. Thus its earlier satisfaction was at least $\mu_t(e)$, and contributes nothing to $D_t(e)$.

Among remaining triples incident to $t$, every triple other than $e$ has a strictly worse partner in at least one ranking and no better partner in either. Its score is therefore at most
$$ \mu_t(e)-\delta<\mu_t(e)-\lambda. $$
All its positive allocation contributes to $D_t(e)$.

Since $\mu_t(e)>0$, we have $\mu_t(e)\geq\delta>\lambda$, so positive unmatched mass contributes as well. Consequently,
$$ D_t(e) = u_t+\sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\text{ remaining},\ f\ne e}}x_f = p'_t-x_e. $$
Stability gives
$$ \min_{t\in e}(p'_t-x_e)=0. $$
Capacities give $x_e\leq\min_{t\in e}p'_t$. Combining these statements proves the formula. $\square$

Each application exhausts at least one type. Hence there are at most $\tau-2$ applications.

The rule gives an exact residual reduction. For a candidate containing only remaining types, previous allocations contribute no dissatisfaction, by the componentwise comparison above. Its original dissatisfaction quantities therefore equal those calculated from residual masses and allocations.

For a candidate containing an exhausted type, choose the endpoint exhausted earliest. It is fully saturated. During every allocation incident to it, the candidate’s other endpoints were still available, so its allocated satisfaction was at least its candidate satisfaction. Its dissatisfaction is zero.

Thus a residual flow is stable exactly when its extension by the forced allocations is stable. Admissibility is preserved, and cost differs by the known constant contributed by those allocations. The residual system uses original scores; denominators are not recomputed after deleting types.

A direct implementation scanning triples at each step takes
$$ O\bigl(\tau m^3L^{O(1)}\bigr) $$
bit time.

7.2. Common rankings give a complete algorithm

Assume there is a strict order on each side such that every type on either other side ranks that side in this same order. Call these common rankings.

Continue to assume $\lambda<\delta$.

Take the first remaining type on each side. Their triple is mutually first.

If all its endpoint scores are positive, Proposition 7 forces the smallest residual mass onto it. Subtract that amount and repeat.

If its score is zero at an endpoint on side $S$, both partners are their original worst choices, with singleton sides interpreted as having their sole choice. Since these are the first remaining types in the common orders, their sides have no other remaining types.

Every residual triple therefore has score zero at its endpoint on $S$: all types on $S$ share the same rankings. Every residual triple is inadmissible. If the allocated total is still below $q$, no feasible extension exists.

Otherwise, continue until total allocated mass is $q$. The forced-allocation reduction proves stability of the resulting flow. Every feasible stable flow must have made each of the same allocations, so the resulting flow is unique.

Theorem 8. Under common rankings and $\lambda<\delta$, the instance is either infeasible or has exactly one feasible stable flow. Exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable, with bit running time
$$ O\bigl((\tau m+\tau)L^{O(1)}\bigr). $$

Checking common rankings requires $O(\tau m)$ list comparisons. Maintaining the three current heads and subtracting masses requires at most $\tau-2$ allocation steps. All arithmetic has polynomial bit length because residual masses are differences of input masses and their sums.

In particular, this theorem applies to every common-ranking instance with $\lambda=0$, regardless of side sizes, masses, or positive global weights.

8. What remains unresolved

The proofs above leave open the general decision problem for
$$ 0\leq\lambda<\tfrac12 $$
with unrestricted rankings.

The singleton integrality theorem removes a fractional-rounding obstruction in that restricted case, but a hardness reduction would still need to preserve the rank-derived scores, global weights, singleton’s combination preferences, admissibility, and cost.

The cited $\mathrm{PPAD}$-completeness theorem establishes hardness of finding stable fractional matchings in a general hypergraphic preference system of maximum degree three. It does not supply the required reduction for this cost decision problem. [Ishizuka and Kamiyama, official paper record](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11)

Accordingly, no polynomial-time algorithm or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness classification for the unrestricted problem is claimed here.
