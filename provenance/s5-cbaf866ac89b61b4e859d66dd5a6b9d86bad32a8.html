The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(d\) rational, finitely supported probability measures \(\nu_i=\sum_{x\in X_i}w_{i,x}\delta_x\) on \(\mathbb{Q}^d\), with binary-encoded coordinates and weights and \(\sum_x w_{i,x}=1\), find a rational hyperplane \(H=\{z:a\cdot z=b\}\), \(a\ne0\), and boundary allocations \(r_{i,x}\in[0,w_{i,x}]\) for \(x\in H\), such that for every \(i\), \(\sum_{a\cdot x>b}w_{i,x}+\sum_{a\cdot x=b}r_{i,x}=1/2\).

1. Boundary allocation is equivalent to two mass inequalities

For coefficients $z=(a,b)$ with $a\ne0$, define
$$ M_i^+(z)=\sum_{x\in X_i:a\cdot x>b}w_{i,x}, \qquad M_i^-(z)=\sum_{x\in X_i:a\cdot x<b}w_{i,x}, $$
and
$$ M_i^0(z)=1-M_i^+(z)-M_i^-(z). $$

Lemma 1. The required boundary allocations exist precisely when
$$ M_i^+(z)\le\frac12 \quad\text{and}\quad M_i^-(z)\le\frac12 $$
for every ingredient $i$.

Proof. The boundary must supply exactly
$$ R_i=\frac12-M_i^+(z). $$
This is possible if and only if
$$ 0\le R_i\le M_i^0(z). $$
The first inequality is equivalent to $M_i^+(z)\le1/2$. The second is equivalent to
$$ \frac12-M_i^+(z)\le1-M_i^+(z)-M_i^-(z), $$
which is $M_i^-(z)\le1/2$.

For sufficiency, process the boundary points in any order, assigning the smaller of their mass and the remaining amount $R_i$. Since the available boundary mass is at least $R_i$, the remaining amount becomes zero. Every allocation lies in its required interval.

This construction uses rational arithmetic. It is performed separately for each ingredient, so shared locations cause no conflict. $\square$

Call coefficients satisfying these inequalities a valid cut.

2. Integer representation and encoding bounds

Index support occurrences by
$$ \Omega=\{(i,x):1\le i\le m,\ x\in X_i\}. $$
Thus occurrences at the same location in different ingredients remain distinct.

Put $n=m+1$. For each occurrence $\omega=(i,x)$, let $Q_\omega$ be the product of the positive denominators of the coordinates of $x$, and set
$$ c_\omega=Q_\omega(x_1,\ldots,x_m,-1)\in\mathbb Z^n. $$
For $z=(a,b)$,
$$ c_\omega\cdot z=Q_\omega(a\cdot x-b). $$
Because $Q_\omega>0$, this preserves the sign of the hyperplane evaluation.

All these rows can be computed in polynomial time. Their entries satisfy the conservative bound
$$ |(c_\omega)_j|\le H, \qquad H=2^{2L+2}. $$
Indeed, the bit length of a product of coordinate denominators is bounded by their aggregate encoding length, and multiplying by a coordinate numerator adds at most its encoding length.

Let $C$ be the matrix containing these $\tau$ rows. Its rank can be computed by exact rational linear algebra in $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time.

3. The weighted problem belongs to $\mathrm{PPA}$

3.1. An odd piecewise linear circuit

Define
$$ N=n+1,\qquad D=(NH)^N,\qquad \delta=\frac1{2nD}. $$
These constants have polynomial encoding length; specifically,
$$ \log D=O\bigl(m(L+\log(m+2))\bigr). $$

Let
$$ h(t)=\max\{-1,\min\{1,t\}\}, $$
and define $F:\mathbb R^{m+1}\to\mathbb R^m$ by
$$ F_i(y)=\sum_{x\in X_i} w_{i,x}\, h\!\left(\frac{c_{(i,x)}\cdot y}{\delta}\right). $$
The function $h$ is continuous, piecewise linear, and odd. Consequently $F$ has these properties as well.

The circuit for $F$ uses rational constants, addition, multiplication by constants, minimum, and maximum. It has $O(m\tau)$ gates and polynomial total encoding length. In particular, the binary weights are used directly; they are not expanded into repeated points.

We use the following established theorem: for a linear arithmetic circuit $G:\mathbb R^{m+1}\to\mathbb R^m$, finding an exact antipodal equality on the sphere
$$ \{y:\|y\|_1=1\} $$
belongs to $\mathrm{PPA}$. Its reduction recovers a rational solution of polynomial encoding length. The permitted circuit operations are exactly sufficient for the circuit above. [Deligkas, Fearnley, Melissourgos, and Spirakis, Section 3.1 and Lemma 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1903.03101).

Thus a $\mathrm{PPA}$ reduction supplies a polynomial-length rational point $y$ with
$$ \|y\|_1=1, \qquad F(y)=F(-y). $$
Oddness gives
$$ F(y)=0. $$
Polynomial output length explicitly ensures that the following classification and arithmetic can be performed in polynomial time.

3.2. Mass information at the continuous zero

Partition the occurrence set into
$$ P=\{\omega:c_\omega\cdot y>\delta\}, $$
$$ T=\{\omega:c_\omega\cdot y<-\delta\}, $$
and
$$ E=\{\omega:|c_\omega\cdot y|\le\delta\}. $$
These are sets of ingredient-indexed occurrences, not merely sets of locations.

For each occurrence $(i,x)$, define
$$ q_{i,x}= \frac{w_{i,x}}2 \left( 1+h\!\left(\frac{c_{(i,x)}\cdot y}{\delta}\right) \right). $$
Since $h$ takes values in $[-1,1]$,
$$ 0\le q_{i,x}\le w_{i,x}. $$
Using normalization of the ingredient masses and $F_i(y)=0$ gives
$$ \sum_{x\in X_i}q_{i,x} = \frac12\sum_{x\in X_i}w_{i,x}+\frac12F_i(y) = \frac12. $$

On $P$, $q_{i,x}=w_{i,x}$; on $T$, $q_{i,x}=0$. Therefore
$$ \sum_{x\in X_i:(i,x)\in P}w_{i,x}\le\frac12. $$
Also,
$$ \sum_{x\in X_i}(w_{i,x}-q_{i,x})=\frac12. $$
On $T$, the summand is $w_{i,x}$, so
$$ \sum_{x\in X_i:(i,x)\in T}w_{i,x}\le\frac12. $$

The occurrences in $E$ must now be placed exactly on a hyperplane. Simply treating $y$ as the desired coefficients would not justify an exact cut.

3.3. Exact recovery by a determinant gap

Because $\|y\|_1=1$, choose a coordinate $j$ with
$$ |y_j|\ge\frac1n, $$
and let $s$ be its sign.

Consider the following rational linear program in $z\in\mathbb R^n$ and $t\in\mathbb R$:
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize }&t\\ \text{subject to }& z_j=s,\\ &-n\le z_k\le n &&(1\le k\le n),\\ &0\le t\le n^2H,\\ &c_\omega\cdot z\ge0 &&(\omega\in P),\\ &c_\omega\cdot z\le0 &&(\omega\in T),\\ &-t\le c_\omega\cdot z\le t &&(\omega\in E). \end{aligned} $$

Its feasible region is bounded and nonempty. A feasible witness is
$$ z=\frac{y}{|y_j|}, \qquad t=n\delta. $$
The normalization constraint holds, all coordinates have magnitude at most $n$, and every evaluation on $E$ has magnitude at most $n\delta$. Hence the optimum satisfies
$$ 0\le t^*\le n\delta=\frac1{2D}. $$

Claim. The optimum is $t^*=0$.

Proof. There are $N=n+1$ variables. All constraint coefficients and right-hand sides are integers, and every constraint coefficient has magnitude at most $H$.

A linear objective on a nonempty bounded polytope attains its minimum at a vertex. At a vertex, the tight constraint normals span the entire variable space: otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to them would permit sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality. Thus an optimal vertex is determined by $N$ independent tight constraints.

Let $A$ be their integer coefficient matrix. Hadamard’s inequality gives
$$ |\det A|\le(\sqrt N\,H)^N\le D. $$
Cramer’s rule expresses the vertex’s $t$-coordinate as a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator of magnitude at most $D$. If this coordinate were positive, it would therefore be at least $1/D$. This contradicts the upper bound $1/(2D)$. Hence $t^*=0$. $\square$

Choose an optimal rational vertex $z=(a,b)$. Every occurrence in $E$ now has evaluation zero. The remaining constraints imply
$$ \{\omega:c_\omega\cdot z>0\}\subseteq P, \qquad \{\omega:c_\omega\cdot z<0\}\subseteq T. $$
The inequalities from Section 3.2 therefore show that both strict-side masses are at most $1/2$ for every ingredient.

Moreover, $a\ne0$. If $a=0$, the equation $z_j=s$ makes $z$ nonzero and hence $b\ne0$. Every support point would then have evaluation $-b$, placing all mass of every ingredient strictly on one side. That contradicts the strict-side mass bound.

Lemma 1 supplies the boundary allocations.

3.4. Complexity and polynomial-length certificates

4. A finite candidate lemma

The following geometric lemma gives a simpler enumeration algorithm and the new parameter bound.

4.1. Rank-deficient inputs

If
$$ \operatorname{rank}C<n, $$
compute any nonzero rational $z=(a,b)$ in $\ker C$.

Then every support point lies on the resulting hyperplane. Also $a\ne0$: if $a=0$, any row equation gives
$$ -Q_\omega b=0, $$
forcing $b=0$ and contradicting $z\ne0$.

The allocations
$$ r_{i,x}=\frac{w_{i,x}}2 $$
solve the problem. This construction is polynomial-time.

Hence only $\operatorname{rank}C=n$ requires enumeration.

4.2. Preserving validity while adding boundary equations

Lemma 2. Suppose $\operatorname{rank}C=n$. From any valid cut $u=(a,b)$, there exists another valid cut $u'$ whose boundary rows have rank $n-1=m$. Every occurrence on the boundary of $u$ remains on the boundary of $u'$.

Proof. For each occurrence not on the original boundary, set
$$ \sigma_\omega=\operatorname{sign}(c_\omega\cdot u). $$
Consider coefficient vectors satisfying
$$ c_\omega\cdot z=0 \quad\text{when }c_\omega\cdot u=0, $$
and
$$ \sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot z\ge0 \quad\text{otherwise}. $$
Any nonzero vector satisfying these constraints retains every original boundary occurrence and cannot move an occurrence to the opposite strict side. Thus each strict-side occurrence set is a subset of its original counterpart. Lemma 1 shows that such a vector remains a valid cut; its normal is nonzero by the same all-mass-on-one-side argument used above.

Start at $z=u$. Let $J$ be its current set of zero-evaluation rows, and put
$$ r=\operatorname{rank}\{c_\omega:\omega\in J\}. $$
If $r=n-1$, stop.

Otherwise the subspace
$$ W=\{v:c_\omega\cdot v=0\text{ for every }\omega\in J\} $$
has dimension $n-r\ge2$. Since $z\in W$, choose $v\in W$ linearly independent of $z$.

The rows of $C$ span $\mathbb R^n$, so some row has $c_\omega\cdot v\ne0$. Such a row is outside $J$. Reverse the sign of $v$ if necessary so that, for at least one such occurrence,
$$ \sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot v<0. $$

For every occurrence outside $J$,
$$ \sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot z>0. $$
Consequently the number
$$ \alpha= \min_{\substack{\omega\notin J\\ \sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot v<0}} \frac{\sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot z} {-\sigma_\omega c_\omega\cdot v} $$
exists and is strictly positive.

Set
$$ z'=z+\alpha v. $$
All current zero rows remain zero. For a row whose signed evaluation along $v$ is negative, the definition of $\alpha$ prevents its signed evaluation at $z'$ from becoming negative. All other signed evaluations also remain nonnegative. At least one additional row becomes zero.

Moreover, a newly zero row realizing the minimum has nonzero evaluation on $v$. It cannot lie in the span of the current zero rows, since every row in that span annihilates $v$. Thus the zero-row rank increases.

Finally, $z'\ne0$ because $z$ and $v$ are linearly independent. The zero-row rank of a nonzero vector can never exceed $n-1$. Repeating this operation therefore terminates with rank exactly $n-1$, while preserving validity and every original boundary equation. $\square$

Consequence. In the full-rank case, a solution exists whose hyperplane passes through $m$ affinely independent support locations.

Indeed, select $m$ independent boundary rows from Lemma 2. Their independence is equivalent to affine independence of the corresponding locations, because each row is a nonzero scalar multiple of $(x,-1)$.

5. Exact enumeration algorithms

5.1. Enumeration of $m$ support occurrences

First handle the rank-deficient case as in Section 4.1.

In the full-rank case, enumerate all $m$-element subsets of $\Omega$. For each subset:

  • Reject it if its rows have rank less than $m$.
  • Otherwise compute a nonzero rational vector $z$ in their one-dimensional kernel.
  • Compute both strict-side masses of every ingredient.
  • If all are at most $1/2$, construct the allocations using Lemma 1 and return.

The kernel vector has nonzero normal: it annihilates at least one support row, so a zero normal would also force its intercept to vanish. Either orientation may be used, since the validity test treats both strict sides symmetrically.

Existence from Section 3 and Lemma 2 guarantees that some enumerated subset succeeds. All steps for a candidate take polynomial time. The total running time is
$$ \left(1+\binom{\tau}{m}\right)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L), $$
and hence also
$$ (\tau+1)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

For an explicit coefficient bound, an integer $m\times(m+1)$ candidate matrix $A$ of rank $m$ has a nonzero kernel vector with coordinates
$$ z_j=(-1)^j\det A_{\widehat j}, $$
where $A_{\widehat j}$ deletes column $j$. Laplace expansion gives $Az=0$, and rank $m$ guarantees that at least one coordinate is nonzero. Hadamard’s inequality bounds every coordinate by
$$ (\sqrt m\,H)^m. $$
Thus candidate coefficients have polynomial bit length.

This enumeration includes degenerate inputs directly; it requires no strict-inequality feasibility tests.

5.2. Fixed-parameter tractability in $\tau-m$

Put
$$ k=\tau-m=\sum_{i=1}^m(\tau_i-1). $$
Let $t$ be the number of ingredients with more than one support point. Since each such ingredient contributes at least one to this sum,
$$ t\le k. $$
The number $q$ of occurrences belonging to these ingredients is
$$ q=\sum_{i:\tau_i>1}\tau_i=t+k\le2k. $$

An ingredient with one support point assigns that point mass $1$. Every valid cut must place it on the boundary: placing it strictly on either side would violate Lemma 1.

Choose a basis $\mathcal B$ for the span of all singleton-ingredient rows, and let its size be $r$. As before, handle $\operatorname{rank}C<n$ immediately. Assume henceforth that $C$ has full rank.

By Lemma 2, a valid cut exists with zero-row rank $m$, retaining all singleton rows. Its zero-row basis can be chosen to contain $\mathcal B$ and then extended by $m-r$ rows from non-singleton ingredients. To see this, start with $\mathcal B$ and extend it within the complete zero-row set; singleton rows add no rank because they already lie in its span.

Therefore it suffices to enumerate the $(m-r)$-element subsets of the $q$ non-singleton occurrences. Adjoin each subset to $\mathcal B$, reject dependent choices, and test the resulting kernel vector as above.

The number of candidates is bounded by
$$ \binom q{m-r}\le2^q\le4^k. $$
Each candidate has polynomial processing cost. Thus the running time is
$$ 4^{\tau-m}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

This proves fixed-parameter tractability in the excess support size $\tau-m$. It also yields polynomial time whenever $\tau-m=O(\log L)$.

5.3. Another polynomial-time special case

Suppose every ingredient has an occurrence of mass at least $1/2$. Choose such a location $p_i$ for each ingredient.

The $m$ homogeneous equations
$$ a\cdot p_i-b=0 \qquad(1\le i\le m) $$
in $m+1$ unknowns have a nonzero rational solution. Its normal is nonzero, since a zero normal would force $b=0$.

For each ingredient, all mass outside its chosen boundary occurrence totals at most $1/2$. Consequently each strict-side mass is at most $1/2$. Lemma 1 supplies the allocations.

This gives a $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ algorithm for this special case. In particular, it covers every instance with $\tau_i\le2$ for all $i$.

6. What $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness would imply

Specify the search-oracle convention as follows: a deterministic polynomial-time reduction must produce the correct decision for every valid choice of oracle solutions.

The polynomial certificate bound established above permits restriction to polynomial-length solutions. This restriction suffices for the argument: a reduction correct for every valid solution is correct for these solutions in particular. Such solutions can be verified exactly in polynomial time by checking rational signs, allocation bounds, and ingredient mass equalities.

Suppose a polynomial-time oracle machine $M$ decides a language $A$ using this search problem.

A certificate for $x\in A$ can give the complete sequence of polynomial-length oracle answers encountered while simulating $M(x)$. A verifier:

  • simulates $M$ and its adaptively generated queries;
  • checks each supplied answer;
  • requires repeated queries to receive identical answers;
  • accepts if the simulation accepts.

If $x\in A$, totality supplies valid polynomial-length answers to every query, and correctness makes the simulation accept. If $x\notin A$, no valid transcript can make it accept.

Requiring consistent answers for repeated queries ensures that a transcript agrees with a solution-selection oracle. Totality extends that selector to any unqueried instances. Thus correctness for every valid oracle applies to every accepted transcript.

The same construction, accepting when the simulation rejects, certifies $x\notin A$. Consequently
$$ A\in\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}. $$

In particular, if such a reduction decides $\mathrm{SAT}$, then
$$ \mathrm{UNSAT}\in\mathrm{NP}. $$
Since $\mathrm{UNSAT}$ is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete, this gives $\mathrm{coNP}\subseteq\mathrm{NP}$; taking complements gives the reverse inclusion. Therefore
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

This is a necessary consequence of the proposed hardness, not a proof that hardness is impossible.
