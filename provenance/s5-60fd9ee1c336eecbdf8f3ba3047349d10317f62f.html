The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), complete diminishing-marginal utility laws \(u_{t,j,\ell}\), caps \(c_t\), and \(\varepsilon\), compute a finite-support feasible mass allocation \(\lambda_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\) achieving the paper's NSW approximation \( (1+\varepsilon)e^{e^{-1/(1+4\varepsilon)}} \) and satisfying, for every uncapped supported \((t,a)\) and supported \((s,b)\), \(u_t(b-e_j)\le(2+4\varepsilon)u_t(a)\) for some \(j\) with \(b_j>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) goods. Input includes positive integer multiplicity bounds \(K_j\), binary-rational per-capita supplies \(0\le q_j\le K_j\), positive integer caps \(c_t\), and binary-rational \(0<\varepsilon\le1/4\). Complete marginal-utility tables are explicitly listed and satisfy \(c_t\ge u_{t,j,1}\ge\cdots\ge u_{t,j,K_j}\ge0\), with integer entries.
Admissible bundles are \(B=\prod_{j=1}^m\{0,\ldots,K_j\}\). Define \(u_t(b)=\sum_{j=1}^m\sum_{\ell=1}^{b_j}u_{t,j,\ell}\) and \(\bar u_t(b)=\min\{c_t,u_t(b)\}\).
A feasible mass allocation consists of nonnegative rational masses \(\lambda_{t,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_{b\in B}\lambda_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}b_j=q_j\). Define \(\mathrm{NSW}(\lambda)=\exp\bigl(\sum_{\lambda_{t,b}>0}\lambda_{t,b}\log\bar u_t(b)\bigr)\), taking it to be zero if any positively supported bundle has zero capped utility; let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be its maximum over all feasible allocations, without fairness constraints.
Require \(\mathrm{NSW}(\lambda)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\rho(\varepsilon)\), where \(\rho(\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon)e^{e^{-1/(1+4\varepsilon)}}\). Also require that whenever \(\lambda_{t,a}>0\), \(u_t(a)<c_t\), and \(\lambda_{s,b}>0\) with \(b\ne0\), some \(j\) with \(b_j>0\) satisfies \(u_t(b-e_j)\le(2+4\varepsilon)u_t(a)\), where \(e_j\) is the unit vector; comparisons with empty bundles are automatically satisfied.
Does computing a finite-support feasible allocation with both guarantees admit an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,\sum_jK_j,L,1/\varepsilon\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, or is attaining these guarantees NP-hard?
For finitely many agents, a polynomial-time algorithm achieves \(\rho(\varepsilon)\) for original utilities; its allocation satisfies the stated individual guarantee for rounded utilities and caps. [Discrete approximation and fairness results](https://arxiv.org/html/1805.06232).
No result settling this precise mass-allocation approximation problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so supplies are per capita and the NSW exponent needs no additional normalization.
  • Made complete utility laws explicit finite tables, with input bounds K_j defining admissible integral bundles; these bounds are distinct from per-capita supplies.
  • Retained nonnegative integer marginal utilities, positive integer caps, marginal utilities bounded by their type's cap, and the approximation-parameter range from the discrete model.
  • Required exact allocation of supplies, rather than permitting disposal, following the discrete feasibility equations.
  • Defined NSW as the geometric mean of realized capped utilities across allocated mass, rather than the geometric mean of types' expected utilities; defined its zero-utility convention.
  • Used all feasible allocations as the approximation benchmark, without imposing the requested fairness condition on the optimum.
  • Treated empty comparison bundles as automatically satisfying the individual guarantee, avoiding an impossible requirement to remove an item from an empty bundle.
  • Required rational allocation masses and interpreted uncapped support using the given utilities and caps, preserving the graded question's guarantee.

Partial resolution: a polynomial algorithm when caps do not truncate utility

The general question remains unresolved. I prove two additional results:

  • If no cap truncates any admissible bundle, both requested guarantees are attainable in polynomial time, for arbitrary supplies. In fact, the algorithm achieves welfare factor $1+\varepsilon$ and fairness factor $2+\varepsilon$.
  • For one good with arbitrary caps, the best fair welfare is approximable within $1+\varepsilon$ in polynomial time. This reduces that case to a quantitative comparison between the fair and unrestricted optima.

The proofs use the binary rational masses directly, without expanding the society into individual agents. They also supply the explicit feasibility construction and rational linear-programming details requested by the verifier.

1. Definitions and preliminary facts

Write
$$ S=\sum_{j=1}^m K_j $$
and define the prefix utilities
$$ U_{t,j}(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^k u_{t,j,\ell}. $$
Thus
$$ u_t(b)=\sum_j U_{t,j}(b_j). $$

For a positive-welfare allocation, its logarithmic welfare is
$$ F(\lambda)=\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}\log\bar u_t(b). $$
Its welfare is $\exp(F(\lambda))$.

The principal special case considered below is
$$ c_t\ge M_t:=\sum_{j=1}^m U_{t,j}(K_j) \qquad\text{for every }t. $$
Under this hypothesis,
$$ \bar u_t(b)=u_t(b) \qquad\text{for every admissible }b. $$
I call this the nontruncating-cap hypothesis.

1.1 An explicit feasible allocation always exists

Put
$$ d_j=\frac{q_j}{K_j}\in[0,1]. $$
Partition $[0,1]$ at the distinct values among $0,1,d_1,\ldots,d_m$. For each interval $I_h$ of positive length $w_h$, choose an interior point $\theta_h$ and define
$$ b_j^h= \begin{cases} K_j,&\theta_h<d_j,\\ 0,&\theta_h>d_j. \end{cases} $$
Assign mass
$$ \lambda_{t,b^h}=\mu_t w_h, $$
combining equal bundles if necessary.

The type masses are correct because $\sum_h w_h=1$. For every good,
$$ \sum_{t,h}\mu_t w_h b_j^h = K_j d_j = q_j. $$
The construction has at most $\tau(m+1)$ supported pairs. All endpoints and masses are rational with polynomial encoding length, and sorting the breakpoints takes polynomial time.

This explicitly handles any algorithmic branch in which only feasibility is needed.

1.2 Positive welfare is decidable in polynomial time

For each type, define
$$ J_t=\{j:u_{t,j,1}>0\}. $$

Lemma 1. Positive welfare is feasible if and only if there are nonnegative rational numbers $r_{t,j}$, for $j\in J_t$, such that
$$ \sum_{j\in J_t}r_{t,j}=\mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t, $$
and
$$ \sum_{t:j\in J_t}r_{t,j}\le q_j \qquad\text{for every }j. $$

Proof. Suppose an allocation has positive welfare. Every supported bundle has positive uncapped utility. Because the marginal utilities are nonnegative, some included marginal is positive. Because they are nonincreasing, the first marginal of that good is positive.

Select one such good from each supported bundle and assign the bundle’s mass to that good. The resulting $r_{t,j}$ have the required row sums. Each selection of good $j$ comes from a bundle containing at least one copy of $j$, so its column sum is at most $q_j$.

Conversely, initially give mass $r_{t,j}$ of type $t$ the singleton $e_j$. Every such bundle has positive utility. Let
$$ R_j=\sum_t r_{t,j}\le q_j $$
be the initial usage of good $j$.

Fill the residual supply of each good separately. A cell of mass $w$ currently holding $b_j$ copies can absorb quantity
$$ w(K_j-b_j). $$
Before processing good $j$, its aggregate remaining capacity is $K_j-R_j$, which is at least $q_j-R_j$.

Fill whole cells to $K_j$ until either the residual supply is exhausted or it is smaller than the next cell’s capacity. In the latter case, if that cell has remaining capacity $d>0$ per person and the residual quantity is $z<wd$, split off mass $z/d$ and fill that part to $K_j$.

This preserves rationality and positivity of utility. Each good requires at most one split, so the construction has polynomially many cells. All arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity. The final supplies are exact. $\square$

To test the displayed conditions, use a flow network with source-to-type capacities $\mu_t$, type-to-good capacities one for $j\in J_t$, and good-to-sink capacities $q_j$. A flow of value one is equivalent to the conditions: it saturates every source-to-type edge because their capacities sum to one.

Exact rational maximum flow is polynomial-time here. For example, clearing all capacity denominators produces integer capacities of polynomial binary length, after which a standard polynomial-time maximum-flow algorithm applies.

1.3 Attainment and the linear-programming theorem used below

If positive welfare is feasible, forbid all zero-utility type–bundle pairs. The remaining allocation polytope is nonempty and compact: every coordinate lies between zero and its type mass. On this polytope, $F$ is a finite linear function, so it attains its maximum at a vertex. The vertex is rational because all constraints are rational.

Consequently, the unrestricted optimum in the question is attained by a rational allocation. If positive welfare is infeasible, every feasible allocation has welfare zero.

For the algorithms below I use the standard computational theorem:

A linear program with rational coefficients can be solved in time polynomial in its numbers of variables and constraints and in its total coefficient encoding length. If its feasible region is nonempty and its objective is bounded, an optimal rational solution of polynomial encoding length can be produced.

Every algorithmic linear program below has an explicitly polynomial-size rational formulation. None uses logarithms as input coefficients.

2. Main theorem under nontruncating caps

Theorem 2. Under the nontruncating-cap hypothesis, there is an algorithm polynomial in
$$ m,\tau,S,L,1/\varepsilon $$
that outputs an exactly feasible rational allocation satisfying
$$ \mathrm{NSW}(\lambda)\ge\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{1+\varepsilon}. $$
Moreover, for every supported pair $(t,a)$ and every supported pair $(s,b)$ with $b\ne0$, some $j$ with $b_j>0$ satisfies
$$ u_t(b-e_j)\le(2+\varepsilon)u_t(a). $$

Thus the theorem implies both guarantees requested in the question, since
$$ 1+\varepsilon\le\rho(\varepsilon) \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2+\varepsilon\le2+4\varepsilon. $$

The fairness conclusion even applies to owners whose bundles reach their caps.

2.1 A small utility offset

Set
$$ \gamma=\frac{\varepsilon}{8}, \qquad \delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{16mS}, \qquad r=1+\delta. $$
Define the shifted bundle utility
$$ v_t(b)=u_t(b)+\gamma. $$

First use Lemma 1 to decide whether positive welfare is feasible.

  • If it is feasible, allow only bundles with $u_t(b)>0$.
  • If it is infeasible, allow every bundle.

The offset will permit a fairness proof in the second branch even when a supported owner has utility zero. In the first branch, excluding zero-utility bundles ensures positive output welfare.

2.2 Rounded utility states

For each type, use the rational states
$$ D_t=\{\gamma r^h:h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\ \gamma r^h\le\gamma+M_t\}. $$
For $z\in[\gamma,\gamma+M_t]$, let $R_t(z)$ be the largest state not exceeding $z$. Then
$$ \frac zr\le R_t(z)\le z. $$
Indeed, if $R_t(z)=\gamma r^h$, the next geometric value exceeds $z$, including when that next value lies above $\gamma+M_t$.

For a bundle $b$, define
$$ W_0=\gamma, \qquad W_i=R_t\bigl(W_{i-1}+U_{t,i}(b_i)\bigr), \quad 1\le i\le m. $$
Only states reachable from $W_0$ are used. Hence every transition argument lies in the stated domain.

Let
$$ V_i=\gamma+\sum_{j=1}^i U_{t,j}(b_j). $$
Induction gives
$$ \frac{V_i}{r^i}\le W_i\le V_i. $$
For the lower bound, if it holds at $i-1$, nonnegativity of the new increment gives
$$ W_{i-1}+U_{t,i}(b_i) \ge \frac{V_{i-1}+U_{t,i}(b_i)}{r^{i-1}} = \frac{V_i}{r^{i-1}}. $$
Rounding loses at most one further factor $r$. The upper bound follows because rounding never increases its argument.

Writing $W_t(b)=W_m$, we obtain
$$ \frac{v_t(b)}{r^m}\le W_t(b)\le v_t(b). $$

The terminal state equals $\gamma$ exactly when $u_t(b)=0$. To verify this, a path with no positive increments stays at $\gamma$. Any positive increment is an integer at least one, and
$$ \gamma+1>\gamma r. $$
Its transition therefore reaches a state at least $\gamma r$. Subsequent states cannot decrease.

2.3 The polynomial-size flow formulation

For each type, make a layered directed acyclic network.

  • The initial node has state $\gamma$.
  • At layer $i$, each reachable state records $W_i$.
  • From state $w$ at layer $i-1$, each choice $k\in\{0,\ldots,K_i\}$ gives an arc to

$$ R_t\bigl(w+U_{t,i}(k)\bigr). $$

  • Different choices retain distinct arcs even if their endpoints coincide.

Send flow $\mu_t$ through the network and impose conservation at intermediate nodes. Across all type networks, impose the exact supply equations
$$ \sum_{\substack{\text{arcs at}\\\text{layer }j}} k\,f_{\text{arc}}=q_j. $$

If positive welfare is feasible, prohibit the terminal state $\gamma$. Otherwise retain it.

Give terminal state $\gamma r^h$ reward $h$, and maximize the total flow-weighted reward. This is a rational linear program with integer objective coefficients.

Every bundle determines one path, and every path determines one bundle. Thus allocations correspond to network flows, after path decomposition. Feasibility follows from Lemma 1 in the positive branch and from Section 1.1 in the other branch.

The number of states for type $t$ is
$$ O\!\left( 1+\frac{\log(1+M_t/\gamma)}{\delta} \right). $$
This follows from $\log(1+\delta)\ge\delta/2$, since $\delta\le1$. The logarithm of $1+M_t/\gamma$ is polynomially bounded by the input length and $\log S$. Every rational power $\gamma r^h$ has polynomial encoding length because its exponent and the encoding lengths of $\gamma,r$ are polynomially bounded.

Each state has at most $K_i+1$ outgoing arcs at layer $i$. Therefore the formulation and its coefficient encoding length are polynomial in the required parameters.

Decompose an optimal rational flow by repeatedly selecting a positive source-to-terminal path and subtracting its minimum arc flow. Each iteration eliminates at least one positive arc and never restores an eliminated arc. Hence there are polynomially many output paths. Their masses have polynomial encoding length, and all supplies remain exact.

Let $\widehat\lambda$ denote the resulting allocation. It maximizes
$$ G(\lambda)=\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}\log W_t(b) $$
over the permitted bundles: the logarithm of state $\gamma r^h$ is $\log\gamma+h\log r$, and the total mass is one.

3. Why this allocation is fair

The essential point is that optimality of the rounded objective controls transfers between any two supported cells.

3.1 A supported-cell transfer inequality

Let $(t,a)$ and $(s,b)$ be distinct supported pairs. Suppose $b_j>0$, $a_j<K_j$, and both replacement bundles are permitted. Transferring one copy of $j$ on an equal small mass of these cells replaces
$$ (t,a),(s,b) $$
by
$$ (t,a+e_j),(s,b-e_j). $$
This preserves every type mass and every supply. Choosing the transferred mass smaller than both original positive masses preserves nonnegativity, even if some replacement pairs coincide with existing pairs.

Optimality of $G$ consequently gives
$$ \log W_t(a)+\log W_s(b) \ge \log W_t(a+e_j)+\log W_s(b-e_j). $$
Using the pointwise rounding bounds, this implies
$$ v_t(a+e_j)v_s(b-e_j) \le \eta\,v_t(a)v_s(b), \qquad \eta=r^{2m}. $$

Define
$$ X=u_t(a)+\gamma, \qquad Y=u_s(b)+\gamma, $$
and
$$ g_j=u_{t,j,a_j+1}, \qquad d_j=u_{s,j,b_j}. $$
Under the nontruncating-cap hypothesis, the shifted utilities after the transfer are exactly $X+g_j$ and $Y-d_j$. Since $Y-d_j\ge\gamma>0$, the transfer inequality yields
$$ \frac{g_j}{X} \le \frac{d_j+(\eta-1)Y}{Y-d_j}. $$

3.2 Choosing the copy to remove

Fix any supported $(t,a)$ and any supported $(s,b)$ with $b\ne0$. For every $j$ with $b_j>0$, put
$$ d_j=u_{s,j,b_j}. $$
Choose $k$ maximizing $d_j$ among these goods, and write
$$ b'=b-e_k. $$

Nonincreasing marginal utilities imply
$$ \sum_{j:b_j>0}b_jd_j\le u_s(b). $$

Let
$$ J=\{j:b'_j>a_j\}. $$
For $j\in J$, the recipient can accept a copy because
$$ a_j+1\le b'_j\le K_j. $$

We also check that the transfer used in Section 3.1 is permitted. In the zero-optimum branch, every bundle is permitted. In the positive branch, the recipient remains positive. If the donor became zero after removing $j$, then
$$ d_j=u_s(b)>0. $$
The inequality $\sum_\ell b_\ell d_\ell\le u_s(b)$ would force $b_j=1$ and every other $d_\ell$ to be zero. Thus $j=k$, and $b'_j=0$, contradicting $j\in J$.

Finally, whenever $J$ contains $j$, the two original pairs are distinct: equality of the pairs would give $a=b$, whereas $b'_j>a_j$ is impossible when $a=b$.

Therefore the transfer inequality applies to every $j\in J$.

3.3 Summing the transfer inequalities

Set
$$ n_j=b'_j-a_j>0 \qquad(j\in J). $$
Concavity of each prefix-utility sequence gives
$$ u_t(b') \le u_t(a)+\sum_{j\in J}n_jg_j. $$
Indeed, coordinates with $b'_j\le a_j$ contribute a nonpositive difference, while every added marginal in coordinate $j\in J$ is at most $u_{t,j,a_j+1}=g_j$.

Put $d_{\max}=d_k$. Since $d_j\le d_{\max}<Y$,
$$ \sum_{j\in J}\frac{n_jd_j}{Y-d_j} \le \frac{\sum_j b'_jd_j}{Y-d_{\max}}. $$
Moreover,
$$ \sum_jb'_jd_j = \sum_jb_jd_j-d_{\max} \le u_s(b)-d_{\max} < Y-d_{\max}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_{j\in J}\frac{n_jd_j}{Y-d_j}\le1. $$

For every $j$ with $b'_j>0$, we also have
$$ d_j\le\frac Y2. $$
If $d_{\max}\le Y/2$, this is immediate. If $d_{\max}>Y/2$, the inequality $\sum_j b_jd_j<Y$ forces $b_k=1$, so $b'_k=0$. For every remaining good,
$$ d_j\le\sum_{\ell\ne k}b_\ell d_\ell \le u_s(b)-d_{\max} <\frac Y2. $$

It follows that
$$ \sum_{j\in J}\frac{n_jY}{Y-d_j} \le 2\sum_{j\in J}n_j \le2S. $$

Combining these estimates with the transfer inequalities gives
$$ u_t(b') \le u_t(a)+X\bigl(1+2S(\eta-1)\bigr) \le X\bigl(2+2S(\eta-1)\bigr). $$

3.4 Removing the offset

Our choice of parameters gives
$$ \eta=r^{2m} \le \exp\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{8S}\right) \le 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4S}. $$
For the last step, use $e^x\le1+2x$ on $0\le x\le1/2$; here $x\le1/32$. This elementary inequality follows, for example, because $1+2x-e^x$ starts at zero and has positive derivative on that interval.

Thus
$$ u_t(b')\le \left(2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) \bigl(u_t(a)+\gamma\bigr). $$

If $u_t(a)>0$, its integrality implies $u_t(a)\ge1$, so
$$ u_t(a)+\gamma\le(1+\gamma)u_t(a). $$
Furthermore,
$$ \left(2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) \left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right) = 2+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{16} \le2+\varepsilon. $$
Hence
$$ u_t(b-e_k)\le(2+\varepsilon)u_t(a). $$

If $u_t(a)=0$, then
$$ u_t(b-e_k) \le \left(2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\gamma \le \frac{17}{256} <1. $$
The left side is a nonnegative integer, so it equals zero. The required fairness inequality again holds.

This proves the fairness assertion of Theorem 2, including the zero-welfare branch.

4. Welfare guarantee

If positive welfare is infeasible, $\mathrm{OPT}=0$, and the welfare guarantee is automatic.

Otherwise all permitted output bundles have integer utility at least one. Define shifted logarithmic welfare
$$ H(\lambda)=\sum_{t,b}\lambda_{t,b}\log\bigl(u_t(b)+\gamma\bigr). $$
The rounding bounds and total mass one imply
$$ H(\lambda)-m\log r\le G(\lambda)\le H(\lambda). $$

Let $\lambda^*$ attain the unrestricted optimum. It is permitted in the positive branch. Since $\widehat\lambda$ maximizes $G$,
$$ H(\widehat\lambda) \ge G(\widehat\lambda) \ge G(\lambda^*) \ge H(\lambda^*)-m\log r \ge F(\lambda^*)-m\log r. $$

Every output utility is at least one, so
$$ H(\widehat\lambda) \le F(\widehat\lambda)+\log(1+\gamma). $$
Therefore
$$ F(\widehat\lambda) \ge F(\lambda^*)-m\log r-\log(1+\gamma). $$
Exponentiating,
$$ \mathrm{NSW}(\widehat\lambda) \ge \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{r^m(1+\gamma)}. $$

Finally,
$$ r^m \le \exp\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{16S}\right) \le 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}, $$
and hence
$$ r^m(1+\gamma) \le \left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\right)^2 = 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{64} \le1+\varepsilon. $$

This completes the proof of Theorem 2.

5. One good with arbitrary caps: optimizing over all fair supports

This section retains arbitrary caps but assumes $m=1$. Write $K=K_1$, $q=q_1$, and
$$ U_t(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^k u_{t,1,\ell}. $$
Fix the requested fairness factor
$$ \alpha=2+4\varepsilon. $$
Let $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fair}}$ be the maximum welfare over feasible allocations satisfying this fairness condition.

5.1 A finite union of explicitly described polytopes

For $H\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$, define
$$ A_t(H)= \left\{ k\in\{0,\ldots,H\}: U_t(k)\ge c_t \ \text{or}\ U_t(H-1)\le\alpha U_t(k) \right\}. $$
For $H=0$, put $A_t(0)=\{0\}$.

Let $P_H$ be the allocation polytope using only sizes $k\in A_t(H)$:
$$ x_{t,k}\ge0, \qquad \sum_{k\in A_t(H)}x_{t,k}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,k}kx_{t,k}=q. $$

Proposition 3. The set of fair feasible allocations is exactly
$$ \bigcup_{H=0}^K P_H. $$

Proof. Given a fair allocation, let $H$ be its largest supported size. If $H=0$, it belongs to $P_0$. Otherwise some supported target has size $H$.

For every supported uncapped owner of type $t$ receiving $k$, fairness against that target requires
$$ U_t(H-1)\le\alpha U_t(k). $$
Capped owners are exempt. Thus every supported size belongs to $A_t(H)$.

Conversely, take an allocation in $P_H$. If $H=0$, there are no nonempty targets. If $H>0$, any supported nonempty target has size $h\le H$, so
$$ U_t(h-1)\le U_t(H-1). $$
For every supported uncapped owner receiving $k\in A_t(H)$, the latter is at most $\alpha U_t(k)$. Hence the allocation is fair.

The polytope $P_H$ need not actually support size $H$; using $H$ as an upper bound is sufficient for the converse. $\square$

5.2 A polynomial approximation to the best fair welfare

Theorem 4. For one good and arbitrary caps, there is an algorithm polynomial in
$$ \tau,K,L,1/\varepsilon $$
returning a fair feasible allocation with
$$ \mathrm{NSW}(\lambda) \ge \frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fair}}}{1+\varepsilon}. $$

Proof. Set $r=1+\varepsilon$. For every positive capped utility, compute the integer
$$ h_{t,k}= \max\{h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:r^h\le\min\{c_t,U_t(k)\}\}. $$
These exponents are polynomially bounded. They can be found using exact comparisons between rational powers and integers, without evaluating logarithms.

For each $H$, restrict $P_H$ further to sizes with $U_t(k)>0$, and solve the rational linear program maximizing
$$ \sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}h_{t,k}. $$
There are $K+1$ programs, each with at most $\tau(K+1)$ variables. Choose the feasible program with largest optimal objective value, comparing those rational values exactly.

For every permitted size,
$$ h_{t,k}\log r \le \log\min\{c_t,U_t(k)\} < h_{t,k}\log r+\log r. $$
Thus the rounded logarithmic objective approximates actual logarithmic welfare from below with additive error at most $\log r$, because total mass is one.

By Proposition 3, every positive-welfare fair allocation is represented in one of the programs. The union of their positive-utility polytopes is finite and compact, so a positive fair optimum is attained whenever one exists. Comparing the selected solution with that optimum gives
$$ F(\lambda)\ge \log\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fair}}-\log r. $$
This proves the claimed welfare ratio in that case.

If none of the programs is feasible, every fair allocation has welfare zero. Produce an explicit fair allocation as follows. Put
$$ k=\lfloor q\rfloor, \qquad \theta=q-k. $$
When $\theta>0$, give each type mass $\mu_t(1-\theta)$ size $k$ and mass $\mu_t\theta$ size $k+1$. When $\theta=0$, give everyone size $k$.

The supplies are exact. Every owner receives at least $k$ copies, while removing one copy from any nonempty target leaves at most $k$ copies. Monotonicity therefore gives the stronger inequality
$$ U_t(h-1)\le U_t(k)\le U_t(a) $$
for every relevant comparison. Hence this allocation is fair and meets the zero benchmark. $\square$

This theorem solves the search over fair supports for one good. It does not establish the welfare comparison with the unrestricted optimum required by the original question.

6. Why the cap extension is still incomplete

The main transfer proof depends on the identity
$$ u_t(a+e_j)=u_t(a)+u_{t,j,a_j+1} $$
also describing the welfare-relevant gain. A binding cap breaks that connection: the capped gain can be very small while the uncapped marginal used in the fairness comparison is large.

The following example shows that even an exact unrestricted optimizer can fail the requested fairness.

6.1 An exact optimum that violates fairness

Take two types, each of mass $1/2$, one good, and
$$ K=5,\qquad q=3. $$
Type $1$ has cap $11$ and five marginal utilities all equal to $10$. Type $2$ has cap $5$ and five marginal utilities all equal to $1$.

Consider the allocation giving type $1$ one copy and type $2$ five copies. Its welfare is
$$ \sqrt{10\cdot5}=\sqrt{50}. $$

To prove optimality, set
$$ p=\log(6/5). $$
For type $1$, among positive-utility sizes, the function
$$ \log\bar U_1(k)-pk $$
is uniquely maximized at $k=1$. For $k\ge2$, its difference from the value at one is
$$ \log(11/10)-(k-1)p \le \log(11/10)-\log(6/5) = \log(11/12) <0. $$

For type $2$, the same function is uniquely maximized at $k=5$: for $2\le k\le5$, its successive increment is
$$ \log\!\left(\frac{k}{k-1}\right)-p \ge \log(5/4)-\log(6/5) = \log(25/24) >0. $$

Every positive-welfare feasible allocation consequently satisfies
