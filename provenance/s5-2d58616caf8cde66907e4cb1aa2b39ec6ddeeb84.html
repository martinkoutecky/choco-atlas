The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite goods set \(G\), known desired bundles \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^G\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), a finite-support population distribution \(\mu\) over types \((S,v)\), and a step count \(\gamma\), construct a succinct anonymous direct mechanism. The mechanism observes each agent’s known bundle \(S\) and submitted scalar bid, but not the agent’s true value \(v\); it assigns each unit of mass either the whole desired bundle or nothing, with total allocation of each good \(g\) at most \(q_g\) times total population mass. Require dominant-strategy truthfulness in expectation, participation, the paper’s maximality condition, and a common \(\delta>0\) such that an individual’s winning probability rises by at least \(\delta\) at each of at most \(\gamma\) bid steps unless it reaches \(1\). Require total expected revenue not to increase when any positive-mass cohort is removed and supplies are recalculated at the same per-capita rates. Determine whether such a mechanism can be constructed in time polynomial in \(|G|\), the support size \(\tau\), \(\gamma\), and the input bit length, with \(\delta\) specified by the construction.

Conclusion and the lottery convention

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. A construction uses a lottery over posted prices and polynomial-size linear programs.

There is a measurability distinction worth making explicit. The stated requirements ask for measurable realized allocations, individual winning probabilities, and invariance under relabelings. They do not expressly require joint measurability in the agent and random outcome. I first construct a lottery satisfying those stated requirements, including full relabeling invariance.

If joint measurability is additionally intended, there is still a polynomial-time decision and construction algorithm, but some instances are impossible. That interpretation is treated separately below.

Throughout, agents are nonatomic, as required by the reference to infinitesimal agents. A finite reported support can be represented by finitely many disjoint intervals endowed with completed Lebesgue measure.

1. Thresholds and the linear programs

Set

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\frac12\min\bigl\{1,\min_{g\in G}q_g\bigr\},\qquad \delta=\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}.\)

Both numbers are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length. For every bundle \(S\), choose

\(\displaystyle c_{S,j}=j\quad(0\leq j\leq\gamma),\qquad c_{S,\gamma+1}=\infty.\)

These thresholds do not use true values and remain unchanged after removals.

Consider any reported population of total mass \(M>0\). For each \(k\in\{1,\ldots,\gamma\}\), let \(d_S^k\) denote the mass requesting \(S\) whose bid is at least \(k\).

Define the following linear program, with one variable \(x_S\) for each \(S\in\mathcal B\):

  • Maximize \(\displaystyle \sum_{S\in\mathcal B}x_S\).
  • Require \(\displaystyle \kappa d_S^k\leq x_S\leq d_S^k\) for every \(S\).
  • Require \(\displaystyle \sum_{S:g\in S}x_S\leq q_gM\) for every \(g\).

Write its optimum value as \(F_k\), and choose an optimum \(x^k\) using a fixed lexicographic tie-breaking order on the publicly listed bundles.

The program is always feasible. Indeed, \(x_S=\kappa d_S^k\) satisfies the bounds, and for every good \(g\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S:g\in S}\kappa d_S^k\leq\kappa M\leq q_gM.\)

It is bounded because \(\sum_Sx_S\leq\sum_Sd_S^k\leq M\).

Define

\(\displaystyle r_S^k= \begin{cases} x_S^k/d_S^k,&d_S^k>0,\\ \kappa,&d_S^k=0. \end{cases}\)

Thus \(\kappa\leq r_S^k\leq1\). The zero-mass case specifies the treatment of an infinitesimal agent who alone qualifies for this price.

2. The posted-price mechanism

Choose \(K\) uniformly from \(\{1,\ldots,\gamma\}\).

Conditional on \(K=k\):

  • Agents bidding below \(k\) receive nothing and pay zero.
  • Each eligible agent requesting \(S\) wins with probability \(r_S^k\).
  • Exactly mass \(x_S^k\) of eligible agents requesting \(S\) is served.
  • A winner pays \(k\); an unserved agent pays zero.

Every winner receives her whole desired bundle. Section 7 supplies an anonymous probability law realizing these instructions.

For the empty population, allocate nothing and collect zero.

3. Truthfulness, participation, and the step guarantee

Fix an individual infinitesimal agent requesting \(S\), and fix all other reports. Changing this agent’s report does not change any aggregate mass. Consequently, every \(r_S^k\) is independent of her report.

If her value is \(v>0\), her expected utility conditional on price \(k\), when she reports \(b\), is

\(\displaystyle r_S^k(v-k)\mathbf1_{\{b\geq k\}}.\)

Reporting \(b=v\) maximizes this expression:

  • If \(v>k\), qualifying gives positive utility.
  • If \(v<k\), qualifying gives negative utility.
  • If \(v=k\), both choices give zero utility.

Truthful reporting therefore maximizes each conditional expected utility, and hence their average. This proves dominant-strategy truthfulness in expectation.

An agent bidding in interval \(j\), where \(0\leq j\leq\gamma\), has winning probability and expected payment

\(\displaystyle w_S(j)=\frac1\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{j}r_S^k,\qquad p_S(j)=\frac1\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{j}k\,r_S^k.\)

These quantities depend on bids only through the specified intervals. Expected payments are nonnegative and satisfy \(p_S(0)=0\). Whenever winning probability is zero, expected payment is zero.

For every \(0\leq j<\gamma\),

\(\displaystyle w_S(j+1)-w_S(j)=\frac{r_S^{j+1}}{\gamma}\geq\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}=\delta.\)

Thus the stronger, unconditional increment inequality holds; the alternative allowing winning probability one is unnecessary.

4. Feasibility and maximality

Conditional on price \(k\), exactly mass \(x_S^k\) receives bundle \(S\). Therefore every realized allocation satisfies

\(\displaystyle \text{mass receiving }g=\sum_{S:g\in S}x_S^k\leq q_gM.\)

For maximality, use the bid-independent constants

\(\displaystyle \alpha_S=\gamma\qquad(S\in\mathcal B).\)

Fix a realized price \(k\) and a bundle \(S\) with positive mass of unserved agents bidding above \(\gamma\). These agents are eligible at price \(k\), so \(x_S^k<d_S^k\).

At least one good in \(S\) must have a binding capacity constraint. Otherwise, since \(S\) is finite and \(x_S^k<d_S^k\), one could increase \(x_S^k\) by a sufficiently small positive amount, preserving all constraints and increasing the objective. This would contradict optimality.

Let \(g\in S\) be such a binding good. Every currently served agent bid at least \(k\geq1\), so every served agent has a positive bid. Preserving service to these agents, except possibly on a set of mass zero, preserves consumption of \(g\) up to a set of mass zero. Serving positive additional mass requesting \(S\) would then exceed \(q_gM\).

Thus the required reallocation is impossible. The argument holds for every allocation represented by the lottery.

5. Revenue monotonicity under arbitrary removals

For any truthful population measure \(\nu\),

\(\displaystyle R(\nu)=\frac1\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{\gamma}k\,F_k(\nu).\)

This follows because, conditional on price \(k\), each served agent pays \(k\), and total served mass is \(F_k(\nu)\).

Let \(0\leq\nu'\leq\nu\). Fix \(k\), and abbreviate the corresponding total and eligible masses by \(M',M\) and \(d'_S,d_S\). Put

\(\displaystyle \Delta M=M-M',\qquad \Delta d_S=d_S-d'_S.\)

Then \(\Delta d_S\geq0\), and \(\sum_S\Delta d_S\leq\Delta M\).

Let \(x'\) be an optimum for the smaller population. Define an extension to the larger population by

\(\displaystyle \widehat x_S=x'_S+\kappa\Delta d_S.\)

Its lower bound follows from

\(\displaystyle \widehat x_S\geq\kappa d'_S+\kappa\Delta d_S=\kappa d_S.\)

Its upper bound follows from

\(\displaystyle \widehat x_S\leq d'_S+\kappa\Delta d_S\leq d'_S+\Delta d_S=d_S.\)

For each good \(g\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S:g\in S}\widehat x_S \leq q_gM'+\kappa\sum_{S:g\in S}\Delta d_S \leq q_gM'+\kappa\Delta M \leq q_gM.\)

Hence \(\widehat x\) is feasible for the larger population, and

\(\displaystyle F_k(\nu)\geq F_k(\nu')+\kappa\sum_S\Delta d_S\geq F_k(\nu').\)

Multiplying by the nonnegative price weights and summing gives

\(\displaystyle R(\nu')\leq R(\nu).\)

This proof includes the empty subpopulation. It uses precisely the stipulated recomputation of supplies, without renormalizing remaining masses. In fact, the inequality holds for reported measures as well as truthful ones.

6. Polynomial construction and evaluation costs

Each price requires a rational linear program with \(|\mathcal B|\) variables and \(O(m+|\mathcal B|)\) constraints. Its coefficients have polynomial encoding length.

A canonical lexicographic optimum can be obtained with at most \(|\mathcal B|+1\) successive linear optimizations: first fix the maximum total served mass, then successively fix each coordinate. All these optimizations occur on faces of the original rational polytope, so their optimum values have polynomial encoding length.

Thus:

  • Constructing the thresholds and the mechanism description takes polynomial time.
  • Aggregating a rational reported support into bundle and interval masses takes polynomial time.
  • Evaluating all \(r_S^k\), winning probabilities, and expected payments takes polynomial time using \(\gamma\) families of polynomial-size linear programs.
  • The allocation-lottery representation below has polynomial size.

These bounds are polynomial in \(m,|\mathcal B|,\tau,\gamma,L\) for the supplied support, and in the corresponding support size and bit length for subsequent evaluations. No true value is used by the constructor.

7. An explicit fully anonymous allocation lottery

It remains to justify simultaneous exact masses and full relabeling invariance.

For a fixed price \(k\), partition agents into the finitely many reported cells \(E_{S,j}\), indexed by bundle and bid interval. Let their masses be \(h_{S,j}\).

For an eligible cell, prescribe

\(\displaystyle a_{S,j}=r_S^k h_{S,j}.\)

For an ineligible cell, prescribe zero service. These prescriptions sum to \(x_S^k\) for each bundle.

Here is a probability law realizing such prescriptions.

A single cell

Let \(E\) be a completed Lebesgue cell of mass \(h\), and let \(r\in(0,1)\). Take as outcomes all measurable subsets \(W\subseteq E\) satisfying \(\mu(W)=rh\).

Equip this outcome space with the sigma-algebra generated by the individual membership events \(\{W:t\in W\}\). Give these membership events the independent Bernoulli law with parameter \(r\).

The existence of this law requires justification because every outcome has prescribed mass.

For any countable set \(C\subseteq E\), every prescribed pattern of membership on \(C\) is realizable: begin with a measurable subset of mass \(rh\), then add or remove the specified countably many points. Countable modifications preserve measurability and mass.

Every event in the membership-generated sigma-algebra depends on at most countably many coordinates. Assign its probability using the corresponding countable Bernoulli product measure. This assignment is well-defined:

  • Two descriptions can be compared on the union of their countable coordinate sets.
  • Every coordinate pattern on that union is realizable.
  • Therefore descriptions with the same inverse image have the same product probability.

Countable additivity follows by putting a countable collection of events onto one common countable coordinate set and using countable additivity of its product measure.

Thus this is an actual probability space whose every outcome is a measurable subset of the prescribed mass, and every agent wins with probability \(r\).

When \(r=1\), take \(W=E\) deterministically. Ineligible cells are empty deterministically. A null eligible cell is handled by the same membership construction: under completed measure, all its subsets are measurable and have mass zero.

Several cells and anonymity

Apply the construction to all reported cells, with their prescribed winning probabilities, and take their finite product.

A measure-preserving relabeling preserving bundles and bids maps every reported cell to itself. It maps prescribed-mass measurable subsets to prescribed-mass measurable subsets. On membership coordinates it simply permutes agents with identical Bernoulli parameters.

Consequently, it preserves every cylinder probability and therefore the entire probability law. The allocation and payment lottery is anonymous under the required relabelings.

Its succinct representation consists of the price probabilities, reported cells, rational winning parameters, prescribed cell masses, and the fixed membership-law recipe. It does not enumerate individual agents.

This probability law is generally not jointly measurable in agent and random outcome. Nevertheless, individual outcomes and payments are random variables; every realized allocation is measurable; and the represented served masses, capacity consumption, and total payments are measurable—in fact, fixed conditional on the price. Those are all the quantities required in the stated formulation.

8. If joint measurability is additionally required

For completeness, imposing joint measurability changes feasibility but does not change the polynomial-time complexity answer.

An obstruction for a homogeneous population

Normalize a homogeneous population to the unit interval. Suppose \(X(\omega,t)\in\{0,1\}\) is jointly measurable and its law is invariant under every measure-preserving relabeling.

Anonymity makes \(\mathbb E[X(t)]\) constant. It also makes \(\mathbb E[X(s)X(t)]\) equal to one constant \(\rho\) for all distinct \(s,t\): finite permutations of points are measure-preserving relabelings.

Write

\(\displaystyle Z=\int_0^1X(t)\,dt,\qquad Z_I=\frac1{|I|}\int_I X(t)\,dt\)

for any interval \(I\) of positive length.

By joint measurability and Fubini’s theorem, the diagonal contributes zero to the double integrals, giving

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[Z_I^2]=\rho,\qquad \mathbb E[Z^2]=\rho,\qquad \mathbb E[Z_IZ]=\rho.\)

Hence \(\mathbb E[(Z_I-Z)^2]=0\).

Taking the countable collection of dyadic intervals, almost surely every such interval has served mass \(Z\) times its length. Uniqueness of finite measures on the dyadic generating algebra then implies

\(\displaystyle X(t)=Z\quad\text{for almost every }t.\)

Since \(X\) is an indicator, necessarily \(Z\in\{0,1\}\) almost surely. Thus a fully anonymous jointly measurable lottery serves either zero mass or the entire homogeneous population.

Now suppose a bundle \(S\) that can remain alone has \(\min_{g\in S}q_g<1\). Feasibility excludes serving the entire population, so the lottery serves zero mass almost surely. Choose a common bid above its finite maximality threshold. Because all supplies are positive, positive mass could be served without depriving anyone, contradicting maximality.

Therefore such an instance is impossible under the additional joint-measurability requirement.

The polynomial decision and construction rule

After removals, any bundle having positive public mass can remain alone. Consequently, under joint measurability a mechanism exists exactly when

\(\displaystyle q_g\geq1\)

for every good appearing in a bundle that can occur with positive public mass.

Necessity follows from the obstruction. For sufficiency, use the uniform posted-price lottery above and serve every agent whose bid meets the chosen price. Consumption of any good is at most \(M\leq q_gM\). Truthfulness, participation, maximality, revenue monotonicity, and the step guarantee follow directly, with \(\delta=1/\gamma\). This lottery is jointly measurable and fully anonymous.

The capacity test and construction are polynomial-time.

Thus both the explicitly stated lottery requirements and the stronger jointly measurable interpretation admit polynomial-time algorithms, with the feasibility distinction proved above.
