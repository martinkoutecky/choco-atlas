The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(C\), rational \(\epsilon,\delta\), and one-pass access to an ordered sequence \(((t_i,a_i))_{i=1}^q\) of positive rational mass increments summing to \(1\), where each \(t_i\in\{(+,c),(-,c):c\in C\}\), let \(\mu_t=\sum_{i:t_i=t}a_i\). 1. Exact characterization of the transfer distance

Write
$$ p_c=\mu_{(+,c)},\qquad n_c=\mu_{(-,c)},\qquad s_c=p_c-n_c, $$
and let
$$ S=\max_{d\in C}s_d,\qquad g_c=S-s_c. $$
Define $d(c)$ to be the minimum total transfer mass that makes $c$ a winner. This minimum exists: the source constraints give a compact feasible polytope, the winning constraints are closed, and transferring all mass outside $(+,c)$ into $(+,c)$ makes $c$ win.

1.1. Necessary and sufficient deficit bounds

For distinct candidates $c,d$, define
$$ \phi_{c,d}(t)= \begin{cases} 1,&t=(+,c)\text{ or }t=(-,d),\\ -1,&t=(-,c)\text{ or }t=(+,d),\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
A transfer of mass $x$ from $t$ to $u$ changes $s_c-s_d$ by
$$ x\bigl(\phi_{c,d}(u)-\phi_{c,d}(t)\bigr)\le 2x. $$
Consequently,
$$ d(c)\ge \frac{g_c}{2}. $$
In particular, a candidate with deficit greater than $2\epsilon$ is not an $\epsilon$-winner.

There is also a useful sufficient bound:
$$ d(c)\le \frac{g_c}{1+g_c}. $$
Indeed, put $\lambda=g_c/(1+g_c)$ and transfer the fraction $\lambda$ of every type other than $(+,c)$ into $(+,c)$. The total transferred mass is $\lambda(1-p_c)\le\lambda$, and the resulting society is
$$ \mu'=(1-\lambda)\mu+\lambda e_{(+,c)}. $$
For every $d\ne c$,
$$ s_{\mu'}(c)-s_{\mu'}(d) =(1-\lambda)(s_c-s_d)+\lambda \ge -(1-\lambda)g_c+\lambda=0. $$
Thus a deficit at most $\epsilon$ is sufficient.

Furthermore, for distinct $c,d$,
$$ s_d-s_c\le p_d+n_c\le1. $$
Hence $g_c\le1$ and $d(c)\le1/2$.

Resolved range. If $\epsilon\ge1/2$, outputting candidate $1$ always succeeds. It uses zero mutable bits if a fixed output is allowed, and otherwise constant working memory.

The remainder concerns $0<\epsilon<1/2$.

1.2. An exact formula

Fix $c$, and put
$$ A_c=n_c+\sum_{d\ne c}p_d. $$

Proposition. If $g_c\le2n_c$, then
$$ d(c)=\frac{g_c}{2}. $$
Otherwise, $d(c)$ is the unique solution $h\in(n_c,A_c]$ of
$$ h-n_c=\sum_{d\ne c}(s_d-p_c-h)_+, $$
where $(v)_+=\max\{v,0\}$.

Proof. First, any successful transfer plan can be replaced by one whose only destination is $(+,c)$. Redirect transfers from sources other than $(+,c)$ to $(+,c)$, and discard transfers whose source is $(+,c)$.

This preserves the source constraints and cannot increase total transferred mass. It improves every comparison $s_c-s_d$: the coefficient of $(+,c)$ is $1$, the maximum possible value of $\phi_{c,d}$; discarding a transfer out of $(+,c)$ likewise cannot worsen that comparison.

Call $(-,c)$ and the types $(+,d)$ with $d\ne c$ the useful sources. Transferring their entire mass, of total $A_c$, makes $c$ win: its resulting score is
$$ p_c+n_c+\sum_{d\ne c}p_d\ge0, $$
whereas every rival has score $-n_d\le0$. Thus an optimal plan has cost at most $A_c$.

An optimal plan can use only useful sources. To see this, suppose its cost is $h\le A_c$, and some donated mass comes from $(-,d)$ with $d\ne c$. There is enough unused useful-source mass to replace all such donations. For every rival $f$, a useful source has coefficient at most $0$ in $\phi_{c,f}$, whereas $(-,d)$ has coefficient at least $0$. Replacing a donation from $(-,d)$ by a donation of the same amount from an unused useful source therefore improves every comparison.

Among useful sources, $(-,c)$ can be used first: its coefficient is $-1$ in every $\phi_{c,d}$, while a source $(+,e)$ has coefficient either $-1$ or $0$. Consequently, a successful plan of cost $h\le A_c$ can be normalized to donate
$$ y=\min\{h,n_c\} $$
from $(-,c)$, and amounts $z_d$ from $(+,d)$ satisfying
$$ 0\le z_d\le p_d,\qquad \sum_{d\ne c}z_d=h-y. $$
The resulting scores are
$$ s'_c=s_c+h+y,\qquad s'_d=s_d-z_d. $$
Writing $v=s_c+h+y$, the winning conditions are
$$ z_d\ge(s_d-v)_+. $$

If $h\le n_c$, then $y=h$ and every $z_d$ is zero. Success is therefore equivalent to
$$ s_c+2h\ge S. $$
This proves the first case, including optimality.

Suppose now that $g_c>2n_c$. No cost at most $n_c$ succeeds. For $h\ge n_c$, we have $y=n_c$ and
$$ v=p_c+h\ge0. $$
Therefore $(s_d-v)_+\le p_d$ for every rival. Also,
$$ h-n_c\le A_c-n_c=\sum_{d\ne c}p_d. $$
It follows that suitable $z_d$ exist exactly when
$$ \sum_{d\ne c}(s_d-p_c-h)_+\le h-n_c. $$
For sufficiency, assign the displayed lower bounds first, then distribute any remaining required donation among the unused capacities $p_d-z_d$.

Finally, define
$$ G(h)=h-n_c-\sum_{d\ne c}(s_d-p_c-h)_+. $$
This function is continuous. For $h_2>h_1$,
$$ G(h_2)-G(h_1)\ge h_2-h_1>0, $$
so it is strictly increasing. The assumption gives $G(n_c)<0$. At $h=A_c$, we have $p_c+h\ge p_d\ge s_d$ for every rival, and hence $G(A_c)\ge0$. There is exactly one root, and it is the minimum successful cost. $\square$

This formula also illustrates why a deficit at most $2\epsilon$ is not generally sufficient. If $c$ has zero mass and $k$ rivals each have positive mass $1/k$, then
$$ d(c)=\frac{1}{k+1}, $$
although its score deficit is $1/k$.

2. Parsing rational masses with relative error

The upper bounds require a parser that does not retain an entire numerator or denominator.

Lemma. Under an explicit numerator–denominator encoding, a positive rational $a\le1$ can be read once and replaced by a symbolic dyadic number
$$ b=V2^{-h} $$
satisfying
$$ (1-\epsilon/64)a\le b\le a. $$
The parser uses
$$ O\bigl(\log(1/\epsilon)+\log(L+2)\bigr) $$
bits. The mantissa $V$ has $O(\log(1/\epsilon))$ bits; $h$ is stored as an exponent.

Proof. Set
$$ J=\left\lceil\log(512/\epsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad \rho=2^{1-J}\le\frac{\epsilon}{256}. $$
For a positive binary integer $P$, retain its first $J$ significant bits and count its significant length $\ell_P$.

If $\ell_P\le J$, retain $P$ exactly. Otherwise, if $v_P$ is its retained prefix, define
$$ P^-=v_P2^{\ell_P-J},\qquad P^+=(v_P+1)2^{\ell_P-J}. $$
The powers of two are stored symbolically. In both cases,
$$ (1-\rho)P\le P^-\le P\le P^+\le(1+\rho)P. $$

For $a=N/D$, put $a^-=N^-/D^+$. Then
$$ \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}a\le a^-\le a. $$
Write its symbolic representation as
$$ a^-=\frac{P_0}{Q_0}2^e, $$
where $P_0,Q_0$ have $O(J)$ bits. Choose the integer $k$ such that
$$ z=\frac{P_0}{Q_0}2^{-k}\in[1,2). $$
Comparisons of shifted $O(J)$-bit integers determine $k$. Define
$$ V=\left\lfloor z2^{J-1}\right\rfloor,\qquad b=V2^{e+k-(J-1)}. $$
Thus $b=V2^{-h}$ with $h=J-1-e-k$, and
$$ (1-\rho)a^-\le b\le a^-. $$
Consequently,
$$ b\ge \frac{(1-\rho)^2}{1+\rho}a \ge(1-3\rho)a \ge(1-\epsilon/64)a. $$

The prefixes require $O(J)$ bits. Lengths and exponents require $O(\log(L+J+2))$ bits, absorbed by the stated bound. All normalization arithmetic uses $O(J)$-bit integers. $\square$

Apply this parser to every record, and define
$$ \nu_t=\sum_{i:t_i=t}b_i. $$
Since total original mass is $1$,
$$ 0\le\mu_t-\nu_t,\qquad \sum_t(\mu_t-\nu_t)\le\frac{\epsilon}{64}. $$
This relative-error guarantee is independent of $q$.

3. Deterministic and randomized upper bounds

3.1. The counter dictionary

Set
$$ K=\left\lceil64/\epsilon\right\rceil. $$
For nonnegative integer updates $w_i$, process a virtual stream containing $w_i$ copies of type $t_i$. Maintain at most $K$ counters:

  • Increment the counter if the type is stored.
  • Otherwise insert it with counter $1$ if capacity remains.
  • Otherwise decrement all $K$ counters, remove zeros, and discard the current occurrence.

Let
$$ F_t=\sum_{i:t_i=t}w_i,\qquad W=\sum_iw_i, $$
and let $\widehat F_t$ be the final counter, with value zero for an unstored type.

Each decrement step removes $K+1$ virtual occurrences of distinct types. If there are $D$ such steps, then
$$ (K+1)D\le W. $$
A particular type loses at most one occurrence per step. Therefore, simultaneously for every type,
$$ 0\le F_t-\widehat F_t\le D\le\frac{W}{K+1}. $$
This proof permits arbitrary ordering and repeated records.

Only types occurring in the original stream can enter the dictionary. At most $\min\{\tau,K\}$ entries are present.

3.2. Deterministic integer updates

Choose
$$ B=\left\lceil\log(64q/\epsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad M=2^B, $$
and put
$$ w_i=\lfloor Mb_i\rfloor. $$
Symbolic dyadic arithmetic computes this integer using $O(B+J+\log(L+2))$ bits. Large negative shifts simply produce zero.

We have $W\le M$, and
$$ \sum_i\left(a_i-\frac{w_i}{M}\right) \le\frac{\epsilon}{64}+\frac{q}{M} \le\frac{\epsilon}{32}. $$
Combining this with the dictionary guarantee gives
$$ 0\le\mu_t-\frac{\widehat F_t}{M} \le\frac{\epsilon}{32}+\frac{1}{K+1} \le\frac{3\epsilon}{64} <\frac{\epsilon}{16}. $$

Each dictionary counter uses $O(B)$ bits, and each signed type uses $O(\log m)$ bits. Including the parser and candidate-selection workspace, the space is
$$ O\!\left( \min\{\tau,\epsilon^{-1}\} \bigl(\log m+\log(q/\epsilon)\bigr) +\log(L+2) \right). $$
There are at most $M=O(q/\epsilon)$ virtual occurrences.

3.3. Randomized integer updates without a counter dependence on $q$

Assume $0<\delta<1$. Choose
$$ B=\left\lceil\log\left(\frac{16384}{\delta\epsilon^2}\right)\right\rceil, \qquad M=2^B. $$
Independently for each record, use stochastic rounding:
$$ w_i=\lfloor Mb_i\rfloor+Z_i, $$
where $Z_i$ is Bernoulli with success probability
$$ Mb_i-\lfloor Mb_i\rfloor. $$
Then
$$ \mathbb E[w_i]=Mb_i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(w_i)\le Mb_i. $$

This Bernoulli variable can be generated exactly with bounded working memory and finitely many fair bits. Indeed, $Mb_i=V2^{-r}$ for a stored integer exponent $r$.

  • If $r\le0$, the update is an integer.
  • If $0<r\le J$, generate an $r$-bit uniform integer and compare it with the fractional remainder.
  • If $r>J$, require the first $r-J$ fair bits to be zero, stopping immediately if one is nonzero; if all are zero, generate a $J$-bit uniform integer $U$ and test $U<V$.

In the last case the success probability is exactly
$$ 2^{-(r-J)}\frac{V}{2^J}=\frac{V}{2^r}. $$
The exponent loop uses $O(\log(L+J+2))$ bits.

For the hypothetical uncapped update stream,
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[ \sum_t\left(\frac{F_t}{M}-\nu_t\right)^2 \right] =\frac{1}{M^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(w_i) \le\frac1M. $$
Hence, by Markov's inequality,
$$ \Pr\!\left[ \max_t\left|\frac{F_t}{M}-\nu_t\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{64} \right] \le\frac{4096}{M\epsilon^2} \le\frac{\delta}{4}. $$
The number of possible types does not appear in this bound.

To ensure a worst-case, rather than expected, space bound, maintain $W$ only up to $2M$. If an update would exceed $2M$, enter a failure state, consume the remaining input, and eventually output candidate $1$.

For the uncapped stream,
$$ \mathbb E[W]\le M,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(W)\le M. $$
Chebyshev's inequality gives
$$ \Pr[W>2M]\le\frac1M \le\frac{\delta\epsilon^2}{16384} <\frac{\delta}{4}. $$
Because the updates are nonnegative, the event of ever exceeding the cap is exactly the event that the uncapped final total exceeds it.

Outside these two bad events, the dictionary error satisfies
$$ 0\le\frac{F_t-\widehat F_t}{M} \le\frac{2}{K+1}\le\frac{\epsilon}{32}. $$
Therefore, simultaneously for all types,
$$ \left|\mu_t-\frac{\widehat F_t}{M}\right| \le \frac{\epsilon}{64} +\frac{\epsilon}{64} +\frac{\epsilon}{32} =\frac{\epsilon}{16}. $$
This event has probability at least $1-\delta$.

Every retained counter is bounded by $2M$, including on unsuccessful executions. Thus the worst-case space is
$$ O\!\left( \min\{\tau,\epsilon^{-1}\} \bigl(\log m+\log(1/(\epsilon\delta))\bigr) +\log(L+2) \right). $$
At most $2M=O(1/(\delta\epsilon^2))$ virtual occurrences are processed.

3.4. Selecting an $\epsilon$-winner

For either algorithm, put
$$ \widehat\mu_t=\frac{\widehat F_t}{M},\qquad \widehat s(c)=\widehat\mu_{(+,c)}-\widehat\mu_{(-,c)}. $$
On the corresponding success event,
$$ |s_c-\widehat s(c)|\le\frac{\epsilon}{8}. $$
After the stream, enumerate all candidates and output a maximizer $\widehat c$ of $\widehat s$. This includes candidates absent from the dictionary and requires $O(\log m+B)$ additional bits.

For an actual maximizer $c^\star$,
$$ s_{c^\star} \le\widehat s(c^\star)+\frac{\epsilon}{8} \le\widehat s(\widehat c)+\frac{\epsilon}{8} \le s_{\widehat c}+\frac{\epsilon}{4}. $$
Thus $\widehat c$ has deficit at most $\epsilon/4$, so Section 1 proves that it is an $\epsilon$-winner.

Neither algorithm needs advance knowledge of $\tau$.

3.5. Literal finite binary fractions

If each mass is written as a finite binary expansion with a recognizable field end, the length-counter term can be removed.

For the deterministic algorithm, retain the first $B$ fractional bits, giving $w_i=\lfloor Ma_i\rfloor$. The total discarded mass is at most $q/M$, and the preceding dictionary and selection proofs apply.

For the randomized algorithm, the first $B$ fractional bits give $\lfloor Ma_i\rfloor$. Compare subsequent fractional bits with fresh fair bits until the first mismatch. Return $1$ exactly when the random binary fraction is smaller than the remaining fractional tail. If the field ends before a mismatch, return $0$, since the remaining tail of the encoded mass is zero.

This generates a Bernoulli variable with probability equal to the fractional part of $Ma_i$. It needs only the integer update, a counter up to $B$, and a comparison flag. Here $\nu_t=\mu_t$, so the randomized proof applies without parser bias or length counters.

4. Direct randomized lower bounds

Write $p=1-\delta$. We use the ordinary streaming convention that the retained state contains everything affecting future behavior; future fair bits are independent of the past.

A machine using at most $S_{\mathrm{mem}}$ bits has at most $2^{S_{\mathrm{mem}}+O(1)}$ retained states.

4.1. An information lemma

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_k$ be independent uniform elements of $\{1,\ldots,r\}$, and let $M_0$ be the state after a prefix encoding them. Suppose, for each $i$, a decoder given $M_0$ and $X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1}$ can recover $X_i$ with probability at least $p$.

Then
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}}\ge k(p\log r-1)-O(1). $$

To prove this, let $R_i$ be the decoder's independent fresh randomness and $E_i$ its error indicator. Given $M_0,X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1},R_i$, the decoded symbol is determined. On no error, $X_i$ is determined; on error, it has at most $r$ possibilities. Consequently,
$$ H(X_i\mid M_0,X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1},R_i) \le H(E_i)+(1-p)\log r \le1+(1-p)\log r. $$
Independence of $R_i$ allows it to be removed from the conditional entropy. The chain rule therefore gives
$$ I(X;M_0) =\sum_{i=1}^k \left( \log r-H(X_i\mid M_0,X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1}) \right) \ge k(p\log r-1). $$
Finally, $I(X;M_0)\le H(M_0)\le S_{\mathrm{mem}}+O(1)$.

4.2. Singleton support

For $\epsilon<1/2$, a society concentrated on $(+,c)$ has exactly one $\epsilon$-winner: $c$. Every other candidate has deficit $1>2\epsilon$.

Use one such stream for each $c$, with a common record count $q$, for example by giving every record mass $1/q$. Let $P_c(s)$ be the probability of final state $s$ on this stream, and let $Q_s(c)$ be the conditional probability of outputting $c$ from state $s$. Then
$$ pm \le\sum_c\sum_s P_c(s)Q_s(c) \le\sum_s\sum_c Q_s(c) =|\mathcal S|. $$
Thus, even for $\tau=1$,
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}}\ge\log(pm)-O(1). $$

For fixed $p>0$, this matches an $O(\log m)$ algorithm under the promise $\tau=1$: retain the first signed type; output its candidate if positive and a different candidate if negative.

In particular, a lower bound growing as $\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)$ cannot hold uniformly over all support sizes.

4.3. A block-boosting construction

Theorem. Let $k\ge1$, $r\ge2$, and $kr+1\le m$. Put
$$ w=2^{-\lceil\log(4(k+r))\rceil}. $$
If $2\epsilon<w$, then there is a family with
$$ q=k+r+2,\qquad \tau=k+r+1, $$
and input length
$$ O\bigl((k+r)(\log m+\log(k+r))\bigr) $$
on which
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}}\ge k(p\log r-1)-O(1). $$

Proof. Use candidates $(j,a)$ for $1\le j\le k$, $1\le a\le r$, and a separate candidate $z$.

For $x\in\{1,\ldots,r\}^k$, the prefix contains
$$ ((+,(j,x_j)),w),\qquad j=1,\ldots,k. $$
For a query index $i$, append the $r$ records
$$ ((+,(i,a)),w),\qquad a=1,\ldots,r, $$
followed by
$$ ((+,z),b),\qquad ((-,z),b), \qquad b=\frac{1-(k+r)w}{2}. $$
Since $(k+r)w\le1/4$, we have $b\ge3/8$. All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to $1$.

The candidate $(i,x_i)$ has score $2w$. Every other block candidate has score at most $w$, and $z$ and unused candidates have score zero. Every other candidate therefore has deficit at least $w>2\epsilon$. The unique $\epsilon$-winner is $(i,x_i)$.

There are $k+r+2$ records. The prefix and query suffix overlap in exactly one signed type, giving support size $k+r+1$.

A decoder can append the suffix knowing only $i$, so the information lemma applies. Dyadic mass encodings use $O(\log(k+r))$ bits per record, proving the length bound. $\square$

A matching restricted regime

Fix $\delta<1$. Let
$$ m=\lceil\epsilon^{-2}\rceil,\qquad k=r=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{128\epsilon}\right\rfloor, $$
for sufficiently small $\epsilon$. Then $kr+1\le m$ and
$$ w>\frac{1}{8(k+r)}\ge8\epsilon>2\epsilon. $$
The construction has
$$ q=2k+2,\qquad \tau=2k+1, $$
and its input length is
$$ O\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
Because $p$ is a fixed positive constant, its lower bound is
$$ \Omega\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$

Take a length budget equal to a sufficiently large constant times the displayed length bound. The deterministic upper bound applies to every stream in this restricted class and is
$$ O\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
Thus the minimum worst-case space over this class is
$$ \Theta\bigl(\epsilon^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
This settles a restricted regime, rather than the full parameter-dependent question.

4.4. A cancellation construction for larger candidate sets

Theorem. Let $r\ge2$ and $kr+1\le m$. Define
$$ \Delta=2^{-\lceil\log(4k(k+1))\rceil}. $$
If $\epsilon<3\Delta/8$, then a family with
$$ q=\tau=2k+2 $$
and length $O(k(\log m+\log k))$ requires
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}}\ge k(p\log r-1)-O(1). $$

Proof. Use the same block candidates and neutral candidate as above. Put
$$ w_j=(k-j+1)\Delta,\qquad \eta=\Delta/4, $$
and
$$ A=\sum_{j=1}^kw_j=\frac{k(k+1)\Delta}{2}\le\frac18. $$
For $x\in\{1,\ldots,r\}^k$, the prefix contains
$$ ((+,(j,x_j)),w_j),\qquad j=1,\ldots,k. $$

For index $i$, a decoder knowing $x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1}$ appends:

  • $((-,(j,x_j)),w_j)$ for $j<i$;
  • $((-,(j,1)),\eta)$ for $j\ge i$;
  • $((+,z),b_i)$ and $((-,z),b_i)$, where

$$ D_i=\sum_{j<i}w_j+(k-i+1)\eta,\qquad b_i=\frac{1-A-D_i}{2}. $$

5. A zero-error lower bound

Theorem. Let $1\le k\le m-1$, and put
$$ w=2^{-\lceil\log(8k)\rceil}. $$
If $\delta=0$ and $\epsilon<w/2$, then a family with
$$ q=\tau=2k+1 $$
and input length $O(k(\log m+\log k))$ requires
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}}\ge\log\binom{m-1}{k}-O(1). $$

Proof. Reserve candidate $z$. For every $k$-element subset $A\subseteq C\setminus\{z\}$, form a prefix containing $((+,a),w)$ for each $a\in A$, in a fixed order.

For distinct $A,B$, choose $x\in A\setminus B$. Append a common suffix consisting of $((-,a),w)$ for each $a\in A\setminus\{x\}$, followed by
$$ ((+,z),b),\qquad ((-,z),b), \qquad b=\frac{1-(2k-1)w}{2}>0. $$
Both completions have total mass $1$ and exactly $2k+1$ distinct signed types.

Following prefix $A$, candidate $x$ has score $w$ and every other candidate has score zero. Since $w>2\epsilon$, only $x$ is acceptable.

Following prefix $B$, candidate $x$ has score zero, while any member of $B\setminus A$ has score $w$. Thus $x$ is unacceptable.

The two prefixes cannot reach a common retained state with positive probability. Otherwise the conditional output distribution after their common suffix would be identical. Zero error would require that distribution both to output $x$ with probability one and to exclude $x$ with probability one.

The positive-probability state supports of all $\binom{m-1}{k}$ prefixes are therefore pairwise disjoint. State counting proves the result. $\square$

For $N\ge k$,
$$ \binom Nk =\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{N-j}{k-j} \ge\left(\frac Nk\right)^k. $$
Taking $k=\lfloor1/(64\epsilon)\rfloor$ for sufficiently small $\epsilon$ satisfies the theorem's strict inequality. Since $m\ge1/\epsilon$, the result yields
$$ S_{\mathrm{mem}} =\Omega\!\left( \epsilon^{-1}\log(2+\epsilon m) \right) $$
on zero-error families with $q=\tau=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$.

The positive-error construction removes the earlier $\log q$ dependence from dictionary-counter storage. For fixed $\delta>0$, its bound is
$$ O\!\left( \min\{\tau,\epsilon^{-1}\} \bigl(\log m+\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr) +\log(L+2) \right). $$
The proof includes numerical parsing, arbitrary record repetition, worst-case memory on failed executions, and validity under real transfers.

The lower bounds concern the explicitly specified families and length budgets. They cannot be combined into a matching expression for every joint choice of $m,\tau,L,q,\epsilon,\delta$.

The cited [vote-stream paper](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2015/135/revision/1/download) and [unsigned frequency paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.00213) study related unit-update models.
