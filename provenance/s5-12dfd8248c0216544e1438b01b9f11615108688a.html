Let \(V\) be an item set of size \(m\ge 3\), and let \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\) be a binary rational; logarithms are natural.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type explicitly specifies complete pairwise responses \(W_t(u,v)\in\{0,1\}\) with \(W_t(u,v)+W_t(v,u)=1\) for distinct items; transitivity is not required.
Define \(q_\mu(u,v)=\sum_t\mu_tW_t(u,v)\). For \(U\subseteq V\), let \(\Pi(U)\) denote its strict rankings and define \(C_\mu(\pi;U)=\sum_{u,v\in U:\,u\prec_\pi v}q_\mu(v,u)\), counting each unordered pair once.
An ordered partition \((V_1,\ldots,V_k)\) has nonempty disjoint blocks covering \(V\); its respecting rankings, denoted \(\Pi(V_1,\ldots,V_k)\), place every item of \(V_i\) before every item of \(V_j\) whenever \(i<j\).
Set \(B=\{i:|V_i|>\log m/\log\log m\}\). The partition is \(\varepsilon\)-good if both
\(\sum_{i\in B}\min_{\rho\in\Pi(V_i)}C_\mu(\rho;V_i)\ge\varepsilon^2\sum_{i\in B}\binom{|V_i|}{2}\) and \(\min_{\sigma\in\Pi(V_1,\ldots,V_k)}C_\mu(\sigma;V)\le(1+\varepsilon)\min_{\pi\in\Pi(V)}C_\mu(\pi;V)\); empty sums are zero.
Is computing an \(\varepsilon\)-good ordered partition possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon^{-1}\), allowing randomization with constant success probability, or is it NP-hard?
For a single binary tournament, a randomized polynomial-time algorithm computes such a partition with constant success probability using \(O(\varepsilon^{-6}m\log^5 m)\) expected pair queries ([Ailon](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume13/ailon12a/ailon12a.pdf)).
Weighted ranking with complementary pair weights has a PTAS ([Kenyon-Mathieu–Schudy](https://cs.brown.edu/~claire/Publis/kenyonschudy.pdf)); this is a ranking guarantee, not the stated decomposition guarantee. No result settling this exact mass-input decomposition problem was located.

1. Definitions and a fixed-accuracy ranking tool

Write $q=q_\mu$, and define
$$ \operatorname{OPT}(U)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(U)}C_\mu(\pi;U). $$
For an ordered partition $\mathcal P$ of $U$, write
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(U) =\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal P)}C_\mu(\pi;U). $$

The input assumptions imply
$$ 0\le q(u,v)\le1, \qquad q(u,v)+q(v,u)=1. $$
All weights can be computed in polynomial time. Indeed, the product of the denominators of the input masses is a common denominator whose bit length is at most the sum of those denominator bit lengths. Computing all pair weights therefore takes polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$.

We use the following established result: for every fixed $\eta>0$, a deterministic algorithm polynomial in the instance size and rational encoding length returns a ranking of cost at most $(1+\eta)\operatorname{OPT}$. This follows from the weighted ranking PTAS and its stated derandomization. Its hypotheses hold here by taking the cost of placing $u$ before $v$ to be $q(v,u)$: these costs lie in $[0,1]$ and their complementary sums equal $1$. [Kenyon-Mathieu–Schudy, Theorem 1](https://cs.brown.edu/~claire/Publis/kenyonschudy.pdf).

In particular, we may use deterministic polynomial-time ranking algorithms with approximation factors $2$ and $3/2$. Their accuracies are fixed constants.

2. An exact uniformly tractable class

Theorem 1. Suppose there is a number $r\in[0,1/2]$ such that
$$ \min\{q(u,v),q(v,u)\}=r $$
for every unordered pair. Then the exact task is solvable in randomized time polynomial in $m,\tau,L,\varepsilon^{-1}$, with constant success probability.

Proof. Choose a majority tournament $H$, breaking ties arbitrarily, and let its binary preference matrix be $W_H$. Put
$$ d=1-2r. $$
The hypothesis gives
$$ q(u,v)=r+dW_H(u,v). $$
Consequently, for every subset $U$ and ranking $\pi$,
$$ C_\mu(\pi;U) =r\binom{|U|}{2}+dC_H(\pi;U), $$
where $C_H$ is the binary tournament ranking cost. Because $d\ge0$, minimizing gives
$$ \operatorname{OPT}(U) =r\binom{|U|}{2}+d\operatorname{OPT}_H(U). $$
The same identity holds when the minimization is restricted to rankings respecting any fixed ordered partition.

First suppose $\varepsilon<1/2$. Apply the binary decomposition algorithm supplied in the task to $H$, with accuracy $\varepsilon$. Its input is an explicit binary tournament on the same $m$ items, so its size threshold agrees exactly with the task’s threshold. Its runtime is polynomial in $m,\varepsilon^{-1}$. [Ailon, Theorem 7](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume13/ailon12a/ailon12a.pdf).

On its success event, let $\mathcal P=(V_1,\ldots,V_k)$ be its output, and put
$$ S=\sum_{i\in B}\binom{|V_i|}{2}. $$
Its binary local-chaos guarantee implies
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i\in B}\operatorname{OPT}(V_i) &=rS+d\sum_{i\in B}\operatorname{OPT}_H(V_i)\\ &\ge \bigl(r+d\varepsilon^2\bigr)S\\ &=\bigl(\varepsilon^2+r(1-2\varepsilon^2)\bigr)S\\ &\ge\varepsilon^2S. \end{aligned} $$
The final inequality holds because $r\ge0$ and $\varepsilon<1/2$.

Its binary ranking guarantee also gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(V) &=r\binom m2+d\operatorname{OPT}_{H,\mathcal P}(V)\\ &\le r\binom m2+d(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}_H(V)\\ &\le(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}(V). \end{aligned} $$
Thus the same partition is $\varepsilon$-good for the society.

If $\varepsilon\ge1/2$, compute a $3/2$-approximate weighted ranking and output singleton blocks in that order. Local chaos is vacuous, and
$$ \frac32\operatorname{OPT}(V) \le(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}(V). $$
This completes the proof. $\square$

The hypothesis of this theorem is polynomial-time recognizable by computing all minority weights and testing their equality.

3. A weighted random-cut lemma

The next lemma applies to arbitrary complementary weights. It supplies a quantitative bound on the loss caused by cutting an approximately locally optimal ranking.

For a ranking $\pi$ of an $n$-item set $U$, let $a_v$ denote the position of $v$. Moving $v$ to position $j$, while preserving the order of the other items, produces $\pi_{v\to j}$. Define its improvement by
$$ \Delta_\pi(v,j)=C_\mu(\pi;U)-C_\mu(\pi_{v\to j};U). $$

Lemma 2. Let $\pi,\sigma$ be rankings of $U$, with positions $a_v,b_v$, respectively, and put
$$ F=\sum_{v\in U}|a_v-b_v|. $$
Choose $k$ uniformly from
$$ K_n=\{\lceil n/4\rceil,\ldots,\lfloor3n/4\rfloor\}, $$
where $n\ge2$. Set
$$ L_k=\{v:a_v\le k\}, \qquad R_k=U\setminus L_k, $$
and
$$ X_k=\{v:(a_v\le k)\ne(b_v\le k)\}. $$
Then
$$ \mathbb E_k\!\left[\operatorname{OPT}_{(L_k,R_k)}(U)\right] \le C_\mu(\sigma;U) +\mathbb E_k\!\left[\sum_{v\in X_k}\Delta_\pi(v,b_v)\right] +\frac{24F^{3/2}}n. $$

Proof. We first bound how many displacement intervals can cross one cut. Define
$$ \Psi=\max_{0\le j\le n} \left|\{v:(a_v\le j)\ne(b_v\le j)\}\right|. $$
At any cut $j$, the numbers crossing in the two directions are equal: both rankings have exactly $j$ items in their first $j$ positions. Call their common number $t$.

For the $t$ items with $a_v\le j<b_v$, distinctness of the starting and finishing positions gives
$$ \sum_v(b_v-a_v) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{t}(j+i) -\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(j-i) =t^2. $$
The items crossing in the opposite direction contribute at least another $t^2$. Therefore
$$ F\ge2t^2, \qquad \Psi\le\sqrt{2F}. $$
If $\pi\ne\sigma$, some cut is crossed, and hence $\Psi\ge2$.

Fix $k$ and write $X=X_k$, $h=|X|$. Define keys
$$ c_v= \begin{cases} a_v,&v\in X,\\ b_v,&v\notin X. \end{cases} $$
Let $\rho$ sort the items by $c_v$, breaking ties arbitrarily. Every item in $L_k$ has key at most $k$, and every item in $R_k$ has key greater than $k$. Thus $\rho$ respects $(L_k,R_k)$.

We compare $C_\mu(\rho;U)-C_\mu(\sigma;U)$ with
$$ T_k=\sum_{v\in X}\Delta_\pi(v,b_v). $$

Pairs with neither endpoint in $X$ have the same order in $\rho$ and $\sigma$. For a pair with both endpoints in $X$, its contribution to the actual cost difference has absolute value at most $1$. Its contribution to $T_k$ has absolute value at most $2$, since it occurs at most once in each endpoint’s move. The discrepancy is therefore at most $3$ per such pair.

Now fix $v\in X$ and consider its pairs with $w\notin X$.

If $a_v<b_v$, its contribution to $\Delta_\pi(v,b_v)$ is
$$ q(w,v)-q(v,w) $$
for precisely those $w$ satisfying
$$ a_v<a_w\le b_v. $$
Its contribution to $C_\mu(\rho;U)-C_\mu(\sigma;U)$ has the same value for those $w$ satisfying
$$ a_v<b_w<b_v, $$
with a possible additional contribution at the tie $b_w=a_v$.

If $a_v>b_v$, the corresponding value is
$$ q(v,w)-q(w,v), $$
with membership conditions
$$ b_v\le a_w<a_v $$
and
$$ b_v<b_w<a_v, $$
again with a possible contribution at $b_w=a_v$.

For either direction, replacing $a_w$ by $b_w$ in the relevant integer interval can change membership only if $w$ crosses one of that interval’s two boundary cuts. At most $2\Psi$ items do so. The tie at $b_w=a_v$ involves at most one additional item. Since each contribution has absolute value at most $1$, the discrepancy for this $v$ is at most $2\Psi+1$.

Summing these bounds gives
$$ C_\mu(\rho;U)-C_\mu(\sigma;U) \le T_k+h(2\Psi+1)+3\binom h2. $$
If $F>0$, then $\Psi\ge2$ and $h\le\Psi$, so
$$ h(2\Psi+1)+3\binom h2 \le4h\Psi. $$

There are at least $n/4$ possible cuts in $K_n$. Each item belongs to $X_k$ for at most $|a_v-b_v|$ of those cuts. Hence
$$ \mathbb E_k[h]\le\frac{4F}{n}. $$
It follows that
$$ \mathbb E_k[4h\Psi] \le\frac{16\sqrt2\,F^{3/2}}n \le\frac{24F^{3/2}}n. $$
Because $\operatorname{OPT}_{(L_k,R_k)}(U)\le C_\mu(\rho;U)$, this proves the lemma when $F>0$. When $F=0$, the rankings coincide, $X_k$ is empty, and the conclusion holds directly. $\square$

We also need a distance bound.

Lemma 3. For any two rankings $\pi,\sigma$,
$$ \sum_v|a_v-b_v| \le2\bigl(C_\mu(\pi;U)+C_\mu(\sigma;U)\bigr). $$

Proof. Let $D$ be the number of unordered pairs on which the rankings disagree. On every such pair, their two costs sum to $1$, by complementarity. All remaining costs are nonnegative, so
$$ D\le C_\mu(\pi;U)+C_\mu(\sigma;U). $$
Transforming $\pi$ into $\sigma$ by correcting adjacent inversions takes exactly $D$ swaps. Each adjacent swap has footrule distance $2$. The triangle inequality for the sum of absolute position differences therefore gives
$$ \sum_v|a_v-b_v|\le2D. $$
Combining the inequalities proves the claim. $\square$

Corollary 4. Suppose
$$ \Delta_\pi(v,j)\le\alpha|j-a_v| $$
for every item and destination position. Writing $C=C_\mu(\pi;U)$, a cut sampled as in Lemma 2 satisfies
$$ \mathbb E_k\!\left[ \operatorname{OPT}_{(L_k,R_k)}(U)-\operatorname{OPT}(U) \right] \le4\alpha C+\frac{192C^{3/2}}n. $$

Proof. Take $\sigma$ optimal. Lemma 3 and $\operatorname{OPT}(U)\le C$ give $F\le4C$. Also,
$$ T_k\le\alpha\sum_{v\in X_k}|a_v-b_v|\le\alpha F. $$
Substitute these bounds into Lemma 2. $\square$

4. A recursion-tree estimate

The following estimate prevents the cubic error term from accumulating an additional logarithmic factor.

Lemma 5. Consider a finite binary tree. Each node $X$ has a positive size $n_X$. At every internal node, its children $Y,Z$ satisfy
$$ n_Y+n_Z=n_X, \qquad \frac14n_X\le n_Y,n_Z\le\frac34n_X. $$
Assign nonnegative costs $c_X$ to internal nodes and cost zero to leaves. Suppose
$$ c_Y+c_Z\le c_X, \qquad c_X\le\delta^2n_X^2. $$
Then, if the root is internal,
$$ \sum_{X\text{ internal}}\frac{c_X^{3/2}}{n_X} \le8\delta c_{\mathrm{root}}. $$

Proof. Put
$$ g_X=\frac{c_X^{3/2}}{n_X}, \qquad \Phi_X=8\delta c_X-4g_X. $$
The density bound implies $g_X\le\delta c_X$, so $\Phi_X\ge0$.

Fix an internal node with cost $c>0$, size $n$, child costs $c_1,c_2$, and child sizes $n_1,n_2$. Write
$$ t=\frac{c_1+c_2}{c}\in[0,1]. $$
For a fixed sum $S=c_1+c_2$, minimizing
$$ \frac{c_1^{3/2}}{n_1}+\frac{c_2^{3/2}}{n_2} $$
gives $c_1:c_2=n_1^2:n_2^2$. Substitution yields the minimum
$$ \frac{S^{3/2}}{\sqrt{n_1^2+n_2^2}}. $$
This also holds when $S=0$. Balanced sizes give
$$ n_1^2+n_2^2\le\frac58n^2. $$
Thus, writing $g=c^{3/2}/n$,
$$ g_1+g_2 \ge\frac4{\sqrt{10}}t^{3/2}g \ge\frac54t^{3/2}g. $$
Consequently,
$$ \begin{aligned} \Phi_X-\Phi_Y-\Phi_Z &=8\delta c(1-t)+4(g_1+g_2-g)\\ &\ge\bigl(4-8t+5t^{3/2}\bigr)g\\ &\ge g. \end{aligned} $$
For the first inequality we used $\delta c\ge g$. For the second, the function $4-8t+5t^{3/2}$ decreases on $[0,1]$, because its derivative is
$$ -8+\frac{15}{2}\sqrt t\le-\frac12, $$
and its value at $1$ is $1$.

If $c=0$, both child costs are zero, and the same inequality holds directly.

Summing over internal nodes telescopes the potentials. Leaf potentials are zero, giving
$$ \sum_Xg_X\le\Phi_{\mathrm{root}} \le8\delta c_{\mathrm{root}}. $$
$\square$

5. A general uniform algorithm with explicit slack

Theorem 6. For every rational $\delta\in(0,1)$, there is a randomized algorithm running in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L,\delta^{-1}$ that returns an ordered partition $\mathcal P=(V_1,\ldots,V_k)$ with the following properties:

  • Every nonsingleton block satisfies

$$ \operatorname{OPT}(V_i)\ge\delta^2\binom{|V_i|}{2}. $$

  • With probability at least $1/2$,

$$ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(V) \le(1+10^4\delta)\operatorname{OPT}(V). $$

In particular, the first property implies the task’s local-chaos condition with parameter $\delta$.

Algorithm

Set
$$ \alpha=\frac{\delta}{m}. $$
A recursive call operates on a nonempty subset $U$, optionally with an inherited ranking.

  • If $|U|=1$, return the singleton block.
  • Compute a deterministic $2$-approximate ranking $\gamma$ of $U$, and let $A=C_\mu(\gamma;U)$.
  • If

$$ A\ge2\delta^2\binom{|U|}{2}, $$
return the single block $U$.

  • Otherwise, choose the cheaper of $\gamma$ and the inherited ranking, when one is available.
  • Repeatedly execute any move satisfying

$$ \Delta_\pi(v,j)>\alpha|j-a_v|. $$
Stop when no such move exists.

  • Sample a cut uniformly from $K_{|U|}$, split the resulting ranking there, recurse on its two sides with their inherited restrictions, and concatenate the returned partitions.

All cuts produce two nonempty subsets. Thus recursion returns an ordered partition of $V$.

Local chaos

A nonsingleton block is returned only in step 3. The approximation guarantee gives $A\le2\operatorname{OPT}(U)$, whence
$$ \operatorname{OPT}(U)\ge\frac A2 \ge\delta^2\binom{|U|}{2}. $$
This is an individual certificate for every nonsingleton block. Summing over the big blocks proves local chaos.

Polynomial running time

Every accepted move decreases cost by more than $\alpha$, because it has positive integer displacement. Costs lie between zero and $\binom m2$, so each call executes at most
$$ \frac{\binom m2}{\alpha} $$
moves. Each iteration can evaluate all moves by polynomially many rational operations. There are at most $2m-1$ recursive calls.

All comparison quantities are sums of input weights and rational thresholds with polynomial encoding length. Together with the fixed-accuracy ranking tool, this proves polynomial time in $m,\tau,L,\delta^{-1}$.

Exact uniform integer sampling uses rejection sampling with acceptance probability at least $1/2$. Capping it at
$$ \left\lceil\frac{\log(100m)}{\log2}\right\rceil $$
trials per cut gives a polynomial bound on random bits and runtime. Its total failure probability over at most $m-1$ cuts is less than $1/100$. We first analyze uncapped sampling and then account for this possible failure.

Ranking guarantee

At an internal recursion node $X$, let $n_X=|U_X|$ and let $c_X$ be the cost of the ranking after local improvement.

Since step 3 did not stop this call,
$$ c_X\le A_X <2\delta^2\binom{n_X}{2} <\delta^2n_X^2. $$
At the root,
$$ c_{\mathrm{root}}\le2\operatorname{OPT}(V). $$

Assign cost zero to leaves. An internal child begins with a restriction of its parent’s improved ranking; choosing a cheaper ranking and applying improvements can only lower that cost. Therefore
$$ c_Y+c_Z\le c_X. $$
The cuts also give child sizes between one quarter and three quarters of the parent size. Lemma 5 consequently applies:
$$ \sum_{X\text{ internal}}\frac{c_X^{3/2}}{n_X} \le8\delta c_{\mathrm{root}}. $$

The same cost inequalities show that the sum of internal-node costs at any depth is at most $c_{\mathrm{root}}$. Every path has at most $m$ internal nodes, so
$$ \sum_{X\text{ internal}}c_X \le m c_{\mathrm{root}}. $$

For a realized split at $X$, define its optimality loss by
$$ D_X =\operatorname{OPT}_{(U_Y,U_Z)}(U_X)-\operatorname{OPT}(U_X). $$
This is nonnegative. Conditional on the history preceding the cut, local improvement has terminated, so Corollary 4 gives
$$ \mathbb E[D_X\mid\text{history}] \le4\alpha c_X+\frac{192c_X^{3/2}}{n_X}. $$

These losses telescope exactly. To see this, let $\beta_X$ be the fixed cross-pair cost of placing $U_Y$ before $U_Z$. Then
$$ D_X =\beta_X+\operatorname{OPT}(U_Y) +\operatorname{OPT}(U_Z)-\operatorname{OPT}(U_X). $$
Meanwhile, respecting the final partition fixes all cross-block costs and permits independent optimization within its leaves. Hence
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(V)-\operatorname{OPT}(V) =\sum_{X\text{ internal}}D_X. $$

Taking expectations, using conditional expectation and the two deterministic tree bounds, yields
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\!\left[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(V)-\operatorname{OPT}(V) \right] &\le4\alpha m c_{\mathrm{root}} +192\cdot8\delta c_{\mathrm{root}}\\ &=1540\delta c_{\mathrm{root}}\\ &\le3080\delta\operatorname{OPT}(V). \end{aligned} $$
If the root was returned without a split, the loss is zero and the same bound holds.

When $\operatorname{OPT}(V)>0$, Markov’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr\!\left[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathcal P}(V)-\operatorname{OPT}(V) >10^4\delta\operatorname{OPT}(V) \right] \le\frac{3080}{10^4}. $$
If $\operatorname{OPT}(V)=0$, the nonnegative loss has expectation zero and therefore vanishes with probability one.

Finally, coupling capped and uncapped sampling loses less than $1/100$ success probability. The bounded-runtime implementation thus succeeds with probability at least
$$ 1-\frac{3080}{10^4}-\frac1{100} >\frac12. $$
This proves the theorem. $\square$

6. What remains open

Theorem 6 gives a uniform polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary societies, with the correct exponents in both guarantees. Its explicit constant mismatch remains material.

Taking $\delta=\varepsilon$ gives the required local-chaos threshold, but only a ranking factor of $1+10^4\varepsilon$. Taking $\delta=\varepsilon/10^4$ gives the required ranking factor, but only
$$ \sum_{i\in B}\operatorname{OPT}(V_i) \ge\frac{\varepsilon^2}{10^8} \sum_{i\in B}\binom{|V_i|}{2}. $$
Neither substitution proves $\varepsilon$-goodness as defined in the task.

Thus the exact question remains unresolved for unrestricted complementary rational weights. No hardness reduction is established here.
