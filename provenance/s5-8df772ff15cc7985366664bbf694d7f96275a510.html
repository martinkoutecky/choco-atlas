Result

The approximation is computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). This holds for binary-encoded \(d,T,\varepsilon\), including exponentially large \(d\) or \(T\).

The proof uses two different horizon reductions:

  • For large \(d\), the loss rate becomes negligible after an interval of length \(O(p\log(1/\varepsilon)/d)\).
  • For moderate \(d\), a contraction estimate permits replacing the trajectory after time \(O(p\log(\tau/\varepsilon))\) by its loss rate at that time.

A Taylor algorithm with polynomial dependence on the number of requested precision bits completes the argument. The analysis below concerns the deterministic ODE specified in the question, whose modeling context is [Dai–He (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.09757v2).

1. Aggregate hard mass and basic bounds

Write \(h=py\), \(q=p\lambda\), and \(a=1-q\). Thus \(a,q>0\) and \(a+q=1\). The ODE becomes

\(F(x,h)=a-x-x(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dh}-h(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dh/p}\),

\(G(x,h)=q-2h+he^{-dh/p}-x(1-e^{-dh})\),

with \(\dot x=F(x,h)\), \(\dot h=G(x,h)\), and initial condition \((x,h)=(0,0)\).

Define the loss rate

\(g(x,h)=xe^{-d(x+h)}+he^{-d(x+h/p)}\).

Then \(\ell_T=T^{-1}\int_0^T g(x(t),h(t))\,dt\).

The rectangle \(R=[0,a]\times[0,q]\) is invariant: on its faces,

\(F(0,h)=a>0,\qquad F(a,h)\leq0,\qquad G(x,0)=q>0,\qquad G(x,q)\leq0\).

The vector field is smooth, so these inward-pointing inequalities give invariance. Local existence and uniqueness, together with boundedness in \(R\), give a unique global solution.

Let \(N=x+h\). Expanding and adding the two equations gives the exact identity

\(\dot N=1-2N+g(x,h)\).

Since \(0\leq g(x,h)\leq N\), scalar comparison yields

\(\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2\leq N(t)\leq1-e^{-t}\leq1\).

In particular,

\(N(t)\geq\frac14\min(t,1)\).

Indeed, \(t\mapsto(1-e^{-2t})/2\) is increasing and concave, vanishes at zero, and has value greater than \(1/4\) at \(t=1\).

Because \(p\geq1\),

\(x+h/p\geq N/p,\qquad x+h\geq N/p\).

Consequently,

\(g(x(t),h(t))\leq N(t)e^{-dN(t)/p}\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{d}{4p}\min(t,1)\right).\) \[\tag{1}\]

We will also need a Lipschitz bound independent of \(d\). Put \(r=dx\), \(s=dh\), and \(v=s/p\). Direct differentiation gives

\(g_x=e^{-r-s}(1-r)-se^{-r-v}\),

\(g_h=e^{-r-v}(1-v)-re^{-r-s}\).

For every nonnegative \(u\), \(|1-u|e^{-u}\leq1\) and \(ue^{-u}\leq1\). Hence

\(|g_x|\leq1+p=\tau,\qquad |g_h|\leq2\leq\tau\).

Thus, for \(z,w\in R\),

\(|g(z)-g(w)|\leq\tau\|z-w\|_1.\) \[\tag{2}\]

2. A contraction estimate independent of \(d\)

We prove that the flow contracts at rate at least \(1/(256p)\) in a suitable norm.

At a point of \(R\), use the notation

\(E=e^{-r},\qquad Q=e^{-s},\qquad V=e^{-v}\).

The Jacobian has entries

\(J_{11}=-1-(1-E)Q-E(rQ+sV)\),

\(J_{12}=(1-E)\bigl(rQ-V(1-v)\bigr)\),

\(J_{21}=-(1-Q)\),

\(J_{22}=-2+V(1-v)-rQ\).

Set

\(c=1-Q,\qquad b=J_{12},\qquad B=-J_{22}\),

\(D_1=-J_{11}-c,\qquad D_2=B-|b|\).

Here \(0\leq c\leq1\) and \(B\geq1\). Moreover,

\(D_1=(2-E)Q+E(rQ+sV)\geq Q\),

so

\(D_1+c\geq1.\) \[\tag{3}\]

We next establish

\(D_2\geq k,\qquad k=\frac1{32p}.\) \[\tag{4}\]

If \(b\geq0\), then

\(D_2=2-EV(1-v)+ErQ\geq1\).

If \(b<0\), then \(v<1\), \(rQ<V(1-v)\leq1\), and

\(D_2=2-(2-E)V(1-v)+(2-E)rQ\).

It follows that

\(D_2\geq E,\qquad D_2\geq1-V(1-v)\).

When \(s\leq1\), the inequality \(rQ<1\) implies \(r<e^s\leq e\), giving \(D_2\geq e^{-e}>1/32\). When \(s\geq1\), we have \(v\geq1/p\), and

\(1-(1-v)e^{-v}\geq v\geq1/p\).

This proves (4). Notice also that \(b<0\) implies \(|b|\leq1\).

Define

\(\delta=\frac{k}{8}=\frac1{256p}\),

\(\mathcal W(\xi,\eta)=|\xi|+|\eta|+\delta|\xi+\eta|\).

We claim that every variational equation \((\dot\xi,\dot\eta)=J(\xi,\eta)\) satisfies

\(D^+\mathcal W(\xi,\eta)\leq-\delta\mathcal W(\xi,\eta),\) \[\tag{5}\]

where \(D^+\) denotes the upper right derivative.

If \(\xi,\eta\) have the same sign, then \(\mathcal W=(1+\delta)(|\xi|+|\eta|)\). The relevant column sums satisfy

\(J_{11}+J_{21}=-(D_1+2c)\leq-1\),

\(J_{12}+J_{22}=b-B\leq-1\).

The second inequality uses \(D_2\geq1\) when \(b\geq0\), and \(B\geq1\) when \(b<0\). Thus (5) holds in this case.

For opposite signs, symmetry under simultaneous negation lets us write \(\xi=u\geq0\), \(\eta=-w\leq0\). Then

\(\dot u=-(D_1+c)u-bw,\qquad \dot w=cu-Bw\).

If \(u\geq w\), then \(\mathcal W=(1+\delta)u+(1-\delta)w\), and

\(\dot{\mathcal W}=\bigl(-(1+\delta)D_1-2\delta c\bigr)u+\bigl(-(1+\delta)b-(1-\delta)B\bigr)w\).

The coefficient of \(u\) is at most \(-2\delta\), by (3). If \(b\geq0\), the coefficient of \(w\) is at most \(-(1-\delta)\). If \(b<0\), it is at most

\(-(1-\delta)k+2\delta\leq-k/2\).

Both bounds are at most \(-2\delta\). Since \(\mathcal W\leq(1+\delta)(u+w)\), inequality (5) follows.

If \(u\leq w\), then \(\mathcal W=(1-\delta)u+(1+\delta)w\), and

\(\dot{\mathcal W}=\bigl(-(1-\delta)D_1+2\delta c\bigr)u+\bigl(-(1-\delta)b-(1+\delta)B\bigr)w\).

The first coefficient is at most \(2\delta\). The second is at most \(-(1+\delta)\) when \(b\geq0\), and at most \(-(1+\delta)k\) when \(b<0\). Using \(u\leq w\), we obtain, respectively,

\(\dot{\mathcal W}\leq-(1-\delta)w\),

or

\(\dot{\mathcal W}\leq-(k-2\delta)w\).

Both imply (5), because \(\mathcal W\leq2w\). At boundaries between the cases, the upper right derivative is the maximum of the applicable linear derivatives, so the same bound holds.

Let \(\Phi_t\) denote the flow on \(R\). For two solutions, their difference satisfies a linear equation whose matrix is the average of the Jacobians along the segment joining them. The segment lies in \(R\), and the directional derivative of a norm is convex in its direction. Therefore (5) applies to this averaged matrix. Gronwall’s inequality gives

\(\mathcal W(\Phi_t(z)-\Phi_t(w))\leq e^{-\delta t}\mathcal W(z-w).\) \[\tag{6}\]

We also obtain ordinary \(\ell_1\) nonexpansivity. Indeed,

\(J_{11}+|J_{21}|=-D_1\leq0,\qquad J_{22}+|J_{12}|=-D_2\leq0\),

so the same difference-equation argument gives

\(\|\Phi_t(z)-\Phi_t(w)\|_1\leq\|z-w\|_1.\) \[\tag{7}\]

3. Reducing the integration horizon

Since \(0\leq\ell_T\leq1\), outputting zero suffices when \(\varepsilon\geq1\). Assume henceforth that \(0<\varepsilon<1\).

Choose the integers

\(k_0=\left\lceil\log_2(8/\varepsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad K_0=\left\lceil\log_2(8\tau/\varepsilon)\right\rceil.\)

These can be computed by exact integer comparisons. Their sizes are \(O(L+\log\tau)\).

Large \(d\)

Suppose \(d\geq4pk_0\), and put

\(U=\frac{4pk_0}{d}\leq1,\qquad S=\min(T,U)\).

For every \(t\geq U\), inequality (1) gives

\(g(x(t),h(t))\leq e^{-k_0}\leq2^{-k_0}\leq\varepsilon/8\).

Therefore

\(\left|\ell_T-\frac ST\ell_S\right|\leq\varepsilon/8.\) \[\tag{8}\]

Thus it suffices to integrate through \(S\), with the later loss replaced by zero.

Moderate \(d\)

Suppose \(d<4pk_0\), and put

\(H=256pK_0,\qquad S=\min(T,H)\).

Write \(z(t)=\Phi_t(0)\). For \(t\geq H\), the semigroup identity and (6) imply

\(\mathcal W(z(t)-z(H))\leq e^{-\delta H}\mathcal W(z(t-H))\leq(1+\delta)e^{-K_0}\),

because \(z(t-H)\) has nonnegative coordinates summing to at most one.

Using (2),

\(|g(z(t))-g(z(H))|\leq2\tau\,2^{-K_0}\leq\varepsilon/4\).

Consequently,

\(\left|\ell_T-\left(\frac ST\ell_S+\left(1-\frac ST\right)g(z(S))\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon/4.\) \[\tag{9}\]

When \(T\leq H\), the expression in parentheses equals \(\ell_T\) exactly.

4. Computing the shortened trajectory at binary precision

A fixed-order method with a power-law dependence on \(1/\varepsilon\) would not prove the claimed complexity. We give a Taylor construction whose order grows only logarithmically with the inverse error.

Put \(D=1+d\). On each numerical step, rescale time by \(u=Dt\), and introduce four auxiliary coordinates:

\(E_h=e^{-dh},\qquad E_y=e^{-dh/p},\qquad E_N=e^{-d(x+h)},\qquad E_M=e^{-d(x+h/p)}\).

Define the polynomials

\(\mathcal F=a-x-xE_h+xE_N-hE_y+hE_M\),

\(\mathcal G=q-2h+hE_y-x+xE_h\).

The six-dimensional lifted system is

\(\frac{dx}{du}=\mathcal F/D,\qquad \frac{dh}{du}=\mathcal G/D\),

\(\frac{dE_h}{du}=-(d/D)\mathcal G E_h,\qquad \frac{dE_y}{du}=-(d/(pD))\mathcal G E_y\),

\(\frac{dE_N}{du}=-(d/D)(\mathcal F+\mathcal G)E_N\),

\(\frac{dE_M}{du}=-(d/D)(\mathcal F+\mathcal G/p)E_M\).

The loss rate is the polynomial \(g=xE_N+hE_M\).

With exact exponential initial coordinates, the lifted system agrees with the original ODE, by the chain rule and uniqueness. Its dimension and degree are fixed; every coefficient has uniformly bounded magnitude and polynomial input bit length. In particular, \(d/D\leq1\), \(1/D\leq1\), and \(1/p\leq1\).

Uniform local analyticity

At the start of a step, the physical state belongs to \(R\), and the four exponential coordinates belong to \([0,1]\). On the complex polydisc of radius one about this initial six-vector, every coordinate has modulus at most two. The lifted vector field has sup norm at most \(128\): for example, \(|\mathcal F|\leq19\), \(|\mathcal G|\leq15\), and each auxiliary derivative has modulus at most \(68\).

On the polydisc of radius \(1/2\), Cauchy’s derivative estimate bounds the Jacobian row sums by \(1536\). Therefore Picard iteration gives a holomorphic solution on the time disc of radius

\(\rho=2^{-16}\),

remaining within the state polydisc of radius \(1/2\). Indeed, \(128\rho<1/2\) and \(1536\rho<1\).

For a scaled step length \(\sigma\leq\rho/2\), Cauchy’s coefficient bounds consequently give Taylor truncation errors at most

\(C\,2^{-n}\)

for the physical endpoint and for the step’s average loss, with an absolute constant \(C\).

To see the latter explicitly, if the loss series is \(\sum_{j\geq0}q_j u^j\), its average over the step is

\(\frac1{\sigma}\int_0^\sigma g(u)\,du=\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{q_j\sigma^j}{j+1}\).

The geometric tail bound is uniform, including when \(\sigma\) is extremely small.

Polynomial cost of a local step

For a polynomial vector field \(P\), Taylor coefficients satisfy

\((j+1)U_{j+1}=[u^j]P\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^jU_i u^i\right)\).

Here the dimension and degree are fixed. Naive series convolutions require \(O(n^3)\) rational arithmetic operations.

These operations have polynomial bit cost. More explicitly, let \(C_0\) be a common denominator of the polynomial coefficients and \(Q_0\) a common denominator of the rational initial coordinates. Both have polynomial bit length. The \(j\)-th solution derivative at zero is a polynomial of degree at most \(2j+1\) in the initial coordinates, with coefficient denominator dividing \(C_0^j\). Hence the denominator of the \(j\)-th Taylor coefficient divides

\(j!\,C_0^jQ_0^{2j+1}\).

The uniform analytic bounds control its numerator as well. Thus coefficients, convolution intermediates, and evaluations at rational \(\sigma\) all have polynomial bit length in the input length, precision, and \(n\).

The exponential initial coordinates can also be approximated to absolute error \(2^{-B}\) in polynomial time. For a nonnegative rational argument \(r\), output zero if \(r\geq B+2\). Otherwise \(r=O(B)\); range reduction to an argument at most one, a Taylor expansion, and repeated squaring suffice in polynomial bit time.

Approximating these initial exponential coordinates introduces no large-\(d\) amplification: the lifted system has uniformly bounded coefficients and Jacobian on the short scaled interval. Its solution error is at most a constant times the initial error.

It follows that, for any rational starting state in \(R\), a local physical endpoint and local average loss can each be computed with absolute error at most \(\eta\), in time polynomial in

\(L+\log p+\log(1/\eta)+n\),

using \(n=O(\log(1/\eta))\).

After each step, round the two physical coordinates to a fixed dyadic precision and project them into \(R\). Projection is nonexpansive in \(\ell_1\). Reset the auxiliary coordinates from these rounded physical coordinates at the next step. This keeps every step’s input bit length polynomial.

Accumulated error

Choose equal physical steps and let

\(N_{\mathrm{step}}=\max\left\{1,\left\lceil2^{17}(d+1)S\right\rceil\right\}\).

Their scaled lengths are at most \(\rho/2\). Set

\(\eta=\frac{\varepsilon}{64\tau(N_{\mathrm{step}}+1)}\).

By (7), a physical-state error of at most \(\eta\) per step accumulates to at most \(i\eta\) after \(i\) steps. Projection cannot increase that bound.

By (2) and (7), replacing the true starting state of step \(i\) by the numerical one changes that step’s average loss by at most \(\tau i\eta\). Thus the arithmetic mean of the computed step averages approximates \(\ell_S\) with error at most

\(\eta+\tau N_{\mathrm{step}}\eta\leq\varepsilon/64\).

The endpoint loss \(g(z(S))\) can be approximated with the same error bound.

Use these approximations in (8) or (9), respectively. Since their coefficients are nonnegative and sum to at most one, the numerical error contributes at most \(\varepsilon/64\). Together with the horizon error, the final rational output has additive error strictly less than \(\varepsilon\).

5. Running time and classification

The number of numerical steps satisfies the following bounds.

For \(d\geq4pk_0\),

\(N_{\mathrm{step}}=O(1+(d+1)S)=O(1+pk_0)\),

because \(dS\leq4pk_0\) and \(S\leq1\).

For \(d<4pk_0\),

\(N_{\mathrm{step}}=O(1+(d+1)S)=O\!\left(1+p^2(k_0+1)(K_0+1)\right)\).

The Taylor order and dyadic precision are both

\(O\!\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log\tau+\log(N_{\mathrm{step}}+1)\right)\).

A bit-operation bound is therefore

\((1+N_{\mathrm{step}})\operatorname{poly}\!\left(L+\log\tau+\log(N_{\mathrm{step}}+1)\right)\),

where the polynomial has fixed degree independent of every input parameter. Since \(k_0=O(L)\), \(K_0=O(L+\log\tau)\), and \(p=\tau-1\), this is polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

There is no additional dependence on the numerical magnitudes of \(d\) or \(T\), or on \(1/\varepsilon\). Very small supported masses introduce no inverse-mass factor. The limiting scale \(m\) is absent from the algorithm.

Thus the requested task is polynomial-time computable. NP-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions would consequently imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
