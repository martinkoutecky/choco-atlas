The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\ge k\ge3\), a finite \(P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\), and rational distributions \(\nu_g:P\to[0,1]\) with \(\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)=1\), form types \(t=(g,p)\) of mass \(\mu_{g,p}=\nu_g(p)/m\). Choose \(r_g\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\) and a \(k\)-multiset \(y\) of entries of \((r_g)_{g=1}^m\) to minimize \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{g=1}^m\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)\max_{\ell\in[k]}|p-y_\ell|\); determine the best worst-case approximation ratio, including whether every algorithm has ratio at least \(2k\) and whether the central-median mechanism achieves at most \(2(k+1)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\ge k\ge3\), and let each group \(g\in[m]\) have a finitely supported rational probability distribution \(\nu_g\) on \(\mathbb Q\). Put \(P=\bigcup_g\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\). The society is given by the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types \((g,p)\), with binary rational masses \(\mu_{g,p}=\nu_g(p)/m\); total mass is \(1\), and \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; express running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
A deterministic distributed algorithm uses a common local rule \(r_g=f_{m,k}(\nu_g)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\), independent of group labels and other groups' distributions. Its second phase sees only the representative vector and selects \(k\) distinct group indices; equal coordinates may therefore occur in the output \(y\).
Define \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)=\frac1m\sum_g\sum_{p\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)}\nu_g(p)\max_{\ell\in[k]}|p-y_\ell|\). The centralized benchmark is \(\operatorname{OPT}_\nu=\min_{z\in P^k}\operatorname{SC}_\nu(z)\), allowing repeated coordinates without a representative restriction.
For fixed \(k\), define \(\rho_k(A)=\sup_{m\ge k,\nu}\operatorname{SC}_\nu(A(\nu))/\operatorname{OPT}_\nu\), with \(0/0=1\) and positive cost divided by zero equal to \(+\infty\).
The central-median algorithm chooses \(r_g=\min\{p\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g):\nu_g(({-\infty},p])\ge1/2\}\), sorts representatives by position with ties broken by group index, and selects ranks \(\lceil m/2\rceil+\ell-\lceil k/2\rceil\) for \(\ell\in[k]\).

What is the optimal worst-case approximation ratio \(\inf_A\rho_k(A)\) among deterministic distributed algorithms for each \(k\ge3\)? In particular, does every such algorithm have ratio at least \(2k\), and does the central-median algorithm have ratio at most \(2(k+1)\)?

For finite-agent societies with group-average social cost, deterministic distributed algorithms have approximation ratio at least \(2k\), while the central-median mechanism achieves at most \(2(k+1)\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15258v1)

1. Result.

For every \(k\ge3\), the optimal worst-case approximation ratio in the specified distribution-input model is

\(\displaystyle \inf_A\rho_k(A)=2k.\)

Both bounds asked about hold. In fact, the stated central-median algorithm has ratio exactly \(2(k+1)\). A modification of its second phase, used only when \(m=k+1\), achieves the optimal ratio \(2k\).

The [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15258v1) states the finite-agent bounds in Theorems 6.3 and 6.4. The arguments below work directly with the distributions and the repeated-coordinate centralized benchmark.

2. The centralized benchmark is a single-median cost.

Define the aggregate probability distribution \(\lambda\) by

\(\displaystyle \lambda(p)=\frac1m\sum_{g=1}^m\nu_g(p)=\sum_g\mu_{g,p},\)

where a distribution assigns zero mass outside its support. Write

\(\displaystyle C(c)=\mathbb E_{X\sim\lambda}|X-c|.\)

Let \(c_*\) be the lower median of \(\lambda\). Then \(c_*\in P\), and both \(\lambda((-\infty,c_*])\) and \(\lambda([c_*,\infty))\) are at least \(1/2\).

For completeness, \(c_*\) minimizes \(C\) over the real line. If \(c\ge c_*\), then the change in distance is \(c-c_*\) for \(X\le c_*\), and is at least \(-(c-c_*)\) otherwise. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle C(c)-C(c_*)\ge \bigl(2\lambda((-\infty,c_*])-1\bigr)(c-c_*)\ge0.\)

The case \(c\le c_*\) follows identically using \(\lambda([c_*,\infty))\).

For any \(z\in P^k\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_\nu(z)\ge C(z_1)\ge C(c_*).\)

Conversely, the feasible repeated-coordinate solution \((c_*,\ldots,c_*)\) has cost \(C(c_*)\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_\nu=C(c_*).\)

If this cost is zero, every positive-mass position equals \(c_*\). Every representative and every algorithmic output then equals \(c_*\), so the prescribed zero-cost convention causes no difficulty.

3. Every deterministic distributed algorithm has ratio at least \(2k\).

Fix any algorithm \(A\) and take \(m=k\). Let

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_1,\qquad a=f_{k,k}(\eta),\qquad b=1-a.\)

The support restriction gives \(a\in\{0,1\}\). Consider the society

\(\displaystyle \nu_1=\eta,\qquad \nu_g=\delta_b\quad(2\le g\le k).\)

Local independence ensures that the first group selects \(a\); every other group must select \(b\). Since \(m=k\), the second phase must select every group. Its output therefore contains both coordinates \(0\) and \(1\).

Every positive-mass type has farthest-facility distance \(1\), so

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_\nu(A(\nu))=1.\)

The aggregate mass at \(a\) is \(1/(2k)\), and all remaining mass is at \(b\). Therefore \(b\) is an aggregate median and

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_\nu=\frac1{2k}.\)

The ratio is exactly \(2k\). This construction uses only \(\tau=k+1\) positive-mass types and rational masses. No symmetry or regularity assumption on the local rule is needed.

4. A tail-mass lemma.

We will use the following elementary bound.

Suppose \(a\le b\), \(0<q\le1\), and a probability distribution \(\lambda\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \lambda((-\infty,a])\ge q,\qquad \lambda([b,\infty))\ge q.\)

Set

\(\displaystyle S(a,b)=\mathbb E_\lambda\max\{|X-a|,|X-b|\}.\)

Then, for every real \(c\),

\(\displaystyle S(a,b)\le \frac{C(c)}q.\)

To prove this, first suppose \(c\in[a,b]\). Put

\(\displaystyle T=\mathbb E_\lambda\operatorname{dist}(X,[a,b]),\qquad D=b-a.\)

Pointwise, the farthest-endpoint distance is at most \(\operatorname{dist}(X,[a,b])+D\). Hence \(S(a,b)\le T+D\).

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle |X-c|\ge \operatorname{dist}(X,[a,b])+(c-a)\mathbf1_{\{X\le a\}}+(b-c)\mathbf1_{\{X\ge b\}}.\)

Taking expectations gives \(C(c)\ge T+qD\). Since \(T\ge0\) and \(q\le1\),

\(\displaystyle S(a,b)\le T+D\le\frac{T+qD}{q}\le\frac{C(c)}q.\)

Next suppose \(c<a\), and set \(R=b-c\). The triangle inequality gives the pointwise bound

\(\displaystyle \max\{|X-a|,|X-b|\}\le |X-c|+R.\)

On the event \(X\ge b\), the difference between the left-hand side and \(|X-c|\) is \(c-a\le0\). Writing \(u=\lambda([b,\infty))\ge q\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle S(a,b)\le C(c)+(1-u)R\le C(c)+(1-q)R.\)

Also \(C(c)\ge uR\ge qR\). Substitution proves \(S(a,b)\le C(c)/q\). The case \(c>b\) is symmetric. This proves the lemma, including coincident endpoints.

5. The central-median algorithm’s upper bound.

Every local lower median \(r_g\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \nu_g((-\infty,r_g])\ge\frac12,\qquad \nu_g([r_g,\infty))\ge\frac12.\)

Sort the representatives, with the stipulated tie-breaking, as \(u_1\le\cdots\le u_m\). The selected block has endpoint ranks

\(\displaystyle s=\left\lceil\frac m2\right\rceil+1-\left\lceil\frac k2\right\rceil,\qquad t=s+k-1.\)

These are valid ranks: \(s\ge1\), and

\(\displaystyle m-t+1=\left\lfloor\frac m2\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac k2\right\rfloor+1\ge1.\)

Let \(a=u_s\) and \(b=u_t\). The first \(s\) groups in the representative ordering each put at least half their probability weakly to the left of \(a\). Similarly, the last \(m-t+1\) groups each put at least half weakly to the right of \(b\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \lambda((-\infty,a])\ge\frac{s}{2m},\qquad \lambda([b,\infty))\ge\frac{m-t+1}{2m}.\)

The smaller of these two group counts is

\(\displaystyle h=\left\lfloor\frac{m-k}{2}\right\rfloor+1.\)

Indeed, when \(m-k=2j\), both counts are \(j+1\); when \(m-k=2j+1\), they are \(j+1\) and \(j+2\).

Only the extreme selected coordinates matter for the farthest-facility objective. Applying the tail-mass lemma with \(q=h/(2m)\) and \(c=c_*\) gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_\nu(A_{\mathrm{CM}}(\nu))\le\frac{2m}{h}\operatorname{OPT}_\nu.\)

The dependence on \(m\) is useful:

  • If \(m-k=2j\), then \(k+2j\le k(j+1)\), so the factor is at most \(2k\).
  • If \(m=k+1\), the factor is \(2(k+1)\).
  • If \(m-k=2j+1\) with \(j\ge1\), then \((k-2)j\ge1\), so \(k+2j+1\le k(j+1)\), and the factor is at most \(2k\).

Therefore the central-median algorithm has ratio at most \(2(k+1)\), and already has the desired \(2k\) guarantee whenever \(m\ne k+1\).

6. An optimal algorithm: modify the exceptional case.

Define \(A_*\) as follows. Its local rule is always the lower median. When \(m\ne k+1\), use the stated central-median selection. When \(m=k+1\), compare the two intervals

\(\displaystyle I_L=[u_1,u_k],\qquad I_R=[u_2,u_{k+1}].\)

Choose the shorter interval, breaking ties in favor of \(I_L\), and select its corresponding \(k\) group indices.

This second phase uses only the representative vector. It remains valid when representative coordinates coincide.

We prove that, in the exceptional case, its cost is at most \((k+2)\operatorname{OPT}_\nu\).

First, local median properties imply, for every real \(c\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E_{\nu_g}|X-c|\ge\frac12|r_g-c|.\)

If \(c\le r_g\), use the mass weakly to the right of \(r_g\); if \(c\ge r_g\), use the mass weakly to its left. Summing gives the certificate

\(\displaystyle C(c)\ge\frac1{2m}\sum_{i=1}^m|u_i-c|.\)

Let \([a,b]\) be the chosen shorter interval, and put

\(\displaystyle R(c)=\max\{|a-c|,|b-c|\},\qquad D=b-a.\)

Because \(k\ge3\), the ranks \(1,2,k,k+1\) correspond to four distinct groups. Among their four coordinates, two are \(a,b\); call the other two \(v\le w\). The shorter-interval choice ensures \(w-v\ge D\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle |u_1-c|+|u_2-c|+|u_k-c|+|u_{k+1}-c| \ge |a-c|+|b-c|+D.\)

Here we used \(|v-c|+|w-c|\ge w-v\). The reverse triangle inequality also gives

\(\displaystyle D\ge\bigl||a-c|-|b-c|\bigr|.\)

Combining these inequalities,

\(\displaystyle |u_1-c|+|u_2-c|+|u_k-c|+|u_{k+1}-c|\ge2R(c).\)

The median certificate therefore yields \(C(c)\ge R(c)/m\).

Finally, the triangle inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_\nu(A_*(\nu))\le C(c)+R(c)\le(m+1)C(c).\)

Choose \(c=c_*\). Since \(m=k+1\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{SC}_\nu(A_*(\nu)) \le(k+2)\operatorname{OPT}_\nu \le2k\operatorname{OPT}_\nu.\)

Together with Section 5, this proves \(\rho_k(A_*)\le2k\). Section 3 gives the matching lower bound, establishing the claimed optimum.

7. The stated central-median ratio is exactly \(2(k+1)\).

For odd \(k\), take \(m=k+1\), one group with distribution \(\tfrac12\delta_0+\tfrac12\delta_1\), and all other groups with distribution \(\delta_1\). The representatives are one zero and \(k\) ones. The central block selects ranks \(1,\ldots,k\), so its cost is \(1\), whereas the benchmark cost is \(1/(2m)\). The ratio is \(2(k+1)\).

For even \(k\), take \(m=k+1\), all but one group concentrated at zero, and the remaining group distributed as

\(\displaystyle \left(\frac12-\varepsilon\right)\delta_0+ \left(\frac12+\varepsilon\right)\delta_1,\)

where \(\varepsilon\) is rational and \(0<\varepsilon<1/2\). Its lower median is \(1\). The central block selects ranks \(2,\ldots,m\), again including both zero and one. Its cost is \(1\), while the benchmark cost is \((1/2+\varepsilon)/m\). Letting rational \(\varepsilon\) decrease to zero makes the ratio tend to \(2(k+1)\).

Thus the upper bound from Section 5 is sharp for the specified algorithm.

8. Running time.

Both algorithms can be implemented directly on the \(\tau\) positive-mass types. Sort positions within groups, accumulate their rational masses to locate local medians, and sort the \(m\) representatives. The exceptional selection requires only a comparison of two rational interval lengths.

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, a conservative bit-operation bound is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\bigl(\tau\log(\tau+1)+m\log(m+1)+k\bigr)L^2\right).\)

Local cumulative masses can be tested against \(1/(2m)\) using the supplied masses \(\mu_{g,p}\). Their exact rational representations require \(O(L)\) bits: a common denominator may be the product of the input denominators. Coordinate comparisons and the exceptional span comparison likewise use \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Hence the stated bound is polynomial in the binary input length and requires no expansion into finite agents.
