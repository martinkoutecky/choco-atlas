The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of schools \(C\), types \(T\) with strict school rankings and a strict priority order over types at each school, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a rational downward-closed feasible load polytope \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^C\), and \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), compute an assignment \(x_{t,d}\geq 0\) for \(d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) and school loads in \(\mathcal F\), satisfying both conditions below. For every type-school pair with \(x_{t,c}>0\), the mass \(E_x(t,c)=\sum_{u\succ_c t,\ d:\ c\succ_u d}x_{u,d}\) that envies its recipients is at most \(\kappa\). For every type-\(t\) mass at \(d\) that prefers \(c\) to \(d\) and can move an arbitrarily small positive amount to \(c\) while preserving feasibility, the total mass of objectors is greater than \(\kappa\); an objector is higher priority than \(t\) at \(c\), prefers \(c\) to its current assignment, and cannot move an arbitrarily small positive amount to \(c\) while preserving feasibility. When masses are \(1/n\) per applicant, \(\kappa=k/n\) is the population-scaled threshold corresponding to the paper’s integer parameter.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools. Each type strictly ranks all schools above \(\emptyset\); each school strictly orders the types, all of which it accepts. Members of the same type have equal priority and never count as strictly higher priority than one another.
The input includes a rational \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) and an explicitly listed rational inequality description of a downward-closed polytope \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^C\) containing \(\mathbf0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
An assignment has \(x_{t,d}\geq0\) for \(d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), satisfies \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\), and has loads \(\ell_c=\sum_t x_{t,c}\) with \(\ell\in\mathcal F\).
For \(x_{t,d}>0\), define \(M_x(t,d,c)\) to hold when some \(\eta\in(0,x_{t,d}]\) satisfies \(\ell+\varepsilon(e_c-e_d)\in\mathcal F\) for every \(0<\varepsilon\leq\eta\), where \(e_c\) is the school coordinate vector and \(e_{\emptyset}=\mathbf0\). This transfers mass from its current assignment; it permits no other simultaneous changes.
Define \(E_x(t,c)=\sum_{u\succ_c t}\sum_{d:\,c\succ_u d}x_{u,d}\) and \(O_x(t,c)=\sum_{u\succ_c t}\sum_{d:\,x_{u,d}>0,\ c\succ_u d,\ \neg M_x(u,d,c)}x_{u,d}\), both evaluated at the current assignment.
Require \(E_x(t,c)\leq\kappa\) whenever \(x_{t,c}>0\), and \(O_x(t,c)>\kappa\) whenever \(x_{t,d}>0\), \(c\succ_t d\), and \(M_x(t,d,c)\). For equal applicant masses \(1/n\), the scaled discrete threshold is \(\kappa=k/n\).

Can a rational assignment satisfying these conditions be computed, or its nonexistence determined, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computational problem NP-hard?

For finite individual applicants under hereditary load constraints, an assignment satisfying both discrete conditions always exists and is computable in polynomial time, assuming polynomial-time feasibility tests ([discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00134v1)). No result settling this exact mass problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified an explicit rational inequality representation of the feasible polytope and defined L as the total binary input length.
  • Required the feasible polytope to contain zero, as hereditary feasibility requires.
  • Completed school rankings by placing the unmatched option last and assumed every type-school pair is mutually acceptable.
  • Specified that equal-type mass has equal priority and does not contribute to strictly higher-priority envy or objections.
  • Formalized arbitrarily small feasible moves as transfers from the particular occupied source assignment, including removal of its school load.
  • Evaluated each potential objector's ability to move from its own current assignment at the original allocation.
  • Required exact rational output and allowed reporting nonexistence, since continuous compatibility is not supplied by the discrete existence result.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Polynomial-time construction

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. Every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses has a rational assignment satisfying both conditions.

The construction uses at most \(m\tau+1\) exact rational linear programs. The essential device is to require each successive assignment to improve every type’s distribution in stochastic dominance. This makes all envy masses nonincreasing.

1. Prefix masses and monotonicity of envy

For each type \(t\) and school \(c\), define the preference-prefix mass
\[ P_{t,c}(x)=\sum_{\substack{d\in C\\d\succeq_t c}}x_{t,d}. \]
Here \(d\succeq_t c\) means that \(d=c\) or \(t\) strictly prefers \(d\) to \(c\).

Say that \(y\) dominates \(x\) if
\[ P_{t,c}(y)\geq P_{t,c}(x) \qquad\text{for every }t,c. \]
All assignments under consideration have the same type masses \(\mu_t\).

Since every school is preferred to \(\emptyset\), the mass of type \(u\) assigned strictly below \(c\) is exactly \(\mu_u-P_{u,c}(x)\). Consequently,
\[ E_x(t,c) = \sum_{u\succ_c t}\bigl(\mu_u-P_{u,c}(x)\bigr). \]
Thus, whenever \(y\) dominates \(x\),
\[ E_y(t,c)\leq E_x(t,c) \qquad\text{for every }t,c. \]

Define the set of eligible pairs
\[ \mathcal A(x)=\{(t,c):E_x(t,c)\leq\kappa\}. \]
Dominance therefore implies \(\mathcal A(x)\subseteq\mathcal A(y)\). The first required condition is precisely that every positive school coordinate belongs to \(\mathcal A(x)\).

2. The algorithm

Give each type integer preference scores
\[ w_t(c)=m+1-\operatorname{rank}_t(c), \qquad w_t(\emptyset)=0, \]
where rank \(1\) denotes the favorite school. Define
\[ W(x)=\sum_t\sum_{c\in C}w_t(c)x_{t,c}. \]
A positive transfer to a strictly preferred destination strictly increases \(W\).

Initially, assign everybody to \(\emptyset\):
\[ x^0_{t,\emptyset}=\mu_t, \qquad x^0_{t,c}=0. \]
This assignment is feasible because \(\mathbf0\in\mathcal F\), and it satisfies the first condition vacuously.

At iteration \(j\), set \(\mathcal A_j=\mathcal A(x^j)\). Compute an optimal vertex \(x^{j+1}\) of the following rational linear program:

Maximize \(W(y)\), subject to:

  • \(y_{t,d}\geq0\) and \(\sum_{d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}}y_{t,d}=\mu_t\), for every type \(t\).
  • \(\bigl(\sum_t y_{t,c}\bigr)_{c\in C}\in\mathcal F\).
  • \(y_{t,c}=0\) whenever \((t,c)\notin\mathcal A_j\).
  • \(P_{t,c}(y)\geq P_{t,c}(x^j)\), for every \(t,c\).

If \(\mathcal A(x^{j+1})=\mathcal A_j\), return \(x^{j+1}\). Otherwise continue.

Feasibility and invariants

Suppose \(x^j\) is feasible and satisfies the first condition. Its positive school coordinates then belong to \(\mathcal A_j\), so \(x^j\) itself satisfies every constraint of the next LP. That LP is therefore nonempty.

Its feasible region is bounded: nonnegativity and the mass equalities imply \(y_{t,d}\leq\mu_t\leq1\). It is also closed. Hence it has an optimal vertex.

The prefix constraints imply that \(x^{j+1}\) dominates \(x^j\). If \(x^{j+1}_{t,c}>0\), the support constraint gives \((t,c)\in\mathcal A_j\), and therefore
\[ E_{x^{j+1}}(t,c)\leq E_{x^j}(t,c)\leq\kappa. \]
Thus \(x^{j+1}\) satisfies the first condition. Induction establishes feasibility and this condition at every iteration.

Moreover,
\[ \mathcal A_j\subseteq\mathcal A(x^{j+1}). \]
Every nonterminal iteration strictly enlarges the eligible set. There are only \(m\tau\) type–school pairs, so the algorithm terminates after at most \(m\tau+1\) LPs.

3. Terminal assignments have no eligible preferred transfer

Let \(x=x^{j+1}\) be the returned assignment, and let \(z=x^j\). Termination gives
\[ \mathcal A(x)=\mathcal A(z)=\mathcal A_j. \]

Claim. There cannot be \(t,d,c\) such that
\[ x_{t,d}>0,\qquad c\succ_t d,\qquad M_x(t,d,c),\qquad E_x(t,c)\leq\kappa. \]

Proof. Suppose such a triple exists. By the definition of \(M_x(t,d,c)\), choose a positive amount \(\varepsilon\leq x_{t,d}\) whose transfer from \(d\) to \(c\) preserves load feasibility. Let \(y\) be the resulting assignment.

Nonnegativity and the type-mass equalities are preserved. Also, \((t,c)\in\mathcal A(x)=\mathcal A_j\), so the transfer introduces no forbidden school coordinate.

For any preference prefix of type \(t\), membership of \(d\) implies membership of the strictly preferred school \(c\). If \(d=\emptyset\), it belongs to no school prefix. Thus the transfer decreases no prefix mass: \(y\) dominates \(x\). Since \(x\) dominates \(z\), \(y\) also dominates \(z\).

Consequently, \(y\) is feasible for the LP that produced \(x\). But
\[ W(y)-W(x)=\varepsilon\bigl(w_t(c)-w_t(d)\bigr)>0, \]
contradicting optimality. This proves the claim. \(\square\)

4. The objection condition follows

Fix a school \(c\), and define its set of feasible claimants at the returned assignment:
\[ \mathcal R_c= \left\{ t: \begin{array}{l} \text{there exists }d\text{ with }x_{t,d}>0,\\ c\succ_t d,\text{ and }M_x(t,d,c) \end{array} \right\}. \]

If \(\mathcal R_c\) is empty, there is no objection condition to check at \(c\). Otherwise, let \(u\) be its highest-priority type according to \(\succ_c\).

Because \(u\) has a feasible preferred transfer to \(c\), the claim from Section 3 implies
\[ E_x(u,c)>\kappa. \]

Now consider any type \(v\succ_c u\) and any positive coordinate \(x_{v,d}\) with \(c\succ_v d\). Its transfer to \(c\) must be infeasible: otherwise \(v\in\mathcal R_c\), contradicting the choice of \(u\). Every positive term contributing to \(E_x(u,c)\) therefore contributes to \(O_x(u,c)\), giving
\[ O_x(u,c)=E_x(u,c)>\kappa. \]

For every other claimant \(t\in\mathcal R_c\), either \(t=u\) or \(u\succ_c t\). Hence
\[ \{v:v\succ_c u\}\subseteq\{v:v\succ_c t\}. \]
The summands defining objections depend on \(v,d,c,x\), independently of the claimant except through this priority restriction. Since all summands are nonnegative,
\[ O_x(t,c)\geq O_x(u,c)>\kappa. \]

Thus every feasible preferred transfer has objection mass strictly greater than \(\kappa\), exactly as required. Together with the invariant from Section 2, this proves correctness.

5. Rational encoding length and running time

It remains to control the bit lengths across successive LPs; bounding their number alone would not suffice.

Write \(N=\tau(m+1)\) and \(K=m\tau\). Each LP has \(N\) variables and polynomially many explicitly given constraints.

Clear denominators in the original load inequalities and type-mass equalities. All coefficient matrices that can occur thereafter use rows from a fixed family: these original rows, nonnegativity rows, school-coordinate zero equations, and preference-prefix rows. There is an integer bound \(H\geq1\) on the absolute values of their coefficients with \(\log_2H=O(L)\).

Choose a common denominator \(D_0\) for the initial coordinates, with \(\log_2D_0\leq L\). Suppose all coordinates of \(x^j\) have common denominator \(D_j\). Then all right-hand sides of iteration \(j\)'s LP have denominator dividing \(D_j\): the cleared original right-hand sides are integers, while prefix bounds are sums of coordinates of \(x^j\).

At its chosen vertex, select \(N\) linearly independent active constraint rows, forming an integer matrix \(B_j\). Cramer’s rule shows that the next coordinates have common denominator
\[ D_{j+1}=D_j\,|\det B_j|. \]
Hadamard’s inequality gives
\[ |\det B_j|\leq N^{N/2}H^N. \]
After at most \(K+1\) LPs,
\[ \log_2D_j \leq L+(K+1)N\left(\log_2H+\tfrac12\log_2N\right). \]
This is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Because every coordinate lies in \([0,1]\), its numerator has polynomial bit length as well.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for exact rational linear programming provides an optimal vertex for an explicitly represented, nonempty bounded rational LP. Those hypotheses were checked in Section 2, and the preceding denominator bound shows that every successive LP has polynomial encoding length. Computing envy masses and eligible sets also takes polynomial-time rational arithmetic.

Therefore the entire construction runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

6. Conclusion

Every stated instance admits a rational solution, and the algorithm above computes one in polynomial time. It respects the stipulated single-transfer notion of feasibility and excludes same-type mass from strictly higher-priority envy and objections throughout.
