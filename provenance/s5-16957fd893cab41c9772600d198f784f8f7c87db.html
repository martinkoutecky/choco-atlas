2. Encoding Boolean assignments in one real variable

Let $\Phi$ be a $3$-CNF formula with $n\geq1$ variables and $c$ clauses. Duplicate literals can be removed from a clause, and tautological clauses can be discarded. Thus each remaining clause has at most three literals.

Define
$$ P(v)=\min(1,\max(0,v)), \qquad T(z)=\min(2z,2-2z) $$
for $z\in[0,1]$. The map $T$ takes $[0,1]$ into itself.

Put
$$ \delta=2^{-(n+2)} $$
and define continuous functions
$$ \beta_i(z)= P\left(\frac12+\frac{T^{i-1}(z)-\frac12}{\delta}\right), \qquad 1\leq i\leq n. $$
Here $T^0(z)=z$. All the iterates can share their preceding gates, so the functions $\beta_i$ have a joint circuit with $O(n)$ gates. Multiplication by the binary rational constant $\delta^{-1}$ implements the displayed division.

Every Boolean assignment occurs

Fix an assignment $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in\{0,1\}^n$. Define the inverse branches
$$ \phi_0(v)=\frac v2, \qquad \phi_1(v)=1-\frac v2. $$
Both satisfy
$$ T(\phi_b(v))=v $$
for $v\in[0,1]$.

Set $z_{n+1}=1/2$ and, successively backwards, set
$$ z_i=\phi_{a_i}(z_{i+1}). $$
Then every $z_i$ lies in $(0,1)$ and
$$ T^{i-1}(z_1)=z_i. $$
Moreover, $z_i<1/2$ if $a_i=0$, and $z_i>1/2$ if $a_i=1$.

Starting with $1/2$, each inverse branch produces a dyadic rational with an odd numerator and doubles its denominator. Consequently, $z_i$ has an odd numerator over denominator $2^{n-i+2}$. Since the numerator representing $1/2$ with that denominator is even,
$$ \left|z_i-\frac12\right| \geq 2^{-(n-i+2)} \geq 2^{-(n+1)} >\frac{\delta}{2}. $$
The clipping in the definition of $\beta_i$ therefore gives
$$ \beta_i(z_1)=a_i $$
for every $i$. In particular, every Boolean assignment occurs at some $z\in(0,1)$.

A nonnegative function whose zeros encode satisfiability

For a positive literal on variable $i$, define its value to be $\beta_i(z)$; for a negative literal, define its value to be $1-\beta_i(z)$. Denote these values by $\ell(z)$.

Define
$$ h(z)= \sum_{i=1}^n\beta_i(z)(1-\beta_i(z)) + \sum_{C\in\Phi} \max\left(0,1-\sum_{\ell\in C}\ell(z)\right). $$

Every summand is nonnegative. Therefore, if $h(z)=0$, then
$$ \beta_i(z)(1-\beta_i(z))=0 $$
for every $i$, so all the $\beta_i(z)$ are Boolean. Each clause summand being zero then says that at least one literal in that clause is true. Thus these Boolean values satisfy $\Phi$.

Conversely, if $\Phi$ is satisfiable, choose $z\in(0,1)$ realizing a satisfying assignment, as proved above. Every summand in $h(z)$ is then zero.

We have proved
$$ \Phi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h(z)=0\text{ for some }z\in(0,1). $$
We have also proved that if $\Phi$ is unsatisfiable, then
$$ h(z)>0\qquad\text{for every }z\in[0,1]. $$

The circuit for $h$ has polynomial size. All its constants have polynomial binary encoding length.

3. Turning the encoding into admissible valuations

We need cumulative valuations that remain nondecreasing, although $h$ need not be nondecreasing.

Explicit bounds

The map $T$ is $2$-Lipschitz, and $P$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Hence every $\beta_i$ is $B$-Lipschitz, where
$$ B=2^{2n+1}. $$
Indeed,
$$ \operatorname{Lip}(\beta_i) \leq 2^{i-1}\delta^{-1} \leq 2^{2n+1}. $$

For $b,b'\in[0,1]$,
$$ |b(1-b)-b'(1-b')| = |b-b'|\,|1-b-b'| \leq |b-b'|. $$
Thus each variable summand in $h$ is $B$-Lipschitz. Each clause summand is at most $3B$-Lipschitz. Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{Lip}(h)\leq K, \qquad K=(n+3c)B. $$
Also,
$$ 0\leq h(z)\leq A, \qquad A=n+c. $$

Define
$$ z(u)=P\left(4\left(u-\frac58\right)\right) $$
and
$$ w(u)= \min\left( 1,\, \max\left(0,8\left(u-\frac12\right)\right),\, \max(0,8(1-u)) \right). $$
Then $z$ is $4$-Lipschitz, while $w$ is $8$-Lipschitz and takes values in $[0,1]$. Its relevant properties are
$$ w(u)=0\quad\text{for }0\leq u\leq\frac12, \qquad w(1)=0, $$
$$ w(u)>0\quad\text{for }\frac12<u<1, $$
and
$$ w(u)=1\quad\text{for }\frac58\leq u\leq\frac78. $$

Set
$$ H(u)=w(u)h(z(u)). $$
For $u,v\in[0,1]$, adding and subtracting $w(v)h(z(u))$ gives
$$ |H(u)-H(v)| \leq A|w(u)-w(v)|+|h(z(u))-h(z(v))|. $$
Therefore,
$$ \operatorname{Lip}(H)\leq 8A+4K. $$

The society

Choose the binary-encoded integer
$$ C=1+8A+4K $$
and define
$$ F_1(u)=u, \qquad F_2(u)=Cu+H(u). $$
Give the two types masses $1/2$ and $1/2$.

These are admissible cumulative valuations. To verify this explicitly, all the functions used to construct $H$ are continuous and finitely piecewise polynomial. Thus $H$ is absolutely continuous, with an integrable derivative, and its Lipschitz bound implies
$$ |H'(u)|\leq 8A+4K $$
wherever its derivative exists. Hence
$$ F_2'(u)=C+H'(u)\geq1 $$
almost everywhere. The density of $F_1$ is $1$. Both valuations therefore have strictly positive integrable densities.

Furthermore,
$$ H(0)=H\left(\frac12\right)=H(1)=0, $$
so
$$ F_2(0)=0,\qquad F_2(1)=C. $$
The totals of the two valuations are $1$ and $C>1$, making the types distinct.

Every gate is well-defined throughout $[0,1]$. No division gates are needed. The integer $C$ has binary length $O(n+\log(n+c))$, and the entire society has polynomial encoding length.

4. Proof of the constant distance gap

Satisfiable formulas

Suppose $\Phi$ is satisfiable. Choose $z_*\in(0,1)$ with $h(z_*)=0$, and set
$$ a=\frac18+\frac{z_*}{4}. $$
Then
$$ \frac18<a<\frac38. $$

Consider
$$ x_*=\left(a,-\frac12,\frac12-a\right). $$
Its absolute coordinate sum is $1$. Its sign pattern is positive, negative, positive, and the negative interval is $[a,a+1/2]$.

For the first valuation, this interval has value $1/2$, so the partition is balanced.

For the second valuation, $H(a)=0$ because $a<1/2$. Also,
$$ a+\frac12=\frac58+\frac{z_*}{4}, $$
so
$$ w\left(a+\frac12\right)=1, \qquad z\left(a+\frac12\right)=z_*. $$
It follows that
$$ H\left(a+\frac12\right)=h(z_*)=0. $$
Therefore,
$$ F_2\left(a+\frac12\right)-F_2(a)=\frac C2, $$
which is half the total valuation. Thus $x_*$ is exact.

Finally,
$$ \|x_*-q_0\|_\infty = \left|a-\frac14\right| = \frac14\left|z_*-\frac12\right| <\frac18. $$

Unsatisfiable formulas

Suppose $\Phi$ is unsatisfiable. Then $h(z)>0$ throughout $[0,1]$, and hence
$$ H(u)>0\qquad\text{for }\frac12<u<1. $$

Assume, for a contradiction, that an exact solution $x$ satisfies
$$ \|x-q_0\|_\infty<\frac14. $$
This forces
$$ x_1>0,\qquad x_2<0,\qquad x_3>0. $$

Since the first valuation is uniform, its balance equation is
$$ x_1+x_2+x_3=0. $$
The sphere equation is
$$ x_1-x_2+x_3=1. $$
Together they imply
$$ x_2=-\frac12,\qquad x_1+x_3=\frac12. $$
Writing $a=x_1$, we therefore have $0<a<1/2$, and the negative interval is again $[a,a+1/2]$.

Balance for the second valuation would require
$$ F_2\left(a+\frac12\right)-F_2(a)=\frac C2, $$
or equivalently,
$$ H\left(a+\frac12\right)-H(a)=0. $$
But $H(a)=0$ and $H(a+1/2)>0$, a contradiction. Thus every exact solution has distance at least $1/4$ from $q_0$.

For every formula, the vector
$$ x_0=\left(0,-\frac12,\frac12\right) $$
is an exact solution: both valuations assign exactly half their totals to $[0,1/2]$. Moreover,
$$ \|x_0-q_0\|_\infty=\frac14. $$
Consequently, in the unsatisfiable case,
$$ d_\infty(q_0,Z(I_\Phi))=\frac14. $$

This completes the theorem. Since $2^{-3}=1/8$, deciding whether $q_0$ is a valid output at precision $k=3$ decides satisfiability.

Why this does not prove search hardness

The vector $x_0$ is exact regardless of whether $\Phi$ is satisfiable. A search algorithm may therefore return $x_0$ on every instance in this reduction.

The construction proves that the set of permitted outputs can have an $\mathrm{NP}$-hard membership test. It does not force an arbitrary permitted output to reveal satisfiability. That missing universal-output property is essential for a reduction to the requested search problem.

5. A search lower bound already present with one type

The following search result from the notes remains valid.

Proposition. A polynomial-time algorithm for the requested problem, even restricted to $\tau=1$ and $k=4$, would imply $\mathrm{PosSLP}\in\mathrm P$.

Here $\mathrm{PosSLP}$ asks whether the integer $N$ computed by a division-free arithmetic circuit with constants $0,1$ is positive. This is the standard numerical-computation problem. [Allender, Bürgisser, Kjeldgaard-Pedersen, and Miltersen](https://www.cs.rutgers.edu/~allender/papers/slp.pdf).

Given its input circuit, define
$$ b=\min(1,\max(0,N)). $$
Because $N$ is an integer, $b=0$ when $N\leq0$ and $b=1$ when $N>0$.

Use one type of mass $1$, with cumulative valuation
$$ F(u)=(1-b)u+bu^2. $$
Its density is
$$ (1-b)+2bu, $$
which is nonnegative and integrable. Its total is $1$.

An exact solution has two nonzero coordinates of opposite signs. Otherwise all positive-length intervals have the same sign, contradicting balance. If $a=|x_1|$, its balance equation is
$$ F(a)=\frac12. $$
Both possible functions $F$ are strictly increasing. Thus
$$ a= \begin{cases} \frac12,&N\leq0,\\[1mm] \frac1{\sqrt2},&N>0. \end{cases} $$

Every permitted output at $k=4$ satisfies
$$ \bigl||q_1|-a\bigr|\leq\frac1{16}. $$
If $N\leq0$, then
$$ |q_1|\leq\frac9{16}<\frac58. $$
If $N>0$, then
$$ |q_1|\geq\frac1{\sqrt2}-\frac1{16}>\frac58. $$
The last inequality follows from
$$ \frac1{\sqrt2}>\frac{11}{16}, $$
whose square comparison is $128>121$.

Therefore the rational comparison $|q_1|>5/8$ decides $\mathrm{PosSLP}$ for every permitted output.

Matching one-type oracle upper bound

For an arbitrary one-type instance, write $T=F(1)$. The density assumption makes $F$ continuous and nondecreasing, with $F(0)=0$. Hence some $a\in[0,1]$ satisfies
$$ F(a)=\frac T2. $$

At a rational input, each arithmetic gate can be represented by a numerator and denominator that are integer arithmetic circuits. For example,
$$ \frac{p_1}{d_1}+\frac{p_2}{d_2} = \frac{p_1d_2+p_2d_1}{d_1d_2}, $$
and
$$ \frac{p_1/d_1}{p_2/d_2} = \frac{p_1d_2}{d_1p_2}. $$
Original division gates have nonzero divisors by the instance promise.

The sign of $p/d$ is the sign of $pd$. Queries on an integer circuit and its negative distinguish positive, negative, and zero values. Thus a $\mathrm{PosSLP}$ oracle permits exact comparisons and exact selection at maximum and minimum gates. Binary constants require only polynomially many arithmetic gates, and sharing prevents duplication of subcircuits.

Consequently, the sign of $F(r)-T/2$ can be determined in polynomial time with that oracle.

Bisect $[0,1]$, retaining an interval $[\ell,h]$ satisfying
$$ F(\ell)\leq\frac T2\leq F(h). $$
Continuity ensures that every retained interval contains a median. An equality at a midpoint gives an exact answer immediately. Otherwise, after $k$ bisections, the interval has length $2^{-k}$. Its midpoint $r$ is within $2^{-(k+1)}$ of some median $a$.

The vector
$$ q=(r,-(1-r)) $$
is therefore within $2^{-k}$ of the exact solution
$$ (a,-(1-a)). $$
The same argument handles $T=0$.

Thus the one-type problem is polynomial-time solvable with a $\mathrm{PosSLP}$ oracle. Together with the reduction above,
$$ \boxed{ \text{The one-type search problem is polynomial-time solvable} \iff \mathrm{PosSLP}\in\mathrm P. } $$

This equivalence supplies no unconditional $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness conclusion.

6. Support encoding and the dyadic-output clarification

For completeness, the support encoding preserves the finite-agent search difficulty.

From societies to agents

The masses do not appear in any balance equation. Forgetting them produces a finite-agent instance with the same exact solutions, coordinate dimension, cut bound, and precision requirement.

From agents to distinct supported types

Let a finite-agent instance have $n$ cumulative circuits $F_i$, total gate count $m$, and encoding length $L$. Write $T_i=F_i(1)$.

At input $1$, all gate values are rational. Represent them by unreduced fractions. If operand numerators and denominators have magnitude at most $2^B$, addition and subtraction increase this bound to at most $2^{2B+1}$; multiplication and well-defined division increase it to at most $2^{2B}$; maximum and minimum retain an operand.

Starting with the safe bound $B_0=L+2$, the recurrence $B_{r+1}=2B_r+1$ gives
$$ B_*=2^m(L+3)-1. $$
Thus every positive $T_i$ satisfies
$$ T_i\geq2^{-B_*}. $$

Set
$$ R=m+\left\lceil\log_2(L+3)\right\rceil+1, \qquad \varepsilon=2^{-2^R}. $$
The constant $\varepsilon$ is constructed by squaring $1/2$ exactly $R$ times. Its circuit has polynomial size, although its expanded rational encoding would be enormous. Since $2^R>B_*$, every positive $T_i$ exceeds $\varepsilon$.

Define
$$ b_i=\min(1,T_i/\varepsilon) $$
and
$$ G_i(u)= \frac{iF_i(u)}{\max(T_i,\varepsilon)} +i(1-b_i)u. $$

If $T_i>0$, then $b_i=1$ and
$$ G_i(u)=\frac{iF_i(u)}{T_i}. $$
Its balance equation is equivalent to the original one.

If $T_i=0$, nonnegativity implies that $F_i$ is identically zero, and
$$ G_i(u)=iu. $$
Any partition balancing $G_i$ still balances the original zero valuation.

Every $G_i$ has a nonnegative integrable density, all new divisions are well-defined, and
$$ G_i(1)=i. $$
The types are therefore distinct. Give each mass $1/n$ and set $\tau=n$.

The construction has polynomial size. Every exact solution of the resulting society is an exact solution of the agent instance. Consequently, the identity output map preserves strong approximations for every permitted society output.

Converting arbitrary rational outputs to fixed dyadic outputs

The cited completeness theorem uses a fixed dyadic coordinate encoding. Its strong-approximation formulation and completeness result are stated in Section 2.3 and Theorem 2, respectively. [Batziou, Hansen, and Høgh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.04452).

To obtain a dyadic output at target precision $k$ from the present formulation, request precision $k+1$. For the returned rational vector $q$, round each coordinate to a nearest multiple of $2^{-k}$ in $[-1,1]$, obtaining $\widehat q$. For the exact solution $x_*$ witnessing the returned answer,
$$ \|\widehat q-x_*\|_\infty \leq \|\widehat q-q\|_\infty+\|q-x_*\|_\infty \leq 2^{-(k+1)}+2^{-(k+1)} = 2^{-k}. $$
These coordinates fit the paper’s fixed dyadic encoding.

Rounding uses integer division and takes polynomial time in $k$ and the encoded length of $q$. The resulting output has $O((\tau+1)(k+1))$ bits. In particular, a polynomial-time rational-output solver necessarily produces polynomial-length answers, so this conversion preserves polynomial-time solvability.

Conversely, a fixed dyadic output is already an allowed rational output here. Thus the encoding distinction does not change the computational equivalence. With this normalization made explicit, the support-encoded problem inherits the stated $\mathrm{BU}_a$-completeness.

7. What remains open

The new result proves unconditional $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness of validating a prescribed output, even with two types and constant precision. It also demonstrates exactly why that result does not settle the total search problem: the reduction admits a common, explicitly known exact solution.

For the requested search problem, the proved conclusions remain polynomial-time equivalence with finite-agent strong approximation, $\mathrm{BU}_a$-completeness, and the one-type equivalence with $\mathrm{PosSLP}\in\mathrm P$. A complete resolution still requires either a polynomial-time algorithm or an unconditional hardness reduction whose decoding works for every permitted output.
