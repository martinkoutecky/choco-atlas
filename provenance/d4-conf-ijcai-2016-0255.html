Partial result: the full guarantee for a stochastic subclass

The general polynomial-time-versus-hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove the requested guarantee for a subclass that includes every instance with at most one slot per medium, with arbitrary supported rational masses and arbitrary probabilities.

The subclass also permits multiple slots: after any initial free slots, each type’s success probability per unit cost must be nonincreasing along each medium. The proof compares against the task’s full comparator class, including policies without a realized-cost cap.

I also give a polynomial-size LP upper bound for general inputs and prove that its gap from the optimum is unbounded.

1. Precisely stated positive result

For each medium $v$, suppose there is an integer $z_v$ with $0\le z_v\le b_v$ such that

$$ c(v,i)=0\quad\text{for }i\le z_v, \qquad c(v,i)>0\quad\text{for }i>z_v. $$

Assume additionally that, for every type $t$,

$$ \frac{q_{vt,i}}{c(v,i)} \ge \frac{q_{vt,i+1}}{c(v,i+1)} \qquad (z_v<i<b_v). $$

Thus all free slots form an initial prefix, and the remaining probability-to-cost ratios decrease coordinatewise.

Theorem. Under these additional hypotheses, there is an algorithm with construction and worst-case execution time polynomial in $m,\tau,B,L$ producing an adaptive policy satisfying

$$ \mathbb E_{\omega,r}[F_\omega(x)] \ge (1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{exp}}(k), $$

$$ \mathbb E_r[C(x)]\le k \quad\text{for every full realization }\omega, $$

and

$$ C(x)\le 2k \quad\text{on every run}. $$

When $b_v\le1$ for every medium, the additional hypotheses impose no restriction: a zero-cost slot is an initial free prefix, and a positive-cost singleton has no ratio-ordering condition to check.

The unrestricted-item analogue is fractional-budget adaptive greedy; see [Parthasarathy, Theorem 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v125/parthasarathy20a/parthasarathy20a.pdf). The argument below proves the comparison needed here directly and supplies a bounded-randomness implementation.

2. A valid conditional comparison with arbitrary adaptive policies

An observable history $H$ records the purchased slots, their order, and their outcomes. Throughout the probabilistic arguments, histories have positive probability.

Let $S(H)$ be its purchased slots, $U(H)$ its unreached types, and

$$ f(H)=\sum_{t\notin U(H)}\mu_t. $$

For an unpurchased slot $a=(v,i)$, define

$$ g(a\mid H)=\sum_{t\in U(H)}\mu_t q_{vti}. $$

This is its conditional expected incremental reward if the slot could be purchased immediately, irrespective of whether its predecessors have been purchased.

Indeed, the event specifying $H$ depends only on outcomes already observed. Independence of the trials therefore leaves every unobserved trial with its original probability. A currently unreached type contributes its mass exactly when this new trial succeeds.

Suppose that all zero-cost slots have already been purchased. Define

$$ \rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H) = \max_{a\notin S(H)} \frac{g(a\mid H)}{c_a}, $$

where $c_a=c(v,i)$, and the maximum is defined as zero if no slots remain.

Lemma. For every feasible comparator $\pi$, run from the empty allocation,

$$ \mathbb E_{\omega,r}[F_\omega(Y_\pi)\mid H] \le f(H)+k\rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H). $$

Here $Y_\pi$ denotes the comparator’s terminal allocation on the same full realization.

Proof. Starting after $H$, simulate $\pi$ from its original empty history. When $\pi$ requests a slot already in $S(H)$, supply its stored outcome without buying it again. Otherwise, purchase the requested slot and supply its outcome.

This simulation respects the prefix rule. If a requested slot is beyond the prefix purchased in $H$, its predecessors have already been requested by the simulated comparator and consequently are already available.

At any subsequent simulation history, the unreached types form a subset of $U(H)$. Hence, for every newly purchased slot $a$, its conditional expected incremental reward is at most

$$ g(a\mid H) \le c_a\rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H). $$

Summing these inequalities by conditional expectation over at most $B$ purchases gives

$$ \mathbb E[\text{additional covered mass}\mid H] \le \rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H) \mathbb E[\text{new purchase cost}\mid H]. $$

The new purchase cost is at most the original comparator’s cost on every realization and every choice of its internal randomness. Moreover, feasibility gives

$$ \mathbb E_{\omega,r}[C(Y_\pi)\mid H] = \mathbb E_\omega\!\left[ \mathbb E_r[C(Y_\pi)\mid\omega]\mid H \right] \le k. $$

The terminal union of $H$ and the simulated comparator’s purchases covers every type covered by the comparator. Its reward is therefore at least $F_\omega(Y_\pi)$, proving the lemma. $\square$

This argument never conditions a fixed-prefix reward on an adaptively chosen terminal prefix length.

Why the theorem’s ordering condition matters

After all initial free prefixes have been purchased, let $x$ be the allocation at history $H$. If $i>x_v$, the ordering assumption gives

$$ \frac{g((v,i)\mid H)}{c(v,i)} = \sum_{t\in U(H)} \mu_t\frac{q_{vti}}{c(v,i)} \le \sum_{t\in U(H)} \mu_t\frac{q_{vt,x_v+1}}{c(v,x_v+1)}. $$

Thus the highest density among all unpurchased slots is attained by an available next slot. Consequently,

$$ \rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H) = \max_{v:x_v<b_v} \frac{g((v,x_v+1)\mid H)}{c(v,x_v+1)}. $$

This equality supplies the connection between the valid comparison lemma and an executable greedy policy.

3. The policy and its cost guarantees

Fix a lexicographic order on slots for breaking ties.

Purchase every initial free prefix first. Then repeatedly choose an available next slot maximizing its conditional expected gain divided by its cost.

Let $s$ be the cost already spent and $c$ the selected slot’s cost.

  • If no slots remain, or $s=k$, stop.
  • If $s+c\le k$, buy the slot, observe its outcomes, and continue.
  • If $s+c>k$, buy it with probability $p'$ and then stop, where

$$ 0\le p'\le p:=\frac{k-s}{c}. $$
Section 5 specifies $p'$ using polynomially many fair random bits.

For a fixed full realization $\omega$, all decisions before the final coin are deterministic. On a crossing branch,

$$ \mathbb E_r[C(x)\mid\omega] = s+p'c \le s+pc = k. $$

Every selected slot satisfies $c\le k$, because the entire cost of its medium is at most $k$. Thus the crossing purchase has realized cost

$$ s+c<2k. $$

Branches without a crossing purchase have cost at most $k$. These arguments apply to every full realization, including realizations of probability zero.

4. Approximation with an exact final coin

First analyze the ideal policy using $p'=p$. Write

$$ O=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{exp}}(k). $$

4.1 Continuous interpolation of greedy execution

For analysis only, continue the deterministic greedy rule without a budget limit until all slots have been purchased.

View a positive-cost slot as taking time equal to its cost, with its outcome revealed upon completion. The initial free purchases occur at time zero.

For a fixed time $t$, let $H_t$ be the history of completed purchases. If a slot is in progress, let $s_t$ be its starting time and put

$$ a_t=t-s_t, \qquad \rho_t=\rho_{\mathrm{all}}(H_t). $$

If execution has finished, set $a_t=\rho_t=0$. Define

$$ W(t)=\mathbb E[f(H_t)+a_t\rho_t], \qquad R(t)=\mathbb E[\rho_t]. $$

For each possible $H_t$, the event specifying that history depends only on its observed outcomes: both the next slot and its cost are determined by the history. Thus the conditional comparison lemma applies to these histories.

The greedy decision tree is finite. Its interval endpoints are cumulative purchase costs. Between consecutive endpoints, $W$ is affine and

$$ W'(t)=R(t) $$

except at the finitely many endpoints.

Also, $W$ is continuous. At completion of a slot of cost $c$, the interpolated gain immediately before completion is $c\rho_t$, exactly its conditional expected actual gain. Averaging over the revealed outcome therefore matches the expected reward immediately after completion.

Let

$$ A=W(0) $$

be the expected reward of the initial free purchases. In particular, $A\ge0$.

At time $k$, fractional interpolation matches the exact final coin:

$$ W(k)=\mathbb E[F_\omega(x_{\mathrm{ideal}})]. $$

Indeed, an in-progress slot starting at cost $s<k$ is purchased with probability $(k-s)/c$, contributing expected gain $(k-s)\rho_t$.

4.2 Differential comparison

For every feasible comparator, Section 2 gives

$$ \mathbb E[F_\omega(Y_\pi)] \le \mathbb E[f(H_t)]+kR(t). $$

Taking the supremum over comparators yields

$$ O \le W(t)-\mathbb E[a_t\rho_t]+kR(t). $$

Therefore, almost everywhere,

$$ W'(t)+\frac{W(t)}k \ge \frac Ok+\frac{\mathbb E[a_t\rho_t]}k. $$

Multiplying by $e^{t/k}$ and integrating from zero to $k$ gives

$$ W(k) \ge (1-e^{-1})O+\frac Ae + \frac1{ek} \int_0^k e^{t/k}\mathbb E[a_t\rho_t]\,dt. $$

All the extra terms are nonnegative, so this already proves the ideal policy’s approximation guarantee.

4.3 A positive margin for finite randomness

To implement the final coin with boundedly many fair bits, we need a quantified margin.

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of $k$ and all positive slot costs, and put

$$ \delta=\frac1D. $$

Every cumulative purchase cost and $k$ is an integer multiple of $\delta$. Hence every nonempty greedy interval intersected with $[0,k]$ has length at least $\delta$.

On a single outcome path, let these interval lengths be $h_\ell$ and their densities be $\rho_\ell$. Then

$$ \int_0^k a_t\rho_t\,dt = \frac12\sum_\ell \rho_\ell h_\ell^2 \ge \frac{\delta}{2}\sum_\ell\rho_\ell h_\ell. $$

Taking expectations and using $W'=R$ gives

$$ \int_0^k\mathbb E[a_t\rho_t]\,dt \ge \frac{\delta}{2}(W(k)-A). $$

Since $e^{t/k}\ge1$, the preceding differential bound implies

$$ W(k) \ge (1-e^{-1})O+\frac Ae + \frac{\delta}{2ek}(W(k)-A). $$

Set $\theta=\delta/(2ek)$. Because $k$ is a positive integer multiple of $\delta$, we have $\delta\le k$, and therefore

$$ \frac1e-\theta\ge0. $$

It follows that

$$ W(k)\ge(1-e^{-1})O+\theta W(k). $$

Using $W(k)\ge(1-e^{-1})O$ once more,

$$ W(k) \ge (1-e^{-1})O + \frac{1-e^{-1}}{2e}\frac{\delta}{k}O. $$

The elementary inequalities $2<e<3$ imply

$$ \frac{1-e^{-1}}{2e}>\frac1{12}. $$

For completeness, $e>2$ follows from its power series, while $e<3$ follows by comparing $1/n!$ with $1/2^{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$, with strict inequality for some terms.

Thus

$$ W(k) \ge \left(1-\frac1e+\frac{\delta}{12k}\right)O. $$

The margin can be exponentially small numerically, but its binary description has polynomial length.

5. Bounded randomness and polynomial time

If $B=0$, or every listed probability is zero, stopping immediately is optimal. Otherwise some slot has $q_{vti}>0$.

Buying that medium’s full prefix is feasible, since its total cost is at most $k$. It gives expected reward at least $\mu_t q_{vti}$. A positive input rational is at least $2^{-L}$, so

$$ O\ge\mu_t q_{vti}\ge2^{-2L}. $$

The number

$$ n=kD $$

is a positive integer. Choose

$$ a=2L+\left\lceil\log_2(24n)\right\rceil $$

and use the final purchase probability

$$ p'=\frac{\lfloor2^ap\rfloor}{2^a}. $$

This coin uses exactly $a$ fair random bits: draw a uniform integer from $\{0,\ldots,2^a-1\}$ and compare it with $\lfloor2^ap\rfloor$.

Rounding down the one final coin loses at most $2^{-a}$ expected reward, because total mass is one. Moreover,

$$ 2^{-a} \le \frac{2^{-2L}}{24kD} = \frac{\delta}{24k}2^{-2L} \le \frac{\delta}{24k}O. $$

Combining this loss with Section 4 gives

$$ \mathbb E[F_\omega(x)] \ge \left(1-\frac1e+\frac{\delta}{24k}\right)O \ge (1-1/e)O. $$

The cost guarantees hold because $p'\le p$.

Finally, $D$, $n$, and $a$ have polynomial bit length. Each density calculation uses

$$ g((v,i)\mid H)=\sum_{t\in U(H)}\mu_t q_{vti}, $$

with one summand per listed type. Products, sums, and comparisons have polynomial bit complexity. There are at most $B$ purchases, and each selection examines at most $m$ available slots. The policy stores the current prefixes and reached types; it does not construct the potentially exponential decision tree used in the proof.

This completes the theorem.

6. A general LP upper bound—and an unbounded gap

The following bound applies without the theorem’s additional hypotheses.

Introduce variables $p_{vi}$ for slot purchase probabilities and $z_t$ for type coverage probabilities. Consider the LP maximizing

$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t z_t $$

subject to

$$ 0\le p_{vi}\le1, \qquad p_{v,i+1}\le p_{vi}, $$

$$ 0\le z_t\le1, \qquad z_t\le\sum_v\sum_{i=1}^{b_v}q_{vti}p_{vi}, $$

and

$$ \sum_v\sum_{i=1}^{b_v}c(v,i)p_{vi}\le k. $$

Proposition. Its optimal value is an upper bound on $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{exp}}(k)$.

Proof. For any feasible policy, set $p_{vi}$ equal to the probability that slot $(v,i)$ is bought, and $z_t$ equal to the probability that type $t$ is reached.

The prefix inequalities follow because buying slot $i+1$ requires buying slot $i$. Integrating the pointwise expected-cost constraint over $\omega$ proves the LP’s budget constraint.

The event that a slot is bought is independent of that slot’s previously unobserved outcome. To see this, fix the internal randomness and all other trials. Changing the slot’s outcome cannot change whether it is purchased: the two executions coincide until that purchase, and purchases cannot be undone. Consequently,

$$ \Pr((v,i)\text{ bought and }\omega_{vti}=1) = p_{vi}q_{vti}. $$

The union bound gives the constraint on $z_t$. The LP objective is exactly the policy’s expected reward. Taking the supremum proves the proposition. $\square$

This is a bounded rational LP with $B+\tau$ variables and polynomial encoding length, so polynomial-time rational linear programming computes its optimum.

An explicit unbounded gap

Let $M\ge2$ be a power of two. Take $M$ media and $M$ types, each of mass $1/M$, and set $k=1$. Medium $v$ reaches only its corresponding type.

Each medium has $M+1$ slots:

$$ c(v,1)=\frac12, \qquad c(v,i)=\frac1{2M}\quad(2\le i\le M+1), $$

$$ q_{v,t_v,1}=0, \qquad q_{v,t_v,i}=1\quad(2\le i\le M+1). $$

All other probabilities are zero. Every medium’s total cost is one.

On the unique positive-probability realization, reaching a type requires buying at least the first two slots of its medium, at cost

$$ a_M=\frac{M+1}{2M}. $$

If $N$ types are reached, every run on that realization satisfies $C\ge a_MN$. Hence every feasible comparator satisfies

$$ \mathbb E[F] = \frac{\mathbb E[N]}M \le \frac1{Ma_M} = \frac2{M+1}. $$

Equality is achievable: buy the first two slots of one medium, and do so for a second distinct medium with probability

$$ \frac{M-1}{M+1}. $$

Its expected cost is exactly one on every full realization. Therefore,

$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{exp}}(1)=\frac2{M+1}. $$

In the LP, set every $p_{vi}=1/M$ and every $z_t=1$. The total cost is one, and each type’s probability-sum constraint has right-hand side

$$ M\cdot\frac1M=1. $$

Thus the LP optimum is one, and

$$ \frac{\mathrm{LP}}{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{exp}}(1)} = \frac{M+1}{2}. $$

The gap is unbounded. This relaxation alone cannot supply a constant-factor comparison with the optimum.

7. Why the general case remains open

The proof above requires the unrestricted maximum individual-slot density to be attained by an available slot. Arbitrary input sequences need not satisfy this property.

Replacing unrestricted individual-slot density by fixed-prefix density also fails. Consider one type of mass one, one medium, two slots, and $k=1$, with

$$ (c_1,c_2)=\left(\frac12,\frac12\right), \qquad (q_1,q_2)=\left(\frac12,1\right). $$

At the empty history, both fixed prefixes have expected gain divided by cost equal to one. But buying the first slot and buying the second only after failure gives expected reward one and expected cost

$$ \frac12+\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac34. $$

Thus the proposed comparison

$$ \text{adaptive expected gain} \le \text{maximum fixed-prefix density} \cdot \text{adaptive expected cost} $$

would assert $1\le3/4$.

The unrestricted individual-slot comparison proved in Section 2 remains valid: the second slot has density two. The difficulty is that this slot is initially inaccessible. Accounting for such later opportunities, while retaining the pointwise expected-budget guarantee, is the unresolved part.
