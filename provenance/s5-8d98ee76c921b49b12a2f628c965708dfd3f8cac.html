The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many interaction cohorts (t,μ_t) with ∑_tμ_t=1, baseline and capacity bounds on symmetric cohort-pair exposure masses q_{tu}, a per-capita budget B, and R>0, choose feasible q to minimize the worst-case mass-weighted variance of expressed opinions. Exposure between cohorts is spread uniformly across their agents; for an agent x in cohort t, the Friedkin–Johnsen operator is (\mathcal L_q f)(x)=\sum_{u\ne t}(q_{tu}/\mu_t)(f(x)-\bar f_u), where \bar f_u is the mean of f in cohort u. The adversary chooses any measurable innate-opinion field s with mass-weighted mean zero and squared L^2 norm at most R, and z=(I+\mathcal L_q)^{-1}s; minimize \sup_s\int (z(x)-\bar z)^2\,d\mu(x).

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when all numerical inputs are dyadic rationals and \(a=1\). Under the intended binary-rational encoding of capacities, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

There is an exact algorithm with bit running time

\(\displaystyle 2^m(m+\tau^3)(\tau+L)^{O(1)}.\)

The proof accounts explicitly for adversarial fields varying within cohorts.

1. The spectral objective for atomless cohorts

Let \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau)\), and let \(K(q)\) be the ordinary weighted Laplacian with off-diagonal entries \(-q_{tu}\) and diagonal entries \(\sum_{u\ne t}q_{tu}\). Define

\(\displaystyle d_t(q)=\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_{u\ne t}q_{tu}.\)

Every \(f\in L^2(\mu;\mathbb R)\) has the orthogonal decomposition

\(\displaystyle f=\sum_t\bar f_t\mathbf1_{C_t}+\sum_t h_t,\)

where \(h_t\) is supported on \(C_t\) and has integral zero. On cohort-constant functions, \(\mathcal L_q\) acts as \(D^{-1}K(q)\). On the space of zero-mean functions supported on \(C_t\), it acts as multiplication by \(d_t(q)\).

The latter space is nonzero because \(C_t\) is atomless and has positive measure: partitioning it into two sets of equal measure gives a nonzero zero-mean function.

The cohort-constant operator is self-adjoint for the inner product \(x^\top Dy\), since \(K(q)\) is symmetric. Consequently, \(\mathcal L_q\) is bounded, self-adjoint, and positive semidefinite. More explicitly,

\(\displaystyle \langle f,\mathcal L_qf\rangle = \sum_{t<u}q_{tu}(\bar f_t-\bar f_u)^2 +\sum_t d_t(q)\|h_t\|_{L^2(\mu)}^2. \)

Also, \(\int\mathcal L_qf\,d\mu=0\). Thus \(I+\mathcal L_q\) is invertible and preserves the mean-zero subspace. In particular, the adversary’s mean-zero constraint implies \(\bar z=0\).

For \(\tau\ge2\), define the cohort spectral gap by

\(\displaystyle \lambda_{\mathrm{coh}}(q)= \min_{\substack{x\ne0\\ \sum_t\mu_tx_t=0}} \frac{x^\top K(q)x}{x^\top Dx}. \)

The minimum exists because this is a finite-dimensional Rayleigh quotient. Define

\(\displaystyle \gamma(q)= \min\left\{\lambda_{\mathrm{coh}}(q),\min_t d_t(q)\right\}. \)

For \(\tau=1\), set \(\lambda_{\mathrm{coh}}=+\infty\); then \(\gamma=0\).

The orthogonal decomposition above diagonalizes \(\mathcal L_q\) into a finite-dimensional self-adjoint operator and scalar operators on the within-cohort spaces. Its smallest eigenvalue on the mean-zero subspace is therefore \(\gamma(q)\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \boxed{V(q)=\frac{R}{(1+\gamma(q))^2}.} \)

Indeed, the squared norm of \((I+\mathcal L_q)^{-1}\) on that subspace is \((1+\gamma(q))^{-2}\). Scaling an eigenfunction attaining the smallest eigenvalue to squared norm \(R\) attains the supremum.

Thus both the cohort spectral gap and every within-cohort rate matter.

2. Endpoint witnesses and exact verification

Monotonicity

If \(q'_{tu}\ge q_{tu}\) for every pair, the quadratic-form identity above gives

\(\displaystyle \langle f,\mathcal L_{q'}f\rangle \ge \langle f,\mathcal L_qf\rangle. \)

Taking the minimum Rayleigh quotient on the mean-zero subspace gives \(\gamma(q')\ge\gamma(q)\), and consequently \(V(q')\le V(q)\).

Now let \(q\) be any feasible solution, and let

\(\displaystyle S=\{\{t,u\}:q_{tu}\ne q^0_{tu}\}.\)

Replace it by

\(\displaystyle q(S)_{tu}= \begin{cases} c_{tu},&\{t,u\}\in S,\\ q^0_{tu},&\{t,u\}\notin S. \end{cases} \)

Then \(q(S)\ge q\) coordinatewise. Its actual modified-pair set is contained in \(S\), so its budget cost cannot increase. Therefore every yes-instance has a witness consisting only of a subset of at most \(m\) pairs, with each selected exposure raised to capacity.

Conversely, a subset \(S\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle 2\sum_{\{t,u\}\in S}\mu_t\mu_u\le B\)

produces a budget-feasible \(q(S)\). Thus enumerating these subsets is sufficient.

Rational verification of the threshold

Fix such a rational endpoint witness. Write \(K=K(q(S))\) and \(H=D+K\).

Let \(N\) have columns

\(\displaystyle \mu_\tau e_i-\mu_i e_\tau,\qquad 1\le i<\tau. \)

These columns form a basis of \(\{x:\sum_t\mu_tx_t=0\}\). For \(\tau=1\), \(N\) has no columns.

The threshold \(V(q(S))\le a\) is equivalent to the following rational conditions:

\(\displaystyle a(1+d_t(q(S)))^2\ge R\quad\text{for every }t,\)

and

\(\displaystyle N^\top\bigl[aHD^{-1}H-RD\bigr]N\succeq0. \)

To justify the second condition, on cohort-constant functions put \(A=I+D^{-1}K=D^{-1}H\). The inverse contraction condition is equivalent to

\(\displaystyle R\|x\|_D^2\le a\|Ax\|_D^2\)

for every mean-zero \(x\), because \(A\) maps that subspace bijectively to itself. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \|Ax\|_D^2=x^\top HD^{-1}Hx.\)

The scalar conditions give precisely the same contraction inequality on each within-cohort subspace.

All these conditions are exact rational comparisons. Positive semidefiniteness can be tested by symmetric elimination: a negative diagonal rejects; a zero diagonal with a nonzero off-diagonal entry rejects; a positive diagonal permits a Schur-complement step. These rules follow respectively by testing a coordinate vector, a vector on two coordinates, and completing the square.

Fraction-free elimination, or reduced rational Schur complements, uses polynomial intermediate bit length: after clearing denominators, the relevant minors have polynomial bit length by the bound \(|\det M|\le k!\max_{ij}|M_{ij}|^k\).

A certificate therefore uses at most \(m\) bits and is verifiable in

\(\displaystyle (m+\tau^3)(\tau+L)^{O(1)}\)

bit time. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

3. Reduction from Subset Sum

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete positive-integer Subset Sum problem: given binary-encoded positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) and a target \(T\), decide whether some subset sums to \(T\). This is the equality form of Knapsack in [Karp’s original paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may restrict to

\(\displaystyle 1\le T\le W:=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i,\)

since instances outside this range are immediately decidable.

Choose a power of two \(D\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle 4W\le D<8W,\)

and put \(M=8D\).

Create \(n+2\) cohorts: a distinguished cohort \(0\), item cohorts \(1,\ldots,n\), and a reservoir cohort \(r\). Assign masses

\(\displaystyle \mu_0=\frac12,\qquad \mu_i=\frac{w_i}{D},\qquad \mu_r=\frac12-\frac WD. \)

They are positive dyadic rationals summing to one. Furthermore, \(\mu_i\le1/4\) and \(\mu_r\ge1/4\).

Let \(J=\{1,\ldots,n,r\}\). Its total mass is \(1/2\). Set exposures and capacities as follows:

  • For distinct \(j,k\in J\), set \(q^0_{jk}=c_{jk}=M\mu_j\mu_k\).
  • For every item \(i\), set \(q^0_{0i}=0\) and \(c_{0i}=w_i/2\).
  • Set \(q^0_{0r}=c_{0r}=0\).

Finally, set

\(\displaystyle B=\frac TD,\qquad R=(1+T)^2,\qquad a=1.\)

All inputs are dyadic rationals. The construction has

\(\displaystyle \tau=n+2,\qquad m=n+\binom{n+1}{2},\qquad L=O\bigl(n^2\log(W+1)\bigr). \)

It is therefore computable in polynomial time.

4. Every feasible threshold witness yields a subset sum

Suppose some feasible \(q\) satisfies \(V(q)\le1\). The spectral formula and \(R=(1+T)^2\) imply

\(\displaystyle \gamma(q)\ge T.\)

In particular, \(d_0(q)\ge T\).

Let

\(\displaystyle S=\{i:q_{0i}>0\}.\)

Every pair \(\{0,i\}\) with \(i\in S\) differs from its zero baseline and costs

\(\displaystyle 2\mu_0\mu_i=\frac{w_i}{D}.\)

Other modifications have nonnegative cost. Hence budget feasibility gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}w_i\le T.\)

On the other hand, the capacities imply

\(\displaystyle T\le d_0(q) =2\sum_{i\in S}q_{0i} \le\sum_{i\in S}w_i. \)

Combining the inequalities yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}w_i=T.\)

This argument allows arbitrary real exposures and arbitrary decreases of baseline clique edges.

5. Every subset sum yields a feasible threshold witness

Suppose \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in S}w_i=T\). Keep all baseline edges inside \(J\), and set

\(\displaystyle q_{0i}= \begin{cases} w_i/2,&i\in S,\\ 0,&i\notin S. \end{cases} \)

Its modification cost is exactly \(T/D=B\), and \(d_0(q)=T\). We prove that every other mean-zero mode has rate at least \(T\).

Within-cohort modes

For each item cohort,

\(\displaystyle d_i(q)\ge M\left(\frac12-\mu_i\right) \ge\frac M4=2D\ge T. \)

For the reservoir,

\(\displaystyle d_r(q)=M\left(\frac12-\mu_r\right) =M\frac WD=8W\ge T. \)

Thus all within-cohort rates are at least \(T\).

Cohort-constant modes

Take any mean-zero cohort-constant vector \(x\). Write \(x_0=\alpha\). Since the distinguished cohort and \(J\) each have mass \(1/2\), the weighted mean of \(x\) over \(J\) is \(-\alpha\). Therefore write

\(\displaystyle x_j=-\alpha+y_j,\qquad \sum_{j\in J}\mu_jy_j=0. \)

Put \(E=\sum_{j\in J}\mu_jy_j^2\). Expanding and using the zero weighted mean gives

\(\displaystyle x^\top Dx=\alpha^2+E.\)

The clique contribution satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\substack{j<k\\j,k\in J}} M\mu_j\mu_k(y_j-y_k)^2 = M\left[ \left(\sum_{j\in J}\mu_j\right)E -\left(\sum_{j\in J}\mu_jy_j\right)^2 \right] =\frac M2E. \)

The selected distinguished-to-item edges contribute

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\frac{w_i}{2}(2\alpha-y_i)^2.\)

For all real \(u,v\),

\(\displaystyle (u-v)^2\ge\frac12u^2-v^2, \)

because the difference is \(\frac12(u-2v)^2\). Applying this with \(u=2\alpha\) gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\frac{w_i}{2}(2\alpha-y_i)^2 \ge T\alpha^2-\sum_{i\in S}\frac{w_i}{2}y_i^2. \)

Since \(w_i=D\mu_i\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\frac{w_i}{2}y_i^2 =\frac D2\sum_{i\in S}\mu_iy_i^2 \le\frac D2E. \)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle x^\top K(q)x \ge T\alpha^2+\frac{M-D}{2}E =T\alpha^2+\frac{7D}{2}E \ge T(\alpha^2+E) =T x^\top Dx, \)

where the last inequality uses \(T\le W\le D/4\).

This proves \(\lambda_{\mathrm{coh}}(q)\ge T\). Together with the within-cohort bounds and \(d_0(q)=T\), it gives \(\gamma(q)=T\). Hence

\(\displaystyle V(q)=\frac{(1+T)^2}{(1+T)^2}=1.\)

Both directions of the reduction are now proved.

6. Complexity and boundary cases

The reduction establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness; the endpoint certificate and exact rational verifier establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for binary-rational capacities.

Enumerating all subsets of positive-capacity pairs and applying the verifier gives an exact algorithm with bit running time

\(\displaystyle 2^m(m+\tau^3)(\tau+L)^{O(1)}\)

and polynomial space. In particular, the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(m\). Since \(m\le\binom{\tau}{2}\), it also has running time

\(\displaystyle 2^{\binom{\tau}{2}}(\tau+L)^{O(1)}.\)

The trivial thresholds behave consistently: \(a=0\) always gives a no-instance because \(R>0\) and all capacities are finite; \(a\ge R\) always gives a yes-instance by taking the baseline. For \(\tau=1\), the answer is yes exactly when \(a\ge R\).
