The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given bounded rooted buyer-community profiles \(g\), each specifying a fixed network pattern and member valuations, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over profiles, and rational per-community seller supply \(\nu_s\) at each cost \(s\), consider admissible markets with \(N\mu_g\) copies of each profile and \(N\nu_s\) sellers of each cost. Let \(B_g\) be the highest valuation in profile \(g\). Determine the limit of INA's normalized allocation \(x_{g,s}\) and its welfare gap to the efficient mass matching, where each community and each seller can be matched at most once and welfare is measured by gains from trade \(\sum_{g,s}x_{g,s}(B_g-s)\). Split ties fractionally and give an algorithm for the finite-support instance.

A buyer profile \(g\) is a nonempty connected rooted undirected graph with at most \(m\) members, each having a nonnegative binary-rational valuation; all members participate, distinct communities are disjoint, and \(B_g\) is the maximum member valuation. Each community can receive at most one item; each seller supplies one item at a nonnegative binary-rational cost \(s\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) buyer-profile and seller-cost types with positive binary-rational masses. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(\sum_g\mu_g=1\), while \(\nu_s\) is seller supply per community and need not sum to one. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For every positive integer \(N\) making all \(N\mu_g\) and \(N\nu_s\) integral, form that replicated market. Sort community maxima \(B_{(k)}\) decreasingly and seller costs \(s_{(k)}\) increasingly; define \(q_N=\max(\{0\}\cup\{k\le\min(N,N\sum_s\nu_s):B_{(k)}\ge s_{(k)}\})\).
INA matches community \(k\) to seller \(k\) for \(1\le k\le\max(q_N-1,0)\), allocating within each selected community to a maximum-valued member. Average over independent uniform permutations within equal-value and equal-cost blocks, and split member ties uniformly; this defines fractional tie-breaking.
Let \(x^{(N)}_{g,s}\) be the resulting expected number of matches of types \((g,s)\), divided by \(N\), and write \(x_{g,s}=\lim_{N\to\infty}x^{(N)}_{g,s}\), if it exists along admissible integers. 1. Result. For the allocation rule specified in the question, the limit always exists and satisfies \(W(x)=W^*\). Consequently, the sharp worst-case approximation ratio on instances with \(W^*>0\) is \(1\), and the limiting welfare gap is \(0\). The allocation and its welfare are exactly computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof below treats gains from trade directly.

2. Rank intervals and the trade cutoff. Let the distinct community maxima be \(b_1>\cdots>b_r\), and define
\[ \]
\( \alpha_i=\sum_{g:B_g=b_i}\mu_g,\qquad A_0=0,\qquad A_i=\sum_{h=1}^{i}\alpha_h. \)

Thus \(A_r=1\). Let the distinct seller costs be \(c_1<\cdots<c_d\), with
\( \beta_j=\nu_{c_j},\qquad C_0=0,\qquad C_j=\sum_{h=1}^{j}\beta_h. \)

If there are no sellers, every allocation is empty and all claims follow immediately. Henceforth assume \(d>0\), and set \(T=\min(1,C_d)\).

Associate each buyer-value block and seller-cost block with its rank interval:
\( I_i=(A_{i-1},A_i],\qquad J_j=(C_{j-1},C_j]. \)

For \(t\in(0,T]\), define \(b(t)=b_i\) when \(t\in I_i\), and \(c(t)=c_j\) when \(t\in J_j\). These functions respectively decrease and increase with rank. Therefore
\( h(t)=b(t)-c(t) \)
is nonincreasing.

Write \([z]_+=\max(z,0)\). The length of the common rank interval for blocks \(i,j\) is
\( \ell_{ij}=\bigl[\min(A_i,C_j,T)-\max(A_{i-1},C_{j-1})\bigr]_+. \)

Define
\( Q=\sum_{i,j:\,b_i\ge c_j}\ell_{ij}. \)

Because \(h\) is nonincreasing and constant on finitely many intervals, the ranks with nonnegative gain form exactly the initial interval \((0,Q]\), interpreted as empty when \(Q=0\). In particular, \(0\le Q\le T\), and \(Q\) is rational.

For every admissible \(N\), all \(NA_i\) and \(NC_j\) are integers. At rank \(k\), the replicated market has buyer maximum \(b(k/N)\) and seller cost \(c(k/N)\). Each overlap interval contains exactly \(N\ell_{ij}\) such ranks. Since the qualifying ranks form a prefix, their number is \(NQ\). Thus
\( q_N=NQ. \)

This equality includes zero-gain trades, as required by the condition \(B_{(k)}\ge s_{(k)}\).

3. Exact tie-breaking formula and existence of the limit. For \(u\in[0,T]\), put
\( \ell_{ij}(u)=\bigl[\min(A_i,C_j,u)-\max(A_{i-1},C_{j-1})\bigr]_+. \)

Fix a profile \(g\) with \(B_g=b_i\). Its buyer-value block contains \(N\alpha_i\) communities, of which \(N\mu_g\) have profile \(g\). Under a uniform permutation of that block, the probability that any particular rank has profile \(g\) is
\( p_g=\mu_g/\alpha_i. \)

Every seller at a rank in \(J_j\) has cost type \(c_j\). INA retains the first
\( \max(q_N-1,0) \)
ranks, whose normalized endpoint is
\( u_N=\max(Q-1/N,0). \)

Linearity of expectation therefore gives the exact formula
\( x^{(N)}_{g,c_j}=p_g\,\ell_{ij}(u_N). \)

No independence between different ranks is needed for this calculation.

The function \(\ell_{ij}(u)\) is continuous. Consequently, along all admissible integers,
\( \boxed{x_{g,c_j}=\frac{\mu_g}{\alpha_i}\,\ell_{ij}(Q)\qquad(B_g=b_i).} \)

Admissible integers are unbounded: every positive multiple of a common denominator of the input masses is admissible. Hence this formula proves existence of the defining limit.

It also gives a quantitative bound. Every coordinate satisfies \(x^{(N)}_{g,c_j}\le x_{g,c_j}\), and summing the coordinates yields
\( \sum_{g,j}\bigl|x_{g,c_j}-x^{(N)}_{g,c_j}\bigr|=Q-u_N\le 1/N. \)

For member allocations, let \(M_g\) denote the set of members attaining \(B_g\). Uniform member tie-breaking assigns each member in \(M_g\) the fraction \(x_{g,c_j}/|M_g|\) of matches with seller type \(c_j\); every other member receives zero.

4. Feasibility and welfare optimality. First, \(x\) is feasible. For a profile \(g\) in block \(i\),
\( \sum_jx_{g,c_j}=\frac{\mu_g}{\alpha_i}\,\bigl|I_i\cap(0,Q]\bigr|\le\mu_g. \)

Here the seller intervals cover all selected ranks because \(Q\le C_d\). Likewise, since \(\sum_{g:B_g=b_i}p_g=1\),
\( \sum_gx_{g,c_j}=\bigl|J_j\cap(0,Q]\bigr|\le\beta_j. \)

Its welfare is therefore
\( W(x)=\sum_{i,j}(b_i-c_j)\ell_{ij}(Q)=\int_0^Q h(u)\,du. \)

We next prove that no feasible allocation has greater welfare.

Let \(y\) be any feasible allocation, and let
\( t=\sum_{g,j}y_{g,c_j}. \)
The capacity constraints imply \(0\le t\le T\). Aggregate its buyer and seller marginals as
\( a_i=\sum_{g:B_g=b_i}\sum_jy_{g,c_j},\qquad d_j=\sum_gy_{g,c_j}. \)

Then
\( 0\le a_i\le\alpha_i,\quad 0\le d_j\le\beta_j,\quad \sum_i a_i=\sum_jd_j=t, \)
and
\( W(y)=\sum_i b_i a_i-\sum_jc_jd_j. \)

When \(t=0\), \(W(y)=0\le W(x)\). Suppose \(t>0\).

Choose \(k\) with \(A_{k-1}<t\le A_k\). The value of the highest-valued buyer mass \(t\) is
\( \int_0^t b(u)\,du=b_kt+\sum_{i<k}(b_i-b_k)\alpha_i. \)

On the other hand,
\( \sum_i b_i a_i=b_kt+\sum_{i<k}(b_i-b_k)a_i+\sum_{i>k}(b_i-b_k)a_i \le b_kt+\sum_{i<k}(b_i-b_k)\alpha_i. \)

The inequality follows from \(a_i\le\alpha_i\) for the positive coefficients and \(a_i\ge0\) for the negative coefficients.

Similarly, choose \(l\) with \(C_{l-1}<t\le C_l\). The cost of the cheapest seller mass \(t\) is
\( \int_0^t c(u)\,du=c_lt+\sum_{j<l}(c_j-c_l)\beta_j. \)

Since the coefficients \(c_j-c_l\) are negative for \(j<l\) and positive for \(j>l\),
\( \sum_jc_jd_j=c_lt+\sum_{j<l}(c_j-c_l)d_j+\sum_{j>l}(c_j-c_l)d_j \ge c_lt+\sum_{j<l}(c_j-c_l)\beta_j. \)

Combining these bounds gives
\( W(y)\le F(t),\qquad F(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du. \)

By the definition of \(Q\), \(h(u)\ge0\) on \((0,Q]\) and \(h(u)<0\) on \((Q,T]\). Thus:

  • If \(t\le Q\), then \(F(Q)-F(t)=\int_t^Qh(u)\,du\ge0\).
  • If \(t\ge Q\), then \(F(t)-F(Q)=\int_Q^th(u)\,du\le0\).

Hence \(W(y)\le F(Q)=W(x)\) for every feasible \(y\). Since \(x\) itself is feasible,
\( \boxed{W(x)=W^*.} \)

This proves ratio \(1\) for every instance with positive optimum, so the worst-case ratio is exactly \(1\). Such instances exist even with one-member communities: valuation \(1\), buyer mass \(1\), seller cost \(0\), and seller supply \(1\).

Moreover, writing \(B_{\max}=\max_gB_g\), the finite-market welfare satisfies
\( 0\le W^*-W(x^{(N)})=\int_{u_N}^{Q}h(u)\,du\le B_{\max}/N. \)

The last inequality uses \(0\le h(u)\le B_{\max}\) on the selected ranks and \(Q-u_N\le1/N\).

5. Exact polynomial-time computation. An algorithm can:

  • Scan each profile’s at most \(m\) valuations to compute \(B_g\) and its maximizing members.
  • Group and sort the distinct buyer maxima and seller costs.
  • Compute \(\alpha_i,\beta_j,A_i,C_j,T\).
  • Compute \(Q\) from the at most \(\tau^2\) overlap lengths \(\ell_{ij}\).
  • Compute every \(x_{g,c_j}\) from the boxed formula, then sum its welfare. The welfare gap is exactly \(0\).

This requires \(O(\tau m+\tau\log\tau+\tau^2)\) rational arithmetic operations, apart from reading the input.

For bit complexity, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Its binary length is at most the total input length \(L\). All cumulative masses, overlap lengths, and \(Q\) have denominator dividing \(D\) and polynomial-size numerators. The ratios \(\mu_g/\alpha_i\), allocation entries, and welfare terms consequently have polynomial bit length. Summing at most \(\tau^2\) such terms also preserves polynomial bit length. Standard exact integer arithmetic therefore makes the entire computation polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No replicated market needs to be constructed.

The argument above establishes the gains-from-trade conclusion directly, without invoking its discrete relative-loss bound.
