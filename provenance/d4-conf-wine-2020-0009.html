The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite hospital set \(H\), prescribed \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\subseteq H\), rational population-scale quotas \(0\le \ell_h\le u_h\), and rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), where each type \(t\) has a strict preference order over acceptable hospitals, decide whether there are masses \(x_{t,h},x_{t,\bot}\ge0\) with \(\sum_{h\in H}x_{t,h}+x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\), zero mass at \(H\setminus H_{\mathrm{open}}\), and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t x_{t,h}\le u_h\) for each \(h\in H_{\mathrm{open}}\), such that every undersubscribed open hospital \(h\) satisfies \(\sum_t\sum_{g\in H\cup\{\bot\}:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}=0\) and every closed hospital satisfies \(\sum_t\sum_{g\in H\cup\{\bot\}:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}<\ell_h\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite hospital set \(H\), let \(m=|H|\), prescribe \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\subseteq H\), and give binary rational quotas \(0<\ell_h\le u_h\) for every hospital. Hospitals are indifferent among acceptable residents.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type strictly ranks an acceptable subset \(A(t)\subseteq H\); acceptability is mutual, and every acceptable hospital is preferred to being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\).
An assignment consists of divisible real masses \(x_{t,g}\ge0\), for \(g\in H\cup\{\bot\}\), satisfying \(\sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t\), with \(x_{t,h}=0\) whenever \(h\notin A(t)\cap H_{\mathrm{open}}\). Write \(s_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\); require \(\ell_h\le s_h\le u_h\) for every prescribed open hospital.
Define \(D_h(x)=\sum_{t:h\in A(t)}\sum_{g\in A(t)\cup\{\bot\}:h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}\). An assignment is stable if \(s_h<u_h\Rightarrow D_h(x)=0\) for every open hospital, and \(D_h(x)<\ell_h\) for every closed hospital; equality at the lower quota permits a blocking coalition.

Is deciding whether such a stable assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

The discrete counterpart is NP-complete even with four prescribed open hospitals, at most five acceptable hospitals per resident, and lower quotas at most two. [Boehmer–Heeger](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14171).
No result settling this exact divisible-mass problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Replaced the graded question's allowance of zero lower quotas by strictly positive lower quotas, following the discrete domain and ensuring that prescribed open hospitals carry positive mass.
  • Required zero assignment mass at unacceptable hospitals and made acceptability mutual.
  • Defined the unmatched option as worse than every acceptable hospital; preference comparisons and demand sums use only acceptable hospitals and the unmatched option.
  • Made hospital indifference explicit, as required by the one-sided preference model.
  • Specified divisible real assignment masses and defined L as the total input bit length; zero-mass types are omitted from the support.
  • Retained normalized total population mass one and arbitrary positive rational population-scale quotas, without imposing the discrete hardness construction's special restrictions.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when every type accepts at most five hospitals and every hospital accepts at most three types.

Writing \(k=|H_{\mathrm{open}}|\), there is an exact algorithm with running time

\(2^k\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

Consequently, the divisible problem is polynomial-time solvable for a constant number of prescribed open hospitals, including four. This differs from the discrete fixed-open hardness in [Boehmer–Heeger, Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14171).

The hardness proof below uses a gadget whose stability conditions force exactly two allocations, despite allowing arbitrary real masses.

1. Exact algorithm and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Let \(F\subseteq H_{\mathrm{open}}\) be a guessed set of full hospitals. Retain all assignment and quota constraints, and impose

\(s_h=u_h\quad(h\in F),\qquad D_h(x)=0\quad(h\in H_{\mathrm{open}}\setminus F).\)

Introduce a variable \(\delta\), impose \(0\le\delta\le1\), and impose

\(D_h(x)+\delta\le\ell_h\quad(h\notin H_{\mathrm{open}}).\)

Maximize \(\delta\) by rational linear programming. Accept this branch exactly when its optimum is positive.

Soundness. A solution with \(\delta>0\) satisfies every closed-hospital condition strictly. Every open hospital is either forced full or has zero demand. Thus the assignment is stable. Hospitals outside \(F\) are allowed to be full as well; this does not affect soundness.

Completeness. Given a stable assignment, choose \(F\) to be its actual set of full open hospitals. Every other open hospital has zero demand. If there are closed hospitals, their finitely many strict inequalities have a positive minimum margin; choose \(\delta\) to be the smaller of that margin and one. If there are no closed hospitals, choose \(\delta=1\). Hence this branch has positive optimum.

Each branch has at most \(\tau(k+1)+1\) variables: acceptable open assignment variables, unmatched variables, and \(\delta\). It has \(O(\tau+m)\) constraints apart from nonnegativity bounds, and its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Exact rational linear programming therefore solves each branch in polynomial bit complexity. Enumerating all \(2^k\) branches gives the stated running time.

For membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), a verifier can guess \(F\) and solve its linear program in polynomial time. Alternatively, there is a polynomial-size rational assignment certificate: the branch polytope is bounded because \(0\le x_{t,g}\le\mu_t\) and \(0\le\delta\le1\); a positive optimum is attained at a rational vertex of polynomial encoding length, by the determinant bounds underlying Cramer’s rule.

2. A bounded-occurrence satisfiability source

We first describe a polynomial transformation of ordinary three-literal conjunctive normal form satisfiability.

For a variable with \(d\) literal occurrences, introduce separate copies \(y_1,\ldots,y_d\), one for each occurrence, retaining its sign. When \(d\ge2\), add the cyclic implication clauses

\((\neg y_j\lor y_{j+1})\quad(j=1,\ldots,d),\qquad y_{d+1}=y_1.\)

These clauses force all copies to have the same truth value: if any copy is true, successive implications force every copy true; otherwise all are false. Thus satisfiability is preserved in both directions.

Each copy appears once in an original clause, once positively in the implication cycle, and once negatively in that cycle. Consequently, each variable appears at most twice positively and at most twice negatively. Every clause has two or three literals on distinct copy variables. For \(d=1\), no implication clause is needed.

It follows that satisfiability remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for formulas with these properties. Let such a formula have variables \(y_1,\ldots,y_n\) and clauses \(C_1,\ldots,C_p\), where clause \(C_j\) has \(r_j\in\{2,3\}\) literals. We may assume \(n\ge1\).

3. Construction

We describe masses and quotas in unnormalized units, whose total mass will be \(6n\). At the end, divide every mass and quota by \(6n\).

For each variable \(y_i\), create four prescribed open hospitals \(c_i,d_i,a_i,b_i\), with quotas:

| Hospital | Lower quota | Upper quota |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(c_i\) | \(2\) | \(2\) |
| \(d_i\) | \(1\) | \(1\) |
| \(a_i\) | \(1\) | \(2\) |
| \(b_i\) | \(1\) | \(2\) |

Create four types:

| Type | Mass | Acceptable open hospitals |
|---|---:|---|
| \(t_i^+\) | \(2\) | \(c_i,a_i,d_i\) |
| \(t_i^-\) | \(2\) | \(c_i,b_i,d_i\) |
| \(e_i^a\) | \(1\) | \(a_i\) |
| \(e_i^b\) | \(1\) | \(b_i\) |

For each clause \(C_j\), create a prescribed closed hospital \(h_j\), with lower and upper quotas both equal to \(r_j\).

The additional acceptabilities and preferences are:

  • Type \(t_i^+\) accepts exactly those clause hospitals whose clauses contain \(y_i\). It ranks \(c_i\) first, \(a_i\) second, its acceptable clause hospitals next in increasing clause index, and \(d_i\) last.
  • Type \(t_i^-\) accepts exactly those clause hospitals whose clauses contain \(\neg y_i\). It ranks \(c_i\) first, \(b_i\) second, its acceptable clause hospitals next in increasing clause index, and \(d_i\) last.
  • Each dedicated type \(e_i^a\) or \(e_i^b\) accepts only its indicated hospital.

All acceptable hospitals are preferred to \(\bot\). Hospitals accept exactly the types listing them and are indifferent among those types.

The instance has

\(m=4n+p,\qquad \tau=4n,\qquad |H_{\mathrm{open}}|=4n.\)

Each core type accepts at most five hospitals because its associated literal occurs in at most two clauses. Each open hospital accepts two types, and each clause hospital accepts at most three types.

4. The variable gadget has exactly two stable allocations

Fix a variable \(y_i\), and consider any feasible assignment satisfying stability at the open hospitals. All four types belonging to this variable can be assigned only within its four open hospitals or to \(\bot\): clause hospitals are prescribed closed.

Their total mass is \(6\), while feasibility forces

\(s_{c_i}=2,\qquad s_{d_i}=1.\)

At most one of \(a_i,b_i\) is full. If both were full, their four hospitals would receive total mass

\(2+1+2+2=7>6,\)

which is impossible.

At least one of \(a_i,b_i\) is full. Hospital \(d_i\) receives mass one from the two core types. Hence at least one core type has positive mass at \(d_i\). That type prefers its choice hospital, \(a_i\) or \(b_i\), to \(d_i\), giving positive demand there. Open-hospital stability forces that choice hospital to be full.

Thus exactly one choice hospital is full.

Suppose \(a_i\) is full. Then \(b_i\) is underfull, so \(D_{b_i}(x)=0\). Every unmatched unit of \(e_i^b\) would contribute to this demand; therefore all its mass one is assigned to \(b_i\). Likewise, type \(t_i^-\) can have mass only at \(c_i\) or \(b_i\), since both \(d_i\) and \(\bot\) are worse than \(b_i\).

The total assigned mass is at least

\(s_{c_i}+s_{d_i}+s_{a_i}+s_{b_i}\ge2+1+2+1=6.\)

Because the local population mass is exactly \(6\), equality holds. Consequently, \(s_{b_i}=1\), and no local mass is unmatched.

Hospital \(b_i\) is therefore occupied entirely by \(e_i^b\). Type \(t_i^-\) must put all its mass two at \(c_i\), filling that hospital. Type \(t_i^+\) consequently puts no mass at \(c_i\). The dedicated type \(e_i^a\) puts its mass one at \(a_i\), leaving exactly one unit there for \(t_i^+\). Its remaining unit goes to \(d_i\).

The case where \(b_i\) is full is symmetric. Hence the only possible allocations are:

| Type | \(a_i\) full | \(b_i\) full |
|---|---|---|
| \(t_i^+\) | Mass \(1\) at each of \(a_i,d_i\) | Mass \(2\) at \(c_i\) |
| \(t_i^-\) | Mass \(2\) at \(c_i\) | Mass \(1\) at each of \(b_i,d_i\) |
| \(e_i^a\) | Mass \(1\) at \(a_i\) | Mass \(1\) at \(a_i\) |
| \(e_i^b\) | Mass \(1\) at \(b_i\) | Mass \(1\) at \(b_i\) |

Both allocations satisfy open-hospital stability. The hospitals \(c_i,d_i\) and the selected choice hospital are full. At the remaining choice hospital, its dedicated type is assigned there, and its core type is entirely at the preferred hospital \(c_i\); hence its demand is zero.

Define the encoded truth value by

\(y_i=\mathrm{false}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ a_i\text{ is full},\qquad y_i=\mathrm{true}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ b_i\text{ is full}.\)

This argument applies to arbitrary real assignment masses. It rules out every fractional intermediate state.

5. Closed hospitals test clause satisfaction

Consider a literal \(y_i\) in clause \(C_j\). Its reporting type is \(t_i^+\). Hospital \(h_j\) lies below both \(c_i,a_i\), and above \(d_i\), in this type’s ranking.

If \(y_i\) is true, the reporting type is entirely at \(c_i\), contributing zero to \(D_{h_j}\). If \(y_i\) is false, it has one unit at \(a_i\) and one at \(d_i\), contributing exactly one unit.

Similarly, the reporting type \(t_i^-\) contributes exactly one unit for a false negative literal, and zero for a true negative literal. Dedicated types do not accept clause hospitals.

Therefore, in unnormalized units,

\(D_{h_j}(x)=\bigl|\{\text{false literals in }C_j\}\bigr|.\)

The closed-hospital stability condition is

\(D_{h_j}(x)<r_j.\)

Since the clause contains exactly \(r_j\) literals, this inequality holds precisely when at least one literal is true. Equality, which occurs when every literal is false, is correctly rejected.

6. Correctness and normalization

Satisfiable formula implies stable assignment. Choose the corresponding gadget allocation for each variable. Section 4 verifies feasibility and open-hospital stability. Every clause has a true literal, so Section 5 verifies all closed-hospital inequalities.

Stable assignment implies satisfiable formula. Section 4 forces every variable gadget into one of its two states, defining a Boolean assignment. Stability of each closed clause hospital then implies that its clause has a true literal.

Finally, divide every mass and quota by \(6n\). The resulting masses sum to one, and every quota remains a positive binary rational. Loads and demands scale by the same positive factor, so feasibility, fullness, zero demand, and every strict closed-hospital inequality are preserved in both directions.

All normalized numbers have numerators bounded by three and denominator \(6n\), hence \(O(\log n)\) bits. With the bounded preference lists, the constructed input has

\(L=O((n+p)\log(n+p)).\)

The construction is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

7. Complexity conclusion

The reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, and Section 1 proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). Thus the exact divisible-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Its exact running-time upper bound is

\(2^{|H_{\mathrm{open}}|}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\subseteq2^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

In particular, a constant number of prescribed open hospitals is tractable, while unrestricted instances are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.
