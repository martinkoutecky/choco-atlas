The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given housing categories \(H\), a finite support \(T\) of complete strict preference rankings, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_h\), and fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\), does there exist a feasible assignment \(x_{t,a}\) of each type’s mass to an acceptable category or to being unmatched such that every feasible reassignment \(z_{t,a,b}\) of the same mass, respecting capacities \(q_h\), has strictly improving mass \(\sum_{t,a,b:\,b\succ_t a}z_{t,a,b}<c\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a finite set of housing categories, with \(m=|H|\), and put \(A=H\cup\{\bot\}\), where \(\bot\) denotes being unmatched. Each type \(t\) has a complete strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(H\); every category is acceptable and ranks above \(\bot\). Categories have no preferences.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass \(\mu_t\) as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Capacities \(q_h\) are nonnegative binary rationals; their sum is unrestricted. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,a}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\leq q_h\) for every \(h\in H\).
A feasible reassignment of \(x\) consists of real masses \(z_{t,a,b}\geq0\), indexed by \(t\in T\) and \(a,b\in A\), satisfying \(\sum_b z_{t,a,b}=x_{t,a}\) and \(\sum_{t,a}z_{t,a,h}\leq q_h\) for every \(h\in H\). Mass may stay, improve, worsen, or become unmatched; unmatched mass has no capacity bound.
For a fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\), call \(x\) stable at threshold \(c\) if every feasible reassignment satisfies \(\sum_{t,a,b:\,b\succ_t a}z_{t,a,b}<c\).

For each fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\), is deciding whether an assignment stable at threshold \(c\) exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

In discrete house allocation with complete strict preferences and unit-capacity objects, existence of a matching for which every alternative strictly improves fewer than \(cn\) of the \(n\) agents is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for every fixed \(c\in(0,1)\). [Aziz, Csáji, and Cseh](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/kstable.pdf)
No prior result settling this exact mass-and-reassignment formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Resolved 'acceptable category' under complete rankings by making every housing category acceptable and placing unmatched strictly last.
  • Specified capacities as nonnegative rationals with no required total, matching upper capacity bounds rather than compulsory occupancy.
  • Specified assignment and reassignment variables as nonnegative real masses, allowing each type's mass to split.
  • Defined reassignment conservation separately for each type and original assignment category, with capacity bounds on destination mass.
  • Allowed unchanged and worsening assignments and transitions to unmatched, since alternatives may sacrifice agents who do not improve.
  • Treated c as a fixed constant outside the input and defined L as the total input bit length.

Result

For every fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\), the decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

The key point is that maximum improving mass is a concave function of the initial assignment. Consequently, whenever a stable assignment exists, a stable vertex of the assignment polytope exists. On instances obtained by scaling a discrete house-allocation instance, every such vertex represents a discrete matching.

1. Maximum improving mass

Let \(P\) denote the polytope of feasible assignments. For \(x\in P\), define

\(\displaystyle F(x)=\max_z\sum_{t,a,b:\,b\succ_t a}z_{t,a,b},\)

where the maximum ranges over all feasible reassignments of \(x\).

This maximum exists. The reassignment keeping everyone at their current outcome is feasible. Moreover, every reassignment coordinate lies between zero and its corresponding \(x_{t,a}\), and the constraints define a closed set. Thus the feasible reassignment set is nonempty and compact.

It follows, including at the strict boundary, that

\(\displaystyle x\text{ is stable at threshold }c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F(x)<c.\)

Lemma 1. For rational \(x\), the value \(F(x)\) is computable exactly in polynomial time.

Proof. Construct a directed network with source \(s\), sink \(r\), a node \(u_{t,a}\) for each pair \((t,a)\), and a node for each category \(h\in H\). Its arcs are:

  • \(s\to u_{t,a}\), with capacity \(x_{t,a}\);
  • \(u_{t,a}\to h\), with capacity one, whenever \(h\succ_t a\);
  • \(h\to r\), with capacity \(q_h\).

The capacity one on intermediate arcs suffices because the total population mass is one.

Any feasible reassignment induces a flow consisting of its improving movements. Its value is exactly the improving mass. Conversely, given a feasible flow, put

\(\displaystyle z_{t,a,h}=f(u_{t,a},h)\quad\text{whenever }h\succ_t a,\)

and send every remaining part of each row to \(\bot\):

\(\displaystyle z_{t,a,\bot}=x_{t,a}-\sum_{h\succ_t a}f(u_{t,a},h).\)

Set all other coordinates to zero. Flow conservation makes the residual nonnegative, and the category-to-sink capacities ensure feasibility. Since no movement to \(\bot\) is improving, the improving mass equals the flow value.

Therefore \(F(x)\) is the maximum-flow value. The network has polynomially many vertices and arcs, and exact maximum flow with binary rational capacities is polynomial-time computable. \(\square\)

The ability to send all nonimproving residual mass to \(\bot\) is essential to this equivalence and is explicitly allowed by the problem.

2. Concavity and reduction to vertices

Lemma 2. The function \(F\) is concave on \(P\).

Proof. Take \(x,x'\in P\), and let \(z,z'\) be maximizing reassignments for them. For \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), the reassignment

\(\displaystyle \widehat z=\lambda z+(1-\lambda)z'\)

has row sums \(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\). For every category, its incoming mass is at most

\(\displaystyle \lambda q_h+(1-\lambda)q_h=q_h.\)

Hence it is feasible for \(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\). Linearity of the improving-mass objective gives

\(\displaystyle F\bigl(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\bigr)\geq \lambda F(x)+(1-\lambda)F(x').\)

This is concavity. \(\square\)

Corollary 3. If a stable assignment exists, a stable vertex of \(P\) exists.

Proof. The polytope \(P\) is nonempty, because everyone may be unmatched. It is bounded, because \(0\leq x_{t,a}\leq\mu_t\), and is defined by finitely many linear constraints. Consequently, every point \(x\in P\) is a finite convex combination of vertices:

\(\displaystyle x=\sum_i\lambda_i v_i,\qquad \lambda_i\geq0,\qquad \sum_i\lambda_i=1.\)

By Lemma 2,

\(\displaystyle F(x)\geq\sum_i\lambda_iF(v_i)\geq\min_iF(v_i).\)

If \(F(x)<c\), some vertex therefore satisfies \(F(v_i)<c\). \(\square\)

In fact, the same argument proves

\(\displaystyle \min_{x\in P}F(x)=\min_{v\in\operatorname{vert}(P)}F(v).\)

3. Integrality after scaling

Lemma 4. Suppose \(D\) is a positive integer such that every \(D\mu_t\) and \(Dq_h\) is integral. At every vertex \(v\) of \(P\), every coordinate \(Dv_{t,a}\) is integral.

Proof. Write \(y_{t,a}=Dx_{t,a}\), and introduce a nonnegative slack \(s_h\) for each category. The scaled constraints become

\(\displaystyle \sum_{a\in A}y_{t,a}=D\mu_t,\)

\(\displaystyle -\sum_t y_{t,h}-s_h=-Dq_h.\)

Let \(B\) be their coefficient matrix. A column for \(y_{t,h}\) has a positive one in its type row and a negative one in its category row. A column for \(y_{t,\bot}\) has only a positive one; a slack column has only a negative one.

This matrix is totally unimodular. Indeed, in any square submatrix, a column with at most one nonzero entry permits determinant expansion and induction. If every column has two nonzero entries, those entries have opposite signs, so the sum of the rows is zero and the determinant vanishes. Thus every square minor belongs to \(\{0,1,-1\}\).

The matrix has full row rank: the unmatched-variable columns and slack columns supply signed unit vectors for all rows. At a vertex of the standard-form polyhedron, the columns corresponding to positive variables are linearly independent; otherwise a sufficiently small perturbation in both directions along a dependence would contradict extremality. Extend those columns to a nonsingular square basis. Its determinant is positive or negative one, so its solution is integral by Cramer’s rule, since the right-hand side is integral.

Finally, the slacks are uniquely determined by \(y\), and scaling and adding these slacks preserve vertices. Hence every \(Dv_{t,a}\) is integral. \(\square\)

4. Exact transfer from discrete house allocation

We use the discrete hardness theorem stated in the question: for every fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\), deciding whether a discrete house-allocation instance with complete strict preferences admits a matching for which every alternative improves fewer than \(cn\) agents is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is the complete-preference result of [Aziz, Csáji, and Cseh, Theorem 5.7](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/kstable.pdf).

Take such an instance with \(n\) agents and unit-capacity objects \(H\). Group agents with identical rankings into types. If type \(t\) contains \(n_t\) agents, set

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=\frac{n_t}{n},\qquad q_h=\frac1n\quad(h\in H).\)

This construction is polynomial-time: the support has at most \(n\) types, each mass and capacity has polynomial encoding length, and all rankings remain complete and strict.

For a discrete matching \(M\), define its aggregate assignment by

\(\displaystyle x^M_{t,a}=\frac{|\{i:\ i\text{ has type }t,\ M(i)=a\}|}{n}.\)

Let \(I(M)\) be the maximum number of agents strictly improved by any alternative matching.

Lemma 5. For every discrete matching \(M\),

\(\displaystyle F(x^M)=\frac{I(M)}n.\)

Proof. Every alternative matching induces a feasible reassignment, assigning mass \(1/n\) to each agent’s movement. Therefore \(F(x^M)\geq I(M)/n\).

For the reverse inequality, multiply all capacities in the network of Lemma 1 by \(n\). Source-to-row capacities become integer numbers of agents currently assigned to each pair \((t,a)\). Intermediate capacities become \(n\), and each category-to-sink capacity becomes one.

All capacities are integral, so the integral maximum-flow theorem supplies an integral maximum flow. Each unit routed from a row \((t,a)\) can be assigned to a distinct agent currently in that row. There are enough such agents by the source capacity. Every selected agent receives a strictly better category, and each category is given to at most one agent. Leave all other agents unmatched.

This produces an alternative discrete matching improving exactly \(nF(x^M)\) agents. Hence \(I(M)\geq nF(x^M)\), proving equality. \(\square\)

We now prove both directions of the reduction.

Forward direction. If \(M\) satisfies \(I(M)<cn\), Lemma 5 gives

\(\displaystyle F(x^M)=I(M)/n<c.\)

Thus the mass instance admits a stable assignment.

Reverse direction. Suppose the mass instance admits a stable assignment. By Corollary 3, it admits a stable vertex \(v\). Apply Lemma 4 with \(D=n\). Every \(nv_{t,a}\) is a nonnegative integer, and these integers sum to \(n_t\) within each type. Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle \sum_t nv_{t,h}\leq1\quad(h\in H).\)

Thus these counts can be realized by a discrete matching \(M\): assign the indicated number of agents of each type to each outcome. Its aggregate assignment is \(v\). Lemma 5 then gives

\(\displaystyle I(M)=nF(v)<cn.\)

This proves exact equivalence. In particular, arbitrary real splitting introduces no additional yes-instances.

The strict threshold is preserved literally. Since \(I(M)\) is integral, \(I(M)<cn\) equivalently means \(I(M)\leq\lceil cn\rceil-1\); no approximation is involved.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For arbitrary rational input, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all masses and capacities. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and it makes every scaled mass and capacity integral.

If the instance is a yes-instance, Corollary 3 and Lemma 4 supply a stable vertex \(v\) whose coordinates have the form \(k_{t,a}/D\). Since every assignment coordinate lies in \([0,1]\), each numerator satisfies \(0\leq k_{t,a}\leq D\). Therefore this assignment has polynomial encoding length.

A verifier checks its feasibility, computes \(F(v)\) exactly using Lemma 1, and checks the strict rational inequality \(F(v)<c\). All these operations take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Combining membership with the reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for every fixed rational \(c\in(0,1)\). A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
