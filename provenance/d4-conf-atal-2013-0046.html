The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite good catalogue \(G\), type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an XOR utility \(u_t\), a current bundle \(A_t^0\), a required subset \(P_t\) for each type \(t\), and per-capita supplies \(b_g\), decide whether there are assignments \(y_{t,B}\ge 0\) to integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_{B\supseteq P_t}y_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:\,g\in B}y_{t,B}\le b_g\), with \(\mathcal L_y(q)\ge\mathcal L_x(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,1]\) and strict inequality for some \(q\). Here \(\mathcal L_y(q)\) is the total utility of the poorest \(q\)-fraction under \(y\), and \(\mathcal L_x\) is the corresponding curve for the current bundles.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a finite catalogue of \(m\) goods. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an explicitly listed XOR bid \(\{(S_{tj},w_{tj})\}_j\), where \(\varnothing\ne S_{tj}\subseteq G\) and \(w_{tj}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), defining \(u_t(B)=\max(\{0\}\cup\{w_{tj}:S_{tj}\subseteq B\})\). All weights are binary rationals.
The input also gives current bundles \(A_t^0\subseteq G\), required subsets \(P_t\subseteq G\), and binary-rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\in[0,1]\). Require \(\sum_{t:g\in A_t^0}\mu_t=b_g\) and \(\sum_{t:g\in P_t}\mu_t\le b_g\) for every \(g\); no inclusion between \(P_t\) and \(A_t^0\) is required.
An assignment splits type masses among integral bundles: \(y_{t,B}\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), with \(y_{t,B}=0\) unless \(B\supseteq P_t\), \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}y_{t,B}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}y_{t,B}=b_g\). Thus all supplies are allocated, without transfers of utility.
For any assignment \(z\), its utility distribution places mass \(z_{t,B}\) at \(u_t(B)\). Define \(\mathcal L_z(q)=\int_0^q Q_z(s)\,ds\), where \(Q_z\) is its increasing utility quantile; masses at tied utilities may be split. This curve is not divided by mean utility. The current assignment is \(x_{t,B}=\mu_t\mathbf1_{\{B=A_t^0\}}\).
Continuous Lorenz Improvement asks whether a feasible \(y\) satisfies \(\mathcal L_y(q)\ge\mathcal L_x(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,1]\), with strict inequality for some \(q\). Running times are measured in \(m\), \(\tau\), the total number \(K\) of listed atomic bids, and total input bit length \(L\).

Is Continuous Lorenz Improvement solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents receiving disjoint bundles that partition the goods, deciding whether a current allocation admits a strict Lorenz improvement extending a required partial allocation is NP-complete for XOR utilities. [Endriss, 2013](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2013/docs/p175.pdf)
No result settling this exact mass-splitting problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored complete allocation by replacing the graded question's supply inequalities with equalities, following the discrete allocation convention.
  • Required the current bundles to exhaust the supplied quantities and the required subsets to respect aggregate supplies; required subsets need not be contained in current bundles.
  • Specified supplies as binary rationals in [0,1], since each person receives at most one copy of each catalogue good.
  • Specified XOR bids explicitly, with nonnegative binary-rational weights, nonempty positively valued bundles, and default utility zero; nonempty bid bundles ensure normalization.
  • Specified assignment masses as nonnegative real numbers and excluded all bundles failing to contain the type's required subset.
  • Defined the Lorenz curve using the distribution of realized bundle utilities, with fractional splitting at quantile boundaries and no normalization by mean utility.
  • Defined K as the total number of atomic bids and L as total input bit length.

Result

Continuous Lorenz Improvement is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,L\). A rational linear program with \(\tau+K\) variables suffices.

The proof uses two properties of the stated model: an XOR utility can be witnessed by one atomic bid, and unused supplies can be allocated by splitting masses without decreasing anyone’s utility.

1. A finite linear characterization of Lorenz dominance

Let \(V=\{0\}\cup\{w_{tj}:t,j\}\), with duplicates removed, and let \(M=\max V\). Every attainable utility, including every current utility, belongs to \(V\).

For a utility distribution \(z\) of total mass \(1\), define its shortfall function by

\[ H_z(a)=\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}(a-u_t(B))_+, \qquad (r)_+=\max\{r,0\}. \]

Equivalently, \(H_z(a)=\int_0^1(a-Q_z(s))_+\,ds\). We establish the identities

\[ H_z(a)=\max_{q\in[0,1]}\{aq-\mathcal L_z(q)\}, \qquad \mathcal L_z(q)=\max_{a\in V}\{aq-H_z(a)\}. \]

For the first identity, observe that

\[ aq-\mathcal L_z(q)=\int_0^q(a-Q_z(s))\,ds. \]

Because \(Q_z\) is increasing, this expression is maximized by including all quantile mass with utility below \(a\), any desired portion with utility equal to \(a\), and no mass with utility above \(a\). Its maximum is exactly \(H_z(a)\).

Consequently, \(aq-H_z(a)\le\mathcal L_z(q)\) for every \(a,q\). To prove equality for a suitable \(a\in V\), first suppose \(0<q<1\). Choose a utility value \(a\) for which

\[ p_<:=\Pr_z(U<a)\le q\le\Pr_z(U\le a). \]

Such a value exists because the distribution has finite support. Writing \(I_<=\int_0^{p_<}Q_z(s)\,ds\), we have

\[ H_z(a)=ap_<-I_<, \qquad \mathcal L_z(q)=I_<+a(q-p_<)=aq-H_z(a). \]

At \(q=0\), choose \(a=0\); at \(q=1\), choose \(a=M\), using \(H_z(M)=M-\mathcal L_z(1)\). This proves the second identity, including the case \(M=0\).

It follows that, for distributions supported on \(V\),

\[ \mathcal L_z(q)\ge\mathcal L_x(q)\text{ for every }q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_z(a)\le H_x(a)\text{ for every }a\in V. \]

Indeed, the forward implication follows from the first identity, and the reverse implication from the second.

Moreover, under these weak inequalities, strict Lorenz improvement is equivalent to strict shortfall inequality at some \(a\in V\). If every shortfall value on \(V\) is equal, the second identity gives equality of the Lorenz curves. Conversely, equality of the Lorenz curves implies equality of all shortfall values by the first identity.

Thus finitely many linear inequalities, together with one positive slack, characterize the desired improvement.

2. Polynomially many bundles preserve every possible utility

Let \(k_t\) be the number of atomic bids of type \(t\). Define

\[ D_{t0}=P_t, \qquad D_{tj}=P_t\cup S_{tj}\quad(1\le j\le k_t), \qquad v_{tj}=u_t(D_{tj}). \]

All these bundles and their actual utilities can be computed from the explicitly listed bids in polynomial time.

We claim that every allowed bundle \(B\supseteq P_t\) has a utility-preserving reduction to one of these bundles.

If \(u_t(B)=0\), choose \(D_{t0}\). Since \(D_{t0}\subseteq B\) and utilities are nonnegative and monotone, \(u_t(D_{t0})=0\).

If \(u_t(B)>0\), some listed bid attains the maximum defining its utility: \(S_{tj}\subseteq B\) and \(w_{tj}=u_t(B)\). Then \(D_{tj}\subseteq B\), and

\[ u_t(B)=w_{tj}\le u_t(D_{tj})\le u_t(B). \]

Hence \(u_t(D_{tj})=u_t(B)\).

Applying this reduction separately to each positive-mass cell of an assignment preserves its utility distribution and type masses, preserves all required subsets, and can only decrease consumption of each good. The number of candidate bundles is

\[ N=\sum_t(k_t+1)=K+\tau. \]

3. Unused supplies can always be filled

Suppose an assignment \(z\) satisfies the type-mass and required-subset constraints, but only satisfies the supply upper bounds. Let its consumption of good \(g\) be \(c_g\le b_g\).

The mass of cells whose bundles lack \(g\) is \(1-c_g\). The amount still to allocate is \(\delta_g=b_g-c_g\), and the premise \(b_g\le1\) gives

\[ 0\le\delta_g\le1-c_g. \]

Choose mass \(\delta_g\) among those cells, splitting a cell if necessary, and add \(g\) to their bundles. This makes consumption of \(g\) exactly \(b_g\). It changes no other good’s consumption, no type total, and no required-subset inclusion.

Process every good in this way. The resulting assignment \(y\) meets all supply equalities.

Each descendant cell has a superset of its original bundle, so its utility cannot decrease. Therefore, for every threshold \(a\),

\[ H_y(a)\le H_z(a). \]

In particular, any strict Lorenz improvement achieved before filling remains strict afterward.

This construction also has polynomial output size: processing one good requires at most one cell split if cells are handled sequentially. Starting with at most \(N\) cells, the completed assignment has at most \(N+m\) cells.

4. The linear program

Compute the current utilities \(u_t^0=u_t(A_t^0)\) and the constants

\[ h_a=H_x(a)=\sum_t\mu_t(a-u_t^0)_+ \quad(a\in V). \]

Introduce a nonnegative variable \(\alpha_{tj}\) for the mass assigned to candidate bundle \(D_{tj}\), for \(0\le j\le k_t\).

Impose the type-mass constraints

\[ \sum_{j=0}^{k_t}\alpha_{tj}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \]

the supply upper bounds

\[ \sum_{t,j:g\in D_{tj}}\alpha_{tj}\le b_g \quad\text{for every }g, \]

and the shortfall inequalities

\[ \sum_{t,j}\alpha_{tj}(a-v_{tj})_+\le h_a \quad\text{for every }a\in V. \]

Maximize the total slack

\[ \Phi(\alpha)= \sum_{a\in V} \left( h_a-\sum_{t,j}\alpha_{tj}(a-v_{tj})_+ \right). \]

The decision rule is:

  • If the linear program is infeasible, answer no.
  • Otherwise, answer yes exactly when its optimum is strictly positive.

All objective summands are nonnegative on the feasible region, so positive total slack is equivalent to strict inequality at some threshold.

5. Correctness

If a strict Lorenz improvement exists, the program has positive optimum.

Let \(y\) be such an assignment. Apply the utility-preserving reductions of Section 2 and aggregate the resulting masses into \(\alpha_{tj}\). The type constraints hold, and supply consumption has only decreased. Its utility distribution is exactly that of \(y\).

By Section 1, all shortfall inequalities hold, with at least one strict inequality. Thus \(\alpha\) is feasible and \(\Phi(\alpha)>0\).

If the program has positive optimum, a strict Lorenz improvement exists.

Take a feasible \(\alpha\) with positive objective. Assign its masses to the bundles \(D_{tj}\), obtaining an assignment \(z\) that respects required subsets and supply upper bounds. Its actual utilities are \(v_{tj}\), so the linear inequalities and positive objective imply that \(z\) strictly Lorenz dominates \(x\).

Complete its supplies using Section 3. The resulting feasible assignment \(y\) satisfies \(H_y(a)\le H_z(a)\) for every \(a\). Hence all dominance inequalities remain valid, and any strict one remains strict. Section 1 therefore gives the required strict Lorenz improvement.

Neither direction assumes \(P_t\subseteq A_t^0\).

6. Polynomial running time

The program has \(K+\tau\) variables and, apart from nonnegativity, at most \(\tau+m+K+1\) constraints.

Candidate bundles, their utilities, and current utilities are computable by subset tests against the explicitly listed bids. Every coefficient is rational, and all differences, products, and sums used to construct the program have polynomial encoding length in \(m,\tau,K,L\).

If feasible, the program is bounded because \(0\le\alpha_{tj}\le\mu_t\). The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore permits exact optimization and exact comparison of its optimum with zero. No numerical tolerance or presumed minimum improvement is needed.

This settles the stated mass-splitting problem. The NP-completeness result for allocations to indivisible agents concerns a different feasible set; see [Endriss, Proposition 7](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2013/docs/p175.pdf).
