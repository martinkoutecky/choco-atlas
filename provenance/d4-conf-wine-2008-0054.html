The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) candidates and a finite support of trader-offer types \(t=(A_t,π_t,q_t)\), each with a two-entry bidding matrix, binary-rational mass μ_t, limit price π_t, and per-trader order cap \(q_t\), where \(\sum_t μ_t=1\), compute the maximum per-capita worst-case profit. Choose accepted order volumes \(x_t\) and reserve \(r\) to maximize \(\sum_t π_t x_t-r\), subject to \(r\ge\sum_t x_t\mathbf{1}[A_t\bullet M_σ>0]\) for every permutation σ and \(0\le x_t\leμ_t q_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) trader-offer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge 2\) candidates. Each type has a matrix \(A_t\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}\) with exactly two entries equal to \(1\), a nonnegative binary-rational limit price \(\pi_t\), and a nonnegative binary-rational per-trader order cap \(q_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An outcome is a permutation \(\sigma\in S_m\), with permutation matrix \(M_\sigma\) whose entry \((i,j)\) is \(1\) precisely when candidate \(i\) occupies position \(j\). An accepted unit pays \(1\) if either selected candidate-position pair occurs, and \(0\) otherwise, including a single payment when both occur.
Orders are divisible: choose aggregate volumes \(x_t\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) and reserve \(r\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), with no starting orders. Define the maximum per-capita worst-case profit as \(\max\{\sum_t\pi_t x_t-r:0\le x_t\le\mu_t q_t,\ r\ge\sum_t x_t\mathbf1[A_t\bullet M_\sigma>0]\text{ for every }\sigma\in S_m\}\), where \(\bullet\) is the Frobenius inner product.

Is computing this exact optimum possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

The finite-trader fixed-reward optimization is NP-hard even with exactly two selected entries per bid; it already permits divisible orders. [Agrawal, Wang, and Ye](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/04/1962.pdf).
The stated support-mass model is the same optimization with aggregate order caps, so the published result already covers it; this target is not open.

Result

Computing the exact optimum is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\). The published finite-trader theorem transfers to the stated support model through a polynomial-time transformation that preserves the optimum exactly.

1. The applicable published theorem

Agrawal, Wang, and Ye’s Theorem 3.1 states that their fixed-reward optimization problem, equation (2), is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\) even when each bidding matrix has exactly two nonzero entries. Their orders may be fractional, and equation (2) has no starting orders. Its payout is the indicator that at least one selected entry occurs, so two coincident entries still produce only one payment. These are precisely the relevant features of the present task. [Theorem 3.1, equation (2), and the divisible-order definition](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/04/1962.pdf).

Write an instance of that finite-trader problem as

\(\displaystyle V=\max\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_i y_i-r:\ 0\le y_i\le c_i,\quad r\ge\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_i\mathbf1[B_i\bullet M_\sigma>0]\ \text{for every }\sigma\in S_m\right\}.\)

Here each \(B_i\) has exactly two selected entries. The reserve inequalities already imply \(r\ge0\), because their right-hand sides are nonnegative.

We use this published hardness theorem as a known result. Its optimization–separation proof establishes hardness under polynomial-time oracle reductions; no stronger reduction notion is needed for the conclusion about polynomial-time exact computation.

2. Consolidating identical offers

Partition the finite offers into groups having identical matrix and limit price. For group \(t\), let these common data be \(A_t,\pi_t\), and set

\(\displaystyle C_t=\sum_{i\text{ in group }t}c_i.\)

Replacing the group’s individual accepted volumes by the aggregate volume

\(\displaystyle X_t=\sum_{i\text{ in group }t}y_i\)

preserves both revenue and payout in every outcome.

Moreover, the possible aggregate volumes are exactly \(0\le X_t\le C_t\). One direction follows by summing the individual bounds. For the other, when \(C_t>0\), any such \(X_t\) can be realized by

\(\displaystyle y_i=\frac{c_i}{C_t}X_t.\)

These volumes satisfy \(0\le y_i\le c_i\) and sum to \(X_t\). If \(C_t=0\), every individual volume and the aggregate volume must be zero.

Thus consolidation preserves the optimum exactly. Let \(k\le N\) be the number of resulting groups.

3. Constructing positive support masses

Choose masses as follows. If \(k=1\), set \(\mu_1=1\). If \(k\ge2\), set

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=2^{-t}\quad(1\le t<k),\qquad \mu_k=2^{-(k-1)}.\)

Every mass is positive and dyadic, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t=1}^{k}\mu_t=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}2^{-t}+2^{-(k-1)}=1.\)

For each group, define its per-trader cap by

\(\displaystyle q_t=\frac{C_t}{\mu_t}.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \mu_tq_t=C_t.\)

The resulting society therefore has exactly the consolidated optimization problem:

\(\displaystyle \max\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{k}\pi_tX_t-r:\ 0\le X_t\le C_t,\quad r\ge\sum_{t=1}^{k}X_t\mathbf1[A_t\bullet M_\sigma>0]\ \text{for every }\sigma\in S_m\right\}.\)

Its optimum equals \(V\). The support size is \(\tau=k\), and each matrix still has exactly two selected entries.

4. Encoding size and conclusion

Consolidation requires only comparisons and rational additions, with polynomial encoding size. Every reciprocal \(1/\mu_t\) is a power of two whose exponent is at most \(k-1\); therefore constructing \(q_t\) increases its binary encoding length by at most a polynomial amount. The masses themselves have polynomial total encoding length.

This construction works even under the stricter interpretation that binary-rational numbers must have finite binary expansions.

A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) for the society’s exact optimum would consequently compute the finite-trader optimum in polynomial time. By the published theorem, this is impossible unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
