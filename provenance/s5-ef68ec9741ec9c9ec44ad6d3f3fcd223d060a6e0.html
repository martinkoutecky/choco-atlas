The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a binary-rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many applicant classes, where each class specifies priority, a prior over preferences, search cost, and payoff vector, together with per-capita university capacities \(q_c\), compute each class’s optimal search-stopping and selection policy under the paper’s search technology and sequential priority process, then compute the resulting aggregate assignment and expected net student welfare. Each applicant receives one indivisible seat; assignments may be fractional only when reported as type-level mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible preference rankings. There are \(m\ge2\) universities, with binary-rational capacities \(q_c>0\) and \(\sum_c q_c>1\); \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type specifies a common university priority, a binary-rational search cost \(k_t>0\), and known binary-rational rank utilities \(u_t^1>\cdots>u_t^m>0\), with unmatched utility zero. Applicants are risk neutral.
The prior is specified by a common ordered partition into nonempty university tiers: every applicant prefers every university in a better tier, and within-tier rankings are independent uniform permutations. One tier gives the uniform prior over all strict rankings; utility depends on rank among all \(m\) universities.
Process types in priority order, breaking equal priorities by input order. Each type occupies a consecutive interval of nonatomic applicant mass; within that interval preferences and search randomization have their prescribed distributions at every priority position.
An applicant observes the budget set \(B\) of universities with positive residual capacity before searching. Search takes place separately within each tier: the first paid step compares two available universities, and each subsequent paid step inserts one previously unsearched available university into the learned relative ordering; every step costs \(k_t\), reveals no absolute ranks, and updates beliefs by conditioning the prior.
Within a tier, searched universities are sampled uniformly without replacement. An applicant may stop without searching, may select an unsearched university, and selects an available university maximizing posterior expected utility; selection ties are uniform, and stopping indifference is resolved in favor of fewer paid steps.
Applicants maximize their own expected assignment utility minus search costs. Capacity is depleted continuously at the resulting selection-probability rates; exhausted universities disappear immediately, including simultaneous exhaustions. Every applicant receives one indivisible seat; fractions describe only aggregate masses.
Compute exactly the optimal stopping counts and selection policies for every type and budget-set phase encountered, the type-by-university assignment masses, and the mass integral of expected net utility.

Is this continuous computation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite applicants with uniform priors, the marginal benefit of another search step among available universities is nonnegative and decreasing, and optimal stopping is characterized by comparison with search cost; common tiered priors reduce to within-tier instances. Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The prior domain was unspecified; use the uniform and common tiered-uniform priors defined in the discrete model, encoded by an ordered tier partition.
  • Normalize total applicant mass to one and interpret capacities per unit applicant mass; retain positive capacities, at least two universities, and strictly excess total capacity.
  • Specify binary-rational positive search costs and strictly decreasing rank-based utilities; normalize unmatched utility to zero and all assignment utilities to positive values.
  • Replace individual exam ranks by consecutive nonatomic priority intervals for supported types, resolving equal type priorities by input order and keeping within-class preference and randomization distributions constant along each interval.

Result and output representation

The problem is solvable exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

A selection policy can be represented by a search count and a rule for choosing from the learned ordering. These rules specify the action for every possible search outcome and have polynomial size.

The key facts are:

  • For a fixed budget set, optimal search has a deterministic stopping count, computable by comparing polynomially many rational numbers.
  • Regardless of that stopping count, assignment is uniform among the available universities in the best available tier.
  • Consequently, continuous capacity depletion has only polynomially many phases, and all computed rational numbers have polynomial bit length.

The fixed-budget search result is consistent with Proposition 4 in Appendix A.3 of the [referenced paper](https://rationality-and-competition.de/wp-content/uploads/discussion_paper/280.pdf). The proof below independently establishes the policy and then handles the continuous computation and its bit complexity.

1. Reduction to the best available tier

Write the common tiers as \(T_1,\ldots,T_s\), ordered from best to worst. Define \(N_i=|T_i|\) and \(h_i=\sum_{j<i}N_j\). For type \(t\), the utilities within tier \(T_i\) are

\(v_{t,i}^r=u_t^{h_i+r},\qquad 1\le r\le N_i.\)

At a priority position with budget set \(B\), let \(i\) be the smallest index such that \(A=B\cap T_i\) is nonempty, and put \(a=|A|\).

Every university in \(A\) has greater realized utility than every available university in a worse tier. This remains true after conditioning on any search observations. Thus the applicant always selects from \(A\).

Searching a worse tier cannot improve this selection: its ranking is independent of the ranking in \(T_i\), and the ordering of tiers is already known. Since search costs are positive, an optimal policy searches only within \(A\).

Under the stipulated preference distributions at every priority position, \(B\) does not convey additional information about this applicant’s within-tier ranking. Throughout the fixed-budget analysis, we therefore have a uniform permutation of all \(N_i\) universities in the active tier.

In particular, absolute ranks are drawn from the full tier of size \(N_i\), not just its available subset of size \(a\).

2. Posterior utilities after search

Fix a type and an active tier, and abbreviate its size and utilities by \(N\) and \(v^1>\cdots>v^N\).

Suppose \(n\ge2\) available universities have been searched and their complete relative ordering is known.

Posterior rank lemma

Conditional on any such search history:

  • the set of absolute within-tier ranks occupied by the searched universities is uniform over the \(n\)-element subsets of \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\);
  • every unsearched university has a uniform marginal absolute rank.

To prove the first assertion, fix the searched labels and their observed relative ordering. For each possible \(n\)-element rank set, exactly one placement of the searched labels respects that ordering, and the remaining labels can be placed in \((N-n)!\) ways. Hence every rank set has the same conditional probability.

For the second assertion, fix an unsearched label and its absolute rank. Among the remaining positions, every relative ordering of the searched labels is equally likely, so the probability of the observed ordering is \(1/n!\), independently of that absolute rank. Conditioning therefore preserves the uniform marginal distribution.

The same argument applies to the full sequential history. Earlier comparisons are restrictions of the final learned ordering. Sampling probabilities and any earlier stopping decisions depend only on labels, randomization, and observed relative orders; they impose no further restriction on absolute rank sets.

Optimal selection after stopping

Let \(R_1<\cdots<R_n\) be the searched universities’ absolute ranks in their learned order. Since \(v^{R_1}>\cdots>v^{R_n}\) pointwise, the best searched university has strictly greater posterior expected utility than every other searched university.

Every unsearched university has expected utility

\(V_1=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{r=1}^N v^r.\)

The best searched university has expected utility

\(V_n=\sum_{r=1}^{N-n+1}\frac{\binom{N-r}{n-1}}{\binom{N}{n}}v^r,\qquad 1\le n\le N.\)

Indeed, the minimum of a uniform \(n\)-element rank set equals \(r\) precisely when its other \(n-1\) ranks are chosen from \(\{r+1,\ldots,N\}\).

For \(n\ge2\), \(V_n>V_1\). To see this, randomly order a uniform \(n\)-element rank set. Its first rank is uniform on \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\), while its minimum is no larger and is strictly smaller with positive probability. Strictly decreasing utilities imply the strict expectation inequality.

Thus, after any positive amount of search, the applicant uniquely optimally selects the best searched university. Without search, all universities in \(A\) tie, so the stipulated rule selects uniformly from \(A\).

3. Exact optimal stopping, including adaptive policies

For bookkeeping, use \(n=1\) to represent stopping without search. It involves no paid step and gives expected utility \(V_1\). For \(n\ge2\), searching \(n\) universities takes exactly \(n-1\) paid steps.

Let \(H_n\) denote the optimal expected utility from a history with \(n\ge2\) searched universities, subtracting only future search costs. The posterior rank lemma shows that this value depends on \(n\), not on the particular observed ordering.

Backward induction gives

\(H_a=V_a,\qquad H_n=\max\{V_n,-k_t+H_{n+1}\}\quad\text{for }2\le n<a.\)

The continuation value is independent of the next comparison outcome: each resulting history has \(n+1\) searched universities and value \(H_{n+1}\).

At the initial state, the applicant can stop for \(V_1\), or, if \(a\ge2\), pay \(k_t\) to compare two universities. Expanding the recurrence yields the optimal initial net utility

\(w_t(B)=\max_{1\le n\le a}\{V_n-(n-1)k_t\}.\)

For \(a=1\), this formula correctly permits only stopping without search.

Define

\(n_t^*(B)=\min\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\le n\le a}\{V_n-(n-1)k_t\}.\)

The optimal number of paid steps is \(n_t^*(B)-1\).

This also implements the stopping-indifference convention. Put \(F_n=V_n-(n-1)k_t\). Before reaching \(n_t^*(B)\), stopping gives a strictly smaller value than continuing to that count, because it is the smallest maximizer. At that count, stopping is optimal; any tied continuation uses more paid steps.

Hence an optimal policy is:

  • If \(n_t^*(B)=1\), stop immediately and choose uniformly from \(A\).
  • Otherwise, search exactly \(n_t^*(B)\) universities using the prescribed uniform sampling, then choose the best searched university.

The backward-induction argument proves optimality against all adaptive stopping policies, including randomized ones.

4. Assignment probabilities are uniform

Suppose \(n_t^*(B)=n\ge2\). A particular university \(c\in A\) is included in the searched sample with probability \(n/a\). Conditional on inclusion, its probability of being best in that sample is \(1/n\), by the uniform within-tier ranking. Therefore,

\(\Pr(c\text{ is selected}\mid B,t)=\frac{n}{a}\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{a}.\)

When there is no search, the uniform tie rule gives the same probability.

Consequently, for every type,

\(p_c(B)=\frac{1}{a}\quad\text{if }c\in A,\qquad p_c(B)=0\quad\text{otherwise}.\)

Thus the capacity-depletion rates depend only on the available universities and the common tiers. Search costs and rank utilities affect welfare and search policies, but not these rates.

5. Exact continuous simulation

Order the supported types by priority, breaking ties by input order.

Maintain residual capacities \(r_c\), initially \(q_c\), the remaining mass \(b\) of the current type, assignment totals \(x_{t,c}\), and accumulated expected net utility \(W\).

For the current type \(t\):

  • Set \(B=\{c:r_c>0\}\), determine its best available tier, and let \(A\) be that tier’s available universities, with \(a=|A|\).
  • Compute and record \(n_t^*(B)\), its selection rule, and \(w_t(B)\).
  • Compute the next phase’s applicant mass as

\(\delta=\min\{b,\ a\min_{c\in A}r_c\}.\)

  • For each \(c\in A\), update

\(r_c\leftarrow r_c-\delta/a,\qquad x_{t,c}\leftarrow x_{t,c}+\delta/a.\)

  • Update

\(W\leftarrow W+\delta w_t(B),\qquad b\leftarrow b-\delta.\)

  • Remove every university whose residual capacity is zero. If \(b=0\), move to the next type. When both events occur together, perform both updates.

These updates are exact. Within the phase, the type and budget set are constant, so every active university loses capacity at rate \(1/a\). Its exhaustion occurs after applicant mass \(a r_c\); the earliest exhaustion therefore occurs after mass \(a\min_{c\in A}r_c\). The alternative phase endpoint is the current type’s boundary.

Simultaneous exhaustions are handled by removing all zero residual capacities before the next phase. Phase endpoints themselves have zero applicant mass and do not affect assignment totals or the integral.

The budget set never becomes empty

After processing total applicant mass \(z\), exactly \(z\) seats have been assigned, so

\(\sum_c r_c=\sum_c q_c-z.\)

For \(0\le z\le1\), this is strictly positive because \(\sum_c q_c>1\). Hence some university remains available throughout the computation, and every applicant receives a seat.

Number of phases

Every positive-length phase ends at a type boundary or at an exhaustion event, possibly both. There are \(\tau\) type endings. At most \(m-1\) universities exhaust, since positive total residual capacity remains even after all applicants are processed.

Thus the number \(P\) of positive-length phases satisfies

\(P\le\tau+m-1.\)

The updates give the requested type-by-university masses. They also give the requested mass integral exactly, since the expected net utility is constant within each phase.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

Counting arithmetic operations alone is insufficient; we also bound all rational representations.

Search computations

Precompute binomial coefficients up to \(m\) by Pascal’s recurrence. Every coefficient has \(O(m)\) bits.

For every type and tier, compute \(V_n\) for all \(1\le n\le N_i\). Each value requires at most \(N_i\) summands. Since

\(\sum_i N_i^2\le m^2,\)

this takes \(O(\tau m^2)\) rational arithmetic operations.

Let \(D\) be the product of all input rational denominators, and let

\(Q=\prod_{i=1}^s\prod_{n=1}^{N_i}\binom{N_i}{n}.\)

The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Because \(\binom{N_i}{n}\le2^{N_i}\), the bit length of \(Q\) is \(O(\sum_iN_i^2)=O(m^2)\).

Every \(V_n\) and every candidate \(V_n-(n-1)k_t\) has denominator dividing \(DQ\). Their magnitudes are bounded by \(m\) times the largest input utility or search cost. Their numerators and denominators therefore have polynomial bit length.

The maximum and its smallest maximizing index can be found by exact rational comparisons in \(O(m)\) operations per phase.

Capacity computations

After \(j\) phases, every residual capacity, remaining type mass, and accumulated assignment mass has denominator dividing

\(E_j=D(m!)^j.\)

This follows inductively. At phase \(j+1\), multiplication by \(a\), comparison, and subtraction preserve denominators dividing \(E_j\). Division by \(a\) introduces only a factor dividing \(m!\), so all updated denominators divide \(E_{j+1}\).

Since \(P\le\tau+m-1\), the bit length of \(E_P\) is

\(O\!\left(L+(\tau+m)m\log m\right).\)

Residual capacities are bounded by their input capacities, and applicant and assignment masses are at most one, so their numerators also have polynomial bit length.

Finally, each welfare contribution \(\delta w_t(B)\), and their sum, has denominator dividing \(E_PDQ\). Expected optimal net utility is positive and at most the largest input utility, so the welfare numerators also have polynomial bit length.

There are polynomially many arithmetic operations, and every operation uses polynomially many bits. Sorting priorities, storing phase policies, and accumulating the assignment matrix likewise take polynomial time and space.

Conclusion

The algorithm computes exactly all encountered phase policies, all type-by-university assignment masses, and the expected net-utility integral in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The complete classification is polynomial-time solvable.
