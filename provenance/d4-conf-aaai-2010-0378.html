The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(p\), and partial-ballot types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), each allowing one or two rankings, independently complete each ballot uniformly among its type's allowed rankings. For \(N\) divisible by the mass denominators, let \(P_N\) be the probability that \(p\) is the unique plurality winner among \(N\mu_t\) ballots of each type. Compute \(I_p(\mu)=\lim_{N\to\infty}-N^{-1}\ln P_N\), taking \(I_p(\mu)=+\infty\) if a unique \(p\)-winning completion is infeasible. When it is feasible, equivalently compute \(\inf_{z\in W_p^+}\sum_t\mu_tD(z_t\Vert u_t)\), where \(z_t\) is a distribution over type \(t\)'s rankings, \(u_t\) is uniform, and \(W_p^+\) imposes strict plurality victory for \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) candidates and \(p\in C\). Each ballot type \(t\) is a partial preference order with at most one incomparable pair; its allowed rankings \(A_t\) are all its linear extensions, hence one strict ranking or two differing by an adjacent swap.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total bit length of the society and candidate data.
For each positive integer \(N\) divisible by all mass denominators, independently complete the \(N\mu_t\) ballots of each type uniformly over \(A_t\). Let \(P_N\) be the probability that \(p\) is the unique plurality winner; ties do not count as victories.
Define \(I_p(\mu)=\lim_{N\to\infty}-N^{-1}\ln P_N\), along these admissible integers, with \(-\ln 0=+\infty\).
Equivalently, let \(z_t\) range over distributions on \(A_t\), let \(u_t\) be uniform there, and define \(s_c(z)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{r\in A_t:\,c\text{ is first in }r}z_t(r)\) and \(W_p^+=\{z:s_p(z)>s_c(z)\text{ for every }c\ne p\}\).
Then \(I_p(\mu)=\inf_{z\in W_p^+}\sum_t\mu_t D(z_t\Vert u_t)\), where \(D\) is Kullback–Leibler divergence using natural logarithms and the infimum of the empty set is \(+\infty\).
For finite values, computational evaluation means obtaining a rational approximation with additive error at most \(2^{-k}\), for an input positive integer \(k\); infinite values must be identified exactly.

Is evaluation of \(I_p(\mu)\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is it NP-hard?

Counting unique-winning completions in the discrete model is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard even with at most one incomparable pair per ballot ([Bachrach, Betzler, and Faliszewski](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/main-52.pdf)).
No result settling this specific continuous rate-evaluation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized positive rational masses to sum to one, so N is the total number of ballots.
  • Restricted allowed rankings to all linear extensions of a partial order with at most one incomparable pair, matching the anchor; arbitrary pairs of rankings are excluded.
  • Specified binary-precision evaluation of finite real values, with additive error 2^{-k} and running time measured in k, because the graded question supplied no representation for potentially irrational outputs.
  • Interpreted infeasibility as emptiness of the strict mass-winning region, rather than failure to win at a particular finite replication size.

Result. Evaluation of \(I_p(\mu)\), including exact identification of infinite values, is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

The proof uses the given variational characterization. The essential points are to test strict winning feasibility exactly and to justify polynomial bit complexity despite the entropy function’s unbounded derivatives at the boundary.

1. Reduction to a convex function of at most one variable per supported type

Enumerate the one or two extensions of each supported type. This takes polynomial time because each type has at most one incomparable pair.

If both extensions have the same first candidate, the completion distribution does not affect any plurality score. Its divergence contribution can therefore be minimized independently by choosing the uniform distribution, giving contribution zero. Types with just one extension also contribute zero.

Let \(n\leq\tau\) be the number of remaining types. For type \(j\), write \(w_j>0\) for its mass and \(a_j,b_j\) for the distinct first candidates in its two extensions. Let \(x_j\in[0,1]\) be the probability of choosing the extension headed by \(a_j\).

If \(\beta_c\) denotes the total mass of types whose first candidate is fixed as \(c\), then

\[ s_c(x)=\beta_c+\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_j\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{a_j=c\}}x_j+\mathbf 1_{\{b_j=c\}}(1-x_j)\bigr). \]

Define \(g_c(x)=s_p(x)-s_c(x)\) for every \(c\neq p\). These are affine functions with rational coefficients.

The objective becomes \(F(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_j h(x_j)\), where

\[ h(u)=u\ln(2u)+(1-u)\ln(2(1-u)), \]

with the continuous endpoint convention \(0\ln 0=0\). Thus the given characterization says

\[ I_p(\mu)=\inf\{F(x):x\in[0,1]^n,\ g_c(x)>0\text{ for every }c\neq p\}. \]

For \(0<u<1\), differentiation gives \(h'(u)=\ln(u/(1-u))\) and \(h''(u)=1/u+1/(1-u)>0\). Consequently, \(h\) is continuous and convex on \([0,1]\), with minimum zero at \(1/2\) and maximum \(\ln 2\) at the endpoints. Since \(\sum_jw_j\leq1\), we have \(0\leq F(x)\leq\ln 2<1\).

If \(m=1\), return zero. If \(n=0\), all plurality scores are fixed: return zero when \(p\) strictly beats every other candidate, and \(+\infty\) otherwise. Henceforth assume \(m>1\) and \(n>0\).

2. Exact detection of an infinite rate

Consider the rational linear program

\[ \begin{array}{ll} \text{maximize}&\rho\\ \text{subject to}&0\leq\rho\leq1/2,\\ &\rho\leq x_j\leq1-\rho\quad(1\leq j\leq n),\\ &g_c(x)\geq\rho\quad(c\neq p). \end{array} \]

We claim that a strict winning point exists if and only if this program has a positive optimum.

One direction is immediate: a feasible point with \(\rho>0\) satisfies every strict winning inequality.

For the converse, suppose \(x\) is a strict winning point, and put \(\gamma=\min_{c\neq p}g_c(x)>0\). Scores lie in \([0,1]\), so \(\gamma\leq1\). Let \(q=(1/2,\ldots,1/2)\), set \(\lambda=\gamma/4\), and define \(v=(1-\lambda)x+\lambda q\). Every coordinate of \(v\) lies between \(\gamma/8\) and \(1-\gamma/8\). Moreover, \(g_c(q)\geq-1\), and therefore

\[ g_c(v)\geq(1-\lambda)\gamma-\lambda\geq\gamma-2\lambda=\gamma/2. \]

Thus \((v,\gamma/8)\) is feasible for the linear program.

Solve this program using an exact polynomial-time algorithm for rational linear programming. If it is infeasible or its optimum is zero, the strict winning region is empty, and the answer is exactly \(+\infty\).

Otherwise obtain a rational feasible pair \((v,\rho)\) with \(\rho>0\). Its encoding length is polynomial in the input length. In particular, \(\log(1/\rho)\) is polynomially bounded. These are the usual rational linear programming bit bounds: after clearing denominators, a vertex solution has polynomially bounded numerator and denominator lengths by Cramer’s rule and determinant bounds.

3. Replacing strict inequalities after checking feasibility

Suppose the preceding step produced \((v,\rho)\) with \(\rho>0\). Define the compact rational polytope \(K=\{x\in[0,1]^n:g_c(x)\geq0\text{ for every }c\neq p\}\), and let \(V=\min_{x\in K}F(x)\). The minimum exists because \(F\) is continuous.

We claim that \(I_p(\mu)=V\).

Since every strict winning point belongs to \(K\), we have \(V\leq I_p(\mu)\). Conversely, for any \(x\in K\) and any \(0<\alpha<1\), the point \(x_\alpha=(1-\alpha)x+\alpha v\) satisfies

\[ g_c(x_\alpha)\geq\alpha\rho>0. \]

Convexity gives \(F(x_\alpha)\leq(1-\alpha)F(x)+\alpha F(v)\). Letting \(\alpha\) tend to zero yields \(I_p(\mu)\leq F(x)\). Taking \(x\) to be a minimizer proves the claim.

The preceding feasibility test is necessary here. When strict victory is impossible, the weak winning polytope can still contain tied outcomes, whose finite objective values do not represent the rate.

4. Removing the entropy boundary with a controlled error

Let \(\varepsilon=2^{-k}\), \(\delta=\varepsilon/4\), and \(\eta=\delta\rho\). Define

\[ K_\eta=\{x\in K:\eta\leq x_j\leq1-\eta\text{ for every }j\},\qquad V_\eta=\min_{x\in K_\eta}F(x). \]

We have \(V\leq V_\eta\). For the reverse bound, let \(x^\star\) minimize \(F\) over \(K\), and set \(y=(1-\delta)x^\star+\delta v\). Because \(\rho\leq v_j\leq1-\rho\), every coordinate of \(y\) lies in \([\eta,1-\eta]\); its score margins are at least \(\delta\rho\). Hence \(y\in K_\eta\). Convexity and nonnegativity give

\[ V_\eta\leq F(y)\leq(1-\delta)V+\delta F(v)\leq V+\delta\ln2<V+\varepsilon/4. \]

This bound requires no derivative estimate at an endpoint.

The restricted problem has an explicit interior ball. Write \(g_c(x)=d_c+A_cx\). Each coefficient of \(A_c\) has absolute value at most \(2w_j\), so \(\|A_c\|_2\leq\|A_c\|_1\leq2\). Consequently, every point within Euclidean distance \(\rho/4\) of \(v\) has score margins at least \(\rho/2\) and coordinates between \(3\rho/4\) and \(1-3\rho/4\). Since \(\delta\leq1/8\), this ball lies in \(K_\eta\).

Both \(\eta\) and this ball radius have encoding lengths polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

5. Polynomial-time approximation through weak separation

To make the numerical complexity explicit, use the compact convex epigraph

\[ E=\{(x,t):x\in K_\eta,\ F(x)\leq t\leq2\}. \]

Minimizing the last coordinate over \(E\) gives \(V_\eta\).

We apply the standard weak separation–weak optimization theorem: given an explicitly bounded convex body with a known rational interior ball and a polynomial-time weak separation oracle, a rational linear objective can be optimized to additive accuracy \(\zeta\) in time polynomial in the body’s encoding parameters and \(\log(1/\zeta)\). One output formulation gives a rational point within distance \(\zeta\) of the body whose objective is within \(\zeta\) of optimal. This is the ellipsoid theorem proved by [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf), Sections 2–3.

Here are the required hypothesis checks.

First, \(E\) contains the ball of radius \(\rho/4\) centered at \((v,3/2)\). Its \(x\)-coordinates remain in \(K_\eta\), and its last coordinate lies strictly between \(1\) and \(2\), whereas \(F(x)<1\). Also, \(E\) is contained in the ball of radius \(n+2\) with that same center. These bounds have polynomial encoding length.

Second, \(E\) has the following polynomial-time weak separation oracle. Given a rational query \((x,t)\) and tolerance \(0<\xi\leq1\):

  • If \(x\notin K_\eta\), a violated rational linear inequality separates the query from \(E\). If \(t<0\) or \(t>2\), the corresponding horizontal inequality separates it.
  • Otherwise, compute rational approximations \(\widetilde F\) and \(\widetilde g\) satisfying \(|\widetilde F-F(x)|\leq\xi/8\) and \(|\widetilde g_j-\partial_jF(x)|\leq\xi/(8n)\) for each \(j\).
  • If \(\widetilde F\leq t+\xi/2\), then \(F(x)\leq t+5\xi/8\). Raising the last coordinate to \(\max\{t,F(x)\}\) produces a point of \(E\) at distance less than \(\xi\). Thus the oracle can certify approximate membership.
  • Otherwise return the separating normal \(a=(\widetilde g,-1)\).

For the last case, take any \((x',t')\in E\). Convexity gives

\[ \nabla F(x)\mathbin{\cdot}(x'-x)-(t'-t)\leq t-F(x). \]

The approximation errors and the fact that both \(x,x'\) lie in \([0,1]^n\) imply

\[ a\mathbin{\cdot}\bigl((x',t')-(x,t)\bigr)\leq t-F(x)+\xi/8<-\xi/4. \]

Thus this rational hyperplane strictly separates the query from all of \(E\).

Finally, the required numerical approximations are computable in polynomial time. On the restricted box, \(\partial_jF(x)=w_j(\ln x_j-\ln(1-x_j))\), and all logarithm arguments are positive rationals.

For completeness, logarithms of positive rationals admit polynomial-time absolute approximation. Write a positive rational as \(2^e u\) with \(1\leq u<2\), and use

\[ \ln u=2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1},\qquad z=\frac{u-1}{u+1}. \]

Here \(0\leq z<1/3\), so the tail decays geometrically. The same series with \(z=1/3\) computes \(\ln2\). To obtain error \(2^{-h}\), polynomially many terms and polynomial-length rational arithmetic suffice, including the additional precision needed for the factor \(e\). Hence the oracle runs in time polynomial in the query length, \(m,\tau,L,k\), and \(\log(1/\xi)\).

All hypotheses of weak optimization are therefore satisfied in the binary computational model.

6. Final error and complexity

Apply weak optimization to minimize the last coordinate over \(E\), with tolerance \(\zeta=\varepsilon/4\). Let its rational output be \((\widehat x,\widehat t)\). The objective guarantee gives \(\widehat t\leq V_\eta+\zeta\). Since this output is within distance \(\zeta\) of \(E\), its last coordinate also satisfies \(\widehat t\geq V_\eta-\zeta\). Therefore \(|\widehat t-V_\eta|\leq\varepsilon/4\).

Combining this with the restriction bound and \(I_p(\mu)=V\) gives

\[ |\widehat t-I_p(\mu)|\leq|\widehat t-V_\eta|+(V_\eta-V)<\varepsilon/2\leq2^{-k}. \]

The algorithm uses only the supported types and rational masses; it never expands a society into individual ballots. Its running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

The discrete counting hardness stated in the question is compatible with this conclusion: the continuous rate is a convex optimum that can be approximated without counting winning completions.
