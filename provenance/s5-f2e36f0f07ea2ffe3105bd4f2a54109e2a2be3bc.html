The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses on finite sets of complete worker and firm types, contract templates joining those types, weak preferences over templates, and per-person laminar integral quotas, does there exist a polynomial-time \(3/2\)-approximation for maximum stable contract mass? An allocation gives each type a distribution over feasible integral multisets of template instances; for every template \(e=(a,b)\), the total mass of its worker-side and firm-side instances must agree and be realizable by coupling the atomless populations. Let \(r_{a,e}\) be the mass of type \(a\) whose bundle can add an instance of \(e\), or replace a strictly less-preferred instance with \(e\), while respecting its quotas. Stability requires \(r_{a,e}=0\) or \(r_{b,e}=0\) for every template. The algorithm should return a stable allocation with total contract mass at least \(2/3\) of the maximum, using time polynomial in the type and template counts, input bit length, and maximum nesting depth, with a sparse bundle-mixture output.

Result

Yes. A polynomial-time algorithm exists that returns a stable allocation with \(V\geq \frac23\mathrm{OPT}\). It can return at most \(6m+\tau\) positive bundle masses, all rational, with polynomial total encoding length.

The proof combines two facts: stable aggregate vectors in laminar polymatroids admit stable integral-bundle decompositions; and suitable finite replications of these decompositions satisfy the hypotheses of Yokoi’s approximation theorem. The replication is used only in the proof.

If \(m=0\), assigning every person the empty bundle resolves the instance. Henceforth assume \(m>0\).

1. The two external results used

We use the following precise consequences of the cited papers.

Ordered-polymatroid stability theorem. Given two polymatroid independence polytopes on a common ground set, each with a strict order, one can find a common independent vector such that every coordinate is rejected on at least one side. Rejection means that neither a positive increase nor a positive exchange against a strictly lower coordinate is feasible. With integral rank functions, the algorithm returns an integral vector. Its running time is \(O(n^3\gamma)\), where \(\gamma\) is the time for saturation and exchange-capacity queries. These are Theorems 5.9 and 6.6 of [Iwata–Yokoi](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf).

Finite approximation theorem. In Yokoi’s finite auxiliary construction, each original contract receives three variants, with a quota of one on its triple. For any stable auxiliary matching \(M^*\), its projection \(M\) is stable and satisfies \(|M|\geq \frac23|N|\) for every original stable matching \(N\). This is Theorem 1 of [Yokoi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03076v2).

The required three-variant orders are specified in Section 4 below. We verify all other hypotheses when applying these results.

2. Laminar polytopes and compact bundle decompositions

For a nonisolated type \(a\), define

\(P_a=\{z\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E_a}:z(S)\leq q_a(S)\text{ for every }S\in\mathcal L_a\}\),

where \(z(S)=\sum_{e\in S}z_e\). Its integral points are exactly the feasible bundles.

Lemma 1: \(P_a\) is an integral polymatroid independence polytope

Proof. Empty members of the laminar family impose no restriction and can be removed. Arrange the remaining members as an inclusion tree rooted at \(E_a\). Add missing singleton leaves, giving each added leaf the redundant capacity \(q_a(E_a)\). The children of every internal node partition that node.

Write \(c_S\) for a node’s capacity. For a subset \(T\) of the leaves, define recursively

\(R_{\{e\}}(T)=c_{\{e\}}\mathbf 1_{\{e\in T\}}\),

\(R_S(T)=\min\{c_S,\sum_{C\text{ child of }S}R_C(T)\}\).

The function \(R_{E_a}\) is normalized, nondecreasing, and submodular. Indeed, these properties hold at leaves, sums preserve them, and truncation at a nonnegative constant preserves them. For the last assertion, if \(g\) is nondecreasing and submodular, compare \(g(A),g(B),g(A\cup B),g(A\cap B)\): when truncation affects neither union nor intersection, submodularity is unchanged; otherwise monotonicity and \(g(A)+g(B)\geq g(A\cup B)+g(A\cap B)\) give the truncated inequality.

The recursive inequalities show that any \(z\in P_a\) satisfies

\(z(T\cap S)\leq R_S(T)\)

at every node, and hence \(z(T)\leq R_{E_a}(T)\). Conversely, for every constrained node \(S\),

\(R_{E_a}(S)\leq c_S\),

because only the branch containing \(S\) contributes along its ancestors. Thus the inequalities \(z(T)\leq R_{E_a}(T)\) imply all listed quota inequalities. This proves the polymatroid assertion.

For integrality, route a flow from a source through the singleton leaves toward the root and then to a sink. The flow leaving node \(S\) has capacity \(c_S\); the leaf inflow is \(z_e\). Internal flow conservation makes the flow leaving \(S\) equal to \(z(S)\). Closing the network with a sink-to-source arc of capacity \(q_a(E_a)\) gives an integral-capacity circulation polytope whose projection onto leaf inflows is exactly \(P_a\).

The integral-flow theorem applies: capacities are integral, conservation demands are zero, and the polytope is bounded. Therefore this projection is the convex hull of integral leaf-inflow vectors. Hence \(P_a\) is integral. \(\square\)

No monotonicity assumption on the listed quotas was used.

Lemma 2: Polynomial-time compact decomposition

A rational \(z\in P_a\) can be decomposed, in polynomial time, as

\(z=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\theta_iB^i,\qquad \theta_i>0,\qquad \sum_i\theta_i=1,\qquad s\leq |E_a|+1\),

where the \(B^i\) are feasible integral bundles and the coefficients have polynomial bit length.

Proof. We give a constructive argument that also controls encoding length.

Write the defining inequalities as \(A_jz\leq b_j\), including nonnegativity. Choose a vertex \(v\) in the smallest face containing \(z\). Such a vertex can be found by polynomially many linear programs, and Lemma 1 makes it integral.

If \(z=v\), stop. Otherwise let

\(t=\min_{j:\,b_j-A_jv>0}\frac{b_j-A_jz}{b_j-A_jv}\).

Every inequality tight at \(z\) is tight at \(v\). Thus all ratios in this minimum are positive. Boundedness implies \(t<1\): if every ratio were at least one, the nonzero vector \(z-v\) would be a recession direction.

Set

\(z'=\frac{z-tv}{1-t}\).

The ratio formula proves that \(z'\) is feasible. It preserves every equality defining the current face and makes at least one additional inequality tight. Therefore

\(z=tv+(1-t)z'\)

reduces the face dimension. Repeating requires at most \(|E_a|\) reductions.

For bit complexity, let \(v_0,v_1,\ldots\) be the extracted vertices and \(w_j\) their weights relative to the initial point. At reduction \(i\), choose a newly tight inequality \(\ell_i\). Its slack at \(v_i\) is positive; its slack at every later vertex and residual point is zero. Consequently each prefix of weights solves the triangular system

\(b_{\ell_i}-A_{\ell_i}z=\sum_{j=0}^{i}w_j(b_{\ell_i}-A_{\ell_i}v_j)\).

The diagonal entries are positive integers. All vertex coordinates are bounded by \(q_a(E_a)\), so the integer coefficients have polynomial bit length. Cramer’s rule bounds every prefix weight, and hence every intermediate residual point, by polynomial bit length. This establishes polynomial bit complexity as well as the stated support bound. \(\square\)

Both lemmas also hold when coordinates are replaced by finitely many variants and each quota set is replaced by the union of its variants.

3. Aggregate rejection lifts to bundle rejection

For a strictly ordered coordinate set, say that \(z\in P_a\) rejects \(e\) if neither of the following is possible:

  • \(z+\varepsilon\chi_e\in P_a\) for some \(\varepsilon>0\);
  • \(z+\varepsilon(\chi_e-\chi_f)\in P_a\) for some \(\varepsilon>0\) and some strictly lower coordinate \(f\).

Here \(\chi_e\) is the unit vector at \(e\).

Lemma 3: Every decomposition preserves rejection

If \(z\) rejects \(e\), then every bundle appearing with positive coefficient in any integral-bundle decomposition of \(z\) is nonreceptive to \(e\).

Proof. Some listed set containing \(e\) must be tight. Otherwise all such inequalities have positive slack, and a sufficiently small increase of \(e\) would be feasible.

Let \(T\) be the smallest tight listed set containing \(e\). It exists because the listed sets containing \(e\) form a chain.

Suppose \(f\in T\) is strictly lower than \(e\) and \(z_f>0\). Every tight listed set containing \(e\) contains \(T\), hence contains \(f\). Therefore every constraint increased by \(\chi_e-\chi_f\) has positive slack. Choosing \(\varepsilon>0\) smaller than these finitely many slacks and than \(z_f\) gives a feasible exchange, a contradiction. Thus

\(z_f=0\quad\text{for every strictly lower }f\in T.\)

Now write \(z=\sum_i\theta_iB^i\) with positive coefficients. Since \(z(T)=q_a(T)\) and \(B^i(T)\leq q_a(T)\), every \(B^i\) satisfies \(B^i(T)=q_a(T)\). Since coordinates are nonnegative, \(z_f=0\) also implies \(B^i_f=0\).

Adding \(e\) to \(B^i\) violates the quota on \(T\). A feasible replacement must remove an instance from \(T\), but no strictly lower instance occurs there. Thus \(B^i\) is nonreceptive. \(\square\)

This is a one-way lifting statement; it does not assume that arbitrary stable bundle mixtures are characterized by aggregate polymatroid stability.

4. The polynomial-time algorithm

4.1 Auxiliary templates and orders

Replace each template \(e\) by \(e^1,e^2,e^3\), with the same endpoint types. For every original quota set \(S\), introduce

\(S^*=\{e^j:e\in S,\ j\in\{1,2,3\}\}\)

with quota \(q_a(S)\). These are the auxiliary per-person constraints. Multiplicities remain permitted.

Order each original tie by template label. If the preference tiers are \(C_1,\ldots,C_s\), let \(C_i^j\) be the ordered list of their \(j\)-variants. Use the following strict auxiliary orders:

  • Workers: \(C_1^1,C_1^2,\ldots,C_s^1,C_s^2,C_1^3,\ldots,C_s^3\).
  • Firms: \(C_1^3,C_1^2,\ldots,C_s^3,C_s^2,C_1^1,\ldots,C_s^1\).

These are Yokoi’s orders. In particular, if \(e\) is strictly preferred to \(f\) originally, then \(e^2\) is preferred to every variant of \(f\) on either side.

4.2 Compute a stable aggregate vector

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the input masses. Its bit length is at most their total encoding length. For type \(a\), use the aggregate constraints

\(X(S^*)\leq D\mu_aq_a(S).\)

All these capacities are integral and have polynomial bit length.

On each side, the incident coordinate sets of different types are disjoint. Lemma 1 therefore gives a direct-sum integral polymatroid independence polytope. Extend the local strict orders to a total order on each side.

The required capacity oracles are explicit. For either side’s laminar constraint list, write \(c(S)\) for a capacity. At a feasible vector \(X\),

\(\operatorname{inc}(X,u)=\min_{S\ni u}(c(S)-X(S))\),

and, for distinct coordinates \(u,v\),

\(\operatorname{exch}(X,u,v)=\min\{X_v,\min_{S:\,u\in S,\ v\notin S}(c(S)-X(S))\}\),

where an empty inner minimum is interpreted as infinity. These formulas follow by inspecting exactly the inequalities increased by the proposed direction. They are computable in polynomial time from the explicitly listed sets.

Apply the ordered-polymatroid stability theorem. It returns an integral common independent vector \(X\), with each auxiliary coordinate rejected at one endpoint.

Put \(\xi=X/D\). For every nonisolated type, normalize its incident vector:

\(z_a=\xi|_{E_a^*}/\mu_a.\)

It belongs to the auxiliary per-person polytope. Decompose it using Lemma 2, and assign the resulting bundle masses

\(\lambda_{a,U}=\mu_a\theta_{a,U}.\)

An isolated type receives its empty bundle with mass \(\mu_a\).

The auxiliary coordinate balances hold because these decompositions preserve \(\xi\). By Lemma 3, rejection of an auxiliary coordinate at one endpoint implies zero receptive bundle mass there. Hence the resulting auxiliary bundle allocation is stable.

4.3 Project the bundles

For an auxiliary bundle \(U\), define its projection by

\(B_e=U_{e^1}+U_{e^2}+U_{e^3}.\)

Keep its mass, combining identical projected bundles if desired. The lifted quota constraints prove original feasibility; auxiliary balances prove original template balances. Projection preserves total contract mass.

It also preserves stability. If \(B\) is receptive to \(e\), then every auxiliary bundle projecting to \(B\) is receptive to \(e^2\):

  • A feasible addition of \(e\) lifts to an addition of \(e^2\).
  • A feasible replacement of a strictly lower template \(f\) lifts by removing any present variant of \(f\); \(e^2\) is preferred to that variant.

Thus positive original receptive mass at either endpoint implies positive auxiliary receptive mass for \(e^2\) there. Auxiliary stability prevents this at both endpoints. The projected allocation is stable.

5. Rational realization and existence of a rational optimum

Lemma 4: Rational balanced bundles have measurable pairings

Any finite rational bundle system satisfying the population and template-balance equations admits a measurable pairing with distinct opposite-side individuals for repeated instances of a template.

The same assertion holds for colored variants, while ensuring that two variants of the same underlying template never join the same two individuals.

Proof. Choose an integer \(H\) clearing all bundle-mass denominators. Introduce \(H\mu_a\) base individuals of each type, assigning exactly \(H\lambda_{a,B}\) of them bundle \(B\). For each template, pair worker and firm stubs arbitrarily. Balance makes this possible. This produces a finite bipartite multigraph; retain variant colors when present.

Give every base edge a distinct integer label \(g\) starting at zero, and choose \(T\) larger than the number of base edges. Replace each base individual \(u\) by copies \(u_i\), indexed by \(i\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}\). Replace a labeled base edge \(u v\) by

\(u_i v_{\,i+g\pmod T}\qquad(i\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}).\)

Every copy retains its prescribed bundle. Parallel base edges have different shifts, so their lifted endpoints differ. This remains true for differently colored edges of the same underlying template.

Finally, replace each lifted individual by an interval of measure \(1/(HT)\). For each lifted edge, pair equal relative coordinates in its endpoint intervals. These maps are measurable and realize the prescribed contract masses. Repeated instances have counterparts in distinct intervals, hence with distinct individuals. \(\square\)

This pairing also has a compact description. Order stubs by template, bundle class, slot, and base-individual index. Class prefixes have lengths \(B_eH\lambda_{a,B}\). Quotient and remainder identify a stub’s slot and individual; the edge label is its worker-side global index. These prefixes and the cyclic-shift rule use polynomially many binary integers when the bundle representation has polynomial length.

Lemma 5: \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is attained by a rational allocation

Proof. Temporarily omit the pairing requirement. Each type has finitely many feasible bundles because its root quota bounds every multiplicity. Consequently the balanced bundle systems form a compact polytope.

Stability is closed: for each template its condition is \(r_{a,e}r_{b,e}=0\), where both receptive masses are linear functions of bundle masses. The algorithm above supplies a stable system, so this compact stable set is nonempty. Contract mass therefore attains a maximum.

At a maximizing system, select, for each template, one endpoint with zero receptive mass. At each selected endpoint, forbid all bundles receptive to that template. With these bundle exclusions fixed, feasibility, balance, and the objective form a finite rational linear program. Every feasible point of this program is stable.

This program has a rational optimal vertex. Its value equals the maximum over the compact stable set: it contains the selected maximizer and cannot exceed that maximum. Lemma 4 realizes this rational vertex by measurable pairings. It therefore attains the maximum in the original model, namely \(\mathrm{OPT}\). \(\square\)

The possibly large linear program in this proof is not solved by the algorithm.

6. Transferring the approximation guarantee

Let \(A^*\) be the rational stable auxiliary allocation computed above, and let \(A\) be its projection. Let \(O\) be a rational optimal original allocation supplied by Lemma 5.

We construct two finite matchings on the same individuals.

6.1 Simultaneous finite realization

Choose \(H\) clearing the bundle masses of both \(A^*\) and \(O\). For each type, use the same \(H\mu_a\) base individuals for both allocations. Assign their auxiliary bundles according to \(A^*\), and independently assign their original bundles according to \(O\).

Pair stubs separately for the two allocations, then apply Lemma 4’s cyclic lift with a common sufficiently large \(T\). This produces:

  • a colored matching \(M^*\) realizing \(A^*\);
  • an original matching \(N\) realizing \(O\).

In \(M^*\), at most one variant of a given template joins any individual pair. Both finite realizations preserve every individual’s prescribed bundle.

Let \(J\) be the finite original instance containing, for every template \(e=(a,b)\), a contract of that template between every worker individual of type \(a\) and every firm individual of type \(b\). Its preferences depend only on original template ranks. Each individual has the quota family obtained by lifting its type’s sets to these incident contracts.

These families are laminar; quotas are integral; parallel contracts are allowed. Thus \(J\) satisfies the finite theorem’s hypotheses.

The matching \(N\) is stable in \(J\). For each original template, \(O\) has zero receptive mass at an endpoint type. Every individual of that type has a nonreceptive prescribed bundle. Therefore no unchosen finite contract of that template can be receptive at both endpoints.

6.2 Choose the finite auxiliary tie orders

We must show that \(M^*\) is stable in an auxiliary instance obtained by exactly Yokoi’s construction.

At each finite individual, order contracts within an original tie as follows:

  • Group them by template, retaining the template order used in Section 4.
  • Within each template, place contracts selected in the projection \(M\) before unselected contracts.
  • Order the remaining entries arbitrarily.

Apply Yokoi’s three-variant construction to these lists, including a quota of one on each individual-contract triple.

Comparisons between distinct copied template types agree with the compact auxiliary orders. Moreover, within a fixed copied template type, copies of selected underlying contracts precede copies of unselected underlying contracts.

6.3 Prove finite auxiliary stability

Consider an unchosen variant \(p_g\) of a finite underlying contract \(g\). Let \(p\) denote its copied template type.

Since \(A^*\) is stable, one endpoint type has zero receptive mass for \(p\). Choose the corresponding endpoint individual \(u\). Its auxiliary bundle is nonreceptive to \(p\).

There are two cases.

Case 1: The underlying contract \(g\) is unselected in \(M\).

A feasible addition of \(p_g\) would give a feasible addition of \(p\) to \(u\)’s compact bundle, contradicting nonreceptivity.

For a feasible improving replacement, if the removed variant has a different copied template type, its comparison with \(p_g\) agrees with the compact order. The replacement would therefore contradict compact nonreceptivity.

If the removed variant has the same copied template type, its underlying contract is selected. The chosen-first ordering puts it above \(p_g\), so removing it is not a strictly improving replacement.

Thus \(p_g\) is not receptive at \(u\).

Case 2: The underlying contract \(g\) is selected in \(M\) through another variant \(q_g\).

The triple quota forbids adding \(p_g\). It also forces any feasible replacement to remove \(q_g\). If this replacement is strictly improving, its two variants have distinct copied template types whose comparison agrees with the compact order. The corresponding compact exchange is feasible and strictly improving, again contradicting nonreceptivity.

Thus \(p_g\) is not receptive at \(u\) in this case also.

Every unchosen variant is rejected at an endpoint. Hence \(M^*\) is stable in the finite auxiliary instance.

6.4 Apply the finite theorem

All hypotheses are now verified: the original instance is finite with weak orders and integral laminar quotas; the auxiliary instance uses Yokoi’s prescribed orders and triple quotas; \(M^*\) is auxiliary-stable; and \(N\) is original-stable.

The finite approximation theorem gives

\(|M|=|M^*|\geq \frac23|N|.\)

Every finite individual represents mass \(1/(HT)\). Therefore

\(|M^*|=HT\,V(A^*)=HT\,V(A),\qquad |N|=HT\,\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Dividing proves

\(V(A)\geq \frac23\mathrm{OPT}.\)

7. Running time and output length

The algorithm operates on \(3m\) auxiliary coordinates and the explicitly listed lifted quota sets. It constructs neither the finite replication nor the optimal allocation used in the comparison proof.

The denominator-clearing integer \(D\) has polynomial bit length. The scaled capacities \(D\mu_aq_a(S)\) are integral with polynomial bit length. The ordered-polymatroid algorithm uses polynomially many oracle operations; the explicit oracle formulas above have polynomial cost. Its integral vectors are bounded by the scaled root quotas, so arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity.

For a type with \(d_a=|E_a|\), Lemma 2 uses at most \(3d_a+1\) auxiliary bundles. Since every template has two endpoints,

\(\sum_a d_a=2m.\)

Thus the total number of positive bundle masses is at most

\(\sum_a(3d_a+1)=6m+\tau.\)

Projection can only reduce this count. Every projected multiplicity is bounded by its type’s binary-encoded root quota. Lemma 2 gives polynomial-length rational coefficients, and multiplication by \(\mu_a\) preserves polynomial bit length. Lemma 4 supplies a compact measurable-pairing description from these data.

Consequently the algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\) and returns the requested polynomial-length stable allocation.
