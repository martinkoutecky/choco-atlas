The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given typed real variables with axis-aligned domains and Manhattan metrics, \(\tau\) source types represented by \(I\)-consistent cube bases \(K_t\) with rational bounds, a nonempty cube integrity constraint \(IC^*\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute the exact set \(\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\omega\in\overline{\operatorname{Mod}_I(IC^*)}} \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\inf_{\eta\models_I K_t} d(\omega,\eta)\), represented as a formula or as a product of coordinate intervals.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\) real coordinates, possibly grouped into typed vector variables. Each variable's domain is a nonempty closed axis-aligned cuboid, possibly unbounded; all finite bounds are binary rationals. Let \(W\) be the product of these domains, equipped with the Manhattan metric \(d\).
A cube is a conjunction of coordinate inequalities with rational thresholds, each strict or weak. Satisfaction is evaluated within \(W\); write \(\operatorname{Mod}_I(K)\) for the satisfying assignments. Each source cube \(K_t\) and the integrity cube \(IC^*\) must have nonempty model sets.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) source types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; a source type specifies its cube \(K_t\).
Compute the exact set \(A=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\omega\in\overline{\operatorname{Mod}_I(IC^*)}}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\inf_{\eta\in\operatorname{Mod}_I(K_t)}d(\omega,\eta)\), with closure taken in \(W\), represented as a formula of coordinate inequalities or a Cartesian product of intervals. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including all bounds, inequalities, and masses.

Result

The entire minimizing set \(A\) can be computed exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). It is a nonempty Cartesian product of closed, possibly unbounded intervals. Every finite endpoint in the output is an endpoint selected from the input bounds or thresholds.

The coordinate decomposition also appears in Section 4 of the cited [finite-profile result](https://www.cril.univ-artois.fr/~marquis/condotta-kaci-marquis-schwind-ecai10.pdf). The proof below handles binary-rational masses directly and establishes polynomial bit complexity.

1. Normalize the cubes and take closures

Flatten the typed vector variables into coordinates \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\). Because all domains are axis-aligned cuboids, write \(W=\prod_{j=1}^m W_j\), where each \(W_j\) is a nonempty closed interval, possibly unbounded.

Each cube contains only inequalities on individual coordinates. Consequently, for every source \(t\),

\( \operatorname{Mod}_I(K_t)=\prod_{j=1}^m S_{tj}, \)

where \(S_{tj}\) is the intersection of \(W_j\) with the inequalities of \(K_t\) on coordinate \(j\). The promised nonemptiness of the source model set implies that every \(S_{tj}\) is a nonempty interval.

Let \(a_{tj}\) be its greatest lower bound and \(b_{tj}\) its least upper bound, using infinite endpoints when appropriate. Then

\( B_{tj}:=\overline{S_{tj}}=[a_{tj},b_{tj}]. \)

Throughout, an interval with an infinite endpoint denotes a subset of the real line; infinity is not an element of the interval.

These endpoints are obtained by taking the maximum of all lower bounds and the minimum of all upper bounds, including domain bounds. Strict inequalities affect endpoint inclusion in \(S_{tj}\), but not its closure. Nonemptiness is essential here: it ensures that the resulting closed interval is precisely the closure.

Likewise, write

\( C:=\overline{\operatorname{Mod}_I(IC^*)}=\prod_{j=1}^m C_j,\qquad C_j=[c_j,d_j]. \)

These are closures in \(W\). Since \(W\) is closed in real coordinate space, they agree with ambient closures. Closure commutes with these finite products: coordinatewise convergent sequences prove that every point of the product of the coordinate closures lies in the closure of the original product, and continuity of coordinate projections proves the reverse inclusion.

Thus normalization requires only rational comparisons, and every finite normalized endpoint comes from the input.

2. Separate the objective by coordinate

For a nonempty closed interval \(B=[a,b]\), define its distance function by

\( \delta_B(x)=\sum_{\substack{\text{lower endpoint }a\\a\text{ finite}}}\max\{a-x,0\}+\sum_{\substack{\text{upper endpoint }b\\b\text{ finite}}}\max\{x-b,0\}. \)

Here each sum has either one term or no terms. This is exactly the distance from \(x\) to \(B\): it is zero inside the interval and the distance to the nearest endpoint outside it.

For any nonempty set \(S\), taking its closure does not change the infimum of distances to it. Indeed, \(S\subseteq\overline S\) gives one inequality; approximating any point of \(\overline S\) by points of \(S\), and using continuity of distance, gives the other.

For the closed product \(B_t=\prod_j B_{tj}\), Manhattan distance gives

\( \inf_{\eta\in\operatorname{Mod}_I(K_t)}d(\omega,\eta)=\inf_{\eta\in B_t}\sum_{j=1}^m|\omega_j-\eta_j|=\sum_{j=1}^m\delta_{B_{tj}}(\omega_j). \)

The last equality holds because each summand bounds the corresponding coordinate distance from below, while choosing a nearest point in each interval attains all these bounds simultaneously. Nearest points exist even for unbounded closed intervals.

Define

\( f_j(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\delta_{B_{tj}}(x). \)

The original objective is therefore \(F(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^m f_j(\omega_j)\), and its feasible set is \(\prod_j C_j\).

We next compute the complete minimizing set of each \(f_j\) on \(C_j\).

3. Exact minimization on the real line

Fix a coordinate and suppress its index. Write the source intervals as \(B_t=[a_t,b_t]\), and let

\( T=\sum_{t:\,a_t\text{ finite}}\mu_t. \)

Create an endpoint event of weight \(\mu_t\) at every finite \(a_t\), and another of weight \(\mu_t\) at every finite \(b_t\). If \(a_t=b_t\), both events occur at that point.

Sort and combine events at equal locations. Let the distinct event locations be \(z_1<\cdots<z_r\), with combined positive weights \(e_1,\ldots,e_r\). Define

\( E_0=0,\qquad E_k=\sum_{h=1}^k e_h\quad(1\leq k\leq r). \)

For every point \(x\) outside the event locations, direct differentiation gives

\( f'(x)=-T+\sum_{h:\,z_h<x}e_h. \)

To verify this calculation, a finite lower endpoint contributes slope \(-\mu_t\) before that endpoint and zero afterwards; a finite upper endpoint contributes zero before that endpoint and \(+\mu_t\) afterwards. Thus every event increases the slope by its weight.

Also,

\( E_r=T+\sum_{t:\,b_t\text{ finite}}\mu_t,\qquad 0\leq T\leq E_r. \)

If there are no events, every source interval is the whole real line, so \(f\) is identically zero and its minimizing set is the whole real line.

Otherwise define the extended endpoints \(\ell,u\) as follows:

  • If \(T=0\), set \(\ell=-\infty\). Otherwise, set \(\ell=z_k\), where \(k\) is the smallest index satisfying \(E_k\geq T\).
  • If \(T=E_r\), set \(u=+\infty\). Otherwise, set \(u=z_k\), where \(k\) is the smallest index satisfying \(E_k>T\).

The required indices exist by \(0\leq T\leq E_r\). Moreover, \(\ell\leq u\).

Claim. The complete unconstrained minimizing set is \(M=[\ell,u]\).

Proof. Before \(\ell\), every slope is strictly negative, because the corresponding prefix weight is less than \(T\). After \(u\), every slope is strictly positive, because the corresponding prefix weight is greater than \(T\).

If \(\ell<u\), the slope between them is zero. For finite distinct endpoints, this follows because the first prefix reaching \(T\) equals \(T\), and the next prefix exceeding \(T\) occurs at \(u\). The same calculation handles the initial ray when \(T=0\) and the final ray when \(T=E_r\).

The function is continuous and piecewise affine. Hence it is constant on \([\ell,u]\), strictly decreasing to that interval whenever there are points to its left, and strictly increasing after it whenever there are points to its right. These statements follow by summing slope times length over the finitely many affine pieces between any two points. They prove the claim. \(\square\)

In particular, the minimum exists without any boundedness assumption.

4. Impose the integrity interval

Restore the coordinate index. Let \(M_j=[\ell_j,u_j]\) be the unconstrained minimizing interval and \(C_j=[c_j,d_j]\) the integrity interval.

Its constrained minimizing set \(A_j\) is obtained by these exhaustive cases:

  • If \(C_j\cap M_j\neq\varnothing\), set \(A_j=C_j\cap M_j\).
  • If \(d_j<\ell_j\), set \(A_j=\{d_j\}\).
  • If \(c_j>u_j\), set \(A_j=\{c_j\}\).

In the second case, \(d_j\) is finite and \(f_j\) is strictly decreasing throughout \(C_j\), so its unique constrained minimizer is \(d_j\). In the third case, \(c_j\) is finite and strict increase gives the analogous conclusion. In the first case, precisely the feasible unconstrained minimizers attain the global minimum.

Thus each \(A_j\) is a nonempty closed interval. Finally,

\( A=\prod_{j=1}^m A_j. \)

To justify this equality, let \(v_j=\min_{x\in C_j}f_j(x)\). Every feasible assignment satisfies \(F(\omega)\geq\sum_j v_j\), and choosing each coordinate in \(A_j\) attains equality. Conversely, equality forces every nonnegative difference \(f_j(\omega_j)-v_j\) to vanish. Therefore every minimizing assignment belongs to the displayed product.

Writing \(A_j=[\alpha_j,\beta_j]\), the requested formula consists of \(x_j\geq\alpha_j\) for each finite lower endpoint and \(x_j\leq\beta_j\) for each finite upper endpoint. It uses at most \(2m\) inequalities.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

The endpoint procedure operates on the support directly.

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), with positive binary integers \(p_t,q_t\). Set

\( Q=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t,\qquad n_t=p_t(Q/q_t). \)

The bit length of \(Q\) is at most a constant plus the sum of the denominator bit lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Each \(n_t\) is an integer, and normalization gives

\( \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}n_t=Q. \)

Replace the masses by these integer weights in every endpoint sweep. This multiplies the objective by \(Q\) and preserves its minimizing set.

Each coordinate has at most \(2\tau\) events. Their total integer weight is at most \(2Q\), so all event weights, prefix sums, and target weights have \(O(L)\) bits. Equality and inequality comparisons with the target are therefore exact polynomial-time integer operations.

Normalization scans the input inequalities and compares rational endpoints. Per coordinate, sorting requires \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational comparisons, followed by \(O(\tau)\) additions and comparisons. Rational comparisons are polynomial-time by integer cross multiplication, since every finite endpoint has at most \(L\) bits. Computing \(Q\) and the integer weights also takes polynomial time.

Every finite output endpoint is selected from the normalized source or integrity endpoints, hence ultimately from the input. The output has polynomial length.

This proves an exact polynomial-time algorithm for the complete set \(A\), including strict inequalities, singleton domains, unbounded intervals, and binary-rational masses.
