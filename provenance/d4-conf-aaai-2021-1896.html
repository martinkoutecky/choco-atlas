Given a population scale \(N\), a target set \(T_N\) whose size grows proportionally to \(N\), rational payoff data, and \(\epsilon>0\), let defender types \(\Theta\) have rational shares \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one and per-defender resource rates \(\rho_\theta\). The input includes a finite candidate coalition structure \((q^j,x^j)_{j=1}^r\), with rational type-mass vectors satisfying \(\sum_j q^j=\mu\), coverage vectors \(x^j\in[0,1]^{T_N}\), and \(\sum_{t\in T_N}x_t^j\le N\sum_\theta\rho_\theta q_\theta^j\). Its overall coverage is \(c_t=1-\prod_j(1-x_t^j)\), which gives each type its baseline utility \(u_\theta\) under the paper's attacker response and pessimistic tie-breaking. Decide whether there is no nonzero deviating mass vector \(d\le\mu\) and feasible deviation coverage \(x\), with \(\sum_t x_t\le N\sum_\theta\rho_\theta d_\theta\), such that every type with \(d_\theta>0\) gains at least \(\epsilon\) for every feasible revenger coverage \(y\), where \(\sum_t y_t\le N\sum_\theta\rho_\theta(\mu_\theta-d_\theta)\) and the resulting coverage is \(c'_t=1-(1-x_t)(1-y_t)\).

Proved classification: $\mathrm{coNP}$-completeness

For every nonempty size promise in the question, mass $\epsilon$-core membership is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions. Its complement, existence of a successful deviation, is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete.

The hardness holds even when the candidate has one coalition, $\epsilon=1$, the masses are dyadic rationals, and all payoffs are nonnegative.

This distinguishes two meanings of the question’s hardness alternative:

  • Under polynomial-time Turing reductions, membership is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.
  • Under polynomial-time many-one reductions, membership is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard if and only if $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.
  • Membership is in $\mathrm{P}$ if and only if $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.

I prove the classification below. The final status is conservatively marked partial relative to the literal many-one “$\mathrm{P}$ or $\mathrm{NP}$-hard” alternative.

1. Baselines and full-mass deviations

Compute the candidate’s coverage and utilities exactly:
$$ c_t=1-\prod_{j=1}^r(1-x_t^j), \qquad u_\theta=\min_{t\in B(c)}D_\theta(c,t). $$

Write
$$ W_\theta=u_\theta+\epsilon, \qquad \Delta_t=r^a(t)-p^a(t)>0, \qquad \delta_{\theta t}=r^d_\theta(t)-p^d_\theta(t)>0. $$

Thus
$$ A(z,t)=r^a(t)-\Delta_tz_t, \qquad D_\theta(z,t)=p^d_\theta(t)+\delta_{\theta t}z_t. $$

For a nonempty set $S$ of participating types, define
$$ s_t=\max\left\{ 0,\; \max_{\theta\in S} \frac{W_\theta-p^d_\theta(t)}{\delta_{\theta t}} \right\}. $$

For coverage $z_t\in[0,1]$, all participating types meet their improvement requirements at $t$ exactly when $z_t\ge s_t$. In particular, thresholds exceeding one must remain uncapped.

Lemma 1. Every successful deviation can be replaced by one using the entire mass of every participating type.

Proof. Suppose $(d,x)$ is successful, and let $S=\{\theta:d_\theta>0\}$. Define $d^S_\theta=\mu_\theta$ for $\theta\in S$ and zero otherwise. Then $d^S\ge d$, so
$$ K(d^S)\ge K(d), \qquad K(\mu-d^S)\le K(\mu-d). $$

Consequently, $x$ remains feasible, and the remaining society’s feasible set contracts:
$$ C(\mu-d^S)\subseteq C(\mu-d). $$

The participating types are unchanged. Every remaining-society strategy that must be checked after replacement was already covered by the original universal quantifier. Hence $(d^S,x)$ is successful. $\square$

For a fixed nonempty $S$, put
$$ k=N\sum_{\theta\in S}\rho_\theta\mu_\theta, \qquad R=N\sum_{\theta\notin S}\rho_\theta\mu_\theta. $$

2. A self-contained polynomial test for a fixed type set

This section proves the upper bound used in the classification.

2.1 Suppression costs

For $x\in[0,1]^m$ and a payoff bound $v$, define
$$ H_t(x,v)= \begin{cases} 0,&A(x,t)\le v,\\ +\infty,&A(x,t)>v\text{ and }p^a(t)>v,\\ \dfrac{A(x,t)-v}{\Delta_t(1-x_t)}, &A(x,t)>v\text{ and }p^a(t)\le v. \end{cases} $$

This is the minimum remaining-society coverage at $t$ needed to reduce its attacker payoff to at most $v$. Indeed, combined coverage is $x_t+(1-x_t)y_t$, so
$$ A(c',t)=A(x,t)-\Delta_t(1-x_t)y_t. $$

In the third case, $x_t<1$, and the displayed quotient belongs to $(0,1]$. If $p^a(t)>v$, even full coverage cannot attain the bound.

A target $t$ can be a best response under some feasible remaining-society strategy exactly when
$$ \sum_{j\ne t}H_j(x,A(x,t))\le R. $$

To prove this, first observe that replacing $y_t$ by zero increases the attacker payoff at $t$, leaves the others unchanged, and reduces expenditure. Thus a reachable best response remains reachable with $y_t=0$. With that coordinate fixed, making $t$ a best response requires every other target’s payoff to be at most $A(x,t)$. The independent minimum costs are exactly the summands above.

It follows that a rational feasible $x$ is successful precisely when every target satisfying this reachability inequality has $x_t\ge s_t$. This is a polynomial-time verification procedure.

2.2 Constructing a distinguished strategy

If every $s_t\le1$ and
$$ \sum_ts_t\le k, $$
then $x=s$ is successful.

Otherwise, let
$$ I=\{t:s_t>1\}. $$

When $I$ is nonempty, put $P=\max_{t\in I}p^a(t)$ and restrict the following construction to $v\ge P$. When $I$ is empty, there is no lower restriction on $v$.

Define
$$ h_t(v)=\max\left\{0,\frac{r^a(t)-v}{\Delta_t}\right\}, \qquad f(v)=\sum_t\min\{s_t,h_t(v)\}. $$

Every coordinate $\min\{s_t,h_t(v)\}$ lies in $[0,1]$ on the permitted domain. For $s_t\le1$ this is immediate; for $s_t>1$, the restriction $v\ge P\ge p^a(t)$ gives $h_t(v)\le1$.

Let $v_*$ be the smallest permitted value satisfying $f(v_*)\le k$, and set
$$ x_t^*=\min\{s_t,h_t(v_*)\}. $$

The minimum exists. The function $f$ is continuous and nonincreasing, and it is zero for $v\ge\max_t r^a(t)$. A finite lower endpoint is included. If there is no lower endpoint, failure of the initial case gives $\sum_ts_t>k$, and $f(v)$ tends to this larger value as $v$ tends to $-\infty$.

Partition the targets into
$$ G=\{t:x_t^*=s_t\}, \qquad V=\{t:x_t^*<s_t\}, $$
and define
$$ g=\sum_{t\in G}H_t(x^*,v_*). $$

Lemma 2. In this remaining case, $S$ admits a successful deviation if and only if $g>R$.

Proof. Three properties of the construction will be used.

First, for every $t\in V$,
$$ A(x^*,t)\le v_*. $$
If $x_t^*>0$, then $x_t^*=h_t(v_*)$, giving equality. If $x_t^*=0$, then $r^a(t)\le v_*$.

Second, whenever $x_t^*>0$,
$$ A(x^*,t)\ge v_*. $$
For $t\in V$ this was just proved. For $t\in G$, the inequality $h_t(v_*)\ge s_t=x_t^*$ gives the result.

Third, there is $t_0\in V$ with $A(x^*,t_0)=v_*$, and either $\sum_tx_t^*=k$ or such a target can be chosen with $x_{t_0}^*=1$. To see this, if $v_*=P$ at a finite lower endpoint, choose $t_0\in I$ with $p^a(t_0)=P$. Then
$$ x_{t_0}^*=h_{t_0}(P)=1<s_{t_0}. $$

Otherwise, continuity and minimality imply $f(v_*)=k$. If no unsafe target had payoff $v_*$, every unsafe coordinate would be zero with $r^a(t)<v_*$. A sufficiently small decrease of $v_*$ would keep those coordinates zero and every safe coordinate saturated at $s_t$. It would therefore preserve $f(v_*)=k$, contradicting minimality. This proves the third property.

Suppose first that $g>R$. If an unsafe target became a best response, its payoff would be at most $v_*$. Every safe target would then also have payoff at most $v_*$, requiring remaining-society expenditure at least $g$. This exceeds $R$. Therefore every best response is safe, and $x^*$ is successful.

Now suppose $g\le R$, and write
$$ \beta_t=H_t(x^*,v_*)\quad(t\in G). $$
All these costs are finite and belong to $[0,1]$.

Consider any feasible alternative deviator strategy $z$, and put
$$ Q=\{t:z_t<x_t^*\}. $$

If $Q$ is nonempty, choose $t_*\in Q$ maximizing $A(z,t)$ over $Q$. The second property gives
$$ A(z,t_*)>A(x^*,t_*)\ge v_*. $$

Assign remaining coverage $\beta_t$ on $G\setminus\{t_*\}$ and zero elsewhere. This costs at most $g\le R$. Every target outside $Q$ has combined attacker payoff at most $v_*$: safe targets are suppressed, and unsafe targets satisfy the first property. Targets inside $Q$ have combined payoffs at most their payoffs under $z$, hence at most $A(z,t_*)$. Target $t_*$ receives no remaining coverage, so it is a best response. But
$$ c'_{t_*}=z_{t_*}<x_{t_*}^*\le s_{t_*}. $$
Some participating type therefore receives less than its required utility at this best response.

If $Q$ is empty, then $z\ge x^*$ coordinatewise. Assign $\beta_t$ on $G$ and zero on $V$. All attacker payoffs are at most $v_*$. If $\sum_tx_t^*=k$, feasibility forces $z=x^*$. Otherwise, the third property supplies an unsafe coordinate equal to one, which remains one under $z\ge x^*$. In either case an unsafe target retains payoff $v_*$ and is a best response. Its coverage is below its threshold.

Thus every feasible $z$ is defeated when $g\le R$. $\square$

2.3 Bit complexity and the upper bound

The function $f$ is piecewise affine. Its breakpoints in the permitted domain occur among
$$ r^a(t), \qquad r^a(t)-\Delta_ts_t, $$
together with the finite endpoint $P$, when present. There are $O(m)$ breakpoints. Sorting them and solving an affine equation on the relevant interval computes the smallest feasible $v_*$ exactly, including flat intervals.

All constructed rationals have polynomial bit length: they result from polynomially many rational additions, multiplications, comparisons, and divisions. Baseline computation, the fixed-subset test, and suppression-cost evaluation therefore take time polynomial in $m,\tau,r,L$.

By Lemma 1, a successful deviation exists exactly when some nonempty subset passes this test. That subset is a polynomial-size certificate of nonmembership. Hence
$$ \text{mass }\epsilon\text{-core membership}\in\mathrm{coNP}. $$

The same proof gives the exact algorithm
$$ 2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,r,L). $$

3. A criterion for canonicality

The canonical promise can be checked directly.

Define
$$ P_{\max}=\max_t p^a(t), \qquad F(v)=\sum_t\max\left\{0,\frac{r^a(t)-v}{\Delta_t}\right\}. $$

Lemma 3. Since $K(\mu)>0$, the society is canonical exactly when
$$ K(\mu)\le F(P_{\max}). $$

Proof. Suppose $\bar c$ is canonical, and let $v=\max_tA(\bar c,t)$. Every attacker payoff is at least its fully covered payoff, so $v\ge P_{\max}$.

Whenever $\bar c_t>0$, canonicality gives $A(\bar c,t)=v$, and hence
$$ \bar c_t=\frac{r^a(t)-v}{\Delta_t}. $$
Whenever $\bar c_t=0$, we have $r^a(t)\le v$. Therefore
$$ \bar c_t=\max\left\{0,\frac{r^a(t)-v}{\Delta_t}\right\}, \qquad K(\mu)=F(v)\le F(P_{\max}). $$

Conversely, assume $0<K(\mu)\le F(P_{\max})$. Continuity gives a value $v\ge P_{\max}$ with $F(v)=K(\mu)$, because $F$ vanishes at $\max_t r^a(t)$. Set
$$ \bar c_t=\max\left\{0,\frac{r^a(t)-v}{\Delta_t}\right\}. $$
The condition $v\ge P_{\max}$ ensures $\bar c_t\le1$. Every positive coordinate has attacker payoff $v$, while every zero coordinate has payoff at most $v$. Since $K(\mu)>0$, a positive coordinate exists. Thus $\bar c$ spends exactly $K(\mu)$ and assigns positive coverage only to best responses. $\square$

4. Reduction from PARTITION

I use the classical $\mathrm{NP}$-completeness of PARTITION with positive binary-encoded integer weights. See [Karp, “Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems”](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may restrict to instances with $\ell\ge3$ weights and even total sum. Odd-total instances are automatically negative; instances with fewer than three weights can be decided directly. These exceptional instances can be mapped to fixed positive or negative instances satisfying the restrictions, for example $(1,1,2)$ and $(1,1,4)$.

Let the weights be
$$ w_1,\ldots,w_\ell>0, \qquad A=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}w_i, \qquad W=A/2. $$

The PARTITION question is whether some subset has weight sum $W$.

4.1 Meeting the population-size promise

If $[a,b]$ contains no positive rational, there are no integer pairs satisfying the size promise, so the promised class is empty. We henceforth consider the intended nonempty case.

Choose a fixed positive rational $u/v\in[a,b]$, with positive integers $u,v$. Choose $n\ge\ell$, $n\ge3$, so that $n+1$ is divisible by $u$. Define
$$ m=n+1, \qquad N=\frac{v(n+1)}{u}. $$
Then $m/N=u/v\in[a,b]$. Because $u,v$ are fixed, $n$ and $N$ are polynomially bounded in $\ell$.

There are item targets $1,\ldots,n$ and a control target $0$. For auxiliary item targets $i>\ell$, set $w_i=0$. These auxiliary targets have no associated item type.

4.2 Parameters and attacker payoffs

Define
$$ \beta=\frac1{8n}, \qquad \gamma=\frac1{16A}, \qquad \alpha=\frac{\gamma}{2}, \qquad v_0=\frac{\gamma}{4}, $$
$$ \Delta_0=\frac{\gamma}{2n(1-\beta)}, \qquad \eta=\alpha\Delta_0 =\frac{\gamma^2}{4n(1-\beta)}, $$
$$ E=\frac{n(1-\beta)}{\gamma}-A=(16n-3)A, \qquad k_0=\frac58, $$
and prescribe the total resource budget
$$ K_\star=k_0+n-v_0E+\eta W^2-\frac{\eta}{2}. $$

For each item target, let
$$ \Delta_i= \frac{\gamma}{(1-\beta)(1-\beta-\gamma w_i)}, \qquad r^a(i)=\Delta_i, \qquad p^a(i)=0. $$

At the control target, let
$$ r^a(0)=v_0+\frac{\Delta_0}{2}, \qquad p^a(0)=v_0-\frac{\Delta_0}{2}. $$

These are valid strict payoff pairs. The item denominators are positive because
$$ \beta+\gamma w_i\le\frac1{24}+\frac1{16}=\frac5{48}<1. $$

Also,
$$ p^a(0) =\frac{\gamma}{4} \left(1-\frac1{n(1-\beta)}\right)>0, $$
since $n(1-\beta)=n-\frac18>1$. Thus every attacker payoff is nonnegative.

4.3 Types, resources, and defender payoffs

There are $\ell$ item types, a sponsor type $\sigma$, and a type $\omega$. Their full-mass resource budgets are
$$ \kappa_i=\alpha w_i\quad(1\le i\le\ell), \qquad \kappa_\sigma=k_0, \qquad \kappa_\omega=K_\star-k_0-\alpha A. $$

All are positive. For the last one, use
$$ n-v_0E=\frac{3n}{4}+\frac3{64}, \qquad \alpha A=\frac1{32}, $$
to obtain
$$ \kappa_\omega =\frac{3n}{4}+\frac1{64} +\eta\left(W^2-\frac12\right)>0. $$

To realize these budgets with dyadic masses, choose $h$ such that $2^h\ge\ell+2$. Give every item type and the sponsor mass $2^{-h}$, and give $\omega$ the remaining mass
$$ \mu_\omega=1-(\ell+1)2^{-h}>0. $$
Set
$$ \rho_\theta=\frac{\kappa_\theta}{N\mu_\theta}. $$
Then all rates are positive rational numbers and $K(\mu)=K_\star$.

Define positive numbers $s_{\theta t}$ as follows:

  • For the sponsor, $s_{\sigma i}=\beta$ at every item target.
  • For item type $j$, put $s_{ji}=\beta+\gamma w_j$ when $i=j$, and $s_{ji}=\beta$ otherwise.
  • At the control target, $s_{\theta0}=2$ for every item type and the sponsor.
  • For type $\omega$, put $s_{\omega t}=2$ at every target.

Set defender payoffs to
$$ p^d_\theta(t)=0, \qquad r^d_\theta(t)=\frac1{s_{\theta t}}. $$
Thus
$$ D_\theta(z,t)=\frac{z_t}{s_{\theta t}}. $$

The candidate consists of one coalition with $q=\mu$ and coverage $x=0$. It is feasible. Set $\epsilon=1$.

Every type’s baseline utility is zero, because all defender payoffs at zero coverage are zero. Therefore improvement requires utility at least one, and the numbers $s_{\theta t}$ are exactly the coverage thresholds.

5. Verification of the canonical promise

The largest fully covered attacker payoff is
$$ P_{\max}=p^a(0)=v_0-\frac{\Delta_0}{2}>0. $$

Every item target has $\Delta_i>\gamma>P_{\max}$, so its contribution to $F(P_{\max})$ is $1-P_{\max}/\Delta_i$. The control target contributes one.

From the item definitions,
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{\Delta_i}=(1-\beta)E. $$
Hence
$$ F(P_{\max})=1+n-P_{\max}(1-\beta)E. $$

Subtracting the prescribed budget gives
$$ F(P_{\max})-K_\star = \frac38 +v_0\beta E +\frac{\Delta_0}{2}(1-\beta)E -\eta W^2+\frac{\eta}{2}. $$

All terms except $-\eta W^2$ are positive, and
$$ \eta W^2 = \frac{(\gamma A)^2}{16n(1-\beta)} = \frac1{4096n(1-\beta)} <\frac1{4096}. $$

Consequently,
$$ 0<K_\star<F(P_{\max}). $$
Lemma 3 proves that every constructed society is canonical.

6. Characterizing every possible successful coalition

By Lemma 1, it suffices to consider full-mass participating sets.

6.1 Type $\omega$ cannot participate

For every target and every feasible coverage,
$$ D_\omega(z,t)=\frac{z_t}{2}\le\frac12<1. $$

Thus $\omega$ can never meet its required improvement, and no successful coalition contains it.

6.2 A successful coalition must contain the sponsor

Consider a nonempty coalition of item types without the sponsor. Its budget satisfies
$$ k\le\alpha A=\frac1{32}. $$

For any strategy $x$ of this coalition, let $G$ be the targets where every participant meets its threshold under $x$. The control target is outside $G$. Every target in $G$ is an item target with $x_i\ge\beta$, so
$$ |G|\beta\le\sum_tx_t\le k, \qquad |G|\le\frac{k}{\beta}\le\frac n4. $$

Meanwhile, $K_\star>3n/4$, so the remaining budget satisfies
$$ R=K_\star-k>\frac{3n}{4}-\frac1{32}>\frac n4. $$

The remaining society can therefore assign coverage one on every target in $G$ and zero elsewhere. Every such covered item target then has attacker payoff zero. The control target has attacker payoff at least $p^a(0)>0$, so every best response lies outside $G$.

At targets outside $G$, remaining coverage is zero. Hence some participating type receives less than one at a best response. Pessimistic tie-breaking defeats the deviation.

Therefore every successful coalition contains the sponsor.

6.3 Sponsor-containing coalitions encode PARTITION exactly

Let a coalition consist of the sponsor and item types in $J$. Define
$$ Z=\sum_{j\in J}w_j. $$

Its budget and thresholds are
$$ k=k_0+\alpha Z, \qquad s_i= \begin{cases} \beta+\gamma w_i,&i\in J,\\ \beta,&i\notin J, \end{cases} \qquad s_0=2. $$

Consider the strategy
$$ x_i^*=s_i\quad(1\le i\le n), \qquad x_0^*=\frac12-\alpha Z, $$
and the payoff value
$$ v=v_0+\eta Z. $$

Since $\alpha Z\le\alpha A=1/32$, the control coverage is in $[0,1]$. Moreover,
$$ \sum_tx_t^* =n\beta+\gamma Z+\frac12-\alpha Z =k_0+\alpha Z=k. $$

The control target’s attacker payoff is $v$. Each item target’s attacker payoff is strictly larger:
$$ A(x^*,i)=\Delta_i(1-s_i)\ge\gamma>v. $$

For the last inequality, observe that $\Delta_0<\gamma$ and
$$ v=v_0+\Delta_0\alpha Z \le\frac{\gamma}{4}+\frac{\Delta_0}{32} <\gamma. $$

We now check that this is precisely the distinguished strategy in Lemma 2. Here the sole threshold exceeding one is the control threshold, so the permitted lower endpoint is $p^a(0)$. We have $v>p^a(0)$, every item coordinate is saturated at $s_i$, and the control coordinate equals $h_0(v)$. Therefore $f(v)=k$.

In a neighborhood of $v$, the item coordinates remain saturated while the control coordinate decreases with slope $-1/\Delta_0$. Thus $f$ is strictly decreasing there. Since $f$ is globally nonincreasing, every smaller permitted value has $f>k$. Hence $v=v_*$.

The safe targets are exactly the item targets. Their total suppression cost is
$$ g=\sum_{i=1}^n \left(1-\frac{v}{\Delta_i(1-s_i)}\right). $$

For an unselected item target,
$$ \frac1{\Delta_i(1-\beta)} =\frac{1-\beta-\gamma w_i}{\gamma}. $$

Selecting its associated type increases this reciprocal by exactly $w_i$, because
$$ \frac1{\Delta_i(1-\beta-\gamma w_i)} =\frac{1-\beta}{\gamma}. $$

Consequently,
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{\Delta_i(1-s_i)}=E+Z, $$
and therefore
$$ g=n-(v_0+\eta Z)(E+Z). $$

Lemma 2 says that the coalition succeeds exactly when
$$ g>R=K_\star-k, $$
or equivalently $k+g-K_\star>0$.

The decisive calculation is
$$ \begin{aligned} k+g &=k_0+n-v_0E +(\alpha-v_0-\eta E)Z-\eta Z^2. \end{aligned} $$

Since
$$ \eta E =\frac{\gamma}{4}-\eta A =v_0-\eta A, $$
we have
$$ \alpha-v_0-\eta E=\eta A=2\eta W. $$

Substituting the definition of $K_\star$ yields
$$ k+g-K_\star =\eta\left(\frac12-(Z-W)^2\right). $$

Both $Z$ and $W$ are integers, and $\eta>0$. Thus
$$ k+g-K_\star>0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z=W. $$

We have proved:
$$ \boxed{ \text{A successful deviation exists} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the PARTITION instance is positive}. } $$
