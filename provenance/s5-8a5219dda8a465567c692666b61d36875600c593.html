The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many creditor types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita bounds \(\ell_t,u_t\), and total worth per capita \(V\), with the type-constant core promised to be \(C_\mu=\{x:\ell_t\le x_t\le u_t,\ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=V\}\), compute its egalitarian-core allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has finite rational per-capita bounds \(\ell_t\le u_t\); the per-capita worth \(V\) is rational. Bounds and worth may be negative. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; there is no separate parameter \(m\).
The type-constant core is promised to be the nonempty set \(C_\mu=\{x\in\mathbb R^T:\ell_t\le x_t\le u_t\text{ for all }t,\ \sum_t\mu_t x_t=V\}\).
An egalitarian-core allocation is an \(x\in C_\mu\) admitting no transfer from a richer type to a poorer type: there are no distinct \(s,t\) with \(x_s>x_t\) and real \(\delta>0\) for which \(y_s=x_s-\delta/\mu_s\), \(y_t=x_t+\delta/\mu_t\), and \(y_r=x_r\) for all other types give \(y\in C_\mu\).

Is exact computation of an egalitarian-core allocation polynomial-time in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite two-bound core games, the egalitarian core is single-valued and has an explicit common-level clipping formula using the coordinatewise minimum and maximum core payoffs ([Gong, Dietzenbacher, and Peters, 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00186-022-00800-1)). No result settling the complexity of exactly the normalized mass problem above was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one so that V is total worth per capita.
  • Required nonempty support and a nonempty core, retaining the discrete domain's nonemptiness conditions.
  • Specified finite binary-rational bounds and worth, allowing negative values as in the transferable-utility game domain.
  • Defined the continuous egalitarian core by feasible transfers between type-constant allocations, conserving total payoff through mass-weighted per-capita changes and comparing per-capita payoffs.
  • Interpreted computation as exact computation of an egalitarian-core member; no uniqueness promise was added.
  • Defined L as total binary input length; this scalar-payoff problem has no separate parameter m.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Exact computation is polynomial-time in \(\tau\) and \(L\). The egalitarian-core allocation is unique and has the formula

\( x_t^*=\min\{u_t,\max\{\ell_t,\lambda\}\}, \)

where \(\lambda\) satisfies

\( \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min\{u_t,\max\{\ell_t,\lambda\}\}=V. \)

Below is a direct proof for the normalized mass model, followed by an exact algorithm and its bit-complexity analysis.

1. Characterizing feasible transfers

For \(x\in C_\mu\) and distinct types \(s,t\) with \(x_s>x_t\), a transfer of the stated form is feasible if and only if

\( x_s>\ell_s \quad\text{and}\quad x_t<u_t. \)

Indeed, any feasible positive transfer gives
\( x_s-\delta/\mu_s\geq\ell_s \)
and
\( x_t+\delta/\mu_t\leq u_t. \)
Since \(\delta,\mu_s,\mu_t\) are positive, these inequalities imply both strict inequalities above.

Conversely, if both strict inequalities hold, choose

\( \delta=\frac12\min\{\mu_s(x_s-\ell_s),\mu_t(u_t-x_t)\}>0. \)

The changed coordinates remain within their bounds: decreasing \(x_s\) preserves its upper bound, increasing \(x_t\) preserves its lower bound, and the choice of \(\delta\) preserves their other bounds. Moreover,

\( \sum_{r\in T}\mu_r y_r =\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r x_r-\delta+\delta =V. \)

Thus \(y\in C_\mu\). Consequently, \(x\) is egalitarian precisely when, for every distinct \(s,t\),

\( x_s>x_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_s=\ell_s\ \text{or}\ x_t=u_t. \)

2. Existence of a clipped allocation

Define

\( c_t(\lambda)=\min\{u_t,\max\{\ell_t,\lambda\}\}, \qquad F(\lambda)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_t(\lambda). \)

Each clipping function is continuous and nondecreasing, so \(F\) has both properties.

Nonemptiness of \(C_\mu\), together with positivity of the masses, implies

\( \sum_t\mu_t\ell_t\leq V\leq\sum_t\mu_t u_t. \)

Let \(\alpha=\min_t\ell_t\) and \(\beta=\max_t u_t\). Then \(\alpha\leq\beta\), and

\( F(\alpha)=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t, \qquad F(\beta)=\sum_t\mu_t u_t. \)

The intermediate value theorem therefore supplies \(\lambda\in[\alpha,\beta]\) with \(F(\lambda)=V\). If \(\alpha=\beta\), the endpoint equalities already give this conclusion.

Fix such a \(\lambda\) and set \(z_t=c_t(\lambda)\). By construction, \(z\in C_\mu\).

Two elementary implications of clipping will be useful:

  • If \(z_t>\ell_t\), then \(z_t\leq\lambda\).
  • If \(z_t<u_t\), then \(z_t\geq\lambda\).

For the first, \(z_t>\ell_t\) forces \(\lambda>\ell_t\), hence \(z_t=\min\{u_t,\lambda\}\leq\lambda\). For the second, \(z_t<u_t\) forces \(\lambda<u_t\), hence \(z_t=\max\{\ell_t,\lambda\}\geq\lambda\).

Suppose a richer-to-poorer transfer were feasible from \(s\) to \(t\) at \(z\). Section 1 would give \(z_s>\ell_s\) and \(z_t<u_t\). The clipping implications would then yield

\( z_s\leq\lambda\leq z_t, \)

contradicting \(z_s>z_t\). Therefore \(z\) is egalitarian.

3. Uniqueness

Let \(x\in C_\mu\) be egalitarian. Suppose \(x\neq z\).

Because every mass is positive and

\( \sum_t\mu_t(x_t-z_t)=0, \)

there must be distinct \(s,t\) with

\( x_s>z_s, \qquad x_t<z_t. \)

Otherwise all nonzero differences would have the same sign, contradicting the zero weighted sum.

Since \(x_s\leq u_s\), we have \(z_s<u_s\), so the clipping implication gives \(z_s\geq\lambda\). Similarly, \(x_t\geq\ell_t\) implies \(z_t>\ell_t\), hence \(z_t\leq\lambda\). Thus

\( x_s>z_s\geq\lambda\geq z_t>x_t. \)

Furthermore,

\( x_s>z_s\geq\ell_s, \qquad x_t<z_t\leq u_t. \)

Section 1 now supplies a feasible transfer from the richer type \(s\) to the poorer type \(t\), contradicting egalitarianism. Therefore \(x=z\).

This proves uniqueness of the allocation even when several levels \(\lambda\) satisfy the budget equation.

4. An exact algorithm

Sort the distinct values among all bounds into

\( b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_k, \qquad k\leq2\tau. \)

Evaluate \(F(b_j)\) exactly for every breakpoint.

If \(F(b_j)=V\) for some \(j\), return \(x_t^*=c_t(b_j)\).

Otherwise, the endpoint inequalities and monotonicity imply that some consecutive pair satisfies

\( F(b_j)<V<F(b_{j+1}). \)

Set \(q=(b_j+b_{j+1})/2\), and partition the types into

\( H=\{t:q<\ell_t\}, \qquad A=\{t:\ell_t<q<u_t\}, \qquad K=\{t:q>u_t\}. \)

These sets partition \(T\), because no bound lies strictly between consecutive breakpoints; in particular, \(q\) equals no bound.

Throughout the closed interval \([b_j,b_{j+1}]\), types in \(H\) are clipped to their lower bounds, types in \(K\) to their upper bounds, and types in \(A\) have value \(\lambda\). Hence

\( F(\lambda)=B+M\lambda, \)

where

\( B=\sum_{t\in H}\mu_t\ell_t+\sum_{t\in K}\mu_t u_t, \qquad M=\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t. \)

The strict endpoint inequalities imply

\( M(b_{j+1}-b_j)=F(b_{j+1})-F(b_j)>0, \)

so \(M>0\). Compute

\( \lambda=\frac{V-B}{M}. \)

The same endpoint inequalities show \(b_j<\lambda<b_{j+1}\), and the affine formula gives \(F(\lambda)=V\). Return the clipped allocation.

This algorithm uses \(O(\tau^2)\) exact arithmetic operations, including all breakpoint evaluations, and \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons for sorting.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

It remains to ensure that the arithmetic operands and the exact output have polynomial binary length.

Let \(Q\) be the product of the positive denominators of every input rational, including masses, bounds, and worth. Since \(L\) is the total binary input length, \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Write

\( \mu_t=\frac{a_t}{Q}, \qquad \ell_t=\frac{r_t}{Q}, \qquad u_t=\frac{s_t}{Q}, \qquad V=\frac{v}{Q}. \)

All these integer numerators have \(O(L)\) bits. Positivity and normalization additionally give

\( a_t>0, \qquad \sum_t a_t=Q. \)

A breakpoint has the form \(d/Q\), where \(d\) is one of the integers \(r_t,s_t\). Its budget value is

\( F(d/Q)=\frac{1}{Q^2} \sum_t a_t\min\{s_t,\max\{r_t,d\}\}. \)

Thus comparison with \(V\) is an integer comparison between the displayed numerator and \(vQ\). Each product has \(O(L)\) bits; summing at most \(\tau\) such products produces \(O(L+\log\tau)\)-bit integers.

For an interval solution, define

\( S=\sum_{t\in A}a_t, \qquad R=\sum_{t\in H}a_t r_t+\sum_{t\in K}a_t s_t. \)

Then \(M=S/Q\), \(B=R/Q^2\), and the exact level is

\( \lambda=\frac{vQ-R}{QS}. \)

Its numerator has \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits. Its denominator has \(O(L)\) bits because \(0<S\leq Q\). Every output coordinate is either an input bound or this rational level, so the total output length is polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

All preprocessing, sorting, evaluations, and final arithmetic therefore use polynomially many operations on polynomial-length integers. Standard exact integer arithmetic gives a polynomial-time Turing algorithm. Negative bounds and negative worth do not affect any argument.

6. Relation to the cited formula

The common-level form agrees with the finite two-bound core formula in [Gong, Dietzenbacher, and Peters, Theorem 5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00186-022-00800-1#Sec4). The proof here establishes the normalized mass case directly using the supplied bounds and gives an exact polynomial-time algorithm on the support representation.
