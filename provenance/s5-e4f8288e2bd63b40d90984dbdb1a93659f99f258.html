The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite \(X\), a domain \(D\), a choice correspondence \(c\), and a partial order \(\triangleleft\), does there exist a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(X\), supported on at most two rankings, such that every supported ranking extends \(\triangleleft\) and \(c(A) \subseteq M(\succ_\mu,A)\) for every \(A \in D\), where \(a \succ_\mu b\) exactly when \(\mu(\{t : a \succ_t b\})=1\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given an explicitly listed finite set \(X\), let \(m=|X|\). The input also lists a finite family \(D\) of nonempty subsets of \(X\), nonempty choices \(c(A)\subseteq A\) for each \(A\in D\), and a strict partial order \(\triangleleft\) on \(X\).
A type \(t\) is a strict linear order \(\succ_t\) on \(X\); it extends \(\triangleleft\) when \(a\triangleleft b\) implies \(a\succ_t b\).
A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) distinct types with positive masses, each a binary rational, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. The society is sought, rather than supplied as input.
Define \(a\succ_\mu b\) exactly when \(\mu(\{t:a\succ_t b\})=1\), and \(M(\succ_\mu,A)=\{a\in A:\nexists b\in A\text{ with }b\succ_\mu a\}\).
A feasible society has \(1\leq\tau\leq2\), every supported type extends \(\triangleleft\), and \(c(A)\subseteq M(\succ_\mu,A)\) for every \(A\in D\).
Let \(L\) be the binary input length. State decision running times in \(m,|D|,L\); for procedures receiving a represented society, also account for \(\tau\) and its binary encoding length.

Is deciding whether a feasible rational society exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For two discrete individuals, weak Pareto rationalizability subject to a common dominance partial order is NP-complete; without that restriction, every choice correspondence is weakly Pareto rationalizable ([Demuynck](https://feb.kuleuven.be/research/economics/ces/documents/DPS/2011/DPS1113.pdf)).
No separate result for this rational-distribution formulation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required every menu and its chosen subset to be nonempty, as in the discrete definitions.
  • Interpreted complete rankings as strict linear orders and the dominance relation as a strict partial order, with a triangleleft b meaning that a ranks above b.
  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and required strictly positive binary-rational masses on its distinct supported types.
  • Treated the society and its support as existentially sought, rather than as additional input; separated input length from the encoding length of a supplied society.

1. Conclusion and the theorem used

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The rational masses introduce no additional existence constraint: feasibility depends only on the supported rankings.

The known result we use is Demuynck’s Theorem 3, specialized to two individuals: given explicitly listed \((X,D,c,\triangleleft)\), with nonempty choices and a strict partial order \(\triangleleft\), deciding whether two strict linear orders extending \(\triangleleft\) weakly Pareto rationalize \(c\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Weak rationalization means that each chosen alternative is maximal for the intersection of those orders. The paper’s definitions of linear order, dominance, and weak rationalization agree with those here. [Demuynck, Section 4, Definitions 2–3 and Theorem 3](https://feb.kuleuven.be/research/economics/ces/documents/DPS/2011/DPS1113.pdf).

We prove the equivalence with the requested formulation, including the distinction between two individuals and two distinct supported types.

2. Probability-one preference depends only on support

Lemma. Let a represented society have supported types \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\) and positive masses \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\), where \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i=1\). Then
\[ \text{for every }a,b\in X,\qquad a\succ_\mu b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\succ_{t_i}b\text{ for every }i. \]

Proof. By definition,
\[ \mu(\{t:a\succ_t b\}) = \sum_{\{i:a\succ_{t_i}b\}}p_i. \]
If every supported type ranks \(a\) above \(b\), this sum is \(1\). If some supported type \(t_j\) does not, the sum is at most \(1-p_j<1\), because \(p_j>0\). This proves both implications. Consequently, \(\succ_\mu=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\tau}\succ_{t_i}\). \(\square\)

In particular, replacing positive masses by other positive masses summing to \(1\) preserves all the feasibility conditions.

3. Exact equivalence with two individuals

Let a two-individual witness be a pair of strict linear orders \((\succ_1,\succ_2)\), each extending \(\triangleleft\), such that
\[ c(A)\subseteq M(\succ_1\cap\succ_2,A) \qquad\text{for every }A\in D. \]

From two individuals to a society.

  • If \(\succ_1\ne\succ_2\), use these two orders as distinct types, each with mass \(1/2\).
  • If \(\succ_1=\succ_2\), use their common order as one type with mass \(1\).

Both constructions use binary rational masses and satisfy \(1\leq\tau\leq2\). By the lemma, their probability-one preference relation is exactly \(\succ_1\cap\succ_2\). Thus every required maximality condition holds.

From a society to two individuals.

  • If \(\tau=2\), take its two supported rankings as the individuals’ orders.
  • If \(\tau=1\), give both individuals its sole supported ranking.

Both individuals extend \(\triangleleft\). Again, their intersection equals \(\succ_\mu\), so they weakly Pareto rationalize the choices.

Therefore the two formulations have exactly the same yes-instances. The allowance of one supported type handles repeated individual rankings without violating the requirement that supported types be distinct.

4. Hardness and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Hardness. Map an instance of the two-individual problem to the identical tuple \((X,D,c,\triangleleft)\) in the society problem. This transformation takes \(O(L)\) time. Section 3 proves that it preserves both yes- and no-instances. Demuynck’s theorem therefore implies \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Membership. A certificate consists of two permutations of \(X\), possibly equal. Its binary length is \(O(m\log(m+2))\).

For each permutation, construct its rank function \(r_i\), with smaller ranks representing higher positions. Verify that it contains every element exactly once and that
\[ a\triangleleft b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_i(a)<r_i(b) \]
for both individuals.

Then, for every \(A\in D\), every \(a\in c(A)\), and every \(b\in A\setminus\{a\}\), check
\[ r_1(a)<r_1(b)\quad\text{or}\quad r_2(a)<r_2(b). \]
Because each ranking is strict and total, this condition is equivalent to saying that \(b\) is not above \(a\) in both rankings. Checking it for every such \(b\) is exactly the assertion that \(a\) is maximal for their intersection.

Acceptance therefore certifies a feasible society by Section 3. Conversely, every feasible society supplies an accepted certificate. The certificate is polynomial in the explicit input length, and verification is polynomial. Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

5. Running times and represented societies

Write \(d=|D|\) and
\[ S=\sum_{A\in D}|c(A)|\,|A|\leq dm^2. \]

After indexing \(X\), verification of the two-permutation certificate uses \(O(L+m^2+S)\) indexed elementary operations: at most \(m^2\) dominance checks and \(S\) choice comparisons. A conservative deterministic bit-time bound, including binary input handling, is
\[ O\bigl((L+(d+1)m^2+1)^3\bigr). \]
Thus nondeterministic decision takes this polynomial time. An explicit deterministic decision procedure enumerates all ordered pairs of permutations, giving the upper bound
\[ O\bigl((m!)^2(L+(d+1)m^2+1)^3\bigr). \]
A polynomial-time deterministic decision algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

For completeness, suppose a society is supplied, with support size \(\tau\) and total binary encoding length \(B\). One can:

  • Validate the rankings and compare every pair to check distinctness.
  • Check positivity of every mass and their exact sum.
  • Check extension of \(\triangleleft\) for every type.
  • Construct the unanimous-preference matrix by intersecting all supported rankings.
  • Check the choices using that matrix.

The combinatorial work uses \(O(L+B+\tau^2m+\tau m^2+S)\) indexed elementary operations. For masses encoded as numerator–denominator pairs, exact positivity and normalization can be checked in \(O((B+1)^3)\) bit operations: cumulative numerators and denominators have \(O(B)\) bits, and at most \(O(B)\) schoolbook arithmetic operations suffice. Dyadic encodings are also covered.

A conservative combined bit-time bound is therefore
\[ O\bigl((L+B+\tau^2m+\tau m^2+dm^2+1)^3\bigr). \]
For the requested feasibility test, also reject unless \(1\leq\tau\leq2\). These bounds explicitly account for both support size and mass encoding length.
