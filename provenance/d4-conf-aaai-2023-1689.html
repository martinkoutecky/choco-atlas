The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), innate opinions \(s_t\), roles \(r(t)\), and a rational nonnegative contact kernel \(K_{ru}\), let \(\nu_r=\sum_{t:r(t)=r}\mu_t\). Define \(H_{ru}=(1+\sum_v K_{rv}\nu_v)\mathbf{1}[r=u]-K_{ru}\nu_u\), and let the role equilibrium after intervention \(x\) satisfy \(Hz=\bar{s}(x)\), where \(\bar{s}_r(x)=\sum_{t:r(t)=r}(\mu_t-x_t)s_t/\nu_r\). For a population-share budget \(\alpha\), find \(x\) minimizing \(G(x)=\sum_r\nu_r z_r\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\alpha\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a binary-rational innate opinion \(s_t\in[0,1]\) and a role \(r(t)\in R=\{1,\ldots,m\}\); every role occurs. The input includes a binary-rational contact kernel \(K\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{m\times m}\) and a binary-rational population-share budget \(0<\alpha\leq1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An intervention chooses real masses \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t=\alpha\), changing the innate opinion of that mass of type \(t\) to zero. Population masses, roles, and contacts remain fixed.
Define \(\nu_r=\sum_{t:r(t)=r}\mu_t\), \(H_{ru}=(1+\sum_{v\in R}K_{rv}\nu_v)\mathbf{1}_{\{r=u\}}-K_{ru}\nu_u\), and \(\bar{s}_r(x)=\nu_r^{-1}\sum_{t:r(t)=r}(\mu_t-x_t)s_t\). The role-average equilibrium \(z\) is the unique solution of \(Hz=\bar{s}(x)\).
The optimization problem is to minimize \(G(x)=\sum_{r\in R}\nu_r z_r\) over feasible interventions; any minimizer is acceptable.

Is this continuous opinion-minimization problem solvable exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a finite unweighted directed network, selecting exactly \(k\) agents and setting their innate opinions to zero to minimize the average Friedkin–Johnsen equilibrium opinion has an exact \(O(n^3)\) algorithm, where \(n\) is the number of agents ([Sun and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16847v1)).
No result settling this exact support-encoded continuous formulation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one so that the budget is a population share and the objective is an average.
  • Required a nonempty support and defined the role set to contain only represented roles, ensuring every role mass is positive.
  • Restricted innate opinions to binary rationals in [0,1], carrying over the discrete opinion domain and making input encoding finite.
  • Set the budget domain to binary-rational 0 < alpha <= 1, matching the discrete algorithm's explicit domain 1 <= k <= n rather than imposing an undefined small-budget restriction.
  • Interpreted x_t as divisible mass whose innate opinion becomes zero, with the population and contact kernel unchanged.
  • Used m for the number of represented roles and L for total input bit length; allowed any optimum when minimizers tie.

\( \displaystyle H_{rr}=1+\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru},\qquad H_{ru}=-a_{ru}\quad(u\ne r). \)

\( \displaystyle (Hy)_r=y_r+\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}(y_r-y_u). \)

To prove invertibility, suppose \(Hy=0\) and choose \(r\) with \(|y_r|=\max_u|y_u|\). The row equation implies
\( \displaystyle \left(1+\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}\right)|y_r|\leq\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}|y_u|\leq\left(\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}\right)|y_r|. \)
Consequently \(y_r=0\), and hence \(y=0\). Therefore \(H\) is invertible.

We also have \(H^{-1}\geq0\) entrywise. Indeed, let \(Hy=b\) with \(b\geq0\). If a minimum coordinate \(y_r\) were negative, then
\( \displaystyle b_r=y_r+\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}(y_r-y_u)\leq y_r<0, \)
a contradiction. Applying this argument to each coordinate vector \(b\) proves the assertion.

Moreover, \((H^{-1})_{rr}>0\). For \(y=H^{-1}e_r\), we already know \(y\geq0\). If \(y_r=0\), its \(r\)-th equation would give
\( \displaystyle 1=(Hy)_r=-\sum_{u\ne r}a_{ru}y_u\leq0, \)
which is impossible.

These arguments require neither symmetry nor connectivity of the contact kernel.

2. Exact linearization of the objective.

Write \(\nu=(\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_m)^{\mathsf T}\), and compute the unique rational vector \(w\) satisfying
\( \displaystyle H^{\mathsf T}w=\nu. \)

Because \(w=H^{-\mathsf T}\nu\), the preceding positivity result implies
\( \displaystyle w_r=\sum_u(H^{-1})_{ur}\nu_u\geq(H^{-1})_{rr}\nu_r>0. \)

For every feasible intervention,
\( \displaystyle G(x)=\nu^{\mathsf T}H^{-1}\bar s(x)=w^{\mathsf T}\bar s(x) =\sum_{t\in T}\frac{w_{r(t)}}{\nu_{r(t)}}(\mu_t-x_t)s_t. \)

Define the rational benefit per unit of intervention mass by
\( \displaystyle c_t=\frac{w_{r(t)}}{\nu_{r(t)}}s_t. \)
Then
\( \displaystyle G(x)=G(0)-\sum_{t\in T}c_tx_t,\qquad G(0)=\sum_{t\in T}c_t\mu_t. \)

Here \(G(0)\) denotes the objective without intervention; it is a constant even when the zero intervention is infeasible for the specified budget. Thus the original minimization is exactly equivalent to
\( \displaystyle \max\left\{\sum_{t\in T}c_tx_t:\ 0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t,\ \sum_{t\in T}x_t=\alpha\right\}. \)

Crucially, every coefficient \(c_t\) is independent of \(x\), since population masses and contacts remain fixed.

3. An optimal intervention and its proof.

\( \displaystyle c_{t_1}\geq c_{t_2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{t_\tau}, \)
breaking ties arbitrarily.

Let
\( \displaystyle M_0=0,\qquad M_i=\sum_{h=1}^i\mu_{t_h}. \)

\( \displaystyle x^*_{t_i}= \begin{cases} \mu_{t_i},&i<j,\\ \alpha-M_{j-1},&i=j,\\ 0,&i>j. \end{cases} \)

Minimality of \(j\) gives \(M_{j-1}<\alpha\leq M_j\), so
\( \displaystyle 0<x^*_{t_j}\leq\mu_{t_j}. \)
All capacity constraints hold, and the coordinates sum to \(\alpha\). Hence \(x^*\) is feasible.

For optimality, take any feasible \(x\) and put \(\lambda=c_{t_j}\). Equal total intervention masses imply
\( \displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}c_{t_i}(x^*_{t_i}-x_{t_i}) = \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(c_{t_i}-\lambda)(x^*_{t_i}-x_{t_i}). \)

  • If \(i=j\), the coefficient is zero.
  • If \(i>j\), then \(c_{t_i}-\lambda\leq0\) and \(x^*_{t_i}-x_{t_i}=-x_{t_i}\leq0\).

\( \displaystyle \sum_t c_tx^*_t\geq\sum_t c_tx_t, \)

4. Polynomial bit complexity.

An arithmetic-operation count alone would not establish exact polynomial-time solvability. We therefore bound the sizes of the rational numbers used.

Represent input rationals by binary numerators and positive denominators. Let \(D\) be the product of all input denominators. Because their encodings are included in \(L\),
\( \displaystyle \log_2D\leq L. \)
Every input denominator divides \(D\). Hence every \(\nu_r\) has denominator dividing \(D\), and every entry of \(H\) has denominator dividing \(D^2\).

Consequently the system for \(w\) is equivalent to the integer system
\( \displaystyle Aw=b,\qquad A=D^2H^{\mathsf T},\qquad b=D^2\nu. \)

Every input kernel entry is at most \(2^L\). Using \(\sum_r\nu_r=1\), we obtain
\( \displaystyle |H_{ru}|\leq1+2^L,\qquad 0<b_r\leq D^2. \)
Thus all entries of \(A\) and \(b\) have absolute value at most
\( \displaystyle U=2^{3L+2}. \)

For any \(k\)-by-\(k\) integer matrix with entries bounded by \(U\), Hadamard’s determinant inequality gives
\( \displaystyle |\det B|\leq(\sqrt{k}\,U)^k. \)
Its hypotheses hold because each row has Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt{k}\,U\). Every determinant formed from at most \(m\) rows and columns of the augmented matrix therefore has bit length
\( \displaystyle O\bigl(m(L+\log m)\bigr). \)

Cramer’s rule now shows that each coordinate of \(w\), in reduced rational form, has polynomial bit length: both its numerator determinant and its nonzero denominator determinant obey this bound.

For completeness, \(w\) can also be *computed* using only polynomial-size intermediate values. Every principal submatrix of \(H\) remains strictly row diagonally dominant: deleting indices only decreases its off-diagonal absolute row sums. The invertibility argument in Section 1 applies to each such submatrix. Therefore every leading principal submatrix of \(A\) is invertible, and Gaussian elimination needs no pivot exchanges.

After eliminating an initial index set \(I=\{1,\ldots,k\}\), a remaining coefficient is the Schur-complement entry
\( \displaystyle A_{ij}-A_{iI}A_{II}^{-1}A_{Ij} = \frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}A_{II}&A_{Ij}\\A_{iI}&A_{ij}\end{pmatrix}} {\det A_{II}}. \)
The equality follows by block elimination of the displayed matrix. The corresponding right-hand-side entry has the same formula with the last column replaced by \(b\).

Thus every reduced intermediate coefficient and right-hand-side entry has polynomial bit length by the determinant bound. Each elimination update uses a fixed number of rational arithmetic operations; reducing fractions after each update keeps their encodings polynomial. Back substitution also has polynomial bit complexity: the solution coordinates have the Cramer bound, and each partial sum contains at most \(m\) products of polynomial-size rationals. There are \(O(m^3)\) arithmetic operations, and integer arithmetic and greatest-common-divisor computations on polynomial-size integers take polynomial time.

  • Sorting uses \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) exact rational comparisons, implemented by integer cross multiplication.
  • Prefix sums and the split mass \(\alpha-M_{j-1}\) have denominators dividing \(D\). The output therefore has polynomial encoding length.

The entire algorithm runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), with no expansion of the support into individual agents.
