The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, a committee size \(k\), two candidate groups \(P_1,P_2\) with each candidate in at most one group, integer bounds \(\ell_i,u_i\), and a feasible committee, let \(T\) be a finite support of complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For a size-\(k\) committee \(S\), define \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr)\). Can a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) always find a feasible \(S\) with \(W_\mu(S)\ge(1-1/e+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\), for fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(L\) is the mass encoding length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input consists of a candidate set \(C\) with \(|C|=m\ge1\), an integer \(1\le k\le m\), disjoint groups \(P_1,P_2\subseteq C\), and binary-encoded integer bounds \(0\le\ell_i\le u_i\le |P_i|\). Candidates may belong to neither group; zero bounds are allowed.
A committee \(S\subseteq C\) is feasible when \(|S|=k\) and \(\ell_i\le |S\cap P_i|\le u_i\) for \(i\in\{1,2\}\). A feasible committee is supplied as part of the input.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct complete strict rankings with positive masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}\), each encoded in binary, satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total mass encoding length.
Let \(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) be the position of \(c\) in ranking \(t\), with the favorite first. Define \(W_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}=\max_{S\text{ feasible}}W_\mu(S)\).

For each fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1/e\), is there an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\) that always returns a feasible \(S\) with \(W_\mu(S)\ge(1-1/e+\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}\), or is achieving this approximation ratio NP-hard?

For finite unweighted electorates, this constrained Borda–Chamberlin–Courant problem admits a randomized polynomial-time \((1-1/e)\) approximation in expectation, while a polynomial-time \((1-1/e+\varepsilon)\) approximation is excluded unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), even with two disjoint groups. Result

For every fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1/e\), achieving approximation ratio \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard in the stated binary-rational support model. Thus no polynomial-time algorithm with the requested guarantee exists unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof is an exact, polynomial-size transfer from the finite unweighted electorate model.

1. The finite-electorate hardness theorem

We use the hardness result supplied in the question: for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), finite unweighted constrained Borda–Chamberlin–Courant admits no polynomial-time \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) approximation unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), even with two disjoint groups.

The required restrictions hold in its hardness construction: rankings are complete and strict, one group contains the selectable candidates, and the second contains forbidden candidates, with bounds \(\ell_1=u_1=k\) and \(\ell_2=u_2=0\). Selecting any \(k\) candidates from the first group supplies a feasible committee. See [Celis–Huang–Vishnoi, Theorem 19 and Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.10057v2).

Thus the source hardness applies to instances satisfying the target’s bounds and feasibility requirements.

2. Compressing the electorate

Consider such an unweighted instance with \(n\ge1\) explicitly listed voters and rankings \(r_1,\ldots,r_n\). Its objective is

\(F(S)=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_{r_v}(c)\bigr).\)

Let \(T\) be the set of distinct rankings occurring in this list. For each \(t\in T\), define its multiplicity and mass by

\(a_t=|\{v:r_v=t\}|,\qquad \mu_t=a_t/n.\)

Every \(a_t\) is positive, and \(\sum_{t\in T}a_t=n\). Consequently, all masses are positive rationals and sum to one, as required.

Keep the candidates, committee size, groups, bounds, and supplied feasible committee unchanged. The feasible committees are therefore exactly the same in the two instances.

For every feasible committee \(S\), grouping identical summands gives

\(W_\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\frac{a_t}{n}\bigl(m-\min_{c\in S}\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr)=F(S)/n.\)

Writing \(\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}=\max_{S\text{ feasible}}F(S)\), we obtain

\(\operatorname{OPT}=\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}/n.\)

Thus normalization preserves every multiplicative approximation guarantee exactly.

3. Encoding length and running time

Identical rankings can be grouped by sorting their explicitly encoded candidate sequences. This takes polynomial time in the finite input length.

The support size satisfies \(\tau\le n\). Each mass \(a_t/n\) has numerator and denominator at most \(n\), so its binary encoding length is \(O(\log(n+1))\). Reducing the fraction to lowest terms, if required, cannot increase this bound. Hence

\(L=O(\tau\log(n+1))=O(n\log(n+1)).\)

The output rankings, masses, and unchanged election data therefore have polynomial total encoding length. In particular, any running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\) becomes polynomial in the explicitly represented finite-electorate input length.

This argument compresses a finite electorate into a support representation; it never expands binary rational masses into voters.

4. Transferring an alleged approximation algorithm

Fix \(0<\varepsilon<1/e\), and put \(\alpha=1-1/e+\varepsilon\). Suppose an algorithm with the requested running time always returned a feasible committee satisfying

\(W_\mu(S)\ge\alpha\operatorname{OPT}.\)

Apply it to the compressed instance above. Its output is also feasible for the original instance, and the exact score identities imply

\(F(S)=nW_\mu(S)\ge n\alpha\operatorname{OPT}=\alpha\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}.\)

Together with the polynomial encoding bound, this would give a polynomial-time \(\alpha\)-approximation for the finite unweighted hard instances, contradicting the stated hardness theorem unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The transfer already establishes hardness for masses sharing a common denominator \(n\), where \(n\) is the number of explicitly listed voters in the source instance. Allowing arbitrary binary rational masses cannot remove this hard subclass.
