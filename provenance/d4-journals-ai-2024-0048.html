The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite outcome set \(O\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) of support size \(\tau\) over matched pairs of strict rankings, compute a succinct equilibrium-strategy prescription for each supported pair and the exact mass \(\nu_o=\sum_{\theta:E(\theta)=o}\mu_\theta\) ending at each \(o\in O\), where each pair describes an independent full-information VAOV case.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(O\) be a nonempty finite outcome set, with \(m=|O|\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) ordered pairs \(\theta=(\succ_1,\succ_2)\) of strict rankings of \(O\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_\theta\), where \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible ranking pairs; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For each supported pair, independently, two parties know both rankings and play the following VAOV game. Party \(1\) offers first; the parties alternate offering an available outcome. Acceptance ends the game with that outcome; rejection permanently removes it and makes the rejecting party the next proposer. The sole remaining outcome is accepted automatically. Lotteries are inadmissible, and both parties prefer every agreement to disagreement; preferences depend only on the final outcome.
Compute, for each supported pair, a pure subgame-perfect equilibrium strategy profile, represented by a polynomial-length algorithmic prescription that returns the prescribed offer or response at every legal history in polynomial time. Also compute every exact rational mass \(\nu_o=\sum_{\theta:E(\theta)=o}\mu_\theta\), where \(E(\theta)\) is the outcome induced by the returned profile, including zero masses.

Is this computation solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a single full-information VAOV instance, explicit strategies form a subgame-perfect equilibrium, and the prescribed action at a given history can be computed in time linear in \(m\); the equilibrium outcome is unique. These are individual-instance results, without an explicit support-input mass-computation statement. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00913v1).

Result

The computation is polynomial-time solvable. The individual-instance equilibrium theorem supplies compact strategy prescriptions; explicit support permits computing the outcome separately for each supported pair and summing its mass exactly.

1. A compact equilibrium prescription

Fix an ordering of the outcome labels to make every choice deterministic. For a supported pair, store both rankings.

At a nonterminal offering history, let \(S\) be the remaining outcomes, let \(p\) be the proposer, and let \(q=3-p\). Define \(B_p\) and \(B_q\) as the lowest-ranked \(\lfloor(|S|-1)/2\rfloor\) and \(\lfloor|S|/2\rfloor\) outcomes in their respective rankings restricted to \(S\).

Define \(P(S,p)\) to select the first labeled outcome in \(B_p\cap B_q\) when this intersection is nonempty, and otherwise the outcome worst for \(q\) in \(S\).

For nonempty \(S\), define \(F(S,p)\) by repeatedly deleting \(P(S,p)\) and switching proposers until one outcome remains; return that outcome.

Prescribe the offer \(P(S,p)\). On receiving an offer \(x\), prescribe acceptance exactly when
\(x\succ_q F(S\setminus\{x\},q)\).
With one remaining outcome, prescribe automatic acceptance.

These are the cited paper’s Strategies 1 and 2, with deterministic selection within the intersection. Its Theorem 10 states that they constitute a pure subgame-perfect equilibrium. [Erlich, Hazon, and Kraus, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00913v1).

2. Applicability and representation

The theorem’s hypotheses hold for every supported pair:

  • The available outcome set is finite and nonempty.
  • Both rankings are strict and fully known to both parties.
  • Offers alternate, and rejection permanently removes the offered outcome.
  • Lotteries are excluded, and every agreement is preferred to disagreement.
  • The final available outcome is accepted automatically.

The definition of \(F\) uses hypothetical forced rejections to evaluate a response. Actual play follows the acceptance rule above.

Every legal nonterminal history determines the available set and the proposer: remove all previously rejected outcomes from \(O\), and use the parity of their number to identify whose turn it is. At a response history, also read the pending offer. These operations take polynomial time because there are at most \(m-1\) rejected offers.

Thus, for each supported pair, the output can consist of its two ranking arrays and the fixed program implementing the prescription. The program specifies actions at every legal history, including histories reached after deviations. Subgame perfection follows from the cited theorem, rather than merely from correctness along the initial play path.

After relabeling outcomes by integers, the ranking data occupy \(O(m\log(m+1))\) bits per supported pair. The total strategy representation therefore has polynomial length.

3. Computing each induced outcome

We only need a polynomial implementation; the sharper linear-time individual-instance result is unnecessary.

By scanning the stored rankings, one can compute \(P(S,p)\) in \(O(m)\) elementary operations. Evaluating \(F(S,p)\) makes at most \(m-1\) deletions, so a straightforward implementation takes \(O(m^2)\) operations. Consequently, every prescribed action can be evaluated in polynomial time.

For each supported pair \(\theta\), simulate the returned profile from the initial history. Each round either terminates with acceptance or removes one outcome. Hence there are at most \(m\) rounds. Using the straightforward evaluator, this simulation takes \(O(m^3)\) elementary operations per pair, and \(O(\tau m^3)\) overall.

Let its terminal outcome be \(E(\theta)\). By construction, this is precisely the outcome induced by the returned strategy profile. For \(m=1\), the unique outcome is accepted automatically.

4. Exact masses and bit complexity

Index the support by \(j\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), and write each input mass as
\(\mu_j=a_j/b_j\), where \(a_j,b_j\) are positive binary integers. Reduction to lowest terms is unnecessary.

Form the common denominator
\(Q=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau}b_j\).
For every \(o\in O\), initialize \(N_o=0\). After computing \(E(\theta_j)\), add
\(a_j(Q/b_j)\)
to \(N_{E(\theta_j)}\). Output
\(\nu_o=N_o/Q\)
for every outcome, using \(0/1\) when \(N_o=0\). Fractions may optionally be reduced by greatest common divisors.

The division \(Q/b_j\) is exact. Moreover, the resulting mass is correct because
\(\displaystyle \frac{N_o}{Q} =\sum_{\{j:E(\theta_j)=o\}}\frac{a_j}{b_j} =\nu_o\).

It remains to bound the arithmetic size. Since denominator encodings are part of the input,
\(\log_2 Q=\sum_j\log_2 b_j\le L\).
Thus \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

Normalization gives
\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{\tau}a_j(Q/b_j)=Q\).
All summands are positive integers. Therefore every partial accumulator satisfies
\(0\le N_o\le Q\),
so its bit length is also \(O(L)\). Integer multiplication, exact division, addition, and optional fraction reduction all take polynomial time on these operands.

The complete mass output has \(O(m(L+1))\) bits, including outcomes of zero mass.

5. Conclusion

The strategy representations, their action evaluators, the simulations for all \(\tau\) supported pairs, and the exact rational aggregation all have polynomial cost in \(m,\tau,L\). No enumeration of unsupported ranking pairs is required.
