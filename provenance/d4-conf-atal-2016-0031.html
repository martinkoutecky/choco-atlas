1. Normalization and the optimal partition

Normalization

Let \(q=q_\mu\), and let \(e\) be the endpoint selected by MedianFurthest. The endpoint \(e\) differs from \(q\), since the support contains at least three points.

Apply the rational affine transformation \(x\mapsto(x-q)/(e-q)\), keeping masses unchanged. Both costs scale by the same positive factor, so the approximation ratio is preserved.

In the transformed society:

  • the median facility is at \(0\);
  • the selected endpoint is at \(1\);
  • the opposite endpoint is at \(-A\), where \(0\le A\le1\);
  • MedianFurthest uses \(\{0,1\}\).

Write
\(U=\sum_{x<0}\mu_x\), \(v=\mu_0\), \(V=\sum_{0<x<1}\mu_x\), and \(w=\mu_1\).
Then
\(U+v+V+w=1\), \(U\le1/2\), \(V+w\le1/2\), and \(v,w\ge\delta\).

Reflection can turn the lower median into the upper median, but the two weak mass inequalities remain valid. Moreover, the prescribed tie rules imply

\(\boxed{U<1/2\ \text{or}\ A<1.}\)

Indeed, if the original selected endpoint was the right endpoint, the original lower-median definition gives \(U<1/2\). If it was the left endpoint, that endpoint was selected only on a strict distance inequality, giving \(A<1\).

Let \(N=C_\mu(\{0,1\})\). Directly from the normalized locations,

\[ \text{(written with the requested inline delimiters)} \]

\(N=\sum_{x<0}\mu_x(-x)+\sum_{0<x<1}\mu_x\min\{x,1-x\}\le AU+V/2.\)

Contiguous optimal partitions

For a nonempty subset \(B\) of the support, define
\(D(B)=\min_{t\in\mathbb R}\sum_{x\in B}\mu_x|x-t|\).

A weighted median minimizes this expression. To verify this, between consecutive support points its slope is twice the cumulative mass to the left minus the total mass of \(B\). These slopes are nondecreasing; at a weighted median the left slope is nonpositive and the right slope is nonnegative.

If the support is ordered as \(x_1<\cdots<x_\tau\), then

\(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{1\le j<\tau}\bigl(D(\{x_1,\ldots,x_j\})+D(\{x_{j+1},\ldots,x_\tau\})\bigr).\)

For proof, nearest-facility assignments to two ordered facilities can be chosen contiguous. If one facility receives no points, splitting the occupied group into two nonempty consecutive groups cannot increase the optimized cost. This proves the lower bound by the displayed minimum. Conversely, placing facilities at weighted medians of any two consecutive groups gives nearest-facility cost no larger than their assigned cost, proving the reverse inequality.

Choose a minimizing partition into a prefix \(P\) and a suffix \(Q\), and write \(D_P=D(P)\), \(D_Q=D(Q)\). Thus \(D_P+D_Q=\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\).

2. Upper bounds

Define

\(r_3(\delta)=\dfrac{\min\{1/2,\,1-2\delta\}}{\delta}.\)

Because \(0<\delta\le1/3\), we have \(r_3(\delta)\ge1\).

We distinguish which optimal group contains the median point \(0\).

Case I: \(0\in P\)

Let \(H\) be the mass of points of \(P\) at or to the right of \(0\). Then \(H\ge v\ge\delta\), and all negative points belong to \(P\).

Choose the lower weighted median \(p\) of \(P\). Since the cumulative mass through \(0\) is at least \(1/2\), while half the mass of \(P\) is at most \(1/2\), we have \(p\le0\).

Put \(h=-p\), and decompose its assigned cost as \(D_P=D_-+D_+\), according to negative and nonnegative points. Then \(D_+\ge Hh\). The triangle inequality on negative points, and exact subtraction on nonnegative points, give

\(\sum_{x\in P}\mu_x|x|\le D_-+D_++(U-H)h.\)

If \(U\le H\), this is at most \(D_P\). If \(U>H\), using \(h\le D_+/H\) gives

\(\sum_{x\in P}\mu_x|x|\le D_-+(U/H)D_+\le(U/H)D_P.\)

Consequently, the mechanism’s cost on \(P\) is at most
\(\max\{1,U/H\}D_P\).

The distinct points \(0\) and \(1\) each have mass at least \(\delta\), so
\(U\le\min\{1/2,1-2\delta\}\). Therefore this factor is at most \(r_3(\delta)\).

For \(Q\), let \(K\) be its mass excluding the endpoint \(1\), and choose a weighted median \(r\le1\). With \(h'=1-r\), the same triangle-inequality argument gives

\(\sum_{x\in Q}\mu_x(1-x)\le\max\{1,K/w\}D_Q.\)

Here \(K\le1/2-w\le1/2-\delta\), and also \(K\le1-v-w\le1-2\delta\). Hence \(\max\{1,K/w\}\le r_3(\delta)\). The mechanism’s cost on \(Q\) is no larger than its cost using endpoint \(1\) alone.

Adding the bounds proves

\(\boxed{N\le r_3(\delta)\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu).}\)

Case II: \(0\in Q\)

The suffix \(Q\) contains both \(0\) and \(1\). For any center \(r\),

\(\sum_{x\in Q}\mu_x|x-r|\ge v|r|+w|1-r|\ge\delta\bigl(|r|+|1-r|\bigr)\ge\delta.\)

Thus \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\ge\delta\).

If \(V=0\), then \(N\le AU\le U\le\min\{1/2,1-2\delta\}\), yielding the bound \(r_3(\delta)\).

Suppose \(V>0\). The prefix contains a negative point, and there are also the distinct points \(0\), an interior positive point, and \(1\). Therefore \(\tau\ge4\), \(\delta\le1/4\), and \(V\ge\delta\). It follows that

\(U\le\min\{1/2,1-3\delta\}.\)

Using the normalization bound,

\(N\le U+V/2=(1-v-w+U)/2\le1/2-\delta+\tfrac12\min\{1/2,1-3\delta\}.\)

Define

\(r_4(\delta)=\dfrac{1/2-\delta+\tfrac12\min\{1/2,1-3\delta\}}{\delta}.\)

We have proved the universal upper bounds:

  • if \(T=3\), or if \(\delta>1/4\), the ratio is at most \(r_3(\delta)\);
  • otherwise, it is at most \(\max\{r_3(\delta),r_4(\delta)\}\).

The simplifications are

\(r_4(\delta)=3/(4\delta)-1\) for \(0<\delta\le1/6\),

and

\(r_4(\delta)=1/\delta-5/2\) for \(1/6\le\delta\le1/4\).

On the latter interval,
\(r_4(\delta)-r_3(\delta)=1/(2\delta)-5/2\), which changes sign at \(\delta=1/5\). These calculations give exactly the upper bounds in the opening tables.

3. Sharp rational societies

Three points attaining \(1/(2\delta)\)

For \(0<\delta\le1/4\), choose any rational \(t\) with \(1/2<t<1\), and take

| Location | Mass |

The two possible partition costs are \(\delta t\) and \(\delta(1-t)\). Hence \(\mathrm{OPT}=\delta(1-t)\), and the ratio is exactly \(1/(2\delta)\).

Three points attaining \(1/\delta-2\)

\((1-2\delta,\delta,\delta)\)

\((1-2\delta)/\delta=1/\delta-2.\)

Whenever the support contains \(-1,0,1\), each with mass at least \(\delta\), every two-facility solution costs at least \(\delta\).

Indeed, assign these three points to nearest facilities. Two must share a facility. Their separation is at least \(1\), so the sum of their distances to that facility is at least \(1\). Their weighted contribution is therefore at least \(\delta\).

The mechanism’s cost is \(1-3\delta+\delta/2=1-5\delta/2\). Facilities at \(-1\) and \(1/2\) cost exactly \(\delta\). The three-anchor lower bound proves this is optimal. Thus the ratio is \(1/\delta-5/2\).

At \(\delta=1/5\), this society and the first three-point family both attain \(5/2\).

Four points approaching \(3/(4\delta)-1\)

| Location | Mass |
|---|---:|

Its cost is \(3/4-\delta-\varepsilon_k/2\). Facilities at \(-1\) and \(1/2\) cost exactly \(\delta\), and the three-anchor bound again proves optimality. Therefore the ratio is

\(\dfrac{3}{4\delta}-1-\dfrac{1}{2k}\),

which approaches the claimed supremum.

4. Non-attainment and all equality cases

Non-attainment for \(T\ge4\) and \(0<\delta\le1/6\)

In this range, \(r_4(\delta)>r_3(\delta)\), so equality could only arise in Case II with \(V>0\).

That case has \(U>0\). If \(U<1/2\), then

\(N\le(1-v-w+U)/2<3/4-\delta.\)

If \(U=1/2\), the normalization’s strictness property forces \(A<1\), and consequently

Since \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge\delta\), the ratio is always strictly below \(3/(4\delta)-1\). The approaching sequence above therefore establishes a non-attained supremum.

Distinctness of support therefore forces exactly the four listed points. The tie rule requires the original selected endpoint to be the right endpoint.

For \(T\ge4\) and \(1/5<\delta\le1/4\), the only attaining societies are the first three-point family.

Here \(r_4(\delta)<r_3(\delta)\), so the Case I equality argument applies.

For every \(T\ge3\) and \(1/4<\delta<1/3\), an attaining society has exactly three points. Its endpoint opposite the selected endpoint has mass \(1-2\delta\); the median and selected endpoint each have mass \(\delta\). These conditions are also sufficient.

For completeness, let the three masses be \(u,v,w\), and the two gaps be \(\ell,r\). All masses are strictly below \(1/2\), so the middle point is the median. If \(\ell\le r\), the ratio is

\(\max\left\{\dfrac{u}{\min\{u,v\}},\dfrac{u\ell}{\min\{v,w\}r}\right\}.\)

Each term is at most \((1-2\delta)/\delta\). Since this bound exceeds \(1\), equality forces \(u=1-2\delta\), and then \(v=w=\delta\). If \(\ell>r\), the symmetric calculation forces \(w=1-2\delta\). Conversely, either configuration attains the bound.

At \(\delta=1/3\), every admissible society attains ratio \(1\). There are exactly three support points, all with mass \(1/3\); the mechanism and optimum both incur one-third of the smaller adjacent gap.

5. Exact computation and running time

For the stipulated \(m=2\), MedianFurthest and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) can both be evaluated in
\(O(\tau\log\tau\,\mathrm{poly}(L))\) bit operations, and therefore in
\(O(T\log T\,\mathrm{poly}(L))\).

To obtain this bound, sort the support and compute prefix sums of masses and mass-weighted locations. For each of the \(\tau-1\) splits, binary search the weighted median of its prefix and suffix. Their single-facility costs then follow from the prefix sums using a constant number of rational operations. Taking the minimum gives \(\mathrm{OPT}\) by the partition identity proved above.

This uses \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) rational operations on numbers of \(O(L)\) bits. It does not expand rational masses into a list of agents.

Evaluating the closed-form worst-case ratio requires only a constant number of rational comparisons and arithmetic operations, together with testing whether \(T=3\) or \(T\ge4\).
