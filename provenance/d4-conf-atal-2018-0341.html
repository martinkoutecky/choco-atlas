The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given core and periphery masses \(\mu_C,\mu_P\) with \(\mu_C+\mu_P=1\), per-capita degree-volume weights \(d_C,d_P\), a rational \(q\in[0,1]\) for the periphery’s probability of sampling the frozen-blue core on each draw, a target \(\kappa\), and a horizon \(H\), initialize the core blue and the periphery red. Let \(x_t\) be the blue fraction of the periphery, \(y_t=q+(1-q)x_t\), and \(x_{t+1}=(1-x_t)y_t^2+x_t(1-(1-y_t)^2)\). Decide whether, for some \(t\le H\), the degree-volume-weighted blue share \((\mu_Cd_C+\mu_Pd_Px_t)/(\mu_Cd_C+\mu_Pd_P)\) is at least \(1-\kappa\).

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here the types are core and periphery, \(\tau=2\), and there are \(m=2\) colors.
The input consists of positive rational masses \(\mu_C+\mu_P=1\), positive rational degree-volume weights \(d_C,d_P\), rationals \(q,\kappa\in[0,1]\), and a nonnegative integer horizon \(H\) encoded in binary. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The core stays blue forever. Initially the periphery is red. In each synchronous round, each periphery draw independently samples the core with probability \(q\), and otherwise samples the periphery uniformly by mass; two matching sampled colors replace the current color, while differing colors leave it unchanged.
The deterministic mass evolution is defined exactly by \(x_0=0\), \(y_t=q+(1-q)x_t\), and \(x_{t+1}=(1-x_t)y_t^2+x_t(1-(1-y_t)^2)\), where \(x_t\) is the blue fraction of the periphery.

I do not obtain a classification of the unrestricted problem as belonging to $\mathrm P$ or being $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.

The main new result is a polynomial-time computable estimate of the first crossing time whose error is only $O(L)$ rounds, even when that crossing time is exponentially large. Consequently, for each choice of the non-horizon inputs, all but $O(L)$ integer horizons can be decided in polynomial time.

For the fixed target $x=1/2$ near the critical bias, the estimate can be sharpened to a constant error:

$$ \left| T(q)-\frac{\pi(3-q)}{\sqrt{6q-q^2-1}} \right|\leq128, \qquad 3-2\sqrt2<q\leq\frac15. $$

These results substantially narrow the remaining comparison problem, but do not decide the exceptional horizons.

1. Scalar reduction and exact reachability conditions

Put

$$ A=\mu_Cd_C,\qquad B=\mu_Pd_P. $$

Both numbers are positive. The required volume inequality is equivalent to

$$ x_t\geq r, \qquad r=1-\kappa\frac{A+B}{B}. $$

The rational number $r$ has bit length $O(L)$: its construction uses a fixed number of rational arithmetic operations.

Because $\kappa\geq0$, we have $r\leq1$. If $r\leq0$, time $0$ satisfies the condition. Henceforth suppose $r>0$.

Write $a=1-q$ and denote the update map by $F_q$. Direct expansion gives

$$ F_q(x)-x=(1-x)h_q(x), $$

where

$$ h_q(x)=2a^2x^2+a(3q-1)x+q^2. $$

Equivalently, for $z=1-x$,

$$ 1-F_q(x)=az^2(2+a-2az). $$

The endpoint cases are immediate:

  • If $H=0$, the answer is no when $r>0$.
  • If $q=0$, every iterate is $0$.
  • If $q=1$, every iterate from time $1$ onward is $1$.

Suppose now that $0<q<1$. The original recurrence expresses $F_q(x)$ as a convex combination of $y^2$ and $1-(1-y)^2$, with $y=q+(1-q)x\in[0,1]$. Thus $F_q$ maps $[0,1]$ into itself. Moreover,

$$ F_q'(x)=2a(1-x)\bigl(2+a-3a(1-x)\bigr)>0 \qquad(0\leq x<1), $$

because the expression in parentheses is at least $2-2a=2q>0$.

Since $x_1=q^2>x_0$, applying strict monotonicity inductively proves

$$ 0=x_0<x_1<x_2<\cdots<1. $$

The last strict inequality follows also from the displayed formula for $1-F_q(x)$, which is positive when $x<1$.

Therefore the bounded-horizon question is exactly whether $x_H\geq r$. In particular, $r=1$ is unreachable at every finite time when $0<q<1$.

Define

$$ D(q)=q^2-6q+1, \qquad q_\star=3-2\sqrt2. $$

The discriminant of $h_q$ is $a^2D(q)$. Since $q_\star$ is irrational, rational input cannot satisfy $q=q_\star$.

If $0<q<q_\star$, the roots of $h_q$ are

$$ \alpha= \frac{1-3q-\sqrt{D(q)}}{4(1-q)}, \qquad \beta= \frac{1-3q+\sqrt{D(q)}}{4(1-q)}. $$

They satisfy $0<\alpha<\beta<1$. Indeed, $q_\star<1/3$, and

$$ (1-3q)^2-D(q)=8q^2>0 $$

proves positivity of both numerators. Also $D(q)<1$, so the numerator defining $\beta$ is less than $2$, whereas its denominator exceeds $8/3$.

Because $F_q(\alpha)=\alpha$ and $F_q$ is strictly increasing, every finite iterate lies strictly below $\alpha$. The increasing orbit has a limit $\ell\leq\alpha$. Continuity makes $\ell$ a fixed point; the only fixed points are $\alpha,\beta,1$, so $\ell=\alpha$.

If $q_\star<q<1$, then $D(q)<0$, hence $h_q$ is positive everywhere. The only fixed point in $[0,1]$ is $1$, so the same limit argument gives $x_t\to1$.

Thus, for $0<q<1$ and $0<r<1$,

$$ r\text{ is eventually reached} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} r<\alpha,&q<q_\star,\\ \text{always},&q>q_\star. \end{cases} $$

These conditions are polynomial-time decidable. The sign of $D(q)$ identifies the regime. In the subcritical regime, putting

$$ W=1-3q-4(1-q)r $$

gives the exact equivalence

$$ r<\alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W>0\ \text{and}\ W^2>D(q). $$

From now on, assume these tests establish that $0<r<1$ is reachable. Define its first crossing time by

$$ T=\min\{t\geq0:x_t\geq r\}. $$

Then $T$ exists, $T\geq1$, and the original answer is yes exactly when $H\geq T$.

2. A discrete-time estimate from an integral

Define the positive one-round increase on the interval $[0,r]$ by

$$ v(x)=F_q(x)-x=(1-x)h_q(x). $$

It is positive there: in the subcritical case $r<\alpha$, and in the supercritical case $h_q$ is everywhere positive.

Consider

$$ J(r)=\int_0^r\frac{dx}{v(x)}. $$

The following lemma controls its discrepancy from the integer crossing time.

Lemma 1: integral versus crossing time

Let $F$ be a continuously differentiable, nondecreasing map, and let $v(x)=F(x)-x>0$ on $[0,r]$. Suppose the orbit from $x_0=0$ reaches $r$, with first crossing time $T$. Then

$$ |T-J(r)| \leq 1+\int_0^r\frac{|v'(x)|}{v(x)}\,dx. $$

Proof

Consider an orbit interval starting at $a=x_t<r$, and truncate its endpoint at

$$ b=\min\{F(a),r\}. $$

Integration by parts gives

$$ \int_a^b\frac{dx}{v(x)} = \frac{b-a}{v(a)} - \int_a^b \frac{b-s}{v(s)} \frac{v'(s)}{v(s)}\,ds. $$

For $s\in[a,b]$, monotonicity of $F$ implies

$$ F(s)\geq F(a)\geq b. $$

Consequently,

$$ v(s)=F(s)-s\geq b-s, $$

and therefore

$$ 0\leq\frac{b-s}{v(s)}\leq1. $$

It follows that

$$ \left| \int_a^b\frac{dx}{v(x)} -\frac{b-a}{v(a)} \right| \leq \int_a^b\frac{|v'(s)|}{v(s)}\,ds. $$

There are $T-1$ complete orbit intervals before the last interval; each complete interval has $(b-a)/v(a)=1$. On the last interval this ratio is some $\theta\in(0,1]$. Summing over the partition of $[0,r]$ yields

$$ J(r)=T-1+\theta+E, \qquad |E|\leq\int_0^r\frac{|v'(x)|}{v(x)}\,dx. $$

Since $0\leq1-\theta<1$, the claimed bound follows. $\square$

3. A uniform $O(L)$ error bound

Write the reduced fractions as

$$ q=\frac uv,\qquad r=\frac bc, $$

with positive denominators. In this section, the integer $v$ denotes the denominator of $q$; the function $v(x)$ retains its argument.

Let $N_v,N_c$ be the binary bit lengths of these denominators, and define

$$ K=10+6N_v+3N_c. $$

Both bit lengths are $O(L)$.

Subcritical inputs

For $x\leq r<\alpha$,

$$ h_q'(x)<h_q'(\alpha)=-a\sqrt{D(q)}<0. $$

Since $h_q(x)>0$,

$$ v'(x)=-h_q(x)+(1-x)h_q'(x)<0. $$

Thus

$$ \int_0^r\frac{|v'(x)|}{v(x)}\,dx = \log\frac{v(0)}{v(r)}. $$

The positive rational number $v(r)$ has denominator dividing $v^2c^3$, so

$$ v(r)\geq\frac1{v^2c^3}. $$

Also $v(0)=q^2\leq1$. Hence

$$ \int_0^r\frac{|v'(x)|}{v(x)}\,dx \leq2\log v+3\log c. $$

Supercritical inputs

Completing the square gives

$$ h_q(x) = 2a^2\left(x-\frac{1-3q}{4a}\right)^2-\frac{D(q)}8. $$

The positive rational number $-D(q)$ has denominator dividing $v^2$, so $-D(q)\geq1/v^2$. Since $1-r\geq1/c$,

$$ v(x)\geq\frac1{8v^2c} \qquad(0\leq x\leq r). $$

Also $v(x)\leq1$, because $F_q(x)\leq1$.

The polynomial $v$ has degree three, so $v'$ has at most two roots. Therefore $[0,r]$ can be partitioned into at most three intervals on which $v$ is monotone. On each such interval, the variation of $\log v$ is at most $\log(8v^2c)$. Consequently,

$$ \int_0^r\frac{|v'(x)|}{v(x)}\,dx \leq3\log(8v^2c). $$

Combining either bound with Lemma 1, and using $\log v<N_v$ and $\log c<N_c$, proves the uniform estimate

$$ \boxed{|T-J(r)|\leq K=O(L).} $$

This estimate does not depend on the numerical size of $T$.

4. Polynomial-time evaluation of the estimate

The integral $J(r)$ has an explicit expression involving elementary functions.

Put

$$ U(x)=4ax+3q-1. $$

Then

$$ h_q(x)=\frac{U(x)^2-D(q)}8. $$

The partial fraction identity

$$ \frac1{(1-x)h_q(x)} = \frac1{1-x} + \frac{2a^2x+1-q^2}{h_q(x)} $$

follows by multiplying both sides by $(1-x)h_q(x)$. Furthermore,

$$ 2a^2x+1-q^2 = \frac12h_q'(x)+\frac{a(3-q)}2. $$

Thus

$$ J(r) = -\log(1-r) +\frac12\log\frac{h_q(r)}{q^2} +\frac{a(3-q)}2\int_0^r\frac{dx}{h_q(x)}. $$

Writing $U_0=U(0)$ and $U_r=U(r)$ gives the following formulas.

If $D(q)<0$, put $s=\sqrt{-D(q)}$. Then

$$ J(r)= -\log(1-r) +\frac12\log\frac{h_q(r)}{q^2} +\frac{3-q}{s} \left( \arctan\frac{U_r}{s} - \arctan\frac{U_0}{s} \right). $$

If $D(q)>0$, put $s=\sqrt{D(q)}$. Reachability gives $U_0<U_r<-s$, and

$$ J(r)= -\log(1-r) +\frac12\log\frac{h_q(r)}{q^2} +\frac{3-q}{2s} \log \frac{(U_r-s)(U_0+s)} {(U_r+s)(U_0-s)}. $$

All logarithm arguments are positive.

Bit complexity

These formulas permit approximation of $J(r)$ with absolute error at most $1/4$ in polynomial time.

Here are the required size and precision checks:

  • Because $D(q)\neq0$ and its denominator divides $v^2$,

$$ \frac1v\leq\sqrt{|D(q)|}\leq2. $$

  • The positive rational $h_q(r)$ has denominator dividing $v^2c^2$, so

$$ h_q(r)\geq\frac1{v^2c^2}. $$

  • In the subcritical logarithm, none of the four factors $U_r\pm s,U_0\pm s$ is zero. Their magnitudes are bounded below by $2^{-O(L)}$: for example,

$$ |U_r-s|\,|U_r+s|=8h_q(r), $$

while each factor is bounded above by an absolute constant. The same argument at $0$ uses $h_q(0)=q^2$.

Thus all arguments and intermediate nonzero divisors have magnitudes between $2^{-O(L)}$ and $2^{O(L)}$. A fixed number of arithmetic operations therefore requires only $O(L)$ working precision, with a sufficiently large constant, to attain the stated absolute error.

For completeness, the elementary functions needed here admit polynomial-time approximations at that precision:

  • Square roots can be approximated by rational bisection.
  • For a positive logarithm argument, multiplication by a power of $2$ reduces it to $[1,2]$. On that interval, the series

$$ \log z = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad w=\frac{z-1}{z+1}, $$

has $|w|\leq1/3$, so a linear number of terms in the requested precision suffices. The same series computes $\log2$.

  • For arctangent, reciprocal reduction handles arguments of magnitude exceeding $1$. On $[0,1]$, the identity

$$ \arctan u = 2\arctan\frac{u}{1+\sqrt{1+u^2}} $$

reduces the argument below $1/2$. Its power series then converges geometrically. This also computes $\pi=4\arctan1$.

The rational arithmetic in these truncated series has polynomial bit complexity. Hence a dyadic rational $\widehat J$ satisfying

$$ |\widehat J-J(r)|\leq\frac14 $$

is computable in polynomial time.

Consequence for binary horizons

The following answers are certified:

$$ H<\widehat J-K-\frac14 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H<T, $$

and

$$ H\geq\widehat J+K+\frac14 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H\geq T. $$

Both comparisons are exact rational comparisons computable in polynomial time, including when $H$ is encoded in binary.

The remaining interval has length $2K+1/2$ and contains at most $2K+2=O(L)$ integer horizons.

What this proves: a polynomial-time procedure decides all horizons outside an explicitly computable interval containing only $O(L)$ integers.

What it does not prove: a polynomial-time decision procedure for the horizons inside that interval.

5. A constant-error estimate near the phase transition

For the fixed target $r=1/2$, the error can be bounded independently of input length.

We first give a sharper general estimate.

Lemma 2: a corrected integral estimate

Under the assumptions of Lemma 1, suppose additionally that $v$ is twice continuously differentiable and

$$ |v'(x)|\leq\frac12, \qquad |v''(x)|\leq M \qquad(0\leq x\leq r). $$

Define

$$ \Phi(r)=J(r)+\frac12\log\frac{v(r)}{v(0)}. $$

Then

$$ |T-\Phi(r)| \leq 6\int_0^r\frac{v'(x)^2}{v(x)}\,dx +12M^2r^2+2Mr+3. $$

Proof

Consider a complete orbit interval $[a,a+v(a)]$ contained in $[0,r]$. Write

$$ v_i=v(a),\qquad k_i=v'(a), \qquad w(u)=\frac{v(a+v_i u)}{v_i} \quad(0\leq u\leq1). $$

The derivative bound gives $1/2\leq w(u)\leq3/2$. Taylor's theorem gives

$$ |w(u)-(1+k_i u)| \leq\frac12Mv_i u^2. $$

The increment of $\Phi$ over this interval is

$$ \int_0^1\frac{du}{w(u)} +\frac12\log w(1). $$

Replacing $w(u)$ by $1+k_i u$ changes the integral by at most $2Mv_i/3$, because both denominators are at least $1/2$. It changes the half-logarithm by at most $Mv_i/2$. Their sum is therefore at most $2Mv_i$.

For $|k|\leq1/2$,

$$ \int_0^1\frac{du}{1+ku} = 1-\frac k2+R, \qquad |R|\leq\frac23k^2, $$

using

$$ \frac1{1+ku}=1-ku+\frac{k^2u^2}{1+ku}. $$

Also $|\log(1+k)-k|\leq k^2$, obtained by integrating the bound $|s/(1+s)|\leq2|s|$. Consequently,

$$ \left| \int_0^1\frac{du}{1+ku} +\frac12\log(1+k)-1 \right| \leq2k^2. $$

The discrepancy of the complete increment of $\Phi$ from $1$ is therefore at most

$$ 2k_i^2+2Mv_i. $$

For every $x$ in that interval,

$$ k_i^2\leq2v'(x)^2+2M^2v_i^2. $$

Moreover,

$$ \frac23 \leq \int_a^{a+v_i}\frac{dx}{v(x)} \leq2. $$

Integrating the preceding inequality against $dx/v(x)$ yields

$$ k_i^2 \leq 3\int_a^{a+v_i}\frac{v'(x)^2}{v(x)}\,dx +6M^2v_i^2. $$

The complete intervals are disjoint, and their lengths satisfy

$$ \sum_i v_i\leq r, \qquad \sum_i v_i^2\leq r^2. $$

Summing their discrepancies gives the first three terms in the asserted bound.

Finally, on the last, possibly truncated interval,

$$ \Phi'(x)=\frac{1+v'(x)/2}{v(x)}>0. $$

Its increment is at most $5/2$: throughout that interval, $v(x)\geq v(a)/2$, and its length is at most $v(a)$. Its discrepancy from one round is thus at most $3$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

Application to $q_\star<q\leq1/5$ and $r=1/2$

First,

$$ \frac16<q_\star<\frac7{40}, $$

because $D$ is decreasing on $[0,1]$ and

$$ D\left(\frac16\right)=\frac1{36}>0, \qquad D\left(\frac7{40}\right)=-\frac{31}{1600}<0. $$

Hence

$$ \frac45\leq a<\frac56. $$

For $z=1-x\in[1/2,1]$,

$$ F_q'(x)=2az(2+a-3az). $$

This is a concave quadratic in $z$. Its endpoint values exceed $1/2$:

$$ 4aq>\frac8{15}, \qquad a\left(2-\frac a2\right)\geq\frac{19}{15}. $$

Its maximum is at most

$$ \frac{(2+a)^2}{6} \leq\frac{289}{216}<\frac32. $$

Thus $|v'(x)|\leq1/2$.

Also,

$$ v''(x)=2a\bigl(6a(1-x)-(2+a)\bigr). $$

The bracket lies between $-2/5$ and $13/6$, so $|v''(x)|<4$. We may take $M=4$.

Put $s=\sqrt{-D(q)}$ and $b_0=(1-3q)/(4a)$. Then

$$ h_q(x)=2a^2(x-b_0)^2+\frac{s^2}{8}. $$

Since $h_q$ is convex and

$$ h_q(0)=q^2,\qquad h_q(1/2)=q, $$

we have $h_q(x)\leq q\leq1/5$ on $[0,1/2]$. Furthermore,

$$ \frac{h_q'(x)^2}{h_q(x)}\leq8a^2. $$

Using $v'=(1-x)h_q'-h_q$, we obtain

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{v'(x)^2}{v(x)} &\leq 2(1-x)\frac{h_q'(x)^2}{h_q(x)} +\frac{2h_q(x)}{1-x}\\ &\leq16a^2+4q\\ &\leq\frac{536}{45}<12. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore

$$ \int_0^{1/2}\frac{v'(x)^2}{v(x)}\,dx\leq6. $$

Lemma 2 now gives

$$ |T-\Phi(1/2)| \leq36+48+4+3=91. $$

Since $v(1/2)=q/2$ and $v(0)=q^2$,

$$ \Phi(1/2)-J(1/2) = \frac12\log\frac1{2q}, $$

which lies between $0$ and $1$. Thus

$$ |T-J(1/2)|\leq92. $$

6. An explicit bottleneck formula and exponential crossing times

For $r=1/2$,

$$ U_r=1+q,\qquad U_0=3q-1,\qquad h_q(1/2)=q. $$

Hence

$$ J(1/2)= \log2+\frac12\log\frac1q +\frac{3-q}{s} \left( \arctan\frac{1+q}{s} - \arctan\frac{3q-1}{s} \right). $$

In the range $q_\star<q\leq1/5$, the first arctangent argument is positive and the second is negative. Therefore

$$ \arctan\frac{1+q}{s} - \arctan\frac{3q-1}{s} = \pi -\arctan\frac{s}{1+q} -\arctan\frac{s}{1-3q}. $$

For $u\geq0$, $0\leq\arctan u\leq u$. Since $1+q\geq1$, $1-3q\geq2/5$, and $3-q\leq3$, replacing the arctangent difference by $\pi$ changes its contribution by at most $21/2$.

The remaining logarithms have sum less than $7/2$, using $q>1/6$ and $\log z\leq z-1$. Consequently,

$$ \left| J(1/2)-\frac{\pi(3-q)}s \right|\leq14. $$

Together with the preceding section, this proves

$$ \boxed{ \left| T(q)-\frac{\pi(3-q)}{\sqrt{6q-q^2-1}} \right|\leq128 } $$

for every rational $q_\star<q\leq1/5$.

The displayed expression is approximable to absolute error $1/4$ in polynomial time by the methods of Section 4. Therefore, in this regime with target $1/2$, at most $258$ integer horizons remain undecided by this estimate, independently of input length.

Exact leading asymptotic

Put $\delta=q-q_\star>0$. Factoring $-D(q)$ gives

$$ s^2=\delta(6-q-q_\star). $$

The constant-error estimate consequently implies

$$ \lim_{\substack{q\downarrow q_\star\\q\in\mathbb Q}} \sqrt{q-q_\star}\,T(q) = \frac{\pi(3-q_\star)}{\sqrt{6-2q_\star}} = \pi\,2^{1/4}. $$

Indeed, multiplying the bounded error by $\sqrt\delta$ makes it tend to zero, and the remaining coefficient is continuous at $q_\star$.

A controlled exponential family

For $n\geq10$, define

$$ q_n= \frac{\lceil2^nq_\star\rceil+1}{2^n}. $$

Irrationality of $q_\star$ gives

$$ 2^{-n}<q_n-q_\star<2^{1-n}. $$

The ceiling can be computed in polynomial time by binary search, comparing dyadic rationals to $q_\star$ through the sign of $D$.

The upper-margin check is explicit:

$$ q_n< \frac7{40}+\frac1{512} < \frac9{50} < \frac15. $$

Thus the constant-error estimate applies and proves

$$ T(q_n)=\Theta(2^{n/2}). $$

To realize the target $r=1/2$ in the original inputs, take

$$ \mu_C=\mu_P=\frac12, \qquad d_C=d_P=1, \qquad \kappa=\frac14. $$

Taking $H_n=2^{n+10}$ gives total input length $\Theta(n)$ and includes the crossing. For example, the estimate implies

$$ T(q_n)\leq6\,2^{n/2}+128<H_n. $$

Hence exponentially long crossing times occur, while the crossing-time estimate itself remains polynomial-time computable. Long transients alone therefore do not establish computational hardness.

7. Remaining unresolved comparison

The unrestricted decision problem has been reduced to exact placement of the first crossing time within an explicitly computable interval containing $O(L)$ integers. For the fixed target $1/2$ near the phase transition, the interval contains only a constant number of integers.

However, a short uncertainty interval does not itself resolve its remaining integer comparisons. None of the estimates above determines whether an exceptional horizon satisfies $x_H\geq r$, including a potentially very close strict comparison.

The [cited stochastic paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00446-021-00396-5) studies random graph dynamics. Its probabilistic conclusions are not used to decide these exact deterministic comparisons.

A uniform polynomial-time algorithm for the exceptional horizons, or a hardness reduction exploiting them, remains unproved here.
