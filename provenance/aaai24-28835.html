The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational payoff matrices \(R\) and \(C\), row strategies \(r_0\) and \(r^*\), and rational population distributions \(\mu_0\) and \(\mu^*\) over column strategies, with both endpoints population Nash equilibria, compute the minimum total normalized reward over finite transition sequences \((r_0,\mu_0),\ldots,(r^*,\mu^*)\) where each step costs \(A(\mu^t,r^{t+1})+B(r^t,\mu^{t+1})\), \(A(\mu,b)=\max_a \sum_q \mu_q R(a,c_q)-\sum_q \mu_q R(b,c_q)\), and \(B(a,\nu)=\max_q C(a,c_q)-\sum_q \nu_q C(a,c_q)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(R,C\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{m\times n}\), with \(m,n\geq1\), describe one pure-strategy row player and a unit-mass column population whose available strategies are \(c_1,\ldots,c_n\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here types are occupied column strategies; for the two input societies use \(\tau=\max\{|\operatorname{supp}(\mu_0)|,|\operatorname{supp}(\mu_*)|\}\).
A state is \((a,\mu)\), where \(a\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) and \(\mu\in\Delta_n\); masses are nonnegative and sum to one. Intermediate populations may use any available column strategy and real masses.
Define \(A(\mu,b)=\max_{a\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\mu_qR(a,c_q)-\sum_{q=1}^{n}\mu_qR(b,c_q)\) and \(B(a,\nu)=\max_{q\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}C(a,c_q)-\sum_{q=1}^{n}\nu_qC(a,c_q)\).
The input endpoints \((r_0,\mu_0)\) and \((r_*,\mu_*)\) are population Nash equilibria: \(A(\mu,a)=B(a,\mu)=0\). Ties permit any maximizing strategy.
A finite transition sequence may change both components simultaneously, including leaving either unchanged; its cost is \(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}[A(\mu^t,r^{t+1})+B(r^t,\mu^{t+1})]\). Intermediate states need not be equilibria. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the infimum of these costs between the endpoints.
Write \(S_R=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\sum_{q=1}^{n}R(a,c_q)\), \(S_C=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\sum_{q=1}^{n}C(a,c_q)\), and let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Can a transition sequence of cost at most \(\mathrm{OPT}+2m(2S_R+S_C)\) be computed in time polynomial in \(m,n,\tau,L\), or is achieving this additive guarantee NP-hard?

For finite populations, a polynomial-time algorithm achieves additive error \(2m(2S_R+S_C)\) in total, unnormalized reward, independently of population size. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.07603)

Result

The stated continuous model is polynomial-time solvable exactly. Consequently, the requested additive guarantee can be achieved.

The additive guarantee alone is immediate: the direct transition has cost at most \(S_R+S_C\), which is at most \(2m(2S_R+S_C)\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}\geq 0\). We therefore address the stronger, nontrivial question of computing an optimal sequence.

1. Optimizing an intermediate population

For row strategies \(i,j\), define

\(w(i,j)=\min_{\nu\in\Delta_n}\bigl[B(i,\nu)+A(\nu,j)\bigr].\)

This minimum exists because the objective is continuous and \(\Delta_n\) is compact. Moreover, it can be computed by one linear program.

Let \(\beta_i=\max_q C(i,c_q)\) and \(M_R=\max_{a,q}R(a,c_q)\). Use variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_n,z\), with objective

\(\displaystyle \min\ \beta_i-\sum_{q=1}^{n}x_qC(i,c_q)+z,\)

subject to

  • \(\displaystyle \sum_{q=1}^{n}x_q=1,\qquad x_q\geq 0\quad(q=1,\ldots,n);\)
  • \(\displaystyle z\geq\sum_{q=1}^{n}x_q\bigl[R(a,c_q)-R(j,c_q)\bigr]\quad(a=1,\ldots,m);\)
  • \(0\leq z\leq M_R.\)

For each fixed \(x\in\Delta_n\), the smallest feasible \(z\) is exactly \(A(x,j)\). Indeed, the second family of constraints gives its defining maximum. The constraint indexed by \(a=j\) gives \(z\geq 0\), and nonnegativity of \(R\) implies \(A(x,j)\leq M_R\). Thus the last constraint does not exclude any minimizing population.

The LP consequently has optimum \(w(i,j)\). Its feasible region is nonempty and compact: any \(x\in\Delta_n\), together with \(z=M_R\), is feasible. Store an optimal rational population \(\nu^{ij}\) for each ordered pair.

Construct a directed graph \(G\) on the \(m\) row strategies, giving edge \(i\to j\) weight \(w(i,j)\). All weights are nonnegative. Let \(D\) be its shortest-path distance from \(r_0\) to \(r_*\), allowing the empty path when these strategies coincide.

We will prove

\(\mathrm{OPT}=2D.\)

2. Every transition sequence costs at least twice this distance

Consider a conceptual directed bipartite graph \(H\). Its two vertex classes are the row strategies and the populations in \(\Delta_n\), treated as disjoint classes. Its edge weights are

\(\operatorname{wt}(i\to\nu)=B(i,\nu),\qquad \operatorname{wt}(\nu\to j)=A(\nu,j).\)

Every finite walk in \(H\) alternates between row strategies and populations.

First consider any such walk from \(r_0\) to \(r_*\). Group its edges into consecutive pairs \(i\to\nu\to j\). Each pair costs at least \(w(i,j)\), by the definition of that minimum. The corresponding row walk in \(G\) therefore costs no more than the original walk, and its cost is at least \(D\). Hence every row-to-row walk in \(H\) from \(r_0\) to \(r_*\) costs at least \(D\).

The equilibrium hypotheses give four zero-cost edges:

\(r_0\to\mu_0,\quad \mu_0\to r_0,\quad r_*\to\mu_*,\quad \mu_*\to r_*.\)

Consequently, every finite walk in \(H\) starting in \(\{r_0,\mu_0\}\) and ending in \(\{r_*,\mu_*\}\) costs at least \(D\). To verify this, prepend \(r_0\to\mu_0\) if it starts at \(\mu_0\), and append \(\mu_*\to r_*\) if it ends at \(\mu_*\). These additions give a row-to-row walk without changing its cost.

Now take an arbitrary transition sequence \((r^t,\mu^t)\), indexed by \(t=0,\ldots,T\). Form two alternating walks:

  • The first uses \(r^t\) at even times and \(\mu^t\) at odd times.
  • The second uses \(\mu^t\) at even times and \(r^t\) at odd times.

At every time step, their two edge costs are exactly

\(A(\mu^t,r^{t+1})\quad\text{and}\quad B(r^t,\mu^{t+1}),\)

with their assignment to the two walks determined by parity. Thus their total cost equals the transition sequence’s cost.

Both walks start in \(\{r_0,\mu_0\}\) and end in \(\{r_*,\mu_*\}\). Each costs at least \(D\), so the sequence costs at least \(2D\). This applies to every finite sequence, including those with arbitrary real intermediate masses. Therefore

\(\mathrm{OPT}\geq 2D.\)

3. Constructing a sequence of cost exactly twice the distance

Choose a shortest path in \(G\),

\(a_0=r_0,\ a_1,\ldots,a_k=r_*.\)

Because edge weights are nonnegative, deleting cycles cannot increase cost. We may therefore choose \(k\leq m-1\). When \(r_0=r_*\), choose \(k=0\).

For each edge, let \(\nu_i=\nu^{a_{i-1}a_i}\) be its stored optimal population. Expand the path into the alternating walk

\(\mu_0\to a_0\to\nu_1\to a_1\to\cdots\to\nu_k\to a_k\to\mu_*.\)

The first and last edges cost zero by the endpoint equilibrium hypotheses. Each internal pair \(a_{i-1}\to\nu_i\to a_i\) costs \(w(a_{i-1},a_i)\). Thus this alternating walk has total cost \(D\).

Turn every consecutive pair of vertices into a state, putting its row component first. For \(k\geq 1\), the resulting sequence is

\((r_0,\mu_0),\ (a_0,\nu_1),\ (a_1,\nu_1),\ (a_1,\nu_2),\ldots,(a_k,\nu_k),\ (r_*,\mu_*).\)

For \(k=0\), it is simply the direct transition between the endpoints.

To calculate its cost precisely, write the expanded alternating walk as \(v_0,\ldots,v_\ell\), where \(\ell=2k+2\), and let \(e_j\) be the weight of \(v_j\to v_{j+1}\). The constructed state indexed by \(j\) consists of \(v_j,v_{j+1}\), ordered with the row first.

The transition between two consecutive constructed states costs \(e_{j-1}+e_j\):

  • If their shared vertex is a row \(a\), the population changes, and the cost is \(A(\text{old population},a)+B(a,\text{new population})\).
  • If their shared vertex is a population \(\nu\), the row changes, and the cost is \(B(\text{old row},\nu)+A(\nu,\text{new row})\).

These are exactly the two incident directed edge weights. Summing gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{cost}=e_0+2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-2}e_j+e_{\ell-1}=2D,\)

because \(e_0=e_{\ell-1}=0\).

Together with the lower bound, this proves \(\mathrm{OPT}=2D\). In particular, the infimum is attained. The constructed sequence uses \(2k+1\leq 2m-1\) transitions.

4. Polynomial running time and output length

The algorithm solves \(m^2\) rational LPs, each with \(n+1\) variables and \(O(m+n)\) constraints, and then computes one shortest path.

We use the standard theorem that an explicitly given feasible rational LP with finite optimum admits an exact optimal rational solution computable in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Polynomial-time LP algorithms and polynomial bounds on rational optimal-solution encodings are covered in these [algorithm notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-854j-advanced-algorithms-fall-2008/c61e649bf05c7123da984519955da0bd_lec9.pdf) and [bit-complexity notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/18/Notes/n12-duality.html).

Here every LP is feasible and bounded, as verified above. Its coefficients are input rationals or differences of input rationals, so its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,n,L\). The stored populations and edge weights consequently have polynomial binary length. Shortest-path arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity.

The output contains at most \(2m\) states, each with at most \(n\) occupied strategies and rational masses of polynomial encoding length. Intermediate support need not be bounded by \(\tau\); the problem explicitly permits all available strategies. Reading and reproducing the endpoints takes polynomial time in their support encoding.

Thus the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,n,\tau,L\) and achieves zero additive error.

The [cited finite-population paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.07603) uses alternating paths and optimization over integer strategy counts. In the stated continuous model, the corresponding population optimization is exactly the LP above, so no rounding is necessary.
