The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), fixed lexicographic tie-breaking, a focal voter of type \(\theta^\star=(u^\star,\succ^\star,\lambda^\star)\) and rational mass \(\alpha>0\), and a rational distribution \(\nu_{\theta,A}\) of total mass \(1-\alpha\) over opponents’ types and current ballots, compute a ballot \(B\subseteq C\) maximizing the focal voter’s OWA utility from the committee selected by approval masses \(p_c+\alpha\mathbf{1}[c\in B]\), where \(p_c=\sum_{\theta,A:c\in A}\nu_{\theta,A}\). Return an optimal ballot and its resulting committee.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) candidates, let \(1\leq k\leq m\), and fix a lexicographic priority order for breaking score ties.
A type is \(\theta=(u,\succ,\lambda)\), where \(u:C\to\mathbb Q\) has pairwise distinct, possibly negative values, \(\succ\) is their decreasing order, and \(\lambda\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}^{k}\) has at least one positive entry; weights need not sum to one. For \(W=\{c_1,\ldots,c_k\}\) ordered by \(\succ\), define \(U_\theta(W)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_j u(c_j)\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational; here types include a current ballot or a focal designation. Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types or ballots.
The input specifies a focal type \(\theta^\star\) of rational mass \(0<\alpha\leq1\) and opponents’ type–ballot masses \(\nu_{\theta,A}>0\), with \(A\subseteq C\) and \(\sum_{\theta,A}\nu_{\theta,A}=1-\alpha\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
The entire focal mass chooses one common ballot \(B\subseteq C\), including possibly the empty ballot, without a ballot-length restriction or voting cost. Put \(p_c=\sum_{\theta,A:c\in A}\nu_{\theta,A}\); the winning committee \(W(B)\) consists of the \(k\) candidates with highest \(p_c+\alpha\mathbf1_{\{c\in B\}}\), resolving ties by the fixed priority order.
Return any ballot maximizing \(U_{\theta^\star}(W(B))\), together with its resulting committee.

Is computing an optimal ballot and its resulting committee solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite unweighted electorates, a sincere best response always exists, yielding polynomial-time best-response computation by examining the \(m+1\) preference-prefix ballots ([Lee](https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05092)).
No result settling exactly the rational-mass problem above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted cardinal utilities and OWA weights to binary-encoded rationals to define a finite computational input; retained signed utilities, strict candidate preferences, nonnegative weights with at least one positive entry, and no weight normalization.
  • Required 1 <= k <= m and 0 < alpha <= 1; normalized total society mass to one and allowed empty opponent support when alpha = 1.
  • Counted support over augmented types carrying current ballots or a focal designation, since distinct ballots of the same preference type are separate entries in the supplied distribution.
  • Interpreted the focal mass as choosing one common ballot without splitting across ballots, as specified by the approval-mass formula.
  • Used unrestricted, costless ballots including the empty ballot, with any utility-maximizing ballot acceptable and no secondary preference among optimal ballots.
  • Defined L as the total binary input length, including masses, utilities, weights, ballots, and tie-breaking data.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\). The algorithm examines at most \(m\) score thresholds and constructs an optimal committee for each feasible threshold.

Write \(u=u_{\theta^\star}\) and \(\lambda=\lambda_{\theta^\star}\). All comparisons and arithmetic below are exact.

1. Incorporating tie-breaking into scores

Let \(r(c)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) be the priority rank of candidate \(c\), with smaller ranks having higher priority. Define the unboosted and boosted score keys by

\( \ell_c=(p_c,-r(c)),\qquad h_c=(p_c+\alpha,-r(c)). \)

Compare these pairs lexicographically, with larger keys winning. This reproduces precisely the specified score comparison and tie-breaking rule.

Because \(\alpha>0\), we have \(h_c>\ell_c\). Keys belonging to different candidates are distinct, even when their numerical scores coincide.

2. Characterizing attainable committees

Lemma 1. A set \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) is attainable by some ballot if and only if

\( h_w>\ell_d\quad\text{for every }w\in W,\ d\in C\setminus W. \)

Whenever this condition holds, the ballot \(B=W\) induces exactly \(W\).

Proof. Suppose a ballot \(B\) induces \(W\). Let \(s_c(B)\) denote candidate \(c\)’s score key under that ballot. For every winner \(w\) and nonwinner \(d\),

\( h_w\geq s_w(B)>s_d(B)\geq\ell_d. \)

The middle inequality is strict because the score keys incorporate the fixed priority order. Hence the stated condition is necessary.

Conversely, under the ballot \(B=W\), every member \(w\in W\) has key \(h_w\), and every candidate \(d\notin W\) has key \(\ell_d\). The condition therefore places every member of \(W\) above every nonmember. Since \(|W|=k\), the winning committee is \(W\). This also covers \(k=m\), when there are no nonmembers. ∎

Thus committee optimization suffices: an optimal attainable committee itself supplies an implementing ballot.

3. At most \(m\) threshold families cover all attainable committees

For each threshold \(q\in\{h_c:c\in C\}\), define

\( M_q=\{c\in C:\ell_c\geq q\},\qquad E_q=\{c\in C:h_c\geq q\}. \)

The set \(M_q\) contains the candidates that must be included at this threshold, while \(E_q\) contains the eligible candidates. Since \(h_c>\ell_c\), we always have \(M_q\subseteq E_q\).

Define the family

\( \mathcal F_q=\{W\subseteq C:|W|=k,\ M_q\subseteq W\subseteq E_q\}. \)

Lemma 2. The attainable committees are exactly the members of the union of these \(m\) families.

Proof. First, suppose \(W\in\mathcal F_q\). Every \(w\in W\) satisfies \(h_w\geq q\). Every \(d\notin W\) lies outside \(M_q\), so \(\ell_d<q\). Consequently \(h_w>\ell_d\), and Lemma 1 proves attainability.

Conversely, suppose \(W\) is attainable. It is nonempty because \(k\geq1\), so define

\( q=\min_{w\in W}h_w. \)

This threshold is one of the enumerated boosted keys, and \(W\subseteq E_q\). By Lemma 1, every nonmember \(d\) satisfies \(\ell_d<q\). Therefore \(M_q\subseteq W\), proving \(W\in\mathcal F_q\). ∎

The family \(\mathcal F_q\) is nonempty exactly when

\( |M_q|\leq k\leq|E_q|. \)

Necessity follows from its definition. For sufficiency, extend \(M_q\) with any \(k-|M_q|\) candidates from \(E_q\setminus M_q\).

4. Optimizing within a threshold family

For a feasible threshold \(q\), let \(T_q\) consist of the \(k-|M_q|\) candidates with highest utility in \(E_q\setminus M_q\), and put

\( G_q=M_q\cup T_q. \)

Lemma 3. The committee \(G_q\) maximizes \(U_{\theta^\star}\) over \(\mathcal F_q\).

Proof. We first justify the utility comparison used in the argument. Replacing one committee member by a candidate with larger utility cannot decrease any coordinate of the committee’s decreasingly sorted utility vector. Indeed, for every real threshold \(t\), this replacement cannot decrease the number of committee members having utility at least \(t\). If the \(j\)-th largest utility decreased, taking \(t\) equal to its old value would contradict this counting property.

Because every \(\lambda_j\geq0\), such a replacement cannot decrease the weighted utility. This holds also for negative candidate utilities and zero weights.

Now take any \(W\in\mathcal F_q\). Both \(W\) and \(G_q\) contain \(M_q\) and select exactly \(k-|M_q|\) optional candidates. If they differ, choose

\( a\in G_q\setminus W,\qquad b\in W\setminus G_q. \)

Both candidates belong to \(E_q\setminus M_q\). Because \(T_q\) contains the highest-utility optional candidates and utilities are distinct, \(u(a)>u(b)\). Replacing \(b\) by \(a\) preserves membership in \(\mathcal F_q\) and weakly increases utility.

Each replacement reduces the number of differences from \(G_q\). Repeating finitely many times yields \(G_q\), proving \(U_{\theta^\star}(G_q)\geq U_{\theta^\star}(W)\). ∎

No monotonicity of the sequence \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k\) is required; componentwise nonnegativity suffices.

5. Algorithm and correctness

The algorithm is:

  • Compute all opponent scores \(p_c\), and form \(\ell_c,h_c\).
  • Sort candidates by decreasing focal utility.
  • For each \(q=h_c\), compute \(M_q,E_q\). Discard the threshold if \(|M_q|>k\) or \(|E_q|<k\).
  • For every remaining threshold, construct \(G_q\) as above and evaluate its utility.
  • Choose a committee \(G\) with greatest evaluated utility, and return the ballot \(B=G\) together with committee \(G\).

At least one threshold is feasible: any ballot produces an attainable committee, which belongs to some threshold family by Lemma 2.

To prove global optimality, let \(B^\star\) be any optimal ballot and \(W^\star=W(B^\star)\). By Lemma 2, \(W^\star\in\mathcal F_q\) for some enumerated threshold. Lemma 3 gives

\( U_{\theta^\star}(G_q)\geq U_{\theta^\star}(W^\star). \)

The algorithm selects a committee \(G\) with utility at least that of \(G_q\). Lemma 1 guarantees that the returned ballot \(B=G\) induces \(G\). Therefore the returned ballot is optimal.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

Computing the opponent scores uses at most \(O(m\tau)\) rational additions. Sorting by focal utility uses \(O(m\log m)\) comparisons.

For each of the \(m\) thresholds, computing the two candidate sets, selecting optional members, and evaluating utility take \(O(m)\) exact arithmetic operations or comparisons: use the previously sorted preference list for selection and utility evaluation. Thus the total operation count is

\( O(m\tau+m\log m+m^2). \)

These operations also have polynomial bit cost. A common denominator for the input masses can be taken as the product of their denominators, whose binary length is bounded by the total input length. Hence each computed score has polynomially bounded binary length. Similarly, committee utility is a sum of at most \(k\) products of input rationals; its denominator divides the product of all focal utility and weight denominators, and its numerator has polynomially bounded binary length. Exact additions, multiplications, and comparisons therefore operate on numbers of polynomial size.

Consequently, the complete algorithm runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), without expanding rational masses into individual voters.

Because \(\alpha>0\), the inequality \(h_c>\ell_c\) holds for every candidate. Keys for distinct candidates cannot coincide because their priority ranks differ. Thus winners have strictly larger keys than nonwinners, including when numerical scores tie.

Assessment: Correct.

2. Lemma 1: Attainable committees

For any ballot inducing \(W\), the inequalities
\[ h_w\geq s_w(B)>s_d(B)\geq\ell_d \]
hold for every winner \(w\) and nonwinner \(d\). They establish the necessary condition with the required strictness.

Conversely, choosing \(B=W\) assigns precisely \(h_w\) to each member and \(\ell_d\) to each nonmember. The proposed condition therefore guarantees that all members precede all nonmembers. Since \(W\) has size \(k\), it is exactly the winning committee.

When \(k=m\), the condition is vacuous and \(B=C\) correctly implements the only committee.

Assessment: Correct. Optimizing over attainable committees and returning the selected committee as a ballot is justified.

3. Lemma 2: Threshold-family characterization

The inclusion \(M_q\subseteq E_q\) follows from \(h_c>\ell_c\).

For \(W\in\mathcal F_q\), every member satisfies \(h_w\geq q\), while every nonmember satisfies \(\ell_d<q\). Hence \(h_w>\ell_d\), exactly as required by Lemma 1. The use of a weak inequality for eligibility and a strict inequality for excluded candidates handles boundary ties correctly.

Conversely, an attainable committee is nonempty because \(k\geq1\). Its minimum boosted key \(q\) is consequently well-defined and belongs to the enumerated set. Lemma 1 implies that every nonmember’s unboosted key is strictly below this minimum. Therefore \(M_q\subseteq W\subseteq E_q\).

The characterization does not require every committee in \(\mathcal F_q\) to have minimum boosted key exactly \(q\); the proof correctly uses only the defining inequalities.

Assessment: Correct. The enumerated families cover exactly all attainable committees.

4. Feasibility of a threshold family

The condition \(|M_q|\leq k\leq|E_q|\) is necessary.

It is also sufficient: because \(M_q\subseteq E_q\), there are at least \(k-|M_q|\) eligible optional candidates available to extend \(M_q\). This includes the boundary case \(|M_q|=k\), where no optional candidate is required.

Assessment: Correct.

5. Lemma 3: Utility optimization within a family

The sorted-vector monotonicity argument is valid. Replacing a member by a candidate with greater utility cannot decrease the number of selected utilities at or above any real threshold. A decrease in any sorted coordinate would contradict that counting property at the coordinate’s previous value.

Consequently, every sorted coordinate weakly increases. Since every weight is nonnegative, the weighted sum cannot decrease. This remains valid for negative utilities, zero weights, and weights that are neither normalized nor monotone.

In the exchange argument, both difference sets consist exclusively of optional candidates. Because \(T_q\) selects the highest-utility optional candidates and utilities are pairwise distinct, every selected optional candidate omitted from \(W\) has strictly greater utility than every optional candidate of \(W\) omitted from \(G_q\). Each exchange preserves family membership and reduces the discrepancy from \(G_q\).

If no optional candidates are selected, or the committees already coincide, no exchange is needed.

Assessment: Correct. The claimed maximizing committee follows without additional assumptions on the weights.

6. Global correctness and returned ballot

An optimal ballot exists because the ballot space is finite. Its committee belongs to an enumerated threshold family by Lemma 2, and Lemma 3 supplies a committee in that family with at least as much utility.

The algorithm selects the best of all such constructed committees. Each constructed committee is attainable, and Lemma 1 guarantees that the returned ballot \(B=G\) induces exactly the reported committee \(G\).

The existence of at least one feasible threshold also follows as stated: any ballot produces a committee, and Lemma 2 places that committee in a threshold family.

Assessment: Correct. Both requested outputs are supplied.

  • Select the required number of optional candidates in preference order.
  • Evaluate the resulting committee in that same order.

Each threshold therefore requires \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations or comparisons, yielding the claimed total
