The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 1.6 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant types \(A\), slot types \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\ge 0\), acceptable edges \(E\subseteq A\times B\), and nonnegative rational costs \(\gamma_{ab}\), choose \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with \(x_{ab}=0\) off \(E\), \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le \mu_a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le \nu_b\). Require that whenever \(s_b:=\sum_a x_{ab}>0\), every \(a\) with \((a,b)\in E\) is fully matched, \(\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}=\mu_a\). First maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), then minimize \(\sum_{a,b}\gamma_{ab}x_{ab}\) among maximizers.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite supports of applicant and slot types, with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\). Masses need not sum to one, and the two total masses may differ. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; here \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input also includes acceptable edges \(E\subseteq A\times B\) and binary rational costs \(\gamma_{ab}\geq0\) for \((a,b)\in E\). Write \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation has real entries \(x_{ab}\geq0\), with \(x_{ab}=0\) off \(E\), \(\sum_bx_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), and \(\sum_ax_{ab}\leq\nu_b\). Unmatched applicant mass and unused slot mass are allowed, including the zero allocation.
It is envy-free if, for every \(b\in B\), \(\sum_ax_{ab}>0\) implies \(\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}=\mu_a\) for every \(a\) with \((a,b)\in E\). Costs affect only the objective.
The objective is first to maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), then to minimize \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E}\gamma_{ab}x_{ab}\) among maximizers; any optimum is acceptable.

Is this continuous lexicographic optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete bipartite matchings, where envy-freeness means that no unmatched applicant is adjacent to a matched slot, maximum cardinality and minimum cost among maximum-cardinality envy-free matchings are computable in polynomial time. [Aigner-Horev and Segal-Halevi](https://arxiv.org/html/1901.09527v6)
No result settling this precise continuous mass formulation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Zero-mass types are omitted from both supports; the support size counts applicant and slot types together.
  • Masses are unnormalized and their totals may differ, preserving the discrete model's allowance for unequal bipartition sizes.
  • Allocation entries are real divisible masses; input masses and costs are binary rationals.
  • No tie-breaking is imposed: any lexicographic optimum is acceptable.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). One ordinary maximum flow identifies a subgraph containing every envy-free allocation. On that subgraph, the maximum-mass envy-free allocations are exactly the feasible allocations saturating every remaining applicant type, so the cost objective becomes a rational linear program.

The proof below is self-contained. It extends the partition principle in the cited [discrete matching result](https://arxiv.org/html/1901.09527v6) directly to masses, without expanding types into individual applicants or slots.

1. Constructing the partition. For any allocation \(z\), write \(z_a=\sum_bz_{ab}\) and \(z_b=\sum_az_{ab}\). For subsets \(U\subseteq A\) and \(T\subseteq B\), write \(\mu(U)=\sum_{a\in U}\mu_a\), \(\nu(T)=\sum_{b\in T}\nu_b\), and \(z(U,T)=\sum_{a\in U,b\in T}z_{ab}\). Entries off \(E\) are understood to be zero.

Let \(M=\mu(A)\) and \(C=M+1\). Construct the directed network with vertices \(s,A,B,t\) and arcs:

  • \(s\to a\), of capacity \(\mu_a\), for each \(a\in A\);
  • \(a\to b\), of capacity \(C\), for each \((a,b)\in E\);
  • \(b\to t\), of capacity \(\nu_b\), for each \(b\in B\).

Compute an ordinary maximum flow, and denote its applicant-to-slot entries by \(f_{ab}\). Flow conservation makes \(f\) a feasible allocation, without any envy-freeness requirement.

Every middle arc has strictly positive forward residual capacity: \(f_{ab}\leq M<C\).

Define a directed graph \(H\) on \(A\cup B\) containing:

  • \(a\to b\) for every \((a,b)\in E\);
  • \(b\to a\) whenever \(f_{ab}>0\).

Let \(A_0=\{a\in A:f_a<\mu_a\}\). Let \(A_R\) and \(B_R\) be the applicant and slot vertices reachable in \(H\) from \(A_0\), including the starting vertices. Define the remaining sets by \(A_G=A\setminus A_R\) and \(B_G=B\setminus B_R\).

The following properties hold.

  • There are no acceptable edges from \(A_R\) to \(B_G\). Every acceptable edge is a forward arc of \(H\), so its slot endpoint is reachable whenever its applicant endpoint is reachable.
  • No positive \(f\)-flow goes from \(A_G\) to \(B_R\). If \(f_{ab}>0\) and \(b\in B_R\), the reverse arc \(b\to a\) makes \(a\) reachable.
  • Every applicant in \(A_G\) is saturated by \(f\). Otherwise it would belong to \(A_0\subseteq A_R\).
  • Every slot in \(B_R\) is saturated by \(f\). Suppose \(b\in B_R\) and \(f_b<\nu_b\). A simple path in \(H\) from some \(a_0\in A_0\) to \(b\), preceded by \(s\to a_0\) and followed by \(b\to t\), is a residual augmenting path. The source and sink arcs have positive residual capacity by assumption; every forward middle arc does because its capacity is \(C>M\); and every reverse middle arc does by its definition in \(H\). Augmenting by the minimum of these finitely many positive residual capacities would increase the flow value, contradicting maximality.

In particular, \(f\) restricted to \(A_G\times B_G\) saturates every applicant in \(A_G\).

2. The exclusion lemma. We prove the crucial statement:

Every envy-free allocation uses only edges in \(A_G\times B_G\).

Let \(y\) be any envy-free allocation, with arbitrary real entries. Define

\(T=\{b\in B_R:y_b>0\}\)

and

\(U=\{a\in A_R:\text{there exists }b\in T\text{ with }(a,b)\in E\}\).

We will show that \(T\) is empty.

Every \(a\in U\) is adjacent to a slot used by \(y\), so envy-freeness implies \(y_a=\mu_a\). Property 1 says that applicants in \(A_R\) have no acceptable edges to \(B_G\). Moreover, every slot in \(B_R\setminus T\) has zero \(y\)-allocation. Thus all allocation from \(U\) goes to \(T\), giving

\(y(U,T)=\mu(U)\).

Every slot in \(T\) is saturated by \(f\), by Property 4. Property 2 implies that all its incoming \(f\)-flow comes from \(A_R\), and any applicant in \(A_R\) adjacent to \(T\) belongs to \(U\). Consequently,

\(f(U,T)=\nu(T)\).

Combining feasibility of \(y\) and \(f\), we obtain

\(\mu(U)=y(U,T)\leq y(A,T)\leq\nu(T)=f(U,T)\leq f(U,B)\leq\mu(U)\).

All inequalities are therefore equalities. In particular:

  • \(f_a=\mu_a\) for every \(a\in U\), since the nonnegative applicant slacks sum to zero;
  • \(f(U,B\setminus T)=0\), since \(f(U,T)=f(U,B)\).

Hence \(U\cap A_0=\varnothing\).

Suppose now that \(T\neq\varnothing\). Choose a path in \(H\) from \(A_0\) to a vertex of \(T\). Every vertex on the path belongs to \(A_R\cup B_R\). Its starting vertex lies outside \(U\cup T\), because \(U\cap A_0=\varnothing\). Consider the first entry of this path into \(U\cup T\).

There are two possibilities.

  • The path first enters at \(b\in T\). Its preceding vertex is some \(a\in A_R\setminus U\), and the path arc \(a\to b\) is an acceptable edge. This contradicts the definition of \(U\).
  • The path first enters at \(a\in U\). Its preceding vertex is some \(b\in B_R\setminus T\), and the path arc \(b\to a\) requires \(f_{ab}>0\). This contradicts \(f(U,B\setminus T)=0\).

Both possibilities are impossible. Thus \(T=\varnothing\): every slot in \(B_R\) is unused by \(y\).

Finally, Property 1 says that applicants in \(A_R\) can allocate only to \(B_R\). Since those slots are unused, every applicant in \(A_R\) also receives zero allocation. This proves the lemma.

3. Characterizing all maximum-mass envy-free allocations. By the exclusion lemma, every envy-free allocation satisfies

\(\sum_{a,b}y_{ab}\leq\mu(A_G)\).

Conversely, restrict \(f\) to \(A_G\times B_G\), setting all other entries to zero. Properties 2 and 3 imply that this restriction saturates every applicant in \(A_G\).

This restriction is envy-free: every used slot belongs to \(B_G\), and Property 1 ensures that all its acceptable applicant neighbors belong to \(A_G\), where every applicant is saturated. Therefore the maximum envy-free allocated mass is exactly

\(M_{\mathrm{EF}}=\mu(A_G)\).

More precisely, the maximum-mass envy-free allocations are exactly the allocations satisfying all three conditions:

  • they are supported on \(E_G=E\cap(A_G\times B_G)\);
  • every applicant in \(A_G\) is saturated;
  • every slot in \(B_G\) respects its capacity.

For necessity, the exclusion lemma gives the support restriction. Attaining total mass \(\mu(A_G)\) then forces every applicant slack in \(A_G\) to vanish. For sufficiency, the same neighbor argument used above proves envy-freeness, and the total mass is \(\mu(A_G)\).

Thus the primary objective has been completely resolved.

4. Minimizing cost among maximizers. Solve the following linear program, with variables \(x_{ab}\) for \((a,b)\in E_G\):

Minimize \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E_G}\gamma_{ab}x_{ab}\), subject to

  • \(\sum_{b:(a,b)\in E_G}x_{ab}=\mu_a\) for every \(a\in A_G\);
  • \(\sum_{a:(a,b)\in E_G}x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\) for every \(b\in B_G\);
  • \(x_{ab}\geq0\) for every \((a,b)\in E_G\).

The restriction of \(f\) supplies a feasible point. The feasible set is bounded, since each variable is bounded by its applicant mass, so a minimum exists. Section 3 proves that its feasible points, extended by zero outside \(E_G\), are exactly the primary-objective maximizers. Hence any optimum of this linear program is a lexicographic optimum of the original problem.

5. Polynomial bit complexity. The initial network has \(\tau+2\) vertices and \(m+\tau\) arcs. Its capacities have polynomial encoding length.

For an explicit arithmetic bound, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all applicant and slot masses as encoded in the input. Then \(\log D=O(L)\). Multiplying every capacity by \(D\) produces integer capacities of bit length \(O(L+\log(\tau+1))\), including the middle capacity \(DC=D+D\mu(A)\).

The shortest-augmenting-path maximum-flow algorithm uses a number of arithmetic operations polynomial in the vertex and arc counts, independently of the numerical capacities. On the scaled network, all residual quantities remain integers of polynomial bit length. Thus this step has polynomial bit complexity. See [Edmonds and Karp](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~pettie/matching/Edmonds-Karp-network-flow.pdf). Reachability in \(H\) then takes \(O(m+\tau)\) graph operations.

The cost linear program has at most \(m\) variables, at most \(\tau\) applicant and slot constraints, and rational coefficients of polynomial total encoding length. The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore applies: it computes an exact rational optimum in polynomial bit time. See [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5239).

No expanded population is constructed. Empty supports, isolated types, unequal total masses, and a zero optimum are all covered by the proof. In particular, when \(A_G=\varnothing\), the unique maximum-mass allocation is the zero allocation.
