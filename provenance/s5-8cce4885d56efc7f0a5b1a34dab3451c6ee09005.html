The continuous mirror question, as graded

Input supported left types \(I\) and right types \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j>0\), strict rankings over partner types, and rational matching costs \(c_{ij}\). A feasible allocation is a flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\); unmatched mass ranks below every partner type. For each type, interpret its allocation as a lottery over partner types and being unmatched, with partner probabilities \(x_{ij}/\mu_i\) on the left and \(x_{ij}/\nu_j\) on the right. Compare \(x\) and any feasible rival \(y\) by independently drawing from those lotteries and summing the expected vote margins, weighted by type masses. Compute a popular feasible \(x\) of minimum cost \(\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}x_{ij}\), where popular means its expected margin against every feasible \(y\) is nonnegative.

Let \(I,J\) be disjoint nonempty sets of left and right types, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\); each type strictly ranks its acceptable partner types above being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\).
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), each a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Normalize \(\sum_i\mu_i+\sum_j\nu_j=1\), allowing unequal side totals. Put \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including rational costs \(c_{ij}\in\mathbb Q\).
A feasible allocation has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\nu_j\). There is no half-integrality restriction.
For left type \(i\), define \(p_i^x(j)=x_{ij}/\mu_i\) and \(p_i^x(\bot)=1-\sum_jx_{ij}/\mu_i\); define right-type lotteries analogously using \(\nu_j\).
Write \(s_i=\mu_i\), \(s_j=\nu_j\), and let \(v_u(a,b)\) equal \(1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) according as type \(u\) prefers \(a\), prefers \(b\), or \(a=b\). For independent draws from the two lotteries, define \(\Delta(x,y)=\sum_{u\in I\cup J}s_u\sum_{a,b}p_u^x(a)p_u^y(b)v_u(a,b)\), where outcomes include \(\bot\).
An allocation \(x\) is popular if \(\Delta(x,y)\geq0\) for every feasible \(y\). Its cost is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}x_{ij}\); unmatched mass costs zero.

Result

Computing a minimum-cost popular feasible allocation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Popular allocations always exist, and their set has a polynomial-size rational extended linear formulation.

The proof uses LP duality and the finite minimax theorem. It requires neither equal side totals nor half-integrality.

1. Expressing a challenger’s advantage

Let \(P\) be the feasible allocation polytope. It is nonempty because \(0\in P\), and compact because every coordinate is nonnegative and bounded by its incident capacities.

For a supported type \(u\), write \(N(u)\) for its acceptable partners, \(x_{uv}\) for its incident allocation, and define

\(d_u(x)=\sum_{v\in N(u)}x_{uv}\), and \(X=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\).

Each allocated edge contributes to the matched mass of both endpoints, so

\(\sum_u d_u(x)=2X\).

Fix \(x\in P\). For an outcome \(b\in N(u)\cup\{\bot\}\), define its expected vote against the lottery under \(x\) by

\(h_u^x(b)=\sum_{a\in N(u)\cup\{\bot\}}p_u^x(a)v_u(b,a)\).

Every acceptable partner is preferred to being unmatched. Consequently,

\(h_u^x(\bot)=-d_u(x)/s_u\),

and, for \(v\in N(u)\),

\(h_u^x(v)=1-d_u(x)/s_u+\sum_{w\in N(u)}(x_{uw}/s_u)v_u(v,w)\).

Subtracting gives

\(h_u^x(v)-h_u^x(\bot)=1+\sum_{w\in N(u)}(x_{uw}/s_u)v_u(v,w)\).

For another feasible allocation \(y\), its advantage over \(x\) is therefore

\(\Delta(y,x)=\sum_u\left[s_u h_u^x(\bot)+\sum_{v\in N(u)}y_{uv}\bigl(h_u^x(v)-h_u^x(\bot)\bigr)\right]\).

For each edge \((i,j)\in E\), define the affine function

\(b_{ij}(x)=2+\sum_{k\in N(i)}(x_{ik}/\mu_i)v_i(j,k)+\sum_{\ell\in N(j)}(x_{\ell j}/\nu_j)v_j(i,\ell)\).

Collecting the two endpoint contributions of each edge yields

\(\Delta(y,x)=-2X+\sum_{(i,j)\in E}b_{ij}(x)y_{ij}\). (1)

Also, \(v_u(a,b)=-v_u(b,a)\), so exchanging the two lottery draws gives

\(\Delta(x,y)=-\Delta(y,x)\), and \(\Delta(x,x)=0\).

Thus \(x\) is popular exactly when

\(\max_{y\in P}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}b_{ij}(x)y_{ij}\leq 2X\). (2)

2. A compact linear formulation

For fixed \(x\), the maximum in (2) is the following capacitated matching LP:

  • Maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}b_{ij}(x)y_{ij}\).
  • Require \(y_{ij}\geq0\).
  • Require \(\sum_{j\in N(i)}y_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) for every \(i\in I\).
  • Require \(\sum_{i\in N(j)}y_{ij}\leq\nu_j\) for every \(j\in J\).

Its dual is:

  • Minimize \(\sum_{i\in I}\mu_i\alpha_i+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_j\alpha_j\).
  • Require \(\alpha_u\geq0\) for every supported type.
  • Require \(\alpha_i+\alpha_j\geq b_{ij}(x)\) for every \((i,j)\in E\).

The primal is feasible and has a finite attained optimum, since its feasible region is \(P\). LP strong duality therefore applies and supplies an attained dual optimum.

It follows that \(x\in P\) is popular if and only if there are nonnegative variables \(\alpha_u\) satisfying

\(\alpha_i+\alpha_j\geq b_{ij}(x)\qquad((i,j)\in E)\),

and

\(\sum_{i\in I}\mu_i\alpha_i+\sum_{j\in J}\nu_j\alpha_j\leq2\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\). (3)

For clarity, both directions follow directly:

  • If these inequalities hold, weak duality bounds every challenger’s objective by the left side of (3), hence by \(2X\). Equation (1) then gives \(\Delta(y,x)\leq0\) for every \(y\in P\).
  • If \(x\) is popular, (2) holds. An optimal dual solution has objective equal to that maximum, so it satisfies (3).

All these constraints are linear in \((x,\alpha)\).

We may additionally impose \(\alpha_u\leq4\). Indeed, each sum appearing in \(b_{ij}(x)-2\) has absolute value at most \(1\), because incident allocation probabilities sum to at most \(1\). Hence

\(0\leq b_{ij}(x)\leq4\).

Given any nonnegative dual witness, replace each \(\alpha_u\) by \(\min\{\alpha_u,4\}\). For an edge whose endpoint values are unchanged, its inequality remains valid. For any other edge, a capped endpoint contributes \(4\), which alone is at least \(b_{ij}(x)\). The weighted objective can only decrease. Thus the additional upper bounds preserve exactly the popular allocations.

This is a capacitated version of the LP-duality formulation for popular fractional matchings described in [Kavitha (2016), Section 2](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol055-icalp2016/LIPIcs.ICALP.2016.22/LIPIcs.ICALP.2016.22.pdf); the calculation above establishes it directly for the specified mass model.

3. Popular allocations always exist

We verify existence independently; the LP formulation alone does not yet establish its feasibility.

Because \(P\) is a nonempty bounded polytope, it is the convex hull of finitely many vertices \(z^1,\ldots,z^r\). Define the finite matrix

\(A_{ab}=\Delta(z^a,z^b)\).

Antisymmetry gives \(A^\mathsf{T}=-A\).

Let \(\mathcal S_r=\{p\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^r:\sum_a p_a=1\}\). The finite minimax theorem states that, for any finite real payoff matrix,

\(\max_{p\in\mathcal S_r}\min_{q\in\mathcal S_r}p^\mathsf{T}Aq=\min_{q\in\mathcal S_r}\max_{p\in\mathcal S_r}p^\mathsf{T}Aq\),

with optimal strategies attained. Its hypotheses hold here: \(A\) is a finite real matrix, and both strategy sets are finite-dimensional probability simplexes.

For every \(p\), skew-symmetry gives \(p^\mathsf{T}Ap=0\). Taking \(q=p\) shows that the left-hand minimax value is at most \(0\). Likewise, taking \(p=q\) shows that the right-hand value is at least \(0\). Their equality therefore implies that the game value is \(0\).

Choose an optimal maximizing strategy \(p^*\), and put

\(x^*=\sum_a p_a^*z^a\in P\).

The map from allocations to each type’s lottery is affine, and the vote expression is linear in each lottery separately. Therefore, for \(y=\sum_b q_bz^b\),

\(\Delta(x^*,y)=\sum_{a,b}p_a^*q_b\Delta(z^a,z^b)=(p^*)^\mathsf{T}Aq\geq0\).

Every \(y\in P\) has such a representation. Thus \(x^*\) is popular, proving existence.

The vertex enumeration in this argument is only an existence proof; the algorithm does not perform it.

4. Exact polynomial-time optimization

Solve the rational LP with variables \(x_{ij}\) and \(\alpha_u\), objective

\(\min\sum_{(i,j)\in E}c_{ij}x_{ij}\),

and the following constraints:

  • All feasibility constraints defining \(P\).
  • \(0\leq\alpha_u\leq4\) for every supported type.
  • \(\alpha_i+\alpha_j\geq b_{ij}(x)\) for every acceptable edge.
  • Inequality (3).

Section 2 proves that its projection onto the allocation coordinates is exactly the set of popular feasible allocations. Section 3 proves that it is nonempty. The bounds on \(x\) and \(\alpha\) make its feasible region compact, so the minimum is attained.

There are \(m+\tau\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. Explicitly expanding the affine edge expressions uses at most \(O(m^2)\) coefficient occurrences. Every coefficient is rational, obtained from input masses, their reciprocals, preference signs, and fixed integers. Since every supported mass is strictly positive, all reciprocals are defined and have polynomial binary encoding length. The complete LP therefore has encoding length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming consequently computes an exact rational optimal solution in polynomial time. Its allocation component is a minimum-cost popular feasible allocation for the original problem.
