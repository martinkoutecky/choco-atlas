The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a common societal axis, voter types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), single-peaked type profiles, misrepresentations \(r_t(c)\), integer \(k\), and threshold \(\rho\), decide whether there exist \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) and \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{c\in W}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for every \(c\in W\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in W}r_t(c)x_{t,c}\le \rho\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A type profile consists of a finite candidate set \(C\) with a common linear axis \(\prec\), finite types \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each type has a \(\prec\)-single-peaked ranking, and its rational misrepresentation function \(r_t\) is compatible with that ranking.

Given \(k\) and \(\rho\), does there exist \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) and fractional assignments \(x_{t,c}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_{c\in W}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for every \(c\in W\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in W}r_t(c)x_{t,c}\le\rho\)?

Target. Is this continuous single-peaked Monroe winner-determination problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

Known results. The discrete analogue is NP-hard for single-peaked profiles and integer-valued misrepresentation functions, even when every winner represents exactly three voters. That result uses integral voter-to-winner assignments rather than fractional mass assignments.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The stated discrete hardness result transfers because, for a fixed winner set, fractional assignments with integer supply and capacity have an integral optimum.

We use the stated hardness result as a premise and prove the required integrality property explicitly.

1. Integrality of assignments.

Let \(I,J\) be finite sets, with nonnegative integer supplies \(a_i\) and capacities \(b_j\). Suppose a nonnegative real matrix \(y\) satisfies
\( \sum_{j\in J}y_{i,j}=a_i \) and \( \sum_{i\in I}y_{i,j}=b_j \).
For arbitrary real costs \(d_{i,j}\), there exists a nonnegative integer matrix \(z\) with the same margins and with
\( \sum_{i,j}d_{i,j}z_{i,j}\leq\sum_{i,j}d_{i,j}y_{i,j} \).

To prove this, form a bipartite graph on \(I\) and \(J\), including precisely those edges whose matrix entries are noninteger. Every vertex incident to an edge has degree at least two: otherwise its prescribed integer margin would be a sum of integers and exactly one noninteger, which is impossible.

If this graph is nonempty, it therefore contains a cycle. Since the graph is bipartite, the cycle has even length. Alternately increase and decrease the entries around this cycle by a common amount \(\varepsilon\). This preserves every margin. There are two choices of direction, and their changes in cost are opposites, so choose a direction whose cost change is nonpositive.

For each increased entry, consider its distance to the next larger integer; for each decreased entry, consider its distance to the next smaller integer. All these distances are strictly positive. Choose \(\varepsilon\) to be their minimum. No entry becomes negative, at least one becomes integer, and every entry previously integer remains unchanged. Thus the number of noninteger entries strictly decreases without increasing cost.

Repeating finitely many times produces the required integer matrix. This proves the claim.

2. Reduction from the stated discrete hard problem.

Take an instance of the stated \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard discrete restriction. Write its explicitly listed voters as \(V\), with \(n=|V|=3k\). Its question is whether one can select \(k\) winners and assign each voter to one winner, with exactly three voters per winner and total misrepresentation at most \(B\).

Construct the continuous instance as follows:

  • Keep the same candidates, axis, rankings, misrepresentation functions, and committee size \(k\).
  • Introduce one type \(t_v\) for each voter \(v\), with mass \(\mu_{t_v}=1/n\).
  • Set \(\rho=B/n\).

All single-peakedness and ranking-compatibility conditions are preserved because neither rankings nor misrepresentation functions change. Distinct type labels may have identical data; alternatively, identical types can be combined by adding their masses.

The construction is polynomial in the explicitly represented discrete input: it introduces one type per voter, and its new rational numbers have polynomial encoding length.

For the forward implication, take a feasible discrete assignment and give mass \(1/n\) from each type to its assigned winner. Every winner receives \(3/n=1/k\), and the continuous cost is the discrete cost divided by \(n\), hence at most \(B/n\).

For the reverse implication, suppose the constructed continuous instance has a feasible committee \(W\) and assignments \(x_{t_v,c}\). Define
\( y_{v,c}=n x_{t_v,c} \).
Then, for every voter and every selected winner, respectively,
\( \sum_{c\in W}y_{v,c}=1 \) and \( \sum_{v\in V}y_{v,c}=n/k=3 \).
Its total cost satisfies
\( \sum_{v\in V}\sum_{c\in W}r_v(c)y_{v,c}\leq B \).

Apply the integrality claim with row margins one, column margins three, and costs \(r_v(c)\). It gives an integer matrix \(z\) with the same margins and cost at most \(B\). Nonnegativity and row sum one imply that every row contains exactly one entry equal to one and all other entries zero. Thus \(z\) assigns each voter to exactly one winner, and every winner receives exactly three voters.

This is a feasible discrete solution. The reduction therefore proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Assume \(1\leq k\leq |C|\), since selecting a committee is impossible when \(k>|C|\), and the stated capacities require positive \(k\).

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), with positive integer denominator \(q_t\), and choose
\( D=k\prod_{t\in T}q_t \).
Its binary encoding length is polynomial in the input length because
\( \log D=\log k+\sum_{t\in T}\log q_t \).

Suppose the instance is feasible, with committee \(W\) and assignments \(x\). The scaled matrix \(y=Dx\) has integer row margins \(D\mu_t\) and integer column margins \(D/k\). By the integrality claim, there exists an integer matrix \(z\) with these margins and
\( \sum_{t,c}r_t(c)z_{t,c}\leq D\rho \).

Every entry satisfies \(0\leq z_{t,c}\leq D\), since the sum of all entries is \(D\). Consequently, \(W\) and the integers \(z_{t,c}\) form a polynomial-size certificate. A verifier checks nonnegativity, all margins, and the cost inequality using exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time. Conversely, any certificate passing these checks yields a feasible assignment \(x=z/D\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
