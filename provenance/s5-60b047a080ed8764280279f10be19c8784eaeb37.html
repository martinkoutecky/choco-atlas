The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite bipartite conflict graph \(H=(U,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), integer profits \(p_\theta(u)\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_u\), and a threshold \(q\), decide whether there are masses \(x_{\theta,S} \ge 0\) over independent sets \(S\) of \(H\) such that \(\sum_S x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,S:\,u\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_u\), and \(x_{\theta,S}>0\) only if \(\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\ge q\). Equivalently, maximize the minimum utility guarantee \(q\) while each unit of population mass receives an integral, conflict-free bundle.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H=(U,F)\) be a finite simple undirected bipartite conflict graph, with \(m=|U|\ge 1\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\ge 1\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_\theta\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an explicitly listed additive profit function \(p_\theta:U\to\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\). Inputs also include supplies \(b_u\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) per unit population and a threshold \(q\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), all encoded in binary; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Writing \(\mathcal I(H)\) for the independent sets of \(H\), feasibility means that real masses \(x_{\theta,S}\ge 0\) exist satisfying \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal I(H)}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_\theta\sum_{S\in\mathcal I(H):u\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_u\), and \(x_{\theta,S}>0\Rightarrow\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\ge q\).
Each individual receives a subset of items, so an item occurs at most once in a bundle; supplies may remain unused.

Is this feasibility problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete agents receiving pairwise disjoint independent bundles, the decision problem is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete on bipartite graphs for every fixed \(k\ge 2\), even with identical profits. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.11313)
Exact configuration-LP feasibility for restricted Santa Claus allocation is already \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; its conflict-free formulation is a special case of the mass model above after population normalization. [Source](https://doi.org/10.4230/OASIcs.SOSA.2018.11)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the unspecified integer profits to nonnegative integers: the discrete definitions and constructions permit zero profits.
  • Made the threshold a positive binary integer, matching the discrete decision problem.
  • Made supplies nonnegative binary rationals, without imposing a total-supply normalization.
  • Specified real allocation masses, explicit profit tables, and total binary input bit length L.
  • Required a nonempty item set and positive support size; imposed no lower bound of two on support size because types are not discrete agents.

Result

The feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when the graph is edgeless, there is only one type, and every item has supply \(1/2\). In fact, the general problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Thus an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. A direct reduction from PARTITION

We use the classical \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), encoded in binary, decide whether some index set \(T\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in T}a_i=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i.\)

The standard hardness result is due to [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Write \(A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\). Construct the following instance of the mass feasibility problem:

  • \(U=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}\) and \(F=\varnothing\);
  • one type \(\theta\), with \(\mu_\theta=1\);
  • \(p_\theta(u_i)=2a_i\) for every \(i\);
  • \(b_{u_i}=1/2\) for every \(i\);
  • \(q=A\).

The graph is bipartite, and every subset of \(U\) is independent. All profits are positive integers, the threshold is a positive integer, and all masses and supplies satisfy the required encoding conditions. Doubling the integers and computing their sum take polynomial time and produce polynomially many bits.

For brevity, write \(p(S)=\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\) and \(x_S=x_{\theta,S}\).

A partition produces a feasible mass assignment

Suppose \(T\) solves PARTITION, and let \(S=\{u_i:i\in T\}\). Then

\(\displaystyle p(S)=2\sum_{i\in T}a_i=A=q,\qquad p(U\setminus S)=2A-A=A=q.\)

Assign mass \(1/2\) to \(S\) and mass \(1/2\) to \(U\setminus S\), with every other bundle receiving zero mass.

The total mass is \(1\). Each item belongs to exactly one of these two bundles, so its consumption is exactly \(1/2\), equal to its supply. Both bundles meet the threshold. Hence the constructed instance is feasible.

A feasible mass assignment produces a partition

Conversely, suppose the constructed instance is feasible. Define the consumption of item \(u_i\) by

\(\displaystyle c_i=\sum_{S:u_i\in S}x_S.\)

The supply constraints give \(c_i\le 1/2\). Since every bundle with positive mass has profit at least \(A\), and the total mass is \(1\),

\(\displaystyle A=A\sum_Sx_S\le\sum_Sx_Sp(S).\)

Interchanging the two finite sums and applying the supply bounds gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_Sx_Sp(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}2a_i c_i\le\sum_{i=1}^{n}2a_i\frac12=A.\)

Consequently, equality holds throughout. In particular,

\(\displaystyle \sum_Sx_S\bigl(p(S)-A\bigr)=0.\)

Every summand is nonnegative: if \(x_S>0\), feasibility gives \(p(S)\ge A\), while if \(x_S=0\), the summand is zero. Therefore every bundle with positive mass satisfies \(p(S)=A\).

At least one such bundle exists because \(\sum_Sx_S=1\). Choose one and put \(T=\{i:u_i\in S\}\). Then

\(\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\in T}a_i=p(S)=A,\)

so \(T\) solves PARTITION.

This argument also handles odd \(A\): all bundle profits are even, so equality with odd \(A\) is impossible, as required.

We have therefore proved a polynomial-time many-one reduction, establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness with \(\tau=1\).

2. Polynomial-size certificates

Although feasibility allows arbitrary real masses and exponentially many possible bundles, every feasible instance has a polynomial-size rational certificate.

Call \((\theta,S)\) an admissible configuration if \(S\) is independent and \(\sum_{u\in S}p_\theta(u)\ge q\). Only admissible configurations need variables.

Introduce one nonnegative slack variable \(s_u\) per item. The constraints become

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S:(\theta,S)\text{ admissible}}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\)

for each type, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\theta,S:u\in S}x_{\theta,S}+s_u=b_u\)

for each item.

Write this finite system as \(Cy=d\), \(y\ge0\). It has \(r=\tau+m\) rows, every entry of \(C\) is either zero or one, and \(d\) consists of the input masses and supplies.

A feasible solution uses at most \(r\) positive variables

Among all feasible solutions, choose one whose positive support \(J\) has minimum cardinality. Such a choice exists because the system has finitely many variables.

The columns indexed by \(J\) are linearly independent. Otherwise, there would be a nonzero vector \(h\), supported on \(J\), with \(Ch=0\). Replacing \(h\) by \(-h\) if necessary, assume that some \(h_j>0\), and define

\(\displaystyle t=\min_{j\in J:h_j>0}\frac{y_j}{h_j}>0.\)

Then \(y-th\) remains nonnegative and satisfies \(C(y-th)=d\). At least one positive coordinate becomes zero, and no zero coordinate becomes positive because \(h\) is supported on \(J\). This contradicts minimality.

Thus \(k=|J|\le r\). In particular, there are at most \(r\) positive configuration variables.

The masses have polynomial bit length

Select \(k\) rows on which these independent columns form an invertible \(k\times k\) matrix \(B\). Its entries are zero or one, and

\(\displaystyle B y_J=d_R,\)

where \(d_R\) is the corresponding subvector of \(d\).

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all entries of \(d\). Because those denominators are part of the input, \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits, and \(Dd_R\) is an integer vector whose entries have \(O(L)\) bits.

Let \(B_i\) be obtained from \(B\) by replacing column \(i\) with \(Dd_R\). Cramer’s rule gives

\(\displaystyle y_i=\frac{\det(B_i)}{D\det(B)}.\)

The determinant expansion yields \(|\det(B)|\le k!\). If \(M=\max(1,\max_j|(Dd_R)_j|)\), it also yields \(|\det(B_i)|\le k!M\), since each term uses exactly one entry from the replaced column. Hence every mass has \(O(L+k\log(k+1))\) bits.

A certificate therefore lists at most \(m+\tau\) configurations, each specified by its type and an \(m\)-bit bundle indicator, together with rational masses of polynomial bit length. A verifier checks independence, profit thresholds, type totals, and supply inequalities using exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time.

This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

3. Conclusion

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The decisive constraint in the reduction is the threshold on each bundle with positive mass: the supplies cap average profit at that same threshold, forcing every used bundle to have exactly the partition target profit.

No transfer of integral allocation hardness to fractional masses is needed; the reduction directly proves hardness for the stated mass model.
