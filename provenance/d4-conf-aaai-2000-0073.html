The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a decision tree \(D\), outcomes \(O\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete utility profiles \(u^t\in[0,1]^O\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), choose an outcome \(o\) and threshold \(s\) for one common question. For each answer group \(A_-=\{t:u_o^t<s\}\) and \(A_+=\{t:u_o^t>s\}\), choose a strategy maximizing that group's total expected utility, and maximize the resulting value \(W_1(o,s)-W_0\), where \(W_1(o,s)=\sum_{a\in\{-,+\}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{t\in A_a}\mu_t\sum_{z\in O}P_D(z\mid\pi)u_z^t\) and \(W_0=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{z\in O}P_D(z\mid\pi)u_z^t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: an explicitly represented finite decision tree \(D\) with \(n\) nodes, decision nodes, chance nodes whose outgoing probabilities are nonnegative binary rationals summing to \(1\), and leaves labelled by outcomes in \(O\), where \(m=|O|\geq2\). A strategy \(\pi\in\Pi\) selects one outgoing edge at every decision node; \(P_D(z\mid\pi)\) is its induced outcome probability.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) distinct complete utility profiles \(u^t\in([0,1]\cap\mathbb Q)^O\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. All profiles use common best and worst reference outcomes with utilities \(1\) and \(0\); these references need not occur among the leaves. No independence of utility coordinates is assumed. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
One common, cost-free standard-gamble question chooses \(o\in O\) and rational \(s\in(0,1)\) with \(s\ne u_o^t\) for every \(t\). Its answer groups are \(A_- =\{t:u_o^t<s\}\) and \(A_+=\{t:u_o^t>s\}\). Each group receives its own strategy, common to all its types; an empty group contributes \(0\).
Define \(V(A)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\sum_{z\in O}P_D(z\mid\pi)u_z^t\), \(W_0=V(T)\), and \(W_1(o,s)=V(A_-)+V(A_+)\). The optimization problem is to maximize \(W_1(o,s)-W_0\); its decision version additionally takes a binary-rational threshold \(b\).

Is deciding whether some admissible \((o,s)\) satisfies \(W_1(o,s)-W_0\geq b\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,n,L\).

The verified anchor is an intersection-point characterization for independent utility coordinates under a continuous density, rather than a discrete complexity classification: a highest-value split can be chosen at an intersection of viable strategies' expected-utility functions. [Chajewska, Koller and Parr (2000)](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-056.pdf).

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). The optimization problem can also be solved exactly in polynomial time, including recovery of an optimal admissible question and its two strategies.

An explicit bit-operation bound is
\[ O\!\left(\left[m\tau\log(\tau+1)+m^2\tau+mn(\tau+1)\right]L^2\right). \]
The proof uses the explicitly represented tree and support; it requires no independence of utility coordinates.

1. Computing the value of one answer group

For any group \(A\subseteq T\), define its aggregate utility vector by
\[ g_A(z)=\sum_{t\in A}\mu_tu_z^t,\qquad z\in O. \]
For every strategy \(\pi\), rearranging the finite sums gives
\[ \sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\sum_{z\in O}P_D(z\mid\pi)u_z^t =\sum_{z\in O}P_D(z\mid\pi)g_A(z). \]
Thus \(V(A)\) is the optimal expected utility in the same tree with leaf utilities \(g_A\). This aggregation preserves the requirement that all types in \(A\) receive one common strategy.

Let \(F_A(v)\) denote the optimal value in the subtree rooted at node \(v\). Compute it from the leaves upward:

  • At a leaf labelled \(z\), set \(F_A(v)=g_A(z)\).
  • At a decision node with children \(c\), set \(F_A(v)=\max_c F_A(c)\).
  • At a chance node with outgoing probabilities \(p_{vc}\), set \(F_A(v)=\sum_c p_{vc}F_A(c)\).

These recurrences follow by induction on subtree height. At a decision node, the strategy chooses one child and then an optimal strategy in that child’s subtree. At a chance node, every child strategy contributes with a nonnegative coefficient. Because the child subtrees have disjoint decision nodes, their maximizing strategies can be combined into a single strategy attaining the displayed sum.

Consequently, if \(r\) is the root, then \(F_A(r)=V(A)\). The tree has \(n-1\) edges, so this calculation takes \(O(n)\) arithmetic operations. Recording maximizing children recovers an optimal strategy. The same recurrence gives \(V(\varnothing)=0\).

2. Only finitely many thresholds need consideration

Fix an outcome \(o\). Sort and deduplicate
\[ S_o=\{0,1\}\cup\{u_o^t:t\in T\} \]
to obtain \(a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_k\), where \(a_0=0\) and \(a_k=1\).

Every admissible threshold belongs to exactly one interval \((a_i,a_{i+1})\). Throughout that interval, its answer groups are
\[ A_-=\{t:u_o^t\leq a_i\}, \qquad A_+=T\setminus A_-. \]
Indeed, no supported coordinate value lies strictly between the two adjacent boundaries. Therefore \(W_1(o,s)\) is constant on each interval.

Choose the representative
\[ s_i=\frac{a_i+a_{i+1}}2. \]
It is rational, lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), and differs from every \(u_o^t\). Its encoding has \(O(L)\) bits.

There are at most \(\tau+2\) distinct boundaries and hence at most \(\tau+1\) representatives for each outcome. Thus at most \(m(\tau+1)\) questions suffice to attain the optimum. This also handles coordinates taking only endpoint values and questions with an empty answer group.

3. Exact optimization algorithm

First compute all weighted utility entries \(\mu_tu_z^t\), their totals \(g_T(z)\), and the baseline \(W_0=V(T)\).

For each outcome \(o\):

  • Sort the types by \(u_o^t\), keeping equal values together.
  • Sweep the adjacent boundary intervals from Section 2. Maintain the vectors \(g_{A_-}\) and \(g_{A_+}=g_T-g_{A_-}\). At each boundary, move its entire tied block into \(A_-\).
  • For each interval, apply the tree recurrence to both vectors and evaluate \(V(A_-)+V(A_+)-W_0\).

Keep a representative attaining the largest value. Every admissible question induces a partition examined by the algorithm, and every examined representative is admissible. Hence the retained value is exactly the requested optimum.

All outcomes are examined, including outcomes absent from the leaves: their utility coordinates can still usefully separate types with different utilities for leaf outcomes.

Sorting costs \(O(m\tau\log(\tau+1))\) comparisons. During one outcome’s sweep, each type moves at most once, updating \(m\) utility coordinates. All sweeps therefore use \(O(m^2\tau)\) updates. The tree calculations use \(O(mn(\tau+1))\) arithmetic operations.

4. Polynomial bit complexity

To justify exact computation, let \(Q\) be the product of the positive denominators of all input rational occurrences, including the threshold \(b\) for the decision version. Denominators are counted separately for each encoded occurrence. Their total binary length is at most \(L\), so \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

For a fixed strategy in any subtree, its group value is a sum of terms of the form
\[ \mu_tu_z^t\prod_{e\text{ on a subtree path}}p_e. \]
Each term uses one mass occurrence, one utility occurrence, and each path edge at most once. Its denominator therefore divides \(Q\). The sum’s denominator divides \(Q\) as well. Since there are finitely many strategies, every maximizing subtree value is attained by some strategy and also has denominator dividing \(Q\).

We can consequently store every group utility and subtree value multiplied by \(Q\), as an integer.

At a chance edge with probability \(a_e/d_e\), the scaled contribution is computed as \(a_eH(c)/d_e\), where \(H(c)=QF_A(c)\). This division is exact: the denominator of \(F_A(c)\) divides the product of the mass and utility denominators and the chance-edge denominators inside the child subtree. That product excludes the parent-edge occurrence \(d_e\), so it divides \(Q/d_e\).

Every unscaled group value lies between \(0\) and \(\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\leq1\). Thus stored values lie between \(0\) and \(Q\). Intermediate multiplication results, sorting cross-products, and the scaled threshold \(Qb\) also have \(O(L)\) bits.

Elementary integer multiplication and division on these operands take \(O(L^2)\) bit operations. Constructing \(Q\) takes \(O(L^2)\) bit operations as well: successive schoolbook multiplications have total cost bounded by the square of the sum of the factors’ bit lengths. Combining this with Section 3 gives
\[ O\!\left(\left[m\tau\log(\tau+1)+m^2\tau+mn(\tau+1)\right]L^2\right). \]
Finally, compare the maximum scaled gain with the integer \(Qb\). This decides the question exactly.

5. Relation to the cited anchor

The cited paper describes bottom-up expectimax for explicit decision trees. [Chajewska, Koller and Parr (2000)](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2000/AAAI00-056.pdf). The finite-support classification proved here follows from enumerating coordinate-induced partitions and computing each group’s aggregate utility vector; it does not require a continuous density or independent coordinates.
