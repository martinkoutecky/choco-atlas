The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 4.1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a party partition, a distinguished party \(P^\star\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported complete rankings of \(C\), decide whether there is a nomination containing exactly one candidate from each party such that the nominee from \(P^\star\) is a nonunique Schulze winner. Pairwise margins are \(M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t\); path strengths use positive margins as arc weights.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set with \(m=|C|\), partitioned into \(k\) nonempty parties, and let \(P^\star\) be a distinguished party.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
A nomination \(N\subseteq C\) contains exactly one candidate from each party; all rankings are restricted to \(N\).
For distinct nominees, set \(M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t\). The directed graph on \(N\) has arc \((x,y)\) exactly when \(M_\mu(x,y)>0\), weighted by that margin.
Let \(s_N(x,y)\) be the maximum, over directed paths from \(x\) to \(y\) using only nominees, of the minimum arc weight; set it to \(0\) if no such path exists. A nominee \(p\) wins when \(s_N(p,y)\ge s_N(y,p)\) for every other nominee \(y\); co-winners are allowed, with no tie-breaking.

Is deciding whether some nomination makes the nominee of \(P^\star\) a winner solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For a discrete list of voters, this decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with parties of size at most \(2\) and any fixed odd number of voters at least \(3\).
For exactly two discrete voters, it is solvable in linear time.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass 1 and explicitly required positive binary-rational masses on distinct supported rankings.
  • Made the candidate set and every party nonempty, as required by the intended nomination model.
  • Specified strict complete rankings, paths confined to the nominated candidates, and beatpath strength 0 when no path exists.
  • Interpreted nonunique winner as allowing co-winners, without requiring an actual tie or applying tie-breaking.
  • Defined L as total input bit length and k as the number of parties; retained the unrestricted continuous question rather than imposing the anchor's hardness restrictions.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when every party has size at most \(2\), the support size satisfies \(\tau\leq 4\), and every mass is a positive integer multiple of \(1/8\).

The proof uses the three-voter hardness result supplied in the question. The construction works even if “binary-rational” means a dyadic rational.

1. Scaling margins preserves winners

Fix a nomination \(N\). Suppose two margin functions satisfy \(M'(x,y)=aM(x,y)\) for every distinct \(x,y\in N\), where \(a>0\).

The positive-margin arcs are identical. For every directed path, its minimum arc weight under \(M'\) is \(a\) times its minimum arc weight under \(M\). Consequently, taking maxima over paths gives \(s'_N(x,y)=a s_N(x,y)\). This also holds when there is no path, since both strengths are then zero.

Thus \(s'_N(p,y)\geq s'_N(y,p)\) holds exactly when \(s_N(p,y)\geq s_N(y,p)\). Scaling all margins by a positive constant preserves every winner, including co-winners.

2. Hardness with at most four distinct rankings

Take an instance of the stated \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem with exactly three discrete voters and parties of size at most \(2\). Denote their rankings by \(R_1,R_2,R_3\); these rankings need not be distinct.

For a strict ranking \(R\), define \(\epsilon_R(x,y)=1\) if \(R\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\), and \(\epsilon_R(x,y)=-1\) otherwise. Let \(\overline R\) be its reversal. Strictness and completeness imply \(\epsilon_{\overline R}(x,y)=-\epsilon_R(x,y)\).

Keep the candidates, parties, and distinguished party unchanged. Construct a weighted society from these four entries:

| Ranking | Mass |
|---|---:|
| \(R_1\) | \(3/8\) |
| \(R_2\) | \(1/4\) |
| \(R_3\) | \(1/4\) |
| \(\overline R_1\) | \(1/8\) |

Merge entries with identical rankings by adding their masses. The resulting support contains at most four distinct rankings. All masses are positive dyadic rationals, and their sum is \(1\).

For every pair of distinct candidates, the new margin is

\(\displaystyle M_\mu(x,y)=\frac38\epsilon_{R_1}(x,y)+\frac14\epsilon_{R_2}(x,y)+\frac14\epsilon_{R_3}(x,y)+\frac18\epsilon_{\overline R_1}(x,y)=\frac14\bigl(\epsilon_{R_1}(x,y)+\epsilon_{R_2}(x,y)+\epsilon_{R_3}(x,y)\bigr).\)

The parenthesized expression is exactly the unnormalized margin of the three discrete voters. Therefore every margin is scaled by \(1/4\). If the original margins are normalized by the number of voters, the scaling factor is instead \(3/4\); either convention gives the same winners.

Restricting rankings to a nomination preserves all pairwise comparisons between nominees. Hence the scaling identity holds for every nomination. By Section 1, a nomination makes its distinguished-party nominee a winner in the original instance if and only if it does so in the constructed instance.

The construction preserves party sizes. Reversing one ranking and merging at most four entries take polynomial time; a straightforward implementation uses \(O(m\log m)\) bit operations beyond reading the source instance. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, proving the claimed hardness.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate specifies one candidate from each party. Its length is \(O(k\log(m+1))\). We can check that it specifies a valid nomination and identify its distinguished-party nominee.

We next show that winner verification uses exact arithmetic in polynomial time. Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive integers encoded in binary, and set \(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t\). The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because the total bit length of all denominators is at most \(L\).

Define the integer weight \(w_t=a_t(D/b_t)\). The mass-sum assumption gives \(\sum_t w_t=D\). For nominees \(x,y\), compute the integer margin

\(\displaystyle A(x,y)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t\epsilon_{R_t}(x,y)=D\,M_\mu(x,y).\)

Each such integer has \(O(L)\) bits. By Section 1, these integer margins produce exactly the required winners.

Initialize \(B(x,y)=\max\{A(x,y),0\}\) for distinct nominees and \(B(x,x)=D\). Process the nominees one at a time as intermediate vertices, applying the Floyd–Warshall recurrence

\(\displaystyle B(x,y)\leftarrow\max\{B(x,y),\min\{B(x,z),B(z,y)\}\}.\)

This computes maximum bottleneck path strengths. Indeed, a path whose permitted intermediate vertices now include \(z\) either avoids \(z\), or splits at \(z\) into two paths whose bottleneck combines by taking their minimum. Maximizing these alternatives gives the recurrence. Directed walks suffice for this argument: removing a cycle cannot decrease their bottleneck, so the maximum equals the maximum over paths. The diagonal value \(D\) acts as an identity for taking minima, since every positive arc weight is at most \(D\). Zero represents absence of a positive-margin path.

Finally, the distinguished nominee \(p\) wins exactly when \(B(p,y)\geq B(y,p)\) for every other nominee \(y\).

Using ordinary quadratic-time integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for the entire verification is \(O((\tau m^2+k^3+\tau+1)L^2)\) bit operations. Thus verification is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\), and the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

The reduction and verifier establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness under the stated support representation. The supplied tractability result for exactly two discrete voters is consistent with this conclusion: the hardness construction uses up to four distinct weighted rankings.
