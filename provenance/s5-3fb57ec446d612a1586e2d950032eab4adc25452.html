Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational cohort masses \(\mu_q\), a dispatch rule that samples worker types according to those masses, and pairwise response observations with counts \(S_{qr}\), recover each cohort confusion matrix \(A_q\), up to column permutation, with the error guarantee of Theorem 1. The promise includes full rank, the stated lower bound \(\eta\) on empirical column mass, and a near-dominant neighboring cohort for each class; the pairwise laws are \(R_{qr}=A_q\operatorname{Diag}(d)A_r^\top\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge2\) cohort identifiers with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_q\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge2\) response classes. Cohort identifiers are observed, but their confusion matrices are unknown.
Each cohort has an unknown nonnegative, column-stochastic, full-rank matrix \(A_q\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}\), with \(A_q(i,k)=\Pr(X_q=i\mid Y=k)\). The unknown prior satisfies \(d_k>0\) and \(\sum_k d_k=1\). Responses are conditionally independent given \(Y\).
Dispatch independently samples cohort identifiers according to \(\mu\), conditioned on their being distinct, independently of labels and responses. For each supplied pair \(\{q,r\}\in E\), the input gives binary-encoded response counts \(C_{qr}(i,j)\) from \(S_{qr}>0\) independent items, with \(\sum_{i,j}C_{qr}(i,j)=S_{qr}\). Write \(\widehat R_{qr}=C_{qr}/S_{qr}\); its population law is \(R_{qr}=A_q\operatorname{Diag}(d)A_r^\top\).
Let \(N_q=\{r:\{q,r\}\in E\}\), \(t_q=|N_q|>0\), \(S_q=\min_{r\in N_q}S_{qr}\), and \(\widehat Z_q=[\widehat R_{qr}]_{r\in N_q}\). The empirical promise is \(\|\widehat Z_q(:,\ell)\|_1\ge\eta\) for every column, where \(\eta\in(0,1]\).
For every \(q,k\), some \(r\in N_q\) satisfies \(d_kA_r(k,k)\ge(1-\epsilon)\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_jA_r(k,j)\), where \(\epsilon\in[0,1]\). Define \(\sigma_q=\sigma_{\max}(A_q)\), \(\kappa_q=\sigma_{\max}(A_q)/\sigma_{\min}(A_q)\), and \(\nu_q=\sqrt m\,\sigma_q\epsilon+2\sqrt m\,(1+\sqrt{\ln(t_q/\delta)})/(\sqrt{S_q}\eta)\), with \(\delta\in(0,1)\).
Promise also \(\nu_q\le\sigma_{\min}(A_q)\min\{1/(2\sqrt{m-1}),1/4\}/(1+80\kappa_q^2)\) for every \(q\).
Recovery means producing column-stochastic estimates satisfying, for each \(q\), \(\min_{\Pi_q}\|\widehat A_q\Pi_q-A_q\|_{2,\infty}\le C\sqrt m\,\kappa_q^2\max\{\sigma_q\epsilon,(1+\sqrt{\ln(t_q/\delta)})/(\sqrt{S_q}\eta)\}\), for a universal constant \(C\); \(\Pi_q\) ranges over permutation matrices and \(\|B\|_{2,\infty}=\max_k\|B(:,k)\|_2\). For each cohort, the probability that the empirical promise holds but this bound fails is at most \(\delta\).
Is this promised cohort-recovery problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total binary input length, including counts and the rational parameters \(\eta,\epsilon,\delta\)?
For finitely many annotators, a polynomial arithmetic-time recovery algorithm and a conditioning-dependent statistical error bound are claimed in [Ibrahim et al.](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/c0e19ce0dbabbc0d17a4f8d4324cc8e3-Paper.pdf); the formulation above makes the normalized dominance and small-noise conditions explicit.
No separate result for this cohort-distribution formulation was located in the search.

  • Specified nonnegative column-stochastic confusion matrices, a strictly positive unknown class prior, at least two classes and cohorts, and nonempty observed neighborhoods.

Result

The promised recovery problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). A deterministic algorithm using exact rational arithmetic achieves the required statistical guarantee with the universal constant \(C=243\).

The algorithm applies the successive projection algorithm (SPA) separately to each cohort’s normalized empirical matrix. The proof below establishes both its recovery guarantee and its polynomial bit complexity.

1. Algorithm

Orient each supplied count matrix so that rows correspond to cohort \(q\) and columns to its neighbor \(r\), transposing when necessary.

For each cohort \(q\):

  • Check its empirical column-mass promise. If it fails, output \(I_m\).
  • Otherwise, normalize every column of \(\widehat Z_q\), obtaining a matrix \(V_q\) with \(n_q=mt_q\) columns:

\(V_q(:,\ell)=\widehat Z_q(:,\ell)/\|\widehat Z_q(:,\ell)\|_1.\)

  • Select \(m\) columns by SPA. If \(B\) contains the previously selected columns, choose a column maximizing

\(\|P_B^\perp V_q(:,\ell)\|_2^2,\)

where \(P_B^\perp\) projects onto the orthogonal complement of \(\operatorname{span}(B)\). Initially \(P_B^\perp=I_m\). Break ties by maximizing the original column’s squared norm, then by a fixed index order.

  • Output the selected original columns as \(\widehat A_q\). If all residuals vanish before \(m\) selections, output \(I_m\).

Every output is nonnegative and column-stochastic. The algorithm uses neither the unknown prior nor any unknown singular value.

2. Concentration before normalization

Fix a cohort \(q\). Write

\(\alpha_q=1+\sqrt{\ln(t_q/\delta)},\qquad b_q=\alpha_q/(\sqrt{S_q}\eta).\)

For a supplied pair \(\{q,r\}\), abbreviate \(S=S_{qr}\), \(R=R_{qr}\), and \(\widehat R=\widehat R_{qr}\).

Each item contributes a matrix having exactly one entry equal to one. Independence of the \(S\) items therefore gives

\(\mathbb E\|\widehat R-R\|_F^2=\dfrac{1-\|R\|_F^2}{S}\le\dfrac1S.\)

Consequently, Jensen’s inequality gives

\(\mathbb E\|\widehat R-R\|_F\le S^{-1/2}.\)

Changing one item changes its empirical matrix by Frobenius norm at most \(\sqrt2/S\). The reverse triangle inequality shows that the function \(\|\widehat R-R\|_F\) has the same bounded-difference constant.

The bounded-differences inequality states that, for a function of independent variables with change bounds \(c_i\),

\(\Pr(f-\mathbb Ef>u)\le\exp\!\left(-2u^2/\sum_i c_i^2\right).\)

Here \(\sum_i c_i^2=2/S\). Thus

\(\Pr\!\left(\|\widehat R-R\|_F>S^{-1/2}+u\right)\le e^{-Su^2}.\)

Taking \(u=\sqrt{\ln(t_q/\delta)/S}\) and applying a union bound over the \(t_q\) neighbors yields an event \(G_q\), with \(\Pr(G_q^c)\le\delta\), on which

\(\|\widehat R_{qr}-R_{qr}\|_F\le\dfrac{\alpha_q}{\sqrt{S_{qr}}}\le\dfrac{\alpha_q}{\sqrt{S_q}}\)

for every \(r\in N_q\). Independence between different pairs is unnecessary for this union bound.

Effect of normalization

Consider a population column \(x\) and its empirical counterpart \(\widehat x\). Let \(s=\mathbf1^\top x\) and \(\widehat s=\mathbf1^\top\widehat x\).

Every population column has positive mass: \(A_r\) has no zero row because it is full-rank, and all coordinates of \(d\) are positive. Hence \(s>0\).

On the empirical promise, \(\widehat s\ge\eta\). Since \(x/s\) is a probability vector,

\(\dfrac{\widehat x}{\widehat s}-\dfrac{x}{s} =\dfrac{\widehat x-x}{\widehat s} +\dfrac{s-\widehat s}{\widehat s}\dfrac{x}{s}.\)

Using \(\|x/s\|_2\le1\) and \(|s-\widehat s|\le\sqrt m\,\|\widehat x-x\|_2\), we obtain

\(\left\|\dfrac{\widehat x}{\widehat s}-\dfrac{x}{s}\right\|_2 \le\dfrac{1+\sqrt m}{\eta}\|\widehat x-x\|_2.\)

Let \(U_q\) denote the population matrix obtained by normalizing the columns of \(Z_q=[R_{qr}]_{r\in N_q}\). Because a column’s Euclidean norm is at most its block’s Frobenius norm, on \(G_q\) and the empirical promise,

\(\|V_q(:,\ell)-U_q(:,\ell)\|_2\le(1+\sqrt m)b_q\)

for every column \(\ell\).

3. Dominance produces a perturbed separable matrix

Index columns by \((r,j)\), where \(r\in N_q\) and \(j\) is the neighbor’s response class. Define

\(p_{rj}=\sum_{k=1}^m d_kA_r(j,k)>0,\qquad h_{rj}(k)=\dfrac{d_kA_r(j,k)}{p_{rj}}.\)

Each \(h_{rj}\) is a probability vector. Column-stochasticity of \(A_q\) and the population factorization give

\(R_{qr}(:,j)=p_{rj}A_qh_{rj},\qquad U_q(:,(r,j))=A_qh_{rj}.\)

Thus \(U_q=A_qH_q\), where every column of \(H_q\) belongs to the probability simplex.

For each class \(k\), choose a neighbor \(r_k\) supplied by the dominance promise. That promise says exactly

\(h_{r_k,k}(k)\ge1-\epsilon.\)

The selected indices \((r_k,k)\) are distinct, since their response-class coordinates differ.

For any probability vector \(h\) satisfying \(h(k)\ge1-\epsilon\), set \(u=1-h(k)\). Then

\(\|h-e_k\|_2^2=u^2+\sum_{j\ne k}h(j)^2 \le u^2+\left(\sum_{j\ne k}h(j)\right)^2 =2u^2\le2\epsilon^2.\)

Construct \(H_q^0\) by replacing each selected column \(h_{r_k,k}\) with \(e_k\). All columns remain probability vectors, and \(H_q^0\) contains an identity submatrix. Moreover,

\(\|A_q(h_{r_k,k}-e_k)\|_2 \le\sqrt2\,\sigma_q\epsilon \le\sqrt m\,\sigma_q\epsilon.\)

Therefore, on \(G_q\) and the empirical promise, we have a decomposition

\(V_q=A_qH_q^0+N_q^0\)

whose maximum column-noise norm \(\beta_q=\max_\ell\|N_q^0(:,\ell)\|_2\) satisfies

\(\beta_q\le\sqrt m\,\sigma_q\epsilon+(1+\sqrt m)b_q <\sqrt m\,\sigma_q\epsilon+2\sqrt m\,b_q=\nu_q.\)

The strict inequality follows from \(m\ge2\) and \(b_q>0\). This strict margin handles equality in the stated small-noise promise.

4. Applying the SPA robustness theorem

We use the Euclidean SPA specialization of [Gillis–Vavasis, Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.1237).

Precisely, suppose \(M=WH+N\), where \(W\) has full column rank \(r\ge2\), \(H\ge0\), every column of \(H\) sums to at most one, and \(H\) contains \(I_r\). Define

\(K=\max_k\|W(:,k)\|_2,\qquad a_r=\min\{1/(2\sqrt{r-1}),1/4\},\qquad \beta=\max_\ell\|N(:,\ell)\|_2.\)

The theorem, specialized to the squared Euclidean norm, gives a permutation matching the \(r\) extracted columns to \(W\), with error at most

\(\left(1+80K^2/\sigma_{\min}(W)^2\right)\beta,\)

provided

\(\beta<\dfrac{\sigma_{\min}(W)a_r} {1+80K^2/\sigma_{\min}(W)^2}.\)

Since \(K\le\sigma_{\max}(W)\), a sufficient condition is

\(\beta<\dfrac{\sigma_{\min}(W)a_r}{1+80\kappa(W)^2},\)

and the resulting error is at most \((1+80\kappa(W)^2)\beta\).

Hypothesis checks

Apply this result with \(W=A_q\), \(r=m\), \(H=H_q^0\), and \(N=N_q^0\).

Full rank is promised. Section 3 established nonnegative simplex columns and an identity submatrix. Finally,

\(\beta_q<\nu_q \le\dfrac{\sigma_{\min}(A_q)a_m}{1+80\kappa_q^2}.\)

SPA also completes all \(m\) selections. Indeed, its observed anchor submatrix is \(A_q+E_q\), with

\(\|E_q\|_2\le\|E_q\|_F\le\sqrt m\,\beta_q<\sigma_{\min}(A_q),\)

because

\(\sqrt m\,a_m\le\dfrac{\sqrt m}{2\sqrt{m-1}}\le\dfrac1{\sqrt2}<1.\)

For every nonzero vector \(z\),

\(\|(A_q+E_q)z\|_2 \ge(\sigma_{\min}(A_q)-\|E_q\|_2)\|z\|_2>0.\)

Hence \(V_q\) has rank \(m\), so its columns cannot all lie in the span of fewer than \(m\) selected independent columns. The early fallback is therefore never invoked on the event under consideration.

The theorem now gives

\(\min_{\Pi_q}\|\widehat A_q\Pi_q-A_q\|_{2,\infty} \le(1+80\kappa_q^2)\beta_q.\)

Since \(\kappa_q\ge1\),

\((1+80\kappa_q^2)\beta_q \le81\kappa_q^2\sqrt m\,(\sigma_q\epsilon+2b_q) \le243\sqrt m\,\kappa_q^2\max\{\sigma_q\epsilon,b_q\}.\)

This is the required bound with \(C=243\).

If \(F_q\) denotes failure of this bound and \(H_q^{\mathrm{emp}}\) denotes cohort \(q\)’s empirical promise, the preceding argument proves

\(H_q^{\mathrm{emp}}\cap F_q\subseteq G_q^c.\)

Consequently,

\(\Pr(H_q^{\mathrm{emp}}\cap F_q)\le\delta.\)

The globally stated empirical promise implies \(H_q^{\mathrm{emp}}\), so the requested per-cohort guarantee follows. This is a joint-event guarantee; it does not condition the sampling distribution on the empirical promise.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

Polynomial arithmetic time alone would be insufficient. Here exact rational implementation gives the stronger required result.

Normalization

For an oriented count column \(c\), let \(z=\sum_i c_i\). Its normalized empirical column is exactly

\(v=c/z,\)

because the factor \(S_{qr}\) cancels. Both numerator entries and \(z\) have polynomial bit length; indeed \(z\le S_{qr}\). No individual samples need to be reconstructed.

Exact projection and comparison

At a stage with \(s<m\) selected columns, let \(B\) contain their integer count vectors, rather than their normalized versions. Positive rescaling preserves their span. When these columns are independent,

\(P_B^\perp=I_m-B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top.\)

This is rational. For a candidate \(v=c/z\), its score is

\(\|P_B^\perp v\|_2^2 =v^\top P_B^\perp v =\dfrac{c^\top P_B^\perp c}{z^2}.\)

Thus both primary and secondary scores are rational, and exact comparisons require only integer cross-multiplication. No square roots are computed.

This projector formulation implements SPA exactly: if \(u=P_B^\perp v\ne0\), then \(u\) is orthogonal to the current selected span, and projecting additionally orthogonally to \(u\) gives the projector onto the complement of \(\operatorname{span}(B,v)\).

Bounds on intermediate sizes

Every count has at most \(L\) bits. Entries of the integer Gram matrix \(B^\top B\) therefore have \(O(L+\log m)\) bits. Hadamard’s determinant inequality shows that its determinants and cofactors have \(O(m(L+\log m))\) bits.

The adjugate formula consequently bounds the bit lengths of the inverse, the projector, and the candidate scores by polynomials in \(m,L\). These quantities can be computed using exact fraction-free elimination. Recomputing each projector from the selected original count vectors ensures this bound at every stage.

There are \(m\) stages per cohort and

\(\sum_q n_q=\sum_q mt_q=2m|E|\le m\tau(\tau-1)\)

candidate columns across the cohorts. Hence the number of arithmetic operations is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), each operates on integers of polynomial bit length, and the total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The output consists of rational normalized count columns and also has polynomial length.

6. Role of the cohort distribution

Dispatch is independent of labels and responses. Conditioning on a supplied pair therefore preserves the prior and gives the stated population law \(R_{qr}=A_q\operatorname{Diag}(d)A_r^\top\). If the dispatch itself is random, the concentration argument can be applied conditional on its cohort assignments and sample sizes.

The masses affect how frequently pairs are observed. Once the count tables are supplied, recovery processes only the \(\tau\) listed support identifiers. It neither enumerates possible cohort types nor spends time proportional to the sample counts’ numerical values.

Thus the stated support-based formulation admits the polynomial-time recovery algorithm proved above.
