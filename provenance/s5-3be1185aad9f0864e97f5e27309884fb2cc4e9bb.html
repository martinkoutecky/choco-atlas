The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite-support distributions over application types \(i\) and operator types \(j\), population sizes \(N_A,N_O\), declarations \(d_i,r_i,p_i\) and \(q_j,s_j,\ell_j\), and a threshold \(T\), decide whether two chains can reach total throughput at least \(T\). Choose masses \(x_{ic},y_{jc}\ge 0\) for \(c\in\{1,2\}\), subject to \(\sum_c x_{ic}\le\mu_i^A\) and \(\sum_c y_{jc}\le\mu_j^O\). Set \(D_c=N_A\sum_i d_i x_{ic}\), \(S_c=N_O\sum_j s_j y_{jc}\), \(R_c=\max_{i:x_{ic}>0}r_i\), and \(Q_c=\min_{j:y_{jc}>0}q_j\); require \(S_c\ge R_c\), and choose \(p_c\) between the maximum assigned operator price floor and minimum assigned application price cap. A chain lacking either role contributes zero; otherwise its throughput is \(p_c\min(D_c,Q_c)\). Is the sum across the two chains at least \(T\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Each society is given by its support: the list of types with positive binary-rational masses. Application masses \(\mu_i^A\) and operator masses \(\mu_j^O\) each sum to \(1\); \(\tau=\tau_A+\tau_O\) is the total support size, not the number of possible types.
Population sizes \(N_A,N_O\) are positive binary-encoded integers. Application type \(i\) declares demand \(d_i\), stake requirement \(r_i\), and price cap \(p_i\); operator type \(j\) declares capacity \(q_j\), stake \(s_j\), and price floor \(\ell_j\). All declarations are positive binary rationals; the threshold \(T\) is a binary rational.
There are \(m=2\) chain slots. Choose real masses \(x_{ic},y_{jc}\ge0\) with \(\sum_{c=1}^2x_{ic}\le\mu_i^A\) and \(\sum_{c=1}^2y_{jc}\le\mu_j^O\); types may split across slots and mass may remain unassigned.
For each slot let \(A_c=\{i:x_{ic}>0\}\) and \(O_c=\{j:y_{jc}>0\}\). A slot lacking either role contributes \(0\) and has no further constraints.
Otherwise define \(D_c=N_A\sum_i d_ix_{ic}\), \(S_c=N_O\sum_j s_jy_{jc}\), \(R_c=\max_{i\in A_c}r_i\), and \(Q_c=\min_{j\in O_c}q_j\). Require \(S_c\ge R_c\) and choose a real clearing price \(p_c\) satisfying \(\max_{j\in O_c}\ell_j\le p_c\le\min_{i\in A_c}p_i\).
Such a slot contributes \(F_c=p_c\min(D_c,Q_c)\). Thus capacity is the minimum capacity among positively assigned operator types, regardless of their assigned masses; throughput means total fees. The decision problem asks whether a feasible allocation has \(F_1+F_2\ge T\).
State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), including the encodings of \(N_A,N_O\).

Is this continuous two-chain throughput decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The indivisible-agent throughput decision problem is NP-hard, already with two chain slots and two operators ([discrete hardness](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22230v1#S4.SS8)).
No result settling this exact continuous problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize each role's distribution separately to total mass one; population sizes provide the absolute scaling.
  • Take population sizes to be positive binary-encoded integers, declarations to be positive binary rationals, and the threshold to be any binary rational.
  • Follow the core definition's strictly positive application stake requirements despite the zero requirements used in the hardness construction.
  • Treat two chains as two available slots, allowing unused slots; slots lacking either role have zero contribution and no stake or price constraints.
  • Allow real allocation masses and clearing prices, with no minimum positive assigned mass.
  • Use m for the fixed number of chain slots, tau for the combined support size, and L for the total binary input length.

Result

The continuous two-chain throughput decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\).

More generally, with \(\tau_A\) application types, \(\tau_O\) operator types, and \(m\) slots, it can be decided in time

\[ \bigl(1+\tau_A^2\tau_O\bigr)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \]

Thus, for \(m=2\), the running time is \(O\bigl(\tau^6\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\bigr)\). The dependence on the population sizes is through their binary encoding lengths, already included in \(L\).

The proof enumerates three bounds for each slot and solves a rational linear feasibility problem. Crucially, it does not require any designated type to receive strictly positive mass.

1. Candidate descriptions of a slot

If \(T\le 0\), the empty allocation answers yes. Henceforth assume \(T>0\).

Define the finite sets

\[ \mathcal R=\{r_i:1\le i\le\tau_A\},\qquad \mathcal P=\{p_i:1\le i\le\tau_A\},\qquad \mathcal Q=\{q_j:1\le j\le\tau_O\}. \]

For each slot, enumerate either an inactive description or a triple

\[ (\rho,\pi,\kappa)\in\mathcal R\times\mathcal P\times\mathcal Q. \]

For such a triple, define its eligible types by

\[ I(\rho,\pi)=\{i:r_i\le\rho,\ p_i\ge\pi\}, \]

\[ J(\kappa,\pi)=\{j:q_j\ge\kappa,\ \ell_j\le\pi\}. \]

Here \(\rho\) bounds application stake requirements from above, \(\kappa\) bounds operator capacities from below, and \(\pi\) is the proposed clearing price.

These are bounds, not requirements that the actual support extrema equal the enumerated values.

2. The linear feasibility problem

Fix one description for each of the \(m\) slots. Let \(C\) be the set of slots described by triples, with parameters \((\rho_c,\pi_c,\kappa_c)\).

Introduce nonnegative variables \(x_{ic},y_{jc}\) for every type and slot, and a nonnegative variable \(z_c\) for each \(c\in C\). Impose the population mass budgets

\[ \sum_{c=1}^{m}x_{ic}\le\mu_i^A \quad\text{for every application type }i, \]

\[ \sum_{c=1}^{m}y_{jc}\le\mu_j^O \quad\text{for every operator type }j. \]

For an inactive description, impose \(x_{ic}=y_{jc}=0\) for all types.

For each \(c\in C\), impose the eligibility restrictions

\[ x_{ic}=0\quad\text{if }i\notin I(\rho_c,\pi_c), \qquad y_{jc}=0\quad\text{if }j\notin J(\kappa_c,\pi_c), \]

and the stake and throughput inequalities

\[ N_O\sum_j s_jy_{jc}\ge\rho_c, \]

\[ z_c\le N_A\sum_i d_ix_{ic}, \qquad z_c\le\kappa_c. \]

Finally, impose the fee threshold

\[ \sum_{c\in C}\pi_cz_c\ge T. \]

All coefficients are fixed rationals, so this is a linear feasibility problem.

The algorithm answers yes if at least one enumerated problem is feasible, and no otherwise.

3. Soundness: an LP solution gives a valid allocation

Suppose one of these linear systems is feasible. Use its masses as the original allocation.

Every inactive slot is empty and contributes zero.

Consider a slot \(c\in C\). Since \(\rho_c>0\), its stake inequality implies that some operator has positive assigned mass. Thus \(O_c\ne\varnothing\).

If \(A_c=\varnothing\), then its demand is zero. The inequalities \(0\le z_c\le N_A\sum_i d_ix_{ic}\) imply \(z_c=0\). The original slot contributes zero and requires no further constraints, exactly as specified in the task.

Now suppose \(A_c\ne\varnothing\). Eligibility gives

\[ R_c=\max_{i\in A_c}r_i\le\rho_c, \qquad Q_c=\min_{j\in O_c}q_j\ge\kappa_c. \]

Consequently,

\[ S_c=N_O\sum_j s_jy_{jc}\ge\rho_c\ge R_c. \]

Furthermore, every positively assigned operator satisfies \(\ell_j\le\pi_c\), and every positively assigned application satisfies \(p_i\ge\pi_c\). Therefore choosing the clearing price \(p_c=\pi_c\) is feasible.

Writing \(D_c=N_A\sum_i d_ix_{ic}\), the throughput inequalities give

\[ z_c\le\min(D_c,\kappa_c)\le\min(D_c,Q_c). \]

Because \(\pi_c>0\), the actual fee contribution satisfies

\[ F_c=\pi_c\min(D_c,Q_c)\ge\pi_cz_c. \]

Summing over slots proves that the allocation earns at least \(T\).

This also resolves the strict-positivity issue: if types disappear from the support, the actual maximum stake requirement can only decrease, and the actual minimum capacity can only increase. No positive-mass witness for an enumerated extremum is needed.

4. Completeness: a valid allocation satisfies an enumerated LP

Conversely, suppose an original feasible allocation earns at least \(T\).

First delete all assigned masses from every slot lacking either role. Such slots contributed zero, and deletion only releases mass from the population budgets. Describe those slots as inactive.

For each remaining slot, both supports are finite and nonempty. Choose

\[ \rho_c=\max_{i\in A_c}r_i,\qquad \pi_c=\min_{i\in A_c}p_i,\qquad \kappa_c=\min_{j\in O_c}q_j. \]

These values belong to \(\mathcal R,\mathcal P,\mathcal Q\), respectively, so the algorithm enumerates this collection of descriptions.

Every positively assigned application is eligible by the definitions of \(\rho_c\) and \(\pi_c\). Every positively assigned operator has \(q_j\ge\kappa_c\). Moreover, feasibility of the original clearing price gives

\[ \ell_j\le p_c\le\pi_c \quad\text{for every }j\in O_c, \]

so all positively assigned operators are eligible as well.

The original stake condition is precisely

\[ N_O\sum_j s_jy_{jc}\ge\rho_c. \]

Set

\[ z_c=\min(D_c,\kappa_c). \]

This satisfies both LP throughput bounds. Since \(\kappa_c=Q_c\) and \(\pi_c\ge p_c\),

\[ \pi_cz_c =\pi_c\min(D_c,Q_c) \ge p_c\min(D_c,Q_c) =F_c. \]

Thus the original masses, together with these \(z_c\), satisfy the enumerated LP, including its fee threshold.

Combining soundness and completeness proves the algorithm correct.

5. Exact arithmetic and running time

There are at most \(1+\tau_A^2\tau_O\) descriptions per slot, hence at most

\[ \bigl(1+\tau_A^2\tau_O\bigr)^m \]

linear feasibility problems.

Each problem has \(O(m\tau)\) variables and constraints. Its coefficients are input rationals or products such as \(N_A d_i\) and \(N_O s_j\). Multiplying these binary-encoded numbers produces rational coefficients of bit length \(O(L)\); the algorithm never expands the populations into individual agents.

Rational linear programming admits exact feasibility decisions in time polynomial in the binary encoding length of the system. This is the standard polynomial-time LP theorem; see [Theorem 17.1 in these CMU notes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs.cmu.edu/academic/class/15859-s15/www/lectures/lecture17.pdf). Its hypotheses apply here: every coefficient is rational and the system is explicitly represented with polynomial encoding length. Additionally, all mass variables are bounded by their mass budgets, and each \(z_c\) is bounded by \(\kappa_c\).

Therefore the total running time is

\[ \bigl(1+\tau_A^2\tau_O\bigr)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \le \bigl(1+\tau^3\bigr)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \]

For \(m=2\), this is polynomial in the full input length. The decision is exact, including the case where the achievable fees equal \(T\).
