The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a fixed unit-supply item environment \(S\) and a finite set of buyer types \(k\), each specifying a complete valuation function \(v_k\) and per-capita budget \(b_k\), let the population type distribution be \(\mu\), with \(\sum_k \mu_k b_k=1\). For \(N\) buyers sampled independently from \(\mu\), each with budget \(b_k/N\), and the observed quasilinear Fisher equilibrium of that market, does its revenue estimate the long-run revenue \(R(\mu)=\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_k v_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta)\), where \(\beta\) minimizes \(\int_\Theta \max_k\{\beta_k v_k(\theta)\}\,S(d\theta)-\sum_k\mu_k b_k\log\beta_k\) over \(0<\beta_k\leq1\)? Establish consistency and finite-sample error guarantees as \(N\) grows.

Buyer-sampling revenue convergence

1. Result and explicit bounds

Both guarantees hold under the stated assumptions. Equilibrium prices are unique, even when allocations are not unique. Moreover,
\(\widehat R_N\to R(\mu)\) almost surely, and the estimation error has explicit exponential tail bounds.

Define
\[ \]
\(q_{N,k}=N^{-1}\sum_{a=1}^N\mathbf 1_{\{K_a=k\}},\qquad B_k=\mu_kb_k,\qquad B_{N,k}=q_{N,k}b_k.\)

The observed total budget is \(\sum_k B_{N,k}\); it is not renormalized.

Set
\(b_{\max}=\max_k b_k,\qquad A=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}b_k\sqrt{\mu_k(1-\mu_k)},\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_k\mu_k,\)
and write \((x)_+=\max\{x,0\}\).

The following deterministic bound holds for every sample:
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle |\widehat R_N-R(\mu)| \leq \max\left\{ \sum_k b_k(q_{N,k}-\mu_k)_+,\, \sum_k b_k(\mu_k-q_{N,k})_+ \right\}. \tag{1}\]

Consequently,
\(\displaystyle \mathbb E|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|\leq A/\sqrt N.\)

For every \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \Pr\bigl(|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|>\varepsilon\bigr) \leq \min\left\{ 1,\, 2^{\tau+1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2N\varepsilon^2}{b_{\max}^2}\right),\, \exp\!\left[-\frac{2N}{b_{\max}^2} \left(\varepsilon-\frac A{\sqrt N}\right)_+^2\right] \right\}. \tag{2}\]

A complementary bound, which avoids budget magnitudes, holds for \(0<\varepsilon\leq1\):
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \Pr\bigl(|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|>\varepsilon\bigr) \leq \min\left\{ 1,\, 2\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}\exp\!\left(-\frac{N\mu_k\varepsilon^2}{3}\right) \right\} \leq \min\left\{ 1,\, 2\tau\exp\!\left(-\frac{N\mu_{\min}\varepsilon^2}{3}\right) \right\}. \tag{3}\]

Also, the probability is zero whenever \(\varepsilon\geq V\).

In particular, for \(0<\delta<1\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\),
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle |\widehat R_N-R(\mu)| \leq \frac{A+b_{\max}\sqrt{\tfrac12\log(1/\delta)}}{\sqrt N}. \tag{4}\]

These bounds have no dependence on \(m\), the individual supplies \(S_j\), or input bit length \(L\). The valuation bound \(V\) supplies the deterministic cutoff. Dependence on the support and budgets appears explicitly through \(\tau,\mu,b\). Indeed, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle A\leq \sqrt{(\tau-1)\sum_k\mu_kb_k^2} \leq b_{\max}\sqrt{\tau-1}. \tag{5}\]

The proof requires no differentiability of the maximum term and permits minimizers with \(\beta_k=1\).

2. Equilibrium characterization, including absent types

Keep the valuations and supplies fixed. For an arbitrary vector of aggregate budgets \(B\in[0,\infty)^\tau\), let
\(I(B)=\{k:B_k>0\}\).
Types outside \(I(B)\) are absent.

Define
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle f(\beta)=\sum_j S_j\max_k\{\beta_kv_{kj}\},\qquad F_B(\beta)=f(\beta)-\sum_{k\in I(B)}B_k\log\beta_k. \)

We minimize \(F_B\) on \([0,1]^\tau\), assigning value \(+\infty\) if an active coordinate is zero. For absent types, we choose the canonical value \(\beta_k=0\).

Existence and uniqueness of the canonical minimizer

Setting an absent coordinate to zero cannot increase \(f\) and does not change the logarithmic term. Thus a canonical minimizer is also a global minimizer on the full cube.

The extended objective is lower semicontinuous on the compact cube. It is finite at the vector whose active coordinates are one and whose absent coordinates are zero. Therefore it attains its minimum, and every active coordinate of a minimizer is positive.

On the active coordinates, \(-\sum_k B_k\log\beta_k\) is strictly convex, while \(f\) is convex. Hence the active coordinates of the minimizer are unique. Denote the canonical minimizer by \(\beta^B\), and put
\[ \]
\(p_j^B=\max_k\{\beta_k^Bv_{kj}\},\qquad \mathcal R(B)=\sum_j S_jp_j^B.\)

When \(B=0\), these definitions give \(\beta^B=0\), \(p^B=0\), and \(\mathcal R(B)=0\).

The minimizer produces a quasilinear equilibrium

Suppose \(I(B)\neq\varnothing\). The convex optimality conditions provide \(g\in\partial f(\beta^B)\) and upper-bound multipliers \(\eta_k\geq0\), for active types, satisfying
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle g_k-\frac{B_k}{\beta_k^B}+\eta_k=0,\qquad \eta_k(\beta_k^B-1)=0. \tag{6}\]

These conditions apply because the active minimizer has positive coordinates and the upper-bound constraints have strictly feasible points.

The subgradient formula for a finite maximum yields numbers \(\lambda_{kj}\geq0\) such that, for each good, they sum to one over active types attaining the maximum, and
\(g_k=\sum_j S_jv_{kj}\lambda_{kj}\).
Set \(x_{kj}=S_j\lambda_{kj}\).

Thus positive allocation occurs only where
\(\beta_k^Bv_{kj}=p_j^B\), and supply is fully allocated. On a zero-price good, every active valuation is zero, so its allocation is immaterial.

Type \(k\)'s expenditure is
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle e_k^B=\sum_jp_j^Bx_{kj} =\beta_k^Bg_k =B_k-\beta_k^B\eta_k \leq B_k. \tag{7}\]

Moreover, \(e_k^B=B_k\) whenever \(\beta_k^B<1\).

For any affordable bundle \(y\), the inequalities \(\beta_k^Bv_{kj}\leq p_j^B\) imply
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \sum_j(v_{kj}-p_j^B)y_j \leq \left(\frac1{\beta_k^B}-1\right)\sum_jp_j^By_j \leq \left(\frac1{\beta_k^B}-1\right)B_k. \tag{8}\]

The constructed allocation attains this upper bound when \(\beta_k^B<1\). When \(\beta_k^B=1\), it attains zero, which is again the upper bound. Hence every active type receives an optimal affordable bundle, establishing equilibrium existence.

Every equilibrium has these prices

Conversely, consider any quasilinear equilibrium for the active types.

A negative-price good would make every buyer's objective unbounded by increasing its quantity. A zero-price good with positive value to an active type would also make that type's objective unbounded. Thus equilibrium prices are nonnegative, and zero-price goods have zero value to all active types.

For an active type, define
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \alpha_k=\max\left\{1,\max_{j:p_j>0}\frac{v_{kj}}{p_j}\right\}, \qquad \beta_k=\frac1{\alpha_k}. \)

An expenditure of \(z\) on a positive-price good \(j\) contributes \(z(v_{kj}/p_j-1)\) to utility. Therefore:

  • If \(\alpha_k>1\), optimality requires spending the entire budget on goods with ratio \(v_{kj}/p_j=\alpha_k\).
  • If \(\alpha_k=1\), positive expenditure can occur only on goods with ratio one.

Consequently, \(\beta_kv_{kj}\leq p_j\) for all goods, with equality on allocated positive-price goods; expenditure equals \(B_k\) whenever \(\beta_k<1\).

Every positive-price good is fully allocated, so some allocated buyer supplies the equality. Hence
\(p_j=\max_k\beta_kv_{kj}\).

The allocation now supplies a subgradient \(g_k=\sum_jv_{kj}x_{kj}\). With
\(\eta_k=(B_k-e_k)/\beta_k\),
the conditions in (6) hold. Therefore \(\beta\) minimizes \(F_B\).

Uniqueness of its active coordinates proves that all equilibria have the same prices \(p^B\). Their revenue is \(\mathcal R(B)\), although allocations can differ.

Market clearing and budget feasibility also give
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \mathcal R(B)=\sum_k e_k^B,\qquad 0\leq\mathcal R(B)\leq\min\left\{V,\sum_kB_k\right\}. \tag{9}\]

3. Exact aggregation of sampled buyers

For fixed prices, a buyer's feasible set and linear objective scale with its budget. Thus the maximum utility obtainable with budget \(cB\) is \(c\) times the maximum obtainable with budget \(B\), for every \(c>0\).

Suppose type \(k\) occurs \(n_k>0\) times in the sample. Its aggregate budget is
\(n_kb_k/N=B_{N,k}\).

Summing the optimal allocations of these identical-value buyers gives an optimal allocation for a single type-\(k\) buyer with budget \(B_{N,k}\): the summed utility equals the maximum utility for the summed budget.

Conversely, dividing an optimal aggregate allocation equally among the \(n_k\) copies gives each copy an optimal bundle with budget \(b_k/N\). This preserves supply feasibility and market clearing. Types with \(n_k=0\) are simply absent.

Therefore,
\[ \]
\[\widehat R_N=\mathcal R(B_N),\qquad R(\mu)=\mathcal R(B). \tag{10}\]

This identity uses the realized budgets exactly as specified.

4. Revenue stability under budget changes

We prove the key deterministic statement:
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle B\leq C \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0\leq\mathcal R(C)-\mathcal R(B) \leq\sum_k(C_k-B_k). \tag{11}\]

The analogous sensitivity property for fixed buyers appears in Propositions 5 and 11 of [Conitzer et al., *Pacing Equilibrium in First Price Auction Markets*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.07166). The following proof includes zero budgets and arbitrary divisible supplies.

Monotonicity of the multipliers

For vectors \(a,c\), let \(a\wedge c\) and \(a\vee c\) denote coordinatewise minimum and maximum.

For each good, writing \(M_j(a)=\max_k a_kv_{kj}\), nonnegative valuations give
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle M_j(a\vee c)=\max\{M_j(a),M_j(c)\},\qquad M_j(a\wedge c)\leq\min\{M_j(a),M_j(c)\}. \)

Multiplying by supplies and summing yields
\[ \]
\[f(a\wedge c)+f(a\vee c)\leq f(a)+f(c). \tag{12}\]

Suppose \(B\leq C\), and write \(a=\beta^B\), \(c=\beta^C\). We claim
\[ \]
\[F_B(a\wedge c)+F_C(a\vee c)\leq F_B(a)+F_C(c). \tag{13}\]

For the maximum terms, use (12). For each logarithmic coordinate with \(a_k\leq c_k\), the contribution is unchanged. If \(a_k>c_k\), then both budgets are positive, and the change in the logarithmic contribution is
\[ \]
\((C_k-B_k)\log(c_k/a_k)\leq0.\)

Absent coordinates cause no logarithmic difficulty: if \(B_k=0\), then \(a_k=0\), so the case \(a_k>c_k\) cannot arise.

Optimality of \(a\) and \(c\) gives the reverse inequality in (13), term by term. Thus \(a\wedge c\) is a minimizer for \(F_B\). It is canonical on absent coordinates, so uniqueness implies \(a\wedge c=a\). Therefore
\[ \]
\[\beta^B\leq\beta^C,\qquad p^B\leq p^C. \tag{14}\]

This proves revenue monotonicity.

Bounding the revenue increase

Let
\(T=\{k:\beta_k^C>\beta_k^B\}\)
and \(D=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\setminus T\).

For \(k\in T\) with \(B_k>0\), we have \(\beta_k^B<1\), so its old expenditure is \(B_k\). If \(B_k=0\), its old expenditure is also \(B_k=0\). Its new expenditure is at most \(C_k\). Hence
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \sum_{k\in T}(e_k^C-e_k^B)\leq\sum_{k\in T}(C_k-B_k). \tag{15}\]

We next show that the total expenditure of \(D\) cannot increase.

Consider a good on which \(D\) makes positive expenditure in the new equilibrium. Some \(i\in D\) receives a positive quantity, and
\(\beta_i^Cv_{ij}=p_j^C>0\).
Since its multiplier is unchanged,
\[ \]
\(p_j^B\geq\beta_i^Bv_{ij}=\beta_i^Cv_{ij}=p_j^C.\)

Together with (14), this gives \(p_j^B=p_j^C\).

Every \(k\in T\) has an old bid strictly below this price: if \(v_{kj}>0\), then
\(\beta_k^Bv_{kj}<\beta_k^Cv_{kj}\leq p_j^C\);
if \(v_{kj}=0\), its old bid is zero. Thus the old equilibrium allocated the entire good to \(D\). Its old expenditure from \(D\) was \(S_jp_j^B\), while its new expenditure from \(D\) is at most that amount.

Goods with no new expenditure from \(D\) contribute zero to its new expenditure. Summing proves
\(\sum_{k\in D}e_k^C\leq\sum_{k\in D}e_k^B\).
Combining this with (15) and (9) proves (11).

Arbitrary changes

For arbitrary \(B,C\), compare them through \(B\wedge C\) and \(B\vee C\). Applying (11) gives
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle -\sum_k(B_k-C_k)_+ \leq\mathcal R(C)-\mathcal R(B) \leq\sum_k(C_k-B_k)_+. \tag{16}\]

In particular,
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle |\mathcal R(C)-\mathcal R(B)| \leq\sum_k|C_k-B_k|. \tag{17}\]

Equations (10) and (16) establish the deterministic sampling bound (1).

5. Almost-sure convergence and concentration

Almost-sure convergence

For every type \(k\), the variables \(\mathbf1_{\{K_a=k\}}\) are independent, identically distributed Bernoulli variables with mean \(\mu_k\). The strong law gives \(q_{N,k}\to\mu_k\) almost surely.

There are finitely many types, so these convergences hold simultaneously almost surely. Consequently,
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \sum_k b_k|q_{N,k}-\mu_k|\longrightarrow0 \quad\text{almost surely}. \)

Applying (17) proves
\(\widehat R_N\to R(\mu)\) almost surely.

Expected error

The variance of \(q_{N,k}\) is \(\mu_k(1-\mu_k)/N\). Cauchy–Schwarz therefore gives
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \mathbb E|q_{N,k}-\mu_k| \leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu_k(1-\mu_k)}N}. \)

Summing the weighted inequalities and using (17) proves
\(\mathbb E|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|\leq A/\sqrt N\).

A union bound over subsets

For real numbers \(d_k\),
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \max\left\{\sum_k(d_k)_+,\sum_k(-d_k)_+\right\} =\max_{H\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\tau\}}\left|\sum_{k\in H}d_k\right|. \tag{18}\]

Indeed, selecting the positive coordinates maximizes the subset sum, and selecting the negative coordinates minimizes it.

For a fixed subset \(H\), the variables
\(Z_a^H=b_{K_a}\mathbf1_{\{K_a\in H\}}\)
are independent and lie in \([0,b_{\max}]\). Their empirical mean minus their expectation is
\(\sum_{k\in H}b_k(q_{N,k}-\mu_k)\).

Hoeffding's inequality gives
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Pr\left(\left|\sum_{k\in H}b_k(q_{N,k}-\mu_k)\right|>\varepsilon\right) \leq2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2N\varepsilon^2}{b_{\max}^2}\right). \)

Combining (1), (18), and a union bound over the \(2^\tau\) subsets proves the second term in (2).

Concentration around the expected absolute error

Let \(G=|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|\), viewed as a function of the independent draws.

Replacing a draw of type \(k\) by type \(\ell\neq k\) decreases one aggregate budget by \(b_k/N\) and increases another by \(b_\ell/N\). Equation (16) implies
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle |\widehat R_N^{\,\mathrm{new}}-\widehat R_N^{\,\mathrm{old}}| \leq\frac{\max\{b_k,b_\ell\}}N \leq\frac{b_{\max}}N. \)

The same bound holds for the change in \(G\), by the reverse triangle inequality.

McDiarmid's inequality states that an independent-input function with coordinate change bounds \(c_a\) satisfies
\(\Pr(G-\mathbb EG>t)\leq\exp(-2t^2/\sum_a c_a^2)\).
Here \(c_a=b_{\max}/N\), so
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Pr(G-\mathbb EG>t)\leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{2Nt^2}{b_{\max}^2}\right). \)

Using \(\mathbb EG\leq A/\sqrt N\) proves the last term of (2), and choosing \(t=b_{\max}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2N)}\) proves (4).

Relative-frequency concentration

Suppose
\(|q_{N,k}-\mu_k|\leq\varepsilon\mu_k\)
for every type. Since \(\sum_k\mu_kb_k=1\), both one-sided sums in (1) are at most \(\varepsilon\). Thus an error exceeding \(\varepsilon\) requires at least one relative-frequency violation.

For independent Bernoulli variables, the multiplicative Chernoff bound gives, for \(0<\varepsilon\leq1\),
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \Pr(|q_{N,k}-\mu_k|>\varepsilon\mu_k) \leq2\exp\!\left(-\frac{N\mu_k\varepsilon^2}{3}\right). \)

A union bound proves (3).

Finally, every equilibrium price lies in \([0,V]\), and supplies sum to one. Both revenues therefore belong to \([0,V]\), proving the cutoff at \(\varepsilon\geq V\).

6. Why parameter dependence matters

The guarantees above concern each fixed society. A vanishing error bound uniform over both type masses and budgets cannot hold, even with fixed \(m,\tau,V\).

Take one good with supply one and two types, both valuing it at \(2\). Choose a positive dyadic rational \(\rho<1/2\), and set
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \mu_1=1-\rho,\quad \mu_2=\rho,\quad b_1=\frac12,\quad b_2=\frac{1+\rho}{2\rho}. \)

All assumptions hold, including
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle (1-\rho)b_1+\rho b_2 =\frac{1-\rho}{2}+\frac{1+\rho}{2}=1. \)

For this one-good market, equilibrium price is the minimum of \(2\) and total budget: below price \(2\), every buyer strictly prefers spending its entire budget, and market clearing forces price to equal total budget. At price \(2\), buyers are indifferent, and clearing is possible exactly when total budget is at least \(2\).

The population revenue is therefore \(R(\mu)=1\). If no type-\(2\) buyer appears, the observed total budget and revenue are both \(1/2\). This event has probability \((1-\rho)^N\). Hence
\[ \]
\[\displaystyle \Pr\left(|\widehat R_N-R(\mu)|>\frac14\right) \geq(1-\rho)^N. \tag{19}\]

For any fixed \(N\), this lower bound approaches one as \(\rho\) decreases. Thus fixed \(m=1,\tau=2,V=2\) alone cannot yield a uniform vanishing bound; dependence on masses or budget magnitudes is necessary.

The buyer-sampling result proved here follows from exact type aggregation and budget stability. It does not require transferring the item-sampling asymptotic arguments in the cited [revenue-inference treatment](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15522v2#S5.SS1).
