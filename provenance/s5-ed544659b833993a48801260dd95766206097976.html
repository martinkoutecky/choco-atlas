The continuous mirror question, as graded

1. Scope of the results

The following statements are proved here:

  • Focal expected utilities and payments need not converge, even under truthful bidding.
  • An exact welfare-maximizing mass allocation is computable in polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$.
  • The DSL welfare assertion is false, even when every bidder submits exactly one identity.
  • With one good, focal expected utilities and payments converge for every fixed finite action and rational nature state; truthful payment limits are polynomial-time computable.
  • With three goods, all truthful payment limits can exist and nevertheless be irrational.

The last result advances the remaining computational question: exact payment computation requires an output representation capable of expressing irrational numbers. I do not establish a general polynomial-time algorithm or hardness result under the promise that the payment limits exist.

2. Exact mass allocation is polynomial-time computable

Consider the rational linear program
$$ \max \sum_{t,S}v_t(S)x_{t,S} $$
subject to
$$ x_{t,S}\geq0,\qquad \sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\leq q_g. $$
These are precisely the constraints and objective defining $W^*$.

There are $\tau2^m$ variables. Because every valuation table is explicitly listed, $\tau2^m$ is bounded by the input length up to an encoding constant. The matrix has $\tau+m$ rows, coefficients in $\{0,1\}$, and rational right-hand sides and objective coefficients of polynomial encoding length.

The feasible region is nonempty: allocate every type its entire mass on the empty bundle. It is closed and bounded, since nonnegativity and the type equations imply
$$ 0\leq x_{t,S}\leq\mu_t. $$
Thus an optimum exists.

The exact rational linear-programming theorem states that a feasible rational linear program with bounded objective can be optimized, with an exact rational optimal solution returned, in time polynomial in its encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here. Consequently, an optimal mass allocation and $W^*$ are polynomial-time computable. See [Gleixner–Steffy, Section 1.3](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/7507.pdf) for the exact version of the polynomial-time theorem.

There is also a useful distinction between normalized total welfare and unscaled marginal quantities. Let $T_N$ denote optimal truthful welfare at replication $N$, and let $x^*$ be an optimal mass allocation. Assign $\lfloor Nx^*_{t,S}\rfloor$ bidders of type $t$ each nonempty bundle $S$, and assign the remaining bidders empty bundles. Rounding down preserves all supply inequalities and leaves nonnegative integer remaining type counts. Hence
$$ NW^*-C\leq T_N\leq NW^*, \qquad C=\sum_{t,S\neq\varnothing}v_t(S). $$
The upper bound follows because dividing any replicated allocation’s bundle counts by $N$ gives a feasible mass allocation. Therefore
$$ \frac{T_N}{N}\longrightarrow W^*. $$
The next section shows that unscaled truthful utilities and payments can still oscillate.

3. Truthful focal utilities and payments can fail to converge

3.1 Society and welfare optima

Take $m=3$, $\epsilon=1$, and
$$ q_1=q_2=q_3=\frac12. $$
There are four types, each with mass $1/4$. For $ij\in\{12,13,23\}$, define
$$ v_{ij}(S)= \begin{cases} 1,&\{i,j\}\subseteq S,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
For the fourth type, denoted $T$, define
$$ v_T(S)= \begin{cases} 2,&S=\{1,2,3\},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
These are monotone integer-valued valuations vanishing on the empty bundle.

Admissible replications are exactly $N=4k$. The opponent society contains $k$ bidders of each type and $2k$ copies of each good. All opponents bid truthfully, and an additional focal bidder of type $T$ also bids truthfully.

Let $F_k$ be optimal welfare without the focal bidder and $G_k$ optimal welfare with her.

In any allocation, let $r$ count type-$T$ identities receiving all three goods, and let $p$ count pair-type identities receiving positive value. Welfare is $2r+p$. These identities consume at least $3r+2p$ copies, so
$$ 3r+2p\leq6k. $$
Consequently,
$$ 2r+p \leq 2r+\left\lfloor\frac{6k-3r}{2}\right\rfloor = 3k+\left\lfloor\frac r2\right\rfloor. $$
Using $r\leq k$ without the focal bidder and $r\leq k+1$ with her gives
$$ F_k\leq2k+\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{2}\right\rfloor, $$
$$ G_k\leq2(k+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{3(k-1)}2\right\rfloor. $$

Both bounds are attained. Assign full bundles to all available type-$T$ bidders. The remaining capacity of each good is $h=k$ without the focal bidder and $h=k-1$ with her.

For any nonnegative integer $h$, the pair types can achieve $\lfloor3h/2\rfloor$ winners:

  • If $h=2s$, assign $s$ winners to each pair.
  • If $h=2s+1$, assign $s+1$ winners to pair $12$ and $s$ winners to each other pair.

In the second case, goods $1$ and $2$ are each used $h$ times and good $3$ is used $h-1$ times. All pair counts in these constructions are at most $k$, so the available bidders suffice. Thus
$$ F_k=2k+\left\lfloor\frac{3k}{2}\right\rfloor, \qquad G_k=2(k+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{3(k-1)}2\right\rfloor. $$
Subtracting yields
$$ G_k-F_k= \begin{cases} 0,&k\text{ even},\\ 1,&k\text{ odd}. \end{cases} $$

3.2 The specified tie-break always allocates the focal bidder the full bundle

If $k$ is odd, the welfare upper bound for $r\leq k$ is $F_k<G_k$. Therefore every optimum has $r=k+1$, and the focal bidder receives all three goods.

Suppose $k$ is even. The upper bound excludes $r\leq k-1$, so an optimum has $r=k$ or $r=k+1$.

If $r=k$, optimality requires $p=3k/2$. Their minimum copy consumption is
$$ 3k+2\left(\frac{3k}{2}\right)=6k. $$
Thus every pair winner receives exactly two goods, and no other identity receives goods. There are exactly $k$ bundles of size three.

An optimum with $r=k+1$ can have $k+2$ bundles of size three. Write
$$ k-1=2s+1. $$
After assigning full bundles to the $k+1$ type-$T$ bidders, use the odd-capacity pair construction above. It leaves one copy of good $3$. Give it to a winning pair-$12$ identity, enlarging that identity’s bundle to size three without changing welfare. Such an identity exists because $s+1\geq1$.

This allocation has more size-three bundles than any optimum with $r=k$. Its descending bundle-size list is therefore lexicographically larger. The prescribed tie-break excludes all optima with $r=k$.

Hence the focal bidder receives value two for every positive $k$.

3.3 Oscillating payments

The Clarke-pivot payment of the truthful focal bidder is
$$ p_{T,4k}=F_k-(G_k-2)=2-(G_k-F_k). $$
Her utility is
$$ u_{T,4k}=G_k-F_k. $$
These quantities are constant across all remaining tied allocations:

| Parity of $k$ | Utility | Payment |
|---|---:|---:|
| Even | $0$ | $2$ |
| Odd | $1$ | $1$ |

Thus neither expected sequence converges.

The opponent state is a permitted rational nature state: mass $1/4$ on each truthful singleton action. Integer bids satisfy the required bid grid. In particular, the truthful limit $P_T$ fails to exist on this valid input.

4. One good: convergence and polynomial-time truthful limits

This section gives a complete positive result for $m=1$, including arbitrary finite Sybil actions.

Let supply be $q>0$, and let the focal bidder’s true singleton value be $V$. A fixed focal action consists of finitely many singleton bids.

For each opponent bid level $b$, define
$$ w_b=\sum_a\nu_a\,\#\{\text{identities in }a\text{ bidding }b\}. $$
Only finitely many $w_b$ are positive, and they are rational. Let
$$ M=\sum_bw_b $$
be the total opponent identity mass. At an integral replication, there are exactly $Nw_b$ opponent identities bidding $b$.

4.1 Limiting formula

If $M<q$, then eventually all opponent and focal identities fit within the supply. The bundle-size tie-break allocates a copy to every identity, and every payment is zero. Thus the limiting focal payment is zero, and limiting utility is $V$ for a nonempty action and zero for the empty action.

Now suppose $M\geq q$. Choose the unique opponent bid level $\beta$ satisfying
$$ A=\sum_{b>\beta}w_b<q\leq A+w_\beta. $$
Define
$$ \alpha=\frac{q-A}{w_\beta}\in(0,1]. $$
Let $H$ and $E$ be the numbers of focal identities bidding strictly above $\beta$ and exactly $\beta$, respectively.

Then
$$ \lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E[p_N] = \beta(H+\alpha E), $$
where $p_N$ is the sum of the focal identities’ payments, and
$$ \lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E[u_N] = V\left(1-\mathbf 1_{\{H=0\}}(1-\alpha)^E\right) -\beta(H+\alpha E). $$
When $E=0$, the factor $(1-\alpha)^E$ is interpreted as one.

To prove these formulas, first suppose $\alpha<1$. For all sufficiently large replications:

  • Every focal identity bidding above $\beta$ wins.
  • Every focal identity bidding below $\beta$ loses.
  • After allocating to identities bidding above $\beta$, the remaining

$$ N(q-A)-H $$
copies are allocated uniformly among the $Nw_\beta+E$ identities bidding $\beta$.

Uniformity follows from the final uniform tie-break: all such choices have the same welfare and bundle-size list. If $J_N$ counts winning focal identities bidding $\beta$, then
$$ \mathbb E[J_N] = \frac{E\bigl(N(q-A)-H\bigr)}{Nw_\beta+E} \longrightarrow E\alpha. $$
Also,
$$ \Pr(J_N=0) = \prod_{j=0}^{E-1} \frac{Nw_\beta+E-N(q-A)+H-j}{Nw_\beta+E-j} \longrightarrow(1-\alpha)^E. $$
There are both winning and losing opponent identities bidding $\beta$. Removing any winning focal identity allows a losing opponent identity bidding $\beta$ to replace it. Consequently, each winning focal identity pays $\beta$, and each losing identity pays zero. This proves the formulas when $\alpha<1$.

If $\alpha=1$, exactly $Nq$ opponents bid at least $\beta$. Focal identities bidding above $\beta$ win, those bidding below $\beta$ lose, and only finitely many identities bidding $\beta$ are excluded. Since $Nw_\beta$ grows linearly, every fixed focal identity bidding $\beta$ wins with probability tending to one.

Whenever at least one focal identity bids at least $\beta$, sufficiently large replications have a losing opponent identity bidding $\beta$. Winning focal identities therefore pay $\beta$. If no focal identity bids at least $\beta$, every focal identity loses and the total payment is zero. This establishes the same formulas for $\alpha=1$.

Therefore both expected sequences converge for every fixed finite action and rational nature state when $m=1$.

4.2 Truthful payment computation

For truthful opponents, $M=1$. If $q>1$, every truthful payment limit is zero.

If $q\leq1$, compute $\beta$ and $\alpha$ from the supported singleton values and their masses. A truthful focal bidder of type $t$, with value $v_t$, has payment limit
$$ P_t= \begin{cases} \beta,&v_t>\beta,\\ \alpha\beta,&v_t=\beta,\\ 0,&v_t<\beta. \end{cases} $$
Sorting the supported values, summing rational masses, and performing the displayed rational arithmetic take polynomial time in the input bit length. Thus the promised payment-computation question is completely resolved for one good.

5. DSL welfare failure with one identity per bidder

The empty action is allowed in the task, but welfare failure persists with universal participation. I first prove the relevant DSL claim against all finite Sybil deviations.

5.1 A zero-bid identity is pure DSL

Fix a one-good environment with rational supply $q>0$ and true value $V>0$. Let $z$ be the action consisting of one identity bidding zero.

For every nature state, its payment is zero. Indeed, a winning zero-bid identity contributes nothing to reported welfare; removing it leaves its opponents’ chosen allocation feasible with the same reported welfare. Its pivot payment is therefore zero. Consequently,
$$ U_V(z,\nu)\geq0 $$
for every $\nu$.

Fix an arbitrary finite deviation $a'$.

Empty deviation

Let opponents each bid zero, with rational identity mass
$$ M=\frac q\alpha, \qquad 0<\alpha<1. $$
By the one-good formula,
$$ U_V(z,\nu)=V\alpha>0, \qquad U_V(a',\nu)=0. $$
Taking rational $\alpha\downarrow0$ shows that, on the distinguishability set,
$$ \inf U_V(z,\nu)=0=\inf U_V(a',\nu). $$

Nonempty deviation containing only zero bids

Let $h$ be its number of identities. If $h=1$, the actions are identical and the distinguishability set is empty.

If $h>1$, use the same zero-bid opponents. Then
$$ U_V(z,\nu)=V\alpha, \qquad U_V(a',\nu)=V\bigl(1-(1-\alpha)^h\bigr). $$
For $0<\alpha<1$,
$$ 1-(1-\alpha)^h>\alpha, $$
because $(1-\alpha)^h<1-\alpha$. These states distinguish the actions. Both displayed utilities approach zero as rational $\alpha\downarrow0$.

Both actions always have nonnegative utility, since all their bids and payments are zero. Thus both infima over the distinguishability set equal zero.

Deviation with positive maximum bid

Let $d>0$ be its maximum bid, and let $h\geq1$ count identities bidding $d$.

Use opponents each submitting one bid $d$, with mass $q/\alpha$, where rational $0<\alpha<1$. The zero-bid action loses and has utility zero. The deviation’s limiting utility is
$$ f(\alpha) = V\bigl(1-(1-\alpha)^h\bigr)-hd\alpha. $$

There are three exhaustive cases.

If $hd>V$, then
$$ f(1)=V-hd<0. $$
By continuity, some rational $\alpha<1$ has $f(\alpha)<0$. This is a distinguishing state, so the deviation’s infimum is negative while the zero action’s infimum is zero.

If $hd\leq V$ and $(h,d)\neq(1,V)$, then $d<V$ and
$$ f(0)=0,\qquad f'(0)=h(V-d)>0. $$
Thus $f(\alpha)>0$ for all sufficiently small positive $\alpha$, and these positive values approach zero. These are distinguishing states where the zero action has utility zero. Hence the zero action’s infimum equals zero, and the deviation’s infimum is at most zero.

Finally, suppose $h=1$ and $d=V$. Let $e<V$ be the maximum of the other focal bids, taking $e=0$ if there are no others. Choose a rational bid
$$ e<r<V, $$
and opponents of mass $2q$ each bidding $r$. The focal identity bidding $V$ wins and pays $r$; every other focal identity loses. Thus
$$ U_V(a',\nu)=V-r>0, \qquad U_V(z,\nu)=0. $$
Taking rational $r\uparrow V$ again gives distinguishing positive deviation utilities approaching zero.

In every case, writing $D=D_V(z,a')$, the required inequality holds:
$$ \inf_{\nu\in D}U_V(z,\nu) \geq \inf_{\nu\in D}U_V(a',\nu). $$
The identical-action case uses the stipulated convention $\inf\varnothing=+\infty$. Therefore $z$ is pure DSL against every finite rational Sybil deviation.

5.2 A strictly inefficient participating profile

Take one good, $\epsilon=1$, supply $q=1/2$, and two types:
$$ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12, \qquad v_1(\{1\})=1, \qquad v_2(\{1\})=2. $$
Have both types use the single zero-bid identity action. By the preceding proof, each action is pure DSL for its respective type.

At every admissible replication, there are $N$ identities and $N/2$ copies. All reported welfare is zero. The bundle-size tie-break allocates all copies, and the final tie-break selects a uniformly random winning subset of size $N/2$. Every bidder wins with probability $1/2$. Therefore
$$ \frac{\mathbb E[W_N]}N = \frac12\left(\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot2\right) = \frac34. $$

The mass allocation assigning the good to every type-$2$ bidder has welfare one and uses exactly the available supply. Conversely, every allocated unit contributes at most two, so
$$ W^*\leq2q=1. $$
Hence $W^*=1$, and
$$ \liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[W_N]}N = \frac34<1=W^*. $$

This refutes the proposed universal DSL welfare guarantee with one identity per bidder. The construction uses arbitrarily large finite rational nature masses to obtain distinguishing positive utilities approaching zero. The finite discrete welfare theorem in [Gafni–Tennenholtz, Theorem 4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.15181v3) does not establish the assertion under these replica-limit and infimum definitions.

6. Existing truthful payment limits can be irrational

This example addresses the computational question on inputs where all truthful payment limits exist.

6.1 Society and optimal allocations

Take three goods, $\epsilon=1$, three types $A,B,C$, and
$$ (\mu_A,\mu_B,\mu_C) = \left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac12\right), $$
$$ (q_1,q_2,q_3) = \left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac12\right). $$
Set
$$ (p_1,p_2,p_3)=(1,2,3) $$
and define allowed-good sets
$$ E_A=\{1,2\},\qquad E_B=\{2,3\},\qquad E_C=\{1,3\}. $$
For every bundle $S$, define
$$ v_t(S)=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{p_g:g\in S\cap E_t\}\bigr). $$
These are explicitly specified monotone integer-valued valuations.

For every type and bundle,
$$ v_t(S)\leq\sum_{g\in S}p_g. $$
Equality holds exactly for the empty bundle or an allowed singleton. For a disallowed singleton the inequality is strict; for any bundle of size at least two, the sum of its positive good prices strictly exceeds its maximum.

Admissible replications are $N=4k$. Total available good-price value is
$$ \sum_gNq_gp_g=9k. $$
This is an upper bound on truthful welfare, even after adding a focal bidder.

It is attained without a focal bidder: give good $2$ to all $k$ type-$A$ bidders, good $3$ to all $k$ type-$B$ bidders, and divide the $2k$ type-$C$ bidders equally between goods $1$ and $3$. Thus welfare is exactly $9k$ both without and with any additional truthful focal bidder.

Equality in the welfare bound requires every copy to be allocated and every nonempty bundle to be an allowed singleton. With a focal bidder present, exactly $N$ identities receive allowed singletons and exactly one identity receives the empty bundle.

All such allocations have the same descending bundle-size list. Therefore the mechanism selects uniformly among them.

For every truthful focal type,
$$ G_N-F_N=0. $$
The pivot formula consequently gives, in every selected allocation,
$$ u_{t,N}=0, \qquad p_{t,N}=v_t(S_{t,N}). $$
It remains to find the focal bidder’s limiting singleton probabilities.

6.2 Counting uniformly selected allocations

Fix the type $t_0$ of the additional focal bidder. Let
$$ n_t=N\mu_t+\mathbf1_{\{t=t_0\}} $$
be the total number of identities of type $t$.

For each allowed pair $(t,g)$, let $k_{tg}$ be the number of type-$t$ identities receiving good $g$. Let $e_t$ count empty-bundle identities of type $t$. Then
$$ \sum_te_t=1, $$
$$ \sum_{g\in E_t}k_{tg}=n_t-e_t, \qquad \sum_{t:g\in E_t}k_{tg}=Nq_g. $$

For a feasible pattern $(k,e)$, the number of labeled identity allocations is
$$ \prod_t\frac{n_t!}{e_t!\prod_{g\in E_t}k_{tg}!} = \frac{\prod_tn_t!}{\prod_{t,g\in E_t}k_{tg}!}. $$
The last equality holds because each $e_t$ is zero or one.

The numerator is constant across patterns. Thus their probabilities are proportional to
$$ w_N(k)=\frac1{\prod_{t,g\in E_t}k_{tg}!}. $$
If copies themselves are distinguished, assigning the allocated copies introduces the additional constant factor $\prod_g(Nq_g)!$, so the same pattern probabilities result.

Set
$$ z_{tg}=\frac{k_{tg}}N, \qquad H(z)=-\sum_{t,g\in E_t}z_{tg}\log z_{tg}, $$
with $0\log0=0$.

There are six allowed entries, each at most $N$. The elementary factorial estimate
$$ \log(j!)=j\log j-j+O(\log(N+1)), \qquad 0\leq j\leq N, $$
holds uniformly; for $j\geq1$ it follows by comparing the sum of $\log i$ with its integral, and $j=0$ is immediate. Since $\sum_{t,g}k_{tg}=N$, it gives
$$ \log w_N(k) = -N\log N+N+NH(z)+O(\log(N+1)), $$
uniformly across feasible patterns.

Every subsequential limit of normalized feasible patterns has row sums $\mu_t$ and column sums $q_g$. Solving these equations shows that all limiting feasible patterns have the form
$$ z(x)= \begin{pmatrix} x&\frac14-x&0\\ 0&x&\frac14-x\\ \frac14-x&0&\frac14+x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad 0\leq x\leq\frac14. $$
The rows are $A,B,C$ and the columns are goods $1,2,3$.

6.3 The unique entropy maximizer

On this segment,
$$ H(x)= -2x\log x -3\left(\frac14-x\right)\log\left(\frac14-x\right) -\left(\frac14+x\right)\log\left(\frac14+x\right). $$
For $0<x<1/4$,
$$ H'(x) = \log \frac{(\frac14-x)^3}{x^2(\frac14+x)}, $$
and
$$ H''(x) = -\frac3{\frac14-x} -\frac2x -\frac1{\frac14+x} <0. $$
Moreover, $H'(x)$ tends to positive infinity at the left endpoint and negative infinity at the right endpoint. Hence there is a unique maximizer $x^*\in(0,1/4)$, characterized by
$$ (x^*)^2\left(\frac14+x^*\right) = \left(\frac14-x^*\right)^3. $$

I now justify concentration at this maximizer.

The normalized feasible patterns lie in a fixed compact set. Their row sums differ from the limiting row sums by at most $1/N$, so every accumulation point belongs to the segment above. Uniqueness of the maximizer and continuity of $H$ imply the following: outside any fixed neighborhood of $z(x^*)$, all normalized feasible patterns have entropy at most $H(x^*)-\eta$ for some $\eta>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$. Otherwise, a convergent subsequence outside that neighborhood would produce a second maximizer.

There is also a feasible pattern converging to $z(x^*)$: leave the additional focal bidder empty and use the baseline row counts, choosing the integer entry $k_{A1}$ nearest to $Nx^*$. The other five entries are determined by the displayed matrix. They are nonnegative for sufficiently large $N$ because $x^*$ is strictly interior.

There are at most $3(N+1)^6$ feasible patterns. Comparing their weights using the uniform factorial estimate, the probability of patterns outside the fixed neighborhood is bounded, for sufficiently large $N$, by
$$ 3(N+1)^6\exp\left(-\frac{N\eta}{2}+O(\log(N+1))\right), $$
which tends to zero.

Therefore normalized counts converge in probability to $z(x^*)$. Because they are bounded, their expectations also converge to $z(x^*)$.

6.4 Irrational payment limits

Identities of the same type are exchangeable under the uniform allocation rule. Thus, for a focal bidder of type $t$,
$$ \Pr(S_{t,N}=\{g\}) = \frac{\mathbb E[k_{tg}]}{n_t}. $$
Since $n_t/N\to\mu_t$,
$$ P_t= \frac{\sum_{g\in E_t}p_gz_{tg}(x^*)}{\mu_t}. $$
All three limits therefore exist.

Let
$$ y=4x^*\in(0,1). $$
The equation for $x^*$ becomes
$$ y^2(1+y)=(1-y)^3, $$
or
$$ 2y^3-2y^2+3y-1=0. $$
The payment limits are
$$ P_A=2-y,\qquad P_B=3-y,\qquad P_C=2+y. $$

The polynomial has no rational root. By the rational-root theorem, its only possible rational roots are
$$ 1,\quad -1,\quad \frac12,\quad -\frac12. $$
Its values at these candidates are, respectively,
$$ 2,\quad -8,\quad \frac14,\quad -\frac{13}{4}. $$
Thus $y$ is irrational, and so are all three payment limits.

This establishes that the existence promise alone does not make truthful payment limits rational. An exact computation problem for these limits must accommodate irrational outputs, for example through algebraic-number representations. Irrationality by itself does not establish computational hardness.

7. What remains open

The convergence and universal DSL welfare questions have negative answers. Exact mass allocation has a polynomial-time algorithm. The one-good payment problem is polynomial-time solvable, and the three-good example proves that existing truthful payment limits can be irrational algebraic numbers.

The unresolved part is the general complexity of exactly computing existing truthful payment limits, using an output representation that accommodates their possible values. No general polynomial-time algorithm or NP-hardness reduction is proved here.
