The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), home categories \(X\) with rational supplies \(q_x\), and each type’s encoded general unit-demand preference over \(X\cup\{0\}\) and payments, compute an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and allocation masses \(a_{t,x}\) at the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector. Require \(\sum_{x\in X}a_{t,x}\le \mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}a_{t,x}\le q_x\), and \(a_{t,x}>0\) only when \(x\) is demanded by type \(t\) at prices \(p\); any unassigned mass must demand \(0\), and any unused supply must have price zero. For a finite input encoding, represent preferences by rational piecewise-linear utilities that are strictly decreasing in payment and need not be quasi-linear.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) home categories \(X\), each with positive binary-rational supply \(q_x\); supplies need not sum to \(1\).
For each \(t\) and \(x\in X\cup\{0\}\), utility \(u_{t,x}:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is continuous, strictly decreasing in payment, and explicitly encoded by finitely many rational breakpoints and rational affine pieces, including both unbounded tails. Utility values may have either sign; quasi-linearity is not required. Every bundle has an indifferent bundle in every other category at some finite payment.
Prices satisfy \(p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{X}\) and \(p_0=0\), with zero reserve prices. Define \(D_t(p)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in X\cup\{0\}}u_{t,x}(p_x)\), retaining all ties.
An equilibrium consists of prices and allocation masses \(a_{t,x}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_xa_{t,x}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_ta_{t,x}\le q_x\), and \(a_{t,x}>0\Rightarrow x\in D_t(p)\). If \(\sum_xa_{t,x}<\mu_t\), then \(0\in D_t(p)\); if \(\sum_ta_{t,x}<q_x\), then \(p_x=0\). Mass may split among demanded categories.
The required output is an exact equilibrium \((p^{\min},a)\) with \(p^{\min}_x\le p_x\) for every category and every equilibrium price vector \(p\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including all utility pieces.

Is exact computation of this least-price equilibrium polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents and indivisible objects, the Serial Vickrey mechanism finds a minimum-price equilibrium after finitely many indifference-price reports; this guarantee does not assert polynomial running time. [Zhou–Serizawa](https://www.iser.osaka-u.ac.jp/static/resources/docs/dp/2020/DP1095.pdf)
A strongly polynomial algorithm is known for discrete assignment markets under an additional consistency restriction on utilities. [Chen–Deng–Ghosh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.2565) No result settling the exact mass problem above was located.

  • Required positive supplies for listed categories, reflecting actual objects in the discrete model; imposed no supply normalization or relation between total supply and society mass.
  • Retained all demand ties and allowed type mass to split among demanded categories, with residual mass assigned to the outside option.
  • Specified exact equilibrium output and total binary input length as the computational encoding.

Partial resolution: an exact reduction to unique equilibrium prices

I do not settle polynomial-time solvability versus NP-hardness when both $m$ and $\tau$ vary.

The main new result is an exact perturbation theorem:

Let $D$ be a common denominator of all masses and supplies. Choose a positive dyadic rational $\varepsilon$ satisfying $m\varepsilon<1/D$, and replace every supply $q_x$ by $q_x+\varepsilon$. The perturbed market has a unique equilibrium price vector, and that vector is exactly the original market’s least equilibrium price vector.

Thus computing least prices reduces, in polynomial time and with one oracle call, to computing any equilibrium in an explicitly constructed market with unique equilibrium prices. A supporting allocation for the original market can then be recovered by a flow computation.

I prove this theorem below. I also give an explicit counterexample to a price-update rule that overlooks demand changes within the categories whose prices are being increased.

1. Notation and equilibrium existence

Let $\mathcal T$ denote the listed support of types. Introduce outside allocation by defining

$$ a_{t,0}:=\mu_t-\sum_{x\in X}a_{t,x}. $$

The allocation conditions become

$$ \sum_{x\in X\cup\{0\}}a_{t,x}=\mu_t, \qquad a_{t,x}>0\Longrightarrow x\in D_t(p). $$

For subsets $A\subseteq\mathcal T$ and $S\subseteq X$, write

$$ \mu(A):=\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t, \qquad q(S):=\sum_{x\in S}q_x. $$

Set

$$ c_t:=u_{t,0}(0), \qquad V_t(p):=\max_{x\in X\cup\{0\}}u_{t,x}(p_x). $$

Every affine piece on an interval of positive length has negative slope. In particular, the two unbounded tails show that every utility function has range $\mathbb R$. Consequently, for each real category there is a unique reservation payment $r_{t,x}$ satisfying

$$ u_{t,x}(r_{t,x})=c_t. $$

It is rational and computable by scanning the encoded pieces. Define

$$ R_x:=\max\left\{0,\max_{t\in\mathcal T}r_{t,x}\right\}. $$

These numbers have polynomial encoding length.

Proposition 1. Every valid market has an equilibrium, and every equilibrium price satisfies $0\le p_x\le R_x$.

Proof. Set $B_x:=R_x+1$. Let $\mathcal A$ be the product of the allocation simplexes with row sums $\mu_t$, including outside allocation; temporarily omit supply constraints. For prices $p$, let $\mathcal A(p)$ contain the allocations supported on $D_t(p)$.

The correspondence $\mathcal A(p)$ has nonempty compact convex values. Its graph is closed: if $p^{(n)}\to p$, $a^{(n)}\to a$, and $a_{t,x}>0$, then eventually $a^{(n)}_{t,x}>0$; continuity therefore gives $x\in D_t(p)$.

On the compact convex domain

$$ \prod_{x\in X}[0,B_x]\times\mathcal A, $$

define

$$ H_x(p,a):= \min\left\{ B_x,\max\left\{0,p_x+\sum_ta_{t,x}-q_x\right\} \right\}, $$

and

$$ F(p,a):=\{H(p,a)\}\times\mathcal A(p). $$

The map $H$ is continuous. The closed graph of $\mathcal A(p)$, together with its compact codomain, gives upper hemicontinuity. Thus $F$ satisfies the hypotheses of Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem: its domain is nonempty, compact, and convex, and its values are nonempty, compact, and convex.

At a fixed point, $p_x=B_x$ is impossible. Every type then strictly prefers its outside option to category $x$, so its allocation is zero and $H_x(p,a)<B_x$, because $q_x>0$.

With the upper boundary excluded, the fixed-point equation implies

$$ p_x>0\Longrightarrow\sum_ta_{t,x}=q_x, \qquad p_x=0\Longrightarrow\sum_ta_{t,x}\le q_x. $$

These are precisely the remaining equilibrium conditions.

Finally, in any equilibrium, a positive-price category is filled. Since its supply is positive, some type demands it, giving

$$ u_{t,x}(p_x)\ge c_t, $$

and hence $p_x\le r_{t,x}\le R_x$. Zero prices satisfy the bound as well. $\square$

The equilibrium conditions are closed under limits. Indeed, demand support and market clearing can be written as

$$ a_{t,x}\bigl(V_t(p)-u_{t,x}(p_x)\bigr)=0, $$

and

$$ p_x\left(q_x-\sum_ta_{t,x}\right)=0, $$

along with the linear feasibility conditions. This observation also applies when supplies converge.

2. An exact flow criterion at fixed prices

Fix a nonnegative price vector $p$. Define

$$ P(p):=\{x\in X:p_x>0\}, \qquad \mathcal F(p):=\{t\in\mathcal T:0\notin D_t(p)\}. $$

For $A\subseteq\mathcal F(p)$ and $S\subseteq X$, define

$$ N_p(A):=\bigcup_{t\in A}D_t(p)\subseteq X, $$

and

$$ T_p(S):=\{t\in\mathcal T:D_t(p)\cap S\ne\varnothing\}. $$

Lemma 2. Prices $p$ admit an equilibrium allocation if and only if both families of inequalities hold:

$$ \mu(A)\le q(N_p(A)) \qquad \text{for every }A\subseteq\mathcal F(p), $$

and

$$ q(S)\le\mu(T_p(S)) \qquad \text{for every }S\subseteq P(p). $$

Proof. Necessity follows directly from allocation feasibility. Types in $A$ must allocate their entire mass within $N_p(A)$. Categories in $S$ must be filled, and only types in $T_p(S)$ can receive them.

For sufficiency, construct a circulation network with vertices $s,z$, all types, and all categories including $0$:

  • The arc $s\to t$ has lower and upper bounds both equal to $\mu_t$.
  • The arc $t\to x$ exists exactly when $x\in D_t(p)$ and has lower bound zero and sufficiently large upper bound.
  • Each real-category arc $x\to z$ has upper bound $q_x$ and lower bound $q_x$ if $x\in P(p)$, otherwise zero.
  • The outside arc $0\to z$ has bounds $[0,1]$.
  • The arc $z\to s$ has lower bound zero and sufficiently large upper bound.

A feasible circulation is exactly a supporting allocation. All nominally unbounded capacities may be replaced by a rational number larger than the sum of all finite lower bounds.

The feasible-circulation theorem states that such a circulation exists exactly when, for every vertex subset $U$, the sum of lower bounds entering $U$ is at most the sum of upper bounds leaving $U$.

Here are all possible nontrivial cut conditions. Let $A$ and $B$ be the type and category vertices in $U$.

If neither $s$ nor $z$ belongs to $U$, a cut can fail only when $N_p(A)\subseteq B$; its condition is

$$ \mu(A)\le\sum_{x\in B}\operatorname{cap}(x\to z). $$

If $0\in B$, the right side is at least $1$, so this holds. Otherwise $A\subseteq\mathcal F(p)$, and the first family implies it.

If both $s$ and $z$ belong to $U$, a cut can fail only when $N_p(A)\subseteq B$. Set $S:=P(p)\setminus B$. The condition is

$$ q(S)\le\mu(\mathcal T\setminus A). $$

Since $T_p(S)\subseteq\mathcal T\setminus A$, the second family implies it.

If $s\in U$ and $z\notin U$, every incoming lower bound is zero. If $s\notin U$ and $z\in U$, the large-capacity arc $z\to s$ prevents failure. These exhaust the cases, proving sufficiency. $\square$

Although these inequalities are exponentially numerous, feasibility at a specified rational $p$ is computable in polynomial time through the circulation network.

3. Strictly increasing every supply orders all equilibrium prices

The following comparison does not assume that least prices already exist.

Lemma 3. Consider two markets with identical types, masses, and utilities, but supplies $q$ and $\widehat q$ satisfying

$$ \widehat q_x>q_x \qquad \text{for every }x\in X. $$

If $\widehat p$ is any equilibrium price vector for $\widehat q$ and $r$ is any equilibrium price vector for $q$, then

$$ \widehat p\le r $$

coordinatewise.

Proof. Suppose

$$ S:=\{x:r_x<\widehat p_x\} $$

is nonempty. Every category in $S$ has positive price at $\widehat p$, so filling these categories requires

$$ \mu(T_{\widehat p}(S))\ge\widehat q(S). $$

For $t\in T_{\widehat p}(S)$, choose $x\in D_t(\widehat p)\cap S$. Strict decrease gives

$$ u_{t,x}(r_x)>u_{t,x}(\widehat p_x)=V_t(\widehat p). $$

For every real $y\notin S$,

$$ u_{t,y}(r_y)\le u_{t,y}(\widehat p_y)\le V_t(\widehat p). $$

The outside utility is unchanged and also at most $V_t(\widehat p)$. Thus

$$ D_t(r)\subseteq S, \qquad 0\notin D_t(r). $$

At prices $r$, every type in $T_{\widehat p}(S)$ must allocate its entire mass within $S$. Consequently,

$$ \mu(T_{\widehat p}(S))\le q(S). $$

Combining the inequalities contradicts $\widehat q(S)>q(S)$. $\square$

4. Least prices exist and satisfy strict filling inequalities

Theorem 4. The original market has a least equilibrium price vector $p^*$. Moreover,

$$ q(S)<\mu(T_{p^*}(S)) $$

for every nonempty $S\subseteq P(p^*)$.

Proof. For each positive integer $n$, increase every supply by $1/n$ and choose an equilibrium $(p^{(n)},a^{(n)})$ using Proposition 1.

The price bounds $R_x$ are independent of supplies, and allocations lie in fixed compact simplexes. A subsequence therefore converges to some $(p^*,a^*)$. Closedness of the equilibrium conditions shows that this limit is an equilibrium for the original supplies.

Lemma 3 gives

$$ p^{(n)}\le r $$

for every original equilibrium price vector $r$. Passing to the limit proves $p^*\le r$. Thus $p^*$ is least.

Fix a nonempty $S\subseteq P(p^*)$. Along the convergent subsequence, all categories in $S$ eventually have positive prices.

Also, eventually

$$ T_{p^{(n)}}(S)\subseteq T_{p^*}(S). $$

To justify this inclusion, if a type does not belong to $T_{p^*}(S)$, every category in $S$ has a strictly positive utility gap from that type’s maximum at $p^*$. Continuity preserves these finitely many strict gaps in a neighborhood of $p^*$.

Filling $S$ in a sufficiently late perturbed equilibrium therefore gives

$$ q(S)+\frac{|S|}{n} \le \mu(T_{p^{(n)}}(S)) \le \mu(T_{p^*}(S)). $$

The first expression is strictly greater than $q(S)$, proving the assertion. $\square$

This proof establishes least prices without constructing a logarithmic transportation objective or a local price-descent direction.

5. The exact perturbation theorem

Let $D$ be a common positive integer denominator of all $\mu_t$ and $q_x$. For example, the product of their encoded denominators works and has polynomial bit length.

Choose a positive dyadic rational $\varepsilon$ such that

$$ m\varepsilon<\frac1D, $$

and define

$$ q^\varepsilon_x:=q_x+\varepsilon. $$

Such an $\varepsilon$ is computable with polynomial encoding length.

Theorem 5. The market with supplies $q^\varepsilon$ has a unique equilibrium price vector. It equals the original least price vector $p^*$.

Proof.

First, $p^*$ remains an equilibrium price vector after the perturbation.

The upper inequalities of Lemma 2 continue to hold because supplies increase. For each nonempty $S\subseteq P(p^*)$, Theorem 4 gives

$$ \mu(T_{p^*}(S))-q(S)>0. $$

This difference is an integer multiple of $1/D$, so it is at least $1/D$. Hence

$$ q^\varepsilon(S) = q(S)+|S|\varepsilon < q(S)+\frac1D \le \mu(T_{p^*}(S)). $$

The lower inequalities of Lemma 2 also hold. Therefore $p^*$ supports an equilibrium allocation for $q^\varepsilon$.

Second, there are no subset-mass equalities in the perturbed market. For every $A\subseteq\mathcal T$ and nonempty $S\subseteq X$, put

$$ d:=\mu(A)-q(S)\in\frac1D\mathbb Z. $$

If $d\le0$, then $d-|S|\varepsilon<0$. If $d>0$, then

$$ d-|S|\varepsilon \ge \frac1D-m\varepsilon > 0. $$

Thus

$$ \mu(A)\ne q^\varepsilon(S) $$

for all such pairs.

Finally, suppose $p$ and $r$ were distinct equilibrium price vectors for the perturbed market. Interchanging their names if necessary, assume

$$ S:=\{x:r_x<p_x\} $$

is nonempty. Filling $S$ at $p$ gives

$$ q^\varepsilon(S)\le\mu(T_p(S)). $$

The strict-decrease argument in Lemma 3 shows that all types in $T_p(S)$ demand only categories in $S$ at $r$, excluding outside allocation. Therefore

$$ \mu(T_p(S))\le q^\varepsilon(S). $$

Equality follows, contradicting the absence of subset-mass equalities. Hence equilibrium prices are unique, and must equal $p^*$. $\square$

Computational consequence

The perturbation changes only the supplies and increases input length polynomially. Therefore the following procedure is exact:

  • Construct $q^\varepsilon$.
  • Compute any equilibrium of the perturbed market.
  • Retain its prices.
  • At those prices, compute an allocation for the original supplies using Lemma 2.

This is a polynomial-time reduction. The unresolved operation is step 2.

There is also a converse characterization useful for verification:

$$ p=p^* \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\text{ admits an equilibrium allocation for }q^\varepsilon. $$

Thus least-price verification at a specified rational price vector requires only utility evaluation and a flow feasibility check.

6. Polynomial certificates and oracle upper bounds

For completeness, the perturbation argument also gives a short proof of the polynomial output-length result.

Construct a rational hyperplane arrangement in price space containing:

  • Utility breakpoint hyperplanes.
  • The boundaries $p_x=0$ and $p_x=R_x$.
  • Equalities between affine utility pieces for the same type.
  • Equalities between affine utility pieces and $c_t$.

Its size and coefficient lengths are polynomial in $L$. On every relatively open face within the price box, all demand sets and all price-zero statuses are constant.

Let $F$ be the face containing the unique perturbed equilibrium prices $p^*$. A supporting allocation at $p^*$ supports every point of $F$: demand sets and mandatory filling conditions are unchanged. Uniqueness therefore forces $F$ to be zero-dimensional.

Consequently, $p^*$ satisfies $m$ independent rational hyperplane equations from the arrangement. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule gives a polynomial bound on the encoding length of every coordinate.

At these prices, an original supporting allocation also has polynomial encoding length. Scaling the circulation network by $D$ gives integral bounds, and feasible integral circulation yields allocation masses that are integer multiples of $1/D$.

It follows that, for a rational threshold $h$, the problem

$$ p^*_x\le h $$

belongs to $\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}$. For either answer, a certificate is the polynomial-length vector $p^*$: verify perturbed equilibrium feasibility by flow, then check the appropriate comparison with $h$.

Exact computation also belongs to $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$. With a fixed polynomial-length certificate encoding, an NP oracle can test whether a proposed prefix extends to a valid least-price certificate. Determining successive bits produces $p^*$, after which an original allocation is obtained by flow.

These upper bounds do not establish polynomial-time computation.

7. Dependence on masses and supplies is through subset-sum signs

The flow criterion gives another structural consequence.

Proposition 6. Fix the utilities and the support of types. If two positive mass-and-supply vectors have the same signs of

$$ \mu(A)-q(S) $$

for every $A\subseteq\mathcal T$ and $S\subseteq X$, then their equilibrium price sets are identical. In particular, their least price vectors are identical.

Proof. At any fixed $p$, the sets $\mathcal F(p)$, $P(p)$, $N_p(A)$, and $T_p(S)$ depend only on utilities and prices.

Every inequality in Lemma 2 is determined by one of the specified subset-sum signs. Thus the two markets admit an equilibrium allocation at exactly the same price vectors. $\square$

If no equality $\mu(A)=q(S)$ holds for nonempty $S$, the uniqueness proof in Theorem 5 applies directly. Such a market has unique equilibrium prices. Furthermore, the finitely many nonzero subset-sum signs persist under sufficiently small changes in masses and supplies, so those prices are locally constant.

The perturbation in Theorem 5 preserves every nonzero subset-sum sign and changes every equality to

$$ \mu(A)-q^\varepsilon(S)<0. $$

The exact selection of least prices therefore has a finite combinatorial interpretation in the space of masses and supplies.

8. A verified obstruction to an ascending-price argument

A tempting rule raises prices of the categories in an alternating tree until either an outside category becomes demanded or a utility breakpoint is reached. With unequal slopes, demand can change between categories already inside that tree.

This issue is explicitly identified in footnote 13 of [Oki–Sato, *Ascending Auctions for Combinatorial Markets with Frictions*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10563). The following example independently demonstrates the failure.

Take two categories and four types $a,b,c,d$, with

$$ \mu_a=\mu_b=\mu_c=\mu_d=\frac14, \qquad q_1=q_2=\frac14. $$

Every outside utility is $u_{t,0}(z)=-z$. The real-category utilities are globally affine:

| Type | $u_{t,1}(z)$ | $u_{t,2}(z)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $a$ | $2-z$ | $-100-z$ |
| $b$ | $10-z$ | $10-z$ |
| $c$ | $9-z/8$ | $10-z$ |
| $d$ | $-1-z$ | $-1-z$ |

All input data are dyadic rational. All utilities satisfy the task’s continuity, strict decrease, tail, and indifference requirements.

At zero prices,

$$ D_a=\{1\}, \qquad D_b=\{1,2\}, \qquad D_c=\{2\}, \qquad D_d=\{0\}. $$

Consider the partial allocation assigning $b$ to category $1$ and $c$ to category $2$, with $a$ awaiting allocation. Its alternating tree contains both real categories. The direction

$$ d=(1,1) $$

preserves all its demanded edges for sufficiently small positive increases: all utility slopes on those edges have magnitude $1$.

Along prices $p(s)=(s,s)$, however, type $c$ becomes indifferent between the two real categories when

$$ 9-\frac{s}{8}=10-s, $$

namely at

$$ s=\frac87. $$

For larger $s$, category $2$ ceases to be demanded by $c$. This is an internal demand change: both categories already belong to the tree.

There are no utility breakpoints. The first outside-option event occurs at $s=2$, when type $a$ becomes indifferent to outside allocation. Thus a rule ignoring internal changes can update prices to $(2,2)$.

But the least prices are

$$ p^*=\left(2,\frac54\right). $$

Here is a complete verification.

If an equilibrium had $p_1<2$, type $a$ would demand only category $1$ and fill it. Type $b$ could not take outside allocation, because $10-p_1>0$, so it would fill category $2$. Type $c$ would also require a real category, because $9-p_1/8>0$. No capacity would remain. Hence every equilibrium satisfies $p_1\ge2$.

If an equilibrium had $p_2<5/4$, then

$$ u_{c,2}(p_2)>\frac{35}{4} \ge u_{c,1}(p_1), $$

so type $c$ would demand only category $2$ and fill it. Type $b$ would require a real category and therefore receive category $1$. For category $1$ to be demanded by $b$, one would need

$$ p_1\le p_2, $$

contradicting $p_1\ge2>p_2$. Thus every equilibrium also satisfies $p_2\ge5/4$.

At $(2,5/4)$, allocate $b$ to category $2$, $c$ to category $1$, and $a,d$ outside, each with mass $1/4$. Every allocated bundle is demanded and both positive-price categories are filled. This proves the displayed vector is least.

The update to $(2,2)$ therefore overshoots the least price of category $2$. Accounting for internal demand changes is necessary even with globally affine utilities.

9. What remains unresolved

The perturbation theorem removes the need to construct least prices separately from arbitrary equilibrium prices: one exact equilibrium computation in the perturbed market suffices.

It does not provide that computation in polynomial time. Nor does the internal-demand counterexample establish hardness; it only rules out the stated shortcut in an ascending-price argument.

The general classification remains open in this write-up.
