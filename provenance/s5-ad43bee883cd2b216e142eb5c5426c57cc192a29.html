The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite outcome set \(\mathcal O\), a harmful event \(A\subseteq\mathcal O\), and supported types \(t\in T\) with strictly positive rational distributions \(q_t\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, form the log-pooled baseline \(P_\mu(o)\propto\prod_{t\in T}q_t(o)^{\mu_t}\). Let \(v_t=\log q_t-\mathbb E_{P_\mu}[\log q_t]\), \(g_A=\mathbf 1_A-P_\mu(A)\), and \(D_\mu=\operatorname{span}\{v_t-v_{t_0}:t\in T\}\) for any supported reference type \(t_0\). For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), determine the optimal change and value and return a realizing \(\delta\).

1. The linear map and the remaining rank question

Number the supported types $0,\ldots,n$, where $n=\tau-1$, and abbreviate $P=P_\mu$. Define
$$ f_j=\log q_j-\log q_0,\qquad d_j=f_j-\mathbb E_P[f_j],\qquad 1\leq j\leq n. $$
Thus $d_j=v_j-v_0$. Let
$$ D:\mathbb R^n\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{\mathcal O}, \qquad Dx=\sum_{j=1}^n x_jd_j. $$
Its image is $D_\mu$. Its adjoint, using the Euclidean inner product on $\mathbb R^n$ and the prescribed $P$-inner product on functions, is
$$ (D^\ast h)_j=\langle d_j,h\rangle_P. $$
Set
$$ G=D^\ast D,\qquad b=D^\ast g_A. $$
In particular,
$$ G_{ij}=\langle d_i,d_j\rangle_P,\qquad b_j=\langle d_j,g_A\rangle_P. $$

Mass conservation gives $\delta_0=-\sum_{j=1}^n\delta_j$, hence
$$ \sum_{t=0}^n\delta_tv_t = \sum_{j=1}^n\delta_j(v_j-v_0) = D(\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_n)^{\mathsf T}. $$
Therefore every feasible $h$ belongs to $D_\mu$.

Because
$$ x^{\mathsf T}Gx=\|Dx\|_P^2, $$
the matrix $G$ is positive semidefinite and $\ker G=\ker D$. Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}G=\dim D_\mu. $$

Fix an outcome $o_\ast$. Define the $(m-1)\times n$ contrast matrix
$$ B_{o,j} = \log\frac{q_j(o)q_0(o_\ast)} {q_j(o_\ast)q_0(o)}, \qquad o\neq o_\ast. $$
Its entries are logarithms of positive rationals.

The contrast map
$$ h\longmapsto \bigl(h(o)-h(o_\ast)\bigr)_{o\neq o_\ast} $$
is an isomorphism from the space of $P$-centered functions to $\mathbb R^{m-1}$. Indeed, a centered function with zero contrasts is a centered constant and therefore zero. Conversely, prescribed contrasts can be realized by first assigning a function with those contrasts and then subtracting its $P$-mean.

This map sends $d_j$ to column $j$ of $B$. Thus
$$ r:=\dim D_\mu = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}B = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}G. $$
In particular, $r$ depends on the supported profiles, but not on their positive masses.

2. Polynomial-time construction when the rank is supplied

2.1 A polynomial acting as the inverse on the range

Assume temporarily that the correct integer $r>0$ is supplied.

Let the positive eigenvalues of $G$ be $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r$. For $0\leq k\leq n$, let $e_k$ be the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree $k$ in all eigenvalues of $G$, with $e_0=1$. Since the other eigenvalues are zero,
$$ e_r=\prod_{a=1}^r\lambda_a>0, \qquad e_k=0\quad(k>r). $$
Also,
$$ \kappa:=\frac{e_{r-1}}{e_r} = \sum_{a=1}^r\frac1{\lambda_a}>0. $$

These coefficients have polynomial-size arithmetic DAGs. Specifically, Newton’s identities give
$$ e_k = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}\operatorname{tr}(G^i), \qquad 1\leq k\leq n. $$
For completeness, these identities follow by taking the formal logarithmic derivative of
$$ \prod_{a=1}^n(1+z\lambda_a) = \sum_{k=0}^n e_kz^k $$
and using
$$ \sum_{a=1}^n\frac{\lambda_a}{1+z\lambda_a} = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{i-1}\operatorname{tr}(G^i)z^{i-1}. $$
Comparing coefficients proves the recurrence.

Define the matrix polynomial
$$ R_r(G) = \frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{e_r} \sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(-1)^k e_kG^{r-1-k}. $$
The polynomial whose roots are the positive eigenvalues is
$$ \phi(x) = x^r-e_1x^{r-1}+\cdots+(-1)^re_r. $$
For every positive eigenvalue $\lambda$,
$$ \lambda \sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(-1)^ke_k\lambda^{r-1-k} = (-1)^{r+1}e_r. $$
Therefore
$$ R_r(\lambda)=\frac1\lambda. $$
At zero,
$$ R_r(0)=\frac{e_{r-1}}{e_r}=\kappa. $$
It follows from the spectral theorem that $R_r(G)$ is positive definite. On $\operatorname{range}G$, it acts exactly as the inverse of $G$.

We also have $b\in\operatorname{range}G$. To prove this, take $x\in\ker G=\ker D$. Then
$$ x^{\mathsf T}b=\langle Dx,g_A\rangle_P=0. $$
Thus $b$ is orthogonal to $\ker G$, which equals the range of the symmetric matrix $G$.

Set
$$ c=R_r(G)b,\qquad p=Dc. $$
The range-inverse property gives $Gc=b$. Hence
$$ \langle d_j,g_A-p\rangle_P = b_j-(Gc)_j=0 $$
for every generator $d_j$. Consequently, $p$ is the $P$-orthogonal projection of $g_A$ onto $D_\mu$.

Moreover,
$$ \|p\|_P^2 = c^{\mathsf T}Gc = c^{\mathsf T}b = b^{\mathsf T}R_r(G)b. $$
If $b\neq0$, positive definiteness of $R_r(G)$ implies that
$$ s:=\sqrt{b^{\mathsf T}R_r(G)b}>0. $$

2.2 A radius valid without selecting a basis

Define
$$ \rho= \left(\sum_{t=0}^n\frac1{\mu_t}\right)^{-1}, \qquad C=\sqrt{n\kappa}, \qquad \varepsilon_0=\frac{\rho}{2(1+C)}. $$
All three quantities are positive, and $\rho\leq\mu_t$ for every supported type.

We show that every $h\in D_\mu$ has a realizing coefficient vector $x$ satisfying
$$ \|x\|_2^2\leq\kappa\|h\|_P^2. $$

Choose Euclidean orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1,\ldots,u_r$ of $G$ associated with its positive eigenvalues. The functions
$$ w_a=\frac{Du_a}{\sqrt{\lambda_a}} $$
form a $P$-orthonormal basis of $D_\mu$: their inner products are
$$ \langle w_a,w_b\rangle_P = \frac{u_a^{\mathsf T}Gu_b}{\sqrt{\lambda_a\lambda_b}} = \begin{cases} 1,&a=b,\\ 0,&a\neq b. \end{cases} $$
They span $D_\mu$ because $D$ vanishes on $\ker G$.

Write $h=\sum_a\alpha_aw_a$, and choose
$$ x=\sum_{a=1}^r\frac{\alpha_a}{\sqrt{\lambda_a}}u_a. $$
Then $Dx=h$, and
$$ \|x\|_2^2 = \sum_{a=1}^r\frac{\alpha_a^2}{\lambda_a} \leq \kappa\sum_{a=1}^r\alpha_a^2 = \kappa\|h\|_P^2. $$
This $x$ belongs to $(\ker D)^\perp$ and is the unique realizing coefficient vector in that subspace.

Now suppose $\|h\|_P\leq\varepsilon$. Define
$$ \delta_j=x_j\quad(1\leq j\leq n), \qquad \delta_0=-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j. $$
These coordinates conserve mass and realize $h$. Since $n\geq1$,
$$ |\delta_j|\leq\|x\|_2\leq C\varepsilon, \qquad |\delta_0|\leq\sqrt n\,\|x\|_2\leq C\varepsilon. $$
For $0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$,
$$ |\delta_t| < \frac{\rho C}{2(1+C)} < \frac{\rho}{2} \leq \frac{\mu_t}{2}. $$
Thus every reweighted mass remains positive.

We have proved that, on this radius, the feasible set of $h$ is exactly
$$ \{h\in D_\mu:\|h\|_P\leq\varepsilon\}. $$

2.3 Exact optimal outputs

For $h\in D_\mu$, projection orthogonality and Cauchy–Schwarz give
$$ -\langle h,g_A\rangle_P = -\langle h,p\rangle_P \leq \|h\|_P\|p\|_P \leq \varepsilon s. $$
When $b\neq0$, equality is attained by the following outputs:
$$ V^\star(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon s, \qquad h^\star(\varepsilon)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{s}Dc, $$
$$ \delta_j^\star(\varepsilon) = -\frac{\varepsilon c_j}{s} \quad(1\leq j\leq n), \qquad \delta_0^\star(\varepsilon) = \frac{\varepsilon}{s}\sum_{j=1}^n c_j. $$
The coefficient vector $c$ lies in $\operatorname{range}G=(\ker D)^\perp$. Thus the coefficient bound just proved applies to these realizing coordinates, establishing feasibility for every $0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$.

If $b=0$, the objective vanishes on all of $D_\mu$, and therefore on every feasible perturbation. In this case one can return
$$ \varepsilon_0=1,\qquad V^\star=0,\qquad h^\star=0,\qquad\delta^\star=0. $$
This also covers $r=0$ and $n=0$.

2.4 Bit complexity

The probabilities have permitted DAG representations:
$$ W_o=\exp\left(\sum_t\mu_t\log q_t(o)\right), \qquad Z=\sum_oW_o, \qquad P(o)=W_o/Z. $$
The denominator $Z$ is positive.

Constructing $d_j$, $G$, and $b$ takes polynomially many DAG nodes. Matrix powers through $G^n$, Newton’s recurrence, $R_r(G)$, and all output coordinates also take polynomially many nodes. Intermediate expressions are shared rather than expanded.

The only divisions beyond rational constants and positive pooling denominators are by $e_r$, $s$, and positive quantities in the radius. Their validity was proved above. Every square root has a positive argument in the nonzero branch.

The zero/nonzero branch is decidable in polynomial time by Section 3.

Proved conclusion. With the correct rank $r$ supplied as a promise, the entire local problem is solvable in polynomial Turing time. In particular, one query returning the real rank of $B$ suffices; no basis-selection queries are needed.

3. Polynomial-time decision of the zero-objective branch

Here is a self-contained proof of the decision procedure used above.

3.1 Multiplicative normal form

In polynomial time, construct pairwise coprime integers $a_1,\ldots,a_\ell>1$, none a perfect power, and integers $z_{t,o,k}$ such that
$$ q_t(o)=\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}a_k^{z_{t,o,k}}. $$

To do this without factorization, start with a tagged multiset of the nonunit numerators and denominators of all reduced input probabilities. Whenever two unequal factor values have a nonunit gcd, properly split at least one of them by that gcd, retaining its original tag on both pieces. Such a proper split exists: otherwise both values would equal their gcd.

Every split increases the number of factors and preserves their product. The logarithm to base two of that product is at most $L$, so there are at most $L$ factors and at most $L$ splits. At termination, distinct factor values are pairwise coprime.

For each distinct value $c$, test integer roots to find its largest perfect-power exponent, and write $c=a^d$. Exponents up to $\log_2c$ suffice. The resulting $a$ is not a perfect power; taking these roots preserves pairwise coprimality. Reassembling the tagged factors produces the integers $z_{t,o,k}$.

Gcd computations and integer-root tests take polynomial time. The number and bit lengths of the resulting data are polynomially bounded. In particular, $|z_{t,o,k}|\leq L$.

The logarithms $\log a_k$ are $\mathbb Q$-linearly independent. Indeed, clearing denominators in a rational relation and exponentiating would give a multiplicative relation among pairwise coprime integers, forcing every exponent to vanish.

We will also use
$$ \prod_k a_k^{\eta_k}\in\mathbb Q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \eta_k\in\mathbb Z\text{ for every }k, \qquad \eta_k\in\mathbb Q. $$
For the forward implication, write $\eta_k=u/v$ in lowest terms. Prime valuations of a rational product must be integers. Pairwise coprimality therefore forces $v$ to divide every prime valuation of $a_k$. Those valuations have gcd one because $a_k$ is not a perfect power. Hence $v=1$. The reverse implication is immediate.

3.2 Covariances reduce to rational equalities

Put
$$ \alpha_{o,k}=\sum_t\mu_tz_{t,o,k}, \qquad W_o=\prod_k a_k^{\alpha_{o,k}}. $$
The exponents are rational, have polynomial bit length, and satisfy $|\alpha_{o,k}|\leq L$.

For $o\in A$ and $u\notin A$, define
$$ U_{ou}=W_oW_u. $$
Partition these monomials into classes according to whether their ratio is rational. By the preceding criterion, two monomials are in the same class precisely when their exponent vectors differ by an integer vector. This is an exact polynomial-time rational-arithmetic test.

Choose a representative $U_K$ for each class $K$, and write
$$ U_{ou}=r_{ou}U_K,\qquad r_{ou}\in\mathbb Q_{>0}. $$
The integer exponent differences defining $r_{ou}$ have absolute value at most $4L$. Consequently, each $r_{ou}$ has polynomial bit length and can be explicitly computed.

The covariance identity is
$$ b_j = \sum_{\substack{o\in A\\u\notin A}} P(o)P(u)\bigl(f_j(o)-f_j(u)\bigr). $$
To verify it, expanding the right-hand side gives
$$ (1-P(A))\sum_{o\in A}P(o)f_j(o) - P(A)\sum_{u\notin A}P(u)f_j(u), $$
which equals $\mathbb E_P[f_j\mathbf1_A]-P(A)\mathbb E_P[f_j]$.

Define
$$ H_{j,ou,k} = z_{j,o,k}-z_{0,o,k}-z_{j,u,k}+z_{0,u,k}. $$
Then
$$ Z^2b_j=\sum_k C_{j,k}\log a_k, \qquad C_{j,k} = \sum_{\substack{o\in A\\u\notin A}}H_{j,ou,k}U_{ou}. $$
Every $C_{j,k}$ is algebraic, since each $U_{ou}$ is a product of rational powers of positive integers.

We use Baker’s linear-independence theorem: logarithms of algebraic numbers that are independent over $\mathbb Q$ remain independent over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. Its hypotheses hold for the $\log a_k$, so
$$ b_j=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_{j,k}=0\text{ for every }k. $$
[Dasgupta, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.02037)

Grouping by classes gives
$$ C_{j,k} = \sum_K U_K \left( \sum_{(o,u)\in K}H_{j,ou,k}r_{ou} \right). $$
The representatives $U_K$ are positive real numbers; each has some positive integer power in $\mathbb Q$; and distinct representatives have irrational ratios. The real-radical independence theorem of Carr and O’Sullivan therefore makes them $\mathbb Q$-linearly independent. For one or two representatives, the same conclusion follows directly from nonzeroness and the irrational-ratio condition. Thus
$$ C_{j,k}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{(o,u)\in K}H_{j,ou,k}r_{ou}=0 \text{ for every }K. $$
[Carr–O’Sullivan, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0703522)

These are rational equalities. There are polynomially many of them, and all rational summands have polynomial bit length. Their exact sums therefore also have polynomial bit length.

Finally, $b=0$ is equivalent to the projection of $g_A$ onto $D_\mu$ being zero, because the $d_j$ generate that space.

Proved conclusion. The zero-objective branch is decidable in polynomial time for unrestricted inputs.

4. A new polynomial-time family: two-dimensional logarithmic coefficient space

Define
$$ d=\dim_{\mathbb Q} \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q} \{B_{o,j}:o\neq o_\ast,\ 1\leq j\leq n\}. $$

Theorem. If $d\leq2$, the exact local problem is solvable in polynomial time.

4.1 Recognizing the hypothesis

Using the normal form from Section 3, write
$$ B_{o,j}=\sum_k N_{o,j,k}\log a_k, $$
where
$$ N_{o,j,k} = z_{j,o,k}+z_{0,o_\ast,k} -z_{j,o_\ast,k}-z_{0,o,k}. $$
Because the $\log a_k$ are $\mathbb Q$-independent, $d$ equals the rational rank of the matrix whose rows are the coefficient vectors
$$ (N_{o,j,1},\ldots,N_{o,j,\ell}). $$
Thus $d$ is computable in polynomial time by rational linear algebra.

If $d=0$, then $B=0$ and $r=0$.

If $d=1$, choose a nonzero contrast entry $\ell_1$. Rational linear algebra constructs a rational matrix $U$ with
$$ B=\ell_1U. $$
Since $\ell_1\neq0$, the real rank of $B$ equals the rational rank of $U$.

4.2 The case $d=2$

Choose two contrast entries $\ell_1,\ell_2$ forming a $\mathbb Q$-basis of the entries. Construct rational matrices $U,V$ such that
$$ B=\ell_1U+\ell_2V. $$
The coefficients in $U,V$ have polynomial bit length: they are obtained by solving rational linear systems of polynomial size and bit length.

Set
$$ \theta=\frac{\ell_2}{\ell_1}. $$
This number is transcendental. Otherwise,
$$ \ell_2-\theta\ell_1=0 $$
would be a nontrivial algebraic linear relation between two $\mathbb Q$-independent logarithms of positive rationals, contradicting Baker’s theorem.

Let
$$ M(X)=U+XV,\qquad k=\min\{m-1,n\}, $$
and let $r_{\mathrm{gen}}$ be its rank over $\mathbb Q(X)$.

Every minor of $M(X)$ is a polynomial with rational coefficients. Since $\theta$ is transcendental, a minor vanishes at $\theta$ exactly when it is the zero polynomial. Therefore
$$ \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}B = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb R}M(\theta) = r_{\mathrm{gen}}. $$

This generic rank can be computed deterministically without multivariate polynomial identity testing:
$$ r_{\mathrm{gen}} = \max_{z\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}} \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Q}(U+zV). $$
Indeed, specialization cannot increase generic rank. Conversely, a nonzero minor of order $r_{\mathrm{gen}}$ has degree at most $r_{\mathrm{gen}}\leq k$, so it cannot vanish at all $k+1$ distinct sample points.

There are polynomially many samples. Their entries have polynomial bit length, and each rational rank computation takes polynomial time. Thus the exact real rank $r$ is obtained in polynomial time.

Applying Sections 2 and 3 completes the algorithm.

This theorem includes the single-logarithm family from the notes, but allows two independent logarithms and arbitrary numbers of outcomes and supported types.

5. A second polynomial-time family from the six exponentials theorem

For the same matrix $B$, define
$$ a= \dim_{\mathbb Q} \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q} \{\text{rows of }B\}, \qquad b_{\mathrm{col}}= \dim_{\mathbb Q} \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q} \{\text{columns of }B\}. $$
These are dimensions of rational spans of real vectors, not real matrix ranks.

Both are computable in polynomial time. For example, a rational relation among rows holds precisely when it holds in every column and every coefficient of $\log a_k$. Flattening those coefficient arrays converts the question to rational linear algebra. The same argument applies to columns.

Every rational spanning set is also a real spanning set, so
$$ r\leq\min\{a,b_{\mathrm{col}}\}. $$

Theorem. The rank, and therefore the exact local problem, is polynomial-time computable whenever either
$$ \min\{a,b_{\mathrm{col}}\}\leq1, $$
or
$$ \min\{a,b_{\mathrm{col}}\}=2 \quad\text{and}\quad \max\{a,b_{\mathrm{col}}\}\geq3. $$

If the minimum is zero, $B=0$ and $r=0$. If the minimum is one and $B\neq0$, the preceding upper bound gives $r=1$.

For the second case, the upper bound gives $r\leq2$. Suppose for contradiction that $r=1$. Write
$$ B=xy^{\mathsf T} $$
with nonzero real vectors $x,y$. Multiplication by the fixed nonzero vector $y$ is an injective $\mathbb Q$-linear map on scalars, so
$$ a=\dim_{\mathbb Q}\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{x_i\}. $$
Similarly,
$$ b_{\mathrm{col}} = \dim_{\mathbb Q}\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{y_j\}. $$

After transposing if necessary, select two $\mathbb Q$-independent coordinates of $x$ and three $\mathbb Q$-independent coordinates of $y$. The resulting $2\times3$ submatrix has real rank one, but its two rows and three columns are respectively $\mathbb Q$-independent.

Its entries are logarithms of positive rationals. This contradicts the six exponentials theorem, which states that a $2\times3$ matrix of logarithms of algebraic numbers with $\mathbb Q$-independent rows and columns has rank two. Thus $r=2$. [Dasgupta, Theorem 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.02037)

All recognition and output-construction steps are polynomial-time.

Example beyond the two-logarithm family

Consider
$$ B= \begin{pmatrix} \log2&\log3&\log5\\ \log3&\log5&\log2 \end{pmatrix}. $$
Its two rows are $\mathbb Q$-independent: a rational relation between them, restricted to the first coordinate, would give a relation between $\log2$ and $\log3$.

Its three columns are $\mathbb Q$-independent: a rational relation between them, restricted to the first row, would give a relation between $\log2,\log3,\log5$.

Thus $a=2$, $b_{\mathrm{col}}=3$, and $r=2$. However, its entries span three dimensions over $\mathbb Q$, so Section 4 does not apply.

This is realized by the society
$$ q_0=(1/3,1/3,1/3), $$
$$ q_1=(1/6,1/3,1/2),\qquad q_2=(1/9,1/3,5/9),\qquad q_3=(1/8,5/8,1/4), $$
with masses $1/4$ each and the first outcome as $o_\ast$.

Here $D_\mu$ is the entire two-dimensional space of centered functions. For every nonempty proper event $A$, the local value and optimal direction therefore simplify to
$$ V^\star(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon\sqrt{P(A)(1-P(A))}, $$
$$ h^\star(\varepsilon) = -\frac{\varepsilon g_A} {\sqrt{P(A)(1-P(A))}}. $$
Section 2 supplies the radius and a realizing mass-conserving perturbation.

6. What these reductions do and do not establish

6.1 Arbitrary rational-logarithm matrices occur

Let $R=(R_{ij})$ be any positive rational matrix with $k$ rows and $n$ columns. Use outcomes $o_\ast,o_1,\ldots,o_k$, let $q_0$ be uniform, and define
$$ q_j(o_\ast)=\frac1{1+\sum_iR_{ij}}, \qquad q_j(o_i)=\frac{R_{ij}}{1+\sum_iR_{ij}}. $$
These are positive normalized rational distributions.

Choose a power of two $M>n$, and set
$$ \mu_j=1/M\quad(1\leq j\leq n), \qquad \mu_0=1-n/M. $$
All masses are positive and sum to one. The construction has polynomial bit length, and its contrast matrix is exactly
$$ B_{ij}=\log R_{ij}. $$

Consequently, normalization imposes no special restriction that would eliminate general rational-logarithm rank questions.

Sections 2 and 3 establish a polynomial-time reduction from local optimization to one exact rank computation on such a matrix.

6.2 The converse is a computability reduction

Suppose a terminating algorithm solves the stated local problem on every input. For a fixed society, run it for every singleton event $\{o\}$.

For each call, substitute half its returned positive radius into its optimal-value DAG and divide by that substituted radius. Section 2’s projection argument shows that this produces
$$ s_o=\|\Pi_{D_\mu}g_{\{o\}}\|_P. $$
These quantities satisfy
$$ \sum_{o\in\mathcal O}\frac{s_o^2}{P(o)} = \dim D_\mu. $$
To prove the identity, take a $P$-orthonormal basis $w_1,\ldots,w_r$ of $D_\mu$. Each $w_a$ is centered, so
$$ \langle w_a,g_{\{o\}}\rangle_P=P(o)w_a(o). $$
Hence
$$ s_o^2=\sum_{a=1}^rP(o)^2w_a(o)^2, $$
and summing after division by $P(o)$ gives
$$ \sum_o\frac{s_o^2}{P(o)} = \sum_{a=1}^r\sum_oP(o)w_a(o)^2 = r. $$

Permitted finite DAGs represent computable reals on their valid domains. Thus the last expression can eventually be approximated with error less than $1/4$, and its integer value recovered. Together with the preceding embedding, this yields a terminating algorithm for rational-logarithm matrix rank.

This does not prove a polynomial-time converse. A polynomial-size returned DAG need not admit polynomial-time certified evaluation; small denominators and cancellation can require substantial precision.

6.3 The unresolved case

The algorithms above leave, among other cases, a $2\times2$ logarithmic matrix with $\mathbb Q$-independent rows and columns and a coefficient space of dimension at least three.

The assertion that every such matrix is nonsingular is a rational-logarithm special case of the Four Exponentials Conjecture. That assertion is not used here. [Dasgupta, Conjecture 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.02037)

This connection identifies a gap in one proposed rank algorithm. It does not prove that local optimization requires proving that conjecture, and it supplies no NP-hardness reduction.

For unrestricted inputs, an unconditional polynomial-time algorithm or a valid NP-hardness reduction in the specified DAG output model remains unproved.
