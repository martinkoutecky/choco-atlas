The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given firms \(i\in[N]\), categories \(m\in[M]\), and worker types \(s\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\nu_s\) summing to one, where each type has category \(g(s)\), a strict ranking \(\pi_s\) of firms, and an unknown firm-specific reward law shared by all its mass, can a polynomial-time policy choose a stable mass allocation each round and achieve sublinear Bayesian regret? Each allocation \(x_{i,s,t}\ge 0\) must satisfy \(\sum_i x_{i,s,t}\le\nu_s\), per-capita category minima \(\sum_{s:g(s)=m}x_{i,s,t}\ge\underline q_i^m\), and per-capita total capacities \(\sum_s x_{i,s,t}\le\overline Q_i\). Stability means no positive amount of a type prefers another firm that can accept it using spare capacity or by replacing less-preferred assigned mass without violating its category minima. Assigning mass \(x_{i,s,t}\) yields a Poisson number of independent reward observations with mean \(\lambda x_{i,s,t}\); regret is measured against the firm-optimal stable mass allocation under the true reward means.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(N\ge1\) firms and \(M\ge1\) categories. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) worker types with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_s\), summing to one; \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Each type has a category \(g(s)\) and a known strict ranking \(\pi_s\) of all firms, all preferred to unemployment.
Quotas are binary rationals satisfying \(\underline q_i^m\ge0\), \(\overline Q_i>0\), \(\sum_m\underline q_i^m\le\overline Q_i\), \(\sum_i\overline Q_i<1\), and \(\sum_i\underline q_i^m\le\sum_{s:g(s)=m}\nu_s\). Feasible allocations satisfy \(x_{i,s}\ge0\), \(\sum_i x_{i,s}\le\nu_s\), \(\sum_{s:g(s)=m}x_{i,s}\ge\underline q_i^m\), and \(\sum_s x_{i,s}\le\overline Q_i\).
Unknown means \(\mu_{i,s}\in(0,1)\) are drawn independently from the known uniform prior once and remain fixed; observations for pair \((i,s)\) are independent Bernoulli rewards with mean \(\mu_{i,s}\). Firms rank types across categories by decreasing mean and have additive utilities \(U_i(x;\mu)=\sum_s\mu_{i,s}x_{i,s}\); distinct types have distinct means almost surely.
For specified strict firm rankings, an allocation is stable if no positive mass of a type, unemployed or assigned to a less-preferred firm, can enter a preferred firm that accepts it through spare capacity or removal of at most the entering mass of lower-ranked types, while preserving the receiving firm's category minima and capacity. These deviations test the receiving firm's constraints; they need not preserve the departing firm's minima.
A policy knows the horizon \(T\) and, using only previous feedback, declares strict firm rankings and outputs a feasible allocation stable for those declared rankings each round. Conditional on \(x_t\), it observes independent counts \(C_{i,s,t}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda x_{i,s,t})\), with known binary-rational \(\lambda>0\), and that many rewards for each pair; workers and quotas reset each round.
Promise that almost every prior draw admits a feasible true-stable allocation \(x^*(\mu)\) simultaneously maximizing every \(U_i\) over all feasible true-stable allocations. Define \(R(T)=\mathbb E[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_i(U_i(x^*(\mu);\mu)-U_i(x_t;\mu))]\), with expectation over the prior, feedback, and policy randomness. Let \(L\) be the input bit length and \(B_t\) the number of observations received before round \(t\).
Is there a policy running per round in time polynomial in \(N,M,\tau,L,t,B_t\) that guarantees \(R(T)=o(T)\) for every promised input, or is achieving this guarantee NP-hard?
For finite workers, MMTS has the stated Bayesian regret bound \(8Q\log(QT)\sqrt{K_{\max}T}+NK/Q\), where \(Q\) is total capacity, \(K\) the worker count, and \(K_{\max}\) the largest category count; its matching subroutine has stated runtime \(O(NK)\). Roundwise rankings are sampled, whereas the regret benchmark uses true means. [Li, Cheng, and Dai](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.10230v3).
A different static continuum model has a unique stable outcome computable by a deferred-acceptance analogue, with Poisson school-interest counts rather than reward observations. It does not establish the learning guarantee above. [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881). No exact continuous result settling this target was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Roundwise stability was unspecified as to firm rankings; it is evaluated under the policy's declared rankings, following the discrete algorithm, while the benchmark uses true means.
  • The reward law and Bayesian prior were unspecified; use Bernoulli rewards and independent uniform priors on means, consistent with the bounded-reward discrete setting.
  • All firms are acceptable to workers, unemployment is last, and firms compare types across categories using their reward means.
  • The continuous firm-optimal benchmark was undefined; use simultaneous maximization of additive firm utilities and explicitly promise its existence almost surely.
  • Quota domains and supply conditions were unspecified; require nonnegative minima, positive capacities, adequate category supply, and total capacity strictly below normalized worker mass, formalizing worker surplus.
  • Blocking deviations preserve the receiving firm's constraints, permit combined spare capacity and replacement, and do not require preservation of the departing firm's minima.
  • Rewards are mass-weighted in regret rather than weighted by the realized observation count; Poisson counts determine information only.
  • The horizon is known, the same society returns each round, and computation is measured in input bits and received observations because Poisson feedback has unbounded length.

A polynomial-per-round policy exists

The answer is yes under the stated runtime and regret quantifiers. The policy below satisfies, for every fixed promised input,
$$ R(T)=o(T), $$
and its time in round $t$ is polynomial in $N,M,\tau,L,t,B_t$.

The construction uses a finite, potentially exponential, initial delay. During that delay it computes an exhaustive table incrementally while outputting a polynomial-time computable stable allocation. Once the table is complete, it uses an implementable approximation to posterior sampling. The delay is compatible with the pointwise requirement $R(T)=o(T)$.

Write
$$ q_i^m=\underline q_i^m,\qquad Q_i=\overline Q_i,\qquad Q=\sum_iQ_i,\qquad d=N\tau. $$
Thus $0<Q<1$. For any mean vector $\theta$ and allocation $x$, define
$$ F(\theta,x)=\sum_{i,s}\theta_{i,s}x_{i,s}. $$

1. Stability and a finite table of candidate allocations

Fix strict firm rankings. Write $r\succ_i s$ when firm $i$ ranks type $r$ above type $s$, and $j\succ_s i$ when type $s$ prefers firm $j$ to firm $i$.

For a feasible allocation, define
$$ y_i=\sum_sx_{i,s},\qquad z_i^m=\sum_{s:g(s)=m}x_{i,s}, $$
$$ d_{i,s}=\nu_s-x_{i,s}-\sum_{j:j\succ_s i}x_{j,s}, $$
and
$$ \ell_{i,s}^m=\sum_{r:g(r)=m,\ s\succ_i r}x_{i,r}. $$
Here $d_{i,s}$ is the mass of type $s$ that is unemployed or assigned to a firm it likes less than $i$. All these quantities are nonnegative.

Lemma 1: exact blocking criterion

A type $s$ blocks with firm $i$ precisely when $d_{i,s}>0$ and at least one of the following holds:

  • $y_i<Q_i$;
  • $\ell_{i,s}^{g(s)}>0$;
  • for some $m\ne g(s)$, both $\ell_{i,s}^m>0$ and $z_i^m>q_i^m$.

Proof. In the first case, sufficiently small positive willing mass enters without removals. In the second, exchange sufficiently small equal masses within category $g(s)$. In the third, exchange sufficiently small equal masses, choosing the removed mass smaller than the category surplus $z_i^m-q_i^m$.

Conversely, suppose all three conditions fail. The firm is full. Because removal is at most the entering mass, any permissible entry must remove exactly that mass. There are no lower-ranked incumbents in the entering category. Every other category containing lower-ranked incumbents is at its minimum, so no positive mass can be removed from it. Entry is therefore impossible. This argument tests only the receiving firm’s constraints, as required. $\square$

Lemma 2: every stable allocation fills every firm

For every stable allocation,
$$ y_i=Q_i\qquad\text{for all }i. $$

Proof. Every feasible allocation leaves unemployed mass at least $1-Q>0$. Some listed type therefore has positive unemployed mass. Such a type would block any firm with spare capacity. $\square$

Let $P$ be the rational polytope defined by the allocation constraints together with $y_i=Q_i$ for every firm. Lemmas 1 and 2 show that an allocation is stable precisely when it belongs to $P$ and satisfies
$$ d_{i,s}\ell_{i,s}^{g(s)}=0 $$
and
$$ d_{i,s}\ell_{i,s}^m(z_i^m-q_i^m)=0 \qquad(m\ne g(s)). $$

Every factor here is affine and nonnegative on $P$. The zero set of any such factor is a face of $P$, possibly empty. Each product equation is a finite disjunction of zero-factor conditions. Consequently:

For each strict ranking profile, the stable set is a finite union of faces of $P$.

In particular, whenever stable allocations exist, a linear objective has a stable maximizer that is a vertex of $P$: maximize on a face attaining the largest value and then choose a maximizing vertex of that face.

Rational encoding and enumeration

There are $d$ coordinates. The constraint coefficients of $P$ belong to $\{0,1,-1\}$. Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of its rational right-hand sides; $\log_2D$ is bounded by $L$.

At any vertex, the active constraint normals span the full coordinate space. Otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality. Thus a vertex is determined by $d$ linearly independent active constraints,
$$ Ax=b. $$
Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s inequality give
$$ \text{coordinate denominator divides }D|\det A|, \qquad |\det A|\le d^{d/2}. $$
Since every coordinate lies in $[0,1]$, each coordinate has encoding length
$$ O\!\left(L+d\log(d+1)\right). $$

List the scalar constraints of $P$, counting an equality as one row. Their number can be taken as
$$ c=d+\tau+NM+N. $$
Enumerate all $d$-element subsets of these rows. For each subset, solve the corresponding equalities if nonsingular and retain the solution if it satisfies all constraints of $P$.

Every retained solution is a vertex: any convex decomposition would have to satisfy all selected active equalities, whose solution is unique. Every vertex occurs. Each candidate takes polynomial time in $N,M,\tau,L$ using exact rational linear algebra.

The number of candidates is the finite, input-dependent integer
$$ J=\binom cd. $$
Its binary encoding has length at most $c+1$.

2. Consequences of the promise

Every strict firm-ranking profile has prior probability
$$ \frac{1}{(\tau!)^N}>0. $$
Existence of a stable allocation depends only on the ranking profile. Therefore the promise implies:

Every strict firm-ranking profile admits a feasible stable allocation.

Otherwise its entire positive-probability ranking event would violate the promise.

For a mean vector $\theta$ with strict firm rankings, let $v(\theta)$ be a stable vertex maximizing $F(\theta,\cdot)$, with deterministic tie-breaking. Such a vertex exists by Section 1.

For almost every prior draw, $v(\mu)$ has exactly the benchmark’s utilities. Indeed, the promised simultaneous maximizer $x^*(\mu)$ satisfies
$$ U_i(x^*(\mu);\mu)-U_i(x;\mu)\ge0 $$
for every true-stable $x$ and every firm $i$. It therefore maximizes $F$ over the stable set. A sum-maximizing stable vertex exists, and its individual deficits relative to $x^*(\mu)$ are nonnegative and sum to zero. Each deficit is consequently zero.

Thus, almost surely,
$$ F(\mu,v(\mu))=F(\mu,x^*(\mu)). $$
The function $v$ is measurable: it selects from a finite vertex set using finitely many ranking and linear-objective comparisons.

3. A polynomial-time stable allocation for the initial rounds

We now construct an allocation stable for one known ranking profile, without exhaustive search. Declare that every firm ranks types in the same fixed order
$$ s_1\succ s_2\succ\cdots\succ s_\tau. $$

Split firm $i$ into the following auxiliary employers:

  • a reserved employer $R_i^m$ of capacity $q_i^m$, accepting only category $m$;
  • a flexible employer $A_i$ of capacity

$$ W_i=Q_i-\sum_mq_i^m. $$

Zero-capacity employers may be omitted. A type $s$ orders these employers according to its original firm ranking, placing $R_i^{g(s)}$ immediately before $A_i$ within firm $i$. All are preferred to unemployment.

Process types in the common order. Assign each type’s mass greedily to its most-preferred auxiliary employer with remaining capacity, then to the next, and so on. Merge assignments to the auxiliary employers of each original firm. Call the result $x^0$.

This takes polynomial time: for each type there are at most $2N$ acceptable auxiliary employers, and the calculations use rational subtraction and minima.

The nontrivial point is that the promised existence of an original stable allocation forces this greedy construction to fill every reserved employer and to produce an original stable allocation.

Lemma 3: the construction produces a feasible stable allocation

Proof. By Sections 1 and 2, the common-ranking profile has a rational stable allocation $x$.

Choose a positive integer $h$ clearing the denominators of the masses, capacities, minima, and coordinates of $x$. Replace type $s$ by $h\nu_s$ individual workers, indexed in increasing order. Give every auxiliary employer the common strict worker priority: first compare type order, then compare index within type.

Lift $x$ to an individual assignment as follows.

First, distribute each type’s worker indices to original firms in that type’s preference order: earlier indices go to better firms, and unemployed indices come last. At each firm and within each category, assign its highest-priority workers to the reserved employer, filling exactly $hq_i^m$ seats. Assign the remainder to the flexible employer.

All auxiliary employers are full, because the original stable allocation fills every firm.

We claim that this lifted assignment is stable in the ordinary individual-worker auxiliary market. Consider a worker who prefers some auxiliary employer to its current assignment.

If the worker is currently at the same original firm, the only possible improvement is from the flexible employer to its own category’s reserved employer. That reserved employer contains the highest-priority workers assigned to the firm in that category, so no lower-priority incumbent can be displaced.

If the worker is unemployed or at a worse original firm, it cannot displace a worker of its own type: by the index assignment, every worker of that type assigned to the desired firm has an earlier index.

It remains to consider displacement of a lower-ranked different type. Displacement at a reserved employer is a same-category original blocking deviation. Displacement at a flexible employer is also an original blocking deviation:

  • if the categories agree, it is a same-category exchange;
  • if they differ, the displaced worker’s category has positive flexible employment in addition to its full reserved quota, and hence has strict surplus above its minimum.

Lemma 1 excludes all these possibilities. There are no vacancies, so the lifted assignment is stable.

Now consider any finite market in which every employer has the same strict worker priority. Its stable assignment is uniquely determined by serial dictatorship in that priority order. The highest-priority worker must receive its most-preferred employer with positive capacity: otherwise that employer has either a vacancy or a lower-priority incumbent, creating a block. Remove this worker and, if employed, reduce the relevant capacity by one. The remaining assignment is stable in the residual market. Induction fixes every worker’s assignment.

Therefore the lifted stable assignment equals this serial dictatorship. Processing all workers of a type consecutively and dividing quantities by $h$ is exactly the rational greedy construction defining $x^0$. Its merged allocation consequently equals $x$. Thus $x^0$ is feasible and stable for the declared common rankings. The construction never needs to compute $x$ or $h$. $\square$

4. The policy

The policy ignores the supplied horizon, so its decisions are consistent across horizons.

Initial rounds

For rounds $1$ through $J$:

  • Process one candidate from the vertex enumeration in Section 1 and retain it if feasible.
  • Declare the common rankings from Section 3 and output $x^0$.

After these rounds, the stored table contains every vertex of $P$, possibly with duplicates. The table contains at most $J$ entries.

Subsequent rounds

For a firm–type coordinate $e$, let $n_e(t)$ and $S_e(t)$ be its number of observed rewards and successes before round $t$.

Set
$$ k_t=3\left\lceil\log_2(t+1)\right\rceil, \qquad \varepsilon_t=2^{-k_t}. $$
In particular,
$$ \varepsilon_t\le(t+1)^{-3}. $$

For every coordinate $e$:

  • Generate $n_e(t)+1$ independent uniforms on the dyadic grid

$$ \left\{0,2^{-k_t},\ldots,1-2^{-k_t}\right\}. $$

  • Let $\widehat\theta_{e,t}$ be their $(S_e(t)+1)$-st order statistic.

Declare each firm’s ranking by decreasing $\widehat\theta_{i,s,t}$, breaking ties by type label.

Scan the completed vertex table. Among vertices stable for these declared rankings, select one maximizing
$$ F(\widehat\theta_t,x), $$
again with deterministic tie-breaking. Output the selected vertex $X_t$.

The promise ensures that every declared strict ranking profile has a stable vertex, so this selection is always possible. Stability testing uses the exact equations in Section 1. Every round therefore outputs a feasible allocation stable for its declared rankings.

Unobserved coordinates cause no initialization gap: when $n_e(t)=S_e(t)=0$, the construction simply draws one grid uniform.

5. Regret proof

5.1 Posterior distribution

Let $H_t$ be the history before round $t$, including previous policy randomness. Conditional on this history,
$$ \mu_e\mid H_t \sim \operatorname{Beta}\bigl(S_e(t)+1,n_e(t)-S_e(t)+1\bigr), $$
independently across coordinates.

To verify this under adaptive allocations, factor the likelihood chronologically. Conditional on previous history, the policy’s action probabilities have no further mean dependence. Conditional on an allocation, the Poisson count likelihood is also mean-independent. The reward likelihood contributes exactly
$$ \prod_e\mu_e^{S_e(t)}(1-\mu_e)^{n_e(t)-S_e(t)}. $$
Multiplication by the independent uniform prior gives the asserted posterior.

Write its mean and variance as $m_e(t)$ and $v_e(t)$. The beta variance formula gives
$$ v_e(t)\le\frac{1}{4(n_e(t)+3)} \le\frac{1}{n_e(t)+1}. $$
The posterior is absolutely continuous relative to the prior, so prior-null failures of the benchmark promise remain posterior-null.

5.2 Coupling the grid samples to exact posterior samples

Couple each grid uniform to a continuous uniform $U$ by rounding $U$ down to the grid. Their difference is less than $\varepsilon_t$.

The $a$-th order statistic of $b$ independent continuous uniforms has distribution $\operatorname{Beta}(a,b-a+1)$. This follows by differentiating its distribution function: its density is
$$ \frac{b!}{(a-1)!(b-a)!}\, u^{a-1}(1-u)^{b-a}. $$
Applying this with $a=S_e(t)+1$ and $b=n_e(t)+1$ produces an exact posterior sample $\theta_{e,t}$.

Order statistics preserve coordinatewise inequalities. Consequently the coupling satisfies
$$ |\widehat\theta_{e,t}-\theta_{e,t}|\le\varepsilon_t $$
for every coordinate. Conditional on $H_t$, the vector $\theta_t$ is an independent draw from the posterior of $\mu$, independent of the true vector.

Its unconditional distribution is exactly the original independent uniform prior. Indeed, for every measurable set $E$,
$$ \mathbb P(\theta_t\in E) = \mathbb E\!\left[\mathbb P(\mu\in E\mid H_t)\right] = \mathbb P(\mu\in E). $$

Let
$$ A=N\binom{\tau}{2}. $$
If the rankings induced by $\theta_t$ differ from the declared rankings, some pair of types at some firm has exact sampled means within $2\varepsilon_t$. For two independent uniform means, this event has probability at most $4\varepsilon_t$. A union bound therefore gives
$$ \mathbb P(\text{ranking disagreement})\le4A\varepsilon_t. $$

On agreement, $X_t$ and $v(\theta_t)$ optimize over the same stable vertices. Every such allocation has total employment $Q$, so
$$ |F(\theta_t,x)-F(\widehat\theta_t,x)| \le Q\varepsilon_t. $$
Optimality of $X_t$ for the approximate objective then implies
$$ 0\le F(\theta_t,v(\theta_t))-F(\theta_t,X_t) \le2Q\varepsilon_t. $$

On disagreement, the absolute difference is at most $Q$, since both utilities lie in $[0,Q]$. Thus
$$ \mathbb E\left[ \left|F(\theta_t,v(\theta_t))-F(\theta_t,X_t)\right| \right] \le Q(2+4A)\varepsilon_t. $$
These errors are summable because
$$ \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_t \le\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(t+1)^{-3}<1. $$

5.3 Posterior uncertainty

For a round after the initial enumeration, the conditional expected regret equals
$$ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[ F(\mu,v(\mu))-F(\mu,X_t)\mid H_t]\\ &\quad= \mathbb E[ F(\theta_t,v(\theta_t))-F(\theta_t,X_t)\mid H_t] + \sum_e\mathbb E[ (\theta_{e,t}-m_e(t))X_{e,t}\mid H_t]. \end{aligned} $$
The first term uses identical conditional distributions of $\mu$ and $\theta_t$. For the second, conditional independence of the true means and current policy randomness gives
$$ \mathbb E[F(\mu,X_t)\mid H_t] = \sum_em_e(t)\mathbb E[X_{e,t}\mid H_t]. $$

Put $p_e(t)=\mathbb E[X_{e,t}\mid H_t]$. Because $0\le X_{e,t}\le1$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$ \left| \mathbb E[(\theta_{e,t}-m_e(t))X_{e,t}\mid H_t] \right| \le \sqrt{v_e(t)p_e(t)}. $$
Applying Cauchy–Schwarz over histories, coordinates, and rounds bounds the total absolute uncertainty contribution by
$$ \sqrt{ dT\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_e \mathbb E[v_e(t)X_{e,t}] }. $$
Here extending the sum to the initial rounds only increases the bound. No nonnegativity assumption on regret is used.

5.4 Poisson exposure bound

Fix a coordinate and a round. Conditional on $H_t,X_t$, write
$$ n=n_e(t),\qquad x=X_{e,t},\qquad C\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda x). $$
On $C\ge1$,
$$ \log(n+C+1)-\log(n+1) \ge \log\left(1+\frac1{n+1}\right) \ge\frac1{2(n+1)}. $$
Also,
$$ \mathbb P(C\ge1) =1-e^{-\lambda x} \ge\frac{\lambda x}{1+\lambda x} \ge\frac{\lambda x}{1+\lambda}, $$
using $e^z\ge1+z$ and $x\le1$. Hence
$$ \mathbb E[ \log(n+C+1)-\log(n+1)\mid H_t,X_t] \ge \frac{\lambda x}{2(1+\lambda)(n+1)}. $$

The logarithmic increments telescope. Since
$$ \mathbb E[n_e(T+1)] = \lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb E[X_{e,t}] \le\lambda T, $$
Jensen’s inequality yields
$$ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb E\left[\frac{X_{e,t}}{n_e(t)+1}\right] \le \frac{2(1+\lambda)}{\lambda}\log(1+\lambda T). $$
Combining this with the variance bound and summing over coordinates gives an uncertainty contribution at most
$$ d\sqrt{ \frac{2(1+\lambda)}{\lambda}\, T\log(1+\lambda T) }. $$

5.5 Final regret bound

Every initial-round regret has absolute value at most $Q$. Combining the initial delay, sampling error, and uncertainty estimates proves
$$ |R(T)| \le Q\min\{T,J\} + Q(2+4A) + d\sqrt{ \frac{2(1+\lambda)}{\lambda}\, T\log(1+\lambda T) }. $$
For every fixed input, $J,A,d,\lambda$ are fixed and finite. Dividing by $T$ proves
$$ \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{R(T)}{T}=0. $$

6. Per-round runtime and conclusion

The initial stable allocation uses polynomially many rational operations. Each enumeration round processes one linear-system candidate in polynomial time. At every time, at most $t$ candidates have been stored, each with polynomial encoding length.

After enumeration, the table has at most $J<t$ entries, so scanning it, checking stability, and comparing rational objectives takes time polynomial in $N,M,\tau,L,t$.

The total number of grid uniforms generated in round $t$ is
$$ \sum_e(n_e(t)+1)=B_t+d. $$
Each uses $k_t=O(\log(t+1))$ random bits. Computing the order statistics, updating observation counts, and performing exact comparisons therefore takes time polynomial in $N,M,\tau,L,t,B_t$. All loops have deterministically bounded lengths; exact continuous sampling is unnecessary.

Thus the required policy exists for arbitrary $N$ and $M$ on every promised input. Its initial delay $J$ may be exponential in input size, but the task allows runtime polynomial in the current round number and requires convergence separately for each fixed input. These two features make the exhaustive stage admissible.
