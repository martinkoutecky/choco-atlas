Proposition 3.2 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), a union map \(g:T\to[m]\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), an explicitly represented differentiable rational polynomial \(F\) on the feasible mass vectors, a type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\), and accuracy and confidence parameters \(\varepsilon,\alpha\), estimate \(\Phi_t^O=\mathbb{E}[\partial_t F(Z)]\) within additive error \(\varepsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\alpha\). Here \(K\) is uniform on \(\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), \(R\) is uniform among the \(K\)-element subsets of \([m]\setminus\{g(t)\}\), \(U\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), and \(Z_s=\mu_s\mathbf{1}[g(s)\in R]+U\mu_s\mathbf{1}[g(s)=g(t)]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support \(T\): the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_s\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(1\le m\le\tau\), and let the surjection \(g:T\to\{1,\ldots,m\}\) specify nonempty unions. Feasible coalition masses form \(D=\prod_{s\in T}[0,\mu_s]\).
The input includes a polynomial \(F\in\mathbb{Q}[z_s:s\in T]\), represented by an explicit list of monomials with binary-rational coefficients and binary-encoded nonnegative integer exponents, with \(F(0)=0\). Coefficients may have either sign; no monotonicity is assumed.
Fix an input type \(t\in T\). Draw \(K\) uniformly from \(\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), then draw \(R\) uniformly among the \(K\)-element subsets of \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus\{g(t)\}\), and independently draw \(U\) uniformly from \([0,1]\).
Define \(Z_s=\mu_s\mathbf{1}[g(s)\in R]+U\mu_s\mathbf{1}[g(s)=g(t)]\) and \(\Phi_t^O=\mathbb{E}[\partial_tF(Z)]\), where \(\partial_t\) differentiates with respect to the coordinate \(z_t\).
Given binary-rational \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(0<\alpha<1\), the estimation problem asks for \(\widehat\Phi\) satisfying \(\Pr(|\widehat\Phi-\Phi_t^O|\le\varepsilon)\ge1-\alpha\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Does this additive estimation problem admit a randomized algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon^{-1},\log(1/\alpha))\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For finite TU-games with nonempty a priori unions, equal independent sample counts in each stratum give an Owen-value additive-error guarantee once \(\ell\ge w^2\ln(2/\alpha)/(2\varepsilon^2)\), where \(\ell\) is the total sample count and \(w\) is the largest within-stratum marginal-contribution range; strata fix the numbers of preceding unions and preceding players within the focal union. Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The estimation problem admits a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm, which therefore satisfies the requested randomized guarantee with success probability \(1\). The proof applies to arbitrary signed coefficients and binary-encoded exponents.

The key is to integrate each listed monomial analytically, then approximate its mass powers with controlled precision. Exact rational evaluation of those powers is unnecessary.

1. Exact contribution of a monomial.

Write the input polynomial as \(F(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_i\prod_{s\in T}z_s^{a_{i,s}}\), and let \(h=g(t)\). Monomials with \(a_{i,t}=0\) contribute nothing to \(\partial_tF\); henceforth consider an index with \(a_{i,t}>0\).

Define

  • \(\beta_{i,s}=a_{i,s}-\mathbf 1[s=t]\);
  • \(A_i=\sum_{s:g(s)=h}a_{i,s}\);
  • \(H_i=\{g(s):g(s)\ne h,\ a_{i,s}>0\}\), and \(r_i=|H_i|\);
  • \(P_i=\prod_{s\in T}\mu_s^{\beta_{i,s}}\).

All \(\beta_{i,s}\) are nonnegative, and \(A_i\ge a_{i,t}\ge1\). Powers with exponent zero are interpreted as \(1\), including when their base is zero.

Under the specified sampling distribution, the derivative of this monomial equals

\(\displaystyle c_i a_{i,t}P_i\,U^{A_i-1}\mathbf 1[H_i\subseteq R].\)

Indeed, the differentiated exponents in the focal union sum to \(A_i-1\). Outside that union, the product is nonzero precisely when every union containing a positive exponent belongs to \(R\).

We next compute the indicator probability. Consider a uniformly random permutation of the \(m\) unions, and take \(R\) to be the unions preceding \(h\). For any fixed subset \(R_0\) of size \(k\),

\(\displaystyle \Pr(R=R_0)=\frac{k!(m-1-k)!}{m!}=\frac{1}{m\binom{m-1}{k}}.\)

This is exactly the distribution in the question. Consequently, \(H_i\subseteq R\) precisely when \(h\) is last in the permutation restricted to \(H_i\cup\{h\}\). That restricted permutation is uniform, so

\(\displaystyle \Pr(H_i\subseteq R)=\frac{1}{r_i+1}.\)

Independence of \(U\) and \(R\), together with \(\mathbb E[U^{A_i-1}]=\int_0^1u^{A_i-1}\,du=1/A_i\), now gives the exact formula

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\Phi_t^O=\sum_{i:a_{i,t}>0}d_iP_i,\qquad d_i=\frac{c_i a_{i,t}}{A_i(r_i+1)}.}\)

In particular, \(|d_i|\le |c_i|\). This formula also covers \(m=1\), for which every \(r_i=0\).

2. Approximating powers with binary-encoded exponents.

Since the positive masses sum to \(1\), every \(\mu_s\) lies in \((0,1]\). This bound makes fixed-precision arithmetic sufficient.

Fix a nonnegative integer \(p\), set \(\delta=2^{-p}\), and define downward dyadic rounding by

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{fl}_p(x)=2^{-p}\lfloor 2^p x\rfloor.\)

For \(x\in[0,1]\), compute an approximation \(Q_e(x)\) to \(x^e\) recursively:

  • \(Q_0(x)=1\), and \(Q_1(x)=\operatorname{fl}_p(x)\);
  • \(Q_{2k}(x)=\operatorname{fl}_p(Q_k(x)^2)\) for \(k\ge1\);
  • \(Q_{2k+1}(x)=\operatorname{fl}_p(\operatorname{fl}_p(Q_k(x)^2)Q_1(x))\) for \(k\ge1\).

There is only one recursive call at each level, so the computation uses \(O(\log(e+1))\) multiplications. Every computed value remains in \([0,1]\).

We claim that, for every positive integer \(e\),

\(\displaystyle |Q_e(x)-x^e|\le(2e-1)\delta.\)

For \(e=1\), this follows from the rounding definition. To prove the induction step, use the elementary inequality

\(\displaystyle |ab-a'b'|\le |a-a'|+|b-b'|\qquad(a,b,a',b'\in[0,1]).\)

For \(e=2k\), the error is at most

\(\displaystyle 2(2k-1)\delta+\delta=(4k-1)\delta=(2e-1)\delta.\)

For \(e=2k+1\), the two roundings and the approximation of \(x\) give error at most

\(\displaystyle 2(2k-1)\delta+2\delta+\delta=(4k+1)\delta=(2e-1)\delta.\)

This completes the induction.

For monomial \(i\), let \(b_i=\sum_s\beta_{i,s}\). Approximate each positive-exponent factor by the preceding procedure and multiply the resulting factors, rounding after every multiplication. Call the result \(\widetilde P_i\).

If \(b_i=0\), set \(\widetilde P_i=1\) exactly. Otherwise, if there are \(q_i\) positive-exponent factors, repeated application of the product inequality gives

\(\displaystyle |\widetilde P_i-P_i|\le\sum_{\beta_{i,s}>0}(2\beta_{i,s}-1)\delta+(q_i-1)\delta=(2b_i-1)\delta.\)

Thus large exponents affect the required precision through their bit length, rather than requiring that many multiplications.

3. Algorithm and additive-error guarantee.

Discard zero coefficients and monomials with \(a_{i,t}=0\). If none remain, output \(0\), which is exact.

Otherwise, define

\(\displaystyle C=\sum_i|c_i|>0,\qquad W=\max\{1,\max_i b_i\}.\)

Choose a nonnegative integer \(p\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle 2^{-p}\le\frac{\varepsilon}{4WC}.\)

Compute every \(\widetilde P_i\) at this precision and form the exact rational sum

\(\displaystyle S=\sum_i d_i\widetilde P_i.\)

The coefficient signs cause no difficulty: the triangle inequality and the bounds proved above imply

\(\displaystyle |S-\Phi_t^O|\le\sum_i|d_i|\,|\widetilde P_i-P_i|\le2WC\,2^{-p}\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.\)

To obtain a dyadic output, choose a nonnegative integer \(q\) with \(2^{-q}\le\varepsilon/2\), and output

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi=2^{-q}\lfloor2^qS\rfloor.\)

This rounding works for negative \(S\) as well, and satisfies \(|\widehat\Phi-S|<2^{-q}\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle |\widehat\Phi-\Phi_t^O|\le\varepsilon.\)

The algorithm is deterministic, so the required probability is \(1\), independently of \(\alpha\).

4. Bit complexity.

It remains to verify that the precision and rational arithmetic have polynomial cost.

Each input exponent is less than \(2^L\), so \(b_i\le\tau2^L\). Likewise, each coefficient satisfies \(|c_i|\le2^L\), giving \(C\le N2^L\). Consequently, the smallest admissible precision satisfies

\(\displaystyle p=O\!\left(L+\log(N+1)+\log(\tau+1)+\max\{0,\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\}\right).\)

It can be selected by exact integer comparisons against the rational quantity \(\varepsilon/(4WC)\), without numerical logarithms.

Each power requires \(O(\log(\beta_{i,s}+1))=O(L)\) multiplications. The dyadic operands have \(O(p)\) bits, and their products have \(O(p)\) bits before rounding. Rounding an input rational mass also uses polynomial-size integer arithmetic.

The sums defining \(A_i\) and \(b_i\) have polynomial bit length. Thus each \(d_i\widetilde P_i\) has polynomial bit length. Forming \(S\) exactly is also polynomial-time: a common denominator obtained by multiplying the individual denominators has bit length at most the sum of their bit lengths, which is polynomial because the monomial list is explicit and \(N\le L\).

The final output rounding has polynomial cost. Altogether, the running time is polynomial in

\(\displaystyle m,\ \tau,\ L,\ \max\{0,\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\},\)

and therefore satisfies the requested bound in \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon^{-1},\log(1/\alpha)\).

5. Relation to the sampling result.

The cited [finite-game result](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10479-022-05044-0) analyzes stratified sampling. Its range-dependent sample bound does not impose a lower bound on other algorithms.

Here, explicit monomials permit direct integration. The proof handles exponentially large coefficients by increasing numerical precision only polynomially, and handles exponentially large degrees by repeated squaring. Neither a monotonicity assumption nor a polynomial marginal-range bound is needed.
