The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported types \(t=(\succ_t,\rho_t)\), where \(\succ_t\) is a strict ranking and \(\rho_t\) identifies a constituency representative, and a fixed sequence \(\pi\) of \(m-1\) turns labelled by representatives, compute the unique SPNE winner \(c^\star\) and its weighted Borda welfare \(W_\mu(c^\star)=\sum_t \mu_t(m-r(t,c^\star))\). Each representative maximizes the ranking \(\succ_t\) over the final winner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(t=(\succ_t,\rho_t)\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq 1\) candidates \(C\). Each \(\succ_t\) is a strict ranking of \(C\), and \(\rho_t\) names a representative. Types naming the same representative must have the same ranking, which is that representative's preference over the final winner.
The representatives are the distinct labels in the support. An explicitly given sequence \(\pi\) of length \(m-1\) names the acting representative at each turn; labels may repeat, and representatives may have no turns.
The game has complete information and observed actions. At each turn, the acting representative eliminates exactly one remaining candidate; the sole surviving candidate wins. Representatives maximize their own preference over that winner.
Compute the common winner \(c^\star\) of all subgame-perfect Nash equilibria and its exact weighted Borda welfare \(W_\mu(c^\star)=\sum_t\mu_t(m-r_t(c^\star))\).
Is computing \((c^\star,W_\mu(c^\star))\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total binary input length \(L\), or is it NP-hard?
For a finite list of voters with strict rankings and a fixed elimination sequence, all subgame-perfect Nash equilibria have the same winner, and that winner is polynomial-time computable. No separate result about rational mass inputs is stated in the supplied material.

Result

Computing \((c^\star,W_\mu(c^\star))\) is polynomial-time solvable in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total binary input length \(L\), using the finite-voter theorem supplied in the question.

The rational masses enter only the welfare calculation. We can compute the strategic winner without expanding those masses into an explicit population.

1. Backward induction depends only on the acting preferences

For each representative \(\rho\), let \(\succ_\rho\) be its ranking. This is well-defined because all types naming \(\rho\) have the same ranking.

Number the turns from \(1\) to \(m-1\). For a remaining candidate set \(S\) at turn \(i\), define \(F_i(S)\) recursively. At a terminal state, set \(F_m(\{c\})=c\). At a nonterminal state, set
\(F_i(S)=\operatorname{best}_{\succ_{\pi_i}}\{F_{i+1}(S\setminus\{x\}):x\in S\}\),
where \(\operatorname{best}_{\succ}\) selects the most preferred candidate in the indicated set.

We justify that this recursion describes the common equilibrium winner. Proceed by induction on the number of remaining turns.

At a terminal state, the sole remaining candidate wins. At a nonterminal state, the induction hypothesis gives a unique equilibrium winner following each possible elimination. The acting representative therefore chooses an elimination whose continuation winner is its most preferred among those available. Strict rankings make that preferred candidate unique, although several eliminations may yield it. Thus every subgame-perfect equilibrium has the same winner at this state. Backward induction also constructs such an equilibrium.

If mixed strategies are included, the conclusion is unchanged under any utility representation consistent with the strict ranking: each child subgame produces a deterministic winner, and an optimal mixture can assign positive probability only to actions yielding the unique best continuation candidate.

Consequently, \(c^\star=F_1(C)\). This recursion establishes outcome equivalence below; we do not propose evaluating its exponentially many possible states.

2. A polynomial-size finite-voter instance

Suppose \(m\geq 2\). Construct \(m-1\) distinct voters \(v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1}\). Give voter \(v_i\) the ranking \(\succ_{\pi_i}\), and use the elimination sequence \(v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1}\).

This construction accommodates repeated representatives: each occurrence receives a separate voter with the same ranking. Representatives with no turns require no voter in the constructed instance.

At every turn and every remaining candidate set, the acting voter in the constructed game has exactly the ranking of the acting representative in the original game. Hence the two games have identical backward-induction recursions. By Section 1, they have the same common subgame-perfect equilibrium winner. This argument justifies splitting a representative across turns; it does not assume that the two games have identical strategy profiles.

The constructed instance has \(m-1\) voters and at most \(m(m-1)\) ranking entries. After indexing candidates and representatives, its encoding length is polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\). It satisfies the supplied finite-voter theorem’s hypotheses: a finite voter list, strict rankings, and a fixed elimination sequence. Applying that theorem therefore computes \(c^\star\) in polynomial time.

For \(m=1\), the sole candidate is immediately the winner.

3. Exact weighted welfare has polynomial bit complexity

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(a_t,b_t\) are positive integers encoded in binary. Once \(c^\star\) is known, obtain its rank in each listed type and set \(s_t=m-r_t(c^\star)\). Thus \(0\leq s_t\leq m-1\).

Define
\(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t\)
and
\(N=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_t(D/b_t)s_t\).
Every quotient \(D/b_t\) is an integer, and direct substitution gives \(W_\mu(c^\star)=N/D\).

The binary length of \(D\) is at most \(1+\sum_t\lceil\log_2 b_t\rceil\), hence is \(O(L)\). Because the masses sum to \(1\),
\(0\leq N/D\leq m-1\),
so \(N\) has binary length \(O(L+\log m)\).

The product, exact divisions, multiplications, and additions above therefore involve only polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial binary length. Computing the ranks requires at most \(O(\tau m)\) ranking-entry inspections. If a reduced fraction is required, divide \(N\) and \(D\) by their greatest common divisor, also computable in polynomial time.

For \(m=1\), every Borda score is zero, so the welfare is exactly \(0\).

Conclusion

The supplied finite-voter algorithm computes the winner on a polynomial-size instance, and exact rational arithmetic computes its weighted Borda welfare in polynomial time. Thus the pair \((c^\star,W_\mu(c^\star))\) is polynomial-time computable without any separate theorem for rational-mass populations.
