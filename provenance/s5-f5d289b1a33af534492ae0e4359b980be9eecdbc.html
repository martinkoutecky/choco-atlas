The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(\mathcal T\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict rankings over acceptable partner types, and nonnegative rational pair costs \(w_{tu}\), find in time polynomial in the input size a stable mass assignment \(x_{tu}\) with cost at most twice the minimum stable-assignment cost. Require \(\sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}\le \mu_t\), and require for every acceptable pair \(tu\) that at least one of the two types has zero mass unmatched or assigned below the other type in its ranking. Assume a stable assignment exists and that each type has at most two possible partner types across all stable mass assignments.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\mathcal T\) lists the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Mutually acceptable pairs form a simple undirected graph \(G=(\mathcal T,E)\), with \(m=|E|\). Each type strictly ranks its neighbors, and every acceptable partner is preferred to being unmatched. Each unordered pair \(tu\in E\) has a nonnegative binary-rational cost \(w_{tu}\).
A mass assignment has one variable \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge0\) per unordered pair and satisfies \(\sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}\le\mu_t\). Write \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}\).
For \(tu\in E\), define \(d_t(u)=r_t+\sum_{v:tv\in E,\ u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\), where \(u\succ_t v\) means that \(t\) prefers \(u\) to \(v\). The assignment is stable exactly when \(d_t(u)=0\) or \(d_u(t)=0\) for every \(tu\in E\).
Promise that a stable assignment exists and that \(\left|\{u:tu\in E,\ x_{tu}>0\text{ for some stable assignment }x\}\right|\le2\) for every \(t\).
Let \(C(x)=\sum_{tu\in E}w_{tu}x_{tu}\), \(\mathrm{OPT}=\min\{C(x):x\text{ is stable}\}\), and \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Does this promised problem admit an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) that finds a stable assignment \(x\) with \(C(x)\le2\mathrm{OPT}\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete stable roommates with strict incomplete preferences, arbitrary nonnegative edge costs, and at most two possible partners per agent across all stable matchings, a polynomial-time \(2\)-approximation is known. [Farczadi–Guričanová](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09083)
For the broader edge-capacitated non-bipartite stable-allocation model, Dean and Munshi claim a polynomial-time \(2\)-approximation; their result does not specifically study the support promise above. [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize positive support masses to sum to one, as the society is a distribution.
  • Use a simple undirected acceptability graph without within-type pairs, following the discrete graph model; use one assignment variable and one cost per unordered pair.
  • Interpret the blocking condition as zero combined unmatched and strictly worse-assigned mass at at least one endpoint; acceptable partners rank above being unmatched.
  • Interpret the two-partner promise globally across all stable assignments, counting a partner whenever its assigned mass is positive.
  • Define costs per unit of paired mass, count each unordered pair once, and set m to the number of acceptable pairs.
  • Retain unmatched mass: the discrete treatment reduces to perfect matchings without loss of generality rather than requiring perfectness in the original input.

Result

The promised problem admits a deterministic polynomial-time \(2\)-approximation.

The proof uses the structural rotation representation of stable allocations, then derives the approximation guarantee explicitly. In particular, it does not require expanding rational masses into individual agents.

1. A symmetric bipartite representation

Create two copies of every type, with quota \(\mu_t\) on each copy. For every original edge \(tu\), include the directed bipartite edges \(t_{\mathrm L}u_{\mathrm R}\) and \(u_{\mathrm L}t_{\mathrm R}\), each with capacity \(\min(\mu_t,\mu_u)\). Each copy inherits its type’s preferences.

These capacities do not change the stability condition. If an edge reaches capacity, the endpoint with the smaller quota assigns its entire quota to that edge, so its deficiency for that partner is zero.

Add a dummy vertex \(0\) on each side, with quota \(1\). Connect every real vertex to the opposite dummy, with capacity \(\mu_t\), and connect the two dummies with capacity \(1\). Real vertices rank the dummy last. The two dummies use the same arbitrary ordering of real types, followed by the opposite dummy.

For a feasible matrix \(A\) on the real bipartite edges, its unique completion to an allocation filling every quota is

\[ \text{} \]

\(Z_{tu}=A_{tu},\qquad Z_{t0}=\mu_t-\sum_u A_{tu},\qquad Z_{0t}=\mu_t-\sum_u A_{ut},\qquad Z_{00}=\sum_{t,u}A_{tu}.\)

All auxiliary edges are nonblocking: on an edge incident with a real vertex and a dummy, the real vertex has no allocation worse than the dummy; the dummy–dummy edge is last at both endpoints.

Consequently, stability of \(Z\) is equivalent to stability on the real edges, with dummy allocations representing unmatched mass. Moreover, \(Z\) is symmetric exactly when \(A\) is symmetric. Thus original stable assignments correspond exactly to symmetric stable allocations of this augmented instance.

The augmented graph has \(2m+2\tau+1\) edges, strict preferences, positive rational quotas, and rational capacities. It therefore satisfies the hypotheses of the following structural result.

Known structural theorem. A bipartite stable-allocation instance has a polynomially computable rotation representation

\(Z(a)=Z^0+\sum_{\rho\in R}h_\rho a_\rho\Delta^\rho,\)

where \(h_\rho>0\), each \(\Delta^\rho\) is a signed alternating-cycle vector, and \(a\in[0,1]^R\). Stable allocations correspond exactly to vectors satisfying

\(a_\sigma=0\ \text{or}\ a_\rho=1\qquad\text{whenever }\rho\to\sigma\)

in a precedence DAG. For a symmetric instance, there is a computable involution \(\rho\mapsto\rho^*\), with equal paired multiplicities, and symmetry is equivalent to

\(a_\rho+a_{\rho^*}=1.\)

There are linearly many rotations in the number of bipartite edges. Dummy-edge allocations are invariant across stable allocations. These statements are established in Lemmas 10–14 and the dummy-invariance observation following Lemma 12 of [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

In particular, every original type has an invariant matched mass

\(q_t=\sum_{u:tu\in E}x_{tu}=\mu_t-r_t\)

across all stable assignments, because \(r_t=Z_{t0}\) is invariant.

2. Reducing the rotation representation to Boolean assignments

A self-dual rotation necessarily has \(a_\rho=\tfrac12\). For each pair of distinct dual rotations, introduce one variable \(b_i\), and write their coordinates as \(b_i\) and \(1-b_i\).

Restrict these variables to \(\{0,1\}\). Every precedence constraint becomes a clause with at most two literals. A constraint involving a self-dual coordinate becomes a unit clause, or an impossibility. Let the resulting \(2\)-SAT formula be \(\Phi\).

We need more than existence of these Boolean solutions: they must capture the support of every stable assignment.

Lemma 1. Every stable assignment is a convex combination of stable assignments obtained from satisfying Boolean assignments of \(\Phi\).

Proof. Consider the normalized rotation vector of any symmetric stable allocation. Keep self-dual coordinates at \(\tfrac12\), and keep every non-self-dual pair already at endpoints unchanged. Round each remaining pair arbitrarily to either \((0,1)\) or \((1,0)\).

Every precedence constraint was satisfied because its successor coordinate was exactly zero or its predecessor coordinate was exactly one. Such a coordinate belongs to a pair already at endpoints, which remains unchanged. Therefore every choice of rounding preserves all precedence constraints and symmetry.

The original vector is a convex combination of these rounded vectors: independently choose each rounded coordinate with expectation equal to its original value. The allocation map is affine, so its image is the corresponding convex combination of stable allocations. Projecting onto original edges proves the claim. \(\square\)

The existence promise and Lemma 1 imply that \(\Phi\) is satisfiable.

3. Computing the entire stable support

The following elementary property makes support computation polynomial.

Lemma 2. Given a satisfiable \(2\)-SAT formula on \(k\) variables, one can compute polynomially many satisfying assignments whose affine hull contains every satisfying assignment.

Proof. Form its implication graph. Within each strongly connected component, all literals have the same truth value in every satisfying assignment. Complementation pairs these components; each pair determines a group of variables tied together by equalities or negations.

Fix a satisfying assignment \(a\). For every component pair, use a satisfiability test to determine whether its orientation can differ from its orientation in \(a\). If so, obtain a satisfying assignment \(b\) with the opposite orientation.

There is a path from \(a\) to \(b\) through satisfying assignments, changing one complementary component pair at each step. To see this, let \(S\) consist of components that are false in the current assignment and true in \(b\). Choose a sink \(C\) in the implication DAG induced by \(S\), and make \(C\) true and its complement false.

No implication becomes violated:

  • An implication from a newly true literal ends at a literal true in \(b\). If that literal was previously false, its component belongs to \(S\), and the sink property forces it to lie in \(C\).
  • An implication into a newly false literal is checked by its contrapositive, which gives the same argument.

This move removes one disagreement with \(b\). Repeating reaches \(b\) after at most \(k\) moves.

Collect \(a\) and every assignment on these paths. For every component pair whose orientation is not fixed, the collection contains a difference vector changing precisely that group. These vectors span all possible differences between satisfying assignments, because every variable belongs to exactly one such group. The collection therefore has the required affine hull, and has polynomial size. \(\square\)

Apply Lemma 2 to \(\Phi\), and map its witnesses through the rotation representation. Call the resulting original stable assignments \(W\).

By Lemma 1 and affinity of the allocation map, the affine hull of \(W\) contains every stable assignment. Define

\(H=(\mathcal T,E_H),\qquad E_H=\{e\in E:x_e>0\text{ for some }x\in W\}.\)

This is exactly the true stable-support graph. Indeed, if an edge coordinate vanishes on every member of \(W\), it vanishes throughout their affine hull, hence on every stable assignment. Conversely, every positive coordinate in a witness is realized by a stable assignment.

Thus \(H\), a stable assignment, and the invariant masses \(q_t\) are all computable in polynomial time. By the promise, \(H\) has maximum degree at most two.

4. The feasible assignments on the support form a product of intervals

Consider the polytope

\(P=\{x\ge0:x_e=0\text{ for }e\notin E_H,\ \sum_{u:tu\in E_H}x_{tu}=q_t\text{ for every }t\}.\)

Every stable assignment belongs to \(P\). Every member of \(P\) is feasible for the original mass constraints because \(q_t\le\mu_t\).

Each component of \(H\) is an isolated vertex, a path, or a cycle. The degree equations have the following structure:

  • On a path, edge values are uniquely determined by starting at a leaf.
  • On an odd cycle, they are uniquely determined.
  • On an even cycle, they have one degree of freedom.

For completeness, if two solutions differ by \(z\), then consecutive cycle-edge differences satisfy \(z_{j+1}=-z_j\). Traversing an odd cycle forces \(z_j=-z_j\), hence \(z=0\); an even cycle leaves one free parameter.

On an even cycle, number the edges from zero. Its assignments can therefore be written as

\(x_{e_j}=a_j+(-1)^j s.\)

Nonnegativity gives the closed interval

\(\alpha=\max_{j\text{ even}}(-a_j),\qquad \beta=\min_{j\text{ odd}}a_j,\qquad s\in[\alpha,\beta].\)

The interval is nonempty because \(P\) contains a stable witness. Components with degenerate intervals are fixed. Therefore \(P\) is a product of nondegenerate intervals, together with fixed edge coordinates.

For any acceptable pair \(tu\), the deficiency \(d_t(u)\) is either constant or an affine function of the single interval parameter belonging to \(t\)’s component. It is nonnegative throughout that interval, since unmatched mass is the fixed nonnegative quantity \(\mu_t-q_t\), and every edge allocation is nonnegative.

We use the following consequence:

If a nonnegative affine function on a nondegenerate interval vanishes at an interior point, it vanishes identically.

Indeed, a nonzero slope would make the function negative on one side of an interior zero.

5. An optimum exists at interval endpoints

The stable set is compact: feasibility is bounded and closed, and stability is expressed by the closed equations \(d_t(u)d_u(t)=0\). Hence an optimum exists.

Take any stable assignment. For every interval parameter lying strictly inside its interval, replace it by either endpoint. Leave parameters already at endpoints unchanged.

This preserves stability. For each original edge, choose an endpoint whose deficiency was zero. If its parameter was interior, the preceding observation shows that this deficiency is identically zero on the interval. If its parameter was already at a boundary, or its component was fixed, its deficiency remains unchanged. Thus every edge retains a zero deficiency at some endpoint.

Each component’s cost is affine in its parameter. For an interior parameter, choose the endpoint with smaller component cost. Its cost cannot exceed the interior cost, which is a convex combination of the endpoint costs.

It follows that some optimal stable assignment uses only interval endpoints.

Introduce a Boolean variable \(b_i\) for every nondegenerate interval: zero selects its lower endpoint, and one selects its upper endpoint.

For any type–partner pair, the predicate \(d_t(u)=0\), evaluated at endpoints, is one of:

  • true;
  • false;
  • \(b_i\);
  • \(\neg b_i\).

For each original edge \(tu\), impose

\((d_t(u)=0)\ \lor\ (d_u(t)=0).\)

After removing constants and simplifying, this is a \(2\)-SAT formula \(\Psi\). It is satisfiable by the endpoint-rounding argument. Its satisfying assignments correspond exactly to stable endpoint assignments.

Let \(K\ge0\) be the cost of all fixed components, and let \(c_i^0,c_i^1\ge0\) be component \(i\)’s costs at its two endpoints. The cost becomes

\(K+\sum_i\bigl(c_i^0(1-b_i)+c_i^1b_i\bigr).\)

Its minimum over satisfying assignments of \(\Psi\) is exactly \(\mathrm{OPT}\).

6. A factor-two approximation for the Boolean objective

Relax each \(b_i\) to \(p_i\in[0,1]\). Give a positive literal value \(p_i\), and a negative literal value \(1-p_i\). For each two-literal clause require the sum of its literal values to be at least one; for each unit clause require its literal value to be one.

Minimize

\(K+\sum_i\bigl(c_i^0(1-p_i)+c_i^1p_i\bigr).\)

Let \(p\) be an optimal LP solution, and let \(b^{\mathrm{ref}}\) be any satisfying assignment of \(\Psi\). Round by

\(b_i=0\text{ if }p_i<\tfrac12,\qquad b_i=1\text{ if }p_i>\tfrac12,\qquad b_i=b_i^{\mathrm{ref}}\text{ if }p_i=\tfrac12.\)

This satisfies \(\Psi\). A clause containing a literal whose LP value exceeds \(\tfrac12\) is satisfied by rounding. Otherwise, LP feasibility forces both literal values to equal \(\tfrac12\), and the reference assignment satisfies the clause. Unit clauses have literal value one and are also satisfied.

For each variable,

\(c_i^{b_i}\le 2\bigl(c_i^0(1-p_i)+c_i^1p_i\bigr).\)

If \(p_i>\tfrac12\), the LP contribution contains more than half of \(c_i^1\); the case \(p_i<\tfrac12\) is analogous. At equality, either chosen cost is at most \(c_i^0+c_i^1\), twice the LP contribution.

Since \(K\ge0\), the resulting stable assignment satisfies

\(C(x)\le2\,\mathrm{LP}_{\min}\le2\,\mathrm{OPT}.\)

This argument also covers \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\).

7. Bit complexity

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the masses. Its bit length is at most \(L\). Scaling quotas and capacities by \(D\) produces integral data without increasing the graph size.

The bipartite rotation construction uses polynomially many operations. Its initial allocation and rotation multiplicities are integral on integral input, obtained through residual-capacity updates; their magnitudes are bounded by the scaled quotas. Thus their bit lengths are polynomial in \(L\). No step iterates \(D\) times.

The \(2\)-SAT witness construction uses polynomially many satisfiability tests and component flips. Interval endpoints, component costs, and LP coefficients consequently have polynomial bit length. Rational linear programming and exact rounding are polynomial-time operations.

Therefore the entire algorithm, including writing its rational output, runs in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).
