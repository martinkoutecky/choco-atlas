The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), group memberships \(a_{tj}\), a nonnegative rational type-mixing kernel \(K\), a rational seed-mass budget \(\kappa\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). Define \(q^{(0)}=x\) and \(q_u^{(r+1)}=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)(1-\exp(-\sum_t K_{tu}q_t^{(r)}))\), and let \(q(x)\) be its limit. For each group \(j\), let \(\mu_j=\sum_t a_{tj}\mu_t>0\) and \(Q_j(x)=\sum_t a_{tj}q_t(x)\). Compute a feasible \(x\) whose value \(\rho(x)=\min_j Q_j(x)/\mu_j\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the maximum feasible value.

Result

The multiplicative guarantee is attainable in polynomial time. In fact, a deterministic algorithm achieves the stronger guarantee \( \rho(x)\ge (1-1/e-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT} \), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\). There is no additional polynomial dependence on \(1/\kappa\); only \(\log(1/\kappa)\), already included in \(L\), is needed.

The additive approximation problem is NP-hard, even with \(m=1\), \(\kappa=1/4\), and contractive diffusion. A randomized algorithm with the requested polynomial running time and success probability would imply \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{RP}\).

The proofs below concern the given mass recurrence directly. They do not require the group-count condition in the discrete results.

1. Fixed points and a branching representation

Write \(F_x(q)\) for the recurrence map:

\( \displaystyle (F_x(q))_u=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)\left(1-\exp\left(-\sum_tK_{tu}q_t\right)\right). \)

On the box \(0\le q\le\mu\), this map is coordinatewise nondecreasing and satisfies \(x\le F_x(q)\le\mu\). Consequently, iteration from \(x\) increases to a fixed point. Every fixed point dominates every iterate, so this is the least fixed point.

Set \(p_t=x_t/\mu_t\). Consider the following random rooted tree, with a specified root type \(u\):

  • A vertex of type \(v\) has independently Poisson-distributed numbers of children of each type \(t\), with means \(\mu_tK_{tv}\).
  • Each vertex of type \(t\) is independently marked with probability \(p_t\).

Every finite-depth tree is finite almost surely. The probability that the root’s depth-\(r\) tree contains a marked vertex is \(q_u^{(r)}/\mu_u\). Indeed, the depth-zero probability is \(p_u\), and Poisson thinning gives precisely the stated recurrence.

It follows that

\( \displaystyle \frac{q_u(x)}{\mu_u}=\Pr(\text{some vertex of the full tree is marked}). \)

For a finite tree having \(N_t\) vertices of type \(t\), its conditional marking probability is

\( \displaystyle H_N(p)=1-\prod_t(1-p_t)^{N_t}. \)

Thus finite-depth adoption functions are averages of these functions, and the infinite-depth functions are their increasing limits.

Two consequences will be used:

  • All first partial derivatives are nonnegative, and all second partial derivatives, including the diagonal ones, are nonpositive wherever differentiation is justified.
  • For \(0\le\lambda\le1\),

\( \displaystyle q_u(\lambda x)\ge\lambda q_u(x). \)

For the second assertion, \(H_N(\lambda p)\) is concave as a function of \(\lambda\), and vanishes at zero. Apply its concavity inequality, average over finite trees, and then take the increasing depth limit. This argument also applies when some seed coordinates vanish.

2. Polynomial evaluation after positive seeding

We first establish the numerical fact needed by the approximation algorithm.

Lemma. If \(x\) is rational and \(0<x_t\le\mu_t\) for every type, then \(q(x)\), and its first derivatives with respect to rationally scaled seed coordinates, can be approximated to absolute error \(h>0\) in time polynomial in the input length and \(\log(1/h)\).

2.1 Uniqueness

For positive \(x\), the fixed point is unique.

Suppose \(q,r\) are fixed points and \(\lambda=\max_u q_u/r_u>1\). Concavity of \(F_x\), together with \(F_x(0)=x\), gives

\( \displaystyle F_x(\lambda r)\le\lambda F_x(r)-(\lambda-1)x. \)

Monotonicity and \(q\le\lambda r\) then imply

\( \displaystyle q=F_x(q)\le\lambda r-(\lambda-1)x. \)

This contradicts equality \(q_u=\lambda r_u\) at a coordinate attaining the maximum ratio. If two fixed points differ, one of the two orders of comparison has a maximum ratio exceeding one. Hence they must coincide.

2.2 Convex computation of the fixed point

Define

\( \displaystyle C_x=\{w:0\le w\le\mu,\ w\le F_x(w)\}. \)

This is convex because every coordinate of \(F_x(w)-w\) is concave.

Every \(w\in C_x\) satisfies \(w\le q(x)\): iteration from \(w\) increases to a fixed point, which is unique. Since \(q(x)\in C_x\), it is the coordinatewise maximum of \(C_x\). Therefore it is the unique maximizer of

\( \displaystyle \max_{w\in C_x}\sum_u w_u. \)

This convex optimization problem is well bounded with explicitly controlled binary parameters. Let

\( \displaystyle x_{\min}=\min_t x_t,\qquad M=\max\{1,\max_{t,u}K_{tu}\}. \)

At \(w=x/2\), every inequality \(F_x(w)-w\ge0\) has slack at least \(x_{\min}/2\). On the population box, each constraint has gradient norm at most \(\tau M+1\) in the sum norm. Thus a Euclidean ball centered at \(x/2\), of radius

\( \displaystyle r=\frac{x_{\min}}{8(\tau M+1)}, \)

lies in \(C_x\). The set lies in a ball of radius \(2\tau\) about that center. Both radii have polynomial binary description length.

There is also a polynomial-time weak separation oracle. Box violations have exact separating hyperplanes. Inside the box, the concave constraints and their gradients can be evaluated to arbitrary binary precision. If all constraint violations are at most \(d\), mixing the query point with \(x/2\) produces a feasible point at distance at most \(2d\sqrt{\tau}/x_{\min}\). Otherwise, a sufficiently accurate tangent hyperplane separates the query point from \(C_x\).

Here exponential evaluation requires only polynomial time in the requested precision and argument length: sufficiently large positive arguments can be truncated, while the remaining arguments have magnitude bounded by the precision requirement.

The weak separation-to-optimization theorem for a convex body with known inner and outer balls now applies. It produces a rational \(y\) within distance \(\gamma\) of \(C_x\), with objective value at least its optimum minus \(\gamma\), in time polynomial in the dimension, binary radii, and \(\log(1/\gamma)\). This is the standard ellipsoid theorem of [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf).

Because every point of \(C_x\) lies coordinatewise below \(q(x)\), the returned point satisfies

\( \displaystyle \|y-q(x)\|_1\le(2\tau+1)\gamma. \)

Indeed, its positive coordinate errors total at most \(\tau\gamma\), and its objective deficit is at most \(\gamma\). Thus the convex optimization computes \(q(x)\) to any requested absolute precision with polynomial bit complexity.

2.3 Derivatives and conditioning

Let \(J\) be the Jacobian of \(F_x\) with respect to \(q\), evaluated at the fixed point. Writing \(H_u=\sum_tK_{tu}q_t\),

\( \displaystyle J_{ut}=(\mu_u-x_u)e^{-H_u}K_{tu}. \)

The inequality \(H_ue^{-H_u}\le1-e^{-H_u}\) implies

\( \displaystyle Jq\le q-x. \)

Put \(p_{\min}=\min_u x_u/\mu_u>0\). Since \(q\le\mu\),

\( \displaystyle Jq\le(1-p_{\min})q. \)

In the weighted maximum norm \(\|v\|_q=\max_u|v_u|/q_u\), the operator norm of \(J\) is therefore at most \(1-p_{\min}\). In particular, \(I-J\) is invertible, and

\( \displaystyle \|(I-J)^{-1}\|_\infty\le\frac{1}{x_{\min}p_{\min}}. \)

For scaled coordinates \(x_t=b_tz_t\), implicit differentiation gives

\( \displaystyle \frac{\partial q}{\partial z}=(I-J)^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(e^{-H_u})\operatorname{diag}(b_t). \)

All required conditioning parameters enter through their logarithms. More explicitly, a fixed-point error \(\zeta\) changes \(J\) by at most \(\tau^2M^2\zeta\) in maximum row-sum norm. Choosing this below half the inverse-norm bound ensures that matrix inversion remains stable. The resulting derivative error is bounded by an explicit expression polynomial in \(M,\tau,1/x_{\min},1/p_{\min}\), times \(\zeta\).

Accordingly, only a polynomial number of precision bits is needed. Group normalization similarly requires \(\log(1/\mu_j)\) precision bits. This proves the lemma.

3. Smoothness bounds independent of \(1/\kappa\)

Define

\( \displaystyle b_t=\min\{\mu_t,\kappa\},\qquad c_t=\frac{b_t}{\mu_t},\qquad x_t=b_tz_t. \)

Every feasible allocation corresponds exactly to a point in

\( \displaystyle P=\{z\in[0,1]^\tau:\sum_tb_tz_t\le\kappa\}. \)

For group \(j\), let

\( \displaystyle f_j(z)=\frac{\sum_ta_{tj}q_t(bz)}{\mu_j}. \)

Fix \(s>0\) and restrict attention to \(z_t\ge s\). Then \(p_t=c_tz_t\ge sc_t\).

For a finite tree, write \(S=\sum_tN_tp_t\). Differentiating its conditional marking probability with respect to \(z_i\), and deleting the corresponding factor from the product, gives

\( \displaystyle 0\le\partial_iH_N\le\frac{e}{s}Se^{-S}. \)

For a second derivative, deleting two factors gives

\( \displaystyle |\partial_{ij}H_N|\le\frac{e^2}{s^2}S^2e^{-S}. \)

Also \(H_N\ge1-e^{-S}\). Using

\( \displaystyle \frac{S}{e^S-1}\le1,\qquad \frac{S^2}{e^S-1}\le2, \)

and \(\max_{S\ge0}Se^{-S}=1/e\), we obtain

\( \displaystyle 0\le\partial_iH_N\le\min\{1/s,(e/s)H_N\}, \)

\( \displaystyle -\frac{2e^2}{s^2}H_N\le\partial_{ij}H_N\le0. \)

These bounds pass to the full-tree expectation. To justify differentiation, couple all depths within the same full tree. On a finite full tree, the derivatives eventually stabilize. On an infinite tree, \(S\) tends to infinity because every marking probability is positive, so the derivatives tend to zero. Uniform bounds on a neighborhood allow dominated convergence and differentiation under the expectation.

Group functions are convex combinations of root-type adoption probabilities, with weights \(a_{tj}\mu_t/\mu_j\). Hence

\( \displaystyle 0\le\partial_if_j(z)\le\min\{1/s,(e/s)f_j(z)\}, \)

\( \displaystyle -\frac{2e^2}{s^2}f_j(z)\le\partial_{ij}f_j(z)\le0. \)

The essential point is that these bounds contain no inverse power of \(\kappa\).

4. The multiplicative algorithm

Let \(c=1-1/e\), and choose

\( \displaystyle \eta=\varepsilon/32,\qquad s=\eta/\tau,\qquad z^0=s\mathbf1,\qquad D=(1-\eta)P. \)

The baseline has seed mass at most \(\eta\kappa\). Any path obtained by starting at \(z^0\) and integrating directions in \(D\) for total time one remains feasible: its budget is at most \(\kappa\), and each coordinate is at most \(s+1-\eta\le1\).

Define the benchmark

\( \displaystyle B=\max_{y\in P}\min_j f_j(z^0+(1-\eta)y). \)

The maximum exists because positive baseline seeding makes the objective continuous on this compact set. Monotonicity and the scaling inequality from Section 1 give

\( \displaystyle B\ge(1-\eta)\mathrm{OPT}. \)

Furthermore, the proportional allocation \(x_t=\kappa\mu_t\) gives every group adoption at least \(\kappa\), so \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge\kappa\).

4.1 A direction satisfying every deficient group

If \(z\ge z^0\), diminishing partial derivatives imply

\( \displaystyle f_j(z^0+v)-f_j(z)\le\nabla f_j(z)\cdot v\qquad(v\in D). \)

For verification, take the coordinatewise maximum \(w=z\vee(z^0+v)\). Monotonicity bounds the left side by \(f_j(w)-f_j(z)\). Along the segment from \(z\) to \(w\), the gradient can only decrease, and \(0\le w-z\le v\).

Consequently, for every target \(\beta\le B\), there exists one direction \(v\in D\) satisfying simultaneously

\( \displaystyle \nabla f_j(z)\cdot v\ge\beta-f_j(z)\quad\text{for every }j. \)

Take \(v=(1-\eta)y\) from a benchmark optimizer.

4.2 Finite-precision implementation

For each target \(\beta\), put \(h=\eta\beta\) and

\( \displaystyle C_0=32\tau^2/s^2,\qquad N=\lceil C_0/\eta\rceil. \)

Starting at \(z^0\), perform \(N\) steps. At a step, approximate every group value with error at most \(h\), and every group gradient with sum-norm error at most \(h\). Denote these approximations by \(\widetilde f_j,\widetilde g_j\).

Call a group active when \(\widetilde f_j<\beta\). Solve the rational linear feasibility problem

\( \displaystyle v\in D,\qquad \widetilde g_j\cdot v\ge\beta-\widetilde f_j-2h\quad\text{for active }j. \)

If it is infeasible, abort this target. If \(\beta\le B\), the direction established above is feasible, even after the oracle errors, so the run cannot abort.

Round the returned direction downward to a fixed binary grid fine enough that its true gradient inner products decrease by at most \(h\). The bound \(\partial_if_j\le1/s\) ensures that grid spacing at most \(sh/\tau\) suffices. Downward rounding preserves membership in \(D\). Update \(z\) by \(v/N\).

For an active group, the rounded direction satisfies

\( \displaystyle \nabla f_j(z)\cdot v\ge\beta-f_j(z)-5h. \)

For an inactive group, its true value is already at least \(\beta-h\) and cannot decrease.

All allocations remain rational with polynomial description length: directions use a fixed binary grid, and updates use the common denominator \(N\).

4.3 Discretization error

For an active group, \(f_j(z)\le\beta+h\). Along its step, concavity in the nonnegative direction and the relative gradient bound give

\( \displaystyle f_j(z+t v)\le f_j(z)\left(1+\frac{e\tau}{sN}\right)\le4\beta\qquad(0\le t\le1/N). \)

The chosen \(N\) ensures the last inequality. The Hessian bound therefore gives the Taylor estimate

\( \displaystyle f_j(z+v/N)\ge f_j(z)+\frac{\beta-f_j(z)-5h}{N}-\frac{C_0\beta}{N^2}. \)

Let \(d_\ell=\max\{0,\beta-f_j(z^\ell)\}\). For an active group,

\( \displaystyle d_{\ell+1}\le(1-1/N)d_\ell+5h/N+C_0\beta/N^2. \)

For an inactive group, \(d_{\ell+1}\le h\). Both cases are bounded inductively by

\( \displaystyle d_\ell\le(1-1/N)^\ell\beta+(5h+C_0\beta/N)\bigl(1-(1-1/N)^\ell\bigr). \)

Since \(C_0/N\le\eta\) and \((1-1/N)^N\le e^{-1}\), every completed run satisfies

\( \displaystyle \min_jf_j(z^N)\ge(c-6\eta)\beta. \)

4.4 Target search and running time

Run this procedure for geometric targets

\( \displaystyle \beta_\ell=(\kappa/2)(1+\eta)^\ell\le1, \)

and return the allocation from the largest completed target.

There are \(O(\eta^{-1}\log(2/\kappa))\) targets. Since \(B\ge(1-\eta)\kappa>\kappa/2\), a target immediately below \(B\) completes and is at least \(B/(1+\eta)\). Therefore

\( \displaystyle \rho(x)\ge\frac{(c-6\eta)(1-\eta)}{1+\eta}\mathrm{OPT}\ge(c-8\eta)\mathrm{OPT}\ge(c-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}. \)

The step count is polynomial in \(\tau,1/\varepsilon\). The oracle precision requirements depend on \(\log(1/h)\). At every query,

\( \displaystyle \frac{x_t}{\mu_t}\ge sc_t\ge s\kappa. \)

Thus Section 2’s conditioning requires only \(O(L+\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log\tau)\) precision bits, apart from polynomial factors. The target count and all rational arithmetic also have polynomial complexity.

This proves the claimed running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\). Since \(c>c^2\), it establishes the requested \(((1-1/e)^2-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\) guarantee.

5. Additive hardness with a fixed budget

We give a polynomial reduction from CLIQUE.

Start with a graph on \(n\) vertices and a target \(2\le k\le n\). Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two at least \(2n\). Add \(N-n\) mutually adjacent universal vertices, obtaining a graph \(G\). Write

\( \displaystyle r=N-n+k,\qquad \omega=\omega(G). \)

Then the original instance is a yes-instance exactly when \(\omega\ge r\), and always \(\omega\ge N/2\).

Create \(N\) vertex types and one sink type. Give each vertex type mass \(1/(2N)\), and the sink mass \(1/2\). There is one group containing every type, and

\( \displaystyle \kappa=1/4. \)

These masses are dyadic rationals.

5.1 The quadratic graph identity

Let \(A_G\) be the adjacency matrix and define

\( \displaystyle B=\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}-A_G. \)

For every nonnegative vector \(z\), with \(s=\sum_i z_i\),

\( \displaystyle z^{\mathsf T}Bz\ge s^2/\omega. \)

Here is a proof. Normalize to \(\sum_i y_i=1\). If two supported vertices are nonadjacent, moving their combined weight entirely to one of them can only increase \(y^{\mathsf T}A_Gy\): with their sum fixed, that expression is affine in the split. Repeating this operation leaves support on a clique.

On a clique of size \(\ell\le\omega\),

\( \displaystyle y^{\mathsf T}A_Gy=1-\sum_i y_i^2\le1-1/\ell\le1-1/\omega. \)

Therefore \(y^{\mathsf T}By\ge1/\omega\). Equality is attained by uniform weight on a maximum clique.

5.2 Diffusion matrix

Let \(d_i=\sum_jB_{ij}\). Define a nonnegative matrix \(C\) by

\( \displaystyle C_{ij}=B_{ij},\qquad C_{i,\mathrm{sink}}=2-d_i/N,\qquad C_{\mathrm{sink},u}=0. \)

All entries lie between zero and two. Every vertex row has weighted sum

\( \displaystyle \sum_u\mu_uC_{iu}=d_i/(2N)+\tfrac12(2-d_i/N)=1. \)

Set

\( \displaystyle K=\alpha C,\qquad \alpha=\frac{1}{1024N^2}. \)

The recurrence is contractive in the sum norm: its Jacobian’s column sums are at most \(\alpha\sum_u\mu_uC_{tu}\le\alpha<1\).

5.3 A uniform first-order approximation

For any feasible allocation \(x\), let \(q=q(x)\) and \(H_u=\sum_tC_{tu}q_t\). Since \(C_{tu}\le2\) and \(\sum_tq_t\le1\), we have \(H_u\le2\).

Using \(0\le z-(1-e^{-z})\le z^2/2\),

\( \displaystyle q=x+\alpha\operatorname{diag}(\mu-x)C^{\mathsf T}q+e,\qquad \|e\|_1\le2\alpha^2. \)

Furthermore,

\( \displaystyle \|q-x\|_1\le\alpha\sum_tq_t\sum_u\mu_uC_{tu}\le\alpha, \)

and the sum-norm operator bound for \(\operatorname{diag}(\mu-x)C^{\mathsf T}\) is at most one. Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \left\|q-x-\alpha\operatorname{diag}(\mu-x)C^{\mathsf T}x\right\|_1\le3\alpha^2. \)

Write \(z\) for the vertex seed coordinates, \(x_{\mathrm{sink}}\) for the sink seed, and \(s=\sum_i z_i\). Define the rational expression

\( \displaystyle A(x)=\sum_t x_t+\alpha\left(s-z^{\mathsf T}Bz-x_{\mathrm{sink}}\sum_iC_{i,\mathrm{sink}}z_i\right). \)

Because the single group has mass one, the preceding estimate says

\( \displaystyle |\rho(x)-A(x)|\le3\alpha^2. \)

5.4 The first-order optimum

The graph identity and feasibility give

\( \displaystyle A(x)\le\kappa+\alpha(s-s^2/\omega)\le\kappa+\alpha(\kappa-\kappa^2/\omega). \)

The last inequality holds because \(s\mapsto s-s^2/\omega\) increases on \(0\le s\le\kappa=1/4\).

Equality is attained by putting no seed in the sink and distributing total seed mass \(\kappa\) uniformly over a maximum clique. This allocation respects the type caps because \(\omega\ge N/2\). Hence, defining

\( \displaystyle U_\ell=\kappa+\alpha(\kappa-\kappa^2/\ell), \)

we have

\( \displaystyle U_\omega-3\alpha^2\le\mathrm{OPT}\le U_\omega+3\alpha^2. \)

6. Recovering CLIQUE from an additive approximation

Request additive tolerance

\( \displaystyle \varepsilon_{\mathrm{add}}=\frac{\alpha}{128N^2}. \)

Its inverse is polynomial in \(N\). The consecutive clique gap satisfies

\( \displaystyle U_r-U_{r-1}=\frac{\alpha}{16r(r-1)}\ge\frac{\alpha}{16N^2}. \)

If the CLIQUE instance is a yes-instance, a successful additive solver returns

\( \displaystyle A(x)\ge\rho(x)-3\alpha^2\ge U_r-6\alpha^2-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{add}}>U_{r-1}. \)

The strict inequality follows from

\( \displaystyle 6\alpha^2+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{add}}=\frac{\alpha}{N^2}\left(\frac6{1024}+\frac1{128}\right)<\frac{\alpha}{16N^2}. \)

If the instance is a no-instance, then \(\omega\le r-1\), and every feasible allocation satisfies

\( \displaystyle A(x)\le U_\omega\le U_{r-1}. \)

Thus the exact rational test \(A(x)>U_{r-1}\) distinguishes the instances whenever the approximation succeeds. No evaluation of exponentials is required for this test.

The construction has polynomial input length, \(m=1\), and the fixed budget \(\kappa=1/4\). Therefore a deterministic algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

For a randomized solver, reject any infeasible output and otherwise apply the rational test. No no-instance can be accepted, while yes-instances are accepted with probability at least \(2/3\). This places CLIQUE in \(\mathrm{RP}\), implying \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{RP}\).
