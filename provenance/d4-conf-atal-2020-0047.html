The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) item classes with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), a finite set \(T\) of complete linear-utility types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute a competitive equilibrium for a negative mixed-manna market with type-level allocation masses \(z_{tj}\), \(\sum_t z_{tj}=q_j\), representative bundles \(a_{tj}=z_{tj}/\mu_t\), prices \(p_j\), per-capita virtual budget \(-1\), and conditions \(\sum_j p_j a_{tj}=-1\) and \(a_t\in\arg\max_{y\ge0}\{\sum_j u_{tj}y_j:\sum_j p_jy_j\le-1\}\) for every \(\mu_t>0\). For fixed \(m\), determine whether one equilibrium, or a finite cell-and-polytope representation of all equilibria, can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\) and the input encoding length.

There are \(m\ge1\) divisible, nondisposable item classes with supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\). Each type has a complete utility vector \(u_t\in\mathbb Q^m\), allowing positive, negative, and zero entries; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Classification

Under the stated definition, computing one equilibrium is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed \(m\).

Computing the specified representation of all equilibria has a different answer:

| Number of item classes | One exact equilibrium | All equilibria in the requested representation |
|---|---|---|
| \(m=1\) | Polynomial time | Polynomial time |
| \(m=2\) | Polynomial time | Polynomial time |
| Every fixed \(m\ge3\) | Polynomial time | The representation need not exist |

The obstruction in the last row is a continuum of equilibrium prices, already with two types of mass \(1/2\). Thus the proposed polynomial-time versus NP-hard dichotomy for the second task does not hold as stated.

The allowance of all maximizing bundles matters here. Nevertheless, the substantive algorithmic extension survives: for every fixed \(m\), all equilibria in which every type obtains strictly negative utility can be computed in polynomial time. At least one such equilibrium always exists.

A sufficient running-time bound for this algorithm is
\[ (\tau m^2+m+1)^{m+1}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,m,L) =\tau^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L) \]
for fixed \(m\). Below, the polynomial factors have absolute, constant exponents.

1. Aggregate budgets and optimality

Write \(u_t\cdot y=\sum_j u_{tj}y_j\). Since \(a_t=z_t/\mu_t\), the representative budget equation is equivalent to
\[ p\cdot z_t=-\mu_t. \]

Moreover, multiplication by \(\mu_t>0\) gives a bijection between the two demand problems
\[ \max\{u_t\cdot y:y\ge0,\ p\cdot y\le-1\} \]
and
\[ \max\{u_t\cdot x:x\ge0,\ p\cdot x\le-\mu_t\}. \]
Their objectives differ by the positive factor \(\mu_t\). Consequently, the support-encoded problem is exactly a market with \(\tau\) aggregate consumers and budgets \(-\mu_t\). No population expansion is required computationally.

We use the following optimality characterization.

Lemma 1. Suppose \(a_t\ge0\) and \(p\cdot a_t=-1\). Then \(a_t\) is a maximizing bundle if and only if there is \(\lambda_t\ge0\) such that
\[ u_{tj}\le\lambda_t p_j\qquad\text{for every }j, \]
and
\[ a_{tj}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{tj}=\lambda_t p_j. \]
In that case,
\[ u_t\cdot a_t=-\lambda_t. \]

Proof. The dual of the representative demand linear program is
\[ \min\{-\lambda:\lambda\ge0,\ \lambda p_j\ge u_{tj}\text{ for every }j\}. \]
If \(a_t\) is optimal, its attained objective is finite. Linear-programming strong duality gives an optimal dual multiplier, and complementary slackness gives the displayed support condition.

Conversely, suppose the conditions hold. For every affordable \(y\ge0\),
\[ u_t\cdot y\le\lambda_t p\cdot y\le-\lambda_t. \]
The support condition and the budget equation give
\[ u_t\cdot a_t=\lambda_t p\cdot a_t=-\lambda_t, \]
so \(a_t\) attains this upper bound. \(\square\)

In particular, strictly negative equilibrium utility is equivalent to \(\lambda_t>0\). The possibility \(\lambda_t=0\) must not be discarded.

2. Existence of an equilibrium with strictly negative utilities

Partition the item classes as
\[ G=\{j:\max_t u_{tj}>0\},\qquad B=\{j:\max_t u_{tj}<0\},\qquad Z=\{j:\max_t u_{tj}=0\}. \]

First, \(B\ne\varnothing\). Otherwise, each item could be assigned entirely to a type valuing it nonnegatively. Every type would then receive nonnegative utility, contradicting the promise.

Delete the classes in \(Z\) temporarily. The resulting instance is still negative: any allocation giving all types nonnegative utility could be extended by assigning each deleted item to a type valuing it at zero.

We use the finite-agent existence theorem for negative linear mixed-manna instances: with equal budgets \(-1\), a competitive equilibrium exists. This is stated as Theorem 2.4 in [Garg–McGlaughlin](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p420.pdf). Arbitrary positive supplies are covered by the change of coordinates \(x_j=y_j/q_j\), with utility coefficients \(q_j u_{tj}\).

Here is a precise application to rational masses; it establishes existence without prescribing an algorithm that expands them.

Choose an integer \(D\) such that
\[ k_t=D\mu_t\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\qquad \sum_t k_t=D. \]
Create \(k_t\) identical agents of each type \(t\), retaining the reduced supplies. This finite instance is negative: if all individual utilities were nonnegative, summing their allocations within each type would yield a forbidden allocation for the reduced society.

Let \(r\) be equilibrium prices for this finite instance. Average the bundles of the \(k_t\) identical agents, obtaining \(\bar x_t\). Their common demand set is convex, so \(\bar x_t\) is also optimal and satisfies \(r\cdot\bar x_t=-1\). Define
\[ z_t=k_t\bar x_t,\qquad a_t=D\bar x_t,\qquad p=r/D. \]
Then
\[ p\cdot a_t=-1, \]
and \(a_t\) is optimal: a bundle \(y\) is affordable at \(p\) precisely when \(y/D\) is affordable at \(r\), and utilities scale by \(D\).

For completeness, every equilibrium of the reduced instance has the following price signs:

  • If \(j\in G\), then \(p_j>0\). A type with \(u_{tj}>0\) would otherwise improve without bound by adding item \(j\) to an affordable bundle.
  • If \(j\in B\), then \(p_j<0\). Some type receives a positive amount of \(j\); Lemma 1 gives \(u_{tj}=\lambda_t p_j<0\), forcing both \(\lambda_t>0\) and \(p_j<0\).

Every type has negative expenditure and therefore consumes a class in \(B\). Its coefficient there is strictly negative, so Lemma 1 forces \(\lambda_t>0\). Thus every type obtains strictly negative utility.

Finally, restore each \(j\in Z\), set \(p_j=0\), and assign its supply to any type with \(u_{tj}=0\). The same multipliers certify optimality: all coefficients on these classes are nonpositive, and allocated coefficients are zero.

We have proved:

Lemma 2. Every promised instance admits an equilibrium in which every type has strictly negative utility, with prices positive on \(G\), negative on \(B\), and zero on \(Z\).

The integer \(D\) was used only in this existence proof.

3. Computing all equilibria with strictly negative utilities

We now give an algorithm operating directly on the \(\tau\) types and their rational masses.

3.1 Demand graphs are constant on price faces

Consider prices on \(G\cup B\), with the signs from Lemma 2. For each type, define
\[ \lambda_t(p)=\min_{b\in B}\frac{u_{tb}}{p_b}>0. \]

Lemma 1 shows that demand is bounded precisely when
\[ \frac{u_{tg}}{p_g}\le\lambda_t(p)\qquad\text{for every }g\in G. \]
Indeed, on a bad class the dual inequality requires
\[ \lambda_t\le\frac{u_{tb}}{p_b}, \]
whereas on a good class it requires the reverse inequality. An optimal bundle spends a negative amount, so it uses a bad class with equality; hence its multiplier must be the displayed minimum.

For prices satisfying the boundedness condition, define the bipartite demand graph \(E(p)\) by
\[ (t,j)\in E(p) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u_{tj}=\lambda_t(p)p_j, \qquad j\in G\cup B. \]

Use the hyperplanes
\[ p_j=0 \]
and
\[ u_{tj}p_k-u_{tk}p_j=0 \qquad (t\in T,\ j<k,\ j,k\in G\cup B). \]
There are at most
\[ N=m+\tau\binom m2 \]
such hyperplanes. On each relatively open face, including faces of lower dimension, all ratio comparisons have fixed signs. Consequently, boundedness and the graph \(E(p)\) are constant there.

Faces of lower dimension are essential because they encode ties.

3.2 A demand graph determines at most one equilibrium price

Discard a face if demand is unbounded or an item vertex is isolated.

Consider a connected component \(C\) of its demand graph. Every type has an incident bad class. On every graph edge, both the utility coefficient and price are nonzero. If a type connects item classes \(j\) and \(k\), then
\[ \frac{p_k}{p_j}=\frac{u_{tk}}{u_{tj}}. \]
Following paths therefore expresses all prices in \(C\) as
\[ p_j=c_j\rho_C, \]
where the \(c_j\) are nonzero rational numbers. Any inconsistent relation causes the face to be discarded.

All types in \(C\) spend only on its item classes, and these classes can be assigned only to its types. Summing aggregate budgets gives
\[ \sum_{j\in C}q_jp_j=-\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t. \]
Therefore
\[ \rho_C = -\frac{\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t} {\sum_{j\in C}q_jc_j}. \]
A zero denominator makes equilibrium impossible, since the numerator is strictly positive. Otherwise, the component prices are uniquely determined and rational.

Compute these prices and check that they belong to the original face.

This is the weighted version of the price reconstruction in Section 3.3 of [Garg–McGlaughlin](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p420.pdf); the normalization uses component mass rather than an agent count.

3.3 All allocations at a candidate price form one rational polytope

Restore \(p_j=0\) on \(Z\). For a surviving candidate \(p\), define \(P(p)\) by
\[ z_{tj}\ge0, \qquad \sum_tz_{tj}=q_j, \qquad \sum_jp_jz_{tj}=-\mu_t, \]
together with
\[ z_{tj}=0 \quad\text{if }j\in G\cup B\text{ and }(t,j)\notin E(p), \]
and
\[ z_{tj}=0 \quad\text{if }j\in Z\text{ and }u_{tj}<0. \]

These are rational linear constraints. Lemma 1 proves both directions:

  • Every point of \(P(p)\), together with \(p\), is an equilibrium.
  • Every equilibrium with strictly negative utilities at \(p\) belongs to \(P(p)\).

All these allocations give type \(t\) representative utility \(-\lambda_t(p)<0\).

Test \(P(p)\) for feasibility by rational linear programming. Retain each nonempty polytope. Lemma 2 ensures that at least one survives; a rational feasible point gives one exact equilibrium.

3.4 Enumeration and bit complexity

An arrangement of \(N\) hyperplanes in dimension at most \(m\) has
\[ O\bigl((m+1)^2(N+1)^m\bigr) \]
faces of all dimensions. To see a sufficient bound, a face of codimension \(k\) has its affine span determined by \(k\) independent hyperplanes. For each such span, the restricted arrangement has at most
\[ \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-k}\binom N\ell \]
full-dimensional regions. Summing over choices of defining hyperplanes gives the stated bound.

Faces can be enumerated incrementally. When adding a hyperplane, test the three possible signs for each current face by linear programming. Strict feasibility can be tested by imposing
\[ -1\le p_j\le1 \]
and replacing all required strict inequalities by inequalities with a common margin \(\delta\), then maximizing \(\delta\). Homogeneity makes a positive margin equivalent to nonemptiness of the specified relatively open face. Thus at most
\[ O\bigl((m+1)^2(N+1)^{m+1}\bigr) \]
linear-programming tests suffice.

Price reconstruction has polynomial bit complexity. An item-to-item simple path uses at most \(m-1\) connecting types, so each \(c_j\) is a product of at most \(m-1\) input ratios. Component mass sums and subsequent rational arithmetic also have bit length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Each allocation polytope has \(\tau m\) variables and \(O(\tau m+\tau+m)\) constraints.

Consequently, a sufficient total bound is
\[ (\tau m^2+m+1)^{m+1}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,m,L). \]
The number of retained pieces is at most
\[ O\bigl((m+1)^2(\tau m^2+m+1)^m\bigr). \]

This proves polynomial-time computation of one exact equilibrium for every fixed \(m\), without expanding binary-encoded masses.

4. A continuum of prices with three item classes

Take two types \(A,B\), with
\[ \mu_A=\mu_B=\frac12,\qquad q_1=q_2=q_3=1, \]
and utilities
\[ u_A=(0,0,-1),\qquad u_B=(-1,-1,-1). \]

The instance is negative. Nonnegative utility for \(B\) would require \(z_B=0\); nonnegative utility for \(A\) would require \(z_{A3}=0\). Together these contradict the positive supply of class \(3\).

For every real \(s\in[0,1/2]\), set
\[ p(s)=\left(-s,-\frac12+s,-\frac12\right) \]
and use the aggregate allocation
\[ z_A=(1,1,0),\qquad z_B=(0,0,1). \]
The representative bundles are
\[ a_A=(2,2,0),\qquad a_B=(0,0,2). \]
Both spend exactly \(-1\).

Type \(A\)'s utility is always nonpositive and its allocated utility is zero, so its bundle is maximizing.

For type \(B\), take \(\lambda_B=2\). Since \(s\in[0,1/2]\),
\[ (-1,-1,-1) \le 2p(s)=(-2s,-1+2s,-1) \]
coordinatewise, with equality on class \(3\). Lemma 1 certifies that \(a_B\) is maximizing.

Hence every \(s\in[0,1/2]\) gives an equilibrium, with a distinct price vector. A finite union
\[ \bigcup_{k=1}^K\bigl(P_k\times\{p^{(k)}\}\bigr) \]
has only finitely many price vectors and cannot contain this equilibrium set. Requiring the listed prices to be rational creates a further obstruction: irrational choices of \(s\) are also equilibria.

For every \(m>3\), add item classes valued at zero by both types, with positive rational supplies and zero prices, and assign them to \(A\). The same proof applies.

Thus, for every fixed \(m\ge3\), the requested representation can fail to exist.

5. All equilibria for one or two item classes

5.1 One item class

Negativity forces
\[ u_{t1}<0\qquad\text{for every }t: \]
otherwise the entire supply could be assigned to a type valuing it nonnegatively.

Summing budgets gives \(p_1q_1=-1\), and each type's budget then determines its allocation:
\[ p_1=-\frac1{q_1},\qquad z_{t1}=\mu_tq_1. \]
These bundles are optimal by Lemma 1. The equilibrium is unique and computable by polynomial-time rational arithmetic.

5.2 Two item classes without a neutral class

There is at least one class in \(B\). If the other class is also in \(B\), or is in \(G\), the price-sign argument in Section 2 applies to every equilibrium.

Every type must consume a negatively priced bad class, whose utility coefficient is strictly negative. Hence every multiplier is positive. Section 3 therefore already computes all equilibria.

5.3 One strictly bad class and one neutral class

Let \(b\in B\) and \(n\in Z\). In every equilibrium, \(p_b<0\).

We first prove \(p_n\le0\). If \(p_n>0\), any type consuming \(n\) satisfies, by Lemma 1,
\[ u_{tn}=\lambda_t p_n. \]
Because \(u_{tn}\le0\), this forces \(\lambda_t=0\) and \(u_{tn}=0\). Such a type cannot consume \(b\), since \(u_{tb}<0\). Its expenditure is therefore nonnegative, contradicting its budget \(-1\). No type could consume \(n\), contradicting market clearing.

If \(p_n=0\), budgets and market clearing force
\[ p_b=-\frac1{q_b},\qquad z_{tb}=\mu_tq_b. \]
The neutral class can be assigned exactly to types with \(u_{tn}=0\). This gives one rational allocation polytope.

It remains to handle \(p_n<0\). Both prices are now negative, all utility coefficients are nonpositive, and the optimal multiplier is
\[ \lambda_t(p) = \min\left\{\frac{u_{tb}}{p_b},\frac{u_{tn}}{p_n}\right\}. \]
Demand edges are exactly the classes attaining this minimum.

The same two-dimensional hyperplane arrangement enumerates these graphs. The only new issue is a zero multiplier. If \(\lambda_t=0\), then \(u_{tn}=0\), while \(u_{tb}<0\); thus this type has only one demand edge, to \(n\).

Therefore zero multipliers do not invalidate price reconstruction. Every type connecting the two item vertices has a positive multiplier and imposes a nonzero rational price ratio. If the vertices are connected, this ratio and the component budget sum determine both prices. If they are disconnected, each one-item component is normalized separately by
\[ q_jp_j=-\sum_{t\in C_j}\mu_t. \]
In either case there is at most one rational equilibrium price vector per face.

At each candidate price, impose market clearing, aggregate budgets, nonnegativity, and zero allocation outside the demand graph. Lemma 1 again proves that the resulting rational polytope contains exactly all equilibrium allocations at that price, including those with zero utility.

This enumerates all equilibria for \(m=2\). A sufficient running-time bound is
\[ (\tau+1)^3\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L), \]
with \(O((\tau+1)^2)\) output pieces.

6. What the finite-agent results extend to

Binary-encoded masses are not an obstacle to fixed-item polynomial-time computation: the component normalization uses \(\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t\), and allocation feasibility uses budgets \(-\mu_t\).

The obstacle to the requested representation of all equilibria is instead the demand convention. Without tie-breaking, zero-utility maximizers can generate a continuum of prices. Under the additional requirement that every equilibrium utility be strictly negative, Section 3 gives the requested finite rational representation for every fixed \(m\); under the task's actual definition, Section 4 prevents that conclusion from \(m=3\) onward.
