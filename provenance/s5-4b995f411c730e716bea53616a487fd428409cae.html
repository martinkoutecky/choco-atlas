The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a listed family \(E\) of contract templates \(e\subseteq T\), and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over templates containing each type \(t\), find nonnegative rational masses \(x_e\) such that \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\leq\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and for every template \(e\), some \(t\in e\) satisfies \(\sum_{f\ni t:\,f\succeq_t e}x_f=\mu_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also lists a family \(E\) of nonempty subsets of \(T\), with \(m=|E|\), and, for each type \(t\), a strict total ranking \(\succ_t\) of all templates containing \(t\). Write \(f\succeq_t e\) when \(f=e\) or \(f\succ_t e\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Find \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{E}\) satisfying \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\leq\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and such that every \(e\in E\) contains some \(t\) with \(\sum_{f\ni t:\,f\succeq_t e}x_f=\mu_t\). Each template uses the same mass \(x_e\) of every participating type; unused mass is allowed.

Can this search problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

The unit-capacity version is already a fractional search problem and is PPAD-complete ([Kintali et al.](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/120874655)).
For equal masses, the stated problem is that same problem up to uniform normalization; thus its PPAD-hardness already applies. Unit-capacity hardness persists with maximum degree three ([Ishizuka and Kamiyama](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Excluded empty templates: stability requires a witnessing member, and an empty edge would contradict the intended existence guarantee.
  • Defined m as the number of explicitly listed templates and L as the total binary input length.
  • Specified polynomial-time Turing reductions for NP-hardness because the requested problem is a search problem.
  • Retained the supplied normalization and strict rankings; equality is included in weak preference.

1. Feasibility, domination, and existence

If $m=0$, the empty vector solves the instance. Assume henceforth that $m>0$.

Let $A$ be the incidence matrix:
$$ A_{t,e}= \begin{cases} 1,&t\in e,\\ 0,&t\notin e. \end{cases} $$
Define
$$ P=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}:x\geq 0,\ Ax\leq\mu\}. $$

The polytope $P$ is nonempty because $0\in P$. It is bounded: for every template $e$, choose $t\in e$, which is possible because templates are nonempty. Then every $x\in P$ satisfies
$$ 0\leq x_e\leq\sum_{f\ni t}x_f\leq\mu_t\leq 1. $$

For a feasible vector $x$, say that $t$ dominates $e$ if:

  • $t\in e$;
  • $\sum_{f\ni t}x_f=\mu_t$;
  • every incident template $f$ with $x_f>0$ satisfies $f\succeq_t e$.

This is precisely the witness condition in the question. Indeed,
$$ \sum_{f\ni t}x_f = \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\ f\succeq_t e}}x_f + \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\ e\succ_t f}}x_f. $$
If the first sum equals $\mu_t$, feasibility and nonnegativity force the total sum to equal $\mu_t$ and every coordinate appearing in the second sum to vanish. Conversely, those two properties imply the required equality.

I use the following established form of Scarf’s lemma: a bounded polytope $\{z\geq0:Bz\leq b\}$, with $B$ nonnegative, no zero column, $b>0$, and a total preference order on each row’s nonzero columns, has a vertex dominating every column. Its rational computational formulation takes those inequalities and row orders as input and returns such a vertex; this formulation is $\mathrm{PPAD}$-complete. Both statements appear explicitly in [Király and Pap, Theorem 13 and the subsequent computational formulation](https://real.mtak.hu/20824/1/dm_submission.pdf).

All hypotheses hold for $A$ and $\mu$: every column is nonzero, every mass is positive, $P$ is bounded, and the supplied rankings give the row orders. Rows corresponding to isolated types can be discarded without changing any solution condition.

Thus a stable vertex exists. Moreover, constructing $A$, retaining $\mu$, and supplying the corresponding row orders is a polynomial-time reduction to that computational Scarf formulation. By the domination equivalence, its output solves the present problem.

Proven conclusion: a stable rational vertex exists, and finding one belongs to $\mathrm{PPAD}$.

2. Stable faces and polynomial-size solutions

2.1. A face containing a stable point is entirely stable

Let $x\in P$ be stable. Define its tight rows and zero coordinates by
$$ I(x)=\left\{t:\sum_{e\ni t}x_e=\mu_t\right\}, \qquad Z(x)=\{e:x_e=0\}. $$
Consider
$$ F(x)= \left\{ y\in P: \sum_{e\ni t}y_e=\mu_t\text{ for }t\in I(x), \quad y_e=0\text{ for }e\in Z(x) \right\}. $$

This is a nonempty face of $P$. Each additional equality makes a valid inequality defining $P$ tight, and $x$ satisfies all the equalities.

Proposition. Every point of $F(x)$ is stable.

Proof. Fix $e\in E$, and choose a type $t$ dominating $e$ at $x$. Then $t\in I(x)$, and every incident $f$ with $e\succ_t f$ belongs to $Z(x)$.

Consequently, every $y\in F(x)$ satisfies
$$ \sum_{f\ni t}y_f=\mu_t, \qquad y_f=0\quad\text{whenever }f\ni t\text{ and }e\succ_t f. $$
Subtracting the vanishing terms gives
$$ \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\ f\succeq_t e}}y_f=\mu_t. $$
This provides a stability witness for $e$. The argument applies to every template. $\square$

Because there are finitely many possible pairs $(I(x),Z(x))$, the stable solution set is a finite union of faces of $P$.

2.2. Sparse vertices and an encoding bound

Proposition. Every vertex of $P$ has at most $\tau$ positive coordinates. Each coordinate has a rational representation using
$$ O\bigl(L+\tau\log(\tau+1)\bigr) $$
bits.

Proof. Let $v$ be a vertex, let
$$ S=\{e:v_e>0\},\qquad k=|S|, $$
and let $I$ be its tight capacity rows.

The matrix $A_{I,S}$ must have column rank $k$. Otherwise there would be a nonzero vector $h$, supported on $S$, with
$$ A_{I,S}h_S=0. $$
Choose $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small that
$$ \varepsilon|h_e|<v_e $$
for every $e\in S$ with $h_e\neq0$, and
$$ \varepsilon|(Ah)_t|<\mu_t-(Av)_t $$
for every slack row with $(Ah)_t\neq0$. There are finitely many such strictly positive bounds, so this is possible.

Both $v+\varepsilon h$ and $v-\varepsilon h$ are feasible: positive coordinates remain nonnegative, zero coordinates remain zero, tight rows remain tight, and slack rows remain feasible. Their midpoint is $v$, contradicting extremality. Therefore
$$ k\leq |I|\leq\tau. $$

If $k=0$, the encoding assertion is immediate. Otherwise, choose $k$ tight rows giving a nonsingular square matrix
$$ B=A_{I',S}. $$
Then
$$ Bv_S=\mu_{I'}. $$

Write the masses as ratios of binary-encoded integers, and let $D$ be the product of their positive denominators. Their total encoding length gives
$$ \log_2D\leq L. $$
Since $D\mu_{I'}$ is integral, Cramer’s rule represents every coordinate of $v_S$ with denominator
$$ D|\det B|. $$
Hadamard’s inequality, applied to the $0$–$1$ matrix $B$, gives
$$ |\det B|\leq k^{k/2}. $$
Finally, $0\leq v_e\leq1$, so its numerator in this representation is between $0$ and its denominator. The stated bit bound follows. $\square$

2.3. Conversion to a bounded witness

Given any rational stable $x$, construct $F(x)$ by exact arithmetic. Obtain a vertex of this face by successively minimizing each of the $m$ coordinates, retaining each optimum as an equality before minimizing the next coordinate.

Each optimization is over a nonempty bounded rational polytope. After all coordinates have been fixed, the remaining set is a singleton face, hence a vertex of $F(x)$. It is also a vertex of $P$: any feasible decomposition in $P$ must preserve the tight equalities defining the face.

The proposition in Section 2.1 guarantees that this vertex remains stable. Exact rational linear programming makes the procedure polynomial-time in the instance length and the supplied solution length. Intermediate optimum values have polynomial encoding length because each is attained at a vertex of a face of $P$, hence at a vertex of $P$.

This also repairs the polynomial-balance issue. Fix a polynomial $p(L)$ large enough to bound the full encoding of every vertex described above, and define
$$ R(I,x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |x|\leq p(|I|)\text{ and }x\text{ is a stable feasible rational vector for }I. $$
The relation $R$ is polynomially balanced, polynomially verifiable, and total. It therefore belongs to $\mathrm{TFNP}$.

The unrestricted rational-output task and this bounded formulation are equivalent for polynomial-time solvability: a bounded answer solves the unrestricted task, while any polynomial-time algorithm for the unrestricted task can be followed by the conversion above.

3. Hardness under normalization

The supplied unit-capacity hardness applies through a normalization reduction that also works when input masses must be dyadic.

Take a unit-capacity instance on $n\geq1$ vertices. Let $N$ be the smallest power of two with $N\geq n$. Add $N-n$ isolated types, assign every type mass $1/N$, and retain all original templates and rankings.

The masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to $1$. For every original type,
$$ \sum_{e\ni t}y_e\leq1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{e\ni t}\frac{y_e}{N}\leq\frac1N. $$
Likewise,
$$ \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\ f\succeq_t e}}y_f=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\ f\succeq_t e}}\frac{y_f}{N}=\frac1N. $$
Thus $x_e=y_e/N$ maps unit-capacity solutions to normalized solutions, and $y_e=Nx_e$ decodes any normalized solution. Isolated types impose no additional stability requirements.

Since $N<2n$, the construction and decoding have polynomial complexity. Padding does not increase maximum degree. The unit-capacity $\mathrm{PPAD}$-hardness at maximum degree three is established by [Ishizuka and Kamiyama](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11).

Proven classification. The bounded formulation is $\mathrm{PPAD}$-complete, even for equal dyadic masses and maximum degree three.

Consequently, the original search problem admits a uniform polynomial-time algorithm if and only if every $\mathrm{PPAD}$ search problem admits one.

4. The obstruction to Turing $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness

A multivalued search oracle can return different valid solutions. Here I use the standard robust interpretation: the reduction must be correct for every oracle selecting a valid answer on each queried instance. It is enough for the following argument that correctness hold for every oracle selecting answers satisfying the bound $p$ from Section 2.3.

Theorem. If a decision language $K$ has such a polynomial-time Turing reduction to this search problem, then
$$ K\in\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}. $$

Proof. Let $M$ implement the reduction, and let $r(n)$ be a polynomial bound on its running time for inputs of length $n$. Queries therefore have length at most $r(n)$, and there are at most $r(n)$ of them. As usual, the reduction queries valid instances of the search problem.

For a claimed output bit $b$, a certificate specifies the oracle responses along a computation of $M$. The verifier:

  • simulates $M$ for at most $r(n)$ steps;
  • rejects any response longer than $p$ of its query length;
  • checks feasibility and every stability equality by exact rational arithmetic;
  • checks that repeated identical queries receive identical responses;
  • accepts only if the simulation halts with output $b$.

The certificate length is at most a polynomial multiple of
$$ r(n)\,p(r(n)). $$
All verification steps are polynomial-time.

For soundness, every passing transcript extends to a valid bounded oracle. Its recorded answers are valid and consistent; on unqueried instances, choose any bounded stable answer, whose existence was proved above. Correctness of $M$ for every such oracle forces the recorded output bit to equal the true membership bit.

For completeness, choose a bounded valid answer for every instance. Running $M$ with this oracle gives a passing transcript for the correct output bit.

Thus transcripts ending in $1$ certify $K$, while transcripts ending in $0$ certify its complement. $\square$

In particular, an every-valid-answer Turing reduction deciding $\mathrm{SAT}$ would imply
$$ \mathrm{UNSAT}\in\mathrm{NP}, $$
and hence
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

This is a conditional obstruction, not an impossibility proof. A specially selected optimum or canonical solution would constitute a different oracle specification.

5. A parameterized algorithm in the support size

For each type, write
$$ E(t)=\{e\in E:t\in e\}, \qquad d_t=|E(t)|. $$
Enumerate choices
$$ q_t\in E(t)\cup\{\bot\}. $$

A choice vector passes the coverage test if
$$ \text{for every }e\in E,\text{ some }t\in e \text{ satisfies }q_t\neq\bot\text{ and }q_t\succeq_t e. $$

For each vector passing this test, solve the rational linear feasibility system
$$ x\geq0,\qquad Ax\leq\mu, $$
together with
$$ \sum_{f\ni t}x_f=\mu_t \quad\text{whenever }q_t\neq\bot, $$
and
$$ x_f=0 \quad\text{whenever }q_t\neq\bot,\ f\ni t,\text{ and }q_t\succ_t f. $$

Soundness. For a template $e$, choose a covering type $t$. Its row is saturated. Every positive incident template ranks at least as highly as $q_t$, and therefore at least as highly as $e$. Hence $t$ dominates $e$.

Completeness. Take a stable solution. At each saturated type, choose its worst-ranked positive incident template as $q_t$; such a template exists because $\mu_t>0$. At an unsaturated type, choose $\bot$.

If $t$ witnesses stability of $e$, all positive templates incident to $t$ rank at least as highly as $e$. Thus $q_t\succeq_t e$, proving coverage. The original solution satisfies every equality and zero restriction in the corresponding linear system.

There are
$$ \prod_{t\in T}(d_t+1)\leq(m+1)^\tau $$
choices, each requiring polynomial-time rational linear feasibility. Therefore the running time is
$$ (m+1)^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

Under the usual set-family interpretation
$$ E\subseteq 2^T\setminus\{\varnothing\}, $$
templates are distinct, so
$$ m+1\leq2^\tau. $$
The running time consequently improves to
$$ 2^{\tau^2}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
This is fixed-parameter tractability in $\tau$. It also gives polynomial running time for any class of instances satisfying $\tau^2=O(\log L)$.

If repeated labelled templates with identical participating subsets are allowed instead, the proof still establishes the preceding $(m+1)^\tau$ bound; the bound using $2^{\tau^2}$ requires distinct subsets.

6. Polynomial-time solvability for acyclic preferences

Define a directed graph $D$ whose vertices are the templates. Include an arc
$$ e\longrightarrow f $$
whenever some shared type ranks $e\succ_t f$.

Theorem. If $D$ is acyclic, a stable solution can be found in polynomial time for arbitrary input masses.

Proof. Choose a topological ordering
$$ e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_m. $$
At each type, this ordering places every preferred incident template before every less preferred incident template.

Initialize residual capacities $r_t=\mu_t$. For $i=1,\ldots,m$, set
$$ x_{e_i}=\min_{t\in e_i}r_t, $$
and subtract $x_{e_i}$ from every residual $r_t$ with $t\in e_i$.

Residuals remain nonnegative, proving feasibility.

Fix $e_i$, and choose a type $t\in e_i$ attaining the minimum. Immediately after processing $e_i$, its residual is zero:
$$ \sum_{\substack{j\leq i\\t\in e_j}}x_{e_j}=\mu_t. $$
Every later template containing $t$ receives zero mass, because its minimum residual includes $r_t=0$. Every earlier incident template is preferred by $t$ to $e_i$, by the topological ordering. Therefore
$$ \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\succeq_t e_i}}x_f=\mu_t. $$
So $t$ witnesses stability of $e_i$.

The comparison graph has polynomial size and can be topologically sorted in polynomial time. All residuals and assigned masses are integer multiples of $1/D$, where $D$ is the denominator product from Section 2.2. They lie between $0$ and $1$, so their representations have $O(L)$ bits. The arithmetic therefore also takes polynomial time. $\square$

Why unrestricted greedy processing fails

Let
$$ T=\{a,b,c\},\qquad \mu_a=\mu_b=\mu_c=\frac13, $$
and take
$$ e_1=\{a,c\},\qquad e_2=\{a,b\},\qquad e_3=\{b,c\}, $$
with rankings
$$ e_1\succ_a e_2,\qquad e_2\succ_b e_3,\qquad e_3\succ_c e_1. $$

The unique stable solution is
$$ x_{e_1}=x_{e_2}=x_{e_3}=\frac16. $$

To prove uniqueness, suppose first that $x_{e_1}=1/3$. Feasibility forces the other two coordinates to vanish. Then $e_3$ has no stability witness: its qualifying sum at $b$ is $x_{e_2}+x_{e_3}=0$, and at $c$ it is $x_{e_3}=0$. The cyclically corresponding argument excludes $x_{e_2}=1/3$ and $x_{e_3}=1/3$.

Thus no template can be witnessed at the type ranking it first, since that would require its own mass to equal $1/3$. Stability must instead use the other type in each template, giving
$$ x_{e_1}+x_{e_3}=\frac13,\qquad x_{e_1}+x_{e_2}=\frac13,\qquad x_{e_2}+x_{e_3}=\frac13. $$
These equations imply that all three coordinates equal $1/6$, and that vector satisfies all requirements.

Greedy processing in any order assigns $1/3$ to the first template and zero to the remaining two. The preceding argument proves that this output is unstable. Hence the acyclicity hypothesis is essential to the algorithm proved here.
