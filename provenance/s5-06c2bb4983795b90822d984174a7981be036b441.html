Partial resolution: polynomial-time inversion for a globally polynomial density

The full polynomial-time versus NP-hard classification remains unresolved. I prove the following results:

  • For every promised piecewise-polynomial density and every binary-rational $q>1$, the scalar outcome $F_g(\mu)$ is computable in polynomial time.
  • If the density is one polynomial on all of $[0,1]$, the entire requested approximation problem is polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary binary-rational $q>1$. There is no restriction on the numerical magnitude of $q$.
  • For arbitrary piecewise-polynomial densities, the entire approximation problem is polynomial-time solvable for each fixed integer $q>1$.
  • The exact empirical rules satisfy the required axioms and expected-loss optimality. I give a direct proof, including a characterization of the competing rules.
  • The finite-support moment and population-minimizer algorithms have polynomial bounds involving the actual support size $\tau$. Zero moments are handled explicitly.

The second result advances beyond the cases established in NOTES: it obtains an inverse precision bound from a nonnegative polynomial numerator, rather than from a positive lower bound on the derivative.

1. Definitions and input conventions

Write
$$ s=q-1>0, $$
and define
$$ P(x)=\int_0^x p(t)\,dt,\qquad Q(x)=\int_x^1p(t)\,dt, $$
$$ M_-(x)=\int_0^x(x-t)^s p(t)\,dt,\qquad M_+(x)=\int_x^1(t-x)^s p(t)\,dt. $$

Let $e$ be the binary encoding length of $\varepsilon$, and let $b$ be the encoding length of a rational evaluation query $u$. The bounds below are polynomial in $m,d,L,e$, and additionally in $b$ when evaluating the constructed program. If $L$ includes $\varepsilon$, the separate parameter $e$ can be absorbed into $L$.

Full topological support means that every nonempty open subinterval of $[0,1]$ has positive $\mu$-mass. Consequently, for $0<x<1$, all four quantities $P(x),Q(x),M_-(x),M_+(x)$ are positive. Atomlessness gives $P(x)+Q(x)=1$, and therefore
$$ G(x)= \frac{M_-(x)Q(x)} {M_-(x)Q(x)+M_+(x)P(x)}. $$

The moment functions are continuous: the kernels $(x-t)_+^s$ and $(t-x)_+^s$ are continuous in $x$, bounded by $1$, and dominated convergence applies.

Moreover,
$$ A(x)\le x^s,\qquad B(x)\le(1-x)^s. $$
As $x$ tends to $0$, $B(x)$ tends to the positive number $\int_0^1t^s p(t)\,dt$. Thus $G(x)$ tends to $0$. The reflected argument gives limit $1$ at the other endpoint. The promised strict increase therefore makes the extended $G$ a continuous increasing bijection of $[0,1]$.

For interior points, put
$$ R(x)=\frac{M_-(x)}{M_+(x)},\qquad J(x)=\log\frac{G(x)}{1-G(x)}. $$
Then
$$ J(x)=\log R(x)+\log Q(x)-\log P(x). $$

The exact inverse $g=G^{-1}$ defines the voting rules. Numerical approximations are used only to answer the computational requests.

2. The population outcome and its conditioning

Proposition 1

There is a unique $x_*\in(0,1)$ satisfying
$$ M_-(x_*)=M_+(x_*). $$
It equals $F_g(\mu)$ and uniquely minimizes
$$ \mathcal L(x)=\int_0^1|t-x|^q p(t)\,dt. $$

Proof

For $0<x<1$, invertibility gives
$$ g(Q(x))\ge x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q(x)\ge G(x). $$
Multiplication by the positive denominator of $G$ gives
$$ Q(x)\ge G(x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(x)Q(x)\bigl(M_+(x)-M_-(x)\bigr)\ge0. $$
Since $P(x)Q(x)>0$, feasibility is equivalent to $M_-(x)\le M_+(x)$.

The function $M_-$ is strictly increasing on the interior. Indeed, for $x<y$, its kernel strictly increases on $(0,x)$, a set of positive mass, and the newly included contribution is nonnegative. Similarly, $M_+$ is strictly decreasing.

At $0$, the left moment is zero and the right moment is positive. At $1$, the signs are reversed. Continuity and strict monotonicity give exactly one crossing $x_*$. The feasible set in the supremum definition has supremum $x_*$; its endpoint conventions agree with this conclusion.

For fixed $q>1$, the derivative of $|t-x|^q$ exists even at $t=x$ and has absolute value at most $q$. Differentiation under the integral is therefore justified and yields
$$ \mathcal L'(x)=q\bigl(M_-(x)-M_+(x)\bigr). $$
This derivative is negative before $x_*$ and positive after it. Hence $x_*$ is the unique minimizer. $\square$

Proposition 2

For $0<x<y<1$,
$$ \log R(y)-\log R(x)\ge4s(y-x). $$
In particular,
$$ |x-x_*|\le\frac{|\log R(x)|}{4s}. $$

Proof

For $0\le t<x$,
$$ \frac{y-t}{x-t}\ge\frac yx. $$
Retaining the original integration region in the left moment gives
$$ M_-(y)\ge\left(\frac yx\right)^s M_-(x). $$

For $y<t\le1$,
$$ \frac{t-y}{t-x}\le\frac{1-y}{1-x}. $$
The right moment at $y$ integrates over a subset of the region for the moment at $x$, so
$$ M_+(y)\le \left(\frac{1-y}{1-x}\right)^s M_+(x). $$

Taking logarithms and subtracting,
$$ \log R(y)-\log R(x) \ge s\log\frac{y(1-x)}{x(1-y)} = s\int_x^y\frac{dt}{t(1-t)} \ge4s(y-x). $$
Apply this inequality between $x$ and $x_*$, in the appropriate order, using $\log R(x_*)=0$. $\square$

This quantitative bound holds without using the strict-increase promise for $G$. That promise is used to identify the population outcome with the moment crossing.

3. Polynomial-time logarithmic evaluation and scalar approximation

Lemma 3

Given an interior rational $x$ of encoding length $h$ and an integer precision parameter $k\ge1$, one can approximate $\log R(x)$ and $J(x)$ to absolute error $2^{-k}$ in time polynomial in $m,d,L,h,k$.

Proof

The exponent $s$ may have exponentially large numerical value. We therefore normalize before evaluating moments:
$$ U(x)=\frac{M_-(x)}{x^s},\qquad V(x)=\frac{M_+(x)}{(1-x)^s}. $$
Both lie in $(0,1]$.

Positive lower bounds. Let the first partition interval have right endpoint $a_1>0$, and set
$$ \delta=\min\left\{a_1,\frac{x}{2q}\right\}. $$
For $0\le t\le\delta$,
$$ \left(1-\frac tx\right)^s \ge \left(1-\frac1{2q}\right)^s \ge\frac12. $$
For $s\ge1$, Bernoulli's inequality gives the last bound because $s/(2q)<1/2$. For $0<s<1$, use $(1-z)^s\ge1-z$.

Thus
$$ U(x)\ge\frac12\int_0^\delta p(t)\,dt. $$
The integral is positive by full support. It is an exactly computable rational of polynomial encoding length: it integrates one rational polynomial of degree at most $d$ at rational endpoints of polynomial encoding length.

The reflected construction near $1$ gives the same conclusion for $V$. Hence we can compute an integer $B$, polynomially bounded in $m,d,L,h$, such that
$$ 2^{-B}\le U(x),V(x)\le1. $$

Finite normalized formulas. On a clipped partition piece $[a,b]\subseteq[0,x]$, expand
$$ p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^d c_j(x-t)^j. $$
Its contribution to $U(x)$ is
$$ \sum_{j=0}^d \frac{c_j}{s+j+1} \left[ (x-a)^{j+1}\left(\frac{x-a}{x}\right)^s - (x-b)^{j+1}\left(\frac{x-b}{x}\right)^s \right]. $$
This follows by integrating $(x-t)^{s+j}$ and dividing by $x^s$. Expanding the right pieces in powers of $t-x$ gives the analogous formula for $V$.

All shifted coefficients and rational multipliers have polynomial encoding length. There are polynomially many signed terms. Their total absolute multiplier magnitude is at most $2^H$ for an effectively computable polynomial bound $H$.

Thus, even with cancellation, approximating every remaining power sufficiently accurately computes $U,V$ to absolute error
$$ 2^{-(B+k+O(1))}. $$
The required extra precision is polynomial: it accounts for $H$, the number of terms, and the lower bound $2^{-B}$.

Elementary numerical primitives. For a positive rational $r$ of encoding length $H_r$, $\log r$ is computable to absolute error $2^{-K}$ in time polynomial in $H_r,K$. To see this, write $r=2^\ell z$ with $1\le z<2$, and use
$$ \log z= 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac1{2j+1} \left(\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right)^{2j+1}. $$
The fraction inside the powers is at most $1/3$, so the remainder decreases geometrically. The same series computes $\log2$. Since $|\ell|$ is bounded by the rational's encoding length, only polynomially many terms and precision bits are needed.

For rational $a\in[0,1]$, the value $a^s$ is likewise computable to absolute error $2^{-K}$ in polynomial time in its input lengths and $K$. The cases $a=0,1$ are exact. Otherwise compute $v=s\log a$. Multiplication by $s$ requires only its encoding length in extra logarithm precision bits. If a certified upper bound for $v$ is sufficiently negative, returning zero meets the required error. Otherwise $v$ lies in an interval of magnitude $O(K)$. Divide it by a suitable power of two to put it in $[-1,0]$, compute the exponential by its Taylor series, and repeatedly square. There are $O(\log(K+1))$ squarings; polynomially many extra accuracy bits control their accumulated error.

These series computations use polynomially many rational operations on numbers of polynomial encoding length. Elementary integer arithmetic therefore suffices for polynomial bit complexity.

The lower bounds on $U,V$ allow their logarithms to be recovered from the absolute moment approximations: on arguments at least $2^{-B-1}$, the derivative of the logarithm is at most $2^{B+1}$.

Finally,
$$ \log R(x) = s\bigl(\log x-\log(1-x)\bigr) +\log U(x)-\log V(x). $$
The factor $s$ costs only polynomially many additional precision bits.

Both $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are positive rationals computable exactly with polynomial encoding length. Therefore
$$ J(x)=\log R(x)+\log Q(x)-\log P(x) $$
has the same complexity bound. $\square$

Theorem 4

For every allowed density input and exponent, a rational approximation to $F_g(\mu)$ with error at most $\varepsilon$ is computable in polynomial time in $m,d,L,e$.

Proof

Maintain a bracket containing $x_*$, initially $[0,1]$. At its midpoint $x$, approximate $\log R(x)$ with certified error at most
$$ \eta=\min\left\{\frac18,\frac{s\varepsilon}{8}\right\}. $$
The needed precision has polynomial length because $s$ and $\varepsilon$ have binary encodings.

If the approximation is greater than $2\eta$, the true sign is positive; replace the right endpoint by $x$. If it is less than $-2\eta$, replace the left endpoint by $x$.

Otherwise,
$$ |\log R(x)|\le3\eta, $$
and Proposition 2 gives
$$ |x-x_*| \le\frac{3\eta}{4s} \le\frac{3\varepsilon}{32}. $$
Return $x$ in that case.

If numerical stopping does not occur, stop once the bracket width is at most $2\varepsilon$ and return its midpoint. The number of iterations is $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$, and all midpoint encoding lengths and oracle precisions are polynomially bounded. Lemma 3 completes the complexity argument. $\square$

4. New result: arbitrary rational exponents for one polynomial piece

Theorem 5

Suppose
$$ p(t)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i t^i $$
on all of $[0,1]$, with the promises in the task.

Then the requested scalar approximation and inverse evaluation program can be constructed in time polynomial in $d,L,e$. The program has polynomial size and answers a rational query of encoding length $b$ in time polynomial in $d,L,e,b$.

This holds for arbitrary binary-rational $q>1$, including numerically very large exponents.

4.1 Exact rational factors of the moments

Define
$$ \beta_i(s)=\int_0^1(1-v)^s v^i\,dv. $$
Then
$$ \beta_0(s)=\frac1{s+1},\qquad \beta_i(s)=\frac{i}{s+i+1}\beta_{i-1}(s), $$
and consequently
$$ \beta_i(s)= \frac{i!}{(s+1)(s+2)\cdots(s+i+1)}. $$

For completeness, the recurrence follows by integrating the derivative of $v^i(1-v)^{s+1}$. Its boundary values vanish when $i\ge1$, and hence
$$ 0= i\int_0^1v^{i-1}(1-v)^{s+1}\,dv -(s+1)\beta_i(s). $$
The first integral is $\beta_{i-1}(s)-\beta_i(s)$, which gives the recurrence.

Write the reflected polynomial as
$$ p(1-t)=\sum_{i=0}^d\widehat a_i t^i. $$
Define rational polynomials
$$ H_-(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i\beta_i(s)x^i,\qquad T_-(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d\frac{a_i}{i+1}x^i, $$
$$ H_+(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d\widehat a_i\beta_i(s)(1-x)^i,\qquad T_+(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d\frac{\widehat a_i}{i+1}(1-x)^i. $$

Substitution $t=xv$ in the left moment, and reflection followed by the same substitution in the right moment, give
$$ M_-(x)=x^{s+1}H_-(x),\qquad M_+(x)=(1-x)^{s+1}H_+(x), $$
$$ P(x)=xT_-(x),\qquad Q(x)=(1-x)T_+(x). $$
All four polynomial factors are positive on $(0,1)$, by the positivity of the corresponding integrals.

Their degrees are at most $d$. Their coefficient encoding lengths are polynomial in $d,L$: the products defining $\beta_i(s)$ have only $i+1$ rational factors, and the reflected coefficients are obtained by a binomial expansion.

It follows that
$$ J(x)= s\log\frac{x}{1-x} +\log H_-(x)-\log H_+(x) +\log T_+(x)-\log T_-(x). $$
At rational interior points, all arguments of these logarithms are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length. Thus this formula provides a polynomial-time logarithmic evaluator directly, without evaluating fractional powers.

4.2 A nonnegative polynomial controls the increase

Differentiate:
$$ J'(x)= \frac{s}{x(1-x)} +\frac{H_-'(x)}{H_-(x)} -\frac{H_+'(x)}{H_+(x)} +\frac{T_+'(x)}{T_+(x)} -\frac{T_-'(x)}{T_-(x)}. $$

Set
$$ D(x)=x(1-x)H_-(x)H_+(x)T_-(x)T_+(x). $$
It is positive on $(0,1)$. The numerator $N$ in $J'=N/D$ is
$$ \begin{aligned} N(x)={}&sH_-H_+T_-T_+\\ &+x(1-x)\bigl( H_-'H_+T_-T_+ -H_+'H_-T_-T_+\\ &\hspace{38mm} +T_+'H_-H_+T_- -T_-'H_-H_+T_+ \bigr). \end{aligned} $$
Every factor here is evaluated at $x$.

This is a rational polynomial of degree at most $4d+1$, with coefficient encoding length polynomial in $d,L$.

Because $G$ is strictly increasing and the logarithm of the odds is strictly increasing, $J$ is strictly increasing. A differentiable increasing function has nonnegative derivative: each difference quotient used to define the derivative is nonnegative. Therefore
$$ N(x)\ge0\qquad(0<x<1). $$
Also, $N$ is not identically zero. Otherwise $J'=0$ everywhere on $(0,1)$, so the mean value theorem would make $J$ constant, contradicting strict increase.

For a rational polynomial $H$, let $\|H\|_1$ denote the sum of the absolute values of its monomial coefficients. Compute
$$ W= \prod_{H\in\{H_-,H_+,T_-,T_+\}} \max\{1,\|H\|_1\}. $$
For $x\in[0,1]$, $|H(x)|\le\|H\|_1$, so
$$ 0<D(x)\le W\qquad(0<x<1). $$

Now take rational $0<x<y<1$. Define the exactly computable rational
$$ \gamma(x,y)=\frac1W\int_x^yN(t)\,dt. $$
It is strictly positive. Indeed, a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish throughout an open interval; since $N$ is continuous and nonnegative, its integral over every nonempty interior interval is positive.

Consequently,
$$ J(y)-J(x) = \int_x^y\frac{N(t)}{D(t)}\,dt \ge \frac1W\int_x^yN(t)\,dt = \gamma(x,y)>0. $$

Crucially, if $x,y$ have encoding length at most $h$, then $\gamma(x,y)$ has encoding length polynomial in $d,L,h$. Integrating a rational polynomial of degree at most $4d+1$ at rational endpoints uses polynomially many rational operations, whose numerator and denominator lengths remain polynomial. Thus a positive $\gamma(x,y)$ cannot require more than polynomially many bits to obtain a smaller positive power-of-two precision.

This remains valid when $J'$ vanishes at some points. No positive pointwise lower bound on $J'$ is needed.

4.3 The inverse algorithm and its precision bound

Queries $u=0,1$ are answered exactly. For $0<u<1$, put
$$ T=\log\frac{u}{1-u},\qquad H_u(x)=J(x)-T. $$
The unique zero of $H_u$ is $g(u)$.

Maintain a bracket $[a,b]$ containing that zero, initially $[0,1]$. While its width exceeds $2\varepsilon$, take
$$ x=a+\frac{b-a}{3},\qquad y=a+\frac{2(b-a)}{3}. $$
Compute $\gamma=\gamma(x,y)>0$ exactly. Approximate both $H_u(x)$ and $H_u(y)$ with certified absolute error at most $\gamma/8$.

At least one approximation has absolute value greater than $\gamma/4$. To prove this, suppose both had absolute value at most $\gamma/4$. Their difference would be at most $\gamma/2$, and the two error bounds would imply
$$ H_u(y)-H_u(x)\le\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{4} =\frac{3\gamma}{4}. $$
But
$$ H_u(y)-H_u(x)=J(y)-J(x)\ge\gamma, $$
a contradiction.

An approximation of magnitude greater than $\gamma/4$ certifies the true sign, because its error is at most $\gamma/8$. At such a test point $z$, replace $a$ by $z$ if the sign is negative, and replace $b$ by $z$ if it is positive. The zero remains bracketed, and the width contracts by a factor at most $2/3$.

When the width is at most $2\varepsilon$, return the midpoint.

After $O(e)$ iterations, the bracket endpoints have encoding length $O(e)$. The encoding length of every corresponding $\gamma$ is polynomial in $d,L,e$. Hence the required logarithm precision is polynomial in those parameters. Evaluation of $T$ additionally costs polynomial time in $b$.

The program stores the rational input polynomials, their derived factors, $N,W$, the precision input, and fixed rational-arithmetic and logarithm routines. Both construction time and program size are polynomial.

For every rational query $u$, the returned midpoint differs from $g(u)$ by at most $\varepsilon$. Therefore the program satisfies the required uniform bound
$$ \sup_{u\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]} |\widetilde g(u)-g(u)|\le\varepsilon. $$
Together with Theorem 4, this proves Theorem 5. $\square$

5. Further polynomial-time cases

Fixed integer exponents

If $q>1$ is an integer, then $s=q-1$ is an integer, and both moments at rational $x$ are exactly rational. Polynomial integration or the formulas in Lemma 3 compute them with bit complexity polynomial in
$$ m,d,q,L,h. $$

For an interior query $u$, the sign of $G(x)-u$ is exactly the sign of
$$ (1-u)M_-(x)Q(x)-uM_+(x)P(x). $$
Thus exact-comparison bisection locates $g(u)$ to error $\varepsilon$ without a derivative lower bound.

The evaluation time is polynomial in $m,d,q,L,e,b$. In particular, this solves the entire requested approximation problem for every fixed integer $q$, and when the numerical value of $q$ is polynomially bounded by the other input parameters. This particular exact-arithmetic argument alone does not give a polynomial bound for unrestricted binary-encoded integers.

Uniform density

For $p(t)=1$, direct integration gives
$$ A(x)=\frac{x^s}{s+1},\qquad B(x)=\frac{(1-x)^s}{s+1}, $$
and hence
$$ G(x)=\frac{x^s}{x^s+(1-x)^s},\qquad J(x)=s\log\frac{x}{1-x}. $$
Therefore
$$ g(u)= \frac1{1+\bigl((1-u)/u\bigr)^{1/s}} \qquad(0<u<1), $$
and $F_g(\mu)=1/2$.

Theorem 5 includes this case for arbitrary binary-rational $q$. For $q=2$, it gives $g(u)=u$, consistent with the published quadratic uniform-prior optimality of the linear median. [Jennings–Laraki–Puppe–Varloot](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-023-02010-x).

6. Exact empirical axioms and expected-loss optimality

This section establishes the exact-rule requirements for every density in the task, independently of the approximation algorithms.

6.1 Median representation and the axioms

Fix $n\ge1$, and define
$$ c_k=g(k/n)\qquad(0\le k\le n). $$
Then
$$ 0=c_0<c_1<\cdots<c_n=1. $$

For $n\ge2$, consider the median $M$ of the $n$ reports and the $n-1$ fixed values
$$ c_1,\ldots,c_{n-1}. $$
For $y>0$, let $k$ be the number of reports weakly above $y$. The median is weakly above $y$ precisely when at least $n$ of the $2n-1$ entries are weakly above $y$.

For $1\le k\le n-1$, this requires at least $n-k$ phantoms weakly above $y$, which is equivalent to $c_k\ge y$. When $k=0$, both conditions fail; when $k=n$, both hold. Thus
$$ M\ge y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(k/n)\ge y. $$
At $y=0$, both conditions hold. Taking the supremum over $y$ proves $M=\phi_g$, including ties. For $n=1$, $\phi_g(r_1)=r_1$.

The median is anonymous. If every report equals $a$, the $n$ identical reports force the median to equal $a$, so the rule is unanimous and voter-sovereign.

For strategy-proofness, fix one voter and sort the other reports and all phantoms as
$$ z_1\le\cdots\le z_{2n-2}. $$
As a function of that voter's report $r$, the outcome is
$$ \min\{z_n,\max\{z_{n-1},r\}\}. $$
It is projection onto the fixed interval $[z_{n-1},z_n]$. A peak inside the interval is attained by truthful reporting. A peak below the interval obtains the least attainable alternative, which every strict single-peaked preference with that peak prefers to any higher attainable alternative. The argument for a peak above the interval is symmetric. The identity rule for $n=1$ is also strategy-proof.

6.2 Characterization of the competing rules

I use the usual interpretation that strategy-proofness must hold for every strict single-peaked preference having the reported true peak, and voter sovereignty means that the rule is onto $[0,1]$.

Let $f$ be any anonymous, voter-sovereign, strategy-proof rule on peak reports. Fix all reports except one, and write $h(r)$ for the resulting one-variable function. Put
$$ \alpha=h(0),\qquad \beta=h(1). $$

A voter with peak $0$ strictly prefers smaller alternatives, so strategy-proofness implies
$$ \alpha\le h(r)\qquad\text{for every }r. $$
A voter with peak $1$ strictly prefers larger alternatives, giving
$$ h(r)\le\beta\qquad\text{for every }r. $$

If $\alpha\le r\le\beta$, then $h(r)=r$. Otherwise, suppose for example that $h(r)>r$. Reporting $0$ gives $\alpha\le r$. If $\alpha=r$, this attains the peak. If $\alpha<r<h(r)$, there is a strict single-peaked preference with peak $r$ that prefers $\alpha$ to $h(r)$: choose a sufficiently steep decline to the right of the peak relative to the decline to its left. Either possibility contradicts strategy-proofness. The case $h(r)<r$ uses report $1$ and the reflected preference construction.

The terms for $k=0,n$ are fixed by $c_0=0,c_n=1$. Each interior phantom occurs in just one remaining term.
