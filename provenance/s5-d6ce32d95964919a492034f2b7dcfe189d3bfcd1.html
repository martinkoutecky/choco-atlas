The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(N>0\) and a support of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational locations \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^2\), and rational per-person resources \(r_t>0\), each type has mass \(N\mu_t\). For any group mass vector \(q\in[0,N\mu]\), let \(R(q)=\sum_t q_t r_t\), let \(D(q)\) be the greatest distance between locations with positive mass (zero for a single location), and set \(U(q)=R(q)/(1+D(q))\), with \(U(0)=-\infty\). Compute a partition of the population that recursively selects, from the remaining mass, a group maximizing \(U\), breaking ties by largest total mass, and satisfies the SAE condition: for every source group \(j\), destination group \(k\ne j\) or empty destination, and real submass vector \(0\le s\le q^{(j)}\), either \(U(q^{(j)})\ge U(q^{(k)}+s)\) or \(U(q^{(k)})>U(q^{(k)}+s)\). Is such a partition computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the binary input length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The input also gives binary rationals \(N>0\), \(x_t\in\mathbb Q^2\), and \(r_t>0\); type \(t\) has divisible population mass \(b_t=N\mu_t\).
For a nonzero real group vector \(0\leq q\leq b\), define \(R(q)=\sum_tq_tr_t\), \(D(q)=\max_{t,u:q_tq_u>0}\|x_t-x_u\|_2\), and \(U(q)=R(q)/(1+D(q))\); set \(U(0)=-\infty\). Every member receives the group's utility, independently of other groups.
A partition is a finite ordered list of nonzero rational mass vectors \(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(m)}\) summing to \(b\). At step \(j\), with remaining mass \(a=b-\sum_{i<j}q^{(i)}\), the selected vector must maximize \(U(q)\) over all real \(0\leq q\leq a\), breaking ties by largest \(\sum_tq_t\); remaining ties are arbitrary.
Require strong acceptance equilibrium (SAE): for every source \(j\), every destination \(k\ne j\) or empty destination \(q^{(k)}=0\), and every real \(0\leq s\leq q^{(j)}\), either \(U(q^{(j)})\geq U(q^{(k)}+s)\) or \(U(q^{(k)})>U(q^{(k)}+s)\).
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length and \(m\) the number of output groups; express running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is computing such a recursively maximizing SAE partition possible in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite societies of \(n\) agents, recursive maximum-utility selection with largest-cardinality tie-breaking produces an SAE, and a running-time bound of \(\mathcal O(n^3)\) is stated for diameter-based power functions. [Discrete source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.12729)

The recursive maximization problem is polynomial-time solvable, including maximization over real mass vectors and the specified mass tie-break. However, the literal SAE requirement introduces a separate feasibility question: some inputs admit no required partition, and on other inputs feasibility depends on the remaining tie choices.

Below I prove the polynomial-time result, give an explicit example demonstrating that dependence, and settle literal feasibility for collinear locations. The complexity of literal feasibility for general planar locations remains open here.

1. Saturation reduces real optimization to subsets

For a nonzero remaining vector $a$, let $q$ be a nonzero feasible vector, and put
$$ S=\{t:q_t>0\}. $$
Define its saturation by
$$ \widehat q_t= \begin{cases} a_t,&t\in S,\\ 0,&t\notin S. \end{cases} $$
The supports of $q$ and $\widehat q$ coincide, so their diameters coincide. If $q\ne\widehat q$, positivity of the $r_t$ gives
$$ R(\widehat q)>R(q), \qquad U(\widehat q)>U(q). $$
Consequently, every maximizer saturates its support. A maximizer exists because only finitely many nonempty saturated supports are possible.

Starting from $a=b$, a selected type is therefore exhausted immediately. Every recursive partition has disjoint type supports, every output coordinate belongs to $\{0,b_t\}$, and
$$ m\leq\tau. $$
In particular, its mass vectors are rational.

For a remaining type set $A$, define
$$ w_t=\mu_t r_t,\qquad h_t=\mu_t, $$
and, for nonempty $S\subseteq A$,
$$ w(S)=\sum_{t\in S}w_t,\qquad h(S)=\sum_{t\in S}h_t,\qquad F(S)=\frac{w(S)}{1+D(S)}. $$
The saturated group with support $S$ has utility $NF(S)$ and mass $Nh(S)$. Thus selection is exactly lexicographic maximization of
$$ \bigl(F(S),h(S)\bigr). $$

2. Polynomial-time planar selection

A geometric lemma

Let $p,q\in\mathbb R^2$ have distance $d>0$. Each closed half of the lens
$$ B(p,d)\cap B(q,d), $$
separated by the line through $p,q$, has diameter at most $d$.

To prove this, translate, rotate, and scale to $p=(0,0)$, $q=(1,0)$. A lens point $(x,y)$ satisfies
$$ 0\leq x\leq1, \qquad y^2+\max\{x^2,(1-x)^2\}\leq1. $$
For two upper-half points $(x,y),(u,v)$, interchange them if necessary so that $y\geq v\geq0$. Then
$$ (x-u)^2+(y-v)^2 \leq \max\{x^2,(1-x)^2\}+y^2 \leq1. $$
Here the first inequality follows from $u\in[0,1]$ and $0\leq y-v\leq y$. Reflection proves the lower-half statement, and rescaling proves the lemma.

Selection with a prescribed diameter pair

Fix remaining types $p,q$ at distinct locations, and let
$$ d=\|x_p-x_q\|_2. $$
Define
$$ T_{pq} = \{t\in A: \|x_t-x_p\|_2\leq d,\ \|x_t-x_q\|_2\leq d\}. $$
Every support containing $p,q$ and having diameter $d$ is contained in $T_{pq}$.

Partition $T_{pq}$ into two sides $T^+,T^-$ of the line through $x_p,x_q$, assigning boundary points to $T^+$. Construct a conflict graph whose edges are precisely the pairs at distance greater than $d$. The lemma shows that all edges run between $T^+$ and $T^-$, so this graph is bipartite.

A subset of $T_{pq}$ has diameter at most $d$ exactly when it is independent in the conflict graph. Both $p$ and $q$ are isolated vertices, because every lens point is within distance $d$ of both.

We now compute an independent set maximizing resource weight and then population weight. Choose integer common denominators $D_w,D_h$, and set
$$ W_t=D_ww_t,\qquad H_t=D_hh_t,\qquad H_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}H_t. $$
Give vertex $t$ the positive integer weight
$$ C_t=(H_{\mathrm{tot}}+1)W_t+H_t. $$
A strict difference in integer resource weight is at least $1$, while every population-weight difference has absolute value at most $H_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Hence maximizing total $C$-weight implements exactly the required ordering of $w(S)$ and $h(S)$.

For completeness, this weighted independent-set problem reduces to minimum cut. Use a source $\alpha$, a sink $\omega$, and arcs

  • $\alpha\to u$ of capacity $C_u$ for $u\in T^+$;
  • $v\to\omega$ of capacity $C_v$ for $v\in T^-$;
  • $u\to v$ of capacity $K$ for each conflict edge, where

$$ K=1+\sum_{t\in T_{pq}}C_t. $$

A cut of capacity less than $K$ exists, so a minimum cut crosses no conflict arc. If $Z$ is its source side, then
$$ X=(T^+\setminus Z)\cup(T^-\cap Z) $$
is a vertex cover, and the cut capacity is $\sum_{t\in X}C_t$. Conversely, every vertex cover $X$ gives a cut of that capacity by taking source side
$$ \{\alpha\}\cup(T^+\setminus X)\cup(T^-\cap X). $$
Thus the complement of a minimum-weight vertex cover is a maximum-weight independent set.

Since $p,q$ are isolated and have positive weights, every such independent set includes them. Its actual diameter is therefore exactly $d$. It is the best support with this prescribed diameter pair.

Complete candidate enumeration

At each recursive step, enumerate:

  • For every remaining location, all remaining types at that location.
  • For every remaining pair at distinct locations, the support computed above.

Compare candidates by $F(S)$, then $h(S)$.

To prove optimality, consider any nonempty support $S$.

If $D(S)=0$, adding every remaining type at its common location preserves diameter and strictly increases resources whenever a type is added. Its location candidate therefore dominates it.

If $D(S)>0$, choose $p,q\in S$ attaining its diameter. Then $S$ is an independent set in the corresponding conflict graph. The computed candidate has the same diameter and greater resource weight, or equal resource weight and at least as much population weight. It therefore dominates $S$.

Every support is dominated by an enumerated candidate. By saturation, every real feasible mass vector is also dominated by one. This proves exact recursive maximization with the required tie-break.

3. Exact arithmetic and running time

All geometric decisions use rational arithmetic: compare squared distances for lens membership and conflicts, and determinant signs for sides of lines.

Utility comparisons also admit exact rational computation. For positive rationals $A,B$ and nonnegative rationals $\delta,\varepsilon$, the comparison
$$ \frac{A}{1+\sqrt\delta} \quad\text{versus}\quad \frac{B}{1+\sqrt\varepsilon} $$
has the sign of
$$ A-B+A\sqrt\varepsilon-B\sqrt\delta. $$
Set
$$ c=A-B,\qquad u=A^2\varepsilon,\qquad v=B^2\delta, \qquad t=c+\sqrt u. $$
If $t<0$, then $t-\sqrt v<0$. If $t=0$, equality holds precisely when $v=0$. If $t>0$, multiplication by the positive quantity $t+\sqrt v$ shows that the desired sign is the sign of
$$ t^2-v=c^2+u-v+2c\sqrt u. $$
These computations require only signs of expressions $a+b\sqrt z$. When their two nonzero terms have opposite signs, compare $a^2$ and $b^2z$, retaining the original signs to determine the result. This handles equality without approximate arithmetic.

The common denominators can be products of the input denominators: for $w_t=\mu_tr_t$, each relevant input denominator need appear only once. Their bit lengths are $\mathcal O(L)$. Subset sums, encoded capacities, squared distances, and the constant number of operations in each utility comparison also have $\mathcal O(L)$ bits. Reduction of rational representations prevents denominator growth from repeated computations.

Each flow network has $\mathcal O(\tau)$ vertices and $\mathcal O(\tau^2)$ arcs. The Edmonds–Karp theorem gives $\mathcal O(VE^2)$ elementary operations for finite networks with nonnegative capacities; our integer-capacity networks satisfy these hypotheses. See the [MIT network-flow notes](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-046j-design-and-analysis-of-algorithms-spring-2015/bdafe05f4f3c20cabfd08d2fbd099f63_MIT6_046JS15_lec14A.pdf).

Thus one network requires $\mathcal O(\tau^5)$ operations. There are $\mathcal O(\tau^2)$ networks per selection. Using schoolbook arithmetic gives the conservative bound
$$ \boxed{\mathcal O(m\tau^7L^2)} $$
bit operations for the recursive partition. Geometry, candidate comparisons, and output writing fit within this bound. Since $m\leq\tau$, it is polynomial in $\tau,L$.

4. What recursive maximization proves about SAE

First consider the explicitly amended condition that restricts deviations to $s\ne0$.

Let $q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(m)}$ be any recursively maximizing partition with the prescribed tie-break. Fix a source $j$ and a nonzero real vector
$$ 0\leq s\leq q^{(j)}. $$

For a later destination $k>j$, the vector $q^{(k)}+s$ was feasible at step $j$, because its source and destination supports were both still present. Therefore
$$ U(q^{(j)})\geq U(q^{(k)}+s). $$

For an empty destination, $s$ was feasible at step $j$, so
$$ U(q^{(j)})\geq U(s). $$

For an earlier destination $k<j$, the vector $q^{(k)}+s$ was feasible at step $k$. Hence
$$ U(q^{(k)})\geq U(q^{(k)}+s). $$
Equality would make $q^{(k)}+s$ another maximizer with strictly greater mass, contradicting the tie-break. Consequently,
$$ U(q^{(k)})>U(q^{(k)}+s). $$
These cases prove SAE for every nonzero real deviation.

For the literal condition, take $s=0$ and a nonempty destination $k$. Its second alternative is false, so it requires
$$ U(q^{(j)})\geq U(q^{(k)}). $$
Applying this to both orders of every pair forces equality of all group utilities. Conversely, if all group utilities are equal, zero deviations satisfy the first alternative; nonzero deviations satisfy the preceding proof. Zero deviations to an empty destination also satisfy the first alternative because $U(0)=-\infty$.

We have therefore proved:
$$ \boxed{ \text{A recursively maximizing partition satisfies literal SAE} \iff \text{all its group utilities are equal}. } $$

5. Literal nonexistence and dependence on tie choices

Some valid inputs have no required partition

Take
$$ \tau=2,\quad N=4,\quad (\mu_1,\mu_2)=\left(\frac14,\frac34\right),\quad r_1=r_2=1, $$
with locations $(0,0)$ and $(10,0)$.

The three saturated supports have utilities
$$ 1,\qquad 3,\qquad \frac4{11}. $$
Thus recursive maximization uniquely chooses the second type, then the first. Their utilities are unequal, so literal SAE fails.

This proves that universal construction under the literal specification is impossible.

A planar example where one tied choice succeeds and another fails

Take eight types, with
$$ N=8,\qquad \mu_t=\frac18,\qquad r_t=\frac34. $$
Thus every type has population mass $1$ and resource contribution $3/4$. Place them at
$$ (2i,0),\ (2i,1),\qquad i\in\{0,1,2,3\}. $$
Call each pair at a common horizontal coordinate a column.

Set
$$ u=\frac3{1+\sqrt5}. $$
I claim that the only initial maximizing supports are the four types in two adjacent columns.

Consider a saturated support according to the number of columns it meets.

  • In one column, either the singleton or the full column has utility $3/4$.
  • In two adjacent columns, a support of at most three types has diameter at least $2$, so its utility is at most $3/4$. The full four-type rectangle has diameter $\sqrt5$ and utility $u$.
  • In two nonadjacent columns, its resource sum is at most $3$ and diameter at least $4$, giving utility at most $3/5$.
  • In three columns, its resource sum is at most $9/2$ and diameter at least $4$, giving utility at most $9/10$.
  • In four columns, its resource sum is at most $6$ and diameter at least $6$, giving utility at most $6/7$.

Each displayed upper bound is strictly below $u$. Indeed,
$$ u>\frac34 \iff \sqrt5<3, $$
$$ u>\frac9{10} \iff \sqrt5<\frac73, $$
and
$$ u>\frac67 \iff \sqrt5<\frac52. $$
All three inequalities follow by squaring positive quantities. The bound $3/5$ is smaller than $3/4$.

Therefore the three adjacent-column rectangles are exactly the maximizers, all with mass $4$.

Choosing the left rectangle leaves the right rectangle. Both groups have utility $u$, so this is a recursively maximizing literal SAE partition.

Choosing the middle rectangle leaves the outer columns at horizontal coordinates $0$ and $6$. Each full remaining column has utility $3/4$ and mass $2$. Any support meeting both columns has diameter at least $6$ and resources at most $3$, so its utility is at most $3/7$. The next selection therefore takes one full column, and the last takes the other.

This recursive partition has utilities
$$ u,\quad \frac34,\quad \frac34, $$
so it fails literal SAE.

Thus an arbitrary greedy run followed by an equality check does not decide planar literal feasibility: a failed run can coexist with a successful run.

6. Literal feasibility is polynomial-time decidable for collinear locations

Here is a nontrivial special case of the exact condition.

Theorem. Suppose all locations are collinear. If a recursively maximizing literal SAE partition exists, every recursively maximizing partition with the prescribed tie-break satisfies literal SAE. Existence can consequently be decided, and a partition produced when one exists, in
$$ \mathcal O(m\tau^2L^2) $$
bit operations.

Proof. Suppose such a partition exists, with supports $P_1,\ldots,P_\ell$. By Section 4, their actual utilities all equal some $v>0$. The first selection shows that $v$ is the maximum utility over the full society.

Write
$$ \rho_t=b_tr_t,\qquad \rho(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\rho_t. $$
For every nonempty support $S$,
$$ \rho(S)\leq v(1+D(S)), $$
and equality holds for every $P_i$.

Each $P_i$ contains every input type located in its closed interval hull. Otherwise adding a missing type in that interval would preserve its diameter and strictly increase its utility above $v$. Consequently, the interval hulls of the $P_i$ are pairwise disjoint: if two intervals intersect, an endpoint type of one belongs to the other interval, contradicting the preceding containment property and disjointness of supports.

Consider two such intervals, with diameters $d_i,d_j$ and intervening gap $g$. At the earlier selection of these two groups, both groups were still available. Their union has resource sum
$$ v(2+d_i+d_j) $$
and diameter
$$ d_i+g+d_j. $$
Maximality at that step forces
$$ \frac{v(2+d_i+d_j)}{1+d_i+g+d_j}\leq v, $$
hence $g\geq1$. Equality $g=1$ would make their union another maximizer with strictly greater mass than the selected group, violating the tie-break. Thus
$$ g>1. $$

Now consider an arbitrary recursive run. Inductively suppose its remaining society consists of whole groups from the partition $P_1,\ldots,P_\ell$. The remaining maximum utility is $v$: no feasible support exceeds the original maximum, and each remaining whole $P_i$ attains $v$.

Let its next selected support be $Q$. Suppose $Q$ meets $h\geq2$ of the remaining $P_i$, ordered along the line. Let its nonempty intersections be $S_1,\ldots,S_h$, with diameters $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_h$. Let $\gamma_i$ be the gap between the interval hulls of $S_i$ and $S_{i+1}$. The established separation gives $\gamma_i>1$.

Since each $S_i$ is feasible in the original society,
$$ \rho(S_i)\leq v(1+\delta_i). $$
Distances along a line give
$$ D(Q)=\sum_{i=1}^h\delta_i+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}\gamma_i. $$
Therefore
$$ \rho(Q) \leq v\left(h+\sum_{i=1}^h\delta_i\right) < v\left(1+D(Q)\right). $$
This contradicts $U(Q)=v$.

Thus $Q$ is contained in a single remaining $P_i$. If the containment were proper, that whole $P_i$ would have the same utility and strictly greater mass, again contradicting the tie-break. Hence $Q=P_i$.

Induction proves that every recursive run removes whole groups of the successful partition and ends with equal utilities. This proves the asserted independence of tie choices.

To implement selection, sort the distinct locations along the line and combine coincident types into location buckets. Every maximizing support is an interval of the remaining buckets: adding an omitted bucket between its endpoints would preserve diameter and increase utility.

There are $\mathcal O(\tau^2)$ intervals per step. Prefix sums give their resource and population weights, and endpoint distances give their diameters. Exact comparisons use Section 3.

Sorting can use rational scalar products with a rational direction vector between two distinct input locations. If all locations coincide, one group is selected. These operations, distance preprocessing, and interval enumeration yield $\mathcal O(m\tau^2L^2)$ bit operations.

Run this algorithm with any remaining tie choices. Equal output utilities certify literal SAE. Unequal utilities certify nonexistence by the independence result just proved. $\square$

7. The remaining planar complexity question

To make literal feasibility a total decision problem, define:

Given the input society, does some recursively maximizing partition, respecting largest-mass tie-breaking, satisfy literal SAE?

This decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

A certificate is an ordered partition of the type set. Saturation ensures that every possible valid output has this form, so the certificate has polynomial length. At each certified step, run the planar selection algorithm and compare the certified support’s utility and mass with the computed optimum. Equality of both quantities verifies the required selection rule, while permitting arbitrary remaining ties.

Finally, check that all certified group utilities are equal. Section 4 proves that this is exactly literal SAE for a verified recursive partition, including all real deviations. The verification takes
$$ \mathcal O(m\tau^7L^2) $$
bit operations.

The results established here are therefore:

  • Exact recursive maximization is polynomial-time solvable.
  • The explicitly amended condition with $s\ne0$ always admits an SAE, constructible in $\mathcal O(m\tau^7L^2)$ bit operations.
  • Literal SAE can be infeasible, and planar feasibility can depend on tie choices.
  • Literal feasibility is polynomial-time decidable for collinear locations and belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ for general planar locations.

No argument above establishes either a polynomial-time decision algorithm or NP-hardness for literal feasibility in the general planar case.
