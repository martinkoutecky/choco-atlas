The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), given a finite conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\), rational supplies \(b_v\geq 0\), and rational common additive values \(w_v\), with \(\rho=\max_{v:b_v>0}\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}b_u\leq 1/(3+\varepsilon)\), can one output in time polynomial in \(|V|,|E|\), and the binary input length \(L\) a rational finite-support distribution \(x_k\) over integral bundles \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{V}\)? Require \(\sum_k x_k=1\), \(\sum_k x_k k_v=b_v\) for every \(v\), and \(\operatorname{supp}(k)\) independent in \(G\) for every bundle in the support. Also require that for every ordered pair \(k,l\) in the support there are integer removal vectors \(p\leq k\), \(q\leq l\), with \(\|p\|_1,\|q\|_1\leq 1\) and \(\sum_v w_v(k_v-p_v)\geq\sum_v w_v(l_v-q_v)\)?

Result

The search problem is deterministically polynomial-time solvable for each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

The construction uses a polynomial number of rounded discrete instances. Their allocations can be mixed because every resulting bundle satisfies the same ordered-block conditions, which imply EF[1,1] between bundles from different instances.

1. The strong-coloring theorem used

We use the following established algorithmic theorem.

Strong-coloring theorem. For every fixed \(\eta>0\), given a finite graph \(H\), a positive integer \(D\), and a partition of its vertices into blocks of size exactly \(D\), if \(D\geq(3+\eta)\Delta(H)\), a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm partitions the vertices into \(D\) independent sets, each containing exactly one vertex from every block.

This is [Harris’s Theorem 1.6(1)](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.08065#S1.SS3), also stated as [Haviv’s Theorem 6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01059v1#S6).

Fix a rational constant \(\eta\) with \(0<\eta<\varepsilon\). Choose a positive integer constant \(C\), depending only on \(\varepsilon\), sufficiently large that

\(\displaystyle \frac{1}{3+\varepsilon}+\frac1C\leq\frac{1}{3+\eta}.\)

Such a constant exists because \(\eta<\varepsilon\).

2. Removing large supplies by a common bundle

Let \(S=\{v:b_v>0\}\), and let \(I\) be the isolated vertices of the induced graph \(G[S]\). Define an integer vector \(h\) by

\(\displaystyle h_v=\begin{cases}\lfloor b_v\rfloor,&v\in I,\\0,&v\notin I,\end{cases}\qquad a_v=b_v-h_v.\)

Then \(0\leq a_v<1\) for every vertex. For \(v\in I\), this follows from the definition. If \(v\in S\setminus I\), choose a neighbor \(u\in S\). The promise, applied at \(u\), gives

\(\displaystyle b_v\leq\sum_{z\in N_G(u)}b_z\leq\rho\leq\frac{1}{3+\varepsilon}<1.\)

Vertices outside \(S\) have \(a_v=0\). Also, for every \(v\in S\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{u\in N_G(v)}a_u\leq\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}b_u\leq\rho.\)

The support of \(h\) consists of isolated vertices of \(G[S]\). Consequently, adding \(h\) to any feasible bundle supported in \(S\) preserves feasibility.

It also preserves EF[1,1] when added to every bundle: the value \(w\cdot h\) cancels from each comparison, and any removals available before adding \(h\) remain available afterward.

It therefore suffices to construct the required allocation for supplies \(a\). If \(a=0\), return the single bundle \(h\) with mass \(1\). Henceforth assume \(a\neq0\).

3. Fixed ordered blocks and their fairness property

Separate vertices with positive residual supply into goods, with \(w_v\geq0\), and chores, with \(w_v<0\).

For goods, sort vertices by nonincreasing \(w_v\). Place consecutive intervals of lengths \(a_v\) in that order, and cut the resulting interval at integer positions. If the last block has mass less than \(1\), append a dummy interval of value zero to complete it.

Do the same separately for chores, ordering them by nonincreasing cost \(-w_v\), and again padding the last block with a zero-valued dummy if necessary. A sign class with zero total supply contributes no blocks.

Every block now has mass exactly \(1\). A real vertex interval has length less than \(1\), so it intersects at most two blocks. Call these intersections pieces. Include each dummy interval as a piece. Write:

  • \(n\) for the total number of pieces;
  • \(\alpha_i>0\) for the mass of piece \(i\);
  • \(v(i)\) for its original vertex, when it is real;
  • \(\mathcal B\) for the partition of pieces into blocks.

Thus

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in B}\alpha_i=1\quad(B\in\mathcal B),\qquad \sum_{i:v(i)=v}\alpha_i=a_v.\)

There are at most \(2m+2\) pieces and at most \(m+2\) blocks.

We next prove a fairness property that depends only on these fixed blocks.

Lemma. Any two bundles obtained by choosing one piece from each block, interpreting real choices as one copy of their vertex and dummy choices as nothing, satisfy the ordered EF[1,1] comparison.

Proof. Let the goods blocks number \(s\), and the chores blocks number \(t\). For such a bundle \(k\), let \(g_j(k)\) denote the value selected from goods block \(j\), and let \(c_j(k)\) denote the nonnegative cost selected from chores block \(j\). Dummy selections contribute zero. Define

\(\displaystyle P(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}g_j(k),\qquad T(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}c_j(k).\)

Because of the block ordering, for any two bundles \(k,l\),

\(\displaystyle g_j(k)\geq g_{j+1}(l)\quad(1\leq j<s).\)

Since the final goods value is nonnegative, summing gives

\(\displaystyle P(k)\geq P(l)-g_1(l).\)

When there are no goods blocks, interpret \(g_1(l)=0\); the inequality still holds.

Similarly, the ordering of chore costs gives

\(\displaystyle c_j(l)\geq c_{j+1}(k)\quad(1\leq j<t),\)

and hence

\(\displaystyle T(k)-c_1(k)\leq T(l).\)

When there are no chores blocks, interpret \(c_1(k)=0\).

Remove from \(k\) its selection from the first chores block, if that selection is real. Remove from \(l\) its selection from the first goods block, if real. These removals use at most one copy on each side. The resulting values satisfy

\(\displaystyle P(k)-T(k)+c_1(k)\geq P(l)-T(l)-g_1(l).\)

This is the required EF[1,1] comparison. A dummy selection requires an empty removal. \(\square\)

In particular, bundles constructed in different subsequent rounding steps will still satisfy EF[1,1] with one another.

4. An exact decomposition into grid roundings

Set \(D=Cn\). We construct at most \(n+1\) integer vectors \(z^{(r)}\), with positive rational coefficients \(\lambda_r\), such that

\(\displaystyle \sum_r\lambda_r=1,\qquad \sum_r\lambda_r z_i^{(r)}=D\alpha_i,\)

and, for every rounding \(r\),

\(\displaystyle z_i^{(r)}\in\{\lfloor D\alpha_i\rfloor,\lceil D\alpha_i\rceil\},\qquad \sum_{i\in B}z_i^{(r)}=D\quad(B\in\mathcal B).\)

Here is an explicit deterministic construction.

Within each block \(B\), enumerate its pieces as \(i_1,\ldots,i_q\), and define

\(\displaystyle A_0=0,\qquad A_j=D\sum_{h=1}^{j}\alpha_{i_h}.\)

Thus \(A_q=D\). For \(0\leq\theta<1\), put

\(\displaystyle z_{i_j}(\theta)=\lfloor A_j+\theta\rfloor-\lfloor A_{j-1}+\theta\rfloor.\)

For any real \(y\) and nonnegative real \(x\), the integer \(\lfloor y+x\rfloor-\lfloor y\rfloor\) is either \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) or \(\lceil x\rceil\). Therefore each coordinate has the claimed rounding property. Telescoping gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in B}z_i(\theta)=\lfloor D+\theta\rfloor-\lfloor\theta\rfloor=D.\)

Furthermore, for any nonnegative real \(A\),

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1\lfloor A+\theta\rfloor\,d\theta=A.\)

Indeed, writing \(A=q+f\), with \(q\) integer and \(0\leq f<1\), the integrand equals \(q\) on an interval of length \(1-f\) and \(q+1\) on the remaining interval. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1z_i(\theta)\,d\theta=D\alpha_i.\)

Each cumulative sum \(A_j\) contributes at most one breakpoint in \((0,1)\), namely \(1-\{A_j\}\) when its fractional part is nonzero. Across all blocks there are at most \(n\) breakpoints. Sort the distinct breakpoints together with \(0,1\). On each intervening open interval, the entire vector \(z(\theta)\) is constant.

For each interval \(r\), take its rational midpoint to compute \(z^{(r)}\), and let \(\lambda_r\) be its length. The integral identity proves all the required decomposition equations. No random sampling is used.

5. Polynomial-size graphs for the rounded instances

For each rounding \(r\), construct a graph \(H_r\) as follows.

Replace piece \(i\) by \(z_i^{(r)}\) distinct copies. For copies of real pieces \(i,j\), add an edge exactly when their original vertices \(v(i),v(j)\) are adjacent in \(G\). Dummy copies are isolated. Copies of the same original vertex have no edges between them, as required by the allowance of repeated copies.

Group copies by their fixed blocks. Each block contains exactly \(D\) copies.

Consider a copy of a real piece labeled \(v\). Its degree is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i:\,v(i)\in N_G(v)}z_i^{(r)}.\)

Using \(z_i^{(r)}\leq D\alpha_i+1\), the residual neighborhood bound, and the fact that there are at most \(n\) pieces, we obtain

\(\displaystyle \deg_{H_r}(v\text{-copy})\leq D\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}a_u+n\leq D\rho+n.\)

Dummy copies have degree zero. Hence

\(\displaystyle \frac{\Delta(H_r)}D\leq\rho+\frac nD=\rho+\frac1C\leq\frac1{3+\eta}.\)

All hypotheses of the strong-coloring theorem are now satisfied. Apply it to obtain \(D\) independent color classes, each selecting exactly one copy from every block. If \(H_r\) has no edges, such a coloring can instead be produced directly by assigning all \(D\) colors within each block.

For color \(c\), let \(k^{(r,c)}\) be the corresponding bundle of original vertex counts, discarding dummy copies.

Each bundle is feasible: if two adjacent original vertices both occurred, their selected copies would be adjacent in \(H_r\). Also,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{c=1}^{D}k_v^{(r,c)}=\sum_{i:v(i)=v}z_i^{(r)}.\)

6. Combining the allocations

Give each bundle \(h+k^{(r,c)}\) the provisional mass \(\lambda_r/D\). Merge identical bundles by adding their masses.

All masses are positive rationals, and their total is

\(\displaystyle \sum_r\sum_{c=1}^{D}\frac{\lambda_r}{D}=\sum_r\lambda_r=1.\)

For every original vertex \(v\), the expected residual count is exactly

\(\displaystyle \sum_r\sum_{c=1}^{D}\frac{\lambda_r}{D}k_v^{(r,c)}=\frac1D\sum_{i:v(i)=v}\sum_r\lambda_r z_i^{(r)}=\sum_{i:v(i)=v}\alpha_i=a_v.\)

Adding \(h\) therefore gives expected count \(h_v+a_v=b_v\).

Every residual bundle chooses one piece from every fixed block. The lemma in Section 3 thus proves EF[1,1] for every ordered pair of residual bundles, including pairs originating in different rounded graphs. Adding the common bundle \(h\) preserves that property and feasibility. Merging identical bundles changes neither property.

The resulting allocation satisfies every requirement of the task.

7. Running time and binary encoding

For fixed \(\varepsilon\), the constants \(C,\eta\) are fixed. We have \(n\leq2m+2\) and \(D=Cn\). Each rounded graph has

\(\displaystyle |\mathcal B|D\leq(m+2)D=O_\varepsilon((m+1)^2)\)

vertices, and there are at most \(n+1=O(m+1)\) such graphs. Their explicit construction and all strong-coloring calls therefore take polynomial time.

The provisional output contains at most

\(\displaystyle (n+1)D=O_\varepsilon((m+1)^2)\)

bundles.

To check bit complexity, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the input supplies. Its binary length is at most \(L\). Residual supplies, piece masses, cumulative sums, and rounding breakpoints have denominators dividing \(Q\). Interval lengths \(\lambda_r\) also have denominators dividing \(Q\). Thus provisional and merged output masses have denominators dividing \(DQ\), and polynomial binary length.

A residual bundle contains at most one item per block, so its coordinates are polynomially bounded in \(m\). The possibly large coordinates of \(h\) are encoded in binary and have polynomial length in \(L\). Sorting the rational values and performing all rational arithmetic also take polynomial time.

This proves deterministic polynomial-time solvability in \(m,|E|,\tau,L\), with \(\tau=1\), for each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).
