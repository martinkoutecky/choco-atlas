The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(M,E)\), rational per-agent resource capacities \(q_g\), and finitely many complete agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), where each bundle contains at most one copy of each resource label and must be an independent set of \(G\), decide whether there exist masses \(\pi_{t,S}\ge0\) over types \(t\) and independent-set bundles \(S\) such that \(\sum_S\pi_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}\pi_{t,S}\le q_g\), every pair of bundles in the positive support satisfies support-wise EF1 under \(v_t\), and every resource with positive residual supply is blocked by every positive-support bundle. The search version must output such a family \(\pi\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. Fix \(r\ge4\). An instance has a conflict graph \(G=(M,E)\), one type of positive rational mass \(\mu\), rational item values \(v_g\), and \( ho_g=1/r\) for every \(g\). Let \(\mathcal I(G)\) be the independent vertex sets and \(v(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_g\).
A fractional allocation is \(x_S\ge0\) for \(S\in\mathcal I(G)\), with \(\sum_Sx_S=\mu\) and \(\sum_{S i g}x_S\le1/r\). It is maximal if every \(g\) with unused supply and every \(S\) with \(x_S>0\) satisfy \(\exists h\in S:\{g,h\}\in E\); it is EF1 if \(x_Sx_R>0\) and \(R\ne\varnothing\) imply \(\exists g\in R:v(S)\ge v(R\setminus\{g\})\).

Target. For every fixed \(r\ge4\), is deciding whether a maximal EF1 fractional allocation exists in P or NP-hard?

Known results. For \(r\) discrete agents with identical additive valuations, deciding whether a maximal EF1 allocation exists is NP-hard for every fixed \(r\ge4\).

Status

The target classification is not resolved here. However, the exact fractional problem is in \(\mathrm{NP}\), maximality admits a useful exact characterization, and the known discrete hardness reduction provably does not transfer: its constant NO-gadget—and indeed every instance produced by its standard Independent Set lifting—has a maximal EF1 fractional allocation.

Throughout the principal arguments, values may be arbitrary rationals unless nonnegativity is stated explicitly.

1. Saturation forced by maximality

For an allocation \(x\), write

\[ y_g=\sum_{S\ni g}x_S. \]

Lemma 1

In every maximal fractional allocation, each item satisfies \(y_g\in\{0,1/r\}\).

Proof

Suppose \(0<y_g<1/r\). Since \(y_g>0\), there is a supported bundle \(S\) with \(g\in S\). Because \(g\) has unused supply, maximality requires an \(h\in S\) adjacent to \(g\). But \(g,h\in S\), contradicting that \(S\) is independent. Thus a used item must be saturated. ∎

Consequently, if \(M\neq\varnothing\) and \(\mu<1/r\), no maximal allocation exists. Indeed, a nonempty supported bundle would contain an item whose usage is at most \(\mu<1/r\), contradicting Lemma 1. If only the empty bundle were supported, no unused item could be blocked.

2. Exact support characterization

Let \(U=\{g:y_g=1/r\}\) be the set of used items and put \(p_S=x_S/\mu\). Then \((p_S)\) is a probability distribution.

Proposition 2

A maximal EF1 fractional allocation exists if and only if there are a set \(U\subseteq M\), a finite family \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal I(G[U])\), and a probability distribution \(p\) on \(\mathcal F\) such that:

  • For every \(g\in U\),

\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal F:g\in S}p_S=\frac{1}{r\mu}. \]

  • Every \(S\in\mathcal F\) dominates every vertex outside \(U\): for every \(g\in M\setminus U\), some \(h\in S\) satisfies \(\{g,h\}\in E\).
  • The sets in \(\mathcal F\) are pairwise EF1-compatible.

Proof

Given a maximal allocation, Lemma 1 gives the stated marginals after division by \(\mu\). Maximality for unused items gives condition 2, and EF1 gives condition 3.

Conversely, put \(x_S=\mu p_S\). Condition 1 saturates every item of \(U\), while items outside \(U\) are unused. Condition 2 gives maximality, and condition 3 gives EF1. ∎

For additive values, define for nonempty \(R\)

\[ d(R)=\min_{g\in R}v(R\setminus\{g\})=v(R)-\max_{g\in R}v_g. \]

A family \(\mathcal F\) is pairwise EF1-compatible precisely when

\[ \min_{S\in\mathcal F}v(S)\ \ge\ \max_{\substack{R\in\mathcal F\\R\neq\varnothing}}d(R). \]

Thus all supported bundles must cross one common value threshold after deletion of their largest-valued item.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Theorem 3

For every fixed \(r\), the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Proof

Suppose a solution exists, and retain its used set \(U\) and supported family \(\mathcal F\). The weights solve the rational linear system

\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal F}x_S=\mu,\qquad \sum_{\substack{S\in\mathcal F\\g\in S}}x_S=\frac1r \quad(g\in U),\qquad x_S\ge0. \]

There are at most \(|U|+1\) equality constraints. Hence this system has a basic feasible solution with at most \(|U|+1\le |M|+1\) positive variables. Replacing the original weights by this basic solution preserves:

  • the used set and its saturated marginals;
  • independence of supported bundles;
  • domination of every unused item;
  • pairwise EF1, because the new support is a subfamily of the old support.

The coefficient matrix is a \(0\)-\(1\) matrix. By Cramer’s rule and the Hadamard determinant bound, the basic weights have encoding length polynomial in the input length.

A certificate therefore consists of \(U\), at most \(|M|+1\) explicitly listed bundles, and their rational weights. Independence, supply equalities, domination, and every pairwise EF1 inequality can all be checked in polynomial time. ∎

Therefore, any eventual NP-hardness result would yield NP-completeness.

4. The discrete constant NO-gadget becomes a fractional YES-instance

Consider \(G=K_{3,r-1}\), with bipartition \(L\cup R\), where \(|L|=3\) and \(|R|=r-1\). Give every item of \(L\) value \(2\) and every item of \(R\) value \(3\). This is the additive obstruction used in the discrete hardness framework of [Igarashi, Manurangsi, and Yoneda](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14149).

Proposition 4

For \(r\ge4\), the discrete \(r\)-agent instance has no maximal EF1 allocation.

Proof

Every maximal discrete allocation is complete. Otherwise an unallocated item \(g\) would need a distinct neighbor in each of the \(r\) disjoint agent bundles, although \(\deg(g)\le r-1\).

Let \(z\) be the number of agents receiving no item from \(R\). Since bundles cannot mix the two sides, all three items of \(L\) are allocated among these \(z\) agents.

If \(z=1\), that agent receives all of \(L\), of value \(6\). Each other agent receives one item of \(R\), of value \(3\), and still envies the \(L\)-bundle after one left item is removed, since \(4>3\).

If \(z\ge2\), some such agent receives at most one left item and hence value at most \(2\). The \(r-1\) right items are distributed among at most \(r-2\) agents, so some right bundle contains at least two items. After deleting one item, that bundle still has value at least \(3>2\). Thus EF1 fails. ∎

Proposition 5

The corresponding mass instance with \(\mu=1\) has a maximal EF1 fractional allocation.

Proof

Define the nonzero weights as follows:

  • for every two-element \(P\subseteq L\), set \(x_P=1/(2r)\);
  • for every \(b\in R\), set \(x_{\{b\}}=(r-2)/(r(r-1))\);
  • for every two-element \(Q\subseteq R\), set

\[ x_Q=\frac{1}{r(r-1)(r-2)}. \]

Their total mass is

\[ \frac{3}{2r}+\frac{r-2}{r}+\frac{1}{2r}=1. \]

Each left item belongs to two supported left pairs, so its usage is \(1/r\). For a right item, the singleton contribution is \((r-2)/(r(r-1))\), while its \(r-2\) incident right pairs contribute \(1/(r(r-1))\). Its total usage is therefore \(1/r\). Hence every item is saturated, so maximality is vacuous.

Supported bundles have the following values and deletion residuals:

  • a left pair has value \(4\) and residual \(2\);
  • a right singleton has value \(3\) and residual \(0\);
  • a right pair has value \(6\) and residual \(3\).

The minimum supported value is \(3\), and the maximum deletion residual is also \(3\). Proposition 2 therefore implies EF1. ∎

This proves that no general purification theorem can extract a discrete maximal EF1 allocation from a fractional one.

5. The standard Independent Set lifting always becomes fractional YES

The published discrete reduction adds, for each \(i\in[r]\), sets \(X_i=\{x_{i,w}:w\in V(H)\}\) and \(Y_i=\{y_{i,w}:w\in V(H)\}\). Edges inside \(X_i\) copy \(H\); each \(x_{i,w}\) is adjacent to \(y_{i,w}\); and different groups \(X_i\cup Y_i\) and \(X_j\cup Y_j\) are completely joined. Items in \(Y_i\) have value \(0\).

Proposition 6

Starting from the gadget in Proposition 5, every instance produced by this lifting has a maximal EF1 fractional allocation, independently of \(H\) and the Independent Set threshold.

Proof

Let \((x_B)\) be the allocation from Proposition 5. For every supported base bundle \(B\) and every \(i\in[r]\), support

\[ B\cup Y_i \]

with weight \(x_B/r\).

The total mass is \(1\). Every base item retains usage \(1/r\). Each \(y_{i,w}\) occurs in all bundles indexed by \(i\), whose total weight is \(1/r\). Thus every base item and every \(Y\)-item is saturated. All \(X\)-items are unused.

For an unused \(x_{i,w}\), a bundle indexed by \(i\) contains its neighbor \(y_{i,w}\). A bundle indexed by \(j\neq i\) contains \(Y_j\), every element of which is adjacent to \(x_{i,w}\) through the complete join. Hence maximality holds.

The \(Y\)-items have value \(0\), so the value and deletion residual of \(B\cup Y_i\) are no worse than those of \(B\). Removing the same base item that witnesses EF1 between \(B\) and \(B'\) witnesses EF1 between \(B\cup Y_i\) and \(B'\cup Y_j\). ∎

Thus the known discrete reduction does not merely contain a small gap: under the fractional formulation, all of its outputs are YES-instances.

6. Polynomially decidable boundary cases

Assume here that all \(v_g\ge0\).

  • If \(M\neq\varnothing\) and \(\mu<1/r\), the answer is NO by Lemma 1.
  • If \(\mu=1/r\), the answer is always YES. Choose any maximal independent set \(I\) and set \(x_I=1/r\). Every unused item is adjacent to \(I\), and the single supported bundle is EF1 with itself because deleting a nonnegative-valued item cannot increase its value.
  • If \(\mu\ge |M|/r\), the answer is always YES. Give every singleton bundle weight \(1/r\) and give the empty bundle the remaining mass \(\mu-|M|/r\). Every item is saturated, and empty bundles and singleton bundles are pairwise EF1.
  • More generally, if all values are \(0\) and \(r\mu=k\) is a positive integer, the answer is always YES. Choose an inclusion-maximal induced \(k\)-colorable subgraph \(G[U]\), with color classes \(C_1,\ldots,C_k\), and give each \(C_i\) weight \(1/r\). If \(g\notin U\), then \(g\) has a neighbor in every \(C_i\); otherwise \(g\) could be added using that color, contradicting maximality of \(U\). EF1 is automatic because all values vanish.

7. Remaining obstruction

The exact classification for the intermediate, nonzero-valued regime remains open in this analysis. Proposition 2 isolates the unresolved core: one must either find, in polynomial time, a used set \(U\), a common EF1 threshold, and a dominating configuration family whose convex hull contains \((1/(r\mu))\mathbf 1_U\), or prove that this compatible-support selection problem is NP-hard.

Any hardness proof must contain a gadget sound against fractional mixing; the known discrete obstruction and its Independent Set amplification cannot serve this purpose.

Verification report

Overall assessment

The prover explicitly states that it does not resolve the requested P-versus-NP-hard classification. This is therefore a partial result, not a solution to the target.

The substantive intermediate results are largely correct:

  • maximality forces every used item to be saturated;
  • maximal allocations admit the stated support characterization;
  • the problem belongs to \( \mathrm{NP} \);
  • the \( K_{3,r-1} \) discrete obstruction becomes a fractional YES-instance;
  • conditional on the stated description of the published lifting, the proposed allocation makes every lifted instance a fractional YES-instance;
  • the listed boundary cases are correct under the stated nonnegativity assumption.

No central mathematical assertion was found to be false. There are, however, several minor points requiring clarification.

Step-by-step audit

1. Lemma 1 and the small-mass consequence

The proof of Lemma 1 is correct. If \( 0<y_g<1/r \), some supported independent set contains \( g \). Maximality applied to the still-unsaturated item \( g \) requires a neighbor of \( g \) in that same set, contradicting independence.

The deduction for \( M\neq\varnothing \) and \( \mu<1/r \) is also correct:

  • every item in a nonempty supported set has usage at most the total mass \( \mu<1/r \), contradicting Lemma 1;
  • if only the empty set is supported, an unsaturated item cannot have a neighbor in that supported bundle.

This uses the given assumption that \( \mu>0 \).

2. Proposition 2

The forward implication is correct:

  • Lemma 1 implies that every used item has marginal \( 1/r \);
  • division by \( \mu \) gives probability marginals \( 1/(r\mu) \);
  • maximality supplies domination of unused items by every supported set;
  • EF1 supplies compatibility for every ordered pair of supported sets.

The converse is also correct. Conditions 1 and 2 respectively establish saturation on \( U \) and maximality for every item outside \( U \), while condition 3 establishes EF1.

A minor definitional clarification is needed: \( \mathcal F \) should be the positive support of \( p \), or the statement should explicitly require \( p_S>0 \) for every \( S\in\mathcal F \). Ordinary usage of “a probability distribution on \( \mathcal F \)” can allow zero-probability members, whereas maximality and EF1 only constrain positive-support bundles. This does not invalidate the existential equivalence because zero-probability members can simply be removed.

The threshold reformulation is correct. For nonempty \( R \),

\( d(R)=\min_{g\in R}v(R\setminus\{g\})=v(R)-\max_{g\in R}v_g \),

including when values are negative. Requiring \( v(S)\ge d(R) \) for every ordered supported pair is equivalent to the displayed minimum-versus-maximum inequality.

There is one omitted edge convention: if \( \mathcal F=\{\varnothing\} \), then the maximum over nonempty \( R\in\mathcal F \) is over an empty index set. This can occur when \( M=\varnothing \). The threshold statement therefore needs either a separate empty-ground-set case or an explicit convention.

3. Theorem 3: membership in \( \mathrm{NP} \)

The sparsification argument is sound. The feasible weight polytope is bounded by \( \sum_Sx_S=\mu \) and \( x_S\ge0 \), and it has at most \( |U|+1 \) equality constraints. A basic feasible solution consequently has at most \( |U|+1 \) positive coordinates.

Passing to a subfamily of the original support preserves:

  • all marginal equalities;
  • independence;
  • domination of unused items;
  • EF1 compatibility.

The polynomial bit-length assertion is standard and valid: the constraint coefficients are \( 0 \)-\( 1 \), the rational right-hand side has input-size encoding, and determinants of square submatrices have polynomial bit length by Hadamard’s bound.

The proposed certificate is polynomial-sized and all stated checks are polynomial-time. The conclusion that an NP-hardness proof would yield NP-completeness is correct under the standard polynomial-time many-one meaning of NP-hardness.

4. Proposition 4

The discrete argument is correct.

If an item were unallocated, maximality would require a neighbor in each of the \( r \) pairwise disjoint agent bundles. These neighbors would be distinct, contradicting maximum degree at most \( r-1 \). Hence every item is allocated.

The two cases for the number \( z \) of agents receiving no right-side item are exhaustive:

  • If \( z=1 \), each other agent receives exactly one right-side item of value \( 3 \), while deleting any one item from the unique left bundle leaves value \( 4 \).
  • If \( z\ge2 \), some left-side agent receives at most one left item, while some right-side bundle contains at least two items. Deleting one right item leaves value at least \( 3>2 \).

Thus no maximal discrete allocation is EF1.

5. Proposition 5

All weight calculations are correct.

The total mass is

\( 3/(2r)+(r-2)/r+1/(2r)=1 \).

Every left item occurs in two left pairs and therefore has usage \( 1/r \). Every right item receives singleton usage \( (r-2)/(r(r-1)) \) and pair usage \( 1/(r(r-1)) \), again totaling \( 1/r \).

Since every item is saturated, the maximality condition has no unsaturated item to which it could apply.

The supported values are \( 4,3,6 \), and their deletion residuals are \( 2,0,3 \). Thus the minimum supported value and maximum residual are both \( 3 \), proving EF1.

This correctly shows that an unconditional purification result mapping every fractional solution to a discrete solution cannot hold.

6. Proposition 6

Given the graph description in the prover output, the construction is correct:

  • \( Y_i \) is independent;
  • different added groups are completely joined;
  • \( x_{i,w} \) is adjacent to \( y_{i,w} \);
  • all \( Y \)-items have value zero.

The total mass and all claimed marginal calculations are correct. Each unused \( x_{i,w} \) is blocked by \( y_{i,w} \) in bundles indexed by \( i \), and by a member of \( Y_j \) in bundles indexed by \( j\neq i \). EF1 is inherited by deleting the same base item as in the base allocation.

However, the write-up never explicitly states that there are no edges between the base gadget and the added \( X_i\cup Y_i \) vertices. That fact is needed to show that \( B\cup Y_i \) is independent. The cited reduction does list the relevant edge types and supports this interpretation, but the proof as written should state this hypothesis explicitly. The reduction description should also say that the listed edge classes are exhaustive. This is an expositional omission, not a mathematical failure of the construction.

The phrase that zero-valued \( Y \)-items make the deletion residual “no worse” is correct but unnecessary: the subsequent same-witness argument directly proves EF1 and works even with arbitrary rational base values.

7. Boundary cases

All three principal boundary claims are correct under \( v_g\ge0 \):

  • The case \( \mu<1/r \) follows from Lemma 1.
  • For \( \mu=1/r \), a maximal independent set supported with weight \( 1/r \) gives maximality, and its only nonvacuous self-comparison satisfies EF1 by nonnegativity.
  • For \( \mu\ge |M|/r \), weight \( 1/r \) on every singleton saturates every item, and the remaining mass can be assigned to the empty set. Every ordered EF1 comparison among empty and singleton bundles is valid.

The all-zero, integral-\( r\mu \) construction is also correct. Inclusion-maximality of \( G[U] \) among induced \( k \)-colorable subgraphs ensures that every outside vertex has a neighbor in every color class. If empty color classes occur, then necessarily \( U=M \), so maximality is vacuous for outside vertices. The write-up should mention that repeated empty color classes are represented by aggregating their weights into the single allocation coordinate \( x_{\varnothing} \).

8. Resolution of the target

The target asks for a classification as polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard for every fixed \( r\ge4 \). The prover supplies neither a polynomial-time algorithm for the general problem nor a valid hardness reduction. It explicitly leaves the intermediate nonzero-valued regime open.

Consequently, the target is not settled. The NP-membership result and the failure analysis of the known discrete reduction are genuine and useful partial progress, but they do not answer the classification question.
