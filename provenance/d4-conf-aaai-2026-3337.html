The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(A\) arms, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_x\), a finite-support reward-vector law \(P_x\) for each type, per-capita arm capacities \(q_a\), a probe budget \(I\), and overhead \(\alpha\), choose a probe set \(S\), \(|S|\le I\), and assignment probabilities \(p_a(x,r_S)\). Here the observed group mass is \(\nu_S(x,r_S)=\mu_xP_x(r_S)\), and the probabilities must satisfy \(\sum_a p_a(x,r_S)=1\) and \(\sum_{x,r_S}\nu_S(x,r_S)p_a(x,r_S)\le q_a\). With \(v(x,r_S,a)=r_a\) for \(a\in S\) and \(v(x,r_S,a)=\mathbb E_{P_x}[R_a]\) otherwise, maximize \((1-\alpha(|S|))\exp\!\left(\sum_{x,r_S}\nu_S(x,r_S)\log\!\left(\sum_a p_a(x,r_S)v(x,r_S,a)\right)\right)\).

Result

Attaining the ratio \(\frac{e-1}{(2e-1)\zeta}\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every prescribed constant \(\zeta\ge1\). In fact, every fixed positive multiplicative approximation ratio is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with one type, binary rewards, dyadic probabilities, and \(q_a=1\) for every arm.

Thus a deterministic algorithm with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L,I\), for fixed \(\zeta\), would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

For completeness, assignment for a fixed probe set admits a polynomial-time multiplicative approximation. Enumerating probe sets gives a \((1-\varepsilon)\)-approximation in time
\(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{I-1}\binom mj\right)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L,\varepsilon^{-1})\).
The hardness concerns selecting the probe set when \(I\) varies.

1. The coverage gap used in the proof

We use the following known gap theorem. Fix constants \(s_0>0\) and \(\delta>0\). Given a universe \(U\) of size \(n\) and explicitly listed sets \(C_1,\ldots,C_N\), all of size \(d\), with integer \(t=n/d\), it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard to distinguish:

  • YES: \(t\) pairwise disjoint listed sets cover \(U\).
  • NO: Every collection of \(s\le s_0t\) listed sets covers at most the fraction \(1-(1-1/t)^s+\delta\) of \(U\).

This is stated in [Proposition 4.2 of *PASS Approximation*](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mirrokni/pass_approximation_journal.pdf).

Apply the theorem with \(s_0=1\) and \(\delta=1/16\). For \(t\ge2\), every collection of at most \(t\) sets in a NO instance leaves at least \(3n/16\) elements uncovered. Indeed,
\((1-1/t)^t\ge1/4\).
To verify this inequality, the derivative of \(f(t)=t\log(1-1/t)\), for real \(t>1\), is
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle f'(t)=\frac1{t-1}-\log\!\left(1+\frac1{t-1}\right)>0,\)
because \(\log(1+y)<y\) for \(y>0\). The value at \(t=2\) therefore gives the claimed bound.

Consequently, the NO coverage bound is \(13n/16\).

2. Construction of a society

Start with such a coverage instance. If \(N<t\), it cannot be a YES instance, so this case can be decided immediately. Hence assume \(N\ge t\).

Dyadic outcome probabilities

Choose a power of two \(D\) satisfying \(4n\le D<8n\). Choose integers \(h_u\), for \(u\in U\), each equal to either \(\lfloor D/n\rfloor\) or \(\lceil D/n\rceil\), such that \(\sum_{u\in U}h_u=D\). Define \(w_u=h_u/D\).

These probabilities are positive dyadic rationals and satisfy
\(\displaystyle \frac{3}{4n}\le w_u\le\frac{5}{4n}.\)

Arms, rewards, and overhead

Construct the following instance:

  • There is one type, with mass \(\mu_1=1\).
  • There are \(m=N+1\) arms: one for each listed set and one dummy arm.
  • Outcome \(u\in U\), occurring with probability \(w_u\), has reward

\(\displaystyle R_a(u)=\mathbf 1_{\{u\in C_a\}}\) for \(a\le N\), and \(R_{N+1}(u)=0\).

  • Every capacity is \(q_a=1\).
  • Set \(I=t+1\), with \(\alpha(j)=0\) for \(0\le j\le t\), and \(\alpha(t+1)=1\).

All requirements in the question hold, including \(I\le m\). Identical reward vectors may be merged by summing their probabilities; this preserves the law and dyadic encoding.

For every genuine arm,
\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[R_a]=\sum_{u\in C_a}w_u\le\frac{5d}{4n}=\frac{5}{4t}\le\frac2t.\)

The capacities impose no further restriction: an arm’s total assignment mass is always at most the society’s total mass, which is one.

3. Welfare bounds

Let \(S\) be a probe set with \(|S|\le t\), and define its covered outcomes by
\(\displaystyle B(S)=\bigcup_{\substack{a\in S\\a\le N}}C_a.\)

An outcome belongs to \(B(S)\) exactly when some genuine probed arm returns reward one. Thus coverage is determined by the observation group, as required by the policy’s information restriction.

YES instances have optimum one

Probe the \(t\) sets of a disjoint cover. Every positive-mass observation group then contains a probed arm with reward one. Assign that group entirely to such an arm.

Every group has utility one, the overhead factor is one, and the policy is feasible. Therefore \(F(S,p)=1\). Since all utilities and overhead factors are at most one, \(\mathrm{OPT}=1\).

Insufficient coverage forces small welfare

Suppose \(|B(S)|\le13n/16\). The probability mass of uncovered outcomes satisfies
\(\displaystyle W=\sum_{u\notin B(S)}w_u\ge\frac{3n}{16}\frac{3}{4n}=\frac9{64}\ge\frac18.\)

On an uncovered outcome, every probed reward is zero and every unprobed arm has mean at most \(2/t\). Since group utility is a convex combination of these values, it is at most \(2/t\). On covered outcomes it is at most one.

If any group has zero utility, the objective is zero. Otherwise, for \(t>2\),
\(\displaystyle \log F(S,p)\le W\log(2/t)\le\frac18\log(2/t),\)
and hence
\(\displaystyle F(S,p)\le(2/t)^{1/8}.\)

This bound holds for every feasible policy, including fractional policies.

In a NO instance, every set of at most \(t\) probes satisfies the coverage hypothesis. Sets of \(t+1\) probes have overhead factor zero. Therefore
\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{NO}}\le(2/t)^{1/8}.\)

4. Hardness for the requested ratio

Fix \(\zeta\ge1\), and write
\(\displaystyle \beta=\frac{e-1}{(2e-1)\zeta},\qquad Z=\lceil\zeta\rceil,\qquad T=2(6Z)^8.\)

Because \(e>2\),
\(\displaystyle \beta>\frac1{3\zeta}\ge\frac1{3Z}.\)

For \(t\ge T\), the preceding bound becomes
\(\displaystyle (2/t)^{1/8}\le\frac1{6Z}<\beta.\)

Assume a polynomial-time \(\beta\)-approximation algorithm exists. We could decide the coverage gap as follows.

  • If \(t<T\), enumerate all collections of at most \(t\) listed sets and test whether one covers \(U\).
  • If \(t\ge T\), construct the society and run the approximation algorithm.
  • Accept exactly when the returned probe set contains genuine arms whose union has more than \(13n/16\) elements.

For a YES instance, the returned solution must have value at least \(\beta\), since \(\mathrm{OPT}=1\). It cannot use \(t+1\) probes, whose value is zero. It also cannot cover at most \(13n/16\) elements, since then its value would be at most \(1/(6Z)<\beta\). Thus the test accepts.

For a NO instance, no collection of at most \(t\) sets passes the coverage test, and a returned set of size \(t+1\) can simply be rejected. Thus the test rejects.

The small-\(t\) enumeration takes
\(N^{O(T)}\operatorname{poly}(n)=N^{O(\zeta^8)}\operatorname{poly}(n)\)
time, which is polynomial for each prescribed constant \(\zeta\). The construction has \(\tau=1\), \(m=N+1\), \(K\le n\), \(I=t+1\), and input length
\(L=O(nm+n\log n+t\log t)\).
It is polynomial in the coverage input size.

This contradicts the coverage gap theorem unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The same argument works for any fixed positive ratio by choosing a sufficiently large constant \(T\).

The decision procedure only checks coverage. It needs no exact comparison of logarithms, exponentials, or irrational objective values.

5. Assignment and enumeration running times

Here is a justified upper bound for the general input model.

Fix \(S\), and index its positive-mass observation groups by \(g\). There are at most \(K\) groups. Write their masses as \(\nu_g\), their reward coefficients as \(v_{ga}\), and utilities as \(u_g=\sum_a p_{ga}v_{ga}\).

Detecting zero optimum

Let \(Q=\sum_aq_a\), so \(1\le Q\le m\). The constant policy \(p^0_{ga}=q_a/Q\) is feasible, because its aggregate assignment to arm \(a\) is \(q_a/Q\le q_a\).

If some group has \(v_{ga}=0\) for every arm with \(q_a>0\), its utility is zero under every feasible policy. Indeed, a zero-capacity arm receives zero assignment in every positive-mass group.

Otherwise, \(p^0\) gives every group positive utility. Under binary encoding, positive input rationals are at least \(2^{-L}\). Every positive coefficient \(v_{ga}\) is therefore at least \(2^{-2L}\), including an unprobed mean. Consequently,
\(\displaystyle u_g(p^0)\ge b_0:=2^{-3L}/m.\)

A rational linear-program approximation

Fix rational \(0<\varepsilon<1\), and let \(\theta=\varepsilon/4\). Blending an optimal policy \(p^*\) with \(p^0\) gives
\(\displaystyle p^\theta=(1-\theta)p^*+\theta p^0.\)

It remains feasible, has every utility at least \(b=\theta b_0\), and has geometric welfare at least \((1-\theta)\) times the optimum for \(S\). Thus restricting utilities to \([b,1]\) loses at most \(-\log(1-\theta)\le2\theta\) in log welfare.

On \([b,1]\), interpolate \(\log u\) at rational breakpoints with consecutive ratios at most \(1+\theta\). The concave piecewise-linear interpolant \(\ell\) satisfies
\(\displaystyle \log u-\theta\le\ell(u)\le\log u.\)
Indeed, on each interval both functions lie between its endpoint logarithms, whose difference is at most \(\log(1+\theta)\le\theta\).

There are
\(J=O((L+\log m+\log\varepsilon^{-1})/\varepsilon)\)
segments. Concavity means that \(\ell\) is the minimum of the extended segment lines. Approximate each line’s coefficients by rationals and shift downward so that, throughout \([b,1]\), its error is between zero and \(\theta\). This requires polynomially many precision bits. The resulting function \(\widetilde\ell\) satisfies
\(\displaystyle \log u-2\theta\le\widetilde\ell(u)\le\log u.\)

Maximizing \(\sum_g\nu_g\widetilde\ell(u_g)\) is a rational linear program: introduce one variable per group bounded above by every segment line. It has \(O(Km+K)\) variables and \(O(Km+KJ+m)\) constraints, all with polynomial encoding length.

Its solution loses at most \(4\theta=\varepsilon\) in log welfare compared with the unrestricted optimum. Hence its welfare is at least \(e^{-\varepsilon}\ge1-\varepsilon\) times that optimum. The fixed overhead factor preserves this guarantee.

Thus fixed-set assignment takes
\(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L,\varepsilon^{-1})\)
time. Enumerating all probe sets of size at most \(I-1\) gives the stated running time
\(\displaystyle \left(\sum_{j=0}^{I-1}\binom mj\right)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L,\varepsilon^{-1}).\)

Choosing among the sets using their approximate log objectives and log overheads, to additional accuracy \(O(\varepsilon)\), preserves a \((1-\varepsilon)\)-guarantee after adjusting the internal accuracy by a constant factor. Sets with zero overhead are skipped. For fixed \(I\), this running time is polynomial; its dependence on \(I\) is exponential in general.
