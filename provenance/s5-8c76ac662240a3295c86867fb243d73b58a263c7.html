The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant and project types with rational masses, capacities, compatible edges, and strict rankings of counterpart types, choose a feasible mass matching \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) minimizing \(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i q_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j r_j\). For each type and any challenger flow \(y\), pair lost and gained partner mass by a fractional transport that minimizes the vote under that type's ranking; call \(x\) popular if its summed vote is nonnegative against every feasible \(y\).

Let \(A,B\) be nonempty sets of applicant and project types, with compatible pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\) and strict rankings of compatible counterpart types. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) need not sum to one. Capacities \(q_i,r_j\) are positive integers; costs \(\gamma_{ij}\) are arbitrary signed binary rationals.
Set \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Feasible mass matchings are real arrays satisfying \(0\le x_{ij}\le\mu_i\nu_j\), \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\mu_iq_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\nu_jr_j\). Assume this feasible set is nonempty. The pair bound encodes the prohibition on repeated individual pairs.
For each type \(v\), write \(x_{vu}\) for its incident mass with counterpart \(u\). Against feasible \(y\), cancel common mass and set \(a_u=\max\{x_{vu}-y_{vu},0\}\), \(b_u=\max\{y_{vu}-x_{vu},0\}\).
Define \(s_v(u,w)=1\) when \(u\succ_v w\), \(-1\) when \(w\succ_v u\), and \(0\) when \(u=w\). The vote is \(V_v(x,y)=\min\{\sum_{u,w}s_v(u,w)t_{uw}:t_{uw}\ge0,\ \sum_wt_{uw}=a_u,\ \sum_ut_{uw}=b_w\}\), with value zero when no mass changes.
Call \(x\) popular if \(\sum_{v\in A\cup B}V_v(x,y)\ge0\) for every feasible real \(y\). Its cost is \(C(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\); the optimization problem minimizes this cost over popular feasible matchings.

Is deciding, for a binary rational threshold \(K\), whether a popular feasible mass matching \(x\) exists with \(C(x)\le K\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Discrete many-to-many instances with strict, possibly incomplete preferences and a perfect matching admit polynomial-time minimum-cost popular perfect matching algorithms. [Kavitha–Makino](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00384).
For one-to-one matchings, utility-optimal popular lotteries under expected pairwise votes are polynomial-time computable; that voting definition differs from the adversarial transport above. [Huang–Kavitha](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf). No result settling the exact mass problem above was located.

The principal additional results are:

  • Unpopularity has a purely combinatorial certificate: a negative alternating closed trail using each compatibility edge at most once.
  • Consequently, an integral candidate with integral pair bounds is popular against real challengers if and only if it is popular against integral challengers.
  • Let $c$ be the number of connected components of the supported compatibility graph, and let

$$ k=m-\tau+c $$
be its cycle rank. The general optimization problem admits an exact algorithm with running time
$$ 4^{\min(m,3k)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
In particular, it is fixed-parameter tractable in $k$.

I also give self-contained proofs of the rational-witness and polynomial-hierarchy bounds, and establish the unit-mass polynomial-time special case with the missing identification of transport votes and bijection votes repaired.

1. Feasible directions and optimal vertices

Write $G=(A\cup B,E)$. For $e=(i,j)$, define
$$ U_e=\mu_i\nu_j. $$
Define the prescribed incident masses by
$$ \beta_i=\mu_iq_i,\qquad \beta_j=\nu_jr_j. $$
The feasible polytope is
$$ \mathcal P= \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^E: 0\le x_e\le U_e,\ \sum_{e\ni v}x_e=\beta_v\text{ for every }v \right\}. $$
It is nonempty by assumption and compact because all pair bounds are finite.

For a balanced vector $d$, meaning
$$ \sum_{e\ni v}d_e=0\qquad\text{for every }v, $$
let $\Phi(d)$ be the sum of the local minimum transport costs with source amounts $(-d_e)_+$ and destination amounts $(d_e)_+$. Thus
$$ \sum_vV_v(x,y)=\Phi(y-x). $$
The local transportation problems have equal source and destination totals, so they are feasible. Their feasible sets are compact, and their minima are attained. Scaling transport plans in both directions gives
$$ \Phi(\lambda d)=\lambda\Phi(d)\qquad(\lambda\ge0). $$

Define
$$ T(x)= \left\{ d: \sum_{e\ni v}d_e=0,\ d_e\ge0\text{ if }x_e=0,\ d_e\le0\text{ if }x_e=U_e \right\}. $$

Lemma 1. A feasible $x$ is popular precisely when $\Phi(d)\ge0$ for every $d\in T(x)$.

Proof. Every feasible challenger $y$ satisfies $y-x\in T(x)$.

Conversely, fix $d\in T(x)$. At every active bound, its sign points inward. At every inactive bound, there is positive slack. Since there are finitely many coordinates, $x+\varepsilon d$ is feasible for all sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$. Popularity therefore implies
$$ 0\le\Phi(\varepsilon d)=\varepsilon\Phi(d). $$
This proves the equivalence. $\square$

Let
$$ F(x)= \left\{ z\in\mathcal P: z_e=0\text{ whenever }x_e=0,\ z_e=U_e\text{ whenever }x_e=U_e \right\}. $$
This is a nonempty face of $\mathcal P$. If $z\in F(x)$, then $T(z)\subseteq T(x)$.

Corollary 2. Every point of $F(x)$ is popular whenever $x$ is popular. If popular points exist, a minimum-cost popular point exists at a vertex of $\mathcal P$.

Proof. The first assertion follows from Lemma 1. A linear cost attains its minimum over the compact face $F(x)$ at a vertex of that face, which is also a vertex of $\mathcal P$. Hence every popular point has a popular vertex of no greater cost. The cheapest popular vertex is therefore globally optimal. $\square$

Signed costs present no difficulty in this argument.

2. A combinatorial certificate of unpopularity

An alternating closed trail consists of a closed walk in $G$ with no repeated edge, together with signs $+$ and $-$ alternating along the walk.

Its signs are admissible at $x$ if
$$ x_e<U_e\quad\text{on every positive edge},\qquad x_e>0\quad\text{on every negative edge}. $$
At each visit to a vertex $v$, the two consecutive trail edges have opposite signs. If they are $e^-$ and $e^+$, the contribution of that visit is
$$ s_v\bigl(u_v(e^-),u_v(e^+)\bigr), $$
where $u_v(e)$ is the counterpart of $v$ on $e$. Let $W(T)$ be the sum of these contributions over all visits of the trail. A vertex may be visited more than once.

Theorem 3. A feasible $x$ is unpopular if and only if there is an admissible alternating closed trail $T$ with
$$ W(T)<0. $$
Such a certificate uses at most $m$ edges.

Proof: a negative trail implies unpopularity

Set
$$ d_e= \begin{cases} 1,&e\text{ is a positive trail edge},\\ -1,&e\text{ is a negative trail edge},\\ 0,&e\text{ is outside the trail}. \end{cases} $$
Every visit pairs one positive and one negative incidence. Consequently, $d$ is balanced at every vertex.

Admissibility ensures that $x+\varepsilon d$ is feasible for sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$. At every trail visit, transport mass $\varepsilon$ from its negative edge to its positive edge. Since no edge repeats, these pairings have exactly the required local marginals. Their total cost is $\varepsilon W(T)$.

The minimum transport cost cannot exceed this particular transport cost. Thus
$$ \Phi(\varepsilon d)\le\varepsilon W(T)<0, $$
and $x$ is unpopular.

Proof: unpopularity implies a negative trail

By Lemma 1, choose $d\in T(x)$ with $\Phi(d)<0$. Discard coordinates with $d_e=0$, and assign every remaining edge its sign in $d$. Choose minimizing local transports.

Construct a directed graph $H$ whose nodes are these original edges. Its arcs represent local transport pairings:

  • At an applicant vertex, put an arc $e^-\to g^+$.
  • At a project vertex, put an arc $g^+\to e^-$.

In both cases the arc cost is
$$ s_v\bigl(u_v(e^-),u_v(g^+)\bigr), $$
and its flow is the corresponding transport amount.

These flows form a circulation. Indeed, at a positive edge-node, the total incoming flow at its applicant endpoint and total outgoing flow at its project endpoint both equal $d_e$. At a negative edge-node, the incoming flow at its project endpoint and outgoing flow at its applicant endpoint both equal $-d_e$.

Every nonzero nonnegative circulation decomposes into positive multiples of simple directed cycles. For completeness, follow positive-flow arcs until a node repeats, extract a simple directed cycle, and subtract the smallest flow on that cycle. This preserves nonnegativity and balance and removes at least one positive-flow arc. Repeating terminates after finitely many steps.

The circulation has total cost $\Phi(d)<0$. Therefore at least one cycle in this decomposition has negative cost.

Interpret a positive original edge as traversed from $A$ to $B$, and a negative edge as traversed from $B$ to $A$. The definition of $H$ ensures that successive edge-nodes of its directed cycle meet at the appropriate endpoint. Thus the cycle projects to an alternating closed trail in $G$. Simplicity in $H$ means that no original edge repeats. Its cost is exactly $W(T)$.

Finally, its signs are admissible because $d\in T(x)$ and all its edge coordinates are nonzero. $\square$

Consequences

A certificate consists of an ordered edge list and its alternating signs. Its encoding length is
$$ O(m\log(m+1)). $$
For a rational feasible candidate, checking the incidences, distinctness of edges, admissibility, and negativity of $W(T)$ takes polynomial time. Thus popularity of a supplied rational feasible candidate belongs to $\mathrm{coNP}$ without requiring rational transport variables in the certificate.

There is also a useful integrality consequence.

Corollary 4. Suppose all $U_e$ are integers and $x$ is an integral feasible matching. Then $x$ is popular against real feasible challengers if and only if it is popular against integral feasible challengers.

Proof. Only the reverse implication requires proof. If $x$ is unpopular, take the trail from Theorem 3 and its vector $d$. Every admissible slack is a positive integer, hence at least one. Therefore $x+d$ is an integral feasible challenger.

The trail pairings give a transport of cost $W(T)<0$, so
$$ \Phi(d)\le W(T)<0. $$
Thus an integral challenger already witnesses unpopularity. $\square$

3. Exact popularity testing by directed cycles

For a candidate $x$, let
$$ f=\bigl|\{e:0<x_e<U_e\}\bigr|. $$
At a lower-bound edge, only a positive nonzero direction is possible. At an upper-bound edge, only a negative nonzero direction is possible. Enumerate the two signs only for the $f$ interior edges.

For each sign assignment, construct the directed graph from the proof of Theorem 3, now including every locally compatible opposite-sign pair. Its arc costs are in $\{-1,1\}$.

Proposition 5. The candidate is unpopular exactly when one of these graphs contains a negative directed cycle.

Proof. A negative simple directed cycle gives the admissible negative trail of Theorem 3. Conversely, assign the signs of a negative trail to its edges and extend arbitrarily to the other interior edges. That trail appears as a directed cycle in the corresponding graph. $\square$

Each graph has at most $m$ nodes and
$$ O\left(\sum_v\deg(v)^2\right)=O(m\tau) $$
arcs. Negative-cycle detection by Bellman–Ford, initialized with distance zero at every node, takes $O(m^2\tau)$ arithmetic operations. All arc weights are small integers.

Including rational feasibility and bound comparisons, this gives a popularity test in
$$ 2^f\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+\ell(x)), $$
where $\ell(x)$ is the candidate’s encoding length.

The sign enumeration is essential to this argument: an original edge receives one sign throughout a trail.

4. Rational witnesses and general complexity bounds

4.1 Vertices and their encoding length

For feasible $x$, let
$$ I(x)=\{e:0<x_e<U_e\}. $$

Lemma 6. A feasible $x$ is a vertex of $\mathcal P$ if and only if the graph with edge set $I(x)$ is a forest.

Proof. If it contains a cycle, bipartiteness makes the cycle even. Alternating positive and negative perturbations preserve all vertex sums. Since these edges are interior, sufficiently small perturbations in both directions remain feasible. Hence $x$ is not a vertex.

Conversely, suppose $I(x)$ is a forest and $x=(y+z)/2$ for feasible $y,z$. Every coordinate at a bound in $x$ has the same value in $y,z$. Thus $y-z$ is balanced and supported on $I(x)$. At a leaf its sole supported coordinate must vanish. Repeated leaf removal gives $y=z$. $\square$

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all supplied masses. Then
$$ D\beta_v\in\mathbb Z,\qquad DU_e\in\mathbb Z,\qquad \log D=O(L). $$
For a pair bound, the denominator product of its two endpoint masses divides $D$, since the endpoints are distinct supported types.

At a vertex, coordinates outside $I(x)$ are zero or $U_e$. After scaling by $D$, leaf removal in the interior-edge forest determines every remaining coordinate as a difference of integers. Therefore
$$ Dx_e\in\mathbb Z $$
at every vertex. Since $0\le Dx_e\le DU_e$, each coordinate has $O(L)$ bits.

Together with Corollary 2, this proves that every yes-instance has a popular witness of encoding length $O(mL)$.

4.2 A general exponential algorithm

Enumerate a forest $F\subseteq E$ and assign each edge outside $F$ either zero or its upper bound. Solve the balance equations on $F$ and retain feasible solutions.

For a fixed assignment, there is at most one solution: a difference of two solutions would be balanced and supported on a forest, hence zero. Lemma 6 ensures that every vertex appears.

If $|F|=h$, there are at most
$$ \binom mh2^{m-h} $$
assignments, and each resulting candidate has at most $h$ interior edges. Its popularity test needs at most $2^h$ negative-cycle tests. Hence the total number of such tests is at most
$$ \sum_{h=0}^m\binom mh2^{m-h}2^h=4^m. $$
Testing costs against $K$ therefore decides the threshold problem in
$$ 4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

4.3 Polynomial-hierarchy membership

Guess a polynomial-size rational vertex witness, check feasibility and its cost, and universally exclude the polynomial-size negative-trail certificates of Theorem 3. This gives
$$ \text{the threshold problem}\in\Sigma_2^p. $$
This is an upper bound, not a resolution of the requested classification.

5. An algorithm parameterized by cycle rank

Let $c$ be the number of connected components of $G$, and define
$$ k=m-\tau+c. $$

Theorem 7. The threshold problem, and minimum-cost optimization when popular matchings exist, can be solved in
$$ 4^{\min(m,3k)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time. Popularity of a supplied rational candidate can be tested in
$$ 8^k\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+\ell(x)) $$
time.

5.1 Compressing the direction space

Obtain a rational feasible point $x^0$ by polynomial-time rational linear programming.

First repeatedly delete degree-one vertices and their incident edges, and delete isolated vertices. Every balanced direction is zero on the deleted edges: at the moment an edge is deleted, the other incident direction coordinates have already been proved zero.

The remaining graph, if nonempty, has minimum degree two. Partition its edges into:

  • maximal paths whose internal vertices have degree two and whose endpoints have degree at least three;
  • entire connected components in which every vertex has degree two.

The latter components are cycles. Paths may return to their starting branching vertex.

Call these paths and cycles chains, and let their number be $p$. Order the edges of each chain. Balance at each internal degree-two vertex forces
$$ d_{e_t}=(-1)^{t-1}z_a $$
on chain $a$. For a cycle component, consistency holds because its length is even.

Thus every balanced direction is uniquely represented by a vector $z\in\mathbb R^p$, subject to the remaining balance equations at branching vertices. Write
$$ d=Pz, $$
where each column of $P$ has alternating entries $1,-1$ on one chain and zero elsewhere.

Each original bound becomes an interval constraint on its chain variable. Specifically, an edge with coefficient $1$ gives
$$ -x_e^0\le z_a\le U_e-x_e^0, $$
and an edge with coefficient $-1$ gives
$$ x_e^0-U_e\le z_a\le x_e^0. $$
Intersect these intervals within each chain to obtain $\ell_a\le z_a\le u_a$.

Consequently, the feasible polytope is affinely isomorphic to
$$ Q=\{z:Rz=0,\ \ell_a\le z_a\le u_a\text{ for all }a\}, $$
where $Rz=0$ is the system of balance equations at branching vertices. The isomorphism is
$$ z\longmapsto x^0+Pz. $$
All its data have polynomial encoding length.

5.2 Bounding the number of chains

Let $n_0,m_0,c_0$ be the numbers of vertices, edges, and connected components remaining after deletion. Deleting leaves and isolated vertices preserves cycle rank, so
$$ k=m_0-n_0+c_0. $$

Let $B_0$ be the set of branching vertices. Every such vertex has degree at least three. Since all other remaining vertices have degree two,
$$ \sum_{v\in B_0}(\deg(v)-2)=2(m_0-n_0)=2(k-c_0). $$
It follows that
$$ |B_0|\le2(k-c_0). $$

Every chain in a component containing a branching vertex has exactly two branching incidences, including both incidences of a returning path. Therefore the number of these chains is
$$ \frac12\sum_{v\in B_0}\deg(v) = |B_0|+k-c_0 \le3(k-c_0). $$
If $c_2$ is the number of pure cycle components, then
$$ p\le3(k-c_0)+c_2\le3k. $$
Also $p\le m$, because the chains are nonempty and edge-disjoint. If the remaining graph is empty, $k=p=0$.

Hence
$$ p\le\min(m,3k). $$

5.3 Popularity testing after compression

At a candidate $z\in Q$:

  • if $z_a=\ell_a<u_a$, a nonzero chain direction must be positive;
  • if $z_a=u_a>\ell_a$, it must be negative;
  • if $\ell_a<z_a<u_a$, either sign is possible;
  • if $\ell_a=u_a$, its direction is zero.

Enumerate the signs of the interior chain coordinates. They induce alternating signs on all original edges of each chain. Omit deleted edges and chains with fixed coordinates.

These signs respect every original active bound. This follows directly from the interval construction: a direction pointing inward at an endpoint of the intersection interval points inward at every constituent active bound.

For each assignment, use the directed negative-cycle test of Section 3. It remains exact because every balanced direction has the chain representation above, while any negative directed cycle supplies a balanced admissible original direction.

There are at most $2^p$ assignments. Since $p\le3k$, the claimed $8^k$ popularity-testing bound follows.

5.4 Enumerating vertices and optimizing

For each coordinate of $Q$, choose one of three statuses:

  • fix it to $\ell_a$;
  • fix it to $u_a$;
  • leave it unspecified.

Solve $Rz=0$ together with the specified equalities. Retain a solution only if it is unique and belongs to $Q$.

Every vertex of $Q$ appears. Indeed, fix all bounds active at that vertex. If these equalities did not determine it uniquely, their homogeneous system would have a nonzero direction supported on the strictly interior coordinates. Small perturbations in both directions would remain feasible, contradicting extremality.

A retained candidate from a pattern with $s$ unspecified coordinates has at most $s$ interior coordinates. Its popularity test therefore uses at most $2^s$ sign assignments. Summing over all patterns gives
$$ \sum_{\text{patterns}}2^s=(1+1+2)^p=4^p. $$
Each linear-system computation, feasibility check, negative-cycle test, and cost comparison has polynomial bit complexity.

Affine isomorphisms preserve vertices. Corollary 2 therefore guarantees that these candidates include an optimal popular matching whenever one exists. Since $p\le\min(m,3k)$, the stated optimization bound follows. $\square$

6. Polynomial-time special cases

6.1 Unit masses and arbitrary capacities

Suppose every supported mass is one. Then $U_e=1$ and all prescribed incident masses are integers. Lemma 6 and the scaling argument with $D=1$ show that every feasible vertex is a zero-one incidence vector of a discrete perfect many-to-many matching.

The two voting definitions also coincide on discrete matchings. After cancellation, every nonzero transport marginal equals one. The transportation polytope is then the polytope of nonnegative matrices with all row and column sums one.

An optimal transport can be chosen at a vertex of this polytope. By the forest argument of Lemma 6, such a vertex is integral; with unit row and column sums, it is a permutation matrix. Thus the minimum over real transports equals the minimum over residual-set bijections.

Corollary 4 shows that popularity of each discrete candidate against discrete perfect challengers is equivalent to popularity against all real feasible challengers. Corollary 2 shows that restricting optimization to discrete vertices does not increase the optimum.

We may therefore apply the polynomial-time minimum-cost popular perfect matching theorem of Kavitha–Makino. Its hypotheses hold: preferences are strict and possibly incomplete, and feasibility supplies a discrete perfect matching. Moreover, each capacity is at most its vertex degree, so binary capacities cause no hidden expansion. The resulting running time is
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
See [Kavitha–Makino, Theorem 1.1 and voting definition](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00384v1).

The same argument applies when all pair bounds equal a common positive rational $a$ and every $\beta_v/a$ is integral: divide all matching masses by $a$. Votes scale by $a$, and the transformed costs remain binary rational.

6.2 Degree at most two

Suppose every supported type has at most two compatible counterparts.

A degree-one vertex has fixed incident mass. At a degree-two vertex $v$, write its neighbors as $u_v\succ_v w_v$. Balance gives
$$ x_{vu_v}-y_{vu_v}=-(x_{vw_v}-y_{vw_v}). $$
After cancellation, there is at most one source and one destination. In either direction,
$$ V_v(x,y)=x_{vu_v}-y_{vu_v}. $$

Define
$$ W(x)=\sum_{\deg(v)=2}x_{vu_v}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_vV_v(x,y)=W(x)-W(y). $$
Hence popularity is exactly maximization of $W$ over $\mathcal P$.

First compute $W^*=\max_{x\in\mathcal P}W(x)$, then minimize $C(x)$ over $\mathcal P$ subject to $W(x)=W^*$. These two rational linear programs give running time
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

7. What remains open

The arguments establish combinatorial losing certificates, integral-challenger equivalence for integral candidates, polynomial-size optimal witnesses, and an algorithm parameterized by cycle rank.

They do not settle the general classification. When $k$ grows with the input, the factor $64^k$ can be exponential. Likewise, the negative-trail characterization does not eliminate sign enumeration for interior edges.

No polynomial-time algorithm or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction for the exact general mass problem is proved here.
