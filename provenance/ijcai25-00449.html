The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite location-type set \(X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_\tau\}\subseteq G\), a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(X)\), and a fixed anonymous, neutral, possibly randomized lifting convention \(\mathcal R\) to odd-\(N\) profiles, define \(M^{\mathcal R}_{1/2,\infty}(\mu)\) as the limit of the paper’s RD+PCD mixture on lifted profiles; determine whether the limit is single-valued, verify finite-lift strategyproofness, and compute or bound \(\sup_{\mu\in\Delta(X)} \operatorname{SC}_{\mu}(M^{\mathcal R}_{1/2,\infty}(\mu))/\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\), in particular whether it is at most \(7/4\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\) be the unit circle with shortest-path distance \(d\); a facility may be placed anywhere in \(G\). Agents minimize expected distance. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) distinct location types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with total mass \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. For computational statements, coordinates are binary rationals, \(L\) is the total input bit length, and running times use \(\tau,L\), lift size \(N\), and any representation parameters of the lifting convention; there is no separate candidate-count parameter \(m\).
For an odd profile size \(N=2k+1\geq3\), order the reports clockwise as \(y_0\leq\cdots\leq y_{N-1}\) in a coordinate interval of length \(1\), extend by \(y_{j+N}=y_j+1\), and put \(g_j=y_{j+1}-y_j\). RD selects each reported copy with probability \(1/N\); PCD selects copy \(j\) with probability \(g_{j+k}\), with gap indices taken modulo \(N\). Coincident copies may be ordered arbitrarily, and probabilities at the same location are added. Set \(M_N=(\mathrm{RD}+\mathrm{PCD})/2\).
A fixed lifting convention \(\mathcal R=(\mathcal R_N)_{N\text{ odd}}\) assigns each society a possibly randomized labeled profile \(B_N\in X^N\). Its law is invariant under agent permutations and equivariant under circle isometries, and its empirical masses \(\widehat\mu_N\) satisfy \(\|\widehat\mu_N-\mu\|_1\to0\) in probability. Define \(Q_N^{\mathcal R}(\mu)=\mathbb E[M_N(B_N)]\), averaging both sources of randomness, and define \(M_{1/2,\infty}^{\mathcal R}(\mu)\) only when these lotteries converge in total variation along all odd \(N\).
Finite-lift strategyproofness concerns one labeled agent changing its report to any point of \(G\) after a lift is drawn, without relifting the society. Write \(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(\ell)=\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)\mathbb E_{Y\sim\ell}d(x,Y)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{z\in G}\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)d(x,z)\); assign ratio \(1\) to a unanimous society. For fixed finite \(X\), take suprema over rational probability vectors on \(X\), omitting zero entries from the supplied support.

For every admissible fixed \(\mathcal R\), does \(M_{1/2,\infty}^{\mathcal R}(\mu)\) exist as a unique lottery for every society, and is the finite-lift mechanism strategyproof in expectation?
When the limits exist, what is \(A_{\mathcal R}(X)=\sup_\mu \operatorname{SC}_\mu(M_{1/2,\infty}^{\mathcal R}(\mu))/\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\); in particular, is \(A_{\mathcal R}(X)\leq7/4\) uniformly over finite \(X\) and admissible \(\mathcal R\)?

For finite odd populations, the equal RD–PCD mixture is strategyproof in expectation and has approximation ratio at most \(7/4\). [Rogowski–Dziubiński](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12943v1)
A subsequent preprint establishes the stronger discrete bound \(3/2\) for the same odd-population mixture; it does not formulate the mass-lifting limit problem above. No result settling that exact continuous problem was located. [Chan–Lin–Wang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12792)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined an admissible lift as a profile supported on the society's types whose empirical masses converge to the prescribed masses in probability; anonymity and neutrality apply to its law.
  • Averaged lifting randomness before taking the limit, and specified total-variation convergence along all odd population sizes. Uniqueness is required for each fixed convention, without assuming independence from the convention.
  • Used consecutive cyclic gaps to define opposing-arc lengths, retaining the wrap-around gap even when reports coincide.
  • Interpreted finite-lift strategyproofness as a unilateral deviation by one labeled agent after lifting, allowing any circle report and performing no relifting.
  • Normalized total mass to one, allowed facility locations throughout the circle, and assigned unanimous societies approximation ratio one.
  • Restricted supplied masses and computational coordinates to binary rationals; zero masses in the fixed-set supremum are omitted from the actual support.

Results

Universal existence fails: an admissible fixed lifting convention can produce two distinct subsequential limits for the same society. Moreover, two admissible conventions whose limits exist for every society can give different lotteries at that society.

Finite-lift strategyproofness holds for every admissible convention.

Whenever the limits exist for every rational society on a nonempty finite set \(X\), the exact approximation ratio is

\(A_{\mathcal R}(X)=1\) if \(|X|=1\), and \(A_{\mathcal R}(X)=3/2\) if \(|X|\geq2\).

In particular, the requested bound \(7/4\) holds wherever the limiting mechanism is defined.

1. The finite-population theorem used

Write \(c_x(\ell)=\mathbb E_{Y\sim\ell}d(x,Y)\). We use the following supplied finite-population result: for every odd \(N\geq3\), every profile \(b\in G^N\), every agent \(i\), and every alternative report \(z\in G\),

\(c_{b_i}(M_N(b))\leq c_{b_i}(M_N(b[i\to z]))\),

and, writing \(\nu_b=N^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{b_i}\),

\(\operatorname{SC}_{\nu_b}(M_N(b))\leq \frac32\operatorname{OPT}(\nu_b)\).

These are the odd-population assertions of Theorem 1 in [Chan–Lin–Wang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12792). Its Mechanism 2 uses precisely the opposing-gap probabilities in the question, including aggregation at coincident reports. Its domain is the continuous circle, so deviations and optimal facilities may be arbitrary circle points. Thus its hypotheses match the present finite-lift problem.

All limiting arguments below are proved separately.

2. PCD as a median after a random cut

Lemma. Choose a cut uniformly with respect to circle length, unwrap the reports clockwise from that cut, and return their median. The resulting lottery is PCD.

Proof. A cut in the gap after copy \(r\) produces the order

\(y_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{r+N}\).

Its median is copy \(r+k+1\). Since \(N=2k+1\), this index is congruent to \(r-k\) modulo \(N\). The gap has length \(g_r\); therefore copy \(j\) receives probability \(g_{j+k}\), exactly as required. Cuts through reported locations have probability zero. Coincident copies cause no problem because their probabilities are aggregated. ∎

One immediate consequence is useful repeatedly:

Majority property. If at least \(k+1\) copies report \(x\), then \(\operatorname{PCD}(b)=\delta_x\).

Indeed, in every unwrapped order, strictly more than half the reports equal \(x\), so the median equals \(x\).

3. A baseline convention with limits everywhere

We first establish a convenient globally defined convention. Let \(\mathcal I_N(\mu)\) consist of \(N\) independent samples from \(\mu\), with their sampling labels.

This law is invariant under label permutations and equivariant under circle isometries. For every support point \(x\),

\(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_N(x))=\mu(x)(1-\mu(x))/N\).

Chebyshev’s inequality and the finiteness of the support therefore give the required convergence of empirical masses.

We next prove that its expected lotteries converge in total variation.

Fix one gap between consecutive support locations. Starting immediately clockwise after this gap, list the support as \(z_1,\ldots,z_\tau\), and set

\(P_j=\sum_{r=1}^j\mu(z_r)\).

For every cut in this gap, the selected PCD location is the sample median in this order.

There are two cases.

  • If \(P_{j-1}<1/2<P_j\), empirical convergence implies that this median equals \(z_j\) with probability tending to one.
  • If \(P_j=1/2\), positivity of all supplied masses gives \(P_{j-1}<1/2<P_{j+1}\). Consequently, the median belongs to \(\{z_j,z_{j+1}\}\) with probability tending to one. The number \(S_j\) of sampled reports in \(\{z_1,\ldots,z_j\}\) has distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(N,1/2)\). Because \(N=2k+1\), binomial symmetry gives

\(\Pr(S_j\geq k+1)=\Pr(S_j\leq k)=1/2\).
Together with concentration at the neighboring prefixes, this proves that the median lottery converges to \((\delta_{z_j}+\delta_{z_{j+1}})/2\).

The random-cut lemma expresses expected PCD as the sum of these median lotteries weighted by the finitely many support-gap lengths. Thus expected PCD converges coordinatewise, hence in total variation on the fixed finite support. Expected RD is exactly \(\mu\), so \(Q_N^{\mathcal I}(\mu)\) also converges.

This supplies an admissible convention with limits for every society.

4. An admissible convention without a limit

Consider the binary-rational society

\(\mu_*=\frac12\delta_0+\frac14\delta_{1/8}+\frac14\delta_{3/8}\).

For \(N=2k+1\), define two count profiles, listing counts at \(0,1/8,3/8\):

\(H_N=\bigl(k+1,\lfloor k/2\rfloor,k-\lfloor k/2\rfloor\bigr)\),

\(L_N=\bigl(k,\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor,k+1-\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor\bigr)\).

Both empirical mass vectors converge to \(\mu_*\). For each count profile, choose the labeled assignment uniformly among all assignments with those counts.

4.1 Their limiting lotteries

The majority property gives

\(\operatorname{PCD}(H_N)=\delta_0\).

For \(L_N\), write \(n_1=\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor\). Its three positive gaps have lengths \(1/8,1/4,5/8\), after copy indices

\(k-1,\quad k+n_1-1,\quad 2k\),

respectively. Each gap at index \(r\) is assigned to copy \(r-k\) modulo \(N\). These receiving indices are

\(2k,\quad n_1-1,\quad k\),

which lie at \(3/8,0,1/8\), respectively. Hence

\(\operatorname{PCD}(L_N)=\frac14\delta_0+\frac58\delta_{1/8}+\frac18\delta_{3/8}\).

Since RD converges to \(\mu_*\), the corresponding mixture limits are

\(\ell_H=\frac34\delta_0+\frac18\delta_{1/8}+\frac18\delta_{3/8}\),

\(\ell_L=\frac38\delta_0+\frac7{16}\delta_{1/8}+\frac3{16}\delta_{3/8}\).

Their total variation distance is \(3/8\), so they are distinct.

4.2 Extending the construction equivariantly

Let \(\mathcal O\) be the orbit of \(\mu_*\) under all circle isometries.

The stabilizer of \(\mu_*\) is trivial. Any preserving isometry must fix \(0\), the unique location with mass \(1/2\). The only circle isometries fixing \(0\) are the identity and reflection \(x\mapsto-x\); reflection does not preserve the other two support locations.

Therefore, for every society in \(\mathcal O\), there is a unique isometry transporting \(\mu_*\) to that society.

Define a fixed convention \(\mathcal R^{\mathrm{osc}}\) as follows:

  • On \(\mu_*\), use \(H_N\) when \(N\equiv1\pmod4\), and \(L_N\) when \(N\equiv3\pmod4\).
  • On \(\mathcal O\), transport these labeled laws by the unique transporting isometry.
  • Outside \(\mathcal O\), use the independent-sampling convention \(\mathcal I\).

Uniform labeling ensures permutation invariance. The unique transport ensures isometry equivariance on \(\mathcal O\); independent sampling ensures it outside \(\mathcal O\). Both count sequences converge to the required masses, so the convention is admissible.

Nevertheless,

\(Q_{4t+1}^{\mathcal R^{\mathrm{osc}}}(\mu_*)\longrightarrow\ell_H\),

\(Q_{4t+3}^{\mathcal R^{\mathrm{osc}}}(\mu_*)\longrightarrow\ell_L\).

Thus the limit along all odd population sizes does not exist.

4.3 Convention dependence even with universal existence

Define \(\mathcal R^H\) by always using \(H_N\) on \(\mathcal O\), and define \(\mathcal R^L\) by always using \(L_N\) there. Use \(\mathcal I\) outside \(\mathcal O\) in both cases.

The preceding arguments prove that both conventions are admissible and have limits for every society. Yet

\(M_{1/2,\infty}^{\mathcal R^H}(\mu_*)=\ell_H\neq\ell_L=M_{1/2,\infty}^{\mathcal R^L}(\mu_*)\).

Hence admissibility does not specify a canonical limiting lottery. For a fixed convention and society, an existing total variation limit is, of course, unique.

5. Finite-lift strategyproofness

Fix any realized labeled lift \(b\). The finite-population strategyproofness inequality holds for every labeled agent and every alternative point \(z\in G\).

This is exactly the deviation model specified in the question: one report changes after the lift is drawn, and the society is not relifted. Since the inequality holds for each realized lift separately, averaging over the lifting randomness preserves it. It also holds when the alternative report is chosen as a function of the realized lift.

Therefore finite-lift strategyproofness holds for every admissible \(\mathcal R\), regardless of whether its limiting lotteries exist.

6. Every existing limit satisfies the stronger bound

Fix a society \(\mu\), and write

\(e_N=\|\widehat\mu_N-\mu\|_1\).

For any two mass vectors \(\mu,\nu\) on the same finite support and any lottery \(\ell\), the circle’s diameter \(1/2\) gives

\(\bigl|\operatorname{SC}_\mu(\ell)-\operatorname{SC}_\nu(\ell)\bigr|\leq\frac12\|\mu-\nu\|_1\).

Applying the same bound uniformly to deterministic facility locations and then taking minima gives

\(\bigl|\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)-\operatorname{OPT}(\nu)\bigr|\leq\frac12\|\mu-\nu\|_1\).

For every realized lift, the finite-population theorem consequently yields

\(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(M_N(B_N)) \leq\frac32\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)+\frac54e_N\).

Because \(0\leq e_N\leq2\) and \(e_N\to0\) in probability, its expectation tends to zero. Explicitly, for every \(\eta>0\),

\(\mathbb E e_N\leq\eta+2\Pr(e_N>\eta)\).

Averaging the previous cost inequality therefore gives

\(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(Q_N^{\mathcal R}(\mu)) \leq\frac32\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)+\frac54\mathbb E e_N\).

If \(Q_N^{\mathcal R}(\mu)\) converges in total variation to \(\ell\), its expected bounded distance costs converge. Passing to the limit proves

\(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(\ell)\leq\frac32\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\).

The same argument applies to any convergent subsequence, even when the full sequence does not converge.

7. The exact supremum on every finite set

Assume first that \(|X|\geq2\), and choose distinct \(x,y\in X\). Let

\(\mu_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_x+\varepsilon\delta_y\),

where \(0<\varepsilon<1/2\) is binary rational. Put \(D=d(x,y)>0\).

For every admissible convention, empirical convergence implies that \(x\) has strictly more than half the lifted copies with probability tending to one. The majority property therefore gives convergence of expected PCD to \(\delta_x\). Expected RD converges to \(\mu_\varepsilon\), because empirical convergence is bounded and hence also holds in expectation.

Thus the limit exists for this society under every admissible convention, and equals

\(\ell_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon/2)\delta_x+(\varepsilon/2)\delta_y\).

The optimum is \(\varepsilon D\). Indeed, for every \(z\in G\), the triangle inequality gives

\((1-\varepsilon)d(x,z)+\varepsilon d(y,z) \geq\varepsilon D+(1-2\varepsilon)d(x,z) \geq\varepsilon D\),

with equality at \(z=x\).

The costs at \(x\) and \(y\) are \(\varepsilon D\) and \((1-\varepsilon)D\), respectively. Hence

\(\operatorname{SC}_{\mu_\varepsilon}(\ell_\varepsilon) =\varepsilon D(3/2-\varepsilon)\),

and the ratio is \(3/2-\varepsilon\). Taking \(\varepsilon=2^{-r}\) and letting \(r\to\infty\) proves the lower bound \(3/2\). Combined with Section 6, this proves

\(A_{\mathcal R}(X)=3/2\)

whenever all limits required in its definition exist.

If \(|X|=1\), every society is unanimous, so the assigned ratio is \(1\).

For a convention that lacks a limit at some society on \(X\), the displayed definition of \(A_{\mathcal R}(X)\) is not defined on its entire stated domain. If one instead takes the supremum only over societies whose limits exist, the same proof gives \(3/2\) for \(|X|\geq2\): the two-location witnesses always have limits.
