The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite tree \(G=(V,E)\), a finite category set \(B\), and rational-mass types \(t=(v_t,\succ_t,e_t)\) with strict rankings over \(B\), let \(\mu_t\) be each type's mass and set the supply of category \(b\) to \(\sum_{t:e_t=b}\mu_t\). Start with \(x_{t,e_t}=\mu_t\) and all other \(x_{t,b}=0\). A legal step chooses types \(t,s\) at adjacent nodes, categories \(b,c\) with positive assigned mass, and real \(\delta\) satisfying \(0<\delta\le\min(x_{t,b},x_{s,c})\), \(c\succ_t b\), and \(b\succ_s c\); it decreases \(x_{t,b},x_{s,c}\) by \(\delta\) and increases \(x_{t,c},x_{s,b}\) by \(\delta\). Is there an allocation reachable by finitely many such steps that is Pareto-efficient among reachable allocations under type-preserving couplings and has voting mass \(\sum_t\sum_{b\succ_t e_t}x_{t,b}\ge q\), for a given rational threshold \(q\)?

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a nonempty finite undirected tree and \(B\) a nonempty category set, with \(m=|B|\). A type \(t=(v_t,\succ_t,e_t)\) has \(v_t\in V\), a strict total ranking over \(B\), and initial category \(e_t\in B\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Set \(M=\sum_t\mu_t>0\); masses need not sum to \(1\).
Category \(b\) has supply \(s_b=\sum_{t:e_t=b}\mu_t>0\). An allocation is a real matrix \(x\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{t,b}=s_b\). Initially \(x^0_{t,e_t}=\mu_t\), with other entries zero.
A legal step chooses \(t,s,b,c\) with \(\{v_t,v_s\}\in E\), \(c\succ_t b\), \(b\succ_s c\), and real \(0<\delta\le\min(x_{t,b},x_{s,c})\); it subtracts \(\delta\) from \(x_{t,b},x_{s,c}\) and adds it to \(x_{t,c},x_{s,b}\). Allocation \(y\) dominates \(x\) under type-preserving couplings if there are real \(\gamma_{t,b,c}\ge0\) with \(\sum_c\gamma_{t,b,c}=x_{t,b}\), \(\sum_b\gamma_{t,b,c}=y_{t,c}\), and \(\gamma_{t,b,c}=0\) unless \(c=b\) or \(c\succ_t b\), such that \(\sum_{t,b,c:c\succ_t b}\gamma_{t,b,c}>0\). The following results are established below:

The fifth result identifies a stronger obstacle to welfare-maximization arguments than nonclosure alone.

For $m=1$, or for $|V|=1$, no legal step exists. The initial allocation is then efficient, and the answer is yes exactly when $q=0$. The subsequent arguments concern the general, nondegenerate case.

2. Dominance and voting mass

For each supported type $t$, write its ranking as

$$ b_{t,1}\succ_t b_{t,2}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t b_{t,m}. $$

Define

$$ H_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{t,b_{t,i}}, \qquad 1\le k<m, $$

and set $H_{t,0}(x)=0$.

Lemma 1. An upward type-preserving coupling from $x$ to $y$ exists exactly when

$$ H_{t,k}(y)\ge H_{t,k}(x) \qquad \text{for every }t,k. $$

Such a coupling moves positive mass strictly upward exactly when at least one inequality is strict.

Proof. Necessity follows because mass already in a preference prefix cannot leave that prefix under an upward coupling.

For sufficiency, fix $t$ and partition $[0,\mu_t]$ into consecutive intervals, in preference order, with lengths $x_{t,b_{t,i}}$. Construct the analogous partition for $y$. Couple categories using the lengths of intersections between their intervals.

The prefix inequalities imply that the destination category is weakly preferred to the source category almost everywhere. Indeed, if a point belongs to source category $b_{t,i}$ and destination category $b_{t,j}$ with $j>i$, then that point lies below the source prefix endpoint $H_{t,i}(x)$ but above the destination prefix endpoint $H_{t,i}(y)$, contradicting $H_{t,i}(y)\ge H_{t,i}(x)$. Interval endpoints have zero measure. The intersection lengths therefore give the required coupling.

Give category $b_{t,i}$ utility $m-i$. Then

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{m}(m-i)x_{t,b_{t,i}} = \sum_{k=1}^{m-1}H_{t,k}(x). $$

Under an upward coupling, a strictly upward transfer increases this utility by a positive amount per unit transferred. Consequently, positive strictly transferred mass is equivalent to a positive utility increase, which, under the prefix inequalities, is equivalent to at least one strict prefix inequality. $\square$

Thus dominance is expressed by the prefix inequalities together with

$$ \sum_t\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}H_{t,k}(y) > \sum_t\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}H_{t,k}(x). $$

If $e_t=b_{t,j_t}$, define total voting mass by

$$ W(x)=\sum_t H_{t,j_t-1}(x). $$

Lemma 1 shows that domination cannot decrease $W$.

Every legal step weakly increases every prefix mass and strictly increases at least one. In particular, every reachable allocation assigns each type only categories weakly preferred to its endowment.

3. Polynomial-time reachability with linear targets

3.1 Encoding the exchanges

There are

$$ D=\tau m $$

allocation coordinates. Enumerate the legal swap rules determined by supported types, adjacency, and rankings, without requiring their inputs to be currently positive. Let their number be $N$.

For each adjacent unordered pair of supported types and each unordered pair of categories, at most one mutually improving exchange exists. Therefore

$$ N=O(\tau^2m^2). $$

Let $C$ be the $D$ by $N$ matrix whose rule column contains $-1$ at its two input coordinates and $+1$ at its two output coordinates. The four coordinates are distinct. A firing of rule $j$ by amount $\delta$ changes the allocation by $\delta C_{\cdot,j}$.

Every column preserves all type masses and all category supplies.

For an allowed transition set $S$, call $S$ fireable from a marking if some finite sequence uses precisely those rules, each with positive total amount.

Fireability depends only on the positive coordinates of the starting marking. It is computed as follows: mark those coordinates; whenever both inputs of an allowed rule are marked, mark that rule and its outputs; repeat until no change occurs.

Necessity follows by examining an actual sequence in firing order. For sufficiency, fire each newly marked rule by a sufficiently small positive amount to preserve positivity of every previously positive coordinate. This constructs a finite sequence using all marked rules and retaining positivity at all their incident coordinates.

3.2 Exact finite reachability criterion

Lemma 2. An allocation $y$ is reachable from $x^0$ exactly when there exists $z\ge0$ such that

$$ y=x^0+Cz, $$

and, writing $S=\operatorname{supp}(z)$:

  • $S$ is fireable from $x^0$;
  • $S$ is fireable from $y$ after exchanging every rule’s inputs and outputs.

This is the continuous-net reachability criterion of [Fraca–Haddad, Theorem 20](https://lsv.ens-paris-saclay.fr/Publis/PAPERS/PDF/FH-fundi15.pdf). Here is a direct proof for the present unit-incidence system.

Proof. Necessity follows by taking firing totals of a legal sequence and reversing that sequence in the reversed system.

For sufficiency, the case $S=\varnothing$ gives $y=x^0$. Otherwise, the fireability construction above supplies a forward sequence whose final marking is positive at every coordinate incident to $S$. Scale its firing amounts sufficiently that its totals $\alpha$ satisfy

$$ 0<\alpha_j\le z_j/3 \qquad (j\in S). $$

Scaling preserves legality: each intermediate marking becomes a convex combination of the original intermediate marking and the starting marking. Its final marking is

$$ u=x^0+C\alpha, $$

and remains positive at every incident coordinate.

Apply the same construction in reverse from $y$, obtaining totals $\beta$ with

$$ 0<\beta_j\le z_j/3, \qquad v=y-C\beta, $$

where $v$ is positive at every incident coordinate. Put

$$ w=z-\alpha-\beta. $$

Then $w\ge0$ and $v=u+Cw$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$ be the minimum of the incident coordinates of $u$ and $v$, and put $A=\sum_jw_j$. Choose an integer $K>2A/\varepsilon$.

Execute $K$ rounds, each firing every rule $j$ by amount $w_j/K$. At the beginning of round $k$, the marking is

$$ u+\frac{k}{K}Cw, $$

whose incident coordinates are at least $\varepsilon$, because it lies on the segment between $u$ and $v$. During a round, any coordinate changes from its round-start value by at most $A/K$. Thus every input available before a firing exceeds

$$ \varepsilon-A/K>\varepsilon/2>w_j/K. $$

All firings are legal. After the rounds the marking is $v$. Finally, invert the chosen reverse sequence to go from $v$ to $y$ in the original system. This is a finite legal sequence. $\square$

3.3 Linear target constraints

Proposition 3. Let $\mathcal H$ be specified by rational linear equalities and weak or strict inequalities in $y$. Deciding whether

$$ R\cap\mathcal H\ne\varnothing $$

takes polynomial time.

Proof. Maintain an allowed rule set $U$, initially all rules. Consider

$$ z\ge0,\qquad y=x^0+C_Uz,\qquad y\ge0,\qquad y\in\mathcal H. $$

If this system is infeasible, return no.

Otherwise find a feasible pair $(z^*,y^*)$ with simultaneously maximal coordinate supports. For each coordinate, test whether it can be positive and, when possible, obtain a witness. Average these witnesses and one feasible witness. Nonnegativity ensures that the average contains every possible positive coordinate; all target constraints, including strict ones, survive averaging.

Write

$$ S=\operatorname{supp}(z^*), \qquad J=\operatorname{supp}(y^*). $$

Within $S$, compute the maximal forward-fireable subset $F$ from $x^0$ and maximal reverse-fireable subset $K$ from support $J$.

If $F=K=S$, Lemma 2 proves that $y^*$ is reachable. Otherwise replace $U$ by $F\cap K$ and repeat.

To prove completeness, suppose a reachable target has firing support $T\subseteq U$. Its state-equation witness is feasible, so maximality gives

$$ T\subseteq S, \qquad \operatorname{supp}(y)\subseteq J. $$

Its forward sequence implies $T\subseteq F$. Its reverse sequence and the final-support inclusion imply $T\subseteq K$. Thus no genuine witness is discarded.

Every unsuccessful iteration deletes at least one allowed rule. There are at most $N+1$ iterations. $\square$

Strict feasibility is handled by replacing all strict inequalities with inequalities having a common slack $\eta$, imposing $0\le\eta\le1$, and testing whether its maximum is positive.

For completeness, the relevant firing variables are bounded. Define

$$ \Phi(y)=\sum_t\sum_{i=1}^{m}(m-i)y_{t,b_{t,i}}. $$

Every rule increases $\Phi$ by at least twice its firing amount. Hence every nonnegative state-equation solution satisfies

$$ 2\sum_jz_j \le \Phi(y)-\Phi(x^0) \le (m-1)M. $$

Also, $0\le y_{t,b}\le\mu_t$. Thus the slack programs have bounded feasible regions.

If $\mathcal H$ has $h$ constraints and binary encoding length $L_{\mathcal H}$, the algorithm uses

$$ O\bigl((N+1)(N+D+1)\bigr) $$

linear programs, each with $O(N+D)$ variables, $O(N+D+h)$ constraints, and polynomial encoding length in $L+L_{\mathcal H}+N+D$. Its bit running time is therefore polynomial in

$$ m,\tau,|V|,L,L_{\mathcal H}. $$

Two consequences are immediate:

  • Voting feasibility without efficiency: use $W(y)\ge q$.
  • Verification of a specified rational allocation $x$: test reachability using $y=x$, and test inefficiency using the dominance constraints from Lemma 1.

The latter takes polynomial time in the instance length and the encoding length of $x$. It searches for dominators anywhere in $R$, rather than only among continuations from $x$.

4. Polynomial-size witnesses and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$

A polynomial-time verification procedure needs a polynomial-size rational witness. The following argument supplies it and makes the projection step explicit.

4.1 A projection coefficient bound

Consider a rational system in retained variables $u$ and eliminated variables $w$, with polynomially many variables and constraints and polynomial coefficient lengths. Equalities can be represented by two weak inequalities.

After clearing denominators, replace each strict inequality by a weak inequality with common slack $\eta>0$. The system has the form

$$ Aw\le b+Bu-d\eta, $$

where $d_i=1$ for originally strict rows and $d_i=0$ otherwise. Impose also $\eta\le1$.

The applicable Farkas alternative states that, for fixed $u,\eta$, such a $w$ exists exactly when

$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}(b+Bu-d\eta)\ge0 \qquad \text{for every }\lambda\in K, $$

where

$$ K=\{\lambda\ge0:A^{\mathsf T}\lambda=0\}. $$

The cone $K$ is polyhedral and pointed. It is enough to check its finitely many extreme rays.

Let $r$ be the number of eliminated variables. An extreme ray has support of size at most $r+1$. Indeed, on its support the kernel must be one-dimensional; otherwise a sufficiently small perturbation in two opposite kernel directions would contradict extremality. Consequently its coordinates can be chosen as integer minors of a matrix of dimension at most $r$. Determinant bounds give polynomial binary lengths for these coordinates and for

$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}(b+Bu). $$

Because $\lambda^{\mathsf T}d\ge0$, eliminating $\eta$ produces exactly:

$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}(b+Bu)\ge0 \quad \text{if }\lambda^{\mathsf T}d=0, $$

and

$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}(b+Bu)>0 \quad \text{if }\lambda^{\mathsf T}d>0. $$

For the converse direction, finitely many positive upper bounds on $\eta$ have a positive minimum, so a common $\eta\in(0,1]$ exists.

Thus a projection is described by finitely many weak and strict affine inequalities whose individual coefficient lengths are polynomially bounded. The number of inequalities need not be polynomial.

4.2 Applying the bound

Choose a firing support $S$ and allocation support $J$. When the corresponding forward and reverse fireability tests pass, allocations with these supports are exactly the projection of

$$ x=x^0+C_Sz, \qquad z_j>0\quad(j\in S), $$

$$ x_i>0\quad(i\in J), \qquad x_i=0\quad(i\notin J). $$

Including the empty firing support covers $x^0$. Therefore $R$ is a finite union of projections of systems having polynomially many variables and constraints.

Similarly, the set of feasible allocations having a reachable dominator is a finite union of projections: choose supports for $y$ and its firing vector, and add the dominance inequalities relating $y$ to $x$.

The preceding coefficient bound shows that the set of successful witnesses,

$$ R\cap\{x:W(x)\ge q\} \setminus \{x:\text{some reachable allocation dominates }x\}, $$

is a Boolean combination of finitely many affine predicates with uniformly polynomial coefficient lengths.

Suppose it contains a real point $x^*$. Record the sign of every affine expression occurring in those predicates at $x^*$. The corresponding conjunction of equalities and strict inequalities is nonempty, and all its points have the same predicate truth values.

Intersect this conjunction with

$$ 0\le x_{t,b}\le\mu_t. $$

Replace its strict inequalities by a common slack $\eta$, impose $0\le\eta\le1$, and maximize $\eta$. The feasible set is compact, and the optimum is positive: finitely many strictly positive margins at $x^*$ permit a positive common slack.

An optimal vertex is determined by at most $D+1$ independent active constraints. Cramer’s rule and determinant bounds therefore give polynomial numerator and denominator lengths. This bound depends on the dimension and coefficient lengths, not on the number of predicates.

Its allocation component is a polynomial-size rational successful witness.

Theorem 4. The full decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

Proof. Guess the rational allocation just established. Check voting mass, reachability, and absence of a reachable dominator using Proposition 3. All checks take polynomial time in $m,\tau,|V|,L$. $\square$

5. An algebraic normal form for a fixed pair of types

This lemma concerns firing totals. It does not assert that the transformed totals can be scheduled legally.

Lemma 5. For any nonnegative firing vector, its rules involving a fixed pair of types $t,s$ can be replaced by nonnegative firing totals with the same aggregate allocation change such that no category is transferred both ways between that pair.

The minimum possible total firing amount for that pair, given its aggregate change, is

$$ \frac12\sum_{b\in B}|d_b|, $$

where $d_b$ is the net amount of category $b$ transferred from $t$ to $s$.

Proof. Represent a rule in which $t$ gives $b$ and receives $c$ by an arc $b\to c$. Such an arc satisfies

$$ c\succ_t b, \qquad b\succ_s c. $$

This category digraph is acyclic, since every arc strictly improves $t$’s ranking.

Interpret the firing totals as a nonnegative flow on this digraph. Its divergence at category $b$ is $d_b$. Add a source supplying $d_b$ when $d_b>0$ and a sink receiving $-d_b$ when $d_b<0$. The augmented flow decomposes into finitely many source-to-sink paths: repeatedly subtract the minimum positive flow along a path, thereby eliminating at least one positive arc.

A category path

$$ b_0\to b_1\to\cdots\to b_k $$

can be replaced by the single rule $b_0\to b_k$. Transitivity gives

$$ b_k\succ_t b_0, \qquad b_0\succ_s b_k, $$

so this rule is legal as a rule. Its column is the sum of the path’s columns, because intermediate category changes telescope at both types.

After all replacements, only categories with positive divergence are sent by $t$, and only categories with negative divergence are received. The total firing amount is

$$ \sum_{b:d_b>0}d_b = \frac12\sum_b|d_b|. $$

Every representation must transfer at least $d_b$ units outward for each $d_b>0$, proving minimality. $\square$

This simplifies the state equation but leaves the forward and reverse scheduling requirements intact.

6. A broader polynomial-time case: two attainable categories per type

For each type $t$, define its attainable category set by

$$ A_t=\{b:\text{some }x\in R\text{ has }x_{t,b}>0\}. $$

The forward support-closure computation in Section 3 computes all $A_t$: its marked coordinates are exactly those that can become positive. The preserving-positivity construction proves attainability of every marked coordinate, and an actual sequence cannot create an unmarked coordinate.

Theorem 6. If

$$ |A_t|\le2 \qquad \text{for every supported type }t, $$

then the full decision problem is polynomial-time solvable.

In particular, this applies whenever every endowment is among its type’s top two categories.

Proof. Each $A_t$ contains $e_t$, and all its other categories are strictly preferred to $e_t$. A type with a second attainable category therefore has

$$ A_t=\{e_t,f_t\}, \qquad f_t\succ_t e_t. $$

Call such types active. Other types remain fixed.

An active type can exchange only initial-category mass for $f_t$. Mass already assigned $f_t$ cannot participate: a further improvement would produce a third attainable category. Consequently, two active types $t,s$ can trade exactly when they are adjacent and

$$ f_t=e_s, \qquad f_s=e_t. $$

Bipartition the tree. Construct a flow network with:

  • a source arc of capacity $\mu_t$ to each active type on the first side;
  • an arc of capacity $\mu_s$ from each active type on the second side to the sink;
  • an arc of capacity $M$ for every compatible adjacent pair.

Let $F^*$ be the maximum flow value.

Every legal sequence yields a feasible flow by recording the amount exchanged by each compatible pair. Improved mass never moves again, so vertex capacities hold. Each exchanged unit creates two units of voting mass. Therefore

$$ W(x)\le2F^* \qquad (x\in R). $$

Conversely, implement any feasible flow by performing its pair exchanges in any order. At each type, the total amount assigned to its incident arcs is at most its initial mass; thus every exchange has sufficient unused initial-category mass. All exchanges are legal. This gives a reachable allocation with voting mass twice the flow value.

Finally, strict domination between reachable allocations strictly increases voting mass. Each active type has only the categories $e_t,f_t$, so an upward coupling strictly improves mass exactly by moving some mass from $e_t$ to $f_t$. Inactive types are fixed.

A maximum-flow allocation consequently cannot have a reachable dominator. It is efficient, and the decision criterion is

$$ 2F^*\ge q. $$

If every endowment is among its type’s top two choices, individual rationality already restricts $A_t$ to at most those two categories, verifying the stated sufficient condition. $\square$

Running time

Computing the attainable sets takes

$$ O(\tau^2m^2+|V|) $$

elementary operations using rule enumeration and support closure.

If $K$ compatible pairs exist, the flow network has $O(\tau)$ vertices and $O(\tau+K)$ arcs, with $K\le\tau^2$. Edmonds–Karp takes

$$ O\bigl(\tau(\tau+K)^2\bigr) $$

arithmetic operations.

Scaling by the product of the input denominators gives integer capacities with $O(L+\log\tau)$ bits. The total bit running time is therefore bounded by

$$ O\!\left( \left( |V|+\tau^2m^2+\tau(\tau+K)^2 \right)\operatorname{poly}(L) \right). $$

Under the directly given top-two-endowment condition, attainable-set computation can be omitted, giving

$$ O\!\left( \left( |V|+\tau m+\tau^2+\tau(\tau+K)^2 \right)\operatorname{poly}(L) \right). $$

The two-category result follows as a special case.

7. Efficient allocations can approach an unattained welfare optimum

Use the two-vertex tree with vertices $\ell,r$, five categories, and five mass-one types:

| Type | Vertex | Endowment | Ranking |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $\ell$ | $c_4$ | $c_1\succ h\succ c_4\succ c_2\succ c_3$ |
| $2$ | $r$ | $c_1$ | $c_2\succ h\succ c_1\succ c_3\succ c_4$ |
| $3$ | $\ell$ | $c_2$ | $c_3\succ h\succ c_2\succ c_4\succ c_1$ |
| $4$ | $r$ | $c_3$ | $c_4\succ h\succ c_3\succ c_1\succ c_2$ |
| $5$ | $r$ | $h$ | $c_4\succ h\succ c_1\succ c_2\succ c_3$ |

Every category has supply $1$.

Let $y$ assign categories $c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4,h$ in full to types $1,2,3,4,5$, respectively.

7.1 Reachable approximations and nonreachability

Initially, only types $1,5$ can exchange. The other adjacent pairs reject their initial-category exchange:

  • type $2$ rejects exchanging $c_1$ for type $1$’s $c_4$;
  • type $1$ rejects exchanging $c_4$ for type $4$’s $c_3$;
  • type $3$ rejects exchanging $c_2$ for type $2$’s $c_1$;
  • type $4$ rejects exchanging $c_3$ for type $3$’s $c_2$;
  • type $5$ rejects exchanging $h$ for type $3$’s $c_2$.

Thus every nonempty legal sequence gives type $5$ positive mass of its top category $c_4$. That mass can never leave type $5$. Since $y_{5,c_4}=0$, we have $y\notin R$.

For a positive integer $n$, put $\delta=1/n$. First exchange $\delta$ between types $1,5$, giving $h$ to type $1$. Define the cycle:

  • type $1$ exchanges $h$ for type $2$’s $c_1$;
  • type $2$ exchanges $h$ for type $3$’s $c_2$;
  • type $3$ exchanges $h$ for type $4$’s $c_3$;
  • type $4$ exchanges $h$ for type $1$’s $c_4$.

Each exchange has amount $\delta$ and is legal by the rankings.

Execute exactly $n-1$ complete cycles, followed by the first three exchanges of the cycle. For $n=1$, there are no complete cycles.

Before complete cycle $k$, type $1$ has $1-k\delta$ units of $c_4$, so its fourth exchange is available for every $k\le n-1$. Types $2,3,4$ likewise retain enough initial-category mass for their exchanges. After the complete cycles, all three final exchanges have exactly the required remaining initial mass.

The resulting allocation $x^{(n)}$ gives types $1,2,3$ their top categories in full and satisfies

$$ x^{(n)}_{4,c_4}=1-\delta, \qquad x^{(n)}_{4,h}=\delta, $$

$$ x^{(n)}_{5,c_4}=\delta, \qquad x^{(n)}_{5,h}=1-\delta. $$

Therefore $x^{(n)}\in R$ and $x^{(n)}\to y$, proving nonclosure.

7.2 Every approximating allocation is globally efficient

Proposition 7. Each $x^{(n)}$ is Pareto-efficient among all feasible allocations.

Proof. In any weak dominator, types $1,2,3$ must retain their top categories in full. These exhaust the supplies of $c_1,c_2,c_3$, leaving only $c_4,h$ for types $4,5$.

Their top-category prefix inequalities require

$$ z_{4,c_4}\ge1-\delta, \qquad z_{5,c_4}\ge\delta. $$

The supply of $c_4$ is $1$, so both inequalities are equalities. Their row sums then fix their $h$ masses as well. Hence every weak dominator equals $x^{(n)}$, and strict domination is impossible. $\square$

The same argument with $\delta=0$ proves that $y$ is globally efficient.

7.3 A positive welfare objective has no reachable maximizer

Let $u_t(b)$ be the rank utility taking values $4,3,2,1,0$, and put

$$ U_t(z)=\sum_bu_t(b)z_{t,b}. $$

Consider the positive weighted objective

$$ \Psi(z)=U_1(z)+U_2(z)+U_3(z)+2U_4(z)+U_5(z). $$

At $y$,

$$ \Psi(y)=23. $$

For every feasible $z$,

$$ U_i(z)\le4 \qquad (i=1,2,3), $$

$$ U_4(z)\le3+z_{4,c_4}, \qquad U_5(z)\le3+z_{5,c_4}. $$

Consequently,

$$ 23-\Psi(z) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{3}(4-U_i(z)) + 2(1-z_{4,c_4})-z_{5,c_4}. $$

Supply feasibility gives $1-z_{4,c_4}\ge z_{5,c_4}$. The right-hand side is therefore nonnegative.

Equality forces $z_{5,c_4}=0$, $z_{4,c_4}=1$, and $U_i(z)=4$ for $i=1,2,3$. Strict rankings then force the first four types to receive their top categories in full, leaving $h$ for type $5$. Thus $y$ is the unique feasible maximizer of $\Psi$.

But

$$ \Psi(x^{(n)})=23-\frac1n. $$

Hence

$$ \sup_{x\in R}\Psi(x)=23, $$

and this supremum is not attained.

This example has reachable efficient allocations, but maximizing a fixed positive weighted welfare objective need not produce one.

8. Remaining gap

A sufficient missing statement is:

Every $x\in R$ has an efficient $y\in R$ whose preference-prefix masses are all at least those of $x$.

If proved, this would combine with voting monotonicity and Proposition 3 to give a polynomial-time algorithm for the full problem. The example above neither proves nor refutes that statement.

Alternatively, a hardness proof must control fractional exchanges and efficiency within the entire reachable set. The discrete hardness results do not supply that control by themselves.

Thus the general classification remains open in this write-up; the proved upper bound is membership in $\mathrm{NP}$.
