The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) goods, \(N\), and a finite list of complete additive valuation types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each good \(j\) has supply \(1/N\), decide whether there is an allocation assigning each unit of mass an integral bundle \(B\subseteq[m]\) that is ex-post envy-free and attains the maximum social welfare over all supply-feasible allocations; if so, compute one, otherwise report infeasibility. State complexity in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the binary input length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).
The input includes integers \(m,N\geq1\), encoded in binary, and complete additive valuation vectors \(v_t\in\mathbb{Q}^m\cap[0,1]^m\). Each good has supply \(1/N\); \(L\) is the total binary input length.
An allocation specifies masses \(x_{t,B}\geq0\) receiving integral bundles \(B\subseteq[m]\), including the empty bundle, with \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\). Types may split their mass among bundles.
All supply must be allocated: \(\sum_t\sum_{B\ni j}x_{t,B}=1/N\) for every good \(j\).
Ex-post envy-freeness means \(v_t(B)\geq v_t(C)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(x_{s,C}>0\), including \(s=t\); indifference is allowed.
Social welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}v_t(B)\). Its benchmark is the maximum over all supply-feasible allocations, without an envy-freeness requirement.

Is deciding whether an ex-post envy-free allocation attains this unrestricted welfare maximum in \(\mathsf{P}\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? If it is polynomial-time solvable, can such an allocation also be computed in polynomial time? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), accounting for the binary encoding of \(N\).

For discrete random instances, a sampling algorithm gives an envy-free, welfare-maximizing online allocation with high probability when \(m=\Omega(n\log n)\), under independent, nondegenerate finite-support per-good valuations in \([0,1]\) containing \(1\), with fixed distribution bounds. [Kale, Kulkarni, and Garg](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17238v1#S6).
No result settling exactly the continuous problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, already when \(\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=1\), and \(N=2\). Thus a polynomial-time decision algorithm—or an algorithm that decides feasibility and computes a qualifying allocation when one exists—would imply \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).

The hardness persists with strictly positive dyadic valuations. It therefore does not arise from a large binary-encoded \(N\).

1. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the standard \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary-encoded integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), decide whether some \(B\subseteq[k]\) satisfies
\(\sum_{j\in B}a_j=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^k a_j\).
Its \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness is established in [Karp’s original paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let \(H\) be the smallest power of two at least \(\max_j a_j\). Construct the society with

  • \(m=k\), \(N=2\), and one type of mass \(\mu_1=1\);
  • valuations \(v_1(j)=a_j/H\).

These valuations belong to \((0,1]\) and are dyadic rationals. If the PARTITION input has length \(\ell\), the constructed instance has length \(O(k\ell)\), and the construction takes polynomial time.

Write \(S=\sum_{j=1}^k a_j\).

Every supply-feasible allocation has the same welfare. Indeed, using additivity and the supply equalities,
\[ \]
\(W(x)=\sum_B x_{1,B}\sum_{j\in B}\frac{a_j}{H} =\sum_{j=1}^k\frac{a_j}{H}\sum_{B\ni j}x_{1,B} =\frac{S}{2H}.\)

Supply-feasible allocations exist: assign mass \(1/2\) to \([k]\) and mass \(1/2\) to the empty bundle. Consequently, the unrestricted welfare maximum is \(S/(2H)\), and every supply-feasible allocation attains it.

An envy-free allocation implies a partition. Suppose \(x\) is ex-post envy-free. For any two used bundles \(B,C\), the envy inequalities apply in both directions, including within the same type. Hence
\(v_1(B)\geq v_1(C)\) and \(v_1(C)\geq v_1(B)\), so all used bundles have one common value \(u\).

Because their masses sum to one,
\(W(x)=\sum_B x_{1,B}u=u\).
The preceding calculation therefore gives \(u=S/(2H)\). At least one bundle \(B\) is used, and it satisfies
\(\sum_{j\in B}a_j=H v_1(B)=S/2\).
Thus the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

A partition gives a qualifying allocation. Conversely, suppose \(B\subseteq[k]\) has sum \(S/2\). Assign mass \(1/2\) to \(B\) and mass \(1/2\) to its complement. Every good receives total mass \(1/2\), and both bundles have value \(S/(2H)\). The allocation is ex-post envy-free and attains the unrestricted welfare maximum.

This proves the equivalence and establishes \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness.

2. The unrestricted benchmark is computable in polynomial time

For the general instance, put \(q=1/N\) and define the marginal allocation
\(y_{t,j}=\sum_{B\ni j}x_{t,B}\).
Every feasible allocation satisfies
\(0\leq y_{t,j}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,j}=q\).

Conversely, every matrix satisfying these conditions is realizable by a bundle allocation. Since \(\mu_t>0\), define \(p_{t,j}=y_{t,j}/\mu_t\), and set
\(x_{t,B}=\mu_t\prod_{j\in B}p_{t,j}\prod_{j\notin B}(1-p_{t,j})\).
The product distribution has total mass \(\mu_t\) and the prescribed good marginals.

Therefore, the unrestricted maximum is
\(W^*=\sum_{j=1}^m\max\{\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}v_t(j)y_t:\ 0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t,\ \sum_t y_t=q\}\).

For each good, sort types in decreasing order of \(v_t(j)\), then allocate its supply greedily, up to each type’s capacity \(\mu_t\). To justify optimality, if a lower-valued type receives positive mass while a higher-valued type has unused capacity, transferring mass between them preserves feasibility and increases welfare. Ties permit transfers without changing welfare. These transfers transform an optimum into the greedy allocation.

Computing \(W^*\) takes \(O(m\tau\log(\tau+1))\) exact rational operations and \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit time. The relevant rational numbers have polynomial encoding length: marginal quantities are sums and differences of the input masses and \(1/N\), and welfare adds their products with input valuations. The dependence on \(N\) is through its binary encoding length \(\log N\), already included in \(L\).

3. Polynomial-size certificates

Although there are exponentially many possible bundles, every yes-instance has a qualifying allocation using at most \(m+\tau\) type–bundle pairs, with polynomial-size rational masses.

Support reduction. Start with any qualifying allocation and let \(S\) be its positive support. Form a matrix \(A\) with one column for each pair \((t,B)\in S\). The column contains the type indicator \(e_t\) followed by the good-incidence vector \(\mathbf{1}_B\). Feasibility is precisely
\(Ax=b\), where \(b=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau,q,\ldots,q)\).

If the columns are dependent, choose a nonzero vector \(d\) with \(Ad=0\). The type rows imply \(\sum_i d_i=0\), so \(d\) has both positive and negative coordinates. Because every supported coordinate of \(x\) is positive, both \(x+\varepsilon d\) and \(x-\varepsilon d\) remain nonnegative for sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\).

Let \(c_{t,B}=v_t(B)\) be the welfare coefficients. Necessarily \(c^\mathsf{T}d=0\): otherwise one of these two feasible perturbations would have welfare greater than \(W^*\).

Now choose \(\varepsilon=\min_{i:d_i>0}x_i/d_i\) and replace \(x\) by \(x-\varepsilon d\). This preserves feasibility and welfare, and removes at least one positive coordinate. It also preserves envy-freeness, because every remaining type–bundle pair belonged to the original support.

Repeating this operation leaves linearly independent columns. Thus the final support size is \(K\leq m+\tau\).

Rational encoding length. Choose \(K\) rows forming a nonsingular square submatrix \(M\) of these columns. Let \(Q\) be \(N\) times the product of the denominators of the input masses. Then \(\log Q=O(L)\), and every entry of \(Qb\) is an integer between zero and \(Q\).

Cramer’s rule expresses each surviving mass as
\(x_i=\det(M_i)/(Q\det M)\),
where \(M_i\) replaces column \(i\) of \(M\) by the corresponding entries of \(Qb\). Since \(M\) has zero–one entries, the determinant expansion gives
\(|\det M|\leq K!\) and \(|\det M_i|\leq QK!\).
Each mass therefore has encoding length
\(O(L+K\log(K+1))\).

A certificate lists these \(K\) type–bundle pairs and their positive rational masses. A verifier checks:

  • every type-mass equality and every supply equality;
  • every ordered envy inequality between listed pairs;
  • equality of the allocation’s welfare with the polynomial-time computable \(W^*\).

These checks take \(O(mK^2+m\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational operations on numbers of polynomial encoding length, hence \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit time.

The problem consequently belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\). Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness.

4. Computation and the random-instance result

Qualifying allocations always have polynomial-size explicit representations, as proved above. Nevertheless, a general polynomial-time algorithm returning such an allocation or correctly reporting that none exists would decide this \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete problem.

The [cited sampling result](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17238v1#S6) concerns high-probability success under specified random-instance assumptions. Those assumptions do not restrict the worst-case societies allowed here, so that result is compatible with this hardness classification.
