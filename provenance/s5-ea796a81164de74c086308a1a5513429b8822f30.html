The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite supported type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s ranking of partner types and being unmatched, and a feasible incumbent matching recorded by masses \(a_{t,u}\), decide whether an atomless rematching can make every agent weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. Equivalently, maximize strictly improved mass over transfers \(y_{t,u,v}\) from incumbent partner type \(u\) to new partner type \(v\), subject to preserving incumbent row masses, permitting only weakly preferred assignments, and balancing the two sides of every cross-type match; efficiency holds exactly when the optimum is zero.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type comprises an atomless population.
Let \(S=T\cup\{\bot\}\), where \(\bot\) means unmatched. Each type \(t\) has a strict total ranking \(\succ_t\) of \(S\); partners of the same type are permitted and distinct from being unmatched. Partners ranked below \(\bot\) are permitted.
The incumbent is specified by nonnegative binary rational endpoint masses \(a_{t,u}\), for \(t\in T,u\in S\), satisfying \(\sum_{u\in S}a_{t,u}=\mu_t\) and \(a_{t,u}=a_{u,t}\) for distinct \(t,u\in T\). Within-type pairs have pair mass \(a_{t,t}/2\).
An admissible rematching is specified by divisible real masses \(y_{t,u,v}\geq0\): the mass of type \(t\) moving from incumbent outcome \(u\) to outcome \(v\). Require \(\sum_{v\in S}y_{t,u,v}=a_{t,u}\), \(y_{t,u,v}=0\) unless \(v\succeq_t u\), and \(\sum_{u\in S}y_{t,u,v}=\sum_{u\in S}y_{v,u,t}\) for distinct \(t,v\in T\). Within-type endpoint masses are paired internally; unmatched masses require no counterpart.
A Pareto improvement exists when such a rematching satisfies \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{u,v\in S:\,v\succ_t u}y_{t,u,v}>0\); the incumbent is efficient otherwise.

Is deciding whether a Pareto improvement exists in this atomless roommates model in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m=|T|=\tau\) and the total input bit length \(L\).

For finite societies with strict preferences, allowing unmatched agents and unacceptable incumbent partners, efficiency can be checked in \(O(n^2)\) time ([Kuwahara](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16960v2)). With only mutually acceptable pairs permitted, the discrete problem has an \(O(e)\) algorithm, where \(e\) counts acceptable pairs ([Abraham–Manlove](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dabraham/papers/am04.pdf)).
No result settling this exact atomless decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total mass to one, since the continuous question did not specify normalization.
  • Use strict total rankings, as in the discrete definition; distinguish the unmatched outcome from a partner of one's own type.
  • Permit within-type matches between distinct agents and count diagonal incumbent entries as endpoint masses, twice their pair masses.
  • Make incumbent masses nonnegative binary rationals and specify their row-sum and cross-type symmetry conditions.
  • Permit unacceptable partners and transitions to or from being unmatched, preserving the discrete model's unrestricted matching domain.
  • Allow arbitrary real subdivision of incumbent outcome groups; impose counterpart balance only on matched partner types.
  • Take the support to be nonempty without requiring three types: the discrete lower bound on the number of individuals does not restrict an atomless society's support size.
  • Set m equal to the number of supported partner types, hence m equals τ, and let L include the entire encoded input.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). It can be solved by an exact rational linear program with \(\Theta(m^2)\) variables and constraints, in \((m+L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

The proof below includes an explicit bound that converts strict improvement into an ordinary, non-strict rational feasibility test. It applies to within-type partners, unmatched outcomes, and partners ranked below unmatched.

1. Characterizing admissible final masses

Put \(q=m+1\). For each type \(t\), list its outcomes from best to worst:

\(s_{t,1}\succ_t s_{t,2}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t s_{t,q}\).

Let \(x_{t,v}\) denote the final endpoint mass of type \(t\) assigned outcome \(v\). Define cumulative incumbent and final masses by

\(A_{t,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{t,s_{t,i}}\), and \(X_{t,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{t,s_{t,i}}\),

with \(A_{t,0}=X_{t,0}=0\).

Lemma. Final masses \(x\) arise from an admissible rematching if and only if they satisfy:

  • \(x_{t,v}\geq0\) and \(\sum_{v\in S}x_{t,v}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\).
  • \(x_{t,v}=x_{v,t}\) for distinct \(t,v\in T\).
  • \(X_{t,k}\geq A_{t,k}\) for every \(t\) and \(1\leq k\leq m\).

Proof of necessity. Given an admissible \(y\), set \(x_{t,v}=\sum_u y_{t,u,v}\). The first two conditions follow from the row-mass and endpoint-balance constraints.

If an incumbent outcome is among the best \(k\) outcomes, every weakly preferred destination is also among those outcomes. Consequently,

\(X_{t,k}=\sum_i\sum_{j\leq k}y_{t,s_{t,i},s_{t,j}}\geq\sum_{i\leq k}\sum_j y_{t,s_{t,i},s_{t,j}}=A_{t,k}\).

Proof of sufficiency. Fix \(t\). Partition the interval \((0,\mu_t]\) using the incumbent and final cumulative masses:

\(I_{t,i}=(A_{t,i-1},A_{t,i}]\), and \(J_{t,j}=(X_{t,j-1},X_{t,j}]\).

Define

\(y_{t,s_{t,i},s_{t,j}}=\operatorname{length}(I_{t,i}\cap J_{t,j})\).

Because both collections partition the same interval, these masses have the required incumbent row sums and final column sums.

If \(j>i\), then \(i\leq m\), and

\(X_{t,j-1}\geq X_{t,i}\geq A_{t,i}\).

Thus \(J_{t,j}\) starts at or beyond the right endpoint of \(I_{t,i}\), so their intersection has zero length. Positive transitions therefore satisfy \(j\leq i\), exactly the weak-preference requirement.

Finally, the symmetry of \(x\) supplies every required balance between distinct types. Within-type and unmatched masses require no additional balance constraints under the stated model. This proves sufficiency. \(\square\)

2. Detecting strict improvement

Assign integer rank scores

\(r_t(s_{t,i})=q-i\),

and define

\(W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{v\in S}r_t(v)x_{t,v}\).

These scores are used only to detect improvement; they impose no additional preference assumptions.

For any admissible rematching \(y\) with final masses \(x\), the row and column sums give

\(W(x)-W(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}(i-j)y_{t,s_{t,i},s_{t,j}}\).

Every positive transition has \(j\leq i\). Its contribution is zero when \(j=i\), and is an integer between \(1\) and \(m\) when \(j<i\). Writing \(I(y)\) for the strict-improvement mass specified in the question, we therefore obtain

\(I(y)\leq W(x)-W(a)\leq mI(y)\).

Hence \(I(y)>0\) if and only if \(W(x)>W(a)\).

Equivalently, expanding the rank scores gives

\(W(x)-W(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(X_{t,k}-A_{t,k})\).

Every summand is nonnegative by the lemma.

It follows that the target decision is exactly whether the maximum of \(W(x)\), subject to the lemma’s constraints, exceeds \(W(a)\).

3. The polynomial-size linear program

Use one variable for each unordered pair of distinct types, one within-type endpoint variable per type, and one unmatched variable per type. Thus the number of variables is

\(d=\binom{m}{2}+2m=\frac{m(m+3)}{2}\).

An unordered-pair variable supplies both \(x_{t,v}\) and \(x_{v,t}\), enforcing symmetry automatically.

The constraints are:

  • \(d\) nonnegativity inequalities;
  • \(m\) type-mass equalities;
  • \(m^2\) cumulative inequalities \(X_{t,k}\geq A_{t,k}\).

The incumbent \(x=a\) is feasible. Every variable lies between \(0\) and \(1\), because it is a nonnegative term in a type-mass equation and \(\mu_t\leq1\). Thus the feasible polytope is nonempty and compact.

All constraint coefficients are integers in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), after orienting the inequalities. In these variables, the objective coefficients are integers of absolute value at most \(2m\): a distinct-type pair contributes the rank scores of both endpoints.

There are no integrality requirements.

4. An exact rational test for positivity

We now justify the strict comparison without relying on numerical tolerance.

Let \(D\) be the least common multiple of the denominators of all input masses. Every constraint right-hand side, and \(W(a)\), has denominator dividing \(D\). Since the total binary input length is \(L\),

\(D\leq2^L\).

A linear functional on a nonempty compact polytope attains its maximum at a vertex. Let \(z\) be a maximizing vertex in the \(d\) independent variables.

At a vertex, the active constraint normals span the full variable space. Indeed, otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving every constraint, contradicting extremality. We can therefore select \(d\) independent active constraints and write

\(Bz=b\),

where \(B\) is an invertible integer matrix with entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and \(Db\) is integral.

Cramer’s rule shows that each coordinate of \(z\) has a representation with denominator \(D|\det B|\). Because the objective coefficients are integral and \(DW(a)\) is integral, a positive objective difference satisfies

\(W(z)-W(a)\geq\frac{1}{D|\det B|}\).

The determinant expansion contains \(d!\) terms, each of absolute value at most \(1\). Hence

\(|\det B|\leq d!\),

and therefore

\(W(z)-W(a)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad W(z)-W(a)\geq\frac{1}{2^Ld!}\).

Set \(\varepsilon=1/(2^Ld!)\). A Pareto improvement exists if and only if the preceding constraints together with

\(W(x)\geq W(a)+\varepsilon\)

are feasible. Necessity follows by choosing a maximizing vertex and applying the gap bound. Sufficiency follows from Section 2 and the coupling constructed in Section 1.

The encoding length of \(\varepsilon\) is \(O(L+d\log(d+1))\), so this is an ordinary rational feasibility problem of polynomial size.

5. Running time and conclusion

There are \(\Theta(m^2)\) variables and constraints. Each cumulative right-hand side is a sum of input rationals and has \(O(L)\) bits: its denominator divides \(D\), and its value lies between \(0\) and \(1\).

Including the improvement constraint, a dense encoding of the linear program has length

\(B_{\mathrm{LP}}=O(m^4+m^2L)\).

Its construction takes polynomial time in \(m+L\). Exact rational linear-programming feasibility is decidable in polynomial time in its binary encoding length, by the polynomial-time LP theorem. [Khachiyan, *A polynomial algorithm in linear programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

Consequently, for some universal constant \(C\), the entire decision procedure takes

\(O((m^4+m^2L)^C)\)

bit operations. In particular, its running time is \((m+L)^{O(1)}\), and hence \(L^{O(1)}\) under the explicit input representation.

This completely settles the stated atomless decision problem: it is polynomial-time solvable. No expansion into a population whose size depends on mass denominators is needed.
