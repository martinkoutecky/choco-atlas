The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), a symmetric graph \(H=(T,F)\) with \(\deg_H(t)=4\) for every \(t\in T\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether the population can be divided into exactly three nonempty coalitions while keeping every enemy pair apart. Equivalently, is there a rational matrix \(x_{tj}\geq 0\), for \(t\in T\) and \(j\in\{1,2,3\}\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tj}>0\) for each \(j\), and, for every \(\{t,u\}\in F\) and \(j\), at least one of \(x_{tj},x_{uj}\) equal to zero?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a finite list \(T\) of \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input includes a simple undirected enemy graph \(H=(T,F)\) with \(\deg_H(t)=4\) for every \(t\in T\). There are no friendships; all pairs outside \(F\), including individuals of the same type, are neutral. Mass of a type may be split among coalitions.
A feasible division into exactly three coalitions is a matrix \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{T\times\{1,2,3\}}\) satisfying \(\sum_{j=1}^{3}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{tj}>0\) for every \(j\), and \(x_{tj}=0\) or \(x_{uj}=0\) for every \(\{t,u\}\in F\) and every \(j\). There are no further coalition size constraints.
Let \(m=|F|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding whether such a feasible division exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The discrete problem of finding exactly three nonempty individually rational coalitions under friend-oriented preferences is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with symmetric enmities and exactly four enemies per agent; hardness holds without friendships. In that restriction, individual rationality requires every coalition to contain no enemy pair.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Allowing a type’s mass to split among coalitions does not change feasibility.

We use the discrete \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness theorem supplied in the question.

1. Equivalence with a discrete partition

A proper three-coloring of \(H\) is a map \(c:T\to\{1,2,3\}\) satisfying \(c(t)\ne c(u)\) for every \(\{t,u\}\in F\). Its nonempty color classes are independent sets.

Because \(H\) is simple and every vertex has degree four, \(\tau\geq5\). Consequently, any proper coloring using at most three colors can be converted into one using exactly three colors. Indeed, if there are \(q<3\) nonempty color classes, then \(\tau>q\), so some class contains at least two vertices. Move one vertex from that class to an unused color. The original class remains nonempty, and the new singleton class is independent. Repeat until all three colors are used.

Claim. A feasible division exists if and only if \(T\) can be partitioned into exactly three nonempty independent sets.

Proof. Suppose \(x\) is feasible. For every \(t\), positivity of \(\mu_t\) and the equation \(\sum_{j=1}^{3}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) imply that at least one entry \(x_{tj}\) is positive. Choose one such index as \(c(t)\).

For any enemy edge \(\{t,u\}\), the equality \(c(t)=c(u)=j\) would imply both \(x_{tj}>0\) and \(x_{uj}>0\), contrary to feasibility. Thus \(c\) is a proper coloring using at most three colors. The refinement above gives exactly three nonempty independent sets.

Conversely, let \(T=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_2\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_3\) be such a partition. Define \(x_{tj}=\mu_t\) when \(t\in C_j\), and \(x_{tj}=0\) otherwise. These entries are nonnegative rationals and have the required row sums. Every coalition has positive mass because its class is nonempty and every \(\mu_t\) is positive. Independence of each class verifies every enemy constraint. Thus \(x\) is feasible. \(\square\)

In particular, the numerical values of the positive masses have no effect on the decision.

2. Polynomial reduction and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

In the stated discrete restriction, an individually rational coalition is precisely an independent set of the enemy graph. Thus the supplied theorem establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of partitioning a simple four-regular enemy graph into exactly three nonempty independent sets.

Given such a discrete instance with vertices \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\), preserve its enemy graph and assign positive dyadic masses as follows. Set \(k=\lceil\log_2\tau\rceil\), and take

\(\mu_{t_i}=2^{-k}\) for \(1\leq i<\tau\), and \(\mu_{t_\tau}=(2^k-\tau+1)2^{-k}\).

The final numerator is positive because \(2^k\geq\tau\). The masses sum to one, and each has an encoding of length \(O(\log\tau)\). This construction therefore works even if “binary rational” is interpreted specifically as a dyadic rational.

The equivalence proved above shows that this reduction preserves both yes-instances and no-instances. It also preserves simplicity, degree four, and the absence of friendships. Hence the target problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

For membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), a certificate assigns one of three labels to each type. Check that all three labels occur and that every enemy edge has differently labeled endpoints. By Section 1, this certificate exists exactly when a feasible division exists. Its length is \(O(\tau)\), and verification is polynomial. Therefore the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

3. Running times and encoding bounds

Counting degrees gives \(2m=4\tau\), hence \(m=2\tau\).

On a standard word RAM, after reading the promised valid input, certificate verification takes \(O(m+\tau)\) time; including input reading, the bound is \(O(L+m+\tau)\). Converting a verified partition into a rational feasible matrix takes \(O(L+\tau)\) time by copying each mass into its assigned column.

If the discrete source has encoding length \(L_{\mathrm{disc}}\), the reduction takes \(O(L_{\mathrm{disc}}+\tau\log\tau)\) bit operations using direct copying and binary arithmetic. With an edge-list encoding, the resulting input length is \(L=O((m+\tau)\log\tau)\).

For completeness, exhaustive enumeration of the \(3^\tau\) labelings decides feasibility in \(O(L+(m+\tau)3^\tau)\) word-RAM time. This is an upper bound, not a polynomial-time algorithm.
