Corollary B.2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates with a fixed tie-breaking order, a scale \(q\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on a listed set \(T\subseteq\Pi_C\), where \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/q\) and \(\sum_{\pi\in T}n_\pi=q\), run STV by eliminating the candidate with least first-place mass among the remaining candidates. Define \(d(\mu)=\inf_{\nu:\operatorname{STV}(\nu)\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)}\frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|\), with \(\nu\) ranging over distributions on all rankings. For each fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\), distinguish the promised cases \(d(\mu)\le 1/q\) and \(d(\mu)\ge c_\zeta q^{-\zeta}\), for a suitable constant \(c_\zeta>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge2\) candidates, let \(\Pi_C\) be their strict complete rankings, and fix a total order specifying which tied candidate is eliminated first.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\subseteq\Pi_C\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses. Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The input includes a binary-encoded integer \(q\ge2\), with \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/q\), positive integers \(n_\pi\), and \(\sum_{\pi\in T}n_\pi=q\); unlisted rankings have mass zero. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For any distribution \(\rho\), STV repeatedly eliminates the candidate having least mass of rankings that place it highest among the remaining candidates, using the fixed order for ties; the last candidate wins.
Define \(d(\mu)=\inf_{\nu:\operatorname{STV}(\nu)\ne\operatorname{STV}(\mu)}\frac12\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_C}|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|\), where \(\nu\) ranges over all nonnegative real distributions of total mass one on \(\Pi_C\), without a denominator or support restriction.

For every fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\), is distinguishing the promised cases \(d(\mu)\le1/q\) and \(d(\mu)\ge q^{-\zeta}\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this approximation-gap problem NP-hard?

For discrete elections, the margin is the minimum number of rankings replaced to change the winner. Approximating it within \(n^{1-\zeta}\) is NP-hard for every fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\); the underlying hardness distinguishes margin at most one from margin at least \(n^{1-\zeta}\). [Hillebrand et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18772v1)
No result settling exactly this distributional promise problem was located in the literature search.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

For every fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\), the stated distributional promise problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

The key step is a rounding lemma: allowing arbitrary real masses can decrease the discrete margin by at most an additive quantity polynomial in the number of candidates. The strengthened discrete hardness theorem makes this additive loss small enough to preserve the promised gap.

1. Definitions and the discrete hardness theorem

For a nonnegative mass vector \(x\) on rankings and a nonempty candidate set \(S\), define
\[ s_S(c;x)=\sum_{\pi:\,c\text{ is highest in }\pi|_S}x_\pi \qquad(c\in S). \]
This definition applies to unnormalized mass vectors. Each score is linear in \(x\).

Let \(P\) be a discrete election with \(q\) voters, ranking counts \(b_\pi\), and empirical distribution \(\mu_\pi=b_\pi/q\). Write \(M(P)\) for its discrete margin: the minimum number of rankings replaced to change its winner.

We use the following consequence of Hillebrand et al., Theorem B.1:

For every fixed \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard to distinguish discrete STV elections satisfying \(M(P)\le 1\) from those satisfying \(M(P)\ge q^{1-\varepsilon}\), even under the additional restriction \(m\le q^\varepsilon\), with a consistent fixed tie-breaking order.

The candidate-count restriction is explicitly part of that theorem. [Hillebrand et al., Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18772v1#A2)

We will prove the distributional lower bound independently; it does not follow merely by dividing the discrete lower bound by \(q\).

2. A rounding lemma preserving the winner

Lemma. For every discrete election \(P\) on \(m\ge 2\) candidates, with empirical distribution \(\mu\),
\[ M(P)\le q\,d(\mu)+4m^4. \]
Also, \(d(\mu)\le M(P)/q\).

Proof. The second inequality follows immediately: replacing \(k\) rankings changes the empirical distribution by total variation at most \(k/q\).

For the first inequality, fix any real distribution \(\nu\) whose winner differs from that of \(\mu\), and put
\[ \delta=\frac12\sum_\pi|\mu_\pi-\nu_\pi|. \]
We construct a discrete election with the same winner as \(\nu\), using at most \(q\delta+4m^4\) replacements.

2.1. Coordinates for the desired elimination order

Let \(e_1,\ldots,e_m\) be the elimination order of \(\nu\), with \(e_m\) its winner. Set \(S_i=\{e_i,\ldots,e_m\}\).

Record every score \(s_{S_i}(c;x)\), for \(1\le i\le m\) and \(c\in S_i\). There are
\[ D=\frac{m(m+1)}2 \]
coordinates. Denote their vector by \(V(x)\).

Because \(\nu\) eliminates \(e_i\) at stage \(i\),
\[ s_{S_i}(e_i;\nu)\le s_{S_i}(e_j;\nu) \qquad(i<j). \]
These weak inequalities hold regardless of how ties were resolved.

Define integers
\[ S=2(D+1),\qquad K=2S+1,\qquad B=K\frac{m(m-1)}2,\qquad A=B+D+1. \]

If \(q\le B\), replacing all voters by rankings beginning with \(e_m\) gives the desired winner using at most \(q\le A\) replacements. Hence assume \(q>B\).

2.2. Retaining original ballots and compressing the additions

Set \(x=q\nu\) and \(a=1-B/q\). Split the target vector \(ax\), of total mass \(q-B\), as
\[ r_\pi=\min\{b_\pi,ax_\pi\},\qquad \eta_\pi=ax_\pi-r_\pi. \]
Both vectors are nonnegative, and \(r_\pi\le b_\pi\).

We first replace \(r\) by a vector \(r'\) that preserves \(V(r)\) and its total mass, still satisfies \(0\le r'_\pi\le b_\pi\), and has at most \(D+1\) coordinates strictly between their bounds.

To justify this, suppose more than \(D+1\) coordinates lie strictly between their bounds. Their augmented ranking vectors—consisting of their \(D\) score coordinates and a final coordinate \(1\)—are linearly dependent. Moving the corresponding masses along a nonzero dependence preserves all scores and total mass. Move until a coordinate reaches either \(0\) or \(b_\pi\). Repeating decreases the number of interior coordinates until it is at most \(D+1\). Since every \(b_\pi\) is an integer, \(r'\) consequently has at most \(D+1\) noninteger coordinates.

Similarly, replace \(\eta\) by a nonnegative vector \(\eta'\), supported on at most \(D+1\) rankings, preserving its scores and total mass. Indeed, if more than \(D+1\) rankings have positive mass, their augmented vectors are linearly dependent. The dependence coefficients sum to zero, so they include both signs. Moving along that dependence until a mass becomes zero preserves nonnegativity and reduces the support. Repetition proves the assertion.

Therefore,
\[ V(r'+\eta')=V(ax),\qquad \sum_\pi(r'_\pi+\eta'_\pi)=q-B. \]

2.3. Rounding with bounded score error

Round the masses of \(r'\) and \(\eta'\) down separately. The total loss \(H\) is an integer because their combined total is the integer \(q-B\). Moreover,
\[ 0\le H<2(D+1)=S. \]
Add \(H\) ballots of any one complete ranking. Call the resulting integer mass vector \(z\). Its total is \(q-B\).

For every recorded score, rounding down removes between \(0\) and \(H\), and the added ballots contribute between \(0\) and \(H\). Thus
\[ |s_{S_i}(c;z)-s_{S_i}(c;ax)|\le H<S. \]

2.4. Enforcing the desired eliminations strictly

Add an integer profile \(h\) as follows. For each \(j<m\), give \((j-1)K\) ballots the ranking beginning \(e_j\succ e_m\); give \((m-1)K\) ballots a ranking beginning with \(e_m\). Complete all rankings arbitrarily. The total number of these ballots is \(B\).

Under the desired elimination prefix, the boost score of \(e_i\) is \((i-1)K\). Every later candidate \(e_j\), with \(j<m\), has boost score \((j-1)K\). Candidate \(e_m\) has at least \((m-1)K\), since ballots of already eliminated candidates transfer to it. Hence, for every \(i<j\),
\[ s_{S_i}(e_j;h)-s_{S_i}(e_i;h)\ge K. \]

The corresponding difference for \(ax\) is nonnegative. The two rounding errors together have magnitude at most \(2S\). Consequently,
\[ s_{S_i}(e_j;z+h)-s_{S_i}(e_i;z+h) \ge K-2S=1. \]
Induction on \(i\) shows that the integer profile \(z+h\) eliminates \(e_1,\ldots,e_{m-1}\) in exactly that order. Its winner is \(e_m\), independently of tie-breaking.

2.5. Bounding the replacements

Keep \(\lfloor r'_\pi\rfloor\) original ballots of each type. This is possible because \(r'_\pi\le b_\pi\). All other ballots in \(z+h\) can be supplied by replacing the remaining original voters.

There are at most \(D+1\) noninteger coordinates in \(r'\), so the number of replacements is at most
\[ q-\sum_\pi\lfloor r'_\pi\rfloor \le q-\sum_\pi r_\pi+D+1. \]

Because \(b\) and \(x\) both have total mass \(q\),
\[ \sum_\pi\min\{b_\pi,x_\pi\}=q-q\delta. \]
Scaling \(x\) to \(ax\) removes total mass \(B\). Using
\[ \min\{b_\pi,ax_\pi\} \ge \min\{b_\pi,x_\pi\}-(1-a)x_\pi \]
and summing gives
\[ \sum_\pi r_\pi\ge q-q\delta-B. \]
Thus the constructed election requires at most \(q\delta+A\) replacements.

Finally, for \(m\ge2\), \(D+1\le m^2\) and \(K=4(D+1)+1\le5m^2\). Therefore
\[ A=K\frac{m(m-1)}2+D+1 \le \frac52m^4+m^2\le4m^4. \]
We have proved \(M(P)\le q\delta+4m^4\) for every winner-changing \(\nu\). Taking the infimum over all such \(\nu\) proves the lemma. No attainment of the infimum is required.

3. Preserving the approximation gap

Fix the target \(\zeta\in(0,1)\). Choose a fixed rational \(\varepsilon\) satisfying
\[ 0<\varepsilon<\min\{\zeta/2,1/10\}. \]

Apply the cited discrete hardness theorem with this \(\varepsilon\). Compress each resulting election into its distinct ranking types, and output their counts with denominator \(q\).

In the discrete YES case, \(M(P)\le1\), so
\[ d(\mu)\le \frac{M(P)}q\le\frac1q. \]

In the discrete NO case, the rounding lemma and \(m\le q^\varepsilon\) give
\[ q\,d(\mu)\ge M(P)-4m^4 \ge q^{1-\varepsilon}-4q^{4\varepsilon}. \]
Since \(1-5\varepsilon>0\), for all sufficiently large \(q\),
\[ 4q^{4\varepsilon}\le\frac12q^{1-\varepsilon}. \]
Since \(\zeta-\varepsilon>0\), increasing this fixed threshold if necessary also ensures
\[ \frac12q^{1-\varepsilon}\ge q^{1-\zeta}. \]
Therefore every sufficiently large NO instance satisfies
\[ d(\mu)\ge q^{-\zeta}. \]

The threshold depends only on the fixed constants. Smaller discrete instances have bounded \(q\) and, by \(m\le q^\varepsilon\), bounded candidate count; they can be decided by exhaustive search and mapped to fixed promised instances. Such instances exist: a two-candidate tied election has \(d(\mu)=0\), while a two-candidate unanimous election has \(d(\mu)=1/2\), allowing the large-distance promise with a sufficiently large fixed denominator.

4. Encoding and conclusion

The discrete hardness reduction produces explicit elections in polynomial time for fixed \(\varepsilon\). Aggregating equal rankings is polynomial-time, and produces \(\tau\le q\) positive integer counts summing to \(q\). Their binary encodings, the candidate count, and the total input length are all polynomial in the original reduction input.

Thus compression is a polynomial-time reduction to precisely the support-based input format in the question. The rounding lemma covers all real perturbations on all rankings, including rankings absent from the input support.

Consequently, the distributional promise problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every fixed \(\zeta\in(0,1)\). A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
