The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support of complete rankings \(\rho\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\rho\) summing to \(1\), a target candidate \(a\), and a rational threshold \(q\), let \(p_\rho\) be the position of \(a\) in \(\rho\). For each supported type and each \(d\in\{0,\ldots,p_\rho-1\}\), choose mass \(x_{\rho,d}\) to move to the ranking obtained by moving \(a\) up \(d\) adjacent positions, subject to \(\sum_d x_{\rho,d}=\mu_\rho\), \(x_{\rho,d}\ge 0\), and \(\sum_{\rho,d:\,a\text{ ranks above }b}x_{\rho,d}\ge\tfrac12\) for every \(b\ne a\). Is the minimum per-capita swap cost \(\sum_{\rho,d}d\,x_{\rho,d}\) at most \(q\)? The weak pairwise inequalities give the infimum cost for making \(a\) strictly beat every opponent.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) be a set of \(m\ge3\) candidates. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(\rho\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_\rho\), with \(\sum_\rho\mu_\rho=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The input also specifies a target candidate \(a\in A\) and a binary-rational threshold \(q\in\mathbb Q\). Let \(p_\rho\) be the position of \(a\), numbered from \(1\), and let \(\rho^{(d)}\) move \(a\) upward by \(d\in\{0,\ldots,p_\rho-1\}\) adjacent positions, preserving the relative order of all other candidates.
Mass may be divided: choose real \(x_{\rho,d}\ge0\) with \(\sum_d x_{\rho,d}=\mu_\rho\). Require \(\sum_{\rho,d:\,a\succ_{\rho^{(d)}}b}x_{\rho,d}\ge\tfrac12\) for every \(b\in A\setminus\{a\}\).
Define \(C(\mu,a)\) as the minimum of \(\sum_{\rho,d}d\,x_{\rho,d}\) over these choices. Pairwise ties are permitted; this score is the infimum cost of obtaining strict pairwise victories, and the decision concerns \(C(\mu,a)\le q\), including equality.

Is deciding whether \(C(\mu,a)\le q\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite electorates, Dodgson score decision is NP-hard; semi-random score decision has no polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{ZPP}\) under neutral, polynomially samplable models admitting a fixed top-\(K\) ranking with probability at least \(1-1/K\), where \(K=\lceil m^{1/d}\rceil\) for a constant \(d>1\). [Xia–Zheng](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.08173v1)
The continuous score is the normalized Dodgson Clone score: fractional vote splitting and weak pairwise victories are already defined, with polynomial-time score computation established for finite profiles. [McCabe-Dansted](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1501)

Conclusion

The decision problem is solvable in deterministic time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof uses a rational linear feasibility problem with at most \(m\tau\) variables. In particular, it does not enumerate all possible rankings or expand rational masses into individual voters.

1. A finite minimum exists

Let \(P\) denote the set of arrays \(x=(x_{\rho,d})\) satisfying the mass conservation, nonnegativity, and pairwise constraints in the question.

This set is nonempty: set \(x_{\rho,p_\rho-1}=\mu_\rho\) for every supported ranking and all other variables to zero. This moves \(a\) to the top everywhere, giving it pairwise support \(1\) against every opponent.

Furthermore, mass conservation and nonnegativity imply
\(0\le x_{\rho,d}\le\mu_\rho\).
Thus \(P\) is bounded. It is closed because all its constraints are non-strict linear equalities or inequalities. Consequently, \(P\) is compact, and the continuous linear objective attains its minimum on \(P\). Hence the stated \(C(\mu,a)\) is an attained minimum.

2. Exact reduction to rational linear feasibility

For every supported ranking \(\rho\), allowed displacement \(d\), and opponent \(b\), define the constant
\(E_{\rho,d,b}=1\) if \(a\succ_{\rho^{(d)}}b\), and \(E_{\rho,d,b}=0\) otherwise.

Construct the following system, whose unknowns are real numbers \(x_{\rho,d}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{\rho,d}&\ge0 &&\text{for every }\rho,d,\\ \sum_{d=0}^{p_\rho-1}x_{\rho,d}&=\mu_\rho &&\text{for every }\rho,\\ \sum_\rho\sum_{d=0}^{p_\rho-1}E_{\rho,d,b}x_{\rho,d}&\ge\tfrac12 &&\text{for every }b\ne a,\\ \sum_\rho\sum_{d=0}^{p_\rho-1}d\,x_{\rho,d}&\le q. \end{aligned} \]

The first three groups describe exactly \(P\). Therefore:

  • If this system is feasible, its feasible point has cost at most \(q\), so \(C(\mu,a)\le q\).
  • If \(C(\mu,a)\le q\), an attained minimizer from Section 1 satisfies the entire system.

Thus feasibility is equivalent to the requested decision, including equality at the threshold.

3. Polynomial encoding size and running time

The number of variables is
\(N=\sum_\rho p_\rho\le m\tau\).
Besides nonnegativity, there are \(\tau\) equalities, \(m-1\) pairwise inequalities, and one cost inequality. Replacing each equality with two inequalities leaves polynomially many constraints.

Every matrix coefficient belongs to
\(\{-1,0,1,\ldots,m-1\}\):
the pairwise coefficients are \(0\) or \(1\), and the cost coefficients are displacements. These coefficients require \(O(\log(m+1))\) bits. The right-hand sides consist of the supplied rational masses, the supplied rational threshold, and the constant \(1/2\); their encoding lengths are bounded by the input length.

The coefficients \(E_{\rho,d,b}\) can be constructed by examining each supported ranking and each allowed displacement, in \(O(m^2\tau)\) elementary operations. Even a dense encoding of the resulting system has size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

We now apply the standard polynomial-time rational linear programming feasibility theorem: a finite system of linear inequalities with explicitly encoded rational coefficients can be tested for feasibility over the reals in deterministic time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here because all coefficients are rational and the entire system is explicitly constructed with polynomial size.

This gives the required algorithm. It uses exact rational feasibility, so no numerical tolerance or assumed separation from \(q\) is needed.

4. Strict victories and the cited hardness results

The weak constraints also justify the stated interpretation as an infimum for strict victories. Let \(x^*\) be a weakly feasible minimizer, and let \(y\) be the all-top assignment from Section 1. For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), the assignment
\(x^\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)x^*+\varepsilon y\)
preserves all masses. Against every opponent, its pairwise support is at least
\((1-\varepsilon)/2+\varepsilon=(1+\varepsilon)/2>1/2\).
Its cost tends to \(C(\mu,a)\) as \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero. Conversely, every strictly feasible assignment is weakly feasible, so its cost is at least \(C(\mu,a)\). The two infima therefore agree.

The LP relaxation and polynomial computation of Dodgson Clone scores are described by [McCabe-Dansted, Sections 1 and 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1501). The argument above additionally verifies polynomial complexity for the specified rational support representation.

The [Xia–Zheng hardness theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.08173v1) concerns finite-electorate Dodgson scores. Indivisible ballots impose integer choices; the present problem explicitly permits fractional splitting. That hardness result consequently does not establish hardness of this continuous decision problem.
