The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_j\) with \(\sum_j\beta_j\ge1\), per-capita benefits \(a^k_{tj}\in[0,1]\), and \(q\) rational posterior weight vectors \(w^1,\ldots,w^q\), let \(\mathcal A=\{x\ge0:\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\ \forall t,\ \sum_tx_{tj}\le\beta_j\ \forall j\}\) and \(f_w(x)=\sum_{k,t,j}w_k a^k_{tj}x_{tj}\). Compute an exact minimizer \(x^*\), a best challenger \(y^*\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal A}\frac1q\sum_{s=1}^q\max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x^*)\}\), and the value \(\min_{x\in\mathcal A}\max_{y\in\mathcal A}\frac1q\sum_{s=1}^q\max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x)\}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) resources, \(n\ge1\) criteria, and \(q\ge1\) explicitly supplied weight vectors. Capacities \(\beta_j\ge0\) and benefits \(a^k_{tj}\in[0,1]\) are binary rationals, with \(\sum_j\beta_j\ge1\). Each \(w^s\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^n\) satisfies \(\sum_k w^s_k=1\); the vectors have equal probability \(1/q\).
Allocations may split each type's mass: \(\mathcal A=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\tau\times m}:\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\ \forall t,\ \sum_tx_{tj}\le\beta_j\ \forall j\}\). Every type can receive every resource.
Define \(f_w(x)=\sum_{k=1}^n\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^m w_k a^k_{tj}x_{tj}\), with larger values preferred, and \(R(x,y)=q^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^q\max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x)\}\).
The exact computational problem returns \(V=\min_{x\in\mathcal A}\max_{y\in\mathcal A}R(x,y)\), an optimizer \(x^*\), and \(y^*\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal A}R(x^*,y)\); any tied optimizers are acceptable. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is this exact computational problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,n,q,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite feasible sets, exact challenger generation terminates and returns the minimax expected regret value, an optimizer, and its best challenger; this termination result gives no polynomial running-time bound. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14778)
No result settling the exact continuous allocation problem above was identified in the literature search.

  • Restricted the previously unspecified weight-vector domain to the nonnegative rational simplex, including boundary weights, as in the stated parameter space.
  • Required capacities to be nonnegative; retained the resource-specific capacities explicitly supplied in the continuous question.
  • Made all benefits binary rational so exact computation has a finite input encoding.
  • Interpreted the allocation variables as divisible masses, with every type eligible for every resource and any tied optimizers acceptable.

Result

The exact computational problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with two resources, benefits in \(\{0,1\}\), dyadic rational masses, and weight vectors that are standard basis vectors.

The reduction below proves hardness of computing the minimax value itself. Consequently, it also proves hardness of returning the required triple consisting of the value, an optimizer, and a best challenger.

1. Source problem

For a simple unweighted graph \(G=(U,E)\), define its maximum cut size by

\( C(G)=\max_{S\subseteq U}|\{\{u,v\}\in E:|\{u,v\}\cap S|=1\}|. \)

The decision problem asking whether \(C(G)\ge K\), for a supplied integer \(K\), is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is the Simple Max Cut result of [Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(76)90059-1).

Write \(r=|U|\) and \(M=|E|\). We may assume \(M\ge1\) and delete isolated vertices: instances without edges are trivial, and deleting isolated vertices preserves every attainable cut size. Thus \(r\ge2\).

2. Constructing the allocation instance

Let \(D\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(D\ge r\). Create one type for each vertex, with mass

\( \mu_u=\frac1D. \)

If \(D>r\), create one additional dummy type of mass \((D-r)/D\). Hence all masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one, and the support size satisfies \(\tau\le r+1\).

Use two resources with capacities

\( \beta_1=\beta_2=1. \)

Because the total mass is one, both capacity constraints hold automatically whenever the row constraints and nonnegativity hold. Therefore every vertex type can independently split its mass between the resources.

For any allocation \(x\), define its normalized vertex coordinates by

\( b_u=D x_{u1}. \)

The row constraint gives \(x_{u2}=(1-b_u)/D\), so \(b\in[0,1]^r\). Conversely, every \(b\in[0,1]^r\) extends to a feasible allocation; the dummy type, when present, may be split arbitrarily.

Choose an arbitrary ordering \((u,v)\) of the endpoints of each edge. Introduce two criteria \((e,+)\) and \((e,-)\) for each edge, so

\( n=q=2M. \)

For \(e=(u,v)\), set the following benefits to one:

\( a^{e,+}_{u1}=a^{e,+}_{v2}=1, \qquad a^{e,-}_{u2}=a^{e,-}_{v1}=1. \)

Set every other benefit to zero, including all dummy-type benefits. Supply one standard basis weight vector for each criterion. These vectors are nonnegative, sum to one, and have the required equal probabilities.

The utility functions associated with the edge are consequently

\( f_{e,+}(x)=x_{u1}+x_{v2} =\frac{1+b_u-b_v}{D}, \)

\( f_{e,-}(x)=x_{u2}+x_{v1} =\frac{1-b_u+b_v}{D}. \)

All benefits belong to \(\{0,1\}\), and every type remains eligible for every resource.

3. The regret becomes a sum of absolute differences

Let \(y\) have normalized vertex coordinates \(z_u=D y_{u1}\). For an edge \(e=(u,v)\), put

\( \Delta_e=(z_u-z_v)-(b_u-b_v). \)

The two utility differences are \(\Delta_e/D\) and \(-\Delta_e/D\). Using the identity

\( \max\{0,h\}+\max\{0,-h\}=|h|, \)

and averaging over the \(2M\) weight vectors gives

\( R(x,y)= \frac1{2MD} \sum_{e=(u,v)\in E} \left|(z_u-z_v)-(b_u-b_v)\right|. \)

The dummy allocation has no effect on this expression. Thus the constructed minimax value is exactly

\( V= \frac1{2MD} \min_{b\in[0,1]^r} \max_{z\in[0,1]^r} \sum_{e=(u,v)\in E} \left|(z_u-z_v)-(b_u-b_v)\right|. \)

We now prove that the unscaled minimum is \(C(G)\).

4. Upper bound: equal splitting attains maximum cut size

Choose \(b_u=1/2\) for every vertex. This corresponds to the feasible allocation

\( x^0_{t1}=x^0_{t2}=\frac{\mu_t}{2} \)

for every type, including the dummy type.

At this allocation, the unscaled challenger objective is

\( g(z)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}|z_u-z_v|. \)

We claim that

\( \max_{z\in[0,1]^r}g(z)=C(G). \)

To prove the upper bound, hold every coordinate except \(z_u\) fixed. As a function of \(z_u\), the objective is a sum of absolute values of affine functions, hence is convex. For any convex function \(\phi\) on \([0,1]\),

\( \phi(t)\le(1-t)\phi(0)+t\phi(1) \le\max\{\phi(0),\phi(1)\}. \)

Therefore we can replace \(z_u\) by one of its endpoints without decreasing \(g\). Repeating this operation for every coordinate produces \(c\in\{0,1\}^r\) with \(g(c)\ge g(z)\).

For a binary vector \(c\), an edge contributes one precisely when its endpoints have different coordinates. Hence \(g(c)\) is a cut size and is at most \(C(G)\). Conversely, the indicator vector of a maximum cut attains \(C(G)\). This proves the claim.

It follows that

\( \max_{y\in\mathcal A}R(x^0,y)=\frac{C(G)}{2MD}, \qquad V\le\frac{C(G)}{2MD}. \)

5. Lower bound: no allocation improves on equal splitting

Fix an arbitrary \(b\in[0,1]^r\). Let \(c\in\{0,1\}^r\) be the indicator vector of a maximum cut. Both \(c\) and its complement \(\mathbf1-c\) are feasible normalized challenger vectors.

For an edge \(e=(u,v)\), define

\( d_e=c_u-c_v, \qquad h_e=b_u-b_v. \)

The edge contributions for these two challengers are respectively \(|d_e-h_e|\) and \(|-d_e-h_e|\). The triangle inequality yields

\( |d_e-h_e|+|-d_e-h_e| = |d_e-h_e|+|d_e+h_e| \ge |2d_e| =2|d_e|. \)

Summing over all edges, the average of the two challenger objectives is therefore at least

\( \frac12\sum_{e\in E} \left(|d_e-h_e|+|-d_e-h_e|\right) \ge \sum_{e\in E}|d_e| =C(G). \)

The maximum over all feasible challengers is at least the average of these two particular challenger values. Thus every \(b\) satisfies

\( \max_{z\in[0,1]^r} \sum_{e=(u,v)\in E} \left|(z_u-z_v)-(b_u-b_v)\right| \ge C(G). \)

Together with the upper bound, this proves the exact identity

\( \boxed{V=\frac{C(G)}{2MD}}. \)

In particular, \(x^0\) is a minimax optimizer.

6. Polynomial reduction and conclusion

The construction has \(m=2\), \(\tau\le r+1\), and \(n=q=2M\). Since \(D<2r\), the masses require \(O(\log r)\) bits each. Benefits, capacities, and weight-vector entries are all zero or one. The explicitly supplied tables contain \(O(rM+M^2)\) entries, so the construction has polynomial binary length and can be produced in polynomial time.

Given an exact returned value \(V\), we recover

\( C(G)=2MD\,V. \)

Equivalently, for any supplied integer \(K\),

\( C(G)\ge K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V\ge\frac{K}{2MD}. \)

The threshold has polynomial binary length, so an exact polynomial-time solver for the requested problem would decide Simple Max Cut in polynomial time.

Therefore the exact continuous allocation problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. It has no polynomial-time algorithm in the stated parameters unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). The reduction fully permits fractional allocations; the lower-bound argument proves that fractional choices cannot reduce the minimax value below the encoded maximum cut size.
