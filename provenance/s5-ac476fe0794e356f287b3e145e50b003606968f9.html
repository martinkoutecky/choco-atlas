The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) of binary valuation vectors over one class \(G\) of identical indivisible units and divisible classes \(D\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b\) and \(c_d\), return a finite list of actions \((t,q,x)\) with masses defining \(\nu\), where \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\)\), \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{D}\)\), goods go only to types that value them, the type marginal is \(\mu\), \(\int q\,d\nu=b\)\), and \(\int x_d\,d\nu=c_d\)\), minimizing \(\int (v_{tG}q+\sum_{d\in D}v_{td}x_d)^2\,d\nu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There is one class \(G\) of identical indivisible units and a nonempty set \(D\) of divisible classes; put \(m=1+|D|\). Each type has additive binary valuations \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^{\{G\}\cup D}\), with value one per desired indivisible unit or unit amount of a desired divisible class.
The per-capita supplies \(b\) and \(c_d\) are positive binary rationals. Every class is valued by at least one support type; types valuing no class are allowed. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible allocation is a finitely supported probability measure \(\nu=\sum_{j=1}^k a_j\delta_{(t_j,q_j,x_j)}\), with \(a_j>0\), \(q_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and \(x_j\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{D}\). A type's mass may be split among multiple actions.
Require \(q_j=0\) when \(v_{t_jG}=0\), and \(x_{jd}=0\) when \(v_{t_jd}=0\). Require \(\sum_{j:t_j=t}a_j=\mu_t\), \(\sum_j a_jq_j=b\), and \(\sum_j a_jx_{jd}=c_d\) for every \(d\in D\); there are no additional individual bundle caps.
The objective is \(F(\nu)=\sum_j a_j\bigl(v_{t_jG}q_j+\sum_{d\in D}v_{t_jd}x_{jd}\bigr)^2\). The computational problem is to return an exact minimizing finite allocation, encoding its masses and divisible amounts as binary rationals and its unit counts as binary integers.

Is this exact mass allocation optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a finite list of agents, minimizing the sum of squared utilities is NP-hard even with identical indivisible goods; it is polynomial-time solvable when all divisible goods have the same valuation column. Both facts are established by [Kawase, Nishimura, and Sumita](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.2024.0659).
No result settling the exact normalized mass problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Supply signs were unspecified: use positive supplies and a nonempty divisible-class set to retain the mixed-goods setting; absent zero-supply classes can be omitted.
  • Carry over the assumption that every supplied class is desired by some support type; do not require every type to desire a class.
  • Define m as the number of supplied classes, including G, and L as total input bit length; supplies are binary encoded rather than expanded into individual goods.
  • Interpret action masses as freely split portions of a nonatomic society, with integer unit counts per action and only aggregate supply constraints; impose no individual cap derived from per-capita supplies.
  • Pin down the exact computational output using binary rational action masses and divisible amounts and binary integer unit counts, while retaining real divisible amounts in the feasible domain.

Status and new results

The prescribed rational output does not exist on every permitted input. I reprove the constant-size counterexample below.

For the intended question, allowing exact algebraic output, I establish the following additional results:

  • Some optimum uses at most $2\tau$ actions, with every indivisible count belonging to a common set $\{0,n,n+1\}$.
  • Some optimum has a polynomial-size encoding in a single real algebraic field of degree at most three.
  • If an instance admits a rational minimizing allocation, it admits one of polynomial encoding length.

These are structural and representation results. They do not establish an exact polynomial-time algorithm or NP-hardness.

1. Local convexification and existence

For $g,y\geq 0$, let $f(g,y)$ be the minimum of
$$ \mathbb E[(q+x)^2] $$
over finitely supported distributions satisfying
$$ q\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0},\qquad x\geq 0,\qquad \mathbb E[q]=g,\qquad \mathbb E[x]=y. $$

Here $x$ denotes the total desired divisible amount.

Lemma 1. Put $s=g+y$ and $n=\lfloor s\rfloor$.

If $g\leq n$, then
$$ f(g,y)=s^2. $$
If $g>n$, put $\theta=g-n$ and $r=y/(1-\theta)$. Then
$$ f(g,y)=(1-\theta)(n+r)^2+\theta(n+1)^2. $$
In either case, at most two actions suffice.

Proof. If $g\leq n$, choose $q$ from $\{\lfloor g\rfloor,\lceil g\rceil\}$ with mean $g$, and set $x=s-q$. Since $q\leq n\leq s$, these amounts are nonnegative. Every action has utility $s$. Jensen’s inequality gives the matching lower bound
$$ \mathbb E[(q+x)^2]\geq \bigl(\mathbb E[q+x]\bigr)^2=s^2. $$

If $g>n$, then
$$ 0<\theta<1,\qquad 0\leq y<1-\theta,\qquad 0\leq r<1. $$
Use $(q,x)=(n,r)$ with probability $1-\theta$ and $(q,x)=(n+1,0)$ with probability $\theta$. This has the required means.

For optimality, set
$$ \lambda=2n+1+r^2,\qquad p=2(n+r), $$
and consider
$$ H(q,x)=(q+x)^2-\lambda q-px. $$
For integers $q\leq n$, minimizing over $x\geq 0$ gives $x=n+r-q$ and value
$$ -\frac{p^2}{4}+(p-\lambda)q. $$
Because
$$ p-\lambda=-(1-r)^2<0, $$
the minimum in this range occurs at $q=n$.

For integers $q\geq n+1$, the minimizing amount is $x=0$. The successive differences of $q^2-\lambda q$ are
$$ 2q+1-\lambda>0. $$
Thus the minimum in this range occurs at $q=n+1$. Finally,
$$ H(n+1,0)-H(n,r)=2n+1-\lambda+r^2=0. $$
The proposed two actions minimize $H$. Since the means of $q$ and $x$ are fixed, they also minimize the original objective. $\square$

Mixing feasible distributions proves that $f$ is convex. It is continuous: in the second case,
$$ f(g,y)-s^2 = \frac{\theta}{1-\theta}(n+1-s)^2. $$
This correction tends to zero when $\theta$ tends to zero. At an upper strip boundary, use
$$ 0\leq n+1-s\leq 1-\theta $$
to bound the correction by $n+1-s$. The two formulas therefore agree continuously at their boundaries.

For the society, introduce aggregate amounts $z_t$ and $w_{td}$, representing respectively the indivisible and divisible supplies assigned to type $t$. Their feasible polytope is
$$ P= \left\{ (z,w): \begin{array}{l} z_t,w_{td}\geq 0,\\ \sum_tz_t=b,\quad \sum_tw_{td}=c_d,\\ z_t=0\text{ if }v_{tG}=0,\\ w_{td}=0\text{ if }v_{td}=0 \end{array} \right\}. $$
Define
$$ g_t=\frac{z_t}{\mu_t}, \qquad y_t=\frac{\sum_dw_{td}}{\mu_t}. $$

The real allocation problem equals
$$ \min_{(z,w)\in P}\sum_t\mu_t f(g_t,y_t). $$

Indeed, conditioning any allocation on its type gives the lower bound from Lemma 1. Conversely, reconstruct the local two-action optimum for each type. If $W_t=\sum_dw_{td}>0$, replace a local scalar amount $x$ by
$$ x_d=\frac{w_{td}}{W_t}x. $$
Then
$$ \mu_t\mathbb E[x_d] = \mu_t\frac{w_{td}}{W_t}y_t=w_{td}. $$
Only desired classes receive positive amounts. If $W_t=0$, all divisible amounts are zero.

The polytope $P$ is nonempty because every class has a valuing type. It is compact, and the objective is continuous. Consequently, a real optimum exists and can be reconstructed with at most $2\tau$ actions.

2. Supporting resource prices

Write $\Phi(b,c)$ for the attained real minimum, keeping the types and masses fixed.

The function $\Phi$ is finite and convex on the open positive orthant of resource vectors. Finiteness follows from feasibility. Convexity follows by mixing allocations, which preserves every type’s mass.

The supporting-hyperplane theorem for a finite convex function on an open convex domain therefore supplies real numbers $\lambda,p_d$ such that, at the given resource vector $R=(b,c)$,
$$ \Phi(R')\geq \Phi(R)+\lambda(b'-b)+\sum_dp_d(c'_d-c_d) $$
for all positive $R'$.

The resulting inequality also applies to every allocation satisfying the type-mass and valuation restrictions, even if its resource vector has zero coordinates. To see this, let $\nu_*$ be an optimum at $R$, and let $\nu$ be such an allocation with resource vector $R_\nu$. For $0<\varepsilon<1$, their mixture has positive resource vector
$$ R_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)R+\varepsilon R_\nu. $$
Applying the supporting inequality and bounding $\Phi(R_\varepsilon)$ by the objective of that mixture gives, after cancellation,
$$ F(\nu)\geq F(\nu_*)+\lambda(b_\nu-b)+\sum_dp_d(c_{\nu d}-c_d). $$

Thus $\nu_*$ minimizes
$$ F(\nu)-\lambda b_\nu-\sum_dp_dc_{\nu d} $$
among all allocations with the prescribed type masses.

This minimization separates across types. For each type $t$, every positive-mass action in $\nu_*$ minimizes
$$ H_t(q,x)= \left(q+\sum_dx_d\right)^2-\lambda q-\sum_dp_dx_d $$
over its permitted actions, with $q=0$ for a type not valuing $G$.

For completeness, let $\eta_t$ be the conditional average of $H_t$ under $\nu_*$. Replacing type $t$’s entire conditional allocation by any one permitted action shows that the action’s value is at least $\eta_t$. Each action already used by $\nu_*$ therefore has value at least $\eta_t$; their average equals $\eta_t$, so all attain equality.

The prices satisfy
$$ \lambda\geq 1,\qquad p_d>0. $$
For the first inequality, select an optimal action with $q\geq 1$, which exists because $b>0$. On a sufficiently small mass $\varepsilon$, decrease its count by one. If its utility was $U\geq 1$, the objective decreases by
$$ \varepsilon(2U-1)\geq \varepsilon. $$
The supporting inequality consequently gives $\lambda\geq 1$.

For a divisible class $d$, select an action of mass $a$ with $x_d>0$. Decreasing its assigned aggregate amount by a small $\varepsilon$ decreases the objective by
$$ 2U\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{a}>0. $$
The supporting inequality gives
$$ p_d\geq 2U-\frac{\varepsilon}{a}>0 $$
when $\varepsilon$ is sufficiently small.

3. A common set of indivisible counts

For each type, put
$$ p_t=\max\bigl(\{p_d:v_{td}=1\}\cup\{0\}\bigr). $$
An action minimizing $H_t$ assigns positive divisible amounts only to desired classes priced $p_t$: transferring an amount from a lower-priced class to a highest-priced class leaves utility unchanged and decreases $H_t$.

For a type valuing $G$, pointwise minimization therefore reduces to
$$ (q+x)^2-\lambda q-p_tx,\qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0},\quad x\geq 0. $$
If the type values no divisible class, then $p_t=0$, and allowing $x\geq 0$ does not change the minimum because its minimizing amount is zero.

Put
$$ h=\frac{\lambda}{2}, \qquad n=\lfloor h\rfloor. $$

Lemma 2. For a type valuing $G$:

  • If $p_t>\lambda$, its unique minimizing action has $q=0$ and $x=p_t/2$.
  • If $p_t=\lambda$, its minimizing actions have

$$ q\in\{0,\ldots,n\},\qquad x=h-q. $$

  • If $p_t<\lambda$, its minimizing counts belong to $\{n,n+1\}$.

In the last case, the minimizing divisible amounts at those two counts are
$$ x_n=\max\left(\frac{p_t}{2}-n,0\right), \qquad x_{n+1}=0, $$
and the difference between their minimized values is
$$ \Delta_t = 2n+1-\lambda+ \left(\max\left(\frac{p_t}{2}-n,0\right)\right)^2. $$

Proof. For real $q\geq 0$, minimizing over $x$ gives
$$ J(q)=q^2-\lambda q- \left(\max\left(\frac{p_t}{2}-q,0\right)\right)^2. $$
On $q<p_t/2$, its derivative is $p_t-\lambda$; on $q>p_t/2$, its derivative is $2q-\lambda$.

If $p_t>\lambda$, $J$ is strictly increasing, giving $q=0$. If $p_t=\lambda$, it is constant until $q=h$ and strictly increasing thereafter. If $p_t<\lambda$, it is strictly decreasing until $q=h$ and strictly increasing thereafter. Hence only the neighboring integers $n,n+1$ can minimize it. Substitution gives the displayed amounts and difference. $\square$

A type not valuing $G$ has a constant total divisible amount $p_t/2$ in every minimizing action, unless it values no divisible class, in which case it receives the zero bundle.

When $p_t=\lambda$, a type’s allocation can be replaced by one supported on counts $0,n$, preserving its mean count and the constant utility $h$. For $n>0$, use the unique mixture of those counts with the required mean. Distribute each action’s divisible amount using its aggregate class proportions. When $n=0$, only count zero is needed.

Consequently:

Proposition 3. Some optimal allocation uses at most two actions per type, and all its indivisible counts belong to
$$ \{0,n,n+1\}. $$

The binary length of $n$ is polynomial in the input length. Here is an explicit bound. Set
$$ S=b+\sum_dc_d,\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t. $$
Assign each class entirely to a selected valuing type. For $G$, mix its neighboring integer counts, while keeping each type’s divisible amounts constant. If $R_t$ is the total aggregate utility supply assigned to type $t$, this construction has objective at most
$$ \sum_t\frac{R_t^2}{\mu_t}+\frac14 \leq \frac{S^2}{\mu_{\min}}+\frac14. $$
Thus the same expression bounds $F(\nu_*)$.

Doubling every bundle of $\nu_*$ gives an allocation with doubled resources and objective $4F(\nu_*)$. The supporting inequality gives
$$ \lambda b+\sum_dp_dc_d\leq 3F(\nu_*). $$
Since all prices are nonnegative,
$$ \lambda\leq \frac{3}{b}\left(\frac{S^2}{\mu_{\min}}+\frac14\right). $$
This bound has polynomial binary length, as does $n$.

4. The cubic-field theorem

Theorem 4. Every permitted instance has an optimal finite allocation whose masses and divisible amounts belong to one real algebraic field of degree at most three.

The proof also identifies the only regime in which irrationality is needed.

4.1. Components of the positive divisible flow

Fix an optimum and supporting prices. Form a bipartite graph whose vertices are divisible classes and types receiving a positive aggregate divisible amount. Join $d$ and $t$ when $w_{td}>0$.

Every divisible class occurs in this graph because $c_d>0$. Along every edge,
$$ p_d=p_t. $$
Thus each connected component has one common price $p$.

For a component, let $C$ be the sum of its class supplies and $M$ the sum of its type masses. Both are rational.

If every type in the component has a fixed count $k_t$ and constant utility $p/2$, summing utilities gives
$$ \frac p2 M=C+\sum_t\mu_tk_t. $$
Consequently its utility level is rational:
$$ \frac p2= \frac{C+\sum_t\mu_tk_t}{M}. $$

4.2. The lower half of a count interval

First suppose
$$ 2n\leq\lambda\leq 2n+1. $$
For $p_t<\lambda$, Lemma 2 gives $\Delta_t\geq 0$. Except at $\lambda=2n+1$, count $n$ is the unique minimizer. At that endpoint, a tie with count $n+1$ is possible only when $p_t\leq 2n$; both actions then have zero divisible amount.

Therefore every positive-flow component with price different from $\lambda$ has fixed counts and rational utility level.

If $\lambda$ equals either endpoint, then $h=\lambda/2$ is already rational. Consider instead
$$ 2n<\lambda<2n+1. $$
Every type with $p_t=\lambda$ receives a positive divisible amount: its utility is $h>n$, while its count is at most $n$. Let $M_\lambda$ and $C_\lambda$ be the total type mass and divisible supply across all components priced $\lambda$, and let $Z_\lambda$ be their total indivisible allocation. Constant utility gives
$$ Z_\lambda=hM_\lambda-C_\lambda. $$
All other types have fixed, rational aggregate counts. If $M_\lambda>0$, the indivisible supply equation determines $h$ rationally. If $M_\lambda=0$, this utility level is unused.

Thus in this entire regime the required typewise utility levels can be chosen rational.

4.3. The upper half and its two exceptional prices

Now suppose
$$ 2n+1<\lambda<2n+2. $$
Define
$$ r=\sqrt{\lambda-(2n+1)},\qquad p_0=2(n+r). $$
Then
$$ 0<r<1,\qquad h=n+\frac12+\frac{r^2}{2}, \qquad \lambda-p_0=(1-r)^2>0. $$

Lemma 2 yields the following classification for a type valuing $G$:

| Its price | Minimizing actions, using total divisible amount |
|---|---|
| $p_t<p_0$ | $(n+1,0)$ |
| $p_t=p_0$ | $(n,r)$ and $(n+1,0)$ |
| $p_0<p_t<\lambda$ | $(n,p_t/2-n)$ |
| $p_t=\lambda$ | $q\leq n$, with total utility $h$ |
| $p_t>\lambda$ | $(0,p_t/2)$ |

To verify the threshold, for $p_t\leq 2n$ the difference $\Delta_t$ is $-r^2<0$. For $2n<p_t<\lambda$, it is
$$ \Delta_t=\left(\frac{p_t}{2}-n\right)^2-r^2, $$
whose sign changes precisely at $p_t=p_0$.

Every positive-flow component priced neither $p_0$ nor $\lambda$ therefore has a rational utility level by the component equation above.

Let
$$ M_0^G,\quad M_0^N,\quad C_0 $$
denote, across all components priced $p_0$, respectively the mass of types valuing $G$, the mass of types not valuing $G$, and the divisible supply.

For their $G$-valuing types, let $A$ be the total mass assigned the lower action $(n,r)$. Their divisible allocation is $rA$, and their indivisible allocation is
$$ Z_0=(n+1)M_0^G-A. $$
The types not valuing $G$ have divisible amount $n+r$ per capita. Hence
$$ C_0=rA+(n+r)M_0^N. $$
Eliminating $A$ gives
$$ Z_0 = (n+1)M_0^G+M_0^N- \frac{C_0-nM_0^N}{r}. $$

As before, let $M_\lambda,C_\lambda,Z_\lambda$ describe the components priced $\lambda$. Then
$$ Z_\lambda=hM_\lambda-C_\lambda. $$

Every remaining type has a fixed aggregate indivisible allocation. In particular, a $G$-valuing type with zero divisible allocation must use count $n+1$ in this regime. Let $B_{\mathrm{fix}}$ be the total indivisible allocation outside the two exceptional groups. It is rational.

The indivisible supply equation becomes
$$ b= B_{\mathrm{fix}} +(n+1)M_0^G+M_0^N +hM_\lambda-C_\lambda -\frac{C_0-nM_0^N}{r}. $$
Substituting $h$ and multiplying by $r$ gives
$$ \frac{M_\lambda}{2}r^3+ \left( B_{\mathrm{fix}} +(n+1)M_0^G+M_0^N +\left(n+\frac12\right)M_\lambda -C_\lambda-b \right)r - (C_0-nM_0^N) =0. $$

If the $p_0$ group is nonempty, then
$$ C_0-nM_0^N=r(A+M_0^N)>0. $$
Indeed, every component contains a class of positive supply. It therefore contains either a non-$G$ type of positive mass or a $G$ type with positive lower-action mass.

The displayed polynomial is consequently nonzero, and $r$ has degree at most three over $\mathbb Q$. All required utility levels belong to $\mathbb Q(r)$.

Moreover, writing this polynomial as
$$ P(X)=aX^3+\beta X-K, \qquad a\geq 0,\quad K>0, $$
shows that it has exactly one positive root. On $X>0$,
$$ \frac{P(X)}X=aX^2+\beta-\frac KX $$
is strictly increasing, tends to $-\infty$ at zero, and is positive for sufficiently large $X$ whenever a positive root exists. Here existence is supplied by the optimum.

If the $p_0$ group is empty, the term involving $1/r$ disappears. When $M_\lambda>0$, the supply equation determines $h$ rationally; when $M_\lambda=0$, neither exceptional utility level is used. A rational optimum can then be reconstructed.

4.4. Reconstruction over the field

It remains to show that knowing the utility levels suffices to obtain an allocation over the same field.

Fix the graph and the type categories of the original optimum. Use aggregate variables $w_{td}$ only on its edges. Impose the class supply equations, the indivisible supply equation, and these typewise linear conditions:

  • For an ordinary type with fixed count $k_t$ and utility $\ell_t$,

$$ \sum_dw_{td}=\mu_t(\ell_t-k_t). $$

  • For a $G$-valuing type in the $p_0$ group, use a lower-action mass $a_t$ satisfying

$$ 0\leq a_t\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_dw_{td}=ra_t,\qquad z_t=(n+1)\mu_t-a_t. $$

  • For a $G$-valuing type in the $\lambda$ group,

$$ 0\leq z_t\leq n\mu_t,\qquad \sum_dw_{td}=\mu_th-z_t. $$

  • For types not valuing $G$, set $z_t=0$ and impose their constant-utility equation.
  • At the endpoint $\lambda=2n+1$, zero-divisible types allowed to mix $n,n+1$ receive the corresponding bounded mixture variable.

This is a nonempty bounded polyhedron over the relevant field: the original optimum supplies a feasible point. A vertex is obtained by solving a full-rank system of active linear constraints, so its coordinates belong to that field.

Reconstruct its actions as follows.

For an ordinary type, use its fixed count and divisible amounts $w_{td}/\mu_t$.

For a $p_0$ type, assign its lower action mass $a_t$, count $n$, and amounts
$$ x_d=\frac{w_{td}}{a_t} $$
when $a_t>0$. Their sum is $r$. The remaining mass receives count $n+1$ and no divisible amount.

For a $\lambda$ type, mix counts $0,n$ to attain mean $z_t/\mu_t$, and give every action total utility $h$. Distribute its divisible amount according to $w_{td}/W_t$, where $W_t=\sum_dw_{td}$, whenever $W_t>0$. The zero-total case is handled by its single zero-divisible action.

Every reconstructed action still minimizes its type’s original supporting expression: all positive divisible amounts use edges of the appropriate maximum price, and its count and utility satisfy the classification above. The supplies are preserved, so the resulting allocation is optimal.

This proves Theorem 4, with at most two actions per type.

5. Polynomial encoding length and the rational promise

Proposition 5. The algebraic allocation in Theorem 4 can be encoded with polynomially many bits in $m,\tau,L$.

Proof. The integer $n$ has polynomial binary length by Proposition 3. All coefficients of the cubic are sums and products of input rationals and $n$; therefore they have polynomial binary length. Ordinary utility levels have the same property because they are ratios of component supply and mass sums.

The reconstruction polyhedron has $O(m\tau)$ variables and constraints. Its coefficients are rational, or rational polynomials of degree at most two in $r$, with polynomial coefficient length.

Here are the needed size bounds for its vertex coordinates. Let $N$ be the number of variables and $B$ bound the coefficient lengths. Clear the rational denominators of an active $N$-by-$N$ system. Its determinant, viewed initially as a polynomial in an indeterminate, has degree at most $2N$. The determinant expansion has at most $N!$ terms; each is a product of $N$ polynomials of degree at most two. Consequently, its coefficient lengths are polynomial in $N,B$.

The minimal polynomial of $r$ has degree at most three and polynomial coefficient length. To check the latter assertion even if the displayed cubic is reducible, clear its denominators. A primitive irreducible factor has leading coefficient dividing that of the resulting integer polynomial. Every root is bounded by the elementary polynomial root bound. Since the degree is at most three, expressing factor coefficients through their roots bounds their lengths polynomially.

Reducing determinant polynomials modulo this minimal polynomial preserves polynomial length: there are $O(N)$ reduction steps, each using a fixed-degree relation with polynomial coefficient length. Inverting a nonzero reduced determinant is a linear algebra problem of dimension at most three over $\mathbb Q$, again preserving polynomial length. Cramer’s rule therefore gives vertex coordinates with polynomial-size field representations.

Finally, reconstruction uses polynomially many field operations on these coordinates. There are at most $2\tau$ actions and polynomially many amounts. Their encodings are polynomial in size. The number $r$ can be specified by the cubic and its unique positive root. $\square$

A further consequence addresses one open point from the notes.

Corollary 6. If a permitted instance has a rational minimizing allocation, then it has one whose rational masses, amounts, and integer counts have polynomial total encoding length.

Proof. The lower-half regime, and the upper-half regime without a $p_0$ group, already admit the rational reconstruction established above.

Consider the remaining regime, with a nonempty $p_0$ group. If $r$ were irrational, choose a divisible class in that group. Its price is $p_0$, and its supply is positive.

Every optimum minimizes the same supporting Lagrangian. Thus an optimal action receiving a positive amount of this class must have maximum desired price $p_0$. If its type values $G$, its positive total divisible amount is exactly $r$. If its type does not value $G$, that amount is exactly $n+r$. Both are irrational. A finite vector of rational divisible amounts cannot have either irrational sum.

Hence a rational minimizing allocation forces $r$ to be rational. The rational-root theorem applied to the polynomial-size cubic bounds the numerator and denominator lengths of $r$ polynomially. The reconstruction polyhedron then has rational coefficients of polynomial length. Cramer’s rule gives a rational vertex of polynomial length, and reconstruction preserves that bound. $\square$

This proves an output-size bound under the rational-optimum promise. It does not give an algorithm for finding such an optimum.

6. The rational-output obstruction

The following instance satisfies every input requirement:
$$ \mu_A=\mu_B=\frac12, \qquad b=\frac54,\qquad c_A=\frac12,\qquad c_B=\frac18. $$
There are two types:
$$ v_A=(1,1,0),\qquad v_B=(1,0,1), $$
with coordinates ordered as $G,A,B$.

Let
$$ s=2^{-1/3},\qquad u=\frac{3+s^2}{2},\qquad \gamma=\frac{1+s^2}{4},\qquad a=\frac{s^2}{4}. $$
Consider these four actions:

| Type | Mass | Count | Amount of $A$ | Amount of $B$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | $\frac12-\gamma$ | $0$ | $u$ | $0$ |
| $A$ | $\gamma$ | $1$ | $u-1$ | $0$ |
| $B$ | $a$ | $1$ | $0$ | $s$ |
| $B$ | $\frac12-a$ | $2$ | $0$ | $0$ |

All masses are positive and all amounts are nonnegative. Supplies are
$$ \gamma+a+2\left(\frac12-a\right)=\frac54, $$
$$ \frac u2-\gamma=\frac12, \qquad as=\frac{s^3}{4}=\frac18. $$

To certify global optimality, use
$$ \lambda=3+s^2,\qquad p_A=\lambda,\qquad p_B=2(1+s). $$
For every type-$A$ action,
$$ (q+x_A)^2-\lambda q-p_Ax_A = \left(q+x_A-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)^2-\frac{\lambda^2}{4} \geq-\frac{\lambda^2}{4}. $$
Both displayed type-$A$ actions attain equality.

For type $B$, minimize
$$ (q+x_B)^2-\lambda q-p_Bx_B. $$
At $q=0$ the minimum is $-(1+s)^2$. At $q=1$ it is $-2-2s^2$, attained at $x_B=s$. For $q\geq 2$, the minimizing amount is zero, and the values $q^2-\lambda q$ strictly increase because
$$ 2q+1-\lambda\geq 2-s^2>0. $$
Their minimum is attained at $q=2$, again with value $-2-2s^2$. Moreover,
$$ -(1+s)^2-(-2-2s^2)=(1-s)^2>0. $$
Thus the type-$B$ equality set is exactly
$$ (q,x_B)=(1,s),\qquad (2,0). $$

Summing these bounds over any feasible allocation gives
$$ F(\nu)\geq \lambda b+p_Ac_A+p_Bc_B-\frac{\lambda^2}{8}-1-s^2. $$
The displayed allocation attains the bound and is globally optimal.

Its objective is
$$ \begin{aligned} F_* &=\frac12u^2+a(1+s)^2+4\left(\frac12-a\right)\\ &=\frac{25}{8}+\frac{s^3}{2}+\frac{3s^4}{8}\\ &=\frac{27}{8}+\frac{3s}{16}. \end{aligned} $$
The rational-root theorem shows that $2X^3-1$ has no rational root, so $s$ and $F_*$ are irrational.

Every finite allocation with rational masses and rational divisible amounts has rational objective. It therefore cannot attain $F_*$. More strongly, equality in the certificate and positive $B$-supply force some positive-mass action to have $x_B=s$.

This example also realizes the cubic mechanism proved above: $n=1$, the $A$ component is priced $\lambda$, and the $B$ component is priced $p_0$. The cubic equation becomes
$$ \frac14r^3-\frac18=0, $$
whose positive root is $r=s$.

7. What remains open

The literal rational-output task fails on a valid constant-size input. For the intended complexity question with algebraic outputs, the results above remove two potential obstacles: unbounded output size and arbitrarily high algebraic degree are unnecessary.

The remaining algorithmic gap is substantial. The proof obtains the count interval, component partition, and exceptional price groups from an already existing optimum. It does not show how to find them in polynomial time. Nor does it establish hardness of doing so.
