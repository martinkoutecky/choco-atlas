The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates and oracle access to an unknown single-crossing ranking map \(R:[0,1]\to L(C)\), whose rational change points have at most \(L\) bits and whose mass order is known, what is the optimal worst-case number of queries \((u,x,y)\) needed to recover \(R\) almost everywhere?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, and let \(\mathcal L(C)\) denote strict rankings. Voters occupy \([0,1]\) with Lebesgue mass, and increasing coordinates give the known single-crossing order.
A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) ranking types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(m!\). In this elicitation model, this list is unknown and available only through the oracle.
The unknown map \(R:[0,1]\to\mathcal L(C)\) is piecewise constant and right-continuous on \([0,1)\), with \(R(1)\) equal to its left limit. For each distinct \(x,y\), the indicator that \(R(u)\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\) is monotone in \(u\), in either direction.
Every interior change point is a reduced rational \(p/q\) with \(\operatorname{bits}(p)+\operatorname{bits}(q)\le L\), where \(L\ge2\) is given. The change points and rankings are unknown.
A deterministic adaptive algorithm may query any rational \(u\in[0,1]\) and distinct \(x,y\in C\); the answer states whether \(R(u)\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\). Count distinct coordinate–unordered-candidate-pair queries, with repeats cached.
Recover the exact ordered interval rankings and rational boundaries, identifying maps that agree almost everywhere. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and the bit lengths of query coordinates.

What is the optimal worst-case number \(Q(m,L)\) of comparison queries needed to recover \(R\) almost everywhere, up to constant factors?

For \(n\) discrete voters with known single-crossing order and random access, the established lower bound is \(\Omega(m\log m+m\log n)\), with upper bound \(O(m^2\log n)\). [Dey–Misra](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/039.pdf)
Later work claims the stronger discrete lower bound \(\Omega(m^2\log n)\). [Dey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04175) No result for precisely the bounded-rational continuous oracle model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized voter mass to Lebesgue measure on the unit interval, interpreting the known mass order as increasing coordinates.
  • Preserved oracle-only access: the support list is the society's finite representation to be elicited, rather than an explicitly revealed input.
  • Required strict rankings and at most one reversal for each candidate pair, matching the discrete single-crossing definition for both ordered orientations.
  • Specified a piecewise-constant representative with right-continuous boundary values and a left-limit value at the final endpoint.
  • Defined rational bit length as the sum of the binary lengths of the reduced numerator and denominator; the bound is known.
  • Allowed arbitrary rational query coordinates and deterministic adaptive algorithms, counting repeated or reversed comparisons only once.
  • Interpreted almost-everywhere recovery as exact recovery of the ordered positive-length intervals and their boundaries.
  • Used the nondegenerate candidate domain and allowed constant ranking maps.

Optimal query complexity

All logarithms below are base two. I interpret “binary rational” as a rational whose numerator and denominator are encoded in binary. I also give the answer when it means a dyadic rational.

Let \(\mathcal B_L\) be the set of admissible interior boundaries, and put \(n_L=|\mathcal B_L|+1\). For binary-encoded rationals,

\(\mathcal B_L=\{p/q:0<p<q,\ \gcd(p,q)=1,\ \operatorname{bits}(p)+\operatorname{bits}(q)\le L\}\).

The optimal worst-case query complexity is

\[ Q(m,L)=\Theta\!\left( m\log m+ mn_L\log(1+m/n_L)+ m^2\log(1+n_L/m) \right). \]

Equivalently, defining \(F(m,n)\) by

\[ F(m,n)= \begin{cases} m\log m+mn\log(1+m/n),&n\le m,\\ m\log m+m^2\log(1+n/m),&n\ge m, \end{cases} \]

we have \(Q(m,L)=\Theta(F(m,n_L))\).

For binary-encoded rationals, \(n_L=\Theta(L2^L)\). For dyadic rationals, \(n_L=\Theta(2^{L/2})\). Substituting either estimate in the formula preserves its constant-factor accuracy.

Consequently, \(Q(m,L)=\Theta(m^2L)\) when \(L\) is sufficiently large relative to \(\log m\), but this is not a uniform answer: for every fixed \(L\), the complexity is \(\Theta(m\log m)\).

The algorithms below use query coordinates of \(O(L)\) bits and can be implemented in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

1. Reduction to a finite ordered domain

Write the admissible boundaries as \(b_1<\cdots<b_N\), where \(N=|\mathcal B_L|\), and set \(b_0=0\), \(b_{N+1}=1\). There are \(n=N+1\) elementary cells.

Every permitted map is constant on each cell \([b_i,b_{i+1})\), with the final cell including \(1\). Right-continuity makes a query at \(b_i\) return that cell’s ranking. Thus the problem is exactly recovery of a single-crossing sequence of \(n\) rankings, allowing repetitions.

Conversely, every single-crossing sequence on these cells defines a permitted map. Its masses are differences of rational boundaries; in the dyadic interpretation, these differences are dyadic.

This reduction applies to lower bounds even when the algorithm queries arbitrary rational coordinates: every such coordinate belongs to a known elementary cell.

Let \(K=\binom m2\). Each pair changes orientation at most once. Every change of ranking changes at least one pair, so there are at most \(K\) changes. Moreover, a ranking cannot disappear and later reappear: if the rankings at two coordinates agree, monotonicity forces every pair to keep that orientation between them. Hence

\(\tau\le\min\{n,K+1\}\),

and the distinct positive-mass rankings occur in a uniquely ordered interval representation.

It remains to prove that the finite-domain complexity is \(\Theta(F(m,n))\).

2. Upper bounds

2.1 Eliciting a ranking close to a known ranking

Suppose a known ranking lists candidates as \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\), and the new ranking differs from it on \(d\) unordered pairs.

Insert \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\), in that order, into a list sorted according to the new ranking. Let \(d_i\) be the number of previously inserted candidates that \(c_i\) must pass. Then \(\sum_i d_i=d\).

Exponential search from the right end, followed by binary search, locates the insertion position using \(O(1+\log(1+d_i))\) comparisons. Concavity of the logarithm gives the total bound

\(O\!\left(m+m\log(1+d/m)\right)\).

Ordinary linear insertion instead uses \(O(m+d)\) comparisons.

These are comparison algorithms for an unknown strict order, so each comparison is an allowed oracle query.

2.2 At most \(m\) cells

Suppose \(n\le m\). Sort the first cell using \(O(m\log m)\) comparisons, then elicit successive cells using exponential insertion.

Let \(d_j\) count the pairs changing between cells \(j\) and \(j+1\). Single crossing gives \(\sum_jd_j\le K\). For \(n\ge2\), concavity therefore bounds the total cost by

\(O\!\left(m\log m+mn+m(n-1)\log\!\left(1+\frac{K}{m(n-1)}\right)\right)\).

Because \(K\le m^2/2\) and \(n\le m\), this is

\(O\!\left(m\log m+mn\log(1+m/n)\right)\).

For \(n=1\), only the initial sorting is needed.

2.3 At least \(m\) cells

Suppose \(n\ge m\). Choose at most \(m+1\) cell indices, including the first and last, so that successive selected indices differ by \(O(1+n/m)\).

Elicit their rankings in increasing order, using ordinary linear insertion after the first. Across these selected rankings, the total number of changed pairs is at most \(K\). Their total elicitation cost is therefore \(O(m\log m+m^2)\).

For each pair:

  • If its orientations in the first and last cells agree, monotonicity proves that it never changes.
  • Otherwise, the selected rankings identify its unique bracket of change. Binary search within that bracket locates the exact change boundary using \(O(\log(1+n/m))\) further comparisons.

Thus the total cost is

\(O\!\left(m\log m+m^2\log(1+n/m)\right)\).

After finding all pairwise thresholds, their sorted distinct values are exactly the society’s boundaries. The ranking on every resulting interval follows from the known pair orientations.

This proves the upper bound \(O(F(m,n))\).

3. Lower bounds

A deterministic algorithm making at most \(q\) distinct queries has at most \(2^q\) possible answer transcripts. Cached repetitions introduce no additional branching. Therefore a family of \(M\) pairwise different permitted maps requires at least \(\log M\) queries in the worst case.

3.1 Sorting

Constant maps provide \(m!\) possible rankings. Hence every algorithm needs \(\Omega(\log(m!))=\Omega(m\log m)\) queries.

3.2 Independent thresholds in parallel swap layers

We first construct the lower bound for many cells, and also a bound of order \(mn\) when \(n\le m\).

A reversal network

For \(s\) candidates, start with their identity order. Perform \(s\) layers of adjacent swaps, alternating between position pairs

\((1,2),(3,4),\ldots\)

and

\((2,3),(4,5),\ldots\).

Swaps within a layer are disjoint.

After these \(s\) layers, the order is reversed. Indeed, a candidate initially at an odd position moves right, and one initially at an even position moves left. At an endpoint it pauses for one layer and then reverses direction. Counting its moves shows that a candidate initially at position \(j\) finishes at position \(s+1-j\).

The total number of swaps is \(\binom s2\): for \(s=2r\) it is \(r^2+r(r-1)\), and for \(s=2r+1\) it is \(r(r+1)+r^2\).

Reversal has \(\binom s2\) inversions. Every adjacent swap changes the inversion count by one, so every swap increases it. Consequently, every candidate pair crosses exactly once.

Choosing the crossing thresholds

Assume \(N=n-1\ge4\), and set

\(s=\min\{m,\lfloor N/2\rfloor\}\), \(g=\lfloor m/s\rfloor\), and \(r=\lfloor N/s\rfloor\).

Partition \(gs\) candidates into \(g\) consecutive blocks of size \(s\), keeping the relative order of different blocks fixed. Run the reversal network independently inside each block.

Allocate \(r\) consecutive admissible boundaries to each layer. Each swap independently chooses one of its layer’s \(r\) boundaries as its crossing threshold. This produces a valid ranking sequence: swaps in one layer are disjoint and commute, and every earlier layer finishes before the next begins.

Different threshold choices produce maps differing on a positive-length interval. The family therefore has size

\(r^{\,g\binom s2}\).

Since \(g\ge m/(2s)\) and \(\binom s2\ge s^2/4\), its logarithm is at least \((ms/8)\log r\).

It follows that:

  • If \(N\ge2m\), the lower bound is \(\Omega(m^2\log(1+n/m))\).
  • If \(4\le N<2m\), it is \(\Omega(mN)\).

This proves the required lower bound throughout \(n\ge m\), and supplies \(\Omega(mn)\) when \(n\le m\).

3.3 The extra logarithm when cells are scarce

For \(n\le m\), the preceding construction lacks the factor \(\log(1+m/n)\) when \(m/n\) is large. The following construction supplies it.

Assume \(n\ge12\) and \(m\ge4096n\). Put

\(T=\lfloor n/3\rfloor\), \(k=\lfloor\sqrt{m/(8T)}\rfloor\), and \(B=\lfloor m/k\rfloor\).

Use \(kB\) candidates labelled \((a,b)\), with \(0\le a<k\) and \(1\le b\le B\). Associate to each candidate the affine function

\(f_{a,b}(z)=b+az\).

Any unused candidates occupy fixed positions below these candidates.

For each integer \(t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}\), create three consecutive rankings:

  • Increasing order of \(f_{a,b}(t-\varepsilon)\).
  • Increasing order of \(f_{a,b}(t)\), with arbitrary strict ordering inside each tie class.
  • Increasing order of \(f_{a,b}(t+\varepsilon)\).

Here \(\varepsilon=1/(2k)\). The first and third rankings have no ties: the difference of two scores is an integer plus or minus a nonzero fraction of absolute value less than one.

There are \(3T\le n\) rankings. Assign them to consecutive elementary cells and repeat the last ranking on any remaining cells.

Single-crossing verification

For two candidates, the score difference is affine in \(z\).

If their slopes agree, their order is constant. Otherwise, their scores cross at most once. At a sampled integer crossing, the arbitrary order in the middle ranking chooses one of the two surrounding orientations. It therefore cannot create a second change.

Thus every constructed profile is single crossing.

Counting independent rankings

At time \(t\), every integer score \(y\) satisfying

\(1+(k-1)t\le y\le B\)

has exactly \(k\) candidates in its tie class: one for each slope \(a\), with intercept \(b=y-at\).

From \(k^2\le m/(8T)\), we obtain \(B\ge2kT\). Consequently there are at least \(B/2\) such classes at every time \(t\).

Their orders can be chosen independently, giving at least

\((k!)^{TB/2}\)

different maps. Distinct choices differ on the cell assigned to the corresponding middle ranking, which has positive length.

For \(k\ge4\), \(\log(k!)\ge(k/4)\log k\). Also,

\(T\ge n/4\), \(kB\ge m/2\), and
\(\log k\ge\frac12\log(m/n)-3\ge\frac14\log(m/n)\).

Hence the logarithm of the family size is

\(\Omega(mn\log(m/n))=\Omega(mn\log(1+m/n))\).

This proves the missing lower bound.

3.4 Combining the cases

When \(n\ge m\), Section 3.2 gives the required term \(m^2\log(1+n/m)\).

When \(12\le n\le m\):

  • If \(m/n\ge4096\), Section 3.3 applies.
  • Otherwise, \(\log(1+m/n)\) is bounded by an absolute constant, and Section 3.2 suffices.

When \(n<12\), the entire expression \(F(m,n)\) is \(O(m\log m)\), so the sorting lower bound suffices.

Taking the maximum of this bound and the sorting bound is, within a constant factor, their sum. Thus the lower bound is \(\Omega(F(m,n))\), completing the query-complexity proof.

4. Counting admissible boundaries

4.1 Binary-encoded rationals

The upper bound \(N=O(L2^L)\) follows by grouping numerator-denominator pairs by bit lengths \(a,b\). For fixed \(a+b\), there are at most \(L\) groups, each containing at most \(2^{a+b}\) pairs.

For completeness, the matching lower bound includes the coprimality restriction.

Take \(a+b=L\), with \(L/4\le a<L/2\), and write \(P=2^{a-1}\), \(H=2^{b-1}\). Consider

\(P\le p<2P\), \(H\le q<2H\).

Then \(p<q\), and there are \(PH\) pairs. The number with a common divisor is at most

\[ PH\sum_{d=2}^{\infty}d^{-2} +(P+H)\sum_{d=2}^{2P}d^{-1} +2P. \]

The first sum is at most \(3/4\). The remaining terms divided by \(PH\) tend uniformly to zero as \(P\) grows, because \(H\ge P\). Thus a fixed positive fraction of these pairs are coprime for sufficiently large \(L\).

There are \(\Theta(L)\) disjoint choices of \(a,b\), each contributing \(\Omega(2^L)\) reduced fractions. Therefore

\(N=\Theta(L2^L)\).

For \(L=2\), there are no interior boundaries; this finite exception does not affect the stated constant-factor estimate for \(n_L=N+1\).

4.2 Dyadic rationals

If every mass is dyadic, every boundary, being a sum of masses, is dyadic. Conversely, any selection of dyadic boundaries gives dyadic masses.

A reduced interior dyadic boundary is \(p/2^k\), with \(p\) odd. Its denominator has \(k+1\) bits. Consequently,

\[ N=\sum_{k=1}^{L-2} 2^{\,\min\{k,L-k-1\}-1}. \]

The summands grow geometrically toward the middle and decrease geometrically afterward. Hence

\(n_L=N+1=\Theta(2^{L/2})\).

5. Polynomial running time and coordinate lengths

Enumerating the boundary universe can take exponential time in \(L\). We can nevertheless attain the optimal query bound in polynomial time by using two implementations.

Let \(B_0=L+\lceil\log(m+1)\rceil\).

5.1 Smaller \(L\)

When \(L<16\log(m+1)\), enumerate and sort \(\mathcal B_L\), then apply Section 2.

There are \(O(L2^L)\) numerator-denominator pairs to inspect. All queried coordinates are admissible boundaries or \(0\), so their numerator-plus-denominator lengths are at most \(L\), apart from an irrelevant constant for \(0\).

A conservative bit-operation bound is

\(O((L2^L+m^2+\tau m\log m)B_0^5)\).

Because this branch has \(2^L<(m+1)^{16}\), the bound is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

5.2 Larger \(L\)

When \(L\ge16\log(m+1)\), use pairwise dyadic bisection directly.

Sort the rankings at \(0\) and \(1\). A pair agreeing at both endpoints is constant. For a disagreeing pair, maintain a bracket \(a<t\le b\) for its crossing threshold and make \(2L+2\) midpoint queries.

Every admissible denominator is less than \(2^{L-1}\). Distinct admissible fractions therefore differ by more than \(2^{-2L+2}\). The final bracket has length \(2^{-2L-2}\), so it contains exactly one admissible threshold.

That threshold can be recovered in polynomial time by continued fractions. Explicitly, for the bracket midpoint \(v\),

\(|v-t|\le2^{-2L-3}<1/(2q^2)\),

where \(q\) is the threshold’s denominator. The classical continued-fraction criterion says that a reduced fraction satisfying this inequality is a convergent of \(v\). Compute its convergents and select the unique admissible one in \((a,b]\).

This implementation uses \(O(m\log m+m^2L)\) queries. Its query coordinates have numerator-plus-denominator length at most \(4L+5\).

Even the dyadic boundary universe satisfies
\(n_L\ge2^{\lfloor(L-1)/2\rfloor}\). In this branch, therefore,
\(\log(n_L/m)=\Omega(L)\), while \(\log n_L=O(L)\). The proved optimum is consequently \(\Theta(m^2L)\), so bisection attains it within a constant factor.

A conservative bit-operation bound is

\(O((m^2+\tau m\log m)B_0^5)\).

In either implementation, reconstructing the output requires no further oracle queries. Sorting candidates by the recovered pair orientations on each interval takes \(O(\tau m\log m)\) known comparisons. Boundary differences have \(O(L)\)-bit rational representations.

6. Relation to the cited discrete claims

The pairwise-search upper bound and sorting-based lower bound agree with the established results of [Dey–Misra](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/039.pdf).

The proof of Theorem 5 in [Dey’s later paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.04175) does not establish its stated bound as written. With a fixed single-crossing voter order, copies of \(\ell\) fixed ranking types must occupy consecutive blocks. If each type appears, the number of profiles is \(\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}\), rather than \(\ell^n\).

The formula proved here also shows why the parameter range matters. At \(n=m\), the optimal discrete complexity is \(\Theta(m^2)\), so a uniform lower bound \(\Omega(m^2\log n)\) cannot hold. When \(n\ge m^2\), our bound becomes \(\Theta(m^2\log n)\), validating that order of growth in the large-voter regime.
