The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), and \(\tau\) complete ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), run PFR on the type masses, with each type consuming at a rate proportional to its mass, candidate capacities \(1\), and stopping when total consumed mass is \(k\). Let \(p_c\) be the consumed amount of candidate \(c\). Can an exact sampler output a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) with \(\Pr[c\in W]=p_c\) for every candidate and with every sampled committee satisfying continuous PSC: for every set \(S\subseteq C\) and integer \(\ell\in[k]\), if the mass of types ranking all of \(S\) above \(C\setminus S\) is at least \(\ell/k\), then \(|W\cap S|\ge\min(|S|,\ell)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 1\) candidates and let \(k\) be an integer with \(1\le k\le m\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete ranking types \(\succ_t\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.

Phragmén’s fractional rule (PFR) starts with every candidate unconsumed and of capacity \(1\). Each type \(t\) continuously consumes its highest-ranked candidate with remaining capacity at aggregate rate \(\mu_t\), switching when that candidate is exhausted. Simultaneous exhaustions are processed together. Stop when total consumption reaches \(k\), and let \(p_c\) be the amount consumed of candidate \(c\).

For \(S\subseteq C\), define \(M(S)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b\ \forall a\in S,\,b\in C\setminus S}\mu_t\). A committee \(W\subseteq C\) satisfies continuous proportionality for solid coalitions (PSC) if \(|W|=k\) and, for every \(S\subseteq C\) and integer \(1\le\ell\le k\), \(M(S)\ge\ell/k\) implies \(|W\cap S|\ge\min\{|S|,\ell\}\).

An exact sampler uses independent unbiased random bits, terminates almost surely, and outputs only continuous-PSC committees, with \(\Pr[c\in W]=p_c\) for every \(c\in C\).

Does such an exact sampler exist with expected running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is this exact sampling problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For explicitly listed finite voters, PFR is candidate monotone, satisfies ex-ante PSC, and admits a lottery supported entirely on PSC committees. [Peters (2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10095v2)
No result settling exact sampling complexity for the binary-rational support representation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made rankings strict and complete, and restricted committee size to an integer between 1 and m, following the discrete domain.
  • Fixed each type’s aggregate consumption rate to its mass; simultaneous candidate exhaustions are processed together.
  • Specified unbiased random bits, almost-sure termination, and expected bit running time because exact rational probabilities need not permit bounded-time sampling with unbiased bits.
  • Defined L as total input bit length and treated the listed positive-mass types as distinct support elements.

Result

An exact sampler exists with expected running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

The construction uses aggregate consumption by ranking type, followed by rounding on a bipartite graph. It extends the prefix-coalition construction underlying [Peters’s Theorem 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10095v2). The proof below establishes correctness directly for binary-rational masses and includes exact arithmetic and random-bit complexity.

1. Consumption and prefix coalitions

For each type \(t\) and candidate \(c\), let \(q_{tc}\) be the amount of \(c\) consumed by type \(t\) during PFR.

Before all candidates are exhausted, the aggregate consumption rate is \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Consequently, stopping after total consumption \(k\) means stopping at time \(k\), including when \(k=m\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \sum_c q_{tc}=k\mu_t,\qquad p_c=\sum_t q_{tc},\qquad 0\le p_c\le1,\qquad \sum_c p_c=k.\)

Let \(P_t(r)\) be the set of the first \(r\) candidates in type \(t\)’s ranking. For a nonempty set \(S\), define

\(\displaystyle T(S)=\{t:P_t(|S|)=S\}.\)

Because rankings are strict and complete, these are exactly the types ranking every member of \(S\) above every candidate outside \(S\). Hence

\(\displaystyle M(S)=\sum_{t\in T(S)}\mu_t.\)

There are at most \(\tau m\) distinct nonempty sets among the \(P_t(r)\). Every nonempty set with positive \(M(S)\) occurs among them.

We will use the following consumption property.

Lemma 1. If some candidate in \(S\) has \(p_c<1\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T(S)}\sum_{c\in S}q_{tc}=kM(S).\)

Proof. Such a candidate retains positive capacity throughout the consumption process: consumption is nondecreasing, and its final consumption is less than one. Therefore, every type in \(T(S)\) always has an available candidate in \(S\), and never consumes outside \(S\). Each such type consumes a total of \(k\mu_t\). Summing proves the identity. \(\square\)

2. Constructing integral group quotas

Initialize residual allocations \(a_{tc}=q_{tc}\). We construct a bipartite graph whose right vertices are the candidates. Each left vertex will have an integer weighted degree.

Process ranks \(r=1,\ldots,m\). At rank \(r\), process every distinct set \(S=P_t(r)\), in any fixed order. Compute its residual budget

\(\displaystyle b_S=\sum_{t\in T(S)}\sum_{c\in S}a_{tc},\qquad d_S=\lfloor b_S\rfloor.\)

If \(d_S=0\), make no change. Otherwise, choose quantities \(x_{tc}\), indexed by \(t\in T(S)\) and \(c\in S\), satisfying

\(\displaystyle 0\le x_{tc}\le a_{tc},\qquad \sum_{t\in T(S)}\sum_{c\in S}x_{tc}=d_S.\)

These quantities can be obtained greedily: scan the eligible entries and take the smaller of the entry’s residual amount and the still-needed amount. This succeeds because their total is \(b_S\ge d_S\).

Create a left vertex labelled \(S\), with edge weights

\(\displaystyle w_{Sc}=\sum_{t\in T(S)}x_{tc}\quad(c\in S),\)

and subtract \(x_{tc}\) from the corresponding residual entries. This vertex has weighted degree exactly \(d_S\), a positive integer, and all its neighbors lie in \(S\).

For subsequent arguments, index the created vertices by \(j\), writing their labels, degrees, and weights as \(S_j,d_j,w_{jc}\).

Lemma 2. At the end of this construction,

\(\displaystyle \sum_jd_j=k,\qquad \sum_jw_{jc}=p_c\quad\text{for every }c.\)

Moreover, there are at most \(k\) left vertices, and every edge weight lies in \([0,1]\).

Proof. Each operation removes exactly the integer \(d_j\) from the residual total. Initially that total is \(k\).

At rank \(m\), the only prefix set is \(C\), and every residual entry is eligible. Its budget is therefore

\(\displaystyle b_C=k-\sum_{\text{previous }j}d_j,\)

which is a nonnegative integer. Processing \(C\) removes this entire remaining budget. All residual entries are then zero, since they are nonnegative.

Thus every initial allocation \(q_{tc}\) has been assigned to graph edges, proving both identities. Each created vertex removes at least one unit, so there are at most \(k\) vertices. Finally, every edge weight is nonnegative and at most its candidate’s total incident weight \(p_c\le1\). \(\square\)

3. Why these quotas guarantee PSC

Consider any integral edge selection satisfying

\(\displaystyle \deg(j)=d_j\quad\text{for every left vertex }j,\qquad \deg(c)\le1\quad\text{for every candidate }c.\)

Let \(W\) be the candidates incident to selected edges. By Lemma 2, \(|W|=\sum_jd_j=k\).

We prove that \(W\) satisfies PSC, provided additionally that every candidate with \(p_c=1\) is selected. The rounding procedure in the next section guarantees this condition.

Fix \(S\subseteq C\) and \(1\le\ell\le k\) with \(M(S)\ge\ell/k\). The case \(S=\varnothing\) is automatic.

If every candidate in \(S\) has \(p_c=1\), then \(S\subseteq W\), giving the required bound. Otherwise, Lemma 1 gives an initial coalition budget

\(\displaystyle B=\sum_{t\in T(S)}\sum_{c\in S}q_{tc}=kM(S)\ge\ell.\)

Also,

\(\displaystyle B\le\sum_{c\in S}p_c<|S|,\)

so the required representation in this case is \(\ell\).

Since \(M(S)>0\), the construction processes \(S\). Let \(b\) be its residual budget immediately before that step. Let

\(\displaystyle A=\sum_{\substack{j\text{ created before this step}\\S_j\subseteq S}}d_j.\)

In particular, \(A\) is an integer.

We claim that

\(\displaystyle B-b\le A.\)

To justify this, consider any earlier operation that removes allocation belonging to a type \(t\in T(S)\). If its prefix length is smaller than \(|S|\), its label must be \(P_t(r)\subseteq S\). An earlier operation at the same prefix length cannot remove such allocation: distinct prefix sets of that length have disjoint sets of supporting types.

Thus every earlier operation removing any of the budget \(B\) has its label contained in \(S\). Its removal from this particular coalition is at most its total removal \(d_j\). Summing proves the claim. This argument allows an earlier group to include types outside \(T(S)\).

When \(S\) is processed, it contributes a quota of \(\lfloor b\rfloor\), interpreted as zero if no vertex is created. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle A+\lfloor b\rfloor=\lfloor A+b\rfloor\ge\lfloor B\rfloor\ge\ell.\)

All vertices counted here have neighbors only in \(S\). Their prescribed degrees select \(A+\lfloor b\rfloor\) distinct candidates in \(S\), because candidate degrees are at most one. Hence

\(\displaystyle |W\cap S|\ge\ell=\min\{|S|,\ell\}.\)

This proves every PSC constraint.

4. Exact randomized rounding

Add a dummy left vertex \(z\), with an edge to each candidate \(c\) of weight

\(\displaystyle w_{zc}=1-p_c.\)

The resulting graph has integer weighted degree at every vertex:

  • real left vertex \(j\) has degree \(d_j\);
  • the dummy vertex has degree \(m-k\);
  • every candidate has degree one.

We now round all edge weights to zero or one while preserving these degrees and preserving every edge weight in expectation.

Consider the subgraph consisting of edges with weights strictly between zero and one. Any vertex incident to such edges has at least two of them. Indeed, exactly one fractional incident edge, together with integral incident edges, would give a noninteger weighted degree.

Consequently, if this subgraph is nonempty, it contains a cycle. Take a simple cycle; its length is even because the graph is bipartite. Assign alternating signs \(+1,-1\) to its edges, and write \(u_e\) for these signs, with \(u_e=0\) off the cycle.

Define

\(\displaystyle \alpha=\min\bigl(\{1-w_e:u_e=1\}\cup\{w_e:u_e=-1\}\bigr),\)

\(\displaystyle \beta=\min\bigl(\{w_e:u_e=1\}\cup\{1-w_e:u_e=-1\}\bigr).\)

Both are positive. Make one of the following updates:

\(\displaystyle w\leftarrow w+\alpha u\quad\text{with probability }\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\)

\(\displaystyle w\leftarrow w-\beta u\quad\text{with probability }\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}.\)

Both updates keep weights in \([0,1]\), preserve every vertex’s weighted degree, and make at least one previously fractional edge integral. Integral edges are never modified.

Moreover, conditional on the current graph, every edge’s expected change is zero, since

\(\displaystyle \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\beta=0.\)

Repeat until all edges are integral. There are at most as many updates as graph edges, because each update strictly reduces the number of fractional edges. This deterministic bound, together with the conditional expectation identity, shows that each final edge indicator has expectation equal to its initial weight.

Output the candidates whose selected edge is incident to a real left vertex. Every candidate has exactly one selected incident edge. Hence the output has size \(k\), and

\(\displaystyle \Pr[c\in W]=\sum_j\mathbb{E}[w^{\mathrm{final}}_{jc}]=\sum_jw_{jc}=p_c.\)

If \(p_c=1\), its dummy edge has weight zero and remains zero throughout rounding, so \(c\) is always selected. Section 3 therefore proves that every possible output satisfies continuous PSC.

5. Polynomial bit complexity and unbiased bits

Computing the consumption allocation

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=u_t/v_t\), and set

\(\displaystyle H=\prod_tv_t,\qquad h_t=H\mu_t.\)

The \(h_t\) are positive integers summing to \(H\), and the binary length of \(H\) is \(O(L)\).

PFR can be simulated by exhaustion events. Between events, every type consumes a fixed candidate, and each candidate’s rate is of the form \(a_c/H\), where \(a_c\) is an integer between zero and \(H\). The next exhaustion time is obtained by dividing remaining capacity by its positive rate. Compare this time with the remaining time until \(k\), and process all simultaneous exhaustions together.

There are at most \(m\) exhaustion events, since each exhausts at least one previously unexhausted candidate.

Here is an explicit denominator bound. Suppose time, remaining capacities, and accumulated allocations currently have common denominator \(D\). If the next event exhausts a candidate with remaining capacity \(b/D\) and rate \(a/H\), its duration is

\(\displaystyle \Delta=\frac{bH}{Da}.\)

The time increment has denominator dividing \(Da\), and a consuming type receives an increment

\(\displaystyle \mu_t\Delta=\frac{h_tb}{Da}.\)

Thus all quantities after the event have common denominator \(Da\). For a final interval ending at time \(k\), multiplying the current denominator by \(H\) suffices.

Starting with denominator one, we therefore obtain a common denominator \(D_*\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle D_*\le H^{m+1},\qquad \log_2D_*=O((m+1)L).\)

All quantities have magnitude bounded by \(m\), so their numerators also have polynomial bit length. Event simulation consequently uses polynomially many exact arithmetic operations on integers of polynomial bit length.

Constructing and rounding the graph

There are at most \(\tau m\) prefix groups and only \(\tau m\) allocation entries. Enumerating the groups and processing them by direct scans takes polynomial time.

Greedy extraction uses subtraction and minima with integer targets. Thus every residual allocation and every graph weight remains an integer multiple of \(1/D_*\).

The rounding graph has at most \(k+1\) left vertices and at most \((k+1)m\) edges. Every rounding update also preserves the denominator grid: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are minima of multiples of \(1/D_*\). Cycle searches and weight updates therefore take polynomial time.

Each required random choice has a rational probability whose denominator is at most \(2D_*\). To generate a Bernoulli outcome of probability \(a/b\), generate \(s=\lceil\log_2b\rceil\) independent unbiased bits, interpreting them as an integer \(J\) uniformly distributed on \(\{0,\ldots,2^s-1\}\). Reject and repeat if \(J\ge b\); otherwise return success exactly when \(J<a\). Handle probabilities zero and one directly.

Conditional on acceptance, \(J\) is uniform on \(\{0,\ldots,b-1\}\), so this generates the probability exactly. Acceptance probability is at least one half, giving at most two trials in expectation and almost-sure termination. Each trial uses polynomially many bit operations.

There are at most \((k+1)m\) random rounding choices. Combining these bounds proves almost-sure termination and expected running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
