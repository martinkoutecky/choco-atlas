The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(K\) alternatives and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over their complete strict rankings, with rational masses encoded in binary, decide whether there exists a center ranking \(r_0\) such that for every pair \(r',r\), \(\mu(r')>\mu(r)\) implies \(d(r',r_0)\le d(r,r_0)\); return such a center or report that none exists. The comparison ranges over all rankings, including those outside the support with mass zero.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m=K\ge 3\) alternatives, and let \(\mathcal R\) be all complete strict rankings of \(A\). Use Kendall tau inversion distance \(d\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct ranking types with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Set \(\mu(r)=0\) outside the support. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
A center is any \(r_0\in\mathcal R\) satisfying \(\mu(r')>\mu(r)\Rightarrow d(r',r_0)\le d(r,r_0)\) for every \(r',r\in\mathcal R\), including rankings outside the support. No additional strictness or nonuniformity condition is imposed.
Decide whether a center exists, returning one if it does and reporting none otherwise.

Is this recognition problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For an explicitly listed finite electorate, Flexible Consensus recognition and finding a center admit a polynomial-time algorithm. Its stated bound is \(O(s^2m\sqrt{\log m}+s^2\log s)\), where \(s=\min(m!,n)\) and \(n\) is the number of voters; this theorem does not state a bound for binary-rational support input.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The undefined distance is Kendall tau inversion distance, as in the discrete definition.
  • Restricted the number of alternatives to K≥3, matching the discrete domain.
  • Defined L as the total binary input length, including rankings and rational masses.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The recognition problem is in \(\mathrm P\), and a center can be returned in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof gives an algorithm directly for the support representation, without expanding the rational masses into an electorate.

Write \(S=\{r:\mu(r)>0\}\), so \(|S|=\tau\), and let \(M=\binom m2\), the maximum Kendall distance.

1. Every center belongs to the support

Lemma. If \(c\) is a center, then \(c\in S\).

Proof. Suppose \(c\notin S\). Choose \(r\in S\), which exists because the masses sum to one. Then \(\mu(r)>\mu(c)=0\), so the center condition requires

\[ d(r,c)\le d(c,c)=0. \]

A distance of zero means \(r=c\), contradicting \(r\in S\) and \(c\notin S\). Thus \(c\in S\). \(\square\)

Consequently, there are only \(\tau\) candidates to examine.

2. Reducing comparisons with unlisted rankings to a count

Fix a candidate \(c\in S\), and define

\[ D_c=\max_{r\in S}d(r,c). \]

Every supported ranking has strictly greater mass than every unsupported ranking. Therefore, all center inequalities involving one supported and one unsupported ranking hold exactly when

\[ d(q,c)\ge D_c\qquad\text{for every }q\notin S. \]

Equivalently, define the open ball

\[ B_{<D_c}(c)=\{q\in\mathcal R:d(q,c)<D_c\}. \]

The required condition is \(B_{<D_c}(c)\subseteq S\).

Let

\[ a_c=\bigl|\{r\in S:d(r,c)<D_c\}\bigr|. \]

Since the set counted by \(a_c\) is precisely \(S\cap B_{<D_c}(c)\), we obtain the following exact test:

\[ B_{<D_c}(c)\subseteq S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_c=|B_{<D_c}(c)|. \]

The strict inequality defining the ball is essential: an unsupported ranking at distance exactly \(D_c\) is permitted by the problem’s weak distance inequality.

3. Counting Kendall-distance balls in polynomial time

Let \(h_j(k)\) denote the number of permutations of \(j\) objects having exactly \(k\) inversions. Set \(h_0(0)=1\), and set \(h_j(k)=0\) whenever \(k<0\) or \(k>\binom j2\).

For every center \(c\), labeling each alternative by its position in \(c\) identifies rankings with permutations of \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\). Under this identification, \(d(q,c)\) is the inversion count of the corresponding permutation. Hence

\[ |\{q\in\mathcal R:d(q,c)=k\}|=h_m(k), \]

independently of \(c\).

To compute these numbers, insert the largest object \(j\) into a permutation of \(j-1\) objects. Its position contributes exactly one of \(0,\ldots,j-1\) new inversions. Removing \(j\) reverses this construction uniquely, so

\[ h_j(k)=\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}h_{j-1}(k-t). \]

Subtracting the corresponding formula at \(k-1\) yields

\[ h_j(k)=h_j(k-1)+h_{j-1}(k)-h_{j-1}(k-j). \]

With \(h_j(-1)=0\), this computes each entry using a constant number of additions and subtractions. There are \(O(mM)=O(m^3)\) entries.

Finally, precompute

\[ F(D)=\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}h_m(k),\qquad 0\le D\le M, \]

with \(F(0)=0\). Then \(|B_{<D}(c)|=F(D)\) for every \(c\).

Every count is at most \(m!\), and therefore has \(O(m\log m)\) bits. Thus this entire computation takes polynomial bit time; schoolbook addition and subtraction give a bound of \(O(m^4\log m)\).

4. Checking comparisons within the support

Partition the support into its distinct mass classes \(S_1,\ldots,S_g\), ordered so that their common masses satisfy

\[ w_1>w_2>\cdots>w_g>0. \]

For a candidate \(c\), define

\[ \ell_i(c)=\min_{r\in S_i}d(r,c), \qquad u_i(c)=\max_{r\in S_i}d(r,c). \]

Lemma. All center inequalities between supported rankings hold if and only if

\[ u_i(c)\le\ell_{i+1}(c)\qquad\text{for }1\le i<g. \]

Proof. Necessity follows by comparing a ranking attaining \(u_i(c)\) with one attaining \(\ell_{i+1}(c)\).

For sufficiency, suppose these adjacent-class inequalities hold. For any \(i<j\),

\[ u_i(c)\le\ell_{i+1}(c)\le u_{i+1}(c) \le\cdots\le\ell_j(c). \]

Thus, for \(r'\in S_i\) and \(r\in S_j\),

\[ d(r',c)\le u_i(c)\le\ell_j(c)\le d(r,c). \]

These are exactly the required comparisons when \(\mu(r')>\mu(r)\). Equal-mass rankings impose no condition. \(\square\)

5. Algorithm and correctness

Perform the following steps:

  • Sort and partition the support into decreasing mass classes.
  • Compute all Kendall distances between supported rankings.
  • Compute the table \(F(D)\).
  • For every candidate \(c\in S\):
  • Compute \(D_c\), \(a_c\), and the class extrema \(\ell_i(c),u_i(c)\).
  • Check \(u_i(c)\le\ell_{i+1}(c)\) for every adjacent pair of classes.
  • Check \(a_c=F(D_c)\).
  • If both checks pass, return \(c\).
  • If no candidate passes, report that no center exists.

Soundness. A returned candidate satisfies all comparisons within the support by Section 4. Its count equality implies that every unsupported ranking has distance at least \(D_c\), so it satisfies all comparisons from positive mass to zero mass by Section 2. These exhaust the pairs with strictly unequal masses. Hence the returned candidate is a center.

Completeness. Every center belongs to \(S\), by Section 1, and therefore is examined. A center necessarily satisfies both checks, by Sections 2 and 4. Thus, if a center exists, the algorithm returns one.

The argument also covers uniform masses. If the support is all of \(\mathcal R\) and all masses are equal, every ranking is a center; the algorithm returns one.

6. Running time for binary-rational input

The distance table requires \(O(\tau^2m^2)\) elementary operations: for each pair of rankings, count disagreements over all unordered alternative pairs.

Once this table and the mass classes are available, processing all candidates requires \(O(\tau^2)\) elementary operations. The ball-count table requires polynomial bit time as established in Section 3.

Sorting requires \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) exact rational comparisons. Comparing two binary-encoded rationals uses cross-multiplication of their numerators and denominators, whose bit lengths are bounded by \(L\); this also takes polynomial bit time.

All remaining integer comparisons and counts involve numbers with polynomially bounded bit lengths. Consequently, the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The finite-electorate theorem supplied in the question is therefore unnecessary for this conclusion: the support representation itself admits a direct polynomial-time recognition and search algorithm.

Adversarial review (gpt-6.1-sol / medium, Bolzano verifier prompt)

The prover’s claim is correct: the argument establishes a polynomial-time recognition and search algorithm for binary-rational support input. No substantive or minor gaps were found.

1. Restricting candidates to the support

The lemma is valid. Since the masses sum to \(1\), the support is nonempty. If a proposed center \(c\) lies outside the support, any supported ranking \(r\) satisfies \(\mu(r)>\mu(c)=0\). Applying the center condition to this pair gives
\[ d(r,c)\le d(c,c)=0. \]
Kendall distance is zero exactly for identical rankings, contradicting their different support membership.

Thus examining the \(\tau\) supported rankings exhausts every possible center.

2. Comparisons involving unsupported rankings

For a fixed supported candidate \(c\), every supported ranking has greater mass than every unsupported ranking. The relevant inequalities therefore require
\[ d(r,c)\le d(q,c) \]
for every \(r\in S\) and \(q\notin S\). Taking the maximum over supported rankings gives exactly the stated condition \(D_c\le d(q,c)\).

Consequently, all unsupported rankings must lie outside the open ball of radius \(D_c\). The count test is also exact: \(S\cap B_{<D_c}(c)\) is a subset of the finite ball, so its cardinality equals the ball’s cardinality precisely when it is the entire ball.

The strict boundary is correct. Unsupported rankings at distance \(D_c\) satisfy the required weak inequality.

3. Counting the distance ball

Relabeling alternatives by their positions in \(c\) correctly identifies Kendall distance from \(c\) with permutation inversion count. Hence the distance distribution is independent of the candidate.

The insertion recurrence is justified by a bijection: inserting the largest element into a permutation of \(j-1\) elements creates exactly one of \(0,\ldots,j-1\) additional inversions, and removal uniquely recovers the original permutation and insertion position.

Subtracting consecutive recurrence equations correctly gives
\[ h_j(k)=h_j(k-1)+h_{j-1}(k)-h_{j-1}(k-j). \]
The specified zero values handle negative indices and indices beyond the possible inversion range. The initialization \(h_0(0)=1\) is sufficient.

There are
\[ \sum_{j=0}^{m}\left(\binom j2+1\right)=O(m^3) \]
relevant table entries. Computing prefix sums then gives exactly the required open-ball counts for every possible \(D_c\in\{0,\ldots,M\}\).

The bit-complexity argument is sound. Stored counts and prefix sums are at most \(m!\), with \(O(m\log m)\) bits. Temporary addition results require at most one additional bit, which does not change the bound. Thus the stated \(O(m^4\log m)\) bound for this computation is valid.

4. Comparisons within the support

The mass classes are nonempty by construction, so their minimum and maximum distances exist.

Necessity of each adjacent-class inequality follows from comparing rankings attaining the corresponding extrema. For sufficiency, the displayed chain correctly uses both the assumed adjacent inequalities and the automatic inequalities \(\ell_i(c)\le u_i(c)\). It therefore implies the required inequality for every pair of distinct mass classes.

Equal-mass rankings correctly impose no condition. When there is only one mass class, the adjacent-class test is vacuous, as required.

5. Overall correctness and edge cases

The soundness argument exhausts all pairs with strictly unequal masses: both rankings are supported, or the greater-mass ranking is supported and the other is unsupported. Two unsupported rankings have equal mass and require no comparison.

Completeness follows because every center is examined and both tests are necessary.

The boundary cases are covered:

  • For \(\tau=1\), the sole candidate has \(D_c=0\), and both the open-ball count and \(a_c\) are zero.
  • For full support, the ball-count equality holds automatically because every ranking is supported.
  • For uniform full support, all comparisons are vacuous, and the algorithm correctly returns a center.
  • For uniform proper support, the unsupported-ranking test remains necessary and is retained by the algorithm.

6. Complexity and assignment coverage

The distance table can be computed by checking all unordered alternative pairs for each pair of supported rankings. Position tables for rankings permit these checks within the claimed \(O(\tau^2m^2)\) elementary-operation bound.

Afterward, each candidate requires one support scan and at most \(\tau-1\) adjacent-class checks. Processing all candidates therefore requires \(O(\tau^2)\) elementary operations.

Exact rational sorting uses polynomially many comparisons. Cross-multiplication involves integers of polynomially bounded bit length, so these comparisons take polynomial bit time. Distance values, support counts, and ball counts likewise have polynomially bounded bit lengths.

The proof consequently resolves both recognition and returning a center in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It does not rely on expanding rational masses into voters or applying the finite-electorate theorem.
