The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of item classes \(K\), buyer and seller types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and binary-rational package values, budgets, endowments, and reservation values, return a welfare-maximizing core outcome or determine that none exists. Each unit of mass receives or supplies one whole package, while a type's mass may be divided across packages; per-capita supplies scale with the population. Assignments and payments must respect type masses, item availability, individual budget and reservation constraints, and aggregate budget balance. Core stability requires that no measurable coalition can reallocate its available endowments with feasible payments so that every member strictly improves. What is the exact computational complexity in \(|K|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is an atomless probability space given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) buyer and seller types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(m=|K|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has an explicitly listed finite menu of whole packages, represented by nonnegative binary-rational quantity vectors over \(K\), including the zero package. Buyer types have nonnegative binary-rational package values \(v_t\) and budgets \(B_t\); seller types have nonnegative binary-rational per-capita endowments \(e_t\) and package reservation costs \(c_t\), with every offered package \(z\le e_t\). Values and costs respect free disposal and vanish at the zero package; budgets may be zero.
An outcome measurably assigns each individual one whole package and a personalized nonnegative real payment; individuals of one type may receive different packages and payments. A buyer receiving \(q\) and paying \(p\) has utility \(v_t(q)-p\), with \(0\le p\le\min\{B_t,v_t(q)\}\); a seller supplying \(z\) and receiving \(r\) has utility \(r-c_t(z)\), with \(r\ge c_t(z)\). Nonparticipants receive the zero package and make or receive no payment.
For each class, integrated buyer demand cannot exceed integrated seller supply; integrated buyer payments equal integrated seller receipts. Unsold goods may be retained and excess supply discarded. Welfare is integrated buyer value minus integrated seller reservation cost.
A blocking coalition is any measurable set of positive mass, including the whole society, that can choose new packages and payments satisfying the same constraints using only its sellers' original endowments, and strictly increase almost every member's individual utility. The weak core consists of feasible outcomes without such a coalition.

Is computing a welfare-maximizing weak-core outcome, or determining that the weak core is empty, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this continuous problem NP-hard?

For finite agents, computing a welfare-maximizing core outcome with buyer budgets is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-hard ([Bichler–Waldherr](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08253)); the [published version](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2021.2132) retains core-computation hardness.
For atomless exchange with purely indivisible commodities, [Inoue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304406805000182) proves core–Walras equivalence for a different blocking notion and without exogenous buyer spending caps; this does not settle the problem defined here. No complexity classification for this exact continuous problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one and interpreted every seller endowment as per capita, so aggregate supplies scale with participating mass.
  • Specified finite explicit package menus with rational quantity vectors to give the class-based formulation a finite input encoding; each individual chooses at most one nonzero package, following the discrete XOR allocation model.
  • Carried over nonnegative values, reservation costs, budgets and payments, free disposal, zero outside options, buyer individual rationality, seller individual rationality, and exact aggregate budget balance.
  • Allowed personalized payments and heterogeneous treatment within a type rather than imposing anonymous prices or equal treatment.
  • Interpreted measurable blocking as positive-mass coalitions with strict improvement almost everywhere, including the grand coalition, using original seller endowments and satisfying the same individual and aggregate constraints.
  • Specified welfare as uncapped gains from trade maximized among core outcomes, rather than unrestricted welfare or budget-capped value.

Result

The task is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. More precisely, the following decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete:

Given a society and a rational threshold \(W_0\), does its weak core contain an outcome with welfare at least \(W_0\)?

The hardness holds even when every buyer has at most two nonzero packages and every seller has just one nonzero package. The reduction below handles atomless mixing explicitly.

1. An exact characterization of blocking

Let \(\mathcal B\) and \(\mathcal S\) denote the buyer and seller types. Fix a feasible outcome, and write \(u(a)\) for individual \(a\)'s current utility. For each type \(t\), define its utility floor by

\(\ell_t=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{a\text{ of type }t}u(a).\)

All these floors are finite and nonnegative. Buyer utilities are bounded by their finite menu values. Seller utilities are integrable because total receipts equal total buyer payments, which are bounded by the input budgets.

For a buyer type \(t\), retain only packages satisfying \(v_t(q)>\ell_t\), and define

\(b_{tq}=\min\{B_t,v_t(q)-\ell_t\}.\)

Consider the following linear program. Its variables \(x_{tq}\) and \(y_{sz}\) are nonnegative masses of buyers and sellers choosing the indicated packages.

Its objective is to maximize

\(F=\sum_{t\in\mathcal B,q}b_{tq}x_{tq}-\sum_{s\in\mathcal S,z}(c_s(z)+\ell_s)y_{sz},\)

subject to

  • \(\sum_qx_{tq}\leq\mu_t\) for every buyer type;
  • \(\sum_zy_{sz}\leq\mu_s\) for every seller type;
  • \(\sum_{t,q}x_{tq}q\leq\sum_{s,z}y_{sz}z\), coordinatewise.

Buyer variables range over the retained packages; seller variables range over their menus.

Lemma 1. The outcome is blocked if and only if this linear program has positive optimal value.

Proof. Suppose first that a coalition blocks. Every improving buyer choosing \(q\) satisfies

\(v_t(q)-p>u(a)\geq\ell_t,\)

so \(q\) is retained and \(p\leq b_{tq}\). Every improving seller satisfies

\(r>c_s(z)+u(a)\geq c_s(z)+\ell_s.\)

A blocking coalition must contain a positive mass of sellers: without sellers, goods feasibility forces buyer demand to be zero, and the zero package cannot strictly improve a nonnegative utility.

Aggregate the coalition's package choices into \(x,y\). These masses satisfy the linear-program constraints. Payment balance and strict improvement of its sellers give

\(F\geq\int_{\text{coalition buyers}}p-\int_{\text{coalition sellers}}(c_s(z)+\ell_s)>0.\)

Conversely, suppose a feasible \(x,y\) has \(F>0\). Delete any zero-package seller variables. This preserves goods feasibility and weakly increases \(F\). A positive mass of sellers remains, because otherwise positive buyer demand could not be supplied.

Let \(M\) be the total selected mass. Choose \(\delta>0\) sufficiently small that, for every selected buyer package,

\(v_t(q)-\ell_t>2\delta,\)

and \(2\delta M<F\). Give selected buyers the payments

\(p_{tq}=\min\{B_t,v_t(q)-\ell_t-2\delta\},\)

and initially give selected sellers receipts

\(r_{sz}=c_s(z)+\ell_s+2\delta.\)

The resulting financing surplus \(F_\delta\) satisfies

\(F_\delta\geq F-2\delta M>0.\)

For every used type, the set of individuals with current utility below \(\ell_t+\delta\) has positive measure. Scale all selected masses by a common sufficiently small factor \(\lambda>0\). Atomlessness then permits selecting and partitioning individuals from these sets into precisely the required masses.

The proposed buyer utilities are at least \(\ell_t+2\delta\), and the initial seller utilities equal \(\ell_s+2\delta\). Thus all selected individuals strictly improve. Distribute the remaining surplus \(\lambda F_\delta\) among the selected sellers. This achieves payment balance, preserves all other constraints, and leaves every selected individual strictly better off. Hence these individuals form a blocking coalition. \(\square\)

Supporting prices

The zero allocation is feasible in this linear program. Its dual has nonnegative goods prices \(\pi\) and nonnegative type-capacity multipliers \(\gamma_t\), with constraints

\(\gamma_t+\pi\cdot q\geq b_{tq}\)

for retained buyer packages, and

\(\gamma_s-\pi\cdot z\geq-c_s(z)-\ell_s\)

for seller packages. Its objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t\gamma_t\).

By finite-dimensional linear-programming duality, the primal optimum is zero exactly when there exists \(\pi\geq0\) satisfying

\(\pi\cdot q\geq\min\{B_t,v_t(q)-\ell_t\}\quad\text{whenever }v_t(q)>\ell_t,\)

and

\(\pi\cdot z\leq c_s(z)+\ell_s\quad\text{for every seller package}.\)

Indeed, a zero dual objective forces every \(\gamma_t=0\), because every \(\mu_t>0\).

The buyer inequalities can equivalently be written, for every menu package, as the disjunction

\(\pi\cdot q\geq B_t\quad\text{or}\quad\ell_t+\pi\cdot q\geq v_t(q).\) \((1)\)

For \(v_t(q)\leq\ell_t\), the second alternative holds automatically.

Lemma 2. A feasible outcome is in the weak core exactly when such supporting prices exist. Moreover, at every weak-core outcome, almost every buyer pays \(\pi\cdot q\), almost every seller receives \(\pi\cdot z\), and every seller of type \(s\) has utility \(\ell_s\).

Proof. The equivalence follows from Lemma 1 and duality. It remains to establish the payment assertion.

For an actual buyer package with \(v_t(q)>\ell_t\),

\(p\leq\min\{B_t,v_t(q)-\ell_t\}\leq\pi\cdot q.\)

If \(v_t(q)\leq\ell_t\), feasibility and \(u(a)\geq\ell_t\) instead force \(v_t(q)=\ell_t\) and \(p=0\), so again \(p\leq\pi\cdot q\).

For an actual seller,

\(r=c_s(z)+u(a)\geq c_s(z)+\ell_s\geq\pi\cdot z.\)

Let \(D,S\) be aggregate demand and supply. Payment balance gives

\(\int_{\text{buyers}}(\pi\cdot q-p)+\int_{\text{sellers}}(r-\pi\cdot z)=\pi\cdot(D-S)\leq0.\)

Both integrands are nonnegative. Consequently both vanish almost everywhere, proving the payment assertion. For sellers this also gives

\(u(a)=\pi\cdot z-c_s(z)\leq\ell_s,\)

so their utilities equal \(\ell_s\) almost everywhere. \(\square\)

These lemmas apply directly to the blocking notion in the question.

2. The reduction

Start with a \(3\)-CNF satisfiability instance having \(n\) variables and \(C\) clauses. We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete restriction in which each clause has three distinct variables and contains no complementary pair.

There are \(n+1\) goods. Write \(f_i\) for the unit vector of variable good \(i\), and \(h\) for the unit vector of one additional good.

For clause \(j\), let \(P_j\) and \(N_j\) be its positive and negative variable sets, and put \(k_j=|N_j|\). Let \(\nu_i\) be the number of negative occurrences of variable \(i\).

Every menu contains the zero package, with zero value or cost.

Buyer types

| Type | Nonzero packages and values | Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Variable buyer \(i\) | \(2f_i\), value \(10\); \(f_i+h\), value \(11\) | \(2\) |
| Clause buyer \(j\) | \(h+\sum_{i\in N_j}f_i\), value \(10\); \(2h+\sum_{i\in P_j}f_i\), value \(12-k_j\) | \(10\) |
| Anchor buyer | \(h\), value \(10\) | \(1\) |

Call the first package listed for each buyer its prescribed package.

Seller types

For each variable good \(i\), introduce a seller with endowment and sole nonzero package

\((2+\nu_i)f_i,\)

of cost zero.

Introduce one additional seller with endowment and sole nonzero package

\((C+1)h,\)

of reservation cost \(C+1\).

There are \(T=2n+C+2\) types so far. Set

\(\alpha=2^{-\lceil\log_2T\rceil}.\)

Give each listed type mass \(\alpha\). If \(T\alpha<1\), add a dummy buyer type of mass \(1-T\alpha\), with budget zero and only the zero package.

All masses are positive binary rationals and sum to one. All quantities, values, budgets, costs, and endowments are nonnegative integers of polynomial encoding length.

The free-disposal requirements also hold. The variable buyer's two nonzero packages are incomparable. For a clause with a negative literal, its two nonzero packages are incomparable; for an all-positive clause, the second package dominates the first and has larger value. Seller costs are monotone on their two-package menus.

3. A sharp welfare bound prevents fractional evasion

Consider the allocation in which every buyer receives its prescribed package and every seller supplies its nonzero package. Demand equals supply for every good.

This allocation is feasible with personalized payments:

  • variable buyers pay zero;
  • clause buyers and the anchor each pay \(1\);
  • the \(h\)-seller receives \(C+1\) per individual;
  • variable-good sellers receive zero.

Its welfare is

\(W_*=\alpha(10n+9C+9).\) \((2)\)

We now prove that \(W_*\) is the maximum feasible welfare and that attaining it forces the prescribed package assignment.

Use the auxiliary vector \(w=3h\). For any feasible outcome,

\(W=\int_{\text{buyers}}(v(q)-w\cdot q)+\int_{\text{sellers}}(w\cdot z-c(z))+w\cdot(D-S).\)

The last term is nonpositive.

For a variable buyer, the values of \(v(q)-w\cdot q\) are:

  • prescribed package: \(10\);
  • alternative package: \(8\);
  • zero package: \(0\).

For clause buyer \(j\), they are:

  • prescribed package: \(7\);
  • alternative package: \(6-k_j\), which is at most \(6\);
  • zero package: \(0\).

For the anchor, they are \(7\) at its prescribed package and \(0\) at zero.

The \(h\)-seller's maximal \(w\cdot z-c(z)\) is \(2(C+1)\), attained uniquely by its nonzero package. Every variable-good seller has auxiliary surplus zero.

Therefore

\(W\leq\alpha\bigl(10n+7C+7+2(C+1)\bigr)=W_*.\)

Every non-dummy buyer has a unique maximizing package in this bound. Thus equality forces every buyer to receive its prescribed package almost everywhere, and forces the \(h\)-seller to supply its full package almost everywhere.

Prescribed demand for variable good \(i\) equals \(\alpha(2+\nu_i)\), which is that good's maximum possible supply. Goods feasibility therefore also forces every variable-good seller to supply its nonzero package almost everywhere.

Consequently:

Every outcome of welfare \(W_*\) has exactly the prescribed package assignment, up to null sets.

This conclusion excludes partial service and package mixing as ways to evade the reduction.

4. A weak-core outcome attaining the bound yields a satisfying assignment

Suppose a weak-core outcome has welfare at least \(W_*\). By the bound, its welfare equals \(W_*\), and its packages are prescribed.

Apply Lemma 2. Write

\(p_i=\pi\cdot f_i,\qquad p_h=\pi\cdot h.\)

The \(h\)-seller receives \((C+1)p_h\), which must cover its cost \(C+1\). Hence \(p_h\geq1\). The anchor pays \(p_h\) and has budget \(1\), so \(p_h\leq1\). Therefore

\(p_h=1.\)

Variable buyer \(i\) pays \(2p_i\), so its budget implies

\(0\leq p_i\leq1.\)

Its utility floor is exactly \(10-2p_i\), because its package and payment are constant almost everywhere. Applying disjunction \((1)\) to its alternative package \(f_i+h\) gives

\(p_i+1\geq2\quad\text{or}\quad10-2p_i+p_i+1\geq11.\)

Equivalently,

\(p_i\geq1\quad\text{or}\quad p_i\leq0.\)

Together with the preceding bounds, this forces

\(p_i\in\{0,1\}.\) \((3)\)

Interpret variable \(i\) as true exactly when \(p_i=1\).

Clause buyer \(j\)'s utility floor is

\(\ell_j=10-\left(1+\sum_{i\in N_j}p_i\right).\)

Its alternative package costs

\(2+\sum_{i\in P_j}p_i\leq5<10.\)

Thus the budget alternative in disjunction \((1)\) cannot hold. The utility alternative requires

\(10-\left(1+\sum_{i\in N_j}p_i\right)+2+\sum_{i\in P_j}p_i\geq12-k_j.\)

Rearranging gives

\(\sum_{i\in P_j}p_i+\sum_{i\in N_j}(1-p_i)\geq1.\) \((4)\)

By \((3)\), the left-hand side counts the clause's true literals. Therefore every clause is satisfied.

5. A satisfying assignment gives a weak-core outcome attaining the bound

Conversely, suppose the formula is satisfiable. Set \(p_h=1\), and set \(p_i=1\) or \(0\) according to a satisfying assignment.

Use the prescribed package allocation, charging and paying the linear package prices \(\pi\cdot q\) and \(\pi\cdot z\).

These payments are feasible:

  • variable buyers pay \(2p_i\leq2\);
  • clause buyers pay \(1+\sum_{i\in N_j}p_i\leq4\);
  • the anchor pays \(1\);
  • variable-good sellers receive nonnegative receipts;
  • the \(h\)-seller receives exactly its reservation cost \(C+1\).

Every buyer payment is also below its prescribed package value. Since demand equals supply coordinatewise, total payments equal total receipts.

Take each type's floor to be its actual utility. For prescribed packages, the supporting inequalities hold with equality.

For a variable buyer's alternative, disjunction \((1)\) holds because either \(p_i=1\), making its price equal to the budget, or \(p_i=0\), making the utility alternative an equality.

For a clause buyer's alternative, its supporting inequality is exactly \((4)\), which holds because the assignment satisfies that clause.

All zero-package inequalities hold because utilities are nonnegative. Seller inequalities hold with equality at their supplied packages and hold at zero.

Lemma 2 therefore establishes weak-core membership. The outcome has welfare \(W_*\).

We have proved the exact equivalence

\(\text{formula satisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{a weak-core outcome has welfare at least }W_*.\)

The construction has \(m=n+1\), at most \(2n+C+3\) types, and polynomial total input length. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

6. Polynomial certificates and exact attainability

For completeness, the welfare-threshold decision problem also belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

A certificate consists of nonnegative prices \(\pi\), nonnegative utility floors \(U_t\), and package masses \(x_{tq},y_{sz}\). Require the masses for each type to sum to its input mass, goods feasibility, and the following conditions:

  • For every buyer package: \(\pi\cdot q\geq B_t\) or \(U_t+\pi\cdot q\geq v_t(q)\).
  • For every allocated buyer package: \(\pi\cdot q\leq B_t\) and \(U_t+\pi\cdot q\leq v_t(q)\).
  • For every seller package: \(\pi\cdot z\leq c_s(z)+U_s\).
  • For every allocated seller package: \(\pi\cdot z=c_s(z)+U_s\).
  • For each good \(k\): \(\pi_k=0\) or \(D_k=S_k\).
  • The allocation's welfare is at least the threshold.

“Allocated” is expressed by the disjunction that its mass is zero or the listed conditions hold.

These conditions assign payments \(\pi\cdot q\) and receipts \(\pi\cdot z\). They imply individual feasibility and payment balance. They also exclude blocking: an improving buyer can pay at most its new package price, while every improving seller must receive strictly more than its new package price. Because a block contains a positive mass of sellers, this contradicts payment balance and goods feasibility.

Conversely, Lemma 2 supplies such a certificate for every weak-core outcome.

There are polynomially many linear constraints and disjunctions, all with rational input coefficients. Selecting one branch of each disjunction yields a rational linear program of polynomial size. Whenever such a program is feasible, it has a rational feasible point of polynomial encoding length by the basic-feasible-solution determinant bound. A certificate can therefore be checked in polynomial time.

The same description shows that core welfare maximization is well defined whenever the core is nonempty. The certificate space is a finite union of rational polyhedra. Welfare is linear and bounded above; on every nonempty constituent polyhedron its maximum is attained. Hence the global maximum is attained and has a polynomial-size rational representation.

Thus the threshold problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and computing a welfare-maximizing weak-core outcome while reporting emptiness when appropriate is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm for the requested task would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
