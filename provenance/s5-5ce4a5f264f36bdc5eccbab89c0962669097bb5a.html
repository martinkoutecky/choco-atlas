The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given τ roles with binary-rational masses μ_i summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational contact matrix \(A\), and fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\), define \(q_i(x)=\frac{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_jx_j}{\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_j}\) for roles with contacts. Decide whether there is an initial state \(x^0\in[0,1]^\tau\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^0\le 1/4-\varepsilon\) and a finite sequence of legal mass updates reaching a stable state \(x^*\) with \(\sum_i\mu_ix_i^*\ge 1/2\). A legal update increases \(x_i\) by a positive amount when \(q_i(x)>1/2\), or decreases it when \(q_i(x)<1/2\); roles with no contacts never update. Stability means no positive mass in any role is unhappy.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) roles with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=2\) opinions; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Fix a rational constant \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\). The input includes a symmetric matrix \(A\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau\times\tau}\); diagonal entries may represent contacts between distinct members of the same role.
A state \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\) assigns opinion \(1\) to mass \(\mu_i x_i\) of role \(i\), and opinion \(0\) to its remaining mass. Put \(d_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_j\) and, when \(d_i>0\), \(q_i(x)=\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_jx_j/d_i\).
One legal update changes exactly one coordinate: replace \(x_i\) by \(x_i+\delta\) when \(q_i(x)>1/2\), or by \(x_i-\delta\) when \(q_i(x)<1/2\), for any real \(\delta>0\) keeping the state in \([0,1]^\tau\). Conditions are evaluated before the update; ties and roles with \(d_i=0\) permit no update.
A state is stable precisely when, for every role with \(d_i>0\), \(x_i<1\) implies \(q_i(x)\le1/2\), and \(x_i>0\) implies \(q_i(x)\ge1/2\). Isolated roles impose no stability condition.
The decision problem asks whether some real initial state \(x^0\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^0\le1/4-\varepsilon\) reaches, through a finite sequence of legal updates, a stable state \(x^*\) with \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i^*\ge1/2\).

For each fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\), is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite undirected graphs with binary opinions, sequential strict-majority updates, and ties preserving opinions, the corresponding problem with at most \(n(1/4-\varepsilon)\) initial supporters is NP-hard; with the initial profile supplied, majority reachability is decidable in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.4050v4)
No result settling exactly the mass-update problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted contact weights to nonnegative rationals, consistent with contacts in an undirected social network.
  • Allowed diagonal contact weights to describe contacts between distinct members of one mass role; these do not represent individual self-loops.
  • Took epsilon to be a fixed rational constant so the threshold has an effective exact representation.
  • Made updates sequential, changing exactly one coordinate, with the strict-majority condition evaluated before the update and the amount bounded by available mass.
  • Allowed real initial states and real update amounts, as suggested by the stated continuous domain.
  • Expanded stability into explicit conditions for both opinion masses, retaining inert isolated roles and no switching at ties.

Result

For every fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\), the decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

Hardness holds even when every matrix entry belongs to \(\{0,1\}\), all masses are dyadic rationals, and the society consists of disjoint pairs of roles, one role with only diagonal contacts, and one isolated role.

1. Two elementary components

Write \(M(x)=\sum_i\mu_i x_i\).

A pair. Consider two roles of equal mass \(2c>0\), connected only to each other, with contact weight \(1\). Their neighborhood fractions are \(q_1(x)=x_2\) and \(q_2(x)=x_1\).

Their stable states are exactly
\((0,0)\), \((1/2,1/2)\), and \((1,1)\). Indeed:

  • If one coordinate is \(0\), stability forces the other to be \(0\).
  • If one coordinate is \(1\), stability forces the other to be \(1\).
  • If a coordinate lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), stability forces its neighbor to equal \(1/2\), which then forces both coordinates to equal \(1/2\).

Two consequences apply to every finite legal trajectory.

First, reaching \((1,1)\) requires initial supporter mass strictly greater than \(c\). If both initial coordinates were at most \(1/2\), neither could increase: each sees a neighborhood fraction at most \(1/2\), and decreases preserve this property. Consequently, at least one initial coordinate must exceed \(1/2\), contributing mass greater than \(c\).

Second, reaching \((1/2,1/2)\) requires starting there. Otherwise, consider the last update within this pair. Its neighbor already has its final value \(1/2\), so the update is forbidden by the tie rule.

Thus, a pair ending at \((1,1)\) has final supporter mass \(4c\) and gains strictly less than \(3c\). A pair ending at either other stable state gains no supporter mass.

A diagonal role. Consider one role of mass \(2c_0>0\), whose only contact is a diagonal entry \(1\). Here \(q(x)=x\). Its stable states are \(0\), \(1/2\), and \(1\).

Updates below \(1/2\) decrease the coordinate, updates above \(1/2\) increase it, and \(1/2\) permits no update. Therefore, reaching \(1\) requires initial supporter mass strictly greater than \(c_0\), and gains strictly less than \(c_0\). Ending at \(0\) or \(1/2\) gains no supporter mass.

An isolated role never changes.

2. Reduction parameters

We reduce from SUBSET SUM: given positive binary integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and a target \(t\), decide whether some subset sums to \(t\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; restricting to \(1\le t\le\sum_i a_i\) preserves hardness, since targets outside this range are trivial. See [Karp’s original completeness results](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Fix \(\varepsilon\), and define

\(B=1/4-\varepsilon,\qquad g=1/8-\varepsilon>0,\qquad K=\lceil 1/g\rceil.\)

Let \(W=\sum_i a_i\), append the integer \(H=KW\), and denote the resulting list by \(b_1,\ldots,b_{n+1}\). Set

\(T=H+t,\qquad W'=H+W.\)

Because \(H>W\), a subset of the appended list sums to \(T\) precisely when it contains \(H\) and its remaining integers sum to \(t\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \frac{W'}{T}\le 1+\frac1K\le 1+g.\)

Choose an integer \(q\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle 2^{-q}<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2T^2},\frac{g}{4T}\right\},\)

and define the dyadic rational

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\frac{\lfloor 2^q\varepsilon/T\rfloor+1}{2^q}.\)

Put \(s=\kappa T\) and \(\delta=s-\varepsilon\). The rounding rule gives

\(\displaystyle 0<\delta\le T2^{-q}<\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2T},\frac g4\right\}.\)

Since \(\kappa>\varepsilon/T\), we obtain

\(0<\delta<\kappa/2,\qquad s<\varepsilon+g/4<1/8.\)

We also need the following bound:

\(\displaystyle \kappa W'\le s(1+g)<\varepsilon+\frac g4+\frac g8=\varepsilon+\frac{3g}{8}<\varepsilon+\frac{2g}{3}=\frac{1/4+\varepsilon}{3}.\)

In particular,

\(B+3\kappa W'<1/2.\)

3. Constructing the society

For each integer \(b_i\), create a pair as in Section 1, with each role having mass \(2\kappa b_i\).

Create a diagonal role of mass

\(\mu_{\mathrm d}=1/2-4s,\)

and an isolated role of mass

\(\mu_{\mathrm r}=1/2-4\kappa(W'-T).\)

The matrix has entry \(1\) between the two roles of each pair, diagonal entry \(1\) for the diagonal role, and all other entries \(0\).

All masses are positive dyadic rationals. Positivity of \(\mu_{\mathrm d}\) follows from \(s<1/8\). For the isolated mass, the ratio bound gives

\(\kappa(W'-T)\le gs<g/8,\)

so \(\mu_{\mathrm r}>1/2-g/2>0\). Their total is exactly

\((1/2-4s)+4\kappa W'+(1/2-4\kappa(W'-T))=1.\)

There are \(2(n+1)+2\) roles. Since \(\varepsilon\) is fixed, \(K\) is constant, and \(q=O(\log T)\). Every mass therefore has polynomial binary encoding length, and the entire construction takes polynomial time. Large integers are encoded in masses; they are not expanded into additional roles.

For later use, define

\(c_0=\mu_{\mathrm d}/2=1/4-2s.\)

4. Completeness

Suppose a subset \(I\) of the appended integers sums to \(T\). Let \(\rho=\delta/2>0\).

Initially, assign coordinate \(1/2+\rho\) to the diagonal role and to one role of each pair indexed by \(I\). Assign coordinate \(0\) to every other role.

These coordinates lie in \([0,1]\), because \(\delta<g/4<1/32\). The initial supporter mass is

\(\displaystyle M(x^0)=c_0+s+\rho(\mu_{\mathrm d}+2s)=1/4-s+\rho(1/2-2s).\)

Since \(0<1/2-2s<1\),

\(M(x^0)<1/4-s+\delta/2=B-\delta/2<B.\)

The diagonal role can legally increase to \(1\). In each selected pair, first increase the coordinate initially equal to \(0\) to \(1\): its neighbor exceeds \(1/2\). Then increase the seeded coordinate to \(1\): its neighbor now equals \(1\).

The resulting state is stable. Its supporter mass is exactly

\(\displaystyle \mu_{\mathrm d}+4\kappa\sum_{i\in I}b_i=(1/2-4s)+4s=1/2.\)

Thus every yes-instance of SUBSET SUM produces a yes-instance of the society problem.

5. Soundness for arbitrary real trajectories

Suppose an arbitrary real initial state with \(M(x^0)\le B\) reaches a stable state with \(M(x^*)\ge1/2\).

Let \(I\) index the pairs ending at \((1,1)\), and define

\(\displaystyle S=\kappa\sum_{i\in I}b_i.\)

Let \(D=1\) if the diagonal role ends at \(1\), and \(D=0\) otherwise.

By Section 1, the pairs indexed by \(I\) gain strictly less than \(3S\) in total when \(I\) is nonempty. All other pairs gain no mass. The diagonal role gains strictly less than \(c_0\) when \(D=1\), and otherwise gains no mass. The isolated role gains no mass.

If \(D=0\), these bounds imply

\(M(x^*)\le B+3S\le B+3\kappa W'<1/2,\)

a contradiction. Hence \(D=1\).

The initial mass on the diagonal role is strictly greater than \(c_0\), and the initial mass on every selected pair is strictly greater than \(\kappa b_i\). Therefore,

\(c_0+S<M(x^0)\le B.\)

The strict gain bound also gives

\(1/2\le M(x^*)<M(x^0)+c_0+3S\le B+c_0+3S.\)

Substituting \(B=1/4-\varepsilon\) and \(c_0=1/4-2s\), these two inequalities become

\(\displaystyle s-\frac{\delta}{3}<S<s+\delta.\)

Both \(S\) and \(s=\kappa T\) are integer multiples of \(\kappa\). Since \(0<\delta<\kappa/2\), this interval contains only one such multiple, namely \(s\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in I}b_i=T.\)

The original SUBSET SUM instance is therefore a yes-instance.

This proves the reduction in both directions, including all real initial states and all finite legal trajectories. Hence the society problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

6. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

We now justify that allowing real states and arbitrary finite trajectories does not prevent polynomial certificates.

Define the affine field

\(\displaystyle F_i(x)=\sum_j a_{ij}\mu_j(x_j-1/2).\)

For a nonisolated role, its sign agrees with that of \(q_i(x)-1/2\). For an isolated role it is identically zero. Nonnegative contacts imply coordinatewise monotonicity:

\(x\le y\ \Longrightarrow\ F_i(x)\le F_i(y).\)

A finite normal form

Starting from any state \(x^0\), perform two phases:

  • Whenever \(x_i<1\) and \(F_i(x)>0\), replace \(x_i\) by \(1\), until no such coordinate remains. Call the resulting state \(u\).
  • Whenever \(x_i>0\) and \(F_i(x)<0\), replace \(x_i\) by \(0\), until no such coordinate remains. Call the resulting state \(v\).

Every update is legal. Each coordinate changes at most once per phase, so there are at most \(2\tau\) updates.

The state \(v\) is stable. At termination of Phase 2, \(v_i>0\) implies \(F_i(v)\ge0\). If \(v_i<1\) and coordinate \(i\) was never lowered, then \(v_i=u_i<1\), so

\(F_i(v)\le F_i(u)\le0.\)

If it was lowered, its field was negative before that update and can only decrease during the remainder of Phase 2. Thus \(F_i(v)<0\).

Furthermore, \(v\) dominates every stable state reachable from \(x^0\). To prove this, first observe that every reachable state is bounded above by \(u\). Initially \(x^0\le u\). At any state \(y\le u\), a coordinate with \(u_i<1\) cannot increase, because

\(F_i(y)\le F_i(u)\le0.\)

Coordinates with \(u_i=1\) cannot exceed their upper bound, and decreases preserve the bound.

Now let \(z\le u\) be any stable state. During Phase 2, suppose its current state is \(y\ge z\) and coordinate \(i\) is lowered because \(F_i(y)<0\). If \(z_i>0\), stability and monotonicity would give

\(0\le F_i(z)\le F_i(y)<0,\)

a contradiction. Hence \(z_i=0\), and lowering coordinate \(i\) preserves \(y\ge z\). Induction gives \(v\ge z\).

Therefore every yes-instance has a witnessing sequence of at most \(2\tau\) updates, each replacing a coordinate by an endpoint.

Polynomial-size rational witnesses

Fix such a sequence, including its coordinates and update directions. Every intermediate coordinate is then either an initial variable, \(0\), or \(1\). Legality of every update is expressed by rational linear inequalities, including the strict field sign and the positive update size.

For each nonisolated terminal coordinate, also fix whether its value is \(0\), strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), or \(1\). Stability then becomes, respectively,

\(F_i(x^*)\le0,\qquad F_i(x^*)=0,\qquad F_i(x^*)\ge0.\)

Together with coordinate bounds, the initial budget, and the terminal mass threshold, this is a polynomial-size system of rational linear equalities and weak or strict inequalities. Its coefficient encoding length is polynomial in the input length.

Such a system, if feasible over the reals, has a polynomial-size rational solution here. To see this, replace every strict inequality \(\ell(x)>0\) by \(\ell(x)\ge\eta\), introduce \(0\le\eta\le1\), and maximize \(\eta\). A real solution to the original finite system supplies a feasible positive \(\eta\). The resulting polytope is bounded, since all initial coordinates and \(\eta\) lie in \([0,1]\). Thus an optimal vertex exists with \(\eta>0\).

After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule expresses its coordinates as ratios of determinants of order at most \(\tau+1\). The integer matrix entries have polynomial bit length, so these determinants, and hence the vertex coordinates, have polynomial bit length.

A certificate can therefore supply a polynomial-size rational initial state and the endpoint-update sequence. Exact rational arithmetic verifies legality, stability, and both mass bounds in polynomial time. The problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Combining membership with the reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for every fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<1/8\).
