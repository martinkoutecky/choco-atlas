The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 5.3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given goods \(G\), a finite type support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), budgets \(b_t\), and desired bundles \(D_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and threshold \(\kappa\), does there exist a price vector \(p\geq 0\) and nonnegative masses \(x_{t,S}\) assigned to integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\leq q_g\), every bundle receiving positive mass is affordable and utility-maximizing for its type under \(v_t(S)=1\) iff \(D_t\subseteq S\), and \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\geq\kappa\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) contain \(m\geq1\) goods. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a positive binary-rational per-person budget \(b_t\) and a nonempty desired bundle \(D_t\subseteq G\), with \(v_t(S)=\mathbf{1}\{D_t\subseteq S\}\). Budgets of distinct types are pairwise distinct. Supplies \(q_g\geq0\) and the threshold \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A feasible outcome consists of finite real prices \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{m}\) and real masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) on integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\), including the empty bundle, satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}\leq q_g\) for every good.
Write \(p(S)=\sum_{g\in S}p_g\). Whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\), require \(p(S)\leq b_t\) and \(v_t(S)=\max\{v_t(R):R\subseteq G,\ p(R)\leq b_t\}\). Any utility-maximizing affordable bundle may be assigned; budgets apply separately to each recipient, rather than to average spending. Unused supply is permitted without a restriction on its price, and budgets need not be exhausted.

Is deciding whether such an outcome exists with \(\sum_t\sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\geq\kappa\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents with pairwise distinct positive budgets, deciding whether a single-minded market admits an equilibrium meeting a welfare threshold is NP-complete ([Lev et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2012.14838v3)).
For a different continuous model—divisible goods allocated to finitely many single-minded agents with equal budgets—a competitive allocation can be computed in polynomial time ([Brânzei, Lv and Mehta](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/025.pdf)); this does not settle the mass model with integral recipient bundles. No result settling this exact formulation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so supplies and welfare are per capita.
  • Required positive rational budgets and pairwise distinct budgets across types, carrying over the discrete model's budget assumption; recipients within a type share its budget.
  • Required nonempty desired bundles, consistent with the intended single-minded domain and its welfare bounds.
  • Specified nonnegative rational supplies, a rational threshold between zero and one, and nonempty goods and type support.
  • Allowed arbitrary ties among utility-maximizing affordable bundles and included the empty bundle.
  • Specified finite real prices and real allocation masses, with affordability imposed on each positively assigned bundle.
  • Retained the stated supply inequalities without adding market clearance, zero prices for unused supply, or budget exhaustion.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when all type masses are equal dyadic rationals and every supply is either zero or equal to one type’s mass.

The proof below gives a reduction directly for the stated mass model. Its use of private goods parallels the finite-agent construction in [Lev et al., Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2012.14838v3#A2).

1. A type’s satisfied mass is all or nothing

For nonnegative prices \(p\), define the set of affordable types by

\(A(p)=\{t:p(D_t)\leq b_t\}\).

Lemma. At prices \(p\), a feasible outcome exists if and only if

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\substack{t\in A(p)\\g\in D_t}}\mu_t\leq q_g\)

for every good \(g\). Whenever an outcome exists at these prices, its welfare is exactly

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in A(p)}\mu_t\).

Proof. If \(t\in A(p)\), the desired bundle \(D_t\) is affordable and gives utility one. Consequently, every bundle assigned positive mass to this type must have utility one and therefore contain \(D_t\). Its entire mass \(\mu_t\) is satisfied and consumes at least \(\mu_t\) units of each good in \(D_t\).

If \(t\notin A(p)\), no affordable bundle can contain \(D_t\): nonnegative prices imply \(p(S)\geq p(D_t)>b_t\) whenever \(D_t\subseteq S\). Thus every recipient of this type has utility zero.

These observations prove the necessary supply inequalities and the welfare formula. Conversely, if the inequalities hold, assign bundle \(D_t\) to the entire mass of each affordable type and the empty bundle to every other type. These assignments are affordable, utility-maximizing, and satisfy every supply constraint. This proves the lemma.

In particular, allowing a type’s allocation to be split among bundles does not allow only part of that type to be satisfied.

2. Polynomial reduction from Independent Set

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Independent Set problem: given a simple undirected graph \(H=(V,E)\) with \(n\geq1\) vertices and an integer \(1\leq K\leq n\), decide whether it has an independent set of size at least \(K\).

Label the vertices \(1,\ldots,n\). Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Thus \(N\leq2n\).

Construction

Create the following goods:

  • An edge good \(a_e\) for every edge \(e\in E\).
  • A private good \(h_i\) for every index \(1\leq i\leq N\).

Create exactly \(N\) types, with

\(\displaystyle \mu_i=\frac1N,\qquad b_i=i.\)

For vertex types \(1\leq i\leq n\), set

\(\displaystyle D_i=\{h_i\}\cup\{a_e:e\text{ is incident to vertex }i\}.\)

For dummy types \(n<i\leq N\), set \(D_i=\{h_i\}\).

Supplies are

\(\displaystyle q_{a_e}=\frac1N,\qquad q_{h_i}=\begin{cases}\frac1N,&i\leq n,\\0,&i>n.\end{cases}\)

Finally, set \(\kappa=K/N\).

Every desired bundle is nonempty. All masses and budgets are positive, the masses sum to one, and the budgets are pairwise distinct. Because \(N\) is a power of two, the masses, supplies, and threshold are dyadic rationals. The construction has \(m=|E|+N\) goods and \(\tau=N\) types, with total encoding length polynomial in the graph’s encoding length.

An independent set gives a feasible outcome

Suppose \(I\subseteq V\) is independent and \(|I|\geq K\). Set

\(\displaystyle p_{a_e}=0\quad(e\in E),\qquad p_{h_i}=\begin{cases}0,&i\in I,\\N+1,&i\notin I.\end{cases}\)

All prices are finite and nonnegative.

For \(i\in I\), the desired bundle costs zero, so it is affordable. For every other type, its desired bundle contains its private good priced at \(N+1>b_i\), so it is unaffordable. Hence \(A(p)=I\).

Each selected private good is demanded by mass \(1/N\), exactly its supply. Each edge good is demanded by at most one selected endpoint, because \(I\) is independent, so its demand is at most \(1/N\). Dummy private goods have zero demand.

The lemma therefore gives a feasible outcome of welfare

\(\displaystyle \frac{|I|}{N}\geq\frac K N=\kappa.\)

A feasible outcome gives an independent set

Conversely, suppose the constructed instance has a feasible outcome meeting the threshold, at prices \(p\).

No dummy type can belong to \(A(p)\). If a dummy type were affordable, the lemma would force its entire mass \(1/N\) to consume its private good, whose supply is zero.

Thus \(A(p)\subseteq V\). If two adjacent vertices \(i,j\) both belonged to \(A(p)\), their types would each consume mass \(1/N\) of the good corresponding to their shared edge. Total consumption would be at least

\(\displaystyle \frac2N>\frac1N=q_{a_{\{i,j\}}},\)

contradicting feasibility. Therefore \(A(p)\) is an independent set.

By the lemma, welfare equals \(|A(p)|/N\). Meeting the threshold implies \(|A(p)|\geq K\).

This proves both directions of the reduction and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

We use the standard theorem that rational linear programming is solvable in time polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints and the total coefficient bit length.

A certificate consists only of a subset \(A\) of the input types. The verifier first checks

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\geq\kappa,\qquad \sum_{\substack{t\in A\\g\in D_t}}\mu_t\leq q_g\quad\text{for every }g.\)

It remains to determine whether some prices make precisely these types affordable.

Let \(B=1+\max_t b_t\). We may restrict every price to the interval \([0,B]\) without changing any attainable affordability pattern. Indeed, replace each price \(p_g>B\) by \(B\):

  • An affordable desired bundle cannot contain such a good, since its original price alone exceeds every budget. Its price is therefore unchanged.
  • An unaffordable desired bundle either is unchanged or contains a capped good whose new price \(B\) still exceeds its type’s budget.

Now maximize a variable \(\delta\) subject to

\(0\leq p_g\leq B\quad\text{for every }g,\qquad 0\leq\delta\leq1,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{g\in D_t}p_g\leq b_t\quad(t\in A),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{g\in D_t}p_g\geq b_t+\delta\quad(t\notin A).\)

This rational linear program has \(m+1\) variables, polynomially many constraints, and polynomial coefficient bit length. Its feasible region is bounded and closed.

If the desired affordability pattern exists, cap its prices as above. When there are excluded types, their finitely many strictly positive unaffordability gaps have a strictly positive minimum. Choosing \(\delta\) to be the smaller of that minimum and one makes the program feasible with \(\delta>0\). If there are no excluded types, take \(\delta=1\).

Conversely, any feasible solution with \(\delta>0\) makes every type in \(A\) affordable and every excluded type strictly unaffordable. Thus the verifier accepts exactly when the program is feasible and its optimal value is positive.

The certificate has \(\tau\) bits, and all verification steps take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

The stated mass model is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and therefore is not polynomial-time solvable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The decisive property is that affordability forces satisfaction of an entire type’s mass; fractional allocation across recipients does not remove the combinatorial selection problem.
