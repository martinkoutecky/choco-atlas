The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita budgets \(b_t\), and normalized nonnegative piecewise-linear valuations \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\) with \(K\) shared segments, define \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\) and let \(U_t^*\) be type \(t\)'s aggregate utility at the Fisher equilibrium. Compute a feasible pure aggregate allocation \(y_t=\mathbf{1}_{A_t}\), where each \(A_t\) is a union of at most \(K\) intervals and \(\sum_t y_t\leq 1\), such that \(\int_0^1 v_t(\theta)y_t(\theta)\,d\theta\geq U_t^*-\epsilon\) for every type \(t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a positive binary-rational per-capita budget \(b_t\) and a nonnegative valuation density \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\), normalized by \(\int_0^1v_t(\theta)\,d\theta=1\). The input specifies rational breakpoints \(0=a_0<\cdots<a_K=1\) and rational coefficients such that \(v_t(\theta)=c_{tk}\theta+d_{tk}\) on each segment; discontinuities and identically zero segments are allowed.
The item interval has Lebesgue measure and unit supply at every point. Set \(B_t=\mu_tb_t\). Aggregate utility is \(U_t(x_t)=\int_0^1v_t(\theta)x_t(\theta)\,d\theta\), without an additional mass factor. Define \(U^*\) as the unique utility vector maximizing \(\sum_tB_t\log U_t(x_t)\) over nonnegative essentially bounded measurable allocations satisfying \(\sum_tx_t\leq1\) almost everywhere; these are the aggregate Fisher equilibrium utilities.
Given a binary-rational tolerance \(0<\epsilon<1\), compute sets \(A_t\subseteq[0,1]\), each a union of at most \(K\) intervals with rational endpoints, such that \(y_t=\mathbf{1}_{A_t}\), \(\sum_ty_t\leq1\) almost everywhere, and \(\int_0^1v_t(\theta)y_t(\theta)\,d\theta\geq U_t^*-\epsilon\) for every type. Empty sets and leftover supply are allowed. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; there is no finite item-count parameter \(m\) in this model.

Is this allocation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau,K,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many buyers, Gao and Kroer establish polynomial-time computation of the same additive utility guarantee with at most \(K\) intervals per buyer; their analysis counts arithmetic operations. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03025).
The stated aggregate-type formulation is also a finite-buyer Fisher market with arbitrary positive budgets, so the verified result already covers its mathematical allocation problem; a distinct open continuous analogue has not been established.

Result. The problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau,K,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\), in the binary bit model.

Gao and Kroer’s Theorem 8 establishes the finite-buyer utility guarantee and the bound of \(K\) intervals per buyer, with arithmetic-operation complexity. The proof below separately handles bit complexity and rational endpoints. [Gao–Kroer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03025)

1. Normalize the budgets and bound equilibrium utilities.

Set
\[ \]
\(S=\sum_t B_t\), \(w_t=B_t/S\), and \(w_{\min}=\min_t w_t\). Multiplying the objective by \(1/S\) leaves its maximizer unchanged. Thus we maximize \(\sum_t w_t\log U_t\), where \(\sum_t w_t=1\).

These rational numbers have bit lengths polynomial in \(L\). In particular, \(\log(1/w_{\min})\) is polynomially bounded in \(L\).

Every feasible utility satisfies \(0\leq U_t\leq1\). The feasible utility set is convex and compact: the allocation set is weak-star compact in the product of the spaces \(L^\infty([0,1])\), and integration against each \(v_t\in L^1([0,1])\) is weak-star continuous. Allocating the constant share \(1/\tau\) to every type gives strictly positive utilities. Consequently, the logarithmic objective attains its maximum at positive utilities, and strict concavity makes its maximizing utility vector unique.

We will use the bound \(U_t^*\geq w_t\). To prove it, consider the feasible utility vector \(e_t\), obtained by giving the whole interval to type \(t\). The directional derivative at the optimum toward \(e_t\) is nonpositive, so

\(\displaystyle 0\geq \sum_j\frac{w_j}{U_j^*}\bigl((e_t)_j-U_j^*\bigr) =\frac{w_t}{U_t^*}-1. \)

This proves the bound.

For \(\tau=1\), simply output \(A_1=[0,1]\). Henceforth assume \(\tau\geq2\).

2. Construct a well-bounded finite-dimensional utility body.

Write \(I_k=[a_{k-1},a_k]\) and define the rational number

\(\displaystyle \Lambda_{tk}=\int_{I_k}v_t(\theta)\,d\theta.\)

Call \((t,k)\) active when \(\Lambda_{tk}>0\). If \(\Lambda_{tk}=0\), nonnegativity implies that the affine density on that segment is identically zero. Such pairs can be omitted.

For an active pair, put \(\widehat v_{tk}=v_t/\Lambda_{tk}\) on \(I_k\); its integral is one. Let \(C_k\) be the set of utility vectors attainable on \(I_k\) under these normalized densities, with one coordinate for each active type. Omit segments with no active types, and set

\(\displaystyle C=\prod_k C_k,\qquad D=\sum_k\#\{t:\Lambda_{tk}>0\}\leq\tau K.\)

Each \(C_k\) is compact and convex by the same weak-star argument used above. It is also downward closed: decreasing a buyer’s desired utility can be implemented by multiplying that buyer’s allocation by a constant in \([0,1]\).

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \{z\geq0:\sum_t z_t\leq1\}\subseteq C_k\subseteq[0,1]^{\dim C_k}. \)

The first inclusion follows by giving each type the constant allocation \(z_t\).

Let \(c\in\mathbb Q^D\) have every coordinate equal to \(1/(2\tau)\), and set \(r=1/(4\tau)\). The Euclidean ball of radius \(r\) around \(c\) lies in \(C\): every coordinate in that ball is positive, and the coordinate sum in any segment is at most \(3/4\). Also \(C\subseteq B_2(c,D)\). Thus we have explicit rational inner and outer ball bounds.

For \(z\in C\), define aggregate utilities by

\(\displaystyle U_t(z)=\sum_{k:\Lambda_{tk}>0}\Lambda_{tk}z_{tk}.\)

The image \(U(C)\) is exactly the original feasible utility set. Indeed, feasible allocations can be restricted to the segments, and independently feasible segment allocations can be concatenated.

3. Give an exact rational linear optimization oracle.

For a rational objective vector \(q\), linear optimization over \(C\) separates over segments. On a segment, assign each point to a type maximizing

\(\displaystyle q_{tk}\widehat v_{tk}(\theta),\)

including the additional option of leaving it unallocated, with objective value zero.

These functions are affine with rational coefficients. Compute all pairwise intersection points lying in the segment, partition at those points, and determine a maximizing function at a rational midpoint of each resulting open interval. Identical functions can be resolved by a fixed index order.

This allocation maximizes the objective pointwise, hence maximizes its integral. Its utility coordinates are rational, because each integral has the form

\(\displaystyle \int_\ell^h(c\theta+d)\,d\theta =\frac c2(h^2-\ell^2)+d(h-\ell). \)

There are at most polynomially many intersections and integrals. Every intermediate rational has bit length polynomial in \(L\) and the query length. Therefore this is an exact linear optimization oracle with polynomial bit complexity.

We use the standard Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver oracle theorem: for a compact convex body with supplied rational inner and outer ball bounds, weak linear optimization and weak separation are polynomially interconvertible in the binary model. Weak optimization, with tolerance \(\delta\), returns a rational point within distance \(\delta\) of the body whose objective is within \(\delta\) of the optimum. Weak separation can be expressed as either a distance certificate or a rational normal \(q\), normalized by \(\|q\|_\infty=1\), satisfying \(q\cdot x\leq q\cdot y+\delta\) throughout the body. The norm conventions are polynomially equivalent. [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf)

The compactness, dimension, ball bounds, and oracle bit complexity required by this theorem have all been verified for \(C\).

4. Optimize a smooth extension of the logarithmic objective.

Set \(a=w_{\min}/2\). Define a convex function on the whole real line by

\(\displaystyle \phi_a(s)=-\log s\quad\text{when }s\geq a,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \phi_a(s)=-\log a-\frac{s-a}{a}+\frac{(s-a)^2}{2a^2} \quad\text{when }s<a. \)

The derivatives match at \(a\). On \([0,1]\), its second derivative is at least one: it equals \(1/a^2\) below \(a\) and \(1/s^2\) above \(a\).

Define

\(\displaystyle G(u)=\sum_t w_t\phi_a(u_t),\qquad f(z)=G(U(z)).\)

Because \(U_t^*\geq w_t\geq2a\), the gradient of \(G\) at \(U^*\) equals the gradient of the original negative logarithmic objective there. Its first-order optimality inequality therefore holds on the entire feasible utility set. Convexity implies that \(U^*\) also minimizes \(G\). Furthermore, for every feasible \(u\),

\(\displaystyle G(u)-G(U^*)\geq \frac{w_{\min}}2\|u-U^*\|_2^2. \)

Here the linear term in the strong-convexity inequality is nonnegative by first-order optimality.

At rational arguments, \(\nabla f\) is exactly rational. Function values can be approximated to any prescribed accuracy in polynomial bit time. For completeness, logarithms can be computed by scaling their arguments into \([1,2]\) and using

\(\displaystyle \log x=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{\bigl((x-1)/(x+1)\bigr)^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \)

whose ratio parameter has absolute value at most \(1/3\) on that interval.

On \([-1,2]^D\), each aggregate utility lies in \([-1,2]\), since \(\sum_k\Lambda_{tk}=1\). The derivative formula gives the valid Lipschitz bound

\(\displaystyle H=\frac{4D}{a^2}\)

for \(f\) on that box.

To make the convex optimization application explicit, consider the compact epigraph body

\(\displaystyle E=\{(z,s):z\in C,\ f(z)\leq s\leq8/a\}. \)

For \(z\in C\), we have \(0\leq f(z)\leq3/a\). Thus \(E\) contains the ball of radius \(r\) centered at \((c,4/a)\), and it lies in the ball of radius \(D+8/a\) with that center.

It also has a polynomial-time weak separation oracle. Given \((z,s)\) and tolerance \(0<\delta\leq1\):

  • If \(s>8/a\), the upper-bound constraint separates.
  • Otherwise, query weak separation for \(C\) with tolerance \(\delta/[4(1+H)]\). Any returned normal extends to a valid weak separating normal for \(E\).
  • If the query instead certifies proximity to \(C\), approximate \(f(z)\) within \(\delta/4\). If the approximation exceeds \(s+\delta/4\), convexity gives a separating normal \((\nabla f(z),-1)\).
  • Otherwise, choose \(z_0\in C\) within the certified distance. Then \(f(z_0)\leq s+3\delta/4\), by the Lipschitz bound. The point \((z_0,\max\{s,f(z_0)\})\) belongs to \(E\) and is within distance \(\delta\) of the query.

In the last two cases, the proximity certificate places \(z\) inside \([-1,2]^D\), so the stated Lipschitz bound applies. All cuts and value approximations have polynomial bit complexity.

Apply weak linear optimization to minimize \(s\) over \(E\), with tolerance

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac{w_{\min}\epsilon^2}{8}, \qquad \xi=\frac{\eta}{2(1+HD)}. \)

It returns rational \((z,s)\) within distance \(\xi\) of \(E\), with \(s\leq G(U^*)+\xi\).

Recover an exactly feasible rational vector by setting

\(\displaystyle q_{tk}=\max\{0,\min\{1,z_{tk}\}-\xi\}. \)

Indeed, for some \((z_0,s_0)\in E\) within distance \(\xi\), each coordinate satisfies \(0\leq q_{tk}\leq(z_0)_{tk}\). Downward closure gives \(q\in C\). Also \(\|q-z_0\|_2\leq2D\xi\), whence

\(\displaystyle f(q)\leq f(z_0)+2HD\xi \leq G(U^*)+2\xi+2HD\xi =G(U^*)+\eta. \)

The strong-convexity inequality now gives

\(\displaystyle \|U(q)-U^*\|_2 \leq\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{w_{\min}}} =\epsilon/2. \)

Thus \(U_t(q)\geq U_t^*-\epsilon/2\) for every type. Every numerical parameter used above has encoding length polynomial in \(\tau,K,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\).

5. Prove that feasible segment utilities admit ordered interval allocations.

Fix a segment \(I=[\ell,h]\) and its active types. Transform its normalized densities to \([0,1]\):

\(\displaystyle g_i(s)=(h-\ell)\widehat v_i(\ell+(h-\ell)s) =\alpha_i s+\beta_i. \)

Their integrals are one, so \(\alpha_i=2(1-\beta_i)\), and nonnegativity gives \(0\leq\beta_i\leq2\). Sort types by decreasing \(\beta_i\), allowing ties.

Every \(g_i\) is strictly positive in the open interval. For an earlier type \(i\) and a later type \(j\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{d}{ds}\frac{g_j(s)}{g_i(s)} =\frac{2(\beta_i-\beta_j)}{g_i(s)^2}\geq0. \)

We prove that any feasible target utilities can be attained or exceeded by giving types consecutive intervals in this order.

Start with a feasible allocation attaining at least the targets. Scale the first type’s allocation down to attain its target exactly. Write its allocation as \(x_1\), and let \(r\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \int_0^r g_1(s)\,ds=\int_0^1g_1(s)x_1(s)\,ds.\)

For each later type \(j\), let \(m_j=\int_0^r g_1x_j\), and put \(T=\int_r^1g_1x_1\). Supply feasibility implies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j>1}m_j \leq\int_0^r g_1(1-x_1) =\int_r^1g_1x_1=T. \)

Give the first type the whole prefix \([0,r]\). If \(0<r<1\) and \(T>0\), remove later types from that prefix and, outside it, replace their allocations by

\(\displaystyle y_j=x_j+(m_j/T)x_1.\)

This remains supply feasible because \(\sum_{j>1}m_j/T\leq1\). Monotonicity of \(g_j/g_1\) implies that the added utility is at least \((g_j(r)/g_1(r))m_j\), while the removed utility is at most that quantity. Thus no later type loses utility.

If \(T=0\), all \(m_j\) vanish and no transfer is needed. The cases \(r=0\) and \(r=1\) are immediate; in the latter case all later feasible utilities are zero.

Repeat on the suffix with the remaining types. Their ratio inequalities continue to hold on every suffix. Induction proves the claim. The last type can receive the entire remaining suffix.

Hence every \(q_{\cdot k}\in C_k\) can be implemented with one interval per active type on segment \(k\). This proof includes repeated normalized densities.

6. Compute rational cuts with controlled loss.

Let

\(\displaystyle M=1+\max_{t,k}\max\{ c_{tk}a_{k-1}+d_{tk},\ c_{tk}a_k+d_{tk}\}. \)

Endpoint values here are evaluated using the affine formula for that segment. Thus \(M\) bounds all densities almost everywhere and has polynomial bit length.

Use the rational cut tolerance \(\rho=\epsilon/(2KM)\). On each segment, process types in the order from Section 5. For each type except the last, use its original-density target \(\Lambda_{tk}q_{tk}\).

Starting at the current rational left endpoint, find the cut by bisection, returning a lower endpoint within \(\rho\) of the exact cut. Every comparison uses the exact rational integral formula from Section 3. Zero targets receive empty intervals.

These cuts always exist. To see this rigorously, compare with the exact ordered construction from Section 5. Inductively, the current rational left endpoint is no later than its exact counterpart. Nonnegativity implies that the exact cut from this earlier starting point is no later than the corresponding ideal cut. Rounding it downward preserves this invariant.

Each computed interval attains its target minus at most \(M\rho\). The last type receives the remaining suffix and attains at least its target. Consequently, for every type,

\(\displaystyle \int_{A_t}v_t \geq \sum_k\Lambda_{tk}q_{tk}-KM\rho \geq U_t^*-\epsilon. \)

Each \(A_t\) is a union of at most one interval from each original segment, hence at most \(K\) intervals. The intervals are disjoint almost everywhere and have rational endpoints.

There are at most \(\tau K\) cuts. Each requires \(O(\log(1/\rho))\) bisections, with polynomially bounded rational bit lengths throughout. Together with the oracle optimization, this proves the claimed polynomial running time.
