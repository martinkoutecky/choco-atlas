Yes—and a stronger statement holds: the capacity inequalities are necessary and sufficient for realizability of \(Q\) itself. Consequently, they are sufficient for realizability of \(\tfrac12Q\).

More precisely, the following three conditions are equivalent:

  • \(Q\) is realizable.
  • Every capacity inequality holds.
  • The following row and column bounds hold:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n\in N}Q_{hn}\leq 1\quad\text{for every }h\in H,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h\in H}\mu_hQ_{hn}\leq s_n\quad\text{for every }n\in N.\)

This uses the stated assumption that rankings impose no additional assignment restrictions.

2. Measurable splitting lemma

Lemma. Let \(E\subseteq[0,1]\) be Lebesgue measurable. If \(r_1,\ldots,r_m\) are nonnegative real numbers satisfying \(\sum_{n=1}^m r_n\leq\lambda(E)\), then there are disjoint measurable subsets \(B_1,\ldots,B_m\subseteq E\) with \(\lambda(B_n)=r_n\).

Proof. First, every measurable set \(F\subseteq[0,1]\) contains a measurable subset of any prescribed measure \(r\in[0,\lambda(F)]\). Indeed, define

\(\displaystyle f(t)=\lambda(F\cap[0,t]),\qquad t\in[0,1].\)

For \(u\leq v\), we have \(0\leq f(v)-f(u)\leq v-u\). Thus \(f\) is continuous. Also, \(f(0)=0\), since a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero, and \(f(1)=\lambda(F)\). The intermediate value theorem supplies \(t\) with \(f(t)=r\); take \(F\cap[0,t]\).

Now choose \(B_n\) successively from \(E\setminus\bigcup_{j<n}B_j\). At step \(n\), this remainder has measure

\(\displaystyle \lambda(E)-\sum_{j<n}r_j\geq\sum_{j=n}^m r_j\geq r_n.\)

The preceding construction therefore applies at every step. The resulting sets are measurable, disjoint, and have the required measures. \(\square\)

3. Proof of the characterization

Realizability implies every capacity inequality

Suppose \(a\) realizes \(Q\). For every \(A\subseteq H\times N\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{(h,n)\in A}\mu_hQ_{hn} = \sum_{(h,n)\in A}\lambda(E_h\cap a^{-1}(n)) \leq C(A). \)

The last inequality follows directly from the definition of \(C(A)\), because \(a\) is one of the feasible assignments over which the supremum is taken.

Capacity inequalities imply the row and column bounds

Fix \(h\in H\) and take \(A_h=\{h\}\times N\). For any feasible assignment \(a\), the sets \(E_h\cap a^{-1}(n)\), as \(n\) varies, are disjoint subsets of \(E_h\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n\in N}\lambda(E_h\cap a^{-1}(n))\leq\mu_h, \)

so \(C(A_h)\leq\mu_h\). The corresponding capacity inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \mu_h\sum_{n\in N}Q_{hn}\leq C(A_h)\leq\mu_h.\)

Since \(\mu_h>0\), division yields the row bound.

Next fix \(n\in N\) and take \(A^n=H\times\{n\}\). Because the type sets partition \([0,1]\), every feasible assignment satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{h\in H}\lambda(E_h\cap a^{-1}(n)) =\lambda(a^{-1}(n)) \leq s_n. \)

Thus \(C(A^n)\leq s_n\), and its capacity inequality gives the column bound.

The row and column bounds imply realizability

Define the required cell masses by \(x_{hn}=\mu_hQ_{hn}\). They are nonnegative, and the row bounds imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n\in N}x_{hn}\leq\mu_h=\lambda(E_h).\)

Apply the splitting lemma separately to each \(E_h\). It supplies disjoint measurable subsets \(B_{hn}\subseteq E_h\) satisfying \(\lambda(B_{hn})=x_{hn}\).

Assign each point of \(B_{hn}\) to school \(n\), and assign every remaining point to \(0\). This defines a measurable map: each school’s preimage is the finite union \(\bigcup_{h\in H}B_{hn}\).

The column bounds give feasibility:

\(\displaystyle \lambda(a^{-1}(n)) =\sum_{h\in H}\lambda(B_{hn}) =\sum_{h\in H}\mu_hQ_{hn} \leq s_n. \)

Moreover, \(E_h\cap a^{-1}(n)=B_{hn}\), so every required share is realized. This completes the equivalence.

Finally, the row and column bounds also hold for \(\alpha Q\) whenever \(\alpha\in[0,1]\): scaling decreases every nonnegative row sum and school load. Taking \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) proves the requested sufficiency.

4. Running time and constructive representation

Exact realizability, and equivalently satisfaction of all capacity inequalities, can be decided by checking only the \(\tau+m\) row and column bounds. This requires \(O(\tau m)\) rational arithmetic operations.

A conservative bit complexity bound is \(O(\tau mL^2)\). To verify it, write the inputs as

\(\displaystyle \mu_h=\frac{a_h}{b_h},\qquad Q_{hn}=\frac{c_{hn}}{d_{hn}},\qquad s_n=\frac{e_n}{f_n},\)

with positive denominators. Let \(D\) be the product of all these input denominators, counting their occurrences. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). The quantities

\(\displaystyle w_{hn}=a_hc_{hn}\frac{D}{b_hd_{hn}},\qquad z_h=a_h\frac{D}{b_h},\qquad k_n=e_n\frac{D}{f_n} \)

are integers of \(O(L)\) bits. The checks become

\(\displaystyle \sum_{n\in N}w_{hn}\leq z_h,\qquad \sum_{h\in H}w_{hn}\leq k_n.\)

There are \(O(\tau m)\) integer operations, each costing at most \(O(L^2)\) bit operations using elementary multiplication and division. No enumeration of rankings outside the supplied support is needed.

For a canonical representation in which the \(E_h\) are consecutive intervals, a realizing assignment can also be output within this bound: subdivide each type interval into school intervals of lengths \(w_{hn}/D\), followed by its unassigned remainder. All endpoints have \(O(L)\)-bit rational representations. For arbitrary measurable type sets, Section 2 proves existence; the rational input alone does not encode those sets computationally.
