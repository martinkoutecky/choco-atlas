The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support rational distribution over initial belief types, a target formula φ, a controllable portion of the mass, and a common policy \(R^k_{Δ_{d_D,Σ}}\) for some \(k\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\), does there exist a mass transfer from controllable types \((B,e)\) to bases \(B'\) with \(B'\preceq_\varphi B\) such that, under complete interaction and mass-weighted merging, every resulting positive-mass cohort's cycle outcome entails φ?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let there be \(m\geq1\) propositional variables. Initial bases and the target \(\varphi\) are satisfiable propositional formulas, encoded succinctly; bases are identified up to logical equivalence.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \((B,e)\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, summing to \(1\); \(e\in\{0,1\}\) indicates controllability. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A control divides each controllable type's mass among finitely many satisfiable bases \(B'\), using nonnegative real masses that conserve its mass; each destination must satisfy \(B'\preceq_\varphi B\), meaning \(B\wedge\varphi\models B'\models B\vee\varphi\). Uncontrollable mass stays at its initial base; retaining controllable mass unchanged is allowed. There is no additional budget or cost.
For a weighted profile \(Q\), define \(\Delta_\mu(Q)\) by retaining all interpretations satisfying \(\mu\) that maximize \(\sum_{(D,w)\in Q}w\mathbf{1}_{\omega\models D}\); write \(\Delta(Q)=\Delta_\top(Q)\). Ties retain every maximizer.
The population is atomless and interaction complete: every individual sees the entire current population profile \(Q\), including the rest of its own cohort. Singleton profiles explicitly inserted below have weight \(1\), and \(\uplus\) adds profile weights.
Fix a common policy index \(k\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\). With \(M=\Delta(Q)\), the six updates of a current base \(B\) are \(R^1(B,Q)=M\), \(R^2(B,Q)=\Delta_M(\{(B,1)\})\), \(R^3(B,Q)=\Delta(Q\uplus\{(B,1)\})\), \(R^4(B,Q)=\Delta(\{(B,1),(M,1)\})\), \(R^5(B,Q)=\Delta_B(\{(M,1)\})\), and \(R^6(B,Q)=\Delta_B(Q)\).
After the initial control, updates are synchronous and cohort masses remain fixed. A cohort's outcome is the disjunction of its bases on the eventual cycle, considered up to logical equivalence.
Target. For each fixed \(k\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\), is deciding whether a control makes every positive-mass cohort's outcome entail \(\varphi\) solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).
Nearest known results. In finite societies where successful unanimous acceptance survives every initial promotion, replacing every controllable base by \(\varphi\) succeeds whenever any control succeeds; this is a structural result, not a complexity classification.
Finite complete societies with a common one of these six drastic-distance sum policies satisfy that preservation property. [Source](https://www.cril.univ-artois.fr/~marquis/ijcai16-long.pdf). No result settling precisely the mass model defined above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined the otherwise unspecified type component e as a binary controllability flag; partially controllable belief types can be represented by separate flagged types.
  • Normalized population mass to one and interpreted complete interaction as atomless interaction, so an individual excludes no positive mass from its context.
  • Assigned weight one to each explicitly inserted singleton base in the policy definitions; the graded question did not specify how such singleton weights compare with population mass.
  • Allowed arbitrary nonnegative real transfer masses over finitely many destinations, preserving every source mass; imposed no additional budget or cost.
  • Used succinct propositional-formula input encoding and defined m and L.
  • Required satisfiable initial bases, destination bases, and target, following the consistent-base interpretation of distance-based merging and excluding vacuous acceptance through inconsistent controlled bases.
  • Retained all maximizing interpretations rather than imposing a tie-breaking order, and defined cohort outcomes using eventual cycles.

1. Classification

For every fixed \(k\in\{1,\ldots,6\}\), the decision problem is NP-hard, under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds already with:

  • support size \(\tau=2\);
  • one controllable type and one uncontrollable type;
  • fixed masses \(1/4\) and \(3/4\);
  • a target consisting of one propositional variable.

Thus a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The following deterministic upper bounds also hold. They are not claimed to be optimal.

| Fixed policy \(k\) | Classification | Decision running time |
|---|---|---|
| \(1,2,4\) | NP-hard | \(2^m(\tau+1)\operatorname{poly}(L)\) |
| \(5\) | NP-hard | \(2^m(\tau+1)\operatorname{poly}(L)\) |
| \(3,6\) | NP-hard | \(2^{O(m4^m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) |

The algorithms allow arbitrary finite splitting into real masses and retain every tied maximizer. In particular, all six problems are polynomial-time solvable when \(m\) is fixed.

2. Dynamics of an arbitrary controlled profile

Let \(\Omega=\{0,1\}^m\), let \(N=|\Omega|=2^m\), and identify each base with its nonempty model set. Write \(G=\operatorname{Mod}(\varphi)\).

Consider any finite profile with positive cohort masses \(w_i\), summing to \(1\), and current model sets \(D_i\). Define

\[ s(\omega)=\sum_i w_i\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in D_i}, \qquad S=\max_{\omega\in\Omega}s(\omega), \qquad M=\{\omega:s(\omega)=S\}. \]

Here and below, these displayed expressions use the usual mathematical interpretation. Because some satisfiable base has positive mass, \(S>0\).

2.1. Policies \(3\) and \(6\) coincide

For cohort \(i\), policy \(3\) maximizes \(s(\omega)+\mathbf{1}_{\omega\in D_i}\).

If \(\omega\in D_i\), then \(s(\omega)\geq w_i>0\), so its augmented score exceeds \(1\). If \(\omega\notin D_i\), its augmented score is at most \(1\). Therefore every maximizer belongs to \(D_i\), and within \(D_i\) the added score is constant.

Consequently,

\[ R^3(D_i,Q)=R^6(D_i,Q) =\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\omega\in D_i}s(\omega). \]

This uses both normalization of the population mass and positive cohort mass.

2.2. Policies \(1,2,4,5\)

Their updates are:

| Policy | If \(D_i\cap M\neq\varnothing\) | If \(D_i\cap M=\varnothing\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(1\) | \(M\) | \(M\) |
| \(2\) | \(D_i\cap M\) | \(M\) |
| \(4\) | \(D_i\cap M\) | \(D_i\cup M\) |
| \(5\) | \(D_i\cap M\) | \(D_i\) |

For policy \(4\), this follows because the two singleton bases give score \(2\) on their intersection, or, when disjoint, score \(1\) on their union.

Let \(a\) be the total mass of cohorts whose initial bases miss \(M\).

Under policy \(2\), after one update every base is contained in \(M\), and every \(\omega\in M\) has score \(S+a\): its original supporters retain it, and all cohorts missing \(M\) acquire it. Thus the new maximizing set is exactly \(M\), and the profile is fixed.

Under policy \(4\), after one update every \(\omega\in M\) again has score \(S+a\). Every interpretation outside \(M\) has score at most its original score, which is strictly below \(S\). The maximizing set therefore remains \(M\). At the second update, bases previously missing \(M\) become \(M\); all other bases remain their intersections with \(M\). This profile is fixed.

Policy \(1\) plainly reaches the unanimous base \(M\) after one update.

In all three cases the union of final bases is \(M\). Indeed, every \(\omega\in M\) originally had a positive-mass supporter, which retains it. Therefore:

For policies \(1,2,4\), a given controlled profile succeeds exactly when \(M\subseteq G\).

For policy \(5\), every interpretation in \(M\) retains exactly its original supporters, so its score remains \(S\). Scores outside \(M\) cannot increase and remain strictly below \(S\). Thus \(M\) remains unchanged, and the profile is fixed after one update.

It follows that:

For policy \(5\), a given controlled profile succeeds exactly when \(M\subseteq G\) and every initial cohort whose base contains a model outside \(G\) intersects \(M\).

Necessity of \(M\subseteq G\) follows because each interpretation in \(M\) has a positive-mass supporter and survives in that supporter’s final base.

2.3. Policies \(3,6\) stabilize within \(N\) rounds

Both policies contract every current base to its score-maximizing models. We prove the uniform bound, independently of the number of cohorts and their masses.

Initially regard all cohorts as active. Among their supported interpretations, take the set \(A\) of interpretations having maximal active score.

Every active cohort meeting \(A\) contracts to its intersection with \(A\). Every interpretation in \(A\) retains all its active supporters, so its score remains unchanged. Every interpretation outside \(A\) had strictly smaller active score, and contraction cannot increase that score. Consequently, the cohorts meeting \(A\) have reached fixed bases.

Their resulting bases are contained in \(A\). The remaining active cohorts have bases disjoint from \(A\), and contraction preserves this disjointness. Hence the fixed cohorts no longer contribute to scores on active bases.

Repeat this argument on the remaining active profile. Each round fixes some cohorts and uses a nonempty set of interpretations disjoint from all sets used previously. There can be at most \(N\) such rounds.

Thus every cohort is fixed after at most \(N\) updates. In particular, eventual outcomes are fixed bases; nontrivial eventual cycles do not occur.

3. A common NP-hardness reduction

Let \(F\) be an arbitrary propositional SAT instance on variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). Introduce a fresh variable \(p\), set \(\varphi=p\), and construct two types:

| Initial base | Controllable? | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| \(B=\neg p\vee(p\wedge F)\) | No | \(3/4\) |
| \(C=\neg p\) | Yes | \(1/4\) |

Both initial bases and the target are satisfiable, regardless of whether \(F\) is satisfiable. The construction has \(m=n+1\), \(\tau=2\), and size linear in the SAT input size.

The controllable type is genuinely changeable. Indeed, \(C\wedge p\) is unsatisfiable and \(C\vee p\) is a tautology, so every satisfiable destination base is admissible.

3.1. If \(F\) is satisfiable, a successful control exists

Replace the entire controllable mass by \(p\). The score is:

\[ s(\omega)= \begin{cases} 1,&\omega\models p\wedge F,\\ 1/4,&\omega\models p\wedge\neg F,\\ 3/4,&\omega\models\neg p. \end{cases} \]

Hence \(M=\operatorname{Mod}(p\wedge F)\), which is nonempty.

Both current bases contain \(M\). Policies \(1,2,4,5\) therefore update both cohorts to \(M\). Policy \(6\) also does so, because \(M\) is precisely the set of maximum-score models within either base. Policy \(3\) coincides with policy \(6\).

Once both cohorts hold \(M\), that profile is fixed under every policy. Every outcome entails \(p\).

3.2. If \(F\) is unsatisfiable, no control succeeds

Now the uncontrollable base is equivalent to \(\neg p\).

Under any admissible splitting of the controllable mass:

  • every interpretation satisfying \(\neg p\) has score at least \(3/4\);
  • every interpretation satisfying \(p\) has score at most \(1/4\).

Thus the initial maximizing set is nonempty and contained in \(\operatorname{Mod}(\neg p)\).

For policies \(1,2,4\), Section 2.2 implies failure.

For policies \(5,6\), every update contracts the uncontrollable cohort’s current base. Its base therefore remains a nonempty subset of \(\operatorname{Mod}(\neg p)\) forever. Its outcome cannot entail \(p\). Policy \(3\) again coincides with policy \(6\).

We have proved, for each fixed policy, that the constructed instance admits a successful control if and only if \(F\) is satisfiable. This establishes all six NP-hardness claims.

4. Singly exponential algorithms

Write \(w_i\) for the input type masses. Define:

\[ c=\sum_{i:e_i=1}w_i, \qquad u(\omega)=\sum_{i:e_i=0}w_i\mathbf{1}_{\omega\models B_i}. \]

Thus \(c\) is the total controllable mass and \(u\) is the contribution of uncontrollable types.

Enumerating all interpretations evaluates all input formulas and computes these quantities in \(2^m(\tau+1)\operatorname{poly}(L)\) bit operations. Every rational sum and comparison has bit length polynomial in \(L\).

4.1. Policies \(1,2,4\)

Replacing every controllable type by \(\varphi\) gives score \(u(\omega)+c\) on \(G\), and \(u(\omega)\) outside \(G\).

Under any control, a good interpretation’s score is at most \(u(\omega)+c\), while a bad interpretation’s score is at least \(u(\omega)\). Therefore a successful control exists exactly when:

\[ \max_{\omega\in G}\bigl(u(\omega)+c\bigr) > \max_{\omega\notin G}u(\omega). \]

To justify necessity, a successful controlled profile must have only good global maximizers. Its maximum good score strictly exceeds every bad score, and the preceding bounds imply the displayed inequality. Sufficiency follows by replacing all controllable mass by \(\varphi\).

If \(G=\Omega\), the answer is immediately yes.

This proves the stated running time for these three policies.

4.2. Policy \(5\)

Define the forced controllable contribution on good interpretations:

\[ f(\omega)= \sum_{i:e_i=1}w_i\mathbf{1}_{\omega\models B_i}, \qquad \omega\in G. \]

Promotion requires every destination of type \(i\) to contain \(\operatorname{Mod}(B_i)\cap G\). Hence every control gives a good interpretation score at least \(u(\omega)+f(\omega)\).

Let \(J\) consist of the uncontrollable types whose bases contain at least one bad interpretation.

If \(J=\varnothing\), the answer is yes: replace all controllable mass by \(\varphi\).

If some \(i\in J\) has no good model, the answer is no, since policy \(5\) always contracts that uncontrollable base.

Otherwise define:

\[ A=\max_{\omega\in G}\bigl(u(\omega)+f(\omega)\bigr), \qquad D=\max_{\omega\notin G}u(\omega), \]

and

\[ H= \min_{i\in J} \max_{\omega\in G\cap\operatorname{Mod}(B_i)} \bigl(u(\omega)+c\bigr). \]

The exact decision criterion is \(H\geq A\) and \(H>D\).

Necessity

Suppose a successful control exists. Its initial maximizing set \(M\) is contained in \(G\).

Every positive-mass controlled destination must contain a good model: otherwise contraction could never make that cohort accept \(\varphi\). Remove all bad models from every controlled destination. This preserves satisfiability and admissibility. Good scores remain unchanged and bad scores decrease, so \(M\) remains exactly unchanged. Uncontrollable cohorts consequently have the same updates, while controlled cohorts now already entail \(\varphi\).

Let \(T\) be the maximum score of this modified profile. Forced good contributions imply \(T\geq A\). Since all bad scores now equal \(u(\omega)\), and no bad interpretation maximizes, \(T>D\).

Every type \(i\in J\) must meet \(M\). For such a meeting interpretation \(\omega\),

\[ T=s(\omega)\leq u(\omega)+c. \]

Thus \(T\leq H\), proving \(H\geq A\) and \(H>D\).

Sufficiency

Assume these inequalities. We construct a finite control with good scores

\[ s(\omega)=\min\{u(\omega)+c,H\}, \qquad \omega\in G, \]

and bad scores \(s(\omega)=u(\omega)\).

The desired total controllable contribution at each good interpretation lies between \(f(\omega)\) and \(c\). Therefore it can be divided among controllable types, respecting the forced inclusion of every model in \(\operatorname{Mod}(B_i)\cap G\).

Moreover, there is a good interpretation \(z\) with \(u(z)+c=H\): choose a type attaining the minimum defining \(H\), then an interpretation attaining its inner maximum. At \(z\), the desired contribution is \(c\), so every controllable type includes \(z\) with its entire mass.

For each controllable type, realize the chosen inclusion fractions by independently including its optional good interpretations. Always include its forced good interpretations and \(z\). This is a distribution over finitely many bases. Every destination is nonempty, is contained in \(G\), and contains the required intersection with \(G\); hence every destination is admissible.

The resulting maximizing set is exactly:

\[ M=\{\omega\in G:u(\omega)+c\geq H\}. \]

Bad scores are strictly below \(H\), because \(H>D\). Every type in \(J\) meets \(M\), by the definition of \(H\). All controlled destinations and all other uncontrollable bases already entail \(\varphi\). Section 2.2 therefore proves success.

All quantities in this criterion require only enumeration of interpretations and rational sums and comparisons. This establishes the singly exponential bound for policy \(5\).

5. A finite algorithm for policies \(3,6\)

Here arbitrary real splitting is handled through rational linear programming, without assuming rational controls in advance.

5.1. Enumerate score orders

A weak order is an ordered partition of \(\Omega\), representing equal-score blocks in decreasing score order. There are at most \(N^N\) weak orders: assign each interpretation its block rank in \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\).

Enumerate sequences of \(N\) weak orders, one for each update round. Their number is at most \(N^{N^2}\).

For a fixed sequence and any nonempty initial model set \(E\), its hypothetical trajectory is determined: at each round, retain the elements of its current set in the highest score block that it meets.

5.2. Encode control masses

For each controllable input type \(i\), enumerate every admissible nonempty destination \(E\), namely every set satisfying:

\[ \operatorname{Mod}(B_i)\cap G \subseteq E \subseteq \operatorname{Mod}(B_i)\cup G. \]

Introduce its mass variable \(x_{i,E}\geq0\), with conservation constraint:

\[ \sum_E x_{i,E}=w_i. \]

Uncontrollable types retain fixed initial masses. There are at most \(\tau 2^N\) variables.

At each round, each interpretation’s score is a rational linear expression in these variables: the coefficient of a destination variable is \(1\) exactly when its hypothetical current base contains that interpretation.

Require equal scores within each proposed weak-order block and strictly decreasing scores between consecutive blocks. Also require \(x_{i,E}=0\) whenever the hypothetical base after \(N\) updates contains a bad interpretation. Reject the sequence if an uncontrollable cohort’s hypothetical final base contains a bad interpretation.

To handle strict inequalities, introduce \(0\leq\delta\leq1\), replace each required strict score difference by a difference at least \(\delta\), and maximize \(\delta\). Accept the sequence exactly when the linear program is feasible with optimum strictly positive.

This transformation is exact: finitely many strict inequalities have a common positive margin whenever they all hold. If there are no strict inequalities, feasible mass assignments allow \(\delta=1\).

5.3. Correctness and running time

If the linear program accepts, its score constraints ensure, by induction on rounds, that every positive-mass cohort follows its hypothetical trajectory. Section 2.3 guarantees stabilization by round \(N\), and the final-base constraints guarantee success.

Conversely, any successful real-mass control determines a sequence of actual weak score orders. Its masses satisfy the associated linear constraints, with a positive common margin for all strict inequalities. Therefore that sequence is accepted.

The programs have rational coefficients, explicitly encoded polynomial size in \(\tau 2^N,N,L\). Rational linear programming is solvable in polynomial time in this encoded size; allowing real control masses introduces no additional feasibility issue.

The total running time is bounded by:

\[ N^{N^2}\operatorname{poly}(\tau 2^N,N,L) = 2^{O(m4^m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). \]

Policy \(3\) has the same algorithm because it coincides with policy \(6\).

6. Why replacing all controllable bases by the target is insufficient

The cited [finite-society preservation result](https://www.cril.univ-artois.fr/~marquis/ijcai16-long.pdf) is not used in these proofs. Under the specified mass updates, the replacement shortcut fails for policies \(3,5,6\).

The following examples use distinct interpretations \(a,b,c,d\) with \(m=2\), and target model set \(G=\{a,c\}\). Every listed model set is expressible by a satisfiable propositional formula.

Policy \(5\)

Take uncontrollable bases \(\{a,b\}\) and \(\{c\}\), with masses \(1/4\) and \(1/2\), and controllable base \(\{a,b\}\), with mass \(1/4\).

Replacing the controllable base by \(\{a\}\) is admissible. Scores are \(s(a)=s(c)=1/2\) and \(s(b)=1/4\). The maximizing set is \(\{a,c\}\), and every cohort’s update entails the target.

Replacing it instead by \(G\) gives \(s(c)=3/4\), \(s(a)=1/2\), and \(s(b)=1/4\). Now \(M=\{c\}\). The uncontrollable base \(\{a,b\}\) misses \(M\), remains unchanged, and fails.

Policies \(3,6\)

Take uncontrollable bases \(\{a\}\), \(\{a,b\}\), and \(\{c,b\}\), with masses \(1/2,1/8,1/4\), and controllable base \(\{b\}\), with mass \(1/8\).

Replacing the controllable base by \(\{c\}\) is admissible. Initially:

\[ s(a)=5/8,\qquad s(c)=s(b)=3/8. \]

The base \(\{a,b\}\) first contracts to \(\{a\}\), while \(\{c,b\}\) retains both models. The next scores satisfy \(s(c)=3/8>s(b)=1/4\), so \(\{c,b\}\) contracts to \(\{c\}\). Every final base entails the target.

Replacing the controllable base by \(G\) instead gives initial scores \(s(a)=3/4\) and \(s(c)=s(b)=3/8\). After one update, the controllable cohort holds \(\{a\}\), and the scores of \(c,b\) both become \(1/4\). The base \(\{c,b\}\) remains tied and fixed, so unanimity fails.

These examples also show why the upper-bound arguments must respect the exact interaction and tie conventions in the question.
