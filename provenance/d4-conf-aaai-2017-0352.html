Global existence and the physical domain

The original ordinary-agent simplex is

$$ x,y,z\ge0,\qquad x+y+z=1-p. $$

On its three boundary faces, the corresponding inward derivatives are

$$ \left.\dot x\right|_{x=0}=z^2+\frac32pz\ge0, $$

$$ \left.\dot y\right|_{y=0}=z^2\ge0, $$

$$ \left.\dot z\right|_{z=0}=2xy+py\ge0. $$

These inequalities imply forward invariance: the locally Lipschitz vector field lies in the tangent cone of the simplex at every boundary point. Compactness then prevents finite-time escape. Consequently, the solution exists uniquely for all nonnegative times, and

$$ 0\le s_p(t)\le r_p(t)\le1-p. $$

Strict monotonicity in the converted mass

Let $p_1<p_2$, put $\delta=p_2-p_1$, and define

$$ R=r_{p_1}-r_{p_2},\qquad S=s_{p_1}-s_{p_2}. $$

Subtracting the two systems yields

$$ \dot R =-A_{p_1}R+ \bigl(B_{p_1}-s_{p_1}-s_{p_2}\bigr)S +\frac{\delta}{2}(r_{p_2}+s_{p_2}), $$

$$ \dot S=(r_{p_1}+r_{p_2})R-S. $$

The off-diagonal coefficients are nonnegative. Indeed,

$$ B_{p_1}-s_{p_1}-s_{p_2} \ge B_{p_1}-(1-p_1)-(1-p_2) =\frac{p_1}{2}+p_2\ge0. $$

$$ \dot R\ge-A_{p_1}R, $$

so

$$ r_{p_1}(t)-r_{p_2}(t) \ge(p_2-p_1)e^{-(1+p_1/2)t}>0. $$

$$ \boxed{p_*\le q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_q(H)\le\epsilon.} $$

This reduction is exact, but it does not itself settle the complexity of the remaining comparison.

2. Monotone trajectories and the equilibrium transition

Fix $0<p<1$. Define

$$ g=-\dot r,\qquad w=-\dot s=s-r^2. $$

Differentiation gives

$$ \dot g=-Ag+(B-2s)w,\qquad \dot w=2rg-w. $$

On the physical domain,

$$ B-2s\ge B-2(1-p)=\frac32p>0. $$

Initially,

$$ g(0)=0,\qquad w(0)=p(1-p)>0, $$

and

$$ \dot g(0)=\frac32p^2(1-p)>0. $$

Thus both coordinates are positive for sufficiently small positive times. Their integrating-factor formulas prevent any later first zero. Consequently,

$$ g(t)>0,\qquad w(t)>0\qquad(t>0). $$

In particular, $r$ and $s$ strictly decrease at positive times, and

$$ s(t)\ge r(t)^2. $$

Their bounded monotone coordinates converge. Their limiting state is an equilibrium: otherwise, continuity of the vector field would force some coordinate to keep changing at a rate bounded away from zero.

At an equilibrium, $s=r^2$, and

$$ 0=-Ar+Br^2-r^4=-rC_p(r), $$

where

$$ C_p(r)=r^3-Br+A. $$

The minimum of this cubic on the nonnegative half-line occurs at

$$ \rho_p=\sqrt{\frac B3}, $$

and has value

$$ d_p=A-2\rho_p^3. $$

Define the integer polynomial

$$ Q(p)=2p^3+3p^2+204p-20. $$

Direct expansion gives

$$ d_p(A+2\rho_p^3)=\frac{Q(p)}{108}. $$

The second factor is positive. Also,

$$ Q'(p)=6p^2+6p+204>0. $$

The exact evaluations

$$ Q\left(\frac{39}{400}\right) =-\frac{2548081}{32000000}<0, $$

$$ Q\left(\frac{49}{500}\right) =\frac{1418399}{62500000}>0 $$

show that there is a unique critical fraction $p_c$ satisfying

$$ Q(p_c)=0,\qquad \frac{39}{400}<p_c<\frac{49}{500}<\frac1{10}. $$

For $p>p_c$, we have $d_p>0$, so the cubic has no nonnegative root. The limiting equilibrium is therefore $(0,0)$.

For $0<p\le p_c$, let $R_+(p)$ be the largest nonnegative root of $C_p$. It satisfies

$$ \rho_p\le R_+(p)<1-p. $$

Indeed, $C_p(\rho_p)\le0$, the cubic is strictly increasing above $\rho_p$, and

$$ C_p(1-p)=p^2\left(\frac52-p\right)>0. $$

Moreover, $\rho_p<5/6<1-p$, since $p\le p_c<1/10$.

The equilibrium $(R_+,R_+^2)$ lies strictly below the initial state in both coordinates. Differences between the trajectory and this equilibrium satisfy

$$ \dot R=-AR+(B-s-R_+^2)S,\qquad \dot S=(r+R_+)R-S. $$

Both off-diagonal coefficients are nonnegative, because $s,R_+^2\le1-p$. Strictly positive initial differences remain positive by the integrating-factor argument. Thus $r(t)>R_+$ at every finite time. The limiting equilibrium must consequently have $r$-coordinate $R_+$.

We have proved

$$ \lim_{t\to\infty}r_p(t)= \begin{cases} R_+(p),&0<p\le p_c,\\ 0,&p>p_c. \end{cases} $$

Finally, $p_c$ is irrational. By the rational-root theorem, a rational root of $Q$ in lowest terms has denominator dividing $2$. No positive such rational lies in $(0,1/10)$. Hence a rational budget never equals the critical fraction. This excludes equality with the *critical parameter*, but does not exclude $r_q(H)=\epsilon$.

$$ V=r+Bs. $$

Its derivative is

$$ \dot V=-Ar+Br^2-s^2. $$

Since $s\ge r^2$,

$$ \boxed{\dot V\le-rC_p(r).} $$

Initially,

$$ V(0)=(1+B)(1-p)\le3. $$

Below the transition

$$ 0<p\le p_c,\qquad \epsilon>R_+(p). $$

$$ r(t)\ge\epsilon>R_+(p)\ge\rho_p. $$

The cubic increases above $\rho_p$, so

$$ r(t)C_p(r(t))\ge\epsilon C_p(\epsilon)>0. $$

Consequently,

$$ \dot V\le-\epsilon C_p(\epsilon) $$

until the tolerance is reached. If $r(H)\ge\epsilon$ and

$$ H\ge\frac{(1+B)(1-p)}{\epsilon C_p(\epsilon)}, $$

monotonicity ensures $r(t)\ge\epsilon$ throughout $[0,H]$, and integration gives $V(H)\le0$. This contradicts $V(H)\ge r(H)\ge\epsilon>0$.

Therefore,

$$ \boxed{ H\ge\frac{(1+B)(1-p)}{\epsilon C_p(\epsilon)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_p(H)<\epsilon } $$

under the stated hypotheses.

This supplies a new polynomial-time sufficient test using only rational arithmetic. For rational $q$ with $0<q<p_c$, the required condition $\epsilon>R_+(q)$ is equivalent to

$$ \epsilon^2>\frac{4-q}{6} \qquad\text{and}\qquad C_q(\epsilon)>0. $$

To verify this equivalence, the first inequality says $\epsilon>\rho_q$. On that interval, strict increase of the cubic makes its positive sign equivalent to $\epsilon>R_+(q)$.

Thus the following entirely rational conditions imply feasibility:

$$ Q(q)<0,\qquad \epsilon^2>\frac{4-q}{6},\qquad C_q(\epsilon)>0, $$

$$ H\epsilon C_q(\epsilon)\ge(1+B_q)(1-q). $$

Conversely, for $0<q<p_c$, the conditions

$$ \epsilon^2\le\frac{4-q}{6} \qquad\text{or}\qquad C_q(\epsilon)\le0 $$

are equivalent to $\epsilon\le R_+(q)$ and therefore imply infeasibility at every finite horizon.

Above the transition

For $p>p_c$, $C_p(r)\ge d_p>0$ for every nonnegative $r$. The same argument gives

$$ H\ge\frac{(1+B)(1-p)}{\epsilon d_p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_p(H)<\epsilon. $$

Moreover,

$$ A+2\rho_p^3<3, $$

so the identity relating $d_p$ and $Q(p)$ implies

$$ d_p\ge\frac{Q(p)}{324}. $$

A rational sufficient feasibility test is therefore

$$ \boxed{ Q(q)>0,\qquad H\epsilon Q(q)\ge324(1+B_q)(1-q) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_q(H)<\epsilon. } $$

All displayed rational tests have polynomial bit complexity in $L$: they use a fixed number of additions, multiplications, and exact comparisons of rational expressions of fixed degree. They are sufficient decision regions, not a complete algorithm.

4. New matching bounds near the transition

$$ T(p,\epsilon)=\inf\{t\ge0:r_p(t)\le\epsilon\}. $$

The preceding bound makes this time finite. Continuity and strict decrease give

$$ r_p(T(p,\epsilon))=\epsilon. $$

The main new quantitative result is the following.

Proposition. If

$$ p_c<p\le\frac1{10},\qquad \delta=p-p_c, $$

then

$$ \boxed{ \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\delta}} \le T\left(p,\frac12\right) \le\frac{144}{\sqrt{\delta}}. } $$

Thus the time needed to reach the fixed tolerance $1/2$ is bounded above and below by constant multiples of $\delta^{-1/2}$.

4.1. A scalar speed bound

Write

$$ h(r)=rC_p(r),\qquad \kappa=p\left(\frac52-p\right)>0. $$

Using $s=r^2+w$ in the equation for $r$ gives

$$ g=-\dot r=h(r)-(B-s-r^2)w. $$

The physical bounds imply

$$ B-s-r^2 \ge B-(1-p)-(1-p)^2 =\kappa. $$

Since $g,w\ge0$,

$$ 0\le g\le h(r),\qquad 0\le w\le\frac{h(r)}{\kappa}. $$

These inequalities will give the lower bound and control the upper-bound calculation.

4.2. The lower bound

The cubic has the exact expansion

$$ C_p(r)=d_p+(r-\rho_p)^2(r+2\rho_p). $$

Differentiating its minimum value gives

$$ d_p'=\frac{1+\rho_p}{2}. $$

Because $d_{p_c}=0$ and $0<\rho_p<1$,

$$ \frac{\delta}{2}\le d_p\le\delta. $$

Our range of $p$ also gives

$$ 0<\delta<\frac1{400},\qquad \frac34<\rho_p<\frac56. $$

The interval

$$ [\rho_p-\sqrt{\delta},\,\rho_p+\sqrt{\delta}] $$

lies strictly between $1/2$ and the initial value $1-p$. Indeed,

$$ \rho_p-\sqrt{\delta}>\frac34-\frac1{20}>\frac12, $$

$$ \rho_p+\sqrt{\delta}<\frac56+\frac1{20}<\frac9{10}\le1-p. $$

The trajectory must cross this interval before reaching $1/2$.

Within it, writing $u=r-\rho_p$, we have

$$ C_p(r) =d_p+u^2(3\rho_p+u) \le \delta+\delta\left(\frac52+\frac1{20}\right) <4\delta. $$

Because $r\le1$, the speed bound gives

$$ -\dot r=g\le rC_p(r)<4\delta. $$

Crossing an interval of width $2\sqrt{\delta}$ therefore takes at least

$$ \frac{2\sqrt{\delta}}{4\delta} =\frac1{2\sqrt{\delta}}. $$

This proves the lower bound.

4.3. An upper hitting-time estimate

I first prove a bound valid for every $p>p_c$ and $0<\epsilon<1-p$:

$$ T(p,\epsilon) \le \frac{5\pi}{\epsilon\sqrt{d_p}} + \frac{B}{\kappa} \left( 1+2\log\frac{3}{\epsilon d_p} \right). $$

Set $T=T(p,\epsilon)$ and observe that

$$ V=r+Bs=r+Br^2+Bw. $$

Since $-\dot V\ge h(r)>0$,

$$ T\le\int_0^T\frac{-\dot V}{h(r)}\,dt. $$

Substituting the expression for $V$ and using $g=-\dot r$ gives

$$ T \le \int_\epsilon^{1-p} \frac{1+2Br}{rC_p(r)}\,dr + B\int_0^T\frac{-\dot w}{h(r)}\,dt. $$

The first integral is bounded by

$$ \frac5\epsilon\int_\epsilon^{1-p}\frac{dr}{C_p(r)}. $$

The cubic expansion and $2\rho_p>1$ imply

$$ C_p(r)\ge d_p+(r-\rho_p)^2. $$

Extending the integral to the real line therefore gives

$$ \int_\epsilon^{1-p} \frac{1+2Br}{rC_p(r)}\,dr \le \frac5\epsilon \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{du}{d_p+u^2} = \frac{5\pi}{\epsilon\sqrt{d_p}}. $$

It remains to bound the integral involving $\dot w$. Let

$$ u(t)=\frac1{h(r(t))}. $$

Integration by parts yields

$$ \int_0^T\frac{-\dot w}{h(r)}\,dt = \frac{w(0)}{h(r(0))} -\frac{w(T)}{h(\epsilon)} +\int_0^T w(t)\dot u(t)\,dt. $$

The middle term is nonpositive. Furthermore,

$$ \frac{w(0)}{h(r(0))} = \frac{p(1-p)} {(1-p)p^2(5/2-p)} =\frac1\kappa. $$

On intervals where $\dot u>0$, the bound $w\le h/\kappa$ gives

$$ w\dot u \le\frac1\kappa\frac{\dot u}{u} =\frac1\kappa\frac{d}{dt}\log u. $$

Terms where $\dot u\le0$ can be discarded in an upper bound.

To count the increasing intervals of $u$, calculate

$$ h'(r)=4r^3-2Br+A,\qquad h''(r)=12r^2-2B. $$

On the nonnegative half-line, $h'$ first decreases and then increases. Hence $h'>0$ on at most two intervals. Because $r$ decreases, these give at most two intervals where $u$ increases.

Throughout $[0,T]$,

$$ \epsilon d_p\le h(r(t))\le3. $$

Each increasing interval of $\log u$ contributes at most

$$ \log\frac{3}{\epsilon d_p}. $$

Therefore,

$$ \int_0^T\frac{-\dot w}{h(r)}\,dt \le \frac1\kappa \left( 1+2\log\frac{3}{\epsilon d_p} \right), $$

which proves the asserted general upper estimate.

4.4. Specializing the upper bound

Now take $\epsilon=1/2$ and $p_c<p\le1/10$. We have

$$ d_p\ge\frac{\delta}{2}, $$

and

$$ \kappa =p\left(\frac52-p\right) > \frac{39}{400}\cdot\frac{12}{5} >\frac15. $$

$$ T\left(p,\frac12\right) \le \frac{10\pi\sqrt2}{\sqrt{\delta}} + 10\left(1+2\log\frac{12}{\delta}\right). $$

For $x\ge1$, $\log x\le\sqrt x$. One way to verify this is to maximize $(\log x)/\sqrt x$; its maximum is $2/e<1$. Consequently,

$$ \sqrt{\delta}\,T\left(p,\frac12\right) \le 10\pi\sqrt2+10\sqrt{\delta}+20\sqrt{12}. $$

Using $\pi<4$, $\sqrt2<3/2$, $\sqrt{12}<7/2$, and $\sqrt{\delta}<1/20$, the right side is less than

This completes the proof of the proposition.
