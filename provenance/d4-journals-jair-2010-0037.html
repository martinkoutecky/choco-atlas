The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(A\), rational masses \(\mu\), per-agent weights \(w_a\), rational task types \(t_j=(T_j,u_j)\), and a finite-support outcome \(\mathcal O\) assigning each type a complete contribution/payoff signature across named old streams, decide whether a measurable deviating submass \(q_a\le\mu_a\), possibly partitioned across deviation patterns, can form or modify overlapping task streams with feasible resource and threshold constraints such that nondeviators preserve their contributions and each deviating unit keeps an old stream's payoff exactly when its contribution there is unchanged, while every deviating unit receives strictly higher payoff.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(a\in A\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_a\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Realize type \(a\) as an atomless interval of length \(\mu_a\), with resource weight \(w_a>0\) per unit of mass; masses need not sum to one.
There are \(m\ge1\) task types with binary-rational thresholds and utilities satisfying \(0<T_1<\cdots<T_m\) and \(0\le u_1<\cdots<u_m\). Arbitrarily many copies of each task type are available.
A coalition structure is a finite list of streams with measurable contribution fractions \(r_\ell(z)\ge0\), satisfying \(\sum_\ell r_\ell(z)\le1\) almost everywhere. Stream \(\ell\) has resource \(R_\ell=\int w(z)r_\ell(z)\,dz\) and value \(V(R_\ell)=\max(\{0\}\cup\{u_j:T_j\le R_\ell\})\); each stream earns this fixed value, without fractional task completion.
An outcome assigns integrable real payoff densities \(x_\ell(z)\), with \(x_\ell(z)=0\) where \(r_\ell(z)=0\), \(\int x_\ell(z)\,dz=V(R_\ell)\), and nonnegative total payoff almost everywhere. Transfers occur only within streams; individual rationality uses the zero value attainable by an atomless singleton.
The input is a feasible outcome \(\mathcal O\) with \(k\) named old streams, specified by binary-rational contribution and payoff densities constant on each type. Types therefore include their complete old contribution/payoff signatures; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A deviation chooses a measurable set \(D\) of positive mass, with type submasses \(0\le q_a\le\mu_a\), and another feasible finite-stream outcome. Members of a type may follow different measurable deviation patterns.
The old and new streams involving positive nondeviator contribution must admit a bijection preserving every nondeviator's contribution almost everywhere; all remaining new streams involve deviators only. Deviators may decrease or increase contributions, subject to their resource budgets.
For each matched pair, every deviator retains exactly its old payoff density if the entire stream contribution profile is unchanged almost everywhere; otherwise every deviator receives zero from that matched stream. Payoffs on streams involving only deviators may be redistributed freely. Nondeviator payoffs need only satisfy outcome feasibility.
A deviation is profitable when each deviating unit receives strictly higher total payoff almost everywhere on \(D\).

Is deciding whether such a profitable deviation exists solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents, refined-core membership is strongly coNP-complete, equivalently profitable-deviation existence is strongly NP-complete ([verified result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3856)).
No result settling this exact atomless, support-encoded decision problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Realized each positive type mass as an atomless interval; allowed arbitrary positive total mass rather than imposing probability normalization.
  • Interpreted contributions as fractions of per-unit resource weights and payoffs as densities integrated against mass; specified rational input signatures and measurable deviations.
  • Required positive thresholds to exclude unlimited positive utility from zero-resource task copies; retained positive weights, nonnegative utilities, and the monotone task list.
  • Kept task copies and coalition structures finite and streams individually thresholded, with fixed utility rather than utility proportional to stream mass.
  • Repaired the graded question's individual retention condition: the discrete refined rule retains all deviators' old stream payoffs only when the entire matched contribution profile is unchanged.
  • Allowed the favorable bijection between streams involving nondeviators, rather than forcing named streams to retain their original correspondence.
  • Allowed contribution increases as well as withdrawals, and unrestricted reorganization and payoff redistribution on streams involving only deviators.
  • Required positive deviating mass and interpreted contribution equality and strict individual improvement almost everywhere.
  • Allowed signed stream payoff densities as in the formal allocation definition, while requiring nonnegative total payoff; atomless singleton individual rationality has value zero.
  • Preserved nondeviator contributions, but did not impose preservation of their payoffs, which the discrete refined deviation definition does not require.

Result

Profitable-deviation existence is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

I use the standard bit-model interpretation that the resource weights \(w_a\) are rational input numbers. Without an encoding for these weights, a bit-complexity question is not defined.

The key fact is that failure of one particular deviation test forces every positive old stream to operate at the same maximal utility per unit of resource. After that, testing deviations that release at most two old streams suffices.

1. A resource-selection calculation

Write \(r_{\ell a}\) and \(x_{\ell a}\) for the old contribution and payoff densities. Define

\(p_a=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}x_{\ell a}\),
\(W=\sum_a\mu_aw_a\),
\(R_\ell=\sum_a\mu_aw_ar_{\ell a}\), and
\(P=\sum_a\mu_ap_a=\sum_\ell V(R_\ell)\).

Feasibility gives \(p_a\geq0\).

For nonnegative resource and cost densities \(c_a,y_a\), with \(y_a=0\) whenever \(c_a=0\), define

\(C_{c,y}(t)=\min\left\{\sum_a y_aq_a:\ 0\leq q_a\leq\mu_a,\ \sum_a c_aq_a=t\right\}\).

Set this minimum to infinity when \(t>\sum_a\mu_ac_a\).

This calculation is polynomial-time solvable. For each type with \(c_a>0\), its available resource is \(\mu_ac_a\), at cost \(y_a/c_a\) per resource unit. Sort these ratios, take resource from types in that order, and use a fraction of the final type.

To justify optimality, suppose an allocation uses resource from a more expensive type while a cheaper type still has unused capacity. Moving an equal amount of resource from the former to the latter preserves feasibility and cannot increase cost. Repeating this exchange gives the stated allocation.

We will use

\(C_{\mathrm{all}}(t)=C_{w,p}(t)\).

For a set \(S\) of old streams, we will also use

\(c_a^S=w_a\sum_{\ell\in S}r_{\ell a}\),
\(y_a^S=\sum_{\ell\in S}x_{\ell a}\), and
\(C_S(t)=C_{c^S,y^S}(t)\),

after establishing that the old stream payoff densities are nonnegative.

2. Two sufficient deviation tests

Abandoning all old participation

If \(C_{\mathrm{all}}(T_j)<u_j\), a profitable deviation exists.

Take a minimizing vector \(q\), and select measurable subintervals of these masses as the deviating set \(D\). Every deviator withdraws from every old stream and contributes fully to one new stream. Its resource is exactly \(T_j\), so its value is \(u_j\).

Let \(M_D=\sum_aq_a>0\). Give each deviating unit of type \(a\) the new payoff density

\(p_a+\varepsilon\), where
\(\varepsilon=\left(u_j-\sum_ap_aq_a\right)/M_D>0\).

These payoffs integrate to \(u_j\), and every deviator strictly improves.

For each old stream with positive nondeviator contribution, preserve those contributions and give its entire resulting value to its nondeviating contributors, using nonnegative densities. Remove old streams without nondeviator contribution. This respects the required matching. If an old profile happens to remain unchanged, its old contribution on \(D\) was zero, and therefore its old payoff on \(D\) was also zero. The retention rule is consequently satisfied.

Releasing a specified set of streams

Suppose all old stream payoff densities are nonnegative. If \(C_S(T_j)<u_j\), a profitable deviation exists.

Choose \(D\) from a minimizing vector \(q\), omitting types with \(c_a^S=0\). On \(D\), withdraw from streams in \(S\), preserve contributions and payoffs on all other old streams, and contribute

\(\sum_{\ell\in S}r_{\ell a}\)

to one new deviator-only stream. This fraction respects every unit’s budget. The new stream has resource \(T_j\).

Give each deviator its lost payoff density \(y_a^S\), plus the positive uniform increment

\(\varepsilon=\left(u_j-\sum_ay_a^Sq_a\right)/M_D\).

Together with its retained payoffs, its new total payoff is \(p_a+\varepsilon\).

Nondeviator feasibility requires checking both kinds of old streams:

  • On an unchanged stream \(\ell\), the value remaining after retained deviator payments is \(V(R_\ell)-\int_Dx_\ell\geq0\), because the old densities are nonnegative. Allocate this remainder nonnegatively to its nondeviating contributors.
  • On a stream from \(S\), deviators receive zero and its resulting value is allocated nonnegatively to nondeviators. If it has no nondeviating contributors, remove it.

If an unchanged stream has no nondeviating contributors, retain it as a deviator-only stream with its original payoffs. Thus every required stream and payoff constraint is satisfied.

These constructions use at most \(k+1\) new streams.

3. Failure of the first test forces resource efficiency

Consider the task types with \(T_j\leq W\), and let

\(\delta=\max_{j:T_j\leq W}u_j/T_j\).

If no attainable task has positive utility, every feasible outcome has total value zero. Its nonnegative total payoff density must therefore be zero almost everywhere, so no profitable deviation exists.

Assume henceforth that \(\delta>0\). Choose an attaining task with smallest threshold, denoted \(t_*\). Its utility is \(\delta t_*\).

Every old stream satisfies \(V(R_\ell)\leq\delta R_\ell\). Consequently,

\(P\leq\delta\sum_\ell R_\ell\leq\delta W\).

The uniformly selected submasses \(q_a=(t_*/W)\mu_a\) are feasible in the definition of \(C_{\mathrm{all}}(t_*)\). Hence

\(C_{\mathrm{all}}(t_*)\leq(t_*/W)P\leq\delta t_*\).

If the first inequality test detects \(C_{\mathrm{all}}(t_*)<\delta t_*\), Section 2 supplies a profitable deviation.

Otherwise all these bounds are equalities. In particular,

\(P=\delta W\),
\(\sum_\ell R_\ell=W\), and
\(V(R_\ell)=\delta R_\ell\) for every old stream.

Since all contribution fractions are nonnegative and their sum is at most one, equality of the total resource implies

\(\sum_\ell r_{\ell a}=1\)

for every type \(a\).

Discard zero-resource streams; they have zero contributions and hence zero payoff densities. Let \(\kappa\) be the number of remaining streams.

Every remaining stream has positive value and resource at least \(t_*\). Indeed, if its value is \(u_j\), then

\(u_j=\delta R_\ell\), \(T_j\leq R_\ell\), and \(u_j/T_j\leq\delta\).

These relations force \(R_\ell=T_j\) and \(u_j/T_j=\delta\). The definition of \(t_*\) gives \(R_\ell\geq t_*\).

4. Negative old stream payoffs yield immediate deviations

Under the conclusions of Section 3, suppose \(x_{\ell a}<0\).

Choose a sufficiently small positive submass \(q<\mu_a\) of type \(a\). These deviators withdraw only from stream \(\ell\), retaining their contributions and old payoffs on every other stream. Their new total payoff density is

\(p_a-x_{\ell a}>p_a\).

Every remaining old stream still has nondeviating contributors: the deviating set is a proper submass of one type.

For an unchanged stream \(h\), choose \(q\) small enough that \(q x_{ha}\leq V(R_h)\) whenever \(x_{ha}>0\). This is possible because there are finitely many streams and each has positive value. Its value remaining after retained deviator payments is then nonnegative. Allocate that remainder nonnegatively to nondeviators. On the changed stream, allocate its resulting value nonnegatively to nondeviators.

The deviators’ total payoffs are nonnegative and strictly improved. Thus any negative old stream payoff density is a polynomial-time recognizable certificate of a profitable deviation.

We may therefore assume below that \(x_{\ell a}\geq0\) for every remaining stream and type.

5. Any deviation using a given released set implies a single-task test

Consider an arbitrary profitable deviation, allowing different measurable patterns within each type.

Let \(U\) contain the old streams matched to new streams whose entire contribution profiles are unchanged. Let \(S\) be the complementary set of positive old streams. In particular, old streams having no nondeviating contributors belong to \(S\).

Write \(q_a\) for the deviating submasses and define

\(H=\sum_ac_a^Sq_a\), and
\(Y=\sum_ay_a^Sq_a\).

Every deviator retains exactly its old payoff from \(U\), and receives zero from changed matched streams. Let \(B\) be the total value of the new deviator-only streams. Since all their payoffs go to deviators, integrating strict improvement gives

\(B>Y\).

Here strict improvement almost everywhere on a set of positive mass implies a strictly positive integral: an integrable function positive almost everywhere on such a set cannot have integral zero.

Original resource budgets are saturated by Section 3. Preserving contributions on \(U\) leaves at most resource \(H\) for the deviator-only streams; contributions to changed matched streams can only reduce that amount.

List the positive-valued deviator-only streams, and let \(j_\nu\) be the task type giving stream \(\nu\) its value. Then

\(B=\sum_\nu u_{j_\nu}\), and
\(\sum_\nu T_{j_\nu}\leq H\).

In particular, \(H>0\). There must be some \(\nu\) satisfying

\(u_{j_\nu}>(T_{j_\nu}/H)Y\).

Otherwise summing the reverse inequalities would give \(B\leq Y\).

For this task, the vector

\(q'_a=(T_{j_\nu}/H)q_a\)

satisfies \(0\leq q'_a\leq\mu_a\), has resource exactly \(T_{j_\nu}\) under \(c^S\), and costs \((T_{j_\nu}/H)Y\). Therefore,

\(C_S(T_{j_\nu})<u_{j_\nu}\).

Thus any profitable deviation with released set \(S\) implies one of the single-task tests for that same set. This argument uses only the type submasses, so it covers arbitrary measurable within-type deviation patterns.

6. Why sets of at most two old streams suffice

For each positive old stream define its imbalance vector by

\(d_{\ell a}=x_{\ell a}-\delta w_ar_{\ell a}\).

A pair with nonzero imbalance is profitable

Take two streams \(i,h\), and put \(S=\{i,h\}\). Their combined resource is

\(R_S=R_i+R_h\geq2t_*>t_*\),

and their combined payoff is \(\delta R_S\).

Suppose \(d_i+d_h\neq0\). Equivalently, \(y^S\neq\delta c^S\). Since their total payoff equals \(\delta\) times their total resource, there are types \(a,b\) with positive \(c_a^S,c_b^S\) such that

\(y_a^S/c_a^S<\delta<y_b^S/c_b^S\).

Start with \(q_a=\theta\mu_a\), where \(\theta=t_*/R_S\in(0,1)\). This selects resource \(t_*\) at cost \(\delta t_*\).

Move resource amount \(\eta\) from type \(b\) to type \(a\), replacing their submasses by

\(q_a+\eta/c_a^S\), and
\(q_b-\eta/c_b^S\).

Choose

\(0<\eta<\min\{(1-\theta)\mu_ac_a^S,\theta\mu_bc_b^S\}\).

The new submasses remain feasible, resource is unchanged, and cost decreases by the positive amount

\(\eta(y_b^S/c_b^S-y_a^S/c_a^S)\).

Consequently,

\(C_S(t_*)<\delta t_*\),

which is a profitable-deviation test from Section 2.

Three or more streams

Suppose \(\kappa\geq3\) and every pair test fails. The preceding argument gives

\(d_i+d_h=0\)

for every pair of distinct streams.

For any three distinct streams, these equations imply all three imbalance vectors are zero: the first two pair equations give \(d_h=d_j=-d_i\), and the third gives \(-2d_i=0\). Pairing with these streams then gives \(d_\ell=0\) for every remaining stream.

Thus

\(x_{\ell a}=\delta w_ar_{\ell a}\)

for every stream and type.

For any arbitrary released set \(S\), this implies \(Y=\delta H\). Every new stream has value at most \(\delta\) times its resource, so the notation of Section 5 gives

\(B\leq\delta H=Y\),

contradicting the strict improvement requirement \(B>Y\). No profitable deviation exists.

One or two streams

If \(\kappa\leq2\), every possible nonempty released set already has size at most two. Section 5 therefore proves directly that failure of all its single-task tests excludes every profitable deviation.

The empty released set cannot yield a deviation: unchanged old contributions exhaust the deviators’ budgets, leaving no resource for deviator-only streams and no change in their payoffs.

7. Algorithm and complexity

The complete algorithm is:

  • Compute \(W\) and the maximal attainable density \(\delta\). If no positive-utility task is attainable, return no.
  • Choose \(t_*\) as above. If \(C_{\mathrm{all}}(t_*)<\delta t_*\), return yes.
  • Discard zero-resource old streams. If any remaining \(x_{\ell a}<0\), return yes.
  • For every nonempty set \(S\) of at most two remaining streams and every task \(j\), test \(C_S(T_j)<u_j\). If any test succeeds, return yes.
  • Return no.

Sections 2 and 4 prove soundness of every affirmative answer. Sections 3, 5, and 6 prove completeness of every negative answer.

There are \(O(k^2)\) stream sets. For each, sort at most \(\tau\) resource-price ratios once; then evaluate the cost function at the already ordered \(m\) thresholds using cumulative capacities and costs. This requires \(O(k^2(\tau\log\tau+m+\tau))\) rational arithmetic operations, in addition to reading and preparing the input. All intermediate numbers have polynomial bit length, so the bit complexity is polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

The supplied finite-agent hardness result therefore does not extend to this atomless model. The decisive operation is selecting a common fractional submass of every type, followed by small resource-preserving exchanges between types.

Both constructions are valid.

Abandoning all old participation. A minimizing selection at positive threshold has positive mass. Its selected old payoff is strictly below the new stream’s value, so the stated uniform increment is positive and the new payments integrate to exactly that value.

Every retained old stream has positive nondeviator contribution and can allocate its resulting nonnegative value among those contributors. Old streams without nondeviator contribution may be removed.

  • All available resource is used.

Zero-resource streams have zero contributions and consequently zero payoff densities. Every remaining stream has positive value. The inequalities involving its attained task force its resource to equal that task’s threshold and its utility-to-threshold ratio to equal \(\delta\). Its resource is therefore at least \(t_*\).

No unjustified equality or domain restriction occurs here.

4. Negative old stream payoffs

For each unchanged stream, the stated smallness condition ensures that retained deviator payments do not exceed its positive value. Negative retained payments cause no obstruction, since only total payoff must be nonnegative.

5. Reduction of an arbitrary deviation to a single-task test

The extraction of a task with

is correct. Here the already established nonnegativity of old stream payoff densities supplies \(Y\geq0\), which is needed when using the bound on the sum of thresholds.

The scaling factor lies in \((0,1]\), so the scaled submasses satisfy their capacity bounds. Their resource and cost are exactly those stated. The resulting strict single-task test follows.

6. Sufficiency of sets containing at most two streams
