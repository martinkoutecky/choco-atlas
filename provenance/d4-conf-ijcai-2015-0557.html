The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support distribution μ over single-minded buyer types \(t=(k_t,w_t)\), with rational masses \(μ_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational supply \(β\) of units per unit mass, choose a uniform item price \(p\ge 0\) and allocation masses \(x_{t,q}\ge 0\) to maximize revenue \(p\sum_{t,q}q x_{t,q}\). Require Σ_q x_{t,q}=μ_t\), Σ_{t,q}q x_{t,q}\le\beta\), and every quantity \(q\) assigned positive mass to maximize \(v_t(q)-pq\) over integral \(q\ge 0\), where \(v_t(q)=0\) for \(q<k_t\) and \(v_t(q)=w_t\) for \(q\ge k_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge 1\) distinct buyer types \(t=(k_t,w_t)\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each \(k_t\) is a positive binary integer and each \(w_t\) is a nonnegative binary rational. Let \(m=\max_t k_t\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Supply \(\beta\ge 0\) and a revenue threshold \(R\ge 0\) are binary rationals. Quantities are integral: \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\). Define \(v_t(q)=0\) for \(q<k_t\) and \(v_t(q)=w_t\) for \(q\ge k_t\).
An outcome consists of a uniform real price \(p\ge 0\) and nonnegative real allocation masses \(x_{t,q}\), satisfying \(\sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,q}q x_{t,q}\le\beta\).
Envy-freeness requires \(x_{t,q}>0\Rightarrow q\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}}(v_t(a)-pa)\). The seller may split a type's mass among tied maximizers. Zero allocations are permitted, buyers have no budgets, and supply may remain unsold.
Revenue is \(p\sum_{t,q}q x_{t,q}\). The decision problem asks whether an envy-free outcome has revenue at least \(R\).

Is this continuous revenue-threshold problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(\tau\), \(\log m\), and \(L\), with polynomial time measured in the binary input length.

For finitely many indivisible buyers, revenue-maximizing envy-free uniform item pricing with single-minded valuations and free disposal is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and admits an FPTAS under binary quantity encoding. Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). In fact, its maximum revenue is attained and can be computed exactly in

\(O\!\left(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2\right)\)

bit operations using elementary integer arithmetic. The dependence on the quantities is through their binary encodings, including \(\log m\); there is no enumeration of quantities up to \(m\).

The essential feature is the permission to split a type’s mass among tied maximizers.

1. Demand at a positive price

Define the value per required unit and the full demand mass of type \(t\) by

\(r_t=w_t/k_t,\qquad d_t=k_t\mu_t.\)

Fix \(p>0\). For \(0<a<k_t\), utility is \(-pa<0\), whereas quantity zero gives utility zero. For \(a\ge k_t\), utility is \(w_t-pa\), which strictly decreases with \(a\). Therefore the only possible maximizers are zero and \(k_t\), and

\(\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}}(v_t(a)-pa)= \begin{cases} \{k_t\},&r_t>p,\\ \{0,k_t\},&r_t=p,\\ \{0\},&r_t<p. \end{cases}\)

Consequently, define

\(A(p)=\sum_{t:r_t>p}d_t,\qquad B(p)=\sum_{t:r_t=p}d_t.\)

Every envy-free allocation at price \(p\) must allocate \(k_t\) to the entire mass of types with \(r_t>p\). Types with \(r_t=p\) may split their mass between zero and \(k_t\). Thus \(A(p)\) is mandatory demand, and \(B(p)\) is optional demand.

2. Exact optimization at a fixed positive price

Claim. A positive price \(p\) is feasible exactly when \(A(p)\le\beta\). When feasible, its maximum revenue is

\(F(p)=p\min\{\beta,A(p)+B(p)\}.\)

Proof. Necessity follows because mandatory demand alone consumes \(A(p)\) units.

For sufficiency and the revenue formula, set

\(Q=\min\{\beta,A(p)+B(p)\},\qquad \delta=Q-A(p).\)

The assumed inequality \(A(p)\le\beta\) implies \(0\le\delta\le B(p)\).

List the tied types as \(t_1,\ldots,t_s\). Starting with residual \(\delta_0=\delta\), assign buying mass

\(y_i=\min\{\mu_{t_i},\delta_{i-1}/k_{t_i}\},\qquad \delta_i=\delta_{i-1}-k_{t_i}y_i.\)

Inductively,

\(\delta_i=\max\{0,\delta-\sum_{j=1}^{i}k_{t_j}\mu_{t_j}\}.\)

Since \(\delta\le B(p)\), we have \(\delta_s=0\). Allocate \(k_{t_i}\) to mass \(y_i\) and zero to its remaining mass. Allocate \(k_t\) to all strictly demanding types and zero to all other types.

Every allocated quantity maximizes the corresponding type’s utility, and total consumption is exactly \(Q\le\beta\). This proves feasibility and achieves revenue \(pQ\).

Conversely, no envy-free allocation can consume more than either \(\beta\) or \(A(p)+B(p)\). Since \(p>0\), the constructed allocation maximizes revenue. \(\square\)

This construction splits at most one type strictly between its two maximizers.

3. Only the positive value-per-unit prices need examination

Let

\(\mathcal C=\{r_t:r_t>0\}.\)

Claim. Every feasible outcome with positive revenue is weakly dominated in revenue by a feasible outcome whose price belongs to \(\mathcal C\).

Proof. Positive revenue requires a positive price and positive consumption.

If the outcome’s price \(p\) belongs to \(\mathcal C\), the fixed-price result already gives its optimal revenue.

Otherwise no type is tied at \(p\), so the outcome’s consumption is necessarily

\(D=\sum_{t:r_t>p}d_t>0.\)

In particular, the set of types with \(r_t>p\) is nonempty. Let

\(r=\min\{r_t:r_t>p\}.\)

By this definition,

\(\{t:r_t>p\}=\{t:r_t\ge r\}.\)

At price \(r\), allocate \(k_t\) to all types in this set and zero to all other types. Types with \(r_t>r\) strictly demand \(k_t\); types with \(r_t=r\) are indifferent and may receive \(k_t\). Hence this allocation is envy-free. Its consumption remains \(D\le\beta\), and its revenue is \(rD>pD\). \(\square\)

Zero revenue is always attainable: take \(p=1+\max_t r_t\) and allocate zero to every type. This also handles instances with no positive values or with zero supply; feasibility at price zero is unnecessary.

It follows that the exact global maximum is

\(V^*=\max\!\left(\{0\}\cup \left\{ r\min\!\left(\beta,\sum_{t:r_t\ge r}d_t\right): r\in\mathcal C,\ \sum_{t:r_t>r}d_t\le\beta \right\}\right).\)

Every nonzero candidate included in this expression is attainable by Section 2, and Section 3 bounds every positive-revenue outcome by an included candidate. Thus this is a maximum, not merely a supremum.

The answer to the decision problem is “yes” exactly when \(V^*\ge R\).

4. Algorithm and binary running time

Sort the types with positive \(r_t\) into groups of equal ratios, in decreasing order:

\(r_1>r_2>\cdots>r_s>0,\qquad s\le\tau.\)

Let the demand mass of group \(i\) be

\(D_i=\sum_{t:r_t=r_i}k_t\mu_t,\)

and maintain the demand from preceding groups,

\(S_i=\sum_{j<i}D_j.\)

At price \(r_i\), mandatory demand is \(S_i\), and total available demand is \(S_i+D_i\). Therefore, whenever \(S_i\le\beta\), evaluate

\(r_i\min\{\beta,S_i+D_i\}.\)

Take the maximum of these values and zero. Sorting takes \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) exact comparisons, and grouping and scanning take \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations.

To verify that these operations have polynomial bit cost, write the input rationals as

\(\mu_t=a_t/b_t,\quad w_t=c_t/e_t,\quad \beta=u/v,\quad R=z/h,\)

with positive denominators. Use the common denominator

\(H=v\prod_t b_t.\)

Its bit length is \(O(L)\). The scaled demand of type \(t\) and scaled supply are the integers

\(C_t=k_ta_t(H/b_t),\qquad U=u(H/v).\)

The divisions are exact. All these integers have \(O(L)\) bits. In particular, normalization gives

\(\sum_t C_t=H\sum_t k_t\mu_t\le Hm,\)

so every cumulative demand has \(O(L+\log m)=O(L)\) bits.

Ratio comparisons use cross multiplication of

\(r_t=c_t/(e_tk_t).\)

For a feasible group, let \(T\) be its scaled mandatory demand and \(G\) its scaled group demand. Set

\(Q'=\min\{U,T+G\}.\)

Using any representative type \(t\) of that group, its candidate revenue is

\(\frac{c_tQ'}{e_tk_tH}.\)

Comparison with the threshold is the exact integer comparison

\(c_tQ'h\ge z e_tk_tH.\)

All operands and intermediate products have \(O(L)\) bits. Common-denominator construction, scaling, sorting, and scanning together require \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) integer operations. Elementary multiplication and division on these operands cost \(O(L^2)\) bit operations each, giving the stated bound

\(O\!\left(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2\right).\)

Equivalently, an explicit bound retaining the quantity parameter is \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)(L+\log m)^2)\). Since \(\log m\le L\), this is polynomial in the binary input length.

The allocation construction also produces a rational optimal witness of polynomial size, with at most one partially served type.

5. Why the indivisible-buyer hardness does not transfer

For indivisible buyers, tied buyers must be selected as whole bundles; the cited paper identifies the resulting knapsack step and proves hardness in its Theorem 2. [Monaco, Sankowski, and Zhang, Section 3.2](https://ricerca.univaq.it/retrieve/e2188bfa-63a3-f28d-e053-d805fe0ac2bb/envyfree-944-revised.pdf)

Here, a tied type contributes any real quantity in the interval \([0,k_t\mu_t]\). Section 2 proves that these contributions fill every quantity between mandatory and total demand, eliminating that discrete selection step.

For example, take one type with \(k=2\), \(w=2\), mass \(1\), and supply \(1\). At price \(1\), allocating two units to half the mass and zero to the other half yields revenue \(1\). The corresponding single indivisible buyer cannot receive its two-unit bundle under supply \(1\) and yields no positive feasible revenue.
