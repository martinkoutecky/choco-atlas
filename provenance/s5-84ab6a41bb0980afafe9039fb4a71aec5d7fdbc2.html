The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supports of ordered time-window classes \(f\) and value-budget classes \(e\), with masses \(\nu_f\) and \(\rho_e\) matched according to the secretary model so that \(\mu_{f,e}=\nu_f\rho_e\), and supply of \(q\) units per person, construct a randomized online mechanism for finite populations of size \(N\) with \(qN\) indivisible units. Each realized bundle must be integral, reports of \((a,d,v,b)\) must be individually truthful in every finite implementation, allocations must respect true time windows and supply, and payments must respect budgets. Require expected revenue relative to the paper's offline heterogeneous-price benchmark to approach a \(32\)-approximation as the large-market parameter \(\epsilon\) tends to zero, while the mechanisms admit a limiting mass-policy interpretation.

Result and interpretation

The guarantee is achievable under expected individual rationality, the usual notion for risk-neutral agents buying lotteries. One can take
\[ \]
\(r(\epsilon)=\min\{\epsilon^{1/4},1/32\}\).

The construction below has integral allocations and budget-feasible payments in every realization. Its truthful allocation and payment measures converge along all admissible population sizes to a causal mass policy.

I interpret the window and budget requirements on truthful play, and individual rationality as nonnegative expected utility in each finite implementation. If individual rationality instead means nonnegative utility in every realization, the guarantee is impossible; a counterexample appears at the end.

The published asymptotic factor \(32\) already includes comparison with heterogeneous-price revenue. It should not be multiplied by another factor of two. See the conclusion of the revenue analysis in [Eden–Feldman–Vardi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.03625). The proof below establishes the requested guarantee directly.

1. Benchmark comparison

Fix a finite population and write \(R=\mathrm{OPT}_N\), \(W=\mathrm{OPT}^{*}_N\), and \(b_{\max}=\max_i b_i\). Its economic multiset is deterministic; matching economic types to windows does not change either benchmark.

We first prove
\(W\le 2R\).

Take any feasible fractional allocation with liquid welfare
\(w=\sum_i\min\{v_i x_i,b_i\}>0\), and set \(p=w/(2m)\). Agents with \(v_i<p\) contribute at most
\(p\sum_i x_i\le w/2\).
Consequently, agents with \(v_i\ge p\) have total budgets at least \(w/2\). Therefore
\[ \]
\(\min\{mp,\sum_{i:v_i\ge p}b_i\}\ge w/2\).
Maximizing over allocations proves the claim.

Also, \(R\le \sum_i b_i\le Nb_{\max}\). Thus, for \(\epsilon=b_{\max}/R\),
\[ \]
\(\epsilon\ge 1/N\).

Finally, removing any one agent gives a uniform-price optimum \(R'\) satisfying
\[ \]
\(R'\ge R-b_{\max}=(1-\epsilon)R\).
Indeed, evaluating the original optimal price after removing that agent reduces its revenue by at most her budget.

2. A finite mechanism

The mechanism uses only \(N,m\) and arriving reports. Reports may be arbitrary admissible rational types.

2.1 Pricing and rounding

Set
\[ \]
\(\eta_N=1/\lceil N^{1/4}\rceil,\qquad h(p)=p/(1+p)\).

For a set \(S\) of reports, define
\[ \]
\(B_S(p)=\sum_{i\in S:\widehat v_i\ge p}\widehat b_i,\qquad F_S(p)=\min\{mp/2,B_S(p)\}\).

Let \(P_N(S)\) maximize
\[ \]
\(F_S(p)(1-\eta_N h(p))\)
over the distinct reported values in \(S\), breaking ties toward the smallest price. For an empty set, use price \(1\).

This price satisfies
\[ \]
\(F_S(P_N(S))\ge(1-\eta_N)\max_{p>0}F_S(p)\).
To check the maximization domain, between consecutive reported values the eligible budget is constant and \(F_S(p)\) is nondecreasing. Thus its unperturbed maximum occurs at a reported value.

Assign each agent independent hidden random variables:

  • an arrival priority for breaking simultaneous-report ties;
  • a fair label \(C_i\in\{1,2\}\);
  • a serial priority \(U_i\), uniform on \((0,1)\);
  • a rounding variable \(V_i\), uniform on \((0,1)\).

For a price \(p\), define
\[ \]
\(d_i(p)=\mathbf 1_{\{\widehat v_i\ge p\}}\min\{\widehat b_i/p,m\}\),
and
\[ \]
\(X_i(p)=\lfloor d_i(p)\rfloor+\mathbf 1_{\{V_i<d_i(p)-\lfloor d_i(p)\rfloor\}}\).

The same hidden \(U_i,V_i\) are used whenever agent \(i\) appears in a virtual calculation. For every integer \(K\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\),
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\min\{X_i(p),K\}]=\min\{d_i(p),K\}\).

This identity follows immediately by considering whether \(K\le\lfloor d_i(p)\rfloor\) or \(K\ge\lceil d_i(p)\rceil\).

2.2 Sampling cutoff and four virtual markets

Independently draw
\(J=1+\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\).

Let \(A\) contain the first \(J\) reports, let \(j\) be its last report, and let \(T\) be that report’s physical arrival time. Let \(B\) contain the remaining reports. Partition both sets using their labels:
\(A_c=\{i\in A:C_i=c\}\) and \(B_c=\{i\in B:C_i=c\}\).

There are four virtual markets, each with integer supply \(m\): two sampling markets and two performance markets.

Sampling market \(c\). Use price \(p=P_N(A_{3-c})\). For \(i\in A_c\), let
\[ \]
\(K_i=\bigl(m-\sum_{\ell\in A_c:U_\ell<U_i}X_\ell(p)\bigr)^+\).

Its virtual allocation and payment are
\[ \]
\(x_i=\min\{X_i(p),K_i\},\qquad \pi_i=p\min\{d_i(p),K_i\}\).

Deliver and charge at time \(T\) only if \(\widehat d_i\ge T\). Departed agents remain in the virtual serial calculation, although they receive and pay nothing.

Performance market \(c\). Put \(G_c=A_c\setminus\{j\}\), and fix
\(p_c=P_N(G_{3-c})\).
Its remaining virtual stock starts at \(m\). When \(i\in B_c\) reports, compute the phantom residual
\[ \]
\(K_i^{\mathrm{ph}}=\bigl(m-\sum_{\ell\in G_c:U_\ell<U_i}X_\ell(p_c)\bigr)^+\).

If its current stock is \(K_c\), its virtual allocation and payment are
\[ \]
\(x_i=\min\{X_i(p_c),K_i^{\mathrm{ph}},K_c\},\qquad \pi_i=p_c\min\{d_i(p_c),K_i^{\mathrm{ph}},K_c\}\).
Decrease \(K_c\) by \(x_i\).

At the sampling cutoff, choose one of the four virtual markets uniformly and independently. Implement only that market. No mechanism outcome is revealed before the cutoff. The other markets’ calculations are virtual.

Thus physical supply is always at most \(m\), including when \(m=1\).

3. Finite feasibility, individual rationality, and incentives

3.1 Feasibility and expected individual rationality

All realized bundles are integral. Each virtual market uses at most \(m\) units, so the selected market is supply-feasible.

Every truthful payment satisfies
\[ \]
\(0\le\pi_i\le p\,d_i(p)\le b_i\).

On truthful play, sampling allocations occur at \(T\in[a_i,d_i]\) whenever delivered, and performance allocations occur at \(a_i\).

For an eligible truthful agent, condition on her price, serial priority, and all randomness determining her residual integer stock \(K\), excluding her own rounding variable. Her expected utility from a transaction within her window is
\[ \]
\((v_i-p)\min\{d_i(p),K\}\ge0\).

Agents with \(v_i<p\), and sampling agents who have departed, receive and pay nothing. This proves expected individual rationality for every finite population.

3.2 Economic and departure reports

Fix an agent’s report time. Her own market’s price and residual stock before processing her are independent of her value and budget report.

At a fixed price, reporting eligibility when \(v_i<p\) gives nonpositive expected utility. When \(v_i\ge p\), expected utility is nondecreasing in the budget-feasible offered quantity.

Understating a budget can only reduce that quantity. If overstating it creates positive probability of a payment exceeding the true budget, expected utility is \(-\infty\). Otherwise it cannot improve the offered quantity: for a residual stock \(K\), if \(b'>b_i\) and
\(p\min\{b'/p,K\}\le b_i\), then
\[ \]
\(\min\{b'/p,K\}=\min\{b_i/p,K\}\).
The corresponding integral allocation is identical as well, because such clipping occurs at the integer \(K\).

Departure reports affect only whether a sampling transaction is executed. Suppressing an in-window transaction removes nonnegative conditional expected utility. Executing a transaction outside the true window gives zero value and a nonnegative payment, hence nonpositive expected utility. Performance transactions occur upon reporting.

Consequently, truthful economic and departure reports are optimal at any fixed legal report time.

3.3 Delaying arrival

Couple alternative report times using the same hidden arrival priority, label, serial priority, and rounding variable. Since reporting before true arrival is prohibited, delaying cannot improve the agent’s arrival rank.

There are three cases.

The agent is in \(B\) under both reports. The sampling sets, prices, and phantom offers are unchanged. Reporting later encounters weakly less remaining performance stock. Conditional expected utility at truthful economic reports is therefore weakly smaller.

The agent is in \(A\) under both reports. The sampling set is unchanged. Its prices and serial calculation are unchanged, while its transaction time can only move later. Delay cannot create an in-window transaction that truthful reporting would have missed.

The agent moves from \(A\) to \(B\). Let \(S\) be the first \(J-1\) other reports. Under truthful reporting, the sampling set is \(S\cup\{i\}\). Under delayed reporting, it is \(S\cup\{k\}\), where \(k\) is the new cutoff agent. Removing \(k\) leaves exactly \(S\).

Conditional on label \(c\), the truthful sampling price and the delayed performance price are therefore the same:
\(P_N(S_{3-c})\).
The truthful sampling residual and delayed phantom residual are also identical, using the shared serial priorities and rounding variables. Performance adds only an additional stock cap.

The truthful sampling market and the corresponding delayed performance market each have implementation probability \(1/4\). Their expected-utility comparison follows from the rounding identity and the additional cap. If the truthful sampling time is already after true departure, a delayed performance transaction cannot occur within the true window either.

The implementation lottery is drawn only at the cutoff. An agent cannot first learn its outcome and then choose sampling membership. After the cutoff, choosing a later performance report cannot improve stock or the fixed phantom offer. Thus observing the implementation outcome does not improve this comparison.

These cases prove dominant-strategy truthfulness in expectation. The economic-report argument also covers joint deviations in time, value, budget, and departure.

4. Two allocation lemmas

The revenue proof uses only performance transactions, which always occur within truthful windows.

4.1 Integer greedy allocation loses at most a factor of two

Fix a set \(S\), price \(p\), and integer supply \(m\). Let \(d_i=\mathbf 1_{\{v_i\ge p\}}\min\{b_i/p,m\}\), and independently round these demands to \(X_i\) as above.

A serial greedy allocation delivers
\[ \]
\(\min\{\sum_{i\in S}X_i,m\}\)
units, regardless of serial order.

For \(m\ge2\), set \(t=1-1/m\). For an integer \(s\ge0\),
\[ \]
\(\min\{s,m\}\ge m(1-t^s)\).
For \(s\le m\), this is Bernoulli’s inequality; for \(s>m\), the right-hand side is at most \(m\).

Writing \(d_i=\lfloor d_i\rfloor+f_i\),
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[t^{X_i}]=t^{\lfloor d_i\rfloor}(1-f_i/m)\le e^{-d_i/m}\).

Independence now gives, with \(D=\sum_i d_i\),
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\min\{\sum_iX_i,m\}]\ge m(1-e^{-D/m})\ge(1-e^{-1})\min\{D,m\}\).

The last inequality follows from concavity on \(D/m\in[0,1]\) and monotonicity thereafter. For \(m=1\), the same bound follows from
\(\Pr(\sum_iX_i=0)=\prod_i(1-d_i)\le e^{-D}\).

Since
\(\min\{D,m\}=\min\{B_S(p)/p,m\}\), we obtain
\[ \]
\(p\,\mathbb E[\text{greedy units}]\ge\tfrac12\min\{mp,B_S(p)\}\).

4.2 The phantom performance market retains half the greedy allocation

Suppose a fixed set \(S\) is independently split into a phantom set \(G\) and a performance set \(H\), with probability \(1/2\) for each membership. Fix a price independent of this split.

Couple the phantom mechanism with greedy allocation on all of \(S\), using the same \(U_i,X_i\). For \(i\in H\), removing other performance agents from the prefix can only increase her residual stock. Her planned phantom allocation is therefore at least her greedy allocation on \(S\), pathwise.

The total performance allocation is the minimum of \(m\) and the sum of its planned allocations. It consequently dominates the total greedy allocation assigned to \(H\).

The greedy allocation itself is independent of the membership split. Hence the expected greedy allocation assigned to \(H\) is half the full greedy allocation.

Finally, expected performance revenue equals \(p\) times expected performance allocation: each agent’s rounding variable is independent of her phantom residual and preceding performance stock. Combining this identity with the preceding lemma gives
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\text{performance revenue}\mid S,p]\ge\tfrac14\min\{mp,B_S(p)\}\).

5. A uniform finite revenue bound

Condition on the identity of the cutoff economic copy \(j\), and remove it. Write \(R'\) for the remaining population’s uniform-price optimum.

The remaining copies are independently assigned to
\(G_1,G_2,B_1,B_2\), each with probability \(1/4\). Indeed, conditional on the cutoff identity, \(J-1\) still has distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\), and the preceding economic copies form a uniform subset of that size. Independent fair labels give the four-way partition.

Put
\(\epsilon'=b_{\max}/R'\).
For \(\epsilon<1\), Section 1 gives
\[ \]
\(R'\ge(1-\epsilon)R,\qquad \epsilon'\le\epsilon/(1-\epsilon)\).

5.1 Uniform concentration over prices

Order the remaining copies by decreasing value. Truncate their budget sequence when its cumulative sum reaches \(4R'\), allowing the last retained budget to be partial. Call the resulting weights \(c_i\). Then
\[ \]
\(0\le c_i\le b_{\max},\qquad \sum_i c_i\le4R'\).

For any cohort whose independent membership probability is \(z\in\{1/4,1/2\}\), the centered weighted prefix sums have independent increments and total variance at most
\[ \]
\(z(1-z)\sum_i c_i^2\le b_{\max}R'\).

Kolmogorov’s maximal inequality therefore bounds the probability of any prefix deviation exceeding \(\delta R'\) by \(\epsilon'/\delta^2\). Its hypotheses hold because the membership indicators are independent, centered after subtracting \(z\), and square-integrable.

A price-eligible set is a prefix in this order. Thus, simultaneously for every price:

  • if its full eligible budget is at most \(4R'\), its cohort budget differs from \(z\) times that budget by at most \(\delta R'\);
  • if its full eligible budget exceeds \(4R'\), its cohort budget is at least \(4zR'-\delta R'\).

For performance market \(1\), apply this to its price sample \(G_2\) and its union \(S=G_1\cup B_1\). Both conclusions hold on an event \(E\) with
\[ \]
\(\Pr(E)\ge1-2\epsilon'/\delta^2\).

Importantly, \(E\) depends on \(G_2\) and \(S\), but not on the fair split of \(S\) into \(G_1,B_1\).

5.2 Revenue at the selected price

At an optimal full-population price \(p^*\), we have
\(mp^*\ge R'\) and \(B(p^*)\ge R'\).
On \(E\), its sample objective therefore satisfies
\[ \]
\(F_{G_2}(p^*)\ge(1/4-\delta)R'\).

Let \(p=P_N(G_2)\) and
\(h=(1-\eta_N)(1/4-\delta)\).
The pricing guarantee gives
\[ \]
\(mp\ge2hR',\qquad B_{G_2}(p)\ge hR'\).

If \(B(p)\le4R'\), concentration implies
\(B(p)\ge4(h-\delta)R'\), followed by
\[ \]
\(B_S(p)\ge B(p)/2-\delta R'\ge(2h-3\delta)R'\).

If \(B(p)>4R'\), concentration gives the stronger bound
\(B_S(p)\ge(2-\delta)R'\).
Consequently, whenever \(2h-3\delta\) is positive,
\[ \]
\(\min\{mp,B_S(p)\}\ge(2h-3\delta)R'\).

Conditional on the price sample and union \(S\), their internal phantom/performance split remains fair. Section 4.2 yields
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\mathrm{Rev}^{\mathrm{virtual}}_{B_1}] \ge\tfrac14(2h-3\delta)R'(1-2\epsilon'/\delta^2)\).

The same bound holds for market \(2\). Each performance market is physically selected with probability \(1/4\), and sampling revenue is nonnegative. Therefore
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\mathrm{Rev}_N]\ge\tfrac18(2h-3\delta)R'(1-2\epsilon'/\delta^2)\).

Using \(W\le2R\) and
\(2h-3\delta\ge(1-\eta_N-10\delta)/2\), we obtain
\[ \]
\(\frac{\mathbb E[\mathrm{Rev}_N]}{W} \ge\frac1{32}(1-\eta_N-10\delta)(1-\epsilon) \bigl(1-\frac{2\epsilon}{(1-\epsilon)\delta^2}\bigr)\),
when the displayed factors are nonnegative.

5.3 An explicit error function

If \(\epsilon^{1/4}\ge1/32\), the asserted guarantee with
\(r(\epsilon)=1/32\) follows from nonnegative revenue.

Otherwise, set \(\delta=\epsilon^{1/4}<1/32\). Since \(\epsilon\ge1/N\),
\(\eta_N\le\delta\).
Also,
\[ \]
\(\frac{2\epsilon}{(1-\epsilon)\delta^2}\le4\delta^2\).

All factors above are positive. Using
\((1-a)(1-b)(1-c)\ge1-a-b-c\) for \(a,b,c\in[0,1]\), their total deficit from \(1\) is at most
\(11\delta+\delta^4+4\delta^2<32\delta\).
Hence
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[\mathrm{Rev}_N]\ge(1/32-\delta)\mathrm{OPT}^{*}_N\).

This proves the requested uniform bound with
\(r(\epsilon)=\min\{\epsilon^{1/4},1/32\}\).
The mechanism does not need to know \(\epsilon\).

6. The limiting mass policy

Fix the support and \(q\), and let \(N\) run through all admissible sizes. This section concerns truthful play.

Write
\[ \]
\(B_\rho(p)=\sum_{e:v_e\ge p}\rho_e b_e,\qquad F(p)=\min\{qp/2,B_\rho(p)/4\}\).

Let \(p_0\) be the smallest supported value maximizing \(F\). This maximum is positive. Define
\[ \]
\(D=\frac{B_\rho(p_0)}{4p_0},\qquad \alpha=\min\{1,q/D\},\qquad \theta_e=\mathbf1_{\{v_e\ge p_0\}}\alpha b_e/p_0\).

6.1 The sampling mass and cutoff time

Let the distinct arrival times be
\(t_1<\cdots<t_k\), and let
\(C_\ell=\sum_{f:a_f\le t_\ell}\nu_f\).

If
\(C_{\ell-1}<1/2<C_\ell\), define
\[ \]
\(\lambda=\frac{1/2-C_{\ell-1}}{C_\ell-C_{\ell-1}}\).
Set \(T=t_\ell\), and let \(w_f\) equal \(\nu_f\) before that batch, \(\lambda\nu_f\) within that batch, and \(0\) afterwards.

If \(C_\ell=1/2\), set \(w_f=\nu_f\) for \(a_f\le t_\ell\) and \(0\) otherwise. Independently choose
\(T=t_\ell\) or \(T=t_{\ell+1}\), each with probability \(1/2\).

In both cases,
\[ \]
\(0\le w_f\le\nu_f,\qquad \sum_f w_f=1/2\).

Independently choose sampling or performance implementation, each with probability \(1/2\).

6.2 Allocation and payment measures

Let \(\delta_t\) denote unit point mass at time \(t\).

For sampling implementation, define the type allocation measure
\[ \]
\(\mathcal A_{f,e} =\frac{\rho_e w_f\theta_e}{2}\mathbf1_{\{d_f\ge T\}}\delta_T\).

For performance implementation, define
\[ \]
\(\mathcal A_{f,e} =\frac{\rho_e(\nu_f-w_f)\theta_e}{2}\delta_{a_f}\).

In either case, set
\(\mathcal P_{f,e}=p_0\mathcal A_{f,e}\).

These measures are feasible. Sampling allocations have \(a_f\le T\le d_f\); performance allocations occur at \(a_f\). Total allocation is at most
\[ \]
\(\alpha D\le q\).
Each type’s total payment is at most
\[ \]
\(\rho_e\nu_f b_e/2\le\mu_{f,e}b_e\).

7. Convergence along all admissible sizes

We prove convergence to the policy just displayed, rather than merely existence of a convergent subsequence.

7.1 Cohort proportions and cutoff

Random matching gives convergence in probability of empirical joint-type proportions to \(\nu_f\rho_e\). The relevant counts are hypergeometric, with variance bounded by a constant times \(N\).

Since \(J/N\to1/2\), the empirical sampling-window proportions converge to \(w_f\). Inside a simultaneous-arrival batch, independent uniform arrival priorities select the limiting fraction uniformly across its window and economic types. Independent labels split these proportions equally between the two sampling markets and between the two performance markets.

If the median lies strictly inside a batch, \(T\) is its arrival time with probability tending to one.

If exactly half the window copies arrive by \(t_\ell\), every admissible \(N\) is even. Symmetry of \(\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\) gives
\[ \]
\(\Pr(J\le N/2)=1/2\).
Concentration implies that the cutoff lies in either batch \(t_\ell\) or batch \(t_{\ell+1}\), with probability tending to one. Thus its limiting law is exactly the stated fair two-point law.

Removing or adding the single cutoff agent changes normalized quantities by a vanishing amount.

7.2 Price convergence

For every supported candidate price, each sampling market’s eligible budget divided by \(N\) differs from \(B_\rho(p)/4\) by \(O_{\mathbb P}(N^{-1/2})\). There are finitely many candidates. Each supported economic type appears in each sample with probability tending to one.

Therefore all normalized sampling objectives converge to \(F\), with uniform error \(O_{\mathbb P}(N^{-1/2})\).

The perturbation has order \(N^{-1/4}\), which dominates this error. Nonmaximizers of \(F\) are eventually rejected with probability tending to one. Among distinct maximizers, the strict increase of \(h\) makes the smallest price win: their normalized score difference is a positive constant times \(\eta_N\), while the stochastic error is \(o_{\mathbb P}(\eta_N)\).

Consequently, all sampling and performance prices equal \(p_0\) with probability tending to one.

7.3 Serial allocations and payments

Condition on this price event. The unrounded demands \(b_e/p_0\) and rounded demands are bounded constants because the support is fixed.

In each phantom sample, the cumulative rounded demand before serial priority \(u\), divided by \(N\), converges uniformly in \(u\in[0,1]\) to \(Du\).

Here uniform convergence follows by proving convergence on a finite grid using bounded variance, then using monotonicity between grid points and refining the grid.

Thus:

8. Causality of the limit

The displayed policy has an implementation using only available reports.

Process arrival batches and retain the first half of reported mass. If a batch crosses mass \(1/2\), retain the necessary uniform fraction of that batch and close sampling immediately.

If a batch ends at exactly mass \(1/2\), an independent fair coin chooses whether to close immediately or at the next arriving batch. Waiting requires no knowledge of that batch’s time or contents.

Split retained mass equally between the two labels. At sampling closure, compute the smallest maximizing price from the stored economic report measure using objective
\[ \]
\(\min\{qp/2,\text{stored eligible budget mass}\}\).
That stored mass equals \(B_\rho(p)/4\) in the truthful limit. Compute the serial acceptance fraction \(\alpha\) from the stored phantom demand.

Sampling implementation then uses stored window reports and the current time to omit expired agents. Performance implementation applies the resulting fixed offer to subsequent arriving report mass.

This procedure yields exactly the measures in Section 6. Every decision through time \(t\) depends only on reports available by \(t\) and independent mechanism randomness. No future report is needed.

9. Construction and implementation time

The finite mechanism does not use the support in setting prices or allocations.

  • Initialization: \(O(N)\) coin tosses for the cutoff, plus polynomial work in \(\log N+\log m\) for the perturbation parameter. Random variables for individual agents are generated lazily.
  • Finite implementation: \(O(N\log N)\) rational arithmetic operations and priority comparisons suffice. Sort each sample by value for pricing and by serial priority for phantom prefix sums; each performance offer then uses a binary search and a stock update.
  • Bit complexity: if each report has length at most \(L\), a conservative expected bound, using ordinary integer arithmetic, is

\[ \]
\(O\!\left(N\log^2(N+2)\bigl(NL+\log(m+N+2)\bigr)^2+\tau L+m\right)\).
The \(m\) term applies when delivered units are explicitly enumerated; compact integral bundle counts omit it. Exact uniform priorities can be compared using lazy random bit strings, and rational rounding probabilities can be sampled exactly.

  • Limiting-policy construction from the supplied society: aggregation and sorting require \(O(\tau\log(\tau+2))\) rational operations, with polynomial bit complexity in \(\tau,L\). Only listed positive-mass types are processed.

Thus both construction and finite implementation are polynomial in the stated parameters; no enumeration of possible unsupported types is required.

10. If individual rationality is required in every realization

Under that stronger interpretation, the uniform guarantee is impossible, even without online or mass-limit constraints.

For each integer \(n\ge2\), take a one-type society with window \([0,0]\), value \(n\), budget \(1\), and \(q=1/n\). Consider its admissible population size \(N=n\), so \(m=1\).

Then
\[ \]
\(\mathrm{OPT}^{*}_N=\mathrm{OPT}_N=n,\qquad \epsilon_N=1/n\).
The heterogeneous benchmark allocates \(1/n\) unit to each agent.

Ex-post individual rationality and nonnegative payments force every agent receiving zero units to pay zero. At most one agent receives a unit, and her payment is at most her budget \(1\). Thus every realization has revenue at most \(1\).

The proposed lower bound would approach \(n/32\), contradicting this upper bound. This is why the expected-utility IR convention is essential.
