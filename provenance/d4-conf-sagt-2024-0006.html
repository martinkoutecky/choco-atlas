The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support of types \(t=(q_t,y_t)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and \(q_t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(1/2,1)\), where \(b_t=q_t\) for \(y_t=A\) and \(b_t=1-q_t\) for \(y_t=B\), compute a Kelly competitive-equilibrium price \(p\) and each type’s per-capita investments \(x_t^A,x_t^B\). Require type-wise best responses under unit per-capita endowment and market clearing, \(\frac{1}{p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^A=\frac{1}{1-p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^B\); determine whether binarizing \(p\) agrees with majority using weights \(2q_t-1\) and masses \(\mu_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list of \(\tau\) types \(t=(q_t,y_t)\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), \(q_t\in\mathbb Q\cap(1/2,1)\), and \(y_t\in\{A,B\}\). Set \(b_t=q_t\) when \(y_t=A\), and \(b_t=1-q_t\) otherwise. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(m=2\) is the number of alternatives and \(L\) is the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

At price \(p\in(0,1)\), securities for \(A\) and \(B\) cost \(p\) and \(1-p\), respectively, and pay one unit precisely in their corresponding state. Each type has unit per-capita endowment and chooses investments \(x_t^A,x_t^B\geq0\) with \(x_t^A+x_t^B\leq1\) and \(x_t^Ax_t^B=0\), retaining cash \(c_t=1-x_t^A-x_t^B\).

A type's best response maximizes \(b_t\ln(c_t+x_t^A/p)+(1-b_t)\ln(c_t+x_t^B/(1-p))\) over all these choices, including switching securities and abstaining; use \(\ln0=-\infty\). A Kelly competitive equilibrium consists of type-wise best responses at a fixed price satisfying \(p^{-1}\sum_t\mu_tx_t^A=(1-p)^{-1}\sum_t\mu_tx_t^B\). Zero aggregate investment is permitted when these conditions hold.

Binarization selects \(A\) for \(p>1/2\), \(B\) for \(p<1/2\), and \(\{A,B\}\) at equality. Mass-weighted majority compares \(\sum_{t:y_t=A}\mu_t(2q_t-1)\) with \(\sum_{t:y_t=B}\mu_t(2q_t-1)\), returning the same set-valued tie convention.

Can an exact equilibrium price and per-capita investment profile always be computed in polynomial time in \(\tau,L\), or is this computation \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? Does binarizing every equilibrium price always agree with mass-weighted majority?

For finite societies with equal endowments, Kelly equilibrium-price binarization equals majority with weights proportional to \(2q_i-1\). [Condorcet Markets](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05028)
Finite Kelly markets with arbitrary normalized trader wealth have a wealth-weighted mean-belief equilibrium price; this result concerns finite traders rather than a distribution of unit-endowment types. [Learning Performance of Prediction Markets with Kelly Bettors](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.6655)

Result

An exact equilibrium price and investment profile can always be computed in polynomial time. Every equilibrium-price binarization agrees with mass-weighted majority, including ties.

In fact, the equilibrium price and profile are unique. They are

\(\displaystyle p^\star=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t b_t,\qquad x_t^{A\star}=\frac{\max\{b_t-p^\star,0\}}{1-p^\star},\qquad x_t^{B\star}=\frac{\max\{p^\star-b_t,0\}}{p^\star}.\)

A conservative running-time bound is \(\displaystyle O(m\tau L^3)\) bit operations, where \(m=2\). The proof below works directly with the support and does not expand masses into individual traders.

1. Global best responses

Fix a type with belief \(b\in(0,1)\) and a price \(p\in(0,1)\). The constraint that at most one security is purchased means every feasible investment belongs to one of two branches.

Investing \(x\in[0,1)\) in security \(A\) gives utility

\(\displaystyle U_A(x)=b\ln\!\left(1+\frac{1-p}{p}x\right)+(1-b)\ln(1-x).\)

Investing \(x\in[0,1)\) in security \(B\) gives utility

\(\displaystyle U_B(x)=b\ln(1-x)+(1-b)\ln\!\left(1+\frac{p}{1-p}x\right).\)

Both branches give utility zero at \(x=0\). At \(x=1\), both give utility \(-\infty\), because both states have strictly positive subjective probability.

Differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle U_A'(x)=\frac{b-p-(1-p)x}{(p+(1-p)x)(1-x)},\qquad U_B'(x)=\frac{p-b-px}{(1-p+px)(1-x)}.\)

The denominators are strictly positive for \(x\in[0,1)\). We therefore obtain the following exhaustive cases.

  • If \(b>p\): the derivative of \(U_A\) is positive before \(x=(b-p)/(1-p)\) and negative afterward. This point lies strictly between zero and one and is the unique maximizer on the \(A\) branch. Its utility is positive: \(U_A'(0)>0\), so some sufficiently small positive investment improves on abstention. Meanwhile, \(U_B'(x)<0\) everywhere, so the unique maximizer on the \(B\) branch is abstention. Thus the \(A\) investment is the unique global best response.
  • If \(b<p\): the same derivative argument shows that the unique global best response is investment \((p-b)/p\) in security \(B\).
  • If \(b=p\): both derivatives are strictly negative for every \(x>0\). The unique global best response is abstention.

Consequently, at every fixed price, the unique best response is

\(\displaystyle x^A(b,p)=\frac{\max\{b-p,0\}}{1-p},\qquad x^B(b,p)=\frac{\max\{p-b,0\}}p.\)

These investments are feasible: at most one is positive, and any positive investment is strictly less than one. This argument checks switching securities as well as abstention; the purchased security need not match the type’s signal.

2. Existence and uniqueness of equilibrium

For the best response just derived, the difference between the quantities of the two securities purchased by a type is

\(\displaystyle \frac{x^A(b,p)}p-\frac{x^B(b,p)}{1-p}=\frac{b-p}{p(1-p)}.\)

This identity holds in all three cases, including \(b=p\). Thus aggregate excess demand is

\(\displaystyle \frac1p\sum_t\mu_t x_t^A(p)-\frac1{1-p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^B(p)=\frac{\sum_t\mu_t b_t-p}{p(1-p)},\)

where we used \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

Since \(p(1-p)>0\), market clearing holds if and only if

\(\displaystyle p=\sum_t\mu_t b_t.\)

Each belief lies strictly between zero and one. Their average under positive masses summing to one therefore also lies strictly between zero and one. Hence \(p^\star\) is an admissible price, and its best-response profile clears the market.

Conversely, every equilibrium must use the unique best responses at its price, so the same identity forces its price to equal \(p^\star\). The equilibrium profile is consequently unique as well.

Zero-investment case. Aggregate investment is zero precisely when all beliefs equal \(p^\star\). Indeed, positivity of every mass makes zero aggregate investment equivalent to every type investing zero; the best-response formulas then imply \(b_t=p^\star\) for every type. Conversely, these equalities imply abstention by every type. Thus the permitted zero-investment equilibrium is fully covered.

3. Exact computation and bit complexity

Write the input masses and derived beliefs as

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=\frac{a_t}{d_t},\qquad b_t=\frac{h_t}{e_t},\)

with positive integer denominators. Computing \(b_t\) from \(q_t\) requires either retaining its numerator or subtracting that numerator from its denominator.

Define integers

\(\displaystyle D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}d_te_t,\qquad N=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_th_t\,\frac{D}{d_te_t}.\)

Every indicated division is exact, and \(p^\star=N/D\).

The sum of the bit lengths of these input denominators is \(O(L)\), so \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Moreover, \(0<N<D\), because \(N/D=p^\star\in(0,1)\). Each summand and every partial sum is nonnegative and at most \(N\), so these also have \(O(L)\) bits.

For each type, compute

\(\displaystyle R_t=h_tD-e_tN.\)

The sign of \(R_t\) determines the investment:

  • If \(R_t>0\), output \(\displaystyle x_t^{A\star}=\frac{R_t}{e_t(D-N)}\) and \(x_t^{B\star}=0\).
  • If \(R_t<0\), output \(x_t^{A\star}=0\) and \(\displaystyle x_t^{B\star}=\frac{-R_t}{e_tN}\).
  • If \(R_t=0\), output both investments as zero.

Cash is then \(c_t^\star=1-x_t^{A\star}-x_t^{B\star}\). All output numerators and denominators have \(O(L)\) bits.

There are \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations on integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Schoolbook arithmetic therefore produces exact, possibly unreduced fractions in \(O(m\tau L^2)\) bit operations. If reduced fractions are required, ordinary Euclidean reduction gives the conservative overall bound

\(\displaystyle O(m\tau L^3),\qquad m=2.\)

Thus the computation is polynomial in the support size and total binary input length.

4. Agreement with mass-weighted majority

Let the majority margin be

\(\displaystyle M=\sum_{t:y_t=A}\mu_t(2q_t-1)-\sum_{t:y_t=B}\mu_t(2q_t-1).\)

By the definition of \(b_t\), we have \(2b_t-1=2q_t-1\) for an \(A\)-signal type and \(2b_t-1=-(2q_t-1)\) for a \(B\)-signal type. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle 2p^\star-1=\sum_t\mu_t(2b_t-1)=M.\)

It follows that:

  • \(M>0\) exactly when \(p^\star>1/2\), so both rules select \(A\).
  • \(M<0\) exactly when \(p^\star<1/2\), so both rules select \(B\).
  • \(M=0\) exactly when \(p^\star=1/2\), so both rules return \(\{A,B\}\).

Since the equilibrium price is unique, this proves agreement for every equilibrium price.

5. Relation to the cited results

The finite equal-endowment counterparts are Lemmas 4–5 and Theorem 4 of [Condorcet Markets](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05028). The finite wealth-weighted mean-belief result appears in [Learning Performance of Prediction Markets with Kelly Bettors](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.6655).

The proof above independently establishes the result for the stated distribution of unit-endowment types, including zero aggregate investment, and supplies a polynomial-time algorithm for its compressed rational representation.
