The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set (\Theta), rational masses (\mu_i) summing to \(1\), nonnegative rational contact intensities (a_{ij}), and a rational intervention budget (\beta), choose rational intervention masses (x_i\in[0,\mu_i]) with (\sum_i x_i\le\beta). Set (y_j^{(0)}=x_j) and (y_j^{(1)}=y_j^{(0)}+(\mu_j-y_j^{(0)})(1-\exp(-\sum_i a_{ij}y_i^{(0)}))). For rational (\varepsilon>0), compute a feasible allocation whose total influenced mass (\sum_j y_j^{(1)}) is within (\varepsilon) of the maximum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support \(\Theta=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\): the list of types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Directed contact intensities \(a_{ij}\ge0\) are binary rationals; within-type contacts \(a_{ii}\) are allowed. Let \(m\) count positive contact intensities and \(L\) be the total input bit length, including the rational budget \(0<\beta\le1\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\).
All mass is initially uninfluenced. One intervention chooses rational masses \(0\le x_i\le\mu_i\), with \(\sum_i x_i\le\beta\), which become influenced with certainty.
After exactly one propagation step, \(y_j^{(0)}=x_j\) and \(y_j^{(1)}=x_j+(\mu_j-x_j)(1-\exp(-\sum_i a_{ij}x_i))\). Define \(F(x)=\sum_j y_j^{(1)}\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max\{F(z):z\in\mathbb R^\tau,\ 0\le z_i\le\mu_i,\ \sum_i z_i\le\beta\}\).
The computational problem is to return a feasible rational \(x\) satisfying \(F(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon\).

Is this additive-\(\varepsilon\) optimization problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Finite-agent dynamic influence maximization is NP-hard, already with one intervention, one propagation step, known edges, and propagation probabilities equal to \(1\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.00165)
Continuous discount allocation with probabilistic seed selection is also NP-hard in general, but uses a different objective. [Source](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~jpei/publications/CIM%20SIGMOD16.pdf) No result settling the exact mass objective above was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the budget to a positive fraction at most one, corresponding to a positive intervention size no greater than the population.
  • Defined the benchmark maximum over real feasible allocations while retaining the requested rational output.
  • Used binary encoding for all rational inputs, including epsilon, and included epsilon's encoding in L; polynomial time therefore means polynomial in input bit length.
  • Defined m as the number of positive directed contact intensities, since the graded question supplied no separate size parameter m.
  • Allowed diagonal contact intensities as contacts between distinct individuals of the same type; no zero-diagonal restriction was supplied.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The additive-\(\varepsilon\) optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

This holds even when all masses are equal dyadic rationals, contacts are symmetric, every type has exactly three positive outgoing contacts, all positive intensities equal \(1/16\), and there are no within-type contacts. The reduction uses \(\varepsilon=1/(64\tau^2)\).

1. Source problem and binary-rational masses

We reduce from Independent Set on simple cubic graphs: given a graph \(G\) in which every vertex has degree three and an integer \(k\), decide whether its independence number satisfies \(\alpha(G)\ge k\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; the reduction in the proof of [Mohar’s Theorem 4.1(a)](https://www.sfu.ca/~mohar/Papers/ApexGenus.pdf) establishes hardness even for planar cubic graphs.

We may assume \(1\le k\le n\), where \(n=|V(G)|\), because other values are trivial. Since \(3n=2|E(G)|\), the integer \(n\) is even.

To obtain equal masses that are dyadic rationals, let \(N\) be the least power of two satisfying \(N\ge 2n\). Form a graph \(H\) from two disjoint copies of \(G\) and \(r=(N-2n)/4\) disjoint copies of the complete graph on four vertices. The number \(r\) is a nonnegative integer because \(2n\) and \(N\) are divisible by four.

The graph \(H\) is simple and cubic, has \(N<4n\) vertices, and satisfies

\( \displaystyle \alpha(H)=2\alpha(G)+r. \)

Set \(K=2k+r\). Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \alpha(H)\ge K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha(G)\ge k. \)

Also \(1\le K\le N\). Thus this padding preserves the decision problem and increases its size only linearly.

2. A rounding lemma that controls fractional allocations

For any simple graph \(H\), define

\( \displaystyle Q(u)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(H)}u_i u_j. \)

Lemma. If \(u\in[0,1]^N\) and \(\sum_i u_i=K\), where \(K\) is an integer, there exists \(v\in\{0,1\}^N\) satisfying \(\sum_i v_i=K\) and \(Q(v)\le Q(u)\).

Proof. Suppose two coordinates \(u_p,u_q\) are strictly between zero and one. Change them to \(u_p+t,u_q-t\), keeping all other coordinates fixed. The feasible interval is

\( \displaystyle -\min\{u_p,1-u_q\}\le t\le\min\{1-u_p,u_q\}. \)

At either endpoint, at least one of these coordinates becomes zero or one. As a function of \(t\), the objective has the form

\( \displaystyle Q(u+t e_p-t e_q)=Q(u)+ct-\mathbf{1}_{\{\{p,q\}\in E(H)\}}t^2 \)

for some real coefficient \(c\). Indeed, edges incident to only one of \(p,q\) contribute linear terms, while their mutual edge, if present, contributes \((u_p+t)(u_q-t)\).

This function is concave, so at least one endpoint has objective value no greater than its value at zero. Move to that endpoint. The sum remains \(K\), the objective does not increase, and the number of fractional coordinates decreases.

Repeating this operation leaves at most one fractional coordinate. Exactly one is impossible because the coordinate sum is an integer. The resulting vector proves the lemma. \(\square\)

In particular, if \(\alpha(H)<K\), every integral vector selecting \(K\) vertices contains an edge. Therefore the lemma implies

\( \displaystyle Q(u)\ge 1\qquad\text{for every }u\in[0,1]^N\text{ with }\sum_i u_i=K. \)

Thus fractional allocation cannot eliminate the required graph penalty.

3. Constructing the society

Write \(b=1/N\) and \(a=1/16\). Create one type for each vertex of \(H\), with

\( \displaystyle \tau=N,\qquad \mu_i=b,\qquad \beta=Kb,\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{b^2}{64}. \)

For distinct vertices, set \(a_{ij}=a\) when \(\{i,j\}\in E(H)\), and set all other contact intensities to zero.

All masses are positive and sum to one. All input numbers are dyadic rationals because \(N\) is a power of two. Moreover, \(0<\beta\le1\), and there are exactly \(m=3N\) positive directed intensities.

For a feasible allocation \(x\), write \(u_i=x_i/b\), let \(t=\sum_i x_i\), and define

\( \displaystyle s_j=a\sum_{i:\{i,j\}\in E(H)}x_i. \)

Since every vertex has three neighbors and \(0\le x_i\le b\),

\( \displaystyle 0\le s_j\le 3ab. \)

The objective is exactly

\( \displaystyle F(x)=t+\sum_j(b-x_j)(1-e^{-s_j}). \)

4. A uniform bound for the exact exponential objective

For every \(s\ge0\),

\( \displaystyle 0\le s-(1-e^{-s})\le\frac{s^2}{2}. \)

To verify this, observe that

\( \displaystyle s-(1-e^{-s})=\int_0^s(1-e^{-z})\,dz, \)

and \(0\le1-e^{-z}\le z\) for \(z\ge0\).

Define

\( \displaystyle P(x)=t+\sum_j(b-x_j)s_j. \)

Applying the preceding inequality, using \(b-x_j\ge0\) and \(\sum_j(b-x_j)\le1\), gives

\( \displaystyle 0\le P(x)-F(x)\le\frac12\sum_j(b-x_j)s_j^2\le\frac92a^2b^2=\frac{9b^2}{512}. \)

We now compute \(P\) exactly. Cubic regularity gives \(\sum_j s_j=3at\). Symmetry gives

\( \displaystyle \sum_j x_j s_j=2a\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(H)}x_i x_j=2ab^2Q(u). \)

Hence

\( \displaystyle P(x)=(1+3ab)t-2ab^2Q(u). \)

For allocations using the full budget, let \(T=(1+3ab)\beta\). Since \(2a=1/8\), the resulting uniform bounds are

\( \displaystyle T-\frac{b^2}{8}Q(u)-\frac{9b^2}{512}\le F(x)\le T-\frac{b^2}{8}Q(u),\qquad \sum_i u_i=K. \)

These inequalities bound the given exponential objective itself.

5. Using an approximate solution to decide Independent Set

Assume an algorithm returns a feasible rational \(x\) satisfying \(F(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon\).

First extend its allocation, if necessary, to a rational vector \(\widehat{x}\) using the full budget. This can be done greedily within the coordinate capacities because their sum is one and \(\beta\le1\).

This extension cannot decrease \(F\). In fact, writing \(s_j=\sum_i a_{ij}x_i\), differentiation yields

\( \displaystyle \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_\ell}=e^{-s_\ell}+\sum_j(\mu_j-x_j)a_{\ell j}e^{-s_j}>0. \)

Every summand in the sum is nonnegative, and the first term is positive.

Set \(\widehat{u}=\widehat{x}/b\).

Yes case. Suppose \(\alpha(H)\ge K\). Choose an independent set of exactly \(K\) vertices and allocate mass \(b\) to each selected vertex. Its normalized vector has \(Q=0\). Section 4 therefore gives

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\ge T-\frac{9b^2}{512}. \)

Because \(\varepsilon=b^2/64=8b^2/512\),

\( \displaystyle F(\widehat{x})\ge T-\frac{17b^2}{512}. \)

Combining this with the upper bound from Section 4 gives

\( \displaystyle T-\frac{b^2}{8}Q(\widehat{u})\ge T-\frac{17b^2}{512}, \)

so

\( \displaystyle Q(\widehat{u})\le\frac{17}{64}<1. \)

No case. Suppose \(\alpha(H)<K\). The rounding lemma gives

\( \displaystyle Q(\widehat{u})\ge1 \)

for every feasible allocation using the full budget, regardless of its approximation quality.

Thus one call to the presumed optimization algorithm decides the source instance: compute \(Q(\widehat{u})\) exactly and answer yes precisely when \(Q(\widehat{u})<1\). This requires only rational arithmetic; no numerical evaluation of exponentials is needed.

6. Complexity conclusion

The construction has \(N<4n\), \(m=3N\), and polynomial input length. The tolerance \(\varepsilon=1/(64N^2)\) requires only logarithmically many bits.

If the optimization algorithm ran in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), its rational output would have polynomial length. Extending that output to the full budget and computing the rational quadratic \(Q\) would also take polynomial time. We would therefore obtain a polynomial-time algorithm for an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem.

This proves the claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the exact mass objective. Because \(1/\varepsilon=64N^2\) in the reduction, even a running-time guarantee polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
