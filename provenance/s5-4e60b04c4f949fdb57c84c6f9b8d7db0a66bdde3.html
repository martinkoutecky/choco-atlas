Theorem 4 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many rooted component types \(H\), each a hypergraph or multi-hypergraph of girth at least \(3\) with multiplicity at most \(2\), additive valuations, and rational masses \(\lambda_H\) of independent copies, compute a finite-support distribution \(x_{H,\sigma}\) over complete integral allocations \(\sigma\) of each component, with \(\sum_{\sigma}x_{H,\sigma}=\lambda_H\), such that every positive-mass pair of bundles satisfies \(v_a(B)\geq \frac{2}{3}v_a(B'\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in B'\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) rooted component types \(H=(V_H,E_H,r_H)\), each with positive mass \(\lambda_H\) encoded as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; masses need not sum to \(1\).
Each component has a nonempty finite agent set, indivisible goods represented by nonempty hyperedges, and edge multiplicity at most \(2\). After duplicate edges are suppressed, distinct edges intersect in at most one vertex; this is the girth-at-least-\(3\) restriction.
Valuations are additive, with nonnegative binary rational singleton values, and are zero for goods whose edges do not contain the agent. The root is merely a designated vertex; fairness applies to every agent.
Copies have distinct agents and goods. Agents value goods outside their own copy at zero, and allocations assign every good to any agent within its copy, including agents outside its edge.
Compute an explicitly listed finite-support family of nonnegative rational masses \(x_{H,\sigma}\) over complete integral allocations \(\sigma=(B_a^\sigma)_{a\in V_H}\), with \(\sum_\sigma x_{H,\sigma}=\lambda_H\).
Require, whenever \(x_{H,\sigma}>0\), that \(v_a(B_a^\sigma)\geq\frac{2}{3}v_a(B_b^\sigma\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(a,b\in V_H\) and every \(g\in B_b^\sigma\), including goods of zero value to \(a\). Comparisons between bundles in distinct copies use the stipulated zero valuations.
Let \(m=\sum_H|E_H|\), \(n=\sum_H|V_H|\), and \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Can this \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX guarantee always be attained by computing such a distribution in time polynomial in \(m,n,\tau,L\), or is attaining this guarantee NP-hard?
Discrete instances under these restrictions admit complete \(\frac{2}{3}\)-EFX allocations computable in pseudo-polynomial time. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26948v1#S4.SS2)
No result settling polynomial-time computation for this exact mass formulation was identified in the literature search.

Partial resolution: polynomial time for a fixed number of value levels

I do not settle the unrestricted polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question. I prove a further tractable case: for every fixed positive integer $K$, the requested distribution can be computed in polynomial time if each agent has at most $K$ distinct positive singleton values. These values may be arbitrary binary rationals, with arbitrarily large ratios.

The main step is an exact compression lemma: replace the values by polynomially bounded integers while preserving every required fairness inequality.

1. The mass formulation reduces exactly to discrete allocation

For a supported component $H$, let $\mathcal F_H$ be the set of its complete integral allocations satisfying
$$ 3v_a(B_a^\sigma)\geq 2v_a(B_b^\sigma\setminus\{g\}) $$
for every agent pair $a,b$ and every $g\in B_b^\sigma$.

Proposition 1. A valid mass family exists if and only if $\mathcal F_H$ is nonempty for every supported component. Whenever one exists, one allocation per component suffices.

Proof. In a valid family, positivity of $\lambda_H$ and
$$ \sum_\sigma x_{H,\sigma}=\lambda_H $$
imply that some listed allocation has positive mass. The pointwise fairness requirement places that allocation in $\mathcal F_H$.

Conversely, choose $\sigma_H\in\mathcal F_H$ and assign it mass $\lambda_H$. This satisfies both the mass equation and fairness.

An agent values every bundle in a different copy at zero. Thus comparisons across copies impose only an inequality with right-hand side zero, which follows from nonnegativity. The roots impose no further condition. $\square$

Consequently, polynomial-time computation of the mass family is equivalent to polynomial-time computation of one fair allocation for one permitted component. In one direction, solve the components separately and copy their input masses. In the other, use a society with one component and mass $1$, then extract any positive-mass allocation from the explicitly listed output.

For a component with at most as many goods as agents, assigning its goods to distinct agents gives an exact EFX allocation: every compared bundle becomes empty after its sole good is removed. The results below also address components with more goods than agents.

2. External algorithmic input

I use the supplied discrete theorem: the permitted additive instances admit complete $\frac23$-EFX allocations computable in pseudo-polynomial time. In particular, for integer singleton values bounded by $W$, its running time is polynomial in the numbers of agents and goods and in $W$. Its fairness definition includes removal of zero-valued goods. [Almost EFX in Hypergraphs, Sections 2, 4.5, and 4.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26948v1#S4.SS5)

The integer interpretation is also visible in its potential analysis. Virtual utilities scale bundle values by either $1$ or $\frac32$. With $q$ goods and maximum singleton value $W$, their sum belongs to $\frac12\mathbb Z$ and is at most $\frac32qW$. The secondary potential counts allocated goods, including at most $p+1$ dummy goods for $p$ agents. The numerical bounds used for the improving loops are therefore polynomial in $p,q,W$. [Running-time analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26948v1#S4.SS6)

The compression below leaves the agents, edges, multiplicities, roots, and zero singleton values unchanged. Thus every structural hypothesis of this external theorem remains satisfied.

3. An exact integer compression lemma

Lemma 2. Let $q\geq 1$, and let
$$ c=(c_1,\ldots,c_k)\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^k, \qquad k\geq 1. $$
There is a positive integer vector $z=(z_1,\ldots,z_k)$ such that
$$ z_j\leq k!(3q)^{k-1} $$
for every $j$, and
$$ \operatorname{sgn}(d\cdot z)=\operatorname{sgn}(d\cdot c) $$
for every
$$ d\in\{-2q,-2q+1,\ldots,3q\}^k. $$
For fixed $k$, such a vector can be found in time polynomial in $q$ and the binary encoding length of $c$.

Proof: a feasible polyhedron

For every coefficient vector $d$ in the displayed box, impose the following constraint on a real vector $y$:
$$ \begin{cases} d\cdot y\geq 1,& d\cdot c>0,\\ -d\cdot y\geq 1,& d\cdot c<0,\\ d\cdot y=0,& d\cdot c=0. \end{cases} $$
Let $P$ be the resulting polyhedron.

There are finitely many constraints. Moreover, their strict-sign cases are nonempty because the coordinate unit vectors belong to the box and $c_j>0$. Set
$$ \delta=\min_{d:\,d\cdot c\neq 0}|d\cdot c|>0. $$
Then $tc\in P$ whenever $t\geq 1/\delta$, so $P$ is nonempty.

For each coordinate unit vector, its constraint is $y_j\geq 1$. Hence
$$ P\subseteq [1,\infty)^k. $$

Proof: existence of a vertex

Choose $y^0\in P$. The set
$$ P\cap\left\{y:\sum_j y_j\leq\sum_j y_j^0\right\} $$
is nonempty, closed, and bounded, hence compact. It follows that $\sum_j y_j$ attains its minimum on $P$. Its minimizing set $F$ is also nonempty and compact.

Within $F$, minimize the first coordinate; within the resulting minimizing set, minimize the second coordinate; continue through all coordinates. These successive compact minimizing sets yield a unique point $y^*$.

This point is extreme in $P$. Indeed, if
$$ y^*=\frac{u+v}{2}, \qquad u,v\in P, $$
minimality of the coordinate sum forces $u,v\in F$. Minimality of the first coordinate then forces both first coordinates to equal that of $y^*$; the successive coordinate minima force the same conclusion for every coordinate. Thus $u=v=y^*$.

The normals of the constraints active at $y^*$ span $\mathbb R^k$. Otherwise, choose a nonzero vector $h$ perpendicular to all active normals. Every inactive inequality has positive slack. Since there are finitely many such inequalities, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ both
$$ y^*+\varepsilon h \quad\text{and}\quad y^*-\varepsilon h $$
satisfy every constraint. This contradicts extremality.

We may therefore select $k$ linearly independent active constraints. Their equations have the form
$$ Ay^*=b, $$
where $A$ is an integer matrix with entries of absolute value at most $3q$, and every entry of $b$ is either $0$ or $1$.

Proof: integer bounds

Put $D=\det A$. Since $A$ is nonsingular, $D$ is a nonzero integer. Cramer’s rule gives
$$ y_j^*=\frac{\det A_j}{D}, $$
where $A_j$ replaces column $j$ of $A$ by $b$.

Define
$$ z=|D|y^*. $$
Every $z_j$ is an integer and is positive because $y_j^*\geq 1$.

Each term in the determinant expansion of $A_j$ contains one entry of absolute value at most $1$ and $k-1$ entries of absolute value at most $3q$. There are $k!$ terms. Consequently,
$$ 0<z_j=|\det A_j|\leq k!(3q)^{k-1}. $$

Scaling by the positive number $|D|$ preserves equalities and strict signs. Thus every constraint defining $P$ proves the required sign identity.

Proof: computation

There are at most
$$ R=(5q+1)^k $$
constraints. Their signs can be classified using exact rational arithmetic.

Enumerate every choice of $k$ constraint rows. For a nonsingular choice, solve its equations and check whether the resulting point satisfies all constraints. The vertex argument guarantees that some choice succeeds. Scale that solution by the absolute determinant as above.

There are at most $R^k$ choices and at most $R$ checks per choice. Matrix determinants and candidate coordinates have polynomial bit length; for example, the determinant bound is
$$ |\det A|\leq k!(3q)^k. $$
Thus this enumeration takes polynomial time for fixed $k$. $\square$

4. Polynomial time with a fixed number of positive value levels

Theorem 3. Fix $K\geq 1$. Suppose that, for every agent $a$, the set
$$ \{v_a(g):v_a(g)>0\} $$
has cardinality at most $K$. Then the required mass family can be computed in time polynomial in $m,n,\tau,L$.

Proof. Consider one component with $q$ goods. If $q=0$, its empty allocation is complete and fair, so assume $q\geq 1$.

For an agent $a$ with positive values, list its distinct positive levels as
$$ c_{a,1},\ldots,c_{a,k_a}, \qquad k_a\leq K. $$
Apply Lemma 2 to obtain positive integers
$$ z_{a,1},\ldots,z_{a,k_a}. $$
Define a new additive valuation $w_a$ by
$$ w_a(g)= \begin{cases} 0,&v_a(g)=0,\\ z_{a,j},&v_a(g)=c_{a,j}. \end{cases} $$
For an identically zero agent, set $w_a$ identically zero.

I claim that a complete allocation is $\frac23$-EFX for $v$ if and only if it is $\frac23$-EFX for $w$.

Fix any complete allocation, agents $a,b$, and good $g\in B_b$. For an agent with positive values, let $s_j$ count level-$j$ goods in $B_a$, and let $t_j$ count level-$j$ goods in $B_b\setminus\{g\}$. Then
$$ 3v_a(B_a)-2v_a(B_b\setminus\{g\}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k_a}(3s_j-2t_j)c_{a,j}. $$
Each count lies between $0$ and $q$, so
$$ -2q\leq 3s_j-2t_j\leq 3q. $$
Lemma 2 therefore gives
$$ \begin{aligned} &\operatorname{sgn}\left( 3v_a(B_a)-2v_a(B_b\setminus\{g\}) \right)\\ &\qquad= \operatorname{sgn}\left( 3w_a(B_a)-2w_a(B_b\setminus\{g\}) \right). \end{aligned} $$
In particular, one slack is nonnegative exactly when the other is. For an identically zero agent, both slacks are zero.

This argument also covers a removed good valued at zero: it simply contributes nothing to the level counts. It covers recipients outside edges because all zero values remain zero. Hence every required inequality is preserved exactly.

All compressed singleton values are bounded by
$$ W\leq K!(3q)^{K-1}. $$
For fixed $K$, this is polynomial in $q$. The compressed input has polynomial encoding length, and the supplied pseudo-polynomial algorithm consequently runs in polynomial time on it. Its complete $\frac23$-EFX allocation is fair for the original valuations by the preceding sign identities.

Perform this construction separately for each component, and assign mass $\lambda_H$ to its resulting allocation. Proposition 1 proves validity. The aggregate running time is polynomial in $m,n,\tau,L$. $\square$

The theorem permits, for example, positive levels $1$ and $2^B$ for an arbitrarily large binary-encoded exponent $B$. It imposes no bound on their ratio.

5. Why this compression does not settle arbitrary valuations

The following establishes a limitation of preserving every individual inequality sign. It is not a hardness result.

Proposition 4. Within the task’s structural restrictions, there are instances with $k$ positive levels for one agent such that any positive integer replacement preserving every individual fairness-inequality sign must have a value exceeding
$$ \frac1k\left(\frac53\right)^{k-1}. $$

Proof. For $k\geq 2$, use agents
$$ a,b_1,\ldots,b_k. $$
For every $i$, introduce goods $g_i,h_i$ with edges
$$ e_{g_i}=\{a,b_i\}, \qquad e_{h_i}=\{b_i\}. $$
All edges are distinct. Distinct edges intersect in at most one vertex, and multiplicity is $1$.

Set
$$ v_a(g_i)=2^{i-1}, \qquad v_a(h_i)=0, $$
and give every leaf agent identically zero valuation.

For each $j$, consider a complete allocation with
$$ B_a=\{g_j\}, \qquad B_{b_1}=\{h_1,g_1,\ldots,g_{j-1}\}, $$
and all remaining goods assigned to $b_2$. When $a$ compares against $b_1$ after removing $h_1$, its fairness slack is
$$ \begin{aligned} 3v_a(g_j)-2\sum_{i<j}v_a(g_i) &=3\cdot 2^{j-1}-2(2^{j-1}-1)\\ &=2^{j-1}+2>0. \end{aligned} $$

Let $z_i$ be positive integer replacements preserving these signs. They must satisfy
$$ 3z_j>2\sum_{i<j}z_i. $$
Writing $S_j=\sum_{i\leq j}z_i$, for every $j\geq 2$ this implies
$$ S_j>\frac53S_{j-1}. $$
Since $S_1=z_1\geq 1$,
$$ S_k>\left(\frac53\right)^{k-1}. $$
Therefore
$$ \max_i z_i\geq \frac{S_k}{k}> \frac1k\left(\frac53\right)^{k-1}. $$
The original instance has binary length polynomial in $k$, so this numerical lower bound is superpolynomial in that length. $\square$

These instances themselves have an easy exact EFX allocation: give all $g_i$ to $a$ and all null goods to a leaf. Thus the proposition only shows why universal preservation of individual signs can demand unnecessarily large integers.

6. Certificates and the scope of a hardness claim

A complete allocation can be encoded by one recipient per good. Its fairness can be checked exactly in polynomial time. For a nonempty compared bundle, define
$$ U_a=v_a(B_a),\qquad T_{a,b}=v_a(B_b),\qquad d_{a,b}=\min_{g\in B_b}v_a(g). $$
Additivity gives
$$ \max_{g\in B_b}v_a(B_b\setminus\{g\}) =T_{a,b}-d_{a,b}. $$
Thus all removal inequalities for this pair are equivalent to
$$ 3U_a\geq 2(T_{a,b}-d_{a,b}). $$
Empty bundles impose no inequalities. A zero-valued good makes the minimum zero, as required.

Exact rational sums and comparisons have polynomial bit complexity: multiplying the input denominators produces a common denominator whose bit length is at most their total bit length. Combining one allocation per component with the original masses therefore gives polynomial-length, polynomial-time-verifiable witnesses. Existence follows from the supplied theorem.

This totality constrains a particular decision-to-search hardness notion. Suppose polynomial-time maps $f,d$ satisfy:

  • $f(\varphi)$ is a permitted society for every Boolean formula $\varphi$;
  • for every valid output $y$ for $f(\varphi)$, the decoder $d(\varphi,y)$ decides whether $\varphi$ is satisfiable.

Then unsatisfiability has an NP certificate: a polynomial-length valid output for which the decoder reports unsatisfiability. Such a certificate exists for every unsatisfiable formula, and none exists for a satisfiable formula. Hence $\mathrm{UNSAT}\in\mathrm{NP}$, implying $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

This conditional consequence does not rule out every search-hardness notion.
