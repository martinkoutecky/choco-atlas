Characterize the bit complexity in \(N\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

Result

Computing \(F\) is polynomial-time solvable. More precisely, an exact implementation using elementary binary arithmetic takes

\(O\!\left(L^2+mL\log(m+1)\right)\)

bit operations. Under the usual encoding of complete rankings as lists of binary requester indices, this is \(O(L^2)\). These are upper bounds, not claims of optimality.

The proof applies to arbitrary positive binary rational requirements and masses satisfying the stated hypotheses.

1. Convert the vote tally to integers

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/d_t\), where \(a_t,d_t\) are positive integers encoded in binary. Reduction to lowest terms is unnecessary. Let \(h(t)\) be the index of the first requester in ranking \(t\). Define

\(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}d_t,\qquad w_t=a_t(D/d_t),\qquad A_j=\sum_{t:\,h(t)=j}w_t.\)

Each quotient \(D/d_t\) is an integer. Moreover,

\(A_j=D\sum_{t:\,h(t)=j}\mu_t=Dq_j.\)

Because \(D>0\), ordering by decreasing \(A_j\), with ties broken by increasing index, gives exactly the required ordering by \(q_j\).

Let \(\ell(x)\) denote the binary length of a positive integer \(x\). Since the denominator encodings form part of the input,

\(\ell(D)\leq 1+\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\ell(d_t)\leq L+1.\)

The normalization hypothesis gives

\(\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_j=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t=D.\)

All these integers are nonnegative, so every tally and every intermediate partial tally lies between \(0\) and \(D\). Thus tallying requires only integers of \(O(L)\) bits.

2. Convert the funding scan to integers

Write \(B=c_0/e_0\) and \(b_j=c_j/e_j\), with positive binary integers. Define

\(E=\prod_{j=0}^{m}e_j,\qquad C_j=c_j(E/e_j)\quad(0\leq j\leq m).\)

Then \(C_0=EB\) and \(C_j=Eb_j\). Each \(C_j\) has \(O(L)\) bits: the product \(E\) has at most \(L+1\) bits, and each numerator has at most \(L\) bits.

Let \(\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(m)\) be the ordering obtained from the integer tallies. Start with \(H=C_0\) and scan this ordering. Include requester \(\pi(k)\) precisely when \(C_{\pi(k)}\leq H\), then subtract \(C_{\pi(k)}\).

To prove correctness, maintain the invariant

\(H=ER,\)

where \(R\) is the remaining fund in the rule specified in the question. It holds initially. At each step,

\(C_{\pi(k)}\leq H\ \Longleftrightarrow\ Eb_{\pi(k)}\leq ER\ \Longleftrightarrow\ b_{\pi(k)}\leq R.\)

Thus both procedures make the same inclusion decision. If they include the requester, subtraction preserves the invariant; if they skip it, the invariant is unchanged. Induction proves that the integer implementation returns exactly \(F\).

Furthermore, \(0\leq H\leq C_0\) throughout the scan. Consequently, every funding comparison and subtraction uses \(O(L)\)-bit integers.

3. Bit complexity

We first justify the cost of constructing the scaled integers.

Consider any list of positive fractions \(u_i/v_i\) whose numerators and denominators have total binary length \(h\). Its denominator product \(P=\prod_i v_i\) has \(O(h)\) bits.

Schoolbook multiplication of an \(O(h)\)-bit integer by a \(k\)-bit integer costs \(O(hk)\). Binary long division of an \(O(h)\)-bit integer by a \(k\)-bit positive integer also costs \(O(hk)\): there are \(O(h)\) quotient positions, each requiring at most \(O(k)\) work. Therefore:

  • Constructing \(P\) costs \(O\!\left(h\sum_i\ell(v_i)\right)=O(h^2)\).
  • Computing all exact quotients \(P/v_i\) costs \(O(h^2)\).
  • Computing all products \(u_i(P/v_i)\) costs \(O\!\left(h\sum_i\ell(u_i)\right)=O(h^2)\).

Apply this calculation separately to the masses and to the funding quantities. Their combined preprocessing cost is \(O(L^2)\).

Reading the supplied input costs \(O(L)\). Initializing and accumulating the \(m\) tallies costs \(O((m+\tau)L)\), which is \(O(L^2)\), since the explicit input contains at least one encoded quantity per requester and per support entry.

Comparison sorting takes \(O(m\log(m+1))\) comparisons. Each comparison, including the index tie-break, costs \(O(L)\) bit operations. The funding scan costs \(O(mL)\). Hence the total bound is

\(O\!\left(L^2+mL\log(m+1)\right).\)

Under the usual explicit ranking encoding, even one complete ranking requires \(\Omega(m\log(m+1))\) bits. Since \(\tau\geq 1\), we have \(m\log(m+1)=O(L)\), yielding the stated \(O(L^2)\) upper bound.

The algorithm processes the \(\tau\) listed rankings directly. It neither enumerates all possible rankings nor expands rational masses into individual voters.

4. Relation to the cited bound and hardness

The cited paper’s Lemma 1 assigns \(O(N\log N)\) time to its budget-distribution algorithm. That statement does not establish a bit-complexity bound for the rational-mass input here. [Source, Section V](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10206v1#S5).

At the level of ranking entries and unit-cost exact arithmetic operations, a full-input implementation of the present rule takes \(O(\tau m+m\log(m+1))\): reading complete rankings contributes \(O(\tau m)\), tallying contributes \(O(\tau)\), and sorting and scanning contribute the remaining terms. The bit analysis above accounts additionally for exact rational arithmetic.

Thus computing \(F\) belongs to \(\mathrm{FP}\), and deciding whether a specified requester belongs to \(F\) belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\). NP-hardness under standard polynomial-time reductions would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
