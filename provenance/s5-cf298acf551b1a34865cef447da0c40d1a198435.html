The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) of types for each \(r\in T\), and proposed community masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), decide whether \(x\) is stable. It is unstable if there are exit masses \(g_t\le x_t\) and replacement masses \(y_t\le\mu_t-x_t\) with \(0<\sum_t g_t=\sum_t y_t<\sum_t x_t\), such that for every type \(r\) with \(x_r-g_r>0\), there is a nonnegative transport \(q^r_{u,v}\) with row sums \(y_u\), column sums \(g_v\), and \(q^r_{u,v}=0\) unless \(u\succ_r v\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type \(r\in T\) has an explicitly listed strict total ranking \(\succ_r\) of all types, including itself. Here the number of ranked alternatives is \(m=\tau\).
The proposed community has binary rational masses \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and positive total mass \(X=\sum_t x_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
The community is unstable precisely when there exist real exit and replacement masses \(0\le g_t\le x_t\), \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t-x_t\), with \(0<\sum_t g_t=\sum_t y_t<X\), such that every type \(r\) with \(x_r-g_r>0\) admits a real nonnegative transport \(q^r\) satisfying \(\sum_v q^r_{u,v}=y_u\), \(\sum_u q^r_{u,v}=g_v\), and \(q^r_{u,v}=0\) unless \(u\succ_r v\). The masses \(g,y\) are common to all remaining types; their transports may differ. Stability means absence of such a witness.

Is deciding this continuous group stability problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For finite individuals with strict rankings, group preference means existence of a bijection pairing every departing individual with a strictly preferred outsider; deciding group stability is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete. [Borgs et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.5152)

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The problem is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions, and therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. This holds even when every type is either entirely inside or entirely outside the proposed community, and all masses are dyadic rationals.

The proof uses auxiliary types to force both exit and replacement masses to take discrete values. Simply assigning equal masses to a finite instance would not establish hardness, because fractional replacements could create additional witnesses.

1. Transport characterization and membership in \(\mathrm{coNP}\)

Fix a type’s ranking, written from best to worst as \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\). Suppose \(g,y\) are nonnegative and have equal total mass.

A transport using only strictly preferred pairs exists if and only if

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{a_i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}y_{a_i} \qquad\text{for every }k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}. \]

Here and below, displayed formulas use the same mathematical notation as inline formulas.

Necessity follows because any replacement supplying one of the first \(k\) destinations must belong to the first \(k-1\) sources.

For sufficiency, process destinations in ranking order. Before supplying destination \(a_k\), the unused supply among \(a_1,\ldots,a_{k-1}\) is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}y_{a_i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{a_i}\geq g_{a_k}. \]

Allocate the required amount from those sources. Every allocated pair is strictly preferred. After all destinations have been supplied, equality of total masses ensures that all source mass has been used.

Now let \(C=\{t:x_t>0\}\). Guess the set \(R\subseteq C\) of types that remain. Introduce variables \(g,y,\delta\), and maximize \(\delta\) subject to the following rational linear constraints:

  • \(0\leq g_t\leq x_t\), \(0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t-x_t\), and \(\sum_tg_t=\sum_ty_t\).
  • \(g_r=x_r\) for \(r\in C\setminus R\).
  • \(g_r\leq x_r-\delta\) for \(r\in R\).
  • \(\delta\geq0\), \(\delta\leq\sum_tg_t\), and \(\delta\leq X-\sum_tg_t\).
  • For each \(r\in R\), all \(m\) transport inequalities established above, using \(r\)’s ranking.

An instability witness exists with remaining set \(R\) exactly when this linear program has a positive optimum. Indeed, a witness permits any sufficiently small positive \(\delta\), bounded by its positive exit mass, positive remaining total mass, and all positive quantities \(x_r-g_r\). Conversely, a feasible solution with \(\delta>0\) satisfies every strict condition in the definition.

The linear program has \(2\tau+1\) variables and \(O(m\tau+\tau)\) constraints. Its coefficients are rational and its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Exact rational linear programming therefore decides whether its optimum is positive in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations.

Consequently, guessing \(R\) gives an \(\mathrm{NP}\) verification procedure for instability. Stability belongs to \(\mathrm{coNP}\). Enumerating all \(R\) also gives the deterministic upper bound

\[ O\!\left(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\right). \]

2. The finite problem used in the reduction

Use the finite group-stability problem from the question. Its input consists of a finite set \(V=S\mathbin{\dot\cup}U\), strict rankings \(\succ_r^0\), and a nonempty community \(S\). It is unstable precisely when there exist

\[ \varnothing\neq G\subsetneq S,\qquad W\subseteq U,\qquad |G|=|W|, \]

such that every \(r\in S\setminus G\) admits a bijection \(f_r:G\to W\) with \(f_r(v)\succ_r^0v\) for all \(v\in G\). Deciding finite stability is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete. This is exactly the group preference in Definition 7 and the stability problem in Theorem 9 of [Borgs et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.5152).

Write \(n=|S|\) and \(\ell=|U|\). We may assume \(n\geq2\) and \(\ell\geq1\): excluded instances are visibly stable and can be mapped to the stable one-type continuous instance with \(\mu=x=1\).

For any set \(A\), write \(g(A)=\sum_{t\in A}g_t\) and \(y(A)=\sum_{t\in A}y_t\).

3. Construction of the continuous instance

Introduce a selector \(p_z\) for each \(z\in U\), one outside type \(d\), and auxiliary community types

\[ P=\{p_z:z\in U\},\qquad H=\{a_\star,c_1,c_2\}\cup\{a_z,b_z:z\in U\}. \]

The complete type set is

\[ T=S\mathbin{\dot\cup}P\mathbin{\dot\cup}U \mathbin{\dot\cup}\{d\}\mathbin{\dot\cup}H. \]

Let \(E=S\cup P\) and \(O=U\cup\{d\}\). Thus \(m=\tau=n+4\ell+4\).

Choose a power of two \(Q>3n+5\ell\), and set \(h=1/Q\). Assign masses as follows:

\[ \mu_t=h \quad\text{for }t\in S\cup P\cup U\cup(H\setminus\{a_\star\}), \]

\[ \mu_d=(n-1)h,\qquad \mu_{a_\star}=1-(2n+4\ell+1)h. \]

These masses are positive and sum to one. All are dyadic rationals with \(O(\log(n+\ell))\) bits. Set \(x_t=\mu_t\) for \(t\in E\cup H\), and \(x_t=0\) for \(t\in O\).

The total outside mass is \(B=(\ell+n-1)h\). The choice of \(Q\) gives

\[ x_{a_\star}=1-(2n+4\ell+1)h>B. \]

It remains to specify strict rankings. In the following descriptions, every earlier block precedes every later block. Orders within blocks are arbitrary unless explicitly prescribed.

The anchor \(a_\star\) ranks \(H\succ O\succ E\).

Each original community type \(r\in S\) ranks:

  • all of \(H\);
  • itself;
  • \(V\setminus\{r\}\), in its original order \(\succ_r^0\);
  • \(d\);
  • all selectors \(P\).

Each selector \(p_z\) ranks \(H\succ\{p_z\}\succ O\succ(E\setminus\{p_z\})\).

The remaining auxiliary rankings are:

| Type | Ranking, after the initial block \(H\) |
|---|---|
| \(a_z\) | \(\{z\}\succ\{p_z\}\succ(O\setminus\{z\})\succ(E\setminus\{p_z\})\) |
| \(b_z\) | \((O\setminus\{z\})\succ(E\setminus\{p_z\})\succ\{z\}\succ\{p_z\}\) |
| \(c_1\) | \(U\succ S\succ\{d\}\succ P\) |
| \(c_2\) | \(\{d\}\succ P\succ U\succ S\) |

Types in \(O\) receive arbitrary strict total rankings. This explicitly ranks all \(m=\tau\) types, including each ranking type itself.

4. Properties of every continuous witness

Assume the constructed instance has an instability witness.

All auxiliary types remain. Replacement mass is supported on \(O\), so

\[ g_{a_\star}\leq\sum_tg_t=\sum_ty_t\leq B<x_{a_\star}. \]

Hence the anchor remains and its transport condition applies. It ranks every member of \(H\) above every outside type. No positive replacement mass can therefore supply any departure from \(H\). Thus \(g(H)=0\), and all types in \(H\) remain.

Every original or selector type exits entirely or contributes no exit mass. Each \(t\in E\) ranks itself above every outside type. If \(g_t<x_t=h\), then \(t\) remains, and its own transport cannot supply a positive departure from itself. Therefore

\[ g_t\in\{0,h\}\qquad(t\in E). \]

We next identify what the auxiliary rankings enforce.

Consider a ranking \(H\succ A\succ B'\succ C'\succ D'\), where \(A,C'\) partition the outside types and \(B',D'\) partition \(E\). Since \(g(H)=0\), a transport for this ranking exists exactly when \(g(B')\leq y(A)\).

Necessity holds because only sources in \(A\) can supply destinations in \(B'\). For sufficiency, allocate \(g(B')\) from \(A\) to \(B'\); every such pair is permitted. The remaining sources, in \(A\cup C'\), all precede every destination in \(D'\), and their total equals \(g(D')\), so they can supply it. The allocations exist because each relevant source-destination rectangle is complete; zero totals require only zero allocations.

Apply this observation to \(a_z\) and \(b_z\). Their conditions are respectively

\[ g_{p_z}\leq y_z,\qquad g(E\setminus\{p_z\})\leq y(O\setminus\{z\}). \]

Since \(g(E)=y(O)\), the second inequality is equivalent to \(y_z\leq g_{p_z}\). Consequently,

\[ y_z=g_{p_z}\in\{0,h\}\qquad(z\in U). \]

The conditions of \(c_1,c_2\) are \(g(S)\leq y(U)\) and \(g(P)\leq y_d\). Their left sides sum to their right sides, so both are equalities. Together with \(y_z=g_{p_z}\), they give

\[ g(S)=g(P)=y(U)=y_d. \]

Define \(G=\{r\in S:g_r=h\}\) and \(W=\{z\in U:y_z=h\}\). The preceding equalities imply \(|G|=|W|=k\) and \(\sum_tg_t=2kh\).

Positive exit mass gives \(k>0\). The bound \(y_d\leq\mu_d=(n-1)h\) gives \(k\leq n-1\). Hence

\[ \varnothing\neq G\subsetneq S,\qquad |W|=|G|. \]

This verifies both discreteness and the proper-departure requirement.

5. A continuous witness implies a finite witness

Take any \(r\in S\setminus G\). This type remains, so its transport exists.

In its constructed ranking, \(d\) is below every original community type. Thus \(d\) cannot supply any departure in \(G\). Those departures have total mass \(kh\), exactly the total replacement mass on \(W\). Therefore the transport restricted to \(W\times G\) supplies every destination and uses every source, with each marginal equal to \(h\).

Every permitted pair in this restricted transport is also permitted by the original ranking: moving \(r\) upwards changed no comparison between members of \(W\) and \(G\), because \(r\notin G\).

For completeness, such a fractional transport implies a bijection here. List \(G\) as \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\) and \(W\) as \(u_1,\ldots,u_k\), both from best to worst under \(\succ_r^0\). The first \(i\) destinations require mass \(ih\). Every source supplying them is strictly above \(v_i\). Since each source has mass \(h\), at least \(i\) members of \(W\) are strictly above \(v_i\). Therefore \(u_i\succ_r^0v_i\).

The pairing \(f_r(v_i)=u_i\) is the required bijection. This argument applies separately to every \(r\in S\setminus G\), proving finite instability.

6. A finite witness implies a continuous witness

Conversely, suppose \(G,W\) and the bijections \(f_r\) witness finite instability. Let \(k=|G|=|W|\), so \(1\leq k\leq n-1\).

Set

\[ g_r=h\quad(r\in G),\qquad g_{p_z}=h\quad(z\in W), \]

with all other exits zero. Set \(y_z=h\) for \(z\in W\), \(y_d=kh\), and all other replacements zero.

All coordinate bounds hold, including \(y_d\leq(n-1)h\). The exit and replacement totals both equal \(2kh>0\). Because the anchor has positive mass and zero exit, the exit total is strictly below \(X\).

It remains to verify every remaining type’s transport:

  • For \(r\in S\setminus G\), use \(f_r\) to send mass \(h\) from each selected original outsider to its paired original departure. Send mass \(h\) from \(d\) to each departing selector. These are all strictly preferred pairs.
  • A remaining selector ranks every outside type above every departing type: its own exit is zero, and all other departing types lie in \(E\setminus\{p_z\}\).
  • The anchor ranks every outside type above every departing type.
  • For \(a_z,b_z,c_1,c_2\), the inequalities proved sufficient in Section 4 hold, because \(g_{p_z}=y_z\) and \(g(S)=g(P)=y(U)=y_d=kh\).

For the selectors and anchor, a transport exists directly because every positive source can supply every positive destination. Thus the specified common \(g,y\) form a continuous instability witness.

We have proved the exact equivalence

\[ S\text{ is finitely unstable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\text{ is continuously unstable}. \]

7. Complexity conclusion

Let \(N=n+\ell\), and let \(L_{\mathrm{fin}}\) be the finite input length. The construction has \(m=\tau=n+4\ell+4=O(N)\), explicitly listed rankings, and masses with \(O(\log N)\) bits. Its output length is \(O(N^2\log N)\), and it can be produced in \(O(L_{\mathrm{fin}}+N^2\log N)\) bit operations.

The equivalence gives a polynomial-time many-one reduction from finite stability to continuous stability. Hence continuous stability is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hard. Section 1 proves membership, so it is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.

Finally, finite instability is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Given its input, construct the continuous instance, query continuous stability once, and negate the answer. This establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm for the continuous problem would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
