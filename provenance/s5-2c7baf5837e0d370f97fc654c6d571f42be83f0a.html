The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of role types \(T\), a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) of \(T\) for each \(r\in T\), rational population masses \(\mu_r\), and a proposed community profile \(s\) with \(0\le s_r\le\mu_r\) and \(\sum_{r\in T}s_r>0\), decide whether the community is self-approving. It is not self-approving exactly when there are outsider masses \(y_b\) and, for each \(r\) with \(s_r>0\), a transport plan \(z^{(r)}_{ab}\ge0\) such that \(0\le y_b\le\mu_b-s_b\), \(\sum_b y_b=\sum_a s_a\), \(\sum_b z^{(r)}_{ab}=s_a\), \(\sum_a z^{(r)}_{ab}=y_b\), and \(z^{(r)}_{ab}=0\) unless \(b\succ_r a\). Decide whether no such common \(y\) and collection of plans exist.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) role types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_r\), normalized by \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types; the number of ranked roles is \(m=|T|=\tau\).
Each role \(r\in T\) has an explicitly given strict total ranking \(\succ_r\) of \(T\), including itself. The proposed community has binary rational masses \(0\le s_r\le\mu_r\) and total mass \(q=\sum_{r\in T}s_r>0\). Community and outsider mass may coexist in one role.
The community is not self-approving precisely when there exist real outsider masses \(y_b\) and, for every \(r\) with \(s_r>0\), a nonnegative real transport plan \(z^{(r)}\), satisfying
\(0\le y_b\le\mu_b-s_b,\quad\sum_{b\in T}y_b=q,\quad\sum_{b\in T}z^{(r)}_{ab}=s_a,\quad\sum_{a\in T}z^{(r)}_{ab}=y_b,\quad z^{(r)}_{ab}=0\text{ unless }b\succ_r a\), for all applicable \(a,b,r\in T\).
The outsider profile \(y\) must be common to all participating roles; their transport plans may differ. Self-approval means that no such collection exists. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding self-approval for this mass model in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents with strict rankings, deciding self-approval is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete: failure means that some equally large outsider set admits, for each community member, a bijection mapping every insider to a strictly preferred outsider. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.5152)

Result

Deciding self-approval in the stated mass model is in \(\mathrm{P}\). The common outsider profile can be found, or proved nonexistent, by exact rational linear programming.

I prove an equivalent formulation with \(m\) variables and \(O(\tau m)\) constraints. Its running time is \(\bigl(\tau m(m+L)\bigr)^{O(1)}\) bit operations. Since \(m=\tau\), this is \(\bigl(\tau^3+\tau^2L\bigr)^{O(1)}\), hence polynomial in \(L\).

1. Transport feasibility for one ranking

Fix a participating role \(r\), and write its ranking from best to worst as

\(t_1\succ_r t_2\succ_r\cdots\succ_r t_m.\)

Fix nonnegative outsider masses \(y\) satisfying \(\sum_b y_b=q=\sum_a s_a\).

Lemma. A nonnegative transport plan with row sums \(s\), column sums \(y\), and support only on pairs satisfying \(b\succ_r a\) exists if and only if

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{t_i}\le \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}y_{t_j}\qquad\text{for every }k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.\)

The sum on the right is zero when \(k=1\).

Necessity. Every insider role among \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\) can send mass only to outsider roles among \(t_1,\ldots,t_{k-1}\). Thus, using nonnegativity and the prescribed column sums,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{t_i} =\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}z^{(r)}_{t_i,t_j} \le\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\sum_{a\in T}z^{(r)}_{a,t_j} =\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}y_{t_j}.\)

Sufficiency. Construct the transport rows in order \(t_1,\ldots,t_m\), maintaining each outsider role’s remaining capacity.

At step \(k\), all previously assigned mass lies in columns \(t_1,\ldots,t_{k-1}\): row \(t_i\), for \(i<k\), used only columns with index smaller than \(i\). Therefore the remaining capacity in columns available to row \(t_k\) is exactly

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}y_{t_j}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}s_{t_i}.\)

The assumed inequality says this quantity is at least \(s_{t_k}\). Allocate \(s_{t_k}\) among these columns, taking from their remaining capacities until the row is filled. Such an allocation exists because the capacities are nonnegative and have sufficient total mass. Every used column is strictly preferred to \(t_k\).

After all rows have been filled, the allocated mass is \(q\), equal to the total column capacity. Since no capacity was exceeded, every column is filled exactly. This proves the lemma.

2. One linear program for the common outsider profile

Let \(R=\{r\in T:s_r>0\}\) and \(p=|R|\). The premise \(q>0\) implies \(1\le p\le\tau\). For each \(r\in R\), write its ranking as \(t^{(r)}_1,\ldots,t^{(r)}_m\).

Construct the following feasibility problem, whose only variables are the outsider masses \(y_b\):

\(\displaystyle 0\le y_b\le\mu_b-s_b\qquad(b\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{b\in T}y_b=q,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}y_{t^{(r)}_j}\ge\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{t^{(r)}_i}\qquad(r\in R,\;1\le k\le m).\)

Equivalence to failure of self-approval.

If the community is not self-approving, take its witnessing common outsider profile \(y\). The capacity and total-mass constraints hold by definition. Applying the necessity direction of the lemma to each witnessing transport plan gives every prefix inequality. Thus the linear program is feasible.

Conversely, suppose the linear program is feasible. Its single vector \(y\) satisfies the outsider capacity and total-mass requirements. For each \(r\in R\), the sufficiency direction of the lemma constructs a transport plan \(z^{(r)}\) with row sums \(s\), column sums this same \(y\), and support only on strictly preferred pairs. These plans may differ, exactly as the definition permits. Consequently, they jointly witness failure of self-approval.

Hence

\(\displaystyle \text{the community is self-approving}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the linear program is infeasible}.\)

Strict preference has been encoded by the ranking prefixes. No strict numerical inequalities or approximation thresholds are needed.

3. Binary encoding and running time

The program has \(m\) variables. Replacing its one equality by two inequalities gives \(2m+pm+2=O(\tau m)\) inequalities. Every coefficient of a variable belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\).

All right-hand sides are rational numbers with \(O(L)\) bits. To verify this explicitly, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses \(\mu_b,s_b\). Its binary length is \(O(L)\), because the sum of those denominators’ binary lengths is bounded by the input length. Express every mass over denominator \(D\). Differences \(\mu_b-s_b\), the total \(q\), and all community prefix sums then have integer numerators between zero and \(D\), so each has \(O(L)\) bits. Computing these quantities uses polynomial-time integer arithmetic.

A dense encoding of the inequality system therefore has length

\(\displaystyle B=O\bigl((2m+pm+2)(m+L)\bigr)=O\bigl(\tau m(m+L)\bigr).\)

The standard rational linear-programming theorem states that feasibility of an explicitly given rational system can be decided exactly in time polynomial in its total binary encoding length. It applies here because every constraint is linear, every coefficient is rational, and the entire system is explicitly available. See [Nemirovski’s lecture notes, Theorem 6.3.1 and its feasibility proof](https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~nemirovs/OPTILN2024FallNoSol.pdf).

Construct the program, run this exact feasibility algorithm, and return self-approval precisely when it reports infeasibility. The construction and solution together take

\(\displaystyle \bigl(\tau m(m+L)\bigr)^{O(1)}\)

bit operations. With \(m=\tau\), and with the support and rankings explicitly included in the input, this is \(L^{O(1)}\).

4. Relation to the finite-agent result

The cited finite-agent theorem establishes \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness of self-approval for preference networks. [Theorem 8 of the supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.5152).

Its outsider witness selects whole agents. In the present formulation, outsider masses can be fractional and transport mass can be split. Those are precisely the real variables permitted by the task. A linear program that imposed indivisible outsider selection would require additional integrality restrictions; the proof above imposes none.

Thus the finite-agent hardness result does not obstruct this algorithm. The stated mass problem is polynomial-time solvable; under polynomial-time many-one reductions, it could also be \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
