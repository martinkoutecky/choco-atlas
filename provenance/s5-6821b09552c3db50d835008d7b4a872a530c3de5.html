The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(N\), a finite union \(F\) of rational closed intervals in \([0,1]\), and a support \(\{(x_j,\mu_j)\}_{j=1}^{\tau}\) with \(\mu_j=n_j/N\), \(\sum_j n_j=N\), find a deterministic facility rule \(M_N(\mu,F)\in F\), computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of intervals, and input bit length, that is strategyproof when one resident changes location and moves mass \(1/N\), and guarantees \(W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge \operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)/3\), where \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_j\mu_j(1-|x_j-y|)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)=\max_{y\in F}W_\mu(y)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(N\ge 1\) be a binary-encoded integer and let \(F=\bigcup_{r=1}^{m}[a_r,b_r]\ne\varnothing\), with rational endpoints satisfying \(0\le a_r\le b_r\le1\); singleton intervals are allowed.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) distinct types \(x_j\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_j=n_j/N\), where \(n_j\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\) and \(\sum_j n_j=N\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A deterministic rule chooses one rational facility location \(M_N(\mu,F)\in F\), using only the reported distribution and the public inputs \(N,F\). A resident at \(x\) has utility \(1-|x-y|\) from outcome \(y\); equal distances mean indifference.
Strategyproofness requires, for every supported \(x\) and every report \(z\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\), with \(\mu'=\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_z\), that \(|x-M_N(\mu,F)|\le |x-M_N(\mu',F)|\), where \(\delta_x\) is unit mass at \(x\). Merge coincident types and remove zero masses after a report; \(N,F\) remain fixed.
Define \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_j\mu_j(1-|x_j-y|)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)=\max_{y\in F}W_\mu(y)\).

Does there exist a deterministic strategyproof rule, computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), that guarantees \(W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)/3\) for every admissible input, or is this guarantee unattainable under these requirements?

For explicitly listed residents, choosing the nearest feasible location to the lower median, with fixed report-independent tie-breaking, is strategyproof and achieves approximation factor \(3\); no deterministic strategyproof rule achieves a uniformly smaller factor. Result

Yes. The nearest-feasible-location rule for the lower median is deterministic, strategyproof under the specified single-resident reporting operation, and computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It guarantees
\(W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge \operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)/3\).

This is the compressed implementation of the median mechanism discussed in [Walsh, Theorem 3](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~twalsh/wpricai2021.pdf). The proofs below establish the required properties directly, including bit complexity.

1. Definition of the rule

For \(t\in[0,1]\), let \(P_F(t)\) be the smallest feasible point minimizing distance to \(t\). This exists because \(F\) is nonempty and compact. Thus ties always go to the smaller location, independently of the reports.

Sort the support so that \(x_1<\cdots<x_\tau\), and recover the integer counts \(n_j=N\mu_j\). Set
\(k=\lceil N/2\rceil\),
and let
\(h=x_\ell\), where \(\ell=\min\{i:\sum_{j=1}^{i}n_j\ge k\}\).

Define
\(M_N(\mu,F)=P_F(h)\).

Conceptually, \(h\) is the \(k\)-th smallest entry in the list containing \(n_j\) copies of each \(x_j\). The algorithm never constructs that list.

The defining inequalities imply
\(\mu([0,h])\ge k/N\ge 1/2\).
Furthermore, fewer than \(k\) residents lie strictly below \(h\), so
\(\mu([h,1])\ge (N-k+1)/N\ge 1/2\).

Both inequalities will be used in the welfare proof.

2. Strategyproofness

Write \(P=P_F\).

Monotonicity of nearest-point projection

We first prove that \(P\) is nondecreasing. Suppose \(s<t\), but \(p=P(s)>q=P(t)\). Nearest-point optimality gives
\(|s-p|\le |s-q|\) and \(|t-q|\le |t-p|\).

For \(p>q\), the first inequality implies \(s\ge(p+q)/2\), while the second implies \(t\le(p+q)/2\). This contradicts \(s<t\). Hence \(P(s)\le P(t)\).

Fixing the other residents

Fix a resident with true location \(x\), and hold the other \(N-1\) reports fixed. For this proof only, write their sorted locations as
\(s_1\le\cdots\le s_{N-1}\).
Introduce the boundary values \(s_0=0\) and \(s_N=1\), and set
\(u=s_{k-1}\), \(v=s_k\).

If the fixed resident reports \(t\), the lower median is
\(H(t)=\min\{\max\{t,u\},v\}\).

Indeed, when \(t<u\), the \(k\)-th entry of the combined list is \(u\); when \(u\le t\le v\), it is \(t\); and when \(t>v\), it is \(v\). The boundary conventions also cover \(N=1\), in which case \(H(t)=t\).

Let \(a=P(u)\), \(b=P(v)\), and \(r=P(x)\). Monotonicity gives \(a\le b\), and applying \(P\) to the preceding three cases yields
\(P(H(t))=\min\{\max\{P(t),a\},b\}\).

Consequently, every report produces an outcome in \([a,b]\). We distinguish three cases.

  • If \(a\le r\le b\), truthful reporting produces \(r=P(x)\). Since \(r\) minimizes distance from \(x\) over all of \(F\), no report improves the resident’s utility.
  • If \(r<a\), truthful reporting produces \(a\). Since \(r\) is nearest to \(x\) and \(a\in F\),

\(|x-r|\le |x-a|\).
Because \(r<a\), this implies \(x\le(r+a)/2<a\). Every alternative outcome \(y\) satisfies \(y\ge a\), so
\(|x-y|=y-x\ge a-x=|x-a|\).

  • If \(r>b\), truthful reporting produces \(b\). Nearest-point optimality similarly implies

\(x\ge(r+b)/2>b\).
Every alternative outcome \(y\le b\) therefore satisfies
\(|x-y|=x-y\ge x-b=|x-b|\).

Thus truthful reporting always minimizes the resident’s distance among the obtainable outcomes.

Finally, replacing one copy of \(x\) by \(z\) in the conceptual resident list changes its empirical distribution exactly to
\(\mu'=\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_z\).
Merging coincident types and removing zero masses does not change that list’s order statistics. Therefore the rule satisfies precisely the strategyproofness condition in the question.

3. Welfare guarantee

Let \(p=P_F(h)\), and fix any feasible comparator \(q\in F\). We prove
\(W_\mu(q)\le 3W_\mu(p)\).

The case \(p=q\) is immediate. First suppose \(p<q\), and write \(d=q-p>0\).

Because \(p\) is nearest to \(h\),
\(|h-p|\le |h-q|\).
For \(p<q\), this is equivalent to
\(h\le(p+q)/2=p+d/2\).

Let \(A\) consist of the supported types satisfying \(x_j\le h\), and let
\(\alpha=\sum_{j:x_j\le h}\mu_j\).
The median property gives \(\alpha\ge1/2\).

For every \(x_j\in A\), we have \(x_j\le(p+q)/2\), so
\(1-|x_j-q|\le1-|x_j-p|\).
Thus these residents do not gain utility when the outcome changes from \(p\) to \(q\).

Their utility at \(p\) is also at least \(d/2\). To verify this, consider two cases:

  • If \(x_j\le p\), then

\(1-|x_j-p|=1-p+x_j\ge1-p\ge q-p=d\),
where the last inequality uses \(q\le1\).

  • If \(x_j\ge p\), then \(x_j-p\le d/2\), and hence

\(1-|x_j-p|\ge1-d/2\ge d/2\),
where the last inequality uses \(d\le1\).

Every utility is nonnegative, so
\(W_\mu(p)\ge\alpha d/2\ge d/4\).

For each remaining resident, the triangle inequality gives
\((1-|x_j-q|)-(1-|x_j-p|) =|x_j-p|-|x_j-q|\le d\).

Summing these bounds with the masses yields
\(W_\mu(q)-W_\mu(p)\le(1-\alpha)d\le d/2\).
Combining this with \(W_\mu(p)\ge d/4\), we obtain
\(W_\mu(q)\le W_\mu(p)+d/2\le3W_\mu(p)\).

If \(p>q\), reflect every coordinate by \(t\mapsto1-t\). Distances and utilities are preserved, the reflected outcome \(1-p\) is nearest to the reflected median \(1-h\), and the original inequality \(\mu([h,1])\ge1/2\) supplies the required mass on the left of the reflected median. The reflected outcomes satisfy \(1-p<1-q\), so the preceding argument applies.

Finally, \(W_\mu\) is continuous and \(F\) is compact, so an optimal feasible comparator exists. Choosing it as \(q\) proves
\(W_\mu(M_N(\mu,F))\ge\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,F)/3\).

The factor is attained by this rule: with \(N=2\), equal masses at \(1/2\) and \(1\), and \(F=\{0,1\}\), the rule chooses \(0\). Its welfare is \(1/4\), whereas the optimum at \(1\) is \(3/4\).

4. Polynomial bit complexity

The rule can be implemented using exact integer and rational arithmetic:

  • Recover each \(n_j=N\mu_j\). If \(\mu_j\) is represented as a numerator divided by a denominator, multiply its numerator by \(N\) and divide exactly by its denominator. Integrality follows from the input promise.
  • Sort the \(\tau\) rational locations, using cross multiplication for comparisons.
  • Accumulate the counts until their sum reaches \(k=\lceil N/2\rceil\), obtaining \(h\).
  • For every interval, compute its closest point to \(h\):

\(c_r=\min\{\max\{h,a_r\},b_r\}\).
Choose the candidate minimizing \(|h-c_r|\), breaking ties by the smaller candidate.

Each \(c_r\) is the closest point in its interval. Therefore the final scan finds the closest point in their union, including the prescribed tie-breaking. Overlapping intervals and singleton intervals require no special treatment.

All input integers have at most \(L\) bits. Rational comparisons and distance comparisons use a constant number of additions and multiplications on integers with \(O(L)\) bits. Recovered counts and their partial sums are at most \(N\), so they also have \(O(L)\) bits.

There are \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) sorting comparisons, \(O(\tau)\) count operations, and \(O(m)\) interval and candidate comparisons. Standard exact arithmetic therefore gives a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Moreover, the output is either the input coordinate \(h\) or an input interval endpoint. It is rational and has at most \(L\) bits of representation. No step iterates over the \(N\) residents.
