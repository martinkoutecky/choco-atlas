The continuous mirror question, as graded

Input a finite catalogue of \(m\) goods, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and additive type values \(v_{tj}\), with a common goods order. For each type define \(\sigma_t\) as the supremum of \(z\) such that a finite-support distribution \(y_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^m\) satisfies \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=1\), \(\sum_b y_{t,b}b_j=q_j\) for every good \(j\), and \(y_{t,b}>0\) only when \(v_t\cdot b\)\ge z\). For every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), can one compute a finite-support assignment \(x_{t,b}\) with \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=q_j\), and \(x_{t,b}>0\) only when \(v_t\cdot b\)\ge(10/13-\varepsilon)\sigma_t\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for that fixed \(\varepsilon\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input also comprises \(m\ge1\) goods, binary rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\ge0\), and binary rational additive values \(v_{t1}\ge\cdots\ge v_{tm}\ge0\) for every type; ties are allowed. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Bundles are vectors \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), with utility \(v_t\cdot b\); multiple units of each good are permitted.
Define \(\sigma_t\) as the supremum of \(z\ge0\) for which a finitely supported distribution \(y_{t,b}\ge0\) satisfies \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=1\), \(\sum_b y_{t,b}b_j=q_j\) for every \(j\), and \(y_{t,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t\cdot b\ge z\).
For fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<10/13\), an admissible assignment is a finite list of bundles and nonnegative binary rational masses \(x_{t,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=q_j\) for every \(j\), and \(x_{t,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t\cdot b\ge(10/13-\varepsilon)\sigma_t\). Bundle coordinates are encoded in binary.

For every fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<10/13\), can an admissible assignment always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard for some such fixed \(\varepsilon\)?

For finite societies with nonnegative additive valuations and individually listed indivisible goods, a \((10/13-\varepsilon)\)-MMS allocation is computable in polynomial time for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10423v1)); subsequent work gives an FPTAS for \((7/9-\varepsilon)\)-MMS ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13056)).
No result settling exactly the support-encoded mass assignment problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one so that per-capita supplies equal the aggregate supplies in the stated assignment equations.
  • Specified nonnegative rational supplies and nonnegative rational values, following the goods domain; zero supplies and zero values remain allowed.
  • Interpreted the common goods order as weakly decreasing values for every type, allowing ties.
  • Specified binary encoding of bundle coordinates and rational output masses; benchmark distributions may use real nonnegative probabilities.
  • Restricted fixed epsilon to positive rationals below 10/13, the nontrivial approximation range.
  • Did not impose unit MMS normalization, an upper bound on item values, or dummy-good padding: these are internal simplifications of the discrete algorithm rather than restrictions of its allocation problem.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

An admissible assignment can always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, the mass model admits a stronger result: for every rational \(0<\eta<1\), one can compute an assignment guaranteeing \((1-\eta)\sigma_t\) to every positive-mass bundle of type \(t\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\eta\).

Here, rational numbers are encoded by binary numerators and denominators. The proof uses the distributions permitted by the mass model directly; it does not require extending the cited [finite-society MMS theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10423v1).

1. Reduction to one type

Suppose that, for each type \(t\), we compute a finitely supported rational probability distribution \(y_{t,b}\) satisfying

\( \displaystyle \sum_b y_{t,b}b_j=q_j \)

and giving every supported bundle utility at least \(\beta\sigma_t\). Set

\( \displaystyle x_{t,b}=\mu_t y_{t,b}. \)

Then the type masses are correct, and

\( \displaystyle \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=\sum_t\mu_t q_j=q_j. \)

Thus it suffices to solve the following single-type problem: given nonnegative rational values \(v_j\) and rational supplies \(q_j\), compute a distribution with mean \(q\) whose minimum supported utility approximates \(\sigma\).

The construction below does not need the common ordering of values.

2. Completing unused supply and basic bounds

Completing unused supply

Suppose a finitely supported distribution on integer bundles has mean \(d\le q\), coordinatewise. It can be converted into one with mean exactly \(q\), without decreasing any supported utility.

To prove this, put \(r=q-d\), and write

\( \displaystyle r_j=k_j+f_j,\qquad k_j=\lfloor r_j\rfloor,\qquad 0\le f_j<1. \)

For a uniform parameter \(u\in[0,1]\), define an integer vector \(C(u)\) by

\( \displaystyle C_j(u)=k_j+\mathbf 1_{\{u<f_j\}}. \)

This is a finite distribution: partitioning \([0,1]\) at the distinct fractional parts \(f_j\) produces at most \(m+1\) vectors. Its mean is \(r\).

Independently add a vector from this distribution to the original bundle. The resulting mean is \(d+r=q\). Since values are nonnegative, utilities do not decrease.

When the original distribution and \(q\) are rational, the new probabilities are rational. The construction multiplies the support size by at most \(m+1\), and all arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity in the original representation.

Detecting the zero benchmark

Let

\( \displaystyle I=\{j:v_j>0\},\qquad Q=\sum_{j\in I}q_j. \)

If \(Q<1\), every distribution supported on strictly positive-utility bundles would have

\( \displaystyle \sum_{j\in I}\mathbb E[B_j]\ge1, \)

because each such integer bundle contains at least one positively valued unit. Hence \(\sigma=0\).

If \(Q\ge1\), let

\( \displaystyle w=\min_{j\in I}v_j>0. \)

Choose rational numbers \(a_j\) with \(0\le a_j\le q_j\) and \(\sum_{j\in I}a_j=1\). Such numbers can be obtained by taking supply successively until the sum reaches one. Give singleton bundle \(e_j\) probability \(a_j\), and complete unused supply as above. Every supported bundle has utility at least \(w\). Consequently,

\( \displaystyle w\le\sigma\le U:=\sum_jv_jq_j. \)

The upper bound follows because \(U\) is the expected utility of every distribution with mean \(q\).

When \(\sigma=0\), completing unused supply starting from the deterministic zero bundle already solves the single-type problem. Below, assume \(Q\ge1\).

The supremum is attained

Choose an integer \(D>0\) such that every \(Dv_j\) is integral. Every integer bundle then has utility in \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\).

For every finitely supported distribution with mean \(q\), its minimum supported utility belongs to

\( \displaystyle \left\{\frac{k}{D}:0\le k\le\lfloor DU\rfloor\right\}. \)

Indeed, its minimum is a lattice value and cannot exceed its expected utility \(U\). The set of achievable minima is nonempty and contained in this finite set. It therefore has a largest element, equal to \(\sigma\).

3. A polynomial-size approximate threshold test

Fix an integer \(c\ge2\) and a rational threshold \(z>0\). We construct a rational linear program with these properties:

  • If \(z\le\sigma\), the program is feasible.
  • From any feasible solution, we can compute a rational distribution with mean exactly \(q\), all of whose supported bundles have utility at least \((1-1/c)z\).

Binary blocks

For each \(j\in I\), define

\( \displaystyle B_j=\left\lceil\frac{z}{v_j}\right\rceil. \)

Any bundle of utility at least \(z\) contains a coordinatewise minimal subbundle of utility at least \(z\). Such a subbundle has no zero-valued units and satisfies \(b_j\le B_j\): if \(b_j>B_j\), removing one unit of good \(j\) leaves utility at least

\( \displaystyle (b_j-1)v_j\ge B_jv_j\ge z, \)

contradicting minimality.

For good \(j\), introduce binary blocks of sizes

\( \displaystyle 1,2,\ldots,2^{\lfloor\log_2 B_j\rfloor}. \)

Every integer between zero and \(B_j\) is a sum of a subset of these blocks.

Let \(K\) be the total number of blocks. Number them \(1,\ldots,K\). Block \(i\) contains \(n_i\) units of good \(j(i)\), and has utility

\( \displaystyle a_i=n_i v_{j(i)}. \)

Subsets are allowed to produce more than \(B_j\) units; this causes no problem, because they still describe legitimate bundles.

Rounding block utilities

Define

\( \displaystyle h=\frac{z}{cK},\qquad r_i=\left\lfloor\frac{\min(a_i,z)}{h}\right\rfloor,\qquad R=(c-1)K. \)

Call a block subset \(S\) accepted if \(\sum_{i\in S}r_i\ge R\).

Every accepted subset has actual utility at least

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}a_i\ge h\sum_{i\in S}r_i\ge hR=\left(1-\frac1c\right)z. \)

Conversely, any block subset with utility at least \(z\) is accepted. First,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\min(a_i,z)\ge z. \)

This holds because either one block already has utility at least \(z\), or capping changes none of the selected utilities. Rounding down loses at most \(h\) per selected block, so

\( \displaystyle h\sum_{i\in S}r_i \ge \sum_{i\in S}\min(a_i,z)-Kh \ge z-Kh=hR. \)

Thus \(\sum_{i\in S}r_i\ge R\).

A flow linear program

Use a layered directed acyclic graph with states \((i,s)\), where

\( \displaystyle 0\le i\le K,\qquad 0\le s\le R. \)

At layer \(i\), there are two edges from each preceding state:

  • a skip edge from \((i-1,s)\) to \((i,s)\);
  • a take edge from \((i-1,s)\) to \((i,\min\{R,s+r_i\})\).

Keep these edges distinct even when their endpoints coincide. Paths from \((0,0)\) to \((K,R)\) correspond exactly to accepted subsets.

The linear program asks for a nonnegative unit flow \(F\) from \((0,0)\) to \((K,R)\), with the additional constraints

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i:j(i)=j}n_i \sum_{\substack{e\text{ takes}\\\text{block }i}}F_e \le q_j \quad\text{for every }j. \)

These constraints bound expected consumption of each good.

A rational feasible flow can be decomposed into at most as many weighted paths as there are edges. Repeatedly extract a positive-flow source-to-sink path and subtract its smallest edge flow; each extraction eliminates at least one positive-flow edge. The path weights sum to one.

This gives a rational probability distribution on accepted bundles with mean at most \(q\). Complete unused supply using Section 2. Every resulting bundle has utility at least \((1-1/c)z\).

Why the program is feasible when \(z\le\sigma\)

By attainment, there is a finite distribution with mean \(q\) supported on bundles of utility at least \(z\).

Replace every bundle by a coordinatewise minimal qualifying subbundle. Expected consumption can only decrease, and each resulting coordinate is at most \(B_j\). Represent these subbundles by binary block subsets.

The rounding calculation shows that all these subsets are accepted. Their probabilities therefore induce a feasible unit flow satisfying the supply inequalities.

This proves both properties of the threshold test.

4. Choosing the threshold

Define

\( \displaystyle \rho=\frac{c}{c-1},\qquad z_k=w\rho^k. \)

Test every grid point \(z_k\le U\), and retain a feasible flow for the largest accepted grid point \(z_*\). At least \(z_0=w\) is accepted.

Let \(k_0\) be the largest index with \(z_{k_0}\le\sigma\). This grid point is tested because \(\sigma\le U\), and it is accepted by Section 3. Therefore,

\( \displaystyle z_*\ge z_{k_0}\ge\frac{\sigma}{\rho}=\left(1-\frac1c\right)\sigma. \)

The completed distribution obtained from \(z_*\) consequently guarantees

\( \displaystyle v\cdot b\ge \left(1-\frac1c\right)z_* \ge \left(1-\frac1c\right)^2\sigma \)

for every positive-probability bundle.

We scan all grid points; no monotonicity assumption about the rounded threshold test is needed.

5. Polynomial running time and output length

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility of an explicitly encoded rational linear system can be decided in polynomial time, and a feasible rational point of polynomial bit length can be returned.

Its hypotheses apply here as follows.

First, \(U/w\) has logarithm \(O(L)\). Since

\( \displaystyle \log\rho=-\log(1-1/c)\ge\frac1c, \)

there are \(O(cL+1)\) grid points. Each has a rational encoding of length \(O(L+cL\log c)\).

Second, for every tested threshold, \(z\le U\) and \(v_j\ge w\) for \(j\in I\). Hence

\( \displaystyle B_j\le\left\lceil\frac{U}{w}\right\rceil, \)

so each coordinate requires \(O(L)\) binary blocks and \(K=O(mL)\).

Third, the graph has \(O(KR)=O(cK^2)\) states and edges. Its flow constraints, supplies, and integer block sizes all have polynomial-length binary encodings. Thus the explicit linear program has size polynomial in \(m,L,c\).

Finally, flow decomposition and supply completion preserve polynomial output length. Path probabilities have polynomial bit length, each path produces binary-encoded bundle coordinates, and completion increases the number of bundles by at most a factor \(m+1\). All new probabilities and coordinates are obtained by polynomially many rational operations on polynomial-length numbers.

The single-type algorithm therefore runs in time polynomial in \(m,L,c\). Applying it to the \(\tau\) listed types and multiplying probabilities by their masses preserves polynomial running time and output length.

6. The requested guarantee

Take \(c=10\). The algorithm guarantees

\( \displaystyle v_t\cdot b\ge\frac{81}{100}\sigma_t \)

on every positive-mass bundle. Since

\( \displaystyle \frac{81}{100}>\frac{10}{13}>\frac{10}{13}-\varepsilon, \)

the resulting assignment is admissible for every fixed rational \(0<\varepsilon<10/13\).

For the stronger approximation scheme stated initially, take \(c=\lceil2/\eta\rceil\). Then

\( \displaystyle \left(1-\frac1c\right)^2 \ge1-\frac2c \ge1-\eta, \)

and the running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\eta\).

1. Reduction to independent single-type distributions

This reduction is valid. Each constructed distribution has total probability one and mean exactly \(q\). Therefore setting \(x_{t,b}=\mu_t y_{t,b}\) gives

The completion construction is valid.

Each coordinate of \(C(u)\) has expectation \(k_j+f_j=r_j\). Partitioning at the distinct fractional parts produces at most \(m+1\) outcomes; interval endpoints have probability zero and cause no difficulty.

Taking the product with the original finite distribution gives mean exactly \(q\). Because every added coordinate and every value are nonnegative, supported utilities cannot decrease.

Both cases are handled correctly.

The attainment argument is correct and does not rely on compactness of an infinite-dimensional space.

Rational valuations admit a common denominator \(D\). Every finite distribution’s minimum supported utility is therefore a lattice value, and the expected-utility bound confines it to the stated finite set.

There is at least one feasible finite distribution. Consequently, the set of achievable minima is nonempty and finite, and its largest element equals the supremum in the benchmark definition.

No bound on \(D\) is needed here: the algorithm does not enumerate these lattice values.

5. Binary representation of qualifying subbundles

The minimal-subbundle argument is valid.

Starting from a finite integer bundle, deleting units while preserving utility at least \(z\) terminates. A minimal qualifying bundle contains no zero-valued units.

If its coordinate satisfied \(b_j>B_j\), deleting one unit of good \(j\) would leave utility at least \(B_jv_j\ge z\), contradicting minimality. Hence \(b_j\le B_j\).

The binary blocks represent every integer coordinate between zero and \(B_j\). Allowing some subsets to represent larger coordinates is harmless: such subsets still represent admissible integer bundles, and the LP independently enforces their expected supply consumption.

The constants \(h=z/(cK)\) and \(R=(c-1)K\) are consistent. In the positive-benchmark branch, \(I\) is nonempty and \(K\ge1\), so division by \(K\) is legitimate.

The graph correctly records the accumulated rounded score, capped at \(R\). Its source-to-sink paths correspond exactly to accepted subsets. Distinguishing parallel take and skip edges preserves this correspondence when \(r_i=0\) or the score has already reached \(R\).

The supply constraints count the expected number of units taken from each good. Their coefficients are correct.

The graph is acyclic, so a nonnegative unit flow admits the stated path decomposition. Each extraction removes at least one positive-flow edge, giving the claimed polynomial support bound. The resulting path weights sum to one.

Completing unused supply then gives exact mean \(q\), while preserving the utility guarantee.
