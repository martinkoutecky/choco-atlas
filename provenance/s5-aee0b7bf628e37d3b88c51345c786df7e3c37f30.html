The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with masses \(\pi_t\), a normalized interaction kernel \(W_{s,t}\), learning parameters, a hypothesis set, and \(M\) samples of type-level intermediate beliefs, estimate for each ordered pair \((s,t)\) the baseline asymptotic true-hypothesis belief of type-\(t\) receivers minus their asymptotic belief when an \(\alpha\) fraction of type-\(s\) mass is persistently fixed at belief \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\pi_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq2\) hypotheses in a totally ordered set \(\Theta\).
The input kernel satisfies \(W_{u,t}\geq0\) and \(\sum_u\pi_uW_{u,t}=1\); write \(B_{u,t}=\pi_uW_{u,t}\). Its directed support is strongly connected and has a self-loop. Learning parameters are either \((\delta,\beta)=(0,1)\) or \(0<\delta<1,\ \beta>0\).
Type-level intermediate beliefs obey \(\psi_{t,i}(\theta)\propto L_t(\xi_{t,i}\mid\theta)^\beta\mu_{t,i-1}(\theta)^{1-\delta}\), followed by \(\mu_{t,i}(\theta)\propto\prod_u\psi_{u,i}(\theta)^{B_{u,t}}\). Initial beliefs are positive. Observation vectors are independent and identically distributed over time, may be dependent across types, and have finite second moments of log-likelihood ratios. Every wrong hypothesis has positive finite KL divergence from the true hypothesis at some type.
Input data consist of positive binary-rational belief vectors \(\psi_{t,i}\) for \(i=0,\ldots,M\), with \(M\geq1\). Define \(\theta_*\) as the first maximizer of \(\sum_t\pi_t\psi_{t,M}(\theta)\), \(\Lambda_{t,i}(\theta)=\log(\psi_{t,i}(\theta_*)/\psi_{t,i}(\theta))\), and the empirical innovation mean \(\widehat d_t(\theta)=(\beta M)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^M[\Lambda_{t,i}(\theta)-(1-\delta)\sum_uB_{u,t}\Lambda_{u,i-1}(\theta)]\). These empirical means may be negative.
The requested estimate is the following plug-in influence. Write \(g(z)=(1+\sum_{\theta\ne\theta_*}e^{-z(\theta)})^{-1}\). In adaptive learning, set \(b_t=g(\lim_i h_{t,i})\), where \(h_{t,i}(\theta)=\sum_uB_{u,t}[(1-\delta)h_{u,i-1}(\theta)+\beta\widehat d_u(\theta)]\) and \(h_{t,0}=0\); in non-Bayesian learning set \(b_t=1\).
The intervention input is a binary-rational fraction \(0<\alpha\leq1\) and a strictly positive binary-rational probability vector \(p\) on \(\Theta\). For each source \(s\), split its mass into fractions \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\); both retain the original kernel connections. The first fraction ignores incoming information and observations and sends \(p\) at every time.
For each source \(s\), put \(a_u=\alpha\mathbf1_{\{u=s\}}\), \(c(\theta)=\log(p(\theta_*)/p(\theta))\), and define free-receiver mean log-belief ratios by \(f_{t,i}(\theta)=\sum_uB_{u,t}[(1-a_u)((1-\delta)f_{u,i-1}(\theta)+\beta\widehat d_u(\theta))+a_uc(\theta)]\), starting from zero. Define \(\widehat C_{s,t}=b_t-[(1-a_t)g(\lim_i f_{t,i})+a_tp(\theta_*)]\); a zero-mass free cohort contributes nothing. Thus receivers include the clamped fraction when \(s=t\), and beliefs are obtained from limiting mean log-belief ratios, rather than by taking expectations of beliefs.
Given precision \(q\geq1\), the computational estimation problem is to evaluate every \(\widehat C_{s,t}\) to additive numerical error \(2^{-q}\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is this computational estimation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,M,L,q\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite-agent networks, observational causal-influence estimation has a claimed expected absolute-error bound \(O(M^{-1})\) in the 2023 preprint; the [published version](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume27/23-0910/23-0910.pdf) states \(O(M^{-1/2})\), under sufficiently small network-estimation errors and forgetting parameters. Neither statement establishes a bit-complexity bound. No result settling the specified mass-intervention computational problem was located.

Conclusion and input convention

The specified plug-in influence is computable in polynomial time under the standard rational-input interpretation. The algorithm solves rational linear systems and then evaluates logarithms and a normalized exponential expression with controlled precision.

One encoding detail needs to be stated: I interpret the kernel entries and the forgetting parameter \(\delta\) as binary-rational inputs included in \(L\), or as fixed rational constants. Without an effective representation of these quantities, a Turing bit-complexity question is not defined. The parameter \(\beta\) cancels from the requested calculation.

The proof below permits negative empirical innovation means and includes the clamped cohort in the receiver’s belief.

1. Rewrite the recurrences as linear systems

Let \(P=B^{\mathsf T}\), so that \(P_{t,u}=B_{u,t}\). The kernel normalization gives

\(P_{t,u}\geq0,\qquad \sum_uP_{t,u}=1.\)

Thus \(P\) is row-stochastic. Its support is strongly connected: transposition reverses all support edges and preserves strong connectivity.

The distinguished hypothesis \(\theta_*\) can be found by exact rational comparisons of the scores \(\sum_t\pi_t\psi_{t,M}(\theta)\), resolving ties by the given order.

Write \(r=1-\delta\). For each \(\theta\ne\theta_*\), regard \(\Lambda_i(\theta)\) as a column vector indexed by types, and define

\(v(\theta)=\beta\widehat d(\theta)=\dfrac1M\sum_{i=1}^M\bigl[\Lambda_i(\theta)-rP\Lambda_{i-1}(\theta)\bigr].\)

This identity shows that \(\beta\) cancels exactly.

For source \(s\), let \(a^{(s)}=\alpha e_s\) and \(D_s=\operatorname{diag}(1-a^{(s)}_u)\). The recurrences become

\(h_i(\theta)=rPh_{i-1}(\theta)+Pv(\theta)\)

and

\(f_i^{(s)}(\theta)=rPD_sf_{i-1}^{(s)}(\theta)+PD_sv(\theta)+Pa^{(s)}c(\theta).\)

Whenever the inverses exist and the recurrences converge, their limits are therefore

\(h^\infty(\theta)=(I-rP)^{-1}Pv(\theta)\)

and

\(f^{(s),\infty}(\theta)=(I-rPD_s)^{-1}\bigl[PD_sv(\theta)+Pa^{(s)}c(\theta)\bigr].\)

The first formula is needed only in the adaptive case. In the non-Bayesian case, the specified baseline is \(b_t=1\).

2. Prove convergence, including without forgetting

Use the induced infinity norm. Because \(P\) is row-stochastic, \(\|P\|_\infty=1\).

In adaptive learning, \(0<\delta<1\), hence \(0<r<1\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle (I-rP)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^\infty(rP)^j,\qquad \|(I-rP)^{-1}\|_\infty\leq\frac1\delta.\)

This proves convergence of the baseline recurrence.

For interventions, convergence holds even when \(\delta=0\). Let

\(\eta=\min\{P_{t,u}:P_{t,u}>0\},\qquad Q_s=PD_s.\)

The matrix \(Q_s\) describes a substochastic transition rule: first make a transition according to \(P\), then, upon arriving at \(s\), remove a fraction \(\alpha\) of the mass.

From every state different from \(s\), strong connectivity supplies a simple path to \(s\) of length at most \(\tau-1\). From \(s\), it supplies a positive-length return cycle of length at most \(\tau\). When \(\tau=1\), the stochastic matrix is \(P=(1)\), and this return takes one step.

Choose such a path or cycle with no visit to \(s\) before its endpoint. If its length is \(\ell\), following it and being removed at its endpoint has probability at least

\(\alpha\eta^\ell\geq\alpha\eta^\tau.\)

Therefore, from every starting state, at least this much mass has been removed by time \(\tau\). It follows that

\(\|Q_s^\tau\|_\infty\leq1-\kappa,\qquad \kappa=\alpha\eta^\tau>0.\)

Also \(\|Q_s^j\|_\infty\leq1\) for every nonnegative integer \(j\). Since \(0\leq r\leq1\), the same bounds hold for powers of \(A_s=rQ_s\). Writing every exponent as \(k\tau+j\), with \(0\leq j<\tau\), gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty\|A_s^\ell\|_\infty \leq\sum_{k=0}^\infty\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}(1-\kappa)^k =\frac{\tau}{\kappa}.\)

Thus \(A_s^\ell\) tends to zero, and its Neumann series converges. In particular,

\(\displaystyle (I-rPD_s)^{-1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty A_s^\ell,\qquad \|(I-rPD_s)^{-1}\|_\infty\leq\frac{\tau}{\alpha\eta^\tau}.\)

Starting from zero, the intervention recurrence is the corresponding finite partial sum applied to its constant forcing vector. This proves convergence and justifies the formula for \(f^{(s),\infty}\).

The argument covers \(\alpha=1\). Although the formula still computes a free-cohort value at the source, its coefficient in the requested receiver belief is then zero.

3. Bound the precision needed for the linear systems

An exponentially large inverse norm does not by itself require exponential running time. What matters here is its logarithm.

For standard binary-rational encodings of total length \(L\), every positive encoded rational is at least \(2^{-L}\). Since \(P_{t,u}=\pi_uW_{u,t}\), every positive entry of \(P\) is at least \(2^{-2L}\). Hence

\(\eta\geq2^{-2L},\qquad \alpha\geq2^{-L},\)

and, in the adaptive case, \(\delta\geq2^{-L}\). The preceding inverse bounds imply

\(\|(I-rPD_s)^{-1}\|_\infty\leq\tau\,2^{(2\tau+1)L},\qquad \|(I-rP)^{-1}\|_\infty\leq2^L.\)

For definiteness, set

\(H=(2\tau+1)(L+1)+\lceil\log_2(\tau+1)\rceil,\qquad K=2^H.\)

Every inverse used by the algorithm has infinity norm at most \(K\), and \(H\) is polynomially bounded.

Approximate every \(\Lambda_{t,i}(\theta)\) and \(c(\theta)\) by a dyadic rational with absolute error at most

\(\varepsilon=2^{-(q+H+6)}.\)

Use these approximations to construct \(\widetilde v(\theta)\). Stochasticity, \(r\leq1\), and averaging over \(M\) give

\(\|\widetilde v(\theta)-v(\theta)\|_\infty\leq2\varepsilon.\)

Now solve the linear systems exactly over the rationals, using these approximate logarithmic inputs. Their solutions satisfy

\(\|\widetilde h^\infty(\theta)-h^\infty(\theta)\|_\infty\leq2K\varepsilon.\)

For interventions, \(\|PD_s\|_\infty\leq1\) and \(\|Pa^{(s)}\|_\infty\leq1\), so the forcing-vector error is at most \(3\varepsilon\). Therefore,

\(\|\widetilde f^{(s),\infty}(\theta)-f^{(s),\infty}(\theta)\|_\infty\leq3K\varepsilon.\)

These estimates require no sign restriction on \(v(\theta)\).

4. Prove that the rational computations take polynomial time

All coefficient matrices are rational, with entry bit lengths polynomial in \(L\). Their inverses can be computed exactly in polynomial bit complexity.

To spell out the size bound, clear denominators in a coefficient matrix to obtain an integer matrix \(E\). A common denominator formed by multiplying all entry denominators has polynomial bit length, so the entries of \(E\) also have polynomial bit length.

If an integer matrix has entries of magnitude at most \(2^S\), every minor of order \(k\leq\tau\) has magnitude at most

\(k!\,2^{kS},\)

by the determinant expansion. Its bit length is therefore \(O(kS+k\log k)\). The determinant is nonzero by Section 2. The adjugate formula consequently shows that every entry of the inverse has polynomial bit length.

Integer determinants can be computed by fraction-free Gaussian elimination, with row exchanges when necessary. Its intermediate quantities are minors, so the preceding bounds give polynomial bit complexity. Computing the inverse through determinants and cofactors is therefore also polynomial.

The logarithms can likewise be approximated in polynomial time. For a positive rational \(x\), find an integer \(k\) such that \(y=2^{-k}x\) lies in \([1,2)\). Then

\(\log x=k\log2+\log y,\qquad \log y=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2j+1}}{2j+1},\qquad t=\frac{y-1}{y+1}.\)

Here \(0\leq t<1/3\), and the same series with \(t=1/3\) computes \(\log2\). The tail after \(J\) terms is at most \(9^{-J}\). Thus obtaining absolute error \(2^{-n}\), including the multiplication by \(k\) and final dyadic rounding, takes \(O(n+\log(|k|+1))\) terms. For the input ratios, \(|k|=O(L)\), and all intermediate rational bit lengths are polynomial.

Apply this with \(n=q+H+6\). The exact rational solutions constructed in Section 3 then have polynomial bit lengths as well.

5. Evaluate the belief function without enormous exponentials

Two facts control the last numerical step.

First, \(g\) is globally Lipschitz in the infinity norm. Direct differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial g}{\partial z(\theta)}=g(z)^2e^{-z(\theta)},\qquad \sum_{\theta\ne\theta_*}\left|\frac{\partial g}{\partial z(\theta)}\right| =g(z)(1-g(z))\leq\frac14.\)

Integrating along the line segment between two vectors yields

\(|g(z)-g(z')|\leq\frac14\|z-z'\|_\infty.\)

Consequently, replacing the exact limiting ratios by those from Section 3 changes any requested influence by at most

\(\displaystyle \frac14(2K\varepsilon+3K\varepsilon) \leq\frac5{256}\,2^{-q}.\)

In the non-Bayesian case the baseline is exact, so this bound remains valid.

Second, evaluating \(g\) does not require forming exponentials of large positive numbers. Introduce \(m\) coordinates

\(x_*=0,\qquad x_\theta=-z(\theta),\qquad A=\max_jx_j.\)

Then

\(\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{e^{x_*-A}}{\sum_je^{x_j-A}}.\)

All exponents are nonpositive, and at least one denominator term is exactly \(1\).

Suppose each exponential is approximated within \(\eta_0=2^{-n}\). Clip its approximation to \([0,1]\), and retain a maximizing term as exactly \(1\). Both the exact and approximate denominators are at least \(1\). The resulting ratio error is at most

\((m+1)\eta_0.\)

Indeed, the numerator error is at most \(\eta_0\), the denominator error is at most \(m\eta_0\), and the approximate numerator is at most \(1\).

These exponential approximations are polynomial-time computations:

  • If an exponent \(w<-n\), replace \(e^w\) by zero. Its error is smaller than \(2^{-n}\).
  • If \(-n\leq w\leq0\), use the Taylor polynomial of degree \(N-1\), where \(N\) is even and \(N\geq8n^2\). Taylor’s theorem bounds its error by \(n^N/N!\). Since \(N!\geq(N/2)^{N/2}\), this error is at most \((2n^2/N)^{N/2}\leq2^{-N}\leq2^{-n}\).

There are polynomially many rational operations, on operands of polynomial bit length.

Taking \(n=q+\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil+4\) evaluates each occurrence of \(g\) within \(2^{-(q+4)}\).

6. Assemble every requested influence

For every source and receiver, compute

\(\displaystyle \widetilde C_{s,t} =\widetilde b_t-\bigl[(1-a_t^{(s)})\,\widetilde g(\widetilde f_t^{(s),\infty}) +a_t^{(s)}p(\theta_*)\bigr].\)

Use \(\widetilde b_t=1\) in non-Bayesian learning and the approximated adaptive baseline otherwise.

The logarithmic-input error is at most \((5/256)2^{-q}\). The two belief evaluations contribute at most \(2^{-(q+3)}\), because their coefficients have magnitude at most \(1\). Finally, round each output to a dyadic rational with rounding error at most \(2^{-(q+2)}\). The total error is strictly smaller than \(2^{-q}\).

The algorithm uses one adaptive baseline inverse and one intervention inverse per source. Each inverse is reused across all wrong hypotheses. Computing innovations, applying these inverses, and evaluating the \(\tau^2\) output entries requires polynomially many operations of polynomial bit complexity. All matrix dimensions depend on the support size \(\tau\).

Thus the total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,M,L,q\).
