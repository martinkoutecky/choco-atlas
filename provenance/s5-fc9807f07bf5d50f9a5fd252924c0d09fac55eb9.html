The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings with support size \(\tau\), an initial cohort type \(t_0\) with available mass \(\beta\leq\mu_{t_0}\), a target \(p\in C\), and rational bounds \(B\geq0\) and \(\eta>0\), does there exist a mass redistribution \(y_t\geq0\) with \(\sum_t y_t=\beta\) such that \(\nu_t=\mu_t-\beta\mathbf{1}[t=t_0]+y_t\), \(\sum_t y_t d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,t)\leq B\), and \(M_\nu(p)\geq M_\nu(c)+\eta\) for every \(c\ne p\), where \(M_\nu(c)=\min_{d\ne c}\sum_t\nu_t\mathbf{1}[c\succ_t d]\)?

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Require \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and set unlisted masses to zero.
Input also includes a supported ranking \(t_0\), rational cohort mass \(0<\beta\leq\mu_{t_0}\), target \(p\in C\), and rational margin \(\eta>0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), including the binary encodings of \(\beta,\eta\) and any budget \(B\geq0\).
The cohort may split into arbitrary nonnegative real masses \(y_t\), over all \(t\in\mathcal R(C)\), with \(\sum_t y_t=\beta\); destinations need not belong to the original support. Set \(\nu_t=\mu_t-\beta\mathbf1[t=t_0]+y_t\).
Use Kendall swap distance \(d_{\mathrm{swap}}\) with unit adjacent-swap costs, and define \(M_\nu(c)=\min_{d\in C\setminus\{c\}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal R(C)}\nu_t\mathbf1[c\succ_t d]\).
A redistribution is feasible when \(M_\nu(p)\geq M_\nu(c)+\eta\) for every \(c\ne p\); ties therefore do not suffice. Its cost is \(K(y)=\sum_t y_t d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,t)\). Minimize this cost, assigning optimum \(+\infty\) when infeasible; the budget question asks whether the optimum is at most \(B\).

Conclusion

Approximation within \(\delta\log m\) is NP-hard for some constant \(\delta>0\). Consequently, unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), the continuous problem has no polynomial-time \(o(\log m)\)-approximation.

The proof transfers the stated finite-electorate hardness through an exact rounding lemma. On the constructed instances, allowing arbitrary real splitting does not reduce the optimum.

1. The finite hardness result used

For an unweighted electorate with one manipulator, let \(D^*\) be the minimum Kendall distance from the manipulator’s original ranking to a ranking making \(p\) the unique Maximin winner, with \(D^*=+\infty\) if none exists.

We use the supplied hardness result with one restriction verified from its proof: the manipulator’s original ranking places \(p\) first. Indeed, the construction in Theorem 5.2.7 uses an original ranking beginning with \(p\). Thus logarithmic inapproximability already holds under this restriction. The source also formulates approximation as returning a value between the optimum and its prescribed multiplicative upper bound. [Obraztsova, Remark 5.1.4 and Theorem 5.2.7](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/theses/phd-obraztsova.pdf#page=78).

The remaining argument proves that these restricted finite instances embed into the continuous problem without an approximation loss.

2. An exact rounding lemma

Fix integer pairwise counts \(a(c,d)\) from the nonmanipulating voters. Let the manipulator’s reference ranking \(t_0\) place \(p\) first.

Replace the manipulator temporarily by a probability distribution \(q\) over rankings. Define

\(q(c,d)=\sum_t q_t\mathbf1[c\succ_t d]\),

\(S_q(c)=\min_{d\ne c}\bigl(a(c,d)+q(c,d)\bigr)\),

and

\(E(q)=\sum_t q_t d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,t)\).

Call \(q\) successful when

\(S_q(p)\ge S_q(c)+1\qquad(c\ne p)\).

Lemma. Every successful distribution \(q\), including one with arbitrary real probabilities, can be replaced by a single successful ranking of distance at most \(E(q)\). Consequently,

\(\min_{q\text{ successful}}E(q)=D^*\),

including equality when both sides are \(+\infty\).

Proof

For each ranking \(t\), let \(P(t)\) move \(p\) to first place while preserving the relative order of all other candidates. Let \(q^\uparrow\) be the distribution obtained by this operation.

First, promotion preserves success and cannot increase cost.

Promotion increases each pairwise contribution of \(p\). For another candidate \(c\), it preserves contributions against opponents other than \(p\) and weakly decreases the contribution against \(p\). Therefore,

\(S_{q^\uparrow}(p)\ge S_q(p)\),

\(S_{q^\uparrow}(c)\le S_q(c)\qquad(c\ne p)\).

Thus \(q^\uparrow\) is successful.

Kendall distance equals the number of pairs ordered differently: each adjacent swap changes one pair, and sorting by adjacent swaps attains this count. Because \(t_0\) places \(p\) first, promotion removes exactly the disagreements involving candidates above \(p\), while preserving every other pair. Hence

\(d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,P(t))\le d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,t)\),

so \(E(q^\uparrow)\le E(q)\).

Second, every positive-mass ranking of \(q^\uparrow\) is successful individually.

Set

\(s=\min_{d\ne p}a(p,d)\).

Every ranking of \(q^\uparrow\) places \(p\) first, so

\(S_{q^\uparrow}(p)=s+1\).

Success therefore implies \(S_{q^\uparrow}(c)\le s\) for every \(c\ne p\). Choose an opponent \(d_c\ne c\) attaining this minimum. Then

\(a(c,d_c)+q^\uparrow(c,d_c)\le s\).

Both \(a(c,d_c)\) and \(s\) are integers, and \(q^\uparrow(c,d_c)\) is nonnegative. There are only two possibilities:

  • If \(a(c,d_c)\le s-1\), then every ranking \(r\) satisfies

\(a(c,d_c)+\mathbf1[c\succ_r d_c]\le s\).

  • If \(a(c,d_c)=s\), the displayed inequality forces \(q^\uparrow(c,d_c)=0\). Since its summands are nonnegative, every ranking \(r\) with \(q^\uparrow_r>0\) ranks \(d_c\) above \(c\), again giving

\(a(c,d_c)+\mathbf1[c\succ_r d_c]\le s\).

The possibility \(a(c,d_c)>s\) is excluded by the inequality.

Thus every positive-mass ranking \(r\) gives every rival a Maximin score at most \(s\), while giving \(p\) score \(s+1\). Each such ranking is individually successful.

There are finitely many rankings. Choose a positive-mass ranking \(r\) of minimum distance. Then

\(d_{\mathrm{swap}}(t_0,r)\le E(q^\uparrow)\le E(q)\).

This proves the rounding assertion. Conversely, every successful single ranking is a successful point-mass distribution. The equality of optima, and equivalence of infeasibility, follow. \(\square\)

3. Embedding into the specified continuous model

Take a finite hard instance with \(N\) voters, including the manipulator. Let \(n_t\) count all voters originally having ranking \(t\), so \(\sum_t n_t=N\).

To make the encoding valid even if “binary rational” requires dyadic masses, choose \(D\) to be the smallest power of two satisfying \(D\ge N\). Set

\(\varepsilon=\frac{D-N}{2D}\).

Choose any ranking \(r\), and let \(\bar r\) be its reverse. Define

\(\mu_t=\frac{n_t}{D}+\varepsilon\mathbf1[t=r]+\varepsilon\mathbf1[t=\bar r]\),

and choose

\(\beta=\eta=\frac1D\).

These are valid inputs:

  • Every mass is a nonnegative dyadic rational.
  • The masses sum to \(N/D+2\varepsilon=1\).
  • Since the original manipulator has ranking \(t_0\), we have \(\mu_{t_0}\ge1/D=\beta\).
  • After combining identical rankings and omitting zero masses, the support size satisfies \(\tau\le N+2\).

Let \(a(c,d)\) again denote the integer counts from the original nonmanipulators. For any allowed redistribution \(y\), define \(q_t=Dy_t\). Then \(q\) is a probability distribution.

Exactly one of \(r,\bar r\) ranks \(c\) above \(d\). Consequently,

\(\sum_t\nu_t\mathbf1[c\succ_t d]=\varepsilon+\frac{a(c,d)+q(c,d)}D\).

Taking minima gives

\(M_\nu(c)=\varepsilon+\frac{S_q(c)}D\).

Since \(\eta=1/D\), continuous feasibility is exactly

\(S_q(p)\ge S_q(c)+1\qquad(c\ne p)\).

Moreover,

\(K(y)=\frac{E(q)}D\).

The rounding lemma therefore proves the exact identity

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{continuous}}=\frac{D^*}{D}\).

This also preserves infeasibility. For any nonnegative integer budget \(k\), choosing \(B=k/D\) gives

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{continuous}}\le B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D^*\le k\).

4. Encoding, running time, and approximation transfer

Candidate identifiers require \(O(\log m)\) bits. All constructed mass numerators and denominators have \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits. Thus the constructed input length satisfies

\(L=O\bigl((\tau+1)(m\log m+\log(N+1))\bigr)\).

Including a budget \(B=k/D\) adds \(O(\log(k+1)+\log(N+1))\) bits. In particular, the encodings of \(\beta,\eta\), and \(B\) are explicitly included.

The transformation uses \(\operatorname{poly}(m,N,\log(k+1))\) bit operations: combine identical rankings, compute the dyadic masses, and write the support list. The original finite hardness construction has an explicitly listed electorate of polynomial size, so this remains a polynomial reduction.

For comparison, a proposed single destination ranking can be checked for feasibility and budget in \(O((\tau+1)m^2L^2)\) bit operations using schoolbook rational arithmetic.

Now suppose the continuous problem had a \(\rho(m)\)-approximation running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time. Apply it to the constructed instance and multiply its returned cost by \(D\). The exact identity above gives a value \(v\) satisfying

\(D^*\le v\le\rho(m)D^*\).

This would approximate the finite problem with the same factor and the same number of candidates. If the algorithm returns an explicit redistribution, the rounding lemma also produces a successful single ranking with the required cost bound.

The restricted finite hardness result therefore transfers directly: for some constant \(\delta>0\), approximation within \(\delta\log m\) is NP-hard. An \(o(\log m)\)-approximation would violate this bound for all sufficiently large \(m\).
