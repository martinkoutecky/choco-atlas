The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite college set \(C\), per-capita capacities \(q_c\), and a population measure over strict rankings and exam scores \(x\in[0,1]\), represented by rational piecewise-constant score densities for each supported ranking, define atomless TCDM by having each college hold the highest-score applicant mass up to capacity each round. Applicants who remain held retain their applications; rejected applicants apply to their most-preferred attainable option using the prior round’s cutoffs. Compute the exact allocation measure after \(T\) rounds and determine whether the unbounded process reaches a stable allocation in finitely many rounds, with a bound in terms of the college count, supported rankings, density intervals, and input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) colleges with positive binary-rational capacities \(q_c\), measured relative to total population mass \(1\); every applicant is eligible everywhere.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) strict ranking types over \(C\cup\{\varnothing\}\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(p_r\), where \(\sum_r p_r=1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Each type additionally has a conditional score density \(g_r\) on \([0,1]\), nonnegative and piecewise constant with binary-rational endpoints and heights, integrating to \(1\). Write \(K\) for the total number of density intervals and \(L\) for the bit length of the binary input data.
The population measure is \(d\nu(r,x)=p_rg_r(x)\,dx\). All colleges prioritize higher scores; equal-score sets have zero mass. Allocations and application profiles are identified up to \(\nu\)-null sets.
In round \(1\), every applicant chooses her highest-ranked option in \(C\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Each college holds its highest-score current applicants, of mass equal to the smaller of its capacity and applicant mass; rejected applicants and those choosing \(\varnothing\) are unassigned.
After round \(t\), college \(c\)'s cutoff \(f_c^t\) is \(0\) if its held mass is below \(q_c\), and otherwise the essential infimum of its held scores. In round \(t+1\), held applicants retain their applications; every unassigned applicant chooses her highest-ranked option in \(\{c:x\geq f_c^t\}\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Applications are simultaneous and replace previous applications.
An allocation is stable if, for almost every applicant, her assignment is weakly preferred to \(\varnothing\), and every college she strictly prefers to her assignment is full and holds zero mass with scores strictly below hers. Finite termination means that a stable allocation is reached in a finite round and thereafter applications do not change almost everywhere.
For an input horizon \(T\geq1\), encoded in unary, the exact round-\(T\) allocation is the joint measure on ranking, score, and assigned option, including \(\varnothing\).

Are exact round-\(T\) allocation computation and deciding finite termination polynomial-time solvable, or is either NP-hard?
State running times in \(m,\tau,K,L,T\) for allocation computation and \(m,\tau,K,L\) for termination decision; what uniform bound in \(m,\tau,K,L\), if any, holds for finite termination times?

With finitely many applicants and common strict priorities, straightforward play reaches the unique stable matching within the number of applicants, giving a polynomial-time simulation ([Chen, Pereyra, and Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.12179v2)).
A related continuum DA model explicitly assumes finite completion while acknowledging that convergence may occur only in the limit; it does not establish termination for this cutoff-skipping process ([Dreyfuss et al.](https://falk.huji.ac.il/sites/default/files/falkheb/files/dreyfuss_glicksohn_heffetz_romm.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one, interpreting per-capita capacities as positive binary-rational masses.
  • Treated supported types as rankings, with conditional score densities attached to each type; specified rational type masses, density endpoints, and density heights.
  • Included the outside option in every strict ranking and made it always attainable, preserving the baseline domain without imposing universal acceptability or sufficient aggregate capacity.
  • Specified round-one behavior and simultaneous replacement of applications; all applicants remain eligible at every college.
  • Extended the full-college minimum-score cutoff to the essential infimum of held scores, retaining cutoff zero for colleges below capacity.
  • Interpreted allocations, stability, and cessation of revisions almost everywhere, so null score ties and interval endpoints require no consequential tie-breaking.
  • Interpreted exact allocation measure as the joint measure retaining ranking, score, and assignment, rather than only college totals.
  • Encoded the otherwise unspecified horizon in unary so polynomial-time round computation is measured in the explicit number of rounds.
  • Asked for a bound on finite termination times without presupposing that every instance terminates.

Result

Both tasks are polynomial-time solvable. In fact, every valid instance terminates:

  • The allocation is stable by round \(m\).
  • Applications are constant almost everywhere from round \(m+1\) onward.

Both bounds are sharp. They are independent of \(\tau,K,L\).

For exact allocation computation, let \(S=\min\{T,m\}\). A conservative bit-operation bound is

\(O\!\left(T+\left[K\log(K+2)+m\tau S(K+mS)\right](mS+1)^2L^2\right).\)

Finite termination decision simply returns “yes” on every promised valid input: constant time, or \(O(L)\) if reading the input is charged.

1. Basic properties

All sets and statements below are understood up to \(\nu\)-null sets. Write \(H_c^t\) for the applicants held by college \(c\) after round \(t\), and \(E_t\) for the applicants who apply to a college and are rejected in that round.

Absolute continuity of the score distributions implies that every score level has zero mass. Consequently:

  • A rejected applicant at college \(c\) has score strictly below \(f_c^t\), almost everywhere.
  • If \(c\) rejects positive mass, it is full.
  • A full college holds only scores at least its cutoff, almost everywhere.

These properties remain valid when densities vanish on intervals: the cutoff is the essential infimum of the held scores.

Cutoffs are nondecreasing

If \(c\) is full after round \(t\), its entire held set, of mass \(q_c\), retains its application in the next round. Those applicants have scores at least \(f_c^t\). Selecting the highest-score applicants therefore leaves \(c\) full, with cutoff at least \(f_c^t\).

If \(c\) is below capacity, its cutoff is zero, so the same inequality holds automatically. Thus

\(f_c^{t+1}\geq f_c^t\)

for every college and round.

Applicants who choose the outside option stay there

When an unassigned applicant chooses \(\varnothing\), every college she prefers to \(\varnothing\) is unavailable at her score. Since cutoffs never decrease, those colleges remain unavailable. She therefore chooses \(\varnothing\) in every subsequent round.

Accordingly, all new applications to colleges in round \(t+1\) come from \(E_t\).

Held applicants cannot block with a preferred college

Suppose an applicant is held by \(c\) after round \(t\), and consider the round \(s\) when she chose that application.

If \(s=1\), she chose her highest-ranked option overall. If \(s>1\), every college \(d\) she prefers to \(c\) was unavailable then, so her score \(x\) satisfies

\(x<f_d^{s-1}\leq f_d^t.\)

Such a college is full and holds zero mass below her score. Also, every held applicant prefers her assignment to \(\varnothing\), because the outside option was available when she chose her application.

The same reasoning shows that applicants currently choosing \(\varnothing\) cannot block. Hence only the currently rejected applicants can prevent stability.

2. A college with the largest remaining cutoff stops changing

Call a college *frozen* after round \(t\) if it receives no new applications in subsequent rounds and its held set remains unchanged.

We first establish a cutoff bound.

Lemma: entrants below a score bound cannot push a cutoff above it

Suppose \(f_d^t\leq M\), where \(M\geq0\), and every new applicant to \(d\) in round \(t+1\) has score strictly below \(M\). Then

\(f_d^{t+1}\leq M.\)

Indeed, suppose instead that \(f_d^{t+1}>M\). Then \(d\) is full and all its new held applicants have scores above \(M\). None can be a new entrant, so

\(H_d^{t+1}\subseteq H_d^t.\)

The left-hand set has mass \(q_d\), while the right-hand set has mass at most \(q_d\). They must therefore agree almost everywhere. Their cutoffs agree as well, contradicting \(f_d^t\leq M<f_d^{t+1}\).

Freezing lemma

Suppose some colleges are already frozen before round \(t\), and let \(R\) be the nonempty set of remaining colleges. Set

\(M=\max_{d\in R}f_d^t.\)

Previously frozen colleges generate no rejections in round \(t\). Thus every member of \(E_t\) was rejected by a college in \(R\), and has score strictly below \(M\).

If \(M=0\), this means \(E_t\) is null. There are then no further new applications: held applicants retain their applications, and outside-option applicants stay outside. The process has stopped.

Suppose \(M>0\), and choose \(c\in R\) with \(f_c^t=M\). This college is full. We claim that it freezes after round \(t\).

To prove the claim, induct on subsequent rounds while maintaining:

  • every currently rejected applicant has score below \(M\);
  • every college in \(R\) has cutoff at most \(M\);
  • college \(c\) retains its round-\(t\) held set.

The first two properties hold initially. In the next round, every new application comes from a rejected applicant with score below \(M\). Such an applicant cannot apply to \(c\), whose cutoff is \(M\). Consequently \(c\) retains exactly its held set and cutoff.

For every other college in \(R\), the preceding lemma keeps its cutoff at most \(M\). Its rejected applicants consequently have scores below \(M\). Previously frozen colleges generate no rejections. This proves the induction.

Therefore, each round either stops the process or freezes at least one additional college.

3. Stability and application bounds

Apply the freezing lemma starting in round \(1\), with no previously frozen colleges.

If the process stops before round \(m\), its allocation is stable: there are no rejected applicants, and Section 1 excludes blocking by all other applicants.

Otherwise, at least one additional college freezes in each round. By the end of round \(m\), every college is frozen.

Consider round \(m+1\). No college can receive a new application. Therefore every applicant in \(E_m\) must choose \(\varnothing\). By the application rule, this means that, at her score, every college she prefers to \(\varnothing\) is unavailable under the round-\(m\) cutoffs.

Thus every applicant in \(E_m\) also fails to block the round-\(m\) allocation. Together with Section 1, this proves that the allocation is stable by round \(m\).

In round \(m+1\), college applications consist exactly of retained holders; all other applicants choose \(\varnothing\). There are no further rejections or application changes. Hence applications are constant from round \(m+1\) onward.

This distinction matters under the stated definition: a rejected applicant may still replace her final college application with \(\varnothing\) one round after the allocation becomes stable.

Sharpness

Take one ranking type with uniform scores and preferences

\(c_1\succ c_2\succ\cdots\succ c_m\succ\varnothing.\)

Give every college capacity \(q=2^{-(m+1)}\). These are valid dyadic capacities, and \(mq<1\).

In round \(t\), for \(1\leq t\leq m\), college \(c_t\) receives the applicants rejected in the preceding round and holds the next highest-score mass \(q\). Specifically, college \(c_j\), for \(j\leq t\), holds scores in

\([1-jq,\;1-(j-1)q]\),

with endpoints irrelevant to the allocation.

Before round \(m\), positive unassigned mass strictly prefers an empty college, so the allocation is unstable. In round \(m\), the allocation is stable. However, the remaining rejected mass \(1-mq>0\) still applies to \(c_m\). It changes its application to \(\varnothing\) in round \(m+1\).

Thus the stable-allocation bound \(m\) and the application-stabilization bound \(m+1\) are both attained, even with \(\tau=K=1\).

4. Exact allocation computation

It suffices to simulate \(S=\min\{T,m\}\) rounds, because the allocation is constant after round \(m\).

Define the joint score density of type \(r\) by

\(h_r(x)=p_rg_r(x).\)

An exact output representation consists of a rational partition into score intervals, together with an assigned-option label for each type on each interval. The joint measure on an interval \(I_j\), with density \(h_{rj}\) and assignment label \(a_{rj}\), is

\(\nu_T(\{r\}\times dx\times\{a\})=h_{rj}\mathbf{1}_{\{a=a_{rj}\}}\,dx,\qquad x\in I_j.\)

Labels include \(\varnothing\), so this represents the requested entire allocation.

Number of intervals and arithmetic operations

Start with all density endpoints and the endpoints \(0,1\). There are \(O(K)\) intervals in the resulting common partition.

Every round adds at most one cutoff per college. All previous assignment labels, eligibility decisions, and densities are constant between the accumulated endpoints. After \(S\) rounds, the common partition therefore has

\(O(K+mS)\)

intervals.

For each round:

  • Retain applications of held applicants.
  • For every other type-interval pair, scan its ranking to choose the highest attainable option.
  • Aggregate applicant densities by college and interval.
  • Scan scores downward to select each college’s highest-score mass up to capacity.
  • Insert the resulting cutoffs and update assignment labels.

These steps use \(O(m\tau(K+mS))\) arithmetic operations per round. Including initial sorting, the total is

\(O\!\left(K\log(K+2)+m\tau S(K+mS)\right).\)

Rational bit lengths remain polynomial

A separate denominator argument is needed; a polynomial number of rational operations alone would not suffice.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all rational input numbers. Then \(\log D=O(L)\). Every joint density can be written as

\(h_r(x)=a/D^2\),

where \(a\) is a nonnegative integer of \(O(L)\) bits. Capacities and initial endpoints also have denominators dividing \(D^2\).

Suppose the current endpoints share denominator \(Q\). On any current interval, a college’s applicant density has the form

\(A/D^2\),

where \(A\) is a sum of at most \(\tau\) input density integers. Hence \(A\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

When a cutoff falls inside an interval with positive applicant density, let its upper endpoint be \(b/Q\), and let the remaining mass to be selected there be \(u/(D^2Q)\). Its cutoff is exactly

\(\frac{b}{Q}-\frac{u/(D^2Q)}{A/D^2}=\frac{Ab-u}{AQ}.\)

Thus its denominator divides \(AQ\). Zero-density intervals are skipped. If the desired mass ends exactly at an interval boundary, scanning the positive-density intervals from above gives the correct essential-infimum cutoff.

All cutoffs in one round can therefore be accommodated by multiplying \(Q\) by at most \(m\) positive integers \(A\), each of \(O(L)\) bits. Starting with \(Q=D^2\), after \(S\) rounds,

\(\log Q=O((mS+1)L).\)

The endpoints, intermediate masses, and other arithmetic quantities have this same polynomial bit bound. Using schoolbook integer arithmetic gives the stated running time:

\(O\!\left(T+\left[K\log(K+2)+m\tau S(K+mS)\right](mS+1)^2L^2\right).\)

The \(O(T)\) term accounts for reading the unary horizon. The represented output also has polynomial bit length.

5. Complexity conclusion

Exact round-\(T\) allocation computation is polynomial-time solvable, with simulation requiring at most \(m\) rounds.

Finite termination decision is trivial on the promised input class: every instance terminates. Its uniform termination bound is \(m+1\), with the stable allocation already reached by round \(m\).
