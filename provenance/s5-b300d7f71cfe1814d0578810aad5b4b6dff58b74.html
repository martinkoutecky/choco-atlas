The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given projects \(P\), lecturers \(L\), an offer map \(\lambda:P\to L\), and a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), each type specifies a complete student preference order with ties and acceptable projects, and each lecturer specifies a preference tier for each type. Project and lecturer capacities \(\kappa_p,\delta_\ell\) are rational fractions of the population. An allocation has variables \(x_{tp}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_p x_{tp}\le\mu_t\), respecting capacities and acceptability; each individual still receives at most one project. For type \(t\) and project \(p\), let \(d_{tp}=\mu_t-\sum_{q:\,q\succeq_t p}x_{tq}\), the mass unassigned or assigned strictly worse than \(p\). A pair blocks if \(d_{tp}>0\) and one of the paper’s three cases holds: \(p\) and \(\lambda(p)\) both have spare capacity; \(p\) has spare capacity and its lecturer is full, with positive dissatisfied mass of type \(t\) already assigned to that lecturer or the lecturer strictly preferring \(t\) to its worst occupied tier; or \(p\) is full and its lecturer strictly prefers \(t\) to the worst occupied tier at \(p\). Stability means no such blocking mass. Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|P|,|L|,|T|\), and input bit length that returns a stable allocation whose total assigned mass is at least two-thirds of the maximum stable mass?

Capacities \(\kappa_p,\delta_\ell\) are nonnegative binary rationals measured relative to total student mass, with \(\delta_\ell\le\sum_{p\in P_\ell}\kappa_p\); they need not sum to \(1\).

1. A grid lemma for the given stability definition

I first establish the structural result needed to compare a finite auxiliary instance with the optimum over real allocations.

Let
$$ E=\{(t,p):p\in A_t\}. $$
For $e=(t,p)$ and $\ell=\lambda(p)$, define
$$ w^p_{tp}(x)= \sum_{\substack{u:\ p\in A_u\\t\succ_\ell u}}x_{up}, \qquad w^\ell_{tp}(x)= \sum_{q\in P_\ell} \sum_{\substack{u:\ q\in A_u\\t\succ_\ell u}}x_{uq}. $$
These sums are exactly the lower lecturer-tier masses at the project and across the lecturer, respectively.

On the feasible allocation polytope $F$, all of
$$ d_{tp},\quad z_{tp},\quad w^p_{tp},\quad w^\ell_{tp}, \quad \kappa_p-a_p,\quad \delta_\ell-b_\ell $$
are nonnegative, and $w^p_{tp}\le w^\ell_{tp}$.

Lemma 1. A feasible allocation is stable if and only if, for every acceptable pair $(t,p)$, at least one of the following holds:
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm A_{tp}:&\quad d_{tp}=0,\\ \mathrm B_{tp}:&\quad a_p=\kappa_p,\qquad w^p_{tp}=0,\\ \mathrm C_{tp}:&\quad b_\ell=\delta_\ell,\qquad z_{tp}=0,\qquad w^\ell_{tp}=0. \end{aligned} $$

Proof. If $d_{tp}=0$, the pair cannot block. Suppose instead that $d_{tp}>0$.

If $p$ is full, absence of blocking case (iii) is exactly $w^p_{tp}=0$. This gives $\mathrm B_{tp}$.

If $p$ has spare capacity, case (i) requires the lecturer to be full. With the lecturer full, absence of case (ii) is exactly $z_{tp}=w^\ell_{tp}=0$. This gives $\mathrm C_{tp}$.

Conversely, $\mathrm A_{tp}$ and $\mathrm B_{tp}$ prevent blocking directly. Condition $\mathrm C_{tp}$ prevents blocking when the project has spare capacity. When the project is full, it also prevents blocking because $w^p_{tp}\le w^\ell_{tp}=0$. This includes zero-capacity resources. $\square$

Select one of these three conditions for each acceptable pair. Every selected equality sets a nonnegative affine function on $F$ to zero, so their common solution set, if nonempty, is a face of $F$. Consequently, the stable set is a finite union of faces.

Explicitly choose
$$ D=\operatorname{lcm}\{\text{denominators of all input masses and capacities}\}. $$
Its binary length is $O(L)$: the least common multiple is at most the product of those denominators. Write
$$ M_t=D\mu_t,\qquad K_p=D\kappa_p,\qquad H_\ell=D\delta_\ell. $$
These are integers, and $\sum_tM_t=D$.

Lemma 2. If the stable set is nonempty, maximum stable assigned mass is attained by an allocation satisfying
$$ Dx_{tp}\in\mathbb Z \qquad\text{for every }(t,p)\in E. $$

Proof. Represent feasibility by a circulation network with arcs
$$ s\to t,\qquad t\to p,\qquad p\to\lambda(p), \qquad \ell\to v,\qquad v\to s, $$
having upper bounds
$$ \mu_t,\qquad 1,\qquad \kappa_p,\qquad \delta_\ell,\qquad 1, $$
respectively. Assignment coordinates determine all other arc flows uniquely by conservation.

Every selected stability condition becomes a collection of fixed arc values. Indeed,
$$ d_{tp} = \left(\mu_t-\sum_{q\in A_t}x_{tq}\right) + \sum_{\substack{q\in A_t\\p\succ_t q}}x_{tq}. $$
Thus $d_{tp}=0$ fixes $s\to t$ at its upper bound and fixes all strictly worse assignment arcs at zero. Each equality involving $z_{tp}$, $w^p_{tp}$, or $w^\ell_{tp}$ fixes its summand assignment arcs at zero. Project and lecturer fullness fix their capacity arcs at their upper bounds.

After scaling by $D$, each selected face is therefore affinely isomorphic to a circulation polytope with integer lower and upper bounds. Such a polytope has integral vertices. To justify this standard integrality fact, the directed incidence matrix is totally unimodular: expanding a square submatrix along a column with at most one nonzero entry reduces its determinant inductively; otherwise every column has two opposite entries and the rows are dependent. Adding coordinate-bound rows preserves total unimodularity.

The feasible allocation polytope is compact. Its stable subset, being a finite union of closed faces, is compact as well. A stable optimum therefore exists whenever that subset is nonempty. Maximize assigned mass on a face containing an optimum and take an optimal vertex of that face. The circulation integrality argument puts this vertex on the stated grid. $\square$

2. Encoding stability by contract preferences under Hypothesis H

For each lecturer, construct a weak order on acceptable type-project contracts as follows:

  • Preserve the order of the lecturer’s type tiers.
  • Within a tier containing multiple types, tie all its contracts.
  • Within a singleton tier consisting of type $t$, order its contracts according to $t$’s project preferences.

This is a complete weak order. Hypothesis H is what makes the second rule compatible with the absence of strict within-lecturer project comparisons for those types.

For the argument only, construct a finite contract instance $B$. For every $e=(t,p)\in E$, introduce $D$ distinct contracts
$$ e^1,\ldots,e^D $$
between worker $t$ and firm $\lambda(p)$.

Worker $t$ has quota $M_t$ and orders these contracts by its original project preferences; all copies of one project are tied. Lecturer $\ell$ has quota $H_\ell$, and its project subsets have quotas $K_p$. Its contract order is the order constructed above, with copies tied.

The constraints at each worker are laminar. At each lecturer they are also laminar: project subsets are disjoint and lie inside the lecturer’s full contract set.

In this finite contract model, an unselected contract is free for an endpoint if that endpoint can either add it feasibly or replace a strictly less preferred selected contract by it. A contract blocks if it is free for both endpoints.

Lemma 3. Under Hypothesis H, a matching of $B$ is stable if and only if its aggregate allocation
$$ x_{tp}=\frac{\#\{\text{selected copies of }(t,p)\}}D $$
is stable in the question’s model.

Proof. Feasibility corresponds directly.

At the worker endpoint, a new $(t,p)$ contract is free precisely when worker $t$ has an unused quota unit or holds a contract for a strictly worse project. For an aggregate grid allocation, this is equivalent to
$$ Dd_{tp} = M_t-\sum_{q\succeq_t p}Dx_{tq}>0. $$
Moreover, this inequality implies that fewer than $D$ copies of $(t,p)$ are selected, so an unselected copy exists.

Consider the lecturer endpoint.

If $p$ and $\ell$ both have spare capacity, the contract can be added.

If $p$ is full, feasibility requires removing a contract at $p$. At that project, contracts of the same type are tied, while contracts of different types retain the original lecturer comparison. Hence the new contract is free exactly when
$$ w^p_{tp}>0. $$

Suppose $p$ has spare capacity and $\ell$ is full. Any contract of $\ell$ may be removed. A strictly less preferred contract is either:

  • A contract belonging to a strictly lower lecturer type tier; or
  • A contract of the same type $t$ for a strictly worse project, when $t$ occupies a singleton lecturer tier.

The first alternative is exactly $w^\ell_{tp}>0$. The second is exactly $z_{tp}>0$. If $t$ belongs to a nonsingleton tier, Hypothesis H makes $z_{tp}=0$.

Thus endpoint freedom reproduces all three blocking cases in the question. Positivity of every relevant mass is equivalent to existence of a selected contract witnessing it, because all counts are integers. $\square$

The instance $B$ may be exponentially large. It will be used only to justify the approximation guarantee.

3. The two external algorithmic results

The construction below uses two established results, with their hypotheses verified in subsequent sections.

Finite three-copy theorem. Yokoi’s theorem applies to finite bipartite contract instances with weak preferences and integral laminar upper quotas. Replace each original contract by three variants $X,Y,Z$, impose quota one on each triple, and lift all original quotas. At a worker, each preference tier lists its $X$ variants followed by its $Y$ variants; all $Z$ variants come last. At a firm, interchange $X$ and $Z$. Break remaining ties consistently. Every stable auxiliary matching projects to a stable original matching of size at least two-thirds of optimum. [Yokoi, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.03076v2)

Ordered-polymatroid theorem. For two totally ordered polymatroids on one finite ground set, Iwata–Yokoi compute a stable common independent vector using a polynomial number of saturation and exchange capacity computations. Integral rank functions yield an integral output. Here stability means that no coordinate can be increased on both sides, either directly or by decreasing a strictly less preferred coordinate. [Iwata–Yokoi, Definition 1.1 and algorithmic result](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf)

The next sections show why these results can be combined without explicitly constructing $B$.

4. A compressed auxiliary instance

Introduce only three coordinates for each acceptable pair:
$$ U=\{X_e,Y_e,Z_e:e\in E\}. $$
Let
$$ G_e=\{X_e,Y_e,Z_e\}. $$

On the worker side, impose
$$ \sum_{u\in G_e}g_u\le D, \qquad \sum_{\substack{e=(t,p)\\u\in G_e}}g_u\le M_t. $$
On the lecturer side, impose
$$ \sum_{u\in G_e}g_u\le D, $$
$$ \sum_{\substack{e=(t,p)\\u\in G_e}}g_u\le K_p \qquad\text{for every project }p, $$
and
$$ \sum_{\substack{e=(t,p),\ \lambda(p)=\ell\\u\in G_e}}g_u\le H_\ell. $$
These families are laminar on each side.

The preferences are strict orders on the compressed coordinates. Choose a fixed order of the original pairs to resolve positions within each original preference tier. For tiers $C_1,\ldots,C_s$, write $X(C_i)$ for the corresponding list of $X$ coordinates in that order, and similarly for $Y$ and $Z$.

A worker’s list is
$$ X(C_1),Y(C_1),\ldots,X(C_s),Y(C_s), Z(C_1),\ldots,Z(C_s). $$
A lecturer’s list is
$$ Z(C_1),Y(C_1),\ldots,Z(C_s),Y(C_s), X(C_1),\ldots,X(C_s). $$
In particular, for every original pair $e$, its worker has
$$ X_e\succ Y_e\succ Z_e, $$
while its lecturer has the opposite order
$$ Z_e\succ Y_e\succ X_e. $$

4.1 Why the feasible sets are integral polymatroids

Here is a direct verification for a laminar quota system.

Represent the system by a forest, with coordinates as leaves and quota sets as internal nodes. Add the redundant singleton upper bounds $g_u\le D$. For a subset $A\subseteq U$, assign value $D$ to leaves in $A$ and zero to other leaves. Recursively, a node $C$ with quota $q_C$ has value
$$ r_C(A)=\min\left\{q_C,\sum_{\text{children }J\text{ of }C}r_J(A)\right\}. $$
Sum the root values to obtain $r(A)$.

Induction on the forest shows that $r(A)$ is the maximum feasible mass supported on $A$. At an internal node, its children can supply the sum of their maxima, limited by the node quota. Any smaller nonnegative total can be obtained by reducing child allocations. With integral quotas, an integral maximizing allocation can be chosen throughout this induction.

The function $r$ is normalized, monotone, and submodular. At leaves it is modular; sums preserve submodularity. Truncation of a monotone submodular function $f$ by a constant $c$ preserves submodularity: its marginal increase upon adding an element is
$$ \min\left\{f(A+u)-f(A),\ \max\{0,c-f(A)\}\right\}, $$
and both terms inside this minimum decrease when $A$ increases.

Let $Q$ be the original laminar quota polytope. If $g\in Q$, restricting $g$ to $A$ remains feasible, so
$$ g(A)\le r(A). $$
Conversely, for every quota set $C$, its rank satisfies $r(C)\le q_C$. Hence any nonnegative vector satisfying all rank inequalities also satisfies every original quota inequality. Therefore
$$ Q=\{g\ge0:g(A)\le r(A)\text{ for all }A\subseteq U\}. $$
Thus $Q$ is an integral polymatroid.

This argument applies separately to the worker and lecturer sides. Concatenating their respective agent lists gives the two required total orders. Exchanges between different agents do not cause an additional issue: if increasing a coordinate requires a compensating decrease, the tight quota of its own agent forces that decrease to occur within that agent.

4.2 Polynomial capacity oracles

For either laminar system $\mathcal C$, the maximum feasible direct increase of coordinate $u$ is
$$ \min_{\substack{C\in\mathcal C\\u\in C}} \left(q_C-g(C)\right). $$
For distinct coordinates $u,v$, the maximum feasible exchange increasing $u$ and decreasing $v$ is
$$ \min\left\{ g_v,\ \min_{\substack{C\in\mathcal C\\u\in C,\ v\notin C}} \left(q_C-g(C)\right) \right\}, $$
where a minimum over no sets is interpreted as infinity.

These formulas follow by inspecting every quota inequality and the nonnegativity constraint on $g_v$. They can be evaluated in polynomial time.

The ground set has size $3|E|\le3\tau m$. All quotas are integers of polynomial binary length. The ordered-polymatroid theorem therefore computes an integral stable vector $g$ in time polynomial in $m,h,\tau,L$.

5. Lifting the compressed output to the finite auxiliary instance

This is the essential compression lemma.

Let $B^*$ be the three-copy auxiliary instance obtained from the expanded finite instance $B$. Each original copy $e^i$ has variants
$$ X_e^i,\quad Y_e^i,\quad Z_e^i, $$
with quota one on its triple at both endpoints.

Within each compressed coordinate block, order copy indices increasingly at both endpoints. This is a permitted choice of tie-breaking in the finite construction and agrees with the compressed coordinate orders.

For a fixed pair $e$, put
$$ s_e=g_{X_e}+g_{Y_e}+g_{Z_e}\le D. $$
Use precisely the indices
$$ 1,\ldots,s_e. $$
Assign one variant at each used index, choosing exactly $g_{X_e}$ indices with variant $X$, exactly $g_{Y_e}$ with variant $Y$, and exactly $g_{Z_e}$ with variant $Z$. The order of these choices is immaterial.

This defines a feasible matching $M^*$ of $B^*$: the counts satisfy every lifted quota, and every original-copy triple has at most one selected variant.

Lemma 4. The matching $M^*$ is stable.

Proof. Suppose an unselected auxiliary contract $V_e^i$ blocks.

First suppose index $i$ is used, holding another variant $W_e^i$. Because the triple has quota one at each endpoint, either endpoint must remove $W_e^i$ to accept $V_e^i$. The worker and lecturer have opposite orders on the three variants of this original contract. Therefore they cannot both strictly prefer $V_e^i$ to $W_e^i$. This case cannot block.

Now suppose index $i$ is unused. Then $i>s_e$.

At either endpoint, freedom of $V_e^i$ is witnessed by a feasible addition or by removal of a strictly less preferred selected contract. A removal witness cannot be another copy of the same compressed coordinate $V_e$: every selected such copy has index at most $s_e$, and is therefore preferred to $V_e^i$.

Consequently, aggregating an addition gives a feasible increase of coordinate $V_e$. Aggregating a replacement gives a feasible increase of $V_e$ at the expense of a different, strictly less preferred compressed coordinate. Every aggregate quota changes exactly as its finite counterpart does.

Thus freedom of $V_e^i$ at both endpoints would make $V_e$ improvable at both sides of the compressed instance. This contradicts stability of $g$. $\square$

No expanded matching needs to be constructed by the algorithm. Its existence is sufficient for applying the finite theorem.

6. Proof of the approximation theorem under Hypothesis H

Project $M^*$ to a matching $M$ of $B$. By the finite three-copy theorem and Lemma 4,
$$ M\text{ is stable in }B, \qquad |M|\ge\frac23\operatorname{OPT}_B, $$
where $\operatorname{OPT}_B$ is the maximum stable cardinality in $B$.

The algorithm returns
$$ x_{tp}= \frac{g_{X_{(t,p)}}+g_{Y_{(t,p)}}+g_{Z_{(t,p)}}}{D}. $$
These are exactly the aggregate counts of $M$. Lemma 3 proves that $x$ is stable in the question’s model. In particular, the stable set is nonempty for these instances.

It remains to relate the two optima.

Every stable matching of $B$ gives a stable grid allocation by Lemma 3. Conversely, every grid allocation can be realized by selecting the prescribed number of the $D$ copies of each pair, and Lemma 3 gives the reverse stability implication. Lemma 2 supplies a grid optimum for the mass model. Therefore
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_B=D\operatorname{OPT}. $$
It follows that
$$ \sum_{t,p}x_{tp} =\frac{|M|}{D} \ge\frac23\operatorname{OPT}. $$

All computation takes place on $3|E|$ coordinates. The denominator $D$ has polynomial binary length, and each output numerator is at most $D$. Thus both running time and output encoding length are polynomial in $m,h,\tau,L$.

If $E$ is empty, the zero allocation is stable and optimal, and can be returned immediately. Zero capacities are already covered by the quota formulations and the grid lemma.

This completes the proof of the stated partial theorem.

7. Why this proof does not settle the unrestricted task

The difficulty is specifically the combination of lecturer ties and strict project preferences within one lecturer.

Consider two types $u,v$, each of mass $1/2$, and one lecturer offering projects $p,q,r$. Every project has capacity $1/2$, and the lecturer has capacity $1/2$. Preferences are
$$ u:\ p\succ q,\qquad v:\ r, $$
while the lecturer ties $u$ and $v$.

Assign mass $1/2$ of $u$ to $q$, leaving $v$ unassigned.

If all lecturer contracts inherit only the lecturer’s type order, this allocation is contract-stable: the lecturer is full and is indifferent between every candidate and its occupant. However, in the question’s model,
$$ d_{up}=\frac12,\qquad z_{up}=\frac12, $$
and $p$ has spare capacity. Hence $(u,p)$ blocks by case (ii).

A weak contract order cannot simultaneously preserve all the original cross-type indifferences and represent this strict same-type move. Preserving those indifferences would require
$$ (u,p)\sim_\ell(v,r)\sim_\ell(u,q). $$
Transitivity then forces
$$ (u,p)\sim_\ell(u,q), $$
whereas representing the move from $q$ to $p$ through a strictly preferred replacement requires
$$ (u,p)\succ_\ell(u,q). $$

Hypothesis H removes precisely this conflict in the encoding used above. The example does not prove hardness, nor does it exclude another compressed algorithm. It shows why the present contract-order reduction cannot simply be applied to unrestricted inputs.

The remaining target is therefore to handle instances in which a lecturer ties distinct types and at least one of those types strictly ranks two projects offered by that lecturer. No polynomial-time two-thirds approximation or hardness proof for that unrestricted case is established here.
