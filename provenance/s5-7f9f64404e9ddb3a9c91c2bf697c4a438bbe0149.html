The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of student types \(\mathcal{T}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_t=1\), each type’s school ranking and priority-relevant attributes, schools with per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and mass-valued school choice maps \(\widehat C_s\) extending the chapter’s choice rules and satisfying mass analogues of substitutability and IRE, does type-mass deferred acceptance terminate and find a stable assignment \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\), where \(\sum_s x_{t,s}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le\kappa_s\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) student types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) schools with nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_s\); each type has a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of schools and the outside option \(0\).
Each school's deterministic choice map \(\widehat C_s:B\to B\), where \(B=\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), is supplied by an acyclic circuit using rational constants, addition, rational scalar multiplication, minimum, and maximum. All priority-relevant information is incorporated in these maps. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including circuits.
The maps are promised to satisfy \(0\le\widehat C_s(a)\le a\) and \(\sum_t\widehat C_s(a)_t\le\kappa_s\). Coordinatewise substitutability means \(\min\{\widehat C_s(b),a\}\le\widehat C_s(a)\) whenever \(a\le b\); irrelevance of rejected mass means \(\widehat C_s(b)\le a\le b\Rightarrow\widehat C_s(a)=\widehat C_s(b)\). Empty choice is allowed.
An assignment satisfies \(x_{t,s}\ge0\), \(\sum_s x_{t,s}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le\kappa_s\), and \(x_{t,s}=0\) when \(0\succ_t s\). Set \(x_{t,0}=\mu_t-\sum_s x_{t,s}\), \(x_s=(x_{t,s})_t\), and \(d_{t,s}=\sum_{r\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal S:\,s\succ_t r}x_{t,r}\).
Call an assignment stable if \(\widehat C_s(x_s)=x_s\) and \(\widehat C_s(x_s+z)=x_s\) for every school and every real vector \(0\le z\le d_s\): no mass that strictly prefers a school can disrupt its choice.
Define simultaneous applicant-keeping mass deferred acceptance as follows. Initialize cumulative offers \(a^0_s=0\) and rejections \(r^0_s=0\). Partial solution

I interpret the intended promises as including coordinatewise substitutability and irrelevance of rejected mass (IRE).

The finite-termination question has a complete negative answer: the specified deferred acceptance can run forever, even with two schools and two types. Its offers nevertheless converge, and its limiting assignment is stable and rational.

For the complexity question, I prove the following additional results:

  • Finding some exact stable assignment is polynomial-time equivalent to finding some fixed point of a monotone piecewise-linear circuit on a cube.
  • The promised search problem reduces to finite-grid Tarski fixed-point search, giving a $\mathrm{PPAD}\cap\mathrm{PLS}$ upper bound. It is polynomial-time solvable when $m\tau$ is fixed.
  • Even with two schools, the problem includes exact valuation of stopping simple stochastic games.
  • Computing the particular deferred-acceptance limit is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard already with two schools and two types.

The last result does not establish hardness of finding an arbitrary stable assignment. I leave that general classification unresolved.

1. Assumptions and an endpoint characterization

If only feasibility of the choice maps is promised, stable assignments need not exist. For example, take one type of mass $1$, one acceptable school of capacity $1$, and
$$ \widehat C(a)=\min\{a,1-a\}. $$
Stability at the endpoint would force $x=\widehat C(1)=0$, but then the permitted deviation $z=1/2$ changes the choice to $1/2$.

Henceforth both substitutability and IRE are assumed.

Lemma 1. A feasible assignment $x$ is stable if and only if
$$ \widehat C_s(x_s+d_s)=x_s \qquad\text{for every school }s. $$

Proof. The coordinate $x_{t,s}+d_{t,s}$ counts disjoint portions of type $t$ assigned to $s$ or to options strictly below $s$. Thus
$$ 0\le x_{t,s}+d_{t,s}\le\mu_t, $$
so every evaluation lies in the choice map’s domain.

Stability implies the displayed equality by taking $z=d_s$. Conversely, suppose that equality holds. For any $0\le z\le d_s$,
$$ \widehat C_s(x_s+d_s)=x_s\le x_s+z\le x_s+d_s. $$
IRE gives
$$ \widehat C_s(x_s+z)=x_s. $$
This includes $z=0$, and proves the full stability definition. $\square$

Consequently, verifying a rational stable assignment requires only feasibility checks and finitely many exact circuit evaluations. Section 4 justifies their polynomial bit complexity.

2. Stable assignments are precisely offer fixed points

Write
$$ R_s(a)=a-\widehat C_s(a). $$

Lemma 2. Each rejection map $R_s$ is coordinatewise nondecreasing.

Proof. Let $a\le b$ and fix a coordinate $t$. If $R_s(a)_t=0$, nonnegativity gives $R_s(a)_t\le R_s(b)_t$.

Otherwise $\widehat C_s(a)_t<a_t$. Substitutability gives
$$ \min\{\widehat C_s(b)_t,a_t\} \le \widehat C_s(a)_t<a_t. $$
Therefore $\widehat C_s(b)_t\le\widehat C_s(a)_t$, and
$$ R_s(b)_t =b_t-\widehat C_s(b)_t \ge a_t-\widehat C_s(a)_t =R_s(a)_t. $$
$\square$

Let
$$ D=\prod_{t,s}[0,\mu_t]. $$
Define $F:D\to D$ as follows. If type $t$ has acceptable schools
$$ s_1\succ_t\cdots\succ_t s_k, $$
set
$$ F(a)_{t,s_1}=\mu_t,\qquad F(a)_{t,s_j}=R_{s_{j-1}}(a_{s_{j-1}})_t \quad(2\le j\le k), $$
and set all other coordinates to zero. A type with no acceptable schools has an entirely zero row.

Feasibility of choice implies $0\le R_s(a)\le a$, so $F$ maps $D$ into itself. Lemma 2 makes $F$ monotone. The permitted circuit operations are continuous, so $F$ is continuous.

Proposition 3. Fixed points of $F$ and stable assignments are in bijection, through
$$ x_s=\widehat C_s(a_s), \qquad a_s=x_s+d_s. $$

Proof, from fixed points to assignments. Suppose $a=F(a)$ and set $x_s=\widehat C_s(a_s)$.

For a type with $k\ge1$ acceptable schools, abbreviate
$$ a_j=a_{t,s_j},\qquad x_j=x_{t,s_j},\qquad r_j=a_j-x_j. $$
The fixed-point equations give
$$ a_1=\mu_t,\qquad a_{j+1}=r_j. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_{j=1}^k x_j =\sum_{j=1}^k(a_j-r_j) =\mu_t-r_k. $$
The outside mass is $r_k\ge0$. Capacities follow from the choice-map promises, and unacceptable-school assignments vanish because their offers vanish.

For an acceptable school $s_j$, telescoping gives
$$ d_{t,s_j} =\sum_{\ell>j}x_\ell+r_k =r_j, $$
hence $a_{t,s_j}=x_{t,s_j}+d_{t,s_j}$.

For a type with no acceptable schools, all offers and school assignments are zero, its outside mass is $\mu_t$, and its demand at every school is zero. For any other type, demand at an unacceptable school is also zero: every option receiving positive mass is above that school. Thus
$$ a_s=x_s+d_s $$
for every school. Lemma 1 now proves stability.

Proof, from assignments to fixed points. Suppose $x$ is stable and put $a_s=x_s+d_s$. Lemma 1 gives $\widehat C_s(a_s)=x_s$.

For a type with acceptable schools $s_1,\ldots,s_k$,
$$ a_{t,s_j} =\mu_t-\sum_{\ell<j}x_{t,s_\ell}. $$
Therefore $a_{t,s_1}=\mu_t$ and
$$ a_{t,s_{j+1}} =a_{t,s_j}-x_{t,s_j} =R_{s_j}(a_{s_j})_t. $$
All unacceptable-school offer coordinates are zero. A type with no acceptable schools again has an entirely zero row. Hence $a=F(a)$.

Both transformations determine their outputs uniquely, establishing the bijection. $\square$

Convergence and existence

Feasibility forces $\widehat C_s(0)=0$, so the specified initialization agrees with
$$ a^{n+1}=F(a^n),\qquad a^0=0. $$
Monotonicity gives
$$ 0=a^0\le a^1\le a^2\le\cdots. $$
Every coordinate is bounded by its type mass. Hence the offers converge coordinatewise to some $a^*\in D$. Continuity gives
$$ F(a^*)=a^*. $$

Moreover, $a^*$ is the least fixed point. Indeed, if $b=F(b)$, induction gives $a^n\le b$ for every $n$, and therefore $a^*\le b$.

By Proposition 3,
$$ x_s^*=\widehat C_s(a_s^*) $$
is stable. Continuity also gives $h^n\to x^*$.

Thus stable assignments always exist under the intended promises, and every finite termination returns a stable assignment.

3. A choice-map construction and failure of finite termination

The following construction will also be used in the complexity reductions.

Lemma 4. Suppose $\phi_i(q)\ge0$ and every $\phi_i$ is coordinatewise nonincreasing. Then
$$ C(p,q)=\bigl((\min\{p_i,\phi_i(q)\})_i,\ q\bigr) $$
satisfies substitutability and IRE on its rectangular domain.

Proof. If $(p,q)\le(p',q')$, then $\phi_i(q')\le\phi_i(q)$, and
$$ \min\{C(p',q')_i,p_i\} =\min\{p_i,\phi_i(q')\} \le\min\{p_i,\phi_i(q)\}. $$
For the coordinates chosen in full, $\min\{q',q\}=q$. This proves substitutability.

For IRE, suppose
$$ C(p',q')\le(p,q)\le(p',q'). $$
The coordinates chosen in full force $q=q'$. For every $i$, put
$$ c_i=\min\{p_i',\phi_i(q')\}. $$
Then $c_i\le p_i\le p_i'$. If $p_i'\le\phi_i(q')$, this forces $p_i=p_i'=c_i$. Otherwise $p_i\ge\phi_i(q')=c_i$, so its new choice is again $c_i$. Thus $C(p,q)=C(p',q')$. $\square$

Counterexample

Take two types $A,B$, each of mass $1/2$, and two schools, each of capacity $1/2$, with rankings
$$ 1\succ_A2\succ_A0, \qquad 2\succ_B1\succ_B0. $$
Define
$$ \widehat C_1(a_A,a_B) =\left( \min\left\{a_A,\frac14-\frac12a_B\right\}, a_B \right), $$
$$ \widehat C_2(a_A,a_B) =\left( a_A, \min\left\{a_B,\frac14-\frac12a_A\right\} \right). $$

The thresholds are nonnegative throughout $[0,1/2]^2$, so the componentwise feasibility bounds hold. Also,
$$ \sum_t\widehat C_1(a)_t \le\frac14-\frac12a_B+a_B =\frac14+\frac12a_B \le\frac12, $$
and the school-$2$ capacity bound follows symmetrically. Lemma 4 verifies substitutability and IRE for both schools.

Let $u_n$ be the offer to each type’s second-choice school in round $n$. Symmetry gives
$$ a_1^n=(1/2,u_n),\qquad a_2^n=(u_n,1/2),\qquad u_1=0. $$
The first-choice holding is $1/4-u_n/2$, so
$$ u_{n+1}=\frac14+\frac12u_n. $$
Induction yields
$$ u_n=\frac12-2^{-n}. $$
Thus, for every $n\ge2$,
$$ u_n-u_{n-1}=2^{-n}>0. $$
Round $1$ also changes the initially zero offers. The stopping condition is therefore never satisfied.

The limit assigns all of $A$ to school $2$ and all of $B$ to school $1$. It is stable by Proposition 3.

Conclusion: the specified procedure does not always terminate after finitely many rounds.

4. Rationality, bit bounds, and an exponential exact algorithm

Introduce variables for all offers and all gates in the circuit computing $F$. Impose the offer bounds and $F(a)=a$.

For each minimum gate $w=\min\{u,v\}$, choose either
$$ w=u,\quad u\le v, $$
or
$$ w=v,\quad v\le u. $$
For maximum gates, reverse the comparison inequalities. All remaining gates impose rational linear equations.

Each branch selection defines a rational polyhedron $P$. Its feasible points evaluate the circuit correctly and represent fixed points. Conversely, every fixed point belongs to at least one branch selection.

Every such $P$ is bounded: offers belong to the compact box $D$, and gate values are uniquely determined continuous functions of those offers. Since the constraints are closed, a nonempty $P$ is a compact polytope.

Polynomial encoding length of the limit

Choose branches consistent with $a^*$. Every offer vector represented in that branch dominates $a^*$, because $a^*$ is the least fixed point. Hence $a^*$ uniquely minimizes
$$ \sum_{t,s}a_{t,s}. $$
Equality of objective values forces equality coordinatewise. Gate values are uniquely determined by offers, so the full offer-and-gate vector is also the unique minimizer.

A unique linear minimizer over a polytope is a vertex. Indeed, if it were a nontrivial convex combination of two feasible points, both points would have the same minimum objective value, contradicting uniqueness.

Let $N$ be the number of variables. After clearing denominators, let $H$ bound the absolute values of all integer coefficients and right-hand sides. Both $N$ and $\log H$ are polynomially bounded in $m,\tau,L$.

At a vertex, $N$ linearly independent active constraints determine the coordinates. Cramer’s rule expresses each coordinate as a ratio of integer determinants, each bounded in absolute value by
$$ N!H^N. $$
Their bit lengths are therefore
$$ O(N\log N+N\log H). $$
Thus $a^*$ has polynomial-size rational coordinates.

The same argument bounds exact circuit evaluation at any rational input: fix the input coordinates and select branches consistent with its evaluation. The gate equations have a unique solution, and the determinant bound controls every intermediate value. Topological evaluation using rational arithmetic and comparisons consequently takes polynomial time. In particular, $x^*$ has polynomial encoding length, and Lemma 1 gives polynomial-time verification of rational stable assignments.

Constructive linear programming

I use the following standard rational linear-programming theorem:

Given rational linear equalities and inequalities defining a bounded polyhedron, feasibility can be decided in polynomial bit complexity. If feasible, an exact rational feasible point of polynomial encoding length can be constructed. For a rational linear objective, an exact rational optimizer and optimal value can likewise be constructed in polynomial bit complexity.

The branch polytopes satisfy precisely these hypotheses.

If there are $q$ minimum and maximum gates, enumerate the $2^q$ branch selections and solve each corresponding feasibility problem. Existence guarantees that at least one branch is feasible. Proposition 3 converts any returned point into a stable assignment.

This gives running time
$$ 2^{O(L)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
Minimizing $\sum_{t,s}a_{t,s}$ over every feasible branch and selecting the smallest value also recovers $a^*$ exactly.

5. Equivalence with arbitrary monotone piecewise-linear fixed points

Consider the auxiliary search problem:

Given an acyclic circuit for a coordinatewise nondecreasing map
$$ > G:[0,1]^d\longrightarrow[0,1]^d > $$
using the permitted operations, find some exact rational fixed point.

From societies to this problem

Normalize each offer coordinate by its positive type mass. The map
$$ u\longmapsto \left( \frac{F((\mu_tu_{t,s})_{t,s})_{t,s}}{\mu_t} \right)_{t,s} $$
is a monotone circuit map on $[0,1]^{m\tau}$. Its description has polynomial size. Proposition 3 converts any fixed point back to a stable assignment.

From this problem to two-school societies

We may first pad $G$ with zero coordinates so that $d$ is a power of two. This increases the dimension by less than a factor of two.

Create types
$$ A_1,\ldots,A_d,\ B_1,\ldots,B_d, $$
all of mass
$$ \mu=\frac1{2d}. $$
These masses are dyadic and sum to $1$. Let
$$ 1\succ_{A_i}2\succ_{A_i}0, \qquad 2\succ_{B_i}1\succ_{B_i}0. $$
Give both schools capacity $1$.

For input vectors $a_A,a_B\in[0,\mu]^d$, define
$$ \widehat C_{1,A_i}(a) =\min\left\{ a_{A_i}, \mu\left(1-G_i(a_B/\mu)\right) \right\}, \qquad \widehat C_{1,B_i}(a)=a_{B_i}, $$
and
$$ \widehat C_{2,A_i}(a)=a_{A_i}, \qquad \widehat C_{2,B_i}(a) =\min\{a_{B_i},\mu-a_{A_i}\}. $$

The thresholds are nonnegative. Since $G$ is nondecreasing, the school-$1$ thresholds are nonincreasing in the fully chosen $B$ coordinates. School $2$ has the same form after exchanging the groups. Lemma 4 proves both axioms.

Componentwise feasibility holds directly. Each school’s total choice is at most the total offered mass, which is at most
$$ 2d\mu=1. $$
Thus the capacity promises hold over the entire domain. The circuits have polynomial size.

Every stable assignment decodes to a fixed point

In any stable assignment, a type’s second-choice school chooses its offered coordinate in full. Its endpoint offer there is its assignment plus its outside mass. Lemma 1 therefore forces the outside mass to be zero.

Define
$$ y_i=\frac{x_{A_i,2}}{\mu}, \qquad z_i=\frac{x_{B_i,1}}{\mu}. $$
Full assignment gives
$$ x_{A_i,1}=\mu(1-y_i), \qquad x_{B_i,2}=\mu(1-z_i). $$
Endpoint offers at first choices are $\mu$. Applying the school-$1$ endpoint equations yields
$$ y=G(z). $$
Applying the school-$2$ endpoint equations yields
$$ z=y. $$
Hence $y=G(y)$.

Conversely, if $w=G(w)$, assign
$$ x_{A_i,1}=x_{B_i,2}=\mu(1-w_i), \qquad x_{A_i,2}=x_{B_i,1}=\mu w_i. $$
This assignment is feasible, has zero outside mass, and satisfies both schools’ endpoint equations. Lemma 1 proves stability.

Therefore the reductions preserve every possible output, not merely a selected equilibrium.

Proposition 5. Finding some exact rational stable assignment is polynomial-time equivalent to finding some exact rational fixed point of a monotone piecewise-linear circuit on a cube. The reduction to societies uses only two schools.

6. Exact recovery from approximation and a Tarski upper bound

The circuit representation permits exact recovery from sufficiently small fixed-point residuals.

Lemma 6. For a circuit map $G:[0,1]^d\to[0,1]^d$, one can compute an integer $B$, polynomially bounded in its description length, such that any rational $y$ satisfying
$$ \|G(y)-y\|_\infty<2^{-B} $$
can be converted in polynomial time into an exact rational fixed point of $G$.

Proof. Choose branches consistent with evaluation at $y$. Introduce input variables $u$, gate variables, and one variable $\rho$. Impose the chosen branch constraints, $u\in[0,1]^d$, and
$$ 0\le\rho\le1, \qquad -\rho\le G_i(u)-u_i\le\rho \quad(1\le i\le d), $$
where the output gate variables represent $G_i(u)$.

The resulting polytope is nonempty: evaluation at $y$, together with $\rho=\|G(y)-y\|_\infty$, supplies a feasible point. It is compact for the same reason as the branch polytopes in Section 4.

There are polynomially many variables and rational constraints. Obtain a uniform integer coefficient bound $H$ by clearing denominators in all possible gate-branch constraints and the residual constraints. Let $N$ be the number of variables and choose $B$ so that
$$ 2^B>N!H^N. $$
Such a $B$ is computable and polynomially bounded.

The minimum of $\rho$ occurs at a vertex. If that minimum were positive, Cramer’s rule would imply
$$ \rho_{\min}\ge\frac1{N!H^N}>2^{-B}. $$
But the supplied point has smaller residual. Hence $\rho_{\min}=0$.

Constructive rational linear programming now finds an exact feasible point with $\rho=0$, which is an exact fixed point. $\square$

Reduction to a finite grid

Set
$$ M=2^{B+2} $$
and define, on $\{0,\ldots,M-1\}^d$,
$$ g_i(v) =\left\lfloor (M-1)G_i\left(\frac{v}{M-1}\right) \right\rfloor. $$
The range lies in the same grid. Monotonicity of $G$ and of rounding makes $g$ monotone.

If $g(v)=v$, then $y=v/(M-1)$ satisfies
$$ \|G(y)-y\|_\infty <\frac1{M-1} <2^{-B}. $$
Lemma 6 produces an exact fixed point.

The grid coordinates have polynomially many bits. Exact rational evaluation and integer rounding are polynomial-time operations, so $g$ has a polynomial-size Boolean-circuit representation.

I use two established finite-grid Tarski results: finding a fixed point of a monotone Boolean-circuit map on a grid belongs to $\mathrm{PPAD}\cap\mathrm{PLS}$; and, in fixed dimension $d$, a fixed point can be found using $(1+\log M)^{O(d)}$ oracle evaluations with polynomial bookkeeping. The total formulation permits a monotonicity-violation witness, which cannot occur on our promised inputs. These results are stated in [Etessami–Papadimitriou–Rubinstein–Yannakakis](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol151-itcs2020/LIPIcs.ITCS.2020.18/LIPIcs.ITCS.2020.18.pdf).

All hypotheses have been checked: $g$ is monotone, maps the finite grid into itself, and has a polynomial-size Boolean circuit.

Corollary 7.

  • The promised stable-assignment search problem has a $\mathrm{PPAD}\cap\mathrm{PLS}$ upper bound.
  • For every fixed value of $m\tau$, some exact rational stable assignment can be computed in time polynomial in $L$.

The second conclusion concerns finding some stable assignment; it does not select the deferred-acceptance limit.

7. A lower bound from stopping simple stochastic games

A simple stochastic game has Max vertices, Min vertices, fair-random vertices, and terminals of value $0$ and $1$. It is stopping if every pair of stationary policies reaches a terminal almost surely from every vertex.

The standard stopping-game theorem states that its value vector is the unique fixed point in the unit cube of the Bellman map below. Exact valuation of general simple stochastic games reduces in polynomial time to exact valuation of stopping ones. See the precise statements in [Rudich–Rudich–Rue, Section 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02571).

For successors $j,k$, the Bellman map is
$$ G_i(u)= \begin{cases} \max\{u_j,u_k\},&i\text{ is a Max vertex},\\ \min\{u_j,u_k\},&i\text{ is a Min vertex},\\ (u_j+u_k)/2,&i\text{ is a random vertex},\\ 0,&i\text{ is the zero terminal},\\ 1,&i\text{ is the one terminal}. \end{cases} $$
This map is coordinatewise nondecreasing and maps the unit cube into itself. Its circuit is acyclic: all $u_j$ are input gates, even when the game graph has cycles. Its size is linear in the graph description.

Apply Section 5’s two-school construction. Every stable assignment decodes to a Bellman fixed point, which is uniquely the game’s value vector by the stopping promise.

Thus a polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary promised stable-assignment circuits would yield a polynomial-time algorithm for exact stopping-game valuation, and consequently for general simple stochastic games.

This is a reduction, not an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness proof.

8. Computing the deferred-acceptance limit is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard

This section proves hardness of selecting the least offer fixed point, even with two types and two schools.

Let $\Phi$ be a $3$-CNF formula with variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, where $n\ge1$. Its satisfiability problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete.

A continuous encoding of Boolean assignments

Define the tent map
$$ T(x)=\min\{2x,2-2x\}, \qquad 0\le x\le1. $$
Put
$$ \delta=2^{-(n+2)} $$
and define soft bits
$$ b_i(x)= \min\left\{ 1,\, \max\left\{ 0,\, \frac{T^{i-1}(x)-1/2+\delta}{2\delta} \right\} \right\}. $$
These values lie in $[0,1]$, and their circuits share the successive tent-map evaluations.

Lemma 8. Every Boolean vector $\alpha\in\{0,1\}^n$ occurs as
$$ (b_1(x),\ldots,b_n(x)) $$
for some $x\in(0,1)$.

Proof. The inverse branches of $T$ are
$$ K_0(z)=z/2, \qquad K_1(z)=1-z/2. $$
Set $z_{n+1}=1/2$ and, working backwards, set
$$ z_i=K_{\alpha_i}(z_{i+1}). $$
Take $x=z_1$. Then
$$ T^{i-1}(x)=z_i. $$
Also, $z_i<1/2$ if $\alpha_i=0$, and $z_i>1/2$ if $\alpha_i=1$.

Inductively, $z_i$ has odd numerator and denominator $2^{n-i+2}$. Therefore
$$ |z_i-1/2| \ge 2^{-(n-i+2)} \ge 2^{-(n+1)} >\delta. $$
The clipping in the definition of $b_i$ consequently gives $b_i(x)=\alpha_i$. All inverse-branch values lie strictly between $0$ and $1$, so $x\in(0,1)$. $\square$

A nonnegative score that vanishes exactly at satisfying Boolean encodings

For a literal $\ell$, define its violation value by
$$ v_\ell(x)= \begin{cases} 1-b_i(x),&\ell=X_i,\\ b_i(x),&\ell=\neg X_i. \end{cases} $$
For a clause $K_j$, put
$$ v_j(x)=\min_{\ell\in K_j}v_\ell(x). $$
Finally, set
$$ P_\Phi(x)= \max\left( \{\min\{b_i(x),1-b_i(x)\}:1\le i\le n\} \cup \{v_j(x):j\} \right). $$
Every constituent lies in $[0,1]$.

If $P_\Phi(x)=0$, then every $b_i(x)$ is either $0$ or $1$, because $\min\{b_i,1-b_i\}=0$. Each clause has a literal with violation zero, so those Boolean bits satisfy $\Phi$.

Conversely, Lemma 8 realizes every satisfying Boolean assignment at some $x\in(0,1)$, where all constituents of $P_\Phi$ vanish. Hence
$$ \Phi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P_\Phi(x)=0\text{ for some }x\in(0,1). $$
If $\Phi$ is unsatisfiable, $P_\Phi$ has no zero anywhere in $[0,1]$.

Making the fixed-point map monotone

The tent map is $2$-Lipschitz. Clipping is $1$-Lipschitz, so
$$ \operatorname{Lip}(b_i) \le\frac{2^{i-1}}{2\delta} =2^{n+i} \le2^{2n}. $$
Complements preserve Lipschitz constants, and a minimum or maximum of scalar functions with Lipschitz constant at most $K$ also has Lipschitz constant at most $K$. Thus
$$ \operatorname{Lip}(P_\Phi)\le K, \qquad K=2^{2n}. $$

Set
$$ \varepsilon=\frac1{2K}, \qquad f_\Phi(x)=\min\{1,x+\varepsilon P_\Phi(x)\}. $$
For $x<y$,
$$ \begin{aligned} y+\varepsilon P_\Phi(y) -x-\varepsilon P_\Phi(x) &\ge (1-\varepsilon K)(y-x)\\ &=\frac12(y-x)>0. \end{aligned} $$
Thus $f_\Phi$ is nondecreasing. It maps $[0,1]$ into itself and satisfies
$$ f_\Phi(x)\ge x,\qquad f_\Phi(1)=1. $$
For $x<1$,
$$ f_\Phi(x)=x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P_\Phi(x)=0. $$

Let $w^*$ be its least fixed point, which exists by the monotone continuous iteration argument of Section 2. We have proved
$$ \Phi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w^*<1. $$
Indeed, a satisfying formula supplies a fixed point strictly below $1$, while an unsatisfiable formula has only the fixed point $1$.

The circuit size is polynomial in the formula length. All constants, including $2^{2n}$ and its reciprocal, have polynomial binary encoding length.

The two-type society

Take types $A,B$ of mass
$$ \mu=1/2, $$
with opposite rankings as in Section 3. Give both schools capacity $\mu$, and define
$$ \widehat C_1(p,q) =\left( \min\{p,\mu(1-f_\Phi(q/\mu))\}, q \right), $$
$$ \widehat C_2(p,q) =\left( p,\min\{q,\mu-p\} \right). $$

The thresholds are nonnegative and nonincreasing in the fully chosen coordinate. Lemma 4 proves substitutability and IRE.

Because $f_\Phi(v)\ge v$, school $1$ satisfies, throughout its domain,
$$ \sum_t\widehat C_1(p,q)_t \le\mu(1-f_\Phi(q/\mu))+q \le\mu. $$
School $2$ satisfies
$$ \sum_t\widehat C_2(p,q)_t\le p+\mu-p=\mu. $$
All feasibility and capacity promises therefore hold.

By Section 5’s correspondence, stable assignments are exactly
$$ x_{A,1}=x_{B,2}=\mu(1-w), \qquad x_{A,2}=x_{B,1}=\mu w, $$
where $f_\Phi(w)=w$.

The corresponding fixed-point offers have first-choice coordinates $\mu$ and second-choice coordinates $\mu w$. Their coordinatewise order is exactly the order of $w$. Hence the least offer fixed point, and therefore the deferred-acceptance limit, corresponds to $w^*$.

Consequently,
$$ \Phi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^*_{A,1}>0. $$

Proposition 9. Computing the exact deferred-acceptance limiting assignment is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, even when $m=\tau=2$ and both capacities and masses equal $1/2$.

Crucially, $w=1$ is always a fixed point. Thus the assignment sending both types entirely to their second choices is stable for every formula in this reduction. Finding an arbitrary stable assignment in this family requires no satisfiability computation.

9. What remains open

The preceding results separate two tasks:

  • Finding some stable assignment is equivalent to finding some fixed point of a monotone piecewise-linear circuit.
  • Finding the deferred-acceptance limit selects the least fixed point, and is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.

There is also a precise obstacle to one common meaning of hardness for the first task. Suppose a polynomial-time reduction from satisfiability always produced a promised society, together with a polynomial-time decoder that returned the correct satisfiability answer from every stable assignment. Then unsatisfiability would have polynomial-size certificates: guess a rational stable assignment, verify it using Lemma 1, and check that the decoder returns “unsatisfiable.” Existence and the polynomial encoding bound supply such certificates on unsatisfiable inputs, while the decoder’s stipulated correctness excludes them on satisfiable inputs. This would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

This observation is conditional and concerns that explicitly specified form of reduction. It does not prove polynomial-time solvability.

The general variable-$m$, variable-$\tau$ search classification is not settled here. Finite termination is disproved, stable existence and finite-round correctness are proved, and the hardness of the particular limiting outcome is proved.
