The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings of \(\mathcal C\) and a maximin-losing candidate \(l\), define \(p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t\). Choose rational evidence masses \(x_{ab}\) with \(0\le x_{ab}\le p_\mu(a,b)\) and \(x_{ab}+x_{ba}\le 1\). Call \(x\) sound if every ranking distribution \(\nu\) satisfying \(p_\nu(a,b)\ge x_{ab}\) has \(l\notin\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal C}\min_{d\ne c}p_\nu(c,d)\). Compute the infimum of \(\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}\) and, for each rational \(\epsilon>0\), return sound evidence within \(\epsilon\) of that infimum.

Let \(\mathcal C\) contain \(m\geq2\) candidates; types are strict total rankings of \(\mathcal C\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For any ranking distribution \(\rho\), write \(p_\rho(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\rho_t\) and let \(W(\rho)\) be its maximin winner set, including all tied winners. The input includes a society \(\mu\) and \(l\in\mathcal C\setminus W(\mu)\).
Evidence consists of rational numbers \(x_{ab}\) for distinct candidates, satisfying \(0\leq x_{ab}\leq p_\mu(a,b)\) and \(x_{ab}+x_{ba}\leq1\). It is sound if \(l\notin W(\nu)\) for every probability distribution \(\nu\) over all strict rankings satisfying \(p_\nu(a,b)\geq x_{ab}\); these distributions may have real masses and need not share the input support.
Define \(I(\mu,l)=\inf\{\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}:x\text{ is sound rational evidence}\}\). The computational problem is to compute this infimum exactly and, given binary rational \(\epsilon>0\), return sound rational evidence of cost at most \(I(\mu,l)+\epsilon\).
Is this computational problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), including the encoding length of \(\epsilon\), or is it NP-hard?
For complete integer-weighted tournaments, minimum-total-weight evidence excluding a maximin loser from every tournament completion is computable in polynomial time. [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23446v1).
That result permits arbitrary tournament completions rather than only pairwise probabilities induced by ranking distributions. No result settling the continuous problem above was located.

1. Definitions and cyclic distributions

Write
$$ u_{ab}=p_\mu(a,b),\qquad s_\rho(a)=\min_{b\ne a}p_\rho(a,b),\qquad M_\rho=\max_a s_\rho(a). $$
Thus $l$ is a winner precisely when $s_\rho(l)=M_\rho$.

The evidence cost is
$$ c(x)=\sum_{a\ne b}x_{ab}. $$
Strict rankings imply
$$ p_\rho(a,b)+p_\rho(b,a)=1. $$
In particular, the input caps imply the evidence pair constraints:
$$ x_{ab}+x_{ba}\leq u_{ab}+u_{ba}=1. $$

Lemma 1: Universal score bound and equality case

Every ranking distribution satisfies
$$ M_\rho\geq\frac1m. $$
If equality holds, there is a directed Hamiltonian cycle
$$ v_0\longrightarrow v_1\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow v_{m-1}\longrightarrow v_0 $$
such that $\rho$ assigns mass $1/m$ to each ranking
$$ R_i:\quad v_i\succ v_{i-1}\succ\cdots\succ v_{i+1}, $$
where indices are interpreted modulo $m$.

Proof. For each candidate $a$, choose an opponent $f(a)$ attaining $s_\rho(a)$. The graph of arcs $a\to f(a)$ has outdegree one and no loops, so it contains a directed cycle on distinct candidates, say $v_0,\ldots,v_{k-1}$.

Every strict ranking satisfies at least one comparison $v_i\succ v_{i+1}$. Otherwise it would satisfy every reverse comparison around this cycle, contradicting transitivity. Consequently,
$$ 1\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}p_\rho(v_i,v_{i+1}) =\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}s_\rho(v_i) \leq kM_\rho\leq mM_\rho. $$
This proves the bound.

If $M_\rho=1/m$, every inequality is an equality. Hence $k=m$ and every cycle comparison has probability $1/m$.

Let $N(t)$ count the forward cycle comparisons satisfied by ranking $t$. We have $N(t)\geq1$ and
$$ \mathbb E_\rho N =\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}p_\rho(v_i,v_{i+1})=1. $$
Every positive-mass ranking therefore satisfies exactly one forward cycle comparison. If that comparison is $v_i\succ v_{i+1}$, all remaining reverse comparisons force exactly the ranking $R_i$. Only $R_i$ satisfies this unique forward comparison, so its mass is $1/m$. $\square$

Lemma 2: Weighted cyclic profiles

Fix the Hamiltonian cycle above. Assign masses $\lambda_i\geq0$, summing to $1$, to the rankings $R_i$. Then, for $1\leq d\leq m-1$,
$$ p(v_i,v_{i+d})=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\lambda_{i+j}, \qquad s(v_i)=\lambda_i. $$

Proof. In the ranking $R_k$, the comparison $v_i\succ v_{i+d}$ holds exactly when $k$ lies in the forward cyclic segment
$$ v_i,v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{i+d-1}. $$
Summing the corresponding masses proves the probability formula.

Every displayed sum contains $\lambda_i$, and the comparison with $v_{i+1}$ has probability exactly $\lambda_i$. Thus the minimum is $\lambda_i$. $\square$

For uniform masses, this gives
$$ p(v_i,v_{i+d})=\frac dm. $$

2. Exact criterion for evidence only into the loser

Assume $m\geq3$. Consider evidence of the form
$$ x_{al}=q_a\quad(a\ne l), $$
with every other coordinate zero. Let
$$ Q=\max_{a\ne l}q_a, \qquad q_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq q_{(m-1)}=Q $$
be the sorted incoming coordinates. Define
$$ H_m=1-\frac1{m-1}, \qquad T_m=1-\frac1m. $$

Theorem 3: Complete incoming-evidence criterion

The following statements characterize soundness.

  • If $Q>T_m$, the evidence is sound.
  • If $Q\leq H_m$, the evidence is unsound.
  • If $H_m<Q\leq T_m$, the evidence is unsound exactly when

$$ q_{(d)}\leq d(1-Q) \quad\text{for every }1\leq d\leq m-1. $$

In the third case, the last inequality is automatic. Thus soundness is equivalent to a violation for some $1\leq d\leq m-2$.

Proof.

If $Q>T_m$, choose $a$ with $q_a=Q$. Every compatible distribution satisfies
$$ s_\nu(l)\leq p_\nu(l,a)\leq1-Q<\frac1m\leq M_\nu. $$
Hence $l$ cannot win.

Suppose next that $Q\leq H_m$. If $Q\leq1/2$, an equal mixture of a ranking and its reversal has every pairwise probability equal to $1/2$. It satisfies the evidence and makes every candidate a winner.

If $1/2<Q\leq H_m$, put the other $m-1$ candidates in a uniform cyclic profile. Independently insert $l$ above all of them with probability $1-Q$ and below all of them with probability $Q$. Then
$$ p(a,l)=Q\geq q_a \quad(a\ne l), \qquad s(l)=1-Q. $$
Every rival has score at most $1/(m-1)$, because its comparisons with the other rivals retain the uniform cyclic probabilities. Since
$$ 1-Q\geq\frac1{m-1}, $$
candidate $l$ wins.

It remains to consider
$$ H_m<Q\leq T_m. $$
Set $s=1-Q$, so
$$ \frac1m\leq s<\frac1{m-1}. $$

First suppose a compatible distribution makes $l$ a winner. Its maximum score satisfies
$$ M_\nu=s_\nu(l)\leq s. $$
Choose minimizing opponents as in Lemma 1. A cycle of length at most $m-1$ would give
$$ 1\leq kM_\nu\leq(m-1)s<1, $$
a contradiction. The chosen cycle must therefore be Hamiltonian, and each of its arc probabilities is at most $s$.

For a rival $a$, let $d_a$ be its forward distance to $l$ on this cycle. These distances are exactly $1,\ldots,m-1$. If $a\succ l$ in a ranking, at least one forward comparison on the path from $a$ to $l$ must hold: if every comparison were reversed, transitivity would give $l\succ a$. Taking probabilities and applying the union bound yields
$$ q_a\leq p_\nu(a,l) \leq\sum_{\text{arcs on the path}}p_\nu(\text{arc}) \leq d_as. $$
For each $d$, the $d$ candidates whose distances are at most $d$ consequently have incoming requirements at most $ds$. Therefore
$$ q_{(d)}\leq ds. $$

Conversely, assume all these inequalities hold. Label the rivals $v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1}$ so that $q_{v_d}=q_{(d)}$, and use the cycle
$$ l\longrightarrow v_{m-1}\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow v_1\longrightarrow l. $$
Define cyclic masses by
$$ \lambda_l=s, \qquad \lambda_{v_d} =\min\{ds,Q\}-\min\{(d-1)s,Q\}. $$
All masses lie in $[0,s]$. Moreover, $Q\leq T_m$ implies $(m-1)s\geq Q$, so their sum is
$$ s+\min\{(m-1)s,Q\}=s+Q=1. $$
Lemma 2 gives
$$ p(v_d,l)=\min\{ds,Q\}\geq q_{(d)}. $$
It also gives $s(l)=s$ and every rival score at most $s$. Thus this distribution satisfies the evidence and makes $l$ a winner.

Finally,
$$ q_{(m-1)}=Q\leq(m-1)(1-Q) $$
is equivalent to $Q\leq T_m$, so the last inequality is automatic. $\square$

The criterion requires only sorting and rational comparisons, and hence takes polynomial time in the evidence encoding length.

Corollary 4: Sharp cost bound for incoming-only evidence

If incoming-only evidence is sound and $Q\leq T_m$, then
$$ c(x)>2-\frac3m. $$

Proof. By Theorem 3, some $1\leq d\leq m-2$ satisfies
$$ q_{(d)}>d(1-Q). $$
There are $m-d$ coordinates from $q_{(d)}$ through $q_{(m-1)}$. One is $Q$, and each of the other $m-d-1$ is strictly greater than $d(1-Q)$. Hence
$$ c(x)>Q+d(m-d-1)(1-Q). $$
Put $A=d(m-d-1)$. We have
$$ A-(m-2)=(d-1)(m-d-2)\geq0. $$
In particular, $A\geq1$. The expression $A+(1-A)Q$ is nonincreasing in $Q$, so
$$ Q+A(1-Q) \geq T_m+\frac Am \geq\frac{m-1}{m}+\frac{m-2}{m} =2-\frac3m. $$
This proves the claim. $\square$

The bound is sharp. Indeed, take distinct rivals $a,b$ and evidence
$$ x_{al}=T_m,\qquad x_{bl}=\frac{m-2}{m}+\delta, $$
where $0<\delta<1/m$, with all other coordinates zero. Theorem 3 declares it sound because its second-largest incoming requirement exceeds
$$ (m-2)(1-T_m)=\frac{m-2}{m}. $$
Its cost is $2-3/m+\delta$.

This sharpness concerns incoming-only evidence. It does not prove the same lower bound for unrestricted evidence when $m\geq5$.

3. A stronger lower bound for unrestricted four-candidate evidence

Theorem 5

Let $m=4$. Suppose an evidence vector satisfies the pair constraints and
$$ x_{al}\leq\frac34\quad(a\ne l), \qquad c(x)\leq\frac54. $$
Then it is unsound.

The proof constructs a compatible distribution making $l$ a winner in every case.

3.1. Two useful constructions

First, fix a four-candidate Hamiltonian cycle. For $1/2<q\leq3/4$, mix its uniform cyclic profile with a distribution having every pairwise probability $1/2$, using cyclic-profile weight
$$ \alpha=4q-2. $$
By Lemma 2, comparisons at forward distances $1,2,3$ have probabilities respectively
$$ 1-q,\qquad\frac12,\qquad q. $$
Every candidate has score $1-q$.

Second, for three candidates $a,b,c$ and
$$ \frac12<r\leq q\leq\frac34, $$
the following masses define a ranking distribution:

| Ranking | Mass |
|---|---:|
| $a\succ b\succ c$ | $r/2$ |
| $b\succ a\succ c$ | $r/2$ |
| $c\succ a\succ b$ | $1/2-q+r/2$ |
| $c\succ b\succ a$ | $1/2-r/2$ |
| $a\succ c\succ b$ | $q-r$ |

Every mass is nonnegative: the only less immediate one satisfies
$$ \frac12-q+\frac r2 \geq\frac12-\frac34+\frac14=0. $$
The masses sum to $1$, and direct summation gives
$$ p(a,c)=q,\qquad p(b,c)=r,\qquad p(a,b)=\frac12. $$
Place the fourth candidate above or below this entire block, each with probability $1/2$. Its comparisons with the block are all $1/2$.

In this four-candidate distribution, $a,b$ and the fourth candidate have score $1/2$, while $c$ has smaller score. Reversing the rankings instead gives
$$ p(c,a)=q,\qquad p(c,b)=r,\qquad p(a,b)=\frac12, $$
and makes $c$ and the fourth candidate winners. In either construction, every comparison probability is at least $1-q\geq1/4$.

3.2. No large coordinate, or a coordinate exceeding $3/4$

If every coordinate is at most $1/2$, the distribution with every pairwise probability $1/2$ is a counterexample.

Suppose the largest coordinate is $x_{ab}=q>3/4$. The incoming-coordinate hypothesis implies $b\ne l$.

Treat $a,b$ as an adjacent block, putting $a$ above $b$ with probability $q$. Put the block above or below the two other candidates with equal probabilities, and independently order those two candidates uniformly. This realizes
$$ p(a,b)=q,\qquad p(b,a)=1-q, $$
with every other comparison equal to $1/2$.

Because $b\ne l$, candidate $l$ has score $1/2$ and is a winner. Every evidence coordinate other than $x_{ab}$ is at most
$$ \frac54-q<\frac12. $$
The reverse coordinate additionally satisfies $x_{ba}\leq1-q$. Thus the distribution satisfies all evidence.

Henceforth assume the largest coordinate $q$ satisfies
$$ \frac12<q\leq\frac34. $$

3.3. Exactly one coordinate exceeds $1/2$

Write $x_{ab}=q$ and $s=1-q$. The remaining evidence cost is at most
$$ \frac54-q\leq2s. $$
Consequently, at most one remaining coordinate exceeds $s$.

Choose a Hamiltonian cycle containing $b\to a$. If there is an exceptional remaining coordinate $x_{cd}>s$, it satisfies $x_{cd}\leq1/2$. It is not $x_{ba}$, because $x_{ba}\leq1-q=s$.

There is a cycle containing $b\to a$ but avoiding $c\to d$. To see this explicitly, let $e,f$ be the other two candidates. The cycles
$$ b\to a\to e\to f\to b, \qquad b\to a\to f\to e\to b $$
share only the arc $b\to a$.

Apply the first construction in Section 3.1 to such a cycle. It realizes $p(a,b)=q$, while the exceptional comparison, being a noncycle arc, has probability at least $1/2$. Every other comparison has probability at least $s$. All candidates tie, and all evidence is satisfied.

3.4. Two coordinates exceed $1/2$

There cannot be three such coordinates, because their cost would exceed $3/2$. Denote the two large coordinates by $q\geq r>1/2$, and put
$$ h=\frac54-q-r. $$
Then
$$ 0\leq h<\frac14,\qquad r\leq\frac58, $$
and every remaining evidence coordinate is at most $h$.

If the two large arcs have different tails and different heads, their reversals can be included in a Hamiltonian cycle. Indeed, the reversals form either disjoint directed edges or a directed path; a directed two-cycle is impossible because the evidence pair sum would exceed $1$. Complete these edges to a Hamiltonian cycle and apply Section 3.1 with parameter $q$. Both large evidence requirements are met, every other comparison is at least $1-q\geq1/4>h$, and all candidates tie.

The remaining possibilities are a common head or a common tail.

If the common head is not $l$, use the three-candidate block construction. Candidate $l$ is one of its winners, and every comparison is at least $1/4>h$.

If the common tail is $l$, or $l$ is outside the three involved candidates, use the reversed block construction. Again $l$ wins and all evidence is satisfied.

Only the following three configurations remain. Define
$$ A=r-\frac14,\qquad B=\frac54-2r,\qquad D=\frac r2. $$
These numbers satisfy
$$ A+B+2D=1,\qquad A\geq B,\qquad A\geq D, $$
and each is at least $h$. For $B$, this follows from
$$ B-h=q-r\geq0; $$
for $A,D$, it follows from $A,D>1/4>h$.

Common head $l$. Suppose the large requirements are
$$ x_{al}=q,\qquad x_{bl}=r. $$
Let $c$ be the fourth candidate. Use cycle
$$ l\to a\to b\to c\to l $$
with masses $(A,B,D,D)$ in that order. Lemma 2 gives
$$ p(a,l)=B+2D=\frac54-r=q+h\geq q, \qquad p(b,l)=2D=r. $$
Candidate $l$ has the largest score $A$, and every comparison is at least $h$.

Common tail, with the larger requirement into $l$. Suppose
$$ x_{al}=q,\qquad x_{ab}=r. $$
Use cycle
$$ l\to a\to c\to b\to l $$
with masses $(A,D,D,B)$. Then
$$ p(a,l)=q+h\geq q,\qquad p(a,b)=r. $$
Again $l$ has the largest score, and every comparison is at least $h$.

Common tail, with the smaller requirement into $l$. Suppose
$$ x_{ab}=q,\qquad x_{al}=r. $$
Use cycle
$$ l\to b\to a\to c\to l $$
with masses
$$ \left(D,\ 1-\frac{3r}{2},\ D,\ D\right). $$
They are nonnegative and sum to $1$. Moreover,
$$ 1-\frac{3r}{2}\leq D $$
because $r>1/2$. Thus $l$ is a winner. The two relevant comparisons are
$$ p(a,l)=r,\qquad p(a,b)=\frac{3r}{2}\geq q. $$
For the latter inequality, use
$$ q\leq\frac54-r<\frac{3r}{2}, $$
where the strict inequality follows from $r>1/2$.

Every mass is at least $h$: this is already true for $D$, while
$$ 1-\frac{3r}{2}-h =q-\frac r2-\frac14 \geq\frac r2-\frac14>0. $$
Therefore every comparison is at least $h$, and all remaining evidence is satisfied.

These cases exhaust the possibilities, proving Theorem 5. $\square$

4. An exactly solved family of four-candidate inputs

Corollary 6

Suppose $m=4$ and there are distinct rivals $a,b$ such that
$$ \max_{c\ne l}u_{cl}=\frac34, \qquad u_{al}=\frac34, \qquad u_{bl}>\frac12. $$
Then
$$ I(\mu,l)=\frac54. $$
For every rational $\epsilon>0$, sound rational evidence of cost at most $5/4+\epsilon$ can be produced in polynomial time.

Proof. Theorem 5 gives the lower bound.

Set
$$ \delta=\min\left\{ \frac{\epsilon}{2}, \frac{u_{bl}-1/2}{2} \right\}>0 $$
and use only
$$ x_{al}=\frac34,\qquad x_{bl}=\frac12+\delta. $$
These coordinates respect their input caps.

Suppose a compatible distribution makes $l$ a winner. Then
$$ M_\nu=s_\nu(l)\leq p_\nu(l,a)\leq\frac14. $$
Lemma 1 forces equality and a uniform four-candidate cyclic profile. In such a profile, $p(l,a)=1/4$ forces $a$ to be the successor of $l$. Every other rival consequently has incoming probability to $l$ at most $1/2$. This contradicts
$$ p_\nu(b,l)\geq\frac12+\delta. $$
The evidence is therefore sound. Its cost is $5/4+\delta\leq5/4+\epsilon$.

The construction uses rational comparisons and arithmetic of polynomial encoding length. $\square$

This family contains valid inputs with support size four. For example, assign mass $1/4$ to each ranking
$$ a\succ b\succ l\succ c,\qquad a\succ b\succ c\succ l,\qquad a\succ c\succ l\succ b,\qquad b\succ c\succ l\succ a. $$
Every rival beats $l$ with probability $3/4$, so $s_\mu(l)=1/4$. Candidate $a$ beats each opponent with probability $3/4$, so $s_\mu(a)=3/4$. Thus $l$ is a loser, all the corollary’s hypotheses hold, and
$$ I(\mu,l)=\frac54. $$
