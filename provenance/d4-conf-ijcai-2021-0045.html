The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) goods, a finite type set \(T\) with \(|T|=\tau\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational valuations \(v_{tj}\), positive rational per-unit-mass budgets \(\kappa_t\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\), compute prices \(p_j>0\) and per-unit-mass spending vectors \(z_t\) such that \(p_j=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t z_{tj}\), \(\sum_j z_{tj}\leq\kappa_t\), and each type’s spending utility is within \(\varepsilon\) of its maximum at prices \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of the \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) divisible goods, each with supply one, positive binary-rational valuations \(v_{tj}\), positive binary-rational budgets per unit mass \(\kappa_t\), and a positive binary-rational accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including \(\varepsilon\).
At fixed prices \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m\), a type's feasible spending vectors form \(Z_t=\{a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^m:\sum_j a_j\leq\kappa_t\}\), and its utility per unit mass is \(u_t(a\mid p)=\sum_j v_{tj}a_j/p_j-\sum_j a_j\). Unspent budget is allowed; ties may be resolved by any feasible split.
The computational problem is to find binary-rational prices \(p_j>0\) and spending vectors \(z_t\in Z_t\) satisfying \(p_j=\sum_t\mu_t z_{tj}\) for every good and \(u_t(z_t\mid p)\geq\max_{a\in Z_t}u_t(a\mid p)-\varepsilon\) for every type.

Is this computational problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite-agent quasi-linear Fisher markets, proportional response converges from every positive feasible starting portfolio to the equilibrium spending set ([Cheung, Leonardos, and Piliouras](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0016.pdf)); this convergence statement supplies no polynomial bit-complexity bound.
A strongly polynomial equilibrium algorithm is also claimed for finite quasi-linear markets formulated as Arctic Auctions ([Garg, Taherijam, and Vazirani](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24756v1)). No result explicitly addressing the precise support-input mass problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined spending utility using the discrete quasi-linear utility: valuation of quantities purchased minus spending, evaluated with prices held fixed.
  • Made spending nonnegative and retained the option to leave budget unspent; no tie-breaking restriction is imposed.
  • Interpreted 'within epsilon' as additive utility loss per unit mass for every supported type.
  • Carried over one unit of supply for each divisible good and required nonempty support and at least one good.
  • Specified binary-rational accuracy and outputs, with total input bit length L including accuracy; polynomial time means polynomial in the binary input size.

Result

The problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This includes the required exact market-clearing equalities and binary-rational output.

The proof uses convex optimization with controlled precision. It does not require a polynomial convergence rate for proportional response or the recent strongly polynomial algorithm.

1. Absorbing the masses into the budgets

Define the aggregate budget and spending of type \(t\) by

\(B_t=\mu_t\kappa_t,\qquad b_{tj}=\mu_t z_{tj}.\)

Let

\(K=\left\{b\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\tau\times m}:\sum_j b_{tj}\leq B_t\text{ for every }t\right\},\qquad p_j(b)=\sum_t b_{tj}.\)

At positive prices, define aggregate utility by

\(U_t(b_t\mid p)=\sum_j\left(\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j}-1\right)b_{tj}.\)

The substitution \(b_t=\mu_t z_t\) is a bijection between the corresponding spending simplices, and

\(U_t(\mu_t z_t\mid p)=\mu_t u_t(z_t\mid p).\)

Consequently, aggregate utility regret at most \(\mu_t\varepsilon\) implies the required per-unit-mass regret at most \(\varepsilon\).

This transformation creates exactly \(\tau\) buyers. It requires no replication of agents and increases the encoding length only polynomially.

2. A convex program and its equilibrium minimizers

Throughout, logarithms are natural. Put

\(F(b)=\sum_j\bigl(p_j(b)\log p_j(b)-p_j(b)\bigr)-\sum_{t,j}b_{tj}\log v_{tj},\)

using the continuous convention \(0\log0=0\).

This is the quasi-linear Shmyrev objective; it also appears in [Gao and Kroer, Section 4](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2020/file/f75526659f31040afeb61cb7133e4e6d-Paper.pdf). The properties needed here are proved below.

Since \(x\mapsto x\log x-x\) is convex and continuous on the nonnegative real line, \(F\) is convex and continuous on \(K\). The set \(K\) is nonempty and compact, so a minimizer \(b^*\) exists.

Every minimizing price is positive

Suppose \(p_j(b^*)=0\). Choose any type \(t\).

If its budget constraint has positive slack, add an amount \(\eta>0\) to \(b^*_{tj}\). If its constraint is tight, then \(B_t>0\) guarantees some \(b^*_{tk}>0\); transfer \(\eta\) from that coordinate to \(b^*_{tj}\).

For sufficiently small \(\eta\), either modification is feasible. The newly positive column contributes \(\eta\log\eta+O(\eta)\) to the objective change. Every other changed term contributes \(O(\eta)\), because the column from which money is transferred has positive price. Thus the total change is negative for sufficiently small \(\eta\), contradicting minimality.

Therefore \(p_j^*=p_j(b^*)>0\) for every good.

Every minimizer is an exact equilibrium

At a point with positive prices,

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial F}{\partial b_{tj}}=\log\frac{p_j(b)}{v_{tj}}.\)

Optimality of \(b^*\) implies

\(\langle\nabla F(b^*),a-b^*\rangle\geq0\qquad\text{for every }a\in K.\)

Because \(K\) is a product of row simplices, each \(b_t^*\) maximizes

\(\displaystyle \sum_j a_j\log\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j^*}\)

over nonnegative \(a\) with \(\sum_j a_j\leq B_t\).

For a linear objective on this simplex, an optimum spends only on coordinates with maximal positive coefficient; it spends the entire budget if that maximum is positive. If the maximum is zero, it may retain money or split spending among zero-coefficient coordinates.

The coefficients \(\log(v_{tj}/p_j^*)\) and \(v_{tj}/p_j^*-1\) have identical signs, orderings, and ties. They therefore have identical maximizing spending sets. Hence every \(b_t^*\) maximizes aggregate utility at \(p^*\). By definition,

\(p_j^*=\sum_t b_{tj}^*.\)

Thus \((b^*,p^*)\) is an exact equilibrium.

3. An explicit lower bound on equilibrium prices

Define

\(B_{\min}=\min_t B_t,\qquad S=\sum_t B_t,\qquad v_{\min}=\min_{t,j}v_{tj},\qquad V=\max_{t,j}v_{tj},\)

and

\(A=\max\left\{1,\frac{mV}{B_{\min}}\right\},\qquad q=\frac{v_{\min}}{2A}.\)

All these quantities are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length.

For a minimizing equilibrium, let

\(\alpha_t=\max\left\{1,\max_j\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j^*}\right\}.\)

If \(\alpha_t>1\), buyer optimality implies that type \(t\) spends its entire budget and spends only on goods satisfying \(v_{tj}/p_j^*=\alpha_t\). Set \(x_{tj}=b_{tj}^*/p_j^*\). Market clearing gives \(\sum_t x_{tj}=1\), so \(x_{tj}\leq1\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \alpha_t B_t=\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}\leq\sum_j v_{tj}\leq mV.\)

If \(\alpha_t=1\), its bound is immediate. Thus \(\alpha_t\leq A\) for every type.

Every good has some positive spender. For such a spender, optimality gives \(v_{tj}/p_j^*=\alpha_t\), including the case \(\alpha_t=1\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle p_j^*=\frac{v_{tj}}{\alpha_t}\geq\frac{v_{\min}}A=2q.\)

We also have \(p_j^*\leq S\).

Now restrict optimization to the rational polytope

\(P=\{b\in K:p_j(b)\geq q\text{ for every }j\}.\)

It contains every minimizer of \(F\) over \(K\). In particular,

\(\min_{b\in P}F(b)=\min_{b\in K}F(b)=F(b^*).\)

4. Polynomial-time optimization with finite precision

We use the standard finite-precision ellipsoid theorem in the following form:

A convex differentiable function can be minimized to additive accuracy \(\delta>0\) over an explicitly given bounded rational polytope in polynomial bit time, provided that the polytope has a known interior ball, a known outer ball, a Lipschitz bound, and polynomial-time rational approximations to function values and gradients. The dependence on the accuracy and geometric bounds is logarithmic. The returned point is rational and satisfies the polytope’s linear constraints exactly.

The rational implementation and its complexity are standard ellipsoid results; see [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, Chapters 3–4](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

Here are the required hypothesis checks.

Geometry of \(P\)

First, \(q\leq B_{\min}/(2m)\). Indeed, if \(A=mV/B_{\min}\), this follows from \(v_{\min}\leq V\); if \(A=1\), then \(B_{\min}\geq mV\geq mv_{\min}\).

Consider the rational point

\(b^0_{tj}=\frac{3B_t}{4m}.\)

Its row budget slack is \(B_t/4\), and its column sums are \(3S/(4m)\). Since \(q\leq S/(2m)\), its column slack is at least \(S/(4m)\).

The Euclidean ball centered at \(b^0\) with radius

\(r=\frac{B_{\min}}{8m\tau}\)

lies inside \(P\). To check this, each coordinate changes by at most \(r\), each row sum by at most \(mr\), and each column sum by at most \(\tau r\). These bounds are smaller than the respective nonnegativity, row, and column slacks.

For every \(b\in P\),

\(\|b\|_2\leq\sum_{t,j}b_{tj}\leq S.\)

Thus \(R=1+S\) is a valid outer radius. Both \(r\) and \(R\), and hence the logarithm of \(R/r\), have polynomial encoding bounds.

Objective and oracle bounds

On \(P\), every price lies in \([q,S]\). Hence

\(\left|\frac{\partial F}{\partial b_{tj}}\right|=\left|\log\frac{p_j(b)}{v_{tj}}\right|\)

has a polynomially bounded magnitude. An explicit rational bound on the gradient norm, and therefore on the Lipschitz constant, follows by bounding the ratios between \(q/V\) and \(S/v_{\min}\).

Function values and gradients can be approximated to any requested binary precision in polynomial time. For completeness, a positive rational \(x\) can be written \(x=2^k y\), with \(1\leq y<2\), and then evaluated using

\(\displaystyle \log x=k\log2+2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1},\qquad w=\frac{y-1}{y+1}.\)

Here \(0\leq w<1/3\), so the series has a geometrically decreasing tail. The same series computes \(\log2\). Polynomially many terms suffice for polynomially many accuracy bits, and the rational arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity.

Approximate gradients suffice: if \(F(x)>F^*+\delta/2\) and \(F(y)\leq F^*+\delta/4\), convexity gives

\(\langle\nabla F(x),y-x\rangle<-\delta/4.\)

A gradient approximation with Euclidean error at most \(\delta/(16R)\) changes this inner product by at most \(\delta/8\). Its central cut therefore retains that lower sublevel set.

That sublevel set has a known interior-ball bound. If \(M\geq1\) is a Lipschitz bound and \(x^*\) is a minimizer in \(P\), the ball obtained by mixing \(x^*\) with the ball around \(b^0\), with mixing weight

\(\theta=\min\{1,\delta/(8MR)\},\)

has radius \(\theta r\), lies in \(P\), and has objective value at most \(F^*+\delta/4\). This follows from convexity and the bound \(2MR\) on objective variation across \(P\).

Thus finite-precision cuts and ellipsoid volume reduction apply with polynomially many accuracy bits. We can obtain, in polynomial time, a rational \(b\in P\) satisfying

\(F(b)-F(b^*)\leq\delta.\)

The remaining task is to choose \(\delta\) so this objective guarantee implies the required utility guarantees.

5. From objective error to individual utility regret

For \(b\in P\), define its first-order gap over the original spending set \(K\) by

\(g(b)=\max_{a\in K}\langle\nabla F(b),b-a\rangle.\)

Bounding the first-order gap

Let \(a\in K\) attain this maximum, and put \(b_s=(1-s)b+sa\). For \(0\leq s\leq1/2\),

\(p_j(b_s)\geq(1-s)p_j(b)\geq q/2.\)

Differentiating along this segment gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{d^2}{ds^2}F(b_s)=\sum_j\frac{(p_j(a)-p_j(b))^2}{p_j(b_s)}.\)

Both nonnegative price vectors have total sum at most \(S\), so their difference has sum of absolute coordinates at most \(2S\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \frac{d^2}{ds^2}F(b_s)\leq\frac{8S^2}{q}.\)

Set \(C=4S^2/q\). Taylor’s inequality yields

\(F(b_s)\leq F(b)-s g(b)+Cs^2.\)

Since \(b_s\in K\), its objective is at least \(F(b^*)\). Hence, whenever \(F(b)-F(b^*)\leq\delta\),

\(\displaystyle g(b)\leq\frac{\delta}{s}+Cs\qquad(0<s\leq1/2).\)

Bounding each buyer’s utility regret

Write

\(c_{tj}=\log(v_{tj}/p_j(b)),\qquad c_t=\max\{0,\max_j c_{tj}\},\)

and define

\(h_t=B_t c_t-\sum_j b_{tj}c_{tj}.\)

The rowwise linear maximization defining \(g\) gives

\(g(b)=\sum_t h_t,\qquad h_t\geq0.\)

Let \(s_t=\sum_j b_{tj}\), \(\alpha_t=e^{c_t}\), and

\(H=\max\{1,V/q\}.\)

The aggregate best-response utility is \(B_t(\alpha_t-1)\). Its regret at \(b_t\) equals

\(\displaystyle \mathcal R_t=(B_t-s_t)(\alpha_t-1)+\sum_j b_{tj}(\alpha_t-e^{c_{tj}}).\)

Because \(c_t\geq0\), \(c_{tj}\leq c_t\), and \(e^{c_t}\leq H\), integration of the exponential derivative gives

\(\alpha_t-1\leq Hc_t,\qquad \alpha_t-e^{c_{tj}}\leq H(c_t-c_{tj}).\)

Consequently,

\(\mathcal R_t\leq Hh_t\leq Hg(b).\)

This controls every type separately.

6. Choosing the precision and producing binary-rational output

Put

\(e=\min\{\varepsilon,1\},\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t,\qquad \gamma=\frac{\mu_{\min}e}{2H},\)

and choose

\(s_0=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac{\gamma}{2C}\right\},\qquad \delta=\frac{s_0\gamma}{2}.\)

All these are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length.

Apply Section 4 to obtain rational \(b\in P\) with objective error at most \(\delta\). Section 5 gives

\(\displaystyle g(b)\leq\frac{\delta}{s_0}+Cs_0\leq\gamma.\)

Thus every aggregate regret satisfies

\(\mathcal R_t\leq H\gamma=\mu_{\min}e/2\leq\mu_t e/2.\)

Define

\(z_{tj}=b_{tj}/\mu_t,\qquad p_j=\sum_t\mu_t z_{tj}.\)

These vectors are feasible, satisfy \(p_j\geq q\), and have per-unit-mass regret at most \(e/2\).

We now round them while preserving exact clearing. This step even ensures dyadic output if “binary-rational” is interpreted as a finite binary expansion.

Let \(D\) be a positive integer such that \(D\mu_t\) is integral for every type. The product of the input mass denominators is one such choice, and its bit length is polynomial.

Define

\(K_0=2V\left(\frac{\kappa_{\max}}{q^2}+\frac{m}{q}\right),\qquad \kappa_{\max}=\max_t\kappa_t.\)

Choose an integer \(N\) such that the dyadic grid spacing \(h=D2^{-N}\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle h\leq\min\left\{\frac q2,\frac{e}{2K_0}\right\}.\)

Finally output

\(\widehat z_{tj}=h\left\lfloor z_{tj}/h\right\rfloor,\qquad \widehat p_j=\sum_t\mu_t\widehat z_{tj}.\)

Feasibility, positivity, and representation

Rounding down preserves nonnegativity and every budget constraint. Since \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\),

\(0\leq p_j-\widehat p_j\leq h,\qquad \widehat p_j\geq q-h\geq q/2>0.\)

Market clearing holds exactly by the definition of \(\widehat p\).

Both outputs are dyadic: \(\widehat z_{tj}\) is an integer multiple of \(D2^{-N}\), and \(\mu_t\widehat z_{tj}\) is an integer multiple of \(2^{-N}\).

Utility loss caused by rounding

For any feasible spending vector \(a\) of type \(t\),

\(\displaystyle u_t(a\mid\widehat p)-u_t(a\mid p)=\sum_j v_{tj}a_j\frac{p_j-\widehat p_j}{p_j\widehat p_j}\leq\frac{2\kappa_{\max}Vh}{q^2}.\)

Therefore best-response utility increases by at most this amount.

Lower prices weakly increase the utility of the old portfolio \(z_t\). Removing less than \(h\) from each coordinate at prices \(\widehat p\) decreases utility by at most \(2mVh/q\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Regret}_t(\widehat z_t,\widehat p)\leq\frac e2+\frac{2\kappa_{\max}Vh}{q^2}+\frac{2mVh}{q}=\frac e2+K_0h\leq e\leq\varepsilon.\)

Thus the final output satisfies every requirement.

7. Bit complexity

There are \(m\tau\) optimization variables and polynomially many explicit rational linear constraints.

Every positive input number has logarithmic magnitude bounded by its encoding length. Products, sums, divisions, and extrema defining \(B_{\min},S,q,H,C,\gamma,\delta,K_0\) therefore have polynomial encoding length. In particular, the required logarithmic precision in \(\delta\) and the grid exponent \(N\) are polynomially bounded.

The convex optimization uses polynomially many bit operations, and the final divisions and rounding do likewise. Small support masses affect the requested precision through the logarithm of \(1/\mu_{\min}\), which is already controlled by \(L\).

The construction is therefore a polynomial-time algorithm for the stated support-input problem.

Correct.
