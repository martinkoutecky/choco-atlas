The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), room capacities \(\rho_r\), valuations \(v_{tr}\), budgets \(b_t\), rent per resident \(\ell\), and a feasible type-symmetric fractional rent solution \((x,p)\), decide whether there is a finite rational schedule with phase durations \(\lambda_s\) and type-specific distributions over room sequences such that each sequence for type \(t\) spends fraction \(x_{tr}\) in room \(r\), every room has mass \(\rho_r\) in each phase, and total switching mass is at most \(B\). Minimize \(\sum_t \mu_t\) times the expected number of room changes for a resident of type \(t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Residents form a nonatomic population with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). There are \(m\geq1\) rooms with positive rational capacities \(\rho_r\) summing to \(1\).
The input includes rational valuations \(v_{tr}\geq0\), budgets \(b_t\geq0\), rent per resident \(\ell\geq0\), threshold \(B\geq0\), and a fixed rational type-symmetric fractional solution \((x,p)\). Payments \(p_t\) may be negative; valuations need not satisfy any sum normalization.
Feasibility means \(x_{tr}\geq0\), \(\sum_r x_{tr}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tr}=\rho_r\), \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=\ell\), \(p_t\leq b_t\), and, writing \(u_t=\sum_r x_{tr}v_{tr}-p_t\), \(u_t\geq0\) and \(u_t\geq\sum_r x_{t'r}v_{tr}-p_{t'}\) for every \(t,t'\).
A schedule has any finite number \(k\geq1\) of chronologically ordered phases with positive rational durations \(\lambda_s\) summing to \(1\). For each type \(t\), choose a rational probability distribution \(q_t\) over room sequences \(a\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^k\).
Every sequence with \(q_t(a)>0\) must satisfy \(\sum_{s:a_s=r}\lambda_s=x_{tr}\) for every room \(r\); every phase must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{a:a_s=r}q_t(a)=\rho_r\) for every room \(r\). Fairness and payments apply to the complete lease.
The total switching mass is \(C=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_a q_t(a)\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\mathbf{1}\{a_s\neq a_{s+1}\}\). Initial placement and departure incur no cost, and returning to a room is allowed. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether such a schedule exists with \(C\leq B\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many individual residents and unit-capacity rooms, minimizing total room changes over all ordered Birkhoff–von Neumann implementations of a given fractional allocation is NP-complete in decision form; ordering a supplied decomposition is also NP-complete. [Source](https://comsoc-community.org/archive/comsoc-2023/comsoc/papers/paper28.pdf)
No result settling the exact nonatomic scheduling problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total resident mass and total room capacity to one; capacities are positive binary rationals, and residents are nonatomic.
  • Interpreted feasible fractional rent solution as affordable, individually rational, and envy-free under quasi-linear utility, with signed payments and nonnegative rational valuations, budgets, and rent.
  • Required every positive-probability sequence to realize its type's prescribed time shares exactly, rather than requiring those shares only in expectation.
  • Specified rational sequence probabilities and positive rational phase durations, with no prescribed bound on the finite number of phases.
  • Counted switches only between consecutive phases, allowed returns to previously occupied rooms, and applied fairness and payments over the complete lease.
  • Took the switching threshold to be a nonnegative binary rational and defined L as total binary input length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This holds even when all type masses and room capacities are equal dyadic rationals, the supplied solution is feasible, and rent, budgets, and payments are zero.

The reduction uses an asymmetric two-room clock. Its possible orientations switch at different times, which prevents mixtures within a type from canceling the event needed for the reduction.

1. A switching lower bound

For each type \(t\), define \(d_t=|\{r:x_{tr}>0\}|\).

Every positive-probability sequence must visit exactly these rooms: positive quotas require visits, and zero quotas prohibit visits because every phase has positive duration. Consequently, every such sequence makes at least \(d_t-1\) switches. Thus every schedule satisfies

\(C\geq C_0:=\sum_t\mu_t(d_t-1).\)

If \(C\leq C_0\), equality holds, and every positive-probability sequence visits each supported room in exactly one contiguous block. Indeed, compress consecutive occurrences of the same room. The compressed sequence has at least \(d_t\) blocks; equality in the switching bound forces exactly \(d_t\), so each supported room appears once.

The duration of its block in room \(r\) is then exactly \(x_{tr}\). In particular, every switch time is a sum of the quotas of rooms preceding that switch.

2. Encoding PARTITION

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary integers \(a_0,\ldots,a_{N-1}\), decide whether some subset sums to \(S/2\), where \(S=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}a_j\). See [Karp’s original NP-completeness results](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Set \(n=N+1\), and define positive rational weights

\(w_j=\dfrac{a_j}{15S}\quad(0\leq j<N),\qquad w_N=\dfrac{2}{15}.\)

Their total is

\(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}w_j=\dfrac{1}{15}+\dfrac{2}{15}=\dfrac15.\)

We will use the following equivalence:

A subset of these weights sums to \(1/30\) or \(1/6\) if and only if the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

To prove it, a subset summing to \(1/30\) cannot contain \(w_N=2/15\). Therefore its original indices satisfy

\(\sum_{j\in H}a_j=15S\cdot\dfrac1{30}=\dfrac S2.\)

Conversely, a partition subset gives a weight sum of \(1/30\). Finally, because the total weight is \(1/5\), a subset sums to \(1/6\) exactly when its complement sums to

\(\dfrac15-\dfrac16=\dfrac1{30}.\)

3. The scheduling instance

Let \(M\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(M\geq3n\), and write \(\delta=1/M\). Padding to \(M\) makes all masses and capacities dyadic.

Create rooms \(R_i,P_i,Q_i\) and types \(A_i,D_i,E_i\), for \(0\leq i<n\). Add \(M-3n\) further types, each with its own additional private room. Every type has mass \(\delta\), and every room has capacity \(\delta\). Hence \(m=\tau=M\), and both total mass and total capacity equal \(1\).

All unspecified allocation entries are zero. The positive entries are:

| Type | Allocation |
|---|---|
| \(A_i\) | \(x_{A_i,R_{i+j\bmod n}}=w_j\) for every \(0\leq j<n\); \(x_{A_i,P_i}=x_{A_i,Q_i}=2/5\) |
| \(D_i\) | \(x_{D_i,R_i}=4/5,\quad x_{D_i,P_i}=1/6,\quad x_{D_i,Q_i}=1/30\) |
| \(E_i\) | \(x_{E_i,P_i}=13/30,\quad x_{E_i,Q_i}=17/30\) |
| Each additional type | Allocation \(1\) to its private room |

Every row sums to \(1\). For each room \(R_i\), the sum of the allocation entries across types is

\(\sum_jw_j+\dfrac45=\dfrac15+\dfrac45=1.\)

For the other rooms, those sums are

\(\dfrac25+\dfrac16+\dfrac{13}{30}=1\quad\text{for }P_i,\)

and

\(\dfrac25+\dfrac1{30}+\dfrac{17}{30}=1\quad\text{for }Q_i.\)

Private-room columns also sum to \(1\). Multiplying by \(\delta\) verifies every capacity equation.

To complete the economic input, assign each type a distinct positive integer \(c_t\), and set \(v_{tr}=c_t\) for every room \(r\). Set all budgets, payments, and rent to zero. Then

\(u_t=\sum_rx_{tr}c_t=c_t>0,\)

and the utility type \(t\) obtains from any other type’s complete lease is also \(c_t\). Thus individual rationality, envy-freeness, affordability, and rent balance all hold. The distinct constants ensure that these are distinct types.

Finally, set the switching threshold to

\(B=\delta n(n+4).\)

This is exactly the lower bound from Section 1: each \(A_i\) uses \(n+2\) rooms, each \(D_i\) uses three, each \(E_i\) uses two, and the additional types use one. Hence

\(C_0=\delta\bigl(n(n+1)+2n+n\bigr)=\delta n(n+4)=B.\)

4. A partition gives a schedule

Suppose the original instance has a partition subset \(H\subseteq\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\). Its weights sum to \(1/30\). Let \(J=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\setminus H\), whose weights sum to \(1/6\).

Choose any ordering of \(J\) and any ordering of \(H\). Give every type a single deterministic trajectory:

  • Type \(A_i\) first visits \(R_{i+j\bmod n}\), for the ordered indices \(j\in J\), spending \(w_j\) in each. It then visits \(P_i\) for \(2/5\), \(Q_i\) for \(2/5\), and finally the item rooms corresponding to the ordered indices \(j\in H\).
  • Type \(D_i\) visits \(P_i\) for \(1/6\), \(R_i\) for \(4/5\), and \(Q_i\) for \(1/30\).
  • Type \(E_i\) visits \(Q_i\) for \(17/30\), then \(P_i\) for \(13/30\).
  • Each additional type stays in its private room.

The principal intervals are:

| Time interval | \(A_i\) | \(D_i\) | \(E_i\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| \([0,1/6)\) | Item rooms indexed by \(J\) | \(P_i\) | \(Q_i\) |
| \([1/6,17/30)\) | \(P_i\) | \(R_i\) | \(Q_i\) |
| \([17/30,29/30)\) | \(Q_i\) | \(R_i\) | \(P_i\) |
| \([29/30,1)\) | Item rooms indexed by \(H\) | \(Q_i\) | \(P_i\) |

During each item block indexed by \(j\), the map \(i\mapsto i+j\bmod n\) is a bijection. Thus exactly one entire \(A\)-type occupies each \(R\)-room. During the middle interval, each \(D_i\) occupies \(R_i\). The table also shows that each \(P_i\) and \(Q_i\) always has exactly one occupying type.

Therefore every room has occupancy \(\delta\) throughout the lease. All individual quotas hold by construction.

The item blocks and the two middle blocks give \(n+2\) common phases, all with positive rational durations. Taking probability \(1\) on each prescribed trajectory produces distributions of the required form.

Every trajectory visits each supported room once. Its switching cost therefore attains the lower bound, giving \(C=B\).

5. A schedule at the threshold forces a partition

Suppose a schedule satisfies \(C\leq B\). Since \(B=C_0\), Section 1 implies that every positive-probability trajectory visits each supported room in one contiguous block.

Assume, toward a contradiction, that the PARTITION instance is a no-instance. Consider the two times

\(h_1=\dfrac{13}{30},\qquad h_2=\dfrac{17}{30}.\)

The \(A\)-types cannot switch at either time

An \(A_i\)-trajectory has two special blocks of duration \(2/5\), together with item blocks of total duration \(1/5\).

At any switch, the elapsed time is a sum of completed blocks. If no special block has been completed, that time is at most \(1/5\). If both have been completed, it is at least \(4/5\).

Both \(h_1\) and \(h_2\) lie strictly between these bounds. Consequently, a switch at either time must occur after exactly one completed special block. The preceding item blocks would then sum respectively to

\(h_1-\dfrac25=\dfrac1{30},\qquad h_2-\dfrac25=\dfrac16.\)

The equivalence in Section 2 would give a partition, contrary to the assumption. Thus no positive-probability \(A_i\)-trajectory switches at either time.

The \(D\)-types cannot switch at either time

A \(D_i\)-trajectory has block lengths \(4/5,1/6,1/30\). At a switch, a prefix excluding the \(4/5\) block has duration at most \(1/5\), while a prefix including that block has duration at least \(4/5\).

Hence no \(D_i\)-trajectory switches at \(h_1\) or \(h_2\).

The clock mixture cannot preserve capacity

Fix any \(i\). An \(E_i\)-trajectory has only two possible compressed orders:

  • \(P_i\) followed by \(Q_i\), switching at \(h_1\);
  • \(Q_i\) followed by \(P_i\), switching at \(h_2\).

Let \(\alpha_i\) be the total probability assigned to the first order. The probability of the second order is \(1-\alpha_i\).

Only types \(A_i,D_i,E_i\) can visit \(P_i\), since all other types have zero quota there. We proved that \(A_i\) and \(D_i\) cannot switch at either clock time. Therefore the changes in the expected occupancy of \(P_i\) are exactly

\(-\delta\alpha_i\quad\text{at }h_1,\qquad +\delta(1-\alpha_i)\quad\text{at }h_2.\)

Capacity feasibility requires occupancy to equal \(\delta\) in every phase. Its one-sided limits at each interior time must therefore agree, so both changes must be zero. Since \(\delta>0\), this requires simultaneously \(\alpha_i=0\) and \(\alpha_i=1\), a contradiction.

This argument allows arbitrary mixtures within every type and arbitrarily many finite phases. Thus a schedule with \(C\leq B\) can exist only when the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

6. Encoding size and conclusion

The construction has \(M<6n\) rooms and positive-mass types. Its rational numbers have binary encoding lengths polynomial in the encoding length of the original integers: the only variable denominators arise from \(15S\) and \(M\). The valuation constants can be chosen from \(1\) through \(M\). Writing the complete input therefore takes polynomial time and space.

We have proved the equivalence

\(\text{PARTITION is yes}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed instance admits a schedule with }C\leq B.\)

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the exact nonatomic scheduling problem.
