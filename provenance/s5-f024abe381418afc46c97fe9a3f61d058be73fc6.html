The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(B\), a finite supported set of student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), school capacities \(\kappa_b\), each type’s school preferences, and each type’s priority class at each school, define feasible aggregate allocations \(x_{tb}\) with \(\sum_b x_{tb}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tb}\leq\kappa_b\). Each unit of mass receives one school or the outside option. Use positive-mass blocking pairs and the paper’s internal- and external-stability conditions to define the set of legal allocations. Determine whether that set has an allocation that is first-order-stochastic-dominance greatest for students, and compute one if it exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=|B|\geq1\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|T|\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Capacities \(\kappa_b>0\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type strictly ranks its mutually acceptable schools above the outside option \(\varnothing\). At each school, ordered priority classes and an input tie-breaking order on distinct types induce a strict priority order \(\succ_b\); units of the same type remain tied.
Let \(\mathcal F\) contain all real allocations \(x_{tb}\geq0\), zero on unacceptable pairs, with \(\sum_bx_{tb}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tb}\leq\kappa_b\); set \(x_{t\varnothing}=\mu_t-\sum_bx_{tb}\).
An acceptable pair \((t,b)\) blocks \(x\) if \(\sum_{c:\,b\succ_t c}x_{tc}>0\), including \(c=\varnothing\), and either \(\sum_sx_{sb}<\kappa_b\) or \(x_{sb}>0\) for some \(s\) with \(t\succ_b s\). Allocation \(y\) blocks \(x\) if \(y_{tb}>0\) for some pair blocking \(x\).
A family \(\mathcal K\subseteq\mathcal F\) is legal if no \(x,y\in\mathcal K\), including \(x=y\), satisfy this blocking relation, and every \(x\in\mathcal F\setminus\mathcal K\) is blocked by some \(y\in\mathcal K\). Define \(\mathcal A\) as the union of all legal families, empty if none exist.
An allocation \(x^*\in\mathcal A\) is student-greatest if, for every type, its distribution \((x^*_{tc}/\mu_t)_c\) first-order stochastically dominates the corresponding distribution in every allocation in \(\mathcal A\), including the outside option.

Is deciding whether \(\mathcal A\) has a student-greatest allocation, and computing one when it does, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the decision problem NP-hard?

For finite students with strict preferences and school priorities, the legal family is unique and has a student-greatest assignment computable in \(O(|E|)\) time, where \(E\) is the acceptable-pair set. [Faenza–Zhang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.08506)
No result settling this precise continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total student mass to one and expressed positive rational school capacities in the same units.
  • Specified mutually acceptable pairs, strict student preferences, and an outside option below every acceptable school; unacceptable pairs receive zero mass.
  • Resolved priority classes using supplied fixed tie-breaking orders on distinct types, following the discrete model's use of resolved priorities; indistinguishable units within a type remain tied.
  • Allowed real allocations and defined positive-mass blocking using positive mass at worse options together with either spare capacity or positive mass of a strictly lower-priority type.
  • Carried internal and external stability over the entire feasible allocation space, including self-blocking in internal stability.
  • Because existence and uniqueness of a continuous legal family were unspecified, defined legal allocations as the union of all legal families, with an empty union when none exist.
  • Interpreted student optimality as typewise first-order stochastic dominance of normalized assignment distributions, including outside-option mass.

The general polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question remains unresolved. I prove three advances beyond the supplied notes:

  • Every individual legal family has a unique student-greatest allocation, computable in polynomial time from its support.
  • Checking whether a supplied support defines a legal family belongs to $\mathrm{coNP}$.

I also give an explicit example showing that aggregating the student-greatest legal assignment of a finite replicated instance can produce an allocation that is not legal in the continuous instance.

1. Blocking and support fixed points

Let $E\subseteq T\times B$ be the acceptable-pair set and let $N=|E|$. Coordinates outside $E$ are always zero.

For $e=(t,b)\in E$, define
$$ d_e(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{c:\,c\succeq_t b}x_{tc}, \qquad v_b(x)=\kappa_b-\sum_s x_{sb}, \qquad \ell_e(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_b s}x_{sb}. $$
Here the first sum runs over acceptable schools weakly preferred to $b$. Consequently, $d_e(x)$ is the mass assigned strictly below $b$, including the outside option.

All three expressions are nonnegative on $\mathcal F$. The specified blocking condition is therefore exactly
$$ e\text{ blocks }x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_e(x)>0 \ \text{ and }\ \bigl(v_b(x)+\ell_e(x)>0\bigr). $$

For a family $\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal F$, write
$$ \operatorname{supp}(\mathcal H) = \{e\in E:\text{some }x\in\mathcal H\text{ has }x_e>0\}. $$
For $S\subseteq E$, define
$$ X(S)=\{x\in\mathcal F:\text{no pair in }S\text{ blocks }x\}, \qquad H(S)=\operatorname{supp}(X(S)). $$

Proposition 1. A family $\mathcal K$ is legal if and only if
$$ \mathcal K=X(S),\qquad H(S)=S $$
for some $S\subseteq E$.

Proof. Suppose $\mathcal K$ is legal and set $S=\operatorname{supp}(\mathcal K)$.

If $x\in\mathcal K$ were blocked by $e\in S$, some $y\in\mathcal K$ would satisfy $y_e>0$ and hence block $x$. Internal stability excludes this, including when $x=y$. Thus $\mathcal K\subseteq X(S)$.

If $x\in X(S)\setminus\mathcal K$, external stability would supply $y\in\mathcal K$ blocking $x$. The witnessing pair would belong to $S$, contradicting $x\in X(S)$. Thus $\mathcal K=X(S)$, and taking supports gives $H(S)=S$.

Conversely, suppose $H(S)=S$. Every positive coordinate of an allocation in $X(S)$ belongs to $S$, and no pair in $S$ blocks another allocation in $X(S)$. This proves internal stability. If $x\notin X(S)$, some $e\in S$ blocks $x$. Because $S=H(S)$, an allocation $y\in X(S)$ has $y_e>0$ and blocks $x$. This proves external stability. $\square$

Define the feasibility polytope restricted to $S$ by
$$ \mathcal F_S=\{x\in\mathcal F:x_e=0\text{ for }e\notin S\}, $$
and let
$$ R(S)=\{x\in\mathcal F_S:\text{no pair in }S\text{ blocks }x\}. $$
These are precisely the ordinarily stable allocations in the instance obtained by retaining only $S$. Deleting other pairs does not change blocking on retained pairs for allocations supported on $S$: all deleted coordinates are zero, and outside mass is unchanged.

Always,
$$ R(S)\subseteq X(S). $$
At a fixed point $H(S)=S$, every allocation in $X(S)$ is supported on $S$. Hence
$$ H(S)=S \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X(S)=R(S). $$

Thus every legal family is an ordinary stable-allocation family in its support subinstance.

2. Faces and polynomial-size witnesses

The following facts justify using finite rational certificates while retaining all real allocations.

Proposition 2. For every $S\subseteq E$, the set $X(S)$ is a finite union of faces of $\mathcal F$. Every linear functional that has a maximizer over $X(S)$ has a maximizing vertex of $\mathcal F$ in $X(S)$.

Proof. Nonblocking at $e=(t,b)$ is equivalent to
$$ d_e(x)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad v_b(x)=0,\ \ell_e(x)=0. $$
Each imposed equality sets a nonnegative affine function on $\mathcal F$ to zero, so its nonempty solution set is a face. Intersections of these faces remain faces when nonempty.

Distributing the two alternatives for each $e\in S$ expresses $X(S)$ as a union of at most $2^{|S|}$ faces. Each nonempty face is the convex hull of its vertices, which are vertices of $\mathcal F$. A linear functional attains its maximum on that face at one of those vertices. Taking the finite union proves the assertion. $\square$

The polytope $\mathcal F$ is compact because $0\leq x_{tb}\leq\mu_t$. Consequently, every $X(S)$ is compact. Proposition 1 also makes $\mathcal A$ a finite union of such sets, so $\mathcal A$ is compact.

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all masses and capacities. Its binary length is bounded by $L$. Define
$$ G=\mathcal F\cap D^{-1}\mathbb Z^E. $$

Proposition 3. Every vertex of $\mathcal F$, and every vertex of $\mathcal F_S$, belongs to $G$ and has polynomial-size coordinates.

Proof. After replacing $x$ by $Dx$, all row and column bounds are integers. The coefficient matrix consists of the bipartite vertex-edge incidence matrix and nonnegativity rows.

Negating the school rows turns every edge column into a column with one entry $1$ and one entry $-1$. This matrix is totally unimodular: for any square submatrix, expand along a column having at most one nonzero entry; if there is no such column, all columns have two opposite entries and the rows are linearly dependent. Appending nonnegativity rows preserves total unimodularity by determinant expansion.

At a vertex, a nonsingular square system of active constraints therefore has determinant $1$ or $-1$, so its solution is integral after scaling. Also,
$$ 0\leq Dx_e\leq D, $$
which gives polynomial-size encodings.

Finally, $\mathcal F_S$ is a face of $\mathcal F$, obtained by setting nonnegative coordinates to zero. Its vertices are vertices of $\mathcal F$. $\square$

In particular,
$$ H(S)\nsubseteq S $$
has a polynomial-size witness: a grid allocation $z\in G\cap X(S)$ with a positive coordinate outside $S$. Indeed, any real witness lies in a branch face from Proposition 2, and maximizing its positive outside coordinate on that face gives such a grid vertex.

3. A greatest allocation within every legal family

For each type $t$, list its acceptable schools in preference order as
$$ b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,d_t}, $$
and define prefix masses
$$ C_{t,j}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}x_{t,b_{t,i}} \qquad (1\leq j\leq d_t). $$
Because $\mu_t>0$ is fixed, student stochastic dominance is equivalent to comparison of all these prefixes. The prefix including the outside option always has mass $\mu_t$.

3.1 Existence under stochastic dominance

Theorem 4. For every $S\subseteq E$, the set $R(S)$ has a unique allocation $g_S$ that stochastically dominates every allocation in $R(S)$ for every type.

Proof. First consider the finite instance obtained by scaling by $D$ and replacing each type $t$ by $D\mu_t$ individual copies. School $b$ has capacity $D\kappa_b$. Each copy has its type’s preferences restricted to $S$.

At every school, order copies first by their type’s priority, and within each type use the same order
$$ t^1\succ_b t^2\succ_b\cdots\succ_b t^{D\mu_t}. $$

We use finite student-proposing deferred acceptance. Its needed properties follow directly:

  • Each individual proposes in preference order.
  • A school retains its highest-priority proposals up to capacity.
  • Each individual-school pair is proposed at most once, so the procedure terminates.
  • If an individual prefers a school to the final assignment, that school rejected the individual and subsequently retained only higher-priority individuals. Hence the final assignment is stable.

For completeness, no rejected pair can belong to a stable assignment. Suppose otherwise and consider the first rejection of a pair $(i,b)$ belonging to some stable assignment $M$. At that rejection, $b$ retains its capacity’s worth of individuals ranked above $i$. Every retained individual $j$ proposed to $b$. If $j$ preferred $M(j)$ to $b$, then $j$ would already have been rejected by $M(j)$, contradicting the choice of the first rejection. Since $M$ assigns $i$ to $b$, at least one retained $j$ is not assigned to $b$ in $M$. That individual prefers $b$ to $M(j)$ and has higher priority than $i$, so $(j,b)$ blocks $M$, a contradiction.

It follows that the final finite assignment $M^*$ gives every individual a school weakly preferred to that individual’s assignment in every finite stable assignment.

Aggregate $M^*$ and divide by $D$, obtaining $g_S$. This allocation belongs to $R(S)$: a continuous blocking pair would produce a finite blocking pair by choosing a copy assigned below the school and pointing either to a vacancy or to an occupant of a strictly lower-priority type.

Now let $x\in G\cap R(S)$. Construct a finite assignment $M_x$ by assigning, for each type, its earliest copies to its best school in the quantities $Dx_{tb}$, then its next copies to its next school, and finally the remaining copies outside.

This assignment is finite stable. To verify this, take a copy of type $t$ assigned below $b$. Any copy of the same type assigned to $b$ has an earlier index and therefore higher priority. Moreover, stability of $x$ implies that $b$ is full and contains no strictly lower-priority type. Thus the proposed individual-school pair cannot block.

Individual optimality of $M^*$ against $M_x$ gives, after summing indicators for each preference prefix,
$$ C_{t,j}(g_S)\geq C_{t,j}(x) $$
for every grid allocation $x\in R(S)$.

Finally, let $x\in R(S)$ be real. The face argument of Proposition 2 applies to $\mathcal F_S$, so $x$ belongs to a face whose vertices also belong to $R(S)$. Those vertices are grid allocations by Proposition 3. Expressing $x$ as their convex combination and using linearity of each prefix proves the same inequalities for $x$.

The prefixes determine all school coordinates and then the outside mass. Therefore two allocations satisfying this greatestness property must coincide. $\square$

The replication in this proof establishes existence and dominance; its potentially exponential size is not used as an efficient algorithm.

3.2 Polynomial computation from the support

We use the following established optimization result: on a finite bipartite graph with strict orders on incident edges, rational vertex quotas and edge capacities, a linear objective can be optimized over stable real allocations in polynomial time. Stability requires an unsaturated edge whose two endpoints can increase it using unused quota or mass on less-preferred edges. This is the bipartite optimal stable-allocation result of [Dean and Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf), Section 4.

Its hypotheses hold for $R(S)$: the vertices are types and schools, the quotas are $\mu_t$ and $\kappa_b$, and the incident-edge orders are strict. Give $(t,b)\in S$ the redundant edge bound
$$ u_{tb}=\min(\mu_t,\kappa_b). $$
If $x_{tb}=u_{tb}$, either $d_{(t,b)}(x)=0$ or $v_b(x)+\ell_{(t,b)}(x)=0$. Thus the extra edge-saturation exception creates no difference between the two stability definitions. All data have polynomial binary length.

Set
$$ F(x)=\sum_t\sum_{j=1}^{d_t}C_{t,j}(x). $$
By Theorem 4, $g_S$ maximizes every summand over $R(S)$. If $x\neq g_S$, some prefix differs, and all prefix differences
$$ C_{t,j}(g_S)-C_{t,j}(x) $$
are nonnegative. Hence $F(g_S)>F(x)$. Optimizing this linear objective therefore computes $g_S$ uniquely.

Corollary 5. Given the support $S$ of a legal family, its unique student-greatest allocation can be computed in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

This does not yet find a legal support, nor compare greatest allocations belonging to different legal families.

4. Recognizing a legal support is in $\mathrm{coNP}$

Define
$$ J(S)=\operatorname{supp}(R(S)). $$
The set $J(S)$ is computable in polynomial time: for each $e\in S$, optimize $x_e$ over $R(S)$ using the optimization theorem above. The optimum is positive exactly when $e\in J(S)$.

Theorem 6. For every $S\subseteq E$,
$$ H(S)=S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J(S)=S\ \text{ and }\ H(S)\subseteq S. $$

Proof. If $H(S)=S$, Section 1 gives $X(S)=R(S)$, so $J(S)=S$.

Conversely, $R(S)\subseteq X(S)$ always implies
$$ J(S)\subseteq H(S). $$
Thus $J(S)=S$ and $H(S)\subseteq S$ force $H(S)=S$. $\square$

The first condition is polynomial-time decidable. The failure of the second has the polynomial-size grid witness established in Section 2. Therefore recognizing a legal support belongs to $\mathrm{coNP}$.

More explicitly, let $Q(S,z)$ mean
$$ z\in G\cap X(S) \quad\text{and}\quad z_e>0\text{ for some }e\notin S. $$
This predicate is checkable by exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time. Malformed encodings are assigned value false. We have
$$ H(S)=S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J(S)=S\ \text{ and }\ \forall z\,\neg Q(S,z), $$
where $z$ ranges over polynomial-length encodings.

Consequently, both legal-family existence and membership of a polynomially encoded rational allocation in $\mathcal A$ belong to $\Sigma_2^p$.

5. A $\Delta_3^p$ algorithm for the general task

Theorem 7. The general decision problem belongs to
$$ \Delta_3^p=\mathrm P^{\Sigma_2^p}. $$
When a student-greatest allocation exists, it can be computed in
$$ \mathrm{FP}^{\Sigma_2^p}. $$

Proof. First ask a $\Sigma_2^p$ oracle whether a legal support exists, using the characterization in Section 4. If none exists, then $\mathcal A=\varnothing$, and the answer is negative.

Otherwise, define
$$ M_{t,j}=\max_{x\in\mathcal A}C_{t,j}(x). $$
These maxima exist by compactness. Since each legal family $X(S)=R(S)$ has greatest allocation $g_S$,
$$ M_{t,j}=\max_{S:\,H(S)=S}C_{t,j}(g_S). $$
Theorem 4 constructs $g_S$ on the grid, so every $DM_{t,j}$ is an integer between zero and $D$.

For an integer $k$, the threshold query
$$ M_{t,j}\geq \frac{k}{D} $$
is equivalent to
$$ \exists S\ \forall z: \quad J(S)=S,\quad \neg Q(S,z),\quad D C_{t,j}(g_S)\geq k. $$
The predicates and $g_S$ are polynomial-time computable. Hence this is a $\Sigma_2^p$ query.

Binary search computes every $DM_{t,j}$ using $O(\log D)$ queries. There are $N$ prefixes and $\log D$ is polynomially bounded by $L$.

Set $M_{t,0}=0$ and construct
$$ \widehat x_{t,b_{t,j}}=M_{t,j}-M_{t,j-1}, \qquad \widehat x_{t\varnothing}=\mu_t-M_{t,d_t}. $$
For a type with no acceptable school, set its outside mass to $\mu_t$.

The maxima are nondecreasing in the prefix index and bounded by $\mu_t$, so these quantities are nonnegative and satisfy the row constraints. Check school capacities by exact arithmetic. If feasibility fails, reject.

If feasible, ask the $\Sigma_2^p$ membership query
$$ \widehat x\in\mathcal A \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists S\ \forall z: \quad J(S)=S,\quad \neg Q(S,z),\quad \widehat x\in X(S). $$

If membership holds, $\widehat x$ attains every prefix maximum and is student-greatest.

Conversely, any student-greatest allocation must attain every $M_{t,j}$. Its coordinates are therefore exactly those of $\widehat x$. Thus rejection is correct when feasibility or membership fails.

All non-oracle work, all query encodings, and the output have polynomial size. $\square$

For the degenerate case $E=\varnothing$, the unique feasible allocation assigns everyone outside and is student-greatest. The theorem addresses arbitrary nonempty acceptable-pair sets as well.

This upper bound is stronger than the supplied $\Sigma_3^p$ membership result, but it does not settle the requested dichotomy.

6. Actual finite legal optima need not aggregate to continuous legal allocations

The following example strengthens the replication obstruction: the failure occurs even for the finite student-greatest legal assignment.

Take three types $u,v,w$ and three schools $a,b,c$, with
$$ \mu_u=\frac12,\qquad \mu_v=\mu_w=\frac14, $$
and
$$ \kappa_a=\frac12,\qquad \kappa_b=\kappa_c=\frac14. $$

Preferences and priorities, restricted to acceptable pairs, are:

| Type | Preferences |
|---|---|
| $u$ | $a\succ_u b\succ_u\varnothing$ |
| $v$ | $b\succ_v a\succ_v c\succ_v\varnothing$ |
| $w$ | $a\succ_w c\succ_w\varnothing$ |

| School | Priority |
|---|---|
| $a$ | $w\succ_a v\succ_a u$ |
| $b$ | $u\succ_b v$ |
| $c$ | $w\succ_c v$ |

For the proof, measure all quantities in units of $\frac14$. Supplies are then $2,1,1$ and capacities are $2,1,1$. Scaling all quantities by the same positive factor preserves feasibility and every strict positivity condition defining blocking.

6.1 The continuous legal family is a singleton

Consider
$$ g_{ub}=1,\qquad g_{va}=g_{wa}=1, $$
with one unit of $u$ outside and all other coordinates zero.

This allocation is ordinarily stable. Type $u$ cannot displace $v$ or $w$ at $a$, and $b$ is full with $u$. Type $v$ cannot displace $u$ at $b$. Type $w$ receives its first choice.

Let
$$ S_g=\{(u,b),(v,a),(w,a)\}. $$
I claim that $X(S_g)=\{g\}$.

Take $x\in X(S_g)$. Nonblocking at $(w,a)$ forces $x_{wa}=1$: otherwise $w$ has mass below $a$, while capacity $2$ cannot be filled using only $w$, whose total mass is $1$. There must be vacancy or lower-priority occupancy.

Given $x_{wa}=1$, nonblocking at $(v,a)$ forces
$$ x_{va}+x_{vb}=1. $$
Indeed, if $v$ has mass below $a$, a full school containing no type below $v$ would require $x_{va}=1$.

Nonblocking at $(u,b)$ implies
$$ x_{ua}+x_{ub}=2 \quad\text{or}\quad x_{ub}=1, $$
because no acceptable type at $b$ has priority above $u$.

The first alternative would force $x_{ua}=x_{ub}=1$: $a$ has at most one unit available after $w$, and $b$ has capacity one. It would then force $x_{va}=x_{vb}=0$, contradicting the previous equality. Hence $x_{ub}=1$. School $b$ then excludes $v$, so $x_{va}=1$. School $a$ is full with $v,w$, and all remaining coordinates are determined. Thus $x=g$.

Therefore $H(S_g)=S_g$, and $\{g\}$ is legal.

Every legal family must contain every ordinarily stable allocation, because an ordinarily stable allocation cannot be blocked by any allocation. It must consequently contain $g$. Since $g$ blocks every allocation outside $X(S_g)=\{g\}$, internal stability excludes every other allocation. Hence
$$ \mathcal A=\{g\}. $$

6.2 The finite replicated legal optimum is different

Replicate at denominator four. There are individuals $u_1,u_2,v,w$, and school capacities are $2,1,1$. Use priorities
$$ a:\ w\succ v\succ u_1\succ u_2, \qquad b:\ u_1\succ u_2\succ v, \qquad c:\ w\succ v. $$

Consider the two finite assignments:

| Individual | $M_0$ | $M_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $u_1$ | $b$ | $a$ |
| $u_2$ | $\varnothing$ | $\varnothing$ |
| $v$ | $a$ | $b$ |
| $w$ | $a$ | $a$ |

Assignment $M_0$ is ordinarily stable. The only blocking pair of $M_1$ is $(u_2,b)$, which occurs in neither assignment. Thus the two assignments do not block each other.

To prove external stability, let
$$ S_f=\{(u_1,a),(u_1,b),(v,a),(v,b),(w,a)\}. $$
Any assignment unblocked by $S_f$ must assign $w$ to $a$: otherwise $(w,a)$ blocks. It must assign $u_1$ to $a$ or $b$: otherwise $(u_1,b)$ blocks.

If $u_1$ is at $a$, then $a$ is full with $w,u_1$. Nonblocking at $(v,a)$ forces $v$ to $b$, giving $M_1$.

If $u_1$ is at $b$, then nonblocking at $(v,a)$ forces $v$ to $a$, giving $M_0$.

In both cases $a$ and $b$ are full, and $u_2$ is outside. Thus every other finite assignment is blocked by an edge in $S_f$, and that edge occurs in $M_0$ or $M_1$. The family $\{M_0,M_1\}$ is legal.

It is also the unique finite legal family: every legal family contains $M_0$, and assignments unblocked by $M_0$ are already restricted to these two possibilities by the same argument. External stability then forces inclusion of $M_1$, which $M_0$ does not block.

Assignment $M_1$ weakly improves every individual over $M_0$, so it is the finite student-greatest legal assignment.

Its aggregate $h$ has
$$ h_{ua}=1,\qquad h_{vb}=h_{wa}=1, $$
and one unit of $u$ outside. But $(u,b)$ blocks $h$: type $u$ has outside mass, and $b$ contains lower-priority type $v$. Since $g_{ub}>0$, allocation $g$ blocks $h$. Consequently,
$$ h\notin\mathcal A. $$

Thus even aggregating an actual finite student-greatest legal assignment does not solve the continuous problem.

7. What remains open

The new extremal theorem resolves the stochastic-dominance issue within a given legal family. The unresolved work concerns legal supports across the entire instance:

  • Whether every instance has a legal family, and whether that family is unique.
  • Whether the union of legal families always has a student-greatest allocation.
  • Whether a relevant legal support can be found in polynomial time, or whether deciding global greatestness is NP-hard.

The $\Delta_3^p$ algorithm is an oracle upper bound, not a polynomial-time algorithm without an oracle.
