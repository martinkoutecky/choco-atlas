Partial result: stable bounds encode extremal monotone fixed points

I do not settle polynomial-time solvability versus $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. I prove a new reduction for the precise input model:

Computing either requested stable bound is at least as hard, under a polynomial-time reduction using one call to the bound-computation procedure, as computing the corresponding extremal fixed point of a system of elementary monotone piecewise-affine equations.

In particular, exact values of stopping simple stochastic games reduce to either stable-bound operation. The constructed markets have dyadic masses, at most two polyhedral cells per worker choice, and at most eight cells per firm choice.

All results below are proved independently of the results in NOTES.

1. The source fixed-point problem

Consider a finite system
$$ z_v=g_v\bigl((z_w)_{w\in P_v}\bigr),\qquad v\in V, $$
where every variable lies in $[0,1]$. Each gate has at most two input occurrences and is one of:

  • a constant in $[0,1]$;
  • the identity;
  • the minimum or maximum of two inputs;
  • a rational affine function with nonnegative coefficients that maps its input cube into $[0,1]$.

Repeated input occurrences are permitted. The system defines a continuous, coordinatewise nondecreasing map
$$ G:[0,1]^V\longrightarrow[0,1]^V. $$

Both extremal fixed points exist. Indeed, define
$$ \ell^{(0)}=0,\qquad \ell^{(t+1)}=G(\ell^{(t)}). $$
Since $0\le G(0)$, monotonicity proves inductively that this sequence is coordinatewise nondecreasing. It is bounded by $1$, so it converges coordinatewise to some $\ell$. Continuity gives $G(\ell)=\ell$.

If $q$ is any fixed point, then $\ell^{(0)}\le q$; monotonicity proves $\ell^{(t)}\le q$ for every $t$. Taking limits gives $\ell\le q$. Thus $\ell$ is the least fixed point.

Starting at $1$ and iterating $G$ similarly yields the greatest fixed point.

These existence arguments provide no polynomial iteration bound.

2. A family of admissible choice maps

The following lemma supplies all choices used in the reduction.

Lemma. Partition the coordinates of a box into input coordinates $s$, output coordinates $t$, and inactive coordinates $r$. For each output coordinate $o$, let $f_o(s)$ be continuous, nonnegative, bounded by that coordinate’s capacity, and coordinatewise nonincreasing. Define
$$ H(s,t,r)= \left( s,\, \bigl(\min\{t_o,f_o(s)\}\bigr)_o,\, 0 \right). $$
Then $H$ is continuous, contractive, and path independent.

Proof. Continuity and $0\le H(u)\le u$ follow directly from the formula.

Write two offers as $u=(s,t,r)$ and $v=(s',t',r')$. Put
$$ \widehat s=s\vee s'. $$
Because $f_o$ is nonincreasing,
$$ f_o(\widehat s)\le f_o(s). $$
For any nonnegative numbers $a,b,p,q$ with $q\le p$,
$$ \min\{\max\{\min\{a,p\},b\},q\} = \min\{\max\{a,b\},q\}. $$
To verify this identity, truncating either side at $q$ makes the inner truncation at $p$ immaterial, since $p\ge q$.

Consequently, the output coordinate $o$ of $H(H(u)\vee v)$ is
$$ \begin{aligned} &\min\left\{ \max\{\min\{t_o,f_o(s)\},t'_o\}, f_o(\widehat s) \right\}\\ &\hspace{1cm}= \min\{\max\{t_o,t'_o\},f_o(\widehat s)\}. \end{aligned} $$
This is exactly the same coordinate of $H(u\vee v)$. Both maps return $\widehat s$ on input coordinates and zero on inactive coordinates. Hence
$$ H(u\vee v)=H(H(u)\vee v). $$
This proves the lemma. $\square$

For the elementary gates in Section 1, the function $1-g_v$ is nonnegative, continuous, and nonincreasing. Therefore it can serve as an output cap in this lemma.

3. Encoding a gate system as a market

3.1. Preparing the directed gate graph

Represent each input occurrence by a directed wire from the gate supplying that input to the gate receiving it.

We first make three polynomial-size modifications.

  • For every original gate, add an identity readout gate receiving its output.
  • Replace fan-out larger than two by binary trees of identity gates.
  • Subdivide every wire by a fresh identity gate.

The resulting graph has at most two incoming wires and at most two outgoing wires at each gate. Every original gate has an outgoing wire, and no wire has identical endpoints.

These modifications preserve fixed points bijectively. At a fixed point, every added identity gate must equal its predecessor; conversely, the values of all added gates are uniquely determined by the original values. They also preserve coordinatewise comparison: added coordinates are copies of original coordinates.

Denote the expanded graph by $(V,E)$. Gates with no outgoing wires are the identity readouts.

3.2. Types, masses, and capacities

Create:

  • one firm type $F_v$ for every gate $v\in V$;
  • one worker type $W_e$ for every wire $e\in E$.

Pad each side with dummy types until its cardinality is a power of two. Let the resulting numbers of workers and firms be $N_W$ and $N_F$. Give every worker mass $1/N_W$ and every firm mass $1/N_F$.

Thus all masses are positive dyadic rationals, both mass sums equal $1$, and every pair has the same capacity
$$ c=\frac{1}{N_WN_F}. $$

Dummy types choose zero. The padding increases each type count by less than a factor of two.

For a wire $e=(v,w)$, the worker $W_e$ has two active pairs, with firms $F_v$ and $F_w$. Define normalized quantities
$$ y_e=\frac{X_{W_e,F_v}}c,\qquad x_e=\frac{X_{W_e,F_w}}c. $$
All other pairs involving $W_e$ are inactive.

3.3. Worker choices

For $e=(v,w)$, the worker keeps its offer from $F_v$ unchanged and caps its offer from $F_w$:
$$ [C_e(u)]_{F_v}=u_{F_v}, $$
$$ [C_e(u)]_{F_w} = \min\{u_{F_w},c-u_{F_v}\}. $$
Every other coordinate is returned as zero.

The endpoints $v,w$ are distinct, so these are distinct coordinates. This choice satisfies the lemma in Section 2.

3.4. Firm choices

Firm $F_v$ keeps all incoming-wire offers unchanged. Its common cap on every outgoing-wire offer is
$$ c\left( 1-g_v\left( \left(\frac{u_{W_e}}c\right)_{e\text{ incoming to }v} \right) \right). $$
Accordingly, for an outgoing wire $e$,
$$ [D_v(u)]_{W_e} = \min\left\{ u_{W_e}, c\left( 1-g_v\left( \left(\frac{u_{W_f}}c\right)_{f\text{ incoming to }v} \right) \right) \right\}. $$
Inactive coordinates are returned as zero.

For a readout firm, there are no outgoing coordinates; it simply keeps its incoming offer.

Since $g_v$ is nondecreasing, the output cap is nonincreasing in the incoming offers. The lemma again proves all required choice axioms.

3.5. Explicit polyhedral descriptions

Each elementary gate has at most two closed rational polyhedral regions with affine formulas:

  • minimum and maximum split according to the comparison of their two inputs;
  • constants, identities, and affine gates need one region.

On each such region, split according to whether each outgoing offer is below or above the affine cap. There are at most two outgoing coordinates, so this requires at most four further cases.

Thus each firm choice has at most
$$ 2\cdot 4=8 $$
closed rational polyhedral cells. Each worker choice has at most two.

The formulas agree on all boundaries because they represent continuous minimum and maximum functions. These cells cover the entire choice domain, including unrestricted inactive offer coordinates.

There are polynomially many types and pairs. The capacities, cell inequalities, and affine formulas have polynomial encoding length. Even if all matrices and formulas are written densely, the total description length remains polynomial in the source description length.

3.6. Stability is exactly the gate equations

Proposition. Stable matrices in the constructed market correspond bijectively to fixed points of the expanded gate system.

Proof. Individual rationality forces inactive coordinates to be zero. On an active wire $e=(v,w)$, it implies
$$ x_e\le 1-y_e $$
and
$$ y_e\le 1-g_v\bigl((x_f)_{f\text{ incoming to }v}\bigr). $$

Suppose the first inequality is strict. Increasing the pair $(W_e,F_w)$ changes only the worker’s output offer and a firm input offer. The firm keeps the increased input unchanged, while the worker accepts an increase up to its unchanged cap $1-y_e$. A sufficiently small positive increment therefore blocks.

Hence stability requires
$$ x_e=1-y_e. $$

Similarly, if the second inequality is strict, increase the pair $(W_e,F_v)$. The worker keeps this input unchanged; the firm accepts an increase up to its output cap, which does not depend on that outgoing coordinate. This pair blocks. Thus
$$ y_e= 1-g_v\bigl((x_f)_{f\text{ incoming to }v}\bigr). $$
The choices may discard other links during these increases, as the task permits.

Combining the equalities gives
$$ x_e= g_v\bigl((x_f)_{f\text{ incoming to }v}\bigr) \qquad(e\text{ outgoing from }v). $$
In particular, all outgoing wires of $v$ carry the same value. Define that value as $z_v$. For a readout gate, define $z_v$ as its incoming value. The displayed equations are exactly
$$ z_v=g_v\bigl((z_w)_{w\in P_v}\bigr). $$

Conversely, given a fixed point $z$, set, for every wire $e=(v,w)$,
$$ x_e=z_v,\qquad y_e=1-z_v, $$
and set inactive entries to zero. The matrix is individually rational. Every active output is at its owner’s cap, so that owner rejects every increase in the targeted coordinate. Every inactive pair is rejected by its worker. There is therefore no blocking pair.

The construction in each direction is unique, proving bijectivity. $\square$

3.7. The firm order becomes coordinatewise order

Proposition. If $X,Y$ correspond to fixed points $z,\widetilde z$, then
$$ X\succeq_F Y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\ge\widetilde z $$
coordinatewise.

Proof. Every firm keeps its incoming coordinates unchanged. Therefore
$$ D_v(X^v\vee Y^v)=X^v $$
requires every incoming coordinate of $X$ to be at least the corresponding coordinate of $Y$.

Conversely, suppose these incoming inequalities hold. Monotonicity of $g_v$ implies that the output cap at $X$ is at most the output cap at $Y$. Stability identifies outgoing quantities with those caps. Thus the maximum outgoing offer is the outgoing quantity at $Y$, and firm choice truncates it to the cap at $X$. Consequently,
$$ D_v(X^v\vee Y^v)=X^v. $$

The firm order is therefore equivalent to comparison of all wire signals $x_e$. Every non-readout gate has an outgoing wire, so these comparisons cover its signal $z_v$. Readout signals equal their predecessors. Hence the comparisons are equivalent to $z\ge\widetilde z$. $\square$

4. Two known stable matrices expose the extremal fixed point

We now reduce extremal fixed-point computation to the operations in the task.

4.1. Least fixed point from a least stable upper bound

Let
$$ x=F(x) $$
be an elementary monotone system on $[0,1]^n$. Introduce control variables $a,b$ and auxiliary variables $r,t_1,\ldots,t_n$, with equations
$$ a=a,\qquad b=b,\qquad r=\min\{a,b\}, $$
$$ t_v=F_v(x),\qquad x_v=\min\{r,t_v\}. $$
Every equation is an elementary gate equation.

There are two explicitly known fixed points:
$$ A:\quad a=1,\ b=0,\ r=0,\ x=0,\ t=F(0), $$
$$ B:\quad a=0,\ b=1,\ r=0,\ x=0,\ t=F(0). $$
Their corresponding market matrices are rational and stable by Section 3.

In coordinatewise order, every common upper fixed point of $A,B$ must have
$$ a=b=1. $$
It then has $r=1$, so its remaining equations become
$$ t=F(x),\qquad x=t. $$
Thus $x$ is a fixed point of $F$.

Conversely, every fixed point $q$ of $F$ yields a common upper fixed point by setting
$$ a=b=r=1,\qquad x=t=q. $$
To check the comparison with $A,B$, the only nonautomatic comparison is
$$ q\ge F(0). $$
It holds because $q=F(q)$, $q\ge0$, and $F$ is nondecreasing.

Therefore common upper fixed points correspond exactly to fixed points of $F$, preserving their order. The least common upper fixed point has
$$ x=\operatorname{lfp}(F). $$

The order isomorphism in Section 3 shows that the least stable upper bound of the two supplied matrices encodes this vector. A designated wire carrying $x_v$ recovers its coordinate by dividing the relevant matching entry by $c$.

This is a polynomial-time reduction using one bound-computation call.

4.2. Greatest fixed point from a greatest stable lower bound

Use instead
$$ a=a,\qquad b=b,\qquad r=\max\{a,b\}, $$
$$ t_v=F_v(x),\qquad x_v=\max\{r,t_v\}. $$
The two known fixed points are
$$ A:\quad a=1,\ b=0,\ r=1,\ x=1,\ t=F(1), $$
$$ B:\quad a=0,\ b=1,\ r=1,\ x=1,\ t=F(1). $$

Every common lower fixed point must have $a=b=0$, hence $r=0$ and $x=F(x)$. Conversely, a fixed point $q$ of $F$ gives a common lower fixed point because
$$ q=F(q)\le F(1). $$

The greatest common lower fixed point consequently encodes
$$ \operatorname{gfp}(F). $$
Section 3 transfers this statement to the greatest stable lower bound.

Both reductions take bounds among all real stable matrices: the fixed-point correspondence was established for all real quantities, without an integrality restriction.

5. Consequence for stopping simple stochastic games

Here is a concrete computational problem covered by the reduction.

5.1. Game definition and Bellman equations

A binary simple stochastic game has a finite directed graph with two terminal vertices, paying $0$ and $1$. Every nonterminal vertex has two successor occurrences and is controlled by:

  • a maximizing player;
  • a minimizing player; or
  • a fair random choice between its successors.

The payoff is $1$ if the play reaches the terminal paying $1$, and $0$ otherwise.

Assume the game is stopping: from every starting vertex, under every pair of strategies, a terminal is reached with probability one.

Its Bellman map is
$$ F_v(q)= \begin{cases} 0,&v\text{ is the zero terminal},\\ 1,&v\text{ is the one terminal},\\ \max\{q_u,q_w\},&v\text{ is maximizing},\\ \min\{q_u,q_w\},&v\text{ is minimizing},\\ (q_u+q_w)/2,&v\text{ is random}. \end{cases} $$
This is an elementary monotone system of precisely the kind used above.

For completeness, I prove that its unique fixed point is the value vector.

5.2. A uniform termination bound

Let $n$ be the total number of vertices. Start with the set $S_0$ of terminals, and repeatedly add:

  • a controlled vertex if both successors have already been added;
  • a random vertex if at least one successor has already been added.

All vertices must eventually be added. Otherwise, in the remaining nonempty set, each controlled vertex has a successor remaining in the set and each random vertex has both successors remaining. Both players could select remaining successors at their controlled vertices. Starting in this set, the play would never reach a terminal, contradicting stopping.

Every vertex is therefore added within $n$ rounds. Give it the rank of the round in which it was added.

Induction on rank shows that, under every strategy pair, a vertex of rank $r$ reaches a terminal within $r$ steps with probability at least $2^{-r}$:

  • at a controlled vertex, every successor has smaller rank;
  • at a random vertex, a smaller-rank successor is selected with probability at least $1/2$.

It follows that, from every history ending at a nonterminal, the conditional probability of termination within the next $n$ steps is at least
$$ \varepsilon=2^{-n}. $$
Consequently, for every strategy pair,
$$ \Pr(\text{not terminated after }kn\text{ steps}) \le(1-\varepsilon)^k. $$

5.3. The value vector is the unique fixed point

Assign the terminal coordinates their terminal payoffs and initialize all other coordinates at zero. Repeated Bellman updates give the finite-horizon values for reaching the one terminal within the permitted number of steps. This follows by backward induction: the controlling player takes the appropriate extremum, and random choice takes the average.

For any fixed strategy pair, the difference between the infinite-horizon payoff probability and its finite-horizon counterpart is at most the probability of surviving beyond that horizon. The uniform bound above tends to zero. Since the bound holds for every strategy pair, it also bounds the difference after taking either order of supremum and infimum over strategies.

Finite-horizon games have equal lower and upper values by backward induction. Their limits therefore give an infinite-horizon value vector $q$. Continuity of $F$ gives
$$ F(q)=q. $$

To prove uniqueness, let $p,q$ be two vectors with the prescribed terminal coordinates. Interpret $F^t(p)$ as the value of the finite-horizon game that pays the terminal payoff if termination occurs within $t$ steps, and otherwise pays the boundary value $p$ at the current vertex.

For every strategy pair, replacing boundary values $p$ by $q$ changes expected payoff by at most
$$ \Pr(\text{survival beyond }t)\,\|p-q\|_\infty. $$
Taking game values preserves this bound. At $t=n$,
$$ \|F^n(p)-F^n(q)\|_\infty \le(1-\varepsilon)\|p-q\|_\infty. $$
If both vectors are fixed points, the left side equals $\|p-q\|_\infty$. Since $\varepsilon>0$, they must be equal.

Thus the value vector is simultaneously the least and greatest fixed point.

5.4. Exact rational values have polynomial encoding length

At the fixed point, select a successor attaining the prescribed maximum or minimum at each controlled vertex. Keep the random transitions unchanged.

On nonterminal coordinates, the fixed-point equations then become
$$ q=Pq+d, $$
where entries of $P,d$ are drawn from a fixed finite set of rationals with denominator at most two.

The uniform stopping bound applies to this selected transition system. In particular,
$$ \|P^n\|_\infty\le1-\varepsilon. $$
Hence the series $\sum_{k\ge0}P^k$ converges and is the inverse of $I-P$. Thus
$$ q=(I-P)^{-1}d. $$

Multiplying the equations by two gives an integer linear system with bounded entries. Cramer’s rule and the determinant expansion bound its numerators and denominators by quantities with $O(n\log n)$ bits. The exact value vector is therefore rational with polynomial encoding length.

Applying either construction in Section 4 now proves:

Exact values of stopping binary simple stochastic games reduce in polynomial time, using one call, to either exact stable-bound operation in the task.

6. An explicit limitation of iteration

Even a one-dimensional affine source system can make direct fixed-point iteration exponentially slow in the input bit length.

For an integer $k\ge1$, put
$$ \eta=2^{-k},\qquad F_\eta(x)=(1-\eta)x+\eta. $$
Its coefficients have $O(k)$ bits, and its unique fixed point is $1$.

Starting from zero gives
$$ x^{(t)}=1-(1-\eta)^t. $$
Reaching $x^{(t)}\ge1/2$ requires
$$ t\ge\frac{\log2}{-\log(1-\eta)}. $$
For $0<\eta\le1/2$,
$$ -\log(1-\eta) =\int_0^\eta\frac{ds}{1-s} \le2\eta. $$
Therefore
$$ t\ge\frac{\log2}{2}\,2^k. $$
Moreover, no finite iterate equals the exact fixed point.

Section 4 encodes this extremal fixed point as a requested stable bound using a market of constant size and choices with constantly many cells. This does not lower-bound other algorithms—the affine fixed point is easy to solve directly—but it rules out obtaining a polynomial-time guarantee merely from convergence of value iteration.

7. What remains unresolved

The proved reductions show that the exact market model can encode cyclic monotone min–max–affine systems and exact stopping stochastic-game values. They do not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness or a polynomial-time algorithm.

A tempting existing hardness theorem concerns extremal fixed points of monotone maps on exponentially large integer intervals, represented by Boolean circuits. Its satisfiability reduction defines a map that fixes an integer exactly when its binary encoding satisfies a formula, and otherwise increments it. [Etessami–Papadimitriou–Rubinstein–Yannakakis, Proposition 1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol151-itcs2020/LIPIcs.ITCS.2020.18/LIPIcs.ITCS.2020.18.pdf).

That representation does not supply the missing reduction here. Direct interpolation can require exponentially many explicit pieces, and the Boolean circuit is not shown to admit a polynomial-size compilation into the elementary monotone gates used above.

The remaining task is therefore to prove a polynomial algorithm for the promised market descriptions, or to construct an applicable polynomial-size $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.
