The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational piecewise-constant probability density \(g\) on a rational polyhedral partition of the simplex \(\Delta(S_3)\) of masses over the six complete rankings, compute exactly the probability that \(\operatorname{IRV}(\mu)\) is a Borda loser for \(\mu\sim g\), using a fixed tie-breaking rule.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(m=3\) candidates \(A,B,C\); \(S_3\) denotes their six strict complete rankings, and \(\Delta(S_3)=\{\mu\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{6}:\sum_{r\in S_3}\mu_r=1\}\).
A rational society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass ranking types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Random societies below may have real masses.
Apply standard IRV and Borda with rankings weighted by their masses. Break IRV elimination ties by eliminating the alphabetically first tied candidate, and final winner ties by electing the alphabetically first. A Borda loser is any candidate attaining the minimum Borda score.
Let \(\lambda\) be five-dimensional affine Lebesgue measure normalized by \(\lambda(\Delta(S_3))=1\). The input explicitly lists \(K\) rational polyhedral cells covering the simplex with disjoint relative interiors, each specified by rational linear inequalities, and a nonnegative binary rational density value \(g_j\) on each cell; assume \(\int g\,d\lambda=1\). Shared boundaries may be assigned arbitrarily.
Define \(p(g)=\Pr_{\mu\sim g\,d\lambda}[\operatorname{IRV}(\mu)\text{ is a Borda loser}]\). Let \(H\) be the total number of cell inequalities and \(L\) the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,K,H,L\), with \(m=3\) and \(\tau\leq6\) fixed.

Can \(p(g)\) be computed exactly in polynomial time, or is its exact computation NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For uniformly sampled integer ranking-count profiles of a fixed electorate size, the limiting probability is \(4301/241920\); the same value holds for uniform density on the simplex. [Verified IAC result](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21504v2).
No result for the exact computation problem with arbitrary input piecewise-constant densities was identified in the literature search.

  • Specified normalized affine Lebesgue measure as the reference measure for the density.
  • Specified an explicit inequality encoding of the partition, nonnegative binary rational density values, and normalization to total probability one.
  • Allowed arbitrary assignments on shared cell boundaries.
  • Made the fixed IRV tie-breaking rule alphabetical and interpreted a Borda loser as any minimum-score candidate.

Result

Exact computation is polynomial-time solvable. For the explicitly listed piecewise-constant densities in the question, the function \(g\mapsto p(g)\) belongs to \(\mathrm{FP}\).

The proof gives an exact rational-arithmetic algorithm. Its essential feature is that the probability space has fixed dimension \(m!-1=5\).

1. Expressing the event by six polytopes

For a candidate \(a\), define its first-place mass and Borda score by

\(F_a(\mu)=\sum_{r:\,a\text{ is first in }r}\mu_r\),

\(B_a(\mu)=\sum_{r\in S_3}(3-\operatorname{pos}_r(a))\mu_r\),

where \(\operatorname{pos}_r(a)\in\{1,2,3\}\). For distinct candidates \(a,b\), also define

\(Q_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{r:\,a\text{ precedes }b\text{ in }r}\mu_r\).

These are linear functions with integer coefficients. Completeness of the rankings gives \(Q_{ab}+Q_{ba}=1\).

Let \(N\) be the union, over unordered candidate pairs, of the hyperplanes

\(F_a=F_b,\qquad Q_{ab}=\tfrac12,\qquad B_a=B_b.\)

Each is a proper hyperplane in the affine hull of the simplex: evaluating at suitable pure-ranking profiles gives different values on its two sides. Consequently, \(\lambda(N)=0\). Since the distribution has density with respect to \(\lambda\), it also assigns probability zero to \(N\). Thus the specified tie rules cannot affect \(p(g)\).

For each ordered triple \((e,w,v)\) of distinct candidates, define

\(R_{e,w,v}=\{\mu\in\Delta(S_3):F_e\leq F_w,\ F_e\leq F_v,\ Q_{wv}\geq\tfrac12,\ B_w\leq B_e,\ B_w\leq B_v\}.\)

There are six such rational polytopes, each using five voting inequalities.

To verify their interpretation, consider \(\mu\notin N\). The first two inequalities say precisely that \(e\) is the unique first candidate eliminated. After eliminating \(e\), each complete ranking contributes to whichever of \(w,v\) it ranks higher. Their final masses are therefore \(Q_{wv}\) and \(Q_{vw}\), so the third inequality says that \(w\) wins. The last two inequalities say that \(w\) is a Borda loser.

This interpretation also handles IRV’s early majority stopping rule. A candidate with first-place mass greater than \(\tfrac12\) cannot be eliminated first and has pairwise mass greater than \(\tfrac12\) against either opponent. Continuing to eliminate the first-place minimum therefore preserves that winner.

It follows that, outside \(N\),

\(\{\mu:\operatorname{IRV}(\mu)\text{ is a Borda loser}\}=\bigcup_{(e,w,v)}R_{e,w,v}.\)

Moreover, the six regions are disjoint outside \(N\), since the first elimination and final winner are unique there.

Let \(C_j\) denote the \(j\)-th input cell. Two full-dimensional cells cannot have a full-dimensional intersection because their interiors are disjoint. Lower-dimensional cells have measure zero. Hence

\(p(g)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}g_j\sum_{(e,w,v)}\lambda(C_j\cap R_{e,w,v}).\)

This identity reduces the problem to six exact polytope-volume computations per cell.

2. Rational coordinates and normalization

Order the six rankings as \(r_1,\ldots,r_6\), and use coordinates

\(x_i=\mu_{r_i}\quad(1\leq i\leq5),\qquad \mu_{r_6}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{5}x_i.\)

The simplex becomes

\(T=\{x\in\mathbb R^5:x_i\geq0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{5}x_i\leq1\}.\)

Every input-cell inequality and every voting inequality remains rational and linear after substitution.

The coordinate map has constant Jacobian. Because ordinary five-dimensional volume satisfies \(\operatorname{vol}_5(T)=1/5!\), normalization gives

\(\lambda(P)=5!\operatorname{vol}_5(\widetilde P),\)

where \(\widetilde P\) is the coordinate image of \(P\).

In particular, a simplex with coordinate vertices \(z_0,\ldots,z_5\) has normalized volume

\(\lambda(P)=\left|\det(z_1-z_0,\ldots,z_5-z_0)\right|.\)

Thus normalized volumes can be obtained entirely by rational arithmetic.

3. Fixed-dimensional exact volume: a constructive proof

We prove the algorithmic fact needed here.

Lemma. For fixed positive dimension \(d\), the volume of a bounded rational polytope in \(\mathbb R^d\), specified by \(n\) linear inequalities, is computable exactly in polynomial bit time.

Proof. Write the polytope as \(P=\{x:a_i\cdot x\leq b_i,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}\).

Enumerating vertices

At any vertex, the tight constraint normals span \(\mathbb R^d\). Otherwise, a nonzero vector orthogonal to all tight normals would permit sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality.

Enumerate every subset of \(d\) constraints, solve its equalities when nonsingular, and retain the solution if it satisfies every inequality. Deduplication gives exactly the vertices. There are at most \(\binom nd\) candidates.

We also use the elementary fact that a bounded polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. One direct justification is to start at any feasible point whose tight normals have rank less than \(d\), move in a direction annihilating those normals, and express the point as a convex combination of the two endpoints of its feasible interval. Boundedness makes both endpoints finite, and each endpoint has strictly greater tight-normal rank. Repeating terminates at vertices.

It follows that an empty vertex list means \(P\) is empty. Otherwise, the affine dimension of \(P\) is obtained from the vertex differences. If it is smaller than \(d\), its \(d\)-dimensional volume is zero.

Enumerating faces

Suppose now that \(P\) is full-dimensional. Every nonempty face can be specified by making at most \(d\) input inequalities tight.

Here is a justification that also covers redundant inequalities. Let \(F\) be a face maximizing a linear functional \(\ell\), and let \(z\) be the average of all vertices of \(F\). Let \(J\) be the constraints tight at \(z\). Because every constraint slack is nonnegative, a constraint is tight at this average exactly when it is tight at every vertex of \(F\).

We claim

\(F=P\cap\{x:a_i\cdot x=b_i\text{ for all }i\in J\}.\)

The forward inclusion follows from the preceding observation. For the reverse inclusion, take \(y\) in the right-hand side. For sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), the point \(z-\varepsilon(y-z)\) remains feasible: the constraints in \(J\) remain equalities, and all other constraints have positive slack at \(z\). If \(\ell(y)<\ell(z)\), this feasible point would have functional value greater than the maximum \(\ell(z)\), a contradiction. Therefore \(y\in F\).

Choose a basis from the normals indexed by \(J\). It contains at most \(d\) normals, and its equalities imply all the others: their constants obey the same linear relations because all equalities hold at \(z\). This proves the claim about at most \(d\) inequalities.

Accordingly, enumerate subsets of at most \(d\) constraints and record the vertices satisfying their equalities. Each nonempty recorded set determines a face; identical sets are deduplicated. There are at most

\(M=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom nk=O((n+1)^d)\)

faces. Their dimensions and inclusion relations are computed from their vertex sets by exact linear algebra and set comparison.

Triangulation and volume

For each nonempty face \(F\), let \(b_F\) be the average of its vertices. This point lies in the relative interior of \(F\): every proper supporting face omits at least one vertex, so a strictly positive average of all vertices cannot lie in it.

Triangulate recursively by coning \(b_F\) over the triangulated facets of \(F\). This gives simplices indexed by complete flags

\(F_0\subsetneq F_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq F_d=P,\qquad \dim F_i=i,\)

with vertices \(b_{F_0},\ldots,b_{F_d}\).

The construction is valid by induction on dimension. From an interior point, rays reach the boundary; coning the facets covers the polytope with disjoint interiors. The recursive triangulations agree on shared faces because they use the same face centers. Each cone is nondegenerate because its center lies outside the affine hull of its base facet.

There are at most \(M^d\) complete flags, since \(P\) is fixed as the final face. Sum the simplex volumes

\(\operatorname{vol}_d(P)=\frac1{d!}\sum_{\text{complete flags}}\left|\det(b_{F_1}-b_{F_0},\ldots,b_{F_d}-b_{F_0})\right|.\)

For fixed \(d\), all enumeration counts are polynomial in \(n\).

Finally, the arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule bounds every vertex coordinate’s bit length polynomially in the input length. A face center averages polynomially many such coordinates, so its bit length is also polynomial. Fixed-size determinants and a sum of polynomially many rational values retain polynomial bit length. All required operations therefore take polynomial bit time. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

4. Algorithm and running time

Let \(h_j\) be the number of inequalities describing \(C_j\), so \(\sum_j h_j=H\). For each of the six triples, form the coordinate polytope

\(P_{j,e,w,v}=\widetilde C_j\cap\widetilde R_{e,w,v}\cap T.\)

It has at most \(h_j+11\) inequalities: \(h_j\) cell inequalities, five voting inequalities, and six simplex inequalities.

Compute its normalized volume using the lemma and the determinant normalization above. Return the rational number

\(\sum_{j=1}^{K}g_j\sum_{(e,w,v)}\lambda(C_j\cap R_{e,w,v}).\)

Section 1 proves that this output equals \(p(g)\).

For dimension five, there are \(O((n+1)^5)\) faces and at most \(O((n+1)^{25})\) complete flags. Vertex enumeration, face construction, and inclusion comparisons fit within this polynomial bound, with polynomial bit cost per operation. Thus a deliberately coarse bound is

\(O\!\left(3!\sum_{j=1}^{K}(h_j+12)^{25}\operatorname{poly}(L)\right)\subseteq O\!\left(3!K(H+12)^{25}\operatorname{poly}(L)\right).\)

The polynomial in \(L\) has an absolute fixed exponent; the exact rational output also has polynomial bit length.

The support parameter \(\tau\) does not reduce the dimension of this probability problem. It describes a separately specified society, whereas \(\lambda\)-almost every random society has all six masses positive. For a rational society given by its support, IRV and Borda evaluation takes \(O(m^2\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations and polynomial bit time. Here \(m=3\) and \(\tau\leq6\) are fixed; the probability algorithm’s growing parameters are \(K,H,L\).

5. Uniform case and the hardness alternative

The supplied uniform value is consistent with this construction. Proposition 2 of the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21504v2) reports \(4301/241920\). An independent exact rational-arithmetic check of the construction above gives normalized volume \(4301/1451520\) for \(R_{C,A,B}\); candidate permutations give six equal-volume regions and reproduce that value. The complexity proof does not depend on this numerical check.

Since the exact function has a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm, NP-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\): replace each oracle call in such a reduction by this algorithm.
