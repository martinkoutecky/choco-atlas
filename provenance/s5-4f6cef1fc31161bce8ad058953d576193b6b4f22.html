The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem V.2 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite group set \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_{g,s}\) on supported types \((g,s)\) with \(s\in[-1,1]\), symmetric nonnegative rational interaction rates \(a^+_{gh},a^-_{gh}\), and a budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), choose a measurable reassignment \(y(x)\in[-1,1]\) that changes at most mass \(\kappa\) from its baseline opinion. For each group, require equilibrium expressed opinions to satisfy \((1+d_g)\bar z_g=\bar y_g+\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh})\bar z_h\), where \(d_g=\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh})\); maximize \(\int z_y(x)\,d\mu(x)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \((g,s)\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_{g,s}\), where \(s\in\mathbb Q\cap[-1,1]\) and \(\sum_{g,s}\mu_{g,s}=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(G\) contain exactly the represented groups, \(m=|G|\), and \(\mu_g=\sum_s\mu_{g,s}\). Realize each type as an atomless interval of length \(\mu_{g,s}\), with baseline \(s(x)=s\); write \(X_g\) for the union of group \(g\)'s intervals.
Input interaction rates satisfy \(a^\pm_{gh}=a^\pm_{hg}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\); within-group rates and simultaneous positive and negative rates are allowed. The graph joining distinct groups whenever \(a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh}>0\) is connected. Rates are listed sparsely, with \(q\) nonzero entries and omitted entries zero.
For binary rational \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), a feasible intervention is measurable \(y:X\to[-1,1]\) with \(\mu(\{x:y(x)\ne s(x)\})\le\kappa\); each type's mass may be split, and interaction rates remain fixed.
Define \(\bar y_g=\mu_g^{-1}\int_{X_g}y\,d\mu\) and \(d_g=\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh})\). Equilibrium is specified by \((1+d_g)\bar z_g=\bar y_g+\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh})\bar z_h\), with individual opinions \(z_y(x)=(y(x)+\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh})\bar z_h)/(1+d_g)\) for \(x\in X_g\).
Let \(F(y)=\int_Xz_y\,d\mu=\sum_g\mu_g\bar z_g\), and let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be its supremum over feasible interventions. Let \(L\) be the maximum binary bit length of an input rational, including rational accuracy \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\).

Does this problem admit an algorithm producing a feasible intervention with \(\mathrm{OPT}-F(y)\le\epsilon\) in expected time \(\widetilde O(m+\tau+q)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log(1/\epsilon))\), where the suppressed factors are logarithmic in \(m+\tau+q\)?

For finite connected undirected signed graphs, modifying at most \(k\) internal opinions admits an exact \(O(n^3)\) algorithm and a claimed additive-\(\epsilon\) approximation in \(\widetilde O(|E|)\) time for given \(k,\epsilon\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10680v2)
No result settling this precise atomless intervention problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one, interpreting the budget as a fraction of society and approximation error in total normalized expressed opinion.
  • Realized supported types as atomless intervals, allowing interventions to split the mass of a type.
  • Required baseline opinions and the budget to be binary rationals, and removed groups with zero mass.
  • Defined group means and individual equilibrium opinions explicitly, completing the group equilibrium equations.
  • Carried over connectedness by requiring the graph of positive total interaction rates between represented groups to be connected.
  • Retained within-group interactions and simultaneous positive and negative rates as allowed by the graded rate model; these describe interactions among distinct individuals within population blocks.
  • Specified sparse interaction input and its size q; used m for the number of groups and L for the maximum rational bit length.
  • Made accuracy dependence explicit in the approximation target, since the discrete theorem states its runtime for given k and epsilon.

Result

Yes. There is an always-correct randomized algorithm that outputs a feasible intervention satisfying \(\mathrm{OPT}-F(y)\le\epsilon\), with expected bit running time
\(\widetilde O(m+\tau+q)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log(1/\epsilon))\).

The intervention has a finite description: for each represented type, specify a rational length of its interval on which to replace the baseline by an endpoint of \([-1,1]\).

The proof uses a fractional knapsack reduction, a controlled dyadic approximation of the input, and certified solves of symmetric diagonally dominant systems.

1. The influence coefficients

Write \(\mu=(\mu_g)_{g\in G}\), \(M=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_g)\), and
\(c_{gh}=a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh}\), \(t_{gh}=a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh}\).
Define
\[ \]
\(B=\operatorname{diag}(1+d_g)-CM\), where \(C=(c_{gh})\), and \(H=MB\).

Every \(\mu_g\) is positive. Symmetry of the rates makes \(H\) symmetric. For every real vector \(v\),
\[ \]
\(v^\top Hv =\sum_g\mu_gv_g^2 +\sum_{g<h}\mu_g\mu_h\bigl[a^+_{gh}(v_g-v_h)^2+a^-_{gh}(v_g+v_h)^2\bigr] +2\sum_g\mu_g^2a^-_{gg}v_g^2.\)

This identity follows by expanding the squares: off-diagonal entries are
\(-\mu_g\mu_hc_{gh}\), and diagonal entries are
\(\mu_g+\sum_{h\ne g}\mu_g\mu_ht_{gh}+2\mu_g^2a^-_{gg}\).
Consequently, \(H\succeq M\succ0\).

For an intervention \(y\), put \(p_g(y)=\int_{X_g}y\,d\mu\). Multiplying the equilibrium equations by \(\mu_g\) gives
\(H\bar z=p(y)\). Thus equilibrium is unique, and symmetry gives
\[ \]
\(F(y)=\mu^\top H^{-1}p(y)=\sum_g u_g\int_{X_g}y\,d\mu,\qquad u=H^{-1}\mu=B^{-1}\mathbf1.\)

We will also need a uniform bound. The diagonal dominance margin of \(B\) satisfies
\[ \]
\(B_{gg}-\sum_{h\ne g}|B_{gh}| =1+2\mu_ga^-_{gg} +\sum_{h\ne g}\mu_h(t_{gh}-|c_{gh}|)\ge1.\)

Choose a coordinate \(g\) maximizing \(|v_g|\). The triangle inequality then yields
\(|(Bv)_g|\ge\|v\|_\infty\). Therefore
\(\|B^{-1}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\le1\), and in particular \(\|u\|_\infty\le1\).

These arguments include simultaneous positive and negative rates and all within-group rates.

2. Exact optimization is fractional knapsack

Index the represented types by \(i\), with group \(g(i)\), baseline \(s_i\), and mass \(\mu_i\).

On a changed subset of type \(i\) having mass \(x_i\), the largest possible objective gain is
\(c_i x_i\), where
\[ \]
\(c_i=\max_{r\in[-1,1]}u_{g(i)}(r-s_i) =|u_{g(i)}|-u_{g(i)}s_i.\)

The maximizing replacement is \(1\) when \(u_{g(i)}>0\), and \(-1\) when \(u_{g(i)}<0\). If the coefficient is zero, no change is necessary. Moreover,
\(0\le c_i\le2\).

Atomlessness permits every allocation satisfying
\[ \]
\(0\le x_i\le\mu_i,\qquad \sum_i x_i\le\kappa.\)

Hence
\[ \]
\(\mathrm{OPT} =F(s)+ \max\left\{\sum_i c_ix_i:\ 0\le x_i\le\mu_i,\ \sum_i x_i\le\kappa\right\}.\)

The maximum exists because this finite-dimensional feasible set is compact. It is attained by sorting the coefficients in nonincreasing order and filling capacities in that order. Indeed, transferring an amount from a later selected type to an earlier unsaturated type changes the gain by that amount times the difference of their coefficients, which is nonnegative. Successively fixing the allocations in sorted order gives the greedy allocation.

Thus the remaining issue is computing sufficiently accurate influence coefficients within the required bit complexity.

3. Dyadic approximation with a proved error bound

Use the conservative encoding bounds
\(\mu_i\ge2^{-L}\) and \(a^\pm_{gh}\le2^L\).

Choose an integer \(P\) such that, with \(\delta=2^{-P}\),
\[ \]
\(\delta\le\frac{\epsilon}{2^{L+8}(\tau+1)},\qquad P=O(L+\log(\tau+1)+\log(1/\epsilon)).\)

Round downward:
\[ \]
\(\nu_i=\delta\lfloor\mu_i/\delta\rfloor,\quad \nu_g=\sum_{i:g(i)=g}\nu_i,\quad r^\pm_{gh}=\delta\lfloor a^\pm_{gh}/\delta\rfloor.\)

Then
\[ \]
\(\sum_g\nu_g\le1,\qquad \nu_g\ge2^{-(L+1)},\qquad 0\le\sum_g(\mu_g-\nu_g)<\tau\delta.\)

The lower bound follows because \(\delta\le2^{-(L+1)}\), so every rounded type mass is at least half its original mass.

Construct \(\widehat B\) and \(\widehat H=\operatorname{diag}(\nu_g)\widehat B\) by substituting \(\nu_g,r^\pm_{gh}\) for \(\mu_g,a^\pm_{gh}\). Define
\(\widehat u=\widehat B^{-1}\mathbf1\).
The preceding positive-definiteness and diagonal-dominance proofs apply without requiring the masses to sum to one. Therefore
\[ \]
\(\|\widehat B^{-1}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\le1,\qquad \|\widehat u\|_\infty\le1.\)

To bound the perturbation, for each row observe that
\[ \]
\(\sum_h\left|\mu_ht_{gh}-\nu_g^0\nu_h(r^+_{gh}+r^-_{gh})\right| \le2^{L+1}\tau\delta+2\delta,\qquad \nu_g^0:=1.\)

Here the first term bounds the mass-rounding contribution; the second bounds rate rounding because \(\sum_h\nu_h\le1\). The same bound holds with \(t_{gh}\) replaced by \(c_{gh}\) and \(r^+_{gh}+r^-_{gh}\) replaced by \(r^+_{gh}-r^-_{gh}\). Consequently,
\[ \]
\(\|B-\widehat B\|_{\infty\to\infty} \le E:=2^{L+2}\tau\delta+4\delta \le\epsilon/32.\)

Finally,
\(B(u-\widehat u)=(\widehat B-B)\widehat u\), so
\[ \]
\(\|u-\widehat u\|_\infty\le E\le\epsilon/32.\)

All rounded data are dyadic. Thus their sums and products have
\(O(P+L+\log(m+\tau+q))\) bits; large exact denominators from summing the original rational masses never arise.

4. Certified nearly linear computation

We use the nearly linear SDDM solver theorem of Spielman and Teng, including its finite-precision analysis. Theorem 5.5 supplies relative energy-norm accuracy with constant success probability; Theorem 6.6 bounds the required precision by a polynomial in logarithmic condition number, inverse accuracy, and dimension. After power-of-two normalization, these give nearly linear bit complexity when those quantities have polynomially bounded logarithms. Irreducible components can be handled separately. [Spielman–Teng, Theorems 5.5 and 6.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0607105).

Here is an explicit reduction and certificate checking the required hypotheses.

Write
\(\widehat H=D-N+R\), where \(D\) is its diagonal, and \(N,R\) are nonnegative symmetric matrices with zero diagonal containing, respectively, the magnitudes of its negative off-diagonal entries and its positive off-diagonal entries. Define
\[ \]
\(T=\begin{pmatrix}D-N&-R\\-R&D-N\end{pmatrix}, \qquad b=\begin{pmatrix}\nu\\-\nu\end{pmatrix}.\)

This matrix has \(O(m+q)\) nonzero entries. Its off-diagonal entries are nonpositive, and every row has diagonal-dominance margin at least the corresponding \(\nu_g\). Therefore it is positive definite and SDDM. Also,
\[ \]
\(T\begin{pmatrix}\widehat u\\-\widehat u\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\nu\\-\nu\end{pmatrix}.\)

Put \(\alpha=2^{-(L+1)}\) and \(S=2^{L+3}\).
Diagonal dominance, the rounded rate bounds, and \(\sum_g\nu_g\le1\) give
\[ \]
\(\lambda_{\min}(T)\ge\alpha,\qquad \|T\|_{\infty\to\infty}\le S,\qquad \lambda_{\max}(T)\le S.\)

For the upper bound, each diagonal entry of \(\widehat H\) is at most
\(\nu_g(1+2^{L+1}\sum_h\nu_h)\le1+2^{L+1}\), and an SDD matrix has absolute row sum at most twice its diagonal entry. Thus
\(\operatorname{cond}_2(T)\le S/\alpha=2^{2L+4}\).

Accuracy and certification

Set \(\zeta=\epsilon/16\). For each irreducible component \(T_J\), run independent solver trials and accept a candidate \(v_J\) only when the exactly computed dyadic residual satisfies
\[ \]
\(\|T_Jv_J-b_J\|_\infty\le\alpha\zeta.\)

The maximum-coordinate argument from Section 1, now using margin \(\alpha\), gives
\(\|T_J^{-1}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\le1/\alpha\).
Every accepted candidate therefore satisfies
\[ \]
\(\|v_J-T_J^{-1}b_J\|_\infty\le\zeta.\)

To verify that trials succeed with constant probability, ask the solver for infinity-norm accuracy
\(\gamma=\alpha\zeta/(4S)\).
Its energy-norm guarantee provides this accuracy using only logarithmic dependence on inverse accuracy: if the component has order \(n_J\), its exact solution has infinity norm at most one, hence energy norm at most \(\sqrt{n_JS}\). A relative energy tolerance
\(\gamma\alpha/(4n_JS)\) therefore gives infinity error at most \(\gamma/4\). The finite-precision theorem supplies the remaining accuracy with
\(\operatorname{poly}(L,\log(m+\tau+q),\log(1/\epsilon))\) bits.

Before checking the residual, clip the candidate to \([-2,2]\) and round it to a fixed dyadic grid of spacing at most \(\gamma\). On successful trials, clipping cannot increase error because the exact solution lies in \([-1,1]\). The resulting error is at most \(2\gamma\), so its residual is at most
\(2S\gamma=\alpha\zeta/2\), and the certificate passes.

Trials can be capped at the theorem’s running-time bound; successful trials finish within that bound. Singleton components are solved directly. Thus each component needs a constant expected number of trials, while certification guarantees correctness of every accepted result.

Combine the accepted component vectors into \(v\), and set
\[ \]
\(w_g=\operatorname{clip}_{[-1,1]}\bigl((v_g-v_{g+m})/2\bigr).\)

It follows that
\[ \]
\(\|w-\widehat u\|_\infty\le\zeta,\qquad \|w-u\|_\infty\le E+\zeta\le3\epsilon/32.\)

The total expected bit running time of this step is
\(\widetilde O(m+q)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log(1/\epsilon))\).

5. Constructing the intervention

Round the budget downward:
\(\kappa'=\delta\lfloor\kappa/\delta\rfloor\).

For every type \(i\), compute
\[ \]
\(b_i=|w_{g(i)}|-w_{g(i)}s_i.\)

Sort these coefficients in nonincreasing order and greedily allocate lengths subject to capacities \(\nu_i\) and total budget \(\kappa'\). On each allocated initial segment, use replacement \(1\) if \(w_{g(i)}>0\), and \(-1\) if \(w_{g(i)}<0\). Types with zero gain can be left unchanged.

Every allocated length is dyadic, at most \(\nu_i\le\mu_i\), and their total is at most \(\kappa'\le\kappa\). The resulting intervention is feasible.

This greedy rule maximizes
\[ \]
\(F_w(y):=\sum_g w_g\int_{X_g}y\,d\mu\)

over interventions with per-type changed masses at most \(\nu_i\) and total changed mass at most \(\kappa'\). This follows from the same endpoint and exchange arguments as in Section 2.

Sorting and constructing the output take
\(\widetilde O(\tau)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log(1/\epsilon))\) bit operations.

6. Approximation guarantee

First, restricting capacities and budget loses little. Start with an exact optimal endpoint intervention from Section 2. Reduce its changed mass in each type to at most \(\nu_i\). Since every gain per unit mass is at most two, the loss is at most
\(2\sum_i(\mu_i-\nu_i)<2\tau\delta\).

If necessary, further reduce its total changed mass to \(\kappa'\). The additional reduction is at most \(\kappa-\kappa'<\delta\), costing at most \(2\delta\). Atomlessness permits these reductions. Consequently, there is a restricted feasible intervention \(y'\) with
\[ \]
\(F(y')\ge\mathrm{OPT}-2(\tau+1)\delta.\)

Second, for every intervention \(y\),
\[ \]
\(|F(y)-F_w(y)| \le\|u-w\|_\infty\int_X|y|\,d\mu \le3\epsilon/32,\)

because \(|y|\le1\) and the original society has total mass one.

Let \(y_{\mathrm{alg}}\) be the intervention constructed above. Its optimality for the restricted surrogate problem gives
\[ \]
\(F(y_{\mathrm{alg}}) \ge F_w(y_{\mathrm{alg}})-3\epsilon/32 \ge F_w(y')-3\epsilon/32 \ge F(y')-3\epsilon/16.\)

Therefore
\[ \]
\(\mathrm{OPT}-F(y_{\mathrm{alg}}) \le2(\tau+1)\delta+3\epsilon/16 \le\epsilon/128+3\epsilon/16 =25\epsilon/128<\epsilon.\)

Combining preprocessing, certified solves, sorting, and output construction proves the claimed expected running time. Connectivity is not needed for this argument.
