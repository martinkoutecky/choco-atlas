The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(G\) of good classes, a finite set \(T\) of agent types with additive values \(a_{tg}\in\{1,p\}\) for integral \(p>1\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(s_g\ge 0\), compute a feasible mass allocation maximizing Nash social welfare. A configuration \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\) gives each recipient an integral bundle, and \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving that bundle. Require \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}z_gx_{t,z}\le s_g\) for each \(g\); maximize \(\exp(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log(\sum_g a_{tg}z_g))\), with \(\log 0=-\infty\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a set of \(m\ge 1\) good classes. A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge 1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has additive values \(a_{tg}\in\{1,p\}\), where the common integer \(p>1\) is supplied in binary. Supplies \(s_g\ge 0\) are binary rationals, with \(\sum_g s_g\ge 1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A configuration \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}\) is an integral bundle, with utility \(u_t(z)=\sum_g a_{tg}z_g\). Configurations have no individual size bound; supplies constrain aggregate consumption.
An allocation assigns nonnegative real masses \(x_{t,z}\), satisfying \(\sum_zx_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}z_gx_{t,z}\le s_g\) for every \(g\).
Its Nash social welfare is \(\exp(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log u_t(z))\), summing only over positive masses and taking \(\log 0=-\infty\). Compute an exact maximizing allocation, represented by a finite list of configurations and their binary-rational masses; any maximizer is acceptable.

Is this exact mass-allocation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents and explicitly listed indivisible goods, exact Nash social welfare maximization with values \(\{1,p\}\) is polynomial-time solvable; a [later full treatment](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.10949v5) confirms this result and assumes at least as many goods as agents.
No complexity result for exactly the mass-allocation problem defined above was located in the search.

  • Imposed total per-capita supply at least one, carrying over the later full treatment's condition that the number of goods is at least the number of agents.
  • Treated the common integer p as part of the binary-encoded input and defined L as the total input bit length.

New partial results: an explicit local solution and a finite-dimensional reduction

The general polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove two additional results:

  • The problem is polynomial-time solvable when there is one supported type, even with arbitrarily many good classes and binary-encoded $p$.
  • For arbitrary support, the problem is exactly equivalent to a concave optimization problem with $m\tau$ variables. Its objective has an explicit formula, and rational feasible solutions can be converted into allocations in polynomial time.

The second result does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for exact global optimization.

1. The local problem

Fix an integer $p>1$. Consider bundles described by two nonnegative integers $(k,j)$: $k$ goods of value $p$ and $j$ goods of value $1$.

For real numbers $H,J\ge 0$ satisfying $H+J\ge 1$, define
$$ F(H,J)= \sup \mathbb E[\log(pK+J_0)], $$
where the supremum is over distributions on
$$ (K,J_0)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\} $$
with
$$ \mathbb E[K]=H, \qquad \mathbb E[J_0]=J. $$

Thus $H$ and $J$ specify expected quantities, rather than quantities in every bundle.

For $v\ge 1$, write
$$ r=\lfloor v\rfloor, \qquad \beta=v-r, $$
and define the linear interpolation
$$ \phi(v)=(1-\beta)\log r+\beta\log(r+1). $$

Theorem: explicit formula for $F$

Write
$$ n=\lfloor H\rfloor, \qquad \alpha=H-n, \qquad V=pH+J. $$

If $\alpha=0$, or if
$$ J\ge (p-1)(1-\alpha), $$
then
$$ F(H,J)=\phi(V). $$

Otherwise, $0<\alpha<1$ and
$$ J<(p-1)(1-\alpha). $$
Define
$$ R=\frac{J}{1-\alpha}, \qquad d=\lfloor R\rfloor, \qquad \theta=R-d, \qquad q=pn+d, \qquad U=p(n+1). $$
Then
$$ F(H,J) = (1-\alpha)(1-\theta)\log q + (1-\alpha)\theta\log(q+1) + \alpha\log U. $$

In every case, the supremum is attained by a distribution on at most four bundles. When $H,J$ are binary rationals, such a distribution can be computed using polynomial-time rational arithmetic, without evaluating logarithms.

2. Proof of the local formula

2.1 A universal upper bound

For every positive integer $\ell$, let
$$ \Delta_\ell=\log(\ell+1)-\log\ell. $$
The sequence $\Delta_\ell$ is positive and strictly decreasing.

Consequently, for positive integers $v,r$,
$$ \log v\le \log r+(v-r)\Delta_r. $$
Indeed, when $v>r$, telescope the increments from $r$ to $v-1$, each of which is at most $\Delta_r$. When $v<r$, telescope in the opposite direction, using increments at least $\Delta_r$.

Equality holds exactly when $v\in\{r,r+1\}$.

Apply this inequality with $r=\lfloor V\rfloor$ to the random integer utility $pK+J_0$. Taking expectations gives
$$ \mathbb E[\log(pK+J_0)] \le \log r+(V-r)\Delta_r = \phi(V). $$
These expectations are well defined: utilities are positive, and
$$ 0\le \log(pK+J_0)\le pK+J_0 $$
has finite expectation.

2.2 Exactly when the universal bound is attainable

Write
$$ r=\lfloor V\rfloor, \qquad \beta=V-r, $$
and define
$$ C=(1-\beta)\left\lfloor\frac r p\right\rfloor + \beta\left\lfloor\frac{r+1}{p}\right\rfloor. $$

I claim that the bound $\phi(V)$ is attainable precisely when
$$ H\le C. $$

For necessity, equality in the preceding upper bound forces every supported utility to belong to $\{r,r+1\}$. Their probabilities must be $1-\beta$ and $\beta$, respectively. A bundle of utility $v$ has at most $\lfloor v/p\rfloor$ heavy goods, so its expected heavy count is at most $C$.

For sufficiency, suppose first that $C>0$, and set
$$ \eta=\frac H C\in[0,1]. $$
Choose utility $v=r$ with probability $1-\beta$, and utility $v=r+1$ with probability $\beta$. Conditional on $v$, choose
$$ \left(\left\lfloor\frac v p\right\rfloor,\, v-p\left\lfloor\frac v p\right\rfloor\right) $$
with probability $\eta$, and choose $(0,v)$ with probability $1-\eta$.

Every bundle has its chosen utility. The expected heavy count is $\eta C=H$, and the expected light count is
$$ V-pH=J. $$
This uses at most four bundles and attains $\phi(V)$.

If $C=0$, the condition $H\le C$ forces $H=0$, and the two light-only bundles $(0,r)$ and $(0,r+1)$ suffice.

It remains to express $H\le C$ in terms of $H,J$.

If $\alpha=0$, then $V\ge pn$, so $C\ge n=H$.

Suppose $0<\alpha<1$, and put $U=p(n+1)$. Since $V\ge pH>pn$, there are three possibilities:

  • If $V\le U-1$, then $C=n<H$.
  • If $U-1<V<U$, then

$$ C=n+V-U+1. $$

  • If $V\ge U$, then $C\ge n+1>H$.

Therefore
$$ H\le C \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V\ge U-1+\alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J\ge(p-1)(1-\alpha). $$

This proves the first branch of the formula.

2.3 Construction when light goods are scarce

Now assume
$$ 0<\alpha<1, \qquad J<(p-1)(1-\alpha). $$
Use the notation $R,d,\theta,q,U$ from the theorem.

Since $R<p-1$, we have
$$ 0\le d\le p-2, \qquad q+1<U. $$
Also $q\ge 1$. This is immediate if $n\ge 1$. If $n=0$, the hypothesis $H+J\ge 1$ gives
$$ J\ge 1-\alpha, $$
so $R\ge 1$ and $d\ge 1$.

Assign probabilities
$$ (1-\alpha)(1-\theta),\qquad (1-\alpha)\theta,\qquad \alpha $$
to the respective bundles
$$ (n,d),\qquad (n,d+1),\qquad (n+1,0). $$
All have positive utility.

Their expected counts are
$$ \mathbb E[K]=(1-\alpha)n+\alpha(n+1)=H $$
and
$$ \mathbb E[J_0]=(1-\alpha)(d+\theta)=J. $$
Their objective is exactly the claimed expression. It remains to prove optimality.

2.4 An affine certificate of optimality

Define
$$ \lambda=\log(q+1)-\log q, $$
$$ \gamma=\log U-\log q+d\lambda, $$
and
$$ c=\log q-n\gamma-d\lambda. $$

I will prove that every nonzero integer bundle $(k,j)$ satisfies
$$ \log(pk+j)\le c+\gamma k+\lambda j. $$

First, because $U-q=p-d$,
$$ \gamma = \sum_{\ell=q}^{U-1}\Delta_\ell+d\Delta_q. $$
This expression consists of $p$ increments, counting repetitions. Each is at most $\Delta_q=\lambda$ and at least $\Delta_U$. Hence
$$ p\Delta_U\le\gamma\le p\lambda. $$
In particular, $\gamma,\lambda>0$.

If $k\le n$, put $v=pk+j\ge 1$. Then
$$ c+\gamma k+\lambda j = \log q+\lambda(v-q)+(\gamma-p\lambda)(k-n). $$
The last term is nonnegative, since both factors are nonpositive. The integer supporting-line inequality therefore gives
$$ c+\gamma k+\lambda j \ge \log q+\lambda(v-q) \ge\log v. $$

If $k\ge n+1$, put $b=k-n-1\ge 0$. Then
$$ pk+j=U+pb+j. $$
Every logarithmic increment after $U$ is at most $\Delta_U$, so
$$ \log(pk+j)-\log U \le(pb+j)\Delta_U \le b\gamma+j\lambda. $$
Because
$$ c+\gamma(n+1)=\log U, $$
this proves the desired inequality in the second case.

Taking expectations gives
$$ \mathbb E[\log(pK+J_0)] \le c+\gamma H+\lambda J. $$
Substituting $H=n+\alpha$ and $J=(1-\alpha)(d+\theta)$ yields
$$ c+\gamma H+\lambda J = (1-\alpha)(1-\theta)\log q + (1-\alpha)\theta\log(q+1) + \alpha\log U. $$

The constructed three-bundle distribution attains equality. This completes the proof of the theorem.

3. Consequences of the local theorem

3.1 Concavity and explicit supporting planes

The function $F$ is concave on
$$ D=\{(H,J):H,J\ge 0,\ H+J\ge 1\}. $$
Indeed, mix optimal distributions for two points of $D$. Their mixture has the corresponding mixed means and the mixed objective. By the definition of $F$, its value at the mixed means is at least that objective.

Moreover, every point of $D$ has an explicit affine upper bound that is tight there.

In the first branch, with $r=\lfloor pH+J\rfloor$, the bound is
$$ F(H',J') \le \log r+(pH'+J'-r)\Delta_r. $$
Its slopes are $p\Delta_r$ and $\Delta_r$.

In the second branch, the bound is
$$ F(H',J')\le c+\gamma H'+\lambda J', $$
with the coefficients proved above.

All these slopes are positive. These global upper bounds also prove upper semicontinuity: at any fixed $(H,J)$, its tight affine bound gives
$$ \limsup_{(H',J')\to(H,J)}F(H',J')\le F(H,J). $$

For rational $H,J$, the branch, bundle counts, and probabilities require only rational arithmetic and floors. The value and supporting-plane coefficients have symbolic representations involving at most three logarithms of positive integers.

3.2 The integer-utility upper bound need not be attainable

For example, take
$$ p=3,\qquad H=\frac32,\qquad J=\frac14. $$
The optimal distribution is
$$ \Pr[(K,J_0)=(1,0)]=\frac14, $$
$$ \Pr[(K,J_0)=(1,1)]=\frac14, $$
$$ \Pr[(K,J_0)=(2,0)]=\frac12. $$
Thus
$$ F\left(\frac32,\frac14\right) = \frac14\log3+\frac14\log4+\frac12\log6. $$

Here $V=19/4$, and the universal bound would be
$$ \phi(V)=\frac14\log4+\frac34\log5. $$
The inequality is strict: the optimal distribution assigns positive probability to utility $6$, where the supporting line through utilities $4$ and $5$ is strictly above the logarithm.

Thus optimizing only expected utilities, even with integer interpolation, loses a real constraint.

4. Polynomial-time solution for one supported type

Suppose $\tau=1$. Partition the classes into
$$ G_H=\{g:a_g=p\}, \qquad G_L=\{g:a_g=1\}, $$
and define
$$ H=\sum_{g\in G_H}s_g, \qquad J=\sum_{g\in G_L}s_g. $$
The hypothesis on total supply gives $H+J\ge 1$.

Compute the optimal local distribution from the theorem.

This distribution uses the category supplies exactly. To prove that it also bounds allocations that leave supplies unused, take the tight affine upper bound at $(H,J)$:
$$ \log(pk+j)\le c+\gamma k+\lambda j, $$
where $\gamma,\lambda>0$. Any feasible original allocation has expected category counts $h\le H$ and $j'\le J$, so
$$ W\le c+\gamma h+\lambda j' \le c+\gamma H+\lambda J =F(H,J). $$

It remains to realize the local distribution using the actual classes.

When $H>0$, set
$$ \rho_g=\frac{s_g}{H} \qquad(g\in G_H). $$
When $J>0$, set
$$ \sigma_h=\frac{s_h}{J} \qquad(h\in G_L). $$

Replace a local bundle $(k,j)$ with $k,j>0$ by the lottery on
$$ ke_g+je_h $$
having probabilities $\rho_g\sigma_h$. If one count is zero, use only the corresponding one-category lottery.

Utilities are unchanged. Expected consumption of a heavy class $g$ is
$$ \mathbb E[K]\rho_g=H\rho_g=s_g, $$
and the light-class calculation is identical.

This constructs an exact maximizing allocation with at most $4m^2$ configurations.

All bundle counts and probabilities are computed by polynomially many rational operations. Their encoding lengths are polynomial: the quantities $H,J,pH+J$, their floors, and the ratios in the construction all have polynomial bit length. No logarithmic comparison is required.

Therefore the exact problem is polynomial-time solvable for $\tau=1$.

The same argument applies when all supported types have identical valuation vectors: give each type the constructed distribution, multiplied by its mass $\mu_t$.

5. Exact reduction for arbitrary support

For each type-class pair introduce a variable $y_{tg}\ge 0$, interpreted as aggregate consumption of class $g$ by type $t$.

Consider the rational polytope
$$ P= \left\{ y\ge 0: \sum_t y_{tg}\le s_g\ \text{for every }g,\quad \sum_g y_{tg}\ge\mu_t\ \text{for every }t \right\}. $$

For each type define its expected category counts
$$ H_t(y)=\frac1{\mu_t}\sum_{g:a_{tg}=p}y_{tg}, $$
$$ J_t(y)=\frac1{\mu_t}\sum_{g:a_{tg}=1}y_{tg}. $$
The type constraint ensures $H_t(y)+J_t(y)\ge 1$.

The finite-dimensional objective is
$$ \mathcal F(y)=\sum_t\mu_tF(H_t(y),J_t(y)). $$

Theorem: equivalence

The original maximum log-welfare equals
$$ \max_{y\in P}\mathcal F(y). $$

From an allocation to aggregate consumption

An allocation with finite log-welfare assigns no positive mass to the zero bundle. Define
$$ y_{tg}=\sum_z z_gx_{t,z}. $$
Supply feasibility gives the class constraints of $P$. Since every supported bundle contains at least one good,
$$ \sum_g y_{tg}\ge\sum_zx_{t,z}=\mu_t. $$

Normalize type $t$’s masses by $\mu_t$ and replace each bundle by its heavy and light counts. This is a feasible distribution for the local problem with means $H_t(y),J_t(y)$. Hence
$$ \sum_zx_{t,z}\log u_t(z) \le \mu_tF(H_t(y),J_t(y)). $$
Summing over types proves
$$ W(x)\le\mathcal F(y). $$

From aggregate consumption to an allocation

Conversely, take $y\in P$. For every type, construct the local optimal distribution at $(H_t(y),J_t(y))$.

For heavy classes with $H_t(y)>0$, use class-selection probabilities
$$ \rho_{tg}=\frac{y_{tg}}{\mu_tH_t(y)}. $$
For light classes with $J_t(y)>0$, use
$$ \sigma_{tg}=\frac{y_{tg}}{\mu_tJ_t(y)}. $$

Apply the class-splitting construction from Section 4 and multiply the resulting probabilities by $\mu_t$. Its consumption of each class is exactly $y_{tg}$, and its objective for type $t$ is exactly
$$ \mu_tF(H_t(y),J_t(y)). $$

Thus the reconstructed allocation is feasible and attains $\mathcal F(y)$.

If $y$ is rational, reconstruction uses polynomial-time rational arithmetic and produces at most $4\tau m^2$ configurations. Its encoding length is polynomial in the input length and the encoding length of $y$.

Attainment and concavity

The polytope $P$ is nonempty. Writing $S=\sum_gs_g$, the assignment
$$ y_{tg}=\mu_t\frac{s_g}{S} $$
belongs to $P$: each type consumes total mass $\mu_t$, and each class consumes $s_g/S\le s_g$.

It is compact, since it is closed and
$$ 0\le y_{tg}\le s_g. $$

The function $\mathcal F$ is concave, because each $F$ is concave and its arguments depend linearly on $y$. It is upper semicontinuous by the supporting-plane argument in Section 3. Therefore it attains its maximum on $P$. Reconstruction proves, independently, that the original problem has a finite-support maximizer.

6. What remains open

The reduction removes configuration enumeration from the formulation: there are only $m\tau$ aggregate variables, rational linear constraints, and explicit local objective formulas.

However, this is not yet an exact polynomial-time algorithm. The objective is piecewise affine with logarithmic coefficients. Its integer indices, such as $\lfloor H_t\rfloor$ and $\lfloor pH_t+J_t\rfloor$, can range over exponentially many values under binary encoding. I have not proved an algorithm that finds the globally maximizing regions and performs all necessary exact arithmetic in polynomial time.

The cited [finite-agent theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.10949v5) establishes polynomial-time solvability for explicitly listed agents and goods with integral two-value valuations. An additional argument is needed to obtain a polynomial-time algorithm for this succinct mass formulation.
