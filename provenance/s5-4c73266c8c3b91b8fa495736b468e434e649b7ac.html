The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type and good-class sets \(V\) and \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\), rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{G}\), and additive values \(w_{v,g}\), compute finite-support rational masses \(x_{v,b}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\) such that \(\sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v\), \(\sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g\), and \(x_{v,b}>0\) implies \(u_v(b)\ge\alpha q_v(\sigma)\), for \(3/4<\alpha\le3/4+3/3836\), where \(q_v(\sigma)\) is the maximum guarantee achievable by a distribution over integral bundles with mean \(\sigma\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(V\) and \(G\) be nonempty finite sets of types and good classes, with \(m=|G|\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|V|\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_v\), where \(\sum_v\mu_v=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Inputs also include binary-rational supplies \(\sigma\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{G}\), values \(w_{v,g}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), and \(\alpha\in\mathbb Q\) with \(\frac34<\alpha\le\frac34+\frac{3}{3836}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Bundles are unrestricted vectors \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\), with utility \(u_v(b)=\sum_g w_{v,g}b_g\).
Define \(q_v(\sigma)=\sup\{t\ge0:\exists p,\ \sum_b p_b=1,\ \sum_b p_bb=\sigma,\ p_b>0\Rightarrow u_v(b)\ge t\}\), where \(p\) ranges over probability distributions on integral bundles with finite coordinate means.
An \(\alpha\)-fair allocation consists of finitely many positive rational masses \(x_{v,b}\), with omitted entries zero, satisfying \(\sum_bx_{v,b}=\mu_v\), \(\sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g\) for every \(g\), and \(x_{v,b}>0\Rightarrow u_v(b)\ge\alpha q_v(\sigma)\).

Can an \(\alpha\)-fair allocation always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), throughout the stated range of \(\alpha\), or is attaining this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For finite agents and indivisible goods with nonnegative additive valuations, an algorithm guarantees \(\alpha\)-MMS allocations up to \(\alpha=\frac34+\frac{3}{3836}\); its normalization uses exact MMS partitions and is not polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07304)

1. Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable throughout the stated range. In fact, we can compute an allocation satisfying the stronger guarantee \(u_v(b)\ge \frac79 q_v(\sigma)\) on every positively allocated bundle.

The key feature of the formulation is that types can be handled independently. Suppose that, for each type \(v\), we compute a finite rational probability distribution \(p^{(v)}\) with mean \(\sigma\), supported on bundles of utility at least \(\frac79 q_v(\sigma)\). Setting \(x_{v,b}=\mu_v p^{(v)}_b\) gives

\(\sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v\),

and, for every good class \(g\),

\(\sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sum_v\mu_v\sigma_g=\sigma_g\).

Thus it suffices to solve the problem for one valuation vector. Below, fix that vector, write \(u(b)=\sum_g w_gb_g\), and abbreviate the benchmark by \(q\).

2. Completing a lottery to an exact prescribed mean

We first give an elementary construction used throughout the proof.

Lemma 1. For every nonnegative rational vector \(d\), one can compute, in polynomial time, a rational probability distribution on at most \(m+1\) nonnegative integral vectors whose mean is \(d\).

Proof. Write \(d_g=n_g+f_g\), where \(n_g=\lfloor d_g\rfloor\) and \(0\le f_g<1\). For a uniformly distributed auxiliary variable \(R\) on \([0,1]\), define

\(Z_g=n_g+\mathbf 1_{\{R<f_g\}}\).

Then \(\mathbb E[Z_g]=n_g+f_g=d_g\). The vector \(Z\) changes only at the distinct values among the \(f_g\), so its distribution has at most \(m+1\) support vectors. Their probabilities are the lengths of the intervals between consecutive breakpoints, including \(0\) and \(1\). These probabilities are rational.

Sorting the breakpoints constructs the distribution using polynomially many rational arithmetic operations. Its encoding length is polynomial in the encoding length of \(d\). \(\square\)

Consequently, suppose a finite rational bundle lottery has mean \(a\le \sigma\) coordinatewise. Apply Lemma 1 to \(d=\sigma-a\), and add an independently chosen residual vector to each bundle. The resulting lottery has mean exactly \(\sigma\). Since every valuation is nonnegative, this operation preserves every lower utility guarantee. Its support size increases by a factor of at most \(m+1\).

3. A polynomial-size LP for a candidate threshold

For a positive rational threshold \(t\), we construct an LP with the following two properties:

  • If \(t\) is feasible in the original definition of \(q\), the LP is feasible.
  • From any feasible LP solution, we can compute a finite rational lottery of mean exactly \(\sigma\), supported on bundles of utility at least \(\frac78t\).

These implications suffice; the LP need not decide exact feasibility at threshold \(t\).

3.1. Bounding and encoding the relevant bundles

Let \(H=\{g:w_g>0,\ \sigma_g>0\}\). In this section assume \(H\ne\varnothing\).

Under any lottery with mean \(\sigma\), coordinates with zero supply vanish almost surely: a nonnegative integer-valued random variable with expectation zero is zero almost surely. Goods with zero value can be discarded without affecting utility.

Consider a bundle \(b\), supported on \(H\), with \(u(b)\ge t\). Choose a coordinatewise minimal integral vector \(c\le b\) satisfying \(u(c)\ge t\). Such a vector exists because the set of integral vectors bounded by \(b\) is finite. For every \(g\) with \(c_g>0\), minimality gives

\(u(c)-w_g<t\).

Since \((c_g-1)w_g\le u(c)-w_g\), it follows that

\(c_g\le C_g:=\lceil t/w_g\rceil\).

For each \(g\in H\), let \(d_g\) be the smallest positive integer satisfying \(2^{d_g}>C_g\). Every integer between \(0\) and \(C_g\) can therefore be represented using the quantities \(1,2,\ldots,2^{d_g-1}\), each at most once.

Introduce one auxiliary binary item for each pair \((g,k)\), where \(g\in H\) and \(0\le k<d_g\). Selecting this item contributes \(2^k\) copies of good class \(g\). Let

\(N=\sum_{g\in H}d_g\).

Index these items by \(j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\). Write \(g(j)\) for the item's class, \(a_j\) for its quantity, and \(z_j=a_jw_{g(j)}\) for its utility.

The potentially large bundle coordinates have now been encoded by \(N\) binary choices.

3.2. Rounding utilities

Define

\(h=t/(8N),\qquad K=7N,\qquad s_j=\lfloor \min\{z_j,t\}/h\rfloor\).

The numbers \(s_j\) are nonnegative integers.

Suppose a selected set of auxiliary items has total utility at least \(t\). Its total capped utility is also at least \(t\): if one selected item has utility at least \(t\), that item alone contributes capped utility \(t\); otherwise capping changes nothing.

Therefore its rounded score satisfies

\(\sum_{j\text{ selected}}s_j\ge \frac{1}{h}\sum_{j\text{ selected}}\min\{z_j,t\}-N\ge 8N-N=K\).

Conversely, any selected set with rounded score at least \(K\) has true utility at least

\(\sum_{j\text{ selected}}z_j\ge h\sum_{j\text{ selected}}s_j\ge hK=\frac78t\).

Thus rounding preserves every truly \(t\)-good bundle, while every accepted rounded bundle is \(\frac78t\)-good.

3.3. The flow LP

Construct a layered directed acyclic graph. Its nodes are \((j,s)\), where \(0\le j\le N\) and \(0\le s\le K\).

For each item \(j\) and state \(s\), include two distinct arcs:

  • a skip arc from \((j-1,s)\) to \((j,s)\);
  • a take arc from \((j-1,s)\) to \((j,\min\{K,s+s_j\})\).

The arcs remain distinct even when they have the same endpoints. A take arc carries quantity \(a_j\) of class \(g(j)\); a skip arc carries zero quantity.

Let the source be \(S=(0,0)\) and the sink be \(T=(N,K)\). Introduce a nonnegative variable \(f_e\) for each arc. Impose unit-flow conservation:

\(\sum_{e\in\delta^+(a)}f_e-\sum_{e\in\delta^-(a)}f_e=\mathbf1_{\{a=S\}}-\mathbf1_{\{a=T\}}\)

at every node \(a\). For each class \(g\), additionally impose

\(\sum_{j:g(j)=g}a_j\sum_{e\text{ a take arc for }j}f_e\le \sigma_g\).

These are rational linear constraints. Rational LP feasibility admits a polynomial-time algorithm that either reports infeasibility or returns a rational feasible point of polynomial encoding length. We use this standard theorem in its exact rational form. [Khachiyan, *Polynomial algorithms in linear programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=zvmmf&option_lang=eng&paperid=5239&wshow=paper).

3.4. Why the LP has the required properties

Completeness for the original threshold. Suppose \(t\) is feasible in the definition of \(q\), possibly through an infinite-support lottery.

Trim each supported bundle to a minimal vector \(c\) as in Section 3.1. The resulting lottery has mean at most \(\sigma\). Its support is finite because each coordinate is bounded by \(C_g\).

Encode each trimmed bundle by its binary auxiliary-item choices. Section 3.2 shows that its rounded score is at least \(K\), so it defines a source-to-sink path. Taking the probability-weighted sum of these path flows gives a feasible LP solution. Its supply consumption equals the trimmed lottery's mean.

This argument establishes LP feasibility even if the original lottery had irrational probabilities.

Constructing a good rational lottery. Conversely, let \(f\) be a rational feasible LP solution. Because the graph is acyclic, \(f\) decomposes into source-to-sink paths with positive rational coefficients summing to one.

For completeness, repeatedly choose a path consisting of positive-flow arcs and subtract the minimum flow on that path. Each subtraction eliminates at least one positive arc. Hence at most \(|E|\) paths are needed, and the decomposition is polynomial-time.

Each path specifies an integral bundle. Reaching state \(K\) implies that its selected items have rounded score at least \(K\), so its utility is at least \(\frac78t\). The path coefficients define a rational probability distribution with mean \(a\le\sigma\), by the supply constraints.

Finally, apply Lemma 1 to \(\sigma-a\). The completed lottery has mean exactly \(\sigma\), and every supported bundle retains utility at least \(\frac78t\). This proves both required properties.

4. Finding a sufficient threshold without computing \(q\)

4.1. Detecting the zero-benchmark case

Define \(S_H=\sum_{g\in H}\sigma_g\).

If \(S_H<1\), no positive threshold is feasible. Indeed, every bundle with positive utility must contain at least one good from \(H\), whereas a lottery with mean \(\sigma\) has expected number of such goods equal to \(S_H<1\). Thus \(q=0\).

In this case, Lemma 1 applied directly to \(\sigma\) produces a suitable lottery: all bundle utilities are nonnegative.

Now suppose \(S_H\ge1\), and define

\(W=\min_{g\in H}w_g,\qquad U=\sum_gw_g\sigma_g\).

Choose singleton bundle \(e_g\) with probability \(\sigma_g/S_H\). These probabilities sum to one, and their mean is coordinatewise at most \(\sigma\). Every singleton has utility at least \(W\). Completing the mean using Lemma 1 proves that \(W\) is feasible.

Also, every feasible threshold is at most the expected utility \(U\). Hence

\(0<W\le q\le U\).

4.2. Geometric enumeration

Enumerate the rational thresholds

\(t_j=W(9/8)^j\)

for all nonnegative integers \(j\) such that \(t_j\le U\). For each threshold, construct and solve the LP from Section 3. Choose the largest threshold whose LP is feasible, and extract its completed lottery. At least \(t_0=W\) has a feasible LP.

We now prove that the chosen threshold is at least \(\frac89q\).

If \(q=W\), the feasible threshold \(W\) already suffices. Otherwise, let \(t_k\) be the largest enumerated threshold strictly below \(q\).

Every threshold strictly below \(q\) is feasible in the original definition: by the definition of supremum, some feasible threshold is larger, and its witnessing lottery also witnesses the smaller threshold. Therefore the LP at \(t_k\) is feasible.

The next geometric threshold satisfies

\(q\le t_{k+1}=\frac98t_k\).

Indeed, if \(t_{k+1}\le U\), this follows from maximality of \(k\); if \(t_{k+1}>U\), it follows from \(q\le U\). Consequently,

\(t_k\ge\frac89q\).

The selected feasible threshold is at least \(t_k\). Its completed lottery therefore guarantees

\(u(b)\ge\frac78t_k\ge\frac78\cdot\frac89q=\frac79q\).

This argument does not require the supremum defining \(q\) to be attained.

5. Bit complexity and output size

We verify polynomial time in the binary input model, including cases where bundles contain very large numbers of goods.

Using the total input length \(L\) as a crude bound, every positive input rational lies between \(2^{-L}\) and \(2^L\). Thus

\(U\le m2^{2L},\qquad W\ge2^{-L},\qquad U/W\le m2^{3L}\).

There are consequently only \(O(L+\log m)\) enumerated thresholds. Each threshold has polynomial encoding length because it is \(W\) multiplied by \(O(L+\log m)\) powers of \(9/8\).

For every tested threshold and every \(g\in H\),

\(C_g\le\lceil U/w_g\rceil\le m2^{3L}+1\).

Therefore \(d_g=O(L+\log m)\), and

\(N=O(m(L+\log m))\).

The graph has \(O(N^2)\) nodes and arcs, since \(K=7N\). Its LP has polynomially many constraints and variables. Every supply coefficient \(a_j=2^k\) has polynomial bit length, as do the remaining coefficients and right-hand sides. The hypotheses of polynomial-time rational LP feasibility therefore hold.

A rational LP solution has polynomial encoding length. Its decomposition uses at most \(O(N^2)\) paths, each of length \(N\). The resulting bundle coordinates have polynomial bit length because they are sums of the binary quantities \(2^k\). Rational subtraction during decomposition also preserves polynomial bit length: a common denominator for the polynomially many initial flow entries has polynomial bit length.

Mean completion multiplies the support size by at most \(m+1\). All residual means, probabilities, and completed bundle coordinates retain polynomial encoding length.

Thus, for each type, the entire computation and its explicit finite rational output are polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\). Repeating it for the \(\tau\) listed types takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

6. Assembling the allocation and checking the requested factor

For each type \(v\), let \(p^{(v)}\) be the computed lottery. It has mean exactly \(\sigma\), and every supported bundle satisfies \(u_v(b)\ge\frac79q_v(\sigma)\).

Set \(x_{v,b}=\mu_vp^{(v)}_b\), combining duplicate bundles within each type and omitting zero entries. These are finitely many positive rational masses of polynomial total encoding length. The equations in Section 1 verify both population masses and exact supply equality.

Finally,

\(\frac34+\frac3{3836}=\frac{720}{959}<\frac79\),

with difference \(\frac{233}{8631}>0\). Since \(q_v(\sigma)\ge0\), the computed allocation is \(\alpha\)-fair for every \(\alpha\) in the stated range.
