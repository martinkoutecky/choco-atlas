The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type and good-class sets \(V\) and \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\), rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{G}\), and additive values \(w_{v,g}\), compute finite-support rational masses \(x_{v,b}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\) such that \(\sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v\), \(\sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g\), and \(x_{v,b}>0\) implies \(u_v(b)\ge\alpha q_v(\sigma)\), for \(3/4<\alpha\le3/4+3/3836\), where \(q_v(\sigma)\) is the maximum guarantee achievable by a distribution over integral bundles with mean \(\sigma\).

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Under the standard convention that additive goods have nonnegative, binary-encoded rational values, the problem is solvable in polynomial time. In fact, a polynomial-time algorithm achieves the stronger factor \(\frac45\).

The proof below constructs the required rational lotteries directly.

1. The types can be handled separately.

Fix a type \(v\). Suppose we can compute a finite rational distribution \(p_{v,b}\) satisfying
\(\sum_b p_{v,b}=1\), \(\sum_b p_{v,b}b=\sigma\), and
\(p_{v,b}>0\Rightarrow u_v(b)\ge\frac45q_v(\sigma)\).

Set \(x_{v,b}=\mu_vp_{v,b}\). Then
\(\sum_bx_{v,b}=\mu_v\), and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b=\sum_v\mu_v\sigma=\sigma.\)

Thus it suffices to solve the single-type problem. Fix nonnegative rational weights \(w_g\), write \(u(b)=\sum_gw_gb_g\), and abbreviate the benchmark by \(q(\sigma)\).

2. Completing a lottery to the exact mean.

We first establish a useful construction.

Suppose a finite rational lottery on integral bundles has mean \(m\le\sigma\), coordinatewise. Put \(d=\sigma-m\). There is a rational lottery on nonnegative integral vectors \(Z\), with at most \(|G|+1\) outcomes, such that \(\mathbb E Z=d\).

Indeed, write \(d_g=n_g+r_g\), where \(n_g=\lfloor d_g\rfloor\) and \(0\le r_g<1\). For a uniform parameter \(T\in[0,1)\), define

\(\displaystyle Z_g(T)=n_g+\mathbf1_{\{T<r_g\}}.\)

The vector \(Z(T)\) changes only at the distinct fractional parts \(r_g\). These breakpoints give at most \(|G|+1\) outcomes with rational probabilities, and \(\mathbb E Z_g=n_g+r_g=d_g\).

Add an independent copy of \(Z\) to the original bundle. The resulting lottery has mean exactly \(\sigma\). Because all weights are nonnegative, this preserves every lower bound on utility. Its support size increases by at most a factor of \(|G|+1\), and all computations use rational arithmetic of polynomial bit complexity.

3. A polynomial-time approximate threshold test.

For any positive rational threshold \(t\), we construct a polynomial-time procedure with these two properties:

  • Completeness: if a distribution of mean \(\sigma\) guarantees utility at least \(t\), the procedure succeeds.
  • Soundness: whenever the procedure succeeds, it produces a finite rational lottery of mean exactly \(\sigma\) guaranteeing utility at least \(\frac9{10}t\).

The procedure may also succeed for some infeasible thresholds \(t\). The algorithm below does not require exact threshold decisions.

*Bounding the bundles needed for completeness.*

Let \(G_+=\{g:w_g>0\}\). Define \(k_g=\lceil t/w_g\rceil\) for \(g\in G_+\).

Every integral bundle \(b\) with \(u(b)\ge t\) contains a subbundle \(c\le b\) such that

\(\displaystyle u(c)\ge t,\qquad 0\le c_g\le k_g\quad(g\in G_+),\)

and \(c_g=0\) outside \(G_+\).

To see this, remove zero-valued goods and then remove individual items while maintaining utility at least \(t\). The process terminates. In the resulting bundle, whenever \(c_g>0\), minimality gives

\(\displaystyle (c_g-1)w_g\le u(c)-w_g<t,\)

which implies \(c_g\le\lceil t/w_g\rceil\).

Consequently, any distribution guaranteeing \(t\) can be mapped to a distribution over a finite box of such bundles, with mean at most \(\sigma\). This trimming is used only to prove completeness; the algorithm will not enumerate the box.

*Representing counts in binary.*

For each \(g\in G_+\), choose the smallest positive integer \(h_g\) satisfying \(2^{h_g}>k_g\). Introduce chunks of sizes

\(\displaystyle 1,2,\ldots,2^{h_g-1}\)

of good \(g\), each selectable at most once. Every integer count from zero through \(k_g\) has such a binary representation.

Let \(N=\sum_{g\in G_+}h_g\) be the total number of chunks, indexed by \(i\). Chunk \(i\) has good \(g(i)\), multiplicity \(c_i\), and utility \(a_i=w_{g(i)}c_i\).

Set

\(\displaystyle K=10N,\qquad H=9N,\qquad r_i=\left\lfloor\frac{K\min\{a_i,t\}}t\right\rfloor.\)

Two implications justify this rounding.

First, if a subset \(S\) of chunks has actual utility at least \(t\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}\min\{a_i,t\}\ge t.\)

Indeed, either one chunk already has utility at least \(t\), or clipping changes none of the selected utilities. Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}r_i \ge \frac Kt\sum_{i\in S}\min\{a_i,t\}-|S| \ge K-N=H.\)

Second, if \(\sum_{i\in S}r_i\ge H\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}a_i \ge\frac tK\sum_{i\in S}r_i \ge\frac HKt =\frac9{10}t.\)

Thus every genuinely covering bundle passes the rounded test, while every bundle passing the rounded test achieves the claimed approximate threshold.

*A flow linear program.*

Construct a layered directed acyclic graph with vertices \((i,s)\), where \(0\le i\le N\) and \(0\le s\le H\). The state records the rounded utility accumulated so far, capped at \(H\).

For each chunk \(i\) and state \(s\), include two arcs from layer \(i-1\) to layer \(i\):

  • a skip arc to \((i,s)\), labeled by the zero bundle;
  • a take arc to \((i,\min\{H,s+r_i\})\), labeled by \(c_i e_{g(i)}\).

Parallel arcs remain distinct. Every path from \((0,0)\) to \((N,H)\) selects a subset whose rounded utility is at least \(H\).

Introduce nonnegative arc flows \(f_e\). Require a unit flow from \((0,0)\) to \((N,H)\), and impose, for every good \(g\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\substack{e\text{ is a take arc}\\g(i(e))=g}} c_{i(e)}f_e\le\sigma_g. \)

This is an ordinary rational linear feasibility problem.

For completeness, start with a distribution guaranteeing \(t\), trim it as above, and represent each resulting bundle in binary. Each representation gives an accepting path. The distribution over paths induces a feasible flow because its expected resource use is at most \(\sigma\).

For soundness, a feasible rational flow decomposes into rational source-to-sink path flows. A constructive decomposition repeatedly extracts a positive-flow path and subtracts its minimum arc flow. Each iteration eliminates an arc, so at most the number of arcs many paths are needed. Their probabilities sum to one.

Each path’s bundle has utility at least \(\frac9{10}t\), and their mean is at most \(\sigma\). Apply the exact-mean completion from Section 2.

*Polynomial complexity.*

Each \(k_g\) has bit length polynomial in the encoding lengths of \(t\) and \(w_g\). Therefore \(N\) is polynomial in those lengths. The graph has \(O(NH)=O(N^2)\) vertices and arcs, and every coefficient has polynomial bit length.

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility of an explicitly represented rational system can be decided in polynomial time, and a feasible system admits a rational solution of polynomial bit length that can be computed in polynomial time. Our system has exactly these hypotheses; no full-dimensionality assumption is needed.

Flow decomposition and exact-mean completion preserve polynomial support size and polynomial encoding length. This proves the threshold-test claim.

4. Finding a sufficiently good threshold.

Let

\(\displaystyle U=\sum_gw_g\sigma_g.\)

Every distribution of mean \(\sigma\) has expected utility \(U\), so \(q(\sigma)\le U\).

The benchmark is attained. To justify this, take a common denominator \(D\) of the rational weights. Every integral bundle has utility in \(\frac1D\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). Any feasible threshold can therefore be increased to a feasible lattice threshold. There are only finitely many such thresholds between zero and \(U\), and zero is feasible by the construction in Section 2.

If all weights are zero, or \(U=0\), output any rational lottery of mean \(\sigma\). It already meets the required guarantee.

Otherwise let \(w_{\min}=\min\{w_g:w_g>0\}\). If \(q(\sigma)>0\), every bundle in a positive-guarantee lottery has positive utility, hence utility at least \(w_{\min}\). Thus

\(\displaystyle q(\sigma)>0\ \Longrightarrow\ w_{\min}\le q(\sigma)\le U.\)

Run the threshold procedure at every grid point

\(\displaystyle t_j=w_{\min}\left(\frac98\right)^j,\qquad j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\quad t_j\le U.\)

If any call succeeds, retain the lottery for the largest successful grid point. If none succeeds, output an arbitrary rational lottery of mean \(\sigma\).

Suppose first that \(q(\sigma)>0\). Let \(t_j\) be the largest grid point not exceeding \(q(\sigma)\). Then

\(\displaystyle t_j\le q(\sigma)<\frac98t_j,\qquad t_j>\frac89q(\sigma).\)

Since \(q(\sigma)\) is feasible, the threshold procedure succeeds at \(t_j\). The retained threshold is at least \(t_j\). Its lottery therefore guarantees at least

\(\displaystyle \frac9{10}t_j \ge\frac9{10}\cdot\frac89q(\sigma) =\frac45q(\sigma). \)

If \(q(\sigma)=0\), every lottery satisfies this guarantee because utilities are nonnegative.

The number of grid points is polynomial: the positive rational ratio \(U/w_{\min}\) has polynomial bit length, and hence its logarithm is polynomially bounded. Every grid point also has polynomial bit length. Together with Section 3, this proves polynomial running time and polynomial output size.

5. Applying the result to the requested factor.

Apply the single-type algorithm separately to each \(v\), and combine the resulting lotteries as in Section 1. Every positive output entry satisfies

\(\displaystyle u_v(b)\ge\frac45q_v(\sigma)\ge\alpha q_v(\sigma),\)

because

\(\displaystyle \alpha\le\frac34+\frac3{3836} =\frac{720}{959}<\frac45. \)

The output has finite polynomial-size support, rational probabilities, the prescribed type masses, and exactly the prescribed aggregate mean. This settles the computational problem for nonnegative rational additive valuations without using the finite-agent maximin-share theorem.

Scope of the valuation assumption. Nonnegativity is necessary for the formulation to be universally feasible. If signed values were intended, consider one type and one good with \(\mu=1\), \(\sigma=1\), and \(w=-1\). The deterministic bundle \(b=1\) shows \(q(\sigma)=-1\), and the expectation bound proves equality. For every requested \(\alpha<1\), the condition \(-b\ge-\alpha\) forces the nonnegative integer \(b\) to equal zero, contradicting mean one. Thus signed valuations would make the stated search problem infeasible on some inputs.
