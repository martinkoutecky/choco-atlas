The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite firm set \(F\), worker types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), explicit type-specific utility tables, integral salary intervals \([a_{tj},b_{tj}]\), and per-capita firm capacities \(\kappa_j\), find a stable mass allocation. Use variables \(y_{t,\bot}\) for unmatched mass and \(y_{t,j,z}\) for type-\(t\) mass hired by firm \(j\) at salary \(z\), with \(y_{t,\bot}+\sum_{j,z}y_{t,j,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}y_{t,j,z}\leq\kappa_j\). Require every positive-mass contract to be mutually acceptable; a firm's payoff is \(0\) when it has unused capacity and otherwise the minimum firm valuation among its positive-mass hires. Stability means that no positive-mass worker status and other firm have a feasible integral salary at which both strictly prefer to form a contract.

Each firm has a positive binary-rational capacity \(\kappa_j\). Each type–firm pair has integer bounds \(a_{tj}\leq b_{tj}\), allowing salaries \(Z_{tj}=[a_{tj},b_{tj}]\cap\mathbb Z\), and explicit signed binary-rational utility tables \(u_{tj}(z),v_{jt}(z)\): worker utility strictly increases and firm valuation strictly decreases with \(z\). Ties across contracts are allowed.
An allocation consists of nonnegative rational masses \(y_{t,\bot},y_{t,j,z}\), satisfying \(y_{t,\bot}+\sum_{j\in F}\sum_{z\in Z_{tj}}y_{t,j,z}=\mu_t\) and \(h_j:=\sum_t\sum_{z\in Z_{tj}}y_{t,j,z}\leq\kappa_j\). Every positive-mass contract must satisfy \(u_{tj}(z)\geq0\) and \(v_{jt}(z)\geq0\).
A worker status is \(\bot\), with utility \(0\), or \((j,z)\), with utility \(u_{tj}(z)\). The firm payoff is \(r_j=0\) if \(h_j<\kappa_j\), and otherwise \(r_j=\min\{v_{jt}(z):y_{t,j,z}>0\}\).
Stability requires that for every positive-mass type–status \((t,s)\), every firm \(j\) other than the status's current employer, and every \(\theta\in Z_{tj}\), either \(u_{tj}(\theta)\leq q_{t,s}\) or \(v_{jt}(\theta)\leq r_j\), where \(q_{t,s}\) is that status's utility. For unmatched status, every firm is eligible; ties do not block.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including all table entries.

Result

Finding a stable mass allocation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). A reduction to the known stable-flow problem gives a complete proof. The reduction computes an allocation satisfying a stronger condition than the stability required here.

1. The stable-flow theorem used

Consider a finite directed network with source \(S\), sink \(H\), nonnegative capacities, and strict preference orders on the incoming and outgoing arcs of each nonterminal vertex, ordered separately. A feasible flow respects capacities and conserves flow at every nonterminal vertex.

A blocking walk is a directed walk whose arcs are all unsaturated, such that:

  • Its initial vertex is \(S\), or prefers the walk’s first arc to another outgoing arc carrying positive flow.
  • Its final vertex is \(H\), or prefers the walk’s last arc to another incoming arc carrying positive flow.

A flow is stable if it has no blocking walk.

Cseh and Matuschke’s Theorem 2 provides an algorithm computing a stable flow using \(O(|V||A|)\) arithmetic operations, where \(V\) and \(A\) are the network’s vertices and arcs. Their algorithm starts with zero flow and augments along paths or cycles by minimum residual capacities. Consequently, integer capacities yield integer flows. [Source: *New and simple algorithms for stable flow problems*, Definition 2, Algorithm 1, and Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3701).

We apply this theorem to a simple, acyclic network.

2. Constructing the network

Let the set of mutually acceptable contracts be

\(\displaystyle E=\{(t,j,z):z\in Z_{tj},\ u_{tj}(z)\geq0,\ v_{jt}(z)\geq0\},\qquad N=|E|.\)

Discarding other contracts is legitimate: every individually rational allocation has nonnegative worker utilities and nonnegative firm payoffs. A contract with either utility or valuation negative therefore cannot strictly improve both sides.

If \(E\) is empty, assigning all workers to \(\bot\) is stable. Assume henceforth that \(E\) is nonempty.

Construct a network with:

  • Source \(S\) and sink \(H\).
  • A vertex for each type and firm incident to a contract in \(E\).
  • A distinct vertex \(c_e\) for each contract \(e=(t,j,z)\in E\).

Add the following arcs and capacities:

| Arc | Capacity |
|---|---:|
| \(S\to t\) | \(\mu_t\) |
| \(t\to c_e\), for \(e=(t,j,z)\) | \(2\) |
| \(c_e\to j\), for \(e=(t,j,z)\) | \(2\) |
| \(j\to H\) | \(\kappa_j\) |

At a type vertex \(t\), rank outgoing contract arcs by decreasing worker utility \(u_{tj}(z)\). At a firm vertex \(j\), rank incoming contract arcs by decreasing firm valuation \(v_{jt}(z)\). Break ties arbitrarily to obtain strict orders.

Every other incoming or outgoing preference list has one element. In particular, the construction has no parallel arcs, despite allowing multiple salaries for a type–firm pair.

The stable-flow theorem’s hypotheses hold: all capacities are positive, \(S\) has only outgoing arcs, \(H\) has only incoming arcs, and each nonterminal vertex has both an incoming and an outgoing arc.

3. Extracting a feasible allocation

Let \(x\) be a stable flow in this network. For every retained contract \(e=(t,j,z)\), define

\(\displaystyle y_{t,j,z}=x(t,c_e)=x(c_e,j).\)

The equality follows from flow conservation at \(c_e\). Set all discarded contract masses to zero, and define

\(\displaystyle y_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_{j,z}y_{t,j,z}.\)

For types omitted from the network, this sets \(y_{t,\bot}=\mu_t\).

For an included type, conservation and the source-arc capacity give

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j,z}y_{t,j,z}=x(S,t)\leq\mu_t.\)

Thus unmatched masses are nonnegative and the type mass equations hold. Similarly, for an included firm,

\(\displaystyle h_j=\sum_{t,z}y_{t,j,z}=x(j,H)\leq\kappa_j.\)

Omitted firms have hiring mass zero. Every positive-mass contract belongs to \(E\), so individual rationality also holds.

A useful consequence is that every contract arc is unsaturated. Indeed, for \(e=(t,j,z)\),

\(\displaystyle x(t,c_e)=x(c_e,j)\leq\mu_t\leq1<2.\)

4. Proof of stability in the requested model

Suppose, for contradiction, that the extracted allocation is blocked. Then there exist a positive-mass type–status \((t,\sigma)\), an eligible firm \(j\), and a salary \(\theta\in Z_{tj}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle u_{tj}(\theta)>q_{t,\sigma},\qquad v_{jt}(\theta)>r_j.\)

Feasibility and individual rationality imply \(q_{t,\sigma}\geq0\) and \(r_j\geq0\). Hence \(e=(t,j,\theta)\) belongs to \(E\). Both arcs of \(t\to c_e\to j\) are unsaturated.

We now construct a blocking path for \(x\), checking its initial and final conditions.

Initial condition

If \(\sigma=\bot\), then \(y_{t,\bot}>0\), so

\(\displaystyle x(S,t)=\mu_t-y_{t,\bot}<\mu_t.\)

Thus \(S\to t\) is unsaturated, and the path can begin at \(S\).

Otherwise, let \(\sigma=(j_0,z_0)\), with corresponding contract \(e_0\). Since this status has positive mass,

\(\displaystyle x(t,c_{e_0})=y_{t,j_0,z_0}>0.\)

The blocking inequality gives

\(\displaystyle u_{tj}(\theta)>u_{tj_0}(z_0).\)

Therefore \(t\) strictly prefers \(t\to c_e\) to the positive-flow arc \(t\to c_{e_0}\), even after tie breaking. The path can begin at \(t\).

Final condition

If \(h_j<\kappa_j\), then \(j\to H\) is unsaturated, so the path can end at \(H\).

Otherwise \(h_j=\kappa_j>0\). There are finitely many contracts, and at least one has positive mass at \(j\). Consequently, some contract \(e_1=(t_1,j,z_1)\) attains the minimum defining \(r_j\):

\(\displaystyle x(c_{e_1},j)>0,\qquad v_{jt_1}(z_1)=r_j.\)

Since \(v_{jt}(\theta)>r_j\), firm \(j\) strictly prefers \(c_e\to j\) to this positive-flow incoming arc. The path can end at \(j\).

Combining the conditions

According to the two cases at each end, we obtain one of the following paths:

| Worker status | Firm hiring | Blocking path |
|---|---|---|
| Unmatched | \(h_j<\kappa_j\) | \(S\to t\to c_e\to j\to H\) |
| Unmatched | \(h_j=\kappa_j\) | \(S\to t\to c_e\to j\) |
| Matched | \(h_j<\kappa_j\) | \(t\to c_e\to j\to H\) |
| Matched | \(h_j=\kappa_j\) | \(t\to c_e\to j\) |

Every arc is unsaturated, and both endpoint conditions hold. This contradicts stability of \(x\).

Therefore the extracted allocation is stable.

Notice that this argument does not need the exclusion of same-employer deviations: the constructed flow also rules out such deviations whenever both strict inequalities hold. Computing an allocation with this stronger property suffices for the question asked.

5. Binary running time and rational output

Because the salary tables are explicit, the number \(N\) of retained contracts is \(O(L)\). The constructed network satisfies

\(\displaystyle |V|\leq2+\tau+m+N,\qquad |A|\leq\tau+m+2N.\)

Construction and preference sorting take polynomial time using exact comparisons of binary rationals.

To check binary arithmetic and output length explicitly, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators appearing in the masses and firm capacities. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Multiply every network capacity by \(D\), obtaining integer capacities of binary length \(O(L)\).

Run the stable-flow algorithm on this scaled network. Starting from integer zero flow, each augmentation adds or subtracts the minimum of integer residual capacities, so every intermediate and final flow is integral. All arc flows and residual capacities remain bounded by their corresponding capacities and therefore have polynomial binary length.

The algorithm uses \(O(|V||A|)\) arithmetic operations. Its binary running time is consequently polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Divide the final flow by \(D\); positive scaling preserves saturation and blocking-walk conditions. The resulting allocation is rational and has polynomial total output length.

Thus the reduction proves both existence and polynomial-time computability for exactly the stated mass problem. No salary-adjustment running-time bound is needed.
