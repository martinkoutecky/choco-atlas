The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of types \(T\) and states \(\Omega\), rational type masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), rational full-support initial beliefs \(q_{a,0}\), and a rational interaction matrix \(W\) for which \(A_{ab}=W_{ab}\mu_b\) is primitive, compute the limiting consensus mode \(\arg\max_{\omega\in\Omega}\sum_{b\in T}\lambda_b\log q_{b,0}(\omega)\), where \(\lambda\) is the normalized positive left Perron–Frobenius eigenvector of \(A\). Assume the maximizing state is unique with a supplied rational score-gap lower bound, and determine the computation’s dependence on \(|T|\), \(|\Omega|\), input bit length, and the gap.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_a\), where \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(\Omega\) be an explicitly listed state set of size \(m\ge2\).
Each type has a rational probability distribution \(q_{a,0}\) with \(q_{a,0}(\omega)>0\) for every state. The common prior is uniform. A nonnegative rational matrix \(W\) specifies interaction weights, with \(A_{ab}=W_{ab}\mu_b\); promise that \(A\) is primitive and its spectral radius \(\rho(A)>1\).
Mass-weighted Bayesian Peer Influence updates are \(q_{a,k+1}(\omega)=\frac{\prod_b q_{b,k}(\omega)^{A_{ab}}}{\sum_{v\in\Omega}\prod_b q_{b,k}(v)^{A_{ab}}}\).
Let \(\lambda\) be the positive left Perron–Frobenius eigenvector, normalized by \(\sum_b\lambda_b=1\), and set \(s(\omega)=\sum_b\lambda_b\log q_{b,0}(\omega)\), using natural logarithms. A supplied binary rational \(\delta>0\) promises a unique maximizer \(\omega^*\) satisfying \(s(\omega^*)-s(v)\ge\delta\) for every \(v\ne\omega^*\). The required output is \(\omega^*\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including \(\delta\).

Is this promised consensus-mode problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? What is its bit-complexity dependence on \(m,\tau,L\), and \(\delta\), particularly on \(\log(1+\delta^{-1})\)?

For finite connected undirected networks with self-links and at least two agents, Bayesian Peer Influence converges to the unique Perron-weighted product mode; this is a convergence characterization, not a bit-complexity theorem. [Levy–Razin](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/86554/7/Levy_information_diffusion_in_networks_accepted.pdf)

Result

The promised problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time. Its bit complexity is polynomial in
$$ m,\quad \tau,\quad L,\quad \log(1+\delta^{-1}). $$
In particular, the algorithm does not require a number of steps proportional to $\delta^{-1}$. Since $\delta$ is a positive binary rational included in the input,
$$ \log_2(1+\delta^{-1})\le L+1, $$
so the running time is polynomial in the total input length itself.

Consequently, NP-hardness under polynomial-time, promise-preserving reductions would imply $\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}$.

The proof computes the Perron-weighted scores directly. It supplies a bit-complexity argument separate from the convergence characterization in [Levy–Razin](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/86554/7/Levy_information_diffusion_in_networks_accepted.pdf).

1. Integer scaling and basic bounds

Write $n=\tau$ and $A_{ab}=W_{ab}\mu_b$.

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators appearing in $W$ and in the masses. Then
$$ B=DA $$
is an integer matrix. Indeed, the denominator of each product $W_{ab}\mu_b$ divides $D$. This construction uses the supported types directly, without expanding their rational masses into agents.

The matrices $A$ and $B$ have the same left and right Perron eigenvectors. Moreover, $B$ is primitive. Put
$$ r=\rho(B),\qquad H=\max_{a,b}B_{ab},\qquad U=nH+1. $$
The input bounds give
$$ D\le 2^L,\qquad H\le 2^{2L},\qquad \log_2 U=O(L+\log(n+1)). $$
Every eigenvalue of $B$ has modulus at most $nH$, by the maximum row-sum bound. Thus
$$ 0<r<U. $$

We use the following consequences of the Perron–Frobenius theorem: because $B$ is primitive, $r$ is algebraically simple; there are strictly positive vectors $\lambda,h$ satisfying
$$ B^\top\lambda=r\lambda,\qquad Bh=rh, \qquad \sum_a\lambda_a=1. $$

Finally, every input probability satisfies $2^{-L}\le q_{a,0}(\omega)\le1$. Therefore, with
$$ M=L+1, $$
we have
$$ \left|\log q_{a,0}(\omega)\right|\le M. $$

2. A lower bound needed for eigenvector approximation

Let
$$ p(x)=\det(xI-B). $$
This is a monic integer polynomial of degree $n$. Define
$$ \gamma=(2U)^{-(n-1)^2}. $$
We first prove
$$ p'(r)\ge\gamma. $$

Let $f$ be the monic minimal polynomial of $r$ over $\mathbb Q$, of degree $d$. By Gauss’s lemma, $f$ belongs to $\mathbb Z[x]$ and divides $p$. Its distinct roots $\alpha_1=r,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_d$ are roots of $p$.

Because $r$ is a simple root of $p$, we have $p'(r)\ne0$. Irreducibility of $f$ then implies
$$ p'(\alpha_j)\ne0 $$
for every $j$: otherwise $f$ would divide $p'$.

Multiplication by $p'$ on $\mathbb Z[x]/(f)$ has an integer matrix in the basis $1,x,\ldots,x^{d-1}$. Over $\mathbb C$, evaluation at the distinct roots of $f$ diagonalizes this multiplication map. Hence its determinant is the nonzero integer
$$ \prod_{j=1}^{d}p'(\alpha_j). $$
Its absolute value is at least $1$.

Every root of $p$ has modulus less than $U$. At any simple root $\alpha_j$,
$$ |p'(\alpha_j)| =\prod_{\substack{\beta\text{ a root of }p\\ \beta\ne\alpha_j}} |\alpha_j-\beta| \le(2U)^{n-1}, $$
where roots in the product are counted with multiplicity. Consequently,
$$ |p'(r)| \ge(2U)^{-(n-1)(d-1)} \ge\gamma. $$
Also $p'(r)>0$: every other real eigenvalue is less than $r$, and conjugate nonreal eigenvalues contribute positive factors $|r-\beta|^2$ to $p'(r)$. This proves the claim.

The minimal polynomial is used only to establish this bound; computing it is unnecessary.

Now define the polynomial vector
$$ y(t)=\operatorname{adj}(tI-B^\top)\mathbf1, \qquad S(t)=\mathbf1^\top y(t). $$
At the simple eigenvalue $r$,
$$ \operatorname{adj}(rI-B^\top) =\frac{p'(r)}{h^\top\lambda}\lambda h^\top. $$
To justify this identity, the adjugate’s columns and rows lie in the respective one-dimensional nullspaces of $rI-B^\top$. Thus it is a scalar multiple of $\lambda h^\top$. Differentiating the determinant gives
$$ p'(r)=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\operatorname{adj}(rI-B^\top)\right), $$
which determines the scalar.

It follows that
$$ y(r)=p'(r)\frac{h^\top\mathbf1}{h^\top\lambda}\lambda, \qquad S(r)=p'(r)\frac{h^\top\mathbf1}{h^\top\lambda}\ge\gamma. $$
The inequality uses $\lambda_a\le1$ and $h_a>0$.

3. Locating the Perron eigenvalue by rational bisection

For rational $t$, test whether $tI-B^\top$ is nonsingular and the exact solution of
$$ (tI-B^\top)x=\mathbf1 $$
has every coordinate strictly positive.

This test is true exactly when $t>r$.

If $t>r$, the convergent Neumann series gives
$$ (tI-B^\top)^{-1} =\frac1t\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(B^\top/t)^k. $$
Every summand is nonnegative, and its identity term ensures $x_a>0$.

Conversely, if a solution has $x>0$, multiplication by $h^\top$ gives
$$ (t-r)h^\top x=h^\top\mathbf1>0. $$
Thus $t>r$. If the matrix is singular, $t$ is an eigenvalue of $B^\top$, so it cannot exceed $r$.

All these tests use exact rational arithmetic. Therefore bisection on $[0,U]$ maintains an interval containing $r$, with upper endpoint strictly greater than $r$. After $K$ steps its upper endpoint $t$ satisfies
$$ r<t,\qquad t-r\le U2^{-K}. $$

4. Certified approximation of the Perron vector

We next bound how accurately $t$ must approximate $r$.

For $t\in[0,U]$, every entry of $tI-B^\top$ has absolute value at most $2U$. Expanding each cofactor determinant and differentiating its products shows, for $n\ge2$,
$$ |y_a'(t)| \le n(n-1)!(n-1)(2U)^{n-2}. $$
For $n=1$, the derivative is zero. Hence the following convenient bound works for all $n$:
$$ |y_a'(t)|\le C,\qquad C=n^2n!(2U)^n. $$
The mean-value theorem yields
$$ \|y(t)-y(r)\|_1\le nC|t-r|. $$

For $t>r$, let
$$ \widehat\lambda=\frac{x(t)}{\mathbf1^\top x(t)}. $$
This is an exactly computable positive rational probability vector. Since $p(t)>0$,
$$ \widehat\lambda=\frac{y(t)}{S(t)}. $$

Put $E=\|y(t)-y(r)\|_1$. Both vectors are positive, and normalization gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \left\|\frac{y(t)}{S(t)}-\frac{y(r)}{S(r)}\right\|_1 &\le \frac{E}{S(r)} +\frac{|S(t)-S(r)|}{S(r)}\\ &\le\frac{2E}{S(r)}. \end{aligned} $$
Therefore
$$ \|\widehat\lambda-\lambda\|_1 \le\frac{2nC}{\gamma}|t-r|. $$

Choose
$$ \eta=\frac{\delta\gamma}{16nCM} $$
and run bisection until $U2^{-K}\le\eta$. Then
$$ \|\widehat\lambda-\lambda\|_1\le\frac{\delta}{8M}. $$
The required number of steps satisfies
$$ K= O\!\left( n^2(L+\log(n+1)) +\log(1+\delta^{-1}) +\log(L+1) \right). $$
This follows by taking binary logarithms of $U/\eta$, using the explicit formulas for $C$ and $\gamma$.

Thus no additional quantitative promise on the spectral gap is required.

5. Computing logarithms and identifying the winner

Here is an explicit rational procedure for approximating the natural logarithms.

For an input probability $q$, choose an integer $e$ such that
$$ u=2^e q\in[1,2),\qquad 0\le e\le L. $$
The exponent is obtained from numerator and denominator bit lengths followed by a comparison. Then
$$ \log q=\log u-e\log2. $$

For $0\le z\le1/3$, define
$$ F_N(z)=2\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. $$
Expanding the geometric series and integrating gives
$$ \log\frac{1+z}{1-z} =2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. $$
Its tail obeys
$$ \begin{aligned} 0\le \log\frac{1+z}{1-z}-F_N(z) &\le\frac{2z^{2N+1}}{1-z^2}\\ &\le9^{-N}. \end{aligned} $$
Apply this with $z=(u-1)/(u+1)$ for $\log u$, and with $z=1/3$ for $\log2$. The resulting approximation to $\log q$ has error at most
$$ (L+1)9^{-N}. $$

Choose $N\ge1$ with
$$ (L+1)9^{-N}\le\frac{\delta}{16}, $$
and round the resulting rational number to a common dyadic precision whose rounding error is at most $\delta/16$. We obtain rational numbers $\ell_a(\omega)$ satisfying
$$ |\ell_a(\omega)-\log q_{a,0}(\omega)|\le\frac{\delta}{8}. $$
The required parameters obey
$$ N=O\!\left(\log(L+1)+\log(1+\delta^{-1})\right), $$
and the dyadic precision is $O(1+\log(1+\delta^{-1}))$ bits after the binary point.

Now compute, exactly,
$$ \widehat s(\omega)=\sum_a\widehat\lambda_a\ell_a(\omega). $$
Because $\widehat\lambda$ is a probability vector,
$$ \begin{aligned} |\widehat s(\omega)-s(\omega)| &\le M\|\widehat\lambda-\lambda\|_1 +\sum_a\widehat\lambda_a |\ell_a(\omega)-\log q_{a,0}(\omega)|\\ &\le\frac{\delta}{8}+\frac{\delta}{8} =\frac{\delta}{4}. \end{aligned} $$

For every $v\ne\omega^*$, the supplied promise therefore implies
$$ \widehat s(\omega^*)-\widehat s(v) \ge\delta-\frac{\delta}{4}-\frac{\delta}{4} =\frac{\delta}{2}>0. $$
Returning the exact maximizer of the rational scores $\widehat s$ returns $\omega^*$.

6. An explicit conservative bit-complexity bound

Let
$$ G=\left\lceil\log_2(1+\delta^{-1})\right\rceil, $$
and use the parameters $K,N$ above. Define
$$ P= n\bigl(L+K+\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil\bigr) + N\bigl(L+\lceil\log_2(N+1)\rceil\bigr). $$
A conservative implementation using schoolbook integer arithmetic has bit complexity
$$ O\!\left( \left[(K+1)n^4+mn(N+1)\right]P^2 \right). $$

Here are the arithmetic bounds supporting this estimate.

  • Bisection and eigenvector recovery. At each step, clear the dyadic denominator in the linear system. The resulting integer matrices have entry lengths $O(L+K+\log(n+1))$. Cramer’s rule uses at most $n+1$ determinants to test nonsingularity and positivity. Fraction-free elimination computes each determinant in $O(n^3)$ integer operations. Its intermediate values are minors, whose bit lengths are $O(n(L+K+\log(n+1)))$ by Hadamard’s inequality. Thus each step costs $O(n^4P^2)$ bit operations. The final normalized vector can be represented with a common integer denominator of $O(P)$ bits.
  • Logarithms. If $z=a/b$, a common denominator for $F_N(z)$ is

$$ b^{2N-1}\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}(2j+1). $$
Its bit length is $O(N(L+\log(N+1)))$. Precomputing the necessary powers and assembling the terms uses $O(N)$ integer operations of $O(P)$ bits. Range reduction and dyadic rounding have the same polynomial bounds.

  • Scores. All logarithm approximations have a common dyadic denominator, and all coordinates of $\widehat\lambda$ have a common integer denominator. Computing and comparing the $m$ scores takes $O(mn)$ further operations of $O(P)$ bits.

Integer scaling and input processing fit within the displayed bound.

This estimate is deliberately coarse, but it gives the requested dependence explicitly:
$$ K=O\!\left(n^2(L+\log(n+1))+G+\log(L+1)\right), \qquad N=O(G+\log(L+1)). $$
In particular, every dependence on gap precision is polynomial in $G$, rather than in $\delta^{-1}$. Since $G\le L+1$ and both $m$ and $n=\tau$ are explicitly represented, the algorithm is polynomial in $L$.

7. Connection to the Bayesian dynamics

The same assumptions also justify calling the output the consensus mode.

For each competing state $v$, define the vector
$$ d_{a,k}(v)= \log\frac{q_{a,k}(\omega^*)}{q_{a,k}(v)}. $$
Cancellation of the update’s normalization factors gives
$$ d_{k+1}(v)=Ad_k(v), \qquad d_k(v)=A^kd_0(v). $$

Because $A$ is primitive, Perron–Frobenius theory gives
$$ \rho(A)^{-k}A^k \longrightarrow \frac{h\lambda^\top}{\lambda^\top h}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \rho(A)^{-k}d_k(v) \longrightarrow \frac{h\,[s(\omega^*)-s(v)]}{\lambda^\top h}. $$
Every coordinate of the limit is strictly positive by the gap promise. Since $\rho(A)>1$, each $d_{a,k}(v)$ tends to $+\infty$. Finally,
$$ q_{a,k}(\omega^*) = \frac{1}{1+\sum_{v\ne\omega^*}\exp(-d_{a,k}(v))} \longrightarrow1. $$
The polynomial-time algorithm above identifies this state without simulating the dynamics.

2. The derivative lower bound

The argument establishing
