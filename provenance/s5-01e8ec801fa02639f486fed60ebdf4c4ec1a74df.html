The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(C\), a target \(p\in C\), a rational coalition mass \(\alpha\), a distribution \(\nu\) of total mass \(1-\alpha\) over the \(\tau\) supported rankings of the nonmanipulators, and a choice of whether revoting is allowed, does there exist an allocation \(x\) of coalition mass \(\alpha\) over rankings of \(C\) that makes \(p\) an overall Veto winner? Veto winners are the candidates with minimum veto mass; if the first-round winner set \(W\) has more than one candidate, restrict nonmanipulator rankings to \(W\) and run Veto again. Without revoting, restrict the coalition’s rankings from \(x\) to \(W\); with revoting, allow a new allocation \(y\) of mass \(\alpha\) over rankings of \(W\). The instance masses are binary rationals; the question is feasibility.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set with \(m=|C|\geq 1\), and let \(p\in C\). Rankings are strict linear orders.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Here the input society \(\nu\) consists of nonmanipulators and has total mass \(1-\alpha\), where \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is a binary rational. Empty support is allowed when \(\alpha=1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The coalition may allocate arbitrary nonnegative real masses \(x_r\) over all rankings \(r\) of \(C\), with \(\sum_r x_r=\alpha\); its allowed rankings are not restricted to the input support.
Run Veto on \(\nu+x\), retaining every tied winner in the first-round winner set \(W\). If \(|W|=1\), that candidate is the overall winner.
If \(|W|>1\), conduct exactly one Veto runoff on \(W\). Restrict each nonmanipulator ranking to \(W\), preserving its mass. Without revoting, also restrict the coalition rankings from \(x\); with revoting, the coalition may instead choose arbitrary nonnegative real masses \(y_s\) over rankings \(s\) of \(W\), with \(\sum_s y_s=\alpha\).
Every tied runoff winner is an overall winner; no tie-breaking or further rounds occur. Feasibility means that some permitted allocation or allocations make \(p\) an overall winner.

For each variant, without revoting and with revoting, is this continuous feasibility problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete unweighted coalitional manipulation of same-system Veto runoffs is NP-complete both without and with revoting, whereas ordinary unweighted Veto manipulation is in \(\mathrm{P}\). [Fitzsimmons, Hemaspaandra, and Hemaspaandra](https://mathcs.holycross.edu/~zfitzsim/papers/Fitzsimmons2016_Article_ManipulationComplexityOfSame-S.pdf)
No result settling exactly this continuous feasibility problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified strict linear rankings, as required by the discrete model.
  • Specified a nonempty candidate set, allowing a single candidate because the discrete definition permits it.
  • Set the coalition mass domain to the closed interval [0,1], allowing zero coalition mass and an empty nonmanipulator support at coalition mass 1.
  • Interpreted divisible coalition allocations as nonnegative real masses over all rankings, without an integrality constraint or restriction to the nonmanipulator support.
  • Made explicit that a unique first-round winner ends the election, all runoff ties count as wins, and there is exactly one runoff.
  • Defined L as total input bit length and tau as the positive-mass nonmanipulator support size.

Classification

Both variants are in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof gives exact feasibility criteria and polynomial-size rational witnesses.

The decisive observation is that any positive surplus beyond the mass needed to make \(p\) a first-round co-winner allows the coalition to make \(p\) the unique first-round winner. At the boundary where there is no surplus, the first-round winner set and the coalition’s last-place masses are forced.

1. Last-place masses

For each candidate \(c\in C\), let \(a_c\) be its nonmanipulator last-place mass. For a coalition allocation, let \(b_c\) be its last-place mass. Thus

\[ a_c=\sum_{r:\,c\text{ is last in }r}\nu_r, \qquad b_c=\sum_{r:\,c\text{ is last in }r}x_r, \qquad \sum_{c\in C}b_c=\alpha. \]

Because the total society mass is \(1\), candidate \(c\)’s Veto score is \(1-a_c-b_c\). Hence the first-round winners are precisely the candidates minimizing \(a_c+b_c\).

Every nonnegative vector \(b\) summing to \(\alpha\) is realizable: for each \(c\) with \(b_c>0\), choose one full ranking ending in \(c\) and assign it mass \(b_c\). This uses at most \(m\) rankings.

If \(m=1\), then \(p\) always wins. Henceforth assume \(m\geq 2\), and define

\[ D=\sum_{c\in C\setminus\{p\}}\max\{0,a_p-a_c\}. \]

2. Complete first-round analysis

Insufficient mass: \(\alpha<D\)

If \(p\) is a first-round winner, then, for every \(c\neq p\),

\[ a_c+b_c\geq a_p+b_p, \]

and therefore

\[ b_c\geq\max\{0,a_p-a_c\}. \]

Summing gives

\[ \alpha=b_p+\sum_{c\neq p}b_c\geq b_p+D\geq D. \]

Thus, when \(\alpha<D\), candidate \(p\) cannot reach the runoff or win outright. Both variants are infeasible.

Surplus mass: \(\alpha>D\)

Put \(\delta=\alpha-D>0\), and choose

\[ b_p=0, \qquad b_c=\max\{0,a_p-a_c\}+\frac{\delta}{m-1} \quad(c\neq p). \]

These masses are nonnegative and sum to \(\alpha\). Moreover, for every \(c\neq p\),

\[ a_c+b_c =\max\{a_c,a_p\}+\frac{\delta}{m-1} >a_p=a_p+b_p. \]

Consequently, \(p\) is the unique first-round winner. Both variants are feasible.

Boundary mass: \(\alpha=D\)

Suppose \(p\) is a first-round winner. The necessity argument above gives

\[ D=\alpha\geq b_p+D, \]

so \(b_p=0\). Each other coordinate satisfies \(b_c\geq\max\{0,a_p-a_c\}\), and their sum equals the sum of these lower bounds. Thus every coordinate is forced:

\[ b_c=\max\{0,a_p-a_c\} \quad(c\in C). \]

Conversely, this vector makes \(p\) a first-round winner. The resulting totals satisfy

\[ a_c+b_c=\max\{a_c,a_p\}. \]

Therefore every potentially successful manipulation has exactly the same first-round winner set,

\[ W=\{c\in C:a_c\leq a_p\}. \]

If \(W=\{p\}\), then \(p\) wins outright. It remains to analyze the boundary case with \(|W|>1\).

3. Exact runoff criteria at the boundary

For each \(c\in W\), let \(v_c\) be its nonmanipulator last-place mass after restricting the input rankings to \(W\):

\[ v_c=\sum_{r:\,c\text{ is last in }r|_W}\nu_r. \]

The runoff also has total mass \(1\), so its winners minimize their runoff last-place masses.

Without revoting

The forced vector \(b\) has \(b_c=0\) outside \(W\). Hence every coalition ranking carrying positive mass ends in a candidate belonging to \(W\).

If a full ranking ends in \(c\in W\), its restriction to \(W\) still ends in \(c\). Consequently, the coalition’s runoff last-place mass at each \(c\in W\) is necessarily \(b_c\), regardless of the remaining positions in its rankings.

Since \(b_p=0\), candidate \(p\) wins the runoff exactly when

\[ v_p\leq v_c+b_c =v_c+a_p-a_c \quad\text{for every }c\in W\setminus\{p\}. \]

This condition is necessary by the forced-mass argument. It is sufficient because the forced vector \(b\) is realizable, and every realization produces these same runoff last-place masses.

With revoting

Let \(q_c\) be the coalition’s new runoff last-place mass at \(c\in W\). Every nonnegative vector \(q\) summing to \(\alpha\) is realizable by rankings of \(W\).

Define

\[ E=\sum_{c\in W\setminus\{p\}}\max\{0,v_p-v_c\}. \]

If \(p\) wins, then \(v_c+q_c\geq v_p+q_p\) for every \(c\neq p\). Since \(q_p\geq0\), this implies

\[ q_c\geq\max\{0,v_p-v_c\}, \qquad \alpha\geq E. \]

Conversely, suppose \(E\leq\alpha\). Initially assign

\[ q_p=0, \qquad q_c=\max\{0,v_p-v_c\} \quad(c\neq p). \]

Assign the remaining mass \(\alpha-E\) to any one candidate in \(W\setminus\{p\}\), which exists because \(|W|>1\). Every rival then has runoff last-place mass at least \(v_p\), so \(p\) is a runoff winner.

Thus revoting is feasible exactly when \(E\leq\alpha\).

4. Algorithm and bit complexity

The complete decision procedure is:

  • If \(m=1\), return YES.
  • Compute the nonmanipulator last-place masses \(a_c\) and the threshold \(D\).
  • If \(\alpha<D\), return NO. If \(\alpha>D\), return YES.
  • If \(\alpha=D\), compute \(W=\{c:a_c\leq a_p\}\). If \(W=\{p\}\), return YES.
  • Otherwise compute the restricted last-place masses \(v_c\).
  • Without revoting: return YES exactly when \(v_p\leq v_c+a_p-a_c\) for every \(c\in W\setminus\{p\}\).
  • With revoting: return YES exactly when \(\sum_{c\in W\setminus\{p\}}\max\{0,v_p-v_c\}\leq\alpha\).

Computing \(a\) and \(v\) requires scanning the \(\tau\) supplied rankings, each of length \(m\). There is no enumeration of possible rankings or candidate subsets.

For exact arithmetic, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and of \(\alpha\). Its binary length is at most the sum of their encoded denominator lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Every quantity used in the decision procedure is an integer multiple of \(1/Q\), with magnitude at most polynomial in \(m\). Thus all arithmetic operands have polynomial bit length, and the entire procedure runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The witness constructions also have polynomial bit length. The surplus construction introduces only the additional denominator factor \(m-1\); the boundary constructions use sums and differences of input rationals. At most \(m\) coalition rankings are needed in either round.

5. Relation to the discrete result

The discrete hardness result concerns a different constraint: eliminating a tied rival requires at least one whole veto. Arbitrarily divisible coalition mass permits a positive surplus to be spread across every rival, making \(p\) unique immediately. At zero surplus, the proof above shows that no choice of first-round winner set remains. This explains why the [discrete NP-completeness theorem](https://mathcs.holycross.edu/~zfitzsim/papers/Fitzsimmons2016_Article_ManipulationComplexityOfSame-S.pdf) does not transfer to this continuous model.
