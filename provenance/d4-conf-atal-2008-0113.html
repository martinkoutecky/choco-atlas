The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(A,B,C\), the cup agenda, a target, and the paper’s target-favourable tie rule, let each type \(t\) have rational mass \(\mu_t\), initial ranking \(r_t\), and a set \(M_t\) of comparisons the campaign may change. Let \(R_t\) be the total rankings that preserve all comparisons outside \(M_t\). Does there exist an allocation \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\), for \(r\in R_t\), with \(\sum_{r\in R_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t\) for every type, such that the resulting population elects the target?

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\) for fixed \(m=3\). The proof uses at most two rational linear-programming feasibility problems and handles the stated tie rules exactly.

1. Feasible allocations and pairwise margins

For each type \(t\), enumerate all \(m!\) strict total rankings and retain precisely those agreeing with \(r_t\) on every pair outside \(M_t\). This constructs \(R_t\) exactly. Since \(m=3\), each \(R_t\) contains at most six rankings. Moreover, \(r_t\in R_t\), so \(R_t\) is nonempty.

Introduce one real variable \(x_{t,r}\) for each \(r\in R_t\), with constraints

\[ x_{t,r}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{r\in R_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t. \]

These constraints describe exactly the permitted allocations: each type’s mass can be divided arbitrarily among its allowed total rankings.

For distinct candidates \(u,v\), define

\[ \sigma_r(u,v)= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{if }r\text{ ranks }u\text{ above }v,\\ -1,&\text{if }r\text{ ranks }v\text{ above }u, \end{cases} \qquad D_{uv}(x)=\sum_t\sum_{r\in R_t}\sigma_r(u,v)x_{t,r}. \]

Every ranking is strict and total, so \(D_{uv}(x)\) is exactly the mass preferring \(u\) to \(v\) minus the mass preferring \(v\) to \(u\).

Consequently:

  • In a match involving \(p\), candidate \(p\) advances exactly when \(D_{pv}(x)\geq 0\).
  • In a match between two candidates other than \(p\), the chair can advance a specified candidate \(u\) exactly when \(D_{uv}(x)\geq 0\).

The first statement uses the mandatory tie rule favouring \(p\). The second uses the chair’s freedom to choose either candidate at equality.

2. Exhaustive analysis of the agenda

A binary cup with three leaves has one preliminary match and one final. There are two cases.

Case I: \(p\) enters directly into the final

Let \(a,b\) be the other candidates; they contest the preliminary match.

For each \(w\in\{a,b\}\), let \(\ell\) be the other candidate. Construct a linear feasibility problem consisting of the allocation constraints and

\[ D_{w\ell}(x)\geq 0,\qquad D_{pw}(x)\geq 0. \]

Accept if at least one of these two systems is feasible.

Necessity. Suppose an allocation and permitted chair choices make \(p\) win. Let \(w\) be the actual preliminary winner. Its advancement implies \(D_{w\ell}(x)\geq 0\): a negative margin would force \(\ell\) to advance. Since \(p\) wins the final against \(w\), we also have \(D_{pw}(x)\geq 0\). Thus the system indexed by \(w\) is feasible.

Sufficiency. Suppose the system indexed by \(w\) is feasible. Its solution is a permitted allocation. If \(D_{w\ell}(x)>0\), then \(w\) wins the preliminary match outright. If \(D_{w\ell}(x)=0\), the chair chooses \(w\); this choice favours an overall victory for \(p\), because the second constraint guarantees that \(p\) wins the final against \(w\). Therefore \(p\) becomes the cup winner.

Case II: \(p\) plays in the preliminary match

Let \(a\) be \(p\)’s preliminary opponent and \(b\) the candidate entering directly into the final.

Construct one linear feasibility problem consisting of the allocation constraints and

\[ D_{pa}(x)\geq 0,\qquad D_{pb}(x)\geq 0. \]

Necessity. To win the cup, \(p\) must win both its preliminary match against \(a\) and its final against \(b\). The two inequalities therefore hold.

Sufficiency. The first inequality makes \(p\) advance from the preliminary match, and the second makes \(p\) win the final. Equality in either match advances \(p\) by the specified tie rule.

These cases exhaust all three-candidate agendas, proving correctness of the algorithm.

3. Exact feasibility and running time

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility of a finite system of non-strict linear inequalities with rational coefficients, over real variables, is decidable in time polynomial in its binary encoding length.

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • All coefficients in the allocation and match constraints belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\).
  • All right-hand sides are input rationals \(\mu_t\) or zero.
  • Every inequality is non-strict.

In particular, exact ties require no numerical tolerance. The theorem decides feasibility over real allocations directly.

Constructing the allowed rankings takes \(O(\tau m!m^2)\) elementary comparison operations, which is \(O(\tau)\) for \(m=3\).

Each feasibility problem has at most \(6\tau\) variables. Expressing every equality as two inequalities and counting nonnegativity constraints gives at most

\[ 6\tau+2\tau+2=8\tau+2 \]

inequalities. A dense binary encoding of one system has length \(O(L+\tau^2)\): its matrix has \(O(\tau^2)\) constant-size coefficients, and the masses contribute \(O(L)\) bits.

Writing \(T_{\mathrm{LP}}(N,H,B)\) for a polynomial bound for rational feasibility with \(N\) variables, \(H\) inequalities, and encoding length \(B\), the running time is bounded by

\[ O(\tau m!m^2) + 2T_{\mathrm{LP}}\!\left(6\tau,8\tau+2,O(L+\tau^2)\right), \qquad m=3. \]

Thus the running time is \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\). Since the input explicitly lists the \(\tau\) types, \(\tau=O(L)\), so this is also \(\operatorname{poly}(L)\).

4. Why the indivisible-vote hardness does not transfer

Walsh’s three-candidate hardness construction uses indivisible weighted votes to enforce a partition of their weights. [Walsh, Section 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0903.1137)

Here, a type’s mass may be divided among several allowed rankings. That division is represented exactly by the continuous variables above. For each possible preliminary winner, the remaining conditions are linear inequalities, so the indivisible-choice obstruction does not apply.

Therefore the stated divisible-mass problem is polynomial-time solvable. It could also be \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
