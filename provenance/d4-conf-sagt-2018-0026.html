The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

For each δ = 1/(2n), let a society be a finite set of buyer and seller valuation types with rational mass distribution μ, total buyer mass and seller mass each 1/2, and every positive type mass a multiple of δ. The society arrives as a uniformly random permutation of packets of mass δ; a packet's role and valuation are revealed on arrival, and an online posted-price policy may trade only when the packet accepts and inventory permits. Does there exist a policy A_δ, independent of μ, such that for every such society, E[W_Aδ(μ)] ≥ (1 - \widetilde{O}(δ^{1/3})) OPT(μ), where OPT assigns the item mass to the highest-valued population mass 1/2?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support: \(\tau\) role–valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\) and nonnegative binary-rational valuations \(v_i\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total encoding length. State running times in \(\tau,L,n\); there is no candidate parameter \(m\).
For \(n\geq1\), let \(\delta=1/(2n)\). Buyer and seller masses each total \(1/2\), and each \(\mu_i\) is an integer multiple of \(\delta\). Replace each type by \(\mu_i/\delta\) packets and present these packets in a uniformly random permutation.
Each seller packet initially owns item mass \(\delta\); buyers initially own none, and the intermediary starts with zero inventory. Each packet can trade its entire mass once, at its arrival, or leave without trading.
The policy knows \(n\), but not the support or masses. It observes the arriving role, then declines or posts a nonnegative price using only previous observations and private randomness; the current valuation is revealed after this decision, whether or not trade occurs.
A seller accepts price \(p\) when \(v_i\leq p\); a buyer accepts when \(v_i\geq p\). Accepted offers execute, except that sales require inventory at least \(\delta\). Inventory can be stored indefinitely; there is no monetary budget or budget-balance constraint. Equal valuations are allowed.
Realized welfare \(W_A\) is the sum of valuation times final item mass held by sellers and buyers; leftover intermediary inventory contributes zero. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\max\{\sum_i v_i x_i:0\leq x_i\leq\mu_i,\ \sum_i x_i=1/2\}\), without arrival-order restrictions.
Does there exist a family of policies \(A_\delta\), independent of \(\mu\), and universal constants \(C>0\), \(k\geq0\), and \(\delta_0>0\), such that every admissible society with \(\delta\leq\delta_0\) satisfies \(\mathbb E[W_{A_\delta}(\mu)]\geq[1-C\delta^{1/3}(\log(1/\delta))^k]\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\), with expectation over arrival order and policy randomness?
For discrete societies with equally many buyers and sellers, a posted-price welfare guarantee of \(1-\widetilde O(n^{-1/3})\) is established; the formal model assumes distinct valuations for convenience. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.11149v2).
No separate theorem for the stated mass-support model was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted valuations to nonnegative binary rationals, making welfare approximation meaningful and supplying a finite valuation encoding.
  • Resolved the revelation-order ambiguity in favor of the discrete posted-price definition: the current valuation is revealed after the offer decision, including when no trade occurs.
  • Specified zero initial intermediary inventory, seller ownership of item mass equal to packet mass, indivisible packet trades, and zero welfare for unsold intermediary inventory.
  • Used weak acceptance inequalities from the formal discrete model and allowed repeated valuations, which packet multiplicities necessarily introduce.
  • Made declining an encounter explicit and imposed no monetary budget or budget-balance constraint.
  • Expanded the soft-O guarantee into universal constants and an asymptotic threshold, with no additive welfare loss.

Result

Yes. There is a family of policies independent of the support and masses satisfying the requested guarantee with \(k=1\).

The proof below treats repeated and zero valuations explicitly. The cited [discrete-market paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.11149v2) establishes the corresponding asymptotic guarantee under distinct valuations; the argument here proves the guarantee directly for the stated model.

1. Exact reduction to packets

Label the \(2n\) packets individually, even when several packets have the same type. Write their valuations as \(v_a\). There are exactly \(n\) seller packets and \(n\) buyer packets.

For the proof, measure item quantities in packets. Let \(P\) be the sum of the \(n\) largest packet valuations. Then

\(P=\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)/\delta.\)

Indeed, the mass allocation problem is equivalent to choosing numbers \(y_a\) satisfying \(0\leq y_a\leq1\) and \(\sum_a y_a=n\), maximizing \(\delta\sum_a v_a y_a\). Sorting valuations decreasingly, the maximum is attained by allocating to the first \(n\) packets. To verify optimality, use the \(n\)-th largest valuation \(c\):

\(\sum_a v_a y_a=nc+\sum_a(v_a-c)y_a\leq nc+\sum_{a:\,v_a>c}(v_a-c)=P.\)

The proposed allocation attains this bound, including when there are ties.

Likewise, if \(Z\) denotes a policy’s realized welfare in packet units, then \(W_A=\delta Z\). Thus it suffices to prove an approximation to \(P\).

2. The policy

All parameters below depend only on \(n\). Set

\(r=\lceil n^{2/3}\rceil,\qquad h=\lceil\log_2(2n)\rceil,\qquad K=1024rh,\qquad D=2^{8h}.\)

We analyze sufficiently large \(n\), so that \(K\leq n/2\). For smaller \(n\), define the policy arbitrarily, for example by declining every trade.

At each arrival, independently draw an integer \(U\), uniformly from \(\{1,\ldots,D\}\), before posting a price. After the valuation \(v\) is revealed, associate the virtual valuation

\(w=v\left(1+\frac{U}{nD}\right).\)

Consequently,

\(v\leq w\leq(1+1/n)v.\)

The policy has two phases.

  • Sampling: the first \(K\) arrivals. Decline every buyer. To a seller, offer the maximum actual valuation of previously observed sellers, taking this maximum to be zero if no seller has been observed. Record every revealed actual and virtual valuation.
  • Trading: the remaining arrivals. Let \(q_{\mathrm{med}}\) be the lower median of the \(K\) sampled virtual valuations, and let \(M\) be the maximum sampled actual valuation. Set the virtual threshold to
  • \(q=q_{\mathrm{med}}\) if \(q_{\mathrm{med}}>0\);
  • \(q=M/(2n^2)\) if \(q_{\mathrm{med}}=0<M\);
  • \(q=0\) if \(M=0\).

At each subsequent arrival, offer the price

\(p=\dfrac{q}{1+U/(nD)}.\)

Execute accepted trades subject to the inventory requirement.

This is an admissible policy. Every price is nonnegative and rational, and uses only previous observations, the current role, and private randomness. In the trading phase, acceptance is exactly equivalent to

\(w\leq q\quad\text{for sellers},\qquad w\geq q\quad\text{for buyers}.\)

In particular, the policy never needs to observe the current valuation before choosing its price.

3. Concentration and sampling losses

We establish the probability estimates used in the proof.

Sampling without replacement

Suppose \(j\) entries are sampled without replacement from a fixed population whose entries lie in an interval of length \(b\). If \(X\) is their sum, then

\(\Pr[X-\mathbb EX\leq-u]\leq\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{u^2}{2jb^2}\right),\)

and the same bound holds for the upper tail.

To justify this, expose the sampled entries sequentially and use the Doob martingale for their eventual sum. After the \(i\)-th exposure, its increment is

\(\dfrac{N-j}{N-i}\left(X_i-\overline X_{i-1}\right),\)

where \(N\) is the population size and \(\overline X_{i-1}\) is the mean of the remaining population before that exposure. Its absolute value is at most \(b\). Azuma’s inequality gives the stated bounds. If \(j=N\), the sum is deterministic.

Virtual ties

We may equivalently assign all packets their independent marks \(U_a\) before drawing the arrival permutation.

For two packets with positive valuations, condition on the mark of one. The equation equating their virtual valuations has at most one possible mark for the other. Hence its probability is at most \(1/D\).

By a union bound, the probability of any positive virtual tie is at most

\(\binom{2n}{2}/D\leq2n^{-6}.\)

Conditional on the marks, order the packets by increasing virtual valuation, breaking zero ties by fixed packet labels. These labels are used only in the analysis.

Uniform concentration of sampled ranks

Let

\(t=4\sqrt{K\log n}.\)

For each prefix of \(H\) packets in the fixed rank order, let \(h_H\) be its number of sampled packets. The sampling bound and a union bound over all \(2n\) prefixes give

\(\Pr\!\left[\exists H:\left|h_H-\frac{KH}{2n}\right|>t\right]\leq4n^{-7}.\)

Call its complement the rank event.

Sample roles and missed purchases

Let \(S_0,B_0\) be the sampled seller and buyer counts. Their expectations are \(K/2\), so

\(\Pr[S_0<K/3\text{ or }B_0<K/3]\leq2e^{-K/72}.\)

Let \(e\) be the number of sampled sellers from whom the policy does not buy. Each such seller is a strict record in the sequence of observed seller valuations. For the \(j\)-th seller, the probability of being a strict record is at most \(1/j\): among the first \(j\) sellers, a unique maximum is equally likely to occupy any position. This argument allows repeated valuations.

Therefore, writing \(H_n=\sum_{j=1}^n1/j\),

\(\mathbb Ee\leq H_n,\qquad \Pr[e>r]\leq H_n/r.\)

The inventory at the end of sampling is exactly \(R=S_0-e\).

4. The inventory reserve serves every eligible remaining buyer

First suppose \(q>0\). Work on the rank event, with \(S_0,B_0\geq K/3\), \(e\leq r\), and no positive virtual ties.

Let

\(H=\#\{a:w_a<q\},\qquad h_q=\#\{\text{sampled }a:w_a<q\}.\)

We claim that \(H\geq n-r\).

If \(q=q_{\mathrm{med}}>0\), distinctness gives \(h_q=K/2-1\). The rank event implies

\(H\geq n-\frac{2n(t+1)}K\geq n-r.\)

If \(q=M/(2n^2)>0\), the sample median is zero, so at least \(K/2\) sampled packets have zero valuation. Applying the rank event to the prefix consisting of all zero packets shows that their total number is at least

\(n-\frac{2nt}K\geq n-r.\)

All these packets lie strictly below \(q\), proving the claim.

The inequalities used here hold for sufficiently large \(n\), because

\(\frac{2nt}K=8n\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{K}}\leq\frac{n^{2/3}}4.\)

Now let \(S,B\) be the numbers of eligible sellers and buyers in the remaining sequence. If every eligible buyer were served, the final inventory would be \(F=R+S-B\).

Let \(E_S\) count remaining sellers whose virtual valuation equals \(q\). Counting packets strictly below \(q\) gives

\(S-B=H-h_q-n+B_0+E_S.\)

Consequently,

\(F=H-n+K-h_q-e+E_S\geq H-n+K-h_q-e.\)

Using the rank event and \(H\geq n-r\),

\(F\geq(H-n)\left(1-\frac K{2n}\right)+\frac K2-t-e\geq\frac K2-t-2r.\)

For sufficiently large \(n\), this is at least \(K/4\). Also,

\(R=S_0-e\geq K/3-r\geq K/4.\)

Thus both endpoints of the inventory path that would result from executing every eligible trade are at least \(K/4\).

The all-zero sample

If \(M=0\), all sampled packets have zero valuation. The rank event implies that the number of positive packets is at most

\(\frac{2nt}K\leq r.\)

The threshold is \(q=0\). All remaining buyers are eligible, while exactly the zero-valued remaining sellers are eligible. Every sampled seller was purchased, so \(R=S_0\). If \(P_S\) is the total number of positive sellers, the hypothetical final inventory is

\(F=B_0-P_S\geq K/3-r\geq K/4.\)

The initial inventory is also at least \(K/4\).

Controlling the entire remaining path

Condition on all marks and the ordered sample. The remaining packets are uniformly permuted.

Give each remaining packet increment \(+1\) if it is an eligible seller, \(-1\) if it is an eligible buyer, and zero otherwise. The expected hypothetical inventory after any number of remaining arrivals is the linear interpolation between \(R\) and \(F\), hence is at least \(K/4\).

The increments lie in an interval of length two. The sampling bound, followed by a union bound over at most \(2n\) remaining positions, shows that the probability the hypothetical inventory ever becomes negative is at most

\(2n\exp\!\left(-\frac{K^2}{256n}\right).\)

If this path never becomes negative, every eligible buyer is served. Indeed, at a buyer step the hypothetical inventory decreases by one; its nonnegativity implies that the preceding inventory was at least one. Induction therefore identifies the hypothetical path with the actual inventory path.

Combining the preceding estimates, all these conclusions hold outside an event of probability at most

\(\beta_n=2n^{-6}+4n^{-7}+2e^{-K/72}+\frac{H_n}{r}+2n\exp\!\left(-\frac{K^2}{256n}\right).\)

In particular,

\(\beta_n=O(n^{-2/3}\log n).\)

5. Welfare on the successful event

Let \(\widetilde P\) be the sum of the \(n\) largest virtual valuations. Let \(T\) be a fixed set attaining this sum, with deterministic tie-breaking, and define

\(Q=\sum_{a\in T:\,a\text{ sampled}}w_a.\)

Let \(\widetilde Z\) be realized welfare in virtual packet units. Pointwise,

\(P\leq\widetilde P\leq(1+1/n)P,\qquad Z\geq\widetilde Z/(1+1/n).\)

We now bound \(\widetilde Z\) on the successful event.

Positive sample median

Here \(h_q=K/2-1\). The rank event gives

\(H+1\leq n+\frac{2nt}K+1\leq n+r.\)

Therefore every one of the top \(n-r\) virtual-valued packets outside the sample lies strictly above \(q\). Sellers among them retain their items, and buyers among them receive items by the inventory result.

For nonnegative numbers, the sum of the largest \(n-r\) is at least \((1-r/n)\) times the sum of the largest \(n\). Thus

\(\widetilde Z\geq(1-r/n)\widetilde P-Q.\)

Zero sample median with a positive observation

Here \(q=M/(2n^2)\). Every packet outside the sample with virtual valuation above \(q\) ends with an item if it belongs to \(T\): sellers retain theirs and buyers are served.

The total virtual value of packets in \(T\) with valuation at most \(q\) is at most

\(nq=M/(2n)\leq P/(2n),\)

because every actual packet valuation, including \(M\), is at most \(P\). Hence

\(\widetilde Z\geq\widetilde P-Q-P/(2n).\)

All-zero sample

There are at most \(r<n\) positive packets. All positive sellers retain their items, and all positive buyers receive items. Therefore

\(\widetilde Z=\widetilde P.\)

All three cases imply the common bound

\(\widetilde Z\geq(1-r/n)\widetilde P-Q-P/n.\)

6. Taking expectations

Conditional on all marks, \(T\) is fixed and the sample is a uniformly random \(K\)-element subset. Therefore,

\(\mathbb E[Q\mid(U_a)_a]=\frac K{2n}\widetilde P.\)

On failure, welfare remains nonnegative. Since \(\widetilde P\leq(1+1/n)P\), the preceding successful-event bound yields

\(\mathbb E\widetilde Z\geq\left(1-\frac rn-\frac K{2n}\right)\mathbb E\widetilde P-\frac Pn-(1+1/n)P\beta_n.\)

For sufficiently large \(n\), the coefficient of \(\mathbb E\widetilde P\) is positive. Using \(\mathbb E\widetilde P\geq P\) and dividing by \(1+1/n\), we obtain

\(\mathbb EZ\geq\left(1-\frac rn-\frac K{2n}-\frac2n-\beta_n\right)P.\)

Now \(r/n=O(n^{-1/3})\), \(K/n=O(n^{-1/3}\log n)\), and \(\beta_n=O(n^{-2/3}\log n)\). Thus, for a universal constant \(C_1\),

\(\mathbb EZ\geq[1-C_1n^{-1/3}\log n]P.\)

Multiplying by \(\delta\) and using \(n=1/(2\delta)\) proves that universal constants \(C,\delta_0>0\) exist such that

\(\mathbb E[W_{A_\delta}(\mu)]\geq[1-C\delta^{1/3}\log(1/\delta)]\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\)

for every admissible society with \(\delta\leq\delta_0\). When the optimum is zero, the inequality follows directly from nonnegative welfare.

7. Running time

The policy uses \(8h=O(\log n)\) random bits per packet. Virtual valuations and posted prices have encoding length \(O(L+\log n)\).

Using elementary exact rational arithmetic and comparison sorting, processing the stream takes

\(O\!\left(n\log n\,(L+\log n)^2\right)\)

bit operations and stores

\(O\!\left(K(L+\log n)\right)\)

bits. It does not need the support as input.

If a supplied support representation is used to generate the packet stream, forming its multiplicities costs \(O(\tau(L+\log n)^2)\), and explicit packet generation costs \(O(n(L+\log n))\). The dependence on \(n\) is unavoidable for explicitly processing \(2n\) arrivals; it need not be polynomial in the compressed support encoding length alone.
