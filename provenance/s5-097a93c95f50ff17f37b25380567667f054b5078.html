The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a bipartite type graph with rational masses \(\mu_t\) and \(\nu_b\) on its two sides, an optimal coupling \(x_{tb}\), and dual potentials, costs incident to one type vertex change. Compute a new optimal coupling and dual potentials, and determine the exact reoptimization complexity in the type counts, edge count, and input bit length \(L\), independently of the expanded population.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=(T,B;E)\) be a fixed simple bipartite graph, with \(r=|T|\), \(q=|B|\), \(\tau=r+q\), and \(m=|E|\). Both sides are nonempty. A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Require \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=\sum_{b\in B}\nu_b=1\).
A feasible coupling has \(x_{tb}\geq0\) on \(E\), row sums \(\mu_t\), and column sums \(\nu_b\); transport outside \(E\) is forbidden. Assume a feasible coupling exists. Costs \(c_{tb}\in\mathbb Q\) may have either sign, and the objective is to minimize \(\sum_{(t,b)\in E}c_{tb}x_{tb}\).
The input includes an optimal rational coupling \(x\) and rational dual potentials \(a_t,b_b\), satisfying \(a_t+b_b\leq c_{tb}\) on every edge, with equality whenever \(x_{tb}>0\). A specified vertex \(s\in T\cup B\) receives arbitrary rational cost changes on its incident edges; all other costs, masses, and edges remain fixed. Return any optimal coupling and dual potentials for the updated costs; ties need no prescribed resolution. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the supplied solutions and updated costs.

Is this exact reoptimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), independently of any expanded population, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(r,q,m,\tau,L\).

For discrete minimum-cost perfect matching on two sides of size \(n\), a single-vertex cost update admits an optimal matching and potential update in \(O(m+n\log n)\) arithmetic operations. [Discrete update result](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.11325v2)
No result specifically analyzing the rational-mass single-vertex reoptimization problem was verified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each side's positive rational masses to total mass one; the two support sizes may differ because aggregation need not preserve equal vertex counts.
  • Required full marginal satisfaction and feasibility, carrying over the perfect-matching requirement; absent edges forbid transport.
  • Specified arbitrary signed binary-rational costs and updates, replacing the discrete real-cost domain with an exact finite encoding.
  • Specified dual potentials through feasibility and complementary slackness, and required the supplied coupling and potentials to be rational.
  • Kept the graph and masses fixed during the update, allowed the updated vertex on either side, and permitted any optimum when ties occur.
  • Defined L to include all input data, including the old optimal solutions and the new costs.

Result and running times

The exact reoptimization problem is polynomial-time solvable, independently of any expanded population. This holds even when costs change on every edge.

A self-contained algorithm below uses, after clearing denominators, \(O((r+q)m^2L)=O(\tau m^2L)\) arithmetic operations. Including exact rational preprocessing and output, a conservative bound is

\(O\!\left(L^3+(r+q)m^2L\bigl(L+\log(r+q)\bigr)\right)\)

bit operations. Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\); NP-hardness under polynomial-time reductions would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof uses the supplied feasible coupling. Its old optimality and the old dual potentials are unnecessary for this bound.

1. Clearing denominators

Write \(c'_{tb}\) for the updated costs.

Let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators appearing in the masses and supplied coupling, and let \(K\) be the product of the positive denominators of the updated costs. Define

\(X^0_{tb}=Dx_{tb},\qquad C_{tb}=Kc'_{tb}.\)

These numbers are integers. Since \(x\) is feasible and the total mass is \(1\), every \(X^0_{tb}\) lies between \(0\) and \(D\).

The sum of the binary lengths of the factors defining either product is at most \(L\). Consequently, \(D\), \(K\), and every \(C_{tb}\) have \(O(L)\) bits. Although \(D\) can have exponentially large numerical value, its binary length is polynomially bounded.

2. Exact reduction to minimum-cost circulation

Construct a directed network on the vertex set \(T\cup B\). For each edge \((t,b)\in E\), introduce two distinct arcs:

| Arc | Capacity | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(t\to b\) | \(D+1\) | \(C_{tb}\) |
| \(b\to t\) | \(X^0_{tb}\) | \(-C_{tb}\) |

There are \(N=\tau\) vertices and \(M=2m\) arcs, including arcs of zero capacity.

A feasible circulation \(y\) assigns nonnegative flow to these arcs, respects their capacities, and has equal inflow and outflow at every vertex. Denote its two flows associated with \((t,b)\) by \(y^+_{tb}\) and \(y^-_{tb}\). Set

\(X_{tb}=X^0_{tb}+y^+_{tb}-y^-_{tb}.\)

Every feasible circulation produces a feasible scaled coupling. Indeed, \(y^-_{tb}\le X^0_{tb}\), so \(X_{tb}\ge0\). Conservation at a top vertex gives

\(\sum_b(y^+_{tb}-y^-_{tb})=0,\)

and conservation at a bottom vertex gives the corresponding column identity. Hence \(X\) has exactly the row and column sums of \(X^0\).

Conversely, every feasible scaled coupling \(X\) produces a feasible circulation. Set

\(y^+_{tb}=\max\{X_{tb}-X^0_{tb},0\},\qquad y^-_{tb}=\max\{X^0_{tb}-X_{tb},0\}.\)

Equality of the marginals implies conservation. The reverse capacity is respected, and \(y^+_{tb}\le X_{tb}\le D<D+1\).

Finally,

\(\sum_{(t,b)\in E}C_{tb}X_{tb} =\sum_{(t,b)\in E}C_{tb}X^0_{tb} +\sum_{(t,b)\in E}C_{tb}(y^+_{tb}-y^-_{tb}).\)

The last sum is exactly the circulation cost. Therefore minimizing circulation cost is equivalent to minimizing the updated transportation objective. Dividing the resulting \(X\) by \(D\) returns an optimal coupling.

3. A polynomial algorithm for the circulation problem

The residual-cycle optimality criterion and capacity scaling are standard; see the [MIT minimum-cost-flow notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/16/Notes/n10-mincostflow.html). The particular algorithm and its correctness are proved here.

3.1 Residual optimality criterion

For an arc \(u\to v\) of capacity \(U_e\), cost \(w_e\), and flow \(f_e\), its residual arcs are:

  • \(u\to v\), of capacity \(U_e-f_e\) and cost \(w_e\), if \(f_e<U_e\);
  • \(v\to u\), of capacity \(f_e\) and cost \(-w_e\), if \(f_e>0\).

Residual arcs retain the identity of their original arc, including when parallel arcs occur.

Lemma. A feasible circulation \(f\) is minimum-cost if and only if its residual graph has no negative-cost directed cycle.

Proof. A negative residual cycle permits a positive augmentation around that cycle, preserving feasibility and strictly decreasing cost.

Conversely, let \(g\) be another feasible circulation. Represent each positive difference \(g_e-f_e\) on the forward residual arc and each negative difference on the reverse residual arc. This gives a nonnegative feasible residual circulation whose cost is \(\operatorname{cost}(g)-\operatorname{cost}(f)\).

Every nonnegative circulation decomposes into directed cycles: follow positive-flow arcs until a vertex repeats, subtract the minimum flow on the resulting cycle, and repeat. Conservation prevents the walk from getting stuck, and each subtraction eliminates at least one positive-flow arc. If every residual cycle has nonnegative cost, the difference in costs is nonnegative. This proves the lemma, also for real-valued competing circulations. ∎

3.2 Increasing one capacity by one

Suppose capacities and the current optimal circulation \(f\) are integral. Increase the capacity of one arc \(e=u\to v\), of cost \(w_e\), by \(1\).

If its forward residual arc already existed, the set of residual arcs does not change. The residual graph still has no negative cycle, so \(f\) remains optimal.

Otherwise, the old flow saturated \(e\), and the increase introduces one new residual arc of capacity \(1\). Let \(R\) denote the old residual graph. Compute a shortest path from \(v\) to \(u\) in \(R\).

Such shortest paths are well-defined whenever the destination is reachable: \(R\) has no negative cycles, and deleting cycles from a walk cannot increase its cost. Thus a shortest path can be chosen simple.

If there is no such path, keep \(f\). Otherwise, let \(d\) be its cost and put \(\gamma=w_e+d\).

  • If \(\gamma\ge0\), keep \(f\).
  • If \(\gamma<0\), augment exactly \(1\) unit around the new arc followed by a shortest path.

The augmentation is feasible because every positive old residual capacity is an integer and therefore at least \(1\). In the negative case, the chosen path cannot simply be the reverse residual arc of \(e\), since that would give cycle cost \(0\).

To prove optimality, compare any feasible circulation \(g\) under the increased capacities with \(f\). Its difference decomposes into cycles in the residual graph before augmentation. Cycles avoiding the new arc have nonnegative cost. Every simple cycle using that arc consists of the arc followed by an old residual path from \(v\) to \(u\), and therefore has cost at least \(\gamma\). The total amount assigned to cycles using the new arc is at most \(1\).

If \(\gamma\ge0\), no improvement is possible. If \(\gamma<0\), every competing circulation satisfies

\(\operatorname{cost}(g)-\operatorname{cost}(f)\ge\gamma.\)

The prescribed augmentation attains equality. If no return path exists, no cycle can use the new arc, so again no improvement is possible.

Thus one shortest-path computation restores optimality after a unit capacity increase.

3.3 Binary capacity scaling

Let

\(h=\left\lceil\log_2(U_{\max}+1)\right\rceil,\)

where \(U_{\max}\) is the largest target capacity. Pad every capacity’s binary representation to \(h\) bits.

Start with all capacities and flows zero. Process the bits from most significant to least significant. At each stage:

  • Double all current capacities and flows.
  • For every arc whose next capacity bit is \(1\), increase its capacity by \(1\), using the procedure above.

Doubling preserves feasibility. It also preserves the residual graph’s arc set and costs, while doubling its positive residual capacities. Hence it preserves the absence of negative cycles and therefore optimality.

Each subsequent unit increase preserves integrality and restores optimality by Section 3.2. Induction proves that, after the last bit, the resulting integral circulation is optimal for the target capacities.

Bellman–Ford computes each required shortest path in \(O(NM)\) arithmetic operations. There are at most \(M\) unit increases per stage and \(h\) stages, giving

\(O(NM^2h)\)

arithmetic operations. For our network, \(U_{\max}=D+1\), so \(h=O(L)\). With \(N=\tau\) and \(M=2m\), this becomes \(O(\tau m^2L)\).

4. Recovering optimal dual potentials

Let \(y\) be the final optimal circulation and let \(X\) be its associated coupling. Its residual graph has no negative cycle.

Add an auxiliary vertex with zero-cost arcs to every vertex and compute shortest-path distances \(p(v)\). All distances are finite. Every residual arc \(u\to v\) of cost \(w\) satisfies

\(w+p(u)-p(v)\ge0.\)

For each transportation edge, the forward original arc remains unsaturated. Indeed,

\(y^+_{tb} =X_{tb}-X^0_{tb}+y^-_{tb} \le X_{tb}\le D<D+1.\)

Thus the residual graph contains \(t\to b\) with cost \(C_{tb}\), giving

\(p(b)-p(t)\le C_{tb}.\)

If \(X_{tb}>0\), then

\(X_{tb}=y^+_{tb}+(X^0_{tb}-y^-_{tb})>0.\)

At least one summand is positive. A positive first summand supplies a reverse residual arc of the forward original arc. A positive second summand supplies unused capacity on the reverse original arc. In either case, the residual graph contains \(b\to t\) with cost \(-C_{tb}\). Therefore

\(p(b)-p(t)\ge C_{tb}.\)

Define the returned rational potentials by

\(a'_t=-p(t)/K,\qquad b'_b=p(b)/K.\)

They satisfy

\(a'_t+b'_b\le c'_{tb}\)

on every edge, with equality whenever the returned coupling \(x'_{tb}=X_{tb}/D\) is positive.

For completeness, these inequalities certify optimality directly. For any feasible coupling \(z\),

\(\sum_{(t,b)\in E}c'_{tb}z_{tb} \ge \sum_{t\in T}a'_t\mu_t+\sum_{b\in B}b'_b\nu_b.\)

For \(x'\), equality holds because every positive entry uses a tight edge. Thus \(x'\) and the returned potentials are primal and dual optimal.

5. Bit complexity and population independence

All circulation capacities and flows have \(O(L)\) bits. All arc costs have \(O(L)\) bits. A shortest path can be chosen simple, so its cost is a sum of at most \(O(\tau)\) arc costs and has \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits. Bellman–Ford can be implemented with labels for walks of bounded length, giving the same bound for intermediate labels.

Consequently, each addition or comparison in the circulation algorithm costs \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bit operations. Denominator clearing and rational output take at most \(O(L^3)\) additional bit operations using elementary integer arithmetic. The final dual computation takes \(O(\tau m)\) arithmetic operations: feasibility and positive masses imply every support vertex is incident to an edge, so \(\tau\le2m\).

This yields the stated overall bound

\(O\!\left(L^3+\tau m^2L(L+\log\tau)\right).\)

The algorithm processes \(O(L)\) capacity bits rather than \(D\) population units. It therefore establishes exact polynomial-time reoptimization without any population expansion. The sharper discrete single-vertex update bound is not needed to settle this complexity question.
