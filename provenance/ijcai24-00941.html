The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), ranking-type set \(T\), rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and finite propositional encoding \(\varphi_A\) of a nontrivial axiom set over variables \(p_{\nu,c}\), decide whether every anonymous population-domain voting rule \(F_\infty:\Delta(T)\to 2^C\setminus\{\varnothing\}\) satisfying \(\varphi_A\) returns the same nonempty outcome \(O\) on \(\mu\); output \(O\) if forced and otherwise report that no outcome is forced.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

Let \(C\) be finite, let \(T\) be a finite set of ranking types over \(C\), and let \(\Delta(T)\) be the rational distributions on \(T\). An anonymous population-domain voting rule is a function \(F_\infty:\Delta(T) o 2^C\setminus\{\varnothing\}\).
Interpret \(p_{\nu,c}\) as \(c\in F_\infty(\nu)\). A finite propositional formula \(\varphi_A\) is nontrivial if some such rule satisfies it. It forces \(O\ne\varnothing\) at \(\mu\) if every rule satisfying \(\varphi_A\) has \(F_\infty(\mu)=O\).

Target

Is deciding whether a nontrivial \(\varphi_A\) forces an outcome at a rational \(\mu\), and returning that outcome when it exists, solvable in polynomial time or NP-hard?

Nearest known results

Exact complexity classification

Theorem

Under the standard explicit encoding of the finite formula, candidates, ranking types, and rational distributions, the problem of deciding whether a promised-nontrivial formula \(\varphi_A\) forces some outcome at \(\mu\) is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.

Its complement—deciding whether two satisfying rules can produce different outcomes at \(\mu\)—is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

The hardness holds even when \(C=\{a,b\}\) and \(T\) consists of two fixed ranking types. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm for deciding and returning the forced outcome would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Finite-footprint lemma

Let \(S\) be the finite set consisting of \(\mu\) and every distribution occurring in an atom of \(\varphi_A\). Introduce all variables

\[ \left\{p_{\nu,c}:\nu\in S,\ c\in C\right\}. \]

For every \(\nu\in S\), define the nonemptiness clause

\[ N_\nu=\bigvee_{c\in C}p_{\nu,c}, \]

and let

\[ G=\varphi_A\land\bigwedge_{\nu\in S}N_\nu. \]

The size of \(G\) is polynomial in the combined input size.

Lemma

The satisfying assignments of \(G\) are exactly the restrictions to \(S\) of rules satisfying \(\varphi_A\).

Proof

If \(F_\infty\) satisfies \(\varphi_A\), assign \(p_{\nu,c}\) the value true exactly when \(c\in F_\infty(\nu)\). Since every outcome is nonempty, every \(N_\nu\) is true, so the resulting assignment satisfies \(G\).

Conversely, let \(v\) satisfy \(G\). For \(\nu\in S\), define

\[ F_v(\nu)=\{c\in C:v(p_{\nu,c})=\mathrm{true}\}. \]

Every such set is nonempty because \(v\models N_\nu\). Fix an arbitrary \(c_0\in C\), and for every \(\nu\notin S\) put \(F_v(\nu)=\{c_0\}\). This defines a rule on all of \(\Delta(T)\), and its interpretation of every atom in \(\varphi_A\) agrees with \(v\). Hence it satisfies \(\varphi_A\). \(\square\)

Thus the infinitude of \(\Delta(T)\) introduces no additional constraint: only finitely many population points matter.

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{coNP}\)

Take two disjoint copies \(X\) and \(Y\) of the variables of \(G\), and form

\[ H=G(X)\land G(Y)\land \bigvee_{c\in C}\left(p^{X}_{\mu,c}\oplus p^{Y}_{\mu,c}\right). \]

Because \(\varphi_A\) is nontrivial, \(G\) is satisfiable.

By the finite-footprint lemma, \(H\) is satisfiable exactly when there are two rules satisfying \(\varphi_A\) whose outcomes at \(\mu\) differ. Therefore,

\[ \varphi_A\text{ does not force an outcome at }\mu \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\text{ is satisfiable}. \]

A satisfying assignment of \(H\) is a polynomial-size certificate. Hence non-forcing belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), and forcing belongs to \(\mathrm{coNP}\).

3. \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hardness

We reduce from \(\mathrm{UNSAT}\). Let \(\psi(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) be a propositional formula.

Fix \(C=\{a,b\}\) and two ranking types \(T=\{t_0,t_1\}\). Let

\[ \mu(t_0)=0,\qquad \mu(t_1)=1. \]

For every \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), define the distinct rational distribution

\[ \nu_i(t_0)=\frac{i}{n+1}, \qquad \nu_i(t_1)=1-\frac{i}{n+1}. \]

These distributions have polynomial-size binary encodings. Let \(\psi^\star\) be obtained from \(\psi\) by replacing every \(x_i\) with \(p_{\nu_i,a}\). Define

\[ \varphi_\psi = p_{\mu,b}\land \left(\neg p_{\mu,a}\lor\psi^\star\right). \]

Nontriviality

The rule that chooses \(\{b\}\) at every distribution satisfies \(\varphi_\psi\), because \(p_{\mu,b}\) is true and \(p_{\mu,a}\) is false. Thus every produced instance satisfies the promised nontriviality condition.

If \(\psi\) is unsatisfiable

Suppose \(F_\infty\) satisfies \(\varphi_\psi\). We necessarily have \(b\in F_\infty(\mu)\).

If \(a\in F_\infty(\mu)\), then \(p_{\mu,a}\) is true, so \(\psi^\star\) must be true. But the values of \(p_{\nu_i,a}\) would then give a satisfying assignment of \(\psi\), contradicting unsatisfiability.

Thus \(a\notin F_\infty(\mu)\). Since \(C=\{a,b\}\), every satisfying rule has

\[ F_\infty(\mu)=\{b\}. \]

Hence \(\varphi_\psi\) forces \(\{b\}\).

If \(\psi\) is satisfiable

The constant rule choosing \(\{b\}\) everywhere satisfies \(\varphi_\psi\).

Let \(\sigma\) be a satisfying assignment of \(\psi\). Define another rule by choosing

\[ F_\infty(\mu)=\{a,b\}, \]

and, for each \(i\),

\[ F_\infty(\nu_i)= \begin{cases} \{a\},&\text{if }\sigma(x_i)=\mathrm{true},\\ \{b\},&\text{if }\sigma(x_i)=\mathrm{false}. \end{cases} \]

Choose \(\{b\}\) elsewhere. Under this rule, both \(p_{\mu,a}\) and \(p_{\mu,b}\) are true, while \(\psi^\star\) is true by the choice of \(\sigma\). Hence this rule also satisfies \(\varphi_\psi\), but its outcome at \(\mu\) is \(\{a,b\}\), different from \(\{b\}\).

Therefore \(\varphi_\psi\) does not force an outcome.

We have proved

\[ \psi\in\mathrm{UNSAT} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \varphi_\psi\text{ forces an outcome at }\mu. \]

This is a polynomial-time, promise-preserving reduction, proving \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hardness. Together with membership, the forcing problem is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.

4. Returning the outcome

The outcome can be returned with a polynomial number of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-oracle queries:

  • Test satisfiability of \(H\). If \(H\) is satisfiable, report that no outcome is forced.
  • Otherwise, for every \(c\in C\), test whether \(G\land p_{\mu,c}\) is satisfiable.
  • Return

\[ O=\{c\in C:G\land p_{\mu,c}\text{ is satisfiable}\}. \]

When \(H\) is unsatisfiable, all satisfying assignments of \(G\) agree at \(\mu\), so this procedure returns exactly the forced outcome. Thus the outcome-returning problem belongs to \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\), and its decision component is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.

5. Interpretation of “NP-hard”

Under standard polynomial-time many-one reductions, the precise classification of the forcing language is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete. Proving that this same language is also \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard would imply \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

Nevertheless, the combined decide-and-return task is at least as hard as solving \(\mathrm{SAT}\): applying it to \(\varphi_\psi\) distinguishes satisfiable from unsatisfiable \(\psi\). In particular, it cannot be polynomial-time solvable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Verification report

Overall assessment

The central classification is correct under the stated explicit encoding and the promise that \(\varphi_A\) is nontrivial. The finite-footprint argument, membership proof, hardness reduction, and outcome-recovery procedure are logically sound.

The only issues are minor formalization gaps concerning promise-problem terminology and representation details. No mathematical error affects the conclusion.

Step-by-step audit

1. Finite-footprint lemma

The construction includes \(\mu\) and every distribution mentioned by \(\varphi_A\), and adds one nonemptiness clause for every relevant distribution. This correctly captures all restrictions imposed by the definition of a voting rule.

The extension outside \(S\) is valid because an anonymous population-domain rule is otherwise an arbitrary function on \(\Delta(T)\); the task imposes no consistency, continuity, neutrality, or other cross-distribution conditions.

Nontriviality guarantees that \(C\) is nonempty, so choosing \(c_0\in C\) is legitimate.

A minor encoding detail is omitted: if the same rational distribution appears in different syntactic representations, the construction must identify those occurrences as the same point of \(\Delta(T)\). Under ordinary binary rational encodings, equality and normalization are polynomial-time operations, so this does not affect the result.

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{coNP}\)

The formula \(H\) is satisfiable exactly when two satisfying restrictions give different subsets of \(C\) at \(\mu\). The disjunction of XOR terms correctly expresses inequality of the two outcome sets.

Because \(G\) is satisfiable under the nontriviality promise, unsatisfiability of \(H\) means that all satisfying rules agree at \(\mu\), rather than agreement holding vacuously.

The XOR notation is only an abbreviation and should formally be expanded into the chosen propositional basis. This is a routine linear-size expansion.

Thus non-forcing has polynomial certificates and forcing is in \(\mathrm{coNP}\), on the promised input domain.

3. Hardness reduction

All constructed distributions belong to \(\Delta(T)\). The points \(\nu_i\) are mutually distinct and distinct from \(\mu\), and their total binary encoding length is polynomial.

The produced formula is always nontrivial: the constant rule with outcome \(\{b\}\) satisfies it independently of \(\psi\).

If \(\psi\) is unsatisfiable, setting \(p_{\mu,a}\) to true would force \(\psi^\star\), whose atom values would constitute a satisfying Boolean assignment for \(\psi\). Hence every satisfying rule has outcome exactly \(\{b\}\) at \(\mu\).

If \(\psi\) is satisfiable, the two displayed rules are valid, satisfy the formula, and have different outcomes at \(\mu\). The construction therefore proves the stated equivalence with \(\mathrm{UNSAT}\).

The reduction is polynomial and promise-preserving. It establishes \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hardness of forcing and, equivalently through the same construction, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of non-forcing.

4. Returning the outcome

When \(H\) is unsatisfiable, all satisfying assignments of \(G\) agree on every \(p_{\mu,c}\). Therefore \(G\land p_{\mu,c}\) is satisfiable exactly for candidates belonging to the common outcome.

The procedure uses \(1+|C|\) SAT queries and polynomial additional work, so the claimed \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\) upper bound is valid. Nonemptiness of the returned set follows from satisfiability of \(G\) and the clause \(N_\mu\).

The prover does not claim a matching functional completeness result, and none is required by the target.

5. Interpretation of hardness

The distinction between \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness of forcing and \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of non-forcing is correct. Claiming ordinary many-one \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the forcing language itself would imply \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

A polynomial-time decision-and-return algorithm would decide both satisfiability and unsatisfiability through the reduction, so the conclusion that such an algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\) is justified.

Required clarifications

The write-up should formally specify whether it is describing:

  • a promise problem whose legal instances have nontrivial \(\varphi_A\), or
  • a total language together with a stated convention for trivial formulas.

Ordinary terms such as “\(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete” and “complement” apply directly to languages; for a promise problem, the corresponding promise-class terminology or an explicit totalization should be given. This is a formal presentation issue and does not alter the reduction or classification on promised instances.

It should also state that rational distributions denoting the same mathematical point are canonically identified when constructing \(S\).
