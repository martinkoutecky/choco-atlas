Theorem 22 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two continuous populations of total mass \(1\), each represented by finitely many types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), and shared rankings over mutually acceptable opposite-side types with ties only in the final rank block, find a maximum-mass stable coupling. A coupling is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses; for each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), stability requires at least one of \(b_i(j)=\mu_i-\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}\) and \(b_j(i)=\nu_j-\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}x_{\ell j}\) to be zero. Determine the exact, approximation, and parameterized complexity of maximizing \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\) as a function of the type supports and input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Two populations have type sets \(U\) and \(V\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), respectively, satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1\). Each society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass and their masses; \(\tau=|U|+|V|\) is the total support size, not the number of possible types.
Mutual acceptability is specified by \(A\subseteq U\times V\). All members of a type share a weak ordering of its acceptable opposite-side types; each ordering is strict except for an optional final tie block, on either side. Every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched; empty lists are permitted, and ties are not broken.
A coupling has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(A\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\mu_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\nu_j\).
Write \(k\succeq_i j\) when type \(i\) weakly prefers \(k\) to \(j\), and define \(b_i(j)=\mu_i-\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}\) and \(b_j(i)=\nu_j-\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}x_{\ell j}\), summing only over acceptable types. Stability means \(b_i(j)=0\) or \(b_j(i)=0\) for every \((i,j)\in A\).
The objective is maximum matched mass \(\sum_{(i,j)\in A}x_{ij}\). Let \(m=|A|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length; polynomial running time means polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Does approximation within every fixed factor \(1\leq\alpha<33/29\) remain NP-hard for this continuous problem, or does a polynomial-time algorithm exist for some such factor?
Under the unique games conjecture, does the analogous hardness hold for every fixed \(1\leq\alpha<4/3\), or is some such factor achievable in polynomial time?

For finite agents with ties only at the ends of lists on both sides, maximum-cardinality weakly stable matching is NP-hard to approximate below \(33/29\), and UG-hard below \(4/3\). [Huang et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2015/5312/pdf/22.pdf)
For unit-capacity fractional matching with the same ordinal stability condition, maximum welfare and maximum number of fully matched agents are polynomial-time solvable on bipartite graphs with strict preferences; these results do not cover arbitrary rational type masses with final ties. [Chen, Roy, and Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)

Result

Both hardness bounds remain valid. The continuous problem is NP-hard to approximate within every fixed factor \(1\leq\alpha<33/29\), and, under the unique games conjecture, within every fixed factor \(1\leq\alpha<4/3\).

The reduction uses only equal dyadic masses. Its essential ingredient is a rounding lemma that preserves stability and does not decrease the scaled objective.

1. A stability-preserving rounding lemma

Consider a finite bipartite acceptability graph \(A\subseteq U\times V\), with arbitrary weak preference orderings. Let \(y\) be a unit-capacity fractional matching:

\(y_{ij}\geq0,\qquad \sum_jy_{ij}\leq1,\qquad \sum_iy_{ij}\leq1.\)

Suppose that, for every \((i,j)\in A\),

\(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}y_{ik}=1\quad\text{or}\quad\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}y_{\ell j}=1.\)

Lemma. There exists a weakly stable integral matching \(M\) satisfying

\(|M|\geq\sum_{(i,j)\in A}y_{ij}.\)

When \(y\) is rationally represented, such a matching can be found in polynomial time.

Proof

Define the positive-support edges and saturated vertices by

\(E^+=\{(i,j)\in A:y_{ij}>0\},\)

\(S_U=\{i\in U:\sum_jy_{ij}=1\},\qquad S_V=\{j\in V:\sum_iy_{ij}=1\}.\)

We first find an integral matching using only \(E^+\) and covering every vertex in \(S_U\cup S_V\).

Construct a directed circulation network with vertices \(s,t,U,V\) and these arcs:

  • \(s\to i\), for each \(i\in U\), with upper capacity \(1\) and lower capacity \(1\) if \(i\in S_U\), otherwise \(0\);
  • \(i\to j\), for each \((i,j)\in E^+\), with lower capacity \(0\) and upper capacity \(1\);
  • \(j\to t\), for each \(j\in V\), with upper capacity \(1\) and lower capacity \(1\) if \(j\in S_V\), otherwise \(0\);
  • \(t\to s\), with lower capacity \(0\) and upper capacity \(\min\{|U|,|V|\}\).

The fractional matching \(y\) gives a feasible circulation: put \(y_{ij}\) on \(i\to j\), its row and column sums on the incident source and sink arcs, and \(\sum_{ij}y_{ij}\) on \(t\to s\).

Maximize the flow on \(t\to s\). The integral-circulation theorem applies: the node–arc incidence matrix is totally unimodular, and all lower and upper capacities here are integers. Thus the nonempty, bounded circulation polytope has an integral optimum. Maximum-cost circulation can also be solved in polynomial time.

In an integral optimum, the arcs \(i\to j\) carrying \(1\) form a matching \(M\). The source and sink capacities ensure that each vertex is incident to at most one selected edge. The lower capacities ensure that every vertex in \(S_U\cup S_V\) is covered. Its cardinality equals the flow on \(t\to s\), so feasibility of the circulation obtained from \(y\) gives

\(|M|\geq\sum_{ij}y_{ij}.\)

It remains to prove stability. Fix any acceptable pair \((i,j)\). Suppose first that its stability witness is

\(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}y_{ik}=1.\)

Because the total row sum is at most \(1\), nonnegativity implies both that \(i\in S_U\) and that \(y_{ik}=0\) for every acceptable \(k\) strictly worse than \(j\). Therefore \(M\) matches \(i\), and its partner, being connected by an edge in \(E^+\), is weakly preferred to \(j\). Hence \(i\) does not strictly prefer \(j\) to its partner.

If instead the column equality witnesses stability, the same argument shows that \(j\) is matched to a partner weakly preferred to \(i\).

Thus every acceptable pair has an endpoint that would not strictly improve by deviating. No pair weakly blocks \(M\).

The network has polynomial size. For rational \(y\), identifying its positive entries and saturated vertices requires only polynomial-time exact rational arithmetic. This proves the algorithmic assertion. ∎

The lemma does not require strict preferences or restrict where ties occur.

2. A reduction with equal dyadic masses

Let \(I\) be a finite maximum-cardinality weakly stable matching instance with \(n\geq1\) agents on each side and ties only at the ends of lists. Write \(C(I)\) for its maximum stable matching cardinality.

Let \(N\) be the least power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Add \(N-n\) isolated agents to each side, with empty preference lists. This preserves \(C(I)\), since isolated agents cannot participate in any matching or blocking pair.

Make every padded agent a separate type and assign every type mass \(1/N\). Retain all original acceptable pairs and preferences. Denote the resulting continuous instance by \(J\).

All masses are positive dyadic rationals, and each population has total mass \(1\). The preference restriction is preserved. Moreover,

\(\tau=2N<4n,\)

the number of acceptable pairs is unchanged, and each mass has binary encoding length \(O(\log n)\). Consequently, the construction has polynomial input length and running time.

3. Exact correspondence of optimum values

We prove

\(\operatorname{OPT}(J)=C(I)/N.\)

First inequality. Let \(M\) be any finite weakly stable matching in the padded instance. Set \(x_{ij}=1/N\) on its edges and \(x_{ij}=0\) elsewhere.

For every acceptable pair, finite weak stability means that at least one endpoint is matched to a partner weakly preferred to the other endpoint. At that endpoint the relevant weak-prefix sum is \(1/N\), so the corresponding deficit is zero. Thus \(x\) is stable under the stated continuous definition.

Taking a maximum finite stable matching yields

\(\operatorname{OPT}(J)\geq C(I)/N.\)

Reverse inequality. Let \(x\) be any stable coupling for \(J\), and put \(y_{ij}=Nx_{ij}\). Its row and column sums are at most \(1\). The deficit condition becomes precisely

\(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}y_{ik}=1\quad\text{or}\quad\sum_{\ell\succeq_j i}y_{\ell j}=1.\)

The rounding lemma therefore produces a finite weakly stable matching \(M\) satisfying

\(|M|\geq\sum_{ij}y_{ij}=N\sum_{ij}x_{ij}.\)

The added isolated vertices cannot be matched, so \(M\) is also a matching of the original instance. Hence

\(C(I)\geq N\sum_{ij}x_{ij}.\)

This holds for every stable coupling, proving the reverse inequality and the claimed optimum identity.

4. Transfer of approximation hardness

For this maximization problem, an \(\alpha\)-approximation must return a feasible stable coupling \(x\) with

\(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\geq\operatorname{OPT}(J)/\alpha.\)

Suppose such an algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Apply it to the constructed instance \(J\), scale its output by \(N\), and apply the rounding lemma. The resulting finite weakly stable matching satisfies

\(|M|\geq N\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\geq N\operatorname{OPT}(J)/\alpha=C(I)/\alpha.\)

This is a polynomial-time \(\alpha\)-approximation for the finite problem. In the usual binary computational model, a polynomial-time algorithm has polynomial output length, so the rational-output rounding step also takes polynomial time.

Theorem 22 of [Huang, Iwama, Miyazaki, and Yanagisawa](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2015/5312/pdf/22.pdf) states that the finite problem with final ties on both sides is NP-hard to approximate below \(33/29\), and UG-hard below \(4/3\). Its hypotheses hold for the source instances used here. The approximation-preserving reduction therefore establishes both requested hardness statements.

The rounding algorithm requires an already stable fractional solution; it does not find an optimal one. Thus it provides no polynomial-time optimization algorithm that could circumvent these bounds.
