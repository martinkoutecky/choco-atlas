The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), target \(c\in C\), deletion budget \(k\), and rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\), does there exist \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{c\)\) with \(|D|\le k\) such that \(c\) is the unique Kemeny winner after restricting all rankings to \(C\setminus D\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(m=|C|\ge 2\), a distinguished candidate \(c\in C\), and a binary integer budget \(1\le k<m\), consider constructive control by deleting candidates.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(C\), each with positive binary rational mass, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
A legal action deletes an indivisible set \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{c\}\) with \(|D|\le k\). Restrict each ranking to \(S=C\setminus D\), preserving its mass and combining identical restricted rankings.
Use the mass-weighted Kemeny rule: optimal rankings minimize expected Kendall tau distance to the restricted society. Its winners are the candidates appearing first in some optimal ranking; thus \(c\) is the unique winner exactly when every optimal ranking starts with \(c\). Apply no tie-breaking.

Is deciding whether some legal deletion makes \(c\) the unique Kemeny winner solvable in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions?

For explicitly listed finite unweighted electorates, constructive control by deleting candidates is \(\Theta_2^p\)-hard; testing whether a candidate is the unique Kemeny winner is \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The budget domain was unspecified: use the stated discrete control domain, a positive integer k smaller than the number of candidates, encoded in binary; consequently m is at least two.
  • Interpret the rational distribution as positive support masses summing to one, with strict complete rankings as types.
  • Extend Kemeny to masses by minimizing expected Kendall tau distance, retaining every optimal ranking and using no tie-breaking.
  • Candidate deletion preserves voter mass; identical rankings created by restriction have their masses combined.
  • Define L as the total input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Proven result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. In fact, it is \(\Theta_2^p\)-hard even when \(k=1\).

The proof uses the unique-Kemeny-winner hardness theorem supplied in the question. The unique-winner hardness is also recorded in [*Bypassing Combinatorial Protections*](https://pub.dss.in.tum.de/brandt-research/sp2_journal.pdf). No assumption about the winner convention of the supplied control-hardness theorem is needed.

1. Normalizing and grouping votes preserves optimal rankings

Let an explicitly listed unweighted electorate consist of \(N\geq1\) rankings \(v_1,\ldots,v_N\) of a candidate set \(A\). Let its distinct rankings be \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_\tau\), with multiplicities \(n_1,\ldots,n_\tau\).

Assign \(\rho_i\) mass \(\mu_i=n_i/N\). These masses are positive binary rationals and satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\).

For any surviving set \(S\subseteq A\) and any ranking \(\pi\) of \(S\), its expected Kendall distance is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,\rho_i|_S) = \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\pi,v_j|_S). \]

Thus normalization multiplies every objective value by the same positive factor \(1/N\). Consequently, it preserves exactly the optimal rankings and their possible first candidates. Combining identical restricted rankings also preserves this sum, by adding their masses.

2. A universally first candidate forces the deletion

Lemma. Suppose a candidate \(d\) is first in every ranking of a society of total mass \(1\). For every surviving candidate set containing \(d\), every optimal Kemeny ranking starts with \(d\).

Proof. Consider a ranking \(\pi\) in which \(t\geq1\) candidates precede \(d\). Move \(d\) to first position while preserving the relative order of all other candidates.

Only the \(t\) pairs involving \(d\) and these preceding candidates change. Every support ranking prefers \(d\) in each such pair. Therefore, the move reduces the Kendall distance to every support ranking by exactly \(t\), and reduces expected distance by \(t\sum_i\mu_i=t>0\).

Hence \(\pi\) cannot be optimal. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial-time many-one reduction

Take an instance \((A,v_1,\ldots,v_N,c)\) of unique-Kemeny-winner testing for explicitly listed unweighted electorates. Write \(n=|A|\).

Construct the target instance as follows:

  • Introduce a fresh candidate \(d\), and set \(C=A\cup\{d\}\).
  • Put \(d\) first in every original ranking.
  • Group repeated rankings and assign each resulting ranking its multiplicity divided by \(N\).
  • Keep \(c\) distinguished and set \(k=1\).

The output satisfies \(m=n+1\geq2\) and \(1=k<m\).

If a legal deletion retains \(d\), the lemma implies that every optimal ranking starts with \(d\). Since \(c\neq d\), that deletion cannot succeed.

A successful deletion must therefore contain \(d\). Because the budget is \(1\), its deletion set must be exactly \(\{d\}\). After this deletion, the society is precisely the normalized original electorate. Section 1 then gives

\[ \text{the constructed instance admits a successful deletion} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c\text{ is the unique Kemeny winner of the original electorate}. \]

This establishes both directions of the reduction, including all ties.

For completeness, allowing empty electorates in the source convention causes no difficulty: their unique-winner question is immediately decidable, so they can be mapped directly to fixed valid yes- or no-instances.

Encoding size and construction time. The output support has \(\tau\leq N\) distinct rankings. Each mass \(n_i/N\) requires \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits, including numerator and denominator. With candidates encoded by integer identifiers, the output length is

\[ L=O\!\left(\tau m\log(m+1)+\tau\log(N+1)+\log(m+1)\right). \]

Grouping can be performed by sorting the explicitly listed rankings. Comparisons inspect at most \(m\) candidate identifiers; counting multiplicities and reducing the fractions also take polynomial time. Therefore the construction is polynomial in the source input length.

By the supplied \(\Theta_2^p\)-hardness of unique-winner testing, the target problem is \(\Theta_2^p\)-hard. Since \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\Theta_2^p\), it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm would consequently imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

4. An exact algorithm and its running time

The following upper bound makes the dependence on \(m,\tau,L,k\) explicit.

Write the input masses as \(\mu_i=a_i/b_i\). Set \(Q=\prod_i b_i\) and \(A_i=a_iQ/b_i\). Then \(\sum_iA_i=Q\), and these integers have \(O(L)\) bits.

For distinct candidates \(x,y\), define

\[ w_{xy}=\sum_{\{i:\,y\text{ precedes }x\text{ in }\rho_i\}}A_i. \]

For any ranking, its expected Kendall distance multiplied by \(Q\) equals the sum of \(w_{xy}\) over pairs for which it places \(x\) before \(y\). Restriction to a surviving set preserves these pairwise weights.

For every \(T\subseteq C\), let \(F(T)\) be the minimum scaled cost of a ranking of \(T\). The recurrence is

\[ F(\varnothing)=0,\qquad F(T)=\min_{x\in T}\left(F(T\setminus\{x\})+ \sum_{y\in T\setminus\{x\}}w_{xy}\right). \]

Indeed, fixing \(x\) first fixes every pair involving \(x\), and leaves an independent optimum on \(T\setminus\{x\}\).

Precompute \(H_x(U)=\sum_{y\in U\setminus\{x\}}w_{xy}\) by subset recurrences. This requires \(O(m2^m)\) integer additions. The recurrence for \(F\) then requires \(O(m2^m)\) additions and comparisons.

For a surviving set \(S\) and \(x\in S\), define

\[ G_x(S)=F(S\setminus\{x\})+H_x(S\setminus\{x\}). \]

This is exactly the minimum cost among rankings starting with \(x\). Therefore \(c\) is the unique winner precisely when

\[ G_c(S)<G_x(S)\qquad\text{for every }x\in S\setminus\{c\}. \]

Check this condition for every legal deletion set. Their number is

\[ B(m,k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{m-1}{j}. \]

All costs are at most \(Q\binom m2\), so they use \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) bits. A conservative bit-operation bound, including rational preprocessing, is

\[ O\!\left( \bigl(\tau m^2+m2^m+mB(m,k)\bigr) \bigl(L+\log(m+1)\bigr)^2 \right). \]

Thus the problem has an exact algorithm with polynomial dependence on the support size and rational encoding length, but the hardness already holds for the constant budget \(k=1\).
