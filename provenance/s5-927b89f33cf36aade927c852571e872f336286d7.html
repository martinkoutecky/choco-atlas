The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite school set \(S\), complete-ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational capacity masses \(q_s\ge 0\) with \(\sum_sq_s=1\), compute the limiting normalized type-school assignment matrix \(y\) generated by common-lottery RP in all valid replications with \(n\mu_t\) students and \(nq_s\) seats, prove \(Y_n^{\mathrm{RP}}\to y\) in probability, and determine whether independent-school-lottery TTC/PRP has the same limit.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools, with indifferent schools and students demanding exactly one seat. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) strict complete-ranking types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Assume \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Capacities are binary rationals \(q_s\geq0\) with \(\sum_s q_s=1\); zero-capacity schools are unavailable. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, and state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A valid replication has \(n\mu_t\) labeled students of each type and \(nq_s\) seats at each school, all integers. Common-lottery RP uses a uniformly random student ordering and serial dictatorship. Independent-school-lottery PRP draws a uniform student ordering independently at each school and applies priority-based TTC: remaining students point to their favorite nonfull school, each nonfull school points to its highest-priority remaining student, and every directed cycle assigns its students to the schools they point to, removes those students, and decrements those schools’ capacities; repeat.
For either mechanism \(M\), define the realized normalized matrix by \(Y^M_{n,ts}=n^{-1}\lvert\{\text{type-}t\text{ students assigned to }s\}\rvert\). Hold the input fixed as \(n\) tends to infinity through all valid replications.

Does there exist a deterministic matrix \(y\) with \(Y_n^{\mathrm{RP}}\to y\) in probability, and does \(Y_n^{\mathrm{PRP}}\to y\) in probability as well? Is exact computation of \(y\) polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

Discrete: common and independent school lotteries produce identical distributions over complete assignments under TTC ([Pathak–Sethuraman](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/150145/1/v06-i1-p001-TE816.pdf)).

Thus the supplied limit and lottery-equivalence questions are already covered by prior work.

Result

Both mechanisms converge in probability to the same deterministic matrix $y$, and $y$ is exactly computable in polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$. Specifically, an exact implementation uses
$$ O(\tau m^4L^2) $$
bit operations.

The matrix is the aggregate probabilistic-serial assignment, so its row sums are $\mu_t$ and its column sums are $q_s$.

The convergence proof below repairs the gap identified in the verifier’s report: it proves concentration of the full-population exhaustion cutoffs directly, without invoking concentration of different, hypothetical cutoffs.

1. Construction of the deterministic assignment

Let
$$ S^+=\{s\in S:q_s>0\}. $$
Delete zero-capacity schools from the rankings, retaining zero columns for them in the output. The set $S^+$ is nonempty. Restricted rankings remain strict and complete; different listed types may have identical restricted rankings, which causes no difficulty.

For a nonempty available set $A\subseteq S^+$, let $f_t(A)$ be type $t$’s favorite school in $A$.

Initially set residual capacities $r_s=q_s$, assignments $y_{ts}=0$, and elapsed time $T=0$. At each stage, set
$$ A=\{s:r_s>0\}, \qquad d_s=\sum_{t:f_t(A)=s}\mu_t \quad(s\in A). $$
Because each type has exactly one favorite,
$$ \sum_{s\in A}d_s=1. $$
Define the stage duration
$$ \delta=\min_{s\in A:\,d_s>0}\frac{r_s}{d_s}. $$
Advance time by $\delta$ and update simultaneously:
$$ r_s\leftarrow r_s-d_s\delta, \qquad y_{t,f_t(A)}\leftarrow y_{t,f_t(A)}+\mu_t\delta. $$
Remove every school with zero residual capacity, including all tied schools.

The minimum exists and is positive. Its definition prevents negative residual capacities and exhausts at least one school. Thus there are at most $|S^+|\leq m$ stages.

The invariant
$$ \sum_s r_s=1-T $$
holds because aggregate consumption has rate $1$. Also,
$$ \delta \leq \sum_{s:d_s>0}d_s\frac{r_s}{d_s} = \sum_{s:d_s>0}r_s \leq 1-T. $$
Here the first inequality compares a minimum with a weighted average whose weights sum to $1$. Hence time never exceeds $1$. At termination all residual capacities vanish, so $T=1$.

Consequently,
$$ \sum_s y_{ts}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_t y_{ts}=q_s. $$
This constructs probabilistic serial exactly. Denote each positive-capacity school’s exhaustion time by $T_s$.

2. A uniqueness lemma for exhaustion cutoffs

This lemma supplies the missing convergence argument.

For $c\in[0,1]^{S^+}$, define
$$ a_{ts}(c)= \max\bigl(\{c_r:r\in S^+,\ r\succ_t s\}\cup\{0\}\bigr), $$
where $r\succ_t s$ means that type $t$ prefers $r$ to $s$. Put
$$ \Phi_{ts}(c)=\mu_t\bigl(c_s-a_{ts}(c)\bigr)_+, \qquad G_s(c)=\sum_t\Phi_{ts}(c), $$
with $x_+=\max\{x,0\}$.

Lemma. The equations
$$ G_s(c)=q_s \qquad(s\in S^+) $$
have exactly one solution in $[0,1]^{S^+}$, namely $c=(T_s)_{s\in S^+}$.

Proof

First interpret an arbitrary vector $c$ as a prescribed removal schedule. At time $u$, declare the available schools to be
$$ A_c(u)=\{s:u<c_s\}. $$
Each type consumes its favorite school in this set at aggregate speed $\mu_t$. If the set is empty, consumption stops. Let $g_s^c(u)$ be the resulting aggregate consumption rate at school $s$.

A type consumes $s$, except possibly at finitely many endpoints, precisely during
$$ a_{ts}(c)<u<c_s. $$
Therefore
$$ G_s(c)=\int_0^{c_s}g_s^c(u)\,du. $$

Suppose now that $G_s(c)=q_s$ for every school. Since $q_s>0$, necessarily $c_s>0$.

For fixed $s$, the function $g_s^c$ is nondecreasing on $[0,c_s)$. Indeed, once a type chooses $s$, all schools it prefers to $s$ have been removed; they never return, and $s$ remains available until $c_s$. Thus that type continues choosing $s$.

Define residual capacities under this schedule by
$$ \rho_s(u)=q_s-\int_0^u g_s^c(v)\,dv. $$
For $u<c_s$, the capacity equation gives
$$ \rho_s(u)=\int_u^{c_s}g_s^c(v)\,dv>0. $$
To justify strict positivity, the positive integral $q_s$ implies that $g_s^c(v_0)>0$ for some $v_0<c_s$. Monotonicity then gives a positive lower bound on the interval from $\max\{u,v_0\}$ to $c_s$, which has positive length. For $u\geq c_s$, the school has received its entire capacity and consumes nothing further, so $\rho_s(u)=0$.

It follows that, at every time,
$$ A_c(u)=\{s:\rho_s(u)>0\}. $$
Thus this prescribed schedule always has each type consuming its favorite school with positive residual capacity.

Let $c_{\max}=\max_s c_s$. For $u<c_{\max}$ the available set is nonempty, so aggregate consumption has rate $1$. Therefore
$$ 1=\sum_s q_s =\int_0^{c_{\max}}\sum_s g_s^c(u)\,du =c_{\max}. $$

The schedule is consequently a probabilistic-serial execution over the entire interval $[0,1]$. To check uniqueness explicitly, between consecutive removal times its available set and consumption rates are constant. Starting with residual capacities $r_s$ and rates $d_s$, the first residual capacity reaches zero after exactly
$$ \min_{s:d_s>0}\frac{r_s}{d_s}. $$
Every school attaining this minimum must be removed then, since availability equals positive residual capacity. These are exactly the stage updates of Section 1. Induction over the finitely many removal times gives $c_s=T_s$ for every school.

Conversely, the probabilistic-serial execution of Section 1 has available set $\{s:u<T_s\}$ and fills each school exactly. Its consumption-interval formula gives $G_s(T)=q_s$. This proves existence and uniqueness. $\square$

In particular,
$$ y_{ts}=\Phi_{ts}(T). $$

3. Direct convergence of the realized RP matrix

3.1 Valid replications and empirical score distributions

Let $d$ be the least common multiple of all reduced denominators appearing in the masses and capacities. Integrality holds exactly when $d$ divides $n$: for a reduced fraction $a/b$, the quantity $na/b$ is integral exactly when $b$ divides $n$. Hence the valid replication sizes are precisely $n=kd$.

For each valid $n$, give every labeled student an independent uniform score $U_i$ in $[0,1]$, and process students in increasing score order. Scores are distinct and lie in $(0,1)$ almost surely. By exchangeability, the ordering is uniform, so this implements RP.

For each type define
$$ F_{n,t}(u)= \frac1n\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t} \mathbf 1\{U_i\leq u\}, $$
and let
$$ E_n=\max_t\sup_{u\in[0,1]} |F_{n,t}(u)-\mu_tu|. $$
We claim that
$$ E_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0. $$

At any fixed $u$,
$$ \mathbb E[F_{n,t}(u)]=\mu_tu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(F_{n,t}(u)) = \frac{\mu_tu(1-u)}n \leq\frac1{4n}. $$
For a fixed grid $\{j/K:0\leq j\leq K\}$, Chebyshev’s inequality and a union bound imply convergence of the maximum grid error to zero in probability, simultaneously for all types.

For $u$ between two neighboring grid points, monotonicity of $F_{n,t}$ bounds its error by the maximum grid error plus $\mu_t/K$, hence by that error plus $1/K$. Given any positive error tolerance, choose $K$ sufficiently large and then let $n$ tend to infinity through valid replications. This proves the claim.

3.2 Exact identities for full-population cutoffs

Every student receives a seat: before any remaining student’s turn, the number of remaining seats equals the number of remaining students, and complete rankings allow them to choose any available school. Thus every seat is filled.

For $s\in S^+$, let $C_{n,s}$ be the score of the student receiving its last seat. These are the full-population cutoffs whose concentration was missing from the previous argument.

At a student’s turn with score $u$, school $s$ is available exactly when $u\leq C_{n,s}$. Hence a type-$t$ student receives $s$ exactly when
$$ a_{ts}(C_n)<u\leq C_{n,s}. $$
If the lower endpoint is at least the upper endpoint, no such student exists. Therefore, almost surely,
$$ Y^{\mathrm{RP}}_{n,ts} = F_{n,t}(C_{n,s}) - F_{n,t}\bigl(\min\{a_{ts}(C_n),C_{n,s}\}\bigr). $$
The uniform empirical error gives
$$ \bigl|Y^{\mathrm{RP}}_{n,ts}-\Phi_{ts}(C_n)\bigr| \leq 2E_n. $$
Since school $s$ is filled exactly,
$$ \sum_tY^{\mathrm{RP}}_{n,ts}=q_s, $$
and consequently
$$ \|G(C_n)-q\|_\infty\leq 2\tau E_n. $$

3.3 Concentration follows from uniqueness and compactness

The map $G$ is continuous, since it is formed from finitely many maxima, positive parts, and sums. By Section 2, $T$ is its unique solution to $G(c)=q$ in the compact cube $[0,1]^{S^+}$.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and consider
$$ K_\varepsilon= \{c\in[0,1]^{S^+}:\|c-T\|_\infty\geq\varepsilon\}. $$
If this set is empty, the desired bound is automatic. Otherwise, continuity, compactness, and uniqueness imply
$$ \eta_\varepsilon= \min_{c\in K_\varepsilon}\|G(c)-q\|_\infty>0. $$
Indeed, a zero minimum would produce another solution in $K_\varepsilon$.

Thus
$$ \Pr\bigl(\|C_n-T\|_\infty\geq\varepsilon\bigr) \leq \Pr\bigl(2\tau E_n\geq\eta_\varepsilon\bigr) \longrightarrow0. $$
This proves concentration of the full-population cutoffs, including schools with $T_s=1$.

Finally, maxima and minima change by at most the largest coordinate perturbation. Using
$$ \Phi_{ts}(c) = \mu_t\bigl(c_s-\min\{a_{ts}(c),c_s\}\bigr), $$
we obtain
$$ |\Phi_{ts}(C_n)-\Phi_{ts}(T)| \leq 2\mu_t\|C_n-T\|_\infty. $$
Combining the bounds,
$$ |Y^{\mathrm{RP}}_{n,ts}-y_{ts}| \leq 2E_n+2\mu_t\|C_n-T\|_\infty. $$
There are finitely many entries, and zero-capacity columns are identically zero. Therefore
$$ \|Y^{\mathrm{RP}}_n-y\|_\infty \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0. $$

4. Transfer to independent-school-lottery PRP

Use the supplied theorem: with strict student preferences, indifferent schools, and integral capacities totaling the student population, TTC under independent uniform school priorities and TTC under a common uniform priority have identical distributions over complete assignments. This is [Pathak–Sethuraman, Theorem 3](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/150145/1/v06-i1-p001-TE816.pdf).

To identify its TTC with the stated rule, replace each school by its seats, ranked consecutively in the same fixed order by every student, and give all its seats its priority list. Only the first remaining seat of a school receives student edges; consequently only that seat can belong to a cycle. Seat-level TTC therefore implements the stated capacity-decrementing rule.

All hypotheses hold at every valid $n$. Under common priorities, all nonfull schools point to the first remaining student. Any cycle must contain that student, whose favorite school points back to them; this is the unique cycle. Repeating gives serial dictatorship.

Hence
$$ Y^{\mathrm{PRP}}_n\overset{d}=Y^{\mathrm{RP}}_n. $$
Equality in distribution transfers the convergence just proved:
$$ \|Y^{\mathrm{PRP}}_n-y\|_\infty \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0. $$

5. Exact computation and bit complexity

The stage construction uses at most $m$ events. To prove polynomial bit complexity, we must also bound the sizes of its exact rational representations.

5.1 Integer implementation

Let $D$ be the product of all input denominators. Write
$$ \mu_t=\frac{a_t}{D}, \qquad q_s=\frac{b_s}{D}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_ta_t=\sum_sb_s=D, \qquad \log_2(D+1)=O(L). $$
Computing this denominator does not require constructing a replication of size $D$.

Maintain integers $P>0$, $R_s$, and $Z_{ts}$ representing
$$ r_s=\frac{R_s}{DP}, \qquad y^{\mathrm{current}}_{ts}=\frac{Z_{ts}}{DP}. $$
Initially,
$$ P=1,\qquad R_s=b_s,\qquad Z_{ts}=0. $$

At each stage compute integer rate numerators
$$ A_s=\sum_{t:f_t(A)=s}a_t, $$
taking $A_s=0$ outside the available set. Select a school $u$ with $A_u>0$ minimizing $R_s/A_s$. These comparisons use cross multiplication.

Cache
$$ v=R_u,\qquad b=A_u. $$
The stage duration is exactly
$$ \delta = \frac{R_u/(DP)}{A_u/D} = \frac{v}{Pb}. $$
Using old values on the right, update simultaneously:
$$ P'=Pb, $$
$$ R'_s=bR_s-A_sv, $$
$$ Z'_{ts} = bZ_{ts} + a_tv\,\mathbf 1\{f_t(A)=s\}. $$
For example,
$$ \frac{R'_s}{DP'} = \frac{R_s}{DP} - \frac{A_s}{D}\frac{v}{Pb}, $$
and the assignment update is verified identically. Thus these integer operations implement Section 1 exactly. Zero residual numerators detect exhaustion ties exactly.

5.2 Integer sizes

Every positive rate numerator satisfies
$$ 1\leq A_s\leq D. $$
After $j$ stages,
$$ P\leq D^j, \qquad DP\leq D^{j+1}. $$
Every residual capacity and accumulated assignment entry belongs to $[0,1]$. Consequently,
$$ 0\leq R_s\leq DP, \qquad 0\leq Z_{ts}\leq DP. $$
Since $j\leq m$, all stored integers have $O((m+1)L)$ bits. Temporary products in comparisons and updates satisfy the same asymptotic bit bound.

At termination, output
$$ y_{ts}=\frac{Z_{ts}}{DP}. $$
These are exact rational representations of polynomial length.

5.3 Running time

A straightforward implementation scans each ranking to find its favorite available school. Each stage uses $O(\tau m)$ integer or elementary operations: finding favorites, accumulating rates, selecting the minimum, and updating residual and assignment numerators. With at most $m$ stages, this gives $O(\tau m^2)$ operations.

Common-denominator preprocessing uses $O(m+\tau)$ arithmetic operations. With schoolbook arithmetic, operations on the relevant integers cost at most $O(((m+1)L)^2)$ bit operations. Including input reading, the total is bounded by
$$ O\!\left( L+(m+\tau+\tau m^2)((m+1)L)^2 \right) = O(\tau m^4L^2). $$

The algorithm processes only the $\tau$ supplied rankings. It neither enumerates all possible rankings nor expands the population into labeled students. This establishes polynomial-time exact computation in the requested support representation.
