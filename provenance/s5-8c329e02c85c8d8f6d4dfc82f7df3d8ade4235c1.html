Theorem 10 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given hospitals \(H\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), acceptable pairs, strict type preferences over hospitals, strict hospital rankings over types, and rational share quotas \([\ell_h,u_h]\), choose assignment masses \(x_{t,h}\) and unmatched masses \(x_{t,\bot}\) with \(\sum_{h\in H\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t x_{t,h}\le u_h\). Minimize \(\sum_{t\in T,\,h_0\in H\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h_0}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists h\in H:\ h\succ_t h_0\ \land\ \sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0\right]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=|H|\) hospitals, mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq T\times H\), strict type preferences over acceptable hospitals, and strict hospital rankings over acceptable types. Write \(H_t=\{h:(t,h)\in E\}\); every hospital in \(H_t\) is preferred to being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\).
Hospital quotas are binary rationals satisfying \(0\le\ell_h\le u_h\le1\) and \(\sum_h\ell_h\le1\). A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,h_0}\ge0\), with \(x_{t,h}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, \(\sum_{h_0\in H\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h_0}=\mu_t\), and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t x_{t,h}\le u_h\).
Its envious mass is \(F(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{h_0\in H_t\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h_0}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists h\in H_t:\ h\succ_t h_0\ \land\ \sum_{s:(s,h)\in E,\ t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0\right]\). Each assigned or unmatched portion is counted once if it envies any occupied hospital; vacancies alone do not create envy, and portions of the same type do not envy one another.
Given a binary rational threshold \(b\in[0,1]\), the decision problem asks whether a feasible assignment with \(F(x)\le b\) exists; infeasible instances have answer no. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is this continuous minimum-envious-mass decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete minimum-envy-resident hospitals/residents matching with lower quotas is NP-hard ([Hu–Garg](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15559)); deciding existence of a feasible envy-free discrete matching is polynomial-time solvable ([Yokoi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04888)).
No result settling this exact continuous mass objective was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one and interpreted quotas as shares, with nonnegative lower and upper quotas bounded by one.
  • Carried over the necessary condition that total lower quotas do not exceed total society mass; other infeasible instances receive answer no.
  • Restricted assignments and envy comparisons to mutually acceptable pairs, and placed being unmatched below every acceptable hospital.
  • Specified assignment masses as nonnegative reals; hospital rankings compare distinct acceptable types, so same-type portions cannot generate justified envy.
  • Introduced a binary rational threshold between zero and one to state the exact optimization question as a decision problem, and defined total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. In particular, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with dyadic rational masses and quotas.

The essential point is that fractional splitting cannot improve the optimum in the instances used below: every feasible fractional assignment can be rounded to a discrete matching without increasing envious mass.

1. Rounding without increasing envy

Write \(\mathcal A=\{(t,h):h\in H_t\cup\{\bot\}\}\) for the permitted assignment coordinates.

Lemma. Suppose \(D\) is a positive integer such that \(D\mu_t\), \(D\ell_h\), and \(Du_h\) are integers for every type and hospital. For every feasible real assignment \(x\), there is a feasible assignment \(y\) satisfying
\[ \]
\(Dy_{t,h_0}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\) for every \((t,h_0)\in\mathcal A\), and \(F(y)\le F(x)\).

Proof. Fix \(x\). Define its coordinate support by
\(S=\{(t,h_0)\in\mathcal A:x_{t,h_0}>0\}\), and fix the coefficients
\[ \]
\(c_{t,h_0}=\mathbf 1\!\left[\exists h\in H_t:\ h\succ_t h_0\ \land\ \sum_{s:(s,h)\in E,\ t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0\right]\).
Thus \(F(x)=\sum_{(t,h_0)\in\mathcal A}c_{t,h_0}x_{t,h_0}\).

Consider the linear program that minimizes
\(C(z)=\sum_{(t,h_0)\in S}c_{t,h_0}z_{t,h_0}\)
over feasible assignments \(z\), with the additional restrictions \(z_{t,h_0}=0\) outside \(S\). Its feasible set contains \(x\).

For every such \(z\), an envy witness under \(z\) is also an envy witness under \(x\). Indeed, a positive sum of lower-ranked occupants at a hospital contains some coordinate \(z_{s,h}>0\); the support restriction then implies \(x_{s,h}>0\). Consequently, coordinate by coordinate, the envy indicator under \(z\) is at most \(c_{t,h_0}\). Hence
\[ \]
\(F(z)\le C(z)\).

After scaling flows by \(D\), this linear program is a minimum-cost circulation problem on the following directed network:

  • An arc from a source \(a\) to each type \(t\), with lower and upper bounds both \(D\mu_t\).
  • An arc from \(t\) to \(h_0\) for each \((t,h_0)\in S\), with bounds \(0\) and \(D\), and cost \(c_{t,h_0}\).
  • An arc from each hospital \(h\) to a sink \(q\), with bounds \(D\ell_h\) and \(Du_h\).
  • An arc from \(\bot\) to \(q\), with bounds \(0\) and \(D\).
  • An arc from \(q\) to \(a\), with lower and upper bounds both \(D\).

All other costs are zero. Flow conservation enforces the type totals and hospital quotas. The network is feasible because \(Dx\) supplies a feasible circulation.

The integral minimum-cost circulation theorem states that a nonempty circulation polytope with integral lower and upper arc bounds has an integral minimizer for every linear objective. It follows from total unimodularity of the directed node–arc incidence matrix. Here all bounds are integral by hypothesis, and all capacities are finite, so an integral minimum-cost circulation exists.

Divide its assignment flows by \(D\), obtaining \(y\). Then \(y\) is feasible, its coordinates are multiples of \(1/D\), and
\[ \]
\(F(y)\le C(y)\le C(x)=F(x)\).
This proves the lemma.

The support restriction is crucial: the linear objective can overcount envy after rounding, but cannot undercount it.

2. The discrete hardness result used

For a discrete matching \(M\), let \(\operatorname{ER}(M)\) count residents \(r\) for which some acceptable hospital \(h\), preferred to \(M(r)\), contains a resident ranked below \(r\). Unmatched residents prefer every acceptable hospital to being unmatched.

Theorem 10 and Lemmas 11–12 of [Hu–Garg, *Minimal Envy Matchings in the Hospitals/Residents Problem with Lower Quotas*](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.15559v1#S4) establish that deciding whether a feasible matching satisfies \(\operatorname{ER}(M)\le k\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Their construction uses strict preferences, exact integer hospital quotas summing to the number \(n\) of residents, and always admits a feasible matching. Thus hardness holds with quotas between \(0\) and \(n\). We may restrict to thresholds \(0\le k\le n\), since thresholds outside that range have immediate answers.

This counts each envious resident once, exactly as required by the present objective.

3. Polynomial reduction to continuous masses

Take an instance of that discrete decision problem, with resident set \(R\), \(|R|=n\ge 1\), hospital quotas \(a_h\) and \(q_h\), and threshold \(k\).

Let \(D\) be the smallest power of two at least \(n\). Thus \(n\le D<2n\).

Construct the continuous instance as follows:

  • For each resident \(r\), create a type \(t_r\) of mass \(1/D\).
  • Preserve all acceptable pairs, resident preferences, and hospital rankings among these types.
  • Set \(\ell_h=a_h/D\) and \(u_h=q_h/D\).
  • Set \(b=k/D\).
  • If \(D>n\), add one type of mass \((D-n)/D\) with no acceptable hospital.

All type masses are positive and sum to one. The quota conditions hold because \(a_h\le q_h\le n\le D\) and \(\sum_h a_h\le n\). The added type, when present, must be unmatched and never envies.

Every rational introduced is dyadic, and the construction has polynomial binary length. Its support size is \(n\) or \(n+1\).

Forward implication

Given a feasible discrete matching \(M\), assign each resident type’s entire mass \(1/D\) to its matched hospital, or to \(\bot\) if unmatched. Assign the added type to \(\bot\).

The resulting assignment \(x^M\) is feasible. For each resident type, the envy indicator equals the corresponding discrete resident’s envy indicator: hospital occupancy by a lower-ranked type is equivalent to occupancy by the corresponding lower-ranked resident. Therefore
\[ \]
\(F(x^M)=\operatorname{ER}(M)/D\).

In particular, \(\operatorname{ER}(M)\le k\) implies \(F(x^M)\le b\).

Reverse implication

Suppose a feasible continuous assignment \(x\) satisfies \(F(x)\le k/D\).

Apply the lemma with this same \(D\). Every scaled mass and quota is integral, including the added type’s mass \(D-n\). We obtain a feasible \(y\) with integral scaled coordinates and \(F(y)\le F(x)\).

For each original resident type \(t_r\),
\[ \]
\(\sum_{h_0}Dy_{t_r,h_0}=D\mu_{t_r}=1\).
Since these coordinates are nonnegative integers, exactly one equals \(1\), and all others equal \(0\). Thus each original resident type is assigned wholly to one hospital or wholly to \(\bot\).

These assignments define a feasible discrete matching \(M_y\). The added type has no acceptable hospital and contributes no envy. Consequently,
\[ \]
\(\operatorname{ER}(M_y)=D F(y)\le D F(x)\le k\).

We have proved both directions of the reduction. Hence the continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For a general input, take \(D\) to be the product of the positive denominators appearing in the masses and quotas. Its binary length is at most the sum of those denominators’ binary lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). All scaled masses and quotas are integral.

If a real feasible assignment with \(F(x)\le b\) exists, the lemma gives such an assignment \(y\) whose coordinates have the form \(p_{t,h_0}/D\). Because every coordinate is between \(0\) and \(1\), each integer numerator satisfies \(0\le p_{t,h_0}\le D\). There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) coordinates, so this certificate has polynomial length.

A verifier can check feasibility by exact rational arithmetic. It can also compute every envy indicator by checking whether a preferred acceptable hospital has a positive coordinate belonging to a lower-ranked type, then sum the affected portions and compare with \(b\). These operations take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Combined with the reduction, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and therefore admits a polynomial-time algorithm only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
