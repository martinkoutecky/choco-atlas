The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite schools \(C\), finite student types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita capacities and diversity targets, and school priorities over tiers, does a school-proposing deferred-acceptance procedure on mass—allowing a type’s mass to split at a priority-tier cutoff—compute an assignment flow \(x_{tc}\) (including an outside option) that is fair-with-diversity and maximal under the mass version of \(>_q\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) student types \(T\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq1\) schools \(C\) and \(k\geq1\) disjoint diversity groups, with each type belonging to a group \(g(t)\).
Each type has a strict preference order \(\succ_t\) over \(C\cup\{0\}\), where \(0\) is the outside option. Each school specifies acceptable types and ordered priority tiers; break ties between distinct types by their support-list order, obtaining \(\succ_c\). Identical mass within a type has equal priority.
Capacities \(q_c\) and soft diversity targets \(r_{ch}\) are nonnegative binary rationals with \(\sum_h r_{ch}\leq q_c\). All quantities are per capita; targets need not be feasible. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible flow satisfies \(x_{td}\geq0\), \(\sum_{d\in C\cup\{0\}}x_{td}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\leq q_c\). Individual rationality means positive school flows use mutually acceptable pairs; outside mass is permitted. Write \(N_{ch}(x)=\sum_{t:g(t)=h}x_{tc}\).
A type demands school \(c\) if it is acceptable to \(c\) and \(x_{td}>0\) for some \(d\) with \(c\succ_t d\). A flow is fair-with-diversity if it is individually rational and no demanding pair \((t,c)\) satisfies any of: \(\sum_u x_{uc}<q_c\); \(N_{c,g(t)}(x)<r_{c,g(t)}\); or there exists \(u\) with \(x_{uc}>0\), \(t\succ_c u\), and either \(g(u)=g(t)\) or \(N_{c,g(u)}(x)>r_{c,g(u)}\).
Define \(y>_q x\) when \(N_{ch}(y)\geq N_{ch}(x)\) for every pair with \(N_{ch}(x)\leq r_{ch}\), and \(N_{ch}(y)>N_{ch}(x)\) for at least one pair with \(N_{ch}(x)<r_{ch}\). A fair flow is maximal if no other fair flow dominates it under \(>_q\).
School choice from available amounts first discards unacceptable types, then selects up to \(r_{ch}\) mass from each group in priority order, then fills remaining capacity from residual mass in priority order; cutoff types may be split.
Mass school-proposing deferred acceptance starts with availability \(a_{tc}=\mu_t\). In each simultaneous round schools propose their choice vectors \(p_{tc}\); each type retains its preferred acceptable offers up to total mass \(\mu_t\), giving \(b_{tc}\), and rejects the rest. Update \(a_{tc}\leftarrow a_{tc}-(p_{tc}-b_{tc})\). Stop when there are no rejections, returning retained flows and assigning residual mass outside.

Does this mass school-proposing deferred-acceptance procedure always terminate with a fair-with-diversity flow maximal under \(>_q\)? Is computing such a flow possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite students with strict priorities, school-proposing deferred acceptance with these reserves returns a fair-with-maximal-diversity assignment ([Bó, 2016](https://inaciobo.com/files/Bo2016_GEB.pdf)).
Ordinary real-valued stable allocation admits a strongly polynomial algorithm ([Baïou–Balinski, corrected 2002 version](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302)); this result does not state the reserve-based diversity-maximality guarantee above. No result settling this exact mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Distinguished support types, which encode preferences and priorities, from disjoint diversity groups to which reserve targets apply.
  • Normalized total population mass to one and interpreted capacities and targets as nonnegative rational per-capita quantities, with targets summing to at most each school's capacity.
  • Retained strict student preferences, mutual acceptability, and an outside option; allowed unmatched mass, following the source's procedure and proof despite its overly strict displayed individual-rationality definition.
  • Resolved school priority ties between distinct support types by support-list order; identical mass within a type remains tied, and fairness uses strict priority comparisons.
  • Defined mass demand through positive mass at a strictly worse destination and carried over all three diversity-demand conditions together with non-wastefulness.
  • Required school acceptability for diversity demands, consistent with the reserve choice function and the intended equivalence between fairness and absence of blocking.
  • Specified simultaneous proposals, partial retention and rejection, permanent reduction of rejected availability, and exact termination when no rejection occurs.
  • Preserved the stated diversity comparison literally, including its weak inequality at a satisfied target, rather than replacing it with a capped-count comparison.

Result

Yes. For the rational inputs in the question, the stated procedure terminates and returns a fair-with-diversity flow maximal under \(>_q\).

Computing such a flow is polynomial-time solvable. However, executing the stated procedure round by round can require exponentially many rounds. A polynomial algorithm follows from a reduction to ordinary stable allocation, proved below.

Throughout, comparisons between vectors are coordinatewise. Competing fair flows may have arbitrary real coordinates; maximality is not restricted to rational competitors.

1. Two properties of reserve choice

Write \(\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)\) for school \(c\)'s choice from availability vector \(a\). Unacceptable coordinates are ignored.

1.1. Quantitative substitutability

For \(z\leq a\), reserve choice satisfies

\[ \operatorname{Ch}_c(z)_t\geq \min\{z_t,\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)_t\}. \tag{1} \]

Here is a justification that also handles split types.

First suppose all quantities are rational. Multiply by a common denominator and replace each type's available amount by unit copies, ordered consecutively within that type. Capacities and reserves become integers. The finite reserve choice rule is substitutable: deleting candidates cannot cause a previously selected, undeleted candidate to become unselected. This is the finite choice-function property in [Bó, Lemma 1](https://inaciobo.com/files/Bo2016_GEB.pdf); its hypotheses hold because priorities on copies are strict and groups are disjoint.

For each type, both reserve selection and subsequent residual selection together select a prefix of its copies. Reducing availability from \(a_t\) to \(z_t\) deletes a suffix. Thus at least

\( \min\{z_t,\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)_t\} \)

of its selected amount survives, proving (1).

For real availability vectors, approximate \(z\leq a\) by rational vectors preserving that inequality. Reserve choice is continuous: its finitely many operations are sums, differences, minima, and positive parts. Passing to the limit proves (1).

This is a local argument about one school's choice rule; it does not assume a global equivalence between mass deferred acceptance and deferred acceptance on individual copies.

1.2. Offers to a type that demands a school

Lemma. Let \(y\) be fair-with-diversity, and suppose \(a_u\geq y_{uc}\) for every type \(u\). If \(t\) demands \(c\) under \(y\), then

\[ \operatorname{Ch}_c(a)_t\leq y_{tc}. \tag{2} \]

Proof. Put \(h=g(t)\). Fairness, together with demand, implies:

  • school \(c\) is full;
  • \(N_{ch}(y)\geq r_{ch}\);
  • no lower-priority type in group \(h\) has positive mass at \(c\);
  • every lower-priority type \(u\) with positive mass at \(c\) belongs to a group whose total at \(c\) is at most its reserve.

Define \(z\) by \(z_u=y_{uc}\) for \(u\neq t\), and \(z_t=a_t\).

Adding this extra availability of \(t\) leaves the reserve-stage selection unchanged. Other groups are unchanged. In group \(h\), the original mass of types strictly above \(t\), together with \(y_{tc}\), already fills the reserve: the group's total meets its reserve and no lower-priority type has positive mass.

Moreover, every lower-priority type's original mass is entirely selected in the reserve stage. Indeed, such a type's group total is at most its reserve. Consequently, the original residual mass consists only of types above \(t\) and \(t\) itself.

Since \(y\) fills the school, that original residual mass fills the entire residual capacity. Additional residual availability of \(t\) therefore cannot increase its selected amount. Hence

\( \operatorname{Ch}_c(z)_t=y_{tc}. \)

Now \(z\leq a\) and \(z_t=a_t\). Applying (1) gives

\( y_{tc}=\operatorname{Ch}_c(z)_t \geq \min\{a_t,\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)_t\} =\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)_t. \)

This proves (2). \(\square\)

2. Correctness of the stated mass procedure

Index rounds by \(n\), with availability \(a^n\), proposals \(p^n\), and retained offers \(b^n\).

2.1. Finite termination

Choose a positive integer \(D\) such that every input mass, capacity, and reserve is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). For example, take the product of their denominators.

Every quantity generated by the procedure remains on this grid, because all operations are sums, differences, minima, and positive parts. Also,

\( a^{n+1}_{tc}=a^n_{tc}-p^n_{tc}+b^n_{tc} \)

is nonnegative and no greater than \(a^n_{tc}\).

Initially,

\( \sum_{t,c}a^1_{tc}=m. \)

Every nonterminal round decreases this sum by at least \(1/D\). There are therefore at most \(mD\) nonterminal rounds.

The terminating flow is feasible and individually rational: school proposals respect capacities and school acceptability, types retain only acceptable offers within their masses, and remaining mass is assigned outside.

2.2. Retained offers remain available

Since \(p^n_{tc}\leq a^n_{tc}\), the update satisfies

\( a^{n+1}_{tc}\geq b^n_{tc}. \)

Using (1),

\[ p^{n+1}_{tc} \geq \min\{a^{n+1}_{tc},p^n_{tc}\} \geq b^n_{tc}. \tag{3} \]

Thus the next round's proposals include every previously retained amount.

It follows that, for each type and each acceptable school \(c\), retained mass at schools weakly preferred to \(c\) cannot decrease. In fact, greedy retention assigns to such a preference prefix the minimum of the type's mass and the total offers in that prefix; (3) makes that total at least the previously retained prefix mass.

If a type rejects a positive amount from an acceptable school \(c\), it retains its entire mass at schools weakly preferred to \(c\). The preceding monotonicity then prevents it from having mass strictly below \(c\) at termination.

Consequently, if \(t\) demands \(c\) in the terminal flow \(x\), it never rejected \(c\), and hence

\[ a^{\mathrm{fin}}_{tc}=\mu_t>x_{tc}. \tag{4} \]

Demand and individual rationality also imply that \(c\) is acceptable to the type.

2.3. An unselected available amount cannot witness unfairness

Consider any school choice \(p=\operatorname{Ch}_c(a)\) and acceptable type \(t\) with \(p_t<a_t\). We show that none of the school-side conditions in the question holds for \(t\).

Vacancy. Reserve choice is work-conserving. If the school were underfilled, it would select all acceptable available mass, contradicting \(p_t<a_t\).

Own-group deficit. Reserve selection chooses at least the minimum of the reserve and the available group total. If the selected group total is below its reserve, all available mass of that group is selected. Again \(p_t=a_t\), a contradiction.

Lower-priority mass in the same group. If a lower-priority type \(u\) receives any reserve-stage mass, all available mass of the higher-priority \(t\) must already have been selected in that stage. Otherwise, positive selected mass of \(u\) comes from the residual stage. That stage cannot select \(u\) while leaving residual availability of the higher-priority \(t\) unselected.

Lower-priority mass in a group above its reserve. Suppose \(p_u>0\), \(t\succ_c u\), and group \(j=g(u)\) has selected total above \(r_{cj}\). Some selected mass of group \(j\) therefore comes from the residual stage.

If \(u\) has no reserve-stage mass, use its own positive residual selection. If \(u\) has reserve-stage mass, the reserve stage has exhausted all available group-\(j\) types above \(u\). Thus any selected residual group-\(j\) mass has priority no higher than \(u\).

In either case, a residual-stage selected type lies strictly below \(t\), contradicting the unselected residual availability of \(t\).

At termination, \(x_{\cdot c}=\operatorname{Ch}_c(a^{\mathrm{fin}}_{\cdot c})\). Applying this argument to (4) proves that no demanding pair violates fairness.

The terminal flow is fair-with-diversity.

2.4. Availability dominates every fair flow

The crucial invariant is:

\[ a^n_{tc}\geq y_{tc} \quad\text{for every fair-with-diversity flow }y. \tag{5} \]

It holds initially because \(y_{tc}\leq\mu_t\).

Fix a fair flow \(y\) and assume (5) holds before a round. Consider a coordinate with \(y_{tc}>0\). For every school \(e\succ_t c\) that accepts \(t\), type \(t\) demands \(e\) under \(y\), because \(y_{tc}>0\). Lemma (2) gives \(p_{te}\leq y_{te}\). Schools that do not accept \(t\) propose zero. Therefore

\( \sum_{e\succ_t c}p_{te} \leq \sum_{e\succ_t c}y_{te} \leq \mu_t-y_{tc}. \)

When processing its offer from \(c\), the type consequently has room for at least \(y_{tc}\), so

\( b_{tc}\geq \min\{p_{tc},y_{tc}\}. \)

Hence

\( a'_{tc} =a_{tc}-p_{tc}+b_{tc} \geq a_{tc}-p_{tc}+\min\{p_{tc},y_{tc}\} \geq y_{tc}. \)

The last inequality follows from \(a_{tc}\geq y_{tc}\) and \(a_{tc}\geq p_{tc}\), considering respectively \(p_{tc}\leq y_{tc}\) and \(p_{tc}>y_{tc}\).

Coordinates with \(y_{tc}=0\) satisfy the invariant by nonnegativity. This completes the induction.

2.5. Maximality

The reserve stage at termination, together with (5), gives, for every fair flow \(y\),

\[ N_{ch}(x)\geq \min\{r_{ch},N_{ch}(y)\} \quad\text{for every }c,h. \tag{6} \]

Only school-acceptable availability enters reserve selection; all positive school coordinates of \(y\) are acceptable, so the availability bound suffices.

If \(N_{ch}(x)<r_{ch}\), (6) implies

\( N_{ch}(y)\leq N_{ch}(x) \)

for every fair \(y\). Thus no fair flow can improve even one deficient coordinate of \(x\). In particular, no fair flow dominates \(x\) under \(>_q\).

This proves the stated procedure's guarantee.

3. The round-by-round procedure can take exponential time

Let \(N\) be a positive integer. Take two types of mass \(1/2\), one diversity group, two schools, and zero reserves. Set

\( q_A=\frac{N+1}{2N}, \qquad q_B=\frac12. \)

All pairs are mutually acceptable. Preferences and priorities are

\( B\succ_1 A\succ_1 0,\qquad A\succ_2 B\succ_2 0, \)

\( 1\succ_A 2,\qquad 2\succ_B 1. \)

For calculations, multiply amounts by \(2N\). Each type then has mass \(N\), and capacities are \(N+1\) and \(N\).

For \(j=0,\ldots,N-1\), proposals in round \(2j+1\), listed in type order, are

\( p_A=(N-j,j+1),\qquad p_B=(j,N-j). \)

Type \(2\) rejects one unit from \(B\). In round \(2j+2\), proposals are

\( p_A=(N-j,j+1),\qquad p_B=(j+1,N-j-1), \)

and type \(1\) rejects one unit from \(A\).

These formulas follow by induction: after each two-round pair, availability of type \(1\) at \(A\) and type \(2\) at \(B\) has decreased by one; the other two availabilities remain \(N\).

After \(2N\) rejecting rounds, the next proposals are

\( p_A=(0,N),\qquad p_B=(N,0), \)

and there are no rejections. Thus termination requires exactly \(2N+1\) rounds.

Choosing \(N\) to be a power of two makes the input length \(O(\log N)\). The round count is exponential in that length.

4. A polynomial reduction to ordinary stable allocation

We now construct a different algorithm that computes a maximal fair flow in polynomial time.

4.1. Expanded schools

Replace school \(c\) by these capacity classes:

  • a reserve class \(R_{ch}\) of capacity \(r_{ch}\) for every group \(h\);
  • an open class \(O_c\) of capacity \(q_c-\sum_h r_{ch}\).

Delete zero-capacity classes. Their total capacity is \(q_c\).

Every class accepts exactly the types mutually acceptable at \(c\). Priorities are strict:

  • \(R_{ch}\) ranks group-\(h\) types first, preserving \(\succ_c\) within that group; it then ranks all other types according to \(\succ_c\);
  • \(O_c\) uses \(\succ_c\).

A type ranks classes in blocks according to its original school preference. Within school \(c\), it ranks its own reserve class first, then the open class, then other reserve classes in any fixed strict order. Omitted classes are skipped.

Each type retains supply \(\mu_t\). Unallocated supply represents the outside option.

This is an ordinary bipartite stable-allocation instance. A pair blocks precisely when the type has unused supply or positive mass at a less preferred class, and the class has unused capacity or positive mass of a lower-priority type.

Any required edge upper bounds can be set to \(\mu_t\); these are redundant. A demanding type-class pair necessarily has current flow strictly below \(\mu_t\).

4.2. Internal stability forces canonical reserve use

Let \(z\) be an expanded allocation with no blocking pair involving a move between classes of the same school. Aggregate it as

\( x_{tc}=\sum_{s:\,s\text{ is a class of }c}z_{ts}. \)

Define the own-group mass in a reserve class by

\( Z_{ch}=\sum_{t:g(t)=h}z_{t,R_{ch}}, \)

with value zero for an omitted class. Then

\[ Z_{ch}=\min\{r_{ch},N_{ch}(x)\}. \tag{7} \]

To prove this, suppose some group-\(h\) mass is assigned to another class of \(c\). Its type prefers \(R_{ch}\). Internal stability therefore requires \(R_{ch}\) to be full of group-\(h\) mass: vacancy or a non-\(h\) occupant would create a blocking pair. Hence \(Z_{ch}=r_{ch}\).

If no group-\(h\) mass is assigned elsewhere, then \(Z_{ch}=N_{ch}(x)\leq r_{ch}\). This proves (7), including the immediate zero-reserve case.

Internal stability also implies:

A group-\(h\) type assigned outside \(R_{ch}\) cannot have strictly higher school priority than a group-\(h\) type assigned inside \(R_{ch}\).

Otherwise, the former type blocks the reserve class.

4.3. Every expanded stable allocation aggregates to a fair flow

Feasibility and individual rationality follow immediately. Suppose \(t\) demands school \(c\) in the aggregate. It then demands every class of \(c\), because some of its mass lies outside or at a less preferred school.

Each possible fairness violation yields an expanded blocking pair.

Vacancy. Aggregate vacancy implies vacancy in some class of \(c\).

Own-group deficit. If \(N_{c,g(t)}(x)<r_{c,g(t)}\), the own reserve class has vacancy or a non-own-group occupant, by (7).

Lower-priority occupant in the same group. Between two types of the same group, every class of \(c\) preserves their original school priority. Thus \(t\) blocks the class containing that occupant.

Lower-priority occupant in a group above its reserve. Let \(u\) be such an occupant, with group \(j\).

If some positive mass of \(u\) lies outside \(R_{cj}\), type \(t\) outranks it at that class: \(u\) has no own-group protection there.

Otherwise, \(u\) lies in \(R_{cj}\). Since \(N_{cj}(x)>r_{cj}\), some group-\(j\) type \(v\) has positive mass outside \(R_{cj}\). The internal priority property above implies that \(v\) has priority no higher than \(u\). Consequently \(t\succ_c v\), and \(t\) blocks the class containing that outside-reserve mass.

All cases contradict expanded stability. The aggregate is therefore fair-with-diversity.

4.4. Every fair flow has an expanded stable lift

Let \(y\) be any fair flow, including a real-valued one.

For each school \(c\), consider a local ordinary stable-allocation instance:

  • type supplies are \(y_{tc}\), omitting zero supplies;
  • classes and their capacities are those constructed above;
  • all these local type-class pairs are acceptable;
  • preferences and priorities are the expanded ones.

A local stable allocation exists. It assigns every local type's supply: if a type had unused supply, total assigned mass would be below \(\sum_t y_{tc}\leq q_c\), leaving vacancy in some class. The complete local acceptability graph would then give a blocking pair.

Combining these local allocations produces a lift \(w\) of \(y\). It satisfies (7) and has no internal blocking pair.

Suppose an expanded pair \((t,s)\), where \(s\) belongs to school \(c\), blocks this lift. If its demand comes from mass at a worse class of the same school, local stability rules it out. Otherwise, \(t\) demands \(c\) under \(y\).

Fairness then requires \(c\) to be full, so all its classes are full. A block must displace a lower-priority occupant \(u\).

  • At \(t\)'s own reserve class, an occupant from another group implies an own-group deficit by (7), contradicting fairness. An own-group occupant lower than \(t\) violates same-group fairness.
  • At another reserve class, an occupant from that class's own group outranks \(t\) and cannot be displaced. Every other occupant lies outside its own reserve class, so (7) implies that its group's total exceeds its reserve.
  • At the open class, every occupant likewise lies outside its own reserve class and belongs to a group above its reserve.

In the latter two cases, the class comparison between \(t\) and \(u\) preserves their original school priority. Displacement therefore contradicts fairness under the excess-group condition.

Thus \(w\) is globally stable. We have proved both directions of the reduction.

5. Polynomial computation and diversity maximality

The ordinary stable-allocation results needed here are:

  • stable allocations exist for arbitrary real supplies and capacities;
  • an allocation optimal for either chosen side can be computed in polynomial time;
  • in a chosen-side-optimal allocation, every preference prefix of each chosen-side node receives at least as much mass as in any other stable allocation.

These follow from the corrected [Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302), specifically its inductive algorithm, Lemma 8, and Theorem 10 with Corollary 4.

For clarity, the prefix statement follows from their optimality order and equal-total property. If another stable allocation increases a coordinate of a chosen-side node, the optimal allocation has zero mass at every less preferred coordinate. If a prefix gained mass in the alternative, choose an increased coordinate in that prefix; the optimal allocation then places its entire assigned total inside the prefix. Equal assigned totals contradict the alleged prefix gain.

All hypotheses hold in the expanded instance: it is bipartite, supplies and capacities are nonnegative, preferences are strict, and zero-capacity nodes have been deleted.

Compute an allocation \(z^\star\) optimal for the class side, and aggregate it to \(x^\star\). Section 4.3 proves fairness.

Fix any fair flow \(y\), and lift it to an expanded stable allocation \(w\), using Section 4.4. At reserve class \(R_{ch}\), its own group is a priority prefix. Class-side optimality and (7) give

\[ \min\{r_{ch},N_{ch}(x^\star)\} \geq \min\{r_{ch},N_{ch}(y)\}. \tag{8} \]

If \(N_{ch}(x^\star)<r_{ch}\), inequality (8) implies \(N_{ch}(y)\leq N_{ch}(x^\star)\). Hence no fair flow can improve any deficient coordinate, and \(x^\star\) is maximal under \(>_q\).

Finally, the expanded instance has at most

\( \tau+m(k+1) \)

nodes and at most

\( m\tau(k+1) \)

acceptable edges. Its rational data have polynomial encoding length: the only new numerical capacities are \(q_c-\sum_h r_{ch}\). Constructing the instance, computing the class-optimal stable allocation, and aggregating its flows therefore take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

The reduction supplies the reserve-based maximality guarantee; that guarantee is not being assumed as part of the ordinary stable-allocation theorem.

  • Excess-group lower priority: If the lower-priority occupant receives reserve mass, all higher-priority availability in that group was already exhausted there. Any residual selection from that group therefore has priority no higher than that occupant. This gives the required contradiction.

The two cases in the final availability inequality are correct. Zero coordinates follow from nonnegativity.

There is no quantifier interchange problem: the induction works for each arbitrary fair \(y\), yielding the stated universal invariant.

Section 2.5: Maximality

Inequality (6) follows from reserve-stage selection and the availability invariant.

For a coordinate below its target, (6) excludes any fair competitor with a strictly larger value. This is stronger than the required exclusion of a competitor satisfying \(>_q\), and therefore proves maximality.

Coordinates exactly at their targets require no separate improvement argument: domination requires a strict improvement in at least one deficient coordinate.
