The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supports of advisor, student, and co-advisor types, rational masses \(\alpha_a,\sigma_s,\chi_c\), and complete type-level preferences and acceptability matching the paper’s assumptions, determine whether a stable mass allocation exists and, if so, compute one. An allocation is \(x_{asc}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{s,c}x_{asc}\leq\alpha_a\), \(\sum_{a,c}x_{asc}\leq\sigma_s\), and \(\sum_{a,s}x_{asc}\leq\chi_c\); unmatched mass is allowed. Stability rules out any positive mass reassignment to a mutually acceptable triple for which the selected advisor and co-advisor mass and student mass satisfy the paper’s blocking condition: for an unmatched student slice both two-sided pairs block, while for a matched slice either pair blocks.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,S,C\) be finite, nonempty supports of advisor, student, and co-advisor types. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|S|+|C|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses \(\alpha_a,\sigma_s,\chi_c\) need not sum to one or have equal side totals. Set \(m=\max\{|A|,|S|,|C|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each advisor and co-advisor has a total preorder over student types and an unmatched option \(\bot\); each student has separate total preorders over advisor and co-advisor types and \(\bot\). No partner is tied with \(\bot\). Members of a type are indistinguishable; ties between types are allowed. Acceptability means strict preference to \(\bot\), and a triple is mutually acceptable when both adjacent pairs are mutually acceptable. Students rank partner pairs lexicographically, prioritizing advisors; advisors and co-advisors care only about student type. All advisor–co-advisor pairs are compatible.
An allocation consists of \(x_{asc}\geq0\), zero on unacceptable triples, with \(\sum_{s,c}x_{asc}\leq\alpha_a\), \(\sum_{a,c}x_{asc}\leq\sigma_s\), and \(\sum_{a,s}x_{asc}\leq\chi_c\). Let \(u_a,u_s,u_c\) denote the corresponding unused masses; partial student matches are forbidden.
Define \(D_a(s)=u_a+\sum_{t,d:\,s\succ_a t}x_{atd}\) and \(D_c(s)=u_c+\sum_{b,t:\,s\succ_c t}x_{btc}\). These are the masses an acceptable student type could strictly improve upon at the respective partner.
A mutually acceptable \((a,s,c)\) blocks if either \(u_s>0\), \(D_a(s)>0\), and \(D_c(s)>0\), or some \(x_{bsd}>0\) satisfies \((a\succ_s b\land D_a(s)>0)\lor(c\succ_s d\land D_c(s)>0)\). In the latter case a student slice may change just one partner, retaining the other. Stability means absence of such positive-mass blocks.

Is determining whether a stable mass allocation exists, and computing an exact rational allocation when one exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the problem NP-hard?

Discrete stable matchings under these preference and acceptability assumptions always exist and can be computed in polynomial time; the integer-quota extension also has a stable-matching algorithm. [Mordig et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11834v2)
No result settling this exact mass problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Unspecified normalization: masses are arbitrary positive binary rationals, preserving potentially unequal populations on the three sides.
  • Ambiguous completeness: preference lists rank every supported partner type and the unmatched option, but may place partners below that option, preserving incomplete acceptability.
  • Unspecified ties: allow total preorders, exclude ties with the unmatched option, and require strict improvements for blocking, as in the nonstrict-preference extension.
  • Unspecified compatibility: every advisor–co-advisor pair is compatible, as required by the anchor's setting.
  • Unspecified slice blocking: positive unused mass or mass assigned to strictly worse student types supplies a potential partner slice; matched student slices may retain one partner while changing the other.
  • Missing individual rationality: allocation mass is permitted only on mutually acceptable triples.

1. Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. Every instance satisfying the stated assumptions has a stable allocation, and an exact rational allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The construction reduces the problem to a single-commodity stable-flow problem. Splitting each student vertex enforces complete matches; blocking paths that end or start at a student vertex capture changes of just one partner.

The proof below uses exactly the blocking condition in the question.

2. The stable-flow theorem used

Consider a finite directed network with source \(\rho\), sink \(\omega\), capacities \(\kappa(e)\), and strict, separate preferences over each nonterminal vertex’s incoming and outgoing edges. A feasible flow satisfies capacities and conservation at every nonterminal vertex.

A nonempty directed walk \(W=(v_0,\ldots,v_k)\) blocks a flow \(f\) when:

  • Every edge of \(W\) is unsaturated: \(f(e)<\kappa(e)\).
  • Either \(v_0=\rho\), or \(v_0\) strictly prefers the first edge of \(W\) to some outgoing edge carrying positive flow.
  • Either \(v_k=\omega\), or \(v_k\) strictly prefers the last edge of \(W\) to some incoming edge carrying positive flow.

A flow is stable if it has no blocking walk.

Known theorem. A stable flow always exists. Cseh and Matuschke’s Algorithm 1 computes one using \(O(|V||E|)\) arithmetic operations. It starts with zero flow and augments by bottleneck residual capacities, so integer capacities give integer output. See Definitions 1–2, Theorem 2, and Algorithm 1 of [*New and simple algorithms for stable flow problems*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3701).

We verify the network hypotheses below and address binary bit complexity in Section 6.

3. Constructing the network

Acceptability and ties

Call an advisor–student or student–co-advisor pair acceptable when both participants strictly prefer the other to \(\bot\).

Refine each preference preorder to a strict order by breaking ties between types arbitrarily, while preserving every original strict comparison and preserving acceptability. This is possible because no partner is tied with \(\bot\).

The flow network uses these refined orders. In the stability proof, every strict comparison used by an original block remains strict after refinement.

A student type with no acceptable advisor or no acceptable co-advisor belongs to no mutually acceptable triple. Its allocation must therefore be zero. Discard these student types from the network, and discard advisor and co-advisor types having no acceptable neighbor among the remaining students. Every mutually acceptable triple survives this operation.

If no student type remains, the zero allocation is stable. Otherwise, write \(A_*,S_*,C_*\) for the retained types.

Vertices, edges, and capacities

Set

\(B=1+\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a+\sum_{s\in S}\sigma_s+\sum_{c\in C}\chi_c.\)

Create source \(\rho\), sink \(\omega\), a vertex for each retained advisor and co-advisor, and two vertices \(s^-,s^+\) for each retained student.

The edges are:

| Edge | Condition | Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| \(\rho\to a\) | \(a\in A_*\) | \(\alpha_a\) |
| \(a\to s^-\) | \((a,s)\) acceptable | \(B\) |
| \(s^-\to s^+\) | \(s\in S_*\) | \(\sigma_s\) |
| \(s^+\to c\) | \((s,c)\) acceptable | \(B\) |
| \(c\to\omega\) | \(c\in C_*\) | \(\chi_c\) |

At advisor \(a\), rank outgoing edges by \(a\)’s refined student preferences. At \(s^-\), rank incoming edges by \(s\)’s refined advisor preferences. At \(s^+\), rank outgoing edges by \(s\)’s refined co-advisor preferences. At co-advisor \(c\), rank incoming edges by \(c\)’s refined student preferences.

All other incoming or outgoing lists are singletons.

This is a finite directed network without loops or parallel edges. The source has only outgoing edges and the sink only incoming edges. Every nonterminal has both incoming and outgoing edges, by the definition of the retained types. All capacities are positive rationals and all relevant rankings are strict. Thus the stable-flow theorem applies.

Partner edges are always unsaturated

For any feasible flow, conservation and the quota edges imply

\(f(a,s^-)\leq f(\rho,a)\leq\alpha_a<B\)

and

\(f(s^+,c)\leq f(c,\omega)\leq\chi_c<B.\)

Consequently, every edge representing an acceptable adjacent pair is unsaturated. The artificial capacity \(B\) never restricts an allocation or a blocking path.

4. Turning a flow into complete student matches

Let \(f\) be the stable flow supplied by the theorem. Define

\(y_{as}=f(a,s^-),\qquad z_{sc}=f(s^+,c),\qquad t_s=f(s^-,s^+),\)

using zero for absent edges and discarded types.

Conservation gives

\(\sum_a y_{as}=t_s=\sum_c z_{sc},\)

\(\sum_s y_{as}=f(\rho,a)\leq\alpha_a,\)

and

\(\sum_s z_{sc}=f(c,\omega)\leq\chi_c,\qquad t_s\leq\sigma_s.\)

For each student type \(s\), couple the vectors \((y_{as})_a\) and \((z_{sc})_c\) into a nonnegative matrix \((x_{asc})_{a,c}\) with these row and column sums.

For completeness, this can be done greedily. While positive residual entries \(y_{as}\) and \(z_{sc}\) remain, assign

\(\delta=\min\{y_{as},z_{sc}\}\)

to \(x_{asc}\), and subtract \(\delta\) from both residual entries. Their residual totals remain equal, and each step exhausts at least one entry. The procedure therefore terminates with the prescribed margins.

A positive entry uses two acceptable adjacent pairs. Because all advisor–co-advisor pairs are compatible, its triple is mutually acceptable.

Thus \(x\) is a feasible allocation, with

\(u_a=\alpha_a-f(\rho,a),\qquad u_s=\sigma_s-t_s,\qquad u_c=\chi_c-f(c,\omega).\)

Every allocated student slice has both partners: only triples receive mass.

The quantities in the question can now be written entirely in terms of flow margins:

\(D_a(s)=u_a+\sum_{t:\,s\succ_a t}y_{at},\)

\(D_c(s)=u_c+\sum_{t:\,s\succ_c t}z_{tc}.\)

5. Proof of stability

We first translate positive improvement masses into paths satisfying the appropriate endpoint condition.

Left and right witness paths

Suppose \((a,s)\) is acceptable and \(D_a(s)>0\). Since all summands defining \(D_a(s)\) are nonnegative, at least one of the following holds:

  • If \(u_a>0\), use the path \((\rho,a,s^-)\). Its first edge is unsaturated because \(f(\rho,a)<\alpha_a\); its second edge is unsaturated by Section 3. It satisfies the start condition because it begins at \(\rho\).
  • Otherwise, some \(t\) satisfies \(s\succ_a t\) and \(y_{at}>0\). Use \((a,s^-)\). Advisor \(a\) strictly prefers this edge to the positive-flow edge \(a\to t^-\), including under the refined order.

In either case we obtain an unsaturated path \(P_L(a,s)\) ending at \(s^-\) and satisfying the blocking-walk start condition.

Similarly, if \((s,c)\) is acceptable and \(D_c(s)>0\), we obtain an unsaturated path \(P_R(s,c)\) starting at \(s^+\) and satisfying the end condition:

  • If \(u_c>0\), use \((s^+,c,\omega)\).
  • Otherwise, choose \(t\) with \(s\succ_c t\) and \(z_{tc}>0\), and use \((s^+,c)\). At its endpoint, \(c\) prefers the final edge to the positive-flow edge \(t^+\to c\).

Now suppose, for contradiction, that the extracted allocation has a block under the question’s definition. Its mutually acceptable triple survives the preprocessing.

Case 1: An unmatched student slice blocks

Here

\(u_s>0,\qquad D_a(s)>0,\qquad D_c(s)>0.\)

The middle edge is unsaturated because

\(f(s^-,s^+)=t_s=\sigma_s-u_s<\sigma_s.\)

Concatenate \(P_L(a,s)\), the edge \(s^-\to s^+\), and \(P_R(s,c)\). Every edge is unsaturated; the left witness supplies the start condition and the right witness supplies the end condition.

This is a blocking walk for \(f\), contradicting flow stability.

Case 2: A matched student slice improves its advisor

Here some \(x_{bsd}>0\) satisfies

\(a\succ_s b,\qquad D_a(s)>0.\)

The positive slice implies

\(f(b,s^-)=y_{bs}=\sum_c x_{bsc}>0.\)

Student vertex \(s^-\) strictly prefers incoming edge \(a\to s^-\) to incoming edge \(b\to s^-\), since the refinement preserves \(a\succ_s b\).

The path \(P_L(a,s)\) already satisfies the start condition. Its final edge therefore also satisfies the end condition at \(s^-\). It is a blocking walk, again a contradiction.

This short path represents precisely a change of advisor while retaining the current co-advisor.

Case 3: A matched student slice improves its co-advisor

Here some \(x_{bsd}>0\) satisfies

\(c\succ_s d,\qquad D_c(s)>0.\)

The positive slice implies

\(f(s^+,d)=z_{sd}=\sum_a x_{asd}>0.\)

Student vertex \(s^+\) strictly prefers outgoing edge \(s^+\to c\) to the positive-flow edge \(s^+\to d\).

Thus \(P_R(s,c)\) satisfies both endpoint conditions and is a blocking walk, a contradiction.

These cases exhaust the given blocking definition. Therefore the extracted allocation is stable under the original preorders.

6. Exact rational output and polynomial bit complexity

Write the input masses as positive fractions \(p_i/q_i\), and set

\(Q=\prod_i q_i.\)

The binary length of \(Q\) is at most the sum of the denominator lengths, hence \(O(L)\). Multiply all network capacities by \(Q\). They become integers, including the partner-edge capacity

\(QB=Q+\sum_i p_i(Q/q_i).\)

Every scaled capacity has \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits.

Run the cited stable-flow algorithm on this scaled network. Its integer output follows directly from its operations: starting from zero, each augmentation adds or subtracts a bottleneck residual capacity, which is an integer whenever the current flow and capacities are integers.

Moreover, intermediate flows remain between zero and their capacities. Residual capacities, bottlenecks, and updated edge flows therefore also have polynomially many bits. Consequently, the polynomial arithmetic-operation bound gives polynomial binary running time.

Divide the resulting flow by \(Q\). Scaling preserves saturation, positivity, and every preference comparison, so this is a stable flow for the original network.

Perform the greedy coupling in scaled integer units and divide the resulting triple masses by \(Q\). The output allocation is exact, with each mass an integer multiple of \(1/Q\). The coupling creates at most \(|A_*|+|C_*|-1\) positive entries for each student type with positive matched mass.

Finally, the network has

\(|V|=|A_*|+2|S_*|+|C_*|+2=O(\tau)\)

and

\(|E|\leq\tau+|A||S|+|S||C|=O(\tau+m^2).\)

Thus stable-flow computation uses \(O(\tau(\tau+m^2))\) arithmetic operations on numbers of polynomial bit length. Network construction, preference refinement, coupling, and even writing a dense allocation with at most \(m^3\) entries all take polynomial time.

No expansion into individual agents or unit quotas is required.
