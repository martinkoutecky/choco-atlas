The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite resource set \(C\), a finite set of complete tenant types \(T\), and a finite ordered sequence of \(K\) arrival stages, stage \(k\) brings a rational mass \(\rho_k\) of type \(t_k\). Type \(t\) has per-unit-mass resource demand \(d_c(t)\) and revenue \(v(t)\); resource capacities are one and costs are \(f_c(y)=q_c y\), under Theorem 2’s earning-density assumptions. At each stage, a deterministic causal policy chooses an accepted mass \(x_k\in[0,\rho_k]\), subject to \(\sum_{i\le k}d_c(t_i)x_i\le1\) for every resource \(c\) and prefix \(k\). What is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio against an offline policy that knows the full stage sequence, and does fractional cohort acceptance improve on Theorem 2’s ratio?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) resources, each of capacity \(1\). A complete tenant type \(t\) specifies rational demands \(d_c(t)\in[0,1]\), with at least one positive demand, and rational revenue \(v(t)>0\), all per unit mass.
Known rational parameters satisfy \(0<q_c<\underline p_c\leq\overline p_c<\infty\); costs are \(f_c(y)=q_cy\), and every type satisfies \(\underline p_c\leq v(t)/d_c(t)\leq\overline p_c\) whenever \(d_c(t)>0\).
A society is encoded by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each with binary rational mass \(M(t)>0\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; total mass need not equal \(1\). Running-time parameters are \(m,\tau,K,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.
An adversarial finite sequence \(S=((t_k,\rho_k))_{k=1}^{K}\), with rational \(\rho_k>0\), satisfies \(M(t)=\sum_{k:t_k=t}\rho_k\). Initially a policy knows only the resources, costs and density bounds; the support, total masses, horizon and future stages are not disclosed. At stage \(k\), it observes the complete current type and cohort mass.
A deterministic causal policy irrevocably chooses \(x_k\in[0,\rho_k]\), with \(\sum_{i\leq k}d_c(t_i)x_i\leq1\) for every resource and prefix; rejected mass cannot be recalled. Acceptance scales every demand and revenue proportionally.
Its welfare is \(W(x;S)=\sum_k\bigl(v(t_k)-\sum_c q_cd_c(t_k)\bigr)x_k\). Let \(\operatorname{OPT}(S)\) maximize this same welfare over fractional feasible decisions with full sequence knowledge.
For fixed resources, costs and density bounds, define \(\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=\inf\{\alpha\geq1:\exists A\ \forall S,\ W_A(S)\geq\operatorname{OPT}(S)/\alpha\}\), over deterministic causal policies and all admissible finite sequences. Define the comparison value \(\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}=\max_{c\in C}\left[1+\ln\left(\frac{\sum_{c'\in C}(\overline p_{c'}-q_{c'})}{\underline p_c-q_c}\right)\right]\).

What is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio \(\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}\), and for which admissible resource and density parameters, if any, is \(\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}<\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}\)?

For indivisible tenants, Zhao et al. report that DPoS attains \(\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}\), optimally among online algorithms ([published result](https://dsg.tuwien.ac.at/~sd/papers/Zeitschriftenartikel_2022_SD_DPoS.pdf)).
Yang et al. establish logarithmic competitive guarantees for fractional multidimensional knapsack under positive bounded aggregate profit density; they do not characterize the optimum for the welfare and coordinatewise density assumptions above ([primary source](https://ali-zeynali.github.io/CompetitiveAlgorithmsforOnlineMultidimensionalKnapsackProblems.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified social welfare as revenue minus total linear resource costs, and gave the offline benchmark the same fractional acceptance domain.
  • Made the complete current type observable while withholding future types, aggregate support masses and the horizon; the support table encodes the society without supplying advance online advice.
  • Allowed arbitrary positive rational total mass without probability normalization, preserving the discrete model's unrestricted population size.
  • Excluded zero-demand types and restricted unit-mass demands to individually feasible resource amounts; specified positive rational revenues, costs and finite density bounds.
  • Specified irrevocable proportional acceptance, with no recall of rejected mass or removal of accepted mass.
  • Defined competitiveness through a welfare inequality over all admissible finite sequences, avoiding undefined ratios when optimal welfare is zero.
  • Used the stated DPoS ratio as the comparison value, rather than silently treating whole cohorts as single indivisible tenants.

Partial solution and scope

I do not determine the optimum for all admissible parameters. I prove:

  • An exact criterion for whether $\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}$ is finite.
  • The exact optimum when profitable types use only one resource.
  • The exact optimum for two resources with arbitrary fixed revenue densities, extending the equal-density case in the notes.
  • A new lower bound combining increasing welfare levels with subsequent singleton demand.

The fourth result shows that $\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}$ can grow arbitrarily large across a family of finite-ratio instances while $\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}$ remains bounded, even though every admissible type has positive welfare.

All proofs below concern the fractional model in the question directly. They do not transfer the optimality claim from the cited [DPoS formulation](https://dsg.tuwien.ac.at/~sd/papers/Zeitschriftenartikel_2022_SD_DPoS.pdf) or the guarantees from the cited [fractional knapsack formulation](https://ali-zeynali.github.io/CompetitiveAlgorithmsforOnlineMultidimensionalKnapsackProblems.pdf).

1. Welfare geometry and the exact finiteness criterion

Write
$$ \ell_c=\underline p_c,\qquad u_c=\overline p_c,\qquad h_c=u_c-q_c,\qquad H=\sum_{c\in C}h_c. $$
The comparison value simplifies to
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}} = 1+\ln\frac{H}{\min_{c\in C}(\ell_c-q_c)}. $$

For a type $t$, let
$$ w(t)=v(t)-\sum_cq_cd_c(t) $$
be its welfare per unit mass. Its demanded set is the nonempty set
$$ J(t)=\{c:d_c(t)>0\}. $$

For each nonempty $J\subseteq C$, define
$$ a_J=1-\sum_{c\in J}\frac{q_c}{\ell_c}, \qquad b_J=1-\sum_{c\in J}\frac{q_c}{u_c}. $$
If $J(t)=J$, setting $r_c=d_c(t)/v(t)$ gives
$$ \frac1{u_c}\le r_c\le\frac1{\ell_c}, \qquad \frac{w(t)}{v(t)}=1-\sum_{c\in J}q_cr_c. $$
Therefore
$$ a_J\le \frac{w(t)}{v(t)}\le b_J. $$

Conversely, every rational vector in this rectangle is realizable: choose a sufficiently small positive rational $v$, and set $d_c=vr_c$ on $J$. Then every demand is at most $1$, and the specified density bounds hold.

Theorem 1.
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=+\infty \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists\,\varnothing\ne J\subseteq C: \sum_{c\in J}\frac{q_c}{u_c}<1 \le \sum_{c\in J}\frac{q_c}{\ell_c}. } $$

Here $+\infty$ means that no finite competitive guarantee exists.

Proof of the infinite-ratio direction

Suppose $a_J\le0<b_J$. By rational interpolation between the two endpoint vectors of the rectangle, there is a rational vector $r^0$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{c\in J}q_cr_c^0=1. $$
Put $r_c^+=1/u_c$. For rational $h\in(0,b_J]$, define
$$ r_c(h)= \left(1-\frac{h}{b_J}\right)r_c^0+ \frac{h}{b_J}r_c^+. $$
Then
$$ 1-\sum_{c\in J}q_cr_c(h)=h, \qquad \frac1{u_c}\le r_c(h)\le\frac1{\ell_c}. $$

Let
$$ D=\max_{c\in J}\frac1{\ell_c}, \qquad \theta=\frac1D. $$
Use types having revenue $\theta$, demands $\theta r_c(h)$ on $J$, and zero demands elsewhere. These demands are at most $1$, and their welfare is $\theta h>0$.

For an arbitrary positive integer $N$, present the levels
$$ h_i=b_J2^{\,i-N}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,N, $$
with cohort masses
$$ \rho_i=\frac1{\theta\max_{c\in J}r_c(h_i)}. $$
Each cohort alone is feasible in its entirety. Hence the offline optimum of the prefix ending at stage $i$ is at least
$$ \theta h_i\rho_i = \frac{h_i}{\max_c r_c(h_i)} \ge \frac{h_i}{D}. $$

Suppose a policy has a finite guarantee $\alpha$. Its decisions on this sequence must satisfy that guarantee on every prefix, since the horizon is undisclosed. Let
$$ z_i=\theta x_i,\qquad W_i=\sum_{j=1}^ih_jz_j,\qquad W_0=0. $$
Thus $W_i\ge h_i/(\alpha D)$. Summation by parts gives
$$ \sum_{i=1}^Nz_i = \frac{W_N}{h_N} + \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} W_i\left(\frac1{h_i}-\frac1{h_{i+1}}\right). $$
Since $h_{i+1}=2h_i$,
$$ \sum_{i=1}^Nz_i \ge \frac{N+1}{2\alpha D}. $$
For any fixed $c\in J$, feasibility also gives
$$ 1\ge\sum_{i=1}^Nr_c(h_i)z_i \ge\frac1{u_c}\sum_{i=1}^Nz_i. $$
Consequently
$$ \alpha\ge\frac{N+1}{2Du_c}. $$
As $N$ is arbitrary, no finite $\alpha$ is possible. All constructed inputs are rational, and all presented types have positive welfare.

Proof of the finite-ratio direction

Suppose no subset satisfies $a_J\le0<b_J$. Then $b_J>0$ implies $a_J>0$. Define
$$ \delta= \min_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq C:\ b_J>0}a_J>0. $$
The indexing set is nonempty because every singleton has $a_{\{c\}}>0$.

Every positive-welfare type satisfies
$$ w(t)\ge\delta v(t). $$
Consider the policy that rejects nonpositive-welfare types and accepts as much as feasible of every positive-welfare cohort.

If it accepts all positive-welfare mass, it is optimal. Otherwise a resource $c$ becomes saturated. For every accepted type,
$$ w(t)\ge\delta v(t)\ge\delta\ell_cd_c(t), $$
where the second inequality is also valid when $d_c(t)=0$. Thus its final welfare is at least
$$ \delta\ell_c\ge\delta\min_c\ell_c. $$

For every type, the coordinatewise upper bounds imply
$$ v(t)\le\sum_cu_cd_c(t). $$
Therefore
$$ w(t)\le\sum_ch_cd_c(t), $$
and every offline feasible solution has welfare at most $H$. We obtain the finite guarantee
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} \le \frac{H}{\delta\min_c\ell_c}. } $$
This proves the theorem.

2. Exact optimum when profitable types are independent

We first prove the one-resource result, including the policy-definition repair identified in the verifier report.

Lemma 2. For one resource, suppose welfare per unit resource lies in $[l,u]$, where $0<l\le u$. Then
$$ \boxed{\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=1+\ln\frac ul.} $$

Lower bound

For $l<u$, take a rational grid
$$ l=r_0<r_1<\cdots<r_n=u. $$
At stage $i$, offer one unit of resource at welfare density $r_i$. In the original model, this is a type with demand $1$, revenue $q+r_i$, and mass $1$.

Let $s_i$ be accepted resource and $W_i=\sum_{j=0}^ir_js_j$. The prefix optimum is $r_i$, so $W_i\ge r_i/\alpha$. Consequently
$$ 1\ge\sum_{i=0}^ns_i = \frac{W_n}{r_n} + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} W_i\left(\frac1{r_i}-\frac1{r_{i+1}}\right) \ge \frac1\alpha \left[ 1+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{r_{i+1}-r_i}{r_{i+1}} \right]. $$
Uniform rational grids make the sum converge to $\ln(u/l)$. Hence $\alpha\ge1+\ln(u/l)$. For $l=u$, the lower bound is $1$.

Matching policy

When $l<u$, put $R=1+\ln(u/l)$ and define
$$ \phi(y)= \begin{cases} l,&0\le y\le1/R,\\ l\exp(Ry-1),&1/R<y\le1. \end{cases} $$
Here $y$ is current resource utilization, and $\phi(1)=u$.

For a cohort of offered resource amount $b$ and welfare density $r$:

  • If $r<\phi(y)$, accept zero.
  • Otherwise accept the largest $s\in[0,\min(b,1-y)]$ satisfying $\phi(y+s)\le r$.

This explicitly defines rejection when the density is below the current threshold.

Let
$$ F(y)=\int_0^y\phi(s)\,ds. $$
Accepted increments have density at least their threshold, so $W_A\ge F(y)$. Direct integration gives
$$ F(y)=\frac{\phi(y)}R \qquad\text{when }y\ge1/R. $$

If final utilization is below $1/R$, every cohort was fully accepted, and the policy is optimal. Otherwise let $\lambda=\phi(y)$. For offered amounts $b_i$, densities $r_i$, and any offline feasible amounts $z_i$,
$$ \sum_ir_iz_i \le \lambda+\sum_ib_i(r_i-\lambda)_+. $$
Indeed, $\sum_i z_i\le1$ and $z_i\le b_i$.

Every cohort with $r_i>\lambda$ was fully accepted. Its contribution $b_i(r_i-\lambda)$ is at most the integral of $r_i-\phi(s)$ over its accepted increments. All other accepted increments contribute nonnegatively to this integral. Therefore
$$ \sum_ib_i(r_i-\lambda)_+ \le W_A-F(y). $$
It follows that
$$ \operatorname{OPT} \le \lambda+W_A-F(y) = W_A+(R-1)F(y) \le RW_A. $$
For $l=u$, greedy acceptance is optimal. This proves the lemma.

In particular, restricting the adversary to any single resource gives
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} \ge \max_c\left[1+\ln\frac{u_c-q_c}{\ell_c-q_c}\right]. $$

Theorem 3. If
$$ \frac{q_c}{u_c}+\frac{q_{c'}}{u_{c'}}\ge1 \qquad\text{for all distinct }c,c', $$
then
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} = \max_c\left[1+\ln\frac{u_c-q_c}{\ell_c-q_c}\right]. } $$
For every such instance with $m\ge2$,
$$ \boxed{\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}<\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}.} $$

Proof. Every demanded set of size at least two has $b_J\le0$, so every multiresource type has nonpositive welfare. Rejecting those types leaves independent one-resource problems. Applying Lemma 2 to each resource and adding the welfare guarantees proves the upper bound; the single-resource restriction proves equality.

Choose $c_*$ attaining the displayed maximum. Since $m\ge2$,
$$ H>u_{c_*}-q_{c_*}. $$
Hence
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}} \ge 1+\ln\frac{H}{\ell_{c_*}-q_{c_*}} > 1+\ln\frac{u_{c_*}-q_{c_*}}{\ell_{c_*}-q_{c_*}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}. $$

The hypothesis is exactly the absence of profitable multiresource types: if a pair violates it, the corresponding upper-density endpoint realizes positive welfare.

For $m=1$, Lemma 2 instead gives
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}. $$

3. A lower bound combining density variation and future demand

The following result is useful outside the exact classes above.

Choose a nonempty demanded set $J$ and rational numbers $g_c\in(0,1]$ for $c\in J$, with
$$ \max_{c\in J}g_c=1. $$
Set $g_c=0$ outside $J$, and define
$$ Q=\sum_{c\in J}q_cg_c,\qquad V_{\min}=\max_{c\in J}\ell_cg_c,\qquad V_{\max}=\min_{c\in J}u_cg_c. $$
Assume
$$ V_{\min}\le V_{\max}, \qquad l=V_{\min}-Q>0, \qquad u=V_{\max}-Q. $$
Thus a type with demand vector $g$ can have any rational welfare in $[l,u]$.

For a nonempty $I\subseteq J$, put
$$ H_I=\sum_{c\in I}h_c, \qquad G_I=\sum_{c\in I}h_cg_c. $$

Theorem 4. Whenever $G_I\ge u$,
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} \ge \max\left\{ 1+\ln\frac ul,\; 1+\frac{G_I\left(1+\ln(u/l)\right)-u}{H_I} \right\}. } $$
The choice $I=J$ always satisfies $G_I\ge u$.

Proof

Take a rational grid
$$ l=r_0<r_1<\cdots<r_n=u. $$
At stage $i$, offer mass $1$ of a type with demands $g$ and revenue $Q+r_i$. The revenue lies in $[V_{\min},V_{\max}]$, so every coordinatewise density constraint holds.

Since $\max_cg_c=1$, the prefix optimum is exactly $r_i$: total accepted mass cannot exceed $1$, and accepting the last cohort in full achieves $r_i$.

Let $x_i$ be the policy's accepted masses, and write
$$ Z_n=\sum_{i=0}^nx_i, \qquad W_i=\sum_{j=0}^ir_jx_j. $$
An $\alpha$-competitive policy satisfies $W_i\ge r_i/\alpha$. The calculation from Lemma 2 yields
$$ \alpha\ge 1+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{r_{i+1}-r_i}{r_{i+1}}. $$

Now append, for each $c\in I$, a singleton cohort having demand $1$, revenue $u_c$, and mass $1$. Offline can ignore the initial cohorts and obtain welfare $H_I$.

The initial accepted mass consumed $g_cZ_n$ of resource $c$. Therefore final online welfare is at most
$$ W_n+H_I-G_IZ_n. $$
Competitiveness implies
$$ \alpha\ge 1+\frac{G_I\alpha Z_n-\alpha W_n}{H_I}. $$

Summation by parts gives
$$ G_I\alpha Z_n-\alpha W_n = \left(\frac{G_I}{u}-1\right)\alpha W_n + G_I\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \alpha W_i \left(\frac1{r_i}-\frac1{r_{i+1}}\right). $$
All coefficients on the right are nonnegative because $G_I\ge u$. Substituting $\alpha W_i\ge r_i$ gives
$$ G_I\alpha Z_n-\alpha W_n \ge G_I-u+ G_I\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{r_{i+1}-r_i}{r_{i+1}}. $$
Passing to increasingly fine rational grids proves the claimed bound. If $l=u$, the same proof uses one initial cohort and an empty sum.

Finally,
$$ G_J=\sum_{c\in J}u_cg_c-Q \ge V_{\max}-Q=u. $$
Thus the full demanded set always qualifies.

Consequence: an arbitrarily large finite gap above the comparison value

Take two resources with
$$ \ell_1=\ell_2=1,\qquad u_1=u_2=2,\qquad q_1=q_2=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}, $$
where $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ is rational.

Every admissible type has positive welfare. Indeed, for every demanded set,
$$ \frac{w(t)}{v(t)} \ge 1-\sum_{c\in J(t)}q_c \ge\varepsilon. $$
Theorem 1 therefore gives a finite ratio; its greedy bound is
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}\le\frac{3+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}. $$

Apply Theorem 4 with $g_1=g_2=1$ and $I=\{1,2\}$. Then
$$ l=\varepsilon,\qquad u=1+\varepsilon,\qquad G_I=H_I=3+\varepsilon. $$
Consequently
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} \ge 2+\ln\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon} -\frac{1+\varepsilon}{3+\varepsilon}. } $$
This lower bound tends to $+\infty$ as $\varepsilon$ tends to zero. Meanwhile,
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}} = 1+\ln\frac{2(3+\varepsilon)}{1+\varepsilon} < 1+\ln6. $$

For a concrete rational example, choose $\varepsilon=1/3$, so $q_1=q_2=1/3$. Then
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} \ge\frac85+\ln4 > 1+\ln5 = \alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}}. $$
The strict inequality follows from
$$ \ln\frac54 = \int_1^{5/4}\frac{dx}{x} < \frac14 < \frac35. $$

Thus the comparison expression is not a universal upper bound for the fractional model in the question.

4. Exact optimum for two arbitrary fixed revenue densities

Assume now
$$ m=2,\qquad \ell_j=u_j=p_j,\qquad 0<q_j<p_j, \qquad j=1,2. $$
Define
$$ B_j=1-\frac{q_j}{p_j}, \qquad A=1-\frac{q_1}{p_1}-\frac{q_2}{p_2}, $$
and
$$ P=\min(p_1,p_2), \qquad H=B_1p_1+B_2p_2=(p_1-q_1)+(p_2-q_2). $$

Theorem 5. If $A\le0$, then $\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=1$. If $A>0$, then
$$ \boxed{ \alpha_{\mathrm{frac}} = 1+\max\left\{ \frac P H,\; \frac{Pq_2}{p_2(p_1-q_1)},\; \frac{Pq_1}{p_1(p_2-q_2)} \right\}. } $$

Reduction to three patterns

A demanded resource $j$ necessarily has $d_j=v/p_j$. Measure each acceptance by its accepted revenue mass
$$ z=vx. $$
Resource $j$ then has capacity $p_j$ in these units.

There are three patterns:

  • A singleton on resource $1$, with welfare $B_1z$.
  • A singleton on resource $2$, with welfare $B_2z$.
  • A joint type, consuming $z$ of both normalized capacities and having welfare $Az$.

When $A\le0$, reject joint types and accept singletons greedily. This is offline optimal, proving the first case. Henceforth assume $A>0$. Notice
$$ B_j>A,\qquad B_1+B_2-A=1. $$

Lower bound

First offer a joint cohort with normalized amount $P$. This is realizable by revenue $P$, demands $P/p_j$, and mass $1$.

Let $y$ be its accepted normalized amount. If the sequence ends there, competitiveness requires
$$ Ay\ge\frac{AP}{\alpha}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad y\ge\frac P\alpha. $$

If a singleton cohort of normalized amount $p_j$ subsequently arrives, offline welfare is at least $B_jp_j$, while online welfare is at most
$$ Ay+B_j(p_j-y) = B_jp_j-(B_j-A)y. $$
Therefore
$$ \alpha\ge 1+\frac{(B_j-A)P}{B_jp_j}. $$

Alternatively, append full singleton cohorts on both resources. Offline welfare is at least $H$, while online welfare is at most
$$ Ay+B_1(p_1-y)+B_2(p_2-y) = H-y. $$
Thus
$$ \alpha\ge1+\frac P H. $$

Since
$$ B_1-A=\frac{q_2}{p_2}, \qquad B_2-A=\frac{q_1}{p_1}, $$
these are exactly the three terms in the theorem.

Matching policy

Set
$$ R= 1+\max\left\{ \frac P H,\; \frac{(B_1-A)P}{B_1p_1},\; \frac{(B_2-A)P}{B_2p_2} \right\}, \qquad r=\frac1R. $$
Let $y$ be accepted joint amount and $s_j$ accepted singleton amount on resource $j$.

Accept singleton cohorts greedily. For a joint cohort of offered normalized amount $b$, accept
$$ \min\{rb,\ rP-y,\ p_1-y-s_1,\ p_2-y-s_2\}. $$
All entries are nonnegative under the maintained invariants. The policy is causal and feasible, with $0\le y\le rP$.

Let $T$ be total offered joint amount. Let $a_j$ be total offered singleton amount on resource $j$, truncated at $p_j$.

At termination,
$$ s_j=\min(a_j,p_j-y). $$
To justify this, if any singleton mass on resource $j$ was rejected, that resource became saturated and remains saturated, so $s_j=p_j-y$. Otherwise all offered singleton mass was accepted.

If neither resource is saturated at termination, no joint acceptance was capacity-limited. Hence in that case
$$ y=r\min(T,P). $$

Offline optimum

Offline accepts all $a_j$ singleton amounts before allocating joint amount. Indeed, if singleton amount on resource $j$ is unaccepted, either there is slack to accept it, or some joint amount can be replaced by it. The latter replacement improves welfare by $(B_j-A)$ per unit, which is strictly positive.

Consequently
$$ \operatorname{OPT} = B_1a_1+B_2a_2+ A\min(T,p_1-a_1,p_2-a_2). $$
Online welfare is
$$ W_A = Ay+B_1\min(a_1,p_1-y)+B_2\min(a_2,p_2-y). $$

Competitive inequality

If neither resource is saturated, both singleton amounts are fully accepted and $y=r\min(T,P)$. Since the offline joint amount is at most $\min(T,P)$,
$$ W_A\ge r\operatorname{OPT}. $$

Otherwise choose a saturated resource $j$, and let $k$ be the other resource. Then $a_j\ge p_j-y$. Bounding offline joint amount by $p_j-a_j$ gives
$$ \begin{aligned} W_A-r\operatorname{OPT} \ge{}& Ay+B_j(p_j-y)+B_k\min(a_k,p_k-y)\\ &-r\bigl[B_ja_j+B_ka_k+A(p_j-a_j)\bigr]. \end{aligned} $$
The coefficient of $a_j$ is $-r(B_j-A)<0$. Replacing $a_j$ by its upper bound $p_j$ therefore gives
$$ W_A-r\operatorname{OPT} \ge B_jp_j(1-r)-(B_j-A)y + B_k\bigl[\min(a_k,p_k-y)-ra_k\bigr]. $$

The bracketed expression is concave and piecewise linear in $a_k\in[0,p_k]$, so its minimum occurs at an endpoint. Its endpoint values are $0$ and $p_k(1-r)-y$. Thus
$$ W_A-r\operatorname{OPT} \ge \min\left\{ B_jp_j(1-r)-(B_j-A)y,\; H(1-r)-y \right\}. $$
Both expressions decrease with $y$. Using $y\le rP$ gives
$$ W_A-r\operatorname{OPT} \ge \min\left\{ B_jp_j(1-r)-(B_j-A)rP,\; H(1-r)-rP \right\}. $$
Both entries are nonnegative by the definition of $R=1/r$. Therefore
$$ W_A\ge r\operatorname{OPT}. $$
This matches the lower bound and proves the theorem.

5. Exact comparison in the fixed-density two-resource class

For the parameters of Theorem 5,
$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{DPoS}} = 1+\ln\frac{H}{\min(p_1-q_1,p_2-q_2)}. $$

When $A\le0$, strict improvement always holds because $\alpha_{\mathrm{frac}}=1$ and the displayed logarithm is positive.

When $A>0$, the exact necessary and sufficient condition within this class is
$$ \boxed{ \max\left\{ \frac P H,\; \frac{Pq_2}{p_2(p_1-q_1)},\; \frac{Pq_1}{p_1(p_2-q_2)} \right\} < \ln\frac{H}{\min(p_1-q_1,p_2-q_2)}. } $$

Every term inside the maximum is strictly below $1$. For the first, use
