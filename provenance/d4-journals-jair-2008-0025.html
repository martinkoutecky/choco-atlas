The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and \(0\)-\(1\) additive valuations \(u_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}v_{t,g}\), does there exist a mass allocation \(z_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) satisfying \(\sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}z_{t,B}\le q_g\), envy-freeness, and Pareto efficiency?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) goods \(G\), nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and explicitly given values \(v_{t,g}\in\{0,1\}\), with \(u_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}v_{t,g}\).
An allocation assigns actual integral bundles to individuals: nonnegative real masses \(z_{t,B}\), indexed by \(B\subseteq G\), satisfy \(\sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}z_{t,B}\le q_g\). Empty bundles and disposal are allowed; there are no transfers.
Envy-freeness means \(u_t(B)\ge u_t(C)\) whenever \(z_{t,B}>0\) and \(z_{s,C}>0\), including \(s=t\).
A Pareto improvement consists of another feasible allocation \(z'\) and nonnegative real transport masses \(w_{t,B,C}\) satisfying \(\sum_Cw_{t,B,C}=z_{t,B}\), \(\sum_Bw_{t,B,C}=z'_{t,C}\), and \(w_{t,B,C}>0\Rightarrow u_t(C)\ge u_t(B)\), with \(\sum_{t,B,C:u_t(C)>u_t(B)}w_{t,B,C}>0\). Pareto efficiency means no such improvement exists; competing allocations need not be envy-free.
Is deciding whether an envy-free, Pareto-efficient mass allocation exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\).
For finite agents and single-copy indivisible goods, existence with binary additive valuations is NP-complete ([Bouveret–Lang](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~lang/papers/bouveret-lang08.pdf)).
For finite agents, envy-free and Pareto-efficient lotteries exist under expected-utility comparisons and swap-closed feasibility ([Cole–Tao](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07257)); this concerns ex ante fairness, whereas the conditions above compare realized bundles. No result settling the stated mass problem was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one and took supplies to be nonnegative binary rationals, consistent with per-capita quantities.
  • Interpreted mass allocations as assignments of realized integral bundles, with arbitrary nonnegative real masses, rather than lotteries evaluated by expected utility.
  • Made envy-freeness apply to every pair of positive-mass bundle classes, including individuals of the same type; utility ties are allowed.
  • Defined Pareto improvements through within-type transport preserving each individual's utility and strictly improving a positive mass of individuals, rather than comparing type-average utilities.
  • Allowed empty bundles, disposal, and unrestricted feasible reallocations in Pareto comparisons, as in the discrete allocation definition.
  • Used positive support size and a nonempty goods set; allowed zero valuations and zero supplies because the anchor imposes no positivity restriction on these.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when all masses and supplies are dyadic rationals, every supply is positive, \(\tau=m\), and every type except one values at most three goods.

The proof below applies directly to the stated mass model. The neighboring results of [Bouveret–Lang, Proposition 21](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~lang/papers/bouveret-lang08.pdf) and [Cole–Tao](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07257) concern finite agents and ex ante comparisons, respectively.

1. Characterizing Pareto efficiency

For each good \(g\), define its total valuing mass and its useful consumption by

\(M_g=\sum_{t:v_{t,g}=1}\mu_t,\qquad x_g(z)=\sum_{t:v_{t,g}=1}\sum_{B:g\in B}z_{t,B}.\)

Write

\(c_g=\min\{q_g,M_g\},\qquad W=\sum_{g\in G}c_g.\)

Every feasible allocation satisfies \(x_g(z)\le c_g\). Its total utility is

\(U(z)=\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}u_t(B)=\sum_g x_g(z)\le W.\)

Lemma 1. A feasible allocation is Pareto-efficient exactly when \(x_g(z)=c_g\) for every good \(g\), equivalently when \(U(z)=W\).

Proof. Suppose \(x_g(z)<c_g\) for some good. First remove \(g\) from every bundle assigned to a type that does not value it. This preserves all individuals’ utilities and leaves consumption of \(g\) equal to \(x_g(z)\).

Since \(x_g(z)<M_g\), there is a type \(t\) valuing \(g\) and a bundle \(B\) missing \(g\) with \(z_{t,B}>0\). Since \(x_g(z)<q_g\), choose

\(0<\delta\le\min\{z_{t,B},q_g-x_g(z)\}.\)

Give \(B\cup\{g\}\), instead of \(B\), to mass \(\delta\) of these individuals. Consumption of every other good is unchanged, and consumption of \(g\) remains at most \(q_g\).

These operations define an admissible transport: individuals losing an unvalued good retain their utility, the selected mass gains one utility unit, and everyone else retains their bundle. Thus the original allocation is not Pareto-efficient.

Conversely, suppose \(U(z)=W\). Any Pareto improvement with transport \(w\) would satisfy

\(U(z')-U(z)=\sum_{t,B,C}w_{t,B,C}\bigl(u_t(C)-u_t(B)\bigr)>0.\)

Every summand with positive transport is nonnegative, and strictly improving transport has positive total mass. This contradicts \(U(z')\le W\). Finally, because each \(x_g(z)\le c_g\), equality of their sums holds exactly when equality holds for every good. \(\square\)

This lemma uses the specified transport definition and allows the competing allocation to violate envy-freeness.

2. Uniform utilities and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Lemma 2. In any envy-free allocation, every type \(t\) has a single integer utility \(k_t\): every positively assigned bundle \(B\) satisfies \(u_t(B)=k_t\).

Proof. For two positively assigned bundles \(B,C\) of type \(t\), self-comparison gives both \(u_t(B)\ge u_t(C)\) and \(u_t(C)\ge u_t(B)\). Hence they are equal. Their common value is an integer between \(0\) and \(m\). \(\square\)

For an integer vector \(k=(k_1,\ldots,k_\tau)\), define

\(\mathcal B_t(k)=\{B\subseteq G:u_t(B)=k_t\text{ and }u_s(B)\le k_s\text{ for every }s\}.\)

An allocation has envy-free utility vector \(k\) exactly when it uses only bundles from \(\mathcal B_t(k)\) for each type \(t\). Its total utility is then \(\sum_t\mu_t k_t\).

Consequently, existence is equivalent to finding a vector \(k\) satisfying

\(\sum_t\mu_t k_t=W\)

for which the following linear system is feasible:

  • \(z_{t,B}\ge0\), for \(B\in\mathcal B_t(k)\);
  • \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_t(k)}z_{t,B}=\mu_t\), for every type \(t\);
  • \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}z_{t,B}\le q_g\), for every good \(g\).

Polynomial certificate size. Put \(R=\tau+m\). Add one nonnegative slack variable to each supply inequality. The resulting system has \(R\) equality rows, coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\), and rational right-hand sides.

If feasible, this system has a basic feasible solution with at most \(R\) positive variables, hence at most \(R\) positive bundle masses. To justify this support bound, more than \(R\) positive columns would be linearly dependent; a sufficiently small perturbation in both directions along that dependence would preserve feasibility, contradicting extremality.

These positive masses also have polynomial bit length. Take a common denominator of the input masses and supplies; its bit length is \(O(L)\). A nonsingular basis has order at most \(R\), and Hadamard’s inequality bounds its determinant by \(R^{R/2}\). Cramer’s rule therefore bounds each mass’s numerator and denominator bit lengths by \(O(L+R\log R)\).

Thus a certificate consists of at most \(m+\tau\) bundles, their type indices, and rational masses of polynomial bit length. Feasibility, all realized-bundle envy comparisons, and \(U(z)=W\) can be checked in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time. Lemma 1 verifies Pareto efficiency. The problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

3. Hardness reduction

We reduce from Independent Set on a simple graph \(\Gamma=([n],E)\), with threshold \(2\le K\le n\). This restriction remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete: instances outside this range are decidable directly and can be mapped to fixed yes- or no-instances within the range.

Let \(\ell=|E|\) and \(s=n+\ell\). Set

\(N=n+1+s(K+2),\qquad P=2^{\lceil\log_2 N\rceil},\qquad a=P^{-1},\qquad b=(K+2)a,\qquad \Delta=1-Na.\)

Then \(\Delta\ge0\), and all these numbers are dyadic rationals.

Construction

There are vertex goods \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), one additional good \(h\), and one distinct private good \(p_o\) for each auxiliary type \(o\) below.

| Type | Valued goods | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Distinguished type \(T_*\) | \(\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}\) | \(a\) |
| Receiver \(R_i\), for each vertex \(i\) | \(\{x_i,h\}\) | \(a\) |
| Vertex observer \(A_i\), for each vertex \(i\) | \(\{x_i,h,p_{A_i}\}\) | \(b\), except \(A_1\) has mass \(b+\Delta\) |
| Edge observer \(F_e\), for each edge \(e=\{i,j\}\) | \(\{x_i,x_j,p_{F_e}\}\) | \(b\) |

All unlisted values are zero. Supplies are

\(q_{x_i}=a,\qquad q_h=Ka,\qquad q_{p_o}=\mu_o.\)

The masses sum to

\((n+1)a+sb+\Delta=Na+\Delta=1.\)

Every mass and supply is positive. The valuation vectors are pairwise distinct, so the support size is exactly

\(\tau=1+n+s=2n+\ell+1=m.\)

The distinguished type values \(n\) goods; every other type values at most three.

All supplies are at most the corresponding valuing masses:

  • \(x_i\) is valued by \(T_*\), whose mass alone equals \(q_{x_i}\);
  • the receivers valuing \(h\) have total mass \(na\ge Ka=q_h\);
  • \(p_o\) is valued exclusively by type \(o\), of mass \(q_{p_o}\).

Therefore Lemma 1 says that efficiency requires every good’s entire supply to be consumed by individuals valuing it. Here

\(W=(n+K)a+\sum_o\mu_o,\)

where the sum ranges over all observers.

An independent set gives an envy-free, efficient allocation

Suppose \(I\subseteq[n]\) is an independent set of size \(K\). Assign one bundle to each type:

  • \(T_*\) receives \(\{x_i:i\in I\}\);
  • \(R_i\) receives \(\{h\}\) when \(i\in I\), and \(\{x_i\}\) otherwise;
  • every observer \(o\) receives \(\{p_o\}\).

Each vertex good is consumed by mass \(a\), either of \(T_*\) or its receiver. Good \(h\) is consumed by exactly \(K\) receivers, of total mass \(Ka\). Every private good is consumed by its owner’s entire mass. All supplies are therefore consumed by valuers, proving efficiency.

For envy-freeness:

  • \(T_*\) has utility \(K\), and values every other assigned singleton at most one.
  • Each receiver has utility one. It values the distinguished bundle at most one and every singleton at most one.
  • Each vertex observer has utility one and values the distinguished bundle at most one.
  • Each edge observer has utility one and values the distinguished bundle at most one because \(I\) is independent.
  • Every observer values every assigned singleton at most one.

These comparisons cover every pair of positively assigned bundles, proving envy-freeness.

Any envy-free, efficient allocation gives an independent set

Now suppose an envy-free, Pareto-efficient mass allocation exists. Let \(k_t\) be its uniform utilities from Lemma 2.

Step 1: Every observer has utility exactly one.

Efficiency forces useful consumption of \(p_o\) to equal \(\mu_o\). Since only observer \(o\) values \(p_o\), every positively assigned bundle of \(o\) contains \(p_o\). Thus \(k_o\ge1\).

For a vertex observer, its other valued goods have total supply

\(q_{x_i}+q_h=(K+1)a<b\le\mu_{A_i}.\)

For an edge observer, its other valued goods have total supply

\(q_{x_i}+q_{x_j}=2a<b\le\mu_{F_e}.\)

If \(k_o\ge2\), every individual of observer type \(o\) would receive at least one other valued good. Their consumption of those goods would be at least \(\mu_o\), contradicting the displayed supply bound. Hence \(k_o=1\). In particular, observers receive none of their other valued goods in positive-mass bundles.

Step 2: Every receiver has utility exactly one.

Vertex observer \(A_i\), whose utility is one, values both \(x_i\) and \(h\). Envy-freeness therefore prohibits any positively assigned receiver bundle from containing both. Thus \(k_{R_i}\le1\).

Efficiency requires useful consumption \(q_h=Ka>0\) of \(h\). Its valuers are receivers and vertex observers; Step 1 excludes the latter. Hence some positive-mass receiver bundle contains \(h\). Every receiver values that bundle at least one, so envy-freeness gives \(k_{R_i}\ge1\) for every \(i\).

Therefore \(k_{R_i}=1\) for all receivers.

Step 3: The distinguished type has utility exactly \(K\).

Using Steps 1–2, total utility is

\(U(z)=a k_{T_*}+na+\sum_o\mu_o.\)

Efficiency and Lemma 1 give

\(U(z)=W=(n+K)a+\sum_o\mu_o.\)

Because \(a>0\), these equalities imply \(k_{T_*}=K\).

Step 4: A distinguished bundle identifies an independent set.

The distinguished type has positive mass, so choose any positively assigned bundle \(B\) of that type. Define

\(I=\{i:x_i\in B\}.\)

Step 3 gives \(|I|=K\). For every edge \(e=\{i,j\}\), edge observer \(F_e\) has utility one and values both \(x_i,x_j\). Envy-freeness implies

\(|B\cap\{x_i,x_j\}|\le u_{F_e}(B)\le1.\)

Thus no edge has both endpoints in \(I\). The set \(I\) is independent.

This necessity argument permits arbitrary splitting across bundles and arbitrary real masses. It does not assume that a type receives only one bundle.

Reduction size

The construction has \(m=\tau=2n+\ell+1\). Since \(K\le n\), every numerical entry has \(O(\log(n+\ell))\) bits. The explicitly written valuation table and numerical data have total length \(O((n+\ell)^2)\), and the construction takes polynomial time.

The reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Together with Section 2, it proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

4. Running times in \(m,\tau,L\)

Certificate verification takes \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time.

An explicit deterministic algorithm enumerates all utility vectors \(k\in\{0,\ldots,m\}^{\tau}\). For each vector, it:

  • checks \(\sum_t\mu_t k_t=W\);
  • enumerates the at most \(\tau 2^m\) allowed type–bundle pairs;
  • solves the rational feasibility system from Section 2.

Polynomial-time linear programming therefore gives running time

\((m+1)^\tau\,2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

A polynomial-time algorithm for unrestricted instances would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
