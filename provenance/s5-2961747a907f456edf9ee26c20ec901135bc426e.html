The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support of complete agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), contract preferences, precedence-ordered slot classes with per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and priorities over type-contract categories, find a feasible mass assignment \(y_{t,x,s}\) that is slot-stable under sequential slot choice with fractional rationing at priority cutoffs and weakly rank-dominates every other slot-stable mass assignment for each type. Determine whether such an assignment always exists and how to compute it in terms of support size and input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) complete types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type represents a divisible population of otherwise identical unit-demand agents.
There are branches \(B\), contract options \(X_t\) for each type, and \(q\) slot classes partitioned among branches. Each option specifies its branch and terms; write \(m=|\bigcup_t X_t|\). Every branch has a strict precedence order on its classes, and each class \(s\) has a positive binary-rational capacity \(\kappa_s\).
Each type strictly ranks its options and an unmatched option \(\varnothing\). Each class strictly ranks eligible type-contract categories \((t,x)\) and its vacant option; options below either participant's outside option are unacceptable. Copies within a category have equal priority.
An agent ranks slot-labelled options \((x,s)\) first by contract preference and, for copies of the same contract, by earlier branch precedence. Being unmatched retains its original position.
Sequential branch choice fills classes in precedence order from acceptable offers of as-yet-unchosen agents, taking higher-priority categories first and splitting the offered mass of the cutoff category to fill remaining capacity. Once an agent portion is selected, all its other offers are removed; other portions of its type remain eligible.
A feasible assignment has \(y_{t,x,s}\geq0\), \(\sum_{x,s}y_{t,x,s}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,x}y_{t,x,s}\leq\kappa_s\); residual type mass is unmatched. Positive assignments must be mutually acceptable, and sequential choice from their projected contract offers must reproduce their slot-labelled allocation.
Such an assignment is slot-stable if no acceptable \((t,x,s)\) has both positive type mass assigned to strictly worse slot-labelled options (including unmatched) and either spare capacity at \(s\) or positive mass there in a strictly lower-priority category. Equal-priority copies cannot displace one another.
An assignment weakly rank-dominates another if, for every type and every cutoff in its original contract ranking including \(\varnothing\), its mass receiving contracts weakly above that cutoff is at least as large, aggregating over slot classes.
Does every instance admit a slot-stable assignment that weakly rank-dominates every other slot-stable assignment? Is finding such an assignment, or determining that none exists, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,q,|B|,L\), or NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
Discrete: the cumulative offer process computes the projection of the agent-optimal slot-stable outcome in polynomial time. [Kominers–Sönmez](https://www.tayfunsonmez.net/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Kominers_Sonmez_Slot-Specific_Priorities_Theory.pdf)

[Iwata–Yokoi](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf) A continuum slot-priority model also analyzes a single school's sequential choice under atomless scores. Result

Every instance admits a slot-stable assignment that weakly rank-dominates every other slot-stable assignment. Such an assignment can be computed in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,q,|B|,L\).

The proof uses a stable-allocation reduction. Two details are essential: stability makes the sequential-choice requirement automatic, and the continuous algorithm’s optimality guarantee implies dominance separately for each type.

1. The lifted allocation problem

Let \(E\) contain precisely the mutually acceptable triples \(e=(t,x,s)\), where \(s\) belongs to the branch specified by \(x\). Put \(N=|E|\); then \(N\leq \tau m q\).

Write \(E_t\) for the edges incident to type \(t\), and \(E_s\) for those incident to class \(s\). For a vector \(y\), write \(y(A)=\sum_{e\in A}y_e\).

Initially omit the sequential-choice requirement. The lifted feasible vectors are exactly those satisfying

\(y_e\geq 0,\qquad y(E_t)\leq\mu_t,\qquad y(E_s)\leq\kappa_s.\)

Each type strictly orders its incident edges by the specified slot-labelled preference. Each class strictly orders its incident edges by category priority. Thus different edges incident to either endpoint have distinct ranks; equal-priority copies are represented by mass on the same edge.

For \(e=(t,x,s)\), define type refusal to mean

\(y(E_t)=\mu_t\quad\text{and}\quad y_a>0,\ a\in E_t\ \Longrightarrow\ a\succeq_t e.\)

Define class refusal to mean

\(y(E_s)=\kappa_s\quad\text{and}\quad y_a>0,\ a\in E_s\ \Longrightarrow\ a\succeq_s e.\)

Type refusal fails exactly when there is positive unmatched mass or positive mass assigned to an edge strictly worse than \(e\). Here unmatched is worse than every edge retained in \(E\). Class refusal fails exactly when the class has spare capacity or positive mass in a strictly lower-priority category.

Consequently, the task’s no-blocking condition is equivalent to:

For every \(e\in E\), at least one endpoint refuses \(e\).

Call a lifted feasible vector satisfying this condition *lifted-stable*.

2. Stability forces sequential-choice consistency

Lemma. Every lifted-stable vector is reproduced by sequential branch choice from its projected contract offers.

Proof. Partition each type’s population into portions of masses \(y_{t,x,s}\) and an unmatched portion. Projecting the assignment gives each assigned portion its assigned contract offer. Portions assigned different contracts remain distinct, even when they have the same type.

Fix a branch, and process its classes in precedence order. Suppose inductively that choice has reproduced the assignment at all preceding classes. At the current class \(s\), the remaining offered mass of category \(c=(t,x)\) is therefore

\(a_c=\sum_{\substack{r\text{ in this branch}\\r=s\text{ or }r\text{ later than }s}}y_{t,x,r}.\)

Let \(u_c=y_{t,x,s}\), and let \(g_c\) be the mass chosen by the current class’s greedy priority rule. Restrict attention to categories acceptable at \(s\); all positive \(u_c\) lie among these categories.

Suppose \(g\neq u\), and let \(c\) be the highest-priority category where they differ. At higher-priority categories the vectors agree. Hence, with

\(R=\kappa_s-\sum_{d\succ_s c}u_d,\)

the greedy rule gives \(g_c=\min\{a_c,R\}\). Feasibility gives \(u_c\leq a_c\) and \(u_c\leq R\), so the first difference necessarily satisfies \(g_c>u_c\). In particular,

\(a_c>u_c\quad\text{and}\quad R>u_c.\)

The first inequality implies positive mass of category \(c\) is assigned to a later class \(r\). That mass strictly prefers \((x,s)\) to \((x,r)\), because the contract is the same and \(s\) has earlier precedence.

The second inequality, together with

\(R-u_c=\bigl(\kappa_s-y(E_s)\bigr)+\sum_{d\prec_s c}u_d,\)

implies either spare capacity at \(s\) or positive mass there in a lower-priority category. Thus \((t,x,s)\) blocks, contradicting lifted stability.

Choice therefore reproduces the current class’s assignment. Induction proves the assertion for this branch, and applying it to every branch completes the proof. \(\square\)

It follows that the lifted-stable vectors are exactly the slot-stable assignments in the question. The converse follows directly from the specified no-blocking condition.

3. Polymatroid representation and the algorithmic theorem

Define rank functions on \(E\) by

\(h(A)=\sum_{t:A\cap E_t\neq\varnothing}\mu_t,\qquad f(A)=\sum_{s:A\cap E_s\neq\varnothing}\kappa_s.\)

Both functions are normalized, nondecreasing, and submodular. Indeed, each is a positive weighted sum of functions \(A\mapsto \mathbf 1[A\cap R\neq\varnothing]\). Such a function satisfies the submodular inequality: if neither set meets \(R\), all terms vanish; if exactly one meets \(R\), the union meets \(R\) and the intersection does not; if both meet \(R\), the left side is \(2\) and the right side is at most \(2\).

For a rank function \(g\), define its polymatroid polytope by

\(P(g)=\{y\geq0:y(A)\leq g(A)\text{ for every }A\subseteq E\}.\)

Here,

\(P(h)=\{y\geq0:y(E_t)\leq\mu_t\text{ for every }t\},\)

and analogously \(P(f)\) expresses exactly the class capacities. To verify this, block-capacity inequalities imply every subset inequality by summing over its nonempty blocks; conversely, apply the subset inequality to each nonempty whole block.

Choose a total order \(\succ_H\) on \(E\) extending every type’s edge order, and a total order \(\succ_F\) extending every class’s priority order. These extensions exist: concatenate the type blocks for the first order and the class blocks for the second.

For completeness, an edge is *\(h\)-saturated* at \(y\) when no positive increase of that coordinate remains in \(P(h)\). For a saturated edge \(e\), its dependence set consists of the edges \(a\) for which some positive exchange \(y+\epsilon(\mathbf 1_e-\mathbf 1_a)\) remains feasible. For our rank function, if \(e\in E_t\),

\(e\in\operatorname{sat}_h(y)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ y(E_t)=\mu_t,\)

and, when saturated,

\(\operatorname{dep}_h(y,e)=\{e\}\cup\{a\in E_t:y_a>0\}.\)

These formulas follow directly from the block-capacity constraint. Corresponding formulas hold for \(f\) and class blocks. Thus the ordered-polymatroid stability condition—saturation with no worse dependent edge on at least one side—is exactly the refusal condition from Section 1.

We use the following established algorithmic guarantee. Iwata–Yokoi’s algorithm computes a stable vector \(y^\star\) in \(O(N^3\gamma)\) time, where \(\gamma\) is the cost of a saturation or exchange-capacity query. Its terminal invariants give, for every stable vector \(z\) and every edge \(e\),

\(y^\star_e\geq z_e\quad\text{or}\quad \bigl[e\in\operatorname{sat}_h(y^\star),\ \operatorname{dep}_h(y^\star,e)\succeq_H e\bigr].\)

This stronger edgewise guarantee appears in the proof of Lemma 7.1; its invariants are established in their optimality proof. Stability and running time are Theorems 5.9 and 6.6. [Iwata–Yokoi, *Finding a Stable Allocation in Polymatroid Intersection*](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf).

All hypotheses have now been checked: both rank functions are polymatroid ranks, both orders are total, and their stability notion agrees with lifted stability. The returned vector is therefore slot-stable by Section 2.

4. Optimality holds separately for every type

Fix another slot-stable assignment \(z\), a type \(t\), and any prefix \(A\) of that type’s incident edges.

If \(y^\star_e\geq z_e\) for every \(e\in A\), summation gives \(y^\star(A)\geq z(A)\).

Otherwise choose \(e\in A\) with \(z_e>y^\star_e\). The algorithmic guarantee forces the second alternative. By the explicit saturation and dependence formulas,

\(y^\star(E_t)=\mu_t,\qquad y^\star_a>0,\ a\in E_t\ \Longrightarrow\ a\succeq_t e.\)

Since \(A\) is a prefix containing \(e\), all positive mass of type \(t\) in \(y^\star\) lies in \(A\). Therefore

\(y^\star(A)=\mu_t\geq z(A).\)

Thus \(y^\star\) dominates \(z\) at every slot-labelled prefix of every type.

Now take a cutoff in the original contract ranking that lies above \(\varnothing\). The acceptable edges whose contracts are weakly above that cutoff form a slot-labelled prefix: all copies of each contract are consecutive, and contract preference takes precedence over slot precedence. The proved inequality therefore gives precisely the required cumulative contract-mass inequality.

At the cutoff \(\varnothing\), or any cutoff below it, both cumulative masses equal \(\mu_t\): all assigned contracts are acceptable, and residual mass is unmatched.

Hence \(y^\star\) weakly rank-dominates every other slot-stable assignment in the question’s original contract rankings.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

The reduction creates at most \(N\leq\tau m q\) edges and uses only the explicitly listed support types.

For either partition-capacity polymatroid, let \(R(e)\) denote the block containing \(e\), with quota \(K_{R(e)}\). Saturation capacity is

\(\widehat c(y,e)=K_{R(e)}-y(R(e)).\)

For distinct edges \(e,a\), exchange capacity is

\(\overline c(y,e,a)=y_a\quad\text{if }R(e)=R(a),\)

and otherwise

\(\overline c(y,e,a)=\min\{y_a,K_{R(e)}-y(R(e))\}.\)

These formulas follow by checking nonnegativity of the decreased coordinate and the quota of the increased block. Each query takes \(O(N)\) elementary arithmetic operations by direct summation. Thus the stated algorithm bound yields, conservatively, \(O(N^4)\) elementary operations, plus polynomial preprocessing.

To justify exact binary arithmetic, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and capacities. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Scaling every quota by \(D\) gives integer quotas with \(O(L)\) bits. Positive scaling preserves feasibility, blocking, and dominance.

The algorithm’s updates use additions, subtractions, and minima of saturation and exchange capacities. Starting from zero with integer quotas, these operations remain integral. Every allocation coordinate is bounded by its scaled type mass, hence by \(D\); quotas and query values also have polynomial bit length. Consequently, the polynomial operation bound gives a polynomial bit-time bound. Dividing the output by \(D\) produces an exact rational assignment of polynomial encoding length.

If \(E\) is empty, the all-unmatched assignment handles the instance directly.

Therefore existence is universal, and finding the required dominating slot-stable assignment is polynomial-time solvable.
