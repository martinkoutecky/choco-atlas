The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to \(1\), a target \(c\in C\), and one of Theorem 11’s distance limits, let \(R(t)\) be the rankings within that limit of \(t\). Decide whether there are \(x_{t,\rho}\geq 0\) for \(\rho\in R(t)\) such that \(\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and \(c\) is the unique Borda winner in the resulting society; equivalently, for every \(a\neq c\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}\bigl((|C|-\operatorname{pos}(c,\rho))-(|C|-\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho))\bigr)>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\geq 2\) candidates and \(c\in C\). Rankings are strict complete orders; \(\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho)\) is the position of \(a\), starting at \(1\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct rankings with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Consider separately three fixed distance constraints: Kendall swap distance at most \(1\), Spearman footrule distance at most \(2\), and maximum displacement at most \(1\), where maximum displacement is \(\max_{a\in C}|\operatorname{pos}(a,t)-\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho)|\).
For the chosen constraint, let \(R(t)\) contain all permitted destination rankings, including \(t\) itself; these sets are defined implicitly by the constraint.
Mass may be split arbitrarily: choose \(x_{t,\rho}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) with \(\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\). There are no prices or global budget constraints.
The decision problem asks whether such a redistribution makes \(c\) the unique Borda winner, equivalently whether \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\rho\in R(t)}x_{t,\rho}(\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho)-\operatorname{pos}(c,\rho))>0\) for every \(a\in C\setminus\{c\}\); ties are unsuccessful.

For each of the three fixed distance constraints, is this decision problem in \(\mathsf{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For indivisible, explicitly listed voters, unique-winner Borda local distance constrained bribery is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete under each of these constraints, without budget restrictions ([Dey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08711)).
No result settling exactly this mass-splitting problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Resolved 'one of the distance limits' into three separate fixed variants: swap bound 1, footrule bound 2, and maximum displacement bound 1.
  • Made the candidate domain explicit as at least two candidates, consistent with the nonconstant scoring-rule domain.
  • Specified that destination-ranking sets are implicit, rather than additional explicitly enumerated input.

Result

All three decision problems are in \(\mathsf{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)\). Each admits an exact linear programming formulation with \(\tau(m-1)+1\) variables.

The main point requiring proof is that, for maximum displacement at most \(1\), simple inequalities on adjacent-swap masses describe precisely the realizable redistributions.

1. Characterizing the permitted rankings

Fix a source ranking \(t=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)\). Let edge \(i\) mean swapping the candidates originally at positions \(i\) and \(i+1\).

Kendall swap distance at most \(1\). By the definition as the minimum number of adjacent swaps, the permitted destinations are \(t\) and the \(m-1\) rankings obtained by one adjacent swap.

Spearman footrule distance at most \(2\). Write \(d_a=\operatorname{pos}(a,\rho)-\operatorname{pos}(a,t)\). These are integers satisfying \(\sum_a d_a=0\). If \(\sum_a|d_a|\leq2\), then either all displacements vanish, or exactly one candidate has displacement \(+1\) and exactly one has displacement \(-1\). In the latter case, all other candidates retain their positions, so the two moving candidates exchange positions. Their positions are adjacent. Thus this neighborhood is exactly the Kendall neighborhood above.

Maximum displacement at most \(1\). The permitted destinations are precisely those obtained by a set of pairwise disjoint adjacent swaps.

To prove this, inspect positions from left to right. Suppose positions before \(i\) have already been accounted for by fixed candidates or adjacent swaps. If \(a_i\) occupies position \(i\), proceed to the next position. Otherwise, its displacement bound forces it to occupy position \(i+1\): earlier positions are already occupied. The candidate occupying position \(i\) must originally have been at position \(i-1\), \(i\), or \(i+1\). The first possibility is already accounted for, and the second candidate has moved, so that candidate is \(a_{i+1}\). Consequently, positions \(i,i+1\) form an adjacent swap; proceed past both. This proves the characterization. Conversely, disjoint adjacent swaps move every candidate by at most one position.

2. An exact description using swap masses

For every \(t\in T\) and \(1\leq i\leq m-1\), introduce \(y_{t,i}\), the total mass originating at \(t\) whose destination uses edge \(i\). Set \(y_{t,0}=y_{t,m}=0\) as constants.

Kendall and footrule

The realizable swap masses are exactly those satisfying

\[ y_{t,i}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}y_{t,i}\leq\mu_t. \]

Necessity follows because each destination uses at most one swap. For sufficiency, send mass \(y_{t,i}\) to the destination obtained by swapping edge \(i\), and send the remaining mass \(\mu_t-\sum_i y_{t,i}\) to \(t\).

Maximum displacement

The realizable swap masses are exactly those satisfying

\[ y_{t,i}\geq0,\qquad y_{t,i-1}+y_{t,i}\leq\mu_t \quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq m. \]

Necessity follows because a matching cannot contain both edges incident with the same position.

Here is a constructive proof of sufficiency. Fix \(t\), abbreviate \(\mu=\mu_t\) and \(y_i=y_{t,i}\), and associate to each edge the interval

\[ A_i= \begin{cases} [0,y_i),&i\text{ odd},\\ [\mu-y_i,\mu),&i\text{ even}. \end{cases} \]

The stated inequalities imply \(0\leq y_i\leq\mu\), so these intervals lie in \([0,\mu)\). They also imply that neighboring intervals are disjoint: the left interval ends no later than the right interval begins.

For every \(s\in[0,\mu)\), define \(M(s)=\{i:s\in A_i\}\). Since neighboring intervals are disjoint, \(M(s)\) is a matching. Its corresponding ranking therefore satisfies the displacement constraint.

Partition \([0,\mu)\) at all interval endpoints. On each resulting half-open subinterval, \(M(s)\) is constant. Assign that subinterval’s length as mass to its corresponding ranking, aggregating equal rankings if necessary. The total assigned mass is \(\mu\), and the mass using edge \(i\) is exactly the length of \(A_i\), namely \(y_i\).

This proves sufficiency using a finite redistribution. There are at most \(m-1\) interior endpoints, hence at most \(m\) destination rankings in this construction.

3. Computing Borda margins from swap masses

Write \(p_t(a)=\operatorname{pos}(a,t)\). A candidate initially at position \(i\) moves down one position when edge \(i\) is used and up one position when edge \(i-1\) is used. These events are mutually exclusive in every permitted destination.

Consequently, the total mass-weighted position of candidate \(a\) is exactly

\[ P_a(y)= \sum_{t\in T} \left( \mu_t p_t(a)+y_{t,p_t(a)}-y_{t,p_t(a)-1} \right). \]

Since total mass is \(1\), the Borda score of \(a\), under scores \(m-1,\ldots,0\), is \(m-P_a(y)\). Therefore the score advantage of \(c\) over \(a\) is

\[ D_a(y)=P_a(y)-P_c(y) = \sum_{t\in T} \left[ \mu_t\bigl(p_t(a)-p_t(c)\bigr) +y_{t,p_t(a)}-y_{t,p_t(a)-1} -y_{t,p_t(c)}+y_{t,p_t(c)-1} \right]. \]

Thus \(c\) is the unique Borda winner exactly when \(D_a(y)>0\) for every \(a\ne c\). The realizability proofs above establish this equivalence in both directions.

4. An exact polynomial-time algorithm

Introduce a common-margin variable \(\varepsilon\). Maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to:

  • The appropriate swap-mass constraints from Section 2.
  • \(D_a(y)\geq\varepsilon\) for every \(a\in C\setminus\{c\}\).
  • \(-m\leq\varepsilon\leq m\).

Let the optimum be \(\varepsilon^\star\).

The feasible region is nonempty: take every swap mass to be zero and \(\varepsilon=-m\). This is feasible because every position difference lies between \(-(m-1)\) and \(m-1\). The region is closed and bounded, since \(0\leq y_{t,i}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\varepsilon\) is bounded. Hence the optimum is attained.

The decision algorithm accepts exactly when \(\varepsilon^\star>0\).

Indeed, if a successful redistribution exists, its swap masses satisfy the appropriate constraints. There are finitely many rivals, so the minimum of its strictly positive margins is positive. Using this minimum as \(\varepsilon\) gives a feasible positive objective value.

Conversely, if \(\varepsilon^\star>0\), an optimal swap-mass vector has every rival margin positive. Section 2 constructs a permitted redistribution realizing that vector, so \(c\) is the unique winner.

It remains to check the computational hypotheses. The LP has \(\tau(m-1)+1\) variables and \(O(\tau m+m)\) constraints. All data are rational. The coefficients multiplying swap masses have absolute value at most \(2\); the constant terms are sums of input masses multiplied by integers of absolute value at most \(m-1\). Exact rational arithmetic constructs these terms with bit length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Thus the entire explicit LP has polynomial encoding length.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming computes a finite optimum exactly in time polynomial in that encoding length; see [Bockmayr’s statement of the theorem](https://www.mi.fu-berlin.de/wiki/pub/ABI/LinearOptimizationWS11/LP-ILP.pdf). Its hypotheses hold here. Comparing the resulting rational \(\varepsilon^\star\) with zero is therefore an exact polynomial-time operation, including when the optimum is zero.

This settles all three constraints. The indivisible-voter hardness results of [Dey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08711) concern a different feasible set; arbitrary mass splitting permits the exact formulations proved above.

Verification report

The proof resolves all three assigned decision problems. The neighborhood characterizations, realizability constraints, margin formula, and exact polynomial-time decision procedure are correct. No substantive mathematical gaps were found.

1. Permitted destination rankings

Kendall distance. The characterization follows directly from the definition: distance at most \(1\) permits either no swap or one adjacent swap. This includes the source ranking.

Spearman footrule. The displacement argument is correct. Integer displacements with sum zero and total absolute value at most \(2\) are either all zero or consist of one \(+1\) and one \(-1\). Since every other candidate retains its position, the two moving candidates must exchange their original positions. Their unit displacements force these positions to be adjacent. Thus this neighborhood equals the Kendall neighborhood.

Maximum displacement. The left-to-right argument correctly characterizes permitted rankings as disjoint adjacent swaps. At each unprocessed position, earlier positions are occupied by already accounted-for candidates. If the original candidate moves, it must therefore move one position to the right, and the candidate filling its position must be the original next candidate. At the final position, movement to the right is impossible, so the remaining candidate is fixed. The converse is immediate from the stated displacement bound.

These arguments also cover \(m=2\).

2. Realizable swap masses

Kendall and footrule. The nonnegativity and total-mass inequality are necessary because each destination uses at most one edge. The proposed assignment to individual swap destinations and the unchanged ranking proves sufficiency exactly.

Maximum displacement. The incident-edge inequalities are necessary because permitted swap sets are matchings.

The interval construction proves sufficiency:

  • The inequalities, including the boundary conventions, imply \(0\leq y_i\leq\mu\).
  • Adjacent indices have opposite parity. Their intervals occupy opposite ends of \([0,\mu)\), and \(y_i+y_{i+1}\leq\mu\) ensures disjointness.
  • Half-open endpoints prevent overlap when equality holds.
  • Consequently, every \(M(s)\) is a matching.
  • Partitioning at all endpoints gives finitely many constant matching sets, with total assigned mass \(\mu\) and edge marginal exactly \(y_i\).

The construction allows zero-length intervals and repeated endpoints without difficulty. Each edge contributes at most one interior endpoint, so the asserted bound of at most \(m\) resulting destination rankings is correct.

3. Borda-margin calculation

For a candidate originally at position \(i\), edge \(i\) increases its position by \(1\), while edge \(i-1\) decreases it by \(1\). These events cannot occur together in a permitted destination. Thus the formula for \(P_a(y)\), including its boundary terms, is correct.

With total mass \(1\), the Borda score is \(m-P_a(y)\). Therefore \(D_a(y)=P_a(y)-P_c(y)\) has the correct sign: positivity means that \(c\) defeats \(a\).

The displayed expansion of \(D_a(y)\) is correct. Together with the realizability results, it establishes both directions of the claimed equivalence between feasible swap masses with positive margins and successful redistributions.

A successful redistribution has finitely many strictly positive rival margins, whose minimum is positive. This minimum also respects the imposed upper bound on \(\varepsilon\). Conversely, a positive optimum yields strictly positive margins against every rival, and Section 2 realizes the corresponding swap masses.
