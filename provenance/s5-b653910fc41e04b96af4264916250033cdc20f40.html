The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supported type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_t,\nu_u\) summing to one on each side, and strict rankings of opposite-side types, choose a nonnegative coupling \(x_{tu}\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\nu_u\). For each \((t,u)\), let \(A_{t,u}(x)\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to types it ranks below \(u\), and \(B_{u,t}(x)\) the mass of type \(u\) assigned to types it ranks below \(t\); require \(A_{t,u}(x)=0\) or \(B_{u,t}(x)=0\). Maximize \(\exp\!\left(\frac12\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}(\log U_t(u)+\log V_u(t))\right)\), where \(U_t(u)=|T_B|-\operatorname{rank}_t(u)\) and \(V_u(t)=|T_A|-\operatorname{rank}_u(t)\), with best rank zero.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T_A,T_B\) be nonempty finite type sets. A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; masses sum to one on each side. Set \(\tau=|T_A|+|T_B|\), the support size rather than the number of possible types, \(m=\max\{|T_A|,|T_B|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type strictly ranks all opposite-side types, all acceptable; ranks start at zero. Define \(U_t(u)=|T_B|-\operatorname{rank}_t(u)\) and \(V_u(t)=|T_A|-\operatorname{rank}_u(t)\), which are positive.
A coupling \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T_A\times T_B}\) satisfies \(\sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tu}=\nu_u\). Define \(A_{t,u}(x)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\) and \(B_{u,t}(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_u s}x_{su}\); stability requires \(A_{t,u}(x)=0\) or \(B_{u,t}(x)=0\) for every pair.
The objective is \(F(x)=\exp\!\left(\frac12\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}(\log U_t(u)+\log V_u(t))\right)\), with natural logarithms.

Is exact maximization of \(F(x)\) over stable couplings polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\) in the binary Turing model, or is it NP-hard?

Discrete rank-utility NSW stable marriage admits an \(O(n^4\log n)\) algorithm for \(n\) agents per side ([discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02354v1)).
Bipartite stable allocation with general linear costs has a polynomial-time algorithm ([Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)); its stated operation bound does not establish exact binary complexity for this logarithmic objective.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the supports explicitly nonempty and rankings complete, with every opposite-side type acceptable, as in the discrete model.
  • Interpreted equal society sizes as equal total mass, preserving the graded question's potentially unequal support sizes and its support-based utility scales.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size, m as the maximum opposite-side ranking length, and L as the total binary input length.
  • Specified exact optimization in the binary Turing model; logarithms are natural and are determined by the rank utilities rather than supplied as numerical approximations.

Partial result: a uniform subexponential exact algorithm

I do not settle the requested polynomial-time versus NP-hardness classification. I prove a stronger exact upper bound than the fixed-$m$ result in the notes:

Theorem. Let $\pi(m)$ denote the number of primes at most $m$. An exact maximizing stable coupling can be computed in the binary Turing model in time
$$ 2^{O(\pi(m)\log(2+\log m))}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). $$
Consequently, the running time is also bounded by
$$ 2^{O\left(m\log(2+\log m)/\log m\right)} \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). $$

Thus exact optimization is fixed-parameter tractable in $m$, with a subexponential dependence on $m$. In particular, it is polynomial-time solvable on families satisfying $m=O(\log L)$, even with arbitrary binary-encoded masses.

The proof below also repairs the dummy-allocation and logarithm-rounding issues identified by the verifier.

1. Linearization and short exact optimizers

Put
$$ a=|T_A|,\qquad b=|T_B|,\qquad E=ab, $$
and define
$$ q_{tu}=U_t(u)V_u(t). $$
Then
$$ 1\leq q_{tu}\leq ab\leq m^2. $$
Because the exponential is strictly increasing, maximizing $F$ is equivalent to maximizing
$$ W(x)=2\log F(x)=\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\log q_{tu}. $$

If either side has only one type, the marginal equations determine a unique coupling. It is stable because the worse-partner sum on the singleton side is always zero. Hence assume henceforth that $a,b\geq2$.

Let $Q$ be the least common multiple of the denominators of the input masses, and write
$$ b_t=Q\mu_t,\qquad d_u=Q\nu_u. $$
These are positive integers, with
$$ \sum_t b_t=\sum_u d_u=Q. $$
Since the least common multiple is at most the product of the denominators,
$$ \log_2 Q\leq L. $$

First, the stable set is nonempty. Replace each type $t$ by $b_t$ individual agents and each type $u$ by $d_u$ individual agents. Refine preferences strictly within each opposite-side type, preserving every comparison between different types. A perfect stable matching exists by Gale–Shapley. Its projected matching-count matrix, divided by $Q$, is stable: if both worse-partner sums for a type pair were positive, an individual of each type matched to a worse type would form a blocking pair.

This expansion establishes existence only; it is not an algorithmic step.

Lemma 1. Some maximizing stable coupling satisfies
$$ Qx_{tu}\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0} $$
for every pair.

Proof. Let $P$ be the transportation polytope with the prescribed marginals. For every pair, select one equation from
$$ A_{t,u}(x)=0,\qquad B_{u,t}(x)=0. $$
All entries are nonnegative, so a selected equation merely forces its constituent coordinates to zero. Each simultaneous selection therefore defines a possibly empty face of $P$. The stable set is exactly the union of these finitely many faces.

The union is nonempty and compact. The linear functional $W$ consequently has a maximizing vertex of one of its nonempty faces.

Scale such a vertex to $z=Qx$. Its positive-support bipartite graph is a forest. Indeed, a cycle would permit sufficiently small alternating additions and subtractions along its edges. These preserve all marginals and prescribed zero coordinates, expressing $z$ as the midpoint of two distinct feasible points.

At a leaf of the positive-support forest, the sole incident edge equals that vertex’s integer marginal. Remove the leaf and subtract this integer edge value from its neighbor’s marginal. Repeating proves that every positive entry of $z$ is integral. $\square$

Every entry of $z$ is at most $Q$. Hence an exact optimizer has encoding length
$$ O(E(1+\log Q)). $$
Its objective can, if needed, be recorded symbolically as
$$ F(x)= \left(\prod_{t,u}q_{tu}^{\,Qx_{tu}}\right)^{1/(2Q)}. $$

2. Stable-allocation applicability, including dummies

We work with scaled allocations $z=Qx$. Give every original edge capacity
$$ c_{tu}=\min(b_t,d_u). $$

For a feasible original allocation, both worse-partner sums being positive implies
$$ z_{tu}<b_t,\qquad z_{tu}<d_u, $$
and therefore $z_{tu}<c_{tu}$. Conversely, saturating $c_{tu}$ exhausts at least one endpoint’s quota, so that endpoint has no allocation to worse partners. Thus the task’s stability condition agrees exactly with capacitated stable-allocation stability.

To match the augmented formulation used by Dean–Munshi, add one dummy type on each side, denoted $A_0,B_0$, each with quota $Q$. Give the dummy edges capacities
$$ c_{tB_0}=b_t,\qquad c_{A_0u}=d_u,\qquad c_{A_0B_0}=Q. $$
Every real endpoint ranks its dummy partner last. Each dummy ranks all real opposite-side types above the other dummy, using an arbitrary strict order among the real types.

An original stable allocation extends to an augmented stable allocation by setting
$$ z_{A_0B_0}=Q,\qquad z_{tB_0}=z_{A_0u}=0. $$
Original pairs retain their stability conditions; a real–dummy pair cannot block because the real endpoint ranks the dummy last; the dummy–dummy edge is saturated.

Conversely, every augmented stable allocation has zero allocation between real and dummy types. To prove this, let
$$ S=\sum_{t,u}z_{tu}. $$
The original marginal equations in the augmented instance give
$$ \sum_t z_{tB_0}=\sum_u z_{A_0u}=Q-S. $$
If $S<Q$, choose $t,u$ with
$$ z_{tB_0}>0,\qquad z_{A_0u}>0. $$
Then
$$ z_{tu}<b_t,\qquad z_{tu}<d_u, $$
so the real edge $(t,u)$ has residual capacity. Both endpoints currently assign positive amounts to worse dummy partners. Therefore $(t,u)$ blocks, a contradiction.

It follows that the augmented stable allocations correspond exactly to the original stable couplings after scaling. In particular, optimizing with zero costs on dummy edges preserves the required original marginals.

3. Polynomial binary complexity for dyadic linear costs

The external structural result used here is the following: a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences admits a polynomial-size rotation DAG. Starting from an extremal stable allocation $z^0$, every stable allocation is obtained by applying rotations with amounts $y_\rho$ satisfying
$$ 0\leq y_\rho\leq r_\rho, $$
where applying a positive amount of a rotation requires every predecessor to be fully applied. The construction uses polynomially many allocation operations. These facts are established in [Dean–Munshi, Sections 3–4](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

The hypotheses hold for the augmented instance above: its graph is bipartite, quotas and capacities are finite, and all preferences are strict.

Lemma 2. Given dyadic edge costs with a common denominator $2^J$, a maximizing stable coupling can be computed in time polynomial in $\tau,L$ and the cost encoding length. The algorithm can return a coupling on the $Q$-grid.

Proof. The rotation construction operates on allocation quantities using additions, subtractions, minima and comparisons. Starting with integral scaled quotas and capacities, these operations preserve integrality. Thus $z^0$ and every full rotation amount $r_\rho$ are integral.

The number of operations is polynomial. Consequently, all intermediate integers have polynomial bit length: each addition or subtraction increases the maximum operand bit length by at most one, while taking a minimum selects an existing operand.

Let $d^\rho$ be the signed direction of rotation $\rho$, with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$. For costs $c_e$, the change from fully applying the rotation is
$$ w_\rho=r_\rho\sum_e d^\rho_e c_e. $$
These weights have denominator dividing $2^J$ and polynomial encoding length.

A linear optimum can be chosen with every rotation either unapplied or fully applied. Indeed, suppose
$$ 0<y_\rho<r_\rho. $$
Every predecessor is already fully applied, and every successor has amount zero. Replacing $y_\rho$ by either endpoint therefore preserves feasibility. Select the endpoint that does not decrease the objective. Repeating removes all partial rotations.

It remains to maximize
$$ \sum_{\rho\in S}w_\rho $$
over predecessor-closed subsets $S$ of the rotation DAG.

Multiply the weights by $2^J$, obtaining integral weights $g_\rho$. Construct a cut network as follows:

  • For $g_\rho>0$, add a source arc of capacity $g_\rho$.
  • For $g_\rho<0$, add a sink arc of capacity $-g_\rho$.
  • For each precedence relation $\rho\to\sigma$, add an arc from $\sigma$ to $\rho$ of capacity

$$ H=1+\sum_\eta |g_\eta|. $$

A minimum cut never cuts a precedence arc: the cut placing every rotation on the sink side costs at most $\sum_\eta|g_\eta|<H$. Its source-side rotation set is therefore predecessor-closed.

For a closed source-side set $S$, the cut capacity equals
$$ \sum_{\rho:g_\rho>0}g_\rho-\sum_{\rho\in S}g_\rho. $$
Thus minimum cut gives the desired maximizing set. Integer-capacity minimum cut has polynomial binary complexity, and all capacities here have polynomial bit length.

Applying the selected full rotations yields an integral $z$. Returning $x=z/Q$ proves the claim. $\square$

4. Dyadic logarithm approximation and its error budget

For completeness, the logarithm approximation needed below can be constructed directly.

For a positive integer $q$, put
$$ j=\lfloor\log_2 q\rfloor,\qquad y=q/2^j. $$
Then $1\leq y<2$ and
$$ \log q=j\log2+\log y. $$
For $1\leq y\leq2$, set
$$ z=\frac{y-1}{y+1}. $$
Thus $0\leq z\leq1/3$, and integrating the geometric series gives
$$ \log y = 2\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2h+1}}{2h+1}. $$
After $N$ terms, the nonnegative remainder is at most
$$ 2\sum_{h=N}^{\infty}z^{2h+1} \leq \frac34\,9^{-N}. $$
The same expression with $z=1/3$ approximates $\log2$.

Taking
$$ N=P+2+\left\lceil\log_2(j+1)\right\rceil $$
therefore produces a rational approximation to $\log q$ with error less than $2^{-(P+2)}$. All rational terms and their sum have encoding length polynomial in $N$ and $\log q$, so this computation has polynomial binary complexity.

Round the result to the nearest multiple of $2^{-(P+2)}$. The rounding adds error at most $2^{-(P+3)}$. The resulting dyadic value $\widetilde c_q$ consequently satisfies
$$ |\widetilde c_q-\log q|<2^{-P}. $$

Let $\widehat x$ maximize these dyadic costs over stable couplings, using Lemma 2, and let $x^\star$ maximize the true objective. Set $\delta=2^{-P}$. Since every coupling has total mass one,
$$ \left| \sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\widetilde c_{q_{tu}}-W(x) \right| \leq\delta. $$
Optimality for the approximated objective gives
$$ 0\leq W(x^\star)-W(\widehat x)\leq2\delta. $$
Both $x^\star$ and $\widehat x$ may be chosen on the $Q$-grid, by Lemmas 1 and 2.

Thus a separation bound between distinct grid objective values will turn this approximation into an exact algorithm.

5. A uniform separation bound from linear forms in logarithms

Let $x=z/Q$ and $x'=z'/Q$ be two grid couplings. Every prime factor of $q_{tu}$ is at most $m$, because $q_{tu}$ is the product of two integers at most $m$.

Writing $v_p(q)$ for the exponent of prime $p$ in $q$, we obtain
$$ Q\bigl(W(x)-W(x')\bigr) = \sum_{p\leq m}h_p\log p, $$
where
$$ h_p=\sum_{t,u}(z_{tu}-z'_{tu})v_p(q_{tu}). $$

Define
$$ k=\pi(m),\qquad s=\lceil\log_2 m\rceil,\qquad r=\lceil\log_2 Q\rceil. $$
Since $q_{tu}\leq m^2$,
$$ 0\leq v_p(q_{tu})\leq2s. $$
Both $z$ and $z'$ have total mass $Q$, so
$$ 0\leq\sum_{t,u}z_{tu}v_p(q_{tu})\leq2Qs, $$
and the same holds for $z'$. Hence
$$ |h_p|\leq2Qs. $$

We use the following known bound for a nonzero linear form in logarithms of positive integers. If
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_i\log a_i\neq0, $$
with $a_i\geq2$ and nonzero integer coefficients $c_i$, then
$$ |\Lambda| \geq \exp\left( -C'(d) \prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\{\log a_i,1\} \log B \right), $$
where
$$ B=\max\{e,|c_1|,\ldots,|c_d|\}, \qquad C'(d)=2.9(2e)^{2d+6}(d+2)^{9/2}. $$
This form of Matveev’s bound is stated in [Etessami–Stewart–Yannakakis, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1304.5429).

Here its hypotheses hold after deleting zero coefficients: the bases are positive primes, the coefficients are nonzero integers, and unequal objective values give a nonzero form. There are at most $k$ terms.

Introduce the explicitly computable integers
$$ K(k)=2^{6k+20}(k+2)^5, $$
$$ t=r+\left\lceil\log_2(2s)\right\rceil+2, $$
and
$$ D=K(k)s^k t. $$
For $1\leq d\leq k$, using $e<3$ gives
$$ C'(d)\leq K(k). $$
Every height factor is at most $s$. Also, $|h_p|\leq2Qs$ implies $\log B\leq t$. Therefore, whenever $W(x)\neq W(x')$,
$$ \left| Q\bigl(W(x)-W(x')\bigr) \right| \geq e^{-D}>2^{-2D}. $$
Because $Q\leq2^r$,
$$ |W(x)-W(x')|>2^{-(r+2D)}. $$

Now choose
$$ P=r+2D+3. $$
Section 4 and Lemma 2 produce a grid coupling $\widehat x$ with
$$ 0\leq W(x^\star)-W(\widehat x) \leq2^{1-P} < 2^{-(r+2D)}. $$
The separation bound excludes a positive difference. Consequently,
$$ W(\widehat x)=W(x^\star). $$
This proves that the algorithm returns an exact optimizer.

6. Running time and growing-support cases

The algorithm runs in time polynomial in $\tau,L,P$. Moreover,
$$ t=O(L+\log(s+1)), $$
and
$$ P=O\left(K(k)s^k(L+\log(s+1))\right). $$
The factor depending on $m$ satisfies
$$ \log_2\bigl(K(k)s^k\bigr) = 6k+20+5\log_2(k+2)+k\log_2 s = O\bigl(k\log(2+\log m)\bigr). $$
Absorbing the fixed degree of the polynomial algorithm proves
$$ 2^{O(\pi(m)\log(2+\log m))} \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). $$

To obtain the second bound without invoking a prime-number asymptotic, define
$$ \vartheta(n)=\sum_{p\leq n}\log p. $$
The product of primes in $(n,2n]$ divides $\binom{2n}{n}$, so
$$ \vartheta(2n)-\vartheta(n) \leq\log\binom{2n}{n} \leq2n\log2. $$
Summing over dyadic intervals gives
$$ \vartheta(m)\leq4m\log2. $$
There are at most $\sqrt m$ primes at most $\sqrt m$. Every larger prime contributes more than $\frac12\log m$ to $\vartheta(m)$. Therefore
$$ \pi(m) \leq \sqrt m+\frac{2\vartheta(m)}{\log m} \leq \sqrt m+\frac{8m\log2}{\log m} = O\left(\frac m{\log m}\right). $$
The last step follows from $\log m\leq\sqrt m$ for $m\geq2$.

We have consequently proved the uniform bound
$$ 2^{O\left(m\log(2+\log m)/\log m\right)} \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). $$
Its exponent is $o(m)$ as $m$ tends to infinity.

In particular:

  • For fixed $m$, the algorithm is polynomial-time, with a polynomial degree independent of $m$.
  • It is polynomial-time on any family satisfying

$$ \pi(m)\log(2+\log m)=O(\log L). $$

  • The simpler sufficient condition $m=O(\log L)$ permits growing support and arbitrary binary-encoded masses.

These conclusions strengthen the previous fixed-$m$ result.

7. Other established consequences

The same framework also preserves two useful bounds from the notes.

Polynomial dependence on the numerical denominator. For a grid coupling, define
$$ P_x=\prod_{t,u}q_{tu}^{\,Qx_{tu}}. $$
This is an integer satisfying
$$ 1\leq P_x\leq(ab)^Q. $$
If $P_y>P_x$, then
$$ \log P_y-\log P_x = \int_{P_x}^{P_y}\frac{dv}{v} \geq\frac1{(ab)^Q}. $$
Thus distinct grid values of $W$ differ by at least
$$ \frac1{Q(ab)^Q}. $$
Choosing
$$ P=\lceil\log_2 Q\rceil +Q\lceil\log_2(ab)\rceil+3 $$
in Section 4 forces exactness. This gives running time polynomial in $\tau,L,Q$.

Efficient multiplicative approximation. For a requested precision integer $j\geq0$, supplied in unary, use $P=j+1$. Then
$$ W(x^\star)-W(\widehat x)\leq2^{-j}, $$
and therefore
$$ F(\widehat x) \geq e^{-2^{-j-1}}F(x^\star) \geq e^{-2^{-j}}F(x^\star). $$
The running time is polynomial in $\tau,L,j$.

8. What remains unproved

The exact algorithm above has no exponential dependence on the binary length of the masses, but its dependence on variable $m$ remains superpolynomial.

The number-theoretic estimate is a sufficient separation bound. Its size does not prove that superpolynomial precision is necessary, and it supplies no NP-hardness reduction.

A complete resolution still requires either a uniform polynomial-time exact algorithm exploiting additional structure, or an NP-hardness proof for these specific rank utilities and stability constraints.
