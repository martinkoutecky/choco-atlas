The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given alternatives \(A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a support \(\mathcal{P}\subseteq\{0,1,?\}^m\) of partial approval reports, with \(\tau=|\mathcal{P}|\), decide whether every unit of mass can receive a complete ballot extending its report, with the completed population ordered so each alternative’s approving set is a prefix or suffix. Equivalently, decide whether there are ballots \(b_p\in\{0,1\}^m\) extending each \(p\in\mathcal{P}\) and a permutation \(\sigma\) of \(\mathcal{P}\) such that, for every \(a_i\), the sequence \(b_{\sigma(1)}(a_i),\ldots,b_{\sigma(\tau)}(a_i)\) has form \(1^*0^*\) or \(0^*1^*\); assign each type a consecutive interval of length \(\mu_p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given alternatives \(A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}\), with \(m\geq2\), a society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal P\subseteq\{0,1,?\}^m\) of \(\tau\geq1\) distinct types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_p\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible reports; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
A report entry \(1\) means approval, \(0\) disapproval, and \(?\) an unrestricted unknown entry. A complete ballot \(b_p\in\{0,1\}^m\) extends \(p\) when it preserves every known entry.
A feasible completion chooses one ballot \(b_p\) for each type and a permutation \(\sigma\) of the support. Type \(\sigma(j)\) occupies a consecutive interval of length \(\mu_{\sigma(j)}\) in \([0,1)\), with its entire mass receiving its chosen ballot. For each alternative, its approving intervals must form a prefix or suffix of \([0,1)\); empty and full approving sets are permitted, and the choice of prefix or suffix may differ between alternatives.

Is deciding whether such a feasible completion exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of voters, deciding whether partial approval reports admit a completion whose approving sets are prefixes or suffixes of a common voter order is polynomial-time solvable, and an order and completion can also be constructed in polynomial time ([Terzopoulou, Karpov, and Obraztsova](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p2023.pdf)).
The [full version](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/16718/16525) retains this discrete result. No prior result explicitly treating the exact rational-mass formulation above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the distribution normalization explicit: all listed masses are positive binary rationals and sum to one.
  • Carried over the full version's restriction to at least two alternatives.
  • Allowed support size one: the discrete lower bound of two voters does not require two distinct reports, and a positive mass represents an atomless population.
  • Used half-open intervals in [0,1) to fix boundary conventions.
  • Explicitly permitted empty and full approving sets, as the discrete model allows constant approval rows.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Proven result. The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). An order, one completed ballot per type, and exact rational interval endpoints can also be constructed in polynomial time.

Reduction to a discrete profile. Enumerate the support as \(\mathcal P=\{p^1,\ldots,p^\tau\}\). Construct the incomplete matrix \(X\in\{0,1,?\}^{m\times\tau}\) by setting \(X_{i,k}=p^k_i\). Thus, each support type becomes exactly one discrete voter.

A completion of column \(k\) is precisely a single ballot extending \(p^k\). Consequently, this construction preserves the requirement that an entire type receives one completed ballot, including when different types receive identical ballots.

We prove that \(X\) admits a completion with the discrete prefix-or-suffix property if and only if the given rational-mass society admits a feasible completion.

Proof of equivalence. Fix any permutation \(\sigma\) of the support indices and any completed ballots \(b_{p^k}\) extending the reports. Define \(s_0=0\) and, for \(1\leq j\leq\tau\),

\(s_j=\sum_{h=1}^{j}\mu_{p^{\sigma(h)}}.\)

Positivity and normalization of the masses imply \(0=s_0<s_1<\cdots<s_\tau=1\). The interval occupied by the type at position \(j\) is \(I_j=[s_{j-1},s_j)\).

For alternative \(a_i\), let \(S_i=\{j:b_{p^{\sigma(j)},i}=1\}\). Its approving set in the society is exactly \(U_i=\bigcup_{j\in S_i}I_j\).

First suppose the discrete completion has the required property. If \(S_i=\{1,\ldots,r\}\), then the adjacent intervals give \(U_i=[0,s_r)\), a prefix. If \(S_i=\{r+1,\ldots,\tau\}\), then \(U_i=[s_r,1)\), a suffix. Taking \(r=0\) or \(r=\tau\) also covers empty and full approving sets. Hence a discrete feasible completion yields a rational-mass feasible completion.

Conversely, suppose the rational-mass completion is feasible. If \(U_i\) is a prefix, we show that \(S_i\) is an initial segment. Take \(j\in S_i\) and \(k<j\). Both \(I_k\) and \(I_j\) are nonempty, so choose \(x\in I_k\) and \(y\in I_j\). Then \(x<y\) and \(y\in U_i\). A prefix contains every earlier point, so \(x\in U_i\). Because the intervals partition \([0,1)\) and each entire interval has one ballot, this implies \(k\in S_i\). Thus \(S_i\) is an initial segment.

If \(U_i\) is a suffix, the same argument with \(k>j\) shows that \(j\in S_i\) implies \(k\in S_i\); therefore \(S_i\) is a final segment. Empty and full sets satisfy both conditions. This proves the equivalence for every alternative, allowing its orientation to be chosen independently.

Application of the discrete theorem. Proposition 7 of Terzopoulou, Karpov, and Obraztsova’s [full version](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/16718/16525) states that, for an incomplete dichotomous profile, deciding whether a completion satisfies Voter External Interval is polynomial-time solvable; an appropriate voter order and completion can also be constructed. Their Definition 1 defines this property by requiring each alternative’s approvers to form a prefix or suffix of one common voter order.

The constructed matrix satisfies these hypotheses: it has \(m\) alternatives, \(\tau\) voters, unrestricted unknown cells, and exactly the required ordering property. Apply that algorithm with its voter parameter equal to \(\tau\). By the proved equivalence, its answer is also the answer to the rational-mass problem.

Bit complexity and exact construction. Constructing \(X\) takes \(O(m\tau)\) time. The discrete algorithm takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\); deciding feasibility requires no arithmetic on the masses.

For an exact interval witness, write each input mass as \(\mu_{p^k}=u_k/v_k\), with positive integer denominator \(v_k\), and use the common denominator \(Q=\prod_{k=1}^{\tau}v_k\). Its binary length is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the input denominators’ binary lengths is at most \(L\). Every endpoint \(s_j\) can be represented as \(N_j/Q\), where \(0\leq N_j\leq Q\). Computing these numerators uses polynomially many integer operations on \(O(L)\)-bit integers, hence polynomial bit complexity.

Therefore both decision and exact witness construction are polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).
