The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two candidates \(c^\star,c_1\), a finite set \(T\) of complete voter-and-network-block types, a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a finite scenario distribution \(\rho\), typed kernels \(\lambda^\omega_{ut}\), and seed fraction \(\kappa\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\) to maximize \(\Pr_{\omega\sim\rho}[2\sum_{t:\pi_t(2)=c^\star}r_t^\omega(x)>a_1-a_\star]\), where \(a_c=\sum_{t:\pi_t(1)=c}\mu_t\) and \(r^\omega(x)\) is the least fixed point of \(r_t=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)(1-e^{-\sum_u\lambda^\omega_{ut}r_u})\); return an optimum or an \(\alpha\)-approximation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=2\) candidates, \(c^\star,c_1\). Each type specifies a strict ranking \(\pi_t\) and a network block; distinct blocks may have identical rankings.
The input explicitly lists \(s\ge1\) scenarios \(\Omega\), positive binary-rational probabilities \(\rho_\omega\) summing to \(1\), and finite nonnegative binary-rational influence intensities \(\lambda^\omega_{ut}\) from type \(u\) to type \(t\). The seed budget is a binary rational \(\kappa\in[0,1]\).
Choose seed masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\), before the scenario is drawn. Every type may be seeded and transmit influence, including types already ranking \(c^\star\) first.
In scenario \(\omega\), the influenced masses \(r^\omega(x)\) are the coordinatewise least fixed point in \(\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) of \(r_t=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)(1-\exp(-\sum_u\lambda^\omega_{ut}r_u))\). Influenced voters promote \(c^\star\) one position; those already ranking it first retain their vote. Ties count as failure.
Define \(a_c=\sum_{t:\pi_t(1)=c}\mu_t\) and maximize \(F(x)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\rho_\omega\mathbf{1}\{2\sum_{t:\pi_t(2)=c^\star}r_t^\omega(x)>a_{c_1}-a_{c^\star}\}\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum over feasible real seed vectors. Algorithms use the Turing bit model and output binary-rational feasible seed vectors; inequalities have their exact mathematical meaning, without a tolerance or margin promise. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

For each fixed \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), is obtaining a feasible \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge\alpha\mathrm{OPT}\) polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,s,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite voters with independent-cascade diffusion, obtaining any positive multiplicative approximation to probability of victory is NP-hard, even with two candidates and deterministic propagation. [Wilder and Vorobeychik](https://www.cais.usc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/aamas_elections_final.pdf)
No result settling this explicitly represented scenario model with fractional seeds and the exponential least-fixed-point dynamics was identified in the literature search.

  • Interpreted complete voter-and-network-block types as strict rankings paired with block identities; identical rankings need not identify the same type.
  • Made scenarios explicitly listed with positive binary-rational probabilities, and kernels finite nonnegative binary-rational intensities rather than probabilities bounded by one.
  • Set the normalized seed budget domain to [0,1], allowing seeding and transmission by every type, including existing supporters.

Result

For every fixed \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), obtaining the requested approximation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already with \(m=2\) and a single scenario \(s=1\).

The proof below handles arbitrary fractional seeds, permits seeding every type, and avoids evaluating transcendental expressions when decoding an algorithm’s output.

1. Source problem and a fractional rounding lemma

We reduce from Independent Set on simple cubic graphs. An instance consists of a simple undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\), with every vertex having degree three, and an integer \(k\). The question is whether \(G\) contains an independent set of size at least \(k\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; see Theorem 3.2 and the discussion extending the result to graphs of degree exactly three in [Alimonti and Kann, *Hardness of Approximating Problems on Cubic Graphs*](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/aspnes/pinewiki/attachments/MarkovChains/alimanti-mis-cubic.pdf).

Write \(n=|V|\). We restrict to \(1\le k\le n\), since instances outside this range are trivial.

For a positive rational \(h\), define

\( P_k=\{z\in[0,h]^V:\sum_{v\in V}z_v\le kh\} \)

and

\( Q(z)=3h\sum_{v\in V}z_v-2\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}z_uz_v. \)

Rounding lemma. For every \(z\in P_k\), there is a subset \(A\subseteq V\), with \(|A|\le k\), such that

\( Q(z)\le Q(h\mathbf 1_A)=h^2(3|A|-2e(A)), \)

where \(e(A)\) denotes the number of edges with both endpoints in \(A\).

Proof. Suppose two coordinates \(z_i,z_j\) lie strictly between zero and \(h\). Replace them by \(z_i+t,z_j-t\), allowing \(t\) to range over the closed interval on which both coordinates remain in \([0,h]\). This preserves the total seed mass.

If \(\{i,j\}\notin E\), the resulting function of \(t\) is affine. If \(\{i,j\}\in E\), its quadratic term is \(2t^2\), because the contribution of that edge is

\( -2(z_i+t)(z_j-t). \)

All other contributions are affine in \(t\). Thus the function is convex, and at least one endpoint has value no smaller than its value at \(t=0\). At either endpoint, at least one of the two coordinates becomes zero or \(h\).

Repeating this operation leaves at most one coordinate strictly between zero and \(h\), without decreasing \(Q\).

If one such coordinate remains and there are \(j\) coordinates equal to \(h\), feasibility implies \(j\le k-1\). Consequently, the remaining coordinate can vary throughout \([0,h]\) while preserving feasibility. Since \(Q\) is affine in that coordinate, setting it to zero or \(h\) can again avoid decreasing \(Q\). The resulting vector is \(h\mathbf 1_A\), with \(|A|\le k\). Substitution gives the stated formula. \(\square\)

Two consequences will be used:

  • If \(G\) has an independent set of size at least \(k\), there is \(z\in P_k\) with \(Q(z)=3kh^2\).
  • If \(G\) has no independent set of size \(k\), then every \(z\in P_k\) satisfies

\( Q(z)\le(3k-2)h^2. \)

For the second consequence, apply the lemma. If \(|A|\le k-1\), then \(3|A|-2e(A)\le3k-3\). If \(|A|=k\), then \(e(A)\ge1\), giving the bound \(3k-2\).

2. Construction of the society

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\ge n\). Set

\( h=\frac1{8N},\qquad \ell=\frac1{128N},\qquad \kappa=kh, \)

and define the rational victory threshold

\( \theta=\kappa+\ell(3k-1)h^2. \)

Construct \(n+2\) types, each with its own network block:

  • For every \(v\in V\), a vertex type of mass \(h\), ranking \(c_1\) above \(c^\star\).
  • An isolated type \(b\), ranking \(c_1\) above \(c^\star\), of mass

\( \mu_b=\frac12+\theta-nh. \)

  • An isolated type \(p\), ranking \(c^\star\) above \(c_1\), of mass

\( \mu_p=\frac12-\theta. \)

There is one scenario, of probability one. For each graph edge \(\{u,v\}\in E\), put intensity \(\ell\) in both directions between the corresponding vertex types. Every other intensity is zero.

All types remain seedable.

We verify that this is a valid input. First, \(nh\le1/8\), so \(\kappa\le1/8\). Also \(3\ell h<1\), and therefore

\( 0<\theta <\kappa(1+3\ell h) <2\kappa \le\frac14. \)

Hence both auxiliary masses are positive. The masses sum to one.

All numerical parameters are dyadic rationals with bit length \(O(\log n)\). The support size is \(\tau=n+2\); even explicitly listing the entire intensity matrix takes only polynomial space and time.

The initial mass supporting \(c_1\) is

\( B=nh+\mu_b=\frac12+\theta, \)

and the initial mass supporting \(c^\star\) is \(1-B\). Thus, writing

\( S(x)=\sum_{v\in V}r_v(x)+r_b(x), \)

the exact victory condition is

\( 2S(x)>B-(1-B)=2\theta, \)

or equivalently \(S(x)>\theta\).

3. A uniform bound for the exponential dynamics

Fix any feasible seed vector. Write \(z_v=x_v\) for its vertex coordinates.

Because the auxiliary types are isolated,

\( r_b=x_b,\qquad r_p=x_p. \)

On the vertex types, the fixed-point equations are

\( r_v=z_v+(h-z_v)\left(1-\exp\left(-\ell\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}r_u\right)\right). \)

These equations have a unique fixed point in \([0,h]^V\). Indeed, their right-hand side maps this box into itself. Since \(a\mapsto1-e^{-a}\) is Lipschitz with constant one on the nonnegative reals, the map has Lipschitz constant at most \(3\ell h<1\) in the maximum norm. The contraction mapping theorem therefore applies. In particular, this unique fixed point is the stipulated least fixed point.

We next prove the uniform estimate

\( \left|\sum_{v\in V}r_v-\sum_{v\in V}z_v-\ell Q(z)\right|\le\mathcal E, \qquad \mathcal E=\frac{27}{2}n\ell^2h^3. \)

Define

\( \delta_v=r_v-z_v,\qquad a_v=\ell\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}r_u. \)

Since \(0\le r_u\le h\) and each vertex has three neighbors,

\( 0\le a_v\le3\ell h. \)

Using \(0\le1-e^{-a}\le a\) for \(a\ge0\), we obtain

\( 0\le\delta_v=(h-z_v)(1-e^{-a_v})\le3\ell h^2. \)

Also, for every \(a\ge0\),

\( |1-e^{-a}-a|\le\frac{a^2}{2}. \)

For completeness, the difference satisfies
\( a-(1-e^{-a})=\int_0^a(1-e^{-t})\,dt, \)
which lies between zero and \(\int_0^a t\,dt=a^2/2\).

Consequently,

\( \delta_v-\ell(h-z_v)\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}z_u = (h-z_v)\left((1-e^{-a_v})-a_v+\ell\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}\delta_u\right). \)

Its absolute value is at most

\( h\left(\frac{(3\ell h)^2}{2}+\ell\cdot3\cdot3\ell h^2\right) = \frac{27}{2}\ell^2h^3. \)

Summing over vertices proves the estimate, because cubic regularity gives

\( \sum_v(h-z_v)\sum_{u\in N_G(v)}z_u = 3h\sum_vz_v-2\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}z_uz_v = Q(z). \)

Finally, the error is smaller than the required gap:

\( \frac{\mathcal E}{\ell h^2} = \frac{27}{2}n\ell h \le\frac{27}{256} <\frac14, \)

where we used \(n\le N\), \(\ell=1/(128N)\), and \(h\le1\). Thus

\( \mathcal E<\frac14\ell h^2. \)

4. Correctness of the reduction

Yes-instances

Suppose \(G\) contains an independent set \(A\) of size \(k\). Fully seed its vertex types:

\( z=h\mathbf1_A,\qquad x_b=x_p=0. \)

This is a feasible binary-rational vector, and \(Q(z)=3kh^2\). The uniform estimate yields

\( S(x)\ge\kappa+3k\ell h^2-\mathcal E = \theta+\ell h^2-\mathcal E >\theta. \)

Thus this seed vector wins, and \(\mathrm{OPT}=1\).

No-instances, including arbitrary fractional and auxiliary seeds

Suppose \(G\) has no independent set of size \(k\). Consider any feasible real seed vector.

Its vertex coordinates belong to \(P_k\). Moreover, the budget constraint gives

\( \sum_vz_v+x_b\le\kappa. \)

This remains true when some budget is spent on the initially preferred type \(p\).

Using the uniform estimate and the rounding lemma,

\( S(x) \le \sum_vz_v+x_b+\ell Q(z)+\mathcal E \le \kappa+\ell(3k-2)h^2+\mathcal E. \)

Therefore,

\( S(x)\le\theta-\ell h^2+\mathcal E<\theta. \)

No feasible real seed vector wins, and \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\).

This proves that deciding whether \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard in the constructed single-scenario family.

5. Decoding an approximation algorithm in the Turing bit model

Fix any \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm outputs a feasible binary-rational vector \(x\) satisfying

\( F(x)\ge\alpha\mathrm{OPT}. \)

Because there is one scenario, \(F(x)\in\{0,1\}\). On every yes-instance, \(\mathrm{OPT}=1\), so the algorithm must output a winning vector.

We must still explain how to distinguish the source instances in polynomial time from that vector. Directly testing the exponential victory inequality could raise exact-comparison difficulties. The reduction avoids this issue.

Compute the rational number \(Q(z)\) from the output’s vertex coordinates and accept precisely when

\( Q(z)>\left(3k-\frac32\right)h^2. \)

On a no-instance, the rounding lemma guarantees

\( Q(z)\le(3k-2)h^2 < \left(3k-\frac32\right)h^2, \)

so the test rejects.

On a yes-instance, the output must satisfy \(S(x)>\theta\). The uniform upper bound and budget constraint imply

\( \theta<S(x)\le\kappa+\ell Q(z)+\mathcal E. \)

Hence

\( Q(z)> \frac{\theta-\kappa-\mathcal E}{\ell} = (3k-1)h^2-\frac{\mathcal E}{\ell} > \left(3k-\frac54\right)h^2 > \left(3k-\frac32\right)h^2. \)

Thus the test accepts.

The output of a polynomial-time Turing algorithm has polynomial bit length. Evaluating \(Q(z)\) and comparing these rationals therefore takes polynomial time.

The hypothetical approximation algorithm would consequently decide Independent Set on cubic graphs in polynomial time. This establishes the claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for every fixed positive approximation factor. The proof does not require an exact transcendental comparison oracle or a margin promise on the general input.

  • Fractional rounding lemma

The pairwise rounding argument is valid.

For two fractional coordinates, the allowed interval of \(t\) is closed, contains zero, and preserves both coordinate bounds and total mass. For a nonedge, the objective is affine in \(t\). For an edge, the contribution
\(-2(z_i+t)(z_j-t)\) has quadratic coefficient \(2\), so the objective is convex. In either case, an endpoint has objective at least its value at zero.

Each operation reduces the number of fractional coordinates by at least one. Previously integral coordinates are unaffected, so the procedure terminates.

If one fractional coordinate remains and \(j\) coordinates equal \(h\), feasibility and the positive fractional remainder imply \(j<k\). Since \(j\) and \(k\) are integers, \(j\le k-1\). Therefore the last coordinate may range over all of \([0,h]\) while preserving feasibility. Affinity permits rounding it to an endpoint without decreasing \(Q\).

Substitution into \(Q\) gives
\(Q(h\mathbf 1_A)=h^2(3|A|-2e(A))\).
Both consequences follow with the stated constants:

  • An independent set of size at least \(k\) contains one of size exactly \(k\), yielding \(3kh^2\).

The bound therefore applies to every real \(z\in P_k\), not merely rational or integral vectors.
