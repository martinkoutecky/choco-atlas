The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(q\) recurring indivisible chore classes with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\), and binary vectors \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^q\), output \(\lambda_{t,S}\ge0\) over integral bundle vectors \(S\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^q\) satisfying \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S}S_g\lambda_{t,S}=\rho_g\), such that for every \(\lambda_{t,S}>0\), \(\lambda_{u,R}>0\), and \(g\) with \(S_g>0\), \(c_t(S-e_g)\le c_t(R)\), and the allocation is Pareto-optimal among feasible mass allocations, where \(c_t(S)=\sum_g a_{t,g}S_g\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=q\geq1\) recurring indivisible chore classes with finite nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_g\). Type \(t\) has costs \(a_t\in\{0,1\}^q\), and an integral bundle \(S\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^q\) costs \(c_t(S)=\sum_g a_{t,g}S_g\).
A feasible mass allocation has real entries \(\lambda_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S}S_g\lambda_{t,S}=\rho_g\). Individuals receive integral bundles; masses of individuals may be split without restriction.
It is EFX if \(c_t(S-e_g)\leq c_t(R)\) whenever \(\lambda_{t,S}>0\), \(\lambda_{u,R}>0\), and \(S_g>0\), where \(e_g\) is the unit vector; this includes removal of zero-cost chores.
A feasible allocation \(\lambda'\) Pareto-dominates \(\lambda\) if there are nonnegative couplings \(\gamma_{t,S,R}\) with \(\sum_R\gamma_{t,S,R}=\lambda_{t,S}\), \(\sum_S\gamma_{t,S,R}=\lambda'_{t,R}\), and \(c_t(R)\leq c_t(S)\) on every positive entry, with strict inequality on positive total mass. PO means no such feasible competitor exists, whether or not it is EFX; competitors may have countably many supported bundles.
The computational problem is to output an EFX and PO allocation as a finite list of positive binary-rational masses and binary-encoded integral bundles, or report that no such allocation exists. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous allocation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many agents and individually listed chores with additive binary costs, EFX and PO allocations always exist and can be computed in polynomial time ([Tao, Wu, Yu, and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12177)).
No result settling this exact mass-allocation formulation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so the stated supplies are per capita; required a nonempty society and at least one chore class, while allowing zero supplies and zero-cost types.
  • Defined Pareto dominance through within-type couplings that preserve each individual's weak cost improvement and require strict improvement on positive mass, rather than comparing only aggregate type costs.
  • Allowed arbitrary real feasible competitors, including countably supported allocations, and did not restrict Pareto competitors to EFX allocations.
  • Specified finite rational output with binary-encoded bundle coordinates and a nonexistence response, since existence and output representation were unspecified.
  • Set m=q, the number of chore classes, and defined L as total input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. Every instance admits an EFX and PO allocation.

The construction below outputs at most \(m+2\tau-1\) records, each with positive rational mass and a binary-encoded integral bundle. An elementary implementation takes \(O((m+\tau)^2L^2)\) bit operations.

1. Separate the universally costly classes

Define
\(H=\{g:a_{t,g}=1\text{ for every }t\}\)
and
\(Z=\{1,\ldots,m\}\setminus H\).

Thus every class in \(Z\) has a zero-cost recipient type. This partition also appears in the [finite-agent proof of Tao, Wu, Yu, and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.12177v1#S3).

Let
\(U=\sum_{g\in H}\rho_g\),
\(k=\lfloor U\rfloor\), and
\(r=U-k\).
Then \(0\leq r<1\).

We will construct an allocation satisfying these properties:

  • Every individual receives either \(k\) or \(k+1\) chores from \(H\).
  • Individuals receiving \(k+1\) chores receive only classes from \(H\).
  • Every chore from \(Z\) goes to a zero-cost recipient.

First, these properties imply both required guarantees.

EFX. Every bundle contains at least \(k\) universally costly chores, so every type values every supported bundle at cost at least \(k\).

An individual receiving \(k\) universally costly chores has own cost exactly \(k\), by property 3. Removing any chore leaves cost at most \(k\).

An individual receiving \(k+1\) universally costly chores has no other chores, by property 2. Removing any chore leaves cost exactly \(k\).

Consequently, every required EFX inequality holds, including removal of zero-cost chores.

PO. Define the social cost of any feasible allocation \(\eta\) by
\(C(\eta)=\sum_{t,S}c_t(S)\eta_{t,S}\).
Nonnegativity and feasibility give
\(U\leq C(\eta)\leq\sum_g\rho_g<\infty\).
The lower bound holds because every chore from \(H\) costs its recipient one.

Our allocation has \(C(\lambda)=U\), by property 3. Suppose a feasible allocation \(\lambda'\) Pareto-dominated it through a coupling \(\gamma\). The marginal conditions imply
\(C(\lambda)-C(\lambda')=\sum_{t,S,R}\gamma_{t,S,R}(c_t(S)-c_t(R))\).
Both marginal costs are finite, so this identity is valid for countably supported competitors. Every summand is nonnegative. Moreover, costs are integers, so the cost difference is at least one on every strict-improvement entry. Positive total mass of such entries therefore implies \(C(\lambda')<U\), contradicting the lower bound.

It remains to realize the three properties with the prescribed supplies and polynomial-size output.

2. Partition the population into high and low groups

Represent the population by the interval \([0,1)\), with interval length representing mass.

Order the types and set
\(M_0=0\) and
\(M_t=\sum_{u=1}^{t}\mu_u\).
In particular, \(M_\tau=1\).

Assign type \(t\) to the two intervals

\(I_t^+=[rM_{t-1},rM_t)\),

\(I_t^-=[r+(1-r)M_{t-1},r+(1-r)M_t)\).

The intervals \(I_t^+\) partition \([0,r)\), and the intervals \(I_t^-\) partition \([r,1)\). Type \(t\) has total mass
\(|I_t^+|+|I_t^-|=r\mu_t+(1-r)\mu_t=\mu_t\).

Call these the high and low groups, respectively. Crucially, every type has positive low-group mass
\(w_t=|I_t^-|=(1-r)\mu_t>0\).
High-group intervals may be empty.

3. Allocate all universally costly supplies

Order \(H\) as \(g_1,\ldots,g_h\). Define
\(P_0=0\) and
\(P_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\rho_{g_\ell}\).
Thus \(P_h=U\).

For a population position \(x\in[0,1)\), define

\(S_{g_j}(x)=\#\{n\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}:P_{j-1}\leq x+n<P_j\}\).

These are finite nonnegative integers. Equivalently,

\(S_{g_j}(x)=\lceil P_j-x\rceil-\lceil P_{j-1}-x\rceil\).

Indeed, the allowed integers lie in \([P_{j-1}-x,P_j-x)\), whose smallest possible integer is nonnegative because \(P_{j-1}-x>-1\).

For each class, its supplied mass is exactly correct:

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1 S_{g_j}(x)\,dx =\sum_{n\geq0}|[P_{j-1},P_j)\cap[n,n+1)| =P_j-P_{j-1} =\rho_{g_j}. \)

Only finitely many summands are nonzero.

Summing the bundle coordinates telescopes:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{g\in H}S_g(x) =\lceil U-x\rceil-\lceil-x\rceil. \)

Since \(\lceil-x\rceil=0\) on \([0,1)\), this sum equals \(k+1\) when \(x<r\), and \(k\) when \(x\geq r\). If \(H\) is empty, then \(U=k=r=0\), and the same conclusion holds with an empty sum.

Thus the high group receives \(k+1\) universally costly chores per person, and the low group receives \(k\).

4. Allocate the remaining supplies to low-group zero-cost recipients

For each \(g\in Z\), choose a type \(t(g)\) with \(a_{t(g),g}=0\).

Write its low-group interval as \(I_{t(g)}^-=[b_g,b_g+w_{t(g)})\). Define

\(q_g=\left\lfloor\rho_g/w_{t(g)}\right\rfloor\),

\(\delta_g=\rho_g-q_gw_{t(g)}\).

Then \(0\leq\delta_g<w_{t(g)}\). Assign:

  • \(q_g+1\) copies of class \(g\) to positions in \([b_g,b_g+\delta_g)\);
  • \(q_g\) copies to the rest of \(I_{t(g)}^-\);
  • zero copies everywhere else.

Its total supply is

\((q_g+1)\delta_g+q_g(w_{t(g)}-\delta_g)=\rho_g\).

All recipients have zero cost for this class, and all lie in the low group. Hence properties 2 and 3 hold. Together with Section 3, this completes the existence proof and the proofs of EFX and PO.

5. Extract a finite allocation

The type and bundle assigned to \(x\) are constant between finitely many rational breakpoints. Collect:

  • \(0\) and \(1\);
  • every fractional part \(P_j-\lfloor P_j\rfloor\), for \(1\leq j\leq h\);
  • all endpoints of the type intervals;
  • \(b_g+\delta_g\), for every \(g\in Z\).

Sort the distinct breakpoints as
\(0=e_0<e_1<\cdots<e_N=1\).

For each consecutive interval, evaluate its type and bundle at the midpoint and output that bundle with mass \(e_{i+1}-e_i\). All masses are positive. The finitely many boundary positions have zero mass and do not affect feasibility or support-based EFX. Duplicate type–bundle records can be combined.

There are at most \(m+2\tau\) distinct breakpoints: two outer endpoints, at most \(h\) fractional-prefix breakpoints, at most \(2(\tau-1)\) internal type boundaries, and at most \(|Z|\) additional breakpoints. The boundary \(r\) is already a fractional-prefix breakpoint when \(h>0\); when \(h=0\), it equals zero.

Therefore,

\(N\leq m+2\tau-1\).

6. Encoding lengths and running time

Let \(D\) be the product of all input rational denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Every input mass and supply is an integer multiple of \(1/D\).

The quantities \(r\) and \(M_t\) are multiples of \(1/D\). Thus every type boundary and every \(w_t\) is a multiple of \(1/D^2\). Since

\(\delta_g=\rho_g-q_gw_{t(g)}\),

all remaining breakpoints are also multiples of \(1/D^2\). Every output mass consequently has a representation with denominator \(D^2\) and numerator at most \(D^2\), using \(O(L)\) bits.

Bundle coordinates also have \(O(L)\) bits. To see this explicitly, positivity gives \(\mu_t\geq1/D\), and \(r<1\) gives \(1-r\geq1/D\). Hence \(w_t\geq1/D^2\). With input supplies bounded by \(2^L\),

\(q_g+1\leq2^LD^2+1\).

A universally costly coordinate counts integers in an interval of length \(\rho_g\), so it is at most \(\lceil\rho_g\rceil\). These bounds establish the claimed encoding lengths.

There are \(O(m+\tau)\) breakpoints and output records. Their construction, sorting, type identification, and evaluation of all bundle coordinates use \(O((m+\tau)^2)\) arithmetic operations on \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Using elementary multiplication and division yields the stated bound

\(O((m+\tau)^2L^2)\)

in bit operations. The output length is \(O(m(m+\tau)L)\).

The construction also preserves dyadic masses when all input rationals are dyadic, since output breakpoints are multiples of \(1/D^2\).

Thus the nonexistence report is never needed.

1. Partition and sufficient conditions

The partition into \(H\) and \(Z\) is exhaustive. Since costs belong to \(\{0,1\}\), every class outside \(H\) has a zero-cost recipient among the listed types.

The three proposed properties imply EFX for every required pair of supported bundles:

  • Every comparison bundle \(R\) contains at least \(k\) universally costly chores, so \(c_t(R)\geq k\) for every type \(t\).
  • A low-group recipient has own cost exactly \(k\). Removing any present chore leaves cost at most \(k\), including when the removed chore costs zero.
  • A high-group recipient receives only universally costly chores and has own cost \(k+1\). Removing any present chore leaves cost exactly \(k\).

These inequalities cover all type pairs and all coordinates with \(S_g>0\). Empty bundles impose no removal conditions.

The historical claim about the partition is accurate: the cited paper’s Section 3 partitions chores according to whether every agent incurs cost one or some agent incurs cost zero. This is contextual attribution; the continuous construction does not rely on the finite-agent theorem. [Tao, Wu, Yu, and Zhou, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.12177v1#S3).

2. Pareto optimality

The social-cost bounds are valid for every feasible allocation:
\[ U\leq C(\eta)\leq\sum_g\rho_g<\infty. \]
The lower bound counts universally costly supply; the upper bound uses binary costs and nonnegative supplies. Nonnegative summation justifies these bounds even for countably supported allocations.

The constructed allocation has social cost \(U\), because all other chores cost their recipients zero.

The coupling identity is also justified. The marginal conditions give finite sums for both coupled costs, and the absolute cost difference is bounded by their sum. Thus the displayed subtraction and rearrangement remain valid for countably supported competitors.

On every positive coupling entry, the improvement is nonnegative. On every strict-improvement entry, it is at least one because costs are integers. Positive total mass of strict improvements therefore strictly decreases social cost below \(U\), contradicting the universal lower bound.
