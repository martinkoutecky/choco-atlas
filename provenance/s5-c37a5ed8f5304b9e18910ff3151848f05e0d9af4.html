The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many complete household types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), housing categories \(M\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_x\), and rational piecewise-linear preferences satisfying money monotonicity and possibility of compensation, compute an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and type-to-category assignment masses \(y_{tx}\). Require \(\sum_{x\in M\cup\{0\}}y_{tx}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}y_{tx}\le q_x\), \(y_{tx}>0\Rightarrow x\in D_t(p)\), and \(p_x(q_x-\sum_t y_{tx})=0\); return the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) complete household types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) housing categories \(M\), with positive binary rational per-capita supplies \(q_x\), and an unlimited null category \(0\).
Each household receives at most one category and pays its price. Type \(t\)'s preferences over category-payment pairs are represented by explicitly listed continuous piecewise-linear functions \(u_{tx}:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), with rational breakpoints and coefficients, strictly decreasing in payment. Require possibility of compensation: for every \(x,z\in M\cup\{0\}\) and \(a\in\mathbb R\), some \(b\in\mathbb R\) satisfies \(u_{tx}(a)=u_{tz}(b)\). Utility values may have either sign; there are no household budget constraints.
Prices satisfy \(p_x\ge0\) and \(p_0=0\). Define \(D_t(p)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in M\cup\{0\}}u_{tx}(p_x)\), retaining all ties. An equilibrium consists of prices and assignment masses \(y_{tx}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{x\in M\cup\{0\}}y_{tx}=\mu_t\), and, for every \(x\in M\), \(\sum_t y_{tx}\le q_x\) and \(p_x(q_x-\sum_t y_{tx})=0\); also \(y_{tx}>0\Rightarrow x\in D_t(p)\). Mass may split among tied categories.
The exact computational problem is to return an equilibrium whose price vector is coordinatewise no larger than every other equilibrium price vector. Let \(K\) be the total number of listed linear pieces and \(L\) the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,K,L\).

Is this exact minimum-equilibrium-price computation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Partial result: polynomial time for fixed support size

I do not settle the classification when both $m$ and $\tau$ vary. I prove a new exact algorithm that is polynomial for fixed support size $\tau$, even with arbitrarily many categories and binary rational masses.

Combining this with an independently proved price-space enumeration gives running time

$$ \bigl(K^2+2K+2m+2\tau\bigr)^{\min\{m,\tau\}} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L). $$

The substantive new observation is that every equilibrium price vector is determined by its $\tau$ attained type utilities. The maximum equilibrium utility vector is a vertex of an explicitly constructed arrangement in $\mathbb R^\tau$.

1. Inverses and existence of minimum equilibrium prices

Write

$$ h_t=u_{t0}(0). $$

Every utility function in the input is a continuous, strictly decreasing bijection from $\mathbb R$ to $\mathbb R$. Indeed, its slope on each linear interval of positive length is negative. In particular, its two unbounded tails have negative slopes, giving limits $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ at the two ends of the real line.

For each real category define the inverse-payment function

$$ r_{tx}(w)=u_{tx}^{-1}(w). $$

These functions are continuous, strictly decreasing, and piecewise linear. If a utility piece is

$$ u_{tx}(a)=\alpha a+\beta, \qquad \alpha<0, $$

then its inverse piece is

$$ r_{tx}(w)=\frac{w-\beta}{\alpha}. $$

An inverse breakpoint corresponding to a payment breakpoint $b$ is $u_{tx}(b)$. Thus all inverse pieces and breakpoints are rational, have binary lengths $O(L)$, and can be constructed in polynomial time. The number of inverse pieces for real categories is at most $K$.

We will use the following finite-market theorem: for finitely many unit-demand agents and objects, preferences satisfying strict payment monotonicity and possibility of compensation admit an equilibrium with nonnegative prices and a zero-price null object; moreover, there is an equilibrium price vector coordinatewise no larger than every other equilibrium price vector. Identical objects are permitted. These are Facts 1–2 in [Zhou–Serizawa, Section 3](https://www.iser.osaka-u.ac.jp/library/dp/2019/DP1047.pdf).

Here is the transfer to the stated mass model.

Choose a positive integer $N$ clearing the denominators of every $\mu_t$ and $q_x$. Construct a finite market with $N\mu_t$ agents of type $t$ and $Nq_x$ copies of category $x$. Give each copy of $x$ the utility function $u_{tx}$ for type $t$. The input utilities represent complete, transitive preferences and satisfy the finite theorem’s monotonicity and compensation hypotheses.

In any finite equilibrium, copies of the same category have equal prices. Otherwise, if two copies have prices $a<b$, every agent strictly prefers the cheaper copy. The more expensive copy is unassigned, so its equilibrium price must be zero, contradicting $b>a\ge0$.

Consequently, a finite equilibrium projects to a mass equilibrium by dividing assignment counts by $N$.

Conversely, suppose $(p,y)$ is a mass equilibrium. After scaling by $N$, its assignment is a feasible transportation flow with:

  • integer type totals $N\mu_t$;
  • integer category upper capacities $Nq_x$;
  • exact category totals $Nq_x$ whenever $p_x>0$;
  • only demanded type-category edges;
  • a null-category capacity of $N$.

The integral-flow theorem supplies an integral feasible flow with the same constraints. Distribute its units among the agent and object copies. Every agent receives a demanded object or the null object, and every positive-price copy is assigned. This constructs a finite equilibrium at the replicated prices $p$.

The two markets therefore have exactly corresponding equilibrium price vectors. The finite minimum projects to the coordinatewise minimum price vector of the mass market, denoted $p^*$.

This replication proves existence; its potentially exponential number of agents is not used by the algorithms below.

2. Equilibrium utilities determine all prices

For any nonnegative price vector define the attained utility of type $t$ by

$$ v_t(p)= \max\left\{h_t,\max_{x\in M}u_{tx}(p_x)\right\}. $$

Also define

$$ U_t= \max\left\{h_t,\max_{x\in M}u_{tx}(0)\right\}. $$

Payment monotonicity implies

$$ h_t\le v_t(p)\le U_t. $$

Hence all equilibrium utility vectors lie in the rational box

$$ \mathcal V=\prod_{t\in T}[h_t,U_t]. $$

For $w\in\mathcal V$, define a price vector by

$$ P_x(w)= \max\left\{0,\max_{t\in T}r_{tx}(w_t)\right\}, \qquad x\in M, $$

and set $P_0(w)=0$.

Lemma 1. If $(p,y)$ is an equilibrium and $w_t=v_t(p)$, then $p=P(w)$.

Proof. For every type and real category,

$$ u_{tx}(p_x)\le w_t. $$

Since $u_{tx}$ is strictly decreasing, this is equivalent to

$$ p_x\ge r_{tx}(w_t). $$

Thus $p_x\ge P_x(w)$.

If $p_x=0$, this already forces $P_x(w)=0$. If $p_x>0$, complementarity gives

$$ \sum_t y_{tx}=q_x>0. $$

There is therefore a type $t$ with $y_{tx}>0$. Demand support gives

$$ u_{tx}(p_x)=w_t, $$

so $p_x=r_{tx}(w_t)\le P_x(w)$. The two inequalities establish equality. $\square$

Define the eligible assignment edges at $w$ by

$$ E(w)= \left\{(t,x):x\in M,\ r_{tx}(w_t)=P_x(w)\right\} \;\cup\; \left\{(t,0):w_t=h_t\right\}. $$

Lemma 2. A vector $w\in\mathcal V$ is an equilibrium utility vector if and only if there is an assignment $y$ satisfying the original mass and capacity constraints, supported on $E(w)$, and satisfying

$$ \sum_t y_{tx}=q_x \quad\text{whenever }P_x(w)>0. $$

When such an assignment exists, $(P(w),y)$ is an equilibrium and its attained utilities are exactly $w$.

Proof. Necessity follows from Lemma 1 and demand support.

For sufficiency, the definition of $P$ gives

$$ P_x(w)\ge r_{tx}(w_t), $$

and hence

$$ u_{tx}(P_x(w))\le w_t. $$

The null utility also satisfies $h_t\le w_t$. Every eligible real edge attains equality, as does an eligible null edge.

Because $\mu_t>0$ and the type’s assignment masses sum to $\mu_t$, each type receives positive mass on at least one eligible edge. Consequently,

$$ v_t(P(w))=w_t. $$

Every positive assignment is thus demanded. The stipulated occupancy equalities give complementarity at positive prices, while zero prices satisfy complementarity automatically. $\square$

A useful consequence is that minimum prices correspond to maximum attained utilities.

Lemma 3. Let

$$ w_t^*=v_t(p^*). $$

Then every equilibrium utility vector $w$ satisfies $w\le w^*$ coordinatewise.

Proof. Its equilibrium prices $p$ satisfy $p\ge p^*$. For every real category,

$$ u_{tx}(p_x)\le u_{tx}(p_x^*). $$

Taking maxima, including the unchanged null utility, gives $w_t\le w_t^*$. $\square$

By Lemma 1, the greatest equilibrium utility vector determines the required prices:

$$ p^*=P(w^*). $$

3. A polynomial feasibility test at specified utilities

Given rational $w\in\mathcal V$, compute $P(w)$ and $E(w)$ exactly.

To test the condition in Lemma 2, construct a circulation with a source $s$, a sink $d$, one vertex per type, and one vertex per category, including the null category. Its arcs have the following lower and upper bounds:

  • $s\to t$: both bounds equal $\mu_t$;
  • $t\to x$, for $(t,x)\in E(w)$: lower bound zero and upper bound $\mu_t$;
  • $x\to d$, for real $x$: upper bound $q_x$, and lower bound $q_x$ if $P_x(w)>0$, otherwise zero;
  • $0\to d$: bounds zero and $1$;
  • $d\to s$: both bounds equal $1$.

Conservation at a type enforces its assignment total. Conservation at a category enforces its capacity and, where required, full occupancy. Thus the circulation is feasible exactly when Lemma 2’s assignment exists.

The network has $O(m+\tau)$ vertices and $O(\tau(m+1))$ arcs. Lower-bound circulation reduces to maximum flow, so feasibility and an assignment can be computed in polynomial bit time.

The assignment can always have polynomial encoding length. Choose $N$ as in Section 1, for instance as the product of the input denominators. Then $\log N=O(L)$. Scaling the circulation by $N$ gives integer bounds, so an integral feasible circulation exists whenever it is feasible. Each type-category flow is at most $N\mu_t\le N$. Dividing by $N$ gives assignment entries of binary length $O(L)$.

4. An arrangement in utility space

Construct a set $\mathcal H_v$ of rational hyperplanes in $\mathbb R^\tau$ containing:

  • the box boundaries $w_t=h_t$ and $w_t=U_t$;
  • every inverse-breakpoint hyperplane $w_t=c$;
  • $f(w_t)=0$ for every listed affine piece $f$ of every $r_{tx}$;
  • $f(w_t)=g(w_s)$ for distinct types $t,s$, the same real category $x$, and every pair of affine pieces $f,g$ of $r_{tx},r_{sx}$.

Delete duplicates. Comparisons between pieces outside their actual domains merely add extra hyperplanes and cause no difficulty.

If $H_v=|\mathcal H_v|$, then

$$ H_v\le K^2+2K+2\tau. $$

Indeed, there are at most $K$ inverse breakpoints, at most $K$ equations comparing a piece to zero, at most $K^2$ pairwise piece comparisons, and $2\tau$ box boundaries.

Lemma 4. Suppose two sufficiently close utility vectors belong to the same arrangement face inside $\mathcal V$. Then their eligible edge sets $E(w)$ and their positive-price category sets are identical.

Proof. The inverse-breakpoint hyperplanes fix the affine piece used by each inverse function. If a coordinate lies at an inverse breakpoint, remaining in the same face keeps that coordinate fixed.

The piece-comparison hyperplanes preserve the ordering and all equalities among the values

$$ \{r_{tx}(w_t):t\in T\} $$

for each category $x$. The zero-comparison hyperplanes also preserve their signs.

If their maximum is positive, the types attaining $P_x(w)$ therefore remain exactly the same. If their maximum is nonpositive, $P_x(w)=0$, and exactly the types whose inverse value is zero are eligible. These zero equalities also remain unchanged.

Finally, eligibility for the null category is the equality $w_t=h_t$, which is preserved by the box-boundary hyperplanes. $\square$

It follows from Lemma 2 that feasibility is constant on each such face: the permissible assignment edges and the categories requiring full occupancy do not change.

Theorem 5. The greatest equilibrium utility vector $w^*$ is the intersection of $\tau$ hyperplanes in $\mathcal H_v$ with linearly independent normals.

Proof. Suppose the normals of all hyperplanes containing $w^*$ have rank less than $\tau$. Choose a nonzero vector $d$ orthogonal to all those normals.

For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both

$$ w^*+\varepsilon d \quad\text{and}\quad w^*-\varepsilon d $$

remain inside $\mathcal V$ and in the same arrangement face as $w^*$. To justify this, hyperplanes through $w^*$ are preserved by orthogonality, and the finitely many remaining hyperplanes retain their strict signs for small enough $\varepsilon$. The included box boundaries ensure that boundary coordinates stay fixed.

Take an equilibrium assignment $y^*$ at $p^*$. Lemma 4 preserves its eligible edges and all categories requiring full occupancy. Lemma 2 consequently shows that the same assignment yields equilibria at both perturbed utility vectors.

Since $d\ne0$, one of these perturbations increases at least one utility coordinate. This contradicts Lemma 3, which says that every equilibrium utility vector is coordinatewise at most $w^*$.

The normals therefore have rank $\tau$. Choosing $\tau$ independent ones proves the assertion. $\square$

Exact enumeration and running time

Enumerate every $\tau$-element subset of $\mathcal H_v$. For each subset:

  • discard it if its normals are dependent;
  • solve its linear system exactly;
  • discard solutions outside $\mathcal V$;
  • apply the circulation feasibility test from Section 3.

Among feasible candidates, select one maximizing

$$ \sum_{t\in T}w_t. $$

Theorem 5 guarantees that $w^*$ appears. By Lemma 3, every feasible candidate satisfies $w\le w^*$. Equality of the coordinate sums is possible only when $w=w^*$. Thus the selected candidate is exactly $w^*$, and the algorithm returns $(P(w^*),y)$.

Its running time is

$$ \binom{H_v}{\tau}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L) \le \bigl(K^2+2K+2\tau\bigr)^\tau \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L). $$

This is polynomial for every fixed $\tau$.

Exact encoding lengths

Each arrangement equation has only constantly many rational coefficients, all with binary length $O(L)$. After clearing the denominators in each row, its integer coefficients still have binary length $O(L)$.

For an independent $\tau$-equation system, Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound give coordinate lengths

$$ O\bigl(\tau(L+\log(\tau+1))\bigr). $$

Evaluating inverse affine pieces at these coordinates preserves a polynomial length bound. Thus the algorithm uses exact rational arithmetic with polynomial-length numbers at each candidate; its exponential factor comes from enumeration, not arithmetic precision.

5. Combining utility-space and price-space enumeration

For completeness, the price-space algorithm used in the combined bound can be proved directly.

Define

$$ B_x=\max\left\{0,\max_t r_{tx}(h_t)\right\}. $$

Every equilibrium satisfies $0\le p_x\le B_x$. Otherwise, if $p_x>B_x$, every type strictly prefers the null category to $x$. The category receives zero mass, contradicting complementarity because $p_x>0$ and $q_x>0$.

In the box

$$ \mathcal B=\prod_{x\in M}[0,B_x], $$

construct hyperplanes for:

  • its boundaries;
  • every real-category utility breakpoint;
  • every comparison between affine pieces of two distinct real categories for the same type;
  • every comparison of a real-category affine piece with $h_t$.

Their number satisfies

$$ H_p\le K^2+2K+2m. $$

The minimum price vector $p^*$ is an arrangement vertex. Indeed, if the normals through $p^*$ had rank less than $m$, a nonzero orthogonal direction would permit two small opposite perturbations preserving the box, utility pieces, all demand ties and comparisons, and the zero-price categories. The original assignment would remain an equilibrium assignment at both perturbed prices. One perturbation would lower a coordinate, contradicting minimum prices.

Enumerating intersections of $m$ independent hyperplanes therefore includes $p^*$. At each candidate, evaluate demand and use the circulation of Section 3, with type-category edges restricted to demand, to test equilibrium feasibility. Among feasible candidates, minimizing $\sum_xp_x$ selects $p^*$ because every equilibrium price vector dominates it.

The resulting time bound is

$$ \bigl(K^2+2K+2m\bigr)^m \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L). $$

Choosing price-space enumeration when $m\le\tau$ and utility-space enumeration otherwise gives

$$ \boxed{ \bigl(K^2+2K+2m+2\tau\bigr)^{\min\{m,\tau\}} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,L). } $$

6. An explicit polynomial algorithm for one supported type

The case $\tau=1$ also admits a simpler formula, without arrangements. This case permits arbitrarily many categories, arbitrary listed payment utilities, and arbitrary positive rational supplies.

Suppress the type index. Its mass is $1$. Set

$$ h=u_0(0), \qquad a_x=u_x(0). $$

For a prospective attained utility $w\ge h$, the reconstructed prices are

$$ P_x(w)=\max\{0,r_x(w)\}. $$

Strict decrease gives three cases:

  • if $a_x>w$, then $P_x(w)>0$, and category $x$ must receive its full supply $q_x$;
  • if $a_x=w$, then $P_x(w)=0$, and category $x$ can receive any mass up to $q_x$;
  • if $a_x<w$, then $P_x(w)=0$ and category $x$ is not demanded.

The null category is eligible exactly when $w=h$. Define

$$ Q(w)=\sum_{x:a_x>w}q_x, \qquad R(w)=\sum_{x:a_x\ge w}q_x. $$

By the three cases above, a utility level $w=h$ is feasible exactly when $Q(h)\le1$. A level $w>h$ is feasible exactly when

$$ Q(w)\le1\le R(w). $$

List the distinct values above $h$ in decreasing order:

$$ b_1>b_2>\cdots>b_k>h, $$

and define cumulative supplies

$$ C_j=\sum_{x:a_x\ge b_j}q_x, \qquad C_0=0. $$

If no $C_j$ reaches $1$, then $Q(h)<1$. Fill every category with $a_x>h$ to capacity and assign the remaining mass to the null category. This achieves utility $h$. Every utility level above $h$ has total eligible real supply less than $1$, so none is feasible. Thus $w^*=h$.

Otherwise, let $j$ be the smallest index with $C_j\ge1$. At $w=b_j$,

$$ Q(b_j)=C_{j-1}<1\le C_j=R(b_j). $$

Fill the categories with $a_x>b_j$ to capacity and distribute the remaining mass among categories with $a_x=b_j$. This achieves utility $b_j$.

For any $w>b_j$, every eligible real category has $a_x>b_j$, so its total eligible supply is at most $C_{j-1}<1$. The null category is ineligible. Hence no greater utility level is feasible, and $w^*=b_j$.

Finally return

$$ p_x^*=\max\{0,r_x(w^*)\}. $$

For any other equilibrium utility $w\le w^*$, decreasing inverses imply $P(w^*)\le P(w)$ coordinatewise. The returned prices are therefore minimum.

Computing the zero-payment utilities, sorting, scanning cumulative supplies, and performing the inversions takes

$$ O(K+m\log m) $$

rational arithmetic operations, with polynomial bit complexity. Utility signs play no role in the argument.

7. What remains open

The utility-space reduction resolves the problem for every fixed support size, complementing the fixed-category result. It also proves that the required output has polynomial encoding length.

The unrestricted classification remains unproved. Both exact enumeration algorithms have an exponent that grows when $m$ and $\tau$ grow together. Replacing rational masses by agent copies still does not give a polynomial algorithm in the original binary input length, because the replication factor can be exponential.

A further substantive result is required: either an algorithm locating the relevant vertex in time polynomial in all of $m,\tau,K,L$, or a valid hardness reduction for the exact mass model. No such result is established here.
