The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(A\) with binary-rational population fractions \(\alpha_a\) summing to \(1\), seat types \(B\) with binary-rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_b\), compatible pairs \(E\), and bounded integer utilities \(u_i(a,b)\), find a nonnegative allocation \(x_{ab}\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\beta_b\) whose profile \((P_1(x),\ldots,P_r(x))\), with \(P_i(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}u_i(a,b)x_{ab}\), is lexicographically maximal.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given disjoint finite sets of applicant types \(A\) and seat types \(B\), let \(E\subseteq A\times B\) be the compatible pairs and \(m=|E|\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Applicant masses satisfy \(\alpha_a>0\) and \(\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a=1\); seat masses are per-capita capacities \(\beta_b>0\), with no prescribed total.
The input includes \(r\geq1\) utility functions \(u_i:E\to\{0,1,\ldots,U_i\}\), with nonnegative integer bounds \(U_i\) and all utilities encoded in binary. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of divisible masses \(x_{ab}\geq0\) for \((a,b)\in E\), satisfying \(\sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\beta_b\). Unmatched applicant mass and unused capacity are permitted; incompatible pairs receive no mass.
Its profile is \((P_1(x),\ldots,P_r(x))\), where \(P_i(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}u_i(a,b)x_{ab}\); maximize it lexicographically, with \(P_1\) having highest priority. Any allocation attaining the optimal profile is acceptable.

Is finding an exact rational allocation with lexicographically maximal profile polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,r\) and the utility bounds \(U_i\).

Result

Finding an exact rational allocation with lexicographically maximal profile is polynomial-time solvable. This holds when the number of utilities and all masses and utilities are encoded in binary.

The proof uses one minimum-cost-flow problem on a network with \(O(\tau)\) vertices and \(O(m+\tau)\) arcs. Clearing denominators does not require expanding the society into individual applicants.

If \(m=0\), the empty allocation is optimal. Below, assume \(m>0\).

1. Clearing denominators

Every feasible allocation satisfies

\( \displaystyle \sum_{(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a=1. \)

Consequently, replacing each seat capacity by \(\min\{\beta_b,1\}\) leaves the feasible set unchanged. Make this replacement.

Write every mass as a fraction with positive integer denominator, and let \(D\) be the product of these denominators. Then

\( \displaystyle A_a=D\alpha_a,\qquad B_b=D\beta_b \)

are positive integers, with

\( \displaystyle \sum_{a\in A}A_a=D,\qquad B_b\leq D. \)

Because the denominator encodings form part of the input,

\( \displaystyle \log_2 D\leq L. \)

Set \(y_{ab}=Dx_{ab}\). The scaled feasible polytope is

\( \displaystyle Q=\left\{y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}: \sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}y_{ab}\leq A_a\ \text{for every }a,\quad \sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}y_{ab}\leq B_b\ \text{for every }b \right\}. \)

Scaling gives a bijection between feasible allocations and \(Q\). Define

\( \displaystyle T_i(y)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}u_i(a,b)y_{ab}=DP_i(x). \)

Multiplying every profile coordinate by the same positive number preserves lexicographic order. Thus it suffices to optimize \((T_1,\ldots,T_r)\) over \(Q\).

The polytope \(Q\) is nonempty because it contains zero, and compact because every coordinate lies between zero and \(D\).

2. Every vertex of the scaled polytope is integral

Let \(C\) be the matrix of the applicant and seat constraints. Each column, corresponding to an edge \((a,b)\), contains exactly two entries equal to one: one in row \(a\), and one in row \(b\).

Multiply every seat row by minus one. The resulting matrix has one entry equal to one and one equal to minus one in each column.

We verify total unimodularity directly. Consider any square submatrix.

  • If some column has at most one nonzero entry, expansion along that column reduces its determinant to zero or, up to sign, the determinant of a smaller square submatrix.
  • Otherwise every column contains both its nonzero entries. Each column then sums to zero, so the rows are linearly dependent and the determinant is zero.

Induction on the size of the submatrix proves that every square determinant is zero, one, or minus one. Changing row signs preserves this property. Adding the nonnegativity rows from \(-I\) also preserves it, by expansion along those rows. Hence the full constraint matrix defining \(Q\) is totally unimodular.

At any vertex, choose \(m\) linearly independent active constraints. Their coefficient matrix has determinant one or minus one, and their right-hand sides are integers. Its inverse therefore has integer entries. The vertex is integral.

In particular, at every vertex \(v\),

\( \displaystyle T_i(v)\in\mathbb Z,\qquad 0\leq T_i(v)\leq DU_i. \)

The upper bound follows from nonnegative utilities and \(\sum_e v_e\leq D\).

This integrality statement concerns vertices. Arbitrary feasible divisible allocations need not lie on this grid.

3. One scalar objective suffices

Define integer radices and priority multipliers by

\( \displaystyle R_i=DU_i+1,\qquad W_i=\prod_{j=i+1}^{r}R_j, \)

where an empty product equals one. Define the edge weights

\( \displaystyle c_{ab}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}u_i(a,b)W_i. \)

The corresponding scalar objective is

\( \displaystyle S(y)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}c_{ab}y_{ab} =\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_iT_i(y). \)

Claim. For two vertices \(v,w\) with different profiles, the sign of \(S(v)-S(w)\) agrees with their lexicographic comparison.

Let \(k\) be the first differing coordinate, and suppose \(T_k(v)>T_k(w)\). Integrality gives

\( \displaystyle T_k(v)-T_k(w)\geq1. \)

The greatest possible loss from all lower-priority coordinates is bounded by

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i=k+1}^{r}DU_iW_i =\sum_{i=k+1}^{r}(R_i-1)W_i =W_k-1. \)

The last equality telescopes because \(R_iW_i=W_{i-1}\). Therefore

\( \displaystyle S(v)-S(w)\geq W_k-(W_k-1)=1. \)

This proves the claim. It also covers zero utility bounds: when \(U_i=0\), the corresponding radix is one and that coordinate is identically zero.

Why this settles the continuous problem

Let \(p^\star\) be the lexicographically greatest profile among the finitely many vertices of \(Q\).

Every point of \(Q\) is a convex combination of vertices. Its first profile coordinate cannot exceed \(p^\star_1\). If equality holds, every vertex used with positive coefficient must attain \(p^\star_1\). Among those vertices, the second coordinate cannot exceed \(p^\star_2\). Repeating this argument proves that \(p^\star\) is lexicographically maximal over all of \(Q\).

By the preceding claim, precisely the vertices with profile \(p^\star\) maximize \(S\). If an arbitrary point maximizes \(S\), every vertex appearing with positive coefficient in a convex decomposition must also maximize \(S\); otherwise the scalar value would be strictly smaller. All those vertices have profile \(p^\star\), so their convex combination does too.

Thus every maximizer of \(S\) over \(Q\) is lexicographically optimal.

4. Reduction to minimum-cost flow

Construct a directed network with vertices \(s,t\), the applicant types, and the seat types. All lower capacities are zero.

| Arc | Upper capacity | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(s\to a\) | \(A_a\) | zero |
| \(a\to b\), for \((a,b)\in E\) | \(D\) | \(-c_{ab}\) |
| \(b\to t\) | \(B_b\) | zero |
| \(a\to t\) | \(A_a\) | zero |

Require a flow of value \(D\). The last family of arcs represents unmatched applicants.

A feasible flow always exists: send \(A_a\) units along \(s\to a\to t\) for each applicant type.

Because the total capacity leaving \(s\) equals \(D\), every feasible flow of value \(D\) saturates all arcs \(s\to a\). Its flows on compatible arcs therefore define a point \(y\in Q\).

Conversely, any \(y\in Q\) extends to a flow of value \(D\): send

\( \displaystyle A_a-\sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}y_{ab} \)

along \(a\to t\), and send \(\sum_a y_{ab}\) along \(b\to t\).

The flow cost is exactly \(-S(y)\). Hence a minimum-cost flow yields the required allocation

\( \displaystyle x_{ab}=\frac{y_{ab}}{D}. \)

All capacities and costs are integers. Standard integral minimum-cost-flow algorithms return an integral optimal flow, so this formula produces an exact rational allocation.

The network has

\( \displaystyle N=\tau+2,\qquad M=m+2|A|+|B|\leq m+2\tau. \)

Every capacity is at most \(D\). Feasibility and boundedness have been established, so the hypotheses of the minimum-cost-flow algorithms used below hold.

5. Encoding lengths and running times

All logarithms in this section have base two. Define

\( \displaystyle d=1+\left\lceil\log_2(D+1)\right\rceil,\qquad s_U=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lceil\log_2(U_i+1)\right\rceil, \)

and use the bit-length bound

\( \displaystyle H=L+r(d+1)+s_U+ \left\lceil\log_2(m+\tau+r+2)\right\rceil+2. \)

Then \(d=O(L+1)\) and

\( \displaystyle H=O\bigl((r+1)(L+1)+s_U\bigr). \)

To check the weight lengths, put \(R=\prod_iR_i\). The same telescoping identity gives

\( \displaystyle 0\leq c_{ab} \leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}DU_iW_i=R-1. \)

Furthermore,

\( \displaystyle DU_i+1\leq D(U_i+1),\qquad \log_2R\leq r\log_2D+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\log_2(U_i+1). \)

Thus all constructed capacities and weights have \(O(H)\) bits.

An explicit polynomial bit bound

Capacity scaling solves an integral minimum-cost-flow instance using \(O((N+M)\log(D+1))\) shortest-path computations; this classical bound is recorded in [Orlin’s minimum-cost-flow paper](https://personal.math.ubc.ca/~anstee/math523/StronglyPolynomialNetworkFlow.pdf). Implementing shortest paths with Bellman–Ford gives

\( \displaystyle O\bigl(NM(N+M)d\bigr) =O\bigl(NM^2d\bigr) \)

arithmetic operations, since here \(M\geq\tau=N-2\).

For completeness, the numerical quantities in this implementation have polynomial bit length. Flows and capacities are bounded by \(D\). If \(C=\max_e c_e\), finite shortest-path distances in a residual graph without negative cycles have absolute value at most \(NC\), because a shortest path can be chosen simple. Residual potentials can be recomputed from a zero-cost auxiliary source, giving the same bound. These are the quantities used by capacity scaling and shortest-path augmentation. They have \(O(H)\) bits.

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, a conservative total bit-operation bound, including construction and output, is therefore

\( \displaystyle O\!\left( \left[L+mr+(\tau+2)(m+\tau)^2(L+1)\right]H^2 \right). \)

The construction term follows by multiplying the denominators, forming the scaled masses and priority multipliers, and computing the \(m\) edge weights using \(r\) utilities each. Each such integer operation uses at most \(O(H^2)\) bit operations.

This bound is polynomial in the input length. Its dependence on utility magnitudes is through their binary encoding lengths, rather than through the numerical values \(U_i\).

A faster arithmetic-operation bound

Alternatively, Orlin’s strongly polynomial capacitated minimum-cost-flow algorithm uses

\( \displaystyle O\bigl(M\log N\,(M+N\log N)\bigr) \)

arithmetic operations. Consequently, including the arithmetic needed to construct the instance, one obtains

\( \displaystyle O\!\left( \tau+mr+ (m+\tau)\log(\tau+2) \left[m+\tau+(\tau+2)\log(\tau+2)\right] \right). \)

This is an arithmetic-operation bound, not a bit-operation bound; reading, manipulating, and outputting binary numbers still costs time depending on \(L\). The cited theorem supplies the flow-stage bound. [Orlin, 1993](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.41.2.338).

Finally, each output numerator is at most \(D\), and the common denominator is \(D\). Thus the allocation has total rational encoding length \(O(m(L+1))\), apart from edge identifiers.

6. Relation to the discrete anchor

The [given source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00047v1) concerns discrete bipartite matchings and mixed-radix edge weights. The argument above independently establishes the divisible result: denominator scaling gives integral vertices, mixed-radix weights compare their profiles, and convexity extends the conclusion to every feasible allocation. The network uses only the listed support types counted by \(\tau\).
