The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given resident types \(R\) and position types \(H\), with binary-rational masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_h\) summing to \(1\) on each side, strict preference lists of length at most three, and an acquaintance relation \(A\) on acceptable type pairs, does there exist a nonnegative flow \(x_{rh}\) on acceptable pairs such that \(\sum_h x_{rh}=\mu_r\) and \(\sum_r x_{rh}=\nu_h\), and for every \((r,h)\in A\), either \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\)? Here \(D_{rh}\) is the mass of type \(r\) assigned below \(h\), and \(W_{rh}\) is the mass of type \(h\) assigned below \(r\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(R\) and \(H\) be resident and position types. Each side is a society given by its support: a list of types with positive binary-rational masses, with \(\sum_{r\in R}\mu_r=\sum_{h\in H}\nu_h=1\). Write \(\tau=|R|+|H|\) for the total support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type strictly ranks at most three opposite-side types. Acceptability is mutual; let \(E\subseteq R\times H\) be the acceptable pairs and \(A\subseteq E\) the acquainted pairs. Acquaintance applies to every individual pair having those types. Unacquainted acceptable pairs may receive assignments but cannot block.
A complete assignment is a divisible flow \(x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}\) satisfying \(\sum_{h:(r,h)\in E}x_{rh}=\mu_r\) and \(\sum_{r:(r,h)\in E}x_{rh}=\nu_h\).
Writing \(\succ_r\) and \(\succ_h\) for the strict preferences, define \(D_{rh}=\sum_{h':(r,h')\in E,\ h\succ_r h'}x_{rh'}\) and \(W_{rh}=\sum_{r':(r',h)\in E,\ r\succ_h r'}x_{r'h}\). The assignment is socially stable if every \((r,h)\in A\) satisfies \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\).
Let \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding whether a complete socially stable assignment exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finite one-to-one societies with strict lists of length at most three on both sides, existence of a complete socially stable matching is NP-complete ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.2041v3)). No result settling this exact continuous formulation was identified in the literature search.

  • Made acceptability explicitly mutual, as in the discrete definition, and distinguished acceptable pairs E from acquainted pairs A.
  • Interpreted type-level acquaintance uniformly: every individual resident-position pair with an acquainted type pair can block.
  • Specified that flow values may be arbitrary nonnegative reals and that masses are divisible.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size of the two separately normalized societies, m as the number of acceptable type pairs, and L as total binary input length.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when every type has the same dyadic mass and both preference-list lengths are at most three.

The key fact is that, with equal masses, every complete socially stable fractional assignment contains a complete socially stable discrete matching in its positive support.

1. A support-preserving rounding lemma

Lemma. Suppose \(|R|=|H|=n\) and every type has mass \(1/n\). A complete socially stable assignment exists if and only if the corresponding finite one-to-one instance has a perfect socially stable matching.

Proof. First suppose \(x\) is a complete socially stable assignment. Define its positive support by

\(S=\{(r,h)\in E:x_{rh}>0\}\).

We show that the bipartite graph \((R,H,S)\) has a perfect matching. For any subset \(U\subseteq R\), let \(N_S(U)\) denote its neighborhood in this graph. Completeness and nonnegativity give

\[ \frac{|U|}{n} =\sum_{r\in U}\sum_{h\in N_S(U)}x_{rh} \leq \sum_{h\in N_S(U)}\sum_{r\in R}x_{rh} =\frac{|N_S(U)|}{n}. \]

Thus \(|N_S(U)|\geq |U|\) for every \(U\subseteq R\). Hall’s marriage theorem therefore supplies a matching \(M\subseteq S\) saturating \(R\); because the two sides have equal size, \(M\) is perfect.

We next prove that every perfect matching contained in \(S\) is socially stable. Suppose otherwise that an acquainted pair \((r,h)\in A\) blocks such a matching \(M\). Write \(M(r)\) for the position assigned to \(r\), and \(M(h)\) for the resident assigned to \(h\). Blocking means

\(h\succ_r M(r)\) and \(r\succ_h M(h)\).

Because \(M\subseteq S\), both \(x_{r,M(r)}>0\) and \(x_{M(h),h}>0\). These entries occur in the respective dissatisfaction sums, so

\(D_{rh}\geq x_{r,M(r)}>0\) and \(W_{rh}\geq x_{M(h),h}>0\).

This contradicts social stability of \(x\).

Conversely, suppose \(M\) is a perfect socially stable matching. Set \(x_{rh}=1/n\) on its edges and zero elsewhere. This satisfies all marginal equalities. For any acquainted pair, its two dissatisfaction sums are simultaneously positive exactly when both endpoints strictly prefer each other to their partners in \(M\). Social stability of \(M\) excludes this possibility. Hence \(x\) is socially stable. \(\square\)

The lemma uses precisely the stated disjunctive condition \(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\).

2. Polynomial reduction and dyadic normalization

The discrete source problem asks whether a finite one-to-one society admits a matching covering every agent that has no acquainted blocking pair. It is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with strict lists of length at most three on both sides, by [Askalidis et al., Theorem 4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.2041).

We may restrict to instances with equally many agents on both sides: unequal sizes immediately imply a negative answer and can be mapped to a fixed balanced negative instance. Let a balanced source instance have \(n\geq 1\) agents on each side.

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Add \(N-n\) isolated resident-position pairs. Each added agent finds only its designated partner acceptable, and that pair is acquainted. There are no acceptable edges between these pairs and the original instance.

The padded instance has a perfect socially stable matching if and only if the original instance does:

  • Any original perfect socially stable matching extends by matching every added pair. Each added agent receives its only acceptable partner, so no added pair blocks.
  • Any perfect matching of the padded instance must use every added pair. Its restriction to the original agents is perfect, and any original acquainted blocking pair would also block the padded matching.

Now create one type for each padded agent, copy the acceptable edges, strict preferences, and acquaintance relation, and assign every type mass \(1/N\). Since \(N\) is a power of two, these are positive dyadic masses. Each side has total mass one, and every list still has length at most three.

By the rounding lemma, the resulting continuous instance has a complete socially stable assignment if and only if the padded discrete instance has a perfect socially stable matching. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

For size and running time, if the source has \(m_0\) acceptable edges and binary input length \(L_0\), then

\(\tau=2N<4n\), and \(m=m_0+(N-n)\).

Each mass needs \(O(\log N)\) bits, so the output length is \(L=O(L_0+n\log n)\). The reduction takes \(O(L_0+n\log n)\) bit operations under an explicit list encoding. In particular, it does not expand a society into a number of individuals determined by its mass denominators.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For each acquainted pair \((r,h)\in A\), guess one of the two equations

\(D_{rh}=0\) or \(W_{rh}=0\).

The certificate consists of \(|A|\leq m\) bits.

Every entry of \(x\) is nonnegative. Consequently:

  • Choosing \(D_{rh}=0\) requires deleting every edge \((r,h')\) with \(h\succ_r h'\).
  • Choosing \(W_{rh}=0\) requires deleting every edge \((r',h)\) with \(r\succ_h r'\).

Let \(F\subseteq E\) be the edges remaining after all these deletions. Since each preference list has length at most three, processing the guesses requires \(O(m+\tau)\) elementary graph operations.

For this certificate, feasibility is exactly a transportation-flow problem on \(F\). Construct a network with source \(s\), sink \(t\), and arcs

\(s\to r\) of capacity \(\mu_r\), \(r\to h\) of capacity \(1\) for \((r,h)\in F\), and \(h\to t\) of capacity \(\nu_h\).

A flow of value one saturates every source arc and every sink arc: their capacities sum to one on each side. Its middle-arc values therefore form a complete assignment. Conversely, every complete assignment supported on \(F\) yields a flow of value one. Capacity one on middle arcs imposes no additional restriction because total assigned mass is one.

The verifier is correct in both directions. A socially stable assignment satisfies at least one of the guessed equations for each acquainted pair, and the corresponding deletions leave its support intact. Conversely, a complete assignment on the remaining edges satisfies every selected zero equation and is therefore socially stable.

For an explicit bit-complexity bound, write the input masses as binary-encoded rational numbers and let \(Q\) be the product of their denominators. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Multiplying all capacities by \(Q\) produces integer capacities with \(O(L)\) bits; feasibility becomes the question whether the maximum flow has value \(Q\).

Using elementary integer arithmetic, constructing these scaled capacities takes \(O(L^3)\) bit operations. The network has \(\tau+2\) vertices and at most \(m+\tau\) arcs. Edmonds–Karp uses \(O(\tau(m+\tau)^2)\) elementary operations, each involving integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Thus a certificate can be verified in

\(O\!\left(L^3+\tau(m+\tau)^2L\right)\)

bit operations. This is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Conclusion and running times

Combining the reduction with the verifier proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for the exact continuous formulation.

Enumerating all certificates also gives a deterministic decision algorithm with running time

\(O\!\left(L^3+2^{|A|}\tau(m+\tau)^2L\right)\),

and hence at most

\(O\!\left(L^3+2^m\tau(m+\tau)^2L\right)\).

Because the degree bound implies \(m\leq 3\tau/2\), this is also \(2^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\). A polynomial-time algorithm for all instances would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
