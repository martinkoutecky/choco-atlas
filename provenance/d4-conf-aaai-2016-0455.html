The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a fixed three-candidate axis \(L\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \(t=(w,b)\), where \(w\) is the voting weight and \(b\) is a legal top-truncated single-peaked ballot, a preferred candidate \(p\), and a budget \(\beta\), can at most \(\beta\) mass be bribed so that \(p\) is the unique winner under eliminate(veto), using the paper’s round-up and vote-deletion conventions?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a candidate set \(C\) with \(m=3\) and an axis \(\ell\). Legal ballots are nonempty prefixes of strict single-peaked rankings on \(\ell\); omitted candidates are tied below all listed candidates. Complete ballots are allowed.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \(t=(w,b)\) with positive mass \(\mu_t\), each mass a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Weights \(w\) are positive integers encoded in binary. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Given \(p\in C\) and a binary rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), bribery may split each type's mass arbitrarily among legal replacement ballots, preserving its weight. Changing mass \(x\) costs \(x\), independently of weight; unchanged mass costs nothing, and total cost must be at most \(\beta\).
Under \(\operatorname{eliminate}(\mathrm{veto})\), restrict ballots to surviving candidates and discard a ballot once none of its listed candidates survives. In each round apply veto with round-up scoring: listed candidates receive their positional veto score and omitted candidates receive zero; mass \(x\) of weight \(w\) multiplies these scores by \(wx\). Eliminate one minimum-score candidate and repeat until one remains. The winner set contains every possible final survivor over all choices among tied minimum-score candidates; unique victory means this set is \(\{p\}\).

Is deciding whether an admissible bribery makes \(p\) the unique winner polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m=3\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).

For finite single-peaked weighted electorates with three candidates, unique-winner bribery is NP-complete with top-truncated ballots and polynomial-time solvable with complete ballots ([Menon–Larson](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/papers/ML.AAAI2016.pdf)). No result settling this exact divisible-mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and restricted the rational mass budget to the interval from zero to one.
  • Specified positive binary-encoded integer voting weights, preserving the discrete weighted-voter domain.
  • Made mass divisible among arbitrary legal replacement ballots, with weights preserved and cost equal to changed voter mass rather than changed voting weight.
  • Made legal ballots nonempty prefixes of strict single-peaked rankings, including complete rankings; two-candidate prefixes are retained even though their scoring behavior here is equivalent to completing the ballot.
  • Specified the winner set through all sequential resolutions of elimination ties, so unique victory requires the preferred candidate to survive every such resolution; the supplied and published definitions do not explicitly resolve elimination ties.
  • Renamed the axis to avoid using the same symbol for the axis and the input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The divisible-mass problem is polynomial-time solvable for \(m=3\). It reduces to three rational linear programs, each with \(11\tau+1\) variables and \(O(\tau)\) constraints. The resulting bit running time is \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\).

The proof explicitly handles every permitted choice among tied minimum-score candidates.

1. Legal ballots and mass transfers

Write the axis as \(a\prec_\ell b\prec_\ell c\). The strict single-peaked complete rankings are precisely

\((a,b,c),\quad(b,a,c),\quad(b,c,a),\quad(c,b,a).\)

Indeed, an endpoint peak forces the remaining order toward the opposite endpoint; a middle peak permits either order of the endpoints. Their distinct nonempty prefixes form the legal ballot set \(\mathcal B\):

  • Three ballots of length one: \((a),(b),(c)\).
  • Four ballots of length two: \((a,b),(b,a),(b,c),(c,b)\).
  • The four complete rankings above.

Thus \(|\mathcal B|=11\).

For each input type \(t=(w_t,b_t)\) and each \(b\in\mathcal B\), introduce a variable \(x_{t,b}\): the mass originating from type \(t\) whose final ballot is \(b\). Exactly the admissible mass transfers satisfy

\[ \]
\(x_{t,b}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}x_{t,b}=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t,\)

together with the budget constraint

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{\substack{b\in\mathcal B\\b\neq b_t}}x_{t,b}\leq\beta.\)

To justify this representation, any admissible bribery determines these variables by recording its destination masses. Conversely, any feasible assignment specifies an admissible split of every type. The mass \(x_{t,b_t}\) can be left unchanged, while each remaining portion is changed exactly once. Hence the displayed budget counts exactly the required cost and preserves every portion’s weight.

2. Scores are linear in the transfer variables

For a surviving set \(U\subseteq C\) with \(|U|\in\{2,3\}\), let \(b|_U\) be the ballot obtained by deleting candidates outside \(U\).

Define \(v_U(b,d)\) to equal \(1\) if \(d\) occurs in \(b|_U\) at a position strictly less than \(|U|\), and \(0\) otherwise. These are exactly the prescribed round-up veto coefficients:

  • With three survivors, the first two listed positions score \(1\), and the third scores \(0\).
  • With two survivors, the first listed position scores \(1\), and the second scores \(0\).
  • Omitted candidates score \(0\).
  • If \(b|_U\) is empty, all coefficients are \(0\), implementing ballot discard.

Consequently, the score of \(d\in U\) is the linear function

\(\displaystyle S_U(d;x)=\sum_t\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}w_t\,v_U(b,d)\,x_{t,b}.\)

Restriction and discard depend only on the ballot and the surviving set, so these formulas apply to every elimination branch.

3. Exact characterization of unique victory

Let the two rivals of \(p\) be \(q\) and \(r\), and abbreviate \(S_C(d;x)\) by \(S(d)\).

Lemma. The winner set is \(\{p\}\) if and only if one of the following three cases holds.

| First-round minimum set | Necessary and sufficient conditions |
|---|---|
| \(\{q\}\) | \(S(p)>S(q)\), \(S(r)>S(q)\), and \(S_{\{p,r\}}(p;x)>S_{\{p,r\}}(r;x)\) |
| \(\{r\}\) | \(S(p)>S(r)\), \(S(q)>S(r)\), and \(S_{\{p,q\}}(p;x)>S_{\{p,q\}}(q;x)\) |
| \(\{q,r\}\) | \(S(q)=S(r)\), \(S(p)>S(q)\), \(S_{\{p,r\}}(p;x)>S_{\{p,r\}}(r;x)\), and \(S_{\{p,q\}}(p;x)>S_{\{p,q\}}(q;x)\) |

Proof. If \(p\) belongs to the first-round minimum set, an allowed tie-breaking choice eliminates \(p\). The eventual survivor is then a rival, contradicting unique victory. Thus the nonempty minimum set must be one of \(\{q\},\{r\},\{q,r\}\). The first-round conditions in the table describe these sets exactly.

Following an allowed first elimination, only \(p\) and one rival remain. Then \(p\) survives every allowed elimination choice exactly when its score strictly exceeds that rival’s score. Equality permits eliminating \(p\), and a smaller score forces its elimination.

For a singleton first-round minimum set there is one resulting pair; for \(\{q,r\}\), both resulting pairs must satisfy the strict comparison. This proves both directions. \(\square\)

4. Testing strict inequalities exactly

Construct one linear program for each row of the table. Include the mass and budget constraints, introduce a variable \(\varepsilon\), and impose

\(0\leq\varepsilon\leq1.\)

Keep the row’s equality, if present. Replace each strict comparison \(A(x)>B(x)\) by

\(A(x)-B(x)\geq\varepsilon,\)

and maximize \(\varepsilon\).

Accept precisely when at least one program is feasible and has optimal value \(\varepsilon^\star>0\).

Correctness. Suppose a bribery satisfies one table row. Its finitely many required score differences are all positive. Let \(\delta\) be their minimum. Taking \(\varepsilon=\min\{1,\delta\}>0\) gives a feasible point of that row’s program.

Conversely, any feasible point with \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfies all the original strict comparisons, as well as the required equality and bribery constraints. The lemma therefore guarantees unique victory.

Finally, each feasible region is compact: conservation and nonnegativity give \(0\leq x_{t,b}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\varepsilon\) lies in \([0,1]\). All constraints are closed. Thus every feasible program attains its optimum; the test does not rely on an unattained supremum or on numerical tolerance.

5. Bit complexity and conclusion

Each program has \(11\tau+1\) variables and at most \(12\tau+7\) constraint rows, counting nonnegativity constraints and counting each equality as one row.

All coefficients are rational. Score coefficients belong to \(\{0,w_t,-w_t\}\); other coefficients are small integers, and the right-hand sides use the input masses and budget. Each weight appears only a constant number of times in the score rows. Therefore the full dense encoding of each program has size \(O(\tau^2+L)\), including its zero entries.

Rational linear programming admits exact polynomial-time optimization in the binary encoding length, including infeasibility detection and exact comparison of the optimal value with zero. This applies also to programs with equality constraints and lower-dimensional feasible regions. See [MIT’s notes on polynomial-time linear programming](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

More explicitly, if \(T_{\mathrm{LP}}(n,k,B)\) denotes a polynomial bit-time bound for rational linear programming with \(n\) variables, \(k\) constraint rows, and encoding length \(B\), our running time is bounded by

\(O(\tau^2+L)+3T_{\mathrm{LP}}\!\left(11\tau+1,\;12\tau+7,\;O(\tau^2+L)\right)=\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L),\qquad m=3.\)

A positive instance also has a rational successful bribery of polynomial encoding length: a bounded rational polytope has an optimal vertex, whose coordinates have polynomial bit length by Cramer’s rule and determinant bounds.

The finite-electorate hardness result in [Menon–Larson](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/papers/ML.AAAI2016.pdf) does not transfer to this model. Here arbitrary splitting makes the transfer constraints linear, and three candidates leave only three first-round cases to check.
