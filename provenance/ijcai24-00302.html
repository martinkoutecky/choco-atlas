The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given disjoint institution sets \(H_1,H_2\), rational masses \(\mu_k\) of complete couple types, optional single-doctor type masses, rational capacities \(q_h\), sub-responsive and sub-complete acceptable pairs, and strict priorities over type classes, find a mass matching \(y_{k,h_1,h_2}\) and \(x_{s,h}\) satisfying capacities and feasibility, with no positive-mass blocking deviation under the paper's HRC predicate generalized to priority classes.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

A market has singleton types σ of masses ν_σ, couple types κ with labelled members κ_1,κ_2 and masses μ_κ, and hospitals \(h\) of rational capacities \(q_h\), where \(\sum_\sigma ν_σ+2\sum_\kappa\mu_\kappa=1\). Types have strict preferences over acceptable hospitals or ordered hospital pairs, and each hospital strictly prioritizes singleton and labelled couple-member types.

A mass allocation assigns \(x_{\sigma,h}\) and \(x_{\kappa,a,b}\) mass to acceptable outcomes, allowing unmatched residual mass, and satisfies \(\sum_\sigma x_{\sigma,h}+\sum_{\kappa,a,b}x_{\kappa,a,b}(\mathbf 1[a=h]+\mathbf 1[b=h])\le q_h\) for every \(h\). It is stable if no positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of a type can move from a strictly worse current outcome to a preferred acceptable outcome while every newly required hospital place is supplied by slack capacity or by displacing \(\varepsilon\) mass of strictly lower-priority occupants; when both members seek the same hospital, the required places must be distinct. A member already assigned to its proposed hospital keeps that place.

Target

Is deciding whether a stable mass allocation exists, and constructing one when it does, solvable in polynomial time or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Nearest known results

For indivisible doctors, stable \(\mathrm{HRC}\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with unit capacities and sub-responsive, sub-complete couple preferences.

Finding a stable fractional solution for a different, contract-order fractional formulation of \(\mathrm{HRC}\) is \(\mathrm{PPAD}\)-complete.

Result

Under the natural reading that “slack capacity” means slack in the allocation before the deviation, every well-formed instance admits a stable mass allocation.

Consequently:

  • deciding existence is polynomial-time solvable—in fact, every valid instance is a yes-instance;
  • a rational stable allocation of polynomial encoding length always exists;
  • constructing one reduces in polynomial time to the computational Scarf problem and therefore belongs to \( \mathrm{PPAD} \);
  • the present argument does not establish a polynomial-time construction or \( \mathrm{PPAD} \)-hardness for this exact, weaker stability notion.

Thus the proposed “polynomial-time versus \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hard” dichotomy is not the correct one: existence is trivial, while construction is a total-search problem.

Interpretation of a deviation

I use the following interpretation of the definition.

A place is supplied by:

  • slack already present at the target hospital;
  • displacement of strictly lower-priority incumbent mass; or
  • retention by the same labelled member when that member is already assigned to the proposed hospital.

In particular, a place vacated by the other member of the couple is not automatically a retained place. This is the usual member-specific interpretation of the last sentence in the definition.

A stronger contract order

For every acceptable outcome \(e\), construct a column with one row for the relevant singleton or couple type and one row for every hospital used by \(e\).

For a singleton outcome \(e=(\sigma,h)\), put

\[ r_e(\sigma)=1,\qquad r_e(h)=1. \]

For a couple outcome \(e=(\kappa,a,b)\), put

\[ r_e(\kappa)=1, \]

and put \(r_e(a)=r_e(b)=1\) when \(a\neq b\), while \(r_e(h)=2\) when \(a=b=h\).

The capacity of a type row is its mass, and the capacity of hospital row \(h\) is \(q_h\). Thus a nonnegative column vector \(x\) satisfies \(Ax\leq b\) exactly when it is a feasible mass allocation.

Order the columns in each type row according to that type’s strict preferences.

For a column \(e\) incident with hospital \(h\), let \(w_h(e)\) be the lowest-priority labelled member assigned to \(h\) by \(e\). For a singleton or a couple outcome using \(h\) once, this is its unique member at \(h\); for \((h,h)\), it is the worse of the two members according to \(h\).

Order hospital columns primarily by \(w_h(e)\). If two columns have the same worst member, they necessarily belong to the same underlying singleton or couple type; break the tie according to that type’s preference over the corresponding outcomes. Denote this strict order by \(\succ_h^*\).

Application of Scarf’s lemma

The required form of Scarf’s lemma is the following.

Given a nonnegative matrix \(A\), a nonnegative capacity vector \(b\), and a strict ordering of the positive columns in every row, there exists an extreme point \(x\) of

\[ \{x\geq 0:Ax\leq b\} \]

such that every column \(e\) is dominated in some incident row \(v\): row \(v\) is saturated and every positive column incident with \(v\) is weakly preferred to \(e\) in that row.

This is the standard fractional stable-allocation consequence of Scarf’s lemma; see [Biró and Fleiner](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • \(A\) has entries only in \(\{0,1,2\}\);
  • every column contains a type-row entry equal to \(1\);
  • the feasible polytope is bounded because every column consumes mass from its type row;
  • all row orders constructed above are strict.

Let \(x\) be the resulting dominating extreme point.

The Scarf allocation is stable in the stated sense

Suppose, for contradiction, that positive mass from a current outcome \(f\), or from the unmatched residual, can deviate to a strictly preferred outcome \(e\).

The type row does not dominate \(e\)

If \(f\) is an assigned outcome, then \(x_f>0\) and \(f\) is strictly worse than \(e\). Hence \(f\) is excluded from the type-row prefix consisting of \(e\) and outcomes preferred to \(e\). Therefore that prefix has mass strictly below the type capacity.

If the deviating mass is unmatched, then the total assigned mass of the type is already strictly below its capacity. Again, the type row cannot dominate \(e\).

No target hospital dominates \(e\)

Fix a hospital \(h\) used by \(e\).

If \(h\) has positive slack, then the total load at \(h\) is below \(q_h\), so \(h\) is not saturated and cannot dominate \(e\).

Suppose now that \(h\) is full.

First consider the case in which the worst member \(w_h(e)\) is a newly required member. Because the proposed deviation is feasible, that member must displace positive mass of some strictly lower-priority occupant \(\lambda\). Let \(g\) be the outcome containing that occupant. Since

\[ w_h(g)\preceq_h \lambda \prec_h w_h(e), \]

we have \(e\succ_h^* g\). Because \(x_g>0\), positive hospital load lies strictly below \(e\) in \(\succ_h^*\). Thus the prefix of columns weakly preferred to \(e\) has load strictly below \(q_h\), so \(h\) does not dominate \(e\).

It remains to consider the case in which \(w_h(e)\) is retained from \(f\). Then \(f\) and \(e\) involve the same labelled member at \(h\). Either \(w_h(f)\) is lower-priority than \(w_h(e)\), or they are equal and the tie is broken in favor of \(e\) because the couple strictly prefers \(e\) to \(f\). Hence \(e\succ_h^* f\). Since \(x_f>0\), column \(f\) supplies positive load below \(e\), and once again \(h\) does not dominate \(e\).

This argument also covers \(e=(h,h)\). If its worst member is new, the distinct-place condition supplies a strictly lower-priority displaced place for that member; if its worst member is retained, the source column \(f\) is strictly below \(e\).

Thus neither the type row nor any target hospital dominates \(e\), contradicting Scarf domination. Therefore \(x\) is stable under the stated blocking rule.

Complexity consequences

Existence decision

Every valid instance has a stable allocation. Hence existence can be decided by validating the input and returning yes. The decision problem is in \( \mathrm{P} \), and the indivisible \( \mathrm{HRC} \) \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hardness does not transfer.

Polynomial-size rational witnesses

The Scarf allocation may be chosen as an extreme point of a rational polytope whose matrix entries lie in \(\{0,1,2\}\). An extreme point is determined by a nonsingular subsystem of tight constraints. Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound therefore give a rational allocation whose numerator and denominator lengths are polynomial in the input length.

A proposed rational allocation can also be verified in polynomial time: enumerate every positive source outcome and every preferred target outcome, and test the at most two required hospital places for slack, retention, or positive lower-priority incumbent mass.

Construction

The construction above is a polynomial-time reduction to the computational Scarf problem. Since Scarf is in \( \mathrm{PPAD} \), the exact stable-mass-allocation search problem is also in \( \mathrm{PPAD} \). Computational Scarf is \( \mathrm{PPAD} \)-complete in general; see [Kintali et al.](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/120874655).

This proves only an upper bound. A reduction to a stronger search problem does not prove that the weaker mass-stability problem is \( \mathrm{PPAD} \)-hard.

Why the known fractional hardness does not transfer directly

The distinction between member-type priorities and contract priorities is genuine.

Consider one couple type \(\kappa\) of mass \(1/2\), with outcomes

\[ e=(A,B)\succ_\kappa f=(A,C)\succ_\kappa g=(D,B). \]

Give every hospital capacity \(1/4\), and allocate

\[ x_f=x_g=1/4,\qquad x_e=0. \]

Every hospital is full. Hospital \(A\) contains \(\kappa_1\)-mass from \(f\), hospital \(B\) contains \(\kappa_2\)-mass from \(g\), and similarly \(C,D\) contain the other members.

This allocation is stable under the stated rule:

  • mass at \(f\) cannot move to \(e\), because its second member would need to displace equal-priority \(\kappa_2\)-mass at \(B\);
  • mass at \(g\) cannot move to \(e\), because its first member would need to displace equal-priority \(\kappa_1\)-mass at \(A\);
  • mass at \(g\) cannot move to \(f\), for the same reason at \(A\) and \(C\).

By contrast, a contract-order formulation that breaks equal-member ties according to the couple’s preference ranks \(e\) above both \(f\) at \(A\) and \(g\) at \(B\). It therefore regards \(e\) as fractionally blocking.

Hence the known \( \mathrm{PPAD} \)-completeness result for contract-order fractional \( \mathrm{HRC} \), proved by [Csáji](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcs.2022.07.025), cannot be applied verbatim to this model.

Conclusion

The existence question is settled: every instance admits a stable mass allocation, so existence is polynomial-time decidable and is not governed by the discrete \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hardness result.

For construction, the rigorous classification obtained here is

\[ \text{stable-mass-allocation search}\in\mathrm{PPAD}. \]

Whether this exact weaker search problem is polynomial-time solvable or \( \mathrm{PPAD} \)-hard remains open.

Step-by-step audit

1. Encoding feasible allocations

The proposed column matrix correctly represents the stated capacity constraints:

  • A singleton column consumes one unit per unit mass in its type row and hospital row.
  • A couple assigned to distinct hospitals consumes one unit in each hospital row.
  • A couple assigned twice to the same hospital consumes two units in that hospital row.
  • Every column consumes one unit in its type row, so type-row capacities enforce the available type masses.

Allowing unused type mass and unused hospital capacity through inequalities correctly represents unmatched residual mass and slack capacity.

No substantive error appears here.

2. Construction of hospital orders

Ordering an outcome at hospital \(h\) by its lowest-priority member \(w_h(e)\), followed by the couple’s preference when the worst member is the same, gives a strict order.

The claim that equal worst members imply the same underlying type is justified by the assumption that hospital priorities are strict over singleton and labelled couple-member types. A labelled member identifies its couple type.

This order is deliberately stronger than the original member-priority relation, but the subsequent proof only needs domination under this stronger order to imply stability under the weaker blocking rule. That logical direction is appropriate.

3. Form of Scarf’s lemma

The output states an inequality-form corollary:

“Given a nonnegative matrix \(A\), a nonnegative capacity vector \(b\), and a strict ordering of the positive columns in every row, there exists an extreme point…”

The cited Biró–Fleiner paper does prove solvability of stable-allocation problems with arbitrary positive contribution coefficients, but its displayed Scarf lemma uses an extended equality system containing identity and auxiliary columns. Its stable-allocation theorem likewise constructs extended membership, edge-capacity, and preference matrices. See [Biró and Fleiner, Theorems 5, 12, and 13](https://real.mtak.hu/26264/7/BF16do_last.pdf).

The output does not explicitly derive its inequality-form, extreme-point version from that theorem. In particular, it omits:

  • the slack/identity columns converting \(Ax\leq b\) into an equality system;
  • the preference-matrix values assigned to auxiliary and nonincident columns;
  • the argument that an original outcome cannot be dominated in a nonincident row;
  • the argument that the projected Scarf solution is an extreme point of the original packing polytope.

These appear to be standard, local additions rather than evidence that the claimed corollary is false. Nevertheless, under the requested verification standard, the cited theorem has not been applied with all hypotheses and transformations made explicit.

Minimum clarification needed: state the extended matrices and prove that Scarf domination projects exactly to the displayed row-domination property, including the extreme-point assertion.

4. Failure of type-row domination

For assigned source outcome \(f\), the reasoning is correct:

  • \(x_f>0\);
  • \(e\) is strictly preferred to \(f\);
  • therefore \(f\) is outside the set of outcomes weakly preferred to \(e\);
  • hence that preferred-prefix load is strictly below the type capacity, whether or not the type row itself is saturated.

For deviation from unmatched residual, assigned type mass is strictly below the type capacity, so the row is unsaturated.

This correctly excludes type-row domination.

5. Hospital with pre-existing slack

If hospital \(h\) has positive slack before the deviation, it is not saturated and hence cannot dominate \(e\). This is correct under the output’s interpretation of slack.

6. Full hospital and newly required worst member

Suppose \(h\) is full and \(w_h(e)\) is newly required. Under the assumed blocking rule, feasibility requires displacement of positive mass of an occupant strictly below \(w_h(e)\).

If that occupant occurs in outcome \(g\), then \(w_h(g)\) is no higher than that occupant. Consequently \(g\) is strictly below \(e\) in the constructed hospital order. Since \(x_g>0\), not all positive hospital load is in columns weakly preferred to \(e\), excluding hospital domination.

This reasoning also remains valid when \(g\) uses \(h\) twice, since the column then contributes \(2x_g>0\) below \(e\).

7. Full hospital and retained worst member

If \(w_h(e)\) is retained from source outcome \(f\), then the same labelled member occurs at \(h\) in both outcomes. The worst member of \(f\) at \(h\) is either that member or a still lower-priority member. Thus \(w_h(f)\) is no higher than \(w_h(e)\).

When they are equal, the tie-break follows the couple’s strict preference \(e\succ_\kappa f\). Therefore \(e\succ_h^* f\), and the positive \(f\)-load excludes domination.

This is correct. The same reasoning covers the case where \(f\) uses \(h\) twice and \(e\) uses it once, as well as the reverse configuration.

8. Same-hospital target

For \(e=(h,h)\):

  • if the worse member is new, the distinct-place requirement forces a separate displaced place for that member;
  • if the worse member is retained, the positive source column lies below \(e\) by the preceding argument.

This treatment is sufficient under the assumed pre-deviation-slack convention.

9. Interpretation of slack

This is the main scope problem. The proof begins with:

“Under the natural reading that ‘slack capacity’ means slack in the allocation before the deviation…”

The task does not explicitly state that timing convention. In a simultaneous move, a position vacated by the other member could plausibly become slack available to a newly arriving member. The task’s separate statement that a member already at the proposed hospital retains that place does not conclusively decide whether another member’s newly vacated place may be reused.

The proof does not cover the departure-created-slack convention and expressly leaves it open. Therefore its unconditional conclusion that “every instance admits a stable mass allocation” is not established for every reasonable reading of the given definition.

Minimum clarification needed: the problem statement must specify whether departure-created capacity counts as slack, or the prover must analyze both interpretations.
