The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite school set \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(g_t,\succ_t,(\pi_{it})_{i\in C})\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_i\) with \(\sum_i\kappa_i\ge 1\), and a parameter \(\alpha\) with \(\alpha\sum_i\kappa_i\ge\sum_{t:g_t=D}\mu_t\), compute the mass assignment \(x_{ti}\) from type-aggregated student-proposing deferred acceptance: first assign \(D\)-mass against reserved capacities \(\alpha\kappa_i\), then assign \(F\)-mass against residual capacities. Determine whether the resulting assignment is fully allocated, group-stable, and \(\alpha\)-fair, allowing fractional allocation at priority cutoffs.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) types \(t=(g_t,\succ_t,(\pi_{it})_{i\in C})\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(g_t\in\{D,F\}\), \(\succ_t\) strictly ranks all schools above being unmatched, and \(\pi_{it}\) is an integer priority rank, with smaller ranks preferred and ties allowed; every type is acceptable at every school.
Capacities \(\kappa_i\geq0\) and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) are binary rationals satisfying \(\sum_i\kappa_i\geq1\) and \(\alpha\sum_i\kappa_i\geq\sum_{t:g_t=D}\mu_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For a group and capacity vector, define synchronous mass deferred acceptance as follows: initially all mass proposes to its first choice; each school retains cumulative proposed mass in priority order up to capacity, retaining the same proportion of every type's cumulative proposed mass at a tied cutoff. Newly rejected portions propose to their next school in the next round; mass rejected by every school remains unmatched. The outcome is the limit of the held-mass matrices, if it exists.
Run this procedure first on \(D\) with capacities \(\alpha\kappa_i\), then on \(F\) with capacities \(\kappa_i-d_i\), where \(d_i\) is the first-stage mass held at school \(i\). Denote the combined outcome by \(x\).
A feasible assignment satisfies \(x_{ti}\geq0\), \(\sum_i x_{ti}\leq\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\leq\kappa_i\); it is fully allocated when every row sum equals \(\mu_t\). Write \(a_{gi}=\sum_{t:g_t=g}x_{ti}\), \(q_{Di}=a_{Di}\), \(q_{Fi}=\kappa_i-a_{Di}\), and \(e_{ti}=\mu_t-\sum_{j:\,j=i\text{ or }j\succ_t i}x_{tj}\).
Group stability means that whenever \(e_{ti}>0\), school \(i\) fills its ex-post group quota, \(a_{g_t i}=q_{g_t i}\), and every type \(u\) with \(g_u=g_t\) and \(x_{ui}>0\) satisfies \(\pi_{iu}\leq\pi_{it}\). Thus all unused capacity is attributed to \(F\).
The assignment is \(\alpha\)-fair when, for every school \(i\), \(a_{Di}>\alpha\kappa_i\) implies \(\sum_{t:g_t=F}e_{ti}=0\), and \(a_{Fi}>(1-\alpha)\kappa_i\) implies \(\sum_{t:g_t=D}e_{ti}=0\).

Does the two-stage mass procedure always have a well-defined, fully allocated, group-stable, \(\alpha\)-fair outcome? Is computing its exact outcome and deciding these properties polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite students with strict preferences and priorities, the analogous two-stage procedure guarantees full allocation, group stability, fairness with upward-rounded disadvantaged reserves, and strategy-proofness. [Alcalde–Subiza](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/29145/1/MPRA_paper_29145.pdf)
For a different finite one-to-one fractional matching model with indifferences, polynomial-time computation of a doubly-strong ex ante stable matching is known; this does not directly settle the rational-mass two-stage procedure defined here. [Cookson–Shah](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~nisarg/papers/dfda.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified strict complete student rankings, universal school acceptability, and unmatched as worse than every school, following the discrete model.
  • Specified nonnegative binary-rational capacities and binary-rational alpha in the closed unit interval.
  • Interpreted school priorities as integer ranks with possible ties; resolved cutoff ties by proportional retention of cumulative proposed mass, since the mass question supplies no individual tie-breaking.
  • Specified synchronous proposals, forwarding of newly rejected portions, and a limiting outcome rather than assuming finite termination.
  • Defined fractional envy through positive mass assigned below the school or left unmatched; equal school priority does not create a priority violation.
  • Carried over ex-post group quotas: disadvantaged quota equals its assigned mass, while favored quota includes all unused capacity.
  • Replaced the discrete ceiling and floor fairness thresholds by their unrounded mass counterparts.

1. Proven conclusions and remaining question

The two-stage procedure always has a well-defined, fully allocated, group-stable, $\alpha$-fair outcome. Thus, on the promised input domain, deciding these four properties is polynomial-time: every instance has answer “yes.”

The general computation question requires an exact-output convention. I prove the following additional results:

  • Every outcome coordinate is algebraic and exactly computable.
  • Comparing an outcome coordinate with a rational threshold belongs to $\mathrm{EXPTIME}$.
  • Such comparison is at least as hard as comparing a sum of square roots of positive integers with an integer.
  • If each coordinate must be output as a dense univariate rational polynomial and an isolating interval, polynomial-time exact computation is impossible because the output can have superpolynomial length.
  • For $m\leq2$, exact computation is polynomial-time and produces rational coordinates.

These results do not establish NP-hardness or a polynomial-time algorithm for general threshold comparison or for exact computation under succinct output representations. The overall resolution is therefore partial.

2. Single-stage convergence

Consider one group with types $T$, masses $\mu_t$, total mass
$$ M=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t, $$
and nonnegative real capacities $c_i$. Allowing real capacities is necessary because second-stage capacities need not be rational.

For cumulative proposals $p=(p_{ti})$, define
$$ H_{ti}(p)=\sum_{u:\,\pi_{iu}<\pi_{it}}p_{ui}, \qquad S_{ti}(p)=\sum_{u:\,\pi_{iu}=\pi_{it}}p_{ui}. $$
The school retains
$$ h_{ti}(p)= \begin{cases} p_{ti}\min\!\left\{1,\dfrac{(c_i-H_{ti}(p))_+}{S_{ti}(p)}\right\}, & S_{ti}(p)>0,\\[6pt] 0,&S_{ti}(p)=0. \end{cases} $$
Write
$$ R_{ti}(p)=p_{ti}-h_{ti}(p) $$
for cumulative rejection.

Lemma 1: rejection is continuous and coordinatewise nondecreasing

Continuity follows directly from the formula when $S_{ti}>0$. When $S_{ti}$ tends to zero, the bound
$$ 0\leq h_{ti}(p)\leq p_{ti}\leq S_{ti}(p) $$
shows continuity there as well.

Increasing proposals from higher-priority types reduces available capacity and therefore weakly increases $R_{ti}$. Increasing proposals from other tied types weakly decreases the retained proportion, again weakly increasing rejection. Lower-priority proposals have no effect.

For the type’s own proposal, hold other coordinates fixed and put
$$ s=p_{ti},\qquad B=S_{ti}-s,\qquad A=(c_i-H_{ti})_+. $$
If $s+B\leq A$, rejection is zero. If $s+B>A$, then
$$ R(s)=s-\frac{As}{s+B}, \qquad R'(s)=1-\frac{AB}{(s+B)^2}\geq0. $$
The inequality follows from $A<s+B$ and $B\leq s+B$. The two formulas agree at the boundary. This proves monotonicity.

Lemma 2: the procedure converges to the least fixed point

Write type $t$’s preference list as
$$ i_t(1),\ldots,i_t(m), $$
and use $p_{t,k}$ and $R_{t,k}$ for the corresponding coordinates.

Initially,
$$ p^0_{t,1}=\mu_t,\qquad p^0_{t,k}=0\quad(k>1). $$
Cumulative rejection cannot decrease by Lemma 1. Consequently, the synchronous update is
$$ p^{n+1}_{t,1}=\mu_t, \qquad p^{n+1}_{t,k+1}=R_{t,k}(p^n). $$
Denote this update map by $F$.

The map $F$ is continuous and coordinatewise nondecreasing. Also $p^1\geq p^0$, so induction gives
$$ p^{n+1}\geq p^n. $$
Every coordinate is bounded above by $\mu_t$, since
$$ 0\leq R_{ti}(p)\leq p_{ti}. $$
Thus the finite-dimensional vector sequence converges coordinatewise to some $p^*$. Continuity gives
$$ p^*=F(p^*), \qquad h(p^n)\longrightarrow h(p^*). $$
This proves existence of the outcome.

Moreover, $p^*$ is the least fixed point in the box
$$ p_{t,1}=\mu_t,\qquad 0\leq p_{t,k}\leq\mu_t. $$
Indeed, if $q$ is any fixed point in that box, then $p^0\leq q$. Monotonicity gives $p^n\leq q$ for every $n$, hence $p^*\leq q$.

3. Single-stage allocation and stability

Let
$$ r_{t,k}=R_{t,k}(p^*), \qquad h_{t,k}=h_{t,i_t(k)}(p^*). $$
The fixed-point equations imply
$$ p^*_{t,1}=\mu_t,\qquad p^*_{t,k+1}=r_{t,k},\qquad h_{t,k}=p^*_{t,k}-r_{t,k}. $$
Therefore, telescoping gives
$$ \sum_{\ell=1}^{k}h_{t,\ell}=\mu_t-r_{t,k}. $$
In particular, row feasibility holds, and residual demand at school $i_t(k)$ is exactly $r_{t,k}$. School feasibility follows from the retention rule.

If $r_{t,k}>0$, priority-order retention fills school $i_t(k)$ to capacity. Furthermore, no strictly lower-priority type can be retained there: positive rejection means that the cutoff is at type $t$’s priority or above. Thus
$$ r_{t,k}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_u h_{u,i_t(k)}=c_{i_t(k)} $$
and every retained type $u$ satisfies
$$ \pi_{i_t(k),u}\leq\pi_{i_t(k),t}. $$
This includes zero-capacity schools.

Now assume
$$ \sum_i c_i\geq M. $$
If some type has positive unmatched mass $w_t$, then its residual demand at every school is at least $w_t$. The preceding stability implication makes every school full. Hence
$$ \sum_{t,i}h_{ti}=\sum_i c_i\geq M. $$
But row feasibility and $w_t>0$ give
$$ \sum_{t,i}h_{ti}<M, $$
a contradiction. The single-stage outcome is therefore fully allocated whenever total capacity is sufficient.

4. The two-stage guarantees

Put
$$ M_D=\sum_{t:g_t=D}\mu_t,\qquad M_F=1-M_D. $$

The first stage has capacities $\alpha\kappa_i$. By hypothesis,
$$ \sum_i\alpha\kappa_i\geq M_D. $$
Sections 2–3 therefore give a well-defined, fully allocated first-stage outcome, with school totals satisfying
$$ 0\leq d_i\leq\alpha\kappa_i, \qquad \sum_i d_i=M_D. $$

The second-stage capacities are nonnegative and satisfy
$$ \sum_i(\kappa_i-d_i) =\sum_i\kappa_i-M_D \geq 1-M_D=M_F. $$
Applying the same results proves that the combined assignment is well-defined, feasible, and fully allocated.

For group $F$, the single-stage capacities are exactly
$$ q_{Fi}=\kappa_i-d_i. $$
Thus single-stage stability gives both required group-stability conclusions.

For group $D$, the priority conclusion follows from first-stage stability. The filling equality is automatic from the definition
$$ a_{Di}=q_{Di}=d_i. $$
The combined assignment is group-stable.

For fairness, the first antecedent never occurs, because
$$ a_{Di}=d_i\leq\alpha\kappa_i. $$
If instead
$$ a_{Fi}>(1-\alpha)\kappa_i, $$
feasibility implies
$$ (1-\alpha)\kappa_i<a_{Fi}\leq\kappa_i-d_i, $$
so $d_i<\alpha\kappa_i$. School $i$ is underfilled in the first stage. First-stage stability then forces every disadvantaged type’s residual demand there to be zero:
$$ \sum_{t:g_t=D}e_{ti}=0. $$
This proves $\alpha$-fairness.

The argument also covers empty groups, zero capacities, and $\alpha\in\{0,1\}$.

5. Algebraic computability and an exponential-time comparison bound

Polynomial description of retention

For each school and type, abbreviate the higher-priority and tied totals by $H$ and $S$. Retention is characterized by the following disjunction:
$$ \begin{aligned} & H\geq c_i,\quad h_{ti}=0;\\ \text{or}\quad& H<c_i,\quad H+S\leq c_i,\quad h_{ti}=p_{ti};\\ \text{or}\quad& H<c_i<H+S,\quad S h_{ti}=(c_i-H)p_{ti}. \end{aligned} $$
The cases cover every possibility and uniquely determine $h_{ti}$. In the last case, $S>0$ follows from its strict inequalities.

Let $\operatorname{Fix}_c(p,h)$ assert these retention conditions together with
$$ 0\leq p_{t,k}\leq\mu_t,\qquad p_{t,1}=\mu_t,\qquad p_{t,k+1}=p_{t,k}-h_{t,k}. $$
All constraints are polynomial, of degree at most two.

By Lemma 2, the procedure’s proposal vector is selected precisely by
$$ \operatorname{Fix}_c(p,h) \;\land\; \forall(q,z)\, \bigl(\operatorname{Fix}_c(q,z)\Longrightarrow p\leq q\bigr). $$

For two stages, introduce proposal and held variables for both groups. The second-stage capacities are the linear expressions
$$ c_i^F=\kappa_i-\sum_{t:g_t=D}h^D_{ti}. $$
For a selected outcome coordinate $y=x_{ti}$, its defining formula has the form
$$ \exists(p^D,h^D,p^F,h^F)\; \forall(q^D,z^D,q^F,z^F)\;\Psi(y), $$
where $\Psi$ asserts:

  • Both selected pairs satisfy their fixed-point conditions.
  • Every first-stage fixed point lies above $p^D$.
  • Every fixed point for capacities $c^F(h^D)$ lies above $p^F$.
  • $y$ equals the selected held coordinate.

The same selected first-stage holdings appear in both second-stage fixed-point conditions. Thus the formula characterizes exactly the sequential procedure, rather than allowing comparison with different first-stage outcomes.

After clearing rational denominators, this formula has polynomial size, polynomial coefficient bit length, degree at most two, and $O(m\tau)$ quantified variables in at most two blocks.

Algebraicity

Effective real quantifier elimination turns the formula into rational polynomial sign conditions defining the singleton $\{x_{ti}\}$.

Such a singleton consists of an algebraic number. Indeed, if its point were transcendental, every nonzero rational polynomial occurring in the finite description would be nonzero there. All their signs would remain unchanged in a sufficiently small neighborhood. The formula would then hold throughout that neighborhood, contradicting uniqueness.

Effective elimination followed by univariate root isolation therefore computes each coordinate exactly as a rational polynomial with an isolating rational interval.

Threshold comparison belongs to $\mathrm{EXPTIME}$

Use the fixed-block quantitative quantifier-elimination theorem: with a fixed number of quantifier blocks, its arithmetic-operation and intermediate coefficient-size bounds are singly exponential in a polynomial of the input parameters. These bounds are stated in [Basu’s survey, Theorem 2.18](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf).

Here there are at most two blocks, polynomially many variables and constraints, fixed degree two, and polynomial coefficient bit length. Appending $x_{ti}\geq r$, for a rational threshold $r$, gives a sentence decidable in
$$ 2^{\operatorname{poly}(L+\operatorname{bits}(r))} $$
bit operations. Thus rational-threshold comparison belongs to $\mathrm{EXPTIME}$.

6. A square-root-sum reduction

This construction gives a new lower bound for a precise decision formulation.

Define $\mathrm{OutcomeCompare}$ to ask whether
$$ x_{ti}\geq r $$
for a specified coordinate and binary-rational threshold.

I prove that the problem
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{N_j}\leq K, $$
where the $N_j$ are positive binary integers and $K$ is a binary integer, reduces to $\mathrm{OutcomeCompare}$ in polynomial time.

Construction

For each $j$, let $B_j$ be the smallest power of two satisfying
$$ B_j^2>N_j. $$
Then
$$ q_j=\frac{N_j}{B_j^2}\in\left[\frac14,1\right). $$
Choose a positive dyadic $s$ such that
$$ s\sum_j B_j\leq\frac1{32}. $$
For example, choose the smallest integer $h$ with
$$ 2^h\geq32\sum_jB_j $$
and set $s=2^{-h}$.

Define
$$ A_j=sB_j,\qquad Z_j=\frac{A_j}{2}, \qquad c_j=\frac54 A_j(1-q_j). $$
There are schools $U_j,V_j$ for each gadget, and two common schools $R,S$. Set
$$ \alpha=\frac12,\qquad \kappa_{U_j}=\kappa_{V_j}=2c_j,\qquad \kappa_R=\frac12,\qquad \kappa_S=1. $$

For gadget $j$, introduce three disadvantaged types:

| Type | Mass | Preference prefix |
|---|---:|---|
| $a_j$ | $A_j$ | $U_j\succ V_j\succ S$ |
| $b_j$ | $A_j$ | $V_j\succ U_j\succ S$ |
| $z_j$ | $Z_j$ | $U_j\succ R\succ S$ |

Complete every prefix by appending all remaining schools in a fixed order. Give every type priority rank $1$ at every school.

The disadvantaged mass is
$$ G=\sum_j(2A_j+Z_j) =\frac52s\sum_jB_j \leq\frac5{64}. $$
Introduce one favored type $f$ of mass $1-G$, with preference prefix
$$ R\succ S, $$
again followed by every remaining school.

All masses are positive, sum to one, and are dyadic. All capacities are dyadic. Moreover,
$$ \sum_i\kappa_i\geq\frac32\geq1, \qquad \alpha\sum_i\kappa_i\geq\frac34>G. $$
Thus the instance satisfies every promise in the question.

Independence of the gadgets

In the disadvantaged stage, capacities at $R,S$ are respectively $\frac14,\frac12$. Cumulative proposals at either school are bounded by the entire disadvantaged mass $G\leq\frac5{64}$. Both schools therefore retain every proposal in every round.

Consequently, each gadget’s types stop at its displayed destinations: no mass reaches another gadget’s schools. The gadgets evolve independently.

Also,
$$ 0<c_j\leq\frac{15}{16}A_j<A_j. $$
Hence both $U_j$ and $V_j$ are oversubscribed initially and remain so, because cumulative proposals never decrease.

Solving a gadget

Fix $j$ and abbreviate $A=A_j$, $Z=A/2$, $c=c_j$, and $q=q_j$.

Let $u$ and $v$ be the limiting retained proportions at $U_j$ and $V_j$. Since all priorities are tied, these proportions apply to every proposal at the corresponding school.

Type $a_j$ sends rejection $A(1-u)$ from $U_j$ to $V_j$. Type $b_j$ sends rejection $A(1-v)$ from $V_j$ to $U_j$. Both schools fill capacity, so
$$ c=u\bigl(A+Z+A(1-v)\bigr) =A u\left(\frac52-v\right), $$
and
$$ c=v\bigl(A+A(1-u)\bigr) =A v(2-u). $$
Equating the expressions and cancelling the common term $uv$ gives
$$ \frac52u=2v, \qquad v=\frac54u. $$
Therefore,
$$ \frac cA=\frac54u(2-u). $$
By the definition of $c$,
$$ u(2-u)=1-q, \qquad (u-1)^2=q. $$
Since $0\leq u\leq1$ and $q>0$, the only possible root is
$$ u=1-\sqrt q. $$
The rejected mass of probe type $z_j$ is consequently
$$ Z(1-u) =\frac{sB_j}{2}\sqrt{\frac{N_j}{B_j^2}} =\frac s2\sqrt{N_j}. $$
It is retained completely at $R$. Thus
$$ d_R=\frac s2\sum_j\sqrt{N_j}. $$

Reading the sum in the favored stage

The single favored type has mass
$$ 1-G>\frac12. $$
Its first-choice capacity is
$$ \kappa_R-d_R=\frac12-d_R\leq\frac12. $$
It therefore fills that capacity:
$$ \boxed{x_{fR}=\frac12-\frac s2\sum_j\sqrt{N_j}.} $$
Its remaining mass is
$$ \frac12-G+d_R\leq\frac12, $$
because $d_R\leq G$. School $S$ has second-stage capacity
$$ 1-d_S\geq1-G>\frac12, $$
so it retains all that remaining mass.

Set the comparison threshold to
$$ r=\frac12-\frac s2K. $$
Then
$$ x_{fR}\geq r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_j\sqrt{N_j}\leq K. $$

Size and meaning of the reduction

The construction has
$$ m=2n+2,\qquad \tau=3n+1. $$
The bit lengths of $B_j$, $h$, masses, capacities, and $r$ are polynomial in the source input length. Writing the complete preference lists also takes polynomial time. This is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

The construction uses only dyadic society inputs, both groups, $\alpha=\frac12$, and completely tied priorities.

This establishes square-root-sum hardness, not NP-hardness. Polynomial-time square-root-sum comparison is itself a longstanding open question; see [Eisenbrand–Haeberle–Singer, 2024](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54).

7. Irrationality and a dense-output obstruction

A concrete irrational instance

Take the preceding construction with
$$ n=1,\qquad N_1=2,\qquad B_1=2,\qquad s=\frac1{64}. $$
Its school capacities are
$$ (\kappa_U,\kappa_V,\kappa_R,\kappa_S) = \left(\frac5{128},\frac5{128},\frac12,1\right). $$
The disadvantaged masses are
$$ \mu_a=\mu_b=\frac1{32},\qquad \mu_z=\frac1{64}, $$
and the favored mass is $\frac{59}{64}$. The preference prefixes are those in Section 6.

The proven formula gives
$$ x_{fR}=\frac12-\frac{\sqrt2}{128}. $$
Thus an outcome coordinate is irrational despite entirely dyadic inputs.

Every finite round uses rational arithmetic on this instance, including the second-stage capacities if the first stage were to terminate. Since
$$ d_R=\frac{\sqrt2}{128} $$
is irrational, the first stage cannot attain its limit in finitely many rounds. Exact binary-rational output is also impossible.

A coordinate of degree $2^n$

Apply Section 6 with $N_j=p_j$, where $p_1,\ldots,p_n$ are distinct primes. Then
$$ x_{fR}=\frac12-\frac s2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{p_j}. $$
I now prove that this coordinate has degree $2^n$ over $\mathbb Q$.

Let
$$ K_r=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_r}). $$
Inductively, $K_r$ has basis
$$ e_J=\sqrt{\prod_{j\in J}p_j}, \qquad J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}, $$
and admits all independent sign changes of the square roots.

For the induction step, suppose $\sqrt{p_r}\in K_{r-1}$, and call this element $v$. Since $v^2=p_r$, every sign-change automorphism $\sigma$ of $K_{r-1}$ satisfies
$$ \sigma(v)=\pm v. $$
Expand $v$ in the inductive basis. The basis vectors have distinct sign-change characters: two different subsets are distinguished by changing the sign of a square root in their symmetric difference. Thus an element that is an eigenvector for every sign change can have only one nonzero basis coefficient. Hence
$$ v=a e_J $$
for some $a\in\mathbb Q$ and $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r-1\}$.

Squaring gives
$$ p_r=a^2\prod_{j\in J}p_j. $$
The exponent of $p_r$ on the right is even, whereas its exponent on the left is one. This is impossible. Therefore adjoining $\sqrt{p_r}$ doubles the degree, supplies the displayed basis, and allows all previous sign changes together with the new sign flip. The induction proves
$$ [K_n:\mathbb Q]=2^n. $$

For
$$ w=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}, $$
the $2^n$ independent sign changes give distinct images
$$ \sum_j\varepsilon_j\sqrt{p_j}, \qquad \varepsilon_j\in\{-1,1\}. $$
Distinctness follows from the linear independence of the singleton basis vectors $\sqrt{p_j}$. Each image is a root of the minimal polynomial of $w$. Its degree is therefore at least $2^n$, and at most $[K_n:\mathbb Q]=2^n$. Thus
$$ [\mathbb Q(w):\mathbb Q]=2^n. $$
A nonconstant rational affine transformation preserves the generated field, so $x_{fR}$ also has degree $2^n$.

Consequence for dense polynomial output

Choose the first $n$ primes. Bertrand’s postulate states that every integer $k\geq2$ has a prime strictly between $k$ and $2k$. Applying it successively gives
$$ p_j\leq2^j. $$
Consequently, all numerical data in the construction have bit length polynomial in $n$, and the complete society input has length polynomial in $n$.

Every nonzero rational polynomial vanishing at $x_{fR}$ has degree at least $2^n$. A dense coefficient-list representation therefore requires at least $2^n+1$ coefficient positions.

Hence no algorithm can output every coordinate as a dense polynomial and isolating interval in time polynomial in the society input length. This is an output-length lower bound. It does not exclude succinct representations; the coordinate displayed here already has a short radical expression.

8. Polynomial-time exact computation for at most two schools

Consider a single stage with sufficient total capacity.

With one school, all mass is retained.

With two schools, let $P_1,P_2$ be the initial proposal totals. Both schools cannot be oversubscribed, since that would imply
$$ P_1+P_2>c_1+c_2\geq M=P_1+P_2. $$
If school $1$ alone is oversubscribed, its total rejection is
$$ R=P_1-c_1. $$
The other school’s unused capacity satisfies
$$ c_2-P_2\geq P_1-c_1=R. $$
Thus all rejected mass is accepted in the next round. There are no further proposals. Each stage finishes in at most two rounds.

Here is the bit-complexity accounting. Suppose current rational numerators and denominators have bit length at most $B$. A sum of at most $\tau$ such rationals has bit length
$$ O\bigl(\tau B+\log(\tau+1)\bigr), $$
by using the product of their denominators as a common denominator. The retention formula then uses a fixed number of additions, multiplications, and divisions per coordinate. One round increases bit length by at most a constant multiple of
$$ (\tau+1)\bigl(B+L+\log(\tau+1)\bigr). $$
Forming the first-stage school totals and second-stage capacities has the same kind of bound.

There are at most four rounds and one intervening capacity computation. Iterating this bound a fixed number of times remains polynomial in $\tau,L$. Each round requires at most $O(\tau^2)$ rational operations, plus polynomial-time priority sorting. Rational arithmetic on these polynomial-length operands is polynomial-time.

Therefore both stages, and their exact rational outcome, are computable in polynomial time for $m\leq2$.

9. What remains open

The qualitative guarantees are completely proved. General exact computation has a representation-sensitive obstruction, and rational-threshold comparison now has the rigorous bounds
$$ \text{square-root-sum hardness} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{EXPTIME}\text{ membership}. $$

A polynomial-time algorithm or an NP-hardness proof for general threshold comparison remains unproved here. Likewise, the complexity of exact computation under a specified succinct algebraic representation remains open in this write-up.
