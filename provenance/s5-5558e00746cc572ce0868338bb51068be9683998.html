The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), type data \((d_t,\phi_t,E_t)\), and per-capita capacities \(c_{i,r}\), find a Nash equilibrium of the per-server allocation game. For each eligible pair, set \(\gamma_{t,i}=\min_{r:d_{t,r}>0} c_{i,r}/d_{t,r}\); choose per-member task rates \(q_{t,i}\ge 0\), let \(q_t=\sum_{j:t\in E_j}q_{t,j}\), require \(\sum_{t:i\in E_t}\mu_t q_{t,i}d_{t,r}\le c_{i,r}\), and give server \(i\) payoff \(\sum_{t:i\in E_i}\mu_t\phi_t g(q_t/(\phi_t\gamma_{t,i}))\), with \(g(z)=z/(1+z)\). Require that for every server-resource pair \((i,r)\), some positive-mass type demanding \(r\) is ineligible for \(i\); ask for a feasible profile in which no server can improve its payoff by changing its own rates while the other servers’ rates remain fixed.

Partial classification and additional arithmetic reductions

1. Proven conclusions and scope

Under the conventional encoding in which each algebraic coordinate is represented by an explicit integer polynomial and a rational isolating interval, polynomial total running time is impossible. This holds for both dense coefficient lists and explicit sparse term lists.

The reason is an unconditional output-size obstruction: there are admissible instances of polynomial input length whose unique equilibrium has a coordinate for which every representing polynomial has exponentially many nonzero terms.

I reprove that result below and establish two additional facts:

  • Equilibria have a polynomial-size characterization by polynomial equations and inequalities of degree at most $3$.
  • Comparing a sum of square roots with an integer reduces, in polynomial time, to an equilibrium-coordinate threshold question, even for games with two servers, disjoint eligibility sets, and a unique equilibrium.

These results do not prove NP-hardness of exact equilibrium search. The requested hardness classification therefore remains partial.

2. Normalization, existence, and polynomial characterization

2.1. An exact normalization removes the masses from the constraints

For every eligible pair $(t,i)$, define
$$ x_{t,i}=\mu_tq_{t,i}, \qquad x_t=\sum_{i\in E_t}x_{t,i}, \qquad w_t=\mu_t\phi_t. $$
Because $\mu_t>0$, this is an invertible change of allocation coordinates, with
$$ q_{t,i}=\frac{x_{t,i}}{\mu_t}, \qquad q_t=\frac{x_t}{\mu_t}. $$
The capacity constraints become
$$ \sum_{t\in T_i}x_{t,i}d_{t,r}\le c_{i,r}. $$
Moreover,
$$ U_i(x) = \sum_{t\in T_i}w_tg\!\left(\frac{x_t}{w_t\gamma_{t,i}}\right) = \sum_{t\in T_i} \frac{w_tx_t}{w_t\gamma_{t,i}+x_t}. $$

Thus the mass game is exactly equivalent to a finite-user game with one user for each supported type and weight $w_t$. This correspondence preserves feasible deviations and payoffs, hence preserves equilibria. There is no need to expand a type into a number of users proportional to its mass denominator.

All transformed input data have polynomial bit length: $w_t$ is the product of two input rationals, and the coordinate transformation uses the input rational $\mu_t$.

2.2. Every admissible instance has an equilibrium

Let $P_i$ be server $i$’s feasible allocation set in the $x$ coordinates. It is nonempty, closed, and convex. It contains the zero vector.

For every eligible pair $(t,i)$, feasibility implies
$$ 0\le x_{t,i}\le \min_{r:d_{t,r}>0}\frac{c_{i,r}}{d_{t,r}} = \gamma_{t,i}. $$
Indeed, the contribution of type $t$ alone to each capacity constraint cannot exceed its capacity. The minimum is over a nonempty set because $d_t$ is nonzero. Consequently, $P_i$ is bounded and therefore compact.

Fix the allocations of the other servers. The derivative of server $i$’s payoff with respect to its own coordinate $x_{t,i}$ is
$$ h_{t,i}(x_t) := \frac{w_t^2\gamma_{t,i}} {(x_t+w_t\gamma_{t,i})^2}. $$
The corresponding second derivative is
$$ -\frac{2w_t^2\gamma_{t,i}} {(x_t+w_t\gamma_{t,i})^3}<0. $$
Mixed second derivatives between distinct types vanish. All denominators are positive because $x_t\ge0$, $w_t>0$, and $\gamma_{t,i}>0$. Hence server $i$’s payoff is strictly concave in its own allocation vector.

Continuity and compactness give a maximizer on $P_i$; strict concavity makes it unique. Write this best response as $B_i(x_{-i})$.

The best response is continuous. To verify this, suppose $z_{-i}^{(j)}$ converges to $z_{-i}$. Any convergent subsequence of $B_i(z_{-i}^{(j)})$ has a limit $b\in P_i$, by compactness. For every $y_i\in P_i$,
$$ U_i(B_i(z_{-i}^{(j)}),z_{-i}^{(j)}) \ge U_i(y_i,z_{-i}^{(j)}). $$
Passing to the subsequential limit shows that $b$ maximizes $U_i(\,\cdot\,,z_{-i})$. Uniqueness forces $b=B_i(z_{-i})$. Compactness then implies convergence of the entire best-response sequence.

Therefore the simultaneous best-response map is continuous and maps the nonempty compact convex set $\prod_iP_i$ to itself. Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem applies, and its fixed point is a Nash equilibrium.

This proof does not require the ineligibility condition.

2.3. Necessary and sufficient equilibrium conditions

Define the capacity slack
$$ s_{i,r}(x) = c_{i,r}-\sum_{t\in T_i}x_{t,i}d_{t,r}. $$
A feasible allocation $x$ is an equilibrium if and only if there are multipliers $\lambda_{i,r}\ge0$ such that
$$ \lambda_{i,r}s_{i,r}(x)=0 $$
and, for every eligible pair,
$$ p_{t,i}:=\sum_{r=1}^m\lambda_{i,r}d_{t,r} \ge h_{t,i}(x_t), $$
$$ x_{t,i}\bigl(p_{t,i}-h_{t,i}(x_t)\bigr)=0. $$

Here is a justification of both directions.

At a maximizer of a differentiable function on a convex set, its gradient has nonpositive inner product with every feasible displacement. For a polyhedron, the polar-cone form of Farkas’ lemma says that such a gradient is a nonnegative linear combination of the active constraint normals.

To apply this fact here, a vector satisfying all active linearized constraints is a feasible displacement for a sufficiently small positive step: every inactive constraint has positive slack, and there are only finitely many constraints. The active normals are the resource-demand rows and the vectors $-e_t$ for zero allocation coordinates. Thus, at a best response,
$$ h_{t,i}(x_t)=p_{t,i}-\nu_{t,i}, $$
where $\lambda_{i,r}\ge0$, $\nu_{t,i}\ge0$, and
$$ \lambda_{i,r}s_{i,r}(x)=0, \qquad \nu_{t,i}x_{t,i}=0. $$
These are exactly the stated conditions.

Conversely, suppose the conditions hold, and put
$$ \nu_{t,i}=p_{t,i}-h_{t,i}(x_t)\ge0. $$
For any feasible alternative $y_i$, concavity gives
$$ U_i(y_i,x_{-i})-U_i(x) \le \sum_{t\in T_i}h_{t,i}(x_t)(y_{t,i}-x_{t,i}). $$
The right-hand side equals
$$ \sum_r\lambda_{i,r} \sum_{t\in T_i}d_{t,r}(y_{t,i}-x_{t,i}) - \sum_{t\in T_i}\nu_{t,i}(y_{t,i}-x_{t,i}). $$
The first sum is nonpositive: if $\lambda_{i,r}>0$, the corresponding capacity constraint is tight at $x_i$. The second sum is also nonpositive, because $\nu_{t,i}x_{t,i}=0$ and $y_{t,i}\ge0$. Therefore no feasible deviation increases server $i$’s payoff.

2.4. A polynomial-size system of degree at most three

Introduce variables $v_{t,i}$ for the marginal utilities. Replace their rational expressions by
$$ v_{t,i}(x_t+w_t\gamma_{t,i})^2 = w_t^2\gamma_{t,i}. $$
Together with feasibility, impose
$$ \lambda_{i,r}\ge0, \qquad \lambda_{i,r}s_{i,r}(x)=0, $$
$$ p_{t,i}-v_{t,i}\ge0, \qquad x_{t,i}(p_{t,i}-v_{t,i})=0. $$
The totals $x_t$ can either be substituted as sums or introduced as additional variables with linear defining equations.

Feasibility ensures $x_t+w_t\gamma_{t,i}>0$, so the cubic equation forces
$$ v_{t,i}=h_{t,i}(x_t). $$
The system is therefore equivalent to the equilibrium conditions, rather than merely a relaxation.

There are polynomially many variables, constraints, and explicitly written monomials in $m,\tau,k$. Every polynomial has degree at most $3$. The constants $\gamma_{t,i}$ can be computed by rational comparisons, and clearing rational coefficient denominators produces integer polynomials of polynomial bit length.

It follows that the following auxiliary decision problem belongs to $\exists\mathbb R$: given an instance, an eligible pair $(t,i)$, and a rational $\theta$, decide whether some equilibrium satisfies
$$ q_{t,i}\ge\theta. $$
One simply adds the linear inequality
$$ x_{t,i}\ge\mu_t\theta $$
and existentially quantifies the displayed system.

There is also always an equilibrium with algebraic coordinates. The standard real-algebraic sampling theorem states that a nonempty semialgebraic set defined over $\mathbb Q$ contains a point whose coordinates are real algebraic numbers, and such a point is computable. Its hypotheses hold here: the displayed rational polynomial system is nonempty by equilibrium existence. Thus exact equilibrium search is computable in principle, without a polynomial-time claim. These sampling and quantifier-elimination results are treated in [Basu’s survey of algorithms in real algebraic geometry](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf).

3. A two-server construction with a unique equilibrium

The following construction will serve both lower-bound arguments.

Let $N$ be a power of two. Use two servers, resources $0,1,\ldots,N$, and types $0,1,\ldots,N$.

Type $0$ has
$$ \mu_0=\frac12,\qquad \phi_0=1,\qquad E_0=\{2\}, $$
and demands one unit of every resource.

For $j=1,\ldots,N$, type $j$ has
$$ \mu_j=\frac1{2N},\qquad E_j=\{1\}, $$
and demands one unit of resources $0$ and $j$, with zero demand elsewhere. Choose numbers
$$ 0<a_j\le1,\qquad w_j>0, $$
and set
$$ \phi_j=2Nw_j. $$
Capacities are
$$ c_{1,0}=1,\qquad c_{1,j}=a_j, \qquad c_{2,r}=1. $$

The masses are positive and sum to one. Both servers have eligible types. Moreover,
$$ \gamma_{j,1}=a_j>0, \qquad \gamma_{0,2}=1. $$
The ineligibility condition holds for every resource:

  • Type $0$ is ineligible for server $1$ and demands every resource.
  • For server $2$, type $j$ witnesses the condition for resource $j$, while type $1$ witnesses it for resource $0$.

In particular, every type has a nonempty eligibility set.

Write
$$ x_j=\mu_jq_{j,1}. $$
Server $1$’s constraints and payoff are
$$ x_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^Nx_j\le1,\qquad x_j\le a_j, $$
$$ F(x)=\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{w_jx_j}{w_ja_j+x_j}. $$
Define
$$ A_w=\sum_{j=1}^Nw_ja_j, \qquad S_w=\sum_{j=1}^Nw_j\sqrt{a_j}, \qquad C_w=1+A_w. $$

Template lemma. If the numbers
$$ x_j^* = w_j\left(\frac{C_w\sqrt{a_j}}{S_w}-a_j\right) $$
satisfy $0<x_j^*<a_j$ for every $j$, then the constructed game has a unique equilibrium, with
$$ q_{j,1}^*=2Nx_j^*, \qquad q_{0,2}^*=2. $$

Proof. Summing the proposed coordinates gives
$$ \sum_jx_j^*=C_w-A_w=1. $$
At these coordinates,
$$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_j}(x^*) = \frac{w_j^2a_j}{(w_ja_j+x_j^*)^2} = \frac{S_w^2}{C_w^2}, $$
independently of $j$. Therefore, for every feasible $y$, concavity gives
$$ F(y)-F(x^*) \le \frac{S_w^2}{C_w^2}\sum_j(y_j-x_j^*) \le0. $$
Furthermore,
$$ \frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_j^2} = -\frac{2w_j^2a_j}{(w_ja_j+x_j)^3}<0, $$
and mixed second derivatives vanish. Thus $F$ is strictly concave and $x^*$ is its unique maximizer.

Server $2$’s feasible interval is $0\le q_{0,2}\le2$, and its payoff
$$ \frac12\frac{q_{0,2}}{1+q_{0,2}} $$
is strictly increasing. Its unique maximizer is $2$.

Eligibility sets are disjoint, so the two optimization problems are independent. Their unique maximizers constitute the unique equilibrium. $\square$

4. Exponentially large exact output

4.1. Instances of polynomial input length

Let $n\ge16$ be a power of two, and apply the template with $N=n$ and
$$ w_j=\frac1{2n}. $$
Thus every type has weight $\phi_j=1$.

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_{n-1}$ be the first $n-1$ odd primes. Set
$$ a_1=1. $$
For $2\le j\le n$, let $b_j$ be the least nonnegative integer with
$$ p_{j-1}\le4^{b_j}, $$
and set
$$ a_j=\frac{p_{j-1}}{4^{b_j}}. $$
Minimality gives
$$ \frac14<a_j\le1. $$
All game data are dyadic rationals.

For the input-length bound, use Bertrand’s postulate: for every integer $u\ge2$, there is a prime strictly between $u$ and $2u$. Starting from $p_1=3<4$ and applying the theorem with $u=p_\ell$ gives inductively
$$ p_\ell<2^{\ell+1}. $$
Its hypothesis holds because every $p_\ell\ge3$. Consequently, each prime and each denominator $4^{b_j}$ has $O(n)$ bits. The dense demand matrix has $O(n^2)$ constant-size entries, and the remaining data require $O(n^2)$ bits in total. Thus
$$ L=O(n^2). $$
A formal proof of the invoked prime theorem is available in [Mathlib’s Bertrand module](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/NumberTheory/Bertrand.html).

4.2. Explicit unique equilibrium

Define
$$ A=\sum_{j=1}^na_j, \qquad S=\sum_{j=1}^n\sqrt{a_j}, \qquad C=2n+A. $$
The template proposes
$$ q_j^*=\frac{C\sqrt{a_j}}{S}-a_j. $$
Since $1/4<a_j\le1$,
$$ \frac n2<S\le n, \qquad 2n<C\le3n. $$
Hence
$$ 2<\frac CS<6. $$
It follows that
$$ q_j^*>2\sqrt{a_j}-a_j>0, \qquad q_j^*<6. $$
Therefore
$$ x_j^*=\frac{q_j^*}{2n} < \frac3n \le\frac3{16} < \frac14 < a_j. $$
The template lemma applies and proves uniqueness of the equilibrium.

Because $a_1=1$, one required equilibrium coordinate is
$$ \alpha=q_1^*=\frac CS-1. $$

4.3. Independent square roots and conjugates

We need the following field fact, proved here.

Multiquadratic lemma. For distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_\ell$, the field
$$ K_\ell=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_\ell}) $$
has degree $2^\ell$. A basis consists of
$$ e_J=\prod_{j\in J}\sqrt{p_j}, \qquad J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}, $$
and every independent choice of signs for the square roots defines an automorphism.

Proof. Induct on $\ell$, starting with $K_0=\mathbb Q$.

Assume the assertion for $K_{\ell-1}$. Suppose that $x\in K_{\ell-1}$ satisfies $x^2=p_\ell$. Every sign automorphism $\sigma$ then satisfies
$$ \sigma(x)^2=p_\ell, $$
so $\sigma(x)=x$ or $\sigma(x)=-x$.

Expand $x$ in the inductive basis. Two distinct basis monomials have different sign characters: changing the sign of a square root indexed by their symmetric difference changes the sign of exactly one monomial. Since $x$ must be an eigenvector of every sign automorphism, its expansion can have only one nonzero coefficient. Thus
$$ x=b\prod_{j\in J}\sqrt{p_j} $$
for some nonzero $b\in\mathbb Q$ and $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}$.

Squaring gives
$$ p_\ell=b^2\prod_{j\in J}p_j. $$
This contradicts prime factorization: the exponent of $p_\ell$ on the left is odd, while on the right it is even. This argument also covers the initial induction step.

Therefore $X^2-p_\ell$ is irreducible over $K_{\ell-1}$, adjoining $\sqrt{p_\ell}$ doubles the degree, and the stated basis follows. Each previous sign automorphism extends with either sign for the new square root, proving the automorphism assertion. $\square$

For $j\ge2$,
$$ \sqrt{a_j}=\frac{\sqrt{p_{j-1}}}{2^{b_j}}. $$
The sign automorphisms therefore send $S$ to
$$ S_\varepsilon = 1+\sum_{j=2}^n\varepsilon_j\sqrt{a_j}, \qquad \varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{n-1}. $$
Basis independence shows that these values are pairwise distinct and none is zero. Accordingly, the images of $\alpha$ are the pairwise distinct real numbers
$$ \alpha_\varepsilon=\frac{C}{S_\varepsilon}-1. $$
There are $2^{n-1}$ such images, so $\alpha$ has algebraic degree $2^{n-1}$.

At least $2^{n-2}$ of these images are positive. Pair $\varepsilon$ with $-\varepsilon$. Since
$$ S_\varepsilon+S_{-\varepsilon}=2, $$
at least one value in each pair is positive. Whenever $S_\varepsilon>0$,
$$ S_\varepsilon\le S\le n<C, $$
and hence $\alpha_\varepsilon>0$. There are $2^{n-2}$ pairs.

4.4. Every representing polynomial has exponentially many terms

Positive-root lemma. A nonzero real polynomial with $h$ nonzero terms has at most $h-1$ distinct positive roots.

Proof. Induct on $h$. A single monomial has no positive root. For $h>1$, divide by the smallest power of $X$ occurring in the polynomial. This preserves its positive roots and leaves a polynomial with a nonzero constant term and $h$ terms.

Its derivative has exactly $h-1$ terms, so by induction it has at most $h-2$ distinct positive roots. Rolle’s theorem then bounds the original polynomial’s number of distinct positive roots by $h-1$. $\square$

Let $P\in\mathbb Z[X]$ be any nonzero polynomial with $P(\alpha)=0$. Every sign automorphism fixes its coefficients, so
$$ P(\alpha_\varepsilon)=0 $$
for every $\varepsilon$. Thus $P$ has at least $2^{n-2}$ distinct positive roots. The positive-root lemma forces $P$ to have at least
$$ 2^{n-2}+1 $$
nonzero terms.

This applies to every representing polynomial, including nonminimal ones. Consequently, both explicit dense and explicit sparse encodings require exponentially many output entries. An isolating interval does not remove the polynomial-output requirement.

Here
$$ k=2,\qquad m=\tau=n+1,\qquad L=O(n^2). $$
Writing the required coordinate therefore takes more time than every polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L$. This proves the unconditional obstruction to polynomial total running time.

5. A polynomial reduction from square-root-sum comparison

This section gives an additional arithmetic reduction without using the large-output argument.

5.1. Source and target decision problems

The source problem takes positive binary integers $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ and a nonnegative binary integer $H$, and asks whether
$$ \sum_{\ell=1}^n\sqrt{b_\ell}\le H. $$

The target is the equilibrium-coordinate threshold problem defined in Section 2.4. I construct, in polynomial time, a game with a unique equilibrium and a rational threshold $\theta$ such that
$$ \sum_{\ell=1}^n\sqrt{b_\ell}\le H \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_{j_*,1}^*\ge\theta. $$

5.2. Construction

Let $N$ be the smallest power of two satisfying
$$ N\ge16n+1, $$
and put
$$ f=N-16n. $$
Then $f\ge1$, $N\ge32$, and $N=O(n)$.

For each $\ell$, choose the least nonnegative integer $e_\ell$ with
$$ b_\ell\le4^{e_\ell}. $$
Put
$$ e_*=\max_\ell e_\ell, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{4N2^{e_*}}. $$

Apply the template with the following $N$ types eligible for server $1$:

  • For each $\ell$, introduce $16$ types, each with

$$ a=\frac{b_\ell}{4^{e_\ell}}, \qquad w=\varepsilon2^{e_\ell}. $$

  • Introduce $f$ additional types, each with

$$ a=1,\qquad w=\varepsilon. $$

Choose one of the additional types as $j_*$.

Every $a$ lies in $(1/4,1]$. For $b_\ell=1$, this follows from $e_\ell=0$; otherwise it follows from minimality of $e_\ell$. All game data are dyadic rationals, and the template already verifies every required admissibility condition.

The construction has polynomial bit length. Indeed, $e_\ell$ and $e_*$ are bounded by the bit lengths of the source integers. The exponents defining $a,w,\varepsilon$, and $\phi=2Nw$ therefore have polynomial magnitude. There are $O(n)$ types and resources, and the demand matrix has $O(n^2)$ entries. The integer powers and rational sums used in the construction can be computed in polynomial time.

5.3. Verifying the equilibrium formula

Write
$$ B=\sum_{\ell=1}^n\sqrt{b_\ell}, \qquad D=f+16B. $$
For the template quantities,
$$ S_w=\varepsilon D, $$
because each original copy contributes
$$ w\sqrt a = \varepsilon2^{e_\ell} \frac{\sqrt{b_\ell}}{2^{e_\ell}} = \varepsilon\sqrt{b_\ell}, $$
and each additional type contributes $\varepsilon$.

Also,
$$ A_w = \varepsilon\left( f+16\sum_{\ell=1}^n\frac{b_\ell}{2^{e_\ell}} \right), \qquad C_w=1+A_w. $$

Every type satisfies
$$ w\le\varepsilon2^{e_*}=\frac1{4N}. $$
Since $a\le1$ and $\sqrt a\le1$, summing over the $N$ types gives
$$ 0<A_w\le\frac14, \qquad 0<S_w\le\frac14, \qquad 1<C_w\le\frac54. $$
In particular,
$$ \frac{C_w}{S_w}>4. $$
Thus every proposed allocation
$$ x^*=w\sqrt a\left(\frac{C_w}{S_w}-\sqrt a\right) $$
is positive.

For a copy associated with $b_\ell$, the formula is
$$ x^* = \frac{C_w\sqrt{b_\ell}}{D} - \frac{\varepsilon b_\ell}{2^{e_\ell}}. $$
Because $D\ge16\sqrt{b_\ell}$,
$$ x^* < \frac{C_w}{16} \le\frac5{64} < \frac14 < a. $$

For an additional type,
$$ x^*=\frac{C_w}{D}-\varepsilon. $$
Each $b_\ell\ge1$, so
$$ D=f+16B\ge f+16n=N. $$
Consequently,
$$ x^* < \frac{C_w}{N} \le\frac5{4N} \le\frac5{128} < 1=a. $$

All hypotheses of the template lemma have now been verified. The equilibrium is unique, and its distinguished coordinate is
$$ q_{j_*,1}^* = 2N\left(\frac{C_w}{f+16B}-\varepsilon\right). $$

5.4. Exact threshold equivalence

Define the rational threshold
$$ \theta = 2N\left(\frac{C_w}{f+16H}-\varepsilon\right). $$
It has polynomial bit length and is computable from the source instance. Its denominator is positive because $f\ge1$ and $H\ge0$.

Since $C_w>0$ and both denominators below are positive,
$$ q_{j_*,1}^*\ge\theta $$
is equivalent to
$$ \frac{C_w}{f+16B} \ge \frac{C_w}{f+16H}, $$
which is equivalent to
$$ f+16B\le f+16H, $$
and therefore to
$$ B\le H. $$

This proves a polynomial-time many-one reduction from square-root-sum comparison to equilibrium-coordinate threshold decision. The target games have two servers, disjoint eligibility sets, and a unique equilibrium.

6. What remains unresolved

The exact-output lower bound settles one side of the question: under explicit dense or sparse polynomial encodings, polynomial total running time is impossible, independently of any separation between complexity classes.

It does not establish NP-hardness. Nor does the square-root-sum reduction establish it: no NP-hardness proof for that source problem is supplied here. The membership of equilibrium threshold decision in $\exists\mathbb R$ is an upper bound and likewise gives no hardness result.

The cited finite-user paper’s Assumption 2 is the stated ineligibility condition, and its Theorem 8 concerns iterative convergence. Neither construction above relies on that convergence theorem. Such convergence cannot overcome the proved requirement to write a superpolynomial exact output. [Finite-user convergence paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.10114)
