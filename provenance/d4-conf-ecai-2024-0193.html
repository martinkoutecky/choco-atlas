The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), ballot size \(l\), and disjoint approval parties \(P_i\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), let \(q_i=\lfloor k\mu_i\rfloor\) and assume \(l\le q_i\le |P_i|\). Each party allocates its mass among ballots \(B\in\binom{C}{l}\), with \(x_{i,B}\ge0\) and \(\sum_B x_{i,B}=\mu_i\); candidate \(c\) receives vote mass \(\sum_i\sum_{B\ni c}x_{i,B}\). The winning committees are the size-\(k\) sets with maximum total vote mass, and party \(i\)'s utility is its minimum number of candidates in any winning committee. Does the profile that chooses \(X_i\subseteq P_i\) with \(|X_i|=q_i\) and distributes type \(i\)'s mass uniformly over the \(l\)-subsets of \(X_i\) form an ε-Nash equilibrium against arbitrary unilateral mass reallocations, where \(\epsilon=k-\sum_i q_i\)?

Result

The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses is a yes-instance, including when \(\epsilon=0\).

In fact, writing \(p_i=|P_i|\), we prove the exact formulas
\[ U_i(x^*)=\max\{q_i,k-m+p_i\}, \qquad \max_{x'_i}U_i(x^*_{-i},x'_i)=\min\{p_i,q_i+\epsilon\}. \]

1. Scores at the designated profile

Set \(Q=\sum_jq_j=k-\epsilon\). Because \(q_j\le k\mu_j\) and \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), we have \(Q\le k\), hence \(\epsilon\ge0\).

For each \(c\in X_j\), counting the size-\(l\) subsets containing \(c\) gives
\[ s_{x^*}(c) =\mu_j\frac{\binom{q_j-1}{l-1}}{\binom{q_j}{l}} =\frac{l\mu_j}{q_j} \ge\frac{l}{k}>0. \]
Disjointness of the approval types ensures that other parties contribute nothing to this score. Every candidate outside \(\bigcup_jX_j\) has score zero.

We will repeatedly use the following exchange property: if \(W\) maximizes committee score, \(c\in W\), and \(d\notin W\), then \(s(c)\ge s(d)\). Otherwise, replacing \(c\) by \(d\) strictly increases the committee score.

Since exactly \(Q\le k\) candidates have positive scores, every winning committee contains all of them. Its remaining \(\epsilon\) members can be any zero-score candidates. Consequently,
\[ U_i(x^*)\ge q_i. \]

For completeness, the exact utility follows by counting available zero-score candidates outside \(P_i\). Their number is
\[ Z_i=m-p_i-(Q-q_i). \]
Among the \(\epsilon\) additional committee members, the minimum possible number from \(P_i\) is \(\max\{0,\epsilon-Z_i\}\). All such extensions are winning committees, so
\[ U_i(x^*) =q_i+\max\{0,\epsilon-Z_i\} =\max\{q_i,k-m+p_i\}. \]

2. A bound for every unilateral deviation

Fix a party \(i\) and an arbitrary feasible allocation \(x'_i\), allowing ballots outside \(P_i\). Write \(x'=(x^*_{-i},x'_i)\), and define
\[ D_i=\bigcup_{j\ne i}X_j, \qquad R_i=|D_i|=Q-q_i=k-\epsilon-q_i. \]
The sets are disjoint, and \(D_i\cap P_i=\varnothing\).

Every \(d\in D_i\) retains its original opponent contribution. The deviator adds only nonnegative scores, so
\[ s_{x'}(d)\ge\frac{l}{k}. \]

Meanwhile, opponents contribute nothing to candidates in \(P_i\). Therefore their total score satisfies
\[ \sum_{c\in P_i}s_{x'}(c) =\sum_{B\in\binom{C}{l}}|B\cap P_i|x'_{i,B} \le l\sum_Bx'_{i,B} =l\mu_i <\frac{l(q_i+1)}{k}. \]
The strict inequality follows from \(k\mu_i<q_i+1\).

Suppose a winning committee \(W\) contained
\[ t=|W\cap P_i|\ge q_i+\epsilon+1. \]
Then
\[ |W\setminus P_i|=k-t\le R_i-1. \]
Thus some \(d\in D_i\) is omitted from \(W\). By the exchange property, every \(c\in W\cap P_i\) satisfies
\[ s_{x'}(c)\ge s_{x'}(d)\ge\frac{l}{k}. \]
It follows that
\[ \sum_{c\in P_i}s_{x'}(c) \ge\frac{tl}{k} \ge\frac{l(q_i+1)}{k}, \]
contradicting the score-budget inequality.

Hence every winning committee after every unilateral deviation satisfies
\[ |W\cap P_i|\le q_i+\epsilon. \]
In particular,
\[ U_i(x^*_{-i},x'_i)\le q_i+\epsilon. \]

Combining this with \(U_i(x^*)\ge q_i\) proves
\[ U_i(x^*)\ge U_i(x^*_{-i},x'_i)-\epsilon \]
for every party and every feasible deviation. This argument also applies when \(\epsilon=0\).

3. The deviation bound is attainable

Let
\[ r_i=\min\{p_i,q_i+\epsilon\}. \]
Since \(p_i\ge q_i\ge l\), we have \(r_i\ge l\). Choose \(Y_i\subseteq P_i\) with \(|Y_i|=r_i\), and distribute mass \(\mu_i\) uniformly over its size-\(l\) subsets.

The positive-score candidates are exactly \(Y_i\cup D_i\), whose number satisfies
\[ r_i+R_i\le(q_i+\epsilon)+(k-\epsilon-q_i)=k. \]
Every winning committee therefore contains \(Y_i\), giving utility at least \(r_i\). The preceding bound and the elementary bound \(U_i\le p_i\) give utility at most \(r_i\). Thus
\[ \max_{x'_i}U_i(x^*_{-i},x'_i)=r_i. \]
This construction specifies the allocation without enumerating its ballots.

4. Decision algorithm and running time

On the promised input domain, the algorithm simply returns yes. Its decision time is \(O(1)\); reading the entire input takes \(O(L)\).

If malformed inputs must also be rejected, a straightforward validator checks the candidate lists, disjointness, rational masses, normalization, quotas, and designated sets. Using schoolbook exact integer arithmetic gives a conservative \(O(L^3+m+\tau)\) bit-operation bound. Here \(k,l\le m\), their binary encodings are included in \(L\), and no enumeration of \(\binom{m}{l}\) ballots or \(\binom{m}{k}\) committees is required.

The finite-electorate analogue is stated in [Theorem 14 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17973v1#S7). The proof above independently establishes the exact continuous statement, including unrestricted unilateral ballots and \(\epsilon=0\).
