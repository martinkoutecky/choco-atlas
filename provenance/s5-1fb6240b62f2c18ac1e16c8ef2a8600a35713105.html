The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two types \(A,B\), each with mass \(1/2\), and two arms, suppose type \(B\) has utility vector \((1,1)\) each round and type \(A\) has an adversarially chosen vector \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^2\). The learner chooses \(p_t\in\Delta_2\), samples an arm, and observes that arm's utility for each type. With \(F_{\mu,t}(p)=\sqrt{\langle p,v_t\rangle}\), is worst-case expected regret \(\max_{p\in\Delta_2}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p)-\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p_t)\) necessarily \(\Omega(T)\), or can an algorithm guarantee \(o(T)\) regret uniformly?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Here \(\mu(A)=\mu(B)=1/2\), \(\tau=2\), and the number of arms is \(m=2\). Express any running time in \(m,\tau,L,T\), where \(L\) is the binary input bit length.
For a positive integer horizon \(T\), an oblivious adversary knowing the algorithm fixes an undisclosed sequence \(v_1,\ldots,v_T\in\{0,1\}^2\). Type \(A\) has utilities \(v_t\), and type \(B\) has utilities \((1,1)\).
At round \(t\), the learner selects \(p_t\in\Delta_2\) using past feedback, samples \(i_t\sim p_t\), and observes only \((v_{t,i_t},1)\). Its welfare is \(F_{\mu,t}(p_t)=\sqrt{\langle p_t,v_t\rangle}\).
Define \(R_T=\max_{p\in\Delta_2}\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p)-\mathbb E\sum_{t=1}^T F_{\mu,t}(p_t)\), with expectation over learner randomness.

Is worst-case expected regret necessarily \(\Omega(T)\) for every algorithm, or can an algorithm guarantee \(o(T)\) uniformly over all such sequences?

Known results: Zhang, Vuong, and Luo establish \(\Omega(T)\) regret for two discrete agents and two arms with exactly these utility restrictions and feedback. Equal masses give exactly their two-agent geometric-mean objective, so this stated continuous question is already settled by that result. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20678v1#S4.SS1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified an oblivious adversary that fixes the utility sequence before interaction, knowing the algorithm, as required by the discrete setup.
  • Specified that learner decisions use only past feedback and that the regret expectation is over learner randomness for each fixed sequence.
  • Used normalized total mass one and denoted the number of arms by m.

Result and connection to the cited theorem

Every algorithm has worst-case expected regret \(\Omega(T)\). Uniform \(o(T)\) regret is impossible, regardless of computation time.

Theorem 4.1 of [Zhang, Vuong, and Luo](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20678v1#S4.SS1) proves this lower bound with two agents, two arms, binary utilities, and the second agent’s utilities constantly equal to \((1,1)\). Its feedback and regret definitions match this task.

Indeed, equal masses give the welfare
\[ \textstyle \bigl(\langle p,v_t\rangle\bigr)^{1/2} \bigl(\langle p,(1,1)\rangle\bigr)^{1/2} =\sqrt{\langle p,v_t\rangle}. \]
Here and below, the society has exactly \(\tau=2\) listed types. The following direct proof also establishes the deterministic, oblivious-sequence quantifier explicitly.

Two indistinguishable environments

Write \(p=(x,1-x)\), where \(x\in[0,1]\), and define
\(f_v(x)=\sqrt{xv_1+(1-x)v_2}\).

Consider two distributions on utility vectors:

| Utility vector \(v\) | Distribution \(D_0\) | Distribution \(D_1\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| \((0,0)\) | \(1/2\) | \(1/4\) |
| \((0,1)\) | \(1/4\) | \(1/2\) |
| \((1,0)\) | \(0\) | \(1/4\) |
| \((1,1)\) | \(1/4\) | \(0\) |

In environment \(j\in\{0,1\}\), draw the entire sequence independently from \(D_j\) before play, independently of learner randomness. This is an oblivious randomized construction.

Both distributions have identical coordinate marginals:
\( \Pr(v_1=1)=1/4 \) and \( \Pr(v_2=1)=1/2 \).
Consequently, conditional on selecting arm \(1\), the observed first coordinate is Bernoulli with parameter \(1/4\); conditional on selecting arm \(2\), it is Bernoulli with parameter \(1/2\). The observed second coordinate always equals \(1\).

Therefore every algorithm has the same distribution of actions and feedback histories in both environments. To justify this for randomized algorithms, condition on all learner randomness. The learner’s decision is then a deterministic function of its history. Induction on rounds gives identical history distributions, because the conditional feedback distributions coincide for either selected arm. Integrating over learner randomness proves the assertion. In particular, \(x_t=p_{t,1}\) has a common law, denoted \(\rho_t\), in both environments.

A positive welfare gap

Define \(g_j(x)=\mathbb E_{v\sim D_j}f_v(x)\). The table gives
\(g_0(x)=\frac14+\frac14\sqrt{1-x}\) and
\(g_1(x)=\frac14\sqrt{x}+\frac12\sqrt{1-x}\).

Their maximum values are
\(M_0=\frac12\), attained at \(x_0^\star=0\), and
\(M_1=\frac{\sqrt5}{4}\), attained at \(x_1^\star=\frac15\).
For the second calculation, Cauchy–Schwarz yields
\[ \frac14\sqrt{x}+\frac12\sqrt{1-x} \leq \sqrt{\left(\frac14\right)^2+\left(\frac12\right)^2} \sqrt{x+(1-x)} =\frac{\sqrt5}{4}, \]
with equality at \(x=1/5\).

Applying the same inequality to the sum gives, for every \(x\in[0,1]\),
\[ g_0(x)+g_1(x) =\frac14+\frac14\sqrt{x}+\frac34\sqrt{1-x} \leq\frac{1+\sqrt{10}}4. \]
Hence
\[ \bigl(M_0-g_0(x)\bigr)+\bigl(M_1-g_1(x)\bigr) \geq \delta:=\frac{1+\sqrt5-\sqrt{10}}4>0. \]
Positivity follows from
\((1+\sqrt5)^2=6+2\sqrt5>10\).

Regret and extraction of a fixed sequence

For a deterministic sequence \(S=(v_1,\ldots,v_T)\), let \(R_T(S)\) denote its regret, with expectation only over learner randomness.

In environment \(j\), the best comparator for each realized sequence performs at least as well as the fixed comparator \(x_j^\star\). Moreover, \(v_t\) is independent of \(x_t\): the current utility vector is independent of past feedback and learner randomness. Thus
\[ \mathbb E_{S\sim D_j^T}R_T(S) \geq TM_j-\sum_{t=1}^T\int g_j(x)\,d\rho_t(x). \]
Adding these two inequalities and using the pointwise gap proves
\[ \mathbb E_{D_0^T}R_T(S)+\mathbb E_{D_1^T}R_T(S) \geq\delta T. \]
At least one expectation is therefore at least \(\delta T/2\). Since each distribution has finite support, some deterministic sequence in that support satisfies
\[ R_T(S)\geq cT, \qquad c=\frac{1+\sqrt5-\sqrt{10}}8>0. \]
This sequence can be fixed before play using knowledge of the algorithm, exactly as required.

Finally, welfare lies in \([0,1]\), so \(R_T(S)\leq T\) for every sequence. Thus every algorithm’s worst-case expected regret is \(\Theta(T)\). The lower bound already holds at \(m=\tau=2\) and imposes no restriction on running time as a function of \(m,\tau,L,T\).
