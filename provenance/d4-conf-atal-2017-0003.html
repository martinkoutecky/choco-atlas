The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(M\) and \(W\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and complete deterministic pairwise preference relations over opposite-side types that may be cyclic, does there exist a nonnegative flow \(x_{ij}\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j\) such that for every pair \(i,j\), \(A_{ij}(x)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\) and \(B_{ij}(x)=\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}\) are not both positive?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M,W\) be nonempty finite sets of types. Each society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Write \(\tau=|M|+|W|\) for the total support size, not the number of possible types, \(m=\max\{|M|,|W|\}\), and \(L\) for the total input bit length. Masses satisfy \(\sum_{i\in M}\mu_i=\sum_{j\in W}\nu_j=1\).
Each type has an explicitly given strict pairwise preference relation over opposite-side types: for distinct types exactly one direction holds, self-comparisons never hold, and cycles are allowed on either side. All opposite-side types are acceptable; individuals of the same type are indifferent.
A complete allocation is a real flow \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{M\times W}\) with \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\nu_j\). Define \(A_{ij}(x)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\) and \(B_{ij}(x)=\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}\). It is stable if, for every \((i,j)\in M\times W\), \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\), including pairs with \(x_{ij}>0\).

Is deciding whether such a stable complete allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete agents, existence of a stable complete matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with deterministic pairwise preferences; it is polynomial-time solvable when preferences on one side are transitive. [Aziz et al.](https://www.nickmattei.net/docs/pairwise.pdf)
No complexity result for the exact mass-flow problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete deterministic pairwise preferences as strict tournaments: exactly one comparison direction for distinct opposite-side types, with no self-comparisons or ties between distinct types.
  • Made all opposite-side types acceptable and required all mass to be matched, following the discrete complete-matching domain.
  • Specified indifference between individuals of the same type and allowed real, divisible allocation flows.
  • Restricted the supplied type lists to positive-mass support and defined tau as the combined support size, m as the larger side's support size, and L as the total input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Stable complete mass-flow existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when both sides have the same number of types and every type has the same dyadic mass. The key fact is that any perfect matching contained in a stable flow’s positive support inherits stability.

1. Stability is preserved when support shrinks

For a nonnegative matrix \(x\), define
\[ \operatorname{supp}(x)=\{(i,j):x_{ij}>0\}. \]

Lemma 1. If \(x\) satisfies the stated stability condition and \(y\) is nonnegative with \(\operatorname{supp}(y)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(x)\), then \(y\) also satisfies that condition.

Proof. Suppose some pair \((i,j)\) violates stability for \(y\). Thus \(A_{ij}(y)>0\) and \(B_{ij}(y)>0\).

Because these are finite sums of nonnegative entries, there are types \(j'\) and \(i'\) such that
\[ j\succ_i j',\qquad y_{ij'}>0,\qquad i\succ_j i',\qquad y_{i'j}>0. \]
Support containment gives \(x_{ij'}>0\) and \(x_{i'j}>0\). Consequently \(A_{ij}(x)>0\) and \(B_{ij}(x)>0\), contradicting stability of \(x\). This argument applies to every pair, including pairs with positive allocated mass. \(\square\)

2. Equal-mass flows admit stable supported perfect matchings

Lemma 2. Suppose \(|M|=|W|=n\) and all masses equal \(1/n\). A stable complete allocation exists if and only if the corresponding discrete instance has a stable perfect matching.

Proof. First, let \(P\) be a stable perfect matching. Define \(y_{ij}=1/n\) on \(P\) and \(y_{ij}=0\) elsewhere. This is a complete allocation.

Write \(P(i)\) and \(P(j)\) for the partners of \(i\) and \(j\). Then
\[ A_{ij}(y)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad j\succ_i P(i), \]
and
\[ B_{ij}(y)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\succ_j P(j). \]
Both inequalities hold exactly when \((i,j)\) blocks the discrete matching. For matched pairs, both sums are zero because self-comparisons never hold. Thus \(y\) is stable.

Conversely, let \(x\) be a stable complete allocation. Form the bipartite graph with edge set \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\). For \(S\subseteq M\), let \(N(S)\) denote its neighbors. The marginal constraints imply
\[ \frac{|S|}{n} =\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in N(S)}x_{ij} \leq\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_{i\in M}x_{ij} =\frac{|N(S)|}{n}. \]
Hence \(|N(S)|\geq|S|\) for every \(S\subseteq M\). By Hall’s marriage theorem, the support graph contains a matching \(P\) covering \(M\). Since the two sides have equal size, \(P\) is perfect.

The allocation assigning mass \(1/n\) along \(P\) has support contained in \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\), so Lemma 1 makes it stable. By the first part’s blocking-pair characterization, \(P\) is a stable discrete matching. \(\square\)

Neither lemma assumes transitive preferences.

3. Polynomial reduction and dyadic normalization

The discrete source problem asks whether two equally sized sets of agents, with complete strict pairwise preferences that may contain cycles, admit a stable complete matching. This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete by Theorem 4 of [Aziz et al., *Stable Matching with Uncertain Pairwise Preferences*](https://www.nickmattei.net/docs/pairwise.pdf), even when all comparisons are deterministic. In that restriction, every comparison between distinct candidates has exactly one preferred direction, as required here.

Take a source instance with \(n\geq1\) agents on each side. To ensure that the output masses are dyadic, put
\[ N=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}. \]
Add \(N-n\) dummy agents on each side, arranged into designated pairs.

Specify preferences as follows:

  • Preserve every original comparison, and let each original agent prefer every original candidate to every dummy candidate.
  • Each dummy agent prefers its designated partner to every other candidate.
  • Complete all remaining comparisons using arbitrary strict linear orders.

These preferences are explicit strict pairwise relations, and all candidates remain acceptable.

The padded discrete instance has a stable perfect matching exactly when the original instance does. Indeed, a stable original matching extends by matching all designated dummy pairs. No pair involving a dummy can block because that dummy receives its first choice.

Conversely, every stable matching of the padded instance must contain every designated dummy pair: if such a pair were absent, its two endpoints would strictly prefer each other to their partners and would block. Restricting a stable padded matching to the original agents therefore gives a stable perfect matching of the original instance.

Now give every padded agent its own type and assign every type mass \(1/N\). Copy the padded preferences unchanged. Lemma 2 proves
\[ \text{original discrete instance has a stable perfect matching} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{output mass-flow instance has a stable complete allocation}. \]

The reduction is polynomial: \(N<2n\), each mass \(1/N\) is dyadic with encoding length \(O(\log n)\), and the explicit comparison tables contain \(O(N^3)\) entries. The output has \(m=N\) and \(\tau=2N\).

Thus the stated problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even for equal dyadic masses.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Real-valued feasibility requires justification that polynomial-size certificates exist.

Write each input mass as a rational with positive integer denominator, and let \(Q\) be the product of all these denominators. Then \(Q\mu_i\) and \(Q\nu_j\) are integers, and the bit length of \(Q\) is \(O(L)\).

Suppose a stable complete allocation \(x\) exists, and set \(E=\operatorname{supp}(x)\). Construct a directed network with source \(s\), sink \(t\), and arcs:

  • \(s\to i\) of capacity \(Q\mu_i\), for every \(i\in M\);
  • \(i\to j\) of capacity \(Q\), for every \((i,j)\in E\);
  • \(j\to t\) of capacity \(Q\nu_j\), for every \(j\in W\).

The scaled allocation \(Qx\) induces a feasible flow of value \(Q\). The total capacity leaving \(s\) is exactly \(Q\), so the maximum flow value is \(Q\).

All capacities are nonnegative integers. The integral maximum-flow theorem therefore supplies an integral flow of value \(Q\). Since the source and sink capacities each sum to \(Q\), every source arc and every sink arc is saturated. Its middle-arc values \(z_{ij}\), extended by zero outside \(E\), satisfy
\[ \sum_j z_{ij}=Q\mu_i,\qquad \sum_i z_{ij}=Q\nu_j. \]

Consequently \(y=z/Q\) is complete and has support contained in that of \(x\). Lemma 1 makes \(y\) stable.

Each integer \(z_{ij}\) lies between \(0\) and \(Q\), so the entire matrix has polynomial encoding length. A verifier checks nonnegativity, the displayed marginal equalities, and, for every pair \((i,j)\), that at least one of the integer sums
\[ \sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}z_{ij'} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}z_{i'j} \]
is zero. These checks take polynomial time and are exactly the stability checks for \(z/Q\).

Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

5. Conclusion

Stable complete allocation existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete under the exact stability definition in the question. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
