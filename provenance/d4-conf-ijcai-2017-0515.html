The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix constant \(B\), a finite type set \(T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Let \(\mathcal G_B=\{g\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}:1\le\sum_t g_t\le B\}\). A type-\(t\) agent has unknown utility \(u_t(g)\) for any team profile \(g\) with \(g_t>0\). Define \(x_t(g)\) as the unique maximizer of \(u_t(x)\) over \(x\le g\) with \(x_t\ge1\); require the paper’s top-responsiveness comparison clauses on profiles, and require sampled utilities to reveal comparisons between choice profiles as in its informative-representation condition. Given samples \((h,(u_t(h))_{t:h_t>0})\) from an unknown distribution \(D\) over \(\mathcal G_B\), with team members sampled uniformly from their type populations, find an algorithm with sample and computation complexity polynomial in \(|T|\), \(1/\epsilon\), \(\log(1/\delta)\), and the input bit length that outputs rational frequencies \(q_g\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{g\in\mathcal G_B}g_tq_g=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\) over the samples, satisfies \(\mathbb E_{h\sim D}[\prod_{t:h_t>0}p_t(h;q)^{h_t}]<\epsilon\), where \(p_t(h;q)=\mu_t^{-1}\sum_{g:u_t(h)>u_t(g)}g_tq_g\).

The informative representation supplies known polynomial-time decoders that, from \(u_t(g),u_t(h)\), determine whether \(u_t(x_t(g))>u_t(x_t(h))\) and whether \(x_t(g)=x_t(h)\); they do not reveal the choice profiles themselves.

Partial resolution with a sharper sample bound

The computational classification remains unresolved for unrestricted fixed $B\ge3$. The following results are proved below:

  • For every fixed $B$, sample complexity

$$ O\!\left(\frac{\tau B\log(\tau+B)+\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon}\right) $$
suffices. This improves the bound in the notes.

  • For $B\le2$, the required computation is polynomial.
  • For arbitrary fixed $B$, exact empirical stabilization is polynomial-time computable when the observed strict preferences admit a common ordering of profiles.
  • A polynomial-time algorithm for a precisely defined finite, full-information approximation problem would yield the requested PAC algorithm. Thus exact empirical stabilization is not necessary.
  • The admissible utility class contains approximate stable fractional matching in fixed-uniformity hypergraphs, with polynomial reciprocal-accuracy dependence.

All uses of the notes are stated and justified here.

1. Feasibility, domination, and sparse allocations

Write $|g|=\sum_tg_t$, and let $e_t$ denote the singleton profile of type $t$. Stars and bars gives
$$ N:=|\mathcal G_B|=\binom{\tau+B}{B}-1. $$
In particular, $N$ is polynomial in $\tau$ when $B$ is fixed.

For a feasible allocation $q$, define
$$ a_t(h;q)= \frac1{\mu_t} \sum_{\substack{g:g_t>0\\u_t(g)\ge u_t(h)}}g_tq_g. $$
The mass equation for type $t$ implies
$$ p_t(h;q)=1-a_t(h;q). $$
Consequently, the blocking probability of $h$ is zero if and only if some participating type $t$ receives all its mass in profiles weakly preferred to $h$. Utility ties belong on the weakly preferred side because improvement is strict.

The existence theorem used

We use the following form of Scarf’s lemma. If $A$ is nonnegative with no zero column, $b$ is positive, and each row strictly orders its incident columns, then some extreme point of
$$ P=\{z\ge0:Az\le b\} $$
dominates every column. A column $h$ is dominated in row $t$ when $A_{th}>0$, $A_tz=b_t$, and every positively allocated column incident to $t$ ranks at least as highly as $h$. This is [Lemma 1 of Biró, Csáji, and Schlotter](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31.html).

Apply the theorem with
$$ A_{tg}=g_t,\qquad b_t=\mu_t, $$
and order profiles by utility, resolving ties arbitrarily. Every column is nonzero. The singleton column $e_t$ can be dominated only in row $t$, so domination of all singletons forces
$$ Aq=\mu. $$
A domination witness for $h$ receives mass only in profiles weakly preferred to $h$ under the original utilities: resolving ties never reverses a strict comparison. Hence every profile has zero blocking probability.

Proved conclusion. A zero-risk feasible allocation exists for every utility system, without requiring top responsiveness.

Sparsity and rational encoding length

The positively supported columns of the extreme point are linearly independent. Otherwise there is a nonzero vector $d$, supported on its positive coordinates, with $Ad=0$. For sufficiently small $\lambda>0$, both $q+\lambda d$ and $q-\lambda d$ are nonnegative and preserve every row total. This contradicts extremality.

Thus the support has at most $\tau$ profiles. If its size is $s$, choose $s$ rows giving a nonsingular square subsystem. The supported coordinates are uniquely determined by that subsystem. Its integer coefficients lie between $0$ and $B$; its right-hand side consists of input masses. Cramer’s rule therefore gives rational coordinates of polynomial bit length.

We will also use the following observation:

If a feasible allocation stabilizes a collection of profiles, every feasible allocation whose support is contained in its support stabilizes the same collection.

Indeed, for each stabilized profile, its witness type originally receives mass only in weakly preferred profiles. Restricting the support preserves that fact; feasibility restores the full mass of that type.

Consequently, a feasible empirical stabilizer can be made sparse in polynomial time. On its existing support $F$, find an extreme point of the nonempty rational polytope
$$ \{z\ge0:A_Fz=\mu\}. $$
This polytope is bounded, since every column is nonzero. Polynomial-time rational linear programming can return an extreme point, whose support has at most $\tau$ by the same independence argument.

2. Exact empirical stabilization without utility queries

Let $S$ be the distinct sampled profiles and put
$$ E=S\cup\{e_t:t\in T\}. $$
We allocate mass only to profiles in $E$.

Define auxiliary utilities $\widehat u$ as follows. Preserve every observed utility label. If $e_t$ was not observed but type $t$ appeared in a sample, assign its singleton a value one unit below its minimum observed utility. If type $t$ never appeared, assign its singleton any rational value.

These labels have polynomial bit length. They need not satisfy top responsiveness; Scarf’s lemma permits arbitrary row orders.

Apply Section 1 to the columns in $E$. It gives a feasible allocation supported on at most $\tau$ profiles that dominates every column of $E$ in the auxiliary preferences.

To transfer this statement to the actual utilities, fix an observed profile $h$ and an auxiliary domination witness $t$. An artificial singleton of type $t$ ranks strictly below $h$, so it cannot receive positive mass at this witness. Every other positively allocated profile incident to $t$ was observed and retains its actual label. Therefore
$$ q_g>0,\quad g_t>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_t(g)\ge u_t(h). $$
Feasibility then gives $p_t(h;q)=0$.

Proved conclusion. Every sample has zero actual blocking probability.

This also supplies a finite algorithm. Enumerate independent subsets $F\subseteq E$ of size at most $\tau$, solve
$$ A_Fq_F=\mu, $$
reject inconsistent or negative solutions, and check auxiliary domination of the observed profiles. The guaranteed sparse allocation occurs among the candidates. Each candidate requires polynomial work, but the number of candidates can be exponential in $\tau$.

3. A sharper polynomial sample bound

The improved bound uses sparsity, rather than the probability mass of all unseen profiles.

For a fixed subset $F\subseteq\mathcal G_B$, define
$$ C_F= \left\{ h: \text{for every }t\text{ with }h_t>0,\ \text{some }g\in F\text{ satisfies }g_t>0 \text{ and }u_t(h)>u_t(g) \right\}. $$
The utility system is fixed, so $C_F$ is fixed independently of the samples.

If $F$ is the positive support of a feasible $q$, then
$$ h\in C_F \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \prod_{t:h_t>0}p_t(h;q)^{h_t}>0. $$
This follows because each summand defining $p_t$ is nonnegative, and a summand indexed by an incident profile in the positive support is positive exactly when that profile is strictly worse than $h$. Thus
$$ R_D(q)\le D(C_F). $$

There are at most
$$ K_0:=\sum_{s=0}^{\tau}\binom Ns\le(N+1)^\tau $$
possible supports of size at most $\tau$. For the inequality, encode a subset as its increasing list of indices, padded with zeros to length $\tau$.

For any fixed $F$ with $D(C_F)\ge\epsilon$, the probability that every sample avoids $C_F$ is at most
$$ (1-\epsilon)^m\le e^{-m\epsilon}. $$
A union bound therefore gives
$$ \Pr\!\left[ \exists F,\ |F|\le\tau: D(C_F)\ge\epsilon \text{ and every sample avoids }C_F \right] \le (N+1)^\tau e^{-m\epsilon}. $$

The sparse allocation from Section 2 stabilizes every sample, so its support satisfies the avoidance condition. Hence it is enough to take
$$ m\ge \left\lceil \frac{\tau\ln(N+1)+\ln(1/\delta)+1}{\epsilon} \right\rceil. $$
The displayed failure bound is then at most $\delta/e$, which is strictly below $\delta$. Outside that failure event,
$$ R_D(q)\le D(C_F)<\epsilon. $$

Finally,
$$ N+1=\binom{\tau+B}{B}\le(\tau+B)^B. $$

Proved conclusion. For every fixed $B$, polynomially many samples suffice, with
$$ m= O\!\left( \frac{\tau B\log(\tau+B)+\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon} \right). $$
This is an information-theoretic bound with a finite exponential-time implementation. It does not establish polynomial computation for unrestricted $B\ge3$.

4. Polynomial-time cases

4.1. The cases $B=1$ and $B=2$

For $B=1$, the unique feasible allocation is
$$ q_{e_t}=\mu_t. $$
Every admissible profile has zero blocking probability, without samples.

Now let $B=2$, and use the auxiliary instance $E$ from Section 2. For each type $t$, choose a most-preferred homogeneous profile
$$ H_t\in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{g\in E:\operatorname{supp}(g)=\{t\}} \widehat u_t(g). $$
It is either $e_t$ or $2e_t$; write $k_t=|H_t|$.

Retain an observed mixed pair $h=e_s+e_t$ as a graph edge precisely when
$$ \widehat u_s(h)>\widehat u_s(H_s), \qquad \widehat u_t(h)>\widehat u_t(H_t). $$
Give vertex $t$ quota $\mu_t$ and edge $\{s,t\}$ capacity $\min(\mu_s,\mu_t)$. Order incident edges by auxiliary utility, resolving ties arbitrarily.

We use the nonbipartite fractional stable-allocation theorem: rational quotas and capacities admit a polynomial-time computable allocation $y$ such that every edge is either at capacity or has an endpoint whose quota is entirely assigned to edges ranked at least as highly as it. Unassigned mass is last-ranked. [Dean and Munshi, Section 5](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf) give such an algorithm; explicit rotation comparison gives a strongly polynomial implementation. Its arithmetic uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and division by two, so polynomially many operations preserve polynomial rational encoding length.

All hypotheses hold for the graph just constructed. Define
$$ r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{s:\{s,t\}\text{ retained}}y_{st}, $$
and output
$$ q_{e_s+e_t}=y_{st}, \qquad q_{H_t}=\frac{r_t}{k_t}, $$
with every other coordinate zero. Nonnegativity and the mass equalities follow directly from graph feasibility.

Every observed profile has an auxiliary domination witness:

  • Homogeneous profile. Its type weakly prefers $H_t$ to it, and every retained incident edge is strictly better than $H_t$.
  • Rejected mixed pair. Some endpoint weakly prefers its fallback to that pair; every assigned profile at that endpoint is at least as good as its fallback.
  • Retained mixed pair. If it is at capacity, a smaller-quota endpoint receives its entire mass in that pair. Otherwise, graph stability supplies an endpoint fully assigned to edges ranked at least as highly as the pair.

These cases cover all profiles when $B=2$. Section 2 transfers auxiliary domination to actual stabilization. If necessary, apply the support reduction from Section 1 before using the sharper sample bound.

The graph has at most $\tau$ vertices and $O(\tau^2)$ edges. Its construction, solution, and conversion to $q$ are polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

Proved conclusion. The task is polynomial-time solvable for $B\le2$.

4.2. A common-order condition for arbitrary $B$

This is an additional tractable empirical case.

On the auxiliary profile set $E$, construct a directed graph with an arc $g\to h$ whenever some type incident to both profiles satisfies
$$ \widehat u_t(g)>\widehat u_t(h). $$
Assume this directed graph is acyclic. A topological ordering then places every strict improvement before the profile it improves upon.

Process profiles in that order. Initially let $r_t=\mu_t$. When processing $g$, assign
$$ q_g=\min_{t:g_t>0}\frac{r_t}{g_t}, $$
and replace each incident residual by $r_t-g_tq_g$.

All residuals remain nonnegative. When $e_t$ is processed, it consumes all remaining mass of type $t$. Thus the final allocation is feasible.

Fix a processed profile $h$. If its allocation is zero, some incident type already has zero residual. If its allocation is positive, some incident type reaches zero residual during this step. Choose such a type $t$. Every positively allocated profile incident to $t$ was processed no later than $h$.

None of those earlier profiles can be strictly worse than $h$ for $t$: otherwise the arc from $h$ to that profile would force it to occur later. No later incident profile receives positive mass because $r_t=0$. Hence all mass of $t$ is assigned to profiles weakly preferred to $h$.

Every observed profile is therefore stabilized. Each positive allocation exhausts at least one previously unexhausted type, so the support has at most $\tau$ profiles.

Proved conclusion. If the auxiliary observed preferences admit this common ordering, empirical stabilization is polynomial-time computable for arbitrary fixed $B$.

The condition is efficiently checkable. It is a sufficient condition, not a consequence of the task’s axioms.

5. Approximate empirical stabilization is sufficient

The preceding algorithms stabilize samples exactly. The following conditional result shows that a polynomial approximation algorithm would also suffice.

5.1. The finite full-information problem

Consider this auxiliary problem:

  • A finite set $E\subseteq\mathcal G_B$ contains every singleton.
  • All incident utility labels on $E$ are known; they may induce arbitrary weak preferences.
  • A multiset $H_1,\ldots,H_m$ of profiles from $E$ is specified.
  • Given $\eta>0$, return a rational feasible allocation supported on $E$ whose average blocking probability on that multiset is at most $\eta$.

Feasibility uses the original mass equalities. Blocking probabilities use the supplied labels. Section 1 guarantees that a zero-risk solution exists.

Conditional claim. A solver for this problem running in time polynomial in its input length and $1/\eta$ would solve the original PAC task in the requested polynomial time.

The proof requires two ingredients.

5.2. Artificial singleton labels only increase empirical risk

Construct $E$ and $\widehat u$ from the samples as in Section 2. Let $q$ be any feasible allocation supported on $E$.

Fix an observed $h$ and a participating type $t$. Every assigned profile other than an artificial singleton has its actual observed label. An artificial singleton is strictly worse than $h$ in the auxiliary preferences. Its mass is therefore counted in the auxiliary $p_t(h;q)$, regardless of whether its unknown actual utility is worse than $h$.

Consequently,
$$ p_t^{\mathrm{actual}}(h;q) \le p_t^{\mathrm{auxiliary}}(h;q). $$
Taking products preserves this inequality. Thus the actual empirical average risk is at most the auxiliary empirical average risk.

This comparison holds for approximate allocations, not merely zero-risk allocations.

5.3. Uniform convergence over all feasible allocations

Fix the actual utility system. For every profile $g$, let
$$ c_g=\min_{t:g_t>0}\frac{\mu_t}{g_t}. $$
Every feasible allocation satisfies $0\le q_g\le c_g$, so write
$$ q_g=c_gz_g,\qquad z_g\in[0,1]. $$

For arbitrary $z\in[0,1]^N$, define
$$ P_t(h;z)= \min\!\left\{ 1,\ \sum_{\substack{g:g_t>0\\u_t(h)>u_t(g)}} \frac{g_tc_g}{\mu_t}z_g \right\}, $$
and
$$ F_z(h)=\prod_{t:h_t>0}P_t(h;z)^{h_t}. $$
For a feasible $q$, these equal its actual dissatisfaction fractions and blocking probabilities. Every coefficient in the sum is at most one.

If $\|z-z'\|_\infty\le\rho$, clipping is Lipschitz and therefore
$$ |P_t(h;z)-P_t(h;z')|\le N\rho. $$
View $F_z(h)$ as a product of $|h|$ factors, repeating the factor for type $t$ exactly $h_t$ times. A telescoping expansion of two products whose factors lie in $[0,1]$ gives
$$ |F_z(h)-F_{z'}(h)|\le |h|N\rho\le BN\rho. $$

Set
$$ K=\left\lceil\frac{8BN}{\epsilon}\right\rceil. $$
The coordinate grid with spacing $1/K$ has $(K+1)^N$ points, and every $z$ is within $1/K$ of a grid point.

For a fixed grid point, the sample values $F_z(H_i)$ are independent and lie in $[0,1]$. Hoeffding’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr\!\left[ \left| \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^mF_z(H_i) -\mathbb E_D[F_z(h)] \right|>\frac{\epsilon}{4} \right] \le2e^{-m\epsilon^2/8}. $$
A union bound over the grid shows that, if
$$ m\ge \left\lceil \frac8{\epsilon^2} \left( N\ln(K+1)+\ln(2/\delta)+1 \right) \right\rceil, $$
then with probability at least $1-\delta$, every grid point has error at most $\epsilon/4$.

For arbitrary $z$, transferring to its nearby grid point changes each of the empirical average and expectation by at most
$$ \frac{BN}{K}\le\frac{\epsilon}{8}. $$
Hence, on the same event, every feasible $q$ satisfies
$$ R_D(q)\le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \prod_{t:(H_i)_t>0}p_t(H_i;q)^{(H_i)_t} +\frac{\epsilon}{2}. $$

Invoke the hypothetical finite solver on the auxiliary labels with $\eta=\epsilon/4$. Section 5.2 bounds its actual empirical risk by $\epsilon/4$. Uniform convergence then gives
$$ R_D(q)\le\frac{3\epsilon}{4}<\epsilon. $$

For fixed $B$, the sample bound and all reduction work are polynomial in the required parameters. The grid is used only in the proof; the algorithm does not enumerate it.

Proved conclusion. A polynomial-time approximation solver for the finite full-information problem above would settle the original task positively. Such a solver has not been established here.

6. The hypergraph approximation obstacle

We now justify the embedded matching problem and its accuracy dependence.

6.1. Comparing blocking risk with domination deficit

For any feasible $q$, define
$$ d(h;q)=\min_{t:h_t>0}p_t(h;q). $$
Because all dissatisfaction fractions lie in $[0,1]$ and $1\le|h|\le B$,
$$ d(h;q)^B \le \prod_{t:h_t>0}p_t(h;q)^{h_t} \le d(h;q). $$
The first inequality follows by replacing every factor by the minimum and then using $d^{|h|}\ge d^B$. The second follows by retaining a minimum factor and bounding all other factors by one.

Thus
$$ \mathbb E_D[d(h;q)^B]\le R_D(q)\le\mathbb E_D[d(h;q)]. $$
In particular, under the uniform distribution on $M$ profiles,
$$ R_D(q)<\epsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d(h;q)<(M\epsilon)^{1/B} $$
for every profile in the distribution’s support.

6.2. An admissible embedding

Fix $B\ge2$. Let $(V,\mathcal E)$ be a $B$-uniform hypergraph with strict incident-edge preferences $\succ_t$. A fractional matching consists of nonnegative coordinates $x_e$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{e\ni t}x_e\le1. $$
Call it $\eta$-stable if every edge $e$ has a vertex $t\in e$ satisfying
$$ x_e+\sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\succ_t e}}x_f\ge1-\eta. $$

Pad $V$ with isolated types until its size $\tau$ is a power of two, and give every type mass $1/\tau$. Each edge corresponds to its incidence profile $g_e$.

For each type $t$, rank its incident profiles injectively within each cardinality layer, using ranks
$$ 1\le r_t(g)\le N. $$
In the layer of size $B$, rank all designated edge profiles above every undesignated profile and order the designated profiles according to $\succ_t$. Define
$$ u_t(g)=N|g|+r_t(g). $$

Every proper subprofile has smaller utility: even the largest rank at cardinality $k$ is below the smallest rank at cardinality $k+1$. Therefore
$$ x_t(g)=g $$
uniquely. The choice-value implication becomes the identical utility implication. Equal choices imply equal profiles, so the equal-choice strict-containment requirement has no applicable antecedent.

Labels are injective for each type. Numerical comparison and equality therefore provide the two required informative decoders. Their outputs are the required Boolean values. Label lengths are polynomial.

6.3. Extracting an approximate matching

Given any feasible society allocation $q$, put
$$ x_e=\tau q_{g_e}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_{e\ni t}x_e \le\tau\sum_g g_tq_g=1, $$
so $x$ is a fractional matching.

The profiles weakly preferred to $g_e$ by type $t\in e$ are exactly $g_e$ and the designated incident edges preferred to it. Hence
$$ p_t(g_e;q) = 1-x_e-\sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\succ_t e}}x_f. $$

Let $M=|\mathcal E|\ge1$, and let $J$ be the smallest power of two at least $M$. Sample uniformly from a list of $J$ designated profiles containing every edge at least once. Every edge then has probability at least $1/J$, and the distribution is exactly samplable with $\log_2J$ random bits.

If $R_D(q)<\epsilon$, nonnegativity of the risk contributions gives, for each edge $e$,
$$ \prod_{t\in e}p_t(g_e;q)<J\epsilon. $$
Thus some vertex satisfies
$$ p_t(g_e;q)<(J\epsilon)^{1/B}. $$
For dyadic $0<\eta<1$, choose
$$ \epsilon=\frac{\eta^B}{2J}. $$
The extracted matching is then $\eta$-stable. Moreover,
$$ \frac1\epsilon=\frac{2J}{\eta^B}, $$
which is polynomial in $M$ and $1/\eta$ for fixed $B$. All sampled labels can be computed from the hypergraph preferences.

Conversely, an $\eta$-stable fractional matching yields a feasible society allocation by assigning
$$ q_{g_e}=\frac{x_e}{\tau}, \qquad q_{e_t}=\frac{1-\sum_{e\ni t}x_e}{\tau}. $$
These profiles are distinct because $B\ge2$. The same dissatisfaction formula holds. Every designated edge has some dissatisfaction fraction at most $\eta$, so its blocking probability is at most $\eta$.

Proved conclusion. A polynomial-time PAC algorithm for fixed $B$ would give a randomized algorithm for $\eta$-stable fractional matching in $B$-uniform hypergraphs, with time polynomial in the instance size and $1/\eta$.

6.4. What existing hardness does not establish

The approximate fractional matching theorem cited in the notes uses tolerance
$$ \eta=2^{-20M^4} $$
in its exact-recovery argument. This is explicit in [Ishizuka and Kamiyama, Lemma 8](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol123-isaac2018/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11.pdf).

Its reciprocal is exponential. Consequently, that argument does not exclude an algorithm polynomial in $1/\eta$, and it does not establish the requested $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

7. Remaining question

The sample requirement is polynomial for every fixed $B$, and computation is polynomial for $B\le2$. The new common-order algorithm settles another nontrivial case for arbitrary $B$. The full-information reduction shows that an approximation algorithm, rather than an exact Scarf algorithm, would suffice.

For unrestricted fixed $B\ge3$, neither the required polynomial-time approximation algorithm nor a hardness reduction with compatible reciprocal accuracy has been proved here.
