The anchor (named result in the paper)

Proposition 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Let \(\mathcal C_n\) be the polytope of Choquet capacities and \(T=\operatorname{ext}(\mathcal C_n)\) its extreme capacities, treated as voter types. Given rational alternatives \(X\subseteq[0,1]^n\) and aggregate elicitation statements \(P\) that constrain \(\bar v_\mu(A)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t v^t(A)\), where \(\mu\) is an unknown society distribution over \(T\), choose \(x\in X\) minimizing \(\max_{\mu\text{ consistent with }P}\max_{y\in X}\bigl(C_{\bar v_\mu}(y)-C_{\bar v_\mu}(x)\bigr)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(N=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), where \(m=n\geq1\). Let \(\mathcal C_m\) consist of capacities \(v:2^N\to[0,1]\) satisfying \(v(\varnothing)=0\), \(v(N)=1\), and \(v(A)\leq v(B)\) whenever \(A\subseteq B\); types are \(T=\operatorname{ext}(\mathcal C_m)\). Write \(C_v\) for the standard Choquet integral.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\), and \(\bar v_\mu(A)=\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t v^t(A)\).
Here the society is unknown: neither its support nor its masses are supplied or bounded; admissible societies range over all rational distributions on \(T\).
The input is a nonempty explicit set \(X\subseteq[0,1]^m\) of rational alternatives, a finite list \(P\) of weak bounds \(\bar v_\mu(A)\geq\lambda\) or \(\bar v_\mu(A)\leq\lambda\), and a rational threshold \(r\in[0,1]\). Each coalition is encoded by its incidence vector and each \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) is rational; promise that some society satisfies \(P\).
A lower bound on \(A\) also applies to every superset, and an upper bound applies to every subset. Include the fixed endpoint values in the resulting intervals \([l_A,u_A]\).
Define \(R(X,P)=\min_{x\in X}\max_{\mu\models P}\max_{y\in X}\bigl(C_{\bar v_\mu}(y)-C_{\bar v_\mu}(x)\bigr)\); any minimizing alternative may be chosen.

Is deciding whether \(R(X,P)\leq r\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
State running times in \(m\), \(k=|X|\), \(p=|P|\), and total input bit length \(L\); any dependence on an explicitly represented society uses its actual support size \(\tau\), while the decision input contains no society.

For a single unknown capacity, any partial assignment satisfying the propagated intervals and inclusion monotonicity extends to a compatible capacity; the associated finite-alternative minimax-regret problem has a polynomial-size linear programming formulation. [Capacity elicitation results](https://doi.org/10.3233/978-1-61499-419-0-87).
The [extended treatment](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-01480147v3/document) also establishes this extension property. No result specifically treating the unknown society distribution above was verified.

  • Restricted the undefined aggregate elicitation language to the anchor's weak coalition-value upper and lower bounds, with rational thresholds in the unit interval and their monotonicity implications.
  • Normalized society mass to one and allowed any rational distribution over extreme capacities, without fixing or bounding its unknown support.
  • Distinguished the support encoding of a specified society from this problem's input, which supplies only aggregate information about an unknown society.
  • Required a nonempty explicit alternative set and consistent elicitation statements; retained normalized capacity endpoint values.
  • Introduced a rational regret threshold to formulate the exact optimization question as a decision problem; permitted arbitrary choice among tied minimizers.
  • Used m for the number of criteria, k for the number of alternatives, and L for the total encoded decision-input length.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). The key point is that unrestricted rational distributions on the extreme capacities produce exactly all rational capacities. The unknown society therefore introduces no additional restriction on its mean capacity.

Below is a self-contained proof, including the finite LP construction and its running time.

1. Rational capacities are exactly the means of rational societies

Call a capacity Boolean if all its values belong to \(\{0,1\}\).

Lemma 1. The extreme points of \(\mathcal C_m\) are exactly the Boolean capacities. Every rational capacity is the mean of a rational distribution on these extreme points.

Proof. First, let \(w\) be a Boolean capacity and suppose

\(w=\theta u+(1-\theta)v\),

where \(u,v\in\mathcal C_m\) and \(0<\theta<1\). At any coalition \(A\), both \(u(A)\) and \(v(A)\) lie in \([0,1]\). If \(w(A)=0\), the displayed equality forces \(u(A)=v(A)=0\). If \(w(A)=1\), it forces \(u(A)=v(A)=1\). Thus \(u=v=w\), proving that \(w\) is extreme.

Now take any capacity \(v\). List its distinct positive values as

\(0=a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_s=1\).

For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}\), define

\(w^j(A)=\mathbf 1\{v(A)\geq a_j\}\).

Each \(w^j\) is a Boolean capacity: monotonicity follows from that of \(v\), and its endpoint values are \(0\) and \(1\). Moreover,

\(v=\sum_{j=1}^s(a_j-a_{j-1})w^j\).

Indeed, if \(v(A)=a_h\), the right-hand side at \(A\) is \(\sum_{j=1}^h(a_j-a_{j-1})=a_h\); if \(v(A)=0\), it is zero. The weights are positive and sum to \(1\).

If \(v\) is not Boolean, then \(s\geq2\). At a coalition satisfying \(v(A)=a_1\), we have \(w^1(A)=1\) and \(w^j(A)=0\) for every \(j\geq2\). Consequently, the decomposition expresses \(v\) as a nontrivial convex combination of \(w^1\) and another capacity distinct from \(w^1\). Hence \(v\) is not extreme.

Finally, if \(v\) is rational, all the weights \(a_j-a_{j-1}\) are rational. Conversely, a rational distribution on Boolean capacities has rational mean values and its mean is a capacity. This proves both assertions. \(\square\)

Let \(\mathcal F_{\mathbb Q}(P)\) denote the rational capacities satisfying \(P\). Lemma 1 gives the exact equality

\(\{\bar v_\mu:\mu\text{ is an admissible rational society satisfying }P\}=\mathcal F_{\mathbb Q}(P)\).

This equality uses the absence of restrictions on society support or mass denominators.

2. A finite set of coalition values suffices

For an alternative \(x\), choose a permutation \(\pi_x\) such that

\(x_{\pi_x(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{\pi_x(m)}\),

and put \(x_{\pi_x(0)}=0\). Define the tail coalitions

\(A_i^x=\{\pi_x(i),\ldots,\pi_x(m)\}\).

The standard Choquet formula on nonnegative alternatives is

\(C_v(x)=\sum_{i=1}^m\bigl(x_{\pi_x(i)}-x_{\pi_x(i-1)}\bigr)v(A_i^x)\).

Ties cause no difficulty because the corresponding increments are zero.

Fix an ordered pair \((x,y)\in X^2\). Let \(\mathcal S_{xy}\) contain:

  • \(\varnothing\) and \(N\);
  • every coalition occurring in a bound in \(P\);
  • every \(A_i^x\) and \(A_i^y\).

Write \(q_{xy}=|\mathcal S_{xy}|\). Then

\(q_{xy}\leq Q:=2m+p+2\).

Only values on this set are needed to evaluate \(C_v(y)-C_v(x)\).

For completeness, the propagated intervals can be calculated without enumerating all coalitions. Augment the lower-bound list by \((\varnothing,0)\) and \((N,1)\), and augment the upper-bound list by the same endpoint bounds. Then, for any queried coalition \(A\),

\(l_A=\max\{\lambda:(B,\lambda)\text{ is an augmented lower bound and }B\subseteq A\}\),

\(u_A=\min\{\lambda:(B,\lambda)\text{ is an augmented upper bound and }A\subseteq B\}\).

The augmented lists make both extrema well-defined. Every compatible capacity satisfies these intervals by monotonicity.

3. Exact extension of finite assignments

Consider variables \(z_A\), indexed by \(A\in\mathcal S_{xy}\), with constraints

\(l_A\leq z_A\leq u_A\quad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal S_{xy}\),

\(z_A\leq z_B\quad\text{whenever }A,B\in\mathcal S_{xy}\text{ and }A\subseteq B\).

Denote the resulting polytope by \(H_{xy}\).

Lemma 2. Every point of \(H_{xy}\) extends to a capacity satisfying \(P\). Every rational point extends to a rational capacity satisfying \(P\). Conversely, restricting any capacity satisfying \(P\) to \(\mathcal S_{xy}\) gives a point of \(H_{xy}\).

Proof. The converse follows directly from monotonicity and propagated bounds.

For the extension, given \(z\in H_{xy}\), define on every coalition \(D\subseteq N\)

\(\widehat v(D)=\max\{z_A:A\in\mathcal S_{xy},\ A\subseteq D\}\).

The set in this maximum is nonempty because \(\varnothing\in\mathcal S_{xy}\).

If \(D\in\mathcal S_{xy}\), inclusion monotonicity of the assigned values gives \(z_A\leq z_D\) for every indexed \(A\subseteq D\). Since \(D\) itself is eligible in the maximum, \(\widehat v(D)=z_D\). Thus the extension agrees with every assigned value.

If \(D\subseteq E\), the eligible coalitions for \(D\) form a subset of those for \(E\), so \(\widehat v(D)\leq\widehat v(E)\). Every assigned value lies in \([0,1]\), and the endpoint intervals force \(z_\varnothing=0\) and \(z_N=1\). Therefore \(\widehat v\in\mathcal C_m\).

Every coalition mentioned in \(P\) belongs to \(\mathcal S_{xy}\). Its assigned interval includes its original bound, so the extension satisfies \(P\). If all assigned values are rational, every extension value, being a maximum of finitely many such values, is rational. \(\square\)

Thus the finite constraints are sufficient; no omitted coalition introduces a further restriction.

4. Each pairwise worst regret is a rational LP optimum

For the ordered pair \((x,y)\), solve the LP with feasible polytope \(H_{xy}\) and objective

\(\max_{z\in H_{xy}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^m\bigl(y_{\pi_y(i)}-y_{\pi_y(i-1)}\bigr)z_{A_i^y}-\sum_{i=1}^m\bigl(x_{\pi_x(i)}-x_{\pi_x(i-1)}\bigr)z_{A_i^x}\right]\).

Call its optimal value \(D_{xy}\).

Lemma 3. This LP optimum is attained by an admissible rational society, and

\(D_{xy}=\max_{\mu\models P}\bigl(C_{\bar v_\mu}(y)-C_{\bar v_\mu}(x)\bigr)\).

Proof. By the feasibility promise, some compatible society exists. Its mean restricts to a feasible point of \(H_{xy}\), so this polytope is nonempty. It is closed and contained in \([0,1]^{q_{xy}}\), hence compact.

Every admissible society supplies a feasible point with the same objective value, so its pairwise regret is at most \(D_{xy}\).

The LP has rational constraints and a rational objective. A linear objective on a nonempty compact polytope attains its maximum at a vertex. Every vertex of a rational polytope is rational: it is the unique solution of a full-rank subsystem of tight rational constraints, and rational Gaussian elimination gives rational coordinates.

Choose such a rational optimal vertex \(z^*\). Lemma 2 extends it to a rational compatible capacity \(\widehat v\), and Lemma 1 represents \(\widehat v\) as the mean of an admissible rational society. The Choquet formulas depend only on the assigned values, so this society attains \(D_{xy}\). \(\square\)

In particular, restricting societies to rational distributions does not merely preserve a supremum: the pairwise maxima are attained.

The construction also gives a support bound for an attaining society. The extension in Lemma 2 takes values only among the assigned \(z_A^*\). Since \(z_\varnothing^*=0\), it has at most \(q_{xy}-1\) distinct positive values. The threshold decomposition in Lemma 1 therefore uses at most \(q_{xy}-1\) types. This is an existence result; the algorithm does not need to represent those types.

5. Computing minimax regret

For a fixed \(x\), finiteness of \(X\) and Lemma 3 imply

\(\max_{\mu\models P}\max_{y\in X}\bigl(C_{\bar v_\mu}(y)-C_{\bar v_\mu}(x)\bigr)=\max_{y\in X}D_{xy}\).

To check both inequalities explicitly, every pairwise regret under every society is bounded by the corresponding \(D_{xy}\), giving the upper bound. For the lower bound, choose \(y\) attaining \(\max_yD_{xy}\) and a society attaining that pair’s optimum. Under that society, the maximum over all alternatives is at least \(D_{xy}\).

Consequently,

\(R(X,P)=\min_{x\in X}\max_{y\in X}D_{xy}\).

The algorithm solves the \(k^2\) pairwise LPs, computes each row maximum and then their minimum, and compares the resulting rational number with \(r\). Any alternative attaining the minimum is a valid minimax-regret choice.

6. Running time

The following bounds use bit complexity, with \(L\) the total input bit length and \(Q=2m+p+2\).

Sorting all alternatives requires \(O(km\log(m+1))\) rational comparisons. Each pairwise LP has at most \(Q\) variables and \(O(Q^2)\) constraints.

For each pair:

  • Constructing and deduplicating the coalition set, and testing the inclusions needed for the LP and interval propagation, takes \(O(mQ^2)\) bit operations on incidence vectors.
  • Calculating the propagated interval endpoints requires \(O(pQ)\) rational comparisons.
  • Every numerical coefficient has bit length \(O(L)\): bounds are input rationals or endpoint values, and objective coefficients combine only a constant number of coordinate differences.
  • A dense encoding of the LP has length \(O(Q^3+QL)\).

Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore solves each LP exactly in time polynomial in \(Q\) and \(L\). The optimum values have polynomial bit length, so the final comparisons also take polynomial time. Altogether, the running time is

\(O\!\left(km\log(m+1)\operatorname{poly}(L)+k^2\operatorname{poly}(m+p,L)\right)\).

There is no dependence on \(\tau\), because the decision input contains no represented society and the algorithm never enumerates types or society supports.

Thus the requested decision problem is polynomial-time solvable.
