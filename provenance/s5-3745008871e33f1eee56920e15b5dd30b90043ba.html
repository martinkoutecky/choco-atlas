The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses μ over a finite set \(T\), type valuations \(v_t\), demands \(k_t\in\{1,2,3\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q\) satisfying \(\sum_j q_j=\sum_t\mu_t k_t\), does there exist a causal price rule \(p(r,z)\) that can be updated as type mass arrives and supply is consumed, such that every measurable arrival order and every utility-maximizing bundle choice yields total welfare equal to the optimum for the initial population and supplies? Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle of product categories.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) product categories, binary-rational values \(v_{tj}\geq0\), demands \(k_t\in\{1,2,3\}\), and positive binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) satisfying \(\sum_jq_j=\sum_t\mu_tk_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each individual receives a subset \(B\subseteq[m]\) with \(|B|\leq k_t\), including the empty subset, worth \(v_t(B)=\sum_{j\in B}v_{tj}\); repeated categories within a bundle are forbidden. Type mass may be divided measurably among different integral bundles. An offline allocation respects the type masses and uses at most \(q_j\) mass of category \(j\); its welfare is the integral of bundle values. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the maximum such welfare.
Promise that offline allocations exist and every welfare-maximizing allocation exhausts every supply, gives almost every type-\(t\) individual exactly \(k_t\) categories, and assigns only categories with strictly positive value to their recipients.
An arrival order is a measurable map \(a:[0,1]\to T\) with \(\lambda(a^{-1}(t))=\mu_t\), where \(\lambda\) is Lebesgue measure. At time \(s\), remaining type masses are \(r_t(s)=\mu_t-\int_0^s\mathbf{1}_{\{a(u)=t\}}\,du\), and remaining supplies are \(z(s)\).
A price rule is a Borel-measurable map \(p(r,z)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^m\), fixed from the initial instance and depending only on the current remaining masses and supplies. It posts prices before observing the arriving type. A type \(t\) chooses any bundle maximizing \(\sum_{j\in B}(v_{tj}-p_j(r,z))\) over \(B\subseteq\{j:z_j>0\}\) with \(|B|\leq k_t\); ties are arbitrary.
For every arrival order and every measurable demand-maximizing tie-breaking rule depending on time, arriving type and current state, require a nonnegative absolutely continuous supply trajectory on \([0,1]\), starting at \(z(0)=q\), with \(\dot z_j(s)=-\mathbf{1}_{\{j\in B(s)\}}\) almost everywhere. Call the price rule robustly optimal if every such trajectory has \(\int_0^1v_{a(s)}(B(s))\,ds=\mathrm{OPT}\).

Is deciding whether a robustly optimal price rule exists solvable in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite markets with individual demands at most \(3\) and the corresponding full-allocation assumptions, optimal dynamic prices always exist and have a constructive algorithm [Pashkovich–Xie](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.12869v1). A later treatment retains the full-allocation condition [Kalichman](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/items/a0c700d6-302b-4076-83a0-92083aa2c5d7).
No result settling this exact continuous policy-existence problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, consistent with a distribution over types and per-capita supplies.
  • Specified binary-rational nonnegative category values and positive supplies; categories with zero supply are omitted.
  • Interpreted integral bundles as subsets of categories, with no repeated category, and allowed bundles smaller than demand, including the empty bundle. Under positive prices, restricting to bundles of size at most demand preserves the multi-demand purchase model.
  • Defined offline allocations by measurable division of type mass among integral bundles, with supply inequalities, and carried over the full-allocation and positive-value assumptions.
  • Defined arrival orders using Lebesgue mass on the unit interval, remaining type masses by cumulative arrivals, and supply consumption by absolutely continuous trajectories.
  • Specified a common Borel-measurable, strictly positive state-based price rule posted before the arriving type is observed; depleted categories are unavailable and utility ties remain arbitrary.
  • Required a full feasible trajectory for every measurable tie-breaking rule, and optimality of every resulting trajectory, to exclude vacuous guarantees caused by nonexistent continuous evolutions.

Theorem. If every type has demand $k_t=1$, then every instance satisfying the stated promise admits a robustly optimal price rule. Such a rule has a description constructible in $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time.

The proof establishes both required properties: a supply trajectory exists for every measurable arrival order and admissible tie-breaking rule, and every such trajectory is optimal.

1. Offline allocations and strictly separating prices

Throughout the proof, assume $k_t=1$ for every $t$.

Let $x_{tj}$ be the mass of type $t$ assigned category $j$. The offline problem is exactly the linear program
$$ \max \sum_{t,j}v_{tj}x_{tj} $$
subject to
$$ x_{tj}\geq0,\qquad \sum_jx_{tj}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_tx_{tj}\leq q_j. $$

Indeed, every allocation gives such a matrix. Conversely, a feasible matrix is realized by assigning mass $x_{tj}$ of type $t$ the singleton bundle $\{j\}$ and assigning its remaining mass the empty bundle. Atomlessness permits these partitions.

Write $W=\mathrm{OPT}$ and let $F$ be the optimal face of this linear program. The promise implies, for every $x\in F$,
$$ \sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_tx_{tj}=q_j. $$

Define the optimal-support graph
$$ E=\{(t,j):x_{tj}>0\text{ for some }x\in F\}. $$
Averaging finitely many optimal matrices produces an optimal matrix $x^\circ$ positive on every edge of $E$ and zero elsewhere.

We first prove a strict separation statement that does not require uniqueness.

Lemma 1. There are numbers $u_t>0$ and $p_j^0>0$ satisfying
$$ v_{tj}-p_j^0=u_t\quad\text{if }(t,j)\in E, $$
and
$$ v_{tj}-p_j^0<u_t\quad\text{if }(t,j)\notin E. $$
Moreover,
$$ W=\sum_t\mu_tu_t+\sum_jq_jp_j^0. $$

Proof. The dual linear program is
$$ \min \sum_t\mu_tu_t+\sum_jq_jp_j $$
subject to
$$ u_t,p_j\geq0,\qquad u_t+p_j\geq v_{tj}. $$

Put
$$ \eta_{tj}=2-\mathbf{1}_{\{(t,j)\notin E\}}. $$
For every $x\in F$, full allocation and vanishing entries outside $E$ give
$$ \sum_{t,j}\eta_{tj}x_{tj}=2. $$

Consider perturbed coefficients
$$ v'_{tj}=v_{tj}-\varepsilon\eta_{tj}. $$
For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, their optimal face is still exactly $F$. To justify this, the primal polytope has finitely many vertices. All its optimal vertices have the same perturbed objective $W-2\varepsilon$, while every other vertex has a strictly smaller original objective. Choosing $\varepsilon$ sufficiently small preserves all these finitely many strict inequalities.

The perturbed primal is feasible and bounded, and its dual is feasible by taking sufficiently large nonnegative row multipliers. Linear-programming strong duality therefore supplies an optimal dual $(u',p')$. Define
$$ \rho'_{tj}=u'_t+p'_j-v'_{tj}\geq0. $$
Complementary slackness with $x^\circ$ gives $\rho'_{tj}=0$ on $E$.

Set
$$ u_t=u'_t+\varepsilon,\qquad p_j^0=p'_j+\varepsilon. $$
Then
$$ u_t+p_j^0-v_{tj} = \rho'_{tj}+\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{\{(t,j)\notin E\}}. $$
Thus the dual inequalities are equalities on $E$ and strict elsewhere, and all $u_t,p_j^0$ are positive. Complementary slackness with the fully allocated matrix $x^\circ$ proves dual optimality and the displayed formula for $W$. $\square$

Fix such a dual solution. Let $\Delta$ be the minimum of the positive numbers
$$ u_t,\qquad p_j^0,\qquad u_t+p_j^0-v_{tj}\quad ((t,j)\notin E), $$
omitting the last collection if it is empty. Then $\Delta>0$.

Any prices of the form
$$ p_j=p_j^0+\alpha h_j,\qquad \alpha=\frac{\Delta}{4(m+1)},\qquad h_j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}, $$
have the following property: whenever a type has an available neighbor in $E$, it strictly prefers every available neighbor in $E$ to every available category outside $E$. Its utility from each available neighbor in $E$ is positive.

Indeed, utilities on $E$ exceed $u_t-\Delta/4$, whereas utilities outside $E$ are at most $u_t-\Delta$.

2. Maximum-flow supports at arbitrary states

Consider any nonnegative state $(r,z)$. A flow on $E$ is a matrix satisfying
$$ x_{tj}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{j:(t,j)\in E}x_{tj}\leq r_t,\qquad \sum_{t:(t,j)\in E}x_{tj}\leq z_j. $$
Let $M(r,z)$ be its maximum total mass. Define
$$ G(r,z)=\{(t,j)\in E:x_{tj}>0 \text{ in some maximum flow at }(r,z)\}. $$

Only types with $r_t>0$ and categories with $z_j>0$ can be incident to $G(r,z)$.

Lemma 2. If $r_t>0$ and type $t$ has an available neighbor in $E$, then $t$ has a neighbor in $G(r,z)$.

Proof. Take a maximum flow $x$. If its type-$t$ row has positive mass, the assertion holds.

Otherwise, choose an $E$-neighbor $j$ with $z_j>0$. Category $j$ must be saturated: if not, a small positive flow on $(t,j)$ would increase total flow. Consequently, $x_{hj}>0$ for some other type $h$. Decreasing $x_{hj}$ and increasing $x_{tj}$ by the same sufficiently small positive amount preserves total flow and feasibility. The resulting maximum flow is positive on $(t,j)$. $\square$

We next construct one category ordering that simultaneously makes every type with an available $E$-neighbor choose an edge of $G(r,z)$.

On the active vertices, form a directed graph with:

  • an arc $t\to j$ for every active edge $(t,j)\in E$;
  • an arc $j\to t$ for every edge $(t,j)\in G(r,z)$.

Lemma 3. An edge of $E\setminus G(r,z)$ cannot have both endpoints in the same strongly connected component of this directed graph.

Proof. Average finitely many maximum flows to obtain a maximum flow $\bar x$ positive on every edge of $G(r,z)$.

If an unsupported edge had endpoints in the same strongly connected component, its forward arc would belong to a directed simple cycle. A cycle containing an unsupported edge has length at least four and alternates between type and category vertices.

Increase flow along its forward arcs and decrease flow along its reverse arcs by a common sufficiently small positive amount. Every decreased edge has positive flow in $\bar x$. Every row and column total remains unchanged, so feasibility and maximum total flow are preserved. The unsupported edge becomes positive, contradicting its definition. $\square$

Topologically order the strongly connected components, using a fixed deterministic procedure. List their category vertices in that order, sorting categories within each component by index. Append inactive categories by index. Let $h_j$ be category $j$’s rank in this list, starting at zero.

Every supported neighbor of a type lies in its own component. By Lemma 3, every unsupported active neighbor lies in a strictly later component. Therefore, the available $E$-neighbor with smallest rank belongs to $G(r,z)$.

Define the price rule by
$$ p_j(r,z)=p_j^0+\alpha h_j(r,z). $$

Combining this ordering with the separation established in Section 1 proves:

Demand property. At every nonnegative state, every active type with an available $E$-neighbor has a unique demanded bundle. It is a singleton whose edge belongs to $G(r,z)$.

If a type has no available $E$-neighbor, this assertion imposes no restriction on its demand. The proof below explicitly handles that case.

Outside the nonnegative state domain, extend the rule by $p^0$.

3. A Hall-deficiency invariant

For $S\subseteq T$, write
$$ N(S)=\{j:(t,j)\in E\text{ for some }t\in S\}. $$
Define
$$ d_S(r,z)=\sum_{t\in S}r_t-\sum_{j\in N(S)}z_j, \qquad D(r,z)=\max_{S\subseteq T}d_S(r,z). $$
The empty set gives $D(r,z)\geq0$.

We need two maximum-flow facts, including their justification.

Lemma 4. At every nonnegative state,
$$ M(r,z)=\sum_tr_t-D(r,z). $$
If $S$ attains the maximum defining $D(r,z)$, then every maximum flow satisfies
$$ x_{tj}=0 \quad\text{whenever }t\notin S,\ j\in N(S). $$

Proof. Use the finite network with source-to-type capacities $r_t$, category-to-sink capacities $z_j$, and capacity
$$ H=1+\sum_tr_t+\sum_jz_j $$
on each type-to-category edge of $E$.

A maximum flow exists because its feasible set is nonempty and compact. It has no source-to-sink augmenting path in its residual network, since augmentation by the minimum positive residual capacity on such a path would improve it.

The vertices reachable from the source consequently determine a cut whose capacity equals the maximum-flow value: every forward edge across this cut is saturated, and every backward edge has zero flow. This also proves the usual maximum-flow/minimum-cut equality in this network.

A minimum cut cannot cross a type-to-category edge, since $H>\sum_tr_t$, while cutting all source-to-type edges costs $\sum_tr_t$. Thus, if its source-side types are $S$, its source-side categories contain $N(S)$. Removing additional source-side categories cannot increase cut capacity. Hence the minimum cut value is
$$ \min_{S\subseteq T} \left(\sum_{t\notin S}r_t+\sum_{j\in N(S)}z_j\right) = \sum_tr_t-D(r,z). $$

If $S$ maximizes the deficiency, the cut with source-side vertices consisting of the source, $S$, and $N(S)$ is a minimum cut. Every maximum flow has zero flow on edges directed backward across this cut. These include precisely the edges described in the assertion. $\square$

Now fix an arbitrary measurable arrival order and any nonnegative absolutely continuous trajectory satisfying the demand equations for the constructed prices. Existence will be proved separately.

Almost every arriving type has positive remaining mass. Indeed, $r_t$ is nonnegative and nonincreasing, and its derivative vanishes almost everywhere on the set where $r_t=0$. Since
$$ \dot r_t=-\mathbf{1}_{\{a=t\}} $$
almost everywhere, arrivals of type $t$ on that set have measure zero.

For almost every time, let $t=a(s)$ and let $S$ maximize the current deficiency.

If $t$ has an available $E$-neighbor, the demand property forces a singleton $\{j\}$ with $(t,j)\in G(r,z)$. If $t\in S$, then $j\in N(S)$. If $t\notin S$, Lemma 4 gives $j\notin N(S)$. In either case,
$$ \frac{d}{ds}d_S(r(s),z(s))=0. $$

If $t$ has no available $E$-neighbor, all categories in $N(\{t\})$ have zero remaining supply. Every maximizing set $S$ must then contain $t$: otherwise,
$$ d_{S\cup\{t\}}(r,z)=d_S(r,z)+r_t>d_S(r,z). $$
For such an $S$, any chosen bundle, including the empty bundle, gives
$$ \frac{d}{ds}d_S(r(s),z(s)) = -1+\mathbf{1}_{\{B(s)\cap N(S)\neq\varnothing\}} \leq0. $$

Each $d_S(r(s),z(s))$ is absolutely continuous, and there are finitely many sets $S$. Their maximum is therefore absolutely continuous. At almost every time when all these functions and their maximum are differentiable, the derivative of the maximum is the maximum of the derivatives of its maximizing branches. This follows directly by expanding each branch at that time; branches with a strict value gap cannot maximize sufficiently near it.

Consequently,
$$ \frac{d}{ds}D(r(s),z(s))\leq0 $$
almost everywhere.

Initially, an optimal full flow on $E$ exists, so $D(\mu,q)=0$. Since $D\geq0$, we conclude
$$ D(r(s),z(s))=0 \qquad\text{for every }s\in[0,1]. $$

This is the required invariant.

4. Every trajectory is optimal

The invariant implies that every active type has an available $E$-neighbor. Otherwise, its singleton deficiency would equal its positive remaining mass.

Thus almost every arriving individual uniquely purchases one category on an edge of $E$. Since unit demand gives
$$ \sum_jq_j=\sum_t\mu_t=1, $$
we have
$$ \frac{d}{ds}\sum_jz_j(s)=-1 $$
almost everywhere. Therefore $\sum_jz_j(1)=0$, and nonnegativity implies that every supply is exhausted.

On every purchased edge, Lemma 1 gives $v_{tj}=u_t+p_j^0$. Hence the trajectory’s welfare is
$$ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1v_{a(s)}(B(s))\,ds &= \sum_t\mu_tu_t+ \sum_jp_j^0\int_0^1\mathbf{1}_{\{j\in B(s)\}}\,ds\\ &= \sum_t\mu_tu_t+\sum_jp_j^0q_j\\ &=W. \end{aligned} $$

This proves optimality of every trajectory, conditional on its existence.

5. Trajectories exist for every arrival order and tie-breaking rule

Fix any measurable arrival order. Call a residual state fully feasible if it admits a flow on $E$ with row totals exactly $r_t$ and column totals exactly $z_j$.

We construct a trajectory in finitely many phases, beginning at the fully feasible state $(\mu,q)$.

Suppose a phase begins at time $\sigma$ in a fully feasible state. Let
$$ G_\sigma=G(r(\sigma),z(\sigma)). $$
Full feasibility implies that every maximum flow at this state is full. Average maximum flows to obtain $\bar x$ positive on every edge of $G_\sigma$.

The policy assigns each active type a unique category $f_t$ on an edge of $G_\sigma$. With this assignment held fixed, define candidate supplies by
$$ \widehat z_j(s) = z_j(\sigma) - \sum_{t:f_t=j} \int_\sigma^s\mathbf{1}_{\{a(u)=t\}}\,du. $$
Types inactive at $\sigma$ have no subsequent arrivals except on a null set and may be omitted.

There is a positive initial interval on which this candidate state is fully feasible and has the same support $G_\sigma$. To see this, subtract each type’s consumed mass from $\bar x_{t f_t}$. Because all these entries are positive and each consumed mass is at most $s-\sigma$, sufficiently small elapsed time leaves every edge of $G_\sigma$ positive. The resulting flow has exactly the candidate residual row and column totals.

Furthermore, the residual support cannot gain edges. If a residual full flow used an edge outside $G_\sigma$, adding the already consumed allocation would yield a full flow at time $\sigma$ positive on that edge, contradicting its absence from $G_\sigma$.

Continue this phase while full feasibility and the active vertices and support remain unchanged. The policy’s prices are constant throughout that interval, because their construction depends only on those finite graph data. Its unique demands are therefore exactly the prescribed $f_t$.

Let $b$ be the supremum of the phase interval. The state at $b$ is fully feasible: candidate supplies and masses are continuous, and feasible full flows have convergent subsequences in a bounded finite-dimensional set. Their limits realize the limiting row and column totals.

The support at $b$ is a subset of $G_\sigma$, by the same argument of adding past consumption to a residual full flow. If $b<1$ and neither the support nor active vertices changed, the positive-flow argument above, restarted at $b$, would extend the phase. Thus a phase ending before time $1$ loses a support edge or an active vertex.

Such losses are permanent. An exhausted vertex cannot reactivate because masses and supplies are nonincreasing. An absent support edge cannot reappear because a later residual flow using it, combined with intervening consumption on $E$, would give a flow using it at the earlier state.

There are only $|E|+\tau+m$ objects that can be lost. Repeating the construction therefore reaches time $1$ after finitely many phases.

Concatenating these phase paths gives a nonnegative absolutely continuous supply trajectory. It satisfies the demand equations almost everywhere. Demands are unique throughout the interiors of the phases; their finitely many endpoints do not affect the differential equations. Consequently, the same constructed trajectory works for every admissible measurable tie-breaking rule.

Together with Section 4, this proves robust optimality.

6. Borel measurability and polynomial construction

The remaining requirements concern the rule’s representation.

Initial preprocessing. The offline linear program has $\tau m$ variables. Compute $W$, then maximize each coordinate over its optimal face to determine $E$. This takes polynomially many rational linear programs.

A strict baseline dual can be obtained by maximizing $0\leq\delta\leq1$ subject to dual optimality and
$$ u_t\geq\delta,\qquad p_j^0\geq\delta, $$
$$ u_t+p_j^0-v_{tj}\geq\delta\quad ((t,j)\notin E), $$
$$ u_t+p_j^0=v_{tj}\quad ((t,j)\in E). $$
Lemma 1 proves that a positive $\delta$ is feasible. Rational linear programming supplies a solution of polynomial encoding length. Thus $\Delta$ and $\alpha$ also have polynomial encoding length.

State evaluation. At a rational state, compute $M(r,z)$ and maximize each edge coordinate over the maximum-flow face to determine $G(r,z)$. These are polynomial-size rational linear programs. Strongly connected components, deterministic topological sorting, and category ranking are polynomial-time graph operations. Evaluation is polynomial in the dimensions and the state’s bit length.

Borel measurability. Lemma 4 expresses $M(r,z)$ as a finite minimum of linear functions, so it is continuous and piecewise linear. On each region where one such expression applies, the maximum-flow face has fixed constraint coefficients and affine right-hand sides. Its basic feasible solutions are affine functions of $(r,z)$ wherever their feasibility inequalities hold.

An edge belongs to $G(r,z)$ exactly when some vertex of this bounded face has a positive coordinate on that edge. This is a finite combination of linear weak and strict inequalities, and hence a Borel condition. Active-vertex tests are Borel as well. The deterministic graph procedures then choose among finitely many rank vectors. Therefore $p(r,z)$ is Borel measurable.

The policy description stores $E$, the baseline dual, $\alpha$, and these graph and flow procedures. It need not enumerate all residual-support graphs. Its description is constructible in $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time.

7. What remains open

This theorem resolves the whole promised unit-demand subclass, including cases with multiple optimal marginal matrices.

It does not resolve instances containing demand-two or demand-three types. The unit-demand proof depends on two specific features:

  • Every purchase is a singleton, making the Hall-deficiency derivative calculation exact.
  • Residual feasibility is ordinary bipartite flow feasibility. For larger demands, the bounds $x_{tj}\leq r_t$ are additional substantive constraints arising from the prohibition on repeated categories.

No extension handling those constraints, and no hardness reduction for the general promised problem, is proved here.
