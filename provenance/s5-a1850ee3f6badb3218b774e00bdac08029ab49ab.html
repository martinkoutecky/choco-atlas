Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\min_t\mu_t\ge\delta\), additive values \(v_t(z)=\sum_{g=1}^m a_{tg}z_g\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), allocate mass \(x_{t,z}\ge0\) among integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), subject to \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\). Require EF1 for every positive-mass type-bundle pair \((t,z)\) and every positive-mass bundle \(y\): either \(y\) is empty or some \(g\) with \(y_g>0\) satisfies \(v_t(z)\ge v_t(y-e_g)\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be maximum welfare without EF1 and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}\) maximum welfare subject to it. Determine the sharp worst-case ratio \(\sup \mathrm{OPT}/\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}\) as a function of \(\delta\), and whether an EF1 allocation achieving that asymptotic guarantee can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the binary input length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Inputs are \(m\ge1\) goods categories, rational \(0<\delta\le1\), masses \(\mu_t\ge\delta\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), nonnegative rational coefficients \(a_{tg}\), and nonnegative rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\). Values are additive, \(v_t(z)=\sum_g a_{tg}z_g\), and are unscaled: no common total-value normalization is imposed.
An allocation assigns real masses \(x_{t,z}\ge0\) to integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), satisfying \(\sum_zx_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\) for every \(g\); repeated units are allowed and all supply must be allocated.
EF1 requires that whenever \(x_{t,z}>0\) and \(\sum_sx_{s,y}>0\), either \(y=0\) or some \(g\) with \(y_g>0\) satisfies \(v_t(z)\ge v_t(y-e_g)\), where \(e_g\) is the \(g\)-th unit vector. This includes comparisons within a type.
Welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}v_t(z)\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}\) be supremal welfare without and with EF1. Define \(R(\delta)=\sup_I\mathrm{OPT}(I)/\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}(I)\), over the stated instances with \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\); the ratio is \(+\infty\) if no EF1 allocation exists or its supremal welfare is zero.
What is the sharp asymptotic order of \(R(\delta)\), and can an EF1 allocation attaining that welfare guarantee be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, including \(\delta\)?
For finite societies of \(n\) agents, EF1 has price \(\Theta(n)\) for unscaled additive valuations, with a polynomial-time algorithm achieving welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(2n)\); with scaled valuations the price is \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\). Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question omitted valuation signs and scaling. Coefficients are nonnegative, and the unscaled branch of the anchor is retained because the question imposes no common total-value normalization.
  • Supplies are nonnegative binary rationals; the mass lower bound is a binary rational in (0,1], included in the input length.

Result

The sharp asymptotic order is \(R(\delta)=\Theta(1/\delta)\). Moreover, an EF1 allocation satisfying

\(W(x)\ge \dfrac12\sum_t\mu_t v_t(b)\ge \dfrac{\delta}{2}\mathrm{OPT}\)

can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Here \(v_t(b)=\sum_g a_{tg}b_g\), using the linear extension of the valuation to rational vectors.

More precisely,

  • If \(0<\delta\le 1/2\), then \(1/\delta\le R(\delta)\le 2/\delta\).
  • If \(1/2<\delta\le 1\), then \(R(\delta)=1\).

The algorithm outputs a list of polynomially many pairs \((t,z)\) and their rational masses. Its running time does not depend polynomially on the numerical value of \(1/\delta\), the supplies, or a common denominator.

1. Unconstrained welfare and a benchmark

Define

\(V=\sum_t\mu_t v_t(b)\).

Every allocation satisfies

\(W(x)=\sum_g\sum_{t,z}a_{tg}x_{t,z}z_g\le\sum_g b_g\max_t a_{tg}\).

This upper bound is attainable without fairness. For each category, choose a maximizing type and assign that category’s entire supply to it. The resulting expected bundle for each type can be realized by a distribution over integral bundles: independently round each coordinate to its floor or ceiling with the appropriate probabilities. Consequently,

\(\mathrm{OPT}=\sum_g b_g\max_t a_{tg}\).

For every category, a maximizing type has mass at least \(\delta\). Nonnegativity therefore gives

\(V=\sum_g b_g\sum_t\mu_t a_{tg}\ge\delta\sum_g b_g\max_t a_{tg}=\delta\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

We will construct an EF1 allocation with welfare at least \(V/2\).

For each type, define its best aggregate supply of at most one unit:

\(h_t=\max\left\{\sum_g a_{tg}q_g:\ 0\le q_g\le b_g,\ \sum_g q_g\le1\right\}\),

and put \(H=\sum_t\mu_t h_t\).

2. A welfare inequality implied by EF1

Lemma. Every finitely supported EF1 allocation satisfies \(W(x)\ge V-H\).

Proof. Fix a type \(t\) and one of its occupied bundles \(z\).

For each occupied nonempty bundle \(y\), choose a category \(g_t(y)\) whose deletion witnesses EF1. Thus

\(v_t(z)\ge v_t(y)-a_{t,g_t(y)}\).

For \(y=0\), the corresponding inequality is \(v_t(z)\ge0\), which holds by nonnegativity.

Write \(p_y=\sum_s x_{s,y}\), the population mass receiving \(y\), and define

\(q_g=\sum_{y\ne0:\,g_t(y)=g}p_y\).

Exactly one category is selected for each nonempty bundle, so \(\sum_gq_g\le1\). Moreover, every bundle contributing to \(q_g\) contains at least one unit of category \(g\). Hence

\(q_g\le\sum_y p_y y_g=b_g\).

Averaging the EF1 inequalities over all recipients, whose total mass is one, gives

\(v_t(z)\ge\sum_y p_yv_t(y)-\sum_g a_{tg}q_g =v_t(b)-\sum_g a_{tg}q_g\ge v_t(b)-h_t\).

Averaging over type \(t\)’s own bundles and then summing over types proves

\(W(x)\ge\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(v_t(b)-h_t\bigr)=V-H\). ∎

Thus it suffices to construct an EF1 allocation whose welfare is also at least \(H\).

3. An optimal first-unit assignment

Let \(S=\sum_g b_g\). Solve the transportation problem

\(M=\max\sum_{t,g}a_{tg}f_{tg}\)

subject to

\(f_{tg}\ge0,\qquad \sum_gf_{tg}\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_tf_{tg}\le b_g,\qquad \sum_{t,g}f_{tg}=\min\{1,S\}\).

Interpret \(f_{tg}\) as the mass of type \(t\) receiving one unit of category \(g\). This transportation problem is feasible: its complete bipartite graph has total type capacity one and total category capacity \(S\).

Lemma. \(M\ge H\).

Proof. Choose an optimizer \(q^t\) in the definition of each \(h_t\). Set \(f^0_{tg}=\mu_tq^t_g\). Then

\(\sum_g f^0_{tg}\le\mu_t\),

and

\(\sum_t f^0_{tg}=\sum_t\mu_tq^t_g\le b_g\sum_t\mu_t=b_g\).

Its objective value is \(H\).

If its total flow is smaller than \(\min\{1,S\}\), some type and some category both have residual capacity. Increase their connecting flow until an endpoint is saturated or the required total is reached. Repeating completes the flow to the required total. Because all coefficients are nonnegative, this completion does not decrease its objective value. Therefore \(M\ge H\). ∎

If \(S\le1\), every category constraint is saturated, since total flow equals total supply. Give mass \(f_{tg}\) of type \(t\) the singleton bundle \(e_g\), and give its remaining mass the empty bundle. This allocates all supply and is EF1: deleting the sole unit from any nonempty bundle leaves zero. Its welfare is \(M\ge H\).

It remains to handle \(S>1\), when every type constraint is saturated. We will realize the optimal first-unit assignment as a greedy first round and then continue round robin.

4. Serial realization of the optimal assignment

The following lemma permits a polynomially represented order of recipients.

Lemma. Suppose an optimal transportation flow has positive remaining type capacities \(r_t\), category capacities \(c_g\), and row equalities \(\sum_g f_{tg}=r_t\). Then some positive entry \(f_{tg}\) satisfies

\(a_{tg}=\max_{h:\,c_h>0}a_{th}\).

In other words, some assigned category is a best currently available category for its recipient type.

Proof. Suppose no positive entry has this property. For each remaining type \(t\), select a best available category \(g_t\). Then \(f_{t,g_t}=0\), and every category assigned positively to \(t\) has strictly smaller coefficient than \(a_{t,g_t}\).

If \(g_t\) has unused capacity, move a sufficiently small positive amount of type \(t\)’s flow from any positively assigned category to \(g_t\). This preserves feasibility and strictly increases welfare, contradicting optimality.

Therefore every selected category \(g_t\) is saturated. Since \(c_{g_t}>0\), it is assigned positively to some remaining type \(s(t)\). Form the directed graph with edge \(t\to s(t)\). Every vertex has an outgoing edge, so there is a directed cycle of distinct types

\(t_1\to t_2\to\cdots\to t_k\to t_1\).

For each index, type \(t_{i+1}\) holds positive flow of category \(g_{t_i}\). Choose a positive amount no larger than any of these flows. Transfer that amount of category \(g_{t_i}\) from type \(t_{i+1}\) to type \(t_i\), simultaneously around the cycle.

Every row sum and column sum is preserved. Type \(t_i\) receives its best category \(g_{t_i}\) and releases a category previously assigned positively to it, which by assumption has strictly smaller coefficient. The objective strictly increases. This again contradicts optimality. ∎

Apply the lemma repeatedly to the optimal first-unit flow. When it identifies \((t,g)\), append a population block of type \(t\), of mass \(f_{tg}\), to the recipient order. Assign that entire block one unit of \(g\), and subtract its mass from the row capacity, category capacity, and flow entry.

The residual flow remains optimal for its residual problem: any improvement could be combined with the already fixed assignments to improve the original optimum. Each step removes a positive entry and creates none. Thus there are at most \(\tau m\) blocks.

During a block, only its chosen category’s supply decreases. That category remains available throughout the block except possibly at its endpoint, so it remains a best available category. The resulting block order therefore realizes the optimal first-unit assignment as a greedy first round.

For subsequent rounds, keep this recipient order fixed. Each recipient chooses one unit of a best available category whenever its turn arrives, until all supply is allocated.

5. EF1 and the welfare guarantee

To make the round-robin argument entirely discrete, let \(D\) be a common denominator of all masses and supplies. Conceptually replace type \(t\) by \(D\mu_t\) agents and category \(g\) by \(Db_g\) copies.

An optimal first-unit transportation flow can be chosen on the grid of multiples of \(1/D\): after multiplication by \(D\), this is an integral minimum-cost-flow problem. Each block above therefore consists of an integer number of conceptual agents. The algorithm will represent these groups without expanding them.

Consider two conceptual agents \(i,j\).

If \(i\) occurs before \(j\), then whenever \(j\) receives its \(r\)-th item, agent \(i\) has already received its \(r\)-th item. The item later chosen by \(j\) was available at \(i\)’s earlier turn. Greedy choice implies that \(i\) values its own \(r\)-th item at least as much as \(j\)’s \(r\)-th item. Summing these inequalities proves that \(i\) does not envy \(j\).

If \(i\) occurs after \(j\), compare \(i\)’s \(r\)-th item with \(j\)’s \((r+1)\)-st item. Whenever the latter exists, the former exists and was chosen earlier. Greedy choice again gives the corresponding value inequality. Summing proves that \(i\) does not envy \(j\) after deletion of \(j\)’s first item. If \(j\) receives no item, there is no envy.

Thus the allocation is EF1. Aggregating conceptual agents into type–bundle masses preserves every required comparison, including comparisons within a type.

Nonnegative additive valuations ensure that subsequent rounds never decrease a recipient’s value. Consequently the final welfare satisfies \(W(x)\ge M\ge H\). Section 2 also gives \(W(x)\ge V-H\). Adding these two inequalities yields

\(2W(x)\ge V\ge\delta\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

This proves both the welfare guarantee and \(R(\delta)\le2/\delta\).

6. Polynomial implementation with binary input

It remains to show that neither the conceptual population nor the individual rounds need be expanded.

Transportation and initial order

Choose \(D\) as the product of the denominators of the masses and supplies. Its binary length is bounded by their total input length. Define integer counts

\(n_t=D\mu_t,\qquad N_g=Db_g\).

These integers have polynomial binary length.

The first-unit assignment is an integer transportation problem with total flow \(\min\{D,\sum_gN_g\}\), represented by a network with polynomially many vertices and edges. Standard polynomial-time minimum-cost-flow algorithms handle binary-encoded capacities and rational costs and return an integral optimum. Their hypotheses hold here: capacities are nonnegative integers, the required flow is feasible, and costs have polynomial binary length.

The serial-order lemma can be implemented by inspecting the positive flow entries and each type’s best category with positive remaining supply. There are at most \(\tau m\) iterations. Each iteration uses exact rational or integer arithmetic on numbers of polynomial binary length.

If \(S\le1\), this already produces the complete allocation. Hence assume \(S>1\).

Batching subsequent rounds

Maintain the ordered recipient groups, their sizes, and their current integral bundles. Initially there are at most \(\tau m\) groups.

At the start of a round, let \(c_g\) be the remaining integer stock. Fix a tie-breaking rule. Each type \(t\) has a best available category \(g(t)\). If no category runs out during the round, category \(g\) is consumed by

\(d_g=\sum_{t:\,g(t)=g}n_t\)

agents.

Compute

\(k=\min_{g:\,d_g>0}\left\lfloor c_g/d_g\right\rfloor\).

Execute these \(k\) rounds in one batch:

  • Add \(k\) units of \(g(t)\) to every group of type \(t\).
  • Subtract \(kd_g\) from each category stock.

This batch reproduces greedy round robin. For each category, its stock is at least its total planned consumption over the batch. It therefore remains available until its last planned recipient, possibly running out exactly there. Every recipient’s planned category is available at its turn and was a best category before the batch; removal of other categories cannot make that choice cease to be best.

If the batch exhausts a category, recompute choices with the remaining categories.

If it exhausts none and supply remains, some category now has \(c_g<d_g\). Execute one round explicitly, scanning the ordered groups. Within a group, assign the type’s best category until the group ends or that category runs out. Split the group when exhaustion occurs inside it, then continue with the remaining categories.

This explicit round must exhaust a category: otherwise choices would remain unchanged throughout the round, requiring \(d_g\) copies of a category with fewer than \(d_g\) copies available.

Each category is exhausted once. Consequently:

  • There are at most \(m\) explicit rounds.
  • There are at most \(m\) batches that exhaust categories, and at most \(m\) further batches immediately preceding explicit rounds.
  • At most \(m\) group splits occur, one per category exhaustion.

The final number of groups is at most \(\tau m+m\). Groups with the same type and bundle may be merged.

All group sizes and stocks are integers. Every bundle coordinate is bounded by the total initial number of copies, \(\sum_gN_g\), which has polynomial binary length. Each group’s output mass is its integer size divided by \(D\).

Thus the entire algorithm, including its explicit output, has size and running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

7. Matching asymptotic lower bound

Assume \(0<\delta\le1/2\). Use one category with supply \(b_1=1\), and two types with

\(\mu_1=\delta,\qquad \mu_2=1-\delta,\qquad a_{11}=1,\qquad a_{21}=\varepsilon\),

where \(\varepsilon\) is any rational number satisfying \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Both masses meet the lower bound, and \(\mathrm{OPT}=1\).

Because both coefficients are positive, EF1 between occupied bundles containing \(k\) and \(\ell\) units implies

\(k\ge\ell-1\).

Applying the reverse comparison shows that any two occupied counts differ by at most one.

If count zero were occupied, all occupied counts would be at most one. Its positive mass would then force the mean count to be strictly below one, contradicting the supply. Thus every occupied count is at least one. Since the mean count is exactly one, every recipient must receive exactly one unit.

Therefore every EF1 allocation has welfare

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}=\delta+(1-\delta)\varepsilon\).

The ratio is \(1/(\delta+(1-\delta)\varepsilon)\), which tends to \(1/\delta\) as positive rational \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero. Taking the supremum proves \(R(\delta)\ge1/\delta\).

This lower bound already has \(m=1\) and \(\tau=2\): support size alone cannot bound the welfare loss.

Finally, if \(\delta>1/2\), the mass constraints allow only one type. Every allocation then has welfare \(v_1(b)=\mathrm{OPT}\), and the construction above supplies an EF1 allocation. Hence \(R(\delta)=1\).
