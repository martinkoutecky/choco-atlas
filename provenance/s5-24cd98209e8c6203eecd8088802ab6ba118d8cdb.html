The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a distribution \(\mu\) over sincere voter types \(\theta\), each a strict transitive preference over a finite candidate set \(A\), and current ballot masses \(x_{\theta,b}\) with \(\sum_b x_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta\), does every sequence of permitted positive-mass best-response updates under \(1\)-approval reach a stable state? A permitted update moves mass of one sincere type from ballot \(b\) to ballot \(b'\), using only additions or flips allowed by the paper's minimal-divergence rule, when the resulting winner is strictly preferred by that type; stability means no such positive-mass update can improve its winner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m\geq3\) candidates, with a fixed linear priority order for breaking ties. Sincere types \(\theta\) and ballots \(b\) are strict partial orders on \(A\), possibly incomplete.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A state has nonnegative ballot masses \(x_{\theta,b}\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{\theta,b}=\mu_\theta\). The initial state is sincere: \(x^0_{\theta,\theta}=\mu_\theta\), with all other entries zero.
Under \(1\)-approval, \(s_x(a)=\sum_{\theta,b}x_{\theta,b}\mathbf1[\nexists c\in A:(c,a)\in b]\); the winner \(w(x)\) maximizes this score, with ties resolved by the fixed priority order.
An update selects one occupied pair \((\theta,b)\) and any rational \(0<\delta\leq x_{\theta,b}\), then transfers \(\delta\) from \(b\) to a ballot \(b'\). On each unordered candidate pair, \(b'\) may preserve its status, add a comparison where none existed, or reverse an existing comparison; it may never make a previously compared pair incomparable. The final ballot must remain a strict partial order.
For the selected \((\theta,b,\delta)\), consider all winners achievable by such destination ballots. Choose a \(\theta\)-maximal achievable winner \(a\) with \((a,w(x))\in\theta\), then minimize \(C(b,b')=|b\mathbin{\triangle}b'|\) among ballots achieving \(a\). Thus an addition costs \(1\) and a flip costs \(2\); all ties are permitted.
A state is stable if no allowable positive-mass transfer strictly improves its winner for the moving type. Updates are sequential, with no fairness restriction or positive lower bound on \(\delta\). Universal termination means every maximal permitted update sequence is finite and ends at a stable state.

Is deciding universal termination from the sincere initial state solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this decision problem NP-hard?

For finite electorates, these \(1\)-approval best-response dynamics terminate when omission is forbidden, with at most \(nm\) updates from a sincere profile. [Terzopoulou, Terzopoulos and Endriss (2024)](https://www.illc.uva.nl/~ulle/pubs/files/TerzopoulouEtAlAIJ2024.pdf).
Convergence is also known for certain nonatomic plurality dynamics over binary issues under local dominance and uncertainty restrictions; those use different ballots and update rules. [Kavner et al. (2023)](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0310.pdf). No result settling precisely the mass-update decision problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored sincere initialization, required by the discrete model: the supplied current state is the sincere initial state, rather than an arbitrary profile.
  • Interpreted strict transitive preferences as possibly incomplete strict partial orders, and required reported ballots to remain in that domain.
  • Normalized total mass to one and retained the candidate-domain condition m at least three.
  • Specified the incomplete-preference 1-approval scoring rule and fixed lexicographic tie-breaking.
  • Allowed combinations of additions and flips, while forbidding every deletion that leaves a previously compared pair incomparable.
  • Restored best-response selection before minimal divergence: the chosen winner must be maximal among achievable winners, rather than merely improving.
  • Made the transferred mass an arbitrary positive rational selected before optimizing the destination ballot; best response and minimal divergence are evaluated at that fixed mass.
  • Used the exact pairwise divergence cost, equivalently the cardinality of the symmetric difference of the two relations; mass size is not itself minimized.
  • Interpreted reaching stability as finite termination of every maximal sequence, with arbitrary scheduling and no minimum transfer size.

1. Two elementary facts about linear ballots

A linear order compares every unordered candidate pair. Because omission is forbidden, every allowable destination from a linear ballot also compares every pair. A complete strict partial order is a linear order. Consequently, all ballots reachable from sincere linear orders remain linear.

Under $1$-approval, a linear ballot approves exactly its first candidate.

Lemma 1: The currently approved candidate cannot become a new winner

Suppose the selected source ballot is linear and has first candidate $z$, where $z$ is not the current winner. Then no transfer from that source ballot can make $z$ win.

Proof. Every allowable destination is linear. A destination with first candidate $z$ leaves every score unchanged. Any other destination decreases $z$’s score by $\delta$ and weakly increases every competitor’s score. In particular, the score and priority comparison that currently places the winner ahead of $z$ remains valid. Therefore $z$ cannot win. $\square$

Lemma 2: An improving adjacent swap has minimum cost

Suppose swapping the first two candidates of a linear source ballot produces a specified improving winner. That destination has minimum cost among all allowable destinations producing this winner.

Proof. An adjacent swap changes exactly one pairwise comparison, so its cost is $2$. Every different allowable destination is another linear order and reverses at least one comparison, hence has cost at least $2$. The unchanged ballot cannot produce an improving winner. $\square$

These facts will verify the best-response and minimum-cost requirements separately from the score calculations.

2. A family with an exact twelve-update cycle

Consider three candidates and the following sincere types:

| Type | Sincere order | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| $P$ | $a\succ b\succ c$ | $p$ |
| $Q$ | $b\succ a\succ c$ | $q$ |
| $U$ | $c\succ a\succ b$ | $\alpha$ |
| $V$ | $c\succ b\succ a$ | $\beta$ |

Assume that all four masses are positive rational numbers and
$$ p+q+\alpha+\beta=1. $$

Define
$$ r=\alpha+\beta, \qquad \kappa=2r-p-q. $$

Theorem

If
$$ r>\max\{p,q\}, \qquad p>\kappa, \qquad \alpha>\kappa, $$
then universal termination fails, regardless of the priority order.

More precisely, the sincere state lies on a permitted cycle of twelve updates. Every resulting winner strictly exceeds every competitor’s score.

Proof

The sincere scores are $(p,q,r)$, so $c$ initially wins strictly.

Since $r>p$ and $r>q$,
$$ \kappa=(r-p)+(r-q)>0. $$

Also, $p>\kappa$ and $p<r$ imply $\kappa<r$, and therefore
$$ p+q-r=r-\kappa>0. $$

Choose a positive rational number $k$ satisfying
$$ k< \min\left\{ \beta,\, 2(\alpha-\kappa),\, 8(p+q-r),\, r-p,\, \frac{r-q}{2},\, \kappa \right\}. $$

Every quantity in this minimum is positive, so such a rational number exists.

Define the transfer sizes
$$ t=r-q+\frac{k}{8}, \qquad h=\kappa+\frac{k}{2}, \qquad \ell=r-p-\frac{k}{2}, \qquad T=\kappa-\frac{3k}{8}. $$

They are positive and satisfy
$$ t<p,\qquad h<\alpha,\qquad k<\beta,\qquad \ell<q,\qquad T<p. $$

Indeed:

  • $t<p$ follows from $k<8(p+q-r)$.
  • $h<\alpha$ follows from $k<2(\alpha-\kappa)$.
  • $0<\ell<q$ follows from $k<r-p$ and $r-p<q$.
  • $0<T<\kappa<p$ follows from $k<\kappa$.

Every proposed destination swaps the first two candidates of its linear source ballot. Lemma 2 therefore gives minimum cost $2$ for every update.

Each transfer is subsequently reversed for exactly the same type and mass, between exactly the same two ballots. At step $12$, every type’s entire mass is again on its sincere ballot.

Hence the full state, not merely its score vector, returns to $x^0$. Repeating the twelve updates produces an infinite permitted sequence. Universal termination fails. $\square$
