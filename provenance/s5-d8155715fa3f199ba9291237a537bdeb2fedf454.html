The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(A\) and position types \(R\) with rational masses \(\alpha_a,\beta_r\) summing to the same total, a fixed allowed-pair graph \(E\), rational costs, and an optimal transportation plan with optimal dual potentials, costs incident to one type \(a_0\) are updated. Determine the complexity of returning a new exact optimal plan and dual potentials; in particular, can this be done with one shortest-path computation in \(O(|E|+(|A|+|R|)\log(|A|+|R|))\) arithmetic operations, with bit complexity parameterized by the input length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: nonempty, disjoint lists of applicant types \(A\) and position types \(R\), each with positive binary-rational mass. Set \(\tau=|A|+|R|\); this is the support size, not the number of possible types. Assume \(\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a=\sum_{r\in R}\beta_r=M>0\), without requiring \(M=1\).
The fixed allowed-pair graph is \(E\subseteq A\times R\), with \(m=|E|\). A transportation plan has divisible masses \(x_{ar}\geq0\) on \(E\), satisfies \(\sum_{r:(a,r)\in E}x_{ar}=\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_{a:(a,r)\in E}x_{ar}=\beta_r\), and minimizes \(\sum_{(a,r)\in E}c_{ar}x_{ar}\). Costs are arbitrary signed rationals; no additional capacities are imposed.
For a plan \(x\), optimal dual potentials are unrestricted rationals \(p:A\cup R\to\mathbb Q\) satisfying \(c_{ar}+p(a)-p(r)\geq0\) on \(E\), with equality whenever \(x_{ar}>0\). Any optimal plan and compatible potentials are acceptable.
The input includes an exact optimal plan and compatible potentials for \(c\), a type \(a_0\in A\), and new rational costs \(\widetilde c\) equal to \(c\) outside edges incident to \(a_0\). Masses and \(E\) remain fixed. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the supplied plan, potentials, and update.

Is returning a new exact optimal plan and dual potentials polynomial-time solvable in \((m,\tau,L)\), or NP-hard?
In particular, can one shortest-path computation suffice within \(O(m+\tau\log\tau)\) arithmetic operations and \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations?

For discrete minimum-cost perfect matching, a cost update at one vertex admits an exact matching-and-potential update using one Dijkstra computation in \(O(m+\tau\log\tau)\) time ([Morita et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.11325v2)).

1. Status

Returning an exact updated optimal plan and compatible rational potentials is polynomial-time solvable in $(m,\tau,L)$.

The stronger bound—one shortest-path computation and $O(m+\tau\log\tau)$ total arithmetic operations—is not settled here. The following additional results clarify that question:

  • Solving an arbitrary feasible transportation problem from scratch reduces to a single-edge cost update, with

$$ \tau'=2\tau+4,\qquad m'=m+2\tau+3. $$
The supplied old optimal plan and potentials in the constructed instance are trivial. The reduction also recovers compatible potentials for the original problem.

  • A sparse family requires exactly $\tau/2$ shortest-path computations under the direct successive-shortest-path extension of the matching update.

Neither result proves an impossibility theorem for other algorithms.

2. Polynomial-time computation of an exact primal–dual pair

2.1. Feasibility and boundedness

The supplied old plan remains feasible after the update, since the allowed pairs and all marginal masses remain unchanged.

For every feasible plan,
$$ 0\leq x_{ar}\leq \min\{\alpha_a,\beta_r\}. $$
Indeed, each entry is a nonnegative summand of both its row sum and its column sum. Consequently, the feasible set is a nonempty, closed, bounded polytope. The updated linear objective therefore attains a finite minimum, even with signed costs.

2.2. Exact minimum-cost-flow formulation

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the masses, and let $C$ be the product of the denominators of the updated costs. Both are positive integers of binary length $O(L)$.

Construct an uncapacitated directed network on $A\cup R$, with an arc $a\to r$ for each allowed pair. Using the convention that outgoing flow minus incoming flow equals the balance, set
$$ b(a)=D\alpha_a,\qquad b(r)=-D\beta_r,\qquad k_{ar}=C\widetilde c_{ar}. $$
These quantities are integers, and
$$ \sum_{v\in A\cup R}b(v)=0. $$

The transformation $y_{ar}=Dx_{ar}$ is a bijection between feasible transportation plans and feasible network flows. Furthermore,
$$ \sum_{(a,r)\in E}k_{ar}y_{ar} = CD\sum_{(a,r)\in E}\widetilde c_{ar}x_{ar}. $$
Thus it preserves optimality.

Apply the exact integral version of Orlin’s strongly polynomial algorithm for uncapacitated minimum-cost flow. Its arithmetic bound is
$$ O\!\left(\tau\log\tau\,(m+\tau\log\tau)\right). $$
The required hypotheses hold: the network has $\tau$ vertices and $m$ arcs, integral costs and balances, no upper capacities, and a feasible bounded optimization problem. [Orlin’s theorem](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.41.2.338)

If $y^*$ is the resulting optimal flow, then $x^*=y^*/D$ is an exact updated optimal transportation plan.

2.3. Recovering compatible potentials

Construct the residual graph of $y^*$:

  • Every allowed forward arc $a\to r$ has cost $k_{ar}$.
  • Whenever $y^*_{ar}>0$, include a reverse arc $r\to a$ of cost $-k_{ar}$ and capacity $y^*_{ar}$.

This graph has no negative directed cycle. To prove this, suppose such a cycle existed. The forward graph is acyclic, so the cycle contains a reverse arc. Every reverse arc present has positive capacity. Augmenting by a sufficiently small positive amount around the cycle would preserve balances and nonnegativity while strictly decreasing cost, contradicting optimality.

Add a source with zero-cost arcs to every original vertex. Compute shortest-path distances $h(v)$ by Bellman–Ford. Every vertex is reachable, and the absence of negative cycles makes its distance finite.

For every residual arc $u\to v$ of cost $k(u,v)$,
$$ k(u,v)+h(u)-h(v)\geq0. $$
Set
$$ p^*(v)=\frac{h(v)}{C}. $$
The forward inequalities give
$$ \widetilde c_{ar}+p^*(a)-p^*(r)\geq0. $$
If $x^*_{ar}>0$, the reverse inequality gives the opposite inequality, hence equality.

These are optimal dual potentials. Indeed, for any feasible plan $z$,
$$ \sum_{(a,r)\in E}\widetilde c_{ar}z_{ar} \geq \sum_{r\in R}\beta_r p^*(r) - \sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a p^*(a), $$
by summing the reduced-cost inequalities against $z$. Equality holds for $x^*$ because every positive entry lies on an equality edge.

2.4. Bit complexity

The denominator products have polynomial binary length. Strong polynomiality provides polynomial bounds on the minimum-cost-flow algorithm’s intermediate encoding lengths.

For the additional shortest-path computation, write
$$ K_0=\max\{1,\max_{(a,r)\in E}|k_{ar}|\}. $$
A shortest path can be chosen simple: deleting a repeated-vertex segment cannot increase its cost, because every closed walk has nonnegative cost. Consequently,
$$ -\tau K_0\leq h(v)\leq0. $$
The upper bound follows from the direct zero-cost source arc. Thus the returned potentials have polynomial binary length.

The integral flow entries satisfy
$$ 0\leq y^*_{ar}\leq DM, $$
so the returned plan also has polynomial binary length. Bellman–Ford uses $O(\tau(m+\tau))$ arithmetic operations, absorbed by the bound above, and polynomial bit operations.

This proves the polynomial-time classification without normalizing $M$.

3. A single-edge update encodes arbitrary transportation optimization

The next result strengthens the reduction in the notes.

3.1. Statement

Consider any feasible transportation instance with disjoint supports $A,R$, positive rational masses, allowed pairs $E$, and arbitrary signed rational costs $d_{ar}$. No initial optimal plan or potentials are required.

There is a reduction to the stated update problem that:

  • changes just one edge cost;
  • supplies a diagonal old optimal plan and zero old potentials;
  • has $\tau'=2\tau+4$ and $m'=m+2\tau+3$;
  • recovers an original optimal plan and compatible potentials.

Construction and recovery use $O(m+\tau)$ arithmetic operations and polynomial bit operations.

3.2. Making the original costs nonnegative

Feasibility and positive applicant masses imply that every applicant has at least one allowed neighbor. Define
$$ \ell_a=\min_{r:(a,r)\in E}d_{ar}, \qquad k_{ar}=d_{ar}-\ell_a, \qquad K=\max_{(a,r)\in E}k_{ar}. $$
Then $k_{ar}\geq0$. For every feasible original plan $f$,
$$ \sum_{(a,r)\in E}k_{ar}f_{ar} = \sum_{(a,r)\in E}d_{ar}f_{ar} - \sum_{a\in A}\ell_a\alpha_a. $$
The final term is constant, so the two objectives have exactly the same minimizers.

3.3. Constructing the update instance

Introduce fresh labels $s,t$ and set
$$ W=A\cup R\cup\{s,t\}. $$
Create disjoint applicant and position copies,
$$ A'=\{b_w:w\in W\},\qquad R'=\{d_w:w\in W\}. $$
Here $d_w$ denotes a position label, whereas $d_{ar}$ denotes an original cost.

Give both copies of each label the same mass $\mu_w$, where
$$ \mu_s=\mu_t=M,\qquad \mu_a=\alpha_a,\qquad \mu_r=\beta_r. $$
Every mass is positive, and each side has total mass $4M$.

Allow precisely:

  • all diagonal pairs $(b_w,d_w)$;
  • source pairs $(b_s,d_a)$ for $a\in A$;
  • original pairs $(b_a,d_r)$ for $(a,r)\in E$;
  • sink pairs $(b_r,d_t)$ for $r\in R$;
  • the closing pair $(b_t,d_s)$.

Initially, give original pairs cost $k_{ar}$ and every other pair cost zero. Supply the plan
$$ X^{\mathrm{old}}_{b_wd_w}=\mu_w $$
with all other entries zero, together with zero potentials.

This is an optimal compatible pair: its cost is zero, every cost is nonnegative, and every positive-flow edge has cost zero.

Set
$$ B=(\tau+1)K+1. $$
Change only the closing pair’s cost from zero to $-B$. The designated updated applicant is $b_t$.

3.4. Plans correspond exactly to partial transportation matrices

For any feasible constructed plan $X$, define
$$ f_{ar}=X_{b_ad_r}, \qquad q=X_{b_td_s}. $$
Subtracting the row and column equations at each label gives
$$ X_{b_sd_a}=\sum_{r:(a,r)\in E}f_{ar}, \qquad X_{b_rd_t}=\sum_{a:(a,r)\in E}f_{ar}, $$
and
$$ q=\sum_{(a,r)\in E}f_{ar}. $$
The corresponding diagonal equations imply
$$ \sum_r f_{ar}\leq\alpha_a,\qquad \sum_a f_{ar}\leq\beta_r. $$
Thus $f$ is a nonnegative partial transportation matrix of total mass $q\leq M$.

Conversely, any such partial matrix determines a feasible constructed plan. Set its original-pair entries to $f_{ar}$, and set
$$ X_{b_sd_a}=\sum_r f_{ar}, \qquad X_{b_rd_t}=\sum_a f_{ar}, \qquad X_{b_td_s}=q, $$
$$ X_{b_ad_a}=\alpha_a-\sum_r f_{ar}, \qquad X_{b_rd_r}=\beta_r-\sum_a f_{ar}, $$
$$ X_{b_sd_s}=X_{b_td_t}=M-q. $$
All these entries are nonnegative. Each applicant and position equation follows directly from these formulas. Therefore the correspondence is a bijection.

The updated objective is
$$ F(f)=\sum_{(a,r)\in E}k_{ar}f_{ar}-Bq. $$

3.5. The penalty forces total mass $M$

This step must account for divisible masses; an integrality-gap argument would not suffice.

Represent a partial matrix $f$ as a flow in the auxiliary network
$$ s\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow t. $$
Give arcs $s\to a$ capacity $\alpha_a$, arcs $a\to r$ capacity $M$, and arcs $r\to t$ capacity $\beta_r$. Give only the middle arcs nonzero costs, namely $k_{ar}$. Partial matrices correspond exactly to feasible flows in this network.

The original transportation instance is feasible, so this network admits a flow of value $M$.

Suppose $q<M$. There must be a residual $s$–$t$ path. Here is a direct justification. If no such path existed, let $H$ be the vertices reachable from $s$ in the residual network. Then $t\notin H$, every original arc leaving $H$ is saturated, and every original arc entering $H$ has zero flow. Summing conservation equations over $H$ gives
$$ q=\sum_{e\text{ leaving }H}u_e. $$
Any feasible flow has value at most this cut capacity, contradicting the existence of a flow of value $M>q$.

Choose a simple residual $s$–$t$ path $P$. The network has $\tau+2$ vertices, so $P$ has at most $\tau+1$ arcs. Every residual arc has cost at most $K$: forward costs lie between zero and $K$, and reverse costs are nonpositive. Hence
$$ w(P)\leq(\tau+1)K. $$

Let $\delta>0$ be the minimum residual capacity along $P$. Augmenting by $\delta$ produces another partial matrix, increases $q$ by $\delta$, and changes the updated objective by
$$ \delta\bigl(w(P)-B\bigr)\leq-\delta<0. $$
Thus no updated optimum has $q<M$.

At $q=M$, all original row and column inequalities are equalities. For example, the nonnegative applicant deficits sum to
$$ \sum_{a\in A}\left(\alpha_a-\sum_r f_{ar}\right)=M-q=0, $$
so each deficit is zero. The same reasoning applies to position deficits.

Therefore every updated optimum yields a feasible original transportation plan. Among plans with $q=M$, minimizing $F(f)$ is equivalent to minimizing the original objective, since
$$ F(f)= \sum_{(a,r)\in E}d_{ar}f_{ar} - \sum_{a\in A}\ell_a\alpha_a - BM. $$
Both subtracted terms are constant.

3.6. Recovering original dual potentials

Let $P$ be any compatible potentials returned for the constructed updated optimum. On every original pair,
$$ k_{ar}+P(b_a)-P(d_r)\geq0, $$
with equality whenever $f_{ar}>0$.

Define original potentials by
$$ p(a)=P(b_a)-\ell_a,\qquad p(r)=P(d_r). $$
Then
$$ d_{ar}+p(a)-p(r) = k_{ar}+P(b_a)-P(d_r). $$
Thus $p$ is dual feasible and has equality on every positive entry of the recovered original plan. The dual-objective calculation in Section 2.3 proves its optimality.

3.7. Consequence for the requested bound

The constructed instance has
$$ \tau'=2(\tau+2), $$
and
$$ m'=(\tau+2)+|A|+m+|R|+1=m+2\tau+3. $$
Row minima, $K$, $B$, and the mass $M$ require $O(m+\tau)$ arithmetic operations and have polynomial encoding length.

Consequently, an update algorithm with the requested arithmetic bound would solve arbitrary feasible transportation optimization from scratch, including dual recovery, within
$$ O(m+\tau\log\tau) $$
arithmetic operations and polynomial bit complexity.

This is an algorithmic implication, not an NP-hardness reduction or a lower bound.

4. A sparse family requiring many successive shortest paths

4.1. Construction and exact optimum

Fix an integer $k\geq1$. Let
$$ A=\{s,b_1,\ldots,b_k\},\qquad R=\{u,v_1,\ldots,v_k\}, $$
with masses
$$ \alpha_s=\beta_u=k+1,\qquad \alpha_{b_i}=\beta_{v_i}=1. $$
Allow
$$ E=\{(s,u)\}\cup \{(s,v_i),(b_i,v_i),(b_i,u):1\leq i\leq k\}. $$
Thus
$$ \tau=2k+2,\qquad m=3k+1. $$

Initially, all costs are zero. Supply
$$ x^{\mathrm{old}}_{su}=k+1,\qquad x^{\mathrm{old}}_{b_iv_i}=1, $$
with all other entries zero and zero potentials.

Update only the row of $s$:
$$ \widetilde c_{su}=k,\qquad \widetilde c_{sv_i}=i-1. $$
All costs on rows $b_i$ remain zero.

Writing $\lambda_i=x_{sv_i}$, the marginal equations force
$$ x_{b_iu}=\lambda_i,\qquad x_{b_iv_i}=1-\lambda_i,\qquad x_{su}=k+1-\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i. $$
Feasibility is equivalent to $0\leq\lambda_i\leq1$ for every $i$: these inequalities also imply $x_{su}\geq1$.

The objective equals
$$ k(k+1)-\sum_{i=1}^k(k-i+1)\lambda_i. $$
Every coefficient $k-i+1$ is strictly positive. Therefore the unique optimum has
$$ \lambda_i=1\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad x_{su}=1. $$
Its cost is $k(k+1)/2$.

Compatible potentials are
$$ p(s)=0,\qquad p(u)=p(b_i)=k,\qquad p(v_i)=i-1. $$
The positive edges have reduced cost zero, while
$$ \widetilde c_{b_iv_i}+p(b_i)-p(v_i)=k-i+1>0. $$

4.2. Behavior of the direct shortest-path extension

Consider the procedure that deletes the old row at $s$, then repeatedly computes a shortest residual $s$–$u$ path and augments by the maximum permissible amount.

Initially, $s$ has unassigned supply $k+1$, and $u$ has deficit $k+1$. The simple residual $s$–$u$ paths are:

  • $s\to u$, of cost $k$;
  • $s\to v_i\to b_i\to u$, of cost $i-1$, with bottleneck capacity $1$.

After the first $j$ indirect paths have been used, the reverse arcs $v_i\to b_i$ remain precisely for $i>j$. A saturated $v_i$ can return to $s$, but cannot continue to $b_i$. Hence the remaining simple $s$–$u$ paths are exactly the direct path and the indirect paths indexed by $i>j$.

For completeness, negative residual cycles cannot invalidate this enumeration. At that stage, the potentials
$$ p_j(s)=0,\qquad p_j(u)=j,\qquad p_j(v_i)=i-1,\qquad p_j(b_i)=\max\{j,i-1\} $$
make every residual reduced cost nonnegative:

  • $s\to v_i$ and its available reverse have reduced cost zero;
  • $b_i\to v_i$ has reduced cost $\max\{j,i-1\}-(i-1)$, and its reverse exists only when this is zero;
  • $b_i\to u$ has reduced cost $\max\{j,i-1\}-j$, and its reverse exists only when this is zero;
  • $s\to u$ has reduced cost $k-j$.

Thus shortest paths may be chosen simple.

The procedure consequently uses the indirect paths in the unique order $1,\ldots,k$, sending one unit along each, followed by one unit along the direct path. Their successive lengths are
$$ 0,1,\ldots,k. $$
It performs exactly
$$ k+1=\frac{\tau}{2} $$
shortest-path computations.

This contrasts with the single-path augmentation available in the matching setting of [Morita et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.11325v2). It excludes a universal one-computation guarantee for this specific transportation procedure. It does not exclude algorithms that process the instance differently.

5. Remaining open point

The polynomial-time classification is proved. The reductions and family above do not determine whether some other algorithm always achieves one shortest-path computation with $O(m+\tau\log\tau)$ total arithmetic operations.

A positive resolution would, by Section 3, also give that arithmetic bound for arbitrary feasible transportation optimization from scratch. A negative resolution requires an argument applying to the proposed class of algorithms as a whole; failure of successive shortest-path augmentation is insufficient.
