sufficiently large encoding budgets admit instances requiring

expected samples. Thus the small-error worst-case sample bound is matching.

welfare-flow evaluations, by computing all derivatives after one solve per distinct observed state.

2. Derivatives and their exact recovery

2.1. Every marginal value lies in $[0,V]$

Fix an observed state and a participating type $t$. For
$$ 0<\alpha\le \mu_{t_0}, $$
the perturbation increases only the capacity of $t$ in the welfare optimization. Decreasing inactive mass changes no constraint. Therefore,
$$ W^\star(\mu+\alpha(e_t-e_{t_0}),\xi)\ge W^\star(\mu,\xi). $$

Choose an optimal perturbed flow with no flow on negative-surplus edges. Deleting such flow preserves feasibility and increases welfare, so this choice is possible.

If its incident flow at $t$ exceeds $\mu_t$, delete exactly that excess. The deleted quantity is at most $\alpha$. All other capacity constraints remain satisfied because incident quantities only decrease. Every deleted unit has surplus at most $V$. Hence
$$ 0\le W^\star(\mu+\alpha(e_t-e_{t_0}),\xi)-W^\star(\mu,\xi) \le V\alpha. $$
Taking the right derivative gives
$$ 0\le \Delta_t(\xi)\le V. $$
For the inactive type, the perturbation direction is zero, so $\Delta_{t_0}(\xi)=0$.

2.2. A circulation representation

Fix a state and suppress $\xi$ from the notation. Construct a directed network with source $o$, sink $z$, and the participating types as its other vertices:

  • $o\to b$ has capacity $\mu_b$ and reward zero;
  • $b\to s$ has capacity $1$ and reward $v_b-c_s$ for every allowable pair;
  • $s\to z$ has capacity $\mu_s$ and reward zero;
  • $z\to o$ has capacity $1$ and reward zero.

A feasible circulation corresponds to a welfare flow: its $b\to s$ coordinates are the trades, and all other coordinates are determined by conservation.

The capacities $1$ introduce no additional restriction. Indeed, total trade is at most
$$ \min\left\{\sum_b\mu_b,\sum_s\mu_s\right\}\le \frac12. $$
The same bound holds after the permitted perturbation, since the masses remain nonnegative and sum to $1$.

Thus maximizing circulation reward solves precisely the displayed welfare optimization.

Let $f$ be an optimal circulation. Its residual graph has:

  • a forward arc of reward $r_a$ whenever $f_a<u_a$;
  • a reverse arc of reward $-r_a$ whenever $f_a>0$.

Only arcs with strictly positive residual capacity are included.

There is no positive-reward directed cycle in this residual graph. Otherwise, augmenting by the minimum residual capacity on that cycle would strictly improve $f$.

We will also use the converse. The difference between any feasible circulation and $f$ can be represented as a nonnegative residual circulation, by using forward arcs for positive differences and reverse arcs for negative differences. Such a circulation decomposes into directed cycles. If every residual cycle has nonpositive reward, the difference cannot improve the objective.

2.3. Residual-path formula for a directional derivative

For a participating type $t$, let $a_t$ denote its capacity arc:
$$ a_b=o\to b,\qquad a_s=s\to z. $$
Write $\ell_t$ and $u_t$ for its tail and head.

If $a_t$ is unsaturated, increasing its capacity does not change the residual graph's arc set. That graph still has no positive cycle, so $f$ remains optimal. Consequently,
$$ \Delta_t=0. $$

Suppose instead that $a_t$ is saturated. Since $\mu_t>0$, its reverse arc $u_t\to\ell_t$ has positive residual capacity and reward zero. Define
$$ d_t=\max\{\text{reward of a residual path from }u_t\text{ to }\ell_t\}. $$
This maximum exists and is nonnegative. To verify existence, remove repeated-vertex segments from any walk. Those segments are cycles of nonpositive reward, so removing them cannot reduce reward. It therefore suffices to maximize over finitely many simple paths. The reverse arc supplies a path of reward zero.

Increasing the capacity of $a_t$ by $\alpha$ introduces a new residual forward arc $\ell_t\to u_t$ of capacity $\alpha$ and reward zero.

For an upper bound, decompose the difference between any perturbed feasible circulation and $f$ into residual cycles. Cycles avoiding the new arc have nonpositive reward. A simple cycle containing the new arc has reward at most $d_t$, because its remaining arcs form an original residual path from $u_t$ to $\ell_t$. The total circulation assigned to cycles using the new arc is at most $\alpha$. Hence
$$ W^\star(\mu+\alpha(e_t-e_{t_0}),\xi)-W^\star(\mu,\xi) \le \alpha d_t. $$

For the reverse inequality, choose a simple path attaining $d_t$. If $d_t>0$, augment along this path and the new arc. For every sufficiently small positive $\alpha$, all original path capacities permit an augmentation of size $\alpha$. Its welfare gain is $\alpha d_t$. If $d_t=0$, the unchanged flow already gives the required lower bound.

Therefore,
$$ \boxed{\Delta_t=d_t\quad\text{when }a_t\text{ is saturated}.} $$
This also proves the existence of all the right derivatives in the problem.

2.4. All derivatives require only two shortest-path computations

Negate residual rewards to obtain arc lengths. The resulting graph has no negative cycle.

For saturated buyers, the required paths run from $b$ to $o$. For saturated sellers, they run from $z$ to $s$. Thus all derivative values are obtained from:

  • one shortest-path computation from $o$ in the reversed residual graph;
  • one shortest-path computation from $z$ in the residual graph.

For example, Bellman–Ford applies because negative cycles are absent. The network has $O(m+\tau)$ arcs and $O(\tau)$ vertices, so these two computations use
$$ O\bigl(\tau(m+\tau)\bigr) $$
rational arithmetic operations.

The welfare evaluation is implemented by an exact optimizer returning an optimal flow as well as its value. This is one solve of the displayed optimization. No derivative oracle is used.

All relevant rational quantities have polynomial encoding length. One way to see this for the optimal flow is to multiply capacities by a common denominator $Q$, whose bit length is $O(L)$. The directed incidence matrix is totally unimodular, so an optimal extreme circulation has integral scaled coordinates. Exact rational flow optimization and the subsequent shortest-path computations therefore take
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m+\tau,L) $$
bit operations per distinct state.

3. A general estimator without a logarithmic factor in $\tau$

Let
$$ \lambda_t=\mathbb E_{P_t}[\Delta_t]. $$
The preceding bounds imply
$$ 0\le \lambda_t\le V,\qquad \lambda_{t_0}=0. $$

3.1. A known interval and zero-sample estimation

For every type,
$$ \lambda_t-\theta(t)\ge -M. $$
The inactive type gives $\kappa\le-\theta(t_0)$. A type attaining $M$ gives $\kappa\le V-M$. Therefore,
$$ -M\le\kappa\le \min\{-\theta(t_0),V-M\}=-M+D. $$

When $D\le2\varepsilon$, the midpoint
$$ -M+\frac D2 $$
is $\varepsilon$-accurate without sampling.

If $H=0$, this gives $\kappa=0$ exactly. If $m=0$, all derivatives vanish and $\kappa=-M$ exactly.

3.2. Reduction to clipped scores

Set
$$ a=-\theta(t_0). $$
Types with $\theta(t)\le\theta(t_0)$ cannot lower the minimum below $a$, because
$$ \lambda_t-\theta(t)\ge-\theta(t)\ge a. $$

For each remaining type, define
$$ d_t=\theta(t)-\theta(t_0)>0,\qquad w_t=(d_t-\lambda_t)_+. $$
Include a virtual arm with known score zero. Then
$$ \kappa=a-\max_t w_t. $$

A sample from type $t$, followed by the procedure in Section 2, supplies a reward
$$ X=\Delta_t(\xi)\in[0,V]. $$
For $n$ independent samples, define
$$ \widehat w_t=(d_t-\overline X)_+. $$

3.3. Threshold-sensitive concentration

For every $\eta>0$,
$$ \Pr\bigl(|\widehat w_t-w_t|>\eta\bigr) \le 2\exp\left( -\frac{n\eta^2}{2VD+(2/3)V\eta} \right). $$

Here is a verification of all cases.

If $d_t>V$, then $D=V$, and clipping has no effect. Bernstein's inequality applies with $\operatorname{Var}(X)\le V^2=VD$.

If $d_t\le V$ and $\lambda_t<d_t$, clipping is Lipschitz with constant one, while
$$ \operatorname{Var}(X)\le\mathbb E[X^2]\le V\lambda_t\le Vd_t\le VD. $$
Bernstein's inequality again gives the displayed bound.

Finally, suppose $\lambda_t\ge d_t$. Then $w_t=0$. The error event is impossible if $d_t\le\eta$; otherwise it implies
$$ \overline X<d_t-\eta. $$
The multiplicative lower-tail bound for independent variables in $[0,V]$ gives
$$ \Pr(\overline X<d_t-\eta) \le \exp\left( -\frac{n(\lambda_t-d_t+\eta)^2}{2V\lambda_t} \right). $$
For $\lambda\ge d_t>\eta$, differentiation gives
$$ \frac{d}{d\lambda} \left(\frac{(\lambda-d_t+\eta)^2}{\lambda}\right) = 1-\frac{(d_t-\eta)^2}{\lambda^2}>0. $$
The expression is therefore at least $\eta^2/d_t\ge\eta^2/D$. This proves the claimed bound in the final case.

All these inequalities apply because the state draws are independent and Section 2 supplies the required reward bound.

3.4. Elimination

Assume $D>2\varepsilon$. Let $J\le\tau$ be the number of eligible arms, including the virtual arm.

At round $r$, use
$$ \eta_r=\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\left(\frac34\right)^{r-1}, \qquad \rho_r=\frac{\delta}{2^{r+1}}, \qquad q_r=\frac{\rho_r}{3}. $$
For each surviving nonvirtual arm, obtain fresh samples so that its score error exceeds $\eta_r$ with probability at most $q_r$. The preceding concentration bound requires
$$ n_r= O\left[ \left(\frac{VD}{\eta_r^2}+\frac V{\eta_r}\right) \log\frac1{q_r} \right]. $$
Keep the $\lceil k/2\rceil$ arms with largest empirical scores, where $k$ is the current number of arms.

Condition on all preceding rounds, and choose a surviving arm $t^\star$ with greatest true score. Its empirical score underestimates its true score by more than $\eta_r$ with probability at most $q_r$.

The expected number of arms overestimating their true scores by more than $\eta_r$ is at most $kq_r$. Markov's inequality bounds the probability of at least $\lceil k/2\rceil$ such arms by $2q_r$.

Outside these two events, the best retained true score is at least
$$ w_{t^\star}-2\eta_r. $$
Indeed, if $t^\star$ is discarded and every retained true score were strictly below this threshold, each retained empirical score, being at least the empirical score of $t^\star$, would exceed its own true score by more than $\eta_r$. That would produce at least $\lceil k/2\rceil$ overestimating arms, a contradiction.

Consequently, each round loses at most $2\eta_r$, except with probability at most $\rho_r$. Since
$$ \sum_{r\ge1}2\eta_r=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \qquad \sum_{r\ge1}\rho_r=\frac{\delta}{2}, $$
the final arm has score within $\varepsilon/2$ of the maximum with probability at least $1-\delta/2$. Estimate that arm's score using fresh samples to accuracy $\varepsilon/2$ with failure probability at most $\delta/2$. Returning $a$ minus this estimate has the required guarantee.

To count samples, the number $k_r$ of arms in an active round satisfies
$$ k_r\le 2J\,2^{-(r-1)}. $$
Hence
$$ \frac{k_r}{\eta_r^2} = O\left(\frac J{\varepsilon^2}\left(\frac89\right)^{r-1}\right), \qquad \frac{k_r}{\eta_r} = O\left(\frac J\varepsilon\left(\frac23\right)^{r-1}\right). $$
Also,
$$ \log(1/q_r)=O\left(\log(1/\delta)+r\right). $$
Both resulting geometric sums converge. Including final estimation gives
$$ N= O\left[ J\left(\frac{VD}{\varepsilon^2}+\frac V\varepsilon\right) \log\frac1\delta \right]. $$
Because $D>2\varepsilon$, this is
$$ \boxed{ N=O\left(\tau\frac{VD}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta\right). } $$

4. Matching sample lower bound for small error

Assume $m\ge1$, $R>0$, and $\varepsilon\le R/96$. Write
$$ K=\tau-2. $$
Construct $K$ buyers, one seller, and the inactive type.

Choose dyadic numbers
$$ \frac V4<A\le\frac V2,\qquad \frac R2<T\le R. $$
Give every buyer value $2A$ and the seller cost $A$. Set buyer thresholds to $T$ and seller and inactive thresholds to $-T$.

For
$$ q=\lceil\log_2 K\rceil, $$
choose masses
$$ \mu_{b_i}=2^{-q-3},\qquad \mu_s=\frac12,\qquad \mu_{t_0}=\frac12-K2^{-q-3}. $$
These masses are positive and sum to one.

Buyer $i$'s conditional oracle returns either the empty-edge state or the state with only edge $(b_i,s)$. Since the seller's capacity strictly exceeds that buyer's capacity, the buyer's derivative is $A$ when its edge is present and zero otherwise. Seller and inactive oracles always return the empty-edge state. Every state has at most one edge.

Set
$$ h=\min\{T,A/4\},\qquad p=\frac hA,\qquad p'=\frac{h-3\varepsilon}{A}. $$
The parameter choices imply
$$ h\ge\frac R{16},\qquad Ah\ge\frac{VR}{64},\qquad 0<p'\le p\le\frac14,\qquad p'\ge\frac p2. $$
For the last inequality, use
$$ 3\varepsilon\le\frac R{32}\le\frac h2. $$

In the null instance, all buyers have edge probability $p$. In alternative $i$, only buyer $i$ changes, to probability $p'$. The target values are
$$ \kappa_0=h-T,\qquad \kappa_i=h-3\varepsilon-T. $$
Seller and inactive scores equal $T$, so they cannot attain either minimum.

For an accurate estimator, the event
$$ \mathcal A= \left\{\widehat\kappa>\kappa_0-\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}\right\} $$
satisfies
$$ \Pr_0(\mathcal A)\ge1-\delta,\qquad \Pr_i(\mathcal A)\le\delta. $$

Let $N_i$ count samples from buyer $i$. The adaptive-transcript relative-entropy chain rule gives
$$ \mathbb E_0[N_i]\operatorname{kl}(p,p') \ge \operatorname{kl}(1-\delta,\delta). $$
Only buyer $i$'s draws contribute divergence. All computations on sampled states and all internal randomization have the same conditional law in both instances. For a stopped algorithm, the identity follows by first padding and truncating its transcript, applying the finite chain rule, and then passing to the full transcript. If its expected sample count is infinite, the lower bound already holds.

For $\delta<1/4$,
$$ \operatorname{kl}(1-\delta,\delta)\ge\frac14\log\frac1\delta. $$
Also, the relative-entropy bound by chi-square divergence gives
$$ \operatorname{kl}(p,p') \le \frac{(p-p')^2}{p'(1-p')} \le \frac{24\varepsilon^2}{Ah}. $$
Therefore,
$$ \mathbb E_0[N_i] \ge \frac{Ah}{96\varepsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta. $$
Summing over buyers and using $K=\tau-2\ge\tau/3$ yields
$$ \boxed{ \mathbb E_0[N]\ge c\,\tau\frac{VR}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac1\delta } $$
for a universal positive constant $c$.

If $\ell(A)$ and $\ell(T)$ denote their encoding lengths, the construction fits an encoding budget of order
$$ O\bigl(\tau[\log\tau+\ell(A)+\ell(T)]\bigr). $$
Thus this lower bound requires sufficient $L$; it does not apply uniformly to every restrictive encoding budget.

5. An improved estimator near the zero-sample boundary

This section proves a new upper bound. It uses the known interval more directly than mean estimation does.

Fix a dyadic bound $U$ satisfying
$$ V\le U\le2V. $$
Assume
$$ 0<g:=D-2\varepsilon<\frac D4. $$
Equivalently,
$$ \frac{3D}{8}<\varepsilon<\frac D2. $$

Define
$$ r_t=M-\theta(t)\ge0,\qquad y=\kappa+M=\min_t(\lambda_t+r_t). $$
Section 3 proves
$$ 0\le y\le D. $$

Consider the two possible estimates
$$ \widehat\kappa_{\mathrm{low}}=-M+\varepsilon, \qquad \widehat\kappa_{\mathrm{high}}=-M+D-\varepsilon. $$
The low estimate is accurate whenever
$$ y\le2\varepsilon=D-g, $$
and the high estimate is accurate whenever
$$ y\ge D-2\varepsilon=g. $$
Both estimates are accurate in the intervening interval.

Only participating types satisfying
$$ r_t\le g $$
need to be tested. Let $J$ be their number. If $J=0$, then $y\ge g$, so the high estimate is accurate without sampling.

Assume $J\ge1$, and define
$$ p_*=\frac{D-2g}{2U}, $$
$$ T=\left\lceil p_*^{-1}\log\frac{4J}{\delta}\right\rceil, \qquad N_{\mathrm{bd}}= \left\lceil p_*^{-1}\left(2J+8\log\frac4\delta\right) \right\rceil. $$
The additional condition for the bound is
$$ \boxed{\frac{gT}{U}\le\frac{\delta}{4}.} $$

5.1. Converting a derivative sample into a binary observation

Choose an integer $b$ with
$$ 2^{-b}\le p_*. $$
After observing $X=\Delta_t(\xi)$, generate a binary observation with conditional success probability
$$ z(X)=2^{-b}\left\lfloor 2^bX/U\right\rfloor. $$
This uses exactly $b$ unbiased random bits after computing the integer numerator.

If $p_t$ is the unconditional success probability, then
$$ \frac{\lambda_t}{U}-2^{-b}\le p_t\le\frac{\lambda_t}{U}. $$
The observations for a fixed type are independent.

5.2. The sampling procedure

Process the $J$ eligible types in any fixed order.

For each type, sample until its first binary success or until it has received $T$ samples. If it receives $T$ samples without success, return the low estimate. If every eligible type succeeds, return the high estimate.

Impose a global cap of $N_{\mathrm{bd}}$ samples. If that cap is reached before every type has succeeded, return the low estimate.

5.3. Correctness when the high estimate would fail

Suppose $y<g$. A minimizing type cannot be inactive, because
$$ r_{t_0}=M-\theta(t_0)\ge D>g. $$
A minimizing participating type $t$ satisfies
$$ r_t\le y<g,\qquad \lambda_t=y-r_t<g. $$
It is therefore eligible and has success probability at most $g/U$.

Returning the high estimate requires this type to succeed within its first $T$ samples. By the union bound,
$$ \Pr(\text{return high})\le \frac{Tg}{U}\le\frac{\delta}{4}. $$
Thus the error probability in this case is at most $\delta/4$.

5.4. Correctness when the low estimate would fail

Suppose $y>D-g$. Every eligible type satisfies
$$ \lambda_t+r_t>D-g,\qquad r_t\le g, $$
so
$$ \lambda_t>D-2g. $$
Consequently,
$$ p_t\ge\frac{\lambda_t}{U}-2^{-b}\ge p_*. $$

The probability that a particular eligible type has no success in its first $T$ samples is at most
$$ (1-p_*)^T\le e^{-p_*T}\le\frac{\delta}{4J}. $$
A union bound over eligible types gives probability at most $\delta/4$ for any such failure.

For the global cap, imagine sampling each type until its first success, without individual truncation. Its waiting time is geometrically distributed, and the $J$ waiting times are independent. Their sum is stochastically dominated by the sum of $J$ independent geometric waiting times with success probability $p_*$.

For an integer $n$, the latter sum exceeds $n$ exactly when a binomial variable with parameters $n,p_*$ is less than $J$. With $n=N_{\mathrm{bd}}$,
$$ np_*\ge2J+8\log(4/\delta). $$
The multiplicative binomial lower-tail bound therefore gives
$$ \Pr(\text{sum exceeds }n) \le e^{-np_*/8}\le\frac{\delta}{4}. $$
The probability of incorrectly returning low is at most $\delta/2$.

When $g\le y\le D-g$, either output is accurate. These cases prove the required success probability.

Since $g<D/4$,
$$ p_*>\frac{D}{4U}. $$
Thus the deterministic sample cap satisfies
$$ \boxed{ N_{\mathrm{bd}} = O\left(\frac VD\left(J+\log\frac1\delta\right)\right). } $$

The explicit condition $gT/U\le\delta/4$ is essential to this proof. It describes a nonempty neighborhood of the boundary $g=0$ for every fixed $D,J,\delta$.

6. Matching worst-case bounds in a boundary regime

Here the preceding estimator can be made uniform over all thresholds satisfying their prescribed bound.

Assume
$$ 0<H\le\frac V{32},\qquad \frac{3H}{4}<\varepsilon<H. $$
Define
$$ g_0=2H-2\varepsilon,\qquad p_0=\frac{4\varepsilon-2H}{2U}, $$
$$ T_0= \left\lceil p_0^{-1}\log\frac{4(\tau-1)}{\delta} \right\rceil. $$
Suppose additionally that
$$ \boxed{\frac{g_0T_0}{U}\le\frac{\delta}{4}.} $$

6.1. Uniform upper bound

For any instance, $D\le2H$. If $D\le2\varepsilon$, Section 3 uses no samples.

Otherwise, the parameters of Section 5 satisfy
$$ g=D-2\varepsilon\le g_0, $$
$$ p_*=\frac{4\varepsilon-D}{2U}\ge p_0, \qquad T\le T_0. $$
Also, $\varepsilon>3H/4\ge3D/8$, so $g<D/4$. The required condition $gT/U\le\delta/4$ follows.

Because $p_0>H/(2U)$, the resulting sample cap is

6.2. Matching lower bound

Assume additionally that $H$ is an admissibly encoded positive binary rational and $L$ is sufficient for the following construction.

Use the same $K=\tau-2$ buyers, one seller, and masses as in Section 4. Choose a dyadic $A$ with
$$ \frac V4<A\le\frac V2. $$
Buyer values are $2A$, seller cost is $A$, buyer thresholds are $H$, and seller and inactive thresholds are $-H$.

Put
$$ d=2H,\qquad p=\frac dA. $$
Then
$$ 0<p\le\frac14. $$

In the null instance, every buyer has edge probability $p$. In alternative $i$, buyer $i$ always receives the empty-edge state; other laws are unchanged. The target values are
$$ \kappa_0=H,\qquad \kappa_i=-H. $$
Since $\varepsilon<H$, accuracy forces the event
$$ \mathcal A=\{\widehat\kappa>0\} $$
to satisfy
$$ \Pr_0(\mathcal A)\ge1-\delta,\qquad \Pr_i(\mathcal A)\le\delta. $$

There are two separate lower bounds.

A lower bound proportional to the support size. Couple the null and alternative-$i$ algorithms using the same internal randomness and the same samples from every other oracle. Their transcripts remain identical until the null algorithm observes a present edge from buyer $i$.

Thus the difference between the probabilities of $\mathcal A$ is at most the null probability of observing at least one such edge. Therefore,
$$ \Pr_0(\text{at least one present edge from }i)\ge1-2\delta. $$
The expected number of present edges observed from buyer $i$ is
$$ p\,\mathbb E_0[N_i]. $$
This identity follows by conditioning each adaptive sampling decision on the preceding transcript; the next draw from buyer $i$ has success probability $p$. Hence
$$ p\,\mathbb E_0[N_i]\ge1-2\delta\ge\frac12. $$
Summing gives
$$ \mathbb E_0[N]\ge\frac{K}{2p}. $$

A confidence lower bound. Apply the transcript relative-entropy argument in the direction from alternative $i$ to the null. Only buyer $i$ changes, and its per-sample divergence is
$$ \operatorname{kl}(0,p)=-\log(1-p). $$
Therefore,
$$ \mathbb E_i[N_i]\bigl[-\log(1-p)\bigr] \ge \operatorname{kl}(\delta,1-\delta) \ge\frac14\log\frac1\delta. $$
Since $p\le1/4$,
$$ -\log(1-p)\le\frac{p}{1-p}\le\frac{4p}{3}. $$
It follows that
$$ \mathbb E_i[N]\ge\frac{3}{16p}\log\frac1\delta. $$

Combining the two bounds and using
$$ \frac1p=\frac A{2H}>\frac V{8H}, \qquad K\ge\frac{\tau}{3}, $$
proves
$$ \boxed{ \sup_{\text{admissible instances}}\mathbb E[N] = \Omega\left(\frac VH\left(\tau+\log\frac1\delta\right)\right). } $$

The construction uses at most one allowable edge per state and has encoding length
$$ O\bigl(\tau[\log\tau+\ell(A)+\ell(H)]\bigr). $$

Consequently, under the explicit boundary conditions above, $m\ge1$, and sufficient $L$, the optimal worst-case sample count is
$$ \boxed{ \Theta\left(\frac VH\left(\tau+\log\frac1\delta\right)\right). } $$
The upper bound is a deterministic cap; the lower bound holds for worst-case expected counts.

At $\varepsilon=H$, every instance instead has $D\le2\varepsilon$ and needs no samples. Thus the positive lower bound immediately below that boundary is compatible with the exact zero-sample bound at the boundary.

7. Evaluation counts, running time, and remaining gaps

For each distinct observed state, Section 2 computes the entire derivative vector after one welfare-flow solve. Cache that vector. If $N$ samples are drawn, then
$$ F\le N. $$
There are at most $2^{L+1}-1$ binary strings of length at most $L$, so
$$ \boxed{ F\le\min\{N,2^{L+1}-1\}. } $$
This improves the notes' caching bound by removing its factor of $\tau$.

An implementation of either estimator has bit complexity bounded by
$$ F\,\operatorname{poly}(m+\tau,L) + (N+\tau)\operatorname{poly} \left( \tau,\, L+\log(1+N)+\log_+(V/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta) \right), $$
where $\log_+(x)=\max\{0,\log x\}$.

For the elimination estimator, round derivative rewards to a common dyadic grid in each round with error at most $\eta_r/4$. Applying the concentration bound at tolerance $\eta_r/2$ leaves the required score tolerance valid and changes sample counts only by constant factors. The grid precision is
$$ O\bigl(\log_+(V/\varepsilon)+\log\tau\bigr). $$
This prevents accumulated empirical means from acquiring denominators whose lengths grow linearly with the number of samples. The boundary estimator already uses a dyadic grid and a fixed number of random bits per binary observation.

When $N=0$, computing the threshold maximum and interval midpoint takes polynomial time in the input length. The expression above also remains defined because it uses $\log(1+N)$.

These are evaluation upper bounds. Neither sample lower-bound construction gives an evaluation lower bound: in those constructions, the reward is directly readable from the single edge's presence. Complete-state inspection and unrestricted additional computation prevent a sample lower bound from establishing a corresponding number of welfare solves.

The unresolved statistical region includes the transition between the small-error regime and the narrow boundary regime, as well as other configurations of $V,H,\varepsilon$. The lower bounds also retain their sufficient-encoding-budget qualification.
