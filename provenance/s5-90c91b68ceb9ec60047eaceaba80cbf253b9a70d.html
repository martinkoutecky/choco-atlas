  • Explicit rational support permits exact recovery of the score order in polynomial time in the input length, without queries.

1. Definitions and access assumptions

Let $[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and let $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n$ be positive scores. A query to a nonempty set $S\subseteq[n]$ returns a winner $W\in S$ with conditional probability

$$ \Pr_\theta(W=i\mid S,\text{past history}) = \frac{\theta_i}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}. $$

Thus each response uses fresh randomness, even when the learner chooses $S$ adaptively.

A ranking is a permutation $\sigma=(\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(n))$, listed from highest to lowest. A weighted society is a probability distribution on rankings. Its sampling implementation draws a fresh ranking for each query and returns the highest-ranked member of the query set. The learner observes only that winner.

Since multiplying all scores by the same positive constant preserves every query probability, normalize $\max_i\theta_i=1$. For $\epsilon>0$, a ranking $\sigma$ is an $\epsilon$-ranking if

$$ a<b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_{\sigma(a)}\ge\theta_{\sigma(b)}-\epsilon. $$

Uniform correctness means that the learner returns an $\epsilon$-ranking with probability at least $1-\delta$ on every admissible score vector. This requirement is essential to any worst-case query lower bound.

2. The hidden society reproduces the oracle exactly

Proposition 1: Exponential construction

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be independent exponential random variables with rates $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n$. Rank alternatives in increasing order of their $X_i$. This gives the Plackett–Luce distribution

$$ \Pr_\theta(\sigma) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\theta_{\sigma(k)}}{\sum_{r=k}^{n}\theta_{\sigma(r)}}. $$

Furthermore, for every nonempty $S$ and every $i\in S$,

$$ \Pr_\theta(i\text{ is highest in }S) = \frac{\theta_i}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}. $$

Proof. Independent continuous random variables have no ties with probability one, so the construction gives a ranking almost surely.

Fix a permutation $\sigma$. On the event that the ranking is $\sigma$, write

$$ y_1=X_{\sigma(1)}, \qquad y_k=X_{\sigma(k)}-X_{\sigma(k-1)} \quad (2\le k\le n). $$

The event corresponds to $y_k>0$ for every $k$. This linear change of variables has Jacobian determinant $1$, and

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{\sigma(k)}X_{\sigma(k)} = \sum_{s=1}^{n} y_s\sum_{k=s}^{n}\theta_{\sigma(k)}. $$

Integrating the joint exponential density therefore gives

$$ \Pr_\theta(\sigma) = \prod_{s=1}^{n} \left( \theta_{\sigma(s)} \int_0^\infty e^{-y_s\sum_{k=s}^{n}\theta_{\sigma(k)}}\,dy_s \right) = \prod_{s=1}^{n} \frac{\theta_{\sigma(s)}}{\sum_{k=s}^{n}\theta_{\sigma(k)}}. $$

All integrands are nonnegative, so the factorization is justified by Tonelli’s theorem.

For the query statement, $i$ is highest in $S$ exactly when $X_i<X_j$ for every $j\in S\setminus\{i\}$. Independence yields

$$ \Pr_\theta(i\text{ is highest in }S) = \int_0^\infty \theta_i e^{-\theta_i t} \prod_{j\in S\setminus\{i\}}e^{-\theta_jt}\,dt = \frac{\theta_i}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}. $$

Positivity of the scores ensures convergence. $\square$

Proposition 2: Adaptive transcript equivalence

For any learner whose information about $\theta$ consists solely of query winners, the oracle and the independently sampled society give the same distribution of queries, responses, stopping time, and output.

Proof. Couple the learner’s private randomness identically in the two implementations. Before each query, if their histories agree, the learner chooses the same set $S$.

By Proposition 1, both implementations have the same conditional winner distribution. Use one fresh uniform random variable to select the winner in both implementations, assigning intervals of lengths

$$ \frac{\theta_i}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}, \qquad i\in S, $$

in a fixed order. Their winners, and hence their updated histories, agree.

Induction makes every finite history identical under this coupling. Using the same infinite sequence of fresh uniform variables gives identical complete runs, including nontermination. Consequently, any stopping rule and output determined by the learner’s history agree.

For a query count $T$ with values in the nonnegative integers or infinity,

$$ \mathbb E_\theta[T] = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\Pr_\theta(T>t). $$

This follows by writing $T=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbf 1_{\{T>t\}}$ and applying monotone convergence. Thus expected query counts also agree, including infinite expectations. $\square$

Fresh sampling is necessary. If one sampled ranking were reused, two identical queries would always have the same winner. For a two-element set, the independent oracle has positive probability of different winners.

3. Explicit support changes the information available

Proposition 3: Polynomial-time recovery from explicit rational support

Suppose the input lists rankings $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m$ and positive rational masses $w_1,\ldots,w_m$, summing to $1$. Assume the listed distribution is promised to equal a Plackett–Luce distribution with positive scores.

Then an exact decreasing score order is computable without queries, in polynomial time in the explicit input’s bit length.

Proof. If $n=1$, return the unique ranking. Otherwise fix a reference alternative $r$. For each $i\ne r$, compute

$$ p_i = \sum_{\substack{1\le a\le m\\ i\text{ precedes }r\text{ in }\sigma_a}} w_a. $$

Proposition 1, applied to $\{i,r\}$, gives

$$ p_i=\frac{\theta_i}{\theta_i+\theta_r}. $$

Since both scores are positive, $0<p_i<1$. Hence

$$ q_r=1, \qquad q_i=\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}=\frac{\theta_i}{\theta_r} \quad(i\ne r). $$

Sorting the $q_i$ in decreasing order gives the exact score order. Equal values permit arbitrary tie-breaking.

For bit complexity, write $w_a=u_a/v_a$, with positive integer denominators, and set

$$ D=\prod_{a=1}^{m}v_a. $$

The bit length of $D$ is at most the sum of the input denominator bit lengths, up to a constant. Compute

$$ A_i = \sum_{\substack{1\le a\le m\\ i\text{ precedes }r\text{ in }\sigma_a}} u_a\frac{D}{v_a}. $$

Then $p_i=A_i/D$ and $q_i=A_i/(D-A_i)$. Because the masses sum to $1$, we have $0<A_i<D$.

Scanning the rankings requires polynomially many operations in the input length. All integers used above have polynomially bounded bit length. Comparisons between ratios use cross multiplication of such integers, so sorting also has polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

This proof does not require a polynomial-time procedure for checking the Plackett–Luce promise.

Corollary: Exact Plackett–Luce support is factorial

Every factor in Proposition 1’s permutation formula is positive. Therefore every one of the $n!$ rankings has positive mass.

Consequently, an explicit list representing the entire positive-score Plackett–Luce distribution must contain at least $n!$ distinct rankings. Polynomial time in that list’s length need not be polynomial time in $n$.

This support-size conclusion concerns the full distribution. It does not rule out a different, smaller distribution having the same winner probabilities.

4. The known query bounds and their quantifiers

For

$$ n\ge12,\qquad 2\le l\le n,\qquad 0<\epsilon\le\sqrt{1/32},\qquad 0<\delta<1/4, $$

the supplied lower-bound premise, supported by Theorem 3, concerns algorithms uniformly correct over admissible instances. For every such algorithm $A$, some instance satisfies

$$ \mathbb E_\theta[T_A] = \Omega\!\left(n\epsilon^{-2}\log(n/\delta)\right). $$

Queries have fixed size $l$. The restriction $n\ge12$ ensures the reduction uses $k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\ge6$. [Ren, Liu, and Shroff, Theorems 1 and 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02970).

For a fixed score-ratio bound $C\ge1$, Theorem 5 gives an algorithm $A_\eta$ and a constant $K_C>0$ satisfying, uniformly when $\max_{i,j}\theta_i/\theta_j\le C$,

$$ \Pr_\theta\!\left( T_{A_\eta}\le K_Cn\epsilon^{-2}\log(n/\eta) \ \text{and its output is an }\epsilon\text{-ranking} \right) \ge1-\eta. $$

Its parameter condition is met by choosing

$$ \alpha=\frac{1}{4(C+1)} \le\frac{l-1}{4(l+C-1)}. $$

Indeed, after multiplying positive denominators, this is $C(l-2)\ge0$. [Ren, Liu, and Shroff, Theorem 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02970).

These are approximate-ranking guarantees. The lower bound is not asserted for every restricted ratio class: if $C=1$, all scores are equal and every ranking is correct without queries.

5. A further proved result: an expected-query upper bound

The preceding high-probability guarantee alone does not bound the original algorithm’s expectation. The following construction repairs that limitation.

Proposition 4: Truncation and restart

Assume the uniform guarantee for $A_\eta$ in Section 4. There is a learner that terminates almost surely, returns an $\epsilon$-ranking with probability at least $1-\delta$, and satisfies uniformly

$$ \mathbb E_\theta[T] = O_C\!\left(n\epsilon^{-2}\log(n/\delta)\right). $$

Proof. Put

$$ H=n\epsilon^{-2}, \qquad L=\log(n/\delta). $$

For stages $r=1,2,\ldots$, set

$$ \eta_r=\delta\,2^{-r}, \qquad B_r=\left\lceil K_CH\log(n/\eta_r)\right\rceil. $$

At stage $r$, run a fresh copy of $A_{\eta_r}$, using fresh private randomness and oracle responses. Return its output if it terminates using at most $B_r$ queries. Otherwise discard its state after that budget and proceed to stage $r+1$.

Let $R_r$ be the event that stage $r$ is reached. Conditional on any history reaching that stage, its fresh run satisfies the assumed uniform guarantee. In particular, its conditional probability of exceeding the budget is at most $\eta_r$. Therefore

$$ \Pr_\theta(R_{r+1}) \le\eta_r\Pr_\theta(R_r) \le\eta_r. $$

The events $R_r$ decrease with $r$. Since $\eta_r$ tends to zero,

$$ \Pr_\theta(\text{infinitely many stages}) = \lim_{r\to\infty}\Pr_\theta(R_r) = 0. $$

Each stage uses finitely many queries, so the procedure terminates almost surely.

If a reached stage returns an incorrect output within its budget, the joint success event in the hypothesis has failed. Thus

$$ \Pr_\theta(R_r\text{ and stage }r\text{ returns incorrectly}) \le\eta_r. $$

A union bound gives

$$ \Pr_\theta(\text{incorrect final output}) \le\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\eta_r =\delta. $$

It remains to bound the expectation. Pathwise,

$$ T\le\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}B_r\mathbf 1_{R_r}. $$

Tonelli’s theorem and the reach-probability bound imply

$$ \mathbb E_\theta[T] \le B_1+\sum_{r=2}^{\infty}B_r\eta_{r-1}. $$

Since $\log(n/\eta_r)=L+r\log2$,

$$ B_r\le K_CH(L+r\log2)+1. $$

The geometric-series identities

$$ \sum_{r=2}^{\infty}2^{-(r-1)}=1, \qquad \sum_{r=2}^{\infty}r\,2^{-(r-1)}=3 $$

now yield

$$ \mathbb E_\theta[T] \le K_CH(L+\log2)+1 + \delta\bigl(K_CH(L+3\log2)+1\bigr). $$

For $n\ge2$, $0<\epsilon<1$, and $0<\delta<1/4$, this is $O_C(HL)$, as claimed. $\square$

By Proposition 2, this expected-query guarantee also holds for the independently sampled hidden society.
