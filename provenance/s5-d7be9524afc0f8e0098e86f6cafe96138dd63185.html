3. Proven: exact real solutions exist

We use the classical finite HZ existence theorem in its minimum-cost form:

For finitely many agents and equally many unit-supply goods, an equilibrium exists in which each agent receives a minimum-cost lottery among its utility-maximizing affordable lotteries.

The theorem allows utilities in $[0,1]$, including ties and zeros. The existence result and minimum-cost convention are recorded in [Chen–Chen–Peng–Yannakakis, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05746).

Choose an integer $N$ clearing all denominators of the masses and capacities. Create $N\mu_t$ agents of each type and $Ns_j$ copies of each school. Both totals equal $N$, and all counts are positive integers. Copies of school $j$ give utility $u_{tj}$ to type $t$. The finite existence theorem applies.

Let $P$ be its price vector. Exact clearance and individual budget feasibility give
$$ \sum_gP_g\leq N. $$
Write $a=\min_gP_g$. If $a<1$, replace prices by
$$ P'_g=1+\frac{P_g-1}{1-a}=\frac{P_g-a}{1-a}. $$
These prices are nonnegative and have minimum zero. For every lottery $y$,
$$ P'\cdot y-1=\frac{P\cdot y-1}{1-a}. $$
Hence affordability and the ordering of lottery costs are preserved, so minimum-cost optimality is preserved.

If $a\geq1$, the total-price inequality forces every price to equal one. Replacing all prices by zero preserves utility optimality: all lotteries are affordable at either vector, and at the zero vector every lottery has minimum cost.

At normalized prices, all copies of a school have the same price. Otherwise, transferring positive consumption from an expensive copy to a cheaper copy preserves utility and lowers cost. Minimum-cost optimality would therefore prevent every agent from consuming the expensive copy, contradicting its clearance.

Let $p_j^*$ be the common price of school $j$’s copies. For each type, average its agents’ allocations and sum over school copies, obtaining $x_{tj}^*$. Then
$$ \sum_jx_{tj}^*=1,\qquad \sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}^*=s_j. $$
Projection from copies to schools preserves utility and cost; conversely, every school lottery lifts to a lottery over copies. Thus the affordable benchmark values coincide. Averaging preserves exact affordability and optimality:
$$ \sum_jp_j^*x_{tj}^*\leq1,\qquad \sum_ju_{tj}x_{tj}^*=\operatorname{val}_{p^*}(t). $$

Finally, an agent cannot consume a positive-price, zero-utility good. Transferring that probability to a zero-price good either increases utility, contradicting optimality, or preserves utility and lowers cost, contradicting minimum-cost selection. Averaging preserves this restriction.

Therefore an exact real solution satisfying every requested restriction exists. The denominator-clearing expansion can be exponentially large; it is used only to prove existence.

4. Proven: rational solutions are polynomial-time verifiable

For nonnegative rational prices with minimum zero,
$$ \operatorname{val}_p(t)= \max\left( \{u_{tj}:p_j\leq1\} \;\cup\; \left\{ \frac{(p_k-1)u_{tj}+(1-p_j)u_{tk}}{p_k-p_j} :p_j<1<p_k \right\} \right). $$
The first set is nonempty because some price is zero.

To prove the formula, consider
$$ F_p=\left\{y\geq0:\sum_jy_j=1,\ \sum_jp_jy_j\leq1\right\}. $$
This is a nonempty compact polytope, so a linear utility function attains its maximum at an extreme point.

An extreme point has at most two positive coordinates. Indeed, on a support of size at least three, there is a nonzero direction $d$ satisfying
$$ \sum_jd_j=0,\qquad \sum_jp_jd_j=0. $$
Small perturbations in both directions preserve feasibility, contradicting extremality.

For an extreme point with two positive coordinates, the budget must bind: otherwise small probability transfers remain feasible in both directions. Its two prices must also differ, since equal prices permit such transfers even with a binding budget. A positive mixture with cost one therefore has prices $p_j<1<p_k$, and its probabilities are
$$ y_j=\frac{p_k-1}{p_k-p_j},\qquad y_k=\frac{1-p_j}{p_k-p_j}. $$
These give the displayed expression. One-coordinate extreme points give the affordable pure lotteries.

There are $O(m^2)$ candidates per type. Exact rational arithmetic computes them in time polynomial in the instance and supplied-output lengths. All remaining conditions are directly checkable. Hence validity of a rational solution is polynomial-time decidable.

$$ p_j=\frac{u_{1j}-a}{c-a}. $$

7. Proven: dyadic masses and capacities can be obtained by padding

This section concerns masses and capacities; utilities are left unchanged.

Padding lemma. Suppose a finite market with $n$ agents and $n$ goods contains a good giving utility zero to every agent. It reduces, at exactly the same error $\varepsilon$, to a support instance having dyadic masses and capacities.

Choose a power of two $N$ with
$$ n<N\leq2n, $$
and set $d=N-n$. Add $d$ agents with identically zero utilities and $d$ goods giving zero utility to every agent. Compress identical agents in this padded finite market and set masses to their multiplicities divided by $N$, and every capacity to $1/N$. All these numbers are dyadic, and the construction has polynomial size.

Lift any support solution to the $N$ padded agents. Denote its allocation by $X$ and prices by $p$. Every added good has price zero: otherwise no agent could consume it under the zero-utility restriction, contradicting clearance. Every added agent consumes only zero-price goods.

For each original agent $i$ and original good $j$, define
$$ a_i=\sum_{h\text{ added good}}X_{ih},\qquad b_j=\sum_{k\text{ added agent}}X_{kj}. $$
Counting the original agents’ total demand and the original goods’ total supply gives
$$ R:=\sum_i a_i=\sum_jb_j. $$
If $R=0$, retain the original allocation block. If $R>0$, set
$$ Y_{ij}=X_{ij}+\frac{a_ib_j}{R}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_jY_{ij}=1-a_i+\frac{a_i}{R}\sum_jb_j=1, $$
and
$$ \sum_iY_{ij}=1-b_j+\frac{b_j}{R}\sum_i a_i=1. $$

Whenever $b_j>0$, some added zero-utility agent consumes good $j$, so $p_j=0$. Thus the correction adds only zero-price consumption. It removes only zero-price added goods. Each original agent’s expenditure is unchanged, and its utility weakly increases because all utilities are nonnegative.

Every affordable original-market lottery is also an affordable padded-market lottery. Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{val}^{\mathrm{original}}_p(i) \leq \operatorname{val}^{\mathrm{padded}}_p(i), $$
so approximate optimality transfers at the same error. The correction preserves the zero-utility restriction.

The original globally zero-utility good must itself have price zero, by clearance and the restriction. Therefore the retained original prices are normalized. This completes the reduction.

8. Proven: the conditional obstruction to $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness

Specify the reduction notion as follows. Suppose polynomial-time maps $f,g$ satisfy:

  • For every Boolean formula $\varphi$, $f(\varphi)$ is a valid support instance.
  • Whenever $\varphi$ is satisfiable, every accepted solution $z$ of $f(\varphi)$ makes $g(\varphi,z)$ a satisfying assignment.

This is a standard universal solution-decoding requirement for search reductions.

For an unsatisfiable formula, Section 5 supplies a polynomial-length solution $z$ of $f(\varphi)$. Its decoded output cannot satisfy $\varphi$. Therefore unsatisfiability has a polynomial-size certificate checked by:

  • verifying that $z$ solves $f(\varphi)$ within the output-length bound;
  • checking that $g(\varphi,z)$ is not a satisfying assignment.

Both checks take polynomial time. If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, the reduction property excludes every such certificate. Hence unsatisfiability belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, implying
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$
This is the total-search obstruction discussed by [Megiddo–Papadimitriou](https://theory.stanford.edu/~megiddo/pdf/papadimX.pdf).

Accordingly, the proved classification is $\mathrm{PPAD}$-hardness together with a $\mathrm{TFNP}$ formulation and the special-case algorithms above. Neither a polynomial-time algorithm for variable $m,\tau$ nor $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness has been proved.
