The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational prior \(\theta\) and ability masses \(\nu_a\) for \(a\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), where assessors of ability \(a\) receive signals from \(f_a\) under state \(A\) and \(g_a\) under state \(B\), choose an ordering schedule with those masses and signal-contingent threshold policies based on public ballot history to maximize the probability that sequential majority declares the true state. Determine an optimal schedule and policy.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=2\) states, \(A,B\), with rational prior \(\Pr(A)=\theta\in[0,1]\). A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) ability types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Abilities belong to \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), and \(\sum_a\nu_a=1\).
Signals belong to \(S=\{-9,-7,\ldots,7,9\}\), with conditional probabilities \(f_a(s)=1/10+as/360\) under \(A\) and \(g_a(s)=f_a(-s)\) under \(B\).
An ordering schedule is any finite, predetermined sequence of homogeneous batches \((a_i,w_i)\), with positive rational weights satisfying \(\sum_{i:a_i=a}w_i=\nu_a\); types may be split and revisited. Within each batch, conditional signal proportions equal their specified probabilities exactly.
At batch \(i\), a deterministic policy selects \(t_i(h)\in\{-10,-8,\ldots,10\}\) from the public history \(h\) of earlier batches’ ballot masses. Each assessor votes \(A\) precisely when \(s>t_i(h)\); otherwise they vote \(B\). Thus under state \(A\), that batch contributes \(w_i\sum_{s>t_i(h)}f_{a_i}(s)\) to the mass voting \(A\), and analogously under \(B\). Signals themselves remain private; policies are jointly chosen to maximize collective correctness.
Voting stops when either alternative receives mass strictly greater than \(1/2\); after all batches, a tie declares \(B\). Write \(Q=\theta\Pr(V=A\mid A)+(1-\theta)\Pr(V=B\mid B)\).

Given also a binary rational \(q\in[0,1]\), is deciding whether some ordering schedule and threshold policy achieve \(Q\ge q\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.

For three discrete jurors, median ability first is reported to maximize strategically optimized correctness, and the last two jurors’ order is immaterial; the computational analysis uses a finite prior grid. [Source](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/76488/7/WRAP_preprint_ejor_2016-10-11.pdf)
For finite three-person juries with continuous signals, distinct positive abilities and equiprobable states, honest voting is optimized by middle, highest, then lowest ability. This concerns continuous signals rather than assessor masses; no result settling the mass problem above was located. [Source](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/158928/13/WRAP-optimizing-voting-order-sequential-juries-median-voter-theorem-beyond-2021.pdf)

  • Normalized total assessor mass to one, replacing discrete vote counts by ballot masses.
  • Retained the discrete ten-signal distributions and eleven permitted thresholds, including thresholds that prescribe constant votes; allowed every rational prior in [0,1].
  • Defined an ordering schedule as an arbitrary finite sequence of rational homogeneous batches, fixed before voting, allowing ability masses to be split and revisited.
  • Specified the nonatomic signal interpretation: each batch has exactly the conditional signal proportions, rather than a shared random signal or finite sampling noise.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). In fact, its optimal correctness is

\(Q^\star=1\) if some supported ability is positive, and \(Q^\star=\max\{\theta,1-\theta\}\) otherwise.

The decisive feature is the assumption of exact conditional signal proportions: an arbitrarily small positive-ability batch can reveal the state perfectly. The proof below handles all permitted inputs, including the stopping rule and degenerate priors.

Positive ability permits perfect correctness

Suppose \(\nu_a>0\) for some \(a\in\{1,2,3,4\}\). Set \(w=\nu_a/4\), so \(0<w\le1/4\).

Choose a predetermined schedule whose first batch is \((a,w)\). Follow it with one batch for each supported type, assigning weight \(3\nu_a/4\) to the remaining batch of type \(a\), and weight \(\nu_b\) to each other type \(b\). This schedule has \(\tau+1\) batches. Every weight is positive and rational, and the total weight assigned to each type is its prescribed mass.

Use threshold \(0\) in the first batch. The positive signals are \(1,3,5,7,9\), whose sum is \(25\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{s>0}f_a(s)=\frac12+\frac{25a}{360}=\frac12+\frac{5a}{72},\qquad \sum_{s>0}g_a(s)=\frac12-\frac{5a}{72}.\)

Thus the publicly observed first-batch mass \(x\) voting \(A\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle x=w\left(\frac12+\frac{5a}{72}\right)>\frac w2\quad\text{under }A,\)

and

\(\displaystyle x=w\left(\frac12-\frac{5a}{72}\right)<\frac w2\quad\text{under }B.\)

These are exact identities under the stipulated batch model. Hence comparing \(x\) with \(w/2\) identifies the state without error.

Neither alternative can win during the first batch: its entire weight is at most \(1/4\).

For every subsequent batch, define the deterministic history-dependent threshold by:

  • If the first-batch observation satisfies \(x>w/2\), use threshold \(-10\).
  • Otherwise, use threshold \(10\).

Every signal exceeds \(-10\), and no signal exceeds \(10\). Therefore every subsequent assessor votes for the true state.

To verify the stopping rule, fix either state. The incorrect alternative receives ballots only in the first batch, so its cumulative mass never exceeds \(w\le1/4\). It cannot trigger stopping. If the schedule were completed, the correct alternative would receive at least the entire remaining weight \(1-w\ge3/4>1/2\). Therefore some subsequent batch triggers stopping with the correct verdict.

This proves both conditional correctness probabilities equal \(1\), and hence \(Q=1\), including when \(\theta\in\{0,1\}\). Since correctness cannot exceed \(1\), this construction is optimal.

Zero ability permits only a constant verdict

Suppose every supported ability is zero. Then \(f_0(s)=g_0(s)=1/10\) for every signal.

Fix any allowed schedule and deterministic threshold policy. We prove inductively that its public histories are identical under the two states.

Initially the histories coincide. If the process has not stopped and the histories coincide before a batch, the policy chooses the same threshold under both states. Because the signal proportions coincide, that batch contributes identical ballot masses under both states. The updated histories therefore coincide, as do the stopping decisions.

It follows that the final verdict is the same under both states, including when the tie rule determines it. A constant verdict \(A\) has correctness \(\theta\); a constant verdict \(B\) has correctness \(1-\theta\). Thus every policy satisfies \(Q\le\max\{\theta,1-\theta\}\).

Both bounds are attainable: threshold \(-10\) throughout forces verdict \(A\), while threshold \(10\) throughout forces verdict \(B\). Consequently, \(Q^\star=\max\{\theta,1-\theta\}\).

Decision algorithm and running time

The algorithm is:

  • Scan the support. If any supported ability is positive, answer yes, since \(q\le1=Q^\star\).
  • Otherwise, answer yes exactly when \(q\le\theta\) or \(q\le1-\theta\).

For an explicit exact comparison, write \(\theta=p/r\) and \(q=u/v\), with positive denominators. The second step tests whether \(ur\le pv\) or \(ur\le(r-p)v\). These integer comparisons require no numerical approximation.

Here \(m=2\) and \(\tau\le5\). Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, the running time is \(T(2,\tau,L)=O(\tau+L^2)\) bit operations, with \(O(L)\) working space. The positive-ability construction uses at most \(\tau+1\) batches and has a rational description of size \(O(L+\tau)\). These bounds concern the specified two-state family.

Thus the problem is polynomial-time solvable; an NP-hardness classification under polynomial-time many-one reductions would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
