The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 12 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite multigraph \(G\) with a supplied \(t\)-coloring, chromatic number \(t\), and girth at least \(2t-1\), with monotone cancellable valuations on its vertices, consider a unit mass of identical copies of \(G\). Does there exist a measurable allocation assigning each copy's goods whole to their endpoints such that the allocation is EFX within almost every copy?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite loopless multigraph \(G=(V,E)\), write \(n=|V|\) and \(m=|E|\). A proper \(t\)-coloring is supplied, and \(\chi(G)=t\) is promised. The underlying simple graph has girth at least \(2t-1\), with forests having infinite girth.
Each vertex \(u\) has a monotone valuation \(v_u:2^E\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), normalized by \(v_u(\varnothing)=0\), supplied as a Boolean circuit taking a bundle's incidence vector and returning its value in binary. Nonincident edges have zero marginal value.
Valuations are promised cancellable: for \(S,T\subseteq E\) and \(g\notin S\cup T\), \(v_u(S)\ge v_u(T)\) implies \(v_u(S\cup\{g\})\ge v_u(T\cup\{g\})\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here a type specifies an entire graph and valuation profile, and the support consists of the supplied instance with mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\).
Copies are indexed by \([0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure. An allocation is a measurable map into the finite set of complete orientations of \(G\): every edge in each copy is assigned whole to one endpoint in that copy.
For almost every copy, require EFX with removal of every good, including goods of zero value to the envying vertex: \(v_u(A_u)\ge v_u(A_w\setminus\{g\})\) for all \(u,w\in V\) and \(g\in A_w\). No comparisons or transfers occur between copies.

Is deciding whether such a measurable allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(n,m,t,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.

Complete EFX allocations without the endpoint restriction can be computed in polynomial time on this graph class with cancellable valuations ([Bhaskar–Pandit](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol360-fsttcs2025/html/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2025.15/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2025.15.html)).
Deciding existence of an EFX orientation is NP-complete even for symmetric additive valuations on multi-trees with eight vertices ([Afshinmehr et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.17002v2#S3.SS1)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified looplessness and measured girth in the underlying simple graph, ignoring parallel-edge cycles; forests have infinite girth.
  • Used nonnegative integer valuations as in the discrete model and normalized the empty bundle to zero; subtracting its value preserves all relevant comparisons.
  • Supplied valuations by binary-output Boolean circuits, with monotonicity, cancellability, and graphical dependence promised, to make bit complexity well-defined.
  • Interpreted the single supported type as an entire copy instance, with mass one, and realized its copies using Lebesgue measure on the unit interval.
  • Required complete allocations, prohibited transfers and envy comparisons between copies, and retained the graded question's explicit endpoint restriction.
  • Specified removal of every good in the EFX condition, including goods having zero marginal value to the envying agent.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already when \(n=8\), \(t=2\), and \(\tau=1\). It also has polynomially verifiable certificates, so it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete in the usual promise-problem sense.

  • Measurability and finite existence.

Let \(\Omega\) be the finite set of complete orientations of \(G\), and let \(F\subseteq\Omega\) consist of the orientations satisfying every required EFX inequality.

Suppose a measurable allocation \(a:[0,1]\to\Omega\) satisfies EFX almost everywhere. Then the Lebesgue measure of \(a^{-1}(F)\) is \(1\). Consequently, \(F\) is nonempty: if \(F=\varnothing\), its preimage would have measure \(0\).

Conversely, if \(O\in F\), the constant map \(a(x)=O\) is measurable and satisfies EFX everywhere.

Thus measurable allocation existence is equivalent to existence of a single complete EFX orientation. This equivalence uses the fact that the requirements apply within each copy.

  • Polynomial verification.

A certificate specifies the receiving endpoint of each edge, using \(m\) bits. It determines bundles \(A_u\) for all \(u\in V\).

Evaluate each \(v_u(A_u)\). Then, for every \(u,w\in V\) and every \(g\in A_w\), evaluate \(v_u(A_w\setminus\{g\})\) and check
\[ v_u(A_u)\ge v_u(A_w\setminus\{g\}). \]
These checks include goods with zero value to \(u\).

Because the orientation is complete, for each fixed \(u\) the number of removal checks is
\[ \sum_{w\in V}|A_w|=m. \]
Hence there are \(n(m+1)\) circuit evaluations, including the own-bundle evaluations. Each evaluation, input-vector construction, and binary comparison takes \(O(L+m)\) time under standard gate-by-gate circuit evaluation. A valid verification bound is therefore
\[ O\!\left(L+n(m+1)(L+m)\right). \]

The coloring, chromatic number, girth, monotonicity, and cancellation conditions are promises; the verifier need not recognize them. On inputs satisfying those promises, it accepts exactly the instances admitting the required allocation.

  • Hardness and verification of every promise.

The supplied hardness theorem states that deciding whether a symmetric additive instance on a multi-tree admits an EFX orientation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, with the reduction using eight vertices. Its definition of strong envy quantifies over every good in the compared bundle, so its EFX notion includes removal of zero-valued goods. [Afshinmehr et al., Theorem 3.6 and Definitions 2.6–2.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.17002v2#S3.SS1)

Transform such a hard instance as follows.

Integer values and circuit representation. If its binary-encoded edge weights are rational, write them as \(a_e/b_e\), with \(b_e>0\), and multiply every weight by
\[ D=\prod_{e\in E}b_e. \]
This produces nonnegative integer weights \(w_e\). Since
\[ \log_2 D=\sum_{e\in E}\log_2 b_e, \]
the scaled weights have polynomial binary length. Multiplying all valuations by the same positive number preserves every EFX inequality.

For each vertex, supply a Boolean circuit computing
\[ v_u(S)=\sum_{\substack{e\in S\\e\text{ incident to }u}}w_e. \]
Such a circuit has polynomial size: select each constant weight using its incidence-vector bit, then sum the selected integers with binary adders. If the largest bundle sum needs \(b\) bits, \(O(mb)\) Boolean gates suffice per vertex.

Valuation promises. These valuations are normalized, nonnegative, and monotone. A nonincident edge has zero marginal value. For cancellation, define \(c_{u,g}=w_g\) when \(g\) is incident to \(u\), and \(c_{u,g}=0\) otherwise. Whenever \(g\notin S\cup T\),
\[ v_u(S\cup\{g\})=v_u(S)+c_{u,g}, \qquad v_u(T\cup\{g\})=v_u(T)+c_{u,g}. \]
Therefore \(v_u(S)\ge v_u(T)\) implies the required inequality after adding \(g\), including when \(S\) and \(T\) overlap.

Graph and coloring promises. The underlying simple graph is a nontrivial tree. It is bipartite, so a proper two-coloring can be supplied in \(O(n+m)\) time. Because it contains an edge, its chromatic number is exactly \(2\). Its girth is infinite, and hence satisfies the required bound \(2t-1=3\).

Society representation. Supply exactly one supported type: this graph and valuation profile, with mass \(1\). Thus \(\tau=1\).

Every transformation takes polynomial time and preserves EFX-orientation existence. By the equivalence in item 1, it also preserves measurable-allocation existence. The reduction therefore establishes hardness under all the stated promises, with \(n=8\), \(t=2\), and \(\tau=1\).

  • Running times and the unrestricted-allocation result.

For the specified society, \(\tau=1\). Enumerating all \(2^m\) orientations and applying the verifier gives a deterministic decision algorithm with running time
\[ O\!\left(L+2^m n(m+1)(L+m)\right). \]
Certificate verification takes
\[ O\!\left(L+n(m+1)(L+m)\right). \]
Neither bound requires a further dependence on \(t\) or enumeration of unsupported types.

A decision algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(n,m,t,\tau,L)\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), since hardness already holds at the fixed values \(n=8\), \(t=2\), and \(\tau=1\). The edgeless case is trivially feasible; the hard instances are nontrivial trees.

The polynomial-time unrestricted EFX-allocation theorem permits assigning an edge to a vertex other than its endpoints. Such an allocation need not be an orientation, so that theorem does not provide a decision algorithm for the present endpoint-restricted problem. Both directions are valid.

The set \(\Omega\) of complete orientations is finite, and its subset \(F\) of EFX orientations is measurable under the discrete measurable structure. Thus a measurable map \(a\) has measurable preimage \(a^{-1}(F)\).

The almost-everywhere EFX requirement means precisely that this preimage has measure \(1\). If \(F\) were empty, its preimage would be empty and have measure \(0\), a contradiction.

Conversely, any orientation in \(F\) defines a measurable constant allocation satisfying every required inequality everywhere.

This establishes the claimed equivalence. It uses exactly the task’s requirement that fairness comparisons occur within copies; there is no averaging of valuations or fairness inequalities across copies.

The certificate and verification procedure are correct.

The verifier checks the exact required inequalities for every vertex \(u\), every compared bundle \(A_w\), and every removed edge \(g\in A_w\). It imposes no positive-value restriction on \(g\), so removals of zero-valued goods are included.

\[ \sum_{w\in V}|A_w|=m. \]
