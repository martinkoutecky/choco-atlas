The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), attribute vectors \(a_\theta\), and each type’s preferences over acceptable colleges, decide whether there exists a mass assignment \(x_{\theta,w}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_w x_{\theta,w}\le\mu_\theta\), each college’s per-capita capacity \(q_w\), and attribute quota shares \(\ell_{wz}\le\sum_\theta a_\theta[z]x_{\theta,w}\le u_{wz}\), with no positive-mass blocking opportunity. Such an opportunity exists if, for some type \(\theta\), college \(w\), and current destination \(k\in W\cup\{\perp\}\), \(x_{\theta,k}>0\) and \(\theta\) prefers \(w\) to \(k\), and some \(\delta>0\) of that mass can enter \(w\) by removing masses \(0\le y_s\le x_{s,w}\) only from types \(s\) that \(w\) ranks strictly below \(\theta\), while satisfying \(w\)’s capacity and quota constraints after the change. As in the paper’s local blocking convention, feasibility at the source college is not checked.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support \(T\): the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) colleges \(W\) and \(d\ge1\) binary attributes, with \(a_\theta\in\{0,1\}^d\).
Each type has a weak preference order over its acceptable colleges; each college has a weak preference order over its acceptable types. Acceptability is mutual, every acceptable set is nonempty, and every acceptable college is strictly preferred to being unmatched, denoted \(\perp\). Members of a type share their attributes, preferences, and position in each college’s order; ties are allowed and are not broken.
Capacities and quotas are binary rationals satisfying \(0<q_w\le1\) and \(0\le\ell_{wz}\le u_{wz}\le1\). Quotas measure shares of the entire society, not shares of a college’s enrollment, and apply even to empty colleges.
A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{\theta,w}\ge0\), zero on unacceptable pairs, satisfying \(\sum_{w\in W}x_{\theta,w}\le\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta\in T}x_{\theta,w}\le q_w\), and \(\ell_{wz}\le\sum_{\theta\in T}a_\theta[z]x_{\theta,w}\le u_{wz}\) for every college and attribute. Set \(x_{\theta,\perp}=\mu_\theta-\sum_{w\in W}x_{\theta,w}\).
A blocking opportunity consists of an acceptable pair \((\theta,w)\), a destination \(k\in W\cup\{\perp\}\) with \(x_{\theta,k}>0\) and \(w\succ_\theta k\), and real masses \(0<\delta\le x_{\theta,k}\), \(0\le y_s\le x_{s,w}\), where \(y_s=0\) unless \(\theta\succ_w s\). The destination vector \(x'_{s,w}=x_{s,w}-y_s+\delta\mathbf{1}_{s=\theta}\) must satisfy all capacity and attribute quotas at \(w\). All removals may be zero; feasibility at the source college is not checked. An assignment is stable if it has no blocking opportunity.

Is deciding whether a feasible stable mass assignment exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(d\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

The discrete existence problem is \(\Sigma_2^P\)-complete, remaining hard with strict preferences, four colleges, maximum attribute upper quota three, and guaranteed feasibility ([Chen–Ganian–Hamm](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.10087)).
Fractional stable solutions are known for a different model with categories partitioned at each college and quotas proportional to that college’s enrollment ([Nguyen–Vohra](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~nguye161/proportional.pdf)); that result does not settle the fixed society-share quotas above. No result settling this exact mass problem was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Attribute vectors were unspecified; they are binary membership vectors, allowing overlapping attributes and the all-zero vector.
  • College preferences were omitted from the input; each college supplies a weak order over acceptable support types, and each support type includes all preference and college-ranking information shared by its members.
  • Acceptability is mutual and all acceptable sets are nonempty; unacceptable assignments and blocking pairs are excluded, and every acceptable college is preferred to being unmatched.
  • Preferences may contain ties, with blocking requiring strict improvement on both comparisons and no tie-breaking.
  • Capacities are positive rational shares at most one; quota bounds are nonnegative rational shares at most one. Zero lower and upper quotas are allowed, consistent with the intended discrete model and its constructions.
  • Quota shares use total society mass as their denominator and remain binding at empty colleges.
  • Assignments and deviations use real masses; unmatched mass is explicitly defined, entering mass cannot exceed its source mass, and the removal vector may be zero.
  • The number of attributes is denoted by d to distinguish it from the support size tau; L denotes total input bit length.

Result. The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Hardness already holds with \(\ d=1\), all types having that attribute, equal masses, and guaranteed feasibility. The proof uses the ties permitted in the question.

1. A discrete hardness source

We use the following established theorem: given two sets of equal size with incomplete preference lists and ties, deciding whether there is a perfect weakly stable matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Weak stability means that no mutually acceptable pair strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners. This is Theorem 1 of [Iwama–Manlove–Miyazaki–Morita, *Stable Marriage with Incomplete Lists and Ties*](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/1066/1/ICALP99.pdf); their definition explicitly requires the matching to be perfect.

We can restrict the source problem to instances whose acceptability graph admits a perfect matching. To justify this restriction, test for a perfect matching in polynomial time. If none exists, replace the instance by this fixed negative instance:

  • The left agents are \(\alpha,\beta\), and the right agents are \(u,v\).
  • Preferences are \(\alpha:u\succ v\), \(\beta:u\), \(u:\alpha\succ\beta\), and \(v:\alpha\).

Its unique perfect matching is \(\{(\alpha,v),(\beta,u)\}\), blocked by \((\alpha,u)\). Thus the replacement preserves the answer and has a perfect acceptable matching. Every vertex in every resulting source instance consequently has a nonempty acceptable set.

Let the resulting source instance have \(\ n\) agents on each side. Pad it with isolated acceptable pairs until each side has size \(\ N\), where \(\ N\) is the least power of two satisfying \(\ N\ge n\). Each added agent accepts only its paired partner. Padding preserves perfect weakly stable matching existence, and \(\ N<2n\).

2. Construction of the mass instance

Create one type for each left agent and one college for each right agent. Copy acceptability and preferences, including ties. Set

\(\displaystyle m=\tau=N,\qquad d=1,\qquad \mu_\theta=\frac1N,\qquad a_\theta[1]=1.\)

For every college, set

\(\displaystyle q_w=\ell_{w1}=u_{w1}=\frac1N.\)

These numbers are dyadic rationals. They satisfy all stated input conditions.

Every feasible assignment satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_\theta x_{\theta,w}=\frac1N\quad\text{for every }w.\)

Summing over the colleges gives total assigned mass \(\ 1\). Since the total available mass is also \(\ 1\), and no type can exceed its available mass, necessarily

\(\displaystyle \sum_w x_{\theta,w}=\frac1N,\qquad x_{\theta,\perp}=0\quad\text{for every }\theta.\)

The constructed instance is guaranteed feasible: any perfect matching in the source acceptability graph supplies a feasible assignment by putting mass \(\ 1/N\) on each matched pair.

3. Equivalence of stable existence

First suppose the source instance has a perfect weakly stable matching \(\ M\). Define \(\ x_{\theta,M(\theta)}=1/N\), with every other entry zero.

Suppose this mass assignment has a blocking opportunity at \(\ (\theta,w)\). Its positive source mass must be at \(\ k=M(\theta)\), so \(\ w\succ_\theta M(\theta)\). Let \(\ s=M^{-1}(w)\) be the unique incumbent at \(\ w\).

The college initially contains exactly \(\ 1/N\) mass. Its exact enrollment quota requires

\(\displaystyle \sum_t y_t=\delta>0.\)

Only \(\ s\) has positive mass there. Hence \(\ y_s>0\), which is permitted only if \(\ \theta\succ_w s\). Thus \(\ (\theta,w)\) blocks \(\ M\), contradicting weak stability. Therefore the mass assignment is stable.

Conversely, suppose the constructed instance has a feasible stable mass assignment \(\ x\). Form the bipartite graph \(\ H\) whose edges are precisely the pairs with \(\ x_{\theta,w}>0\).

For any set \(\ S\) of types, let \(\ \Gamma_H(S)\) denote its college neighbors. The equal row and column sums give

\(\displaystyle \frac{|S|}{N} =\sum_{\theta\in S}\sum_{w\in\Gamma_H(S)}x_{\theta,w} \le\sum_{w\in\Gamma_H(S)}\sum_\theta x_{\theta,w} =\frac{|\Gamma_H(S)|}{N}. \)

Therefore \(\ |\Gamma_H(S)|\ge |S|\) for every \(\ S\). Hall’s theorem supplies a perfect matching \(\ M\) contained in the positive support of \(\ x\).

We claim that \(\ M\) is weakly stable. Otherwise an acceptable pair \(\ (\theta,w)\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle w\succ_\theta M(\theta),\qquad \theta\succ_w M^{-1}(w).\)

Write \(\ k=M(\theta)\) and \(\ s=M^{-1}(w)\). Because \(\ M\) lies in the positive support,

\(\displaystyle \delta=\min\{x_{\theta,k},x_{s,w}\}>0.\)

Move this mass from \(\ k\) to \(\ w\), removing exactly \(\ \delta\) mass of \(\ s\) at \(\ w\). The removal is allowed because \(\ \theta\succ_w s\). The destination’s total enrollment and attribute mass remain exactly \(\ 1/N\), so all its constraints remain satisfied. This is a blocking opportunity for \(\ x\), a contradiction.

Consequently, the source instance has a perfect weakly stable matching if and only if the constructed instance has a feasible stable mass assignment. The construction has polynomial size and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Polynomial verification of rational assignments

Let a rational assignment \(\ x\) have total encoding length \(\ B\). First check feasibility by exact rational arithmetic.

For each acceptable pair \(\ (\theta,w)\) and each \(\ k\in W\cup\{\perp\}\) satisfying \(\ w\succ_\theta k\), solve the following linear program:

  • Maximize \(\ \delta\).
  • Impose \(\ 0\le\delta\le x_{\theta,k}\).
  • Impose \(\ 0\le y_s\le x_{s,w}\), with \(\ y_s=0\) unless \(\ \theta\succ_w s\).
  • Require the destination vector \(\ x_{s,w}-y_s+\delta\mathbf1_{s=\theta}\) to satisfy the capacity and all attribute quotas at \(\ w\).

The zero solution is feasible. The program is bounded, and its optimum is positive exactly when this triple admits a blocking opportunity. This equivalence follows directly from the definition; no source-college constraint is introduced.

There are at most \(\ \tau m(m+1)\) such programs, each with at most \(\ \tau+1\) variables and \(\ O(\tau+d)\) constraints. Exact rational linear programming therefore verifies stability in time

\(\displaystyle \tau m(m+1)\operatorname{poly}(\tau+d+L+B).\)

It remains to show that allowing arbitrary real assignments does not prevent polynomial certificates.

5. Polynomial-size rational certificates

Include unmatched masses as variables and impose the equalities

\(\displaystyle \sum_{w\in W}x_{\theta,w}+x_{\theta,\perp}=\mu_\theta.\)

Omit unacceptable college coordinates. Let \(\ P\) be the resulting feasible polytope. Its inequalities consist of coordinate nonnegativity, capacities, and the two bounds for every attribute quota.

We first prove a structural fact:

Blocking depends only on which coordinates are positive and which capacity and quota inequalities are tight.

Indeed, let \(\ x,z\in P\) have identical such patterns, and suppose \(\ x\) has a blocking opportunity with parameters \(\ \delta,y\), source \(\ k\), and destination \(\ w\). Define its destination direction by

\(\displaystyle v_s=\delta\mathbf1_{s=\theta}-y_s.\)

Every destination inequality tight at \(\ x\) has nonpositive change along \(\ v\), because the proposed destination vector is feasible. The same inequalities are tight at \(\ z\), so they remain satisfied along \(\ z_w+t v\) for every \(\ t\ge0\).

Every other destination inequality has strictly positive slack at \(\ z\), so it remains satisfied for sufficiently small positive \(\ t\). Moreover:

  • \(\ z_{\theta,k}>0\), since \(\ x_{\theta,k}>0\);
  • whenever \(\ y_s>0\), also \(\ z_{s,w}>0\).

Thus we can choose \(\ t>0\) small enough that \(\ t\delta\le z_{\theta,k}\) and \(\ ty_s\le z_{s,w}\) for every removal. The scaled parameters \(\ t\delta,ty\) give a blocking opportunity at \(\ z\). Interchanging \(\ x,z\) proves the structural fact.

Now suppose a real stable point \(\ x\in P\) exists. Write the inequalities of \(\ P\) as \(\ A_i z\le b_i\), and let \(\ I\) be those tight at \(\ x\). Consider the rational linear program maximizing \(\ \varepsilon\), subject to the mass equalities and

\(\displaystyle A_i z=b_i\ \ (i\in I),\qquad A_j z+\varepsilon\le b_j\ \ (j\notin I),\qquad 0\le\varepsilon\le1. \)

This program has a feasible point with \(\ \varepsilon>0\): take \(\ z=x\) and choose \(\ \varepsilon\) smaller than every positive slack. Its feasible region is bounded because \(\ z\in P\) and every coordinate lies in \(\ [0,1]\).

Hence it has an optimal rational vertex \(\ (z^*,\varepsilon^*)\) with \(\ \varepsilon^*>0\). The point \(\ z^*\) has exactly the same tight inequalities, and therefore the same positive coordinates, as \(\ x\). By the structural fact, \(\ z^*\) is stable.

For completeness, let \(\ n_P\le\tau(m+1)\) be the number of assignment coordinates. All coefficients of the auxiliary program belong to \(\ \{-1,0,1\}\). A common denominator for its rational right-hand sides has bit length \(\ O(L)\). Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound therefore give coordinate bit lengths

\(\displaystyle O\bigl(L+(n_P+1)\log(n_P+2)\bigr).\)

The total encoding length of \(\ z^*\) is consequently polynomial in \(\ m,\tau,d,L\). Together with polynomial verification, this proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

6. Running times and scope

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Its nondeterministic running time is \(\ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,d,L)\); a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm would imply \(\ \mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

An explicit deterministic upper bound follows by enumerating tight-inequality patterns. There are at most

\(\displaystyle h=\tau(m+1)+m(2d+1)\)

inequalities. For each of the \(\ 2^h\) patterns, solve the auxiliary slack program above and, if its optimum is positive, verify the resulting rational point. The structural fact proves this procedure complete. Its running time is

\(\displaystyle 2^{\,O(m\tau+md)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,d,L).\)

The reduction settles the exact model asked about, including its real masses and destination-only blocking condition. It does not assert the additional discrete restrictions of strict preferences or four colleges.
