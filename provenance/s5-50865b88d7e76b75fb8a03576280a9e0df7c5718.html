The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets on both sides, rational type masses, compatible contracts, and type-level valuations and thresholds, with each type represented by an atomless cohort, find a γ-popular matching whose matched mass is at least ¾ of the maximum possible. Popularity is tested against every feasible alternative matching on the same cohort members: a vote is zero exactly when the same individual contract pair is retained, and otherwise follows the paper’s thresholded vote rule. The matching and challenger must be represented by a joint coupling that records exact pairs retained and makes both matchings jointly feasible.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: finite disjoint type sets \(A,B\), with \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) positive masses \(q_t\), each a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses need not sum to one, and the two sides may have different total masses. Type \(t\) is an atomless Lebesgue interval of length \(q_t\).
There are \(m\) explicitly listed compatible contract labels \(e\), each joining one type in \(A\) to one in \(B\); parallel labels are allowed. Any two individuals of the specified types may use that label. Each endpoint type \(t\) has binary-rational valuation \(p_t(e)\ge0\) and threshold \(\gamma_e^t>0\); equal valuations are allowed. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible matching is a measurable, measure-preserving partial bijection between the two sides, assigning one compatible contract label to each paired individual. Its size \(s(M)\) is the paired mass counted once. An output consists of rational contract masses, realized by partitioning cohorts into labelled intervals and pairing equal-length intervals by translation.
For every challenger \(N\), comparisons use a joint coupling realized by both partial bijections on the same cohort members, recording both partners and labels and which exact individual pairs retain their label. Both matchings must be jointly feasible; challengers may be arbitrary measurable feasible matchings.
An individual of type \(t\) votes \(0\) if the same partner and label are retained or if unmatched in both matchings. Otherwise, becoming matched gives vote \(-1\), becoming unmatched gives \(+1\), and changing between contracts \(e\) in \(M\) and \(f\) in \(N\) gives \(-1\) exactly when \(p_t(f)\ge p_t(e)+\gamma_f^t\), and \(+1\) otherwise.
A matching is \(\gamma\)-popular if the integral of these votes over both sides is nonnegative against every challenger. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum of sizes of feasible \(\gamma\)-popular matchings, taking \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\) if none exist.

Can a \(\gamma\)-popular matching \(M\) with \(s(M)\ge\frac34\mathrm{OPT}\) always be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this approximation NP-hard?

For finite agents, a polynomial-time \(\frac34\)-approximation exists; exact maximization is NP-hard even with one-sided ties, and any \(\frac34+\varepsilon\) approximation is NP-hard under strong-UGC or SSEH. [Verified discrete results](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12269v4).
No result settling this exact atomless, jointly coupled approximation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted maximum possible mass as the optimum among gamma-popular matchings, following the discrete approximation guarantee, rather than among all feasible matchings.
  • Allowed arbitrary positive rational masses without normalization or equal side totals, preserving the discrete allowance for unequal sides.
  • Required binary-rational nonnegative valuations and strictly positive thresholds to give a finite computational input.
  • Interpreted compatibility as permitting every individual pair of the specified endpoint types, with multiple contract labels allowed.
  • Used atomless Lebesgue intervals and measure-preserving partial bijections; joint couplings must be realizable by both matchings on the same individuals.
  • Specified a finite rational output encoding through contract masses and interval pairings.
  • Included zero votes for individuals unmatched in both matchings and explicit votes for becoming matched or unmatched, preserving the unmatched valuation convention.
  • Used a supremum for the popularity-constrained benchmark to avoid assuming attainment; defined it as zero when no popular matching exists.

Result

A \(\frac34\)-approximation is computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof combines the cited finite six-copy construction with two facts proved below:

  • Atomless popularity has an exact finite linear-programming characterization that respects joint feasibility and partner retention.
  • Every popular mass vector has a popular vertex of the mass-feasibility polytope with at least as much size. Such vertices lie on the rational grid determined by the input masses.

The potentially exponential population obtained by discretizing that grid is used only in the proof. The computation takes place on a network with \(O(m+\tau)\) vertices and edges.

1. An exact linear program for jointly feasible comparisons

Write \(E(t)\) for the labels incident to type \(t\). The feasible contract-mass polytope is

\(\displaystyle X=\left\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m}:\sum_{e\in E(t)}x_e\le q_t\text{ for every }t\right\}.\)

Every \(x\in X\) is realizable by the interval construction specified in the question. Its size is \(S(x)=\sum_e x_e\).

Fix any measurable incumbent matching \(M\) with mass vector \(x\). For each type, introduce incumbent states

\(\displaystyle S_t=\{0\}\cup E(t),\)

where state \(0\) means unmatched and state \(e\) means matched under label \(e\). Their masses are

\(\displaystyle b_{t,0}(x)=q_t-\sum_{e\in E(t)}x_e,\qquad b_{t,e}(x)=x_e.\)

For a new label \(f\in E(t)\), define the vote for an individual whose pair and label are not retained:

\(\displaystyle c_t(i,f)= \begin{cases} -1,&i=0,\\ -1,&i\ne0\text{ and }p_t(f)\ge p_t(i)+\gamma_f^t,\\ +1,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\)

Also put \(d_0=0\) and \(d_i=1\) for \(i\ne0\). Thus \(d_i\) is the vote when the challenger leaves that individual unmatched.

The comparison LP has nonnegative variables:

  • \(r_e\): mass of exact incumbent pairs retained with label \(e\);
  • \(y^f_{ij}\), for \(f=(a,b)\), \(i\in S_a\), \(j\in S_b\): challenger pairs under label \(f\), apart from the explicitly retained pairs;
  • \(z_{t,i}\): individuals in incumbent state \(i\) left unmatched by the challenger.

Set \(r_0=0\). The constraints are

\(\displaystyle z_{a,i}+r_i+ \sum_{f=(a,b)}\sum_{j\in S_b}y^f_{ij} =b_{a,i}(x) \qquad(a\in A,\ i\in S_a), \)

and

\(\displaystyle z_{b,j}+r_j+ \sum_{f=(a,b)}\sum_{i\in S_a}y^f_{ij} =b_{b,j}(x) \qquad(b\in B,\ j\in S_b). \)

Crucially, the same variable \(r_e\) occurs at both endpoints of label \(e\).

Minimize

\(\displaystyle C(r,y,z)= \sum_{f=(a,b)}\sum_{i\in S_a}\sum_{j\in S_b} \bigl(c_a(i,f)+c_b(j,f)\bigr)y^f_{ij} + \sum_t\sum_{i\in S_t}d_i z_{t,i}. \)

Denote the minimum by \(\Phi(x)\). The LP is feasible: take \(r_e=x_e\), \(z_{t,0}=b_{t,0}(x)\), and all other variables zero. Its feasible region is bounded, so the minimum is attained. In particular,

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x)\le0.\)

Lemma 1: Exactness

For every realization \(M\) of \(x\),

\(\displaystyle \min_N\Delta(M,N)=\Phi(x),\)

where the minimum ranges over all measurable feasible challengers on the same individuals.

Proof. Any actual challenger defines feasible LP variables by recording exact retained pairs, other challenger pairs classified by both incumbent states, and challenger-unmatched individuals. Its vote integral equals the LP objective. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \Delta(M,N)\ge\Phi(x)\)

for every challenger.

Conversely, take an optimal LP solution. For each label \(e\), choose incumbent pairs of total mass \(r_e\) and retain them. This is possible because their parameter space is atomless; retaining those pairs removes equal masses from the two endpoint states.

Partition each remaining state into pieces of the prescribed \(y\)- and \(z\)-masses. Pair corresponding \(y\)-pieces using measure-preserving bijections and their specified labels. Atomless measurable subsets of Lebesgue intervals are measure-isomorphic, modulo null sets, to intervals of the same measure. Hence these pairings are measurable and jointly feasible with the fixed incumbent \(M\).

Some newly paired individuals might accidentally retain their exact incumbent pair and label. This can only decrease the objective’s predicted vote: when the incumbent and challenger labels are both \(e\), positivity of \(\gamma_e^t\) gives \(c_t(e,e)=+1\), whereas exact retention gives \(0\) at both endpoints. All other votes are as predicted.

The constructed challenger therefore satisfies

\(\displaystyle \Delta(M,N)\le C(r,y,z)=\Phi(x).\)

Together with the opposite inequality, this proves equality. \(\square\)

Thus popularity depends only on contract masses, despite the requirement of a jointly feasible comparison:

\(\displaystyle x\text{ is popular}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Phi(x)=0.\)

This LP also gives polynomial-time verification of a rational candidate. It has \(\tau+2m\) state constraints and \(O(m^3+m+\tau)\) variables.

2. Popularity forces a popular vertex of at least the same size

Lemma 2: Convexity

The function \(\Phi\) is convex on \(X\).

Proof. Its constraint matrix and objective coefficients are independent of \(x\), while its right-hand side \(b(x)\) is affine.

If \(u_1,u_2\) are optimal LP solutions for \(x_1,x_2\), then \(\lambda u_1+(1-\lambda)u_2\) is feasible for \(\lambda x_1+(1-\lambda)x_2\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \Phi\bigl(\lambda x_1+(1-\lambda)x_2\bigr) \le \lambda\Phi(x_1)+(1-\lambda)\Phi(x_2) \)

for \(0\le\lambda\le1\). \(\square\)

Lemma 3: Popular vertex domination

For every popular \(x\in X\), there is a popular vertex \(v\) of \(X\) with \(S(v)\ge S(x)\).

Proof. Since \(X\) is a bounded polytope, write

\(\displaystyle x=\sum_{k=1}^{h}\lambda_k v^k,\qquad \lambda_k>0,\qquad\sum_k\lambda_k=1,\)

where each \(v^k\) is a vertex. Convexity and \(\Phi\le0\) imply

\(\displaystyle 0=\Phi(x) \le\sum_k\lambda_k\Phi(v^k) \le0. \)

Hence \(\Phi(v^k)=0\) for every \(k\): all these vertices are popular. Since size is linear,

\(\displaystyle S(x)=\sum_k\lambda_k S(v^k),\)

so at least one has size at least \(S(x)\). \(\square\)

Choose a common denominator \(D\) for the masses \(q_t\), for example the product of their encoded denominators. Then \(n_t=Dq_t\) is integral, and \(D\) has polynomial bit length.

The constraint matrix of \(X\) is the incidence matrix of a bipartite multigraph. Multiplying the rows indexed by \(B\) by \(-1\) turns its columns into directed incidence columns. It is therefore totally unimodular, including when labels are parallel. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle Dv_e\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\)

for every vertex \(v\) of \(X\).

It follows that, whenever popular matchings exist, the supremum defining \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is attained at a popular vertex on this input grid.

3. Exact equivalence with finite clones on the grid

Define a finite instance \(\mathcal I_D\) containing \(n_t=Dq_t\) individual clones of type \(t\). Every eligible clone pair has every compatible original label, with the original type valuations and thresholds.

Any grid vector \(x\), meaning \(Dx_e\) is integral for every \(e\), can be realized by a finite matching having \(Dx_e\) pairs under label \(e\).

Lemma 4: Finite–atomless equivalence

For any such finite incumbent \(M_D\),

\(\displaystyle \min_{N_D}\Delta(M_D,N_D)=D\Phi(x). \)

In particular, \(M_D\) is popular exactly when the atomless mass vector \(x\) is popular.

Proof. Apply the comparison LP with state capacities multiplied by \(D\). Its optimal value is \(D\Phi(x)\).

Its constraint matrix is totally unimodular. Indeed, after negating the \(B\)-state rows:

  • each \(r\)- or \(y\)-column has one \(+1\) and one \(-1\);
  • each \(z\)-column has a single nonzero entry.

This is a directed incidence matrix with singleton columns, and hence is totally unimodular. The right-hand side is integral, so an integral optimum exists.

Every finite challenger gives a feasible LP solution with exactly its vote total. Thus its vote is at least \(D\Phi(x)\).

Conversely, realize an integral LP optimum on the clones. Retain \(r_e\) existing pairs of each label, allocate remaining individuals according to the integral \(y\)- and \(z\)-counts, and pair the allocated groups arbitrarily. Any accidental additional retention decreases the predicted vote, exactly as in Lemma 1. The resulting finite challenger therefore has vote at most \(D\Phi(x)\).

The two inequalities establish equality. \(\square\)

The clone instance can be exponentially large. We will not construct it.

4. The six-copy construction

For each original label \(e\), introduce auxiliary labels \(a(e),b(e),c(e),x(e),y(e),z(e)\). Their endpoint orders have blocks

\(\displaystyle A:\quad(a,b)\succ c\succ(x,y)\succ z,\)

\(\displaystyle B:\quad(z,y)\succ x\succ(c,b)\succ a.\)

Within a mixed block \((\alpha,\beta)\), sort \(\alpha(e)\) by \(p_t(e)\) and \(\beta(e)\) by \(p_t(e)-\gamma_e^t\), descending; at equal scores put \(\beta\) first. Single-copy blocks use descending valuations. Break remaining ties arbitrarily. Thus

\(\displaystyle \beta(f)\succ_t\alpha(e) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_t(f)\ge p_t(e)+\gamma_f^t. \)

We use the finite six-copy projection theorem: the projection \(R\) of any stable auxiliary matching is popular and satisfies

\(\displaystyle |R|\ge\frac34|N|\)

for every popular finite matching \(N\). The proof uses only auxiliary stability. This is the construction and guarantee in [Csáji–Glitzner, Section 3.2, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.12269v4#S3.SS2).

5. Computing an auxiliary stable allocation in polynomial time

Let \(G'\) be the auxiliary label multigraph, with its strict endpoint orders. For an allocation \(h\), define

\(\displaystyle W_t(g;h)=q_t-\sum_{\ell\succeq_t g}h_\ell. \)

This is the mass at type \(t\) that is unmatched or assigned an auxiliary label worse than \(g\). Call \(h\) stable if, for every \(g=(a,b)\),

\(\displaystyle W_a(g;h)=0\quad\text{or}\quad W_b(g;h)=0.\)

We now compute such an allocation compactly.

If there are no original labels, return the empty matching. Otherwise set \(Q=\sum_t q_t\). Introduce source \(\sigma\), sink \(\omega\), and, for each auxiliary label \(g=(a,b)\), a separate intermediate vertex \(w_g\). Add arcs

\(\displaystyle \sigma\longrightarrow a,\qquad a\longrightarrow w_g\longrightarrow b,\qquad b\longrightarrow\omega. \)

The type arcs have capacities \(q_a,q_b\); both arcs through \(w_g\) have capacity \(Q\). Omit types incident to no labels.

At \(a\), rank outgoing contract arcs by its auxiliary order. At \(b\), rank incoming contract arcs by its auxiliary order. All other nonterminal incoming or outgoing lists are singletons. This is a simple directed network of polynomial size, even when original labels are parallel.

We use the polynomial stable-flow algorithm of [Cseh–Matuschke, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3701). Stability forbids an unsaturated directed walk whose start is the source or prefers its first arc to a positive-flow outgoing arc, and whose end is the sink or prefers its last arc to a positive-flow incoming arc. Their augmentation algorithm runs in \(O(|V||E|)\) arithmetic operations. Starting from zero, it uses residual-capacity bottlenecks and additions/subtractions, so it preserves the grid \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z\).

Let \(F\) be the resulting stable flow and put

\(\displaystyle h_g=F(a,w_g)=F(w_g,b).\)

Flow conservation gives the equality, and the type-arc capacities give feasibility.

Lemma 5: Stability of \(h\)

The allocation \(h\) is stable in the above sense.

Proof. Suppose both \(W_a(g;h)\) and \(W_b(g;h)\) are positive. At each endpoint there is either unused quota or positive allocation to a worse label.

Both contract arcs through \(w_g\) are unsaturated, since

\(\displaystyle h_g\le\min(q_a,q_b)<Q.\)

Start the walk at \(\sigma\) if \(a\) has unused quota, and otherwise at \(a\), where a worse positive-flow outgoing arc witnesses willingness. End at \(\omega\) if \(b\) has unused quota, and otherwise at \(b\), where a worse positive-flow incoming arc witnesses willingness.

All traversed arcs are unsaturated, and these endpoint witnesses satisfy precisely the stable-flow blocking conditions. This contradicts stability of \(F\). \(\square\)

Moreover, every \(Dh_g\) is integral.

6. Lifting the allocation to apply the finite theorem

Conceptually partition the clones of each type into auxiliary-label groups of sizes \(Dh_g\), leaving the remaining clones unmatched. Pair corresponding groups to obtain an auxiliary finite matching \(H_D\).

For each clone, order gadget-label groups by the strict type order. Within a group containing that clone’s incumbent edge, put its incumbent edge first; order the other edges arbitrarily. These refinements are permitted by the six-copy construction: they only refine equal-valued copies within one gadget label.

Lemma 6: \(H_D\) is stable

Proof. Suppose an auxiliary edge of gadget label \(g\) blocks \(H_D\).

An endpoint currently assigned gadget label \(g\) cannot prefer another edge in that group, because its incumbent edge was placed first. Therefore both endpoints are unmatched or assigned gadget labels strictly worse than \(g\).

Their existence implies positive willing mass at both endpoint types:

\(\displaystyle W_a(g;h)>0,\qquad W_b(g;h)>0.\)

This contradicts Lemma 5. \(\square\)

The finite theorem’s hypotheses hold: the instance is bipartite, parallel contracts are allowed, valuations are nonnegative, thresholds are positive, and the refinements respect the prescribed auxiliary orders.

Project \(H_D\) to its original labels, obtaining \(R_D\). Define the algorithm’s output masses by

\(\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{out}}_e=\sum_{\alpha\in\{a,b,c,x,y,z\}}h_{\alpha(e)}.\)

The finite theorem makes \(R_D\) popular. Lemma 4 therefore makes \(x^{\mathrm{out}}\) atomlessly popular, against every measurable jointly feasible challenger.

In particular, popular matchings always exist in the stated atomless model.

7. Approximation guarantee and output complexity

By Lemma 3 and grid integrality, choose a popular vertex \(v^\ast\) with

\(\displaystyle S(v^\ast)=\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Realize its grid counts as a finite matching \(N_D^\ast\). Lemma 4 makes this matching popular, and its cardinality is \(D\,\mathrm{OPT}\). Applying the finite theorem to \(R_D\) gives

\(\displaystyle D\,S(x^{\mathrm{out}}) =|R_D| \ge\frac34|N_D^\ast| =\frac34D\,\mathrm{OPT}. \)

Dividing by \(D>0\) proves the required guarantee.

The actual computation consists of sorting \(6m\) auxiliary labels, constructing a network with \(O(m+\tau)\) vertices and arcs, computing a stable flow, and summing the six masses belonging to each original label. The integer capacities after scaling by \(D\) have polynomial bit length. Grid preservation and bounded capacities keep all intermediate and output numbers at polynomial bit length.

Finally, partition each type interval into pieces of lengths \(x^{\mathrm{out}}_e\), together with its unmatched remainder, and pair corresponding label pieces by translation. Lemma 1 ensures that this specified interval realization is popular.

Thus the requested matching and its rational interval realization are computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).
