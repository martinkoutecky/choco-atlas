The anchor (named result in the paper)

Proposition 4.2 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two finite-support societies μ and ν on \(D × \{1,\ldots,H\}\), with rational incomes and binary-rational masses, decide whether ν is at least as good as μ for every \(U \in U_{22}\): equivalently, whether all three inequalities in Proposition 4.2 hold for every income threshold and ability prefix.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Inputs are two finite-support probability societies \(\mu,\nu\) on \(D\times\{1,\ldots,H\}\), where \(D=[a,b]\), \(a<b\) are rational, and \(H\ge2\) is a binary-encoded integer. Incomes may have either sign; higher ability labels mean greater ability, with consecutive labels equally spaced.
Each society is given by its support: the list of types \((x,h)\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; its masses sum to \(1\). Write \(\tau_\rho=|\operatorname{supp}\rho|\) and \(\tau=\tau_\mu+\tau_\nu\): these are support sizes, not numbers of possible types. Let \(m\) count distinct supported incomes and \(L\) be the total input bit length, including endpoints and ability labels; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any dependence on \(H\).
For \(\rho\in\{\mu,\nu\}\), define \(F_\rho(y,h)=\rho(\{(x,k):x\le y,\ k\le h\})\) and \(W_U(\rho)=\sum_{(x,h)\in\operatorname{supp}\rho}\rho(x,h)U(x,h)\).
Define \(\Delta_h V(y,h)=V(y,h+1)-V(y,h)\). The class \(\mathcal U_{22}\) consists of real-valued utilities twice continuously differentiable in income satisfying \(\Delta_h U\ge0,\ U_y\ge0,\ \Delta_h U_y\le0,\ U_{yy}\le0,\ \Delta_h U_{yy}\ge0,\ \Delta_h^2 U\le0,\ \Delta_h^2 U_y\ge0,\ \Delta_h^2 U_{yy}\le0\), everywhere on \(D\) and wherever the ability differences are defined.
Decide whether \(W_U(\nu)\ge W_U(\mu)\) for every \(U\in\mathcal U_{22}\); equivalently, whether the following three weak inequalities hold:
\(\sum_{r=1}^{h}F_\nu(b,r)\le\sum_{r=1}^{h}F_\mu(b,r)\) for every \(1\le h\le H\);
\(\int_a^y F_\nu(t,H)\,dt\le\int_a^y F_\mu(t,H)\,dt\) for every \(y\in D\);
\(\sum_{r=1}^{h}\int_a^y F_\nu(t,r)\,dt\le\sum_{r=1}^{h}\int_a^y F_\mu(t,r)\,dt\) for every \(y\in D\) and \(1\le h\le H\).

Is this dominance decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), with polynomial running time in the binary input length \(L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agent lists with equal individual weights, utilitarian unanimity over \(\mathcal U_{22}\) is equivalent to these three inequalities; this is a characterization, without a stated bit-complexity classification. [Verified characterization](https://cahiersdugretha.u-bordeaux.fr/2011/2011-23.pdf).
No explicit complexity classification for this exact rational-mass input model was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each society to total mass one, following average utilitarian welfare and the population-replication convention.
  • Specified the previously undefined income domain as a common nondegenerate compact interval with rational input endpoints; allowed signed incomes because the discrete domain imposes no positivity restriction.
  • Retained at least two ability levels and the equally spaced integer ability scale; allowed unused ability labels and encoded H in binary because no finite population size remains to bound it.
  • Specified twice continuously differentiable, unrestricted real-valued utilities with all eight weak sign conditions; difference conditions apply only where their indices exist.
  • Defined support types as income–ability pairs, total support size across the two inputs, distinct-income count m, and total binary input length L.
  • Used inclusive joint cumulative distributions and weak dominance inequalities, without imposing equal income means or equal ability marginals.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result and input interpretation

The dominance decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). An exact algorithm uses
\(O(m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1))\) arithmetic operations and has bit running time
\(O((m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1))L^2)\) using elementary integer arithmetic. There is no multiplicative dependence on \(H\); its binary encoding is accounted for in \(L\).

I use the intended rational input model: supported incomes, as well as masses and endpoints, are encoded as binary rationals. Without a specified encoding of supported incomes, a bit-complexity question would need further specification.

1. Reduction to signed hinge sums

Keep the two support lists as separate records, and enumerate their combined \(\tau\) records by \((x_i,k_i,s_i)\), where \(s_i\) is the positive mass for a record from \(\nu\) and the negative mass for a record from \(\mu\). Thus \(\sum_i s_i=0\). Identical types in the two societies need not be merged.

Write \(u_+=\max\{u,0\}\). Define three real-valued functions:

\( \displaystyle A(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}s_i(z-k_i+1)_+, \qquad 0\le z\le H, \)

\( \displaystyle B(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}s_i(y-x_i)_+, \qquad a\le y\le b, \)

\( \displaystyle C(y,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}s_i(y-x_i)_+(z-k_i+1)_+, \qquad (y,z)\in[a,b]\times[0,H]. \)

For an integer \(h\in\{1,\ldots,H\}\), a record of ability \(k_i\) contributes to \(F_\nu(b,r)-F_\mu(b,r)\) precisely when \(r\ge k_i\). Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{h}\bigl(F_\nu(b,r)-F_\mu(b,r)\bigr) =\sum_i s_i(h-k_i+1)_+=A(h). \)

Because \(x_i\in[a,b]\), direct integration gives

\( \displaystyle \int_a^y \mathbf{1}_{\{x_i\le t\}}\,dt=(y-x_i)_+. \)

The value of the indicator at its jump does not affect this integral. Therefore,

\( \displaystyle \int_a^y\bigl(F_\nu(t,H)-F_\mu(t,H)\bigr)\,dt=B(y), \)

and, interchanging finite sums,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{h}\int_a^y\bigl(F_\nu(t,r)-F_\mu(t,r)\bigr)\,dt=C(y,h). \)

Thus the three conditions in the question are exactly:

  • \(A(h)\le0\) for every integer \(1\le h\le H\);
  • \(B(y)\le0\) for every \(y\in[a,b]\);
  • \(C(y,h)\le0\) for every \(y\in[a,b]\) and integer \(1\le h\le H\).

These identities use no assumption that incomes are nonnegative.

2. A finite grid suffices

Let

\( \displaystyle Y=\{a,b\}\cup\{x_i:1\le i\le\tau\}, \qquad Z=\{0,H\}\cup\{k_i-1:1\le i\le\tau\}, \)

with duplicates removed. Then \(|Y|\le m+2\) and \(|Z|\le\tau+2\).

Lemma. The three dominance conditions hold if and only if

\( \displaystyle A(z)\le0\quad(z\in Z),\qquad B(v)\le0\quad(v\in Y),\qquad C(v,z)\le0\quad((v,z)\in Y\times Z). \)

Proof. Each function \(z\mapsto(z-k_i+1)_+\) is continuous and affine between consecutive elements of \(Z\). Hence \(A\) is affine on every such interval. If \(z_0<z_1\) are consecutive elements and \(z=(1-\lambda)z_0+\lambda z_1\), with \(0\le\lambda\le1\), then

\( \displaystyle A(z)=(1-\lambda)A(z_0)+\lambda A(z_1). \)

Nonpositive endpoint values therefore imply \(A(z)\le0\) throughout the interval, including all integer labels it contains.

Similarly, each income hinge \(y\mapsto(y-x_i)_+\) is continuous and affine between consecutive elements of \(Y\). Endpoint checks therefore establish \(B(y)\le0\) throughout \([a,b]\).

For \(C\), first fix \(v\in Y\). As a function of \(z\), \(C(v,z)\) is affine between consecutive elements of \(Z\). The grid checks imply \(C(v,z)\le0\) for every \(z\in[0,H]\).

Now fix any such \(z\). Between consecutive income points \(v_0<v_1\), write \(y=(1-\theta)v_0+\theta v_1\). Affineness in income gives

\( \displaystyle C(y,z)=(1-\theta)C(v_0,z)+\theta C(v_1,z)\le0. \)

This proves sufficiency.

Conversely, every element of \(Z\setminus\{0\}\) is an integer in \(\{1,\ldots,H\}\), and every element of \(Y\) lies in \([a,b]\). Thus the original conditions imply all grid checks except those at \(z=0\). Those additional checks hold automatically because \(k_i\ge1\) implies \(A(0)=C(v,0)=0\). This proves necessity. \(\square\)

The ability breakpoints are the actual values \(k_i-1\). Their numerical gaps are retained; no iteration over all \(H\) labels is needed.

3. Evaluating the grid efficiently

Sort the records once by ability and construct sorted, deduplicated lists \(Y\) and \(Z\). This takes \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) comparisons.

For each \(v\in Y\), compute

\( \displaystyle c_i(v)=s_i(v-x_i)_+\qquad(1\le i\le\tau). \)

Then \(B(v)=\sum_i c_i(v)\), so its test requires \(O(\tau)\) operations.

To evaluate all \(C(v,z)\) for this fixed \(v\), scan \(Z\) in increasing order while scanning the ability-sorted records. Maintain

\( \displaystyle P=\sum_{k_i\le z}c_i(v),\qquad R=\sum_{k_i\le z}k_i c_i(v). \)

At an integer \(z\), records with \(k_i>z\) have zero ability hinge. Thus

\( \displaystyle C(v,z)=(z+1)P-R. \)

Each record enters the maintained sums once. Evaluating every \(z\in Z\) therefore takes \(O(\tau+|Z|)=O(\tau)\) operations.

The same scan, using coefficients \(s_i\) instead of \(c_i(v)\), evaluates all \(A(z)\) in \(O(\tau)\) operations.

The algorithm rejects upon finding a positive checked value and otherwise accepts. By the lemma, it decides exactly the three inequalities. Its arithmetic-operation count is

\( \displaystyle O\bigl(m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1)\bigr). \)

4. Exact bit complexity

Assume the usual explicit binary numerator-denominator encoding of rationals. Let \(M\) be the product of all mass denominators, and let \(X\) be the product of all denominators of supported incomes and endpoints. Both are positive integers of bit length \(O(L)\): the bit length of a product is bounded by the sum of the factor bit lengths.

Define integers

\( \displaystyle p_i=Ms_i,\qquad \xi_i=Xx_i,\qquad \eta_v=Xv. \)

They all have bit length \(O(L)\). The signs of the grid values can be determined from the following integer expressions:

\( \displaystyle MA(z)=\sum_i p_i(z-k_i+1)_+, \)

\( \displaystyle MXB(v)=\sum_i p_i(\eta_v-\xi_i)_+, \)

\( \displaystyle MXC(v,z)=\sum_i p_i(\eta_v-\xi_i)_+(z-k_i+1)_+. \)

The scaling factors are positive, so these expressions preserve signs exactly.

Implement the scans from the preceding section with the integer coefficients
\(p_i(\eta_v-\xi_i)_+\). Every intermediate integer has \(O(L)\) bits:

  • each input numerator, denominator, income magnitude, and ability label has at most \(L\) bits;
  • each term multiplies only a constant number of integers of \(O(L)\) bits;
  • summing at most \(\tau\) terms adds at most \(O(\log\tau)\) bits, which is \(O(L)\).

Building the common denominators and scaled coordinates takes \(O(\tau)\) elementary integer operations. Sorting rational incomes can use exact cross multiplication.

With elementary multiplication and division, each operation on these integers costs at most \(O(L^2)\) bit operations. Consequently the total bit running time is

\( \displaystyle O\bigl((m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1))L^2\bigr). \)

Because \(m\le\tau=O(L)\), this is polynomial in \(L\); in particular, \(O(L^4)\) is a valid coarse bound. The algorithm manipulates \(H\) and ability labels as binary integers and never performs \(H\) iterations.

5. Applicability to utilitarian unanimity

The supplied equal-weight characterization is [Proposition 4.2 of Moyes’s paper](https://cahiersdugretha.u-bordeaux.fr/2011/2011-23.pdf). Its passage to rational probability masses is exact.

Indeed, choose a positive integer \(N\) divisible by every mass denominator in both societies. Replace each supported type of mass \(q\) by \(Nq\) agents of that type. Both resulting lists contain exactly \(N\) agents, and their empirical distribution functions and average utilitarian welfare coincide with those of the original societies. If needed, enlarge \(N\) by an integer factor so that \(N\ge H\).

Applying the stated characterization to these lists gives the equivalence for rational masses. This is a mathematical reduction only: the algorithm above never constructs the potentially enormous agent lists.

Therefore the original utilitarian-unanimity decision problem is polynomial-time solvable. Under polynomial-time many-one reductions, it could also be \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Each record enters those sums once during a scan. This remains true when consecutive grid coordinates have very large numerical gaps. Sorting and scanning do not require visiting the intervening labels.

Computing all coefficients and testing \(B\) costs \(O(\tau)\) operations per income-grid point; evaluating all corresponding \(C\)-values costs another \(O(\tau)\). The separate scan for \(A\) costs \(O(\tau)\).
