The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 5 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant and vacancy type sets \(U,W\), rational masses \(a_u,b_w\) summing to \(1/2\) on each side, mutually acceptable type pairs, and old and new weak rankings, decide whether there is a new assignment within distance \(\varepsilon\) of a given old stable assignment \(x^1\). Require that ties occur only in vacancy-type rankings and have size at most two, and that the profiles differ by one adjacent swap in the ranking of one positive-mass vacancy type. An assignment \(x\) has \(x_{uw}\geq 0\), with row and column sums at most \(a_u,b_w\); it is stable in profile \(r\) if, for every acceptable \((u,w)\), at least one of \(a_u-\sum_{w':\,w'\succeq_u^r w}x_{uw'}\) and \(b_w-\sum_{u':\,u'\succeq_w^r u}x_{u'w}\) is zero. The distance condition is \(\sum_{(u,w)\text{ acceptable}}|x^2_{uw}-x^1_{uw}|\leq\varepsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: finite disjoint applicant and vacancy type sets \(U,W\), with positive binary rational masses \(a_u,b_w\) satisfying \(\sum_u a_u=\sum_w b_w=1/2\). Let \(\tau=|U|+|W|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(E\subseteq U\times W\) be the fixed mutual acceptability relation, \(m=|E|\), and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Two profiles \(r\in\{1,2\}\) rank each type's acceptable partners; applicants have strict orders, and vacancy orders have indifference classes of size at most two. Every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched; \(\succeq_t^r\) means weak preference, including indifference, without tie-breaking.
The profiles differ only by reversing two consecutive singleton entries in the ranking of one vacancy type.
An assignment has real entries \(x_{uw}\geq0\) for \((u,w)\in E\), with \(\sum_{w:(u,w)\in E}x_{uw}\leq a_u\) and \(\sum_{u:(u,w)\in E}x_{uw}\leq b_w\); unassigned mass is allowed.
It is stable in profile \(r\) if every \((u,w)\in E\) satisfies \(a_u-\sum_{w':(u,w')\in E,\ w'\succeq_u^r w}x_{uw'}=0\) or \(b_w-\sum_{u':(u',w)\in E,\ u'\succeq_w^r u}x_{u'w}=0\).
The input includes a rational assignment \(x^1\) stable in profile \(1\) and a binary rational threshold \(\varepsilon\geq0\).

Is deciding whether there exists an assignment \(x^2\) stable in profile \(2\) with \(\sum_{(u,w)\in E}|x^2_{uw}-x^1_{uw}|\leq\varepsilon\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete incremental stable marriage with ties is NP-hard with one swap; it is also \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by the symmetric-difference budget \(k\), even with one-sided ties of size at most two ([Boehmer, Heeger and Niedermeier](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.01563)); the stated hardness does not restrict the swap to the side containing ties.
For continuous allocations with strict preferences, minimum linear-cost stable allocation is polynomial-time solvable ([Dean and Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)). No result settling the precise problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted an adjacent swap literally as reversing two consecutive singleton ranking entries; ties are preserved.
  • Specified that the acceptability relation remains fixed across profiles and acceptable partners are strictly preferred to unmatched mass.
  • Specified that weak preference includes tied partners, resolving the ambiguity of the ranking symbol.
  • Specified rational encoding for the old assignment and a nonnegative binary rational distance threshold; new assignments may have arbitrary real entries.
  • Defined m as the number of acceptable type pairs and L as the total input bit length.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when all type masses are equal dyadic rationals and \(x^1\) represents a discrete matching.

Two points require proof: the discrete hardness construction can be modified to put the swap on the vacancy side, and fractional allocations cannot create additional yes-instances in the resulting reduction.

1. Moving the discrete swap to the vacancy side

We first establish the required restriction for discrete weakly stable marriage.

The reductions in Proposition 1 and Theorem 3 of [Boehmer, Heeger and Niedermeier](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.01563) produce a hard family with strict applicant preferences and vacancy ties of size at most two. Four distinct trigger agents are applicants \(p,c\) and vacancies \(q,d\). Their initial matching contains \((p,d)\) and \((c,q)\). Applicant \(c\) accepts exactly \(q,d\), and vacancy \(d\) accepts exactly \(c,p\). Their rankings are:

| Agent | Original profile \(P_1\) | Target profile \(P_2\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(c\) | \(q\succ d\) | \(d\succ q\) |
| \(d\) | \(c\succ p\) | \(c\succ p\) |

All other rankings agree between the profiles. These agents are \(m_{\mathrm{single}},m^*_{\mathrm{single}},w_{\mathrm{single}},w^*_{\mathrm{single}}\) in Proposition 1; the forced-edge replacement in Theorem 3 leaves them untouched. Ordinary \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness follows because the underlying [Marx–Schlotter construction, Theorem 3](https://www.cs.bme.hu/~ildi/pub/tie_matching.pdf), reduces from \(\mathrm{Clique}\) in polynomial time, as do the subsequent constructions.

Now replace only the initial profile by \(\widehat P_1\), as follows:

| Agent | Modified initial profile \(\widehat P_1\) | Target profile \(P_2\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(c\) | \(d\succ q\) | \(d\succ q\) |
| \(d\) | \(p\succ c\) | \(c\succ p\) |

Keep the initial matching \(M_1\), target profile, acceptability graph, and budget \(k\).

The initial matching remains stable. Only rankings of \(c,d\) changed. Their incident edges are exactly \((c,q),(c,d),(p,d)\). The first and last belong to \(M_1\). Although \(c\) now prefers \(d\) to its partner \(q\), vacancy \(d\) prefers its partner \(p\) to \(c\). Thus \((c,d)\) does not block. Every other edge has unchanged endpoint rankings and unchanged partners, so it cannot become blocking.

Moreover, \(\widehat P_1\) and \(P_2\) differ exactly by reversing the two consecutive singleton entries of vacancy \(d\). The set of candidate solutions is unchanged: it depends only on \(P_2,M_1,k\).

Consequently, discrete incremental weakly stable marriage is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under exactly the side-of-swap and tie restrictions required here.

2. A rounding lemma for the stated stability predicate

Consider a bipartite acceptability graph with capacity \(1\) at every vertex. Preferences may contain ties. For a vertex \(v\), let \(\delta(v)\) denote its incident edges.

Lemma. Let \(y\) be a stable fractional assignment with \(y(\delta(v))\leq1\), and let \(M_0\) be any matching. There exists a discrete weakly stable matching \(M\) such that
\(\|\chi_M-\chi_{M_0}\|_1\leq\|y-\chi_{M_0}\|_1\).

Proof. Define the positive support and saturated vertices by
\(S=\{e:y_e>0\}\) and \(F=\{v:y(\delta(v))=1\}\).

Consider the polytope
\[ \]
\(Q=\{h\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}:h_e=0\text{ for }e\notin S,\ h(\delta(v))=1\text{ for }v\in F,\ h(\delta(v))\leq1\text{ for }v\notin F\}\).

It is nonempty because \(y\in Q\), and bounded because every coordinate lies in \([0,1]\).

Stability throughout \(Q\)

Fix an acceptable edge \((u,w)\). Since \(y\) is stable, at least one endpoint has zero dissatisfaction. Suppose it is \(u\), so
\(\sum_{w'\succeq_u w}y_{uw'}=1\).

The total incident mass is at most \(1\). Nonnegativity therefore implies both:

  • \(u\in F\);
  • every edge of \(S\) incident to \(u\) leads to a vacancy weakly preferred to \(w\).

Every \(h\in Q\) keeps \(u\) saturated and uses only edges in \(S\). Hence
\(\sum_{w'\succeq_u w}h_{uw'}=1\).

If the zero-dissatisfaction endpoint was \(w\), the same argument applies there. Thus every \(h\in Q\) satisfies the stated stability predicate.

Integrality of \(Q\)

After deleting coordinates outside \(S\), the constraints use the vertex-edge incidence matrix of a bipartite graph. This matrix is totally unimodular: multiplying vacancy rows by \(-1\) makes each column contain one \(+1\) and one \(-1\). For any square submatrix, a column with at most one nonzero permits determinant expansion and induction; if every column has two nonzeros, its column sums are zero and its determinant vanishes.

Adding nonnegativity constraints preserves total unimodularity. Equalities can be represented by opposite inequality rows, which also preserve it. All right-hand sides are integral. Therefore every vertex of \(Q\) is integral, by the determinant formula for a basic solution.

Such a vertex has coordinates in \(\{0,1\}\), and its positive edges form a matching.

Preserving the distance bound

For every \(h\in Q\), the bounds \(0\leq h_e\leq1\) give
\(\|h-\chi_{M_0}\|_1=|M_0|-\sum_{e\in M_0}h_e+\sum_{e\notin M_0}h_e\).

This is a linear function on \(Q\). Since \(Q\) is nonempty and bounded, it has an optimal vertex \(h^*\), and
\(\|h^*-\chi_{M_0}\|_1\leq\|y-\chi_{M_0}\|_1\).

The preceding arguments show that \(h^*=\chi_M\) for a stable matching \(M\). On a matching indicator, zero dissatisfaction at an endpoint means that endpoint is matched to a weakly preferred partner. Thus this is precisely discrete weak stability. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

3. Reduction to normalized rational masses

Take a hard discrete instance from Section 1 with \(n\) applicants and \(n\) vacancies.

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Add \(N-n\) isolated applicant-vacancy pairs, each accepting only its partner, and include these pairs in the initial matching. In every stable discrete matching, each added pair must be matched: otherwise its two unmatched endpoints block. The padding therefore preserves the decision problem and its distance budget.

Write \(M_1'\) for the padded initial matching and set
\(c=1/(2N)\).

Construct the society using one type for each padded agent, with
\(a_u=b_w=c\), \(x^1=c\chi_{M_1'}\), and \(\varepsilon=ck\).

Both sides have total mass \(Nc=1/2\). All masses are positive dyadic rationals. Acceptability and rankings are inherited from the discrete instance, so only vacancy \(d\) undergoes the required singleton swap.

Scaling by \(c\) preserves stability: each dissatisfaction expression is multiplied by \(c\). In particular, \(x^1\) is stable in profile \(1\).

Forward implication

If a discrete matching \(M_2'\) is stable in profile \(2\) and satisfies
\(|M_2'\triangle M_1'|\leq k\), then \(x^2=c\chi_{M_2'}\) is feasible and stable, and
\(\|x^2-x^1\|_1=c|M_2'\triangle M_1'|\leq ck=\varepsilon\).

Reverse implication

Suppose a real assignment \(x^2\) satisfies the continuous requirements. Define \(y=x^2/c\). Then \(y\) has unit capacities, is stable in profile \(2\), and satisfies
\(\|y-\chi_{M_1'}\|_1\leq k\).

The rounding lemma supplies a discrete stable matching \(M_2'\) with
\(|M_2'\triangle M_1'|=\|\chi_{M_2'}-\chi_{M_1'}\|_1\leq k\).

Removing the isolated padding pairs gives a solution to the original discrete instance.

Thus the reduction is an equivalence, including for arbitrary real-valued candidate assignments.

Finally, \(N<2n\), the support size is \(\tau=2N\), and the padding adds only \(N-n\) edges. The masses and scaled threshold have polynomial binary encoding length. The reduction is therefore polynomial in the ordinary input size and respects the support-based interpretation of \(\tau\).

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For each acceptable edge, guess which endpoint satisfies its zero-dissatisfaction equality in profile \(2\). This requires \(m\) bits.

For these choices, impose the assignment inequalities and the selected stability equalities. Introduce variables \(d_{uw}\) satisfying
\(d_{uw}\geq x_{uw}-x^1_{uw}\), \(d_{uw}\geq x^1_{uw}-x_{uw}\), and \(d_{uw}\geq0\), together with
\(\sum_{(u,w)\in E}d_{uw}\leq\varepsilon\).

This is a rational linear feasibility problem of polynomial size in \(m,\tau,L\), decidable in polynomial time.

If the LP is feasible, every edge has a selected zero-dissatisfaction endpoint and the distance bound holds. Conversely, any desired stable assignment supplies valid endpoint choices and admits \(d_{uw}=|x_{uw}-x^1_{uw}|\). Hence the certificate verifier accepts exactly the yes-instances.

Combining membership with the reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
