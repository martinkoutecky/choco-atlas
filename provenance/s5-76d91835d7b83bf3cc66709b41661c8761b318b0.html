The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem D.2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational additive valuations \(v_j\) and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\in[0,1]\) for item slots \(j\in J\), decide whether there exists a finite-support mass allocation \(x_b\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\{0,1\}^{J}\) such that \(\sum_bx_b=1\), \(\sum_bx_bb_j=\rho_j\) for every \(j\), and \(x_b>0\Rightarrow\sum_jb_jv_j>0\); equivalently, decide whether the continuous MMS value is positive.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here the society has one valuation type of mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\).
Let \(J\) contain \(m\ge1\) item slots, with common additive values \(v_j\in\mathbb Q\) of either sign and per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb Q\). Each person receives an integral bundle \(b\in\{0,1\}^{J}\), worth \(v(b)=\sum_jb_jv_j\); supplies must be allocated exactly.
Fix a rational constant \(\kappa>0\). Define \(G=\sum_{j:v_j\ge0}\rho_jv_j\) and \(C=\sum_{j:v_j<0}\rho_j|v_j|\), and assume \(G-C>0\) and \(G-C\ge\kappa\min\{G,C\}\).
A mass allocation consists of finitely supported real masses \(x_b\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_b=1\) and \(\sum_bx_bb_j=\rho_j\) for every \(j\). It is positive if \(x_b>0\Rightarrow v(b)>0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether a positive mass allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for each fixed \(\kappa\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finitely many identical agents, deciding whether the MMS is strictly positive is NP-hard even with positive total value and the same fixed imbalance bound, when the number of agents is unrestricted. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.09133v2)
The related AnyPrice share uses bundle distributions with a common marginal upper bound and is NP-hard to compute exactly for additive goods valuations; this differs from signed values and prescribed marginal equalities. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2103.04304)
No result settling the exact positive mass-allocation problem was located.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

For every fixed rational \(\kappa>0\), the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with \(\tau=1\), integer valuations, and supplies belonging to \(\{1/4,1/2,1\}\).

Consequently, a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of PARTITION; the usual reduction from Subset Sum is recorded in these [Berkeley lecture notes](https://ucb-lean-course-sp26.github.io/lectures/lec-08.html).

Let the input consist of positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=2B,\qquad B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}.\)

The question is whether some subset \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in S}a_i=B\). Restricting PARTITION to even-total inputs preserves its hardness: multiplying every input integer by \(2\) produces an even total and preserves the answer.

Fix

\(\displaystyle K=\lceil2\kappa\rceil+2.\)

Construct one valuation type of mass \(1\), with the following \(m=n+2\) slots:

| Slots | Value | Supply |
|---|---:|---:|
| Original good \(i\), for each \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) | \(2a_i\) | \(1/4\) |
| Additional good \(h\) | \(2KB\) | \(1/2\) |
| Chore \(c\) | \(-(2B-1)\) | \(1\) |

All values are integers. Since \(K\) is constant for fixed \(\kappa\), the construction takes polynomial time and has polynomial binary encoding length.

2. Verification of the imbalance promise

For the constructed instance,

\(\displaystyle G=\frac14\sum_{i=1}^{n}2a_i+\frac12(2KB)=(K+1)B,\)

and

\(\displaystyle C=2B-1>0.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle G-C=(K-1)B+1>0.\)

Thus \(\min\{G,C\}=C\). Moreover, \(K-1\ge2\kappa\), so

\(\displaystyle G-C\ge2\kappa B+1>\kappa(2B-1)=\kappa\min\{G,C\}.\)

Every output of the reduction therefore satisfies both promises, regardless of whether its PARTITION input is a yes-instance.

3. A partition gives a positive mass allocation

Suppose \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in S}a_i=B\). Its complement also has sum \(B\).

Allocate the following three bundles:

| Bundle | Mass |
|---|---:|
| Chore \(c\), together with the original goods indexed by \(S\) | \(1/4\) |
| Chore \(c\), together with the original goods indexed by \(S^c\) | \(1/4\) |
| Chore \(c\), together with good \(h\) | \(1/2\) |

These masses sum to \(1\). Every original good appears in exactly one of the first two bundles, giving marginal \(1/4\). Good \(h\) has marginal \(1/2\), and the chore has marginal \(1\).

The first two bundles each have value

\(\displaystyle 2B-(2B-1)=1>0.\)

The third has value

\(\displaystyle 2KB-(2B-1)=2(K-1)B+1>0.\)

Hence this is a positive mass allocation satisfying every supply equality.

4. A positive mass allocation gives a partition

Conversely, suppose a positive mass allocation exists. Regard its masses as the probabilities of a random bundle \(b\).

Because the chore has marginal \(1\), every bundle of positive mass contains it. Indeed,

\(\displaystyle \sum_bx_b(1-b_c)=1-\rho_c=0,\)

and every summand is nonnegative.

Define the original-goods sum and the additional-good indicator by

\(\displaystyle T(b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b_i,\qquad H(b)=b_h.\)

Here \(T(b)\) is a nonnegative integer. The prescribed marginals give

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[T]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\rho_i=\frac14(2B)=\frac B2,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \Pr(H=0)=1-\rho_h=\frac12.\)

Consider a bundle of positive mass with \(H=0\). Its strict positivity requires

\(\displaystyle 2T(b)-(2B-1)>0.\)

Since \(T(b)\) is integral, this implies \(T(b)\ge B\). On bundles with \(H=1\), nonnegativity gives \(T(b)\ge0\). Therefore every supported bundle satisfies

\(\displaystyle T(b)\ge B(1-H(b)).\)

Taking expectations yields

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[T]\ge B\Pr(H=0)=\frac B2.\)

The preceding marginal calculation shows equality. Thus the nonnegative random variable \(T-B(1-H)\) has expectation zero. Because the distribution has finite support, it must vanish on every bundle of positive mass.

In particular, every supported bundle with \(H=0\) satisfies \(T(b)=B\). Such a bundle exists because \(\Pr(H=0)=1/2\). Its original goods therefore give a subset \(S\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in S}a_i=B.\)

This proves the required equivalence and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Although allocations may use real masses, every feasible instance admits a certificate consisting of at most \(m+1\) bundles with rational masses of polynomial binary length.

To prove this, choose a positive feasible allocation having the smallest possible support, with bundles \(b^1,\ldots,b^r\) and masses \(x_1,\ldots,x_r>0\). Define columns

\(\displaystyle u_i=(1,b^i_1,\ldots,b^i_m)^{\mathsf T}.\)

These columns are linearly independent. Otherwise, there is a nonzero vector \(\lambda\) with \(\sum_i\lambda_i u_i=0\). The first row gives \(\sum_i\lambda_i=0\), so \(\lambda\) has both positive and negative entries. Set

\(\displaystyle t=\min_{\lambda_i>0}\frac{x_i}{\lambda_i}.\)

Replacing \(x_i\) by \(x_i-t\lambda_i\) preserves every equality, keeps all masses nonnegative, and makes at least one mass zero. It retains only positive bundles, contradicting minimality of the support. Hence \(r\le m+1\).

Select \(r\) independent rows of the column matrix to obtain an invertible binary matrix \(M\). The selected equalities are

\(\displaystyle Mx=q,\)

where the entries of \(q\) belong to \(\{1,\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_m\}\). Let \(D\) be the product of the encoded denominators of the marginals. Its binary length is polynomial in \(L\), and \(Dq\) is integral with entries between \(0\) and \(D\).

Cramer’s rule gives

\(\displaystyle x_i=\frac{\det M_i(Dq)}{D\det M},\)

where \(M_i(Dq)\) replaces column \(i\) by \(Dq\). By the determinant expansion,

\(\displaystyle |\det M|\le r!,\qquad |\det M_i(Dq)|\le r!D.\)

Thus each mass has polynomial binary length.

A verifier checks the listed bundles, nonnegative rational masses, normalization, all marginal equalities, and strict positivity of every bundle assigned positive mass. Exact rational arithmetic performs these checks in polynomial time. The promises can likewise be checked in polynomial time.

The reduction and certificate argument together establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for every fixed rational \(\kappa>0\).
