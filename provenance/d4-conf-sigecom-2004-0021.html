The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(r\) good types with utilities \(w_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) and per-capita supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), consider a unit-mass population of identical agents with additive utility \(u(b)=\sum_{j=1}^r w_jb_j\) for integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^r\). Choose masses \(x_b\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_b=1\) and \(\sum_b b_jx_b=q_j\) for every \(j\), minimizing \(\rho(x)=\max_{x_b>0}u(b)/\min_{x_b>0}u(b)\), with value \(+\infty\) if a used bundle has zero utility.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given \(r\) good types with values \(w_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) and per-capita supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\), a feasible allocation is a distribution \(x=(x_b)_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}^r}\) with \(x_b\geq0\), \(\sum_b x_b=1\), and \(\sum_b b_jx_b=q_j\) for every \(j\). A bundle \(b\) has utility \(u(b)=\sum_{j=1}^r w_jb_j\). Its envy-ratio is \(\rho(x)=\max_{x_b>0}u(b)/\min_{x_b>0}u(b)\), and is \(+\infty\) if any used bundle has utility \(0\).

Target. Is computing \(\min_x\rho(x)\) for this continuous-population problem polynomial-time solvable, or NP-hard?

For the discrete analogue with finitely many identical agents and indivisible goods, minimizing the ratio of maximum to minimum bundle utility has a PTAS; with a constant number of agents, it has an FPTAS.

Result

Exact computation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when every supply equals \(\tfrac12\) and all values are positive integers. More precisely, deciding whether the optimal envy-ratio equals \(1\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

The proof uses auxiliary types to prevent a bundle from taking multiple copies of an item.

1. Fixed mean utility and attainment

For every feasible allocation, nonnegativity and the supply constraints give
\(\sum_b x_bu(b)=\sum_j w_jq_j\).
Write this fixed mean as \(T\).

Consequently, an allocation has ratio \(1\) exactly when every used bundle has utility \(T>0\).

We also record why a finite optimum is attained. Suppose a feasible allocation has finite ratio \(R\). Any feasible allocation with ratio at most \(R\) has minimum utility at most \(T\), and hence maximum utility at most \(RT\). Every used bundle therefore satisfies
\(b_j\le RT/w_j\).
Since there are finitely many types and all values are positive, only finitely many bundles satisfy these bounds. There are consequently only finitely many possible supports, and each nonempty support determines its ratio. The set of achievable ratios at most \(R\) is thus finite and nonempty, so its minimum is attained and is globally optimal.

2. Polynomial-size construction

We reduce from PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) with total \(2B\), decide whether some subset sums to \(B\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; see [Karp’s original paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf). Restricting the total to be even preserves hardness, since doubling every number in an arbitrary instance preserves its answer and makes its total even.

Define
\(A=\max_i a_i\), \(D=n+1\), and \(C=nA+1\).

Also define
\(P=\sum_{i=1}^nD^{i-1}\), \(L=CD^{n-1}+A\), and \(M=(n+1)L\).

For each index \(i\), introduce two good types with values
\(w_{i,0}=M+CD^{i-1}\) and
\(w_{i,1}=M+CD^{i-1}+a_i\).
Give both types supply
\(q_{i,0}=q_{i,1}=\tfrac12\).

The fixed mean utility is
\(T=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n(w_{i,0}+w_{i,1})=nM+CP+B\).

There are \(2n\) types. Each value has binary length
\(O(n\log(n+1)+\log A)\), so the construction has polynomial size and is computable in polynomial time.

3. Characterizing bundles of utility \(T\)

Consider any bundle of utility \(T\). Let \(b_{i,0}\) and \(b_{i,1}\) denote its multiplicities, and put
\(c_i=b_{i,0}+b_{i,1}\),
\(k=\sum_i c_i\), and
\(s=\sum_i a_i b_{i,1}\).

Its utility equation is
\(kM+C\sum_{i=1}^n c_iD^{i-1}+s=nM+CP+B\).

First, the bundle contains exactly \(n\) goods.

Every good has value between \(M\) and \(M+L\). Moreover,
\(0<CP+B\le nCD^{n-1}+nA=nL<M\),
so
\(nM<T<(n+1)M\).

If \(k\ge n+1\), its utility is at least \((n+1)M>T\), a contradiction.

If \(k\le n-1\), its utility is at most
\((n-1)(M+L)<nM<T\),
where the strict inequality follows from \((n-1)L<M\).
Thus \(k=n\).

Second, the selected numbers sum to \(B\).

Since the bundle contains \(n\) goods,
\(0\le s\le nA<C\).
Also \(0<B<C\).
After subtracting \(nM\), the utility equation implies
\(s\equiv B\pmod C\).
Both numbers lie between \(0\) and \(C-1\), so \(s=B\).

Third, exactly one good comes from each pair.

Substituting \(s=B\) gives
\(\sum_{i=1}^n c_iD^{i-1}=P=\sum_{i=1}^nD^{i-1}\).

Because \(0\le c_i\le n<D\), the coefficients on the left are valid base-\(D\) digits. Uniqueness of base-\(D\) representation therefore gives \(c_i=1\) for every \(i\).

Thus each \(b_{i,1}\) belongs to \(\{0,1\}\), and the set
\(S=\{i:b_{i,1}=1\}\)
satisfies
\(\sum_{i\in S}a_i=B\).

We have proved that the existence of even one bundle of utility \(T\) implies a yes-instance of PARTITION.

4. Equivalence with optimal ratio \(1\)

Suppose first that a subset \(S\) sums to \(B\). Construct a bundle taking type \((i,1)\) when \(i\in S\), and type \((i,0)\) otherwise. Construct a second bundle by reversing every choice.

The first bundle has utility \(T\). Since the complement of \(S\) also sums to \(B\), the second does too. Assign probability \(\tfrac12\) to each bundle. Every type appears in exactly one of them, so every supply constraint is satisfied. This allocation has ratio \(1\), which is the smallest possible ratio.

Conversely, any feasible allocation of ratio \(1\) must use only bundles of utility \(T\), by the fixed-mean identity. Section 3 then produces a partition.

For completeness, the constructed instance always has a finite-ratio feasible allocation: assign probability \(\tfrac12\) to the bundle containing one copy of every type \((i,0)\), and probability \(\tfrac12\) to the bundle containing one copy of every type \((i,1)\). Both utilities are positive. The attainment argument in Section 1 therefore applies.

There is also an explicit separation in no-instances. All utilities and \(T\) are integers, and no bundle has utility \(T\). Any finite-ratio feasible allocation must use a bundle below \(T\) and a bundle above \(T\): otherwise its mean could not equal \(T\). Their utilities are at most \(T-1\) and at least \(T+1\), respectively. Hence every such allocation satisfies
\(\rho(x)\ge (T+1)/(T-1)>1\).

Therefore,
\(\min_x\rho(x)=1\)
if and only if the original PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

5. Complexity conclusion

An algorithm computing the exact optimal ratio in polynomial time would decide PARTITION in polynomial time by comparing its output with \(1\). Thus the target optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and is not polynomial-time solvable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

This proves exact-computation hardness. It does not establish strong hardness or exclude approximation schemes, so it is consistent with the approximation results stated for the discrete analogue.
