The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an integer population scale \(M\), \(N\ge M\) indivisible options, and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete preference types \(t\), with rational preference vectors \(q_t\in\Delta_N\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{ti}\) minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{i=1}^N (x_{ti}/\mu_t-q_{ti})^2\), subject to \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\), \(x_{ti}\ge 0\), and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\le 1/M\) for each option \(i\).

Given integers \(N\ge M\ge3\), let \(m=N\) be the number of options. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type specifies a rational probabilistic preference vector \(q_t\), with \(q_{ti}\ge0\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N q_{ti}=1\); every option is available to every type.
A mass allocation consists of real numbers \(x_{ti}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^N x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\le1/M\) for every option. The resultant selection probability for type \(t\) is \(x_{ti}/\mu_t\).
Find an allocation minimizing \(F(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{i=1}^N(x_{ti}/\mu_t-q_{ti})^2\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the preference vectors, masses, and population scale \(M\).

Is this exact mass-allocation optimization problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(M\).

For a finite population of \(M\) players, a conflict-free joint decision is a probability distribution over assignments giving each player one distinct option. If the sum of players’ preferred probabilities for any option exceeds \(1\), zero total squared marginal loss is impossible; this result supplies no computational complexity classification. 1. Classification

The exact mass-allocation problem is solvable in polynomial time. An algorithm can output the unique optimal allocation as binary-encoded rational numbers, together with its exact objective value, in \((m\tau L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

The dependence on the population scale \(M\) is through its binary encoding, of length \(O(\log M)\). No expansion into \(M\) individual players is required.

2. Feasibility, attainment, and uniqueness

Let \(d=m\tau\) be the number of allocation variables, and let \(P\) denote their feasible set.

Because every option is available to every type, the allocation \(x_{ti}=\mu_t/m\) is feasible: its row sums are \(\mu_t\), and each column sum is

\(\displaystyle \sum_t x_{ti}=\frac1m\le\frac1M.\)

Furthermore, nonnegativity and the row equations imply \(0\le x_{ti}\le\mu_t\). Thus \(P\) is a nonempty, closed, bounded polytope. The continuous objective therefore attains its minimum.

Expanding the objective gives

\(\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{t,i}\frac{x_{ti}^{\,2}}{\mu_t}-2\sum_{t,i}q_{ti}x_{ti}+C,\qquad C=\sum_{t,i}\mu_tq_{ti}^{\,2}.\)

The constant \(C\) does not affect the optimizer. Write the remaining expression as \(G(x)=\frac12x^{\mathsf T}Hx+c^{\mathsf T}x\), where

\(\displaystyle H_{(t,i),(t,i)}=\frac{2}{\mu_t},\qquad H_{(t,i),(s,j)}=0\ \text{if }(t,i)\ne(s,j),\qquad c_{(t,i)}=-2q_{ti}.\)

Every support mass is positive, so \(H\) is positive definite. Hence \(G\) is strictly convex, and its minimizer on the convex set \(P\) is unique.

When \(m=M\), every column constraint is necessarily tight: the column sums total \(1\), and their upper bounds also total \(m/M=1\). This lower-dimensional case causes no difficulty for the argument below.

3. Exact polynomial-time optimization

We use the following classical theorem in its bounded-feasible-set form:

A quadratic function with rational coefficients and positive semidefinite Hessian can be minimized exactly over a nonempty bounded rational polyhedron in time polynomial in the total binary encoding length. The algorithm returns a rational optimal point and the exact optimal value.

This is the exact convex quadratic programming theorem of [Kozlov, Tarasov, and Khachiyan (1980)](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5189).

All its hypotheses hold here:

  • The objective coefficients are rational, because the masses and preferences are rational.
  • The Hessian is positive definite, as proved above.
  • The constraints are rational linear constraints.
  • The feasible set is nonempty and bounded, as proved above.

Equalities can be represented by pairs of inequalities. The resulting quadratic program has \(d=m\tau\) variables and \(k=d+m+2\tau=O(d)\) inequalities. Each numerical coefficient has \(O(L)\) bits: in particular, taking the reciprocal of a positive rational mass merely exchanges its numerator and denominator.

Even using a dense representation, the constructed quadratic program has encoding length

\(\displaystyle S=O(d^2+dL).\)

Since the input explicitly lists all \(m\tau=d\) preference entries, \(L=\Omega(d)\). Consequently \(S=O(dL)\), and the theorem gives an exact running time of

\(\displaystyle S^{O(1)}=(m\tau L)^{O(1)}\)

bit operations. Evaluating \(F\) at the returned rational allocation also takes polynomial time.

This establishes exact optimization, rather than only approximation to a prescribed tolerance.

4. Why the exact optimizer has polynomial bit length

For completeness, the polynomial-size rational output can also be verified directly.

Let \(x^*\) be the unique optimizer. Collect all row equations and all inequalities tight at \(x^*\). Choose a maximal linearly independent subset of their coefficient rows, and write the corresponding equations as \(Ex=b\). Let \(r\le d\) be the number of chosen rows.

Every omitted active equation follows from these chosen equations: its coefficient row is a linear combination of the chosen rows, and its right-hand side satisfies the same combination because all equations hold at \(x^*\).

For any \(u\in\ker E\), both \(x^*+\varepsilon u\) and \(x^*-\varepsilon u\) are feasible for sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Indeed, all active constraints remain equalities, while the finitely many inactive inequalities retain their positive slack. Optimality along these two directions therefore implies

\(\displaystyle (Hx^*+c)^{\mathsf T}u=0\qquad\text{for every }u\in\ker E.\)

The orthogonal complement of \(\ker E\) is the row space of \(E\). Thus there exists \(\lambda\) satisfying the rational linear system

\(\displaystyle Hx^*+E^{\mathsf T}\lambda=-c,\qquad Ex^*=b.\)

This system is nonsingular. To see this, suppose its homogeneous system satisfies \(Hu+E^{\mathsf T}v=0\) and \(Eu=0\). Multiplying the first equation by \(u^{\mathsf T}\) yields \(u^{\mathsf T}Hu=0\). Positive definiteness gives \(u=0\); then \(E^{\mathsf T}v=0\), and the independence of the rows of \(E\) gives \(v=0\).

Consequently \(x^*\) is rational. Its bit length is polynomially bounded as follows. The entries of \(E\) are integers of magnitude at most \(1\). The only potentially nonintegral coefficients occur in the diagonal of \(H\), in \(c\), and in \(b\): there are \(O(d)\) such coefficients, each with \(O(L)\) bits. Clearing their denominators produces an integer system of order at most \(2d\), with entries of \(O(dL)\) bits.

For an integer matrix of order \(n\le2d\) whose entries have magnitude at most \(2^B\), expansion of the determinant gives the bound \(n!\,2^{nB}\). Cramer’s rule therefore represents every coordinate of \(x^*\) using a numerator and denominator with \(O(d^2L+d\log d)\) bits. This proves the required polynomial output bound without assuming that the active constraints can be guessed efficiently.

5. Dependence on \(M\) and the zero-loss condition

If \(L_{\mathrm{data}}\) denotes the encoding length of the remaining input, the running-time guarantee can equivalently be written as

\(\displaystyle \bigl(m\tau(L_{\mathrm{data}}+\log M)\bigr)^{O(1)}.\)

This is a polynomial bound in binary input length; no strongly polynomial bound is asserted.

Finally, positivity of every \(\mu_t\) and nonnegativity of every squared term imply that \(F(x)=0\) holds exactly when \(x_{ti}=\mu_tq_{ti}\) for every type and option. These values automatically satisfy the row equations. Hence zero loss is attainable exactly when

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_tq_{ti}\le\frac1M\qquad\text{for every option }i.\)

That condition decides zero-loss feasibility. The convex quadratic programming argument settles optimization also when the minimum loss is positive. In particular, the rational-threshold decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\), so it cannot be NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
