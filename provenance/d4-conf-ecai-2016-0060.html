The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), target \(c\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings of \(C\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), is there a partition \(C=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_2\) such that the mass-based veto subelections, with the specified TE or TP rule, promote finalists who make \(c\) a winner of the runoff under the specified unique-winner or nonunique-winner model? In each subelection and the runoff, restrict rankings to the candidates present and compute veto scores from the original society’s mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a nonempty candidate set \(C\), let \(m=|C|\) and designate \(c\in C\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct complete strict rankings of \(C\), each with a positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For each candidate subset, use veto with rankings restricted to that subset and scores weighted by the original masses. All maximum-score candidates are winners; a singleton elects its candidate and an empty election has no winners.
Fix one of TE (promote only a unique subelection winner) or TP (promote every subelection winner), and one of the unique-winner or nonunique-winner models. These choices define four separate decision problems.
A control action partitions \(C=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_2\), allowing empty parts and imposing no size constraint. Hold both subelections with the entire society, then hold a veto runoff on the union of their promoted candidates, again with the entire society and restricted rankings.
The decision question is whether some partition makes \(c\) the sole runoff winner in the unique-winner model, or a runoff winner in the nonunique-winner model.

For each of the four fixed model combinations, is this mass-based constructive control problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite unweighted societies, constructive control by runoff partition under TE is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete in both winner models, and under TP in the unique-winner model. The [full version](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/theses/phd-maushagen.pdf) establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness also for TP in the nonunique-winner model.
No result explicitly classifying the normalized binary-rational mass formulation was located in the literature search.

The singleton case \(m=1\) is always a yes-instance. The classification concerns unrestricted candidate sets. Rational masses are represented by binary-encoded integer numerators and denominators.

1. Normalization preserves every restricted election

Let \(V\) be a nonempty explicitly listed unweighted society with \(N\) voters. Let \(T\) contain precisely its distinct rankings, and let \(n_t\) be the number of voters having ranking \(t\). Define \(\mu_t=n_t/N\).

These masses are positive and satisfy \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\).

Fix any nonempty candidate subset \(S\subseteq C\). For \(x\in S\), define \(I_{t,S}(x)\) to equal zero when \(x\) is last in the restriction of \(t\) to \(S\), and one otherwise. The unweighted and mass-based veto scores are respectively

\(\operatorname{score}_{V,S}(x)=\sum_{t\in T}n_t I_{t,S}(x)\)

and

\(\operatorname{score}_{\mu,S}(x)=\sum_{t\in T}(n_t/N)I_{t,S}(x)=\operatorname{score}_{V,S}(x)/N\).

Because \(N>0\), this identity preserves every strict score comparison and every score equality. Hence the two elections have exactly the same winner set on every nonempty \(S\). For a singleton, its sole candidate wins in both formulations; an empty election has no winners.

Now fix a partition \(C=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_2\). The subelection winner sets coincide under the two representations. Consequently, their promoted sets coincide under either TE or TP. Their runoff candidate sets therefore coincide, and the same score identity applies to the runoff.

Thus every partition succeeds in the unweighted instance exactly when it succeeds in the normalized mass instance, in either winner model.

2. Polynomial-time reduction and hardness

The finite-society hardness theorem stated in the question is Theorem 5(1) of the Maushagen–Rothe paper reproduced in the linked dissertation. It establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for constructive veto control by runoff partition under both tie-handling rules and both winner models. Its definitions use complete strict rankings, the entire society in both subelections and the runoff, and rankings restricted to each election’s candidate set. These are the required hypotheses. [Source: definitions and Theorem 5(1)](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/theses/phd-maushagen.pdf#page=38)

For each fixed combination, transform a finite-society instance by keeping \(C\) and \(c\), grouping identical rankings, and assigning the masses \(n_t/N\).

This transformation is polynomial-time:

  • Grouping explicitly listed rankings and counting their multiplicities takes polynomial time in the source input length.
  • There are \(\tau\leq N\) distinct rankings.
  • Each numerator \(n_t\) and denominator \(N\) uses \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits.
  • Reducing the fractions, if required by the encoding, takes polynomial time.

Therefore the resulting input has polynomial length. Section 1 proves the necessary equivalence of yes-instances and no-instances.

The allowance of empty parts creates no mismatch. In particular, the cited hardness construction uses nonempty societies and has a unique full-election winner \(w\neq c\). Its score difference is \(\operatorname{score}(w)-\operatorname{score}(c)=q-1>0\), where \(q>1\) is the number of source clauses. [Source: construction and full-election scores](https://comsoc-community.org/assets/theses/phd-maushagen.pdf#page=47) For either partition with an empty part, the nonempty subelection consequently promotes only \(w\), under both TE and TP. Such a partition cannot elect \(c\).

It follows that each of the four mass-based problems is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions.

3. Polynomial-time certificate verification

A certificate assigns each candidate to one of the two parts, using \(m\) bits. We show that its success can be checked with exact arithmetic in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive integers \(a_t,b_t\). Set

\(D=\prod_{t\in T}b_t\), and \(W_t=a_t(D/b_t)\).

These are positive integers, and normalization gives \(\sum_{t\in T}W_t=D\). Moreover,

\(\log_2 D=\sum_{t\in T}\log_2 b_t=O(L)\).

Thus \(D\), all \(W_t\), and all partial sums of these positive weights have \(O(L)\) bits. They can be computed by polynomial-time integer arithmetic.

For a nonempty subset \(S\), scan each ranking to find its last candidate belonging to \(S\). For each \(x\in S\), compute the integer veto total

\(B_S(x)=\sum_{\{t:\,x\text{ is last in }t|_S\}}W_t\).

The mass-based score is \(1-B_S(x)/D\). Therefore the winners are exactly the candidates minimizing \(B_S(x)\), and uniqueness is determined by exact integer equality. This also handles singletons correctly.

Apply this computation to the two parts, form the promoted set according to TE or TP, and compute its runoff winners. Handle empty sets by returning no winners. Finally, check whether the winner set equals \(\{c\}\), or contains \(c\), as required.

Each election requires at most \(O(\tau m)\) ranking inspections and polynomially many operations on \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Hence the certificate verifier runs in polynomial time, proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) for all four problems.

4. Conclusion

Normalization and aggregation give a polynomial-time reduction preserving every partition’s outcome. Exact integer score comparisons give polynomial-time certificate verification. Together these establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for all four combinations.
