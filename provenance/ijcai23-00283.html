The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational society \(\mu=(\rho,1-\rho)\) of well-informed and misinformed juror types, panel size \(k\), target \(x\in(1/2,1)\), and effort data \(\lambda=f^{-1}(x)\) and \(d=f'(\lambda)\), a panel is sampled from \(\mu\). Choose payment values \(p(1/k),\ldots,p(1)\) minimizing \(k[xA(p)+(1-x)B(p)]\), where \(J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(k-1,x)\), \(A(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Pr[J=j]p((j+1)/k)\), and \(B(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Pr[J=j]p((k-j)/k)\), subject to \(xA(p)+(1-x)B(p)\ge\lambda\), \(d(A(p)-B(p))=1\), and \(p((j+2)/k)-p((j+1)/k)+p((k-j)/k)-p((k-j-1)/k)\ge0\) for \(0\le j\le k-2\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here \(m=2\) alternatives and \(\mu=(\rho,1-\rho)\), with rational \(\rho\in[0,1]\); masses sum to one and zero-mass types are omitted.
Sample \(k\ge1\) jurors independently from \(\mu\). Well-informed and misinformed types have complementary signal-accuracy functions \(f\) and \(1-f\), where \(f:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]\) is continuously differentiable, strictly increasing and strictly concave, with \(f(0)=1/2\). Effort costs equal effort; jurors may follow or reverse their signals. Majority decides, with uniform tie-breaking.
Input includes binary rationals \(x\in(1/2,1)\), \(\lambda>0\), and \(d>0\), promised to satisfy \(f(\lambda)=x\) and \(f'(\lambda)=d\); the function itself is not input. The intended profile has every juror exert effort \(\lambda\), with well-informed jurors following signals and misinformed jurors reversing them; signals are independent.
A juror receives \(p(r/k)\in\mathbb R\) when exactly \(r\) jurors, including herself, cast her vote; payments may have either sign. For \(J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(k-1,x)\), define \(A(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Pr[J=j]p((j+1)/k)\) and \(B(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Pr[J=j]p((k-j)/k)\).
Choose the \(k\) payment values to minimize \(k[xA(p)+(1-x)B(p)]\), subject to \(xA(p)+(1-x)B(p)\ge\lambda\), \(d(A(p)-B(p))=1\), and \(p((j+2)/k)-p((j+1)/k)+p((k-j)/k)-p((k-j-1)/k)\ge0\) for every \(0\le j\le k-2\). Infeasibility is an allowed outcome.

Is this exact minimum-payment problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For a finite list of \(n\) jurors with complementary effort functions, minimum payments satisfying these equilibrium, individual-rationality and payment-shape conditions can be selected in time polynomial in \(n\) using linear programming ([Caragiannis and Schwartzbach](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0283.pdf)).
No published result for exactly the distributional formulation above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the society to total mass one, allowed either type to have zero mass, and omitted zero-mass types from the support.
  • Interpreted panel sampling as independent draws from the society, with independent signals.
  • Made x, lambda and d binary-rational inputs with lambda and d positive, promised consistent with an admissible complementary effort model; the effort function is not supplied computationally.
  • Allowed unrestricted signed real payments, as in the discrete model, without adding nonnegativity or monotonicity.
  • Allowed every positive integer panel size and infeasible instances; for a singleton panel the indexed payment-shape constraints are empty.
  • Measured running time polynomially in the explicit panel size k as well as support size and input bit length, matching the anchor's dependence on jury size.

Result

The exact minimum-payment problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\). More precisely:

  • For \(k=1\), the constraints are infeasible.
  • For every \(k\ge 2\), the exact minimum is \(k\lambda\), attained by an explicitly computable rational payment schedule.

The proof below concerns exactly the stated constraints, including the permission to use negative payments.

1. Sampling types does not change the vote distribution

Under the intended profile, a well-informed juror votes for the ground truth with probability \(f(\lambda)=x\). A misinformed juror reverses her signal, so her probability of voting for the ground truth is also \(1-(1-f(\lambda))=x\).

Consequently, conditional on any sampled type vector, votes are independent and each is correct with probability \(x\). This conditional joint distribution is the same for every type vector, so averaging over sampled types preserves it. Thus the number of correct votes among a juror’s opponents has distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(k-1,x)\), independently of \(\rho\) and the supported-type composition.

This explains why the supplied functionals \(A\) and \(B\) suffice without enumerating type profiles.

2. Lower bound and the infeasible case

Write \(p_r=p(r/k)\) for \(1\le r\le k\), and define the expected payment per juror by \(E(p)=xA(p)+(1-x)B(p)\).

Individual rationality requires \(E(p)\ge\lambda\). Therefore every feasible schedule satisfies \(kE(p)\ge k\lambda\).

For \(k=1\), the random variable \(J\) equals zero, and \(A(p)=B(p)=p_1\). Hence \(d(A(p)-B(p))=0\), contradicting the required equality \(d(A(p)-B(p))=1\). This case is infeasible.

We now attain the lower bound for every \(k\ge 2\).

3. An explicit optimal schedule

Assume \(k\ge 2\), and define

\(q=x^2+(1-x)^2,\qquad D=d(k-1)(2x-1).\)

The input hypotheses \(d>0\) and \(x>1/2\) imply \(D>0\). Choose

\(\displaystyle p_r=\lambda+\frac{r-1-(k-1)q}{D},\qquad 1\le r\le k.\)

All these payment values are rational.

Computing the expected payments

Since \(J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(k-1,x)\), we have \(\mathbb E[J]=(k-1)x\). Substituting the schedule into the definition of \(A\) gives

\(\displaystyle A(p)=\mathbb E[p_{J+1}]=\lambda+\frac{(k-1)(x-q)}{D}.\)

The identity \(x-q=(1-x)(2x-1)\) therefore yields

\(\displaystyle A(p)=\lambda+\frac{1-x}{d}.\)

Similarly,

\(\displaystyle B(p)=\mathbb E[p_{k-J}]=\lambda+\frac{(k-1)(1-x-q)}{D}.\)

Using \(1-x-q=-x(2x-1)\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle B(p)=\lambda-\frac{x}{d}.\)

It follows that the equilibrium equality holds:

\(\displaystyle d(A(p)-B(p))=d\left(\frac1d\right)=1.\)

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle E(p)=x\left(\lambda+\frac{1-x}{d}\right)+(1-x)\left(\lambda-\frac{x}{d}\right)=\lambda.\)

Thus individual rationality holds with equality, and total expected payment equals \(k\lambda\).

Checking every payment-shape inequality

The schedule has constant successive difference:

\(\displaystyle p_{r+1}-p_r=\frac1D,\qquad 1\le r\le k-1.\)

Therefore, for every \(0\le j\le k-2\),

\(\displaystyle p_{j+2}-p_{j+1}+p_{k-j}-p_{k-j-1}=\frac2D>0.\)

Every required shape inequality holds. The schedule is feasible and attains the lower bound, proving optimality.

4. Compatibility with the promised effort function

The construction also directly verifies that the intended effort and signal orientation are best responses for every function satisfying the promise.

Fix a juror while her opponents use the intended profile. We have \(A-B=1/d>0\). At effort \(t\ge 0\), a well-informed juror maximizes her probability of a correct vote by following her signal; a misinformed juror does so by reversing it. Indeed, \(f(t)\ge f(0)=1/2\), and either type can thereby achieve correctness probability \(f(t)\). Randomizing between following and reversing cannot improve this probability.

Her maximum expected utility at effort \(t\) is consequently

\(\displaystyle U(t)=B+\frac{f(t)}d-t.\)

Differentiable concavity gives the supporting-tangent inequality at the interior point \(\lambda>0\):

\(\displaystyle f(t)\le f(\lambda)+f'(\lambda)(t-\lambda)=x+d(t-\lambda).\)

Hence

\(\displaystyle U(t)\le B+\frac xd-\lambda=0.\)

At \(t=\lambda\), equality holds. Thus the intended effort and orientation are best responses, and each juror’s expected utility is zero. No evaluation of the unspecified function beyond the promised values is needed.

5. Exact binary complexity

The algorithm returns infeasibility if \(k=1\). Otherwise, it computes \(q,D\), and the \(k\) payment values above using exact rational arithmetic.

Each payment requires a fixed number of additions, multiplications, and divisions involving the input rationals and integers of magnitude at most \(k\). To check the size bound explicitly, the numerator and denominator of a product have binary lengths at most the sums of the corresponding operand lengths; addition uses a common denominator and has the same additive bound, up to a constant. Division exchanges a nonzero rational’s numerator and denominator before multiplication.

Because each formula uses only a fixed number of these operations, every output rational has binary length \(O(L+\log k)\). Exact computation and writing all \(k\) payments therefore take \(O(k\,\operatorname{poly}(L+\log k))\) bit operations, plus reading the input. This is polynomial in the parameters requested.

The cited finite-juror result uses the same binomial payment expectations and linear constraints in Section 4 and Theorem 7. The explicit construction here independently settles the supplied distributional formulation and its exact binary complexity. [Caragiannis and Schwartzbach, *Outsourcing Adjudication to Strategic Jurors*](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0283.pdf).
