The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite lender and borrower type supports \(I\) and \(J\), rational type masses, budgets \(q_i\), requests \(c_j\), and strict preferences over counterpart types, decide whether a stable allocation exists. Each borrower receives an integral bundle of lenders whose budgets cover its request; assignments may divide each type’s mass among bundles. Stability is judged by the paper’s blocking-pair condition, with no positive-mass set of blocking pairs.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be nonempty finite lender and borrower type supports. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Put \(m=\max\{|I|,|J|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Lender type \(i\) has mass \(a_i>0\) and budget \(q_i>0\); borrower type \(j\) has mass \(b_j>0\) and request \(c_j>0\), all binary rationals. Masses need not sum to one; assume \(\sum_j b_j\leq\sum_i a_i\). Each type strictly ranks all counterpart types; copies of the same type are indifferent, and every counterpart is preferable to being unmatched.
A bundle is a vector \(n\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{I}\) of lender counts, allowing several copies of a type. An allocation consists of nonnegative real masses \(x_{j,n}\), with \(\sum_n x_{j,n}=b_j\), \(\sum_{j,n}n_i x_{j,n}\leq a_i\), and \(x_{j,n}>0\) only when \(\sum_i q_i n_i\geq c_j\). Each lender copy funds at most one borrower with its entire budget; unused lender mass is unmatched. Bundles are finite, with no upper funding bound.
An unassigned lender–borrower pair blocks if the lender is unmatched or strictly prefers this borrower's type to its current partner's type, and the borrower is underfunded or strictly prefers the lender's type to some type in its bundle. No requirement that replacing that lender preserve funding is imposed.
Precisely, stability means there are no \(i,j,n\) with \(x_{j,n}>0\), positive mass of type-\(i\) lenders unmatched or assigned to types ranked below \(j\), and either \(\sum_hq_hn_h<c_j\) or some \(h\) with \(n_h>0\) ranked below \(i\) by \(j\). This specifies absence of a positive-mass set of blocking pairs.

Is deciding whether such a stable allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents, [Sarkar](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.02589v2) claims a linear-inequality characterization of stability, without a complexity classification of existence. [Chen and Ghosh](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/11/Papers/037.pdf) give a strongly polynomial algorithm for a different lending model allowing lenders to split money among borrowers. No result settling the precise mass-and-integral-bundle problem above was located.

  • Used the stated blocking-pair condition directly, rather than treating the claimed linear inequalities as an alternative definition of stability.
  • Required positive rational budgets and requests, nonempty supports, complete strict rankings over counterpart types, and indifference among copies of one type.
  • Treated masses as unnormalized population quantities and carried over the assumption that total borrower population does not exceed total lender population.
  • Interpreted integral bundles as finite multisets of lender copies; several copies of one supported type may occur in a bundle.
  • Required every borrower to be fully funded, allowed overfunding and unmatched lenders, and required each assigned lender to contribute its whole budget.
  • Allowed real allocation masses over bundle configurations and made positive-mass blocking explicit through positive borrower-configuration mass and positive willing-lender mass.
  • Retained the blocking condition's preference-based replacement move without adding a requirement that replacement preserve funding.
  • Defined the otherwise unspecified size parameter m as the larger of the two support sizes and L as total binary input length.

1. Result

Deciding whether a stable allocation exists is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with a single borrower type, lender masses all equal to \(\tfrac12\), borrower mass equal to \(1\), and integer budgets and request.

The reduction below handles both real allocation masses and unrestricted repetition of lender types within bundles.

2. Exact aggregate funding forces exact bundles

First consider any instance with one borrower type, mass \(1\), request \(c\), and
\(\sum_{i\in I}q_i a_i=c\).

Write \(x_n\) for its allocation masses and \(y_i=\sum_n n_i x_n\) for the used mass of lender type \(i\). Feasibility gives

\( c=c\sum_n x_n \leq \sum_n\bigl(\sum_i q_i n_i\bigr)x_n =\sum_i q_i y_i \leq\sum_i q_i a_i=c. \)

All sums have nonnegative terms, so the rearrangement is valid. The capacity constraints make the total funding finite.

Thus every inequality is an equality. Two consequences follow:

  • Every positively used bundle funds exactly \(c\): the nonnegative sum \(\sum_n(\sum_iq_i n_i-c)x_n\) is zero.
  • Every lender type is fully used: \(\sum_iq_i(a_i-y_i)=0\), with \(q_i>0\) and \(a_i-y_i\geq0\), implies \(y_i=a_i\) for every \(i\).

Every feasible allocation in such an instance is therefore stable. There are no unmatched lenders, and with only one borrower type no lender strictly prefers another borrower type to its current partner.

3. Polynomial construction from PARTITION

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_r\), determine whether a subset \(A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\) satisfies \(2\sum_{k\in A}w_k=\sum_{k=1}^r w_k\). Its hardness is established in [Karp’s *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Assume \(r\geq1\), and define

\( W=\sum_{k=1}^r w_k,\qquad B=2rW+r+2,\qquad E=B^r+2W,\qquad H=(r+1)(E+1). \)

Also set \(S=\sum_{k=1}^r B^k+W\). These choices ensure

\( H>rE\geq S,\qquad B>2rW,\qquad B>r. \)

Indeed, \(S\leq rB^r+W\leq r(B^r+2W)=rE\).

Create two lender types \((k,0)\) and \((k,1)\) for each item, with masses and budgets

\( a_{k,0}=a_{k,1}=\tfrac12,\qquad q_{k,0}=H+B^k,\qquad q_{k,1}=H+B^k+2w_k. \)

Create one borrower type \(j\), with \(b_j=1\) and request \(c=rH+S\). Each lender ranks the sole borrower type; the borrower uses any fixed strict ordering of the lender types.

All masses are positive binary rationals. Moreover,

\( \sum_i a_i=r\geq1=b_j, \)

and

\( \sum_iq_i a_i =\tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^r(q_{k,0}+q_{k,1}) =rH+\sum_{k=1}^rB^k+W =c. \)

Thus the preceding exact-funding argument applies.

The construction has \(m=2r\) and \(\tau=2r+1\). Each numerical value has \(O(r\log B+\log r+\log W)\) bits. Since \(\log B=O(\log r+\log W)\), the full output, including preferences, has polynomial length in the source input and is computable in polynomial time.

4. Exact bundles encode partitions, despite unrestricted counts

Let \(n=(n_{k,0},n_{k,1})_{k=1}^r\) be any nonnegative integer bundle satisfying \(\sum_{k,s}q_{k,s}n_{k,s}=c\). Define its total lender count by \(M=\sum_{k,s}n_{k,s}\).

We first prove \(M=r\). Every budget lies between \(H\) and \(H+E\).

If \(M\geq r+1\), its funding is at least

\( (r+1)H>rH+S=c, \)

a contradiction.

If \(M\leq r-1\), its funding is at most

\( (r-1)(H+E) =rH-H+(r-1)E <rH<c, \)

also a contradiction, because \(H>rE\). Hence \(M=r\).

Put \(t_k=n_{k,0}+n_{k,1}\) and \(U=2\sum_{k=1}^r w_k n_{k,1}\). Subtracting \(rH\) from the exact-funding equation gives

\( \sum_{k=1}^r t_k B^k+U =\sum_{k=1}^r B^k+W. \)

Because \(M=r\),

\( 0\leq t_k\leq r<B,\qquad 0\leq U\leq2rW<B,\qquad 0<W<B. \)

Consequently both sides are base-\(B\) expansions with every coefficient smaller than \(B\). Reducing modulo \(B\) first gives \(U=W\). Subtracting this equality and dividing by \(B\), then repeating the same argument, gives \(t_k=1\) for every \(k\).

Therefore \(n_{k,1}\in\{0,1\}\), and the subset \(A=\{k:n_{k,1}=1\}\) satisfies

\( 2\sum_{k\in A}w_k=U=W. \)

Thus every exact-funding integer bundle yields a partition. Repetition was permitted throughout the argument; the encoding itself excludes it.

5. Equivalence of the instances

Suppose the constructed society admits a stable allocation. It is feasible, so Section 2 implies that every positively used bundle funds exactly \(c\). Since the allocation masses sum to \(1\), at least one such bundle exists. Section 4 then produces a partition of the source instance.

Conversely, suppose \(A\) is a partition. Define a bundle \(n^A\) that uses type \((k,1)\) when \(k\in A\) and type \((k,0)\) otherwise, once for each item. Its funding is

\( rH+\sum_{k=1}^r B^k+2\sum_{k\in A}w_k=c. \)

The complementary subset \(A^c\) is also a partition, so its corresponding bundle \(n^{A^c}\) also funds exactly \(c\).

Assign borrower mass \(\tfrac12\) to each of these two bundles. Each lender type occurs in exactly one bundle and consequently uses exactly its available mass \(\tfrac12\). The allocation is feasible and, by Section 2, stable.

We have proved a polynomial many-one reduction:

\( (w_1,\ldots,w_r)\text{ admits a partition} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed society admits a stable allocation}. \)

Therefore the specified decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
