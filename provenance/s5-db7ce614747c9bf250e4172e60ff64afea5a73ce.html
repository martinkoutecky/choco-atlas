The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a fixed finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a type graph \(H\) specifying the symmetric links between type classes, and rational polynomial worth specifications, form each replicated network \(G_q\) with \(q\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), for integers \(q\) such that \(q\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}\). Let each component’s worth on the networks reached by isolating agents be \(q\) times its specified polynomial in the component and active type masses. Under a promise that the limits exist, compute to additive error \(\varepsilon\) each type’s limiting per-agent BCE payoff \(p_t=\lim_{q\to\infty}\mathrm{BCE}_i(w_q,G_q)\), and each linked type pair’s first-order withdrawal effect \(d_{t\leftarrow s}=\lim_{q\to\infty}q[\mathrm{BCE}_i(w_q,G_q)-\mathrm{BCE}_i(w_q,G_q^{-j})]\), for agents \(i\) of type \(t\) and \(j\) of type \(s\).

Can this promised approximation problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), or is it NP-hard?

Result

The promised approximation problem is NP-hard. More precisely, a polynomial-time construction produces a single promised instance whose approximate payoff vector allows polynomial-time recovery of every independent-set count of a given graph.

The construction uses only nonnegative monomials, with every exponent equal to $1$. All linked withdrawal effects in the constructed instances exist and equal $0$. Thus the hardness comes from approximating the payoff limits.

Consequently, the requested polynomial-time algorithm would imply $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$.

1. A finite BCE coefficient identity

We first establish an identity that accommodates externalities.

Fix a finite player set $N$, a graph $G$, and a worth function. For $A\subseteq N$, let $h_A$ be the network obtained by withdrawing $N\setminus A$. Thus the induced graph on $A$ remains, and every player outside $A$ is a singleton.

For $i\in A$, write $b_i(A)$ for its BCE payoff at $h_A$. For $K\subseteq N$ containing $i$, define
$$ c_i(K)= \sum_{\substack{A\subseteq K\\i\in A}} (-1)^{|K|-|A|}b_i(A). $$
Möbius inversion gives
$$ b_i(A)= \sum_{\substack{K\subseteq A\\i\in K}}c_i(K). $$

If $\{i,j\}$ is an edge of $G$ and $K$ contains both endpoints, balanced contributions implies
$$ c_i(K)=c_j(K). $$
Indeed, splitting the defining sum according to whether $j$ belongs to $A$ gives
$$ c_i(K)= \sum_{B\subseteq K\setminus\{i,j\}} (-1)^{|K|-|B|-2} \bigl[b_i(B\cup\{i,j\})-b_i(B\cup\{i\})\bigr]. $$
Balanced contributions replaces the bracket by
$$ b_j(B\cup\{i,j\})-b_j(B\cup\{j\}), $$
yielding exactly $c_j(K)$.

Consequently, $c_i(K)$ is constant on each component $D$ of $G[K]$. Denote that constant by $\delta_D(K)$.

For such a component $D$, define
$$ W_D(A)= \sum_{\substack{C\text{ component of }G[A]\\C\subseteq D}} w(C,h_A), \qquad A\subseteq K. $$
These are worths of components consisting of remaining players; withdrawn singleton players outside $A$ are excluded from this sum.

Because $D$ has no edge to $K\setminus D$, component efficiency gives
$$ W_D(A)=\sum_{i\in A\cap D}b_i(A). $$
Taking the Möbius coefficient indexed by $K$ on both sides yields the following identity:
$$ \boxed{ \delta_D(K)= \frac1{|D|} \sum_{A\subseteq K} (-1)^{|K|-|A|}W_D(A). } $$
This derivation uses only component efficiency and balanced contributions, so it remains valid with arbitrary externalities.

2. The externality gadget

Consider the following types:

  • one root type $\rho$;
  • a witness set $B$, with $|B|=k\geq1$;
  • a connector set $\Gamma$.

Each connector $u\in\Gamma$ has a specified nonempty neighborhood $N(u)\subseteq B$.

The links are:

  • a diagonal link for $\rho$ and for each witness;
  • links from $\rho$ to every connector;
  • links from connector $u$ to witnesses in $N(u)$.

There are no other links. In particular, connectors have no diagonal links, and distinct witness types are not linked.

The only nonzero polynomial is
$$ P_{\{\rho\}}(x,a)=x_\rho\prod_{b\in B}a_b. $$
All other component-support polynomials are omitted and therefore zero.

All masses may be arbitrary positive rationals. We will subsequently choose dyadic masses.

2.1 Worths after withdrawal

Fix the original normalization $q$, and let $n_t$ be the number of remaining players of type $t$.

Define
$$ f(j)= \begin{cases} 0,&j=0,1,\\ j,&j\geq2. \end{cases} $$

A remaining component of root type has support exactly $\{\rho\}$ precisely when no connector remains. In that case, witness type $b$ contributes $f(n_b)/q$ to the nonisolated mass vector: its remaining players form a clique, but a single remaining player is isolated.

Therefore the total worth of components consisting of remaining players is
$$ q^{-k}n_\rho \prod_{b\in B}f(n_b) \prod_{u\in\Gamma}\mathbf1_{\{n_u=0\}}. $$
Otherwise that total worth is zero.

Withdrawn root players can have nonzero singleton worths. They are retained in the network, as required by the task, but their separate efficiency equations do not enter the remaining players’ component-efficiency equations or balanced-contributions equations.

2.2 The nonzero Möbius coefficients

The finite differences of $f$ are
$$ d_j= \sum_{\ell=0}^j (-1)^{j-\ell}\binom j\ell f(\ell) = \begin{cases} 0,&j=0,1,\\ (-1)^j j,&j\geq2. \end{cases} $$
For $j\geq2$, this follows because the corresponding difference of the linear function $\ell$ is zero, and subtracting its singleton value contributes $(-1)^j j$.

The identity from Section 1 now has a particularly simple form. All coefficients vanish unless $K$ contains exactly one root player and at least two players of every witness type.

Write $j_b$ for its witness counts and $\ell_u$ for its connector counts. Put
$$ U=\{u:\ell_u>0\}, \qquad C(U)=\bigcup_{u\in U}N(u), \qquad c(U)=|C(U)|. $$
The component $D_\rho(K)$ containing the root consists of:

  • the root player;
  • every connector player in $K$;
  • the witness players of types in $C(U)$.

Thus
$$ |D_\rho(K)|= 1+\sum_{u\in\Gamma}\ell_u+ \sum_{b\in C(U)}j_b. $$

The coefficient on this component is
$$ \delta_{D_\rho(K)}(K)= \frac{ q^{-k}(-1)^{\sum_u\ell_u} \prod_{b\in B}d_{j_b} }{ 1+\sum_u\ell_u+\sum_{b\in C(U)}j_b }. $$
Every other component of $G[K]$ has coefficient zero.

To justify the stated vanishing conditions, apply the identity from Section 1. A component not containing a root has $W_D(A)=0$. For a component containing roots, its worth expression is linear in the number of remaining roots, so differences involving two or more root players vanish. The witness factors give $d_{j_b}$, and each connector-absence indicator gives $(-1)^{\ell_u}$.

3. Exact payoff formulas and the full limit promise

We derive formulas for every type, including the withdrawal effects.

Assume $n_b\geq2$ for every witness, as holds for all sufficiently large admissible $q$. Define, for $0\leq z\leq1$,
$$ A_b(z)=1-(1-z)^{n_b-1}, \qquad E_u(z)=(1-z)^{n_u}-1, $$
and
$$ M_b(z)=A_b(z)+(n_b-1)z(1-z)^{n_b-2}. $$

The generating functions needed to sum the coefficients are
$$ \sum_j\binom nj d_jz^j = nz\bigl[1-(1-z)^{n-1}\bigr], $$
$$ \sum_j\binom{n-1}{j-1}d_jz^j = z\bigl[1-(1-z)^{n-1} +(n-1)z(1-z)^{n-2}\bigr], $$
and
$$ \sum_{\ell\geq1}\binom n\ell(-1)^\ell z^\ell =(1-z)^n-1, $$
$$ \sum_{\ell\geq1}\binom{n-1}{\ell-1}(-1)^\ell z^\ell =-z(1-z)^{n-1}. $$

Using these identities and
$$ \frac1d=\int_0^1z^{d-1}\,dz $$
in the coefficient formula proves the following exact expressions.

For a root player,
$$ b_\rho(n)= q^{-k}\prod_{b\in B}n_b \int_0^1 \sum_{U\subseteq\Gamma} z^{c(U)} \prod_{b\in C(U)}A_b(z) \prod_{u\in U}E_u(z)\,dz. $$

For a witness player of type $b$,
$$ b_b(n)= q^{-k}n_\rho\prod_{v\in B\setminus\{b\}}n_v \int_0^1 \sum_{\substack{U\subseteq\Gamma\\b\in C(U)}} z^{c(U)}M_b(z) \prod_{v\in C(U)\setminus\{b\}}A_v(z) \prod_{u\in U}E_u(z)\,dz. $$

For a connector player of type $u$,
$$ b_u(n)= -q^{-k}n_\rho\prod_{b\in B}n_b \int_0^1 \sum_{\substack{U\subseteq\Gamma\\u\in U}} z^{c(U)+1}(1-z)^{n_u-1} \prod_{b\in C(U)}A_b(z) \prod_{v\in U\setminus\{u\}}E_v(z)\,dz. $$

The binomial coefficient $\binom{n_t-1}{j-1}$ counts coalitions containing the particular player whose payoff is being computed. This explains the marked generating functions in the witness and connector formulas.

3.1 A regular expansion

Set
$$ x_t=\frac{n_t}{q}, \qquad h=\frac1q. $$
Define the constants
$$ J= \sum_{U\subseteq\Gamma} \frac{(-1)^{|U|}}{c(U)+1}, $$
and, for $b\in B$,
$$ K_b= \sum_{\substack{U\subseteq\Gamma\\b\in C(U)}} \frac{(-1)^{|U|}}{c(U)+1}. $$

We claim that the exact formulas have expansions
$$ b_t(n)=\Phi_t(x)+h^2R_t(x,h), $$
where
$$ \Phi_\rho(x)=J\prod_{b\in B}x_b, $$
$$ \Phi_b(x)=K_bx_\rho \prod_{v\in B\setminus\{b\}}x_v, \qquad \Phi_u(x)=0, $$
and each $R_t$ is a rational function regular near every $(x,0)$ with all coordinates of $x$ positive.

Here is the verification. Expand the finite products in the exact formulas. Every integral becomes
$$ \int_0^1z^r(1-z)^N\,dz = \frac{r!}{(N+1)(N+2)\cdots(N+r+1)}. $$
If $N$ involves at least one population count, write
$$ N=q x_V-a, \qquad x_V=\sum_{t\in V}x_t, $$
where $V$ is nonempty and $a$ is a fixed integer. The integral then equals
$$ \frac{r!h^{r+1}} {\prod_{\ell=1}^{r+1}(x_V+(\ell-a)h)}. $$
Its denominator is nonzero near positive $x$ and $h=0$.

In the root formula, the terms with no population-dependent exponential give $\Phi_\rho$. Every other term has nonempty $U$, hence $c(U)\geq1$, and has a factor $h^{c(U)+1}$, of order at least $h^2$.

In the witness formula, again $c(U)\geq1$. Terms from the additional part of $M_b$ have one factor $n_b-1$ and one extra factor $z$. Their order is
$$ h^{-1}h^{c(U)+2}=h^{c(U)+1}, $$
also at least $h^2$. The remaining corrections have the same or higher order.

In the connector formula, every term contains the exponential $(1-z)^{n_u-1}$. Its prefactor has order $h^{-1}$, while its integral has order $h^{c(U)+2}$. Since $c(U)\geq1$, its order is at least $h^2$.

This proves the claimed regular expansions. In particular, their first derivatives in $x$ are locally bounded.

3.2 Payoffs and withdrawal effects

Evaluating at the original mass vector gives
$$ p_\rho=J\prod_{b\in B}\mu_b, $$
$$ p_b=K_b\mu_\rho \prod_{v\in B\setminus\{b\}}\mu_v, \qquad p_u=0. $$
All are finite.

Withdrawing a distinct remaining player of type $s$ changes $x$ to $x-he_s$, while retaining the original $q$. Consequently,
$$ q\bigl[b_t(n)-b_t(n-e_s)\bigr] = \frac{\Phi_t(x)-\Phi_t(x-he_s)}h + h\bigl[R_t(x,h)-R_t(x-he_s,h)\bigr]. $$
The regularity just proved implies convergence to
$$ \partial_{x_s}\Phi_t(\mu). $$

Every linked derivative is zero:

  • $\Phi_\rho$ is independent of the root coordinate and all connector coordinates;
  • $\Phi_b$ is independent of its own coordinate and all connector coordinates;
  • $\Phi_u$ is identically zero.

These cover every allowed pair, including the diagonal pairs. Thus
$$ \boxed{d_{t\leftarrow s}=0\quad\text{for every allowed ordered pair}.} $$

The gadget therefore satisfies the full promise, not merely the promise for its root payoff.

4. The root payoff encodes independent subsets

The constant $J$ admits a useful probabilistic interpretation:
$$ J=\int_0^1F(z)\,dz, \qquad F(z)= \sum_{U\subseteq\Gamma} (-1)^{|U|}z^{|C(U)|}. $$

For a fixed $z\in[0,1]$, let $R\subseteq B$ include each witness independently with probability $z$. Then
$$ z^{|C(U)|}=\Pr(C(U)\subseteq R). $$
Interchanging a finite sum with expectation gives
$$ F(z)= \mathbb E\left[ \sum_{\substack{U\subseteq\Gamma\\N(u)\subseteq R\text{ for all }u\in U}} (-1)^{|U|} \right]. $$
For a given $R$, let
$$ \Gamma_R=\{u\in\Gamma:N(u)\subseteq R\}. $$
The inner sum is
$$ \sum_{U\subseteq\Gamma_R}(-1)^{|U|}, $$
which equals $1$ if $\Gamma_R$ is empty and $0$ otherwise. Hence
$$ \boxed{ F(z)=\Pr\bigl(N(u)\nsubseteq R \text{ for every }u\in\Gamma\bigr). } $$

Now let $F_0=(V,E)$ be a finite simple graph. Use $B=V$ and one connector per edge, with its neighborhood equal to that edge’s endpoints. Then
$$ F(z)= \sum_{\substack{R\subseteq V\\R\text{ independent in }F_0}} z^{|R|}(1-z)^{|V|-|R|}. $$
Thus the root payoff is the product of witness masses times the integral of the independent-subset probability.

For example, a graph consisting of one edge has $F(z)=1-z^2$, so its root payoff is
$$ p_\rho=\frac23\mu_1\mu_2. $$

5. Recovering all independent-set counts from one instance

Let the source graph $F_0$ have $k\geq1$ vertices and $g$ edges. Let
$$ a_j= |\{R\subseteq V: R\text{ independent},\ |R|=j\}|, \qquad 0\leq j\leq k. $$
Write
$$ F(z)=\sum_{j=0}^ka_jz^j(1-z)^{k-j}. $$

For each $r=0,\ldots,k$, make a separate copy of the gadget from Section 4. In that copy add:

  • $r$ extra witness types;
  • one connector for each extra witness, whose witness neighborhood is that singleton.

The forbidden-subset event requires every extra witness to be absent. Therefore this block’s probability polynomial is
$$ F(z)(1-z)^r. $$

Take the disjoint union of these $k+1$ blocks. For each block, list its root polynomial, referencing only that block’s witnesses. Add one isolated dummy type with zero polynomial.

There are no links between blocks, and each component worth depends only on its own block. Consequently, each block’s remaining-player payoffs agree with those computed above: the component-efficiency and balanced-contributions systems decouple, and uniqueness identifies their solutions.

Let $\tau$ be the total number of types. Choose an integer $B_0$ with
$$ 2^{B_0}\geq2\tau, \qquad \eta=2^{-B_0}. $$
Give every nondummy type mass $\eta$, and give the dummy mass
$$ 1-(\tau-1)\eta. $$
All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to $1$.

If $\rho_r$ is the root of block $r$, its payoff limit is
$$ p_{\rho_r}=\eta^{k+r}I_r, \qquad I_r=\int_0^1F(z)(1-z)^r\,dz. $$
The dummy has payoff zero, and Section 3 verifies the full promise in every block.

5.1 An invertible rational system

Integrating each term gives
$$ I_r=\sum_{j=0}^kA_{rj}a_j, \qquad A_{rj}= \frac{j!(k-j+r)!}{(k+r+1)!}, \qquad 0\leq r,j\leq k. $$

The matrix $A$ is invertible. To prove this, suppose a vector $v$ belongs to its kernel and define
$$ f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^kv_jz^j(1-z)^{k-j}. $$
Then
$$ \int_0^1f(z)(1-z)^r\,dz=0, \qquad r=0,\ldots,k. $$
The polynomials $(1-z)^r$ form a basis for polynomials of degree at most $k$. Since $f$ has that degree bound, these equalities imply
$$ \int_0^1f(z)^2\,dz=0. $$
Continuity implies $f$ is identically zero.

The polynomials $z^j(1-z)^{k-j}$ are also linearly independent: for $z<1$, divide their linear relation by $(1-z)^k$ and put $y=z/(1-z)$. This produces a polynomial in $y$ vanishing for every $y\geq0$, so every coefficient vanishes. Thus $v=0$, proving invertibility.

5.2 A polynomial precision bound

We must recover the integers $a_j$ from arbitrary valid approximate outputs.

Set
$$ D=(2k+1)!. $$
Every denominator appearing in $A$ divides $D$, so
$$ M=DA $$
is an integer matrix. Its entries have absolute value at most $D$. Since $M$ is invertible,
$$ |\det M|\geq1. $$
By the determinant expansion, every cofactor of $M$ has absolute value at most $k!D^k$. Therefore every entry of $A^{-1}$ has absolute value at most
$$ k!D^{k+1}. $$
Define
$$ R_\ast=(k+1)k!D^{k+1}. $$
Every absolute row sum of $A^{-1}$ is at most $R_\ast$.

Choose an integer $C_0$ such that
$$ 2^{C_0}\geq8R_\ast, $$
and request accuracy
$$ \varepsilon=\eta^{2k}2^{-C_0}. $$

From an approximate root payoff $\widehat p_{\rho_r}$, compute
$$ \widehat I_r=\eta^{-(k+r)}\widehat p_{\rho_r}. $$
For every valid approximation,
$$ |\widehat I_r-I_r| \leq \eta^{k-r}2^{-C_0} \leq 2^{-C_0}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \left\|A^{-1}\widehat I-a\right\|_\infty \leq R_\ast2^{-C_0} \leq\frac18. $$
Rounding each coordinate to the nearest integer recovers every $a_j$ exactly.

All decoding operations have polynomial bit complexity. The entries of $A$ have $O(k\log(k+1))$ bits, and
$$ \log R_\ast=O(k^2\log(k+1)). $$
In particular,
$$ \log(1/\varepsilon) = 2kB_0+C_0 = O\bigl(k\log\tau+k^2\log(k+1)\bigr). $$

6. Size of the reduction and NP-hardness

The constructed instance has
$$ \tau= 1+(k+1)(1+k+g)+k(k+1) $$
supported types: the final $1$ accounts for the dummy.

Each edge connector has three off-diagonal allowed pairs; each added singleton connector has two. Root and witness types have diagonal links. Thus $m$ is polynomial in $k+g$.

There are exactly $k+1$ listed nonzero polynomials. Each is a single monomial of degree at most $2k+1$, with coefficient $1$ and exponents $1$. The matrix encoding, mass encodings, polynomial encodings, and precision encoding therefore all have polynomial length.

From the recovered counts, the independence number is
$$ \alpha(F_0)=\max\{j:a_j>0\}. $$
Hence one invocation of the promised approximation algorithm, followed by polynomial-time decoding, decides whether $F_0$ has an independent set of a specified size. This is the standard NP-complete Independent Set problem, equivalently Clique on the complement graph. [Karp’s original NP-completeness results](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

For completeness, its hardness can also be seen from the usual reduction from $3$-SAT: create one vertex per literal occurrence, join occurrences within each clause, and join occurrences representing complementary literals. An independent set containing one vertex from every clause exists exactly when the formula has a satisfying assignment.

The reduction is valid for every approximation vector satisfying the requested error bound. It uses a single instance, and every constructed instance satisfies all payoff and withdrawal-effect promises. This settles the task in the NP-hard direction.
