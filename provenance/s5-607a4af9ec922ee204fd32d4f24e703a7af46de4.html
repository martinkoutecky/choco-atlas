The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two balanced populations represented by finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational type masses \((\alpha_a)_{a\in A}\) and \((\beta_b)_{b\in B}\), and a strict ranking of opposite-side types for each type, decide for specified \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\) whether there exists a stable mass matching \(x\geq 0\) with \(x_{ab}>0\). Here \(x_{ab}\) is the mass matched between \(a\) and \(b\), its row and column sums equal the input masses, and stability requires that for every type pair at least one side has zero mass currently matched to types it ranks below the other type.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be nonempty, disjoint type sets. Each society is given by its support: its listed types have positive binary-rational masses, with \(\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_a=\sum_{b\in B}\beta_b=1\). Set \(\tau=|A|+|B|\), the total support size, not the number of possible preference types; let \(m=\max\{|A|,|B|\}\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each type has a strict, complete ranking \(\succ_a\) or \(\succ_b\) of opposite-side types; every pair is acceptable, and preferences depend only on types.
A mass matching is \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{A\times B}\) satisfying \(\sum_{b\in B}x_{ab}=\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_{a\in A}x_{ab}=\beta_b\).
It is stable if, for every \((a,b)\in A\times B\), either \(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}=0\) or \(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}=0\).
Given specified \(a_0\in A\) and \(b_0\in B\), the decision problem asks whether some stable mass matching satisfies \(x_{a_0b_0}>0\).

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents with strict preferences, extended deferred acceptance enumerates all stable partners of a specified agent in polynomial time, hence decides stable-pair membership. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.03890)
For the related bipartite stable allocation problem with real quotas and edge capacities, finding a stable allocation and minimizing a linear cost over stable allocations have polynomial-time algorithms. [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each population's equal positive total mass to one.
  • Interpreted rankings of opposite-side types as complete lists with all pairs acceptable, retaining the balanced, fully matched specialization in the graded question.
  • Allowed matching entries to be arbitrary nonnegative real numbers, with no minimum positive engagement.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size, m as the maximum side support size, and L as the total input bit length.

Conclusion

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). The key is that its stability condition agrees exactly with bipartite stable allocation after assigning each edge the redundant capacity \(\min\{\alpha_a,\beta_b\}\).

A conservative bound for the algorithm below is
\[ O\!\left(L^3+m^6\tau^2\bigl(L+\log(m+\tau)\bigr)\right) \]
bit operations. It never expands a type into individual agents.

1. Equivalence of stability conditions

Construct the complete bipartite graph on \(A\) and \(B\). Give its vertices quotas \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_b\), retain their given rankings, and give edge \((a,b)\) capacity
\[ u_{ab}=\min\{\alpha_a,\beta_b\}. \]

These edge capacities impose no additional restriction on a mass matching: nonnegativity and the marginal equations already imply
\[ x_{ab}\leq\alpha_a,\qquad x_{ab}\leq\beta_b. \]

In the stable allocation model, \((a,b)\) blocks precisely when it is unsaturated and each endpoint has positive allocation to a strictly worse partner. Write
\[ W_a(b)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}, \qquad W_b(a)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}. \]

If both quantities are positive, the marginal equations imply
\[ \alpha_a-x_{ab}\geq W_a(b)>0, \qquad \beta_b-x_{ab}\geq W_b(a)>0. \]
Consequently,
\[ x_{ab}<\min\{\alpha_a,\beta_b\}=u_{ab}. \]

Thus the additional unsaturation condition is automatic whenever both endpoints have worse allocations. Conversely, an allocation blocking edge has both quantities positive by definition. Therefore the two stability conditions are equivalent.

2. Dummy vertices do not change the problem

To fit the dummy-vertex convention used by Dean–Munshi, add vertices \(a_*\) and \(b_*\), each with quota \(1\).

Every real vertex ranks its dummy partner last. Each dummy ranks all real vertices, in any strict order, above the other dummy. Set
\[ u_{a b_*}=\alpha_a,\qquad u_{a_* b}=\beta_b,\qquad u_{a_*b_*}=1. \]

We prove that stable allocations in this augmented instance correspond exactly to stable mass matchings.

Let \(y\) be an augmented stable allocation, and put
\[ R=\sum_{a\in A,\ b\in B}y_{ab}. \]
The real-vertex quota equations give
\[ \sum_{a\in A}y_{a b_*}=1-R =\sum_{b\in B}y_{a_* b}. \]

Suppose \(R<1\). Choose \(a\) and \(b\) such that
\[ y_{a b_*}>0,\qquad y_{a_* b}>0. \]
Their quota equations then imply
\[ y_{ab}<\alpha_a,\qquad y_{ab}<\beta_b. \]
Hence \((a,b)\) is unsaturated, and both endpoints have positive allocation to their strictly worse dummy partners. It blocks, contradicting stability.

Therefore \(R=1\). All real-to-dummy allocations vanish, the real submatrix has exactly the required marginals, and \(y_{a_*b_*}=1\). Section 1 shows that this real submatrix is a stable mass matching.

Conversely, extend a stable mass matching \(x\) by setting \(y_{a_*b_*}=1\) and all other dummy allocations to zero. Real edges cannot block by Section 1. An edge between a real vertex and a dummy cannot block because the real vertex has no allocation to a worse partner. The dummy-to-dummy edge is saturated. Thus the extension is stable.

In particular, the correspondence preserves \(x_{a_0b_0}\).

3. Apply stable allocation optimization

We use the following established result.

Dean–Munshi optimization theorem. For bipartite stable allocation with strict rankings, vertex quotas, edge capacities, and linear edge costs, a minimum-cost stable allocation can be computed in polynomial time. Their construction produces an initial stable allocation, at most \(2E\) rotations, and a precedence graph with \(O(EN)\) arcs, where \(N\) and \(E\) denote vertex and edge counts. Each rotation specifies an alternating cycle and a multiplicity. Applying a predecessor-closed subset of rotations fully gives a stable allocation, and every linear objective has an optimum of this form. The construction takes \(O(EN+E\log N)\) arithmetic operations; optimization reduces to minimum-weight closure. [Dean–Munshi, Sections 3–4](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

All hypotheses hold here: the augmented graph is bipartite, rankings are strict, quotas and capacities are positive rationals, and dummy vertices have the required preference positions.

Assign costs
\[ c_{ab}= \begin{cases} -1,&(a,b)=(a_0,b_0),\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \]
including zero costs on dummy edges. Then
\[ \sum_e c_e y_e=-y_{a_0b_0}. \]

Let \(y^*\) be a minimum-cost stable allocation. Answer yes exactly when
\[ y^*_{a_0b_0}>0. \]

Indeed, if some stable mass matching has positive designated entry, its augmented extension has negative cost, so the optimum has negative cost. Conversely, negative optimum cost means that the real submatrix of \(y^*\) is a stable mass matching with positive designated entry.

This also establishes attainment of the optimum; no limiting argument concerning strict positivity is needed.

4. Exact arithmetic and running time

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Multiply all quotas and capacities by \(D\). This preserves stability and positivity of the designated entry.

The resulting data are integers. Every quota and edge capacity is at most \(D\), including the dummy quotas. Computing this scaled instance by elementary integer arithmetic takes \(O(L^3)\) bit operations.

The stable allocation construction uses bottleneck minima and additions or subtractions. Starting from integer data, its initial allocation and all full rotation multiplicities are therefore integers. Allocation entries and rotation multiplicities are at most \(D\).

For completeness, minimum-weight closure can be implemented with an ordinary maximum-flow algorithm. Write \(r_\pi\) for a rotation’s multiplicity and \(v^\pi_e\in\{-1,0,1\}\) for its change on edge \(e\) per unit applied. Its objective weight is
\[ w_\pi=r_\pi\sum_e c_e v^\pi_e =-r_\pi v^\pi_{a_0b_0}, \]
so \(|w_\pi|\leq D\).

Create one network vertex per rotation. For \(w_\pi<0\), add a source arc of capacity \(-w_\pi\); for \(w_\pi>0\), add a sink arc of capacity \(w_\pi\). For each precedence constraint requiring \(\pi\) whenever \(\rho\) is selected, add the arc \(\rho\to\pi\) with capacity
\[ K=1+\sum_\pi |w_\pi|. \]

The cut selecting no rotations costs less than \(K\). Hence a minimum cut cannot cut a precedence arc, so its source-side rotations form a predecessor-closed subset. For such a subset \(S\), the cut capacity equals
\[ \sum_{w_\pi<0}(-w_\pi)+\sum_{\pi\in S}w_\pi. \]
Thus a minimum cut minimizes precisely the required rotation objective.

There are \(O(E)\) network vertices and \(O(EN)\) arcs. Edmonds–Karp therefore uses
\[ O(E^3N^2) \]
arithmetic operations. All numerical quantities have \(O(L+\log E)\) bits: the network capacities are bounded by \(1+2ED\), and the same bound controls total flow.

Finally, in our augmented instance,
\[ N=\tau+2,\qquad E=(|A|+1)(|B|+1)\leq(m+1)^2. \]
Including scaling, allocation construction, closure optimization, and reconstruction yields the stated conservative bound
\[ O\!\left(L^3+m^6\tau^2\bigl(L+\log(m+\tau)\bigr)\right). \]

The strict positivity test is exact on the resulting integer allocation.
