1. Definitions and valid transfers

Put $N=2^m$. For a state $x$, write $x_A^\sigma$ for the mass of type $(\sigma,A)$, and define
$$ s(x)=\min\{v(A):x_A^U>0\}, \qquad H(x)=\max\{h(A):x_A^U+x_A^O>0\}. $$
Both extrema exist because both cohorts have positive mass.

Near-EF1 is equivalent to
$$ v(A)\ge H(x) \quad\text{for every supported ordinary bundle }A. $$
Indeed, the comparisons required of an ordinary holder of $A$ are exactly $v(A)\ge h(D)$ for every supported bundle $D$. Consequently, in a near-EF1 state,
$$ x\text{ is EF1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s(x)\ge H(x). $$

1.1. Monotonicity of $h$

For every bundle $A$, monotonicity of $v$ gives $h(A)\le v(A)$.

Also, $h$ is monotone under inclusion. To prove this, let $A\subseteq D$. The case $A=\varnothing$ follows from nonnegativity. Otherwise, for every $q\in D$:

  • If $q\in A$, then

$$ v(D\setminus\{q\})\ge v(A\setminus\{q\})\ge h(A). $$

  • If $q\notin A$, then

$$ v(D\setminus\{q\})\ge v(A)\ge h(A). $$

Taking the minimum over $q\in D$ proves $h(D)\ge h(A)$.

For a proposed transfer of $q\in D\setminus B$, set
$$ E=D\setminus\{q\}, \qquad C=B\cup\{q\}. $$
We have
$$ h(E)\le h(D),\qquad h(B)\le h(C)\le v(B),\qquad v(C)\ge v(B). $$
The upper bound $h(C)\le v(B)$ follows by removing $q$ from $C$.

1.2. Proven exact transfer criterion

Proposition 1. Suppose $x$ is near-EF1 but not EF1. Write $s=s(x)$ and $H=H(x)$, so $s<H$. For every permitted positive amount, the proposed transfer is valid exactly when
$$ v(E)\ge H \quad\text{and}\quad \bigl(h(C)=h(B)\ \text{or}\ h(C)\le s\bigr). $$
In particular, validity is independent of whether the amount exhausts either input type.

Proof: necessity of the recipient condition. Suppose
$$ h(C)>h(B)\quad\text{and}\quad h(C)>s. $$
Since $h(C)\le v(B)$, necessarily $v(B)>s$. Choose a supported marked type $A$ with $v(A)=s$. Its individuals are not recipients because their bundle differs from $B$.

For every such individual and every selected recipient, envy changes from
$$ \max\{0,h(B)-s\} \quad\text{to}\quad \max\{0,h(C)-s\}. $$
This is a strict increase. The two populations are disjoint and have positive mass, so these violating ordered pairs have positive product measure. Thus the required almost-everywhere condition fails.

Proof: necessity of the donor condition. If $h(D)=H$, the definition of $h$ directly gives
$$ v(E)\ge h(D)=H. $$
If $h(D)<H$, some other supported type attains $H$. An ordinary maximizer is unchanged. A marked maximizer is either unchanged or receives an item; in the latter case its $h$ cannot decrease. Therefore the resulting maximum supported $h$ is at least $H$. The selected ordinary donors afterward hold $E$ with positive mass, so near-EF1 requires $v(E)\ge H$.

Proof: sufficiency. Assume both conditions hold. We check all comparisons with a marked first individual.

  • An unchanged marked individual comparing with an unchanged individual has an unchanged comparison.
  • An unchanged marked individual comparing with a selected ordinary donor has weakly decreased envy, because the donor’s $h$ weakly decreases.
  • An unchanged marked individual comparing with a selected recipient has unchanged envy if $h(C)=h(B)$. If $h(C)\le s$, its envy afterward is zero.
  • A selected marked recipient comparing with an unchanged individual has weakly decreased envy, because its own value weakly increases.
  • A selected marked recipient comparing with a selected donor has weakly decreased envy: its own value increases while the donor’s $h$ decreases.
  • Two selected marked recipients have zero envy before and after, since their respective common bundles are $B$ and $C$, and $h(A)\le v(A)$.

This covers every required ordered-pair case, including the case omitted in the supplied notes.

Every resulting bundle has $h$ at most $H$: this follows from monotonicity for donors and from the recipient condition for recipients. Every unchanged ordinary bundle retains value at least $H$, and selected donors have value $v(E)\ge H$. Thus near-EF1 is preserved. $\square$

Each transfer also preserves cohort masses and every supply equality: ownership of $q$ decreases by the same amount in the ordinary cohort as it increases in the marked cohort; other ownership quantities are unchanged.

1.3. A sufficient criterion using threshold bounds

Fix $s<H$. Call a state admissible for $(s,H)$ if:

$$ \begin{aligned} h(A)&\le H &&\text{for every supported bundle }A,\\ v(A)&\ge H &&\text{for every supported ordinary bundle }A,\\ v(A)&\ge s &&\text{for every supported marked bundle }A. \end{aligned} $$

Call a transfer a phase transfer for $(s,H)$ if it satisfies
$$ v(D\setminus\{q\})\ge H, \qquad h(B\cup\{q\})=h(B)\ \text{or}\ h(B\cup\{q\})\le s. $$

The sufficiency proof above uses only these bounds, not equality with the actual extrema. Therefore:

Lemma 2. Every phase transfer from an admissible state is valid and leaves the state admissible.

This remains true if the actual state has already become EF1.

2. Proven linear characterization of a fixed phase

Define two relations on bundles:
$$ \mathcal R_U(s)= \{(B,C): B\subseteq C,\ h(C)=h(B)\ \text{or}\ h(C)\le s\}, $$
and
$$ \mathcal R_O(H)= \{(D,E): E\subseteq D,\ v(E)\ge H\}. $$

The following theorem describes fixed-phase reachability solely through masses assigned to initial and final bundles.

Theorem 3. Let $x,y$ be admissible states for fixed $s<H$. A finite sequence of phase transfers takes $x$ to $y$ if and only if there are nonnegative arrays
$$ (\alpha_{BC})_{(B,C)\in\mathcal R_U(s)}, \qquad (\gamma_{DE})_{(D,E)\in\mathcal R_O(H)} $$
satisfying the marginal equations
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{C:(B,C)\in\mathcal R_U(s)}\alpha_{BC}&=x_B^U,\\ \sum_{B:(B,C)\in\mathcal R_U(s)}\alpha_{BC}&=y_C^U,\\ \sum_{E:(D,E)\in\mathcal R_O(H)}\gamma_{DE}&=x_D^O,\\ \sum_{D:(D,E)\in\mathcal R_O(H)}\gamma_{DE}&=y_E^O, \end{aligned} $$
and, for every $q\in Q$, the item-balance equation
$$ \sum_{(B,C)\in\mathcal R_U(s):\,q\in C\setminus B}\alpha_{BC} = \sum_{(D,E)\in\mathcal R_O(H):\,q\in D\setminus E}\gamma_{DE}. $$

Moreover, any feasible arrays can be implemented using at most $2m4^m$ transfers.

2.1. Necessity

Take a finite phase-transfer sequence. Partition marked individuals according to their initial bundle $B$ and final bundle $C$, and let the mass of each part be $\alpha_{BC}$. Define $\gamma_{DE}$ similarly for ordinary individuals.

Marked bundles only grow, so $B\subseteq C$. If $h(B)>s$, every addition must preserve $h$, because an increase satisfying the phase rule would have to end at an $h$-value at most $s$. Thus $h(C)=h(B)$.

If $h(B)\le s$, every subsequent $h$-value remains at most $s$: the phase rule allows either no increase or an increase ending at most $s$. Thus $h(C)\le s$. This proves $(B,C)\in\mathcal R_U(s)$.

Ordinary bundles only shrink, so $E\subseteq D$. Their final values are at least $H$ by admissibility. Hence $(D,E)\in\mathcal R_O(H)$.

The marginal equations express the initial and final masses. For each item, the mass removed from ordinary individuals equals the mass added to marked individuals, proving the balance equations.

2.2. Sufficiency

Use atomlessness to partition each initial marked type into groups of masses $\alpha_{BC}$, with assigned final bundle $C$. Partition each ordinary type using $\gamma_{DE}$, with assigned final bundle $E$.

Fix an order of the item classes. Process each class $q$ as follows:

  • Match marked groups whose assigned final bundle requires adding $q$ with ordinary groups whose assigned final bundle requires removing $q$.
  • Transfer $q$ between matched groups until all such requirements for $q$ are fulfilled.

The item-balance equation makes their total masses equal. Previous processing of other classes does not change whether $q$ is present, so every selected donor contains $q$ and every selected recipient lacks $q$.

It remains to verify validity. Consider a marked individual with initial bundle $B$, assigned final bundle $C$, and current bundle $A$. Throughout the construction,
$$ B\subseteq A\subseteq C. $$
If $h(C)\le s$, then
$$ h(A\cup\{q\})\le h(C)\le s. $$
If $h(C)=h(B)$, monotonicity gives
$$ h(B)\le h(A)\le h(A\cup\{q\})\le h(C)=h(B), $$
so the addition preserves $h$.

Likewise, an ordinary individual with assigned final bundle $E$ and current bundle $F$ retains $E$ after any assigned removal. Hence
$$ v(F\setminus\{q\})\ge v(E)\ge H. $$
Every constructed transfer is therefore a phase transfer. Lemma 2 proves validity and preservation of admissibility.

After all classes have been processed, every individual holds its assigned final bundle. The marginal equations then give exactly state $y$.

2.3. Transfer-count bound

Groups may be tracked by their initial, current, and assigned final bundles.

For marked individuals these triples satisfy $B\subseteq A\subseteq C$. Each item has four possible positions in such a triple, so there are at most $4^m$ triples. Ordinary triples $E\subseteq F\subseteq D$ have the same bound. Groups with identical triples can be merged.

While processing a fixed class $q$, use the smaller of the two matched pending-group masses as the transfer amount. Each transfer exhausts at least one pending group. Its output groups no longer require processing $q$, so no new pending group for $q$ is created.

Thus at most $2\cdot4^m$ transfers process one class, and at most $2m4^m$ process the phase. $\square$

For prescribed rational endpoints, Theorem 3 is a rational linear feasibility problem with at most $2\cdot3^m$ transport variables: there are exactly $3^m$ ordered subset pairs.

3. Proven bounded witnesses and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$

3.1. A sharper phase bound

During valid transfers before restoration, $s(x)$ cannot decrease because marked individuals only gain items. Proposition 1 shows that $H(x)$ cannot increase.

Let
$$ \mathcal V=\{v(A):A\subseteq Q\}, \qquad M=|\mathcal V|\le N. $$
Both thresholds belong to this same set. For $s$ this is immediate. For nonempty $D$, the minimum defining $h(D)$ is attained at some table entry; also $h(\varnothing)=v(\varnothing)=0$.

Rank the distinct values increasingly, using integer ranks. In a non-EF1 state, the integer
$$ g(x)=\operatorname{rank}(H(x))-\operatorname{rank}(s(x)) $$
is positive. Whenever the threshold pair changes before restoration, $g$ decreases by at least one.

Stop a successful sequence at its first EF1 state and group transfers by the threshold pair in their preceding states. Therefore the number $K$ of phases satisfies
$$ K\le g(x_{\mathrm{initial}})\le M-1\le N-1. $$
If the initial state is EF1, no phase is needed.

3.2. Polynomial-size certificate

For a non-EF1 initial state, guess:

  • A phase count $K\le N-1$.
  • Threshold pairs $(s_i,H_i)\in\mathcal V^2$, with $s_i<H_i$, nondecreasing $s_i$, and nonincreasing $H_i$.
  • Supports $S_0,\ldots,S_K$ of boundary states, with $S_0$ equal to the input support.

Check that both boundary supports of phase $i$ are admissible for $(s_i,H_i)$. Check that the terminal support is EF1:
$$ v(B)\ge h(D) \quad\text{for every two supported terminal bundles }B,D. $$

Now form one linear program. Its variables are the boundary states $x_0,\ldots,x_K$ and the transport arrays of Theorem 3 for each phase. Require:

  • $x_0$ is the input state.
  • Every phase’s arrays satisfy Theorem 3 between $x_{i-1}$ and $x_i$.
  • Each boundary state has exactly its guessed support.

To impose the last condition, introduce $0\le\varepsilon\le1$. Set absent coordinates to zero, require every designated present coordinate to be at least $\varepsilon$, and maximize $\varepsilon$.

The guessed strict-support system is feasible exactly when the optimum is positive. Indeed, finitely many positive coordinates have a positive minimum; conversely, positive $\varepsilon$ ensures every designated coordinate is positive.

All masses and transport variables are bounded by $1$ through their marginal equations, so this linear program has a bounded feasible region. Rational linear programming decides whether its optimum is positive in polynomial time and supplies a rational solution of polynomial bit length.

3.3. Completeness

Suppose some finite real-amount sequence succeeds. Partition it into its threshold phases as above and guess their thresholds and boundary supports.

Proposition 1 places every transfer of a phase under its fixed phase rule. Theorem 3 supplies real transport arrays satisfying all phase equations. The boundary coordinates designated present are positive, so the linear program has positive optimum.

Thus the verifier accepts. No prior rationalization of the original sequence is needed.

3.4. Soundness and witness length

If the verifier accepts, its rational solution gives admissible boundary states and feasible transport arrays. Theorem 3 constructs a valid finite sequence for each phase. Concatenation preserves the supply equalities, cohort identities, near-EF1, and every required marked-pair comparison. The terminal state is EF1.

The constructed sequence has at most

$$ \boxed{\text{The general decision problem belongs to }\mathrm{NP}.} $$

$$ B=O\!\left(L+m\tau+m^2 6^m\right). $$

4. Proven polynomial-time solvability for strictly increasing cardinality valuations

Assume additionally that
$$ v(A)=f(|A|), \qquad 0=f(0)<f(1)<\cdots<f(m). $$
The function values are rational. This includes equal positive additive item values.

Write the cardinality masses as
$$ \mu_j=\sum_{|B|=j}x_B^U, \qquad \nu_j=\sum_{|D|=j}x_D^O. $$
Define
$$ r=\min\{j:\mu_j>0\}, \qquad k=\max\{j:\mu_j+\nu_j>0\}. $$

If $k=0$, the state is EF1. If $k\ge1$, then
$$ s=f(r),\qquad H=f(k-1), $$
because every nonempty bundle of size $j$ has $h=f(j-1)$.

Strict increase gives
$$ \text{EF1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\ge k-1. $$

4.1. Valid transfers are forced by cardinalities

Suppose the state is not EF1. Then $k\ge2$ and $r\le k-2$.

Let a proposed donor have size $d$ and its recipient size $b$. The donor condition of Proposition 1 becomes
$$ f(d-1)\ge f(k-1), $$
so $d\ge k$. Since $k$ is the largest supported size, necessarily $d=k$.

If $b\ge1$, then
$$ h(B\cup\{q\})=f(b)>f(b-1)=h(B). $$
The recipient condition therefore requires $f(b)\le f(r)$, hence $b\le r$. Since every marked bundle has size at least $r$, necessarily $b=r$.

If $b=0$, then necessarily $r=0$, and the recipient condition holds because both the empty bundle and its singleton extension have $h=0$.

Thus every valid transfer has:

  • an ordinary donor of size $k$;
  • a marked recipient of size $r$.

Conversely, every such pair permits a valid transfer. Since $k>r$, the donor has an item absent from the recipient. Its remainder has value $f(k-1)=H$, and the recipient’s new $h$ is $f(r)=s$.

Item identities therefore impose no additional restriction on the existence of a transfer in this case.

4.2. Greedy decision algorithm

Repeat the following procedure.

  • If $r\ge k-1$, accept.
  • If $\nu_k=0$, reject.
  • Otherwise, set

$$ w=\min\{\mu_r,\nu_k\} $$
and update
$$ \begin{aligned} \mu_r&\gets\mu_r-w,& \mu_{r+1}&\gets\mu_{r+1}+w,\\ \nu_k&\gets\nu_k-w,& \nu_{k-1}&\gets\nu_{k-1}+w. \end{aligned} $$
Recompute $r,k$.

The update can be implemented by matching actual supported donor and recipient types and choosing any available item.

4.3. Correctness: no successful choices are lost

Fix a non-EF1 state with cardinalities $r,k$.

Every valid transfer must move mass from the same two cardinality bins, $\nu_k$ and $\mu_r$. Until their total transferred mass reaches
$$ w=\min\{\mu_r,\nu_k\}, $$
both source bins remain positive.

Throughout that interval, $r$ remains unchanged. Also, $k$ remains unchanged: the donor bin at size $k$ remains positive, and recipients acquire size
$$ r+1\le k-1, $$
so they cannot create a larger size.

Consequently, the state cannot become EF1 before total amount $w$ has been transferred. At total amount $w$, the cardinality distribution is exactly the algorithm’s updated distribution, regardless of transfer amounts, item choices, and the selected identities.

If the state remains non-EF1 and $\nu_k=0$, no valid donor exists, so restoration is impossible. Otherwise, the same argument applies to the next update.

Induction proves that the algorithm accepts exactly the restorable instances in this subclass.

4.4. Termination and running time

Each update exhausts at least one source bin.

  • Exhausting $\mu_r$ strictly increases the minimum marked size.
  • Exhausting $\nu_k$ either strictly decreases the global maximum size, or leaves a marked holder at size $k$. In the latter case, the next iteration either accepts or rejects because no ordinary size-$k$ donor remains.

The minimum marked size increases at most $m$ times, and the global maximum decreases at most $m$ times. Hence there are at most $2m+1$ updates.

Recognizing this valuation subclass and deciding its instances takes
$$ \left(L+m\tau+m2^m\right)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations. After forming the cardinality masses, the decision procedure itself uses $O(m)$ rational updates.

A successful sequence can also be constructed. Within one update, no new source bundle of size $r$ or $k$ is created. Matching actual types while exhausting one source at each transfer therefore needs at most $2N+1$ transfers per update. The whole restoration uses $O(m2^m)$ transfers, with rational amounts of polynomial bit length.

4.5. A negative instance within this subclass

Let $Q=\{a,b\}$, $v(A)=|A|$, and
$$ \lambda_\varnothing^U=\tfrac14,\qquad \lambda_{\{a,b\}}^U=\tfrac14,\qquad \lambda_{\{a\}}^O=\tfrac12. $$
The supplies are
$$ \beta_a=\tfrac34,\qquad \beta_b=\tfrac14. $$

Here $H=1$, and the ordinary singleton has value $1$, so the initial state is near-EF1. It is not EF1 because marked empty-bundle holders envy the marked full-bundle holders.

The cardinalities are $r=0$, $k=2$, and $\nu_2=0$. Thus the algorithm correctly rejects. Directly, the only possible donor is the ordinary singleton, whose transfer would leave value $0<H$.

This confirms that even the polynomially solvable subclass contains genuine negative instances.

What remains is coordination across threshold phases. The certificate chooses their order and the boundary supports; the present argument enumerates those choices. I have not shown how to eliminate that enumeration in polynomial time, nor how to use it in an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.
