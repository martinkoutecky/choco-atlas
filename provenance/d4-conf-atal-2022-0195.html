Partial classification and further exact-computation results

The unrestricted problem is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed integer $p\geq 1$ and for $p=\infty$.

I do not settle the classification for unrestricted support and noninteger rational $p$. The following two additional results advance that case:

  • For every fixed rational $p>1$, exact computation takes time

$$ 2^{O_p(r_{\max})}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L), $$
where $r_{\max}$ is the largest number of distinct judgments in one coordinate. Consequently, exact computation is polynomial-time solvable when $r_{\max}$ is logarithmic in $m+\tau+L$.

  • For every fixed $p=h+3/2$, with $h$ a nonnegative integer, deciding whether a coordinate’s minimizer lies below a specified rational threshold is polynomial-time equivalent to signed square-root-sum comparison. Thus a polynomial-time exact consensus algorithm for any such exponent would give a polynomial-time algorithm for that comparison problem.

These are proved below. Neither result establishes NP-hardness or a superpolynomial output-length lower bound.

1. Reduction to a scalar problem

Let
$$ C=V\times A\times\{+,-\}. $$
There are
$$ |C|=2|V||A|\leq m^2/2 $$
coordinates. Write $j_{t,c}$ for a judgment and put
$$ w_t=\mu_tq_t>0. $$

For finite $p$, the outer power in $D_p$ is strictly increasing. Hence minimizing $D_p$ is equivalent to minimizing
$$ \sum_{c\in C}f_c(x_c), \qquad f_c(x)=\sum_{t\in T}w_t|x-j_{t,c}|^p. $$
The feasible set is a Cartesian product. A vector minimizes this sum if and only if each coordinate minimizes its scalar summand: replacing a nonminimizing coordinate would strictly decrease the sum.

For one coordinate, combine equal judgments and write the resulting data as
$$ a_1<\cdots<a_r, \qquad W_i=\sum_{t:j_{t,c}=a_i}w_t>0. $$
Here $r\leq\tau$. Sorting and these rational operations take polynomial bit time. The resulting rational numbers have polynomial bit length: multiplying the relevant denominators produces a common denominator whose bit length is at most the sum of their bit lengths.

For $p>1$, define
$$ G_p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^r W_i\operatorname{sgn}(x-a_i)|x-a_i|^{p-1}, $$
where a summand at $x=a_i$ is zero. Differentiation gives
$$ f_c'(x)=pG_p(x). $$
At a judgment node, the corresponding derivative is zero because
$$ \lim_{u\to0}\frac{|u|^p}{u}=0. $$

The function
$$ u\longmapsto \operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^{p-1} $$
is continuous and strictly increasing. Thus $G_p$ is continuous and strictly increasing.

If $r=1$, the unique minimizer is $a_1$. If $r\geq2$, then
$$ G_p(a_1)<0<G_p(a_r). $$
The intermediate value theorem gives a unique zero $x_*\in(a_1,a_r)$. The derivative is negative before $x_*$ and positive after it, so $x_*$ is the unique minimizer. In particular, it belongs to $[-1,1]$.

We will repeatedly use the resulting equivalence
$$ x_*\leq z \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_p(z)\geq0 $$
for every rational threshold $z$.

2. Polynomial-time computation for integer exponents and infinity

2.1. The case $p=1$

Let $W=\sum_iW_i$. Choose the smallest index $j$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{i\leq j}W_i\geq W/2. $$
The weight strictly below $a_j$ is less than $W/2$, and the weight strictly above it is at most $W/2$.

To verify optimality, let $W_<,W_=,W_>$ denote the weights below, equal to, and above a point $x$. The left and right derivatives of the scalar objective are
$$ f'_{c,-}(x)=W_<-W_>-W_=, \qquad f'_{c,+}(x)=W_<-W_>+W_=. $$
These formulas follow from the slopes of each absolute-value term. Those slopes are nondecreasing, so $x$ minimizes the objective exactly when
$$ f'_{c,-}(x)\leq0\leq f'_{c,+}(x). $$
Equivalently, both $W_<$ and $W_>$ are at most $W/2$. Thus the selected $a_j$ is a minimizer.

All operations are exact rational operations of polynomial bit complexity.

2.2. Every fixed integer $p=k\geq2$

Evaluate $G_k$ at every node. These values are rational and take polynomial bit time to compute because $k$ is fixed.

If a node value is zero, return that node. Otherwise, strict monotonicity identifies consecutive nodes satisfying
$$ G_k(a_j)<0<G_k(a_{j+1}). $$
On their open gap, $G_k$ equals
$$ H_j(x)= \sum_{i\leq j}W_i(x-a_i)^{k-1} - \sum_{i>j}W_i(a_i-x)^{k-1}. $$
This is a rational polynomial of degree at most $k-1$. Clear its denominators to obtain an integer polynomial $P_j$.

The polynomial is nonzero because its endpoint values have opposite signs. On $(a_j,a_{j+1})$, its zeros are exactly the zeros of the strictly increasing function $G_k$. Therefore that rational interval isolates exactly one real root of $P_j$, namely the minimizer.

The polynomial has a constant number of coefficients, each of polynomial bit length. This gives a polynomial-time algorithm in the required output representation.

For $k=2$, the root is
$$ x_*=\frac{\sum_iW_ia_i}{\sum_iW_i}. $$

2.3. The case $p=\infty$

For each coordinate, let
$$ \ell_c=\min_tj_{t,c}, \qquad u_c=\max_tj_{t,c}. $$
For every $x$,
$$ \max_t|j_{t,c}-x| \geq \max\{|\ell_c-x|,|u_c-x|\} \geq \frac{u_c-\ell_c}{2}. $$
The last inequality follows from
$$ u_c-\ell_c\leq|\ell_c-x|+|u_c-x|. $$
The midpoint
$$ x_c=\frac{\ell_c+u_c}{2} $$
attains this coordinatewise lower bound. Thus the midpoint vector attains the global lower bound
$$ \max_c\frac{u_c-\ell_c}{2} $$
and is an exact consensus minimizer.

All coordinates are rational and computable in polynomial time. For any rational output $u/v$, with integers $u,v$ and $v>0$, the polynomial
$$ vz-u $$
and the interval $(u/v-1,u/v+1)$ give the requested encoding.

3. An exact algorithm exponential only in distinct judgments

Fix a noninteger rational $p>1$ and write
$$ p-1=\gamma=\frac{a}{b}, \qquad a,b\in\mathbb Z_{>0}, \qquad \gcd(a,b)=1, \qquad b>1. $$
The integers $a,b$ are fixed throughout this section.

We prove that a scalar instance with $r$ distinct judgments can be solved in time polynomial in $b^r$, $r$, and its input bit length.

3.1. A polynomial containing every possible derivative value

Fix a gap
$$ I=(a_j,a_{j+1}) $$
and define $\varepsilon_i=1$ for $i\leq j$ and $\varepsilon_i=-1$ otherwise. For $x\in I$,
$$ \varepsilon_i(x-a_i)=|x-a_i|>0. $$

Consider the algebra
$$ \mathcal R= \mathbb Q[x,y_1,\ldots,y_r]/ \bigl(y_i^b-\varepsilon_i(x-a_i):1\leq i\leq r\bigr). $$
As a module over $\mathbb Q[x]$, it is free of rank
$$ N=b^r $$
with basis
$$ y_1^{e_1}\cdots y_r^{e_r}, \qquad 0\leq e_i<b. $$
Indeed, division by the monic relations, successively in the separate variables, gives a unique remainder with these exponent bounds.

Let $M_g(x)$ be the multiplication matrix of
$$ g=\sum_i\varepsilon_iW_iy_i^a $$
in this basis, and define
$$ Q_I(x,z)=\det(zI_N-M_g(x)). $$
This polynomial belongs to $\mathbb Q[x,z]$ and is monic of degree $N$ in $z$.

For a real $x\in I$, evaluation at
$$ y_i=|x-a_i|^{1/b} $$
is an algebra homomorphism. Under that homomorphism, $g$ becomes $G_p(x)$. Consequently,
$$ Q_I(x,G_p(x))=0. $$
More explicitly, the nonzero evaluation functional satisfies
$$ \lambda(M_gv)=G_p(x)\lambda(v). $$
It is therefore a left eigenvector with eigenvalue $G_p(x)$, which proves the determinant identity.

The matrices and polynomials have controlled size. Multiplication by $y_i^a$ reduces an exponent $e_i+a$ using at most
$$ c=\left\lceil\frac{a}{b}\right\rceil $$
copies of the defining relation. Thus every matrix entry has degree at most $c$ in $x$, and
$$ \deg_xQ_I\leq cN, \qquad \deg_zQ_I=N. $$
The entry coefficient lengths are polynomial in the input length.

The determinant can be computed in polynomial bit time in $N,r,L$. One explicit method evaluates it on a rational interpolation grid of polynomial size and interpolates. Each evaluation is an $N$-dimensional rational determinant. Its intermediate and final bit lengths are polynomially bounded: determinant expansion has at most $N!$ terms, each a product of $N$ entries. The same bounds control the interpolated coefficient lengths.

3.2. The derivative-zero polynomial is nonzero

Put
$$ P_I(x)=Q_I(x,0). $$
We must prove that this polynomial is not identically zero.

Away from the judgment nodes, each defining equation has $b$ distinct complex roots. Evaluation at every choice of these roots diagonalizes multiplication. Locally choosing analytic roots $y_i$, we obtain
$$ P_I(x)= \prod_{\zeta_1^b=\cdots=\zeta_r^b=1} \left( -\sum_i\varepsilon_iW_i\zeta_i^ay_i^a \right). $$

If this product vanished identically, at least one analytic factor would vanish identically on a disk avoiding the nodes. Continue that identity around a loop enclosing precisely one node, say $a_i$. The corresponding root $y_i$ is multiplied by a primitive $b$th root of unity $\zeta$; every other root returns unchanged. Subtracting the continued identity from the original one gives
$$ \varepsilon_iW_i\zeta_i^a(\zeta^a-1)y_i^a=0. $$
This is impossible. The weight is positive, $y_i$ is nonzero away from its node, and $\zeta^a\neq1$ because $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

Hence $P_I$ is nonzero. If the minimizer lies in $I$, then
$$ P_I(x_*)=0. $$
This also proves that the unique scalar minimizer is algebraic: a minimizer at a node is rational, and every other minimizer satisfies one of these nonzero polynomials.

3.3. An exact zero test using only rational polynomials

The polynomial $P_I$ can have extraneous roots. We now show how to test $G_p(\alpha)=0$ for any real root $\alpha\in I$ without constructing a tower of algebraic extensions.

Take the square-free part $P_0$ of $P_I$ and form
$$ R(z)=\operatorname{Res}_x(P_0(x),Q_I(x,z)). $$
This resultant is nonzero. To see this, write the roots of $P_0$ over $\mathbb C$ as $\lambda$. Up to a nonzero constant, the resultant is the product of the polynomials
$$ Q_I(\lambda,z). $$
Each is monic of degree $N$ in $z$, so their product is nonzero.

For the candidate $\alpha$,
$$ R(G_p(\alpha))=0. $$
Indeed, at $z=G_p(\alpha)$, the two polynomials in the resultant have the common root $x=\alpha$.

Its degree satisfies
$$ \deg R\leq N\deg P_0\leq cN^2. $$
Its coefficient lengths and its computation time are polynomial in $N,r,L$, by the Sylvester determinant formula and polynomial interpolation. Clear denominators to make $R$ an integer polynomial.

We use an elementary separation bound. Remove the largest power of $z$ dividing $R$ and denote the remaining polynomial by
$$ R_0(z)=a_0+a_1z+\cdots+a_dz^d, \qquad a_0\neq0. $$
Let
$$ H=\max_i|a_i|\geq1, \qquad \kappa=\frac1{H+1}. $$
Every nonzero root $\beta$ of $R$ satisfies
$$ |\beta|\geq\kappa. $$
For otherwise,
$$ \left|\sum_{i=1}^da_i\beta^i\right| \leq H\frac{|\beta|}{1-|\beta|} <1 \leq|a_0|, $$
contradicting $R_0(\beta)=0$.

Therefore, a certified rational enclosure of $G_p(\alpha)$ of width less than $\kappa$ decides its sign and whether it is zero. If the enclosure contains zero, every point in it has magnitude less than $\kappa$, so the actual derivative value must be zero. If it excludes zero, its side determines the sign.

Such an enclosure takes polynomial time in $N,r,L$. Here are the precision details. On $[-2,2]$, the signed power
$$ h_\gamma(u)=\operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^\gamma $$
satisfies
$$ |h_\gamma(u)-h_\gamma(v)| \leq \begin{cases} 2|u-v|^\gamma,&0<\gamma\leq1,\\ \gamma2^{\gamma-1}|u-v|,&\gamma\geq1. \end{cases} $$
For the first inequality, same-sign arguments use subadditivity of $t^\gamma$; opposite-sign arguments have both magnitudes at most $|u-v|$. The second inequality follows from the mean value theorem.

Thus $O_p(s+L+\log r)$ bits of precision in $\alpha$ suffice to enclose $G_p(\alpha)$ to width $2^{-s}$. The weights have polynomial bit length. A fixed-order rational power of a nonnegative rational number can itself be enclosed by rational bisection: compare $y^b$ with $d^a$. Taking $s$ larger than $\log_2(H+1)$ by a constant suffices, and this is polynomial in $N,r,L$.

3.4. Nodes and deterministic root isolation

At a node $a_j$, use the finite-dimensional rational algebra
$$ \mathbb Q[y_1,\ldots,y_r]/ \bigl(y_i^b-|a_j-a_i|:1\leq i\leq r\bigr) $$
and the element
$$ g_j=\sum_i W_i\operatorname{sgn}(a_j-a_i)y_i^a. $$
Its characteristic polynomial is monic of degree $N$, and $G_p(a_j)$ is a root by the same evaluation-functional argument. The separation bound and certified approximation above therefore test node values exactly in polynomial time in $N,r,L$. This remains valid when one of the defining constants is zero.

For candidate coordinates, we use deterministic univariate root isolation. The Sturm algorithm in [Strzebonski–Tsigaridas, Theorem 10](https://arxiv.org/html/1101.4369v2), applied with extension degree one, has polynomial bit complexity in polynomial degree and coefficient length. We apply it to square-free integer polynomials. Comparisons with rational gap endpoints and refinement use Sturm root counts, also in polynomial time. No expected-time isolation result is needed.

The complete scalar algorithm is now:

  • Test every judgment node for a zero of $G_p$.
  • For every open gap, construct the nonzero polynomial $P_I$.
  • Isolate its real roots lying in that gap.
  • Accept a candidate precisely when the exact derivative-zero test succeeds.

The minimizer either is a node or belongs to a gap, so the procedure is exhaustive. Strict monotonicity of $G_p$ guarantees exactly one accepted coordinate. For a gap candidate, the integer square-free polynomial and its rational isolating interval are valid output.

There are at most $r-1$ gaps and $cN$ candidate roots per gap. All operations have complexity polynomial in $N,r,L$. Since $N=b^r$ and $p$ is fixed, the scalar running time is
$$ 2^{O_p(r)}\operatorname{poly}(r,L). $$

Applying the procedure independently to all coordinates proves
$$ 2^{O_p(r_{\max})}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
In particular, for every fixed constant $C$, it is polynomial time on instances satisfying
$$ r_{\max}\leq C\log(m+\tau+L). $$
This strengthens the bounded-distinct-judgments case to logarithmically many distinct judgments.

4. Half-integer exponents and square-root-sum comparison

Define signed square-root-sum comparison as follows: given binary-encoded integers $c_i$ and positive integers $d_i$, decide whether
$$ E=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\sqrt{d_i}\geq0. $$
This is the formulation used by [Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54), who describe polynomial-time comparison as an open problem.

Fix
$$ p=h+\frac32, \qquad h\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}. $$
Then $p-1=h+1/2$.

Define the scalar threshold problem to ask whether its unique minimizer satisfies $x_*\leq z$, where $z$ is a given rational number.

4.1. Threshold comparison reduces to square-root-sum comparison

By Section 1,
$$ x_*\leq z \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_p(z)\geq0. $$
Omit terms for which $z=a_i$. For the remaining terms, write
$$ |z-a_i|=\frac{u_i}{v_i}, \qquad u_i,v_i\in\mathbb Z_{>0}. $$
Then
$$ |z-a_i|^{h+1/2} = \frac{u_i^h}{v_i^{h+1}}\sqrt{u_iv_i}. $$
Consequently,
$$ G_p(z)= \sum_i \left( \operatorname{sgn}(z-a_i)W_i \frac{u_i^h}{v_i^{h+1}} \right)\sqrt{u_iv_i}. $$
The parenthesized coefficients are rational. Clearing their denominators yields an integer-coefficient square-root sum with the same sign.

Because $h$ is fixed, all new integer bit lengths and the construction time are polynomial in the threshold input length. This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, including the equality case.

4.2. Square-root-sum comparison reduces to threshold comparison

Start with
$$ E=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\sqrt{d_i}. $$
Discard zero coefficients. If no terms remain, the answer is immediate. Otherwise, combine terms having the same sign and the same radicand; retain the notation $n$ for the remaining number of terms.

Choose a power of two $M$ satisfying
$$ M>\max_i d_i $$
and put
$$ B=\prod_{i=1}^nd_i^h. $$
For a scalar instance, choose judgments and effective weights
$$ a_i=-\operatorname{sgn}(c_i)\frac{d_i}{M}, \qquad W_i=|c_i|\frac{B}{d_i^h}. $$
Every judgment is dyadic and lies in $(-1,1)$. Every weight is a positive integer.

At zero, the derivative divided by $p$ is
$$ \begin{aligned} G_p(0) &= \sum_i \operatorname{sgn}(c_i)W_i \left(\frac{d_i}{M}\right)^{h+1/2}\\ &= \frac{B}{M^{h+1/2}} \sum_i c_i\sqrt{d_i}. \end{aligned} $$
The prefactor is positive. Therefore
$$ E\geq0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_p(0)\geq0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_*\leq0. $$

These effective weights have an admissible society realization. Use singleton value and action sets, so $m=2$, and set the performing judgments to the $a_i$ and all nonperforming judgments to zero.

For $n\geq2$, choose
$$ \mu_i=2^{-i}, \quad q_i=2^iW_i \qquad(1\leq i<n), $$
and
$$ \mu_n=2^{-(n-1)}, \qquad q_n=2^{n-1}W_n. $$
The masses sum to one and satisfy $\mu_iq_i=W_i$. For $n=1$, use $\mu_1=1$ and $q_1=W_1$. All numbers are positive dyadic rationals or integers.

The bit length of $B$ is at most
$$ h\sum_i\bigl(1+\log_2d_i\bigr). $$
The bit lengths of $M$, the judgments, masses, and influences are also polynomial in the square-root-sum input length. Thus this reduction is polynomial time.

Together with Section 4.1, this proves polynomial-time equivalence of the two decision problems.

4.3. Consequence for the requested exact output

Suppose exact consensus computation for this fixed $p$ had polynomial running time. Apply it to the society constructed above. The performing coordinate is unique, so the returned polynomial and isolating interval encode $x_*$.

Its sign can be determined in time polynomial in the returned encoding length. If its isolating interval excludes zero, the sign is immediate. If the interval contains zero, test the polynomial at zero; if that value vanishes, isolation identifies zero as the encoded root. Otherwise, a Sturm root count determines which side contains the unique root.

A polynomial-time algorithm can write only polynomially many output bits. Hence this postprocessing would be polynomial in the original square-root-sum input length. We obtain the proved implication
$$ \text{polynomial-time exact consensus for }p=h+3/2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{polynomial-time signed square-root-sum comparison}. $$

This implication does not establish NP-hardness. It also does not address whether the required exact outputs always have polynomial length.

5. A prescribed rational minimizer can be recognized efficiently

For the same half-integer exponents, testing whether $x_*=z$ for a given rational $z$ is polynomial-time solvable, even with unrestricted support.

Section 4.1 reduces this question to testing
$$ \sum_i c_i\sqrt{d_i}=0. $$
We give a deterministic equality test that requires no integer factorization.

Partition the radicands into classes under
$$ d_i\sim d_j \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_i/d_j\text{ is a square in }\mathbb Q. $$
This relation can be tested in polynomial time: reduce the fraction and test whether both its numerator and denominator are perfect squares.

For each class, choose a representative $e_j$. Every term in that class has the form
$$ c_i\sqrt{d_i} = c_i\sqrt{d_i/e_j}\sqrt{e_j}, $$
where $\sqrt{d_i/e_j}$ is rational and can be computed exactly. Summing within classes gives
$$ E=\sum_{j=1}^s C_j\sqrt{e_j}, \qquad C_j\in\mathbb Q. $$

The radicals $\sqrt{e_j}$ belonging to distinct rational square classes are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. Here is a proof. Let
$$ K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{e_1},\ldots,\sqrt{e_s}), \qquad G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q). $$
This is a finite Galois extension because it is the splitting field of the product of the polynomials $z^2-e_j$. For each $j$, define the character
$$ \sigma(\sqrt{e_j})=\chi_j(\sigma)\sqrt{e_j}, \qquad \chi_j(\sigma)\in\{-1,1\}. $$
The characters are distinct. If $\chi_j=\chi_k$, then $\sqrt{e_j/e_k}$ is fixed by every automorphism and hence rational, contradicting the choice of distinct square classes.

For a nontrivial character, summing its values over $G$ gives zero: multiplication by an element on which the character is negative pairs opposite contributions. Consequently,
$$ \sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi_j(\sigma)\sigma(E) = |G|C_j\sqrt{e_j}. $$
If $E=0$, this identity forces every $C_j=0$. Conversely, those equalities plainly imply $E=0$.

The algorithm only performs rational square tests and rational sums; it does not construct $K$ or enumerate $G$. Its intermediate bit lengths are polynomially bounded. Thus equality is decidable in polynomial time, and
$$ x_*=z \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_p(z)=0 $$
proves the claimed recognition algorithm.

6. What remains open

The proofs completely resolve the requested exact-computation problem for fixed integer exponents and infinity. They also give a deterministic exact algorithm for every fixed rational exponent with logarithmically many distinct judgments per coordinate.

For unrestricted noninteger rational exponents, the polynomial-time versus NP-hard classification remains unproved here. The half-integer reductions identify a precise comparison barrier, but they do not settle exact output length. A superpolynomial output obstruction must be measured against the full input length $L$, and an NP-hardness conclusion requires an actual reduction from an NP-hard problem.
