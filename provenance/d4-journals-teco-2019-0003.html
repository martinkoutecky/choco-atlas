The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of server types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), common no-arrival probability \(R\), a common value distribution \(F\), and a finite price allocation \(\pi_t\) for each type, define \(S_t=\sum_i a_{ti}r_{ti}\), \(D_t(p)=1-(1-R/S_t)F(p)+(1-R)/S_t\), \(w_t(p)=\mathbb{E}[V\mathbf{1}\{V\ge p\}]/D_t(p)\), and \(r_t(p)=p\Pr[V\ge p]/D_t(p)\). For \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), let \(j_t(p)=\lambda w_t(p)+(1-\lambda)r_t(p)\), \(J_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_p\pi_t(p)j_t(p)\), and \(J_\mu(q)=\sum_t\mu_tj_t(q)\). Choose \(q\in\bigcup_t\operatorname{supp}(\pi_t)\) maximizing \(J_\mu(q)\), and characterize the tight worst-case ratio \(\inf_{\pi:J_\mu(\pi)>0}\max_q J_\mu(q)/J_\mu(\pi)\), including its dependence on \(\mu\) and \(M=\max_{t,u}S_t/S_u\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge 1\) customer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Type \(i\) has a positive integer duration \(a_i\) and a nonnegative rational price per time step \(p_i\); let \(m\) be the number of distinct durations.
There is one server and discrete time. Independently at each step, a request occurs with rational probability \(0<R\le 1\), its type is sampled from \(\mu\), and its nonnegative value per time step \(V\) is drawn independently from a common finite-mean law. Define \(F(x)=\Pr[V<x]\).
An idle server accepts a type-\(i\) request exactly when \(V\ge p_i\), then remains occupied for \(a_i\) steps including the current step; requests while occupied are lost. Completion yields welfare \(a_iV\) and revenue \(a_ip_i\). A uniform price \(q\) replaces every \(p_i\).
For computational input, the common value law is specified by exact binary-rational statistics \(F(p_i)\) and \(\mathbb E[V\mathbf 1_{V\ge p_i}]\), promised consistent with such a law. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Choose either welfare or revenue as the objective, and let \(C(\mathbf p)\) and \(C(q)\) denote its long-run expected value per time step.

Does every such instance admit a price \(q\in\{p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\}\), computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), satisfying \(C(q)\ge \tfrac12 C(\mathbf p)\)?

Known results: The stochastic finite-duration model already guarantees a \(\tfrac12\) approximation using one of the supplied prices, separately for welfare and revenue, with arbitrary common value distributions. This includes the support-distribution model above; the stated approximation target is therefore already settled, rather than open. [Original result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08563).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the customer masses to sum to one and required a positive request probability at most one.
  • Restored positive integer durations, one nonpreemptive server, immediate acceptance, loss of requests while occupied, and occupation including the arrival step.
  • Specified nonnegative values and prices, finite expected value, independent draws across steps, and acceptance at equality.
  • Resolved the unspecified computational representation of the common value law using exact rational acceptance probabilities and tail expectations at the supplied prices.
  • Interpreted welfare or revenue as an objective selected separately, rather than requiring one price to approximate both simultaneously.
  • Defined m as the number of distinct durations and L as the total binary input length.

Result

Yes, for either welfare or revenue. Evaluating all supplied prices exactly and returning the best one takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, the selected price satisfies the stronger bound
\(C(q)\ge C(\mathbf p)/(1+R)\ge \tfrac12 C(\mathbf p)\).

The stated approximation agrees with Theorem 3.2 of [Kash, Key, and Suksompong](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08563). The following direct proof also handles types sharing a duration but having different prices.

1. Exact long-run objective

Fix either objective throughout. Define
\(r_i=R\mu_i\), \(t(x)=\Pr[V\ge x]=1-F(x)\), and

  • for welfare, \(h(x)=\mathbb E[V\mathbf 1_{\{V\ge x\}}]\);
  • for revenue, \(h(x)=x\,t(x)\).

Thus \(\sum_i r_i=R\), and all required values \(t(p_i)\) and \(h(p_i)\) are available exactly from the input.

A cycle begins whenever the server is idle at the start of a step. If it accepts a type-\(i\) request, the cycle lasts \(a_i\) steps and ends at completion. Otherwise, the cycle lasts one step and yields zero reward.

These cycles are independent and identically distributed: each new cycle uses fresh independent arrivals, types, and values. Their lengths are positive and bounded by \(\max_i a_i\). Their rewards have finite expectation, because the support is finite and \(\mathbb E[V]\) is finite.

For prices \(\mathbf p\), the expected cycle length is

\(D=1+\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(a_i-1)r_i t(p_i)\).

Indeed, each accepted type-\(i\) request adds \(a_i-1\) steps to the baseline one-step cycle. The expected reward is

\(N=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}a_i r_i h(p_i)\).

The renewal-reward theorem gives the long-run expected reward per time step as expected cycle reward divided by expected cycle length. Its hypotheses hold by the bounded positive cycle lengths and integrable rewards established above. Consequently,

\(C(\mathbf p)=\dfrac{\sum_i a_i r_i h(p_i)}{1+\sum_i(a_i-1)r_i t(p_i)}=\dfrac ND\).

Set
\(S=\sum_i a_i r_i\) and \(B=\sum_i(a_i-1)r_i=S-R\).
Since \(a_i\ge 1\) and \(R>0\), we have \(S>0\) and \(0\le B\le S\).
Under a uniform price \(q\), the same formula becomes

\(C(q)=\dfrac{S h(q)}{1+B t(q)}\).

Every denominator is at least \(1\).

2. A supplied price achieves the guarantee

Let
\(M=\max_{1\le j\le\tau}C(p_j)\),
where each \(C(p_j)\) denotes the objective obtained by using \(p_j\) uniformly.

For every index \(i\),

\(\dfrac{S h(p_i)}{1+B t(p_i)}\le M\),

so

\(h(p_i)\le \dfrac MS\bigl(1+B t(p_i)\bigr)\).

Multiplying by \(a_i r_i\) and summing yields

\(N\le M\left(1+\dfrac BS\sum_i a_i r_i t(p_i)\right)\).

Write
\(x=\sum_i(a_i-1)r_i t(p_i)\)
and
\(y=\sum_i r_i t(p_i)\).
Then \(D=1+x\), \(\sum_i a_i r_i t(p_i)=x+y\), and \(0\le y\le R\), since every acceptance probability lies in \([0,1]\).

Using \(B/S\le 1\), we obtain

\(N\le M(1+x+y)\le M(D+R)\).

Because \(D\ge 1\), we also have \(D+R\le (1+R)D\). Therefore,

\(C(\mathbf p)=\dfrac ND\le (1+R)M\).

Any supplied price attaining \(M\) consequently satisfies

\(C(q)=M\ge \dfrac{C(\mathbf p)}{1+R}\ge \tfrac12 C(\mathbf p)\).

This argument includes zero-objective instances without dividing by their objective value. It imposes no requirement that equal durations receive equal prices.

3. Exact polynomial-time selection

Compute \(S=R\sum_i\mu_i a_i\) and \(B=S-R\). Then, for each supplied price \(p_j\), compute

\(U_j=\dfrac{S h(p_j)}{1+B(1-F(p_j))}\).

Return any \(p_j\) maximizing \(U_j\). Compare rational values exactly by cross multiplication; all denominators are positive.

The procedure uses \(O(\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. With fractions reduced after operations, every intermediate numerator and denominator has bit length polynomial in \(\tau,L\): each expression uses sums and products of input rationals and binary-encoded durations. Standard integer arithmetic and greatest-common-divisor computations therefore give polynomial bit complexity.

In particular, the algorithm never expands a duration into individual time steps. Its running time is polynomial in \(\tau,L\), hence polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), even when durations are numerically large.

The identity \(t(x)=1-F(x)=\Pr[V\ge x]\) is correct under the task’s strict definition \(F(x)=\Pr[V<x]\). Thus atoms at a supplied price are handled correctly.

For welfare, \(h(p_i)\) is supplied directly. For revenue, \(h(p_i)=p_i(1-F(p_i))\) is exactly computable from the input. Both are finite and nonnegative. Also, \(r_i=R\mu_i>0\) and \(\sum_i r_i=R\).

2. Regenerative cycles and the objective formula

The cycles partition time correctly. An acceptance occupies precisely \(a_i\) steps, including the acceptance step; rejection or no arrival consumes one step. The next cycle starts when the server is idle again.

The assertion that cycles are independent and identically distributed is justified by independence of the successive arrival, type, and value draws. Cycle lengths are positive and bounded, and cycle rewards have finite expectation.

The stated renewal-reward theorem applies: for these independent, identically distributed cycles with finite expected length and integrable reward, the long-run expected cumulative reward per time step equals expected cycle reward divided by expected cycle length. Recording reward at completion agrees with the cycle construction.

The computations
\[ D=1+\sum_i(a_i-1)r_i t(p_i), \qquad N=\sum_i a_i r_i h(p_i) \]
are correct. The baseline step is counted once, and an accepted request adds exactly \(a_i-1\) additional steps.

Consequently, both the discriminatory formula \(C(\mathbf p)=N/D\) and the uniform formula
\[ C(q)=\frac{S h(q)}{1+B t(q)} \]

\[ h(p_i)\le \frac{M}{S}\bigl(1+B t(p_i)\bigr) \]

\[ N\le M(1+x+y)\le M(D+R)\le M(1+R)D. \]
