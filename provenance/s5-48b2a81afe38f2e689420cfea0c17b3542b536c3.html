The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite candidates \(C\), an explicitly listed finite type set \(\Theta\) of complete fallback ballots \(\theta=(S_\theta,\prec_\theta)\), rational registered mass \(\mu\), available mass \(\nu\), target \(w\), and budget \(\lambda\), decide whether there exists \(x\) with \(0\le x_\theta\le\nu_\theta\) and \(\lVert x\rVert_1\le\lambda\) such that \(w\) is the unique fallback winner of \(\mu+x\). The deletion variant asks whether such an \(x\) exists with \(0\le x_\theta\le\mu_\theta\) and \(w\) the unique winner of \(\mu-x\), using \(s^\ell_\rho(a)=\sum_{\theta:\,a\in S_\theta,\ \operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\le\ell}\rho_\theta\) and the paper's majority-level and approval-stage rule with masses.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(w\in C\). A fallback ballot \(\theta=(S_\theta,\prec_\theta)\) specifies \(S_\theta\subseteq C\) and a strict linear order on \(S_\theta\); empty and full approval sets are allowed. Positions start at \(1\); disapproved candidates are unordered.
A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Registered masses \(\mu_\theta\), available masses \(\nu_\theta\), and budget \(\lambda\) are nonnegative rationals; masses need not sum to \(1\). For addition, \(\Theta\) is the union of the two supports, with missing masses set to zero; for deletion, it is the registered support. Write \(\tau=|\Theta|\), the support size, not the number of possible ballots, and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For any resulting mass vector \(\rho\), define \(M_\rho=\sum_\theta\rho_\theta\) and \(s^\ell_\rho(a)=\sum_{\theta:\,a\in S_\theta,\,\operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\le\ell}\rho_\theta\). At the smallest level \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) where some candidate has score strictly greater than \(M_\rho/2\), all candidates maximizing \(s^\ell_\rho\) win. If no such level exists, all candidates maximizing \(s^m_\rho\) win. Ties are retained; at zero total mass every candidate wins.
The addition problem asks whether there exists \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\Theta}\) with \(x_\theta\le\nu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_\theta\le\lambda\) such that \(w\) is the unique winner for \(\rho=\mu+x\). The deletion problem instead requires \(x_\theta\le\mu_\theta\) and \(\rho=\mu-x\), with the same budget constraint. Partial masses may be moved, and ballots remain fixed.
For each of these two decision problems, is it in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
For explicitly listed, indivisible voters, constructive control by adding voters and by deleting voters is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.3398v1)). No prior result settling either mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete fallback ballots as approval subsets with strict orders only on approved candidates; empty and full approval sets remain allowed.
  • Used arbitrary nonnegative, unnormalized rational masses and a nonnegative rational budget, preserving the discrete electorate's variable total size.
  • Defined the addition type list as the union of registered and available supports, with absent masses zero; used the registered support for deletion.
  • Allowed real-valued partial mass choices and applied the deletion budget constraint as well as the addition budget constraint.
  • Retained all tied winners and extended the rule to zero total mass by making every candidate a winner, as its approval-stage definition dictates.
  • Required a nonempty candidate set containing the target; imposed no positive-electorate requirement.

Result

Both addition and deletion are in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof uses at most \(m+1\) rational linear programs, each with \(\tau+1\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. Strict majority and unique-winner conditions are handled by maximizing a common slack variable.

1. A common formulation

If \(m=1\), the answer to both problems is yes: \(w\) is always the unique winner, including at zero mass, and choosing \(x=0\) is feasible. Henceforth assume \(m\ge 2\).

Define a sign \(\sigma\) and capacities \(u_\theta\) by:

  • For addition, \(\sigma=1\) and \(u_\theta=\nu_\theta\).
  • For deletion, \(\sigma=-1\) and \(u_\theta=\mu_\theta\).

The feasible control vectors form the bounded rational polytope

\(P=\{x\in\mathbb R^\Theta:0\le x_\theta\le u_\theta\text{ for every }\theta,\ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}x_\theta\le\lambda\}\).

For either problem, the resulting masses are \(\rho_\theta(x)=\mu_\theta+\sigma x_\theta\). These masses are nonnegative: this follows directly for addition, and from \(x_\theta\le\mu_\theta\) for deletion.

For \(a\in C\) and \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), define the fixed indicator

\(A^j_{a\theta}=1\) if \(a\in S_\theta\) and \(\operatorname{pos}_\theta(a)\le j\), and \(A^j_{a\theta}=0\) otherwise.

Also set \(A^0_{a\theta}=0\). Write

\(T(x)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}(\mu_\theta+\sigma x_\theta)\),

\(S^j_a(x)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}A^j_{a\theta}(\mu_\theta+\sigma x_\theta)\).

Thus \(T(x)=M_{\rho(x)}\) and \(S^j_a(x)=s^j_{\rho(x)}(a)\). All these expressions are rational affine functions of \(x\).

Because the masses are nonnegative and the indicators are nondecreasing with the level,

\(0=S^0_a(x)\le S^1_a(x)\le\cdots\le S^m_a(x)\)

for every \(a\) and every feasible \(x\).

2. Exact characterization of successful control

A first majority at level \(r\)

Fix \(r\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). The resulting election has its first majority at level \(r\), with \(w\) its unique winner, if and only if:

  • \(2S^{r-1}_a(x)\le T(x)\) for every \(a\in C\).
  • \(2S^r_w(x)>T(x)\).
  • \(S^r_w(x)>S^r_a(x)\) for every \(a\in C\setminus\{w\}\).

Necessity. If \(r\) is the first majority level, no candidate has a strict majority at level \(r-1\), proving the first condition. At level \(r\), some candidate has score strictly greater than \(T(x)/2\). Since \(w\) is the unique score maximizer at that level, its score also exceeds \(T(x)/2\), proving the second condition. Unique maximality gives the third condition.

Sufficiency. The first condition and score monotonicity imply that no candidate has a strict majority at any level below \(r\). The second condition supplies a strict majority at level \(r\). Hence \(r\) is the first majority level. The third condition makes \(w\) the unique winner there.

For \(r=1\), the first condition holds automatically because \(S^0_a(x)=0\) and \(T(x)\ge0\).

No majority at any level

The resulting election has no majority at any level, with \(w\) its unique winner, if and only if:

  • \(2S^m_a(x)\le T(x)\) for every \(a\in C\).
  • \(S^m_w(x)>S^m_a(x)\) for every \(a\in C\setminus\{w\}\).

Indeed, by monotonicity, the first condition is equivalent to the absence of a strict majority at every level. The rule then chooses all candidates maximizing the level-\(m\) score, so the second condition is exactly unique victory for \(w\).

These \(m+1\) cases exhaust all successful control vectors.

3. Linear programs implementing the strict conditions

For each majority level \(r\), solve the following ordinary linear program in \(x\) and one additional variable \(\varepsilon\):

Maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to

  • \(x\in P\);
  • \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\);
  • \(2S^{r-1}_a(x)\le T(x)\) for every \(a\in C\);
  • \(2S^r_w(x)-T(x)\ge\varepsilon\);
  • \(S^r_w(x)-S^r_a(x)\ge\varepsilon\) for every \(a\ne w\).

For the no-majority case, solve:

Maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to

  • \(x\in P\);
  • \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\);
  • \(2S^m_a(x)\le T(x)\) for every \(a\in C\);
  • \(S^m_w(x)-S^m_a(x)\ge\varepsilon\) for every \(a\ne w\).

Accept the instance if at least one of these programs is feasible and has optimum strictly greater than zero.

We now prove that this test handles the strict inequalities exactly.

If a program admits \(\varepsilon>0\), every expression constrained to be at least \(\varepsilon\) is strictly positive. Its control vector therefore satisfies the corresponding characterization in Section 2.

Conversely, suppose \(x\) satisfies the characterization for majority level \(r\). The finite collection

\(\{2S^r_w(x)-T(x)\}\cup\{S^r_w(x)-S^r_a(x):a\ne w\}\)

consists entirely of positive numbers. Its minimum, capped at \(1\), is therefore a positive feasible value of \(\varepsilon\). The same argument applies to the no-majority case using the collection of rival-score differences. This collection is nonempty because \(m\ge2\).

Each program has a closed feasible region contained in the bounded box defined by \(0\le x_\theta\le u_\theta\) and \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\). Thus, whenever feasible, it attains its maximum. Consequently, existence of a successful control vector is equivalent to a strictly positive optimum in at least one program.

At zero resulting mass, every score difference is zero, so no program can accept. This correctly respects the stipulated tie among all candidates when \(m\ge2\).

4. Polynomial bit complexity

Each program has \(\tau+1\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. The indicator tables can be constructed in polynomial time from the listed ballots; no unlisted ballot types are introduced.

To check the encoding size, expand the affine expressions. For example,

\(2S^j_a(x)-T(x)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}(2A^j_{a\theta}-1)\mu_\theta+\sigma\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}(2A^j_{a\theta}-1)x_\theta\),

and

\(S^j_w(x)-S^j_a(x)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}(A^j_{w\theta}-A^j_{a\theta})\mu_\theta+\sigma\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}(A^j_{w\theta}-A^j_{a\theta})x_\theta\).

The coefficients of \(x\) in these constraints belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The constant terms are signed sums of input rationals and have polynomial bit length. Explicitly, the product of their input denominators has bit length at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths; using this common denominator and summing at most \(\tau\) numerators increases the encoding length only polynomially. Capacities and the budget are already input rationals.

We apply the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and the exact optimal value of a bounded rational linear program can be determined in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here: all coefficients are rational, each program has polynomial encoding length, and every feasible program is bounded. Exact comparison of its rational optimum with zero therefore takes polynomial time; no prescribed numerical tolerance or positive lower bound on \(\varepsilon\) is needed.

There are \(m+1\) programs. Their construction and solution thus take total time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This proves the claimed classification for both addition and deletion.

5. Relation to indivisible voters

The cited paper establishes hardness for constructive voter control in the indivisible setting. [Erdélyi, Piras, and Rothe, *Control Complexity in Fallback Voting*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1004.3398)

Here, partial masses are permitted, so the control variables are continuous. The proof above establishes polynomial-time solvability for precisely that divisible-mass model; it makes no claim that its feasible controls can be rounded to indivisible voters.
