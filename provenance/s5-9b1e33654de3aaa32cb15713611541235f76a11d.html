The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite schools \(X\), finite complete student types \(T\), rational masses \( \mu_t\ge0 \) with \( \sum_t\mu_t=1 \), rational per-capita capacities \( \kappa_x \), strict rankings \(R_t\) over \(X\cup\{0\}\), and weak priorities \( \succeq_x \) over types, compute a mass assignment \(y=(y_{t,x},y_{t,0})\) satisfying \( \sum_x y_{t,x}+y_{t,0}=\mu_t \) and \( \sum_t y_{t,x}\le\kappa_x \), with no positive mass assigned below a desired school \(x\) while \(x\) has slack or contains positive mass of a type \(u\) with \(t\succ_x u\), and such that no other stable assignment typewise first-order stochastically dominates \(y\). Determine an algorithm and running time in \( |X| \), \( |T| \), and the input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

Let \(X\) be finite schools and \(T\) finite student types, with binary-encoded rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), capacities \(\kappa_x\), strict rankings \(R_t\) over \(X\cup\{0\}\), and weak school priorities \(\succeq_x\) over \(T\); write \(\succ_x\) for their strict parts.
A mass assignment \(y\) satisfies \(\sum_{x\in X}y_{t,x}+y_{t,0}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_ty_{t,x}\le\kappa_x\), and is individually rational when \(y_{t,x}>0\) implies \(x\succ_t0\).
It is stable if there are no \(t,x,a,u\) with \(y_{t,a}>0\), \(x\succ_ta\), and either \(\sum_vy_{v,x}<\kappa_x\) or \(y_{u,x}>0\) and \(t\succ_xu\).
It is typewise ordinally undominated if no stable assignment typewise first-order stochastically dominates its normalized lotteries \(y_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\), strictly for some positive-mass type.

Target

Is this compact rational-mass problem solvable in time polynomial in \(|X|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length, or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

Nearest known results

For finite individual students with weak priorities, Erdil and Ergin give a polynomial-time algorithm for a stable matching undominated by another stable matching.
For finite-student random assignments, Kesten and Ünver give a polynomial-time FDAT construction that is ex-ante stable and constrained ordinally efficient; its input model is individual students rather than binary-encoded type masses.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is polynomial-time solvable. A compact algorithm uses a polynomial-time stable-allocation algorithm for initialization, followed by at most \(|T||X|+1\) linear programs. The argument below includes a bound on the bit lengths of intermediate rational numbers.

1. Notation and ordinal dominance

Assume capacities are nonnegative binary-encoded rationals, as required for the stated computational problem. Delete zero-mass types; their assignment rows are necessarily zero. Write \(n=|T|\) and \(m=|X|\).

For each type \(t\), list all alternatives in preference order as \(a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,m+1}\). Define prefix masses
\[ C_{t,k}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{t,a_{t,j}}, \qquad 1\le k\le m. \]
Here and below, these displayed formulas use the same mathematical notation as the inline formulas.

Write \(z\succeq^{\mathrm{SD}}y\) when \(C_{t,k}(z)\ge C_{t,k}(y)\) for every \(t,k\). Because each \(\mu_t\) is positive and fixed, these inequalities are exactly stochastic dominance of the normalized lotteries. Dominance is strict precisely when at least one inequality is strict.

Use the linear objective \(F(y)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=1}^{m}C_{t,k}(y)\). Consequently, strict dominance implies \(F(z)>F(y)\).

For a school \(x\), define its set of demanding types by
\[ D_x(y)=\left\{t:\sum_{a:\,x\succ_t a}y_{t,a}>0\right\}. \]

Two immediate properties will be used repeatedly:

  • If \(z\succeq^{\mathrm{SD}}y\), then \(D_x(z)\subseteq D_x(y)\). Indeed, the mass below \(x\) cannot increase.
  • If \(y\) is stable and \(D_x(y)\ne\varnothing\), then \(x\) is full, and \(y_{u,x}>0\) implies that no \(t\in D_x(y)\) satisfies \(t\succ_xu\).

We construct individually rational assignments. This also covers the interpretation under which individual rationality is separate from stability: every assignment dominating an individually rational assignment is itself individually rational. Its prefix mass through the outside option must still equal \(\mu_t\).

2. A conservation lemma

Lemma 1. Suppose \(y\) is individually rational and stable, and \(z\) is feasible, individually rational, and satisfies \(z\succeq^{\mathrm{SD}}y\). Then every school has the same total mass under \(y\) and \(z\), and every type has the same outside-option mass:
\[ \sum_tz_{t,x}=\sum_ty_{t,x}\quad(x\in X), \qquad z_{t,0}=y_{t,0}\quad(t\in T). \]

Proof. First, individual rationality and dominance imply \(z_{t,0}\le y_{t,0}\), since the total mass assigned to acceptable schools cannot decrease.

Suppose \(z_{t,x}>y_{t,x}\) for some \(t,x\). The difference between the prefix masses strictly above \(x\) is nonnegative. Adding the positive difference at \(x\), the prefix difference through \(x\) is strictly positive. Since the row totals agree, the total difference below \(x\) is strictly negative. Thus \(t\in D_x(y)\), and stability makes \(x\) full at \(y\). Feasibility therefore gives \(\sum_tz_{t,x}\le\sum_ty_{t,x}\).

If no type gains mass at \(x\), this same inequality holds coordinatewise. Hence it holds at every school.

On the other hand, summing \(z_{t,0}\le y_{t,0}\) and using the row totals shows that total school attendance cannot decrease. Thus all school-total inequalities must be equalities. The outside-option inequalities also sum to equality, so each is an equality. ∎

3. A compact improvement-cycle lemma

The following argument works directly with type masses; it does not expand them into individual students.

Lemma 2. Let \(y\) be individually rational and stable. If some stable assignment strictly dominates \(y\), then there is a feasible assignment \(w\) such that:

  • \(w\) strictly dominates \(y\);
  • every school has the same total mass under \(w\) and \(y\);
  • whenever \(w_{u,x}>0\), no type in \(D_x(y)\) has strictly higher priority than \(u\) at \(x\).

Proof. Let \(z\) be a stable assignment strictly dominating \(y\). As noted above, \(z\) is individually rational. Lemma 1 applies.

Define the nonempty set of losing coordinates
\[ V=\{(t,a)\in T\times X:y_{t,a}>z_{t,a}\}. \]
It is nonempty because \(z\ne y\), row totals agree, and outside-option masses agree.

Call \(x\) a gaining school if \(z_{k,x}>y_{k,x}\) for some \(k\). We first prove:

For every gaining school \(x\), there is a losing coordinate \((h,a)\in V\) such that \(x\succ_h a\) and \(h\) has highest priority among \(D_x(y)\).

Choose a gaining type \(k\) at \(x\). The prefix calculation in Lemma 1 shows that some coordinate below \(x\) loses mass for \(k\). In particular, \(k\in D_x(y)\).

If \(k\) has highest priority in \(D_x(y)\), choose \(h=k\) and such a losing coordinate \(a\). Otherwise choose a highest-priority type \(h\in D_x(y)\). Weak priorities are complete preorders, so \(h\succ_xk\). Because \(z_{k,x}>0\), stability of \(z\) requires \(h\) to have zero mass below \(x\) at \(z\). But \(h\in D_x(y)\), so some \(a\) below \(x\) has \(y_{h,a}>0=z_{h,a}\). This \(a\) cannot be the outside option, whose mass is unchanged. The assertion follows.

Now form a directed graph with vertex set \(V\). For each vertex \((u,x)\in V\), school \(x\) is a gaining school: its total mass is unchanged, so the loss of \(u\)'s mass must be offset by another type's gain. Choose \((h,a)\) as in the preceding assertion and add the edge
\[ (h,a)\longrightarrow(u,x). \]

Every vertex has an incoming edge. A finite nonempty directed graph with this property contains a directed cycle. Choose a simple cycle
\[ (t_1,a_1)\longrightarrow(t_2,a_2)\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow(t_\ell,a_\ell)\longrightarrow(t_1,a_1). \]
Indices are cyclic. Its defining properties are:

  • \(y_{t_i,a_i}>0\);
  • \(a_{i+1}\succ_{t_i}a_i\);
  • \(t_i\) has highest priority in \(D_{a_{i+1}}(y)\).

Choose \(0<\varepsilon\le\min_i y_{t_i,a_i}\). For each cycle position, transfer mass \(\varepsilon\) of type \(t_i\) from \(a_i\) to \(a_{i+1}\), adding all transfers simultaneously.

Because the cycle vertices are distinct, each coordinate is debited at most once. Thus the resulting assignment \(w\) is nonnegative. Each type's total mass is unchanged. Each school receives exactly as much mass as it loses, counting cycle occurrences with multiplicity.

Every transfer goes to a strictly preferred school. Hence every prefix weakly improves, and \(F(w)>F(y)\). Individual rationality is preserved.

Finally, an old positive coordinate already satisfies property 3 by stability of \(y\). Any new positive coordinate receives a type having highest priority among the corresponding demanders at \(y\), so it also satisfies property 3. ∎

4. A stability-preserving linear program

For an individually rational stable assignment \(y\), let \(P(y)\) be the set of assignments \(v\) satisfying:

  • feasibility and individual rationality;
  • \(C_{t,k}(v)\ge C_{t,k}(y)\) for all \(t,k\);
  • \(\sum_uv_{u,x}=\kappa_x\) whenever \(D_x(y)\ne\varnothing\);
  • \(v_{u,x}=0\) whenever some \(t\in D_x(y)\) satisfies \(t\succ_xu\).

These are linear constraints, and \(y\in P(y)\).

Lemma 3. Every \(v\in P(y)\) is stable.

Proof. Suppose \(t\) holds positive mass below school \(x\) at \(v\). Then \(t\in D_x(v)\subseteq D_x(y)\).

The fullness constraint therefore makes \(x\) full at \(v\). The zero-coordinate constraints exclude every occupant \(u\) with \(t\succ_xu\). Thus neither kind of blocking event in the question can occur. ∎

The polytope is bounded, since every coordinate is between zero and its type's mass. Thus the linear program
\[ \text{maximize }F(v)\quad\text{subject to }v\in P(y) \]
has an optimum.

Lemma 2 implies an additional fact we will need: if a stable \(y\) is strictly dominated by another stable assignment, then some \(w\in P(y)\) satisfies \(F(w)>F(y)\). Indeed, the cycle preserves school totals and satisfies all the required zero-coordinate constraints.

5. Initialization and algorithm

For initialization, use the known polynomial-time algorithm for stable allocation with strict preferences and rational quotas. This theorem concerns a bipartite graph with vertex quotas and edge capacities; an unsaturated edge blocks when each endpoint either has unused quota or holds positive allocation on a worse edge. Baïou and Balinski give a strongly polynomial algorithm; their corrected paper is [*The Stable Allocation (or Ordinal Transportation) Problem*](https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Here is the reduction, including the relevant hypothesis checks.

Create one vertex per positive-mass type and one per school. Give them quotas \(\mu_t\) and \(\kappa_x\). Include exactly the acceptable edges, with edge capacity \(\min\{\mu_t,\kappa_x\}\). Types retain their strict preferences, and each school priority is refined to a strict order preserving every original strict comparison. Assign unused type quota to the outside option.

A blocking event in the original problem would block this stable-allocation instance. The type either has unused quota or holds mass at a worse acceptable school. The school either has unused quota or holds a strictly lower-priority type, also lower in its refinement. Moreover, the edge is unsaturated: the type-side condition gives \(y_{t,x}<\mu_t\), and the school-side condition gives \(y_{t,x}<\kappa_x\). Thus the computed assignment \(y^0\) is individually rational and stable for the original weak priorities.

Now repeat:

  • Given \(y^r\), find an optimal vertex \(y^{r+1}\) of the LP over \(P(y^r)\).
  • If \(D_x(y^{r+1})=D_x(y^r)\) for every school \(x\), return \(y^{r+1}\).
  • Otherwise continue.

Every iterate is individually rational, stable, and weakly dominates its predecessor.

6. Termination and global undominatedness

Lemma 4. When the algorithm stops, its output is typewise ordinally undominated among all stable assignments.

Proof. Write \(y=y^r\) and \(v=y^{r+1}\) for the final iteration. Suppose a stable assignment strictly dominates \(v\).

By Lemma 2, there is \(w\in P(v)\) with \(F(w)>F(v)\). At termination the demand sets of \(v\) and \(y\) agree. Therefore their school-fullness constraints and forbidden-coordinate constraints agree. Also, \(w\succeq^{\mathrm{SD}}v\succeq^{\mathrm{SD}}y\). Consequently \(w\in P(y)\).

This contradicts the choice of \(v\) as an optimizer over \(P(y)\). ∎

For termination, dominance implies \(D_x(y^{r+1})\subseteq D_x(y^r)\) at every school. Every nonterminal iteration removes at least one pair from
\[ \{(t,x):t\in D_x(y^r)\}. \]
Removed pairs never return, and initially there are at most \(nm\) pairs. Thus there are at most \(nm\) nonterminal iterations and one terminal iteration.

7. Polynomial bit complexity

It remains to rule out excessive growth of denominators.

There are \(d=n(m+1)\) assignment variables. Each LP has polynomially many constraints. In its original constraint matrix, every entry belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The objective coefficients are integers between zero and \(m\).

Choose a positive integer \(Q_0\) divisible by the denominators of all input masses, capacities, and coordinates of \(y^0\). Initialization has polynomial bit complexity, so \(Q_0\) can be chosen with polynomial binary length.

Inductively, suppose all coordinates of \(y^r\), together with the input masses and capacities, are integer multiples of \(1/Q_r\). Every right-hand side of the next LP is then an integer multiple of \(1/Q_r\).

An optimal vertex is determined by \(d\) linearly independent active constraints. Let \(B\) be their nonsingular integer matrix. Cramer's rule shows that all coordinates of this vertex have denominators dividing
\[ Q_r|\det B|. \]
Hadamard's inequality gives \(|\det B|\le d^{d/2}\). We can therefore choose \(Q_{r+1}\) so that
\[ \log_2Q_{r+1}\le\log_2Q_r+\frac d2\log_2d. \]

There are at most \(nm+1\) LP solves. Hence all intermediate common denominators have polynomial binary length. Coordinates lie between zero and one, so their numerators also have polynomial binary length. The remaining right-hand sides are original input numbers or sums of these coordinates.

Exact rational linear programming, including finding an optimal vertex, therefore takes polynomial time at every iteration. Combined with the polynomial iteration bound and polynomial initialization, this proves polynomial running time in \(|T|\), \(|X|\), and the input bit length.

The construction outputs the required stable, typewise ordinally undominated assignment without expanding any binary-encoded mass into individual students.
