The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), resource classes \(\mathcal K\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), strict class rankings for each type, and rational initial and target allocation matrices \(A^0,A^1\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and matching column sums, is \(A^1\) reachable from \(A^0\) by a finite sequence of moves? Each move chooses distinct types \(t,u\), classes \(a,b\), and mass \(\delta>0\) available in cells \((t,a)\) and \((u,b)\), where \(b\succ_t a\) and \(a\succ_u b\), then exchanges that mass between the two types.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \(t\in\mathcal T\) with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) resource classes \(\mathcal K\); each type has a strict total ranking \(\succ_t\) of these classes. Any two distinct types may exchange mass.
The input allocations \(A^0,A^1\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}^{\tau\times m}\) satisfy \(\sum_a A^i_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t A^0_{ta}=\sum_t A^1_{ta}\) for every class \(a\). Entries represent masses assigned to classes.
At a current allocation \(A\), a move chooses \(t\neq u\), \(a\neq b\), and a real amount \(0<\delta\leq\min\{A_{ta},A_{ub}\}\), with \(b\succ_t a\) and \(a\succ_u b\). It subtracts \(\delta\) from \(A_{ta},A_{ub}\) and adds \(\delta\) to \(A_{tb},A_{ua}\).
Reachability means exact attainment of \(A^1\) from \(A^0\) by finitely many moves, allowing zero moves. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this mass-allocation reachability problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For indivisible objects, deciding reachability by strictly improving pairwise swaps is NP-hard even when every pair of agents may trade, with one object per agent (Müller and Bentert, [On Reachable Assignments in Cycles and Cliques](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.02218)). No result settling exactly the mass-allocation problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Allocation entries are nonnegative: they represent assigned mass.
  • Move amounts may be positive real numbers; only the input masses and allocations must be binary rationals.
  • Reachability requires exact attainment in finitely many moves and permits the empty sequence.
  • Strict class rankings are total rankings of all resource classes, as naturally specified by the graded question; the discrete definition also permits lists over subsets.
  • The resource-class set is nonempty, and L denotes the total binary input length.

1. Result. The mass-allocation reachability problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The relevant general result is polynomial-time *finite reachability in continuous Petri nets*, established by Fraca and Haddad; see [their paper, Section 4, Proposition 23](https://lsv.ens-paris-saclay.fr/Publis/PAPERS/PDF/FH-pn13.pdf). Allocation entries correspond to places, and improving swaps correspond to transitions. Below is a direct proof for the present problem, including the finite, exact-attainment argument.

2. Encoding the allowed swaps. Let \(P=\mathcal T\times\mathcal K\), and regard an allocation as a vector in \(\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{P}\). Write \(x^0,x^1\) for the two input allocations and \(p=|P|=\tau m\).

Let \(E\) contain one representative of each allowed improving swap. For \(s=(t,u,a,b)\in E\), define its input and output coordinates by

\(I_s=\{(t,a),(u,b)\},\qquad O_s=\{(t,b),(u,a)\}.\)

Its change vector is

\(C_s=-e_{(t,a)}-e_{(u,b)}+e_{(t,b)}+e_{(u,a)},\)

where \(e_v\) is the unit vector at coordinate \(v\). The four coordinates are distinct because \(t\neq u\) and \(a\neq b\).

Executing \(s\) with amount \(\delta>0\) changes \(x\) to \(x+\delta C_s\), and is permitted exactly when \(x_v\geq\delta\) for every \(v\in I_s\). Thus this encoding reproduces the prescribed moves exactly. Each column also preserves every row sum and every column sum.

Let \(C\) be the matrix with columns \(C_s\), and let \(n=|E|\). For each unordered pair of types and unordered pair of classes, there is at most one improving swap direction. Therefore

\(n\leq\binom{\tau}{2}\binom{m}{2}=O(\tau^2m^2).\)

We also use an auxiliary reverse system: reverse operation \(s\) consumes from \(O_s\), produces at \(I_s\), and has change vector \(-C_s\). A legal forward move, read backward, is a legal reverse operation, and conversely. Indeed, the coordinates consumed by the reversed operation have just received its amount.

3. Computing which swaps can occur. Fix \(S\subseteq E\) and a nonnegative allocation \(x\). Define \(F_x(S)\) by the following support-closure procedure:

  • Initialize \(H=\{v\in P:x_v>0\}\) and \(F=\varnothing\).
  • Whenever \(s\in S\setminus F\) satisfies \(I_s\subseteq H\), add \(s\) to \(F\) and add \(O_s\) to \(H\).
  • Stop when no further addition is possible, and return \(F_x(S)=F\).

Define \(B_x(S)\) by the same procedure in the reverse system, exchanging \(I_s\) and \(O_s\).

We need two properties of this procedure.

Support-closure lemma.

  • Every swap occurring in any finite legal sequence from \(x\), using only swaps in \(S\), belongs to \(F_x(S)\).
  • There is a finite legal sequence from \(x\) that executes every swap in \(F_x(S)\) exactly once. Its amounts can be chosen below arbitrary prescribed positive bounds. Its final allocation is positive at every input or output coordinate of those swaps.

Proof. For the first assertion, consider the terminal sets \(F,H\) produced by the closure procedure. Initially, every positive coordinate belongs to \(H\). Suppose all swaps before a particular move belong to \(F\). Every coordinate positive before that move belongs to \(H\): it was either initially positive or an output of an earlier swap. Consequently, the move’s inputs belong to \(H\). At termination, every swap in \(S\) whose inputs belong to \(H\) belongs to \(F\). Induction proves the assertion.

For the second assertion, execute swaps in their order of discovery by the closure procedure. When a swap is discovered, its inputs are positive in the allocation constructed so far. Choose its amount positive, below its prescribed bound, and at most half the smaller input mass.

This preserves every previously positive coordinate and makes its output coordinates positive. Induction realizes the entire discovery order. Every input and output coordinate of every executed swap is positive afterward. There are at most \(|S|\) moves. The reverse-system assertion follows by the identical argument. \(\square\)

A direct implementation takes \(O(p+n^2)\) time: scan the remaining swaps after each addition, with constant-time checks of their two inputs.

4. An exact finite-reachability criterion. For a nonnegative vector \(h\), write \(\operatorname{supp}(h)=\{s:h_s>0\}\).

Proposition. The target \(x^1\) is reachable from \(x^0\) by finitely many moves if and only if there exists \(h\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}\), with \(S=\operatorname{supp}(h)\), satisfying

\(x^1=x^0+Ch,\qquad F_{x^0}(S)=S,\qquad B_{x^1}(S)=S.\)

Necessity. Given a finite reaching sequence, let \(h_s\) be the total amount exchanged using swap \(s\). Summing its changes gives \(x^1=x^0+Ch\).

The sequence uses exactly the swaps in \(S\), so the support-closure lemma gives \(F_{x^0}(S)=S\). Reading the sequence backward in the reverse system gives \(B_{x^1}(S)=S\). \(\square\)

Sufficiency. If \(S=\varnothing\), the state equation gives \(x^1=x^0\), and zero moves suffice. Assume henceforth that \(S\neq\varnothing\).

By the forward support condition and the lemma, execute each swap in \(S\) once from \(x^0\), choosing amounts \(y_s\) satisfying

\(0<y_s\leq h_s/4.\)

Let the resulting allocation be \(a=x^0+Cy\).

Similarly, execute each reverse operation once from \(x^1\), choosing amounts \(z_s\) satisfying

\(0<z_s\leq h_s/4.\)

Let the resulting allocation be \(b=x^1-Cz\).

Both \(a\) and \(b\) are positive at every coordinate in

\(Q=\bigcup_{s\in S}(I_s\cup O_s).\)

Set \(w=h-y-z\). Then \(w_s\geq h_s/2>0\) for every \(s\in S\), and

\(b-a=Cw.\)

We now construct a finite legal sequence from \(a\) to \(b\). Define

\(\eta=\min_{v\in Q}\min\{a_v,b_v\}>0,\qquad H=\sum_{s\in S}w_s>0.\)

Choose a positive integer \(N\) with \(H/N<\eta/2\). Perform \(N\) rounds, executing each swap \(s\in S\) once per round with amount \(w_s/N\), in any fixed order.

At the start of round \(j\), indexed by \(j=0,\ldots,N-1\), the allocation is

\(a+\frac{j}{N}Cw=\left(1-\frac{j}{N}\right)a+\frac{j}{N}b.\)

Every coordinate in \(Q\) is therefore at least \(\eta\). Within the round, the total decrease of any coordinate is at most

\(\sum_{s\in S}w_s/N=H/N<\eta/2,\)

because every entry of \(C_s\) belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Thus, before any move, both input masses exceed \(\eta/2\), whereas its amount is at most \(H/N<\eta/2\). Every move is legal.

Each round adds \(Cw/N\), so after exactly \(N\) rounds the allocation is \(b\). Finally, read the previously constructed reverse sequence backward, obtaining a legal forward sequence from \(b\) to \(x^1\).

Together with the prefix from \(x^0\) to \(a\), this reaches \(x^1\) exactly in finitely many moves. \(\square\)

5. Finding a suitable support in polynomial time. The criterion involves an unknown support, but we can find one by successive deletion.

First accept if \(x^0=x^1\). In the remainder, assume \(x^0\neq x^1\), and write \(D=x^1-x^0\).

For \(S\subseteq E\), define the rational polyhedron

\(\mathcal P(S)=\{h\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{S}:C_Sh=D\},\)

where \(C_S\) contains the columns indexed by \(S\). When this polyhedron is nonempty, define its maximal possible support by

\(M(S)=\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal P(S)}\operatorname{supp}(h).\)

We can compute \(M(S)\) with rational linear programming. First test feasibility. Then, for each \(s\in S\), maximize \(h_s\) over \(\mathcal P(S)\); include \(s\) precisely when the optimum is positive.

To justify bounded optimization, assign each type integer preference scores \(r_{ta}\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), increasing with preference, and define

\(\Phi(x)=\sum_{t,a}r_{ta}x_{ta}.\)

For each allowed swap \(s=(t,u,a,b)\), its potential increase per unit amount is

\(d_s=(r_{tb}-r_{ta})+(r_{ua}-r_{ub})\geq2.\)

Every \(h\in\mathcal P(S)\) consequently satisfies

\(\sum_{s\in S}d_sh_s=\Phi(x^1)-\Phi(x^0).\)

Since each endpoint has total mass \(1\), its potential lies between \(0\) and \(m-1\). Hence, whenever \(\mathcal P(S)\) is nonempty,

\(0\leq\sum_{s\in S}h_s\leq\frac{\Phi(x^1)-\Phi(x^0)}2\leq\frac{m-1}{2}.\)

Thus \(\mathcal P(S)\) is bounded and closed, and each coordinate maximum is attained. Rational linear programming computes these maxima and their signs in polynomial time.

Moreover, if \(M(S)\neq\varnothing\), there is a single rational feasible vector whose support is exactly \(M(S)\). For each \(s\in M(S)\), take a rational feasible vector \(h^{(s)}\) with \(h^{(s)}_s>0\), and average them:

\(\bar h=\frac1{|M(S)|}\sum_{s\in M(S)}h^{(s)}.\)

Convexity preserves feasibility. Nonnegativity makes every coordinate in \(M(S)\) positive, and every coordinate outside \(M(S)\) is zero by definition.

The decision algorithm is now:

  • Initialize \(S=E\).
  • Reject if \(S=\varnothing\) or \(\mathcal P(S)\) is infeasible.
  • Compute \(M=M(S)\). Reject if \(M=\varnothing\).
  • Compute \(F=F_{x^0}(M)\) and \(B=B_{x^1}(M)\).
  • If \(F=B=M\), accept.
  • Otherwise replace \(S\) by \(F\cap B\) and repeat.

Soundness. When the algorithm accepts, the averaging construction gives a feasible vector with support exactly \(M\). Both support conditions hold for \(M\). The proposition therefore proves finite exact reachability.

Completeness. Maintain the invariant that the support of every genuine reaching sequence is contained in the current \(S\). It holds initially.

Let \(U\subseteq S\) be the support of a genuine reaching sequence. Its total exchanged amounts form a vector in \(\mathcal P(S)\), so \(U\subseteq M(S)\). The forward sequence and its reverse use only swaps in \(M(S)\). The support-closure lemma therefore gives

\(U\subseteq F_{x^0}(M(S))\cap B_{x^1}(M(S)).\)

Thus deletion preserves the invariant.

If \(\mathcal P(S)\) is infeasible, no reaching sequence exists. If \(S\) or \(M(S)\) is empty, any reaching sequence would have empty support and hence zero moves, contradicting \(x^0\neq x^1\). Every rejection is therefore correct.

Termination. Whenever the algorithm continues, \(F\cap B\) is a proper subset of \(M\), because \(F,B\subseteq M\) and acceptance failed. Since \(M\subseteq S\), at least one swap is deleted. There are at most \(n+1\) iterations.

6. Running time and real amounts. There are \(p=\tau m\) coordinates and \(n=O(\tau^2m^2)\) swap variables. The algorithm uses:

  • \(O((n+1)^2)\) rational linear-programming calls, each with at most \(p\) equality constraints and \(n\) nonnegative variables;
  • \(O((n+1)(p+n^2))\) additional time for the support closures.

The LP coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and their rational right-hand sides are differences of input entries. Their binary encoding lengths are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore gives an overall running time of

\((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}.\)

This bound concerns the decision procedure; it does not require explicitly outputting the potentially long sequence constructed in the sufficiency proof.

Finally, the permission to use real amounts causes no additional difficulty. Necessity holds for real sequences. On acceptance, the maximal-support vector can be rational, and every amount in the finite construction can then be chosen rational. Thus, for these rational inputs, finite reachability using real amounts coincides with finite reachability using rational amounts.
