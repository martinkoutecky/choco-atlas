The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\), rational total type masses \(\mu_t\), rational stage masses \(a_1,\ldots,a_H\), and an observed history of revealed rankings and panel assignments, each stage reveals its ranking before the chair acts. Future rankings are hidden and must fill the remaining stages while matching the remaining masses \(\mu_t\). At each stage \(j\), the chair routes a fraction \(x_j\in[0,a_j]\) of that stage's mass to the left panel and the rest to the right. Does there exist a contingent policy that makes a designated candidate \(c\) a plurality winner of the runoff for every compatible future ranking sequence, when all winners of each panel's first-round election advance?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(c\in C\). A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of the \(\tau\) strict ranking types with positive mass, each with its mass \(\mu_t\) as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The input lists \(H\geq1\) positive binary-rational stage masses \(a_1,\ldots,a_H\), with \(\sum_j a_j=\sum_t\mu_t>0\); masses need not sum to one. Each stage consists entirely of one ranking type.
At a current stage \(k\), the input gives the rankings \(t_j\in T\) and binary-rational left-panel assignments \(x_j\in[0,a_j]\) for \(j<k\), and the revealed current ranking \(t_k\). Past assignments are irrevocable.
A compatible completion assigns a type \(t_j\in T\) to every remaining stage so that \(\sum_{j:t_j=t}a_j=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\). Assume the observed history and current ranking admit at least one compatible completion.
After each ranking is revealed, the chair chooses a real amount \(x_j\in[0,a_j]\) for the left panel; the remaining mass \(a_j-x_j\) goes right. A deterministic contingent policy may use all revealed rankings and previous assignments, but no unrevealed rankings.
Both panels hold plurality elections over \(C\), with scores measured by mass. All tied winners advance; a panel of zero mass promotes all candidates. The runoff uses the entire society, with every ranking restricted to the union of the panels' winner sets. Ties in the runoff count as wins.
Is deciding whether such a policy guarantees that \(c\) is a runoff winner for every compatible completion in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,H,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.
For indivisible unweighted voters with unrestricted hidden future rankings, constructive and destructive online plurality control by partition with ties promoting are \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hard. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1203.0411)
No result settling this exact mass-constrained, fractionally routed online problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Ranking types are strict total orders over a nonempty candidate set, as in the discrete definition.
  • Type masses describe the entire society, including observed stages; stage masses are positive, explicitly listed binary rationals, and their sum equals the positive total type mass without normalization to one.
  • Each stage has one homogeneous ranking type, and compatibility requires exact type-mass equalities across all stages.
  • The snapshot includes an already revealed current ranking, past rational split assignments, and at least one compatible completion, avoiding vacuous success on inconsistent histories.
  • Future split amounts may be arbitrary reals, and policies are deterministic and depend only on revealed information.
  • Empty panels are allowed and promote every candidate under plurality; the runoff includes all society mass with rankings restricted to the finalists, and a tied runoff victory suffices.

Result

Under the promised compatibility assumption, the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. It admits a deterministic algorithm running in \(4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,H,L)\) time.

The proof has two parts: the uncertainty about future rankings can be eliminated exactly, but choosing which tied panel winners advance remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

1. Eliminating the online uncertainty

For a ranking \(t\), let \(\operatorname{top}(t)\) denote its first-ranked candidate. For each candidate \(i\), define its total first-place mass by

\(P_i=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=i}\mu_t.\)

From the irrevocable history, define

\(p_i=\sum_{j<k:\operatorname{top}(t_j)=i}a_j,\qquad \ell_i=\sum_{j<k:\operatorname{top}(t_j)=i}x_j.\)

Thus \(p_i\) is the already processed first-place mass of \(i\), and \(\ell_i\) is its already assigned left-panel score. Put

\(b_i=P_i-p_i,\qquad u_i=\ell_i+b_i.\)

Compatibility guarantees \(b_i\geq 0\).

Lemma. A guaranteeing policy exists if and only if there is a vector \(y\) satisfying \(\ell_i\leq y_i\leq u_i\) for every candidate \(i\), such that left scores \(y_i\) and right scores \(P_i-y_i\) produce a runoff won by \(c\).

Proof. Suppose a guaranteeing policy exists. Choose one compatible completion, which exists by hypothesis, and execute the policy on it. Its final left scores satisfy the stated bounds, and its runoff is won by \(c\).

Conversely, suppose such a vector \(y\) exists. For each candidate with \(b_i>0\), put

\(\lambda_i=\dfrac{y_i-\ell_i}{b_i}\in[0,1].\)

Whenever a remaining stage reveals a ranking with first-ranked candidate \(i\), assign \(x_j=\lambda_i a_j\) to the left panel.

In every compatible completion, the remaining first-place mass of \(i\) is exactly \(b_i\). Consequently, its final left score is

\(\ell_i+\lambda_i b_i=y_i.\)

If \(b_i=0\), compatibility implies that no remaining positive-mass stage has first-ranked candidate \(i\); its final score is already \(y_i=\ell_i\).

This policy uses only the currently revealed ranking. Every compatible completion therefore produces the same panel scores and the same runoff finalist set. The runoff uses the fixed entire society, so \(c\) wins in every completion. \(\square\)

In particular, the stage masses and possible reveal orders introduce no additional strategic difficulty once the history is fixed.

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and an exact algorithm

Guess the two nonempty panel winner sets \(W_{\mathrm L}\) and \(W_{\mathrm R}\), and put \(U=W_{\mathrm L}\cup W_{\mathrm R}\).

Reject this guess if \(c\notin U\). Otherwise, compute the runoff scores

\(\rho_U(i)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t|_U)=i}\mu_t,\qquad i\in U.\)

These scores are computed directly from the support in \(O(m\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. Reject the guess unless

\(\rho_U(c)\geq \rho_U(i)\quad\text{for every }i\in U.\)

It remains to determine whether the guessed panel winner sets are attainable. Choose representatives \(r\in W_{\mathrm L}\) and \(s\in W_{\mathrm R}\). Solve the following rational linear program, maximizing \(\delta\):

  • \(\ell_i\leq y_i\leq u_i\) for every \(i\), and \(0\leq\delta\leq1\);
  • \(y_i=y_r\) for \(i\in W_{\mathrm L}\);
  • \(y_i\leq y_r-\delta\) for \(i\notin W_{\mathrm L}\);
  • \(P_i-y_i=P_s-y_s\) for \(i\in W_{\mathrm R}\);
  • \(P_i-y_i\leq P_s-y_s-\delta\) for \(i\notin W_{\mathrm R}\).

The guessed winner sets are attainable exactly when this program is feasible with optimum \(\delta>0\). Indeed, positive \(\delta\) enforces every required strict inequality. Conversely, any realization of the guessed sets has finitely many positive strict gaps; their minimum, capped at \(1\), gives a feasible positive \(\delta\). If there are no strict comparisons, take \(\delta=1\).

This formulation also handles zero-mass panels: all their scores are zero, so their winner set must be the entire candidate set, as required.

The program has \(m+1\) variables and \(O(m)\) constraints. Its rational coefficients have bit length polynomial in \(L\). The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore applies; the feasible region is bounded, so a feasible program attains its optimum.

Thus the two guessed winner sets form a polynomial-size certificate checkable in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,H,L)\) time. The problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\) on the promised domain. If incompatible inputs are to be rejected in a total-language formulation, the certificate can additionally contain one compatible completion, checked by summing its type masses.

There are fewer than \(4^m\) pairs of nonempty winner sets. Enumerating them gives the claimed deterministic running time

\(4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,H,L).\)

An accepted linear-program solution also yields a guaranteeing policy through the lemma.

3. \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness

We reduce from Exact Cover by 3-Sets, the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem: given a universe \(E\) of size \(3q\) and three-element sets \(S_1,\ldots,S_n\), decide whether some \(q\) of them partition \(E\).

We take \(q\geq1\). If an element belongs to no set, the instance is immediately negative; map it to the fixed negative instance consisting of candidates \(c,d\) and one stage of mass \(1\) ranking \(d\) above \(c\). Every routing promotes \(d\), which then defeats \(c\). Henceforth assume every element belongs to at least one set.

3.1 Candidates and society

Define

\(K=3q+2,\qquad Q=3q+1,\qquad \alpha=\dfrac{K+1}{2},\qquad \beta=\dfrac{K-1}{2}.\)

Thus \(\alpha=\beta+1\) and \(\alpha+\beta=K\).

The candidates are:

  • \(c\) and a rival \(d\);
  • one set candidate \(s_i\) for each \(S_i\);
  • \(Q\) distinct filler candidates supporting \(c\);
  • \(Q+n-q\) distinct filler candidates supporting \(d\).

The last count is positive because \(Q+n-q=2q+n+1\).

Construct the society as follows. Every displayed ranking is completed to a strict ranking by placing unspecified candidates afterward in a fixed order.

| Mass | Ranking |
|---|---|
| \(K\) | \(c\) first |
| \(K\) | \(d\) first |
| \(\alpha\), for each \(i\) | \(s_i\succ c\succ d\succ\cdots\), called \(A_i\) |
| \(\beta\), for each \(i\) | \(s_i\succ d\succ c\succ\cdots\), called \(B_i\) |
| \(1\), for each \(e\in E\) | All \(s_i\) with \(e\in S_i\), then \(d\), then \(c\), then the remaining candidates |
| \(1\), for each \(c\)-supporting filler \(f\) | \(f\succ c\succ d\succ\cdots\) |
| \(1\), for each \(d\)-supporting filler \(f\) | \(f\succ d\succ c\succ\cdots\) |

Aggregate any identical rankings when forming the support \(T\).

For each \(i\), let \(w_i\) be the number of element rankings whose first-ranked candidate is \(s_i\). Then

\(0\leq w_i\leq3q.\)

3.2 Stages and fixed history

The past stages and their irrevocable assignments are:

| Past stage | Mass | Left assignment |
|---|---:|---:|
| The \(c\)-first stage | \(K\) | \(K\) |
| The \(d\)-first stage | \(K\) | \(0\) |
| One \(A_i\) stage, for each \(i\) | \(\beta\) | \(\beta\) |
| One \(B_i\) stage, for each \(i\) | \(\beta\) | \(\beta\) |
| Each element stage | \(1\) | \(0\) |
| Each filler stage | \(1\) | \(1/2\) |

There remain \(n\) stages, each of mass \(1\). Their residual ranking-type masses are exactly one unit of each \(A_i\), and zero for every other type. Reveal \(A_1\) as the current ranking.

No element ranking equals any \(A_i\), because element rankings place \(d\) above \(c\), whereas \(A_i\) places \(c\) above \(d\). Thus aggregation of identical rankings does not alter these residual masses.

Compatible completions are precisely the remaining permutations of the \(A_i\), so the compatibility promise holds.

For a completed execution, let \(z_i\in[0,1]\) be the left assignment of the remaining \(A_i\) stage. The final panel scores are:

  • \(c\): left \(K\), right \(0\);
  • \(d\): left \(0\), right \(K\);
  • \(s_i\): left \(K-1+z_i\), right \(w_i+1-z_i\);
  • each filler: left \(1/2\), right \(1/2\).

For every set candidate,

\(w_i+1-z_i\leq3q+1<K.\)

Its left score equals \(K\) exactly when \(z_i=1\), and is otherwise strictly below \(K\). Therefore the panel winner sets are exactly

\(W_{\mathrm L}=\{c\}\cup\{s_i:z_i=1\},\qquad W_{\mathrm R}=\{d\}.\)

Write \(I=\{i:z_i=1\}\), \(s=|I|\), and

\(v=\left|\bigcup_{i\in I}S_i\right|.\)

The runoff finalist set is \(\{c,d\}\cup\{s_i:i\in I\}\). Fractional assignments with \(z_i<1\) simply exclude \(s_i\); they create no additional runoff possibilities.

3.3 Exact runoff calculation

Every unselected set candidate’s \(A_i\) mass transfers to \(c\), and its \(B_i\) mass transfers to \(d\). Every filler is excluded and transfers its mass to its designated candidate.

An element vote goes to a selected set candidate exactly when that element is covered by the selected sets; otherwise it goes to \(d\).

Consequently,

\(\rho(c)=K+Q+(n-s)\alpha,\)

\(\rho(d)=K+(Q+n-q)+(n-s)\beta+3q-v.\)

Using \(\alpha-\beta=1\),

\(\rho(d)-\rho(c)=n-q-(n-s)+3q-v=2q+s-v.\)

No selected set candidate can defeat \(c\). Such a candidate receives its own padding mass \(K\), together with at most all \(3q\) element votes, so its runoff score is at most \(K+3q\). Meanwhile,

\(\rho(c)\geq K+Q=K+3q+1.\)

Hence \(c\) is a runoff winner exactly when

\(v-s\geq2q.\)

But \(v\leq3s\) and \(v\leq3q\). These inequalities imply

\(2q\leq v-s\leq2s,\qquad 2q\leq v-s\leq3q-s.\)

Therefore \(s\geq q\) and \(s\leq q\), so \(s=q\). The winning inequality then forces \(v=3q\). These \(q\) three-element sets cover the entire universe; because their total cardinality is \(3q\), they are pairwise disjoint and form an exact cover.

Conversely, an exact cover has \(s=q\) and \(v=3q\), giving \(\rho(c)=\rho(d)\). All selected set candidates score strictly less, so \(c\) wins by the stipulated runoff tie rule.

3.4 Equivalence with a guaranteeing policy

If an exact cover exists, route the remaining mass-\(1\) stage entirely left precisely for its selected set candidates, and entirely right otherwise. This deterministic policy uses only the revealed ranking and succeeds in every compatible permutation.

If a guaranteeing policy exists, execute it on any compatible completion. Its resulting set \(I\) must make \(c\) win. The calculation above proves that \(I\) is an exact cover.

Thus the reduction is correct in both directions.

4. Encoding size and running-time consequences

The reduction has

\(m=2n+5q+4,\qquad \tau\leq3n+8q+4,\qquad H=4n+8q+4.\)

All masses and past assignments are integers or half-integers of polynomial magnitude. Explicitly listing the strict rankings and stages takes

\(L=O((n+q)^2\log(n+q))\)

bits, so the reduction is polynomial.

Together with Section 2, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness under the compatibility promise. The decision algorithm runs in \(4^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,H,L)\) time and is polynomial for fixed \(m\).
