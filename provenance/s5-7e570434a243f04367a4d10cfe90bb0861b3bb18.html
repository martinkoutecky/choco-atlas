The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a scale \(H\), per-agent payoffs \(r_t\), and type data \(b_t\), \(p_{tj}\), \(u_{tj}\), and item access sets \(R_t\), does there exist a blocking subpopulation? Equivalently, is the maximum positive over \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), continuous aggregate uses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), and integer totals \(y_j\in\mathbb Z_+\), subject to \(x_{tj}\le H u_{tj}q_t\), \(y_j=\sum_{t:j\in R_t}x_{tj}\), \(\sum_j a_jy_j\le H\sum_t b_tq_t\), and \(y_j\le\alpha H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}q_t\), of \(\sum_t\sum_{j\in R_t}p_{tj}x_{tj}-H\sum_t r_tq_t\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is atomless and given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) items. Input consists of a positive rational population scale \(H\), rational pooling parameter \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), positive rational item costs \(a_j\), and, for each type, an access set \(R_t\), nonnegative rational budget \(b_t\), nonnegative rational per-agent payoff \(r_t\), nonnegative rational profits \(p_{tj}\), and finite capacities \(u_{tj}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) for \(j\in R_t\). There are no additional private constraints; all numerical input is binary encoded, with total input bit length \(L\).
A subpopulation selects real masses \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\), with \(\sum_tq_t>0\), and continuous aggregate uses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) for accessible pairs. Its item totals \(y_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) remain indivisible.
Feasibility requires \(x_{tj}\le Hu_{tj}q_t\), \(y_j=\sum_{t:j\in R_t}x_{tj}\), \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_jy_j\le H\sum_tb_tq_t\), and \(y_j\le\alpha H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}q_t\) for every item. Empty sums are zero. The pooling cap applies to every item accessed by positive selected mass, including access through just one type.
The subpopulation blocks precisely when \(\sum_t\sum_{j\in R_t}p_{tj}x_{tj}>H\sum_tr_tq_t\). The whole society is eligible to block; no efficiency promise is imposed on the supplied payoffs.

Is deciding whether a blocking subpopulation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents, deciding whether some subset, including the whole given coalition, can produce more than its assigned total payoff is NP-complete, even with one agent and no private constraints. This discrete result does not classify the atomless problem defined above.

  • The omitted item costs and pooling parameter are input, with positive rational costs and rational alpha in (0,1]; budgets, profits, and supplied payoffs are nonnegative rationals.
  • Capacities are finite nonnegative integers, consistent with capacities on individual integer item uses in the discrete model.
  • Types represent atomless populations: positive selected mass contains multiple agents, so the common-item pooling cap applies even when only one type accesses an item.
  • The omitted private constraints are absent, selecting the resource-allocation specialization explicitly considered in the supplied text.
  • A blocking subpopulation has positive mass, may equal the entire society, and must achieve a strict surplus; supplied payoffs need not be efficient.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and in fact \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even with one supported type, unit capacities, and \(H=\alpha=1\).

Reduction from SUBSET SUM

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard problem SUBSET SUM: given positive binary-encoded integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) and a positive integer \(B\), decide whether some subset has sum exactly \(B\). See, for example, [Polak, Rohwedder, and Węgrzycki](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04035).

Construct an instance with \(m=n\) items and one type, setting

\(\tau=1,\qquad \mu_1=1,\qquad H=1,\qquad \alpha=1,\qquad R_1=\{1,\ldots,n\},\)

\(u_{1j}=1,\qquad a_j=p_{1j}=w_j,\qquad b_1=B,\qquad r_1=B-\frac12.\)

Every stated input condition holds. In particular, \(r_1\ge\frac12\), all costs are positive, and the support mass is a positive binary rational. The construction has polynomial encoding length.

Write \(q=q_1\). Because there is only one type, item accounting gives \(x_{1j}=y_j\). Both the individual capacity constraint and the pooling constraint give

\(0\le y_j\le q\le1.\)

Since \(y_j\) is an integer, every \(y_j\) belongs to \(\{0,1\}\).

Any blocking subpopulation selects the entire society. If every \(y_j=0\), its profit is zero, so it cannot strictly exceed the nonnegative assigned payoff. Thus a blocker has some \(y_j=1\). The inequality \(y_j\le q\le1\) then forces \(q=1\).

Consequently, for any blocker, feasibility and blocking require

\(\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jy_j\le B,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jy_j>B-\frac12.\)

The sum is an integer. These inequalities therefore force

\(\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jy_j=B.\)

Hence the items with \(y_j=1\) constitute a solution to SUBSET SUM.

Conversely, suppose \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{j\in S}w_j=B\). Select \(q=1\), and set \(x_{1j}=y_j=1\) for \(j\in S\), with all other uses and totals zero. All capacity and pooling constraints hold, and the budget constraint holds with equality. The profit is \(B\), which strictly exceeds \(r_1=B-\frac12\). This is a blocking subpopulation.

Thus the constructed instance admits a blocker if and only if the SUBSET SUM instance is positive, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

It remains to justify that continuous mass and use variables admit certificates of polynomial length.

First, every feasible integer total satisfies

\(0\le y_j\le\alpha H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}\mu_t\le H\sum_{t:j\in R_t}u_{tj}.\)

Since \(H\) and the capacities are binary encoded within input length \(L\), this upper bound is at most \(\tau\,2^{2L}\). Thus every \(y_j\) has polynomial binary length.

Fix an integer vector \(y\) occurring in a blocking solution. Let \(P_y\) be the set of continuous vectors \((q,x)\) satisfying all the weak feasibility constraints, temporarily omitting \(\sum_tq_t>0\). This is a nonempty rational polytope. It is bounded because

\(0\le q_t\le\mu_t,\qquad 0\le x_{tj}\le Hu_{tj}\mu_t.\)

Define the linear surplus

\(G(q,x)=\sum_t\sum_{j\in R_t}p_{tj}x_{tj}-H\sum_tr_tq_t.\)

The assumed blocker gives a point of \(P_y\) with \(G>0\). A linear function on a nonempty bounded polytope attains its maximum at a vertex, so some vertex of \(P_y\) also has positive surplus.

Such a vertex has polynomial rational encoding length. To verify this explicitly, let \(N\) be the number of continuous variables. Clear denominators in the defining constraints; the resulting integer coefficients and right-hand sides have polynomial bit length, say at most \(K\). At a vertex, choose \(N\) linearly independent tight constraints. Cramer’s rule expresses each coordinate as a ratio of integer determinants. By the determinant expansion, their absolute values are at most \(N!2^{NK}\), so their binary lengths are \(O(NK+N\log N)\), which is polynomial in the input size.

Finally, positive surplus ensures positive selected mass: if \(\sum_tq_t=0\), nonnegativity forces every \(q_t=0\), and the capacity constraints force every \(x_{tj}=0\), giving \(G=0\).

Therefore a certificate consists of polynomial-length integer totals and rational mass and use variables. Exact rational arithmetic verifies every constraint and the strict blocking inequality in polynomial time. The problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Scope of the conclusion

The reduction is directly within the atomless model: one supported type represents an atomless population of identical agents. Item indivisibility forces full participation whenever any item is used; the remaining item choices encode SUBSET SUM.

Therefore polynomial-time solvability in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
