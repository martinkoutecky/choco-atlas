The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), seed types \(A\), baseline LTM predecessor probabilities \(p^0_{st}\), and explicit feasible integral link bundles \(\mathcal K_t\) with added probabilities \(\eta_s(K)\), choose fractions \(y_{tK}\) summing to \(1\) for each non-seed type, subject to the per-capita budget \(\sum_{t\notin A}\mu_t\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}|K|\le\beta\), to maximize activated mass. For each bundle, define activation by \(h_{tK}=\sum_{s\in A}(p^0_{st}+\eta_s(K))+\sum_{s\notin A}p^0_{st}\bar h_s\), with \(\bar h_s=\sum_K y_{sK}h_{sK}\) and seed activation equal to \(1\); use the least nonnegative solution. Is a \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\)-approximation computable in time polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\), the number of listed bundles \(\kappa\), input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\), or does this model admit a stronger algorithm or a hardness classification?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. A nonempty proper subset \(A\subset T\) consists of seed types.
For each non-seed type \(t\), the input gives baseline predecessor probabilities \(p^0_{st}\ge0\), candidate new links \(C_t\subseteq A\times\{t\}\), and a fixed nonnegative rational weight \(q_{st}\) for each candidate link; candidate links are absent from the baseline. Require \(\sum_{s\in T}p^0_{st}+\sum_{(s,t)\in C_t}q_{st}\le1\), with unused probability representing no predecessor.
An explicitly listed family \(\mathcal K_t\subseteq2^{C_t}\) contains the empty bundle. Define \(\eta_s(K)=q_{st}\) when \((s,t)\in K\), and \(\eta_s(K)=0\) otherwise. Each link costs one unit. Write \(m=\sum_{t\notin A}|C_t|\), \(\kappa=\sum_{t\notin A}|\mathcal K_t|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Given a binary-rational per-capita budget \(\beta>0\), choose fractions \(y_{tK}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}=1\) and \(\sum_{t\notin A}\mu_t\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}|K|\le\beta\).
Set \(\bar h_s=1\) for seeds. For non-seeds, activation is defined by the least componentwise nonnegative solution of \(h_{tK}=\sum_{s\in A}(p^0_{st}+\eta_s(K))+\sum_{s\notin A}p^0_{st}\bar h_s\) and \(\bar h_t=\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}h_{tK}\). Maximize total activated mass \(F(y)=\sum_{s\in A}\mu_s+\sum_{t\notin A}\mu_t\bar h_t\).

For every rational \(0<\epsilon<1-1/e\), can a \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\)-approximation to this maximum be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,\kappa,L,1/\epsilon\), allowing randomization with success probability at least \(2/3\), or is attaining this ratio NP-hard?

1. Result

The stated support-distribution problem is exactly solvable in deterministic polynomial time. Consequently, the requested \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\)-approximation is computable with success probability \(1\).

The proof uses the activation equations exactly as given. Their non-seed coefficient matrix is independent of the chosen bundle fractions. The main point requiring care is how to enforce the *least* nonnegative solution when that matrix has closed stochastic classes.

2. Averaging the activation equations

Write \(N=T\setminus A\), and define, for \(t,s\in N\),

\(B_{ts}=p^0_{st}\).

Thus \(B\) is nonnegative and substochastic: every row sum is at most \(1\). Define

\(a_t=\sum_{s\in A}p^0_{st},\qquad r_{tK}=\sum_{(s,t)\in K}q_{st},\qquad b_t(y)=a_t+\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}r_{tK}\).

Because the bundle fractions for each type sum to \(1\), averaging the prescribed equations gives

\(x=b(y)+Bx,\qquad x_t=\bar h_t\quad(t\in N).\)

Conversely, any nonnegative solution \(x\) of this averaged equation determines a solution of the original equations by setting

\(h_{tK}=a_t+r_{tK}+\sum_{s\in N}B_{ts}x_s.\)

These correspondences also preserve the least solution. Indeed, if \(x\le x'\) componentwise, nonnegativity of \(B\) implies that the corresponding bundle activation vectors satisfy \(h\le h'\).

For every permitted choice of bundle fractions, the probability restriction implies

\(0\le b_t(y)\le a_t+\sum_{(s,t)\in C_t}q_{st}\le 1-\sum_{s\in N}B_{ts}.\)

Consequently,

\(b(y)+B\mathbf 1\le\mathbf 1.\)

Starting with \(x^{(0)}=0\), define

\(x^{(j+1)}=b(y)+Bx^{(j)}.\)

Nonnegativity makes this sequence componentwise nondecreasing, and the preceding inequality bounds every iterate by \(\mathbf 1\). Its componentwise limit \(x^*(y)\) therefore exists and satisfies the averaged equation.

If \(z\ge0\) is any other solution, induction gives \(x^{(j)}\le z\) for every \(j\), so \(x^*(y)\le z\). Thus \(x^*(y)\) is precisely the least nonnegative solution, and

\(0\le x^*(y)\le\mathbf 1.\)

The iteration here proves existence and leastness; the polynomial-time algorithm below does not approximate activation by running this iteration.

3. Enforcing the least solution with fixed linear constraints

Construct the dependency graph on \(N\), with an arc \(t\to s\) exactly when \(B_{ts}>0\). This orientation follows predecessor dependencies.

Let

\(D=\left\{t\in N:\sum_{s\in N}B_{ts}<1\right\}.\)

Let \(R\) consist of all vertices that can reach \(D\), allowing paths of length zero, and put \(U=N\setminus R\). These sets depend only on the baseline matrix.

Lemma 1: The least solution vanishes on \(U\)

For every \(t\in U\), its row sum in \(B\) equals \(1\): otherwise \(t\in D\subseteq R\).

Moreover, there is no dependency arc from \(U\) to \(R\), since such an arc would give its starting vertex a path to \(D\). Hence

\(B_{UR}=0.\)

For \(t\in U\), the input restriction gives

\(1+a_t+\sum_{(s,t)\in C_t}q_{st}\le1.\)

All summands after \(1\) are nonnegative, so \(a_t=0\) and every candidate weight into \(t\) is zero. Therefore \(b_U(y)=0\) for every \(y\).

The iteration from Section 2 consequently satisfies \(x_U^{(j)}=0\) for every \(j\). Taking its limit proves

\(x_U^*(y)=0.\)

Lemma 2: \(I-B_{RR}\) is nonsingular

When \(R\) is empty there is nothing to prove. Otherwise write \(Q=B_{RR}\).

Suppose \(z=Qz\) and \(z\ne0\), and let

\(M=\max_{t\in R}|z_t|>0.\)

At any vertex \(t\) attaining this maximum,

\(M=|z_t|\le\sum_{s\in R}Q_{ts}|z_s|\le M\sum_{s\in R}Q_{ts}\le M.\)

Equality throughout implies that the row sum of \(Q\) at \(t\) equals \(1\), and that every successor \(s\) with \(Q_{ts}>0\) also satisfies \(|z_s|=M\).

By the definition of \(R\), there is a positive-edge path from \(t\) to some \(d\in D\). Every vertex on this path belongs to \(R\), because it too can reach \(D\). Repeatedly applying the preceding implication yields \(|z_d|=M\).

But at \(d\),

\(\sum_{s\in R}Q_{ds}\le\sum_{s\in N}B_{ds}<1,\)

so the equation at \(d\) gives

\(M=|z_d|\le M\sum_{s\in R}Q_{ds}<M,\)

a contradiction. Thus the kernel of \(I-Q\) is zero, proving nonsingularity.

Consequence

For each permitted \(y\), the equations

\(x=b(y)+Bx,\qquad x_U=0\)

have exactly one solution. Indeed, their restriction to \(R\) is

\((I-B_{RR})x_R=b_R(y),\)

which has a unique solution by Lemma 2. Lemma 1 shows that the least solution satisfies these equations, so their unique solution is exactly \(x^*(y)\).

In particular, activation is affine in \(y\), even when \(I-B\) itself is singular.

4. An exact linear-programming formulation

Introduce variables \(y_{tK}\) and \(x_t\). Maximize

\(\sum_{s\in A}\mu_s+\sum_{t\in N}\mu_t x_t\)

subject to the following linear constraints:

  • \(y_{tK}\ge0\) for every \(t\in N\) and \(K\in\mathcal K_t\).
  • \(\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}y_{tK}=1\) for every \(t\in N\).
  • \(\sum_{t\in N}\mu_t\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}|K|y_{tK}\le\beta\).
  • \(x_t=a_t+\sum_{K\in\mathcal K_t}r_{tK}y_{tK}+\sum_{s\in N}B_{ts}x_s\) for every \(t\in N\).
  • \(x_t=0\) for every \(t\in U\).
  • \(0\le x_t\le1\) for every \(t\in N\).

Every feasible allocation in the original problem extends to a feasible point of this linear program: take its least activation vector, whose bounds and vanishing coordinates were proved above.

Every feasible point of this linear program represents the original activation correctly: its allocation satisfies the original bundle and budget constraints, and Section 3 proves that its activation vector must equal the least solution.

The objective is therefore exactly \(F(y)\) in both directions. This is an exact formulation.

The zero constraints on \(U\) are essential. For example, a closed baseline cycle with row sums \(1\) and no seed input admits constant positive fixed points, although its least nonnegative solution is zero.

5. Polynomial bit complexity and approximation guarantee

The dependency graph has at most \(\tau\) vertices and \(\tau^2\) arcs. Exact rational row-sum comparisons and graph reachability compute \(D\), \(R\), and \(U\) in polynomial time.

The linear program has \(\kappa+|N|\) variables and \(O(\kappa+\tau)\) constraints. Its coefficients are obtained from input rationals by sums and products, together with bundle cardinalities bounded by \(m\). Because bundles are explicitly listed, constructing all coefficients requires polynomial time in \(m,\tau,\kappa,L\). Their total encoding length is also polynomial in these parameters.

We apply the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a feasible rational linear program with finite optimum admits an exact rational optimal solution computable in deterministic time polynomial in its encoding length. Polynomial-time solution and polynomial-size exact optimal solutions are covered in [Goemans’s linear-programming notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-854j-advanced-algorithms-fall-2008/908fe54d659623efadda2d033b895caf_notes_lp.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • Feasibility follows by assigning every type its empty bundle and using the least activation vector.
  • The feasible region is closed and bounded, since each bundle vector lies in a simplex and every activation coordinate lies in \([0,1]\).
  • All coefficients are rational.

Thus the algorithm returns an exact optimal allocation \(y^{\mathrm{opt}}\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,\kappa,L\). For every allowed \(\epsilon\),

\(F(y^{\mathrm{opt}})=\operatorname{OPT}\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\operatorname{OPT}.\)

The running time is independent of \(\epsilon\), and hence satisfies the requested polynomial bound in \(1/\epsilon\) as well. Randomization is unnecessary.
