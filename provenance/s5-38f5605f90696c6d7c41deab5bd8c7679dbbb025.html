The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an explicit finite acyclic binary game tree \(G=(S,s_0,T,g)\), rational finite-support distributions \(\mu_{i,v}\) over complete terminal valuations \(v:T\to\mathbb{Q}\) for each of two sides \(i\), with \(\sum_{i,v}\mu_{i,v}=1\) and side masses \(\pi_i=\sum_v\mu_{i,v}>0\), define \(U_i(t)=\pi_i^{-1}\sum_v\mu_{i,v}v(t)\). Using the paper’s tie-breaking and preference refinement, compute for every initial budget share \(\beta\in[0,1]\) the Bottom-Equilibrium outcome and a compact exact strategy description for every subgame, such as its budget cutoffs and actions.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: an explicit finite rooted tree \(G=(S,s_0,T,g)\), with terminal set \(T\) and \(1\leq |g(s)|\leq2\) at every nonterminal; both sides have the same available moves. Write \(m=|T|\) and \(h=\operatorname{height}(G)\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here a type is \((i,v)\), where \(i\in\{1,2\}\) and \(v:T\to\mathbb Q\) is explicitly listed and may have either sign; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Require \(\sum_{i,v}\mu_{i,v}=1\) and \(\pi_i=\sum_v\mu_{i,v}>0\), and set \(U_i(t)=\pi_i^{-1}\sum_v\mu_{i,v}v(t)\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
The two sides are complete-information players with terminal utilities \(U_i\). Side \(i\) compares terminals lexicographically by \((U_i(t),U_{3-i}(t))\); break remaining ties using one supplied common strict order on terminal labels.
At each nonterminal, both sides simultaneously submit a real bid \(0\leq b_i\leq B_i\) and a child to select if they win. The higher bidder wins, with ties always favoring side \(1\); the winner transfers its bid to the loser and selects its submitted child. Budgets initially are \((\beta,1-\beta)\), remain nonnegative, and have no terminal utility.
Define the finite-grid Bottom Equilibrium recursively: use grid \(\varepsilon=1/(4\cdot2^h)\), fix the Bottom Equilibria in the children, and at each grid budget choose equilibrium bid pairs minimizing lexicographically \((\max\{b_1,b_2\},-\min\{b_1,b_2\},\mathbf1\{\text{side }1\text{ wins}\})\); child choices must satisfy PSPE. Its continuous outcome at budget \(\beta\) is the grid outcome at \(\varepsilon\lfloor\beta/\varepsilon\rfloor\), including \(\beta=1\). A continuous Bottom Equilibrium means any PSPE inducing these outcomes at every state and budget, rather than a least continuous bid profile.
Is computing these outcomes for all \(\beta\in[0,1]\), together with a compact exact description of one continuous Bottom Equilibrium in every subgame, possible in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,|S|\), and \(h\).
Known results: finite-grid PSPEs have a Bottom Equilibrium. For arbitrary explicitly specified terminal utilities, continuous binary bidding games admit matching PSPE outcomes, and an algorithm computes their outcome cutoffs and continuation information in \(O(|S|m^4)\) arithmetic operations, with equilibrium bids reconstructible from that information ([Meir–Kalai–Tennenholtz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1311.0913v5)). This result already concerns continuous bids; a separate complexity statement for the distribution-support input is not established by that source.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined m as the number of terminals and L as the total binary input length; retained the explicit tree size and height as additional parameters.
  • Made impartiality and nonempty move sets explicit, permitting one or two children as in the discrete definition.
  • Specified nonnegative affordable continuous bids, simultaneous child submissions, transfer of the winning bid, and budgets with no terminal utility.
  • Resolved terminals with identical utility pairs using a supplied common strict label order, since the stated preference refinement alone does not distinguish them.
  • Defined continuous Bottom Equilibrium by its finite-grid outcome mapping, using an admissible fixed high resolution and including both budget endpoints; requested one realizing PSPE because continuous equilibrium strategies need not be unique.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. The explicitly listed support can be aggregated into two terminal utility vectors in polynomial time. The given binary-game algorithm then computes the required outcomes. These outcomes also admit a compact, exact description of a continuous PSPE.

A sufficient bit-operation bound for constructing the entire description is

\(O\!\left(L+(\tau m+m\log(m+1))(L+1)^2+|S|m^4(h+\log(|S|+1)+1)^2\right).\)

Thus NP-hardness under polynomial-time reductions would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Aggregating the society and encoding its preferences

Index the support by \(a\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), writing its entries as \((i_a,v_a,\mu_a)\). Compute

\(\pi_i=\sum_{a:i_a=i}\mu_a,\qquad A_i(t)=\sum_{a:i_a=i}\mu_av_a(t),\qquad U_i(t)=A_i(t)/\pi_i.\)

This takes \(O(\tau m)\) rational arithmetic operations. The hypotheses \(\pi_i>0\) ensure that every division is defined. Signed valuations cause no difficulty.

These calculations have polynomial bit complexity. For each sum, an unreduced common denominator can be obtained by multiplying the denominators of its input terms. The sum of their binary lengths is \(O(L)\), so this denominator has \(O(L)\) bits. Numerators also have \(O(L)\) bits: products add bit lengths, and summing at most \(\tau\) terms adds at most \(O(\log(\tau+1))\) bits. Normalizing by \(\pi_i\) preserves the \(O(L)\) bound.

For each side, sort the terminals by its specified lexicographic preference, including the common terminal-label order. Let

\(r_i:T\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\}\)

assign larger ranks to preferred terminals. Sorting requires \(O(m\log(m+1))\) comparisons. Using elementary integer multiplication, aggregation and sorting therefore take

\(O\!\left((\tau m+m\log(m+1))(L+1)^2\right)\)

bit operations.

We may now replace the terminal utilities by \(r_1,r_2\). Indeed, every unilateral deviation results in a terminal, and its profitability depends only on the deviating side’s terminal order. Consequently:

  • the PSPE strategy profiles are unchanged;
  • at every grid budget, the equilibrium bid pairs eligible for Bottom minimization are unchanged;
  • the bid-pair ordering in that minimization is unchanged.

Induction from the terminals therefore shows that this replacement preserves every required Bottom outcome.

This reduction uses the listed support, without enumerating any unlisted types or valuation functions.

2. Computing the outcome tables

The transformed game satisfies the hypotheses of the supplied result: it is finite, impartial, has at most two children at each nonterminal, uses strict terminal preferences, transfers the winning bid, and favors side \(1\) in bid ties. Its grid has the required high resolution.

We use the following facts from the binary-game result. The Bottom outcome is monotone in side \(1\)’s budget, so each state needs at most \(m\) outcome intervals. Matching continuous PSPEs exist. The algorithm computes the cutoffs in \(O(|S|m^4)\) arithmetic operations. Its candidate cutoffs are averages of child cutoffs; its budget calculations use addition, subtraction, halving, comparisons, and minimum operations. These are the relevant parts of Theorems 7 and 13 and Proposition 18 with Algorithm 3. [Meir–Kalai–Tennenholtz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1311.0913v5)

Write \(F_s(\beta)\) for the resulting outcome at state \(s\). Store each \(F_s\) as a list of cutoffs and terminal labels, with intervals closed at the lower endpoint.

Exact cutoff lengths

Let \(h_s\) be the height of the subtree rooted at \(s\). Terminal boundary values are \(0\) and \(1\). At a binary node, every candidate cutoff has the form

\(z=(a+b)/2,\)

where \(a,b\) are child cutoffs or boundary values. Inductively, both are multiples of \(2^{-(h_s-1)}\), so \(z\) is a multiple of \(2^{-h_s}\). Filtering candidate cutoffs does not change this property.

Thus every cutoff has \(O(h+1)\) bits. Unary nodes simply inherit their child’s outcome function: with monotone child outcomes, increasing one’s own winning bid cannot improve one’s continuation outcome, so \((0,0)\) is an equilibrium and is necessarily Bottom.

Choose the algorithm’s small increment as

\(\delta=\varepsilon=2^{-(h+2)}.\)

Its budget arithmetic can then use a common dyadic denominator \(2^{h+2}\). All such intermediate quantities have \(O(h+1)\) bits. Consequently, the outcome computation has polynomial bit complexity, not merely polynomial arithmetic complexity.

Agreement with the specified rounding

Every cutoff is a grid point because \(\varepsilon\) divides \(2^{-h_s}\). If an outcome interval is \([a,d)\) and \(\beta\in[a,d)\), then

\(a\leq\varepsilon\lfloor\beta/\varepsilon\rfloor<d.\)

Hence the table gives exactly the grid outcome at the budget required in the question.

Handle \(\beta=1\) explicitly. At every state, its outcome is side \(1\)’s most preferred reachable terminal. To prove this, induct on subtree height. Both bids can be zero; side \(1\) wins and selects the child leading to its most preferred reachable terminal. Side \(2\), having zero budget, cannot win, and side \(1\) cannot improve upon that terminal by any deviation. Since zero winning bid is minimal, this is also the grid Bottom outcome.

3. A compact exact continuous strategy description

For completeness, the following deterministic rule reconstructs a continuous PSPE using only the outcome tables. It avoids listing a bid pair for every real budget.

Fix a nonterminal \(s\), a budget \(\beta\), and the required terminal

\(q=F_s(\beta).\)

Consider legal bids \(b_1,b_2\) and submitted children \(c_1,c_2\). There are at most four ordered child pairs.

The following conditions exactly characterize a local equilibrium with outcome \(q\), given the continuation outcome functions.

Played outcome

If \(b_1\geq b_2\), require

\(F_{c_1}(\beta-b_1)=q.\)

Otherwise require

\(F_{c_2}(\beta+b_2)=q.\)

Winning deviations

Against bid \(b_2\), side \(1\) can win with any bid in \([b_2,\beta]\), when this interval is nonempty. Its attainable continuation budgets are therefore

\(R_1=[0,\beta-b_2]\quad\text{if }b_2\leq\beta,\)

and \(R_1=\varnothing\) otherwise.

Against bid \(b_1\), side \(2\) must bid strictly more. Its attainable continuation budgets are

\(R_2=(\beta+b_1,1]\quad\text{if }b_1<1-\beta,\)

and \(R_2=\varnothing\) otherwise.

For every available child \(c\), require that no outcome \(F_c(y)\), with \(y\in R_i\), is strictly preferred by side \(i\) to \(q\).

Each requirement can be checked by scanning the child’s outcome intervals and testing whether an interval intersects \(R_i\).

Losing deviations

Side \(1\) can deliberately lose precisely when \(b_2>0\). In that case require

\(r_1(F_{c_2}(\beta+b_2))\leq r_1(q).\)

Side \(2\) can always deliberately lose by bidding zero. Require

\(r_2(F_{c_1}(\beta-b_1))\leq r_2(q).\)

These conditions cover every local deviation: a deviation either wins, with some bid and child, or loses and leaves the opponent’s submitted child and transfer unchanged.

4. Checking finitely many bid pairs suffices

Here is an explicit polynomial candidate set for the preceding tests.

Let \(A\) contain all child cutoffs and the boundary values \(0,1\). Form

\(C=\{\beta-a,\ a-\beta:a\in A\}.\)

There are \(O(m)\) values in \(C\).

Construct candidate first bids by taking:

  • all values in \(C\cup\{0,\beta\}\) lying in \([0,\beta]\);
  • the midpoint between each consecutive pair of these sorted values.

For each candidate first bid \(x\), construct candidate second bids by taking:

  • all values in \(C\cup\{0,1-\beta,x\}\) lying in \([0,1-\beta]\);
  • the midpoint between each consecutive pair of these sorted values.

There are \(O(m^2)\) candidate bid pairs.

To justify completeness, observe that all local tests can change only across the following lines in the bid rectangle:

  • vertical or horizontal lines whose coordinates belong to \(C\), or to the rectangle’s boundaries;
  • the diagonal \(b_1=b_2\).

For example, continuation outcomes change when \(\beta-b_1\) or \(\beta+b_2\) crosses a child cutoff. Winning-deviation opportunities change when \(\beta-b_2\) or \(\beta+b_1\) crosses a cutoff. Their boundary coordinates all belong to \(C\).

The vertical partition also includes every horizontal coordinate in \(C\). Thus, within any open vertical strip, the diagonal cannot cross a horizontal partition line. Choosing a strip midpoint, and then the listed second-bid points and midpoints, represents every possible combination of strict inequalities and equalities in that strip. Sampling the vertical partition boundaries represents the remaining boundary cases.

Consequently, for fixed submitted children, every bid pair has a candidate pair with the same truth values in all local tests. If a feasible pair exists, a feasible candidate exists.

Order the candidate pairs and child pairs deterministically, and choose the first passing all tests. This defines exact bid and child-choice functions using only table lookup, arithmetic, and comparisons.

5. Why these rules form a PSPE

The matching-continuous-PSPE theorem guarantees a PSPE inducing all the functions \(F_s\). Its local actions satisfy the tests above. Therefore, a feasible candidate exists at every state and budget, and the selection rule is everywhere defined.

Now induct on subtree height.

At a terminal there is nothing to prove. Suppose the selected continuation strategies are PSPEs in every child and induce their prescribed outcome functions. The local tests exclude every profitable one-stage deviation against those continuations.

They also exclude deviations that change later actions. After any first-stage deviation, play enters a child subgame at a particular budget. By the induction hypothesis, the deviator cannot improve upon that child’s equilibrium outcome through further unilateral deviations. The local winning and losing tests already compare that outcome with \(q\).

Thus the selected strategies form a PSPE at \(s\), and their played outcome is \(F_s(\beta)\). Induction proves this simultaneously in every subgame and at every budget.

The resulting PSPE is a continuous Bottom Equilibrium under the question’s definition.

6. Running times and description size

The construction uses

\(O(\tau m+m\log(m+1)+|S|m^4)\)

arithmetic operations and comparisons. Elementary exact arithmetic gives the sufficient bit-operation bound stated at the start:

\(O\!\left(L+(\tau m+m\log(m+1))(L+1)^2+|S|m^4(h+\log(|S|+1)+1)^2\right).\)

The compact description consists of the outcome tables, terminal ranks, and the fixed deterministic selection rule. Its size is

\(O\!\left(|S|m(h+\log(|S|+1)+1)\right)\)

bits.

For a rational query budget of binary length \(\ell\), the selection rule checks \(O(m^2)\) candidate pairs, each in \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations. It therefore evaluates the strategies at one state in

\(O\!\left(m^3(h+\ell+\log(|S|+1)+1)^2\right)\)

bit operations.

Finally, \(h\leq |S|-1\), and the tree and support are explicitly included in the input. These bounds are polynomial in the stated input parameters. No enumeration of the \(4\cdot2^h+1\) grid budgets is needed.
