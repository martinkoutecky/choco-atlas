The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of reward types Θ, rational masses ν, K arms, horizon H, and unknown bounded type-arm reward laws, a learner chooses one common arm per round and observes the aggregate reward of every positive-mass type; compute adaptive policies with polynomial-time implementation and determine a high-probability bound for per-capita regret relative to the ν-weighted Nash-welfare-optimal policy.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_\theta\), normalized by \(\sum_\theta\nu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; there are \(m=K\geq1\) arms and \(H\geq1\) rounds.
Each arm has an unknown stationary joint reward law on \([0,1]^\Theta\), with type means \(\mu_{\theta,a}\); successive pulls give independent samples, while coordinates within a sample may be dependent.
At round \(t\), using only previous observations, the learner selects \(\pi_t\in\Delta_K\), samples one common arm \(a_t\sim\pi_t\), and observes each type's aggregate \(Y_{\theta,t}=\nu_\theta X_{\theta,a_t,t}\).
Define \(W_\nu(\pi,\mu)=\prod_{\theta\in\Theta}(\sum_a\pi_a\mu_{\theta,a})^{\nu_\theta}\), with value zero if any factor vanishes, and \(\pi^\star\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Delta_K}W_\nu(\pi,\mu)\). Per-capita cumulative regret is \(R_H=\sum_{t=1}^H[W_\nu(\pi^\star,\mu)-W_\nu(\pi_t,\mu)]\).
For \(\delta\in(0,1)\), let \(B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta)=\inf_A\inf\{b\geq0:\sup_D\Pr_{A,D}(R_H>b)\leq\delta\}\), where \(A\) ranges over learners without computational restrictions and \(D\) over the reward laws above.
Use binary-rational reward observations; \(L\) denotes the input bit length plus the largest observation encoding length. Polynomial implementation means time per round polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log H,\log(1/\delta)\).

What is the minimax high-probability regret \(B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta)\), and can it be attained up to logarithmic factors and additive \(1\) with polynomial-time implementation, or is attaining that guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete agents and unnormalized Nash-product regret, an efficient algorithm achieves \(\widetilde O(\sqrt{NKH}+NK)\) with probability \(1-\delta\). [Jones–Nguyen–Nguyen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11817)
For equal masses \(\nu_\theta=1/\tau\), geometric-mean expected regret has upper bound \(\widetilde O(K^{2/\tau}H^{(\tau-1)/\tau}+K)\) and a matching horizon exponent in the stated lower-bound regime; this does not settle arbitrary rational masses and minimax high-probability regret. [Zhang–Vuong–Luo](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20678v1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, as required by a distribution over types.
  • Interpreted per-capita weighted Nash welfare as the weighted geometric mean, and regret as cumulative differences of this welfare applied to expected utilities.
  • Interpreted aggregate feedback as mass times one bounded stochastic type-level reward observation, rather than exact population means; imposed stationary independent samples across pulls and allowed dependence across types.
  • Allowed zero utilities and defined welfare as zero whenever a positive-mass type has zero expected utility; any maximizing comparator is admissible.
  • The graded question supplies no regret threshold. Used unrestricted minimax high-probability regret as the performance benchmark, with logarithmic-factor and additive-one tolerance.
  • Specified binary-rational observations and included their encoding lengths in L to make polynomial-time implementation meaningful.

Status and proved results

The full joint characterization in $K,H,\nu,\delta$ remains partial. The results below establish polynomial-time attainment of the minimax rate up to logarithmic factors and additive $1$ for $0<\delta<1/4$, extend the lower bounds to every $\delta<1$, and characterize exactly when the minimax quantile is zero.

Assume first that $K\geq2$. Define
$$ q=\min\{1,K/H\},\qquad \beta_S=\sum_{\theta\in S}\nu_\theta, $$
and
$$ \mathcal F = H\max\left\{ \sqrt q,\ \max_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\Theta}\beta_Sq^{\beta_S} \right\}, \qquad \Lambda=\log\left(\frac{8\tau KH}{\delta}\right). $$
For the confidence-dependent lower bound, put
$$ s=1-\delta,\qquad \lambda=\max\left\{0,1-\frac1{Ks}\right\}. $$

There are universal constants $c,C>0$ such that, for every $\delta\in(0,1)$,
$$ \begin{aligned} cH\max\left\{ s\lambda^2\sqrt q,\ \max_{\varnothing\neq S\subsetneq\Theta} \beta_S(1-\beta_S)s^{\beta_S+2}q^{\beta_S} \right\} &\leq B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta)\\ &\leq \min\{H,C\Lambda^2\mathcal F+1\}. \end{aligned} $$
An empty maximum over proper subsets is interpreted as zero. The upper bound has an implementation whose time per round is polynomial in $K,\tau,L,\log H,\log(1/\delta)$.

In particular, for $0<\delta<1/4$,
$$ c\mathcal F \leq B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta) \leq \min\{H,C\Lambda^2\mathcal F+1\}. $$

The exact zero-quantile characterization is
$$ B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K=1 \ \text{or}\ \left(\tau=1\ \text{and}\ \delta\geq1-\frac1K\right). $$

The larger-$\delta$ regime cannot have the same uniform mass dependence as the high-confidence regime. Section 8 gives an explicit two-type example demonstrating this.

1. A continuity inequality adapted to Bernstein bounds

For positive weights $w_i$ satisfying
$$ \sum_iw_i\leq1, $$
define
$$ F_w(x)=\prod_i x_i^{w_i}, \qquad \alpha=\min\left\{\frac12,\min_iw_i\right\}. $$

Lemma. Suppose $x,y\in[0,1]^I$, $x\leq y$ coordinatewise, and
$$ y_i-x_i\leq8\sqrt{zx_i}+25z $$
for every $i$, where $z\geq0$. Then
$$ F_w(y)-F_w(x)\leq40z^\alpha. $$

Proof. On the positive orthant,
$$ h^\top\nabla^2F_w(x)h = F_w(x)\left[ \left(\sum_iw_i\frac{h_i}{x_i}\right)^2 - \sum_iw_i\left(\frac{h_i}{x_i}\right)^2 \right]. $$
Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the squared sum by
$$ \left(\sum_iw_i\right) \sum_iw_i\left(\frac{h_i}{x_i}\right)^2. $$
Thus the Hessian is negative semidefinite. Continuity extends concavity to the boundary.

The cases $z=0$ and $z\geq1$ follow immediately from the hypothesis and $0\leq F_w\leq1$. Suppose $0<z<1$. Let
$$ m=\min_i x_i, $$
and choose $j$ with $x_j=m$.

If $m\leq z$, then $y_j\leq34z$. When $34z<1$,
$$ F_w(y)\leq y_j^{w_j} \leq(34z)^\alpha \leq\sqrt{34}\,z^\alpha. $$
When $34z\geq1$, the same bound follows from
$$ F_w(y)\leq1\leq(34z)^\alpha. $$

If $m>z$, the tangent inequality for concavity gives
$$ \begin{aligned} F_w(y)-F_w(x) &\leq F_w(x)\sum_iw_i\frac{y_i-x_i}{x_i}\\ &\leq33\sqrt z\,F_w(x)\sum_i\frac{w_i}{\sqrt{x_i}}. \end{aligned} $$
Here $z/x_i\leq\sqrt{z/x_i}$. Also,
$$ \sum_i\frac{w_i}{\sqrt{x_i}}\leq\frac1{\sqrt m}, \qquad F_w(x)\leq m^{w_j}\leq m^\alpha. $$
Therefore
$$ F_w(y)-F_w(x) \leq33\sqrt z\,m^{\alpha-1/2} \leq33z^\alpha, $$
because $m>z$ and $\alpha\leq1/2$. This proves the lemma.

2. Confidence bounds and adaptive sampling

Because $\nu_\theta>0$, the learner recovers each individual reward exactly:
$$ X_{\theta,a_t,t}=\frac{Y_{\theta,t}}{\nu_\theta}. $$
Dependence between coordinates within a reward vector does not prevent marginal concentration inequalities.

For bounded-size empirical statistics, round each recovered reward upwards to a dyadic grid of mesh
$$ \eta=2^{-\lceil\log_2H\rceil}\leq\frac1H. $$
If $\mu'_{\theta,a}$ is its rounded mean, then
$$ \mu_{\theta,a}\leq\mu'_{\theta,a}\leq\mu_{\theta,a}+\eta. $$
Rounded observations remain in $[0,1]$ and remain independent across successive pulls.

Let
$$ b=\left\lceil\log_2\left(\frac{4\tau KH}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil. $$
For $n\geq1$, let $\widehat\mu_{\theta,a,n}$ denote the rounded empirical mean after $n$ pulls. A bounded random variable in $[0,1]$ with mean $u$ has variance at most $u$. Bernstein’s inequality consequently gives
$$ \Pr\left( \left|\widehat\mu_{\theta,a,n}-\mu'_{\theta,a}\right| > \sqrt{\frac{2\mu'_{\theta,a}b}{n}}+\frac bn \right) \leq2e^{-b}. $$
A union bound over $\theta,a,n$ with $n\leq H$ shows that these inequalities hold simultaneously with probability at least $1-\delta/2$. They concern the fixed sample stream of each arm, so adaptive selection of which stream to observe causes no difficulty.

For a pulled arm, use
$$ U_{\theta,a} = \min\left\{ 1,\ \widehat\mu_{\theta,a,n} +4\sqrt{\widehat\mu_{\theta,a,n}b/n} +8b/n \right\}. $$
Approximate the square root upwards with error at most $b/(4n)$. For an unpulled arm set $U_{\theta,a}=1$.

On the simultaneous concentration event, with $d_a=\max\{1,n_a\}$,
$$ \mu'_{\theta,a}\leq U_{\theta,a} \leq \mu'_{\theta,a} + 8\sqrt{\frac{\mu'_{\theta,a}b}{d_a}} + \frac{16b}{d_a}. $$

To verify the constants, write $h=\widehat\mu_{\theta,a,n}$, $u=\mu'_{\theta,a}$ and $v=b/n$. The concentration event implies
$$ |h-u|\leq\sqrt{2uv}+v. $$
Since $\sqrt{2uv}\leq u/2+v$, it follows that $u\leq2h+4v$, and hence
$$ u-h\leq2\sqrt{hv}+4v. $$
This proves optimism. Also $h\leq2u+2v$, so the upper-estimate error, including the upwards square-root approximation, is at most
$$ 8\sqrt{uv}+16v. $$
Clipping at $1$ preserves optimism. The unpulled case follows from $b\geq1$.

At round $t$, define
$$ z_t=\sum_a\frac{\pi_{t,a}}{\max\{1,n_{t,a}\}}, \qquad \rho_t=bz_t, $$
and
$$ x_{\theta,t}=\sum_a\pi_{t,a}\mu_{\theta,a}, \qquad y_{\theta,t}=\sum_a\pi_{t,a}U_{\theta,a}. $$
Weighted Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$ y_{\theta,t}-x_{\theta,t} \leq \eta+8\sqrt{\rho_t(x_{\theta,t}+\eta)}+16\rho_t. $$
Every denominator in $z_t$ is at most $H$, so $\eta\leq1/H\leq\rho_t$. Consequently,
$$ y_{\theta,t}-x_{\theta,t} \leq8\sqrt{\rho_tx_{\theta,t}}+25\rho_t. $$

We next control the predictable sampling quantities $z_t$. Set
$$ Z_t=\frac1{\max\{1,n_{t,a_t}\}}, \qquad c_0=1-e^{-1}. $$
Conditional on the selected policy, $\mathbb E[Z_t]=z_t$. Since
$$ e^{-u}\leq1-c_0u\qquad(0\leq u\leq1), $$
the process
$$ \exp\left(c_0\sum_{t\leq h}z_t-\sum_{t\leq h}Z_t\right) $$
is a nonnegative supermartingale starting at $1$. Markov’s inequality therefore gives, with probability at least $1-\delta/2$,
$$ c_0\sum_{t=1}^Hz_t \leq \sum_{t=1}^HZ_t+\log(2/\delta). $$
An arm pulled $n\geq1$ times contributes
$$ 1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac1j\leq2+\log H. $$
Thus, on an event of probability at least $1-\delta$ encompassing both estimates,
$$ \sum_{t=1}^H\rho_t\leq Q, \qquad Q=\frac b{c_0}\left[K(2+\log H)+\log(2/\delta)\right]. $$
For a universal constant $C_0$,
$$ Q\leq C_0K\Lambda^2. $$

3. Optimism after discarding a small total mass

Let $J\subseteq\Theta$ be discarded types, and suppose $G=\Theta\setminus J$ is nonempty. Write
$$ \beta=\sum_{\theta\in J}\nu_\theta, \qquad \alpha_G=\min\left\{\frac12,\min_{\theta\in G}\nu_\theta\right\}, $$
and define the retained objective without renormalizing its exponents:
$$ F_G(\pi,\mu) = \prod_{\theta\in G} \left(\sum_a\pi_a\mu_{\theta,a}\right)^{\nu_\theta}. $$

Set
$$ \gamma=\varepsilon=\frac1{2H}, \qquad \mathcal P_\gamma = \{\pi\in\Delta_K:\pi_a\geq\gamma/K\text{ for every }a\}. $$
At each round compute a dyadic policy satisfying
$$ \pi_{t,a}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2K}, $$
and
$$ F_G(\pi_t,U_t) \geq \max_{\pi\in\mathcal P_\gamma}F_G(\pi,U_t)-\varepsilon. $$
Section 5 establishes the polynomial-time implementation, including exact feasibility.

Mixing any policy with the uniform policy at weight $\gamma$ preserves every retained utility by a factor at least $1-\gamma$. The retained exponent sum is at most $1$, so restricting to $\mathcal P_\gamma$ costs at most $\gamma$ in objective value.

Optimism and the continuity lemma now imply
$$ \max_{\pi\in\Delta_K}F_G(\pi,\mu)-F_G(\pi_t,\mu) \leq\gamma+\varepsilon+40\rho_t^{\alpha_G}. $$

For each type, put $M_\theta=\max_a\mu_{\theta,a}$. If some $M_\theta=0$, the original welfare and regret are identically zero. Otherwise define
$$ C_J=\prod_{\theta\in J}M_\theta^{\nu_\theta}. $$
Every implemented policy gives each discarded type utility at least $\gamma M_\theta/(2K)$. Therefore
$$ W_\nu(\pi^\star,\mu) \leq C_J\max_\pi F_G(\pi,\mu), $$
whereas
$$ W_\nu(\pi_t,\mu) \geq C_J\left(\frac{\gamma}{2K}\right)^\beta F_G(\pi_t,\mu). $$
Using $C_J\leq1$, $F_G\leq1$, and $1-e^{-u}\leq u$, we obtain
$$ \begin{aligned} W_\nu(\pi^\star,\mu)-W_\nu(\pi_t,\mu) &\leq \max_\pi F_G(\pi,\mu)-F_G(\pi_t,\mu) +\beta\log(4KH). \end{aligned} $$

Since $u\mapsto u^{\alpha_G}$ is concave,
$$ \sum_{t=1}^H\rho_t^{\alpha_G} \leq H^{1-\alpha_G}Q^{\alpha_G}. $$
It follows that, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$ R_H \leq 40H^{1-\alpha_G}Q^{\alpha_G} +\beta H\log(4KH)+1. $$

4. Selecting discarded types without subset optimization

If $q\geq1/2$, use any policy. Then
$$ R_H\leq H\leq\sqrt2\,\mathcal F. $$
Suppose $q<1/2$. Compute
$$ d=1+\left\lceil\log_2(1/q)\right\rceil, \qquad r=\frac1d, $$
and discard exactly the types satisfying $\nu_\theta<r$. Let $\beta$ be their total mass.

If $\beta\geq r$, accumulate discarded types until their total mass first reaches $r$. Each increment is smaller than $r$, so this gives a subset $S$ satisfying
$$ r\leq\beta_S<2r. $$
Since $\log(1/q)/d<1$,
$$ q^{2r}\geq e^{-2}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \mathcal F\geq H\beta_Sq^{\beta_S}\geq e^{-2}Hr. $$
Any policy then satisfies $R_H\leq e^2d\,\mathcal F$.

If $\beta<r$, the retained set is nonempty, and $\alpha_G\geq r$. Using the estimate on $Q$ from Section 2,
$$ R_H \leq C_1\Lambda Hq^{\alpha_G} +\beta H\log(4KH)+1. $$
If $\alpha_G=1/2$, the ordinary-bandit term in $\mathcal F$ gives
$$ Hq^{\alpha_G}\leq\mathcal F. $$
Otherwise a retained singleton of mass $\alpha_G$ gives
$$ \mathcal F\geq H\alpha_Gq^{\alpha_G}, $$
so in either case
$$ Hq^{\alpha_G}\leq d\,\mathcal F. $$
When $\beta>0$, the discarded set itself gives
$$ \mathcal F\geq H\beta q^\beta\geq e^{-1}\beta H, $$
because $\beta<r$. The required bound is immediate when $\beta=0$.

Finally, $d=O(\Lambda)$ and $\log(4KH)\leq\Lambda$. Thus
$$ R_H\leq C\Lambda^2\mathcal F+1 $$
with probability at least $1-\delta$. Combining this with the deterministic bound $R_H\leq H$ proves the claimed upper bound.

The algorithm uses only comparisons between individual rational masses and $1/d$. It never computes the subset maximum defining $\mathcal F$.

5. Polynomial implementation with exact feasibility

All confidence entries are rational and satisfy
$$ \frac1H\leq U_{\theta,a}\leq1. $$
Indeed, an unpulled entry equals $1$; a pulled entry has an added term at least $8b/n\geq8/H$ before clipping.

For a fixed retained set, maximize
$$ g(\pi)= \sum_{\theta\in G}\nu_\theta \log\left(\sum_a\pi_aU_{\theta,a}\right). $$
It is concave, and maximizing it is equivalent to maximizing $F_G=e^g$. On the simplex,
$$ -\log H\leq g(\pi)\leq0, $$
and
$$ 0\leq \frac{\partial g}{\partial\pi_a} = \sum_{\theta\in G} \frac{\nu_\theta U_{\theta,a}} {\sum_b\pi_bU_{\theta,b}} \leq H. $$
Thus $g$ is $H$-Lipschitz in the $\ell_1$ norm on the simplex.

5.1 The precise weak-optimization guarantee

We use the following consequence of Theorem 3.1 of Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, with weak optimization defined in Section 1: for a compact convex body with an explicitly known interior ball, outer ball, and polynomial-time weak separation, linear weak optimization returns a rational point $y$ such that
$$ \operatorname{dist}(y,Q)\leq\xi, \qquad c^\top y\geq\max_{z\in Q}c^\top z-\xi. $$
Its running time is polynomial in the input lengths and $\log(1/\xi)$, including the encoding lengths of the ball parameters. This guarantee allows approximate feasibility; we repair it explicitly below. [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Theorem 3.1](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf).

Write
$$ p(x)=(x_1,\ldots,x_{K-1},1-\textstyle\sum_{i<K}x_i), $$
and let
$$ B=2+\lceil\log_2H\rceil. $$
Apply weak optimization to the compact convex hypograph
$$ Q= \left\{ (x,v): p(x)\in\mathcal P_\gamma,\ -B\leq v\leq g(p(x)) \right\}, $$
with objective $c^\top(x,v)=v$.

A known interior-ball center is
$$ x_i=\frac1K,\qquad v=-B+1, $$
with radius $1/(8K^2)$. To check this, a perturbation of $x$ within this radius changes the last probability coordinate by at most $K/(8K^2)$, while the distance from the uniform policy to each probability floor is at least $1/(2K)$. Also,
$$ g(p)\geq-\log H\geq-B+2, $$
so the vertical coordinate has adequate slack. An outer-ball radius $B+K+3$ suffices.

The weak separation oracle first checks the rational linear constraints. At a feasible $p(x)$, evaluate $g(p(x))$ to error at most one quarter of the oracle tolerance. A point lying at most that tolerance above the graph is certified as approximately feasible. Otherwise concavity supplies a separating hyperplane using the rational gradient of $g(p(x))$. Rational normalization, or sufficiently accurate normalization with the tolerance budget, gives the required weak separator.

Logarithms are polynomial-time evaluable to the necessary binary precision. For example, after dyadic range reduction to $u\in[1,2)$,
$$ \log u = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad z=\frac{u-1}{u+1}\in[0,1/3). $$
The tail decreases geometrically. Rational gradients are computed exactly. Hence all oracle hypotheses hold.

5.2 Repairing the returned policy

Choose
$$ \xi=\frac{\varepsilon}{4+16HK^2}. $$
Let $(x,v)$ be the weak-optimization output. There exists $(x',v')\in Q$ at distance at most $\xi$. Put
$$ p=p(x),\qquad p'=p(x'). $$
Then
$$ \sum_ap_a=1,\qquad p_a\geq\frac{\gamma}{K}-K\xi, \qquad \|p-p'\|_1\leq2K\xi. $$
Also,
$$ g(p')\geq v'\geq v-\xi\geq\max_{\mathcal P_\gamma}g-2\xi. $$

Let $u$ be the uniform policy and define
$$ e=K\xi,\qquad a=\frac{1-\gamma}{K}, \qquad \ell=\frac{e}{a+e}, \qquad \widetilde\pi=(1-\ell)p+\ell u. $$
For every coordinate,
$$ \widetilde\pi_a \geq (1-\ell)\left(\frac{\gamma}{K}-e\right) +\ell\left(\frac{\gamma}{K}+a\right) =\frac{\gamma}{K}. $$
Thus $\widetilde\pi$ is exactly feasible.

Since $a\geq1/(2K)$, we have $\ell\leq2K^2\xi$. Our choice of $\xi$ also ensures $\|u-p\|_1\leq3$, and therefore
$$ \|\widetilde\pi-p'\|_1 \leq2K\xi+3\ell \leq8K^2\xi. $$
The Lipschitz bound yields
$$ g(\widetilde\pi) \geq \max_{\mathcal P_\gamma}g-(2+8HK^2)\xi = \max_{\mathcal P_\gamma}g-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$
Because $F_G=e^g\leq1$, this entails product-objective error at most $\varepsilon/2$.

Finally, round $\widetilde\pi$ to a dyadic simplex policy with denominator $D$, where $D$ is a power of two satisfying
$$ D\geq \max\left\{\frac{2HK}{\varepsilon},\frac{2K}{\gamma}\right\}. $$
Rounding down all coordinates and distributing the remaining units gives coordinate error at most $1/D$. It preserves the floor $\gamma/(2K)$.

On the simplex, every partial derivative of $F_G$ is at most $H$. Thus rounding changes its value by at most
$$ \frac{HK}{D}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$
This establishes the policy guarantee used in Section 3.

Dyadic policies can be sampled with exactly $\log_2D$ random bits. The empirical statistics, confidence bounds, optimization output, and rounding precision all have polynomial encoding length. The running time depends on rational mass denominators through their bit lengths, never through their numerical values.

6. Lower bounds throughout the confidence range

All constructions below use binary individual rewards. Their aggregate observations are therefore binary rational because the masses are binary rational.

6.1 The ordinary-bandit contribution

Suppose $\lambda>0$, equivalently $\delta<1-1/K$. Give every type the same Bernoulli reward. Then
$$ W_\nu(\pi,\mu)=\sum_a\pi_a\mu_a. $$

Under a reference environment $D_0$, every arm has mean $1/2$. Define
$$ S_j=\sum_{t=1}^H\pi_{t,j}. $$
Since $\sum_jS_j=H$, some $j$ satisfies
$$ \mathbb E_0S_j\leq\frac HK. $$
Let
$$ a=1-\frac{\lambda}{2}. $$
Markov’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr_0(S_j\leq aH) \geq1-\frac1{Ka} = \delta+\frac{s\lambda}{2-\lambda}. $$

In environment $D_j$, raise only arm $j$’s mean by
$$ \epsilon=\frac{s\lambda}{32}\sqrt q. $$
The adaptive likelihood chain rule gives
$$ \operatorname{KL}(P_0\Vert P_j) = \mathbb E_0N_j\, \operatorname{kl}(1/2,1/2+\epsilon). $$
Here $\mathbb E_0N_j=\mathbb E_0S_j$. Since $\epsilon\leq1/32$,
$$ \operatorname{kl}(1/2,1/2+\epsilon) = -\frac12\log(1-4\epsilon^2) \leq3\epsilon^2. $$
Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{KL}(P_0\Vert P_j) \leq\frac{3s^2\lambda^2}{1024}. $$
Pinsker’s inequality gives total variation distance smaller than $s\lambda/8$. The event $S_j\leq aH$ therefore has probability strictly greater than $\delta$ under $D_j$.

On that event,
$$ R_H=\epsilon(H-S_j) \geq \frac{s\lambda^2}{64}H\sqrt q. $$
Every smaller regret threshold fails the minimax probability requirement. Hence
$$ B^\star\geq\frac{s\lambda^2}{64}H\sqrt q. $$

6.2 A hidden rare arm with an unknown optimal mixture

Fix a nonempty proper subset $S$ of types, and write
$$ \beta=\beta_S\in(0,1). $$
Put
$$ q'=\min\{1,(K-1)/H\}, \qquad \epsilon=\frac{s q'}4. $$

In a reference environment, all types in $S$ receive a common Bernoulli reward of mean $\epsilon$ from arm $1$, and zero from every other arm. All types outside $S$ receive a common Bernoulli reward of mean $1-\beta$ from arm $1$, and deterministic reward $1$ from every other arm. The two groups may be sampled independently.

Choose $j\in\{2,\ldots,K\}$ with
$$ \mathbb E_0N_j\leq\frac{H}{K-1}. $$

For a parameter $z\in[1/4,3/4]$, define
$$ a_z=\frac{1-z}{2-\beta-z}. $$
In the alternative environment $D_{j,z}$, change only the reward of group $S$ on arm $j$: it becomes a common Bernoulli reward of mean $\epsilon a_z$.

Couple the reference and alternative histories using the same learner randomness and all unchanged reward samples. Histories can first diverge only when arm $j$ produces a positive group-$S$ reward. Thus their total variation distance is at most
$$ \epsilon a_z\,\mathbb E_0N_j \leq \frac{\epsilon H}{K-1} \leq\frac s4. $$

For a policy, write $p=\pi_1$. Its first-group utility is
$$ \epsilon(p+a_z\pi_j) \leq\epsilon(a_z+(1-a_z)p), $$
and its second-group utility is $1-\beta p$. Define
$$ f_z(p) = (a_z+(1-a_z)p)^\beta(1-\beta p)^{1-\beta}. $$
The policy using probability $z$ on arm $1$ and $1-z$ on arm $j$ attains $\epsilon^\beta f_z(z)$.

We now verify that $z$ is the unique maximizer and quantify the loss away from it. Differentiating the logarithm,
$$ \frac{f_z'(p)}{f_z(p)} = \frac{\beta(1-a_z)}{a_z+(1-a_z)p} - \frac{(1-\beta)\beta}{1-\beta p}. $$
Substitution of $p=z$ gives zero. Moreover,
$$ f_z''(p) = -\beta(1-\beta)f_z(p) \left( \frac{1-a_z}{a_z+(1-a_z)p} + \frac{\beta}{1-\beta p} \right)^2. $$

For all admissible $z,p$,
$$ a_z\geq\frac15, \qquad 1-a_z\geq\frac{1-\beta}{2}. $$
Also,
$$ f_z(p)\geq a_z^\beta(1-\beta)^{1-\beta}\geq\frac1{10}. $$
For the last inequality, $t^t\geq e^{-1/e}>1/2$ for $t\in(0,1)$, as follows by minimizing $t\log t$. Both utility factors are at most $1$, so the expression in parentheses is at least
$$ 1-a_z+\beta\geq\frac12. $$
Therefore
$$ f_z''(p)\leq-\frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{40}. $$
Integrating this inequality from the stationary point $z$ proves
$$ f_z(z)-f_z(p) \geq \frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{80}(p-z)^2. $$
In particular, $z$ is the unique maximizer.

Let
$$ \bar p=\frac1H\sum_{t=1}^H\pi_{t,1}. $$
Concavity of $f_z$ and the preceding welfare bound give, on every alternative history,
$$ R_H \geq \frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{80} H\epsilon^\beta(\bar p-z)^2. $$

Set $r=s/16$. Under the reference law, average over $z$ uniformly in $[1/4,3/4]$. For every fixed value of $\bar p$, the set of parameters within distance $r$ has length at most $2r$. Hence
$$ \frac1{1/2} \int_{1/4}^{3/4} \Pr_0(|\bar p-z|\leq r)\,dz \leq4r=\frac s4. $$
There is consequently a parameter $z$ for which the reference probability is at most $s/4$.

For this parameter, let
$$ b_\beta = \frac{\beta(1-\beta)}{80}H\epsilon^\beta r^2. $$
The event $R_H\leq b_\beta$ implies $|\bar p-z|\leq r$. Transferring this event through the coupling yields
$$ \Pr_{j,z}(R_H\leq b_\beta)\leq\frac s2<s. $$
Thus its complementary probability exceeds $\delta$.

Finally, $q'\geq q/2$, so $\epsilon\geq sq/8$. It follows that
$$ B^\star \geq b_\beta \geq \frac1{163840} \beta(1-\beta)s^{\beta+2}Hq^\beta. $$
This proves the proper-subset lower bound for every $\delta<1$.

6.3 Recovering the high-confidence characterization

When $\delta<1/4$,
$$ s>\frac34,\qquad \lambda\geq\frac13. $$
Thus the ordinary-bandit lower bound is a universal constant times $H\sqrt q$.

For every subset mass $\beta\leq1/2$, the rare-mixture lower bound is a universal constant times $\beta Hq^\beta$, because

A subset with $\beta>1/2$ contributes at most $H\sqrt q$ to $\mathcal F$. The full subset, of mass $1$, also contributes at most $H\sqrt q$.

Taking the largest contribution proves $B^\star\geq c\mathcal F$.

7. Exact zero quantiles and a one-round formula

If $K=1$, there is only one policy, so regret is zero.

Suppose $K\geq2$ and $\tau=1$. Choose one uniformly random arm and use it throughout. Every environment has at least one optimal arm, so this learner has zero regret with probability at least $1/K$. Therefore
$$ B^\star=0 \qquad\text{if}\qquad \delta\geq1-\frac1K. $$
For smaller $\delta$, the ordinary-bandit lower bound is strictly positive.

If $\tau\geq2$, a nonempty proper subset exists and has mass strictly between zero and one. The rare-mixture lower bound is then strictly positive for every $\delta<1$. This proves the zero-quantile characterization stated at the outset.

There is also an exact formula for one type and one round:
$$ B^\star(K,1,(1),\delta) = 1-\frac1{\lceil K(1-\delta)\rceil}. $$

Proof. Put $n=\lceil K(1-\delta)\rceil$. Choose a uniformly random subset of $n$ arms and assign probability $1/n$ to each of them. Any fixed optimal arm belongs to this subset with probability $n/K\geq1-\delta$. On that event, regret is at most $1-1/n$, since rewards lie in $[0,1]$.

For the converse, consider the $K$ environments in which exactly one arm has deterministic reward $1$ and all others have reward zero. If a learner guarantees threshold $b$, its first policy must satisfy
$$ \Pr(\pi_j\geq1-b)\geq1-\delta $$
for every $j$. If $b<1-1/n$, each policy has at most $n-1$ coordinates satisfying $\pi_j\geq1-b$. Summing their probabilities would give
$$ K(1-\delta)\leq n-1, $$
contrary to the definition of $n$. For $n=1$, the lower bound is simply nonnegativity.

8. Why the uniform rate changes at larger $\delta$

Let $\theta_0$ have largest mass, and put
$$ \beta=1-\nu_{\theta_0}. $$
Suppose $\tau\geq2$. Choose a uniformly random arm $j$ and use the fixed policy
$$ \pi=(1-\beta)e_j+\beta u, $$
where $u$ is uniform.

With probability at least $1/K$, arm $j$ maximizes the utility of type $\theta_0$. On this event, if $M_\theta=\max_a\mu_{\theta,a}$, then
$$ W_\nu(\pi^\star,\mu) \leq M_{\theta_0}^{1-\beta} \prod_{\theta\neq\theta_0}M_\theta^{\nu_\theta}, $$
whereas
$$ W_\nu(\pi,\mu) \geq (1-\beta)^{1-\beta} \left(\frac{\beta}{K}\right)^\beta M_{\theta_0}^{1-\beta} \prod_{\theta\neq\theta_0}M_\theta^{\nu_\theta}. $$
If any $M_\theta=0$, regret is zero; otherwise the displayed inequalities apply directly.

Define binary entropy using natural logarithms:
$$ h(\beta)=-\beta\log\beta-(1-\beta)\log(1-\beta). $$
For $\delta\geq1-1/K$, the guessing strategy proves
$$ B^\star \leq H\left[1-e^{-h(\beta)-\beta\log K}\right] \leq H[h(\beta)+\beta\log K]. $$

For example, take
$$ K=2,\qquad \delta=\frac12,\qquad \nu=(H^{-2},1-H^{-2}), $$
with $H$ a power of two, so the masses are binary rational. Since
$$ h(\beta)\leq\beta\log(1/\beta)+\beta, $$
the preceding bound gives
$$ B^\star \leq \frac{2\log H+1+\log2}{H}. $$
But
$$ \mathcal F\geq\sqrt{2H}. $$
Thus $\mathcal F$ cannot characterize the minimax quantile uniformly over masses at $\delta=1/2$, even up to logarithmic factors.

A further statistical comparison identifies a useful scale in this regime. Define
$$ \Psi=\max_{\varnothing\neq S\subsetneq\Theta} \beta_S(1-\beta_S)q^{\beta_S}. $$
For $\tau\geq2$ and $\delta\geq1-1/K$,
$$ c(1-\delta)^3H\Psi \leq B^\star \leq C\Lambda^2H\Psi+1. $$

The lower bound follows from Section 6 because $s^{\beta+2}\geq s^3$. Here is a proof of the upper comparison.

First,
$$ \frac{\mathcal F}{H}\leq\max\{\sqrt q,2\Psi\}. $$
Indeed, masses above $1/2$ contribute at most $\sqrt q$, and for masses at most $1/2$ the factor $1-\beta_S$ is at least $1/2$.

Let $\rho=1-\max_\theta\nu_\theta$. If $\rho\geq1/4$, there is a subset of mass in $[1/4,1/2]$. If the largest mass is at least $1/2$, use its complement. Otherwise accumulate types until their total first reaches $1/4$; that total is below $3/4$, and either it or its complement has the required mass. Consequently,
$$ \Psi\geq\frac3{16}\sqrt q, $$
and the $\mathcal F$ upper bound suffices.

Suppose $\rho<1/4$, and put $T=\log(1/q)$. If $\rho T\leq2$, then
$$ \Psi\geq\rho(1-\rho)q^\rho \geq\frac34e^{-2}\rho. $$
Guessing an arm maximizing the largest-mass type and mixing it with the uniform policy at weight $\gamma=1/(2H)$ gives, on the successful guess,
$$ R_H\leq\frac12+\rho H\log(2KH). $$
Its success probability is at least $1/K$, which suffices in the stated confidence regime.

If $\rho T\geq1$, then
$$ \frac{\Psi}{\sqrt q} \geq \frac34\rho e^{(1/2-\rho)/\rho} \geq\frac{3e}{16}>\frac12. $$
The middle inequality follows because $\rho e^{1/(2\rho)-1}$ decreases on $(0,1/4]$. Again the $\mathcal F$ upper bound suffices. These two cases cover all possibilities.

This comparison leaves a factor polynomial in $1/(1-\delta)$ between the lower and upper bounds, rather than a logarithmic factor. It therefore does not settle the requested joint confidence dependence.

9. Fixed-mass rates and the remaining question

For every fixed mass vector with $\tau\geq2$, put
$$ \alpha=\min_\theta\nu_\theta\leq\frac12. $$
The singleton of mass $\alpha$ in Section 6 gives, for every fixed $\delta<1$,
$$ B^\star\geq c_{\nu,\delta}Hq^\alpha. $$
Conversely, every nonempty subset has mass at least $\alpha$, so
$$ \mathcal F\leq Hq^\alpha. $$
Hence, with constants allowed to depend on $\nu,\delta$,
$$ B^\star(K,H,\nu,\delta) = \widetilde\Theta(Hq^\alpha). $$
In particular, for $H\geq K$,
$$ B^\star = \widetilde\Theta(K^\alpha H^{1-\alpha}). $$
The polynomial algorithm above attains this fixed-mass rate throughout $\delta<1$.

For arbitrary rational masses treated as part of the input, the stronger uniform conclusion is proved for $0<\delta<1/4$: the minimax scale is $\mathcal F$ up to logarithmic factors and additive $1$, and attaining that guarantee admits a polynomial-time implementation. No computational hardness assumption is needed for this result.

What remains unresolved is a characterization with uniform constants and only logarithmic losses across the full joint parameter range, especially near $\delta=1-1/K$ and as $\delta$ approaches $1$. Consequently, the polynomial-attainment versus hardness question for that full guarantee also remains open here.
