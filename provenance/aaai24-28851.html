The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses of agent types, each with a positive entitlement and additive costs for finitely many recurring indivisible chore classes, and rational per-capita supplies of those classes, decide whether and constructively output a finite-support mass allocation over integral chore bundles such that every positive-mass agent assignment satisfies 1WEF against every other assignment, with complexity polynomial in the type count, chore-class count, and binary input length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq 1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq 1\) recurring chore classes with per-capita supplies \(s_k\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\). Type \(t\) has entitlement \(w_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) and additive class costs \(c_{tk}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\); write \(c_t(b)=\sum_{k=1}^m c_{tk}b_k\). Entitlements need not sum to \(1\).
A mass allocation consists of finitely many pairs \((t,b)\), with integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^m\), and positive binary-rational assignment masses \(x_{t,b}\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_k=s_k\) for every class \(k\).
It is 1WEF if every ordered pair of positive-mass assignments \((t,b),(u,d)\), including assignments of the same type, satisfies \(\frac{c_t(b)-\max_{k:b_k>0}c_{tk}}{w_t}\leq\frac{c_t(d)}{w_u}\); the maximum is \(0\) for an empty bundle. Thus one may remove one chore from the comparing agent’s own bundle.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; bundle multiplicities are encoded in binary.

Is feasibility of a 1WEF mass allocation in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can a finite-support allocation also be constructed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For explicitly listed agents and indivisible chores, arbitrary positive entitlements and additive nonnegative costs admit a complete 1WEF allocation computable in polynomial time ([Springer, Hajiaghayi and Yami](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.16081)).
An independent efficient picking-sequence result uses a continuous interpretation of discrete picks ([Wu, Zhang and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08090)); no result settling the exact mass-allocation problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized society masses to sum to one, so supplies are per capita.
  • Specified binary-rational positive entitlements and nonnegative additive costs and supplies; retained zero costs and left entitlements unnormalized.
  • Interpreted recurring chore classes as permitting arbitrary nonnegative integral multiplicities in each bundle, encoded in binary.
  • Defined allocation masses as binary rationals, with exact type-mass conservation and exact allocation of every class supply.
  • Required fairness between every ordered pair of supported assignments, including distinct bundles assigned to the same type, using the comparing type’s costs on both bundles.
  • Made empty bundles automatically satisfy their outgoing 1WEF comparisons, consistent with the discrete guarantee when there are fewer chores than agents.
  • Used positive support size and at least one chore class, while allowing zero total supply.

Result

Feasibility is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Under the interpretation that “binary-rational” means a rational encoded by binary numerator and denominator, every valid instance is feasible, and a finite-support allocation can be constructed in time polynomial in \(\m,\tau,L\), with at most \(\tau(m+1)\) positive-mass assignments.

If “binary-rational” instead means *dyadic rational*, feasibility holds exactly when all supplies are dyadic; the construction below also runs in polynomial time in that case.

The construction compresses the [reversed weighted picking sequence of Wu, Zhang and Zhou](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.08090). Both its fairness and the compressed implementation are proved below.

1. Clearing denominators

First interpret assignment masses as arbitrary rationals encoded in binary.

Choose a positive integer \(\N\) divisible by every denominator occurring in the masses \(\mu_t\) and supplies \(\s_k\). Their product suffices and has binary length at most \(\L\). Define

\[ N_t=N\mu_t,\qquad S_k=Ns_k,\qquad M=\sum_{k=1}^m S_k. \]

These are nonnegative integers, with \(\N_t\geq1\) and \(\sum_tN_t=N\).

Consider a finite instance containing \(\N_t\) identical agents of each type \(\t\), and \(\S_k\) individual chores of class \(\k\). Give every type-\(\t\) agent weight \(\w_t\) and class costs \(\c_{tk}\). Order agents lexicographically by type and then by an index \(\j\in\{1,\ldots,N_t\}\).

A complete 1WEF allocation in this finite instance induces a mass allocation: if exactly \(\ell\) type-\(\t\) agents receive bundle \(\b\), assign

\[ x_{t,b}=\frac{\ell}{N}. \]

The type-mass and supply equations then hold. Every ordered pair of positive-mass assignments has corresponding agents, so finite-agent 1WEF implies the required mass-allocation condition, including comparisons within one type.

The difficulty is that \(\N\) and \(\M\) can be exponentially large. We therefore construct their allocation implicitly.

2. A finite-agent picking procedure and its fairness

This section concerns an arbitrary finite collection of agents with positive weights and additive nonnegative chore costs.

Generate a forward sequence of length \(\M\). Starting with counters \(\a_i=0\), repeatedly select an agent minimizing \(\a_i/w_i\), break ties by the fixed agent order, and increase that agent’s counter by one.

Then process this sequence in reverse, letting each selected agent take a minimum-cost remaining chore.

Prefix-count invariant

Let \(\a_i(K)\) be agent \(\i\)’s number of occurrences in the first \(\K\) positions of the forward sequence. For every prefix and every ordered pair of agents,

\[ \frac{a_i(K)-1}{w_i}\leq\frac{a_j(K)}{w_j}. \tag{1} \]

At \(\K=0\), this follows from positivity of the weights. Suppose it holds before the next selected agent \(\h\) increments its counter. For the new inequalities with \(\i=h\) and \(\j\neq h\), the selection rule gives

\[ \frac{a_h(K+1)-1}{w_h} =\frac{a_h(K)}{w_h} \leq\frac{a_j(K)}{w_j}. \]

The inequality with \(\i=j=h\) is immediate. All other inequalities are preserved because their left sides stay fixed and their right sides either stay fixed or increase. This proves (1).

Consequently, every suffix of the reversed sequence satisfies

\[ \frac{\text{number of occurrences of }i-1}{w_i} \leq \frac{\text{number of occurrences of }j}{w_j}. \tag{2} \]

Indeed, such a suffix consists precisely of an original forward prefix.

Fairness proof

Write \(\B_i\) for the final bundle of agent \(\i\). Fix agents \(\i,j\) and a real threshold \(\z\geq0\). Let \(\n_i(z)\) count chores in \(\B_i\) whose cost to \(\i\) exceeds \(\z\), and let \(\n_j(z)\) count chores in \(\B_j\) whose cost to \(\i\) exceeds \(\z\).

Suppose \(\n_i(z)>0\). Consider the suffix of the reversed picking sequence beginning when \(\i\) takes its first chore costing more than \(\z\). At that pick, every remaining chore costs more than \(\z\) to \(\i\): otherwise the selected chore would not have minimum cost.

Thus that suffix contains all \(\n_i(z)\) costly picks of \(\i\), and every pick of \(\j\) in the suffix contributes to \(\n_j(z)\). Applying (2) gives

\[ \frac{n_i(z)-1}{w_i}\leq\frac{n_j(z)}{w_j}. \]

When \(\n_i(z)=0\), nonnegativity gives the corresponding inequality with zero on the left. Therefore, for every threshold,

\[ \frac{\max\{n_i(z)-1,0\}}{w_i}\leq\frac{n_j(z)}{w_j}. \tag{3} \]

For a finite bundle of nonnegative-cost chores, integrating its threshold counts gives its total cost. Removing a maximum-cost chore changes the threshold count from \(\n_i(z)\) to \(\max\{n_i(z)-1,0\}\). Integrating (3) therefore yields

\[ \frac{c_i(B_i)-\max_{e\in B_i}c_i(e)}{w_i} \leq\frac{c_i(B_j)}{w_j}, \]

with maximum zero for an empty bundle. Hence the procedure produces a complete 1WEF allocation. This proof requires neither normalized weights nor strictly positive costs.

3. Computing forward prefixes without expanding the population

Write each type weight in reduced form as \(\w_t=p_t/q_t\), with positive integers \(\p_t,q_t\). Set

\[ D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}p_t,\qquad A_t=\frac{Dq_t}{p_t}. \]

Each \(\A_t\) is a positive integer of polynomial binary length.

For every type-\(\t\) agent, introduce events at integer times

\[ 0,A_t,2A_t,\ldots. \]

Order all events by time and then by agent order. This is exactly the forward sequence: an agent that has already appeared \(\r\) times has its next event at time \(\r A_t=D r/w_t\).

For integer \(\T\geq0\), the number of events through time \(\T\) is

\[ F(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}N_t \left(\left\lfloor\frac{T}{A_t}\right\rfloor+1\right). \tag{4} \]

Given a positive prefix length \(\K\leq M\), find the smallest integer \(\T\) satisfying \(\F(T)\geq K\). Binary search on \(\{0,\ldots,K A_{\max}\}\), where \(\A_{\max}=\max_tA_t\), finds it in polynomial time.

Each type-\(\t\) agent has exactly

\[ u_t(K)=\left\lceil\frac{T}{A_t}\right\rceil \]

events strictly before \(\T\). The prefix then takes

\[ J=K-\sum_tN_tu_t(K) \]

events at time \(\T\). Only types with \(\A_t\mid T\) have events there. Visiting those types in order, give each type the first \(\z_t(K)\) of its \(\N_t\) events until these \(\J\) events are exhausted.

Thus the number of occurrences of agent \(t,j)\) in the prefix is

\[ v_{t,j}(K)=u_t(K)+\mathbf 1_{\{j\leq z_t(K)\}}, \tag{5} \]

and the aggregate number for type \(\t\) is

\[ P_t(K)=N_tu_t(K)+z_t(K). \tag{6} \]

For \(\K=0\), set all these quantities to zero. Equations (4)–(6) give a polynomial-time prefix oracle using only polynomial-length integers.

4. Simulating reverse picking in at most \(\m\) phases

Maintain remaining supplies \(\R_k\), initially \(\S_k\), and a cursor \(\K\), initially \(\M\). The unprocessed reversed sequence consists of forward positions \(\K,K-1,\ldots,1\).

When \(\K>0\), let \(\f_t\) be type \(\t\)’s minimum-cost class among those with \(\R_k>0\), breaking cost ties by class index.

Temporarily suppose each type always picks its class \(\f_t\). Processing the next \(\H\) reversed positions would use

\[ d_k(H)= \sum_{\{t:f_t=k\}} \bigl(P_t(K)-P_t(K-H)\bigr) \tag{7} \]

chores of class \(\k\).

Every \(\d_k(H)\) is nondecreasing in \(\H\). Use binary search to find the largest \(\H\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\) satisfying

\[ d_k(H)\leq R_k\qquad\text{for every }k. \tag{8} \]

The next hypothetical pick uses a positive-supply class, so \(\H\geq1\).

Why a phase exactly reproduces greedy picking

Throughout these \(\H\) hypothetical picks, no class is overused, by monotonicity and (8). Therefore, at every pick, the selected class \(\f_t\) is still available.

It was type \(\t\)’s preferred class at the phase’s start. Removing other chores cannot make an available \(\f_t\) less preferred. Hence every hypothetical pick agrees with actual minimum-cost picking.

We may therefore process the entire phase at once and update

\[ R_k\leftarrow R_k-d_k(H),\qquad K\leftarrow K-H. \]

If \(\H<K\) before this update, maximality implies that the next hypothetical pick would exceed one class supply. Since quantities are integral, that class is exhausted exactly by the chosen phase.

If \(\H=K\), all remaining picks are processed. The invariant \(\sum_kR_k=K\) implies that all remaining supplies are exhausted.

Thus every phase exhausts at least one previously positive-supply class. There are at most \(\m\) phases.

5. Constructing the bundles and assignment masses

Suppose the phase endpoints are

\[ M=K_0>K_1>\cdots>K_h=0,\qquad h\leq m, \]

and type \(\t\)’s selected class in phase \(\ell\) is \(\f_{t,\ell}\).

Agent \(t,j)\)’s number of picks during that phase is

\[ v_{t,j}(K_{\ell-1})-v_{t,j}(K_\ell). \tag{9} \]

These are nonnegative integers, because they count occurrences in nested prefixes. Its bundle is therefore given by

\[ b_{t,j,k} = \sum_{\{\ell:f_{t,\ell}=k\}} \bigl(v_{t,j}(K_{\ell-1})-v_{t,j}(K_\ell)\bigr). \tag{10} \]

By (5), the bundle depends on \(\j\) only through comparisons with the phase-endpoint values \(\z_t(K_\ell)\). The terminal value \(\z_t(0)\) is zero, so there are at most \(\h\) relevant internal cuts in the ordered indices \(\{1,\ldots,N_t\}\).

Partition those indices at these cuts. There are at most \(\h+1\leq m+1\) nonempty intervals per type, and every agent in an interval receives the same bundle.

For an interval containing \(\ell\) indices and receiving bundle \(\b\), output

\[ (t,b),\qquad x_{t,b}=\frac{\ell}{N}. \]

Identical bundles of the same type may be merged. Hence the output has at most \(\tau(m+1)\) positive-mass assignments.

Because this construction reproduces the finite-agent allocation exactly, Section 2 proves its 1WEF property. The interval lengths sum to \(\N_t\), and the allocation uses exactly \(\S_k\) chores of each class. Consequently,

\[ \sum_bx_{t,b}=\frac{N_t}{N}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_k=\frac{S_k}{N}=s_k. \]

6. Polynomial running time and encoding length

The integers \(\N,N_t,S_k,M,D,A_t\) have binary lengths polynomial in \(\m,\tau,L\). The prefix oracle searches times bounded by \(\M A_{\max}\), also of polynomial binary length.

Each phase uses a binary search over \(\H\leq M\). Each trial evaluates (7) using the prefix oracle and polynomially many arithmetic operations. There are at most \(\m\) phases.

The final partition has at most \(\tau(m+1)\) intervals. Formula (10) computes their bundles in polynomial time. Every bundle coordinate is at most \(\M\), and each output mass has numerator at most \(\N\) and denominator \(\N\). Thus the entire output has polynomial encoding length.

When \(\M=0\), simply assign the empty bundle to each type with mass \(\mu_t\).

This proves both polynomial-time feasibility and polynomial-time construction.

7. If assignment masses must be dyadic

A finite sum of integer multiples of dyadic rationals is dyadic. Therefore, dyadic assignment masses necessarily make every \(\s_k\) dyadic.

Conversely, if the given masses and all supplies are dyadic, choose \(\N\) to be a power of two clearing their denominators. The entire construction above then applies, and every output mass \(\ell/N\) is dyadic.

Testing whether a reduced rational denominator is a power of two takes polynomial time. Thus this interpretation also has a complete polynomial-time feasibility and construction algorithm.
