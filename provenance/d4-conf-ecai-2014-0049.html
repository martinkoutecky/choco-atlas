The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a terminal proposer with voting weight \(w_0\) and a responder society with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), each type \(t\) has voting weight \(w_t\), discount factor \(\delta_t\), and per-capita conflict share \(\alpha_t\). For quota \(q\) and proposer outside payoff \(d_0\), choose offered fractions \(y_t\in[0,1]\) minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t\delta_t\alpha_t\), subject to \(w_0+\sum_t\mu_t y_t w_t\ge q\) and \(1-\sum_t\mu_t y_t\delta_t\alpha_t\ge d_0\). Compute the accepting fractions and the proposer’s residual share.

Result

The exact terminal-offer computation is solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the total binary input length \(L\). A greedy algorithm computes an exact rational minimizer, with at most one type receiving a strictly fractional selection.

Moreover, every input satisfying the stated hypotheses is feasible.

1. The proposer’s outside-payoff constraint is automatic

For any vector \(y\) satisfying \(0\le y_t\le1\),

\(C(y)=\sum_t\mu_ty_t\delta_t\alpha_t \le\sum_t\mu_t\delta_t\alpha_t \le\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t =1-\alpha_0 \le1-\delta_0\alpha_0 =1-d_0.\)

The first two inequalities use \(\mu_t>0\), \(\alpha_t\ge0\), and \(\delta_t\le1\). The last inequality uses \(\alpha_0\ge0\) and \(\delta_0\le1\).

Thus every such vector satisfies \(1-C(y)\ge d_0\). In particular, selecting \(y_t=1\) for every type satisfies both this constraint and the quorum constraint, because the assumed bound on \(q\) gives

\(w_0+\sum_t\mu_tw_t\ge q.\)

Consequently, infeasibility cannot occur under the stated hypotheses.

2. Reduction to fractional knapsack

Partition the support into

\(Z=\{t:\alpha_t=0\},\qquad P=\{t:\alpha_t>0\}.\)

For every \(t\in Z\), set \(y_t=1\), as required by acceptance at equality. These types contribute no cost and supply voting weight

\(W_Z=\sum_{t\in Z}\mu_tw_t.\)

Define the remaining voting requirement by

\(R=\max\{0,q-w_0-W_Z\}.\)

For each \(t\in P\), define its voting capacity, cost per voting-weight unit, and selected voting weight by

\(b_t=\mu_tw_t>0,\qquad \rho_t=\frac{\delta_t\alpha_t}{w_t}>0,\qquad x_t=b_ty_t.\)

Because \(b_t>0\), this substitution gives a bijection between \(y_t\in[0,1]\) and \(x_t\in[0,b_t]\). Moreover,

\(\mu_ty_t\delta_t\alpha_t=\rho_tx_t.\)

The remaining optimization is therefore exactly

\(\min\sum_{t\in P}\rho_tx_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le x_t\le b_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in P}x_t\ge R.\)

The quorum assumption implies

\(R\le\sum_{t\in P}b_t.\)

Indeed, \(q-w_0-W_Z\le\sum_{t\in P}\mu_tw_t\), and the right-hand side is nonnegative.

3. Exact algorithm and output

If \(R=0\), choose \(y_t=0\) for every \(t\in P\). Together with \(y_t=1\) on \(Z\), this gives minimum cost \(C^*=0\) and proposer residual share \(1\): costs are nonnegative, so no smaller cost is possible.

Suppose now that \(R>0\). Then \(P\) is nonempty. Write its types as \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\), ordered so that

\(\rho_{t_1}\le\cdots\le\rho_{t_n}.\)

Ties may be ordered arbitrarily. Abbreviate \(b_i=b_{t_i}\) and \(\rho_i=\rho_{t_i}\), and define

\(B_0=0,\qquad B_j=\sum_{i=1}^j b_i.\)

Let \(k\) be the smallest index with \(B_k\ge R\). It exists because \(B_n\ge R\). By minimality,

\(0<R-B_{k-1}\le b_k.\)

Choose

\(x_i^*= \begin{cases} b_i,&i<k,\\ R-B_{k-1},&i=k,\\ 0,&i>k. \end{cases}\)

Return \(y_{t_i}^*=x_i^*/b_i\), together with \(y_t^*=1\) for \(t\in Z\).

This vector lies within the required bounds and supplies exactly \(R\) voting weight from \(P\). Hence it meets the quorum, and Section 1 proves that it also meets the proposer’s outside-payoff constraint.

Its cost and proposer residual are

\(C^*=\sum_{i<k}\mu_{t_i}\delta_{t_i}\alpha_{t_i} +\rho_k(R-B_{k-1}),\qquad p_0^*=1-C^*.\)

The offer is represented by these fractions: within type \(t\), a fraction \(y_t^*\) receives per-unit-mass payment \(\delta_t\alpha_t\), and the remainder receives zero. Only type \(t_k\) can have a fraction strictly between zero and one.

4. Proof of optimality

Continue with \(R>0\). Let \(x\) be any feasible vector for the reduced problem, and set \(\lambda=\rho_k>0\). Since \(\sum_i x_i^*=R\),

\(\sum_i\rho_ix_i-\sum_i\rho_ix_i^* =\lambda\left(\sum_i x_i-R\right) +\sum_i(\rho_i-\lambda)(x_i-x_i^*).\)

Every term on the right is nonnegative:

  • Feasibility gives \(\sum_i x_i\ge R\), so the first term is nonnegative.
  • For \(i<k\), ordering gives \(\rho_i-\lambda\le0\), while \(x_i-x_i^*=x_i-b_i\le0\). Their product is nonnegative.
  • For \(i=k\), the coefficient \(\rho_i-\lambda\) is zero.
  • For \(i>k\), ordering gives \(\rho_i-\lambda\ge0\), while \(x_i-x_i^*=x_i\ge0\). Their product is nonnegative.

Therefore \(\sum_i\rho_ix_i\ge\sum_i\rho_ix_i^*\). The substitution in Section 2 preserves the objective and feasibility, so \(y^*\) is a minimizer of the original problem.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

Use exact rational arithmetic, representing each rational by an integer numerator and a positive integer denominator, both written in binary.

The algorithm performs \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational comparisons for sorting and \(O(\tau)\) additional arithmetic operations. Comparisons are exact: ratios can be compared by cross-multiplying their positive denominators.

These operation counts also give polynomial bit complexity:

  • Each individual capacity \(b_t\), ratio \(\rho_t\), and full-selection cost \(\mu_t\delta_t\alpha_t\) is formed using a constant number of products or quotients of input rationals. Its numerator and denominator therefore have \(O(L)\) bits.
  • A sum of at most \(\tau\) such rationals can be represented using their product denominator. That denominator has \(O(\tau L)\) bits; the corresponding numerator has \(O(\tau L+\log(\tau+1))\) bits.
  • Consequently, \(W_Z\), \(R\), and every prefix sum \(B_j\) have polynomial bit length. The fractional output \((R-B_{k-1})/b_k\), the cost \(C^*\), and the residual \(1-C^*\) require only a constant number of further rational operations and retain polynomial bit length.

Integer arithmetic and comparisons on these representations take polynomial time. Thus the entire algorithm, including writing its exact rational output, runs in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). The bargaining-cycle count \(m\) is neither used nor needed.

6. Relation to the discrete hardness result

The cited discrete terminal optimization requires binary coalition choices, as displayed in [Fatima–Wooldridge (2014), Theorem 7](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/michael.wooldridge/pubs/ecai2014a.pdf) and [Fatima–Wooldridge (2016), Theorem 12](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/michael.wooldridge/pubs/jaamas2016a.pdf).

Here, arbitrary fractions within every supported type are permitted. This gives precisely the continuous variables used above, for which the greedy optimality proof applies. The discrete hardness result therefore does not obstruct polynomial-time computation for this support-encoded mass problem.
