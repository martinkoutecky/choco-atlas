The anchor (named result in the paper)

Property 4 (Social cohesion) — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite activity set \(A\), a finite set of complete participant types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), rational activity interests \(v_r(a)\) with \(v_r(\theta)=0\), and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_a\), compute a mass assignment \(x_{r,a}\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{\theta\}}x_{r,a}=\mu_r\) for every \(r\), and \(\sum_{r\in T}x_{r,a}\leq\kappa_a\) for every real activity \(a\). Require that whenever \(x_{r,a}>0\), every activity \(b\neq a\) with \(v_r(b)\geq 0\) and \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)\) is full: \(\sum_{r\in T}x_{r,b}=\kappa_b\). What is the computational complexity of finding such an assignment in terms of \(|T|\), \(|A|\), and the input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_r\), with \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(A\) contain \(m\geq1\) real activities, each with a positive binary rational capacity \(\kappa_a\). The inactivity option \(\theta\) has unlimited capacity. Each type specifies binary rational interests \(v_r(a)\in[-1,1]\), with \(v_r(\theta)=0\); ties are allowed.
A mass assignment consists of rational \(x_{r,a}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{\theta\}}x_{r,a}=\mu_r\) and \(\sum_{r\in T}x_{r,a}\leq\kappa_a\) for every \(a\in A\). Types may split their mass among activities.
An assignment is socially cohesive if, whenever \(x_{r,a}>0\), every \(b\in A\setminus\{a\}\) with \(v_r(b)\geq0\) and \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)\) satisfies \(\sum_{s\in T}x_{s,b}=\kappa_b\). No additional utility or individual-rationality condition is imposed.

Is finding a socially cohesive mass assignment solvable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), or is this search problem NP-hard?

For discrete individuals, the inclusive algorithm terminates with a capacity-feasible, socially cohesive matching; its cohesion guarantee holds for either host decision rule ([discrete guarantee](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.07211)). No result settling this exact mass-assignment problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted interests to rational values in [-1,1], following the discrete interest domain.
  • Required at least one real activity and strictly positive rational capacities; capacities may exceed the normalized total mass.
  • Restricted alternative activities in the cohesion condition to real activities; inactivity has unlimited capacity and is not a fullness-tested alternative.
  • Allowed ties without prescribed tie-breaking because cohesion tests only strict improvements.
  • Specified rational output masses and defined L as the total binary input length.
  • Represented types by their activity-interest vectors because peer affinities do not enter social cohesion; imposed no additional individual-rationality constraint or prohibition on negative-interest assignments.
  • Allowed a single support type: the discrete requirement of at least two individuals does not require distinct participant types.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Finding a socially cohesive mass assignment is solvable in polynomial time. A greedy construction suffices.

Construction. Fix any order of the types. For each real activity \(a\), initialize its remaining capacity to \(c_a=\kappa_a\), and initialize all assignment entries to zero.

Process each type \(r\) as follows:

  • Set its unassigned mass to \(u=\mu_r\).
  • List the activities with \(v_r(a)>0\) in decreasing order of interest, breaking ties arbitrarily.
  • For each listed activity \(a\), set \(\delta=\min\{u,c_a\}\), assign \(x_{r,a}=\delta\), and update \(u\leftarrow u-\delta\) and \(c_a\leftarrow c_a-\delta\).
  • Assign the remainder to inactivity: \(x_{r,\theta}=u\).

Activities with nonpositive interest receive no mass from this type.

Feasibility. Each update preserves \(u\geq0\) and \(c_a\geq0\). For a type being processed, the invariant is that its unassigned mass plus its mass already assigned equals \(\mu_r\). Assigning its final remainder to inactivity therefore gives \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{\theta\}}x_{r,a}=\mu_r\).

For every real activity, the invariant is \(c_a=\kappa_a-\sum_s x_{s,a}\), where the sum includes all assignments made so far. Nonnegativity of \(c_a\) consequently proves every capacity constraint. All operations preserve rationality.

Cohesion. Consider any final entry \(x_{r,a}>0\), and any real activity \(b\neq a\) satisfying \(v_r(b)\geq0\) and \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)\). We prove that \(b\) is full.

First suppose \(a\) is a real activity. The construction implies \(v_r(a)>0\), so \(v_r(b)>0\). Thus \(b\) was examined before \(a\) while processing type \(r\).

Because positive mass was subsequently assigned to \(a\), the unassigned mass immediately after examining \(b\) was positive: unassigned mass never increases. Write \(u_b\) and \(c_b\) for the unassigned mass and remaining capacity immediately before examining \(b\). The positive remainder gives
\(u_b-\min\{u_b,c_b\}>0\).
Hence \(c_b<u_b\), and the algorithm assigned exactly \(c_b\) to \(b\), leaving its remaining capacity zero.

Now suppose \(a=\theta\). Since \(v_r(\theta)=0\), the condition on \(b\) implies \(v_r(b)>0\). Moreover, \(x_{r,\theta}>0\) means that unassigned mass remained positive after every positive-interest activity was examined. Applying the same calculation to \(b\) shows that its remaining capacity became zero.

In both cases, later processing only adds assignments and never removes them. An activity whose remaining capacity reaches zero stays full. Therefore \(\sum_s x_{s,b}=\kappa_b\) in the final assignment, proving precisely the required cohesion condition. Arbitrary tie-breaking is valid because the condition concerns only strict improvements.

Polynomial bit complexity. Write the masses and capacities as fractions with positive integer denominators. Let \(D\) be the product of all these denominators. The binary length of \(D\) is at most one plus the sum of their binary lengths, hence is \(O(L)\).

Implement the allocation using integer units of size \(1/D\): initialize each type’s mass to \(D\mu_r\) units and each capacity to \(D\kappa_a\) units. These are integers with \(O(L)\) bits. Every allocation uses an integer minimum and subtraction. Remaining capacities never exceed their initial values, and each assignment entry is at most \(\mu_r\leq1\), so every integer manipulated has \(O(L)\) bits. Output each assigned integer \(z\) as the rational \(z/D\).

There are at most \(\tau m\) allocation steps. Sorting uses \(O(\tau m\log(m+1))\) comparisons; each comparison of rational interests is performed exactly by cross-multiplication on integers with \(O(L)\) bits. Constructing the common denominator and scaled integers also takes polynomial time. Finally, the output has \(\tau(m+1)\) entries, each of polynomial bit length.

Thus the algorithm runs in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), and always returns a socially cohesive rational mass assignment.

Adversarial review (gpt-6.1-sol / medium, Bolzano verifier prompt)

The prover’s claim is correct. The construction produces a feasible, socially cohesive rational assignment and has polynomial bit complexity. No gaps or false statements were found.

  • Construction and domain restrictions. Each type is processed once, and each positive-interest activity is examined once for that type. Thus assigning \(x_{r,a}=\delta\) does not overwrite an earlier allocation to that entry. Entries for nonpositive-interest activities remain zero. The task permits this choice: it requires neither allocating mass to zero-interest activities nor filling available capacity.
  • Nonnegativity and conservation of mass. Initially, \(u=\mu_r>0\) and every remaining capacity is nonnegative. Consequently, \(\delta=\min\{u,c_a\}\) satisfies \(0\leq\delta\leq u\) and \(0\leq\delta\leq c_a\). Both updated quantities remain nonnegative. Each update transfers exactly \(\delta\) from unassigned mass to assigned mass, preserving the stated mass invariant. Allocating the final remainder to inactivity establishes every type’s required equality.
  • Capacity feasibility. The invariant \(c_a=\kappa_a-\sum_s x_{s,a}\) holds initially and is preserved by each update. Since \(c_a\geq0\), every real activity satisfies its capacity constraint. Inactivity has unlimited capacity, so allocating the remainder there is feasible.
  • Cohesion for a real assigned activity. If \(x_{r,a}>0\) for real \(a\), then \(v_r(a)>0\). Any activity \(b\) covered by the cohesion condition therefore has \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)>0\) and appears strictly before \(a\) in the sorted list, independently of tie-breaking.

Positive allocation to \(a\) requires positive unassigned mass immediately before examining \(a\). Since unassigned mass never increases, it was also positive immediately after examining \(b\). The displayed inequality
\[ u_b-\min\{u_b,c_b\}>0 \]
therefore follows. For nonnegative \(u_b,c_b\), this inequality implies \(c_b<u_b\), so the allocation exhausts \(b\)’s remaining capacity. This reasoning also covers the case where \(b\) was already full.

  • Cohesion for inactivity. If \(x_{r,\theta}>0\), the final unassigned remainder is positive. Monotonicity makes the remainder after examining each positive-interest activity positive as well. Every qualifying strict improvement over inactivity has \(v_r(b)>0\), so it was examined and the preceding exhaustion calculation applies. Zero-interest activities do not qualify as strict improvements over inactivity.
  • Persistence and ties. Subsequent processing only decreases remaining capacities. Once an activity’s remaining capacity is zero, further allocations to it are zero, and it stays full. Hence exhaustion during type \(r\)’s processing implies the required final equality. Ties impose no additional obligation because cohesion concerns strict improvements only.
  • Polynomial bit complexity. The product \(D\) of the mass and capacity denominators has binary length \(O(L)\). Every scaled input is an integer because its denominator divides \(D\). Its binary length is \(O(L)\), including capacities exceeding one: multiplying by \(D\) adds at most \(O(L)\) bits to the input numerator’s length.

All subsequent integer quantities remain bounded by their initial scaled capacities or masses. Exact rational-interest comparisons by cross-multiplication also involve integers of \(O(L)\) bits. The stated number of allocation steps and sorting comparisons is polynomial, as are common-denominator construction and output serialization. Reduction of the output fractions is unnecessary.

The argument addresses the exact support-based mass-assignment problem. It relies on no external theorem or discrete guarantee.
