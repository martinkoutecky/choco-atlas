The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given items \(I\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_k\), a directed type-conflict graph \(H\), and fractionally sub-additive valuations \(v_t\) with demand oracles, choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq I}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni k}x_{t,S}\le b_k\). Let \(A(x)=\{t:\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}x_{t,S}>0\}\), and count type \(t\)’s welfare only when \(N_H^+(t)\cap A(x)=\emptyset\). Does there exist an algorithm returning feasible \(x\) with welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/O((\Delta_H\log\log\Delta_H)/\log\Delta_H)\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and the demand-oracle calls?

There are \(m\geq1\) items \(I\), nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supplies \(b_k\), and a loopless directed graph \(H=(T,E)\). Write \(N_H^+(t)=\{u:(t,u)\in E\}\), \(\Delta_H=\max_t|N_H^+(t)|\), and \(D=\max\{4,\Delta_H\}\).
Each type has a monotone fractionally sub-additive valuation \(v_t:2^I\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\), with \(v_t(\emptyset)=0\). An exact demand oracle, at nonnegative rational item prices \(p\), returns any maximizing bundle \(S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq I}(v_t(R)-\sum_{k\in R}p_k)\) and its value.

Result

Yes. The stated mass-allocation model admits a randomized algorithm with the requested guarantee and polynomial bit complexity and demand-oracle complexity.

The crucial additional constraint in the relaxation is that a type’s use of each item must be bounded by its activation variable times that item’s supply. This makes the type’s allocation feasible by itself after amplification. Capacity violations among sampled types can then be resolved by splitting bundle masses.

The proof below uses Gaussian rounding related to the finite-bidder argument of [Cheung et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.09147), but establishes the mass-allocation rounding directly.

Throughout, logarithms are natural.

1. Reduction to allocations with independent active support

Call a subset of types independent if it contains no two endpoints of any directed edge.

For a feasible allocation \(x\), let

\(\displaystyle P(x)=\{t\in A(x):N_H^+(t)\cap A(x)=\emptyset\}.\)

The set \(P(x)\) is independent: if \(t,u\in P(x)\) and \((t,u)\in E\), then \(u\in A(x)\) contradicts the defining condition for \(t\).

Construct \(x'\) by retaining all nonempty-bundle masses of types in \(P(x)\), and assigning every other type entirely to the empty bundle. Then \(x'\) is feasible, its active support is independent, and

\(\displaystyle W(x')=\sum_{t\in P(x)}\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}x_{t,S}v_t(S)=W(x).\)

Consequently, \(\mathrm{OPT}\) equals the maximum ordinary welfare obtainable with independent active support. This maximum exists: there are finitely many independent subsets, and the allocation problem restricted to each subset is a finite, compact linear program.

We make two harmless preprocessing changes.

  • Delete items with zero supply, and replace every remaining supply by \(\min\{b_k,1\}\). Total consumption of any item is at most the total population mass, namely one.
  • Delete types whose valuation on the remaining item set is zero. Monotonicity makes all their remaining bundle values zero, and allocating to such types can only suppress other welfare.

Deleted types will receive empty bundles. Restricting a valuation to the remaining items preserves fractional subadditivity. A restricted demand query is implemented by assigning deleted items a price larger than every valuation value; such a bound is also obtainable from zero-price demand queries on the original item set.

Write \(n\leq\tau\) for the number of remaining types, and retain the original parameter \(D\). If no items or types remain, the empty allocation is optimal. Otherwise all supplies and all full-set values are positive.

Since a positive rational with bit length at most \(L\) is at least \(2^{-L}\), allocating a sufficiently small mass of the full remaining item set to one remaining type shows that

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\geq 2^{-2L}.\)

Indeed, the mass \(\min\{\mu_t,\min_k b_k\}\) is feasible for that type and is at least \(2^{-L}\).

2. An oracle-solvable relaxation

2.1 A single-type allocation function

For a remaining type \(t\), a scalar \(a\in[0,1]\), and nonnegative item capacities \(d=(d_k)_k\), define

\(\displaystyle F_t(a,d)=\max_{y\geq0}\left\{\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}y_Sv_t(S):\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}y_S\leq\mu_ta,\quad \sum_{S\ni k}y_S\leq d_k\ \text{for every }k\right\}.\)

Its dual is

\(\displaystyle F_t(a,d)=\min_{\alpha,p\geq0}\left\{\mu_ta\alpha+\sum_k d_kp_k:\alpha+\sum_{k\in S}p_k\geq v_t(S)\ \text{for every }S\ne\emptyset\right\}.\)

A demand query at prices \(p\) separates the bundle inequalities: it returns a bundle maximizing \(v_t(S)-\sum_{k\in S}p_k\), which is compared with \(\alpha\). If the returned bundle is empty, its utility is zero, and no inequality is violated because \(\alpha\geq0\).

We use the standard rational LP optimization–separation theorem, including primal recovery. Its hypotheses hold here:

  • the dual has only one more variable than the number of remaining items;
  • every inequality has coefficients of polynomial bit length;
  • separation uses nonnegative-price demand queries;
  • both primal and dual optima exist.

Thus \(F_t(a,d)\), an optimal dual solution, and an optimal primal allocation can be obtained with polynomial bit operations and oracle calls. A primal optimum can be chosen with at most \(m+1\) positive bundle variables, by choosing an optimal basic solution.

The dual formula also shows that \(F_t\) is concave. Its hypograph has a strong separation oracle: if \(z>F_t(a,d)\), an optimal dual solution gives the separating inequality

\(\displaystyle z\leq\mu_t\alpha a+\sum_k p_kd_k.\)

These are applications of the rational oracle optimization framework of [Grötschel, Lovász and Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf).

2.2 The finite-dimensional convex program

Introduce activation variables \(a_t\), item-capacity variables \(d_{t,k}\), welfare variables \(z_t\), and a symmetric matrix \(M\) indexed by the types together with an auxiliary index \(0\).

Maximize \(\sum_t z_t\), subject to

\(\displaystyle 0\leq a_t\leq1,\qquad d_{t,k}\geq0,\qquad z_t\geq0,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_t d_{t,k}\leq b_k,\qquad d_{t,k}\leq b_ka_t,\qquad z_t\leq F_t(a_t,d_t),\)

\(\displaystyle M\succeq0,\qquad M_{00}=M_{tt}=1,\qquad M_{0t}=2a_t-1,\)

and, whenever either orientation of an edge joins \(t\) and \(u\),

\(\displaystyle M_{tu}=1-2a_t-2a_u.\)

Denote its optimum by \(Z^*\).

This is a relaxation of the independent-support problem. To check this, take any feasible allocation with independent active support and put \(a_t=1\) on that support and \(a_t=0\) elsewhere. Let \(d_{t,k}\) be its actual item consumption, and let \(z_t\) be its ordinary welfare contribution.

Set \(s_0=1\), \(s_t=2a_t-1\), and \(M=ss^{\mathsf T}\). For an edge, independence gives \(a_ta_u=0\), so

\(\displaystyle M_{tu}=(2a_t-1)(2a_u-1)=1-2a_t-2a_u.\)

Every constraint holds. Therefore

\(\displaystyle Z^*\geq\mathrm{OPT}.\)

2.3 Polynomial-bit optimization

Here the use of convex optimization requires a conditioning check.

Eliminate the matrix equalities, using \(a\), \(d\), \(z\), and the matrix entries corresponding to nonedges as coordinates. The number of coordinates is polynomial in \(m\) and \(n\). All coordinates are bounded: matrix entries have absolute value at most one, \(a_t,d_{t,k}\leq1\), and \(z_t\leq2^L\).

There is also an explicitly known interior point. Put

\(\displaystyle \delta=\frac1{4n},\qquad a_t^0=\delta,\qquad d_{t,k}^0=\frac{\delta b_k}{4n}.\)

Choose \(M^0\) with diagonal entries one, entries \(M^0_{0t}=2\delta-1\), and all distinct type-type entries equal to \(1-4\delta\). Its Schur complement with respect to the auxiliary diagonal entry is

\(\displaystyle 4\delta I-4\delta^2\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\succeq3\delta I.\)

Moreover, \(M^0\) has smallest eigenvalue at least \(1/(4n^2)\). For completeness, writing \(c=2\delta-1\), its quadratic form is at least

\(\displaystyle (x_0+c\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}x)^2+3\delta\|x\|^2.\)

The inequality

\(\displaystyle x_0^2+\|x\|^2\leq2(x_0+c\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}x)^2+(2n+1)\|x\|^2\)

gives the asserted eigenvalue bound.

For each type choose

\(\displaystyle \eta_t=\frac12\min\{\mu_t\delta,\min_k d_{t,k}^0\},\qquad z_t^0=\frac12\eta_tv_t(I).\)

The allocation placing mass \(\eta_t\) on \(I\) proves \(F_t(a_t^0,d_t^0)\geq2z_t^0>0\). Every scalar inequality is strict.

An explicit radius that works in the eliminated coordinates is

\(\displaystyle r=\frac{2^{-4L}}{2^{12}(n+1)^4(m+1)}.\)

To verify this, a coordinate perturbation of norm at most \(r\) changes the matrix by Frobenius norm at most \(5nr\), preserving positive definiteness. It also preserves all scalar capacity inequalities. Finally,

\(\displaystyle \eta_t\geq\frac{2^{-L}}{32n^2},\qquad z_t^0\geq\frac{2^{-2L}}{64n^2},\)

so the same allocation of mass \(\eta_t\) on \(I\) remains feasible throughout the ball and keeps \(z_t<F_t(a_t,d_t)\).

Thus the feasible set has a known outer radius and a known interior radius whose logarithms have polynomial length. Its strong separation oracle combines rational positive-semidefiniteness testing with the single-type LP oracles.

The well-bounded convex optimization theorem consequently returns a rational feasible point satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_tz_t\geq Z^*-2^{-2L-1}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}2.\)

One may use the usual weak-optimization formulation of that theorem and obtain exact feasibility by mixing the returned point with the known interior point: if a point is within distance \(\xi\) of the feasible set, mixing it with the interior point in proportion at least \(\xi/(r+\xi)\) puts it inside the feasible set. Choosing polynomial-bit precision makes the resulting objective loss smaller than the specified tolerance.

At this rational feasible point, recover an optimal single-type plan \(y_{t,S}\) for every \(F_t(a_t,d_t)\). Define

\(\displaystyle V_t=\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}y_{t,S}v_t(S),\qquad \ell_{t,k}=\sum_{S\ni k}y_{t,S}.\)

The plans have polynomial support and satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_Sy_{t,S}\leq\mu_ta_t,\qquad \ell_{t,k}\leq b_ka_t,\qquad \sum_t\ell_{t,k}\leq b_k,\)

and

\(\displaystyle Z:=\sum_tV_t\geq\sum_tz_t\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}2.\)

The remaining task is to round these plans.

3. Fractional subadditivity and capacity trimming

We need the following consequence of fractional subadditivity.

Retention lemma. Suppose \(R\) is a random subset of a fixed bundle \(S\), and \(\Pr(k\in R)\geq\gamma>0\) for every \(k\in S\). Then

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[v_t(R)]\geq\gamma v_t(S).\)

Indeed, give every subset \(Q\subseteq S\) fractional-cover weight \(\Pr(R=Q)/\gamma\). Every item of \(S\) receives total weight at least one. Fractional subadditivity gives the inequality. If the definition restricts cover coefficients to at most one, cap each coefficient at one; this preserves coverage.

We also describe an exact, deterministic trimming operation on bundle masses.

Suppose proposed plans have total item loads \(L_k\). Set

\(\displaystyle r_k=\begin{cases}\min\{1,b_k/L_k\},&L_k>0,\\1,&L_k=0.\end{cases}\)

Replace each proposed bundle \(S\) by the mass distribution of

\(\displaystyle S(\theta)=\{k\in S:\theta\leq r_k\},\qquad \theta\ \text{uniform on }[0,1].\)

This is a description of mass splitting, not an additional sampling step. Sort the finitely many thresholds \(r_k\); their interval lengths determine the output masses exactly.

Every item’s load becomes \(r_kL_k\leq b_k\), and each bundle yields at most \(m+1\) subbundles. All masses are rational when the proposed plans are rational.

4. Rounding when the degree parameter is large

Assume \(D\geq2^{64}\). Choose a rational \(\varepsilon\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \frac{\log\log D}{\log D}\leq\varepsilon\leq2\frac{\log\log D}{\log D}.\)

Such a rational is computable with polynomial precision. In this range, \(\varepsilon<1/4\) and \(\varepsilon D\geq1\).

Partition the types into

\(\displaystyle B_0=\{t:a_t\leq\varepsilon\},\qquad B_1=\{t:\varepsilon<a_t\leq1/2\},\qquad B_2=\{t:a_t>1/2\}.\)

We construct one feasible allocation from each class.

4.1 Low activation: independent sampling and amplification

For each \(t\in B_0\) with \(a_t>0\), independently select \(t\) with a dyadic probability \(\widehat p_t\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \frac{a_t}{8\varepsilon D}\leq\widehat p_t\leq\frac{a_t}{4\varepsilon D}.\)

This is obtained by rounding \(a_t/(4\varepsilon D)\) down to a dyadic rational within a factor of two, using polynomially many bits.

A selected type proposes the amplified plan \(y_{t,S}/a_t\). It respects its population mass and is feasible by itself for every item:

\(\displaystyle \sum_S\frac{y_{t,S}}{a_t}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \frac{\ell_{t,k}}{a_t}\leq b_k.\)

Apply the trimming operation to all proposed plans. Then discard every selected type having a selected out-neighbor. Fill unused population mass with empty bundles. The resulting allocation is feasible and has independent active support.

Fix a type \(t\), and condition on its selection and on the event \(F_t\) that none of its out-neighbors is selected. Since every low-activation selection probability is at most \(1/(4D)\),

\(\displaystyle \Pr(F_t)\geq1-\sum_{u\in N_H^+(t)\cap B_0}\widehat p_u\geq\frac34.\)

Under this conditioning, selections of all other eligible types remain independent with their original probabilities. Let \(O_k\) denote their proposed load of item \(k\). Then

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[O_k\mid t\text{ selected},F_t]\leq\sum_{u\ne t}\frac{\widehat p_u\ell_{u,k}}{a_u}\leq\frac{b_k}{4\varepsilon D}.\)

The type’s own proposed load is at most \(b_k\). Thus its trimming factor satisfies

\(\displaystyle r_k\geq\frac{b_k}{b_k+O_k}.\)

The function \(s\mapsto b_k/(b_k+s)\) is convex, so Jensen’s inequality yields

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[r_k\mid t\text{ selected},F_t]\geq\frac1{1+1/(4\varepsilon D)}\geq\frac12.\)

Apply the retention lemma to each original bundle, averaging over the other selections and over the interval used for mass splitting. Conditional retained welfare is at least \(V_t/(2a_t)\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[W_0]\geq\sum_{t\in B_0}\frac{a_t}{8\varepsilon D}\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac{V_t}{2a_t}=\frac{3}{64\varepsilon D}\sum_{t\in B_0}V_t.\)

Types with \(a_t=0\) have \(V_t=0\) and need not be selected.

4.2 High activation: the types are already independent

Factor \(M\) as a Gram matrix of unit vectors \(w_0,w_t\). Put

\(\displaystyle u_t=\frac{w_0+w_t}{2}.\)

Then \(\|u_t\|^2=a_t\), \(w_0\cdot u_t=a_t\), and an edge implies \(u_t\cdot u_u=0\).

For two adjacent types with positive activation, Bessel’s inequality applied to the orthonormal vectors \(u_t/\sqrt{a_t}\) and \(u_u/\sqrt{a_u}\) gives

\(\displaystyle a_t+a_u\leq\|w_0\|^2=1.\)

Hence \(B_2\) is independent. Retain its original plans and assign all other types empty bundles. This is feasible and has welfare

\(\displaystyle W_2=\sum_{t\in B_2}V_t.\)

4.3 Middle activation: Gaussian selection

For \(t\in B_1\), project \(w_t\) perpendicular to \(w_0\) and normalize:

\(\displaystyle q_t=\frac{w_t-(w_0\cdot w_t)w_0}{\sqrt{1-(w_0\cdot w_t)^2}}.\)

These vectors are well defined. For an edge joining \(t,u\in B_1\),

\(\displaystyle q_t\cdot q_u=-\sqrt{\frac{a_ta_u}{(1-a_t)(1-a_u)}}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.\)

Write \(r_0=\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)\), and set

\(\displaystyle h=\sqrt{2(1-2\varepsilon)(\log D+4)}.\)

Take a standard Gaussian vector \(g\). Select \(t\in B_1\) when \(q_t\cdot g\geq h\), and discard every selected type having a selected out-neighbor. Retain the original plans of the survivors.

We now prove a uniform survival bound. Let \(Q(s)\) denote the standard normal upper-tail probability, and put \(q=Q(h)\). For \(s\geq1\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{e^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,s}e^{-s^2/2}\leq Q(s)\leq\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,s}e^{-s^2/2}.\)

The lower bound follows by integrating the density over the interval from \(s\) to \(s+1/s\); the upper bound follows by integration by parts.

For adjacent types, let \(X=q_t\cdot g\), \(Y=q_u\cdot g\). Their correlation is at most \(-r_0\). Consequently, \(X+Y\) has variance at most \(2(1-r_0)\), and

\(\displaystyle \Pr(X\geq h,Y\geq h)\leq Q\!\left(h\sqrt{\frac2{1-r_0}}\right).\)

Using the tail bounds,

\(\displaystyle \frac{\Pr(X\geq h,Y\geq h)}q\leq\frac{e^{3/2}}{\sqrt2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{h^2(1+r_0)}{2(1-r_0)}\right)=\frac{e^{-5/2}}{\sqrt2\,D}<\frac1{8D}.\)

The equality uses \((1+r_0)/(1-r_0)=1/(1-2\varepsilon)\).

There are at most \(D\) relevant out-neighbors, so a union bound gives

\(\displaystyle \Pr(t\text{ survives})\geq\frac78q.\)

Furthermore, \(h\leq2\sqrt{\log D}\) and \(\varepsilon\log D\geq\log\log D\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle q\geq\frac{e^{-11/2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{(\log D)^{3/2}}D\geq\frac{2^{-16}}{\varepsilon D}.\)

For the last inequality, the constant preceding \((\log D)^{3/2}/D\) exceeds \(2^{-16}\), and \(\varepsilon(\log D)^{3/2}\geq1\) in the stipulated range.

Thus ideal Gaussian rounding gives every middle-activation type survival probability at least

\(\displaystyle \rho=\frac{2^{-17}}{\varepsilon D}.\)

5. Finite-bit Gaussian implementation

The preceding Gaussian construction has a bounded polynomial-bit implementation with survival probability at least \(\rho/2\).

Here are the details needed for that assertion.

A rational positive-semidefinite matrix admits a rational \(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) factorization, with polynomial coefficient bit lengths. Approximating the square roots of its nonnegative diagonal factors gives approximations to the Gram vectors to any specified accuracy in polynomial time. For middle-activation types, the squared projection norm is

\(\displaystyle 1-(2a_t-1)^2=4a_t(1-a_t)\geq2\varepsilon,\)

so normalization does not require excessive precision.

Approximate the unit vectors and the threshold, and generate independent normal coordinates by applying the inverse normal distribution function to finite-bit uniform variables. Couple these variables to genuine uniform variables lying in the same dyadic intervals. After excluding Gaussian tails beyond a bound of order \(\sqrt{\log(n/\rho)}\), polynomially many bits suffice to make every computed inner product differ from its ideal value by at most any prescribed positive rational tolerance.

To quantify the decision error, choose the tolerance

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\frac{\rho}{8n}.\)

Each ideal inner product is standard normal, so

\(\displaystyle \Pr(|q_t\cdot g-h|\leq\kappa)\leq\frac{2\kappa}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.\)

A union bound, together with a Gaussian-tail failure probability at most \(\rho/4\), makes the probability that *any* candidate-selection flag differs from its ideal flag at most \(\rho/2\). Thus every type’s survival probability decreases by at most \(\rho/2\).

All required precision parameters have polynomial bit length. In particular, \(D\leq\max\{4,\tau-1\}\), and \(\log(1/\rho)\) is polynomially bounded. The inverse normal function and the elementary functions used above can be evaluated to this precision by bounded-interval bisection and standard polynomial-bit evaluation.

Regardless of numerical decision errors, pruning is performed exactly on the explicit graph, so every output remains feasible with independent active support. We have proved for the implemented algorithm that

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[W_1]\geq\frac{2^{-18}}{\varepsilon D}\sum_{t\in B_1}V_t.\)

6. Bounded values of the degree parameter

For \(4\leq D<2^{64}\), use the globally feasible plans \(y\) from Section 2 without amplification.

Independently select every type with a dyadic probability \(p\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \frac1{4D}\leq p\leq\frac1{2D}.\)

Discard every selected type having a selected out-neighbor, and retain the original plans of the survivors. Restriction preserves all mass and supply constraints. Each type survives with probability at least

\(\displaystyle p(1-Dp)\geq\frac1{8D}.\)

Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[W]\geq\frac Z{8D}\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{16D}.\)

On the fixed interval from \(4\) to \(2^{64}\), the positive function \(\log D/\log\log D\) is bounded. This is therefore the requested approximation with a universal constant.

7. Combining the bounds and checking output complexity

For large \(D\), construct the three allocations above and return the one with greatest welfare. Their active supports are independent, so their welfare is ordinary bundle welfare.

This comparison uses only polynomially many additional demand queries: to evaluate a bundle \(S\), assign zero prices on \(S\) and sufficiently large prices outside \(S\). Monotonicity ensures that the returned value equals \(v_t(S)\).

Pointwise, the largest of three nonnegative numbers is at least their average. Since \(\varepsilon D\geq1\), all three bounds imply

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[W]\geq\frac{2^{-18}}{3\varepsilon D}\sum_tV_t\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{6\cdot2^{18}\varepsilon D}.\)

Using the upper bound on \(\varepsilon\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[W]\geq\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{(12\cdot2^{18})D\log\log D/\log D}.\)

Combining this with the bounded-\(D\) case gives a universal constant \(C>0\).

Finally, every recovered type plan has at most \(m+1\) positive bundle variables. Trimming replaces each by at most \(m+1\) subbundles, so the output has polynomial support. Its masses have polynomial bit length: amplification, load summation, threshold formation, and interval splitting involve only polynomially many rational operations on polynomial-bit numbers. Unused mass is assigned to empty bundles.

The convex optimization, its demand-oracle LP separation calls, plan recovery, finite-bit sampling, and welfare comparison all use \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations and demand-oracle calls. This proves the requested guarantee for exactly the stated mass-allocation model.
