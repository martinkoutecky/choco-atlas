The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(I\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), their preferences and class memberships, and posts with rational per-capita quotas, compute a feasible allocation with lexicographically maximal aggregate rank vector \((s_1,\ldots,s_r)\), where \(s_k\) is the assigned mass receiving a rank-\(k\) post. For the normalized two-rank target in Theorem 5, ask whether a feasible allocation attains \((1/2,1/2)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(I\) of \(\tau\) applicant types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) posts; each type specifies a preference ordering of its acceptable posts, allowing ties, with ranks given by successive preference tiers and largest rank \(r\).
Each post \(p\) has a positive rational upper quota \(q_p\) and an explicitly listed family \(\mathcal C_p\) of subsets of its acceptable applicant types, each with a nonnegative rational upper quota \(q_{p,C}\). Families may be non-laminar; only posts have classifications.
An allocation consists of divisible assigned masses \(x_{ip}\geq0\), with \(x_{ip}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, satisfying \(\sum_p x_{ip}\leq\mu_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ip}\leq q_p\), and \(\sum_{i\in C}x_{ip}\leq q_{p,C}\) for every \(C\in\mathcal C_p\). Unassigned mass is allowed; each individual receives at most one post, while a type's mass may be split among posts.
Define \(s_k=\sum_{(i,p):\operatorname{rank}_i(p)=k}x_{ip}\). The optimization problem is to compute a feasible allocation with lexicographically maximal \((s_1,\ldots,s_r)\). Let \(c=\sum_p|\mathcal C_p|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including preferences and class memberships; state running times in \(m,\tau,r,c,L\).

Is computing this lexicographically maximal allocation possible in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?
For strict preference lists of length at most two, is deciding whether any feasible allocation attains \((s_1,s_2)=(1/2,1/2)\) in P or NP-hard?

For indivisible applicants, attaining the equal two-rank signature is NP-hard, even with strict lists of length two and classifications only at posts; the reduction establishes exact-signature hardness ([Nasre, Nimbhorkar and Pulath](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.02851v2)).
The later journal version also states NP-hardness with non-laminar classifications and polynomial-time solvability with laminar classifications ([journal article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304397526003087)). No result settling this precise divisible-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made allocation divisibility explicit: a type's mass may be split across acceptable posts, with total assigned mass bounded by its mass.
  • Used the anchor's many-to-one setting: each individual receives at most one post, and only posts have classifications.
  • Interpreted per-capita post and class quotas as bounds on assigned mass in the society normalized to total mass one; imposed only upper quotas and allowed unassigned mass.
  • Specified positive post quotas, nonnegative class quotas, explicitly listed class memberships, and no laminarity requirement.
  • Allowed tied preferences in the general optimization problem, with successive tiers defining ranks; retained strict lists of length at most two for the normalized decision problem.
  • Interpreted attainment of the normalized signature as existence of any feasible allocation with that exact signature, without requiring the witnessing allocation to be rank-maximal.

Result

Both problems are solvable in polynomial time in the binary input model, including when the explicitly listed classification families are non-laminar.

Lexicographic optimization requires at most \(r\) exact rational linear programs. Deciding whether the signature \((1/2,1/2)\) is feasible requires one rational linear-programming feasibility test.

1. The feasible allocations form an explicitly described polytope

Let \(E\) be the set of acceptable type–post pairs, and let \(n=|E|\leq m\tau\). Introduce one real variable \(x_{ip}\) for each \((i,p)\in E\). Unacceptable pairs are assigned zero and need no variables.

Define \(P\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) by the following inequalities:

  • \(x_{ip}\geq 0\) for every \((i,p)\in E\).
  • \(\sum_{p:(i,p)\in E}x_{ip}\leq\mu_i\) for every type \(i\).
  • \(\sum_{i:(i,p)\in E}x_{ip}\leq q_p\) for every post \(p\).
  • \(\sum_{i\in C}x_{ip}\leq q_{p,C}\) for every explicitly listed class \(C\in\mathcal C_p\).

These are exactly the allocation constraints in the question. There are \(n+\tau+m+c\) inequalities, with coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\) and rational right-hand sides. Arbitrary intersections between classes cause no difficulty: each class contributes one linear inequality.

The zero allocation belongs to \(P\), because every upper quota is nonnegative. Moreover, each coordinate satisfies \(0\leq x_{ip}\leq\mu_i\leq 1\). Thus \(P\) is nonempty and compact.

For every rank \(k\), define the linear functional \(s_k(x)=\sum_{(i,p)\in E:\operatorname{rank}_i(p)=k}x_{ip}\). The type constraints and normalization give \(\sum_{k=1}^r s_k(x)\leq\sum_i\mu_i=1\).

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: an explicitly encoded rational LP can be tested for feasibility and, when its optimum is finite and attained, solved exactly in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. This theorem allows equality constraints and lower-dimensional feasible regions. See [the University of Washington notes, Theorem 17.1 and the accompanying bit-complexity discussion](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse521/22au/521-lecture-16.pdf).

2. Exact lexicographic optimization

Set \(P_0=P\). For \(k=1,\ldots,r\), compute

\(v_k=\max\{s_k(x):x\in P_{k-1}\}\),

and define

\(P_k=\{x\in P_{k-1}:s_k(x)=v_k\}\).

Every maximum exists because its feasible set is nonempty and compact. Its maximizing set is again nonempty and compact, so the procedure continues through all ranks. Return an optimizer from the last LP; it belongs to \(P_r\).

To prove correctness, let \(x^\star\in P_r\), and consider any \(y\in P\) whose signature differs from that of \(x^\star\). Let \(k\) be their first differing rank. Then \(s_j(y)=v_j\) for every \(j<k\), so \(y\in P_{k-1}\). By the definition of \(v_k\), we have \(s_k(y)\leq v_k=s_k(x^\star)\). Since these coordinates differ, the inequality is strict. Therefore the signature of \(x^\star\) is lexicographically larger.

This proves that the procedure computes a lexicographically maximal allocation. It remains to check that the exact values inserted into subsequent LPs have polynomial encoding length.

3. Polynomial bit complexity and running time

Assume \(n\geq 1\); if \(n=0\), the only allocation is zero and both questions are immediate.

First, every \(P_k\) is a face of the original polytope \(P\). Here a face has the property that if one of its points is a strict convex combination of two points of \(P\), both endpoints also belong to the face.

Indeed, suppose this holds for \(P_{k-1}\), and write \(x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\in P_k\), where \(y,z\in P\) and \(0<\lambda<1\). Since \(x\in P_{k-1}\), both endpoints belong to \(P_{k-1}\). Consequently \(s_k(y)\leq v_k\) and \(s_k(z)\leq v_k\). Their strict convex combination equals \(s_k(x)=v_k\), forcing both values to equal \(v_k\). Thus both endpoints belong to \(P_k\).

Every nonempty compact polytope has a vertex, and a vertex of a face is a vertex of the original polytope. Hence, for each \(k\), some vertex \(z\) of \(P\) belongs to \(P_k\) and satisfies \(s_k(z)=v_k\).

We now bound the encoding length of these vertex signatures uniformly.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all original masses and quotas, written in lowest terms. Since their encodings are included in \(L\), we have \(\log_2 D\leq L\).

At a vertex \(z\), the active constraint rows span \(\mathbb R^n\). Otherwise, a nonzero vector orthogonal to all active rows would permit sufficiently small feasible movements in both directions, contradicting that \(z\) is a vertex. Select \(n\) independent active rows to obtain \(Hz=b'\), where \(H\) is an invertible integer matrix with entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).

Because \(Db'\) is integral, Cramer’s rule gives \(D\det(H)z\in\mathbb Z^n\). Hadamard’s inequality gives \(1\leq|\det H|\leq n^{n/2}\). Therefore, with \(Q=D|\det H|\), every coordinate of \(Qz\), and hence every \(Qs_k(z)\), is integral.

Since \(0\leq s_k(z)\leq 1\), both numerator and denominator of \(s_k(z)\) have encoding length \(O(L+n\log(n+1))\). This same bound applies to every \(v_k\), independently of the stage number.

Each stage LP consequently has:

  • \(n\leq m\tau\) variables;
  • at most \(n+\tau+m+c+2(r-1)\) inequalities, replacing each earlier rank equality by two inequalities;
  • total dense encoding length \(O\bigl(L+n(n+\tau+m+c+r)+r[L+n\log(n+1)]\bigr)\).

For an explicit polynomial running-time bound in the requested parameters, put \(N=m\tau\) and

\(S=1+L+N(N+\tau+m+c+r)+r\bigl[L+N\log_2(N+1)\bigr]+\tau+m+c+r\).

For some absolute constant \(d\), a fixed exact polynomial-time LP algorithm implements the entire procedure in \(O((r+1)S^d)\) bit operations, including construction and output. In particular, the running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,r,c,L\). The output allocation is rational and has polynomial encoding length.

4. Feasibility of the equal two-rank signature

With strict preference lists of length at most two, append the two equations

\(\sum_{(i,p)\in E:\operatorname{rank}_i(p)=1}x_{ip}=1/2\),

\(\sum_{(i,p)\in E:\operatorname{rank}_i(p)=2}x_{ip}=1/2\)

to the original allocation constraints. An empty sum is interpreted as zero.

The resulting rational system is feasible if and only if a feasible allocation attains the requested signature. Thus a single exact LP feasibility test decides the question.

There are at most \(2\tau\) variables and at most \(3\tau+m+c+4\) inequalities after replacing the two equalities by four inequalities. Its dense encoding length is \(O(L+\tau(\tau+m+c)+\tau+m+c+1)\). Therefore, for some absolute constant \(d\), the decision procedure takes

\(O\bigl((1+L+\tau(\tau+m+c)+\tau+m+c)^d\bigr)\)

bit operations. It can also return an allocation witnessing feasibility.

The equalities force full assignment: total assigned mass equals \(1\), while the sum of all type upper bounds also equals \(1\). The nonnegative deficits \(\mu_i-\sum_p x_{ip}\) therefore sum to zero, so every deficit is zero.

5. Why indivisible hardness does not transfer

The [Nasre–Nimbhorkar–Pulath reduction, Theorem 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.02851v2), concerns indivisible matchings attaining an exact two-rank signature. Its discrete choice restrictions are absent from the divisible model.

Here each class constraint is already a linear inequality in continuous assigned masses. Neither lexicographic optimization nor exact-signature feasibility requires an integral solution or a laminar classification family. Consequently, both stated divisible-mass problems are polynomial-time solvable.
