The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates partitioned into groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), parameters \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) of support size \(\tau\) over complete rankings \(t\) of the candidates, find a top-\(k\) ranking \(\rho\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\left(\sum_{a\in D_\rho}|t(a)-\rho(a)|+\sum_{a\notin D_\rho}|t(a)-(k+1)|\right)\), subject to \(\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\le |D_\rho\cap G_i|\le\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\) for every group \(G_i\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\ge 1\) candidates partitioned into nonempty groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\). Input parameters are binary rationals \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\in[0,1]\) and an integer \(1\le k\le m\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(t\) of \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(t(a)\) denotes the position of candidate \(a\).
A top-\(k\) ranking is a bijection \(\rho:D_\rho\to\{1,\ldots,k\}\), where \(D_\rho\subseteq C\). It is feasible when \(\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\le |D_\rho\cap G_i|\le\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\) for every \(i\). Assume at least one feasible ranking exists.
Minimize \(\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\bigl(\sum_{a\in D_\rho}|t(a)-\rho(a)|+\sum_{a\in C\setminus D_\rho}|t(a)-(k+1)|\bigr)\) over feasible rankings; any minimizer is acceptable.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,g,k\).

Is exact optimization of this distributional fair top-\(k\) rank aggregation problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For an unweighted finite set of strict complete input rankings, exact optimization with these group constraints and this distance is polynomial-time solvable.
The supplied result does not state a separate complexity classification for arbitrary binary-rational masses.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable, including arbitrary positive binary-rational masses. A reduction to integer min-cost flow gives an exact algorithm with polynomial bit complexity.

  • Clearing denominators and constructing assignment costs.

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), and define
\[ Q=\prod_t q_t,\qquad w_t=Q\mu_t. \]
The \(w_t\) are positive integers satisfying \(\sum_t w_t=Q\). The binary length of \(Q\) is at most \(L+1\), so clearing denominators does not cause an exponential increase in bit length.

For candidate \(a\) and position \(j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), define the integer cost
\[ c_{a,j}=\sum_t w_t\bigl(|t(a)-j|-|t(a)-(k+1)|+k\bigr). \]
The reverse triangle inequality gives
\[ -k\le |t(a)-j|-|t(a)-(k+1)|\le k. \]
Consequently, \(0\le c_{a,j}\le 2kQ\).

Let \(F(\rho)\) denote the objective in the question, and set
\[ A=\sum_{a\in C}\sum_t w_t|t(a)-(k+1)|. \]
For every top-\(k\) ranking, expanding the contributions of selected and unselected candidates gives
\[ QF(\rho)=A+\sum_{a\in D_\rho}c_{a,\rho(a)}-k^2Q. \]
Thus minimizing \(F\) is equivalent to minimizing the sum of the assignment costs.

  • A flow network that enforces the group quotas.

Define
\[ \ell_i=\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor,\qquad u_i=\min\{|G_i|,\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\}. \]
The assumed existence of a feasible ranking implies \(\ell_i\le u_i\) and \(\sum_i\ell_i\le k\).

Set \(R=2k^2Q\) and \(M=R+1\). Construct a directed network with a source \(s\), one vertex for each group, one vertex for each candidate, one vertex for each position \(1,\ldots,k\), and a sink \(z\). Its arcs are:

| Arc | Capacity | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(s\to G_i\), first parallel arc | \(\ell_i\) | \(0\) |
| \(s\to G_i\), second parallel arc | \(u_i-\ell_i\) | \(M\) |
| \(G_i\to a\), for \(a\in G_i\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |
| \(a\to j\), for every candidate and position | \(1\) | \(c_{a,j}\) |
| \(j\to z\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |

Zero-capacity arcs can be omitted. We seek a minimum-cost integral flow of value \(k\).

Every integral flow of value \(k\) selects exactly \(k\) distinct candidates and assigns exactly one candidate to each position. Indeed, candidate incoming capacities are \(1\), and the \(k\) position-to-sink arcs each have capacity \(1\). The selected count from group \(i\) is at most \(u_i\).

We now prove that every minimum-cost integral flow also satisfies the lower quotas. Put \(S=\sum_i\ell_i\), and let \(H\) be its total flow through the source arcs of cost \(M\). Since the zero-cost source arcs have total capacity \(S\),
\[ H\ge k-S. \]
Equality holds precisely when every zero-cost source arc is saturated.

A feasible ranking induces a flow saturating all these arcs: send \(\ell_i\) units through the first source arc for group \(i\), and its remaining selected candidates through the second. Its cost is at most
\[ (k-S)M+R, \]
because its \(k\) assignment costs sum to at most \(R\).

Any integral flow failing to saturate a zero-cost source arc has \(H\ge k-S+1\), and hence cost at least
\[ (k-S+1)M>(k-S)M+R. \]
Therefore every minimum-cost integral flow saturates all zero-cost source arcs. Its group counts satisfy both quotas.

Finally, all flows saturating those arcs pay the same source cost \((k-S)M\). Their remaining costs are exactly the assignment-cost sums from step 1. Thus a minimum-cost integral flow yields an optimal feasible ranking.

  • An exact flow algorithm and its correctness.

For completeness, this network can be solved using \(k\) successive shortest-path augmentations, with Bellman–Ford applied to each residual network.

Start with the zero flow. At each iteration, send one unit along a shortest residual path from \(s\) to \(z\). Capacities remain integral.

The following argument proves correctness, including when reverse residual arcs have negative costs. Suppose the current flow \(f\) is minimum-cost among integral flows of value \(r\). Its residual network has no negative-cost directed cycle: augmenting one unit around such a cycle would produce a cheaper integral flow of the same value.

For any integral flow \(h\) of value \(r+1\), represent \(h-f\) by forward and reverse residual arcs. This integral residual flow decomposes into one \(s\)-to-\(z\) path and directed cycles. Every cycle has nonnegative cost, so
\[ \operatorname{cost}(h)-\operatorname{cost}(f) \ge \operatorname{dist}_f(s,z). \]
Augmenting along a shortest residual path attains this bound. It therefore produces a minimum-cost integral flow of value \(r+1\).

The zero flow is optimal at value \(0\), since all original costs are nonnegative. A value-\(k\) flow exists by feasibility; retaining any number of its unit paths shows that every intermediate value is feasible. Induction proves that the algorithm reaches an optimal integral flow of value \(k\).

  • Running time and bit complexity.

The network has
\[ V=m+g+k+2,\qquad E\le mk+m+k+2g. \]
Define
\[ B=L+\left\lceil\log_2(2+m+\tau+g+k)\right\rceil. \]
All integer weights, costs, capacities, and finite shortest-path distances have binary length \(O(B)\). In particular, \(M=2k^2Q+1\) has polynomial binary length.

Computing the quotas and integer weights takes \(O((g+\tau)B^2)\) bit operations using elementary integer arithmetic. Directly computing all assignment costs takes \(O(m\tau k B^2)\). Each Bellman–Ford run uses \(O(VE)\) arithmetic operations, and there are \(k\) augmentations.

A valid overall bound is therefore
\[ O\bigl(L+(\tau+g+m\tau k+kVE)B^2\bigr). \]
Since \(g\le m\) and \(k\le m\), this is also
\[ O\bigl(L+(m\tau k+m^2k^2)B^2\bigr). \]
These are polynomial bounds in the requested input parameters. The algorithm operates directly on the support and its rational masses; it does not expand the society into \(Q\) individual rankings.

Adversarial review (gpt-6.1-sol / medium, Bolzano verifier prompt)

The prover’s claim is correct: exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary positive binary-rational masses. The reduction, quota enforcement, flow algorithm, and bit-complexity bounds all stand. No major or minor issues were found.

  • Clearing denominators and assignment costs

Each denominator \(q_t\) is a positive integer. Thus \(Q=\prod_t q_t\) is divisible by every denominator, and \(w_t=Qp_t/q_t\) is a positive integer. The normalization of the masses gives \(\sum_t w_t=Q\).

The bit-length assertion is valid: \(\log_2 Q=\sum_t\log_2 q_t\), and the denominator encodings contribute at least this many bits to \(L\). Although \(Q\) can have exponentially large numerical value, its binary representation has polynomial length.

The cost bound is correct. For every permitted position \(j\), the reverse triangle inequality gives
\(\bigl||t(a)-j|-|t(a)-(k+1)|\bigr|\le k+1-j\le k\).
Consequently, each summand defining \(c_{a,j}\) lies between \(0\) and \(2kw_t\), so \(0\le c_{a,j}\le 2kQ\).

The objective identity is also correct. Starting from the baseline \(A\), selecting a candidate replaces its unselected contribution by its assigned-position contribution. The added shift in each assignment cost is \(kQ\); exactly \(k\) candidates are selected, giving the total shift \(k^2Q\). Therefore
\(QF(\rho)=A+\sum_{a\in D_\rho}c_{a,\rho(a)}-k^2Q\).
Both omitted terms are independent of the ranking, and \(Q>0\), so the minimizers coincide.

  • Network representation and upper quotas

Replacing the stated upper quota by \(u_i=\min\{|G_i|,\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\}\) preserves feasibility because no ranking can select more than \(|G_i|\) members of a group.

The feasibility assumption justifies \(\ell_i\le u_i\) and \(S=\sum_i\ell_i\le k\). In particular, every source-arc capacity is nonnegative.

The network correctly represents candidate assignments. Each candidate has incoming capacity \(1\), preventing repeated selection. A flow of value \(k\) saturates all \(k\) position-to-sink arcs, each of capacity \(1\). Integrality therefore ensures exactly one candidate is assigned to each position. The two source arcs for group \(i\) have combined capacity \(u_i\), enforcing the upper quota.

  • Lower quotas and preservation of the objective

The penalty argument is valid, including its constants.

Every value-\(k\) flow sends \(k-H\) units through the zero-cost source arcs. Their combined capacity is \(S\), so \(H\ge k-S\). Equality holds exactly when all those arcs are saturated: their individual unused capacities are nonnegative and must sum to zero.

Every feasible ranking admits the proposed routing, with \(\ell_i\) units on the first source arc and the remaining selected count on the second. Its assignment costs sum to at most \(k(2kQ)=R\), giving total cost at most \((k-S)M+R\).

For an integral flow that leaves a zero-cost source arc unsaturated, the total unused capacity is a positive integer. Hence \(H\ge k-S+1\). All other original costs are nonnegative, so its total cost is at least \((k-S+1)M\). Since \(M=R+1\), this is strictly larger than the feasible comparison flow’s bound.

Thus every minimum-cost integral flow saturates the first source arcs and satisfies every lower quota. All such flows pay the same source cost. Their remaining costs are precisely the assignment costs, so an optimal integral flow produces an optimal feasible ranking.

  • Successive shortest-path correctness

The induction argument is sound.

The zero flow is optimal among integral flows of value zero because all original arc costs are nonnegative.

If an integral flow \(f\) is optimal at value \(r\), its residual network cannot contain a negative-cost directed cycle. Every residual arc on such a cycle has positive integral capacity, so augmenting one unit around it would preserve feasibility and flow value while strictly decreasing cost.

For an integral comparison flow \(h\) of value \(r+1\), the difference from \(f\) can be represented within residual capacities. Its divergence is one unit at the source, minus one unit at the sink, and zero elsewhere. The stated decomposition into one source-to-sink path and directed cycles therefore applies. Residual cycles have nonnegative cost, and the path costs at least the shortest-path distance.

Augmenting one unit along a shortest residual path attains that lower bound. The augmentation is feasible because its residual capacities are positive integers. It produces an optimal integral flow of value \(r+1\).

The feasibility assumption supplies a value-\(k\) flow and, by retaining its unit paths, flows of every intermediate value. Comparing such a flow with the current flow also ensures the required residual augmenting path exists. Bellman–Ford is applicable because the induction establishes the absence of negative residual cycles. Exactly \(k\) augmentations suffice.

  • Running time and bit complexity

The counts \(V=m+g+k+2\) and \(E\le mk+m+k+2g\) are correct. Residual networks have at most twice as many arcs, which does not change the asymptotic bound.

The integers \(Q\), \(w_t\), \(c_{a,j}\), and \(M=2k^2Q+1\) have \(O(B)\) bits. Capacities are at most \(k\). Since a shortest residual path can be chosen without repeated vertices when there are no negative cycles, finite shortest-path distances also have \(O(B)\) bits.

The preprocessing bounds are valid with elementary integer arithmetic. Constructing \(Q\), obtaining the weights, and computing quotas fit within \(O((g+\tau)B^2)\) bit operations. Directly evaluating the assignment costs fits within \(O(m\tau kB^2)\).

Bellman–Ford takes \(O(VE)\) arithmetic operations per augmentation. Charging \(O(B^2)\) per operation is conservative and valid. Thus the overall bound
\(O\bigl(L+(\tau+g+m\tau k+kVE)B^2\bigr)\)
is justified.

Because the groups are nonempty, \(g\le m\); also \(k\le m\). These imply \(V=O(m)\), \(E=O(mk)\), and \(kVE=O(m^2k^2)\). The simplified bound
\(O\bigl(L+(m\tau k+m^2k^2)B^2\bigr)\)
follows.

The argument handles arbitrary positive binary-rational masses directly, without expanding them into repeated rankings. It permits any minimizer, as requested.

Zero lower quotas, zero-capacity arcs, fixed group counts, \(k=1\), \(k=m\), and \(m=1\) cause no exception to the proof. The feasibility assumption supplies all required existence conditions.
