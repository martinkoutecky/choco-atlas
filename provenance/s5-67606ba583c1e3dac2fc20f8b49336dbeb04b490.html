The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(H\), rational capacities \(q_h\) with \(\sum_{h\in H}q_h=1\), a finite complete type set \(T\), rational mass distribution \(\mu\), public priority orders over types, and strict school rankings for each type, define weighted continuum TTC by transferring maximal feasible residual mass around cycles to obtain \(x_{t,h}\). Determine, as a function of \(m=|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and encoding length \(L\), the minimum worst-case certificate length \(\log_2|\mathcal C|\) allowing every type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\) to recover \(x_{t,\cdot}/\mu_t\) from its own ranking and a certificate \(C\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be \(m\geq2\) schools with public positive binary-rational capacities \(q_h\) satisfying \(\sum_hq_h=1\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Type labels and each school's strict total priority order over them are public; each type has a private strict complete school ranking \(P_t\). Distinct labels may have identical rankings. Consider \(\tau\geq m^a\) for any fixed \(a>1\).
Weighted TTC starts with residual type masses \(\mu_t\), residual capacities \(q_h\), and \(x_{t,h}=0\). Each positive-residual type points to its favorite positive-capacity school, and each positive-capacity school points to its highest-priority positive-residual type. Simultaneously clear every directed cycle: transfer the minimum residual mass or capacity among its vertices, assigning that amount to each type at the school it points to and subtracting it from every vertex's residual. Delete zero-residual vertices and repeat.
For fixed public \((H,T,q,Q)\), a representation protocol consists of a finite certificate alphabet \(\mathcal C\) and deterministic decoders \(D_t(P_t,C)\). For every mass distribution and ranking profile of total binary encoding length at most \(L\), one common certificate must satisfy \(D_t(P_t,C)=(x_{t,h}/\mu_t)_{h\in H}\) for every \(t\). The encoder sees the entire input; decoders know their labels and the public data, but no masses or other types' rankings. Protocols must cover all these inputs; decoding is exact and computationally unrestricted. Let \(B(m,\tau,L)\) be the maximum, over admissible public data, of the minimum protocol cost \(\log_2|\mathcal C|\).

What is the asymptotically tight worst-case certificate length \(B(m,\tau,L)\) for weighted TTC? State any algorithmic running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and the fixed regime parameter \(a\).

For discrete TTC with public priorities, concurrent representation requires \(\widetilde\Theta(\min\{n,m^2\})\) bits for \(n\) applicants and \(m\) schools ([Gonczarowski–Thomas](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.08709v3)).
Continuum TTC has a school-pair cutoff characterization under density regularity assumptions; this does not establish optimal certificate length for finitely supported rational masses and type-level priorities ([Leshno–Lo](https://jleshno.weebly.com/uploads/3/7/4/6/37467639/leshno_lo-ttc_cutoffs.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one and required every listed mass and school capacity to be strictly positive.
  • Interpreted completeness as strict complete school rankings, with all schools acceptable, rather than enumeration of all possible types.
  • Used persistent type labels for public priorities and decoder identities; rankings remain private, and different labels may share a ranking.
  • Made capacities, support labels, and priorities public; masses are known to the encoder but not supplied to decoders, following the question's restriction to own ranking and certificate.
  • Defined maximal cycle transfers explicitly and cleared disjoint cycles simultaneously to remove cycle-selection ambiguity.
  • Required one fixed protocol to cover every admissible private input, then maximized its minimum cost over public environments; interpreted L as an upper bound on total input encoding length.
  • Carried over the many-more-participants regime using support size: tau is at least m raised to any fixed exponent a greater than one.

Partial progress: a compressed simulation certificate

The bounds below substantially improve the upper bound in NOTES. They also give matching bounds in a wider budget regime, including some budgets dominated by the space needed to store rankings. They do not determine the optimal certificate length for every feasible encoding budget.

1. Encoding convention and proved bounds

I use the convention in NOTES: rankings are explicit lists, and rational masses are represented by binary numerators and denominators. More precisely, store every ranking as $m$ school indices, each using $\lceil\log_2 m\rceil$ bits, and use a fixed self-delimiting encoding for the mass tuple. Put
$$ R=\tau m\lceil\log_2 m\rceil, \qquad M=L-R. $$
Thus $M$ is the budget available for encoding masses. Public capacities are excluded from $L$, as in NOTES.

For every feasible budget, I prove
$$ B(m,\tau,L) \leq \min\left\{ L+1,\; M+1+\bigl(\tau+m(m-1)\bigr)\log_2 m \right\}. $$

There are encoding-dependent constants $c,A,C>0$ such that
$$ B(m,\tau,L) \geq c\bigl(M-A\tau\log_2\tau\bigr)_+-C, $$
where $z_+=\max\{z,0\}$.

Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ B(m,\tau,L)=\Theta(M) \quad\text{if}\quad M\geq C_0\bigl(\tau\log_2\tau+m^2\log_2 m\bigr) } $$
for a sufficiently large encoding-dependent constant $C_0$.

In particular, if $\tau\geq m^2$, this becomes
$$ \boxed{ B(m,\tau,L)=\Theta(M) \quad\text{if}\quad M\geq C_1\tau\log_2\tau. } $$

I also prove a separate lower bound that isolates the information carried by other types’ rankings. For suitably chosen fixed masses, whose total encoding length is $O(\tau\log_2\tau)$, certificates require
$$ \Omega(\tau\log_2 m) $$
bits when $\tau\geq 2m+4$. This lower bound remains valid even if the decoders are additionally given all masses. With two schools, the corresponding bound is $\Omega(\tau)$.

The remaining sections prove these statements from the premises.

2. Clearing cycles one at a time gives the same outcome

Write $r_t$ and $c_h$ for residual masses and capacities. A state also includes the accumulated allocation matrix.

A directed cycle contains equally many types and schools. Clearing it by an amount $\delta$ therefore decreases total residual mass and total residual capacity by the same amount. Hence
$$ \sum_t r_t=\sum_h c_h $$
is preserved.

If this common total is positive, both classes of vertices are nonempty. Every remaining vertex has an outgoing edge, so the finite directed graph contains a cycle. Clearing a cycle exhausts at least one vertex. Thus any sequence of individual cycle clearings terminates after at most $\tau+m$ clearings, and its terminal state has zero residual mass and capacity.

We next prove that the terminal allocation is independent of the order of individual clearings.

Lemma 1: current cycles commute

Suppose two different cycles are present in one state. They are vertex-disjoint because every vertex has one outgoing edge.

After either cycle is cleared, the other remains a cycle:

  • Its type vertices and their chosen schools retain their residual quantities. Since their favorite available schools remain available, their outgoing edges remain unchanged.
  • Its school vertices and their chosen types also retain their residual quantities. A chosen type remains highest priority among remaining types: removing other types cannot displace a still-active highest-priority type.

The transfer amount on the second cycle is unchanged. Clearing both cycles in either order therefore gives the same residual quantities and accumulated allocations.

Lemma 2: the terminal allocation is unique

Use induction on the number of positive-residual vertices.

If two clearing sequences choose the same first cycle, apply the induction hypothesis to the resulting state. If they choose different first cycles, Lemma 1 gives a common state reachable by clearing those two cycles in either order. Every resulting state has fewer positive-residual vertices. The induction hypothesis implies that both sequences have the same terminal state.

This proves uniqueness.

The cycles cleared in a simultaneous TTC round are vertex-disjoint. By Lemma 1, that round can be performed as a sequence of individual clearings with exactly the same result. Therefore the unique terminal allocation just proved is precisely the weighted TTC allocation specified in the question.

3. A certificate consisting of masses and school choices

The new upper bound uses a deterministic simulation in which some type pointers are temporarily unknown.

The simulator knows the initial masses from the certificate. It maintains residual quantities, allocations, and a set of known pointers. A known pointer records a type’s favorite currently available school. Initially, all type pointers are unknown.

At each iteration:

  • Every active school points to its highest-priority active type, determined from public priorities and current residuals.
  • If these school pointers and the known type pointers contain a directed cycle, clear one such cycle using a fixed deterministic rule.
  • Delete exhausted vertices. Erase a surviving type’s known pointer if its chosen school was deleted.
  • If no fully specified cycle exists, choose the first active school, under a fixed ordering, whose pointed-to type has an unknown pointer. Read one school index from the certificate and use it as that type’s pointer.

The encoder supplies the type’s actual favorite available school at step 4.

There is always a school eligible for step 4 when no fully specified cycle exists. Otherwise, every school’s pointed-to type would have a known pointer. Following school-to-type-to-school edges would then give a directed cycle in the finite set of active schools.

Known pointers remain correct until their chosen schools disappear. Consequently, every cycle cleared by this simulation is a genuine current TTC cycle. Lemma 2 proves that its final allocation is correct.

It remains to bound the number of school indices required.

Lemma 3: at most $m$ previously queried types remain active

When a type is queried for the first time, assign it a permanent sponsor: the school selected in step 4.

While that school and type both remain active, the sponsor continues pointing to that type. The type was highest priority when selected, and subsequently only types are removed.

Two currently active, previously queried types cannot have the same sponsor. A school cannot first sponsor another type while its earlier sponsored type remains active. Once the school is deleted, it cannot sponsor any new type.

Thus sponsorship is injective among active, previously queried types. There are at most $m$ such types.

Lemma 4: at most $\tau+m(m-1)$ queries occur

There are at most $\tau$ first queries.

Every later query of a type follows erasure of an earlier pointer, which occurs only when its chosen school is deleted while the type survives. At any school-deletion step, Lemma 3 bounds the number of surviving, previously queried types by $m$. Thus at most $m$ pointers are erased at that step.

There are at most $m-1$ school-deletion steps leaving positive residual capacity. A deletion leaving no capacity also leaves no residual type mass, by residual-total equality, so it causes no later query.

Each subsequent query can be charged to a different preceding erasure. Therefore the total number of queries is at most
$$ J=\tau+m(m-1). $$

Certificate size

Use a certificate alphabet consisting of:

  • a mass-encoding string of length at most $M$;
  • a stream of exactly $J$ school indices, padding unused positions arbitrarily.

There are fewer than $2^{M+1}$ possible mass strings and $m^J$ possible streams. Hence
$$ \log_2|\mathcal C| < M+1+J\log_2 m. $$

Every decoder performs the same simulation and returns its own row normalized by the initial mass supplied in the certificate. The decoder may ignore its private ranking; this is permitted by the representation definition.

Malformed certificates can be assigned an arbitrary deterministic output. Representation requires only that every admissible input have one correct certificate.

Sending the entire private input separately gives an alphabet of size $2^{L+1}-1$. Combining the two protocols proves
$$ B(m,\tau,L) \leq \min\left\{ L+1,\; M+1+\bigl(\tau+m(m-1)\bigr)\log_2 m \right\}. $$

Corollary: sparse allocation support

Every positive allocation occurs along a revealed pointer. A type’s favorite available school changes only when that school is deleted, so the type cannot revisit an earlier allocation school.

Lemma 4 therefore gives
$$ \sum_t|\{h:x_{t,h}>0\}| \leq \tau+m(m-1). $$
Every type is fully assigned, so
$$ \sum_t\left(|\{h:x_{t,h}>0\}|-1\right) \leq m(m-1). $$
In particular, at most $m(m-1)$ types receive positive allocations at multiple schools.

4. A precision lower bound using the numerical budget

This repairs the budget accounting in the previous precision construction by separating the fixed ranking cost $R$.

All schools have the same priority order, starting with a distinguished type $t_*$, followed by a type $s$, followed by the other types. Every ranking is
$$ h_1\succ h_2\succ\cdots\succ h_m. $$

Set
$$ q_{h_1}=\frac14. $$
For $m=2$, set $q_{h_2}=3/4$. For $m\geq3$, set $q_{h_2}=1/2$ and divide the remaining capacity $1/4$ positively among the other schools.

The $\tau-2$ filler types have fixed positive masses summing to $1/4$, with total encoding length $O(\tau\log_2\tau)$. Explicitly, with
$$ r=\tau-2, \qquad N=2^{\lceil\log_2r\rceil}, $$
give the first $r-1$ fillers mass $1/(4N)$ and the last mass $(N-r+1)/(4N)$.

For $k\geq3$, vary
$$ \mu_{t_*}=y_j=\frac14+\frac{j}{2^k}, \qquad \mu_s=\frac34-y_j, \qquad 1\leq j\leq2^{k-2}-1. $$
All masses are positive and sum to one.

Identical school priorities imply that the highest-priority active type is served until exhausted: every school points to it, so its pointer and that school form the only cycle. In particular, $t_*$ first receives $1/4$ at $h_1$, exhausting that school. Its remaining mass satisfies
$$ 0<y_j-\frac14<\frac14<q_{h_2}, $$
so it then finishes at $h_2$. Its normalized allocation at $h_1$ is
$$ \frac{x_{t_*,h_1}}{\mu_{t_*}} = \frac{1}{4y_j}. $$

These values are distinct, while the ranking supplied to $D_{t_*}$ is fixed. Different family members must therefore use different certificates. Thus
$$ \log_2|\mathcal C| \geq \log_2(2^{k-2}-1) \geq k-3. $$

The rankings use exactly $R$ bits. For encoding-dependent constants $A,E$, the mass tuple uses at most
$$ A\tau\log_2\tau+Ek $$
bits. Choosing the largest admissible $k$, and using nonnegativity when this construction does not fit, proves
$$ B(m,\tau,L) \geq \frac1E\bigl(M-A\tau\log_2\tau\bigr)_+-4. $$

Together with the upper bound, this establishes the matching bounds stated in Section 1.

5. Fixed masses can force many bits about rankings

The next construction shows that exact fractional outputs can encode many other types’ preferences even when all masses are fixed and publicly supplied.

5.1 Small distinct primes

We first justify the numerical ingredients without assuming a prime-distribution theorem.

For every positive integer $r$, there are $r$ distinct primes at least $5$, each bounded by $64(r+2)^2$.

To see this, choose
$$ N=2^{2\lceil\log_2(r+2)\rceil+4}. $$
Then
$$ 16(r+2)^2\leq N<64(r+2)^2. $$
The central binomial coefficient satisfies
$$ \binom{N}{N/2}\geq\frac{2^N}{N+1}. $$
For any prime $p$, its exponent in this binomial coefficient is
$$ \sum_{j\geq1} \left( \left\lfloor\frac{N}{p^j}\right\rfloor - 2\left\lfloor\frac{N/2}{p^j}\right\rfloor \right). $$
Each summand is zero or one. Hence the exponent is at most $\lfloor\log_pN\rfloor$, and that prime’s contribution is at most $N$. Writing $\pi(N)$ for the number of primes at most $N$ gives
$$ \binom{N}{N/2}\leq N^{\pi(N)}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \pi(N) \geq \frac{N-\log_2(N+1)}{\log_2N} \geq \frac{N}{2\log_2N}. $$
Since $\log_2N\leq2(r+2)+6$, the last expression is at least $r+2$. Removing primes $2$ and $3$ leaves the required $r$ primes.

5.2 Construction for $m\geq3$

Suppose $\tau\geq2m+4$. Put
$$ s=m-1, \qquad r=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau-m}{2}\right\rfloor, \qquad f=\tau-s-2r. $$
Then $f\in\{1,2\}$ and $r\geq\tau/4$.

Use small schools $h_1,\ldots,h_s$ and a fallback school $h_0$, with capacities
$$ q_{h_j}=\frac{1}{4s}, \qquad q_{h_0}=\frac34. $$

Choose distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ as above. The types are:

  • selectors $u_1,\ldots,u_r$, with masses

$$ \mu_{u_i}=\frac{1}{4rs p_i}; $$

  • partners $v_1,\ldots,v_r$, with masses

$$ \mu_{v_i}=\frac{sp_i-1}{4rs p_i}; $$

  • probes $w_1,\ldots,w_s$, each with mass $1/(2s)$;
  • $f$ fillers, each with mass $1/(4f)$.

Each selector-partner pair has total mass $1/(4r)$. The pair masses, probe masses, and filler masses total $1/4$, $1/2$, and $1/4$, respectively.

Every school has the same strict priority order: selectors first, then probes, then partners and fillers. Fix all rankings except those of the selectors. Probe $w_j$ ranks $h_j$ first and $h_0$ second.

For each function
$$ g:\{1,\ldots,r\}\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,s\}, $$
selector $u_i$ ranks $h_{g(i)}$ first. Complete every ranking strictly in a fixed manner.

Let
$$ \sigma=\sum_i\mu_{u_i}. $$
Because $p_i\geq5$,
$$ \sigma\leq\frac{1}{20s}<\frac{1}{4s}. $$
Thus every selector is fully assigned at its first-choice school. Define
$$ \sigma_j=\sum_{i:g(i)=j}\mu_{u_i}. $$
When probe $w_j$ is served, the remaining capacity of its first-choice school is
$$ \frac{1}{4s}-\sigma_j>0. $$
Earlier probes consume only their own small school and the fallback school. The probes’ total required fallback allocation is
$$ \sum_{j=1}^s \left( \frac{1}{2s}-\frac{1}{4s}+\sigma_j \right) = \frac14+\sigma < \frac34. $$
Therefore the fallback school remains available until every probe finishes.

Probe $w_j$ consequently observes
$$ \frac{x_{w_j,h_j}}{\mu_{w_j}} = \frac12-\frac{1}{2r} \sum_{i:g(i)=j}\frac1{p_i}. $$

5.3 Distinct assignments give distinct probe outputs

Subset sums of $1/p_1,\ldots,1/p_r$ are distinct.

Indeed, suppose
$$ \sum_i\frac{z_i}{p_i}=0, \qquad z_i\in\{-1,0,1\}. $$
Multiply by $P=\prod_i p_i$ and reduce modulo $p_j$. This gives
$$ z_j\frac{P}{p_j}\equiv0\pmod{p_j}. $$
The factor $P/p_j$ is invertible modulo $p_j$, so $z_j\equiv0\pmod{p_j}$. Since $p_j\geq5$, necessarily $z_j=0$.

Thus the probe-output tuple identifies the set of selectors choosing each school, and hence identifies $g$. All probe rankings and all masses are fixed. No two different functions $g$ can share a certificate.

There are $s^r$ functions, so
$$ \log_2|\mathcal C| \geq r\log_2s = \Omega(\tau\log_2m). $$

Every mass numerator and denominator has $O(\log_2\tau)$ bits, because $r,s\leq\tau$ and $p_i=O(r^2)$. The total mass encoding length is therefore $O(\tau\log_2\tau)$.

5.4 Two schools

For $m=2$ and $\tau\geq4$, put
$$ r=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau-2}{2}\right\rfloor, \qquad f=\tau-1-2r\in\{1,2\}. $$
Use capacities $1/4$ and $3/4$, one probe of mass $1/2$, selector masses $1/(4rp_i)$, partner masses $(p_i-1)/(4rp_i)$, and $f$ fillers of mass $1/(4f)$.

Each selector independently ranks either school first. The probe has the fixed ranking $h_1\succ h_2$. Its first-school allocation is
$$ \frac14- \sum_{i:\,u_i\text{ ranks }h_1\text{ first}}\frac{1}{4rp_i}. $$
The selectors’ total mass is at most $1/20$, so both their assignments and the probe’s subsequent completion are feasible exactly as above.

Distinct subsets give distinct probe outputs. Hence at least $2^r$ certificates are necessary, proving $\Omega(\tau)$ bits with fixed masses.

These constructions explain why the discrete concurrent-representation bound cannot simply be transferred to exact weighted outcomes. The discrete result concerns indivisible applicants; here a fixed probe’s fractional row records subset sums of other types’ masses. 6. Consequences and the remaining gap

The compressed simulation removes the need to transmit all ranking lists. Its overhead beyond the mass encoding is at most
$$ O\bigl((\tau+m^2)\log_2m\bigr). $$
This yields tight bounds whenever the numerical budget dominates that overhead and the baseline mass-encoding cost.

In particular, throughout the regime $a\geq2$, the condition $\tau\geq m^a$ implies $\tau\geq m^2$. Therefore
$$ B(m,\tau,L)=\Theta(L-R) $$
whenever
$$ L-R\geq C_1\tau\log_2\tau. $$

For every fixed $a>1$, the new upper bound also rules out a uniform assertion $B=\Theta(L)$ under this encoding convention. Take
$$ \tau=\lceil m^a\rceil, \qquad M=C_2\tau\log_2\tau $$
for a sufficiently large fixed constant $C_2$. Then
$$ L=\Theta(\tau m\log_2m), $$
whereas
$$ B(m,\tau,L) = O\bigl((\tau+m^2)\log_2m\bigr) = o(L). $$
The last equality follows from
$$ \frac{\tau+m^2}{\tau m} = \frac1m+\frac m\tau \longrightarrow0. $$

What remains unresolved is the optimal bound for smaller numerical budgets. In particular:

  • The precision construction does not give a matching lower bound when $M$ is close to the minimum possible mass-encoding length.
  • When $1<a<2$ and $M=O(\tau\log_2\tau)$, the upper bound may contain a dominating $m^2\log_2m$ term. The fixed-mass construction proves only $\Omega(\tau\log_2m)$, so it does not close that gap.

These are mathematical gaps, not conclusions inferred from the discrete or continuum cutoff results.

7. Exact computation and running times

Let $\ell_q$ be the total binary encoding length of public capacities, and put
$$ K=L+\ell_q. $$
Let $D$ be the product of all mass and capacity denominators. It has $O(K)$ bits. Scaling by $D$ makes initial masses and capacities integers. Every subsequent residual and allocation is an integer between zero and $D$.

Scanning rankings and priorities and finding cycles takes $O(m\tau)$ elementary operations per ordinary TTC round. There are at most $m+\tau$ rounds. A conservative exact bit-operation bound is
$$ O\bigl((m+\tau)m\tau K^2\bigr) = O\bigl(\tau^{\,2+1/a}K^2\bigr). $$

The compressed simulation has at most $J$ pointer revelations and at most $\tau+m$ cycle clearings. Recomputing school pointers and finding cycles at each iteration gives encoder and decoder running times
$$ O\bigl((\tau+m^2)m\tau K^2\bigr) = O\left( \tau^{\,1+1/a+\max\{1,2/a\}}K^2 \right). $$
Normalization is exact: a row entry is the ratio of its scaled allocation to the positive scaled initial mass.

These bounds include capacity precision because exact outputs can inherit arbitrarily many bits from public capacities. If capacity encoding is included in $L$, replace $K$ by $O(L)$.

As an auxiliary check, the compressed simulation agreed with simultaneous TTC on $3000$ randomly generated integer-scaled instances, and every instance satisfied the revelation bound. The proofs above do not rely on that check.
