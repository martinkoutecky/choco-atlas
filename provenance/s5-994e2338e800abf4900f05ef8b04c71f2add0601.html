The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many applicant types with rational masses summing to one, strict rankings over a finite contract menu, and specified hospital mass-choice rules satisfying mass analogues of IRC and HK, does every finite terminating schedule yield the same terminal contract-mass vector? A schedule selects positive measurable submasses of eligible types to make their most-preferred fresh acceptable offers; if outcomes differ, provide two finite schedules witnessing the difference.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(M\) be a finite contract menu, \(m=|M|\), with each contract specifying a hospital and terms. Each type has a feasible submenu and a strict ranking including an outside option, with at least one contract ranked above it. Let \(L\) be the binary input length; state any running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and the choice-rule representation parameters.
Realize type \(i\) as an atomless interval \(\Omega_i\) of measure \(\mu_i\). An offer set \(A\) is a measurable subset of feasible applicant–contract pairs; write \(p(A)\) for its applicant projection. All set relations are modulo null sets.
Each hospital has a fixed deterministic choice map \(C_h\), defined on every measurable offer set, returning a measurable subset of its own offered pairs and choosing at most one contract per applicant. It depends only on offers to that hospital; any selection conventions are part of the map.
Mass IRC means \(C_h(A\setminus R)=C_h(A)\) for every measurable \(R\subseteq A\setminus C_h(A)\).
Mass HK means that for every measurable \(B\subseteq\Omega\setminus p(C_h(A))\) and menu contract \(x\) feasible for every applicant in \(B\), \(p(C_h(A\cup(B\times\{x\})))\subseteq p(C_h(A))\cup B\).
Start with no offers. Each round selects a positive-measure measurable subset of applicants currently held by no hospital and having an acceptable contract they have never offered; each selected applicant offers their most-preferred such contract. Retain all past offers, including rejected ones, and recompute every hospital's choice from the cumulative offer set.
A finite schedule terminates exactly when the eligible population has measure zero. Its terminal contract-mass vector has coordinates \(v_{i,x}=\mu(\{a\in\Omega_i:(a,x)\in C_{h(x)}(A_T)\})\).

Under mass IRC and mass HK, must every two finite terminating schedules for the same society and choice maps yield the same terminal contract-mass vector, or can two such schedules yield different vectors?

For finite applicants, IRC and HK guarantee outcome equivalence for arbitrary nonempty subsets of eligible proposers ([Bó, Kratz and Shimoji, 2024](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/211560/1/BoKratzShimoji_-_Generalized_COPs.pdf)). No result settling the precisely defined atomless question above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The mass analogues were undefined: IRC permits removal of arbitrary measurable rejected suboffers; HK prevents previously unheld applicants outside a newly offering subpopulation from becoming held.
  • Applicant histories within a type were unspecified: realize each type as an atomless interval and track offers and reservations for measurable subpopulations.
  • Choice-rule domains and selection conventions were unspecified: use fixed deterministic measurable choice maps on all measurable offer sets, with hospital locality and at most one chosen contract per applicant per hospital.
  • Acceptability and feasible contracts were unspecified: rank the outside option strictly alongside each type's feasible contracts and retain the discrete assumption that every type has at least one acceptable contract.
  • Freshness, cumulative retention and termination were unspecified: offers are fresh for each applicant, rejected offers remain available to hospital choice maps, all hospitals recompute each round, and termination requires zero eligible mass.
  • The outcome vector was unspecified: record reserved mass separately for every applicant type and menu contract; schedules may select arbitrary real measurable masses.

Result

Every two finite terminating schedules yield the same terminal contract-mass vector. In fact, their terminal cumulative offer sets agree modulo null sets, so their terminal chosen applicant–contract pairs agree as well.

The proof establishes the atomless analogue of the cited [finite-applicant outcome-equivalence theorem](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/211560/1/BoKratzShimoji_-_Generalized_COPs.pdf) directly, without replacing the continuum by finitely many applicants.

1. States, eligibility, and null sets

For an offer set \(A\), write

\(H_h(A)=p(C_h(A))\), and \(H(A)=\bigcup_h H_h(A)\).

Thus \(H(A)\) is the population currently held by some hospital. Define the eligible population by

\(E(A)=\{a\notin H(A):a\text{ has an acceptable contract never offered in }A\}\).

For \(a\in E(A)\), let \(q_A(a)\) be that applicant’s most-preferred acceptable contract never offered in \(A\). Strict preferences make \(q_A(a)\) unique. Because the menu and type support are finite and every contract section of \(A\) is measurable, both \(E(A)\) and the contract fibers of \(q_A\) are measurable.

For measurable \(B\subseteq E(A)\), define its proposal set by

\(P_A(B)=\{(a,q_A(a)):a\in B\}\).

A legal round is the transition from \(A\) to \(A\cup P_A(B)\), where \(\mu(B)>0\).

We work modulo null sets throughout. Mass IRC ensures that this convention is compatible with the choice maps. Indeed, suppose \(A=A'\) modulo null sets, and put \(W=A\cup A'\). Both \(W\setminus A\) and \(W\setminus A'\) are null, hence are subsets of \(W\setminus C_h(W)\) modulo null sets. Applying mass IRC twice gives

\(C_h(A)=C_h(W)=C_h(A')\)

modulo null sets. Consequently, held populations, eligibility, and terminality depend only on the offer set modulo null sets.

A state is terminal exactly when it admits no legal round: if \(\mu(E(A))>0\), selecting \(E(A)\) itself gives a legal round.

2. A round cannot newly hold applicants outside its proposer group

Lemma. Let \(B\subseteq E(A)\) be measurable, and put \(A_B=A\cup P_A(B)\). For each hospital \(h\), define

\(B_h=\{a\in B:h(q_A(a))=h\}\).

Then

\(H_h(A_B)\subseteq H_h(A)\cup B_h\).

In particular,

\(H(A_B)\subseteq H(A)\cup B\).

Proof. Fix a hospital \(h\). List its menu contracts as \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\), and define the pairwise disjoint measurable sets

\(B_j=\{a\in B:q_A(a)=x_j\}\).

Each \(x_j\) is feasible for every applicant in \(B_j\). Since \(B\subseteq E(A)\), every \(B_j\) is disjoint from \(H_h(A)\).

Consider the intermediate offer sets

\(A^{(j)}=A\cup\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{j}(B_\ell\times\{x_\ell\})\), for \(j=0,\ldots,k\).

We prove by induction that

\(H_h(A^{(j)})\subseteq H_h(A)\cup\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{j}B_\ell\).

The assertion is immediate for \(j=0\). If it holds for \(j-1\), then \(B_j\) is disjoint from \(H_h(A^{(j-1)})\): it is disjoint both from \(H_h(A)\) and from every earlier \(B_\ell\). Mass HK therefore applies and gives

\(H_h(A^{(j)})\subseteq H_h(A^{(j-1)})\cup B_j\).

Combining this inclusion with the induction hypothesis proves the assertion for \(j\).

Finally, \(A^{(k)}\) and \(A_B\) have exactly the same offers to \(h\). Hospital locality gives \(C_h(A^{(k)})=C_h(A_B)\), proving the hospital-specific inclusion. Taking the union over hospitals proves the last inclusion. ∎

The finite menu is essential to this argument: only finitely many applications of mass HK are needed, with no limiting or continuity argument.

3. Two rounds commute after removing their overlap

Write \(A\rightsquigarrow A'\) if either \(A'=A\) modulo null sets or \(A'\) results from one legal round.

Lemma (union diamond). If \(A\rightsquigarrow A_1\) and \(A\rightsquigarrow A_2\), then

\(A_1\rightsquigarrow A_1\cup A_2\), and \(A_2\rightsquigarrow A_1\cup A_2\).

Proof. If either initial transition is equality, the conclusion follows immediately from the other transition. Otherwise, let \(B,D\subseteq E(A)\) be their positive-measure proposer groups. Thus

\(A_1=A\cup P_A(B)\), and \(A_2=A\cup P_A(D)\).

By the preceding lemma,

\(H(A_1)\subseteq H(A)\cup B\).

Applicants in \(D\setminus B\) were unheld at \(A\) and lie outside \(B\), so they remain unheld at \(A_1\). Furthermore, the first round added offers only for applicants in \(B\). Therefore every applicant in \(D\setminus B\) has exactly the same offer history at \(A_1\) as at \(A\). Consequently,

\(D\setminus B\subseteq E(A_1)\), and \(q_{A_1}(a)=q_A(a)\) for \(a\in D\setminus B\).

If \(D\setminus B\) has positive measure, it can make one legal round from \(A_1\). The resulting offer set is

\(A_1\cup P_{A_1}(D\setminus B)=A\cup P_A(B\cup D)=A_1\cup A_2\).

If \(D\setminus B\) is null, then \(A_1\cup A_2=A_1\) modulo null sets, so the permitted equality transition suffices.

Interchanging \(B\) and \(D\) proves the other transition. ∎

This lemma handles overlapping proposer groups because applicants in the overlap have the same next contract at the common starting state.

4. Finite terminal states are unique

Let two finite terminating schedules have cumulative offer states

\(A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_T\), and \(\widetilde A_0,\widetilde A_1,\ldots,\widetilde A_S\),

where \(A_0=\widetilde A_0=\varnothing\).

Define a finite grid of offer sets by

\(G_{i,j}=A_i\cup\widetilde A_j\), for \(0\le i\le T\) and \(0\le j\le S\).

We claim that every horizontal and vertical grid edge is a \(\rightsquigarrow\) transition:

\(G_{i-1,j}\rightsquigarrow G_{i,j}\), and \(G_{i,j-1}\rightsquigarrow G_{i,j}\),

whenever the indicated indices exist.

The boundary edges are precisely the original schedules. For an interior cell, suppose the two edges from \(G_{i-1,j-1}\) to \(G_{i,j-1}\) and \(G_{i-1,j}\) have already been established. The union diamond gives transitions from both successors to their union. That union is

\(G_{i,j-1}\cup G_{i-1,j}=A_i\cup\widetilde A_j=G_{i,j}\),

where we used the cumulative inclusions \(A_{i-1}\subseteq A_i\) and \(\widetilde A_{j-1}\subseteq\widetilde A_j\). Filling the grid row by row proves the claim.

Now \(G_{T,0}=A_T\) is terminal. A terminal state has no legal round, so every \(\rightsquigarrow\) transition out of it must be equality. Moving along the last row therefore gives

\(G_{T,j}=A_T\) for every \(j\).

Likewise, \(G_{0,S}=\widetilde A_S\) is terminal. Moving along the last column gives

\(G_{i,S}=\widetilde A_S\) for every \(i\).

At their intersection,

\(A_T=G_{T,S}=\widetilde A_S\)

modulo null sets.

By the null-set invariance proved in Section 1, \(C_h(A_T)=C_h(\widetilde A_S)\) for every hospital. Hence, for every type \(i\) and contract \(x\),

\(\mu(\{a\in\Omega_i:(a,x)\in C_{h(x)}(A_T)\})=\mu(\{a\in\Omega_i:(a,x)\in C_{h(x)}(\widetilde A_S)\})\).

These are exactly the terminal contract-mass coordinates in the question. Thus the terminal vectors coincide. ∎

5. Computational scope

This is a set-theoretic uniqueness theorem; no algorithmic running time in \(m,\tau,L\) is asserted. Arbitrary measurable choice maps and proposer sets have no specified effective representation or evaluation cost.

There is also no bound on finite schedule length in those society parameters alone. With \(m=\tau=1\), one hospital, and the rule accepting every offer, partitioning the applicant interval into any prescribed number \(N\) of positive-measure pieces and proposing one piece per round gives a terminating schedule of \(N\) rounds.

The proof requires only the stipulated finiteness of the two schedules and the menu. It does not require a uniform bound on schedule length.
