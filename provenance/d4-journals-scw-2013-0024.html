The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given fixed \(k\), parties, utilities, a decreasing policy function \(X\), and rational masses \(\mu_{d,\theta}\) over a finite support of district-preference types, determine the limiting seat count for \(L\) and resulting policy along finite replications with odd district sizes and type frequencies converging to \(\mu\), when each finite game is solved by pure-strategy perfection. Assume each district has a unique mass median \(m_d\) and \(m_d\ne\alpha_d\), where \(\alpha_d=(X(d)+X(d-1))/2\). Is the eventual outcome \(X(d^*)\), where \(d^*=\max(\{d:m_d<\alpha_d\}\cup\{0\})\), independent of the approximating sequence?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(k\geq1\) districts, each electing one representative by majority rule, and \(m=2\) parties \(L,R\) with rational positions \(0\leq\theta_L<\theta_R\leq1\). A rational policy table satisfies \(1\geq X(0)>\cdots>X(k)\geq0\); if \(L\) wins \(s\) districts, policy is \(X(s)\).
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) district-preference types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_{d,\theta}\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_{d,\theta}\mu_{d,\theta}=1\), with district masses \(M_d=\sum_\theta\mu_{d,\theta}>0\).
Each type has a rational bliss point \(\theta\in[0,1]\) and symmetric single-peaked utility strictly decreasing with distance from \(\theta\); its utility values at the attainable policies are supplied as unrestricted rational numbers. Define \(\alpha_j=(X(j)+X(j-1))/2\), and require \(\theta\neq\alpha_j\) for every supported type and every \(j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\).
Each district has a unique mass median \(m_d\), meaning \(\sum_{\theta<m_d}\mu_{d,\theta}<M_d/2\) and \(\sum_{\theta>m_d}\mu_{d,\theta}<M_d/2\). Relabel districts so \(m_1\leq\cdots\leq m_k\), and require \(m_d\neq\alpha_d\). Set \(d^*=\max(\{d\in\{1,\ldots,k\}:m_d<\alpha_d\}\cup\{0\})\).
An admissible replication sequence consists of finite electorates using only the supported types, with total size \(N_r\to\infty\), positive odd district sizes, and counts satisfying \(n^{(r)}_{d,\theta}/N_r\to\mu_{d,\theta}\). Every voter chooses \(L\) or \(R\), receives utility from the resulting policy, and each finite game is solved by selecting any pure-strategy trembling-hand-perfect equilibrium.
Does every admissible replication sequence eventually yield exactly \(d^*\) seats for \(L\) and policy \(X(d^*)\), independently of the sequence and equilibrium selection?
Is determining the limiting seat count and policy polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For finite electorates with odd district sizes and no voter at any \(\alpha_j\), the pure-strategy trembling-hand-perfect equilibrium outcome exists and is unique; the journal version also confirms uniqueness. [Discrete result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-011-0635-1).
No result settling this exact replication-limit problem was located in the literature search.

  • Normalized masses globally to one, required positive district masses, and interpreted replication frequencies as proportions of the entire electorate.
  • Restricted replications to the supplied support, kept preferences and policies fixed, and required total electorate size to diverge.
  • Restored the ordering of districts by their medians before applying the indexed threshold formula.
  • Interpreted unique mass median as uniqueness over the policy interval, excluding a half-mass gap between distinct supported bliss points.
  • Restored the exclusion of every supported bliss point from every adjacent-policy midpoint, rather than excluding only median-threshold equalities.
  • Interpreted decreasing policy as strictly decreasing, and preferences as strictly decreasing with distance from the bliss point.
  • Used rational bliss points, party positions, policy values, and utility tables at attainable policies to specify a finite binary input; utility values may have either sign.

Result

Yes. Every admissible replication sequence eventually has exactly \(d^*\) seats for \(L\), and hence policy \(X(d^*)\), independently of equilibrium selection. For the stipulated \(m=2\), computing this outcome is polynomial-time solvable.

The proof below establishes the necessary finite-game characterization directly. Thus it does not rely on the finite uniqueness assertion in the [cited journal article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-011-0635-1).

1. Preferences between adjacent policies

Write \(u_i\) for voter \(i\)'s utility and \(\theta_i\) for its bliss point. Define

\(\Delta_i(j)=u_i(X(j))-u_i(X(j-1))\), for \(1\leq j\leq k\).

Because \(X(j)<X(j-1)\), the midpoint \(\alpha_j\) separates the voters preferring these two policies. Symmetry and strict decrease of utility with distance imply

\(\Delta_i(j)>0\) if and only if \(\theta_i<\alpha_j\),

and

\(\Delta_i(j)<0\) if and only if \(\theta_i>\alpha_j\).

Indeed, the difference between the squared distances to the two policies is

\((X(j-1)-\theta_i)^2-(X(j)-\theta_i)^2 =(X(j-1)-X(j))(X(j-1)+X(j)-2\theta_i)\).

Its sign is the sign of \(\alpha_j-\theta_i\). The exclusion of bliss points at every \(\alpha_j\) therefore makes every adjacent-policy comparison strict. No restriction on the magnitudes of the utility differences is needed.

Also, the midpoint sequence is strictly decreasing, since

\(\alpha_j-\alpha_{j+1}=(X(j-1)-X(j+1))/2>0\).

2. A finite-game characterization

Consider any finite electorate satisfying the stipulated odd district sizes and midpoint exclusions. Let \(\widehat m_d\) be the ordinary median bliss point in district \(d\), and order the districts so that

\(\widehat m_1\leq\cdots\leq\widehat m_k\).

Define

\(\widehat s=\max\bigl(\{d:\widehat m_d<\alpha_d\}\cup\{0\}\bigr)\).

We prove that a pure trembling-hand-perfect equilibrium exists and that every such equilibrium gives \(L\) exactly \(\widehat s\) seats.

The pivotality factorization

Fix a voter \(i\) in a district containing \(2h+1\) voters. Under any independent mixed-strategy profile of the other voters, let:

  • \(P_i\) be the probability that the other \(2h\) voters in its district split equally;
  • \(T_i\) be the number of districts won by \(L\) outside its own district.

Switching voter \(i\)'s ballot changes the policy only when its district is tied without it. On that event, voting \(L\) produces \(X(T_i+1)\), whereas voting \(R\) produces \(X(T_i)\).

The tie event depends only on voters in \(i\)'s district, while \(T_i\) depends only on voters outside it. Independence therefore gives the exact identity

\(\mathbb E[u_i\mid L]-\mathbb E[u_i\mid R] =P_i\,\mathbb E[\Delta_i(T_i+1)]\).

For a completely mixed profile, \(P_i>0\). This includes a one-voter district, where \(P_i=1\).

Necessary conditions for perfection

Use the standard definition: a completely mixed profile is \(\varepsilon\)-perfect if every action that is not a best response receives probability at most \(\varepsilon\). A trembling-hand-perfect equilibrium is a limit of such profiles with \(\varepsilon\to0\).

Let \(a\) be a pure perfect equilibrium, with \(s\) districts won by \(L\). Along a sequence witnessing perfection, each voter's action in \(a\) has probability tending to one. Consequently, that action must be a best response for all sufficiently late profiles in the sequence.

If voter \(i\)'s district is won by \(L\) in \(a\), the outcomes outside that district converge to those of \(a\), so \(T_i\) converges in probability to \(s-1\). Hence

\(\mathbb E[\Delta_i(T_i+1)]\longrightarrow\Delta_i(s)\).

This limit is nonzero. By the pivotality factorization, voter \(i\) must therefore vote \(L\) precisely when \(\theta_i<\alpha_s\). Thus every district won by \(L\) satisfies

\(\widehat m_d<\alpha_s\).

Similarly, in every district won by \(R\), \(T_i\) converges in probability to \(s\). Every voter there must vote \(L\) precisely when \(\theta_i<\alpha_{s+1}\). Since \(R\) wins, that district satisfies

\(\widehat m_d>\alpha_{s+1}\).

These statements apply only when the corresponding class of districts is nonempty, so no undefined endpoint midpoint is used.

These conditions force the seat count

Because the medians are nondecreasing and the midpoints strictly decreasing, the sequence \(\widehat m_d-\alpha_d\) is strictly increasing. None of its terms is zero, since every finite median is a supported bliss point. Therefore

\(\widehat m_d<\alpha_d\) for \(d\leq\widehat s\), and
\(\widehat m_d>\alpha_d\) for \(d>\widehat s\).

Suppose first that \(s>\widehat s\). Then \(\widehat m_s>\alpha_s\), so at most \(s-1\) district medians are below \(\alpha_s\). But all \(s\) districts won by \(L\) must have medians below \(\alpha_s\), a contradiction.

Suppose instead that \(s<\widehat s\). Then \(\widehat m_{s+1}<\alpha_{s+1}\), so at least \(s+1\) district medians are below \(\alpha_{s+1}\). None of those districts can be won by \(R\), again contradicting the assumed \(s\) seats for \(L\).

Thus every pure perfect equilibrium has \(s=\widehat s\).

Existence

Set \(s=\widehat s\). Construct a pure profile as follows:

  • In districts \(d\leq s\), vote \(L\) precisely when \(\theta_i<\alpha_s\).
  • In districts \(d>s\), vote \(L\) precisely when \(\theta_i<\alpha_{s+1}\).

A rule for an empty class of districts is simply omitted.

If \(s>0\), then for \(d\leq s\),

\(\widehat m_d\leq\widehat m_s<\alpha_s\),

so those districts elect \(L\). If \(s<k\), then for \(d>s\),

\(\widehat m_d\geq\widehat m_{s+1}>\alpha_{s+1}\),

so those districts elect \(R\).

Now independently reverse every prescribed ballot with probability \(\varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon\to0\), the expected utility difference in the pivotality factorization converges to the corresponding strict adjacent-policy comparison. Thus, for each voter, the prescribed action is its unique best response for all sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\).

There are finitely many voters, so one sufficiently small bound works for all of them. The resulting completely mixed profile is then \(\varepsilon\)-perfect: its only potentially suboptimal action has probability \(\varepsilon\). These profiles converge to the constructed pure profile, proving perfection and existence.

3. Replication preserves every median eventually

Return to the population masses. For each district, define

\(A_d=\sum_{\theta<m_d}\mu_{d,\theta}\), and
\(B_d=\sum_{\theta>m_d}\mu_{d,\theta}\).

The strict mass-median assumptions give

\(\delta=\min_d\{M_d/2-A_d,\;M_d/2-B_d\}>0\).

Each \(m_d\) is necessarily a supported bliss point: otherwise \(A_d+B_d=M_d\), contradicting both strict inequalities.

For an admissible replication sequence, write

\(E_r=\sum_{d,\theta}\left|n^{(r)}_{d,\theta}/N_r-\mu_{d,\theta}\right|\).

The support is finite, so the assumed coordinatewise convergence implies \(E_r\to0\).

Let \(n_d^{(r)}\) be the district size, and let \(a_d^{(r)}\) and \(b_d^{(r)}\) count voters strictly below and strictly above \(m_d\). The coefficients in the expression \(n_d^{(r)}/(2N_r)-a_d^{(r)}/N_r\) are either \(1/2\) or \(-1/2\) on district \(d\)'s types. Consequently,

\(\left|\frac{n_d^{(r)}}{2N_r}-\frac{a_d^{(r)}}{N_r}-(M_d/2-A_d)\right|\leq E_r/2\).

The same bound holds with \(b_d^{(r)}\) and \(B_d\).

For all sufficiently large \(r\), \(E_r<2\delta\). Both bounds then imply

\(a_d^{(r)}<n_d^{(r)}/2\), and
\(b_d^{(r)}<n_d^{(r)}/2\),

simultaneously for every district. Since district sizes are odd, these inequalities say exactly that the finite median is \(m_d\).

Thus, eventually, all finite district medians equal their population medians. The given district ordering remains valid, and the finite characterization yields

\(\widehat s=d^*\).

Every selected pure perfect equilibrium consequently produces exactly \(d^*\) seats and policy \(X(d^*)\). The eventual index can depend on the replication sequence; no convergence rate is required.

4. Computational complexity

The limiting outcome can be computed directly from the support:

  • Sort the supported types by district and bliss point.
  • In each district, sum the masses and find the first bliss point whose cumulative mass exceeds half the district mass. The strict median hypothesis guarantees that this is \(m_d\).
  • Sort the district medians, compute the midpoints, and find the last index satisfying \(m_d<\alpha_d\).
  • Return that index and its policy-table entry.

This uses

\(O(\tau\log(\tau+1)+k\log(k+1)+k)\)

exact arithmetic operations and comparisons.

For a conservative bit bound, use schoolbook arithmetic. A common denominator obtained by multiplying the input mass denominators has \(O(L)\) bits; all scaled mass sums also have \(O(L)\) bits. Bliss-point and midpoint comparisons likewise involve integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Each operation therefore costs at most \(O(L^2)\) bit operations.

Since every district has positive mass, \(k\leq\tau\). Including input reading, the resulting bound for the stipulated \(m=2\) is

\(O\bigl(L+(\tau\log(\tau+1)+k)L^2\bigr)\).

Hence determining the limiting seat count and policy is polynomial-time solvable, without constructing a replicated electorate or computing its equilibrium.

Step-by-step verification

1. Adjacent-policy preferences

The squared-distance calculation is correct:
\[ (X(j-1)-\theta_i)^2-(X(j)-\theta_i)^2 =(X(j-1)-X(j))(X(j-1)+X(j)-2\theta_i). \]

The first factor is positive. Symmetry and strict decrease with distance therefore give exactly the asserted signs of \(\Delta_i(j)\). The midpoint exclusions guarantee nonzero differences.

The calculation establishing strict decrease of the midpoint sequence is also correct, for \(1\leq j<k\). For \(k=1\), there is no adjacent pair of midpoints to check.

2. Pivotality factorization

The factorization is valid under independent mixed strategies. A ballot changes the district winner precisely when the other district voters split equally. On that event, the two possible total seat counts are \(T_i+1\) and \(T_i\).

The tie event and outside-district outcomes depend on disjoint sets of independently randomizing voters, justifying the product
\[ P_i\,\mathbb E[\Delta_i(T_i+1)]. \]

Complete mixing guarantees \(P_i>0\). The one-voter district case is correctly included.

3. Necessary conditions for pure perfection

The stated definition of an \(\varepsilon\)-perfect profile is applicable here. Along a witnessing sequence converging to a pure profile, each prescribed action eventually has probability greater than \(\varepsilon\), and hence must be a best response.

Outside-district outcomes converge to their deterministic outcomes at the pure profile. Since each voter’s adjacent-policy differences form a finite list, the asserted expectation limits follow.

Those limits are nonzero. Together with positive pivotality probabilities, they justify the prescribed ballot signs and consequently the median conditions:

  • Every district won by \(L\) has median below \(\alpha_s\).
  • Every district won by \(R\) has median above \(\alpha_{s+1}\).

The endpoint restrictions are handled correctly: \(\alpha_s\) is used only when \(s>0\), and \(\alpha_{s+1}\) only when \(s<k\).

4. Uniqueness of the seat count

After sorting district medians, \(\widehat m_d-\alpha_d\) is strictly increasing. Every finite median is a voter’s bliss point, so midpoint exclusions rule out equality.

Both contradiction arguments are valid:

  • If \(s>\widehat s\), fewer than \(s\) districts can satisfy the necessary condition for an \(L\) win.
  • If \(s<\widehat s\), more than \(s\) districts are excluded from being \(R\)-winning districts.

These arguments also cover \(\widehat s=0\) and \(\widehat s=k\). They establish uniqueness of the seat count and policy, which is exactly the required outcome claim.

The constructed profile produces the asserted district winners. The inequalities involving \(\widehat m_s\) and \(\widehat m_{s+1}\) are correct, and empty classes are appropriately omitted.

Independent ballot reversals produce completely mixed profiles. The previously established convergence of the expectation factor, its nonzero limiting sign, and positive pivotality probabilities support the best-response claim. Finiteness of the electorate justifies choosing one sufficiently small perturbation bound for all voters. The resulting profiles satisfy the stated \(\varepsilon\)-perfection condition.
