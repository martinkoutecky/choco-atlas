The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite client and provider type sets with rational masses, per-agent loads and capacities, eligibility, client rankings of provider types, and a common provider priority order, find a rational aggregate assignment satisfying capacities and having no blocking type pair. Is this search problem solvable in time polynomial in the type counts, eligibility size, and input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) and \(P\) be nonempty finite client and provider type sets. Each side's society is given by its support: types with positive binary-rational masses \(a_c\) and \(b_p\), respectively, summing to \(1\) on each side. Write \(\tau=|C|+|P|\) for the total support size, not the number of possible types, and \(m=|P|\).
Each client type has a positive binary-rational per-agent load \(\ell_c\), and each provider type a positive binary-rational per-agent capacity \(k_p\). Eligibility is \(E\subseteq C\times P\); every eligible match is preferable to unassigned load. Clients have strict rankings \(\succ_c\) of their eligible provider types; providers share a strict priority order \(\succ\) on client types.
An aggregate assignment consists of divisible loads \(x_{cp}\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) for \((c,p)\in E\), with \(r_c=\sum_{p:(c,p)\in E}x_{cp}\leq a_c\ell_c\) and \(s_p=\sum_{c:(c,p)\in E}x_{cp}\leq b_pk_p\); there are no additional edge capacities.
An eligible pair \((c,p)\) blocks exactly when both \(r_c<a_c\ell_c\) or \(\exists q\colon x_{cq}>0\text{ and }p\succ_c q\), and \(s_p<b_pk_p\) or \(\exists d\colon x_{dp}>0\text{ and }c\succ d\). Stability means absence of such a pair, including pairs already carrying positive load.

Is finding a rational stable aggregate assignment solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|E|,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, or is this search problem NP-hard?

For finite agents with fractional loads and capacities and common provider preferences, a deterministic distributed algorithm computes a stable assignment in polynomially many rounds and is truthful for clients, subject to bounded message representations ([Hirvonen–Ranjbaran](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.OPODIS.2024.30)). No result explicitly formulated for this distribution-over-types input was located.

Result

Finding a rational stable aggregate assignment is polynomial-time solvable. Process client types in the common provider priority order, assigning each type greedily to its eligible providers in its own preference order. This computes the unique stable aggregate assignment.

The proof below applies directly to the given supports and includes a polynomial bound on rational representation sizes.

1. Aggregate demands and capacities

Define \(n=|C|\), and write the common priority order as \(c_1\succ c_2\succ\cdots\succ c_n\). Define aggregate demand and capacity by

\(d_c=a_c\ell_c,\qquad u_p=b_pk_p.\)

These are positive rationals. The feasibility constraints become

\(\sum_{p:(c,p)\in E}x_{cp}\leq d_c,\qquad \sum_{c:(c,p)\in E}x_{cp}\leq u_p.\)

Both feasibility and the specified blocking condition depend on the masses, loads, and capacities only through \(d_c\) and \(u_p\). Thus these products preserve the problem exactly; there is no need to expand a type into individual agents.

2. Algorithm

Initialize \(x_{cp}=0\) on every eligible edge and remaining provider capacities \(h_p=u_p\).

For \(i=1,\ldots,n\):

  • Initialize the remaining demand \(t=d_{c_i}\).
  • Visit all eligible providers of \(c_i\), from most to least preferred.
  • At each visited provider \(p\), perform the updates

\(z=\min\{t,h_p\},\qquad x_{c_ip}=z,\qquad t\leftarrow t-z,\qquad h_p\leftarrow h_p-z.\)

A client with no eligible providers receives zero load. Visiting the remaining providers after \(t=0\) merely assigns zero.

The algorithm makes one allocation step per eligible edge.

3. Feasibility and stability

Feasibility

Every allocation \(z\) is nonnegative and is at most both the remaining client demand and the remaining provider capacity. Consequently, all remaining demands and capacities stay nonnegative.

For a client \(c_i\), its final assigned load equals its initial demand minus its final remaining demand. Hence \(r_{c_i}\leq d_{c_i}\). Likewise, \(s_p=u_p-h_p\leq u_p\) for every provider. Thus the output is feasible.

Stability

Fix any eligible pair \((c_i,p)\). Suppose the client-side blocking condition holds:

\(r_{c_i}<d_{c_i}\), or there exists \(q\) with \(x_{c_iq}>0\) and \(p\succ_{c_i}q\).

Let \(t\) and \(h\) be the remaining demand of \(c_i\) and remaining capacity of \(p\), respectively, immediately before the algorithm visits \(p\). At that visit,

\(x_{c_ip}=\min\{t,h\}.\)

We show that \(t-x_{c_ip}>0\).

  • If \(r_{c_i}<d_{c_i}\), the remaining demand at the end of this client's turn is positive. Remaining demand only decreases, so it was also positive immediately after visiting \(p\).
  • Otherwise, choose the provider \(q\) in the client-side condition. Since rankings are strict and \(p\succ_{c_i}q\), the algorithm visits \(q\) after \(p\). Its later allocation \(x_{c_iq}>0\) implies that the remaining demand immediately after visiting \(p\) was positive.

Therefore \(x_{c_ip}<t\). The minimum formula forces \(x_{c_ip}=h\): provider \(p\) is exhausted at this step.

Provider capacity never increases. Hence \(p\) stays full, and no later client receives positive load at \(p\). All positive load at \(p\) therefore belongs to \(c_i\) or to clients preceding \(c_i\) in the common priority order. In the final assignment,

\(s_p=u_p,\qquad x_{c_jp}=0\ \text{for every }j>i.\)

Neither alternative in the provider-side blocking condition holds: there is no spare capacity, and no positively assigned client has lower priority than \(c_i\).

Thus whenever the client-side condition holds, the provider-side condition fails. No eligible pair blocks. This argument also covers pairs with \(x_{c_ip}>0\), as required.

4. Uniqueness

Although uniqueness is unnecessary for polynomial-time solvability, it follows from the same structure.

Let \(y\) be any feasible stable assignment. We prove that it equals the algorithm's assignment \(x\), by induction through clients in priority order and through each client's providers in preference order.

Fix client \(c_i\), assuming that all higher-priority client rows agree with \(x\). Let its preference list be \(p_1,\ldots,p_k\). Fix \(j\), assuming also that

\(y_{c_ip_v}=x_{c_ip_v}\quad\text{for all }v<j.\)

Define the remaining demand and capacity after these already fixed allocations by

\(T=d_{c_i}-\sum_{v<j}y_{c_ip_v},\qquad H=u_{p_j}-\sum_{h<i}y_{c_hp_j}.\)

By the induction hypotheses, these are exactly the quantities used by the algorithm at edge \((c_i,p_j)\). Thus

\(x_{c_ip_j}=\min\{T,H\}.\)

Feasibility and nonnegativity give

\(y_{c_ip_j}\leq T,\qquad y_{c_ip_j}\leq H.\)

Suppose that \(y_{c_ip_j}<\min\{T,H\}\). Then \(T-y_{c_ip_j}>0\), and

\(T-y_{c_ip_j}=(d_{c_i}-r^y_{c_i})+\sum_{v>j}y_{c_ip_v}.\)

All terms on the right are nonnegative. Therefore either \(c_i\) is underassigned or it assigns positive load to a provider less preferred than \(p_j\). The client-side blocking condition holds.

Likewise, \(H-y_{c_ip_j}>0\), and

\(H-y_{c_ip_j}=(u_{p_j}-s^y_{p_j})+\sum_{h>i}y_{c_hp_j}.\)

Again, all terms are nonnegative. Hence either \(p_j\) has spare capacity or it carries positive load from a lower-priority client. The provider-side blocking condition also holds.

This contradicts stability. Consequently,

\(y_{c_ip_j}=\min\{T,H\}=x_{c_ip_j}.\)

This proves the induction step. Empty preference lists force zero rows by feasibility. Therefore every stable assignment equals \(x\).

5. Rational representation and polynomial bit complexity

Represent each input scalar \(a_c,\ell_c,b_p,k_p\) as a ratio of positive binary integers. This also covers dyadic inputs. Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all these scalar occurrences.

Because their total encoding length is at most \(L\), the binary length of \(Q\) is \(O(L)\). Each aggregate demand or capacity uses two of these denominator occurrences, so

\(D_c=Qd_c\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\qquad U_p=Qu_p\in\mathbb Z_{>0}.\)

For example, if \(a_c=\alpha_c/\beta_c\) and \(\ell_c=\lambda_c/\delta_c\), then

\(D_c=\alpha_c\lambda_c\,\frac{Q}{\beta_c\delta_c}.\)

The quotient is an integer by the definition of \(Q\). Its multiplication by the two input numerators produces an integer with \(O(L)\) bits. The same argument applies to \(U_p\).

Run the algorithm on these scaled integer demands and capacities. Its operations are comparisons and subtractions, and every remaining quantity stays between zero and its initial value. Hence every intermediate integer, including every allocated amount \(X_{cp}\), has \(O(L)\) bits.

Output

\(x_{cp}=X_{cp}/Q.\)

Positive scaling preserves the minimum operations, so this is exactly the rational assignment proved stable above. Each output rational has \(O(L)\) bits, and the entire assignment has \(O(|E|L)\) bits.

Constructing the scaled data requires polynomial-time integer arithmetic on \(O(L)\)-bit integers. The allocation phase uses \(O(\tau+|E|)\) arithmetic operations on integers of that size. The supplied orders can be traversed directly; converting another explicit ranking representation into ordered lists also takes polynomial time.

Thus the total bit complexity is polynomial in \(m,\tau,|E|,L\), independently of the numerical magnitudes of demands and capacities.

Here, taking one vertex per supported type gives exactly that fractional feasibility and blocking model, with demands \(a_c\ell_c\) and capacities \(b_pk_p\). The direct proof above additionally establishes the required centralized bit complexity for arbitrary binary-rational inputs.
