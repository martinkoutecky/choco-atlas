The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite complete patient types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), categories \(C\) with rational per-capita capacities \(\rho_c\), eligibility and weak priority orders over types, and a policy order \(\pi_T\), compute rational \(y_{t,c}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_c y_{t,c}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,c}\le\rho_c\), and \(y_{t,c}=0\) for ineligible pairs. Require that \(y_{t,c}>0\) implies every higher-priority eligible type \(t'\succ_c t\) has unmatched mass \(u_{t'}=\mu_{t'}-\sum_c y_{t',c}=0\); among such allocations, maximize \(\sum_{t,c}y_{t,c}\), then lexicographically maximize matched mass according to \(\pi_T\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) patient types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=|C|\geq1\) categories with nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(\rho_c\); \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each category has an explicitly given weak priority order \(\succeq_c\) on \(T\cup\{\varnothing\}\); type \(t\) is eligible exactly when \(t\succ_c\varnothing\). All patients of a type have identical eligibility and priority positions and are indifferent among eligible categories. The policy order \(\pi_T=(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau)\) is a strict order, with the first type taking precedence.
An allocation consists of rational masses \(y_{t,c}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_c y_{t,c}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,c}\leq\rho_c\), and \(y_{t,c}=0\) for ineligible pairs. Mass may be split among categories. Write \(s_t=\sum_c y_{t,c}\) and \(u_t=\mu_t-s_t\).
Respect of priorities requires \(y_{t,c}>0\Longrightarrow u_{t'}=0\) for every eligible \(t'\succ_c t\); tied types impose no such restriction on one another.
Among allocations satisfying these conditions, first maximize \(\sum_t s_t\), then lexicographically maximize \((s_{t_1},\ldots,s_{t_\tau})\). Any allocation attaining both objectives may be returned.

Is computing such an optimal allocation polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For individual patients and integer quotas, Reverse Rejecting rules compute an eligibility-compliant, priority-respecting matching of maximum feasible size in strongly polynomial time and respect improvements ([Aziz–Brandl, 2024](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/effreserves_journal.pdf)).
For unit-demand individuals, strict priorities, and integer quotas, Maximum Rationing Eating computes a fractional allocation respecting eligibility and priorities and attaining maximum size; it does not impose the policy-order objective ([Aziz, 2023](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2023/pdfs/p949.pdf)). No result located settles the stated rational-mass optimization problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Encoded eligibility through each category's weak order including an outside option: only types strictly above the outside option are eligible.
  • Specified that patients within a type share eligibility and priority positions and are indifferent among eligible categories; fractional masses may be split.
  • Allowed nonnegative rational capacities without imposing a total-capacity normalization, and took the category set to be nonempty.
  • Made the policy order strict and defined lexicographic precedence explicitly, with its first listed type taking precedence; category assignments with identical optimal matched-mass vectors need no further tie-breaking.
  • Defined L as total input bit length and m as the number of categories.

Result

Computing the required allocation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This holds even when all masses and capacities are dyadic rationals and only one category has ties among eligible types.

The reduction makes the maximum total allocated mass explicitly known. The hardness lies in maximizing the first policy coordinate.

1. Construction

We reduce from CLIQUE: given a simple undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether \(G\) contains a clique of size \(k\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. [Karp, *Reducibility among Combinatorial Problems*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

Write \(V=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and \(q=|E|\). We may restrict to \(2\leq k\leq n\) and \(q\geq1\): instances outside these restrictions are decidable directly, so this restriction preserves hardness.

Choose

\(a=\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil,\qquad b=\lceil\log_2(q+1)\rceil,\qquad d=2^{-a},\qquad h=2^{-(a+b)}.\)

These choices ensure

\(qh<d,\qquad 1-nd\geq d.\)

Create one type \(v_i\) for each vertex \(i\), and a distinguished type \(z\). Their masses are

\(\mu_{v_i}=d,\qquad \mu_z=1-nd.\)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals, and they sum to \(1\).

There are two kinds of categories.

  • A shared category \(c_0\). Its capacity is \(\rho_{c_0}=kd\), and its priority order is

\(v_1\sim_{c_0}\cdots\sim_{c_0}v_n\succ_{c_0}\varnothing\succ_{c_0}z.\)
Thus every vertex type is eligible, all vertex types are tied, and \(z\) is ineligible.

  • An edge category \(c_e\) for each \(e=\{i,j\}\in E\), with \(i<j\). Its capacity is \(\rho_{c_e}=h\), and its priority order begins

\(v_i\succ_{c_e}v_j\succ_{c_e}z\succ_{c_e}\varnothing.\)
All remaining types appear below \(\varnothing\), in any fixed strict order. Hence exactly the two endpoint types and \(z\) are eligible.

The policy order is \(\pi_T=(z,v_1,\ldots,v_n)\).

The construction has \(\tau=n+1\) types and \(m=q+1\) categories. Every rational number uses \(O(\log n+\log q)\) bits, and all orders can be listed explicitly in polynomial space. Thus the reduction has polynomial input length.

2. The maximum total allocated mass is known exactly

Define

\(F=kd+qh.\)

Every allocation has total allocated mass at most \(F\), because \(F\) is the sum of all category capacities. We prove that a priority-respecting allocation attaining \(F\) exists for every set \(S\subseteq V\) of size \(k\).

Construct the allocation as follows.

  • Give each \(v_i\) with \(i\in S\) its full mass \(d\) through \(c_0\).
  • For an edge whose two endpoints belong to \(S\), give its entire capacity \(h\) to \(z\).
  • For every other edge, give its entire capacity \(h\) to the highest-priority endpoint outside \(S\).

All unspecified allocations are zero.

Mass feasibility. Each selected vertex type receives exactly \(d\). Each unselected vertex type receives at most \(qh<d\). Type \(z\) also receives at most \(qh<d\leq\mu_z\). Every category is filled exactly to capacity, and every assigned pair is eligible.

Respect of priorities.

  • In \(c_0\), all eligible types are tied, so its assignments impose no strict-priority requirements.
  • If \(z\) receives mass in an edge category, both endpoint types belong to \(S\) and are fully served.
  • Otherwise, the recipient is the highest-priority endpoint outside \(S\). Every eligible type strictly above that recipient must therefore be an endpoint in \(S\), and is fully served.

Thus the constructed allocation respects priorities and has total mass \(F\). Consequently, the first objective’s optimum is exactly \(F\).

Moreover, writing

\(E(S)=\{\{i,j\}\in E:i,j\in S\},\)

this construction gives \(s_z=h|E(S)|\).

3. An upper bound valid for every allocation

Consider any feasible priority-respecting allocation, and let

\(S_y=\{i\in V:s_{v_i}=d\}\)

be its fully served vertex types.

Each such type consumes mass \(d\). Therefore

\(|S_y|d\leq\sum_{t\in T}s_t\leq F=kd+qh<(k+1)d.\)

Since \(|S_y|\) is an integer, it follows that \(|S_y|\leq k\).

Now suppose \(y_{z,c_e}>0\) for an edge \(e=\{i,j\}\). Both endpoint types strictly outrank \(z\) in \(c_e\). Respect of priorities therefore requires

\(s_{v_i}=d,\qquad s_{v_j}=d.\)

Hence \(e\in E(S_y)\). Type \(z\) is ineligible for \(c_0\), so its entire allocated mass comes from such edge categories. Each has capacity \(h\), giving

\(s_z\leq h|E(S_y)|.\)

Because \(|S_y|\leq k\leq n\), we can extend \(S_y\) to a set of exactly \(k\) vertices. Adding vertices cannot remove induced edges. Consequently,

\(s_z\leq h\max_{\substack{S\subseteq V\\|S|=k}}|E(S)|.\)

This upper bound applies even to allocations that do not attain maximum total mass.

4. The first policy coordinate encodes CLIQUE

Let \(s_z^\star\) denote the maximum possible value of \(s_z\) among allocations attaining total mass \(F\).

Section 3 supplies an upper bound, while Section 2 supplies a maximum-total allocation attaining \(h|E(S)|\) for every \(k\)-vertex set \(S\). Therefore

\(s_z^\star=h\max_{\substack{S\subseteq V\\|S|=k}}|E(S)|.\)

Since \(z\) is first in the policy order, every allocation satisfying both requested objectives must have this value of \(s_z\).

A simple graph on \(k\) vertices has at most \(\binom{k}{2}\) edges, with equality exactly when those vertices form a clique. Thus

\(G\text{ contains a clique of size }k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s_z^\star\geq h\binom{k}{2}.\)

An algorithm computing the requested optimal allocation would therefore decide CLIQUE: construct this instance, compute the allocation, sum its entries for \(z\), and compare that rational number with \(h\binom{k}{2}\). Construction, summation, and comparison all take polynomial time.

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. In particular, an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

5. Relation to the cited algorithms

The reduction does not obstruct maximum-size allocation: Section 2 constructs one directly for any \(k\)-vertex set. It establishes hardness of the additional policy-order objective, already at its first coordinate.
