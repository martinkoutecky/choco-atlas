For every fixed $m\ge 4$, the following bounds hold:
$$ \frac43\le \lim_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)\le 2(m-1). $$

The existence of the ordinary limit is a new structural conclusion. More precisely,
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle \lim_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)=\sup_{n\ge1}\rho_n(P_m). } $$

Consequently, equality with $4/3$ holds if and only if every finite denominator admits a strategyproof $4/3$-approximation satisfying the zero-optimum condition. Conversely, a strict obstruction at any single denominator forces the limit to exceed $4/3$.

I also prove that a strategyproof mechanism can be optimal on every public location distribution supported at at most two nodes, without requiring those nodes to be adjacent. In particular,
$$ \rho_1(P_m)=\rho_2(P_m)=1. $$

These results do not decide equality versus strict excess for the full domain. The write-up below is therefore partial.

1. Block deviations follow from individual strategyproofness

Write
$$ \mathcal Z_m=\{(u,v)\in V^2:u\ne v\}. $$

Fix $q$, an admissible mechanism $M_q$, a node $x$, two distinct approval sets $A,B\in\mathcal A$, and an integer $\ell$ such that
$$ 1\le \ell\le q\mu(x,A). $$
Define
$$ \mu^j=\mu+\frac{j}{q} \bigl(\delta_{(x,B)}-\delta_{(x,A)}\bigr), \qquad 0\le j\le\ell. $$

For $j<\ell$, the profile $\mu^j$ has at least $1/q$ mass at $(x,A)$. Strategyproofness therefore gives
$$ c_{x,A}(M_q(\mu^j)) \le c_{x,A}(M_q(\mu^{j+1})). $$
Chaining these inequalities proves
$$ c_{x,A}(M_q(\mu)) \le c_{x,A}(M_q(\mu^\ell)). $$

At $\mu^{j+1}$ there is at least $1/q$ mass at $(x,B)$, so the reverse individual deviation is also permitted. It gives
$$ c_{x,B}(M_q(\mu^{j+1})) \le c_{x,B}(M_q(\mu^j)). $$
Consequently,
$$ c_{x,B}(M_q(\mu^\ell)) \le c_{x,B}(M_q(\mu)). $$

Thus changing an entire co-located block with a common initial report imposes both endpoint inequalities. This conclusion uses only the individual deviations specified in the question.

2. The lower bound survives repeated locations

Fix $q\ge3$ and write
$$ q=3r+s,\qquad r=\lfloor q/3\rfloor,\qquad s\in\{0,1,2\}. $$
Multiply social costs and optima by $q$ throughout this section. These integer costs have the same approximation ratios as the normalized costs.

Consider the profile with $r$ agents at node $1$, $r+s$ at node $2$, and $r$ at node $3$, all approving both facilities. Let
$$ F(v)=r|1-v|+(r+s)|2-v|+r|3-v|. $$
Directly,
$$ F(1)=F(3)=q,\qquad F(2)=2r, $$
and
$$ F(v)=q(v-2)\qquad(v\ge3). $$
Since the facilities must be distinct, the optimum is
$$ 2r+q=5r+s. $$

If an admissible mechanism places a facility at a node at least $4$, that facility contributes at least $2q$, while the other contributes at least $2r$. Its ratio is therefore at least
$$ \frac{2q+2r}{5r+s} =\frac{8r+2s}{5r+s} >\frac43, $$
because
$$ 3(8r+2s)-4(5r+s)=4r+2s>0. $$

Otherwise, both facilities lie in $\{1,2,3\}$. Distinctness ensures that some facility $k$ lies at an endpoint $e\in\{1,3\}$.

Change the entire block of $r$ agents at $e$ from approving both facilities to approving only $k$. By the reverse inequality in Section 1,
$$ |e-M_q(\mu^{\mathrm{final}})_k| \le |e-M_q(\mu^{\mathrm{initial}})_k|=0. $$
Thus facility $k$ remains at $e$.

At the final profile, facility $k$ still has distance objective $F$, so its contribution is $q$. The other facility is supported by $r+s$ agents at node $2$ and $r$ at the opposite endpoint $e'=4-e$. Its objective $G$ satisfies
$$ G(2)=r,\qquad G(e')=r+s,\qquad G(e)=q. $$
For every $v\ne2$, one has $G(v)\ge r+s$: the displayed values verify this within $\{1,2,3\}$, and $G$ is increasing beyond node $3$.

The mechanism's final cost is consequently at least
$$ q+r=4r+s. $$

The final optimum is exactly $3r+s$. Indeed:

  • If facility $k$ is at node $2$, its cost is $2r$, and distinctness forces the other facility to contribute at least $r+s$.
  • If facility $k$ is elsewhere, its cost is at least $q$, and the other facility contributes at least $r$.
  • Placing facility $k$ at node $2$ and the other at $e'$ attains $2r+(r+s)$.

Hence the final approximation ratio is at least
$$ \frac{4r+s}{3r+s}=1+\frac rq. $$

Both possibilities for the initial output therefore imply
$$ \rho_q(P_m)\ge1+\frac{\lfloor q/3\rfloor}{q}. $$
Taking the limit inferior gives
$$ \liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)\ge\frac43. $$

This is a self-contained block extension of the endpoint argument in the [IJCAI lower-bound paper](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2022/0049.pdf).

3. A uniform finite upper bound

Set $D=m-1$. For a reported distribution $\mu$, its public location distribution is
$$ p(x)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\mu(x,A). $$
Every permitted deviation preserves $p$.

For an unordered pair $S=\{u,v\}$ of distinct nodes, define
$$ H_p(S)=\sum_xp(x)\min\{|x-u|,|x-v|\}. $$

Choose a pair minimizing $H_p$, with deterministic tie-breaking depending only on $p$. Then choose the cheaper reported orientation among $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$, with fixed tie-breaking.

Strategyproofness

Fix $p$, and call the two orientations $o^0=(u,v)$ and $o^1=(v,u)$. Ties favor $o^0$. For an agent at $x$, put
$$ d=|x-u|-|x-v|. $$
Her contribution to
$$ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(o^0)-\mathrm{SC}_\mu(o^1) $$
is $d$, $-d$, or $0$, according as she reports $\{1\}$, $\{2\}$, or $\{1,2\}$.

If a singleton agent prefers $o^0$, her truthful contribution is the smallest of these three values. A misreport can only increase the total difference, and therefore cannot change an output $o^1$ into her preferred $o^0$.

If she prefers $o^1$, her truthful contribution is the largest. A misreport can only decrease the total difference, and cannot change an output $o^0$ into her preferred $o^1$.

Agents approving both facilities are indifferent between the orientations. The factor $1/q$ in a single deviation preserves every comparison. This proves strategyproofness, including ties.

Approximation and zero optima

Let $z^*=(a_1,a_2)$ be optimal and let $A^*=\{a_1,a_2\}$. Since every approval set is nonempty,
$$ H_p(S)\le H_p(A^*)\le\mathrm{OPT}(\mu). $$

Choose a bijection from $A^*$ to $S$ fixing their intersection, and let $w_k$ be the image of $a_k$. Then $w=(w_1,w_2)$ is an available orientation.

Consider each approved-facility contribution separately.

If $x\ne a_k$, integrality gives
$$ |x-w_k|\le D\le D|x-a_k|. $$
If $x=a_k\in S$, the contribution remains zero.

A remaining zero contribution has $x=a_k\notin S$ and becomes at most $D$. An agent has zero distance to at most one of the two distinct optimal facilities. Thus the total mass of these contributions is at most
$$ \sum_{x\notin S}p(x)\le H_p(S), $$
where the last inequality uses the fact that every node outside $S$ has distance at least one from $S$.

Summing yields
$$ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(w) \le D\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)+D\,H_p(S) \le2D\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu). $$
The selected orientation is no more expensive. Therefore
$$ \rho_q(P_m)\le2(m-1) $$
for every $q$. The same inequality proves the required zero-optimum condition.

From the support list, this mechanism takes $O(m^2\tau)$ exact rational arithmetic operations. Using a common denominator and elementary integer arithmetic gives bit time
$$ O\!\left(m^2\tau(L+\log m)^2\right). $$
There is no additional dependence on $q$.

4. The limit exists and equals the supremum of finite optima

Fix $m$ and abbreviate
$$ \rho_q=\rho_q(P_m),\qquad D=m-1,\qquad K=2D. $$

For each $q$, the domain has
$$ |\mathcal D_q|=\binom{q+3m-1}{3m-1} $$
elements, and the outcome set has $m(m-1)$ elements. There are therefore finitely many deterministic mechanisms. Section 3 shows that the admissible class is nonempty. It follows that the infimum defining $\rho_q$ is attained.

Moreover, every admissible mechanism with worst-case ratio $R$ satisfies
$$ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(M_q(\mu))\le R\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu) $$
on all profiles: the zero-optimum requirement supplies this inequality when the optimum is zero.

Replication with bounded padding

Fix a positive integer $n$. For $q\ge n$, write
$$ q=kn+s,\qquad k\ge1,\qquad 0\le s<n. $$
Fix the padding type
$$ t_0=(1,\{1\}). $$
For $\mu\in\mathcal D_n$, define
$$ \Phi_q(\mu)=\frac{kn}{q}\mu+\frac{s}{q}\delta_{t_0}. $$
This belongs to $\mathcal D_q$: each integer count in the $n$-agent profile is replicated $k$ times, and $s$ padding agents are added.

Let $M_q$ attain $\rho_q$, and define
$$ N_n(\mu)=M_q(\Phi_q(\mu)). $$

An individual deviation in $\mathcal D_n$ corresponds under $\Phi_q$ to changing a block of $k$ agents at the same location from one common approval report to another. Section 1 consequently proves that $N_n$ is strategyproof.

It remains to check the zero-optimum condition.

Define unnormalized costs
$$ C_\mu(z)=n\,\mathrm{SC}_\mu(z), \qquad O_\mu=n\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu). $$
Both are integers. The padded profile has unnormalized cost
$$ q\,\mathrm{SC}_{\Phi_q(\mu)}(z) =kC_\mu(z)+s\,c_{t_0}(z). $$
Since $t_0$ approves only one facility,
$$ 0\le c_{t_0}(z)\le D. $$

Suppose $O_\mu=0$. Evaluating the padded profile at an original zero-cost optimum gives
$$ q\,\mathrm{OPT}(\Phi_q(\mu))\le sD. $$
Using $\rho_q\le K$,
$$ kC_\mu(N_n(\mu)) \le q\,\mathrm{SC}_{\Phi_q(\mu)}(N_n(\mu)) \le KsD. $$
If
$$ k>KD(n-1), $$
then $k>KsD$. Since $C_\mu(N_n(\mu))$ is a nonnegative integer, it must be zero. Thus $N_n$ is admissible for every sufficiently large $q$.

For a profile with $O_\mu>0$, one has $O_\mu\ge1$. Evaluating the padded profile at an original optimum gives
$$ q\,\mathrm{OPT}(\Phi_q(\mu)) \le kO_\mu+sD. $$
Consequently,
$$ kC_\mu(N_n(\mu)) \le \rho_q(kO_\mu+sD), $$
and hence
$$ \frac{C_\mu(N_n(\mu))}{O_\mu} \le \rho_q\left(1+\frac{sD}{kO_\mu}\right) \le \rho_q\left(1+\frac{sD}{k}\right). $$
Taking the worst case and using admissibility of $N_n$ proves
$$ \rho_n\le \rho_q\left(1+\frac{sD}{k}\right) $$
for every sufficiently large $q$.

For fixed $n$, the factor on the right tends to one. Therefore
$$ \liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q\ge\rho_n. $$
This holds for every $n$, so
$$ \liminf_{q\to\infty}\rho_q \ge\sup_{n\ge1}\rho_n. $$
The reverse upper bound for the limit superior follows immediately from the definition of the supremum:
$$ \limsup_{q\to\infty}\rho_q \le\sup_{n\ge1}\rho_n. $$
The supremum is finite by Section 3. These inequalities prove
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle \lim_{q\to\infty}\rho_q(P_m)=\sup_{n\ge1}\rho_n(P_m). } $$

When $s=0$, padding is unnecessary and the zero-optimum condition is preserved automatically. The same argument gives the additional relation
$$ \rho_{kn}(P_m)\ge\rho_n(P_m) \qquad(k,n\ge1). $$

5. An optimal strategyproof rule for two occupied nodes

Suppose the public support consists of two nodes $a<b$, and put $d=b-a$. Define supporter masses
$$ U_k=\sum_{A:k\in A}\mu(a,A), \qquad R_k=\sum_{A:k\in A}\mu(b,A), \qquad s_k=U_k-R_k. $$

Reduction of the optimization problem

Every feasible outcome can be replaced by a feasible outcome inside $[a,b]$ without increasing any approved distance.

To see this, first clamp both locations to $[a,b]$. If the clamped locations are distinct, the claim follows directly. A collision can occur only at $a$ or $b$.

For a collision at $a$, both original locations were at most $a$. At most one was exactly $a$ because the original locations were distinct. Keep that facility at $a$ if present, and put the other at $a+1$. The moved facility was originally at most $a-1$, so its distance from each occupied node weakly decreases. If neither original facility was at $a$, either labeling of $a,a+1$ works. The collision at $b$ is symmetric.

For an outcome inside $[a,b]$, write
$$ z_k=a+y_k,\qquad y_k\in\{0,\ldots,d\},\qquad y_1\ne y_2. $$
Its social cost is
$$ d(R_1+R_2)+s_1y_1+s_2y_2. $$

The mechanism chooses the lexicographically smallest minimizer of
$$ s_1y_1+s_2y_2 $$
over these feasible integer pairs. It is therefore optimal for the original problem.

The monotonicity properties needed for strategyproofness

The selected pair is given explicitly by
$$ (y_1,y_2)= \begin{cases} (0,1),&s_1,s_2>0,\ s_1\ge s_2,\\ (1,0),&s_1,s_2>0,\ s_1<s_2,\\ (0,d),&s_1\ge0,\ s_2<0,\\ (d,0),&s_1<0,\ s_2\ge0,\\ (d-1,d),&s_1,s_2<0,\ s_1\ge s_2,\\ (d,d-1),&s_1,s_2<0,\ s_1<s_2,\\ (0,1),&s_1>0,\ s_2=0,\\ (1,0),&s_1=0,\ s_2>0,\\ (0,1),&s_1=s_2=0. \end{cases} $$

For two positive coefficients, a minimum must use the two smallest distinct coordinates; comparing their two assignments gives the first two cases. For two negative coefficients, it must use the two largest distinct coordinates, giving the fifth and sixth cases. Opposite signs minimize at opposite endpoints. The remaining cases follow from lexicographic tie-breaking. This also verifies the formula when $d=1$.

Three monotonicity properties follow.

First, $y_k$ is nonincreasing in its own coefficient $s_k$. For example, increase $s_1$ by $h>0$, with old minimizer $y$ and new minimizer $y'$. Optimality at the two coefficient vectors gives
$$ s_1y_1+s_2y_2\le s_1y'_1+s_2y'_2 $$
and
$$ (s_1+h)y'_1+s_2y'_2 \le(s_1+h)y_1+s_2y_2. $$
Adding yields $hy'_1\le hy_1$.

Second, each coordinate is nondecreasing in the other coefficient. For $y_1$, the displayed rule simplifies to
$$ y_1= \begin{cases} 0,&s_1\ge0,\ s_2\le s_1,\\ 1,&s_1\ge0,\ s_2>s_1,\\ d-1,&s_1<0,\ s_2\le s_1,\\ d,&s_1<0,\ s_2>s_1. \end{cases} $$
This is nondecreasing in $s_2$ for fixed $s_1$.

For $y_2$, if $s_2>0$, it changes from $0$ to $1$ as $s_1$ reaches $s_2$; if $s_2=0$, it changes from $0$ to $1$ as $s_1$ reaches zero; and if $s_2<0$, it changes from $d-1$ to $d$ as $s_1$ reaches $s_2$. Thus it is nondecreasing in $s_1$.

Third, $y_1+y_2$ is nonincreasing in either coefficient. Indeed,
$$ y_1+y_2= \begin{cases} 1,&s_1\ge0,\ s_2\ge0,\\ 2d-1,&s_1<0,\ s_2<0,\\ d,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
Increasing either coefficient can only move this value downward, since
$$ 1\le d\le2d-1. $$

Strategyproofness

Let $h=1/q$.

An agent at $a$ approving only facility $1$ wants to minimize $y_1$. Reporting both facilities increases $s_2$ by $h$, which cannot decrease $y_1$ by the second property. Reporting only facility $2$ decreases $s_1$ and increases $s_2$; each change weakly increases $y_1$, by the first and second properties. The argument for a singleton supporter of facility $2$ is symmetric.

An agent at $a$ approving both facilities wants to minimize $y_1+y_2$. Dropping either approval decreases the corresponding coefficient, so the third property shows that her cost cannot decrease.

At $b$, a singleton supporter wants to maximize the corresponding $y_k$. Adding the other approval decreases the other coefficient, which cannot increase $y_k$. Switching singleton reports increases the own coefficient and decreases the other coefficient; both changes weakly decrease $y_k$.

Finally, an agent at $b$ approving both facilities has cost
$$ 2d-(y_1+y_2). $$
Dropping an approval increases its coefficient, which weakly decreases the sum and therefore weakly increases her cost.

These cases exhaust the permitted deviations. The optimal rule is strategyproof.

Single occupied node and consequences

If only node $x$ is occupied, let $w_k$ be the mass approving facility $k$. Choose a fixed adjacent node and orient this pair to put the facility with larger supporter mass at $x$.

Every feasible outcome has cost at least $\min\{w_1,w_2\}$, because at least one facility has integer distance at least one from $x$. The chosen orientation attains this bound. Strategyproofness follows from the fixed-pair orientation argument in Section 3.

The mechanism can use these optimal rules when the public support has at most two nodes, and the mechanism of Section 3 otherwise. Public support is invariant under approval deviations, so this combined mechanism remains strategyproof.

The rules for at most two occupied nodes require $O(\tau)$ exact rational arithmetic operations, or
$$ O\!\left(\tau(L+\log m)^2\right) $$
bit operations. They require no population expansion.

Every profile in $\mathcal D_1$ or $\mathcal D_2$ occupies at most two nodes. Thus the combined mechanism is optimal throughout each of these domains, proving
$$ \rho_1(P_m)=\rho_2(P_m)=1. $$

6. What remains open

The unresolved alternatives are therefore precise:

  • Prove that every finite denominator admits an admissible $4/3$-approximation.
  • Or exhibit a finite denominator for which every admissible mechanism has ratio strictly greater than $4/3$.

The optimal rule above resolves all public supports of size at most two. The remaining obstruction or construction must address public populations occupying at least three nodes.
