The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), posts \(P\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), weak rankings and eligibility by type, and type-symmetric post rank functions \(\rho_p\), decide whether there is a measurable assignment \(f\) giving each individual one eligible post such that, for every feasible assignment \(g\), \(V(f,g)\geq V(g,f)\); return such an \(f\) if one exists. Feasibility requires \(\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,p}\leq\rho_p(S)\) for each post \(p\) and \(S\subseteq T\), where \(x_{t,p}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(P\) contain \(m\) posts. Each type has an eligible set \(E_t\subseteq P\) and a weak ranking on it, with ties allowed.
Each type has a distinct last-resort post \(\ell_t\), eligible only for that type and strictly worse than every other eligible post; \(E_t\setminus\{\ell_t\}\neq\varnothing\). Every post is eligible for some type.
For each post, a Boolean circuit evaluates a rational-valued, normalized, nondecreasing submodular function \(\rho_p:2^T\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\), outputting a binary numerator and positive denominator. These properties are promised. Require \(\rho_p(\{t\})>0\) exactly when \(p\in E_t\), and \(\rho_{\ell_t}(S)=\mu_t\mathbf{1}_{t\in S}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including the circuits.
Realize the society as the disjoint union \(\Omega=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}(\{t\}\times[0,\mu_t))\), with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\). An assignment is a measurable function \(f:\Omega\to P\) with \(f(t,u)\in E_t\) almost everywhere; a type may be split among posts.
Write \(x_{t,p}(f)=\lambda(\{(t,u):f(t,u)=p\})\). Feasibility means \(\sum_{t\in S}x_{t,p}(f)\leq\rho_p(S)\) for every \(p\in P\) and \(S\subseteq T\).
Define \(V(f,g)=\sum_{t\in T}\lambda(\{(t,u):f(t,u)\succ_t g(t,u)\})\); indifference contributes no vote. A feasible assignment \(f\) is popular if \(V(f,g)\geq V(g,f)\) for every feasible measurable assignment \(g\) on the same individuals.
Is deciding whether a popular assignment exists polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can a popular assignment also be computed whenever one exists?
The discrete existence-and-construction problem with applicant ties, private last resorts, and post matroids is polynomial-time solvable ([Kamiyama](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/15M104918X)).
Popular mixed matchings under one-sided preferences and ties always exist and are polynomial-time computable, but use expected votes between lotteries ([Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304397510001659)); no result settling the pointwise measurable-assignment problem above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the population atomless by realizing each type as a Lebesgue interval of its prescribed mass; assignments and challengers act on the same individuals and may split types.
  • Defined the omitted vote function by the mass of individuals strictly preferring their assigned post, with zero votes for ties.
  • Interpreted continuous post ranks as normalized, nondecreasing rational submodular functions, with positive singleton rank for every eligible type and zero singleton rank for ineligible types.
  • Specified compact rank-function input by Boolean evaluation circuits with binary-rational outputs and promised rank properties; included their descriptions in L.
  • Carried over private last resorts as distinct type-private posts with capacity equal to the type's entire mass, strictly ranked last; retained at least one other eligible post per type.
  • Defined m as the number of posts, including last resorts, and excluded posts eligible for no type.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Whenever a popular assignment exists, one can compute one, represented by a partition of each type interval into finitely many intervals with rational endpoints.

The proof uses Kamiyama’s discrete characterization, but does not run his algorithm on an expanded population. Instead, the relevant information in that expansion is computed using a graph with at most \(2m\tau\) vertices and submodular minimization.

1. Aggregate assignments and pointwise voting

Let \(X\) be the polytope of matrices satisfying

\(x_{t,p}\geq 0,\qquad x_{t,p}=0\text{ if }p\notin E_t,\qquad \sum_{p\in E_t}x_{t,p}=\mu_t,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in S}x_{t,p}\leq\rho_p(S)\qquad(p\in P,\ S\subseteq T).\)

Every feasible measurable assignment gives a matrix in \(X\). Conversely, every matrix in \(X\) can be realized by partitioning each interval \([0,\mu_t)\) into intervals of lengths \(x_{t,p}\).

Define the challenger’s vote coefficient by

\(\displaystyle c_t(p,q)=\mathbf 1_{\{q\succ_t p\}}-\mathbf 1_{\{p\succ_t q\}}.\)

For \(x\in X\), consider the following linear program, whose variables are \(z_{t,p,q}\) for \(p,q\in E_t\):

\(\displaystyle H(x)=\max_z\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{p,q\in E_t}c_t(p,q)z_{t,p,q},\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle z_{t,p,q}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{q\in E_t}z_{t,p,q}=x_{t,p},\)

and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in S}\sum_{p\in E_t}z_{t,p,q}\leq\rho_q(S)\qquad(q\in P,\ S\subseteq T),\)

where nonexistent variables contribute zero.

Lemma 1. For any feasible assignment \(f\) with aggregate matrix \(x\),

\(\displaystyle H(x)=\max_{g\text{ feasible}}\bigl(V(g,f)-V(f,g)\bigr).\)

Consequently, popularity depends only on \(x\), and \(f\) is popular exactly when \(H(x)=0\).

Proof. Given \(f,g\), put

\(\displaystyle z_{t,p,q}=\lambda\bigl(\{(t,u):f(t,u)=p,\ g(t,u)=q\}\bigr).\)

These values satisfy the displayed constraints, and their objective value is \(V(g,f)-V(f,g)\).

Conversely, take any feasible \(z\). For each \(t,p\), partition the measurable set on which \(f=p\) into measurable pieces of masses \(z_{t,p,q}\), and assign the corresponding piece to \(q\). Such partitions exist because Lebesgue measure is nonatomic. Explicitly, for a measurable subset \(A\subseteq[0,\mu_t)\), the function \(v\mapsto\lambda(A\cap[0,v))\) is continuous, so successive thresholds produce pieces of any prescribed masses summing to \(\lambda(A)\).

The resulting assignment \(g\) has the required aggregate masses and vote difference. Finally, the diagonal choice \(z_{t,p,p}=x_{t,p}\) is feasible and has value zero, so \(H(x)\geq0\). ∎

An important consequence removes any need to search over irrational matrices.

Lemma 2. If a popular assignment exists, a popular assignment with a rational aggregate matrix exists.

Proof. The function \(H\) is concave on \(X\): if \(z^1,z^2\) are optimal challengers for \(x^1,x^2\), then \(\alpha z^1+(1-\alpha)z^2\) is a feasible challenger for \(\alpha x^1+(1-\alpha)x^2\). Hence

\(\displaystyle H(\alpha x^1+(1-\alpha)x^2)\geq\alpha H(x^1)+(1-\alpha)H(x^2).\)

The polytope \(X\) is bounded and rational. Write a popular matrix as a convex combination of its vertices:

\(\displaystyle x=\sum_i\alpha_i v^i,\qquad \alpha_i>0,\qquad \sum_i\alpha_i=1.\)

Concavity and nonnegativity give

\(\displaystyle 0=H(x)\geq\sum_i\alpha_iH(v^i)\geq0.\)

Thus every \(v^i\) in this combination is popular. Each vertex is rational. ∎

2. An exact discrete expansion for rational matrices

Fix a rational matrix \(x\in X\). Choose an integer \(D\) clearing the denominators of all masses, all entries of \(x\), and all values \(\rho_p(S)\).

This integer is used only in the proof. Its bit length need not be polynomial.

Replace type \(t\) by \(N_t=D\mu_t\) individual clones. A non-last-resort post \(p\) accepts a set \(J\) of eligible clones exactly when

\(\displaystyle |J\cap A(S)|\leq D\rho_p(S)\qquad(S\subseteq T),\)

where \(A(S)\) denotes the clones of types in \(S\). Give each clone its own private last resort, representing its type’s original last resort.

The post constraints are matroids

Write \(R(S)=D\rho_p(S)\). Heredity and inclusion of the empty set are immediate.

For exchange, let \(I,J\) satisfy the constraints and suppose \(|I|<|J|\). If no clone in \(J\setminus I\) can be added to \(I\), then for each such clone of type \(t\), some set \(S_t\ni t\) is tight:

\(\displaystyle |I\cap A(S_t)|=R(S_t).\)

This follows because both sides are integers. Tight sets are closed under union: subtracting the modular function \(S\mapsto|I\cap A(S)|\) from the submodular function \(R\) gives a nonnegative submodular function, whose zero sets are closed under union.

Let \(S\) be the union of these tight sets. Then

\(\displaystyle |J\cap A(S)|\leq R(S)=|I\cap A(S)|.\)

Outside \(A(S)\), every member of \(J\) belongs to \(I\). Therefore \(|J|\leq|I|\), a contradiction. This proves the matroid exchange axiom.

Every eligible singleton is independent: \(R(\{t\})\) is a positive integer, and monotonicity handles every larger set containing \(t\). Thus the expansion meets the singleton and private-last-resort hypotheses of the discrete theorem.

Fractional challengers and discrete challengers agree

Lemma 3. A complete clone matching with aggregate matrix \(x\) is popular in the discrete instance if and only if \(H(x)=0\).

Proof. Scale the challenger program in Section 1 by \(D\), writing \(w=Dz\). On the ground set \(W\) of eligible triples \((t,p,q)\), define

\(\displaystyle r_1(U)=\sum_{\substack{(t,p):\\\exists q,\ (t,p,q)\in U}}Dx_{t,p},\)

and

\(\displaystyle r_2(U)=\sum_{q\in P}D\rho_q\bigl(\{t:\exists p,\ (t,p,q)\in U\}\bigr).\)

Both functions are normalized, nondecreasing, integer-valued and submodular. For \(r_1\), this follows from its expression as a nonnegative weighted sum of coverage indicators. For \(r_2\), the type set of a union is the union of the type sets, whereas the type set of an intersection is contained in their intersection; submodularity and monotonicity of each \(\rho_q\) then give the required inequality.

Let

\(\displaystyle P(r)=\{w\geq0:w(U)\leq r(U)\text{ for every }U\subseteq W\}.\)

The constraints of \(P(r_1)\) are equivalent to the source-row upper bounds \(Dx_{t,p}\). The constraints of \(P(r_2)\) are equivalent to the destination-post constraints in the scaled challenger program. For example, an arbitrary coordinate subset at post \(q\) is bounded by the constraint on its represented types; conversely, taking all coordinates at \(q\) from types in \(S\) gives the original constraint for \(S\).

The scaled challenger polytope is therefore the face

\(\displaystyle \{w\in P(r_1)\cap P(r_2):w(W)=D\}.\)

Indeed, the source-row capacities sum to \(D\), so this equality forces every source-row upper bound to be attained.

By Edmonds’ integral polymatroid intersection theorem, the intersection, and hence this face, has integral vertices. Thus its linear voting objective has an integral optimum. See the integral-intersection theorem in [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10155/10155D.pdf).

An integral optimum assigns integer numbers of clones from each source group \((t,p)\) to each destination \(q\). It can be realized for any fixed clone matching with aggregate \(x\); destinations corresponding to a last resort mean the receiving clone’s own private last resort. The post constraints make this a feasible clone matching.

Therefore a positive measurable challenger exists exactly when a positive discrete challenger exists. ∎

3. The discrete characterization used

Here is the precise part of Kamiyama’s characterization needed below.

Let \(\Gamma\) contain all first-choice edges. Let \(\mathcal B\) and \(\mathcal Q\) be, respectively, the applicant partition matroid and the direct sum of post matroids, restricted to \(\Gamma\). Choose a maximum common independent set \(I\).

For \(d\in I\), \(e\in\Gamma\setminus I\), form the directed exchange graph with arcs

\(\displaystyle d\longrightarrow e\quad\text{if }I+e\notin\mathcal I_{\mathcal B},\ I-d+e\in\mathcal I_{\mathcal B},\)

and

\(\displaystyle e\longrightarrow d\quad\text{if }I+e\notin\mathcal I_{\mathcal Q},\ I-d+e\in\mathcal I_{\mathcal Q}.\)

Let \(Z\) contain the vertices that can reach

\(\displaystyle C=\{e\in\Gamma\setminus I:I+e\in\mathcal I_{\mathcal Q}\},\)

and put \(K=\Gamma\setminus Z\). For each applicant, retain its highest-ranked non-first-choice edges that are nonloops after contracting \(K\) in the full post matroid.

A complete matching is popular exactly when its first-choice part has size \(|I|\) and every edge is either first choice or retained in this way. This is [Kamiyama’s Theorem 9](https://api.lib.kyushu-u.ac.jp/opac_download_md/1525809/MI2015-6.pdf).

The expansion in Section 2 satisfies all its hypotheses. The remaining task is to compute this characterization without creating clones.

4. Computing the first-choice decomposition without clones

For type \(t\), let \(F_t\) be its highest-ranked eligible posts. Private last resorts never belong to \(F_t\).

Compute a rational optimum \(a\) of

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\max\sum_t\sum_{p\in F_t}a_{t,p},\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle a_{t,p}\geq0,\qquad a_{t,p}=0\text{ if }p\notin F_t,\qquad \sum_{p\in F_t}a_{t,p}\leq\mu_t,\)

and the post constraints

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in S}a_{t,p}\leq\rho_p(S).\)

This linear program is polynomial-time solvable. To separate the constraints at post \(p\), minimize the submodular function

\(\displaystyle h_p(S)=\rho_p(S)-\sum_{t\in S}a_{t,p}.\)

A negative minimum supplies a violated inequality. Rational submodular minimization and exact rational optimization from separation have polynomial bit complexity; the necessary oracle results are developed in [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Chapter 10](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf).

For the proof, choose \(D\) also clearing the denominators of \(a\). Allocate \(Da_{t,p}\) clones of type \(t\) to each first-choice post \(p\), obtaining a common independent set \(I\). It is maximum: any larger clone first-choice set, divided by \(D\), would contradict optimality of \(\kappa\). Thus \(|I|=D\kappa\).

Tight-set tests

For every first-choice pair \((t,p)\), compute

\(\displaystyle d_p(t)=\min_{S\ni t}h_p(S).\)

For distinct \(t,s\), compute

\(\displaystyle d_p(t;s)=\min_{\substack{S\ni t\\s\notin S}}h_p(S).\)

All these minima are nonnegative. They are polynomial-time computable: fixing some elements inside and outside a set leaves a submodular minimization problem on the remaining elements.

Because \(D\) clears all relevant denominators, every positive slack is at least \(D^{-1}\). Consequently:

  • Adding a clone of type \(t\) at \(p\) is possible exactly when \(d_p(t)>0\).
  • When addition is blocked, replacing a selected clone of type \(s\) by one of type \(t\) is possible exactly when \(s=t\), or \(d_p(t;s)>0\).

For the second assertion with \(s\neq t\), the only constraints whose left side increases are those containing \(t\) and excluding \(s\). A replacement is feasible exactly when none of them is tight. A same-type replacement leaves every count unchanged.

The compressed graph

Create the following vertices whenever their defining conditions hold:

  • \(v^1_{t,p}\) if \(p\in F_t\) and \(a_{t,p}>0\);
  • \(v^0_{t,p}\) if \(p\in F_t\) and \(a_{t,p}<\mu_t\).

They represent, respectively, selected and unselected first-choice clone edges of that type–post pair.

Add arcs

\(\displaystyle v^1_{t,p}\longrightarrow v^0_{t,q}\qquad(p\neq q),\)

whenever both vertices exist, and

\(\displaystyle v^0_{t,p}\longrightarrow v^1_{s,p}\)

when

\(\displaystyle d_p(t)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl(s=t\text{ or }d_p(t;s)>0\bigr).\)

Let the target vertices be

\(\displaystyle \widehat C=\{v^0_{t,p}:d_p(t)>0\},\)

and let \(\widehat Z\) be all vertices that can reach \(\widehat C\).

Lemma 4. A first-choice clone edge belongs to \(Z\) exactly when its representing vertex belongs to \(\widehat Z\).

Proof. Post-exchange arcs between two represented groups are either absent or complete between those groups, by the tight-set tests. Target membership is also uniform within a group.

For an applicant-exchange arc, every selected clone edge at \(p\) has an arc to that same clone’s unselected edge at every other first-choice post \(q\). Thus every member of the selected source group has an arc into the represented unselected target group.

Start with the target groups and take backward reachability. These observations prove inductively that every compressed marked group consists entirely of expanded vertices reaching \(C\).

Conversely, every expanded arc projects to one of the displayed compressed arcs. Every expanded path to \(C\) therefore projects to a compressed path to \(\widehat C\). ∎

For \(p\in F_t\), define

\(\displaystyle k_{t,p}=a_{t,p}\mathbf 1_{\{v^1_{t,p}\notin\widehat Z\}}+(\mu_t-a_{t,p})\mathbf 1_{\{v^0_{t,p}\notin\widehat Z\}},\)

with a missing vertex contributing zero. Put \(k_{t,p}=0\) when \(p\notin F_t\).

Lemma 4 shows that \(Dk_{t,p}\) is exactly the number of type-\(t\) edges in \(K\) at post \(p\). This conclusion does not require the two states of a pair to have the same reachability status.

5. Computing the retained second choices

We first establish the rank formula needed for contraction.

Lemma 5. In a clone post matroid with capacities \(R(S)\), if an edge set \(U\) contains \(n_t\) clones of type \(t\), then

\(\displaystyle r(U)=\min_{S\subseteq T}\left(R(S)+\sum_{t\notin S}n_t\right).\)

Proof. Every independent subset of \(U\) has at most \(R(S)\) members of types in \(S\), and at most \(\sum_{t\notin S}n_t\) other members. This proves the upper bound.

For equality, take a maximal independent subset \(B\subseteq U\). For every excluded clone, choose a tight set blocking its addition. Their union \(S\) is tight, by the tight-union argument in Section 2, and every member of \(U\) outside \(A(S)\) belongs to \(B\). Hence

\(\displaystyle |B|=R(S)+\sum_{t\notin S}n_t.\)

If \(B=U\), use \(S=\varnothing\). ∎

For every post \(p\), compute

\(\displaystyle b_p=\min_{S\subseteq T}\left(\rho_p(S)+\sum_{s\notin S}k_{s,p}\right),\)

and, for each type \(t\),

\(\displaystyle b_p(t)=\min_{S\ni t}\left(\rho_p(S)+\sum_{s\notin S}k_{s,p}\right).\)

These are again submodular minimizations.

By Lemma 5, the rank of \(K\) at \(p\) is \(Db_p\). For a non-first-choice edge \(e\) of type \(t\) at \(p\), adding \(e\) increases the expression in the rank formula by one exactly for sets not containing \(t\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle e\text{ is a nonloop after contracting }K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b_p(t)>b_p.\)

Indeed, equality supplies a minimizing set containing \(t\), so rank does not increase. If the inequality is strict, every old minimizing set excludes \(t\), while every expression from a set containing \(t\) exceeds the old minimum by at least one after scaling by \(D\); rank therefore increases by one.

For each type, define

\(\displaystyle A_t=\{p\in E_t\setminus F_t:b_p(t)>b_p\},\)

and let \(G_t\) be the highest-ranked posts in \(A_t\).

The set \(A_t\) is nonempty. At \(\ell_t\), there are no first-choice edges, so all \(k_{s,\ell_t}\) vanish. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle b_{\ell_t}=0,\qquad b_{\ell_t}(t)=\mu_t>0.\)

In the clone model, the corresponding statement holds for each clone’s private last resort. Every clone of type \(t\) therefore has retained second choices represented precisely by \(G_t\).

6. The final linear program and correctness

Solve the following feasibility problem:

\(\displaystyle x\in X,\)

\(\displaystyle x_{t,p}=0\qquad\text{if }p\notin F_t\cup G_t,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{p\in F_t}x_{t,p}=\kappa.\)

Lemma 6. For every rational \(x\in X\), this system holds exactly when \(x\) is popular.

Proof. Choose \(D\) clearing all capacity values, masses, and entries of both \(a\) and \(x\). The first-choice common independent set constructed from \(a\) has maximum size \(D\kappa\).

Sections 4 and 5 compute exactly the contracted nonloops and retained second choices in Kamiyama’s characterization. A complete clone matching representing \(x\) thus satisfies that characterization exactly when it satisfies the displayed system.

By Lemma 3, its discrete popularity is equivalent to pointwise measurable popularity of \(x\). ∎

We can now decide existence.

If a popular measurable assignment exists, Lemma 2 supplies a rational popular matrix. Lemma 6 shows that this matrix satisfies the final system.

Conversely, if the final system is feasible, it is a nonempty rational polytope and therefore contains a rational point. Computing such a point and applying Lemma 6 produces a popular assignment.

Running time and output representation

The first and final linear programs have at most \(m\tau\) variables. Both admit polynomial-time strong separation by submodular minimization. The compressed graph has at most \(2m\tau\) vertices; its arcs and all contraction tests require only polynomially many constrained submodular minimizations on \(T\).

The required bit bounds also hold:

  • Circuit evaluation takes polynomial time, and every circuit output has bit length bounded by \(L\).
  • The defining linear inequalities have polynomially bounded coefficient lengths.
  • Rational vertices of these bounded polytopes have polynomially bounded encoding lengths: an independent active subsystem contains at most \(m\tau\) equations, and the usual determinant bounds apply.
  • The functions used in subsequent minimizations add or subtract sums of polynomially many such rationals.

Thus exact separation, optimization, graph construction and feasibility all take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No common denominator \(D\), clone population, or expanded matroid is constructed by the algorithm.

Finally, for the returned rational matrix \(x\), partition each type interval into consecutive intervals of lengths \(x_{t,p}\). This gives a popular measurable assignment using at most \(m\tau\) intervals, with polynomially encoded rational endpoints.

7. Existence is not automatic

For completeness, the answer does not imply that every society has a popular assignment.

Take one type of mass \(1\), with ranking \(a\succ b\succ\ell\), capacities \(\rho_a(\{t\})=\rho_b(\{t\})=1/3\), and the required last-resort capacity \(1\).

A popular assignment must fill both \(a\) and \(b\): otherwise some last-resort individuals can move to an unfilled better post without anyone losing. Its three aggregate masses must therefore all equal \(1/3\).

Now send the individuals at \(a\) to \(\ell\), those at \(b\) to \(a\), and those at \(\ell\) to \(b\). Every aggregate mass remains unchanged, so feasibility is preserved. The challenger gains votes of mass \(2/3\) and loses votes of mass \(1/3\), contradicting popularity.

This also illustrates why pointwise voting requires the challenger analysis above.
