The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), parliament size \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau)\) over complete ranking types \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\), ordered so that for every candidate pair \(a,b\), the types preferring \(a\) to \(b\) form a prefix, choose \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=k\) and masses \(x_{i,c}\ge 0\) satisfying \(x_{i,c}=0\) for \(c\notin S\), \(\sum_{c\in S}x_{i,c}=\mu_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{i,c}=1/k\) for each \(c\in S\), minimizing \(\sum_i\sum_{c\in S}x_{i,c}\alpha_B(\operatorname{pos}_{t_i}(c))\); output an optimal parliament and allocation.

The supplied type order witnesses single-crossing: for every pair \(a,b\) with \(a\succ_{t_1}b\), the indices \(i\) satisfying \(a\succ_{t_i}b\) form a prefix.

Result

The specified continuous utilitarian Monroe problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Consequently, an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The key is that the cited discrete hardness construction satisfies an additional property: its number of voters is divisible by its committee size. On those instances, allowing divisible allocations does not improve the optimal value, even after identical rankings are merged into distinct support types.

1. An integrality lemma

Lemma. Let \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\) and \(q\) be positive integers satisfying \(\sum_i p_i=kq\). Fix a committee \(S\) of size \(k\) and real costs \(d_{i,c}\). The optimization problem

\[ \min \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\sum_{c\in S}d_{i,c}y_{i,c} \]

subject to

\[ y_{i,c}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{c\in S}y_{i,c}=p_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}y_{i,c}=q \]

has an integral optimal solution.

Proof. The feasible set is nonempty: assigning \(y_{i,c}=p_i/k\) satisfies every constraint. It is closed and bounded, since every variable lies between zero and \(p_i\). Therefore an optimum exists. Choose an optimum \(y\) with the fewest positive entries.

Form a bipartite graph whose vertices are the types and committee members, with an edge wherever \(y_{i,c}>0\). Suppose this graph contains a cycle. Its length is even. Add \(\varepsilon\) and subtract \(\varepsilon\) alternately around the cycle. This preserves every row sum and column sum.

Both signs of sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\) are feasible. Since the objective is linear and \(y\) is optimal, the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\) must be zero; otherwise one sign would improve the objective. Thus every feasible perturbation in this direction preserves optimality. Taking positive \(\varepsilon\) equal to the smallest entry on a subtracting edge removes at least one positive entry and introduces none. This contradicts the choice of \(y\).

The support graph is therefore a forest. In any component containing an edge, take a leaf. Its unique incident edge has value equal to that vertex’s prescribed sum, which is an integer. Delete the leaf and its edge, subtracting this integer from the neighboring vertex’s prescribed sum. All remaining prescribed sums remain integers. Repeating this argument proves that every positive entry is integral. \(\square\)

2. Exact equivalence on divisible discrete electorates

Consider an explicit discrete electorate with \(N\) voters, committee size \(K\), and \(K\mid N\). Put \(q=N/K\). Suppose its distinct ranking types are \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\), with multiplicities \(n_1,\ldots,n_\tau\).

Construct a continuous instance on the same candidates and with the same committee size by assigning

\[ \mu_i=\frac{n_i}{N}. \]

Fix any committee \(S\) of size \(K\), and define

\[ d_{i,c}=\operatorname{pos}_{t_i}(c)-1. \]

Positions here are in the original full rankings; no candidates are removed.

Scale a continuous allocation by setting \(y_{i,c}=Nx_{i,c}\). Its constraints become

\[ \sum_{c\in S}y_{i,c}=n_i,\qquad \sum_i y_{i,c}=\frac{N}{K}=q,\qquad y_{i,c}\geq 0. \]

Furthermore,

\[ N D(S,x)=\sum_i\sum_{c\in S}d_{i,c}y_{i,c}. \]

By the lemma, this scaled problem has an integral optimum.

An integral feasible \(y\) gives a discrete Monroe assignment: for each type \(i\), partition its \(n_i\) voters into groups of sizes \(y_{i,c}\), assigning each group to its corresponding candidate. Each voter receives exactly one representative, and each elected candidate receives exactly \(q\) voters.

Conversely, every discrete Monroe assignment yields an integral feasible \(y\) by counting assignments within each ranking type. Its dissatisfaction is precisely the scaled objective above.

Thus, writing \(F_{\mathrm{disc}}(S)\) and \(F_{\mathrm{cont}}(S)\) for the respective optimal values with committee \(S\), we obtain

\[ F_{\mathrm{cont}}(S)=\frac{F_{\mathrm{disc}}(S)}{N} \qquad\text{for every }S\text{ with }|S|=K. \]

Taking minima over committees shows that the two problems have exactly the same optimal committees. This conclusion applies to any continuous optimal committee, regardless of whether the allocation returned with it is integral.

3. Merging identical rankings preserves single-crossing

Suppose the explicit voter order witnesses single-crossing.

First, every ranking type occurs in one contiguous block. Indeed, suppose voters at indices \(r<s\) have identical rankings. For any pair of candidates, its relative order can change at most once along the voter order. Since its order agrees at both endpoints, it agrees throughout the interval from \(r\) to \(s\). Applying this to every pair shows that all intermediate voters have the same ranking.

Replace each such block by its ranking type and assign that type its multiplicity divided by \(N\). The resulting types are distinct, their masses are positive rational numbers summing to one, and their order witnesses single-crossing: a prefix of voters preferring one candidate to another becomes a prefix of ranking blocks.

This compression is polynomial-time. There are at most \(N\) types, and each mass \(n_i/N\) has binary encoding length \(O(\log N)\). Hence the continuous input has bit length polynomial in the explicit discrete input length. The reduction never expands a binary-encoded population.

4. The cited hardness construction satisfies divisibility

We use the established discrete hardness theorem: finding a utilitarian Borda Monroe winning committee is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard on single-crossing electorates. The required divisibility follows from the construction proving Theorem 11. [Skowron, Yu, Faliszewski and Elkind, Theorem 11 and its construction, pp. 10–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.1252).

Here is the count explicitly. Write \(m_0,n_0,k_0\) for the source parameters and \(q_0=n_0/k_0\), which is an integer. Let \(a,b\) be the sizes of the auxiliary candidate collections underlying voter blocks \(V_2,V_5\), and put \(r=a+b\). Besides these candidates, the construction has \(m_0-k_0\) candidates in \(H\) and \(m_0\) original candidates. Its committee size is therefore

\[ K=r+m_0. \]

The five voter blocks have sizes

\[ (m_0-k_0)q_0,\quad a(q_0+1),\quad m_0,\quad n_0,\quad b(q_0+1). \]

Consequently their total is

\[ N=(m_0-k_0)q_0+r(q_0+1)+m_0+n_0 =(r+m_0)(q_0+1)=K(q_0+1). \]

Thus \(K\mid N\). This count strengthens the cited hardness statement to the divisible subfamily needed here.

5. Reduction and conclusion

Take an instance from that \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard discrete subfamily. Merge identical rankings and give each resulting type mass \(n_i/N\). Sections 2 and 3 prove that this is a valid instance of the specified continuous problem, computable in polynomial time, and that its optimal committees are precisely the discrete optimal committees.

Therefore, an exact polynomial-time algorithm returning any optimal continuous parliament and allocation would return a discrete optimal parliament on an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard family. This proves the claimed hardness.

The argument uses integrality only after fixing a committee. It does not relax committee selection. Divisible representation therefore preserves the combinatorial difficulty of choosing the winners.
