The continuous mirror question, as graded

For \(n\) items, \(k\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, each pairwise observation is absent with probability \(1-p\); otherwise, a fresh ranking drawn from \(\mu\) reports which item it prefers. The distribution is hidden from the estimator. Characterize the number \(r\) of trials per pair needed to recover, with high probability, the \(k\) items maximizing \(s_i(\mu)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j \Pr_{t\sim\mu}[i\succ_t j]\), in terms of the gap \(\Delta_k=s_{(k)}-s_{(k+1)}\), including matching upper and lower bounds.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m=n\ge2\), \(1\le k<n\), \(p\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1]\), and \(r\ge1\) be an integer. Types are strict complete rankings of the items.
A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their binary rational masses, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total representation bit length.
This representation specifies the hidden distribution \(\mu\); the estimator receives only \(n,k,p,r\) and the observations, never the support, masses, or sampled rankings.
Independently for every unordered pair and every trial, no comparison occurs with probability \(1-p\); otherwise a fresh independent ranking from \(\mu\) supplies the winner.
For \(i\ne j\), put \(M_{ij}=\Pr_{t\sim\mu}[i\succ_t j]\), set \(M_{ii}=1/2\), and define \(s_i=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^n M_{ij}\). Write the scores in decreasing order as \(s_{(1)},\ldots,s_{(n)}\).
Assume \(\Delta_k=s_{(k)}-s_{(k+1)}>0\), so the true top set \(S_k^*\) is unique; ties within either side are permitted. Any empirical score ties are resolved by increasing item index.
For a positive rational gap bound \(d\) with a nonempty instance class, let \(r^*(n,k,p,d)\) be the smallest positive integer number of trials permitting an estimator with \(\sup_{\mu:\Delta_k\ge d}\Pr_\mu[\widehat S_k\ne S_k^*]\le n^{-14}\), where the supremum ranges over all finite-support rational societies.
What is \(r^*(n,k,p,d)\), up to universal constant factors, including matching upper and lower bounds?
State estimator running times in \(m=n,\tau,L,k,r\) and the bit lengths of the numerical parameters.
For unrestricted pairwise-probability matrices, selecting the items with the most observed wins has error at most \(n^{-14}\) when \(\Delta_k\ge8\sqrt{\log n/(npr)}\); every estimator has worst-case error at least \(1/7\) on the class \(\Delta_k\ge(1/7)\sqrt{\log n/(npr)}\) when \(n\ge7\) and \(npr\ge(\log n)/2\). [Shah–Wainwright](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume18/16-206/16-206.pdf)
The upper guarantee includes ranking-induced matrices; the stated lower guarantee also holds within the Bradley–Terry–Luce class, but is formulated over matrices rather than explicitly encoded rational societies. [Shah–Wainwright](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume18/16-206/16-206.pdf)

Result. Write \(\log\) for the natural logarithm. The instance class is nonempty exactly when \(0<d\le 1/2\). Throughout this range,

\[ r^*(n,k,p,d)=\Theta\!\left(\max\left\{1,\frac{\log n}{npd^2}\right\}\right), \]

with universal constants, uniformly in \(1\le k<n\). More explicitly, putting \(T=\log n/(npd^2)\), we prove

\[ \max\{1,T/16\}\le r^*(n,k,p,d)\le \lceil64T\rceil. \]

The upper bound uses observed-win counting. The lower bound already holds for rational societies supported on at most four rankings.

Feasible gaps and compact rational societies.

Let \(A=S_k^*\), let \(B\) be its complement, and put \(\ell=n-k\) and \(C=\sum_{i\in A,j\in B}M_{ij}\). Strict complete rankings imply \(M_{ij}+M_{ji}=1\). Consequently,

\[ \sum_{i\in A}s_i=\frac{k^2/2+C}{n}, \qquad \sum_{j\in B}s_j=\frac{\ell^2/2+k\ell-C}{n}. \]

Subtracting the averages gives

\[ \frac1k\sum_{i\in A}s_i-\frac1\ell\sum_{j\in B}s_j =-\frac12+\frac{C}{k\ell}\le\frac12. \]

Since the average on \(A\) is at least its minimum and the average on \(B\) is at most its maximum,

\[ \Delta_k=\min_{i\in A}s_i-\max_{j\in B}s_j\le\frac12. \]

Conversely, fix any \(k\)-element set \(A\) and any rational \(d\in(0,1/2]\). Define two ranking distributions:

  • \(U\) assigns equal mass to an arbitrary complete ranking and its reversal.
  • \(V_A\) assigns equal mass to two rankings that place every item of \(A\) above every item of \(B\); the orders within both blocks in the second ranking reverse those in the first.

Under \(U\), every pairwise winning probability is \(1/2\). Under \(V_A\), comparisons within either block have probability \(1/2\), and items of \(A\) always beat items of \(B\).

Thus the rational society

\[ \mu_A=(1-2d)U+2dV_A \]

has pairwise probabilities

\[ M^A_{ij}= \begin{cases} 1/2,&i,j\text{ lie in the same block},\\ 1/2+d,&i\in A,\ j\in B,\\ 1/2-d,&i\in B,\ j\in A. \end{cases} \]

Its scores are

\[ s_i=\frac12+\frac{\ell d}{n}\quad(i\in A), \qquad s_j=\frac12-\frac{kd}{n}\quad(j\in B). \]

Hence \(S_k^*=A\) and \(\Delta_k=d\).

Before merging duplicate rankings, the two rankings from \(U\) each have mass \(1/2-d\), and the two from \(V_A\) each have mass \(d\). Merging duplicates and removing zero masses gives a valid society with \(\tau\le4\). If \(\beta_d\) is the binary representation length of \(d\), its representation length is \(L=O(n\log n+\beta_d)\).

The endpoint can be especially easy: if \(p=1\) and \(d=1/2\), then \(r^*=1\). Indeed, equality in the gap bound forces \(C=k\ell\), so every top item always beats every bottom item. With one observation per pair, each top item wins at least \(\ell\) comparisons, while each bottom item wins at most \(\ell-1\).

Upper bound.

For each item \(i\), let \(W_i\) be its total observed wins over all trials. Return the \(k\) largest values, resolving ties by increasing item index.

Every ranking-induced matrix satisfies the probability-matrix hypotheses of Shah–Wainwright. Moreover, the fresh independent ranking for each pair and trial makes the observation law exactly their independent comparison model: each outcome has probabilities \(1-p\), \(pM_{ij}\), and \(p(1-M_{ij})\).

Their Theorem 1(a) therefore applies and gives error at most \(n^{-14}\) whenever \(\Delta_k\ge8\sqrt{\log n/(npr)}\). [Shah–Wainwright, Theorem 1(a)](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume18/16-206/16-206.pdf)

For \(r=\lceil64T\rceil\), we have \(nprd^2\ge64\log n\). Every society in the specified class consequently satisfies the theorem’s gap condition. This proves the upper bound. The estimator uses neither \(d\) nor the hidden society.

Lower bound for small gaps.

Suppose \(0<d\le1/4\). Choose \(a\in A\) and \(b\notin A\), and define \(A'=(A\setminus\{a\})\cup\{b\}\). Consider the rational societies \(\mu_A,\mu_{A'}\) constructed above. Both have gap exactly \(d\), but their true top sets differ.

Let \(P,Q\) denote their complete observation laws. Their matrices differ only on the \(2n-3\) pairs incident to \(a\) or \(b\). The probability difference is \(d\) on each of the \(2(n-2)\) pairs involving exactly one of these items, and \(2d\) on \(\{a,b\}\). Thus

\[ \sum_{i<j}(M^A_{ij}-M^{A'}_{ij})^2 =2(n-2)d^2+(2d)^2=2nd^2. \]

For Bernoulli parameters \(u,v\), the inequality \(\log x\le x-1\) gives

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\operatorname{Bern}(u)\Vert\operatorname{Bern}(v)) \le\frac{(u-v)^2}{v(1-v)}. \]

Here every parameter belongs to \([1/4,3/4]\), so the denominator is at least \(3/16\). Including the missing-comparison outcome multiplies the one-trial divergence by \(p\). Independence across pairs and trials then yields

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q) \le\frac{16}{3}pr\sum_{i<j}(M^A_{ij}-M^{A'}_{ij})^2 =\frac{32}{3}nprd^2. \]

We next justify the testing bound needed at the specified high confidence. For finite probability laws \(P,Q\), write \(\rho=\sum_x\sqrt{P(x)Q(x)}\). Jensen’s inequality gives

\[ \rho\ge e^{-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)/2}. \]

Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\le\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\). Therefore the sum of the two error probabilities of any binary test is at least

\[ 1-\operatorname{TV}(P,Q) \ge1-\sqrt{1-e^{-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)}} \ge\frac12e^{-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)}. \]

These statements also hold for randomized tests. A top-set estimator induces a binary test by choosing \(P\) exactly when it outputs \(A\). Its testing errors are bounded above by its corresponding set-recovery errors. Consequently,

\[ \max\{P[\widehat S_k\ne A],Q[\widehat S_k\ne A']\} \ge\frac14\exp\!\left(-\frac{32}{3}nprd^2\right). \]

If both errors are at most \(n^{-14}\), then

\[ \frac{32}{3}nprd^2\ge14\log n-\log4\ge12\log n, \]

where the last inequality uses \(n\ge2\). Hence every qualifying estimator requires \(r\ge(9/8)T\).

Lower bound for large gaps.

Suppose \(1/4<d\le1/2\), and use the same two rational societies.

First assume \(p\le1/2\). Let \(E\) be the event that no comparison occurs in any trial on any of the \(D=2n-3\) affected pairs. Then

\[ P(E)=Q(E)=(1-p)^{Dr}. \]

On \(E\), the observation laws are identical: all affected pairs are unobserved, and all unaffected pairs have the same probabilities under both societies. Since the correct sets differ, the sum of the estimator’s two error probabilities is at least \(P(E)\). Thus its maximum error is at least

\[ \frac12(1-p)^{Dr}\ge\frac12e^{-4npr}. \]

The last inequality follows from \(\log(1-p)\ge-2p\) for \(p\le1/2\), and \(D\le2n\). Requiring error at most \(n^{-14}\) implies

\[ 4npr\ge14\log n-\log2\ge13\log n. \]

Consequently,

\[ r\ge\frac{13}{4}\frac{\log n}{np} =\frac{13d^2}{4}T>\frac{13}{64}T. \]

Finally, suppose \(p>1/2\). Since \(d>1/4\),

\[ T<32\frac{\log n}{n}\le16. \]

Here \(\log n\le n/2\) for \(n\ge2\): the function \(x/2-\log x\) is positive at \(x=2\) and nondecreasing thereafter. The required positive-integer restriction therefore gives \(r\ge1\ge T/16\).

Combining all cases proves \(r^*\ge\max\{1,T/16\}\). Together with the upper bound, this settles the stated minimax order for every admissible parameter choice.

Estimator running time and numerical encoding.

Let \(m=n\), and let \(\beta_n,\beta_k,\beta_p,\beta_r,\beta_d\) denote the binary representation lengths of the numerical parameters, including numerator and denominator lengths for rationals. Assume observations arrive in a fixed pair-and-trial order, with a constant-size outcome symbol per position.

Counting wins and retaining the best \(k\) items in a heap takes

\[ O\!\left(m^2r+m\log(k+1)\right) \]

word operations and \(O(m)\) words of working storage. The comparison key is \((W_i,-i)\), which implements the required tie rule exactly.

All counters and indices fit in \(B=\lceil\log_2(mr+1)\rceil\) bits. A direct bit-operation bound is

\[ O\!\left( \bigl[m^2r+m\log(k+1)\bigr]B +\beta_n+\beta_k+\beta_p+\beta_r \right). \]

There is no dependence on \(\tau\) or \(L\): the society is hidden and never read. The estimator also does not read \(d\).

For an entirely rational trial-budget calculation, set \(h=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil\) and choose

\[ r_0=\left\lceil\frac{64h}{npd^2}\right\rceil. \]

Since \(\log n\le h\le2\log n/\log2\), this budget has the same order and satisfies the upper guarantee. Exact integer arithmetic computes it in time polynomial in \(\beta_n+\beta_p+\beta_d\). Estimation time depends on the numerical value of \(r\), as it must when processing \(r\binom m2\) observations.
