The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two types with rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\), \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), task-demand vectors \(d_1,d_2\), and per-tenant capacity \(c\), let \(\mathcal A\) be the saturated vectors \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2\) satisfying \(\sum_{t=1}^2\mu_t a_t d_{tr}\le c_r\) for every resource \(r\), where saturation means no coordinate can be increased by one while remaining feasible. Set \(s_t=\max_r d_{tr}/c_r\), and consider the Case II regime where \(\mathcal A\) contains vectors with \(s_1a_1>s_2a_2\) and vectors with \(s_2a_2>s_1a_1\), but none with equality. A schedule chooses finitely many \(a^j\in\mathcal A\) and durations \(\lambda_j\ge0\) summing to one; does maximizing the common average share subject to \(s_1\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_1=s_2\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_2\) always admit an optimum using at most two distinct allocation vectors?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=2\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\), where \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) resources, capacities \(c\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), and nonzero demands \(d_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state any running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
An allocation \(a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2\) assigns every tenant of type \(t\) exactly \(a_t\) tasks and is feasible when \(\sum_{t=1}^2\mu_t a_t d_{tr}\le c_r\) for every resource \(r\). Let \(\mathcal A\) contain precisely the feasible allocations for which increasing either coordinate by one violates feasibility.
Define \(s_t=\max_{1\le r\le m}d_{tr}/c_r\). Assume Case II: some allocations in \(\mathcal A\) satisfy \(s_1a_1>s_2a_2\), some satisfy the reverse inequality, and none satisfy equality.
A schedule consists of finitely many \(a^j\in\mathcal A\) and real durations \(\lambda_j\ge0\) with \(\sum_j\lambda_j=1\). Its objective is to maximize \(s_1\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_1\), subject to \(s_1\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_1=s_2\sum_j\lambda_j a^j_2\). Count only distinct allocations with positive duration.

Does every Case II instance admit an optimal schedule using at most two distinct allocation vectors, or is there a counterexample?

For two individual users with unweighted capacity constraints and integer task allocations, Zhang (2015) establishes that two allocations suffice in Case II, including when arbitrarily many slots are allowed.
No result settling this exact mass-weighted model was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpret an allocation coordinate as the integer number of tasks assigned uniformly to each tenant of that type, with capacity measured per tenant as in the supplied mass-weighted constraints.
  • Durations were unspecified beyond nonnegativity: allow real durations, and count only distinct allocation vectors receiving positive duration.
  • Define L as the total binary encoding length of masses, demands, and capacities.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Answer. Every Case II instance admits an optimal schedule using exactly \(2\) distinct allocation vectors. The mass weights do not affect this conclusion: it follows from having one fairness equality in addition to the normalization of durations.

1. Finiteness and existence of an optimum.

Since each demand vector is nonzero and all capacities are positive, \(s_t>0\) for each supported type \(t\). For any feasible allocation and any resource \(r\), nonnegativity of the other type’s consumption gives
\(\mu_t a_t d_{tr}\le c_r\).
Taking the maximum of \(d_{tr}/c_r\) over resources therefore yields
\(\mu_t a_t s_t\le1\).
Consequently,
\[ 0\le a_t\le U_t:=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{\mu_t s_t}\right\rfloor. \]
Thus the feasible allocations, and hence \(\mathcal A\), form a finite set.

Enumerate \(\mathcal A=\{a^1,\ldots,a^N\}\), and define
\(p_i=s_1a^i_1\), \(q_i=s_2a^i_2\), and \(\delta_i=p_i-q_i\).
After combining repeated allocations, a schedule is precisely a vector of durations satisfying
\[ \lambda_i\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_i=1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^N\delta_i\lambda_i=0. \]
Its objective is \(\sum_{i=1}^Np_i\lambda_i\).

Case II supplies indices \(u,v\) with \(\delta_u>0>\delta_v\). Assigning durations
\[ \lambda_u=\frac{-\delta_v}{\delta_u-\delta_v}, \qquad \lambda_v=\frac{\delta_u}{\delta_u-\delta_v} \]
gives a feasible schedule. The feasible duration vectors constitute a closed subset of the finite-dimensional probability simplex, so they form a nonempty compact set. The continuous linear objective therefore attains its maximum.

2. An optimal schedule has support at most two.

Among all optimal duration vectors, choose one, denoted \(\lambda\), with the smallest number of positive entries. Write \(S=\{i:\lambda_i>0\}\).

Suppose \(|S|\ge3\). The vectors \((1,\delta_i)\), for \(i\in S\), lie in a vector space of dimension \(2\), so they are linearly dependent. Hence there is a nonzero real vector \(h\), supported on \(S\), such that
\[ \sum_i h_i=0,\qquad \sum_i\delta_i h_i=0. \]
Because every \(\lambda_i\) on \(S\) is positive, both \(\lambda+\varepsilon h\) and \(\lambda-\varepsilon h\) are nonnegative for sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Both preserve normalization and fairness.

It follows that \(\sum_i p_i h_i=0\): otherwise one of these two perturbations would strictly improve the objective, contradicting optimality.

Since \(h\ne0\) and \(\sum_i h_i=0\), \(h\) has both positive and negative entries. Set
\[ \varepsilon_*=\min_{i:h_i<0}\frac{\lambda_i}{-h_i}>0. \]
Then \(\lambda+\varepsilon_*h\) is nonnegative, preserves both equalities and the optimal objective, and has at least one fewer positive entry. No entry outside \(S\) becomes positive. This contradicts the choice of \(\lambda\).

Therefore an optimal schedule uses at most \(2\) distinct allocations. A schedule using only one allocation would require \(\delta_i=0\), which Case II excludes. Thus the optimum uses exactly \(2\), necessarily with imbalances of opposite signs.

3. Explicit construction and running time.

Let \(\mathcal A_+=\{a\in\mathcal A:\delta(a)>0\}\) and \(\mathcal A_-=\{b\in\mathcal A:\delta(b)<0\}\), where \(p(a)=s_1a_1\) and \(\delta(a)=s_1a_1-s_2a_2\).

For each pair \(a\in\mathcal A_+\), \(b\in\mathcal A_-\), its unique fair durations are
\[ \lambda_a=\frac{-\delta(b)}{\delta(a)-\delta(b)}, \qquad \lambda_b=\frac{\delta(a)}{\delta(a)-\delta(b)}. \]
The resulting objective is
\[ V(a,b)= \frac{-\delta(b)p(a)+\delta(a)p(b)} {\delta(a)-\delta(b)}. \]
The preceding proof shows that the optimum equals
\(\max_{a\in\mathcal A_+,\,b\in\mathcal A_-}V(a,b)\).
In particular, an optimal schedule has rational durations.

An elementary exact algorithm enumerates the
\(F=(U_1+1)(U_2+1)\) candidate allocations, retains those that are feasible and for which both unit increments are infeasible, and then examines all opposite-sign pairs. This takes at most
\(O(Fm\tau+F^2)\) rational arithmetic operations, with \(\tau=2\). Each operation involves numbers of bit length polynomial in \(L\), and \(F\le2^{O(L)}\), so the bit running time is
\(2^{O(L)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).
This is an explicit upper bound, without a claim that exponential time is necessary.

Step-by-step verification

  • Positivity and finite coordinate bounds.

Each demand vector is nonzero and nonnegative, and every capacity is positive. Consequently, \(s_t>0\). The masses satisfy \(\mu_t>0\).

For any feasible allocation, nonnegativity of consumption implies \(\mu_t a_t d_{tr}\le c_r\) for every resource. Dividing by \(c_r>0\) and maximizing over resources gives \(\mu_t a_t s_t\le1\). Since \(a_t\) is a nonnegative integer, the bound
\(a_t\le \lfloor1/(\mu_t s_t)\rfloor=U_t\)
follows. Thus the feasible allocation set and its subset \(\mathcal A\) are finite.

This argument permits zero demand on individual resources; only the stipulated nonzero demand vector is needed.

  • Representation of schedules.

Enumerating \(\mathcal A\) and combining repeated allocations correctly represents every allowed schedule by a vector \(\lambda\) with nonnegative entries summing to \(1\).

With \(\delta_i=s_1a^i_1-s_2a^i_2\), the fairness condition is exactly \(\sum_i\delta_i\lambda_i=0\). The objective is exactly \(\sum_i p_i\lambda_i\), where \(p_i=s_1a^i_1\).

Zero-duration allocations do not affect the support count. No assumptions about equal masses or unweighted capacities enter this representation.

  • Feasibility and existence of an optimum.

Case II supplies \(\delta_u>0>\delta_v\). The displayed durations have a positive denominator, are both strictly positive, sum to \(1\), and give weighted imbalance \(0\).

The feasible duration set is therefore nonempty. It is a closed subset of the finite-dimensional probability simplex, which is compact. The linear objective is continuous, so it attains a maximum. All hypotheses of the compactness and maximum-attainment statements are satisfied.

  • Choice of an optimum with minimum support.

An optimum exists, and its support size belongs to the finite set of integers from \(1\) through \(N\). Hence choosing an optimal duration vector with the smallest support size is justified.

If its support \(S\) has at least \(3\) elements, the vectors \((1,\delta_i)\) indexed by \(S\) are linearly dependent in a space of dimension \(2\). The resulting nonzero vector \(h\), extended by zero outside \(S\), satisfies both displayed homogeneous equalities.

  • Perturbation and support reduction.

All entries of \(\lambda\) on \(S\) are strictly positive, and \(S\) is finite. Thus sufficiently small perturbations in both directions remain nonnegative. The two homogeneous equalities for \(h\) preserve normalization and fairness.

If \(\sum_i p_i h_i\ne0\), one perturbation direction strictly improves the objective. Optimality therefore implies \(\sum_i p_i h_i=0\).

Because \(h\ne0\) and its entries sum to zero, it has both positive and negative entries. The minimum defining \(\varepsilon_*\) is consequently taken over a nonempty finite set of positive numbers. At this value, the perturbed vector remains nonnegative and at least one previously positive entry becomes zero. No entry outside \(S\) becomes positive. Both constraints and the objective remain unchanged.

This contradicts minimum support and proves the asserted existence of an optimum with support at most \(2\).

  • Exactly two allocations and the pair formula.

A schedule with one positive-duration allocation would assign it duration \(1\); fairness would then require its imbalance to be zero. Case II excludes this. Thus the selected optimum has exactly \(2\) allocations.

Their imbalances must have opposite signs because both durations are positive and their weighted imbalance is zero. Solving the two duration equalities gives precisely the displayed formulas. Substitution into the objective gives the stated \(V(a,b)\).

Every opposite-sign pair produces a feasible fair schedule, and some such pair realizes an optimum by the support argument. Therefore maximizing \(V(a,b)\) over those pairs is valid. All quantities are rational, so the optimal durations obtained this way are rational.

The coordinate bounds give exactly \(F=(U_1+1)(U_2+1)\) candidates. Testing feasibility and infeasibility of both unit increments implements the definition of \(\mathcal A\), including at the coordinate bounds.

The claimed \(O(Fm\tau+F^2)\) rational-operation bound accounts for these tests and all opposite-sign pairs, with \(\tau=2\).

Input rationals, the computed \(s_t\), coordinate bounds, pair durations, objective values, and comparison intermediates have bit length polynomial in \(L\). Also, each \(U_t\) is at most \(2^{O(L)}\), so \(F\le2^{O(L)}\). The stated bit running-time bound follows.
