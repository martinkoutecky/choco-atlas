The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational finite-support prior \(\nu\) over latent qualities \(p\in(0,1)\), with \(\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(p)>0\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[p]\ne 1/2\), each realized atomless society has signal-type masses \(\mu_1=p\) and \(\mu_0=1-p\). Choose a normalized strictly proper peer-scoring rule and a penalty triggered by unanimous aggregate reports that maximize truth-telling's expected payoff gap over other informative atomless Bayesian Nash equilibria, and determine whether the penalty makes truth-telling strictly higher-paying than every other equilibrium, including equilibria with identity-based splits at indifference.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(m=\tau=2\), with signal masses \(\mu_1=p\), \(\mu_0=1-p\).
The common prior \(\nu\) has \(k\) rational support points in \((0,1)\), positive binary-rational weights summing to \(1\), \(\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(p)>0\), and \(\bar p=\mathbb E_{\nu}[p]\ne\tfrac12\). Agents observe only their own binary signal; identity provides no additional information. Set \(q_1=\mathbb E_{\nu}[p^2]/\bar p\) and \(q_0=\mathbb E_{\nu}[p(1-p)]/(1-\bar p)\).
Identities form a unit atomless probability space \((I,\lambda)\). A strategy is a measurable family \(x_b(i)\in[0,1]\) of probabilities of reporting \(1\) after signal \(b\); define \(a_b=\int_I x_b(i)\,d\lambda(i)\) and the realized report mass \(r_x(p)=(1-p)a_0+pa_1\). An agent reporting \(a\) receives \(h_{Z,a}\), where, conditional on \(p\), the independently sampled peer report satisfies \(Z\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(r_x(p))\).
Optionally subtract a finite penalty \(c\ge0\) whenever \(r_x(p)\in\{0,1\}\); an individual deviation leaves this aggregate unchanged. Write \(U_i^c(x)\) for identity \(i\)'s ex ante expected payment, averaging over the prior, its signal, and private randomization. Bayesian Nash equilibrium requires best responses conditional on each signal for almost every identity.
Truth-telling \(T\) has \(x_0=0,x_1=1\). Only the two globally constant profiles \(\mathbf0,\mathbf1\) are called uninformative. Result

The optimum depends only on the first two moments of the prior. It is always positive under the stated assumptions. Its supremum need not be attained, but arbitrarily near-optimal matrices can always be paired with a finite unanimity penalty making truth-telling focal.

Here is an explicit formula and attainment criterion.

First relabel the signals and reports, if necessary, so that the mean exceeds one half. Thus set

\[ (u,l,v)= \begin{cases} (\bar p,q_0,q_1),&\bar p>\tfrac12,\\ (1-\bar p,1-q_1,1-q_0),&\bar p<\tfrac12. \end{cases} \]

All formulas below use these relabeled quantities. They satisfy

\[ 0<l<u<v<1,\qquad u=\frac{l}{1-v+l}>\frac12,\qquad l+v>1. \]

Define

\[ \alpha=\frac{u}{v},\qquad \beta=\frac{1-u}{1-l},\qquad f=2u-1,\qquad d=v-l,\qquad H=\max\{l,1-l\}. \]

In particular, \(\alpha>\beta>0\). Set

\[ z_0= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha}\,v+\sqrt{\beta}\,l} {\sqrt{\alpha}+\sqrt{\beta}}, \qquad J(t)= \frac{\alpha\beta d(v-t)(t-l)} {t\bigl(\alpha(v-t)+\beta(t-l)\bigr)}, \]

and define the second candidate value by

\[ G_{\mathrm{II}}=J\bigl(\max\{H,z_0\}\bigr). \]

For the first candidate, set \(G_{\mathrm I}=0\) when \(l\geq\tfrac12\). When \(l<\tfrac12\), define

\[ z_{\mathrm I} = l+ \frac{\sqrt{f^2+\beta f(1-2l)}-f}{\beta}, \qquad G_{\mathrm I} = \frac{\beta(z_{\mathrm I}-l)^2}{1-z_{\mathrm I}}. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{\Delta^*(\nu)=\max\{G_{\mathrm I},G_{\mathrm{II}}\}>0.} \]

The exact attainment criterion is

\[ \boxed{ \text{The supremum is attained} \iff z_0>H\ \text{or}\ G_{\mathrm I}\geq G_{\mathrm{II}}. } \]

Consequently:

  • Deciding \(\Delta^*(\nu)>g\) is in \(\mathrm P\).
  • An exactly optimal matrix can be paired with a finite focality penalty precisely when the supremum is attained.
  • For every admitted prior and every positive requested accuracy, an arbitrarily near-optimal matrix can be paired with such a penalty. There are no impossible priors for this latter requirement within the promised class.
  • The penalty \(c=1\) suffices for every matrix having \(\Delta_h>0\).

The proof below concerns the specified atomless model directly. The [finite-population analysis of Kong, Schoenebeck, and Ligett](https://arxiv.org/html/1603.07319) is useful context, but is not used as a theorem establishing these conclusions.

1. Moments and feasible payment matrices

Write \(w=1-u\). Positive variance gives

\[ v-u=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(p)}{u}>0, \qquad u-l=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\nu}(p)}{w}>0. \]

Moreover,

\[ uv+wl=u, \]

because the left-hand side is \(\mathbb E[p^2]+\mathbb E[p(1-p)]\). Hence

\[ u(1-v)=wl,\qquad u=\frac{l}{1-v+l}. \]

Relabeling sends a matrix \(h\) to \(h'_{z,a}=h_{1-z,1-a}\), preserves normalization, and preserves every equilibrium payoff gap. It also preserves strict propriety by replacing \(S(z,q)\) with \(S(1-z,1-q)\). Thus the reduction to \(u>\tfrac12\) loses nothing.

For an arbitrary admissible matrix, let its expected payment after reporting \(a\), when the peer reports one with probability \(y\), be

\[ L_a(y)=(1-y)h_{0,a}+yh_{1,a}. \]

Strict propriety at the distinct forecasts \(l,v\) implies

\[ L_0(l)>L_1(l),\qquad L_1(v)>L_0(v). \]

Their difference is affine, so necessarily

\[ L_1(y)-L_0(y)=D(y-t) \]

for some \(D>0\) and \(l<t<v\). Write

\[ L_0(y)=A-Dsy \]

for a real parameter \(s\). Before normalization, the four entries, after subtracting \(A\) and dividing by \(D\), are

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 0&-t\\ -s&1-s-t \end{pmatrix}. \]

Later we prove that every matrix with a positive gap has \(l<s<v\). For such parameters, put

\[ C=\max\{s,t\},\qquad R=\max\{s,1-s,t,1-t\}. \]

The uniquely normalized matrix with these parameters is

\[ h(s,t)= \frac1R \begin{pmatrix} C&C-t\\ C-s&C+1-s-t \end{pmatrix}. \tag{1} \]

Indeed, the minimum of the four unnormalized entries is \(-C\), and their range is

\[ C+\max\{0,1-s-t\}=R. \]

Thus normalization forces \(A=C/R\) and \(D=1/R\).

Every such matrix comes from a strictly proper rule

There is no additional scoring-rule restriction on \(s\) or \(t\) beyond \(l<t<v\).

To verify this, choose a bounded, strictly positive density \(\rho\) on \([l,v]\) satisfying

\[ \int_l^v\rho(q)\,dq=D, \qquad \int_l^v q\rho(q)\,dq=Dt. \tag{2} \]

Such a density exists: choose two short intervals centered respectively at \(a<t<b\), both contained in \((l,v)\). Add a sufficiently small positive uniform component on \([l,v]\). The remaining two component weights can then be chosen positive so that the overall mean is exactly \(t\).

Construct a continuously differentiable, strictly convex function \(G\) with

\[ G(l)=A-Dsl,\qquad G'(l)=-Ds, \]

and with curvature density \(\rho\) on \([l,v]\). Extend it to \([0,1]\) using positive constant curvature outside this interval. Equation (2) gives

\[ G'(v)=D(1-s), \qquad G(v)-vG'(v)=A-Dt. \]

Now define

\[ S(0,q)=G(q)-qG'(q),\qquad S(1,q)=G(q)+(1-q)G'(q). \]

For a true probability \(p\), the expected score from forecast \(q\) is

\[ G(q)+G'(q)(p-q), \]

which is strictly smaller than \(G(p)\) whenever \(q\ne p\), by strict convexity. Therefore \(S\) is strictly proper and induces exactly the required two payment columns.

2. Atomless equilibria and identity-independent payoffs

For an aggregate strategy \((a_0,a_1)\), the conditional probabilities of a peer report of one are

\[ y_0=a_0+(a_1-a_0)l,\qquad y_1=a_0+(a_1-a_0)v. \tag{3} \]

Every identity faces the same conditional best-response problem. Reporting one is strictly preferable when \(y_b>t\), reporting zero is strictly preferable when \(y_b<t\), and every private mixture is optimal when \(y_b=t\).

This gives the following complete list of equilibrium aggregates:

| Equilibrium | Aggregate \((a_0,a_1)\) | Existence condition |
|---|---|---|
| \(\mathbf0\) | \((0,0)\) | Always |
| \(\mathbf1\) | \((1,1)\) | Always |
| \(T\) | \((0,1)\) | Always |
| \(T_0\) | \((0,t/v)\) | Always |
| \(T_1\) | \(((t-l)/(1-l),1)\) | Always |
| \(Q\) | \((t,t)\) | Always |
| \(F_0\) | \((t/(1-l),0)\) | \(t\leq1-l\) |
| \(F_1\) | \((1,(t-1+v)/v)\) | \(t\geq1-v\) |
| \(F\) | \((1,0)\) | \(1-v\leq t\leq1-l\) |

Boundary coincidences in this table represent the same aggregate.

For completeness, if \(a_1>a_0\), then \(y_1>y_0\). The best-response conditions yield \(T\), or one of \(T_0,T_1\) when one signal is indifferent. If \(a_1<a_0\), then \(y_0>y_1\), yielding \(F,F_0,F_1\). If \(a_1=a_0=a\), both conditional probabilities equal \(a\), so equilibrium requires \(a=0\), \(a=1\), or \(a=t\). These are exactly \(\mathbf0,\mathbf1,Q\).

Because \(u>\tfrac12\), we have \(l>1-v\). Thus \(F_1\) always exists, while \(F_0\) and \(F\) exist exactly when \(t\leq1-l\).

Every aggregate listed is realized by an equilibrium profile, for example by using the same private mixing probabilities at every identity. Conversely, identity-dependent choices at indifference introduce no additional aggregates.

Crucially, they introduce no additional equilibrium payoffs either. At equilibrium, every identity receives the conditional best-response payment for each signal, even if identities choose different mixtures at indifference. Hence every identity has the same ex ante payoff.

Let

\[ M_x=wa_0+ua_1. \]

Using (3), that common equilibrium payoff is

\[ U(x)=A+D\left[-sM_x+w(y_0-t)_++u(y_1-t)_+\right], \tag{4} \]

where \((z)_+=\max\{z,0\}\). Truth-telling has

\[ U(T)=A+D[-su+u(v-t)]. \tag{5} \]

Therefore the essential infimum over identities in the question equals the ordinary payoff difference. The infinitely many profiles at indifference reduce to the finite table above.

3. Explicit payoff gaps

The two equilibria \(T_0,T_1\) exist for every admissible matrix. Substituting into (4)–(5) gives their gaps as

\[ U(T)-U(T_0)=D\frac{u}{v}(v-t)(v-s), \]

and, using \(u(1-v)=wl\),

\[ U(T)-U(T_1)=D\frac{w}{1-l}(t-l)(s-l). \]

Thus a positive gap necessarily requires

\[ l<s<v. \tag{6} \]

We will exhibit positive gaps, so matrices violating (6) cannot affect the optimum.

For matrices satisfying (6), define the gap numerators

\[ N_0=\alpha(v-s)(v-t),\qquad N_1=\beta(s-l)(t-l). \]

Because \(D=1/R\), each equilibrium gap is its numerator divided by \(R\). Direct substitution into (4) gives:

| Equilibrium | Gap numerator |
|---|---|
| \(T_0\) | \(N_0\) |
| \(T_1\) | \(N_1\) |
| \(Q\) | \(N_0+(1-\alpha)st\) |
| \(F_0\) | \(N_1+\dfrac{ud}{1-l}(1-s-t)\) |
| \(F_1\) | \(N_1+(\alpha-\beta)(1-s)(1-t)\) |
| \(F\) | \(f(1-s-t)\) |

The coefficient signs needed below follow from

\[ 0<\alpha<1, \qquad \alpha-\beta = \frac{fd}{v(1-l)}>0. \tag{7} \]

Consequently, \(Q\) and \(F_1\) never determine the minimum gap.

If \(t\leq1-l\), the equilibrium \(F\) exists. A positive gap then requires \(s+t<1\). Under that condition, \(F_0\)'s gap is larger than \(T_1\)'s. Hence every positive gap in this region is

\[ \Delta_{h(s,t)} = \frac{\min\{N_0,N_1,f(1-s-t)\}}{R}. \tag{8} \]

If \(t>1-l\), both \(F\) and \(F_0\) disappear, and

\[ \Delta_{h(s,t)} = \frac{\min\{N_0,N_1\}}{R}. \tag{9} \]

These two regions account for the two candidate values.

4. Optimization in the first region

Consider \(t\leq1-l\) and a positive gap. Necessarily \(s+t<1\). Put

\[ z=\frac{s+t}{2}. \]

Then \(l<z<\tfrac12\). Replacing \((s,t)\) with \((z,z)\) remains in this region.

At fixed \(s+t\), both products

\[ (v-s)(v-t),\qquad (s-l)(t-l) \]

increase when \(s,t\) are replaced by their average, because the product \(st\) increases. The numerator \(f(1-s-t)\) is unchanged. Also,

\[ R=1-\min\{s,t\}\geq1-z. \]

Since the gap is positive, these facts prove that averaging cannot decrease it.

Thus the first region reduces to \(s=t=z\), with \(l<z<\tfrac12\). Its gap is

\[ \frac{ \min\{\alpha(v-z)^2,\ \beta(z-l)^2,\ f(1-2z)\} }{1-z}. \tag{10} \]

The equality of the first two numerator terms occurs at \(z=z_0\). By (7),

\[ z_0>\frac{v+l}{2}>\frac12. \]

Therefore, throughout the domain of (10),

\[ \alpha(v-z)^2>\beta(z-l)^2. \]

It remains to maximize

\[ \min\left\{ \frac{\beta(z-l)^2}{1-z}, \frac{f(1-2z)}{1-z} \right\}. \]

The first function is strictly increasing on \((l,\tfrac12)\); the second is strictly decreasing. At \(z=l\), the first is zero and the second positive. At \(z=\tfrac12\), the second is zero and the first positive. Their unique intersection therefore gives the maximum.

Writing \(x=z-l\), the intersection equation is

\[ \beta x^2+2fx=f(1-2l), \]

whose positive root gives exactly \(z_{\mathrm I}\) stated above. In particular,

\[ l<z_{\mathrm I}<\frac12. \]

The first-region optimum is \(G_{\mathrm I}\), and it is attained.

When \(l\geq\tfrac12\), this region is empty because \(t>l\) and \(t\leq1-l\) are incompatible.

5. Optimization in the second region and attainment

Here

\[ t>H=\max\{l,1-l\}. \]

For every \(s\in(l,v)\), this implies \(s+t>1\), so

\[ R=\max\{s,t\}. \]

If \(s>t\), replacing both parameters by their average remains feasible: the new threshold exceeds the old threshold and therefore still exceeds \(H\). Both numerator products increase and the denominator decreases. Hence it suffices to consider \(s\leq t\), where \(R=t\).

For fixed \(t\), \(N_0\) decreases strictly with \(s\), while \(N_1\) increases strictly. Their intersection is

\[ s(t)= \frac{\alpha v(v-t)+\beta l(t-l)} {\alpha(v-t)+\beta(t-l)}. \tag{11} \]

This lies strictly between \(l\) and \(v\). Moreover,

\[ s(t)\leq t\iff t\geq z_0. \]

Thus the largest gap for a fixed threshold is

\[ \begin{cases} \dfrac{\beta(t-l)^2}{t},&t\leq z_0,\\[6pt] J(t),&t\geq z_0. \end{cases} \tag{12} \]

Indeed, when \(t<z_0\), the intersection lies above the permitted interval \(s\leq t\), and the maximum occurs at \(s=t\). When \(t\geq z_0\), it occurs at (11). Substituting (11) yields the displayed formula for \(J\).

The first branch in (12) is strictly increasing, since

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\beta(t-l)^2}{t}\right) = \beta\left(1-\frac{l^2}{t^2}\right)>0. \]

To study the second branch, observe that

\[ \frac1{J(t)} = \frac1{\alpha\beta d} \left( \frac{\alpha t}{t-l} + \frac{\beta t}{v-t} \right). \]

The derivative of the expression in parentheses is

\[ -\frac{\alpha l}{(t-l)^2} +\frac{\beta v}{(v-t)^2}. \]

It vanishes once, at

\[ t_*= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha l}\,v+\sqrt{\beta v}\,l} {\sqrt{\alpha l}+\sqrt{\beta v}}. \]

Since \(l<v\), the relative weight on \(v\) here is smaller than its relative weight in \(z_0\). Therefore \(t_*<z_0\). The derivative is positive for \(t>t_*\), proving that \(J\) is strictly decreasing on \([z_0,v)\).

It follows that:

  • If \(z_0>H\), the second-region optimum is \(J(z_0)\), attained at \(s=t=z_0\).
  • If \(z_0\leq H\), its supremum is \(J(H)\), approached as \(t\downarrow H\), and is not attained in this region.

These statements prove the formula for \(G_{\mathrm{II}}\).

Also, \(H<v\): both \(l<v\) and \(1-l<v\) hold. Since \(z_0\in(l,v)\), all factors defining \(G_{\mathrm{II}}\) are positive. Thus

\[ G_{\mathrm{II}}>0. \]

Combining the two regions proves both the formula for \(\Delta^*(\nu)\) and the claimed attainment criterion.

Explicit optimal and limiting matrices

For a diagonal parameter choice \(s=t=z\), equation (1) becomes

\[ h(z,z)= \frac1{\max\{z,1-z\}} \begin{pmatrix} z&0\\ 0&1-z \end{pmatrix}. \tag{13} \]

Thus:

  • If \(G_{\mathrm I}\geq G_{\mathrm{II}}\), use \(z=z_{\mathrm I}\).
  • If \(G_{\mathrm{II}}>G_{\mathrm I}\) and \(z_0>H\), use \(z=z_0\).
  • In the nonattained case, choose rational \(t_n\downarrow H\) with \(H<t_n<v\), put \(s_n=s(t_n)\), and use (1). Their gaps are \(J(t_n)\to G_{\mathrm{II}}\).

In the last case, \(H=1-l\). At the limiting threshold, \(F\) reappears. Because \(s(H)>l\),

\[ f(1-s(H)-H)<0. \]

Thus the limiting matrix itself has a negative gap against \(F\). This explains why taking the limit of the matrices does not produce an optimal matrix.

Nonattainment genuinely occurs

For example, take equal prior weights at

\[ p=\frac38,\qquad p=\frac{21}{32}. \]

Then

\[ u=\frac{33}{64},\quad l=\frac{471}{992},\quad v=\frac{195}{352},\quad H=\frac{521}{992}, \]

and

\[ \alpha=\frac{121}{130},\qquad \beta=\frac{961}{1042}. \]

Direct rational comparison gives

\[ \alpha(v-H)^2<\beta(H-l)^2, \]

which is equivalent to \(z_0<H\). Moreover,

\[ G_{\mathrm{II}}=\frac{365025}{142487248}>\frac1{500}, \]

whereas, since \(z_{\mathrm I}<\tfrac12\),

\[ G_{\mathrm I} < 2\beta\left(\frac12-l\right)^2 = \frac{625}{533504} < \frac1{800}. \]

Hence this prior has a strictly nonattained supremum.

Conversely, every relabeled prior with \(l\geq\tfrac12\) has an attained optimum, because then \(H=l<z_0\).

6. Unanimity penalties and focality

For every support point \(p\in(0,1)\),

\[ r_x(p)=(1-p)a_0+pa_1 \]

is a convex combination with both coefficients strictly positive. Consequently,

\[ r_x(p)=0\iff a_0=a_1=0, \qquad r_x(p)=1\iff a_0=a_1=1. \]

Since the strategies take values in \([0,1]\), these aggregate conditions force the respective globally constant profiles almost everywhere.

Therefore unanimity occurs precisely at \(\mathbf0,\mathbf1\). Every other profile, including truth-telling, receives no penalty at any support point.

An individual deviation leaves the aggregate unchanged, so the penalty adds the same conditional subtraction to both available reports. The equilibrium set is therefore unchanged.

For a normalized matrix, every unpenalized payment lies in \([0,1]\). Taking \(c=1\) gives

\[ U_i^1(\mathbf0)\leq0,\qquad U_i^1(\mathbf1)\leq0. \]

On the other hand, \(U_i^1(T)=U_i^0(T)>0\). Indeed, under truth-telling, every signal–peer-outcome pair has positive probability, and at least one normalized matrix entry equals one.

If \(\Delta_h>0\), every other nonconstant equilibrium has strictly smaller payoff than truth-telling, for every identity outside the null set on which equilibrium conditions may fail. Thus \(c=1\) makes truth-telling focal.

Conversely, a unanimity penalty cannot alter any nonconstant equilibrium payoff. Since there are only finitely many equilibrium payoff values, focality requires \(\Delta_h>0\). Hence

\[ \boxed{ \text{Some finite unanimity penalty makes }T\text{ focal} \iff \Delta_h>0. } \]

Because \(\Delta^*(\nu)>0\), every attained optimizer satisfies this condition. In the nonattained case, sufficiently near-optimal matrices satisfy it as well. The limiting matrix discussed above cannot be rescued by this penalty, but arbitrarily close matrices can.

7. Exact decision and running times

Let \(L\) include the entire binary input, including the threshold or requested accuracy.

Computing \(u\) and \(\mathbb E[p^2]\), and hence \(l,v\), takes \(O(k)\) rational arithmetic operations. The intermediate rational numbers have \(O(L)\) bits.

The formulas then require only a constant number of rational operations, square roots, and exact comparisons. In particular:

  • \(z_0\) belongs to a quadratic extension, using \(\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}\).
  • \(z_{\mathrm I}\) belongs to a quadratic extension.
  • Each candidate gap is rational or quadratic algebraic.
  • Comparing the candidates involves at most two square roots.

Such comparisons can be performed exactly by isolating radicals and squaring with the necessary sign checks. They require only a constant number of operations on integers with \(O(L)\) bits. Equality cases are therefore decided exactly.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for exact evaluation, attainment testing, and deciding \(\Delta^*(\nu)>g\) is

\[ O(kL^2+L^3) \]

bit operations. This establishes membership in \(\mathrm P\).

An attained optimum can be output exactly by the constant-size algebraic expression (13). For any positive rational accuracy \(\varepsilon\), a rational matrix with

\[ \Delta_h>\Delta^*(\nu)-\varepsilon, \qquad \Delta_h>0 \]

can also be constructed in \(O(kL^2+L^3)\) bit operations when \(L\) includes the encoding of \(\varepsilon\).

To justify the approximation bound, all relevant coefficients have \(O(L)\) bits and fixed algebraic degree. Nonzero feasibility margins consequently have magnitude at least \(2^{-O(L)}\). On an appropriately chosen feasible neighborhood, the displayed gap functions have derivatives bounded by \(2^{O(L)}\). Thus \(O(L)\) bits of parameter precision suffice. In the nonattained case, approximate from the side \(t>H\) using (11). Choosing the approximation sufficiently accurately also preserves a positive gap.

These bounds are for the promised setting \(m=\tau=2\); there is no enumeration of possible types and no further dependence on them. No complexity claim for unrestricted \(m\) or \(\tau\) is needed for this task.
