The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) alternatives and a finite support \(S\subseteq S_n\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\pi\) summing to \(1\), replace ranking counts by masses in MC1, MC2, and MC3. What is each procedure’s worst-case approximation ratio, measured by \(C_\mu(\sigma)/\min_{\rho\in S_n}C_\mu(\rho)\), as a function of \(n\), \(\tau=|S|\), and mass bit length \(L\); in particular, is it bounded solely in \(n\)?

1. Scope and proved results

I interpret the masses as arbitrary positive rational numbers with reduced numerators and denominators encoded in binary. Write
$$ \ell(z)=\lfloor\log_2 z\rfloor+1 $$
for the bit length of a positive integer. A tuple $(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ denotes the preference order satisfying $\pi(u_i)=i$.

The results below give an exact finite-budget characterization when $n=3$ and $\tau=2$, and bounds with absolute constants for every $n\geq3$ when $\tau=2$. They do not determine all the requested finite-parameter ratios.

For an integer budget $L\geq6$, define
$$ B(L)=\max\left\{b\geq2: 1+\ell(b-1)+2\ell(b)\leq L\right\}. $$
Equivalently, put $k=\lfloor(L-3)/3\rfloor$. Then
$$ B(L)= \begin{cases} 2^k,&L=3k+3,\\ 2^{k+1}-1,&L=3k+4\text{ or }3k+5. \end{cases} $$

The following statements are proved here.

  • For all three procedures,

$$ \boxed{R_r(3,2,L)=\frac{B(L)}2.} $$
Thus, for budgets $L=6,7,8,9,10,11,12$, the exact ratios are respectively
$$ 1,\quad \frac32,\quad \frac32,\quad 2,\quad \frac72,\quad \frac72,\quad 4. $$

  • For every $n\geq3$,

$$ \boxed{ B(L)-1-\frac{B(L)-2}{n-1} \leq R_r(n,2,L)\leq B(L)-1, \qquad r\in\{1,2\}. } $$
For MC3, the construction below gives an explicit lower bound, and in particular
$$ \boxed{ \frac{B(L)+1}{3}\leq R_3(n,2,L)\leq B(L)-1. } $$
Consequently, uniformly over $n\geq3$ and $L\geq6$,
$$ R_r(n,2,L)=\Theta(2^{L/3}), \qquad r\in\{1,2,3\}, $$
with absolute implicit constants. Moreover, for each fixed $L\geq6$,
$$ \boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}R_r(n,2,L)=B(L)-1.} $$

  • For $n=2$, the only possible admissible support size is $\tau=2$, and

$$ R_r(2,2,L)=1 $$
whenever the family is nonempty.

  • For every nonempty admissible family, with $N=\binom n2$,

$$ R_r(n,\tau,L)<N\,2^{L/(\tau+1)}-1. $$

In particular, none of MC1, MC2, or MC3 has an approximation bound depending solely on $n$. This already follows from the exact formula at $n=3,\tau=2$. It recovers the unboundedness established by [Freund–Williamson, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.00738).

2. A structural lemma: all three procedures respect unanimity

For distinct alternatives, strict rankings give
$$ q_{ij}+q_{ji}=1. $$
Put
$$ s_i=\sum_{j\neq i}q_{ij}, \qquad d_i=\sum_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi a_i(\pi)=1+s_i. $$

All three procedures have the same positive off-diagonal transition graph: the edge $i\to j$ exists exactly when some positive-mass ranking puts $j$ before $i$. Under strong connectivity, their stationary distributions have positive entries, and $s_i>0$ for every $i$.

2.1. Relation between MC1 and MC3

Let $y$ be MC3's stationary distribution. Its stationary flow equations are
$$ y_i s_i=\sum_{j\neq i}y_jq_{ji}. $$
Define
$$ x_i=\frac{d_i y_i}{\sum_k d_k y_k}. $$
Since MC1 has off-diagonal transitions $q_{ij}/d_i$,
$$ x_i\sum_{j\neq i}p^{(1)}_{ij} = \frac{y_i s_i}{\sum_kd_ky_k} = \sum_{j\neq i}x_jp^{(1)}_{ji}. $$
Therefore $x$ is MC1's stationary distribution. In particular,
$$ \boxed{x_i^{(1)}\ \propto\ d_i x_i^{(3)}.} $$

2.2. Strict stationary ordering of unanimous pairs

Suppose every support ranking puts $u$ before $v$. Then
$$ q_{uv}=0,\qquad q_{vu}=1. $$
For every $k\notin\{u,v\}$,
$$ q_{ku}\geq q_{kv}, \qquad q_{vk}\geq q_{uk}. $$
These inequalities follow pointwise in each ranking: if $v$ precedes $k$, then $u$ precedes $k$; if $k$ precedes $u$, then $k$ precedes $v$.

For MC3, stationary balance gives
$$ y_u s_u = y_v+\sum_{k\notin\{u,v\}}y_kq_{ku} \geq y_v+\sum_{k\notin\{u,v\}}y_kq_{kv} = y_v(1+s_v). $$
Also $s_v\geq s_u+1$. Thus $y_u>y_v$. For MC1, the relation just proved yields
$$ \frac{x_u^{(1)}}{x_v^{(1)}} = \frac{1+s_u}{1+s_v}\frac{y_u}{y_v} \geq \frac{1+s_u}{s_u}>1. $$

For MC2, write its transition matrix as $P$ and its stationary distribution as $z$. Unanimity implies
$$ P_{uv}=0,\qquad P_{vu}=P_{vv}, \qquad P_{ku}\geq P_{kv}\quad(k\notin\{u,v\}). $$
Hence
$$ z_u(1-P_{uu}) = z_vP_{vu}+\sum_{k\notin\{u,v\}}z_kP_{ku} \geq z_vP_{vv}+\sum_{k\notin\{u,v\}}z_kP_{kv} =z_v. $$
Here $0<P_{uu}<1$: positivity is immediate from the diagonal formula, and equality $P_{uu}=1$ would make $u$ an absorbing state, contradicting strong connectivity. Therefore $z_u>z_v$.

We have proved:

Every output of each procedure respects every unanimous comparison, strictly at the stationary-probability level. Stationary ties cannot reverse a unanimous pair.

One consequence applies to arbitrary support sizes. Let $m_0=\min_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi$, and let $c$ be the number of nonunanimous unordered pairs. Both orientations of each such pair have total mass at least $m_0$. Thus
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_\mu\geq c\,m_0. $$
The output incurs zero cost on unanimous pairs and at most $1-m_0$ on each remaining pair. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ \frac{C_\mu(\sigma)}{\operatorname{OPT}_\mu} \leq\frac{1-m_0}{m_0}. } $$

3. Two support rankings: optimal cost and exact encoding threshold

Suppose the support consists of $\pi_0,\pi_*$ with masses $1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon$, where
$$ 0<\varepsilon\leq\frac12. $$
Put $D=K(\pi_0,\pi_*)$.

There are exactly $D$ nonunanimous pairs. Each has minimum possible cost $\varepsilon$, and $\pi_0$ attains that minimum on every pair. Therefore
$$ \boxed{\operatorname{OPT}_\mu=\varepsilon D.} $$

By unanimity preservation, every output differs from $\pi_0$ only on those $D$ pairs. If
$$ h=K(\sigma,\pi_0), $$
then
$$ C_\mu(\sigma)=\varepsilon(D-h)+(1-\varepsilon)h =\varepsilon D+(1-2\varepsilon)h. $$
Since $0\leq h\leq D$,
$$ \boxed{ \frac{C_\mu(\sigma)}{\operatorname{OPT}_\mu} \leq\frac1\varepsilon-1. } $$

3.1. The smallest encodable minority mass

Write $\varepsilon=a/b$ in reduced form. The other mass is $(b-a)/b$, also reduced, because
$$ \gcd(b-a,b)=\gcd(a,b)=1. $$
Their total encoding length is
$$ E(a,b)=\ell(a)+\ell(b-a)+2\ell(b). $$

I claim that, among two positive masses summing to one with total length at most $L$,
$$ \boxed{\min\varepsilon=\frac1{B(L)}.} $$

The masses $1/B(L)$ and $(B(L)-1)/B(L)$ establish attainability. Conversely, suppose $\varepsilon<1/B(L)$, and abbreviate $B=B(L)$. Then
$$ b>aB. $$
Because these are integers,
$$ b\geq B+1, \qquad b-a\geq a(B-1)+1\geq B. $$
It follows that
$$ E(a,b)\geq1+\ell(B)+2\ell(B+1). $$
The expression on the right is precisely the encoding length of the masses $1/(B+1)$ and $B/(B+1)$. It exceeds $L$ by the definition of $B$, a contradiction.

To obtain the displayed closed form for $B(L)$, observe that
$$ 1+\ell(b-1)+2\ell(b)= \begin{cases} 3k+3,&b=2^k,\\ 3k+4,&2^k<b<2^{k+1}. \end{cases} $$

Every positive mass below one requires at least three bits, so no two-type input exists for $L<6$. For every $n\geq2$, the two reverse rankings with equal masses give an admissible input of length six. Thus the two-type family is nonempty exactly when $L\geq6$.

Combining the encoding threshold with unanimity preservation proves, for every $n\geq2$,
$$ \boxed{R_r(n,2,L)\leq B(L)-1.} $$

4. An explicit construction on every number of alternatives

Fix $n\geq3$, and use
$$ \pi_0=(1,2,\ldots,n), \qquad \pi_*=(n,1,2,\ldots,n-1) $$
with masses $1-\varepsilon,\varepsilon$, where $0<\varepsilon\leq1/2$.

All three positive-transition graphs are strongly connected: the minority ranking supplies $1\to n$, while the dominant ranking supplies $n\to i$ for every $i<n$ and $i\to1$ for every $i>1$. The rankings disagree on precisely the $n-1$ pairs involving $n$, so
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_\mu=(n-1)\varepsilon>0. $$

4.1. MC3's stationary distribution

Let $y$ denote MC3's stationary distribution. Multiplying its flow equations by $n$ removes the common transition factor. Balance at alternative $n$ gives
$$ y_n(n-1)(1-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon(1-y_n), $$
hence
$$ y_n=\frac{\varepsilon}{(n-1)-(n-2)\varepsilon}. $$

For $i<n$, balance gives
$$ y_i(i-1+\varepsilon) = \sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}y_j+(1-\varepsilon)y_n. $$
Starting at $i=n-1$ and applying this equation backwards yields
$$ \boxed{ \frac{y_i}{y_n} = \frac{(n-1+\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)} {(i-1+\varepsilon)(i+\varepsilon)}, \qquad 1\leq i<n. } $$
For verification, set $A=(n-1+\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)$. The telescoping identity
$$ \sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1} \frac1{(j-1+\varepsilon)(j+\varepsilon)} = \frac1{i+\varepsilon}-\frac1{n-1+\varepsilon} $$
shows directly that these ratios satisfy every displayed balance equation.

The probabilities $y_1,\ldots,y_{n-1}$ strictly decrease. Furthermore,
$$ A-\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon) = (n-1)(1-\varepsilon)-2\varepsilon^2 \geq\frac{n-2}{2}>0, $$
so $y_1>y_n$.

4.2. MC1's output

The expected positions are
$$ d_i=i+\varepsilon\quad(i<n), \qquad d_n=n-(n-1)\varepsilon. $$
By Section 2, MC1 sorts the scores $d_i y_i$. For $i<n$,
$$ \frac{d_i y_i}{y_n}=\frac{A}{i-1+\varepsilon}. $$
These scores strictly decrease with $i$. The first exceeds the score of $n$, since
$$ A-\varepsilon d_n = (n-1)(1-2\varepsilon)+(n-2)\varepsilon^2>0. $$
The score of $n$ exceeds that of alternative $2$, since
$$ (1+\varepsilon)d_n-A = 1+(n-1)\varepsilon-(n-2)\varepsilon^2>0. $$
Therefore MC1 always returns
$$ \sigma=(1,n,2,\ldots,n-1). $$

4.3. MC2's output

Let $z$ denote MC2's stationary distribution. For $i<n$, put
$$ h_i=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{i}+\frac{\varepsilon}{i+1}. $$
From state $i<n$, the transition probability to each earlier alternative is $h_i$, and the diagonal probability is also $h_i$. From state $n$, the transition probability to each $i<n$ is $(1-\varepsilon)/n$.

Thus, for $i<n$,
$$ z_i(1-h_i) = \sum_{j=i+1}^{n-1}z_jh_j+ z_n\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}. $$
Comparing consecutive equations gives
$$ z_{i+1}=z_i(1-h_i), \qquad 1\leq i\leq n-2. $$
Balance at $n-1$ consequently gives
$$ \frac{z_n}{z_2} = \frac{n}{1-\varepsilon} \prod_{i=2}^{n-1}(1-h_i). $$
Now
$$ 1-h_i = \frac{i-1}{i}+\frac{\varepsilon}{i(i+1)} > \frac{i-1}{i}, $$
so $z_n>z_2$. On the other hand,
$$ 1-h_i<\frac{i}{i+1}, $$
and $1-h_1=\varepsilon/2$. Hence
$$ \frac{z_n}{z_1} = \frac{n\varepsilon}{2(1-\varepsilon)} \prod_{i=2}^{n-1}(1-h_i) < \frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon} \leq1. $$
The alternatives $1,\ldots,n-1$ have strictly decreasing stationary probabilities. MC2 therefore also returns
$$ \sigma=(1,n,2,\ldots,n-1). $$

This output reverses $n-2$ of the $n-1$ conflicting pairs. For MC1 and MC2, its ratio is
$$ 1+\left(\frac1\varepsilon-2\right)\frac{n-2}{n-1}. $$
Taking $\varepsilon=1/B(L)$ proves
$$ R_r(n,2,L) \geq B(L)-1-\frac{B(L)-2}{n-1}, \qquad r\in\{1,2\}. $$

4.4. MC3's output and lower bound

For MC3, define
$$ t= \min\left\{i\in\{2,\ldots,n-1\}: (i-1+\varepsilon)(i+\varepsilon)\geq A\right\}. $$
This set is nonempty: at $i=n-1$, its defining inequality reduces to
$$ n-2+\varepsilon\geq1-\varepsilon. $$
Equality of stationary probabilities is handled by placing $n$ first within the tie. The worst permitted output for this construction is therefore
$$ \sigma_t=(1,\ldots,t-1,n,t,\ldots,n-1). $$
It reverses exactly $n-t$ conflicting pairs. At $\varepsilon=1/B(L)$, its ratio is
$$ \boxed{ B(L)-1- \frac{(B(L)-2)(t-1)}{n-1}. } $$

For an absolute lower bound, take $i=\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil$. Then
$$ i(i-1)\geq n-1, \qquad A<n-1, $$
so $t\leq i$. Consequently,
$$ \frac{n-t}{n-1} \geq \frac{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}{n-1} \geq\frac13. $$
The construction gives
$$ R_3(n,2,L) \geq 1+\frac{B(L)-2}{3} = \frac{B(L)+1}{3}. $$

Finally, for fixed $L$, the integer $t$ grows at most on the order of $\sqrt n$. Indeed, its defining quadratic has positive root
$$ \frac{1-2\varepsilon+\sqrt{1+4A}}2, $$
and $A$ grows linearly with $n$. Hence $(t-1)/(n-1)\to0$. Together with the upper bound $B(L)-1$, the constructions prove
$$ \lim_{n\to\infty}R_r(n,2,L)=B(L)-1 $$
for each procedure.

5. Exact finite-budget ratios on three alternatives

Relabel the larger-mass ranking as $(1,2,3)$, with mass $1-\varepsilon$, where $0<\varepsilon\leq1/2$.

Of the five possible distinct minority rankings:

  • $(1,3,2)$ leaves alternative $1$ absorbing, so the graph is not strongly connected.
  • $(2,1,3)$ puts alternative $3$ last in both rankings, so there is no transition into $3$ from either other alternative.
  • Each of $(2,3,1)$, $(3,1,2)$, and $(3,2,1)$ gives strong connectivity.

These are therefore all admissible two-type patterns.

The construction in Section 4 already settles the minority ranking $(3,1,2)$: all three procedures return $(1,3,2)$, whose cost is $1$, while the optimum is $2\varepsilon$. Its ratio is exactly
$$ \frac1{2\varepsilon}. $$

It remains to prove that neither of the other two patterns produces a larger ratio.

5.1. Stationary scores for the remaining patterns

For a three-state transition matrix $P$, define
$$ \begin{aligned} T_1&=P_{21}P_{31}+P_{21}P_{32}+P_{23}P_{31},\\ T_2&=P_{12}P_{32}+P_{12}P_{31}+P_{13}P_{32},\\ T_3&=P_{13}P_{23}+P_{13}P_{21}+P_{12}P_{23}. \end{aligned} $$
Direct expansion gives
$$ T_1(P_{12}+P_{13})=T_2P_{21}+T_3P_{31}, $$
and the other two flow equations follow cyclically. Thus, whenever the vector is positive, its normalization is stationary.

Substitution into these formulas gives the following positive score vectors. For MC3, the displayed vector is $9(T_1,T_2,T_3)$. For MC2, the scale is $12$ in the first row and $6$ in the second.

| Minority ranking | MC3 stationary scores | MC2 stationary scores | Expected positions $(d_1,d_2,d_3)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(2,3,1)$ | $((1-\varepsilon)(2-\varepsilon),\,\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon),\,\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon))$ | $((4-\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon),\,4\varepsilon,\,2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon))$ | $(1+2\varepsilon,\,2-\varepsilon,\,3-\varepsilon)$ |
| $(3,2,1)$ | $((1-\varepsilon)(2-\varepsilon),\,3\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon),\,\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon))$ | $((2-\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon),\,2\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon),\,\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon))$ | $(1+2\varepsilon,\,2,\,3-2\varepsilon)$ |

MC1's scores are obtained by multiplying MC3's three scores by the corresponding expected positions.

5.2. Minority ranking $(2,3,1)$

Alternative $3$ has the smallest score for every procedure. Indeed, the differences between the scores of alternatives $1,2$ and that of $3$ are:

| Procedure | Score of $1$ minus score of $3$ | Score of $2$ minus score of $3$ |
|---|---|---|
| MC1 | $(1-\varepsilon)(2-\varepsilon^2)$ | $\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon)$ |
| MC2 | $(1-\varepsilon)(4-3\varepsilon)$ | $2\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)$ |
| MC3 | $2(1-\varepsilon)^2$ | $2\varepsilon$ |

Every entry is positive for $0<\varepsilon\leq1/2$. Thus every output is either $(1,2,3)$ or $(2,1,3)$.

The optimum is $2\varepsilon$. The first output is optimal. The second has distance one from each support ranking, so its cost is $1$. Every permitted ratio is consequently at most
$$ \frac1{2\varepsilon}. $$

5.3. Minority ranking $(3,2,1)$

For MC1, consecutive score differences are
$$ \begin{aligned} u_1-u_2&=(1-\varepsilon)(1-2\varepsilon)(\varepsilon+2),\\ u_2-u_3&=\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)(3-\varepsilon). \end{aligned} $$
For MC3 they are
$$ u_1-u_2=2(1-\varepsilon)(1-2\varepsilon), \qquad u_2-u_3=2\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon). $$
Thus, when $\varepsilon<1/2$, both procedures return $(1,2,3)$ and are optimal.

At $\varepsilon=1/2$, the rankings are reverses with equal masses. Every ranking has cost $3/2$, because its distances to these two reverse rankings sum to three. Every tie-breaking is therefore optimal.

For MC2, the differences are
$$ \begin{aligned} u_1-u_2&=(1-\varepsilon)(2-3\varepsilon)>0,\\ u_1-u_3&=2(1-2\varepsilon)\geq0,\\ u_2-u_3&=\varepsilon(1-3\varepsilon). \end{aligned} $$
For $\varepsilon<1/2$, every output is $(1,2,3)$ or $(1,3,2)$, including both possibilities when $\varepsilon=1/3$. The latter has cost $1+\varepsilon$, while the optimum is $3\varepsilon$. Its ratio satisfies
$$ \frac{1+\varepsilon}{3\varepsilon} \leq\frac1{2\varepsilon}, $$
because $\varepsilon\leq1/2$. At $\varepsilon=1/2$, all rankings again have equal cost.

This completes all support patterns and all stationary ties. Every admissible two-type input on three alternatives has ratio at most $1/(2\varepsilon)$. Section 3 gives $\varepsilon\geq1/B(L)$, and the minority ranking $(3,1,2)$ attains the bound at $\varepsilon=1/B(L)$. Therefore
$$ \boxed{R_r(3,2,L)=B(L)/2} $$
for all three procedures.

6. The exact two-alternative case

For $n=2$, admissibility requires both rankings. Let $p$ be the mass of $(1,2)$.

For MC1,
$$ P_{12}=\frac{1-p}{2-p}, \qquad P_{21}=\frac{p}{1+p}. $$
In a two-state irreducible chain, $x_1\geq x_2$ exactly when $P_{21}\geq P_{12}$. Here,
$$ p(2-p)\geq(1-p)(1+p) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\geq\frac12. $$
For both MC2 and MC3,
$$ P_{12}=\frac{1-p}{2}, \qquad P_{21}=\frac p2, $$
giving the same threshold.

Each procedure returns a majority ranking. At equal masses, both rankings are optimal. Hence
$$ \boxed{R_r(2,2,L)=1\qquad(L\geq6).} $$

7. A universal encoding-dependent upper bound

This section covers arbitrary admissible support sizes. Let
$$ M=\max_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi, \qquad \delta=1-M, \qquad N=\binom n2. $$
Admissibility implies $\tau\geq2$.

For any candidate $\rho\in S$, every other support ranking is at Kendall distance at least one, so
$$ C_\mu(\rho)\geq1-\mu_\rho\geq\delta. $$
For $\rho\notin S$, all support distances are at least one, so $C_\mu(\rho)\geq1\geq\delta$. Thus
$$ \operatorname{OPT}_\mu\geq\delta. $$

Suppose first that $\delta<1/2$, and put $t=\log_2(1/\delta)$. Write the largest mass as $a_0/b_0$ in reduced form. Since $b_0-a_0$ is a positive integer,
$$ b_0\geq\frac1\delta, \qquad a_0>\frac1{2\delta}. $$
Therefore
$$ \ell(b_0)>t,\qquad \ell(a_0)>t-1. $$
Every other mass is at most $\delta$, so its denominator has length greater than $t$, and its numerator uses at least one bit. Adding gives
$$ L>(\tau+1)t+\tau-2\geq(\tau+1)t. $$
Consequently,
$$ \delta>2^{-L/(\tau+1)}. $$

If $\delta\geq1/2$, each mass is strictly below one and uses at least three bits. Hence $L\geq3\tau$, which also implies the same strict bound on $\delta$.

Reversing a ranking complements its disagreements on every unordered pair:
$$ C_\mu(\sigma)+C_\mu(\operatorname{rev}\sigma)=N. $$
Since $C_\mu(\operatorname{rev}\sigma)\geq\operatorname{OPT}_\mu$,
$$ \frac{C_\mu(\sigma)}{\operatorname{OPT}_\mu} \leq \frac{N}{\operatorname{OPT}_\mu}-1 \leq \frac N\delta-1 < N\,2^{L/(\tau+1)}-1. $$

For fixed $n,\tau,L$, the admissible family is finite: bounded bit lengths allow only finitely many rational masses and supports. Thus its nonempty supremum is attained, and the strict inequality also holds for $R_r(n,\tau,L)$.

8. Running times and remaining open questions

For a listed input, each transition matrix can be constructed using $O(\tau n^2)$ rational operations. Let $Q$ be the product of the reduced mass denominators. Its bit length is at most $L$.

  • MC1 entries have numerator and denominator lengths $O(L+\log n)$ after expressing the masses over denominator $Q$.
  • MC2 admits common denominator $Qn!$, of bit length $O(L+n\log n)$.
  • MC3 admits common denominator $Qn$.

The normalized stationary equations therefore have polynomial-size rational coefficients. Clearing denominators and using exact elimination computes the unique stationary distribution in
$$ \operatorname{poly}(n,\tau,L) $$
bit operations; determinant bounds give polynomial bit lengths for the resulting rational probabilities.

Enumerating all $n!$ rankings computes both $\operatorname{OPT}_\mu$ and the worst permitted stationary tie-breaking in
$$ n!\operatorname{poly}(n,\tau,L) $$
bit operations.

For the finite worst-case ratio itself, exhaustive enumeration gives an exact algorithm with running time
$$ 2^{O(L+\tau n\log n)}\operatorname{poly}(n,\tau,L). $$
Indeed, there are at most $2^{O(L)}$ numerator-denominator tuples of total length at most $L$, and at most $(n!)^\tau$ ordered support choices.
