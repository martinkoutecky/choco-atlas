The true shuffled label \(S^*\) is uniform on \(S\). Conditional on \(S^*=s\), all agents respond independently; a type-\(t\) agent chooses \(s\) with probability \(x_t\), and each other label with probability \((1-x_t)/(K-1)\).

$$ \operatorname{argmax}_{s\in S}\ell(s) = \{\text{Bayes-optimal labels}\}. $$
This includes accuracies equal to zero or one.

I do not resolve the unconditional complexity question for variable support size. Below I prove the product-comparison equivalence from the notes and strengthen the partial results in three ways:

Throughout, a *dyadic rational* means a rational whose reduced denominator is a power of two. The general reduction uses only dyadic inputs, so it applies under either interpretation of “binary-rational.”

2. The posterior identity, including zero accuracies

Let $E$ be the observed count event. Define
$$ N_t=D\mu_t,\qquad n_t(u)=D\nu_t(u),\qquad q_t=\frac{1-x_t}{K-1}. $$
By the definition of $D$, these counts are integers, and
$$ \sum_{u\in S}n_t(u)=N_t. $$

Conditional on $S^*=s$, the responses of type $t$ have probabilities $x_t$ for label $s$ and $q_t$ for every other label. Independence therefore gives
$$ \Pr(E\mid S^*=s) = C_E\prod_{t\in T} x_t^{n_t(s)}q_t^{N_t-n_t(s)}, $$
where
$$ C_E=\prod_{t\in T}\frac{N_t!}{\prod_{u\in S}n_t(u)!}. $$
The coefficient $C_E$ is positive and independent of $s$. Here and below, a zero-probability outcome with count zero contributes the factor $0^0=1$.

If every factor with positive exponent has positive base, taking logarithms gives
$$ \Pr(E\mid S^*=s)=C_E\exp(D\ell(s)). $$
If a factor has base zero and positive exponent, the likelihood is zero and $\ell(s)=-\infty$, so the same identity holds with $\exp(-\infty)=0$.

The uniform prior and the positive-marginal-probability promise now imply
$$ \Pr(S^*=s\mid E) = \frac{\exp(D\ell(s))} {\sum_{u\in S}\exp(D\ell(u))}. $$
The denominator is positive. Since $D>0$, the map $a\mapsto\exp(Da)$ preserves order and ties on $\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\}$. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{argmax}_{s\in S}\ell(s) = \{\text{Bayes-optimal labels}\}. } $$

3. Exact selection reduces to product comparison

Define $\mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}$ to be the decision problem
$$ \prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{b_i} \ \ge\ \prod_{j=1}^{r}c_j^{d_j}, $$
where bases are positive integers and exponents are nonnegative integers, all encoded in binary. Empty products equal one.

3.1. Removing labels with zero likelihood

A label $s$ has positive likelihood if and only if every type satisfies the following conditions:
$$ x_t=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \nu_t(s)=0, \qquad x_t=1\ \Longrightarrow\ \nu_t(s)=\mu_t. $$
Indeed, when $x_t=0$, the likelihood vanishes exactly when $n_t(s)>0$. When $x_t=1$, it vanishes exactly when $N_t-n_t(s)>0$. Interior accuracies impose no further restriction.

Let $F$ be the labels satisfying these conditions. Exact rational arithmetic determines $F$ in polynomial time. The promise implies $F\ne\varnothing$, and every optimal label belongs to $F$.

Put
$$ I=\{t\in T:0<x_t<1\}, \qquad r_t=\frac{(K-1)x_t}{1-x_t}. $$
On $F$, endpoint types contribute constants independent of the label. For interior types,
$$ \nu_t(s)\log x_t+ (\mu_t-\nu_t(s))\log q_t = \mu_t\log q_t+\nu_t(s)\log r_t. $$
Thus, for a finite constant $C$,
$$ \ell(s)=C+\sum_{t\in I}\nu_t(s)\log r_t \qquad(s\in F). $$

3.2. Comparing two feasible labels

Write $r_t=p_t/q_t'$ with positive integer numerator and denominator. For $s,u\in F$, let
$$ \delta_t=D\bigl(\nu_t(s)-\nu_t(u)\bigr)\in\mathbb Z. $$
Then
$$ D\bigl(\ell(s)-\ell(u)\bigr) = \sum_{t\in I}\delta_t\log(p_t/q_t'). $$
Define
$$ A_{s,u} = \prod_{\delta_t>0}p_t^{\delta_t} \prod_{\delta_t<0}(q_t')^{-\delta_t}, $$
and
$$ B_{s,u} = \prod_{\delta_t>0}(q_t')^{\delta_t} \prod_{\delta_t<0}p_t^{-\delta_t}. $$
All these products are positive, and
$$ \boxed{ \ell(s)\ge\ell(u) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_{s,u}\ge B_{s,u}. } $$

3.3. Encoding size

After reducing the input mass fractions, $D$ is the least common multiple of their denominators. Hence
$$ D\le \prod_{\text{mass denominators }b}b, $$
so its binary length is $O(L)$. Computing it by successive greatest-common-divisor calculations takes polynomial time.

Also,
$$ 0\le D\nu_t(s)\le D, \qquad |\delta_t|\le D. $$
Thus every exponent has $O(L)$ bits. The numerators and denominators of $r_t$ have polynomial bit length as well.

Each comparison is therefore a polynomial-size instance of $\mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}$. A tournament using at most $m-1$ comparisons selects an optimal label. This proves a polynomial-time Turing reduction from exact selection to $\mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}$.

4. Product comparison reduces to selection with three labels and dyadic inputs

The converse requires representing arbitrary integer bases using dyadic accuracies.

4.1. A representation lemma

For three labels, define
$$ r(x)=\frac{2x}{1-x}. $$

Lemma. For every positive integer $a$, one can construct in polynomial time dyadic accuracies $x_j\in[1/2,1)$ and integer coefficients $z_j$ such that
$$ \log a=\sum_jz_j\log r(x_j). $$
The complete representation has size polynomial in the binary length of $a$.

Proof. Write
$$ a=2^v u, $$
where $u$ is odd. If $u>1$, choose the integer $k$ satisfying
$$ 2^{k-1}<u<2^k. $$
Set
$$ c=2^k-u,\qquad x=\frac{u}{2^k}. $$
Since $u$ is odd,
$$ 0<c<2^{k-1}, $$
and $c$ is odd. Moreover,
$$ \frac12<x<1, \qquad r(x)=\frac{2u}{c}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \log u=\log r(x)-\log2+\log c. $$

The binary length of $c$ is strictly smaller than that of $u$. Repetition therefore reaches one after at most the binary length of $a$ steps. If the successive accuracies are $x_1,\ldots,x_h$, telescoping gives
$$ \log a = \sum_{j=1}^{h}\log r(x_j)+(v-h)\log2. $$
Finally,
$$ r(1/2)=2. $$
Each constructed accuracy has at most the original base’s binary length in its numerator and denominator, and all operations are polynomial-time integer operations. This proves the lemma. $\square$

4.2. Constructing the society

Given a product-comparison instance, write
$$ P=\prod_i a_i^{b_i}, \qquad Q=\prod_j c_j^{d_j}, \qquad \Lambda=\log(P/Q). $$
Applying the lemma to every base and multiplying by its signed exponent yields
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{r}z_j\log r(x_j), $$
where zero coefficients are discarded and
$$ \frac12\le x_j<1. $$
The representation has polynomial size: it introduces at most a linear number of terms per base bit length, and multiplying binary-encoded coefficients increases their bit lengths only polynomially.

Let
$$ M=\sum_{j=1}^{r}|z_j|, $$
and choose a power of two $H>M$. If $M=0$, take $H=1$.

Use labels $S=\{1,2,3\}$. For every remaining term, create a type with accuracy $x_j$ and mass
$$ \mu_j=\frac{|z_j|}{H}. $$
Assign all its response mass to label $1$ when $z_j>0$, and to label $2$ when $z_j<0$.

Add an auxiliary type with
$$ x_{\mathrm{aux}}=\frac12, \qquad \mu_{\mathrm{aux}}=1-\frac MH, $$
and responses
$$ \nu_{\mathrm{aux}}(1) = \nu_{\mathrm{aux}}(2) = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{aux}}}{2}, \qquad \nu_{\mathrm{aux}}(3)=0. $$

All supported masses are positive and sum to one. Every response row sums to its type mass. All accuracies and masses are dyadic, and their encoding length is polynomial. Every conditional response probability is positive because all accuracies lie strictly between zero and one; therefore the observation satisfies the promise.

For this society, define
$$ W(s)=\sum_t\nu_t(s)\log r(x_t). $$
Its maximizers are exactly those of $\ell$. Every constructed weight is positive because $r(x_t)\ge2$. Hence
$$ W(3)=0, \qquad W(1),W(2)\ge \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{aux}}}{2}\log2>0. $$
Label $3$ cannot maximize. The auxiliary contribution cancels between the other labels, giving
$$ W(1)-W(2)=\frac{\Lambda}{H}. $$
Thus:

  • If $P>Q$, label $1$ is uniquely optimal.
  • If $P<Q$, label $2$ is uniquely optimal.
  • If $P=Q$, labels $1$ and $2$ are both optimal.

4.3. Decision reduction and arbitrary tie handling

Consider the decision problem: given a valid society and a specified label, is that label Bayes-optimal? The construction proves
$$ P\ge Q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{label }1\text{ is Bayes-optimal}. $$
Therefore $\mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}$ has a polynomial-time many-one reduction to this decision problem, already with three labels and dyadic interior accuracies.

For an oracle that returns an arbitrary optimal label, equality must be handled separately. The theorem needed is that equality of products of positive integer bases with binary-encoded exponents is decidable in polynomial time. After removing zero exponents and unit bases, its hypotheses apply directly; empty products are handled separately. [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis, Proposition 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1304.5429v3#S2)

First test $P=Q$. If they differ, one selection-oracle call distinguishes their strict ordering through its unique optimal label. Together with Section 3, this establishes
$$ \boxed{ \text{Exact selection is polynomial-time} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}\in\mathsf P. } $$
The equivalence concerns the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm that may return any maximizing label.

5. A sharper characterization for two labels

5.1. General binary-encoded rational accuracies

When $K=2$, the weight is
$$ \log\frac{x}{1-x}. $$
For every integer $a>1$, taking
$$ x=\frac{a}{a+1} $$
gives
$$ \frac{x}{1-x}=a. $$

Thus arbitrary product comparison reduces directly to two-label selection: express $\Lambda$ as a signed sum of logarithms of the input bases, assign masses $|z_j|/H$ to their corresponding types, and place their responses on label $1$ or $2$ according to the coefficient’s sign. Add the remaining mass as an auxiliary type with accuracy $1/2$, splitting its responses equally.

The auxiliary weight is zero, and the resulting score difference is $\Lambda/H$. All accuracies are interior, all masses are dyadic, and the construction has polynomial size. Equality preprocessing handles arbitrary oracle ties exactly as in Section 4.

Therefore, for general binary-encoded rational accuracies, the full product-comparison equivalence already holds with two labels.

5.2. Dyadic accuracies

Define $\mathsf{OddPoE}_{\ge}$ by restricting $\mathsf{PoE}_{\ge}$ to odd positive integer bases.

Proposition. Exact two-label selection with dyadic inputs is polynomial-time Turing equivalent to $\mathsf{OddPoE}_{\ge}$.

Forward reduction. Write an interior dyadic accuracy in reduced form as
$$ x=\frac{n}{2^k}, \qquad 0<n<2^k. $$
Here $k\ge1$ and $n$ is odd. Therefore
$$ \frac{x}{1-x} = \frac{n}{2^k-n} $$
has odd numerator and odd denominator. After removing infeasible labels, the comparison construction in Section 3 consequently uses only odd bases.

Reverse reduction. For any odd integer $u>1$, use
$$ 2^{k-1}<u<2^k,\qquad c=2^k-u,\qquad x=\frac{u}{2^k}. $$
Now the two-label odds ratio satisfies
$$ \frac{x}{1-x}=\frac{u}{c}, $$
so
$$ \log u=\log\frac{x}{1-x}+\log c. $$
The integer $c$ is positive, odd, and has strictly smaller binary length. Iteration expresses $\log u$ as a sum of logarithms of dyadic two-label odds ratios, in polynomial size.

Apply this representation to all odd bases. The signed-mass construction from Section 5.1 then produces a dyadic two-label society whose score difference is $\Lambda/H$. Polynomial-time product equality testing again handles ties. This proves the proposition.

This characterizes the restricted problem; it does not establish a complexity separation between odd-base and unrestricted product comparison.

6. Exact consequences of the reductions

6.1. Fixed number of distinct interior accuracies

Let $d$ be the number of distinct values among the interior accuracies. Types sharing an accuracy have the same odds ratio. In a comparison, group their integer exponents:
$$ \Delta_x = \sum_{\substack{t\in I\\x_t=x}}\delta_t. $$
Each grouped exponent is the difference between two response counts in that group, so
$$ |\Delta_x|\le D. $$
The comparison products then have at most $d$ factors each.

The arithmetic theorem used here states that product comparison is unconditionally polynomial-time when both factor counts are fixed constants, and is polynomial-time for unrestricted factor counts assuming the Lang–Waldschmidt conjecture. [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis, Proposition 3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/1304.5429v3#S3)

Its fixed-factor hypothesis applies when $d$ is fixed. Hence exact selection is polynomial-time for every fixed number of distinct interior accuracies, even if $\tau$ varies. Fixed $\tau$ is a special case.

The general conditional theorem applies to the polynomial-size comparison instances from Section 3. Thus, under the Lang–Waldschmidt conjecture, exact selection is polynomial-time for unrestricted support size.

6.2. Posterior ties are polynomial-time decidable

For two given labels, feasibility distinguishes a zero posterior from a positive posterior. Two infeasible labels both have posterior zero. For two feasible labels,
$$ \Pr(S^*=s\mid E)=\Pr(S^*=u\mid E) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_{s,u}=B_{s,u}. $$
The product-equality theorem therefore decides posterior equality in polynomial time.

Consequently, once one optimal label is known, the entire optimal-label set can be recovered in polynomial time by testing equality with every other label. The unresolved difficulty is ordering unequal scores.

7. Unconditional polynomial-time approximation of posterior correctness

The count interpretation permits a useful guarantee stronger than merely approximating the mass-normalized score.

Proposition. Given a valid society and a rational tolerance $0<\varepsilon<1$, one can select a label $\widehat s$ satisfying
$$ \Pr(S^*=\widehat s\mid E) \ge e^{-\varepsilon} \max_{s\in S}\Pr(S^*=s\mid E) \ge (1-\varepsilon) \max_{s\in S}\Pr(S^*=s\mid E). $$
The algorithm runs in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ and the encoding length of $\varepsilon$. In particular, for $\varepsilon=2^{-j}$ its running time is polynomial in $m,\tau,L,j$.

7.1. Polynomial-time logarithm approximation

For completeness, the logarithms can be approximated using rational arithmetic alone.

For a positive integer $a$, write
$$ a=2^k y,\qquad 1\le y<2, $$
and put
$$ z=\frac{y-1}{y+1}. $$
Then $0\le z<1/3$. Integrating the geometric series for $2/(1-z^2)$ gives
$$ \log y = 2\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2h+1}}{2h+1}. $$
After $N$ terms, the remainder is at most
$$ 2\sum_{h=N}^{\infty}z^{2h+1} \le \frac{2(1/3)^{2N+1}}{1-1/9} < 3\cdot9^{-N}. $$
The same bound approximates $\log2$ by using $z=1/3$. Since
$$ \log a=k\log2+\log y, $$
the combined error is at most
$$ 3(k+1)9^{-N}. $$

Therefore error at most $2^{-j}$ requires only
$$ N=O\bigl(j+\log(k+1)\bigr) $$
terms. Every term is rational. If $a$ has $b$ bits, straightforward exact summation uses rational numbers with polynomial bit length in $N$ and $b$: the powers have bit length $O(Nb)$, and even multiplying all term denominators gives bit length $O(N^2b+N\log N)$.

Thus $\log a$ can be approximated in time polynomial in $b$ and $j$. Approximating $\log p-\log q$ proves the corresponding result for a positive rational $p/q$.

7.2. Approximating the count-scaled scores

Remove infeasible labels as in Section 3. For $s\in F$, define
$$ B(s)=\sum_{t\in I}n_t(s)\log r_t. $$
Because $\ell(s)=C+B(s)/D$, posterior ratios satisfy
$$ \frac{\Pr(S^*=s\mid E)}{\Pr(S^*=u\mid E)} = \exp\bigl(B(s)-B(u)\bigr) \qquad(s,u\in F). $$

Approximate every $\log r_t$ by a rational $\widetilde w_t$ with
$$ |\widetilde w_t-\log r_t| \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2D}, $$
and compute
$$ \widetilde B(s)=\sum_{t\in I}n_t(s)\widetilde w_t. $$
For every label,
$$ \sum_{t\in I}n_t(s) \le \sum_{t\in T}N_t = D. $$
Hence
$$ |\widetilde B(s)-B(s)|\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$

Select $\widehat s$ maximizing the rational values $\widetilde B(s)$. If $s_{\max}$ maximizes $B$, then
$$ \begin{aligned} B(s_{\max})-B(\widehat s) &\le \bigl(\widetilde B(s_{\max})+\varepsilon/2\bigr) - \bigl(\widetilde B(\widehat s)-\varepsilon/2\bigr)\\ &\le\varepsilon. \end{aligned} $$
Therefore
$$ \frac{\Pr(S^*=\widehat s\mid E)} {\Pr(S^*=s_{\max}\mid E)} = \exp\bigl(B(\widehat s)-B(s_{\max})\bigr) \ge e^{-\varepsilon}. $$
The inequality $e^{-\varepsilon}\ge1-\varepsilon$ completes the correctness proof.

Finally, $D$ has $O(L)$ bits. The required logarithmic precision therefore uses
$$ O\bigl(L+\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr) $$
bits after the binary point. Section 7.1 and exact rational comparisons give the claimed polynomial running time.

This approximation theorem does not provide an exact algorithm: it supplies no polynomial precision bound that separates every pair of unequal scores.
