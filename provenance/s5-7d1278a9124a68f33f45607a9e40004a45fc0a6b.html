The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given types \(t\in\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), eligibility sets \(\Gamma_t\subseteq\mathcal R\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and a common monotone submodular utility \(f:2^{\mathcal R}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), each unit of type mass must receive an integral bundle \(S\subseteq\Gamma_t\). Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass receiving \(S\); require \(\sum_{S\subseteq\Gamma_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le b_j\) for each \(j\in\mathcal R\). Find the largest \(z\) such that \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(f(S)\ge z\), or approximate that optimum, with running time polynomial in \(|\mathcal R|\), \(|\mathcal T|\), and the binary input length given value-oracle access to \(f\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m=|\mathcal R|\geq1\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types.
Each type has an eligibility set \(\Gamma_t\subseteq\mathcal R\); resource \(j\) has binary-rational per-capita supply \(b_j\geq0\). A common normalized monotone submodular utility \(f:2^{\mathcal R}\to\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\), with \(f(\varnothing)=0\), is supplied by an exact value oracle. Let \(L\) include the explicit input length and a bound on the binary length of oracle answers.
An allocation divides type mass among integral bundles: \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) for \(S\subseteq\Gamma_t\), with \(\sum_{S\subseteq\Gamma_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\leq b_j\). Unused supply and empty bundles are allowed.
Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the largest \(z\geq0\) admitting such an allocation with \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow f(S)\geq z\). An approximation allocation is represented by its nonzero binary-rational masses and corresponding bundles.

Does this problem admit a randomized \(O(1+\log\log(m+2))\)-approximation in time and oracle queries polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or can such a guarantee be ruled out by computational hardness or an oracle lower bound?
The approximation requires a feasible allocation whose every positive-mass bundle has utility at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha(m)\), with \(\alpha(m)=O(1+\log\log(m+2))\), with probability at least \(2/3\).

For finite players and indivisible resources, a randomized polynomial-time \(O(\log\log m)\)-approximation is known. The fractional configuration formulation with unit player demands and unit resource capacities also has a polynomial-time utility guarantee of \((1-1/e)/2\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.06939)
No result explicitly covering arbitrary binary-rational type masses and supplies was verified.

The proof gives an \(8\)-approximation when masses are rationals represented by binary-encoded numerators and denominators. It also gives a \(32\)-approximation under the stricter interpretation that “binary-rational” means dyadic and the output masses must be dyadic.

Thus one may take \(\alpha(m)=32=O(1+\log\log(m+2))\), with success probability \(1\).

The relevant starting point in the cited paper is its configuration-LP approximation, rather than its integral allocation theorem. See [Theorem 2 and Appendix B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.06939). 2. Configuration feasibility and its alternative system

Replace each supply by \(\min\{b_j,1\}\). This preserves the problem: since the total population mass is \(1\), an allocation uses at most one unit of any resource. Henceforth, \(0\leq b_j\leq1\).

For a threshold \(a>0\), define

\[ \mathcal C_t(a)=\{S\subseteq\Gamma_t:f(S)\geq a\}. \]

Consider the covering configuration system

\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal C_t(a)}x_{t,S}\geq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\sum_{\substack{S\in\mathcal C_t(a)\\j\in S}}x_{t,S}\leq b_j,\qquad x_{t,S}\geq0. \]

This system is feasible exactly when the original problem admits utility threshold \(a\). Indeed, given a covering solution, put \(q_t=\sum_Sx_{t,S}\). Since \(q_t\geq\mu_t>0\), replacing each \(x_{t,S}\) by \((\mu_t/q_t)x_{t,S}\) makes the type constraint an equality and only decreases resource use.

Write \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\). By Farkas’ lemma, the covering system is infeasible exactly when there exist \(y_t,p_j\geq0\) satisfying

\[ p(S)\geq y_t\quad\text{for every }t\text{ and }S\in\mathcal C_t(a), \qquad \sum_t\mu_ty_t-\sum_jb_jp_j>0. \]

The strict objective inequality can be normalized to

\[ \sum_t\mu_ty_t-\sum_jb_jp_j\geq1 \]

by scaling \(y,p\).

For completeness, such a point directly contradicts any covering solution:

\[ \sum_t\mu_ty_t \leq\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}y_t \leq\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}p(S) \leq\sum_jb_jp_j. \]

The masses and supplies occur only in this objective inequality. The configuration inequalities retain their usual form.

3. An elementary strict-budget knapsack lemma

Lemma. Given a normalized monotone submodular function \(F\), nonnegative rational costs \(c_j\), and a rational budget \(B>0\), one can deterministically find a set \(A\) such that

\[ c(A)<B,\qquad F(A)\geq\frac14\max_{c(S)<B}F(S), \]

using polynomial time and \(O(m^2)\) value queries.

Proof. Include all zero-cost items in a set \(Z\), and discard items of cost at least \(B\). Every strictly feasible set can be augmented by \(Z\), and no discarded item can occur in such a set.

On the remaining positive-cost items define

\[ g(A)=F(Z\cup A)-F(Z). \]

This function is normalized, monotone, and submodular. Let \(M\) be its maximum under the strict budget.

Starting with the empty set, repeatedly choose an unselected item maximizing marginal value divided by cost. Stop immediately before the first choice \(e\) that would make the accumulated cost at least \(B\). Let \(P\) be the preceding prefix.

If all items are selected without reaching \(B\), their union with \(Z\) is optimal. Otherwise, both \(Z\cup P\) and \(Z\cup\{e\}\) are strictly feasible: \(c(P)<B\), and every retained item has cost less than \(B\).

We bound their values. At any greedy prefix \(A\), let \(\rho\) be the largest remaining marginal-value-to-cost ratio. For an optimal strictly feasible set \(O\), monotonicity and submodularity imply

\[ M-g(A) \leq g(A\cup O)-g(A) \leq\sum_{j\in O\setminus A}g(j\mid A) \leq B\rho. \]

For the analysis only, take a fraction \(\theta=(B-c(P))/c_e\) of the last item, where \(0<\theta\leq1\). Define the resulting numerical value

\[ V=g(P)+\theta g(e\mid P). \]

Each complete greedy step of cost \(d\), and the final fractional step of cost \(d\), decreases the residual \(M-\text{value}\) by at least the fraction \(d/B\) of that residual. If the value ever reaches \(M\), there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, multiplying these inequalities gives

\[ M-V \leq M\prod_i(1-d_i/B) \leq M\exp\!\left(-\sum_i d_i/B\right) =M/e. \]

Here the step costs sum to \(B\). Consequently, \(V\geq(1-1/e)M\geq M/2\).

By diminishing returns,

\[ V\leq g(P)+g(\{e\}), \]

so the better of \(P\) and \(\{e\}\) has \(g\)-value at least \(M/4\). Adding \(Z\) gives value at least

\[ F(Z)+M/4\geq(F(Z)+M)/4. \]

All choices and comparisons use exact rational arithmetic. There are at most \(m\) greedy steps and \(m\) marginal evaluations per step. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

4. A polynomial threshold procedure

We prove the following algorithmic statement.

Proposition. For every positive rational \(T\), a deterministic polynomial-time procedure returns either:

  • an exactly feasible allocation whose positive-mass bundles have utility at least \(T/4\); or
  • a valid certificate that threshold \(T\) is infeasible.

4.1. The cuts and certificates

Apply an ellipsoid cutting procedure in the variables \(y,p\). At each queried point, first check

\[ y,p\geq0,\qquad \sum_t\mu_ty_t-\sum_jb_jp_j\geq1. \]

Any violated inequality supplies a cut.

If these inequalities hold, then for each type with \(y_t>0\), apply the strict-budget lemma to \(f\) restricted to \(\Gamma_t\), with costs \(p_j\) and budget \(y_t\). It returns \(S_t\subseteq\Gamma_t\) satisfying

\[ p(S_t)<y_t,\qquad f(S_t)\geq\frac14M_t, \]

where

\[ M_t=\max\{f(S):S\subseteq\Gamma_t,\ p(S)<y_t\}. \]

If some returned set has \(f(S_t)\geq T/4\), return the violated configuration inequality

\[ p(S_t)\geq y_t \]

as a cut, and record the pair \((t,S_t)\). This set is nonempty because \(T>0\).

If no returned set reaches \(T/4\), then \(M_t<T\) for every type with \(y_t>0\). Therefore every configuration of utility at least \(T\) has price at least \(y_t\). For types with \(y_t=0\), this inequality follows from nonnegative prices. The current point is consequently a Farkas certificate that threshold \(T\) is infeasible.

Thus cuts use configurations of utility at least \(T/4\), while accepted certificates establish infeasibility at \(T\).

4.2. A uniform bound that ensures polynomial termination

The remaining issue is to justify that, if no certificate is accepted, polynomially many cuts suffice to recover a feasible allocation.

Let \(d=\tau+m\), and let \(H\) be any finite family of recorded configuration pairs. Define

\[ D_H= \left\{(y,p): y,p\geq0,\ \sum_t\mu_ty_t-\sum_jb_jp_j\geq1,\ p(S)\geq y_t\ \text{for }(t,S)\in H \right\}. \]

We establish a uniform interior-ball bound for every nonempty \(D_H\).

Let \(Q\) be a common denominator of the input masses and clamped supplies. Since their total encoding length is bounded by \(L\), we can use \(Q\leq2^L\). The polyhedron \(D_H\) lies in the nonnegative orthant and contains no line. If it is nonempty, it has a vertex: minimize the sum of its coordinates; the minimum is attained, and the minimizing face is compact.

At a vertex, choose \(d\) independent active constraints. After clearing the denominator in the objective row, every other row has entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and the objective row and its right-hand side have absolute values at most \(Q\). Cramer’s rule therefore bounds every coordinate by

\[ B=2^L d!. \]

In particular, \(B\) has polynomial binary length.

Take such a feasible point \(q=(y,p)\), and put

\[ h=\frac1{8m},\qquad v=2q+(h\mathbf1_\tau,\,2h\mathbf1_m). \]

Every coordinate of \(v\) is at least \(h\). Since all recorded configurations are nonempty, each configuration inequality has slack at least

\[ 2h|S|-h\geq h. \]

The objective inequality has slack bounded uniformly away from zero:

\[ \sum_t\mu_tv_{y,t}-\sum_jb_jv_{p,j} \geq2+h-2h\sum_jb_j \geq\frac74. \]

Every constraint normal has Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt d\): this is immediate for configuration and coordinate inequalities, and for the objective follows from \(0\leq\mu_t,b_j\leq1\).

Consequently, the Euclidean ball of radius

\[ r=\frac{h}{4d} \]

around \(v\) lies in \(D_H\). It also lies in the ball about the origin of radius

\[ R=4d(B+1). \]

Both \(\log R\) and \(\log(1/r)\) are polynomially bounded in \(m,\tau,L\).

We now use the standard rational ellipsoid theorem in its volume-reduction form: starting with a radius-\(R\) ball, polynomially many violated rational linear cuts produce an ellipsoid of volume smaller than a radius-\(r\) ball. The implementation preserves containment and uses polynomial-length rational numbers. Here the dimension is \(d\), and every cut has polynomial-length coefficients.

Run the procedure for this polynomial number of cuts unless an infeasibility certificate is accepted earlier. Let \(H\) contain all recorded configuration pairs.

Suppose the covering primal restricted to \(H\) were infeasible. Farkas’ lemma would make \(D_H\) nonempty. The preceding argument would then place a radius-\(r\) ball inside both the initial ellipsoid and every returned halfspace. Ellipsoid containment would preserve that ball throughout the run, contradicting the final volume bound.

Therefore the covering primal restricted to \(H\) is feasible.

4.3. Recovering the allocation

There are only polynomially many recorded configurations. Solve the resulting explicit rational LP, using type equalities instead of covering inequalities; feasibility follows by the scaling argument in Section 2.

A basic feasible solution has at most \(\tau+m\) positive configuration variables. Its masses have polynomial binary length: the constraint matrix has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), and the right-hand sides have encoding length bounded by \(L\), so the usual determinant bounds apply.

Every retained configuration has utility at least \(T/4\). The resulting allocation is exactly feasible, completing the proposition. \(\square\)

5. Searching thresholds gives an \(8\)-approximation

The optimum is attained. Indeed, there are finitely many eligible bundles, and feasibility changes only when the threshold crosses one of their utility values. Threshold zero is always feasible.

Under the oracle-answer length bound, every positive utility lies between \(2^{-L}\) and \(2^L\). Hence either \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\), or

\[ 2^{-L}\leq\mathrm{OPT}\leq2^L. \]

Run the threshold procedure for every \(T=2^k\), where \(-L\leq k\leq L\), and retain an allocation from the largest successful threshold.

If \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\), one tested threshold satisfies

\[ T\leq\mathrm{OPT}<2T. \]

At this threshold the procedure cannot return an infeasibility certificate, so it returns an allocation with every positive-mass bundle having utility at least

\[ T/4>\mathrm{OPT}/8. \]

If no positive-threshold allocation is returned, then \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\), and assigning the entire mass of each type to the empty bundle is valid.

There are \(2L+1\) threshold calls. Thus the running time, query count, support size, and output encoding length are all polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

6. Exact dyadic output, if required

An ordinary rational LP solution need not be dyadic. We therefore prove the stricter output guarantee separately.

Lemma. Suppose all input masses and supplies are dyadic. Given a polynomial-size rational feasible allocation whose bundles have utility at least \(v>0\), one can construct, in polynomial time, a feasible allocation with dyadic masses and bundle utilities at least \(v/4\).

Proof. Partition resources into

\[ F=\{j:f(\{j\})\geq v/4\},\qquad N=\mathcal R\setminus F. \]

Transform each positive-mass bundle \(S\) as follows.

If \(S\) contains a resource \(j\in F\), replace it by the singleton \(\{j\}\), retaining its mass.

Otherwise, \(S\subseteq N\). Add elements of \(S\) until a subset \(A\) first reaches utility \(v/4\). Such a subset exists because \(f(S)\geq v\). Every singleton in \(N\) has utility less than \(v/4\), and submodularity bounds each marginal gain by the corresponding singleton value. Thus

\[ v/4\leq f(A)<v/2. \]

Normalized submodularity gives subadditivity on disjoint sets, so

\[ f(S\setminus A)\geq f(S)-f(A)>v/2. \]

Replace the original mass \(x\) by mass \(x/2\) on each of \(A\) and \(S\setminus A\).

The transformed allocation is feasible and has utility at least \(v/4\). Moreover, every resource in \(N\) now has load at most \(b_j/2\). Resources in \(F\) occur only in singleton bundles.

Let \(K\) be the number of positive bundles contained in \(N\). If \(K=0\), only the flow construction below is needed. Otherwise choose a dyadic grid size \(\delta\) such that every \(\mu_t\) and \(b_j\) is an integer multiple of \(\delta\), and

\[ K\delta\leq\frac14\min_{\substack{j\in N\\b_j>0}}b_j. \]

The minimum exists when \(K>0\): every resource in a positive-mass bundle has positive supply. A suitable \(\delta\) has polynomial encoding length.

For a type with \(n_t>0\) bundles contained in \(N\), write their masses as \(a_1,\ldots,a_{n_t}\), and let \(q_t=\sum_i a_i\). Set

\[ r_t=\delta\left\lceil q_t/\delta\right\rceil. \]

Because \(\mu_t\) is a grid multiple and \(q_t\leq\mu_t\), we have \(q_t\leq r_t\leq\mu_t\).

Round the first \(n_t-1\) masses down to grid multiples, and assign the last bundle mass

\[ r_t-\sum_{i<n_t}\delta\left\lfloor a_i/\delta\right\rfloor. \]

This last mass is positive. It increases by less than \(n_t\delta\); all other masses decrease. Types without such bundles have \(r_t=0\).

Therefore the load of any resource in \(N\) increases by less than \(K\delta\). For positive supply it remains at most

\[ b_j/2+K\delta\leq3b_j/4. \]

A zero-supply resource occurs in none of these bundles.

It remains to allocate mass \(\mu_t-r_t\) of each type to eligible singleton resources in \(F\). The previous singleton allocation served mass \(\mu_t-q_t\), which is at least \(\mu_t-r_t\). Hence this residual bipartite flow problem is feasible.

All its demands and capacities are multiples of \(\delta\). Scale them by \(1/\delta\), solve the resulting integer-capacity maximum-flow problem, and scale back. Flow integrality gives dyadic masses exactly. Polynomial-time flow algorithms depend polynomially on the binary encoding of capacities, rather than their numerical magnitudes.

The resulting allocation has the exact prescribed type masses, respects all supplies, and uses only bundles of utility at least \(v/4\). Its support and encoding length are polynomial. \(\square\)

Applying this lemma to the \(8\)-approximation produces a deterministic \(32\)-approximation with exact dyadic output. This settles the requested guarantee under either rational-encoding interpretation.
