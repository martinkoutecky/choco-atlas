The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational locations \(a_1,\ldots,a_\tau\in[0,1]\), positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), can one compute a rational facility location \(\hat y\in[0,1]\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\), such that \(G_\mu(\hat y)\ge \max_{y\in[0,1]}G_\mu(y)-\varepsilon\), where \(G_\mu(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau}(1-|y-a_j|)^{\mu_j}\) and positive-mass types at zero utility give welfare zero?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge 1\) distinct rational locations \(a_j\in[0,1]\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_j\), where \(\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There is one facility, so the facility-count parameter is fixed at \(m=1\). A facility location \(y\in[0,1]\) gives type \(j\) utility \(u_j(y)=1-|y-a_j|\) and welfare \(G_\mu(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau}u_j(y)^{\mu_j}\); welfare is zero if any positive-mass type has zero utility.
The input also contains a binary-rational \(\varepsilon>0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including locations, masses, and \(\varepsilon\). Any rational facility location meeting the welfare guarantee is admissible.

Does an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\) always compute a rational \(\hat y\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(G_\mu(\hat y)\ge\max_{y\in[0,1]}G_\mu(y)-\varepsilon\)?

For an explicit list of \(n\) agents, additive approximation of the unnormalized product \(\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-|y-x_i|)\) is known in time polynomial in the input and \(1/\varepsilon\); exact optimal locations can be irrational. [Lam, Aziz and Walsh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.04102).
No result settling this precise additive guarantee for arbitrary binary-rational support masses was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Locations identify types, so repeated locations are consolidated by adding their masses; the support is nonempty and may have size one.
  • L denotes the total binary input length, including the accuracy parameter.
  • The generic parameter m is interpreted as the facility count and fixed at one.
  • No tie-breaking rule is imposed: any rational location satisfying the additive welfare guarantee is acceptable.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Claim (proved). Yes. In fact, a rational location satisfying the requested guarantee can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), with no polynomial dependence on \(1/\varepsilon\) needed. The algorithm uses exact rational arithmetic and never evaluates logarithms or fractional powers.

1. Restricting the search interval.

Each function \(u_j(y)^{\mu_j}\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), including where its utility is zero. Thus \(G_\mu\) is continuous and attains a maximum, denoted by \(G^*=G_\mu(y^*)\). Since every utility lies in \([0,1]\), we have \(G^*\leq 1\).

If \(\varepsilon\geq 1\), output \(\hat y=1/2\). Henceforth assume \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Set

\(\delta=\varepsilon/4,\qquad I=[\delta,1-\delta].\)

We first prove that restricting optimization to \(I\) loses at most \(\varepsilon/2\).

The function \(u_j(y)=1-|y-a_j|\) is concave, because absolute value is convex. Consequently, for

\(\bar y=(1-\varepsilon/2)y^*+(\varepsilon/2)(1/2),\)

we have \(\bar y\in I\) and

\(u_j(\bar y)\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)u_j(y^*)+(\varepsilon/2)u_j(1/2)\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)u_j(y^*).\)

Raising these inequalities to the positive powers \(\mu_j\), multiplying, and using \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), gives

\(G_\mu(\bar y)\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)G^*\geq G^*-\varepsilon/2.\)

Therefore, writing \(M_I=\max_{y\in I}G_\mu(y)\), we obtain

\(M_I\geq G^*-\varepsilon/2.\)

2. A quantitative continuity bound.

For every \(y\in I\) and \(a_j\in[0,1]\), the distance \(|y-a_j|\) is at most \(1-\delta\). Thus \(u_j(y)\geq\delta>0\), and we may define

\(F(y)=\log G_\mu(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\log u_j(y).\)

For \(y,z\in I\), the triangle inequality gives \(|u_j(y)-u_j(z)|\leq|y-z|\). Since the derivative of \(\log t\) is at most \(1/\delta\) on \([\delta,1]\), the mean value theorem yields

\(|\log u_j(y)-\log u_j(z)|\leq|y-z|/\delta.\)

Summing with the weights \(\mu_j\) gives

\(|F(y)-F(z)|\leq|y-z|/\delta.\)

Both values of \(F\) are nonpositive. The derivative of the exponential function is at most \(1\) on the nonpositive real axis, so another application of the mean value theorem gives

\(|G_\mu(y)-G_\mu(z)|\leq|F(y)-F(z)|\leq(4/\varepsilon)|y-z|.\)

This bound is independent of the smallest support mass.

3. Exact rational bisection.

The function \(F\) is concave on \(I\). Indeed, for \(x,z\in I\) and \(t\in[0,1]\), concavity of \(u_j\), followed by monotonicity and concavity of the logarithm, gives

\(\log u_j((1-t)x+tz)\geq\log((1-t)u_j(x)+tu_j(z))\geq(1-t)\log u_j(x)+t\log u_j(z).\)

Taking the weighted sum proves concavity of \(F\). Since logarithm is strictly increasing, \(F\) and \(G_\mu\) have exactly the same maximizers on \(I\).

At an interior point \(c\in I\), the left and right derivatives of \(F\) are

\(D_-(c)=\sum_{a_j\geq c}\frac{\mu_j}{u_j(c)}-\sum_{a_j<c}\frac{\mu_j}{u_j(c)},\)

\(D_+(c)=\sum_{a_j>c}\frac{\mu_j}{u_j(c)}-\sum_{a_j\leq c}\frac{\mu_j}{u_j(c)}.\)

To verify these formulas, observe that \(u_j\) has slope \(+1\) to the left of \(a_j\) and slope \(-1\) to its right. At \(c=a_j\), its utility is \(1\), so its contribution to the left derivative is \(+\mu_j\), and to the right derivative is \(-\mu_j\). In particular,

\(D_-(c)-D_+(c)=2\sum_{a_j=c}\mu_j\geq0.\)

When \(c\) is rational, both derivatives are rational and their signs can be determined exactly.

The following bisection rules follow from concavity:

  • If \(D_+(c)>0\), every maximizer lies strictly to the right of \(c\).
  • If \(D_-(c)<0\), every maximizer lies strictly to the left of \(c\).
  • Otherwise, \(D_+(c)\leq0\leq D_-(c)\), and \(c\) is a maximizer.

For completeness, concavity implies the secant inequalities

\(F(x)\leq F(c)+D_-(c)(x-c)\quad\text{for }x<c,\)

\(F(x)\leq F(c)+D_+(c)(x-c)\quad\text{for }x>c.\)

These follow by comparing each secant slope with the corresponding one-sided derivative. If \(D_+(c)>0\), then \(D_-(c)>0\), so every point to the left has value below \(F(c)\); the positive right derivative also implies that some point immediately to the right has value above \(F(c)\). This proves the first rule. The second follows by the same argument with left and right reversed. In the third case, the two displayed inequalities prove \(F(x)\leq F(c)\) throughout \(I\).

The algorithm is therefore:

  • Initialize \(\ell=\delta\) and \(r=1-\delta\).
  • While \(r-\ell>\varepsilon^2/4\), set \(c=(\ell+r)/2\) and compute \(D_-(c)\) and \(D_+(c)\) exactly.
  • If \(D_+(c)>0\), replace \(\ell\) by \(c\). If \(D_-(c)<0\), replace \(r\) by \(c\). Otherwise, output \(c\).
  • If the loop ends, output \(\hat y=(\ell+r)/2\).

The derivative rules ensure that the current interval always contains a maximizer \(z\) of \(G_\mu\) over \(I\). If the algorithm outputs during the loop, its output attains \(M_I\). Otherwise,

\(|\hat y-z|\leq(r-\ell)/2\leq\varepsilon^2/8.\)

The continuity bound from Section 2 then gives

\(G_\mu(\hat y)\geq M_I-(4/\varepsilon)(\varepsilon^2/8)=M_I-\varepsilon/2.\)

Combining this with Section 1 proves the required guarantee:

\(G_\mu(\hat y)\geq G^*-\varepsilon.\)

4. Bit complexity.

Each unsuccessful iteration halves the interval length. Since its initial length is at most \(1\), at most \(\lceil\log_2(4/\varepsilon^2)\rceil\) iterations suffice.

All bisection points are rational. After \(k\) iterations, each endpoint and midpoint has bit length \(O(L+k)\): it is a dyadic interpolation between the initial rational endpoints.

At such a point, every derivative summand \(\mu_j/u_j(c)\) has numerator and denominator of bit length \(O(L+k)\). Summing \(\tau\) such fractions using common denominators produces integers of bit length \(O(\tau(L+k))\). Hence both derivative signs, and all other algorithmic comparisons, can be computed by exact integer arithmetic in polynomial time.

Because a positive rational \(\varepsilon<1\) encoded within \(L\) bits satisfies \(\log_2(1/\varepsilon)=O(L)\), the number of iterations is \(O(L)\). The total running time is therefore polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), and the output has bit length \(O(L)\).

This settles the stated guarantee for arbitrary positive binary-rational support masses.

The mathematical argument is sound and establishes the requested polynomial-time additive approximation. There is one minor ambiguity in the algorithm’s conditional wording; making the branches explicit requires no new mathematics.

1. Restricting the interval

Continuity is valid: every utility is nonnegative and continuous, and taking a positive real power preserves continuity, including at zero. The extreme value theorem applies on the compact interval \([0,1]\). Also, every factor lies in \([0,1]\), so \(G^*\leq 1\).

For \(\varepsilon\geq 1\), the proposed output satisfies the guarantee because its welfare is nonnegative and \(G^*-\varepsilon\leq 0\).

For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), the stated convex combination satisfies

The utility inequality follows from concavity and nonnegativity. Raising it to positive powers preserves its direction. Multiplication gives the claimed factor \(1-\varepsilon/2\), precisely because the masses sum to one.

The resulting bound \(M_I\geq G^*-\varepsilon/2\) is correct. This reasoning also remains valid if a utility at \(y^*\) is zero.

2. Quantitative continuity

On \(I\), the bound \(u_j(y)\geq\delta>0\) holds for every support location, including locations at the endpoints of \([0,1]\). Consequently, all logarithms are defined.

The utility functions are Lipschitz with constant one. The mean value theorem for logarithm applies between their values, which lie in \([\delta,1]\), and gives the stated constant \(1/\delta\). Summing with positive weights totaling one preserves that constant.

Since \(F(y)\leq 0\), the exponential mean value theorem applies on an interval where its derivative is at most one. Thus
\[ |G_\mu(y)-G_\mu(z)|\leq (4/\varepsilon)|y-z| \]
is correct. No lower bound on individual masses is required.

3. Concavity and derivative rules

The displayed composition argument correctly proves concavity of each \(\log u_j\) on \(I\). All logarithm arguments are positive, and both monotonicity and concavity are used in the correct directions. Positive weighted summation proves concavity of \(F\).

The one-sided derivative formulas are correct. In particular, a support point equal to \(c\) contributes \(+\mu_j\) on the left and \(-\mu_j\) on the right because its utility there is one. Hence the stated derivative difference is also correct.

The secant inequalities have the correct directions. They establish the three stated cases:

  • A positive right derivative excludes all maximizers at or to the left of \(c\).
  • A negative left derivative excludes all maximizers at or to the right of \(c\).
  • The remaining derivative signs establish that \(c\) is a maximizer.

Bisection midpoints are interior to \(I\), so the derivatives used are available. The argument permits maximizers at either endpoint of \(I\); it does not assume an interior optimum.

4. Algorithm wording

Minor issue — Section 3, algorithm step 3. The wording “If … If … Otherwise” is ambiguous about which condition governs “Otherwise.”

The surrounding three-case discussion makes the intended rule clear: output \(c\) only when neither strict derivative condition holds. However, reading the text as two independent conditionals with an else attached to the second would cause premature output whenever \(D_+(c)>0\).

For a concrete illustration, take \(\tau=1\), \(a_1=3/4\), \(\mu_1=1\), and \(\varepsilon=1/8\). The first midpoint is \(c=1/2\), with
\[ D_-(c)=D_+(c)=4/3. \]
That literal control-flow reading would output \(1/2\), whose welfare is \(3/4\), below the required \(7/8\).

Minimum clarification: Write step 3 as a single “if / else if / else” chain, or explicitly say that output occurs only if both strict tests fail. This is an expositional correction because the intended branching and its justification are already supplied.

5. Approximation guarantee

Under that intended branching, each retained interval contains a maximizer over \(I\). Updating an endpoint does not discard such a maximizer, by the strict derivative rules.

At termination, the midpoint is within \(\varepsilon^2/8\) of a retained maximizer. Multiplying this distance by the established continuity constant gives exactly \(\varepsilon/2\). Combining the two losses gives the requested additive error \(\varepsilon\).

Early termination at a midpoint satisfying the derivative sign condition is also justified.

6. Bit complexity

The iteration bound is correct: halving an initial interval of length at most one reaches the threshold within \(\lceil\log_2(4/\varepsilon^2)\rceil\) unsuccessful iterations.

The rational-size estimates are sound:

  • Dyadic interpolation increases endpoint and midpoint bit lengths by \(O(k)\).
  • Rational subtraction, division by a positive utility, and multiplication produce derivative summands of bit length \(O(L+k)\).
  • A common denominator for \(\tau\) summands has bit length \(O(\tau(L+k))\); numerator summation respects the same asymptotic bound.
  • Exact arithmetic and sign comparisons on these integers take polynomial time.

For a positive rational encoded within \(L\) bits, \(\log_2(1/\varepsilon)=O(L)\). Thus there are \(O(L)\) iterations, total running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), and an output of bit length \(O(L)\). No evaluation of logarithms or fractional powers is needed by the algorithm.
