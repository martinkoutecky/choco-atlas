The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the complete or partial ballot types for three candidates, decide whether there is a split \(x\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), and a candidate \(q\) such that \(q\) wins under IRV in both sub-societies \(x\) and \(\mu-x\), but loses in the full society \(\mu\); return a witnessing split when one exists. Use unique winners or a fixed tie-breaking rule.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let the candidate set be \(C=\{a,b,c\}\), with \(m=3\). A ballot type is a nonempty strict ranking of a subset of \(C\); complete ballots rank all candidates, and partial ballots may rank one or two.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; require \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Use IRV with mass-weighted tallies and exhausted ballots removed from the active tally. Fix priority \(a\succ b\succ c\): whenever elimination requires resolving a tie for the smallest tally, eliminate the lowest-priority tied candidate; a sole remaining candidate wins. Apply this same convention to every society, including tied profiles.
A split assigns real masses \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), with \(0<\sum_t x_t<1\). Run IRV on the unnormalized sub-societies \(x\) and \(\mu-x\). A witness consists of a candidate \(q\) and rational split masses for which \(q\) wins both sub-societies but loses in \(\mu\).

Is deciding whether such a split exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard, for complete ballots and for partial ballots? In the polynomial-time case, can a rational witnessing split also be returned in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

McCune and Wilson give necessary and sufficient inequalities for discrete three-candidate reinforcement partitions with complete ballots, and an analogous characterization allowing partial ballots. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05185v4)
They also explicitly extend their inequalities to proportional electorates, but omit tied outcomes; this does not cover the fixed-priority problem on all rational inputs stated here. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05185v4)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Resolved the permitted choice between unique winners and fixed tie-breaking by selecting fixed priority a above b above c, used consistently for elimination ties in all three elections.
  • Normalized the input distribution to total mass one; sub-societies retain their assigned masses without renormalization.
  • Excluded empty ballots and required both sub-societies to have positive total mass.
  • Specified partial ballots as nonempty strict rankings of subsets and removed exhausted ballots from active tallies.
  • Allowed real split masses for existence and required rational masses in a returned witness.

Result

Both the complete-ballot and partial-ballot problems are in \(\mathrm{P}\). Whenever a split exists, a rational witnessing split can be returned in time polynomial in \(\tau,L\), hence polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) on the stated domain \(m=3\).

The proof handles the prescribed priority rule on every input, including tied profiles. It does not require extending McCune and Wilson’s characterization, whose preliminaries explicitly set aside tied outcomes. [McCune–Wilson](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05185v4)

1. Tallies and exact elimination inequalities

For a remaining candidate set \(R\subseteq C\), let \(h_R(t)\) be the highest-ranked member of \(R\) appearing on ballot type \(t\). If no member appears, set \(h_R(t)=\bot\), meaning that the ballot is exhausted.

For a nonnegative mass vector \(y=(y_t)\), define the tally of \(d\in R\) by

\(T_{R,d}(y)=\sum_{t:h_R(t)=d}y_t.\)

Thus every tally is a linear function with coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\). Exhausted ballots contribute to none of the tallies.

We may run elimination until only one candidate remains, even if the usual IRV implementation stops upon finding a strict majority. Indeed, if \(d\) has a strict majority of active mass, its tally exceeds every other tally. Eliminating another candidate cannot decrease \(d\)’s tally or increase total active mass, so \(d\) retains its strict majority and cannot subsequently be eliminated. Therefore continuing elimination preserves the winner.

Write \(u\succ v\) when \(u\) has higher priority than \(v\). A candidate \(d\in R\) is eliminated exactly when, for every \(v\in R\setminus\{d\}\), the following appropriate inequality holds:

  • If \(v\succ d\), require \(T_{R,v}(y)-T_{R,d}(y)\geq 0\).
  • If \(d\succ v\), require \(T_{R,v}(y)-T_{R,d}(y)>0\).

Justification. These inequalities say that \(d\) has minimum tally and that every candidate of lower priority has strictly larger tally. Consequently, \(d\) is precisely the lowest-priority candidate among those tied for minimum. Conversely, if the rule eliminates \(d\), both requirements necessarily hold.

In particular, the strict and weak comparisons encode ties exactly.

2. A fixed elimination order is described by three inequalities

Fix a proposed winner \(q\), and write \(C\setminus\{q\}=\{e,f\}\).

Let \(\mathcal W_{q,e}\) denote the set of nonnegative mass vectors satisfying the inequalities for:

  • eliminating \(e\) from \(C\);
  • eliminating \(f\) from \(C\setminus\{e\}\).

There are two comparisons in the first elimination and one in the second. Hence \(\mathcal W_{q,e}\) is described by exactly three homogeneous linear inequalities, each strict or weak according to priority.

By Section 1, a positive-mass society has first elimination \(e\) and winner \(q\) if and only if it belongs to \(\mathcal W_{q,e}\).

A useful consequence is closure under addition: if \(u,v\in\mathcal W_{q,e}\), then \(u+v\in\mathcal W_{q,e}\). For every defining linear form \(g\),

\(g(u+v)=g(u)+g(v).\)

Two nonnegative values have nonnegative sum, and two strictly positive values have strictly positive sum. Thus every defining inequality survives addition.

It follows that a witnessing split must have different first eliminations in its two sub-societies. Otherwise, if both first eliminate \(e\) and elect \(q\), their sum \(\mu\) also belongs to \(\mathcal W_{q,e}\) and elects \(q\), contradicting the witness requirement.

Compute the winner \(w\) of \(\mu\) using exact rational arithmetic. There are two possible witnessing candidates \(q\neq w\). For each such \(q\), fix either ordering \((e,f)\) of its two opponents. By exchanging the two sub-societies if necessary, existence of a witness for \(q\) is equivalent to existence of \(x\) satisfying

\(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t,\qquad 0<\sum_t x_t<1,\qquad x\in\mathcal W_{q,e},\qquad \mu-x\in\mathcal W_{q,f}.\)

Thus only two systems need to be tested.

3. Handling strict inequalities by linear programming

For each candidate \(q\neq w\), introduce variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_\tau,\varepsilon\). Construct the following ordinary, closed linear program, maximizing \(\varepsilon\).

First impose

\(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t,\qquad 0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1,\)

and

\(\sum_t x_t\geq\varepsilon,\qquad 1-\sum_t x_t\geq\varepsilon.\)

Next take the six comparison inequalities defining \(x\in\mathcal W_{q,e}\) and \(\mu-x\in\mathcal W_{q,f}\). Write each comparison with its required nonnegative margin on the left:

  • Keep every weak comparison \(g(x)\geq 0\).
  • Replace every strict comparison \(g(x)>0\) by \(g(x)\geq\varepsilon\).

Here \(g\) is affine when it arises from the complementary society \(\mu-x\).

The program has \(\tau+1\) variables and \(2\tau+10\) inequalities. Its feasible region, when nonempty, is compact because all variables are bounded.

Claim. This program has optimum \(\varepsilon^*>0\) if and only if a witnessing split for \(q\) exists.

Proof. Suppose the program has a feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\). Both sub-societies have positive mass. Every required strict comparison holds strictly, and every weak comparison holds. Therefore \(q\) wins both sub-societies. Since \(q\neq w\), the returned split is a witness.

Conversely, suppose a witnessing split exists. By Section 2, orient it so that the first sub-society eliminates \(e\) and the second eliminates \(f\). Consider the finite list consisting of:

  • the masses of the two sub-societies;
  • all margins required to be strictly positive;
  • the number \(1\).

Every entry is positive. Their minimum is therefore a positive real number \(\varepsilon_0\). The witnessing split, together with \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\), satisfies all constraints of the program. Hence its optimum is positive. This argument applies even if the original witnessing split has irrational coordinates. \(\square\)

The decision algorithm accepts exactly when at least one of the two programs is feasible with positive optimum.

4. Polynomial time and rational witness size

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a linear program with explicitly encoded rational coefficients can be solved in time polynomial in its binary encoding length; infeasibility can be detected, and a finite attained optimum can be returned as an exact rational solution. [Rational linear programming and bit complexity](https://lucatrevisan.wordpress.com/2011/01/24/cs261-lecture-5-linear-programming/)

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • All tally coefficients are in \(\{0,1\}\), so comparison coefficients are in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).
  • All constants are rational combinations of the input masses.
  • There are polynomially many variables, constraints, and encoded coefficients.
  • Every feasible program is bounded, so its optimum is attained.

For completeness, a direct denominator bound also shows that the witness needs only polynomially many bits. Write the input masses as \(\mu_t=p_t/d_t\), and let \(D=\prod_t d_t\). Then \(\log_2 D=O(L)\). Multiplying every constraint by \(D\) gives integer coefficients and right-hand sides of absolute value at most \(D\): each constant involving the masses is a signed sum whose absolute value is at most \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

Let \(n=\tau+1\). A nonempty bounded rational polyhedron has an optimal vertex. At a vertex, some \(n\) active constraints have linearly independent coefficient rows; otherwise a sufficiently small displacement in a common null direction would remain feasible in both directions, contradicting extremality.

Cramer’s rule expresses every coordinate of that vertex as a ratio of integer determinants. Hadamard’s inequality bounds the absolute value of each relevant determinant by

\((\sqrt n\,D)^n.\)

Consequently, every coordinate has numerator and denominator bit length \(O(n(L+\log n))\), and the whole split has polynomial encoding length.

An exact polynomial-time LP solver therefore determines whether \(\varepsilon^*>0\) and, when it is, returns rational split masses satisfying all requirements.

5. Complete versus partial ballots

The argument makes no completeness assumption. Partial ballots affect only the map \(h_R(t)\): a ballot with no remaining ranked candidate contributes zero. All tally expressions remain linear, and all priority inequalities remain valid.

Therefore the same algorithm and witness construction work for both ballot models.

The running-time assertion concerns the stipulated three-candidate domain. It does not assert polynomial time when the number of candidates is unrestricted.
