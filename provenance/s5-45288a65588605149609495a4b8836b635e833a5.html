The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given alternatives \(M\), strict-ranking types \(S\), and rational \(q\in(0,1]\), determine the exact frontier \(\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)=\inf\{D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F):\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon\}\) over rules \(F:\Delta(S)\to\Delta(M)\), and characterize its attaining rules. Here \(\epsilon_q(F)\) is the supremum of a type-\(t\) resident's expected utility gain from reporting \(t'\), when the rule's input changes from \((1-q)\nu+qe_t\) to \((1-q)\nu+qe_{t'}\), over \(\nu\in\Delta(S)\), \(t,t'\in S\), and utilities \(u\in U_t\); \(D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)\) is the worst-case Plurality deficit over \(\Delta(S)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) be a nonempty finite set of \(m\) alternatives, \(S\) an explicitly listed nonempty set of \(k\) strict rankings of \(M\), and \(q\in(0,1]\) a binary rational. Societies have total mass \(1\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. State running times in \(m,k,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Rules are arbitrary maps \(F:\Delta(S)\to\Delta(M)\), with no continuity requirement; their domain and all suprema below include real masses. Write \(e_t\) for unit mass at \(t\).
Let \(U_t=\{u:M\to[0,1]:u(a)\ge u(b)\text{ whenever }a\succ_t b\}\), with no further utility normalization.
Define \(\epsilon_q(F)=\sup_{\nu\in\Delta(S),\,t,t'\in S,\,u\in U_t}\sum_{a\in M}u(a)[F_a((1-q)\nu+qe_{t'})-F_a((1-q)\nu+qe_t)]\); the entire designated mass \(q\) changes its report, and gain is per resident.
Set \(p_a(\mu)=\sum_{t\in S:\,a\text{ is first in }t}\mu_t\) and \(D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)=\sup_{\mu\in\Delta(S)}[\max_{a\in M}p_a(\mu)-\sum_{a\in M}F_a(\mu)p_a(\mu)]\). Lotteries may assign probability freely among tied alternatives.
For \(\epsilon\in[0,1]\), define \(\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)=\inf_{F:\,\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon}D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)\) and \(\operatorname{Opt}_q(\epsilon)=\{F:\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon,\ D_{\mathrm{Plu}}(F)=\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\}\).
What is the exact frontier \(\mathcal D_q\), and what are its attaining sets \(\operatorname{Opt}_q(\epsilon)\), including whether they are nonempty?
For binary rational \(\epsilon,d\in[0,1]\), is deciding \(\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\le d\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
For finite agent populations, the optimal deficit is piecewise linear with finitely many supporting bounds; between adjacent bounds, all optimal rules are exactly pointwise mixtures of endpoint optimal rules. [Mennle–Seuken](https://www.ifi.uzh.ch/ce/publications/EFF.pdf).

1. Incentive constraints and finite components

Index the rankings by $S=\{1,\ldots,k\}$. Write ranking $i$ as
$$ a_{i,1}\succ_i\cdots\succ_i a_{i,m}, $$
and put
$$ A_{i,h}=\{a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,h}\}. $$

If $m=1$, the unique possible rule has zero manipulability and zero deficit. Henceforth assume $m\ge2$.

1.1. Prefix inequalities

For lotteries $x,y$, set $z=y-x$. Since $\sum_a z_a=0$, summation by parts gives
$$ \sum_a u(a)z_a = \sum_{h=1}^{m-1} \bigl(u(a_{i,h})-u(a_{i,h+1})\bigr) \sum_{a\in A_{i,h}}z_a. $$
For $u\in U_i$, the coefficients on the right are nonnegative and sum to at most $1$. Conversely, the zero utility and the indicator of each prefix belong to $U_i$. Therefore
$$ \sup_{u\in U_i}\sum_a u(a)(y_a-x_a) = \max\left\{0,\max_{1\le h<m} \sum_{a\in A_{i,h}}(y_a-x_a)\right\}. $$

It follows that $\epsilon_q(F)\le\epsilon$ is equivalent to
$$ \sum_{a\in A_{i,h}} \left[ F_a(\mu-qe_i+qe_j)-F_a(\mu) \right] \le\epsilon $$
for every $\mu\in\Delta(S)$ with $\mu_i\ge q$, every $j\in S$, and every $h<m$.

For $q<1$, this follows by taking
$$ \nu=\frac{\mu-qe_i}{1-q}. $$
For $q=1$, the eligible societies are precisely $\mu=e_i$, and the same conclusion follows directly. There is no additional factor of $q$.

1.2. Component decomposition

For $\mu\in\Delta(S)$, define
$$ n_i=\left\lfloor\frac{\mu_i}{q}\right\rfloor, \qquad r_i=\mu_i-qn_i, \qquad N=\sum_i n_i. $$
Then
$$ 0\le r_i<q, \qquad \sum_i r_i=1-qN. $$

Define
$$ \mathcal I_q = \{N\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:0\le1-qN<kq\}, $$
$$ R_N = \left\{r\in[0,q)^k:\sum_i r_i=1-qN\right\}, $$
and
$$ C_N = \left\{n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^k:\sum_i n_i=N\right\}. $$

A report change preserves $r$ and changes $n$ by
$$ n\longmapsto n-e_i+e_j, \qquad n_i\ge1. $$
Conversely, every such transfer is an eligible report change.

All compositions in $C_N$ are connected by these transfers: if two compositions differ, transfer a unit from a coordinate with surplus to a coordinate with deficit, and repeat. Thus the incentive components are exactly
$$ \{r+qn:n\in C_N\}, \qquad N\in\mathcal I_q,\quad r\in R_N. $$
Their sizes are
$$ V_N=|C_N|=\binom{N+k-1}{k-1}. $$

Different components impose no constraints on one another. This independence uses the absence of continuity requirements.

2. Exact frontier and all attaining rules

For each $n\in C_N$, introduce a lottery $x_n\in\Delta(M)$. Let $P_N(\epsilon)$ be the polytope consisting of these lottery arrays subject to
$$ \sum_{a\in A_{i,h}} \bigl(x_{n-e_i+e_j,a}-x_{n,a}\bigr) \le\epsilon $$
for all eligible $n,i,j,h$.

The polytope is nonempty: a constant lottery array satisfies every incentive inequality. It is compact because it is a closed subset of a finite product of simplices.

For $r\in[0,q]^k$ satisfying $\sum_i r_i=1-qN$, define
$$ b_a(r,n) = \sum_{i:\,a_{i,1}=a}(r_i+qn_i) $$
and
$$ v_N(r,\epsilon) = \min_{x\in P_N(\epsilon)} \max_{\substack{n\in C_N\\a\in M}} \left[ b_a(r,n)-\sum_{c\in M}b_c(r,n)x_{n,c} \right]. $$
This is a finite linear program: introduce a variable $z$ and require every expression inside the maximum to be at most $z$.

Put
$$ \overline R_N = \left\{r\in[0,q]^k:\sum_i r_i=1-qN\right\}. $$

Theorem 1. Exact frontier

For every $\epsilon\in[0,1]$,
$$ \boxed{ \mathcal D_q(\epsilon) = \max_{N\in\mathcal I_q} \ \max_{r\in\overline R_N} v_N(r,\epsilon). } $$

Proof. A feasible rule restricted to the component $(N,r)$ belongs to $P_N(\epsilon)$. Its global deficit is therefore at least $v_N(r,\epsilon)$.

Conversely, choose a minimizing lottery array independently on every component. These choices define a rule on all real societies, satisfying every incentive inequality, with deficit
$$ \sup_{N\in\mathcal I_q,\ r\in R_N}v_N(r,\epsilon). $$
A definite choice is available without any regularity assumption: successively minimize the finitely many lottery coordinates lexicographically over each compact minimizer set.

It remains to replace this supremum by the displayed maximum. For fixed $x,n,a$, the coefficient of $r_i$ in the deficit expression is
$$ \mathbf1_{\{a_{i,1}=a\}}-x_{n,a_{i,1}}, $$
whose absolute value is at most $1$. Taking maxima and then minima preserves this uniform estimate, so
$$ |v_N(r,\epsilon)-v_N(r',\epsilon)| \le\|r-r'\|_1. $$

Moreover, $\overline R_N$ is the closure of $R_N$. If $1-qN>0$, mix any point of $\overline R_N$ with the vector whose coordinates all equal $(1-qN)/k<q$. If $1-qN=0$, both sets contain only the zero vector.

Finally, $\overline R_N$ is compact, and $\mathcal I_q$ is finite because $N\le\lfloor1/q\rfloor$. Continuity proves the asserted maximum formula. The componentwise construction proves attainment. $\square$

Theorem 2. Every attaining rule

Define
$$ B_{N,r}(\epsilon,d) = \left\{ x\in P_N(\epsilon): b_a(r,n)-\sum_c b_c(r,n)x_{n,c}\le d \text{ for all }n,a \right\}. $$
Under the identification of a rule with its restrictions to the incentive components,
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Opt}_q(\epsilon) = \prod_{N\in\mathcal I_q} \ \prod_{r\in R_N} B_{N,r}\bigl(\epsilon,\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\bigr). } $$
In particular,
$$ \boxed{\operatorname{Opt}_q(\epsilon)\ne\varnothing.} $$

Proof. Membership in the product is exactly the conjunction of all incentive inequalities and all pointwise deficit bounds at the frontier value.

Every factor is nonempty by Theorem 1 and existence of component minimizers. A rule assembled from the factors has deficit at most $\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)$; by the defining infimum it cannot have smaller deficit. Conversely, every attaining rule satisfies these conditions. $\square$

An attaining rule need not minimize each component separately: components whose values lie below the global maximum may have slack.

3. Computational consequences

All computational assertions in this section take $\epsilon$ to be a binary rational included in the input length.

3.1. Only first-choice totals parameterize the objectives

Let $T\subseteq M$ be the set of alternatives occurring first in $S$. For $a\in T$, put
$$ c_a=|\{i:a_{i,1}=a\}|, \qquad s_a=\sum_{i:\,a_{i,1}=a}r_i. $$
The component value depends on $r$ only through $s$, because
$$ b_a(r,n)=s_a+q\sum_{i:\,a_{i,1}=a}n_i. $$

The image of $\overline R_N$ under this aggregation is exactly
$$ \left\{ s: 0\le s_a\le c_aq,\quad \sum_{a\in T}s_a=1-qN \right\}. $$
Indeed, within each first-choice group, any total between $0$ and $c_aq$ can be distributed among its $c_a$ coordinates, each with capacity $q$.

Thus the continuous parameter dimension is at most $|T|-1$, although the component programs still use all $k$ rankings.

3.2. Component computation and evaluation

Let
$$ Q=\lfloor1/q\rfloor, \qquad V=\binom{Q+k-1}{k-1}. $$
A component program has at most $mV+1$ variables and
$$ O(mk^2V) $$
constraints. Its rational coefficients have bit lengths polynomial in the input length. Rational linear programming therefore computes a component value and an optimal lottery array in time
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,k,V,L). $$

For a supplied society of support size $\tau$, computing the remainder decomposition uses its $\tau$ positive rational masses. Evaluating a fixed component-minimizing attaining rule, with lexicographic selection if desired, takes
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,k,\tau,V,L). $$
The algorithm must allow transfers into all $k$ rankings, so its dependence is on $k$, not merely $\tau$.

These are not polynomial bounds in the binary input length in general: $Q$ can be exponentially large.

3.3. Explicit additive approximation

Given a rational accuracy $\eta>0$, set
$$ H=\max\{1,\lceil kq/\eta\rceil\}. $$
Partition $[0,q]^k$ into $H^k$ boxes of side $q/H$. For every admissible $N$, choose one rational point from each box intersecting $\overline R_N$ and solve its component program.

An intersection point can be constructed by starting at the lower corner and distributing the additional required coordinate sum within the coordinate capacities.

If $z$ is the largest sampled value, the Lipschitz estimate from Theorem 1 gives
$$ z\le\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\le z+\eta. $$
The running time is bounded by
$$ (Q+1)H^k\, \operatorname{poly}(m,k,V,L+\log H), $$
where $L$ includes the accuracy input. This global problem has no supplied society and hence no $\tau$ dependence.

3.4. Exact decidability and a restricted complexity upper bound

The exact sublevel condition is
$$ \mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\le d \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigwedge_{N\in\mathcal I_q} \ \forall r\in\overline R_N\ \exists x\in B_{N,r}(\epsilon,d). $$
This is a finite first-order formula over the reals with rational coefficients and polynomial degree at most $2$.

The effective quantifier-elimination theorem for real closed fields states that a finite first-order formula formed from polynomial comparisons over $\mathbb Q$ can be algorithmically replaced by an equivalent quantifier-free formula over $\mathbb Q$ in its free variables. Thus the displayed formula proves exact decidability. It also proves semialgebraicity of the frontier: its graph is described by the sublevel condition together with the assertion that no smaller nonnegative $d$ satisfies it. See [Basu’s survey, Section 2.1](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf).

There is a further upper bound when $Q$ is bounded by a fixed constant. Define $\exists\mathbb R$ as the class of decision problems polynomial-time reducible to feasibility of existential polynomial comparisons over the reals.

Write all constraints defining $B_{N,r}(\epsilon,d)$ as
$$ A_N(r)x\le b_N(r), $$
including nonnegativity and both directions of each simplex equality. Both matrix and right-hand side are affine in $r$.

The finite-dimensional Farkas alternative states that this system, with $x$ otherwise unrestricted, is infeasible exactly when there is $\lambda\ge0$ such that
$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}A_N(r)=0, \qquad \lambda^{\mathsf T}b_N(r)<0. $$
Its hypotheses hold here because all variables and inequalities are finite and real. Consequently,
$$ \mathcal D_q(\epsilon)>d $$
has an existential real formula: choose an admissible $N$, choose $r\in\overline R_N$, and choose such a certificate $\lambda$. Its polynomial expressions again have degree at most $2$.

If $Q$ is bounded by a constant, then $V$ is polynomial in $k$, so this formula has polynomial size. Therefore, on that restriction,
$$ \boxed{ \{\mathcal D_q(\epsilon)\le d\}\in\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R. } $$
This upper bound establishes neither membership in $\mathrm P$ nor $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

4. A rational instance with a nonlinear frontier

Consider
$$ M=\{a,b,c\}, $$
$$ S=\{a\succ b\succ c,\ b\succ c\succ a,\ c\succ a\succ b\}, \qquad q=\frac{13}{50}. $$
Index types by their first alternatives, and interpret $a,b,c$ cyclically.

Define
$$ \rho=\frac{11}{13}, \qquad K=\{(x,y):x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x+2y\le\rho\}, $$
and
$$ h(x,y) = 1+\frac{x+y}{1-x}+\frac{y}{1-y}. $$
All denominators are positive on $K$. Put
$$ \Psi(\epsilon) = \max_{(x,y)\in K} \frac{(x+y)-(x+2y)\epsilon}{h(x,y)}. $$
Write $z_+=\max\{z,0\}$.

Theorem 3. Exact frontier for this instance

For every $\epsilon\in[0,1]$,
$$ \boxed{ \mathcal D_{13/50}(\epsilon) = \frac{13}{50} \max\left\{ \frac{(1-3\epsilon)_+}{3}, \Psi(\epsilon) \right\}. } $$
Moreover,
$$ \boxed{ \mathcal D_{13/50}(\epsilon) = \frac{13}{200}(1-\epsilon) \quad\text{for }\epsilon\in[1/5,1]. } $$

The proof follows by solving the three-reporting-unit components and bounding the remaining components.

5. Proof of the exact example formula

For this instance, $Q=3$ and the admissible component indices are $N=1,2,3$.

5.1. One reporting unit

At node $e_i$, alternative $i$ has score $q+r_i$, while each other alternative has score at most $q$ on the closed parameter set. Selecting $i$ is plurality-perfect.

It is also strategyproof: the true type receives its first alternative, and reporting another type cannot improve its utility. Thus every $N=1$ component has value zero.

5.2. Two reporting units

First suppose
$$ r_a>r_b>r_c, \qquad X=r_a-r_b,\quad Y=r_b-r_c. $$
Let $L_{ab}$ and $L_{bc}$ be the lotteries at $e_a+e_b$ and $e_b+e_c$.

At $ab$, the deficit is at least
$$ X L_{ab,b}. $$
At $bc$, the deficit is at least
$$ Y(1-L_{bc,b}), $$
because the gap from winner $b$ to $c$ is $Y$, and the gap to $a$ is
$$ q-X\ge Y. $$
The latter inequality follows from $r_a-r_c\le q$.

At $bc$, true type $c$ can report $a$, reaching $ab$. Its prefix $\{c,a\}$ gives
$$ L_{bc,b}-L_{ab,b}\le\epsilon. $$
Hence a component deficit bound $d$ must satisfy
$$ d\ge(1-\epsilon)\frac{XY}{X+Y}. $$

This bound is attained. Select the represented first alternative at unanimous nodes. At the three pair nodes put
$$ u=L_{ab,a}, \qquad v=L_{bc,b}, \qquad w=L_{ca,c}, $$
with each pair lottery supported on its two represented alternatives.

For this construction, the incentive inequalities reduce exactly to
$$ u+v\le1+\epsilon,\qquad u+w\le1+\epsilon,\qquad v+w\le1+\epsilon. $$
To verify this reduction, moves from unanimous nodes start at the true first alternative, and moves to unanimous nodes select the partner’s first alternative; both are nonbeneficial. Between pair nodes, the cyclic moves from $ab$ to $bc$, from $bc$ to $ca$, and from $ca$ to $ab$ are nonbeneficial for their respective departing types. The other three moves have nonpositive singleton-prefix gain and two-alternative-prefix gains $v+w-1$, $u+w-1$, and $u+v-1$.

Choose
$$ \ell=\frac{(1-\epsilon)Y}{X+Y}, \qquad u=1-\ell,\quad v=\epsilon+\ell,\quad w=0. $$
The two nonzero deficits are
$$ X\ell = Y(1-\epsilon-\ell) = (1-\epsilon)\frac{XY}{X+Y}. $$

Cyclic rotations cover the other cyclic remainder orders. For the opposite order $r_a>r_c>r_b$, plurality winners correspond to $(u,v,w)=(1,0,0)$ and satisfy the inequalities at $\epsilon=0$. Cyclic rotations cover the remaining opposite orders. Ties follow by continuity of the component value.

For the required global bound, let
$$ R_2=1-2q=\frac{12}{25}. $$
Writing $r_c=z$, a cyclic remainder order satisfies
$$ X+2Y+3z=R_2, \qquad z+X+Y\le q. $$
Therefore
$$ 2X+Y\le3q-R_2=\frac3{10}. $$
Also,
$$ (2X+Y)(X+Y)-5XY = 2X^2-2XY+Y^2\ge0, $$
so
$$ \frac{XY}{X+Y}\le\frac{2X+Y}{5}\le\frac3{50}. $$
Thus all two-unit components have value at most
$$ \frac3{50}(1-\epsilon). $$

5.3. Three reporting units: an exact three-variable program

Here
$$ R_3=1-3q=\frac{11}{50}<q. $$
Every unanimous or majority-count node has its count-majority alternative as unique plurality winner. At the remaining node
$$ T=e_a+e_b+e_c, $$
let the lottery be $Y=(Y_a,Y_b,Y_c)$.

Define
$$ w_a=q+r_c-r_a,\qquad w_b=q+r_a-r_b,\qquad w_c=q+r_b-r_c. $$
These weights are positive and sum to $3q$.

At $T$, true type $c$ can report $a$, reaching $2e_a+e_b$. Its prefix $\{c,a\}$ implies that the lottery at this destination assigns at least $Y_b-\epsilon$ to $b$. The score gap between majority alternative $a$ and minority alternative $b$ is $w_b$; the gap to $c$ is larger. Consequently, every component rule has deficit at least
$$ w_b(Y_b-\epsilon)_+. $$
The cyclic versions give the other two bounds. The deficit at $T$ itself is
$$ r_{\max}-r\cdot Y. $$

These bounds are simultaneously attainable. Put
$$ \alpha_i=(Y_i-\epsilon)_+. $$
Select the first alternative at unanimous nodes. At $2e_a+e_b$, assign probabilities
$$ 1-\alpha_b\text{ to }a, \qquad \alpha_b\text{ to }b. $$
At $2e_a+e_c$, select $a$ with certainty. Rotate these definitions cyclically, and use $Y$ at $T$.

Here is a check of every class of report change.

  • From $T$, true type $a$ reporting $b$ reaches $2e_b+e_c$. Its only possibly positive prefix gain is

$$ Y_c-\alpha_c=\min\{Y_c,\epsilon\}\le\epsilon. $$
Reporting $c$ selects its worst alternative.

  • From $2e_a+e_b$, a majority type $a$ reporting $c$ reaches $T$. Its singleton-prefix gain is at most

$$ Y_a-1+\alpha_b\le Y_a-1+Y_b=-Y_c, $$
and its two-alternative-prefix gain is $-Y_c$. Reporting $b$ selects $b$, which is nonbeneficial. Either report by minority type $b$ selects $a$, losing its probability on its first alternative.

  • From $2e_a+e_c$, a majority type starts at its first alternative. Minority type $c$ reporting $a$ leaves the outcome unchanged; reporting $b$ replaces certainty on $a$ by a lottery over $a,b$, and type $c$ prefers $a$ to $b$.
  • Changes from unanimous nodes start at the true first alternative.

Cyclic rotation covers all types and nodes. The constructed deficits are exactly the lower bounds above. Therefore
$$ v_3(r,\epsilon) = \min_{Y\in\Delta(\{a,b,c\})} \max\left\{ r_{\max}-r\cdot Y,\ \max_i w_i(Y_i-\epsilon)_+ \right\}. $$

5.4. Solving the three-variable program

Order the residual scores as
$$ r_H\ge r_B\ge r_C, $$
and write
$$ X=r_H-r_B,\qquad Y=r_B-r_C. $$
A proposed deficit bound $d\ge0$ imposes
$$ Y_i\le c_i:=\epsilon+\frac d{w_i}. $$
These caps permit a lottery exactly when
$$ \sum_i c_i\ge1. $$

Among capped lotteries, the smallest deficit at $T$ is obtained by filling $H$ first, then $B$, then $C$. Its value is
$$ X\max\{0,1-c_H\} + Y\max\{0,1-c_H-c_B\}. $$
Indeed, the triple-node deficit equals
$$ X(1-Y_H)+YY_C, $$
and the stated allocation maximizes the probability on $H$ and then on $B$. The total-cap condition ensures that the remaining probability fits under $c_C$.

Since $1-c_H-c_B\le1-c_H$, this minimum is
$$ \max\left\{ 0,\ X(1-c_H),\ (X+Y)(1-c_H)-Yc_B \right\}. $$
Solving the resulting inequalities for $d$ gives
$$ v_3(r,\epsilon)=\max\{d_0,d_1,d_2\}, $$
where
$$ d_0 = \frac{(1-3\epsilon)_+}{\sum_i1/w_i}, $$
$$ d_1 = \frac{X(1-\epsilon)}{1+X/w_H}, $$
and
$$ d_2 = \frac{(X+Y)-(X+2Y)\epsilon} {1+(X+Y)/w_H+Y/w_B}. $$

Now normalize
$$ x=X/q,\qquad y=Y/q. $$
Every possible gap pair lies in $K$, because
$$ X+2Y\le R_3. $$
Conversely, every point of $K$ is realized by taking
$$ r_C=\frac{R_3-q(x+2y)}3. $$

There are two residual orders, up to cyclic rotation.

For $r_a\ge r_b\ge r_c$,
$$ \frac{w_H}{q}=1-x-y, \qquad \frac{w_B}{q}=1+x. $$
For $r_a\ge r_c\ge r_b$,
$$ \frac{w_H}{q}=1-x, \qquad \frac{w_B}{q}=1-y. $$

The maximum of $d_0$ is
$$ \frac q3(1-3\epsilon)_+, $$
because $\sum_iw_i=3q$ implies $\sum_i1/w_i\ge3/q$, with equality at equal remainders.

The maximum of $d_1$ is
$$ \frac q4(1-\epsilon). $$
In the opposite order it equals $q(1-\epsilon)x(1-x)$, maximized at $x=1/2,y=0$. This point belongs to $K$. In the cyclic order it is
$$ q(1-\epsilon)\frac{x(1-x-y)}{1-y} \le q(1-\epsilon)x(1-x). $$

For $d_2$, the opposite order gives exactly $q$ times the function defining $\Psi$. The cyclic denominator is
$$ h_{\mathrm{cyc}} = 1+\frac{x+y}{1-x-y}+\frac y{1+x}. $$
Its difference from $h$ is
$$ y(x+y) \left[ \frac1{(1-x-y)(1-x)} - \frac1{(1+x)(1-y)} \right]\ge0. $$
The sign follows from
$$ (1+x)(1-y)-(1-x-y)(1-x) = x(3-x-2y)\ge0. $$
Thus the cyclic order cannot give a larger positive $d_2$. Negative values are irrelevant to the maximum.

Finally,
$$ \Psi(\epsilon)\ge\frac{1-\epsilon}{4} $$

$$ \max_r v_3(r,\epsilon) = q\max\left\{ \frac{(1-3\epsilon)_+}{3}, \Psi(\epsilon) \right\}. $$

6. Proof that the frontier is not finitely piecewise linear

The following argument supplies an interval of nonlinearity without assuming an unproved formula for the maximizing point.

6.1. The unique maximizer at $\epsilon=1/5$

Set
$$ \phi_\epsilon(x,y) = \frac{(1-\epsilon)x+(1-2\epsilon)y}{h(x,y)}. $$
Then $\Psi(\epsilon)=\max_K\phi_\epsilon$.

At $\epsilon=1/5$, the maximum is
$$ \Psi(1/5)=\frac15, $$
attained uniquely at
$$ p_0=(1/2,0). $$

To prove this, first consider $x\ge3/4$. Since $1-2\epsilon\le1-\epsilon$ and
$$ h(x,y)>\frac{x+y}{1-x}, $$
we have, at $\epsilon=1/5$,
$$ \phi_{1/5}(x,y) < \frac45(1-x)\le\frac15. $$

For $x<3/4$, use
$$ h(x,y)=\frac{1+y}{1-x}+\frac y{1-y}. $$
Its Hessian has
$$ h_{xx}=\frac{2(1+y)}{(1-x)^3}, \qquad h_{xy}=\frac1{(1-x)^2}, \qquad h_{yy}=\frac2{(1-y)^3}, $$
and determinant
$$ \det\nabla^2h = \frac{4(1+y)(1-x)-(1-y)^3} {(1-x)^4(1-y)^3}>0. $$
The numerator is positive because $x<3/4$ and $0\le y<1$ give
$$ 4(1+y)(1-x)>1+y\ge(1-y)^3. $$
Thus $h$ is strictly convex on the convex region under consideration.

Consequently,
$$ G(x,y)=x+\frac34y-\frac14h(x,y) $$
is strictly concave. Direct calculation gives
$$ G(p_0)=0,\qquad \nabla G(p_0)=0. $$
The supporting-tangent inequality for a differentiable strictly concave function implies $G(x,y)<0$ at every other point in this region. This is exactly $\phi_{1/5}(x,y)<1/5$.

The same argument proves
$$ \Psi(\epsilon)=\frac{1-\epsilon}{4} \quad\text{for }\epsilon\in[1/5,1]. $$
For $\epsilon<1$, divide the comparison by $1-\epsilon$ and use
$$ \frac{1-2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\le\frac34. $$
At $\epsilon=1$, the numerator is $-y$, so the maximum is zero.

For $\epsilon\ge1/5$,
$$ \frac{(1-3\epsilon)_+}{3}\le\frac{1-\epsilon}{4}. $$
This completes Theorem 3.

6.2. Interior maximizers immediately below $1/5$

For $\epsilon<1/5$, let
$$ A=1-\epsilon,\qquad B=1-2\epsilon. $$
Consider
$$ f(x,y)=Ax+By-\frac A4h(x,y). $$
At $p_0$,
$$ f(p_0)=0, \qquad f_y(p_0)=B-\frac34A=\frac{1-5\epsilon}{4}>0. $$
A sufficiently small increase in $y$ remains in $K$, since $\rho>1/2$. It gives $f>0$, and therefore
$$ \Psi(\epsilon)>\frac{1-\epsilon}{4}. $$
Every point with $y=0$ has value
$$ (1-\epsilon)x(1-x)\le\frac{1-\epsilon}{4}. $$
Thus every maximizer for $\epsilon<1/5$ has $y>0$.

The function $\phi_\epsilon$ is jointly continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times K$. Since its maximizer at $1/5$ is unique, all maximizing points converge to $p_0$ as $\epsilon$ increases to $1/5$. Explicitly, any contrary sequence has a convergent subsequence whose limit, by continuity, would be a different maximizer at $1/5$.

It follows that there exists $\delta>0$ such that, for
$$ I=(1/5-\delta,1/5), $$
all maximizing points satisfy
$$ x>0,\qquad y>0,\qquad x<3/4,\qquad x+2y<\rho. $$
They are therefore interior points of $K$. Shrink $\delta$ so that $I\subseteq(1/9,1/5)$. On this interval,
$$ \frac{1-3\epsilon}{3}<\frac{1-\epsilon}{4}<\Psi(\epsilon), $$
and hence
$$ \mathcal D_{13/50}(\epsilon)=q\Psi(\epsilon). $$

6.3. No affine subinterval

Suppose $\Psi$ were affine on a nonempty open interval $J\subseteq I$. Choose an interior $\epsilon_0\in J$ and a maximizing point $p=(x,y)$ there.

For fixed $p$, the function $\phi_\epsilon(p)$ is affine in $\epsilon$, lies below $\Psi(\epsilon)$, and equals it at $\epsilon_0$. Two affine functions with this property on an open interval must coincide throughout $J$. Thus $p$ would maximize at two distinct values $\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2\in J$.

Since $p$ is interior, the first-order conditions apply. Writing $D=\Psi(\epsilon)$, they give
$$ A=D\,h_x(p), \qquad B=D\,h_y(p). $$
Therefore
$$ \frac{1-2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon} = \frac{h_y(p)}{h_x(p)}. $$
The right side is fixed, whereas the left side has derivative
$$ -\frac1{(1-\epsilon)^2}<0. $$
It cannot take the same value at $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$. This contradiction proves that $\Psi$, and hence $\mathcal D_{13/50}$, has no affine subinterval of $I$.

A function with finitely many affine pieces must have an affine subinterval of every nonempty open interval after deleting finitely many breakpoints. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ \mathcal D_{13/50} \text{ is not piecewise linear with finitely many pieces.} } $$

Thus the finite supporting-bound structure proved for finite populations in [Mennle–Seuken](https://www.ifi.uzh.ch/ce/publications/EFF.pdf) does not extend to the present continuous domain.
