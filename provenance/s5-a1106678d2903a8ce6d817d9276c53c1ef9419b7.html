The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of schools, rational per-capita capacities, and a finite menu of types specifying complete school rankings and priority tiers, does there exist a rational finite-support society distribution \(\mu\) over those types and a pure or mixed acceptance equilibrium in the truthful-report, two-stage fluid deferred-acceptance system such that almost every student weakly prefers their final assignment to their one-stage fluid deferred-acceptance assignment, with a positive mass strictly preferring it? Keep the two stages finite; in each stage, priority tiers fill capacity in order, splitting a boundary tier proportionally, while first-stage acceptances retain their seats and remaining mass enters the second stage under residual capacities.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input consists of \(m\geq1\) schools with positive binary-rational per-capita capacities and a nonempty menu of \(k\) student types. Each type has a strict complete ranking of the schools and an outside option \(\varnothing\), and a priority tier at every school; each school's ordered tiers include an empty-seat threshold. Only mutually acceptable assignments are permitted: the student ranks the school above \(\varnothing\), and the school ranks the student's tier above its threshold.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The society is atomless, its masses sum to \(1\), and its support is contained in the input menu; the distribution \(\mu\) is existentially chosen, rather than supplied as fixed input. State running times in \(m,k,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.
Fluid DA uses synchronous student-proposing rounds: rejected mass applies to its next acceptable school, and each school chooses from its tentative holdings and new applicants. It fills acceptable priority tiers in order; within a boundary tier it retains the same proportion of every applicant cohort. Exhausted mass receives \(\varnothing\). A stage's assignment is the limit of these rounds if they do not terminate finitely; there are exactly two enrollment stages.
Interpret fractional assignments as school lotteries for infinitesimal students. After observing their realized first-stage offer, students simultaneously accept or reject it. Acceptance permanently retains that seat; rejected and unmatched mass enters truthful fluid DA with residual capacities, and second-stage offers are final. The one-stage benchmark is the student's own first-stage offer.
An acceptance strategy specifies a probability in \([0,1]\) for each type and realized school offer; pure strategies use only \(0\) and \(1\). Unmatched students must continue. Use an ordinal equilibrium: every action used with positive probability must weakly first-order stochastically dominate the alternative under the student's ranking. Evaluate a unilateral deviation by the limit of switching a marked mass \(\varepsilon\) of that type and offer to the alternative action as \(\varepsilon\to0^+\), releasing or retaining its first-stage seats accordingly.
Welfare improvement means that almost every student's final lottery assigns probability \(1\) to outcomes weakly preferred to their own benchmark offer, and a positive total mass receives strictly preferred outcomes with positive probability.

Is deciding whether some rational society distribution admits a pure or mixed acceptance equilibrium with this welfare improvement solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finite students with strict priorities and truthful reports, acyclic capacity-priority profiles make every acceptance profile an equilibrium with the one-stage outcome; a cyclic profile admits some preference profile with a Pareto-improving equilibrium. Here a cycle consists of distinct schools \(a,b\), distinct students \(i,j,h\) with \(i\succ_a j\succ_a h\succ_b i\), and disjoint filler sets outside these students containing respectively \(q_a-1\) students above \(j\) at \(a\) and \(q_b-1\) students above \(i\) at \(b\). [Verified discrete characterization](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00199-023-01488-y.pdf).
No result settling the precise continuous decision problem above was located in the literature search.

1. Notation and basic properties of fluid DA

Fix a society with supported types $T$ and masses $\mu_t>0$. Let $\mathcal A(t)$ be type $t$’s mutually acceptable schools, listed in preference order. Write $t\succ_s u$ when school $s$ ranks type $t$’s priority tier strictly above type $u$’s tier.

Let $x_{t,s}$ denote the first-stage mass of type $t$ offered outcome $s$, including $s=\varnothing$, and let

$$ X_s=\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,s}. $$

The following properties apply to either enrollment stage, including a stage with zero residual capacity at some schools.

Lemma 1: convergence, conservation, and fullness

Fluid DA has a limiting assignment. That assignment is feasible and mutually acceptable. If positive mass finishes below a mutually acceptable school $s$, then $s$ is full at the limit.

Proof. Let $P$ be the participating mass. Every student applies to each school at most once, so total application mass over all rounds and schools is at most $mP$.

For a fixed cohort and school, cumulative arrivals and cumulative rejections are bounded, nondecreasing sequences. Their difference is tentative holding mass and therefore converges. Exhausted mass also converges.

The mass applying in each round tends to zero because the sum of application masses is finite. Pending rejected mass applies in the next round or exhausts its list. Thus pending mass tends to zero, and the limiting holdings and exhausted mass account for all $P$.

Feasibility and mutual acceptability pass to the limit.

Finally, positive mass finishing below $s$ entails positive rejection at $s$ in some finite round. An acceptable rejection occurs only when $s$ fills its capacity. Once full, $s$ stays full: its previous holdings alone supply its capacity, and subsequent choices consider those holdings together with new applicants. Hence $s$ is full at the limit. $\square$

Lemma 2: identical types have identical continuation lotteries

All positive-mass cohorts of a given type entering the second stage have the same conditional second-stage lottery, regardless of their first-stage offers.

Proof. These cohorts restart DA with identical preferences and priorities. Initially their application masses are proportional to their participating masses. At each school, the selection rule applies the same retention proportion to every cohort in their common tier. Thus their holdings, rejections, subsequent applications, and exhausted masses remain proportional by induction over rounds. Lemma 1 permits passage to the limit. $\square$

Denote this continuation lottery by $Q_t$ whenever type $t$ participates with positive mass.

Proposition 3: equilibrium implies the conditional no-harm requirement

Every ordinal acceptance equilibrium satisfies the weak welfare requirement in the question.

Proof. Consider a positive-mass cohort offered school $s$ that rejects with positive probability. Switching sufficiently small marked mass from rejection to acceptance guarantees that marked mass $s$, independently of the second-stage DA outcome. Its alternative-action lottery is therefore $\delta_s$.

Equilibrium requires $Q_t$ to weakly first-order stochastically dominate $\delta_s$. Under a strict ranking, this is equivalent to

$$ Q_t\bigl(\{r:r\succeq_t s\}\bigr)=1. $$

Indeed, dominance at the cutoff $s$ requires this equality. Conversely, that equality implies dominance at every cutoff: above $s$, the benchmark cumulative probability is zero; at or below $s$, both cumulative probabilities equal one.

Accepting students retain their benchmark school. Originally unmatched students receive only mutually acceptable schools or $\varnothing$, all weakly preferred to their benchmark. $\square$

This argument does not require continuity of DA or existence of acceptance-to-rejection deviation limits.

2. A conservation theorem for every harmless improvement

The next theorem applies to any feasible final assignment satisfying conditional no harm, whether or not it arises from an equilibrium.

Theorem 4: enrollment and unmatched mass are preserved

Suppose almost every student’s final lottery is supported on outcomes weakly preferred to their realized first-stage offer. Then:

  • Every school has exactly its first-stage enrollment.
  • Almost every originally unmatched student remains unmatched.
  • Every school from which positive mass strictly improves was full in the first stage.
  • The positive strict-improvement flows form a circulation among initially full schools.

Proof. For distinct schools $s,r$, let $f_{s,r}$ be the mass whose benchmark is $s$ and whose final outcome is $r$. Let $f_{\varnothing,r}$ be the originally unmatched mass finally assigned to $r$.

Conditional no harm implies that every positive $f_{s,r}$ represents a strict improvement. It also rules out movement from a school to $\varnothing$.

Define incoming and outgoing movement at school $s$ by

$$ I_s=\sum_{r\ne s}f_{r,s}+f_{\varnothing,s}, \qquad O_s=\sum_{r\ne s}f_{s,r}. $$

Its final enrollment $Y_s$ satisfies

$$ Y_s=X_s-O_s+I_s. $$

If $I_s>0$, some positive type-and-benchmark cohort moves to the mutually acceptable school $s$ from a worse outcome. Lemma 1 applied to the first stage gives $X_s=q_s$. Final capacity feasibility therefore gives

$$ I_s\le O_s. $$

If $I_s=0$, the same inequality holds trivially. Summing over schools yields

$$ \sum_s I_s = \sum_s O_s+\sum_s f_{\varnothing,s} \le \sum_s O_s. $$

Every $f_{\varnothing,s}$ is nonnegative, so

$$ f_{\varnothing,s}=0 \quad\text{for every }s. $$

Consequently, $\sum_s I_s=\sum_s O_s$. Since each $O_s-I_s$ is nonnegative, each must be zero:

$$ I_s=O_s \quad\text{for every }s. $$

Thus $Y_s=X_s$ for every school.

If $O_s>0$, then $I_s=O_s>0$, so the earlier fullness argument gives $X_s=q_s$. The school-to-school flows are nonnegative and balanced at every vertex, which is precisely a circulation. $\square$

A nonzero finite circulation contains a directed cycle: starting with a positive edge, balance supplies a positive outgoing edge at each subsequent vertex, and some vertex must repeat.

Corollary 5: types with unmatched mass cannot reject school offers

In any acceptance profile satisfying conditional no harm, if

$$ x_{t,\varnothing}>0, $$

then type $t$ accepts every positive-mass school offer with probability one. Its second-stage participants receive $\varnothing$ with probability one.

Proof. Its originally unmatched cohort must participate. Theorem 4 says this cohort remains unmatched. By Lemma 2, its continuation lottery is therefore $Q_t=\delta_{\varnothing}$.

If another positive-mass cohort of that type rejected a school offer, it would have the same continuation lottery and would be harmed. $\square$

Thus this conclusion holds for every ordinal equilibrium. In particular, if every supported type has positive first-stage unmatched mass, improvement is impossible, without any assumption that all schools were initially full.

Corollary 6: only fully matched types can label improvement cycles

Call type $t$ fully matched in the first stage when $x_{t,\varnothing}=0$.

Every positive strict-improvement flow in an acceptance equilibrium comes from a fully matched type. A strictly improving equilibrium therefore requires a directed cycle among initially full schools, with each edge labeled by a fully matched type that prefers its destination to its origin.

If all fully matched types share a common relative ordering of schools, no such cycle exists, and improvement is impossible.

Proof. Corollary 5 excludes movement by types with unmatched mass. Theorem 4 supplies a nonzero circulation if strict improvement occurs. Under a common school ordering, every positive improvement edge points upward in that ordering, so no directed cycle is possible. $\square$

This strengthens the common-order obstruction: the preferences of types with unmatched mass need not agree with that common ordering.

3. Unmatched types impose strict priority restrictions

The proportional boundary-tier rule supplies a further restriction, including when distinct types share a tier.

Lemma 7: zero holdings exclude the same and lower tiers

Consider one DA stage. Suppose an acceptable type $u$ sends positive mass to school $s$ but has zero limiting holdings there. Then every type with positive limiting holdings at $s$ has priority strictly above $u$.

Proof. If $s$ has zero capacity, there are no positive holdings, so assume its capacity is positive.

Because $u$ sends positive mass but retains none at the limit, some finite round rejects positive mass of $u$. At that round, holdings from tiers at least as high as $u$ fill the school. They continue to fill it thereafter. Hence no strictly lower tier can have positive limiting holdings.

It remains to exclude $u$’s own tier.

Choose a finite round $n$ in which a positive mass $\delta$ of $u$ arrives. Let $a_j\in[0,1]$ be the retention proportion for its tier in round $j$.

If any $a_j=0$ at or after that arrival while this mass is still held, strictly higher tiers fill the school. They remain sufficient to fill it, so the entire tier has zero holdings thereafter.

Otherwise, all these retention proportions are positive. The chosen arriving mass has holdings

$$ \delta\prod_{j=n}^{N}a_j $$

after round $N$. Its limiting holdings are zero, so

$$ \prod_{j=n}^{N}a_j\longrightarrow 0. $$

Every mass arriving in the same tier in any fixed finite round also has limiting holdings zero: its retention product differs from this product by only finitely many positive factors.

For an arbitrary type in that tier, total arrivals at $s$ are finite. Given $\eta>0$, choose a round after which its total arriving mass is less than $\eta$. Its finitely many earlier arrivals have holdings tending to zero, while its later arrivals can contribute at most $\eta$. Its limiting holdings are therefore zero. $\square$

Proposition 8: new recipients must outrank originally unmatched types

In an acceptance profile satisfying conditional no harm, suppose type $t$ receives positive second-stage mass at school $r$. Then

$$ t\succ_r u $$

for every type $u$ such that $x_{u,\varnothing}>0$ and $r\in\mathcal A(u)$.

Proof. By Corollary 5, such a type $u$ participates through its originally unmatched mass and finishes entirely unmatched. Positive mass of $u$ therefore applies to every mutually acceptable school, including $r$, but its limiting holdings there are zero. Lemma 7 gives the conclusion. $\square$

A necessary directed graph test

For a fixed society and its first-stage assignment, construct a graph with the schools as vertices. Include an edge $s\to r$ only if:

  • Both schools were full in the first stage.
  • Some fully matched type $t$ has $x_{t,s}>0$ and prefers $r$ to $s$.
  • School $r$ is mutually acceptable to $t$.
  • Type $t$ strictly outranks at $r$ every originally unmatched type that finds $r$ mutually acceptable.

Proven necessary condition. If this graph is acyclic, the society admits no improving pure or mixed acceptance equilibrium.

Every positive improvement flow meets these conditions by Theorem 4, Corollary 5, and Proposition 8. A positive circulation would yield a directed cycle in the graph.

Once the exact first-stage positivity and fullness predicates are supplied, the graph can be constructed using $O(L+\tau m^2)$ operations. For each school, first compute the highest priority among relevant originally unmatched types; this makes each subsequent priority test a single comparison. Cycle detection takes $O(m^2)$ operations.

This is a necessary test, not a general decision algorithm: obtaining those exact predicates and existentially choosing the society remain unresolved.

4. Distinct priority tiers give finite rational DA

The irrational-limit phenomenon from the previous work requires a feature absent from the following class.

Theorem 9: finite termination on a rational grid

Suppose, at every school, no two distinct participating types share an acceptable priority tier. Suppose all participating masses and stage capacities are rational.

Let $D$ be a positive integer such that every participating mass and capacity is an integer multiple of $1/D$. If participating mass is $P$, DA has at most $mPD$ rounds containing positive applications. Its aggregate type-by-school assignment is rational, with entries in $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

Proof. Initially, aggregate type application masses belong to the grid $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

At a school, acceptable types occupy distinct tiers. The school retains whole aggregate type masses until reaching a boundary type. The aggregate retained mass of that type is the minimum of its candidate mass and the remaining capacity. All these quantities are grid multiples. Aggregate rejections and next-round applications consequently remain grid multiples by induction.

Every round containing positive applications has application mass at least $1/D$. Lemma 1 bounds total application mass by $mP$. Hence there are at most $mPD$ such rounds. Once applications cease, all mass is held or exhausted. $\square$

For a rational society and a pure acceptance profile, this theorem applies to both stages under the stated priority hypothesis: first-stage offer masses, accepted masses, residual capacities, and second-stage participating masses all remain rational grid multiples.

For clarity about encoding, let $B$ be the bit length of an explicitly supplied society’s masses, while $L$ denotes the menu and capacity input length. Taking the product of the encoded denominators gives

$$ D\le 2^{L+B}. $$

Direct simulation uses $O(\tau m)$ rational operations per round after preprocessing, so the theorem gives an evaluation bound of

$$ O\!\left( \bigl(L+\tau m^2D\bigr)\operatorname{poly}(L+B) \right). $$

This bound is exponential in general. No bound on $B$ as a function of the original existential input length is established here.

5. A fixed menu with improving equilibria, discontinuity, and exponentially many rounds

There are three schools with capacities

$$ q_a=q_b=q=\frac14, \qquad q_c=\frac12. $$

There are four menu types:

| Type | Strict ranking |
|---|---|
| $h$ | $a\succ c\succ b\succ\varnothing$ |
| $j$ | $a\succ b\succ c\succ\varnothing$ |
| $i$ | $b\succ a\succ c\succ\varnothing$ |
| $d$ | $\varnothing\succ a\succ b\succ c$ |

All three schools accept $h,j,i$, in distinct tiers ordered as follows:

$$ a:\ i\succ_a h\succ_a j, \qquad b:\ j\succ_b i\succ_b h, \qquad c:\ h\succ_c j\succ_c i. $$

Type $d$ is below every school’s threshold and has no mutually acceptable school.

For any rational $0<\varepsilon\le q$, choose the society

$$ \mu_h=\varepsilon, \qquad \mu_j=\mu_i=q, \qquad \mu_d=\frac12-\varepsilon. $$

These masses are positive and sum to one.

5.1 First-stage assignment

The limiting offers are

$$ h\mapsto c, \qquad j\mapsto b, \qquad i\mapsto a, \qquad d\mapsto\varnothing. $$

Proof. Type $j$ can never be rejected by $b$: it has highest priority there and total mass exactly $q$. Likewise, type $i$ can never be rejected by $a$. Their preference lists therefore imply that both types finish entirely within $\{a,b\}$.

Their combined mass is $2q$, exactly the combined capacity of those schools. By conservation in Lemma 1, they exhaust that capacity. Thus $h$ has no limiting holdings at $a$ or $b$.

All $h$ mass rejected from $a$ next applies to $c$. School $c$ can retain all of it because $\varepsilon\le q<q_c$. Hence all $h$ finishes at $c$.

Type $h$ applied positive mass to $a$ but has zero limiting holdings there. Lemma 7 excludes the lower-priority type $j$ from $a$. Consequently all $i$ occupies $a$, and all $j$ occupies $b$. Type $d$ is unmatched throughout. $\square$

5.2 A strictly improving pure equilibrium

Let $h$ accept its offer at $c$, and let $j$ and $i$ reject their offers. Type $d$ must continue.

Residual capacities are

$$ q,\quad q,\quad \frac12-\varepsilon. $$

In the second stage, $j$ obtains $a$ and $i$ obtains $b$, their first choices. Type $h$ retains $c$, and $d$ remains unmatched. Thus precisely mass $1/2$ strictly improves.

Types $j$ and $i$ strictly prefer rejection to accepting their respective benchmarks. It remains to verify $h$’s acceptance.

Switch marked mass $\zeta$ of type $h$, with $0<\zeta<\varepsilon$, from acceptance to rejection. Its released seats give residual capacity

$$ \frac12-\varepsilon+\zeta $$

at $c$. This is at least $\zeta$.

As before, $j$ and $i$ finish entirely within $\{a,b\}$ and exhaust their combined capacity. Marked $h$ cannot proceed beyond $c$, which can retain all marked mass reaching it. Consequently all marked mass finishes at $c$.

Thus the rejection-deviation lottery is exactly

$$ \delta_c $$

for every sufficiently small $\zeta$, and its prescribed limit is also $\delta_c$. Acceptance is weakly optimal. The profile is a pure ordinal equilibrium satisfying the required welfare improvement.

5.3 Discontinuity under a marked deviation

Without the marked deviation, second-stage assignments are

$$ j\mapsto a, \qquad i\mapsto b. $$

For every $\zeta>0$ as above, marked $h$ applies to $a$ and ultimately has zero holdings there. Lemma 7 then excludes $j$ from $a$. Hence the perturbed second-stage assignments are

$$ j\mapsto b, \qquad i\mapsto a, \qquad \text{marked }h\mapsto c. $$

Thus an arbitrarily small deviation changes the school of all mass $q$ of each of two other types. The deviator’s own limit exists and equals its benchmark, despite this discontinuity.

Accordingly, evaluating an acceptance-to-rejection deviation by keeping the other types’ baseline DA assignments fixed would be unjustified.

5.4 Exponentially many prescribed synchronous rounds

Set

$$ \varepsilon=\frac qN $$

for a positive integer $N$.

In the first round, $a$ retains $\varepsilon$ of $h$ and $q-\varepsilon$ of $j$, rejecting $\varepsilon$ of $j$. School $b$ holds all $i$.

For each $r=1,\ldots,N-1$:

  • Round $2r$ transfers another $\varepsilon$ of $j$ to $b$, displacing $\varepsilon$ of $i$.
  • Round $2r+1$ transfers that $\varepsilon$ of $i$ to $a$, displacing another $\varepsilon$ of $j$.

After round $2N-1$, school $a$ holds $q-\varepsilon$ of $i$ and $\varepsilon$ of $h$, while the last $\varepsilon$ of $j$ is pending.

Round $2N$ sends that last $j$ mass to $b$, displacing the last $\varepsilon$ of $i$. Round $2N+1$ fills $a$ with $i$ and rejects all $h$. Round $2N+2$ assigns $h$ to $c$ and terminates.

The exact number of rounds is therefore

$$ 2N+2. $$

Taking $N=2^n$ gives

$$ \varepsilon=2^{-(n+2)}. $$

The menu and capacities are fixed, and the society description has bit length $B=\Theta(n)$. Nevertheless, the prescribed synchronous process requires

$$ 2^{n+1}+2 $$

rounds, with fixed $m=3$, $k=4$, and $\tau=4$.

This establishes an exponential lower bound on direct synchronous simulation. It does not establish a lower bound for every algorithm computing the outcome.

5.5 Improving-equilibrium existence is not closed in the society distribution

The preceding societies admit improving pure equilibria for every positive rational $\varepsilon\le q$. As $\varepsilon$ tends to zero, they converge to the rational society

$$ \mu_h=0, \qquad \mu_j=\mu_i=\frac14, \qquad \mu_d=\frac12. $$

In that limiting society, the first stage gives every supported type its first choice: $j$ receives $a$, $i$ receives $b$, and $d$ receives $\varnothing$. No feasible final assignment can strictly improve anyone.

Thus, for this fixed menu and these fixed capacities, the set of society distributions admitting an improving equilibrium is not closed.

6. What remains unresolved

The circulation theorem, unmatched-type restrictions, and graph test constrain possible witnesses substantially. They do not decide whether a suitable rational society exists.

The distinct-tier termination theorem removes infinite-round and irrational-assignment issues for rational baseline pure profiles in that restricted class. The explicit family shows that finite termination does not give a polynomial simulation bound, and that marked deviations require their specified limiting treatment.

A complete classification still requires either a general polynomial-time algorithm with bit-complexity bounds or a valid hardness reduction respecting the existential masses, proportional selection, conditional benchmarks, and deviation limits.
