The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many applicant types \(T\), post types \(P\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational capacities \(\kappa_p\), strict type-specific rankings, and a last-resort option for each type, assign each type’s mass to acceptable posts or its last resort. For a feasible assignment \(x\), call it popular if every feasible challenger \(y\) and every type-preserving coupling \(z\) between their assignments has nonpositive majority margin, \(\sum_{t,p,q} z^t_{p,q}(\mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q])\le 0\). Find a popular assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,p\in P}x_{t,p}\), or report that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P\) be the real posts, with \(m=|P|\geq1\) and positive binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_p\).
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|T|\geq1\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The parameter \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\) strictly ranks a nonempty acceptable set \(A_t\subseteq P\), followed by its private last resort \(\ell_t\notin P\). Write \(S_t=A_t\cup\{\ell_t\}\); the last resort can accommodate all of \(\mu_t\). Only applicants vote.
A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,p}\geq0\) on \(p\in S_t\), with \(\sum_{p\in S_t}x_{t,p}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:p\in A_t}x_{t,p}\leq\kappa_p\) for every real post \(p\).
For feasible assignments \(x,y\), a type-preserving coupling is any collection \(z^t_{p,q}\geq0\), indexed by \(p,q\in S_t\), satisfying \(\sum_qz^t_{p,q}=x_{t,p}\) and \(\sum_pz^t_{p,q}=y_{t,q}\).
Call \(x\) popular if, for every feasible \(y\), including \(y=x\), and every such coupling, \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{p,q\in S_t}z^t_{p,q}(\mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q])\leq0\).
The computational problem is to find a popular assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{p\in A_t}x_{t,p}\), or report that none exists.

Is this problem solvable in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite applicants and unit-capacity posts with strict one-sided preferences, maximum-cardinality popular matching, including detecting nonexistence, is in \(\mathrm{NC}\) ([Hu–Garg](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13386v2)).
Popular mixed matchings always exist and are polynomial-time computable under independent expected comparisons; that comparison does not quantify over all couplings ([Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://cse.iitm.ac.in/~meghana/papers/TCS11-2.pdf)). No result settling the universal-coupling mass problem was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive applicant masses to sum to one, with capacities measured in the same mass units.
  • Required positive real-post capacities and nonempty acceptable real-post sets, carrying over the discrete domain.
  • Made each last resort private to its type, strictly worst, and able to accommodate the type's entire mass, reflecting the private last resorts of individual applicants.
  • Specified divisible real assignment masses, upper capacity bounds, complete allocation of each type, and both coupling marginals.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is solvable in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

I prove an exact characterization of popular assignments and then optimize over the resulting linear program. The proof uses a known characterization for finite capacitated house allocation; the passage from finite applicants to arbitrary real masses is established below.

1. Definitions and characterization

Let \(f_t\) be type \(t\)'s first choice, and define the first-choice demand at each real post by
\[ d_p=\sum_{t:f_t=p}\mu_t. \]

Define \(s_t\) to be the highest-ranked destination in
\[ \{\ell_t\}\ \cup\ \{p\in A_t\setminus\{f_t\}:d_p<\kappa_p\}. \]
This destination exists because the set contains \(\ell_t\). In particular, \(s_t\ne f_t\).

Characterization. A feasible assignment \(x\) is popular if and only if both conditions below hold:

  • For every real post \(p\),

\[ \sum_{t:f_t=p}x_{t,p}=\min\{d_p,\kappa_p\}. \]

  • For every type \(t\), positive assignment mass is supported on \(\{f_t,s_t\}\):

\[ x_{t,p}=0\qquad\text{whenever }p\notin\{f_t,s_t\}. \]

The capacity-equality case belongs to the first condition: if \(d_p=\kappa_p\), every type with first choice \(p\) receives all its mass at \(p\).

2. Popularity passes to components of a convex decomposition

Let \(F\) denote the polytope of feasible assignments.

Lemma. Suppose
\[ x=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i v_i, \qquad v_i\in F,\quad \lambda_i>0,\quad \sum_i\lambda_i=1. \]
If \(x\) is popular, then every \(v_i\) is popular.

Proof. Suppose \(v_i\) is not popular. There are a feasible challenger \(y_i\) and a coupling \(z_i\) from \(v_i\) to \(y_i\) whose vote score is strictly positive.

Set
\[ y=\lambda_i y_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\lambda_jv_j. \]
This assignment is feasible because \(F\) is convex. For each type, form a coupling from \(x\) to \(y\) by combining \(\lambda_i z_i\) with the diagonal couplings of the other components:
\[ z^t_{p,q} = \lambda_i(z_i)^t_{p,q} + \sum_{j\ne i}\lambda_j(v_j)_{t,p}\mathbf{1}[p=q]. \]
Its source and destination marginals are respectively \(x\) and \(y\). Diagonal entries contribute zero votes, so its score is \(\lambda_i\) times the strictly positive score of \(z_i\). This contradicts popularity of \(x\). \(\square\)

This lemma uses the quantification over all couplings.

3. Necessity of the characterization

Integral vertices after scaling

Choose a positive integer \(D\) clearing the denominators of all input masses and capacities. Write
\[ n_t=D\mu_t,\qquad c_p=D\kappa_p. \]
These are positive integers, and \(\sum_t n_t=D\).

Every vertex \(v\) of \(F\) satisfies
\[ Dv_{t,p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}. \]

To justify this, scale all assignment variables by \(D\) and introduce a nonnegative slack variable for each real-post capacity. Multiply every real-post equality by \(-1\). Each assignment-variable column then has:

  • one \(+1\) in its type row;
  • one \(-1\) in its real-post row, if its destination is real.

A last-resort column has only its type-row entry, and a slack column has only a \(-1\) in its post row. This matrix is totally unimodular. Indeed, in any square submatrix, a column with at most one nonzero entry permits determinant expansion; if every column has two entries, they have opposite signs, and the rows sum to zero. Induction therefore shows that every square minor is \(0\), \(1\), or \(-1\).

The right-hand side is integral. Consequently every basic feasible solution is integral: its nonsingular basis matrix has determinant \(1\) or \(-1\), so its inverse is integral. Adding the uniquely determined slacks preserves vertices. This proves the assertion.

The finite characterization used

For finite applicants with strict preferences, positive integer house capacities, and private last resorts, the capacitated house-allocation characterization says:

  • every house receives exactly the minimum of its capacity and its first-choice demand from applicants ranking it first;
  • every applicant receives either its first choice or its highest-ranked different house whose first-choice demand is strictly below capacity.

A private last resort is eligible as the latter destination. These conditions are necessary and sufficient for popularity. This is [Manlove–Sng, Theorem 4](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~davidm/pubs/8240.pdf).

Applying the finite theorem to vertices

Let \(x\) be popular. Since \(F\) is a bounded polytope, write \(x\) as a convex combination of vertices with positive coefficients. By the preceding lemma, every vertex in this decomposition is popular.

Fix such a vertex \(v\). Construct a finite instance with \(n_t\) labelled applicants of type \(t\), each having the type's real-post preferences and its own private last resort. Give real post \(p\) capacity \(c_p\). Because \(Dv\) is integral, these applicants can be assigned so that exactly \(Dv_{t,p}\) type-\(t\) applicants receive destination \(p\), with the type-level last resort replaced by individual private last resorts. Call this matching \(M\).

The matching \(M\) is popular. Otherwise, a finite challenger would give a strictly positive vote count. Dividing its applicant counts by \(D\), and recording each labelled applicant's old and new destinations, would produce a feasible mass assignment and a type-preserving coupling defeating \(v\).

It suffices to consider finite challengers completed using private last resorts. Completing an unmatched applicant cannot decrease the challenger's vote score against the complete matching \(M\).

All relevant hypotheses of the finite theorem hold: preferences are strict, applicant lists are nonempty, capacities are positive integers, and only applicants vote. Houses acceptable to nobody can be deleted. If the theorem's formulation restricts capacities to the number \(D\) of applicants, capacities can be capped at \(D\). This changes neither feasible matchings nor the relevant fallback choices: the only possible change in a comparison between first-choice demand and capacity occurs when a house is first choice for all \(D\) applicants, and that house is excluded from every applicant's fallback candidates.

The finite first-choice demand at post \(p\) is \(Dd_p\). Thus the finite theorem gives
\[ \sum_{t:f_t=p}Dv_{t,p} = \min\{Dd_p,D\kappa_p\}, \]
and every type-\(t\) applicant receives \(f_t\) or \(s_t\). Dividing by \(D\) establishes both characterization conditions for \(v\).

Those conditions are linear equalities. Since every vertex in the decomposition satisfies them, their convex combination \(x\) satisfies them as well. This proves necessity for arbitrary real assignments.

4. Sufficiency for every coupling

Suppose \(x\) is feasible and satisfies the characterization conditions. Define
\[ C=\{p\in P:d_p\geq\kappa_p\}, \]
and give destinations the values
\[ b_p=\mathbf{1}[p\in C]\quad(p\in P), \qquad b_{\ell_t}=0. \]

Every post in \(C\) is full in \(x\), entirely with first-choice mass. Indeed, the first characterization condition already assigns \(\kappa_p\) mass there, leaving no capacity for anything else.

Write the vote for moving type \(t\) from \(p\) to \(q\) as
\[ a_t(p,q) = \mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q]. \]
I claim that whenever \(x_{t,p}>0\),
\[ a_t(p,q)\leq b_q-b_p \qquad\text{for every }q\in S_t. \]

There are two cases.

  • If \(p=f_t\), moving to a different destination gives vote \(-1\). Since \(b_q-b_p\geq-1\), the inequality holds; for \(q=p\), both sides are zero.
  • If \(p=s_t\), positive mass there implies \(d_{f_t}>\kappa_{f_t}\). Otherwise first-choice saturation would assign all of type \(t\)'s mass to \(f_t\). Moreover \(b_{s_t}=0\). Every destination strictly preferred to \(s_t\) belongs to \(C\), by the definition of \(s_t\) and the fact that \(f_t\in C\). An improving move therefore has vote \(1=b_q-b_{s_t}\); an unchanged or worsening move also satisfies the inequality.

Now let \(y\) be any feasible challenger and \(z\) any permitted coupling. A source cell with \(x_{t,p}=0\) has zero coupling mass. Hence
\[ \sum_{t,p,q}z^t_{p,q}a_t(p,q) \leq \sum_{t,p,q}z^t_{p,q}(b_q-b_p) = \sum_{p\in C} \left( \sum_{t:p\in A_t}y_{t,p} - \sum_{t:p\in A_t}x_{t,p} \right). \]
Each parenthesized term is nonpositive: feasibility bounds the challenger's occupancy by \(\kappa_p\), while \(x\)'s occupancy equals \(\kappa_p\). Therefore the coupling's vote score is nonpositive.

This proves popularity, including against every coupling with \(y=x\), and completes the characterization.

5. Polynomial-time optimization

Compute \(f_t,d_p,s_t\), and retain only the two variables \(x_{t,f_t}\) and \(x_{t,s_t}\) for each type. Solve the linear program maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{p\in A_t}x_{t,p} \]
subject to nonnegativity, the type-mass equalities, the real-post capacity inequalities, and
\[ \sum_{t:f_t=p}x_{t,p}=\min\{d_p,\kappa_p\} \qquad(p\in P). \]

There are at most \(2\tau\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. All coefficients and right-hand sides have bit length polynomial in \(L\): first-choice demands are sums of input rationals, and fallback choices require only exact rational comparisons and preference-list scans.

Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore supplies either an optimal rational assignment or an infeasibility certificate. By the characterization, infeasibility means that no popular assignment exists; otherwise the optimum maximizes real-post mass among all real popular assignments.

The scaling factor \(D\) and the labelled applicants are used only in the proof. The algorithm never constructs the potentially exponential finite population.

Verdict: proved. The characterization, its extension to arbitrary real assignments, and the polynomial-time optimization argument are correct. I found no major or minor issues requiring repair.

1. Definitions and claimed characterization

The first choice \(f_t\) exists and is a real post because each acceptable set is nonempty and the last resort is ranked last. The first-choice demand \(d_p\) is well-defined.

The fallback \(s_t\) exists because its defining set contains \(\ell_t\). The explicit exclusion of \(f_t\) ensures \(s_t\ne f_t\), including when the first-choice post has spare capacity.

The equality case is handled correctly. If \(d_p=\kappa_p\), condition 1 assigns the entire first-choice demand to \(p\). Since each contributing type can assign at most its total mass there, every type whose first choice is \(p\) assigns all its mass to \(p\).

2. Convex-decomposition lemma

The lemma is valid.

For a supposedly nonpopular component \(v_i\), the constructed challenger
\[ y=\lambda_i y_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\lambda_jv_j \]
is feasible by convexity.

The proposed coupling has source marginal
\[ \lambda_i v_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\lambda_jv_j=x \]
and destination marginal \(y\). Nonnegativity follows from the nonnegativity of its constituent couplings and coefficients.

All diagonal contributions have zero vote score. The combined score is therefore exactly \(\lambda_i\) times the positive score defeating \(v_i\), which contradicts popularity of \(x\).

This step correctly uses the universal quantification over couplings. It does not assume independent comparisons or make an unjustified assertion that popularity is convex.

3. Integrality after scaling

The scaling argument is correct.

A positive integer \(D\) clearing all input denominators exists. The scaled masses and capacities are integers, and the total number of labelled applicants is \(D\).

The described equality matrix has at most two nonzero entries per column. Whenever there are two, they have opposite signs. The determinant argument proves total unimodularity:

  • A column with no nonzero entries gives determinant zero.
  • A column with one nonzero entry permits expansion to a smaller minor.
  • If every column has two opposite entries, the sum of the rows is zero, so the determinant vanishes.

Thus every nonsingular basis has determinant \(1\) or \(-1\), and an integral right-hand side gives integral basic feasible solutions. The slack variables are uniquely determined by the assignment variables, so their introduction preserves vertices.

The conclusion \(Dv_{t,p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}\) follows.

4. Verification of the cited finite theorem

The cited source supports the characterization used. Its Theorem 4 requires first-choice saturation and an agent-complete matching in the graph retaining first-choice and fallback edges. Its fallback definition agrees with the prover’s strict demand-versus-capacity test, including exclusion of the agent’s own first choice. Private unit-capacity last resorts are explicitly introduced in the source. [Manlove–Sng, Section 2.1 and Theorem 4](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~davidm/pubs/8240.pdf).

The finite instance constructed in the proof satisfies the hypotheses:

  • Preferences are strict.
  • Every applicant has a nonempty list.
  • Capacities are positive integers.
  • Only applicants vote.
  • Each labelled applicant has a private last resort.
  • The constructed matching is complete.

Deleting houses acceptable to nobody is harmless. Capping capacities at \(D\) also preserves feasibility. The stated exceptional comparison case is correct: a fallback eligibility test changes only when first-choice demand equals \(D\) and the original capacity exceeds \(D\). Then every applicant ranks that house first, so its exclusion from every fallback set makes the change irrelevant.

The theorem is therefore applied legitimately.

5. Necessity for arbitrary real assignments

The passage from a scaled vertex to a finite matching is valid: integral type-destination counts can be realized by assigning the corresponding numbers of labelled applicants.

The assertion that this matching is popular is justified. Any defeating complete finite challenger yields, after division by \(D\), a feasible mass challenger and a type-preserving coupling with the same vote score scaled by \(1/D\).

The treatment of incomplete challengers is also correct. Completing an unmatched applicant at its private last resort cannot decrease its vote against the complete matching \(M\): a previous loss remains a loss or becomes a tie. Thus a defeating incomplete challenger would yield a defeating complete one.

Collapsing individual last resorts into the type’s last-resort coordinate preserves comparisons, since each applicant can use only its own private last resort.

The bounded feasible polytope admits a finite vertex decomposition. The convex-decomposition lemma makes every positively weighted component popular. The finite theorem then establishes both characterization conditions at every component. These are linear equalities, including the zero-coordinate restrictions, and hence pass to the original assignment.

Necessity is established for all real assignments, not merely rational assignments.

6. Sufficiency against every coupling

The potential inequality is correct in both cases.

If the source destination is \(f_t\), every different destination is worse, giving vote \(-1\). Since destination potentials belong to \(\{0,1\}\), their difference is at least \(-1\).

If the source destination is \(s_t\), positive mass there implies \(d_{f_t}>\kappa_{f_t}\). Otherwise condition 1 would exhaust every corresponding type’s mass at its first choice. Also, \(b_{s_t}=0\). Every destination preferred to \(s_t\) belongs to \(C\): this holds for \(f_t\), and for other destinations it follows directly from the fallback definition. Improving moves therefore satisfy the inequality with equality; ties and worsening moves satisfy it as well.

A zero source marginal forces every entry in that coupling row to vanish. Consequently, the pointwise inequality applies to all coupling mass.

The subsequent marginal calculation is correct. It reduces the score bound to the challenger’s occupancy minus the incumbent’s occupancy over posts in \(C\). Each such incumbent post is full, entirely with first-choice mass, whereas the challenger cannot exceed its capacity.

The resulting bound is nonpositive for every feasible challenger and every coupling. The argument includes \(y=x\).

7. Optimization and running time

The reduced linear program exactly describes the popular assignments:

  • Necessity excludes every popular assignment outside its feasible region.
  • Sufficiency makes every feasible point popular.
  • Its objective is precisely the required total mass assigned to real posts.

There are at most \(2\tau\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. Demand sums have polynomial bit length, and fallback selection requires only exact rational comparisons and scans of the supplied lists.

Polynomial-time rational linear programming applies. The feasible region is bounded by the type-mass equalities and nonnegativity, so a feasible instance has an attained optimum. Infeasibility correctly certifies nonexistence of a popular assignment.

The potentially large labelled population is used only in the correctness proof. It is not constructed by the algorithm, so it creates no pseudopolynomial running-time dependence.
