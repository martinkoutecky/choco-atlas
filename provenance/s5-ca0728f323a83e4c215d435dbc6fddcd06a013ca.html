The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many institution types \(r\), with masses \(\mu_r\) and finite trees of rational entitlement streams \(v^{r,h}\in[0,1)^n\) whose coordinates sum to an integer, does there exist a causal mass-routing policy? At each node, the policy routes mass among integral subsets \(S\) of size \(H^{r,h}\), containing only categories with positive entitlement; every positive-mass institution history must preserve global quota, and the mass routed to category \(i\) must equal \(\mu_r v_i^{r,h}\) on each permitted path. Determine when such a policy exists, in particular whether the universal guarantee holds exactly for \(n\leq 3\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) institution types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_r\), normalized by \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=n\geq1\) categories. Each type supplies an explicit finite tree of entitlement prefixes, with a dummy initial root; each subsequent node \(h\) has \(v^{r,h}\in\mathbb{Q}^n\cap[0,1)^n\) and \(H^{r,h}=\sum_i v_i^{r,h}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\). Identical entitlement prefixes are identified. Let \(N\) be the total number of nodes and \(L\) the input bit length.
Each root-to-leaf path is a permitted scenario for the whole mass of that type; branches have no probabilities. The policy may be designed using the supplied trees, but its decisions must agree on shared observed prefixes.
Initially all mass has empty allocation history. At node \(h\), the policy divides the mass of each past allocation history into nonnegative real masses assigned to subsets \(S\subseteq\{i:v_i^{r,h}>0\}\) with \(|S|=H^{r,h}\), preserving that history's incoming mass. Each alternative child inherits the same resulting distribution of allocation histories.
For every positive-mass allocation history and every prefix ending at \(h\), require global quota: \(A_i^{r,h}\in\{\lfloor V_i^{r,h}\rfloor,\lceil V_i^{r,h}\rceil\}\), where \(A_i^{r,h}\) counts selections of category \(i\) and \(V_i^{r,h}\) sums its entitlements along that prefix; both start at zero.
At every node, the total mass assigned to subsets containing category \(i\) must equal \(\mu_r v_i^{r,h}\).

Is deciding whether such a causal mass-routing policy exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,N,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For randomized integral online apportionment, universal global quota together with ex-ante proportionality is attainable exactly when \(n\leq3\); for a fixed stream known offline, both properties are attainable for every \(n\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14752v1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized positive rational type masses to sum to one; routing remains separate for each type.
  • Required positive integer house sizes, as in the discrete definition; trees may have depth zero, accommodating the otherwise vacuous one-category domain.
  • Interpreted trees as explicit entitlement-prefix trees, identifying identical observed prefixes so hidden branch labels cannot influence decisions.
  • Interpreted permitted paths as alternative scenarios for the entire type mass, without branch probabilities or selection based on individual allocation histories.
  • Defined causal routing by conserving each allocation history's mass and passing the resulting distribution unchanged to every alternative child; allowed nonnegative real routed masses.
  • Defined global quota per positive-mass institution history, initialized cumulative entitlements and allocations at zero, and imposed the marginal equality separately at every node.
  • Allowed policy design to use the supplied finite trees while requiring decisions to agree on shared prefixes; introduced total tree size N and identified the requested size parameter m with n.

Result

The supplied-tree mass-routing feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. In particular, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with one type of mass \(1\), only two allocation rounds, and every entitlement coordinate belonging to \(\{0,\tfrac12\}\).

The proof uses branching to impose simultaneous constraints on the first allocation. Proportionality is achieved by giving equal mass to an allocation and its complement.

1. Boolean source problem

Write \(\operatorname{NAE}(a_1,\ldots,a_s)\) for the Boolean relation asserting that its arguments are not all equal.

We use the following restricted problem: satisfiability of conjunctions of clauses \(\operatorname{NAE}(\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3)\), where the three literals in each clause concern distinct variables. Negated literals are allowed. This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; here is an explicit reduction establishing the restriction.

Start with the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem of three-literal satisfiability. [Schaefer’s original paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/800133.804350) recalls this starting result. We may first obtain clauses with three distinct variables:

  • Delete tautological clauses and repeated literals.
  • Replace a two-literal clause \((a\lor b)\) by \((a\lor b\lor w)\land(a\lor b\lor\neg w)\), with fresh \(w\).
  • Replace a one-literal clause \((a)\) by the four clauses \((a\lor u'\lor w')\), where \(u'\in\{u,\neg u\}\) and \(w'\in\{w,\neg w\}\), with fresh \(u,w\).

Each replacement preserves satisfiability: if the original clause is false, the added clauses cannot all hold; if it is true, all added clauses hold.

Introduce one fresh variable \(z\). Replace each clause \((a\lor b\lor c)\) by \(\operatorname{NAE}(a,b,c,z)\). The resulting conjunction is satisfiable exactly when the original formula is satisfiable. Indeed, an original satisfying assignment extends by \(z=0\). Conversely, complementing every variable preserves all not-all-equal clauses, so any satisfying assignment can be complemented if necessary to make \(z=0\); each original clause then has a true literal.

Finally, with a fresh variable \(y\) for each clause, use the equivalence

\(\operatorname{NAE}(a,b,c,d)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists y\,[\operatorname{NAE}(a,b,y)\land\operatorname{NAE}(\neg y,c,d)]\).

To verify it, regard the literal values as Boolean bits. If \(a=b=s\), the first clause forces \(y=1-s\), and the second holds exactly when \(c,d\) are not both \(s\). If \(a\ne b\), the first clause holds for either \(y\), and \(y\) can always be chosen to satisfy the second. These are precisely the conditions for the four arguments not to be all equal.

Applied with \(d=z\), both resulting three-literal clauses concern distinct variables. All transformations have polynomial size.

2. A quota test

Consider a first node whose entitlement is \(\tfrac12\) in every category. Let \(S\) be its allocated subset, and write \(a_i=\mathbf 1_{\{i\in S\}}\).

At a child, choose an even-sized set \(T\), give entitlement \(\tfrac12\) to each category in \(T\), and give entitlement zero elsewhere. Its house size is \(H=|T|/2\).

For every \(i\in T\), cumulative entitlement at the child is exactly \(1\). Thus global quota requires

\(a_i+\mathbf 1_{\{i\text{ is selected at the child}\}}=1\).

Consequently, the child’s allocation must be exactly \(T\setminus S\). It has the required size if and only if

\(|S\cap T|=|T|/2\).

Categories outside \(T\) remain at cumulative entitlement \(\tfrac12\), with allocation count zero or one, so their quotas hold automatically.

We have therefore proved:

Quota-test lemma. A first allocation \(S\) can be extended through this child if and only if it selects exactly half of \(T\). When an extension exists, it is unique.

3. Construction of the society

Let \(\Phi\) be a restricted not-all-equal formula with variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\) and clauses \(C_1,\ldots,C_q\).

Introduce an auxiliary Boolean variable \(u_j\) for each clause \(C_j\). Let \(B\) be the set of original and auxiliary variables, and put \(K=k+q\).

For each \(b\in B\), create two categories \((b,0)\) and \((b,1)\). Hence \(m=n=2K\). Use one institution type, with mass \(1\).

After the dummy root, create a common first node \(h_0\) with

\(v_i^{h_0}=\tfrac12\quad\text{for every category }i,\qquad H^{h_0}=K\).

Its children are all leaves, of the following two kinds.

Variable tests

For every \(b\in B\), add a child with entitlement \(\tfrac12\) on

\(T_b=\{(b,0),(b,1)\}\),

and zero elsewhere. Its house size is \(1\).

By the quota-test lemma, every first allocation that can survive this child selects exactly one category from the pair.

Clause tests

Associate a literal with a category by

\(c(x_i)=(x_i,1),\qquad c(\neg x_i)=(x_i,0)\).

For a clause \(C_j=\operatorname{NAE}(\ell_{j1},\ell_{j2},\ell_{j3})\), add a child with entitlement \(\tfrac12\) on

\(T_j=\{c(\ell_{j1}),c(\ell_{j2}),c(\ell_{j3}),(u_j,1)\}\),

and zero elsewhere. Its house size is \(2\).

These are four distinct categories: the clause concerns three distinct original variables, and \(u_j\) is fresh.

The tree has \(K+q+2\) nodes, including the dummy root. All vectors have entries zero or \(\tfrac12\), their sums are positive integers, and their explicit encoding has polynomial size. The children have distinct entitlement prefixes, as required.

4. Correctness of the reduction

A feasible policy yields a satisfying assignment

Suppose the constructed instance admits a policy.

At \(h_0\), some allocated subset \(S\) has positive mass. Every alternative child inherits this same positive mass for history \(S\). Because incoming mass must be preserved, \(S\) must admit an extension through every child.

The variable tests and the quota-test lemma therefore imply

\(|S\cap T_b|=1\quad\text{for every }b\in B\).

Define \(b\) to be true exactly when \((b,1)\in S\). Then a literal \(\ell\) is true exactly when \(c(\ell)\in S\).

For clause \(C_j\), let \(t_j\) be its number of true literals. Its child test requires

\(t_j+\mathbf 1_{\{(u_j,1)\in S\}}=2\).

The indicator is zero or one, so \(t_j\in\{1,2\}\). Thus the clause is not all equal. Every clause of \(\Phi\) holds.

This direction relies on the stipulated inheritance at branches: a positive-mass first history cannot be discarded when a particular child occurs.

A satisfying assignment yields a feasible policy

Conversely, suppose \(\Phi\) has a satisfying assignment. For each clause, its number \(t_j\) of true literals belongs to \(\{1,2\}\). Set

\(u_j=2-t_j\).

Let \(S\) contain exactly one category from each variable pair, selecting \((b,1)\) when \(b\) is true and \((b,0)\) otherwise. Then \(|S|=K\). Every variable test contains exactly one member of \(S\), and every clause test contains exactly two.

Let \(\overline S\) be the complement of \(S\) among all \(2K\) categories. It also has size \(K\), and it also selects half of every test set.

At \(h_0\), assign mass \(\tfrac12\) to \(S\) and mass \(\tfrac12\) to \(\overline S\). Each category receives mass \(\tfrac12\), exactly its entitlement. Every first allocation satisfies global quota.

At a child with test set \(T\), route the two histories deterministically to

\(T\setminus S\quad\text{and}\quad T\setminus\overline S=T\cap S\),

respectively. Both allocations have size \(|T|/2\), and the quota-test lemma proves global quota for both histories.

Every category in \(T\) occurs in exactly one of these two child allocations, so its selected mass is \(\tfrac12\). Categories outside \(T\) receive zero selected mass. Thus proportionality holds at every child.

This is causal: the first distribution is common to all branches, and each child’s action depends only on that child and the inherited allocation history.

Therefore the constructed society is feasible if and only if \(\Phi\) is satisfiable. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Real-valued routing masses do not prevent polynomial certificates.

A deterministic allocation table specifies one allowed subset at every supplied node, consistently with identified prefixes. The subsets along each root-to-node path determine its allocation history. Call the table valid if global quota holds at every node.

A table requires at most \(mN\) indicator bits. Its validity can be checked in polynomial time by computing cumulative entitlements and allocation counts along all prefixes.

Let \(M\leq N\) be the number of non-dummy node occurrences, and put \(D=mM\). Encode a valid table \(P\) by its selection indicators \(a(P)\in\{0,1\}^{D}\). Let \(b\in\mathbb Q^{D}\) collect the corresponding entitlement coordinates.

Since each \(\mu_r\) is positive, dividing each type’s mass constraints by \(\mu_r\) gives the normalized target marginals \(b\).

Policies are equivalent to lotteries over valid tables

Given a routing policy, sample its choices recursively through the whole supplied tree. At a branch, sample continuations for every child conditional on the shared ancestor history. Share sampled choices wherever prefixes are identified.

This sampling is a coupling of alternative continuations; it does not give branches probabilities. There are finitely many choices and nodes. Every resulting table of positive probability is valid, because every sampled path history has positive policy mass and therefore satisfies quota.

For separate type trees, their normalized samplings can be coupled jointly; shared prefixes use the same prescribed conditional choices. Thus a feasible policy gives a finite lottery over valid tables with mean \(b\).

Conversely, a lottery over valid tables implements a routing policy: at each observed prefix and allocation history, condition the lottery on the ancestor allocations and split according to the tables’ current subsets. Every child inherits the same resulting history distribution. This uses only observed information and preserves the lottery’s marginals.

Hence feasibility is equivalent to

\(b\in\operatorname{conv}\{a(P):P\text{ is a valid deterministic table}\}\).

A polynomial-size rational lottery suffices

Start with any finite feasible lottery. If its augmented vectors \((1,a(P))\) are linearly dependent, choose a nonzero dependence \(c\). Its coefficients sum to zero, so some are positive and some negative.

For positive lottery weights \(\lambda_j\), replace them by \(\lambda_j-tc_j\), where

\(t=\min_{c_j>0}\lambda_j/c_j\).

This preserves normalization and all marginals, keeps every weight nonnegative, and eliminates at least one table. Repeating leaves \(s\leq D+1\) tables whose augmented vectors are linearly independent.

Choose \(s\) independent rows of their augmented matrix. They form an invertible matrix \(C\in\{0,1\}^{s\times s}\), and the weights satisfy \(C\lambda=d\), where \(d\) consists of selected coordinates of \((1,b)\).

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the input entitlement coordinates. Its bit length is bounded by the input length \(L\). The vector \(e=Qd\) is integral, with entries between zero and \(Q\). Cramer’s rule gives

\(\lambda_j=\det(C_j(e))/(Q\det C)\).

Hadamard’s determinant bound yields

\(|\det C|\leq s^{s/2},\qquad |\det(C_j(e))|\leq Qs^{s/2}\).

Each weight therefore has bit length \(O(L+s\log s)\).

A certificate consists of at most \(D+1\) valid tables and these rational weights. A verifier checks table validity, nonnegative weights, normalization, and every marginal equality using exact rational arithmetic. All checks take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,N,L\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the classification.
