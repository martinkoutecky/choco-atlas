The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two countries \(i\in\{1,2\}\), each with a finite support of user types \(T_i\) and rational type masses \(\mu_{it}\ge 0\) summing to one within each country, specify each type’s utility, endowment, and relevant location or infrastructure access, along with the river’s resource and production constraints, disagreement utilities, and discount factors. Each proposal assigns per-capita bundles to every type and specifies feasible production or flow plans; country \(i\)’s utility is \(U_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_{it}u_{it}\). Compute the stationary equilibrium proposals by optimizing the asymmetric Nash product subject to the two proposals’ feasibility and continuation-acceptance constraints.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are two countries \(i\in\{1,2\}\) and \(m\ge2\) location-specific commodities. A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive binary-rational masses. Write these supports as \(T_i\), require \(\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_{it}=1\), and let \(\tau=|T_1|+|T_2|\); this counts supported types, not possible types.
Each type has a nonnegative rational per-capita endowment \(w_{it}\) and utility \(u_{it}:\mathbb R_+^m\to\mathbb R\), represented explicitly as the minimum of finitely many rational affine functions with strictly positive slopes. Define \(U_i(z)=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_{it}u_{it}(z_{it})\), \(W=\sum_{i,t}\mu_{it}w_{it}>0\), and \(d_i=U_i(w)\).
A proposal \(z\) specifies nonnegative per-capita bundles \(z_{it}\) and signed net-output vectors \(q_i\in\mathbb R^m\), with negative components denoting inputs. Feasibility comprises explicitly listed rational linear production, river-flow, and location/access constraints, including \(\sum_{i,t}\mu_{it}z_{it}\le W+q_1+q_2\). These define a nonempty bounded polytope \(\mathcal F\) containing the endowment allocation with zero production; some feasible proposal must satisfy \(U_i(z)>d_i\) for both countries.
Countries alternate proposals indefinitely. Acceptance implements a binding constant allocation forever; rejection gives one period of disagreement utility and switches the proposer. Discount factors are binary rationals \(\delta_i\in(0,1)\), and responders accept at indifference. A stationary equilibrium pair \((x,y)\) satisfies \(x\in\arg\max\{U_1(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ U_2(z)\ge(1-\delta_2)d_2+\delta_2U_2(y)\}\) and the analogous condition for \(y\), maximizing \(U_2\) subject to \(U_1(z)\ge(1-\delta_1)d_1+\delta_1U_1(x)\).
The computational problem is to obtain such proposals by maximizing \((s_1-d_1)^\alpha(s_2-d_2)^{1-\alpha}\), where \(\alpha=\ln\delta_2/(\ln\delta_1+\ln\delta_2)\), subject to \(x,y\in\mathcal F\), \(s_i\ge d_i\), \(s_1\le U_1(x)\), \(s_2\le U_2(y)\), \((1-\delta_1)d_1+\delta_1s_1\le U_1(y)\), and \((1-\delta_2)d_2+\delta_2s_2\le U_2(x)\). Any equilibrium pair is admissible when allocations are nonunique. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including all affine pieces and feasibility constraints.

Can these stationary equilibrium proposals be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is this computation \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite-dimensional two-country economies, stationary equilibrium proposals are characterized by this single asymmetric-Nash-product program under strictly increasing concave utilities; the characterization extends to convex production. Result

The stationary equilibrium proposals can be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\). There is an algorithm that outputs rational proposals with polynomially bounded binary encoding length.

The essential reduction is to a scalar equation involving a concave piecewise-linear function evaluated by linear programming. Its unique root, when needed, is rational with polynomially bounded bit length. The algorithm does not need to evaluate the logarithms defining \(\alpha\).

1. A polynomial-size representation of attainable utility gains

Write \(G_i(z)=U_i(z)-d_i\), where the argument \(z\) includes the complete feasible proposal, including production variables.

Define the downward-closed set of nonnegative attainable gains:

\(K=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb R_+^2:\text{there exists }z\in\mathcal F\text{ with }G_1(z)\ge a,\ G_2(z)\ge b\}.\)

We first show that \(K\) has a polynomial-size rational extended formulation.

Write each type’s utility as

\(u_{it}(v)=\min_{k\in J_{it}}\{\gamma_{itk}^{\mathsf T}v+\beta_{itk}\}.\)

Introduce one variable \(h_{it}\) per supported type and impose

\(h_{it}\le\gamma_{itk}^{\mathsf T}z_{it}+\beta_{itk}\quad(k\in J_{it}).\)

Set \(\ell_{it}=\min_{k\in J_{it}}\beta_{itk}\) and additionally impose \(h_{it}\ge\ell_{it}\). This lower bound preserves every actual utility value: nonnegative consumption and positive slopes imply \(u_{it}(z_{it})\ge\ell_{it}\).

Let \(P\) be the rational polytope consisting of these inequalities, the constraints defining \(\mathcal F\), and

\(a,b\ge0,\qquad a\le\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_{1t}h_{1t}-d_1,\qquad b\le\sum_{t\in T_2}\mu_{2t}h_{2t}-d_2.\)

Its projection onto \((a,b)\) is exactly \(K\):

  • Every point of \(P\) has \(h_{it}\le u_{it}(z_{it})\), so its projected gains belong to \(K\).
  • Conversely, for a proposal witnessing membership in \(K\), choose \(h_{it}=u_{it}(z_{it})\).

The polytope \(P\) is bounded. Indeed, proposal coordinates are bounded by hypothesis; the displayed inequalities then bound every \(h_{it}\) above and below, and consequently bound \(a,b\).

The numbers \(d_i\) can be computed exactly by evaluating the explicitly listed affine pieces at the rational endowments and taking weighted sums. All these operations have polynomial bit complexity. Thus the dimension and binary encoding length of \(P\) are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

We use the standard exact rational linear-programming theorem: a rational LP can be solved, including obtaining an exact rational optimizer, in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. See, for example, the bit-complexity discussion in [Dadush, Huiberts, Natura, and Végh](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-023-01956-2).

2. The scalar frontier

Define

\(A=\max\{a:(a,b)\in K\text{ for some }b\}\)

and, for \(t\in[0,A]\),

\(f(t)=\max\{b:(t,b)\in K\}.\)

Both \(A\) and every rational-argument evaluation of \(f\) are computable by exact LPs over \(P\). Equivalently,

\(f(t)=\max\{G_2(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ G_1(z)\ge t\}.\)

The latter maximum is nonnegative on \([0,A]\), because membership of \((A,0)\) in \(K\), together with downward closure, guarantees a feasible proposal with both required gains nonnegative.

The set \(K\) is a compact convex rational polygon, and it is downward closed within \(\mathbb R_+^2\). Consequently, \(f\) is continuous, nonincreasing, concave, and piecewise affine.

The assumed strictly beneficial proposal implies \(A>0\) and \(f(0)>0\). Concavity gives, for \(0\le t<A\),

\(f(t)\ge(1-t/A)f(0)+(t/A)f(A)>0.\)

Now define

\(g(t)=f(t)-\delta_2f(\delta_1t),\qquad 0\le t\le A.\)

In particular,

\(g(0)=(1-\delta_2)f(0)>0.\)

Monotonicity and uniqueness of a zero

Because \(f\) is concave and nonincreasing, its derivative, wherever it exists, is nonpositive and nonincreasing. Except at finitely many points,

\(g'(t)=f'(t)-\delta_1\delta_2f'(\delta_1t)\le(1-\delta_1\delta_2)f'(t)\le0.\)

Here the first inequality uses \(\delta_1t\le t\), hence \(f'(\delta_1t)\ge f'(t)\).

Piecewise-affine functions are absolutely continuous, so integration yields, for \(0\le u<v\le A\),

\(g(v)-g(u)\le(1-\delta_1\delta_2)\bigl(f(v)-f(u)\bigr)\le0.\)

Thus \(g\) is nonincreasing.

Moreover, \(g\) has at most one zero. Suppose \(g(u)=g(v)=0\) for \(u<v\). The preceding inequality forces \(f(u)=f(v)\). A nonincreasing concave function constant on \([u,v]\) is constant on \([0,v]\): concavity makes every earlier secant slope at least the zero slope on \([u,v]\), while monotonicity makes it nonpositive. Therefore,

\(g(u)=(1-\delta_2)f(0)>0,\)

a contradiction.

Define \(a^*,b^*\) as follows:

  • If \(g(A)\ge0\), let \(a^*=A\) and \(b^*=f(\delta_1A)\).
  • If \(g(A)<0\), continuity and the preceding uniqueness proof give a unique \(a^*\in(0,A)\) with \(g(a^*)=0\); let \(b^*=f(\delta_1a^*)\).

In both cases \(a^*,b^*>0\), and

\(f(a^*)\ge\delta_2b^*,\qquad f(\delta_1a^*)=b^*.\)

3. These gains produce stationary equilibrium proposals

Choose \(y\) by solving

\(\max\{G_2(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ G_1(z)\ge\delta_1a^*\}.\)

Its optimal value is \(b^*\), so \(G_2(y)=b^*\).

Choose \(x\) by solving

\(\max\{G_1(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ G_2(z)\ge\delta_2b^*\}.\)

We claim that its optimal value is \(a^*\).

If \(a^*=A\), the claim follows directly: any feasible proposal for this LP has \(G_2(z)>0\), so its first-country gain cannot exceed \(A\). The inequality \(f(A)\ge\delta_2b^*\) guarantees attainability.

Otherwise, \(f(a^*)=\delta_2b^*\), so \(a^*\) is attainable. If some feasible proposal had first-country gain \(a'>a^*\), then

\(f(a')\ge\delta_2b^*=f(a^*).\)

Monotonicity would make \(f\) constant on \([a^*,a']\), and concavity would then make it constant on \([0,a']\). This contradicts

\(f(\delta_1a^*)=b^*>\delta_2b^*=f(a^*).\)

Thus \(G_1(x)=a^*\).

The two LP optimality conditions are therefore exactly

\(x\in\arg\max\{U_1(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ U_2(z)\ge d_2+\delta_2(U_2(y)-d_2)\}\)

and

\(y\in\arg\max\{U_2(z):z\in\mathcal F,\ U_1(z)\ge d_1+\delta_1(U_1(x)-d_1)\}.\)

These are the stationary equilibrium conditions in the question.

4. The same gains maximize the stated Nash-product program

This section verifies directly that the construction solves the specified maximization problem, including boundary cases.

Put \(a=s_1-d_1\) and \(b=s_2-d_2\). After projecting out \(x,y\), the program’s feasible gain pairs are exactly

\(S=\{(a,b):0\le a\le A,\ b\ge0,\ \delta_2b\le f(a),\ b\le f(\delta_1a)\}.\)

Indeed, its two proposals respectively witness \((a,\delta_2b)\in K\) and \((\delta_1a,b)\in K\).

For positive gains, maximizing the product is equivalent to maximizing the strictly concave function

\(H(a,b)=\alpha\ln a+(1-\alpha)\ln b.\)

We will exhibit supporting inequalities showing that \((a^*,b^*)\) maximizes \(H\).

First, for every \(r\in(0,1)\), integration of \(1/t\) over \([r,1]\) gives

\(1<\frac{-\ln r}{1-r}<\frac1r.\)

Applying these strict inequalities to the discount factors yields

\(\frac{\delta_1(1-\delta_2)}{1-\delta_1}<\frac{\ln\delta_2}{\ln\delta_1}<\frac{1-\delta_2}{\delta_2(1-\delta_1)}.\)

For this section abbreviate \(a=a^*\), \(b=b^*\), and set

\(k=\frac{(1-\delta_2)b}{(1-\delta_1)a},\qquad R=\frac{H_a(a,b)}{H_b(a,b)}=\frac{\ln\delta_2}{\ln\delta_1}\frac ba.\)

The preceding inequality becomes

\(\delta_1k<R<k/\delta_2.\)

Interior case

Suppose \(a<A\). Then

\(f(a)=\delta_2b,\qquad f(\delta_1a)=b.\)

Choose supergradients \(-p_x\) and \(-p_y\) of the concave function \(f\) at \(a\) and \(\delta_1a\), respectively. These points are interior to its domain, so finite supergradients exist. Monotonicity implies \(p_x,p_y\ge0\).

Applying the supergradient inequalities across these two points gives

\(p_x\ge k\ge p_y.\)

For every \((a',b')\in S\), the same supergradient inequalities imply

\(p_x(a'-a)+\delta_2(b'-b)\le0,\)

\(\delta_1p_y(a'-a)+(b'-b)\le0.\)

The two normal vectors have first-to-second-coordinate ratios \(p_x/\delta_2\) and \(\delta_1p_y\). Since

\(\delta_1p_y\le\delta_1k<R<k/\delta_2\le p_x/\delta_2,\)

the vector \(\nabla H(a,b)\) is a positive linear combination of these normals. Therefore,

\(\nabla H(a,b)^{\mathsf T}((a',b')-(a,b))\le0.\)

Concavity of \(H\) proves optimality; strict concavity proves uniqueness among positive gain pairs.

Boundary case

Suppose \(a=A\). Choose a supergradient \(-p_y\) at the interior point \(\delta_1A\). Its supergradient inequality at \(A\) gives

\(0\le p_y\le\frac{b-f(A)}{(1-\delta_1)A}\le\frac{(1-\delta_2)b}{(1-\delta_1)A}=k.\)

The last inequality uses \(f(A)\ge\delta_2b\).

Every feasible pair satisfies

\(a'-A\le0,\qquad \delta_1p_y(a'-A)+(b'-b)\le0.\)

Because \(R>\delta_1k\ge\delta_1p_y\), the gradient of \(H\) is a positive linear combination of \((1,0)\) and \((\delta_1p_y,1)\). The same supporting-hyperplane argument proves optimality and uniqueness.

Finally, gain pairs with a zero coordinate have product zero, whereas \((a^*,b^*)\) has positive product. Hence the construction gives the global maximum of the original program.

5. A polynomial bit bound for the scalar root

It remains to justify exact polynomial-time recovery of \(a^*\), rather than merely approximation.

Let \(n\) be the dimension of \(P\), and let \(B\) bound its total binary encoding length together with that of the discount factors. Both are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Clear denominators separately in each defining inequality of \(P\). Conservatively, the resulting integer coefficients and right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(2^{2B}\). Define

\(D=n\bigl(2B+\lceil\log_2(\max\{2,n\})\rceil\bigr)+1.\)

At any vertex of \(P\), choose \(n\) linearly independent tight inequalities. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound

\(|\det M|\le n!\,(2^{2B})^n<2^D\)

show that every vertex coordinate has a reduced numerator and denominator of absolute value at most \(2^D\).

Since \(P\) is the convex hull of its vertices, \(K\) is the convex hull of their projections. Every vertex of \(K\) is therefore the projection of a vertex of \(P\), and inherits the same coordinate bound.

Every affine piece \(f(t)=p+qt\) lies on an upper-boundary edge of \(K\). If its endpoints are \((x_1,y_1)\), \((x_2,y_2)\), then

\(q=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1},\qquad p=y_1-qx_1.\)

The coordinate bounds imply that the reduced numerators and denominators of \(q\) are at most \(2^{4D+2}\), and those of \(p\) at most \(2^{6D+3}\).

Near an interior root, on a one-sided interval adjacent to it, write

\(f(t)=p+qt,\qquad f(\delta_1t)=r+v\delta_1t.\)

Then

\(g(t)=(p-\delta_2r)+(q-\delta_1\delta_2v)t.\)

The slope on this adjacent interval cannot vanish: continuity would otherwise make the whole interval consist of zeros, contradicting uniqueness. Consequently,

\(a^*=\frac{\delta_2r-p}{q-\delta_1\delta_2v}.\)

This formula also applies when the root is a breakpoint, by continuity from the adjacent interval.

The displayed coefficient bounds, together with the encoded discount factors, imply a polynomial bound on the root’s numerator and denominator. For example, the conservative, computable choice

\(K_0=100(D+B+1)\)

bounds both by \(2^{K_0}\). Indeed, products multiply numerator and denominator bounds, and subtraction adds at most one bit beyond the product of the two bounds; the root formula uses only a fixed number of these operations.

Thus \(a^*\) is rational with polynomial encoding length. The boundary value \(A\) already has the vertex-coordinate bound.

6. Exact recovery by polynomially many LP calls

Compute \(A\), \(f(A)\), and \(f(\delta_1A)\) exactly.

If \(g(A)\ge0\), use the boundary construction. Otherwise, maintain a bracket \([\ell,r]\subseteq[0,A]\) with

\(g(\ell)>0,\qquad g(r)<0.\)

At each rational midpoint \(c\), evaluate

\(g(c)=f(c)-\delta_2f(\delta_1c)\)

using two exact LPs. If it is zero, the root has been obtained. Otherwise, its sign determines the next bracket because \(g\) is nonincreasing and has a unique zero.

Let \(Q=2^{K_0}\). Since \(A\le2^D\), after at most \(D+2K_0+3\) bisections the bracket width is less than \(1/(4Q^2)\). Every midpoint and every LP argument used has polynomial encoding length.

If no zero has yet been encountered, let \(c\) be the final bracket midpoint. Writing the reduced root as \(a^*=p/q\), we have \(q\le Q\) and

\(\left|c-\frac pq\right|<\frac1{2Q^2}\le\frac1{2q^2}.\)

Legendre’s continued-fraction criterion implies that \(p/q\) is a convergent of \(c\); see [Hančl and Nguyen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11166). If \(c=a^*\), the finite expansion itself supplies the answer.

Compute the continued-fraction convergents of \(c\) and retain those lying in the bracket with denominator at most \(Q\). Exactly one qualifies:

  • The root qualifies by the criterion.
  • Two distinct reduced rationals with denominators at most \(Q\) differ by at least \(1/Q^2\), exceeding the bracket width.

The Euclidean algorithm computes these convergents in polynomial time. Hence \(a^*\) is recovered exactly with polynomially many polynomial-size LP calls.

Compute \(b^*=f(\delta_1a^*)\), then solve the two proposal LPs from Section 3. Their input lengths are polynomial, so exact rational optimizers \(x,y\) can be returned in polynomial time.

Conclusion

The algorithm computes an exact maximizer of the specified asymmetric-Nash-product program and returns proposals satisfying both stationary best-response conditions.

Its complexity is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), including all explicitly represented utility pieces and feasibility constraints. It neither enumerates the possibly numerous frontier pieces nor evaluates the logarithmic bargaining weight.
