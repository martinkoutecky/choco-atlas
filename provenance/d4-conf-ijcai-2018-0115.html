The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two finite distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) over complete LP-tree types with rational masses, compute the exact value \(\Delta(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{T,U}\mu_T\nu_U d_K(T,U)\), where \(d_K(T,U)\) counts outcome pairs on which the two trees disagree.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\geq 1\) attributes have explicitly listed, finite nonempty domains \(D_1,\ldots,D_m\), shared by all types; outcomes form \(\mathcal O=\prod_{i=1}^{m}D_i\).
A complete LP-tree is an explicitly represented rooted tree with one attribute at each node and every attribute occurring exactly once on each root-to-leaf path. A nonleaf node labelled \(X_i\) has either one unconditional outgoing edge or one outgoing edge for each value in \(D_i\), labelled by that value.
Each node has an explicit conditional preference table: for every assignment to a specified subset of its ancestor attributes, it lists a strict total order on the node's domain. To compare distinct outcomes, traverse using their common values until the first differing attribute; its applicable local order decides the comparison, inducing \(\succ_T\).
A society is given by its support: a list of types with positive binary rational masses. The input consists of two such societies \(\mu,\nu\), each of total mass \(1\); let \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\cup\operatorname{supp}(\nu)|\), the joint support size, not the number of possible LP-tree types. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including domains, trees, tables, and masses; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Let \(d_K(T,U)\) be the unnormalized number of unordered pairs of distinct outcomes ranked oppositely by \(\succ_T\) and \(\succ_U\), and define \(\Delta(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\sum_{U\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)}\mu_T\nu_U d_K(T,U)\).

Is computing the exact rational value \(\Delta(\mu,\nu)\) possible in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For two explicitly represented complete LP-trees, exact Kendall distance has an algorithm with a stated quadratic bound in their representation sizes ([Li and Kazimipour](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2018/0262.pdf)).
No result specifically treating the distribution problem defined here was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • All types use the same explicitly listed attribute domains; domains are finite and nonempty, and there is at least one attribute.
  • Interpreted distributions as probability distributions: each has positive rational masses on its listed support and total mass one.
  • Defined tau as the size of the union of the two supports and L as the total binary encoding length, including all tree structures and preference tables.
  • Resolved the incomplete branching description by making a single edge unconditional and requiring a split to cover every domain value exactly once, consistent with complete outcome coverage and the foundational branching convention.
  • Made conditional preference dependencies subsets of ancestor attributes and required explicit complete tables of strict total orders.
  • Used the unnormalized Kendall count of unordered distinct outcome pairs, and required the exact rational value.

Conclusion

The exact value \(\Delta(\mu,\nu)\) is computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). A conservative bound, using ordinary binary arithmetic, is \(O\!\left(\tau^2(m+1)L^4\right)\).

The key is that the supports are explicitly listed: the definition requires at most \(\tau^2\) pairwise distances, followed by an exact weighted sum.

1. Applying the pairwise algorithm

Write \(S_\mu=\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) and \(S_\nu=\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\). Then \(|S_\mu||S_\nu|\leq\tau^2\).

Li and Kazimipour’s Theorem 1 establishes correctness of their exact Kendall-distance algorithm, and Theorem 2 states a quadratic bound in the explicitly represented tree sizes. Their model permits multi-valued domains, conditional preferences, and different attribute orders and branching structures. Thus it covers the complete LP-trees in this question: every attribute occurs once on each path, and preference tables explicitly list orders conditioned on ancestor attributes. Their distance counts unordered outcome pairs, matching \(d_K\). [Li and Kazimipour, Sections 2–4](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2018/0262.pdf).

Consequently, compute \(k_{T,U}=d_K(T,U)\) for every \(T\in S_\mu\) and \(U\in S_\nu\). No enumeration of other LP-tree types is needed.

2. Bit lengths of the distances

Put \(d_i=|D_i|\) and \(N=|\mathcal O|=\prod_{i=1}^m d_i\). Because the domains are explicitly listed, \(\sum_i d_i=O(L)\). Hence

\(\log_2 N=\sum_{i=1}^m\log_2 d_i\leq\sum_{i=1}^m d_i=O(L).\)

Every distance satisfies

\(0\leq k_{T,U}\leq\binom{N}{2},\)

so its binary representation has \(O(L)\) bits, even when \(N\) is exponential in the input length.

The pairwise algorithm computes counts through local disagreements, products of domain cardinalities, and sums over disjoint classes of outcome pairs. Its counting formulas use domain cardinalities with exponents at most two. These quantities have \(O(L)\) bits: a product with those exponents is at most \(N^2\), and accumulated distances are at most \(\binom{N}{2}\).

Thus the stated quadratic counting bound also yields a polynomial bit-complexity bound. More explicitly, each tree has representation size at most \(L\). Allowing an additional factor \(m+1\) for processing attribute sets and products, and \(O(L^2)\) for schoolbook arithmetic on \(O(L)\)-bit integers, gives the conservative bound

\(O\!\left((m+1)L^4\right)\)

per pair of trees. All cross-support distances therefore take

\(O\!\left(\tau^2(m+1)L^4\right)\)

bit operations.

If \(N=1\), there are no distinct outcome pairs and every distance is zero; this case is immediate.

3. Computing the rational weighted sum exactly

Write the input masses as

\(\mu_T=p_T/q_T,\qquad \nu_U=r_U/s_U,\)

where all numerators and denominators are positive integers encoded in binary. Define

\(Q_\mu=\prod_{T\in S_\mu}q_T,\qquad Q_\nu=\prod_{U\in S_\nu}s_U.\)

Since the total encoding length of the denominators is at most \(L\), both products have \(O(L)\) bits. Define integer weights

\(a_T=p_T(Q_\mu/q_T),\qquad b_U=r_U(Q_\nu/s_U).\)

The divisions are exact because each denominator is a factor of its corresponding product. The total-mass assumptions give

\(\sum_{T\in S_\mu}a_T=Q_\mu,\qquad \sum_{U\in S_\nu}b_U=Q_\nu.\)

Now compute the integer

\(A=\sum_{T\in S_\mu}\sum_{U\in S_\nu}a_Tb_Uk_{T,U}.\)

Substitution into the definition proves

\(\Delta(\mu,\nu)=\dfrac{A}{Q_\mu Q_\nu}.\)

There is no problematic denominator growth. Indeed, nonnegativity and the distance bound imply

\(0\leq A\leq\binom{N}{2}\sum_{T,U}a_Tb_U =\binom{N}{2}Q_\mu Q_\nu.\)

Therefore \(A\), the denominator, and every partial sum have \(O(L)\) bits. Constructing the common denominators and integer weights, evaluating the at most \(\tau^2\) summands, and reducing the final fraction by a greatest common divisor all take polynomial time. With schoolbook arithmetic, a sufficient bound for this stage is \(O(\tau^2L^2+L^3)\).

4. Final complexity classification

Combining the two stages proves the bound

\(O\!\left(\tau^2(m+1)L^4\right).\)

This resolves the stated problem as polynomial-time solvable. A separate literature result about distributions is unnecessary: explicit support makes the distribution computation a polynomial number of applications of the pairwise algorithm, followed by polynomial-time exact rational arithmetic.
