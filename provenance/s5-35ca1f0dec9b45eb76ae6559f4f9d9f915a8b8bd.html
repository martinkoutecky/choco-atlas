The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) product classes, finitely many additive types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), values \(v_{tj}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal ordered mass single-price policy and determine whether the worst-case ratio \(\mathrm{OPT}/B_{\mathrm{SP}}\) can remain \(\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\) when each type’s mass may split across prices and positions.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq3\) product classes, additive values \(v_{tj}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), and binary rational supplies \(q_j\geq0\) with \(\sum_jq_j>0\). Each consumer can receive at most one unit of each class, with quasilinear utility and no budget constraint.
An ordered mass single-price policy is a finite, precommitted sequence of blocks \((t_k,a_k,p_k)\), with positive rational masses \(a_k\), nonnegative rational prices \(p_k\), and \(\sum_{k:t_k=t}a_k=\mu_t\). Thus a type may split across prices and positions, but each consumer is visited once; prices need not decrease along the sequence.
Starting with remaining supplies \(r_j=q_j\), block \(k\) buys \(y_{kj}=\min\{a_k,r_j\}\) whenever \(v_{t_kj}\geq p_k\), and buys zero otherwise; subtract these purchases from supplies. Indifference is resolved in favor of buying. Welfare is \(W=\sum_{k,j}v_{t_kj}y_{kj}\).
Let \(B_{\mathrm{SP}}\) be the supremum of policy welfare, and let \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max\{\sum_{t,j}v_{tj}x_{tj}:0\leq x_{tj}\leq\mu_t,\ \sum_tx_{tj}\leq q_j\}\). For rational \(0<\varepsilon<1\), an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal policy has \(W\geq(1-\varepsilon)B_{\mathrm{SP}}\). Define \(R(m)=\sup_I\mathrm{OPT}(I)/B_{\mathrm{SP}}(I)\), over all admissible instances with \(m\) classes.
How can an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal ordered mass single-price policy be computed, with running time stated in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
Does \(R(m)=\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\) still hold, or does mass splitting permit a uniform \(o(\log m/\log\log m)\) approximation ratio?
For finite additive buyers, even the best ordered policy with one price per buyer has worst-case ratio \(\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\), with integer item values between \(1\) and \(m\); allowing buyer-and-item-specific prices permits optimal welfare ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.02831)).
No result settling this precise mass-policy approximation question was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total consumer mass to one so supplies and welfare are per capita.
  • Interpreted each product class as a replicated item, with each consumer eligible for at most one unit per class; the graded question did not specify individual demand bounds.
  • Allowed nonnegative rational supplies, excluding the all-zero supply instance to make the welfare ratio defined.
  • Defined mass policies as finite sequences of homogeneous rational blocks, with arbitrary repetition of types and unrestricted ordering of nonnegative prices.
  • Required disjoint consumer portions to be visited once, so splitting a type does not permit repeated purchases by the same consumers.
  • Specified mandatory purchases of available eligible classes and buying at equality, consistent with the discrete lower-bound argument.
  • Defined the unrestricted welfare benchmark using the same per-consumer capacity constraints, and used a supremum for policy welfare without assuming attainment.
  • Interpreted epsilon-optimality multiplicatively and took the worst-case ratio over all support sizes and admissible rational inputs.

Results

An exactly optimal ordered mass single-price policy can be computed in time \(m!\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). It can be chosen to have at most \(\tau+m\) blocks. Thus the same algorithm computes an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal policy, with no dependence on \(1/\varepsilon\).

Moreover, mass splitting does not remove the stated obstruction:

\(R(m)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}\right).\)

Both claims are proved below.

1. Exact optimization by supply-exhaustion orders

1.1. Prices and blocks can be normalized

Because every value is an integer in \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\), every nonnegative price has the same purchasing behavior as some price in \(P=\{1,\ldots,m+1\}\). Specifically, replace a price \(p\) by \(\min\{m+1,\max\{1,\lceil p\rceil\}\}\). Price \(m+1\) induces no purchases.

Next, split each block at every supply-exhaustion event occurring during it. To justify this operation, process a block continuously through its mass: while some accepted class has positive remaining supply, take a subblock whose mass is the smaller of the unprocessed block mass and the smallest positive remaining supply among accepted classes. This subblock either finishes the original block or exhausts at least one class.

Splitting preserves purchases because, for any nonnegative \(a,b,r\),

\(\min\{a,r\}+\min\{b,(r-a)_+\}=\min\{a+b,r\}.\)

All resulting masses remain rational. There are at most \(m\) exhaustion events. In every resulting subblock, each class that is available and accepted at its beginning sells exactly the subblock mass; exhaustion can occur at its end.

1.2. The linear programs

Let \(J=\{j:q_j>0\}\) and \(d=|J|\). Enumerate every ordered subset \(E=(e_1,\ldots,e_h)\) of \(J\), including the empty subset.

For this ordered subset, introduce stages \(s=0,\ldots,h\), with available-class sets

\(S_s=J\setminus\{e_1,\ldots,e_s\}.\)

Thus class \(e_i\) is declared exhausted after stage \(i-1\). Classes outside \(E\) are never removed from these sets.

For every stage \(s\), supported type \(t\), and price \(p\in P\), introduce a variable \(z_{stp}\geq0\), representing the mass of that action in that stage. Write

\(A_{tp}=\{j\in J:v_{tj}\geq p\}\)

and define the intended total purchase of class \(j\) by

\(D_j(z)=\sum_{s:j\in S_s}\ \sum_{t,p:j\in A_{tp}}z_{stp}.\)

The linear program maximizes

\(C_E(z)=\sum_{s,t,p}z_{stp}\sum_{j\in S_s\cap A_{tp}}v_{tj}\)

subject to the type-mass constraints

\(\sum_{s,p}z_{stp}=\mu_t\qquad\text{for every }t,\)

the designated-exhaustion constraints

\(D_{e_i}(z)=q_{e_i}\qquad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,h,\)

and the remaining supply constraints

\(D_j(z)\leq q_j\qquad\text{for }j\in J\setminus E.\)

Equality is allowed in the last group. Consequently, a class may actually exhaust earlier than its formal removal, or exhaust without being removed. The reconstruction argument below handles these cases.

1.3. Every feasible rational LP solution gives a policy

Process stages in order. Within a stage, use any fixed order of the pairs \((t,p)\), and output the block \((t,z_{stp},p)\) whenever \(z_{stp}>0\).

We prove inductively that every block realizes precisely its intended purchases.

Consider a current block of mass \(a\) in stage \(s\).

  • If \(j\in S_s\) and \(j\in A_{tp}\), its intended purchase is \(a\). Previous intended purchases of this class plus \(a\) are at most \(D_j(z)\leq q_j\). By induction, previous actual purchases equal previous intended purchases, so at least \(a\) supply remains.
  • If \(j\notin S_s\), then \(j=e_i\) for some \(i\leq s\). All terms in \(D_j(z)\) occur in stages before \(i\), which have already been processed. The equality \(D_j(z)=q_j\) and the induction hypothesis therefore imply that no supply remains.
  • If the class is not accepted, it sells nothing.

Thus actual purchases equal intended purchases. The type-mass constraints ensure that every type's entire mass is visited exactly once. The policy's welfare is \(C_E(z)\).

In particular, premature exhaustion causes no problem: if previous intended purchases already equal \(q_j\), the supply constraint forces every later intended purchase of that class to be zero.

1.4. Every policy is represented by one of the LPs

Take any admissible policy, normalize its prices, and split its blocks as in Section 1.1.

List its actually exhausted positive-supply classes in exhaustion order. If several exhaust simultaneously, order them arbitrarily and insert empty stages between their removals. This gives an ordered subset \(E\).

Within each stage, aggregate the masses having the same type and price into \(z_{stp}\). Every subblock purchases its full mass from every accepted class available at its beginning. Therefore its purchases are exactly those counted in the LP objective and supply constraints.

The aggregated variables satisfy all type-mass constraints, all designated-exhaustion equalities, and all other supply inequalities. Their LP objective equals the original policy's welfare.

Consequently, writing \(\operatorname{LP}(E)\) for the optimal value of each feasible LP,

\(B_{\mathrm{SP}}=\max_E\operatorname{LP}(E).\)

This also proves attainment. Every feasible LP is bounded, since its type-mass constraints bound all variables. Its coefficients are rational, so it has a rational optimal vertex, which reconstructs an admissible finite policy.

1.5. Running time and policy length

The number of ordered subsets is

\(\sum_{h=0}^{d}\frac{d!}{(d-h)!}\leq e\,d!\leq e\,m!.\)

Each LP has at most \((m+1)^2\tau\) variables and \(\tau+m\) type/supply constraints, besides nonnegativity. Its coefficients are integers of polynomial bit length, and its right-hand sides are input rationals. Exact rational linear programming therefore gives total running time

\(m!\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)=2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L).\)

An optimal vertex can be obtained in polynomial time, for example by successively minimizing coordinates on the optimal face.

Such a vertex has at most \(\tau+d\) positive variables. Indeed, if more coordinates were positive, their columns in the matrix of all type/supply constraints would be linearly dependent. A sufficiently small perturbation in both directions along this dependence would preserve nonnegativity and every constraint value, contradicting extremality.

Hence an optimal policy has at most \(\tau+m\) blocks, with rational masses of polynomial bit length. The algorithm uses only the listed supported types.

2. A lower bound that survives arbitrary mass splitting

The construction uses the bucket structure from Theorem 3.1 of [Braverman, Mao, and Weinberg](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.02831). The argument below establishes its lower bound directly for arbitrary mass-split policies.

2.1. Instance construction

Fix an integer \(k\geq2\). There are \(k\) supported types, each with mass \(\mu_t=1/k\).

There are \(k\) buckets, indexed by \(i=0,\ldots,k-1\). Bucket \(i\) contains \(k^{k-i}\) classes. Partition these equally among the types, giving each class a unique designated owner. Thus each type owns \(k^{k-i-1}\) classes in bucket \(i\).

Every class has supply \(q=1/k\). Its owner values it at \(k^{i+1}\), and every other type values it at \(k^i\).

The total number of classes is

\(M_k=\sum_{r=1}^{k}k^r.\)

All values belong to \(\{1,\ldots,M_k\}\), since their maximum is \(k^k\leq M_k\).

Allocating every class's entire supply to its owner is feasible: that supply equals the owner's mass. It is optimal because the owner has the largest value. Therefore

\(\mathrm{OPT}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}k^{k-i}\frac1k\,k^{i+1}=k^{k+1}.\)

2.2. Bounding total owner purchases

Fix an arbitrary policy, with blocks indexed by \(\ell\).

All classes in a bucket with the same owner have identical valuation vectors and initial supplies. They consequently have identical remaining supplies throughout the policy. Let \(z_{ti}\) denote the owner's total purchase of any one class owned by type \(t\) in bucket \(i\).

Split these owner purchases into two categories:

  • high-price purchases, at \(k^i<p_\ell\leq k^{i+1}\);
  • low-price purchases, at \(p_\ell\leq k^i\).

Prices above \(k^{i+1}\) induce no owner purchase.

For high-price purchases, every block contributes at most its mass to \(z_{ti}\). The intervals \((k^i,k^{i+1}]\) are disjoint, so each block contributes to the high-price bound for at most one bucket. Since total visited mass is one,

\(\sum_{t,i}z_{ti}^{\mathrm{high}}\leq1.\)

For low-price purchases, fix bucket \(i\), and let

\(D_i(\ell)=\sum_{u<\ell:p_u\leq k^i}a_u.\)

Before block \(\ell\), every class in bucket \(i\) has remaining supply at most

\((q-D_i(\ell))_+.\)

Here is the induction establishing this assertion. Initially it holds with \(D_i=0\). A block priced above \(k^i\) cannot increase supply and leaves \(D_i\) unchanged. A block of mass \(a\) priced at most \(k^i\) is accepted by every type for every class in the bucket. It changes remaining supply \(r\) into \((r-a)_+\), preserving the claimed bound after increasing \(D_i\) by \(a\).

Thus a low-price block contributes at most

\(\min\{a_\ell,(q-D_i(\ell))_+\}\)

to the owner purchase associated with its type. Summing over the low-price blocks gives at most \(q\): these upper bounds are precisely successive deductions from a capacity initially equal to \(q\). Consequently,

\(\sum_tz_{ti}^{\mathrm{low}}\leq q=\frac1k.\)

Summing over the \(k\) buckets yields

\(\sum_{t,i}z_{ti}^{\mathrm{low}}\leq1.\)

Combining the two categories proves the central bound

\(\sum_{t,i}z_{ti}\leq2.\)

It holds for every finite policy, regardless of how often a type splits or how prices are ordered.

2.3. Welfare bound

For a class in bucket \(i\), its welfare is at most its full supply valued at the background value \(k^i\), plus the owner's premium \(k^{i+1}-k^i\) times the owner's purchase.

There are \(k^{k-i-1}\) classes owned by each type in that bucket. Therefore

\(W\leq \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}k^{k-i}\frac1k\,k^i+\sum_{t,i}k^{k-i-1}(k^{i+1}-k^i)z_{ti}.\)

Simplifying and applying the owner-purchase bound gives

\(W\leq k^k+(k-1)k^{k-1}\sum_{t,i}z_{ti}\leq(3-2/k)k^k.\)

This holds for every policy, so it also holds for their supremum. Hence

\(\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{B_{\mathrm{SP}}}\geq\frac{k}{3-2/k}\geq\frac{k}{3}.\)

2.4. Extending to every sufficiently large number of classes

For a given sufficiently large \(m\), choose the largest integer \(k\) satisfying \(M_k\leq m\). Add \(m-M_k\) classes with zero supply and value one for every type. These classes change neither optimum and preserve all admissibility conditions.

Since \(M_k\geq k^k\) and \(M_{k+1}\leq2(k+1)^{k+1}\),

\(k\log k\leq\log m<\log2+(k+1)\log(k+1)\leq5k\log k.\)

For \(k\geq3\), the first inequality also gives \(\log\log m\geq\log k\). Thus

\(\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}\leq5k.\)

Combining this with the preceding ratio bound proves, for all sufficiently large \(m\),

\(R(m)\geq\frac1{15}\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}.\)

Therefore arbitrary mass splitting cannot permit a uniform \(o(\log m/\log\log m)\) approximation ratio.
