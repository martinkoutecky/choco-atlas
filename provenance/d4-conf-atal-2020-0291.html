The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over applicant types, type preferences, project priorities over types, project capacities, supervisor budgets, and the supervisor-project incidence structure, decide whether there are assignment masses \(x_{t,r}\) for \(r\in P\cup\{\bot\}\) and a feasible funding allocation such that no type \(t\) with positive mass at a source \(r\) has a preferred blocking project \(p\) for which all types assigned to \(p\) have at least as high a priority as \(t\), and some positive mass \(\varepsilon\leq x_{t,r}\) can move from \(r\) to \(p\) while preserving feasibility after funding is reallocated.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P\) be \(m\) projects and \(S\) supervisors, with incidence sets \(P_s\subseteq P\) covering \(P\). Capacities \(c_p\) and budgets \(q_s\) are nonnegative binary rationals; funding is divisible and costs one unit per unit of assigned mass.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; total mass is finite and need not equal \(1\). Each type strictly ranks its acceptable projects above being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\); each project strictly ranks its acceptable types, with equal priority within a type.
An assignment has real masses \(x_{t,r}\geq0\) for \(r\in P\cup\{\bot\}\), with \(\sum_r x_{t,r}=\mu_t\), and assigns mass only to mutually acceptable projects. Write \(z_p=\sum_t x_{t,p}\). Feasibility requires \(z_p\leq c_p\) and real funding \(y_{s,p}\geq0\) on incidences satisfying \(\sum_{s:p\in P_s}y_{s,p}=z_p\) and \(\sum_{p\in P_s}y_{s,p}\leq q_s\).
Call an assignment continuously strongly stable if, whenever \(x_{t,r}>0\) and a mutually acceptable project \(p\succ_t r\), both conditions hold: every \(u\) with \(x_{u,p}>0\) has priority at least as high as \(t\); and there is no real \(0<\varepsilon\leq x_{t,r}\) whose transfer from \(x_{t,r}\) to \(x_{t,p}\), keeping every other assignment entry fixed, remains feasible after funding is reallocated. Thus a preferred project holding lower-priority mass violates stability independently of transfer feasibility.

Is deciding whether a feasible continuously strongly stable assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|S|,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?

For indivisible applicants, strongly stable existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even when every supervisor has budget \(1\) and supervises at most two projects ([discrete result](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p97.pdf)). The extended treatment confirms this hardness and characterizes strong stability as fairness plus strong non-wastefulness ([extended result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-022-01917-1)). No result settling the mass problem defined here was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the omitted fairness condition: a type preferring a project containing lower-priority mass violates stability, independently of whether an undisplacing transfer is feasible.
  • Interpreted masses as population quantities with arbitrary finite positive total, preserving the discrete model's unit funding cost without imposing probability normalization.
  • Allowed nonnegative binary-rational capacities and budgets measured in the same units as mass; assignment masses, transfer amounts, and funding are real and divisible.
  • Used strict preferences over acceptable projects, with unmatched below every acceptable project, and required mutual acceptability for assignments and potential deviations.
  • Used strict project priorities between distinct acceptable types and equal priority within a type, interpreting 'at least as high' reflexively.
  • Required every project to have at least one supervisor, consistent with the intended internship model.
  • Specified that a deviation releases funding at its source, changes only the two indicated assignment entries, and permits all supervisor funding to be reallocated.

Result

The decision problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete, even when every type has mass $1$, every project has capacity $1$, every supervisor has budget $1$, and each project has exactly one supervisor, who supervises at most two projects.

The gap identified in the verifier’s report can be repaired: fairness permits mass to remain at the contested project, but its capacity then forces positive first-choice mass at the next project. Propagating this implication around the gadget gives a budget contradiction.

The proof below establishes both hardness and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ for the stated model with real assignments and funding.

1. Source problem and construction

We use the following known $\mathrm{NP}$-complete problem: deciding whether a stable-marriage instance has a perfect weakly stable matching when one side has strict preferences and each participant on the other side either has strict preferences or accepts exactly two partners and ties them. Weak stability means that no acceptable pair strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners.

This restricted hardness is established in [McDermid’s thesis, Appendix A, Theorem A.0.1](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/2371/1/2010mcdermidphd.pdf). Its hardness construction has equally many participants on the two sides. Interchanging the sides if necessary, write them as
$$ U=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\},\qquad W,\qquad |W|=n, $$
where men have strict preferences, and women have either strict preferences or a single two-person tie. The additional bound of three on list lengths in the source theorem is compatible with everything below.

We use the construction supplied in the notes, adapted from [Aziz, Baychkov, and Biró, Theorem 4.4](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2020/pdfs/p97.pdf). Its correctness for divisible mass is proved independently below.

Every constructed type has mass $1$, every project has capacity $1$, and every supervisor has budget $1$. Each supervisor covers exactly the projects specified for it. Omitted pairs are unacceptable, and every acceptable project is preferred to $\bot$.

External projects

For each man $u_i$, create an external project $E_i$ with its own dedicated supervisor.

For each strictly ranking woman $w$, create a type $a_w$. Its preferences are obtained by replacing every man $u_i$ in her list by $E_i$.

A gadget for each tied woman

Suppose $w$ ties exactly $u_{i_1}$ and $u_{i_2}$. Create four types and four internal projects with the following lists:

| Type | Preferences |
|---|---|
| $a_1^w$ | $H_2^w\succ H_1^w\succ E_{i_1}$ |
| $a_2^w$ | $H_3^w\succ H_2^w$ |
| $a_3^w$ | $H_4^w\succ H_3^w\succ E_{i_2}$ |
| $a_4^w$ | $H_1^w\succ H_4^w$ |

| Project | Priorities |
|---|---|
| $H_1^w$ | $a_1^w\succ a_4^w$ |
| $H_2^w$ | $a_2^w\succ a_1^w$ |
| $H_3^w$ | $a_3^w\succ a_2^w$ |
| $H_4^w$ | $a_4^w\succ a_3^w$ |

One supervisor covers $\{H_1^w,H_3^w\}$; two further supervisors cover $H_2^w$ and $H_4^w$ separately.

For every acceptable source pair $(w,u_i)$, define its representative type $e(w,i)$ as follows:

  • For a strict woman, $e(w,i)=a_w$.
  • For a tied woman, $e(w,i_1)=a_1^w$ and $e(w,i_2)=a_3^w$.

Project $E_i$ ranks these representatives in the strict order in which $u_i$ ranks their corresponding women.

A detector

Create types $v,b,c$ and projects $D_1,D_2$. A single supervisor covers $\{D_1,D_2\}$.

| Type | Preferences |
|---|---|
| $v$ | $E_1\succ E_2\succ\cdots\succ E_n\succ D_1$ |
| $b$ | $D_2\succ D_1$ |
| $c$ | $D_1\succ D_2$ |

| Project | Priorities |
|---|---|
| $D_1$ | $v\succ b\succ c$ |
| $D_2$ | $c\succ b$ |

Append $v$ last to every external project’s priority list.

If there are $k$ strict women and $h$ tied women, then $k+h=n$. The construction has
$$ n+4h+2 $$
projects,
$$ k+4h+3 $$
types, and
$$ n+3h+1 $$
supervisors. It therefore has polynomial size.

Because every project has exactly one supervisor, its funding is necessarily its load. Consequently, funding feasibility in this construction is precisely the requirement that each supervisor’s covered projects have total load at most $1$, together with the individual project capacities.

2. The tied-woman gadget: repaired proof

Lemma 1. In every continuously strongly stable assignment, each tied-woman gadget satisfies
$$ x_{a_2^w,H_2^w}=x_{a_4^w,H_4^w}=1, \qquad x_{a_1^w,H_1^w}+x_{a_3^w,H_3^w}=1. $$
Moreover, all four first-choice entries are zero. Hence the gadget assigns at most one unit externally.

Proof. Suppress the superscript $w$ and read indices cyclically modulo $4$. Define
$$ f_i=x_{a_i,H_{i+1}}, \qquad h_i=x_{a_i,H_i}. $$

Suppose $f_i>0$. Project $H_{i+1}$ ranks $a_{i+1}$ above $a_i$. The fairness condition therefore prohibits $a_{i+1}$ from having positive mass at any destination strictly worse than $H_{i+1}$.

For $a_{i+1}$, the only acceptable project better than $H_{i+1}$ is $H_{i+2}$. Mass at $H_{i+1}$ itself remains permitted. Thus the justified conclusion is
$$ h_{i+1}+f_{i+1}=1. $$
Capacity at $H_{i+1}$ gives
$$ h_{i+1}+f_i\leq1. $$
Subtracting yields
$$ f_{i+1}\geq f_i>0. $$

This implication propagates around the cycle. All four first-choice entries are positive, and the same fairness argument confines every type’s entire unit to its two internal projects. Hence the total internal load is $4$.

On the other hand, feasibility gives
$$ z_{H_1}+z_{H_3}\leq1,\qquad z_{H_2}\leq1,\qquad z_{H_4}\leq1. $$
The total internal load is therefore at most $3$, a contradiction. Consequently,
$$ f_1=f_2=f_3=f_4=0. $$

Type $a_2$ can now place mass only at $H_2$ or $\bot$. If $h_2<1$, it has positive unmatched mass, and $H_2$ has both capacity slack and dedicated-budget slack. A sufficiently small transfer from $\bot$ to $H_2$ is feasible, contradicting stability. Therefore $h_2=1$. The identical argument for $a_4$ gives $h_4=1$.

Write
$$ \alpha=h_1,\qquad \gamma=h_3. $$
Their shared budget gives $\alpha+\gamma\leq1$. Suppose this inequality is strict. Since $\alpha<1$ and $f_1=0$, type $a_1$ has positive mass at some destination
$$ r\in\{E_{i_1},\bot\}, $$
both of which are worse than $H_1$.

Project $H_1$ has capacity slack $1-\alpha$, and its supervisor has budget slack $1-\alpha-\gamma$. Choose
$$ 0<\varepsilon< \min\{x_{a_1,r},\,1-\alpha,\,1-\alpha-\gamma\}. $$
Transferring this amount from $r$ to $H_1$ respects those constraints. If $r$ is external, its load and funding decrease; if $r=\bot$, no other project load increases. The transfer is therefore feasible, contradicting stability.

Thus $\alpha+\gamma=1$. Exactly three units are assigned internally, out of the gadget’s four units. Its external mass is consequently at most one. $\square$

3. The detector forces external fullness

Lemma 2. Every continuously strongly stable assignment satisfies
$$ x_{v,D_1}=1,\qquad x_{b,\bot}=x_{c,\bot}=1, \qquad z_{E_i}=1\quad\text{for every }i. $$

Proof. Suppose $x_{b,D_2}>0$. Since $D_2$ ranks $c$ above $b$, fairness prohibits unmatched mass of $c$. Its entire unit must therefore lie in $\{D_1,D_2\}$. Together with the positive $b$-mass at $D_2$, this exceeds the detector’s shared budget $1$. Hence
$$ x_{b,D_2}=0. $$

Likewise, if $x_{c,D_1}>0$, fairness prohibits unmatched mass of $b$, because $D_1$ ranks $b$ above $c$. The entire unit of $b$ would then lie in the detector region, again contradicting its budget. Thus
$$ x_{c,D_1}=0. $$

Notice that these arguments confine the higher-priority type to the shared region; they do not assert that it occupies only one particular project.

Suppose next that $x_{b,D_1}>0$. Feasibility implies
$$ z_{D_2}\leq1-z_{D_1}<1. $$
A sufficiently small transfer of $b$-mass from $D_1$ to its preferred $D_2$ respects both capacities and preserves total detector expenditure. It is feasible, contradicting stability. Therefore $x_{b,D_1}=0$.

The same argument, transferring $c$-mass from $D_2$ to its preferred $D_1$, shows $x_{c,D_2}=0$. Both $b$ and $c$ are entirely unmatched.

The detector’s only possible assigned mass is now $v$-mass at $D_1$. If $x_{v,D_1}<1$, a small transfer of unmatched $b$-mass to $D_1$ respects both its capacity and the shared budget. Therefore stability forces
$$ x_{v,D_1}=1. $$
Since $v$ has unit mass, it has no external mass.

Finally, $v$ prefers every $E_i$ to $D_1$. If $z_{E_i}<1$, a small transfer from $D_1$ to $E_i$ is feasible: the external project has capacity and dedicated-budget slack, while detector expenditure decreases. Thus every $E_i$ is full. $\square$

4. Extracting a perfect weakly stable matching

Suppose a continuously strongly stable assignment exists. Define a matrix indexed by source women and men:
$$ F_{w,i}=x_{e(w,i),E_i} $$
for acceptable pairs, and $F_{w,i}=0$ otherwise.

For a strict woman, the row sum is at most $1$ because her representative has unit mass. For a tied woman, Lemma 1 gives the same bound. Lemma 2 shows that every external project is full and receives no $v$-mass. Therefore
$$ \sum_wF_{w,i}=1 \quad\text{for every }i. $$

There are $n$ rows, each with sum at most $1$, and the sum of all entries is $n$. Thus every row also has sum $1$.

Consider the bipartite graph consisting of the pairs with $F_{w,i}>0$. For any subset $B$ of women, let $N(B)$ be its neighboring men. Then
$$ |B| =\sum_{w\in B}\sum_iF_{w,i} \leq \sum_{i\in N(B)}\sum_wF_{w,i} =|N(B)|. $$
Hall’s theorem applies to this finite bipartite graph and supplies a perfect matching $M$ using only positive entries.

We prove that $M$ is weakly stable. A tied woman is matched to one of her two equally ranked acceptable men, so she cannot strictly prefer another acceptable partner.

Suppose instead that a strict woman $w$ and man $u_i$ block $M$. Write
$$ M(w)=u_j,\qquad M(u_i)=w'. $$
Since $M$ uses positive entries,
$$ x_{a_w,E_j}>0,\qquad x_{e(w',i),E_i}>0. $$
Woman $w$’s strict preference gives
$$ E_i\succ_{a_w}E_j. $$
Man $u_i$’s strict preference means that $E_i$ ranks $a_w=e(w,i)$ above $e(w',i)$.

Thus $a_w$ has positive mass at a destination worse than $E_i$, while $E_i$ holds positive mass of a lower-priority type. This violates fairness, independently of transfer feasibility. No blocking pair exists.

A continuous solution therefore yields a perfect weakly stable source matching.

5. Constructing a continuous solution from a source matching

Conversely, let $M$ be a perfect weakly stable source matching.

For each strict woman $w$, assign the entire unit of $a_w$ to $E_i$, where $M(w)=u_i$.

For each tied woman $w$, use the following assignment:

| Source partner | Assignments |
|---|---|
| $u_{i_1}$ | $a_1^w$ to $E_{i_1}$; $a_2^w,a_3^w,a_4^w$ to $H_2^w,H_3^w,H_4^w$, respectively |
| $u_{i_2}$ | $a_3^w$ to $E_{i_2}$; $a_1^w,a_2^w,a_4^w$ to $H_1^w,H_2^w,H_4^w$, respectively |

Assign $v$ entirely to $D_1$, and leave $b,c$ entirely unmatched.

Every external project receives one unit. In each tied-woman gadget, $H_2^w$ and $H_4^w$ receive one unit each, and exactly one of $H_1^w,H_3^w$ receives one unit. The detector spends one unit at $D_1$. Thus the assignment is feasible.

We verify both stability conditions for every possible preferred destination.

External destinations

Suppose a strict-woman type $a_w$ prefers $E_i$ to its assigned project. Let $w'=M(u_i)$. Weak stability of $M$ implies that $u_i$ does not prefer $w$ to $w'$. His preferences are strict, so he prefers $w'$ to $w$. Consequently, the occupant of $E_i$ outranks $a_w$.

The type $v$ is ranked below every external occupant. A tied-woman representative has only one acceptable external project and is either assigned there or assigned to an internal project it prefers.

These observations establish fairness at every relevant external destination. Every external project is full, which blocks any positive transfer into it.

Internal destinations

Every occupied internal project holds its highest-priority acceptable type, so fairness holds.

A preferred target $H_2^w$ or $H_4^w$ is full. A preferred target in $\{H_1^w,H_3^w\}$ belongs to a region whose budget is exhausted.

Each type accepts at most one project in $\{H_1^w,H_3^w\}$. Consequently, any contemplated transfer into that pair originates outside it and increases expenditure in the exhausted region. Such a transfer is infeasible.

Detector destinations

Project $D_1$ holds $v$, which outranks both $b$ and $c$, and $D_2$ is empty. Thus fairness holds for the unmatched types. Any transfer of their unmatched mass into either detector project exceeds the exhausted shared budget.

These cases exhaust all preferred destinations. The assignment is continuously strongly stable.

Together with Section 4, this proves the reduction equivalence and hence $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness under the stated unit restrictions.

6. Membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ for arbitrary inputs

Because assignments may be real, membership requires a polynomial-size rational certificate. We establish one without assuming integrality.

6.1 Funding feasibility

For $A\subseteq P$, define
$$ N(A)=\{s\in S:P_s\cap A\neq\varnothing\}, \qquad Q(N(A))=\sum_{s\in N(A)}q_s, \qquad z(A)=\sum_{p\in A}z_p. $$

Lemma 3. A nonnegative load vector $z$ admits feasible funding exactly when
$$ z(A)\leq Q(N(A)) \quad\text{for every }A\subseteq P. $$

Proof. Necessity follows by summing the funding of projects in $A$: their funding comes only from supervisors in $N(A)$.

For sufficiency, set $Z=\sum_pz_p$ and construct a network with:

  • source-to-project arcs of capacities $z_p$;
  • project-to-incident-supervisor arcs of capacity $Z+1$;
  • supervisor-to-sink arcs of capacities $q_s$.

There is a cut of capacity $Z$, so a minimum cut cannot cross an incidence arc of capacity $Z+1$. If its source-side project set is $A$, all supervisors in $N(A)$ must also be on the source side. Their nonnegative budgets imply that its capacity is at least
$$ z(P\setminus A)+Q(N(A)). $$
Conversely, putting exactly $A$ and $N(A)$ on the source side attains this expression. Therefore the minimum-cut value is
$$ \min_{A\subseteq P} \bigl(z(P\setminus A)+Q(N(A))\bigr). $$
The assumed inequalities make this value at least $Z$. By the max-flow/min-cut theorem for finite networks with nonnegative real capacities, a flow of value $Z$ exists. It saturates every source-to-project arc and gives the required funding. $\square$

6.2 Exact certificates for blocked transfers

Fix a feasible assignment, an entry $x_{t,r}>0$, and a mutually acceptable project $p\succ_t r$.

Lemma 4. No positive transfer from $x_{t,r}$ to $x_{t,p}$ is feasible exactly when at least one of the following holds:

  • $z_p=c_p$.
  • There is a set $A\subseteq P$ with

$$ p\in A,\qquad r\notin A,\qquad z(A)=Q(N(A)), $$
where $r\notin A$ is automatic for $r=\bot$.

Proof. A tight target capacity blocks any positive increase at $p$. A tight funding inequality with $p\in A$ and $r\notin A$ also blocks every positive transfer, because its left side increases by the transferred amount.

Conversely, suppose neither condition holds. The target capacity has positive slack. By Lemma 3, the funding inequalities whose left sides increase are exactly those sets containing $p$ but excluding $r$, and each has positive slack.

There are finitely many such sets. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than $x_{t,r}$, the target-capacity slack, and every increasing funding inequality’s slack. All other funding inequalities remain unchanged or decrease. Source nonnegativity is preserved, and the source-project capacity, if applicable, cannot be violated by a decrease.

The transferred assignment therefore satisfies all project capacities and all funding inequalities. Lemma 3 supplies funding after reallocation, proving feasibility. $\square$

6.3 Polynomial-size rational witnesses

If $\tau=0$, the zero assignment and zero funding are feasible and stable. Hence assume $\tau>0$ and that a real continuously strongly stable assignment exists.

Let $E$ be its assignment support, including unmatched entries. Since every type has positive mass, $E$ is nonempty.

For every triple $(t,r,p)$ with $(t,r)\in E$ and $p\succ_t r$, choose one blocking condition supplied by Lemma 4. There are at most
$$ \tau(m+1)m $$
such triples. Each selected set $A$ can be encoded by an $m$-bit membership vector.

Consider the linear program with assignment variables $x$, funding variables $y$, and a variable $\delta$. Impose:

  • all original assignment and funding feasibility constraints;
  • $x_{t,r}=0$ outside $E$;
  • $x_{t,r}\geq\delta$ on $E$;
  • every selected blocking equality;
  • $0\leq\delta\leq1$.

Maximize $\delta$.

The original real witness makes this system feasible with positive $\delta$: choose $\delta$ no larger than $1$ and the smallest positive assignment entry.

The feasible region is bounded. Every assignment entry is bounded by its type mass, every funding entry is bounded by its supervisor’s budget, and $\delta\leq1$. It is a nonempty closed polytope, so the optimum is attained at a vertex. Its optimal value is positive.

This linear program has polynomially many variables and constraints, and polynomial-bit rational coefficients. In particular, each selected right-hand side $Q(N(A))$ is a sum of input rationals and has polynomial bit length.

For completeness, let $D$ be its number of variables. Clearing denominators in each constraint produces integer coefficients of at most $B$ bits, where both $D$ and $B$ are polynomially bounded by the input parameters. A vertex is determined by $D$ linearly independent active constraints. Cramer’s rule expresses its coordinates as ratios of integer determinants. Hadamard’s inequality bounds the numerator and denominator bit lengths by
$$ O(DB+D\log D). $$
Thus the optimal vertex has polynomial-size rational coordinates.

At this vertex, the assignment support is exactly $E$, because $\delta>0$. Fairness is preserved: it depends only on which assignment entries are positive and on the input preferences and priorities. All selected blocking equalities remain valid, so Lemma 4 blocks every relevant transfer. The rational assignment is therefore continuously strongly stable.

A certificate consists of this rational assignment, rational funding, and one selected blocking condition for every relevant triple. A verifier checks:

  • feasibility and mutual acceptability;
  • fairness for all relevant triples and positive project occupants;
  • each selected capacity equality or funding-set equality, including the required set membership.

All these checks use polynomially many operations on polynomial-bit rationals. Every accepted certificate is sound by Lemma 4, and every real solution yields such a certificate. Hence the problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

7. Conclusion

The reduction proves $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness even with unit masses, capacities, and budgets and disjoint supervisor regions of size at most two. The rational-certificate argument proves membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ for the full model.

Thus the decision problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete. A running time polynomial in $m,\tau,|S|,L$ would imply $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
