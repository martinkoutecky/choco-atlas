The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita budgets \(b_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t=1\), and normalized piecewise-linear valuations \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\) with common segments \(I_k=[a_{k-1},a_k]\), choose aggregate allocations \(y_{tk}\in L^\infty_+(I_k)\) satisfying \(\sum_t y_{tk}\leq 1\). Let \(q_{tk}=\int_{I_k}v_t(\theta)y_{tk}(\theta)\,d\theta\) and \(q_t=\sum_k q_{tk}\). Compute feasible segment utilities and allocations with \(q_t\geq q_t^*-\varepsilon\) for every type, where \(q^*\) maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log(q_t/\mu_t)\) over these feasible allocations; the per-capita allocation is \(x_{tk}=y_{tk}/\mu_t\).

Conclusion

The computation is possible in deterministic time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), in the binary bit model. The algorithm returns feasible rational step functions and their exact rational segment utilities.

The proof below establishes bit complexity and polynomial output size directly.

1. Absorb the masses into aggregate budgets

Define \(B_t=\mu_tb_t\) and \(\beta=\min_t B_t\). By hypothesis, \(B_t>0\) and \(\sum_tB_t=1\).

The objective is

\(\sum_t\mu_tb_t\log(q_t/\mu_t)=\sum_tB_t\log q_t-\sum_tB_t\log\mu_t.\)

The second term is constant. Thus the target vector maximizes

\(F(q)=\sum_tB_t\log q_t\)

over the aggregate feasible utility set, denoted by \(U\). This gives exactly \(\tau\) aggregate buyers; it requires no expansion of the masses into individual buyers. The products \(B_t\), and hence \(\beta\), have encoding lengths polynomial in \(L\).

Every \(q\in U\) satisfies \(0\leq q_t\leq1\), because \(0\leq y_t\leq1\) almost everywhere and \(\int_0^1v_t=1\). Moreover,

\(\{q\in\mathbb R^\tau:q_t\geq0,\ \sum_tq_t\leq1\}\subseteq U,\)

since constant allocations \(y_t(\theta)=q_t\) realize these utilities.

For completeness, \(U\) is compact and convex. Indeed, the feasible allocations form a weak-star compact subset of the finite product of unit balls of \(L^\infty([0,1])\), and integration against each \(v_t\in L^1([0,1])\) is weak-star continuous. Uniform allocations \(y_t=1/\tau\) give every type positive utility. Consequently, a maximizer has positive coordinates; strict concavity of \(F\) on the positive orthant gives uniqueness.

A lower bound on equilibrium utilities

Let \(e_t\) be the utility vector obtained by assigning everything to type \(t\). Normalization gives \(e_t\in U\).

The feasible direction \(e_t-q^*\) and optimality imply

\(0\geq\nabla F(q^*)\cdot(e_t-q^*)=B_t/q_t^*-\sum_iB_i=B_t/q_t^*-1.\)

Therefore,

\(q_t^*\geq B_t\geq\beta.\) (1)

Objective accuracy controls utility accuracy

On the positive part of \([0,1]^\tau\),

\(-\nabla^2F(q)=\operatorname{diag}(B_t/q_t^2)\succeq\beta I.\)

For any positive feasible \(q\), the second-order concavity inequality and the first-order optimality condition at \(q^*\) therefore give

\(F(q^*)-F(q)\geq \frac{\beta}{2}\|q-q^*\|_2^2.\) (2)

Thus exponentially small budgets require additional precision only through \(\log(1/\beta)\), which is polynomially bounded by the input length.

2. A fine grid, represented implicitly

Set

\(\delta=\beta^2\varepsilon^2/64,\qquad \eta=\beta\varepsilon^2/32,\qquad C=\max_{t,k}|c_{tk}|.\)

Choose a power of two \(N\) satisfying \(N\geq\max\{1,C/\delta\}\). Its binary encoding has length polynomial in \(L+\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

Divide each original segment \(I_k\) into \(N\) equal cells. Let \(P_N\) be the set of total utility vectors realizable by feasible allocations that are constant on these cells. The cells will remain implicit throughout the algorithm.

Approximation of arbitrary feasible allocations

Consider one cell \(J\subseteq I_k\), of length \(h\) and midpoint \(s\). Replace an allocation \(y_t\) on this cell by its average

\(\bar y_t=h^{-1}\int_Jy_t(\theta)\,d\theta.\)

The averaged allocation is feasible, because averaging preserves nonnegativity and the inequality \(\sum_ty_t\leq1\). Since \(\int_J(\bar y_t-y_t)=0\),

\(\int_Jv_t(\theta)(\bar y_t-y_t(\theta))\,d\theta =\int_J(v_t(\theta)-v_t(s))(\bar y_t-y_t(\theta))\,d\theta.\)

Both allocations lie in \([0,1]\), so the absolute value of this expression is at most \(Ch^2/2\).

Writing \(\ell_k=a_k-a_{k-1}\), summing over all cells gives

\(|\bar q_t-q_t|\leq\frac{C}{2N}\sum_k\ell_k^2\leq C/N\leq\delta,\)

where \(\sum_k\ell_k^2\leq(\sum_k\ell_k)^2=1\).

Applying this averaging operation to an allocation attaining \(q^*\) produces some \(\bar q\in P_N\) with

\(\|\bar q-q^*\|_\infty\leq\delta.\) (3)

By (1) and \(\delta\leq\beta/2\), every \(\bar q_t\geq B_t/2\). Along the interval between \(\bar q_t\) and \(q_t^*\), the derivative of the logarithm is at most \(2/\beta\). Hence

\(F(q^*)-F(\bar q)\leq\sum_tB_t\,\frac{2\delta}{\beta} =\frac{2\delta}{\beta}.\) (4)

3. Exact linear optimization over the implicit grid

Although \(P_N\) may arise from exponentially many cells, exact linear optimization over it takes polynomial bit time.

Given a rational objective vector \(z\), put \(h_k=\ell_k/N\). Number the cells in segment \(k\) by \(j\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\). The value of assigning cell \(j\) wholly to type \(t\) is

\(z_t\int_Jv_t(\theta)\,d\theta =z_th_k\bigl(c_{tk}(a_{k-1}+(j+\tfrac12)h_k)+d_{tk}\bigr).\)

This is an affine function of \(j\). Include the constant zero function as the option of leaving the cell unallocated.

Because each cell has a simplex of feasible allocation fractions, a maximizing allocation assigns that cell to a maximizing option. Fix a deterministic ordering to resolve ties.

To find all winning blocks without enumerating the cells:

  • Compute the crossings of the \(\tau+1\) affine functions.
  • Sort their rational crossing locations.
  • Separate any integer crossing locations as singleton indices.
  • On each remaining consecutive block of integer indices, compare the functions at one index.

Between crossings, every pairwise comparison has a constant sign. Identically equal functions are handled by the fixed tie rule. There are \(O((\tau+1)^2)\) blocks per original segment.

A block of consecutive cells is one interval with rational endpoints. Its utility is computed exactly using

\(\int_l^rv_t(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{c_{tk}}2(r^2-l^2)+d_{tk}(r-l).\)

Thus the oracle returns both an exact maximizing utility vector and a pure rational step allocation using \(O(m(\tau+1)^2)\) intervals. All calculations have polynomial bit complexity.

Polynomial vertex encoding length

The feasible cell fractions form a product of simplices. Therefore \(P_N\), their linear image, is the convex hull of utility vectors of pure cell assignments. Every vertex of \(P_N\) is one of these vectors.

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all valuation coefficients and segment endpoints. Then \(\log Q\) is polynomially bounded by \(L\), and every grid endpoint has denominator dividing \(QN\).

The integral formula shows that every cell utility has denominator dividing

\(D=2Q^3N^2.\)

The same holds for every pure assignment’s total utilities. Since these utilities lie in \([0,1]\), every vertex coordinate can be written with numerator at most \(D\) and denominator \(D\). Consequently, all vertices of \(P_N\) have polynomial encoding length.

This is the essential reason the implicit grid remains computationally tractable.

4. The rational-polytope oracle results used

We use the following standard results for rational polytopes, with running time polynomial in dimension, the supplied vertex encoding bound, and the query encoding lengths:

  • Exact linear optimization yields exact strong separation.
  • A rational feasible point can be expressed as a rational convex combination of at most dimension plus one vertices.
  • Given a vertex, one can compute a rational linear objective uniquely exposing that vertex.

The second statement is the algorithmic Carathéodory theorem. The third follows from the smallest-face algorithm: its proof constructs a supporting objective exposing the smallest face containing the supplied point. For a vertex, that face is a singleton. These are the rational-polytope oracle results of Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver; see especially Theorems 6.4.9, 6.5.8, and 6.5.11 in [*Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization*, Chapter 6](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4_7).

Their hypotheses hold here: \(P_N\) is a nonempty rational polytope, its dimension is \(\tau\), its vertex encoding bound is polynomial, and Section 3 supplies exact linear optimization. A vertex encoding bound also supplies a polynomial facet encoding bound, by determinant bounds for hyperplanes through affinely independent vertices.

Define the truncated domain

\(K=P_N\cap\{q:q_t\geq B_t/2\text{ for every }t\}.\)

A strong separation oracle for \(K\) first checks these explicit lower bounds and then uses the oracle for \(P_N\).

5. Optimize the logarithmic objective in polynomial bit time

We show explicitly that one can compute a rational \(q\in K\) satisfying

\(F(q)\geq\max_{u\in K}F(u)-\eta.\) (5)

Bounds and an interior ball

Let \(z=3B/4\) and \(r=\beta/(8\tau)\). The Euclidean ball of radius \(r\) centered at \(z\) lies in \(K\).

Indeed, for any point \(w\) in this ball,

\(w_t\geq3B_t/4-r\geq B_t/2,\)

and

\(\sum_tw_t\leq3/4+\tau r=3/4+\beta/8<1.\)

Such a vector is realized by constant allocations, so it belongs to \(P_N\).

Also, \(K\subseteq[0,1]^\tau\), and an enclosing ball of radius \(\tau\) is available.

Choose a polynomial-size integer \(A\) with \(A\geq\log(2/\beta)\). On \(K\),

\(-A\leq F(q)\leq0.\)

For example, two plus the bit length of the denominator of \(\beta\) suffices.

An ellipsoid optimization argument

Let \(u\) maximize \(F\) on \(K\), and put \(s=\eta/(4A)\). Convexity implies that the ball centered at \((1-s)u+sz\), of radius \(sr\), lies in \(K\). Every point \(w\) in this ball has the form

\(w=(1-s)u+sv,\qquad v\in K.\)

Concavity and the objective bounds imply

\(F(w)\geq(1-s)F(u)+sF(v)\geq F(u)-sA=F(u)-\eta/4.\) (6)

Run the rational central-cut ellipsoid algorithm. At each center \(x\):

  • If \(x\notin K\), use a separating cut.
  • If \(x\in K\), record it and use the central cut

\(\nabla F(x)\cdot(q-x)\geq0\).

The second cut uses exact rational coefficients, since

\(\nabla F(x)_t=B_t/x_t.\)

Suppose every recorded center had objective below \(F(u)-\eta/2\). By (6), every point \(w\) in the ball just constructed would have \(F(w)>F(x)\). Concavity gives

\(F(w)\leq F(x)+\nabla F(x)\cdot(w-x),\)

so every objective cut would retain that entire ball. Every feasibility cut also retains it.

The standard rational ellipsoid implementation preserves the intersections specified by these cuts while reducing volume by a fixed exponential factor depending polynomially on dimension. After \(O(\tau^2\log(\tau/(sr)))\) iterations, the enclosing ellipsoid has volume smaller than the retained ball, a contradiction. Thus some recorded feasible rational center satisfies \(F(x)\geq F(u)-\eta/2\). The iteration count and intermediate encoding lengths are polynomial. These are the usual rational ellipsoid guarantees; see [GLS, *The Ellipsoid Method*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4_4).

Finally, evaluate \(F\) at recorded centers to absolute error at most \(\eta/8\), and select the largest approximate value. Its true objective is within \(\eta/4\) of the best recorded value, establishing (5).

These logarithm evaluations also take polynomial bit time. Range reduction writes a positive rational as \(2^ez\), with \(1\leq z<2\), and the convergent series

\(\log z=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1},\qquad w=(z-1)/(z+1),\)

has \(0\leq w<1/3\). Polynomially many terms suffice for the requested binary precision; the same series computes \(\log2\).

6. Recover explicit allocations and exact segment utilities

The rational point \(q\in K\subseteq P_N\) from Section 5 can be decomposed in polynomial time as

\(q=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_iw^{(i)},\qquad s\leq\tau+1,\qquad \lambda_i\geq0,\qquad\sum_i\lambda_i=1,\)

where the \(w^{(i)}\) are vertices of \(P_N\), and all coefficients are rational.

For each vertex, compute a rational objective uniquely exposing it. Run the allocation oracle from Section 3 with that objective. Its returned pure allocation \(y^{(i)}\) has utility exactly \(w^{(i)}\), because the maximizing utility vector is unique.

Return

\(y_t(\theta)=\sum_i\lambda_i y_t^{(i)}(\theta).\)

This allocation is feasible pointwise almost everywhere:

\(\sum_ty_t(\theta)=\sum_i\lambda_i\sum_ty_t^{(i)}(\theta)\leq\sum_i\lambda_i=1.\)

Combining the breakpoints of the component allocations produces rational step functions with \(O(m(\tau+1)^3)\) intervals. Their endpoint and height encoding lengths are polynomial.

On each step \([l,r]\subseteq I_k\) with height \(h_t\), compute its contribution to the segment utility as

\(h_t\bigl(\frac{c_{tk}}2(r^2-l^2)+d_{tk}(r-l)\bigr).\)

Summing gives the exact rational values \(q_{tk}\). If per-capita allocations are desired, return \(x_{tk}=y_{tk}/\mu_t\); these remain rational step functions of polynomial encoding length.

7. Accuracy guarantee and final complexity

The averaged vector \(\bar q\) from Section 2 belongs to \(K\). Combining (4) and (5),

\(F(q^*)-F(q)\leq F(q^*)-F(\bar q)+\eta \leq2\delta/\beta+\eta =\beta\varepsilon^2/16.\)

Applying (2),

\(\|q-q^*\|_2^2\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\bigl(F(q^*)-F(q)\bigr) \leq\varepsilon^2/8.\)

In particular, every type satisfies the stronger two-sided guarantee

\(|q_t-q_t^*|\leq\varepsilon/\sqrt8<\varepsilon,\)

and therefore \(q_t\geq q_t^*-\varepsilon\).

The algorithm has polynomially many oracle calls, each oracle call takes polynomial bit time, and the explicit output has polynomial size. Since \(\log N\), \(\log(1/\beta)\), and \(\log(1/\eta)\) are polynomial in \(L+\log(1/\varepsilon)\), the required running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\).
