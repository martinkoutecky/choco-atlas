In particular, the problem belongs to $\mathrm P$ whenever:

  • $q_R$ is polynomially bounded in $\tau,L$ and $n_P$ is fixed; or

The denominator of the *other* group may be exponentially large. Thus, for example, homogeneous rich incomes and any fixed number of distinct poor incomes give an unconditional polynomial-time case with arbitrary binary masses.

1. Definitions and geometric facts

Combining types with identical incomes within a group preserves the group mass, both utilities, all feasibility conditions, and the decision answer. It takes polynomial time. Write the resulting incomes and normalized weights as

$$ y_{R,1},\ldots,y_{R,n_R}, \qquad w_{R,1},\ldots,w_{R,n_R}, $$

and similarly for the poor group, with

$$ w_{i,j}>0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{n_i}w_{i,j}=1. $$

Here $n_R+n_P\le\tau$. Write

$$ w_{i,j}=\frac{r_{i,j}}{q_i}, \qquad r_{i,j}\in\mathbb Z_{>0}, \qquad \sum_jr_{i,j}=q_i, $$

using the least common denominator $q_i$.

These integers can be computed in polynomial time. Indeed, choose a common denominator $D$ of the original masses and put $p_t=D\mu_t$. After combining equal incomes, let $p_{i,j}$ be the corresponding sums and let

$$ P_i=\sum_jp_{i,j}, \qquad h_i=\gcd_jp_{i,j}. $$

Then $q_i=P_i/h_i$ and $r_{i,j}=p_{i,j}/h_i$. One may choose $D\le2^L$, so

$$ q_i\le D\le2^L. $$

Below, $q=\max(q_R,q_P)$ denotes the maximum of the two denominators; it need not itself be a common denominator.

Define

$$ A_{\max}=\min_{T_R}y_t-\bar x, \qquad E_{\min}=\bar x-\min_{T_P}y_t. $$

Let $B$ be the feasible set, and let $C$ be its accepted subset.

Nonemptiness and compactness

The policy

$$ (\bar a,E_{\min},\bar x) $$

is feasible by the two status-quo assumptions, and is accepted with equality. Thus $C$ is nonempty.

Every feasible policy satisfies

$$ \frac{\bar x+M_PE_{\min}}{M_R}\le a\le A_{\max}, $$

$$ E_{\min}\le e\le\frac{M_RA_{\max}-\bar x}{M_P}, $$

and

$$ \bar x\le g\le M_RA_{\max}-M_PE_{\min}. $$

For example, the lower bound on $a$ follows from

$$ M_Ra\ge g+M_Pe\ge\bar x+M_PE_{\min}; $$

the other bounds follow by substituting the corresponding upper or lower bounds into the budget inequality. Both group masses are positive, so the divisions are legitimate.

Consequently, $B$ is closed and bounded. All logarithm arguments on $B$ are at least $\bar x>0$, so both utilities are continuous there. Acceptance is a closed condition; hence $C$ is compact.

Convexity, budget binding, and uniqueness

The Hessians in the relevant coordinates are

$$ \nabla^2_{(e,g)}F = \begin{pmatrix} -\displaystyle\sum_j\frac{w_{P,j}}{(y_{P,j}+e)^2}&0\\ 0&-\displaystyle\frac1{g^2} \end{pmatrix}, $$

and

$$ \nabla^2_{(a,g)}U_R = \begin{pmatrix} -\displaystyle\sum_j\frac{w_{R,j}}{(y_{R,j}-a)^2}&0\\ 0&-\displaystyle\frac1{g^2} \end{pmatrix}. $$

Both are negative definite. Thus $F$ is strictly concave in $(e,g)$, and $U_R$ is strictly concave in $(a,g)$. Rich acceptance is a superlevel condition for a concave function, so $C$ is convex.

A maximizer of $F$ over $C$ exists. Its budget binds: otherwise a sufficiently small increase in $e$ would preserve feasibility and acceptance and strictly increase $F$, since

$$ F_e(e,g)=\sum_j\frac{w_{P,j}}{y_{P,j}+e}>0. $$

If two maximizers had different pairs $(e,g)$, their midpoint would have strictly greater utility. If those pairs coincided, their binding budgets would determine the same $a$. Therefore the maximizing policy is unique.

These conclusions include instances with empty interior.

2. The denominator-dependent exact algorithm

$$ \prod_j(y_{P,j}+e)^{r_{P,j}} $$

has total exponent $q_P$. Each coefficient is a sum of products of at most $q_P$ input numbers and binomial coefficients. A common denominator of bit length $O(q_PL)$ clears all coefficients, and their resulting bit lengths are $O(q_PL)$. The other products obey the same bound.

The integer-coefficient existential real-algebraic decision theorem gives a bit complexity polynomial in the coefficient length and $(sd)^{O(m)}$, where $s$ is the number of polynomials and $d$ their degree. Its hypotheses hold here: one existential block, integer coefficients, and polynomial inequalities. Consequently,

$$ T\le(\tau qL)^{O(m)}, \qquad m=3. $$

This is an exact algorithm, including equality cases. The theorem used is [Renegar’s existential decision theorem](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200018947_On_the_computational_complexity_and_geometry_of_the_first-order_theory_of_the_reals_Part_I_Introduction_Preliminaries_The_geometry_of_semi-algebraic_sets_The_decision_problem_for_the_existential_theor).

In particular, arbitrary instances admit a bound

3. An algebraic description of the optimizer using rational derivatives

Let

$$ D_P(e)=\prod_{j=1}^{n_P}(y_{P,j}+e), $$

and

$$ N_P(e)= \sum_{j=1}^{n_P} w_{P,j}\prod_{k\ne j}(y_{P,k}+e). $$

Then

$$ F_e(e,g)=\frac{N_P(e)}{D_P(e)}, \qquad F_g(e,g)=\frac1g. $$

The degrees are $n_P$ and at most $n_P-1$, respectively. Their rational coefficients have bit length polynomial in $\tau,L$; they use the weights as rational coefficients, without raising anything to $q_P$.

For policies $x=(a,e,g)$ and $z=(a',e',g')$, define

$$ H_P(x,z)= gN_P(e)(e'-e)+D_P(e)(g'-g). $$

Since $gD_P(e)>0$ on $C$,

$$ H_P(x,z)\le0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \nabla F(x)\mathbin{\cdot}(z-x)\le0. $$

Optimality lemma

A policy $x$ maximizes $F$ over $C$ if and only if

$$ x\in C \quad\text{and}\quad H_P(x,z)\le0\ \text{for every }z\in C. $$

Proof. If $x$ is a maximizer, then for every $z\in C$, convexity places $x+t(z-x)$ in $C$ for $0\le t\le1$. The right derivative at $t=0$ cannot be positive, giving the required inequality.

Conversely, differentiable concavity gives

$$ F(z)\le F(x)+\nabla F(x)\mathbin{\cdot}(z-x). $$

The stated inequality therefore implies $F(z)\le F(x)$ for every $z\in C$. $\square$

This proof requires no constraint qualification.

Exact construction of algebraic coordinates

Describe $C$ using feasibility and the expanded rich-acceptance polynomial from Section 2. The formula

$$ \operatorname{Opt}_P(x): \quad C(x)\ \land\ \forall z\bigl(C(z)\Longrightarrow H_P(x,z)\le0\bigr) $$

defines exactly the unique maximizing policy.

After clearing rational denominators, this formula contains:

  • at most $O(\tau)$ polynomial predicates;
  • degrees at most $\max(2q_R,n_P+1)$;
  • coefficient bit lengths polynomial in $\tau,q_R,L$;
  • six real variables, three free and three quantified.

For clarity, the real-algebraic algorithmic fact used here is: for a fixed total number of variables, quantifier elimination has polynomial bit complexity in the number and degrees of the input polynomials and their coefficient bit lengths; the output degrees, coefficient lengths, and number of predicates obey polynomial bounds. This is the fixed-variable consequence of [Renegar’s quantifier-elimination theorem](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200018947_On_the_computational_complexity_and_geometry_of_the_first-order_theory_of_the_reals_Part_I_Introduction_Preliminaries_The_geometry_of_semi-algebraic_sets_The_decision_problem_for_the_existential_theor).

Here is how to extract the optimizer from it without assuming that its coordinates are rational. For each coordinate, existentially quantify the other two coordinates in $\operatorname{Opt}_P$. Quantifier elimination gives a univariate Boolean combination whose solution set is the singleton consisting of that coordinate.

At that singleton, at least one nonzero output polynomial must vanish. Otherwise every polynomial sign would be locally constant, and the Boolean combination would hold on an interval, contradicting the singleton property.

Isolate the real roots of the output polynomials and evaluate the Boolean combination at them. Standard exact univariate root isolation and algebraic sign evaluation take polynomial time in polynomial degree and coefficient bit length. Exactly one root passes.

Thus the unique maximizing policy has an exact algebraic representation constructible in time

$$ (\tau q_RL)^{O(1)} $$

for $m=3$. Its coordinate degrees and defining-polynomial coefficient bit lengths have the same polynomial bound. There is no dependence on the numerical value of $q_P$.

To justify the height bound explicitly, for a nonzero algebraic number $\alpha$ with primitive minimal polynomial having leading coefficient $a_d$ and conjugates $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d$, define

$$ h(\alpha)= \frac{\log|a_d|+\sum_{k=1}^d\log\max(1,|\alpha_k|)}{d}. $$

If $\alpha$ is a root of an integer polynomial with coefficient bit length $H$, Gauss’s lemma bounds its minimal polynomial’s leading coefficient by the original leading coefficient. Cauchy’s root bound bounds all its conjugates by $1+2^H$. These two facts give $h(\alpha)=O(H+1)$.

The logarithmic separation theorem

The form of Matveev’s theorem needed here is the following. Let $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s$ be positive algebraic numbers in a real number field of degree $d$, let $b_j$ be integers, and put

$$ \Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^s b_j\log\alpha_j, \qquad B\ge\max(3,|b_1|,\ldots,|b_s|). $$

For $A_j\ge\max(dh(\alpha_j),|\log\alpha_j|,1)$, there is an explicit constant $c_s\le2^{O(s)}$ such that, if $\Lambda\ne0$,

$$ \log|\Lambda| > -c_s d^2 \left(\prod_{j=1}^sA_j\right) \log(ed)\log(eB). $$

No linear-independence hypothesis is required. This is [Matveev, Corollary 2.3](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/getFT.phtml?jrnid=im&option_lang=eng&paperid=314&what=fullteng).

For $\Lambda_P$, there are at most $s=n_P+2$ terms. Terms with base $1$ may be omitted. All theorem hypotheses have now been checked.

Choose a computable integer $A$, polynomially bounded in $N$, dominating the required height and logarithm bounds. Matveev’s theorem supplies a computable exponent

$$ R\le N^{O(n_P+1)} $$

such that

$$ \Lambda_P\ne0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\Lambda_P|>\exp(-R). $$

Equality is decidable within the same bound

Choose a computable dyadic $\delta>0$ smaller than $\exp(-R)$. Approximate $\Lambda_P$ with absolute error at most $\delta/4$.

If the approximation has absolute value below $\delta/2$, then $\Lambda_P=0$: every nonzero value would produce an approximation of absolute value greater than $3\delta/4$. Otherwise, its sign is the sign of $\Lambda_P$.

Positive real algebraic numbers can be approximated to the required precision in polynomial time in their representation size and the precision. Their logarithms can likewise be evaluated with certified error in polynomial time. One elementary method scales a rational approximation into $[1,2]$ and uses

$$ \log u= 2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1}, \qquad z=\frac{u-1}{u+1}, $$

where $|z|\le1/3$. The tail decays geometrically. An additional $O(L+\log s)$ bits per logarithm compensate for multiplication by the binary integers and summation.

6. Explicit low-degree optimizer when rich incomes are homogeneous

Suppose all rich incomes equal $Y$. Write

$$ R=M_R,\qquad P=M_P,\qquad x=\bar x, $$

and define

$$ C=x(Y-\bar a), \qquad b=Y-\bar a. $$

The assumptions give $b\ge x>0$. Acceptance becomes

$$ (Y-a)g\ge C. $$

For fixed $g$, the largest permitted tax is

$$ a(g)=\min\left(Y-x,\;Y-\frac Cg\right). $$

Increasing $a$ raises the maximal budget-feasible transfer, and poor utility increases with that transfer. Therefore every maximizing policy has

$$ a=a(g), \qquad e=e(g)=\frac{Ra(g)-g}{P}. $$

Conversely, these choices give a feasible accepted policy exactly when

$$ g\ge x, \qquad e(g)\ge E_{\min}. $$

The function $a(g)$ is concave: $C/g$ is convex, its maximum with the constant $x$ is convex, and subtracting that maximum from $Y$ preserves concavity. Hence $e(g)$ is concave. Its feasible superlevel set is an interval, contains $g=x$ by the status-quo budget assumption, and is bounded because $e(g)\to-\infty$.

Let $n=n_P$. On the branch $g\le b$, acceptance determines the tax. Put

$$ P_j(g)=-g^2+(RY+Py_{P,j})g-RC. $$

Then

$$ y_{P,j}+e(g)=\frac{P_j(g)}{Pg}. $$

Every $P_j(g)$ is positive on the feasible interval. Since the weights sum to one,

$$ F(e(g),g) = \sum_{j=1}^n w_{P,j}\log P_j(g)-\log P. $$

Its derivative has numerator

$$ \sum_{j=1}^n w_{P,j}P_j'(g)\prod_{k\ne j}P_k(g), $$

a polynomial of degree $2n-1$. Its leading coefficient is $2(-1)^n$, so it is nonzero.

On the branch $g\ge b$, the rich consumption floor determines the tax. Put

$$ Q_j(g)=R(Y-x)+Py_{P,j}-g. $$

Then

$$ F(e(g),g) = \sum_{j=1}^n w_{P,j}\log Q_j(g)-\log P+\log g. $$

Its derivative has numerator

$$ \prod_{j=1}^nQ_j(g) - g\sum_{j=1}^n w_{P,j}\prod_{k\ne j}Q_k(g), $$

a degree-$n$ polynomial with leading coefficient $2(-1)^n$.

The feasible endpoints are roots of linear or quadratic polynomials: the constraint $e(g)\ge E_{\min}$ is linear on the second branch and, after multiplication by $g>0$, quadratic on the first.

Finally, $F(e(g),g)$ is strictly concave on its feasible interval. Each $y_{P,j}+e(g)$ is positive and concave; composition with the increasing concave logarithm is concave, and $\log g$ is strictly concave. Therefore the maximizer is either an endpoint, the junction $b$, or a derivative root on one branch. One-sided derivative signs identify it exactly, including singleton feasible intervals and a maximum at the junction.

All these polynomials have coefficient bit lengths polynomial in $\tau,L$. Exact root isolation and sign evaluation therefore construct the maximizing policy in polynomial time, with algebraic degree at most

$$ \max(2,2n-1). $$

This explicit calculation confirms that large poor-weight denominators need not make the optimizer’s location difficult. The remaining comparison involves up to $n+2$ logarithms, which is precisely where the support dependence enters Section 4.
