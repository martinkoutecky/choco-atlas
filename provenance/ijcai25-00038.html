The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings \(\pi\in S_d\), candidate groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), parameters \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), and \(k\), find \(L\subseteq[d]\) with \(|L|=k\) and \(\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\le |L\cap G_i|\le\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\) for every \(i\), minimizing \(B_\mu(L)=\sum_{x\in L}\sum_{y\notin L}\Pr_{\pi\sim\mu}[y\prec_\pi x]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\geq1\) be the number of candidates. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct complete strict rankings of \([m]\), each with positive binary-rational mass, with masses summing to \(1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible types.
The input also contains a partition into nonempty groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), binary-rational parameters \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\in[0,1]\), and an integer \(1\leq k\leq m\). Let \(L\) denote the total input bit length.
A feasible selection \(C\subseteq[m]\) satisfies \(|C|=k\) and \(\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\leq|C\cap G_i|\leq\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\) for every group. No quota applies to its complement.
Minimize \(B_\mu(C)=\sum_{x\in C}\sum_{y\notin C}\Pr_{\pi\sim\mu}[y\prec_\pi x]\), where \(y\prec_\pi x\) means that \(y\) precedes \(x\). Any minimizing selection is acceptable; infeasible instances must be reported.

Is deciding whether a feasible selection \(C\) has \(B_\mu(C)\leq q\), for an input binary-rational threshold \(q\geq0\), in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,g,k\).

For finite ranking lists, the corresponding quota-constrained backward-cut problem is covered by an exact weighted-tournament algorithm using \(O(m^2+m\log m)\) arithmetic operations, under probability and triangle constraints ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10006v1)).

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). An exact minimizing selection, or a report of infeasibility, can be computed using

\[ O\!\left(\tau m+m\log(m+1)+g+k\right) \]

arithmetic operations and comparisons. With binary-encoded rationals, a conservative bit-operation bound is

\[ O\!\left(L+\left(\tau m+m\log(m+1)+g+k\right) \left(L+\log(m+1)\right)^2\right). \]

Here \(\tau\) is the supplied support size. The algorithm does not enumerate other rankings or expand rational masses into repeated voters.

1. The objective is linear for fixed selection size

Let \(r_\pi(x)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) be the position of candidate \(x\) in ranking \(\pi\). Define

\[ s(x):=\mathbb E_{\pi\sim\mu}[r_\pi(x)-1]. \]

Because exactly \(r_\pi(x)-1\) candidates precede \(x\),

\[ s(x)=\sum_{y\ne x}\Pr_{\pi\sim\mu}[y\prec_\pi x]. \]

For any selection \(C\) of size \(k\), splitting this sum into candidates inside and outside \(C\) gives

\[ \sum_{x\in C}s(x) = B_\mu(C)+ \sum_{x\in C}\sum_{\substack{y\in C\\y\ne x}} \Pr_{\pi\sim\mu}[y\prec_\pi x]. \]

For each unordered pair \(\{x,y\}\subseteq C\), strict completeness of every ranking implies

\[ \Pr[y\prec_\pi x]+\Pr[x\prec_\pi y]=1. \]

There are \(\binom{k}{2}\) such pairs. Consequently,

\[ \boxed{B_\mu(C)=\sum_{x\in C}s(x)-\binom{k}{2}.} \]

Since \(k\) is fixed, minimizing the backward-cut objective is exactly equivalent to minimizing the sum of the selected scores.

2. Feasibility and the selection algorithm

Write

\[ \ell_i:=\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor, \qquad u_i:=\min\{|G_i|,\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\}. \]

A feasible selection exists exactly when

\[ \ell_i\le u_i\quad\text{for every }i, \qquad \sum_i\ell_i\le k\le\sum_i u_i. \]

Necessity follows by summing the group bounds. The construction below proves sufficiency.

Order candidates by nondecreasing score, breaking ties by candidate identifier. Use the induced order within each group. Let \(M_i\) consist of the first \(\ell_i\) candidates of \(G_i\), and let \(E_i\) consist of its candidates in positions \(\ell_i+1,\ldots,u_i\). Define

\[ M:=\bigcup_i M_i, \qquad E:=\bigcup_i E_i, \qquad r:=k-\sum_i\ell_i. \]

The feasibility inequalities imply

\[ 0\le r\le |E|=\sum_i(u_i-\ell_i). \]

Choose the \(r\) candidates of smallest score in \(E\), calling this set \(T\), and return

\[ C^*:=M\cup T. \]

This selection has size \(k\). In each group it includes all \(\ell_i\) mandatory candidates and at most \(u_i-\ell_i\) additional candidates. Thus it satisfies every quota.

3. Proof of optimality

Let \(C\) be any feasible selection and put \(n_i:=|C\cap G_i|\).

Replace \(C\cap G_i\) by the first \(n_i\) candidates in the ordered list for \(G_i\), for every group. Denote the resulting selection by \(P\). Group counts are unchanged, so \(P\) is feasible. Moreover,

\[ \sum_{x\in P}s(x)\le\sum_{x\in C}s(x), \]

because the first \(n_i\) candidates have minimum total score among all subsets of \(G_i\) of that size.

Since \(\ell_i\le n_i\le u_i\), the selection \(P\) contains \(M\), and

\[ P\setminus M\subseteq E, \qquad |P\setminus M|=r. \]

The set \(T\) minimizes total score among all \(r\)-element subsets of \(E\): after sorting \(E\), the \(j\)-th smallest score of any such subset is at least the \(j\)-th smallest score of \(E\). Therefore,

\[ \sum_{x\in C^*}s(x) = \sum_{x\in M}s(x)+\sum_{x\in T}s(x) \le \sum_{x\in P}s(x) \le \sum_{x\in C}s(x). \]

The identity from Section 1 now gives \(B_\mu(C^*)\le B_\mu(C)\). This proves exact optimality.

4. Exact arithmetic and running time

Write the supplied masses as \(p_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive integers \(a_t,b_t\), for \(t\in[\tau]\). Set

\[ D:=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t, \qquad A_t:=a_t(D/b_t). \]

These are integers, and normalization of the masses gives \(\sum_t A_t=D\). Define integer scores

\[ S_x:=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}A_t\bigl(r_{\pi_t}(x)-1\bigr). \]

Then \(s(x)=S_x/D\), so sorting by \(S_x\) gives precisely the required score order.

Compute the scores by scanning each supplied ranking once, adding \(A_t(j-1)\) to the candidate at position \(j\). This takes \(O(\tau m)\) arithmetic operations. Computing the common denominator and scaled masses takes \(O(\tau)\) additional operations.

Sort all candidates once in \(O(m\log(m+1))\) comparisons. A scan of that order determines each candidate’s position within its group, constructs \(M\), and takes the first \(r\) eligible candidates from \(E\). Quota calculations and this scan take \(O(g+m)\) operations.

Finally, compute

\[ H:=\sum_{x\in C^*}S_x-D\binom{k}{2}. \]

The optimum is exactly \(H/D\). For a threshold \(q=c/d\), with \(c\ge0\) and \(d>0\), answer yes exactly when

\[ dH\le cD. \]

All comparisons are exact.

To justify polynomial bit complexity, the total encoded length of the mass denominators bounds

\[ \log_2 D=\sum_t\log_2 b_t\le L. \]

Also, positivity and normalization imply \(A_t\le D\) and

\[ 0\le S_x\le(m-1)D, \qquad 0\le\sum_{x\in C^*}S_x\le k(m-1)D. \]

Thus scores, objective numerators, quota calculations, and threshold cross-products all use \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) bits. Schoolbook integer multiplication and division take quadratic time in this bit length; addition and comparison take linear time. Applying these bounds to the operation count proves the stated bit-operation bound. Since \(k\le m\), there is no exponential dependence on \(k\).

5. Relation to the cited tournament result

The cited paper’s Theorem 5 gives an exact algorithm for the corresponding backward-cut problem, with quotas imposed only on the selected side. [Chakraborty et al., Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10006v1)

The proof above establishes the binary-rational distribution case directly. It also avoids constructing the full pairwise-weight matrix: expected positions suffice. The objective identity uses only complementary pairwise probabilities; triangle constraints are unnecessary for this selection problem.
