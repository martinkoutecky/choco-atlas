The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite buyer and good type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i=\sum_{j\in J}\beta_j=1\), and explicitly encoded, strictly decreasing rational piecewise-linear utility curves \(u_{ij}(p)\), compute a least-price competitive equilibrium. Return prices \(p_j\), utilities \(U_i=\max(0,\max_{j\in J}u_{ij}(p_j))\), and assignment masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\). Require \(x_{ij}>0\) only when \(u_{ij}(p_j)=U_i\), unmatched buyer mass only when \(U_i=0\), and unmatched good mass only when \(p_j=0\); the returned price vector must be coordinatewise least among all equilibria.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be nonempty finite sets of buyer and good types, with \(m=|J|\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive masses, each a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Buyer masses \(\alpha_i\) and good masses \(\beta_j\) satisfy \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\).
For every pair \((i,j)\), input a continuous, strictly decreasing piecewise-linear function \(u_{ij}:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\), specified by finitely many rational breakpoints and rational affine pieces, including both unbounded tails, all with negative slopes. Utility values may have either sign; the utility of remaining unmatched is \(0\). Let \(K\) be the total number of pieces and \(L\) the total binary input length.
A competitive equilibrium consists of prices \(p_j\ge0\), utilities \(U_i=\max\{0,\max_{j\in J}u_{ij}(p_j)\}\), and assignment masses \(x_{ij}\ge0\), with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\).
Require \(x_{ij}>0\Rightarrow u_{ij}(p_j)=U_i\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}<\alpha_i\Rightarrow U_i=0\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}<\beta_j\Rightarrow p_j=0\). Ties may be split arbitrarily among maximizing goods and, when utility is zero, the unmatched option.
Compute an exact equilibrium whose price vector is coordinatewise least among all equilibrium price vectors.

Is this computation possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite unit-demand buyers and unit-supply goods, an inductive characterization expresses each buyer's least-price equilibrium utility through the greatest-price equilibrium after removing that buyer, and dually characterizes greatest prices by removing a good ([Alaei–Jain–Malekian](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~saeed/archive/competitive_equilibrium_with_general_utilities.pdf)).
For explicitly listed discrete bidders and items with continuous piecewise-linear utilities, a polynomial-time mechanism computes bidder-optimal competitive equilibria ([Dütting–Henzinger–Weber](https://www.ambuehler.ethz.ch/cdstore/www2011/proceedings/p127.pdf)). No result settling the exact problem with binary-encoded rational type masses was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question omitted nonnegative prices; imposed them as required by the discrete equilibrium definition.
  • The utility domain and continuity were unspecified; required continuous functions on the whole real line with finitely encoded rational affine tails and strictly negative slopes, matching the discrete model's domain and invertibility convention.
  • Removed zero-mass types from the input support and defined the combined support size as the number of positive-mass buyer and good types.
  • Made the piecewise-linear encoding explicit, defined the total piece count and input bit length, and interpreted computation as exact.
  • Allowed arbitrary splitting across utility ties, including the zero-utility unmatched option, consistent with arbitrary supporting matchings in the discrete definition.

Partial progress: compressing masses by their comparison signs

The general polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question remains unresolved here. I prove a new exact algorithm with running time
$$ \tau^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(K,L). $$
Thus the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of supported types. Unlike direct replication, this bound has no exponential dependence on the binary lengths of the masses.

The main new result is that the entire equilibrium price set depends on the masses only through the signs of buyer–good subset mass differences. These signs can be represented by bounded integers. I also prove polynomial-time solvability for affine utilities whose slope matrix has rank one, and repair the bounded-witness formulation of the previous $\mathrm{TFNP}$ result.

1. Fixed-price feasibility and dependence on mass signs

Write $n=|I|$, so $\tau=n+m$. For subsets $A\subseteq I$ and $B\subseteq J$, define
$$ \alpha(A)=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_i, \qquad \beta(B)=\sum_{j\in B}\beta_j. $$

Fix any nonnegative price vector $p$. Define
$$ U_i(p)=\max\{0,\max_{j\in J}u_{ij}(p_j)\}, $$
$$ D_i(p)=\{j\in J:u_{ij}(p_j)=U_i(p)\}, $$
and
$$ I_+(p)=\{i:U_i(p)>0\}, \qquad P(p)=\{j:p_j>0\}. $$
For sets of buyers or goods, put
$$ D_p(A)=\bigcup_{i\in A}D_i(p), \qquad N_p(B)=\{i:D_i(p)\cap B\ne\varnothing\}. $$

Proposition 1: two weighted Hall conditions

The price vector $p$ supports an equilibrium if and only if both conditions hold:
$$ \alpha(A)\le \beta(D_p(A)) \quad\text{for every }A\subseteq I_+(p), $$
$$ \beta(B)\le \alpha(N_p(B)) \quad\text{for every }B\subseteq P(p). $$

Proof. Necessity follows directly from an equilibrium assignment. Buyers in $A\subseteq I_+(p)$ must be fully assigned, and can receive only goods in $D_p(A)$. Goods in $B\subseteq P(p)$ must be fully sold, and can be assigned only to buyers in $N_p(B)$.

For sufficiency, consider the circulation network below. Take $M=3$; both total masses are $1$.

| Arc | Lower capacity | Upper capacity |
|---|---:|---:|
| $s\to i$ | $\alpha_i$ if $i\in I_+(p)$, otherwise $0$ | $\alpha_i$ |
| $i\to j$, where $j\in D_i(p)$ | $0$ | $M$ |
| $j\to t$ | $\beta_j$ if $j\in P(p)$, otherwise $0$ | $\beta_j$ |
| $t\to s$ | $0$ | $M$ |

Its feasible circulations are exactly the supporting assignments: flows on buyer–good arcs give $x_{ij}$, and the lower capacities impose the two required full-assignment conditions.

The standard circulation cut criterion is
$$ \ell(\delta^-(S))\le c(\delta^+(S)) \quad\text{for every vertex set }S, $$
where $\ell$ and $c$ denote lower and upper capacities. This criterion follows by subtracting the lower capacities and applying the maximum-flow minimum-cut theorem to the resulting imbalance-routing problem.

We check every kind of cut. Write $A=S\cap I$ and $B=S\cap J$.

  • If neither $s$ nor $t$ belongs to $S$, the incoming lower capacity is $\alpha(A\cap I_+(p))$. If some demand arc leaves $S$, the outgoing upper capacity is at least $M$, so the criterion holds. Otherwise $D_p(A)\subseteq B$, and the first assumed condition gives

$$ \alpha(A\cap I_+(p)) \le \beta(D_p(A\cap I_+(p))) \le \beta(B). $$

  • If both $s$ and $t$ belong to $S$, the incoming lower capacity is $\beta((J\setminus B)\cap P(p))$. Again, an outgoing demand arc makes the criterion automatic. Otherwise

$$ N_p(J\setminus B)\subseteq I\setminus A. $$
The second assumed condition therefore gives
$$ \beta((J\setminus B)\cap P(p)) \le \alpha(N_p((J\setminus B)\cap P(p))) \le \alpha(I\setminus A). $$

  • If $s\notin S$ and $t\in S$, the arc $t\to s$ leaves $S$ and has upper capacity $M$. Every incoming lower-capacity sum is at most $2$.
  • If $s\in S$ and $t\notin S$, the incoming lower-capacity sum is $0$.

All cut inequalities hold, proving sufficiency. $\square$

Corollary 2: invariance under preservation of comparison signs

Consider two positive mass vectors $(\alpha,\beta)$ and $(\widehat\alpha,\widehat\beta)$, on the same supported types and with the same utility functions. Suppose
$$ \operatorname{sign}\bigl(\alpha(A)-\beta(B)\bigr) = \operatorname{sign}\bigl(\widehat\alpha(A)-\widehat\beta(B)\bigr) $$
for every $A\subseteq I$ and $B\subseteq J$.

Then the two markets have exactly the same equilibrium price vectors.

Proof. At any fixed price vector, the demand sets and the positive-utility and positive-price sets depend only on the utilities and prices. Every inequality in Proposition 1 is one of the subset mass comparisons whose sign is preserved. Consequently, that proposition declares the price vector feasible in either both markets or neither market. $\square$

In particular, whenever least equilibrium prices exist, they are identical in these two markets. Assignment masses need not be identical.

2. Bounded integer representatives

The following compression is independent of the utility functions.

Proposition 3: small integers preserving every relevant sign

There exist positive integers $a_i,b_j$ satisfying
$$ \operatorname{sign}\bigl(a(A)-b(B)\bigr) = \operatorname{sign}\bigl(\alpha(A)-\beta(B)\bigr) $$
for every $A\subseteq I$, $B\subseteq J$, and
$$ 1\le a_i,b_j\le \tau^{\tau/2}. $$
Furthermore,
$$ \sum_i a_i=\sum_j b_j=:N, \qquad N\le \tau^{\tau/2+1}. $$
Such integers can be computed in time
$$ 2^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$

Proof. Let
$$ w=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n,\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_m). $$
For each pair $(A,B)$, let $c_{A,B}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{\tau}$ have coefficient $1$ on buyer coordinates in $A$, coefficient $-1$ on good coordinates in $B$, and coefficient $0$ elsewhere. Thus
$$ c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}w=\alpha(A)-\beta(B). $$

Introduce a variable $z\in\mathbb R^\tau$ and the following constraints:

  • $z_r\ge 1$ for every coordinate;
  • $c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}z\ge 1$ when $c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}w>0$;
  • $-c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}z\ge 1$ when $c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}w<0$;
  • $c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}z=0$ when $c_{A,B}^{\mathsf T}w=0$.

Call the resulting polyhedron $\mathcal P$. It is nonempty: sufficiently large positive multiples of $w$ satisfy every constraint. Indeed, there are finitely many nonzero comparisons, and each has a strictly positive absolute value.

Minimize $\sum_r z_r$ over $\mathcal P$. The minimum exists because $z_r\ge 1$, and intersecting $\mathcal P$ with any finite upper bound on $\sum_r z_r$ gives a compact set. Choose a vertex minimizer $z^*$.

There are $\tau$ linearly independent active constraint normals at $z^*$. Otherwise a nonzero direction perpendicular to all active normals would allow sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, contradicting that $z^*$ is a vertex. Hence
$$ Az^*=h $$
for a nonsingular integer matrix $A$ whose entries belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$ and a vector $h$ whose entries belong to $\{0,1\}$.

Put $\Delta=|\det A|$, a positive integer. Cramer’s rule shows that every coordinate of
$$ \widetilde z=\Delta z^* $$
is an integer. Every coordinate is positive because $z_r^*\ge 1$.

Each row of $A$, and each row of a matrix obtained by replacing a column of $A$ by $h$, has Euclidean norm at most $\sqrt{\tau}$. Hadamard’s determinant bound therefore gives
$$ |\det A|\le \tau^{\tau/2}, \qquad |\det A_r|\le \tau^{\tau/2}. $$
By Cramer’s rule,
$$ 1\le \widetilde z_r\le \tau^{\tau/2}. $$
Multiplication by $\Delta>0$ preserves all equalities and strict signs imposed in $\mathcal P$.

Take the buyer coordinates of $\widetilde z$ as $a_i$ and the good coordinates as $b_j$. The original equality $\alpha(I)-\beta(J)=0$ was included among the constraints, so
$$ a(I)=b(J)=N. $$
The asserted bound on $N$ follows by summing at most $\tau$ coordinates.

For computation, enumerate the $2^\tau$ subset pairs and compute their signs exactly. The displayed linear program has $\tau$ variables, $O(2^\tau+\tau)$ constraints, and coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Rational linear programming therefore takes $2^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L)$ time.

A vertex minimizer can be obtained, for example, by successively minimizing each coordinate over the compact optimal face. The resulting unique point is a vertex. Extracting independent active constraints and computing $\Delta z^*$ uses exact rational linear algebra. $\square$

Normalize these integers by $N$:
$$ \widehat\alpha_i=\frac{a_i}{N}, \qquad \widehat\beta_j=\frac{b_j}{N}. $$
Corollary 2 says that this normalized integer-representative market has exactly the original equilibrium price set.

3. An exact algorithm parameterized by supported types

Theorem 4

The requested least-price equilibrium can be computed exactly in time
$$ \tau^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(K,L). $$

Proof. First compute the integers from Proposition 3.

Form an explicitly listed market with $a_i$ identical unit buyers of type $i$ and $b_j$ identical unit goods of type $j$. There are $N$ buyers and $N$ goods. A buyer copy and a good copy use the original utility function for their types.

Use the finite-market algorithm stated in the question: explicitly listed unit-demand buyers and unit-supply goods with continuous rational piecewise-linear utilities admit polynomial-time computation of a bidder-optimal competitive equilibrium. Our utilities satisfy its relevant hypotheses: they are continuous and strictly decreasing, have finitely many rational affine pieces, eventually become negative, and have outside utility and reserve prices equal to zero. See [Dütting–Henzinger–Weber, Theorem 1](https://www.ambuehler.ethz.ch/cdstore/www2011/proceedings/p127.pdf).

We verify carefully that this gives the required type prices.

Equal prices for copies. In any equilibrium of the expanded market, all copies of a good type have equal prices. If two copies had prices $c<d$, then $d>0$, so the expensive copy would be assigned. Its assigned buyer would strictly prefer the cheaper identical copy, contradicting equilibrium.

Equivalence of price sets. Aggregating a clone matching gives an equilibrium assignment for integer type masses $(a,b)$, and dividing assignments by $N$ gives one for $(\widehat\alpha,\widehat\beta)$.

Conversely, suppose a type price vector supports integer masses $(a,b)$. The circulation network from Section 1 has integer capacities after choosing an integer $M>2N$. Feasible circulation integrality provides integer buyer–good flows. Those flows can be implemented by a matching of individual copies. Thus every supporting type price vector is also an expanded-market equilibrium price vector.

Therefore least expanded-market prices, which are constant within each good type, are exactly the least prices of the representative type market. Corollary 2 then identifies them with the least prices of the original market.

Finally, at these prices, solve the original rational-capacity circulation problem to obtain assignment masses for the original $\alpha_i,\beta_j$. Compute the induced utilities directly.

For the running time, Proposition 3 gives
$$ N\le \tau^{\tau/2+1}. $$
The expanded market has at most $N^2K$ utility pieces and binary description length polynomial in $N,K,L$. The explicitly listed-market algorithm is polynomial in that description. Its running time, together with compression and the final flow computation, is therefore
$$ \tau^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(K,L). $$
$\square$

This is a fixed-parameter algorithm in $\tau$: the exponent of the polynomial in $K,L$ is independent of $\tau$.

Why this compression does not give polynomial time

Exponential replication is sometimes necessary if one insists on preserving all subset comparison signs.

Take $d$ buyer types and $d$ good types, with
$$ \alpha_k=\beta_k=\frac{2^{k-1}}{2^d-1}, \qquad 1\le k\le d. $$
For each $k$,
$$ \alpha_k=\beta_k, \qquad \alpha_k>\sum_{\ell<k}\beta_\ell. $$

Any positive integer representative preserving all signs must consequently satisfy
$$ a_k=b_k, \qquad a_k\ge 1+\sum_{\ell<k}a_\ell. $$
Induction gives
$$ a_k\ge 2^{k-1}, \qquad N\ge 2^d-1. $$
Here $\tau=2d$.

Thus universal preservation of the mass comparison signs can require exponentially many copies. This is an obstruction to this particular compression-and-replication approach, not a hardness proof for the equilibrium problem.

4. A polynomial-time case with variable numbers of types

Consider affine utilities
$$ u_{ij}(p)=a_{ij}-c_{ij}p, \qquad c_{ij}>0. $$

Theorem 5: rank-one slope matrices

If the positive slope matrix factors as
$$ c_{ij}=s_i t_j, \qquad s_i,t_j>0, $$
then an exact least-price equilibrium is computable in polynomial time, including with binary-encoded rational masses.

This condition is testable in polynomial time. Fix $i_0,j_0$. It holds exactly when
$$ c_{ij}c_{i_0j_0}=c_{ij_0}c_{i_0j} \quad\text{for every }i,j. $$
When it holds, one may take
$$ s_i=c_{ij_0}, \qquad t_j=\frac{c_{i_0j}}{c_{i_0j_0}}. $$

Proof. Define transformed prices, utilities, and valuations by
$$ q_j=t_jp_j, \qquad W_i=\frac{U_i}{s_i}, \qquad v_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{s_i}. $$
Then
$$ \frac{u_{ij}(p_j)}{s_i}=v_{ij}-q_j. $$
Positive scaling preserves all preferences, ties, and comparisons with outside utility zero. Also $q_j=0$ exactly when $p_j=0$. Thus the original equilibrium conditions are equivalent to those of this weighted quasilinear market.

Consider the primal linear program
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize}\quad& \sum_{i,j}v_{ij}x_{ij}\\ \text{subject to}\quad& \sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i,\qquad \sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j,\qquad x_{ij}\ge0, \end{aligned} $$
and its dual
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad& \sum_i\alpha_iW_i+\sum_j\beta_jq_j\\ \text{subject to}\quad& W_i+q_j\ge v_{ij},\qquad W_i,q_j\ge0. \end{aligned} $$

The primal is nonempty and compact. The dual is feasible, for example with $q_j=0$ and $W_i=\max\{0,\max_jv_{ij}\}$. Strong duality gives a common attained optimum $V$.

For any primal-feasible $x$ and dual-feasible $(W,q)$, their objective gap is
$$ \begin{aligned} &\sum_i\alpha_iW_i+\sum_j\beta_jq_j-\sum_{i,j}v_{ij}x_{ij}\\ &=\sum_iW_i\left(\alpha_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\right) +\sum_jq_j\left(\beta_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\right) +\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}(W_i+q_j-v_{ij}). \end{aligned} $$
All terms are nonnegative. At optimality, they vanish. These are precisely assignment optimality and the buyer and good complementarity conditions.

Moreover, any optimal dual solution satisfies
$$ W_i=\max\{0,\max_j(v_{ij}-q_j)\}. $$
Otherwise $W_i$ could be decreased while preserving feasibility, strictly decreasing the objective because $\alpha_i>0$. Consequently, every optimal primal–dual pair gives an equilibrium. Conversely, an equilibrium makes every displayed gap term vanish and hence is optimal.

It remains to select least prices. If $(W,q)$ and $(W',q')$ are dual optimal, define componentwise
$$ (W^\vee,q^\wedge)=(\max\{W,W'\},\min\{q,q'\}), $$
$$ (W^\wedge,q^\vee)=(\min\{W,W'\},\max\{q,q'\}). $$
Both pairs are feasible. For the first pair, choose the original pair attaining $q_j^\wedge$; its buyer coordinate is at most $W_i^\vee$. For the second pair, choose the original pair attaining $W_i^\wedge$; its good coordinate is at most $q_j^\vee$.

Their objective values sum to $2V$, because componentwise minimum plus maximum equals the sum of the two original coordinates. Each feasible objective is at least $V$, so both pairs are optimal.

Now minimize $\sum_jq_j$ over the dual optimal face. This minimum exists: nonnegative variables and positive masses make the face with weighted objective $V$ bounded. Let its minimizer be $(W^*,q^*)$.

If another optimal dual solution had $q_j<q_j^*$ for some $j$, taking the componentwise price minimum would produce another optimal dual solution with a strictly smaller price sum. This is impossible. Hence $q^*$ is coordinatewise least.

All these are rational linear programs of polynomial size. Recover
$$ p_j^*=\frac{q_j^*}{t_j}, \qquad U_i^*=s_iW_i^*, $$
and take any primal optimal assignment. Positive coordinate scaling preserves leastness. $\square$

The same proof also covers utilities
$$ u_{ij}(p)=s_i\bigl(v_{ij}-f_j(p)\bigr) $$
when a polynomial-size representation of the strictly increasing rational piecewise-linear bijections $f_j$, with $f_j(0)=0$, is available. Set $q_j=f_j(p_j)$ and recover prices by exact inversion.

5. Least-price verification: a self-contained characterization

To repair the witness formulation from the notes, I establish the required leastness criterion here.

Proposition 6

An equilibrium price vector $p$ is coordinatewise least if and only if
$$ \alpha(N_p(T))>\beta(T) \quad\text{for every nonempty }T\subseteq P(p). $$

Proof. At any equilibrium, positive-price goods are fully sold, so
$$ \beta(T)\le\alpha(N_p(T)) \quad\text{for every }T\subseteq P(p). $$

Suppose all the asserted strict inequalities hold, and let $q$ be another equilibrium. If
$$ T=\{j:q_j<p_j\} $$
were nonempty, then $T\subseteq P(p)$. For every $i\in N_p(T)$, some $j\in T$ gives $u_{ij}(p_j)=U_i(p)$, and therefore
$$ U_i(q)\ge u_{ij}(q_j)>U_i(p). $$
For every $k\notin T$,
$$ u_{ik}(q_k)\le u_{ik}(p_k)\le U_i(p)<U_i(q). $$
Thus these buyers have positive utility at $q$ and must receive all their mass from $T$. It follows that
$$ \alpha(N_p(T))\le\beta(T), $$
a contradiction. Hence $p\le q$ coordinatewise.

For the converse, suppose a nonempty $T\subseteq P(p)$ satisfies equality. Set $N=N_p(T)$ and let $x$ support $p$. Equality forces every buyer in $N$ to be fully assigned within $T$. No such buyer is assigned outside $T$, and no buyer outside $N$ is assigned inside $T$.

Let
$$ E=\{(i,j)\in N\times T:j\in D_i(p)\}. $$
For each edge in $E$, let $c_{ij}>0$ be the magnitude of the slope immediately to the left of $p_j$. For sufficiently small $h\ge0$,
$$ u_{ij}(p_j-h)=U_i(p)+c_{ij}h. $$

Consider the transportation program
$$ \max\sum_{(i,j)\in E}y_{ij}\log c_{ij}, $$
with row sums $\alpha_i$, column sums $\beta_j$, and $y_{ij}\ge0$. The restriction of $x$ makes it feasible, and its feasible set is compact. Its dual has constraints
$$ \lambda_i+\mu_j\ge\log c_{ij}. $$
The dual is feasible by choosing sufficiently large $\lambda_i$ and $\mu_j=0$. Strong duality and complementary slackness give optimal solutions with
$$ y_{ij}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i+\mu_j=\log c_{ij}. $$

Set
$$ g_i=e^{\lambda_i}>0,\qquad d_j=e^{-\mu_j}>0. $$
Then
$$ c_{ij}d_j\le g_i, $$
with equality on every positively assigned edge of $y$.

Decrease prices in $T$ to $p_j-\varepsilon d_j$ and keep all other prices unchanged. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small that prices in $T$ remain positive and the relevant left affine pieces remain valid.

Every buyer in $N$ has a positively assigned edge under $y$, because its row mass is positive. Such an edge now gives utility exactly
$$ U_i(p)+\varepsilon g_i. $$
Other previously maximizing edges into $T$ give at most this value; previously maximizing edges outside $T$ stay at $U_i(p)$. All previously nonmaximizing edges remain below this value for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, by continuity and finiteness.

For buyers outside $N$, every good in $T$ initially has a strict utility gap below their induced utility. Finiteness and continuity ensure that these gaps remain strict after a sufficiently small decrease. Their utilities and original assignments therefore remain valid.

Replace the assignment on $N\times T$ by $y$. Every affected buyer remains fully assigned, every affected good remains fully sold, and all assigned edges maximize utility. All other complementarity conditions persist. This gives an equilibrium with every price in $T$ strictly decreased, contradicting leastness. $\square$

The logarithmic program is used only for an existence proof, not as an algorithmic subroutine.

Polynomial-time checking of the strict inequalities

For each $r\in P(p)$, construct a directed network with arcs:

  • $s\to j$ of capacity $\beta_j$ for $j\in P(p)$;
  • $j\to i$ of capacity $2$ whenever $j\in D_i(p)$;
  • $i\to t$ of capacity $\alpha_i$;
  • an additional $s\to r$ arc of capacity $2$.

A cut putting all goods and buyers on the source side has capacity $1$. Thus a minimum cut never crosses a capacity-$2$ arc. Its source-side goods form a set $T$ containing $r$, and its source-side buyers include $N_p(T)$.

For fixed $T$, the cheapest cut includes exactly those buyers, because all buyer masses are positive. Its capacity is
$$ \beta(P(p)\setminus T)+\alpha(N_p(T)). $$
If $C_r$ is the minimum-cut value, then
$$ C_r-\beta(P(p)) = \min_{\substack{T\subseteq P(p)\\r\in T}} \bigl(\alpha(N_p(T))-\beta(T)\bigr). $$
Hence Proposition 6 can be checked with at most $m$ exact rational minimum-cut computations. If $P(p)$ is empty, feasible prices are automatically least.

6. Polynomial-size witnesses and the formal repair

Polynomial encoding length

The existence of least prices follows already from Theorem 4. Their rational encoding length admits a bound polynomial in the original input length.

Write every affine utility piece as
$$ \ell_{ijr}(z)=a_{ijr}-c_{ijr}z. $$
In price space, form the rational hyperplanes comprising:

  • $p_j=0$;
  • every utility breakpoint equation $p_j=b$;
  • every affine-piece zero equation $\ell_{ijr}(p_j)=0$;
  • every same-buyer comparison

$$ \ell_{ijr}(p_j)=\ell_{iks}(p_k), \qquad j\ne k. $$

There are $O(K^2+m)$ hyperplanes, with coefficients of polynomial encoding length.

Let $p^*$ be least equilibrium prices. The normals of hyperplanes through $p^*$ span $\mathbb R^m$. Otherwise choose a nonzero vector perpendicular to all of them. Sufficiently small perturbations in both directions preserve every hyperplane sign.

These perturbations preserve nonnegative prices, the active utility pieces, all maximizing-good sets, and positivity of induced utilities and prices. A coordinate at a breakpoint is fixed by its active breakpoint equation. Therefore an assignment supporting $p^*$ supports both perturbed vectors. Leastness would force the perturbation vector to be both coordinatewise nonnegative and coordinatewise nonpositive, a contradiction.

Thus $p^*$ is the unique solution of $m$ independent rational equations from this family. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule gives polynomial encoding length. Evaluating the utilities preserves a polynomial bound.

For assignments, let $D$ be the product of all input mass denominators. Its binary length is $O(L)$. Scaling the fixed-price circulation by $D$ gives integer capacities, so integrality supplies a supporting assignment satisfying
$$ x_{ij}\in D^{-1}\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$
Each assignment coordinate therefore has polynomial encoding length.

Bounded witness relation

Fix a polynomial $q(L)$ dominating the total encoding bound just proved. Define a valid witness to be an encoded rational triple $(p,U,x)$ that:

  • has total encoding length at most $q(L)$;
  • satisfies all equilibrium conditions;
  • satisfies the leastness test from Section 5.

Such a witness exists for every valid input. Verification is polynomial in the instance and candidate lengths, and the explicit length restriction makes the relation polynomially balanced. This supplies the missing formal condition for membership in $\mathrm{TFNP}$.

Under polynomial-time oracle reductions required to work for every valid bounded witness, NP-hardness would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$. Indeed, one can guess and verify the polynomially many adaptive oracle answers. A transcript ending in acceptance certifies membership; one ending in rejection certifies nonmembership. Totality supplies valid transcripts, and correctness for every valid answer rules out certificates for an incorrect decision.

7. What remains open

The general target is not settled by these arguments.

A polynomial algorithm would therefore need to exploit the compressed market directly, or preserve less information than the universal mass comparison signs. No such algorithm, and no hardness reduction for the full problem, is established here.
