The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(H\), a finite support \(T\) of student ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\) summing to \(1\), find a mass assignment \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=\kappa_h\) that lexicographically maximizes \(q_k=\sum_{t,h:r_t(h)=k}x_{t,h}\). Require weak stability under the induced priorities: no positive mass can be exchanged from \(t\) at \(h'\) and \(u\) at \(h\) to \(t\) at \(h\) and \(u\) at \(h'\) when \(r_t(h)<r_t(h')\) and \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) ranking types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a complete weak order over \(H\), allowing ties; \(r_t(h)\) is the consecutive index of the indifference class containing \(h\), starting at \(1\). All schools are acceptable. School capacities \(\kappa_h\) are positive binary rationals with \(\sum_h\kappa_h=1\).
School \(h\) strictly prioritizes \(t\) over \(u\) exactly when \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\); equal ranks remain tied. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
A mass assignment has \(x_{t,h}\geq0\), \(\sum_hx_{t,h}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_tx_{t,h}=\kappa_h\). It is weakly stable if there are no \(t,u\in T\) and \(h,h'\in H\) with \(x_{t,h'}>0\), \(x_{u,h}>0\), \(r_t(h)<r_t(h')\), and \(r_t(h)<r_u(h)\). Such a block exchanges equal positive masses between these two assignments; no improvement for \(u\) is required.
Let \(z=\max_{t,h}r_t(h)\) and \(q_k(x)=\sum_{t,h:r_t(h)=k}x_{t,h}\). The computational problem is to find a weakly stable assignment lexicographically maximizing \((q_1(x),\ldots,q_z(x))\) among weakly stable assignments.

Is this continuous optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite students with integer capacities totaling the student count, lexicographically optimal weakly stable assignment under these induced priorities is known to be polynomial-time solvable ([discrete result](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03231)).
For fractional marriage markets, maximizing welfare under cardinal stability is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even without ties; this uses a different stability condition and objective ([fractional result](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)). No result settling the exact continuous problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive student masses to sum to one, matching the per-capita capacities.
  • Specified complete weak rankings with ties and consecutive indifference-class ranks, consistent with the discrete examples and full assignment domain.
  • Required a nonempty school set and positive capacities, following the discrete extension to schools with one or more seats.
  • Kept equal induced school priorities tied, without additional tie-breaking.
  • Specified equal-mass exchanges without requiring the displaced type to improve, preserving the discrete blocking-pair condition.
  • Specified optimization among weakly stable assignments and defined the rank-vector length and total binary input length.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, every assignment that lexicographically maximizes the rank profile over all feasible assignments is weakly stable. Thus stability need not be imposed as an optimization constraint.

The proof below handles the continuous feasible set directly and uses no expansion into individual students or seats.

1. Unrestricted lexicographic optimality implies stability

Let \(P\) be the set of all feasible mass assignments. It is nonempty: the assignment \(x_{t,h}=\mu_t\kappa_h\) has the required row and column sums. It is also compact, because it is closed and every coordinate belongs to \([0,1]\).

Consequently, a lexicographic maximum of \((q_1,\ldots,q_z)\) on \(P\) exists: successively maximize each coordinate over the compact set of maximizers of the preceding coordinates.

Lemma. Every such unrestricted lexicographic maximizer is weakly stable.

Proof. Suppose that \(x\) has a block witnessed by \(t,u,h,h'\). Write

\(k=r_t(h),\qquad a=r_t(h'),\qquad b=r_u(h),\qquad s=r_u(h').\)

The blocking conditions give \(a>k\), \(b>k\), \(x_{t,h'}>0\), and \(x_{u,h}>0\). They also imply \(t\ne u\) and \(h\ne h'\).

Choose \(0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{x_{t,h'},x_{u,h}\}\). Form \(x'\) by subtracting \(\varepsilon\) from each of \(x_{t,h'}\) and \(x_{u,h}\), and adding \(\varepsilon\) to each of \(x_{t,h}\) and \(x_{u,h'}\).

All four cells are distinct. Nonnegativity, every row sum, and every column sum are preserved. The new cell \((u,h')\) is permitted because all schools are acceptable. Thus \(x'\in P\).

For each rank \(i\),

\(q_i(x')-q_i(x)=\varepsilon\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{i=k}+\mathbf{1}_{i=s}-\mathbf{1}_{i=a}-\mathbf{1}_{i=b}\bigr).\)

Let \(j=\min\{k,s\}\). Since \(a,b>k\geq j\), no mass is removed at rank \(j\) or at any earlier rank. No mass is added earlier than \(j\), and positive mass is added at rank \(j\). Hence

\(q_i(x')=q_i(x)\ \text{for }i<j,\qquad q_j(x')>q_j(x).\)

This strictly improves the rank profile lexicographically, contradicting optimality. ∎

It follows that an unrestricted lexicographic maximizer solves the stated problem: it is weakly stable and lexicographically dominates every feasible assignment, including every weakly stable one.

2. Integer scaling and transportation vertices

It remains to compute the unrestricted optimum in polynomial bit complexity.

Let \(D\) be the product of all denominators appearing in the masses and capacities. Define

\(a_t=D\mu_t,\qquad b_h=D\kappa_h,\qquad y_{t,h}=Dx_{t,h}.\)

Then \(a_t,b_h\) are positive integers with \(\sum_ta_t=\sum_hb_h=D\). The scaled transportation polytope is

\(\mathcal{P}=\{y\geq0:\ \sum_hy_{t,h}=a_t\text{ for every }t,\ \sum_ty_{t,h}=b_h\text{ for every }h\}.\)

Define \(Q_k(y)=\sum_{t,h:r_t(h)=k}y_{t,h}\). Since \(Q_k(y)=Dq_k(x)\), scaling preserves lexicographic comparisons.

Lemma. Every vertex of \(\mathcal{P}\) is integral.

Proof. For a feasible \(y\), form the bipartite graph whose edges are precisely the cells with \(y_{t,h}>0\). If this graph contains a cycle, alternate positive and negative perturbations around that cycle. For sufficiently small positive perturbation size, both perturbations remain nonnegative and preserve every marginal. Their midpoint is \(y\), so \(y\) is not a vertex.

Thus a vertex has a forest as its positive-support graph. In any component containing an edge, choose a leaf. Its incident edge value equals its current marginal, which is an integer. Remove that edge and leaf, and subtract the edge value from the neighboring marginal. The remaining marginals are still integers. Repeating proves that every positive edge value is integral. ∎

In particular, each vertex profile has integer coordinates satisfying \(0\leq Q_k\leq D\).

3. One polynomial-size linear program suffices

Set \(B=D+1\), and assign the cell \((t,h)\) the integer weight

\(w_{t,h}=B^{\,z-r_t(h)}.\)

Solve the linear program

\(\text{maximize }W(y)=\sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}y_{t,h}=\sum_{k=1}^{z}B^{z-k}Q_k(y)\quad\text{subject to }y\in\mathcal{P}.\)

We now justify why this scalar objective solves the continuous lexicographic problem.

First, it orders vertex profiles exactly lexicographically. Suppose vertices \(v,v'\) first differ at rank \(d\), with \(Q_d(v)>Q_d(v')\). Integrality gives a difference of at least \(1\). Therefore

\(W(v)-W(v')\geq B^{z-d}-D\sum_{k=d+1}^{z}B^{z-k}=1>0,\)

where the equality uses \(B-1=D\). Reversing the comparison proves the converse.

Second, the lexicographic maximum over \(\mathcal{P}\) is attained at a vertex. Indeed, successively maximizing the linear functions \(Q_1,\ldots,Q_z\) produces nested nonempty faces of \(\mathcal{P}\). Every vertex of the final face is a vertex of \(\mathcal{P}\) and has the globally optimal lexicographic profile. Denote that profile by \(p^*\).

The preceding weight comparison shows that the vertices maximizing \(W\) are exactly those with profile \(p^*\). Finally, every point of the bounded polytope \(\mathcal{P}\) is a convex combination of its vertices. If such a combination maximizes \(W\), every vertex with positive coefficient must also maximize \(W\); otherwise its objective value would be strictly below the maximum. All those vertices have profile \(p^*\), so their combination does too.

Thus every optimum of this linear program is an unrestricted lexicographic optimum. By Section 1, dividing it by \(D\) gives the required weakly stable optimum.

4. Bit complexity and conclusion

The binary length of \(D\) is at most the sum of the denominator lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Since consecutive indifference-class indices satisfy \(z\leq m\), each weight has binary length

\(O(z\log(D+1))=O(m(L+1)).\)

The linear program has \(\tau m\) variables and \(\tau+m\) marginal equations. Its entire rational encoding therefore has length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Its feasible set is nonempty and bounded, and all its coefficients are explicitly computable rationals. The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming consequently computes an exact optimum with polynomial output length in polynomial time. Dividing by \(D\) also takes polynomial time.

This extends the rank-weighted assignment approach in the cited [discrete paper, Sections 3.1–3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.03231v1), while addressing the continuous feasible set and binary-encoded masses directly.

Therefore the exact continuous optimization problem in the question is polynomial-time solvable.
