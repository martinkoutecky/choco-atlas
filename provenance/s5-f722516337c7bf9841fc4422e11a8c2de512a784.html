Theorem 3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(m\), let \(r\) be an anonymous voting rule that reduces to majority with two candidates and has a fractional winner extension. Given only independent samples from a society \(\mu\) over complete rankings, promised to have positive total-variation winner margin \(\rho_r(\mu)\), output \(r(\mu)\) with error at most \(\epsilon\). What expected sample count and memory are necessary in the worst case as functions of \(\rho_r(\mu)\) and \(\epsilon\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(m\geq2\). A society is specified by its support: \(\tau\) complete strict rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, and \(L\) is the total encoding length.
Let \(r\) be a fixed, known, anonymous, single-winner rule on nonnegative real histograms of positive total mass, with a specified effective evaluation procedure on rational histograms and fixed deterministic tie-breaking. Its fractional extension agrees with its integer-profile rule, is invariant under positive scaling, satisfies canceling out \(r(h+a\mathbf1)=r(h)\) for \(a\geq0\), and agrees with majority when there are two candidates.
Define \(\rho_r(\mu)=\inf\{\tfrac12\sum_{\pi}|\mu(\pi)-\nu(\pi)|: \nu\text{ is a probability distribution on all complete rankings},\ r(\nu)\neq r(\mu)\}\); competing distributions may introduce new ranking types.
The algorithm receives only independent samples from \(\mu\), together with \(m\), \(r\), and binary-rational \(0<\epsilon<\tfrac12\). The support, masses, \(\tau\), \(L\), and margin are hidden; only \(\rho_r(\mu)>0\) is promised. One uniform classical algorithm must stop almost surely and output \(r(\mu)\) with error at most \(\epsilon\) on every promised society.
Memory means expected peak working bits, including retained samples, counters, and rule evaluation; discarded samples cannot be reread, and no external clock or counter is available. State runtime bounds in \(m,\tau,L,\rho_r(\mu),\epsilon\), allowing dependence on the fixed rule's evaluation costs.

What are the optimal worst-case expected sample count and expected peak memory, including their tradeoffs, as functions of \(\rho_r(\mu)\) and \(\epsilon\) for fixed \(m\), under these assumptions on \(r\)?

Finite-electorate sampling with replacement has published lower-bound claims of \(\Omega((n/\mathrm{MoV})^2(\tfrac12-\epsilon)^2)\) expected runtime and \(\Omega(\log((n/\mathrm{MoV})^2(\tfrac12-\epsilon)^2))\) expected space. [Source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v216/liu23a/liu23a.pdf)

Partial resolution

The assumptions do not imply a common resource characterization for all admissible rules. For every fixed $m\geq3$, an admissible rule can have arbitrarily small positive margins and require no samples; another admissible rule admits no effective predictor at all.

For two candidates, however, the problem is necessarily Bernoulli sign testing. I prove two additional results for that case:

  • An iterated-logarithm expected-sample lower bound directly on dyadic societies, with explicit quantifiers.
  • A matching margin dependence for expected peak memory, for each fixed error tolerance, even when arbitrarily many samples are allowed.

These results substantially narrow the remaining questions. They do not settle all joint dependence on the margin and error tolerance, or all sample–memory tradeoffs.

1. Definitions and the two-candidate reduction

Write
$$ \operatorname{TV}(p,q)=\frac12\sum_i|p_i-q_i|. $$
All logarithms below are natural unless their base is displayed.

For $m=2$, let $p$ be the probability of the ranking placing candidate $1$ first. Majority chooses candidate $1$ when $p>1/2$ and candidate $2$ when $p<1/2$. Fixed tie-breaking determines the winner at $p=1/2$.

The promised societies are exactly those with $p\neq1/2$, and their margin is
$$ \delta=\rho_r(\mu)=|p-1/2|. $$
Indeed, two Bernoulli distributions have total-variation distance equal to the difference between their parameters. The infimum distance to a distribution with a different majority winner is therefore $\delta$, irrespective of tie-breaking.

Let $N$ denote the number of acquired samples and $W$ the peak number of working bits.

For later statements, define
$$ g=1-2\epsilon,\qquad \kappa(\epsilon)=g\log\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}, \qquad H(\delta)=\log\log\frac{e^2}{\delta}. $$
Here $g>0$ and $H(\delta)$ is bounded below by a positive absolute constant for $0<\delta\leq1/2$.

The sample conclusions proved below are:

  • One uniform algorithm satisfies

$$ \mathbb E_p[N] =O\!\left( \delta^{-2}\bigl[\log(1/\epsilon)+H(\delta)\bigr] \right). $$

  • Allowing an initial randomized choice gives a uniform sample upper bound

$$ \mathbb E_p[N] =O\!\left( \delta^{-2}\bigl[\kappa(\epsilon)+gH(\delta)\bigr] \right). $$

  • Every valid algorithm satisfies, for dyadic $0<\delta\leq1/4$ and both signs,

$$ \mathbb E_{1/2\pm\delta}[N] \geq \frac{\kappa(\epsilon)}{16\delta^2}. $$

  • For every fixed $\epsilon\in(0,1/2)$, every valid algorithm satisfies

$$ \limsup_{k\to\infty} \frac{\mathbb E_{1/2+2^{-k^2}}[N]} {2^{2k^2}\log\log(2^{k^2})} \geq \frac{g}{32}. $$

The last statement is a lower bound along a sequence of margins. It is not a pointwise lower bound at every margin.

2. A uniform unknown-margin algorithm

Choose an integer $A\geq1$ such that
$$ 2^{-A}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{8}, \qquad A=O(\log(1/\epsilon)). $$
For a finite binary fractional encoding of $\epsilon$, the position of its first nonzero digit suffices to choose such an $A$; subsequent digits need not be retained.

At stage $k\geq1$, put
$$ \ell_k=A+2\lceil\log_2(k+1)\rceil,\qquad n_k=4^k\ell_k,\qquad b_k=2^{-k}. $$
Acquire a fresh batch of $n_k$ samples and compute its frequency $\widehat p_k$ of candidate-$1$ rankings. Stop when
$$ |\widehat p_k-1/2|>b_k, $$
and output the corresponding majority winner.

The test uses integer counts and exact integer comparisons.

2.1. Error and termination

Hoeffding’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr_p\bigl(|\widehat p_k-p|>b_k\bigr) \leq 2\exp(-2n_kb_k^2) =2e^{-2\ell_k} \leq \frac{\epsilon}{4(k+1)^2}. $$
For the last inequality, use $e^{-2A}\leq2^{-A}$ and
$$ e^{-4\lceil\log_2(k+1)\rceil}\leq(k+1)^{-2}. $$

A wrong stopping decision forces $|\widehat p_k-p|>b_k$. Summing the displayed bounds over all stages proves error at most $\epsilon$.

Let $K$ be the stopping stage, and let $k_0$ be the first stage with
$$ 2^{-k_0}\leq\delta/2. $$
For $k\geq k_0$, failure to stop at stage $k$ forces
$$ |\widehat p_k-p|\geq\delta/2. $$
Freshness of the batch therefore gives
$$ \Pr_p(K>k)\leq2\exp(-n_k\delta^2/2). $$
This tends to zero, proving almost sure termination.

2.2. Expected sample count

Minimality of $k_0$ implies
$$ 4\leq4^{k_0}\delta^2<16. $$
Write $\ell_0=\ell_{k_0}$ and $n_0=n_{k_0}$. For $j\geq0$,
$$ \ell_{k_0+j}\leq\ell_0+2j, \qquad n_{k_0+j}\leq n_0\,4^j(1+2j/\ell_0). $$
Also, for $j\geq1$,
$$ \Pr_p(K\geq k_0+j) \leq2\exp(-2\ell_0\,4^{j-1}). $$

Since stage $k$ is executed exactly when $K\geq k$,
$$ \mathbb E_p[N]=\sum_{k\geq1}n_k\Pr_p(K\geq k). $$
The stages through $k_0$ contribute at most $4n_0/3$, because $\ell_k$ is nondecreasing. The later stages contribute at most
$$ 2n_0\sum_{j\geq1} 4^j(1+2j/\ell_0)e^{-2\ell_0 4^{j-1}} =O(n_0). $$
The series is bounded uniformly for $\ell_0\geq1$: its terms are bounded by a polynomial in $j$ times $4^j e^{-2\cdot4^{j-1}}$.

Consequently,
$$ \mathbb E_p[N] =O\!\left( \delta^{-2}\bigl[\log(1/\epsilon)+H(\delta)\bigr] \right). $$

2.3. Expected peak memory and bit runtime

An implementation retains the stage index, $A$, the batch length, the number of samples acquired in the current batch, and its number of successes. Individual samples are discarded.

Conditional on $K=k$, its working memory satisfies
$$ W\leq C\bigl(k+\log(A+1)+1\bigr) $$
for an absolute implementation constant $C$. In particular, the sample counters require $O(\log(n_k+1))$ bits, and
$$ \log(n_k+1)=O(k+\log(A+1)). $$

The stopping tail gives
$$ \mathbb E_p[K] \leq k_0+ 2\sum_{j\geq1}e^{-2\ell_0 4^{j-1}} =k_0+O(1). $$
Thus
$$ \mathbb E_p[W] =O\!\left( 1+\log(1/\delta)+\log\log(e/\epsilon) \right). $$
This explicitly bounds expected peak memory; it is not inferred from expected samples alone.

Using ordinary binary arithmetic, stage $k$ requires at most
$$ C n_k\log(n_k+1) $$
bit operations. Through stage $k_0$, the expected contribution is
$$ O\bigl(n_0[k_0+\log(A+1)]\bigr). $$
The remaining contribution is bounded by a constant times
$$ n_0[k_0+\log(A+1)] \sum_{j\geq1} 4^j(1+2j/\ell_0)(1+j)e^{-2\ell_0 4^{j-1}}, $$
and this series is uniformly bounded.

Hence, with
$$ N_*=\delta^{-2}\bigl[\log(1/\epsilon)+H(\delta)\bigr], $$
the expected bit runtime is
$$ O\bigl(N_*\log(N_*+1)\bigr). $$
No hidden support encoding is read. For other rational input representations, the costs of obtaining $A$ from the supplied encoding must be added.

2.4. Improving expected samples when the allowed error approaches one half

For $\epsilon\leq1/4$, the preceding upper bound is
$$ O\!\left(\delta^{-2}[\kappa(\epsilon)+gH(\delta)]\right). $$
To check this, $g\geq1/2$, and $\kappa(\epsilon)$ is bounded above and below by absolute constant multiples of $\log(1/\epsilon)$.

For $\epsilon>1/4$, run the preceding algorithm with error $1/4$ with probability $2g$, and otherwise output an independent fair choice of the two candidates. The error is at most
$$ (1-2g)\frac12+(2g)\frac14 =\frac12-\frac g2 =\epsilon. $$
The expected samples are
$$ O\bigl(g\delta^{-2}[1+H(\delta)]\bigr) =O\bigl(g\delta^{-2}H(\delta)\bigr). $$
The initial Bernoulli choice with rational probability $2g$ can be implemented using fair random bits. Its implementation costs are separate from the sample count.

This proves the asserted uniform sample upper bound involving $\kappa(\epsilon)+gH(\delta)$.

3. Sample lower bounds directly in the ranking model

The proofs in this section use only Bernoulli samples. In particular, they do not transfer a Gaussian theorem to the present model. The cited Gaussian sign-testing result has a different observation model. [Jamieson et al., Appendix A](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v35/jamieson14.pdf)

3.1. A finite-transcript information identity

Consider two Bernoulli parameters $p$ and $q$. Stop an algorithm’s execution at a finite computational horizon, optionally also aborting before it would acquire more than a specified number of samples. Let $M$ be the acquired sample count in this truncated execution.

The chain rule for relative entropy gives
$$ D(P_p^{\mathrm{tr}}\Vert P_q^{\mathrm{tr}}) =\mathbb E_p[M]\operatorname{kl}(p,q). $$
Indeed, deterministic transitions and internal random bits have the same conditional laws under both parameters. Each requested sample contributes conditional relative entropy $\operatorname{kl}(p,q)$. Summing over the finitely many computational steps proves the identity.

Here
$$ \operatorname{kl}(p,q) =p\log\frac pq+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}. $$
We will use
$$ \operatorname{kl}(1/2+\delta,1/2)\leq4\delta^2. $$
This follows from $\log u\leq u-1$, which gives
$$ \operatorname{kl}(p,q) \leq\frac{(p-q)^2}{q(1-q)}. $$

We also use the event inequality
$$ D(P\Vert Q)\geq P(E)\log\frac1{Q(E)}-\log2. $$
To prove it, apply data processing to the indicator of $E$, and expand binary relative entropy:
$$ \operatorname{kl}(a,b) =a\log(1/b)+(1-a)\log(1/(1-b))-h(a) \geq a\log(1/b)-\log2. $$

3.2. Pointwise inverse-square lower bound

Fix dyadic $0<\delta\leq1/4$, and consider
$$ p_+=1/2+\delta,\qquad p_-=1/2-\delta. $$
If $\mathbb E_+[N]$ is infinite, the claimed lower bound is immediate. Otherwise, truncate at computational time $T$ and apply the preceding identity. Data processing to the event “candidate $1$ has been output by time $T$” gives a binary relative-entropy lower bound.

As $T$ increases, almost sure termination and correctness imply that this event has limiting probability at least $1-\epsilon$ under $p_+$ and at most $\epsilon$ under $p_-$. Meanwhile, the expected truncated sample count increases to $\mathbb E_+[N]$.

Thus
$$ \mathbb E_+[N]\operatorname{kl}(p_+,p_-) \geq\operatorname{kl}(1-\epsilon,\epsilon) =\kappa(\epsilon). $$
The minimization at these correctness boundaries follows because binary relative entropy increases in its first argument and decreases in its second when the first exceeds the second.

Finally,
$$ \operatorname{kl}(p_+,p_-) =2\delta\log\frac{1+2\delta}{1-2\delta} \leq16\delta^2. $$
For example, apply $\log(1+x)\leq x$ and $-\log(1-x)\leq x/(1-x)$ with $x=2\delta\leq1/2$.

Therefore
$$ \mathbb E_+[N]\geq\frac{\kappa(\epsilon)}{16\delta^2}. $$
Interchanging the candidates proves the same bound under $p_-$.

3.3. Iterated-logarithm lower bound on dyadic societies

Fix $\epsilon\in(0,1/2)$ and write $g=1-2\epsilon$. Let
$$ \delta_k=2^{-k^2},\qquad H_k=\log\log(1/\delta_k),\qquad k\geq2. $$
I prove
$$ \limsup_{k\to\infty} \frac{\mathbb E_{1/2+\delta_k}[N]}{\delta_k^{-2}H_k} \geq\frac g{32}. $$

The tied distribution $p_0=1/2$ is used only as an auxiliary probability law. No termination or correctness promise is imposed there.

First, positive output at the tie has probability at most $\epsilon$.

For an integer $n$, let $A_n$ be the event that the algorithm eventually outputs candidate $1$ after acquiring at most $n$ samples. This event is determined by the first $n$ samples and the full internal random tape.

Under every promised parameter $p<1/2$,
$$ \Pr_p(A_n)\leq\epsilon. $$
For fixed $n$, the total-variation distance between the first $n$ observations under $p$ and under $p_0$ tends to zero as dyadic $p$ increases to $p_0$. Adding the same independent random tape does not change that distance. Hence
$$ \Pr_0(A_n)\leq\epsilon. $$
Taking the increasing union over $n$ yields
$$ \Pr_0(\text{eventually outputs candidate }1)\leq\epsilon. $$

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that the claimed limsup is smaller than $g/32$.

Set $c=g/32$. For all sufficiently large $k$, the supposed failure gives
$$ \mathbb E_{1/2+\delta_k}[N]\leq c\delta_k^{-2}H_k. $$
Define integer cutoffs
$$ b_k=\left\lceil\frac{8c}{g}\delta_k^{-2}H_k\right\rceil. $$
They are increasing for sufficiently large $k$.

The probability of acquiring more than $b_k$ samples under $1/2+\delta_k$ is, by Markov’s inequality, at most $g/8$.

For the early cutoff,
$$ b_{k-1}\delta_k^2\longrightarrow0. $$
Indeed,
$$ \frac{\delta_k^2}{\delta_{k-1}^2}=2^{-4k+2}, \qquad H_{k-1}=O(\log k). $$
Pinsker’s inequality and the preceding Bernoulli entropy bound imply that the first $b_{k-1}$ observations under $1/2+\delta_k$ and under $p_0$ have total-variation distance at most
$$ \sqrt{2b_{k-1}\delta_k^2}, $$
which is at most $g/8$ for sufficiently large $k$. Consequently,
$$ \Pr_{1/2+\delta_k}(A_{b_{k-1}}) \leq\epsilon+g/8. $$

Let $E_k$ be the event that candidate $1$ is output with sample count in
$$ (b_{k-1},b_k]. $$
Correctness, the early bound, and the late bound give
$$ \Pr_{1/2+\delta_k}(E_k) \geq(1-\epsilon)-(\epsilon+g/8)-g/8 =\frac{3g}{4}. $$

Choose a finite computational horizon $T_k$ such that the subevent $E_k'$ of output within that horizon has probability at least $g/2$. Such a horizon exists because finite-time output events increase to $E_k$.

Truncate the execution at time $T_k$, also aborting before sample $b_k+1$. The information identity gives
$$ D(P_{1/2+\delta_k}^{\mathrm{tr}}\Vert P_0^{\mathrm{tr}}) \leq4\delta_k^2\mathbb E_{1/2+\delta_k}[N] \leq4cH_k. $$
Applying the event inequality to $E_k'$ yields
$$ \Pr_0(E_k') \geq \exp\!\left(-\frac{8c}{g}H_k-\frac{2\log2}{g}\right). $$
Since
$$ H_k=\log(k^2\log2)\leq2\log k, $$
we obtain
$$ \Pr_0(E_k')\geq C_\epsilon k^{-16c/g} =C_\epsilon k^{-1/2} $$
for a positive constant $C_\epsilon$.

But the events $E_k'$ are pairwise disjoint: their sample-count windows are disjoint. Their probabilities therefore sum to at most $1$, whereas the displayed lower bounds have divergent sum. This contradiction proves the theorem.

Thus the iterated logarithm is necessary for a uniform expected-sample envelope, even when the promised parameters are restricted to dyadic rationals.

4. Expected peak memory: a lower bound independent of sample count

This section uses the usual classical bit-memory model: the machine has fixed finite control, and all mutable storage and positions used to retain information count toward working memory. There is no uncounted clock, counter, sample history, or computational oracle. Rule-evaluation workspace is included.

Fix an algorithm and a fixed input $\epsilon$. In any standard implementation, the number of configurations accessible with at most $B$ working bits is bounded by
$$ Q_B\leq C_{\!A,\epsilon}2^{c_A B} $$
for constants $C_{\!A,\epsilon}$ and $c_A>0$. Polynomial factors arising from tape-head positions can be absorbed into the exponential. Constants depending on fixed input and finite control are harmless for the margin asymptotic proved here.

4.1. A finite-state absorption lemma

Consider an absorbing machine with at most $Q$ states, driven by Bernoulli samples and independent internal randomness. Suppose it absorbs almost surely at both
$$ p_+=1/2+\delta,\qquad p_-=1/2-\delta, $$
where $0<\delta\leq1/4$.

Let $f_\pm$ be the probabilities of absorption at a particular output. Then
$$ f_+\leq R^{2Q}f_-, \qquad R=\frac{1+2\delta}{1-2\delta}. $$

Proof. Refine transitions into elementary steps so that each step requests at most one sample. Each transition probability has the form
$$ a p+b(1-p)+c $$
with nonnegative coefficients independent of $p$. Its ratio between $p_+$ and $p_-$ lies in $[R^{-1},R]$. The graph of positive transitions is the same at both parameters.

Remove unreachable states and replace each absorbing state by a deterministic transition to the initial state. The resulting chain is irreducible: every reachable state can reach an absorbing state, and hence the initial state. Otherwise almost sure absorption would fail.

For completeness, the directed spanning-tree formula for its stationary probabilities is
$$ \pi_x=\frac{T_x}{\sum_yT_y}, $$
where $T_x$ is the sum of products of transition probabilities over all directed spanning trees oriented toward $x$. This is the Markov chain tree formula. [Aldous and Fill, Section 9.2](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/RWG/Book_Ralph/Ch9.S2.html)

Its balance identity has a direct proof. For $x\neq y$, take a tree oriented toward $x$ and add the edge $x\to y$. This creates a unique directed cycle containing $y$. Removing the outgoing edge of $y$ produces a tree oriented toward $y$. The operation is bijective, with weights preserved, and proves
$$ \sum_{x\neq y}T_xP_{xy} =T_y\sum_{z\neq y}P_{yz}. $$
Adding self-transition terms gives stationarity; irreducibility gives uniqueness after normalization.

Each tree has at most $Q-1$ edges. Hence each tree weight, and then each $T_x$, changes by a factor between $R^{-Q}$ and $R^Q$ between the two parameters.

Almost sure absorption in a finite-state chain has finite expected time. To check this, from every reachable state choose a positive-probability path to absorption. Finitely many states give a common positive lower bound for absorption within a fixed number of steps, and therefore a geometric tail.

In the restarted chain, each excursion visits exactly one absorbing state. Expected occupation counts over an excursion, normalized by its expected length, give the stationary distribution. Thus the absorption probability at output $x$ is
$$ f_x=\frac{\pi_x}{\sum_{y\text{ absorbing}}\pi_y} =\frac{T_x}{\sum_{y\text{ absorbing}}T_y}. $$
Both numerator and denominator change by factors between $R^{-Q}$ and $R^Q$, proving the claimed ratio bound. $\square$

4.2. Applying the lemma to expected peak memory

Let
$$ s_+=\mathbb E_{p_+}[W]. $$
If $s_+$ is infinite, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise choose
$$ B=\max\{1,\lceil2s_+/g\rceil\}. $$
Truncate the original machine when it would exceed $B$ working bits, outputting a special symbol at that point.

Markov’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr_{p_+}(W>B)\leq g/2. $$
Let $f_\pm$ be the probabilities that the truncated machine outputs candidate $1$. Then
$$ f_+\geq1-\epsilon-g/2=\frac12, \qquad f_-\leq\epsilon. $$
The truncated machine absorbs almost surely under both societies because the original machine does.

Its configuration count satisfies
$$ Q\leq C_{\!A,\epsilon}2^{c_A B}. $$
The finite-state lemma gives
$$ R^{2Q}\geq\frac1{2\epsilon}. $$
For $\delta\leq1/4$,
$$ \log R\leq8\delta, $$
so
$$ Q\geq\frac{\log(1/(2\epsilon))}{16\delta}. $$
Combining the two configuration bounds and using $B\leq2s_+/g+1$ yields
$$ s_+\geq \frac{g}{2c_A} \log_2\!\left( \frac{\log(1/(2\epsilon))} {16C_{\!A,\epsilon}\delta} \right)-\frac g2. $$
Interchanging the candidates proves the corresponding bound under $p_-$.

In particular, for every fixed $\epsilon\in(0,1/2)$ and every valid fixed algorithm,
$$ \mathbb E_{1/2\pm\delta}[W] =\Omega_{A,\epsilon}(\log(1/\delta)) $$
as dyadic $\delta\to0$.

This lower bound makes no assumption on expected samples or runtime. Increasing either cannot remove the required logarithmic margin dependence of expected peak memory.

Together with Section 2, it establishes the optimal order
$$ \mathbb E[W]=\Theta(\log(1/\delta)) $$
for fixed $\epsilon$, with algorithm-dependent constants in the lower bound. The sample-envelope upper bound is attained simultaneously with this memory order.

5. Why the sample lower bound needs explicit quantifiers

The iterated-logarithm lower bound cannot simply be changed into a statement asserting the same lower bound at every individual margin.

Here is a constructive reason. Fix an integer $h\geq1$, and replace the confidence parameters in Section 2 by
$$ \ell_k=A+2\lceil\log_2(|k-h|+2)\rceil. $$
Keep the same batch sizes $4^k\ell_k$ and thresholds $2^{-k}$.

The error proof still works, because
$$ 2e^{-2\ell_k} \leq\frac{\epsilon}{4(|k-h|+2)^2}, $$
and the sum over $k\geq1$ is less than $\epsilon$. For every nonzero margin, sufficiently late stages have thresholds below half the margin, so the same concentration argument proves almost sure termination and finite expected samples.

Now fix a target margin $\delta_*$ and choose $h$ to be its first stage with $2^{-h}\leq\delta_*/2$. At this target,
$$ \ell_h=A+2, \qquad \ell_{h\pm j}\leq A+2+2j $$
whenever the corresponding stage exists. Therefore the stages through $h$ contribute at most
$$ 4^h\sum_{j\geq0}4^{-j}(A+2+2j)=O(4^h A) $$
samples. Later stages have the same summable exponential stopping tail as before, and also contribute $O(4^h A)$.

Consequently this algorithm, which remains valid at every promised society, satisfies at its selected target
$$ \mathbb E_{1/2\pm\delta_*}[N] =O\bigl(\delta_*^{-2}\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr). $$
The integer $h$ is part of the algorithm, not supplied by the society.

Thus, for fixed $\epsilon$, minimizing separately over all valid algorithms at each margin gives pointwise order $\delta^{-2}$, whereas one algorithm’s uniform envelope cannot avoid the iterated logarithm along all sufficiently small dyadic margins. These are different optimization quantifiers.

6. For three or more candidates, the rule axioms do not ensure predictability

Both examples below apply to each fixed $m\geq3$. Put $d=m!$ and enumerate the rankings.

The task imposes no consistency condition between different candidate counts, so either construction can be defined to use majority when there are two candidates.

6.1. An easy promised domain

Define
$$ r_0(h)= \begin{cases} 2,&h_1=h_2,\\ 1,&h_1\neq h_2. \end{cases} $$
This rule is anonymous and effectively evaluable on rational histograms. Equality of the coordinates is preserved by positive scaling and by adding $a\mathbf1$.

Every distribution with $p_1=p_2$ has margin zero. If either coordinate is positive, transfer a sufficiently small amount between them. If both are zero, transfer a sufficiently small amount from a positive coordinate into the first one. Either operation makes them unequal and changes the winner.

Hence every promised society has winner $1$, and the algorithm can output it without acquiring samples.

Nevertheless, for dyadic $0<\delta<1/2$,
$$ p_\delta=(1/2+\delta,1/2-\delta,0,\ldots,0) $$
has margin exactly $\delta$. Every competing $q$ has $q_1=q_2$, so
$$ 2\delta =\left|(p_{\delta,1}-q_1)-(p_{\delta,2}-q_2)\right| \leq2\operatorname{TV}(p_\delta,q). $$
Equality is attained by $q=(1/2,1/2,0,\ldots,0)$.

Thus positive margins tending to zero do not force growing sample complexity for every admissible fixed rule.

6.2. An admissible rule without any effective predictor

Enumerate deterministic machines on empty input as $M_1,M_2,\ldots$. If $M_e$ halts, let $H_e\geq1$ be its first halting step.

Define
$$ c_e=2^{-e},\qquad a_e=2^{-e-4}, \qquad J_e=(c_e-a_e,c_e+a_e), $$
and
$$ I_{e,s} =\left(c_e+a_e2^{-s},\ c_e+\frac32a_e2^{-s}\right), \qquad s\geq1. $$
