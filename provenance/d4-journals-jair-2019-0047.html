The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a scoring vector \(\alpha\) in Theorem 22’s constant or non-concentrated classes, a preferred candidate \(p\), and rational-mass types \(t=(r_t,w_t,\psi_t)\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), choose transfers \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\) to destination rankings \(\pi\ne r_t\), with \(\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\le\mu_t\). Each transferred unit keeps its source type’s weight \(w_t\) and price \(\psi_t\). Minimize \(\sum_t\psi_t\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\) subject to \(p\)’s final weighted score being at least every competitor’s, where \(S(c)=\sum_t w_t[(\mu_t-\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi})\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)}+\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\pi}(c)}]\).

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge2\) candidates and let \(p\in C\). The input scoring vector \(\alpha=(\alpha_0,\ldots,\alpha_{m-1})\) has binary rational entries with \(\alpha_0\ge\cdots\ge\alpha_{m-1}\ge0\). It is either constant (integer entries bounded by a fixed constant) or non-concentrated: \(\frac1{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\alpha_j\le(1-\epsilon)\alpha_0\) for a fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type \(t=(r_t,w_t,\psi_t)\) has a strict ranking \(r_t\), positive integer weight \(w_t\), and positive integer price \(\psi_t\); \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
For every destination ranking \(\pi\ne r_t\), choose divisible transfers \(x_{t,\pi}\ge0\) with \(y_t:=\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\le\mu_t\). Transferred mass retains its source weight and pays its source price per unit, independently of its destination.
With positions indexed from \(0\), define \(S(c)=\sum_t w_t[(\mu_t-y_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)}+\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\pi}(c)}]\). A transfer is successful when \(S(p)\ge S(c)\) for every \(c\ne p\); ties count as wins.
Let \(\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) be the minimum successful cost \(\sum_t\psi_t y_t\), and define \(\psi_{\max}=\max_t\psi_t\), \(r_{\max}=\max_t\psi_t/w_t\), and \(\bar r_{\max}=\max_t w_t/\psi_t\). Here \(\widetilde O\) suppresses factors polynomial in \(\log(m\tau+2)\).

Can a polynomial-time randomized algorithm find successful transfers costing at most \(\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}+\widetilde O(\sqrt{\psi_{\max}(\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}+\psi_{\max})}\,\bar r_{\max}r_{\max})\), with exponentially small failure probability in \(L\), or is attaining this guarantee NP-hard?

Result

The problem is exactly solvable by a deterministic algorithm in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time. Thus the requested additive guarantee holds with failure probability zero. The algorithm works for every nonincreasing rational scoring vector; the non-concentration assumption is unnecessary.

The cited [continuous-bribery theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444v1#S3) establishes polynomial-time solvability in the unweighted setting. Below is a direct proof incorporating source weights and explicitly recovering legal transfers.

1. A polynomial-size linear program

For each supported source type \(t\), introduce a variable \(y_t\) and variables \(z_{t,c,j}\), where \(c\in C\) and \(j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\).

The intended meaning of \(z_{t,c,j}\) is the transferred mass from source \(t\) whose destination places candidate \(c\) in position \(j\). Impose

\(\displaystyle 0\le y_t\le\mu_t,\qquad z_{t,c,j}\ge0,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z_{t,c,j}=y_t\quad\text{for every }t,c,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{c\in C}z_{t,c,j}=y_t\quad\text{for every }t,j.\)

Define the linear score expression

\(\displaystyle \widehat S(c)=\sum_t w_t\left[(\mu_t-y_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\alpha_jz_{t,c,j}\right].\)

The winning constraints are

\(\displaystyle \widehat S(p)\ge\widehat S(c)\quad\text{for every }c\ne p.\)

Minimize

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\psi_ty_t.\)

Call the optimum of this LP \(V\). Each source retains its own matrix and its own weight and price.

The LP temporarily permits transferred mass to be assigned its source ranking. Section 3 removes such assignments without changing any score.

2. Every legal transfer gives a feasible LP solution

Given legal transfers \(x_{t,\pi}\), set

\(\displaystyle y_t=\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi},\qquad z_{t,c,j}=\sum_{\substack{\pi\ne r_t\\ \operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)=j}}x_{t,\pi}.\)

Each ranking places every candidate in exactly one position and assigns exactly one candidate to every position. Consequently, all row and column sums of the matrix \(Z_t=(z_{t,c,j})\) equal \(y_t\).

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_j\alpha_jz_{t,c,j} =\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)}. \)

Thus \(\widehat S(c)=S(c)\) for every candidate, and the LP objective equals the transfer cost. In particular,

\(\displaystyle V\le\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}.\)

Successful transfers always exist: fix a ranking \(\rho\) placing \(p\) first, and transfer all mass of every type with \(r_t\ne\rho\) to \(\rho\). Leave the other types unchanged. Every resulting ranking is \(\rho\), so nonincreasing scores and positive weights imply \(S(p)\ge S(c)\) for every \(c\).

The LP is therefore feasible. Its feasible region is closed and bounded: \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\) and \(0\le z_{t,c,j}\le y_t\). Hence its minimum is attained.

3. Recovering legal transfers from any feasible LP solution

We first give a constructive decomposition for each source matrix.

Decomposition lemma. If an \(m\)-by-\(m\) nonnegative rational matrix \(Z\) has every row and column sum equal to \(y\), then

\(\displaystyle Z=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_kP_{\pi_k},\qquad \lambda_k>0,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_k=y,\qquad K\le m^2,\)

where \(P_{\pi_k}\) is the permutation matrix of a ranking \(\pi_k\). For \(y=0\), the decomposition is empty.

Proof. Maintain a residual matrix \(A\) whose row and column sums all equal \(q\), initially \(A=Z\) and \(q=y\). If \(q=0\), nonnegativity implies \(A=0\).

For \(q>0\), form a bipartite graph with candidates on one side and positions on the other, using an edge precisely when \(A_{c,j}>0\). For any set \(U\) of candidates, let \(N(U)\) be its neighboring positions. Then

\(\displaystyle |U|q =\sum_{c\in U}\sum_{j\in N(U)}A_{c,j} \le\sum_{c\in C}\sum_{j\in N(U)}A_{c,j} =|N(U)|q. \)

Dividing by \(q>0\) verifies Hall’s condition. Therefore the graph has a perfect matching, defining a ranking \(\pi\).

Set

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\min_c A_{c,\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)}>0.\)

Subtract \(\lambda P_\pi\). The residual stays nonnegative, and every row and column sum becomes \(q-\lambda\). At least one positive entry becomes zero, and no zero entry becomes positive. Thus there are at most \(m^2\) iterations.

At termination the residual is zero. Summing the subtracted matrices gives the claimed decomposition, and summing their row sums gives \(\sum_k\lambda_k=y\). \(\square\)

Apply this lemma to each \(Z_t\), obtaining coefficients \(\lambda_{t,k}\) and rankings \(\pi_{t,k}\). Define

\(\displaystyle q_t=\sum_{k:\,\pi_{t,k}=r_t}\lambda_{t,k}.\)

For each permitted destination \(\pi\ne r_t\), output

\(\displaystyle x_{t,\pi}=\sum_{k:\,\pi_{t,k}=\pi}\lambda_{t,k}.\)

The actual transferred mass is

\(\displaystyle y'_t=\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}=y_t-q_t,\)

so \(0\le y'_t\le\mu_t\).

To check score preservation, fix a candidate \(c\). Its contribution from source \(t\), before multiplying by \(w_t\), becomes

\(\displaystyle (\mu_t-y'_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)} +\sum_{\pi\ne r_t}x_{t,\pi}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)} \)

\(\displaystyle =(\mu_t-y_t+q_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)} +\sum_{k:\,\pi_{t,k}\ne r_t}\lambda_{t,k} \alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\pi_{t,k}}(c)} \)

\(\displaystyle =(\mu_t-y_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)} +\sum_k\lambda_{t,k}\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{\pi_{t,k}}(c)} \)

\(\displaystyle =(\mu_t-y_t)\alpha_{\operatorname{pos}_{r_t}(c)} +\sum_j\alpha_jz_{t,c,j}. \)

Thus every score is exactly \(\widehat S(c)\), and the recovered transfers are successful. Their cost satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\psi_ty'_t =\sum_t\psi_ty_t-\sum_t\psi_tq_t \le\sum_t\psi_ty_t. \)

Applying this construction to an optimal LP solution yields legal transfers of cost at most \(V\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}\le V\le\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}, \)

which proves exact optimality.

4. Running time and the requested guarantee

The LP has \(\tau(m^2+1)\) variables and \(\mathcal O(\tau m+m)\) constraints apart from nonnegativity. Its coefficients are formed by rational arithmetic on input entries, so its total encoding length is \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

The standard exact rational linear programming theorem therefore computes an optimal rational solution in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations. Its applicability follows from the explicit polynomial-size rational formulation and the feasibility, boundedness, and attainment established above.

For reconstruction, each source requires at most \(m^2\) perfect matchings. Using an \(\mathcal O(m^3)\) matching algorithm gives \(\mathcal O(\tau m^5)\) arithmetic operations. These operations retain polynomial bit length: all residual entries are differences of the original rational entries, whose denominators admit a common multiple of polynomial bit length. Hence reconstruction also takes \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations.

The output lists at most \(\tau m^2\) nonzero source–destination transfers; all omitted transfers are zero. This bound depends on the supplied support size \(\tau\), without enumerating possible types or all rankings.

The returned cost is exactly \(\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}\), so it satisfies the requested bound

\(\displaystyle \Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}} +\widetilde{\mathcal O}\!\left( \sqrt{\psi_{\max}(\Psi_{\mathrm{OPT}}+\psi_{\max})}\, \bar r_{\max}r_{\max} \right). \)

The algorithm is deterministic, so its failure probability is zero, which is stronger than exponentially small failure probability in \(L\). For a constant scoring vector, every candidate already ties and the optimum is zero; the same algorithm also handles all nonconstant cases.
