The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(\tau\) membership types and an explicitly encoded monotone, closed rational polyhedral winning region \(W\subseteq\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), find the least \(d\) for which rational vectors \(w^1,\ldots,w^d\ge 0\) and positive rational quotas \(q_1,\ldots,q_d\) satisfy \(x\in W\) exactly when \(w^j\cdot x\ge q_j\) for every \(j\); output the rules or report that no finite representation exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge 1\) membership types with positive masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), encoded in binary. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; total mass need not equal one.
A coalition is any real vector \(x\in B:=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_t]\). Its winning region is supplied as \(W=\{x\in B:Ax\ge b\}\), with \(m\) explicitly listed rational inequalities. Promise that \(0\notin W\), \(\mu\in W\), and \(x\in W,\ x\le y\in B\) imply \(y\in W\); lower-dimensional regions are allowed.
Define the dimension as the least positive integer \(d\) admitting \(w^j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau}\) and \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) such that, for every \(x\in B\), \(x\in W\iff w^j\cdot x\ge q_j\) for all \(j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\). Equality meets the quota. Set the dimension to \(\infty\) if no finite representation exists.
The computational problem is to find this minimum and output attaining rules, or report that no finite representation exists. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this minimum-dimension problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite simple games, dimension is at most \(|C|\) whenever every maximal losing coalition is within Hamming distance one of some member of \(C\). [Olsen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04960)
Given \(k\) weighted majority games, deciding whether their intersection has dimension exactly \(k\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. [Deineko–Woeginger](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/on-the-dimension-of-simple-monotonic-games/)
No result settling the exact continuous problem specified above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Resolved the unspecified polyhedral encoding as an explicit rational inequality description of a convex polyhedron intersected with the coalition box; m denotes the number of supplied inequalities.
  • Required the zero coalition to lose and the full-mass coalition to win, carrying over the defining conditions of a simple game.
  • Allowed arbitrary positive rational total mass without normalization, matching the discrete society's unrestricted size.
  • Required equivalence for every real coalition vector, including boundary points; quotas use weak inequalities.
  • Required a positive minimum dimension, as in the discrete definition, and allowed lower-dimensional winning regions.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The minimum-dimension problem is polynomial-time solvable in $m,\tau,L$. Every instance satisfying the promises has finite dimension, and attaining rational rules can be produced in polynomial time.

The lower-dimensional case contributes one extra rule, regardless of how many coordinates are forced to their maximum values. The proof below establishes both the construction and its optimality.

1. Removing forced coordinates.

Define
$$ F=\{t:x_t=\mu_t\text{ for every }x\in W\}, \qquad U=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\setminus F. $$
For each coordinate, solve the rational linear program
$$ \ell_t=\min\{x_t:x\in B,\ Ax\ge b\}. $$
These programs are feasible because $\mu\in W$, and their feasible regions are compact. Since $x_t\le\mu_t$ throughout $B$,
$$ t\in F\iff \ell_t=\mu_t. $$
Thus $F$ is computable using $\tau$ exact linear programs.

Let
$$ B_U=\prod_{t\in U}[0,\mu_t], \qquad P=\{u\in B_U:(\mu_F,u)\in W\}. $$
Here $(\mu_F,u)$ denotes the vector with coordinates $\mu_t$ on $F$ and $u_t$ on $U$. By the definition of $F$,
$$ W=\{(\mu_F,u):u\in P\}. $$
Substituting the forced coordinates into each input row gives
$$ a_i=(A_{it})_{t\in U}, \qquad \beta_i=b_i-\sum_{t\in F}A_{it}\mu_t, $$
and hence
$$ P=\{u\in B_U:a_i\cdot u\ge\beta_i\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m\}. $$

If $U$ is nonempty, then $P$ has nonempty interior in $\mathbb R^U$. To prove this, for each $t\in U$ choose $x^{(t)}\in W$ with $x^{(t)}_t<\mu_t$. Such a point exists because $t\notin F$. The average
$$ z=\frac1{|U|}\sum_{t\in U}x^{(t)} $$
belongs to $W$ by convexity. For every $t\in U$, all summands have their $t$th coordinate at most $\mu_t$, and one has it strictly smaller. Therefore $z_t<\mu_t$.

Upward closure implies
$$ \prod_{t\in U}[z_t,\mu_t]\subseteq P. $$
The point
$$ u^\circ=\frac{z_U+\mu_U}{2} $$
lies strictly between the endpoints of every interval in this product and satisfies $0<u^\circ_t<\mu_t$. Consequently, an open ball around $u^\circ$ lies in $P\cap\operatorname{int}(B_U)$.

If $U$ is empty, then $W=\{\mu\}$; we handle this case using the empty reduced system below.

2. Removing redundant inequalities relative to the box.

Start with $J=\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and process its rows in any fixed order. For a current row $i\in J$, solve
$$ v_i=\min\left\{ a_i\cdot u: u\in B_U,\ a_j\cdot u\ge\beta_j\text{ for }j\in J\setminus\{i\} \right\}. $$
Delete $i$ precisely when $v_i\ge\beta_i$.

Each feasible region contains $P$ and is compact, so every such minimum exists. A deletion preserves the represented set. Moreover, a row found nonredundant remains nonredundant after subsequent deletions: its violating witness continues to satisfy the remaining rows.

Thus, after at most $m$ linear programs, the final set $J$ satisfies
$$ P=\{u\in B_U:a_i\cdot u\ge\beta_i\text{ for }i\in J\}, $$
and every retained row is nonredundant relative to $B_U$. Write
$$ r=|J|. $$
When $U$ is empty, every reduced row is a true constant inequality because $\mu\in W$, so all rows are deleted and $r=0$.

We next prove the crucial properties of the retained rows.

3. Every retained row is an admissible weighted rule.

Suppose $U$ is nonempty. For every $i\in J$, we claim that
$$ a_i\ne0,\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^U,\qquad \beta_i>0. $$

A retained row cannot have $a_i=0$: because $P$ is nonempty, its constant inequality would hold everywhere and would be redundant.

The interior point $u^\circ$ from Section 1 satisfies every retained row strictly. Indeed, if a nonzero valid linear inequality were tight at an interior point of $P$, a sufficiently small displacement against its normal would remain in $P$ and violate it.

Fix $i\in J$. Nonredundancy provides a point $v\in B_U$ satisfying
$$ a_i\cdot v<\beta_i, \qquad a_j\cdot v\ge\beta_j\quad(j\in J\setminus\{i\}). $$
For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, the point
$$ y=(1-\varepsilon)v+\varepsilon u^\circ $$
still violates row $i$, lies in $\operatorname{int}(B_U)$, and satisfies all other retained rows strictly. The segment from $y$ to $u^\circ$ therefore contains a point $p_i$ such that
$$ a_i\cdot p_i=\beta_i, \qquad a_j\cdot p_i>\beta_j\quad(j\ne i), \qquad p_i\in\operatorname{int}(B_U). $$
In particular, $p_i\in P$.

For any $t\in U$, a sufficiently small positive displacement $p_i+\delta e_t$ remains in $B_U$. Upward closure gives $p_i+\delta e_t\in P$, so
$$ \beta_i+\delta a_{it} =a_i\cdot(p_i+\delta e_t) \ge\beta_i. $$
Hence $a_{it}\ge0$.

Finally, if $\beta_i\le0$, then nonnegativity of $a_i$ would imply
$$ a_i\cdot u\ge0\ge\beta_i \qquad\text{for every }u\in B_U. $$
The row would be redundant. Therefore $\beta_i>0$, proving the claim.

We will also use the local geometry at $p_i$: because all other rows and all box bounds are strict there, some neighborhood $N_i$ of $p_i$ satisfies
$$ P\cap N_i = \{u\in N_i:a_i\cdot u\ge\beta_i\}. $$

4. Constructing an attaining representation.

For each $i\in J$, output the rule
$$ w^i_t= \begin{cases} a_{it},&t\in U,\\ 0,&t\in F, \end{cases} \qquad q_i=\beta_i. $$
These weights are rational and nonnegative, and their quotas are positive.

If $F$ is nonempty, also output
$$ \sum_{t\in F}x_t\ge\sum_{t\in F}\mu_t. $$
Its quota is positive. Within $B$, every summand satisfies $x_t\le\mu_t$, so this rule holds exactly when
$$ x_t=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t\in F. $$
Together with the retained reduced rows, it therefore represents $W$ exactly.

Consequently,
$$ \dim(W)\le r+\mathbf1_{\{F\ne\varnothing\}}. $$
The right-hand side is positive: if $F$ were empty and $r=0$, we would have $W=B$, contrary to $0\notin W$.

5. Lower bound for arbitrary representations.

Consider any representation of $W$ by $d$ admissible rules,
$$ w^k\cdot x\ge q_k,\qquad k=1,\ldots,d. $$
On the face $x_F=\mu_F$, these become
$$ c_k\cdot u\ge s_k, \qquad c_k=w^k_U, \qquad s_k=q_k-w^k_F\cdot\mu_F. $$
Their intersection inside $B_U$ is exactly $P$.

First suppose $U$ is nonempty. Fix a retained row $i$ and its point $p_i$ from Section 3. At least one restricted rule with $c_k\ne0$ must be tight at $p_i$. Otherwise, all nonconstant restricted rules would be strict in a neighborhood of $p_i$. The constant restricted rules hold everywhere because they hold on the nonempty set $P$. Thus the represented set would contain a neighborhood of $p_i$, contradicting the local halfspace description in Section 3.

Choose such a tight rule. For any vector $v$ satisfying $a_i\cdot v=0$, both $p_i+\delta v$ and $p_i-\delta v$ lie in $P$ for sufficiently small $\delta>0$. Validity and tightness of the chosen rule give
$$ c_k\cdot v=0. $$
Therefore $c_k$ is proportional to $a_i$. Both vectors are nonzero and nonnegative, so the proportionality factor is positive. Tightness at $p_i$ then yields
$$ (c_k,s_k)=\lambda(a_i,\beta_i) \quad\text{for some }\lambda>0. $$

Different retained rows require different rules. Indeed, if the same rule served two rows, their equality hyperplanes would coincide. This is impossible because $p_i$ is tight for row $i$ and strict for every other retained row.

It follows that every representation has at least $r$ rules whose restrictions have nonzero normals:
$$ d\ge r. $$

Now suppose $F$ is nonempty, allowing $U$ to be empty. Choose an interior point $u^\circ$ of $P$ when $U$ is nonempty, or its unique point when $U$ is empty, and set
$$ x^\circ=(\mu_F,u^\circ). $$
Every rule with $c_k\ne0$ is strict at $u^\circ$. This follows from the same interior-point argument used in Section 3.

There must be a tight rule at $x^\circ$ with $c_k=0$. If no such rule existed, every rule would be strict at $x^\circ$. Since there are finitely many rules, decreasing any one coordinate in $F$ by a sufficiently small positive amount would preserve them all and remain inside $B$. The resulting point would be declared winning, contradicting the definition of $F$.

This tight rule with zero restricted normal is additional to the $r$ distinct rules required above. Hence
$$ d\ge r+1\qquad\text{when }F\ne\varnothing. $$

Combining construction and lower bound proves the exact formula
$$ \boxed{\dim(W)=r+\mathbf1_{\{F\ne\varnothing\}}.} $$
In particular, $W=\{\mu\}$ has dimension one, enforced by the single rule
$$ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_t\ge\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t. $$

6. Running time and output size.

The algorithm uses at most $\tau+m$ exact rational linear programs. Each has at most $\tau$ variables and $m+2\tau$ constraints.

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and finite optimal values can be computed exactly in time polynomial in the binary encoding length. This theorem permits lower-dimensional feasible regions; rational LP polynomial-time solvability is discussed, for example, by [Adler and Beling](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf). Here every program is feasible and bounded, as verified above.

Substitution, exact comparisons, and output construction require polynomial-time rational arithmetic. A conservative bound on the encoding length of each constructed LP is
$$ S=O((m+\tau+1)^2L). $$
If $T_{\mathrm{LP}}(n,h,S)$ denotes the polynomial bit-time bound of an exact rational LP solver, the total running time is
$$ O\!\left( (m+\tau)\, T_{\mathrm{LP}}\!\left(\tau,m+2\tau,O((m+\tau+1)^2L)\right) \right) +\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
Thus the total time is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. At most $m+1$ rules are output, with polynomial total binary length.

The cited [Deineko–Woeginger hardness result](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/on-the-dimension-of-simple-monotonic-games/) concerns finite simple games. The proof here uses equality of the winning regions throughout the real box: the local boundary near each $p_i$ forces a distinct rule. This establishes polynomial-time solvability for the specified continuous problem.
