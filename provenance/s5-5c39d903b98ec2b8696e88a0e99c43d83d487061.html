The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of applicant types \(T\), each with a strict ranking of at most two acceptable courses, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita course supplies \(\kappa_c\), and a rational bound \(b\), does there exist a feasible allocation with matched mass at most \(b\) that is Pareto optimal against all feasible measurable reassignments of the same population? Each individual receives at most one acceptable course or remains unassigned, ranked below acceptable courses; a reassignment is a Pareto improvement if it weakly improves every individual and strictly improves a positive mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a finite set of \(m\) courses. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a strict ranking of at most two acceptable courses, with unassignment \(\bot\) ranked last; empty lists are allowed.
The remaining input consists of binary rational supplies \(\kappa_c\geq 0\) and a bound \(b\in\mathbb{Q}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Represent the population by the disjoint union \(\Omega=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}(\{t\}\times[0,\mu_t))\), with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on each component. Individuals of the same type may receive different assignments.
An allocation is a measurable map \(f:\Omega\to C\cup\{\bot\}\) assigning each individual either one acceptable course or \(\bot\). It is feasible when \(\lambda(f^{-1}(c))\leq\kappa_c\) for every \(c\in C\); unused supply is allowed. Its matched mass is \(\lambda(\{u:f(u)\neq\bot\})\).
A feasible allocation \(g\) Pareto improves \(f\) if every individual weakly prefers \(g(u)\) to \(f(u)\), and the set of individuals strictly preferring \(g(u)\) has positive measure. An allocation is Pareto optimal if no feasible measurable allocation Pareto improves it.
Is deciding whether a Pareto optimal allocation with matched mass at most \(b\) exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
The discrete counterpart is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with unit course capacities, at most one course per applicant, and preference lists of length at most two. [Source](https://real.mtak.hu/20946/1/ParetoOptimality.pdf)
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the population atomless by representing each type as an interval of its prescribed mass; allocations may split a type among different assignments.
  • Specified feasibility as upper bounds on course occupancy, allowing unused supply, and required supplies to be nonnegative.
  • Allowed empty preference lists, consistent with the discrete definition as a strictly ordered subset of courses.
  • Kept the rational bound unrestricted because the discrete decision definition imposes no substantive threshold restriction.
  • Defined L as the total binary input length and retained weak improvement for every individual, with strict improvement required on positive mass.

Result

The continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This holds even when all course supplies are equal.

The essential point is an integrality property: after clearing denominators, every Pareto optimal allocation can be rounded to integer assignment counts without increasing its matched mass or losing Pareto optimality.

1. A finite characterization of Pareto optimality

For each type \(t\), let \(A_t\) be its acceptable courses together with \(\bot\). Represent an allocation by the masses

\(x_{t,a}=\lambda(\{u\in\{t\}\times[0,\mu_t):f(u)=a\})\), for \(a\in A_t\).

These satisfy

\(x_{t,a}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{a\in A_t}x_{t,a}=\mu_t,\qquad \ell_c(x):=\sum_{t:c\in A_t}x_{t,c}\leq\kappa_c.\)

Conversely, every matrix satisfying these constraints is realized by a measurable allocation: partition each type interval into consecutive intervals of the specified masses. Its matched mass is

\(M(x)=\sum_t\sum_{c\in A_t\setminus\{\bot\}}x_{t,c}.\)

Define a directed graph \(G_x\) on \(C\cup\{\bot\}\). Put an arc \(a\to c\) whenever some type \(t\) satisfies \(x_{t,a}>0\) and strictly prefers \(c\) to \(a\). No arc enters \(\bot\). Call course \(c\) full when \(\ell_c(x)=\kappa_c\).

Lemma 1. An allocation with mass matrix \(x\) is Pareto optimal if and only if:

  • every course receiving an arc of \(G_x\) is full;
  • \(G_x\) is acyclic.

Proof. Suppose an arc \(a\to c\) enters a course with positive unused supply. Choose a witnessing type \(t\). A positive mass smaller than both \(x_{t,a}\) and the unused supply can be moved from \(a\) to \(c\), strictly improving those individuals and preserving feasibility.

The required measurable subset exists because Lebesgue measure is nonatomic. Explicitly, for a measurable subset \(E\) of a type interval, the function \(s\mapsto\lambda(E\cap[0,s))\) is continuous and takes every value between \(0\) and \(\lambda(E)\).

Next suppose \(G_x\) has a directed cycle with distinct vertices \(c_1,\ldots,c_r\), indexed cyclically. For each arc \(c_i\to c_{i+1}\), choose a witnessing type and a subset of its individuals currently assigned \(c_i\), all subsets having the same sufficiently small positive mass. These subsets are disjoint because their current courses differ. Move each subset to the next course. Every course load remains unchanged, and every moved individual strictly improves. Thus Pareto optimality implies both conditions.

Conversely, suppose both conditions hold and \(g\) Pareto improves \(f\). For distinct assignments \(a,c\), let \(w_{a,c}\) be the mass moving from \(a\) under \(f\) to \(c\) under \(g\). Since preferences are strict and nobody becomes worse off, every positive \(w_{a,c}\) follows an arc of \(G_x\). At least one such quantity is positive.

Write \(I_c\) and \(O_c\) for the total mass entering and leaving course \(c\). Whenever \(I_c>0\), condition 1 says that \(c\) was full. Feasibility of \(g\) therefore gives

\(\kappa_c+I_c-O_c\leq\kappa_c,\qquad\text{hence }O_c\geq I_c>0.\)

Starting with any positive transition, its destination consequently has a positive outgoing transition. Continuing through the finite set of courses produces a directed cycle in \(G_x\), contradicting condition 2. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

In particular, Pareto optimality depends only on the mass matrix, including when allocations differ on null sets.

The same characterization holds for discrete applicants with integer course capacities. For necessity, an unused integer capacity permits moving one applicant, and a cycle permits moving one applicant from each distinct source course. For sufficiency, the preceding transition argument applies with counts instead of masses.

2. Preserving Pareto optimality on a polytope

Let \(x\) be Pareto optimal, and let

\(J=\{c\in C:\ell_c(x)=\kappa_c\}.\)

Consider all feasible mass matrices \(y\) satisfying the additional restrictions

\(y_{t,a}=0\ \text{whenever }x_{t,a}=0,\qquad \ell_c(y)=\kappa_c\ \text{for every }c\in J.\)

Lemma 2. Every such matrix \(y\) is Pareto optimal.

Proof. The zero-coordinate restrictions imply \(G_y\subseteq G_x\), so \(G_y\) is acyclic. Every course receiving an arc of \(G_y\) also receives an arc of \(G_x\); Lemma 1 says that this course belongs to \(J\). The imposed equalities keep it full at \(y\). Both conditions of Lemma 1 hold. \(\square\)

3. Rounding without increasing matched mass

Lemma 3. Suppose \(D\) is a positive integer for which every \(D\mu_t\) and \(D\kappa_c\) is an integer. From any Pareto optimal allocation with matrix \(x\), one can obtain a Pareto optimal allocation with matrix \(y\) satisfying

\(Dy_{t,a}\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}\quad\text{for all }t,a,\qquad M(y)\leq M(x).\)

Proof. Scale the polytope in Lemma 2 by \(D\). Its variables \(z_{t,a}\) satisfy

\(\sum_{a\in A_t}z_{t,a}=D\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:c\in A_t}z_{t,c}\leq D\kappa_c,\qquad z_{t,a}\geq0,\)

together with the specified zero coordinates and equality for courses in \(J\). Add the redundant upper bound \(D\) on the \(\bot\) column. All row totals and column bounds are now integers.

We prove that every extreme point of this polytope is integral. Suppose an extreme point \(z\) has a noninteger coordinate. Form a bipartite graph whose vertices are types and assignment options, and whose edges are exactly the noninteger coordinates.

A type vertex cannot have degree one: its integer row total, minus its other integer coordinates, would force the remaining coordinate to be integer. Likewise, an assignment column that is at its integer upper bound cannot have degree one. Thus every leaf of this graph is a column with strictly positive slack.

If the graph contains a cycle, that cycle has even length. Alternately increase and decrease its coordinates by a sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Both choices of sign preserve every row and column sum and preserve nonnegativity. They produce distinct feasible points whose midpoint is \(z\), contradicting extremality.

Otherwise, take a connected component containing an edge. It is a finite tree and has at least two leaves, both slack columns. Along the path between these leaves, alternately increase and decrease coordinates by \(\varepsilon\). All row sums and internal column sums remain unchanged. Only the two slack endpoint columns change. Choosing \(\varepsilon\) smaller than their slacks and the affected positive coordinates makes both signs feasible. This again contradicts extremality.

Hence every extreme point is integral.

The polytope is nonempty because it contains \(Dx\), and it is bounded because every coordinate is bounded by its row total. A linear objective on a nonempty bounded polytope attains its minimum at an extreme point. Choose an extreme point \(z\) minimizing

\(\sum_t\sum_{c\in A_t\setminus\{\bot\}}z_{t,c}.\)

It is integral, and its objective is no larger than the objective at \(Dx\). Setting \(y=z/D\) gives the asserted bound on matched mass. Lemma 2 proves Pareto optimality, and interval partitions realize \(y\) measurably. \(\square\)

This argument uses only the given support types; it does not expand the population into \(D\) individuals.

4. A polynomial reduction from the discrete problem

Use the discrete problem established as \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete in Theorem 4 of the [cited paper](https://real.mtak.hu/20946/1/ParetoOptimality.pdf): applicants receive at most one course, course capacities are one, preference lists have length at most two, and the question asks for a Pareto optimal matching of cardinality at most an integer \(k\).

Let a discrete instance have \(n\geq1\) applicants. Merge applicants with identical preference lists. If type \(t\) occurs \(n_t\) times, construct the continuous instance with

\(\mu_t=\frac{n_t}{n},\qquad \kappa_c=\frac1n\ \text{for every course }c,\qquad b=\frac{k}{n}.\)

Only occurring lists are included, so all masses are positive, their sum is one, and \(\tau\leq n\). The construction has polynomial size.

Forward direction. Let the discrete instance have a Pareto optimal matching of cardinality at most \(k\). Define \(x_{t,a}\) as the number of type-\(t\) applicants assigned \(a\), divided by \(n\).

The discrete and continuous improvement graphs are identical, and a course is full in one exactly when it is full in the other. The discrete characterization following Lemma 1 therefore proves that \(x\) is Pareto optimal in the continuous instance. Its matched mass is at most \(k/n=b\).

Reverse direction. Suppose the continuous instance has a Pareto optimal allocation of matched mass at most \(k/n\). Apply Lemma 3 with \(D=n\), which is valid because

\(n\mu_t=n_t\in\mathbb Z,\qquad n\kappa_c=1.\)

Obtain a Pareto optimal matrix \(y\) with integer counts \(z_{t,a}=ny_{t,a}\) and no larger matched mass. For each type, distribute its \(n_t\) discrete applicants among assignments according to these integer counts. The row sums ensure that every applicant gets exactly one assignment, possibly \(\bot\); the course inequalities ensure unit capacities.

This discrete matching has the same improvement graph and fullness conditions as \(y\), so it is Pareto optimal. Its cardinality is

\(\sum_t\sum_{c\in A_t\setminus\{\bot\}}z_{t,c}=nM(y)\leq k.\)

Thus the reduction preserves both yes and no instances, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

5. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and running times

For a general continuous input, let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators of all input rationals, including \(b\). Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and multiplication computes it in polynomial time.

By Lemma 3, every yes instance has a witness consisting of integer counts \(z_{t,a}\) with

\(0\leq z_{t,a}\leq D,\qquad x_{t,a}=z_{t,a}/D.\)

There are at most \(3\tau\) counts, so the certificate has \(O(\tau L)\) bits.

A verifier checks:

  • the integer row sums \(D\mu_t\);
  • the course bounds \(D\kappa_c\);
  • the matched-count bound \(Db\);
  • acyclicity of the improvement graph;
  • fullness of every course receiving an improvement arc.

Lemma 1 proves that these checks are sufficient. The graph has \(m+1\) vertices and at most \(3\tau\) arcs. Using elementary integer arithmetic, verification takes \(O((m+\tau)L^2)\) bit operations. Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

For completeness, an exact deterministic algorithm takes \(2^{3\tau}\operatorname{poly}(m+\tau,L)\) time. Enumerate subsets \(S\) of the at most \(3\tau\) type-assignment pairs. Construct the improvement graph treating pairs in \(S\) as positive. If it is acyclic, solve a rational linear feasibility problem imposing zero outside \(S\), fullness of all graph targets, feasibility, and matched mass at most \(b\). Any feasible solution is Pareto optimal by Lemma 1. Conversely, any desired allocation is found when \(S\) equals its positive support.

Therefore the target question is settled: the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
