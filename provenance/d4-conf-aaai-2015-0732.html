The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), complete ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a preferred candidate \(p\), an integer budget \(k\), and a bundle map \(\beta:C\to 2^C\) with \(c\in\beta(c)\), does there exist \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|\le k\) and \(p\notin\beta(S)\) such that \(p\) is a Borda co-winner after deleting \(\beta(S)=\bigcup_{s\in S}\beta(s)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

A type \(t\in T\) is a strict total order of \(C\), with binary-encoded rational mass \(\mu_t\geq 0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A bundle map satisfies \(c\in\beta(c)\) for every \(c\in C\), and \(\beta(S)=\bigcup_{s\in S}\beta(s)\).
For a chosen \(S\subseteq C\), let \(D=\beta(S)\). Rankings are restricted to \(C\setminus D\), and each survivor \(c\) has Borda score \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,|\{d\in C\setminus D:t\text{ ranks }c\text{ above }d\}|\). Candidate \(p\) is a co-winner if its score is maximal.

Target

Is the weighted-type Borda-Comb-CCDC problem defined above NP-hard, or solvable in polynomial time?

Nearest known results

For finite voter lists, Borda-CCDC is NP-hard even with ten voters.
For finite voter lists with bundles, Borda-Comb-CCDC is NP-hard even with one voter, since Borda satisfies unanimity.

Hardness: the one-voter instances embed exactly

Use the stated known result: finite-list Borda-Comb-CCDC is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with one voter. This also follows from Theorem 6 of [*Elections with Few Voters: Candidate Control Can Be Easy*](https://elektronaj.github.io/newzutot/papers/fewvotersaaai.pdf), which applies to voting rules satisfying unanimity. Borda satisfies that hypothesis: a unanimously first-ranked candidate scores strictly more than every other candidate in each vote, and hence in their sum.

Take any instance of that one-voter problem. Write \(t\) for its voter's strict ranking. Construct a weighted-type instance by retaining \(C\), \(p\), \(\beta\), and \(k\), and setting \(T=\{t\}\) and \(\mu_t=1\).

This is a polynomial-time construction. The mass is a binary-encoded rational of constant encoding length, is nonnegative, and satisfies the required normalization.

To verify correctness, fix any \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|\leq k\), and put \(D=\beta(S)\). Both instances delete exactly \(D\). If \(p\in D\), the action is unsuccessful in both instances. Otherwise, for every survivor \(c\in C\setminus D\), its weighted score is

\(\operatorname{score}_{\mathrm{weighted}}(c)=1\cdot|\{d\in C\setminus D:t\text{ ranks }c\text{ above }d\}|=\operatorname{score}_{\mathrm{one\ voter}}(c).\)

Thus all surviving candidates have identical scores in the two instances. In particular, \(p\) is a co-winner in one if and only if it is a co-winner in the other.

The construction therefore preserves yes-instances and no-instances, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Neither rational weights nor normalization weaken this conclusion: the hard instances already use the single mass \(1\).

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is the selected set \(S\), represented by at most \(|C|\) candidate identifiers. A verifier checks \(|S|\leq k\), computes \(D=\beta(S)\), and rejects if \(p\in D\).

It remains to verify that score comparisons take polynomial time despite rational masses. Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with integers \(a_t\geq0\) and \(b_t\geq1\), and define \(Q=\prod_{t\in T}b_t\).

The binary length of \(Q\) is at most the sum of the binary lengths of the denominators, up to a constant. It is therefore polynomial in the input length.

For each survivor \(c\), compute the integer

\(N_c=\sum_{t\in T}a_t(Q/b_t)\,|\{d\in C\setminus D:t\text{ ranks }c\text{ above }d\}|.\)

Every count is computable from the explicitly supplied ranking and lies between \(0\) and \(|C|-1\). All intermediate integers have polynomial binary length, so these computations take polynomial time. Since \(Q>0\) and \(N_c=Q\operatorname{score}(c)\), the verifier accepts precisely when \(N_p\geq N_c\) for every survivor \(c\).

Consequently, the budgeted problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\) and is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. If no budget was intended, that is a different decision problem; the hardness reduction above specifically preserves the standard budget constraint.
