The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a designated candidate \(d\), and a rational baseline distribution \(\nu\) over complete ranking types \(T\), decide whether a controllable coalition of mass \(\rho\) can choose \(x_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t=\rho\), so that \(d\) wins under Borda in the normalized society \((\nu+x)/(1+\rho)\); the optimization version minimizes \(\rho\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq 2\) candidates, let \(d\in C\), and let \(T\) be all complete strict rankings of \(C\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(m!\).
The baseline distribution \(\nu\) has total mass \(1\); the input also includes a binary rational \(\rho\geq 0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The coalition may choose real masses \(x_t\geq 0\) for every \(t\in T\), including rankings outside the baseline support, with \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t=\rho\).
Decide whether \(d\) wins under Borda in \((\nu+x)/(1+\rho)\), with scores aggregated by mass and ties resolved in favor of \(d\). The optimization version minimizes \(\rho\).

Is this continuous decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete unweighted Borda coalitional manipulation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with two manipulators.
The continuous question is already settled: [Xia, Conitzer, and Procaccia](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf) give polynomial-time exact algorithms for divisible weighted coalitional manipulation and minimum manipulating weight under positional scoring rules, covering this decision problem and its optimization version.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined the ballot domain as all complete strict rankings, including rankings absent from the baseline support, matching unrestricted discrete manipulation.
  • Specified baseline mass summing to one and an input coalition mass that is a nonnegative binary rational; chosen coalition masses may be real.
  • Resolved winning ties in favor of the designated candidate, matching the discrete definition.
  • Restricted to at least two candidates to exclude the vacuous single-candidate case.

Result

The continuous decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The minimum coalition mass is also exactly computable in polynomial time.

This agrees with Theorem 3.4 of [Xia, Conitzer, and Procaccia, *A Scheduling Approach to Coalitional Manipulation*](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf), which gives exact polynomial-time algorithms for divisible weighted manipulation and minimum manipulating weight under positional scoring rules. Below is a direct linear-programming proof for the stated input model.

1. Baseline scores and putting the target first

For a ranking \(t\), let \(\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\) be candidate \(c\)'s position, numbered from \(1\). Define its baseline Borda score by

\[ b_c=\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)}\nu_t\bigl(m-\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr). \]

Because normalization divides every score by the same positive number \(1+\rho\), it does not affect the winner. We therefore work with the unnormalized masses \(\nu+x\).

Lemma 1. If a successful coalition of mass \(\rho\) exists, one exists in which every coalition ranking puts \(d\) first.

Proof. Move \(d\) to first place in each coalition ranking, preserving the relative order of all other candidates. If \(d\) originally occupied position \(k\), its Borda score increases by \(k-1\). Each candidate formerly above \(d\) loses one point, and every other candidate's score is unchanged. Thus the score difference between \(d\) and any rival never decreases.

Applying this operation to every coalition ranking and combining masses assigned to identical resulting rankings preserves total mass and preserves \(d\)'s victory. \(\square\)

Henceforth all coalition rankings put \(d\) first. Its coalition score is then exactly \((m-1)\rho\).

2. A polynomial-size linear program

Let \(R=C\setminus\{d\}\), so \(|R|=m-1\). For \(c\in R\) and \(j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}\), introduce a variable \(y_{c,j}\), interpreted as the coalition mass placing \(c\) in position \(j\).

Consider the following feasibility system:

\[ y_{c,j}\geq 0 \qquad(c\in R,\;2\leq j\leq m), \]

\[ \sum_{j=2}^{m}y_{c,j}=\rho \qquad(c\in R), \]

\[ \sum_{c\in R}y_{c,j}=\rho \qquad(2\leq j\leq m), \]

\[ b_c+\sum_{j=2}^{m}(m-j)y_{c,j} \leq b_d+(m-1)\rho \qquad(c\in R). \]

The row equations express that every rival appears once in every ranking. The column equations express that every position below \(d\) contains exactly one rival. The final inequalities express that \(d\)'s score is at least every rival's score, which suffices because ties favor \(d\).

The system has \((m-1)^2\) variables. The remaining issue is whether these marginal constraints describe actual mixtures of complete rankings.

3. Every feasible matrix comes from legal rankings

We prove the required matrix-decomposition fact, including for real entries.

Lemma 2. Let \(Y\) be a nonnegative square matrix of order \(n\), with every row sum and column sum equal to a real number \(a\geq 0\). Then

\[ Y=\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\lambda_\ell P_\ell, \qquad \lambda_\ell>0, \qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\lambda_\ell=a, \]

where each \(P_\ell\) is a permutation matrix and \(q\leq n^2\). When \(a=0\), the sum is empty.

Proof. If \(a=0\), nonnegativity forces \(Y=0\).

Suppose \(a>0\). Form a bipartite graph whose left vertices are rows, whose right vertices are columns, and whose edges correspond to positive matrix entries. For a subset \(A\) of rows, let \(N(A)\) be its neighboring columns. Then

\[ a|A| =\sum_{i\in A}\sum_jY_{i,j} =\sum_{i\in A}\sum_{j\in N(A)}Y_{i,j} \leq\sum_i\sum_{j\in N(A)}Y_{i,j} =a|N(A)|. \]

Dividing by \(a>0\) gives \(|N(A)|\geq|A|\).

Hall's marriage theorem states that a finite bipartite graph has a matching covering its left side if every subset of left vertices has at least as many neighbors. Its hypothesis has just been verified. Since both sides have \(n\) vertices, this matching is perfect and defines a permutation matrix \(P\) supported on positive entries.

Let \(\delta\) be the minimum entry of \(Y\) on this matching. Then \(\delta>0\), and \(Y-\delta P\) is nonnegative with every row and column sum equal to \(a-\delta\). At least one positive entry becomes zero, and no zero entry becomes positive.

Repeat while the residual common row sum is positive. Each iteration eliminates at least one positive entry, so there are at most \(n^2\) iterations. The final residual is zero: otherwise its positive common row sum would permit another iteration. The subtracted coefficients sum to \(a\), as follows by comparing any row sum. \(\square\)

Proposition. The linear system in Section 2 is feasible if and only if a successful coalition of mass \(\rho\) exists.

Proof.

If a successful coalition exists, Lemma 1 lets us assume that all its rankings put \(d\) first. Define \(y_{c,j}\) by summing the masses of rankings placing \(c\) at position \(j\). Every ranking contains each rival and fills each remaining position exactly once, so the row and column equations hold. The score inequalities follow from \(d\)'s victory.

Conversely, suppose the system is feasible. Apply Lemma 2 to its matrix \(Y=(y_{c,j})\), taking \(n=m-1\) and \(a=\rho\). Each permutation matrix assigns the rivals bijectively to positions \(2,\ldots,m\); prepend \(d\) to obtain a complete strict ranking. Assign this ranking the corresponding coefficient \(\lambda_\ell\).

The resulting coalition has total mass \(\rho\) and position masses exactly \(Y\). Its scores therefore satisfy the linear system's victory inequalities.

If \(\rho=0\), the matrix is zero, and the system simply tests whether \(d\) already has maximum baseline score. \(\square\)

These constructed rankings may lie outside the baseline support, exactly as permitted in the question.

4. Exact polynomial-time decision

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility of a finite linear system with rational coefficients can be decided in time polynomial in its binary encoding length; when feasible, a rational feasible solution can be produced.

Here its hypotheses and encoding requirements hold:

  • Computing all baseline scores requires polynomially many arithmetic operations, using only the \(\tau\) supplied rankings.
  • A common denominator for those scores is the product of the input mass denominators. Its bit length is at most the sum of their bit lengths, hence polynomial in \(L\).
  • Since the baseline has total mass \(1\), every score satisfies \(0\leq b_c\leq m-1\). Consequently the resulting score numerators also have polynomial bit length.
  • The linear system has polynomially many variables and constraints. Its other coefficients are integers bounded in absolute value by \(m-1\), and \(\rho\) is supplied as a binary rational.

Thus the system has encoding length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). By the proposition, exact rational linear programming decides the original problem in polynomial time.

Although real coalition masses are allowed, they cause no additional difficulty: every feasible instance admits a rational feasible matrix, and Lemma 2 converts that matrix into rational ranking masses.

5. Exact minimum coalition mass

Replace the fixed parameter \(\rho\) in the system by a variable \(r\geq0\), and minimize \(r\). All constraints remain linear.

To verify feasibility and attainment, observe that mass \(r=m-1\) placed on any single ranking with \(d\) first succeeds. Indeed, for every rival \(c\),

\[ b_d-b_c\geq-(m-1), \]

while each unit of that coalition mass gives \(d\) at least one more Borda point than \(c\). Hence its final score difference is at least

\[ -(m-1)+(m-1)=0. \]

We may therefore add the bound \(0\leq r\leq m-1\) without changing the optimum. Every matrix entry then satisfies \(0\leq y_{c,j}\leq m-1\), by nonnegativity and its row equation. The feasible region is nonempty, closed, and bounded, so the continuous objective attains its minimum.

The exact rational linear-programming theorem also computes the optimum of a feasible rational linear program with bounded objective in polynomial time. Its hypotheses hold here, and the encoding remains polynomial. Thus the minimum mass is an exactly computable rational number. Lemma 2 reconstructs an optimal coalition supported on at most \((m-1)^2\) rankings.

The discrete hardness result concerns indivisible ballots. In the stated continuous problem, arbitrary nonnegative fractional coefficients make the matrix formulation exact.
