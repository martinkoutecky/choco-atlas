The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a depth-two tree \(G=(V,E)\), candidates \(C\), a preference map \(\chi:V\to C\), target \(p\), district count \(k\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\), decide whether \(V\) can be partitioned into exactly \(k\) nonempty connected districts such that \(p\) uniquely leads more districts than any other candidate leads. Each vertex’s mass is assigned wholly to its district; in district \(D\), candidate \(c\)'s support is \(\sum_{v\in D:\chi(v)=c}\mu_v\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: a finite simple undirected tree \(G=(V,E)\) with a specified root whose maximum distance to a vertex is \(2\), candidates \(C\) with \(m=|C|\geq2\), a preference map \(\chi:V\to C\), a target \(p\in C\), and an integer \(1\leq k\leq |V|\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here types are distinct geographical vertices, including their candidate preferences; \(\tau=|V|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses satisfy \(\mu_v>0\) and \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
A feasible partition consists of exactly \(k\) nonempty districts \(\mathcal D\), each inducing a connected subgraph. Each vertex and its entire mass belong to one district; there are no district population constraints.
For a district \(D\), define \(s_c(D)=\sum_{v\in D:\chi(v)=c}\mu_v\) and \(T(D)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c\in C}s_c(D)\). Every member of \(T(D)\) leads; a candidate wins only when it is the sole member. Ties are not broken.
The decision problem asks whether such a partition satisfies \(|\{D\in\mathcal D:T(D)=\{p\}\}|>|\{D\in\mathcal D:c\in T(D)\}|\) for every \(c\in C\setminus\{p\}\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard?
If it is NP-hard, is it FPT parameterized by \(k\)?

Nearest known results: unit-weight Gerrymandering on depth-two trees is \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by \(k\) ([Fraser–Lavallee–Sullivan](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.06857v2)).
With positive integer vertex weights and the same district and tie conditions, Gerrymandering is strongly NP-complete on trees of diameter four ([Ito–Kamiyama–Kobayashi–Okamoto](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2019/pdfs/p1413.pdf)). No separately stated result for normalized rational masses was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted support types as geographical vertices with preferences, preserving distinct vertices even when their preferred candidates coincide.
  • Made depth two precise by specifying a root with maximum vertex distance exactly two.
  • Made the district-count domain explicit as an integer between one and the support size.
  • Required at least two candidates to exclude a vacuous comparison against no rivals.
  • Resolved tie handling using the discrete definition: all tied maximizers lead, while only a sole maximizer wins.
  • Defined m as the candidate count and L as total binary input length.

Result and encoding convention

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete and \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by \(k\). Consequently, it is not FPT parameterized by \(k\) unless \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[2]\).

Here “binary rational” means an exact rational whose numerator and denominator are encoded in binary. This is the usual rational input model; it does not require a finite binary expansion.

1. Normalization preserves every feasible solution

Consider an instance with positive integer vertex weights \(w_v\). Define
\(W=\sum_{v\in V}w_v\) and \(\mu_v=w_v/W\).

All masses are positive and sum to one. The graph, its vertices, their preferences, the candidates, and \(k\) remain unchanged. In particular, the support size remains \(\tau=|V|\).

For every nonempty district \(D\) and candidate \(c\),
\(s_c(D)=W^{-1}\sum_{v\in D:\chi(v)=c}w_v\).
Multiplication by the positive constant \(W^{-1}\) preserves strict inequalities and equalities. Therefore the entire set \(T(D)\) is unchanged. This preserves both the number of districts that \(p\) wins alone and the number that each opponent leads, including tied districts.

The transformation has polynomial output length. If every input weight has at most \(b\) bits, then \(W<\tau 2^b\), so its binary length is at most \(b+\lceil\log_2\tau\rceil+1\). Each output mass therefore uses \(O(b+\log\tau)\) bits. Computing \(W\) and writing these fractions takes polynomial time; reducing the fractions, if required by the encoding, also takes polynomial time.

Thus normalization is a polynomial-time reduction that preserves \(k\) exactly.

2. NP-hardness on the required rooted trees

Theorem 3.3 of Ito–Kamiyama–Kobayashi–Okamoto states that integer-weight Gerrymandering is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete on trees of diameter four. Its definition requires exactly \(k\) nonempty connected districts and compares \(p\)'s sole victories against every opponent's appearances among the district leaders. Its vertex weights are positive integers. These are precisely the district and tie conditions in the question. [Source: definitions in Section 2 and Theorem 3.3](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2019/pdfs/p1413.pdf).

A diameter-four tree can be rooted with maximum root distance exactly two. For completeness, take a diameter path \(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4\) and root at \(x_2\). For any vertex \(v\), let its path to this diameter path first meet it at \(x_i\), and put \(h=\operatorname{dist}(v,x_i)\). Since neither endpoint can be farther than four from \(v\),
\(h+i\leq4\) and \(h+4-i\leq4\).
Hence \(h\leq\min(i,4-i)\), and
\(\operatorname{dist}(v,x_2)=h+|i-2|\leq2\).
The endpoints attain distance two. Finding this root takes polynomial time.

Apply the normalization from Section 1 to the cited hard instances and specify this root. Every resulting instance satisfies the required positivity, normalization, support, and depth conditions, and has exactly the same successful partitions. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Membership in NP with exact arithmetic

Write each mass as \(\mu_v=a_v/b_v\), with positive binary integers \(a_v,b_v\). Define
\(Q=\prod_{v\in V}b_v\) and \(A_v=a_v(Q/b_v)\).
Then \(\mu_v=A_v/Q\).

Because \(L\) includes all numerator and denominator encodings, \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits. The validity condition \(\mu_v\leq1\) implies \(A_v\leq Q\). Moreover,
\(\sum_{v\in V}A_v=Q\),
so every district score computed using the integer weights \(A_v\) also has \(O(L)\) bits. These integer scores preserve every leader set exactly.

A certificate assigns each vertex a district index in \(\{1,\ldots,k\}\). Its length is \(O(\tau\log(k+1))\). Verification proceeds as follows:

  • Check that every district is nonempty and that its induced subgraph is connected.
  • Sum the integers \(A_v\) separately for each district and candidate.
  • Determine every district's full leader set, recording a victory for \(p\) only when that set is \(\{p\}\).
  • Check all \(m-1\) required strict inequalities.

Using ordinary integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for preprocessing and verification is
\(O((\tau+mk+1)(L+1)^2)\) bit operations. In particular, verification is polynomial in the total binary input length. Therefore the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), and Section 2 proves that it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

4. Parameterized hardness

Theorem 3.4 of Fraser–Lavallee–Sullivan proves that Unit Weight Gerrymandering is \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by the number of districts on depth-two trees. Their formal definition counts sole victories for \(p\) against all districts led by each opponent. The construction supplies a root of maximum distance exactly two, has multiple candidates, and uses \(k=t+3\), where \(t\) is the source Set Cover parameter. [Source: Section 2, Definition 3.2, and Theorem 3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.06857v2).

For each such unit-weight instance, assign every vertex the mass \(\mu_v=1/\tau\). By Section 1, this preserves every leader set and the answer. It preserves \(k\), and the mass list uses \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) bits.

Composing this polynomial, parameter-preserving normalization with the cited parameterized reduction proves \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness for the present problem, even when all masses are equal.

An FPT algorithm here would have running time
\(f(k)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\)
for some computable function \(f\), with a constant exponent independent of \(k\). Such an algorithm would make the cited \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard problem FPT and imply \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[2]\). Thus the negative FPT conclusion is conditional on the standard separation assumption.

5. An explicit XP upper bound

Every partition of a tree into exactly \(k\) connected districts corresponds to deleting exactly \(k-1\) edges.

Indeed, the induced tree on a district \(D\) contains \(|D|-1\) edges. Summing over the districts gives \(\tau-k\) internal edges. Since the tree has \(\tau-1\) edges, exactly \(k-1\) edges cross between districts. Deleting those edges yields precisely the districts. Conversely, deleting \(k-1\) edges from a tree produces exactly \(k\) nonempty connected components.

Enumerate all \(\binom{\tau-1}{k-1}\) deletion sets and verify their component partitions by Section 3. A conservative total running time is
\(O\!\left(\left[1+\binom{\tau-1}{k-1}\right](\tau+mk+1)(L+1)^2\right)\).

Since \(\binom{\tau-1}{k-1}\leq\tau^{k-1}\), this is an XP algorithm parameterized by \(k\), and is polynomial for every fixed \(k\). It does not provide an FPT bound because its exponent depends on \(k\).
