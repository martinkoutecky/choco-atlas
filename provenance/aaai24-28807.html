The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), approval cap \(q\), replacement target \(\ell\), rational masses \(\mu\) over approval types \(S \subseteq C\), and fixed tie-breaking, let \(W\) be the AV committee. Add fresh candidates \(D\) of size \(\ell\) and nonnegative masses \(y_B\) over approval types \(B \subseteq C \cup D\) with \(|B| \le q\). Minimize the total added mass \(x = \sum_B y_B\) subject to the resulting tie-broken AV committee replacing exactly \(\ell\) members of \(W\) by \(D\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\) candidates, with integers \(1\leq\ell\leq k\leq m\) and \(1\leq q\leq m+\ell\). Let \(D\) be a specified set of \(\ell\) fresh candidates, disjoint from \(C\), and fix an input total order on \(C\cup D\) for breaking score ties.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) approval types \(S\subseteq C\) with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_S\) a binary rational, with \(|S|\leq q\) and \(\sum_S\mu_S=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval sets.
Let \(W\) be the size-\(k\) approval-voting (AV) committee of this society, using the fixed order restricted to \(C\).
An addition assigns nonnegative real masses \(y_B\) to approval types \(B\subseteq C\cup D\) with \(|B|\leq q\), at cost \(x=\sum_B y_B\). Original ballots and masses remain unchanged; added mass is measured relative to the original total mass.
Let \(W_y\) be the resulting size-\(k\) AV committee using the fixed order. Success means \(D\subseteq W_y\) and \(W_y\cap C\subseteq W\), so exactly \(\ell\) original winners are replaced by \(D\).
Given a nonnegative binary-rational budget \(b\), ask whether a successful addition with \(x\leq b\) exists. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k,q,\ell\).

Is this continuous AV replacement budget-feasibility problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finitely listed weighted voters, AV replacement cost can be evaluated in \(O(1)\) time given the relevant original winner’s approval weight, under unrestricted added ballot length and tie-breaking favoring fresh candidates. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.11408)
That result already permits divisible voting weights, but does not establish the capped, arbitrary-fixed-order problem above; no result settling this exact formulation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized original masses to sum to one, following the weighted-election definition; added mass is measured against this original total.
  • Specified positive integer domains for committee size, replacement target, and approval cap, with the replacement target at most the committee size.
  • Applied the approval cap to original approval types as well as added types, consistent with the ballot-length restriction in the application.
  • Interpreted fixed tie-breaking as an input total order on all original and fresh candidates, retaining the graded question’s arbitrary fixed order.
  • Made exact replacement explicit: every fresh candidate wins, and every retained original candidate belongs to the original winning committee.
  • Allowed nonnegative real added masses and ballots containing both original and fresh candidates, as stated in the graded question.
  • Used exact budget feasibility to express the minimization problem’s complexity without assuming that a minimum is attained under arbitrary fixed tie-breaking.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact classification

The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). In fact, its feasibility criterion has a closed form, including the effect of arbitrary fixed tie-breaking.

The cited [Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.11408) establishes the unrestricted-ballot formula under tie-breaking favoring fresh candidates. The proof below handles the ballot cap and the specified order directly.

Definitions and criterion

Write \(\pi(c)\) for the position of candidate \(c\) in the fixed order, with smaller positions winning ties. Define each original approval score by
\(a(c)=\sum_{S\ni c}\mu_S\).

Order the original candidates as \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\) by decreasing score, breaking ties by increasing \(\pi\). Thus \(W=\{c_1,\ldots,c_k\}\).

Set
\(r=k-\ell\), \(p=c_{r+1}\), \(t=a(p)\), \(h=\min\{q,\ell\}\), and \(\beta=\ell t/h\).

Theorem. A successful addition of cost at most \(b\) exists exactly when either:

  • \(b>\beta\); or
  • \(b=\beta\) and \(\pi(d)<\pi(p)\) for every \(d\in D\).

In particular, scores equal to zero cause no exception: feasibility at zero budget is governed by the same tie-order condition.

Proof of necessity

Consider any successful addition of cost \(x\). For each candidate \(c\), let
\(z(c)=\sum_{B\ni c}y_B\)
be its added approval score. Its resulting score is \(a(c)+z(c)\) for \(c\in C\), and \(z(c)\) for \(c\in D\).

Since every added ballot has mass counted in \(x\), and approves at most \(q\) candidates,
\(z(d)\leq x\) for every \(d\in D\), and
\(\sum_{d\in D}z(d)\leq qx\).

Success selects exactly \(r\) original candidates. Consequently, at least one candidate \(e\in\{c_1,\ldots,c_{r+1}\}\) is excluded.

Candidate \(e\) originally ranks at least as highly as \(p\). Its score cannot decrease under addition. Moreover, every selected candidate must outrank every excluded candidate under the resulting score and tie order. Therefore, every \(d\in D\) must outrank the original score-order position of \(p\). Explicitly,

  • \(z(d)\geq t\) for every \(d\in D\);
  • if \(\pi(d)>\pi(p)\), then \(z(d)>t\).

To verify the strict assertion, suppose instead that \(z(d)=t\) and \(\pi(d)>\pi(p)\). If \(a(e)>t\), then \(e\) outranks \(d\) by score. Otherwise \(a(e)=t\), and the original ordering gives \(\pi(e)\leq\pi(p)<\pi(d)\); hence \(e\) still outranks \(d\). Increasing \(e\)'s score cannot change this conclusion, contradicting its exclusion.

The score requirements imply \(x\geq t\) and \(qx\geq\ell t\). Thus
\(x\geq\max\{t,\ell t/q\}=\beta\).

It remains to characterize feasibility at \(b=\beta\). The lower bound forces \(x=\beta\), and every fresh candidate then has score exactly \(t\):

  • If \(q\geq\ell\), then \(\beta=t\), so \(t\leq z(d)\leq x=t\).
  • If \(q<\ell\), then \(\sum_{d\in D}z(d)\leq qx=q\beta=\ell t\). Since all \(\ell\) summands are at least \(t\), each equals \(t\).

The strict requirement above therefore rules out any fresh candidate appearing after \(p\). This proves necessity.

Proof of sufficiency

We first construct added ballots giving every fresh candidate any prescribed score \(s\geq0\), while giving no additional approvals to original candidates.

Label \(D=\{d_0,\ldots,d_{\ell-1}\}\). For each \(i\in\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}\), use the ballot
\(B_i=\{d_i,d_{i+1},\ldots,d_{i+h-1}\}\),
where indices are taken modulo \(\ell\), with mass \(s/h\).

Each ballot approves \(h\leq q\) distinct candidates. Each fresh candidate belongs to exactly \(h\) of these indexed ballots, so its score is \(s\). The total cost is \(\ell s/h\). If some ballot types coincide, their masses are combined; this preserves both scores and cost.

Suppose first that \(b=\beta\) and every fresh candidate precedes \(p\). Apply the construction with \(s=t\).

Every fresh candidate outranks \(p\), by tie-breaking. Each candidate in \(T=\{c_1,\ldots,c_r\}\) also outranks \(p\), whereas every original candidate outside \(T\) ranks at or below \(p\). All original scores remain unchanged. Consequently, every member of \(T\cup D\) outranks every candidate outside that set. Since \(|T\cup D|=k\), the resulting committee is exactly \(T\cup D\). Its cost is \(\ell t/h=\beta=b\).

Now suppose that \(b>\beta\). Choose
\(s=(t+hb/\ell)/2\).

The inequality \(b>\ell t/h\) gives \(s>t\), and the construction costs
\(\ell s/h=(\beta+b)/2<b\).

Every fresh candidate now outranks \(p\) strictly by score. The same comparison with \(T\) shows that the committee is exactly \(T\cup D\). This proves sufficiency.

Algorithm and running time

The decision algorithm is:

  • Compute all original approval scores.
  • Sort the original candidates and identify \(p=c_{k-\ell+1}\).
  • Compare \(hb\) with \(\ell a(p)\) exactly.
  • Accept if the first quantity is larger; reject if it is smaller. At equality, accept exactly when every fresh candidate precedes \(p\).

Score computation requires at most \(\tau\min\{q,m\}\) score updates. Sorting requires \(O(m\log m)\) comparisons, and checking the fresh candidates requires \(O(\ell)\) order comparisons.

For a conservative explicit bit bound, use a common denominator equal to the product of the input mass denominators. Its bit length is at most \(L\). Exact score computation, comparisons, and the final budget comparison use integers of \(O(L+\log(m+\ell))\) bits. Using elementary integer arithmetic gives running time

\(O\!\left((m+\tau\min\{q,m\}+m\log m+\ell)(L+\log(m+\ell))^2\right)\).

This is polynomial in the stated input parameters. The committee size \(k\) determines only the index \(k-\ell+1\); no committees or possible ballot types need to be enumerated.

Minimum versus infimum

The infimum successful cost is always \(\beta\). It is attained exactly when every fresh candidate precedes \(p\). Otherwise, the successful costs are precisely those strictly greater than \(\beta\).

Thus the equality check is essential for budget feasibility. For example, with one original candidate of score \(1\), one fresh candidate, and \(k=\ell=q=1\), budget \(1\) succeeds exactly when the fresh candidate wins their tie.
