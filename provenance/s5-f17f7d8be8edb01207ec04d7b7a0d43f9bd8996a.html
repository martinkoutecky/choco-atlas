Theorem 2 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant-type and seat-site sets \(P,Q\), rational masses \(\mu_p,\nu_q\) summing to \(1\), ordinal rankings of \(Q\) by each \(p\in P\), and a feasible predicted transport plan \(\widehat X\), is there an ordinal mechanism running in \(\operatorname{poly}(|P|,|Q|,L)\) that returns a feasible plan \(X\) such that for every consistent line realization \(d\) of the type sites and every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), \(C_\alpha(X,d)\le \min\{C_\alpha(\widehat X,d),3\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)\}\)? Here \(C_\alpha(X,d)\) is the maximum cost of a subflow \(Y\le X\) of total mass \(\alpha\), and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)=\min_Z C_\alpha(Z,d)\) over feasible plans \(Z\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P,Q\) be disjoint finite sets of applicant types and seat sites, with \(\tau=|P|\geq1\) and \(m=|Q|\geq2\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_p\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible rankings. Seat masses \(\nu_q\) are also positive binary rationals, with \(\sum_p\mu_p=\sum_q\nu_q=1\).
Each type supplies a strict total ranking of \(Q\). A consistent realization assigns pairwise distinct real coordinates \(s_v\) to all \(v\in P\cup Q\), with \(d(u,v)=|s_u-s_v|\) and \(q\succ_p r\Rightarrow d(p,q)\leq d(p,r)\); equal distances may be ranked either way. Promise that a consistent realization exists.
A feasible plan is a nonnegative matrix \(X\) satisfying \(\sum_qX_{pq}=\mu_p\) and \(\sum_pX_{pq}=\nu_q\). Mass is divisible. The input includes a feasible binary-rational prediction \(\widehat X\), without any accuracy promise; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), define \(C_\alpha(X,d)=\max\{\sum_{p,q}Y_{pq}d(p,q):0\leq Y\leq X,\ \sum_{p,q}Y_{pq}=\alpha\}\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)=\min_{Z\text{ feasible}}C_\alpha(Z,d)\), allowing real-valued subflows and benchmark plans.
Call \(X\) admissible if, simultaneously for every consistent \(d\) and every real \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), \(C_\alpha(X,d)\leq\min\{C_\alpha(\widehat X,d),3\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)\}\). A deterministic ordinal mechanism receives only the rankings, masses, and prediction, and outputs a rational feasible plan.

Can an admissible plan always be produced by a deterministic ordinal mechanism in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), or is producing such a plan NP-hard?

For finite equal-size agent and item sets, a polynomial-time deterministic ordinal mechanism achieves the analogous simultaneous guarantee for every top-\(k\) cost and every consistent line metric. [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09038v1).
No result settling this exact divisible-mass, prediction-augmented guarantee was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined support parameters as applicant support size and seat-site count; required positive masses on both listed supports.
  • Specified complete strict rankings, with weak distance consistency and arbitrary ordering of equal distances.
  • Carried over distinct locations for distinct type and seat-site labels, and the promise of at least one consistent line realization.
  • Required at least two seat sites, carrying over the discrete nondegeneracy condition; applicant support may contain one type.
  • Defined feasible plans by exact row and column marginals, with divisible mass and an arbitrary feasible rational prediction.
  • Specified deterministic mechanisms, rational output, total input bit length, and simultaneous quantification over all real tail masses.

Result

An admissible plan can always be produced in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

The construction follows the prediction-based ordering idea in the [cited discrete paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.09038v1). Below, we prove the additional statements needed for divisible masses: optimality against real-valued benchmark plans, a factor-three bound allowing repeated coordinates, and a compressed implementation whose running time is independent of the numerical denominators.

All choices in the algorithm use only the input rankings, masses, prediction, and fixed deterministic tie-breaking.

1. Tail costs and uncrossing

For a feasible plan \(X\), define

\(H_X(t,d)=\sum_{p,q}X_{pq}(d(p,q)-t)_+\), where \(t\geq 0\) and \((z)_+=\max\{z,0\}\).

For every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\),

\(C_\alpha(X,d)=\min_{t\geq0}\{\alpha t+H_X(t,d)\}.\)

Indeed, every admissible subflow \(Y\) satisfies

\(\sum_{p,q}Y_{pq}d(p,q)\leq \alpha t+\sum_{p,q}X_{pq}(d(p,q)-t)_+.\)

Equality is attained by selecting mass on the most expensive edges first: choose a threshold \(t\), take all mass with cost strictly above \(t\), and fill the remaining required mass from edges of cost \(t\). For \(\alpha=1\), \(t=0\) suffices.

We next establish the uncrossing inequality, including coincident coordinates. If \(x\leq y\) and \(u\leq v\), then

\(\max\{|x-u|,|y-v|\}\leq\max\{|x-v|,|y-u|\}\)

and

\(|x-u|+|y-v|\leq |x-v|+|y-u|.\)

For the maximum inequality, if \(x\geq u\), then \(|x-u|\leq |y-u|\); otherwise \(|x-u|\leq |x-v|\). The corresponding two cases for \(|y-v|\) give the other bound. For the sum inequality, the function \(z\mapsto |z-u|-|z-v|\) is nondecreasing, so its value at \(x\) is at most its value at \(y\).

For nonnegative \(a,b\),

\((a-t)_++(b-t)_+=\max\{0,\max\{a,b\}-t,a+b-2t\}.\)

Consequently, uncrossing weakly decreases the sum of the two hinge costs for every \(t\). It also weakly decreases every top-\(k\) sum in a finite cost vector: a maximizing selection after the swap contains zero, one, or two affected entries, and the maximum and sum inequalities handle these three possibilities.

Assortative transport is optimal against fractional plans

Fix a consistent realization. Order the types and sites by their true coordinates:

\(s_{p_1}<\cdots<s_{p_\tau}\), and \(s_{q_1}<\cdots<s_{q_m}\).

Put

\(A_i=\sum_{h\leq i}\mu_{p_h}\), and \(B_j=\sum_{h\leq j}\nu_{q_h}\),

with \(A_0=B_0=0\). Define the assortative plan

\(X^*_{p_iq_j}=\operatorname{length}\bigl([A_{i-1},A_i]\cap[B_{j-1},B_j]\bigr).\)

This plan is feasible and minimizes \(H_X(t,d)\) for every \(t\geq0\), even among real-valued feasible plans.

To prove this, start with any feasible plan \(Z\). If its first entry is smaller than \(\min\{\mu_{p_1},\nu_{q_1}\}\), there are positive entries \(Z_{p_1q_j}\) and \(Z_{p_iq_1}\), with \(i,j>1\). Transfer an equal amount from these entries to \(Z_{p_1q_1}\) and \(Z_{p_iq_j}\). This is an uncrossing, so every hinge objective weakly decreases. Continue until the first row or column is exhausted, delete that exhausted row or column, and repeat.

This process terminates: within each stage, every transfer eliminates at least one positive entry incident to the current first row or column, without creating another such off-diagonal entry. Its final plan is exactly \(X^*\). Thus, for every feasible \(Z\),

\(H_{X^*}(t,d)\leq H_Z(t,d)\quad\text{for all }t\geq0.\)

The tail-cost formula now gives

\(C_\alpha(X^*,d)\leq C_\alpha(Z,d)\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in(0,1].\)

Therefore,

\(\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d)=C_\alpha(X^*,d).\)

In particular, one rational assortative plan minimizes all tail costs simultaneously.

2. Recovering the relevant site order

Write \(f(p)\) for type \(p\)'s first-ranked site, and let \(F=\{f(p):p\in P\}\).

If \(F\) consists of one site \(g\), define \(I=\{g\}\) and \(O=Q\setminus I\).

Otherwise, compute

\(E=\{\text{last-ranked site in }F\text{ according to }p:p\in P\}.\)

In every consistent realization, \(E\) is exactly the pair of endpoint sites of \(F\). To see this:

  • An interior point of an interval is strictly closer to any fixed point than at least one endpoint. Thus an interior site of \(F\) cannot be last-ranked.
  • A type whose favorite is the left endpoint ranks the right endpoint last in \(F\). Every intermediate site lies to the right of that type; otherwise it would be strictly closer than the favorite. The symmetric statement supplies the other endpoint.

Label the two elements of \(E\) deterministically as \(\ell,r\). For analysis, reflect the realization if necessary so that \(s_\ell<s_r\). Define

\(I=\{q\in Q:\text{some }p\text{ ranks }q\text{ at least as highly as }\ell\text{ or }r\}\),

and \(O=Q\setminus I\).

The following properties hold in every consistent realization.

First, every site between \(\ell\) and \(r\) belongs to \(I\). If \(\ell<q<r\), a type with favorite \(\ell\) lies to the left of \(q\) and strictly prefers \(q\) to \(r\).

Second, every left outlier lies left of every site in \(I\), and every right outlier lies right of every site in \(I\). For example, suppose \(q<o<\ell\), where \(q\in I\) and \(o\in O\). There is a type and an endpoint \(e\in\{\ell,r\}\) with \(d(p,q)\leq d(p,e)\). Since \(o\) lies strictly between \(q\) and \(e\),

\(d(p,o)<\max\{d(p,q),d(p,e)\}=d(p,e).\)

Thus \(o\) is ranked above \(e\), contradicting \(o\in O\).

Third, all types weakly prefer both endpoints to every outlier:

\(d(p,\ell)\leq d(p,o),\qquad d(p,r)\leq d(p,o)\quad(o\in O).\)

We also use the following consequence of the extreme favorites:

\(s_q>s_r\Longrightarrow d(p,r)\leq d(p,q),\)

and symmetrically for sites left of \(\ell\). If \(s_p\leq s_r\), the displayed inequality is immediate. If \(s_p>s_r\), then \(s_{f(p)}\leq s_r\), so

\(d(p,r)\leq d(p,f(p))\leq d(p,q).\)

Explicit recovery procedure

Define

\(I_-=\{q\in I:\text{every type ranks }\ell\text{ above }q\}.\)

This is exactly the subset of \(I\) lying left of \(\ell\).

For the nontrivial direction, suppose \(q<\ell\) and some type ranks \(q\) above \(\ell\). Consistency places that type weakly left of the midpoint of \(q,\ell\), hence strictly left of \(\ell\). Its favorite belongs to \(F\) and therefore lies at or right of \(\ell\). A favorite strictly right of \(\ell\) would be strictly farther than \(q\), while favorite \(\ell\) contradicts the ranking. Thus no such type exists. Conversely, a site strictly between \(\ell,r\) is preferred to \(\ell\) by a type with favorite \(r\); a site right of \(r\) that belongs to \(I\) cannot be below both endpoints for all types.

Choose types \(p_\ell,p_r\) with favorites \(\ell,r\). Recover the site order by concatenating:

  • The reverse of \(p_r\)'s ranking restricted to \(I_-\).
  • \(p_\ell\)'s ranking restricted to \(I\setminus I_-\).

Every site in the first list lies left of \(p_r\), so reversing its distance order gives coordinate order. In the second list, \(\ell\) comes first, and every other site lies right of \(p_\ell\), so its ranking gives coordinate order.

This recovers the true order of \(I\), up to reflection, in polynomial time.

Finally, different favorite sites order their types correctly. If \(s_{f(p)}<s_{f(p')}\), the nearest-site inequalities imply

\(s_p\leq \frac{s_{f(p)}+s_{f(p')}}2\leq s_{p'}.\)

Because the original type coordinates are distinct, \(s_p<s_{p'}\).

3. The compressed algorithm

Fix deterministic orders on type and site labels.

Preserve all predicted outlier assignments:

\(X_{po}=\widehat X_{po}\quad(p\in P,\ o\in O).\)

For each positive prediction entry \(\widehat X_{pq}\) with \(q\in I\), create a block of length \(\widehat X_{pq}\), labeled by \(p\). Sort these blocks lexicographically by:

  • The recovered position of \(f(p)\).
  • The recovered position of the predicted site \(q\).
  • The fixed label order of \(p\).

Concatenate the blocks into an applicant-mass interval. Separately concatenate the sites of \(I\), in recovered order, into intervals of lengths \(\nu_q\).

For \(q\in I\), let \(X_{pq}\) be the total overlap of site \(q\)'s interval with blocks labeled \(p\).

The two interval lists have the same total length, because the preserved outlier columns have their required masses. Their overlaps give every remaining type its residual row mass and every inner site its required column mass. Thus \(X\) is feasible.

We next prove both required bounds.

4. A finite factor-three lemma with repeated coordinates

We use copies only for the proof; the algorithm never constructs them.

Consider equally many applicant and seat copies, allowing copies at the same coordinates. Each applicant retains its original favorite site coordinate. The inner seats are all copies of sites in \(I\); the others are outliers.

Let \(M\) be a perfect matching that:

  • Reserves the outlier seats for arbitrary applicants.
  • Matches the remaining applicants, sorted by favorite-site coordinate with arbitrary order within a favorite group, to the inner seats in coordinate order.

Let \(M^*\) be an assortative matching in true coordinate order. Ties between copies are resolved within their original type or site.

We prove, for every integer \(k\),

\(\operatorname{cost}_k(M,d)\leq3\operatorname{cost}_k(M^*,d).\)

4.1 Transforming the matching

Let \(A_{\mathrm{in}}^*\) be the applicants receiving inner seats under \(M^*\), and let \(R\) be the applicants reserved for outliers under \(M\).

Partition \(A_{\mathrm{in}}^*\) into its nonempty favorite groups \(A_1,\ldots,A_h\), ordered by favorite coordinate. Put \(B_s=M^*(A_s)\). These seat blocks occur in the same order and satisfy \(|B_s|=|A_s|\).

Because all inner seats form a contiguous interval in true seat order, every applicant outside \(A_{\mathrm{in}}^*\) occurs either before or after all applicants in \(A_{\mathrm{in}}^*\). Call them left and right exterior applicants.

An applicant in \(A_s\setminus R\) has a right defect if its current seat is in \(B_t\) with \(t>s\), and a left defect if \(t<s\).

Initially, each right defect in \(A_s\) has an available swap partner lying truly left of \(A_s\) and holding a seat in \(B_s\). Here is the counting argument.

If a right defect exists, then \(s<h\). All applicants from later favorite groups occur after that defect in the algorithm's applicant order, so none can occupy a seat position in \(B_s\). Right exterior applicants also have favorites at least as far right as the last inner favorite group, and therefore cannot occupy those positions. Hence every occupant of \(B_s\) outside \(A_s\) lies truly left of \(A_s\).

If there are \(q\) right defects and \(c=|A_s\setminus R|\), at most \(c-q\) seats of \(B_s\) are held by \(A_s\). Thus at least

\(|B_s|-(c-q)\geq q\)

seats of \(B_s\) have eligible partners. The left-defect statement follows by reversing the order.

Now swap a right-defect applicant in \(A_s\), holding a seat in \(B_t\), with an eligible left partner holding a seat in \(B_s\). This removes one right defect. If the partner belongs to an earlier inner group \(A_u\), it had a right defect before the swap and still has one afterward; if exterior, it has no counted defect.

The partner-count inequalities remain valid:

  • For block \(s\), its right-defect count and left-partner count both decrease by one.
  • The seat in \(B_t\) moves between two applicants lying left of \(A_t\), so that block's partner count is unchanged.
  • No other defect or partner count changes.

The left-defect swap has the symmetric properties. Therefore repeated swaps eliminate all defects, terminating after finitely many steps.

Each swap changes two coordinate-ordered assignments into crossed assignments. It is the reverse of an uncrossing, so every top-\(k\) cost weakly increases.

The resulting matching \(\widetilde M\) consequently satisfies

\(\operatorname{cost}_k(M,d)\leq\operatorname{cost}_k(\widetilde M,d),\)

and whenever an applicant in \(A_{\mathrm{in}}^*\) receives an inner seat under \(\widetilde M\), that seat's optimal recipient has the same favorite.

4.2 Bounding the transformed matching

Fix an applicant \(a\). Write

\(b=\widetilde M(a),\qquad c=M^*(a),\qquad z=(M^*)^{-1}(b).\)

We prove

\(d(a,b)\leq d(a,c)+2d(z,b).\)

There are four cases.

Both \(a,z\) are inner applicants. They share a favorite coordinate \(g\). Hence

\(d(a,b)\leq d(a,g)+d(z,g)+d(z,b)\leq d(a,c)+2d(z,b).\)

Neither is an inner applicant. Both \(c,b\) are outliers. Using endpoint \(\ell\),

\(d(a,b)\leq d(a,\ell)+d(z,\ell)+d(z,b)\leq d(a,c)+2d(z,b).\)

Applicant \(a\) is exterior and \(z\) is inner. Suppose \(c\) is a left outlier; the other side is symmetric. Every inner seat is at or right of \(c\).

If \(b\leq r\), then \(c\leq b\leq r\), so

\(d(a,b)\leq\max\{d(a,c),d(a,r)\}\leq d(a,c).\)

If \(b>r\), the extreme-favorite property gives \(d(z,r)\leq d(z,b)\), while outlier dominance gives \(d(a,r)\leq d(a,c)\). The triangle inequality through \(r,z\) proves the desired bound.

Applicant \(a\) is inner and \(z\) is exterior. Suppose \(b\) is a left outlier.

If \(d(a,r)\leq d(a,c)\), the triangle inequality through \(r,z\), together with \(d(z,r)\leq d(z,b)\), proves the bound.

Otherwise \(d(a,c)<d(a,r)\). The extreme-favorite property implies \(c\leq r\), and outlier separation gives \(b\leq c\). Thus

\(d(z,c)\leq\max\{d(z,b),d(z,r)\}\leq d(z,b).\)

The triangle inequality through \(c,z\) again proves the bound.

Now sum over the \(k\) most expensive assignments of \(\widetilde M\). Their applicants \(a\) are distinct, and their corresponding applicants \(z\) are distinct because both matchings are bijections. Each of the two sums of optimal costs is at most the optimal top-\(k\) sum. Therefore,

\(\operatorname{cost}_k(\widetilde M,d)\leq3\operatorname{cost}_k(M^*,d).\)

Combining the inequalities proves the finite lemma. Every coordinate comparison used here is weak, so repeated coordinates cause no problem.

5. Applying the lemma and proving prediction dominance

Choose a positive integer \(D\) divisible by all denominators appearing in \(\mu,\nu,\widehat X\). Conceptually create:

  • \(D\mu_p\) copies of each type \(p\).
  • \(D\nu_q\) copies of each site \(q\).
  • A predicted perfect matching containing exactly \(D\widehat X_{pq}\) pairs of original labels \(p,q\).

Place copies at their original coordinates.

Within each inner site's copies, order predicted recipients by their type labels. The predicted inner-seat sequence is then the sequence of prediction blocks ordered by predicted site and type label.

Stably sorting this sequence by favorite-site order gives exactly the block order used by the compressed algorithm. The resulting matching aggregates to \(X\). It reserves the predicted outlier assignments and orders all remaining applicants by favorite site. Hence the finite lemma applies.

The assortative copy matching aggregates to the assortative transport \(X^*\) from Section 1. Consequently, for \(j=1,\ldots,D\),

\(C_{j/D}(X,d)=D^{-1}\operatorname{cost}_j(M,d)\leq3C_{j/D}(X^*,d).\)

Prediction dominance

Implement the stable sort using adjacent swaps of applicants with different, inverted favorite groups. If adjacent applicants \(x,y\) are inverted in favorite order, the favorite-order property implies that \(y\) lies weakly left of \(x\).

Before the swap, \(x\) receives the earlier seat and \(y\) the later seat. Swapping their assignments is therefore an uncrossing. Every top-\(j\) cost weakly decreases.

Applicants in the same favorite group are never swapped, and outlier assignments remain fixed. Thus the terminal matching is precisely the matching aggregating to \(X\), and

\(C_{j/D}(X,d)\leq C_{j/D}(\widehat X,d)\quad(j=1,\ldots,D).\)

All real tail masses

Every entry of \(X,\widehat X,X^*\) is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). For any such plan, its tail-cost curve is affine on every interval

\([j/D,(j+1)/D].\)

Indeed, after sorting its finitely many edge costs, every cumulative mass breakpoint is a multiple of \(1/D\).

Define \(C_0=0\). Both required inequalities hold at all grid endpoints, so affine interpolation proves them for every real \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). Using Section 1,

\(C_\alpha(X,d)\leq C_\alpha(\widehat X,d)\)

and

\(C_\alpha(X,d)\leq3C_\alpha(X^*,d)=3\operatorname{OPT}_\alpha(d).\)

The realization \(d\) was arbitrary. The same ordinally constructed \(X\) therefore satisfies these inequalities simultaneously for every consistent realization and every required tail mass.

6. Running time and rational output

There are at most \(\tau m\) prediction blocks. Site-order recovery, block sorting, and the interval-overlap sweep all use polynomially many comparisons and rational arithmetic operations.

For bit complexity, take \(D\) to be the product of the input denominators. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). All interval endpoints and output entries are integer multiples of \(1/D\), between zero and one, and therefore have \(O(L)\) bits. Neither \(D\) nor its copies need to be constructed by the algorithm.

Thus the mechanism is deterministic, outputs a rational feasible plan, and runs in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). The plan is admissible.
