The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given resident types \(a\in\mathcal A\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), hospital types \(b\in\mathcal B\) with rational supplies \(\nu_b\), and a strict ranking of hospital types for each resident type, assume every hospital has quotas \([1,2]\), is indifferent among residents, and \(2\sum_b\nu_b>1\). For each hospital type \(b\) and integral assignment pattern \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\) with \(\sum_a k_a\le2\), let \(z_{b,k}\) be the mass of hospitals assigned that pattern; require \(\sum_k z_{b,k}=\nu_b\) and \(\sum_{b,k}k_a z_{b,k}=\mu_a\). Set \(x_{a,b}=\sum_k k_a z_{b,k}\), \(D_{a,b}=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{a,b'}\), and \(V_b=\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a<2}z_{b,k}\). Among assignments satisfying, for every \(a,b\), \(D_{a,b}=0\) or \(V_b=0\), maximize \(\sum_b\sum_{k:\,\sum_a k_a\ge1}z_{b,k}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: positive-mass resident types \(\mathcal A\) and hospital types \(\mathcal B\), with binary rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) and supplies \(\nu_b>0\). Let \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\), \(m=|\mathcal B|\), and \(L\) be the total input bit length; these count listed types, not all possible types. Assume \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\) and \(2\sum_b\nu_b>1\); hospital supplies need not sum to one.
Each resident type has a complete strict ranking \(\succ_a\) of hospital types and is indifferent among hospitals of the same type. Every hospital accepts every resident, is indifferent among residents, and has quotas \([1,2]\); the lower quota is a satisfaction objective, not a feasibility constraint.
Let \(K=\{k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}:\sum_a k_a\le2\}\). An assignment consists of real masses \(z_{b,k}\ge0\), where \(z_{b,k}\) is the mass of hospitals of type \(b\) receiving integral resident pattern \(k\), subject to \(\sum_{k\in K}z_{b,k}=\nu_b\) and \(\sum_{b,k\in K}k_a z_{b,k}=\mu_a\).
Define \(x_{a,b}=\sum_{k\in K}k_a z_{b,k}\), \(D_{a,b}=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{a,b'}\), and \(V_b=\sum_{k\in K:\,\sum_a k_a<2}z_{b,k}\). The assignment is weakly stable exactly when \(D_{a,b}=0\) or \(V_b=0\) for every \(a,b\).
Its score is \(S(z)=\sum_b\sum_{k\in K:\,\sum_a k_a\ge1}z_{b,k}\), the mass of hospitals satisfying their lower quota.

Given also a binary rational threshold \(q\), is deciding whether a weakly stable assignment with \(S(z)\ge q\) exists in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies, maximizing the number of nonempty hospitals over weakly stable matchings is NP-hard even with complete strict resident preferences, hospital indifference, and quotas \([1,2]\). Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

The key fact is that, on suitably normalized finite instances, every weakly stable continuous assignment can be rounded to a finite weakly stable matching with at least the same normalized score. Thus divisibility does not remove the finite hardness.

1. Load and score identities

For each hospital type \(b\), define its resident load and its score contribution by

\(y_b=\sum_a x_{a,b}=\sum_{k\in K}|k|z_{b,k}\), where \(|k|=\sum_a k_a\),

and

\(s_b=\sum_{k\in K:\,|k|\ge1}z_{b,k}\).

The hospital-mass constraint gives

\(2\nu_b-y_b=\sum_{k\in K}(2-|k|)z_{b,k}\).

Every summand is nonnegative, and its coefficient is positive precisely when \(|k|<2\). Consequently,

\(V_b=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y_b=2\nu_b.\)

Also, \(s_b\le\nu_b\), since nonempty hospitals are a subset of all hospitals of type \(b\), and \(s_b\le y_b\), since each nonempty hospital receives at least one resident. Therefore

\(S(z)=\sum_b s_b\le\sum_b\min\{\nu_b,y_b\}.\)

These statements hold for every feasible pattern assignment.

2. The finite hardness premise and normalization

We use the following finite problem: given residents with complete strict hospital rankings, hospitals indifferent among all residents, quotas \([1,2]\), and an integer threshold \(t\), decide whether a weakly stable matching makes at least \(t\) hospitals nonempty.

This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard by Goko et al., Theorem 14. Their full proof in Appendix D.3 gives an exact relation between optimum values, so the reduction also yields hardness of the threshold decision problem. The paper restricts attention to the nontrivial regime in which the number of residents is smaller than total upper capacity. [Theorem 14](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol219-stacs2022/LIPIcs.STACS.2022.31/LIPIcs.STACS.2022.31.pdf), [full proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.03093).

Let such a finite instance have \(n>0\) residents and \(m\) hospitals, with \(n<2m\). Every weakly stable matching assigns all residents: otherwise, some hospital is undersubscribed, and an unmatched resident forms a blocking pair with it.

Construct a continuous instance as follows.

  • Group residents with identical rankings into resident types. If type \(a\) contains \(c_a\) residents, set \(\mu_a=c_a/n\).
  • Give each finite hospital its own hospital type \(b\), with supply \(\nu_b=1/n\).
  • Preserve all resident rankings and set \(q=t/n\).

Thus \(\tau\le n\), all masses and supplies are positive binary-encoded rationals, and

\(\sum_a\mu_a=1,\qquad 2\sum_b\nu_b=2m/n>1.\)

The construction has polynomial input length and lists only the types it uses.

3. Finite matchings give continuous assignments

Let \(M\) be a finite weakly stable matching. For each hospital \(b\), let \(k^{(b)}\) record the types of its assigned residents. Define

\(z_{b,k^{(b)}}=1/n\),

with every other pattern mass at \(b\) equal to zero.

This assignment satisfies both sets of mass constraints. Its score is

\(S(z)=|\{b:M(b)\ne\varnothing\}|/n.\)

It is weakly stable. Indeed, if \(V_b>0\), then hospital \(b\) receives fewer than two residents in \(M\). Finite stability implies that no resident is assigned to a hospital strictly worse than \(b\), which gives \(D_{a,b}=0\) for every resident type \(a\).

Hence a finite yes-instance produces a continuous yes-instance.

4. Rounding a continuous assignment

We prove the converse, including the required score guarantee.

Let \(z\) be any weakly stable assignment in the constructed continuous instance. Set

\(X_{a,b}=n x_{a,b},\qquad \ell_b=\sum_aX_{a,b}.\)

The mass constraints imply

\(\sum_bX_{a,b}=c_a,\qquad 0\le\ell_b\le2.\)

Let

\(F=\{b:\ell_b=2\},\qquad E=\{(a,b):X_{a,b}>0\}.\)

By Section 1, \(F\) consists exactly of hospital types with \(V_b=0\). Moreover,

\(nS(z)\le\sum_b\min\{1,\ell_b\}.\)

We will find an integral assignment supported on \(E\), keeping every hospital in \(F\) full, whose number of nonempty hospitals is at least this last sum.

4.1. A flow network recording the score

Create a source \(s\), a node for each resident type, a node for each hospital, and a sink \(u\).

  • The arc from \(s\) to resident type \(a\) has lower and upper bounds both equal to \(c_a\).
  • For every \((a,b)\in E\), include an arc from \(a\) to \(b\), with bounds \(0\) and \(2\).
  • From every hospital \(b\) to \(u\), include two parallel arcs, each with upper bound \(1\). Give the first arc weight \(1\) and the second weight \(0\).
  • For \(b\in F\), give both terminal arcs lower bound \(1\). For other hospitals, their lower bounds are zero.

Require flow conservation at resident and hospital nodes. The source supplies \(n\) units and the sink receives \(n\) units. Maximize total arc weight.

A feasible fractional flow is obtained by sending \(X_{a,b}\) through the resident–hospital arcs and sending

\(p_b=\min\{1,\ell_b\},\qquad r_b=\ell_b-p_b\)

through the first and second terminal arcs, respectively. Both quantities belong to \([0,1]\); for \(b\in F\), both equal \(1\). Its objective value is

\(\sum_b\min\{1,\ell_b\}.\)

4.2. Why an integral optimum exists

Here is a direct integrality argument.

The feasible flow polytope is nonempty and bounded, so a linear objective attains its maximum at a vertex. All arc bounds and node balances are integers.

Suppose a feasible vertex has some nonintegral arc flows. Form the undirected multigraph consisting of precisely those arcs. No node can have degree one in this multigraph: its balance equation would then contain exactly one nonintegral term and otherwise integral terms, contradicting its integral balance.

Every nonisolated node therefore has degree at least two, so this finite multigraph contains a cycle, possibly consisting of two parallel arcs. Perturb the flows around that cycle, increasing an arc when its direction agrees with the traversal and decreasing it otherwise. This preserves every node balance.

Every perturbed arc has nonintegral flow and integer bounds, so its flow lies strictly between its bounds. A sufficiently small perturbation is therefore feasible in both signs. The original flow is the midpoint of two distinct feasible flows, contradicting that it is a vertex.

Thus every vertex is integral. In particular, there is an integral optimal flow \(f\) satisfying

\(\operatorname{weight}(f)\ge\sum_b\min\{1,\ell_b\}\ge nS(z).\)

4.3. Recovering a finite matching

For each type \(a\), the integral flow assigns exactly \(c_a\) units to hospitals. Assign the \(c_a\) distinct finite residents of that type accordingly. This gives a matching \(M\): each resident is assigned once, and each hospital receives at most two residents.

Every hospital in \(F\) receives exactly two residents because both its terminal arcs have flow \(1\).

The weighted terminal flow at a hospital is either zero or one, and it can be one only if that hospital is nonempty. Hence

\(|\{b:M(b)\ne\varnothing\}|\ge\operatorname{weight}(f)\ge nS(z).\)

5. Rounding preserves weak stability

Suppose, for contradiction, that resident \(r\), of type \(a\), and hospital \(b\) form a blocking pair in \(M\). Write \(b'=M(r)\).

All residents are assigned. Because hospitals are indifferent among residents, blocking requires both

\(b\succ_a b'\qquad\text{and}\qquad |M(b)|<2.\)

The rounded matching uses only edges in \(E\). Thus its assignment of \(r\) to \(b'\) implies

\(x_{a,b'}>0.\)

Since \(b\succ_a b'\), the definition of dissatisfaction gives

\(D_{a,b}\ge x_{a,b'}>0.\)

Weak stability of \(z\) therefore forces \(V_b=0\). By the load identity, \(\ell_b=2\), so \(b\in F\). The rounding construction keeps every hospital in \(F\) full, giving \(|M(b)|=2\), a contradiction.

Thus \(M\) is weakly stable.

Combining Sections 3–5 establishes the exact threshold equivalence:

\(\text{a finite stable matching with at least }t\text{ nonempty hospitals exists}\)

if and only if

\(\text{a continuous weakly stable assignment with }S(z)\ge t/n\text{ exists}.\)

The normalization is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

6. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For an arbitrary input instance, a certificate consists of a subset \(F\subseteq\mathcal B\), encoded by \(m\) bits. The verifier checks feasibility of the following rational linear system in the pattern variables \(z_{b,k}\):

  • all assignment constraints and nonnegativity constraints;
  • \(S(z)\ge q\);
  • \(z_{b,k}=0\) whenever \(b\in F\) and \(|k|<2\);
  • \(D_{a,b}=0\) for every \(a\) and every \(b\notin F\).

Every feasible solution of this system is weakly stable: hospitals in \(F\) have \(V_b=0\), while hospitals outside \(F\) have zero dissatisfaction for every resident type.

Conversely, every weakly stable assignment satisfies such a system for

\(F=\{b:V_b=0\}.\)

The number of patterns is

\(|K|=1+\tau+\tau(\tau+1)/2.\)

Consequently, the system has \(O(m\tau^2)\) variables and polynomially many constraints, all with rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. Exact rational linear feasibility is decidable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Thus the certificate has a polynomial-time verifier, proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
