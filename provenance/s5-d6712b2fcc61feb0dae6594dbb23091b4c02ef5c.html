The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of patient types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita baseline utilities \(\alpha_t\), treatment gains \(q_t\), costs \(c_t\), budget \(b\), and threshold \(\Delta\), choose \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\) to maximize \(W_\Delta(y)=\Delta+u_{\min}(y)+\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(u_t(y)-u_{\min}(y)-\Delta\bigr)^+\), where \(u_t(y)=\alpha_t+q_ty_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c_t y_t\le b\) and any specified policy constraints. Determine an optimal treatment vector and its welfare value.

The input includes nonnegative binary-rational baseline utilities \(\alpha_t\), treatment gains \(q_t\), per-capita costs \(c_t\), per-capita budget \(b\), and disparity threshold \(\Delta\).

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with no policy restrictions, fixed \(\Delta=1\), positive dyadic masses, and strictly positive baseline utilities, treatment gains, and costs.

Consequently, computing an exact optimal treatment vector or its welfare value is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,r,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Reduction from SUBSET SUM

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of SUBSET SUM: given binary-encoded positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and a positive integer \(B\), decide whether some subset sums exactly to \(B\). This theorem is stated and proved in [these university lecture notes](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~djimenez/utsa/cs3343/lecture24.html).

Let \(D\) be the smallest power of two strictly larger than \(n\). Construct a society with types \(T=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}\), so \(m=\tau=n+1\).

For each item type \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), set

\(\mu_i=1/D,\qquad \alpha_i=2,\qquad q_i=Da_i,\qquad c_i=Da_i.\)

For the additional type \(0\), set

\(\mu_0=(D-n)/D,\qquad \alpha_0=1,\qquad q_0=1,\qquad c_0=D(B+1).\)

Finally, set

\(b=B,\qquad \Delta=1,\qquad K=B+2,\qquad r=0.\)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals because \(D>n\), and their sum is

\(\mu_0+\sum_{i=1}^n\mu_i=(D-n)/D+n/D=1.\)

Every baseline utility, gain, and cost is strictly positive. Thus the construction satisfies the stated input requirements.

Exact behavior of feasible vectors

First, every feasible vector has \(y_0=0\). Indeed, treating type \(0\) alone incurs cost

\(\mu_0c_0=(D-n)(B+1)\ge B+1>B=b.\)

Since all costs are nonnegative, other treatments cannot offset this violation.

Therefore, for every feasible vector,

\(u_0(y)=1,\qquad u_i(y)=2+Da_i y_i\ge2\quad(1\le i\le n).\)

It follows that \(u_{\min}(y)=1\). The additional type contributes zero to the positive-part sum, since

\((u_0-u_{\min}-\Delta)^+=(1-1-1)^+=0.\)

For each item type,

\((u_i-u_{\min}-\Delta)^+=(2+Da_i y_i-1-1)^+=Da_i y_i.\)

The final equality holds because \(Da_i y_i\ge0\). Substituting into the objective gives the exact identity

\(W_1(y)=1+1+\sum_{i=1}^n(1/D)Da_i y_i=2+\sum_{i=1}^n a_i y_i.\)

Similarly, because \(y_0=0\), the budget constraint becomes

\(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tc_ty_t=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i y_i\le B.\)

Thus a feasible vector has \(W_1(y)\ge K=B+2\) if and only if

\(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i y_i=B.\)

Explicitly, a subset summing to \(B\) produces a feasible vector by treating precisely its item types and leaving type \(0\) untreated. Conversely, any feasible vector meeting the welfare threshold identifies a subset summing to \(B\).

This proves both directions of the reduction.

Encoding size and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

The reduction is polynomial in the source input length. We have \(D\le2n\), so \(D\) requires only \(O(\log n)\) bits. Each \(Da_i\) requires \(O(\log n+\log(a_i+1))\) bits, and \(D(B+1)\) requires \(O(\log n+\log(B+1))\) bits. The masses have numerators and denominators of \(O(\log n)\) bits. There are only \(n+1\) listed types; no population expansion is used.

For the general decision problem, a certificate is the \(\tau\)-bit vector \(y\). A verifier checks the budget and every policy inequality, computes each utility and their minimum, evaluates the positive parts, and compares the resulting welfare with \(K\).

These computations use exact rational addition, multiplication, and comparison. Intermediate numerators and denominators have polynomial bit length: a common denominator can be formed as a product of the polynomially many denominators occurring in these expressions, whose bit lengths add. Thus verification takes polynomial time in the binary input length, including the encoding of \(K\).

The decision problem is therefore in \(\mathrm{NP}\), and the reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

Consequences for optimization and sharpness

Every constructed instance is feasible: the zero treatment vector costs zero. Its optimum is

\(\max_{y\text{ feasible}}W_1(y)=2+\max\{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i y_i:\ y_i\in\{0,1\},\ \sum_{i=1}^n a_i y_i\le B\}.\)

Hence an exact optimizer, whether returning an optimal vector or only its welfare value, decides SUBSET SUM by checking whether the optimal welfare is at least \(B+2\). Allowing an optimizer to report infeasibility does not affect this reduction.

The cited sharpness result concerns the welfare formulation before resource and policy constraints are added, as [Hooker–Williams explicitly state](https://johnhooker.tepper.cmu.edu/equity8MSpost.pdf). It therefore does not contradict this hardness result. In this construction, welfare is already affine on every feasible binary vector; selecting treatments under the budget carries the combinatorial difficulty.
