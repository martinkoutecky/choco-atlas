The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite hospital set \(H\), resident types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), mutually acceptable preference lists over hospitals and types, and common rational quotas \(0<\lambda\le\kappa\), choose assignment masses \(x_{ih}\ge0\) with \(\sum_h x_{ih}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\le\kappa\). For each acceptable pair \((i,h)\), let \(D_{ih}\) be the mass of type \(i\) assigned to a strictly worse hospital or unmatched, and \(W_{ih}\) the mass at \(h\) of types that \(h\) ranks strictly below \(i\); require that no pair has \(D_{ih}>0\) and either unused capacity at \(h\) or \(W_{ih}>0\). The score is \(S(x)=\sum_{h\in H}\min\{1,\sum_i x_{ih}/\lambda\}\). What is the best polynomial-time approximation factor \(\rho\), as a function of \(|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), \(\theta=\kappa/\lambda\), and input bit length \(L\), for maximizing \(S(x)\) over stable assignments?

1. Stability branches and integral rounding

Write
$$ r_i(x)=\sum_hx_{ih}. $$
Feasibility makes every summand in $D_{ih}$ and $W_{ih}$ nonnegative. Consequently, stability is equivalent to the following disjunction for every acceptable pair:
$$ D_{ih}=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(q_h=\kappa\ \text{and}\ W_{ih}=0\bigr). $$
Moreover,
$$ D_{ih}=0 \iff r_i=\mu_i \ \text{and}\ x_{ig}=0\quad\text{for every }g\text{ with }h\succ_i g, $$
and
$$ W_{ih}=0 \iff x_{jh}=0\quad\text{for every }j\text{ with }i\succ_h j. $$

Thus, after choosing one branch for each acceptable pair, the remaining constraints are transportation constraints: row and column upper bounds, some rows and columns forced full, and some entries forced to zero. Every assignment satisfying the chosen branches is stable.

This also shows that the stable set is a finite union of closed, bounded polytopes. Once nonemptiness is established below, continuity of $S$ implies that $\mathrm{OPT}$ is attained.

Rounding lemma. Suppose instead that type supplies $a_i$, lower quota $\ell>0$, and upper quota $u\geq\ell$ are integers. For any stable real assignment $x$, there is an integral stable assignment $z$ with
$$ \sum_h\min\{1,q_h(z)/\ell\} \geq \sum_h\min\{1,q_h(x)/\ell\}. $$
Given a rational $x$, such a $z$ is computable in polynomial time in the binary encoding length.

Proof. Choose a valid stability branch at every pair. Construct a circulation network with vertices $s$, the types, hospitals $h$, auxiliary vertices $v_h$, and $t$.

  • The arc $s\to i$ has capacity $a_i$ and lower bound $a_i$ precisely when row $i$ is forced full.
  • Allowed type–hospital entries become zero-cost arcs, each with capacity $\sum_i a_i$.
  • The arc $h\to v_h$ has capacity $u$ and lower bound $u$ precisely when column $h$ is forced full.
  • There are two arcs $v_h\to t$: one of capacity $\ell$ and cost $-1$, and one of capacity $u-\ell$ and cost zero.
  • The return arc $t\to s$ has capacity $\sum_i a_i$ and cost zero.

The chosen branches forbid all other assignment entries. The network is feasible because $x$ supplies a feasible real circulation.

For any fixed hospital total $q_h$, minimum-cost routing uses $\min\{\ell,q_h\}$ units of its negative-cost arc. Hence minimizing circulation cost maximizes
$$ \sum_h\min\{\ell,q_h\}. $$
Integral capacities and lower bounds imply that an integral minimum-cost circulation exists and can be found in polynomial time. Its assignment entries satisfy the chosen branches, and therefore remain stable. Dividing the objective comparison by $\ell$ proves the lemma. $\square$

Let $Q$ be the product of the input denominators. Then $\log Q$ is bounded by the input encoding length, and
$$ Q\mu_i,\qquad Q\lambda,\qquad Q\kappa $$
are integers. Applying the rounding argument to a branch containing a real optimum shows that an optimum exists with every entry in $Q^{-1}\mathbb Z$. This is an existence statement; finding its branch can still be difficult.

2. A compact auxiliary allocation instance

For each hospital $h$, introduce a block $h^0$ of capacity $\lambda$. If $\kappa>\lambda$, also introduce a block $h^1$ of capacity $\kappa-\lambda$.

For every acceptable pair $(i,h)$, introduce the following distinguishable contracts:
$$ a_{ih}^0,\quad b_{ih}^0,\quad c_{ih}^0, $$
and, when $h^1$ exists,
$$ b_{ih}^1,\quad c_{ih}^1. $$
A contract joins type $i$ to the indicated block. Its capacity is
$$ \min\{\mu_i,\text{capacity of its block}\}. $$
Type $i$ has total capacity $\mu_i$.

Fix deterministic orders on types and hospitals to resolve all remaining choices.

Type preferences over contracts

Process the original hospital preference classes from best to worst. Within each class, list:

  • all its $a^0$ contracts;
  • all its $b$ contracts, with the two contracts for each hospital consecutive and its block $0$ contract first.

After this entire list, append all $c$ contracts, in original hospital preference order, again putting block $0$ before block $1$ for each hospital.

All these auxiliary preferences are strict.

Block preferences over contracts

Call $b$ and $c$ contracts high contracts.

At either block of hospital $h$, high contracts are ordered first by the original hospital preference class of their type. Within an original tie, every $c$ contract precedes every $b$ contract. Remaining choices are resolved by the fixed type order.

At $h^0$, every high contract precedes every $a$ contract. The $a$ contracts are ordered deterministically. Block $h^1$ has no $a$ contracts.

The auxiliary instance has at most $5|E|$ contracts and $\tau+2m$ vertices.

3. Polynomial-time computation without cloning

Here is a precise way to compute a stable allocation in the auxiliary instance, avoiding any assumption about algorithms on parallel contracts.

A directed flow respects arc capacities and conservation at every nonterminal vertex. A directed walk blocks a flow when all its arcs are unsaturated, its first vertex is the source or prefers its first arc to a positive-flow outgoing arc, and its last vertex is the sink or prefers its last arc to a positive-flow incoming arc. With strict incoming and outgoing preferences, a stable flow can be computed deterministically in polynomial time. [Cseh–Matuschke, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3701#page=12).

Construct a directed network as follows:

  • Add $s\to i$ with capacity $\mu_i$.
  • For every auxiliary contract $e$ joining $i$ to a block $v$, introduce a distinct vertex $p_e$ and arcs

$$ i\to p_e\to v, $$
both with the contract capacity.

  • Add $v\to t$ with the block capacity.

Type outgoing preferences and block incoming preferences are the contract orders above. Other required preference lists are singletons. Omit types and blocks with no incident contracts.

The network has no parallel arcs. Every retained nonterminal vertex has incoming and outgoing arcs, and all capacities are positive rationals. Thus the stable-flow theorem applies.

By conservation at $p_e$, both arcs representing contract $e$ carry the same amount; call it $z_e$. Conservation at types and blocks gives a feasible auxiliary allocation.

Suppose an unsaturated contract were desired by both endpoints. Start its representing path at the type if the type prefers it to positive allocation elsewhere; otherwise prepend $s\to i$, which is unsaturated because the type has unused capacity. Similarly, terminate at the block if it prefers the contract to positive allocation elsewhere; otherwise append its unsaturated arc to $t$. This constructs a blocking walk. Therefore $z$ is stable in the auxiliary allocation sense.

For bit complexity, scale the network by $Q$. All capacities become integers. The cited algorithm starts from zero and augments by bottleneck residual capacities, so all resulting flows are integers. Scaling back gives
$$ z_e\in Q^{-1}\mathbb Z. $$
The network size is polynomial in $m,\tau$, and all numerical values have polynomial bit length. Consequently, the computation is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

Return the projection
$$ x_{ih} = z_{a_{ih}^0} +z_{b_{ih}^0} +z_{c_{ih}^0} +z_{b_{ih}^1} +z_{c_{ih}^1}, $$
with absent contracts interpreted as zero.

4. The projection is stable

We first record a useful consequence of auxiliary stability.

Block dominance property. If type $i$ has unused capacity, or positive allocation on a contract strictly below a contract $e$, then the block incident to $e$ is full and has no positive allocation on contracts strictly below $e$.

Proof. If $e$ is unsaturated, this follows from stability. If $e$ is saturated, its capacity cannot equal $\mu_i$: saturation would leave neither unused type capacity nor positive allocation elsewhere. Therefore its capacity equals the block capacity, and it fills that block by itself. $\square$

The projection is feasible because type capacities remain $\mu_i$ and the two block capacities sum to $\kappa$.

Suppose $D_{ih}(x)>0$. There is either unused type capacity or positive allocation to an original hospital strictly below $h$. By the type contract order, type $i$ therefore desires the $b_{ih}$ contract at every existing block of $h$.

The block dominance property makes every block of $h$ full. At $h^0$, it also excludes all $a$ contracts. At both blocks, it excludes high contracts from types originally strictly below $i$ in hospital $h$’s order. Thus
$$ q_h(x)=\kappa,\qquad W_{ih}(x)=0. $$
This is exactly the hospital-side stability branch. Hence $x$ is stable.

If $E=\varnothing$, the algorithm instead returns the zero assignment, which is stable and optimal. In all cases stable assignments therefore exist, completing the compactness argument in Section 1.

5. Two stronger properties of the output

5.1 Allocation above the lower quota cannot coexist with a tied deficient alternative

Proposition. If $x_{ih}>0$, type $i$ ties $h$ and $k$, and $q_h(x)>\lambda$, then
$$ q_k(x)\geq\lambda. $$

Proof. The inequality $q_h(x)>\lambda$ implies positive allocation at $h^1$. Choose a positive high contract there. Its type prefers the corresponding block $0$ contract to it. The block dominance property therefore excludes all $a$ contracts at $h^0$. Consequently every positive contract at hospital $h$ is high.

If $q_k(x)<\lambda$, block $k^0$ is undersubscribed. Any positive $b$ contract of type $i$ at $h$ is below $a_{ik}^0$, because $h$ and $k$ are in the same original tie. Any positive $c$ contract is also below $a_{ik}^0$. Thus type $i$ desires $a_{ik}^0$, contradicting the block dominance property at $k^0$. $\square$

5.2 A relative short augmentation is impossible

Proposition. Let $y$ be any stable assignment. There cannot exist types $i,j$ and hospitals $h,k$ satisfying
$$ \mu_i-r_i(x)>0,\qquad y_{ih}>0,\qquad x_{jh}>0,\qquad y_{jk}>0,\qquad q_k(x)<\lambda. $$

Proof. Because type $i$ has unused capacity, it desires each $c_{ih}$ contract. The block dominance property implies that every block of $h$ is full and has no positive contract below the corresponding $c_{ih}$ contract.

It follows that every positive contract of type $j$ at $h$ is either:

  • a high contract with $j\succ_h i$; or
  • a $c$ contract with $j$ tied with $i$ at $h$.

A positive $c$ contract of type $j$ at $h$ is impossible: $y_{jk}>0$ makes $k$ acceptable to $j$, and $a_{jk}^0$ precedes every $c$ contract. Since $q_k(x)<\lambda$, block $k^0$ is undersubscribed, contradicting block dominance.

Thus there is a positive $b$ contract of type $j$ at $h$, and $j\succ_h i$. Since $y_{ih}>0$,
$$ W_{jh}(y)>0. $$
Stability of $y$ forces $D_{jh}(y)=0$. The positive entry $y_{jk}$ then implies
$$ k\succeq_j h. $$

On the other hand, $q_k(x)<\lambda\leq\kappa$ and stability of $x$ force $D_{jk}(x)=0$. Together with $x_{jh}>0$, this gives
$$ h\succeq_j k. $$
Therefore $h$ and $k$ are tied for $j$. The contract $a_{jk}^0$ precedes the positive $b$ contract at $h$, again contradicting undersubscription of $k^0$. $\square$

6. The approximation bound

I use the following discrete comparison result. Its stated sufficient conditions are the algorithm-specific properties used in the published proof of Theorem 9. [Makino–Miyazaki–Yokoi, proof of Theorem 9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06660#page=18).

Discrete comparison theorem. Let $M,N$ be stable matchings with common positive integer quotas $\ell\leq u$. Suppose:

  • If $M(r)=h$, resident $r$ ties $h$ and $k$, and $|M(h)|>\ell$, then $|M(k)|\geq\ell$.
  • There are no residents $r,r'$ and hospitals $h,k$ with

$$ M(r)=\varnothing,\quad M(r')=h,\quad N(r)=h,\quad N(r')=k,\quad |M(k)|<\ell. $$

Then
$$ s(N)\leq\left(1+\frac{u}{2\ell}\right)s(M). $$

To apply this theorem, choose an optimal assignment $y$ with entries in $Q^{-1}\mathbb Z$, whose existence was proved in Section 1. The algorithm’s $x$ also has entries in $Q^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

For the proof only, replace type $i$ by $Q\mu_i$ unit residents. Give these residents identical original preferences, and let each hospital tie residents within a type. Set
$$ \ell=Q\lambda,\qquad u=Q\kappa. $$
Assign the integral counts $Qx_{ih}$ and $Qy_{ih}$ among the same labeled residents, independently, obtaining matchings $M,N$.

Both matchings are stable. Indeed, a blocking individual resident would witness positive aggregate demand for its type. A hospital vacancy or a strictly worse individual assignee would witness the corresponding aggregate hospital-side blocking condition.

Their scores satisfy
$$ s(M)=S(x),\qquad s(N)=S(y)=\mathrm{OPT}. $$
The first hypothesis of the comparison theorem follows from Section 5.1. A violation of its second hypothesis would give precisely the positive masses and deficient hospital forbidden by Section 5.2. Therefore
$$ \mathrm{OPT}\leq \left(1+\frac{\theta}{2}\right)S(x). $$

This population expansion is solely an argument about the output. The algorithm never constructs it.

The additional factor $m$

Every stable assignment $x$ also satisfies
$$ S(y)\leq mS(x) $$
for every feasible $y$.

If some hospital is full in $x$, then $S(x)\geq1$, whereas $S(y)\leq m$.

Otherwise every hospital is undersubscribed. Stability forces every type having an acceptable hospital to be fully assigned. Let their total mass be $M_{\mathrm{acc}}$. Then
$$ S(x)\geq\min\{1,M_{\mathrm{acc}}/\lambda\}, \qquad S(y)\leq\min\{m,M_{\mathrm{acc}}/\lambda\}. $$
If $M_{\mathrm{acc}}/\lambda\leq1$, these inequalities give $S(y)\leq S(x)$; otherwise they give $S(y)\leq mS(x)$.

Combining the two bounds proves
$$ \boxed{ S(x)\geq \frac{\mathrm{OPT}} {\min\{m,1+\theta/2\}}. } $$
No division by $S(x)$ was used, so the conclusion includes $\mathrm{OPT}=0$.

7. Conditional impossibility results

Take a discrete instance with $n$ residents and common positive integer quotas $\ell\leq u\leq n$. Normalize it by
$$ \mu_i=\frac1n,\qquad \lambda=\frac{\ell}{n},\qquad \kappa=\frac{u}{n}. $$
Copy acceptability and preferences. If identical behavioral classes must be merged into types, a class of $a_i$ residents receives mass $a_i/n$; merge only residents with identical preferences who are tied by every accepting hospital.

Every discrete stable matching gives a divisible stable assignment with the same score.

Conversely, scale any rational divisible stable assignment by $n$ and apply the rounding lemma. The resulting integral stable assignment has no smaller score. Distribute each type’s integral counts among its constituent residents. A discrete blocking pair would imply an aggregate blocking pair, so the resulting matching is stable.

A real divisible optimum can also be rounded, by the existential part of the lemma. Consequently,
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{divisible}} = \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}. $$
The construction and the algorithmic reverse conversion have polynomial binary complexity.

Thus, for every fixed rational $\theta\geq1$ and every $\epsilon>0$ for which the displayed factor exceeds $1$:

  • Unless $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$, the factor

$$ 1+\frac{2-\sqrt2}{3}\theta-\epsilon\theta^2 $$
is impossible.

  • Under UGC, the factor

$$ 1+\frac{\theta}{3}-\epsilon\theta^2 $$
is impossible.

These follow from the discrete hardness theorem through the proved reduction. [Makino–Miyazaki–Yokoi, Theorem 13](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.06660v3#S5.SS1).

In particular, exact optimization is NP-hard already at $\theta=1$. These impossibilities concern families with growing input parameters, not every fixed value of $m$ or $\tau$.

8. Exact algorithms when a structural parameter is fixed

The transportation branches also give the exact running-time bound
$$ \min\{(m+1)^\tau,(\tau+1)^m\} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

For resident enumeration, let type $i$ have $k_i$ acceptable preference classes, numbered from best to worst. Choose
$$ c_i\in\{1,\ldots,k_i+1\}. $$
Impose the resident-side branch at hospitals of rank at least $c_i$, and the hospital-side branch at better hospitals.

Every stable assignment is captured: if type $i$ has unmatched mass, choose $c_i=k_i+1$; otherwise choose its worst positively occupied rank. At that rank and worse, demand is zero; at every better rank, demand is positive and stability supplies the hospital-side branch. There are at most $(m+1)^\tau$ choices.

For hospital enumeration, let $h$ have $d_h$ acceptable type classes. Choose either:

  • a mode imposing zero demand from every acceptable type; or
  • a cutoff class, imposing fullness, forbidding assigned types below the cutoff, and imposing zero demand from types above it.

Each mode guarantees stability at that hospital. Every stable assignment is captured: use the first mode for an undersubscribed hospital, and the worst occupied class for a full hospital. A better type must have zero demand, because otherwise its strictly worse assignee would block. There are at most $(\tau+1)^m$ choices.

Each enumerated case is optimized by the circulation network of Section 1. Hence exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed $m$ or fixed $\tau$.
