The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), fixed \(k\ge4\), a tie-breaking order, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete rankings \(v_t\), explicit menus \(\mathcal A_t\) of allowed \(k\)-approval ballots for nonfocal types, and a focal type \(\theta\) of mass \(\rho>0\), let \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) allocate each nonfocal type's mass among \(S\in\mathcal A_t\). For focal ballots \(A,B\in\binom{C}{k}\), define the deterministic \(k\)-approval winners from the resulting normalized scores. Decide whether some \(B\) continuously weakly dominates \(A\): for every feasible \(x\), the focal ranking weakly prefers the winner under \(B\) to that under \(A\), with strict preference for at least one feasible \(x\). If no such \(B\) exists, \(A\) is a continuous level-2 strategy; comparing \(A\) with the truthful top-\(k\) ballot gives the improving variant.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(k\ge4\), candidates \(C\) with \(m=|C|>k\), and a strict tie-breaking order. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\), and strict complete rankings \(v_t\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. A distinguished focal type \(\theta\) has mass \(\rho=\mu_\theta>0\).
Write \(T_t\) for the truthful top-\(k\) set. When everyone else votes truthfully, let \(F_t\) be the winners attainable by assigning all of type \(t\)'s mass to one ballot in \(\binom{C}{k}\). A level-1 ballot attains type \(t\)'s most preferred member of \(F_t\).
Each nonfocal type has an explicit menu \(\mathcal A_t\subseteq\binom{C}{k}\) containing \(T_t\). If that type can obtain a strictly better winner than the truthful winner, its other menu entries may be any subset of its level-1 ballots; otherwise its menu is \(\{T_t\}\). No minimal-change restriction is imposed.
A feasible allocation consists of real numbers \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\ne\theta\). For a focal ballot \(D\in\binom{C}{k}\), its entire mass votes \(D\), and candidate \(c\)'s score is \(s_D(c,x)=\rho\mathbf1_{c\in D}+\sum_{t\ne\theta}\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t:c\in S}x_{t,S}\). Let \(w_D(x)\) be the deterministic \(k\)-approval winner with the given tie-breaking order.
Ballot \(B\) continuously weakly dominates \(A\) if \(w_B(x)\succeq_\theta w_A(x)\) for every feasible \(x\), and \(w_B(x)\succ_\theta w_A(x)\) for at least one feasible \(x\). Ballot \(A\) is a continuous level-2 strategy if no such \(B\) exists; a ballot is improving if it dominates \(T_\theta\).
For each fixed \(k\ge4\), are continuous level-2 recognition and its complementary decision problem, existence of a ballot dominating a supplied \(A\), each in \(\mathrm P\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? What is the same classification for existence of an improving ballot? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), total input bit length \(L\) (including explicit menus), and \(k\).
In finite GS voting games, level-2 recognition is NP-hard and recognition of a supplied improving ballot is coNP-hard for every fixed \(k\ge4\). [Elkind, Grandi, Rossi and Slinko](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.08598). No result settling exactly the continuous decisions above was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to 1, including the focal mass.
  • Required m greater than k, matching the nondegenerate k-approval domain.
  • Made all rankings and the tie-breaking order strict linear orders.
  • Restored the GS-game menu restrictions: truthful voting is always available; nontruthful entries are level-1 ballots for manipulators; nonmanipulators have singleton truthful menus.
  • Defined continuous level-1 feasibility by replacing an entire type's mass with one ballot while all other types remain truthful.
  • Allowed arbitrary real mass allocations satisfying conservation, while requiring the focal type's entire positive mass to use a single ballot.
  • Separated undominated-strategy recognition from its complementary dominator-existence decision and defined the improving existence variant using the truthful focal ballot.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Classification

All three decision problems are in \(\mathrm P\) for every fixed \(k\ge4\):

  • Continuous level-2 recognition of a supplied ballot \(A\).
  • Existence of a ballot dominating a supplied \(A\).
  • Existence of an improving ballot.

In fact, the algorithm works for every fixed \(k\ge1\). The key is that simultaneous attainability of two specified winners under two supplied focal ballots is decidable by rational linear programming.

1. The allocation polytope

Let \(N=\sum_{t\ne\theta}|\mathcal A_t|\), and define

\(X=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{N}:\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\text{ for every }t\ne\theta\}\).

This set is nonempty: assign each nonfocal type’s entire mass to \(T_t\). It is closed and bounded, since every coordinate satisfies \(0\le x_{t,S}\le\mu_t\). Thus \(X\) is a compact rational polytope.

Write \(c\triangleleft d\) when the tie-breaking order gives \(c\) priority over \(d\).

For a focal ballot \(D\), candidate \(p\) wins precisely when, for every \(c\ne p\),

  • \(s_D(p,x)>s_D(c,x)\) if \(c\triangleleft p\);
  • \(s_D(p,x)\ge s_D(c,x)\) if \(p\triangleleft c\).

Indeed, these comparisons say exactly that \(p\) has maximal score and defeats every equally scoring candidate by tie-breaking.

2. Simultaneous winner attainability is polynomial-time decidable

Fix focal ballots \(A,B\) and candidates \(p,q\). We decide whether there exists \(x\in X\) with \(w_A(x)=p\) and \(w_B(x)=q\).

For distinct candidates \(a,c\), put \(\delta_{a,c}=1\) if \(c\triangleleft a\), and put \(\delta_{a,c}=0\) otherwise. Consider the following closed linear program in \(x\) and one additional variable \(\varepsilon\):

Maximize \(\varepsilon\), subject to

\(x\in X,\qquad 0\le\varepsilon\le1,\)

\(s_A(p,x)-s_A(c,x)\ge\delta_{p,c}\varepsilon\qquad(c\ne p),\)

\(s_B(q,x)-s_B(c,x)\ge\delta_{q,c}\varepsilon\qquad(c\ne q).\)

Lemma. The prescribed winner pair is attainable if and only if this linear program is feasible and its optimum is strictly positive.

Proof. Suppose first that \(x\) attains the pair. Every comparison requiring a strict inequality then has a positive score difference. There are finitely many such comparisons. If there are any, choose \(\varepsilon\) to be the minimum of \(1\) and their positive differences; otherwise choose \(\varepsilon=1\). All displayed constraints hold, so the optimum is positive.

Conversely, a feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfies every required strict comparison strictly and every required weak comparison weakly. The winner characterization above therefore gives \(w_A(x)=p\) and \(w_B(x)=q\).

Finally, the LP feasible set is a closed subset of the compact set \(X\times[0,1]\), so a feasible LP attains its optimum. This proves the equivalence. \(\square\)

All coefficients are rational. More explicitly, each score difference is

\(s_D(a,x)-s_D(c,x)=\rho(\mathbf1_{a\in D}-\mathbf1_{c\in D})+\sum_{t\ne\theta}\sum_{S\in\mathcal A_t}(\mathbf1_{a\in S}-\mathbf1_{c\in S})x_{t,S}.\)

Thus the allocation coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and the remaining constants come from the input masses.

We apply the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and exact optimization are computable in time polynomial in the binary encoding length. Consequently, whether the optimum is positive can be decided exactly. The relevant hypotheses—rational coefficients and polynomial-size explicit constraints—hold here. See [Khachiyan’s polynomial-time LP theorem](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

In particular, an optimum of zero is rejected; merely checking feasibility after replacing strict inequalities by weak ones would be insufficient.

3. Testing dominance of supplied ballots

For fixed \(A,B\), define

\(R_{A,B}=\{(p,q)\in C^2:\text{some }x\in X\text{ has }w_A(x)=p,\ w_B(x)=q\}.\)

The preceding lemma decides membership in this relation with one LP.

We claim that \(B\) dominates \(A\) exactly when both conditions hold:

  • No pair \((p,q)\in R_{A,B}\) satisfies \(p\succ_\theta q\).
  • Some pair \((p,q)\in R_{A,B}\) satisfies \(q\succ_\theta p\).

For the first condition, a violating pair is precisely an allocation where \(B\) produces a strictly worse winner than \(A\). Since the focal ranking is strict and complete, absence of such a pair is equivalent to \(w_B(x)\succeq_\theta w_A(x)\) for every \(x\in X\).

The second condition is precisely the required existence of an allocation giving strict improvement. Hence these conditions are equivalent to the definition of dominance.

Testing all distinct ordered candidate pairs uses at most \(m(m-1)\) LPs. Therefore dominance of two supplied ballots is polynomial-time decidable.

4. Deciding the three target problems

Enumerate every \(B\in\binom Ck\) and apply the supplied-ballot dominance test.

  • Existence of a dominator: accept if any enumerated \(B\) dominates \(A\).
  • Continuous level-2 recognition: accept if no enumerated \(B\) dominates \(A\).
  • Existence of an improving ballot: use \(A=T_\theta\) and accept if a dominator exists.

These algorithms examine every permitted focal ballot. Section 3 proves the correctness of each dominance test, so the three decisions are exact.

The proof uses only the explicit menus and their nonemptiness. It therefore applies to all inputs satisfying the stated level-1 menu restrictions, without needing to compute \(F_t\) or generate further menu entries.

5. Running times and input size

Each winner-pair LP has:

  • \(N+1\) variables;
  • \(O(N+\tau+m)\) constraints, counting nonnegativity and writing equalities as pairs of inequalities;
  • binary encoding length \(O((N+\tau+m+L)^2)\).

For the last bound, a dense constraint matrix has \(O((N+1)(N+\tau+m))\) entries of constant bit length. The mass constants have total length \(O(L)\), and repeating \(\rho\) in the winner constraints contributes at most \(O(mL)\).

Because menus are explicit, \(N\le L\). Also,

\(N\le(\tau-1)\binom mk.\)

Thus there is an absolute constant \(d\), independent of \(k\), such that the following are valid bit-operation bounds:

| Decision | Running time |
|---|---|
| Dominance of supplied \(B\) over supplied \(A\) | \(O\!\left(m^2(L+m+\tau)^d\right)\) |
| Existence of a dominator of \(A\) | \(O\!\left(\binom mk\,m^2(L+m+\tau)^d\right)\) |
| Continuous level-2 recognition | \(O\!\left(\binom mk\,m^2(L+m+\tau)^d\right)\) |
| Existence of an improving ballot | \(O\!\left(\binom mk\,m^2(L+m+\tau)^d\right)\) |

Here \(d\) accounts for constructing the LPs and using a polynomial-time rational LP solver.

Since \(\binom mk\le m^k\), every target decision has running time

\(O\!\left(m^{k+2}(L+m+\tau)^d\right).\)

This is polynomial for every fixed \(k\). The bound depends on the actual support and explicit menus; it does not enumerate all possible rankings.

The [finite-game hardness results cited in the task](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.08598) do not contradict this conclusion. Finite choices impose discrete allocation restrictions. Here arbitrary splitting is permitted, so each prescribed winner pair is tested over a rational polytope by linear programming.
