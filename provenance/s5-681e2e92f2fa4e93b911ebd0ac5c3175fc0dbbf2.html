The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), symmetric type-pair valuations \(v_{ab}\), and a fixed team-size limit \(s\ge3\), choose nonnegative densities \(\lambda_S\) for multisets \(S\) of at most \(s\) types, subject to \(\sum_S q_a(S)\lambda_S=\mu_a\) for each type \(a\). A type-\(a\) member of \(S\) receives utility \(u_a(S)=\sum_b(q_b(S)-\mathbf 1[a=b])v_{ab}\); require every assigned member to have nonnegative utility. 2. The finite linear program

Let $\mathcal S_+$ consist of the profiles giving strictly positive utility to every type present:

$$ \mathcal S_+ = \left\{ S\in\mathcal S: u_a(S)>0 \text{ whenever }q_a(S)>0 \right\}. $$

The total number of profiles is

$$ N=|\mathcal S| =\sum_{j=1}^{s}\binom{\tau+j-1}{j} =\binom{\tau+s}{s}-1. $$

Thus $N=O_s(\tau^s)$ for fixed $s$. Each utility contains exactly $|S|-1$ interaction terms, counting multiplicities. Consequently, all utilities and their signs can be computed by rational arithmetic in time polynomial in $N,L,s$.

Define

$$ Q_{aS}=q_a(S), \qquad P_+=\{\lambda\ge0:Q\lambda=\mu\}, $$

where columns are indexed by $\mathcal S_+$.

Proposition 1. Optimal welfare is positive exactly when $P_+$ is nonempty.

Proof. A positive-welfare individually rational assignment cannot use a zero-utility team, so all its used profiles belong to $\mathcal S_+$. Conversely, every point of $P_+$ uses only strictly positive utilities and therefore has positive welfare. If $P_+$ is empty, every individually rational assignment has welfare zero, and the singleton assignment is optimal. $\square$

Rational LP feasibility therefore decides positivity in polynomial time.

Suppose henceforth that $P_+$ is nonempty. Summing its population equations gives

$$ \sum_{S\in\mathcal S_+}|S|\lambda_S=1. $$

In particular, every coordinate lies in $[0,1]$, so $P_+$ is compact.

For each positive profile define the positive rational number

$$ R_S = \prod_{\substack{a\in T\\q_a(S)>0}} u_a(S)^{q_a(S)} $$

and the real coefficient

$$ c_S=\log R_S. $$

The logarithmic welfare is exactly

$$ \ell(\lambda)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_+}c_S\lambda_S. $$

Maximizing welfare is therefore equivalent to maximizing this linear function over $P_+$. A maximum exists and is attained at a vertex.

Choose an integer $H\ge1$ such that the reduced numerators and denominators of every positive utility and every $R_S$ are at most $2^H$. Such an $H$ is computable in polynomial time; for fixed $s$,

$$ H=O_s(L+1). $$

Rational output size

Write the input masses as $\mu_a=n_a/d_a$ and put $D=\prod_a d_a$.

At a vertex, the columns corresponding to positive coordinates must be linearly independent: a dependence would permit a sufficiently small feasible perturbation in both directions. Thus there are at most $\tau$ positive coordinates.

Selecting independent rows gives a square integer matrix $B$. Its column norms are at most $s$, so Hadamard’s inequality gives

$$ 1\le|\det B|\le s^\tau. $$

Cramer’s rule consequently gives a common coordinate denominator $D|\det B|$. Hence an optimal assignment has a rational description of polynomial binary length. Selecting that assignment, rather than representing it, is the unresolved issue.

Approximation

For any integer $p\ge1$, approximate each $c_S$ by a rational $\widetilde c_S$ with

$$ |\widetilde c_S-c_S|\le 2^{-p-1}. $$

These approximations are polynomial-time computable. Indeed, after writing a positive rational as $2^k x$ with $1\le x<2$, use

$$ \log x = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad t=\frac{x-1}{x+1}, $$

where $0\le t<1/3$. The tail after the first $m$ terms is at most

$$ \frac{2t^{2m+1}}{1-t^2}. $$

The same series at $t=1/3$ computes $\log2$. Computing it with the additional accuracy required by the factor $k$ uses only polynomially many terms and bits.

Because $\sum_S\lambda_S\le1$, the approximate objective satisfies

$$ |\widetilde\ell(\lambda)-\ell(\lambda)| \le 2^{-p-1} $$

throughout $P_+$. If $\widehat\lambda$ maximizes $\widetilde\ell$, comparison with a true optimum gives

$$ \ell(\widehat\lambda)\ge\ell(\lambda^*)-2^{-p}. $$

Thus

$$ W(\widehat\lambda)\ge e^{-2^{-p}}W^*. $$

The running time is polynomial in $N,L,s,p$.

3. Exact recovery from reduced-cost separation

The following argument avoids objective-gap bounds involving mass denominators.

Delete dependent rows of $Q$, obtaining a full-row-rank matrix $A$ with $r$ rows, and retain the corresponding entries $b$ of $\mu$. Because feasibility has already been established, the deleted equations follow from the retained equations.

For a column basis $B$ and a nonbasic column $a_j$, define the reduced cost

$$ \rho_j(c) = c_j-c_B^{\mathsf T}B^{-1}a_j. $$

For this maximization problem, a primal-feasible basis is optimal whenever all its reduced costs are nonpositive.

Primitive circuits

A primitive circuit of $A$ is an integer vector $z\ne0$ such that:

  • $Az=0$;
  • its support is minimal among supports of nonzero kernel vectors;
  • the greatest common divisor of its nonzero entries is $1$.

The fundamental dependence associated with $B$ and $a_j$ can be normalized to such a circuit with $z_j>0$. It satisfies

$$ \rho_j(c)=\frac{1}{z_j}\sum_i z_i c_i. $$

It uses at most $r+1$ columns.

Moreover, every primitive circuit satisfies

$$ \|z\|_\infty\le C, \qquad C=s^r. $$

To justify this, select all but one column of the circuit, which are independent, and select equally many independent rows. The dependence coefficients are proportional to determinants of square submatrices. Every such determinant has absolute value at most $s^r$, by Hadamard’s inequality. Dividing their integer vector by its gcd can only decrease the coefficients.

Lemma 2. Suppose $K$ bounds the primitive circuit coefficients, and $g>0$ bounds every nonzero reduced cost away from zero:

$$ |\rho_j(c)|\ge g \quad\text{whenever }\rho_j(c)\ne0. $$

If

$$ |\widetilde c_i-c_i| \le \eta, \qquad \eta=\frac{g}{4(1+rK)}, $$

then any primal-and-dual feasible optimal basis for the rational objective $\widetilde c$ is also optimal for $c$.

Proof. From the circuit representation,

$$ |\rho_j(\widetilde c)-\rho_j(c)| \le \eta\left(1+\sum_{i\in B}\frac{|z_i|}{z_j}\right) \le\eta(1+rK) =\frac g4. $$

An optimal basis for $\widetilde c$ has $\rho_j(\widetilde c)\le0$. If $\rho_j(c)>0$, the assumed separation gives $\rho_j(c)\ge g$, contradicting the error bound. Thus all true reduced costs are nonpositive. Primal feasibility then proves true optimality. $\square$

Obtaining an optimal basis, including degeneracy

For completeness, an appropriate basis can be obtained in polynomial time from the rational auxiliary LP.

First obtain an optimal primal vertex $x$ and an optimal dual point $y$. An optimal primal vertex can be found by successively minimizing and fixing coordinates on the rational optimal face. Its positive-support columns are independent and, by complementary slackness, tight at $y$.

If the tight columns at $y$ do not span $\mathbb R^r$, choose an integer direction $d$ orthogonal to them. Full row rank ensures that some other column has nonzero scalar product with $d$. Choose its sign so that at least one dual inequality decreases, and move until the first new inequality becomes tight.

This movement preserves the dual objective: the primal positive-support columns were already tight, so

$$ b^{\mathsf T}d = \sum_{i:x_i>0}x_i a_i^{\mathsf T}d =0. $$

Each movement increases the rank of the tight columns. After at most $r$ movements, select a tight basis containing the primal support. This basis is primal and dual feasible.

Integer directions obtained from minors have polynomial binary length. Each movement introduces only a denominator of polynomial binary length, so the entire construction has polynomial bit complexity.

4. Exact optimization with bounded circuit coefficients

Let $\kappa$ denote the largest absolute entry of a primitive circuit, taking $\kappa=1$ if there are no circuits.

Theorem 3. Exact optimization is computable in time polynomial in $N,L,s,\kappa$. In particular, it is polynomial-time computable on families with polynomially bounded $\kappa$.

Proof. Consider a circuit with coefficients bounded by $K$. Since $c_i=\log R_i$,

$$ \Lambda=\sum_i z_i c_i = \log\left(\prod_i R_i^{z_i}\right). $$

Write the product, without necessarily reducing it, as $M_0/N_0$. There are at most $r+1$ terms, so

$$ 1\le M_0,N_0\le 2^{H(r+1)K}. $$

If $M_0>N_0$, then

$$ \log(M_0/N_0) \ge\frac{M_0-N_0}{M_0} \ge2^{-H(r+1)K}. $$

The reverse case follows by interchanging numerator and denominator. Consequently, a nonzero reduced cost satisfies

$$ |\rho_j(c)| \ge \frac{2^{-H(r+1)K}}{K}. $$

We may therefore use

$$ g_K = 2^{-\left(H(r+1)K+\lceil\log_2K\rceil\right)}. $$

The precision required by Lemma 2 is polynomial in $N,L,s,K$.

A bound on $\kappa$ need not be supplied. Try $K=1,2,4,\ldots$. For each value, construct the approximate objective and an optimal basis. Compute its fundamental primitive circuits by rational linear algebra. If all their coefficients are bounded by $K$, the preceding gap argument and Lemma 2 certify that basis as truly optimal. Otherwise double $K$.

The procedure stops by the first $K\ge\kappa$, hence uses time polynomial in $N,L,s,\kappa$. $\square$

Why this condition does not settle the task

Circuit coefficients can be exponential even in very simple societies.

Take types $0,\ldots,n$, with all valuations equal to $1$. Among the positive columns are

$$ p_L=2e_0,\qquad p_R=2e_n,\qquad t_i=2e_i+e_{i+1}\quad(0\le i<n). $$

They satisfy

$$ -2^n p_L + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^i2^{n-i}t_i + (-1)^n p_R =0. $$

The columns $p_L,t_0,\ldots,t_{n-1}$ are independent: their determinant, in the displayed order, is $2$. Thus the displayed dependence spans the kernel on these $n+2$ columns. Every coefficient is nonzero, so its support is minimal; the coefficient of $p_R$ has magnitude $1$, so it is primitive.

Therefore

$$ \kappa\ge2^n. $$

All these profiles have size at most $3$, so the example applies to every fixed $s\ge3$. It shows failure of a general polynomial circuit bound, not computational hardness.

5. Exact optimization with fixed multiplicative rank

Let $U$ be the set of distinct positive utilities occurring in $\mathcal S_+$. Define its multiplicative rank by

$$ d = \dim_{\mathbb Q} \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{\log u:u\in U\}. $$

Equivalently, this measures the number of independent utility values under multiplicative relations with integer exponents.

Theorem 4. For every fixed $d$, exact optimization on instances of multiplicative rank at most $d$ is computable in time polynomial in $N,L,s$. The rank is itself computable in polynomial time without prime factorization.

5.1. Computing the rank by gcd refinement

Start with the distinct integers greater than $1$ appearing as numerators or denominators of utilities in $U$. Maintain multiplicative representations of every original integer in the current list of factors.

Whenever distinct factors $a,b$ have $g=\gcd(a,b)>1$, replace them by the nonunit factors among

$$ g,\quad a/g,\quad b/g, $$

merge duplicates, and update the representations.

The product of the distinct current factors decreases by a factor of at least $2$: before duplicate removal, its replacement contribution is

$$ g(a/g)(b/g)=ab/g. $$

Initially the logarithm of that product is at most $2|U|H\log2$. Thus there are at most $2|U|H$ refinement steps.

Every factor remains at most $2^H$. Every exponent representing an original integer is at most $H$, because each factor is at least $2$ and the represented integer is at most $2^H$. Therefore the list and all representation data have polynomial size throughout.

At termination, the factors $h_1,\ldots,h_t$ are pairwise coprime, and each utility has a representation

$$ u=\prod_{j=1}^{t}h_j^{e_j(u)}, \qquad e_j(u)\in\mathbb Z, \qquad |e_j(u)|\le H. $$

The logarithms of these factors are rationally independent. Indeed, a rational relation can be cleared to an integer relation. A prime dividing $h_j$ divides no other factor, so unique prime factorization forces its corresponding coefficient to be zero.

It follows that $d$ is exactly the rational rank of the integer vectors

$$ e(u)=(e_1(u),\ldots,e_t(u)). $$

Gaussian elimination computes this rank in polynomial time.

5.2. Expressing all utilities through a fixed number of generators

If $d=0$, all utilities equal $1$, and every point of $P_+$ is optimal. Assume $d\ge1$.

Choose utilities $g_1,\ldots,g_d$ whose exponent vectors form a basis. Select $d$ coordinates on which these vectors form a nonsingular integer matrix $M$. Set

$$ \delta=|\det M|, \qquad F=d!H^d. $$

The determinant expansion gives $\delta\le F$. Cramer’s rule gives integers $f_i(u)$ such that

$$ \log u = \frac1\delta\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_i(u)\log g_i, \qquad |f_i(u)|\le F. $$

Matching the selected coordinates suffices because every utility exponent vector lies in the span of the chosen basis vectors.

Consequently,

$$ c_S = \frac1\delta\sum_{i=1}^{d}h_i(S)\log g_i, $$

where

$$ h_i(S) = \sum_{\substack{a\in T\\q_a(S)>0}} q_a(S)f_i(u_a(S)), \qquad |h_i(S)|\le sF. $$

The restriction $q_a(S)>0$ ensures that every utility used here is defined.

5.3. A uniform reduced-cost gap

Use the universal circuit bound $C=s^r$. For a fundamental circuit,

$$ \rho_j(c) = \frac1{\delta z_j} \sum_{i=1}^{d}t_i\log g_i, $$

with

$$ |t_i|\le T_0:=(r+1)CsF. $$

We use the following integer specialization of the Baker–Wüstholz theorem: for each fixed number $h$ of terms, an effectively computable constant $K_h$ satisfies

$$ \left|\sum_{i=1}^{h}b_i\log a_i\right| \ge \exp\left( -K_h\log B \prod_{i=1}^{h}\max(1,\log a_i) \right) $$

whenever the form is nonzero, the bases are integers at least $2$, the coefficients are nonzero integers, and $B=\max(3,|b_1|,\ldots,|b_h|)$. Multiplicative independence is unnecessary. This is [Theorem 3.1 of Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis](https://arxiv.org/html/1304.5429).

Write each $g_i=A_i/B_i$ in lowest terms. Replacing its logarithm by $\log A_i-\log B_i$, and discarding unit bases and zero coefficients, produces at most $2d$ terms. All bases are at most $2^H$, and all coefficients have magnitude at most $T_0$.

Thus, for an effectively computable integer $K_d'$ depending only on $d$, every nonzero reduced cost satisfies

$$ |\rho_j(c)| \ge \frac1{FC} \exp\left( -K_d'(H+1)^{2d}\log\max(3,T_0) \right). $$

If $b(x)$ denotes the binary length of a positive integer $x$, define

$$ p_0 = K_d'(H+1)^{2d}b(\max(3,T_0)) + b(FC). $$

The preceding bound implies

$$ |\rho_j(c)|\ge2^{-p_0} $$

whenever the reduced cost is nonzero. Furthermore,

$$ \log_2 C=r\log_2s, \qquad \log_2F=O_d(\log(H+1)). $$

Therefore $p_0$ is polynomial in $N,L,s$ for fixed $d$.

Approximate each $c_S$ to the accuracy required by Lemma 2, using $g=2^{-p_0}$ and $K=C$, and solve the resulting rational LP. Although $C$ can be exponential, its contribution to the approximation precision is only through $\log C$. Lemma 2 proves that the returned basis is exactly optimal. This completes the proof of Theorem 4. $\square$

Consequence for bounded valuation variety

If at most $k$ distinct valuation values occur, each positive utility is a sum of between $1$ and $s-1$ such values. Hence

$$ |U| \le \sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\binom{k+j-1}{j} = \binom{k+s-1}{s-1}-1. $$

Since $d\le|U|$, Theorem 4 gives polynomial-time exact optimization for fixed $s,k$, including every fixed finite valuation alphabet.

6. Comparing supplied assignments captures product-of-powers comparison

Define the comparison problem to take a society and two explicitly listed, binary rational, feasible assignments of positive welfare, and decide whether the first has at least the welfare of the second.

Proposition 5. For every fixed $s\ge3$, this comparison problem and product-of-powers comparison admit polynomial-time many-one reductions to each other.

Proof: assignment comparison to product comparison. For supplied assignments $x,y$,

$$ \log W(x)-\log W(y) = \sum_S(x_S-y_S)\log R_S. $$

A common denominator of the supplied densities has polynomial binary length. Clearing it produces integer coefficients of polynomial binary length. Splitting each rational $R_S$ into numerator and denominator turns the sign test into comparison of two products of binary-encoded integers raised to binary-encoded nonnegative integer powers.

Proof: product comparison to assignment comparison. It suffices to determine the sign of

$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{h}b_i\log a_i, $$

where $a_i>0$ are binary rationals and $b_i$ are nonzero binary integers. Ordinary comparison of two products has this form after moving one product to the other side. The empty form is trivial.

Set

$$ B_0=\sum_i|b_i|, \qquad w_i=\frac{|b_i|}{B_0}. $$

For each term introduce two types $i^+,i^-$, each of mass $w_i/2$. Give them valuations

$$ v_{i^+i^+}=v_{i^-i^-}=1, \qquad v_{i^+i^-}=a_i. $$

Set cross-block valuations to $0$.

Within block $i$, consider two assignments:

  • A mixed pair $\{i^+,i^-\}$ of density $w_i/2$. Every member receives utility $a_i$.
  • Two repeated-type pairs, each of density $w_i/4$. Every member receives utility $1$.

Both assign exactly mass $w_i/2$ to each type. Both have strictly positive welfare. Their block contributions to logarithmic welfare are respectively $w_i\log a_i$ and $0$.

For $b_i>0$, let $x$ use the mixed pair and $y$ the repeated-type pairs. For $b_i<0$, reverse these choices. Then

$$ \log W(x)-\log W(y) = \frac1{B_0}\sum_i b_i\log a_i. $$

All teams have size $2$, all masses sum to $1$, and every constructed number has polynomial binary length. This proves the reduction. $\square$

This is a reduction to comparing prescribed assignments. It does not force an optimizer to choose between those two assignments: other feasible assignments remain available. Therefore it supplies no hardness classification for the target optimization problem.

7. Conditional polynomial time for unrestricted valuations

Consider the following explicitly unproved hypothesis.

Polynomial logarithmic separation hypothesis. There is a fixed polynomial $f$ such that every nonzero form

$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{h}b_i\log a_i, $$

with integer bases $a_i\ge2$, nonzero integer coefficients $b_i$, and total binary encoding length at most $K$, satisfies

$$ |\Lambda|\ge2^{-f(K)}. $$

Theorem 6, conditional. Under this hypothesis, exact optimization is polynomial-time computable for every fixed $s\ge3$.

Proof. Every fundamental circuit has at most $r+1$ terms and coefficients bounded by $C=s^r$. Splitting its rational bases $R_S$ into numerator and denominator gives an integer-logarithm form of encoding length

$$ K=O\bigl((r+1)(H+r\log s+1)\bigr). $$

This is polynomial in the input size for fixed $s$.

The hypothesis and the identity $\rho_j=\Lambda/z_j$ give

$$ |\rho_j(c)|\ge C^{-1}2^{-f(K)} $$

for every nonzero reduced cost. Lemma 2 now requires only polynomially many precision bits, because $\log C=r\log s$. Rational LP optimization then returns an exact optimum. $\square$

For context, the uniform Lang–Waldschmidt conjecture stated in [Conjecture 3.2 of Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis](https://arxiv.org/html/1304.5429) asserts, at its parameter value $1$, a constant $c_0>0$ giving

$$ |\Lambda| \ge \frac{c_0^{h}B} {\left(\prod_i|b_i|\prod_i a_i\right)^2} $$

for nonzero forms, with $B=\max(e,|b_1|,\ldots,|b_h|)$.

Taking logarithms, and using $B\ge1$, yields

$$ -\log|\Lambda| \le h\max(0,-\log c_0) + 2\sum_i\bigl(\log|b_i|+\log a_i\bigr) = O(K). $$

Thus this conjecture implies the separation hypothesis. This implication is conditional; no conjectural bound has been used in the unconditional results.
