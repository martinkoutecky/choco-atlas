The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support initial measure \(\mu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{a_i}\), with binary-rational masses \(w_i>0\), rational priors \(a_i\in[0,1]\), a fixed detection model, and threshold \(\theta\), iterate \(F_{\mu}(a)=\frac{\int_{\{b:d(a,b)\le\theta\}} b\,d\mu(b)}{\mu(\{b:d(a,b)\le\theta\})}\) and \(\mu_{t+1}=(F_{\mu_t})_\#\mu_t\); compute the exact terminal atoms and their masses, and determine the worst-case running time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\mu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{a_i}\), with distinct binary-rational priors \(a_i\in[0,1]\), binary-rational masses \(w_i>0\), and \(\sum_iw_i=1\). Here \(\tau\ge1\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The confidence threshold \(\theta>0\) is binary rational; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Fix two hypotheses, \(m=2\), with observation laws \(Y\mid h_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(Y\mid h_1\sim\mathcal N(1,1)\), unit error costs, and zero costs for correct decisions. Writing their densities as \(f_0,f_1\), let \(g_b(y)=1\) exactly when \((1-b)f_1(y)>bf_0(y)\), and otherwise let \(g_b(y)=0\).
Define \(J(a,b)=a\Pr[g_b(Y)=1\mid h_0]+(1-a)\Pr[g_b(Y)=0\mid h_1]\) and the directed Bayes risk error divergence \(d(a,b)=J(a,b)-J(a,a)\).
Update synchronously by \(F_\mu(a)=\frac{\int_{d(a,b)\le\theta}b\,d\mu(b)}{\mu(\{b:d(a,b)\le\theta\})}\) and \(\mu_{t+1}=(F_{\mu_t})_\#\mu_t\). Each atom includes itself; coincident atoms merge by adding their masses. A terminal measure is \(\mu_T\) with \(\mu_{T+1}=\mu_T\).
Use a Turing machine with an oracle deciding \(d(a,b)\le q\) exactly for binary-rational \(a,b,q\); each query costs one step, and constructing and reading its rational arguments incurs ordinary bit cost. Running times are measured in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(m=2\) fixed.

Do all such trajectories reach a terminal measure in finite time? If they do, is computing its exact atoms and masses polynomial-time in \(\tau,L\) in this oracle model, or is it NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

The discrete Bayes risk result asserts finite-time convergence and separation of distinct limiting opinions by \(\min\{d(a,b),d(b,a)\}\ge\theta\), without a computational complexity bound. [Varshney](https://krvarshney.github.io/pubs/Varshney_jstsp2014.pdf)
For continuum societies with absolute-distance confidence, partial convergence results are known; they concern a different confidence rule. No result settling the exact computation problem above was located. [Blondel–Hendrickx–Tsitsiklis](https://www.mit.edu/~jnt/Papers/J124-09-BHT-Krause.pdf)

Conclusion

No. Finite-time termination fails already for \(\tau=3\), with dyadic inputs and all priors strictly inside \((0,1)\). Consequently, computing a terminal measure is not an everywhere-defined task on the stated input class, so the conditional polynomial-time versus NP-hard question does not arise as formulated.

We prove this using
\(\theta=\tfrac14\) and
\(\mu_0=\tfrac14\delta_{1/256}+\tfrac12\delta_{33/64}+\tfrac14\delta_{255/256}\).

The two outer atoms remain fixed. The middle atom approaches \(\tfrac12\) geometrically but never reaches it.

1. Bayes-risk formulas and bounds

Let \(\Phi\) and \(\varphi\) denote the standard normal distribution function and density. For \(b\in(0,1)\), the likelihood ratio is
\(f_1(y)/f_0(y)=\exp(y-\tfrac12)\).
Thus \(g_b(y)=1\) precisely when
\(y>c(b)\), where
\(c(b)=\tfrac12+\log\!\bigl(b/(1-b)\bigr)\).

Define the two error probabilities by
\(\alpha(b)=\Pr[g_b(Y)=1\mid h_0]=\Phi(-c(b))\) and
\(\beta(b)=\Pr[g_b(Y)=0\mid h_1]=\Phi(c(b)-1)\).
Then
\(J(a,b)=a\alpha(b)+(1-a)\beta(b)\).

Write \(R(a)=J(a,a)\), so \(d(a,b)=J(a,b)-R(a)\). The rule \(g_a\) minimizes the risk: at each observation \(y\), choosing decision \(1\) contributes \(af_0(y)\), while choosing decision \(0\) contributes \((1-a)f_1(y)\). The defining inequality for \(g_a\) selects the smaller contribution.

The two constant decision rules therefore give
\(0\le R(a)\le\min\{a,1-a\}\).
Also, since \(\alpha(b),\beta(b)\in[0,1]\),

\(R(a)\le J(a,b)=R(b)+(a-b)(\alpha(b)-\beta(b))\le R(b)+|a-b|\).

Interchanging \(a,b\) proves
\(|R(a)-R(b)|\le |a-b|\).

At \(a=\tfrac12\), both error probabilities equal \(\Phi(-\tfrac12)\), hence
\(R(\tfrac12)=\Phi(-\tfrac12)\).
Using \(\pi>3\), we have
\(\varphi(u)\le 1/\sqrt{2\pi}<1/\sqrt6<5/12\).
Consequently, for every \(z>0\),

\(\Phi(-z)=\tfrac12-\int_0^z\varphi(u)\,du>\tfrac12-\tfrac5{12}z\).

In particular,
\(R(\tfrac12)>\tfrac7{24}\).

2. A persistent directed confidence pattern

Set
\(e=\tfrac1{256}\) and \(I=[\tfrac12,\tfrac{33}{64}]\).
We show that whenever the support is \(\{e,x,1-e\}\) with \(x\in I\):

  • each outer atom accepts only itself;
  • the middle atom accepts all three atoms.

All inequalities below are strict.

The outer atoms reject the middle atom

For \(x\in I\),

\(\tfrac12\le c(x)\le\tfrac12+\log(\tfrac{33}{31})<\tfrac12+\tfrac2{31}=\tfrac{35}{62}\),

where the strict inequality follows from \(\log(1+u)<u\) for \(u>0\). Thus

\(\alpha(x)>\Phi(-\tfrac{35}{62})>\tfrac12-\tfrac5{12}\tfrac{35}{62}=\tfrac{197}{744}\),

and

\(\beta(x)\ge\Phi(-\tfrac12)>\tfrac7{24}>\tfrac{197}{744}\).

Since \(J(a,x)\) is a convex combination of these two error probabilities,
\(J(a,x)>\tfrac{197}{744}\) for every \(a\in[0,1]\).
For either \(a=e\) or \(a=1-e\), the preceding risk bound gives \(R(a)\le e\). Therefore

\(d(a,x)>\tfrac{197}{744}-\tfrac1{256}=\tfrac{6211}{23808}>\tfrac14\).

Both outer atoms reject the middle atom.

The middle atom accepts both outer atoms

The inequality \(R(e)\le e\), together with nonnegativity of both error terms, implies

\((1-e)\beta(e)\le R(e)\le e\), hence
\(\beta(e)\le\tfrac1{255}\).

Likewise, \(R(1-e)\le e\) implies
\(\alpha(1-e)\le\tfrac1{255}\).
Consequently, for \(x\in I\),

\(J(x,e)\le x+\tfrac{1-x}{255}\le\tfrac{33}{64}+\tfrac1{255}\),

and

\(J(x,1-e)\le\tfrac{x}{255}+1-x\le\tfrac12+\tfrac1{255}\le\tfrac{33}{64}+\tfrac1{255}\).

The Lipschitz bound on \(R\) gives

\(R(x)\ge R(\tfrac12)-|x-\tfrac12|>\tfrac7{24}-\tfrac1{64}\).

For either \(b=e\) or \(b=1-e\), it follows that

\(d(x,b)<\tfrac{33}{64}+\tfrac1{255}-(\tfrac7{24}-\tfrac1{64})=\tfrac{23}{96}+\tfrac1{255}=\tfrac{1987}{8160}<\tfrac14\).

Thus the middle atom accepts both outer atoms.

The outer atoms reject each other

We have
\(c(e)=\tfrac12-\log255<0\) and
\(c(1-e)=\tfrac12+\log255>1\).
Indeed, \(\log255>\log2>\tfrac12\), with the last inequality following from
\(\log2=\int_1^2u^{-1}\,du>\tfrac12\).

Hence
\(\alpha(e)>\tfrac12\) and
\(\beta(1-e)>\tfrac12\).
Using \(R(e),R(1-e)\le e\),

\(d(e,1-e)>(1-e)\tfrac12-e=\tfrac{253}{512}>\tfrac14\),

and

\(d(1-e,e)>(1-e)\tfrac12-e=\tfrac{253}{512}>\tfrac14\).

Finally, each atom accepts itself because \(d(a,a)=0\). This proves the claimed confidence pattern throughout \(I\).

3. The exact trajectory never terminates

Consider the measure
\(\mu_x=\tfrac14\delta_e+\tfrac12\delta_x+\tfrac14\delta_{1-e}\), with \(x\in I\).

By the confidence pattern just proved, both outer atoms remain fixed. The middle atom averages the entire measure, whose total mass is one. Its updated location is therefore

\(F_{\mu_x}(x)=\tfrac14e+\tfrac12x+\tfrac14(1-e)=\tfrac14+\tfrac12x\).

This lies in \(I\), so the same confidence pattern applies at the next step. Starting from \(x_0=\tfrac{33}{64}=\tfrac12+\tfrac1{64}\), induction gives

\(x_t=\tfrac12+2^{-(t+6)}\)

and

\(\mu_t=\tfrac14\delta_{1/256}+\tfrac12\delta_{\frac12+2^{-(t+6)}}+\tfrac14\delta_{255/256}\)

for every nonnegative integer \(t\).

The three locations remain distinct. Moreover,
\(x_t-x_{t+1}=2^{-(t+7)}>0\).
Thus \(\mu_{t+1}\ne\mu_t\) at every finite time: this trajectory has no terminal measure. Every location along it is dyadic, so the counterexample also avoids any issue about the oracle’s permitted rational arguments.

The trajectory does converge to
\(\mu_\infty=\tfrac14\delta_{1/256}+\tfrac12\delta_{1/2}+\tfrac14\delta_{255/256}\).
This limiting measure is fixed, but it is never attained. Furthermore, Section 2 gives
\(d(\tfrac12,e)<\theta\), so its distinct atoms also violate the proposed separation condition
\(\min\{d(a,b),d(b,a)\}\ge\theta\).

Accordingly, the finite-time and separation assertions of the cited [Varshney paper, Proposition 2](https://krvarshney.github.io/pubs/Varshney_jstsp2014.pdf) cannot hold for the directed update rule specified here. The explicit obstruction is that the middle atom continues averaging two fixed outer atoms that reject it.

4. Computational consequence

On this valid input, the requested finite-time terminal measure does not exist. Therefore there is no total function assigning such a terminal measure to every society in the question, regardless of access to the comparison oracle.

This settles the question as stated negatively. It does not classify the different problems of computing an asymptotic limit or computing a terminal measure under a promise of finite termination.
