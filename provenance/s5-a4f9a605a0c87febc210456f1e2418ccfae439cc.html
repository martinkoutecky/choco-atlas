The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational \(c\in(0,1)\) and a piecewise-rational valuation distribution \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\), draw \(n\) independent valuations from \(\mu\), apply the paper's random priority order and bluff equilibrium, and let \(P_n\) be the probability that the winner has less than the highest valuation. For a finite-type society, the input is its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. The distribution below is atomless, so this finite-support convention does not apply; \(k\) counts density pieces, not types.
Input: binary rationals \(c,\varepsilon\in(0,1)\) and an atomless probability measure \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\). Its density is specified on \(k\) rational intervals by nonnegative rational functions with rational coefficients, encoded as dense numerator and denominator polynomials, with denominators nonzero on their intervals; the density is zero elsewhere and integrates to \(1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, \(F\) its CDF, and \(\alpha<\beta\) its support endpoints; exclude the null endpoint \(\beta\) from possible sampled valuations. There is \(m=1\) object.
For each integer \(n\ge2\), sample valuations independently from \(\mu\) and choose an independent uniformly random priority permutation, relabelled \(1,\ldots,n\). Initialize active bidders \(A=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), payment \(p=\alpha\), and upper bound \(u=\beta\).
In each round choose \(q=\max\{x\in[\alpha,\beta]:F(x)=(1-c)F(p)+cF(u)\}\), and query active bidders publicly in increasing priority order. Bidder \(i\) says yes exactly when \(v_i\ge q\), or when \(p\le v_i<q\) and no earlier active bidder has said yes in that round; otherwise it says no.
One yes ends the auction: that bidder wins and pays \(p\). With at least two yes bids, retain precisely those bidders and set \(p=q\), leaving \(u\) unchanged; with zero yes bids retain everyone and set \(u=q\), leaving \(p\) unchanged. Repeat. If infinitely many rounds occur, the largest-labelled bidder remaining forever wins and pays the supremum of the round payments.
Let \(W_n\) be the winner, \(P_n=\Pr[v_{W_n}<\max_i v_i]\), and \(\pi(\mu,c)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}P_n\); probability includes both valuation sampling and priority randomization.

Deterministic polynomial-time approximation

The problem is deterministically polynomial-time solvable. In fact, \(\pi(\mu,c)\) is independent of the promised atomless distribution \(\mu\).

The proof below derives an exact asymptotic formula and then gives a polynomial-time approximation algorithm. The [linked finite-bidder paper](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/64767897/Herings_2012_fraction_auctions_the_tradeoff_between.pdf), Section 6.1, supplies finite-bidder bounds; the asymptotic and computational arguments here are established directly.

1. Quantile reduction and the exact finite-bidder formula

Put \(\rho=1-c\) and \(a=-\log\rho>0\).

Because \(F\) is continuous, the variables \(X_i=F(v_i)\) are independent and uniform on \([0,1]\). Almost surely, they are distinct and avoid every threshold considered below. Monotonicity of \(F\) then preserves their ordering. Flat portions of \(F\) cause no difficulty: comparisons with a threshold of CDF value \(t\) agree almost surely with comparisons of \(X_i\) with \(t\).

We first establish two auction invariants.

  • Every original bidder whose valuation is at least the current payment remains active.
  • The maximum valuation remains active and is at least the current payment.

Initially both statements hold. In any round satisfying them, the first active bidder with valuation at least the payment says yes. Consequently, zero-yes rounds cannot occur.

If at least two bidders say yes, at most one of them has valuation below the query: a below-query yes suppresses every subsequent below-query yes. Thus at least one yes bidder has valuation at least the query. All such bidders remain active, and the new payment is the query. Both invariants are preserved.

It follows that, as long as the auction continues, the payment and query in quantile coordinates are respectively

\[ \text{ } \]

\(p_j^*=1-\rho^j,\qquad q_j^*=1-\rho^{j+1},\qquad j=0,1,\ldots.\)

Here and below starred prices denote CDF values, not valuation-space prices. Since \(\max_i X_i<1\) almost surely, the second invariant also proves almost-sure finite termination: an infinite run would eventually have \(p_j^*>\max_i X_i\).

Consider the bands

\(I_j=[1-\rho^j,\,1-\rho^{j+1}),\qquad j=0,1,\ldots.\)

Let \(I_J\) be the highest nonempty band, and let \(M\) be its occupancy.

If \(M\ge2\), every earlier round has at least two above-query bidders, so it continues. In round \(J\), there are no above-query bidders. The earliest-priority bidder in \(I_J\) is the sole yes bidder and wins. Conditional on the valuations, that bidder is the maximum with probability \(1/M\), because priority is independently randomized.

If \(M=1\), allocation is efficient. Indeed, a one-yes round with an above-query bidder can only allocate to the unique above-query bidder, who is the maximum. A one-yes round without an above-query bidder selects from the highest nonempty band, whose occupancy in this case is one.

Hence, conditional on band occupancies, inefficiency has probability \(1-1/M\). The probability that the highest nonempty band is \(I_j\), with occupancy \(m\), is

\(\binom nm(c\rho^j)^m(1-\rho^j)^{n-m}.\)

Therefore

\[ \text{ } \]

\[\boxed{\displaystyle P_n=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=2}^{n}\left(1-\frac1m\right)\binom nm(c\rho^j)^m(1-\rho^j)^{n-m}.}\tag{1}\]

We use the usual convention \(0^0=1\). As a check, this gives

\(P_2=\dfrac{c^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}=\dfrac{c}{2(2-c)}.\)

In particular, every \(P_n\), and thus \(\pi(\mu,c)\), is distribution-independent.

2. The asymptotic periodic function

Define, for \(t>0\),

\[g_c(t)=e^{-t}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac1m\right)\frac{(ct)^m}{m!}.\tag{2}\]

This is the Poisson analogue of a band's contribution in (1). Two useful bounds are

\[0\le g_c(t)\le \frac{c^2t^2}{4},\qquad g_c(t)\le e^{-\rho t}-e^{-t}.\tag{3}\]

For completeness, the first follows from the integral representation

\[\boxed{\displaystyle g_c(z)=z^2\int_0^c x\log(c/x)e^{-(1-x)z}\,dx.}\tag{4}\]

To verify (4), expand the exponential and use

\(\displaystyle \int_0^c x^{m-1}\log(c/x)\,dx=\frac{c^m}{m^2}.\)

The coefficient of \(e^{-z}z^m\) is then \((1-1/m)c^m/m!\), as required. For positive \(t\), dropping the exponential inside the integral gives the first bound in (3). The second follows from (2) by replacing \(1-1/m\) by one and including the term \(m=1\).

Define

\[\boxed{\displaystyle G_c(s)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}g_c(e^{s-aj}),\qquad s\in\mathbb R.}\tag{5}\]

The bounds (3) prove locally uniform convergence: the small-argument tail is geometric, and the large-argument tail decays exponentially in \(e^{s-aj}\). Thus \(G_c\) is continuous. Reindexing proves that it has period \(a\).

Furthermore, the second bound in (3) telescopes:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}\bigl(e^{-\rho e^{s-aj}}-e^{-e^{s-aj}}\bigr)=1.\)

Consequently,

\[0\le G_c(s)\le1.\tag{6}\]

We next prove the precise connection with finite populations:

\[\boxed{\displaystyle P_n-G_c(\log n)\longrightarrow0.}\tag{7}\]

Let \(h_n(t)\), for \(0<t\le n\), denote the inner sum in (1) after replacing \(\rho^j\) by \(t/n\). Then

\(\displaystyle h_n(t)=\sum_{m=2}^{n}\left(1-\frac1m\right)\binom nm(ct/n)^m(1-t/n)^{n-m}.\)

On every compact \(t\)-interval, \(h_n(t)\to g_c(t)\) uniformly. To justify uniformity explicitly, truncate the sum at a fixed occupancy. Every retained term converges uniformly by the elementary binomial-to-Poisson limit. The omitted terms are uniformly small by Markov's inequality, since band occupancy has mean \(ct\).

There are also uniform tail bounds:

\[h_n(t)\le \frac{c^2t^2}{2},\qquad h_n(t)\le e^{-\rho t}.\tag{8}\]

The first bounds the probability of at least two band occupants by their expected number of unordered pairs. The second bounds the probability of no bidder above the band.

Write \(\log n=aM+s\), where \(M\) is an integer and \(0\le s<a\). Reindexing (1) gives

\(\displaystyle P_n=\sum_{\ell\ge-M}h_n(e^{s-a\ell}),\qquad G_c(\log n)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z}g_c(e^{s-a\ell}).\)

For a fixed finite range of \(\ell\), convergence is uniform in \(s\in[0,a]\). Bounds (3) and (8) make both remaining tails uniformly small. This proves (7).

For any fixed \(s\), choose integers \(n_M\) nearest to \(e^{aM+s}\). Then \(\log n_M-aM\to s\). Continuity, periodicity, and (7) therefore yield

\[\boxed{\displaystyle \pi(\mu,c)=\max_{0\le s\le a}G_c(s).}\tag{9}\]

Thus the limsup becomes a maximum of a periodic function; an ordinary limit of \(P_n\) is not required.

3. A uniform complex-strip bound

The following bound is central to obtaining polynomial dependence on precision, including when \(c\) is very small.

For \(t>0\) and \(|y|<\pi/2\), representation (4) gives

\(\displaystyle |g_c(te^{iy})|\le t^2\int_0^c x\log(c/x)e^{-(1-x)t\cos y}\,dx=\sec^2(y)\,g_c(t\cos y).\)

Using (5) and (6),

\[\boxed{\displaystyle |G_c(s+iy)|\le\sec^2(y),\qquad |\operatorname{Im}(s+iy)|<\pi/2.}\tag{10}\]

The same tail estimates give locally uniform convergence throughout this strip, so \(G_c\) is holomorphic there. In particular,

\[|G_c(s+iy)|\le2\qquad\text{for }|y|\le\tfrac12.\tag{11}\]

Let its Fourier coefficients be

\(\displaystyle b_\ell=\frac1a\int_0^aG_c(s)e^{-2\pi i\ell s/a}\,ds.\)

For positive \(\ell\), shift the contour downward by \(i/2\); for negative \(\ell\), shift upward. Periodicity cancels the vertical sides. By (11),

\[|b_\ell|\le2e^{-\pi|\ell|/a}\le2e^{-2|\ell|/a}.\tag{12}\]

Hence the Fourier series converges absolutely and uniformly, and its tail satisfies

\[\boxed{\displaystyle \sum_{|\ell|>N}|b_\ell|\le\frac{4e^{-2(N+1)/a}}{1-e^{-2/a}}.}\tag{13}\]

The bound is uniform in \(c\); no factor depending exponentially on \(1/c\) appears.

4. The mean and the small-\(c\) case

Let \(D(c)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c^m/m^2\). The mean of \(G_c\) is

\(\displaystyle b_0=\frac1a\int_0^\infty g_c(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}.\)

By (4) and Tonelli's theorem,

\(\displaystyle \int_0^\infty g_c(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}=\int_0^c\frac{x\log(c/x)}{(1-x)^2}\,dx.\)

Call the final integral \(M(c)\). Differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle M'(c)=\frac1c\int_0^c\frac{x}{(1-x)^2}\,dx=\frac1{1-c}+\frac{\log(1-c)}c.\)

On the other hand, \(a'(c)=1/(1-c)\) and \(D'(c)=-\log(1-c)/c\). Both \(M(c)\) and \(a-D(c)\) vanish as \(c\) tends to zero. Therefore

\[\boxed{\displaystyle b_0=1-\frac{D(c)}a.}\tag{14}\]

This mean can be computed to \(b\) bits in time polynomial in \(L+b\). Here are explicit ways to avoid slowly convergent series:

  • For \(c\le1/2\), the defining series for \(D(c)\) has a geometric tail.
  • For \(c>1/2\), use

\(D(c)=K-D(1-c)-\log c\log(1-c)\),
where \(K=2D(1/2)+(\log2)^2\).

The latter identity follows by differentiating \(D(c)+D(1-c)+\log c\log(1-c)\) and evaluating at \(c=1/2\). Thus every dilogarithm series actually evaluated has argument at most \(1/2\).

Binary encoding gives \(c,\rho\ge2^{-L}\), and hence \(a\ge c\ge2^{-L}\). Allowing an additional polynomial number of guard bits controls the division and cancellation in (14).

Set

\(B=\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\rceil+10,\qquad \delta=2^{-B}.\)

If

\[c\le\frac1{2(B+10)},\tag{15}\]

then

\(a\le\frac{c}{1-c}\le\frac1{B+10}.\)

Taking \(N=0\) in (13) gives

\(\displaystyle \sup_s|G_c(s)-b_0|\le\frac{4e^{-2/a}}{1-e^{-2/a}}<\delta.\)

Compute \(b_0\) to absolute error \(\delta\) and return it. By (9), the answer has error less than \(2\delta<\varepsilon\).

This handles exponentially small \(c\) without enumerating a number of bands proportional to \(1/c\).

5. Polynomial-time evaluation in the remaining case

Assume that (15) fails. Since \(a\ge c\),

\[\frac1a<2(B+10).\tag{16}\]

We prove that \(G_c(s)\), for \(s\in[0,a]\), can now be evaluated to error \(2^{-b}\), with \(b\ge B\), in time polynomial in \(L+B+b\).

5.1. Truncating the band sum

Choose

\(D=b+B+L+10,\qquad J=\left\lceil\frac{D+L+1}{c}\right\rceil.\)

By the current lower bound on \(c\), \(J\) is polynomially bounded. Also \(aJ\ge D+L+1\).

For \(j=J+1+\ell\) and \(s\in[0,a]\), we have \(e^{s-aj}\le e^{-aJ-a\ell}\). Therefore the omitted small-argument tail is at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{e^{-2aJ}}{4(1-e^{-2a})}.\)

The elementary inequality \(1/(1-e^{-x})\le1+1/x\), together with (16), bounds this by

\(\displaystyle \frac{B+11}{4}e^{-2(D+L+1)}<2^{-b-2}.\)

For the omitted large-argument tail, write \(j=-J-1-\ell\) and \(R=e^{aJ}\). Then

\(\rho e^{s-aj}\ge R e^{a\ell}.\)

Using \(e^{a\ell}\ge1+a\ell\), the tail is at most

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\ell\ge0}e^{-R e^{a\ell}}\le\frac{e^{-R}}{1-e^{-aR}}<2^{-b-2}.\)

The last inequality follows from \(R\ge e^{D+L+1}\) and (16), which in particular imply \(aR>\log2\).

Thus only \(2J+1\) terms need evaluation.

5.2. Evaluating one term without exponentially many Poisson terms

Define

\(\displaystyle h(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^m}{m\,m!}.\)

Then (2) becomes

\[\boxed{\displaystyle g_c(t)=e^{-\rho t}\bigl(1-e^{-ct}-h(ct)\bigr).}\tag{17}\]

We give a polynomial-time evaluation method for \(h(\lambda)\), even when \(\lambda\) is very large.

For a requested precision \(2^{-q}\), let \(K=q+10\).

If \(\lambda<16K\), truncate the defining series at \(128K\). Beyond that point, the ratio of consecutive terms is at most \(1/8\). Using \(m!\ge(m/e)^m\) and \(e<3\), its tail before multiplication by \(e^{-\lambda}\) is bounded by

\(\displaystyle 2(3/8)^{128K}<2^{-q-2}.\)

The intermediates have polynomial bit length: in this case the unscaled series is at most \(e^{16K}\).

If \(\lambda\ge16K\), use the inverse-power approximation

\(\displaystyle S_K(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{j!}{\lambda^{j+1}}.\)

To bound its error, substitute \(v=1-u\) in an integral representation of \(h\):

\(\displaystyle h(\lambda)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{-\lambda v}-e^{-\lambda}}{1-v}\,dv.\)

On \(0\le v\le1/2\), expand

\(\displaystyle \frac1{1-v}=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}v^j+\frac{v^K}{1-v}.\)

The remainder integral is at most \(2K!/\lambda^{K+1}\). Extending the retained integrals from \(1/2\) to infinity contributes at most \(4e^{-\lambda/4}/\lambda\). The subtracted exponential contributes at most \(e^{-\lambda}\). Finally, on \(1/2\le v\le1\), the mean-value theorem bounds the original integrand by \(\lambda e^{-\lambda/2}\), so that part contributes at most \((\lambda/2)e^{-\lambda/2}\).

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle |h(\lambda)-S_K(\lambda)|\le\frac{2K!}{\lambda^{K+1}}+\frac4\lambda e^{-\lambda/4}+e^{-\lambda}+\frac\lambda2e^{-\lambda/2}.\)

For \(\lambda\ge16K\), this is at most \(6\cdot2^{-4K}<2^{-q-2}\). Thus both regimes require only polynomially many arithmetic operations on numbers of polynomial bit length.

Formula (17) involves bounded factors. Exponentials with sufficiently large negative arguments may be replaced by zero at the requested accuracy. Standard range reduction and Taylor series compute the remaining exponentials and logarithms in polynomial time.

There is also uniform control of errors in the logarithmic argument. From (4), for \(|\operatorname{Im}w|\le1/2\),

\(|g_c(e^w)|\le2.\)

Cauchy's derivative estimate on the radius-\(1/2\) disk therefore gives

\(\displaystyle \left|\frac{d}{dw}g_c(e^w)\right|\le4\qquad(w\in\mathbb R).\)

Thus approximating \(w=s-aj\) to sufficiently many polynomially bounded guard bits controls the error in \(g_c(e^w)\). Here \(|w|\) itself is polynomially bounded because \(a<L\) and \(J\) is polynomially bounded.

Evaluating the retained terms with total error at most \(2^{-b-1}\), and adding the two tail bounds, proves the claimed evaluation procedure.

6. Constructing and maximizing a Fourier approximation

Binary encoding also gives \(a<L\). Choose

\(N=(L+1)(B+L+20).\)

By (13) and \(1/(1-e^{-2/a})\le1+a/2\),

\[\displaystyle \sum_{|\ell|>N}|b_\ell|\le4(1+L/2)e^{-2(N+1)/a}<\delta.\tag{18}\]

Let \(M=4N+1\), and sample \(G_c\) at the \(M\) points \(s_h=ah/M\). For \(|\ell|\le N\), the exact discrete Fourier coefficients are

\(\displaystyle d_\ell=\frac1M\sum_{h=0}^{M-1}G_c(ah/M)e^{-2\pi i\ell h/M}.\)

Absolute convergence of the Fourier series gives the aliasing identity

\(d_\ell=\sum_{q\in\mathbb Z}b_{\ell+qM}.\)

Every alias with \(q\ne0\) has index of magnitude greater than \(N\), and distinct retained indices have disjoint alias classes. Therefore

\[\displaystyle \sum_{|\ell|\le N}|d_\ell-b_\ell|<\delta.\tag{19}\]

Section 5 computes the samples to the required accuracy in polynomial time. Sines and cosines in the discrete Fourier transform also admit polynomial-time evaluation by range reduction and Taylor series. Compute rational coefficients \(\widetilde d_\ell\), preserving conjugate symmetry, such that

\(\displaystyle \sum_{|\ell|\le N}|\widetilde d_\ell-d_\ell|\le\delta.\)

The resulting real trigonometric polynomial

\(\displaystyle T(\theta)=\sum_{|\ell|\le N}\widetilde d_\ell e^{i\ell\theta}\)

then satisfies, by (18) and (19),

\[\boxed{\displaystyle \sup_{0\le\theta\le2\pi}\left|T(\theta)-G_c\!\left(\frac{a\theta}{2\pi}\right)\right|\le3\delta.}\tag{20}\]

Its degree and coefficient bit lengths are polynomially bounded.

It remains to certify its global maximum. Write

\(\displaystyle T(\theta)=A_0+\sum_{\ell=1}^N\bigl(A_\ell\cos(\ell\theta)+B_\ell\sin(\ell\theta)\bigr),\)

with rational coefficients. Under \(x=\tan(\theta/2)\),

\(\cos\theta=\frac{1-x^2}{1+x^2},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{2x}{1+x^2}.\)

The multiple-angle identities consequently give

\(\displaystyle T(\theta)=\frac{Q(x)}{(1+x^2)^N},\)

where \(Q\) is a rational polynomial of degree at most \(2N\), with polynomial coefficient bit length.

Every stationary point with finite \(x\) is a real root of

\[R(x)=(1+x^2)Q'(x)-2NxQ(x).\tag{21}\]

The remaining circle point is \(\theta=\pi\), whose value is the rational number

\(\displaystyle T(\pi)=A_0+\sum_{\ell=1}^NA_\ell(-1)^\ell.\)

If \(R\) vanishes identically, \(T\) is constant. Otherwise, clear denominators, take the square-free part, and isolate all its real roots. Deterministic univariate integer-polynomial root isolation has bit complexity polynomial in degree and coefficient bit length; see, for example, the Sturm bound in [*Amortized Bound for Root Isolation via Sturm*](https://cs.nyu.edu/exact/doc/sturmcomplexity.pdf), Corollary 8. Its hypotheses apply here after clearing denominators and removing repeated factors.

Refining each isolating interval to polynomially many additional bits also takes polynomial time. To verify that this suffices for evaluating \(T\), observe that its coefficients are bounded by a constant, since they approximate discrete averages of a function bounded by one. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \left|\frac{d}{dx}T(2\arctan x)\right|\le2\sum_{\ell=1}^N\ell(|A_\ell|+|B_\ell|)=O(N^2).\)

Thus intervals of width a sufficiently small multiple of \(\delta/N^2\) determine all stationary values to error at most \(\delta\). Evaluate these candidates and \(T(\pi)\), and take their maximum. Every global maximum occurs at one of these candidates.

This computes \(\max_\theta T(\theta)\) to error at most \(\delta\) in polynomial time.

7. Error and running-time conclusion

In the small-\(c\) branch, the returned mean approximation has error less than \(2\delta\).

In the other branch, (9) and (20), followed by the certified maximization, give total error at most

\(3\delta+\delta=4\delta<\varepsilon.\)

Every operation has bit complexity polynomial in \(L+B\), where \(B=O(1+\log(1/\varepsilon))\). The algorithm does not compute the CDF, integrate the density pieces, or enumerate valuation types. On the promised input class, it only needs \(c\) and \(\varepsilon\).

Therefore the stated approximation problem is deterministically solvable in time polynomial in \(k,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\).
