The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many schools with rational per-capita capacities and an atomless population described by rational piecewise-constant densities over student preferences, between-group priority classes, and within-group rank vectors, does there exist, and can one compute in polynomial time, a finite-description measurable assignment that is individually rational and has no positive-mass blocking coalition? For within-group blocks, require each school’s mass by between-group class to remain unchanged and define the interrupter-slack condition by integrating, over coalition mass assigned to that school, the mass of higher-ranked same-class interrupters outside the coalition, bounded by the school’s per-capita capacity slack.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools, with positive binary-rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\); every student is acceptable to every school.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each with binary-rational mass \(w_t\), where \(\sum_t w_t=1\). Here a type specifies a strict preference over \(S\cup\{0\}\), school-specific ordered priority classes, and a positive-volume rational box \(B_t\subseteq[0,1]^m\) on which its within-group rank vector is uniform; \(0\) means unmatched. Thus \(\tau\) is the number of listed density types, not the number of possible preference profiles.
Write \((X,\lambda)\) for this atomless probability space, \(g_s(x)\) for the priority class at \(s\), and \(r_s(x)\) for its rank coordinate; smaller values give higher priority. School priorities compare classes first and ranks second; rank ties have zero mass and are immaterial.
An assignment is a measurable function \(\mu:X\to S\cup\{0\}\) satisfying \(\lambda(\mu^{-1}(s))\leq q_s\); it is individually rational when \(\mu(x)\succeq_x0\) almost everywhere. A finite description specifies, in each input box, each assignment region by a finite Boolean combination of polynomial inequalities with rational coefficients.
A blocking coalition is a measurable \(A\subseteq X\) with \(\lambda(A)>0\), together with a feasible measurable assignment \(\nu\), such that \(\nu(x)\succeq_x\mu(x)\) almost everywhere on \(A\), with strict improvement on positive mass. Outsiders may be reassigned without their consent; blocking deviations need not have finite descriptions.
A between-group block additionally requires, for almost every \(x\in A\) sent to a school \(s\), either \(\lambda(\mu^{-1}(s))<q_s\) or \(\lambda(\{y:\mu(y)=s,\ g_s(y)>g_s(x)\})>0\); choosing \(0\) is unrestricted.
A within-group block instead preserves every school's mass in each priority class: \(\lambda(\nu^{-1}(s)\cap\{g_s=c\})=\lambda(\mu^{-1}(s)\cap\{g_s=c\})\). For each \(s\) receiving positive coalition mass, it also requires \(\int_{A\cap\nu^{-1}(s)}\lambda(\{y\notin A:g_s(y)=g_s(x),\ r_s(y)<r_s(x)\})\,d\lambda(x)\leq q_s-\lambda(\nu^{-1}(s))\).
Does every instance admit a finitely described individually rational assignment with neither kind of block?
Is deciding existence in \(\mathrm{P}\) or NP-hard, and can such an assignment be computed whenever it exists in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\)?
For finite student populations, [DA–TTC](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09357v1) always produces an assignment in the unified core in polynomial time.
[Continuum TTC](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdaa071) has a cutoff characterization computable from preference and priority distributions; that result concerns ordinary TTC, rather than the blocking conditions specified here. No result settling this exact continuum problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Represent rational piecewise-constant densities by positive-mass types carrying uniform distributions on positive-volume rational rank boxes; support size counts these explicitly listed density types.
  • Normalize total population mass to one and retain strictly positive capacities as per-capita quantities.
  • Include strict preferences over schools and the unmatched option, universal school acceptance, and school-specific ordered priority classes refined by within-group ranks.
  • Use smaller rank coordinates for higher priority and disregard null rank ties and other null-set changes.
  • Require weak improvement almost everywhere throughout the coalition and strict improvement on positive mass.
  • Interpret a between-group claim against lower-priority incumbents as requiring positive incumbent mass; allow the unmatched option without school enforcement conditions.
  • Allow feasible deviations to reassign outsiders without consent, as the discrete enforcement definitions impose no outsider-consent requirement.
  • Use total proposed school enrollment, rather than coalition enrollment, in the capacity-slack term.
  • Define finite assignment descriptions using rational semialgebraic regions; quantify blocking over arbitrary measurable coalitions and deviations.

Partial result: exact polynomial-time computation is impossible

I prove a negative answer to the polynomial-time construction question under the usual output convention: the rational coefficients of the output polynomials are written explicitly in binary. The obstruction occurs already with two types, identical preferences, and one priority class at every school.

For this subclass, I prove existence and uniqueness up to null sets. I then construct instances whose unique admissible assignment requires exponentially many bits in every permitted description. Unrestricted finite-description existence and the complexity of deciding unrestricted existence remain open.

1. A subclass with a unique assignment

Assume that every student has the same preference
$$ s_1\succ s_2\succ\cdots\succ s_m\succ0, $$
that every school has just one priority class, and that
$$ Q:=\sum_{j=1}^m q_{s_j}<1. $$
Write $q_j=q_{s_j}$ and $r_j=r_{s_j}$.

Define a sequential assignment as follows. Set $R_0=X$. For each $j$, choose a cutoff $z_j$ satisfying
$$ \lambda\bigl(R_{j-1}\cap\{r_j\leq z_j\}\bigr)=q_j, $$
and put
$$ T_j=R_{j-1}\cap\{r_j\leq z_j\}, \qquad R_j=R_{j-1}\setminus T_j. $$
Assign $T_j$ to $s_j$, and assign $R_m$ to $0$.

These cutoffs exist. Indeed,
$$ \lambda(R_{j-1}) = 1-\sum_{h<j}q_h > q_j. $$
The function
$$ z\longmapsto\lambda(R_{j-1}\cap\{r_j\leq z\}) $$
is continuous because $\lambda(\{r_j=z\})=0$ for every $z$. Its values at $0$ and $1$ are respectively $0$ and $\lambda(R_{j-1})$, so the intermediate value theorem applies. Different cutoff choices attaining the same target produce the same set $T_j$ up to null sets.

Proposition. This sequential assignment is the unique individually rational assignment, up to null sets, having neither kind of block.

Existence: absence of between-group blocks

Every school is full, and there is only one class at each school. Consequently, neither alternative in the between-group eligibility condition holds for a coalition member sent to a school.

Sending coalition members to $0$ cannot provide a strict improvement over an individually rational assignment. Thus there is no between-group block.

Existence: absence of within-group blocks

Suppose that $(A,\nu)$ is a within-group block of the sequential assignment $\mu$.

Preservation of each school's single class mass gives
$$ \lambda(\nu^{-1}(s_j))=q_j $$
for every $j$. Hence every interrupter integral required by the block must be zero.

Because preferences are strict and common, positive-mass strict improvement must go to a school. Let $j$ be the smallest index for which
$$ E:=\{x\in A:\nu(x)=s_j,\ s_j\succ_x\mu(x)\} $$
has positive mass.

Every member of $E$ was originally assigned to a later school or to $0$. Therefore
$$ E\subseteq R_{j-1}\setminus T_j, \qquad r_j(x)>z_j\quad\text{for almost every }x\in E. $$
Conversely, $r_j(y)\leq z_j$ for every $y\in T_j$.

Almost every member of $T_j\cap A$ must retain $s_j$ under $\nu$. Weak improvement permits such a member to receive only $s_j$ or an earlier school. A positive mass moving to an earlier school would contradict the minimality of $j$.

For every $x\in E$, all members of $T_j\setminus A$ are higher-ranked same-class outsiders at $s_j$. Thus the interrupter integral at $s_j$ is at least
$$ \lambda(E)\lambda(T_j\setminus A). $$
Its required value is zero. Since $\lambda(E)>0$, it follows that
$$ \lambda(T_j\setminus A)=0. $$
Almost all of $T_j$ therefore retain $s_j$, while the disjoint set $E$ also receives $s_j$. This gives
$$ \lambda(\nu^{-1}(s_j)) \geq q_j+\lambda(E) > q_j, $$
a contradiction.

Uniqueness

Let $\widehat\mu$ be any individually rational assignment having neither kind of block.

First, every school must be full. Feasibility gives
$$ \lambda(\widehat\mu^{-1}(0))\geq1-Q>0. $$
If a school had a vacancy, a sufficiently small positive-mass subset of unmatched students could move there. This would be a between-group block. The required subset exists by atomlessness.

We now prove inductively that
$$ \widehat\mu^{-1}(s_j)=T_j $$
up to null sets.

Suppose this has been proved for all indices below $j$. Let
$$ P_{j-1}=\bigcup_{h<j}T_h, \qquad M_j=\widehat\mu^{-1}(s_j). $$
Both $M_j$ and $T_j$ have mass $q_j$. If they differ on positive mass, then
$$ \lambda(T_j\setminus M_j)>0. $$

Take the coalition
$$ A=P_{j-1}\cup T_j. $$
Define a deviation that retains each $T_h$, for $h<j$, at $s_h$, and sends all of $T_j$ to $s_j$. Assign outsiders to the later schools in masses $q_{j+1},\ldots,q_m$, and leave the remaining outsiders unmatched.

This outsider assignment exists: the outsider mass is
$$ 1-\sum_{h\leq j}q_h, $$
which exceeds $\sum_{h>j}q_h$ because $Q<1$. Atomlessness permits the required measurable partition. The deviation preserves every school's mass and therefore every school–class mass.

Coalition members in $P_{j-1}$ retain their assignments. Members of $T_j$ cannot originally occupy an earlier school, by the induction hypothesis, so they weakly improve. Members of $T_j\setminus M_j$ strictly improve on positive mass.

It remains to check interrupters. For every $h\leq j$,
$$ X\setminus A\subseteq R_{h-1}\setminus T_h. $$
Hence outsiders have $r_h>z_h$, whereas the coalition members sent to $s_h$ belong to $T_h$ and have $r_h\leq z_h$. There are no higher-ranked outsiders for those receivers. All required interrupter integrals are zero.

The deviation is therefore a within-group block, a contradiction. This completes the induction and proves uniqueness.

2. Rational boxes give a finite description

The preceding unique assignment has rational cutoffs for the box distributions in the question.

Write
$$ B_t=\prod_{h=1}^m[u_{t,h},v_{t,h}], \qquad u_{t,h}<v_{t,h}. $$
After the first $j-1$ admissions, the remaining mass of type $t$ is
$$ p_{t,j-1} = w_t \prod_{h<j} \min\left\{ 1,\max\left\{ 0,\frac{v_{t,h}-z_h}{v_{t,h}-u_{t,h}} \right\} \right\}. $$
This formula follows because the surviving region in each type box is obtained by imposing $r_h>z_h$ for $h<j$, and uniform measure on a product box factors across coordinates.

Consequently, the remaining cumulative mass at school $s_j$ is
$$ H_j(z) = \sum_t p_{t,j-1} \min\left\{ 1,\max\left\{ 0,\frac{z-u_{t,j}}{v_{t,j}-u_{t,j}} \right\} \right\}. $$
If the earlier cutoffs are rational, this is a continuous piecewise affine function with rational breakpoints and coefficients. The rational target $q_j$ is attained either at a rational breakpoint or by solving an affine equation with positive rational slope. Thus $z_j$ can be chosen rational.

Induction proves rationality of all cutoffs. Within each input box, the assignment region for $s_j$ is described by
$$ r_h>z_h\quad(h<j), \qquad r_j\leq z_j. $$
The unmatched region is described by $r_h>z_h$ for every $h$. These are permitted finite Boolean combinations of rational linear inequalities.

This establishes finite-description existence for the subclass. It does not yet give a polynomial bound on coefficient lengths.

3. A two-type family with rapidly growing denominators

For each $m\geq3$, let
$$ n=2^{\lceil\log_2m\rceil}. $$
Thus $m\leq n<2m$.

Give every school capacity
$$ q=\frac1{4n}. $$
There are two types, each of mass $1/2$, both with preference
$$ s_1\succ\cdots\succ s_m\succ0. $$
Every school has one priority class. Their boxes are
$$ B_1=[0,1]^m, \qquad B_2=[0,1/2]^m. $$
All numerical input data are dyadic rationals. The total input length is $O(m\log m)$, and $\tau=2$.

Since
$$ mq\leq\frac14<1, $$
the preceding proposition applies: the sequential assignment is the unique assignment having neither kind of block.

Let $p_k$ and $t_k$ be the remaining masses of types $1$ and $2$ after the first $k$ schools. Since each school admits mass $q$,
$$ p_k+t_k=1-kq. $$
For the next school, the unused rank coordinate remains uniform on $[0,1]$ in type $1$ and on $[0,1/2]$ in type $2$. For $0\leq z\leq1/2$, its cumulative remaining mass is therefore
$$ p_kz+2t_kz. $$
The cutoff is
$$ z_{k+1}=\frac{q}{p_k+2t_k}. $$
This lies below $1/2$: the denominator is at least $p_k+t_k\geq3/4$, while $q\leq1/4$, so $z_{k+1}\leq1/3$.

Normalize masses by $q$. Put
$$ N_k=\frac{p_k+t_k}{q}=4n-k, \qquad h_k=\frac{p_k}{q}. $$
Then
$$ z_{k+1}=\frac1{2N_k-h_k}, \qquad h_{k+1}=h_k(1-z_{k+1}), \qquad h_0=2n. $$

Exact denominator recurrence

Write
$$ h_k=\frac{u_k}{v_k} $$
in lowest terms, with positive integers $u_k,v_k$. Define
$$ d_k=2N_kv_k-u_k. $$
Then
$$ z_{k+1}=\frac{v_k}{d_k}, \qquad h_{k+1} = \frac{u_k(d_k-v_k)}{v_kd_k}. $$
Because $\gcd(u_k,v_k)=1$,
$$ \gcd(v_k,d_k)=1, \qquad \gcd(v_k,d_k-v_k)=1, \qquad \gcd(d_k,d_k-v_k)=1. $$
Thus the cancellation factor in the expression for $h_{k+1}$ is exactly
$$ \gcd(u_k,d_k)=\gcd(u_k,2N_k). $$
It follows that
$$ v_{k+1} = \frac{v_kd_k}{\gcd(u_k,2N_k)}. $$

Since $0<h_k<N_k$,
$$ d_k=(2N_k-h_k)v_k\geq N_kv_k. $$
Also $\gcd(u_k,2N_k)\leq2N_k$. Hence
$$ v_{k+1}\geq\frac{v_k^2}{2}. $$

The first two values are
$$ h_1=\frac{6n-1}{3}, $$
and
$$ h_2= \frac{(6n-1)(18n-8)}{3(18n-5)}. $$
The latter fraction is reduced. Its numerator is coprime to $3$, and
$$ \gcd(6n-1,18n-5)=1, \qquad \gcd(18n-8,18n-5)=1. $$
Therefore
$$ v_2=3(18n-5). $$

Iterating the denominator inequality gives, for $k\geq2$,
$$ \frac{v_k}{2} \geq \left(\frac{v_2}{2}\right)^{2^{k-2}}. $$
In particular,
$$ \log_2v_{m-1} \geq 1+2^{m-3}\log_2\left(\frac{3(18n-5)}2\right). $$

Finally, $z_m=v_{m-1}/d_{m-1}$ is already reduced, and
$$ d_{m-1}\geq N_{m-1}v_{m-1}. $$
Thus the reduced denominator of the last cutoff has binary length at least $2^{m-3}$.

4. Every permitted explicit description inherits this lower bound

A large cutoff denominator alone would not suffice: one must exclude a shorter Boolean description using more complicated polynomials. The following argument does so.

Consider the region assigned to $s_m$ inside type $1$'s box. By uniqueness, it agrees almost everywhere with
$$ C= \left\{ r_h>z_h\ \text{for all }h<m,\quad r_m\leq z_m \right\}. $$
Type $1$ has constant positive density on $[0,1]^m$, so this agreement also holds almost everywhere for Lebesgue measure.

Suppose a finite Boolean combination of rational polynomial inequalities describes that region. Remove any identically zero polynomials, replacing their comparisons by their constant truth values. Denote the remaining defining polynomials by $P_1,\ldots,P_\ell$.

Consider the relatively open hyperplane patch
$$ U= \left(\prod_{h<m}(z_h,1)\right)\times\{z_m\}. $$
At every point of $U$, at least one $P_i$ must vanish. Otherwise, all defining polynomial signs would be constant in a neighborhood of that point, so the Boolean formula would be constant there. But $C$ occupies the portion below $r_m=z_m$ and excludes the portion above it. Both portions have positive volume, contradicting almost-everywhere agreement.

Therefore the restrictions of the $P_i$ to $r_m=z_m$ have zero sets covering $U$. At least one restriction must be the zero polynomial. Indeed, if all restrictions were nonzero, their product would be a nonzero polynomial vanishing on a nonempty open subset of $\mathbb R^{m-1}$, which is impossible.

Choose a nonzero defining polynomial $P$ whose restriction is identically zero. Expand it in the first $m-1$ variables:
$$ P(r_1,\ldots,r_m) = \sum_\beta r_1^{\beta_1}\cdots r_{m-1}^{\beta_{m-1}} f_\beta(r_m). $$
Every $f_\beta$ has rational coefficients and satisfies $f_\beta(z_m)=0$. At least one $f_\beta$ is nonzero.

Write the last cutoff in lowest terms as
$$ z_m=\frac ab, \qquad \gcd(a,b)=1. $$
Let $f$ be a nonzero such coefficient polynomial. Clearing its coefficient denominators gives a nonzero integer polynomial $F$ with $F(a/b)=0$. The rational root theorem implies that $b$ divides the leading coefficient of $F$.

To quantify this, write the coefficients of $f$ as explicit fractions $A_i/B_i$, with $B_i>0$. Clearing denominators by $\prod_iB_i$ gives leading coefficient
$$ A_e\prod_{i\ne e}B_i. $$
Consequently,
$$ \log_2 b \leq \log_2|A_e|+\sum_{i\ne e}\log_2B_i. $$
The right-hand side is at most the total binary length of the explicitly written rational coefficients of $f$, and hence at most that of the assignment description.

Thus every permitted description with explicit binary rational coefficients has length at least $2^{m-3}$ bits. The argument permits arbitrary polynomial degrees and arbitrary finite Boolean combinations; sparse encoding and binary exponents do not avoid the bound.

The family has $\tau=2$ and input length $O(m\log m)$. Every instance in the family admits a finitely described assignment having neither kind of block, but every such description requires exponentially many bits.

Proven negative computational result. No algorithm can always output an assignment in the specified format in time polynomial in $m$, $\tau$, and $L$, when rational output coefficients are explicitly encoded in binary.
