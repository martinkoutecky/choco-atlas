Result

The second alternative holds: under Gap-ETH, every polynomial-time \(\tau^{-o(1)}\) multiplicative approximation is ruled out, including every constant factor.

Conversely, there is a simple deterministic \(\tau^{-2}\)-approximation running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The hardness already holds with \(m=2\), equal dyadic masses, and a uniformly contracting activation recurrence.

These statements do not assert that the exponent \(2\) is optimal.

One technical point requires an interpretation: the selected limit can make the objective discontinuous, and a maximum need not exist. I use
\(\operatorname{OPT}=\sup\{\mathrm{MoV}(x):x\text{ is feasible}\}\).
The approximation guarantee holds against this supremum. In the hardness construction, the objective is continuous and its maximum is attained.

1. Properties of the selected limit

All vector inequalities below are componentwise.

The truncation is redundant

For every round, \(0\leq a_t^r\leq\mu_t\). Indeed, this holds initially, and the update is bounded between \(x_t\) and \(\mu_t\). Consequently,

\[ \text{} \]

\( \displaystyle 0\leq z_t^r=\sum_sK_{st}a_s^r \leq\sum_sK_{st}\mu_s\leq1. \)

Thus the recurrence is exactly

\( \displaystyle a_t^{r+1}=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\sum_sK_{st}a_s^r. \)

For fixed \(x\), this is an affine monotone map. Since \(a^1\geq a^0=x\), induction shows that the iterates increase. Their boundedness proves convergence, and continuity gives

\( \displaystyle a_t(x)=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\sum_sK_{st}a_s(x). \)

The iteration selects the least nonnegative fixed point: every nonnegative fixed point dominates \(x\), hence dominates every iterate.

Monotonicity and a scaling inequality

For \(0\leq u\leq\mu\), write

\( \displaystyle \Phi_x(u)_t=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\sum_sK_{st}u_s. \)

If \(x\leq y\), then

\( \displaystyle \Phi_y(u)_t-\Phi_x(u)_t =(y_t-x_t)\left(1-\sum_sK_{st}u_s\right)\geq0. \)

Together with monotonicity in \(u\), induction and passage to the limit prove

\( \displaystyle x\leq y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(x)\leq a(y). \)

For every \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), we also have

\( \displaystyle a(\lambda x)\geq\lambda a(x). \)

To prove this, the corresponding inequality holds at round zero. If it holds at round \(r\), then

\( \displaystyle a_t^{r+1}(\lambda x) \geq \lambda x_t+\lambda(\mu_t-\lambda x_t)z_t^r(x) =\lambda a_t^{r+1}(x) +\lambda(1-\lambda)x_tz_t^r(x) \geq\lambda a_t^{r+1}(x). \)

Passing to the limit establishes the claim.

Exact evaluation is polynomial-time

For a rational seed vector, define \(B_{ts}=(\mu_t-x_t)K_{st}\). The selected limit is the unique optimum of the rational linear program

\( \displaystyle \min\sum_t u_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\leq u\leq\mu,\qquad u\geq x+Bu. \)

The program is feasible because \(u=\mu\) satisfies its inequalities. Every feasible \(u\) dominates every activation iterate, and hence dominates \(a(x)\). The vector \(a(x)\) itself is feasible. Therefore minimizing the sum uniquely selects \(a(x)\).

Rational linear programming gives exact evaluation in polynomial time, with polynomial output bit length.

2. A general \(\tau^{-2}\)-approximation

Define

\( \displaystyle h(z)=\frac{z}{1+z}, \qquad r_t(x)=a_t(x)h(z_t(x)). \)

The target’s score gain and an opponent’s score loss are, respectively,

\( \displaystyle G(x)=\sum_t(1-\pi_t(c^\star))r_t(x), \qquad D_c(x)=\sum_t\pi_t(c)r_t(x). \)

Put \(M_0=\max_{c\neq c^\star}F_c^0\) and \(d_c=M_0-F_c^0\geq0\). Substitution into the definition of the objective yields

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{MoV}(x) =\min_{c\neq c^\star} \left[ d_c+ \sum_t\bigl(1-\pi_t(c^\star)+\pi_t(c)\bigr)r_t(x) \right]. \)

Every coefficient in this expression is nonnegative.

The algorithm outputs

\( \displaystyle \widehat x_t=\min\left\{\mu_t,\frac b\tau\right\}. \)

It is feasible because \(\sum_t\widehat x_t\leq b\). For every feasible \(x\), both \(x_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(x_t\leq b\), so \(\widehat x\geq x/\tau\). The preceding properties imply

\( \displaystyle a(\widehat x)\geq\frac1\tau a(x), \qquad z(\widehat x)\geq\frac1\tau z(x). \)

The function \(h\) is increasing, and

\( \displaystyle h(\lambda z)=\frac{\lambda z}{1+\lambda z} \geq\lambda\frac{z}{1+z} =\lambda h(z) \quad(0\leq\lambda\leq1). \)

Therefore \(r_t(\widehat x)\geq\tau^{-2}r_t(x)\). Using the displayed formula for \(\mathrm{MoV}\), including \(d_c\geq\tau^{-2}d_c\), gives

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{MoV}(\widehat x)\geq\tau^{-2}\mathrm{MoV}(x). \)

This holds for every feasible \(x\), and consequently

\( \displaystyle \boxed{\mathrm{MoV}(\widehat x)\geq\tau^{-2}\operatorname{OPT}.} \)

Computing and representing \(\widehat x\) requires only polynomial-time rational arithmetic.

3. A rounding lemma for fractional dense subgraphs

This lemma handles the divisible-seed issue in the hardness reduction.

Lemma. Let \(G\) be a simple undirected graph with adjacency matrix \(A\). Suppose \(2\leq k\leq |V(G)|\), \(v\in[0,1]^{V(G)}\), and \(\sum_i v_i\leq k\). Define

\( \displaystyle H(v)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E(G)}v_iv_j=\frac12v^{\mathsf T}Av. \)

In deterministic polynomial time, one can find a set \(S\) of \(k\) vertices satisfying

\( \displaystyle |E(G[S])|\geq\frac16H(v). \)

Proof. A nonnegative linear objective over
\(\{w\in[0,1]^V:\sum_iw_i\leq k\}\)
is maximized by assigning one to \(k\) coordinates with largest coefficients. This follows by moving mass from smaller coefficients to larger ones until those coordinates are filled.

Choose such an indicator vector \(u\) maximizing \(u^{\mathsf T}Av\). Since \(v\) is feasible,

\( \displaystyle u^{\mathsf T}Av\geq v^{\mathsf T}Av. \)

Next choose an indicator vector \(w\), also with exactly \(k\) ones, maximizing \(u^{\mathsf T}Aw\). Again,

\( \displaystyle u^{\mathsf T}Aw\geq u^{\mathsf T}Av\geq v^{\mathsf T}Av. \)

Let \(U\) be the union of their supports, and write \(h=|U|\). Then \(k\leq h\leq2k\). Nonnegativity of \(A\) gives

\( \displaystyle 2|E(G[U])| =\mathbf1_U^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf1_U \geq u^{\mathsf T}Aw \geq2H(v). \)

Repeatedly remove a vertex of minimum degree from the current induced graph until \(k\) vertices remain. In a graph with \(j\) vertices and \(e\) edges, minimum degree is at most \(2e/j\); thus one removal preserves at least \((j-2)e/j\) edges. Multiplication over the removals gives

\( \displaystyle |E(G[S])| \geq\frac{k(k-1)}{h(h-1)}|E(G[U])| \geq\frac{k(k-1)}{2k(2k-1)}H(v) \geq\frac16H(v), \)

where the last inequality uses \(k\geq2\). All operations are polynomial-time. This proves the lemma.

4. An approximation-preserving reduction

Take a Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph instance \(G\) on \(n\) vertices, with \(2\leq k\leq n\). Pad it with isolated vertices to \(N\) vertices, where \(N\) is the smallest power of two at least \(n\). Thus \(n\leq N<2n\).

Padding preserves the optimum number \(D\) of edges induced by \(k\) vertices: any padded solution can have its isolated vertices replaced by unused original vertices without losing edges.

Construct the society as follows:

  • There are \(\tau=N\) types, one per padded vertex, with \(\mu_t=1/N\).
  • There are two candidates, \(c^\star\) and \(c_1\).
  • Every type has \(\pi_t(c^\star)=0\) and \(\pi_t(c_1)=1\).
  • Set \(K_{st}=1/2\) when \(\{s,t\}\) is an edge, and zero otherwise.
  • Set \(b=k/N\).

All masses and the budget are dyadic. For every \(t\),

\( \displaystyle \sum_s\mu_sK_{st}=\frac{\deg(t)}{2N}<\frac12, \)

so the kernel constraint holds. The construction has polynomial binary length.

Bounding activation from arbitrary fractional seeds

Given any feasible seed vector, put

\( \displaystyle p_t=Nx_t,\qquad q_t=Na_t,\qquad W=\frac{A}{2N}. \)

Then \(p,q\in[0,1]^N\), \(\sum_t p_t\leq k\), and the fixed-point equation is

\( \displaystyle q=p+\operatorname{diag}(1-p)Wq. \)

Its affine map contracts in the maximum norm with constant at most \(1/2\). Hence its fixed point is unique and agrees with the selected limit. It can be computed exactly by rational linear algebra in polynomial time.

Summing the fixed-point equations and using nonnegativity gives

\( \displaystyle \sum_tq_t \leq k+\sum_t(Wq)_t =k+\frac1{2N}\sum_s\deg(s)q_s \leq k+\frac12\sum_sq_s. \)

Therefore \(\sum_tq_t\leq2k\). The vector \(v=q/2\) satisfies the rounding lemma’s hypotheses.

Comparing objective values

Here \(z=Wq\), so \(z_t\leq1/2\). Because every initial vote goes to the single opponent,

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{MoV}(x) =\frac2N\sum_t\frac{q_tz_t}{1+z_t}. \)

Dropping the denominators yields

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{MoV}(x) \leq\frac2Nq^{\mathsf T}Wq =\frac2{N^2}H(q). \)

Conversely, take a \(k\)-vertex set \(S\) inducing \(D\) edges and seed its types fully. Then \(p=\mathbf1_S\), and \(q\geq p\). Since \(z_t\leq1/2\),

\( \displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} \geq\frac4{3N}p^{\mathsf T}Wp =\frac4{3N^2}D. \)

Suppose an approximation algorithm returns \(x\) with
\(\mathrm{MoV}(x)\geq\alpha\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\).
Combining the two bounds gives

\( \displaystyle H(q)\geq\frac{2\alpha}{3}D. \)

Apply the rounding lemma to \(v=q/2\). Since \(H(v)=H(q)/4\), the resulting set satisfies

\( \displaystyle |E(G[S])| \geq\frac16H(v) =\frac1{24}H(q) \geq\frac{\alpha}{36}D. \)

Any padded isolated vertices can then be replaced by original vertices.

Thus we have proved the transfer

\( \displaystyle \boxed{ \text{an }\alpha\text{-approximation for the mass problem yields an } \alpha/36\text{-approximation for Densest-}k\text{-Subgraph}. } \)

This transfer applies to arbitrary fractional seeds returned by the algorithm.

5. Consequence under Gap-ETH

Manurangsi’s perfect-completeness hardness theorem implies that, under Gap-ETH, Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph on \(n\) vertices admits no polynomial-time \(n^{-o(1)}\) multiplicative approximation. The underlying gap distinguishes a \(k\)-clique from instances in which every \(k\)-vertex induced subgraph has density at most \(n^{-f(n)}\), for positive \(f(n)=o(1)\). [Manurangsi, Theorem 2](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2016/195/revision/1/download/).

If the mass problem admitted a guarantee \(\alpha(\tau)=\tau^{-o(1)}\), the reduction would give a Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph guarantee \(\alpha(N)/36\). Because \(n\leq N<2n\), this is still \(n^{-o(1)}\), contradicting that theorem.

Hence no such approximation exists under Gap-ETH. In particular, no constant factor exists under that assumption.

This conclusion concerns deterministic algorithms under deterministic Gap-ETH. The corresponding exclusion for randomized algorithms uses randomized Gap-ETH; the reduction and rounding themselves are deterministic.

6. Why a supremum may be necessary

Consider two types and two candidates, with

\( \displaystyle \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad b=\frac14,\qquad K_{11}=2,\quad K_{22}=1,\quad K_{12}=K_{21}=0, \)

and all initial probability assigned to the opponent. These parameters satisfy the kernel constraint.

The selected limits are

\( \displaystyle a_1= \begin{cases} 0,&x_1=0,\\ 1/2,&x_1>0, \end{cases} \qquad a_2=\frac{x_2}{1/2+x_2}. \)

For the first type, positive seeding gives a contracting recurrence whose fixed point is \(1/2\); zero seeding leaves every iterate zero. The second formula follows directly from its fixed-point equation.

Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{MoV}(x) =\frac12\mathbf1_{\{x_1>0\}} +\frac{2a_2^2}{1+a_2}. \)

If \(x_1=0\), its value is at most \(1/6\). If \(x_1>0\), feasibility forces \(x_2<1/4\), so its value is strictly below \(2/3\). Taking \(x_1=\varepsilon\) and \(x_2=1/4-\varepsilon\), with positive rational \(\varepsilon\) tending to zero, makes the value tend to \(2/3\).

Thus the supremum is \(2/3\), but no maximum exists. This issue does not affect either proved approximation statement: the positive guarantee holds against the supremum, and the hardness instances have uniformly contracting, continuous limits.
