Theorem 1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) candidates, a finite support of trader portfolio types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and per-capita holdings \(h_t(i,j)\) for each candidate-position security, set \(Q_{ij}=\sum_t\mu_t h_t(i,j)\). For a designated security \(\langle i\mid j\rangle\), compute its exact LMSR quote and normalizer \(Z=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum_i Q_{i,\sigma(i)}\right)\), hence the cost \(b\log Z\).

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) portfolio types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.

For exact finite output, represent each exponential sum by its collected list of distinct rational exponents and positive integer coefficients, all in binary; represent the quote by the numerator and denominator lists and the cost by the logarithm of the normalizer list.

Is computing the exact designated LMSR quote, normalizer, and cost in this representation possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

Result

Computing each of the exact designated quote, normalizer, and cost in the specified representation is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions, and therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This holds already when \(\tau=1\), \(b=1\), and every holding belongs to \(\{0,1\}\).

The hardness reduction uses only instances whose output lists have polynomial length. Separately, general instances can require factorially many output records, which unconditionally rules out polynomial time in the input parameters for producing these explicit lists.

1. Encoding a permanent in the normalizer

We use Valiant’s theorem: computing the permanent of a square zero–one matrix is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-complete under polynomial-time Turing reductions. Here the permanent is defined by
\(\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}\prod_{k=1}^{m}A_{k,\sigma(k)}\).
The theorem applies specifically to matrices with entries in \(\{0,1\}\). See [Valiant, *The complexity of computing the permanent*, Theorem 1](https://www.math.cmu.edu/users/af1p/Teaching/MCC17/Papers/permanent.pdf).

Given \(A\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}\), with \(m\geq2\), construct a society with
\(\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=1\), \(b=1\), and \(h_1(k,\ell)=A_{k\ell}\).
For the quote, designate \((i,j)=(1,1)\).

These satisfy all input requirements: the sole mass is positive and sums to one, and all holdings and the liquidity parameter are binary rationals. The construction has polynomial input length. Since \(Q=A\), each permutation has exponent
\(s_\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k,\sigma(k)}\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}\).

Define
\(c_r=\bigl|\{\sigma\in S_m:s_\sigma=r\}\bigr|\)
for \(r\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\). The required collected normalizer list represents
\(Z=\sum_{r=0}^{m}c_r\exp(r)\),
with records of coefficient zero omitted.

Because each summand in \(s_\sigma\) is either zero or one, \(s_\sigma=m\) holds exactly when every selected entry \(A_{k,\sigma(k)}\) equals one. Likewise, the product \(\prod_{k=1}^{m}A_{k,\sigma(k)}\) equals one exactly in this case and equals zero otherwise. Consequently,
\(c_m=\operatorname{perm}(A)\).

Thus one normalizer query computes the permanent: read the coefficient of exponent \(m\), returning zero if that exponent is absent.

The oracle response has polynomial length. There are at most \(m+1\) records. Every exponent requires \(O(\log m)\) bits, and every coefficient is at most \(m!\), requiring \(O(m\log m)\) bits. Hence the full list requires \(O(m^2\log m)\) bits, and extracting \(c_m\) takes polynomial time.

Matrices of order less than two can be handled directly, so the restriction \(m\geq2\) does not affect the reduction.

2. Hardness of the designated quote and cost

Under the stipulated quote representation, the output consists of the collected lists for the actual numerator \(N_{11}\) and denominator \(Z\). Its denominator therefore supplies exactly the normalizer list used above. Reading its coefficient at exponent \(m\) computes \(\operatorname{perm}(A)\).

The numerator also has polynomial length on these instances: its exponents lie in \(\{0,\ldots,m\}\), and its coefficients are at most \((m-1)!\). Thus the entire quote response has polynomial length.

For the cost, the representation explicitly contains the normalizer list inside the logarithm. Since our construction sets \(b=1\), the cost is represented as
\(C=\log\bigl(\sum_{r=0}^{m}c_r\exp(r)\bigr)\).
Again, reading the coefficient at exponent \(m\) computes the permanent.

Therefore the permanent polynomial-time Turing-reduces to each requested computation. By Valiant’s theorem, each computation is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-hard.

To make the implication to \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness explicit, counting satisfying assignments belongs to \(\#\mathrm{P}\), so it has a polynomial-time Turing reduction to the zero–one permanent. Replace each permanent query in that reduction by the construction above and coefficient extraction. A Boolean formula is satisfiable exactly when the resulting assignment count is positive. All replacement queries and responses have polynomial length, giving the required polynomial-time Turing reduction for satisfiability.

This argument uses integer holdings throughout and requires no numerical evaluation of exponentials or logarithms.

3. An independent output-length obstruction

For each \(m\geq2\), take \(\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=b=1\), and set
\(h_1(k,\ell)=2^{(k-1)m+(\ell-1)}\)
for \(1\leq k,\ell\leq m\).

Each holding has at most \(m^2\) binary digits, so the total input length is \(L=O(m^4)\). For a permutation,
\(s_\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{m}2^{(k-1)m+(\sigma(k)-1)}\).

The map \((k,\ell)\mapsto(k-1)m+(\ell-1)\) is injective. Consequently, the displayed sum has a binary digit equal to one exactly at the indices of the selected candidate–position pairs. Distinct permutations select distinct sets of pairs, and uniqueness of binary expansion implies that their exponents are distinct.

The collected normalizer therefore has exactly \(m!\) records, each with coefficient one. The quote denominator and the cost’s normalizer list contain those same records.

Any algorithm writing these explicit outputs must take at least \(\Omega(m!)\) time. Since \(m!\) grows faster than every polynomial in \(m\), while \(\tau=1\) and \(L=O(m^4)\), no algorithm can produce the required outputs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) on all inputs, irrespective of complexity-class separations.

The coefficient reduction establishes computational hardness even when this output-length obstruction is absent.
