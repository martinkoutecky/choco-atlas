The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two two-sided typed markets with the same finite type supports and binary-rational masses, where the second market differs only by merging two adjacent preference tiers in one type’s order, decide whether the maximum total matched mass among weakly stable fractional matchings is equal in the two markets.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be disjoint, nonempty finite type supports. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive masses, each encoded as a binary rational. Normalize each side to total mass \(1\). Put \(m=\max\{|A|,|B|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each type ranks its acceptable opposite-side types in ordered, nonempty indifference tiers; acceptability is mutual, empty preference lists are allowed, and every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched. No tie-breaking is imposed.
A fractional matching assigns real masses \(x_{ab}\geq0\) to acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_bx_{ab}\leq\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_ax_{ab}\leq\beta_b\), where \(\alpha_a,\beta_b\) are the type masses. Write \(c\succeq_a b\) when \(a\) weakly prefers \(c\) to \(b\), and similarly on the other side.
The matching is weakly stable if every acceptable pair \((a,b)\) satisfies \(\sum_{c\succeq_a b}x_{ac}=\alpha_a\) or \(\sum_{d\succeq_b a}x_{db}=\beta_b\). Thus a pair blocks precisely when positive mass on each side is unmatched or assigned to strictly worse partners.
Let \(\operatorname{OPT}(I)\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\) over weakly stable fractional matchings. The input consists of markets \(I,\widetilde I\) with identical supports and masses, where \(\widetilde I\) differs only by merging exactly two adjacent tiers in one type's preference list.

Is deciding whether \(\operatorname{OPT}(I)=\operatorname{OPT}(\widetilde I)\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For finite agents, this adjacent-tier comparison admits no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\); the established reduction is a polynomial-time Turing reduction ([Matsuyama–Miyazaki](https://doi.org/10.2197/ipsjjip.29.166)).
For fractional matchings with unit agent capacities, maximizing the number of fully matched agents under ordinal stability has an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete threshold problem when ties are allowed, even on bipartite graphs; this concerns a different objective from the mass comparison above ([Chen–Roy–Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question leaves fractional weak stability undefined; use absence of mutually strictly improving positive submasses, expressed by the stated disjunctive saturation condition.
  • Normalize each side separately to mass 1, preserving the discrete model's equal population sizes.
  • Carry over mutual acceptability, incomplete lists including empty lists, and strict preference for every acceptable partner over being unmatched.
  • Allow real matching masses; only input masses must be binary rationals.
  • Define m as the larger side's support size, tau as total support size, and L as total input bit length.
  • Specify polynomial-time Turing reductions for hardness, matching the anchor's oracle reduction.

Result

The comparison problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions, already when both sides have the same number of types and every type has the same mass. Consequently, a deterministic algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The essential point is that, with unit capacities, allowing fractional weakly stable matchings does not increase the maximum total matched mass. We prove this before transferring the finite-agent hardness result.

1. Decomposing a fractional matching

Let \(A\) and \(B\) each contain \(n\) vertices, with unit capacity at every vertex. Let \(E\subseteq A\times B\) be the acceptable pairs. A feasible fractional matching is a nonnegative matrix \(y\), zero outside \(E\), whose row and column sums are at most \(1\).

Lemma 1. Every such \(y\) is a convex combination of incidence matrices of integral matchings using only edges in \(E\).

Proof. Write \(r_a=\sum_b y_{ab}\) and \(c_b=\sum_a y_{ab}\). Form the following \(2n\times2n\) matrix:

\(D=\begin{pmatrix}y&\operatorname{diag}(1-r)\\ \operatorname{diag}(1-c)&y^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}.\)

Every entry is nonnegative. Each top row sums to \(r_a+(1-r_a)=1\), and each bottom row sums to \((1-c_b)+c_b=1\). Likewise, every column sums to \(1\). Thus \(D\) is doubly stochastic.

The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem states that every finite doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices. Its hypotheses have just been checked, so write

\(D=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_jP_j,\qquad \lambda_j>0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_j=1.\)

The upper-left block of each \(P_j\) has at most one \(1\) in every row and column, and therefore represents an integral matching \(M_j\). If \(y_{ab}=0\), positivity of the coefficients and nonnegativity of the matrix entries imply that every corresponding entry of \(P_j\) is zero. Hence every \(M_j\) uses only acceptable edges.

Taking upper-left blocks gives \(y=\sum_j\lambda_j\chi^{M_j}\), as required. \(\square\)

2. Stability survives every component of the decomposition

An integral matching is weakly stable if no acceptable pair consists of two vertices that are each unmatched or strictly prefer the other vertex to their assigned partner.

Lemma 2. If \(y\) is weakly stable under the task’s unit-capacity definition, then every integral matching in the decomposition of Lemma 1 is weakly stable.

Proof. Fix an acceptable pair \((a,b)\). Stability of \(y\) gives at least one of

\(\sum_{c\succeq_a b}y_{ac}=1,\qquad \sum_{d\succeq_b a}y_{db}=1.\)

Suppose the first equality holds. Substituting the decomposition yields

\(1=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_j\left(\sum_{c\succeq_a b}\chi^{M_j}_{ac}\right).\)

Each parenthesized quantity is either \(0\) or \(1\). Since every coefficient is positive and their sum is \(1\), every parenthesized quantity must equal \(1\). Thus, in every \(M_j\), vertex \(a\) is matched to a partner it weakly prefers to \(b\). Consequently, \((a,b)\) cannot block any \(M_j\).

If the second equality holds instead, the same argument applies to \(b\). Applying this reasoning to every acceptable pair proves that every \(M_j\) is weakly stable. \(\square\)

Corollary. Let \(K(J)\) denote the maximum cardinality of an integral weakly stable matching in a finite-agent instance \(J\). Then

\(\max\left\{\sum_{a,b}y_{ab}:y\text{ is a unit-capacity weakly stable fractional matching of }J\right\}=K(J).\)

Indeed, Lemma 2 gives

\(\sum_{a,b}y_{ab}=\sum_j\lambda_j|M_j|\leq K(J).\)

Conversely, the incidence matrix of any integral weakly stable matching satisfies the fractional stability condition, so an integral optimum attains \(K(J)\).

For completeness, an integral weakly stable matching exists: break each tie into an arbitrary strict order and apply deferred acceptance with incomplete lists. A blocking pair under the original preferences would remain blocking under that refinement, contradicting the resulting matching’s stability.

3. Encoding finite agents as normalized types

Take a finite-agent instance \(J\) with \(n\) agents on each side. Construct a society \(F(J)\) by making each labelled agent a separate type, copying all acceptability and preference tiers, and assigning every type mass \(1/n\).

Both sides then have total mass \(1\), as required.

For any feasible fractional matching \(x\) of \(F(J)\), define \(y_{ab}=nx_{ab}\). This is a bijection between the feasible fractional matchings of \(F(J)\) and the unit-capacity fractional matchings of \(J\). Moreover,

\(\sum_{c\succeq_a b}x_{ac}=1/n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{c\succeq_a b}y_{ac}=1,\)

with the analogous equivalence on the other side. Hence the bijection preserves weak stability. The corollary therefore gives the exact identity

\(\operatorname{OPT}(F(J))=K(J)/n.\)

Now let \(\widetilde J\) result from merging exactly two adjacent tiers in one agent’s list. The societies \(F(J)\) and \(F(\widetilde J)\) have identical supports and masses, and differ by exactly the same permitted merge. Furthermore,

\(\operatorname{OPT}(F(J))=\operatorname{OPT}(F(\widetilde J))\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K(J)=K(\widetilde J).\)

Thus one query to the fractional comparison problem answers the corresponding finite-agent comparison query.

4. Hardness and running time

The finite-agent problem just described is precisely Is Size Same in Matsuyama–Miyazaki: the two instances differ by merging two adjacent ties in one preference list, and the question is equality of their maximum weakly stable matching cardinalities. Their Theorem 1 establishes the polynomial-time Turing hardness stipulated in the question. Their instances have equal side sizes and mutual acceptability; empty lists are permitted. All these conditions fit the construction above. [Matsuyama–Miyazaki, Section 3.1](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ipsjjip/29/0/29_166/_pdf).

In each translated query, \(m=n\) and \(\tau=2n\). Encoding the masses adds \(O(\tau\log(m+1))\) bits, because \(1/n\) has a binary numerator–denominator representation of that length per type. If the finite query has bit length \(L_{\mathrm{fin}}\), the resulting query has length

\(L=O\!\left(L_{\mathrm{fin}}+\tau\log(m+1)\right).\)

Copying the preference data and writing the masses takes \(O(L)\) bit operations in the resulting encoding. Each finite comparison query is replaced by exactly one fractional comparison query. Composing this translation with the established polynomial-time Turing reduction proves the claimed hardness.

The argument uses the linear objective of total matched mass directly; no hardness transfer from the different objective of counting fully matched agents is needed.
