The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given odd \(k\ge 3\), consider a homogeneous continuum population with red mass \(x_t\), initially \(x_0=1\). Each agent samples \(k\) peers uniformly with replacement, and each sampled red opinion is independently corrupted to blue with probability \(p\); agents adopt the sample majority. With \(F_{p,k}(x)=\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}((1-p)x)^j(1-(1-p)x)^{k-j}\) and \(x_{t+1}=F_{p,k}(x_t)\), compute the largest \(p\) for which the trajectory from \(x_0=1\) retains a red majority indefinitely, giving an exact algebraic representation and isolating intervals for the threshold and its positive fixed point.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here there are \(m=2\) opinions, total mass \(1\), homogeneous agents, and initially only red has positive mass, so \(\tau=1\).
Given an odd integer \(k\ge3\), encoded in unary, updates are synchronous: each agent samples \(k\) peers uniformly with replacement; each sampled red opinion independently becomes blue with probability \(p\in[0,1]\), while blue is transmitted correctly.
The deterministic mass evolution is \(x_0=1\) and \(x_{t+1}=F_{p,k}(x_t)\), where \(F_{p,k}(x)=\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}((1-p)x)^j(1-(1-p)x)^{k-j}\).
Define \(p_k^\star=\sup\{p\in[0,1]:x_t>1/2\text{ for every }t\in\mathbb N_0\}\) and \(a_k=\lim_{t\to\infty}x_t\) at \(p=p_k^\star\).
The computational problem is to determine the largest admissible bias and its positive limiting fixed point exactly, representing each by an integer polynomial in dense coefficient encoding and a rational interval containing exactly one distinct real root of that polynomial. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

Can this exact threshold-and-fixed-point problem be solved in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For finite graphs with minimum out-degree \(\omega(\log n)\), fixed odd \(k\ge3\), and independent initial red probability \(q>1/2\), disruption takes superpolynomial time below the critical bias and constant time above it, with high probability.
For the same scalar recurrence, a critical bias and its unique positive fixed point are already characterized; numerical approximation is discussed, without a bit-complexity bound for the exact computation specified here. [Source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00446-023-00444-2).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to 1 and treated the supplied recurrence as the deterministic continuum model.
  • Interpreted retaining a red majority as strictly greater than 1/2 at every finite round; defined the threshold initially as a supremum to avoid assuming attainment.
  • Interpreted the requested positive fixed point as the trajectory's limiting red mass at the critical bias.
  • Chose unary encoding of k so polynomial time in k is polynomial in input length.
  • Specified dense integer polynomial representations with rational root-isolating intervals, without requiring minimal polynomials or a prescribed interval width.
  • Used polynomial-time Turing reductions to give NP-hardness a precise meaning for this exact computation problem.

1. Result

The exact problem is deterministic polynomial-time solvable for the specified family, where \(m=2\), \(\tau=1\), and \(k\) is encoded in unary. Both requested numbers admit integer defining polynomials of degree at most \((k-1)/2\), with polynomial coefficient bit lengths, and rational isolating intervals computable in polynomial time.

Consequently, NP-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The argument below concerns the stated scalar recurrence. The finite-graph disruption results in the [cited paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00446-023-00444-2) are not needed for this exact computation.

2. Characterizing the threshold

Write \(k=2r+1\), where \(r\geq1\), and define

\(B(y)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=r+1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}y^j(1-y)^{k-j}.\)

Thus \(F_{p,k}(x)=B((1-p)x)\). Set \(C=k\binom{k-1}{r}\).

Differentiating the sum and using
\(j\binom{k}{j}=k\binom{k-1}{j-1}\) and
\((k-j)\binom{k}{j}=k\binom{k-1}{j}\), all terms cancel except the boundary term. Hence

\(B'(y)=Cy^r(1-y)^r.\)

In particular, \(B\) is strictly increasing on \((0,1)\). Binomial symmetry, with no ties because \(k\) is odd, gives \(B(1/2)=1/2\); also \(B(0)=0\) and \(B(1)=1\).

Define

\(h(y)=yB'(y)-B(y).\)

Its derivative is

\(h'(y)=yB''(y)=Cr\,y^r(1-y)^{r-1}(1-2y).\)

Therefore \(h\) strictly increases on \((0,1/2)\) and strictly decreases on \((1/2,1)\). Since \(h(0)=0\) and \(h(1)=-1\), there is exactly one \(z\in(1/2,1)\) satisfying \(h(z)=0\).

For \(y>0\), put \(R(y)=B(y)/y\). Then

\(R'(y)=h(y)/y^2.\)

The lowest power occurring in \(B\) is \(y^{r+1}\), so \(R\) extends continuously to \(R(0)=0\). The preceding sign analysis proves that \(R\) has a unique global maximum on \([0,1]\), attained at \(z\). Moreover,

\(1=R(1)<R(z)<2,\)

where the last inequality follows from \(B(z)<1\) and \(z>1/2\).

Define

\(c_\star=\dfrac{z}{B(z)},\qquad p_\star=1-c_\star,\qquad a_\star=B(z).\)

These satisfy

\(\frac12<c_\star<1,\qquad 0<p_\star<\frac12,\qquad \frac12<a_\star<1.\)

We now verify that these are precisely the quantities requested.

Let \(c=1-p\). Since \(x\mapsto B(cx)\) is nondecreasing and maps \([0,1]\) into itself, the trajectory starting at \(x_0=1\) is nonincreasing: \(x_1\leq x_0\), and induction gives \(x_{t+1}\leq x_t\). It therefore converges to a limit \(\ell\), which satisfies \(\ell=B(c\ell)\) by continuity.

If \(0<c<c_\star\), a positive fixed point \(x\) would give, on putting \(y=cx>0\),

\(R(y)=\dfrac{B(y)}{y}=\dfrac1c>\dfrac1{c_\star}=R(z),\)

contradicting maximality. Thus the limit is zero. The same conclusion holds for \(c=0\). Consequently, whenever \(p>p_\star\), some finite iterate is below \(1/2\).

If \(c\geq c_\star\), then

\(B(ca_\star)\geq B(c_\star a_\star)=B(z)=a_\star.\)

Starting from \(x_0=1\geq a_\star\), induction yields \(x_t\geq a_\star>1/2\) for every \(t\).

At \(c=c_\star\), any positive fixed point must satisfy \(R(cx)=R(z)\). Uniqueness of the maximizer forces \(cx=z\), hence \(x=a_\star\). The limiting trajectory is bounded below by \(a_\star\), so its limit equals \(a_\star\).

We have proved that the admissible set is exactly \([0,p_\star]\), including its endpoint, and therefore

\(\boxed{p_k^\star=1-\dfrac{z}{B(z)},\qquad a_k=B(z).}\)

3. Integer defining polynomials

Because the lowest power in \(B\) is \(y^{r+1}\), the polynomial \(h\) is divisible by \(y^{r+1}\). Define

\(G(y)=\dfrac{yB'(y)-B(y)}{y^{r+1}}\in\mathbb Z[y].\)

The degree of \(B\) is \(k\), so \(\deg G=r\). Its constant coefficient is

\(G(0)=r\binom{k}{r+1}\neq0,\)

and \(G(1)=-1\). The number \(z\) is its unique root in \((1/2,1)\).

Define two resultants, eliminating \(y\):

\(P(T)=\operatorname{Res}_y\bigl(G(y),(1-T)B(y)-y\bigr),\)

\(Q(A)=\operatorname{Res}_y\bigl(G(y),A-B(y)\bigr).\)

They have integer coefficients, and the formulas already established imply

\(P(p_k^\star)=0,\qquad Q(a_k)=0.\)

We must also verify that these are nonzero polynomials.

Let \(\beta\) be any complex root of \(G\). Then \(\beta\neq0\). If \(B(\beta)=0\), the identity \(h(\beta)=0\) would imply \(B'(\beta)=0\). But \(B'(y)=Cy^r(1-y)^r\), so \(\beta\) would be \(0\) or \(1\), both excluded by \(G(0)\neq0\) and \(G(1)\neq0\). Thus \(B(\beta)\neq0\).

The resultant product formula, with roots of \(G\) counted with multiplicity, now shows that \(P\) is a nonzero product of \(r\) linear factors in \(T\), and \(Q\) a nonzero product of \(r\) linear factors in \(A\). Hence

\(\deg P=\deg Q=r.\)

Their degrees need not be minimal; minimal polynomials are not required by the output specification.

For coefficient sizes, expanding each term of \(B\) gives the bound
\(\lvert [y^i]B\rvert\leq(k+1)4^k\). Consequently, \(B\) and \(G\) have coefficients of \(O(k)\) bits.

The Sylvester matrices defining \(P\) and \(Q\) have size \(k+r=O(k)\), with entries linear in the output variable and integer coefficients of \(O(k)\) bits. If their size is \(n\) and entry coefficients have absolute value at most \(H_0\), the determinant expansion bounds every coefficient by \(n!2^nH_0^n\). Thus \(P\) and \(Q\) have coefficients of \(O(k^2)\) bits and dense encodings of \(O(k^3)\) bits.

4. Constructing isolating intervals

The following elementary separation bound supplies a polynomial precision guarantee.

*Separation lemma.* Let \(U\in\mathbb Z[X]\) be nonconstant, of degree at most \(d\), and let \(H\geq1\) bound its coefficient absolute values. Any two distinct complex roots of \(U\) are separated by at least

\(\sigma=[2(1+H)]^{-d^2}.\)

*Proof.* Let \(S\) be the primitive squarefree part of \(U\). By Gauss’s lemma, \(S\) divides \(U\) in \(\mathbb Z[X]\), so its leading coefficient \(a\) satisfies \(\lvert a\rvert\leq H\). Every root has absolute value at most \(1+H\), by the Cauchy root bound.

Write \(e=\deg S\). If \(e\leq1\), there is nothing to prove. Otherwise its discriminant is a nonzero integer. For any chosen pair of roots at distance \(\delta\), put \(D=2(1+H)\). The discriminant formula gives

\(1\leq\lvert\operatorname{disc}(S)\rvert \leq H^{2e-2}\delta^2D^{e(e-1)-2}.\)

Therefore

\(\delta\geq H^{-(e-1)}D^{-(e(e-1)-2)/2}\geq D^{-d^2}.\)

This proves the lemma, including for polynomials with repeated roots.

Apply it to \(P\) and \(Q\), using \(d=r\) and a common coefficient bound \(H\). Put \(D=2(1+H)\) and \(\sigma=D^{-r^2}\). Since \(\log_2 H=O(k^2)\), the bit length of \(\sigma\) is \(O(k^4)\).

Bisect the interval \([1/2,1]\) using exact signs of \(G\). On this interval, \(G\) is positive below \(z\) and negative above \(z\), as proved through the sign analysis of \(h\). Thus bisection maintains a bracket \(u<z<v\).

If a midpoint is exactly \(z\), then both requested values are rational. Exact evaluation of their formulas gives linear integer defining polynomials; the interval \([0,1]\) isolates each root because both values lie strictly inside it.

Otherwise let \(\delta=v-u\). Define rational intervals

\(I_a=[B(u),B(v)],\)

\(I_p=\left[1-\dfrac{v}{B(u)},\;1-\dfrac{u}{B(v)}\right].\)

Strict monotonicity and positivity of \(B\) show that these contain \(a_k\) and \(p_k^\star\), respectively, strictly between their endpoints.

For \(y\in[1/2,1]\), we have \(B(y)\geq1/2\) and \(B'(y)\leq C\). Hence

\(\lvert I_a\rvert\leq C\delta,\)

and

\(\lvert I_p\rvert =\dfrac{v-u}{B(u)} +\dfrac{u(B(v)-B(u))}{B(u)B(v)} \leq(2+4C)\delta.\)

Let \(K=2+4C\). Perform

\(N=r^2\lceil\log_2D\rceil+\lceil\log_2K\rceil+2\)

bisections. The resulting width is \(\delta=2^{-N-1}\), so both image intervals have width strictly less than \(\sigma\).

Each interval contains its desired root, while the separation lemma prevents it from containing a second distinct root of the corresponding polynomial. Thus \((P,I_p)\) and \((Q,I_a)\) are valid exact output representations. Repeated roots cause no difficulty because the requirement concerns distinct roots.

5. Bit complexity

Here is a conservative explicit bound; no fast arithmetic is needed.

The defining polynomials can be constructed by evaluating each Sylvester determinant at \(r+1\) integer arguments and interpolating. This suffices because their degrees are \(r\). Determinants and interpolation can be computed by exact rational Gaussian elimination. Reduced intermediate fractions are bounded by ratios of minors, so their bit lengths are polynomial. Using schoolbook arithmetic, a conservative bound for this stage is \(\widetilde O(k^{10})\) bit operations.

For interval construction, \(N=O(k^4)\). Every dyadic bisection endpoint has \(O(k^4)\) bits. A degree-\(O(k)\) polynomial evaluated at such an endpoint has numerator and denominator bit lengths \(O(k^5)\). Exact Horner evaluation uses \(O(k)\) arithmetic operations. Bounding each operation by schoolbook arithmetic on \(O(k^5)\)-bit integers gives a conservative total of \(\widetilde O(k^{15})\) bit operations for bisection and final interval evaluation. The interval endpoints have \(O(k^5)\) bits.

Including input reading, the restricted-family running time is therefore

\(\boxed{T(2,1,k,L)=\widetilde O(L+k^{15}).}\)

The notation \(\widetilde O\) suppresses logarithmic factors. Since unary encoding implies \(k\leq L\), this is polynomial in the total input length. Equivalently, it is polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) on the promised family \(m=2,\tau=1\); no assertion about unrestricted societies is needed.

If an NP-complete decision problem had a polynomial-time Turing reduction to this exact computation, replacing its oracle calls by the algorithm above would give a polynomial-time decision algorithm. Therefore such NP-hardness would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

6. Check at the smallest allowed sample size

For \(k=3\),

\(B(y)=3y^2-2y^3,\qquad G(y)=3-4y,\qquad z=3/4.\)

Consequently,

\(p_3^\star=1/9,\qquad a_3=27/32.\)

Valid defining polynomials are \(9T-1\) and \(32A-27\), respectively. This verifies the construction in the first nontrivial case.
