Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(S\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s strict school ranking, per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and original and adjusted priority orders over types, compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{ts}\geq 0\), including the outside option, with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Require individual rationality, non-wastefulness, and no positive-mass blocking pair under the intersection of the two priority profiles. Call a type improved if some type it outranks under the original profile outranks it under the adjusted profile. The output should be weakly stable and, for every group \(G\) containing all improved types, admit no other weakly stable assignment that Pareto improves \(G\), where improvement is witnessed by typewise couplings of old and new assignments that make every matched unit weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) contain \(m\geq3\) schools and let \(D=S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), where \(\emptyset\) is the outside option. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq3\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a strict ranking \(P_t\) of \(D\). Each school has a positive binary-rational capacity \(\kappa_s\) and original and adjusted strict linear priority orders \(\succ_s^O,\succ_s^A\) on \(T\), differing at some school. Units of the same type have identical preferences and no priority over one another. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible assignment satisfies \(x_{ta}\geq0\), \(\sum_{a\in D}x_{ta}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\) for every school \(s\); the outside option has unlimited capacity. Individual rationality requires \(x_{ts}=0\) whenever \(\emptyset P_t s\).
Write \(d_{ts}(x)=\sum_{a\in D:\,sP_ta}x_{ta}\) and \(t\succ_s u\) when both \(t\succ_s^O u\) and \(t\succ_s^A u\). An assignment is weakly stable if it is feasible and individually rational, \(d_{ts}(x)>0\) implies \(\sum_u x_{us}=\kappa_s\), and no \(t,u,s\) satisfy \(d_{ts}(x)>0\), \(x_{us}>0\), and \(t\succ_s u\).
The improved types are \(H=\{t\in T:\exists u,s,\ u\succ_s^O t\text{ and }t\succ_s^A u\}\).
An assignment \(y\) Pareto improves \(x\) for \(G\subseteq T\) if, for every \(t\in G\), there is a nonnegative coupling \(c^t_{ab}\) with marginals \(\sum_b c^t_{ab}=x_{ta}\), \(\sum_a c^t_{ab}=y_{tb}\), supported only on \(b=a\) or \(bP_ta\), and \(\sum_{t\in G}\sum_{a,b:\,bP_ta}c^t_{ab}>0\). This includes outside-option mass; types outside \(G\) may lose.
The required output is an exact rational weakly stable assignment \(x\) such that, simultaneously for every \(H\subseteq G\subseteq T\), no weakly stable assignment \(y\) Pareto improves \(x\) for \(G\); report nonexistence if there is no such assignment. Competing assignments and couplings may have real entries.

Is this computation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite individual students, efficiency-adjusted deferred acceptance using the adjusted priorities computes in polynomial time a weakly stable matching undominated for every group containing all improved students. [Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12558v1)
No result settling this precise mass computation problem was identified in the literature search.

1. Result

The computation problem is polynomial-time solvable. A required assignment always exists.

The proof uses two established finite results, together with a compression argument. The important point is that units of a type can be ordered consistently at every school. EADA then removes whole suffixes of these orders, so the remaining units of each type continue to have identical preference lists.

2. Two results used

We use the finite EADA theorem stated in the question: with the adjusted priorities as the linear extension, its outcome is weakly stable and undominated for every group containing all improved students.

We also use the following properties of its formal implementation:

  • A round runs student-proposing deferred acceptance on the remaining problem.
  • It fixes students assigned to underdemanded schools, and unmatched students. A school is underdemanded if no remaining student prefers it to their current assignment.
  • After fixing a student, it deletes any still-active school that student prefers to their fixed assignment from the lists of remaining students below them in the intersection priority.
  • Every round fixes at least one student. The successive full assignments are weakly improving under the original preferences and remain stable under the intersection priorities.

These properties are recorded in Appendix A.2, including Lemmas 5 and 6, of [Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12558v1).

The second result concerns ordinary stable allocation with strict preferences on both sides. Given rational vertex quotas and edge upper bounds, a row-optimal stable allocation can be computed in polynomial time. When all stable allocations saturate the row quotas, the row-optimal allocation maximizes, simultaneously, every row’s cumulative allocation to its most preferred partners. The algorithms preserve integrality on integral input: their numerical updates use additions, subtractions, and minima.

This follows from the inductive algorithm, Theorem 10, and the row-optimal algorithm in the corrected [Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302). Its row preference order implies the cumulative dominance just stated: an allocation it ranks higher can be obtained by transferring quantity from less preferred partners to more preferred partners.

We next verify how these results apply here.

3. Strict-priority mass deferred acceptance is computable in polynomial time

Consider a subproblem with remaining masses \(\rho_t\), strict school priorities, and a common remaining preference list for each type. Zero-mass types are omitted.

Construct a stable-allocation instance as follows:

  • The row vertices are the remaining types, with quotas \(\rho_t\).
  • The column vertices are the active schools and an outside-option column.
  • School quotas are \(\kappa_s\); the outside-option quota is \(2\).
  • Include an edge for each currently acceptable school, and an outside-option edge for each type.
  • Give every edge upper bound \(2\).
  • Row preferences are the remaining type preferences, with the outside option last. School preferences are their strict type priorities. The outside column has any strict type order.

The remaining total mass is at most \(1\). Consequently, no edge can reach its upper bound, and the outside column cannot reach its quota.

In a strict stable-allocation instance, an edge \((t,a)\) below its upper bound is nonblocking precisely when either the row quota is already allocated to partners weakly preferred to \(a\), or the column quota is already allocated to partners weakly preferred to \(t\).

Applying this to the outside edge forces every row to be saturated. For an acceptable school edge, therefore, stability is exactly

\[ \text{if type }t\text{ has mass below }s,\quad \sum_{u:\,u=t\text{ or }u\succ_s t}z_{us}=\kappa_s. \]

Equivalently, a school desired by some mass of type \(t\) must be full and have no assigned mass from lower-priority types. This is precisely mass stability for strict school priorities.

For clarity, the displayed condition can also be written using the required inline delimiters:
\(\ d_{ts}(z)>0\Longrightarrow \sum_{u:\,u=t\text{ or }u\succ_s t}z_{us}=\kappa_s.\)

Thus the stable-allocation algorithm provides a polynomial-time primitive for these strict-priority mass subproblems.

We need the primitive to reproduce aggregated student-proposing deferred acceptance, rather than merely some stable allocation. Here is why it does.

Choose a common denominator \(Q\) for all initial masses and capacities. Conceptually replace type \(t\) by \(Q\mu_t\) students and school capacities by \(Q\kappa_s\). Within each type, order its students identically at every school, and place different types in school-priority blocks.

For integral remaining masses, any integral stable allocation has a canonical finite realization: assign each type’s students, in their common internal order, to outcomes from best to worst. This realization is stable:

  • Cross-type blocking would contradict mass stability.
  • Within-type blocking is excluded by the sorted realization.
  • Nonwastefulness follows from the mass condition.

Conversely, aggregation of a stable finite matching satisfies strict-priority mass stability.

Let \(A\) be the aggregation of finite student-proposing deferred acceptance, and let \(Z\) be the integral row-optimal stable allocation computed by the primitive. Finite student optimality implies that \(A\) cumulatively dominates \(Z\) for every type. Row optimality implies that \(Z\) cumulatively dominates \(A\). Their cumulative sums, and hence every coordinate, are equal.

Therefore the primitive computes exactly the aggregated finite deferred-acceptance outcome.

4. EADA admits a polynomial-size type representation

In the conceptual finite instance, lift both original and adjusted priorities by placing types in their respective priority blocks and using the same internal student order within each type, at every school and under both profiles.

Initially, all students of a type have identical preferences. We prove that this remains true among the students still present at every EADA round.

Suppose it holds at the beginning of a round. Deferred acceptance assigns the remaining students of each type in sorted order under their common current preferences. Indeed, if a higher internal-priority student received a worse outcome than a lower internal-priority student, that pair would block the deferred-acceptance matching.

Now consider the students removed from a particular type.

If a student is assigned to an underdemanded school \(s\), no student of the same type can receive an outcome worse than \(s\): such a student would prefer \(s\), contradicting underdemandedness. Thus \(s\) is that type’s worst assigned outcome. All students of the type assigned to \(s\) form a suffix of its remaining internal order.

There cannot be two distinct underdemanded schools receiving students of the same type. Students assigned to the worse school would demand the better school. Likewise, if some students of the type are unmatched, every school receiving that type is demanded by those unmatched students.

Consequently, the students removed from a type are either absent or form a suffix assigned to one common outcome.

Removing these suffixes leaves prefixes of the internal orders. A removed student therefore never has higher internal priority than a remaining student of the same type. Hence the preference-deletion step creates no within-type differences.

For two distinct types, the relevant intersection-priority comparison is uniform across all their students. Because the removed students of a type have one common outcome, any deletion they cause applies to every remaining student of another type.

This proves the invariant.

We can therefore implement each round using only:

  • Remaining masses \(\rho_t\).
  • One remaining acceptable-school list per type.
  • The active schools.
  • The accumulated fixed assignment.

Run the primitive from Section 3, obtaining \(A\). Compute the underdemanded schools using the current lists. Fix all mass assigned to those schools and to the outside option, and subtract it from the remaining type masses.

If positive mass of type \(t\) was fixed at outcome \(a\), delete an active school \(s\) from type \(u\)’s acceptable list whenever
\(sP_ta\) and \(t\succ_s u\).

The suffix argument proves, inductively, that this implementation exactly aggregates finite EADA.

There are at most \(m+1\) rounds. After the first round, every remaining student is matched to a school. The finite EADA monotonicity property ensures that these students cannot subsequently become unmatched. Every nonterminal subsequent round fixes at least one student, so it removes at least one active school. At most \(m\) schools can be removed.

Finally, all computations can retain denominator \(Q\). After scaling by \(Q\), the stable-allocation algorithms and the round updates preserve integrality. Also, \(\log Q\) is bounded by the sum of the input denominator bit lengths. Each round has polynomial-size data, and its exact arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity.

The compressed algorithm consequently runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), and returns an exact rational assignment.

5. Refinement preserves the outcome

The finite theorem alone does not yet exclude competing assignments on finer grids. We establish that link explicitly.

Take any finite instance arising above and replace each student by \(r\) consecutive copies, with identical preferences. Multiply every school capacity by \(r\). At every school, under both priority profiles, put the copies consecutively in their parent student’s position.

Under synchronous deferred acceptance, copies of a student always make the same proposal and are either all held or all rejected. This follows by induction over proposal steps: applicant blocks have size \(r\), capacities are multiples of \(r\), and each school accepts an initial segment of those consecutive blocks.

Thus deferred acceptance is exactly replicated. Underdemanded schools are unchanged, so EADA removes entire copy blocks. Comparisons between removed and remaining blocks reproduce the original priority comparisons; no parent block is partly removed. The preference deletions are therefore replicated as well.

Induction over EADA rounds proves:

Refinement lemma. Replacing every grid student by \(r\) consecutive copies multiplies every EADA assignment count by \(r\). Its aggregated mass assignment remains unchanged.

In addition, the improved students in every such expansion are exactly the copies of types in \(H\). There are no within-type reversals, and every cross-type reversal is precisely an original type-level reversal.

6. Real competitors can be reduced to rational competitors

Let \(x\) be the rational output of the algorithm. Suppose a real weakly stable assignment \(y\), with real couplings, Pareto improves \(x\) for some \(G\) containing \(H\).

We first obtain a rational witness.

Let \(E=\{(t,s):d_{ts}(y)>0\}\). Consider assignments \(z\) and the improvement couplings as variables. Impose feasibility, individual rationality, and:

  • \(d_{ts}(z)=0\) for every \((t,s)\notin E\).
  • \(\sum_u z_{us}=\kappa_s\) for every \((t,s)\in E\).
  • \(z_{us}=0\) whenever \((t,s)\in E\) and \(t\succ_s u\).

Every assignment satisfying these constraints is weakly stable. Any positive demand belongs to \(E\), where fullness and the necessary zero assignments are enforced.

Also impose the coupling constraints for improvement of \(x\) on \(G\), including their permitted support. These are rational linear constraints. Their feasible set is a nonempty bounded rational polytope: the supposed \(y\) and its couplings belong to it.

Maximize the total strictly improving coupling mass. Its maximum is positive, and a rational vertex attains that maximum. Hence there is a rational weakly stable assignment \(y'\), with rational couplings, Pareto improving \(x\) for the same \(G\).

Choose a common denominator \(Q'=rQ\) for \(y'\) and the original grid. By the refinement lemma, finite EADA on this grid still aggregates to \(x\).

Realize \(y'\) canonically by assigning each type’s copies, in internal-priority order, to its outcomes from best to worst. Section 3’s argument shows that this finite matching is weakly stable under the lifted intersection priorities.

It remains to verify finite Pareto improvement.

List type \(t\)’s outcomes from best to worst, and let \(F_t^z(j)\) be the mass assigned by \(z\) to its first \(j\) outcomes. An upward coupling implies
\(F_t^{y'}(j)\geq F_t^x(j)\) for every \(t\in G\) and every \(j\): mass originally in an upper set cannot leave that set.

The finite EADA outcome itself is sorted within each type under the original preferences. Otherwise a higher internal-priority copy and a lower copy at a preferred school would block under both lifted priority profiles.

Suppose copy \(\ell\) of a protected type receives outcome of rank \(j\) under EADA. Then
\(\ell\leq Q'F_t^x(j)\).
Because
\(Q'F_t^{y'}(j)\geq Q'F_t^x(j)\),
its canonical outcome under \(y'\) also has rank at most \(j\). Thus every copy of every type in \(G\) weakly improves.

At least one such copy strictly improves. To see this, give outcomes strictly decreasing utilities according to rank. Positive strictly improving coupling mass makes the protected types’ total utility strictly increase, so their assignments cannot all remain identical.

We have therefore constructed a weakly stable finite matching Pareto improving the refined EADA outcome for the group consisting of all copies of types in \(G\). This group contains every improved student. It contradicts the finite EADA theorem.

No real competitor exists.

7. Conclusion

The compressed algorithm returns an exact rational weakly stable assignment undominated, simultaneously, for every \(H\subseteq G\subseteq T\). It uses at most \(m+1\) polynomial-time strict stable-allocation computations. Nonexistence never occurs under the stated hypotheses.
