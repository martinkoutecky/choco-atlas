The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite types \(I\) and \(J\) on the two sides, rational masses \(p_i,q_j\), strict rankings of counterpart types and being unmatched, and a communication relation \(H\subseteq I\times J\), choose match masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\). Require row and column sums to respect type masses, and \(x_{ij}=0\) unless the match is mutually acceptable. Let \(u_i=p_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=q_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\); for each \((i,j)\in H\), define \(b^I_{ij}=\mathbf 1[j\succ_i\bot]u_i+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(b^J_{ij}=\mathbf 1[i\succ_j\bot]v_j+\sum_{\ell:i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j}\). Require \(b^I_{ij}=0\) or \(b^J_{ij}=0\), and maximize \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). Under UGC, is there no polynomial-time algorithm achieving approximation ratio \(3/2-\epsilon\) for every constant \(\epsilon>0\), with time measured in the type counts and mass bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be disjoint finite sets of types. A society is given by its support: the \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(p_i,q_j\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses need not sum to one or have equal totals on the two sides. Set \(m=\max\{|I|,|J|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a strict total ranking of counterpart types and the unmatched option \(\bot\). A pair \((i,j)\) is mutually acceptable when \(j\succ_i\bot\) and \(i\succ_j\bot\). The input communication relation \(H\subseteq I\times J\) is arbitrary and applies uniformly to the masses of each type pair; matches outside \(H\) are allowed.
A mass matching has real entries \(x_{ij}\ge0\), with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le p_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le q_j\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) for pairs that are not mutually acceptable. Write \(u_i=p_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=q_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\).
For each \((i,j)\in H\), define \(b^I_{ij}=\mathbf1[j\succ_i\bot]u_i+\sum_{k\in J:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(b^J_{ij}=\mathbf1[i\succ_j\bot]v_j+\sum_{\ell\in I:\,i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j}\). The matching is socially stable precisely when \(b^I_{ij}=0\) or \(b^J_{ij}=0\) for every such pair.
Maximize \(S(x)=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\) over socially stable mass matchings. An \(\alpha\)-approximation returns a feasible rational matrix with \(S(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha\).

Under UGC, is a \((3/2-\epsilon)\)-approximation running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) impossible for every fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2)\), or does such an algorithm exist for some such \(\epsilon\)?

For discrete agents, maximum socially stable matching is NP-hard, admits a polynomial-time \(3/2\)-approximation, and is UGC-hard to approximate within \(3/2-\epsilon\) for every fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2)\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.3309v2).

Result

Under UGC, a polynomial-time \((3/2-\epsilon)\)-approximation is impossible for every fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2)\).

The key is a rounding lemma: when every supported type has mass \(1\), any socially stable fractional matching can be converted in polynomial time into a socially stable integral matching of at least the same size. Thus divisibility does not improve the optimum on the instances needed to transfer discrete hardness.

1. Conditions that rounding must preserve

For this section, assume \(p_i=q_j=1\) for every \(i\in I\) and \(j\in J\). Let \(x\) be a socially stable mass matching. Define its positive support and its saturated vertices by

\(E_x=\{(i,j):x_{ij}>0\}\),

\(A_x=\{i\in I:\sum_jx_{ij}=1\}\), and \(B_x=\{j\in J:\sum_ix_{ij}=1\}\).

Claim. Every feasible matching \(y\) supported on \(E_x\) that saturates all vertices in \(A_x\cup B_x\) is socially stable.

Proof. Fix \((i,j)\in H\).

First suppose the pair is not mutually acceptable. If \(j\prec_i\bot\), then every counterpart \(k\) receiving positive mass from \(i\) in any feasible matching satisfies \(k\succ_i\bot\succ_i j\). Consequently, the unmatched coefficient in \(b^I_{ij}(y)\) is zero, and every term in its sum is zero. Hence \(b^I_{ij}(y)=0\). The case \(i\prec_j\bot\) similarly gives \(b^J_{ij}(y)=0\).

Now suppose the pair is mutually acceptable. Social stability of \(x\) gives \(b^I_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(b^J_{ij}(x)=0\).

If \(b^I_{ij}(x)=0\), its definition becomes

\(0=u_i(x)+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\).

All terms are nonnegative. Therefore \(u_i(x)=0\), so \(i\in A_x\), and \(x_{ik}=0\) for every \(k\) ranked below \(j\). Since \(y\) saturates \(i\) and uses only edges in \(E_x\), it follows that

\(u_i(y)=0\) and \(y_{ik}=0\) whenever \(j\succ_i k\).

Thus \(b^I_{ij}(y)=0\). If instead \(b^J_{ij}(x)=0\), the same argument on the other side gives \(b^J_{ij}(y)=0\).

Every pair in \(H\) therefore satisfies the required disjunction. \(\square\)

2. Polynomial-time rounding without loss

Rounding lemma. For unit masses, every socially stable real matching \(x\) admits a socially stable matrix \(y\in\{0,1\}^{I\times J}\) with \(S(y)\ge S(x)\). If \(x\) is rational, such a matrix can be computed in time polynomial in the instance length and the encoding length of \(x\).

Proof. Construct a directed network with vertices \(\{s,t\}\cup I\cup J\) and these arcs:

  • \(s\to i\), with upper bound \(1\) and lower bound \(1\) if \(i\in A_x\), otherwise \(0\);
  • \(i\to j\) for \((i,j)\in E_x\), with bounds \(0\) and \(1\);
  • \(j\to t\), with upper bound \(1\) and lower bound \(1\) if \(j\in B_x\), otherwise \(0\);
  • \(t\to s\), with bounds \(0\) and \(K=\min\{|I|,|J|\}\).

A circulation is an assignment of arc flows satisfying these bounds and flow conservation at every vertex. The matching \(x\) induces a feasible circulation \(f\), given by

\(f_{ij}=x_{ij}\), \(f_{si}=\sum_jx_{ij}\), \(f_{jt}=\sum_ix_{ij}\), and \(f_{ts}=S(x)\).

We next prove constructively that this circulation can be made integral without decreasing \(f_{ts}\).

Consider the undirected graph formed by the arcs whose current flows are nonintegral. No vertex can have exactly one incident nonintegral arc: otherwise its conservation equation would contain one signed nonintegral term and only integer terms, which cannot sum to zero. If any nonintegral arcs remain, this finite graph consequently contains a cycle.

Traverse such a cycle. Increasing flows on arcs agreeing with the traversal and decreasing flows on the other arcs preserves conservation. There are two possible directions for this perturbation. Choose one in which the flow on \(t\to s\) increases, if that arc belongs to the cycle; if it does not, either direction leaves that flow unchanged.

For each cycle arc, consider the distance from its current flow to the next integer in its chosen direction. These distances are positive. Let \(\delta\) be their minimum, and perturb every cycle arc by \(\delta\) in its chosen direction.

This operation is feasible because every arc has integer lower and upper bounds, so the next integer lies within its bounds. At least one previously nonintegral arc becomes integral. No integral arc was on the cycle, so none becomes nonintegral. Conservation is preserved, and \(f_{ts}\) does not decrease.

Repeating this operation at most once per network arc produces an integral feasible circulation \(\widehat f\) with

\(\widehat f_{ts}\ge S(x)\).

Set \(y_{ij}=\widehat f_{ij}\) on \(E_x\), and set all other entries to zero. The unit capacities make \(y\) a discrete matching. Its edges are mutually acceptable because they belong to \(E_x\), and the lower bounds ensure that it saturates \(A_x\cup B_x\). Section 1 therefore proves its social stability. Conservation gives

\(S(y)=\widehat f_{ts}\ge S(x)\).

For rational \(x\), this construction is polynomial-time. The network has \(\tau+2\) vertices and at most \(|I||J|+\tau+1\) arcs, and each iteration requires a cycle search and rational arithmetic. To check bit complexity, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the input entries of \(x\). Its bit length is at most their total encoding length. Every initial flow is a multiple of \(1/D\), and each perturbation preserves that property. All flows remain between \(0\) and \(K\), or between \(0\) and \(1\), so their encoding lengths remain polynomially bounded. Exact support and saturation tests are also polynomial-time. \(\square\)

3. Equality of the unit-mass and discrete optima

Take any discrete socially stable matching instance. Create one type for each agent, assign every type mass \(1\), retain the preferences, replace the unmatched option by \(\bot\), and retain the communication relation \(H\).

If the discrete instance uses incomplete preference lists, append omitted counterparts below \(\bot\) in any strict order. This preserves acceptability and blocking behavior.

For an integral matching matrix, \(b^I_{ij}\) is \(1\) precisely when agent \(i\) prefers \(j\) to its current partner or to being unmatched; otherwise it is \(0\). The analogous statement holds for \(b^J_{ij}\). Thus an integral matrix is socially stable under the task’s definition exactly when its corresponding discrete matching is socially stable.

Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}\) count matched pairs, and let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\) denote the optimum of the resulting mass instance.

Every discrete socially stable matching is a feasible mass matching, giving

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\ge\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}\).

Conversely, the rounding lemma converts every socially stable real mass matching into a discrete socially stable matching of at least the same size. Hence

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\le\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}\).

Therefore

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}\).

If discrete cardinality counts matched agents instead of matched pairs, both the solution value and the optimum are multiplied by \(2\); approximation ratios are unchanged.

4. Transfer of the UGC hardness

The discrete hardness theorem states that, under UGC, maximum socially stable matching has no polynomial-time \((3/2-\epsilon)\)-approximation for any fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1/2)\). Its model has strict preferences, an unmatched option, individual rationality, and an arbitrary communication graph, matching the discrete instances embedded above. See Corollary 4.4 and the definitions in Section 2 of [Askalidis, Immorlica, and Pountourakis, *Socially Stable Matchings*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3309v2).

Fix such an \(\epsilon\), write \(\alpha=3/2-\epsilon\), and suppose the requested mass approximation algorithm existed. Apply it to the unit-mass embedding of a discrete instance. Its rational output \(x\) would satisfy

\(S(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}/\alpha\).

Round \(x\) using Section 2, obtaining a discrete socially stable matching \(y\) with

\(S(y)\ge S(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}/\alpha\).

This composition runs in polynomial time: the embedding has polynomial size, every type is supported, \(m\le\tau\), and a polynomial-time algorithm produces an output of polynomial encoding length. It would therefore contradict the discrete UGC hardness theorem.

This settles the stated approximation question, already on the subclass in which every supported type has mass \(1\).
