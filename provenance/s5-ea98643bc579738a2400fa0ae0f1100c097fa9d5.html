Theorem — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(\tau\) player types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\rho\) integral item types with rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\), and value-oracle access to normalized monotone submodular valuations \(f_t\), can one compute a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq\mathcal R\) that approximates the maximum \(W(x)=\min_{x_{t,B}>0} f_t(B)\)? In particular, can one obtain a \(D^\epsilon\)-approximation in \(D^{O(1/\epsilon)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time for fixed \(\epsilon>0\), and a polylogarithmic approximation in \(D^{O(\log D/\log\log D)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time, where \(D=2+\tau+\rho\) and \(L\) is the input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge 1\) player types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=\rho\ge 1\) item types \(\mathcal R\), with nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\). Each individual receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq\mathcal R\), containing at most one item of each type.
Each type has a normalized monotone submodular valuation \(f_t:2^{\mathcal R}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), accessed through exact value queries. Let \(L\) bound both the explicit input bit length and the encoding length of any oracle answer; count oracle calls and bit operations in running times.
A feasible allocation consists of masses \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\subseteq\mathcal R}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_t\sum_{B\ni r}x_{t,B}\le q_r\) for every \(r\); unused supply is allowed.
Define \(W(x)=\min\{f_t(B):x_{t,B}>0\}\), \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_{x\text{ feasible}}W(x)\), and \(D=2+\tau+m\). An \(\alpha\)-approximation computes a feasible allocation with \(W(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha\). Randomized algorithms with success probability at least \(2/3\) are allowed.

Can this problem achieve a \(D^\epsilon\)-approximation in \(D^{O(1/\epsilon)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\), and a \((\log D)^{O(1)}\)-approximation in \(D^{O(\log D/\log\log D)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time, or are either of these guarantees impossible?

For finite players and indivisible resources, these two approximation guarantees are known for arbitrary normalized monotone submodular valuations in the value-query model. [Bamas–Morell–Rohwedder](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.04824)
For restricted assignment, where valuations are restrictions of one common submodular function, the configuration relaxation admits a polynomial-time constant approximation. [Bamas–Garg–Rohwedder](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol198-icalp2021/LIPIcs.ICALP.2021.22/LIPIcs.ICALP.2021.22.pdf)
No result for the exact rational-mass model above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total player mass to one so that supplies have an unambiguous per-capita interpretation.
  • Required nonempty player support and item ground set, positive supported masses, and nonnegative supplies; zero supplies and zero optimal welfare remain allowed.
  • Defined feasibility by full allocation of each player type's mass and upper bounds on aggregate item consumption, allowing unused supply.
  • Retained the graded question's set-valued integral bundles, so each individual receives at most one item of each type.
  • Made oracle answers exact nonnegative rationals with encoding length bounded by L, to give the requested bit-complexity bounds a precise meaning.
  • Allowed randomized approximation algorithms with success probability at least two thirds, consistent with the anchor's randomized methods.
  • Identified m with the graded question's item-type count rho.

Result

Both guarantees are achievable. In fact, the rational-mass model admits a deterministic \(4\)-approximation in \(D^{O(1)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) time, with exact feasibility and at most \(\tau+m\) positive allocation variables.

For the requested \(D^\epsilon\) guarantee, I use the usual fixed-\(\epsilon\) interpretation: multiplicative running-time constants may depend on \(\epsilon\). The treatment of small \(D\) is given explicitly below.

The decisive feature is that a feasible configuration-LP solution is already an allocation in this model. There is no subsequent integral rounding problem.

1. Threshold feasibility and its certificates

Replace each supply by \(\min\{q_r,1\}\). This preserves feasibility because the total population mass is \(1\), and an individual receives at most one item of each type. Henceforth \(0\le q_r\le1\).

For \(s>0\), define
\( \mathcal C_t(s)=\{B\subseteq\mathcal R:f_t(B)\ge s\}. \)

Consider the finite, potentially exponential, linear system \(P_s\):

\( \sum_{B\in\mathcal C_t(s)}x_{t,B}\ge\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t, \)

\( \sum_t\sum_{\substack{B\in\mathcal C_t(s)\\r\in B}}x_{t,B}\le q_r\quad\text{for every }r,\qquad x_{t,B}\ge0. \)

This system is feasible exactly when an allocation with welfare at least \(s\) exists. Indeed, if a type receives total mass \(M_t\ge\mu_t\), multiply all its allocation variables by \(\mu_t/M_t\). This makes its mass exactly \(\mu_t\), preserves the welfare threshold, and decreases resource usage.

Define
\( H(y,p)=\sum_t\mu_t y_t-\sum_rq_rp_r. \)

By Farkas’ lemma, \(P_s\) is infeasible exactly when there exist nonnegative vectors \(y,p\) satisfying

\( H(y,p)\ge1,\qquad y_t\le\sum_{r\in B}p_r\quad\text{for every }t,\ B\in\mathcal C_t(s). \)

The normalization to \(H\ge1\) is valid because an infeasibility certificate initially has \(H>0\) and can be scaled.

The same certificate characterization holds if each configuration family is replaced by any subfamily. This observation will allow recovery of an allocation using only configurations actually found by the algorithm.

2. A polynomial knapsack subroutine

We need the following elementary lemma.

Lemma. Given nonnegative rational costs \(c_r\), a rational budget \(b\ge0\), and a normalized monotone submodular function \(f\), one can find a set \(S\) satisfying

\( \sum_{r\in S}c_r\le b,\qquad f(S)\ge\frac14\max_{\sum_{r\in A}c_r\le b}f(A), \)

using polynomially many exact value queries and bit operations.

Proof. Remove items whose cost exceeds \(b\), and initially include all zero-cost items. If \(b=0\), this gives an optimum solution by monotonicity.

Suppose \(b>0\). Repeatedly choose an unselected positive-cost item maximizing marginal value divided by cost. Stop when the next item would exceed the budget, when the budget is exhausted, or when all remaining items have been selected. Return the better of the resulting feasible prefix and the best feasible singleton.

Let \(O\) be an optimum knapsack set and put \(V=f(O)\). At a current set \(A\), submodularity and monotonicity give

\( V-f(A)\le f(A\cup O)-f(A) \le\sum_{r\in O\setminus A}\bigl(f(A\cup\{r\})-f(A)\bigr). \)

Consequently, whenever \(f(A)<V\), the maximum available marginal density is at least \((V-f(A))/b\), since the total cost of \(O\setminus A\) is at most \(b\).

If a feasible prefix already has value at least \(V\), the conclusion follows. Otherwise, analyze the greedy sequence by fractionally taking its final item just far enough to bring the total cost to \(b\). A step of cost length \(\ell\), including this fractional step, reduces the deficit by a factor at most \(1-\ell/b\). Since the lengths sum to \(b\), the final virtual value is at least

\( V\left(1-\prod_i(1-\ell_i/b)\right) \ge(1-e^{-1})V. \)

Here the initial deficit is at most \(V\), and
\( \prod_i(1-\ell_i/b)\le\exp(-\sum_i\ell_i/b)=e^{-1}. \)

If the fractional final item is \(r\) and the feasible prefix is \(A\), its virtual value is at most \(f(A)+f(\{r\})\): submodularity and normalization imply that its marginal value is at most \(f(\{r\})\). Both \(A\) and \(\{r\}\) are feasible. Their better value is therefore at least

\( \frac{1-e^{-1}}2V>\frac14V. \)

If all eligible items are selected before reaching budget \(b\), their union has value at least \(V\), so that case also satisfies the claim.

There are at most \(m\) greedy steps, each requiring at most \(m\) value queries. All density comparisons use exact rational arithmetic. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial certificate bounds

We next provide bounds that make the ellipsoid argument valid in the bit model, including allocation recovery.

Put \(n=\tau+m\), and let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the masses and the capped supplies. Define

\( M=n!Q^n,\qquad R=(2m+2)M+4,\qquad r_0=\frac1{4n}. \)

These numbers have encoding lengths polynomial in \(D,L\).

Consider any collection \(\mathcal F_t\) of nonempty configurations, and its certificate system

\( y,p\ge0,\quad H(y,p)\ge1,\quad p(B)-y_t\ge0\quad(B\in\mathcal F_t), \)

where \(p(B)=\sum_{r\in B}p_r\).

Claim. If this system is feasible, its intersection with the box \([0,R]^n\) contains a Euclidean ball of radius \(r_0\).

To prove this, minimize the sum of all certificate coordinates. A minimum exists: intersecting with any nonempty bounded objective sublevel gives a compact set. The minimizing face has a vertex. At such a vertex, \(n\) linearly independent active constraints determine the coordinates.

After multiplying the \(H\)-row by \(Q\), the coefficients and right-hand sides in this defining system are integers of absolute value at most \(Q\). All other rows have coefficients in \(\{0,1,-1\}\). Cramer’s rule therefore bounds every coordinate by \(n!Q^n=M\): the numerator determinant has absolute value at most \(n!Q^n\), and the nonzero denominator determinant has absolute value at least \(1\).

Let \(u^*=(y^*,p^*)\) be this bounded certificate. Set \(y_t^0=1\), \(p_r^0=2\), and define

\( w=(2m+2)u^*+u^0. \)

Every coordinate of \(w\) is at least \(1\). Because every configuration is nonempty,

\( p^0(B)-y_t^0=2|B|-1\ge1. \)

Also, using \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_rq_r\le m\),

\( H(w)\ge(2m+2)+(1-2m)=3. \)

Every coordinate is at most \(R-2\). The normals of the configuration inequalities and the \(H\)-inequality have Euclidean norm at most \(n\), since their coordinates have absolute value at most \(1\). A perturbation of norm at most \(r_0\) changes each such left-hand side by at most \(1/4\). It also preserves the box constraints. Thus the entire radius-\(r_0\) ball around \(w\) is feasible, proving the claim.

Normalization ensures that every configuration at a positive welfare threshold is nonempty, so the claim applies throughout.

4. An exact-feasibility gap algorithm

We now construct a deterministic polynomial-time procedure which, for any positive rational threshold \(T\), returns either:

  • a feasible allocation with welfare at least \(T/4\); or
  • a correct certificate that no allocation has welfare at least \(T\).

Use the standard rational ellipsoid method in dimension \(n\), starting with a ball containing \([0,R]^n\). Its bounded-volume guarantee is the following: after polynomially many rational cutting-plane steps, it either accepts a candidate or produces an enclosing ellipsoid of volume smaller than a radius-\(r_0\) ball. The running time and intermediate encoding lengths are polynomial in the dimension, the encoding lengths of the cuts, and \(\log(R/r_0)\). This is the usual rational ellipsoid volume argument; rational rounding preserves polynomial bit complexity. See the [MIT notes on linear programming algorithms](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

At a candidate \((y,p)\), first check the explicit constraints

\( (y,p)\in[0,R]^n,\qquad H(y,p)\ge1. \)

If one fails, return its violated inequality as a cut.

Otherwise, process each type with \(y_t>0\). Let \(Q_t'\) be the product of the denominators of \(y_t,p_1,\ldots,p_m\), and put

\( b_t=y_t-\frac1{Q_t'}. \)

The number \(b_t\) is nonnegative. Moreover, every subset cost and \(y_t\) is an integer multiple of \(1/Q_t'\). Therefore

\( p(B)<y_t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p(B)\le b_t. \)

Apply the knapsack lemma to \(f_t\), costs \(p_r\), and budget \(b_t\), obtaining \(B_t\).

If \(f_t(B_t)\ge T/4\), return the configuration cut

\( y_t\le p(B_t). \)

It is genuinely violated, because \(p(B_t)\le b_t<y_t\). Record this configuration.

If no type returns such a cut, then no configuration of value at least \(T\) has cost strictly below its corresponding \(y_t\). Indeed, any such configuration would be feasible for that type’s knapsack problem, forcing the returned value to be at least \(T/4\). Types with \(y_t=0\) cannot have a strictly cheaper configuration because prices are nonnegative.

Thus the candidate satisfies all certificate inequalities for \(P_T\), as well as \(H\ge1\). Farkas’ lemma proves that \(P_T\) is infeasible. Return this certificate and stop.

Suppose instead the ellipsoid reaches its volume cutoff. Let \(\mathcal F_t\) consist of the configurations recorded for type \(t\). Each has value at least \(T/4\).

The covering-packing LP restricted to these configurations must be feasible. Otherwise, Farkas’ lemma and Section 3 would give a radius-\(r_0\) ball of bounded certificates satisfying every recorded cut and every explicit constraint. Every successive ellipsoid contains that intersection, contradicting the final volume bound.

There are only polynomially many recorded configurations. Solve their explicit rational LP, using exact type-mass equalities, to obtain the promised allocation.

All returned cuts have polynomial encoding length. Ellipsoid candidate coordinates have polynomial encoding length, so the products \(Q_t'\), the strict budgets, and all knapsack arithmetic do too. This proves polynomial complexity in both oracle calls and bit operations.

This is an adaptation of the configuration-LP separation approach in [Bamas–Garg–Rohwedder, Appendix B.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.06939). The argument above verifies that arbitrary type-dependent valuations and rational right-hand sides are permitted.

5. Obtaining a factor of exactly four

The gap procedure already yields a constant approximation by geometric threshold search. The answer-length bound lets us obtain a factor of exactly \(4\).

Every positive oracle value lies between \(2^{-L}\) and \(2^L\), and its denominator is at most \(2^L\). These conservative bounds follow directly from its binary encoding length.

The optimum is attained and is one of the oracle values: every allocation welfare is the minimum of a nonempty finite set of values, and only finitely many such values exist.

First run the gap procedure at \(T=2^{-L}\). If it declares infeasibility, then \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\); return the allocation assigning the empty bundle to every type.

Otherwise, retain the returned allocation and initialize

\( a=2^{-L},\qquad b=2^L+1. \)

Bisect this interval. On success, replace \(a\) by the queried threshold and retain the new allocation. On certified infeasibility, replace \(b\) by that threshold. The invariant \(b\ge\mathrm{OPT}\) always holds.

After \(O(L)\) iterations, stop when

\( b-a<2^{-2L}. \)

The retained allocation satisfies \(W(x)\ge a/4\), and \(a>\mathrm{OPT}-2^{-2L}\).

For any positively allocated bundle, suppose its value \(v\) were below \(\mathrm{OPT}/4\). Since the denominators of \(v\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) are at most \(2^L\), their positive difference would satisfy

\( \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}4-v\ge\frac1{4\cdot2^{2L}}. \)

But

\( v\ge\frac a4> \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}4-\frac1{4\cdot2^{2L}}, \)

a contradiction. Hence every positively allocated bundle has value at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/4\).

Finally, choose a basic feasible solution of the recovered allocation LP. After introducing supply slack variables, it has \(\tau+m\) equality rows, so it has at most \(\tau+m\) positive allocation variables. Its rational coordinates have polynomial encoding length.

We have therefore proved the stated deterministic polynomial-time \(4\)-approximation with exact masses and supplies.

6. The two requested guarantees

Fix \(0<\epsilon\le1\).

When \(D\ge4^{1/\epsilon}\), the polynomial-time \(4\)-approximation is a \(D^\epsilon\)-approximation.

When \(D<4^{1/\epsilon}\), query every bundle for every type and solve the configuration LP exactly, searching over the finitely many returned valuation levels. This takes

\( 2^{O(4^{1/\epsilon})}\operatorname{poly}(L) \)

time, a constant factor depending only on the fixed \(\epsilon\). Thus the combined algorithm has running time \(O_\epsilon(D^{C/\epsilon}\operatorname{poly}(L))\) for an absolute constant \(C\). This establishes the requested fixed-\(\epsilon\) guarantee for all instances. Values \(\epsilon>1\) follow from the case \(\epsilon=1\).

For the second guarantee, \(D\ge4\), so

\( 4\le(\log_2 D)^2. \)

The same polynomial-time algorithm is consequently a \((\log D)^{O(1)}\)-approximation, within the requested \(D^{O(\log D/\log\log D)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) running time.

The fixed-\(\epsilon\) construction above does not assert a running-time bound with a multiplicative constant uniform in a varying \(\epsilon\). The stronger polynomial-time constant approximation itself is uniform and requires no population expansion.
