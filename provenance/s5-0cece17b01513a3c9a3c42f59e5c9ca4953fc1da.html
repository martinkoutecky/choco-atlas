The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite item classes \(\mathcal G=[m]\), finite complete valuation types \(\Theta\), rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and value-oracle access to monotone submodular valuations \(v_\theta:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb R_+\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{\theta,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S\ni j}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j\), with \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}_\infty/380\), where \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S))\), in time polynomial in the compressed input and oracle complexity.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal G=[m]\), with \(m\ge1\). A society is given by its support: a list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\ge1\) valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), where \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a nonnegative monotone submodular valuation \(v_\theta:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), accessible only through value queries; no empty-set normalization is imposed. Supplies \(b_j\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) are per capita. Let \(L\) include the supplied numerical encoding length and a bound on the bit length of each oracle answer.
An allocation specifies nonnegative rational masses \(x_{\theta,S}\) by listing its nonzero entries, with \(\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{\theta,S}=\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_{\theta,S:j\in S}x_{\theta,S}\le b_j\). A bundle contains at most one item of each class; unused supply is allowed.
Define \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)=\exp(\sum_{\theta,S:x_{\theta,S}>0}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S))\), using natural logarithms and assigning welfare zero whenever positive mass receives a zero-valued bundle. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\) be the maximum over feasible real mass allocations, with the same convention.

Does a randomized algorithm exist that, with probability at least \(2/3\), returns a feasible allocation satisfying \(\mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}_\infty/380\), using time and value queries polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this approximation impossible in that model?

For discrete agents and indivisible items, a polynomial-time randomized \(380\)-approximation is known ([Li–Vondrák](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10536)); a later deterministic algorithm achieves \(4+\varepsilon\) ([Garg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03883)).
A related log-configuration LP with one bundle per discrete agent and unit item capacities admits polynomial-time additive error \(\log(e/(e-1)+\varepsilon)\) ([Feng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02942)). No result for exactly the specified support-encoded mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Supplies were given without a sign domain; they are nonnegative binary rationals, and unused supply remains allowed as in the stated inequalities.
  • Real-valued oracle answers lacked a bit-complexity convention; answers are nonnegative binary rationals with a supplied bit-length bound incorporated into L.
  • The exponential allocation representation was unspecified; allocations are represented by their nonzero bundle-mass entries.
  • Zero-valued bundles made the logarithmic objective undefined; any positive mass assigned such a bundle gives welfare zero, while zero-mass terms are omitted.
  • The optimum's domain was unspecified; it ranges over feasible real mass allocations, while algorithmic output uses rational masses.
  • Randomization was unspecified; randomized algorithms are allowed with success probability at least two thirds, consistent with the anchor's randomized approximation.

Result

Yes. A deterministic polynomial-time algorithm achieves a factor of \(16\), and therefore satisfies the requested factor of \(380\) with probability \(1\).

The proof works directly with the support-encoded masses. Its main ingredients are submodular knapsack approximation and an explicit cutting-plane argument that recovers an exactly feasible rational allocation.

1. Positive welfare and the configuration LP

Replace each supply \(b_j\) by \(\min\{b_j,1\}\). This preserves the feasible allocations because the total population mass is \(1\), and a bundle contains each item at most once.

For any monotone submodular valuation \(v\) with \(v(\varnothing)=0\), diminishing returns gives

\(\displaystyle v(S)\le \sum_{j\in S}v(\{j\}).\)

Consequently, every positive-valued bundle contains a positive-valued singleton.

This yields a polynomial-time test for whether positive welfare is possible. Types with \(v_\theta(\varnothing)>0\) can receive the empty bundle. For the remaining types, solve the rational transportation feasibility problem

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j:v_\theta(\{j\})>0}y_{\theta j}=\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_\theta y_{\theta j}\le b_j,\qquad y_{\theta j}\ge0.\)

Its feasibility is equivalent to positive-welfare feasibility:

  • A feasible transportation solution assigns only positive-valued singletons, while the other types receive positive-valued empty bundles.
  • Conversely, replace each positive bundle of a type with zero empty-set value by a positive singleton contained in it. For types with positive empty-set value, replace their bundles by the empty bundle. Supply consumption can only decrease.

The transportation problem is a polynomial-size rational LP. If it is infeasible, \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty=0\), and assigning everyone the empty bundle satisfies the requested guarantee.

Henceforth assume it is feasible. Retain the positive configurations used by a rational feasible solution as a polynomial-size set of seed columns.

Let

\(\displaystyle \mathcal P_\theta=\{S\subseteq\mathcal G:v_\theta(S)>0\}.\)

Positive-welfare optimization is exactly the finite configuration LP

\(\displaystyle \max \sum_\theta\sum_{S\in\mathcal P_\theta}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S)\)

subject to the mass and supply constraints in the question. Its feasible region is nonempty and compact. Thus its optimum is attained and equals \(\log\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\).

2. Rational objective coefficients

Choose an integer \(B\), polynomially bounded by \(L\), such that every positive oracle answer lies in \([2^{-B},2^B]\). The answer-bit-length assumption provides such a bound.

Fix \(\delta=1/16\). For every queried positive configuration, compute a deterministic dyadic coefficient \(c_{\theta,S}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |c_{\theta,S}-\log v_\theta(S)|\le\delta.\)

These coefficients have polynomial encoding length and can be computed in polynomial time. For completeness, range reduction writes a positive rational answer as \(2^kz\), with \(z\in[1,2]\); then the convergent series

\(\displaystyle \log z=2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1},\qquad u=\frac{z-1}{z+1}\in[0,1/3],\)

and the same series for \(\log2\), give the required precision using polynomially many rational operations. Use a fixed rounding rule so that repeated queries produce the same coefficient.

Set \(H=B+1\). All coefficients satisfy \(|c_{\theta,S}|\le H\).

Let \(W\) be the full configuration LP optimum with objective coefficients \(c_{\theta,S}\). Since every feasible allocation has total mass \(1\),

\(\displaystyle -H\le W\le H,\qquad |W-\log\mathrm{OPT}_\infty|\le\delta.\)

The dual of this rational-objective LP is

\(\displaystyle \min D(a,p):=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta a_\theta+\sum_j b_jp_j,\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle a_\theta+\sum_{j\in S}p_j\ge c_{\theta,S}\quad(S\in\mathcal P_\theta),\qquad p_j\ge0.\)

The variables \(a_\theta\) are unrestricted in sign. Increasing every \(a_\theta\) by \(C\) increases the objective by exactly \(C\), because \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\).

3. An additive-error demand oracle

We first construct a covering subroutine.

Covering claim. Given rational nonnegative item costs \(p_j\) and a feasible positive target \(V\), one can find, in polynomial time, a set \(T\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle v(T)\ge V/2,\qquad p(T)\le\min_{S:v(S)\ge V}p(S),\)

where \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\).

We use the deterministic monotone submodular knapsack theorem: with nonnegative integer costs and an integer budget, it returns a feasible set of value at least \((1-1/e)\) times the optimum, using polynomially many value queries. See [Sviridenko’s theorem](https://thibaut.horel.org/submodularity/papers/sviridenko2004.pdf).

Here are the hypothesis and encoding details. Scale rational costs to integers using the product of their denominators; its bit length is polynomial in the input cost encoding. Include all zero-cost items \(Z\). On the remaining items, apply the theorem to

\(\displaystyle f(T)=v(Z\cup T)-v(Z).\)

This function is normalized, nonnegative, monotone, and submodular. Adding back the baseline \(v(Z)\) shows that the resulting total value is at least \((1-1/e)\) times the optimum total value, and hence at least half of it.

Let \(C^*\) be the minimum integer cost of a set with value at least \(V\). At every integer budget \(q\ge C^*\), the knapsack algorithm therefore returns value at least \(V/2\).

Binary-search integer budgets between \(-1\) and the total integer item cost. Keep a successful upper endpoint and a lower endpoint strictly below \(C^*\):

  • A failed test implies that its budget is below \(C^*\).
  • A successful test supplies a witnessed upper endpoint.

At termination, the endpoints differ by one. The successful upper endpoint is therefore at most \(C^*\). This proves the covering claim. Monotonicity of the algorithm’s actual output is unnecessary; the stated implication for all budgets at least \(C^*\) suffices.

Now fix a type and prices \(p\). Apply the covering subroutine to all feasible targets

\(\displaystyle V=2^k,\qquad -B\le k\le B.\)

For every positive bundle \(S\), one of these targets satisfies \(V\le v(S)<2V\). Its returned bundle \(T\) obeys

\(\displaystyle p(T)\le p(S),\qquad v(T)\ge v(S)/4.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle c_T-p(T)\ge c_S-p(S)-\log4-2\delta>c_S-p(S)-2.\)

Thus polynomially many queries produce candidates whose best value of \(c_T-p(T)\) is within additive error \(2\) of the true maximum.

At a proposed dual point \((a,p)\), this gives the following procedure:

  • If a candidate violates \(a_\theta+p(T)\ge c_{\theta,T}\), return that exact rational inequality.
  • If no candidate violates its inequality, then every configuration satisfies

\(\displaystyle a_\theta+p(S)\ge c_{\theta,S}-2.\)

In the second case, \((a+2\mathbf1,p)\) is truly dual feasible. If also \(D(a,p)\le t\), weak duality gives

\(\displaystyle W\le t+2.\)

4. Bounds for every restricted dual

Let \(\mathcal C\) be any finite column set containing the seed columns. Its restricted primal is feasible and bounded. Write its optimum as \(W_{\mathcal C}\).

Put \(d=m+\tau\). The restricted dual has an optimal vertex. Indeed, strong LP duality gives an attained finite optimum, and its feasible polyhedron contains no line: a line direction must have zero price coordinates because \(p\ge0\), and then zero \(a_\theta\) coordinates because there is at least one column for every type. A pointed polyhedron with an attained linear optimum has an optimal vertex.

At a vertex, \(d\) independent tight inequalities determine the coordinates. Their coefficient matrix has entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\); their right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(H\). Cramer’s rule therefore bounds every coordinate by

\(\displaystyle R_0=d!\,H.\)

The denominator determinant is a nonzero integer, and each numerator determinant has at most \(d!\) terms, each of absolute value at most \(H\). This bound holds uniformly over all restricted column sets.

Set

\(\displaystyle R=R_0+2,\qquad h=\frac14,\qquad s=\frac{h}{4(m+1)},\qquad r=\frac{s}{2d}.\)

We need the following quantitative fact.

Ball claim. If \(W_{\mathcal C}\le t-h\), then the restricted dual region satisfying \(D(a,p)\le t\), \(p\ge0\), and \((a,p)\in[-R,R]^d\) contains a Euclidean ball of radius \(r\).

To prove it, choose an optimal vertex \(z^*=(a^*,p^*)\) with coordinates bounded by \(R_0\), and define \(\bar z=z^*+s\mathbf1\).

Every configuration inequality now has slack at least \(s\), and every price is at least \(s\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle D(\bar z)\le W_{\mathcal C}+s\Bigl(1+\sum_jb_j\Bigr)\le t-h+\frac h4=t-\frac{3h}{4}.\)

Every configuration normal and the objective normal has Euclidean norm at most \(d\): their coordinates have absolute value at most \(1\). A perturbation of Euclidean norm at most \(r\) therefore changes each relevant linear expression by at most

\(\displaystyle dr=s/2.\)

It preserves the configuration constraints, price nonnegativity, and objective cutoff. Finally, \(s+r<1\), so the ball stays inside \([-R,R]^d\). This proves the claim.

5. Explicit recovery by cutting planes

Use the rational central-cut ellipsoid machinery with the following standard geometric guarantee: starting from a rational enclosing ball, adaptive rational cuts excluding the current center can be processed in polynomial time; after polynomially many cuts, the algorithm produces an ellipsoid of any prescribed positive rational volume bound, containing the initial ball intersected with all supplied halfspaces. The iteration and bit bounds are polynomial in dimension, cut encoding length, the logarithm of the initial radius, and the logarithm of the inverse volume bound. This is the containment and rational-rounding machinery in [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Section 3.2](https://www.mpi-inf.mpg.de/fileadmin/inf/d1/ellipsoid-lovasz.pdf).

For each cutoff on the grid

\(\displaystyle t=-H-3,\,-H-3+h,\,\ldots,\,H,\)

run that machinery from a ball of radius \(dR\). At each center:

  • Check the box constraints, \(p\ge0\), and \(D(a,p)\le t\), returning an exactly violated inequality if necessary.
  • Otherwise invoke the demand procedure from Section 3 for every type.
  • Return any configuration inequality it finds violated; if none is found, stop this run as accepted.

Record every configuration inequality used as a cut. If a run is not accepted, continue until its enclosing ellipsoid has volume at most

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac12(r/d)^d.\)

This is strictly below the volume of a radius-\(r\) ball: such a ball contains a cube of side \(2r/d\).

All these runs take polynomial time. In particular, there are polynomially many cutoffs because \(H\) is polynomial in \(L\); the radii and volume target have polynomial bit lengths; and every supplied cut has polynomial encoding length.

Let \(\mathcal C\) consist of the seed columns and all recorded configuration columns. Solve its polynomial-size rational primal LP exactly.

We now prove its objective guarantee. Choose the largest grid cutoff strictly below \(W-2\). It exists and satisfies

\(\displaystyle W-2-h\le t<W-2.\)

This run cannot be accepted: acceptance would imply \(W\le t+2<W\). It therefore produces an ellipsoid of volume at most \(\eta\).

The intersection of the box, price nonnegativity, objective cutoff, and all restricted dual constraints for \(\mathcal C\) lies in that final ellipsoid. Every cut used during the run belongs to these constraints; adding further recorded constraints only shrinks the intersection.

If \(W_{\mathcal C}\le t-h\), the ball claim would put a radius-\(r\) ball inside this intersection, contradicting the final volume bound. Hence

\(\displaystyle W_{\mathcal C}>t-h\ge W-2-2h.\)

This proves the required restricted-primal bound. In particular, the argument handles lower-dimensional cutoff regions: its certificate excludes the explicitly constructed ball under the stronger hypothesis \(W_{\mathcal C}\le t-h\).

6. Welfare, exact feasibility, and output size

Let \(x\) be an optimal solution of the restricted rational primal LP. Its feasibility is exact, and every listed configuration has positive value. The objective approximation bounds give

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\theta,S}x_{\theta,S}\log v_\theta(S)\ge W_{\mathcal C}-\delta>\log\mathrm{OPT}_\infty-2-2h-2\delta.\)

With \(h=1/4\) and \(\delta=1/16\), the loss is \(21/8\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{NSW}_\infty(x)>\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\,e^{-21/8}>\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_\infty}{16}.\)

The last inequality follows from \(\log2>2/3\), which gives \(\log16>8/3>21/8\).

Choose an optimal basic feasible solution of the restricted LP. After adding supply slack variables, there are \(m+\tau\) equality rows, so at most \(m+\tau\) allocation entries are nonzero. Their rational encoding lengths are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), by the usual determinant bounds for rational LP basic solutions.

The resulting algorithm uses polynomial time and polynomially many value queries. It is deterministic, so its success probability is \(1\).
