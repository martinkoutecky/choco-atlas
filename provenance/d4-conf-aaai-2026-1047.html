The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(A\) and a support of \(\tau\) rankings \(t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute the set of alternatives selected by River under at least one descending tie-breaking order of the margin edges, where \(m_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) be a nonempty candidate set with \(m=|A|\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct strict linear rankings \(t\) of \(A\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Define \(m_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t\). The margin graph has edges \(E=\{(x,y):x\ne y,\ m_\mu(x,y)\ge0\}\), weighted by their margins; a zero-margin pair contributes both directed edges.
A descending tiebreaker is any total order of \(E\) with nonincreasing weights. River starts with an empty directed graph on \(A\) and processes this order, adding \((x,y)\) unless \(y\) already has an incoming edge or its addition creates a directed cycle. Its winner is the unique vertex with no incoming edge after processing all edges.
Let \(W_\mu\) be the set of River winners obtained under at least one descending tiebreaker.

Can \(W_\mu\) be computed in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is deciding whether a designated candidate belongs to \(W_\mu\) NP-hard?

For finite electorates, River with parallel-universe tiebreaking has a polynomial-time algorithm on the margin graph, with runtime polynomial in \(m\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14409v2)
No separate result for the binary-rational support formulation was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified rankings as strict linear orders, matching the discrete preference domain.
  • Required a nonempty candidate set; retained the single-candidate case.
  • Specified that margin edges have nonnegative weight, including both directions of zero-margin pairs, matching the discrete rule.
  • Excluded self-loops explicitly; they are rejected by River's cycle condition in the literal edge-set definition.
  • Defined L as the total input bit length and made the NP-hardness alternative precise through designated-candidate membership.

Result

The full set \(W_\mu\) is computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). In particular, designated-candidate membership belongs to \(\mathsf{P}\).

The proof reduces the given society to a polynomial-size finite electorate with exactly the same possible River outcomes, then applies the cited finite-electorate theorem.

For \(m=1\), the sole candidate wins. Henceforth assume \(m\geq 2\).

1. Compute exact margins in polynomial time

Write each encoded mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(a_t,b_t\) are positive integers, and define
\(Q=\prod_t b_t\) and \(c_t=a_t(Q/b_t)\).

The binary length of \(Q\) is at most the sum of the binary lengths of the denominators, hence is \(O(L)\). Each \(c_t\) is an integer, and the normalization assumption gives
\(\sum_t c_t=Q\).

For distinct candidates, compute the scaled margin
\(S(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}c_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}c_t\).
Then \(S(x,y)=Qm_\mu(x,y)\) and \(\lvert S(x,y)\rvert\leq Q\).

Thus every scaled margin has \(O(L)\) bits. By first recording candidate positions in each ranking, all scaled margins can be computed using \(O(\tau m^2)\) additions and subtractions of integers of polynomial bit length. Their signs, equalities, and ordering are therefore computable in polynomial time.

2. River depends only on the edge set and weight ordering

Lemma. Suppose two weighted directed graphs have the same vertex and edge sets, and their weights \(w,w'\) satisfy
\(w(e)\leq w(f)\Longleftrightarrow w'(e)\leq w'(f)\)
for every pair of edges. Then their sets of possible River winners coincide.

Proof. The displayed equivalence preserves both strict comparisons and equalities. Consequently, the descending total edge orders permitted by the two weight functions are exactly the same.

Fix any such order. Induct over its edges. Initially, both River executions have the same empty graph. If their current graphs coincide, the next edge is rejected in one execution precisely when it is rejected in the other: both rejection conditions depend only on the current graph and that edge. Their graphs therefore coincide after every step, and their winners coincide.

Taking the union over the common set of descending orders proves the lemma. \(\square\)

Let \(\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_s\) be the distinct positive values among the scaled margins. There are at most \(p=\binom m2\) such values.

Define an antisymmetric integer margin function \(C\) as follows:

  • If \(S(x,y)=\lambda_j>0\), set \(C(x,y)=2j\).
  • If \(S(x,y)<0\), set \(C(x,y)=-C(y,x)\).
  • If \(S(x,y)=0\), set \(C(x,y)=0\).

The nonnegative-margin edge set is unchanged. In particular, every zero-margin pair still contributes both directed edges. On this edge set, the weights preserve every comparison and equality. By the lemma, replacing \(m_\mu\) by \(C\) preserves \(W_\mu\).

Moreover, every entry of \(C\) has absolute value at most \(2p\), and hence has \(O(\log m)\) bits.

3. Realize the compressed margins by a small finite electorate

We now prove that \(C\) is the margin function of an explicit finite electorate of polynomial size.

Pair gadget. Fix distinct candidates \(x,y\), and enumerate the others as \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\). Introduce two voters with rankings
\(x\succ y\succ z_1\succ\cdots\succ z_k\)
and
\(z_k\succ\cdots\succ z_1\succ x\succ y\).

Both voters rank \(x\) above \(y\), so their combined margin for \((x,y)\) is \(2\). Every other pair is ordered oppositely by the two voters:

  • For two candidates among the \(z_i\), their orders are reversed.
  • For either \(x\) or \(y\) against a \(z_i\), the former is above \(z_i\) in the first ranking and below it in the second.

Thus this gadget contributes \(2\) to the margin of \(x\) over \(y\), contributes \(-2\) in the reverse direction, and contributes zero to every other pair.

For every unordered pair with positive compressed margin \(C(x,y)=2j\), include \(j\) copies of its pair gadget. Since ordinary electorate margins add over voters, the resulting margins equal \(C\) on every pair.

Finally, add any ranking and its reverse. These two voters contribute zero to all margins and ensure that the electorate is nonempty even when \(s=0\).

Call the resulting profile \(\mathcal P\). Its number of voters satisfies
\(N=2+2\sum_{C(x,y)>0}C(x,y)/2\leq 2+2ps\leq 2+2p^2\).

Therefore \(N=O(m^4)\). Writing all rankings explicitly requires \(O(m^5\log m)\) bits under a standard candidate encoding. This profile can be constructed in polynomial time, and its margins are exactly \(C\).

4. Apply the finite-electorate theorem

The cited result establishes that the FUN algorithm computes all River PUT winners from a finite profile’s margin graph in polynomial time in the number of candidates: correctness is Theorem 4.1, and the runtime bound is Theorem 3.1. Its definitions use nonnegative-margin edges, descending total edge orders, and the same branching and cycle rejection conditions as this task. [Döring, Malanowski, and Neubert, *Cost-Free Neutrality for the River Method*, Sections 2–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14409v2).

Its hypotheses hold for \(\mathcal P\): it is a nonempty finite electorate of strict linear rankings, and its ordinary integer margin graph is exactly the graph defined by \(C\), including both directions of every zero-margin pair. Its edge weights also have \(O(\log m)\) bits, so weight comparisons have polynomial bit cost.

Apply that algorithm to this graph. By Section 2, its returned winner set is exactly \(W_\mu\).

All preprocessing takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), and the final finite-electorate computation takes polynomial time. Thus the entire winner set is polynomial-time computable. Designated-candidate membership consequently lies in \(\mathsf{P}\), and cannot be NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions unless \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).
