The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(W\) of positive integer voting weights, rational masses \((\mu_w)_{w\in W}\) summing to one, and an offset \(z\), compute the limiting per-member swing coefficients \(\beta_w=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt{N}\,Bz_{w,N}\) and type shares \(P_w=\mu_w\beta_w/\sum_{v\in W}\mu_v\beta_v\). Here \(N\mu_w\) is integral, and the quotas \(q_N\) lie on the lattice generated by \(W\) and satisfy \(q_N=N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N+O(1)\), where \(\bar w=\sum_w\mu_w w\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(W\subset\mathbb Z_{>0}\) of distinct weight types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_w\), where \(\sum_{w\in W}\mu_w=1\). The support size is \(\tau=|W|\), not the number of possible weights.
The offset \(z\in\mathbb Q\) and weights are binary encoded; \(L\) is their total encoding length together with the masses. There is no candidate-count parameter \(m\) in this model.
Put \(\bar w=\sum_w\mu_w w\) and \(d=\gcd(W)\). Along positive integers \(N\) for which every \(N\mu_w\) is integral, form a game with exactly \(N\mu_w\) players of weight \(w\).
Allow any quota sequence \(q_N\in d\mathbb Z\) satisfying \(q_N=N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N+O(1)\); for sufficiently large \(N\), require \(\max W<q_N\le N\bar w\). A coalition wins precisely when its total weight is at least \(q_N\).
For a player \(p\) of weight \(w\), define \(Bz_{w,N}=2^{-N}|\{S\subseteq A_N:p\in S,\ w(S\setminus\{p\})<q_N\le w(S)\}|\), where \(A_N\) is the player set and \(w(S)\) is its total weight.
The quantities to compute are \(\beta_w=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt N\,Bz_{w,N}\) and \(P_w=\mu_w\beta_w/\sum_v\mu_v\beta_v\), including their existence and independence of the allowed quota sequence.
Computing these real quantities means computing each to absolute error at most \(2^{-k}\), for an input precision \(k\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\); running times are measured in \(\tau,L,k\).

Is computing all \(\beta_w\) and \(P_w\) polynomial-time in \(\tau,L,k\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite games, an algorithm computes all Banzhaf indices in \(O(n+q\log q)\) arithmetic operations, a pseudo-polynomial bound under unit-cost arithmetic on \(n\)-bit integers ([Kakimura–Terai](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14995)).
At exactly half-total quota, convergence of Banzhaf power ratios to weight ratios and an absolute-power asymptotic are known for finitely many positive integer weights whose infinitely recurring values have gcd one ([Lindner–Machover](https://www.lse.ac.uk/cpnss/assets/documents/voting-power-and-procedures/publications/2004/lindner.pdf)); this does not establish the stated computational complexity for variable offset and binary-encoded inputs.

The quantities exist, are independent of the allowed quota sequence, and can all be approximated in time polynomial in \(\tau,L,k\).

Define the second moment
\( \displaystyle A=\sum_{v\in W}\mu_v v^2. \)

The formulas are
\( \displaystyle \boxed{\beta_w=\frac{w}{\sqrt{2\pi A}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2z^2}{A}\right),\qquad P_w=\frac{\mu_w w}{\bar w}.} \)

The proof below establishes both the limits and the bit complexity of computing them.

1. Expressing pivotality as a probability

Fix a type \(w\), and let \(X_{w,N}\) be the total weight of a random subset of the players other than a designated player of weight \(w\). Each other player is included independently with probability \(1/2\).

A uniformly random coalition of all players contains the designated player with probability \(1/2\), independently of its other members. Therefore the definition in the question gives
\( \displaystyle Bz_{w,N}=\frac12\Pr\!\left(q_N-w\le X_{w,N}<q_N\right). \)

In particular, the normalization by \(2^{-N}\) produces a factor \(1/2\) relative to the probability that the other players form a swing coalition.

All values of \(X_{w,N}\) belong to \(d\mathbb Z\). Since \(q_N\in d\mathbb Z\) and \(d\mid w\), the interval above contains exactly the lattice points
\( \displaystyle q_N-rd,\qquad r=1,\ldots,w/d. \)

We will evaluate the probability at each of these points.

2. A local limit lemma, with proof

Lemma. Let \(Y\) be a finitely supported integer-valued random variable with mean \(b\), variance \(s^2>0\), and
\( \displaystyle \gcd\{y-y':y,y'\in\operatorname{supp}(Y)\}=1. \)

Let \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be independent copies of \(Y\), and let \(R\) be an independent, finitely supported integer-valued random variable with mean \(c\). Set
\( \displaystyle T_j=Y_1+\cdots+Y_j+R. \)

If integers \(\ell_j\) satisfy
\( \displaystyle \frac{\ell_j-jb-c}{\sqrt j}\longrightarrow x, \)
then
\( \displaystyle \sqrt j\,\Pr(T_j=\ell_j)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2s^2}\right). \)

Proof. Define the centered characteristic functions
\( \displaystyle \phi(t)=\mathbb E e^{it(Y-b)},\qquad h(t)=\mathbb E e^{it(R-c)}. \)

Finite support permits Taylor expansion at zero:
\( \displaystyle \phi(t)=1-\frac{s^2t^2}{2}+O(|t|^3). \)

Consequently, for some \(\delta>0\) and \(a>0\),
\( \displaystyle |\phi(t)|\le e^{-at^2}\qquad(|t|\le\delta). \)

Indeed, the expansion implies
\( \displaystyle |\phi(t)|^2=1-s^2t^2+O(|t|^3), \)
from which this bound follows after decreasing \(\delta\).

We also have \(|\phi(t)|<1\) whenever \(0<|t|\le\pi\). To see this, equality in the triangle inequality for \(\mathbb E e^{itY}\) would require all \(e^{ity}\), for supported values \(y\), to agree. Thus
\( \displaystyle t(y-y')\in2\pi\mathbb Z \)
for every supported pair. The gcd hypothesis and Bézout’s identity then imply \(t\in2\pi\mathbb Z\), which is impossible in the indicated range. Compactness therefore gives some \(\rho<1\) such that
\( \displaystyle |\phi(t)|\le\rho\qquad(\delta\le|t|\le\pi). \)

Put \(x_j=(\ell_j-jb-c)/\sqrt j\). Fourier inversion gives
\( \displaystyle \sqrt j\,\Pr(T_j=\ell_j)=\frac{\sqrt j}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\phi(t)^j h(t)e^{-it\sqrt j\,x_j}\,dt. \)

The portion with \(|t|\ge\delta\) has absolute value at most \(\sqrt j\,\rho^j\), which tends to zero. In the remaining portion substitute \(u=t\sqrt j\). For each fixed \(u\),
\( \displaystyle \phi(u/\sqrt j)^j\longrightarrow e^{-s^2u^2/2},\qquad h(u/\sqrt j)\longrightarrow1. \)

The absolute value of the resulting integrand is bounded by \(e^{-au^2}\), because \(|h|\le1\). Extending that integrand by zero outside \(|u|\le\delta\sqrt j\), dominated convergence yields
\( \displaystyle \lim_{j\to\infty}\sqrt j\,\Pr(T_j=\ell_j)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-s^2u^2/2}e^{-iux}\,du. \)

For completeness, the Gaussian integral on the right equals
\( \displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{s}e^{-x^2/(2s^2)}. \)
Its value at \(x=0\) follows from the Gaussian integral; differentiating under the integral and integrating by parts shows that its derivative is \(-x/s^2\) times itself. This proves the lemma.

3. Applying the lemma to the society

Write the masses as reduced fractions, and let \(D\) be the least common multiple of their denominators. Then the admissible population sizes are exactly
\( \displaystyle N=Dm,\qquad m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}. \)

Put \(n_v=D\mu_v\). Each \(n_v\) is a positive integer, and \(\sum_v n_v=D\).

Partition the population into \(m\) blocks, each containing \(n_v\) players of type \(v\). Let \(Y\) be the weight selected from one random block, divided by \(d\):
\( \displaystyle Y=\sum_{v\in W}\sum_{a=1}^{n_v}\frac vd\,B_{v,a}, \)
where all \(B_{v,a}\) are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter \(1/2\).

Its mean and variance are
\( \displaystyle b=\frac{D\bar w}{2d},\qquad s^2=\frac{DA}{4d^2}>0. \)

Its support contains zero and every \(v/d\): select no player, or select just one player of type \(v\). Since
\( \displaystyle \gcd\{v/d:v\in W\}=1, \)
the gcd of all support differences is one. Thus the lemma’s lattice hypothesis holds.

Place the designated player of weight \(w\) in one block. Let \(R_w\) be the selected weight from the remainder of that block, divided by \(d\). It is independent of the other \(m-1\) blocks and has mean
\( \displaystyle c_w=\frac{D\bar w-w}{2d}. \)

Consequently,
\( \displaystyle \frac{X_{w,N}}d\ \stackrel{\mathrm{law}}=\ Y_1+\cdots+Y_{m-1}+R_w. \)

This remains valid if \(D=1\), when the remainder block is empty and \(R_w=0\).

Fix \(r\in\{1,\ldots,w/d\}\), and set
\( \displaystyle j=m-1,\qquad \ell_j=\frac{q_N-rd}{d}. \)

The mean of the sum on the right is
\( \displaystyle jb+c_w=\frac{N\bar w-w}{2d}. \)

Using the quota assumption,
\( \displaystyle \ell_j-jb-c_w=\frac{z\sqrt N}{d}+O(1). \)

Here \(w,r,d\) are fixed while \(N\) tends to infinity. Since \(N/j\to D\),
\( \displaystyle \frac{\ell_j-jb-c_w}{\sqrt j}\longrightarrow\frac{z\sqrt D}{d}. \)

The lemma therefore gives
\( \displaystyle \lim_{N\to\infty}\sqrt N\,\Pr(X_{w,N}=q_N-rd)=\frac{2d}{\sqrt{2\pi A}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2z^2}{A}\right). \)

There are exactly \(w/d\) terms in the pivotality interval. Summing these limits and multiplying by \(1/2\) proves
\( \displaystyle \beta_w=\frac12\frac wd\frac{2d}{\sqrt{2\pi A}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2z^2}{A}\right)=\frac{w}{\sqrt{2\pi A}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2z^2}{A}\right). \)

Only the normalized displacement \(z\) survives in this argument. Every bounded perturbation of the quota contributes a term tending to zero after division by \(\sqrt N\). This proves independence from the allowed quota sequence.

The class of allowed sequences is nonempty: rounding \(N\bar w/2+z\sqrt N\) to a nearest multiple of \(d\) incurs a bounded error. Since \(\bar w>0\), the required quota inequalities hold for all sufficiently large admissible \(N\).

4. Normalized powers

Define
\( \displaystyle C=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi A}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2z^2}{A}\right)>0. \)

Then \(\beta_w=Cw\), so
\( \displaystyle \sum_{v\in W}\mu_v\beta_v=C\bar w>0. \)

Hence
\( \displaystyle P_w=\frac{\mu_w Cw}{C\bar w}=\frac{\mu_w w}{\bar w}. \)

Thus every \(P_w\) is an exactly computable rational number. In particular, these quantities do not require numerical division by the possibly extremely small common factor \(C\).

5. Polynomial bit complexity

We now give an approximation algorithm with explicit error bounds. All rational arithmetic below uses the ordinary binary encodings in the question.

5.1 Exact preliminary calculations

Compute
\( \displaystyle \bar w=\sum_v\mu_vv,\qquad A=\sum_v\mu_vv^2,\qquad x=\frac{2z^2}{A}. \)

These rational numbers have polynomial encoding length and can be computed in polynomial time. For example, using the product of the input denominators as a common denominator gives a denominator whose bit length is at most the sum of their bit lengths.

Because the weights are positive integers,
\( \displaystyle A\ge1,\qquad 1\le w<2^L. \)

Write
\( \displaystyle c_w=\frac{w}{\sqrt{2\pi A}},\qquad \beta_w=c_we^{-x}. \)
Since \(\pi>2\), we have \(0<c_w<2^L\).

The exact rational expression for \(P_w\) can immediately be rounded to the requested precision by integer division.

5.2 A cutoff for large offsets

Let
\( \displaystyle T=k+L+2. \)

If the exact rational comparison gives \(x\ge T\), output zero for every \(\beta_w\). This is valid because \(e>2\), so
\( \displaystyle 0<\beta_w<2^Le^{-T}<2^{L-T}=2^{-k-2}. \)

This comparison uses the encoding length of \(x\), rather than time proportional to its numerical magnitude.

It remains to handle \(0\le x<T\).

5.3 Approximating the exponential

Set \(p=k+L+3\). Choose an even integer \(n\) satisfying
\( \displaystyle n\ge8T^2,\qquad n\ge p. \)

Compute the rational Taylor sum
\( \displaystyle \widehat E=\sum_{a=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-x)^a}{a!}. \)

Taylor’s theorem, applied on the nonnegative interval from zero to \(x\), gives
\( \displaystyle |\widehat E-e^{-x}|\le\frac{x^n}{n!}. \)

The last \(n/2\) factors of \(n!\) are each at least \(n/2\). Therefore
\( \displaystyle \frac{x^n}{n!}\le\frac{T^n}{(n/2)^{n/2}}=\left(\frac{2T^2}{n}\right)^{n/2}\le2^{-n}\le2^{-p}. \)

The number of terms is polynomial in \(L+k\). Their exact rational computation also has polynomial bit complexity. Explicitly, if \(x=a/b\), then \(b^{n-1}(n-1)!\) is a common denominator for all terms and has polynomial bit length. Thus cancellation in this sum causes no precision problem.

5.4 Approximating the prefactors

We first explain why \(\pi\) can be approximated with polynomial bit complexity. Use
\( \displaystyle \pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239). \)

To verify this identity, put \(\theta=\arctan(1/5)\) and \(\eta=\arctan(1/239)\). The tangent addition formulas give
\( \displaystyle \tan(2\theta)=5/12,\qquad \tan(4\theta)=120/119,\qquad \tan(4\theta-\eta)=1. \)
Moreover, \(0<4\theta-\eta<\pi/2\): positivity follows from \(\theta>\eta\), and the upper bound follows from \(\tan(2\theta)<1\). Hence \(4\theta-\eta=\pi/4\).

For either required argument, compute
\( \displaystyle \arctan(t)=\sum_{a=0}^{M-1}\frac{(-1)^at^{2a+1}}{2a+1}+\varepsilon_M,\qquad |\varepsilon_M|\le\frac{t^{2M+1}}{2M+1}. \)

This remainder bound follows by integrating the finite geometric-series identity for \(1/(1+t^2)\). Taking \(M\) proportional to the requested precision gives the corresponding approximation to \(\pi\), using polynomially many bits and rational operations.

In particular, compute a rational \(\widehat\pi\) satisfying
\( \displaystyle |\widehat\pi-\pi|\le2^{-(k+L+5)}. \)
This ensures \(\widehat\pi>1\).

For \(t\ge1\), define \(f_w(t)=w/\sqrt{2At}\). Its derivative satisfies
\( \displaystyle |f'_w(t)|=\frac{w}{2\sqrt{2A}\,t^{3/2}}\le2^L. \)

The mean value theorem gives
\( \displaystyle |f_w(\widehat\pi)-c_w|\le2^{-(k+5)}. \)

Approximate \(f_w(\widehat\pi)\) by bisection on \([0,2^L]\). A nonnegative rational trial point \(y\) lies below the target exactly when
\( \displaystyle 2A\widehat\pi\,y^2\le w^2, \)
which is an exact rational comparison.

After \(L+k+5\) bisections, the interval width is \(2^{-(k+5)}\). Choosing a point in this interval yields \(\widehat c_w\) satisfying
\( \displaystyle |\widehat c_w-c_w|\le2^{-(k+4)}. \)

Every bisection uses numbers of polynomial bit length.

5.5 Combining the errors

We have \(|\widehat E|\le2\). Therefore
\( \displaystyle |\widehat c_w\widehat E-\beta_w|\le|\widehat c_w-c_w|\,|\widehat E|+c_w|\widehat E-e^{-x}|. \)

The established bounds imply
\( \displaystyle |\widehat c_w\widehat E-\beta_w|\le2\cdot2^{-(k+4)}+2^L2^{-(k+L+3)}=2^{-(k+2)}. \)

Round this product to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-(k+1)}\). The additional error is at most \(2^{-(k+2)}\), so the total error is at most \(2^{-(k+1)}<2^{-k}\). If desired, replacing a negative rounded result by zero cannot increase its error.

There are \(\tau\) prefactors and outputs. All calculations use polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial bit length. The total running time is therefore polynomial in \(\tau,L,k\).

The block construction in the limit proof is only a mathematical device: the algorithm never constructs those blocks, expands the population, or enumerates the \(w/d\) pivotal lattice points.

6. Relation to the cited finite-game results

The [Kakimura–Terai algorithm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14995) computes finite-game indices in \(O(n+q\log q)\) time and is described as pseudo-polynomial. The algorithm proved here instead computes the limiting formulas directly from the encoded support.
