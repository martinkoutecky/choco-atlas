The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite item set \(M\) and a monotone valuation \(v:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) represented by the paper’s value-and-demand oracles, consider a unit-mass population of buyers all having type \(v\), with one unit of each item available per unit of population. Choose item prices \(p_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\cup\{\infty\}\) and bundle masses \(x_S\ge 0\), for \(S\subseteq M\), maximizing \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_S p(S)\), where \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\), subject to \(\sum_S x_S=1\), \(\sum_{S\ni j}x_S\le 1\) for every item \(j\), and \(x_S>0\) only when \(S\) is demanded at prices \(p\). Compute an outcome attaining the maximum revenue.

A society is given by its support: a list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here \(\tau=1\): buyers have total mass \(1\) and share a monotone valuation \(v:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), with \(v(\varnothing)=0\), for \(m=|M|\) items, each of supply \(1\).
Access to \(v\) consists of a value oracle returning \(v(S)\) and a demand oracle returning any member of \(D(p)=\arg\max_{S\subseteq M}\{v(S)-p(S)\}\), where \(p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_j\). Prices lie in \(\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\cup\{\infty\}\); bundles containing an infinitely priced item have utility \(-\infty\).
Choose prices and masses \(x_S\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_S=1\), \(\sum_{S\ni j}x_S\le1\) for every \(j\in M\), and \(x_S>0\Rightarrow S\in D(p)\). The seller may distribute mass among tied demanded bundles, including ties with the empty bundle; unsold supply imposes no price restriction.
The optimization problem is to compute an outcome maximizing \(\sum_{S:x_S>0}x_Sp(S)\). Let \(L\) bound the binary encoding lengths of the support data and valuation answers; measure computation and oracle calls in \(m,\tau,L\), accounting also for query encoding lengths.

Is this continuous revenue maximization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) with value and demand oracle access, or is it NP-hard even when both oracles have polynomial-time implementations?

For one discrete buyer with a normalized monotone combinatorial valuation, exact revenue maximization under market envy-freeness is NP-hard even with polynomial-time value and demand oracles ([Bilò et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.13704)). No result settling this exact continuous formulation was identified in the literature search.

Result

The problem is NP-hard even when \(\tau=1\) and both oracles have polynomial-time implementations. The key point is that, under the stated supplies and total buyer mass, distributing mass among tied bundles cannot improve optimal revenue.

1. Concentrating the mass preserves feasibility and improves revenue

Fix an allowed price vector \(p\). The empty bundle has utility zero, so every demanded bundle has finite payment and nonnegative utility.

The supply inequalities are redundant: for every item \(j\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S\ni j}x_S\le \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_S=1.\)

Thus the feasible distributions at these prices are exactly the probability distributions supported on \(D(p)\).

Since \(D(p)\) is a nonempty finite set, choose a bundle \(S^\star\in D(p)\) maximizing \(p(S^\star)\). Every feasible distribution satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{S:x_S>0}x_Sp(S)\le \sum_{S:x_S>0}x_Sp(S^\star)=p(S^\star).\)

Assigning mass one to \(S^\star\) attains this upper bound. It satisfies every supply constraint because each item receives allocated mass either zero or one.

Consequently, the continuous problem has exactly the same optimal value as the following discrete problem:

Choose an allowed price vector \(p\) and one bundle \(S\in D(p)\), maximizing \(p(S)\).

This equivalence respects seller selection among ties, including ties with the empty bundle. It also imposes no condition on prices of unsold items.

2. Rational prices and attainment cause no discrepancy

For completeness, the discrete optimum is attained with rational prices; allowing real prices would not increase it.

Let \(V=v(M)\). In any feasible discrete outcome \((S,p)\), comparison with the empty bundle gives

\(\displaystyle p(S)\le v(S)\le V.\)

Raise the prices of all items outside \(S\) to infinity. This preserves the payment for \(S\) and its demanded status: utilities of bundles contained in \(S\) remain unchanged, while all other bundles become unavailable.

For a fixed bundle \(S\), write \(q\) for its finite item prices. Its demanded status is equivalent to the inequalities

\(\displaystyle q_j\ge0\quad(j\in S),\qquad q(S\setminus T)\le v(S)-v(T)\quad(T\subseteq S).\)

Indeed, the second inequality is precisely the rearrangement of

\(\displaystyle v(S)-q(S)\ge v(T)-q(T).\)

These inequalities define a nonempty rational polytope. Nonemptiness follows from monotonicity: \(q=0\) is feasible. Taking \(T=\varnothing\) gives \(q(S)\le v(S)\), so nonnegativity bounds every coordinate and makes the polytope compact.

A linear objective \(q(S)\) therefore attains its maximum. A nonempty bounded rational polytope has rational vertices, and a linear objective has a maximizing vertex. Hence the maximum for this fixed bundle is attained with rational prices. The empty bundle separately yields revenue zero.

There are finitely many bundles, so their attained maxima have an attained maximum. Together with Section 1, this proves that the continuous and discrete problems have identical attained optima under the question’s rational-price convention.

3. Applying the discrete hardness theorem

The applicable known result is Theorem 9 of Bilò, Flammini, Monaco, and Moscardelli (2022): revenue maximization under market envy-freeness is NP-hard for a single buyer with normalized monotone combinatorial valuation. Their proof supplies polynomial-time value and demand oracles. For one buyer, their feasibility condition is exactly \(S\in D(p)\), and revenue is \(p(S)\). See [Theorem 9 and its proof, pp. 1803–1804](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/13704/26842/31702).

The reduction to the present problem preserves the item set, valuation, and both oracles. It replaces the discrete buyer by one supported type of mass one and gives every item supply one. Thus \(\tau=1\).

Both directions of the optimization correspondence have already been proved:

  • A discrete outcome \((S,p)\) becomes a continuous outcome by setting \(x_S=1\), with unchanged revenue.
  • Any continuous outcome can be replaced, at the same prices, by a discrete demanded bundle whose payment is at least its revenue.

In particular, a continuous maximizer yields a discrete maximizer. If its positive masses are explicitly listed, choose any supported bundle with largest payment. This requires only scanning the returned support and summing item prices; a polynomial-time algorithm necessarily has polynomial-length explicit output.

4. Binary encoding and oracle computation

The cited hardness construction uses a formula with \(n\ge4\) variables, \(m=2n\) literal items, and valuation levels \(0,1,3+\varepsilon,n\). A fixed rational choice such as \(\varepsilon=1/4\) suffices. Its oracles use clause inspection, price comparisons, and price sums. The satisfying-case revenue witness uses prices \(1\) and infinity. These are details of the [proof of Theorem 9](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/13704/26842/31702).

Therefore all valuation answers have binary length \(O(\log n)\); the sole support mass is the rational number one. Oracle arithmetic takes polynomial time in the formula length and the encoded query length: sums contain at most \(m\) rational prices, and exact addition and comparison have polynomial bit complexity. Infinite prices can be handled symbolically by discarding bundles containing them.

Suppose an exact algorithm for the present problem ran in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), including query encoding lengths. On these constructed instances, its running time and query lengths would be polynomial in the source formula length. Simulating its polynomial-time oracles and applying the outcome correspondence would then solve the NP-hard discrete problem in polynomial time.

Hence the stated continuous formulation is NP-hard, and a polynomial-time exact algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
