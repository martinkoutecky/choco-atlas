The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) online batches \((q_j,w_j)_{j=1}^m\) with \(q_j,w_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\), a goods-or-chores label, and \(F\in\{\mathrm{EF},\mathrm{EFX},\mathrm{EF1}\}\), let a nonatomic society \(\Omega=[0,1]\) have one complete utility type \(\mu=(1)\). Batch \(j\) supplies \(q_j\) indivisible copies per unit population, and an irrevocable allocation based only on revealed batches is an integer-valued measurable \(z_j\) with \(\int_\Omega z_j(a)\,da=q_j\). Using the paper's individual-bundle fairness definitions with essential extrema, determine the optimal online competitive ratio against the full-information offline optimum for \(U(a)=\sum_j w_jz_j(a)\), and determine whether the goods and chores ratios are reciprocal.

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(\Omega=[0,1]\) has Lebesgue measure and the single common utility type \(\mu=(1)\), of mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.
Fix \(m\geq1\) and \(F\in\{\mathrm{EF},\mathrm{EFX},\mathrm{EF1}\}\). An adversary reveals batches \((q_j,w_j)\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^2\) sequentially; \(w_j\) is the common value or nonnegative cost of one copy. The horizon \(m\) is known, but future batches and total utility are unknown.
A deterministic online policy irrevocably chooses measurable \(z_j:\Omega\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}\) with \(\int_\Omega z_j(a)\,da=q_j\) upon revelation of batch \(j\). Copies remain individually indivisible; allocations may differ within the single type. Write \(z=(z_1,\ldots,z_m)\) and \(U(a)=\sum_jw_jz_j(a)\).
Set \(D_{\mathrm{EF}}(a)=U(a)\), \(D_{\mathrm{EFX}}(a)=U(a)-\min_{j:z_j(a)>0}w_j\), and \(D_{\mathrm{EF1}}(a)=U(a)-\max_{j:z_j(a)>0}w_j\); set all three to zero for an empty bundle. Thus EFX includes zero-valued copies, and EF1 permits removing at most one copy.
Goods are \(\alpha\)-\(F\) when \(U(a)\geq\alpha D_F(b)\) for almost every pair \((a,b)\), with \(\alpha\in[0,1]\); chores are \(\alpha\)-\(F\) when \(D_F(a)\leq\alpha U(b)\), with \(\alpha\geq1\). These conditions use essential extrema, and fairness is evaluated on the final allocation.
Let \(G_F(z)\) be the supremum of admissible goods factors and \(C_F(z)\) the infimum of admissible chores factors, taking \(C_F(z)=\infty\) if none is finite. The full-information benchmarks are \(G_F^*(I)=\sup_zG_F(z)\) and \(C_F^*(I)=\inf_zC_F(z)\), over all feasible measurable allocations.
Define \(\mathrm{CR}_g^F(H;m)=\inf_I G_F(H(I))/G_F^*(I)\) and \(\mathrm{CR}_c^F(H;m)=\sup_I C_F(H(I))/C_F^*(I)\), over all length-\(m\) inputs. Set an instance ratio to \(1\) when \(G_F^*(I)=0\) or \(C_F^*(I)=\infty\), respectively; use \(1/0=\infty\) and \(1/\infty=0\).
Write \(R_g^F(m)=\sup_H\mathrm{CR}_g^F(H;m)\) and \(R_c^F(m)=\inf_H\mathrm{CR}_c^F(H;m)\).

What are the optimal competitive ratios \(R_g^F(m)\) and \(R_c^F(m)\)? Does \(\mathrm{CR}_c^F(H;m)=1/\mathrm{CR}_g^F(H;m)\) hold for every policy \(H\) used identically on matched goods and chores inputs, and are the optimal ratios reciprocal?

For finite societies with identical additive utilities, the competitive ratios of the same allocation policy on matched goods and chores inputs are reciprocal under \(\mathrm{EF},\mathrm{EFX},\mathrm{EF1}\). [Chen–Tan](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15404v1)

2. Reciprocity, including exceptional instances

Fix a feasible allocation \(z\), and write

\(\ell=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{a\in\Omega}U(a)\), \(\qquad d=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{a\in\Omega}D_F(a)\).

Because all quantities and weights are finite and nonnegative,

\(\int_\Omega U(a)\,da=\sum_{j=1}^m q_jw_j<\infty\).

Thus \(\ell\) is finite, although \(d\) may be infinite.

For a positive goods factor \(\alpha\), the almost-everywhere pairwise condition is equivalent to \(\ell\geq\alpha d\). Indeed, Fubini’s theorem implies that for almost every \(a\), the inequality holds for almost every \(b\); taking the essential supremum over \(b\), and then the essential infimum over \(a\), gives this necessary condition. The essential bounds also establish sufficiency. The goods factor \(0\) is always admissible.

Similarly, for a finite chores factor \(\alpha\), the condition is equivalent to \(d\leq\alpha\ell\).

It follows that:

  • If \(d=0\), then \(G_F(z)=C_F(z)=1\).
  • If \(d>0\) and \(\ell=0\), or if \(d=\infty\), then \(G_F(z)=0\) and \(C_F(z)=\infty\).
  • Otherwise, \(G_F(z)=\min\{1,\ell/d\}\) and \(C_F(z)=\max\{1,d/\ell\}\).

In every case,

\(C_F(z)=1/G_F(z)\).

Taking extrema over the same feasible allocations gives

\(C_F^*(I)=1/G_F^*(I)\).

This identity does not require either benchmark to be attained: reciprocal order reversal converts a supremum into an infimum, with the stated extended-real conventions.

If \(G_F^*(I)>0\), the matched instance ratios therefore satisfy

\(\displaystyle \frac{C_F(H(I))}{C_F^*(I)}=\left(\frac{G_F(H(I))}{G_F^*(I)}\right)^{-1}\).

If \(G_F^*(I)=0\), then \(C_F^*(I)=\infty\), and both instance ratios are defined to be \(1\). Hence the reciprocal relation holds on every instance. Taking the supremum for chores and the infimum for goods proves

\(\mathrm{CR}_c^F(H;m)=1/\mathrm{CR}_g^F(H;m)\).

Finally, goods and chores policies on matched batches range over the same allocation rules. Taking the infimum over policies on the chores side yields

\(R_c^F(m)=1/R_g^F(m)\).

This extends the finite-society identity in [Chen–Tan, Theorem 3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15404v1) to the present essential-extremum model and its exceptional-instance conventions.

3. A polynomial-time online policy maintaining exact EF1

We construct a deterministic policy maintaining the invariant

\(D_{\mathrm{EF1}}(a)\leq\ell\quad\text{for almost every }a,\qquad \ell=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}U\).

This is precisely sufficient for both goods and chores fairness at factor \(1\).

The policy maintains a finite partition into positive-length intervals. On each interval, every previously assigned copy count is constant. Initially, there is one interval with an empty bundle.

3.1. A positive-weight batch

Suppose the current intervals have masses \(p_1,\ldots,p_s\) and loads \(u_1,\ldots,u_s\). Consider a batch \((q,w)\) with \(q>0\) and \(w>0\).

Choose an integer \(B>0\) clearing the denominators of the revealed weights. Set

\(A_i=Bu_i\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\), \(\qquad W=Bw\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\).

For integer \(t\geq-1\), define

\(\displaystyle K(t)=\sum_{i=1}^s p_i\max\left\{0,1+\left\lfloor\frac{t-A_i}{W}\right\rfloor\right\}\).

The summand counts the mass of possible copy additions whose load immediately before that addition is at most \(t\), in scaled units.

Let \(T\) be the least nonnegative integer satisfying \(K(T)\geq q\). Such an integer exists: with

\(H=\max_i A_i+\lceil q\rceil W\),

every summand’s integer count is at least \(\lceil q\rceil+1\), so \(K(H)>q\). Also \(K(-1)=0\), and therefore

\(K(T-1)<q\leq K(T)\).

First assign throughout interval \(i\) the integer number of copies

\(\displaystyle n_i=\max\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{T-A_i}{W}\right\rceil\right\}\).

The total quantity assigned so far is \(K(T-1)\). The remaining quantity is

\(r=q-K(T-1)>0\).

Call interval \(i\) eligible when \(A_i\leq T\) and \(T-A_i\) is divisible by \(W\). Directly from the definition,

\(\displaystyle K(T)-K(T-1)=\sum_{i\text{ eligible}}p_i\).

Consequently, the eligible intervals have total mass at least \(r\). Give one additional copy to a subset of their union of mass exactly \(r\). Choose this subset deterministically by scanning intervals from left to right, taking whole eligible intervals until at most one final interval needs to be cut.

The assigned quantity is exactly \(q\), and at most one interval splits.

Every final scaled load is at least \(T\). Moreover, for every recipient of at least one new copy, the load immediately before its last new copy is at most \(T\). Thus, writing \(U'\) for the new load,

\(U'(a)-w\leq T/B\leq\ell'\)

for every such recipient, where \(\ell'=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}U'\).

Let \(M'(a)\) be the largest weight in the recipient’s new bundle. Since \(M'(a)\geq w\),

\(D'_{\mathrm{EF1}}(a)=U'(a)-M'(a)\leq U'(a)-w\leq\ell'\).

For agents receiving no new copies, the previous adjusted load is unchanged. Loads only increase, so \(\ell'\geq\ell\), and the old invariant implies the new one.

3.2. Zero quantities and zero weights

A batch with \(q=0\) receives the zero allocation.

For \(q>0\) and \(w=0\), select the leftmost interval attaining the current minimum load. If its mass is \(p\), give

\(k=\lfloor q/p\rfloor\)

zero-weight copies to everyone in that interval, and one further copy to a subinterval of mass \(q-kp\).

This allocates exactly \(q\) copies and splits at most one interval. Zero-weight additions change neither utility nor the \(\mathrm{EF1}\) adjusted load, so the invariant remains valid.

Starting from empty bundles, induction proves exact \(\mathrm{EF1}\) after every batch. Therefore, on every input,

\(G_{\mathrm{EF1}}(H(I))=C_{\mathrm{EF1}}(H(I))=1\).

The benchmarks also equal \(1\), proving

\(R_g^{\mathrm{EF1}}(m)=R_c^{\mathrm{EF1}}(m)=1\).

3.3. Bit complexity

After \(j\) batches, there are at most \(j+1\) intervals.

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the revealed quantities. Every interval mass and endpoint has denominator dividing \(Q\): each new cut uses a revealed quantity minus integer multiples of existing masses. The products \(Q\) and \(B\) have bit length \(O(L)\).

Every positive interval mass is at least \(1/Q\). Feasibility therefore bounds its count of batch-\(h\) copies by \(q_hQ\), giving polynomial bit length for all stored counts. Loads, scaled loads, and the search bound \(H\) have bit length \(O(L+\log(m+1))\).

Binary search finds \(T\) using \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) evaluations of \(K\). Each evaluation scans at most \(j+1\) cells. Using ordinary quadratic-time integer arithmetic gives the total bound

\(O\!\left(m^2(L+\log(m+1))^3\right)\)

bit operations, and space

\(O\!\left(m^2(L+\log(m+1))\right)\).

These bounds apply to the stated society with \(\tau=1\). Individual copies are represented by binary counts and are never enumerated.

3.4. The offline EFX benchmark is also exactly one

For completeness, the same construction proves

\(G_{\mathrm{EFX}}^*(I)=C_{\mathrm{EFX}}^*(I)=1\)

for every input.

With full information, process the positive-weight batches in nonincreasing weight order using the threshold construction. For any recipient of a batch of weight \(w\), all older copies have weight at least \(w\), so its new minimum copy weight is \(w\). The preceding inequality then gives

\(D'_{\mathrm{EFX}}(a)=U'(a)-w\leq\ell'\).

For nonrecipients, the invariant persists because the minimum load does not decrease.

Process zero-weight batches last, allocating them only within a minimum-load interval as above. A recipient’s new \(\mathrm{EFX}\) adjusted load is its unchanged utility, exactly the minimum load. Other bundles are unchanged.

Reindexing these allocations by the original batch labels gives a feasible full-information allocation. The running-time bound remains polynomial as above.

4. Two batches force zero under EF and EFX

Fix \(m\geq2\) and any deterministic policy \(H\). Reveal first

\((q_1,w_1)=(1,1)\).

Write \(x=z_1\), so \(x\) is nonnegative integer-valued and \(\int_\Omega x=1\).

4.1. If the first allocation is not uniformly one copy

Suppose \(x\neq1\) on a set of positive measure.

Then both \(\{x=0\}\) and \(\{x\geq2\}\) have positive measure. Indeed:

  • If \(x\geq1\) almost everywhere, its mean \(1\) forces \(x=1\) almost everywhere.
  • If \(x\leq1\) almost everywhere, the same conclusion follows.

Reveal zero-quantity batches for all remaining positions. They cannot change the allocation almost everywhere.

There are then agents of utility zero, while a positive-measure set has positive adjusted load under either \(\mathrm{EF}\) or \(\mathrm{EFX}\). Hence the policy’s goods factor is zero.

The offline allocation giving one first-batch copy to everyone has factor \(1\) under both notions. Thus the instance ratio is zero.

4.2. If the first allocation is uniformly one copy

Now suppose \(x=1\) almost everywhere. For any integer \(N\geq2\), reveal

\((q_2,w_2)=(1-1/N,N)\),

and pad the remaining positions with zero-quantity batches.

Let \(y=z_2\). Since

\(\lambda(\{y\geq1\})\leq\int_\Omega y=1-1/N\),

the set \(\{y=0\}\) has mass at least \(1/N\). Thus the final minimum utility is exactly \(1\). Also, \(\{y\geq1\}\) has positive measure because \(\int_\Omega y>0\).

For \(\mathrm{EF}\), recipients of the second batch have utility at least \(N+1\). Therefore,

\(G_{\mathrm{EF}}(H(I))\leq1/(N+1)\).

For \(\mathrm{EFX}\), everyone retains a first-batch copy of weight \(1\), so

\(D_{\mathrm{EFX}}(a)=Ny(a)\).

Consequently,

\(G_{\mathrm{EFX}}(H(I))\leq1/N\).

In contrast, an offline allocation achieves factor \(1\) under both notions. Partition the society into sets \(A,B\) of masses \(1/N\) and \(1-1/N\). Give:

  • \(N\) first-batch copies and no second-batch copies to each agent in \(A\);
  • no first-batch copies and one second-batch copy to each agent in \(B\).

The required quantities are exactly satisfied, and everyone has utility \(N\). Hence the allocation is exactly \(\mathrm{EF}\), and therefore exactly \(\mathrm{EFX}\).

The first batch is identical for every \(N\). Thus a uniform first allocation yields competitive ratios bounded by \(1/(N+1)\) and \(1/N\) for every \(N\); their infima are zero.

Both cases prove that every policy has

\(\mathrm{CR}_g^{\mathrm{EF}}(H;m)=\mathrm{CR}_g^{\mathrm{EFX}}(H;m)=0\).

Nonnegativity gives the matching optimal values. Reciprocity then gives

\(R_c^{\mathrm{EF}}(m)=R_c^{\mathrm{EFX}}(m)=\infty\).

5. One batch is optimally solvable

For \(m=1\), write \(q=k+r\), where \(k=\lfloor q\rfloor\) and \(0\leq r<1\). Give \(k+1\) copies to an interval of mass \(r\), and \(k\) copies to everyone else.

For \(\mathrm{EF1}\) and \(\mathrm{EFX}\), the largest adjusted load is at most \(kw\), while the smallest utility is \(kw\). Thus both factors equal \(1\), including the zero-load cases.

For \(\mathrm{EF}\), if \(w=0\) or \(r=0\), the allocation has equal utilities and factor \(1\). Otherwise its goods factor is \(k/(k+1)\).

This is optimal: any integer-valued allocation with mean strictly between \(k\) and \(k+1\) must have essential minimum count at most \(k\) and essential maximum count at least \(k+1\). Its goods \(\mathrm{EF}\) factor therefore cannot exceed \(k/(k+1)\).

When \(k=0\) and \(r>0\), the offline goods benchmark is zero, so the prescribed instance-ratio convention gives ratio \(1\). On every other instance the balanced allocation attains the benchmark. Hence all three optimal goods competitive ratios for one batch equal \(1\), and reciprocity gives the same chores ratios.

The balanced one-batch allocation takes \(O(L^2)\) bit operations.
