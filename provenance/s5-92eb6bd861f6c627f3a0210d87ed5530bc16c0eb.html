Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) on two sides, positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) each summing to \(1\), weak preference orders over opposite-side types, and one incompatible pair \((i_0,j_0)\), does there exist a rational mass-flow matrix \(x\) with \(x_{i_0j_0}=0\), row sums \(\mu_i\), and column sums \(\nu_j\), such that for every compatible pair \((i,j)\), either \(\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}=0\) or \(\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}=0\)? Determine the complexity in \(|I|, |J|\), and the binary bit length \(L\) of the masses.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be finite nonempty sets of types, with positive binary rational masses satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) positive-mass types and their masses; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Exactly one pair \((i_0,j_0)\) is mutually unacceptable; every pair in \(E=(I\times J)\setminus\{(i_0,j_0)\}\) is compatible. Each type supplies a weak preference order over all its compatible opposite-side types; \(\succ_i\) and \(\succ_j\) denote strict preference, and ties are not broken. Put \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
A perfect mass matching is \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{I\times J}\) satisfying \(x_{i_0j_0}=0\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\mu_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}=\nu_j\).
It is stable if every \((i,j)\in E\) satisfies \(\sum_{j':(i,j')\in E,\ j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}=0\) or \(\sum_{i':(i',j)\in E,\ i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}=0\). The unacceptable pair cannot block.

Is deciding whether such a stable perfect mass matching exists in \(\mathsf{P}\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,|I|,|J|,L\).

The discrete perfect weakly stable matching problem on \(K_{n,n}\) minus one edge is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even with ties of length two confined to the beginning of lists on one side. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.10458).
For unit-marginal random matchings, the analogous condition is called ex-ante weak stability; its feasible set can be nonconvex even with strict preferences. [Source](https://www.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/taxonomy.pdf). No complexity classification for the precise mass-flow decision problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made nonnegativity explicit: every flow entry is a nonnegative rational.
  • Interpreted the incompatible pair as the single missing, mutually unacceptable edge, rather than an acceptable forbidden edge; it cannot block.
  • Specified complete weak orders over compatible partners and restricted preference comparisons to compatible pairs; ties remain unbroken.
  • Defined m as the number of compatible type pairs, tau as the combined support size, and L as the total binary input length.

Result

The problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even with balanced sides, equal dyadic masses, and ties of length two confined to the beginning of lists on one side.

The crucial fact is that a stable mass matching with equal marginals has a positive support containing a discrete perfect matching, and every such supported perfect matching inherits stability.

Write \(a=|I|\), \(b=|J|\), and \(\tau=a+b\). If either \(a=1\) or \(b=1\), the unacceptable pair leaves a positive-mass type isolated, so no perfect matching exists. The hardness concerns \(a,b\geq2\).

1. Stability is inherited by smaller supports

For a nonnegative matrix \(x\), define its positive support by

\(\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{(i,j)\in E:x_{ij}>0\}\).

Lemma 1. Suppose \(x\) is stable and \(y\) is a perfect mass matching with \(\operatorname{supp}(y)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(x)\). Then \(y\) is stable.

Proof. Fix \((i,j)\in E\). Stability of \(x\) says that at least one of the following sums is zero:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'},\qquad \sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}.\)

If the first sum is zero, nonnegativity implies \(x_{ij'}=0\) for every compatible \(j'\) strictly worse than \(j\). Support containment implies \(y_{ij'}=0\) for all those entries, so the corresponding sum for \(y\) is also zero. The argument is identical when the second sum is zero.

Thus every compatible pair satisfies the stability condition for \(y\). This argument respects ties throughout. \(\square\)

2. Equal masses give an exact discrete equivalence

Lemma 2. Suppose \(|I|=|J|=q\) and every marginal equals \(1/q\). A stable perfect mass matching exists if and only if a discrete perfect weakly stable matching exists, with the same compatible pairs and preferences.

Proof. First suppose a stable perfect mass matching \(x\) exists. Form the bipartite graph \(H\) with edge set \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\).

For any \(S\subseteq I\), let \(N_H(S)\) be its neighborhood in \(H\). The marginal equations and nonnegativity give

\(\displaystyle \frac{|S|}{q} =\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{j\in N_H(S)}x_{ij} \leq\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in N_H(S)}x_{ij} =\frac{|N_H(S)|}{q}.\)

Hence \(|N_H(S)|\geq|S|\) for every \(S\subseteq I\). Hall’s marriage theorem therefore supplies a matching saturating \(I\). Since both sides have size \(q\), this is a perfect matching \(M\).

Define \(y_{ij}=1/q\) on \(M\) and \(y_{ij}=0\) elsewhere. It has the required marginals and support contained in \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\), so Lemma 1 makes \(y\) stable.

For this matrix, the row dissatisfaction sum for \((i,j)\) is positive exactly when \(i\) strictly prefers \(j\) to its partner in \(M\); the column sum has the analogous interpretation. Consequently, stability of \(y\) is exactly weak stability of \(M\).

Conversely, given a discrete perfect weakly stable matching \(M\), assigning mass \(1/q\) to each edge of \(M\) produces a stable perfect mass matching by the same observation. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial reduction and hardness

We use the following established result: deciding whether \(K_{n,n}\) minus one edge admits a perfect weakly stable matching is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even when all ties have length two, occur on one side, and appear at the beginning of lists. This is [Cseh–Heeger, Theorem 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.10458).

Given such a discrete instance, setting every mass to \(1/n\) already gives a polynomial reduction by Lemma 2. The following padding makes the masses dyadic as well.

Let \(q\) be the smallest power of two with \(q\geq n\), so \(q<2n\). Add \(q-n\) designated pairs of new vertices, one vertex on each side per pair.

Make every newly introduced pair of opposite-side vertices compatible. Thus the original missing edge remains the unique unacceptable pair. Set preferences as follows:

  • Each original vertex preserves its original list and appends all new opposite-side vertices strictly below it.
  • Each new vertex ranks its designated mate uniquely first and orders all other compatible vertices strictly.

This preserves the stated restrictions on ties.

Every discrete perfect weakly stable matching of the enlarged instance must match each designated new pair: otherwise both endpoints strictly prefer each other to their assigned partners, creating a blocking pair. Removing these pairs therefore leaves a perfect weakly stable matching of the original instance.

Conversely, any original perfect weakly stable matching extends by the designated new pairs. Original pairs cannot block by original stability; every pair involving a new vertex cannot block because that vertex receives its unique first choice.

Now give every vertex mass \(1/q\). These are positive dyadic rationals, and each side has total mass one. Lemma 2 proves that the resulting mass instance is a yes-instance exactly when the original discrete instance is.

The output parameters are

\(\tau=2q,\qquad |I|=|J|=q,\qquad m=q^2-1.\)

With explicitly listed preferences and binary vertex identifiers, construction takes \(O(L_{\mathrm{disc}}+n^2\log n)\) bit operations, and the output length is \(O(m\log\tau)\). This proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness under all the claimed restrictions.

4. Polynomial-size certificates for arbitrary masses

To establish membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\), we must bound certificate sizes even when a supplied stable solution initially has large denominators.

Write the input masses as

\(\mu_i=u_i/d_i,\qquad \nu_j=v_j/e_j,\)

with positive integer denominators, and set

\(\displaystyle D=\prod_{i\in I}d_i\prod_{j\in J}e_j.\)

The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Define integer demands

\(A_i=D\mu_i,\qquad B_j=D\nu_j.\)

They satisfy \(\sum_iA_i=\sum_jB_j=D\).

Suppose a stable perfect mass matching \(x\) exists, and let \(H=\operatorname{supp}(x)\). Construct a directed network with:

  • an arc from the source to each \(i\), of capacity \(A_i\);
  • an arc from \(i\) to \(j\), of capacity \(D\), for each \((i,j)\in H\);
  • an arc from each \(j\) to the sink, of capacity \(B_j\).

The values \(Dx_{ij}\), together with saturated source and sink arcs, define a feasible flow of value \(D\). No flow can exceed \(D\), because the source capacities sum to \(D\).

All capacities are integers. The integral maximum-flow theorem therefore supplies an integral flow of value \(D\). Its middle-arc values \(f_{ij}\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_jf_{ij}=A_i,\qquad \sum_if_{ij}=B_j,\qquad 0\leq f_{ij}\leq D.\)

Consequently, \(y_{ij}=f_{ij}/D\) is a perfect mass matching supported inside \(H\). Lemma 1 shows that it is stable.

Thus every yes-instance has a certificate consisting of \(m\) integers of \(O(L)\) bits each, of total length \(O(mL)\).

For verification, number each vertex’s indifference classes from best to worst. Denote the ranks by \(r_i(j)\) and \(s_j(i)\). After checking the marginal equations, compute the worst positive-support ranks

\(\displaystyle w_i=\max_{j:\,f_{ij}>0}r_i(j),\qquad z_j=\max_{i:\,f_{ij}>0}s_j(i).\)

These maxima exist because all marginals are positive. A compatible pair blocks exactly when

\(r_i(j)<w_i\quad\text{and}\quad s_j(i)<z_j.\)

Indeed, each strict inequality is equivalent to the existence of positive mass assigned to a strictly worse partner at that endpoint. Checking every edge therefore verifies stability exactly.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, preprocessing takes \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations, and certificate verification thereafter takes \(O((m+\tau)L)\). Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\), completing the classification.

5. Explicit deterministic running time

An algorithm can enumerate a proposed worst occupied indifference class for every type.

Let \(k_i\) and \(\ell_j\) be the numbers of indifference classes in the respective lists. Enumerate choices

\(w_i\in\{1,\ldots,k_i\},\qquad z_j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell_j\}.\)

For each choice:

  • Reject it if any \((i,j)\in E\) satisfies both \(r_i(j)<w_i\) and \(s_j(i)<z_j\).
  • Otherwise retain precisely the edges satisfying \(r_i(j)\leq w_i\) and \(s_j(i)\leq z_j\).
  • Test whether the retained graph supports the required marginals, using the integer-capacity network from Section 4.

Correctness. A stable solution’s actual worst occupied classes appear in the enumeration, pass the first check, and retain every positive entry of that solution.

Conversely, a feasible flow in a retained graph assigns each endpoint only partners at least as good as its proposed worst class. If an edge blocked this flow, both endpoints would receive positive mass strictly worse than that edge. The edge would then satisfy both inequalities in the first check, a contradiction.

There are

\(\displaystyle N=\prod_i k_i\prod_j\ell_j\leq b^a a^b\)

choices. Each network has \(\tau+2\) vertices and at most \(m+\tau\) arcs. Edmonds–Karp uses \(O(\tau(m+\tau)^2)\) arithmetic operations; all capacities and residual capacities have \(O(L)\) bits. Thus a valid deterministic bit-time bound is

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\tau L^2+b^a a^b\,\tau(m+\tau)^2L\right).\)

Since \(b^a a^b\leq\tau^\tau\), this is also \(2^{O(\tau\log\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\).

These bounds use the actual positive-mass support size \(\tau\). They require no expansion into individual agents. The \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness result rules out a polynomial-time algorithm in \(L\) unless \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).
