The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a graph \(G=(V,E)\) with \(n=|V|\), rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), and rational threshold \(\theta\), decide whether there is a distribution \(p\) over subsets \(S\subseteq V\) such that \(\sum_S p_S=1\), \(\sum_S p_S|S|=n\alpha\), and \(\sum_S p_S e_G(S)\ge n\theta\), where \(e_G(S)=|\{v\in V\setminus S:N_G(v)\cap S\ne\varnothing\}|\). Interpret \(p\) as the mass of page copies using each integral placement, with high-valued items on \(S\), and maximize average externality across the continuous population of copies.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite simple undirected graph with \(n=|V|\ge1\), and let \(m=n\) be the number of objects on each copy. The input includes binary rationals \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb Q\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here the input society consists of one type of identical graph copies, of total mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\).
On each copy, a placement \(S\subseteq V\) gives value \(1\) to vertices in \(S\) and value \(0\) to all other vertices; every vertex receives exactly one object. Its externality is \(e_G(S)=|\{v\in V\setminus S:N_G(v)\cap S\ne\varnothing\}|\), where \(N_G(v)\) is the open neighborhood.
A feasible allocation splits the population among placements using nonnegative rational masses \(p_S\), subject to \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}p_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}p_S|S|=n\alpha\). Placements and their support are chosen by the allocation, not supplied or restricted in the input; individual copies need not have the same number of value-\(1\) objects.
Decide whether a feasible allocation satisfies \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}p_Se_G(S)\ge n\theta\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,n,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous allocation decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The discrete problem, with exactly \(k\) value-\(1\) objects and \(n-k\) value-\(0\) objects on one graph, is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even on graphs of maximum degree \(3\). [Discrete result](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/FAIA200082).
No result settling this exact distributional problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified a finite simple undirected graph with at least one vertex, so the per-vertex normalization is defined.
  • Specified full occupancy with exactly n objects per copy, normalized to values 0 and 1, matching the binary full-occupancy instances used by the hardness result.
  • Applied the binary-rational mass convention to the chosen placement distribution and distinguished its freely chosen support from the input society's singleton support.
  • Made explicit that the high-value inventory is conserved across the population in expectation, with no fixed cardinality imposed on each copy, as required by the graded question.

Result

The continuous allocation decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete under the standard explicit encoding of graphs. Hardness holds even when \(\tau=1\), \(m=n\), and \(G\) has maximum degree \(3\).

The key is to choose a threshold at which every placement with positive mass must be a dominating set.

1. A bound whose equality forces domination

A set \(D\subseteq V\) is a dominating set if every vertex in \(V\setminus D\) has a neighbor in \(D\). Write \(\gamma(G)\) for the minimum cardinality of a dominating set.

For any placement \(S\subseteq V\), define its number of undominated vertices outside \(S\) by

\[ u_G(S)=|\{v\in V\setminus S:N_G(v)\cap S=\varnothing\}|. \]

The vertices outside \(S\) partition into those counted by \(e_G(S)\) and those counted by \(u_G(S)\). Hence

\[ e_G(S)=n-|S|-u_G(S). \]

Consequently, every feasible allocation satisfies

\[ \sum_Sp_Se_G(S) =n-n\alpha-\sum_Sp_Su_G(S) \le n(1-\alpha). \]

Moreover, equality holds if and only if every placement with positive mass is a dominating set. Indeed, every summand \(p_Su_G(S)\) is nonnegative, so their sum is zero exactly when \(u_G(S)=0\) for every \(S\) with \(p_S>0\).

2. Polynomial reduction from Dominating Set

We use the known theorem that Dominating Set is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even on finite simple planar graphs of maximum degree \(3\): given \(G\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether \(\gamma(G)\le k\). This is the restriction recorded in [Garey–Johnson, problem GT2](https://perso.limos.fr/~palafour/PAPERS/PDF/Garey-Johnson79.pdf).

Restrict to instances with \(n\ge1\) and \(0\le k\le n\); instances outside these bounds are trivial. Construct the allocation instance using the same graph and set

\[ m=n,\qquad \tau=1,\qquad \alpha=\frac{k}{n},\qquad \theta=\frac{n-k}{n}. \]

These are valid binary rationals of encoding length \(O(\log(n+1))\). We prove

\[ \gamma(G)\le k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed allocation instance is a yes-instance}. \]

Forward implication. Suppose \(D\) is a dominating set with \(|D|\le k\). Since \(k\le n\), extend \(D\) to a set \(S\) of cardinality exactly \(k\). A superset of a dominating set is dominating: every vertex outside \(S\) is outside \(D\) and has a neighbor in \(D\subseteq S\).

Put all population mass on \(S\). Then

\[ \sum_Tp_T|T|=k=n\alpha, \qquad \sum_Tp_Te_G(T)=e_G(S)=n-k=n\theta. \]

Thus the allocation is feasible and meets the threshold.

Reverse implication. Suppose a feasible allocation meets the threshold. Section 1 gives

\[ n-k \le \sum_Sp_Se_G(S) =n-k-\sum_Sp_Su_G(S) \le n-k. \]

Therefore every positive-mass placement is a dominating set. Its average cardinality is \(k\). At least one positive-mass placement has cardinality at most \(k\), since otherwise their average would exceed \(k\). That placement witnesses \(\gamma(G)\le k\).

The reduction preserves the graph, including planarity and maximum degree. It takes polynomial time in the source input length. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the exact distributional problem, with unrestricted placement support.

3. Two placements suffice for an optimal allocation

We next prove membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), including the rational-certificate requirement.

Put \(b=n\alpha\). Temporarily allow real masses and consider

\[ P_b= \left\{ p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{2^n}: \sum_Sp_S=1,\quad \sum_Sp_S|S|=b \right\}. \]

This set is nonempty: masses \(1-\alpha\) on \(\varnothing\) and \(\alpha\) on \(V\) are feasible. It is closed and bounded, so the linear objective \(\sum_Sp_Se_G(S)\) attains a maximum.

Choose an optimizer having minimum support cardinality. Suppose its support contains at least three placements. The vectors \((1,|S|)\) indexed by that support are linearly dependent in \(\mathbb R^2\). Thus there is a nonzero vector \(d\), supported there, satisfying

\[ \sum_Sd_S=0,\qquad \sum_Sd_S|S|=0. \]

Both \(p+td\) and \(p-td\) are feasible for sufficiently small positive \(t\), because all coordinates in the support of \(d\) initially have positive mass. Optimality therefore forces

\[ \sum_Sd_Se_G(S)=0; \]

otherwise one of these two perturbations would improve the objective.

Since \(d\ne0\) and \(\sum_Sd_S=0\), some coordinate of \(d\) is negative. Take

\[ t_*=\min_{d_S<0}\frac{p_S}{-d_S}. \]

Then \(p+t_*d\) is feasible, has the same objective, and has strictly smaller support. This contradicts the choice of \(p\). Hence an optimizer uses at most two placements.

If two supported placements have equal cardinality, putting all their mass on the one with greater externality preserves feasibility and does not decrease the objective. Otherwise, let their cardinalities be \(a<c\). Their masses are necessarily

\[ p_A=\frac{c-b}{c-a}, \qquad p_C=\frac{b-a}{c-a}. \]

Because \(b=n\alpha\) is rational, these masses are rational and have binary encoding length \(O(L+\log(n+1))\). A one-placement optimizer has mass \(1\).

Thus the real optimum is attained by a rational allocation using at most two placements. Every yes-instance consequently has a certificate consisting of two vertex subsets and polynomial-length rational masses. A verifier computes their cardinalities and externalities and checks all constraints using exact rational arithmetic.

Under explicit graph encoding, the certificate length and verification time are polynomial in \(L\). Therefore the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Exact algorithm and parameter dependence

For completeness, define

\[ F_j=\max_{\substack{S\subseteq V\\|S|=j}}e_G(S), \qquad 0\le j\le n. \]

Enumerating every subset computes all \(F_j\) using \(O(2^n(n+|E|))\) elementary graph operations.

By Section 3, the optimum is the largest of:

  • \(F_b\), when \(b\) is an integer;
  • \(\dfrac{(c-b)F_a+(b-a)F_c}{c-a}\), over integers \(0\le a<c\le n\) satisfying \(a\le b\le c\).

Every listed candidate is attainable by choosing placements realizing the corresponding \(F_j\). Conversely, every optimal allocation has at most two placements and is bounded by one of these candidates. Thus this calculation is exact.

There are \(O(n^2)\) candidates, requiring exact arithmetic on numbers of bit length \(O(L+\log(n+1))\). The resulting deterministic running time is

\[ 2^n\operatorname{poly}(m,n,\tau,L), \qquad m=n,\quad \tau=1. \]

Certificate verification takes \(\operatorname{poly}(m,n,\tau,L)\) time. A deterministic algorithm polynomial in \(m,n,\tau,L\) for all these instances would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), since the reduction already fixes \(\tau=1\) and \(m=n\).
