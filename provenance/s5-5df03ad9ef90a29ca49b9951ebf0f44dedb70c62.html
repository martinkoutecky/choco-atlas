The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), supported voter types \(t\) with rankings \(\pi_t\), swap-cost schedules, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose masses \(x_{t,\pi}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{\pi}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). For a designated candidate \(c^\star\) and a natural scoring protocol, find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,\pi}d_t(\pi)x_{t,\pi}\), where \(d_t(\pi)\) is the minimum swap cost from \(\pi_t\) to \(\pi\), subject to \(\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c^\star)x_{t,\pi}\geq\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c)x_{t,\pi}\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq 2\) candidates, with designated candidate \(c^\star\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Each type consists of a strict ranking \(\pi_t\) and an explicitly listed swap-cost function \(\sigma_t\). The support size \(\tau\) is not the number of possible rankings or types.
For distinct candidates \(a,b\), the cost \(\sigma_t(a,b)\) is a finite nonnegative binary integer: it applies when \(a\) immediately precedes \(b\), and their positions are exchanged. Costs may be asymmetric and depend on the type, but not on positions. Any sequence of adjacent swaps is allowed, with additive cost; \(d_t(\pi)\) is the minimum cost of transforming \(\pi_t\) into \(\pi\).
The input includes a natural scoring vector \(s\in\mathbb Z^m\) satisfying \(m\geq s_1\geq\cdots\geq s_m\geq0\). Write \(s_\pi(c)=s_{\operatorname{rank}_\pi(c)}\), with rank one highest. All candidates tied for highest total score win.
Over all strict rankings \(\pi\) of \(C\), choose divisible masses \(x_{t,\pi}\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) satisfying \(\sum_\pi x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c^\star)x_{t,\pi}\geq\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c)x_{t,\pi}\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\), minimizing \(\sum_{t,\pi}d_t(\pi)x_{t,\pi}\). Rankings and their transformation costs are implicit, not explicitly enumerated in the input. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is this continuous minimum-cost swap-bribery problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? If it is hard, is it nevertheless \(\mathsf{FPT}\) parameterized by \(m\), with running time \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\)?

Discrete minimum-cost swap bribery under natural scoring protocols admits an algorithm running in \(m^{O(m^2)}O(\tau^3(\log n+\log\sigma_{\max}))\), where \(n\) is the number of voters and \(\sigma_{\max}\) the maximum swap cost.
Discrete budgeted swap bribery is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete already for 2-approval with costs \(\{1,2\}\), and \(\mathsf{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by the budget; this hardness concerns the budget, not \(m\). [Dorn–Schlotter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.1338). No result settling exactly the continuous problem above was located.

Result

The continuous problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard, even with exactly two support types, equal masses, symmetric swap costs, and an approval scoring vector. Nevertheless, it is \(\mathsf{FPT}\) parameterized by \(m\), with running time \(2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\).

The [discrete results of Dorn–Schlotter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.1338) alone do not establish continuous hardness: fractional splitting could change the optimum. The reduction below explicitly handles that issue.

1. Transformation costs are weighted inversion sums

For a type \(t\), let \(I_t(\pi)\) consist of the pairs \((a,b)\) such that \(a\) precedes \(b\) initially, while \(b\) precedes \(a\) in \(\pi\). Then

\(d_t(\pi)=\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in I_t(\pi)}\sigma_t(a,b).\)

To prove the lower bound, every pair in \(I_t(\pi)\) must exchange its relative order. Relative order changes only when that pair is swapped directly. Its first such swap has the initial orientation and therefore costs \(\sigma_t(a,b)\). All additional costs are nonnegative.

For the upper bound, construct \(\pi\) from left to right: move its next candidate left through the remaining candidates until it reaches its required position. The remaining candidates retain their initial relative order. Consequently, this procedure swaps exactly the inverted pairs, each once and in its initial orientation.

This proves the formula, including for asymmetric costs and unrestricted swap sequences.

2. A ranking-cost construction encoding Clique

Let \(G\) be a simple undirected graph on vertices \(v_1,\ldots,v_N\), and let \(2\leq k<N\). Clique remains \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard under this restriction: adding an isolated vertex ensures \(k<N\) for every nontrivial instance.

First consider the candidates \(v_1,\ldots,v_N,p\), initially ranked

\(v_1\succ v_2\succ\cdots\succ v_N\succ p.\)

Use \((k+1)\)-approval. Define

\(W=N^2,\qquad h_i=|\{j<i:\{v_j,v_i\}\in E(G)\}|.\)

Assign symmetric swap costs as follows:

  • Between \(v_i\) and \(v_j\), the cost is \(1\) if they are adjacent in \(G\), and \(0\) otherwise.
  • Between \(v_i\) and \(p\), the cost is \(W+h_i\).

For a vertex set \(S\), write \(e(S)\) for the number of graph edges with both endpoints in \(S\).

Consider any final ranking in which precisely the vertices of \(S\) precede \(p\), and put \(r=|S|\). The inversions involving \(p\) cost

\(\displaystyle\sum_{i:v_i\notin S}(W+h_i).\)

There are also unavoidable inversions between a vertex outside \(S\) and a later initial vertex belonging to \(S\). Their total cost is

\(\displaystyle\sum_{i:v_i\in S}h_i-e(S).\)

Indeed, the sum counts every edge whose later initial endpoint belongs to \(S\); subtracting \(e(S)\) removes exactly the edges internal to \(S\).

Since \(\sum_i h_i=|E(G)|\), every such ranking costs at least

\(F(S)=(N-r)W+|E(G)|-e(S).\)

Equality is attained by placing \(S\) first in initial order, then \(p\), then its complement in initial order. That ranking introduces exactly the inversions just counted.

The candidate \(p\) is approved precisely when \(r\leq k\). If \(r<k\), extend \(S\) to a set \(T\) of size \(k\). Then

\(F(T)-F(S)=-(k-r)W-(e(T)-e(S))<0,\)

because \(W>0\) and \(e(T)\geq e(S)\). Thus the minimum cost of a ranking approving \(p\) is

\(K=(N-k)W+|E(G)|-\displaystyle\max_{|S|=k}e(S).\)

Define the integer threshold

\(B=(N-k)W+|E(G)|-\binom{k}{2}.\)

Every \(k\)-vertex set has at most \(\binom{k}{2}\) edges, so

\(K\leq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\text{ contains a }k\text{-clique}.\)

Also \(B>0\), since \(N-k\geq1\) and \(\binom{k}{2}<N^2=W\).

Finally, set

\(D=NW+2|E(G)|.\)

This is the sum of all unordered-pair costs in the construction. The inversion formula therefore gives \(0\leq K\leq D\).

3. Two support types prevent fractional evasion

Add a guard candidate \(q\). The complete candidate set is now

\(C=\{q,p,v_1,\ldots,v_N\},\qquad m=N+2,\)

and the scoring vector is \((k+2)\)-approval. It satisfies all the required natural-scoring bounds.

Set \(M=2D+1\). There are exactly two types, each of mass \(1/2\).

Active type. Its initial ranking is

\(q\succ v_1\succ\cdots\succ v_N\succ p.\)

Costs among \(p,v_1,\ldots,v_N\) are those from Section 2. Every swap involving \(q\) costs \(M\), in either direction.

Padding type. Its initial ranking begins

\(q\succ p\succ v_1\succ\cdots\succ v_N.\)

Every swap costs \(M\), in either direction. In particular, both \(q\) and \(p\) are initially approved.

We establish three cost bounds.

  • Every active ranking approving both \(p\) and \(q\) costs at least \(K\). Deleting \(q\) from such a ranking leaves at most \(k\) vertices before \(p\), because both \(p\) and \(q\) occupy the first \(k+2\) positions. The non-guard inversion costs are therefore at least \(K\).
  • An active ranking approving both candidates with cost exactly \(K\) exists: put \(q\) first, followed by a minimum-cost core ranking approving \(p\).
  • In either type, a ranking disapproving \(q\) costs at least \(M\). Since \(q\) starts first, such a ranking must reverse its order with another candidate, incurring an inversion of cost \(M\).

Now consider an arbitrary feasible fractional solution. Let

  • \(\alpha\) be the active mass disapproving \(p\);
  • \(\beta\) be the active mass disapproving \(q\);
  • \(\eta\) be the padding mass disapproving \(p\);
  • \(\gamma\) be the padding mass disapproving \(q\).

These are absolute masses, each at most \(1/2\). Put \(\varepsilon=\beta+\gamma\).

Under approval scoring, the total scores of \(p\) and \(q\) are respectively

\(1-\alpha-\eta\quad\text{and}\quad1-\beta-\gamma.\)

Because \(p\) must beat or tie \(q\), feasibility implies

\(\alpha+\eta\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\text{hence }\alpha\leq\varepsilon.\)

Let \(b\) be the active mass approving both \(p\) and \(q\). The union bound gives

\(b\geq\tfrac12-\alpha-\beta.\)

The mass counted by \(b\) and the mass disapproving \(q\) are disjoint. Applying the established cost bounds yields

\(\operatorname{cost}\geq Kb+M\varepsilon \geq\tfrac K2-K(\alpha+\beta)+M\varepsilon.\)

Since \(\alpha\leq\varepsilon\) and \(\beta\leq\varepsilon\),

\(\operatorname{cost}\geq\tfrac K2+(M-2K)\varepsilon\geq\tfrac K2,\)

where the last inequality uses \(K\leq D\) and \(M=2D+1\).

Conversely, leave the padding type unchanged and transform all active mass to the cost-\(K\) ranking approving both \(p\) and \(q\). This costs \(K/2\). Both candidates then score \(1\), and every candidate scores at most \(1\), so \(p\) wins.

Thus the continuous optimum is exactly

\(\operatorname{OPT}=K/2.\)

4. Hardness conclusion

Use designated candidate \(c^\star=p\) and budget \(B/2\). The preceding sections prove

\(\operatorname{OPT}\leq B/2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K\leq B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\text{ contains a }k\text{-clique}.\)

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. There are \(N+2\) candidates, two explicitly listed types, masses \(1/2\), and polynomially bounded integer costs. The scoring vector and budget have polynomial encoding length.

Therefore the continuous budget decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard, and computing the exact minimum is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard as well. The argument permits arbitrary divisible masses throughout.

5. Fixed-parameter algorithm in the number of candidates

Enumerate all \(m!\) strict rankings. For every type and ranking, calculate \(d_t(\pi)\) using the inversion formula in \(O(m^2)\) time.

Then explicitly construct the rational linear program from the question. It has

\(\tau m!\) variables, \(\tau\) mass equalities, and \(m-1\) winner inequalities, together with nonnegativity constraints.

The program is feasible: assign every type entirely to a ranking placing \(c^\star\) first. Every candidate then receives at most the score of \(c^\star\). Its feasible set is also bounded and closed, because each type’s variables form a simplex of mass \(\mu_t\). Hence the minimum is attained.

All coefficients have polynomial encoding length. In particular, if \(\sigma_{\max}\) is the largest input swap cost, then

\(d_t(\pi)\leq\binom m2\sigma_{\max}.\)

Polynomial-time exact rational linear programming therefore computes the optimum in time polynomial in the explicitly constructed LP’s encoding length. That length is bounded by \(m!\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), giving total running time

\((m!)^{O(1)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L) =2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L).\)

This proves \(\mathsf{FPT}\) parameterized by \(m\), using the actual support size \(\tau\).
