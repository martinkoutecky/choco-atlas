The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools, a finite support of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita capacities, and efficiently represented substitutable, LAD, acceptant choice rules over applicant-mass vectors, determine whether the induced legal set of mass allocations has a student-optimal member and compute it. Define blocking by testing whether a type with mass assigned below school \(a\) in one allocation could have an infinitesimal tagged applicant admitted by \(a\), with another allocation assigning positive mass of that type to \(a\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) student types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a strict preference \(\succ_t\) over \(m\) schools and the unassigned option \(\bot\); schools have binary-rational per-capita capacities \(q_a\geq0\).
Each school has a choice map \(C_a:[0,\mu]\to[0,\mu]\), represented by a finite circuit of rational affine operations, minimum, maximum, and conditional branches testing weak affine inequalities. The maps are promised to satisfy \(0\leq C_a(z)\leq z\), substitutability \(\min\{C_a(w),z\}\leq C_a(z)\) for \(z\leq w\), LAD \(\sum_t C_{a,t}(z)\leq\sum_t C_{a,t}(w)\), and acceptance \(\sum_t C_{a,t}(z)=\min\{q_a,\sum_t z_t\}\); vector inequalities and minimum are coordinatewise.
An allocation is a real matrix \(x\geq0\) with \(\sum_{b\in O\cup\{\bot\}}x_{tb}=\mu_t\). Let \(\mathrm{IR}\) contain exactly those allocations assigning no mass below \(\bot\) and satisfying \(C_a(x_a)=x_a\) at every school, where \(x_a=(x_{ta})_t\). Wasteful allocations remain eligible.
For \(z=C_a(z)\) and \(z_t<\mu_t\), an infinitesimal tagged applicant of type \(t\) is admitted when some \(\delta>0\) satisfies \(C_{a,t}(z+\varepsilon e_t)=z_t+\varepsilon\) for every \(0<\varepsilon<\delta\) within the domain; incumbent mass of the same type takes precedence over the tag.
Allocation \(y\) blocks allocation \(x\) if some \(t,a,b\) satisfy \(x_{tb}>0\), \(a\succ_t b\), \(y_{ta}>0\), and the tagged applicant is admitted against \(x_a\). This includes admission to a vacant seat.
A set \(\mathcal L\subseteq\mathrm{IR}\) is legal when no member blocks any member and every allocation outside \(\mathcal L\) is blocked by a member. A member \(x^*\) is student-optimal when \(\sum_{b\succeq_t c}x^*_{tb}\geq\sum_{b\succeq_t c}y_{tb}\) for every \(y\in\mathcal L\), type \(t\), and cutoff \(c\).
Is deciding whether some legal set has a student-optimal member in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard? When one exists, can such a member be computed exactly in polynomial time? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), including the choice circuits.
In finite societies, substitutable, LAD choice functions yield a unique legal set with a student-optimal assignment; with acceptant choices, that assignment coincides with simplified efficiency-adjusted deferred acceptance. A later version also gives a polynomial algorithm for general substitutable, LAD choices ([Ehlers–Morrill](https://thayermorrill.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/12/Illegal.School.Assignments.pdf)).
No result settling this precise mass-allocation problem was located.

  • Normalized total student mass to one, so capacities are per-capita quantities; allowed nonnegative rational capacities, including zero.

  • Specified coordinatewise mass substitutability, mass LAD, and exact capacity-filling acceptance on the available-mass box.

Partial results for the mass-allocation problem

I do not settle the general $\mathrm P$ versus NP-hardness question. The following results substantially narrow the remaining problem:

  • Every legal set, if one exists, has a unique student-optimal member. Thus the decision question reduces to existence of a legal set.
  • The unrestricted mass market always has a student-optimal stable allocation.
  • Given the support mask of a legal set, its student-optimal member can be computed exactly in

$$ 2^{O(L)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time and has polynomial encoding length.

  • The general decision problem belongs to $\Sigma_2^p$, and exact decision and computation are possible in

$$ 2^{O(\tau L+m\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time.

  • If all types’ acceptable-school lists fit one common school order, the unique legal set is a singleton computable in polynomial time. This includes $m=1$ with arbitrary acceptability.
  • A two-type, three-school example shows that the student-optimal legal member can be strictly Pareto dominated by another eligible allocation. Hence the finite theorem’s efficiency conclusion does not extend to this mass model.

All claims below are proved directly for the stated mass choices.

1. Eligible universe and definitions

I interpret external blocking as quantified over $\mathrm{IR}\setminus\mathcal L$. Under the literal interpretation quantifying over all allocation matrices, one type with one acceptable school of capacity $1/2$ already gives a different obstruction: assigning all mass to that school is ineligible but cannot be blocked, because no mass is assigned below the school. The remainder addresses the intended eligible universe $\mathrm{IR}$.

Write $O$ for the school set. A school is acceptable to type $t$ when $a\succ_t\bot$.

For an eligible allocation $x$, define
$$ D_{ta}(x)=\sum_{b:\,a\succ_t b}x_{tb}. $$
Let $R_t(z)$ replace coordinate $t$ of $z$ by $\mu_t$, and define
$$ G_{ta}(x)=C_{a,t}(R_t(x_a))-x_{ta}. $$
We will prove that $G_{ta}(x)\geq0$ and that, whenever $D_{ta}(x)>0$, a tag is admitted precisely when $G_{ta}(x)>0$.

Define
$$ B(x)=\{(t,a):D_{ta}(x)>0,\ G_{ta}(x)>0\}. $$
Call $x$ stable when $B(x)=\varnothing$. In particular, no allocation can block a stable allocation.

2. Consequences of the choice promises

Throughout this section, fix one school and abbreviate its choice map by $C$.

2.1. Individual rationality is a capacity condition

For every $z\in[0,\mu]$,
$$ C(z)=z \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_t z_t\leq q. $$

Indeed, if $C(z)=z$, acceptance gives
$$ \sum_t z_t=\min\left\{q,\sum_t z_t\right\}, $$
so $\sum_t z_t\leq q$. Conversely, when $\sum_t z_t\leq q$, acceptance and $C(z)\leq z$ give equality of the coordinate sums. Coordinatewise domination then forces $C(z)=z$.

Consequently, $\mathrm{IR}$ is the rational polytope specified by nonnegativity, row sums, zero assignments below $\bot$, and school capacity inequalities. The choice circuits affect blocking, but not this eligible polytope.

2.2. Rejection is monotone and continuous

Define the rejection map
$$ \mathcal R(z)=z-C(z). $$

Lemma. If $z\leq w$, then $\mathcal R(z)\leq\mathcal R(w)$. Moreover,
$$ \|\mathcal R(z)-\mathcal R(w)\|_1\leq\|z-w\|_1 $$
for arbitrary $z,w$.

Proof. Fix a coordinate $t$ and suppose $z\leq w$.

If $C_t(w)\leq C_t(z)$, then
$$ w_t-C_t(w)\geq z_t-C_t(z). $$
Otherwise, substitutability implies
$$ \min\{C_t(w),z_t\}\leq C_t(z). $$
Since $C_t(w)>C_t(z)$ and $C_t(z)\leq z_t$, this forces $C_t(z)=z_t$. Thus $\mathcal R_t(z)=0\leq\mathcal R_t(w)$.

This proves coordinatewise monotonicity. For comparable inputs, LAD gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \|\mathcal R(w)-\mathcal R(z)\|_1 &=\sum_t\bigl(\mathcal R_t(w)-\mathcal R_t(z)\bigr)\\ &=\sum_t(w_t-z_t)-\sum_t\bigl(C_t(w)-C_t(z)\bigr)\\ &\leq\|w-z\|_1. \end{aligned} $$
For arbitrary inputs, put $u=z\vee w$. Applying the comparable-input bound twice yields
$$ \begin{aligned} \|\mathcal R(z)-\mathcal R(w)\|_1 &\leq\|u-z\|_1+\|u-w\|_1\\ &=\|z-w\|_1. \end{aligned} $$
This proves the lemma. $\square$

Since $C(z)=z-\mathcal R(z)$, it follows that
$$ \|C(z)-C(w)\|_1\leq2\|z-w\|_1. $$
Thus the promised output maps are continuous, notwithstanding conditional branches in their circuits.

2.3. Own-type choice is a truncation

Fix all inputs except coordinate $t$, and let $v(s)$ denote the vector whose coordinate $t$ is $s$. Put
$$ k=C_t(v(\mu_t)). $$
Then
$$ C_t(v(s))=\min\{s,k\} \qquad (0\leq s\leq\mu_t). $$

Proof. For $s\leq r$, substitutability makes every other output coordinate weakly decrease:
$$ C_i(v(r))\leq C_i(v(s)) \qquad(i\neq t). $$
LAD therefore makes coordinate $t$ weakly increase. Hence
$$ C_t(v(s))\leq k. $$
Also $C_t(v(s))\leq s$. In the opposite direction, substitutability applied to $v(s)\leq v(\mu_t)$ gives
$$ \min\{k,s\}\leq C_t(v(s)). $$
The bounds prove the formula. $\square$

For a fixed point $z=C(z)$ with $z_t<\mu_t$, this formula gives
$$ C_t(z+\varepsilon e_t) = \min\{z_t+\varepsilon,C_t(R_t(z))\}. $$
Therefore the tag is admitted exactly when
$$ C_t(R_t(z))>z_t. $$
This also proves $G_{ta}(x)\geq0$.

Because $D_{ta}(x)>0$ implies $x_{ta}<\mu_t$, the domain condition for tagging is automatic whenever a blocking preference exists. We consequently have the exact characterization
$$ y\text{ blocks }x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists(t,a)\in B(x)\text{ with }y_{ta}>0. $$

2.4. Intermediate inputs preserve choice

We will also use
$$ C(v)\leq w\leq v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C(w)=C(v). $$

Substitutability gives $C(v)\leq C(w)$, while LAD gives
$$ \sum_t C_t(w)\leq\sum_t C_t(v). $$
These inequalities force equality coordinatewise. In particular,
$$ C(C(v))=C(v). $$

3. Restricted markets always have a student-optimal stable allocation

Let $E$ be any subset of acceptable type–school pairs. A restricted allocation assigns positive school mass only at pairs in $E$.

Call a restricted allocation $E$-stable when
$$ B(x)\cap E=\varnothing. $$
Thus tags may block only at allowed pairs.

Theorem 1

For every such $E$, there is a unique student-optimal $E$-stable allocation.

Proof: a monotone rejection system

For each type $t$, list its allowed schools in preference order:
$$ a_{t1}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t a_{tk_t}\succ_t\bot. $$
Introduce variables
$$ p_{tj}\in[0,\mu_t], \qquad 1\leq j\leq k_t+1. $$
The terminal coordinate will represent unassigned mass.

For a school $a$, form its offer vector $\pi_a(p)$ by
$$ \pi_{a,t}(p)= \begin{cases} p_{tj},&a=a_{tj},\\ 0,&(t,a)\notin E. \end{cases} $$
Write $\mathcal R_a(z)=z-C_a(z)$.

Define a map $T$ on the product box by
$$ T_{t1}(p)=\mu_t $$
and
$$ T_{t,j+1}(p) = \mathcal R_{a_{tj},t}(\pi_{a_{tj}}(p)) \qquad (1\leq j\leq k_t). $$
The rejection lemma shows that $T$ is monotone. It maps the product box into itself and is continuous.

Starting from $p^0=0$, iterate
$$ p^{n+1}=T(p^n). $$
The sequence is coordinatewise nondecreasing: $p^1\geq p^0$, and monotonicity propagates this inequality. It is bounded by the product box, so it has a coordinatewise limit $p^*$. Continuity gives
$$ T(p^*)=p^*. $$

Moreover, $p^*$ is the least fixed point. If $u=T(u)$, then $p^0\leq u$, and induction gives $p^n\leq u$ for every $n$. Hence $p^*\leq u$.

Fixed points produce stable allocations

For any fixed point $p$, define
$$ x_{t,a_{tj}}=p_{tj}-p_{t,j+1}, \qquad x_{t\bot}=p_{t,k_t+1}, $$
and set all other school entries to zero.

The fixed-point equations imply
$$ p_{tj}-p_{t,j+1} = C_{a_{tj},t}(\pi_{a_{tj}}(p)). $$
Thus these entries are nonnegative, and telescoping gives the required row sum $\mu_t$. Each school column is $C_a(\pi_a(p))$, so idempotence makes it individually rational.

At an allowed school $a=a_{tj}$, the mass of type $t$ assigned below $a$ equals
$$ D_{ta}(x)=p_{t,j+1}. $$
Suppose this quantity is positive. Put
$$ c=C_a(\pi_a(p))=x_a. $$
Then $c_t<p_{tj}$. For every sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$,
$$ c\leq c+\varepsilon e_t\leq\pi_a(p). $$
The intermediate-input property gives
$$ C_a(c+\varepsilon e_t)=c. $$
The tag is therefore rejected. This proves $E$-stability.

Stable allocations produce fixed points

Conversely, let $y$ be $E$-stable. Define
$$ p_{tj}(y)=\mu_t-\sum_{i<j}y_{t,a_{ti}}. $$
For the terminal coordinate this equals $y_{t\bot}$.

Fix a school $a$ and put $w=\pi_a(p(y))$. For allowed pairs,
$$ w_t=y_{ta}+D_{ta}(y), $$
while forbidden pairs have $w_t=y_{ta}=0$. Hence $w\geq y_a$.

We claim $C_a(w)=y_a$. If $w_t=y_{ta}$, the upper bound $C_a(w)\leq w$ gives $C_{a,t}(w)\leq y_{ta}$.

If $w_t>y_{ta}$, then $(t,a)\in E$ and $D_{ta}(y)>0$. Stability implies $G_{ta}(y)=0$. The own-type truncation formula consequently gives
$$ C_{a,t}\bigl(y_a+(w_t-y_{ta})e_t\bigr)=y_{ta}. $$
This intermediate vector is at most $w$ and has the same coordinate $t$ as $w$. Substitutability therefore implies
$$ C_{a,t}(w)\leq y_{ta}. $$
Thus $C_a(w)\leq y_a$. On the other hand, LAD and $w\geq y_a$ give
$$ \sum_t C_{a,t}(w) \geq \sum_t C_{a,t}(y_a) = \sum_t y_{ta}. $$
Hence $C_a(w)=y_a$.

It follows that
$$ \mathcal R_{a_{tj},t}(\pi_{a_{tj}}(p(y))) = p_{tj}(y)-y_{t,a_{tj}} = p_{t,j+1}(y). $$
Thus $p(y)$ is a fixed point of $T$.

Least offers give best student welfare

For every prefix of the allowed school list,
$$ \sum_{i=1}^j x_{t,a_{ti}} = \mu_t-p_{t,j+1}. $$
Since $p^*\leq p(y)$ for every $E$-stable $y$, the allocation constructed from $p^*$ maximizes every such prefix simultaneously.

For any cutoff in the original preference list, its positively assigned school entries form a prefix of the allowed list. The same inequalities therefore establish student optimality for every original cutoff.

Finally, two student-optimal allocations must have equal cumulative sums at every cutoff, and successive differences determine every entry. Thus the student-optimal allocation is unique. $\square$

Taking $E$ to be all acceptable pairs proves existence of a student-optimal stable allocation in the original market.

The iteration above is an existence proof. It supplies no polynomial bound on the convergence process.

4. Every legal set has a student-optimal member

For an allocation family $A$, define its school-support mask
$$ S(A)=\{(t,a):y_{ta}>0\text{ for some }y\in A\}. $$
For a mask $S$, put
$$ K(S)=\{x\in\mathrm{IR}:B(x)\cap S=\varnothing\}. $$
Equivalently,
$$ x\in K(S) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in\mathrm{IR} \text{ and } \bigl(D_{ta}(x)=0\text{ or }G_{ta}(x)=0\bigr) \text{ for every }(t,a)\in S. $$

Lemma 2: legal-mask characterization

A family $\mathcal L$ is legal exactly when
$$ \mathcal L=K(S) \quad\text{and}\quad S(K(S))=S $$
for some mask $S$.

Proof. Suppose $\mathcal L$ is legal and set $S=S(\mathcal L)$. Internal nonblocking implies $\mathcal L\subseteq K(S)$.

If $x\in K(S)\setminus\mathcal L$, external blocking supplies a member $y\in\mathcal L$ and a pair in $B(x)$ at which $y$ has positive mass. That pair belongs to $S$, contradicting $x\in K(S)$. Hence $\mathcal L=K(S)$.

Conversely, suppose both displayed equalities hold. Every member has school support contained in $S$, and every member’s blocking pairs avoid $S$, so internal blocking is impossible. If $x\notin K(S)$, a pair in $B(x)\cap S$ has positive mass in some member of $K(S)$, which blocks $x$. $\square$

Theorem 3: conditional existence of the legal optimum

Every legal set has a unique student-optimal member.

Proof. Let $\mathcal L$ be legal and put $E=S(\mathcal L)$. All pairs in $E$ are acceptable.

By Lemma 2, $\mathcal L=K(E)$, and every member has support contained in $E$. Consequently, $\mathcal L$ is exactly the set of $E$-stable allocations:

  • a member is restricted to $E$ and satisfies $B(x)\cap E=\varnothing$;
  • every $E$-stable allocation belongs to $K(E)=\mathcal L$.

Theorem 1 now supplies its unique student-optimal member. $\square$

Thus
$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{some legal set has a student-optimal member}\\ &\qquad\Longleftrightarrow \text{some legal set exists}. \end{aligned} $$

There is also a useful necessary inclusion. Every unrestricted stable allocation belongs to every legal set, because no allocation blocks it. Therefore a student-optimal legal member, whenever one exists, dominates the student-optimal unrestricted stable allocation.

5. Exact algorithms and encoding bounds

Let $G$ be the total number of gates in the choice circuits; in particular, $G\leq L$.

I use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and linear optimization for an explicitly given rational linear system take time polynomial in its encoding length. A nonempty bounded rational polyhedron has rational optimal vertices whose encoding lengths are polynomial in that system’s length.

5.1. A legal support mask permits a smaller exact algorithm

Suppose a mask $E$ defining a legal set is supplied. Construct the map $T$ from Section 3, and consider its supersolutions:
$$ Q=\{p\text{ in the product box}:T(p)\leq p\}. $$

The least fixed point $p^*$ is below every supersolution. Indeed, if $u\in Q$, induction gives
$$ p^n\leq u $$
because $p^0=0\leq u$ and
$$ p^{n+1}=T(p^n)\leq T(u)\leq u. $$
Taking limits proves $p^*\leq u$.

Consequently, $p^*$ is the unique minimizer of
$$ \sum_{t,j}p_{tj} $$
over $Q$. It belongs to $Q$, and any distinct supersolution has a strictly larger sum.

Computing $T$ requires one copy of each school’s choice circuit. Resolve all minimum, maximum, and conditional choices. There are at most $2^{O(L)}$ resolutions. Introducing gate-value variables turns each resolution into a rational linear system of polynomial description length.

Some branch inequalities are strict. To test a resolved system, replace each strict inequality $h(v)<0$ by
$$ h(v)\leq-\eta, \qquad 0\leq\eta\leq1, $$
and maximize $\eta$. The original system is feasible exactly when this program is feasible with positive optimum.

For a feasible resolved system, replacing strict inequalities by weak ones gives its closure. To see this, fix a strictly feasible point $v_0$. Every weakly feasible point $v$ is approached by
$$ (1-\lambda)v+\lambda v_0 \qquad(\lambda\downarrow0), $$
which satisfies every original strict inequality.

Each closure is bounded: allocation-offer variables are bounded, and resolved gate values are affine functions of those variables. Its projection lies in $Q$, since $Q$ is closed by continuity of $T$. Conversely, every point of $Q$ follows some circuit resolution. Thus $Q$ is exactly the union of these projected closures.

Minimize the displayed objective over every feasible closure and take the smallest value. Uniqueness of the minimizing projection yields $p^*$, and Section 3 yields the desired allocation.

This proves the exact running time
$$ 2^{O(L)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
The rational-vertex bound gives polynomial encoding length for $p^*$; exact circuit evaluation then gives polynomial encoding length for its allocation.

This is an exponential algorithm, even when the legal mask is supplied.

5.2. Searching for a legal mask

The set $\mathrm{IR}$ is compact. Both $D_{ta}$ and $G_{ta}$ are continuous, so every $K(S)$ is compact.

Moreover, every $K(S)$ is nonempty: an unrestricted stable allocation belongs to all of them.

Membership in $K(S)$ can be represented using:

  • the rational allocation and capacity constraints;
  • at most $\tau$ copies of each school circuit, evaluating the needed quantities $C_{a,t}(R_t(x_a))$;
  • at most $m\tau$ disjunctions $D_{ta}=0$ or $G_{ta}=0$.

Resolving all circuits and disjunctions gives at most
$$ 2^{O(\tau L+m\tau)} $$
polynomial-size rational linear systems.

The same strict-feasibility and closure argument applies. Closedness of $K(S)$ ensures that closure projections introduce no spurious allocations. Therefore linear optimization over $K(S)$ reduces to optimization over these finitely many bounded rational polyhedra.

For each pair, compute
$$ h_{ta}(S)=\max_{x\in K(S)}x_{ta}. $$
Then
$$ S(K(S))=\{(t,a):h_{ta}(S)>0\}. $$
These maxima test the legal-mask equality from Lemma 2.

Enumerate all $2^{m\tau}$ masks. If a legal mask is found, use Section 5.1 to compute its student-optimal member. If none is found, reject.

The resulting total running time is
$$ 2^{O(\tau L+m\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
All numerical calculations and outputs are exact.

5.3. Polynomial-hierarchy upper bound

The decision problem belongs to $\Sigma_2^p$ on promised inputs.

The polyhedral representation supplies polynomial-size rational witnesses whenever
$$ x\in K(S),\qquad x_{ta}>0 $$
is feasible: maximize the relevant coordinate over a branch closure and choose a rational optimal vertex. Its projection belongs to $K(S)$ and has positive coordinate.

A nondeterministic polynomial-time algorithm with an NP oracle can therefore:

  • Guess a mask $S$.
  • For each pair in $S$, guess a polynomial-size rational member of $K(S)$ having positive mass at that pair.
  • Verify these witnesses by exact circuit evaluation.
  • Ask the NP oracle whether some $z\in K(S)$ has positive mass at a pair outside $S$.
  • Accept precisely when the oracle answers no.

The witnesses establish $S\subseteq S(K(S))$, and the negative oracle answer establishes the reverse inclusion. Lemma 2 gives legality, and Theorem 3 supplies the student-optimal member.

Conversely, a legal mask has all required polynomial-size support witnesses. This proves the upper bound.

6. A polynomial case: compatible acceptable-school orders

Construct a directed graph on schools, placing an arc $a\to b$ whenever some type ranks both schools above $\bot$ and has $a\succ_t b$.

Assume this graph is acyclic. Choose a topological order
$$ a_1,\ldots,a_m. $$
For each type, its acceptable-school list is a subsequence of this order.

Start with $r^0=\mu$. At stage $j$, define
$$ v^j_t= \begin{cases} r^{j-1}_t,&a_j\succ_t\bot,\\ 0,&\bot\succ_t a_j, \end{cases} $$
and set
$$ x^*_{a_j}=C_{a_j}(v^j), \qquad r^j=r^{j-1}-x^*_{a_j}. $$
Assign $r^m$ to $\bot$.

Theorem 4

Under this acyclicity hypothesis, the unique legal set is $\{x^*\}$. Its member is computable exactly in $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time.

Proof. The construction preserves nonnegative residuals and row sums. It assigns no unacceptable mass, and idempotence makes each school column individually rational.

Fix an acceptable pair $(t,a_j)$ and write $c=x^*_{a_j}$. Because all earlier acceptable schools are preferred to $a_j$, the mass below $a_j$ equals
$$ r^{j-1}_t-c_t=v^j_t-c_t. $$
If this is positive, sufficiently small perturbations satisfy
$$ c\leq c+\varepsilon e_t\leq v^j. $$
The intermediate-input property rejects the tag. At an unacceptable school, $D_{ta_j}(x^*)=0$. Hence $x^*$ is stable.

Now let $x\neq x^*$ belong to $\mathrm{IR}$, and choose the first school $a_j$ at which its column differs. Earlier columns agree, so both allocations have residual $r^{j-1}$ before this school.

Put
$$ z=x_{a_j}, \qquad c=x^*_{a_j}. $$
Individual rationality and acceptability imply $z\leq v^j$, and $C_{a_j}(z)=z$. LAD gives
$$ \sum_t z_t\leq\sum_t c_t. $$
Since $z\neq c$, some coordinate satisfies $c_t>z_t$.

Let
$$ w=z\vee c. $$
Then $c\leq w\leq v^j$, so $C_{a_j}(w)=c$. For
$$ u=z+(c_t-z_t)e_t\leq w, $$
substitutability gives
$$ c_t=\min\{C_{a_j,t}(w),u_t\}\leq C_{a_j,t}(u)\leq u_t=c_t. $$
Thus $C_{a_j,t}(u)=c_t$. Own-type truncation implies that the admission threshold against $z$ is at least $c_t>z_t$, so the tag is admitted.

The type is acceptable at $a_j$, since $c_t>0$. Its mass below $a_j$ in $x$ is
$$ r^{j-1}_t-z_t\geq c_t-z_t>0. $$
Since $x^*_{t,a_j}=c_t>0$, allocation $x^*$ blocks $x$.

Therefore $\{x^*\}$ is legal. Every legal set must contain the unblocked allocation $x^*$, and internal nonblocking excludes all other allocations. This proves uniqueness.

The preference graph can be constructed in $O(\tau m^2)$ comparisons. The allocation requires one evaluation of each school circuit and $O(m\tau)$ further rational operations. Exact rational evaluation has polynomial bit complexity, proving the claimed time bound. $\square$

This includes common preferences, $\tau=1$, and $m=1$ with arbitrary rankings relative to $\bot$.

7. The finite efficiency property fails for mass allocations

Here is an explicit promised instance whose unique legal member is Pareto inefficient.

There are two types, each of mass $1/2$, and three schools:
$$ q_A=q_B=\frac38, \qquad q_D=\frac14. $$
Preferences are
$$ A\succ_1 B\succ_1 D\succ_1\bot, \qquad B\succ_2 A\succ_2 D\succ_2\bot. $$

At a school of capacity $q$, use
$$ \begin{aligned} C_{q,1}(z)&=\min\left\{z_1,\max\left\{\frac q2,q-z_2\right\}\right\},\\ C_{q,2}(z)&=\min\left\{z_2,\max\left\{\frac q2,q-z_1\right\}\right\}. \end{aligned} $$
These are constant-size rational affine, minimum, and maximum circuits.

Verification of the promises

Nonnegativity and $C_q(z)\leq z$ are immediate.

For substitutability, write
$$ k_t(s)=\max\left\{\frac q2,q-s\right\}. $$
This function is nonincreasing. For $z\leq w$, with $s$ denoting the other type,
$$ \begin{aligned} \min\{C_{q,t}(w),z_t\} &=\min\{z_t,k_t(w_s)\}\\ &\leq\min\{z_t,k_t(z_s)\}\\ &=C_{q,t}(z). \end{aligned} $$

For acceptance:

  • If $z_1+z_2\leq q$, then $q-z_s\geq z_t$, so both coordinates are accepted completely.
  • If $z_1+z_2\geq q$ and both inputs are at least $q/2$, both outputs equal $q/2$.
  • If $z_1+z_2\geq q$ and, for example, $z_1<q/2$, then the outputs are $z_1$ and $q-z_1$. The other case is symmetric.

Thus
$$ C_{q,1}(z)+C_{q,2}(z)=\min\{q,z_1+z_2\}. $$
This proves acceptance, and also LAD because the right-hand side is monotone in the input.

At a full school, $z_1+z_2=q$. Since here $z_t\leq q<\mu_t$, the admission test becomes
$$ \text{type }t\text{ is admitted} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z_t<\frac q2. $$

The unique stable allocation

Consider
$$ \begin{array}{c|cccc} & A&B&D&\bot\\ \hline 1&3/16&3/16&1/8&0\\ 2&3/16&3/16&1/8&0 \end{array} $$
and denote it by $x^0$. All schools are full, and every type receives exactly half of each school’s capacity. No tag is admitted, so $x^0$ is stable.

We show it is the only stable allocation.

In any stable allocation, no type can have positive unassigned mass. Otherwise, because total school capacity is $1$, some school is vacant. That type prefers the vacant school to $\bot$ and is admitted there, contradicting stability. Therefore every school is full.

Write
$$ \alpha=x_{1A}, \qquad \beta=x_{1B}, \qquad \gamma=x_{1D}. $$
Then
$$ \alpha+\beta+\gamma=\frac12, \qquad 0\leq\gamma\leq\frac14. $$

Type $1$ has positive mass below $A$, because $\alpha\leq3/8<1/2$. Stability at $A$ therefore requires
$$ \alpha\geq\frac{3}{16}. $$
Similarly, type $2$ has positive mass below $B$, so
$$ x_{2B}=\frac38-\beta\geq\frac{3}{16}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \beta\leq\frac{3}{16}. $$

If $\gamma=0$, type $2$ has mass $1/4$ at $D$. Stability at $A$ then requires
$$ x_{2A}=\frac38-\alpha\geq\frac{3}{16}, $$
so $\alpha\leq3/16$. Together with $\beta\leq3/16$, this contradicts $\alpha+\beta=1/2$.

If $\gamma=1/4$, type $1$ has positive mass below $B$, so $\beta\geq3/16$. Combined with $\alpha\geq3/16$, this contradicts $\alpha+\beta=1/4$.

Thus $0<\gamma<1/4$. Type $1$ has positive mass below $B$, giving $\beta\geq3/16$. Type $2$ has positive mass below $A$, giving $\alpha\leq3/16$. Therefore
$$ \alpha=\beta=\frac{3}{16}, \qquad \gamma=\frac18. $$
This is exactly $x^0$.

The unique legal set and its inefficiency

Every legal set must contain $x^0$, since it is unblocked. All six type–school entries of $x^0$ are positive. Hence every legal set has the full support mask, and Lemma 2 makes it equal to the stable set. The latter is $\{x^0\}$.

The singleton is indeed legal: every other eligible allocation has some pair in $B(x)$, and $x^0$ has positive mass at that pair, so $x^0$ blocks it.

Nevertheless, for any
$$ 0<\delta\leq\frac{3}{16}, $$
consider
$$ \begin{array}{c|cccc} & A&B&D&\bot\\ \hline 1&3/16+\delta&3/16-\delta&1/8&0\\ 2&3/16-\delta&3/16+\delta&1/8&0. \end{array} $$
This allocation is eligible: row sums and school totals are unchanged. Each type gains $\delta$ at its first-choice school; its cumulative mass through its second-choice school and all lower cutoffs is unchanged. Thus it strictly improves both types in the task’s cumulative-welfare order.

The unique student-optimal legal allocation is therefore Pareto inefficient. This shows directly why the finite efficiency result in [Ehlers–Morrill](https://thayermorrill.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2018/12/Illegal.School.Assignments.pdf) cannot be transferred without a new argument.
