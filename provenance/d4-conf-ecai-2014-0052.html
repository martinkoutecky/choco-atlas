The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(C=\{a,b,p\}\), a rational fixed society distribution \(\nu\) over the \(15\) nonempty top-truncated ballots, rational manipulator mass \(\alpha\) with \(\sum_b\nu_b+\alpha=1\), and target \(p\), choose rational masses \(x_b\ge0\) with \(\sum_b x_b=\alpha\) so that \(p\) wins under the paper's modified Borda rule, with ties favoring \(p\); output such an \(x\) if one exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{a,b,p\}\), with \(m=3\), and let \(B\) be the \(15\) strict ordered lists of distinct candidates of lengths \(1,2,3\); empty ballots are forbidden.
Under modified Borda, a ballot of length \(k<3\) gives its candidate in position \(i\) score \(k-i+1\) and unlisted candidates score \(0\); complete ballots use ordinary Borda. Write \(s_\beta(c)\) for this score.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) ballot types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; omitted types have mass \(0\).
The input is a fixed society \(\nu\) and a binary rational manipulator mass \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), with \(\sum_{\beta\in B}\nu_\beta+\alpha=1\). Manipulator mass may be split freely among all ballots in \(B\).
Decide whether rational masses \(x_\beta\geq0\), with \(\sum_{\beta\in B}x_\beta=\alpha\), make \(p\) win, and produce such masses when they exist. Scores are \(S(c)=\sum_{\beta\in B}(\nu_\beta+x_\beta)s_\beta(c)\); ties favor \(p\), so winning means \(S(p)\geq S(a),S(b)\).
State running times in \(m=3\), \(\tau\leq15\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

Is this continuous manipulation problem solvable in polynomial time, or is its feasibility decision problem NP-hard?

Discrete coalition manipulation with indivisible integer voter weights and these ballot options is NP-hard even with three candidates and ties favoring the target ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.7714)).
Divisible manipulation with complete ballots is polynomial-time solvable for positional scoring rules ([source](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf)); this result does not cover the ballot-length-dependent rule above. No published result settling this exact mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified the scoring convention: incomplete ballots give k-i+1 points to listed candidates and zero to unlisted candidates; complete ballots use ordinary Borda, as required by the discrete definition.
  • Made the ballot domain explicit: strict nonempty lists, including complete rankings, matching the stated 15 options.
  • Specified nonnegative fixed masses, zero masses for omitted types, and manipulator mass in [0,1]; normalization applies to the combined fixed and manipulator society.
  • Retained zero manipulator mass and an empty fixed support as permissible boundary cases because neither the stated question nor the discrete problem imposes a positive-mass restriction.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable, both for deciding feasibility and for producing rational manipulating masses. In fact, whenever manipulation is feasible, two complete ballot types suffice.

For each candidate \(c\), define the fixed-society score
\(F(c)=\sum_{\beta\in B}\nu_\beta s_\beta(c)\).
Define the signed deficits
\(d_a=F(a)-F(p)\) and \(d_b=F(b)-F(p)\).
These deficits may be negative.

Exact criterion. A successful manipulation exists if and only if

\[ d_a\leq 2\alpha,\qquad d_b\leq 2\alpha,\qquad d_a+d_b\leq 3\alpha. \]

When these inequalities hold, set
\(t=\max\{0,d_b-\alpha\}\).
A successful assignment is

\[ x_{(p,a,b)}=t,\qquad x_{(p,b,a)}=\alpha-t, \]

with every other manipulating mass equal to zero.

Necessity

For a ballot \(\beta\), define its contribution to the two winning margins by
\(g_a(\beta)=s_\beta(p)-s_\beta(a)\) and
\(g_b(\beta)=s_\beta(p)-s_\beta(b)\).

Every candidate’s ballot score lies between \(0\) and \(2\). Consequently,
\(g_a(\beta)\leq 2\) and \(g_b(\beta)\leq 2\).

We also have \(g_a(\beta)+g_b(\beta)\leq 3\). To verify this for every permitted length, write
\(T_\beta=s_\beta(a)+s_\beta(b)+s_\beta(p)\). Then
\(g_a(\beta)+g_b(\beta)=3s_\beta(p)-T_\beta\).

  • For length \(1\), \(T_\beta=1\) and \(s_\beta(p)\leq 1\), so this sum is at most \(2\).
  • For length \(2\), the scores are \(2,1,0\), so \(T_\beta=3\) and the sum is at most \(3\).
  • For length \(3\), ordinary Borda likewise gives scores \(2,1,0\), yielding the same bound.

For any manipulating assignment of total mass \(\alpha\), let
\(G_a=\sum_\beta x_\beta g_a(\beta)\) and
\(G_b=\sum_\beta x_\beta g_b(\beta)\).
Multiplying the ballot inequalities by the nonnegative masses and summing gives

\[ G_a\leq 2\alpha,\qquad G_b\leq 2\alpha,\qquad G_a+G_b\leq 3\alpha. \]

The final margins are \(S(p)-S(a)=G_a-d_a\) and
\(S(p)-S(b)=G_b-d_b\).
Thus winning requires \(G_a\geq d_a\) and \(G_b\geq d_b\), which imply all three claimed necessary inequalities.

Sufficiency and construction

Assume the three inequalities hold, and choose
\(t=\max\{0,d_b-\alpha\}\).

First, \(t\geq 0\). Since \(d_b\leq 2\alpha\), we have
\(d_b-\alpha\leq\alpha\); together with \(\alpha\geq 0\), this proves \(t\leq\alpha\).
The proposed masses are therefore nonnegative and sum to exactly \(\alpha\).

The ballot \((p,a,b)\) contributes margins \((1,2)\), while
\((p,b,a)\) contributes margins \((2,1)\). Hence the constructed manipulation gives

\[ G_a=t+2(\alpha-t)=2\alpha-t,\qquad G_b=2t+(\alpha-t)=\alpha+t. \]

By the definition of \(t\), \(\alpha+t\geq d_b\), so \(G_b\geq d_b\).

To check \(G_a\geq d_a\), consider both possible values of \(t\).

  • If \(d_b\leq\alpha\), then \(t=0\) and \(G_a=2\alpha\geq d_a\).
  • If \(d_b>\alpha\), then \(t=d_b-\alpha\), so

\(G_a=3\alpha-d_b\geq d_a\), using \(d_a+d_b\leq3\alpha\).

Both final winning margins are nonnegative. Since ties favor \(p\), this proves sufficiency. All constructed masses are rational because the inputs are rational.

The case \(\alpha=0\) is included: the criterion reduces to
\(d_a\leq0\) and \(d_b\leq0\), and the construction assigns zero mass everywhere.

Algorithm and bit complexity

The algorithm computes the fixed scores from the \(\tau\) listed support types, tests the three inequalities, and either reports infeasibility or returns the explicit masses above. Omitted fixed ballot types require no processing.

For \(m=3\), score aggregation and construction use
\(O(m\tau+1)=O(\tau+1)\) exact arithmetic operations. This counts arithmetic operations, not unit-cost operations on arbitrarily long integers.

For an explicit bit bound, write the input masses as numerator–denominator pairs in binary. Let \(D\) be the product of all their denominators, including the denominator of \(\alpha\). Its bit length is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the input denominator lengths is at most \(L\). Convert every mass to denominator \(D\) using exact integer division.

All subsequent score sums, deficits, comparisons, and output masses can be computed with this same denominator. Since the total mass is \(1\) and ballot scores are at most \(2\), every intermediate integer has \(O(L)\) bits. Using elementary quadratic-time integer arithmetic gives a bit-operation bound of
\(O((\tau+1)L^2)\).

Therefore, with \(m=3\) and \(\tau\leq15\), both decision and construction take \(O(L^2)\) bit operations. The output has \(O(L)\) total bit length, even if all \(15\) masses are written explicitly.

Relation to discrete hardness

The cited discrete hardness theorem requires each manipulating voter’s integer weight to remain indivisible. Its partition reduction forces equal total weight on two ballot types. Divisible mass can always be split equally, so that obstruction disappears. See [Proposition 4 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.7714).

The proof above establishes the exact mass result directly; it does not require extending the [complete-ballot divisible-manipulation theorem](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf) to a ballot-length-dependent scoring rule.

Thus feasibility belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\). It could be NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
