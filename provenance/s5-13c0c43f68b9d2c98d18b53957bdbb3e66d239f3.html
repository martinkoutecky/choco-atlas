Rigorous partial solution

The general complexity classification remains unresolved. The main new result is that eventual nonemptiness is automatic on promised inputs:

For every promised input, every $N\geq2$, every coalition cap $K$, and every $\delta\in(0,1)$, there exists a qualifying mixed stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium. Consequently, $E_N\neq\varnothing$ for every $N$.

The proof gives a finite recursion that computes one member of $E_N$. Its running time is
$$ O\!\left(kN^k\,\operatorname{poly}(L+\log N)\right), \qquad k=\min(K,N), $$
with $m=\tau=1$. This does not characterize every equilibrium or give a polynomial-time algorithm for the population-limit decision problem.

I also prove the complete answer for $K=1$ and give a promised $K=2$ instance with a nonconvergent equilibrium selection.

1. Preliminary facts

All continuation payoffs below are measured before any additional rejection discounting.

Every active agent has a strategy guaranteeing a nonnegative realized payoff. She rejects whenever she is a respondent. As proposer, she proposes her singleton when at least two slots remain. Singleton worth is nonnegative under every completion. In the last slot, she proposes all active agents and assigns the coalition worth to herself. That worth is positive if at least two agents remain and nonnegative otherwise. Endless bargaining pays zero.

Thus every equilibrium continuation payoff of an active agent is nonnegative.

For fixed $N$ and $\delta$, a qualifying stationary equilibrium completes almost surely. Indeed, there are finitely many departed-subpartition and proposer states. The minimum probability of acceptance at these states is some $\varepsilon>0$. The probability of $h$ consecutive rejections before the next departure is at most $(1-\varepsilon)^h$. There are at most $\min(K,N)$ departures. This also proves that the expected number of rejections is finite, for fixed equilibrium and discount factor.

Let $C$ be the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of $W$. Every argument of $W$ lies in $[0,1]$, so $|W|\leq C$. If $D$ is the total number of rejections and the final coalition shares are $x_1,\ldots,x_\ell$, then
$$ q= \mathbb E\!\left[ \delta^D \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} W\!\left(x_i,\sum_hx_h^2,\ldots,\sum_hx_h^{r+1}\right) \right]. $$
Consequently,
$$ 0\leq q\leq \min(K,N)C. $$

Here and below, equilibrium utilities use the ordinary, defined expected-payoff interpretation. These utilities are finite: their nonnegativity excludes infinite negative expectations, while the bounded aggregate realized payoff then excludes an infinite positive expectation for any agent.

2. A recursive existence theorem for arbitrary $K$

Fix $N$ and $\delta\in(0,1)$. We construct equilibria at every admissible state by backward induction on the number of active agents.

A numerical state is the multiset $d$ of departed coalition sizes. Let
$$ n=N-\sum_{t\in d}t $$
be the active population. The strategies will be invariant under permutations of active agents and will depend on departed coalitions only through $d$.

The construction has two additional properties:

  • The numerical final partition from every state is deterministic.
  • If $D_d$ counts future rejections from that state, then

$$ H_d=\mathbb E[\delta^{D_d}]>0 $$
is independent of the designated proposer’s identity.

The terminal state has $H_d=1$. With one active agent, the singleton forms immediately, and again $H_d=1$.

2.1. Quantities obtained from successor states

Suppose $n\geq2$ and the construction is already available after every admissible departure.

An admissible size $s$ satisfies $1\leq s\leq n$, except that $s=n$ is mandatory in the last slot.

For each such $s$, let:

  • $\pi_s$ be the deterministic numerical partition obtained by departing a size-$s$ coalition and then using the constructed successor equilibrium;
  • $H_s$ be the successor’s expected discount factor, with $H_s=1$ if $s=n$;
  • $V_s$ be the proposed coalition’s worth in $\pi_s$;
  • $S_s$ be the sum of the worths of all coalitions formed from the presently active population in $\pi_s$, including the size-$s$ coalition.

Set
$$ A_s=H_sV_s, \qquad B_s=H_s(S_s-V_s). $$

The successor construction gives its active agents nonnegative expected payoffs. Therefore $B_s\geq0$.

The positivity promise supplies an admissible coalition size whose worth is positive under every completion. In particular, its $V_s$ is positive. Since $H_s>0$, define
$$ P=\max_s\frac{A_s}{1+\delta(s-1)}>0. $$
Choose a maximizing size $s$, breaking ties by the largest size. Write
$$ R_{\mathrm a} = \frac{\delta(s-1)P+B_s}{n-1}. $$

The subscript indicates the payoff of a nonproposer conditional on an immediately accepted proposal.

2.2. The accepted proposal

Each proposer samples uniformly a size-$s$ coalition containing herself. For every admissible completion, she offers each respondent the constant transfer
$$ \frac{\delta P}{H_s}, $$
and assigns the remaining coalition worth to herself.

These transfers are feasible in every completion. Under the constructed successor play, each respondent’s expected discounted payoff is $\delta P$, and the proposer’s is
$$ A_s-\delta(s-1)P=P. $$

For any specified nonproposer, the probability of inclusion is $(s-1)/(n-1)$. Conditional on exclusion, symmetry of the successor equilibrium divides the expected outsider aggregate $B_s$ equally among the outsiders. Hence her expected payoff is exactly $R_{\mathrm a}$.

2.3. Mixing when rejection is attractive

There are two cases.

Case I: $\delta R_{\mathrm a}\leq P$.

Use the accepted proposal with probability one, and put
$$ R=R_{\mathrm a}, \qquad \alpha=1. $$

Case II: $\delta R_{\mathrm a}>P$.

Put
$$ R=\frac P\delta. $$
In addition to the accepted proposal, use a deliberately rejected proposal: propose all active agents, offer every respondent zero in every completion, and assign the remaining worth to the proposer.

The response rule below makes the last respondent reject this proposal. Because the respondent order is uniform, its rejector is uniform among the $n-1$ nonproposers.

Conditional on this rejection, a specified nonproposer has continuation payoff
$$ D_{\mathrm r} = \frac{\delta}{n-1}\bigl(P+(n-2)R\bigr). $$
Define
$$ \alpha= \frac{R-D_{\mathrm r}}{R_{\mathrm a}-D_{\mathrm r}}. $$

This is a valid, strictly positive acceptance probability. Indeed,
$$ R-D_{\mathrm r} = \frac{P(1-\delta)(n-1+\delta)}{\delta(n-1)}>0, $$
and the case assumption gives $R_{\mathrm a}>R$. Thus
$$ 0<\alpha<1. $$
Moreover,
$$ R=\alpha R_{\mathrm a}+(1-\alpha)D_{\mathrm r}. $$

Every proposer uses the accepted proposal with probability $\alpha$ and the deliberately rejected proposal with probability $1-\alpha$. An accepted proposal gives her $P$. A rejection gives her $\delta R=P$. Her proposed continuation value is therefore $P$, as required.

2.4. Complete response specification

Consider any proposal of an admissible size $t$, with contingent transfers $y_i(\pi)$, and any realized respondent order.

If it is accepted, the successor construction produces $\pi_t$ with expected discount factor $H_t$. Thus respondent $i$’s expected payoff from unanimous acceptance is
$$ a_i=H_t y_i(\pi_t). $$

Specify responses by backward induction in the realized order:

  • If all later respondents will accept, accept exactly when

$$ a_i\geq\delta P. $$
In particular, accept at equality.

  • If a later respondent will reject, reject when $P\geq R$, and accept when $P<R$.

These are optimal responses. Rejecting herself gives $\delta P$. Accepting when a later rejection will occur gives $\delta R$. When no later rejection will occur, acceptance gives $a_i$.

The prescribed accepted proposal is unanimously accepted in every order: every respondent receives expected payoff $\delta P$.

In Case II, $R=P/\delta>P$. For the zero-transfer proposal, the last respondent rejects because $0<\delta P$, and all preceding respondents accept because permitting the later rejection gives $\delta R>\delta P$. Its rejector is therefore the last respondent, as used above.

This specifies responses to above-threshold, below-threshold, and threshold offers, repairing the omission identified in the notes.

2.5. Proposer deviations

For any accepted proposal of size $t$, each respondent must receive expected payoff at least $\delta P$. The proposer’s expected payoff is consequently at most
$$ A_t-\delta(t-1)P\leq P, $$
by the definition of $P$.

Any rejected proposal gives the proposer $\delta R$. This is at most $P$ in Case I and equals $P$ in Case II.

Thus the prescribed proposals are optimal.

These comparisons also rule out deviations involving arbitrarily many future actions. Here is a discounted verification argument. Replace the deviator’s payoff upon retirement by its conditional expected discounted value under the subsequent play of the other agents. This replacement is legitimate because retirement eliminates all her subsequent decisions.

There are finitely many active states. Their constructed continuation values are bounded and nonnegative. The response and proposal comparisons show that every unilateral action has expected retirement reward plus continuation value at most the prescribed value. Truncating after $h$ proposal rounds therefore gives the corresponding finite-horizon inequality.

If the deviator is still active after $h$ rounds, at most $k=\min(K,N)$ rounds have produced departures; the others have produced rejections. The remaining discounted continuation value is bounded by a constant times $\delta^{h-k}$ and tends to zero. Conditional expected retirement rewards have a uniformly bounded positive part: proposer retirement rewards are bounded above by the preceding inequality, and responder retirement rewards under prescribed proposals range over finitely many values. Passing to the limit proves optimality against any deviation with a defined expected payoff.

Together with the inductively established successor equilibria, this proves subgame perfection at every state.

2.6. Completion and the discount recursion

Every proposer has acceptance probability $\alpha>0$. Hence the constructed equilibrium qualifies.

While the numerical state is unchanged, the number of rejections before acceptance is geometric. Its expected discount factor is
$$ g=\frac{\alpha}{1-(1-\alpha)\delta}. $$
The selected numerical partition is independent of proposer identities and rejection histories. Therefore
$$ H_d=gH_s>0. $$

This completes the induction and proves existence for every finite $N$, every cap $K$, and every $\delta\in(0,1)$.

3. Computing one patient-limit member of $E_N$

The recursion above admits a particularly simple exact representation.

The aggregate payoff before the selected departure, conditional on immediate acceptance, is
$$ A_s+B_s=H_sS_s. $$

In Case I, $g=1$, so $H_d=H_s$. In Case II, the aggregate continuation payoff is
$$ P+(n-1)R = \left(1+\frac{n-1}{\delta}\right)P. $$
It also equals $gH_sS_s$. Using
$$ P=\frac{H_sV_s}{1+\delta(s-1)} $$
gives the unified formula
$$ H_d= \min\left\{ H_s,\; \frac{(\delta+n-1)V_s} {\delta[1+\delta(s-1)]S_s} \right\}. $$
For the selected size, $V_s>0$ and $S_s\geq V_s>0$, so this expression is well defined.

3.1. Stabilization near $\delta=1$

For fixed $N$, all the selected numerical partitions stabilize on some interval $(\delta_0,1)$, and every $H_d$ is a rational function there.

This follows by induction.

At successor states, suppose the numerical partitions have stabilized. Their worths are then fixed rational numbers. Each successor $H_s$ is either $1$ or a function of the form
$$ \frac{(\delta+a)V} {\delta[1+\delta(b-1)]S}, $$
for fixed integers $a,b$ and fixed rational numbers $V,S$.

Thus each candidate proposer value
$$ \frac{H_sV_s}{1+\delta(s-1)} $$
is rational. Its numerator has degree at most one and its denominator degree at most three. Its denominator is positive at $\delta=1$.

For two candidates, the difference has the sign of a polynomial of degree at most four. A nonzero polynomial has constant sign sufficiently close to $1$ from the left. If the polynomial is identically zero, the candidates agree throughout that interval. The largest-size tie rule therefore selects a fixed size near $1$.

The minimum in the formula for $H_d$ similarly selects a fixed rational branch near $1$. This completes the induction.

Each limiting discount factor
$$ h_d=\lim_{\delta\uparrow1}H_d $$
is positive. For the stabilized selected size,
$$ h_d= \min\left\{ h_s,\; \frac{nV_s}{sS_s} \right\}>0. $$

At the initial state, let the stabilized numerical partition be $(s_1,\ldots,s_\ell)$ and let $h_\varnothing$ be its limiting discount factor. Put
$$ x_i=\frac{s_i}{N}, \qquad M_N^*=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_i\delta_{x_i}. $$
Writing $\mathsf D_M$ for the probability law concentrated at $M$, we obtain
$$ e_N^*= \left( \mathsf D_{M_N^*}, \; h_\varnothing \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} W\!\left(x_i,\sum_hx_h^2,\ldots,\sum_hx_h^{r+1}\right) \right) \in E_N. $$

In particular,
$$ E_N\neq\varnothing \qquad\text{for every }N\geq2. $$

3.2. Exact comparison and running time

The stabilized branch can be computed without finding $\delta_0$. For a comparison polynomial $p$, expand
$$ p(1-\varepsilon)=\sum_j c_j\varepsilon^j. $$
The first nonzero coefficient determines its sign for sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$. Identically zero comparison polynomials are handled by the stated tie rule.

There are at most
$$ \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}N^j=O(N^{k-1}), \qquad k=\min(K,N), $$
nonterminal departed-size states, using even an ordered representation as a crude upper bound. Each state has at most $N$ candidate sizes.

With degree and $r$ fixed, a candidate partition’s moments and worths can be computed in $O(k)$ rational operations. Their bit lengths are polynomial in $L+\log N$. Indeed, the moments have denominators that are fixed powers of $N$, and evaluating $W$ introduces only a common coefficient denominator and further fixed powers of $N$.

The formula for $H_d$ selects either a successor expression or one new ratio of bounded degree. Its representation therefore does not accumulate symbolic degree with recursion depth. Exact comparisons involve bounded-degree polynomials with polynomial bit length.

Consequently, one member $e_N^*$ can be computed in
$$ O\!\left(kN^k\,\operatorname{poly}(L+\log N)\right) $$
bit operations. Here $m=\tau=1$; no enumeration of a larger universe of agent types is involved.

This bound is polynomial in $N$ for fixed $K$. It is not a polynomial bound in the binary input size or in variable $K$, and it computes only the exhibited equilibrium limit.

4. Complete answer for $K=1$

Existence follows from the preceding construction. We now show that every qualifying equilibrium has the same patient limit.

Consider the final-slot game with $n\geq2$ active agents and fixed worth $V>0$. For any qualifying equilibrium, let
$$ U_i^j $$
be agent $i$’s continuation payoff when $j$ is proposer. Define
$$ P_i=U_i^i,\qquad r_i=\min_jU_i^j,\qquad S=\sum_iP_i. $$
These values are finite and nonnegative.

4.1. Random respondent order

For $j\neq i$, agent $i$ is first respondent with probability $1/(n-1)$. On that event she can reject and obtain $\delta P_i$.

Otherwise, she can reject if reached. An earlier rejection gives at least $\delta r_i$, and her own rejection gives $\delta P_i\geq\delta r_i$. Thus
$$ U_i^j \geq \frac{\delta}{n-1}P_i + \frac{\delta(n-2)}{n-1}r_i. $$
Setting
$$ b_{n,\delta}= \frac{\delta}{n-1-\delta(n-2)}, $$
and applying the inequality at a minimizing proposer, or using $b_{n,\delta}\leq1$ if that proposer is $i$, gives
$$ U_i^j\geq b_{n,\delta}P_i \qquad\text{for every }i,j. $$

4.2. Strictly acceptable proposals

Proposer $i$ can offer respondent $h$ the amount $\delta P_h+\epsilon$. Backward induction makes unanimous acceptance strictly optimal in every realized order. Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ gives
$$ P_i\geq V-\delta\sum_{h\neq i}P_h. $$
Summing yields
$$ S\geq\frac{nV}{1+\delta(n-1)}. $$

Let $T^j=\sum_iU_i^j$. The aggregate realized payoff is $V\delta^D$, so
$$ \frac{b_{n,\delta}nV}{1+\delta(n-1)} \leq T^j\leq V. $$
Both bounds tend to $V$ as $\delta\uparrow1$, uniformly over equilibrium selection.

For $K=1$, set
$$ w=W(1,1,\ldots,1). $$
The promise implies $w>0$. The partition is forced, $V=Nw$, and $M_\pi=\delta_1$. Therefore
$$ E_N=\left\{\left(\mathsf D_{\delta_1},w\right)\right\} \qquad\text{for every }N\geq2. $$

The decision predicate is true on every promised $K=1$ input. Its common population limit is
$$ \left(\mathsf D_{\delta_1},W(1,1,\ldots,1)\right). $$
This pair is computable in polynomial time in $L$, with $m=\tau=K=1$.

5. A promised negative instance for $K=2$

First specialize the construction to two slots.

For $1\leq s<N$, define
$$ a_s= NW\!\left( \frac{s}{N}, \left(\frac{s}{N}\right)^2+\left(\frac{N-s}{N}\right)^2, \ldots, \left(\frac{s}{N}\right)^{r+1} +\left(\frac{N-s}{N}\right)^{r+1} \right), $$
and set
$$ a_N=NW(1,1,\ldots,1). $$

After the first departure, the complement is forced. Its final-slot construction has no rejection: if its size is at least two, $R_{\mathrm a}=\delta P$, and if it is a singleton, it forms immediately. Hence $H_s=1$ for all initial candidates.

Initially,
$$ P=\max_{1\leq t\leq N}\frac{a_t}{1+\delta(t-1)}. $$
If a selected size $s<N$ leaves complement worth $b_s=a_{N-s}$, maximization gives
$$ b_s\leq[1+\delta(N-s-1)]P. $$
Consequently,
$$ R_{\mathrm a} = \frac{\delta(s-1)P+b_s}{N-1} \leq \frac{1+\delta(N-2)}{N-1}P \leq P. $$
For $s=N$, directly $R_{\mathrm a}=\delta P$. Thus the initial construction also has no rejection.

For fixed $N$, its selected size near $\delta=1$ is
$$ s_N^*= \max\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq N}\frac{a_s}{s}. $$
To verify the tie rule, let $\beta=\max_s a_s/s>0$. Inferior averages remain inferior near $1$ by continuity. Among tied candidates,
$$ \frac{a_s}{1+\delta(s-1)} = \frac{\beta}{\delta+(1-\delta)/s}, $$
which strictly increases with $s$ when $\delta<1$.

Now take a polynomial independent of the moment variables:
$$ W(x)=xA(x), \qquad A(x)=1-\left(x-\frac13\right)^2\left(x-\frac12\right)^2. $$
It has degree five. For $x\in[0,1]$,
$$ \left|x-\frac13\right|\leq\frac23, \qquad \left|x-\frac12\right|\leq\frac12, $$
so
$$ A(x)\geq\frac89. $$
Every nonempty coalition therefore has strictly positive worth under every completion. All promises hold for every $N$.

The candidate averages are
$$ \frac{a_s}{s}=A(s/N). $$
The global maximum is $1$, attained exactly at $1/3$ and $1/2$.

For $N=6j+3$, the grid contains $1/3$ and excludes $1/2$. Hence the exhibited selection is
$$ e_N^*= \left( \mathsf D_{\frac13\delta_{1/3}+\frac23\delta_{2/3}}, \frac{485}{486} \right), $$
because
$$ A(2/3)=1-\frac1{324}, \qquad W(1/3)+W(2/3)=\frac{485}{486}. $$

For $N=6j+2$, only $1/2$ is on the grid, and
$$ e_N^*=(\mathsf D_{\delta_{1/2}},1). $$
These pairs are distinct. Thus the requested convergence predicate is false for this promised input, despite nonemptiness of every $E_N$.

The complete subsequential-limit set of this particular selection is exactly
$$ \left\{ \left( \mathsf D_{\frac13\delta_{1/3}+\frac23\delta_{2/3}}, \frac{485}{486} \right), \; (\mathsf D_{\delta_{1/2}},1) \right\}. $$
Indeed, grid maxima approach $1$ because grid points approach $1/3$. Every accumulation point of maximizing grid shares therefore maximizes $A$, and so is either $1/3$ or $1/2$. Both occur on the displayed subsequences, and the outcome pair depends continuously on the chosen share.

This does not exclude additional limits supplied by other equilibria.

6. Compactness and remaining open questions

For fixed input $K$, pad every vector of coalition shares with zeros to length $K$. The simplex
$$ \Delta_K= \left\{x\in[0,1]^K:\sum_i x_i=1\right\} $$
is compact. The map
$$ \Phi(x)=\sum_i x_i\delta_{x_i} $$
is continuous for weak convergence: for every continuous $f$,
$$ \int f\,d\Phi(x)=\sum_i x_if(x_i). $$
Its image $\mathcal S_K$ is compact. Every outcome law, including every law in $E_N$, is supported on $\mathcal S_K$.

Probability laws on the compact metric space $\mathcal S_K$ form a compact space under weak convergence. Together with $0\leq q\leq KC$, this proves that every population selection has a convergent subsequence.

The existence question is now settled: there are no promised inputs for which eventual nonemptiness fails. The unresolved decision problem is therefore whether all selections from these nonempty sets converge to one common pair.

The finite recursion establishes one attainable limit at each population size. It does not establish uniqueness of the equilibrium accumulation set, and its population asymptotics alone cannot decide a predicate quantified over all equilibria. No polynomial-time decision algorithm or hardness reduction is proved here.
