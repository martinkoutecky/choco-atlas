Given a network \(G\) with rational edge lengths and a rational finite-support society \( \mu \) over location types \(t\in G\), compute a facility location \(Y\sim\mu\) and compare \( \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}[\int d(t,Y)\,d\mu(t)] \) with \( \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)=\min_y\int d(t,y)\,d\mu(t) \), under finite-mass strategyproof deviations; determine whether the resulting \(2\)-approximation can be improved.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a finite connected metric graph with positive rational edge lengths; locations and facilities may lie inside edges, and \(d\) is shortest-path distance. Input locations are specified by an edge and a rational distance from an endpoint.
A society is given by its support: \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{t_i}\), with distinct locations, positive binary rational masses, and \(\sum_iw_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Running times are measured in \(m=|V|+|E|\), \(\tau\), and total input bit length \(L\).
A moneyless randomized mechanism \(F_G\) maps reported societies to probability laws on \(G\); a type \(t\) incurs expected cost \(c_t(F_G(\mu))=\mathbb E_{Y\sim F_G(\mu)}d(t,Y)\).
Finite-mass strategyproofness means \(c_t(F_G(\mu))\le c_t(F_G(\mu-a\delta_t+a\delta_r))\) for every supported \(t\), rational \(0<a\le\mu(\{t\})\), and rationally specified report \(r\in G\); masses cannot be changed or created.
Define \(\mathrm{SC}(P,\mu)=\mathbb E_{Y\sim P}[\int_G d(t,Y)\,d\mu(t)]\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)=\min_{y\in G}\int_G d(t,y)\,d\mu(t)\). An approximation factor \(\alpha\) means \(\mathrm{SC}(F_G(\mu),\mu)\le\alpha\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)\) on every input, including those with zero optimum. Mass random dictatorship is \(F_G(\mu)=\mu\).

Does mass random dictatorship satisfy finite-mass strategyproofness and guarantee factor \(2\), and can a finite-mass strategyproof randomized mechanism guarantee \(2-\varepsilon\) for some universal \(\varepsilon>0\) on all such networks and societies, or is \(2\) optimal?

2. Mass random dictatorship

Write
$$ S_\mu(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i d(t_i,y). $$

A finite metric graph is compact, and $S_\mu$ is continuous. Consequently, there exists $y^*\in G$ with
$$ S_\mu(y^*)=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu). $$

2.1. Finite-mass strategyproofness

Let $R_G(\mu)=\mu$. For an admissible deviation
$$ \mu'=\mu-a\delta_t+a\delta_r, $$
linearity gives
$$ c_t(R_G(\mu'))-c_t(R_G(\mu)) =a\bigl(d(t,r)-d(t,t)\bigr) =a\,d(t,r)\geq 0. $$

This covers partial or complete removal of the mass at $t$, including reports already present in the support.

The mechanism returns the input support with its input probabilities, so its description can be produced in polynomial time without expanding masses into agents.

2.2. Approximation guarantee

The expected social cost is
$$ \mathrm{SC}(R_G(\mu),\mu) =\sum_{i,j}w_iw_jd(t_i,t_j). $$

Applying the triangle inequality term by term,
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SC}(R_G(\mu),\mu) &\leq \sum_{i,j}w_iw_j \bigl(d(t_i,y^*)+d(t_j,y^*)\bigr)\\ &=2\sum_iw_i d(t_i,y^*)\\ &=2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu). \end{aligned} $$

The equality uses $\sum_iw_i=1$.

This argument does not divide by the optimum. If the optimum is zero, positivity of the masses implies that every supported point equals $y^*$. Thus $\mu=\delta_{y^*}$ and the mechanism also has zero cost.

Deleting the diagonal terms gives the additional bound
$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SC}(R_G(\mu),\mu) &\leq 2\sum_iw_i(1-w_i)d(t_i,y^*)\\ &\leq 2(1-w_{\min})\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu), \end{aligned} $$
where $w_{\min}=\min_iw_i$.

2.3. Tightness for this mechanism

On the unit interval, consider
$$ \mu_p=(1-p)\delta_0+p\delta_1, \qquad 0<p<\frac12, $$
with rational $p$. For $y\in[0,1]$,
$$ S_{\mu_p}(y)=p+(1-2p)y. $$
Hence the optimum is $p$, attained at $0$. Random dictatorship has cost $2p(1-p)$, giving ratio
$$ \frac{\mathrm{SC}(R_G(\mu_p),\mu_p)} {\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu_p)} =2(1-p). $$

Taking $p=2^{-k}$ for integers $k\geq2$ makes these ratios tend to $2$. Therefore $2$ is the smallest valid universal factor for mass random dictatorship, even when the support size is only two.

3. A lower bound for every mechanism

Proposition

No finite-mass strategyproof randomized mechanism can guarantee a factor strictly below
$$ \frac{35}{33} $$
on all the networks in the question.

The proof uses a circle of circumference $14$, realized as a cycle with fourteen unit edges. All reports below are vertices, but facilities may be anywhere on the circle.

3.1. A minimization fact on circles

Lemma. A positively weighted sum of distances on a circle attains its minimum at one of its specified locations.

Proof. Consider a closed arc between consecutive specified locations. Its interior contains none of them. On this arc, each distance function is affine except possibly at its antipode, where its slope decreases from $1$ to $-1$. It is therefore concave on the arc.

A positively weighted sum remains concave. A concave function on an interval has a minimum at an endpoint: its value at an interior point is at least the corresponding convex combination of the endpoint values. Applying this to every intervening arc proves the lemma. $\square$

3.2. Symmetrization preserves the requirements

Use coordinates modulo $14$. Let $H$ be the finite group of isometries
$$ h(x)=x+j \quad\text{or}\quad h(x)=-x+j, \qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,13\}. $$

For an arbitrary mechanism $F$, define
$$ \overline F(\mu) =\frac1{28}\sum_{h\in H} (h^{-1})_\#F(h_\#\mu), $$
where $h_\#\mu$ denotes the image measure under $h$.

Each $h$ maps rationally specified locations to rationally specified locations. An admissible deviation from $t$ to $r$ becomes the same mass deviation from $h(t)$ to $h(r)$. Since $h$ preserves distances,
$$ c_t\bigl((h^{-1})_\#F(h_\#\mu)\bigr) =c_{h(t)}\bigl(F(h_\#\mu)\bigr). $$
Thus every summand satisfies finite-mass strategyproofness, and averaging preserves it.

Similarly,
$$ \mathrm{SC}\bigl((h^{-1})_\#F(h_\#\mu),\mu\bigr) =\mathrm{SC}\bigl(F(h_\#\mu),h_\#\mu\bigr), $$
and the optimum is unchanged by $h$. Hence any approximation guarantee for $F$ also holds for $\overline F$.

Finally, reindexing the group average proves equivariance:
$$ \overline F(g_\#\mu)=g_\#\overline F(\mu) \qquad(g\in H). $$

It therefore suffices to obtain a lower bound for $\overline F$.

3.3. Three societies and two deviations

Define
$$ \begin{aligned} \mu_A&=\frac12\delta_0+\frac14\delta_5+\frac14\delta_6,\\ \mu_B&=\frac12\delta_0+\frac14\delta_5+\frac14\delta_8,\\ \mu_C&=\frac14\delta_0+\frac14\delta_1 +\frac14\delta_5+\frac14\delta_6. \end{aligned} $$

Let $Y_X$ have law $\overline F(\mu_X)$, for $X\in\{A,B,C\}$.

Moving mass $1/4$ from $8$ to $6$ transforms $\mu_B$ into $\mu_A$. Moving mass $1/4$ from $1$ to $0$ transforms $\mu_C$ into $\mu_A$. Strategyproofness gives
$$ \mathbb E d(8,Y_B)\leq\mathbb E d(8,Y_A), $$
and
$$ \mathbb E d(1,Y_C)\leq\mathbb E d(1,Y_A). $$

The points $1$ and $8$ are antipodal. Consequently, for every facility location $y$,
$$ d(1,y)+d(8,y)=7. $$
Adding the two incentive inequalities yields
$$ \mathbb E d(8,Y_B)+\mathbb E d(1,Y_C)\leq7. $$

The reflection $h(x)=6-x$ preserves $\mu_C$ and exchanges $1$ with $5$. Equivariance therefore implies
$$ \mathbb E d(1,Y_C)=\mathbb E d(5,Y_C). $$
The triangle inequality gives
$$ d(1,y)+d(5,y)\geq d(1,5)=4. $$
Taking expectations shows
$$ \mathbb E d(1,Y_C)\geq2, $$
and hence
$$ \mathbb E d(8,Y_B)\leq5. $$

3.4. The resulting social cost

Define the unnormalized social-cost function
$$ T_B(y)=2d(0,y)+d(5,y)+d(8,y). $$
Thus $S_{\mu_B}(y)=T_B(y)/4$.

The relevant values are:

| $y$ | $d(0,y)$ | $d(5,y)$ | $d(8,y)$ | $T_B(y)$ | $3T_B(y)+2d(8,y)$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $0$ | $0$ | $5$ | $6$ | $11$ | $45$ |
| $5$ | $5$ | $0$ | $3$ | $13$ | $45$ |
| $8$ | $6$ | $3$ | $0$ | $15$ | $45$ |

The circle minimization lemma applies both to $T_B$ and to
$$ 3T_B(y)+2d(8,y)=6d(0,y)+3d(5,y)+5d(8,y). $$
Therefore
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu_B)=\frac{11}{4}, $$
and, for every $y$,
$$ 3T_B(y)+2d(8,y)\geq45. $$

Taking expectations and using the bound on the expected distance from $8$,
$$ 3\mathbb E T_B(Y_B) \geq45-2\mathbb E d(8,Y_B) \geq35. $$
It follows that
$$ \mathrm{SC}(\overline F(\mu_B),\mu_B) \geq\frac{35}{12}. $$
Any approximation factor $\alpha$ for $\overline F$ must consequently satisfy
$$ \alpha\geq \frac{35/12}{11/4} =\frac{35}{33}. $$

Since symmetrization preserves a guarantee, this proves the proposition for arbitrary $F$. No restriction on its facility distributions was imposed. $\square$

4. Equivalence with uniform finite-agent guarantees

Fix a graph $G$, and restrict finite-agent reports to its rationally specified locations.

Proposition

For a fixed $\alpha\geq1$, the following are equivalent:

  • There exists a finite-mass strategyproof mechanism with factor $\alpha$.
  • For every positive integer $n$, there exists an individually strategyproof randomized mechanism for $n$ agents with factor $\alpha$.

This is an equivalence of existence, without an assertion about efficient implementation.

4.1. From finite masses to finite agents

Given $F$, put
$$ f_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n) =F\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}\right). $$

An individual deviation moves mass $1/n$, which is admissible even when several agents share the same location. Thus $f_n$ is individually strategyproof.

Finite-agent social costs and optima are $n$ times their normalized society counterparts, so the factor is preserved.

4.2. Moving several copies of one type

Suppose an individually strategyproof mechanism receives a profile containing $k$ agents at $t$. Move those agents to a common report $r$, one at a time.

At each step, the agent being moved still reports $t$. Strategyproofness therefore compares expected distance from the same location $t$ at every step. Chaining the inequalities shows that moving all $k$ copies cannot decrease expected distance from $t$.

This argument concerns copies with one common true location; it does not assert group strategyproofness for arbitrary coalitions.

4.3. From finite agents to finite masses

Assume the second statement. First average each finite-agent mechanism over all permutations of agent labels. Each permuted mechanism remains individually strategyproof, and averaging preserves both incentive inequalities and approximation guarantees. We may therefore assume anonymity.

Set $N_j=j!$. If all masses of a society $\mu$ are multiples of $1/N_j$, let $H_j(\mu)$ be the output of the anonymous $N_j$-agent mechanism on the corresponding multiset of copies. Otherwise assign any fixed point mass.

Every rational society is representable for all sufficiently large $j$. For any fixed rational deviation
$$ \mu'=\mu-a\delta_t+a\delta_r, $$
all original masses and $a$ are multiples of $1/N_j$ for sufficiently large $j$. Moving the $N_ja$ copies one at a time then gives
$$ c_t(H_j(\mu))\leq c_t(H_j(\mu')). $$
The factor-$\alpha$ inequality also holds eventually at every fixed society.

There are countably many rational societies. The space of Borel probability measures on compact $G$ is sequentially compact under weak convergence. Successive subsequence extraction and diagonalization therefore produce a common subsequence along which $H_j(\mu)$ converges weakly for every rational society $\mu$. Denote its limit by $F(\mu)$.

Each function $d(t,\cdot)$ and $S_\mu$ is bounded and continuous. Their integrals consequently converge under weak convergence. Passing to the limit proves all finite-mass incentive inequalities and approximation inequalities simultaneously. $\square$

The equivalence also holds for guarantees uniform over all rational metric graphs, by applying the construction separately to each graph.

As one application, the cited circle theorem supplies individually strategyproof factor-$3/2$ mechanisms for every $n\geq3$, including repeated reports. For $n=1,2$, random dictatorship is optimal: for two reports, the triangle inequality makes their distance the minimum unnormalized social cost. The proposition therefore gives a finite-mass factor-$3/2$ mechanism on every rational circle. This uses [Chan, Lin, and Wang, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12792v2) as an external theorem; the compactness construction itself supplies no runtime bound.

5. Impossibility has finite rational certificates

The following result gives an exact justification for searching finite collections of societies and deviations.

Proposition

Fix a rational metric graph $G$ and a rational proposed factor $\alpha\geq1$. If no finite-mass strategyproof factor-$\alpha$ mechanism exists on $G$, then this failure is witnessed by a finite infeasible linear program with rational coefficients.

Facilities anywhere inside edges are accounted for exactly.

5.1. A finite set of constraints suffices

Introduce an unknown output law $P_\mu$ for every rational society. Require all approximation inequalities
$$ \int_G S_\mu(y)\,dP_\mu(y) \leq\alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu), $$
and all admissible incentive inequalities
$$ \int_G d(t,y)\,dP_\mu(y) \leq \int_G d(t,y)\,dP_{\mu'}(y). $$

There are countably many constraints. Each depends on at most two output laws and is closed under weak convergence, because its integrands are bounded and continuous.

Suppose every finite collection were feasible. Enumerate the constraints and, for each positive integer $j$, choose laws satisfying the first $j$ constraints, assigning arbitrary laws at all other societies. Diagonal subsequence extraction over the countably many societies gives weak limits at every society.

Every fixed constraint holds eventually along this subsequence and passes to the limit. The limiting laws would define a mechanism satisfying all constraints, a contradiction. Thus some finite collection is infeasible.

5.2. Exact reduction of continuous facilities to finitely many points

Take such a finite collection. Let $T$ contain every support location of every society appearing in it. Subdivide $G$ at these locations, making all points of $T$ vertices.

On a resulting edge with endpoints $u,v$, length $\ell$, and coordinate $x$ measured from $u$, each $t\in T$ satisfies
$$ d(t,y_x)= \min\bigl\{d(t,u)+x,\ d(t,v)+\ell-x\bigr\}. $$
Indeed, a path reaching an interior point must enter the edge through an endpoint, and either endpoint route is available.

Endpoint distances are rational shortest-path lengths. The only possible interior breakpoint of this formula is
$$ x=\frac{d(t,v)+\ell-d(t,u)}2. $$
Include all such breakpoints and all vertices in a finite set $K$. Every distance function $d(t,\cdot)$ is affine between consecutive points of $K$ on an edge.

If $y$ lies on one such segment with endpoints $u',v'$, length $\ell'$, and coordinate $x$, replace $y$ by the distribution
$$ \left(1-\frac{x}{\ell'}\right)\delta_{u'} +\frac{x}{\ell'}\delta_{v'}. $$
Affineness gives, simultaneously for every $t\in T$,
$$ d(t,y) = \left(1-\frac{x}{\ell'}\right)d(t,u') +\frac{x}{\ell'}d(t,v'). $$

Applying this measurable replacement to an arbitrary output law preserves every expected distance used in the finite constraints. It therefore preserves all their social costs and incentive comparisons exactly.

Thus restricting outputs to $K$ loses no feasible solution.

5.3. The rational linear program

For each society in the collection, use variables $q_{\mu,k}$ satisfying
$$ q_{\mu,k}\geq0, \qquad \sum_{k\in K}q_{\mu,k}=1. $$

The selected constraints become
$$ \sum_{k\in K}S_\mu(k)q_{\mu,k} \leq \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu) $$
and
$$ \sum_{k\in K}d(t,k)q_{\mu,k} - \sum_{k\in K}d(t,k)q_{\mu',k} \leq0. $$

All coefficients are rational. Moreover, each $S_\mu$ is affine on the subdivided segments, so
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mu)=\min_{k\in K}S_\mu(k), $$
which is rational as well.

This proves the finite rational linear-programming claim. By Farkas’ lemma, its infeasibility has a finite rational certificate: after writing the system as $Az\leq b$, there are nonnegative rational multipliers $\lambda$ with
$$ \lambda^{\mathsf T}A=0, \qquad \lambda^{\mathsf T}b<0. $$
Such multipliers combine the proposed inequalities into a contradiction. $\square$

Consequently, nonexistence for a fixed rational factor and fixed graph is semidecidable by enumerating finite constraint collections and checking their rational linear programs. This gives no bound on the size of a necessary certificate and no polynomial-time decision procedure for the full existence question.
