The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite schools \(S\), finite student types \(T\), rational masses \(\lambda_t\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), rational capacity shares \(q_s\), type preferences \(P_t\), and priority profiles \(\succ,\succ'\) where \(\succ'\) improves \(\succ\) for \(G\subseteq T\), compute an exact mass allocation \(x=(x_{t,s})\) using mass EADA for the profile \(\{\succ_s,\succ'_s\}_{s\in S}\). Require \(\sum_{s\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{t,s}=\lambda_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\), individual rationality, non-wastefulness, M-fairness, and \(G\)-Pareto optimality among feasible M-stable allocations; for a second profile \(\succ''\) with \(\succ'\) more improving \(\succ\) than \(\succ''\), compute both outputs and preserve the stated \(G\)-non-domination relation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\lambda_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type has a strict preference order \(P_t\) on \(S\cup\{\varnothing\}\); schools have positive rational capacity shares \(q_s\). Each priority profile consists of strict linear orders on \(T\); individuals within a type have identical priorities.
A profile \(b\) improves \(a\) for \(G\subseteq T\) if every reversal \(u\,a_s\,t\) and \(t\,b_s\,u\) has \(t\in G\). Profile \(b\) more improves \(a\) than \(c\) if both improve \(a\), \(b\) improves \(c\), and \(u\,a_s\,t\) and \(t\,c_s\,u\) imply \(t\,b_s\,u\), at every school.
For a pair \((a,b)\), set \(H_s=a_s\cap b_s\). An allocation has \(x_{t,r}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), \(\sum_{r\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{t,r}=\lambda_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\); individual rationality requires \(x_{t,s}=0\) whenever \(\varnothing P_t s\).
Non-wastefulness requires \(\sum_u x_{u,s}=q_s\) whenever \(x_{t,r}>0\) and \(sP_t r\). M-fairness forbids \(x_{t,r}>0\), \(x_{u,s}>0\), \(sP_t r\), and \((t,u)\in H_s\) simultaneously. M-stability means feasibility, individual rationality, non-wastefulness, and M-fairness.
Allocation \(y\) dominates \(x\) for \(G\) when \(y_t/\lambda_t\) weakly first-order stochastically dominates \(x_t/\lambda_t\) under \(P_t\) for every \(t\in G\), strictly for at least one such type. An allocation is \(G\)-optimal if no M-stable allocation dominates it for \(G\).
Define mass deferred acceptance by simultaneous proposals of all unheld mass to its next acceptable school; each school retains its highest-priority offered mass under \(b_s\), up to capacity, splitting the boundary type if necessary. Rejected mass continues proposing; exhausted mass receives \(\varnothing\).
Mass EADA repeatedly runs this procedure on the remaining masses and schools. A school is underdemanded when no positive remaining mass prefers it to its current assignment under the current, possibly shortened preferences. Permanently settle and remove all mass assigned to underdemanded schools or \(\varnothing\), and remove those schools.
After each settlement, delete a remaining school \(s\) from the acceptable choices of every remaining type \(u\) whenever a positive settled fragment of type \(t\), assigned to \(r\), satisfies \(sP_t r\) and \((t,u)\in H_s\); preserve all other preference comparisons. Repeat until all mass is settled, and aggregate the settled fragments.
Given \((a,b,G)\) with \(b\) improving \(a\), the required exact output is this mass EADA allocation, M-stable and \(G\)-optimal. If also given \(c\) with \(b\) more improving \(a\) than \(c\), compute both outputs and require that the output for \((a,c)\) does not dominate that for \((a,b)\) for \(G\).
Is this exact mass EADA computation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?
For finite individual students with strict school priorities, EADA computes an improved-group optimal M-stable matching and is responsive to improvements. [Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.04780v4)
Continuum stable-matching existence is known under convex, continuous firm choice; this does not establish the mass EADA, group-optimality, and responsiveness requirements above. No result settling this exact continuous problem was located. [Che–Kim–Kojima](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.3982/ECTA13547)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified strict student preferences including the outside option, positive rational capacities without requiring capacities to sum to one, and strict linear priority orders between distinct types, following the anchor's domain.
  • Individuals within a type share priorities; no strict priority comparison is introduced within a type.
  • Interpreted mass non-wastefulness and M-fairness through positive assigned masses, carrying over the discrete blocking conditions.
  • Interpreted group Pareto comparisons through first-order stochastic dominance of each type's normalized allocation.
  • Defined the previously unspecified mass EADA through simultaneous mass proposals, boundary-type splitting, settlement of assigned fragments rather than entire types, and the discrete preference-deletion condition using original preferences.

1. Result

The exact mass EADA allocation is computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Its output is M-stable and \(G\)-optimal, and the two outputs satisfy the required responsiveness condition.

The proof separates two issues:

  • Rational replication transfers the finite EADA welfare guarantees to the mass model.
  • An accelerated stable-allocation algorithm computes each mass DA outcome without expanding the population into individual copies.

The replication is used only in the proof.

2. Two finite algorithmic facts

We use the following established results.

Finite EADA theorem. For finite students, strict preferences, positive integer school capacities, and strict priority profiles \(A,B\), suppose \(B\) improves \(A\) for a group \(I_G\). EADA using DA priorities \(B\) and protected priorities \(A_s\cap B_s\) produces an M-stable matching that no M-stable matching Pareto dominates for \(I_G\). The settlement and deletion rules are those in the question. This is [Kitahara–Okumura, Theorem 3 and Appendix A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.04780v4).

Accelerated DA fact. In a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict orders, vertex quotas, and edge upper capacities, the proposer-optimal allocation can be computed in polynomially many arithmetic operations. It equals the generalized proposer DA outcome. A simple implementation takes \(O(EV)\) operations for \(E\) edges and \(V\) vertices, using minimum residual quantities and additions or subtractions. See [Dean–Munshi, Section 3 and Lemma 5](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

We also use the classical finite DA property: with strict priorities, applicant-proposing DA is independent of proposal order and weakly improves every applicant relative to every stable matching.

The hypotheses of these results are checked below.

3. Rational replication preserves mass EADA

3.1. The replicated instance

Choose an integer \(D\) clearing all denominators of the masses and capacities, with \(D\geq 3\). For example, three times the product of their denominators suffices. Its bit length is \(O(L)\), although its value can be exponentially large.

Replace type \(t\) by

\(n_t=D\lambda_t\)

individual copies, labelled \((t,1),\ldots,(t,n_t)\), and give school \(s\) capacity

\(k_s=Dq_s\).

Every copy of \(t\) has preference \(P_t\). Replace each occurrence of \(t\) in every priority profile by the block

\((t,1),(t,2),\ldots,(t,n_t)\).

Thus earlier copies have higher priority, consistently across all schools and profiles. Write \(A,B\) for the replicated profiles.

Between different types, \(A_s\cap B_s\) agrees exactly with \(H_s\). Within a type, it orders copies by increasing index.

All replicated orders are strict, capacities are positive integers, and there are \(D\geq 3\) students. Moreover, \(B\) improves \(A\) for

\(I_G=\{(t,i):t\in G\}\),

because no within-type comparison reverses, and every intertype reversal promotes copies of a type in \(G\).

3.2. DA commutes with replication

All quantities in mass DA remain multiples of \(1/D\): school retention requires only sums, comparisons, and subtraction from capacity.

Represent each such unit by one copy. At a school, retaining the highest-priority offered copies retains whole type blocks and possibly part of one boundary block. Its aggregate is exactly the mass retention rule.

Consequently, each simultaneous mass-proposal round has a corresponding simultaneous ordinary DA round. Ordinary DA can serialize those proposals without changing its final matching. Hence aggregating the replicated DA outcome and dividing by \(D\) gives precisely the specified mass DA outcome.

3.3. The extra within-type priorities cause no extra deletions

This is the essential point for EADA.

Assume that, at the beginning of an EADA iteration, all remaining copies of a type have the same shortened preferences. This holds initially.

First, the DA outcome orders these copies by their current preferences. Indeed, if an earlier copy \(i\) received a worse assignment than a later copy \(j\), then \(i\) would prefer the school assigned to \(j\) and would have higher \(B\)-priority there. That would contradict DA stability.

Now let a copy \(i\) of type \(t\) be settled, while another copy \(j\) of that type remains.

  • If \(i\) is settled at an underdemanded school \(r\), copy \(j\) cannot prefer \(r\) to its assignment. It also cannot be assigned to \(r\), since every copy assigned there is settled. Its assignment is therefore strictly better than \(r\) under the common shortened preferences.
  • If \(i\) is settled at \(\varnothing\), copy \(j\) remains assigned to an acceptable school, which is strictly better.

The ordering property just proved implies that \(j\) has an earlier index than \(i\). Therefore

\((i,j)\notin A_s\cap B_s\)

at every school. A settled copy cannot trigger a within-type deletion for any remaining copy.

For different types, the deletion condition depends only on their types, their original preferences, and whether \((t,u)\in H_s\). Thus it deletes a school from every remaining copy of \(u\) exactly when the mass procedure deletes it from type \(u\).

Finally, underdemanded schools coincide in the two representations: a positive mass fragment preferring a school exists exactly when a corresponding copy exists.

Induction now proves:

Replication lemma. For every denominator-clearing \(D\), aggregating finite EADA in the replicated instance gives exactly the specified mass EADA allocation.

If all mass is settled before all unused schools are removed, removing those empty schools makes no further allocation change.

4. M-stability and \(G\)-optimality

4.1. M-stability

Let \(x\) be the mass EADA output and \(\mu^D\) the replicated finite output. By the finite EADA theorem, \(\mu^D\) is M-stable.

Feasibility and individual rationality pass directly to aggregates.

For non-wastefulness, suppose \(x_{t,r}>0\) and \(sP_t r\). Some copy of \(t\) is assigned to \(r\), so finite non-wastefulness fills school \(s\) to capacity \(Dq_s\). Dividing by \(D\) gives

\(\sum_u x_{u,s}=q_s\).

For M-fairness, a violation in \(x\) would provide types \(t,u\) and alternatives \(r,s\) with

\(x_{t,r}>0,\quad x_{u,s}>0,\quad sP_t r,\quad (t,u)\in H_s\).

Corresponding copies would violate fairness for \(A_s\cap B_s\), a contradiction.

Thus \(x\) is M-stable.

4.2. Every rational M-stable allocation has a sorted finite lift

Let \(z\) be any rational M-stable allocation, and choose \(D\) also clearing its denominators.

For each type, assign its copies in increasing index order to alternatives in decreasing preference order, using exactly \(Dz_{t,r}\) copies at alternative \(r\). Denote this matching by \(\widehat z^D\).

It is M-stable:

  • Feasibility, individual rationality, and non-wastefulness follow from their mass counterparts.
  • An intertype fairness violation would contradict mass M-fairness.
  • A within-type fairness violation is impossible: an earlier copy receives an assignment weakly preferred to that of every later copy.

Furthermore, \(\mu^D\) itself is sorted under the original preferences. Otherwise an earlier copy would envy a later copy whose school it outranks under both \(A\) and \(B\). Since its aggregate is \(x\), this gives

\(\mu^D=\widehat x^D\).

4.3. Stochastic dominance becomes copywise dominance

List the alternatives for type \(t\) as

\(r_{t,1}P_t r_{t,2}P_t\cdots P_t r_{t,m+1}\).

Define the cumulative mass

\(C_{t,j}(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}z_{t,r_{t,\ell}}\).

Because \(\lambda_t>0\) is fixed, weak first-order stochastic dominance is equivalent to

\(C_{t,j}(y)\geq C_{t,j}(x)\)

for every \(j\).

These inequalities imply that every copy of \(t\) receives a weakly better assignment in \(\widehat y^D\) than in \(\widehat x^D\). To verify this, suppose copy \(i\) receives an alternative of rank \(j\) in \(\widehat x^D\), but a worse alternative in \(\widehat y^D\). Sortedness gives

\(DC_{t,j}(x)\geq i,\qquad DC_{t,j}(y)<i\),

contradicting the cumulative inequality.

If the distributions differ, their sorted assignments differ for some copy. Under weak dominance, that copy is strictly better off.

4.4. \(G\)-optimality

Suppose a rational M-stable allocation \(y\) dominated \(x\) for \(G\).

Choose \(D\) clearing the input denominators and those of \(y\). By the replication lemma, finite EADA gives \(\widehat x^D\). By Section 4.2, \(\widehat y^D\) is M-stable. By Section 4.3, it Pareto dominates \(\widehat x^D\) for all copies belonging to \(I_G\).

This contradicts the finite EADA theorem, whose improvement hypothesis was checked in Section 3.1. Therefore \(x\) is \(G\)-optimal.

5. Responsiveness

Write

\(H_s^b=a_s\cap b_s,\qquad H_s^c=a_s\cap c_s\).

The “more improves” condition implies

\(H_s^b\subseteq H_s^c\).

Indeed, suppose \(t\,a_s\,u\) and \(t\,b_s\,u\). If \(t\,c_s\,u\) failed, strictness would give \(u\,c_s\,t\). The condition preserving reversals from \(a\) to \(c\) would then imply \(u\,b_s\,t\), a contradiction.

Every allocation M-stable for \((a,c)\) is consequently M-stable for \((a,b)\): the feasibility, rationality, and non-wastefulness conditions are identical, while the latter protects a subset of the priority pairs.

Let \(x^b,x^c\) be the respective mass EADA outputs. Since \(x^b\) is \(G\)-optimal, the M-stable allocation \(x^c\) cannot dominate it for \(G\). This proves the required responsiveness condition.

6. Polynomial computation

6.1. Accelerating each DA run

At an EADA iteration, let \(\lambda'_t\) be the remaining type masses. Build a bipartite stable-allocation instance:

  • A type vertex has quota \(\lambda'_t\).
  • A remaining school has quota \(q_s\).
  • An edge joins a type to each currently acceptable school.
  • Type preferences are the shortened preferences; school preferences are \(b_s\).

Give each edge the redundant upper capacity

\(u_{t,s}=\min\{\lambda'_t,q_s\}\).

These upper capacities do not change feasibility.

For completeness, unmatched mass and unfilled capacity can be represented with a dummy school and dummy type. Put

\(A=\sum_t\lambda'_t,\qquad Q=\sum_{s\text{ remaining}}q_s\),

give the dummy school quota \(A\), and give the dummy type quota \(Q\). Connect every real type to the dummy school, every real school to the dummy type, and the two dummies to each other. Each real vertex ranks its dummy partner last; the dummies rank their mutual edge last.

A real type allocated to the dummy school represents outside-option mass. A school allocated to the dummy type represents unused capacity. A blocking real edge therefore means exactly that a type has worse-assigned mass and the school has either a vacancy or lower-priority assigned mass. These are precisely the ordinary mass DA stability conditions.

All orders are strict and all quotas and upper capacities are rational. The accelerated DA fact applies and computes the same proposer DA outcome. Equivalently, this equality follows by replicating rational units and comparing the proposal schedules.

The augmented graph has

\(V=O(m+\tau),\qquad E=O(m\tau)\).

Thus one DA outcome requires \(O(m\tau(m+\tau))\) arithmetic operations.

6.2. Polynomially many EADA iterations

We prove a bound without assuming that the number of proposal rounds is polynomial.

First, in any nonempty finite DA instance where every applicant finishes assigned to a school, some school is underdemanded. Consider the last proposal in a sequential DA execution. It cannot reject an applicant, since that applicant would either make another proposal or finish unmatched. Its school therefore had a vacant seat before accepting. Since a school remains full after its first rejection, this school never rejected anyone. An applicant preferring it to their final assignment would have proposed there and been rejected, so no such applicant exists.

By replication, this statement also holds for mass DA.

Now classify EADA iterations:

  • Some school is removed. There are at most \(m\) such iterations.
  • No school is removed, and a new acceptable type-school pair is deleted. There are at most \(m\tau\) such iterations, because deletions persist.
  • No school is removed and no new pair is deleted. Unless the process finishes, this iteration only removes outside-option mass.

In the third case, restrict the current replicated DA matching to the surviving copies. They were all assigned to schools, and the restriction is stable under the unchanged \(B\)-priorities and shortened preferences. The next DA outcome weakly improves every surviving copy relative to this stable matching, so every survivor remains assigned to a school. The preceding observation then gives an underdemanded school in the next iteration.

Thus each iteration of the third kind is followed by an iteration removing a school, or terminates the process. There are at most \(m+1\) such iterations.

The total number of DA runs is therefore at most

\(m\tau+2m+1\).

Testing underdemandedness and performing the deletion scans is polynomial in \(m,\tau\). For example, scanning all newly settled type-alternative fragments against all remaining type-school pairs uses \(O(m^2\tau^2)\) operations per iteration.

6.3. Bit complexity

Use the original denominator-clearing integer \(D\), without creating copies. Scale every mass and capacity by \(D\).

The accelerated algorithm’s minimum-residual updates, settlement, and aggregation preserve integrality. No new denominator is introduced. The scaled quotas and allocations are bounded by a quantity of order

\(D\bigl(1+\sum_s q_s\bigr)\),

whose bit length is \(O(L+\log m)\). Hence every arithmetic operation and comparison has polynomial bit cost.

The output has only \(\tau(m+1)\) rational entries. Dividing the final integer entries by \(D\) produces their exact binary rational representations in polynomial time. Running the algorithm twice for the additional profile \(c\) preserves this bound.

Therefore the prescribed exact output, together with all its required guarantees, is polynomial-time computable.
