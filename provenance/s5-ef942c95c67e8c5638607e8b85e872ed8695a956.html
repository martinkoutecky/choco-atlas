The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite metric space \((V,d)\), eligible occupied sites \(A\subseteq V\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), and a positive integer \(k\), define \(\rho_\mu(a)=\min\{r:\sum_{b\in A:\,d(a,b)\le r}\mu_b\ge 1/k\}\). Is there a set \(S\subseteq A\), \(|S|\le k\), such that \(d(a,S)\le\rho_\mu(a)\) for every \(a\in A\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: a nonempty finite metric space \((V,d)\), with \(m=|V|\), given by a matrix of binary rational distances; a nonempty support \(A\subseteq V\); and a positive integer \(k\) encoded in binary.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|\) occupied types, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_a\), where \(\sum_{a\in A}\mu_a=1\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
For each \(a\in A\), define \(\rho_\mu(a)=\min\{r\ge0:\sum_{b\in A:\,d(a,b)\le r}\mu_b\ge1/k\}\); balls are closed and include their center's mass.
A feasible solution is a nonempty set \(S\subseteq A\) with \(|S|\le k\) satisfying \(\min_{s\in S}d(a,s)\le\rho_\mu(a)\) for every \(a\in A\). Centers are selected as sites, without fractional opening; a zero-radius site must itself be selected.

Is deciding whether a feasible solution exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finite populations, deciding whether \(1\)-fairness is achievable with at most \(k\) occupied centers is NP-complete. [Discrete result](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09041).
Related priority \(k\)-center optimization admits a \(2\)-approximation; this is not an exact-feasibility result for the mass formulation. [Related result](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2021/14090/pdf/LIPIcs-ICALP-2021-21.pdf). No separate treatment of this exact mass-input decision problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified a full binary rational distance matrix and defined m and total input bit length L, since the metric encoding was unspecified.
  • Required nonempty support and a nonempty center set, making nearest-center distance well-defined and preserving the nonempty-population domain.
  • Restricted radii to nonnegative values and explicitly retained closed balls, self-mass, and the zero-radius feasibility condition.
  • Kept k as any positive binary-encoded integer: normalized masses provide no population cardinality corresponding to the discrete upper bound on k.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(A=V\), every mass equals \(1/m\), and every distance between distinct sites belongs to \(\{1,2\}\).

The cases \(k=1\) and \(k\geq\tau\) always admit a feasible solution. The hardness result concerns the remaining range.

1. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a nonempty list of distinct supported centers \(S\), with \(|S|\leq\min\{k,\tau\}\). Its length is \(O(\min\{k,\tau\}\log(\tau+1))\) bits.

For each \(a\in A\), let \(c_a=\min_{s\in S}d(a,s)\). We claim that

\[ c_a\leq\rho_\mu(a) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{\substack{b\in A\\d(a,b)<c_a}}\mu_b<\frac1k. \]

Indeed, if \(\rho_\mu(a)<c_a\), the closed ball of radius \(\rho_\mu(a)\) lies inside the strict ball of radius \(c_a\), so the latter has mass at least \(1/k\). Conversely, if the strict ball has mass at least \(1/k\), it is nonempty. Since \(A\) is finite, its largest distance from \(a\) is some \(r<c_a\). The closed ball of radius \(r\) then has mass at least \(1/k\), giving \(\rho_\mu(a)\leq r<c_a\). This also handles \(c_a=0\), since its strict ball is empty.

This equivalence gives an exact verifier, including all distance ties. Write \(\mu_b=p_b/q_b\), define \(Q=\prod_{b\in A}q_b\), and set \(w_b=p_b(Q/q_b)\). The verifier checks, for every \(a\),

\[ k\sum_{\substack{b\in A\\d(a,b)<c_a}}w_b<Q. \]

The relevant integers have \(O(L)\) bits: the denominator bit lengths sum to at most \(L\), and the binary encoding of \(k\) is included in \(L\). Using elementary integer arithmetic, verification takes

\[ O\!\left(L+\bigl(\tau^2+\tau\min\{k,\tau\}\bigr)L^2\right) \]

bit operations. The dependence on \(m\) is through reading the supplied matrix, already included in \(L\). If input validity must also be checked, all metric axioms can be checked in \(O(m^3L^2)\) bit operations. Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. A polynomial reduction from Dominating Set

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Dominating Set problem: given a finite simple graph \(G=(U,E)\) and a budget \(b\), decide whether some \(D\subseteq U\), with \(|D|\leq b\), contains or neighbors every vertex. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is recalled in [Alber–Fellows–Niedermeier](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0207066).

Let \(n=|U|\), and restrict to \(n\geq1\) and \(1\leq b\leq n\). The excluded cases are immediately decidable, so this restriction preserves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Construction

Set \(q=3(n-b)\). Construct a graph \(H\) as follows:

  • Retain \(G\).
  • For every \(u\in U\), add two private leaves \(u^{(1)},u^{(2)}\), each adjacent only to \(u\).
  • Add \(q\) disjoint cycles of length five, with no edges to the other vertices.

Let \(V=V(H)\), take \(A=V\), and define the distance matrix by \(d(x,x)=0\); for distinct \(x,y\), set \(d(x,y)=1\) when they are adjacent in \(H\), and \(d(x,y)=2\) otherwise.

This is a metric. Symmetry and positive definiteness hold by construction. For three pairwise distinct vertices, \(d(x,y)\leq2\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)\); if vertices coincide, the triangle inequality is immediate.

Let \(N=|V|=3n+5q\), assign every site mass \(\mu_x=1/N\), and choose

\[ k=b+2q. \]

The identity

\[ N-3k=3n+5q-3b-6q=3(n-b)-q=0 \]

gives \(N=3k\). Consequently, the mass threshold is \(1/k=3/N\): a ball reaches the threshold exactly when it contains at least three sites.

All sites are occupied, all masses are positive, and their sum is one.

Radius calculation

Every radius-zero ball contains only one site, so every neighborhood radius is positive.

For an original vertex \(u\in U\), its closed radius-one ball contains \(u,u^{(1)},u^{(2)}\). Hence \(\rho_\mu(u)=1\).

For a cycle vertex, its closed radius-one ball consists of itself and its two cycle neighbors. Hence its neighborhood radius is also \(1\).

For a private leaf, the closed radius-one ball contains exactly itself and its parent, so its mass is \(2/N<3/N\). Its radius-two ball contains all sites. Thus every private leaf has neighborhood radius \(2\).

3. Correctness of the reduction

A dominating set gives feasible centers

Suppose \(D\subseteq U\) dominates \(G\) and \(|D|\leq b\).

In each added cycle, label the vertices cyclically as \(z_0,z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4\), and select \(z_0,z_2\). Their closed radius-one balls cover all five vertices: the first covers \(z_4,z_0,z_1\), and the second covers \(z_1,z_2,z_3\).

Let \(S\) consist of \(D\) together with these cycle centers. Then

\[ |S|\leq b+2q=k. \]

Every original vertex is within distance one of \(D\), as required by its neighborhood radius. Every cycle vertex is covered by the chosen cycle centers. Every private leaf is within distance at most two of any center, because the metric has diameter two.

Since \(U\) is nonempty, its dominating set \(D\) is nonempty. Therefore \(S\) is a feasible solution.

Feasible centers give a dominating set

Conversely, suppose \(S\subseteq V\) is feasible and \(|S|\leq k\).

Each cycle requires at least two centers inside that cycle. Indeed, outside centers have distance two from every cycle vertex, exceeding those vertices’ radius one. A single center inside the cycle covers only itself and its two neighbors, leaving two vertices uncovered.

Let \(Y\) be the centers selected among the original vertices and their private leaves. The cycle requirement implies

\[ |Y|\leq k-2q=b. \]

Replace every selected private leaf by its parent, retaining each selected original vertex, and remove duplicates. This produces \(D\subseteq U\) with \(|D|\leq|Y|\leq b\).

To verify domination, fix \(u\in U\). Feasibility and \(\rho_\mu(u)=1\) give a selected center at distance at most one from \(u\). Such a center is either \(u\), an original neighbor of \(u\), or one of \(u\)’s private leaves. After replacement, \(D\) therefore contains either \(u\) or an original neighbor of \(u\).

Thus \(D\) dominates \(G\), proving both directions of the reduction.

4. Encoding size and conclusion

The constructed instance satisfies \(m=\tau=N=3k\leq18n\). Distances have constant bit length; every mass \(1/N\) uses \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits. Therefore its total encoding length satisfies

\[ L=O\!\left(m^2+\tau\log(\tau+1)+\log(k+1)\right). \]

The construction can be implemented in \(O(n^2\log(n+1))\) bit operations, a conservative polynomial bound including writing the explicit matrix and all rational masses.

The reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the stated rational-mass input model. Together with the exact polynomial-time verifier, it proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness. In particular, a polynomial-time decision algorithm exists if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
