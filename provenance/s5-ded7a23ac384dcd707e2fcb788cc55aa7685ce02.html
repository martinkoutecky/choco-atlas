In particular, does a continuous population analogue of EFTT attain \((1-e^{-\gamma})\)-CEF and \(\left(1-\frac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\right)\)-USW, and does every randomized non-wasteful policy obey the Theorem 4.4 USW cap \(\frac{1+\alpha-e^{\alpha-1}}{1+\alpha}\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type represents an atomless population with unit capacity per individual, belongs to one of \(k\) nonempty classes, and has a fixed binary eligibility profile. Thus its aggregate capacity is \(\mu_t\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible profiles.
An oblivious adversary fixes a finite stream of \(m\) divisible cohorts \((b_r,A_r)\), where \(b_r>0\) is binary rational and \(A_r\subseteq T\). Compatibility with future cohorts is disclosed only on arrival; the horizon and future supply are unknown. A causal policy irrevocably chooses \(x_{rt}\geq0\), zero outside \(A_r\), satisfying \(\sum_t x_{rt}\leq b_r\) and \(\sum_r x_{rt}\leq\mu_t\). Every eligible unit has value one.
Write \(V_i(X)=\sum_{r,t:c(t)=i}x_{rt}\) and \(y_{jr}=\sum_{t:c(t)=j}x_{rt}\). For a bundle \(y\), define \(V_i^*(y)\) as the maximum total eligible allocation to class \(i\), with cohort capacities \(y_r\) and type capacities \(\mu_t\), independently of its actual allocation.
A policy is \(\alpha\)-CEF if \(\mathbb E[V_i(X)]\geq\alpha\mathbb E[V_i^*(y_j)]\) for every pair of classes, and \(\beta\)-USW if \(\mathbb E[\sum_iV_i(X)]\geq\beta\operatorname{OPT}\), where \(\operatorname{OPT}\) is the maximum offline feasible allocation. Guarantees hold for every finite stream, including every stopping prefix. Non-wastefulness means that any unallocated cohort supply has no compatible type with remaining capacity when that cohort finishes processing, almost surely.
For \(\gamma\in[0,1]\), define mass EFTT using loads \(\ell_t=(\sum_{s\leq r}x_{st})/\mu_t\): continuously split incoming supply equally among classes with a compatible type below \(\gamma\), water-filling the minimum-load compatible types within each class; when none remains, water-fill globally among compatible types below capacity. Split ties proportionally to type masses and stop at exhaustion of supply or compatible capacity. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\) and any encoded policy parameters, where \(L\) is total binary input length.

Which simultaneous CEF and USW competitive guarantees are attainable by causal policies in this mass model?
For every \(\gamma\in[0,1]\), does mass EFTT attain \((1-e^{-\gamma})\)-CEF and \(\left(1-\frac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\right)\)-USW, or can either guarantee fail; and for every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), is \(\frac{1+\alpha-e^{\alpha-1}}{1+\alpha}\) a universal USW cap for randomized non-wasteful \(\alpha\)-CEF policies, or can such a policy exceed it?

Result and scope

Both proposed extensions hold in the support-encoded mass model.

For every \(\gamma\in[0,1]\), mass EFTT is deterministic, causal, and non-wasteful, and simultaneously guarantees

\[ \text{placeholder} \]

\(a(\gamma)=1-e^{-\gamma}\) CEF and \(b(\gamma)=1-\dfrac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\) USW.

For every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), a randomized non-wasteful policy guaranteeing \(\alpha\)-CEF uniformly over all societies and finite streams has USW competitive ratio at most

\(U(\alpha)=\dfrac{1+\alpha-e^{\alpha-1}}{1+\alpha}\).

The qualification about uniformity matters: on a fixed society consisting of one type, greedy allocation guarantees both \(1\)-CEF and \(1\)-USW. I interpret “universal cap” in its usual competitive sense, allowing the adversary to choose the society, including its number of classes.

The proof uses exact finite representations. Their potentially enormous size affects neither the guarantees nor the running time of an implementation working directly with the supported types.

1. Finite-model results used

We use the two finite-model facts supplied in the question: finite-agent EFTT has the stated simultaneous guarantees, and every randomized non-wasteful \(\alpha\)-CEF policy has USW competitive ratio at most \(U(\alpha)\). These are Theorems 4.1 and 4.4 of [Borst and Springer](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23408v1).

Their model has unit-capacity agents, unit-supply divisible items, compatibility revealed on arrival, and the same expectation-based definition of randomized CEF. EFTT continuously fills classes equally below its threshold and water-fills within classes; ties can be implemented symmetrically among equal-load agents.

We prove that all relevant quantities and policy properties transfer exactly.

2. An exact aggregation lemma

Fix a positive integer \(D\). Suppose a finite instance contains:

  • \(n_t=D\mu_t\) unit-capacity copies of each type \(t\);
  • several items indexed by \(s\) in each cohort \(r\), all having neighborhood \(A_r\);
  • item capacities \(d_{rs}\), with \(S_r=\sum_s d_{rs}\).

Consider the corresponding mass instance with type capacities \(\mu_t\) and cohort capacities \(S_r/D\).

Lemma. The finite optimum equals \(D\) times the mass optimum. The same statement holds when allocation is restricted to any specified class.

Proof. A feasible finite allocation \(z\) projects to

\(w_{rt}=\dfrac1D\sum_s\sum_{a=1}^{n_t}z_{rs,ta}\).

Its cohort and type constraints follow by summing the corresponding finite constraints. Its value is the finite value divided by \(D\).

Conversely, let \(w\) be mass-feasible. For \(S_r>0\), define

\(z_{rs,ta}=\dfrac{D w_{rt}}{n_t}\dfrac{d_{rs}}{S_r}\).

For \(S_r=0\), set all these allocations to zero; feasibility forces \(w_{rt}=0\).

For each item,

\(\sum_t\sum_{a=1}^{n_t}z_{rs,ta} =\dfrac{D d_{rs}}{S_r}\sum_t w_{rt} \leq d_{rs}\).

For each copy of type \(t\),

\(\sum_r\sum_s z_{rs,ta} =\dfrac{D}{n_t}\sum_r w_{rt} \leq\dfrac{D\mu_t}{n_t}=1\).

Compatibility is preserved because every subitem of cohort \(r\) has neighborhood \(A_r\). The lifted value is \(D\sum_{r,t}w_{rt}\). Restricting both constructions to a class proves the class-specific assertion. \(\square\)

In particular, if \(Y_j\) is a finite class bundle and its aggregate mass bundle is

\(y_{jr}=\dfrac1D\sum_s Y_{j,rs}\),

apply the lemma with \(d_{rs}=Y_{j,rs}\). It gives the exact identity

\(V_i^*(y_j)=\dfrac1D V_{i,\mathrm{finite}}^*(Y_j)\).

This argument allows different bundle amounts on the subitems of one cohort.

3. Mass EFTT inherits both guarantees

Fix an arbitrary finite mass stream and an arbitrary \(\gamma\in[0,1]\). Since all masses and cohort supplies are rational, choose an integer \(D\) such that

\(n_t=D\mu_t\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), and \(q_r=Db_r\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\).

Because \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), there are exactly \(D\) cloned agents.

Replace cohort \(r\) by \(q_r\) consecutive unit-supply items, each with neighborhood consisting of all copies of its eligible types. Run finite EFTT with symmetric tie splitting.

Equality of the allocation processes

All copies of a type start at equal load. Symmetric water-filling preserves this equality. If their common load is \(z_t\), projecting their allocation gives mass load

\(\ell_t=\dfrac{n_tz_t/D}{\mu_t}=z_t\).

During the first phase, each active class receives the same amount of finite allocation. After division by \(D\), each receives the same amount of mass allocation.

Within a class, suppose the minimum-load eligible types form a set \(S\). Symmetric allocation among their copies assigns type \(t\in S\) the fraction

\(\dfrac{n_t}{\sum_{u\in S}n_u} =\dfrac{\mu_t}{\sum_{u\in S}\mu_u}\).

Thus the projected process has exactly the proportional tie splitting specified for mass EFTT. The same calculation applies to global water-filling in the second phase.

Finally, subdividing a cohort does not change this process. At a boundary between consecutive identical-neighborhood subitems, the allocation rule depends only on current loads and that neighborhood. Once no compatible type remains below \(\gamma\), subsequent identical-neighborhood subitems cannot reactivate the first phase.

Consequently, the projection of finite EFTT is precisely mass EFTT.

Transfer of the inequalities

Class values scale by \(D\), and Section 2 gives the same scaling for optimistic valuations and the offline optimum. Therefore finite EFTT’s guarantees imply

\(D V_i(X)\geq a(\gamma)D V_i^*(y_j)\),

and

\(D\sum_i V_i(X)\geq b(\gamma)D\operatorname{OPT}\).

Dividing by \(D\) proves both requested guarantees.

The argument applies independently to every finite stopping prefix. Choosing \(D\) using the entire tested prefix is a proof device: mass EFTT itself uses neither \(D\) nor future information.

Mass EFTT is non-wasteful because it stops with unallocated supply only after all compatible capacity is exhausted. \(\square\)

4. The randomized upper cap transfers

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that a mass policy \(\mathcal P\) is randomized, causal, non-wasteful, and uniformly \(\alpha\)-CEF, and guarantees USW ratio \(\beta>U(\alpha)\).

We construct a finite-agent policy with the same guarantees.

Take any finite instance with \(n\) unit-capacity agents. Choose a power of two \(D\geq n\). Represent each original agent by a type of mass \(1/D\), retaining its class.

If \(D>n\), add one dummy type of mass \((D-n)/D\), assigned to an existing class. The dummy type is incompatible with every item. Total mass is now one, and all masses are positive dyadic rationals.

Represent each arriving finite item by a cohort of supply \(1/D\), with the corresponding eligible types. Run \(\mathcal P\), and multiply its allocations by \(D\).

This defines a causal finite policy: \(D\) depends only on the initially known number of agents, and each simulated cohort requires only the current item’s neighborhood.

For every realization of the policy’s randomness,

\(V_{i,\mathrm{finite}}=D V_{i,\mathrm{mass}}\),

\(V_{i,\mathrm{finite}}^*(Y_j)=D V_{i,\mathrm{mass}}^*(y_j)\),

and

\(\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{finite}}=D\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\).

The dummy type contributes nothing to any of these quantities.

Non-wastefulness also transfers pathwise: positive unallocated finite-item supply corresponds to positive unallocated cohort supply, and an original compatible agent has remaining capacity exactly when its corresponding type does.

Taking expectations therefore gives a finite randomized non-wasteful policy with \(\alpha\)-CEF and \(\beta\)-USW. This contradicts the supplied finite-model upper bound.

If “types” must have distinct complete eligibility profiles, append type-specific diagnostic cohorts after the tested prefix. These make the active profiles distinct without changing any allocation or benchmark on that prefix. Unknown horizon and prefix guarantees make this modification legitimate.

For completeness, the endpoint \(\alpha=1\) follows even if the finite upper-bound theorem is invoked only for \(\alpha<1\): a \(1\)-CEF policy is \(a\)-CEF for every \(a<1\), so its USW ratio is at most \(\lim_{a\uparrow1}U(a)=1/2\). \(\square\)

5. Running time on supported types

The cloning construction is unnecessary for implementation.

Assume \(\gamma\) is rational, with encoding length \(P\). For an arbitrary real \(\gamma\), the competitive guarantees remain mathematical statements; a computational bound requires an effective representation of that parameter.

During the first phase, let \(h\) be the number of active classes. For each active class \(i\), maintain its minimum compatible load \(z_i\), the total mass \(M_i\) tied at that load, and the next load event \(\theta_i\), which is either another compatible load level or \(\gamma\).

When total incoming supply is processed at rate one, this tied set’s load rises at rate \(1/(hM_i)\). Thus the next event occurs after processing

\(\Delta=\min\left\{R,\ \min_i hM_i(\theta_i-z_i)\right\}\),

where \(R\) is remaining supply.

Each event either exhausts supply, joins at least one additional type to a class’s minimum-load set, or removes a class at the threshold. Within one cohort, each type joins at most once and each class is removed at most once. Hence this phase has \(O(\tau+k)\) events.

Global water-filling similarly has \(O(\tau)\) events. Recomputing all relevant sets by scanning the support costs \(O(\tau+k)\) rational operations per event. Since \(k\leq\tau\), total arithmetic time is

\(O\bigl(m\tau(\tau+k)\bigr)=O(m\tau^2)\).

Exact rational arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity. A conservative bound on intermediate numerator and denominator lengths is

\(B=O\!\left((1+m\tau k)\bigl(L+P+\log(\tau+k+2)\bigr)\right)\).

To justify this bound, between events the updates are affine rational functions of the current loads and remaining supply. Their coefficients use sums of input masses, integers at most \(k\), and the encoded threshold. Each mass sum has bit length \(O(L)\). At an event, a common denominator for all coefficients can be chosen with bit length \(O\bigl(k(L+P+\log(\tau+k+2))\bigr)\). Multiplying the previous common denominator by this denominator bounds growth additively over \(O(m\tau)\) events. Loads remain in \([0,1]\), and remaining supply is bounded by its encoded initial amount, so numerators satisfy the same polynomial bound.

Using a conservative \(O(B^3)\) cost per exact rational operation yields bit running time

\(O\bigl(m\tau(\tau+k)B^3\bigr)\).

Thus implementation is polynomial in the support encoding and parameter length, independently of the number of clones in the proof.

6. Attainable guarantees

The universally attainable region includes every pair \((\alpha,\beta)\) satisfying, for some \(\gamma\in[0,1]\),

\(\alpha\leq1-e^{-\gamma}\), and \(\beta\leq1-\dfrac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\).

In particular, for \(0<\alpha\leq1-e^{-1}\), choosing \(\gamma=-\log(1-\alpha)\) guarantees \(\alpha\)-CEF together with

\(\beta=1-\dfrac{1}{e(1-\alpha)(1-\log(1-\alpha))}\).

Every uniformly non-wasteful randomized \(\alpha\)-CEF policy is subject to the cap \(U(\alpha)\). These achievable and upper-bound curves generally differ; the transfer results settle the two explicit extension questions without establishing an exact Pareto frontier.
