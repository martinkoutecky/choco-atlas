The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of complete household types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a piecewise-linear demand schedule \(q_t(p)\) for slot \(h\) at fixed prices in other slots, and a per-capita supply cost \(g(z)\), compute the lowest nonnegative price \(p\) with positive trade satisfying \(p z(p)=g(z(p))\), where \(z(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t q_{t,h}(p)\). Return that price and each type’s demand there, allowing mass to split across feasible schedules at ties; return no trade if no such price exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(m\geq1\) time slots, a slot \(h\), and nonnegative rational prices in the other slots. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\geq1\) complete household types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type supplies its bounded, nonnegative, weakly downward-sloping demand schedule for slot \(h\), explicitly described by rational piecewise-affine segments on \([0,\infty)\). At a jump price, the two endpoint demands represent tied feasible household schedules; any convex mixture is allowed by splitting that type's mass. At other prices its demand is the specified value. These schedules are input, rather than household optimization problems to solve.
The per-capita total supply cost \(g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)\) is continuous and nondecreasing, supplied as rational polynomial pieces of degree at most \(d\), with rational breakpoints. Let \(K\) count all demand and cost pieces and tie endpoints, and let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
For selected admissible per-household demands \(y_t\) at price \(p\), define \(z=\sum_t\mu_t y_t\). Compute the least \(p\geq0\) admitting \(z>0\) and \(pz=g(z)\), together with each type's demand and any mass split used; return no trade if no such price exists. Prices and demands are exact real algebraic quantities.

Is this minimum-price computation solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,d,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many bidders, bidder-submodular coalition worth (a bidder's marginal contribution decreases as the coalition grows) and computed minimum competitive equilibrium prices yield VCG allocations and straightforward bidding as a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. 1. Result and computational model. The computation is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,d,L\), with exact real algebraic output. Whenever positive trade is possible, a least feasible price exists. A witnessing allocation can be chosen with at most one type split nontrivially between its tied endpoints.

The argument uses the supplied demand schedules directly. It does not require solving household optimization problems or enumerating possible household types.

An exact algebraic number may be represented by a square-free integer polynomial and a rational interval containing exactly one of its real roots. We use the standard fact that real-root isolation, including square-free reduction, takes polynomial bit time in the polynomial’s degree and coefficient bit length, and produces isolating intervals of polynomial bit length. Comparisons with rational numbers also take polynomial time, using polynomial evaluation and Sturm root counts. See Theorem 5.1, Remark 5.2, and Section 6 of [Emiris, Mourrain, and Tsigaridas](https://who.paris.inria.fr/Elias.Tsigaridas/files/emt-lncs-2006.pdf).

All applications below involve univariate polynomials of polynomially bounded degree and coefficient bit length.

2. Aggregate demand and recovery of mass splits. At price \(p\), let the admissible mean demands of type \(t\) form the interval \([\ell_t(p),u_t(p)]\). At a price without a tie, this interval is a singleton. At a jump, it is the interval between the two tied endpoint demands, because the type’s mass may be split.

Define \(Z_-(p)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\ell_t(p)\) and \(Z_+(p)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_tu_t(p)\).

The set of admissible aggregate demands is exactly \([Z_-(p),Z_+(p)]\). Here is a constructive proof, which also supplies the required allocation.

Fix any \(z\) in this interval. Start every type at its lower endpoint. The additional aggregate demand required is \(R=z-Z_-(p)\), and type \(t\) has additional capacity \(c_t=\mu_t(u_t(p)-\ell_t(p))\). Thus \(0\le R\le\sum_t c_t\).

Process the types in any fixed order. Give type \(t\) additional aggregate demand \(w_t=\min\{R,c_t\}\), and replace \(R\) by \(R-w_t\). The final residual is zero: after processing the first \(s\) types, it equals \(\max\{0,z-Z_-(p)-\sum_{t=1}^{s}c_t\}\).

For a type with \(u_t(p)>\ell_t(p)\), set \(\lambda_t=w_t/(\mu_t(u_t(p)-\ell_t(p)))\). Assign mass \(\mu_t\lambda_t\) to demand \(u_t(p)\), and mass \(\mu_t(1-\lambda_t)\) to demand \(\ell_t(p)\). Its mean demand is \(\ell_t(p)+w_t/\mu_t\).

Every type processed before the residual first reaches zero is assigned its full additional capacity. Every subsequent type receives zero additional demand. Consequently, at most one type has \(0<\lambda_t<1\).

3. A polynomial-size partition of the price axis. Collect \(0\) and every finite demand breakpoint. Between consecutive collected prices, and on the final unbounded interval, every type has a single affine demand formula \(y_t(p)=a_t+b_tp\), where \(b_t\le0\).

Therefore aggregate demand on such an interval is \(z(p)=A+Bp\), with \(A=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\) and \(B=\sum_t\mu_tb_t\le0\). There are \(O(K)\) initial open intervals.

For each initial interval with \(B<0\), and each finite cost breakpoint \(c\), insert the crossing price \(p=(c-A)/B\) if it lies inside that interval. When \(B=0\), no crossing price is needed.

After sorting and removing duplicates, this gives \(O(K^2)\) rational boundary prices and open intervals. On each resulting open interval \(I\):

  • aggregate demand has one affine formula \(z(p)=A+Bp\);
  • one cost polynomial \(G\) satisfies \(g(z(p))=G(z(p))\) throughout \(I\).

To justify the second assertion, a nonconstant aggregate demand cannot cross a cost breakpoint inside a refined interval. If aggregate demand is constant at a cost breakpoint, either adjacent cost polynomial may be used: continuity makes their values agree there.

All boundary prices are rational and have polynomial bit length.

4. Exact feasibility at a boundary price. Fix one rational boundary price \(q\). Evaluate each type’s admissible endpoints and obtain the rational aggregate interval \([Z_-(q),Z_+(q)]\).

For each cost piece \(G_j\), let its closed domain be \(C_j\), with the final domain allowed to be unbounded. Cost continuity ensures that using closed domains is valid at shared breakpoints. Form \(J_j=[Z_-(q),Z_+(q)]\cap C_j\) and \(H_j(X)=G_j(X)-qX\).

There is feasible positive trade at \(q\) precisely when some \(J_j\) contains a positive zero of \(H_j\).

If \(H_j\) is nonzero, isolate its real roots and retain exactly those satisfying \(z\in J_j\) and \(z>0\). These are comparisons with rational bounds.

If \(H_j\) is identically zero, write the nonempty interval \(J_j\) as \([v,w]\). Its upper endpoint is finite because aggregate demand is bounded. It contains a positive feasible demand exactly when \(w>0\); in that case choose \(z=w\).

Thus boundary feasibility, and a witnessing aggregate demand, can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Section 2 then recovers the individual demands and mass splits.

This procedure explicitly excludes zero trade, including when \(g(0)=0\).

5. Exact feasibility inside an open interval. Let \(I=(a,b)\) be a refined open interval, allowing \(b=\infty\). Its balance polynomial is \(F(P)=P(A+BP)-G(A+BP)\), of degree at most \(D=\max\{2,d\}\).

First, a nonnegative affine function on an open interval is either identically zero or strictly positive throughout. Indeed, if it vanishes at an interior point and has nonzero slope, it is negative on one side of that point. Therefore \(z(p)\) is either identically zero on \(I\), in which case the interval admits no positive trade, or positive throughout \(I\).

Suppose it is positive.

If \(F\) is nonzero, the feasible prices inside \(I\) are exactly its real roots lying in \(I\). Root isolation and rational-bound comparisons determine whether this set is empty and, when it is nonempty, find its least member.

If \(F\) is identically zero, every price in \(I\) admits positive trade. The following endpoint argument is essential.

For each type, let \(y_t^a=a_t+b_ta\), its demand limit as \(p\) approaches \(a\) from above. This is admissible at \(a\): at a demand jump it is the right tied endpoint, and otherwise it is the specified continuous value. Hence \(z_a=\sum_t\mu_ty_t^a=A+Ba\) is admissible at \(a\).

For any \(p_0\in I\), the inequality \(B\le0\) gives \(z_a\ge A+Bp_0>0\). Taking the limit in \(p(A+Bp)=g(A+Bp)\), and using continuity of \(g\), gives \(az_a=g(z_a)\).

Therefore the left endpoint \(a\) itself admits positive trade. An identically vanishing balance polynomial cannot produce feasible trade whose least price is an excluded endpoint.

6. Algorithm and proof of minimality. Scan the refined partition in increasing price order, starting at \(0\). At each boundary price, first apply the feasibility procedure of Section 4. If feasible, return that price and its witness.

Otherwise examine the following open interval. Skip it if aggregate demand is identically zero. If its balance polynomial is nonzero, return its smallest root in the interval, if one exists. An identically zero balance polynomial with positive demand would imply that the boundary just checked was feasible, by Section 5.

If the scan finishes, including the final unbounded interval, return no trade.

Every returned witness satisfies admissibility, \(z>0\), and \(pz=g(z)\). Before returning, the scan has excluded every earlier boundary and open interval; within the current interval it selects the smallest feasible root. The returned price is therefore the global least feasible price.

Conversely, any feasible price belongs either to a boundary or to an open interval. Boundary feasibility is tested exactly. In an open interval, feasibility is either a root of a nonzero balance polynomial or part of an identically balanced interval whose left endpoint is feasible. Thus the scan cannot miss positive trade. This also proves that a least feasible price exists whenever any feasible price exists.

7. Bit complexity and exact output. There are \(O(K^2)\) boundaries and open intervals. Each boundary requires at most \(O(K)\) univariate polynomial tests. Each open interval requires at most one root-isolation computation. Every polynomial has degree at most \(D\).

Their coefficient lengths are polynomially bounded. Aggregating the demands requires rational products and sums over the \(\tau\) explicitly listed types. For a cost polynomial \(G(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_iX^i\), affine substitution gives the coefficient formula \([P^j]G(A+BP)=\sum_{i=j}^{d}\gamma_i\binom{i}{j}A^{i-j}B^j\).

The exponents are at most \(d\), the binomial coefficients have \(O(d)\) bits, and there are polynomially many rational arithmetic operations. Powers and products increase bit lengths additively; clearing denominators adds the lengths of polynomially many denominators. Thus the resulting integer polynomials have coefficient bit lengths polynomial in \(\tau,d,L\).

The cited univariate algorithms consequently apply with polynomially bounded inputs. Sorting boundaries, testing roots against rational bounds, and recovering the allocation also take polynomial bit time.

For an interior solution, the returned price is an algebraic root \(\alpha\), and each demand is the affine expression \(a_t+b_t\alpha\). For a boundary solution, the price is rational and the aggregate demand is rational or an algebraic root \(\alpha\). The greedy recovery procedure produces rational quantities and affine expressions in this same \(\alpha\).

These are exact algebraic outputs of polynomial description length. If separate polynomial-and-isolating-interval representations are desired, an affine expression \(r+s\alpha\), with \(s\ne0\), is represented by transforming the defining polynomial \(P\) to \(s^{\deg P}P((X-r)/s)\), clearing denominators, and applying the same affine transformation to the isolating interval.

The runtime and output length are therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,d,L\). The parameter \(m\) introduces no additional optimization because the slot’s demand schedules are already supplied.
