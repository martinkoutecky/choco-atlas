The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) and category supplies \(\rho_j\), each summing to \(1\), and normalized values \(v_t(j)\in[0,1]\), define a market with \(D\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(D\rho_j\) items of category \(j\), for common-denominator multiples \(D\). What is the limiting per-capita expected welfare \(W_\infty=\lim_{D\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SW}(\mathrm{RSD})]/D\), and can it be computed to additive error \(\varepsilon\)? Compute also \(\nu^*=\max_x\sum_{t,j}v_t(j)x_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tj}=\rho_j\), and determine whether \(W_\infty\ge(\nu^*)^2/e\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) item categories with positive binary-rational supplies \(\rho_j\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_j\rho_j=1\); type values \(v_t(j)\in[0,1]\) are binary rationals, and equal values are allowed.
For each positive integer \(D\) divisible by a common denominator of the masses and supplies, form \(D\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(D\rho_j\) distinct items of category \(j\); every such item has value \(v_t(j)\) to type \(t\).
Random Serial Dictatorship (RSD) chooses a uniformly random agent ordering; each agent takes a highest-valued remaining item, uniformly among all tied individual items, including when all remaining values are zero. Every agent receives exactly one item; \(\mathrm{SW}\) is the sum of assigned values.
Write \(W_\infty=\lim_{D\to\infty}\mathbb E[\mathrm{SW}(\mathrm{RSD})]/D\), along these admissible replication sizes, whenever the limit exists.
Define \(\nu^*=\max_{x\ge0}\sum_{t,j}v_t(j)x_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tj}=\rho_j\). Let \(0<\varepsilon<1\) be binary rational and \(L\) the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\).

Does \(W_\infty\) exist for every such input, and can it be characterized and computed to additive error \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), together with \(\nu^*\)?
Does the universal approximation guarantee \(W_\infty\ge(\nu^*)^2/e\) hold, or is there a counterexample?

For finite normalized-value assignments, RSD satisfies \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{SW}]\ge\nu(O)^2/(en)\), where \(\nu(O)\) is optimal welfare and \(n\) is the common number of agents and items. [Adamczyk–Sankowski–Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.3957)
For strict category rankings and equal category quotas, replica RSD converges to continuum probabilistic serial; this result does not address ties between categories resolved uniformly over remaining individual items. [Che–Kojima](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d1677.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required values to be binary rationals so the computational input has a finite bit length.
  • Specified uniform tie-breaking over remaining individual items, rather than uniformly over tied categories, following the discrete RSD definition.
  • Required assignment even when every remaining item has value zero, following the complete-assignment model.
  • Removed zero-supply categories and used positive supplies for the listed categories.
  • Added nonnegativity of allocation masses to the definition of the continuous optimum.
  • Made existence of the replica limit part of the target instead of assuming it.
  • Specified a binary-rational accuracy parameter and polynomial dependence on its reciprocal.

Result

For every input in the question, \(W_\infty\) exists. It is characterized by the remaining-supply flow defined below, and it can be approximated deterministically to additive error \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\). The transportation optimum \(\nu^*\) can be computed exactly in polynomial time.

The universal guarantee also holds:

\( \displaystyle W_\infty\ge \frac{(\nu^*)^2}{e}. \)

The proof includes the specified rule for ties between categories and for zero-valued remaining items.

1. The remaining-supply flow

Throughout, the type masses \(\mu_t\) are fixed and satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

For a nonzero inventory vector \(r\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m\), define

\( \displaystyle S(r)=\{j:r_j>0\},\qquad B_t(r)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{j\in S(r)}v_t(j). \)

Define the type-specific choice probabilities, aggregate consumption rates, and instantaneous welfare by

\( \displaystyle q_{tj}(r)= \begin{cases} \frac{r_j}{\sum_{k\in B_t(r)}r_k},&j\in B_t(r),\\ 0,&j\notin B_t(r), \end{cases} \qquad f_j(r)=\sum_t\mu_tq_{tj}(r), \)

\( \displaystyle G(r)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_{j\in S(r)}v_t(j). \)

Set \(q_{tj}(0)=f_j(0)=G(0)=0\).

The candidate limiting inventory satisfies

\[ \displaystyle \dot r_j(s)=-f_j(r(s)),\qquad r(0)=\rho. \tag{1} \]

Categories with zero inventory remain unavailable. Notice that the probabilities within a top tie are proportional to remaining supplies. These are precisely the probabilities induced by uniform choice among tied individual items.

For an arbitrary initial inventory \(a\ge0\), write \(\Phi_s(a)\) for its flow, continued as zero after exhaustion, and define

\[ \displaystyle H(a)=\int_0^\infty G(\Phi_s(a))\,ds. \tag{2} \]

We will prove that

\[ \displaystyle W_\infty=H(\rho)=\int_0^1G(r(s))\,ds. \tag{3} \]

Existence of the flow

On any fixed positive support \(S\), the sets \(B_t\) are fixed. The field \(f\) is then a smooth rational function of the positive coordinates. Moreover,

\[ \displaystyle 0\le f_j(r)\le1,\qquad \sum_jf_j(r)=1 \quad(r\ne0). \tag{4} \]

Consequently, a solution exists uniquely until it approaches the boundary of that support. Its coordinates are nonincreasing and have bounded derivatives, so they have limits at the boundary time.

If the limiting inventory is nonzero, delete all coordinates that have reached zero and restart on the smaller support. If no earlier boundary occurs, conservation of total inventory forces exhaustion at time \(\sum_ja_j\). Each restart deletes at least one category, so there are at most \(m\) extinction times.

This constructs an absolutely continuous solution of (1), with

\[ \displaystyle \sum_j\Phi_s(a)_j=\max\!\left\{\sum_ja_j-s,0\right\}. \tag{5} \]

We next establish uniqueness and the stability needed for computation.

2. Order preservation and contraction

Choice monotonicity

Let \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), and suppose inventories \(r,z\) satisfy

\( \displaystyle r_j\ge z_j\quad(j\in I),\qquad r_j\le z_j\quad(j\notin I). \)

For every type \(t\),

\[ \displaystyle \sum_{j\in I}q_{tj}(r)\ge\sum_{j\in I}q_{tj}(z). \tag{6} \]

Here is a direct verification. If the probability on the right is zero, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let \(a\) be that type’s best available value inside \(I\) under \(z\). Its best value outside \(I\) under \(z\) is at most \(a\).

Under \(r\), the best value inside \(I\) is at least \(a\), and the best value outside \(I\) is at most \(a\). If either comparison becomes strict in favor of \(I\), the choice probability of \(I\) under \(r\) is one. In the remaining case, both sides compete at value \(a\). The mass inside \(I\) at that value has increased, while the competing mass outside \(I\) has decreased. The fraction of tied mass belonging to \(I\) therefore cannot decrease. This proves (6), including empty-inventory cases.

After averaging over types, (6) also holds for \(f\).

Contraction lemma

For any two flow solutions,

\[ \displaystyle \sum_j\bigl(\Phi_s(a)_j-\Phi_s(b)_j\bigr)_+ \le \sum_j(a_j-b_j)_+. \tag{7} \]

Indeed, at almost every time let \(I\) consist of the coordinates where the first inventory exceeds the second. Differentiating the positive-part sum gives

\( \displaystyle -\sum_{j\in I}f_j(\Phi_s(a)) +\sum_{j\in I}f_j(\Phi_s(b)) \le0 \)

by (6). For absolutely continuous coordinate differences, their derivatives vanish almost everywhere on their zero sets; thus equality coordinates cause no missing terms in this differentiation.

Applying (7) in both directions proves

\[ \displaystyle \|\Phi_s(a)-\Phi_s(b)\|_1\le\|a-b\|_1. \tag{8} \]

In particular, the flow is unique. Also, applying (7) with the inventories reversed shows that

\[ \displaystyle a\ge b\ \Longrightarrow\ \Phi_s(a)\ge\Phi_s(b)\quad\text{for every }s. \tag{9} \]

3. Welfare stability

Although \(G\) is discontinuous when a category disappears, its integrated value is stable:

\[ \displaystyle |H(a)-H(b)|\le m\|a-b\|_1. \tag{10} \]

We prove this first when \(a\ge b\). Put \(\delta=\sum_j(a_j-b_j)\), and let \(r(s)=\Phi_s(a)\), \(z(s)=\Phi_s(b)\).

Order preservation gives \(r(s)\ge z(s)\). Until \(z\) is exhausted, the total inventory difference is exactly \(\delta\); afterwards, the total remaining inventory of \(r\) is at most \(\delta\).

For each category \(j\), let \(\sigma_j\) be its extinction time in \(z\), taking \(\sigma_j=0\) when \(b_j=0\). Then

\[ \displaystyle r_j(\sigma_j)\le\delta. \tag{11} \]

Consider a type whose best available value in \(r(s)\) exceeds its best available value in \(z(s)\). Every category in that type’s top tie under \(r(s)\) must already be exhausted in \(z(s)\). Otherwise, the same best value would still be available in \(z(s)\).

Since values lie in \([0,1]\), that type’s contribution to \(G(r(s))-G(z(s))\) is at most its consumption rate into categories already exhausted in \(z\). This remains true after \(z\) itself is exhausted. Therefore,

\[ \displaystyle 0\le H(a)-H(b) \le\sum_j\int_{\sigma_j}^{\infty}f_j(r(s))\,ds =\sum_jr_j(\sigma_j) \le m\delta. \tag{12} \]

For general \(a,b\), use their coordinatewise minimum and maximum. Their flows bracket both original flows by (9), and \(G\) is nondecreasing with respect to inventory inclusion. The difference between the maximum and minimum inventories has total mass \(\|a-b\|_1\), so (12) proves (10).

4. Convergence of replica RSD

We now prove (3), without using a strict-ranking convergence theorem.

For replication size \(D\), let \(R^D(s)\) be the remaining category inventories divided by \(D\), linearly interpolated between successive assignments at times \(k/D\). Every sample path satisfies

\[ \displaystyle R^D(0)=\rho,\qquad \sum_jR_j^D(s)=1-s. \tag{13} \]

Its coordinates are nonincreasing, and the path is \(1\)-Lipschitz in the \(\ell_1\) norm.

Type frequencies and martingale errors

Let \(U_t^D(k/D)\) be the number of type-\(t\) agents among the first \(k\) positions, divided by \(D\). Uniform random ordering gives

\[ \displaystyle \sup_{0\le s\le1}|U_t^D(s)-\mu_ts| \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0. \tag{14} \]

For completeness, at a fixed time the prefix count is hypergeometric, with variance at most \(D/4\). Chebyshev’s inequality gives convergence at every point of a finite time grid. Monotonicity between grid points, followed by refinement of that grid, proves (14).

Conditioned on the first \(k\) assignments, the probability that the next agent has type \(t\) is

\( \displaystyle p_t^D(k)=\frac{\mu_t-U_t^D(k/D)}{1-k/D}. \)

Consequently, for each fixed \(\eta>0\), (14) implies

\[ \displaystyle \sup_{k/D\le1-\eta}|p_t^D(k)-\mu_t| \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0. \tag{15} \]

Conditional on this next type and on the inventory, its category-choice probabilities are exactly \(q_{tj}\). Thus the conditional expected category-consumption rate is

\( \displaystyle f_j(R^D,p^D)=\sum_tp_t^Dq_{tj}(R^D). \)

Subtracting these conditional expectations from actual normalized category consumptions produces martingales. Each increment has absolute value at most \(1/D\), so its terminal second moment is at most \(1/D\). Doob’s maximal inequality therefore shows that the supremum of each martingale error tends to zero in probability. The same argument applies to normalized welfare.

Identification of every subsequential limit

Consider any sequence of sample paths on which the errors in (14) and the preceding martingale errors tend to zero. Compactness of the uniformly Lipschitz inventory paths gives a uniformly convergent subsequence with limit \(r\).

Fix a closed interval \([a,b]\subset[0,1)\) on which the positive support \(S\) of \(r\) is constant. Its coordinates in \(S\) have a positive lower bound. Coordinates outside \(S\) converge uniformly to zero.

For type \(t\), let \(q_t^S(y_S)\) be its choice distribution restricted to inventory \(y_S\), and let \(c_t(y)\) be its actual probability of choosing outside \(S\). Whenever all categories in \(S\) are available,

\[ \displaystyle q_t(y)|_S=(1-c_t(y))q_t^S(y_S). \tag{16} \]

To verify (16), if an outside category has strictly higher value than every category in \(S\), its left side and the factor \(1-c_t(y)\) are both zero. Otherwise, choices inside \(S\), conditional on making such a choice, are proportional to the inventories in its best tie within \(S\).

Actual consumption outside \(S\) on \([a,b]\) tends to zero, because those inventories converge to zero. The category martingale bounds imply that its conditional expected consumption also tends to zero:

\[ \displaystyle \int_a^b\sum_tp_t^D(s)c_t(R^D(s))\,ds\longrightarrow0. \tag{17} \]

Equation (16) gives

\[ \displaystyle \left\|f(R^D,p^D)|_S-f^S(R_S^D,\mu)\right\|_1 \le \|p^D-\mu\|_1+\sum_tp_t^Dc_t(R^D). \tag{18} \]

On this interval, \(f^S\) is continuous in the positive coordinates. Equations (15), (17), and (18), together with the category martingale identities, therefore imply

\[ \displaystyle r(b)-r(a)=-\int_a^bf(r(s))\,ds. \tag{19} \]

Each coordinate of \(r\) is nonincreasing and can become zero only once. Hence there are at most \(m\) support-change times. Applying (19) within the intervening intervals identifies \(r\) as an absolutely continuous solution of (1) almost everywhere. Uniqueness from Section 2 identifies it with \(\Phi_s(\rho)\).

This proves convergence in probability of the inventory paths. Explicitly, if uniform convergence in probability failed, one could select paths a fixed distance from \(\Phi_s(\rho)\) while all the preceding errors tend to zero; compactness and (19) would contradict uniqueness.

Welfare convergence

On the same interval \([a,b]\), let \(g_p(y)\) be conditional expected welfare of the next assignment. Then

\[ \displaystyle 0\le g_p(y)-\sum_tp_t\max_{j\in S}v_t(j) \le\sum_tp_tc_t(y). \tag{20} \]

Indeed, any strictly higher best value outside \(S\) forces that type to choose outside \(S\), and the value difference is at most one.

Equations (15), (17), and (20) show convergence of the integrated conditional welfare on every such interval. The welfare martingale error vanishes as well. Small neighborhoods of the finitely many support-change times and of the endpoint contribute arbitrarily little, since welfare per assignment is bounded by one. Therefore,

\[ \displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{SW}(\mathrm{RSD}_D)}{D} \xrightarrow{\mathbb P}\int_0^1G(\Phi_s(\rho))\,ds. \tag{21} \]

The random variables on the left belong to \([0,1]\), so convergence in probability also implies convergence of expectations. This proves existence of \(W_\infty\) and formula (3).

One may additionally characterize the limiting assignment by

\( \displaystyle x_{tj}^{\infty}=\int_0^1\mu_tq_{tj}(r(s))\,ds. \)

Its row sums are \(\mu_t\), its column sums are \(\rho_j\), and its welfare is \(W_\infty\).

5. A deterministic polynomial-time approximation

Direct numerical integration near a vanishing inventory could have a large derivative bound. We instead discard small inventories and use the welfare stability bound (10) to control the cost.

Choose rational parameters

\[ \displaystyle \alpha=\frac{\varepsilon}{16m^2},\qquad h=\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{16m} =\frac{\varepsilon^2}{256m^3},\qquad \beta=\frac{\varepsilon h}{32m^2}. \tag{22} \]

A cleanup operation rounds each coordinate down to a multiple of \(\beta\), then deletes every positive coordinate smaller than \(\alpha\).

Start with the cleaned inventory \(y=\rho\) and accumulator \(A=0\). While \(\sum_jy_j\ge h\):

  • Compute \(f(y)\) and \(G(y)\) exactly.
  • Add \(hG(y)\) to \(A\).
  • Form \(z=y-hf(y)\).
  • Replace \(y\) by the cleanup of \(z\).

Return \(A\). Any remaining inventory of mass less than \(h\) is ignored.

Local integration error

At the start of each iteration, every positive coordinate is at least \(\alpha\). Since \(h\le\alpha/2\) and \(f_j\le1\), neither the exact flow nor the Euler step loses a category during that step.

For a fixed top tie \(B\), the choice map \(q_j=y_j/\sum_{k\in B}y_k\) has Jacobian with induced \(\ell_1\) norm at most \(2/\sum_{k\in B}y_k\). Averaging over types shows that, where every positive coordinate is at least \(\alpha/2\),

\[ \displaystyle \|Df\|_{1\to1}\le\frac4\alpha. \tag{23} \]

Also, by total consumption,

\( \displaystyle \|\Phi_u(y)-y\|_1=u\qquad(0\le u\le h). \)

Thus

\[ \displaystyle \|\Phi_h(y)-(y-hf(y))\|_1 \le\int_0^h\frac{4u}{\alpha}\,du =\frac{2h^2}{\alpha}. \tag{24} \]

Because the support is unchanged throughout the exact step, its welfare is exactly \(hG(y)\).

Global error

Each iteration removes at least mass \(h\), so the number \(N\) of iterations is at most \(1/h\).

Each category is deleted at most once, at inventory less than \(\alpha\); total deletion loss is therefore at most \(m\alpha\). Each cleanup rounds away at most \(m\beta\), including the initial cleanup.

Apply (10) after each local Euler error and each cleanup. The identity

\( \displaystyle H(y)=hG(y)+H(\Phi_h(y)) \)

then telescopes over all iterations. The final omitted welfare is at most \(h\). Consequently,

\[ \displaystyle |H(\rho)-A| \le \frac{2mh}{\alpha} +m^2\alpha +m^2\beta(1/h+1) +h. \tag{25} \]

With (22), the four terms are bounded respectively by \(\varepsilon/8\), \(\varepsilon/16\), \(\varepsilon/16\), and \(\varepsilon/256\). In particular,

\[ \displaystyle |A-W_\infty|<\varepsilon. \tag{26} \]

Bit complexity and running time

There are

\( \displaystyle O(m^3/\varepsilon^2) \)

iterations. Each needs \(O(\tau m)\) rational arithmetic operations and value comparisons.

At iteration boundaries every inventory is an integer multiple of \(\beta\) and lies in \([0,1]\). Within a tie, its choice probabilities are therefore ratios of integers of bit length \(O(\log(m/\beta))\). Summing over the explicitly supplied \(\tau\) types gives intermediate rational operands of bit length

\( \displaystyle O\!\left(L+\tau\log(m/\varepsilon)\right). \)

Rounding resets inventories to the grid after every step. Accumulated rewards can use a fixed common denominator formed from the input denominators and the denominator of \(h\), so their bit lengths also remain polynomial.

A resulting bit-time bound is

\[ \displaystyle O\!\left( \frac{\tau m^4}{\varepsilon^2} \operatorname{poly}\!\left(L+\tau\log(m/\varepsilon)\right) \right). \tag{27} \]

The bound uses only the listed support types, and has no reciprocal dependence on the smallest mass or supply.

6. Computing \(\nu^*\)

The defining transportation program has \(m\tau\) variables, \(\tau+m\) marginal equalities, rational coefficients, and a nonempty bounded feasible region. Nonemptiness follows, for example, from \(x_{tj}=\mu_t\rho_j\).

The standard polynomial bit-complexity theorem for rational linear programming states that an exact optimal objective value of a feasible bounded rational linear program can be computed in time polynomial in its encoding length. Its hypotheses hold here, so \(\nu^*\) is computable exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Combined with (27), both requested quantities are obtained in polynomial time.

7. Passing the welfare guarantee to the limit

Fix an admissible replication size \(D\), and let \(\nu(O_D)\) be optimal welfare in its finite assignment instance. Then

\[ \displaystyle \nu(O_D)=D\nu^*. \tag{28} \]

To justify equality, the scaled transportation program has integer row margins \(D\mu_t\) and column margins \(D\rho_j\). It has an integral optimum.

Here is a verification of that integrality fact. At a vertex, the bipartite graph of positive entries cannot contain a cycle: alternating sufficiently small increases and decreases around a cycle would express the point as the midpoint of two distinct feasible points. Thus its positive support is a forest. Repeatedly remove a leaf edge. Its value equals the remaining integer margin at its leaf; subtracting it preserves integer margins. Induction proves that every positive entry is integral.

Any such integer matrix can be realized by assigning the corresponding numbers of distinct category items to agents of each type. Conversely, every finite assignment gives such a matrix. Scaling the objective therefore proves (28).

The finite theorem supplied in the question applies: there are exactly \(D\) agents and \(D\) items, all values lie in \([0,1]\), and RSD chooses a best remaining item with uniform random tie-breaking. These are also the model assumptions in [Adamczyk–Sankowski–Zhang](https://arxiv.org/html/1407.3957v2).

It yields

\( \displaystyle \mathbb E[\mathrm{SW}(\mathrm{RSD}_D)] \ge\frac{\nu(O_D)^2}{eD} =\frac{D(\nu^*)^2}{e}. \)

Divide by \(D\) and use the convergence proved in Section 4:

\( \displaystyle \boxed{W_\infty\ge\frac{(\nu^*)^2}{e}}. \)
