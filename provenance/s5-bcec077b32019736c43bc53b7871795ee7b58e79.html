The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two centers with population mass \(1/2\) each, a finite set \(G\) of good categories, rational per-capita supplies \(b_g \geq 0\) with \(\sum_{g \in G} b_g = 1\), and finitely many agent types \(t\), each with center \(i(t)\), rational mass \(\mu_t\), and a nonnegative additive value \(u_t(g)\) for each category, does there exist a mass allocation \(x_{t,g} \geq 0\) such that \(\sum_g x_{t,g} = \mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,g} = b_g\), and \(W_i(\eta_i) \geq W_i(\eta_j)\) for both centers \(i \neq j\)? Here \(\eta_{j,g} = \sum_{t:i(t)=j} x_{t,g}\), and \(W_i(\eta)\) is the maximum total value obtainable by assigning category mass \(\eta_g\) to center \(i\)’s type masses, with each unit of mass receiving one whole good. Agent inter-EF1 is automatic for singleton bundles; center envy-freeness is the mass-scale counterpart of center EFX.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) good categories, with supplies \(b_g\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) satisfying \(\sum_g b_g=1\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), center affiliations \(i(t)\in\{1,2\}\), and additive values \(u_t(g)\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\). Require \(\sum_{t:i(t)=i}\mu_t=1/2\) for each center. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,g}\geq0\) with \(\sum_gx_{t,g}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{t,g}=b_g\). Each individual receives one whole good; types may split into subpopulations receiving different categories.
Write \(\eta_{i,g}=\sum_{t:i(t)=i}x_{t,g}\). For any \(\eta\geq0\) of total mass \(1/2\), define \(W_i(\eta)=\max\{\sum_{t:i(t)=i}\sum_g u_t(g)y_{t,g}:y\geq0,\ \sum_gy_{t,g}=\mu_t,\ \sum_{t:i(t)=i}y_{t,g}=\eta_g\}\).
Both own and other centers' category masses are evaluated by this potential welfare, preserving singleton bundles under reassignment. Center envy-freeness requires \(W_i(\eta_i)\geq W_i(\eta_j)\) for both \(i\neq j\); agent inter-EF1 is automatic.

Is deciding whether such an allocation exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For indivisible goods, deciding existence of center EFX together with agent inter-EF1 is NP-hard even with identical additive values; the hardness construction uses two equally sized centers and forces singleton bundles. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27743v1).
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The value encoding was unspecified; all per-category values are nonnegative binary rationals, included in the total input bit length L.
  • Made the center population constraints explicit: each center's positive type masses sum to 1/2.
  • Specified W_i through reassignment masses with exact type and category marginals, using potential welfare for both own and other centers.
  • Interpreted splitting a type's mass as partitioning its population into singleton-good recipients, rather than dividing an individual's good.
  • Retained the graded question's heterogeneous nonnegative values; identical valuations are a restriction of the discrete hardness result, not a restriction on the continuous input.

Conclusion

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable: every valid instance admits an allocation. Equal category masses at the two centers make both envy inequalities equalities. This uses exactly the permitted splitting of types into subpopulations.

Construction and proof

Define \(x_{t,g}=\mu_t b_g\) for every type \(t\) and category \(g\). These masses are nonnegative.

For each type, the normalization of supplies gives
\[ \sum_g x_{t,g}=\mu_t\sum_g b_g=\mu_t. \]
For each category, the two center-mass constraints give \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), hence
\[ \sum_t x_{t,g}=b_g\sum_t\mu_t=b_g. \]
Thus \(x\) is a feasible allocation. It assigns a subpopulation of mass \(\mu_t b_g\) one whole good of category \(g\), as allowed in the question.

For either center \(i\), its category masses satisfy
\[ \eta_{i,g}=\sum_{t:i(t)=i}\mu_t b_g =b_g\sum_{t:i(t)=i}\mu_t=\frac{b_g}{2}. \]
Consequently, \(\eta_1=\eta_2=b/2\). Therefore, for each center \(i\), evaluating its own and the other center’s masses applies the same function to the same argument:
\[ W_i(\eta_i)=W_i(b/2)=W_i(\eta_j). \]
Both center envy-freeness inequalities hold.

These welfare maxima are well-defined: the restriction of \(x\) to center \(i\) is feasible for \(W_i(b/2)\); its feasible set is closed and bounded because \(0\leq y_{t,g}\leq\mu_t\). A linear objective therefore attains a finite maximum.

Computational complexity and interpretation

On valid inputs, the decision algorithm simply returns “yes.” An explicit allocation requires \(\tau m\) rational multiplications. Each product \(\mu_t b_g\) has encoding length bounded by the combined encoding lengths of its factors, so construction takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The result also survives an additional requirement that each center’s actual assignment attain its own potential welfare: independently solve the two transportation linear programs defining \(W_i(b/2)\). Their optimal assignments preserve the category masses \(b/2\), so combining them preserves feasibility and envy-freeness.

The [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27743v1) concerns indivisible goods. Here, allowing subpopulations to split across categories permits identical center inventories; the indivisible hardness does not transfer to this formulation.
