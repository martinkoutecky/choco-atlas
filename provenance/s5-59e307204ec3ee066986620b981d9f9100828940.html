Theorem 4 (Convergence with stubborn agents) — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), candidate stubborn types \(C\), rational interaction rates \(K_{uv}\), truth \(\theta\), fixed stubborn mean \(\eta\), and rational update parameters \(q\in(0,1)\) and \(\delta\), hold each candidate type \(s\in C\) at mean \(\eta\) and let responsive type \(v\) update by \(m_v[k+1]=q\bigl(m_v[k]+\delta\sum_{u\in T}\mu_uK_{uv}(m_u[k]-m_v[k])\bigr)+(1-q)\theta\). Assuming the responsive update matrix for each \(s\) has spectral radius below \(1\), compute its limiting mean vector \(\gamma^{(s)}\) and score \(D_s=\sum_{v\ne s}\mu_v|\gamma_v^{(s)}-\theta|\), then return a maximum-score candidate.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq2\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Inputs also include a nonempty candidate set \(C\subseteq T\), signed rational interaction coefficients \(K_{uv}\) directed from \(u\) to \(v\), with \(K_{vv}=0\), rational scalars \(\theta,\eta\), and rational parameters \(0<q<1\) and \(\delta>0\).
For each candidate \(s\in C\), consider a separate experiment in which the entire mass of type \(s\) is stubborn: \(m_s[k]=\eta\). Every other type is responsive and follows \(m_v[k+1]=q\bigl(m_v[k]+\delta\sum_{u\in T}\mu_uK_{uv}(m_u[k]-m_v[k])\bigr)+(1-q)\theta\), starting from arbitrary finite real means.
Let \(A^{(s)}\) be the coefficient matrix of the responsive means in this affine recurrence. The input promises \(\rho(A^{(s)})<1\) for every \(s\in C\). Define \(\gamma_v^{(s)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}m_v[k]\) and \(D_s=\sum_{v\in T\setminus\{s\}}\mu_v|\gamma_v^{(s)}-\theta|\).
The computational problem is to compute all responsive limiting vectors and scores exactly and return any candidate attaining \(\max_{s\in C}D_s\). Let \(L\) denote the total binary input length; there is one scalar mean per type and no separate candidate or alternative dimension \(m\).

Is this promised computational problem solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(|C|\), and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents, spectral stability guarantees convergence of expected responsive means to a uniquely specified equilibrium with one stubborn agent; this is a convergence result, without a complexity classification. [Discrete convergence result](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01123v1#S4)
For continuum societies, convergence of opinion distributions is known under symmetric interactions in other opinion models. [Continuum convergence result](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/130943923) No result settling the exact computational problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, interpreting the society as a distribution over types.
  • Interpreted candidates as separate experiments with exactly one whole type made stubborn; all other candidate types remain responsive in that experiment.
  • Allowed signed rational interaction coefficients, since the discrete model explicitly permits positive and negative influence weights; set self-interaction coefficients to zero.
  • Required positive delta, matching the discrete mean mixing rate, and restricted truth and stubborn mean to finite rational scalars for binary input.
  • Required a nonempty candidate set and at least two support types so that candidate selection and responsive means are defined.
  • Used arbitrary finite initial means and interpreted the recurrence as dynamics of expected means, rather than stochastic sample-path convergence.
  • Specified exact rational outputs and allowed any maximizing candidate in a tie.
  • Defined L as total binary input length; no independent dimension m occurs in this scalar-mean problem.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The promised problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(|C|\), and \(L\). All limiting means and scores are rational numbers with polynomial encoding length.

1. Deriving the equilibrium system. Fix a candidate \(s\in C\), and write \(R_s=T\setminus\{s\}\) and \(n=\tau-1\). Define the centered means \(x_v[k]=m_v[k]-\theta\) and the stubborn displacement \(a=\eta-\theta\). Thus \(x_s[k]=a\).

For every responsive type \(v\in R_s\), the given recurrence becomes

\(\displaystyle x_v[k+1]=q\left(1-\delta\sum_{u\in T}\mu_uK_{uv}\right)x_v[k]+q\delta\sum_{u\in R_s\setminus\{v\}}\mu_uK_{uv}x_u[k]+q\delta\mu_sK_{sv}a.\)

Here the sum in the diagonal coefficient includes the stubborn type, because its interaction also contributes the term involving \(-x_v[k]\). We have used \(K_{vv}=0\).

Consequently, with coordinates indexed by \(R_s\),

\(\displaystyle x[k+1]=A^{(s)}x[k]+h^{(s)},\qquad h^{(s)}_v=q\delta\mu_sK_{sv}(\eta-\theta).\)

Set \(B^{(s)}=I-A^{(s)}\). Its entries are

\(\displaystyle B^{(s)}_{vu}=\begin{cases}1-q+q\delta\sum_{w\in T}\mu_wK_{wv},&u=v,\\-q\delta\mu_uK_{uv},&u\ne v.\end{cases}\)

All these entries and all coordinates of \(h^{(s)}\) are rational and can be constructed using polynomially many rational arithmetic operations.

2. Stability identifies the unique limit. The promise \(\rho(A^{(s)})<1\) implies that \(1\) is not an eigenvalue of \(A^{(s)}\). Hence \(B^{(s)}\) is nonsingular, and the unique centered equilibrium is

\(\displaystyle x^{(s),*}=(B^{(s)})^{-1}h^{(s)}.\)

Subtracting its fixed-point equation from the recurrence gives, by induction,

\(\displaystyle x[k]-x^{(s),*}=(A^{(s)})^k\bigl(x[0]-x^{(s),*}\bigr).\)

For completeness, the spectral-radius promise implies \((A^{(s)})^k\to0\): in complex Jordan form, every block has eigenvalue \(\lambda\) satisfying \(|\lambda|<1\). When \(\lambda\ne0\), entries of its powers are finite sums of terms \(\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{k-j}\), which tend to zero because exponential decay dominates each fixed-degree polynomial. A block with \(\lambda=0\) is nilpotent. Conjugating back preserves convergence to zero.

Therefore every finite initial vector has the same limit,

\(\displaystyle \gamma_v^{(s)}=\theta+x_v^{(s),*},\qquad v\in R_s,\)

and

\(\displaystyle D_s=\sum_{v\in R_s}\mu_v|x_v^{(s),*}|.\)

This argument permits signed, directed interactions and requires no symmetry.

3. Exact solutions have polynomial encoding length. We next account for binary arithmetic, rather than counting arithmetic operations alone.

Represent every input rational with a positive integer denominator, and let \(P\) be the product of all these denominators. Their total encoding length is at most \(L\), so \(\log_2P=O(L)\).

After expanding the displayed expressions, every entry of \(B^{(s)}\) is a sum of products of at most four input rationals. Every entry of \(h^{(s)}\) is a sum of products of at most five input rationals. Since every input denominator divides \(P\), the integer \(Q=P^5\) clears all these denominators. Define

\(\displaystyle M^{(s)}=QB^{(s)},\qquad y^{(s)}=Qh^{(s)}.\)

These are an integer matrix and an integer vector, and the equilibrium equation is equivalently

\(\displaystyle M^{(s)}x^{(s),*}=y^{(s)}.\)

Every input rational has absolute value at most \(2^L\), and \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Each entry above contains at most \(O(\tau)\) terms, each involving at most five input factors. Thus, if \(H\) is the maximum of \(1\) and the absolute values of all entries of \(M^{(s)}\) and \(y^{(s)}\), then

\(\displaystyle \log_2H=O(L+\log\tau).\)

Let \(d_s=\det M^{(s)}\), and let \(d_{s,v}\) be the determinant obtained by replacing column \(v\) of \(M^{(s)}\) with \(y^{(s)}\). Nonsingularity gives \(d_s\ne0\). Cramer’s rule yields

\(\displaystyle x_v^{(s),*}=\frac{d_{s,v}}{d_s}.\)

The determinant expansion has \(n!\) terms, each with absolute value at most \(H^n\). Hence

\(\displaystyle |d_s|,\ |d_{s,v}|\le n!H^n.\)

It follows that all these determinants have

\(\displaystyle O\bigl(n(L+\log\tau)\bigr)\)

bits. The same polynomial bound holds for the rational coordinates \(x_v^{(s),*}\) and, after adding the input rational \(\theta\), for \(\gamma_v^{(s)}\).

4. The determinants can be computed in polynomial bit time. The determinant bounds alone do not establish an efficient algorithm, so we justify that step explicitly.

For an integer matrix with entries bounded by \(H\), Gaussian elimination with row pivoting uses \(O(n^3)\) rational arithmetic operations. Reduce each rational after every operation.

After \(k\) successful pivots, let \(F\) be the corresponding row-permuted original matrix, partitioned as

\(\displaystyle F=\begin{pmatrix}E&G\\J&D\end{pmatrix},\)

where the leading block \(E\) is nonsingular and has size \(k\). The remaining elimination matrix is the Schur complement \(D-JE^{-1}G\). Each of its entries satisfies

\(\displaystyle (D-JE^{-1}G)_{ij}=\frac{\det\begin{pmatrix}E&G_{:j}\\J_{i:}&D_{ij}\end{pmatrix}}{\det E}.\)

Indeed, subtracting \(J_{i:}E^{-1}\) times the first \(k\) rows from the last row of the bordered matrix leaves a block triangular matrix with determinant \(\det E\,(D_{ij}-J_{i:}E^{-1}G_{:j})\).

Both determinants in this ratio are minors of the row-permuted original matrix. By the same determinant-expansion bound, their bit lengths are \(O(n(\log H+\log n))\). Thus every reduced entry encountered during elimination has polynomial bit length. Each update involves only a constant number of arithmetic operations on such rationals; reduction by the Euclidean algorithm also takes polynomial time.

If no nonzero pivot exists in the current column, the remaining Schur complement has a zero column, so the determinant is zero. Otherwise, elimination finishes and the determinant is the signed product of its pivots. This also takes polynomial bit time.

This procedure applies both to \(M^{(s)}\) and to every column-replacement matrix, including singular replacement matrices. Computing the \(n+1\) determinants for one candidate therefore takes \(O(n^4)\) rational arithmetic operations on numbers of polynomial bit length.

5. Computing scores and selecting a maximizer. Since the masses are positive,

\(\displaystyle D_s=\frac{1}{|d_s|}\sum_{v\in R_s}\mu_v|d_{s,v}|.\)

Each \(P\mu_v\) is a positive integer. Define

\(\displaystyle N_s=\sum_{v\in R_s}(P\mu_v)|d_{s,v}|.\)

Then an exact rational representation of the score is

\(\displaystyle D_s=\frac{N_s}{P|d_s|}.\)

The integers \(P\mu_v\) have \(O(L)\) bits, and the determinant bounds show that \(N_s\) and the score denominator have polynomial bit length. Thus the score is computable exactly in polynomial time.

For two candidates \(s,t\), their scores can be compared without approximation:

\(\displaystyle D_s\ge D_t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N_s|d_t|\ge N_t|d_s|.\)

The denominators are positive, so this cross-multiplication preserves the inequality. Scanning the nonempty list \(C\), retaining a candidate whenever its score exceeds the current maximum, returns a maximizer; ties may be resolved arbitrarily.

Repeating the construction for all \(|C|\) candidates computes every requested limiting vector and score and returns a maximizing candidate in polynomial bit time. The stability promise is used to prove convergence and nonsingularity; the algorithm does not need to verify it or simulate convergence. In particular, NP-hardness under polynomial-time reductions whose outputs satisfy the promise would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
