The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite product states \(\Theta\), signals \(S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_M\}\), rational prior \(p_\theta\), likelihoods \(f(s_j\mid\theta)\), reporting-cost bound \(C\), lying-benefit bounds \(\Delta(j,h)\), and a fixed number \(N\) of reference reports, set \(\mu_j=\sum_{\theta}p_\theta f(s_j\mid\theta)\). For each histogram \(\mathbf a=(a_1,\ldots,a_M)\) with \(\sum_\ell a_\ell=N\), let \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)\) be the paper’s conditional probability of that reference-report histogram given signal \(s_j\). Choose nonnegative payments \(\tau(r,\mathbf a)\) minimizing \(\sum_j\mu_j\sum_{\mathbf a}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)\tau(j,\mathbf a)\), subject to truthful-reporting payoff differences of at least \(\Delta(j,h)+\eta\) for every \(j\ne h\), and truthful expected payments of at least \(C+\eta\) for every \(j\), for a specified \(\eta>0\). Return the optimal payment table and per-capita budget, and compare that optimum across values of \(N\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) signal types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_j\), normalized to sum to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Write \(S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_m\}\), with \(m=\tau\ge2\).
Input also includes \(k\) product states \(\Theta\), a positive rational prior \(p\), rational likelihoods \(f(s_j\mid\theta)\ge0\) summing to \(1\) for each state, and \(\mu_j=\sum_\theta p_\theta f(s_j\mid\theta)>0\). All buyers share this model and are risk-neutral.
Reference reporters report honestly; their signals and the focal buyer's signal are independent conditional on the same product state. For a supplied unary integer \(N\ge1\), let \(A_N=\{\mathbf a\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m:\sum_\ell a_\ell=N\}\) and
\(q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{p_\theta f(s_j\mid\theta)}{\mu_j}\frac{N!}{\prod_\ell a_\ell!}\prod_\ell f(s_\ell\mid\theta)^{a_\ell}\), with \(0^0=1\).
Given binary-rational \(C\ge0\), \(\Delta(j,h)\ge0\) for \(j\ne h\), and \(\eta>0\), choose nonnegative payments \(T(r,\mathbf a)\) satisfying, for every \(j\ne h\), \(\sum_{\mathbf a\in A_N}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)[T(j,\mathbf a)-T(h,\mathbf a)]\ge\Delta(j,h)+\eta\), and, for every \(j\), \(\sum_{\mathbf a\in A_N}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)T(j,\mathbf a)\ge C+\eta\). Abstention yields zero payoff.
Let \(W_N\) be the minimum per-capita budget \(\sum_j\mu_j\sum_{\mathbf a\in A_N}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)T(j,\mathbf a)\), or \(+\infty\) if infeasible. The optimization problem returns this value and an attaining payment table; zero entries may be omitted. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Is deciding \(W_N\le B\), for a supplied binary-rational \(B\ge0\), in \(\mathsf P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,N,L\), or is it NP-hard? How does \(W_N\) compare across successive values of \(N\)?

For finite agents, minimum-budget payments have an explicit histogram LP with \(m\binom{N+m-1}{m-1}\) variables; the established budget comparison is nonincrease with additional reference reporters. [Jurca–Faltings (2006)](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/www/cs15-892F13/p190-jurca.pdf)
Later work explicitly uses the posterior mixture of binomial reference-count distributions for binary signals. These verified results do not provide a uniform complexity classification for the general succinct input above. [Jurca–Faltings (2009)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3451)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted a fixed number of reference reports as fixed within each instance, while allowing N to vary between instances; encoded N in unary to make the requested uniform polynomial bound precise.
  • Defined histogram probabilities using independence conditional on the shared product state, consistent with the original model and the later binary formulation.
  • Represented society types by positive-mass signal types, normalized their masses to one, and excluded zero-probability signals so every posterior is defined.
  • Specified rational probability normalizations, nonnegative reporting costs and lying benefits, risk neutrality, truthful reference reporting, and zero abstention payoff.
  • Allowed infeasible instances and assigned them infinite optimal budget rather than assuming informative signals always permit truthful payments.
  • Allowed omission of zero payment entries so explicit enumeration of the entire histogram table is not itself a computational output requirement.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions. Hardness holds even when all model probabilities and signal masses are dyadic rationals, \(\eta=C=1\), \(B=2\), and every constructed instance is feasible with \(W_N\le4\).

Consequently, a uniform algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,N,L\) would imply \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\). Allowing zero payment entries to be omitted does not remove the hardness: whenever the optimum is finite, an optimal table with at most \(m^2\) nonzero entries and polynomial encoding length exists.

For successive reference counts,

\(W_{N+1}\le W_N.\)

Equality and strict inequality both occur.

1. Attainment, sparse optimal tables, and membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

Write

\(E_j[T_r]=\sum_{\mathbf a\in A_N}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)T(r,\mathbf a).\)

The problem is a finite rational LP with \(m^2\) incentive and participation inequalities. Its histogram formulation agrees with the formulation in [Jurca–Faltings (2006)](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/www/cs15-892F13/p190-jurca.pdf); its potentially exponential number of columns requires a separate complexity argument.

For any specified histogram, its probabilities \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)\) can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Their bit lengths are polynomial in the input length and unary \(N\): the formula uses factorials, powers with exponents at most \(N\), and sums over the explicitly supplied states.

First, every feasible instance attains its minimum. If a variable \(T(r,\mathbf a)\) has zero objective coefficient, then \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid r)=0\), because \(\mu_r>0\). Setting this variable to zero preserves participation and weakly improves every incentive inequality: it contributes nothing to report \(r\)'s truthful expectation and can only increase another type's expected payment from falsely reporting \(r\). Thus all zero-cost variables may be discarded.

Every remaining variable has a strictly positive objective coefficient. Given one feasible table of budget \(K\), the feasible set restricted to budget at most \(K\) is closed and bounded in its finite-dimensional space. It is therefore compact, so the objective attains a minimum.

We next prove the sparse rational optimum claim. Introduce nonnegative surplus variables to express the \(d=m^2\) inequalities as equalities. Among optimal solutions, choose one with the smallest number of positive coordinates, counting payments and surplus variables. The corresponding columns of the equality matrix must be linearly independent. Otherwise a nonzero dependence permits sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving feasibility. Optimality forces the objective change along that dependence to be zero. Continuing in one direction until a positive coordinate becomes zero then contradicts minimality of the support.

There are consequently at most \(d\) positive coordinates. An invertible minor determines them from the rational right-hand side. Cramer's rule gives polynomial bit length, because the minor has order at most \(m^2\) and all its coefficients have polynomial bit length.

If \(W_N\le B\), this optimal table is therefore a polynomial-size certificate: list its nonzero payments and their histogram indices. A verifier computes the required probabilities only at those histograms and checks all constraints and the budget exactly. Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\).

2. Source problem for the hardness reduction

We reduce from Set Cover. Its input consists of a nonempty universe \(U\), sets \(F_1,\ldots,F_n\subseteq U\), and an integer \(t\) with \(1\le t\le n\). The question is whether at most \(t\) sets cover \(U\).

This restricted problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard. To recall a direct reduction from \(\mathsf{3SAT}\), create one universe element for each variable and each clause. For each variable, create two sets: both contain its variable element, and each contains the clause elements satisfied by its corresponding literal. With \(t\) equal to the number of variables, any cover must select exactly one set for each variable. Such a cover exists precisely when the formula is satisfiable. Thus \(t\) is polynomially bounded and may be supplied in unary.

We will construct an instance satisfying

\(W_{t+1}\le2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U\text{ has a cover using at most }t\text{ sets}.\)

3. Construction of the Bayesian model

Use the signal alphabet

\(S=\{r,c,x_1,\ldots,x_n\},\qquad m=\tau=n+2,\qquad N=t+1.\)

Let \(u=|U|\) and \(R=u+2\). Choose \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) to be the largest reciprocal powers of two satisfying, respectively,

\(n\alpha\le\frac14,\qquad (n+1)\beta\le\frac12,\qquad R\gamma\le\frac{\beta}{16}.\)

Set \(\varepsilon=R\gamma\). These choices have polynomial bit length.

There are four kinds of product states.

| State | Prior | Likelihoods |
|---|---:|---|
| Desired state \(\theta_0\) | \(\gamma\) | \(f(r\mid\theta_0)=1-n\alpha\), \(f(c\mid\theta_0)=0\), \(f(x_i\mid\theta_0)=\alpha\) |
| Element state \(\theta_e\), for each \(e\in U\) | \(\gamma\) | \(f(c\mid\theta_e)=\frac14\), \(f(x_i\mid\theta_e)=\alpha\) if \(e\notin F_i\), and \(0\) otherwise; \(f(r\mid\theta_e)=\frac34-\alpha|\{i:e\notin F_i\}|\) |
| Blocker state \(\theta_b\) | \(\gamma\) | \(f(r\mid\theta_b)=0\), \(f(x_i\mid\theta_b)=\alpha\), \(f(c\mid\theta_b)=1-n\alpha\) |
| Pure state \(\phi_h\), for every \(h\ne r\) | Defined below | Signal \(h\) occurs with probability \(1\) |

The pure state \(\phi_{x_i}\) has prior \(\beta\). The pure state \(\phi_c\) has prior

\(p_{\phi_c}=1-n\beta-\varepsilon.\)

All likelihood vectors sum to one. The element-state likelihood of \(r\) is at least \(\frac12\), and the desired-state likelihood of \(r\) is at least \(\frac34\). All priors are positive and sum to one. Indeed,

\(p_{\phi_c}\ge\frac12+\beta-\varepsilon\ge\beta.\)

Define the input masses by the required marginalization,

\(\mu_h=\sum_\theta p_\theta f(h\mid\theta).\)

Every signal has positive mass: each \(h\ne r\) has its pure state, and \(r\) has positive likelihood in \(\theta_0\). All supplied probabilities and masses are dyadic rationals.

We will use the bounds

\(\mu_r\le\varepsilon,\qquad \mu_h\ge p_{\phi_h}\ge\beta\quad(h\ne r),\qquad \mu_h\le p_{\phi_h}+\varepsilon.\)

Finally, set

\(C=1,\qquad\eta=1,\qquad B=2,\)

and

\(\Delta(c,r)=1,\qquad\Delta(j,h)=0\text{ for every other ordered pair }j\ne h.\)

The number of states is \(u+n+3\), and \(N=t+1\le n+1\). Thus the construction, including unary \(N\), has polynomial size.

4. The key histogram characterization

We claim that

\(\exists\mathbf a\in A_N:\ q_N(\mathbf a\mid r)>0,\ q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)=0\)

holds exactly when the Set Cover instance has a cover of size at most \(t\).

Because mixture terms are nonnegative and every prior is positive, \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)>0\) precisely when some state has both positive focal likelihood for \(j\) and positive likelihood for the reference histogram.

Suppose \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid r)>0\) and \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)=0\). The only states with positive focal likelihood for \(r\) are \(\theta_0\) and the element states. An element state contributing to the former probability would also contribute to the latter, since its likelihood of \(c\) is positive. Therefore \(\theta_0\) must give the histogram positive probability. In particular, \(a_c=0\).

Moreover, \(a_r\ge1\). Otherwise the histogram uses only the \(x_i\), all of which have positive likelihood in \(\theta_b\). Since the blocker also has positive focal likelihood for \(c\), this would contradict \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)=0\).

For an element state \(\theta_e\), the histogram now has positive likelihood exactly when every selected \(x_i\) satisfies \(e\notin F_i\). Its contribution to \(q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)\) must vanish. Hence, for every \(e\in U\), at least one \(i\) with \(a_{x_i}>0\) satisfies \(e\in F_i\). These selected sets cover \(U\), and their number is at most

\(\sum_i a_{x_i}=N-a_r\le N-1=t.\)

Conversely, suppose a set of indices \(I\) covers \(U\), with \(1\le|I|\le t\). Choose the histogram

\(a_{x_i}=1\text{ for }i\in I,\qquad a_{x_i}=0\text{ otherwise},\qquad a_c=0,\qquad a_r=N-|I|.\)

The desired state gives it positive probability. Every element state gives it zero probability because the cover includes a signal absent from that state. The blocker and every pure state give it zero probability because \(a_r\ge1\). Thus only \(\theta_0\) can generate it, and

\(q_N(\mathbf a\mid r)>0,\qquad q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)=0.\)

This proves the characterization.

5. Equivalence with budget at most \(2\)

Necessity

Participation requires \(E_j[T_j]\ge2\) for every \(j\). Consequently, every feasible table has budget at least

\(\sum_j\mu_j\,2=2.\)

If its budget is at most \(2\), positivity of every \(\mu_j\) forces

\(E_j[T_j]=2\quad\text{for every }j.\)

The special incentive constraint for \((c,r)\) is

\(E_c[T_c]-E_c[T_r]\ge2.\)

Since \(E_c[T_c]=2\) and payments are nonnegative, \(E_c[T_r]=0\). On the other hand, \(E_r[T_r]=2\), so some histogram with positive payment satisfies

\(q_N(\mathbf a\mid r)>0,\qquad q_N(\mathbf a\mid c)=0.\)

Section 4 then gives a cover of size at most \(t\). By attainment, this argument also applies whenever \(W_N\le2\).

Sufficiency

Suppose a cover exists, and let \(\mathbf a^\star\) be the histogram constructed in Section 4. Pay report \(r\) only there:

\(T(r,\mathbf a^\star)=\frac{2}{q_N(\mathbf a^\star\mid r)}.\)

Then \(E_r[T_r]=2\) and \(E_c[T_r]=0\).

Only \(\theta_0\) generates \(\mathbf a^\star\). Therefore, for every \(x_i\),

\(\frac{q_N(\mathbf a^\star\mid x_i)}{q_N(\mathbf a^\star\mid r)} =\frac{\alpha\mu_r}{(1-n\alpha)\mu_{x_i}} \le\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{(3/4)\beta} \le\frac1{48}.\)

In particular, \(E_{x_i}[T_r]\le1\).

For every report \(h\ne r\), let \(\mathbf e_h\) denote its coordinate unit vector and pay only at the unanimous histogram:

\(T(h,N\mathbf e_h)=\frac{2}{q_N(N\mathbf e_h\mid h)}.\)

The denominator is positive, and

\(q_N(N\mathbf e_h\mid h)\ge\frac{p_{\phi_h}}{\mu_h} \ge\frac{p_{\phi_h}}{p_{\phi_h}+\varepsilon}\ge\frac12.\)

For \(j\ne h\), we have \(q_N(N\mathbf e_h\mid j)\le\frac14\). There are two cases:

  • If \(j\ne r\), pure states contribute nothing to this cross-probability. The posterior mass on the other states is at most \(\varepsilon/\mu_j\le1/16\).
  • If \(j=r\), only the desired and element states have positive posterior weight. In each, the likelihood of \(h\ne r\) is at most \(\frac14\). Since \(N\ge1\), its unanimous-histogram probability is at most \(\frac14\).

Thus, for every \(j\ne h\),

\(E_j[T_h] =2\frac{q_N(N\mathbf e_h\mid j)}{q_N(N\mathbf e_h\mid h)} \le1.\)

All truthful expectations equal \(2\). Every ordinary incentive constraint therefore has truthful advantage at least \(1\); the special constraint for \((c,r)\) has advantage exactly \(2\). Participation holds, and the budget is \(2\).

We have proved

\(W_{t+1}\le2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the Set Cover instance is a yes-instance}.\)

Together with Section 1, this establishes \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness.

Every constructed instance has finite optimum

The hardness does not depend on infeasibility in no-instances.

Pay each report only at its own unanimous histogram, scaling the payment to make its truthful expectation \(4\). For reports \(h\ne r\), the preceding estimates give every cross-expectation at most \(2\).

For the unanimous \(r\)-histogram and any \(h\ne r\),

\(\frac{q_N(N\mathbf e_r\mid h)}{q_N(N\mathbf e_r\mid r)} \le\frac{\mu_r}{\mu_h} \max_{\theta:\,f(r\mid\theta)>0} \frac{f(h\mid\theta)}{f(r\mid\theta)} \le\frac{\varepsilon}{2\beta}\le\frac1{32}.\)

Here only the desired and element states enter the maximum, and their likelihood ratio is at most \(\frac12\). Hence a false report of \(r\) has expected payment at most \(\frac18\).

All required incentive margins, including the special margin \(2\), hold. Participation holds as well, and the budget is \(4\). Thus every constructed instance satisfies

\(2\le W_N\le4.\)

6. Nonincrease with additional reference reporters

Fix any feasible table \(T_N\). For a histogram \(\mathbf b\in A_{N+1}\), define

\(T_{N+1}(r,\mathbf b) =\sum_{\ell:\,b_\ell>0}\frac{b_\ell}{N+1}T_N(r,\mathbf b-\mathbf e_\ell).\)

This averages the old payment after deleting one uniformly selected reference report. The resulting payment is nonnegative.

For completeness, write the state-conditional multinomial probability as

\(M_N^\theta(\mathbf a)=\frac{N!}{\prod_\ell a_\ell!}\prod_\ell f(s_\ell\mid\theta)^{a_\ell}.\)

Direct calculation gives, for every \(\mathbf a\in A_N\),

\(M_{N+1}^\theta(\mathbf a+\mathbf e_\ell)\frac{a_\ell+1}{N+1} =f(s_\ell\mid\theta)M_N^\theta(\mathbf a).\)

The identity also holds when likelihoods vanish, with the stipulated convention \(0^0=1\). Summing over \(\ell\) gives

\(\sum_\ell M_{N+1}^\theta(\mathbf a+\mathbf e_\ell)\frac{a_\ell+1}{N+1} =M_N^\theta(\mathbf a).\)

Consequently, conditional on each state, the new table has exactly the old expected payment for every report. Mixing with the posterior conditional on focal signal \(j\) yields

\(\sum_{\mathbf b\in A_{N+1}}q_{N+1}(\mathbf b\mid j)T_{N+1}(r,\mathbf b) =\sum_{\mathbf a\in A_N}q_N(\mathbf a\mid j)T_N(r,\mathbf a).\)

Every incentive expectation, participation expectation, and budget is preserved. Therefore

\(W_{N+1}\le W_N,\)

including the extended-real cases. This supplies a proof of the established nonincrease that directly respects the posterior-mixture model.

7. Equality and strict decrease

Equality can occur

Suppose each signal has its own pure state and there are no other states. Conditional on focal signal \(j\), every reference reports \(j\), regardless of \(N\).

Define

\(d_j=\max\{C+\eta,\ \max_{h\ne j}(\Delta(j,h)+\eta)\}.\)

Nonnegativity and the constraints imply truthful expected payment at least \(d_j\). Paying \(d_j\) only for report \(j\) at histogram \(N\mathbf e_j\) attains these bounds. Hence, for every \(N\ge1\),

\(W_N=\sum_j\mu_jd_j.\)

Strict decrease can occur

Take two signals, two equally likely states, and likelihood vectors

\((3/4,1/4)\quad\text{and}\quad(1/4,3/4).\)

Set \(C=0\), \(\eta=1\), and both lying gains to zero. The signal masses are \((1/2,1/2)\).

Swapping the two signal labels preserves the LP and its objective. Averaging any feasible table with its swapped table therefore produces a symmetric feasible table of the same budget.

For \(N=1\), the reference distributions conditional on focal signals \(1\) and \(2\) are

\((5/8,3/8)\quad\text{and}\quad(3/8,5/8).\)

In a symmetric table, let \(x\) be the payment for a matching reference signal and \(y\) the payment for an opposing signal. Incentives require

\((x-y)/4\ge1.\)

The budget is \(5x/8+3y/8\), so nonnegativity gives a minimum of \(5/2\), attained by \(x=4,y=0\). Participation is satisfied.

For \(N=2\), ordered by the number of signal-\(1\) references, the conditional distributions are

\((3/16,3/8,7/16)\quad\text{and}\quad(7/16,3/8,3/16).\)

Let \(x\) be the payment for matching unanimous references, \(y\) for opposing unanimous references, and \(z\) for mixed references. Incentives again require \((x-y)/4\ge1\). The budget is

\(7x/16+3y/16+3z/8.\)

Its minimum is \(7/4\), attained by \(x=4,y=z=0\), which also satisfies participation. Thus

\(W_2=\frac74<\frac52=W_1.\)
