The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidate sets \(C_1,C_2\), committee sizes \(k_1,k_2\), community shares \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\), and binary-rational distributions \(\mu_i\) over approval sets \(S\subseteq C_i\), choose disjoint committees \(w_i\subseteq C_i\) of sizes \(k_i\) maximizing either \(\min\{U_1(w_1),U_2(w_2)\}\) or \(U_1(w_1)U_2(w_2)\), where \(U_i(w_i)=\alpha_i\sum_S\mu_i(S)q_{f_i}(S,w_i)\) and \(f_i\in\{\mathrm{SAV},\mathrm{MSAV}\}\). What is the computational complexity of finding such an allocation as a function of the candidate counts, support sizes, and input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C_1,C_2\) be finite, possibly overlapping candidate sets, and write \(m=|C_1\cup C_2|\). Let \(k_1,k_2\) be nonnegative integers satisfying \(k_i\le |C_i|\) and \(k_1+k_2\le m\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau_i\) approval types \(S\subseteq C_i\) with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_i(S)\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_S\mu_i(S)=1\). The support size \(\tau_i\) counts listed types, not all possible approval sets; put \(\tau=\tau_1+\tau_2\).
Community shares \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) are nonnegative binary rationals with \(\alpha_1+\alpha_2=1\). Each community uses a specified rule \(f_i\in\{\mathrm{SAV},\mathrm{MSAV}\}\).
Define \(q_{\mathrm{SAV}}(S,w)=|S\cap w|/|S|\) and \(q_{\mathrm{MSAV}}(S,w)=|S\cap w|/\min\{|S|,|w|\}\), taking either expression to be zero whenever its denominator is zero. Set \(U_i(w)=\alpha_i\sum_S\mu_i(S)q_{f_i}(S,w)\).
An allocation consists of ordinary, indivisible committees \(w_i\subseteq C_i\) with \(|w_i|=k_i\) and \(w_1\cap w_2=\varnothing\); candidates may remain unallocated. Any maximizing allocation is acceptable when there are ties.
For each fixed rule pair \((f_1,f_2)\), is finding an allocation maximizing \(\min\{U_1(w_1),U_2(w_2)\}\) polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard, and is finding one maximizing \(U_1(w_1)U_2(w_2)\) polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k_1,k_2\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For explicit finite electorates, both optimization problems are NP-hard for every rule pair in \(\{\mathrm{SAV},\mathrm{MSAV}\}^2\). For weakly additive utilities, the discrete problems are fixed-parameter tractable in \(|C_1\cap C_2|\) and also in \(k_1+k_2\).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each approval distribution to total mass one and interpreted community shares as nonnegative binary rationals summing to one; zero shares are permitted, consistent with allowing empty discrete communities.
  • Specified the SAV and MSAV per-type utilities, including the zero-denominator convention for empty approval sets and empty committees.
  • Carried over the discrete feasibility conditions on committee sizes, including permission for zero sizes and for candidates to remain unallocated.
  • Kept committees indivisible and allowed any optimizer in a tie.
  • Treated each fixed rule pair and each objective as a separate complexity question; maximizing the product is equivalent to maximizing its square root.

Classification

Both optimization problems are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every fixed rule pair. Their rational-threshold decision versions are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

| Rule pair | Maximize \(\min\{U_1,U_2\}\) | Maximize \(U_1U_2\) |
|---|---|---|
| \((\mathrm{SAV},\mathrm{SAV})\) | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard |
| \((\mathrm{SAV},\mathrm{MSAV})\) | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard |
| \((\mathrm{MSAV},\mathrm{SAV})\) | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard |
| \((\mathrm{MSAV},\mathrm{MSAV})\) | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard | \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard |

The hardness holds with identical candidate sets and societies, equal committee sizes and shares, and only singleton or empty approval types. All masses in the reduction are dyadic rationals.

There are also exact algorithms with running times exponential in either the overlap size or the total committee size, as proved below.

1. Feasibility and degenerate cases

Write \(\displaystyle T=C_1\cap C_2,\quad t=|T|,\quad P_i=C_i\setminus T,\quad K=k_1+k_2.\)

The stated cardinality assumptions guarantee a feasible allocation. Indeed, community \(i\) needs at least \(\displaystyle x_i=\max\{0,k_i-|P_i|\}\) shared candidates.

We have \(x_i\le t\), because \(k_i\le |P_i|+t\). Also \(x_1+x_2\le t\): when both are positive, this follows from
\(\displaystyle x_1+x_2=K-|P_1|-|P_2|\le t\);
when at most one is positive, it follows from \(x_i\le t\).

Thus we can choose disjoint shared subsets of sizes \(x_1,x_2\), and fill each committee with \(k_i-x_i\) private candidates.

If either \(k_i=0\) or \(\alpha_i=0\), then \(U_i\) is identically zero on feasible allocations. Both objectives are consequently zero, and the preceding construction gives an optimum in polynomial time. The hardness proof concerns instances with positive committee sizes and shares.

2. Fixed committee sizes give additive candidate weights

Assume first that \(k_i>0\). For each listed approval type, define
\(\displaystyle d_i(S)= \begin{cases} |S|,&f_i=\mathrm{SAV},\\ \min\{|S|,k_i\},&f_i=\mathrm{MSAV}. \end{cases}\)

For \(c\in C_i\), put
\(\displaystyle a_i(c)=\alpha_i\sum_{\substack{S\ni c\\d_i(S)>0}}\frac{\mu_i(S)}{d_i(S)}.\)

If \(k_i=0\), instead set every \(a_i(c)=0\).

For every committee of the required size, exchanging the finite sums gives
\(\displaystyle U_i(w_i) =\alpha_i\sum_{\substack{S\\d_i(S)>0}} \frac{\mu_i(S)}{d_i(S)} \sum_{c\in w_i}\mathbf{1}_{c\in S} =\sum_{c\in w_i}a_i(c). \)

For \(k_i=0\), both sides are zero, so the equality still holds. All candidate weights are nonnegative.

Both objectives are nondecreasing in each utility on the nonnegative quadrant. Therefore, any replacement that increases one community’s additive utility while leaving the other unchanged cannot worsen either objective. This observation will justify both exact algorithms.

Exact arithmetic

Define
\(\displaystyle B=2+L+\tau\left\lceil\log_2(m+1)\right\rceil.\)

There is a common positive integer denominator \(D\) of bit length at most \(B\) for all candidate weights. To construct one, multiply:

  • the denominators of both shares;
  • the denominators of all listed masses;
  • every positive integer \(d_i(S)\).

The first two groups contribute at most \(L\) bits, and the last contributes at most \(\tau\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil\) bits.

Consequently,
\(\displaystyle A_i(c)=Da_i(c)\)
is a nonnegative integer. We can compare objectives exactly using
\(\displaystyle \min\left\{\sum_{c\in w_1}A_1(c),\sum_{c\in w_2}A_2(c)\right\}\)
or
\(\displaystyle \left(\sum_{c\in w_1}A_1(c)\right) \left(\sum_{c\in w_2}A_2(c)\right). \)

For a feasible committee, \(U_i(w_i)\le1\), since every approval satisfaction lies between zero and one. Hence feasible scaled utility sums are at most \(D\), and their products are at most \(D^2\). Even sums over all candidates are at most \(mD\), so all arithmetic uses \(O(B)\)-bit integers.

Using ordinary quadratic-time integer arithmetic, computing the weights, sorting candidates by weight, and preparing prefix sums takes
\(\displaystyle O\!\left((m+1)(\tau+\log_2(m+2))B^2\right)\)
bit operations. There are at most \(m\tau\) candidate–type incidences to process.

3. A common hardness reduction for all four rule pairs

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), decide whether some subset has sum half their total. This classical hardness result appears in [Karp’s *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let
\(\displaystyle A=\sum_{j=1}^n a_j.\)

Construct \(2n\) candidates with positive integer weights
\(\displaystyle b_j=1+a_j\quad(1\le j\le n),\qquad b_{n+j}=1\quad(1\le j\le n).\)

Their total weight is
\(\displaystyle W=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}b_j=A+2n.\)

A subset of exactly \(n\) candidates corresponds to a subset \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) of the original candidates, together with \(n-|I|\) dummy candidates. Its weight is therefore
\(\displaystyle n+\sum_{j\in I}a_j.\)

Conversely, every original subset \(I\) can be completed in this way, because there are \(n\) dummy candidates. Thus
\(\displaystyle \text{some $n\)-candidate subset has weight \(W/2\)}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance}.
\)

Encoding the weights as normalized societies

Let \(D\) be the smallest power of two strictly greater than \(W\). Set
\(\displaystyle C_1=C_2=\{c_1,\ldots,c_{2n}\},\qquad k_1=k_2=n,\qquad \alpha_1=\alpha_2=\frac12.\)

Use identical societies:
\(\displaystyle \mu_i(\{c_j\})=\frac{b_j}{D}\quad(1\le j\le2n), \qquad \mu_i(\varnothing)=\frac{D-W}{D}. \)

Every listed mass is positive, and their sum is
\(\displaystyle \frac{W}{D}+\frac{D-W}{D}=1.\)

All masses are dyadic rationals. Each society has \(2n+1\) listed types, so
\(\displaystyle m=2n,\qquad \tau=4n+2.\)

Because \(D\le2W\), the binary descriptions of these masses and of the thresholds below have polynomial length in the original input length. Thus this is a polynomial-time reduction in the required support representation.

Why the rule pair does not matter

For a singleton approval type and a committee of positive size, both rules give
\(\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{SAV}}(\{c_j\},w) =q_{\mathrm{MSAV}}(\{c_j\},w) =\mathbf{1}_{c_j\in w}. \)

The empty approval type contributes zero under both rules. Therefore, independently of the chosen rule pair,
\(\displaystyle U_i(w_i)=\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{c_j\in w_i}b_j.\)

The committees are disjoint and their sizes sum to \(2n\), so they exhaust the candidate set. If
\(\displaystyle s=\sum_{c_j\in w_1}b_j,\)
then
\(\displaystyle U_1=\frac{s}{2D},\qquad U_2=\frac{W-s}{2D}.\)

Egalitarian objective

For every feasible allocation,
\(\displaystyle \min\{U_1,U_2\} =\frac{W}{4D}-\frac{|s-W/2|}{2D} \le\frac{W}{4D}. \)

Equality holds exactly when \(s=W/2\). By the cardinality argument above, this happens exactly for yes-instances of PARTITION.

Hence deciding whether an allocation has minimum utility at least \(W/(4D)\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every rule pair.

Product objective

Likewise,
\(\displaystyle U_1U_2 =\frac{s(W-s)}{4D^2} =\frac{W^2}{16D^2}-\frac{(s-W/2)^2}{4D^2} \le\frac{W^2}{16D^2}. \)

Equality again holds exactly when \(s=W/2\). Thus deciding whether an allocation has product at least \(W^2/(16D^2)\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every rule pair.

An exact maximizing-allocation algorithm would solve either threshold problem by returning an allocation whose objective can be evaluated exactly. Therefore both optimization problems are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

Finally, an allocation is a polynomial-size certificate. Its eligibility, cardinalities, disjointness, and objective threshold can all be checked with polynomial-bit arithmetic. Both threshold decision versions are therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

4. Exact algorithms and running times

Let
\(\displaystyle r=\min\{k_1,k_2\}, \qquad Q=(m+1)(\tau+\log_2(m+2)). \)

The following bounds apply to both objectives and every rule pair.

An algorithm parameterized by total committee size

For community \(i\), let \(H_i\) contain its \(\min\{K,|C_i|\}\) highest-weight candidates, resolving ties by any fixed ordering.

Claim. Some optimum has \(w_i\subseteq H_i\) for both communities.

To prove this, start from an optimum. Suppose \(c\in w_i\setminus H_i\). Then \(|H_i|=K\); otherwise \(H_i=C_i\), contradicting the choice of \(c\).

Exactly \(K\) candidates are allocated in total, including \(c\notin H_i\). Thus at most \(K-1\) members of \(H_i\) are allocated, and some \(d\in H_i\) is unused. Replacing \(c\) by \(d\) preserves feasibility and cannot decrease \(U_i\). It leaves the other utility unchanged.

The replacement also decreases \(|w_i\setminus H_i|\). Repeating these replacements for both communities terminates after at most \(K\) steps. The resulting allocation remains optimal, proving the claim.

Now choose a community \(i\) with \(k_i=r\). Enumerate every \(r\)-element subset \(W\subseteq H_i\). For each \(W\), give the other community its highest-weight \(k_j\) candidates in \(C_j\setminus W\), rejecting \(W\) if fewer than \(k_j\) are available.

This completion maximizes the other utility for fixed \(W\), so it also maximizes either objective for that \(W\). The claim guarantees that one enumerated \(W\) belongs to an optimum.

It suffices to scan \(H_j\) for each completion. If \(|C_j|\le K\), this is the entire candidate set. Otherwise, \(H_j\) has \(K\) candidates and at most \(r\) are blocked, leaving at least \(K-r=k_j\) available.

There are at most \(\binom{K}{r}\) enumerated subsets, and each evaluation takes \(O((K+1)B^2)\) bit operations. The total running time is
\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\left[Q+(K+1)\binom{K}{r}\right]B^2\right). \)

Since \(\binom{K}{r}\le2^K\), this proves fixed-parameter tractability in \(K=k_1+k_2\).

An algorithm parameterized by overlap size

Enumerate every assignment of the \(t\) shared candidates to three states: community one, community two, or unallocated. There are \(3^t\) assignments.

For each assignment:

  • Reject it if either community receives more than its required committee size.
  • Fill each committee’s remaining positions with its highest-weight private candidates.
  • Reject it if insufficient private candidates exist.
  • Evaluate the objective.

For a fixed shared assignment, the private choices are independent between communities. Choosing the highest-weight private candidates maximizes both utilities simultaneously, and hence maximizes either objective for that assignment. Every feasible allocation has one of the enumerated shared assignments, proving correctness.

After sorting private candidates and preparing prefix sums, an assignment can be evaluated in \(O((t+1)B^2)\) bit operations. The total running time is
\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\left[Q+(t+1)3^t\right]B^2\right). \)

This proves fixed-parameter tractability in \(t=|C_1\cap C_2|\). In particular, disjoint candidate sets give a polynomial-time case.

Choosing the smaller of the two enumeration bounds yields
\(\displaystyle O\!\left( \left[ Q+\min\left\{(K+1)\binom{K}{r},(t+1)3^t\right\} \right]B^2 \right). \)

These algorithms use the listed support directly; their polynomial factors depend on \(\tau\) and \(L\), without expanding rational masses into an explicit electorate.
