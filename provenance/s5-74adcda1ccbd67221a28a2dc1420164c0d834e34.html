The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(C\) with rational per-capita capacities, and a finite set of student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), strict school rankings, and priority tiers, compute a feasible type-mass assignment that weakly improves on the fractional deferred-acceptance assignment and is Pareto efficient. Weak improvement is defined by a feasible reassignment of each type's baseline mass to schools it ranks at least as highly; Pareto efficiency means no feasible reassignment weakly benefits all mass and strictly benefits positive mass.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools with positive binary rational per-capita capacities \(q_c\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type specifies a strict ranking of all schools and a priority tier at each school; each school's tiers are totally ordered. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
All schools are acceptable. Append an unassigned option \(\bot\), ranked last by every type, with unlimited capacity. Write \(D=C\cup\{\bot\}\). A feasible assignment satisfies \(x_{ta}\geq0\), \(\sum_{a\in D}x_{ta}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\leq q_c\).
Define the fractional deferred-acceptance baseline \(b\) by synchronous rounds: initially every type applies with its entire mass to its first school; subsequently each rejected portion applies to its type's best option that has not previously rejected any of that type's mass. Schools reconsider held and newly applying mass together, serving priority tiers in order.
Within a tier, if type \(t\) offers mass \(p_t\), increase its held mass from zero at rate \(\mu_t\), stopping that type at \(p_t\); stop serving when capacity is exhausted and reject the excess. The option \(\bot\) accepts everything. Define \(b\) by the limiting held masses, including cases where the rounds do not terminate finitely.
For feasible assignments \(x,y\), say \(y\) weakly improves \(x\) if there are nonnegative transport masses \(z_{t,a,d}\) with \(\sum_d z_{t,a,d}=x_{ta}\), \(\sum_a z_{t,a,d}=y_{td}\), and \(z_{t,a,d}=0\) unless \(d\succeq_t a\). Improvement is strict if \(\sum_{t,a,d:\,d\succ_t a}z_{t,a,d}>0\). An assignment is Pareto efficient if no feasible assignment strictly improves it; priorities impose no additional restrictions on these reassignments.

Is computing an exact rational feasible assignment that weakly improves \(b\) and is Pareto efficient solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite students with strict school priorities, deferred acceptance followed by compensating top trading cycles computes a Pareto-efficient assignment weakly improving every student's deferred-acceptance placement in polynomial time ([Alcalde–Romero-Medina](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/62831/1/MPRA_paper_62831.pdf)).
For finite random assignments with priority ties, fractional deferred acceptance and trading improves its fractional baseline and guarantees ordinal efficiency within the ex-ante-stable assignments; this is a restricted efficiency guarantee, rather than the unrestricted requirement above ([Kesten–Ünver](https://doi.org/10.3982/TE1558)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to total mass one, interpreting capacities as per-capita quantities; retained nonempty schools and positive capacities from the discrete domain.
  • Retained the discrete assumption that every school is acceptable and added an unlimited-capacity unassigned option ranked strictly last; imposed no requirement that school capacities sum to one.
  • The graded question did not specify how priority ties determine the fractional baseline. Chose equal per-capita, capped sharing within each tier, extending the fractional deferred-acceptance convention to unequal type masses.
  • Specified synchronous proposal rounds and defined the baseline by their limit because fractional deferred acceptance need not terminate finitely.
  • Formalized weak and strict mass improvement through transport within each type, allowing nonnegative real transport masses and requiring an exact rational output assignment.
  • Specified that priority tiers determine the baseline but do not constrain subsequent feasible assignments or Pareto comparisons, matching the discrete efficiency definition.

1. Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The unrestricted improvement step is a linear program once the baseline is known. The main issue is computing the exact limit defining \(b\). Below I prove that this limit can also be computed in polynomial time, without expanding rational masses into individual students.

This distinction matters: a counterexample to the termination claim for the earlier FDA cycle-resolution algorithm appears in [Cookson–Shah, *Fairly Stable Two-Sided Matching with Indifferences*](https://openreview.net/pdf/dffcc4edbb2500931a4ea72822fdb0ecb32ff51c.pdf). The algorithm proved here computes the limit by a different sequence of linear programs.

2. A school’s rejection map

Fix a school \(c\). For a vector of offers \(p=(p_t)_{t\in T}\), let \(H_c(p)\) be its held vector under the rule in the question, and let

\(R_c(p)=p-H_c(p)\).

For a type \(t\), define:

  • \(A_{tc}\): the types in the same priority tier as \(t\) at \(c\);
  • \(B_{tc}(p)=\sum_{s:\,s\text{ has higher priority than }t\text{ at }c}p_s\);
  • \(\mu(S)=\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s\).

Every set \(S\) used below contains \(t\), so \(\mu(S)>0\).

Define the following function:

\[ \]
\(G_{tc}(p)= \min\left\{ p_t,\ \min_{\substack{S\subseteq A_{tc}\\t\in S}} \left[ p_t+\frac{\mu_t}{\mu(S)} \left(B_{tc}(p)+\sum_{s\in A_{tc}\setminus S}p_s-q_c\right) \right] \right\}.\)

Lemma 1. For every nonnegative offer vector,

\(R_{tc}(p)=\max\{0,G_{tc}(p)\}.\)

Consequently, \(R_c\) is continuous and coordinatewise nondecreasing. Moreover, \(G_{tc}\) is the minimum of finitely many affine functions, each having nonnegative coefficients on the offer variables.

Proof. Fix \(v>0\). I first show that \(R_{tc}(p)\geq v\) is equivalent to

\(v\leq p_t,\qquad q_c\leq B_{tc}(p)+ \sum_{s\in A_{tc}} \min\left\{p_s,\frac{\mu_s}{\mu_t}(p_t-v)\right\}.\) (1)

If \(B_{tc}(p)\geq q_c\), type \(t\)'s tier receives no capacity. Its rejection is \(p_t\), and the equivalence follows immediately.

Suppose \(B_{tc}(p)<q_c\). Its tier has residual capacity \(Q=q_c-B_{tc}(p)>0\). If this tier is rationed, its held amounts have the form \(\min\{p_s,\mu_s\lambda\}\), where the water level \(\lambda\) satisfies

\(\sum_{s\in A_{tc}}\min\{p_s,\mu_s\lambda\}=Q.\)

Type \(t\)'s rejection is at least \(v\) exactly when \(\lambda\leq(p_t-v)/\mu_t\), which is precisely (1). If the tier is fully served, (1) cannot hold: its right-hand tier sum is at most \(\sum_{s\in A_{tc}}p_s-v<Q\). This proves the equivalence.

Write \(h=(p_t-v)/\mu_t\). Since \(v>0\), the summand for \(t\) in (1) equals \(\mu_t h\). Independently choosing either \(p_s\) or \(\mu_s h\) for every other summand gives

\(\sum_{s\in A_{tc}}\min\{p_s,\mu_s h\} = \min_{\substack{S\subseteq A_{tc}\\t\in S}} \left\{\mu(S)h+\sum_{s\in A_{tc}\setminus S}p_s\right\}.\)

Thus (1) is equivalent to \(v\leq p_t\) and, for every such \(S\),

\(v\leq p_t+\frac{\mu_t}{\mu(S)} \left(B_{tc}(p)+\sum_{s\in A_{tc}\setminus S}p_s-q_c\right).\)

This proves the rejection formula. Its continuity and monotonicity follow because every displayed affine function has nonnegative variable coefficients. ∎

Two additional properties of the holding rule will connect cumulative offers to the specified rounds.

Lemma 2.

  • If \(0\leq\gamma\leq R_c(p)\), then \(H_c(p-\gamma)=H_c(p)\).
  • For every \(\delta\geq0\),

\(H_c(H_c(p)+\delta)=H_c(p+\delta)\).

  • If \(R_{tc}(p)>0\), then adding further offers from \(t\) alone leaves the entire held vector unchanged.

Proof. For the first assertion, consider the priority tier where capacity is exhausted. Removing rejected offers leaves every offer at least as large as its held amount. At the original water level, the held amounts are therefore unchanged. Higher tiers had no rejected offers, and lower tiers still receive no capacity.

By Lemma 1, \(R_c(p+\delta)\geq R_c(p)\). Applying the first assertion to \(p+\delta\), with \(\gamma=R_c(p)\), proves the second assertion.

For the third assertion, a rejected type is either below the tier exhausting capacity or has an offer strictly above its held amount at that tier’s water level. Increasing that offer changes neither the water level nor any held amount. ∎

3. The baseline is a least fixed point

List type \(t\)'s schools as \(c_{t1},\ldots,c_{tm}\), from best to worst. Introduce one coordinate \(r_{tc}\) for every type–school pair, interpreted as cumulative rejected mass. There are \(n=m\tau\) coordinates.

Given \(r\), define cumulative offers by

\(p_{t,c_{t1}}(r)=\mu_t,\qquad p_{t,c_{tk}}(r)=r_{t,c_{t,k-1}}\quad(k>1).\)

Define the map \(F\) by applying each school’s rejection rule:

\(F_{tc}(r)=R_{tc}\bigl((p_{sc}(r))_{s\in T}\bigr).\)

It is a continuous, coordinatewise nondecreasing self-map of the box

\(\mathcal B=\prod_{t,c}[0,\mu_t].\)

Starting with \(r^{(0)}=0\), the sequence \(r^{(k+1)}=F(r^{(k)})\) is nondecreasing and bounded. Its limit \(r^\star\) exists and, by continuity, satisfies \(F(r^\star)=r^\star\). It is the least fixed point: induction gives \(r^{(k)}\leq u\) for every fixed point \(u\in\mathcal B\).

Lemma 3. The baseline specified in the question is

\(b_{tc}=p_{tc}(r^\star)-r^\star_{tc},\qquad b_{t\bot}=r^\star_{t,c_{tm}}.\) (2)

Proof. Track cumulative applications and cumulative rejections during the specified algorithm. When the algorithm skips a school that previously rejected some of that type’s mass, insert a virtual application at that school and reject it immediately. Lemma 2(3) shows that this insertion changes no held amount.

Cumulative offers remain consistent with the actual holding rule: when new mass \(\delta\) arrives, the school considers its old held vector plus \(\delta\); Lemma 2(2) identifies the resulting holdings with its choice from the cumulative offer vector.

Every portion moves only downward through its ranking. Thus cumulative applications and cumulative rejections at each type–school pair are nondecreasing and bounded by \(\mu_t\).

They are also bounded by the offers and rejections at \(r^\star\). This follows inductively: offers at the first school are \(\mu_t\); offers at later schools cannot exceed the cumulative rejections at the preceding school; and applying a school’s monotone rejection map preserves the bound because \(F(r^\star)=r^\star\).

It remains to establish flow conservation at the limit. The total cumulative rejected mass over all pairs is bounded by \(m\sum_t\mu_t=m\). Hence its increase per round tends to zero. All mass awaiting an application after a round was newly rejected during that round, so the total waiting mass also tends to zero.

Consequently, at the limit, cumulative offers at each school after the first equal cumulative rejections at its predecessor. Continuity of the school choices therefore makes the limiting cumulative rejection vector a fixed point of \(F\). It is bounded above by \(r^\star\); leastness of \(r^\star\) gives equality.

Subtracting cumulative rejections from cumulative offers proves (2), including the mass reaching \(\bot\). ∎

In particular, once \(r^\star\) is computed, (2) is feasible: nonnegativity and school capacities follow from school choices, and each type’s row sum telescopes to \(\mu_t\).

4. A linear-programming lemma for concave maps

The following lemma is the acceleration step. The treatment of zero coordinates is essential.

Lemma 4. Let \(K\) be a coordinatewise nondecreasing self-map of a box \([0,U]\subseteq\mathbb R^N\), with

\(K_i(s)=\min_{\ell\in J_i} \left\{a_{i\ell}+\sum_j A_{i\ell j}s_j\right\},\qquad a_{i\ell}\geq0,\quad A_{i\ell j}\geq0,\)

where every \(J_i\) is finite. Let \(s^\star\) be the limit of iteration from zero, and put \(P=\{i:s_i^\star>0\}\).

Then \(P\) can be identified with at most \(N+1\) evaluations of \(K\). Furthermore, \(s^\star\) is the unique optimizer in the \(s\)-variables of

\(\max\sum_i s_i\)

subject to

\(0\leq s\leq U,\qquad s\leq K(s),\qquad s_i=0\quad(i\notin P).\) (3)

Proof. Every affine piece is nonnegative on the box. Whether \(K_i(s)>0\) therefore depends only on which coordinates of \(s\) are positive: every affine piece must have either a positive constant or a positive contribution from such a coordinate.

The supports of successive iterates from zero are nondecreasing. Once two consecutive supports agree, they agree forever. There are at most \(N\) strict increases, proving the evaluation bound.

Continuity and monotonicity imply that \(s^\star\) is a fixed point and is feasible for (3). I show that every feasible \(y\) satisfies \(y\leq s^\star\).

Otherwise, define

\(\alpha=\max_{i\in P}\frac{y_i}{s_i^\star}>1,\qquad S=\{i\in P:y_i=\alpha s_i^\star\}.\)

For each \(i\in S\), choose an affine piece attaining \(K_i(s^\star)=s_i^\star\). Write this piece as \(a_i+\sum_j A_{ij}s_j\). Because \(y\leq K(y)\) and \(y\leq\alpha s^\star\),

\(\alpha s_i^\star =y_i \leq a_i+\sum_j A_{ij}y_j \leq a_i+\alpha\sum_j A_{ij}s_j^\star =\alpha s_i^\star-(\alpha-1)a_i.\)

Thus \(a_i=0\). Equality also implies \(A_{ij}=0\) for every \(j\in P\setminus S\), since \(y_j<\alpha s_j^\star\).

Each chosen piece therefore has zero constant and depends only on coordinates in \(S\) or outside \(P\). Coordinates outside \(P\) are zero in every iterate from zero. Inductively, the coordinates in \(S\) must also remain zero: the selected pieces give upper bounds of zero for their next values. This contradicts \(S\subseteq P\).

Hence every feasible \(y\leq s^\star\). Maximizing the sum uniquely selects \(s^\star\). ∎

5. Computing the rejection fixed point

Call \(r\) post-fixed when \(r\leq F(r)\). The algorithm maintains a post-fixed vector with \(r\leq r^\star\).

Initialize \(r=0\). Repeat the following steps.

  • Compute \(v=F(r)\). If \(v=r\), stop.
  • Set \(r=v\), and let \(E=\{(t,c):r_{tc}>0\}\).
  • Keep all coordinates outside \(E\) at zero, and compute the least limit of iteration on the coordinates in \(E\), starting from the current \(r\).
  • Replace \(r\) by that limit.

I now show that step 3 is an exact polynomial-size LP computation.

5.1. Rejected pairs give a concave map

Let \(r^0\) be the vector at the start of step 3. Restrict to

\(r^0_i\leq r_i\leq\mu_t\quad(i=(t,c)\in E),\qquad r_i=0\quad(i\notin E).\)

For \(i\in E\), monotonicity and post-fixedness give

\(F_i(r)\geq F_i(r^0)\geq r_i^0>0.\)

Thus the outer maximum with zero in Lemma 1 is inactive throughout this domain. Consequently, \(F_i(r)=G_i(p(r))\), a minimum of affine functions with nonnegative variable coefficients.

Shift the variables by \(s_i=r_i-r_i^0\). Every shifted affine piece has a nonnegative constant: its value at \(r^0\) is at least \(F_i(r^0)\geq r_i^0\).

The restricted iteration therefore satisfies all hypotheses of Lemma 4. Its positive-increment coordinates can be identified by at most \(|E|+1\) ordinary evaluations of the school choices.

5.2. A polynomial-size representation of the LP

The subset formula defining \(G_i\) has potentially exponentially many pieces, but the constraints needed in (3) have a compact formulation.

For \(i=(t,c)\in E\), introduce variables \(u_{i,s}\) for \(s\in A_{tc}\). Using the offer expressions \(p_{sc}(r)\), impose

\(r_{tc}\leq p_{tc}(r),\)

\(0\leq u_{i,s}\leq p_{sc}(r),\)

\(u_{i,s}\leq\frac{\mu_s}{\mu_t}\bigl(p_{tc}(r)-r_{tc}\bigr),\)

\(B_{tc}(p(r))+\sum_{s\in A_{tc}}u_{i,s}\geq q_c.\) (4)

Because \(r_{tc}\geq r_{tc}^0>0\), equivalence (1), with \(v=r_{tc}\), applies. The existence of these auxiliary variables is therefore equivalent to \(r_{tc}\leq F_{tc}(r)\):

  • Any feasible auxiliaries are bounded above by the corresponding minima in (1).
  • Conversely, choosing each auxiliary equal to its corresponding minimum satisfies (4) whenever (1) holds.

Add the domain bounds and impose \(r_i=r_i^0\) for every coordinate identified as having zero increment. Maximize

\(\sum_{i\in E}(r_i-r_i^0).\)

Lemma 4 proves that the resulting \(r\)-vector is exactly the restricted iteration limit. There are at most \(m\tau^2\) auxiliary variables and a polynomial number of linear constraints.

5.3. Correctness and number of phases

Initially, \(0\leq F(0)\), and \(0\leq r^\star\).

If \(r\) is post-fixed and bounded above by \(r^\star\), monotonicity gives

\(F(r)\leq F(F(r)),\qquad F(r)\leq F(r^\star)=r^\star.\)

Thus step 2 preserves the invariants.

The restricted iterates in step 3 are likewise bounded above by \(r^\star\). At their limit, coordinates in \(E\) satisfy \(r_i=F_i(r)\); coordinates outside \(E\) are zero. Hence the resulting vector remains post-fixed.

After step 3, any discrepancy \(F(r)\ne r\) must occur outside \(E\). The next phase therefore adds at least one previously zero coordinate to \(E\). Positive coordinates never become zero.

There are only \(n=m\tau\) coordinates, so there are at most \(n\) phases. At termination, \(r\) is a fixed point bounded above by \(r^\star\). Leastness gives \(r=r^\star\).

This proves an exact algorithm using at most \(m\tau\) polynomial-size LPs and a polynomial number of water-filling evaluations.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

A bound on the number of phases alone would not suffice for binary rational inputs. The intermediate rational numbers must also have polynomial bit length.

Water-filling evaluation uses rational comparisons, additions, and divisions by sums of original masses, such as \(\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s\). Each such sum has bit length polynomial in \(L,\tau\).

For the phase LPs, all variable coefficients come from the original input: they are integers or ratios \(\mu_s/\mu_t\). Generated values appear only as right-hand-side bounds \(r_i^0\), never as variable coefficients.

To make the denominator bound explicit, track a common denominator \(D\) for all generated coordinates.

  • During one water-filling evaluation, new denominators divide \(D\) times a product of polynomially many factors of polynomial bit length derived from original masses.
  • For a phase LP, clear its coefficient denominators using original-input factors. A vertex is obtained by solving a full-rank integer linear system whose coefficient bit lengths are bounded independently of \(D\). Cramer’s rule shows that its coordinate denominators divide \(D\) times the determinant of that system, with any original right-hand-side denominator factors included.
  • Hadamard’s inequality bounds that determinant’s bit length polynomially in the LP dimension and original coefficient bit lengths.

Therefore each evaluation or LP increases \(\log D\) by at most a polynomial amount. The number of evaluations and LPs is polynomial.

All rejection coordinates and auxiliary variables lie in \([0,1]\), so the same common-denominator bound also controls their numerators. Every LP thus has polynomial input bit length and can be solved exactly in polynomial time.

It follows that \(r^\star\), and hence \(b\) from (2), are rational, have polynomial bit length, and are computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

7. Computing an unrestricted Pareto-efficient improvement

For type \(t\), list all options as

\(a_{t1}\succ_t a_{t2}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t a_{t,m+1}=\bot.\)

Define its prefix masses by

\(X_{tk}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^k x_{t,a_{tj}}\qquad(1\leq k\leq m).\)

Lemma 5. Assignment \(y\) weakly improves \(x\) in the transport sense of the question if and only if

\(X_{tk}(y)\geq X_{tk}(x)\qquad\text{for every }t,k.\) (5)

Improvement is strict if and only if at least one inequality is strict.

Proof. Necessity follows because an allowed transport cannot move mass out of a preference prefix.

For sufficiency, partition \([0,\mu_t]\) into successive intervals of lengths \(x_{t,a_{tj}}\), and separately into successive intervals of lengths \(y_{t,a_{tj}}\). Transport from source option \(a_{ti}\) to destination option \(a_{tj}\) the length of intersection of their two intervals.

These intersection lengths have the required marginals. If \(j>i\), the destination interval starts at

\(\sum_{\ell<j}y_{t,a_{t\ell}} \geq\sum_{\ell\leq i}y_{t,a_{t\ell}} \geq\sum_{\ell\leq i}x_{t,a_{t\ell}},\)

which is the end of the source interval. Thus no positive mass moves downward.

Finally, give option \(a_{tj}\) weight \(m+1-j\). Under any allowed transport, the change in total weight is positive exactly when positive mass moves strictly upward. The same weight equals the sum of the prefix masses. Under (5), that sum increases exactly when some prefix inequality is strict. ∎

Now solve the following rational LP:

\(\max W(x),\qquad W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=1}^m X_{tk}(x),\)

subject to assignment feasibility and

\(X_{tk}(x)\geq X_{tk}(b)\qquad\text{for every }t,k.\) (6)

This LP is feasible because \(b\) is feasible. It is bounded because each assignment coordinate lies between zero and \(\mu_t\). Its size and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial, so an exact rational optimizer \(x^\star\) is computable in polynomial time.

By Lemma 5, \(x^\star\) weakly improves \(b\).

Suppose a feasible assignment \(y\) strictly improved \(x^\star\). Lemma 5 would give

\(X_{tk}(y)\geq X_{tk}(x^\star)\geq X_{tk}(b)\)

for all \(t,k\), with at least one strict inequality in the first comparison. Thus \(y\) would satisfy (6) and have \(W(y)>W(x^\star)\), contradicting optimality.

Therefore \(x^\star\) is Pareto efficient among all feasible assignments, with no priority restrictions on the improvements.

8. Conclusion

Both the exact limiting baseline and an unrestricted Pareto-efficient assignment weakly improving it can be computed in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The algorithm operates on the listed support types and never expands their rational masses into individual students.
