Proposition 3.7 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a peak \(p_t\in\Delta^m\) and specified \(\ell_r\) utility for each type, find allocations \(x_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\) with \(q=\sum_t x_t\) such that every type is best responding: \(u_t(q)\ge u_t(x'_t+\sum_{s\ne t}x_s)\) for every \(x'_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\geq2\) and \(\Delta^m(a)=\{z\in[0,1]^m:\sum_{j=1}^m z_j=a\}\), with \(\Delta^m=\Delta^m(1)\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Each type has a binary rational peak \(p_t\in\Delta^m\) and a specified finite binary rational exponent \(r_t\geq1\), with utility \(u_t(q)=-\|p_t-q\|_{r_t}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type controls its entire mass: its strategy is \(x_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\), and the outcome is \(q=\sum_t x_t\).
The computational problem is to find an exact pure Nash equilibrium: allocations satisfying \(u_t(q)\geq u_t(x'_t+\sum_{s\ne t}x_s)\) for every type \(t\) and every \(x'_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\).

Is this equilibrium computation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For equal shares among explicitly listed agents, a pure Nash equilibrium exists and can be computed in polynomial time, even simultaneously for all finite norm exponents at least \(1\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01228v1)
For rational unequal shares and \(\ell_1\) utilities, an equilibrium algorithm polynomial in the common denominator of the shares is known; this does not establish polynomial time in their binary encoding length. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08767v2#S8)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified utilities lacked a finite input encoding; peaks and type-specific finite exponents are taken to be binary rationals, with exponents at least one.
  • Retained the minimum of two alternatives. Allowed one supported type because multiple identical agents may yield a society with singleton support.
  • Interpreted each type as controlling its entire mass, as required by the stated deviation condition and the weighted budget-aggregation model.
  • Made equilibrium computation exact and defined L as the total input bit length.

Result

The problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). An exact rational equilibrium can be computed by solving one convex quadratic program. The resulting allocations are an equilibrium simultaneously for every finite norm exponent at least \(1\).

The proof below works directly with the supported types and their binary-encoded masses.

1. A sufficient condition for simultaneous equilibrium

For a feasible allocation profile \(x=(x_t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\), write \(q=\sum_t x_t\) and define

\(D_t(q)=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}(p_{t,j}-q_j).\)

We first prove that the following condition is sufficient:

\(x_{t,j}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ p_{t,j}-q_j=D_t(q).\) \((1)\)

Thus, each type contributes only to coordinates with maximal deficit relative to its peak. This is the sufficient condition used in the cited equal-share argument; we prove its validity for arbitrary masses directly. [Proposition 3.7 and Lemma A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01228v1#A1)

Lemma. Any feasible allocation profile satisfying \((1)\) is a pure Nash equilibrium simultaneously for all finite exponents \(r_t\geq1\).

Proof. Fix a type \(t\), an exponent \(r\geq1\), and an arbitrary alternative allocation \(x'_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\). Put

\(e_j=q_j-p_{t,j},\qquad \delta_j=x'_{t,j}-x_{t,j},\qquad a=\min_j e_j.\)

The outcome after the deviation has error \(e_j+\delta_j\) in coordinate \(j\). Condition \((1)\) says that \(x_{t,j}>0\) only where \(e_j=a\).

Let \(h_r(z)=|z|^r\). Choose a supporting slope \(g_r(z)\) by

\(g_r(z)=r\,\operatorname{sgn}(z)|z|^{r-1}\) when \(r>1\), with \(g_r(0)=0\), and

\(g_1(z)=\operatorname{sgn}(z)\), with \(\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0\).

For \(r>1\), this is the derivative of the convex function \(h_r\); for \(r=1\), it is a subgradient of the absolute-value function. In both cases, \(g_r\) is nondecreasing and the supporting-line inequality gives

\(h_r(e_j+\delta_j)\geq h_r(e_j)+g_r(e_j)\delta_j.\)

Set \(c=g_r(a)\). Since \(e_j\geq a\), we have \(g_r(e_j)\geq c\) for every \(j\). Moreover, \(g_r(e_j)=c\) whenever \(x_{t,j}>0\). Consequently,

\(\sum_j g_r(e_j)x'_{t,j}\geq c\sum_j x'_{t,j}=c\mu_t,\)

whereas

\(\sum_j g_r(e_j)x_{t,j}=c\sum_j x_{t,j}=c\mu_t.\)

Subtracting shows that \(\sum_j g_r(e_j)\delta_j\geq0\). Summing the supporting-line inequalities therefore yields

\(\sum_j |e_j+\delta_j|^r\geq\sum_j |e_j|^r.\)

Taking the increasing \(r\)-th root proves that the deviation cannot decrease the distance from \(p_t\). Thus it cannot increase the type’s utility. The type, deviation, and exponent were arbitrary. \(\square\)

2. A convex quadratic program enforcing the condition

Consider the following program in the \(m\tau\) variables \(x_{t,j}\):

Minimize

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}x_{t,j}\right)^2-\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{t,j}x_{t,j}\)

subject to

\(x_{t,j}\geq0\quad\text{for every }t,j,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{t,j}=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t.\) \((2)\)

These constraints are exactly the required allocation constraints: nonnegativity and the row sum imply \(x_{t,j}\leq\mu_t\leq1\).

The feasible set is nonempty, since one can assign each type’s entire mass to coordinate \(1\). It is closed and bounded, hence compact. Therefore the continuous objective attains its minimum.

The objective is convex. Indeed, its Hessian has quadratic form

\(\displaystyle v^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2\Phi\,v=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}v_{t,j}\right)^2\geq0.\)

Lemma. Every minimizer of \((2)\) satisfies \((1)\).

Proof. Let \(x\) be a minimizer and \(q=\sum_t x_t\). Fix a type \(t\) and a coordinate \(j\) with \(x_{t,j}>0\). For any different coordinate \(k\), transferring an amount \(\varepsilon\) from \(x_{t,j}\) to \(x_{t,k}\) is feasible whenever \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq x_{t,j}\).

The objective’s partial derivatives are

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{t,j}}=q_j-p_{t,j}.\)

Hence the right derivative at \(\varepsilon=0\) along this feasible transfer is

\((q_k-p_{t,k})-(q_j-p_{t,j}).\)

It must be nonnegative: a negative right derivative would give a feasible allocation with smaller objective for sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Thus

\(q_j-p_{t,j}\leq q_k-p_{t,k}\)

for every \(k\). Equivalently, \(p_{t,j}-q_j\) is a maximal deficit. This proves \((1)\). \(\square\)

Combining the two lemmas, every minimizer of \((2)\) is an equilibrium for all the specified utilities.

3. Exact computation and binary complexity

We use the following standard theorem on convex quadratic programming:

Given a rational symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, a rational linear objective term, and rational linear constraints, an exact rational optimizer can be computed in time polynomial in the total binary encoding length whenever the feasible set is nonempty and the minimum is attained.

This is the exact, binary-complexity result of Kozlov, Tarasov, and Khachiyan, rather than merely an approximation guarantee. [The polynomial solvability of convex quadratic programming](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5189)

All its hypotheses have been verified for \((2)\):

  • The Hessian is symmetric and positive semidefinite, as shown above. Its entries are \(0\) or \(1\).
  • The linear objective coefficients are the rational numbers \(-p_{t,j}\).
  • The constraints have coefficients \(0\) or \(1\) and rational right-hand sides \(\mu_t\).
  • The feasible set is nonempty and compact, so the minimum is attained.

There are \(m\tau\) variables, \(m\tau\) nonnegativity constraints, and \(\tau\) equalities. Even a dense representation of the Hessian has size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\); all remaining rational data come directly from the input. Equalities may be represented by pairs of inequalities. If integer coefficients are required, clearing denominators increases encoding length only polynomially, because the bit length of a product of denominators is bounded by the sum of their bit lengths.

The theorem therefore computes an exact rational minimizer in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). By Section 2 and Section 1, its allocations constitute the requested exact pure Nash equilibrium.

The exponents do not occur in the quadratic program. Their numerical magnitude creates no additional computational difficulty, because the proof establishes the equilibrium inequalities simultaneously for every finite exponent at least \(1\).

4. Conclusion

The algorithm is simply to construct \((2)\), solve it exactly, and return its allocation variables. It handles each supported type as one player controlling its entire mass. No expansion into a number of agents proportional to a common denominator is required.
