1. Risk and comparator conventions

For one valuation, write
$$ R_\alpha(f,g)= \Pr_{S,S'\sim\mathcal D} \bigl[ f(S)<f(S'),\ \alpha f(S)\leq f(S'),\ g(S,S')\text{ chooses }S \bigr]. $$
The society risk is
$$ R_\alpha=\sum_t\mu_t R_\alpha(f_t,g_t), $$
because the test type is independent of the two test bundles.

For every comparator,
$$ 0\leq R_\alpha(f,g)\leq\frac12. $$
Indeed, independence and exchangeability give
$$ \Pr[f(S)<f(S')] = \Pr[f(S')<f(S)] \leq\frac12. $$
Thus $\epsilon\geq1/2$ is trivial. The substantive lower bounds below use fixed smaller error and failure probabilities.

Every comparator constructed here first returns equality on identical bundles. On distinct bundles it selects a physical bundle according to a rule invariant under swapping the arguments. Consequently, it always chooses a bundle, and swapping the arguments reverses the reported choice. True-value ties are broken by a fixed lexicographic order.

2. Structural separation after fixing one item

2.1. Established approximation theorem

We use the following existential ellipsoidal approximation theorem:

If $h$ is a nonnegative, normalized, monotone submodular function on a ground set of size $q\geq1$, there are nonnegative weights $w_i$ such that, for every $U$,
$$ > h(U)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\in U}w_i}\leq\sqrt q\,h(U). > $$

This includes functions with zero-valued singleton coordinates. The theorem follows from the ellipsoidal approximation of the symmetrized polymatroid. We use its existence statement only; no cardinal oracle is needed to compute these weights. [Goemans–Harvey–Iwata–Mirrokni, *Approximating Submodular Functions Everywhere*](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~harvey/Publications/SubmodularEverywhere/SubmodularEverywhere.pdf).

2.2. Slice-separation lemma

For $j\in[n]$ and $z\in\{0,1\}$, define the slice
$$ C_{j,z}=\{S\subseteq[n]:\mathbf 1_{\{j\in S\}}=z\}. $$
Set
$$ \beta=\sqrt{n-1}. $$

Lemma. If $0\leq a<b$ and $b>\beta a$, then, on every slice $C_{j,z}$, an affine halfspace strictly separates
$$ \{S\in C_{j,z}:f(S)\leq a\} $$
from
$$ \{S\in C_{j,z}:f(S)\geq b\}. $$

Proof. Put $B=\varnothing$ when $z=0$, and $B=\{j\}$ when $z=1$. Let $c=f(B)$ and define, on $[n]\setminus\{j\}$,
$$ h(U)=f(B\cup U)-c. $$
This function is normalized. Monotonicity of $f$ makes it nonnegative and monotone. Submodularity follows because, for $U,V\subseteq[n]\setminus\{j\}$,
$$ \begin{aligned} h(U)+h(V) &=f(B\cup U)+f(B\cup V)-2c\\ &\geq f(B\cup(U\cup V))+f(B\cup(U\cap V))-2c\\ &=h(U\cup V)+h(U\cap V). \end{aligned} $$
Thus the approximation theorem applies with $q=n-1$.

If $a<c$, the lower family is empty, so the constant positive classifier suffices. Otherwise $a\geq c$, and
$$ b-c>\beta(a-c), $$
since
$$ b-c-\beta(a-c) = b-\beta a+(\beta-1)c>0. $$
For the theorem’s weights, writing $S=B\cup U$ gives
$$ f(S)\leq a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w\cdot\chi(U)\leq(n-1)(a-c)^2, $$
and
$$ f(S)\geq b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w\cdot\chi(U)\geq(b-c)^2. $$
The latter bound is strictly larger than the former. A threshold between them gives the required affine halfspace. $\square$

The constants $c,a,b$ and the weights are used only to prove existence. The learner will determine sample constraints using comparisons.

2.3. Why the direct endpoint argument needs repair

The strictness issue is substantive. Let $n=d^2$, with $d\geq2$, partition the items into $d$ groups of size $d$, and define
$$ f(S)=\text{number of groups intersected by }S. $$
This is a partition-matroid rank function.

Each entire group has value $1$, whereas each transversal containing one item from every group has value $d=\sqrt n$. The average indicator vector of the groups equals the average indicator vector of all transversals: every coordinate has average $1/d$. Therefore these two families cannot be strictly separated by an affine halfspace.

The slice lemma avoids this obstruction by reducing the ground-set dimension.

3. A single-valuation learner at the endpoint

Fix a desired error $\eta\in(0,1)$ and failure probability $\zeta\in(0,1)$.

3.1. Landmarks and two coverage properties

Put
$$ h=\frac{\eta}{16}. $$
Draw an independent landmark sample of size
$$ K=O\!\left(\frac1h\log\frac1{h\zeta}\right). $$
Sort it by the comparison oracle and retain representatives of its distinct value levels
$$ v_1<\cdots<v_q,\qquad q\leq K. $$
These numerical levels are analytical notation. The learner knows only their order and equality classes.

With probability at least $1-\zeta/2$, both of the following properties hold:

  • Every open gap between consecutive distinct landmark values, including the two tails, has probability at most $h$ under the distribution of $f(S)$.
  • Every bundle $S$ with $\mathcal D(S)\geq h$ occurs in the landmark sample.

Here are distribution-free proofs.

For the first property, let $V=f(S)$, and write
$$ F(v^-)=\Pr[V<v],\qquad F(v)=\Pr[V\leq v]. $$
Conditional on $V=v$, choose an auxiliary point uniformly in
$$ [F(v^-),F(v)]. $$
The resulting auxiliary points are independent uniform points in $[0,1]$. Any value gap containing no landmark corresponds to an interval containing no auxiliary point, whose length equals the gap’s probability mass. A grid of spacing $h/2$ and a union bound imply that all such empty intervals have length at most $h$ with failure probability at most $\zeta/4$, for the stated $K$.

For the second property, there are at most $1/h$ bundles with probability at least $h$. Each is missed with probability at most $e^{-Kh}$. A union bound gives failure probability at most $\zeta/4$, after increasing the constant in $K$.

These arguments allow arbitrary atoms and true-value ties.

3.2. A landmark interval inside most separated pairs

Condition on the first coverage property. Let
$$ x=f(S),\qquad y=f(S') $$
for independent test bundles.

Lemma. Except on a set of ordered-pair probability at most $3h$, every pair with $y>\beta x$ has landmark levels satisfying
$$ x\leq a<b\leq y,\qquad b>\beta a. $$

Proof. Let $a$ be the least landmark level at least $x$.

First, if no such $a$ exists, $x$ lies above the largest landmark. This has probability at most $h$.

Second, suppose $y\leq\beta a$. If $x$ is a landmark level, then $a=x$, contradicting $y>\beta x$. Otherwise $x$ lies in a landmark gap with upper endpoint $a$ and lower endpoint $c$, using $c=0$ for the initial gap. Necessarily
$$ \beta c<y\leq\beta a. $$
The intervals $(\beta c,\beta a]$ are disjoint across these gaps. Independence gives the bound
$$ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{\text{gaps}} \Pr[x\text{ lies in the gap}] \Pr[\beta c<y\leq\beta a]\\ &\hspace{2em}\leq h\sum_{\text{gaps}}\Pr[\beta c<y\leq\beta a] \leq h. \end{aligned} $$

Finally, suppose $y>\beta a$, but there is no landmark in $(\beta a,y]$. For each fixed $x$, all such $y$ lie in one landmark gap or the upper tail, of probability at most $h$. Averaging over $x$ gives another $h$.

Outside these three exceptions, choose a landmark $b\in(\beta a,y]$. $\square$

3.3. The finite classifier construction

Take a second sample, independent of the landmarks.

For each of the $2n$ slices $C$, and each pair of distinct landmark levels $a<b$, solve the following feasibility problem for an affine halfspace on the remaining $n-1$ coordinates:

  • Every second-sample bundle in $C$ with $f(S)\leq a$ must receive sign $-$.
  • Every second-sample bundle in $C$ with $f(S)\geq b$ must receive sign $+$.
  • All other bundles impose no constraint.

Membership in these tails is determined solely by comparisons with the landmark representatives.

Store exactly one feasible halfspace for each feasible slice–interval pair. This repairs the ambiguity identified by the verifier. There are at most $2nK^2$ stored halfspaces.

Separately within each slice, retain only intervals minimal under containment among the feasible intervals. Fix an order on the resulting finite list.

The prediction rule is:

  • Return equality on identical inputs.
  • If both inputs occurred among the landmarks, use their stored true order, breaking ties lexicographically.
  • Otherwise scan the retained list. A halfspace is applicable only if both inputs belong to its slice. The first applicable halfspace giving different signs chooses the bundle assigned $+$.
  • If none does, use lexicographic order.

This rule meets every output convention in the question.

3.4. Generalization from the second sample

For a fixed slice and landmark interval, define the tail-error set of a halfspace $u$ by
$$ E_u= \{S\in C:f(S)\leq a,\ u(S)=+\} \cup \{S\in C:f(S)\geq b,\ u(S)=-\}. $$
Conditional on the landmarks, these tails and the slice are fixed.

The class of sets $E_u$ has VC dimension at most $n$. Indeed, on the two tails, its membership labels are obtained from affine-halfspace labels on $n-1$ coordinates by fixed flips; outside those tails membership is always false. Such fixed flips preserve the number of label patterns.

Set
$$ \rho=\frac{\eta}{16nK^2}. $$
The consistent-class VC bound states that a class of VC dimension at most $d$ needs
$$ O\!\left( \frac{d\log(1/\rho)+\log(1/\xi)}{\rho} \right) $$
independent observations to ensure that every empirically consistent member has true error at most $\rho$, with failure probability at most $\xi$.

Apply this theorem conditional on the landmarks, with $d=n$ and
$$ \xi=\frac{\zeta}{4nK^2}. $$
Every stored feasible halfspace has zero empirical tail error. A union bound over at most $2nK^2$ slice–interval pairs therefore shows that a second sample of size
$$ M= O\!\left( \frac{n\log(1/\rho)+\log(4nK^2/\zeta)}{\rho} \right) = \widetilde O\!\left( \frac{n\bigl(n+\log(1/\zeta)\bigr)}{\eta^3} \right) $$
makes every stored halfspace’s tail error at most $\rho$, with probability at least $1-\zeta/2$.

Conditional on this event, the probability that either test bundle belongs to any stored halfspace’s tail-error set is at most
$$ 2(2nK^2)\rho=\frac{\eta}{4}. $$

3.5. Correctness on noncomplementary pairs

Consider a pair outside both the landmark exceptions and the tail-error event, satisfying
$$ x<y,\qquad y>\beta x. $$
Suppose the bundles are not complements.

They agree on membership of at least one item, so at least one slice contains both. The landmark lemma supplies an interval
$$ x\leq a<b\leq y,\qquad b>\beta a. $$
By the slice-separation lemma, this interval is feasible in every slice.

Choose one slice containing both test bundles. Since the feasible intervals form a finite family, there is a retained minimal feasible interval $[c,d]$ contained in $[a,b]$. Hence
$$ x\leq c<d\leq y. $$
Outside its tail-error set, its halfspace labels $S$ negative and $S'$ positive.

Furthermore, no applicable retained halfspace can separate them in the wrong direction. Suppose a halfspace belonging to interval $[e,\ell]$ labels $S$ positive and $S'$ negative. Correctness on its tails forces
$$ e<x,\qquad y<\ell. $$
Consequently,
$$ e<x\leq a<b\leq y<\ell. $$
Thus $[e,\ell]$ strictly contains the feasible interval $[a,b]$ in the same slice, contradicting its minimality.

There is therefore a correctly separating applicable halfspace, and every applicable separating halfspace chooses correctly. The prediction rule succeeds.

3.6. Complementary pairs are handled by memorization

Write
$$ \overline S=[n]\setminus S,\qquad p_S=\mathcal D(S). $$
On the second landmark coverage property, every bundle absent from the landmark sample has $p_S<h$.

If complementary test bundles both occurred among the landmarks, their order is known exactly. The probability that a complementary pair has an unmemorized endpoint is at most
$$ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{S\text{ absent}}p_Sp_{\overline S} + \sum_{S:\overline S\text{ absent}}p_Sp_{\overline S}\\ &\hspace{2em}\leq h\sum_{S\text{ absent}}p_{\overline S} + h\sum_{S:\overline S\text{ absent}}p_S \leq2h. \end{aligned} $$
The complement map is a bijection, which justifies the two bounds by $h$.

Combining the preceding arguments, the error on pairs with $x<y$ and $y>\beta x$ is at most
$$ 3h+2h+\frac{\eta}{4} = \frac{9\eta}{16} <\eta. $$
The two training-success events hold jointly with probability at least $1-\zeta$.

If $\alpha>\beta$, every pair eligible for $R_\alpha$ satisfies $y>\beta x$. For $x>0$, this follows from $y\geq\alpha x>\beta x$. For $x=0$, it follows from the strict inequality $y>x$.

We have proved
$$ M_1= \widetilde O\!\left( \frac{n\bigl(n+\log(1/\zeta)\bigr)}{\eta^3} \right) \qquad \text{for every }\alpha>\sqrt{n-1}. $$
In particular, this proves the exact $\alpha=\sqrt n$ endpoint.

3.7. Oracle legality and running time

All oracle calls compare bundles observed with this valuation. Two directional calls detect equality. Neither the slice baseline $f(B)$ nor any other cardinal value is queried or computed.

For a feasibility problem, write the affine score as $w\cdot\chi(S)+b$. Impose score at most $-1$ on negative sample points and at least $1$ on positive sample points. A strict separator on a finite sample can be scaled to satisfy these unit margins. The resulting linear program has rational coefficients of polynomial bit length.

There are polynomially many such programs, each of polynomial size. Rational linear programming supplies polynomial-size feasible solutions in polynomial time. Sorting, interval minimization, and prediction also take polynomial time.

3.8. The sharper bound above $\sqrt n$

For $\alpha>\sqrt n$, use the whole domain as one slice and set $\beta=\sqrt n$.

The approximation theorem directly separates every interval with $b>\beta a$. The landmark lemma and minimality proof apply unchanged, and there are no uncovered complementary pairs. There are now at most $K^2$ classifiers of VC dimension at most $n+1$.

Taking
$$ \rho=\frac{\eta}{16K^2} $$
gives the sharper single-valuation bound
$$ M_1= \widetilde O\!\left( \frac{n+\log(1/\zeta)}{\eta^3} \right). $$
This recovers the strict-separation upper bound from NOTES with an explicitly finite classifier list.

4. Transfer to an explicitly supported society

First,
$$ 1\leq A\leq\tau^{1/3}. $$
The lower bound follows from $\mu_t^{2/3}\geq\mu_t$. Jensen’s inequality gives
$$ \frac1\tau\sum_t\mu_t^{2/3} \leq \left(\frac1\tau\sum_t\mu_t\right)^{2/3} = \tau^{-2/3}, $$
proving the upper bound.

To make the allocation computable using rational arithmetic, choose rational approximations satisfying
$$ A\leq\widehat A\leq2A, \qquad \mu_t^{1/3}\leq b_t\leq2\mu_t^{1/3}. $$
They can be computed to constant relative precision in polynomial time in $L$, for example by rational bisection. The required precision has polynomial bit length because every mass is a positive binary rational.

Assign type $t$ the allowance
$$ \eta_t=\frac{\epsilon}{2\widehat A b_t}. $$
Ignore types with $\eta_t\geq1$, giving them lexicographic comparators. For active types, use the single-valuation learner with error $\eta_t$ and failure probability
$$ \zeta_t=\frac{\delta}{2\tau}. $$
On success, the weighted risk satisfies
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_t\mu_t R_\alpha(f_t,g_t) &\leq\sum_t\mu_t\min\{1,\eta_t\}\\ &\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\widehat A} \sum_t\frac{\mu_t}{b_t}\\ &\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\widehat A} \sum_t\mu_t^{2/3} \leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \end{aligned} $$

Let $B$ be a common bound consisting of
$$ n\bigl(n+\log(\tau/\delta)\bigr) $$
times sufficient logarithmic factors. Such a common bound exists because, for every active type,
$$ \eta_t\geq\frac{\epsilon}{8A} \geq\frac{\epsilon}{8\tau^{1/3}}. $$
The required number $K_t$ of observations for type $t$ obeys
$$ \begin{aligned} K_t &\leq CB\eta_t^{-3}\\ &=CB\left(\frac{2\widehat A b_t}{\epsilon}\right)^3\\ &\leq CB\left(\frac{4\widehat A}{\epsilon}\right)^3\mu_t. \end{aligned} $$

Take
$$ N= \left\lceil C'B\left(\frac{4\widehat A}{\epsilon}\right)^3 \right\rceil $$
total observations, for a sufficiently large absolute constant $C'$.

For an active type, $\eta_t<1$ and $b_t\leq2\mu_t^{1/3}$ imply
$$ \mu_t>\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4\widehat A}\right)^3. $$
Thus its count $N_t$ has binomial mean $N\mu_t$ exceeding both $2K_t$ and a sufficiently large multiple of $\log(2\tau/\delta)$. A Chernoff bound gives
$$ \Pr[N_t<K_t]\leq\frac{\delta}{2\tau}. $$
A union bound shows that all active types receive enough observations with failure probability at most $\delta/2$.

Conditional on the complete sequence of type identifiers, all associated bundles remain independent draws from $\mathcal D$. Selecting the first $K_t$ observations for each active type therefore supplies the required independent per-type samples. Another union bound bounds the learner failures by $\delta/2$.

Consequently, with probability at least $1-\delta$, society risk is at most $\epsilon$. Since $\widehat A\leq2A$,
$$ N= \widetilde O\!\left( \frac{n\bigl(n+\log(\tau/\delta)\bigr)A^3}{\epsilon^3} \right) $$
for $\alpha>\sqrt{n-1}$.

Using the sharper single-valuation learner gives
$$ N= \widetilde O\!\left( \frac{\bigl(n+\log(\tau/\delta)\bigr)A^3}{\epsilon^3} \right) $$
for $\alpha>\sqrt n$.

The arithmetic, sample processing, comparisons, and output representation all have polynomial running time in the parameters requested in the question.

5. Quantitative lower bounds

5.1. Established independently assignable matroid ranks

We use the following established construction. Along ground-set sizes $n=d^3$, let
$$ k=2^r,\qquad r\geq3,\qquad r=o(d). $$
For sufficiently large dimensions, there is a family $\mathcal A$ of $k$ bundles, each of size $d$, such that every assignment of the two ranks
$$ \ell=8r,\qquad d $$
to these bundles is realized by a matroid rank function. [Balcan–Harvey, Theorem 8](https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~harvey/Publications/LearningSubmodular/1008.2159v1.pdf).

Matroid ranks meet all valuation hypotheses: they are nonnegative, normalized, and monotone. For completeness, their submodularity follows by extending a basis of $X\cap Y$ to one of $X$, and then extending that basis to one of $X\cup Y$. The newly added elements lie in $Y\setminus X$; together with the original intersection basis, they form an independent subset of $Y$. Hence
$$ r(X\cup Y)-r(X)\leq r(Y)-r(X\cap Y), $$
which is the required submodular inequality.

Assume
$$ \ell<d,\qquad \alpha\leq\frac d\ell. $$
Let $\mathcal D$ be uniform on $\mathcal A$. Independently for every type and bundle, choose a fair low/high label, and choose a realizing matroid rank function for each type.

For the lower bound, grant the learner the cardinal labels of its observed bundles, knowledge of $\mathcal D$, and knowledge of the realizing family. These are additional information, so impossibility in this stronger observation model implies impossibility in the question’s model.

5.2. Unobserved labels force error

Fix a type $t$ and distinct bundles $B,C\in\mathcal A$. The probability that neither is observed with type $t$ in $N$ observations is
$$ \left(1-\frac{2\mu_t}{k}\right)^N. $$
Conditional on this event, the training information, and the learner’s randomness, their labels remain independent fair bits.

The comparator must choose one physical bundle as larger. With probability $1/4$, its chosen bundle has rank $\ell$ and the other has rank $d$. This is a strict, $\alpha$-separated error.

For this unordered pair, exactly one ordered presentation then contributes to the risk. Each ordered presentation has test probability $1/k^2$. Thus the expected contribution of the unordered pair, conditional on absence, is
$$ \frac1{4k^2}. $$
Summing over unordered pairs and types yields
$$ \mathbb E[R_\alpha]\geq \frac18\left(1-\frac1k\right) \sum_t\mu_t \left(1-\frac{2\mu_t}{k}\right)^N. $$
This holds for arbitrary randomized learners and unrestricted computation.

For uniform masses, if
$$ N\leq\frac{\tau k}{16}, $$
Bernoulli’s inequality gives
$$ \left(1-\frac{2}{\tau k}\right)^N \geq1-\frac{2N}{\tau k} \geq\frac78. $$
Since $k\geq8$,
$$ \mathbb E[R_\alpha]\geq\frac{49}{512}. $$

Averaging over the random hidden society fixes a deterministic society with at least this expected risk over training and learner randomness. For any risk variable bounded by $1/2$,
$$ \mathbb E[R_\alpha] \leq \epsilon+ \left(\frac12-\epsilon\right)\Pr[R_\alpha>\epsilon]. $$
Therefore, on that deterministic society,
$$ \Pr[R_\alpha>\epsilon] \geq \frac{49/512-\epsilon}{1/2-\epsilon}. $$
At $\epsilon=\delta=1/100$, this exceeds $\delta$. Thus the stated guarantee fails.

These uniform societies require $\Omega(\tau k)$ observations. For arbitrary masses, the same constant-risk conclusion follows whenever $N\leq k/16$, because $\mu_t\leq1$.

5.3. Superpolynomial necessity at the lower separation scale

Suppose
$$ \alpha(n)=o\!\left(\frac{n^{1/3}}{\log n}\right). $$
Along $n=d^3$, choose
$$ r= \left\lfloor \sqrt{\frac{d\log_2 n}{16\alpha(n)}} \right\rfloor, \qquad k=2^r. $$
The assumption gives
$$ \frac{d}{16\alpha(n)\log_2 n}\longrightarrow\infty. $$
Consequently,
$$ \frac{r}{\log_2 n}\longrightarrow\infty. $$
Also, using $\alpha(n)\geq1$,
$$ \frac rd \leq \sqrt{\frac{\log_2 n}{16d}} \longrightarrow0. $$
Finally, eventually
$$ r\leq\frac{d}{16\alpha(n)}, $$

$$ \frac d{8r}\geq2\alpha(n). $$
