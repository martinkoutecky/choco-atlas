The continuous mirror question, as graded

Here \(i\) dominates \(j\) if \(q_t(i)\geq q_t(j)\) for every \(t\in T\) and strictly for some \(t\), and \(s_\mu(i)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t q_t(i)^\lambda\).

For every fixed integer $\lambda\geq1$, the problem is polynomial-time solvable.

For a fixed noninteger $\lambda=a/b\geq1$ in lowest terms, polynomial-time solvability is equivalent to polynomial-time sign determination for signed sums of $b$-th roots. The reduction below strengthens the result in the notes: two items suffice, and the constructed society can be represented by a polynomial-size list of equally weighted users with dyadic ratings.

This does not settle the requested classification for noninteger exponents. In particular, $\lambda=3/2$ encounters the signed square-root-sum problem.

1. Computing the skyline and handling integer exponents

For every ordered pair of distinct items $i,j$, test whether
$$ q_t(i)\geq q_t(j)\quad\text{for every }t\in T, $$
and whether at least one inequality is strict. These are exact rational comparisons, performed by integer cross-multiplication. There are at most $m(m-1)\tau$ coordinate comparisons, each having polynomial bit complexity. Thus $\mathrm{US}_\mu$ is computable in polynomial time.

The skyline is nonempty. Indeed, choose an item maximizing the rational quantity
$$ \sum_{t\in T}q_t(i). $$
Any item dominating it would have a strictly larger sum, a contradiction.

If $k\geq|\mathrm{US}_\mu|$, returning the entire skyline solves the problem. This includes $m=1$. Comparing binary-encoded $k$ with the skyline cardinality takes polynomial time; no iteration proportional to the numerical value of $k$ is needed. Hence the interesting case is
$$ 1\leq k<|\mathrm{US}_\mu|. $$

Proposition 1. For every fixed integer $a\geq1$, selection with $\lambda=a$ is polynomial-time solvable.

Proof. Write a rating as $q_t(i)=p_{ti}/h_{ti}$, where $p_{ti}$ is a nonnegative integer and $h_{ti}$ is a positive integer. Then
$$ q_t(i)^a=\frac{p_{ti}^a}{h_{ti}^a}. $$
Because $a$ is fixed, these powers have bit lengths at most a constant times the original bit lengths. Multiplication by the rational mass preserves polynomial encoding length.

For each score, the product of its term denominators supplies a common denominator whose bit length is bounded by the sum of their bit lengths. The resulting numerator also has polynomial bit length: each scaled numerator has polynomial bit length, and adding $\tau$ such integers adds at most $O(\log\tau)$ further bits.

Compute the scores of all skyline items, sort them by exact rational comparison, and return the first $\min(k,|\mathrm{US}_\mu|)$ items. Equal scores may be ordered by item index. Every step has polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

2. The comparison problem for noninteger exponents

Fix $b\geq2$. Define $\mathrm{SIGN}_b$ to be the following function problem:

Given binary-encoded rational coefficients $c_1,\ldots,c_n$ and positive integers $d_1,\ldots,d_n$, determine the sign of
$$ R=\sum_{\ell=1}^n c_\ell d_\ell^{1/b}, $$
where all roots are positive real roots. Zero coefficients can be discarded.

We use one established theorem to distinguish equality from strict inequality.

Radical equality theorem. Given rational coefficients $C_\ell$, positive rational bases $A_\ell$, and rational exponents $X_\ell\in[0,1]$, whether
$$ \sum_\ell C_\ell A_\ell^{X_\ell}=0 $$
can be decided deterministically in polynomial time. Hunter, Bouyer, Markey, Ouaknine, and Worrell prove the stronger containment in uniform $\mathrm{TC}^0$. [Computing Rational Radical Sums in Uniform TC⁰, Theorem 1](https://people.mpi-sws.org/~joel/publications/fsttcs10.pdf).

For $\mathrm{SIGN}_b$, the substitutions $C_\ell=c_\ell$, $A_\ell=d_\ell>0$, and $X_\ell=1/b\in[0,1]$ satisfy every hypothesis. The theorem supplies an equality test, not a sign test.

Proposition 2. Selection for fixed $\lambda=a/b$, with $a,b$ coprime and $b\geq2$, has a polynomial-time oracle algorithm using $\mathrm{SIGN}_b$.

Proof. For a positive rating $q=p/h$,
$$ q^{a/b} = \frac{\bigl(p^a h^{a(b-1)}\bigr)^{1/b}}{h^a}. $$
Indeed, the right-hand side equals
$$ p^{a/b}h^{a(b-1)/b-a} = p^{a/b}h^{-a/b}. $$

Consequently, each difference $s_\mu(i)-s_\mu(j)$ is a signed sum of at most $2\tau$ positive $b$-th roots of integers, with rational coefficients. Zero ratings contribute nothing and are omitted.

Since $a,b$ are fixed, all constructed radicands and coefficients have polynomial bit length and are computable in polynomial time. Compute the skyline and sort its items using this oracle comparison. There are polynomially many oracle calls. $\square$

3. A finite-difference lemma for eliminating coefficient weights

The following elementary identity allows large integer coefficients to be encoded inside ratings without creating correspondingly many users.

Lemma 3. Fix an integer $a\geq2$. Every positive integer $h$ satisfies
$$ a!h = \sum_{j=0}^{a-1} (-1)^{a-1-j}\binom{a-1}{j} \bigl((h+j)^a-j^a\bigr). $$
Thus $a!h$ is a signed sum of at most $2^a$ positive integer $a$-th powers, counting repetitions. The bases have bit length $O(\log(h+1))$, with constants depending on $a$.

Proof. Define the forward-difference operator by
$$ (\Delta f)(x)=f(x+1)-f(x). $$
Induction on $r$ gives
$$ \Delta^r f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{r}(-1)^{r-j}\binom rj f(x+j). $$

If a polynomial has degree $d\geq1$ and leading coefficient $c$, its forward difference has degree $d-1$ and leading coefficient $cd$. This follows by expanding $(x+1)^d-x^d$.

Applying this fact $a-1$ times to $f(x)=x^a$ shows that
$$ \Delta^{a-1}f(x)=a!x+\beta $$
for some constant $\beta$. Subtracting its value at $0$ from its value at $h$ proves the identity.

Replace each binomial coefficient by that many repeated terms, and omit $0^a$. The number of terms is at most
$$ 2\sum_{j=0}^{a-1}\binom{a-1}{j}=2^a. $$
Every remaining base is between $1$ and $h+a-1$, proving the encoding bound. $\square$

4. Radical comparison reduces to two-item selection with equal user weights

Proposition 4. Fix a noninteger $\lambda=a/b\geq1$ in lowest terms. A polynomial-time selection solver yields a polynomial-time algorithm for $\mathrm{SIGN}_b$.

The selection instances need only have $m=2$, $k=1$, dyadic ratings and masses. Moreover, they admit an explicit representation by a polynomial-size list of equally weighted users.

Proof. Consider
$$ R=\sum_{\ell=1}^n c_\ell d_\ell^{1/b}. $$
Discard zero coefficients. An empty sum is handled directly, so assume $n\geq1$.

4.1. Converting the exponent

Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, choose
$$ u\in\{1,\ldots,b-1\}, \qquad au\equiv1\pmod b, $$
and define
$$ v=\frac{au-1}{b}. $$
Then $v$ is a nonnegative integer and
$$ au=bv+1. $$
These are constants depending only on $\lambda$.

Write $c_\ell=e_\ell/f_\ell$, with nonzero integer $e_\ell$ and positive integer $f_\ell$. Put
$$ D=\prod_{\ell=1}^n f_\ell d_\ell^v, \qquad h_\ell=\frac{Dc_\ell}{d_\ell^v}. $$
Every $h_\ell$ is a nonzero integer, because $D$ is divisible by $f_\ell d_\ell^v$. Furthermore,
$$ \sum_{\ell=1}^n h_\ell d_\ell^{v+1/b}=DR. $$

4.2. Replacing coefficients by a constant number of powers

Apply Lemma 3 to $|h_\ell|$ and multiply the resulting signs by $\operatorname{sign}(h_\ell)$. After expanding the fixed binomial multiplicities and discarding zero bases, obtain a list
$$ (\varepsilon_r,z_r,\ell_r), \qquad 1\leq r\leq N, $$
where $\varepsilon_r\in\{-1,1\}$ and $z_r$ is a positive integer, such that
$$ N\leq2^a n $$
and
$$ \sum_{r=1}^N \varepsilon_r z_r^a d_{\ell_r}^{v+1/b} = a!DR. $$
The list is nonempty, since the expansion for each nonzero $h_\ell$ represents a nonzero integer coefficient.

Choose a power of two $C$ satisfying
$$ C^b>\max_{1\leq r\leq N}z_r^b d_{\ell_r}^{u}, $$
and define the dyadic ratings
$$ \rho_r=\frac{z_r^b d_{\ell_r}^{u}}{C^b}. $$
Then $0<\rho_r<1$, and
$$ \rho_r^{a/b} = \frac{z_r^a d_{\ell_r}^{au/b}}{C^a} = \frac{z_r^a d_{\ell_r}^{v+1/b}}{C^a}. $$
Therefore,
$$ \sum_{r=1}^N\varepsilon_r\rho_r^{a/b} = \frac{a!D}{C^a}R. $$

4.3. Constructing normalized two-item profiles

Let the two items be $A,B$. Initially construct one user for each $r$:

  • If $\varepsilon_r=1$, give the user profile $(\rho_r,1)$.
  • If $\varepsilon_r=-1$, give the user profile $(1,\rho_r)$.

The first coordinate is the rating of $A$. Every profile has maximum rating $1$. Its contribution to the unweighted score difference is
$$ q_r(A)^{a/b}-q_r(B)^{a/b} = \varepsilon_r\bigl(\rho_r^{a/b}-1\bigr). $$

Put
$$ F=\sum_{r=1}^N\varepsilon_r. $$
The total difference from these users is consequently
$$ \frac{a!D}{C^a}R-F. $$

Cancel the rational term $-F$ as follows. If $F>0$, add $F$ users with profile $(1,0)$. If $F<0$, add $|F|$ users with profile $(0,1)$. If $F=0$, add none at this step. Their total contribution to the difference is $F$.

Next add one user of each profile $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$. Their contributions cancel. They also ensure that neither item dominates the other: the first profile witnesses $q(A)>q(B)$, and the second witnesses $q(B)>q(A)$.

The current number of users is
$$ B_0=N+|F|+2. $$
This is even. Indeed, $F\equiv N\pmod2$, because each $\varepsilon_r$ is congruent to $1$ modulo $2$, and $|F|\equiv F\pmod2$.

Let $M$ be the least power of two with $M\geq B_0$. Add
$$ \frac{M-B_0}{2} $$
further pairs of users with profiles $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$. This is a nonnegative integer, and these pairs contribute zero to the score difference.

Give all $M$ users weight $1/M$. Then
$$ s_\mu(A)-s_\mu(B) = \frac{a!D}{MC^a}R. $$
The scaling factor is positive.

4.4. Support validity, ties, and encoding length

Merge users with identical profiles. A profile appearing $w$ times becomes a support type of mass $w/M$. Thus the support types are distinct, every mass is positive and dyadic, and the masses sum to $1$. All ratings are dyadic and every profile remains normalized.

Merging preserves both scores and dominance: repeated identical coordinates impose precisely the same dominance conditions. The two anchor profiles remain present, so
$$ \mathrm{US}_\mu=\{A,B\}. $$

Use the radical equality theorem first. If $R=0$, output zero without calling selection. Otherwise, a valid selection answer with $k=1$ is uniquely determined: it selects $A$ exactly when $R>0$, and $B$ exactly when $R<0$. Arbitrary tie-breaking therefore causes no ambiguity.

Finally, the construction has polynomial size. The bit length of $D$ is bounded by the sum of the bit lengths of its factors. The integers $h_\ell$ and $z_r$ consequently have polynomial bit length. Fixed-degree powers and the chosen $C$ also have polynomial bit length.

Because $a$ is fixed,
$$ N=O_\lambda(n). $$
Moreover, $|F|\leq N$ and the least-power-of-two choice gives
$$ M<2B_0\leq4N+4. $$
Hence even the explicitly expanded equally weighted user list has polynomial size. Constructing and merging its profiles takes polynomial time. $\square$

5. Consequences and the square-root barrier

Propositions 2 and 4 establish
$$ \text{selection for fixed }\lambda=a/b \text{ is polynomial-time solvable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{SIGN}_b \text{ is polynomial-time solvable}. $$
The reverse implication from a selection solver holds already for the two-item instances in Proposition 4. Thus the difficulty persists without large item sets or exponentially compressed user populations.

For $b=2$, $\mathrm{SIGN}_2$ is signed square-root-sum comparison with rational coefficients. Multiplication by a positive common denominator converts the coefficients to integers, preserving the sign and using polynomial encoding length. Polynomial-time sign determination for these signed integer-coefficient sums is identified as open by Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer. [An Improved Bound on Sums of Square Roots via the Subspace Theorem](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54).

Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm for selection at $\lambda=3/2$ would provide a polynomial-time algorithm for that comparison problem.

Every even reduced denominator also inherits this barrier. For fixed even $b$,
$$ \sqrt d=\bigl(d^{b/2}\bigr)^{1/b}. $$
Replacing each square-root radicand accordingly gives a polynomial-time reduction from $\mathrm{SIGN}_2$ to $\mathrm{SIGN}_b$, because $b$ is fixed.

These are comparison reductions. They do not prove $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

The reported Sorted Lists-based Algorithm bound counts score computations and comparisons under its average-case assumptions. [Recommending Unanimously Preferred Items to Groups, Theorem 3.5](https://openproceedings.org/2023/conf/edbt/paper-24.pdf). It does not supply a polynomial bit-cost procedure for the exact comparisons above. In the constructed two-item instances, each user’s sorted rating list is immediate from the profile, so providing those lists does not remove the comparison barrier.

6. An exact algorithm for small support

Proposition 5. For every fixed rational $\lambda=a/b\geq1$, selection has an exact algorithm with running time
$$ b^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
In particular, it is polynomial-time solvable for bounded support and, more generally, for families satisfying $\tau=O(\log(2+L))$.

Proof. Integer exponents were handled in Proposition 1. Suppose $b\geq2$.

Using Proposition 2’s transformation and clearing coefficient denominators by a positive common denominator, a score difference has the same sign as
$$ \alpha=\sum_{\ell=1}^N C_\ell d_\ell^{1/b}, \qquad N\leq2\tau, $$
where the $C_\ell$ are nonzero integers and the $d_\ell$ are positive integers. All have polynomial bit length. An empty sum is zero.

Apply the radical equality theorem. If $\alpha=0$, the scores tie. Otherwise, define
$$ K=\mathbb Q(d_1^{1/b},\ldots,d_N^{1/b}), \qquad H=\sum_{\ell=1}^N|C_\ell|d_\ell. $$
Here $H\geq1$. Each adjunction has degree at most $b$, because its generator satisfies $x^b-d_\ell=0$. The tower law therefore gives
$$ [K:\mathbb Q]\leq b^N. $$

Each root is an algebraic integer, since it satisfies a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. Thus $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer. Since it is nonzero, its field norm is a nonzero integer: its minimal polynomial is monic with integer coefficients, and the field norm is a positive integer power of its signed constant coefficient.

For every embedding $\sigma:K\to\mathbb C$, the number $\sigma(d_\ell^{1/b})$ is a root of $x^b-d_\ell$. Therefore,
$$ |\sigma(d_\ell^{1/b})|=d_\ell^{1/b}\leq d_\ell, $$
and the triangle inequality yields $|\sigma(\alpha)|\leq H$.

The field has characteristic zero, so it is separable and its norm is the product over its embeddings. Separating out the identity embedding gives
$$ 1 \leq \left|\mathrm N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)\right| \leq |\alpha|H^{[K:\mathbb Q]-1}. $$
Consequently,
$$ |\alpha|\geq H^{-(b^N-1)}. $$

Put $W=\sum_\ell|C_\ell|$. Approximate each root with absolute error less than $2^{-P}$, choosing an integer $P$ satisfying
$$ P>(b^N-1)\log_2 H+\log_2(2W). $$
The weighted sum then has error less than
$$ W2^{-P}<\frac12H^{-(b^N-1)}\leq\frac12|\alpha|, $$
so its sign agrees with that of $\alpha$.

Each positive root can be approximated by rational bisection on $[0,d_\ell]$. To test a rational midpoint $x$, compare $x^b$ with $d_\ell$ exactly. Since $b$ is fixed, every such comparison has polynomial cost in the midpoint’s bit length. The number of bisection steps is $O(P+\log d_\ell)$.

The sufficient precision satisfies
$$ P=O\!\left(b^N\log(2H)+\log(2W)\right). $$
Thus each score comparison takes $b^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)$ time. Polynomially many comparisons suffice to sort the skyline, proving the bound.

If $\tau\leq c\log_2(2+L)$ for a fixed constant $c$, the exponential factor in $\tau$ is polynomial in $L$. $\square$
