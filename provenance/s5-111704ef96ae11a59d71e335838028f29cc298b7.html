The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set of applicant types \(T\), project capacities \(c_p\), regional quota rates \(q_r\), type preferences, and weak project priorities over types, choose assignment masses \(y_{t,p}\ge0\), boundary rationing, and project cutoffs \(d\) such that \(\sum_p y_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,p}\le c_p\), and \(\sum_{r}\sum_{p\in r}\sum_t y_{t,p}\le q_r\) as appropriate, with the assignment cutoff-induced, fair, and minimal under cutoff decreases; maximize \(\sum_{t,p}y_{t,p}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) projects, partitioned into nonempty regions \(R\), with nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(c_p\) and regional upper-quota rates \(q_r\). Each type strictly ranks its acceptable projects above being unmatched; projects have weak priorities over mutually acceptable types.
Represent type \(t\) by a nonatomic population \(\Omega_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\). At project \(p\), assign successive priority classes scores \(k_p,k_p-1,\ldots,1\). A cutoff \(d_p\in\{0,\ldots,k_p+1\}\) admits every mutually acceptable applicant scoring above \(d_p\), an arbitrary measurable subset scoring exactly \(d_p\), and nobody below it. Boundary subsets may be correlated across projects.
Each applicant takes her most preferred admitting project, or remains unmatched. Let \(y_{t,p}\) be the resulting mass; feasibility requires \(\sum_p y_{t,p}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{t,p}\le c_p\), and \(\sum_{p\in r}\sum_t y_{t,p}\le q_r\) for every \(r\in R\).
Fairness means that if positive mass of type \(t\) prefers \(p\) to its assignment, no positive mass assigned to \(p\) has strictly lower priority than \(t\); priority ties may be rationed.
Cutoff stability means feasibility and fairness, together with the following minimality: no single project's admission set can be enlarged by positive mass, retaining the cutoff form and all its previously admitted applicants, while every other admission set stays fixed, without making the induced assignment infeasible. This includes increasing boundary admission and lowering the score threshold; applicants switching projects release their previous assignments.
The optimization problem maximizes \(\sum_{t,p}y_{t,p}\). Its threshold version additionally takes a binary-rational \(K\in[0,1]\); let \(L\) denote total input bit length.

Is deciding whether a cutoff-stable assignment has total matched mass at least \(K\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|R|,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete maximum-cardinality cutoff-stable matching is NP-hard even with unit project capacities and disjoint regions containing at most two projects; finding some discrete cutoff-stable matching is polynomial-time [Aziz–Baychkov–Biró](https://www.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/summerintern_journal.pdf).
Continuum stable matching without regional quotas has a market-clearing cutoff characterization [Azevedo–Leshno](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/687476?download=true). No result settling the precise boundary-rationed regional threshold problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total applicant mass to one and interpreted capacities and regional quota rates as absolute upper bounds in that normalization.
  • Used a partition into nonempty regions, as in the discrete regional model, and replaced the malformed regional sum by a separate inequality for each region.
  • Retained strict applicant preferences, mutual acceptability, and the option of remaining unmatched; weak project priorities are retained from the graded question.
  • Replaced applicant-count-dependent scores by consecutive scores for project priority classes, with cutoffs admitting all, some, or none of a boundary class.
  • Made boundary rationing arbitrary measurable subsets of nonatomic type populations, allowing correlation across projects rather than imposing independent lotteries or a common tie-breaking order.
  • Extended fairness to weak priorities by prohibiting justified envy toward strictly lower-priority assigned mass while permitting rationing within ties.
  • Defined continuous cutoff decreases as positive-mass enlargements of one admission set, including boundary relaxation, holding other admission sets fixed and releasing assignments at previous projects.
  • Used the minimal-cutoff characterization as the solution concept; did not impose the conflicting literal requirement of ordinary non-wastefulness that remains in the checked full version.
  • Added a rational matched-mass threshold to state the exact optimization complexity question as a decision problem.

Result

The threshold problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when project priorities are strict over distinct types and every region contains at most two projects.

The key point is that, for appropriately scaled discrete instances, divisibility does not improve the optimum. Fair fractional allocations admit support-preserving integral rounding, and fair discrete matchings can be extended to cutoff stability without decreasing cardinality. Conversely, discrete stable cutoffs remain stable against arbitrary positive-mass enlargements.

1. The discrete starting problem

We use the following discrete decision problem. There are \(n\) applicants, strict applicant preferences, strict project priorities, unit project capacities, and a partition into regions of size at most two, each with upper quota one. Given an integer \(B\), decide whether a cutoff-stable matching has cardinality at least \(B\).

This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard by the size-preserving reduction underlying Theorem 6 of [Aziz–Baychkov–Biró](https://www.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/summerintern_journal.pdf). The strict-priority restriction follows from their construction in the proof of Theorem 2, part i): a strict preference list becomes a strict project priority list, while a two-applicant tie is represented by two separate projects, each accepting just one applicant. Their construction uses unit project capacities and unit regional quotas.

We describe admissions in this discrete problem as priority prefixes. If project \(p\) has \(k_p\) acceptable applicants, a prefix contains its first \(j_p\) applicants, where \(j_p\) is an integer between \(0\) and \(k_p\). Each applicant chooses her most preferred admitting project.

A feasible prefix profile is cutoff-stable when adding the next excluded applicant at any project makes the induced matching infeasible. With strict priorities, these prefix profiles are exactly the discrete cutoff profiles, after removing redundant numerical cutoff values.

2. Support-preserving rounding of fair allocations

We first establish a rounding lemma. Here all masses and capacities are measured in discrete units.

Lemma 1. Suppose each of \(n\) types has mass one, project capacities \(C_p\) and regional quotas \(Q_r\) are nonnegative integers, and regions partition the projects. Every feasible fair fractional allocation \(x\) admits a feasible fair discrete matching \(M\) satisfying
\[ |M|\geq \sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}. \]
Moreover, each applicant receives an option having positive mass in \(x\), with unmatched treated as an additional option.

Proof. Define the unmatched mass of type \(t\) by
\[ x_{t,\varnothing}=1-\sum_p x_{t,p}. \]

Construct a directed network with source \(s\), one vertex for each type, one vertex for each project, one vertex for each region, and sink \(z\). Its arcs are:

  • \(s\to t\), with capacity one;
  • \(t\to p\), with capacity one, exactly when \(x_{t,p}>0\);
  • \(t\to z\), with capacity one, exactly when \(x_{t,\varnothing}>0\);
  • \(p\to r(p)\), with capacity \(C_p\);
  • \(r\to z\), with capacity \(Q_r\).

The allocation \(x\), including its unmatched masses, defines a feasible flow of value \(n\). Put cost \(-1\) on every arc \(t\to p\), and cost zero on every other arc.

We use the integral minimum-cost-flow theorem: a feasible fixed-value flow problem with integer capacities and integer prescribed flow value has an integral optimum. Its hypotheses hold here: every capacity is an integer, the prescribed value is \(n\), and the feasible region is nonempty and bounded. This integrality follows from total unimodularity of the directed node–arc incidence matrix, together with integer capacity bounds.

Consequently, there is an integral flow of value \(n\) whose cost is at most that of the fractional flow. Every source–type arc is saturated, so each type sends its unit either to one project or directly to the sink. This defines a discrete matching \(M\). Project and regional bounds follow from the corresponding arc capacities, and comparison of costs gives
\[ -|M|\leq-\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}. \]

It remains to check fairness. Suppose, contrary to fairness of \(M\), that applicant \(t\) prefers \(p\) to her assigned option \(q=M(t)\), while applicant \(u\) assigned to \(p\) has strictly lower priority than \(t\).

By construction,
\[ x_{t,q}>0 \quad\text{and}\quad x_{u,p}>0, \]
where \(q\) may be unmatched. Thus positive mass of type \(t\) prefers \(p\) to its assignment, while positive mass of a strictly lower-priority type is assigned to \(p\). This contradicts fairness of \(x\). Therefore \(M\) is fair. \(\square\)

The restriction on unmatched arcs is essential: the rounding cannot leave an applicant unmatched unless the fractional allocation already leaves positive mass of that type unmatched.

3. Extending fair discrete matchings to stability

Lemma 2. With strict project priorities and disjoint regional upper quotas, every feasible fair discrete matching can be extended to a cutoff-stable discrete matching of at least the same cardinality.

Proof. Let \(M\) be feasible and fair.

For each nonempty project, admit the priority prefix ending with its lowest-priority assigned applicant. For each empty project, admit nobody.

These admissions induce exactly \(M\). Every assigned applicant is admitted to her assigned project. If applicant \(t\) were admitted to a project \(p\) preferred to \(M(t)\), then \(p\) would be nonempty. Let \(u\) be its lowest-priority assigned applicant. Since priorities are strict and \(t\) is not assigned to \(p\), membership in that prefix would give \(t\) strictly higher priority than \(u\), contradicting fairness.

Now repeatedly add the next excluded applicant at any project whenever the resulting induced matching is feasible. Each such step enlarges only that project's admission set. Every applicant's assignment therefore weakly improves: she either keeps her assignment or switches to the newly admitting project. In particular, no matched applicant becomes unmatched.

There are at most
\[ \sum_p k_p \]
prefix additions, so this process terminates. At termination, no immediate prefix addition is feasible.

For completeness, an infeasible immediate addition cannot become feasible by extending the same prefix further. When only admissions at \(p\) increase:

  • the number assigned to \(p\) cannot decrease;
  • the number assigned within \(r(p)\) cannot decrease;
  • loads at other projects and other regions cannot increase.

Thus any violated project capacity or destination-region quota remains violated under a further extension. The terminal profile is therefore cutoff-stable, and its cardinality is at least \(|M|\). \(\square\)

4. Discrete stable cutoffs withstand fractional enlargement

The next lemma checks the precise minimality required in the question.

Lemma 3. Consider a discrete cutoff-stable priority-prefix profile with integer project capacities and integer regional quotas. Replace each applicant by a nonatomic population of mass one, preserving preferences and priorities. Admit each population either wholly or not at all, according to the discrete prefix. These admissions constitute a cutoff-stable nonatomic assignment.

Proof. Feasibility and fairness follow immediately from the discrete matching.

Fix a project \(p\). If its prefix contains every acceptable applicant, its admission set cannot be enlarged.

Otherwise, let \(a\) be its highest-priority excluded applicant. The discrete profile is stable, so extending the prefix to include \(a\) is infeasible.

Applicant \(a\) must prefer \(p\) to her current assignment. Otherwise, adding her to the admission set would leave the induced matching unchanged and hence feasible.

Let \(q\) denote her current assignment, possibly unmatched. Adding \(a\) changes the discrete project load at \(p\) by one. It changes the load of \(r(p)\) by zero if \(q\) belongs to \(r(p)\), and by one otherwise. Loads at other projects and regions can only decrease.

Because the bounds and existing loads are integers, infeasibility implies at least one of the following:

  • project \(p\) is already at capacity;
  • region \(r(p)\) is already at its quota, and \(a\) is unmatched or assigned outside \(r(p)\).

Represent the original prefix in the nonatomic model by setting
\[ d_p=s_p(a) \]
and admitting no boundary applicants. Exactly the types scoring above \(s_p(a)\) are admitted. When the prefix contains every acceptable type, use \(d_p=0\).

Consider any positive-mass enlargement of this admission set that retains cutoff form and all previously admitted applicants. It must admit positive mass of type \(a\):

  • if it adds applicants at score \(s_p(a)\), they belong to type \(a\), since priorities over distinct types are strict;
  • if it adds applicants at a lower score, its new threshold must lie below \(s_p(a)\), forcing admission of the entire population of type \(a\).

Every newly admitted applicant of type \(a\) switches to \(p\). Every other applicant either keeps her assignment or also switches to \(p\). Consequently, the load at \(p\) cannot decrease, and the load within \(r(p)\) cannot decrease.

In case 1, the positive mass of type \(a\) switching to \(p\) violates its capacity. In case 2, that mass comes from outside the region or from unmatched applicants and violates the regional quota. Switches originating within the region contribute zero to its total load and cannot offset this increase.

Thus every positive-mass enlargement is infeasible. This argument permits arbitrary measurable boundary subsets and arbitrary correlations across projects, since all other admission sets remain fixed. \(\square\)

Uniformly scaling all populations and bounds by the same positive factor preserves this conclusion.

5. The reduction

Take an instance of the discrete decision problem from Section 1, with \(n\) applicants and threshold \(B\). Choose the least power of two \(D\) satisfying \(D\geq n\).

Construct the nonatomic instance as follows:

  • Replace each original applicant \(t\) by a type of mass

\[ \mu_t=\frac1D. \]

  • Preserve all original preferences and project priorities.
  • Give every project capacity \(1/D\) and every region quota \(1/D\).
  • If \(D>n\), add one type of mass

\[ \frac{D-n}{D} \]
with no acceptable projects.

  • Set

\[ K=\frac BD. \]

All masses are positive, sum to one, and are dyadic rationals. The support size is at most \(n+1\), and every region still has at most two projects.

Forward implication

Suppose the discrete instance has a cutoff-stable matching of cardinality at least \(B\).

Use its priority-prefix admissions on whole nonatomic type populations. Lemma 3, followed by scaling by \(1/D\), proves stability under the exact positive-mass minimality in the question. Its matched mass is at least
\[ \frac BD=K. \]

Reverse implication

Suppose the constructed nonatomic instance has a cutoff-stable assignment \(y\) of matched mass at least \(B/D\). The added type, if present, is necessarily unmatched.

For every original type, set
\[ x_{t,p}=D y_{t,p}. \]
The allocation \(x\) has unit type masses, unit project capacities, and unit regional quotas. It is feasible and fair, and
\[ \sum_{t,p}x_{t,p} = D\sum_{t,p}y_{t,p} \geq B. \]

Lemma 1 produces a feasible fair discrete matching of cardinality at least \(B\). Lemma 2 extends it to a discrete cutoff-stable matching without decreasing cardinality.

Hence
\[ \text{discrete stable cardinality at least }B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{nonatomic stable matched mass at least }\frac BD. \]

Indeed, the same argument proves the exact equality
\[ \max_{\text{nonatomic cutoff-stable }y} \sum_{t,p}y_{t,p} = \frac1D \max_{\text{discrete cutoff-stable }M}|M| \]
for these constructed instances.

Encoding size

Since \(D<2n\), its binary representation has length proportional to \(\log n\). The construction adds at most one type, preserves the preference and priority lists, and introduces only binary-rational numbers of polynomial encoding length. It is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

Conclusion

Deciding whether a cutoff-stable assignment has matched mass at least \(K\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The hardness survives arbitrary correlated boundary rationing and holds with strict priorities over distinct types and disjoint regions containing at most two projects. A running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|R|,L\) would consequently imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
