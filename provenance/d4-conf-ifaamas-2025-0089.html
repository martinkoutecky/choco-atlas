1. Conclusions and representation convention

The convergence and optimization guarantee is false. The non-strict weight bound permits a two-cycle. A strict bound gives convergence, but the limit can select a return-suboptimal greedy policy, violate efficiency, or give efficient credits that do not minimize excess.

The complexity answer depends on how transition pieces are listed:

  • Separate scalar-coordinate lists: if each map $x\mapsto P(s'\mid s,x)$ has its own piece list, computing the requested approximation is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, even with $m=\tau=1$, $\gamma=1/2$, and $\varepsilon=1/8$. Exact output is computable in $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ and in deterministic exponential time.
  • An explicit common transition-vector list: if each state has one supplied piece list on which the entire vector $P(\cdot\mid s,x)$ is affine, exact policy and credit optimization is polynomial-time solvable.

I use the first convention for the main complexity classification, since the task writes the transition maps as scalar functions $P(s'\mid s,x)$. I also prove the second classification, so neither reading is left unsettled.

All computational statements use binary rational input and output and concern inputs satisfying the stated validity assumptions. The algorithms below produce exact rational solutions, which in particular satisfy every positive requested $\varepsilon$.

The cited paper states its analogous convergence claim in [Theorem 3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.00372v1). None of the arguments below relies on that claim.

2. The operator: strict contraction, but failure at equality

Let $\mathcal B$ be the vector space of all bounded credit functions. Write
$$ M=\sum_t\mu_t>0, \qquad \|Q\|_\mu = \max_{s\in S}\sum_t\mu_t\sup_{x\in D}|Q_t(s,x)|. $$
If $\|Q\|_\infty$ denotes the supremum over all $t,s,x$, then
$$ \left(\min_t\mu_t\right)\|Q\|_\infty \leq \|Q\|_\mu \leq M\|Q\|_\infty. $$
Indeed, the upper bound follows by bounding every coordinate by $\|Q\|_\infty$, and the lower bound follows by retaining a coordinate whose supremum approaches $\|Q\|_\infty$. Because there are finitely many types and every $\mu_t$ is positive, these norms are equivalent. Thus $\mathcal B$ is complete in $\|\cdot\|_\mu$.

For bounded functions $f,g$ on the same nonempty domain,
$$ |\sup f-\sup g|\leq\sup|f-g|. $$
Consequently,
$$ |M_Q(s)-M_{\widetilde Q}(s)| \leq \|Q-\widetilde Q\|_\mu, $$
and, since $0\leq\alpha_t\leq\mu_t$,
$$ \begin{aligned} |E_Q(s,x)-E_{\widetilde Q}(s,x)| &\leq \sup_{\alpha\in[0,\mu]} \left|\sum_t\alpha_t(Q_t-\widetilde Q_t)(s,x)\right|\\ &\leq \sum_t\mu_t|Q_t(s,x)-\widetilde Q_t(s,x)|\\ &\leq \|Q-\widetilde Q\|_\mu. \end{aligned} $$

Define
$$ W=\max_s\sum_t\mu_t\sup_{x\in D}w_t(s,x). $$
Nonnegative weights and probability normalization now imply
$$ \|\mathcal HQ-\mathcal H\widetilde Q\|_\mu \leq (\gamma+\lambda)W\|Q-\widetilde Q\|_\mu. $$

The operator maps $\mathcal B$ into itself. To see this, $D$ and $[0,\mu]$ are compact, so the continuous input functions $R,U,w_t$ are bounded; boundedness of $Q$ then bounds both suprema appearing in $\mathcal H$.

Therefore, if
$$ c=(\gamma+\lambda)W<1, $$
the contraction theorem gives a unique bounded fixed point $Q^*$ and
$$ \|\mathcal H^kQ^{(0)}-Q^*\|_\mu \leq c^k\|Q^{(0)}-Q^*\|_\mu. $$
Norm equivalence gives convergence in the supremum norm.

Under the stated non-strict bound, this argument proves only nonexpansiveness. The following example shows that convergence can fail.

A two-cycle for every permitted value of $\lambda$

Take one type of mass $1$, one action, and two states. Thus $D$ is a singleton. Let transitions deterministically swap the states, and set
$$ R(s_i,x)=-\lambda, \qquad U(s_i,x,\alpha)=\alpha, \qquad w_1(s_i,x)=\frac{1}{\gamma+\lambda}. $$
These are rational affine functions satisfying all assumptions, including the weight bound with equality.

Represent a credit function by $q=(q_1,q_2)$. Whenever $q_i<1$,
$$ E_Q(s_i,x) = \max_{0\leq\alpha\leq1}\alpha(1-q_i) = 1-q_i. $$
Hence
$$ (\mathcal Hq)_i = \frac{\gamma q_{3-i}-\lambda q_i}{\gamma+\lambda}. $$
For
$$ q^{(0)}=(1/2,-1/2), $$
both coordinates remain below $1$, and direct substitution gives
$$ \mathcal Hq^{(0)}=-q^{(0)}, \qquad \mathcal H^2q^{(0)}=q^{(0)}. $$
Thus iteration need not converge under the stated bound, including when $\lambda=0$.

3. Convergence does not establish the optimization objectives

The bilinear example in the notes is inadmissible: $10x\alpha$ is not jointly piecewise affine on a finite polyhedral cover. The following replacement satisfies the required representation exactly.

A strictly convergent operator with a return-suboptimal greedy policy

Take one state, one type of mass $1$, and two actions assigned to the same subtask. Let $x\in[0,1]$ denote the mass on the second action. Set
$$ P(s\mid s,x)=1, \qquad R(s,x)=1-x, \qquad \gamma=\frac12, \qquad \lambda=\frac14, \qquad w_1(s,x)=1. $$
Define
$$ U(s,x,\alpha)=10\min\{x,\alpha\}. $$
Its explicit jointly affine pieces are
$$ U(s,x,\alpha)= \begin{cases} 10\alpha,&\alpha\leq x,\\ 10x,&x\leq\alpha. \end{cases} $$
The pieces agree on their intersection. The function is continuous, nonnegative, and zero when $\alpha=0$.

Here $c=3/4<1$, so the operator has a unique bounded fixed point. I claim that it is
$$ Q^*(s,x) = \frac{\frac83+\frac32x}{1+\frac14x}. $$
Its derivative is
$$ \frac{d}{dx}Q^*(s,x) = \frac{5/6}{(1+x/4)^2}>0, $$
and its endpoint values are $8/3$ and $10/3$. In particular,
$$ M_{Q^*}(s)=\frac{10}{3}, \qquad 0<Q^*(s,x)<10. $$

For a credit $q$ with $0<q<10$, the excess expression is increasing in $\alpha$ on $[0,x]$ and decreasing on $[x,1]$:
$$ 10\min\{x,\alpha\}-q\alpha = \begin{cases} (10-q)\alpha,&\alpha\leq x,\\ 10x-q\alpha,&\alpha\geq x. \end{cases} $$
Its maximum is therefore attained at $\alpha=x$, giving
$$ E_{Q^*}(s,x)=x(10-Q^*(s,x)). $$
Substitution yields
$$ \begin{aligned} (\mathcal HQ^*)(s,x) &= 1-x+\frac12\frac{10}{3} +\frac14x(10-Q^*(s,x))\\ &= \frac83+\frac32x-\frac14xQ^*(s,x)\\ &= Q^*(s,x), \end{aligned} $$
where the last equality is precisely the defining formula for $Q^*$. Uniqueness follows from contraction.

The unique action maximizing aggregate fixed-point credit is $x=1$. Its policy value is
$$ V^{x=1}(s)=0. $$
The return-optimal action is $x=0$, with value
$$ V^{x=0}(s)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}=2. $$
Moreover, at the greedy action the fixed-point credit is $10/3$, while efficiency requires the sole type’s credit to equal $0$.

Thus strict convergence, positive weights, and even
$$ \sum_t\mu_tw_t(s,x)=1 $$
do not ensure return-optimal greedy selection or efficient fixed-point credits.

Efficient fixed-point credits can fail to minimize excess, for every $\lambda$

Fix any rational $\lambda\geq0$. Take one state, two types of mass $1$ each, and one action per type. Let
$$ P(s\mid s)=1, \qquad R(s)=1, \qquad \gamma=\frac12, \qquad U(s,x,\alpha)=3\alpha_2. $$
There is a unique policy, of value $2$.

Put
$$ b=2+\frac52\lambda, \qquad w_1=\frac{3}{2b}, \qquad w_2=\frac{1}{2b}. $$
These weights are positive and satisfy a strict contraction bound:
$$ (\gamma+\lambda)(w_1+w_2) = \frac{1+2\lambda}{2+\frac52\lambda} <1. $$

Consider
$$ q^*=(3/2,1/2). $$
Its aggregate credit is $2$, so it is efficient. Its excess is
$$ E(q^*) = \max_{\alpha\in[0,1]^2} \left(3\alpha_2-\frac32\alpha_1-\frac12\alpha_2\right) = \frac52. $$
The scalar expression multiplied by the weights is
$$ 1+\frac12\cdot2+\lambda\frac52=b. $$
Hence $\mathcal Hq^*=q^*$, and contraction makes it the unique fixed point.

For every efficient credit vector, the grand coalition gives the lower bound
$$ E(q)\geq U(s,x,(1,1))-(q_1+q_2)=3-2=1. $$
The efficient credits $(0,2)$ attain this bound:
$$ E(0,2)=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]^2}\alpha_2=1. $$
Thus the fixed-point credits have excess $5/2$, whereas the minimum is $1$.

This proves failure of the credit objective even when convergence, efficiency, and return optimality all hold.

4. Existence of a simultaneously return-optimal policy

Write
$$ N=\sum_t|A_t|, \qquad d=N-\tau. $$
Because every supported mass is positive and every action set is nonempty, $D$ is a nonempty compact product of simplices, of affine dimension $d$.

Define the ordinary return Bellman operator on $\mathbb R^S$:
$$ (Tv)(s) = \max_{x\in D} \left\{ R(s,x)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,x)v(s') \right\}. $$
Continuity and compactness give an attained maximum. Probability normalization gives
$$ \|Tv-Tu\|_\infty\leq\gamma\|v-u\|_\infty. $$
Therefore $T$ has a unique fixed point $V^*$.

Choose a maximizing profile $\pi^*(s)$ at each state. Then
$$ V^*=R^{\pi^*}+\gamma P^{\pi^*}V^*. $$
For any stochastic matrix $P^\pi$, the inverse
$$ (I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1} = \sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\gamma^r(P^\pi)^r $$
exists and is entrywise nonnegative. Thus the displayed equation implies $V^{\pi^*}=V^*$.

For any other stationary deterministic policy $\pi$, the Bellman fixed-point equation implies
$$ V^*\geq R^\pi+\gamma P^\pi V^*. $$
Subtracting the policy-evaluation equation and multiplying by the nonnegative inverse gives
$$ V^*-V^\pi = (I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1} \left((I-\gamma P^\pi)V^*-R^\pi\right) \geq0. $$
Hence $\pi^*$ is return-optimal from every state. Signed rewards and arbitrary total mass cause no difficulty.

5. Minimum-excess credits for a fixed rational policy

The only algorithmic facts about linear programming used below are strong duality and polynomial-time exact solution of rational linear programs. These standard facts are stated in the [linear-programming lecture notes](https://theory.stanford.edu/~jvondrak/CS369P/lec1.pdf).

Fix a rational policy $\pi$ and a state $s$. Policy evaluation computes $V^\pi$ by solving
$$ (I-\gamma P^\pi)V^\pi=R^\pi. $$
The inverse exists by the argument above. Rational linear algebra computes the value exactly in polynomial time.

Set $x=\pi(s)$ and $V=V^\pi(s)$. Slice every listed piece of $U$ at this fixed $x$ and intersect it with $[0,\mu]$. Discard empty slices using linear-programming feasibility tests. Each remaining slice has a rational description
$$ F_j=\{\alpha:B_j\alpha\leq d_j\}, \qquad U(s,x,\alpha)=b_j^\top\alpha+c_j. $$
Each $F_j$ is nonempty and compact because the coalition box has been included.

On this slice, maximum excess equals
$$ c_j+\max_{\alpha\in F_j}(b_j-q)^\top\alpha. $$
The inner linear program has a finite attained optimum. Strong duality therefore says that its value is
$$ \min\left\{ d_j^\top z_j: z_j\geq0,\ B_j^\top z_j=b_j-q \right\}, $$
with an attained dual optimum.

It follows that the slice’s maximum excess is at most $e$ exactly when there exists $z_j$ satisfying
$$ z_j\geq0, \qquad B_j^\top z_j=b_j-q, \qquad d_j^\top z_j+c_j\leq e. $$

Thus minimum-excess credits are obtained from the rational linear program
$$ \min e $$
subject to the preceding constraints for every nonempty slice, and
$$ \sum_t\mu_tq_t=V, \qquad e\geq0. $$
The variables $q_t$ are unrestricted in sign, as required.

To check feasibility, choose $q_t=V/M$ for all types. This is efficient, and compactness makes its maximum excess finite. Taking $e$ at least that excess and using dual attainment supplies feasible $z_j$. The objective is bounded below by $0$, since the empty coalition has worth $0$ and contributes excess $0$.

The linear program consequently has an attained rational optimum. Its description length is polynomial in the original input and the policy’s encoding length. Therefore:

For a fixed rational policy, exact minimum-excess credits are polynomial-time computable, independently at every state.

This argument minimizes only the maximum excess; it introduces no lexicographic refinement.

6. Approximation hardness under separate-coordinate encoding

I give a polynomial-size reduction from satisfiability of a three-literal conjunctive formula with $n\geq1$ variables and $k\geq1$ clauses.

Action profiles encode the entire Boolean cube

Use one type of mass $1$ and $n+1$ actions, all assigned to one subtask:
$$ D=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^{n+1}_{\geq0}: \sum_{i=0}^{n}x_i=1\right\}. $$
Define
$$ y_i(x)=\min\{1,nx_i\}, \qquad 1\leq i\leq n. $$
The map $x\mapsto y(x)$ is onto $[0,1]^n$. Indeed, for any $y$ in that cube, choose
$$ x_i=\frac{y_i}{n}\quad(i\geq1), \qquad x_0=1-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ny_i. $$
These masses are nonnegative, sum to $1$, and yield the prescribed $y$.

For a positive literal use $y_i$, and for a negative literal use $1-y_i$. For clause $j$, let
$$ f_j(x)= \max\{\ell_{j1}(y(x)),\ell_{j2}(y(x)),\ell_{j3}(y(x))\}. $$
Each $f_j$ is continuous and lies in $[0,1]$.

Each clause function has an explicit constant-size piecewise-affine description: choose one of the two clipping regimes for each of its three literal occurrences, and then choose a largest literal. This yields at most
$$ 2^3\cdot3=24 $$
rational polyhedral pieces. On each piece, the selected literal is affine in $x$. Ties give agreeing formulas, and inconsistent regimes may be discarded. The description of $1-f_j$ has the same bound.

The discounted system evaluates satisfied clauses

Use states
$$ S=\{s_0\}\cup\{g_j,b_j:1\leq j\leq k\}. $$
Every $g_j$ and $b_j$ is absorbing. Set
$$ R(s_0,x)=0, \qquad R(g_j,x)=\frac{k}{2}, \qquad R(b_j,x)=0, \qquad \gamma=\frac12. $$
At the initial state, define
$$ P(g_j\mid s_0,x)=\frac{f_j(x)}{k}, \qquad P(b_j\mid s_0,x)=\frac{1-f_j(x)}{k}. $$
These probabilities are nonnegative and sum to $1$. Each scalar coordinate has at most $24$ pieces.

The absorbing-state values are $k$ and $0$. Therefore any policy selecting $x$ at $s_0$ has
$$ V^\pi(s_0)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^kf_j(x). $$

Set $U=0$. For any fixed rational $\lambda\geq0$, one can take the positive constant weight
$$ w_1=\frac{1}{2(\gamma+\lambda)}, $$
which even satisfies the strict operator bound. The weights have no role in the policy-and-credit optimization objective.

The return gap

Define on the cube
$$ F(y)= \sum_{j=1}^k \max\{\ell_{j1}(y),\ell_{j2}(y),\ell_{j3}(y)\}. $$
Each summand is a maximum of affine functions and is therefore convex. Hence $F$ is convex.

Every cube point is a convex combination of Boolean vertices. Convexity consequently gives
$$ F(y)\leq\max_{z\in\{0,1\}^n}F(z). $$
At a Boolean vertex, each clause term is $1$ exactly when that clause is satisfied, and otherwise is $0$. Thus $F(z)$ counts satisfied clauses.

Using surjectivity of $x\mapsto y(x)$, we obtain
$$ V^*(s_0)= \begin{cases} k/2,&\text{if the formula is satisfiable},\\ \text{at most }(k-1)/2,&\text{if it is unsatisfiable}. \end{cases} $$

Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm returned the requested approximation with $\varepsilon=1/8$. On satisfiable instances, its policy would satisfy
$$ V^\pi(s_0)\geq k/2-1/8. $$
On unsatisfiable instances, every policy satisfies
$$ V^\pi(s_0)\leq k/2-1/2. $$
Evaluating the returned rational policy and comparing with
$$ k/2-1/4 $$
would decide satisfiability.

The credit requirement creates no obstruction: all policy values in this construction are nonnegative; the sole type’s efficient credit is its policy value, and $U=0$ then gives excess $0$, its minimum.

The construction has polynomial encoding length and parameters
$$ m=\tau=1, \qquad |S|=2k+1, \qquad N=n+1, \qquad \gamma=\frac12, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac18. $$
Therefore the approximation problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under separate-coordinate encoding.

7. Exact algorithms and deterministic running-time bounds

A finite sufficient set of rational profiles

Eliminate one action coordinate per type using the mass equalities. This identifies the affine hull of $D$ with $\mathbb R^d$.

For each state, intersect $D$ with one reward piece and one piece from each transition-coordinate list. The nonempty intersections form a finite cover by compact rational polytopes. On each such cell, the Bellman objective is affine for every fixed $v$.

An affine function on a nonempty compact polytope attains its maximum at a vertex. Thus cell vertices suffice for every Bellman maximization.

Let $H$ be the total number of inequality rows in the reward and transition piece descriptions and in $D$, over all states, after coordinate elimination. Represent any equalities by pairs of inequalities.

Every cell vertex has $d$ linearly independent active rows. Indeed, if its active rows had rank less than $d$, there would be a nonzero direction annihilating them. A sufficiently small displacement in either direction would preserve every inactive strict inequality as well, contradicting extremality.

Enumerate all choices of $d$ rows from this global pool. For each independent choice, solve the corresponding equalities and retain the solution if it belongs to $D$. The resulting set $Z$ has at most $H^d$ elements and contains every relevant cell vertex. Additional retained profiles are feasible actions, so they cannot increase the optimum beyond the original action domain.

When $d=0$, $D$ itself is a singleton and no enumeration is needed.

Every candidate has polynomial bit length. After elimination, all coefficients have polynomial bit length. Clearing denominators in a selected system and applying Cramer’s rule expresses each coordinate as a ratio of determinants of dimension $d$; the determinant bounds give polynomial bit length in $L+N$.

A finite-action Bellman linear program

For every $v$, the Bellman maximum over $D$ equals its maximum over $Z$. Consider
$$ \min_v\sum_sv(s) $$
subject to
$$ v(s)\geq R(s,z)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,z)v(s') \qquad (s\in S,\ z\in Z). $$

These constraints say $v\geq Tv$. The operator $T$ is monotone because transition probabilities are nonnegative. Iteration therefore gives
$$ v\geq Tv\geq T^2v\geq\cdots. $$
Contraction implies $T^kv\to V^*$, so every feasible $v$ satisfies $v\geq V^*$.

Conversely, $V^*=TV^*$ is feasible. Thus $V^*$ is the unique minimizing value vector. Choosing a maximizing candidate at each state gives an exact return-optimal rational policy. Section 5 then computes its exact minimum-excess credits.

The resulting deterministic bound is
$$ (H+1)^{O(d)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|S|,N,L). $$
Since the explicit row count is bounded by a polynomial in the input size, a coarser bound is
$$ (L+N+|S|+1)^{O(N-\tau)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|S|,N,L). $$

An alternative bound using cell enumeration

At each state, explicitly enumerate the intersections of one reward piece and one piece from each of its $|S|$ transition-coordinate lists. Their number is at most
$$ (L+1)^{|S|+1}. $$
The construction in Section 9 solves the problem by a linear program polynomial in this expanded description size. This gives another bound:
$$ (L+1)^{O(|S|+1)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|S|,N,L). $$

One may use whichever algorithm has the smaller bound. In particular, either fixed action dimension $N-\tau$ or fixed state count $|S|$ gives polynomial time under separate-coordinate encoding.

8. Decision complexity and an $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ upper bound

The return-threshold problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete

Consider the decision problem
$$ V^*(s_0)\geq b $$
for an input rational threshold $b$.

A certificate specifies a candidate profile at each state by the indices of its $d$ independent active rows. The verifier solves these systems, checks feasibility in $D$, evaluates rewards and transitions, and solves the rational policy-evaluation system.

All these operations are polynomial-time, and the certificate has polynomial length. An optimal candidate policy exists by Section 7. Hence the decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

The reduction in Section 6, with threshold $b=k/2$, proves $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. Thus the return-threshold problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete on the specified valid-input class.

Exact output is computable in $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$

For every candidate policy, the action coordinates have polynomial bit length. Its value is obtained by solving an $|S|$-dimensional rational linear system, so determinant bounds give polynomial bit length for every value coordinate and for
$$ J(\pi)=\sum_sV^\pi(s). $$
Consequently, there is a computable polynomial bound $B$ such that every candidate-policy sum has numerator and positive denominator of bit length at most $B$.

Distinct such sums differ by at least
$$ 2^{-2B}. $$
Indeed, the difference of two distinct rational numbers with denominators at most $2^B$ has a nonzero integer numerator over a denominator at most $2^{2B}$.

The query
$$ \text{“Does a candidate policy satisfy }J(\pi)\geq a\text{?”} $$
belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ by the preceding verifier.

9. Polynomial time with an explicit common transition-vector list

$$ C_{sj}=\{x:A_{sj}x\leq b_{sj}\}, $$
including all domain constraints, and
$$ P(\cdot\mid s,x)=p_{sj}+B_{sj}x, \qquad R(s,x)=r_{sj}+a_{sj}^\top x. $$
The cell is nonempty and compact. Its Bellman inequality is
$$ v(s)\geq r_{sj}+\gamma p_{sj}^\top v + \max_{x\in C_{sj}} \left(a_{sj}+\gamma B_{sj}^\top v\right)^\top x. $$
For each fixed $v$, the inner linear program has a finite attained optimum. Strong duality makes this inequality equivalent to existence of $z_{sj}$ satisfying
$$ z_{sj}\geq0, $$
$$ A_{sj}^\top z_{sj} = a_{sj}+\gamma B_{sj}^\top v, $$
$$ b_{sj}^\top z_{sj} +r_{sj}+\gamma p_{sj}^\top v \leq v(s). $$
These are linear constraints jointly in $v$ and $z_{sj}$.

Minimize $\sum_sv(s)$ subject to these constraints over all cells. This is a polynomial-size rational linear program. The constraints are equivalent to $v\geq Tv$; the argument in Section 7 shows that its minimizing value vector is exactly $V^*$.

At this rational $V^*$, maximize the affine Bellman expression on each cell by linear programming and choose a best profile at each state. This yields a rational return-optimal policy in polynomial time. Section 5 supplies exact minimum-excess credits in polynomial time.

Therefore the entire problem is polynomial-time solvable under explicit common transition-vector encoding.
