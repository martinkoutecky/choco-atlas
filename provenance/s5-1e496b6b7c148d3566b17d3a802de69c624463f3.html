1. Unique finite equilibrium and its outcome

Write $\mu_j=\mu_{t_j}$ and define
$$ \varepsilon_j= \begin{cases} 1,&0<d_{t_j}<c_{t_j}<G_{t_j},\\ -1,&G_{t_j}<c_{t_j}<d_{t_j}<0. \end{cases} $$
Encode Yes, Abstain, and No by score contributions $1,0,-1$, respectively.

Consider any finite ordered society and any integer threshold $k$. Before voter $i$, let $B_i$ be the score already cast, and let $R_i$ be the true-preference score of the voters strictly after $i$. Put
$$ h_i=k-B_i-R_i. $$

Lemma 1. The unique equilibrium ballot is given by the following table.

| Condition | Positive, with abstention | Positive, without abstention | Negative, with abstention | Negative, without abstention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $h_i\le -1$ | No | No | Yes | Yes |
| $h_i=0$ | Abstain | Yes | No | No |
| $h_i=1$ | Yes | Yes | Abstain | No |
| $h_i\ge 2$ | No | No | Yes | Yes |

Moreover, every continuation subgame produces the outcome obtained by having its remaining voters vote according to their true preferences.

Proof. Use backward induction, allowing the effective threshold of a continuation subgame to lie outside its attainable score range. The assertion is immediate when there are no remaining voters.

Suppose it holds for all continuations after the current voter. If that voter chooses contribution $a$, the continuation accepts exactly when
$$ B_i+a+R_i\ge k, $$
equivalently $a\ge h_i$.

If $h_i\le-1$, every available ballot induces acceptance. If $h_i\ge2$, every ballot induces rejection. In either case a positive voter uniquely minimizes ballot cost by choosing No; a negative voter uniquely maximizes the ballot subsidy by choosing Yes.

For a positive voter at $h_i=0$, the utilities of No, Abstain, and Yes are
$$ 0,\qquad G_t-d_t,\qquad G_t-c_t. $$
The restrictions give $G_t-d_t>G_t-c_t>0$, yielding the stated choices. At $h_i=1$, Yes gives $G_t-c_t>0$, whereas No gives $0$ and Abstain gives $-d_t<0$, so Yes is uniquely optimal.

For a negative voter at $h_i=0$, No gives $0$, while the two accepting ballots give $G_t-d_t<0$ and $G_t-c_t<0$. Thus No is uniquely optimal. At $h_i=1$, Abstain gives $-d_t>0$, No gives $0$, and Yes gives $G_t-c_t<0$. The stated choices follow.

In every row, the chosen action induces acceptance exactly when $\varepsilon_i\ge h_i$. This is precisely the outcome of the current voter’s true ballot followed by truthful continuation. The induction closes, and all choices are strict. $\square$

For the societies in the question, define
$$ D_n=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\varepsilon_j n_j(n). $$
Lemma 1 proves, for both ballot versions,
$$ z_n=\mathbf 1_{\{D_n\ge k_n\}}. $$

Thus equilibrium outcomes and ballots depend on the utility parameters only through the signs $\varepsilon_j$.

2. The two equilibria differ in at most one ballot

Before voter $i$, define the integer slack
$$ \delta_i=B_i+\varepsilon_i+R_i-k. $$
Then
$$ h_i=\varepsilon_i-\delta_i. $$
If voter $i$ casts contribution $a_i$, the slack before the next voter satisfies
$$ \delta_{i+1}=\delta_i+a_i-\varepsilon_i. $$

The two versions have identical histories until their first differing ballot. By Lemma 1, a first difference can occur only:

  • At a positive voter with $\delta_i=1$: Abstain with abstention, Yes without it.
  • At a negative voter with $\delta_i=-2$: Abstain with abstention, No without it.

After a positive first difference, the two slacks are $(0,1)$. Thereafter they remain of the form
$$ (\delta_{\mathrm A},\delta_{\mathrm{YN}})=(2q,2q+1), \qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$
Indeed, a negative voter chooses Yes in both games and increases $q$ by one. A positive voter chooses Yes in both games if $q=0$; otherwise both choose No and decrease $q$ by one.

After a negative first difference, the slacks are $(-1,-2)$. Thereafter they remain of the form
$$ (\delta_{\mathrm A},\delta_{\mathrm{YN}}) =(-2q-1,-2q-2), \qquad q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$
A positive voter chooses No in both games and increases $q$ by one. A negative voter chooses No in both games if $q=0$; otherwise both choose Yes and decrease $q$ by one.

These invariants follow directly from the slack recurrence and the ballot table. Neither permits another abstention in the abstention game. Before the first difference, an abstention would itself have been a difference.

Consequently, there is at most one abstention, and every other voter casts the same ballot in the two versions. Therefore
$$ \left\|x^{(n)}_{\mathrm A}-x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{YN}}\right\|_\infty \le \frac1n, \qquad \sum_t x^{(n)}_{t,\mathrm{Abstain}}\le\frac1n. $$
Their finite outcomes also coincide. Corresponding joint sequences consequently have identical subsequential limits:
$$ \boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm A}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{YN}}.} $$
Every point in this common set has zero abstention coordinates.

3. Polynomial-time formulas for the candidate points

Define the true-preference margin
$$ \lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\varepsilon_j\mu_j. $$
Both sign classes have positive mass, so
$$ -1<\lambda<1. $$

From the prescribed rounding,
$$ n_j(n)-n\mu_j =\{nS_{j-1}\}-\{nS_j\}, $$
where $\{u\}=u-\lfloor u\rfloor$. Hence $n_j(n)/n\to\mu_j$, and therefore
$$ \frac{D_n}{n}\longrightarrow\lambda. $$
The threshold definition also gives
$$ \frac{k_n}{n}\longrightarrow\kappa, $$
including the clipped endpoint cases $\kappa=-1$ and $\kappa=1$.

We now compute the candidate profile $X^{(z)}$ for each outcome that can occur infinitely often.

For acceptance, initialize $c_0=\lambda-\kappa$. For rejection, initialize $c_0=\kappa-\lambda$. Only candidates with nonnegative initial state are relevant.

Process blocks in their given order. For incoming state $c$ and block mass $\mu$, put
$$ q=\min\{\mu,c/2\}. $$
Use the following updates, and set every Abstain coordinate to zero.

| Outcome $z$ | Type sign | Yes mass | No mass | Outgoing state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $\mu-q$ | $q$ | $c-2q$ |
| $1$ | $-1$ | $\mu$ | $0$ | $c+2\mu$ |
| $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | $\mu$ | $c+2\mu$ |
| $0$ | $-1$ | $q$ | $\mu-q$ | $c-2q$ |

Proof of convergence. First consider voting without abstention.

On an accepting path, the initial slack is $D_n-k_n\ge0$, and Lemma 1 preserves nonnegativity. Negative voters always choose Yes and increase the slack by two. In a positive block of $N$ voters with incoming slack $d$, exactly
$$ \min\{N,\lfloor d/2\rfloor\} $$
voters initially choose No, after which all remaining voters choose Yes.

On a rejecting path, the slack is at most $-1$. Use the nonnegative state
$$ r=-1-\delta. $$
Positive voters always choose No and increase $r$ by two. In a negative block of $N$ voters, exactly
$$ \min\{N,\lfloor r/2\rfloor\} $$
voters initially choose Yes; the remaining voters choose No.

The initial normalized accepting and rejecting states tend to $\lambda-\kappa$ and $\kappa-\lambda$, respectively. Moreover,
$$ \left|\frac{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}{n}-\frac{d}{2n}\right|<\frac1n. $$
Dividing the exact block recurrences by $n$, continuity of minimum and addition proves the displayed limiting recurrences by induction over the blocks.

Thus, along every subsequence with fixed outcome $z$,
$$ x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{YN}}\longrightarrow X^{(z)}. $$
Section 2 transfers this conclusion to voting with abstention. $\square$

Each profile requires a linear number of rational operations. State updates only add or subtract twice an input mass, or reset the state to zero, so all intermediate bit lengths are polynomial in the input length.

A convergent sequence of binary outcomes is eventually constant. Therefore every joint limit is one of
$$ (X^{(1)},1),\qquad (X^{(0)},0). $$

In particular, away from the critical threshold, the full answer is polynomial-time computable:
$$ \lambda>\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm A}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{YN}} =\{(X^{(1)},1)\}, $$
$$ \lambda<\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm A}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{YN}} =\{(X^{(0)},0)\}. $$

4. Exact arithmetic characterization at the critical threshold

Assume now that $\kappa=\lambda$. Since $-1<\lambda<1$, clipping is inactive for all sufficiently large $n$. Integrality of $D_n$ then gives
$$ z_n=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_n\ge\lceil n\lambda+\beta\rceil \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_n-n\lambda\ge\beta. $$

Telescoping the block counts yields
$$ D_n=\varepsilon_\tau n+ \sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1} (\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_{j+1})\lfloor nS_j\rfloor. $$
Likewise,
$$ \lambda=\varepsilon_\tau+ \sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1} (\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_{j+1})S_j. $$
Subtracting gives the exact error
$$ e(n):=D_n-n\lambda =-\sum_{j=1}^{\tau-1} (\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_{j+1})\{nS_j\}. $$

Let
$$ J=\{j:1\le j<\tau,\ \varepsilon_j\ne\varepsilon_{j+1}\}, \qquad s=|J|. $$
Only boundaries in $J$ affect $e$. Choose $Q$ to be the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of $S_j$ for $j\in J$. Then
$$ e(n+Q)=e(n). $$
This choice can be smaller than a common denominator of all type masses.

Define
$$ E_{\min}=\min_{0\le r<Q}e(r), \qquad E_{\max}=\max_{0\le r<Q}e(r). $$
Every residue class has arbitrarily large representatives. Section 3 therefore proves
$$ \boxed{ \mathcal L_{\mathrm A}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{YN}} = \{(X^{(1)},1):E_{\max}\ge\beta\} \ \cup\ \{(X^{(0)},0):E_{\min}<\beta\}. } $$
The strict inequality for rejection follows from acceptance including equality.

This is an exact characterization and gives an exhaustive algorithm. It does not give a polynomial-time bound for unrestricted inputs, because $Q$ can be exponentially large.

Nevertheless, $\log Q$ is polynomially bounded by the input length. Indeed, every cumulative-mass denominator divides a common denominator of the input masses, which can be taken as the product of their denominators. Thus a residue $r<Q$ is a polynomial-length certificate, and $e(r)$ can be computed and compared with $\beta$ in polynomial time.

Consequently, each candidate-membership decision belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. Since $e(0)=0$:

  • If $\beta>0$, the rejecting candidate certainly occurs.
  • If $\beta\le0$, the accepting candidate certainly occurs.

General explicit computation therefore requires polynomial-time arithmetic and at most one unresolved $\mathrm{NP}$ existence test.

5. New result: polynomial computation for a fixed number of sign changes

Theorem 2. For every fixed integer $s_0$, exact computation of both joint limit sets is polynomial-time on societies with at most $s_0$ sign changes. In particular, it is polynomial-time for every fixed support size.

Proof. Only the critical case requires additional work.

Enumerate the sign-change boundaries as $j_1,\ldots,j_s$. Set
$$ a_\ell=QS_{j_\ell}, \qquad c_\ell=\varepsilon_{j_\ell}-\varepsilon_{j_\ell+1} \in\{-2,2\}. $$
Each $a_\ell$ is an integer satisfying $0<a_\ell<Q$.

Introduce integer variables
$$ r,u_1,\ldots,u_s. $$
Impose
$$ 0\le r\le Q-1, $$
and, for every $\ell$,
$$ 0\le a_\ell r-Qu_\ell\le Q-1. $$
Because the variables and coefficients are integral, these inequalities are equivalent to
$$ u_\ell=\left\lfloor\frac{a_\ell r}{Q}\right\rfloor. $$
Thus
$$ a_\ell r-Qu_\ell =Q\left\{\frac{a_\ell r}{Q}\right\}. $$

Define the integer linear expression
$$ H(r,u)=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{s} c_\ell(a_\ell r-Qu_\ell). $$
On the feasible integer points,
$$ H(r,u)=Qe(r). $$

It follows that acceptance occurs infinitely often exactly when this integer system, augmented by
$$ H(r,u)\ge\lceil Q\beta\rceil, $$
is feasible.

Rejection occurs infinitely often exactly when the system augmented by
$$ H(r,u)\le\lceil Q\beta\rceil-1 $$
is feasible. This last equivalence uses the elementary fact that an integer $H$ satisfies $H<Q\beta$ exactly when $H\le\lceil Q\beta\rceil-1$.

Both are integer linear feasibility problems in $s+1$ variables. Their coefficients are integral, and their total binary encoding length is polynomial in $\tau$ and $L$: the numbers $Q,a_\ell$, and $\lceil Q\beta\rceil$ have polynomial bit length.

Lenstra’s theorem states that integer linear feasibility for an integer-coefficient system is polynomial-time solvable when the number of integer variables is fixed. Its hypotheses apply here because there are at most $s_0+1$ variables and the constructed systems have polynomial encoding length. [Lenstra, *Integer Programming with a Fixed Number of Variables*](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~lenstrahw/PUBLICATIONS/1983i/art.pdf)

The two feasibility decisions determine the explicit limit set, whose candidate points are already computable in polynomial time. $\square$

The same construction computes the extrema exactly for fixed $s$. Since
$$ |H(r,u)|<2sQ, $$
binary search on integer objective thresholds needs only
$$ O(\log(sQ+1)) $$
feasibility calls. Dividing the resulting integer extrema by $Q$ gives $E_{\min}$ and $E_{\max}$.

The fixed-variable hypothesis is essential to this application. This argument does not establish polynomial time when $s$ grows with the input.

6. New result: several unrestricted-support critical cases

The periodic error has three immediately computable values:
$$ e(0)=0, \qquad e(1)=\varepsilon_\tau-\lambda, \qquad e(Q-1)=\lambda-\varepsilon_1. $$

For the second identity, use $\{S_j\}=S_j$ and the telescoping formula for $\lambda$.

For the third, $QS_j$ is integral at every sign-change boundary, and $0<S_j<1$, so
$$ \{(Q-1)S_j\}=1-S_j. $$
Therefore
$$ \begin{aligned} e(Q-1) &=-\sum_{j\in J}(\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_{j+1})(1-S_j)\\ &=-(\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_\tau) +(\lambda-\varepsilon_\tau)\\ &=\lambda-\varepsilon_1. \end{aligned} $$
These are evaluations of the periodic arithmetic function; their residue classes have arbitrarily large representatives, regardless of whether the original construction was stipulated only for sufficiently large societies.

Put
$$ a=\min\{0,\varepsilon_\tau-\lambda,\lambda-\varepsilon_1\}, $$
$$ b=\max\{0,\varepsilon_\tau-\lambda,\lambda-\varepsilon_1\}. $$
Then $E_{\min}\le a$ and $E_{\max}\ge b$. Consequently, whenever
$$ a<\beta\le b, $$
both candidate points occur. This gives additional polynomial-time cases without bounding the number of sign changes.

In particular, suppose $\beta=0$. Acceptance always occurs infinitely often because $e(0)=0$. If $\varepsilon_1=1$, then
$$ e(Q-1)=\lambda-1<0, $$
so rejection also occurs infinitely often. If $\varepsilon_\tau=-1$, then
$$ e(1)=-1-\lambda<0, $$
with the same conclusion.

Thus
$$ \boxed{ \kappa=\lambda,\quad \beta=0,\quad (\varepsilon_1=1\ \text{or}\ \varepsilon_\tau=-1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm A}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{YN}} =\{(X^{(1)},1),(X^{(0)},0)\}. } $$

The remaining endpoint pattern at $\beta=0$ can exhibit either behavior:

  • With a negative block followed by a positive block, each of mass $1/2$, one has $e(r)\in\{0,1\}$. Only the accepting point occurs.
  • With signs $(-1,1,-1,1)$ and masses

$$ \left(\frac18,\frac14,\frac14,\frac38\right), $$
one has $\lambda=1/4$ and
$$ e(2)=2\left(\frac14-\frac34+\frac14\right)=-\frac12. $$
At $\kappa=\lambda$ and $\beta=0$, both points occur.

Valid utility parameters can be assigned, for example, using $(G,c,d)=(3,2,1)$ for positive types and $(-3,-2,-1)$ for negative types.

7. One sign change and an explicit two-point example

If there is exactly one sign change, let its cumulative mass be $a/q$ in lowest terms and let $\varepsilon$ be the sign before that boundary. Then
$$ e(r)=-2\varepsilon\left\{\frac{ra}{q}\right\}. $$
Coprimality gives the fractional-part range
$$ \left\{0,\frac1q,\ldots,\frac{q-1}{q}\right\}. $$
Hence:

| Initial sign $\varepsilon$ | $E_{\min}$ | $E_{\max}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $-2(q-1)/q$ | $0$ |
| $-1$ | $0$ | $2(q-1)/q$ |

This provides an elementary polynomial-time algorithm for one sign change, including arbitrary numbers of types within each sign run.

For a concrete two-point example, take a positive block followed by a negative block, each of mass $1/2$, with
$$ \kappa=0,\qquad \beta=-\frac12. $$
Here $\lambda=0$, and
$$ e(n)= \begin{cases} 0,&n\text{ even},\\ -1,&n\text{ odd}. \end{cases} $$
The accepting candidate has both types casting Yes with their full mass. The rejecting candidate has the positive type casting No and the negative type casting Yes, each with mass $1/2$. Both have zero abstention mass.

Thus uniqueness of every finite equilibrium does not imply uniqueness of the joint subsequential limit.

8. Precisely what remains open

The ballot-set comparison, the candidate profiles, the periodic characterization, and the tractable cases above are proved.

For unrestricted numbers of sign changes, the outstanding decision is whether some residue satisfies
$$ -\sum_{j\in J} (\varepsilon_j-\varepsilon_{j+1})\{rS_j\}\ge\beta, \qquad 0\le r<Q. $$
The cumulative masses are strictly ordered, and the nonzero coefficients alternate between $2$ and $-2$. A hardness reduction must preserve these restrictions; hardness of a more general fractional-part optimization problem would not suffice.

Neither an exponentially large period nor the growing dimension of the integer-programming formulation proves NP-hardness. I have not established a polynomial-time algorithm or a valid hardness reduction for this remaining problem.
