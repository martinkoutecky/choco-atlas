The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational separation intensity \(\sigma\), and rational piecewise-constant densities \(f_t\) on \([0,1]\), define \(q_t=\sup_p\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{z\in[0,1]} f_t(X_p(z))p(z)\), where the supremum ranges over measurable \(p\ge0\) with \(X_p(z)=\int_0^z(p(u)+\sigma)\,du\) and \(\int_0^1(p(z)+\sigma)\,dz\le1\). Decide whether there exist a measurable type order \(\theta\) and piece-length profile \(p\) with \(\operatorname{Leb}\{z:\theta(z)=t\}=\mu_t\) and \(f_{\theta(z)}(X_p(z))p(z)\ge q_{\theta(z)}\) almost everywhere; more generally, compute the maximum \(\rho\) after replacing the right side by \(\rho q_{\theta(z)}\), with a finite cell-and-boundary encoding of the schedule and gaps.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The resource is \([0,1]\). Each type has an explicitly given nonnegative piecewise-constant density \(f_t\), with binary-rational breakpoints and heights and \(\int_0^1 f_t(x)\,dx=1\); zero-density intervals are allowed. Let \(m\) be the number of cells in the common breakpoint refinement and \(L\) the total input bit length. The binary-rational separation intensity satisfies \(0<\sigma<1\).
An admissible profile is a measurable integrable \(p:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) with \(X_p(z)=\int_0^z(p(u)+\sigma)\,du\) and \(X_p(1)\le1\). Here \(z\) indexes population mass, \(p(z)\) is resource length per unit mass, and \(\sigma\) is mandatory separation length per unit mass; any suffix beyond \(X_p(1)\) is unused.
Define \(q_t=\sup_{p\text{ admissible}}\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{z\in[0,1]}f_t(X_p(z))p(z)\), independently of \(\mu_t\).
A schedule is an admissible \(p\) and a measurable \(\theta:[0,1]\to T\) with \(\operatorname{Leb}(\theta^{-1}(t))=\mu_t\). At factor \(\rho\ge0\), it must satisfy \(f_{\theta(z)}(X_p(z))p(z)\ge\rho q_{\theta(z)}\) almost everywhere. A zero benchmark imposes no positive utility requirement.
The decision problem asks whether a schedule exists at \(\rho=1\); its threshold version takes a binary-rational \(\rho\ge0\) as input. The optimization value is \(\rho^*=\sup\{\rho\ge0:\text{a schedule exists at factor }\rho\}\), allowing \(+\infty\) and distinguishing a finite attained optimum from an unattained supremum.
A finite schedule encoding consists of rational boundaries partitioning the population interval, a type and a nonnegative rational constant profile on each interval, and the resulting affine position maps and unused suffix; intervals may be refined at resource-cell crossings. Feasibility is defined over all measurable schedules, without assuming that such finite representatives exist.

Is continuous MMS feasibility in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard, and is exact optimization of \(\rho^*\), including attainment and finite schedule representation, polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and the threshold bit length when applicable.

For finitely many agents, connected separated MMS-fair allocations exist and can be computed in polynomial time with explicit rational piecewise-constant densities; individual MMS values are also exactly computable in polynomial time. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.06682).
No result settling this exact population-mass model was identified in the literature search.

  • Restricted separation intensity to the nondegenerate domain 0 < sigma < 1, interpreting sigma as total mandatory separation length for unit population mass.
  • Kept the cumulative-position formula stated in the graded question: separation is distributed at exactly the prescribed intensity, and unused resource is an optional suffix.
  • Defined m as the number of cells in the common density-breakpoint refinement and L as total binary input length.

Partial resolution

Feasibility at factor $1$ is in $\mathrm P$: every valid input is feasible. The general threshold and compact exact-value complexity classifications remain unresolved here.

The following additional results are proved below:

  • Every finite optimum is attained.
  • Exact threshold feasibility takes

$$ m^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+B) $$
bit operations, where $B$ is the threshold bit length. Thus it is polynomial-time solvable for fixed support size.

  • A finite optimum has algebraic degree at most

$$ m\prod_{t:q_t>0}\deg P_t\le m^{\tau+1}, $$
for explicitly constructed benchmark polynomials $P_t$.

  • Even with two cells and exclusively dyadic input, the exact optimum can have algebraic degree $2^{\tau-1}$ while the input length is polynomial in $\tau$. Consequently, dense polynomial output for the exact value cannot always be produced in polynomial time.
  • The stipulated rational schedule representation need not exist, even for one type at factor $1$.
  • General rational-threshold feasibility is square-root-sum-hard under the convention that rational numerators and denominators are encoded in binary.
  • Exact algebraic values and attaining algebraic schedules can be constructed with explicit exponential bounds. The schedule construction below uses linear-program bases and Cramer’s rule, repairing the witness-construction gap identified in the verifier report.

Throughout, “binary-rational” means a rational numerator and denominator encoded in binary. All results except the stated square-root-sum reduction also apply when inputs are restricted to dyadic rationals.

1. Individual benchmarks

Let the common refinement be
$$ 0=b_0<b_1<\cdots<b_m=1, $$
with cell lengths $d_j=b_j-b_{j-1}$ and density heights $h_{tj}$.

For type $t$, let $r_t$ be the number of cells in its initial consecutive positive-density run, and put
$$ \ell_t=\sum_{j=1}^{r_t}d_j. $$
An empty initial run has $\ell_t=0$.

Proposition. If $\ell_t\le\sigma$, then $q_t=0$. Otherwise $q_t$ is the unique positive solution of
$$ F_t(Q)=\sum_{j=1}^{r_t}\frac{d_jh_{tj}}{\sigma h_{tj}+Q}=1. $$
This positive benchmark is attained.

Proof. Every admissible position map is absolutely continuous, with
$$ X_p'(z)=p(z)+\sigma\ge\sigma $$
almost everywhere. Hence it is continuous and strictly increasing. Breakpoint preimages have measure zero.

Suppose a profile gives homogeneous utility at least $v>0$. It cannot traverse a positive-length zero-density cell: its population preimage would be a nondegenerate interval on which the utility requirement fails. Therefore $X_p(1)\le\ell_t$.

On a positive cell of height $h$, the utility requirement implies
$$ p\ge\frac vh, \qquad \frac{h}{\sigma h+v}(p+\sigma)\ge1. $$
Integration, separately over the population preimages of the cells, gives
$$ 1\le \int_0^{X_p(1)}\frac{f_t(x)}{\sigma f_t(x)+v}\,dx \le F_t(v). $$
The change of variables follows from absolute continuity: on each cell preimage, the integral of $X_p'$ equals the traversed resource length.

The function $F_t$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on positive arguments, tends to zero at infinity, and satisfies
$$ F_t(0)=\frac{\ell_t}{\sigma}. $$
Thus, if $\ell_t\le\sigma$, no positive target is feasible.

If $\ell_t>\sigma$, there is a unique positive root $q$ of $F_t(q)=1$. Assign population length
$$ w_{tj}=\frac{d_j}{\sigma+q/h_{tj}} $$
to each initial positive cell, using profile $p=q/h_{tj}$. These population lengths sum to one, each block traverses its cell exactly, and utility equals $q$. Every larger target violates the necessary bound above. Hence $q_t=q$. $\square$

Clearing denominators produces
$$ P_t(Q)= \sum_{j=1}^{r_t}d_jh_{tj} \prod_{\substack{i=1\\i\ne j}}^{r_t}(\sigma h_{ti}+Q) - \prod_{i=1}^{r_t}(\sigma h_{ti}+Q). $$
After clearing rational coefficient denominators and changing its sign if necessary, this is an integer polynomial with positive leading coefficient, degree at most $m$, and coefficient bit length
$$ H=O\bigl(m(L+\log(m+1))\bigr). $$
This bound follows by expanding products of at most $m$ input factors and clearing their rational denominators.

There are no positive extraneous roots, since every cleared denominator is positive there. Moreover,
$$ F_t(1) < \sum_{j=1}^{r_t}d_jh_{tj} \le1, $$
so every positive benchmark lies in $(0,1)$. Thus the polynomial together with the isolating interval $(0,1)$ already gives an exact benchmark encoding.

All benchmarks can therefore be encoded in
$$ \tau\,\operatorname{poly}(m,L) $$
bit operations. Approximating them to $k$ binary places also takes polynomial time in $m,\tau,L+k$: bisect $(0,1)$ and compare the rational value $F_t(Q)$ with one.

2. Exact finite-dimensional characterization

Fix $\rho>0$. A type-cell pair is allowed when either $q_t=0$ or $h_{tj}>0$. Let $K$ be the number of cells before the first cell having no allowed type; put $K=m$ if no such cell exists.

For an allowed pair, define
$$ c_{tj}(\rho)= \begin{cases} \sigma,&q_t=0,\\ \sigma+\rho q_t/h_{tj},&q_t>0. \end{cases} $$

Consider the system
$$ y_{tj}\ge0, $$
$$ \sum_{\substack{j\le K\\(t,j)\text{ allowed}}}y_{tj}=\mu_t \quad(t\in T), $$
$$ \sum_{\substack{t\\(t,j)\text{ allowed}}} c_{tj}(\rho)y_{tj}\le d_j \quad(j\le K). $$

Proposition. A measurable schedule exists at factor $\rho$ if and only if this system is feasible.

Necessity

Given a schedule, put
$$ G_{tj}=\{z:X_p(z)\in(b_{j-1},b_j),\ \theta(z)=t\}, \qquad y_{tj}=\operatorname{Leb}(G_{tj}). $$
Forbidden pairs have zero mass. A cell having no allowed type cannot be entered through a positive resource length: its population preimage would have positive measure, but its intersection with every type population would have measure zero. Consequently, $X_p(1)\le b_K$.

All population except finitely many breakpoint preimages is accounted for by the sets $G_{tj}$, giving the type equations. On an allowed pair, $p+\sigma\ge c_{tj}(\rho)$ almost everywhere, whence
$$ \sum_t c_{tj}(\rho)y_{tj} \le \sum_t\int_{G_{tj}}(p+\sigma)\,dz \le d_j. $$

Sufficiency and empty-cell repair

Let $y$ satisfy the system. Originally empty cells require repair, since a positive resource interval cannot be traversed instantaneously.

For each originally empty cell $j$, choose an allowed type $t(j)$. Its positive mass supplies an original donor entry
$$ y_{t(j),i(j)}>0. $$
Move from that donor to cell $j$ the amount
$$ \delta_j= \frac{d_jy_{t(j),i(j)}} {2m\bigl(d_j+c_{t(j),j}(\rho)y_{t(j),i(j)}\bigr)}. $$
It satisfies
$$ 0<\delta_j\le \frac{y_{t(j),i(j)}}{2m}, \qquad c_{t(j),j}(\rho)\delta_j\le\frac{d_j}{2m}. $$
At most $m$ transfers charge any donor entry, so every original positive entry retains at least half its mass. Donor capacities decrease, recipient capacities hold, and type totals are preserved.

Write the repaired vector as $\widehat y$. Every cell is now occupied. Start with block lengths
$$ a_{tj}=c_{tj}(\rho)\widehat y_{tj}. $$
Give the remaining length of each cell to any one of its positive-mass entries. Then
$$ \sum_t a_{tj}=d_j, \qquad a_{tj}\ge c_{tj}(\rho)\widehat y_{tj}. $$
Omit every zero-mass entry. For each remaining block, use population length $\widehat y_{tj}$ and constant profile
$$ p_{tj}=\frac{a_{tj}}{\widehat y_{tj}}-\sigma. $$
This is nonnegative and meets the required utility inequality. Concatenate the blocks in cell order. Their population lengths sum to one, their resource lengths sum to $b_K$, and the unused suffix is $[b_K,1]$.

The resulting schedule has at most $m\tau$ blocks. Its finite profile is integrable and admissible. $\square$

This proves finite real representability from measurable feasibility, rather than assuming it.

3. Every society is feasible at factor one

For each positive-benchmark type, take the homogeneous attaining profile from Section 1. For a zero-benchmark type, take $p=0$, ending at position $\sigma$.

Let $w_{tj}$ be the population mass and $D_{tj}$ the resource length of this homogeneous schedule in cell $j$. Then
$$ \sum_j w_{tj}=1, \qquad D_{tj}=c_{tj}(1)w_{tj}, \qquad 0\le D_{tj}\le d_j. $$
These schedules remain within the accessible prefix. Indeed, a positive-benchmark schedule traverses only its initial positive-density run. If a zero benchmark exists, every cell has an allowed type.

Set
$$ y_{tj}=\mu_t w_{tj}. $$
The type equations hold, and each capacity satisfies
$$ \sum_t c_{tj}(1)y_{tj} =\sum_t\mu_tD_{tj} \le d_j\sum_t\mu_t=d_j. $$
Section 2 constructs the required schedule.

Therefore every valid input answers YES at factor $1$, and every factor in $[0,1]$ is feasible. On the promised valid-input domain, the decision takes constant time; reading, validation, and refinement take $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time.

The same construction gives a polynomial-size description by arithmetic expressions in the individually encoded benchmarks. Rational coefficients, however, are not guaranteed.

4. Unboundedness and attainment

Let
$$ H_t=\max_j h_{tj}, \qquad A=\sum_t\frac{\mu_tq_t}{H_t}. $$
Normalization ensures $H_t>0$.

Every feasible factor satisfies
$$ \int_0^1p(z)\,dz \ge \rho\sum_t\frac{\mu_tq_t}{H_t} =\rho A. $$
Admissibility gives $\int p\le1-\sigma$. Hence, if any benchmark is positive,
$$ 1\le\rho^*\le\frac{1-\sigma}{A}<+\infty. $$
If all benchmarks are zero, $p=0$ works at every finite factor, so $\rho^*=+\infty$.

Suppose now that $\rho^*$ is finite. Choose positive feasible factors $\rho_n\to\rho^*$ and corresponding cell vectors $y^{(n)}$. Each coordinate is bounded by its type mass. A subsequence converges to $y^*$.

The allowed pairs and accessible prefix are independent of the positive factor. Nonnegativity and type equations are closed, and
$$ c_{tj}(\rho_n)y_{tj}^{(n)} \longrightarrow c_{tj}(\rho^*)y_{tj}^*. $$
Thus all capacity inequalities survive the limit. Section 2 constructs an attaining schedule.

Consequently, the feasible-factor set is either $[0,+\infty)$ or $[0,\rho^*]$ with a finite attained endpoint. A finite unattained supremum cannot occur.

5. A degree bound for the optimum

Let
$$ \mathbb K=\mathbb Q(q_t:t\in T). $$
For positive benchmarks, put $d_t=\deg P_t$, and define
$$ D_0=\prod_{t:q_t>0}d_t, $$
with empty product one. Successively adjoining the benchmarks gives
$$ [\mathbb K:\mathbb Q]\le D_0\le m^\tau. $$

Introduce one nonnegative slack variable per capacity constraint. The cell system becomes
$$ A(R)x=b,\qquad x\ge0. $$
There are $r=\tau+K$ rows and at most $m\tau+K$ columns. The matrix has full row rank: slack columns isolate the cell rows, and population columns then isolate the type rows.

For any basis $B$, Cramer’s rule gives
$$ x_{B,i}(R)=\frac{E_{B,i}(R)}{\Delta_B(R)}, \qquad \Delta_B(R)=\det A_B(R). $$
Only the $K$ cell rows depend on $R$, each affinely. Therefore
$$ \deg_R\Delta_B\le K, \qquad \deg_R E_{B,i}\le K. $$

At $\rho^*$ the feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded: population coordinates are bounded by type masses, and slack coordinates by cell lengths. It therefore has a feasible basic solution.

Take its basis $B$, so $\Delta_B(\rho^*)\ne0$. If every basic coordinate vanishing there had an identically zero numerator, all positive coordinates would remain positive in a sufficiently small neighborhood, and identically zero coordinates would stay zero. This would extend feasibility above $\rho^*$, a contradiction.

Thus some nonzero polynomial $E_{B,i}$ vanishes at $\rho^*$. It has degree at most $K$, proving
$$ [\mathbb K(\rho^*):\mathbb K]\le K. $$
In particular,
$$ [\mathbb Q(\rho^*):\mathbb Q] \le K D_0 \le m^{\tau+1}. $$

6. Exact threshold decision in polynomial time for fixed support

The argument here supplies an exact bit algorithm, including equality cases.

A capacity-violation linear program

Fix rational $\rho>0$, of bit length $B$. Let $\mathcal A$ be the compact assignment polytope defined by nonnegativity and the type equations, using only allowed pairs in the accessible prefix. Every type has an allowed first-cell pair, so $\mathcal A$ is nonempty.

Define
$$ v= \min_{y\in\mathcal A} \max\left\{ 0,\ \max_{j\le K} \left(\sum_t c_{tj}(\rho)y_{tj}-d_j\right) \right\}. $$
By Section 2, threshold feasibility is equivalent to $v=0$.

This is a linear program after introducing $w$ with
$$ w\ge0,\qquad w\ge\sum_t c_{tj}(\rho)y_{tj}-d_j. $$
Its optimal face, with $w=v$, is nonempty and compact. A vertex of that face is also a vertex of the feasible polyhedron. At such a vertex, active constraint rows span the full variable space; otherwise a sufficiently small perturbation in both directions along a common null vector would preserve feasibility.

Hence Cramer’s rule expresses $v$ as a ratio
$$ v=\frac{E(q)}{F(q)}, \qquad F(q)\ne0. $$
The numerator and denominator are integer polynomials after clearing rational coefficients. Their benchmark degree is at most $K\le m$, because benchmark coefficients occur only in cell rows.

An explicit positive-gap bound

Let $n$ be the number of variables, including $w$, so $n\le m\tau+1$. Clear all rational coefficients in the constraint system by one positive common denominator. Let $H_0$ bound the bit lengths of the resulting integer coefficients. It is computable with
$$ H_0=\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+B). $$

Use the benchmark coefficient bound $H$ from Section 1. Every conjugate of a benchmark has modulus at most $2^{H+1}$, by the Cauchy root bound.

Let $a_t$ be the positive leading coefficient of $P_t$. The number $a_tq_t$ is an algebraic integer: substituting $Z=a_tQ$ into $P_t$ and multiplying by $a_t^{d_t-1}$ gives a monic integer polynomial in $Z$. Put
$$ U=\prod_{t:q_t>0}a_t, \qquad U\le2^{\tau H}. $$
Because $E$ has total benchmark degree at most $m$, the number
$$ \beta=U^mE(q) $$
is an algebraic integer.

For every embedding of $\mathbb K$ into $\mathbb C$, determinant expansion gives
$$ |E(q)|,\ |F(q)|\le2^V, $$
where one may take
$$ V=n\bigl(H_0+H+2+\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil\bigr). $$
Indeed, each matrix entry has modulus at most $2^{H_0+H+2}$, and there are at most $n!$ determinant terms.

Set
$$ Z=m\tau H+V+2. $$
Every conjugate of $\beta$ has modulus at most $2^Z$.

If $v>0$, then $\beta\ne0$. The field norm of a nonzero algebraic integer is a nonzero integer. Thus
$$ 1\le \left|N_{\mathbb K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)\right|, $$
and, bounding every factor except the distinguished one,
$$ |\beta|\ge2^{-Z([\mathbb K:\mathbb Q]-1)}. $$
Combining this with $U^m\le2^{m\tau H}$ and $|F(q)|\le2^V$ yields
$$ v\ge2^{-ZD_0}. $$
Therefore
$$ v=0 \quad\text{or}\quad v\ge g, \qquad g:=2^{-ZD_0}. $$

The algebraic-integer facts used here are closure under addition and multiplication, and the norm formula as the product over field embeddings. Their hypotheses hold for $\beta$ by the explicit denominator clearing above.

Rational approximation decides exactly

Approximate every coefficient $c_{tj}(\rho)$ by a rational coefficient within $\varepsilon=g/8$. This requires
$$ ZD_0+\operatorname{poly}(L+B) $$
binary places of benchmark precision.

Let $\widehat v$ be the exact optimum of the resulting rational linear program. For every $y\in\mathcal A$,
$$ \sum_t y_{tj}\le1. $$
Therefore each cell-load error is at most $\varepsilon$. Taking a maximum and then a minimum preserves that error bound:
$$ |\widehat v-v|\le\varepsilon. $$
Consequently,
$$ v=0\implies\widehat v\le g/8, $$
whereas
$$ v>0\implies\widehat v\ge7g/8. $$
Comparing $\widehat v$ with $g/2$ decides the original threshold exactly.

Rational linear programming is polynomial-time solvable in its dimensions and coefficient bit lengths. Benchmark bisection also has polynomial bit complexity in the requested precision. Since $D_0\le m^\tau$, the resulting running time is
$$ m^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+B). $$
The case $\rho=0$ answers YES immediately.

7. Rational finite schedule encoding can fail

Take one type, mass one, with $\sigma=1/2$ and density heights $1/2$ and $3/2$ on the two half-cells. All inputs are dyadic.

Its benchmark equation is
$$ \frac1{1+4q}+\frac3{3+4q}=1, $$
giving
$$ q=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}. $$
With one type, the definition of its benchmark prohibits any feasible factor above one. Benchmark attainment proves that $\rho^*=1$.

For any schedule at factor one, define
$$ c(x)=\frac1{\sigma+q/f(x)}. $$
The utility inequality and cellwise change of variables give
$$ 1 \le \int_0^1c(X_p(z))(p(z)+\sigma)\,dz = \int_0^{X_p(1)}c(x)\,dx \le \int_0^1c(x)\,dx =1. $$
Every inequality is therefore equality. Positivity of $c$ forces $X_p(1)=1$. The first integrand is at least one almost everywhere, so equality of its integral forces
$$ p(z)=\frac{q}{f(X_p(z))} $$
almost everywhere.

The profile must consequently equal
$$ \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \quad\text{on the first cell preimage}, \qquad \frac{\sqrt3}{6} \quad\text{on the second}. $$
Both preimages have positive population measure. A finite profile with rational constants cannot have these required values.

Thus a universal algorithm producing the stipulated rational schedule encoding cannot exist—even for factor-one feasibility.

8. A two-cell formula and square-root-sum hardness

Consider two half-cells, $\sigma=1/2$, and a uniform type of mass $1/2$. Other types have total mass $1/2$ and heights
$$ 0<h_i^-<h_i^+, \qquad h_i^-+h_i^+=2. $$
Their benchmark equation gives
$$ q_i^2=\frac{h_i^-h_i^+}{4}. $$
Define
$$ a_i=\frac{q_i}{h_i^+} =\frac12\sqrt{\frac{h_i^-}{h_i^+}}<\frac12, \qquad W=\sum_i\mu_i a_i<\frac14. $$

Two-cell formula.
$$ \rho^*=\frac{1/2}{1/4+W}. $$

The maximum-height bound proves the upper bound. Write the displayed value as $R$, so $1<R<2$. At factor $R$, give the uniform type profile $R/2$, and type $i$ profile $Ra_i$ entirely in the second cell.

Their resource lengths are
$$ U=\frac{1+R}{4}>\frac12, \qquad V=\frac14+RW<\frac12, \qquad U+V=1. $$
Use uniform population mass $1/(1+R)$ to traverse the first half-cell. Then place all other types in the second cell, followed by the remaining uniform population. The schedule ends at one and gives every type its required utility. This proves the formula.

Now take a square-root-sum instance consisting of positive integers $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ and a nonnegative integer $b$. Choose a power of two $C>\sqrt{\max_i a_i}$, and set
$$ r_i=\frac{a_i}{C^2}, \qquad h_i^-=\frac{2r_i}{1+r_i}, \qquad h_i^+=\frac2{1+r_i}, \qquad \mu_i=\frac1{2n}. $$
The uniform type has mass $1/2$. These are positive normalized rational densities, with polynomial encoding length. Their benchmarks satisfy
$$ q_i=\frac{\sqrt{r_i}}{1+r_i}, \qquad \frac{q_i}{h_i^+}=\frac{\sqrt{a_i}}{2C}. $$
Thus the two-cell formula gives
$$ \rho^*= \frac{2nC}{nC+\sum_i\sqrt{a_i}}. $$
For the rational threshold
$$ \rho_b=\frac{2nC}{nC+b}, $$
we obtain
$$ \rho_b\text{ feasible} \iff \sum_i\sqrt{a_i}\le b. $$

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction with $m=2$ and support size at most $n+1$. It proves square-root-sum hardness under general rational encoding. It supplies neither a polynomial-time algorithm nor an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness proof. The cited square-root-sum literature identifies its polynomial-time exact comparison as an open problem. [Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54)

9. Exponential algebraic degree with dyadic input

The following obstruction applies even under the stricter dyadic reading.

Let $n\ge8$ be a power of two. Choose $n$ distinct odd primes
$$ r_1,\ldots,r_n\le2n^3. $$
Such primes exist, and can be found by a polynomial-time sieve. For completeness, the elementary bound
$$ \binom{2N}{N}\ge\frac{4^N}{2N+1}, \qquad \binom{2N}{N}\le(2N)^{\pi(2N)} $$
implies
$$ \pi(2N)\ge \frac{N\log4-\log(2N+1)}{\log(2N)}. $$
The upper bound follows because, for each prime, every summand in its factorial-valuation difference is zero or one, so its contribution to the binomial coefficient is at most $2N$. Taking $N=n^3$ gives $\pi(2n^3)>n+1$ for $n\ge8$: use $\log4>1$, $\log(2n^3+1)<3n$, $\log(2n^3)<3n$, and $n^2>3n+6$.

Put
$$ M_i=2^{r_i}-1. $$
Use the two-cell society of Section 8, with
$$ h_i^-=2^{-(r_i-1)}, \qquad h_i^+=2-2^{-(r_i-1)}, \qquad \mu_i=\frac1{2n}. $$
All inputs are dyadic, and their total bit length is $O(n^4)$. The heights satisfy
$$ q_i=\frac{\sqrt{M_i}}{2^{r_i}}, \qquad \frac{q_i}{h_i^+}=\frac1{2\sqrt{M_i}}. $$
Therefore
$$ \rho^*= \frac{2n}{n+\sum_{i=1}^n1/\sqrt{M_i}}. $$

Independence of the square classes

The identity
$$ \gcd(2^r-1,2^s-1)=2^{\gcd(r,s)}-1 $$
follows by applying the Euclidean algorithm to the exponents. Hence the $M_i$ are pairwise coprime.

Each $r_i$ is odd and at least three, so $M_i$ is congruent to seven modulo eight and is not a square. Each therefore has a prime divisor occurring to an odd exponent; pairwise coprimality makes that prime absent from every other $M_j$. Consequently, no nonempty product of the $M_i$ is a rational square.

It follows that
$$ \mathbb F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{M_1},\ldots,\sqrt{M_n}) $$
has degree $2^n$, with all independent sign changes as automorphisms. Here is an induction proof of the needed fact.

Suppose the first $k-1$ square roots give a field with the expected monomial basis and sign changes. If $M_k$ were a square in that field, a square root $\alpha$ would satisfy $g(\alpha)=\pm\alpha$ under every sign change. Expanding $\alpha$ in the monomial basis shows it must be a rational multiple of one basis monomial: the independent sign characters distinguish all monomials. Squaring would express $M_k$ as a rational square times a product of earlier $M_i$, contradicting the odd valuation at a prime unique to $M_k$. Thus adjoining $\sqrt{M_k}$ doubles the degree. Existing sign changes extend while fixing the new root, and its own sign change supplies the additional automorphism.

Now put
$$ S=\sum_i\frac1{\sqrt{M_i}} =\sum_i\frac{\sqrt{M_i}}{M_i}. $$
No nonidentity sign change fixes $S$, since the individual square-root monomials are linearly independent and all their coefficients here are nonzero. Hence $S$ has $2^n$ distinct conjugates and degree $2^n$.

Finally,
$$ \rho^*=\frac{2n}{n+S}, \qquad S=\frac{2n}{\rho^*}-n, $$
so $\mathbb Q(\rho^*)=\mathbb Q(S)$. Therefore
$$ \deg_{\mathbb Q}\rho^*=2^n=2^{\tau-1}. $$

Any nonzero rational polynomial defining this optimum has degree at least $2^n$. A dense coefficient-list encoding therefore has superpolynomial output length relative to the input length $O(n^4)$.

almost everywhere. Thus any finite algebraic schedule represented by dense defining polynomials also requires a coefficient of degree $2^n$.

These are unconditional output-size lower bounds for the specified dense algebraic encoding. They do not classify computation in a compact expression encoding.
