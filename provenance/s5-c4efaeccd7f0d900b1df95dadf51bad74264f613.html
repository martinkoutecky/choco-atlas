Theorem 4.9 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(m\) houses, a true ranking \(t^\star\) with mass at least \(\alpha>0\), and truthful reports by the rest of society, choose a common report \(\rho\) for a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) from \(t^\star\). After moving that mass to \(\rho\), run PS with unit supply of each house and mass-weighted eating rates; return a report maximizing the cohort's per-agent allocation lexicographically according to \(t^\star\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) contain \(m\geq 1\) houses. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) strict complete rankings with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. The distribution \(\mu\) satisfies \(\sum_t\mu(t)=1\).
The input also includes a supported true ranking \(t^\star\) and a binary rational \(0<\alpha\leq\mu(t^\star)\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Choose a strict complete ranking \(\rho\), used by the entire cohort of mass \(\alpha\); all remaining mass reports truthfully. The reported distribution is \(\mu_\rho=\mu-\alpha\delta_{t^\star}+\alpha\delta_\rho\), where \(\delta_t\) denotes unit mass at ranking \(t\).
Mass-weighted probabilistic serial gives each house unit supply: every reporting group of mass \(w\) consumes its highest-ranked unexhausted house at aggregate rate \(w\). All groups start simultaneously; simultaneously exhausted houses are removed together; consumption continues until every house is exhausted, at time \(m\).
Let \(c_\rho(h)\) be the amount of house \(h\) consumed by the cohort and \(x_\rho(h)=c_\rho(h)/\alpha\) its per-agent allocation. These are consumption amounts, which may exceed \(1\). Return any report maximizing \(x_\rho\) lexicographically in the order \(t^\star\).

Is computing such a maximizing common report solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a finite society of \(n\) equally weighted agents, a single agent's downward-lexicographic best response is computable in \(O(nm^3)\) time ([verified result](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~tw/agmmnwaamas2015.pdf)). No complexity classification for the precise rational-mass cohort problem above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted distribution as a probability distribution with total society mass one.
  • Specified alpha as a binary rational input satisfying 0 < alpha <= mu(t-star), and L as the total input bit length.
  • Required the common report to be a strict complete ranking, as in the discrete best-response problem; any maximizing report is acceptable.
  • Specified mass-weighted rates as aggregate rate w for mass w, with simultaneous exhaustion handled simultaneously.
  • Preserved consumption until all supplies are exhausted, without a per-agent quota or a time-one cutoff; per-agent allocations are cohort consumption divided by alpha and can exceed one.

Status and new partial results

I do not settle the complexity classification for arbitrary rational masses.

The main new result is a polynomial-time characterization of joint full acquisition: for any specified set $S\subseteq H$, one can decide whether some common report gives the cohort every house in $S$ entirely, and construct such a report when it exists.

I also give a polynomial algorithm for a restricted next-coordinate optimization problem. The restriction is stated explicitly; its removal remains open.

1. Normalization and exact computation

Write the true order as
$$ h_1\succ h_2\succ\cdots\succ h_m. $$
Set $a=\alpha$, and discard zero residual masses from
$$ w_t=\mu(t)-a\mathbf 1_{\{t=t^\star\}}. $$
Index the remaining opponent groups by $j$, with weights $b_j>0$ and complete rankings $\pi_j$. Their total weight is
$$ b=\sum_j b_j=1-a. $$

Since $a>0$, lexicographically maximizing $x_\rho$ is equivalent to maximizing $c_\rho$. Every complete report satisfies
$$ 0\leq c_\rho(h)\leq 1, \qquad \sum_{h\in H}c_\rho(h)=am. $$
Indeed, the cohort consumes continuously at rate $a$ until time $m$, and each house has unit supply.

If $a=1$, the cohort consumes every house entirely under every report. Henceforth assume $a<1$, so $b>0$.

Exact simulation lemma

All PS simulations used below have polynomial bit complexity in $m,\tau,L$.

To prove this, let $Q$ be the product of the denominators of the input masses and $a$. Its bit length is $O(L)$. Write every group weight as $A_j/Q$. The numerators of active group weights sum to at most $Q$.

Maintain a common denominator $D$ for remaining supplies, elapsed time, and cohort consumption. Write these quantities as
$$ r_h=\frac{R_h}{D}, \qquad t=\frac{T}{D}, \qquad c_h=\frac{C_h}{D}. $$
Initially $D=1$, $R_h=1$, and $T=C_h=0$.

During a phase with fixed choices, let $S_h$ be the sum of weight numerators of groups consuming $h$. For $S_h>0$, its exhaustion delay is
$$ \Delta_h=\frac{Q R_h}{D S_h}. $$
Choose a minimizing house $g$. With $A_c/Q=a$, the updates are
$$ D'=D S_g, $$
$$ R'_h=S_gR_h-S_hR_g, $$
$$ T'=S_gT+Q R_g, $$
$$ C'_h=S_gC_h+ A_cR_g\mathbf 1_{\{\text{cohort currently consumes }h\}}. $$
All simultaneous minimizers are removed together. These formulas follow directly by multiplying each rate by the elapsed delay $\Delta_g$.

There are at most $m$ exhaustion phases, and $S_g\leq Q$. Thus
$$ D\leq Q^m. $$
Remaining supplies and cohort consumption are at most $1$. In opponent-only simulations, completion time is at most $m/b\leq mQ$, because $b$ is a positive integer multiple of $1/Q$. Consequently, all maintained integers have polynomial bit length.

The delayed-start simulations below introduce one additional event at a rational time $s=k/a$. Its denominator is at most $Q$. One may include this denominator in the initial $D$ and compare the next exhaustion with the activation event. Updating at activation introduces at most one additional factor $Q$ into the supply denominator. The same polynomial bit bound therefore applies.

The number of arithmetic operations is polynomial: there are polynomially many groups and houses, and each preference pointer skips each house at most once.

2. A comparison principle

The following elementary fact will be used to establish necessity in the full-acquisition characterization.

Lemma: additional supply removal cannot delay exhaustion

Consider two processes on the same finite house set, with the same initial supplies and the same opponent groups.

In the baseline process, only the opponents consume. In the augmented process, an additional eater may remove supply from available houses, while the opponents continue following their fixed rankings.

Every house exhausts no later in the augmented process than in the baseline process.

Proof. Induct over the distinct exhaustion times of the baseline process.

Suppose $g$ exhausts in the baseline at time $u$, but remains available in the augmented process at $u$. Whenever an opponent consumes $g$ in the baseline before $u$, every house it ranks above $g$ has already exhausted in the baseline. By induction, those houses have also exhausted in the augmented process by that time.

Since $g$ remains available throughout the augmented process up to $u$, that opponent must also consume $g$ there whenever it consumes $g$ in the baseline. The augmented process may additionally consume $g$ through its extra eater. Its cumulative consumption of $g$ by time $u$ is therefore at least the baseline consumption, contradicting the continued availability of $g$.

For houses exhausted simultaneously at $u$, this argument uses only exhaustion events strictly before an opponent starts consuming the house under consideration. Thus simultaneous events create no circularity. $\square$

3. Polynomial characterization of joint full acquisition

Call a set $S\subseteq H$ fully acquirable if some complete report satisfies
$$ c_\rho(h)=1\qquad\text{for every }h\in S. $$

Fix such a candidate set $S$, and write
$$ C=H\setminus S. $$

Run an opponent-only PS process on $C$: remove all houses in $S$ from every opponent ranking, and let the opponents consume the remaining unit-supply houses. For $g\in C$, let $E(g)$ be its exhaustion time.

For every opponent $j$ and house $h\in S$, define
$$ d_j(h)= \max\bigl(\{E(g):g\in C,\ g\succ_{\pi_j}h\}\cup\{0\}\bigr), $$
and put
$$ d(h)=\min_j d_j(h). $$

Thus $d_j(h)$ is the first time at which no available house in $C$ is ranked above $h$ by opponent $j$.

Theorem: full acquisition is an equal-length scheduling problem

Let $s=|S|$, and order its houses as $g_1,\ldots,g_s$ so that
$$ d(g_1)\leq d(g_2)\leq\cdots\leq d(g_s). $$
Then $S$ is fully acquirable if and only if
$$ \frac{i}{a}\leq d(g_i) \qquad \text{for every }i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}. $$

When these inequalities hold, the report beginning with
$$ g_1,g_2,\ldots,g_s $$
and followed by all other houses in any order acquires every house in $S$ entirely.

Proof of necessity. Suppose a complete report acquires every house in $S$ entirely. No opponent can consume a positive amount of any house in $S$: the cohort already consumes its entire unit supply.

Let $T_h$ be the exhaustion time of $h\in S$. Delete $S$ from the opponent rankings, and retain the cohort’s realized supply removals from $C$ as an additional eater. This reproduces the original process on $C$. Denote its exhaustion times by $E_A(g)$.

By the comparison lemma,
$$ E_A(g)\leq E(g)\qquad(g\in C). $$

For a fixed opponent $j$ and house $h\in S$, set
$$ d_{A,j}(h)= \max\bigl(\{E_A(g):g\in C,\ g\succ_{\pi_j}h\}\cup\{0\}\bigr). $$
We claim
$$ T_h\leq d_{A,j}(h). $$
Otherwise, during a positive interval after $d_{A,j}(h)$ and before $T_h$, house $h$ remains available and every house in $C$ preferred to $h$ has exhausted. Opponent $j$ must then consume an available house in $S$, either $h$ or a house it prefers to $h$. This contradicts the cohort’s full acquisition of every house in $S$.

Hence, for every $j$,
$$ T_h\leq d_{A,j}(h)\leq d_j(h), $$
and therefore
$$ T_h\leq d(h). $$

Let $p_1,\ldots,p_s$ be the order in which the cohort consumes houses in $S$. Consuming each such house takes exactly $1/a$ units of cohort time. Other houses consumed between them can only add time. Thus
$$ \frac{i}{a}\leq T_{p_i}\leq d(p_i). $$
So there exists an ordering meeting the deadlines when its houses occupy consecutive slots of length $1/a$.

Sorting by nondecreasing deadline preserves feasibility. Indeed, suppose adjacent houses $u,v$ have $d(u)>d(v)$ and are assigned consecutive completion times $t<t'$. Feasibility gives
$$ t'\leq d(v)<d(u). $$
After exchanging them, $v$ completes at $t\leq d(v)$ and $u$ completes at $t'\leq d(u)$. Repeated exchanges produce the claimed sorted ordering.

Proof of sufficiency. Suppose the displayed inequalities hold. Consider the proposed trajectory in which the cohort consumes $g_i$ exclusively during
$$ \left[\frac{i-1}{a},\frac{i}{a}\right], $$
while the opponents follow their opponent-only trajectory on $C$.

We verify that no opponent would choose an unexhausted house in $S$.

If $h=g_i$ remains available at a time $t$ before its scheduled completion, then
$$ t<\frac{i}{a}\leq d(h)\leq d_j(h) $$
for every opponent $j$. By the definition of $d_j(h)$, some house in $C$ preferred by $j$ to $h$ remains available in the opponent-only trajectory at $t$. Thus $j$’s favorite available house in $C$ is preferred to every currently available house in $S$.

Consequently, the proposed opponent choices are valid in the full process. Each cohort house is consumed exclusively at rate $a$ and exhausts at its scheduled completion time.

If a deadline equals a scheduled completion time, any simultaneous opponent switch occurs after the exhausted house is removed. It causes no positive opponent consumption of that house.

The proposed trajectory is therefore a valid PS trajectory through completion of $S$. Appending the other houses cannot alter these completed consumption amounts. $\square$

Computational consequence

The theorem gives a polynomial algorithm:

  • Simulate the opponents on $H\setminus S$.
  • Compute the deadlines $d(h)$.
  • Sort $S$ by deadline.
  • Check the stated inequalities.

This works for arbitrary permitted rational masses, with no integrality assumption on $b_j/a$.

In particular, allowing the cohort to consume houses outside $S$ before completing $S$ does not enlarge the collection of fully acquirable sets.

4. An exactly computable part of the lexicographic optimum

Let
$$ H_k=\{h_1,\ldots,h_k\}, \qquad H_0=\varnothing. $$
Using the preceding theorem, compute the largest $k$ for which $H_k$ is fully acquirable.

Every lexicographically maximizing report then satisfies
$$ c_\rho(h_i)=1\qquad(1\leq i\leq k). $$
To justify this, there exists a report with all these coordinates equal to $1$, and no coordinate can exceed $1$. Any report whose first difference from this prefix has a coordinate below $1$ is lexicographically worse.

If $k<m$, every maximizing report also satisfies
$$ c_\rho(h_{k+1})<1. $$
Otherwise, $H_{k+1}$ would be fully acquirable.

Thus the entire initial block of coordinates equal to $1$ is polynomial-time computable.

If $k=m-1$, this already settles the whole instance. Conservation forces
$$ c_\rho(h_m)=am-(m-1) $$
for every report acquiring $H_{m-1}$ entirely. Any report constructed by the theorem for $H_{m-1}$ is therefore lexicographically maximizing.

This supplies another polynomial-time subclass for arbitrary rational masses.

5. Exact optimization of one additional house under a stated restriction

The full-acquisition theorem extends to a useful restricted optimization problem.

Fix $S\subseteq H$ and $h\notin S$. Consider only complete reports whose first $|S|+1$ positions form a permutation of
$$ S\cup\{h\}. $$
Require every house in $S$ to be acquired entirely, and maximize $c_\rho(h)$.

The restriction excludes consumption of other houses before this leading block finishes.

Theorem: the restricted problem is polynomial-time solvable

Write $s=|S|$ and $C=H\setminus S$. For each
$$ k\in\{0,\ldots,s\}, $$
perform the following auxiliary simulation:

  • Opponents report their rankings restricted to $C$.
  • The cohort is inactive until time

$$ u_k=\frac{k}{a}. $$

  • At $u_k$, it consumes $h$ if $h$ remains available, continues until $h$ exhausts, and then stops.

Let its consumption of $h$ be $v_k$, and define
$$ \delta_k=\frac{v_k}{a}. $$
If $h$ has already exhausted at $u_k$, both quantities are zero.

Let $E_k(g)$ be the auxiliary exhaustion time of $g\in C$. Define deadlines for $z\in S$ by
$$ d_k(z)= \min_j \max\bigl(\{E_k(g):g\in C,\ g\succ_{\pi_j}z\}\cup\{0\}\bigr). $$
Sort $S$ as $z_1,\ldots,z_s$ by nondecreasing $d_k$. Call $k$ feasible if
$$ \frac{i}{a}+\delta_k\mathbf 1_{\{i>k\}} \leq d_k(z_i) \qquad(1\leq i\leq s). $$

The restricted optimum is
$$ \max_{\text{feasible }k}v_k. $$
For a feasible $k$, a realizing report begins with
$$ z_1,\ldots,z_k,h,z_{k+1},\ldots,z_s. $$

Proof. First consider any admissible restricted report acquiring all of $S$ entirely. Let $k$ be the number of houses from $S$ listed before $h$.

Those $k$ houses each take exactly $1/a$ time, so the cohort reaches $h$ at $u_k=k/a$. Opponents never consume houses in $S$. Therefore, until the leading block finishes, deleting $S$ from opponent rankings gives exactly the auxiliary process just defined. The cohort obtains $v_k$ from $h$ and spends $\delta_k$ time consuming it.

If $p_1,\ldots,p_s$ is the report order of houses in $S$, their completion times are consequently
$$ F_i=\frac{i}{a}+\delta_k\mathbf 1_{\{i>k\}}. $$

Every house $p_i$ must satisfy
$$ F_i\leq d_k(p_i). $$
Indeed, if an opponent’s deadline for that house occurred strictly before its completion, that opponent would consume an available house in $S$ during a positive interval. The auxiliary and actual trajectories coincide throughout that interval, so this contradicts full acquisition.

The completion times $F_i$ are increasing. The adjacent-exchange argument from the preceding theorem therefore shows that sorting $S$ by deadline also meets the same completion slots. Thus this $k$ passes the stated feasibility test.

Conversely, suppose $k$ passes the test. Use the auxiliary opponent trajectory and the proposed cohort sequence
$$ z_1,\ldots,z_k,h,z_{k+1},\ldots,z_s. $$
Before a scheduled house $z_i$ completes, its completion-time inequality guarantees that every opponent has an available house in $C$ it prefers to $z_i$. Hence opponents stay within $C$.

The cohort consequently consumes every scheduled house in $S$ exclusively. It reaches $h$ at $u_k$, obtains exactly the auxiliary amount $v_k$, and then resumes its exclusive consumption of $S$. This constructs a valid PS trajectory through completion of the leading block.

At that point all houses in $S\cup\{h\}$ have exhausted. Appending the remaining houses preserves their cohort consumption amounts.

There are $s+1$ auxiliary simulations, and their arithmetic and deadline calculations have polynomial complexity. $\square$

What this does—and does not—establish

If $S=H_k$ is the fully acquirable initial true-preference block from Section 4 and $h=h_{k+1}$, this theorem constructs a report preserving that entire block and attaining a rigorously computed value of the next coordinate.

That value is a lower bound on the unrestricted next-coordinate optimum.

I have not proved that an unrestricted optimum always has a leading block consisting only of $S\cup\{h\}$. Without that normal-form statement, the restricted algorithm is not a solution of the general task.

6. Completing partial lists and an upper complexity bound

Completion lemma

Suppose a partial cohort list $P$ is simulated with the cohort stopping after its list is exhausted. Append all missing houses in any order to obtain a complete report $\widehat P$.

The two processes agree until the partial-list cohort stops. At that time every house in $P$ has exhausted. Therefore
$$ c_{\widehat P}(h)=c_P(h)\qquad(h\in P), $$
while
$$ c_{\widehat P}(h)\geq c_P(h)=0\qquad(h\notin P). $$

In particular, if $P$ is lexicographically optimal among all partial lists on $H$, its completion has the same optimal vector: a strict improvement by completion would contradict partial-list optimality.

This supplies the missing completion justification for a partial list returned by the single-agent construction in [Section 4 of Aziz et al.](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~tw/agmmnwaamas2015.pdf). It does not establish that the construction applies to arbitrary cohort and opponent speeds.

General oracle upper bound

For completeness, exact evaluation also yields
$$ \text{the report-finding problem belongs to }\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}. $$

Here is a self-contained argument. The simulation in Section 1 shows that every complete-report coordinate $c_\rho(h)$ has reduced denominator at most $Q^m$. Distinct attainable coordinate values therefore differ by at least
$$ Q^{-2m}. $$

Choose an integer
$$ B\geq\max\{1,\lceil\log_2 Q\rceil\}, \qquad K=2mB+1. $$
Define
$$ z_\rho(h)=\left\lfloor 2^Kc_\rho(h)\right\rfloor. $$
Because $2^K>Q^{2m}$, this transformation preserves equality and strict order among attainable coordinate values.

Each transformed coordinate fits in $K+1$ bits. Concatenate these fixed-width strings in true-ranking order to form an integer $Z_\rho$. Its bit length is polynomial, and maximizing it is equivalent to the required lexicographic maximization.

For any threshold $z$, the predicate
$$ \exists\rho:\ Z_\rho\geq z $$
belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$: a permutation is a polynomial-length certificate, and exact simulation verifies it in polynomial time.

Binary search with this oracle determines the maximum $Z_\rho$. A maximizing permutation can then be recovered position by position using $\mathrm{NP}$ queries asking whether a proposed prefix has a completion attaining that maximum. Both procedures use polynomially many queries.

This upper bound does not imply either polynomial-time solvability or NP-hardness.

7. Remaining open points

The joint acquisition of any specified set of entire houses is now polynomial-time decidable. The unresolved difficulty is preserving previously optimized coordinates while maximizing partially acquired houses.

Two concrete gaps remain:

  • The restricted next-coordinate theorem needs a proof that its report restriction preserves the unrestricted optimum, or a counterexample identifying how outside houses can help.
  • Even such a result for an initial block of entire houses would leave the continuation after a partially acquired coordinate to be analyzed.

No polynomial algorithm for the general input, and no NP-hardness reduction, has been proved here.
