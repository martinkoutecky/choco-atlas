The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(\delta<1/2\) and a finite-support prediction distribution \(\nu\) on rational locations with rational masses, compute a location \(y\) from \(\nu\) and \(\delta\) such that, for every finite-support joint distribution \(\pi\) over true and predicted locations \((x,z)\) whose \(z\)-marginal is \(\nu\) and whose mass on \(x\ne z\) is at most \(\delta\), \(\int |x-y|\,d\mu(x)\le\left(1+\frac{4\delta}{1-2\delta}\right)\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\int |x-w|\,d\mu(x)\), where \(\mu\) is the \(x\)-marginal of \(\pi\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here types are locations, masses sum to \(1\), and the ambient space is \(\mathbb R\) with absolute distance.
The input is a prediction distribution \(\nu\) supported on rational locations and a binary rational \(\delta\in[0,1/2)\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; the ambient dimension is \(m=1\).
An admissible corruption is any finitely supported probability distribution \(\pi\) on \(\mathbb R\times\mathbb Q\) with predicted-location marginal \(\nu\) and \(\pi\{(x,z):x\ne z\}\le\delta\). Write \(\mu\) for its true-location marginal. Corruptions may split an atom's mass and have arbitrary dependence and unbounded displacement.
For a location \(y\), define \(C_\mu(y)=\int|x-y|\,d\mu(x)\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{w\in\mathbb R}C_\mu(w)\). The output is a binary rational location chosen solely from \(\nu\) and \(\delta\); any location satisfying the guarantee is acceptable.

Can a deterministic algorithm, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\) with \(m=1\), always output \(y\) such that \(C_\mu(y)\le\left(1+\frac{4\delta}{1-2\delta}\right)\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\) simultaneously for every admissible corruption, or is this approximation guarantee unattainable in polynomial time?

For finite lists of agents, a median of the predicted locations guarantees the same factor whenever at most a \(\delta\)-fraction of predictions are incorrect. [Verified discrete result](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2024/file/ea2e5f8777105309a900d30dc4898095-Paper-Conference.pdf).

Result

The guarantee is attainable in deterministic polynomial time. Output a weighted median of the prediction distribution. The proof below permits arbitrary mass splitting, dependence, and displacement.

Here, “binary rational” means a rational number whose numerator and denominator are encoded in binary.

1. Algorithm and predicted optimality

Write the prediction distribution as \(\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\delta_{z_i}\), where the distinct locations are sorted as \(z_1<\cdots<z_\tau\), each \(p_i>0\), and \(\sum_i p_i=1\). Here \(\delta_{z_i}\) denotes unit mass at \(z_i\).

Choose
\(j=\min\{k:\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_i\ge 1/2\}\)
and output \(y=z_j\).

This choice satisfies
\(\nu((-\infty,y])\ge 1/2\)
and
\(\nu([y,\infty))\ge 1/2\):
the first follows from the definition of \(j\), and the second follows because \(\sum_{i<j}p_i<1/2\).

We verify that \(y\) minimizes predicted cost. If \(w>y\), put \(t=w-y\). For every \(x\le y\),
\(|x-w|-|x-y|=t\).
For every other \(x\), the reverse triangle inequality gives
\(|x-w|-|x-y|\ge-t\).
Consequently,
\(C_\nu(w)-C_\nu(y)\ge(2\nu((-\infty,y])-1)t\ge0\).

If \(w<y\), put \(t=y-w\). The same argument, using \(x\ge y\), gives
\(C_\nu(w)-C_\nu(y)\ge(2\nu([y,\infty))-1)t\ge0\).
Equality is immediate when \(w=y\). Thus \(C_\nu(y)\le C_\nu(w)\) for every \(w\in\mathbb R\).

2. Decomposing an arbitrary corruption

Fix any admissible corruption \(\pi\), and let
\(\varepsilon=\pi\{(x,z):x\ne z\}\le\delta<1/2\).

For every Borel set \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\), define three nonnegative measures:
\(G(A)=\pi\{(x,z):x=z,\ x\in A\}\),
\(B(A)=\pi\{(x,z):x\ne z,\ x\in A\}\), and
\(H(A)=\pi\{(x,z):x\ne z,\ z\in A\}\).

They satisfy
\(\mu=G+B\), \(\nu=G+H\),
\(G(\mathbb R)=1-\varepsilon\), and
\(B(\mathbb R)=H(\mathbb R)=\varepsilon\).

Thus \(G\) is the common mass supplied by correct predictions. These identities hold even when an individual predicted atom is split among several true locations.

For any of these finite measures \(\rho\), write
\(C_\rho(a)=\int|x-a|\,d\rho(x)\).
All such costs are finite because \(\pi\) has finite support. The reverse triangle inequality implies, for any \(a,b\in\mathbb R\),
\[ \]
\[ |C_\rho(a)-C_\rho(b)|\le\rho(\mathbb R)|a-b|. \tag{1} \]

The displayed inequality follows by integrating the pointwise bound
\(\bigl||x-a|-|x-b|\bigr|\le|a-b|\).

3. Approximation guarantee

A true optimum \(w\) exists. Indeed, finite support makes \(C_\mu\) continuous, and
\(C_\mu(u)\ge |u|-\int|x|\,d\mu(x)\);
hence \(C_\mu(u)\) tends to infinity as \(|u|\) does. Fix a minimizer \(w\), so \(C_\mu(w)=\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\), and put \(d=|y-w|\).

Predicted optimality and \(\nu=G+H\) give
\(C_G(y)+C_H(y)\le C_G(w)+C_H(w)\).
Applying (1) to \(H\), whose mass is \(\varepsilon\), yields
\(C_G(y)\le C_G(w)+\varepsilon d\).

On the other hand, the triangle inequality gives
\(|x-y|+|x-w|\ge d\).
Integrating against \(G\) therefore gives
\((1-\varepsilon)d\le C_G(y)+C_G(w)\le2C_G(w)+\varepsilon d\).

Subtracting \(\varepsilon d\) and using \(C_G(w)\le C_\mu(w)\), we obtain
\((1-2\varepsilon)d\le2\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\).
Because \(\varepsilon<1/2\), this implies
\(d\le 2\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)/(1-2\varepsilon)\).

Now use \(\mu=G+B\). The bound on \(C_G(y)-C_G(w)\) above and (1) applied to \(B\) imply
\(C_\mu(y)-C_\mu(w)\le\varepsilon d+\varepsilon d=2\varepsilon d\).
Consequently,
\(C_\mu(y)\le\left(1+\frac{4\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\right)\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\).

Since \(0\le\varepsilon\le\delta<1/2\), cross-multiplication by positive denominators gives
\(\frac{\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\le\frac{\delta}{1-2\delta}\).
Therefore
\(C_\mu(y)\le\left(1+\frac{4\delta}{1-2\delta}\right)\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\).

No step divides by \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\), so the proof also covers zero optimal cost. Since \(\pi\) was arbitrary and \(y\) depends only on \(\nu\), the guarantee holds simultaneously for every admissible corruption.

This directly proves the distributional analogue of the discrete robustness statement in [Corollary 3 of the cited paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2024/file/ea2e5f8777105309a900d30dc4898095-Paper-Conference.pdf).

4. Bit complexity

Sorting uses \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) exact rational comparisons. Each comparison uses integer cross-products of polynomial bit length.

For exact cumulative masses, write \(p_i=a_i/b_i\) with positive integer denominators and take \(Q=\prod_i b_i\). Its bit length is \(O(L)\), since the sum of the denominator encoding lengths is at most \(L\). Each mass becomes the integer weight \(a_i(Q/b_i)\) over denominator \(Q\). All partial sums are between zero and \(Q\), so they also have \(O(L)\) bits.

Finding the first partial sum \(S\) satisfying \(2S\ge Q\) therefore takes polynomial time. The output is an input location, so its encoding length is at most \(L\). The entire algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\).
