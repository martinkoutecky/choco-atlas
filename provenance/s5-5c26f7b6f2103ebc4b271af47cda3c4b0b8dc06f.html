The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite rational candidate set \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported types \(t=(x_t,p_t)\), where \(p_t\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\), does weighted Conditional-Median, using a fixed clone-compatible weighted-median rule, satisfy \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 11\min_{y_1,y_2\in C,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y_1,y_2)\), where \(c_t(y_1,y_2)=\max_{j:p_{tj}=1}|x_t-y_j|\) and \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y_1,y_2)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(y_1,y_2)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\subset\mathbb Q\) contain \(m\ge2\) distinct candidate locations for two labelled facilities. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(t=(x_t,p_t)\), with \(x_t\in\mathbb Q\), \(p_t\in\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}\), and positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Write \(N_j=\{t:p_{tj}=1\}\) and \(\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\); assume \(\mu(N_1),\mu(N_2)>0\). Feasible outcomes are \(y=(y_1,y_2)\in C^2\) with \(y_1\ne y_2\), and \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_{j:p_{tj}=1}|x_t-y_j|\).
For each nonempty type subset \(S\), let \(q(S)\) be its lower weighted median position, using the masses restricted to \(S\). This fixed rule is unchanged by splitting a mass into identical types or scaling all masses equally. Let \(a_D(x)\) denote the nearest candidate to \(x\) in \(D\), breaking distance ties toward the smaller coordinate.
Weighted Conditional-Median selects \(h\in\{1,2\}\) maximizing \(\mu(N_h)\), breaking ties toward \(1\), and puts \(\ell=3-h\).
If \(\mu(N_h\setminus N_\ell)\ge\mu(N_1\cap N_2)\), set \(w_h=a_C(q(N_h\setminus N_\ell))\) and \(w_\ell=a_{C\setminus\{w_h\}}(q(N_\ell))\).
Otherwise set \(w_h=a_C(q(N_1\cap N_2))\) and \(w_\ell=a_{C\setminus\{w_h\}}(q(N_1\cap N_2))\). Denote this outcome by \(M(\mu)\).

Does \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le11\min_{y_1,y_2\in C,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y_1,y_2)\) hold for every such rational-mass society, or is there a counterexample?

Conclusion

Yes. Every rational-mass society in the question satisfies
\(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 11\min_{y\in C^2,\;y_1\ne y_2}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)\).

The proof is an exact reduction to the stated finite unweighted guarantee.

1. The unweighted result and tie conventions

The unweighted result says that Conditional-Median produces an outcome \(w\) satisfying \(\mathrm{SC}(w)\le 11\mathrm{SC}(o)\) for an optimal feasible outcome \(o\). Its domain allows coincident agent positions and requires distinct facility locations, as here. See [Lotfi and Voudouris, Theorem 2.2](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37432/1/1-s2.0-S0167637723002018-main.pdf).

The lower-median and smaller-coordinate conventions are admissible choices in that theorem. Its proof uses the median’s distance-minimizing property and the presence of at least half the agents weakly on either side; both hold for the lower median. Candidate choices enter through weak nearest-distance inequalities, which hold with the prescribed tie-breaking. Its facility-order convention also gives priority to facility \(1\) when approval counts tie.

Thus the stated unweighted guarantee applies with exactly the conventions needed below.

2. Construct an unweighted society

Write each positive rational mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(a_t,b_t\) are positive integers. Define
\(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t\) and \(k_t=D\mu_t\).

Each \(k_t\) is a positive integer. Since the masses sum to \(1\),
\(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}k_t=D\).

Construct a finite unweighted society by replacing type \(t\) with \(k_t\) distinct agents, all having position \(x_t\) and approval vector \(p_t\). Keep the candidate set \(C\) unchanged.

For a type subset \(S\), let \(\widehat S\) denote all its replicated agents. Then
\(|\widehat S|=\sum_{t\in S}k_t=D\mu(S)\).

Consequently, every comparison of approval masses in the weighted mechanism agrees exactly with the corresponding comparison of approval counts in the replicated society. This includes equality cases, so both mechanisms choose the same first facility and the same branch.

The required median sets are nonempty. Indeed, in the first branch, if \(\mu(N_h\setminus N_\ell)=0\), its branch inequality forces \(\mu(N_1\cap N_2)=0\), contradicting \(\mu(N_h)>0\). In the second branch, the strict reversed inequality forces \(\mu(N_1\cap N_2)>0\).

3. Medians and outcomes agree exactly

For a nonempty subset \(S\), its lower weighted median is the smallest supported position \(z\) satisfying
\(\sum_{t\in S:\,x_t\le z}\mu_t\ge \mu(S)/2\).

Multiplication by \(D\) makes this condition equivalent to
\(\sum_{t\in S:\,x_t\le z}k_t\ge |\widehat S|/2\).

The left-hand side counts replicated agents at or to the left of \(z\). Therefore the smallest position satisfying it is precisely the lower unweighted median of \(\widehat S\), namely the position at sorted rank \(\lceil|\widehat S|/2\rceil\).

Thus every median used by the two mechanisms has the same position. Their candidate sets and candidate tie-breaking also agree. After the first facility is placed, both exclude the same candidate when placing the second. Hence
\(\operatorname{CM}(\widehat{\mathcal S})=M(\mu)\).

4. Costs scale uniformly

For every feasible labelled outcome \(y\), the replicated society has social cost
\(\mathrm{SC}_{\widehat{\mathcal S}}(y)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}k_t\max_{j:p_{tj}=1}|x_t-y_j|=D\,\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)\).

The feasible outcome set is identical in the two societies. It is finite and nonempty because \(m\ge 2\). Uniform scaling therefore also gives
\(\min_y\mathrm{SC}_{\widehat{\mathcal S}}(y)=D\min_y\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)\).

Applying the unweighted guarantee and the equality of mechanism outcomes yields
\(D\,\mathrm{SC}_\mu(M(\mu))\le 11D\min_y\mathrm{SC}_\mu(y)\).

Since \(D>0\), division by \(D\) proves the claimed inequality. This argument also covers an optimal social cost of zero.

5. Running time on the support representation

Replication is a proof construction; its number of agents can be exponential in \(L\). It need not be performed.

To compute \(M(\mu)\), sort the \(\tau\) support entries by position, compute the approval masses, and find the required medians by cumulative-mass scans. Two scans of the \(m\) candidates suffice for the placements.

With schoolbook exact integer arithmetic, this takes
\(O((\tau\log(\tau+1)+m)L^2)\) bit operations. Indeed, denominators can be cleared using the same \(D\), whose bit length is \(O(L)\); all required counts and coordinate comparisons also use \(O(L)\)-bit integers.

If the optimal cost is also required, enumeration of the \(m(m-1)\) feasible outcomes takes \(O(m^2\tau L^2)\) bit operations.
