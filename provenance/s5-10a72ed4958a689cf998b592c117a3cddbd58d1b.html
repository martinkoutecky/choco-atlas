A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct types with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\); unlisted types have mass \(0\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Decide whether there exists \(c\in[m]\) such that every \(\sigma\in S_m\) with \(\sigma\mu=\mu\) satisfies \(\sigma(c)=c\); return any such candidate when one exists.

Let $D$ denote the decision problem in the question. The following results are proved below:

$$ \mathrm{GI}\leq_T^p D\leq_T^p\mathrm{GI}, \qquad D\in\mathrm{coNP}. $$

Consequently,

$$ D\in\mathrm{P} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{GI}\in\mathrm{P}. $$

These results do not settle the requested choice between polynomial-time solvability and $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

The additional progress beyond the verified notes is a stronger lower bound: the problem remains $\mathrm{GI}$-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions even when every alternative occurs, the support graph is connected with diameter at most $3$, and there are exactly two distinct dyadic masses. Thus the difficulty persists under substantial restrictions on the distribution.

Throughout, $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness means hardness under polynomial-time many-one reductions unless stated otherwise.

1. Distributions as weighted graphs

For a valid input $\mu$, define the weighted simple graph $G_\mu$ on $[m]$ by placing an edge on every supported pair $A$, with weight $\mu(A)$. There are exactly $\tau$ edges.

For any permutation $\sigma$,

$$ \sigma\mu=\mu \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu(\sigma(A))=\mu(A) \text{ for every two-element subset }A\subseteq[m]. $$

Indeed, substitute $\sigma(A)$ into the defining equality
$(\sigma\mu)(B)=\mu(\sigma^{-1}(B))$. Hence distribution-preserving permutations are precisely the weight-preserving automorphisms of $G_\mu$. A valid candidate is a vertex fixed by every such automorphism.

Let $V$ be the set of endpoints of supported pairs, and set

$$ n=|V|, \qquad z=m-n. $$

Then $n\leq 2\tau$. Vertices in $V$ have positive degree, whereas the other $z$ vertices have degree zero. Therefore every automorphism preserves $V$, and every weighted automorphism of $G_\mu[V]$ extends independently by an arbitrary permutation of the unsupported alternatives.

It follows that:

  • If $z=1$, the unique unsupported alternative is a valid candidate.
  • If $z\geq2$, each unsupported alternative can be moved by a transposition.
  • An active alternative is a valid candidate exactly when every weighted automorphism of $G_\mu[V]$ fixes it.

No enumeration of all $m$ alternatives is necessary. When $z=1$, the missing label is

$$ c=\frac{m(m+1)}2-\sum_{v\in V}v. $$

All integers in this computation have $O(\log(m+2))$ bits.

2. Reduction to ordinary graph isomorphism

We prove an algorithm for both decision and candidate selection using polynomially many ordinary graph-isomorphism queries.

2.1 Encoding exact masses

Partition the supported edges into their $q\leq\tau$ exact mass-equality classes. For binary-encoded rationals $a/b$ and $c/d$, equality is decided by comparing $ad$ and $bc$. Thus the partition can be computed in time polynomial in $L+\tau$.

Construct a vertex-colored incidence graph $B$:

  • Each vertex of $V$ becomes a vertex of one common alternative color.
  • Each supported pair becomes a new edge vertex, colored by its mass-equality class.
  • An edge vertex is adjacent to its two endpoints.

The alternative color differs from every edge color. The graph has

$$ s=n+\tau\leq3\tau $$

vertices.

Every weighted automorphism of $G_\mu[V]$ extends to a color-preserving automorphism of $B$: map an edge vertex to the vertex representing the image pair. Conversely, a color-preserving automorphism of $B$ preserves alternative vertices, edge-weight classes, and incidence, so its restriction is a weighted automorphism of $G_\mu[V]$.

For $u\in V$, let $B_u$ be obtained by recoloring $u$ with a fresh distinguished color. Use the same distinguished color in every compared graph. Then

$$ B_u\cong B_v \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{some weighted automorphism of }G_\mu[V]\text{ maps }u\text{ to }v, $$

where isomorphism on the left preserves colors.

For the forward implication, an isomorphism must map the uniquely distinguished vertex $u$ to $v$. Forgetting that distinguished color gives an automorphism of $B$, since both vertices originally had the alternative color. The reverse implication follows by extending a weighted automorphism to $B$.

Thus $u$ is a valid candidate precisely when all tests against $v\in V\setminus\{u\}$ return NO. At most $n(n-1)$ queries suffice.

2.2 Removing colors

Consider two colored simple graphs with $s$ vertices and common color labels $1,\ldots,k$. At each original vertex of color $i$, attach $(s+1)i$ new leaves.

All new vertices have degree one; every original vertex has degree at least $s+1$. Moreover, an original vertex of color $i$ has degree in

$$ [(s+1)i,\ (s+1)i+s-1]. $$

These intervals are disjoint. Therefore an uncolored isomorphism preserves original vertices and their colors. Its restriction is a color-preserving isomorphism. Conversely, a color-preserving isomorphism extends by bijectively matching the attached leaves.

The resulting graph has at most

$$ s+s(s+1)k $$

vertices. Here $k\leq\tau+2$, so every ordinary GI query has $O((\tau+1)^3)$ vertices. This proves

$$ D\leq_T^p\mathrm{GI}. $$

2.3 Running time

Let $T_{\mathrm{GI}}(N)$ bound the time for ordinary graph isomorphism on graphs with at most $N$ vertices. For an absolute constant $C$, the preceding algorithm takes

$$ (L+\tau+\log(m+2))^{O(1)} + O(\tau^2)\, T_{\mathrm{GI}}\!\left(C(\tau+1)^3\right). $$

The polynomial term includes exact arithmetic and construction of all queries. This states the dependence on $m,\tau,L$ and, in particular, avoids expansion of either the alternatives outside the support or the rational masses into voters.

Babai’s theorem gives an ordinary GI algorithm with running time $\exp((\log(N+2))^{O(1)})$. Its hypotheses apply to the uncolored simple graphs just constructed. Consequently, decision and candidate selection take time

$$ (L+\tau+\log(m+2))^{O(1)} + \exp((\log(\tau+2))^{O(1)}). $$

The external theorem used here is [Babai, *Graph Isomorphism in Quasipolynomial Time*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03547).

3. Stronger hardness: two dyadic masses and connected support

We construct a polynomial-time many-one reduction from graph nonisomorphism to $D$. The output always has connected support of diameter at most $3$, all alternatives are active, and exactly two distinct dyadic masses occur.

Negating the answer then yields a polynomial-time Turing reduction from GI.

3.1 Padding the source graphs

First suppose the input graphs $G,H$ each have $n_0\geq1$ vertices.

Let $n$ be the least power of two with $n\geq n_0$. Add $n-n_0$ isolated vertices to each graph, obtaining $\widehat G,\widehat H$. Then

$$ n<2n_0, \qquad G\cong H \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \widehat G\cong\widehat H. $$

The forward implication is immediate by mapping the added isolates.

For the reverse implication, an isomorphism of the padded graphs restricts to an isomorphism between their induced graphs on positive-degree vertices. The original graphs therefore have isomorphic positive-degree parts with the same number of vertices. Because both original graphs have order $n_0$, they also have the same number of original isolated vertices. Extending the isomorphism by matching those isolates gives $G\cong H$.

Write

$$ E=|E(\widehat G)|+|E(\widehat H)|. $$

3.2 Choosing two masses

Define

$$ B=4n+4E+1. $$

Choose the least power of two $Q$ satisfying

$$ Q\geq B+2n+1. $$

Set

$$ a=Q-B, \qquad b=2n, \qquad P=2nQ. $$

These numbers satisfy

$$ a\geq2n+1>b>0. $$

Since $n$ and $Q$ are powers of two, $P$ is also a power of two. Thus $a/P$ and $b/P$ are distinct positive dyadic masses.

The choice of a power-of-two order is what permits normalization using only these two masses.

3.3 The support graph

For each $K\in\{\widehat G,\widehat H\}$, construct a separate rooted graph $C(K)$:

  • Introduce a root $r_K$.
  • Replace every vertex $v$ of $K$ by two vertices $v_0,v_1$, joined by an edge of integer weight $a$.
  • Join $r_K$ to both members of every pair by edges of integer weight $b$.
  • For every edge $\{u,v\}$ of $K$, place all four edges between $\{u_0,u_1\}$ and $\{v_0,v_1\}$, each with integer weight $b$.

Finally, join $r_{\widehat G}$ and $r_{\widehat H}$ by one edge of integer weight $b$.

Divide every integer edge weight by $P$. All pairs used are distinct. There are

$$ m=4n+2 $$

alternatives and

$$ \tau=6n+4E+1 $$

supported pairs.

There are $2n$ edges of weight $a$ and $B$ edges of weight $b$. Therefore the total integer weight is

$$ 2na+Bb = 2n(Q-B)+B(2n) = 2nQ = P. $$

The masses consequently sum exactly to one.

Every alternative has positive degree. The graph is connected: within each half, the root is adjacent to every other vertex, and the roots are adjacent. Any two vertices in the same half have distance at most $2$; vertices in different halves have distance at most $3$.

Both masses occur: every represented vertex contributes an $a$-edge, and root edges supply $b$-edges.

Finally, $B=O(n+E)$ and the least admissible power of two satisfies

$$ Q<2(B+2n+1). $$

Hence all labels and masses have $O(\log(n+E+2))$ bits. The output length is

$$ O((n+E)\log(n+E+2)), $$

so the construction is polynomial-time.

3.4 Every nonroot vertex can move

For each represented vertex $v$, the transposition of $v_0,v_1$ fixing all other vertices is a weighted automorphism.

Their mutual edge has weight $a$. Outside that pair, both vertices have the same root neighbor and the same neighbors arising from original graph edges, all with weight $b$. Swapping them therefore preserves every edge and its weight.

Thus no nonroot vertex is fixed by every automorphism. Only the two roots can be valid candidates.

3.5 Root interchange characterizes source isomorphism

The roots are exactly the vertices not incident to an edge of mass $a/P$. Every nonroot vertex belongs to one such edge. Therefore every weighted automorphism preserves the two-element root set.

Suppose an automorphism interchanges the roots. The root-to-root edge is then preserved setwise. Delete that edge. The resulting graph has exactly two connected components, namely $C(\widehat G)$ and $C(\widehat H)$, because each root is adjacent to every nonroot vertex in its half. The automorphism restricts to a weighted isomorphism

$$ C(\widehat G)\cong C(\widehat H). $$

Within either component, the edges of mass $a/P$ identify precisely the pairs representing original vertices. The isomorphism therefore induces a bijection between the original vertex sets. For two distinct pairs, edges between them exist exactly when their corresponding original vertices are adjacent. Consequently, the induced bijection is an isomorphism

$$ \widehat G\cong\widehat H. $$

Conversely, an isomorphism $f:\widehat G\to\widehat H$ extends to a weighted component isomorphism by sending the root to the root and $v_i$ to $f(v)_i$ for $i\in\{0,1\}$. Use this map on the first half and its inverse on the second. Their union preserves the root-to-root edge and gives an automorphism interchanging the roots.

We have proved

$$ \text{an automorphism interchanges the roots} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\cong H. $$

If $G\not\cong H$, the two roots cannot be interchanged. Since their set is invariant, both are fixed by every automorphism, and the society answers YES.

If $G\cong H$, an automorphism moves both roots, and the pair transpositions move every nonroot vertex. The society answers NO.

Thus

$$ D(\mu_{G,H})=\mathrm{YES} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\not\cong H. $$

3.6 Exceptional source inputs

If the graph orders differ, they are nonisomorphic. Output a path on three alternatives with edge masses $1/4$ and $3/4$. Its middle vertex is fixed by every automorphism, so this is a YES instance satisfying all the stated restrictions.

If both graphs have order zero, replace them by two one-vertex edgeless graphs and apply the construction. They are isomorphic, so the output is a NO instance satisfying the restrictions.

The reduction therefore covers arbitrary GI inputs. Together with Section 2, it proves $\mathrm{GI}$-completeness under polynomial-time Turing reductions, including the claimed restricted hardness.

4. Membership in $\mathrm{coNP}$

A NO instance means that every alternative can be moved by some distribution-preserving permutation. Different alternatives may require different permutations.

Compute $V,n,z$ as in Section 1. If $z=1$, the instance is necessarily YES, so a verifier for NO certificates rejects.

Otherwise, a certificate supplies, for every $v\in V$, a permutation $\pi_v$ of $V$ such that

$$ \pi_v(v)\neq v $$

and $\pi_v$ preserves every supported pair and its exact mass.

Indexing $V$ by $[n]$, these $n$ permutations require $O(n^2\log(n+2))$ bits. Since $n\leq2\tau$, the certificate has polynomial length.

The verifier checks that each supplied map is a permutation, moves its designated vertex, and maps every supported pair to a supported pair of identical mass. These checks take polynomial time in $L+\tau+\log(m+2)$.

Preserving the supported pairs suffices also to preserve nonedges: a vertex permutation acts bijectively on all pairs, so its injective image of the finite support, contained in that support, must equal the support.

For soundness, each verified permutation extends to the full domain by fixing unsupported alternatives, so every active vertex can move. If $z\geq2$, each unsupported vertex can move by an isolated-vertex transposition. Hence no valid candidate exists.

For completeness, in any NO instance, each active vertex is moved by some full automorphism. Its restriction to $V$ supplies the required permutation.

Therefore,

$$ D\in\mathrm{coNP}. $$

If $D$ were $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, closure of $\mathrm{coNP}$ under such reductions would imply

$$ \mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{coNP}. $$

Taking complements gives the reverse inclusion, and hence

$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

This is a conditional consequence, not a proof that $D$ is not $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.

5. A polynomial-time special case: all masses distinct

Suppose all $\tau$ supported masses are pairwise distinct. Then every supported edge is preserved setwise by every weighted automorphism.

If a vertex $v$ has degree at least $2$, choose two distinct incident edges. Their intersection is $\{v\}$, so every automorphism fixes $v$.

If a connected component has at least three vertices, each degree-one vertex has a neighbor of degree at least $2$. That neighbor is fixed, and their edge is preserved setwise, so the degree-one vertex is also fixed. Thus every vertex of such a component is fixed.

Conversely, in a component consisting of one edge, exchanging its endpoints is an automorphism. If the active graph is a matching, no active candidate exists.

Consequently, under the distinct-mass hypothesis, the instance is YES exactly when

$$ z=1 \quad\text{or}\quad G_\mu[V]\text{ has a vertex of degree at least }2. $$

The unique unsupported vertex or any vertex of degree at least $2$ supplies a candidate. Exact mass comparisons and degree computation give a polynomial-time algorithm in $L+\tau+\log(m+2)$.
