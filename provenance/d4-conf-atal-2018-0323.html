The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an initial setting \(\alpha\) and a finite-support distribution of rational masses over types \(z=(x_z,A_z)\), where \(x_z\in[0,1]\) and \(A_z\subseteq[H]\), find a schedule \(y_1,\ldots,y_H\) minimizing \(\sum_{t=1}^{H}|y_t-y_{t-1}|\), with \(y_0=\alpha\), subject to \(\min_{z:t\in A_z,\,\mu_z>0}x_z\le y_t\le\max_{z:t\in A_z,\,\mu_z>0}x_z\) for every period with active positive mass; periods with none impose no constraint.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a positive integer horizon \(H\), let \([H]=\{1,\ldots,H\}\). The facility domain is \([0,1]\), with spatial dimension \(m=1\), and the initial location is a binary rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(z=(x_z,A_z)\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_z\) summing to \(1\). Here \(x_z\in[0,1]\) is a binary rational and \(A_z\subseteq[H]\) is encoded by an \(H\)-bit vector; active sets may be empty or nonconsecutive. The parameter \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
A feasible schedule has \(y_0=\alpha\) and \(y_t\in[0,1]\) for every \(t\in[H]\). Whenever \(S_t=\{z:t\in A_z\}\) is nonempty, require \(\min_{z\in S_t}x_z\le y_t\le\max_{z\in S_t}x_z\); an empty \(S_t\) imposes no additional constraint.
Minimize the replacement cost \(\sum_{t=1}^{H}|y_t-y_{t-1}|\). Any minimizing schedule is acceptable; there is no terminal-location requirement. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including a binary rational threshold \(B\ge0\), and state running times in \(m,\tau,H,L\).

Is deciding whether a feasible schedule has replacement cost at most \(B\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite populations, an online rule computable in polynomial time minimizes replacement cost over all schedules satisfying these periodwise interval constraints, including offline schedules. [Wada et al., 2018](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2018/pdfs/p336.pdf).

Conclusion

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). An optimal schedule is obtained by projecting the previous location onto the current feasible interval. This works directly on the support list, regardless of the masses or the shape of the active sets.

The rule agrees with the dynamic target rule of [Wada et al. (2018)](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2018/pdfs/p336.pdf). The proof below establishes its optimality for the precise input and constraints in the question.

Algorithm

For each \(t\in[H]\), define the nonempty closed interval
\[ I_t=[\ell_t,u_t] = \begin{cases} [\min_{z\in S_t}x_z,\max_{z\in S_t}x_z],&S_t\ne\varnothing,\\ [0,1],&S_t=\varnothing. \end{cases} \]

Set \(g_0=\alpha\), and recursively compute
\[ g_t=\min\{u_t,\max\{\ell_t,g_{t-1}\}\}. \]

Thus \(g_t\) is the projection of \(g_{t-1}\) onto \(I_t\). In particular, \(g_t\in I_t\), so the resulting schedule is feasible. Return yes precisely when
\[ G_H:=\sum_{t=1}^{H}|g_t-g_{t-1}|\le B. \]

The masses do not enter this computation: the stated constraints depend only on which support types are active and their locations.

Proof of optimality

Projection identity. Let \(I=[\ell,u]\), let \(a\in\mathbb R\), and let \(p=\min\{u,\max\{\ell,a\}\}\). For every \(b\in I\),
\[ |a-b|=|a-p|+|p-b|. \]

Indeed, if \(a\in I\), then \(p=a\). If \(a<\ell\), then \(a<p=\ell\le b\); if \(a>u\), then \(b\le p=u<a\). In each case, the displayed equality follows by adding distances between ordered points.

Now fix an arbitrary feasible schedule \(y_0=\alpha,y_1,\ldots,y_H\), whose locations may be real. Define its prefix cost by
\[ C_t=\sum_{s=1}^{t}|y_s-y_{s-1}|, \qquad G_t=\sum_{s=1}^{t}|g_s-g_{s-1}|. \]

We prove by induction that, for every \(t\in\{0,\ldots,H\}\),
\[ C_t\ge G_t+|y_t-g_t|. \]

For \(t=0\), both costs are zero and \(y_0=g_0=\alpha\).

Suppose the inequality holds at \(t-1\). The induction hypothesis and the triangle inequality give
\[ \begin{aligned} C_t &=C_{t-1}+|y_t-y_{t-1}|\\ &\ge G_{t-1}+|y_{t-1}-g_{t-1}|+|y_t-y_{t-1}|\\ &\ge G_{t-1}+|y_t-g_{t-1}|. \end{aligned} \]

Because \(y_t\in I_t\) and \(g_t\) is the projection of \(g_{t-1}\) onto \(I_t\), the projection identity yields
\[ |y_t-g_{t-1}| = |g_t-g_{t-1}|+|y_t-g_t|. \]

Substitution proves the induction step. At \(t=H\), consequently,
\[ C_H\ge G_H+|y_H-g_H|\ge G_H. \]

The greedy schedule itself has cost \(G_H\), so it attains the global minimum. No terminal-location assumption or advance knowledge of future intervals was used.

Exact arithmetic and running time

For the stated dimension \(m=1\), computing all interval endpoints requires \(O(\tau H)\) location comparisons; constructing the schedule requires \(O(H)\) further operations.

To bound bit complexity, write each of \(\alpha,x_1,\ldots,x_\tau,B\) as a ratio of binary integers with positive denominator. Let \(D\) be the product of these denominators. Their total encoding length is at most \(L\), so \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Scale these rational numbers by \(D\); every scaled value is an integer with \(O(L)\) bits. Ordinary integer multiplication and division perform this preprocessing in \(O((\tau+1)L^2)\) bit operations.

Every greedy location is either \(\alpha\) or an interval endpoint, so all scaled locations remain integers in \([0,D]\). The scaled accumulated cost is at most \(HD\), requiring \(O(L+\log H)\) bits. Endpoint comparisons, projections, cost accumulation, and the final comparison can therefore all be performed exactly.

Since the input contains an \(H\)-bit active-set vector for each of the \(\tau\ge1\) types, \(\tau H\le L\), and hence \(H\le L\). A valid overall bound is
\[ O\bigl((\tau+1)L^2+\tau H L\bigr) \qquad\text{for }m=1, \]
which is \(O(L^3)\). The algorithm never expands the masses into a finite population or enumerates possible types.

Thus the stated decision problem is polynomial-time solvable.
