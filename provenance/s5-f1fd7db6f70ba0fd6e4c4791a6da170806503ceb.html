The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given hospitals \(H\), applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\), utilities \(v_{th}\), type rankings \(\succ_t\), and hospital priorities \(\succ_h\) over types, choose feasible assignment masses \(x_{th}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{th}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{th}\le\kappa_h\). For each type–hospital pair define \(D_{th}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{j\succeq_t h}x_{tj}\) and \(L_{th}(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{sh}\), and let \(B(x)=\sum_{t,h}D_{th}(x)\mathbf{1}[\sum_s x_{sh}<\kappa_h\text{ or }L_{th}(x)>0]\). Given a rational allowance \(\varepsilon\), compute an assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\) subject to \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\), or report infeasibility; varying \(\varepsilon\) yields the utility–blocking frontier.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a nonempty hospital set, \(m=|H|\). A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input includes positive rational capacities \(\kappa_h\), nonnegative rational applicant and hospital utilities \(a_{th},b_{th}\), and a nonnegative rational allowance \(\varepsilon\). Set \(v_{th}=a_{th}+b_{th}\); utilities induce strict complete rankings \(\succ_t\) of hospitals and priorities \(\succ_h\) of types, with no ties. All pairs are acceptable; unassigned applicants are willing to join any hospital.
Assignments are divisible masses \(x_{th}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_hx_{th}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{th}\le\kappa_h\). Hospitals are indifferent between applicants of the same type; only strictly lower-priority types can be displaced.
Define \(D_{th}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{j\succeq_t h}x_{tj}\), where \(\succeq_t\) includes equality, and \(L_{th}(x)=\sum_{s:t\succ_hs}x_{sh}\).
The blocking mass is \(B(x)=\sum_{t,h}D_{th}(x)\mathbf1[\sum_sx_{sh}<\kappa_h\ \text{or}\ L_{th}(x)>0]\), where \(\mathbf1\) is the indicator. This counts dissatisfied mass separately for each hospital it can block.
Given \(\varepsilon\), the problem is to maximize \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\) subject to feasibility and \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\), or report infeasibility. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), including the encoding of \(\varepsilon\).

Is this exact continuous optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete residents with positive integer capacities \(q_h\), stability is equivalent to \(q_hx_{rh}+q_h\sum_{j\succ_rh}x_{rj}+\sum_{i\succ_hr}x_{ih}\ge q_h\) for every pair; this is a characterization, not a complexity classification ([Shimada–Yamazaki–Takano](https://doi.org/10.2197/ipsjjip.28.406)).
Welfare maximization for stable fractional matchings with cardinal utility-based blocking and ties is NP-hard ([Caragiannis et al.](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/29049/1/Stable-Fractional-Matchings-AIJ.pdf)); its blocking notion differs from \(B\). No result settling the exact mass-based problem above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required nonempty hospitals and positive rational capacities, carrying over positive discrete hospital capacities without imposing an unstated total-capacity condition.
  • Specified nonnegative rational component utilities inducing strict complete rankings, with total pair utility equal to their sum, as in the discrete model.
  • Specified complete acceptability and willingness of unassigned applicants to join any hospital, including pairs with zero utility.
  • Specified hospital indifference within a type and displacement only of strictly lower-priority types, following the graded blocking formula.
  • Allowed a nonnegative rational allowance, including zero, to retain the stable endpoint used in the discrete model.
  • Defined the input bit length and interpreted the frontier request as exact optimization for an input allowance, rather than requiring enumeration of the entire frontier.

Result

The exact optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. More precisely, given an additional rational welfare threshold \(Q\), deciding whether there is a feasible assignment satisfying
\(B(x)\le\varepsilon\) and \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\ge Q\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

The hardness holds with strictly positive utilities, strict utility-induced rankings, and total hospital capacity equal to one. Divisibility does not invalidate the reduction.

There is also an exact algorithm running in
\((\tau+1)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time, where \(L\) includes the binary encoding of \(\varepsilon\).

1. A finite union of closed rational polyhedra

First, the discontinuous indicators do not prevent attainment of the maximum.

For each hospital \(h\), choose one of the following \(\tau+1\) modes.

  • Unrestricted mode: impose no additional constraint and declare every type active at \(h\).
  • Cutoff mode \(w\in T\): require

\(\sum_t x_{th}=\kappa_h\) and \(x_{sh}=0\) for every \(s\) with \(w\succ_h s\). Declare precisely the types \(t\) satisfying \(t\succ_h w\) active at \(h\).

For a vector of modes, let \(A_h\) be the types declared active at \(h\), and define the linear function
\(C(x)=\sum_h\sum_{t\in A_h}D_{th}(x)\).
The corresponding polyhedron consists of feasible assignments obeying the mode constraints and \(C(x)\le\varepsilon\).

We claim that the original feasible set is exactly the union of these polyhedra.

Every assignment in one of the polyhedra satisfies \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\).
Feasibility implies \(D_{th}(x)\ge0\). In unrestricted mode, every possible blocking contribution is included in \(C(x)\). In cutoff mode \(w\), the hospital is full, and a type \(t\) not ranked above \(w\) has no strictly lower-priority assigned type: all assigned types rank at least as high as \(w\). Thus every type omitted from \(A_h\) has indicator zero. Consequently \(B(x)\le C(x)\).

Every original feasible assignment belongs to one of the polyhedra.
At an underfilled hospital, choose unrestricted mode. At a full hospital, choose its lowest-priority type \(w\) with positive assigned mass; such a type exists because \(\kappa_h>0\). Then \(L_{th}(x)>0\) holds exactly for \(t\succ_h w\). These choices therefore give \(C(x)=B(x)\).

Each polyhedron is closed and bounded, since \(0\le x_{th}\le\mu_t\). There are finitely many. Hence, whenever the feasible set is nonempty, the maximum is attained.

Each polyhedron has \(m\tau\) variables and \(O(m\tau+m+\tau)\) constraints. Its rational encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Enumerating the modes and solving their rational linear programs gives the claimed running time:
\((\tau+1)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

For the threshold decision problem, add the linear constraint \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\ge Q\). Any nonempty resulting rational polytope contains a vertex whose encoding length is polynomial in the input length. Such an assignment is an \(\mathrm{NP}\) certificate: feasibility, welfare, and every indicator in \(B(x)\) can be checked by exact rational arithmetic. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), including the encodings of both \(\varepsilon\) and \(Q\).

2. Reduction from Set Cover

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Set Cover decision problem. Its input is a universe \(U=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), sets \(S_1,\ldots,S_s\), and an integer \(k\); the question is whether at most \(k\) sets cover \(U\). We use instances with \(n,s\ge1\), \(\bigcup_jS_j=U\), and \(1\le k\le s\). These restrictions preserve hardness because the excluded cases are directly decidable. See [Karp’s original completeness theorem](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Create the hospitals
\(H=\{F_1,\ldots,F_s,P,Z\}\), so \(m=s+2\).
Create:

  • a client type \(u_i\) for every element \(i\);
  • a filler type \(f_j\) for every set \(S_j\);
  • one probe type \(p\).

Thus \(\tau=n+s+1\).

Choose
\(d=\lceil\log_2(4(m+1)n(s+1))\rceil\),
\(\gamma=2^{-d}\),
\(\delta=n(s+1)\gamma\), and
\(\rho=1-\delta\).

These choices give
\(0<\delta\le1/(4(m+1))\) and \(m\delta<\rho\).
All masses below are positive dyadic rationals.

Assign masses and capacities as follows:

| Type | Mass | Hospital | Capacity |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(u_i\) | \(\gamma\) | \(F_j\) | \(n\gamma\) |
| \(f_j\) | \(n\gamma\) | \(P\) | \(\rho\) |
| \(p\) | \(\rho\) | \(Z\) | \(n\gamma\) |

The total type mass and total hospital capacity both equal
\(n\gamma+sn\gamma+\rho=1\).

Set the allowance and welfare threshold to
\(\varepsilon=k\rho+m\delta\) and \(Q=20\delta+11\rho\).
In particular,
\(\varepsilon<(k+1)\rho\).

We next construct utilities so that reaching \(Q\) forces precisely the assignment restrictions needed for the reduction.

3. Explicit utilities and strict rankings

Define the designated hospitals of each nonprobe type by
\(E_{u_i}=\{F_j:i\in S_j\}\) and
\(E_{f_j}=\{F_j,Z\}\).
These sets describe welfare-maximizing pairs; every other pair remains acceptable.

Index the nonprobe types by \(i=1,\ldots,\tau-1\), with clients first and filler \(f_j\) having index \(n+j\). Index the hospitals by \(h=1,\ldots,m\), with \(F_j\) indexed by \(j\), \(P\) by \(s+1\), and \(Z\) by \(s+2\).

For each nonprobe type, define
\(r_{ih}=\frac{i}{\tau+1}+\frac{h}{(\tau+1)(m+1)}\).

Set \(b_{ih}=r_{ih}+4\) when the pair is \((f_j,F_j)\), and set \(b_{ih}=r_{ih}\) otherwise. Then define
\(a_{ih}=20-b_{ih}\) if \(h\in E_i\), and
\(a_{ih}=h/(m+1)\) otherwise.

For the probe, use:

| Hospital | \(a_{ph}\) | \(b_{ph}\) | \(v_{ph}\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(F_j\) | \(4+j/(s+1)\) | \(2\) | \(6+j/(s+1)\) |
| \(P\) | \(3\) | \(8\) | \(11\) |
| \(Z\) | \(1\) | \(2\) | \(3\) |

We verify the required properties.

Hospital priorities are strict.
Every \(r_{ih}\) lies strictly between zero and one. At a fixed hospital, these numbers are distinct across nonprobe types. At \(F_j\), its own filler has utility strictly between four and five, the probe has utility two, and every other type has utility strictly between zero and one. In particular,
\(f_j\succ_{F_j}p\succ_{F_j}u_i\) for every client \(u_i\).
At \(P\) and \(Z\), the probe’s utility is respectively eight and two, whereas every other type’s utility is below one.

Applicant rankings are strict.
For a nonprobe type, designated-pair applicant utilities lie strictly between fifteen and twenty, whereas other applicant utilities lie strictly between zero and one. The latter are distinct across hospitals. The former are distinct because \(b_{ih}\) is distinct across designated hospitals: the baseline varies with \(h\), and the filler’s own-hospital bonus separates its two designated hospitals. The probe strictly ranks all \(F_j\) above \(P\), and \(P\) above \(Z\); its utilities at the \(F_j\) are distinct.

All utilities are strictly positive. Every nonprobe type has welfare \(20\) exactly at its designated hospitals and welfare below \(20\) elsewhere. The probe has welfare \(11\) exactly at \(P\) and welfare below \(11\) elsewhere.

4. What reaching the welfare threshold forces

Let \(W_t=20\) for nonprobe types and \(W_p=11\). Then
\(Q=\sum_tW_t\mu_t\), and every pair satisfies \(v_{th}\le W_t\).

For every feasible assignment, the following identity holds:

\(Q-\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th} =\sum_tW_t\left(\mu_t-\sum_hx_{th}\right) +\sum_{t,h}(W_t-v_{th})x_{th}\).

Every summand on the right is nonnegative. Thus welfare is at most \(Q\). Welfare at least \(Q\) forces equality and consequently:

  • every type is fully assigned;
  • every client \(u_i\) is assigned only to \(F_j\) with \(i\in S_j\);
  • every filler \(f_j\) is assigned only to \(F_j\) and \(Z\);
  • the probe is assigned entirely to \(P\).

Because assigned mass and total capacity both equal one, every hospital must then be full.

For any such assignment, define
\(J(x)=\{j:\sum_i x_{u_iF_j}>0\}\).

At every \(F_j\), the probe prefers \(F_j\) to its assignment \(P\), so
\(D_{pF_j}(x)=\rho\).
The only types that can be assigned to \(F_j\) are its own filler and its incident clients. Its filler ranks above the probe, and all clients rank below the probe. Therefore
\(L_{pF_j}(x)=\sum_i x_{u_iF_j}\).

Since \(F_j\) is full, the probe’s contribution at \(F_j\) is exactly \(\rho\) when \(j\in J(x)\), and zero otherwise. At \(P\) and \(Z\), its dissatisfied mass is zero. Its total contribution to \(B(x)\) is therefore exactly \(\rho|J(x)|\).

The total contribution of all nonprobe types is always at most \(m\delta\): feasibility gives \(D_{th}(x)\le\mu_t\), and their combined mass is \(\delta\).

5. Correctness of the reduction

A cover gives a qualifying assignment

Suppose \(C\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}\) is a cover with \(|C|\le k\). For each element \(i\), choose \(j(i)\in C\) satisfying \(i\in S_{j(i)}\), and assign all \(\gamma\) mass of \(u_i\) to \(F_{j(i)}\).

Let \(y_j\) be the total client mass assigned to \(F_j\). Then
\(0\le y_j\le n\gamma\) and \(\sum_jy_j=n\gamma\).

Assign \(n\gamma-y_j\) mass of \(f_j\) to \(F_j\), and its remaining \(y_j\) mass to \(Z\). Assign the probe’s mass \(\rho\) to \(P\).

Every type is fully assigned, and every hospital is exactly full. All used pairs attain their type’s maximum welfare, so welfare is \(Q\). Furthermore,
\(B(x)\le\rho|\{j:y_j>0\}|+m\delta \le k\rho+m\delta=\varepsilon\).

Thus a cover of size at most \(k\) produces a qualifying assignment.

A qualifying assignment gives a cover

Conversely, suppose an assignment has welfare at least \(Q\) and satisfies \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\). Section 4 gives all the forced assignment restrictions.

Every client has positive mass and is fully assigned to incident set hospitals. Consequently, \(J(x)\) covers \(U\), even when clients split their mass among several hospitals.

The probe’s blocking contribution gives
\(\rho|J(x)|\le B(x)\le\varepsilon<(k+1)\rho\).
Since \(\rho>0\) and \(|J(x)|\) is an integer, \(|J(x)|\le k\).

Hence the original Set Cover instance is a yes-instance exactly when the constructed welfare-threshold instance is a yes-instance.

6. Encoding and complexity conclusion

The constructed instance has
\(m=s+2\) and \(\tau=n+s+1\).
The exponent \(d\) is \(O(\log(n+s))\). All masses, capacities, utilities, \(\varepsilon\), and \(Q\) have binary encodings of length \(O(\log(n+s))\). In particular, the allowance has no hidden large encoding cost.

The total constructed input length is
\(O(m\tau\log(m+\tau))\), and the construction takes polynomial time.

This proves that the welfare-threshold decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; together with Section 1, it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. An exact optimization algorithm would decide that problem by comparing its attained optimum with \(Q\). Therefore the exact continuous optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and cannot run in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
