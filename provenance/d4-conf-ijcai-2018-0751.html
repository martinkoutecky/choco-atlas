The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), activities \(A\), and rational approval intervals \(I_{t,a}\subseteq[0,1]\), find a stable allocation \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\), including a void option \(a_0\), with \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{a_0\}}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). Let \(z_a=\sum_t x_{t,a}\); require \(x_{t,a}>0\Rightarrow z_a\in I_{t,a}\), and, whenever type \(t\) has positive mass left in the void, require that \(z_a\) is not a right-accumulation point of \(I_{t,a}\) for every activity \(a\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(A\) contain \(m\) activities; there are no friend or enemy relations.
For each \((t,a)\in T\times A\), the approval set \(I_{t,a}\) is either empty or a closed interval \([\ell_{t,a},u_{t,a}]\subseteq[0,1]\) with binary-rational endpoints; singleton intervals are allowed. Types are distinguished by their approval profiles. Approved outcomes are mutually indifferent and strictly preferred to the void option \(a_0\); disapproved outcomes are strictly worse than void.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,a}\geq0\) for \(a\in A\cup\{a_0\}\), with \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{a_0\}}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\); write \(z_a=\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,a}\) for \(a\in A\).
It is stable precisely when \(x_{t,a}>0\Rightarrow z_a\in I_{t,a}\) for every real activity, and \(x_{t,a_0}>0\Rightarrow I_{t,a}\cap(z_a,z_a+\varepsilon) =\varnothing\) for some \(\varepsilon>0\), for every \(a\in A\). This latter condition excludes right-accumulation points, including at empty activities; joining does not require existing participants' consent.
The problem is to decide whether a stable allocation exists and find one when it does. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with parameter \(k=m+\tau\).

Is deciding whether a stable allocation exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, is the continuous problem \(\mathrm{FPT}\) parameterized by \(k=m+\tau\)?

For finite agents without friend or enemy relations and with interval approvals, stable allocation is solvable in \(2^{O(\tau(m+1))}n^4\log n\) time; with arbitrary approval sets, the discrete problem is \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by \(m\). [Eiben, Ganian and Ordyniak](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2018/0028.pdf)
Discrete stable-existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with interval approvals, as follows from the interval-preserving stable-existence reduction applied to the increasing-approval perfect-assignment hardness result. [Darmann et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00182-017-0596-4)
No result settling this exact continuous model was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified closed approval intervals with rational endpoints, allowing empty sets and singleton intervals, to match consecutive discrete approval sets.
  • Retained zero as an allowed endpoint, as in the graded continuous domain; normalization remains total mass one.
  • Identified types by their complete approval profiles, merging identical profiles, and used m+tau as the continuous counterpart of the anchor's parameter.
  • Made explicit the absence of friend and enemy relations and the approval-based indifference convention.
  • Defined right-accumulation through an explicit one-sided neighborhood condition and applied it to empty activities as well.
  • Allowed real divisible allocations and formulated stable-existence as the decision problem alongside the requested construction task.

Result

Every instance in the stated continuous model admits a stable allocation. Moreover, one can construct a rational stable allocation in
\(O\!\left(L^3+m\tau\log(\tau+1)L\right)\) bit operations.

Thus stable existence is in \(\mathrm{P}\), and construction is also \(\mathrm{FPT}\) parameterized by \(k=m+\tau\).

The key is divisibility: at the largest feasible load of an activity, all remaining mass that would want to join that activity fits into its current load.

1. Right accumulation for closed intervals

For a nonempty approval interval \(I=[\ell,u]\) and a load \(z\in[0,1]\), the following equivalence holds:

\[ I\cap(z,z+\varepsilon)\ne\varnothing\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell\le z<u. \]

Indeed:

  • If \(\ell\le z<u\), every sufficiently short interval immediately to the right of \(z\) contains approved points.
  • If \(z<\ell\), taking \(\varepsilon=\ell-z>0\) gives an empty intersection.
  • If \(z\ge u\), there are no approved points strictly to the right of \(z\).

In particular, singleton intervals never create right accumulation.

For an activity \(a\), define its willing and right-accumulating types at load \(z\) by

\[ W_a(z)=\{t:I_{t,a}\ne\varnothing,\ \ell_{t,a}\le z\le u_{t,a}\}, \]

and

\[ M_a(z)=\{t:I_{t,a}\ne\varnothing,\ \ell_{t,a}\le z<u_{t,a}\}. \]

Consequently, \(M_a(z)\subseteq W_a(z)\). Stability requires that every type in \(M_a(z_a)\) have zero mass in void.

2. A single-activity saturation lemma

Consider one activity and remaining type masses \(r_t\ge0\), with \(\sum_t r_t\le1\). These are masses still available for assignment.

Write \(W(z)\) and \(M(z)\) for the sets defined above, and let

\[ F(z)=\sum_{t\in W(z)}r_t. \]

A load \(z\) can be formed using only approving remaining mass if and only if \(z\le F(z)\).

Necessity follows by summing the available masses of approving types. Sufficiency follows from divisibility: take mass from those types, up to their respective capacities \(r_t\), until the total reaches \(z\).

Lemma. There exists a largest feasible load \(z^\star\). At that load,

\[ \sum_{t\in M(z^\star)}r_t\le z^\star. \]

Furthermore, one can fill the activity to \(z^\star\) while assigning the entire remaining mass of every type in \(M(z^\star)\).

Proof. Let

\[ \mathcal F=\{z\in[0,1]:z\le F(z)\}. \]

This set contains \(0\). It is also closed. To prove this, suppose \(z_j\in\mathcal F\) and \(z_j\to z\). If a type does not approve \(z\), closedness of its approval interval implies that it does not approve \(z_j\) for all sufficiently large \(j\). There are finitely many types, so eventually \(W(z_j)\subseteq W(z)\). Hence

\[ z_j\le F(z_j)\le F(z), \]

and passing to the limit gives \(z\le F(z)\). Thus \(\mathcal F\) is a nonempty compact set and has a maximum \(z^\star\).

Suppose, for a contradiction, that

\[ B:=\sum_{t\in M(z^\star)}r_t>z^\star. \]

Every type in \(M(z^\star)\) has \(\ell_t\le z^\star<u_t\). Because there are finitely many such types, we can choose \(\delta>0\) satisfying

\[ \delta<B-z^\star \quad\text{and}\quad \delta<u_t-z^\star\ \text{for every }t\in M(z^\star). \]

All these types approve \(z^\star+\delta\), so

\[ F(z^\star+\delta)\ge B>z^\star+\delta. \]

Also, \(z^\star+\delta<B\le1\). Therefore \(z^\star+\delta\) is feasible, contradicting maximality.

It remains to construct the claimed filling. First assign all remaining mass of types in \(M(z^\star)\). Its total \(B\) is at most \(z^\star\). The other willing types have available mass

\[ F(z^\star)-B\ge z^\star-B. \]

Use those types to supply the remaining \(z^\star-B\) mass. Every participant approves \(z^\star\), and no mass of a type in \(M(z^\star)\) remains unassigned. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

3. Constructing a stable allocation

Process the activities in any order \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\). Initially set \(r_t=\mu_t\) for every type.

For each activity \(a_i\):

  • Using the current remaining masses \(r_t\), compute its largest feasible load \(z_i^\star\).
  • Assign to \(a_i\) the entire remaining mass of every type in \(M_{a_i}(z_i^\star)\).
  • Fill the remaining capacity up to \(z_i^\star\) using types in \(W_{a_i}(z_i^\star)\).
  • Subtract the assigned masses from \(r_t\).

The lemma guarantees that every step is possible. Assign all masses left after the final step to void.

Proof of stability. Assignments to a processed activity are never changed. Consequently, its final load is exactly the load used when selecting its participants. Every participant was selected from its willing set, establishing individual rationality.

Now suppose type \(t\) has positive mass in void at the end. Its remaining mass was therefore positive immediately before processing every activity. If \(t\) had belonged to \(M_{a_i}(z_i^\star)\), step 2 would have assigned its entire remaining mass, leaving no mass of that type for void. Thus

\[ t\notin M_{a_i}(z_i^\star)\qquad\text{for every }i. \]

By Section 1, each activity therefore has some right neighborhood containing no approved point for this void type. This is precisely the required void-stability condition.

If the definition requires a common positive \(\varepsilon\) across activities for each void type, take the minimum of the finitely many positive choices. Mass conservation follows directly from the updates to \(r_t\). Hence the resulting allocation is stable. \(\square\)

4. Computing the largest feasible load

Fix an activity and its current remaining masses. Form the sorted list of distinct breakpoints consisting of \(0\), \(1\), and all endpoints of its nonempty approval intervals:

\[ 0=b_0<b_1<\cdots<b_s=1. \]

There are at most \(2\tau+2\) breakpoints. On each open gap \((b_i,b_{i+1})\), the willing set is constant. Let \(S_i\) be its total remaining mass.

Consider the following candidate loads:

  • Every breakpoint \(b_i\) satisfying \(b_i\le F(b_i)\).
  • Every \(S_i\) satisfying \(b_i<S_i<b_{i+1}\).

The largest candidate is exactly the largest feasible load.

To verify completeness, consider a feasible load \(z\) in an open gap. Then \(z\le S_i\).

  • If \(S_i<b_{i+1}\), the feasible candidate \(S_i\) lies in that gap and is at least \(z\).
  • If \(S_i\ge b_{i+1}\), every interval approving points in the gap also approves its right endpoint, by closedness. Therefore \(F(b_{i+1})\ge S_i\ge b_{i+1}\), making that endpoint a feasible candidate.

The breakpoint \(0\) is always a candidate, so the candidate list is nonempty.

All breakpoint and gap totals can be computed by a sweep after sorting endpoints. At each breakpoint, add masses of intervals beginning there, evaluate the closed-point total, and then remove masses of intervals ending there. This ordering also handles singleton intervals correctly.

Thus one activity requires \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) arithmetic operations, including the subsequent allocation. All activities require \(O(m\tau\log(\tau+1))\) arithmetic operations.

5. Bit complexity and parameterization

Let \(D\) be the product of all denominators appearing in the input masses and interval endpoints. Since their total encoding length is at most \(L\), the integer \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

Express all input numbers over this common denominator. Every number subsequently constructed remains an integer multiple of \(1/D\):

  • Candidate loads are input endpoints or sums of remaining masses.
  • Assigned masses are obtained by taking remaining masses, differences, and minima.
  • Remaining masses are updated by subtraction.

All these values lie in \([0,1]\), so their scaled numerators have \(O(L)\) bits. Additions, subtractions, and comparisons therefore take \(O(L)\) bit operations.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, common-denominator preprocessing takes \(O(L^3)\) bit operations. The complete construction consequently takes

\[ O\!\left(L^3+m\tau\log(\tau+1)L\right) \]

bit operations and produces an allocation of total encoding length \(O(\tau(m+1)L)\).

Because \(m,\tau\le k\), this is also bounded by

\[ O\!\left(L^3+k^2\log(k+1)L\right), \]

which is an \(\mathrm{FPT}\) bound for \(k=m+\tau\). On valid inputs, the decision answer is always yes.

6. Why the discrete hardness does not transfer

In the discrete model, stability tests whether an unassigned agent approves the group size *after* joining. That agent need not approve the current group size; this distinction already underlies the one-activity nonexistence example in [Darmann et al., Proposition 1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00182-017-0596-4).

In the stated continuous model, a type with right accumulation at \(z\) already approves \(z\), because its interval is closed. Moreover, finitely many such intervals admit a common sufficiently small positive increase in load. These are exactly the properties used in the saturation lemma. Divisibility then lets us prioritize all right-accumulating mass when filling each activity.
