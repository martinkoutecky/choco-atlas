The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_s\) for single-resident types and \(\nu_\kappa\) for couple types, rational hospital capacity rates \(q_h\), complete type-level rankings, and preference lists, decide whether nonnegative variables \(x_{s,h}\) and \(y_{\kappa,(h,h')}\), plus unassigned mass, satisfy the type row sums and hospital loads \(\ell_h\le q_h\), while no positive-mass portion has an MM-blocking deviation under the measure versions of Definition 1. Study this problem also under the paper's no-single, list-length-two restriction.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) hospitals with positive binary-rational capacities \(q_h\). A society is specified by its support: \(\tau\) single or ordered-couple types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_s\) or \(\nu_\kappa\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses need not sum to one; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each single type strictly ranks an acceptable subset of \(H\); each couple type strictly ranks an acceptable subset of \(H\times H\). Hospitals strictly rank acceptable resident roles \(s,(\kappa,1),(\kappa,2)\); copies of the same role are indifferent. Acceptability is reciprocal through the projections of couple lists. All listed assignments outrank unassignment; partial assignment of a couple is forbidden.
An allocation consists of nonnegative real masses satisfying \(\sum_h x_{s,h}+u_s=\mu_s\), \(\sum_{(a,b)}y_{\kappa,(a,b)}+v_\kappa=\nu_\kappa\), and \(\ell_h=\sum_s x_{s,h}+\sum_\kappa\sum_{(a,b)}(\mathbf1_{a=h}+\mathbf1_{b=h})y_{\kappa,(a,b)}\le q_h\); assignment sums range only over listed options.
Define a positive-mass MM block as follows. Select \(\delta>0\) of one type from a single current assignment or unassignment, and a strictly preferred listed destination. For couples, the destination may change either one coordinate or both.
Each moving role requires \(\delta\) places at its destination, supplied by unused capacity in the original allocation and/or by displacing incumbent mass strictly worse than that incoming role. Supplies must be disjoint across incoming roles; two movers to the same hospital therefore require \(2\delta\) places.
A nonmoving partner's selected \(\delta\) mass is protected from displacement. Admission tests use original hospital loads, without first freeing movers' old places; displaced residents' partners need not also be displaced. The allocation is MM-stable if no such block exists.
The restricted class has no singles, at most two listed pairs per couple type, at most two acceptable resident roles per hospital, and \(q_h=1\) for every hospital.

Is existence of an MM-stable mass allocation decidable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? Resolve the same question for the restricted class.

Discrete stable-matching existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with no singles, unit hospital capacities, and couple and hospital lists of length at most two. [Source](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/97774/1/97774.pdf)
Fractional stable allocations are known to exist in a related model that ranks applications and treats same-hospital couples through quota-responsive choices; this is a different stability definition and does not settle the MM decision problem above. [Source](https://real.mtak.hu/33935/1/BFI16amai_last.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Mass normalization was unspecified; masses are unnormalized, with each unit of couple mass contributing one unit of each resident role.
  • Capacity domains were unspecified; every hospital has positive rational capacity, and the unit-capacity condition is retained in the restricted class.
  • Complete type-level rankings are interpreted as strict orders on acceptable options, permitting incomplete acceptability lists; hospitals rank the two roles of each couple separately, with indifference only between copies of one role.
  • Unassignment is worse than every acceptable assignment; couples are jointly assigned or jointly unassigned, and only listed options may receive mass.
  • Measure versions of MM-blocking were undefined; a block moves an arbitrary positive portion from one current assignment, admits moving roles using original vacancies and disjoint strictly worse incumbent portions, and protects the selected nonmoving partner portion.
  • One-coordinate couple deviations are included, and original-load admission tests do not automatically credit vacancies created by the movers; displacement need not preserve the displaced couples' assignments.
  • The hospital list-length-two restriction counts acceptable resident roles, while couple list length counts ordered hospital pairs.
  • Allocation variables range over nonnegative reals; the input masses and capacities are binary rationals.

Result

Every valid instance admits an MM-stable mass allocation. Consequently, existence is polynomial-time decidable for both the general class and the restricted class: on valid inputs, the decision algorithm always answers yes.

The proof uses Scarf’s lemma, together with an explicit argument that its allocation prevents the MM blocks defined in the question. That implication includes one-coordinate moves and same-hospital destinations.

1. Auxiliary preferences over assignments

Let \(T\) be the set of supported types. Call a type together with one of its listed assignments a contract, and let \(E\) be the set of contracts.

For a contract \(e\), write \(t(e)\) for its type. For a hospital \(h\), let \(R_h(e)\) be the set of resident roles that \(e\) assigns to \(h\). Thus \(R_h(e)\) has size zero, one, or two. When it is nonempty, define \(w_h(e)\) to be its worst role according to the hospital’s original strict ranking.

We introduce auxiliary preferences over contracts solely for the proof:

  • Each type orders its contracts by its given preference list.
  • Each hospital orders its incident contracts first by \(w_h(e)\), preferring the contract whose worst role it prefers.
  • If two contracts have the same worst role, the hospital breaks the tie according to that role’s type preferences.

The last rule is well defined: a resident role identifies its type, so contracts with the same worst role belong to the same type. The auxiliary hospital order is therefore strict.

These auxiliary orders do not change the original hospital rankings used to test MM blocks.

Two properties will be used. First, if a hospital weakly prefers contract \(f\) to contract \(e\) in its auxiliary order, every role that \(f\) assigns there is weakly preferred, in the original role order, to \(w_h(e)\). Indeed, the worst role of \(f\) is weakly preferred to \(w_h(e)\).

Second, suppose a type strictly prefers \(e\) to \(f\), and a role \(r=w_h(e)\) is assigned to \(h\) by both contracts. Then the hospital strictly prefers \(e\) to \(f\) in its auxiliary order. To see this, the worst role of \(f\) is either strictly worse than \(r\), in which case the primary comparison decides, or equals \(r\), in which case the tie-breaking rule decides.

2. Applying Scarf’s lemma

We use the following precise form of Scarf’s lemma. Let \(A=(I_N\ D)\), let \(b\) be a positive vector, and suppose the set \(P=\{z\geq 0:Az=b\}\) is bounded. Let \(C\) have the same dimensions as \(A\), with

\(C_{i,i}<C_{i,N+k}<C_{i,j}\)

for every row \(i\), every additional column \(N+k\), and every identity column \(j\neq i\). Then there exists \(z\in P\) such that, for every column \(c\), some row \(i\) satisfies

\(C_{i,c}\leq C_{i,j}\quad\text{for every }j\text{ with }z_j>0.\)

This is the ordinal-domination formulation stated as Theorem 5 in [Biró–Fleiner–Irving](https://real.mtak.hu/33935/1/BFI16amai_last.pdf).

We now construct the matrices and check all hypotheses.

If \(E\) is empty, assigning every type entirely to unassignment is already MM-stable. Assume henceforth that \(K=|E|>0\).

There is one row for each type and each hospital, so \(N=|T|+|H|\). Define

\(b_t=\mu_t\) for a single type, \(b_t=\nu_t\) for a couple type, and \(b_h=q_h\) for a hospital.

For each contract \(e\), its column in \(D\) has coefficient one in its type row and coefficient \(|R_h(e)|\) in hospital row \(h\). All other coefficients are zero.

The first \(N\) variables are slack variables: unassigned mass at type rows and unused capacity at hospital rows. The remaining variables are contract masses. Hence

\(Az=b,\qquad z\geq 0\)

is exactly the allocation-feasibility system in the question.

This system is feasible: take all contract masses zero and all slacks equal to \(b\). It is bounded: each slack is at most its row’s bound, and every contract mass is at most its type’s mass.

For each row \(i\), let \(d_i\) be its number of incident contracts. Give those contracts scores \(1,\ldots,d_i\), increasing with the auxiliary preference order. Number all contracts \(e_1,\ldots,e_K\), and define

\(C_{i,i}=0;\)

\(C_{i,N+k}=\text{the auxiliary preference score of }e_k\) if \(e_k\) is incident with \(i\);

\(C_{i,N+k}=K+k\) otherwise;

\(C_{i,j}=2K+j\) for identity columns \(j\neq i\).

Every additional-column score is positive and at most \(2K\), whereas every other identity-column score exceeds \(2K\). Thus Scarf’s inequalities hold. The vector \(b\) is positive by the problem’s hypotheses, and boundedness was checked above.

Scarf’s lemma therefore supplies a feasible allocation with the stated domination property.

The resulting certificate for each contract

For every contract \(e\), there is an incident type or hospital \(i\) such that:

  • its slack is zero; and
  • every positive-mass contract incident with \(i\) is weakly preferred to \(e\) in its auxiliary order.

Here is why the dominating row must be incident with \(e\). Otherwise its score for \(e\) exceeds \(K\). Since its row bound is positive, either its own slack or some incident contract must have positive mass. Their scores are respectively zero or at most \(K\), contradicting domination.

Once the row is incident with \(e\), its score for \(e\) is positive. Its own slack, whose score is zero, must consequently vanish. The preference statement follows directly from domination and the score construction.

3. The certificates exclude every MM block

Suppose, for contradiction, that this allocation has an MM block. Let \(e\) be its destination contract, let \(\delta>0\) be the selected mass, and let its source be either unassignment or a contract \(f\) of the same type.

The source has positive mass, and the type strictly prefers \(e\) to that source.

Take a certificate for \(e\) from Section 2. Its certifying row cannot be the type:

  • If the source is unassignment, the type has positive slack.
  • If the source is \(f\), a positive-mass contract strictly worse than \(e\) violates the certificate’s preference condition.

The certifying row is therefore a hospital \(h\). Its certificate says that \(h\) is full and every positive-mass contract incident with \(h\) is weakly preferred to \(e\) in the auxiliary order.

Put \(r=w_h(e)\). By the first property in Section 1, no incumbent mass at \(h\) has a role strictly worse than \(r\).

We distinguish whether \(r\) moves in the proposed block.

Case 1: \(r\) is moving

This moving role needs \(\delta\) places at \(h\). But \(h\) has no unused capacity, and no incumbent mass strictly worse than \(r\). Neither permitted source of places can supply any positive amount.

Thus the admission requirement fails. This uses the specified test against original hospital loads: movers’ old places are not first freed.

Case 2: \(r\) is nonmoving

The source must be an assigned contract \(f\), and \(f\) assigns this same role \(r\) to \(h\). Its mass is positive.

The type strictly prefers \(e\) to \(f\). Since \(r=w_h(e)\), the second property in Section 1 says that the hospital strictly prefers \(e\) to \(f\) in its auxiliary order. This contradicts the hospital’s certificate, which requires every positive-mass incident contract to be weakly preferred to \(e\).

Both cases are impossible. Hence the allocation is MM-stable.

This argument also covers the delicate destinations:

  • If both movers enter the same hospital, its worst destination role cannot obtain even its own \(\delta\) places.
  • If one partner stays at the destination hospital, either the worst destination role moves and Case 1 applies, or it stays and Case 2 applies.
  • If the certifying hospital receives only a nonmoving partner, Case 2 applies directly.

The protection and disjoint-supply requirements cannot create a block after one of these necessary conditions has failed.

4. Consequences for the two decision problems

We have proved existence for arbitrary positive masses and capacities, without restrictions on list lengths. The restricted class is a subclass, so it also always admits an MM-stable mass allocation.

On the promised valid inputs, both decision problems therefore have the constant answer yes. Checking input validity, if required, takes polynomial time in the explicit input length.

This is an existence argument; it does not assert that a stable allocation can be constructed in polynomial time.

Why discrete hardness does not transfer

A small instance within the restricted class illustrates the difference. Take three hospitals and three couple types, each of mass one, with sole listed assignments

\(\kappa_A:(h_1,h_2),\qquad \kappa_B:(h_2,h_3),\qquad \kappa_C:(h_3,h_1).\)

All capacities are one. Hospital rankings are

\(h_1:(\kappa_A,1)\succ(\kappa_C,2),\)

\(h_2:(\kappa_B,1)\succ(\kappa_A,2),\)

\(h_3:(\kappa_C,1)\succ(\kappa_B,2).\)

In a discrete matching, at most one couple can be assigned. If none is assigned, a couple blocks. If \(\kappa_A\), \(\kappa_B\), or \(\kappa_C\) is assigned, respectively \(\kappa_B\), \(\kappa_C\), or \(\kappa_A\) blocks. Thus no discrete stable matching exists.

In the mass model, assign mass \(1/2\) of each couple to its sole pair and leave its remaining mass unassigned. Every hospital is full. Each couple has one destination hospital where its incoming role is the hospital’s lower-ranked role; that role has no unused capacity or strictly worse incumbent mass available. Consequently, no couple blocks.
