The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given institutions \(I\), rational capacities \(q_i\), finite complete student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), type preferences and institution priorities, categories \(C\), and rational bounds \(\ell_i^c,u_i^c\), choose masses \(x_{t,i}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_i x_{t,i}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,i}\le q_i\). Require positive-mass stability: no type \(t\) has mass assigned to an institution it ranks below \(i\) while \(i\) has capacity or assigns positive mass to a lower-priority type. Minimize \(\delta\) subject to \(\ell_i^c-\delta\le\sum_{t:c\in A_t}x_{t,i}\le u_i^c+\delta\) for every \(i,c\), and return an aggregate student-optimal solution among the minimizers.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) institutions and \(C\) a nonempty finite category set. A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,|C|,L\).
Each type \(t\) specifies categories \(A_t\subseteq C\) and a strict preference order \(\succ_t\) over all institutions; every institution has a strict priority order \(\succ_i\) over types. All pairs are acceptable, and being unmatched is worst. Input capacities and soft bounds are binary rationals satisfying \(0<q_i\le1\) and \(0\le\ell_i^c\le u_i^c\le q_i\).
An allocation consists of divisible masses \(x_{t,i}\ge0\), with \(\sum_i x_{t,i}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,i}\le q_i\). Write \(x_{t,\emptyset}=\mu_t-\sum_i x_{t,i}\).
Positive-mass stability means there are no \(t,i\) such that \(x_{t,\emptyset}+\sum_{j:i\succ_t j}x_{t,j}>0\) and either \(\sum_s x_{s,i}<q_i\) or \(x_{s,i}>0\) for some \(s\) with \(t\succ_i s\). Category bounds do not affect blocking.
Minimize \(\delta\ge0\) over stable allocations satisfying \(\ell_i^c-\delta\le\sum_{t:c\in A_t}x_{t,i}\le u_i^c+\delta\) for every \(i,c\); assigned mass counts toward every category it belongs to.
An aggregate student-optimal minimizer \(x\) weakly dominates every other minimizer \(y\): for every \(t,i\), \(\sum_{j:j\succeq_t i}x_{t,j}\ge\sum_{j:j\succeq_t i}y_{t,j}\).

Is computing the minimum \(\delta\) and an aggregate student-optimal minimizer solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|C|,L\), or is this continuous problem NP-hard?

For finite students with strict preferences and priorities, a student-optimal stable matching minimizing maximum overlapping-category bound violation is computable in \(\mathcal{O}(|E||S|)\) time, where \(E\) is the acceptability graph and \(S\) the student set. [Discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.27823v1).
Bipartite stable allocation without these diversity objectives has a polynomial-time algorithm. [Munshi](https://open.clemson.edu/all_theses/193/). No result settling the exact continuous optimization problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to 1, with capacities and bounds measured in the same units.
  • Required nonempty institutions, support, and categories, positive capacities at most 1, and ordered nonnegative bounds at most institutional capacity, following the intended quota domain.
  • Interpreted complete types as specifying strict preferences over all institutions, with mutual acceptability and strict institutional priorities over types; members of one type share its priority.
  • Included unmatched positive mass in blocking, as the discrete stability definition requires.
  • Required nonnegative delta to preserve maximum positive-part violation rather than allow negative objective values.
  • Defined aggregate student optimality as simultaneous dominance of every minimizer in every type's cumulative preferred-institution masses.
  • Used one-to-all category counting, allowing overlapping and empty category memberships.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In particular, an aggregate student-optimal minimizer always exists and can be computed exactly.

The proof uses the continuous rotation representation of stable allocations. The essential property is that the rotations affecting any one institution form a chain. Consequently, that institution’s category masses follow a piecewise linear path, and feasibility at a fixed violation level can be decided by monotone propagation.

A conservative bit-operation bound is

\( \displaystyle \mathcal O\!\left( \left[ (m\tau)^2(m+\tau+2)^2 +m\tau(m+\tau+2)(|C|+1) \right]L^3 \right). \)

Here \(\tau\) is the supplied support size.

1. Reduction to the standard stable-allocation model

The standard model has quotas at both vertex classes, upper capacities on edges, strict preferences, and allocations that fill every vertex quota. An edge blocks if it is unsaturated and both endpoints hold positive allocation on less-preferred edges.

We first verify that this model represents the stated positive-mass stability exactly.

Write \(Q=\sum_{i\in I}q_i\). Introduce a dummy type \(t_0\) with quota \(Q\) and a dummy institution \(i_0\) with quota \(1\). Real types have quotas \(\mu_t\), and real institutions have quotas \(q_i\).

Every real agent ranks its dummy partner last. Dummy preferences over real partners are arbitrary strict orders, with the other dummy last. Give each edge capacity equal to the smaller endpoint quota.

For a feasible allocation \(x\), write \(a=\sum_{t,i}x_{t,i}\), and extend it by

\( \displaystyle \widehat x_{t,i_0}=x_{t,\emptyset},\qquad \widehat x_{t_0,i}=q_i-\sum_t x_{t,i},\qquad \widehat x_{t_0,i_0}=a. \)

These quantities are nonnegative and fill all quotas. For example, the dummy type receives total allocation

\( \displaystyle \sum_i\left(q_i-\sum_t x_{t,i}\right)+a=Q. \)

Moreover, \(a\le \min(Q,1)\), so the dummy-to-dummy edge capacity is respected.

No dummy edge can block: a real type has no partner worse than its dummy institution, and a real institution has no type worse than its dummy type.

For a real pair \((t,i)\), the type endpoint holds positive allocation on worse edges exactly when

\( \displaystyle x_{t,\emptyset}+\sum_{j:i\succ_t j}x_{t,j}>0. \)

The institution endpoint holds positive allocation on worse edges exactly when it has a vacancy or holds positive mass of a lower-priority real type. If both conditions hold, then

\(x_{t,i}<\mu_t\) and \(x_{t,i}<q_i\),

so the edge is unsaturated. Thus standard blocking and the blocking definition in the question coincide.

Conversely, the quota equations uniquely determine all dummy allocations from the real allocation. Hence this construction gives a bijection between the two sets of stable allocations.

The augmented graph has

\( \displaystyle e=(m+1)(\tau+1)=\mathcal O(m\tau), \qquad v=m+\tau+2. \)

2. The continuous rotation representation

We use the following established result from Dean and Munshi, Section 4, Lemmas 6–12 of [*Faster Algorithms For Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

For a strict bipartite stable-allocation instance, one can compute in \(\mathcal O(ev)\) arithmetic operations an initial stable allocation \(x^0\), a rotation DAG \(\mathcal D\), positive rotation lengths \(r_\rho\), and rotation directions \(d^\rho\), with \(R\le 2e\) rotations, such that:

  • Stable allocations correspond exactly to vectors \(z\) satisfying

\(0\le z_\rho\le r_\rho\) and
\(z_\sigma>0\Longrightarrow z_\rho=r_\rho\)
for every DAG arc \(\rho\to\sigma\), through
\(x(z)=x^0+\sum_\rho z_\rho d^\rho\).

  • Rotations containing any fixed agent form a chain in the DAG.
  • A rotation transfers equal mass around a simple alternating cycle. Each participating institution exchanges one type for a higher-priority type; each participating type transfers mass to a less-preferred institution.

Call vectors satisfying the displayed conditions closed.

All hypotheses hold here: the augmented instance is finite, bipartite, has strict orders, rational quotas and edge capacities, and the blocking convention verified above.

We now derive the optimization algorithm from this representation.

A useful consequence is

\( \displaystyle z\le w \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(z)\text{ weakly dominates }x(w) \text{ for every real type}. \)

Indeed, each rotation direction transfers a type’s mass from a preferred partner to a less-preferred partner. Therefore, its contribution to every preferred-prefix sum is nonpositive. Since

\(x(w)-x(z)=\sum_\rho(w_\rho-z_\rho)d^\rho\)

and every coefficient is nonnegative, every preferred-prefix sum in \(x(w)\) is at most the corresponding sum in \(x(z)\).

3. Each institution has a piecewise linear path

Fix a real institution \(i\). List its rotations in chain order as

\(\rho_{i,1}\prec\cdots\prec\rho_{i,k_i}\).

For a closed vector \(z\), its coordinates on this chain consist of an initial sequence of fully applied rotations, possibly one partially applied rotation, and then zero coordinates. This follows because a positive later coordinate forces every earlier coordinate to be full.

Define its local progress by

\( \displaystyle s_i(z)=\sum_{h=1}^{k_i}z_{\rho_{i,h}}, \qquad H_i=\sum_{h=1}^{k_i}r_{\rho_{i,h}}. \)

The scalar \(s_i\in[0,H_i]\) uniquely determines the chain coordinates: apply rotations in order until the prescribed total progress is reached. Each individual coordinate is a nondecreasing function of \(s_i\).

Rotations outside this chain do not change institution \(i\). Consequently, its allocation column, and therefore each category mass, is a piecewise linear function of \(s_i\).

More explicitly, on the segment corresponding to rotation \(\rho_{i,h}\), let \(u\in[0,r_{\rho_{i,h}}]\) be its applied amount. Then

\( \displaystyle \eta_i^c(u) =\sum_{t:c\in A_t}x_{t,i} =\alpha_{i,h}^c+\beta_{i,h}^c u, \qquad \beta_{i,h}^c\in\{-1,0,1\}. \)

The coefficient is the incoming type’s category indicator minus the outgoing type’s category indicator. This remains valid with overlapping categories: compute the indicator difference separately for every category.

4. Feasibility at a fixed violation level

Fix a rational \(\delta\ge0\). Define

\( \displaystyle F_i(\delta)= \left\{ s\in[0,H_i]: \ell_i^c-\delta\le\eta_i^c(s)\le u_i^c+\delta \text{ for every }c\in C \right\}. \)

On each path segment, all these inequalities intersect to give a closed interval, possibly empty.

For clarity, the interval contributed by a category is:

  • If \(\beta=1\), then

\(\ell_i^c-\delta-\alpha\le u\le u_i^c+\delta-\alpha\).

  • If \(\beta=-1\), then

\(\alpha-u_i^c-\delta\le u\le\alpha-\ell_i^c+\delta\).

  • If \(\beta=0\), the category either permits the entire segment or excludes it.

Intersect these conditions with \(0\le u\le r_{\rho_{i,h}}\), and translate into the \(s_i\) coordinate. Thus \(F_i(\delta)\) is a computable finite union of closed intervals.

If \(i\) has no rotations, its column is constant, and its bounds can be checked immediately.

The propagation algorithm

Start with \(z=0\). Repeat the following:

  • If every \(s_i(z)\) belongs to \(F_i(\delta)\), return \(x(z)\).
  • Otherwise choose a violating institution \(i\), and find

\( \displaystyle b_i=\min\bigl(F_i(\delta)\cap[s_i(z),H_i]\bigr). \)
If this set is empty, return infeasible.

  • Increase the coordinates on institution \(i\)’s chain to the canonical chain vector with progress \(b_i\).
  • Restore closedness: whenever a positive coordinate has a predecessor, set that predecessor to its full rotation length. Propagate this rule backwards through the DAG.

Since \(F_i(\delta)\) is closed, the minimum in step 2 exists whenever the set is nonempty. Since the current progress is infeasible, \(b_i>s_i(z)\). Thus every successful iteration strictly increases at least one coordinate.

Correctness and student optimality

We prove the invariant

\( \displaystyle z\le z^* \quad\text{for every closed feasible vector }z^*. \)

It holds initially because all rotation coordinates are nonnegative.

Suppose it holds before processing institution \(i\). Then

\(s_i(z^*)\ge s_i(z)\),

and feasibility gives \(s_i(z^*)\in F_i(\delta)\). Therefore,

\(s_i(z^*)\ge b_i\).

The canonical chain coordinates are nondecreasing functions of local progress, so the chain update remains coordinatewise below \(z^*\).

The closedness updates also preserve the invariant. If the current successor coordinate is positive, its coordinate in \(z^*\) is positive. Closedness of \(z^*\) then forces the predecessor coordinate to equal its full length.

If step 2 fails, no feasible \(z^*\) can exist: its local progress would have to be an element of the set declared empty.

If the algorithm returns, its vector is closed and satisfies every institution’s bounds. The invariant says it is the coordinatewise least feasible closed vector. By the dominance implication proved in Section 2, its allocation weakly dominates every stable allocation feasible at this \(\delta\).

Polynomial termination

Let

\(K=\sum_i k_i\le mR\).

For fixed \(\delta\), every segment contributes at most one feasible interval. When an infeasible progress is advanced, the new progress is a left endpoint of one of these intervals.

Consequently, every coordinate can only be increased to:

  • its full length; or
  • an interior left endpoint supplied by a segment of an institution containing that rotation.

Across all coordinates there are at most \(R+K\) such positive candidate values. Closedness propagation introduces only full-length values. Hence there are at most \(R+K\) successful iterations.

This proves polynomial feasibility testing and polynomial computation of the student-optimal allocation at a fixed rational \(\delta\).

5. An exact grid for the optimum

It remains to find the minimum \(\delta\) exactly, with a polynomial number of feasibility tests.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, capacities, and bounds. Its binary length is \(\mathcal O(L)\), and every input quantity belongs to \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z\).

The stable-allocation and rotation constructions use additions, subtractions, and minima of residual capacities. Thus \(x^0\) and all rotation lengths also belong to \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z\).

We claim that

\( \displaystyle \delta^*\in(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z. \)

Proof of the grid claim

Cover the closed-vector set by finitely many cells as follows. In each cell, some coordinates are fixed at their full lengths, some are fixed at zero, and the remaining coordinates vary over their complete intervals \([0,r_\rho]\). Require every predecessor of a varying or full coordinate to be fixed full.

Every closed vector belongs to such a cell: take its full coordinates, its strictly partial coordinates, and its zero coordinates.

The varying rotations in a cell form an antichain. Since rotations containing an institution form a chain, each institution is affected by at most one varying rotation.

Therefore, throughout a cell, each category mass is either constant or has the form

\( \displaystyle a+b u_\rho, \qquad a\in D^{-1}\mathbb Z, \qquad b\in\{-1,0,1\}. \)

Define the allocation’s maximum violation by

\( \displaystyle g(x)= \max\left( 0,\ \max_{i,c}\{\ell_i^c-\eta_i^c(x),\ \eta_i^c(x)-u_i^c\} \right). \)

On the cell, this separates as

\( \displaystyle g(x)=\max\left(G_0,\ \max_\rho G_\rho(u_\rho)\right), \)

where \(G_0\) is constant, and each \(G_\rho\) is a maximum of affine functions whose slopes belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\) and whose intercepts belong to \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z\).

Because the varying coordinates have independent interval domains, the cell optimum is

\( \displaystyle \max\left( G_0,\ \max_\rho\min_{0\le u_\rho\le r_\rho}G_\rho(u_\rho) \right). \)

Each univariate function \(G_\rho\) is convex and piecewise linear. A minimum is attained at a domain endpoint or at an intersection of two affine pieces with different slopes. If the minimum lies on a constant interval, one of that interval’s endpoints suffices.

Domain endpoints belong to \(D^{-1}\mathbb Z\). At an intersection, the slope difference has absolute value one or two, so both the intersection coordinate and its function value belong to \((2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z\). Constant values have the same property.

Thus every cell optimum belongs to this grid. There are finitely many cells, and their union is the entire closed-vector set, so the global optimum also belongs to the grid. This proves the claim.

6. Exact optimization and the requested minimizer

Every feasible allocation has category mass between zero and one, and all input bounds lie between zero and one. Thus \(\delta=1\) permits every stable allocation.

Feasibility is monotone in \(\delta\). By the grid claim, binary search over the integers

\(0\le k\le2D\),

testing \(\delta=k/(2D)\), finds the exact minimum \(\delta^*\). This takes \(\mathcal O(L)\) feasibility tests, although the numerical value of \(D\) may be exponentially large.

Run the propagation algorithm at \(\delta^*\). Its allocation weakly dominates every stable allocation feasible at \(\delta^*\), including every minimizer. It is therefore precisely an aggregate student-optimal minimizer as defined in the question.

7. Running time and bit complexity

Use \(e=\mathcal O(m\tau)\), \(v=m+\tau+2\), and \(R=\mathcal O(e)\).

The rotation representation has \(\mathcal O(ev)\) total size. The institutional paths have \(K=\mathcal O(me)\) segments. At a fixed \(\delta\):

  • Constructing the feasible intervals costs \(\mathcal O(ev(|C|+1))\) arithmetic operations.
  • There are \(\mathcal O(me)\) propagation iterations.
  • A direct implementation of each iteration, including DAG propagation and scanning the local paths, costs \(\mathcal O(ev)\).

A conservative bound per feasibility test is consequently

\( \displaystyle \mathcal O\bigl(e^2v^2+ev(|C|+1)\bigr) \)

arithmetic operations.

During binary search, scale all quantities by \(2D\). Interval endpoints require no divisions beyond division by a slope of absolute value one. All arithmetic therefore uses integers of \(\mathcal O(L)\) bits; sums of polynomially many rotation lengths add only \(\mathcal O(\log e)\) bits, absorbed by \(\mathcal O(L)\).

Using ordinary \(\mathcal O(L^2)\)-cost integer arithmetic and \(\mathcal O(L)\) feasibility tests gives the stated bound

\( \displaystyle \mathcal O\!\left( \left[ (m\tau)^2(m+\tau+2)^2 +m\tau(m+\tau+2)(|C|+1) \right]L^3 \right). \)

Thus both the exact minimum violation and an aggregate student-optimal minimizer are computable in polynomial time in the supplied input parameters.
