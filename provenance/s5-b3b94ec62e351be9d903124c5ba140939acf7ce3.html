Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant and post type sets with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs, strict rankings for applicant types, and either strict rankings or one all-neighbors tie for each post type, does there exist a measurable one-to-one partial matching \(M\) of the atomless type populations such that, for every feasible alternative \(M'\), the measure of agents who prefer \(M\) to \(M'\) is at least the measure who prefer \(M'\) to \(M\)? Each agent remains matched to at most one partner, and unmatched is worse than any acceptable match.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite applicant and post type sets, with compatibility relation \(E\subseteq A\times B\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) positive-mass types, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each applicant type strictly ranks its compatible post types; each post type either strictly ranks its compatible applicant types or ties all of them. Preferences depend only on partner type, and every acceptable partner is strictly better than being unmatched; equal outcomes receive no vote.
Represent each type by a disjoint interval of Lebesgue measure \(\mu_t\), and write \(\lambda\) for the combined measure. A feasible matching is a measure-preserving measurable bijection, with measurable inverse, between measurable subsets of the applicant and post populations, using only compatible pairs; unmatched agents are outside these subsets. Matchings and their constraints are understood modulo null sets.
For two feasible matchings on these same populations, define \(\phi(M,M')=\lambda(\{v:v\text{ strictly prefers its outcome in }M\text{ to its outcome in }M'\})\), counting both sides. A matching \(M\) is popular when \(\phi(M,M')\geq\phi(M',M)\) for every feasible \(M'\).

Is deciding whether such a society admits a popular matching in \(\mathsf{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents, existence with these preference restrictions is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete; when every post ties all neighbors, it is solvable in \(O(n^2)\), where \(n\) counts agents ([Cseh–Huang–Kavitha](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.07168)).
Popular mixed matchings in finite two-sided markets always exist and are computable in polynomial time under expected voting between independently drawn matchings ([Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://cse.iitm.ac.in/~meghana/papers/TCS11-2.pdf)); this compares lotteries, rather than outcomes for fixed atomless agents. No result settling the exact atomless problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified canonical atomless populations as disjoint Lebesgue intervals of the given masses.
  • Required matching bijections to preserve measure and have measurable inverses, so paired populations have equal mass; imposed constraints modulo null sets.
  • Specified that preferences depend only on partner type, so different individuals of the same partner type are indifferent.
  • Interpreted the normalization as total mass one across both sides, without requiring equal applicant and post mass.
  • Specified that alternative matchings act on the same individual populations and that both sides vote with their population measure.
  • Defined m as the number of compatible type pairs and L as total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even when all positive type masses are equal dyadic rationals.

The main point is that the maximum vote margin against an incumbent is a concave function of its type-pair masses. This allows us to transfer finite-agent hardness without assuming that atomless matchings behave like lotteries.

1. Type-pair masses and measurable realizations

Write \(\bot\) for being unmatched. For a feasible matching \(M\), define

\( x_{ab}=\lambda(\{\text{applicants of type }a\text{ matched to posts of type }b\}) \)

for every \((a,b)\in E\). Measure preservation implies that this is also the mass of posts of type \(b\) matched to applicants of type \(a\).

Consequently, the possible mass vectors form the polytope

\( P_\mu=\left\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}: \sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\ \text{for every }a,\quad \sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\leq\mu_b\ \text{for every }b \right\}. \)

Every vector in \(P_\mu\) is realizable: partition each type interval into intervals of the prescribed incident-edge masses and its remaining unmatched mass. For each edge, match the two corresponding intervals by translation. This gives a measure-preserving measurable bijection with measurable inverse.

We will also need the following measurable fact.

Measurable partition and pairing fact. A measurable subset of an interval can be partitioned into finitely many measurable pieces of any prescribed nonnegative masses summing to its measure. Two such subsets of equal positive measure admit a measure-preserving measurable bijection with measurable inverse, modulo null sets.

Here is a justification. For a measurable set \(S\) of measure \(c>0\), its cumulative-measure function

\( F_S(t)=\lambda(S\cap(-\infty,t]) \)

is continuous. Restricted to \(S\), it sends Lebesgue measure to Lebesgue measure on \((0,c)\): the inverse image of \((0,u]\) has measure \(u\), since any plateau of \(F_S\) contains zero \(S\)-mass. Its generalized inverse is

\( Q_S(u)=\inf\{t:F_S(t)\geq u\}. \)

Continuity gives \(F_S(Q_S(u))=u\) for \(0<u<c\). At every density point \(s\) of \(S\) with \(0<F_S(s)<c\), every interval immediately to the left of \(s\) has positive \(S\)-measure, so \(Q_S(F_S(s))=s\). The density theorem therefore makes these maps inverse modulo null sets. Partitioning \((0,c)\) into intervals proves the partition assertion; composing \(F_S\) with \(Q_T\) proves the pairing assertion.

2. An exact linear program for a challenger

For each type \(t\), define its signed vote by

\( d_t(q,p)= \begin{cases} 1,&q\text{ is strictly preferred to }p,\\ -1,&p\text{ is strictly preferred to }q,\\ 0,&q\text{ and }p\text{ are indifferent}. \end{cases} \)

Thus the challenger’s vote margin is

\( \Delta(M',M)=\phi(M',M)-\phi(M,M'). \)

Fix an incumbent matching \(M\) with mass vector \(x\). Partition its population into outcome classes:

  • applicant classes \((a,p)\), where \(p\in N(a)\cup\{\bot\}\);
  • post classes \((b,r)\), where \(r\in N(b)\cup\{\bot\}\).

Their masses are

\( c_{a,p}(x)= \begin{cases} x_{ap},&p\neq\bot,\\ \mu_a-\sum_{b\in N(a)}x_{ab},&p=\bot, \end{cases} \)

and

\( c_{b,r}(x)= \begin{cases} x_{rb},&r\neq\bot,\\ \mu_b-\sum_{a\in N(b)}x_{ab},&r=\bot. \end{cases} \)

These are affine functions of \(x\).

Construct a bipartite graph \(\mathcal H\) on the outcome classes. Join \((a,p)\) to \((b,r)\) whenever \((a,b)\in E\). Assign that edge weight

\( w_{(a,p),(b,r)}=d_a(b,p)+d_b(a,r). \)

For a class \(v=(t,p)\), assign unmatched weight

\( \ell_v=d_t(\bot,p). \)

Consider the linear program

\( F(x)=\max_{z,u\geq0} \left\{ \sum_{e\in E(\mathcal H)}w_ez_e+\sum_v\ell_vu_v: \sum_{e\ni v}z_e+u_v=c_v(x)\ \text{for every }v \right\}. \)

Here \(z_e\) is the mass paired along challenger edge \(e\), and \(u_v\) is the mass of class \(v\) left unmatched by the challenger.

Claim. For every incumbent \(M\) with vector \(x\),

\( F(x)=\max_{M'\text{ feasible}}\Delta(M',M). \)

Proof. A challenger \(M'\) induces feasible variables: \(z_e\) records how much it pairs between the two specified incumbent outcome classes. Measure preservation makes the amount counted from either endpoint equal. Its unmatched portions give \(u_v\). The objective counts precisely the signed votes of all agents.

Conversely, take any feasible \(z,u\). Partition each actual incumbent outcome class into pieces of masses \(z_e\) for its incident edges and a piece of mass \(u_v\). For each edge, pair its two equal-measure pieces using the measurable pairing fact. The finite union of these maps is a feasible challenger with the prescribed objective value. The incumbent pairs impose no additional restriction on a challenger’s pairing of these pieces.

The linear program is nonempty and compact: the choice \(z=0,u=c(x)\) is feasible, and every variable is bounded by a class mass. Its optimum is therefore attained and realizable. This proves the claim. \(\square\)

In particular, popularity depends only on \(x\). Comparing \(M\) with itself gives

\( F(x)\geq0, \qquad M\text{ is popular}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ F(x)=0. \)

3. Concavity forces a popular vertex

Lemma. The function \(F\) is concave on \(P_\mu\).

Proof. Let \(x,y\in P_\mu\), and choose optimal challenger variables \((z^x,u^x)\) and \((z^y,u^y)\). For \(0\leq\theta\leq1\), their coordinatewise convex combination is feasible at \(\theta x+(1-\theta)y\), because

\( c(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)=\theta c(x)+(1-\theta)c(y). \)

The objective coefficients are independent of the incumbent vector. Hence

\( F(\theta x+(1-\theta)y) \geq \theta F(x)+(1-\theta)F(y). \)

\(\square\)

The polytope \(P_\mu\) is nonempty and bounded: it contains zero, and every coordinate \(x_{ab}\) is bounded by both endpoint masses. Therefore every \(x\in P_\mu\) is a convex combination of vertices. Write such a decomposition as

\( x=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_i v_i, \qquad \theta_i>0,\quad \sum_i\theta_i=1. \)

If \(x\) is popular, concavity and nonnegativity imply

\( 0=F(x)\geq\sum_i\theta_iF(v_i)\geq0. \)

Every summand must consequently be zero.

Conclusion. A society admits a popular matching if and only if \(P_\mu\) has a vertex \(v\) with \(F(v)=0\). More strongly, every vertex occurring with positive coefficient in any decomposition of a popular vector is popular.

4. Equal masses give exact equivalence with finite agents

Let \(G=(A\cup B,E)\) be a finite-agent instance, and give each vertex a distinct population type of common mass \(c>0\), with the same compatibility and preferences.

The vertices of \(P_\mu\) are exactly

\( c\,\chi_H, \)

where \(H\) ranges over finite matchings of \(G\).

To justify this integrality assertion, let \(C\) be the vertex-edge incidence matrix of \(G\). Multiplying its post rows by \(-1\) turns it into an oriented incidence matrix. This matrix is totally unimodular: in any square submatrix, a column with at most one nonzero permits determinant expansion; otherwise every column has one \(1\) and one \(-1\), making the rows linearly dependent. Induction gives determinants in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Row sign changes preserve total unimodularity, as does adjoining unit rows for nonnegativity constraints.

Thus the polytope

\( P_{\mathbf1}=\{y\geq0:Cy\leq\mathbf1\} \)

has integral vertices. Its integral feasible vectors are precisely finite matching incidence vectors. Each such vector is a vertex as well: its zero coordinates must remain zero in any convex decomposition, and its coordinates equal to one must remain one because all feasible coordinates are at most one. Finally, \(P_\mu=cP_{\mathbf1}\).

For a finite matching \(H\), every type has just one positive-mass incumbent outcome class. Therefore the challenger linear program at \(c\chi_H\), after dividing all masses by \(c\), has feasible edge vectors exactly \(P_{\mathbf1}\). Eliminating unmatched variables makes its objective affine in those edge vectors. Its maximum is attained at a finite matching incidence vector, giving the exact identity

\( F(c\chi_H) = c\max_{K\text{ a finite matching of }G} \bigl(\phi_{\mathrm{fin}}(K,H)-\phi_{\mathrm{fin}}(H,K)\bigr). \)

Consequently,

\( c\chi_H\text{ is popular atomlessly} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\text{ is popular finitely}. \)

Combining this identity with Section 3 proves

\( \boxed{ G\text{ admits a finite popular matching} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{its equal-mass atomless society admits a popular matching}. } \)

This equivalence accounts for arbitrary measurable challengers, including those that split types.

5. Hardness and polynomial-size certificates

Hardness. Cseh–Huang–Kavitha’s Theorem 2 states that finite popular-matching existence is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete when applicants have strict preferences and each post either has strict preferences or ties all its neighbors. Their voting rule also treats unmatched outcomes as worse than acceptable partners. These are exactly the preference and voting hypotheses needed here. [Cseh–Huang–Kavitha, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.07168)

Given such a finite instance with \(n\) vertices, pad it with isolated applicant vertices until its vertex count is a power of two \(\tau\), where \(n\leq\tau<2n\). Isolated vertices are unmatched in every matching and cast no votes, so padding preserves popular-matching existence.

Give every resulting type mass \(1/\tau\). These masses are positive dyadic rationals summing to one. Preserve all original edges and preferences. Section 4 proves that the resulting atomless instance is a yes-instance exactly when the finite instance is.

The reduction preserves \(m\), increases the support size by less than a factor of two, and adds only \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) encoding bits. Its construction takes \(O(L)\) bit operations in the resulting encoding. Thus the atomless problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even with uniform dyadic masses.

Membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\). For arbitrary rational masses, let \(D\) be the product of their encoded denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and each \(D\mu_t\) is an integer.

The same total-unimodularity argument shows that every vertex of \(DP_\mu\) is integral. Hence every vertex of \(P_\mu\) has coordinates

\( v_{ab}=k_{ab}/D, \qquad 0\leq k_{ab}\leq D. \)

Such a vector has an \(O(mL)\)-bit encoding. By Section 3, every yes-instance has a popular vector of this form.

A verifier checks feasibility and computes \(F(v)\) by exact rational linear programming. The challenger graph has \(2m+\tau\) class vertices and

\( Q=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}(\deg(a)+1)(\deg(b)+1) \leq m(\tau+1)^2 \)

edges. The linear program therefore has \(Q+2m+\tau\) variables and \(2m+\tau\) equality constraints. Its objective coefficients belong to \(\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\), and its rational capacities have polynomial encoding length.

Exact rational linear programming consequently verifies \(F(v)=0\) in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations. Feasibility and the realization argument then guarantee an actual popular measurable matching. This establishes membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\).

6. Why mixed-matching existence does not change the conclusion

Voting between independently drawn finite matchings averages over the incumbent’s outcome. Here, a challenger can assign different new outcomes to different measurable subsets according to their actual incumbent outcomes. Section 2 captures this freedom exactly.

Accordingly, the existence theorem for popular mixed matchings concerns a different comparison rule and does not imply atomless existence. [Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://cse.iitm.ac.in/~meghana/papers/TCS11-2.pdf)
