The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(t\ge3\), given \(m\) candidates, a target \(p\), and a society supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings \(r_i\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\), what is the minimum total mass-weighted number of demotion operations needed to make \(p\) a co-winner under \(t\)-approval, when each ranking type's mass may be split among campaign outcomes? Equivalently, choose masses \(y_{i,S}\ge0\) ending with each size-\(t\) approval set \(S\), minimize \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\), and require \(\sum_{i,S}y_{i,S}(\mathbf{1}[p\in S]-\mathbf{1}[c\in S])\ge0\) for every \(c\ne p\), where \(\sum_S y_{i,S}=\mu_i\) and \(\kappa_i(S)=\rho_i(w_i(S))-t\) for the least-preferred member \(w_i(S)\) of \(S\) in \(r_i\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix an integer \(t\ge3\). Let \(C\) contain \(m>t\) candidates, including a preferred candidate \(p\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) distinct strict complete rankings \(r_i\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(\rho_i(c)\) be the position of \(c\) in \(r_i\), with position \(1\) best.
A demotion operation moves any candidate downward by a positive integer number of positions in the current ranking, preserving the relative order of the other candidates. Each operation costs one per unit mass, independently of its distance; operations act sequentially, and each type's mass may be split among different operation sequences.
For every \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=t\), define \(\kappa_i(S)=\max_{c\in S}\rho_i(c)-t\), the minimum number of operations making \(S\) the top approval set of type \(i\).
Choose real masses \(y_{i,S}\ge0\) with \(\sum_{S:|S|=t}y_{i,S}=\mu_i\), and minimize \(\sum_i\sum_{S:|S|=t}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\).
Under \(t\)-approval, require \(p\) to be a co-winner: \(\sum_i\sum_{S:|S|=t}y_{i,S}(\mathbf{1}[p\in S]-\mathbf{1}[c\in S])\ge0\) for every \(c\ne p\); ties count as wins.
For each fixed \(t\ge3\), can this minimum be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is deciding whether it is at most a given nonnegative binary-rational budget \(B\) NP-hard? Here \(L\) is the total input bit length, including \(B\) in the decision version.
For finite lists of indivisible voters, unit-cost constructive demotion campaigning under \(t\)-approval is NP-hard for every fixed \(t\ge3\), and admits a polynomial-time \(\min\{t,m-t\}\)-approximation ([source](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.21371)).
No prior result settling this exact mass-splitting problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, as appropriate for a distribution over types.
  • Required m>t, carrying over the exclusion of trivial approval domains.
  • Specified strict complete rankings and sequential, unit-cost demotions by any valid positive integer distance, preserving other candidates' relative order.
  • Allowed demotion of any candidate, including p, following the formal discrete operation domain.
  • Introduced a nonnegative binary-rational budget B to state the decision counterpart, and defined L as total input bit length.

Result

For every fixed \(t\ge 3\), the minimum is exactly computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Consequently, the budget decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

The decisive facts are that mass splitting permits real-valued linear-programming variables and that, for fixed \(t\), there are only polynomially many possible approval sets.

1. Approval sets capture the demotion operations exactly

Fix a ranking \(r_i\) and a set \(S\) of size \(t\). Write \(q=\max_{c\in S}\rho_i(c)\), and let \(s\in S\) occupy position \(q\).

Among the first \(q\) candidates, exactly \(q-t\) lie outside \(S\). Each such candidate \(x\) initially precedes \(s\), but must follow \(s\) once \(S\) occupies the top \(t\) positions.

An operation on a candidate other than \(x\) or \(s\) preserves their relative order. Demoting \(s\) cannot change their order from \(x\) preceding \(s\) to \(s\) preceding \(x\). Thus each of those \(q-t\) candidates must itself be demoted at least once. This proves the lower bound \(q-t\).

For the matching upper bound, demote each of those candidates to the bottom, once each. At its operation, each still precedes \(s\), so the demotion has positive distance. Afterward, the first \(t\) candidates are precisely \(S\). Hence the minimum operation count is \(\kappa_i(S)=q-t\).

Therefore any campaign inducing masses \(y_{i,S}\) costs at least \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\). Conversely, any such masses can be realized at exactly that cost: split type \(i\) into portions of mass \(y_{i,S}\), and apply the construction above to each portion. No information about the final ranking beyond its approval set is needed.

2. The explicit linear program

Define \(\mathcal S_t=\{S\subseteq C:|S|=t\}\) and \(N=\tau\binom{m}{t}\). Introduce one variable \(y_{i,S}\) for each type \(i\) and set \(S\in\mathcal S_t\).

Minimize the linear objective

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_t}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\)

subject to the following constraints:

  • \(y_{i,S}\ge 0\) for every \(i,S\);
  • \(\displaystyle \sum_{S\in\mathcal S_t}y_{i,S}=\mu_i\) for every \(i\);
  • \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_t}\bigl(\mathbf 1[p\in S]-\mathbf 1[c\in S]\bigr)y_{i,S}\ge 0\) for every \(c\ne p\).

The second family preserves each type’s mass. In the third family, the left-hand side is exactly \(p\)’s approval score minus \(c\)’s approval score. Thus these constraints impose precisely the required co-winner condition. Section 1 establishes that the objective also agrees exactly with campaign cost.

The feasible region is nonempty. Choose any \(S_0\in\mathcal S_t\) containing \(p\), and assign \(y_{i,S_0}=\mu_i\), with all other variables zero. Then \(p\) scores \(1\); every other candidate scores either \(1\) or \(0\).

Furthermore, nonnegativity and mass conservation give \(0\le y_{i,S}\le\mu_i\le 1\). The feasible region is therefore closed and bounded. Since \(0\le\kappa_i(S)\le m-t\) and the total mass is \(1\), the objective lies between \(0\) and \(m-t\). In particular, its minimum exists.

3. Polynomial size and exact computation

Because \(t\) is fixed, \(N=\tau\binom{m}{t}\le\tau m^t\) is polynomial in \(m,\tau\).

The inverse position tables \(\rho_i(c)\) can be constructed from the rankings in polynomial time. Enumerating every \(S\in\mathcal S_t\) then allows each coefficient \(\kappa_i(S)\) to be computed by taking a maximum over \(t\) positions.

After replacing each mass equality by two inequalities, the program has \(N+2\tau+m-1\) inequality rows. Its constraint coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\); its objective coefficients are integers between \(0\) and \(m-t\); and its right-hand sides are zero or signed copies of the input rationals \(\mu_i\). Even a dense encoding consequently has total bit length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

We now apply the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a linear program with explicitly encoded rational coefficients can be solved in time polynomial in its total binary encoding length, obtaining an exact rational optimum when feasible and bounded. This is a bit-complexity guarantee, including exact recovery, rather than merely numerical approximation; see [Trevisan’s linear-programming notes, Sections 2.4–2.5](https://lucatrevisan.wordpress.com/2011/01/24/cs261-lecture-5-linear-programming/).

All hypotheses hold here: the coefficients are rational, the explicit encoding has polynomial length, and the program is feasible and bounded. Thus its exact optimal value, and an optimal rational allocation, can be computed in the required time.

For the decision version, append the single rational constraint \(\sum_{i,S}\kappa_i(S)y_{i,S}\le B\). Because the minimum is attained, this augmented program is feasible exactly when the minimum is at most \(B\). Its encoding remains polynomial, so rational LP feasibility decides the question in polynomial time, including equality with the budget.

4. Relation to indivisible voters

For indivisible voters, allocating an individual voter to approval sets requires a discrete choice. The present problem expressly permits splitting each type’s mass, so no integrality constraint applies to \(y_{i,S}\). The polynomial-size linear program solves exactly the stated mass-splitting problem; the supplied hardness result for indivisible voters does not transfer.
