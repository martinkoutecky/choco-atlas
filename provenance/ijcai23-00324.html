The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\), and each type's strict order \(\succ_\theta\) over partner types, form \(A=\bigsqcup_{\theta\in\Theta}A_\theta\) with \(\lambda(A_\theta)=\mu_\theta\), treating partners of the same type as tied. On mass-IMB instances, obtained by iteratively removing \(\delta=\min(r_\theta,r_\phi)\) of mutually best residual types, decide whether there exists a measurable fixed-point-free involution \(M:A\to A\) such that \(\Delta(M',M)\le 0\) for every feasible involution \(M'\), where \(\Delta(M',M)\) is the mass preferring its partner type under \(M'\) minus the mass preferring its partner type under \(M\); equivalently, construct such an \(M\) or a symmetric type-flow \(x_{\theta\phi}\) with a realization.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), summing to \(1\), and a strict order \(\succ_\theta\) over all partner types \(\Theta\) for each type. Here \(m=\tau\) is the number of partner types; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference orders, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Let \(A=\bigsqcup_{\theta\in\Theta}(\{\theta\}\times[0,\mu_\theta))\), with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\), and let \(t(a)\) denote an agent's type. All distinct agents may be partners, including agents of the same type; partners of equal type are tied.
Define mass-IMB as follows: start with \(r_\theta=\mu_\theta\). A permitted step chooses distinct positive-residual types that rank each other first among positive-residual types, and subtracts \(\delta=\min(r_\theta,r_\phi)\) from both residuals. A type ranking itself first may instead have its entire residual removed by internal pairing. The input is promised to admit a sequence exhausting all residual mass.
A feasible matching is a measurable, measure-preserving, fixed-point-free involution \(M:A\to A\); every agent is matched. Define \(\Delta(M',M)=\lambda\{a:t(M'(a))\succ_{t(a)}t(M(a))\}-\lambda\{a:t(M(a))\succ_{t(a)}t(M'(a))\}\). A matching is popular if \(\Delta(M',M)\leq 0\) for every feasible \(M'\).
A type-flow is \(x_{\theta\phi}=\lambda\{a\in A_\theta:t(M(a))=\phi\}\), so \(x_{\theta\phi}=x_{\phi\theta}\geq0\) and \(\sum_\phi x_{\theta\phi}=\mu_\theta\); diagonal entries count agent mass, and popularity refers to a realization on the same society.

Is deciding whether a mass-IMB society admits a popular matching in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with strict preferences admitting exhaustive iterative removal of mutually best agent pairs, a popular perfect roommate matching always exists and is computable in polynomial time ([Wilczynski](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0324.pdf)).
Related fractional-roommate algorithms use specified pairings of fractional allocations to count votes ([Csáji](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16173v2)); these are not the measurable-involution problem above. No result settling this exact continuous problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Discard zero-mass types and retain the stated normalization to total mass one.
  • Use labeled half-open intervals with Lebesgue measure to specify an atomless society, and require matchings to preserve measure.
  • Include a type's own type in its partner ranking: another agent of the same type is an admissible partner, while pairing an agent with itself is forbidden.
  • Complete mass-IMB by allowing internally mutually best same-type agents to exhaust that type's residual mass, and require exhaustion of the whole society, reflecting perfect matching in a 2-cluster graph.
  • Interpret diagonal type-flow entries as agent mass, and retain popularity of a realization rather than introduce an independent fractional voting convention.
  • Set the otherwise unused size parameter m equal to the number of partner types, hence to the support size.

Proven partial results

The general complexity classification remains unresolved. I prove four additional structural results:

  • Every popular support has a witness whose potentials belong to $ \{-1,-\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12,1\} $.
  • If the masses have common denominator $D$, every realizable popular support admits a flow with denominator dividing $2D$.
  • All permitted mass-IMB sequences produce the same accumulated flow and terminal residuals. Consequently, the mass-IMB promise is recognizable in polynomial time.
  • Popular-matching existence has an exact characterization for star societies. In particular, a mass-IMB society with only three types can have no popular matching.

The proofs below include the support criterion needed from the earlier work, with nonnegativity explicitly included in the partition-realization lemma.

1. Realization and the exact popularity criterion

For each type $\theta$, let $A_\theta=\{\theta\}\times[0,\mu_\theta)$. Define
$$ s_\theta(\psi,\phi)= \begin{cases} 1,&\psi\succ_\theta\phi,\\ 0,&\psi=\phi,\\ -1,&\phi\succ_\theta\psi. \end{cases} $$

Realizing a type-flow

Every matrix satisfying
$$ x_{\theta\phi}=x_{\phi\theta}\geq 0, \qquad \sum_\phi x_{\theta\phi}=\mu_\theta $$
is realized by a feasible matching.

Indeed, partition each $A_\theta$ into half-open intervals of lengths $x_{\theta\phi}$. For distinct types, pair the corresponding equal-length intervals by inverse translations. For a diagonal interval, exchange its two halves by translation. The resulting map is measurable, measure-preserving, involutive, and fixed-point-free.

We also require realization on the classes of an existing matching.

Partition-realization lemma. Let $B_u$, for $u$ in a finite index set, partition the society up to a null set, with $b_u=\lambda(B_u)>0$. Suppose
$$ y_{uv}=y_{vu}\geq 0 $$
for distinct indices, and $y_{uu}\geq 0$, with
$$ 2y_{uu}+\sum_{v\ne u}y_{uv}=b_u. $$
Then there is a feasible matching having external pair mass $y_{uv}$ between $B_u$ and $B_v$, and internal agent mass $2y_{uu}$ in $B_u$.

Proof. Each $B_u$ is a finite-measure nonatomic Lebesgue space. The standard isomorphism theorem for nonatomic Lebesgue spaces identifies it, modulo null sets, with an interval of length $b_u$. Subdivide the intervals according to the prescribed masses and use translations and half-exchanges as above.

The identifications may omit null sets. Remove these exceptional sets and their images under the interval involution; the remaining domain is invariant and conull. Pulling the construction back gives an involution on a conull subset of the society.

Finally, remove countably infinitely many complete pairs from this subset and add their endpoints to the exceptional null set. That exceptional set is now infinite and can be partitioned into pairs. Extend the involution by exchanging those pairs. Every subset of this null set is Lebesgue measurable, so the extension is measurable and preserves measure. It changes none of the prescribed masses. $\square$

The challenger linear program

Fix a feasible matching $M$ with flow $x$. Its positive classes are indexed by
$$ G=\{(\theta,\phi):x_{\theta\phi}>0\}. $$
For $u=(\theta,\phi)$, put
$$ B_u=\{a\in A_\theta:t(M(a))=\phi\}, \qquad b_u=x_{\theta\phi}, \qquad \bar u=(\phi,\theta). $$
Symmetry implies $\bar u\in G$ and $b_u=b_{\bar u}$.

For $u=(\theta,\phi)$ and $v=(\psi,\chi)$, define
$$ w_{uv}=s_\theta(\psi,\phi)+s_\psi(\theta,\chi). $$
Choose an ordering of $G$. The maximum attainable challenger margin is exactly the optimum of
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize}\quad& \sum_{u\leq v}w_{uv}y_{uv},\\ \text{subject to}\quad& 2y_{uu}+\sum_{v<u}y_{vu}+\sum_{v>u}y_{uv}=b_u \quad(u\in G),\\ &y_{uv}\geq 0. \end{aligned} $$

A challenger induces these pair masses, with the displayed objective equal to its vote margin. Conversely, the partition-realization lemma realizes every feasible solution on the actual classes of $M$. Thus this linear program compares matchings on the same society.

The baseline matching supplies a feasible solution of objective zero. The feasible region is bounded, because each variable is bounded by the relevant class masses. Its dual is
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad& \sum_{u\in G}b_u\alpha_u,\\ \text{subject to}\quad& \alpha_u+\alpha_v\geq w_{uv} \quad(u,v\in G), \end{aligned} $$
where the potentials are unrestricted in sign. For $u=v$, the constraint is $2\alpha_u\geq w_{uu}$. Finite-dimensional linear-programming strong duality applies.

Support criterion

Proposition. A realization of $x$ is popular if and only if there are potentials satisfying
$$ \alpha_u+\alpha_v\geq w_{uv} \quad(u,v\in G), \qquad \alpha_u+\alpha_{\bar u}=0 \quad(u\in G). $$
Consequently, popularity depends only on the positive support among feasible flows.

Proof. If $M$ is popular, the primal and dual optima are zero. In the baseline primal solution, each off-diagonal reverse pair $u,\bar u$ carries positive mass $b_u$, and a diagonal class carries loop pair mass $b_u/2$. These pairings all have weight zero.

For an optimal dual solution, the primal-dual gap is
$$ \sum_{u\leq v} y_{uv}\bigl(\alpha_u+\alpha_v-w_{uv}\bigr)=0. $$
Every summand is nonnegative. Applying this identity to the baseline solution forces zero slack on every baseline pairing. Hence
$$ \alpha_u+\alpha_{\bar u}=0. $$
For a diagonal class this gives $\alpha_{(\theta,\theta)}=0$.

Conversely, the reverse-pair equations and $b_u=b_{\bar u}$ give
$$ \sum_{u\in G}b_u\alpha_u=0. $$
Weak duality therefore bounds every challenger margin by zero. $\square$

2. Two partners per type and five possible potential values

At most two positive partner types

The loop constraint for $u=(\theta,\phi)$ gives
$$ \alpha_u\geq s_\theta(\theta,\phi)\geq -1. $$
Applying this to $\bar u$ and using antisymmetry gives
$$ -1\leq\alpha_u\leq 1. $$

Suppose a type $\theta$ has positive allocations to distinct partners $p,q$, with $p\succ_\theta q$. The constraints between $(\theta,q)$ and $(p,\theta)$, and between $(\theta,p)$ and $(q,\theta)$, give respectively
$$ \alpha_{(\theta,q)}-\alpha_{(\theta,p)}\geq 1, \qquad \alpha_{(\theta,p)}-\alpha_{(\theta,q)}\geq -1. $$
The second endpoint's vote is zero in each comparison because its old and new partner types are both $\theta$. Thus
$$ \alpha_{(\theta,q)}-\alpha_{(\theta,p)}=1. $$
This calculation also applies when one partner equals $\theta$.

Three positive partners $p\succ_\theta q\succ_\theta r$ would imply
$$ \alpha_{(\theta,r)}-\alpha_{(\theta,p)}=2 $$
by the successive comparisons, but the direct comparison gives $1$. Therefore:

Proposition. Every popular matching assigns each type positive mass to at most two partner types, counting its own type as one partner type.

Half-integral witness theorem

Theorem. Whenever the support criterion is feasible, it has a witness with
$$ \alpha_u\in \left\{-1,-\frac12,0,\frac12,1\right\} \quad(u\in G). $$

Proof. Start with any witness $\alpha$, and set
$$ \beta_u=\lfloor\alpha_u\rfloor. $$
Because $\alpha_{\bar v}=-\alpha_v$, every support inequality can be written as
$$ \alpha_u-\alpha_{\bar v}\geq w_{uv}. $$
The right side is an integer. Taking floors therefore preserves this inequality:
$$ \beta_u-\beta_{\bar v}\geq w_{uv}. $$
Interchanging $u$ and $v$ also gives
$$ \beta_v-\beta_{\bar u}\geq w_{uv}. $$

Define
$$ \widehat\alpha_u=\frac{\beta_u-\beta_{\bar u}}2. $$
Then $\widehat\alpha_{\bar u}=-\widehat\alpha_u$, and adding the preceding two inequalities yields
$$ \widehat\alpha_u+\widehat\alpha_v = \frac{\beta_u+\beta_v-\beta_{\bar u}-\beta_{\bar v}}2 \geq w_{uv}. $$
Thus $\widehat\alpha$ is a witness.

The bounds $-1\leq\alpha_u\leq1$ imply $\beta_u\in\{-1,0,1\}$. Hence every $\widehat\alpha_u$ belongs to the five stated values. $\square$

This theorem makes the dual witness discrete. It does not itself provide a polynomial-time procedure for choosing a support.

3. A denominator bound and compact certificates

Let $D$ be a positive integer such that
$$ D\mu_\theta\in\mathbb Z \quad\text{for every }\theta. $$

Theorem. If a society admits a popular matching, it admits one whose flow satisfies
$$ x_{\theta\phi}\in\frac1{2D}\mathbb Z \quad\text{for every }\theta,\phi. $$
More strongly, every feasible popular support admits such a flow with exactly the same support.

Proof. Fix a feasible popular support. By Section 2, each type has at most two positive partners.

Construct an undirected graph from its positive off-diagonal entries and record each positive diagonal entry as a loop. The off-diagonal graph has maximum degree two. A vertex with a loop has off-diagonal degree at most one. Therefore every connected component is one of:

  • an isolated vertex with a loop;
  • a path, with possible loops at its endpoints;
  • a cycle of length at least three, with no loops.

We analyze their mass equations separately.

An isolated loop has flow $\mu_\theta$, which belongs to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

For a path $v_1,\ldots,v_n$, write $z_i$ for the edge flow between $v_i$ and $v_{i+1}$. At an internal vertex,
$$ z_i=\mu_{v_i}-z_{i-1}. $$
Consequently, writing $s=z_1$, every edge flow has the form
$$ z_i=(-1)^{i-1}s+c_i, \qquad c_i\in\frac1D\mathbb Z. $$
A present endpoint loop also has the form $\pm s+c$, with $c\in D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

If either endpoint has no loop, its row equation fixes $s$ to a value in $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. All flows then belong to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

If both endpoints have loops, positivity of all edges and loops gives an open interval of permitted values of $s$. Each inequality is of the form $\pm s+c>0$, so both endpoints of this interval belong to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. The interval is bounded because the first endpoint requires
$$ 0<s<\mu_{v_1}. $$
It is nonempty by the assumed feasibility. Choosing its midpoint gives $s\in(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z$ and preserves strict positivity of every proposed entry.

For a cycle $v_1,\ldots,v_n$, let $z_i$ be the edge flow from $v_i$ to $v_{i+1}$, with cyclic indexing. Again,
$$ z_i=(-1)^{i-1}s+c_i, \qquad c_i\in\frac1D\mathbb Z. $$
The closing equation is
$$ s+z_n=\mu_{v_1}. $$
If $n$ is odd, this fixes
$$ 2s=\mu_{v_1}-c_n, $$
so every flow belongs to $(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

If $n$ is even, the closing equation is a consistency condition independent of $s$. Positivity gives a nonempty bounded open interval whose endpoints belong to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$: the inequalities $z_1=s>0$ and $z_2=\mu_{v_2}-s>0$ already bound it. Choose its midpoint.

Combining the componentwise choices produces a flow with exactly the original support and the claimed denominator. The support criterion preserves popularity, and Section 1 realizes the flow. $\square$

Consequences for certification and search

Under the usual binary encoding of rational numbers, one can take $D$ to be the product of the input denominators. Its binary length is polynomial in $L$.

A certificate for existence can therefore consist of:

  • a symmetric flow with at most two positive entries per row, each encoded as an integer divided by $2D$;
  • a five-valued potential for every positive oriented entry.

The verifier checks the row sums, nonnegativity, symmetry, reverse-pair equations, and support inequalities by exact rational arithmetic. These checks take polynomial time. The realization and support propositions prove their sufficiency.

Thus the existence problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, including under the mass-IMB promise.

For completeness, a deterministic support search still gives
$$ \tau^{2\tau}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time. For each type, enumerate a nonempty partner set of size at most two. There are at most
$$ \tau+\binom{\tau}{2}\leq\tau^2 $$
choices. Reject nonsymmetric supports.

For each remaining support, check the potential inequalities by rational linear programming. Check exact-support flow feasibility by maximizing $0\leq\varepsilon\leq1$ subject to the flow equations, zero entries outside the support, and entries at least $\varepsilon$ inside it. The support is feasible exactly when the optimum is positive. Both programs have polynomial size.

Neither this search nor the discrete certificate establishes polynomial-time existence when $\tau$ varies.

4. Mass-IMB removal is confluent

A removal state consists of its residual vector $r$ and its accumulated symmetric flow $g$. A cross-type step on $\theta,\phi$ adds
$$ \delta=\min(r_\theta,r_\phi) $$
to both $g_{\theta\phi}$ and $g_{\phi\theta}$, and subtracts $\delta$ from both residuals. An internal step adds $r_\theta$ to $g_{\theta\theta}$ and sets $r_\theta=0$.

A state is terminal if no permitted step exists.

Theorem. Starting from any fixed state, every maximal permitted sequence reaches the same terminal residual vector and the same accumulated flow.

Proof. Every step removes at least one positive-residual type. Hence every sequence terminates after at most the initial number of positive types.

Two distinct simultaneously permitted steps involve disjoint type sets. Indeed, a positive type has a unique first-ranked type among the positive-residual types. It can therefore belong to at most one mutually best pair, or to its internal step when it ranks itself first.

Such disjoint steps commute. Performing either step does not change the other step's endpoint masses. Removing unrelated types also preserves the other endpoints' first choices. Thus the second step remains permitted, uses the same mass, and the two orders yield identical residuals and accumulated flows.

We now prove terminal-state uniqueness by induction on the number of positive-residual types. A terminal initial state has a unique outcome. Otherwise, consider two possible first steps.

If they coincide, uniqueness follows from induction after that step. If they differ, they commute to a common state after both steps. Each state after one step has fewer positive types. By induction, its terminal outcome is unique; because the other step remains permitted, that outcome equals the unique terminal outcome from the common state. Therefore the two first-step choices have the same terminal outcome. This proves uniqueness for the initial state. $\square$

Corollary. The mass-IMB promise is recognizable in polynomial time, and its exhaustive greedy flow is unique.

Proof. Repeatedly choose any permitted step until none remains. There are at most $\tau$ steps. Finding first choices and performing the rational updates takes polynomial time in $\tau,L$. By the theorem, an exhaustive sequence exists exactly when this procedure terminates with every residual zero. Its accumulated flow is independent of all choices. $\square$

This rules out searching over removal orders as a way to obtain a different greedy flow. It does not imply that the unique greedy flow is popular.

5. Exact classification of star societies

Consider types
$$ \Theta=\{c,1,\ldots,n\}, \qquad n\geq1, $$
with preferences
$$ 1\succ_c2\succ_c\cdots\succ_cn\succ_cc. $$
Each leaf $i$ ranks $c$ first, itself second, and all other leaves below itself in an arbitrary strict order.

Write
$$ b=\mu_c, \qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^n\mu_i. $$

Every such society is mass-IMB. While the center and some leaf remain positive, the center and its highest-ranked remaining leaf rank each other first. Once the center disappears, all remaining leaves can be removed internally. If the leaves disappear first, the center can be removed internally.

Theorem. A star society admits a popular matching exactly in the following cases:
$$ n=1, \qquad\text{or}\qquad n\geq2\ \text{and}\ b\leq\mu_1+\mu_2. $$

Necessity

Suppose a popular flow exists.

No edge between distinct leaves can be positive. If $x_{ij}>0$, the loop inequalities give
$$ \alpha_{(i,j)}\geq1, \qquad \alpha_{(j,i)}\geq1, $$
because each leaf prefers its own type to the other leaf. This contradicts their reverse-pair sum being zero.

Consequently,
$$ \sum_i x_{ii}=S-b+x_{cc}. $$
If both $x_{cc}>0$ and some $x_{ii}>0$, their diagonal potentials are zero, but the constraint between these diagonal classes has weight $2$: both types prefer each other to internal pairing. This is impossible.

If $b>S$, then $x_{cc}\geq b-S>0$. All leaf diagonals must therefore vanish, so every leaf's entire mass goes to the center. For $n\geq2$, the center has all $n$ leaves and itself as positive partners, contradicting the two-partner bound.

Now suppose $b\leq S$. A positive $x_{cc}$ would imply
$$ \sum_i x_{ii}=S-b+x_{cc}>0, $$
again giving the forbidden pair of positive diagonals. Hence $x_{cc}=0$.

The center has one or two positive leaf partners.

If its sole partner is $k$, every other leaf is internally paired. If some $j<k$, the constraint between $(c,k)$ and $(j,j)$ has weight $2$, whereas its potential sum is at most $1$. Therefore $k=1$, and feasibility requires $b\leq\mu_1$.

If its partners are $p<q$, every leaf before $q$ other than $p$ is internally paired. Any such leaf gives the same weight-$2$ contradiction with $(c,q)$. Thus $p=1$ and $q=2$.

Moreover, leaf $1$ cannot have a positive diagonal. Otherwise its two positive partners, $c$ and itself, force
$$ \alpha_{(1,c)}=-1, \qquad \alpha_{(c,1)}=1. $$
The center prefers $1$ to $2$, so
$$ \alpha_{(c,2)}=\alpha_{(c,1)}+1=2, $$
contradicting the potential bound. Hence
$$ x_{c1}=\mu_1, \qquad x_{c2}=b-\mu_1, $$
and feasibility requires
$$ \mu_1<b\leq\mu_1+\mu_2. $$
This proves necessity for $n\geq2$.

Sufficiency

We verify witnesses using a convenient equivalent form of the support inequalities. For each type $i$, define
$$ H_i(j)= \max_{p:\,x_{ip}>0} \bigl(s_i(j,p)-\alpha_{(i,p)}\bigr). $$
For fixed types $i,j$, maximizing the two sides over their positive classes shows that all their support inequalities are equivalent to
$$ H_i(j)+H_j(i)\leq0. $$
For $i=j$, this condition is $2H_i(i)\leq0$. Reverse-pair equations must also hold.

Case 1: $b\leq\mu_1$. Set
$$ x_{c1}=b,\qquad x_{11}=\mu_1-b,\qquad x_{jj}=\mu_j\quad(j\geq2), $$
with symmetric entries understood and all other entries zero. Assign
$$ \alpha_{(c,1)}=1,\qquad \alpha_{(1,c)}=-1, $$
and potential zero to every positive diagonal.

Direct substitution gives
$$ H_c(1)=-1,\qquad H_1(c)=1, $$
and, for $j\geq2$,
$$ H_c(j)=-2,\qquad H_j(c)=1. $$
Between distinct leaves,
$$ H_1(j)=0,\qquad H_j(1)=-1\quad(j\geq2), $$
and $H_j(k)=-1$ for distinct $j,k\geq2$. Finally,
$$ H_c(c)=-2,\qquad H_1(1)=0,\qquad H_j(j)=0\quad(j\geq2). $$
Every required sum is nonpositive. The reverse-pair equations hold, so the flow is popular.

Case 2: $\mu_1<b\leq\mu_1+\mu_2$. Set
$$ x_{c1}=\mu_1,\qquad x_{c2}=b-\mu_1,\qquad x_{22}=\mu_2-b+\mu_1, $$
and
$$ x_{jj}=\mu_j\quad(j\geq3). $$
Assign
$$ \alpha_{(c,1)}=\alpha_{(1,c)}=0, \qquad \alpha_{(c,2)}=1,\qquad \alpha_{(2,c)}=-1, $$
and potential zero to every positive diagonal.

The center-leaf quantities are
$$ H_c(1)=H_1(c)=0, $$
$$ H_c(2)=-1,\qquad H_2(c)=1, $$
and, for $j\geq3$,
$$ H_c(j)=-1,\qquad H_j(c)=1. $$
For distinct leaves, $H_2(j)=0$, while $H_i(j)=-1$ whenever $i\ne2$. Thus every leaf-leaf sum is nonpositive. The self quantities are
$$ H_c(c)=-1,\qquad H_1(1)=-1,\qquad H_2(2)=0, $$
with $H_j(j)=0$ for $j\geq3$. Again the criterion proves popularity.

Case 3: $n=1$ and $b>\mu_1$. Set
$$ x_{c1}=\mu_1,\qquad x_{cc}=b-\mu_1. $$
Assign
$$ \alpha_{(c,1)}=-1,\qquad \alpha_{(1,c)}=1,\qquad \alpha_{(c,c)}=0. $$
Then
$$ H_c(1)=1,\qquad H_1(c)=-1,\qquad H_c(c)=0,\qquad H_1(1)=-2. $$
These satisfy every inequality. Cases 1 and 3 cover all one-leaf societies. $\square$

A smallest-support nonexistence example

Take
$$ \mu_c=\frac34,\qquad \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac18, $$
with preferences
$$ 1\succ_c2\succ_cc, \qquad c\succ_11\succ_12, \qquad c\succ_22\succ_21. $$
The permitted sequence pairs $c$ with $1$, then with $2$, removing $1/8$ from each endpoint at each step, and internally removes the remaining center mass $1/2$. Thus the society is mass-IMB.

However,
$$ \mu_c=\frac34>\mu_1+\mu_2=\frac14, $$
so the theorem proves that no popular matching exists.

Three types are minimal for this phenomenon. With one type, every comparison has zero margin. With two types, if at least one type ranks itself first, internally pairing both types satisfies the support criterion with zero potentials: the cross-type weight is at most zero. If both types rank the other first, the one-leaf star result applies.

6. What remains open

The results prove $\mathrm{NP}$ membership, a finite potential normal form, a bounded-denominator flow normal form, polynomial-time recognition of mass-IMB, and an exact solution for star societies.

They do not provide a polynomial-time support-selection algorithm or an NP-hardness reduction for arbitrary mass-IMB societies. The general question remains to select a symmetric support that simultaneously satisfies the mass equations and the support inequalities. Confluence settles the choice of greedy removal order, but it does not settle that support-selection problem.
