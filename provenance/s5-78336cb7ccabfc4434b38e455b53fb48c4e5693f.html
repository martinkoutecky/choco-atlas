The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type and object-class sets \(T\) and \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita class supplies \(q_j>0\) summing to \(1\), rational values \(v_{tj}\), and compensation ceilings \(c_j\), find a fair allocation with a componentwise greatest payment vector. The decision variables are mass assignments \(y_{tj}\ge 0\) and class payments \(x_j\le c_j\), with \(\sum_j y_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{tj}=q_j\); fairness requires that whenever \(y_{tj}>0\), \(v_{tj}+x_j\ge v_{t\ell}+x_\ell\) for every \(\ell\in J\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T\) and \(J\) be nonempty finite sets, with \(\tau=|T|\) and \(m=|J|\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Class supplies \(q_j>0\) are binary rationals with \(\sum_jq_j=1\). Values \(v_{tj}\) and finite compensation ceilings \(c_j\) are arbitrary signed binary rationals. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real mass assignments \(y_{tj}\ge0\) and real per-person class payments \(x_j\le c_j\), satisfying \(\sum_jy_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_ty_{tj}=q_j\). Every person receives one object; a type's mass may split across classes. Payments have no lower bound or aggregate budget constraint, and there is no outside option.
An allocation is fair when \(y_{tj}>0\) implies \(v_{tj}+x_j\ge v_{t\ell}+x_\ell\) for every \(\ell\in J\). Ties permit splitting without a prescribed tie-breaking rule.
Its payment vector is componentwise greatest if \(x_j\ge x'_j\) for every \(j\in J\) and every feasible fair allocation \((y',x')\).

Is finding a fair allocation with a componentwise greatest payment vector solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For discrete societies with equally many agents and objects, fair allocations coincide with optimal assignment–dual pairs, with payments equal to negated object dual variables; a greatest feasible payment vector can be computed in polynomial time. [Shioura, Sun and Yang](https://orsj.org/wp-content/or-archives50/pdf/e_mag/49-2-144-150.pdf)
No result settling this exact support-encoded continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Unspecified ceiling encoding and signs: ceilings are finite signed binary rationals; values may also have either sign.
  • Unspecified solution domains: assignments and payments are real, with payments unrestricted below, as in the discrete model.
  • Unspecified financial and participation constraints: impose no aggregate payment budget or outside option, consistently with the discrete feasible-allocation definition.
  • Unspecified tie handling: weak utility comparisons allow arbitrary splitting among tied best classes.
  • Unspecified meaning of greatest: the payment vector must dominate those of all feasible fair allocations, including allocations with different mass assignments.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Polynomial-time solvability

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). A componentwise greatest feasible fair payment vector always exists. It can be computed by solving one transportation linear program and then one shortest-path problem, directly on the supported types.

The proof extends the assignment–dual and shortest-path approach in [Shioura, Sun and Yang, Section 2](https://orsj.org/wp-content/or-archives50/pdf/e_mag/49-2-144-150.pdf). The essential point is that every fair payment vector is compatible with every optimal transportation allocation, including allocations that split types.

1. Transportation optimality and fairness

Consider the transportation linear program:

\( \displaystyle (P)\qquad \max_y \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in J}v_{tj}y_{tj} \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle \sum_{j\in J}y_{tj}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in T}y_{tj}=q_j\quad(j\in J),\qquad y_{tj}\ge 0. \)

Its feasible region is nonempty: \(y_{tj}=\mu_tq_j\) satisfies both families of equalities. It is closed and bounded, since \(0\le y_{tj}\le\mu_t\). Thus its optimum is finite and attained.

The dual is

\( \displaystyle (D)\qquad \min_{u,p} \left(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_t+\sum_{j\in J}q_jp_j\right) \)

subject to \(u_t+p_j\ge v_{tj}\) for every \(t,j\), with \(u,p\) unrestricted in sign. By linear-programming strong duality, an optimal dual solution exists and has the same objective value as an optimal primal solution.

For every primal-feasible \(y\) and dual-feasible \((u,p)\), the marginal constraints give the identity

\( \displaystyle \sum_t\mu_tu_t+\sum_jq_jp_j-\sum_{t,j}v_{tj}y_{tj} = \sum_{t,j}y_{tj}(u_t+p_j-v_{tj}). \)

Every summand on the right is nonnegative. Consequently, whenever \(y\) and \((u,p)\) are both optimal,

\( \displaystyle y_{tj}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ u_t+p_j=v_{tj}. \)

This implication holds for any pairing of an optimal primal solution with an optimal dual solution.

Lemma. An allocation \(y\) and real payment vector \(x\) satisfy the fairness conditions if and only if \(y\) is optimal for \((P)\) and \((u,-x)\) is optimal for \((D)\) for some \(u\).

Proof. Suppose first that \((y,x)\) is fair. Define

\( \displaystyle u_t=\max_{\ell\in J}(v_{t\ell}+x_\ell), \qquad p_j=-x_j. \)

Then \(u_t+p_j\ge v_{tj}\), so \((u,p)\) is dual-feasible. Fairness implies equality whenever \(y_{tj}>0\). The displayed gap identity therefore has value zero. By weak duality, both solutions are optimal.

Conversely, suppose \(y\) and \((u,-x)\) are optimal. If \(y_{tj}>0\), the zero-gap identity gives \(u_t=v_{tj}+x_j\). Dual feasibility gives \(u_t\ge v_{t\ell}+x_\ell\) for every \(\ell\). These are precisely the required fairness inequalities. \(\square\)

2. Fixing one optimal allocation captures all fair payments

Fix any optimal solution \(y^*\) of \((P)\), and define its positive support by

\( \displaystyle S=\{(t,j)\in T\times J:y^*_{tj}>0\}. \)

Let \(F\) denote the set of payment vectors occurring in feasible fair allocations.

We claim that

\( \displaystyle F= \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^m: x_j\le c_j\ \text{for all }j,\quad x_\ell-x_j\le v_{tj}-v_{t\ell} \ \text{for all }(t,j)\in S,\ \ell\in J \right\}. \)

To prove the forward inclusion, take any feasible fair allocation \((y',x)\). The lemma supplies an optimal dual solution \((u,-x)\). Pairing this dual solution with the fixed optimal allocation \(y^*\), the zero-gap identity shows that \((y^*,x)\) is fair. Hence all the displayed inequalities hold.

For the reverse inclusion, the displayed inequalities say exactly that every class receiving positive mass from type \(t\) in \(y^*\) maximizes that type’s utility. Together with the ceilings, they make \((y^*,x)\) a feasible fair allocation.

Moreover, \(F\) is nonempty. Choose an optimal dual solution and let \(x^0=-p\). The lemma shows that \((y^*,x^0)\) is fair. Set

\( \displaystyle a=\min_{j\in J}(c_j-x^0_j), \qquad \widehat x_j=x^0_j+a. \)

All ceilings are finite, so \(a\) is finite. We have \(\widehat x_j\le c_j\) for every \(j\). Adding the same constant to all payments preserves every utility comparison, so \(\widehat x\in F\).

This step uses the absence of payment lower bounds, a budget constraint, and an outside option.

3. Shortest paths give the greatest vector

Because \(q_j>0\), the equality \(\sum_t y^*_{tj}=q_j\) implies that every class \(j\) receives positive mass from at least one type. Thus, for every ordered pair \(j,\ell\), the following minimum is over a nonempty set:

\( \displaystyle w_{j\ell} = \min_{t:\,y^*_{tj}>0}(v_{tj}-v_{t\ell}). \)

The characterization of \(F\) becomes

\( \displaystyle F= \{x\in\mathbb R^m: x_j\le c_j\ \text{for all }j,\quad x_\ell\le x_j+w_{j\ell}\ \text{for all }j,\ell\}. \)

Construct a directed graph with vertex set \(J\cup\{s\}\), where \(s\) is a new source. Add:

  • an edge \(s\to j\) of length \(c_j\) for each \(j\);
  • an edge \(j\to\ell\) of length \(w_{j\ell}\) for each ordered pair \(j,\ell\).

There is no negative directed cycle. Indeed, choose any \(\widehat x\in F\) and extend it by \(\widehat x_s=0\). Every edge \(a\to b\) of length \(w\) satisfies \(\widehat x_b-\widehat x_a\le w\). Summing these inequalities around a directed cycle gives \(0\le\) its total length.

Every class is reachable directly from \(s\). Since all cycle lengths are nonnegative, removing cycles from a walk cannot increase its length. Hence each shortest-path distance \(d_j\) from \(s\) is finite and is attained by a simple path.

The distance vector is feasible. Direct source edges give \(d_j\le c_j\). Appending \(j\to\ell\) to a shortest path to \(j\) gives

\( \displaystyle d_\ell\le d_j+w_{j\ell}. \)

Thus \(d\in F\), and \((y^*,d)\) is a feasible fair allocation.

The distance vector dominates every feasible fair payment vector. Take any \(x\in F\), extend it by \(x_s=0\), and consider a path

\( \displaystyle s=a_0\to a_1\to\cdots\to a_r=j. \)

Summing its edge inequalities yields \(x_j\le\) the length of that path. Applying this to a shortest path gives \(x_j\le d_j\).

Therefore \(d\) is the componentwise greatest vector in \(F\). It is unique, since any two greatest vectors must dominate each other.

4. Exact polynomial-time computation

The algorithm is:

  • Compute an exact rational optimal transportation allocation \(y^*\).
  • Compute the rational weights \(w_{j\ell}\).
  • Compute shortest-path distances \(d_j\) from \(s\).
  • Return \((y^*,d)\).

For the first step, the standard rational linear-programming theorem gives an exact rational optimum in time polynomial in the encoded LP size, with polynomial output bit length; see [Trevisan’s linear-programming notes, Sections 2.4–2.5](https://lucatrevisan.github.io/cs261/lecture05.pdf). Its hypotheses hold here: all coefficients are rational, and the feasible region is nonempty and bounded. The LP has \(\tau m\) variables, \(\tau+m\) marginal equalities, and \(\tau m\) nonnegativity constraints. Its encoding size is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Exact output also permits exact detection of \(y^*_{tj}>0\).

Computing all weights requires at most \(O(\tau m^2)\) rational arithmetic operations and comparisons. Each weight is a difference of two input values, so its bit length is \(O(L)\).

The graph has \(m+1\) vertices and \(m^2+m\) edges. Bellman–Ford computes its distances with \(O(m^3)\) rational arithmetic operations. For completeness, these distances can also be obtained by starting with \(d^{(0)}_j=c_j\) and performing \(m-1\) rounds of

\( \displaystyle d^{(r+1)}_\ell = \min\left\{ d^{(r)}_\ell,\, \min_{j\in J}(d^{(r)}_j+w_{j\ell}) \right\}. \)

By induction, this considers paths with at most \(r+1\) edges from the source. Every shortest simple path has at most \(m\) edges, so the final values are the desired distances.

All intermediate finite values are sums of at most \(m\) edge lengths and therefore have polynomial bit length. Consequently, the entire algorithm runs in polynomial bit time in \(m,\tau,L\), and returns an exact rational allocation and payment vector. It requires no expansion of rational masses into individual agents or objects.
