The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), a proposed committee \(W\subseteq C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many argumentative-ballot types \(t\), each specified by an attack graph \(R_t\), decide whether every subpopulation vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\ge 1/k\), and nonempty grounded extension of \(\bigcup_{t:x_t>0}R_t\) contains a subpopulation vector \(y\) with \(0\le y_t\le x_t\), \(\sum_t y_t>0\), whose pooled graph’s grounded extension intersects \(W\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a candidate set with \(m=|C|\geq1\), let \(1\leq k\leq m\) be an integer, and let \(W\subseteq C\) satisfy \(|W|=k\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type specifies an attack relation \(R_t\subseteq C\times C\), encoded as an adjacency matrix; self-attacks are allowed. For \(z\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\tau}\), let \(G(z)\) be the grounded extension of \((C,\bigcup_{t:z_t>0}R_t)\).
The verification condition is: every \(x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\tau}\) with \(x\leq\mu\), \(\sum_t x_t\geq1/k\), and \(G(x)\neq\varnothing\) admits \(y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\tau}\) with \(y\leq x\), \(\sum_t y_t>0\), and \(G(y)\cap W\neq\varnothing\). Vector inequalities are coordinatewise.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

Is deciding this verification condition in \(\mathsf P\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For finite electorates, argumentative justified-representation verification is \(\mathsf{coNP}\)-hard for every cohesion-strength setting, while a satisfying committee can be constructed in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23247v2#S4.SS4)

Result

The verification problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. More strongly, it is \(\mathsf{coNP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, even when \(k=2\) and every mass is dyadic.

If \(k=m\), verification is trivially satisfied: whenever \(G(x)\neq\varnothing\), choosing \(y=x\) gives \(G(y)\cap W\neq\varnothing\). The reduction below concerns the general problem.

1. Eliminating the real-valued quantifiers

Write \([\tau]=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). For \(S\subseteq[\tau]\), define

\(\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t,\qquad R(S)=\bigcup_{t\in S}R_t,\qquad G_S=G(C,R(S)).\)

Lemma. The verification condition is equivalent to the following finite condition:

For every nonempty \(S\subseteq[\tau]\) satisfying \(\mu(S)\geq 1/k\) and \(G_S\neq\varnothing\), there exists a nonempty \(T\subseteq S\) satisfying \(G_T\cap W\neq\varnothing\).

Proof. Suppose the original condition holds, and let \(S\) satisfy the lemma’s premises. Set \(x_t=\mu_t\) for \(t\in S\), and \(x_t=0\) otherwise. This vector satisfies all the original premises. For the resulting witness \(y\), its support \(T=\{t:y_t>0\}\) is a nonempty subset of \(S\), and \(G(y)=G_T\).

Conversely, suppose the finite condition holds and \(x\) satisfies the original premises. Its support \(S\) is nonempty, and

\(\mu(S)\geq\sum_t x_t\geq 1/k.\)

Moreover, \(G(x)=G_S\). Choose the nonempty subset \(T\subseteq S\) supplied by the finite condition. Set \(y_t=x_t\) for \(t\in T\), and \(y_t=0\) otherwise. Then \(y\leq x\), its total mass is positive, and \(G(y)=G_T\) intersects \(W\). This proves the equivalence. \(\square\)

Thus a violation is exactly a sufficiently massive support \(S\) with a nonempty grounded extension such that every nonempty subset of \(S\) is unrepresented.

2. The source problem and the quota

We reduce from \(\mathsf{CLIQUE}\): given an undirected simple graph with \(n_0\) vertices and an integer \(q_0\), decide whether it has a clique of size at least \(q_0\).

We may restrict to \(2\leq q_0\leq n_0\). This restriction remains \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete: excluded instances are decidable immediately and can be mapped to fixed yes or no instances satisfying the restriction.

Add \(n_0\) new vertices, each adjacent to every other vertex, including every other new vertex. Call the resulting graph \(H=(V,E)\), and put

\(n=|V|=2n_0,\qquad q=n_0+q_0.\)

Then \(n/2<q\leq n\). Also, the original graph has a clique of size at least \(q_0\) if and only if \(H\) has a clique of size at least \(q\):

  • An original clique of size \(q_0\), together with all new vertices, gives a clique of size \(q\).
  • A clique of size at least \(q\) contains at least \(q-n_0=q_0\) original vertices, which form an original clique.

We now construct positive dyadic masses whose quota distinguishes cardinalities \(q-1\) and \(q\).

Let \(P\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(P\geq 8n^2\), and set

\(h=\left\lceil P/(2q)\right\rceil,\qquad a=h/P.\)

Assign mass \(a\) to each of \(n\) vertex types, and assign mass \(1-na\) to one additional type. Set \(k=2\).

These masses are positive and sum to one. Indeed,

\(\frac{1}{2q}\leq a<\frac{1}{2q}+\frac{1}{P}.\)

Since \(n\leq 2q-1\) and \(P>2nq\),

\(na<\frac{n}{2q}+\frac{n}{P}<\frac{n+1}{2q}\leq 1.\)

Furthermore,

\(qa\geq\frac12,\)

whereas, using \(P>2q(q-1)\),

\((q-1)a<\frac{q-1}{2q}+\frac{q-1}{P}<\frac12.\)

Consequently, for every integer \(s\) with \(0\leq s\leq n\),

\(sa\geq\frac12\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\geq q.\)

All masses have numerator and denominator bit lengths bounded by \(\mathrm O(\log n)\).

3. Constructing the attack relations

Label the vertices of \(H\) by \([n]\), and let

\(\mathcal N=\{\{i,j\}:i\neq j,\ \{i,j\}\notin E\}\)

be its set of unordered nonedges.

The candidate set consists of distinct candidates

\(C=\{r,b,w_1,w_2\}\cup\{a_i:i\in[n]\}\cup\{d_e:e\in\mathcal N\}.\)

Set \(W=\{w_1,w_2\}\), so \(|W|=k=2\).

Define a common attack relation

\(B=\{(b,b),(b,w_1),(b,w_2)\}\cup \bigcup_{\{i,j\}\in\mathcal N} \{(a_i,d_{\{i,j\}}),(a_j,d_{\{i,j\}}),(d_{\{i,j\}},b)\}.\)

For each vertex \(i\), its type has relation

\(R_i=B\cup\{(r,a_i)\}.\)

The additional type, denoted by \(\star\), has relation

\(R_\star=B\cup\{(r,b)\}.\)

The relations are pairwise distinct: each has a different single attack originating at \(r\), and \(B\) contains no such attack. Thus the support really has \(\tau=n+1\) distinct types.

4. Exact grounded extensions

We prove the behavior of this construction directly.

For an attack relation \(R\), its characteristic operator is

\(F_R(A)=\{c\in C:\text{ for every }u\text{ with }(u,c)\in R,\text{ some }v\in A\text{ satisfies }(v,u)\in R\}.\)

This operator is monotone. Starting from \(A_0=\varnothing\) and iterating \(A_{\ell+1}=F_R(A_\ell)\) gives an increasing sequence whose eventual fixed point is the grounded extension. Leastness follows because induction places every iterate inside every fixed point.

Fix a nonempty support \(S\subseteq[n]\cup\{\star\}\), and write

\(I=S\cap[n],\qquad D_I=\{d_{\{i,j\}}:\{i,j\}\in\mathcal N,\ i,j\in I\}.\)

Say that the trigger holds when \(\star\in S\) or \(D_I\neq\varnothing\).

Lemma. The grounded extension is exactly

\(G_S=\{r\}\cup\{a_i:i\notin I\}\cup D_I\cup \begin{cases} W,&\text{if the trigger holds},\\ \varnothing,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}\)

Proof. Denote the displayed set by \(A\). Because \(S\) is nonempty, its union relation contains \(B\).

We first verify \(F_{R(S)}(A)=A\).

  • Candidate \(r\). It has no attackers, so it is defended by \(A\).
  • Candidate \(a_i\). If \(i\notin I\), it has no attackers. If \(i\in I\), its attacker is \(r\), and no candidate attacks \(r\). Therefore \(a_i\) is defended exactly when \(i\notin I\).
  • Candidate \(d_{\{i,j\}}\). Its attackers are exactly \(a_i,a_j\). If \(i,j\in I\), both are attacked by \(r\in A\), so this candidate is defended. Otherwise, one of these attackers is an unattacked \(a_i\) or \(a_j\), making defence impossible.
  • Candidate \(b\). It is never defended by \(A\). If \(\star\in S\), it has the unattackable attacker \(r\). If \(\star\notin S\) and \(D_I\neq\varnothing\), choose \(d_{\{i,j\}}\in D_I\). This attacks \(b\), but its only attackers \(a_i,a_j\) are absent from \(A\). Finally, if \(\star\notin S\) and \(D_I=\varnothing\), no member of \(A\) attacks \(b\), so its self-attack remains undefended.
  • Candidates \(w_1,w_2\). Their only attacker is \(b\). A member of \(A\) attacks \(b\) exactly when the trigger holds: such a defender is \(r\) when \(\star\in S\), or a member of \(D_I\).

Thus \(A\) is a fixed point.

Every member of \(A\) also appears in the grounded iteration. Candidate \(r\) and all \(a_i\) with \(i\notin I\) appear in the first iterate. Every member of \(D_I\) appears in the second iterate, defended by \(r\). If the trigger holds, both winners appear by the third iterate, defended against \(b\) by \(r\) or by a member of \(D_I\).

The least fixed point is therefore both contained in \(A\), because \(A\) is a fixed point, and contains \(A\), because all its members enter the iteration. This proves the formula. \(\square\)

In particular, every nonempty support has \(r\in G_S\), and

\(G_S\cap W=\varnothing \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \star\notin S\text{ and }I\text{ is a clique in }H.\)

The same characterization applies to all nonempty subsets of \(S\). Since every subset of a clique is a clique, we obtain

\(\forall\,\varnothing\neq T\subseteq S,\quad G_T\cap W=\varnothing \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \star\notin S\text{ and }I\text{ is a clique}.\)

For the reverse implication, use the lemma on every subset. For the forward implication, take \(T=S\).

5. Correctness and hardness

By Section 1, verification fails exactly when there is a nonempty support \(S\) satisfying:

  • \(\mu(S)\geq 1/2\);
  • \(G_S\neq\varnothing\);
  • every nonempty subset of \(S\) is unrepresented.

Section 4 shows that the second condition always holds, and that the third condition is equivalent to \(S\) consisting solely of vertex types forming a clique.

For such a support, \(\mu(S)=|S|a\). Section 2 therefore gives

\(\mu(S)\geq 1/2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |S|\geq q.\)

Hence

\(\text{constructed instance fails verification} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\text{ has a clique of size at least }q.\)

Combining this with the padding construction,

\(\text{constructed instance satisfies verification} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the original }\mathsf{CLIQUE}\text{ instance is a no-instance}.\)

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from the complement of \(\mathsf{CLIQUE}\) to verification, proving \(\mathsf{coNP}\)-hardness. Equivalently, one verification-oracle query followed by negating its answer decides \(\mathsf{CLIQUE}\), proving the requested \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions.

The construction has

\(k=2,\qquad \tau=n+1,\qquad m=n+|\mathcal N|+4=\mathrm O(n^2).\)

Its adjacency-matrix encoding has length

\(L'=\mathrm O(\tau m^2+n\log n)=\mathrm O(n^5),\)

and can be produced in \(\mathrm O(n^5)\) bit operations. Thus the reduction is polynomial in the source input length.

6. An exact general running-time bound

For completeness, the finite characterization gives an exact algorithm for arbitrary \(m,\tau,L,k\).

For every nonempty \(S\subseteq[\tau]\), compute \(\mu(S)\), \(G_S\), and the Boolean value \(b(S)\) indicating whether \(G_S\cap W\neq\varnothing\). Set \(h(\varnothing)=\mathrm{false}\), and compute in increasing subset size

\(h(S)=b(S)\lor\bigvee_{t\in S}h(S\setminus\{t\}).\)

By induction, \(h(S)\) is true exactly when some nonempty \(T\subseteq S\) is represented. Verification fails exactly when some \(S\) has \(\mu(S)\geq1/k\), \(G_S\neq\varnothing\), and \(h(S)=\mathrm{false}\).

Building a union relation takes \(\mathrm O(\tau m^2)\) time. Grounded iteration takes \(\mathrm O(m^3)\): each characteristic-operator evaluation takes \(\mathrm O(m^2)\), and there are at most \(m\) strict increases before stabilization.

For exact mass arithmetic, put all masses over a common denominator \(D\), whose bit length is \(\mathrm O(L)\). This preprocessing takes \(\mathrm O(L^3)\) bit operations using elementary arithmetic. Each subset mass then has an integer numerator \(A(S)\), and its quota test is

\(A(S)\geq\lceil D/k\rceil.\)

Subset numerators can be computed by one addition per subset. The resulting total running time is

\(\mathrm O\!\left(L^3+2^\tau(\tau m^2+m^3+\tau+L)\right).\)

This bound applies throughout \(1\leq k\leq m\); the dependence on \(k\) is included in the exact threshold arithmetic. The hardness result rules out a general polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\).

1. Eliminating the real-valued quantifiers

The finite characterization is correct in both directions.

For a support \(S\) meeting the finite premises, setting each included coordinate to its full positive mass produces a feasible vector \(x\) with support exactly \(S\). Any witness \(y\) has nonempty support \(T\subseteq S\), and its attack relation is exactly \(R(T)\).

Conversely, a feasible vector \(x\) has nonempty support \(S\), since its total mass is at least \(1/k>0\). Coordinatewise domination gives \(\mu(S)\geq\sum_t x_t\). Given a nonempty represented subset \(T\subseteq S\), retaining the coordinates of \(x\) on \(T\) produces a positive-mass witness \(y\leq x\) with precisely that support.

This handles the positive-mass requirement and the fact that grounded extensions depend on positivity of coordinates, rather than their magnitudes.

2. Source restriction and graph padding

The restriction \(2\leq q_0\leq n_0\) is legitimate: instances outside this range have immediately decidable answers and can be replaced by fixed restricted instances with the same answer.

The padding equivalence is correct. Adding \(n_0\) universal vertices extends an original clique of size \(q_0\) to one of size \(n_0+q_0\). Conversely, a clique of that size in the padded graph contains at least \(q_0\) original vertices.

The resulting parameters satisfy \(n=2n_0\) and \(n/2<q\leq n\), as required for the mass construction.

3. Mass construction and quota separation

All stated inequalities check out.

Since \(q\leq n\) and \(P\geq8n^2\), both \(P>2nq\) and \(P>2q(q-1)\) hold. The ceiling definition gives
\[ \frac{1}{2q}\leq a<\frac{1}{2q}+\frac{1}{P}. \]

Consequently, \(na<1\), so the additional type has strictly positive mass. The vertex masses are also strictly positive, and all masses sum to one.

The two critical quota bounds are valid:
\[ qa\geq\frac12,\qquad (q-1)a<\frac12. \]

Positivity of \(a\) then gives the claimed equivalence between meeting the quota and having at least \(q\) vertex types.

Because \(P\) is the smallest qualifying power of two, \(P<16n^2\). Thus the denominator and all relevant numerators have \(\mathrm O(\log n)\) bits. The masses are indeed dyadic.

4. Attack relations and input validity

The construction satisfies the task’s encoding requirements.

All candidates are distinct, \(W\) has exactly two members, and \(k=2\leq m\). Self-attacks are permitted, so the attack \((b,b)\) is valid.

Each vertex type contributes a distinct attack \((r,a_i)\), while the additional type contributes \((r,b)\). None of these attacks belongs to \(B\). Hence all \(\tau=n+1\) types are pairwise distinct.

Every nonempty support includes the common relation \(B\). The proof correctly restricts its grounded-extension formula to nonempty supports.

5. Exact grounded-extension formula

The characteristic operator is correctly stated. Its monotonicity, iteration from the empty set, and least-fixed-point argument are valid on this finite candidate set.

The fixed-point verification checks every candidate class:

  • \(r\) is unattacked and therefore accepted.
  • An \(a_i\) is accepted exactly when \(i\notin I\); otherwise its attacker \(r\) cannot be attacked.
  • A nonedge candidate \(d_{\{i,j\}}\) is defended exactly when both endpoints lie in \(I\). If an endpoint is outside \(I\), that endpoint candidate is unattacked, making defence impossible.
  • \(b\) is excluded in all three cases considered. With the additional type present, its attacker \(r\) is unattackable. With an active nonedge candidate, that attacker has no attacker in the proposed extension. With neither trigger present, its self-attack has no defender in the proposed extension.
  • Each winner is defended exactly when the trigger holds, since its sole attacker is \(b\).

The iteration argument also establishes leastness, rather than merely exhibiting a fixed point: the unattacked candidates enter the first iterate, active nonedge candidates enter the second, and triggered winners enter by the third. Thus every member of the displayed fixed point belongs to the grounded extension.

There is no unsupported assumption that adding attacks preserves grounded acceptance.

6. Characterization of unrepresented supports

The formula implies that a nonempty support is unrepresented precisely when it omits the additional type and its vertex set contains no nonedge—that is, when its vertices form a clique.

The universal statement about all nonempty subsets is also correct. One direction follows by taking \(T=S\); the other follows because every subset of a clique is a clique and still omits the additional type.

Every nonempty support has a nonempty grounded extension because it contains \(r\).

\[ \text{verification fails} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the padded graph has a clique of size at least }q. \]
