Result

The radius guarantee holds. A deterministic truncation of deferred acceptance gives the required measurable matching. The essential step is to replace finite counting arguments by measure identities proved directly from the graphing’s invariance.

The algorithm below uses the proposal mechanism from [Floréen–Kaski–Polishchuk–Suomela](https://jukkasuomela.fi/doc/almost-stable.pdf). We give a complete proof for graphings, without assuming that the graphing is approximable by finite graphs.

1. Counting edges from either colour class

For every measurable edge set \(F\subseteq E\), invariance implies
\( \displaystyle \int_R\deg_F(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_B\deg_F(x)\,d\mu(x)=\nu(F). \)

Here is a direct justification. Partition \(F\) according to its preference ranks at its two endpoints. For \(1\leq i,j\leq\Delta\), let \(F_{ij}\) contain the edges having rank \(i\) at their red endpoint and rank \(j\) at their blue endpoint.

Every vertex is incident to at most one edge of \(F_{ij}\). Consequently, \(F_{ij}\), oriented from red to blue, defines a measurable partial bijection between its endpoint sets \(D_{ij}\subseteq R\) and \(H_{ij}\subseteq B\). The assumed invariance gives \(\mu(D_{ij})=\mu(H_{ij})\). Summing over the finitely many rank pairs proves equality of the two integrals; their common value is \(\nu(F)\) by its definition.

In particular, for a measurable matching \(N\),
\( \displaystyle \nu(N)=\mu(\{b\in B:b\text{ is matched in }N\}). \)

The measurability used here follows from standard Borel measurable enumeration: a Borel relation with countable sections admits an enumeration by Borel partial functions. Its hypotheses hold because \(E\) is a graphing relation on a standard Borel space and its sections have size at most \(\Delta\). Ordering these finitely many neighbours by the supplied measurable preferences gives measurable incident-port maps. All subsequent constructions use finite operations on these maps.

2. The deterministic process

Initially every vertex is unmatched. Each red vertex maintains its preference list with rejected or terminated partnerships deleted. A round consists of two communication steps:

  • Every unmatched red vertex with a nonempty remaining list proposes to its first remaining candidate.
  • Each blue vertex retains its favourite among its incumbent partner, if any, and its new proposers. It informs the new partner of acceptance, rejects unsuccessful proposals, and informs any displaced incumbent. Red vertices process these responses, deleting every rejected or terminated partnership.

Let \(M_j\) denote the matching after round \(j\), with \(M_0=\varnothing\).

These outputs are mutually consistent. Indeed, each red vertex makes at most one proposal, each blue vertex retains at most one partner, and every partnership change is communicated before the round ends. Induction therefore proves that every \(M_j\) is a matching.

The same induction gives three useful properties:

  • A blue vertex, once matched, remains matched and changes partners only to a strictly preferred neighbour.
  • Each red vertex’s remaining list is a suffix of its original preference list.
  • A matched red vertex is partnered with the first entry of its remaining list.

All states and edge sets after finitely many rounds are measurable by the measurable port maps described above.

3. The measure of active red vertices decreases

Call a red vertex active after round \(j\) if it is unmatched and its remaining list is nonempty. Write \(A_j\) for this measurable set and \(a_j=\mu(A_j)\).

We prove \(a_j\leq a_{j-1}\) for every \(j\geq1\).

Let \(K_j\subseteq A_{j-1}\) be the red vertices whose proposals are accepted in round \(j\). Let \(H_j\) be the red vertices matched before the round but displaced during it. Finally, let \(S_j\subseteq B\) be the blue vertices that replace an incumbent with a new proposer.

The old matching gives a measurable partial bijection between \(H_j\) and \(S_j\). The new matching gives one between \(S_j\) and a subset \(K_j^{\mathrm{switch}}\subseteq K_j\). Thus invariance yields
\( \displaystyle \mu(H_j)=\mu(S_j)=\mu(K_j^{\mathrm{switch}})\leq\mu(K_j). \)

A formerly unmatched vertex with an empty list cannot become active. A formerly matched vertex can become active only by being displaced. Hence
\( \displaystyle A_j\subseteq(A_{j-1}\setminus K_j)\cup H_j. \)

The two sets on the right are disjoint, since their vertices were respectively unmatched and matched before the round. Therefore
\( \displaystyle a_j\leq a_{j-1}-\mu(K_j)+\mu(H_j)\leq a_{j-1}. \)

This proves the required monotonicity using only the stated invariance.

4. Charging activity to deleted edges

Let \(L_j\) be the set of edges deleted from red preference lists by the end of round \(j\), and put \(D_j=L_j\setminus L_{j-1}\), where \(L_0=\varnothing\).

Every vertex in \(A_j\) deletes an edge during round \(j\). To check this, consider its state before the round:

  • If it was unmatched, its list was nonempty because lists only shrink. It therefore proposed and was rejected.
  • If it was matched, it was displaced.

In either case, the corresponding edge is newly deleted. A red vertex deletes at most one edge in a round: it either proposes while unmatched or waits with its existing partner. Consequently,
\( \displaystyle a_j\leq\int_R\deg_{D_j}(x)\,d\mu(x)=\nu(D_j). \)

The sets \(D_1,\ldots,D_t\) are disjoint. Combining their edge measures with the monotonicity of \(a_j\) gives
\( \displaystyle t a_t\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_j\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\nu(D_j)=\nu(L_t). \)

We next bound deleted edges using the current matching. If an edge incident to a blue vertex has been deleted, that blue vertex rejected or displaced its red endpoint while retaining a strictly preferred partner. It subsequently stays matched and only improves its partner. Its current matching edge is therefore not among its deleted incident edges. Pointwise on \(B\),
\( \displaystyle \deg_{L_t}(b)\leq(\Delta-1)\mathbf 1_{\{b\text{ is matched in }M_t\}}. \)

Integrating from the blue side yields
\( \displaystyle \nu(L_t)\leq(\Delta-1)\nu(M_t). \)

Thus, for every integer \(t\geq1\),
\( \displaystyle t a_t\leq(\Delta-1)\nu(M_t). \)

5. Bounding unstable edges

Let \(U_t=E_{\mathrm{unstable}}(M_t)\). Every edge of \(U_t\) has its red endpoint in \(A_t\).

Indeed, consider an edge outside \(M_t\).

If it has been deleted from its red endpoint’s list, its blue endpoint currently holds a strictly preferred partner, so it cannot be unstable.

Otherwise, its blue endpoint remains on the red vertex’s list. If the red vertex is matched, its current partner is the first remaining candidate, so it strictly prefers that partner to the edge under consideration. Thus instability requires that the red endpoint be unmatched with a nonempty remaining list, which is exactly membership in \(A_t\).

Since degrees are at most \(\Delta\),
\( \displaystyle \nu(U_t)=\int_R\deg_{U_t}(x)\,d\mu(x)\leq\Delta a_t\leq\frac{\Delta(\Delta-1)}{t}\nu(M_t). \)

This inequality is valid directly on the graphing.

6. Choosing the stopping time and radius

Choose the integer
\( \displaystyle t=1+\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta(\Delta-1)}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor. \)

For \(\Delta>1\), the preceding estimate gives
\( \displaystyle \nu(E_{\mathrm{unstable}}(M_t))\leq\varepsilon\nu(M_t). \)

For \(\Delta=1\), the same estimate gives zero unstable-edge measure; in fact the first round matches every edge.

There are two communication steps per round. Initial incident lists are determined by radius-one information, and each communication step increases the required radius by at most one. Thus radius
\( \displaystyle r=2t+1 \)
suffices. The extra initial radius also ensures that every vertex whose initial state influences the output has all its incident edges visible.

Its bound is
\( \displaystyle r\leq3+\frac{2\Delta(\Delta-1)}{\varepsilon}<4+\frac{2\Delta^2}{\varepsilon}. \)

The process uses only colours, preference ports, and a fixed stopping time. Its output is invariant under rooted neighbourhood isomorphisms and uses neither identifiers nor randomness. Simulating it on the radius-\(r\) neighbourhood therefore defines a uniform deterministic local rule. Its measurable outputs form the consistent matching \(M_t\) proved above.

No finite-support encoding or global information about the graphing is used. Atomlessness is compatible with the proof but is not needed for these inequalities.
