The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), an initial distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings specified by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary-rational masses, and a binary-rational budget \(\epsilon\), does there exist a transfer \(x_{t,t'} \ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t' \ne t} x_{t,t'} \le \mu_t\), total changed mass \(\sum_{t \ne t'} x_{t,t'} \le \epsilon\), and resulting distribution \(\nu\) whose Schulze winner set differs from that under \(\mu\)? The output rankings may be any ranking of \(C\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, and let \(\mathcal R(C)\) be their strict complete rankings. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass ranking types, each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; extend \(\mu\) by zero outside its support. The budget \(\epsilon>0\) is binary rational, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For any distribution \(\rho\), use the signed pairwise margins \(D_\rho(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\rho_t-\sum_{t:b\succ_t a}\rho_t\) as weights on every directed edge between distinct candidates, including edges of zero or negative weight. Let \(P_\rho(a,b)\) be the maximum, over simple directed paths from \(a\) to \(b\), of the minimum edge weight on the path. The Schulze winner set is \(W(\rho)=\{a\in C:P_\rho(a,b)\ge P_\rho(b,a)\text{ for every }b\ne a\}\); retain all tied winners.
A transfer consists of real masses \(x_{t,t'}\ge0\) for distinct \(t,t'\in\mathcal R(C)\), satisfying \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\le\epsilon\). Its resulting distribution is \(\nu_t=\mu_t-\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}+\sum_{s\ne t}x_{s,t}\). Destination rankings are unrestricted.
Is deciding whether such a transfer exists with \(W(\nu)\ne W(\mu)\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?
For an explicitly listed finite electorate, deciding whether changing at most a given positive integer number of entire ballots changes the Schulze winner set is NP-complete ([Reisch, Rothe, and Schend](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/publication/37214)). No result settling the exact divisible-mass problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one.
  • Required at least two candidates and a positive binary-rational budget, matching the nondegenerate margin-of-victory setting and its positive discrete budget.
  • Specified strict complete rankings and the signed-margin Schulze variant, with every directed edge retained and all tied winners included.
  • Allowed arbitrary nonnegative real transfer masses, making the society divisible without an integrality restriction.
  • Defined the resulting distribution explicitly and extended initial masses by zero to all unsupported rankings.
  • Defined L as the total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when all input masses and the budget are dyadic rationals and the original society has a unique winner.

The reduction below directly handles arbitrary real transfers and unrestricted destination rankings. Its key feature is a saturation constraint: every successful transfer must exhaust the budget using designated ranking types, and a path requirement then forces selected types to be transferred in full.

1. A graph encoding satisfiability

We reduce from \(3\mathrm{SAT}\), which is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete by the [Cook–Levin theorem](https://introtcs.org/public/lec_13_Cook_Levin.html).

Let \(\varphi\) have variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\), with \(n\ge1\), and clauses \(K_1,\ldots,K_q\), with \(q\ge1\). Each clause has three literal occurrences. Restricting to positive numbers of variables and clauses preserves hardness, since trivial instances can be handled separately.

Construct a directed acyclic graph \(G\) with \(n+q\) consecutive layers. Its boundary vertices are

\(h_0=v,h_1,\ldots,h_{n+q}=z\).

Each alternative in layer \(j\) receives two fresh vertices \(p_e,q_e\), with edges

\(h_{j-1}\longrightarrow p_e\longrightarrow q_e\longrightarrow h_j\).

The middle edge \(p_e\longrightarrow q_e\) is an activation edge, labelled by a literal. The other two edges are routing edges.

The alternatives are as follows:

  • Variable layer \(i\) has two alternatives, labelled \(X_i\) and \(\neg X_i\).
  • Clause layer \(n+j\) has one alternative for each literal occurrence in \(K_j\), labelled by that literal.

All activation-edge endpoint pairs are disjoint.

Every directed path from \(v\) to \(z\) chooses exactly one alternative in every layer. Consequently:

Graph property. A \(v\)-to-\(z\) path using at most \(n\) distinct literal labels exists if and only if \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

Indeed, its variable layers already use \(n\) distinct labels, one for each variable. If no additional label is used, every clause layer chooses one of these selected literals, so the corresponding assignment satisfies every clause. Conversely, a satisfying assignment selects its true literal in each variable layer and a true literal in each clause layer.

2. Candidates and prescribed margins

Add three candidates \(c,b,a\) to the vertices of \(G\).

We first construct a profile with integer, unnormalized masses. Write \(M_0(u,w)\) for its pairwise margins. Later we divide all masses by a common power of two. This divides every margin and strongest-path strength by the same positive number and therefore preserves the winner set.

Set

\(T=10n+10,\qquad R=T+2n,\qquad H=T+6n+2\).

Prescribe these margins:

| Directed pair or edge | Margin |
|---|---:|
| \(c\longrightarrow b\) | \(R\) |
| \(b\longrightarrow v\) | \(T-2n\) |
| \(c\longrightarrow v\) | \(R\) |
| \(c\longrightarrow a\) | \(H\) |
| \(a\longrightarrow u\), for every \(u\in V(G)\) | \(H\) |
| \(z\longrightarrow c\) | \(R\) |
| Every routing edge of \(G\) | \(R\) |
| Every activation edge of \(G\) | \(T-2\) |

Reverse margins are their negatives. Every unordered pair not specified in the table has margin zero. The specified pairs are distinct.

The unnormalized transfer budget will be \(n\).

3. Realizing the margins while controlling eligible sources

For a ranking \(r\), let \(\sigma_r(u,w)\) equal \(1\) when \(r\) ranks \(u\) above \(w\), and equal \(-1\) otherwise.

We construct the profile with these additional properties:

  • For every literal \(\ell\), there is a distinct ranking \(r_\ell\) of mass exactly \(1\).
  • Each \(r_\ell\) ranks \(c\succ v\succ b\).
  • On an activation edge labelled \(\ell\), ranking \(r_\ell\) reverses that edge, while every other literal ranking favors it.
  • Every other support ranking has, on the triple \(\{c,b,v\}\), either \(c\succ b\succ v\) or \(v\succ b\succ c\).

Thus the literal rankings are exactly the support types that rank both \(c\) and \(v\) above \(b\).

Literal rankings and the initial profile

For each literal \(\ell\), put \(c,v,b\) first, in that order. Then list the disjoint activation endpoint pairs as blocks: use \(q_e,p_e\) when edge \(e\) is labelled \(\ell\), and \(p_e,q_e\) otherwise. Place all remaining candidates arbitrarily.

This satisfies properties 2 and 3. The rankings are distinct because every literal labels an alternative in a variable layer. Give each of the \(2n\) literal rankings mass \(1\).

Add mass \(T\) on any ranking whose restriction to the distinguished triple is \(c\succ b\succ v\). The resulting triple margins are exactly

\(M_0(c,b)=T+2n,\qquad M_0(b,v)=T-2n,\qquad M_0(c,v)=T+2n\).

Correcting every other pair

We use the following elementary two-ranking construction.

Let \(u,w\) be a pair not wholly contained in \(\{c,b,v\}\). Choose a ranking \(r\) in which \(u\) immediately precedes \(w\), and the distinguished triple has order \(c\succ b\succ v\). Such a ranking exists: if one endpoint belongs to the triple, insert the other immediately beside it; otherwise insert the two endpoints as an adjacent block.

Reverse \(r\), then swap the now-adjacent endpoints \(u,w\). Call the resulting ranking \(r'\). Both rankings favor \(u\) over \(w\), while they disagree on every other pair. Moreover, their triple orders are respectively

\(c\succ b\succ v\quad\text{and}\quad v\succ b\succ c\).

Giving each ranking mass \(k\) adds margin \(2k\) to \(u\longrightarrow w\) and zero to every other pair. Interchanging \(u,w\) gives the opposite correction.

The initial total mass \(T+2n\) is even, so every initial margin is even. All prescribed margins are also even. Therefore every required correction is an even integer and can be implemented with nonnegative integer masses using this construction.

Correct every unordered pair outside the distinguished triple. These corrections are independent, leave the triple margins unchanged, and introduce only rankings satisfying property 4. The desired profile is therefore realized.

Dyadic normalization and input size

Let its total mass be \(N_0\). It is an even integer. Choose a power of two \(N\ge N_0\). Add mass \((N-N_0)/2\) to a ranking with triple order \(c\succ b\succ v\), and the same mass to its reverse. These additions cancel on every pair and preserve property 4.

Divide all masses by \(N\), and set

\(\epsilon=n/N\).

All input masses and the budget are now positive dyadic rationals, after omitting zero-mass types.

The construction has polynomial size. There are \(O(n+q)\) candidates and \(O(n+m^2)\) support rankings. Every prescribed margin and every correction has magnitude \(O(n)\), so \(N_0=O(m^2n)\). Hence all masses, rankings, and the budget have polynomial encoding length. Identical auxiliary rankings can be merged; none can coincide with a literal ranking because their triple orders differ.

4. Only \(b\) can change the winner set

For the correctness proof, multiply all transferred masses by \(N\). The budget is then \(n\), and each literal source has capacity \(1\).

Consider any feasible transfer of total unnormalized mass \(s\le n\). Let \(M\) be its resulting margin matrix and \(S(u,w)\) its strongest-path strengths.

Each transferred unit changes a pairwise margin by at most \(2\). Therefore

\(\lvert M(u,w)-M_0(u,w)\rvert\le2s\le2n\).

Candidate \(c\) strictly beats every candidate except possibly \(b\)

For every \(u\in V(G)\), the path \(c\longrightarrow a\longrightarrow u\) has strength at least

\(H-2n=T+4n+2\).

The direct edge from \(c\) to \(a\) has the same lower bound.

Every edge entering \(c\) initially has margin at most \(R\), so after the transfer its margin is at most

\(R+2n=T+4n\).

Every path ending at \(c\) therefore has strength at most \(T+4n\). It follows that

\(S(c,u)>S(u,c)\)

for every \(u\notin\{c,b\}\). None of these candidates can be a winner.

Candidate \(c\) always weakly beats \(b\)

The direct edge satisfies

\(M(c,b)\ge R-2n=T\).

Every other edge entering \(b\) initially has nonpositive margin, so its resulting margin is at most \(2n<T\). A non-direct simple path from \(c\) to \(b\) ends with such an edge. Thus

\(S(c,b)=M(c,b)\ge T\).

Every edge leaving \(b\), except \(b\longrightarrow v\), initially has nonpositive margin, so its resulting margin is below \(T\). The exceptional edge satisfies

\(M(b,v)\le(T-2n)+2n=T\).

Hence

\(S(b,c)\le T\le S(c,b)\).

Therefore \(c\) remains a winner under every feasible transfer, and the winner set is always either \(\{c\}\) or \(\{c,b\}\).

Initially, \(S_0(c,b)=R\), while every path from \(b\) to \(c\) has strength at most \(T-2n<R\). Consequently,

\(W(\mu)=\{c\}\).

5. A successful transfer forces a satisfying assignment

Suppose a feasible transfer changes the winner set. By the preceding section, \(b\) must become a winner. Its comparison with \(c\) forces

\(S(b,c)=S(c,b)=T\).

In particular, \(M(c,b)=T\). A path attaining \(S(b,c)=T\) must begin with \(b\longrightarrow v\), because every other edge leaving \(b\) is below \(T\). Therefore

\(M(b,v)=T\).

These equalities require both extremal changes:

\(M(c,b)-M_0(c,b)=-2n,\qquad M(b,v)-M_0(b,v)=2n\).

Saturation excludes every auxiliary source

For a transfer from ranking \(r\) to ranking \(r'\), the change per unit on \(c\longrightarrow b\) is

\(\sigma_{r'}(c,b)-\sigma_r(c,b)\ge-2\).

Since total transferred mass is at most \(n\), obtaining change \(-2n\) requires total mass exactly \(n\), and every positive transfer must have source \(c\succ b\) and destination \(b\succ c\).

Similarly, obtaining change \(2n\) on \(b\longrightarrow v\) requires every positive transfer to have source \(v\succ b\) and destination \(b\succ v\).

Thus every transferred source must rank both \(c\) and \(v\) above \(b\). By the construction, it must be a literal ranking.

Let \(y_\ell\) denote the amount transferred from literal ranking \(r_\ell\). We have

\(0\le y_\ell\le1,\qquad \sum_\ell y_\ell=n\).

No restriction has been imposed on how these amounts are divided among destination rankings.

A threshold path must traverse the layered graph

Take a simple path from \(b\) to \(c\) whose every edge has margin at least \(T\). Its first edge is \(b\longrightarrow v\).

Its last edge must be \(z\longrightarrow c\): every other edge entering \(c\) initially has nonpositive margin and therefore remains below \(T\).

Between \(v\) and \(z\), it cannot visit \(a\), since every graph-to-\(a\) edge initially has margin \(-H\). It cannot revisit \(b\), and cannot visit \(c\) before its endpoint because the path is simple.

Within \(V(G)\), every edge other than a routing or activation edge initially has nonpositive margin. These edges remain below \(T\). Thus the internal portion is a directed \(v\)-to-\(z\) path in the constructed layered graph.

Every used activation label consumes a full unit

Consider an activation edge \(p_e\longrightarrow q_e\) labelled \(\ell\). Its initial margin is \(T-2\).

Among eligible sources, only \(r_\ell\) opposes this edge. Transfers from that source can increase its margin by at most \(2y_\ell\). Every other eligible source already favors the edge, so transferring from it cannot increase the margin. Consequently,

\(M(p_e,q_e)\le T-2+2y_\ell\).

If this edge lies on the threshold path, then \(M(p_e,q_e)\ge T\), forcing \(y_\ell=1\).

The variable layers select \(n\) distinct literal labels. Each consumes a full unit, exhausting \(\sum_\ell y_\ell=n\). Every clause layer must therefore use one of those same selected labels.

Setting each variable according to its selected literal satisfies every clause. Hence \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

6. A satisfying assignment yields a successful transfer

Conversely, suppose \(\varphi\) is satisfiable. Select its true literal for each variable and a true literal in each clause. These choices define a directed path from \(v\) to \(z\).

Transfer the full unit from each of the \(n\) selected literal rankings to one common destination ranking that:

  • places \(b\) first;
  • places the vertices of the selected graph path in their path order, starting with \(v\);
  • places \(c\) last.

All remaining candidates may be placed arbitrarily between these positions. This is a valid strict ranking, distinct from every selected source. The transfer respects every source capacity and has total mass \(n\).

Every selected source ranks \(c\succ v\succ b\), while the destination ranks \(b\) above both \(c\) and \(v\). Thus

\(M(c,b)=T,\qquad M(b,v)=T\).

On each selected activation edge, its own literal source contributes an increase of \(2\). Every other transferred source already favors that edge, as does the destination, so it contributes zero change. Each selected activation edge consequently has margin exactly \(T\).

Routing edges and \(z\longrightarrow c\) retain margins at least

\(R-2n=T\).

Therefore the selected path, preceded by \(b\longrightarrow v\) and followed by \(z\longrightarrow c\), gives

\(S(b,c)\ge T\).

The universal bound from Section 4 gives equality.

To verify that \(b\) is a winner, first recall \(S(c,u)>T\) for every \(u\notin\{b,c\}\). Concatenating a strength-\(T\) path from \(b\) to \(c\) with such a path from \(c\) to \(u\) produces a walk of strength at least \(T\). Deleting closed subwalks yields a simple path without decreasing its minimum edge weight. Hence

\(S(b,u)\ge T\).

Every edge entering \(b\) has margin at most \(T\), including \(c\longrightarrow b\), whose margin is exactly \(T\). Thus

\(S(u,b)\le T\)

for every \(u\ne b\). Candidate \(b\) is therefore a winner, and

\(W(\nu)=\{c,b\}\ne\{c\}=W(\mu)\).

This proves the reduction and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

7. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Because transfers may use real masses and arbitrarily many destinations, membership requires a certificate-size argument.

Polynomially many transfer pairs suffice

Let \(d=\binom m2\), and choose one orientation for each unordered candidate pair.

For each allowable transfer from an input support type \(t\) to a distinct ranking \(r\), associate the vector

\(v_{t,r}=\bigl(e_t,\sigma_r-\sigma_t\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{\tau+d}\),

where \(e_t\) records its source type and the second component records its margin change.

A transfer's nonnegative combination of these vectors records both its source-removal amounts and its resulting margin changes.

Any such combination can be represented using at most \(\tau+d\) vectors. Here is an elementary elimination proof. If more vectors have positive coefficients, they are linearly dependent. In a dependence

\(\sum_i\alpha_i v_i=0\),

summing the first \(\tau\) coordinates gives \(\sum_i\alpha_i=0\). Thus a nonzero dependence has coefficients of both signs. For positive current coefficients \(\lambda_i\), set

\(\theta=\min_{\alpha_i>0}\lambda_i/\alpha_i\)

and replace \(\lambda_i\) by \(\lambda_i-\theta\alpha_i\). All coefficients remain nonnegative, at least one becomes zero, and the represented vector is unchanged. Repeating proves the bound.

This preserves source capacities, total transferred mass, and all final margins. Hence every yes-instance has a successful real transfer using at most \(\tau+d\) transfer pairs.

Polynomial-bit rational masses suffice

Fix those transfer pairs and the weak ordering of all directed edge margins in the successful result. Let their masses be variables \(\lambda_i\).

Form a rational linear system imposing:

  • nonnegative masses, source capacities, and the budget;
  • equality between edge margins belonging to the same ordering class;
  • a gap of at least \(\delta\) between successive distinct classes;
  • \(0\le\delta\le1\).

The original real witness makes this system feasible with \(\delta>0\): finitely many distinct classes have a positive minimum successive gap. If there is only one class, take \(\delta=1\).

Maximize \(\delta\). This feasible region is compact because every transfer variable is bounded by its source capacity and \(\delta\) is bounded by \(1\). A positive optimum is therefore attained at a vertex.

The system has polynomially many variables and constraints, with coefficients of polynomial bit length in \(m,\tau,L\). A vertex is determined by a full-rank collection of active constraints; clearing denominators and applying Cramer's rule gives polynomial-bit rational coordinates. Thus a positive-optimum vertex supplies a polynomial-bit rational transfer.

Preserving the weak edge ordering preserves the winner set. For each path, its minimum edge belongs to the same ordering class; maximizing over paths likewise selects the same class. Every comparison between opposing strongest-path strengths consequently has the same outcome.

Finally, a verifier checks the listed rational transfers, computes the resulting margins, and computes both winner sets in polynomial time. Strongest paths can be computed by the max–min version of Floyd–Warshall; its use of walks is valid because removing cycles cannot decrease a walk's minimum edge weight.

Therefore the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

The exact divisible-mass problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness already holds with dyadic input masses, a dyadic positive budget, and a unique original winner; a successful transfer adds one tied winner while the original winner remains.
