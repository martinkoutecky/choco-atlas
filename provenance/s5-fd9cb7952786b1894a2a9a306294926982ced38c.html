The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite panel \(\mathcal Q\) of binary-rational mass distributions over the three types \(\{x\text{-preferring},\text{indifferent},y\text{-preferring}\}\), how many distinct labelings \(\lambda:\mathcal Q\to\{-1,0,1\}\) extend to a map \(F\) on the full mass simplex that is neutral under swapping the \(x\)- and \(y\)-preferring masses and monotone under increases in \(x\)-support and decreases in \(y\)-support?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=2\) alternatives, \(x,y\), and three preference types: preferring \(x\), indifference, and preferring \(y\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\); omitted types have mass zero.
The input is a finite set \(\mathcal Q\) of such societies; write \(k=|\mathcal Q|\), \(\tau\) for the maximum support size, and \(L\) for the total input bit length. The full domain is \(\Delta=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{3}:a+b+c=1\}\), with coordinates in the type order above.
An admissible rule is any map \(F:\Delta\to\{-1,0,1\}\), where the labels mean preferring \(y\), indifference, and preferring \(x\), respectively. It satisfies \(F(c,b,a)=-F(a,b,c)\) and \(F(a',b',c')\ge F(a,b,c)\) whenever \(a'\ge a\) and \(c'\le c\). No continuity or unanimity condition is imposed.
Let \(C(\mathcal Q)\) be the number of distinct maps \(\lambda:\mathcal Q\to\{-1,0,1\}\) for which some admissible \(F\) satisfies \(F|_{\mathcal Q}=\lambda\); each labeling is counted once regardless of how many extensions it has.

Is exact computation of \(C(\mathcal Q)\) polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard under polynomial-time counting reductions?

For \(n\ge2\) discrete voters, the number of anonymous, neutral, weakly monotone rules on all ternary preference profiles is \(\binom{n+1}{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}\). [Llamazares](https://eco.uva.es/boni/Papers/2014IJPAM.pdf)
No result settling this exact finite-panel extension-counting problem on the real mass simplex was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each society to total mass one, included boundary points, and interpreted the full simplex as containing real masses.
  • Made monotonicity weak: either an increase in x-preferring mass or a decrease in y-preferring mass is allowed independently, subject to remaining in the simplex.
  • Interpreted continuous as describing the society, without requiring topological continuity of the rule.
  • Retained collective indifference and the identically zero rule; imposed no unanimity requirement, consistently with the discrete definitions.
  • Used exact counting complexity, with polynomial-time counting reductions for hardness, because the anchor is an enumeration formula rather than a decision-complexity result.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Exact computation of \(C(\mathcal Q)\) is polynomial-time solvable. The proof gives an exact recurrence using \(O(k^2)\) rational comparisons and integer additions.

1. Order and symmetry

Write \(p=(a_p,b_p,c_p)\), and define
\[ p\preceq q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_p\le a_q\ \text{and}\ c_p\ge c_q. \]
This is a partial order: antisymmetry follows because \(b_p=1-a_p-c_p\). Admissibility requires \(F\) to be nondecreasing in this order.

Let \(s(a,b,c)=(c,b,a)\). Reflection reverses the order:
\[ p\preceq q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s(q)\preceq s(p). \]

Partition the simplex into \(H_+=\{p:a_p>c_p\}\), \(H_0=\{p:a_p=c_p\}\), and \(H_-=\{p:a_p<c_p\}\).

If \(p\in H_0\), neutrality gives \(F(p)=-F(p)\), hence \(F(p)=0\). If \(p\in H_+\), then \(s(p)\preceq p\), so
\[ -F(p)=F(s(p))\le F(p). \]
Consequently \(F(p)\in\{0,1\}\). By neutrality, \(F(p)\in\{-1,0\}\) on \(H_-\).

Thus an admissible rule is determined by its binary-valued restriction to \(H_+\).

2. Exact characterization of extendible labelings

For each off-diagonal society, define its positive representative by
\[ r(p)= \begin{cases} p,&p\in H_+,\\ s(p),&p\in H_-. \end{cases} \]
Let \(P=\{r(p):p\in\mathcal Q\setminus H_0\}\), removing duplicates. Its size \(r\) satisfies \(r\le k\).

An upper set of \(P\) is a subset \(U\subseteq P\) such that \(u\in U\), \(v\in P\), and \(u\preceq v\) imply \(v\in U\).

Claim. Extendible labelings of \(\mathcal Q\) are in bijection with upper sets of \(P\).

Proof. An admissible \(F\) determines the upper set
\[ U=\{u\in P:F(u)=1\}. \]
Its labeling on \(\mathcal Q\) is recovered by assigning \(0\) on \(H_0\), assigning the indicator of \(U\) on \(H_+\), and assigning its negative after reflection on \(H_-\).

Conversely, fix an upper set \(U\subseteq P\). On the entire positive half of the real simplex, define
\[ f(z)= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{if some }u\in U\text{ satisfies }u\preceq z,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
This function is nondecreasing: if \(z\preceq w\), every witness \(u\preceq z\) also satisfies \(u\preceq w\). Moreover, \(f|_P\) is precisely the indicator of \(U\). Indeed, members of \(U\) witness their own value \(1\); if \(v\in P\) has such a witness, upper-set closure implies \(v\in U\).

Define \(F\) on all of \(\Delta\) by
\[ F(z)= \begin{cases} f(z),&z\in H_+,\\ 0,&z\in H_0,\\ -f(s(z)),&z\in H_-. \end{cases} \]
Neutrality follows directly. To check monotonicity, suppose \(z\preceq w\). Then
\[ (a_w-c_w)-(a_z-c_z) =(a_w-a_z)+(c_z-c_w)\ge0. \]
Therefore movement between the three regions can only proceed from \(H_-\) toward \(H_0\) toward \(H_+\). Across different regions, \(F(z)\le F(w)\) follows from their respective value ranges. Within \(H_+\), it follows from monotonicity of \(f\). Within \(H_-\), order reversal gives \(s(w)\preceq s(z)\), hence
\[ F(z)=-f(s(z))\le-f(s(w))=F(w). \]
Within \(H_0\), both values are zero. Thus \(F\) is admissible.

Finally, distinct upper sets give distinct panel labelings: every point of \(P\) represents at least one society in \(\mathcal Q\), whose label determines membership of that point in \(U\). This proves the bijection. \(\square\)

In particular, the construction establishes extension to the whole real simplex, not merely to a finite grid.

3. Counting upper sets by antichains

An antichain is a subset whose distinct elements are pairwise incomparable.

For any finite poset, upper sets are in bijection with antichains through their minimal elements. Here is the justification. The minimal elements of an upper set form an antichain, and every element of that upper set lies above a minimal element, because the poset is finite. Conversely, the upward closure of an antichain has precisely that antichain as its minimal elements. Indeed, any additional minimal element would lie strictly above an antichain member; and any antichain member lying strictly above another point of the closure would lie above a different antichain member, contradicting incomparability. The empty sets correspond as well.

It remains to count antichains of \(P\). Represent its points by their coordinate pairs \((a,c)\); the omitted coordinate is determined by their sum.

Two distinct points with equal \(a\)-coordinates are comparable, as are two with equal \(c\)-coordinates. When \(a_i<a_j\), the reverse comparison \(p_j\preceq p_i\) is impossible, while
\[ p_i\preceq p_j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c_i\ge c_j. \]
Hence they are incomparable exactly when \(c_i<c_j\).

It follows that antichains are precisely the subsets whose points, listed by increasing \(a\), have strictly increasing \(a\) and strictly increasing \(c\).

4. The exact recurrence

Sort \(P=\{p_1,\ldots,p_r\}\) by nondecreasing \(a\), breaking ties arbitrarily. Let \(D_i\) count nonempty antichains whose last point in this ordering is \(p_i\). Then
\[ D_i = 1+ \sum_{\substack{j<i\\a_j<a_i\\c_j<c_i}}D_j. \]

The initial \(1\) counts the singleton \(\{p_i\}\). Every larger antichain has a unique penultimate point \(p_j\), satisfying both strict inequalities. Removing \(p_i\) produces an antichain counted by \(D_j\).

Conversely, appending \(p_i\) to any antichain counted by such a \(D_j\) preserves strict increase in both coordinates, so produces an antichain. These operations are inverse, proving the recurrence without overcounting.

Including the empty antichain gives the answer:
\[ \boxed{C(\mathcal Q)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{r}D_i.} \]
If \(r=0\), this yields \(C(\mathcal Q)=1\), as required because all panel societies lie on the diagonal.

5. Bit complexity and conclusion

Omitted types can be filled with mass zero, positive representatives formed, and duplicates removed using exact rational comparisons. Each input rational has at most \(O(L)\) bits. Comparisons can therefore be performed by cross multiplication in polynomial time.

The recurrence uses \(O(k^2)\) comparisons and additions. There are at most \(2^r\) upper sets, so the answer and every intermediate count require at most \(k+1\) bits. Using elementary arithmetic, a sufficient total bit-complexity bound is \(O(L+k^2L^2+k^3)\).

Thus the algorithm is polynomial in the stated parameters; here \(m=2\) and \(\tau\le3\).

As an additional check, exhaustive enumeration agrees with the recurrence for every panel drawn from the full two-voter and three-voter grids. Their full-grid counts are respectively \(3\) and \(6\), consistent with the cited [Llamazares enumeration](https://eco.uva.es/boni/Papers/2014IJPAM.pdf). The proof above establishes the arbitrary rational-panel result independently.
