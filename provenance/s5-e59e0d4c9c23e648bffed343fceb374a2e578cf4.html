Theorem 5 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite initial type set \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu\), computably represented sub-Gaussian reward kernels, arm-capacity shares \(b_j\), type priorities, horizon \(n\), and threshold \(B\), evolve mass \(\nu_s(\theta,h)\) over private histories under the paper’s UCB rankings and capacity-constrained continuum Gale–Shapley matching, and decide whether expected aggregate agent-pessimal stable regret \(R^-_\mu(n)\le B\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(\Theta\) with positive masses \(\mu_\theta\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge2\) arms with positive binary-rational capacity shares \(b_j\) satisfying \(\sum_j b_j\ge1\), and each arm has a strict priority ordering of the types. Every arm and agent finds every potential partner acceptable.
For each \((\theta,j)\), an explicit finite table specifies a reward distribution \(P_{\theta j}\) with rational outcomes and rational probabilities, promised to be \(1\)-sub-Gaussian. Its mean \(r_{\theta j}\) may have either sign; each type's arm means are pairwise distinct. Rewards are fresh independent draws conditional on type and assigned arm.
Within each type, agents have a persistent label \(u\in[0,1]\), with initial mass \(\mu_\theta\,du\). Each arm orders agents first by its type priority and then by increasing label. Labels resolve priorities only; they convey no reward information.
A private history \(h\) records an agent's past assigned arms and rewards. Initially all histories are empty. Before round \(s\), rank arms by decreasing \(U_j(h,s)=+\infty\) if \(T_j(h)=0\), and otherwise \(U_j(h,s)=\widehat r_j(h)+\sqrt{3\log s/(2T_j(h))}\); ties favor smaller arm indices. Here \(T_j\) and \(\widehat r_j\) are that history's sample count and empirical mean.
In each round use agent-proposing continuum deferred acceptance: proposing mass applies down its ranking, and each arm retains its highest-priority mass up to capacity, rejecting the rest. A stable assignment has no positive mass preferring an arm with slack or with positive mass of lower-priority occupants. Assignments are understood up to null sets.
Maintain the joint mass over \((\theta,u,h)\), append each assignment and split its mass according to \(P_{\theta j}\); \(\nu_s(\theta,h)\) is its label marginal. Agents use only their own histories. Let \(x_{sj}\) be the total type-by-label-by-history mass assigned to arm \(j\) in round \(s\).
Using true mean rankings and the same capacities and priorities, let \(M^-\) be the arm-proposing stable assignment, and set \(V^-=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\int_0^1 r_{\theta,M^-(\theta,u)}\,du\). Define \(R^-_\mu(n)=nV^- -\sum_{s=1}^n\sum_{\theta,j}r_{\theta j}\,x_{sj}(\theta)\), where \(x_{sj}(\theta)\) denotes the type-\(\theta\) component of the assigned mass.
The input includes a unary horizon \(n\ge1\) and a binary-rational threshold \(B\), of either sign; \(L\) is the total input bit length.

Is deciding whether \(R^-_\mu(n)\le B\) in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,n,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents with sufficient arms, centralized UCB with agent-proposing Gale–Shapley has a problem-dependent \(O(\log n)\) upper bound on each agent's expected agent-pessimal stable regret. This is a regret guarantee, not an exact regret-evaluation complexity result. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05363v2)
No result settling the continuum exact-threshold problem specified above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The reward representation was unspecified. Chose explicit finite tables of rational outcomes and probabilities, a concrete subclass of computably represented kernels; retained the 1-sub-Gaussian promise and allowed signed rewards.
  • Normalized total society mass to one and required positive capacities with total capacity at least one, carrying over the sufficient-supply condition.
  • Required distinct arm means for each type to obtain strict true preferences; declared all partners acceptable.
  • Type priorities do not distinguish agents within a type. Added persistent uniform labels as strict priority refinements, and retained their joint distribution with private histories.
  • Specified agent-proposing deferred acceptance for each learning round and arm-proposing deferred acceptance for the true agent-pessimal benchmark.
  • Resolved the malformed sample-count denominator and time indexing in the displayed UCB formula by using the pre-round empirical mean and arm-specific count with the current round's logarithm; initialized unsampled indices to infinity and fixed index ties.
  • Specified independent stationary reward draws, empty initial histories, and exact distributional mass splitting rather than shared observations.
  • Chose unary encoding of the horizon so polynomial dependence on the number of rounds has its ordinary complexity meaning; allowed a signed rational threshold.

Exact regret-threshold complexity

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, even when \(\tau=1\), \(m=2\), and \(b_1=b_2=1\). The reduction below uses only dyadic reward outcomes, probabilities, and threshold. It therefore also applies if “binary rational” is interpreted as requiring dyadic rationals.

1. Encoding SUBSET SUM by an odd-length zero sum

We reduce from the classical \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete SUBSET SUM problem: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_N\) and a nonnegative integer \(T\), decide whether some subset sums to \(T\). This is the equality-form knapsack problem in [Karp’s original paper](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

Append a zero item if necessary so that \(N\) is even and \(N\ge2\). This does not change feasibility. Define

\[ k=N+1,\qquad H=1+T+\sum_{i=1}^N a_i,\qquad Q=4kH+1. \]

Thus \(k\ge3\) is odd. For each item define two positive integers

\[ v_{i,0}=Q^i,\qquad v_{i,1}=Q^i+a_i, \]

and define a target integer

\[ v_*=\sum_{i=1}^N Q^i+T. \]

Start with the multiset containing both signs of every \(v_{i,0}\), \(v_{i,1}\), and \(v_*\). It has \(4N+2\) entries. Let \(D\) be the least power of two at least \(4N+2\), and pad the multiset to size \(D\) using equally many additional copies of \(v_{1,0}\) and \(-v_{1,0}\).

Let \(Z\) be a uniform draw from these \(D\) entries. Its distribution is symmetric about zero. For independent draws \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_k\), write

\[ S=\sum_{\ell=1}^k Z_\ell,\qquad p_0=\Pr(S=0). \]

Lemma. The SUBSET SUM instance is feasible if and only if \(p_0>0\). Moreover, feasibility implies \(p_0\ge D^{-k}\).

Proof. A feasible subset gives a zero sum of exactly \(k\) entries: choose \(v_{i,1}\) for its selected items, \(v_{i,0}\) for the other items, and \(-v_*\). This ordered sequence has probability \(D^{-k}\).

Conversely, suppose exactly \(k\) entries sum to zero. Regard padding entries as belonging to the \(v_{1,0}\) category. Let \(\alpha_{i,0}\), \(\alpha_{i,1}\), and \(\beta\) denote signed occurrence counts: positive occurrences minus negative occurrences. Put

\[ c_i=\alpha_{i,0}+\alpha_{i,1},\qquad z=-\beta. \]

The zero-sum equation becomes

\[ 0=\sum_{i=1}^N(c_i-z)Q^i+e, \qquad e=\sum_{i=1}^N\alpha_{i,1}a_i-zT. \]

There are only \(k\) occurrences, so

\[ |e|\le k\max\{a_1,\ldots,a_N,T\}<Q, \qquad |c_i-z|\le k<Q. \]

Reducing the equation modulo \(Q\) gives \(e=0\). Dividing by \(Q\) and repeatedly reducing modulo \(Q\) then gives

\[ c_i=z\quad\text{for every }i. \]

If \(z=0\), each item category has equally many positive and negative occurrences, and the target category does too. Every category consequently contains an even number of occurrences, contradicting the odd total \(k\).

Thus \(z\ne0\). The number of occurrences is at least the sum of the absolute signed counts:

\[ k\ge|\beta|+\sum_{i=1}^N|c_i| =(N+1)|z|=k|z|. \]

Hence \(|z|=1\). Each of the \(N\) item categories and the target category must occur exactly once. Every item occurrence has sign \(z\), and the target occurrence has sign \(-z\). Since \(e=0\), the items using \(v_{i,1}\) satisfy

\[ \sum_{\{i:\,v_{i,1}\text{ was used}\}}a_i=T. \]

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

2. Constructing the bandit instance

Let \(W\) be the largest absolute entry in the multiset. Choose the least integer \(q\) such that

\[ 2^q\ge64k^2W, \qquad \varepsilon=2^{-q}. \]

The stochastic reward will be \(Y=\varepsilon Z\). It satisfies

\[ \mathbb E Y=0,\qquad |Y|\le\varepsilon W\le\frac{1}{64k^2}. \]

For positive integers \(t\), define

\[ A_t=\sqrt{\frac{3\log(t+2)}{2}} \left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt t}\right). \]

Choose a dyadic rational \(c\) satisfying

\[ \left|c+A_k+\frac{\varepsilon}{2k}\right| \le\frac{\varepsilon}{8k}. \]

Section 6 proves that such a \(c\) can be computed in polynomial time. In particular,

\[ -\frac{5\varepsilon}{8k} \le c+A_k\le -\frac{3\varepsilon}{8k}, \]

so \(c+A_k\) lies strictly between \(-\varepsilon/k\) and zero, and \(c<0\).

Construct the society as follows:

  • There is one type, of mass \(1\).
  • There are two arms, each of capacity \(1\).
  • Arm \(1\) gives reward \(Y\); arm \(2\) gives deterministic reward \(c\).
  • The horizon is \(n=k+2\).
  • Writing \(\Delta=-c>0\), set the threshold to

\[ B=\Delta\left(\frac32-\frac{1}{2D^k}\right). \]

Repeated reward outcomes are merged in the explicit table. Their probabilities are integer multiples of \(D^{-1}\), hence dyadic.

3. Checking the model’s promises and benchmark

The arm means are \(r_{\theta1}=0\) and \(r_{\theta2}=c<0\), so they are distinct.

Both reward distributions are \(1\)-sub-Gaussian. The deterministic distribution has centered moment-generating function equal to \(1\). For the symmetric, mean-zero variable \(Y\), the inequality \(\cosh x\le e^{x^2/2}\) gives, for every real \(\lambda\),

\[ \mathbb E e^{\lambda Y} =\mathbb E\cosh(\lambda Y) \le e^{\lambda^2(\varepsilon W)^2/2} \le e^{\lambda^2/2}. \]

Here the first inequality follows term by term from \((2j)!\ge2^j j!\).

In every learning round, the mass proposing to either arm is at most the total mass \(1\), which equals that arm’s capacity. Thus all first proposals are accepted. Deferred acceptance assigns each history to its highest-ranked arm, and labels never affect an assignment.

Under true means, everyone prefers arm \(1\). Any assignment placing positive mass at arm \(2\) leaves slack at arm \(1\), and is therefore unstable. Consequently the unique true stable assignment places everyone at arm \(1\), including the arm-proposing assignment. Hence

\[ V^-=0. \]

4. Proving the UCB sampling schedule

Round \(1\) assigns everyone to arm \(1\), because both indices are infinite and the index tie favors arm \(1\). Round \(2\) assigns everyone to the unpulled arm \(2\).

Suppose that before round \(s=t+2\), arm \(1\) has been sampled \(t\) times and arm \(2\) once. Let \(\overline Y_t\) be the empirical mean of the arm-\(1\) samples. The index difference is

\[ U_1-U_2 = \overline Y_t- \left[ c+\sqrt{\frac{3\log(t+2)}2} \left(1-\frac1{\sqrt t}\right) \right] =\overline Y_t-(c+A_t). \]

We first show that arm \(1\) is selected for every \(t<k\).

Both factors defining \(A_t\) are nonnegative and increasing. Moreover,

\[ A_k-A_{k-1} \ge \sqrt{\frac{3\log(k+1)}2} \left(\frac1{\sqrt{k-1}}-\frac1{\sqrt k}\right) > \frac1{2k^{3/2}} \ge\frac1{2k^2}. \]

For the strict inequality, the square-root factor exceeds \(1\), since \(k+1\ge4\), and

\[ \frac1{\sqrt{k-1}}-\frac1{\sqrt k} = \frac1{\sqrt{k-1}\sqrt k(\sqrt k+\sqrt{k-1})} >\frac1{2k^{3/2}}. \]

Therefore, for \(1\le t<k\),

\[ c+A_t \le c+A_k-(A_k-A_{k-1}) <-\frac1{2k^2}. \]

Every possible empirical mean satisfies

\[ \overline Y_t\ge-\varepsilon W \ge-\frac1{64k^2} >-\frac1{2k^2}. \]

Thus \(U_1>U_2\). Induction proves that rounds \(3,\ldots,k+1\) all select arm \(1\).

Before the final round \(n=k+2\), everyone has consequently received exactly \(k\) independent arm-\(1\) draws and one arm-\(2\) draw. Now

\[ \overline Y_k=\frac{\varepsilon S}{k}. \]

Because \(S\) is an integer and \(c+A_k\) lies strictly between \(-\varepsilon/k\) and zero, the final assignment is exactly

\[ \text{arm }1\ \Longleftrightarrow\ S\ge0, \qquad \text{arm }2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ S<0. \]

All comparisons after initialization are strict.

5. Exact regret and reduction correctness

By the prescribed mass splitting, an ordered sequence of the \(k\) stochastic draws has precisely its product probability as mass. Thus the final mass assigned to arm \(2\) is \(\Pr(S<0)\). This uses the exact history-mass recursion, without any approximation or population-limit argument.

The distribution of \(S\) is symmetric, so

\[ \Pr(S<0)=\frac{1-p_0}{2}. \]

Before the final round, arm \(2\) was selected exactly once, contributing mean reward \(c\); all other assignments contributed mean reward zero. The final round contributes \(c\Pr(S<0)\). Since \(V^-=0\),

\[ R^-_\mu(n) =-c\bigl(1+\Pr(S<0)\bigr) =\Delta\left(\frac32-\frac{p_0}{2}\right). \]

Consequently,

\[ R^-_\mu(n)\le B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_0\ge D^{-k} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the SUBSET SUM instance is feasible}. \]

The last equivalence is exactly the lemma from Section 1.

6. Polynomial-time construction and encoding length

Let \(\ell\) denote the source-instance bit length. Padding adds at most one item, and \(k\) is polynomially bounded in \(\ell\). The integers \(H\) and \(Q\) have polynomial bit length, and

\[ \log_2 W=O(N\log_2 Q). \]

Hence \(q\), the reward-outcome encoding lengths, and the table’s total encoding length are polynomial in \(\ell\). Also \(D=O(N)\), and the unary horizon \(n=k+2\) has polynomial length.

It remains to justify computing \(c\). Logarithms and square roots here can be approximated with certified rational intervals in polynomial time at the required precision. Explicitly, with

\[ \rho=\frac{k+1}{k+3}, \]

we have

\[ \log(k+2) =2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. \]

After the first \(J\) terms, the positive remainder is at most

\[ \frac{2\rho^{2J+1}}{1-\rho^2} \le 2(k+3)e^{-4J/(k+3)}. \]

For precision \(2^{-p}\), taking \(J=(k+3)(p+k+3)\) suffices. The partial sum and the geometric remainder bound are rational and have polynomial encoding length. Square roots of the resulting rational interval endpoints, and of \(k\), admit certified approximations by rational bisection.

For completeness, write

\[ A_k=a(1-b^{-1}), \qquad a=\sqrt{\frac{3\log(k+2)}2},\qquad b=\sqrt k. \]

Both \(a\) and \(b\) lie between \(1\) and \(k+2\). If they are approximated within \(\delta<1/2\), the resulting error in \(A_k\) is at most \((2k+5)\delta\): the reciprocal error is at most \(2\delta\), and the error from the first factor is at most \(\delta\). Thus precision \(p=O(q+\log k)\) suffices to approximate \(A_k\) within \(\varepsilon/(32k)\). Rounding the resulting approximation of \(-A_k-\varepsilon/(2k)\) to a dyadic grid of spacing at most \(\varepsilon/(32k)\) produces the required \(c\).

This construction requires no exact comparison involving a transcendental number. The dyadic rational \(c\) has polynomial bit length. Finally, \(D\) is a power of two, so \(B\) is dyadic and has encoding length bounded by the encoding length of \(c\) plus \(O(k\log D)\).

The construction is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction, proving the stated \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. A polynomial-time algorithm for the requested exact threshold problem would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). No membership claim for the full problem is needed.
