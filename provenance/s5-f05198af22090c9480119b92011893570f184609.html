The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite task set \(M\), conflict graph \(G=(M,E)\), common additive valuation \(v:M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\), and rational population scale \(N\geq 1\), choose nonnegative masses \(x_B\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\). Require \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni g}x_B=1/N\) for each task \(g\), EF1 between every pair of bundles with positive mass, and \(\bigl||B|-|D|\bigr|\leq 1\) for all bundles \(B,D\) with positive mass. Is there such a mass allocation with \(N\sum_B x_B\,|E\cap\binom{B}{2}|\leq |E|/N\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite set \(M\) of \(m\) goods, a simple undirected conflict graph \(G=(M,E)\), a common additive valuation \(v:M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\), and a binary rational population scale \(N\geq1\), consider the following feasibility problem.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here the sole valuation type is \(v\), with mass \(1\), so \(\tau=1\); support size counts listed types, not possible types or bundles.
Choose masses \(x_B\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq M\), including the empty bundle, with \(\sum_{B\subseteq M}x_B=1\) and \(\sum_{B\ni g}x_B=1/N\) for every \(g\in M\).
Every ordered pair of positive-mass bundles must satisfy goods EF1, allowing an empty envied bundle, and every such pair \(B,D\) must satisfy \(\bigl||B|-|D|\bigr|\leq1\).
Writing \(c(B)=|\{\{g,h\}\in E:g,h\in B\}|\), also require \(N\sum_{B\subseteq M}x_Bc(B)\leq |E|/N\). Bundle overlaps are permitted; completeness is expressed by the marginal equalities.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including \(N\); state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and any additional parameters.

Is this mass-allocation feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For an integer number \(n\) of discrete agents with identical nonnegative additive valuations, a complete balanced EF1 allocation with at most \(|E|/n\) conflicts can be computed in \(O(m\log m+|E|)\) time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20929v1).
No result settling this exact mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted tasks as goods with nonnegative utility and used goods EF1, including its empty-envied-bundle convention.
  • Specified the conflict graph as simple, undirected, and unweighted.
  • Specified allocation masses as nonnegative rationals, with no prescribed bundle support or support-size bound.
  • Applied the society support convention to valuation types: the common valuation gives one input type of normalized mass 1; bundle masses are allocation variables.
  • Retained rational N >= 1 as stated, including noninteger scales, and included its binary encoding in L.
  • Allowed empty bundles and overlaps between bundle types; the given marginal equalities replace discrete completeness and disjointness.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, every valid instance with \(\tau=1\) is feasible, even when \(N\) is nonintegral.

A feasible allocation can be constructed with at most \(m+2\) positive-mass bundles, using polynomial time in \(m,L\). This bound concerns bundles; the society still has just one listed valuation type.

The [supplied paper’s Theorem 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20929v1) covers integer numbers of discrete agents. The proof below directly handles arbitrary binary rational \(N\), without invoking that theorem.

1. Ordered fractional slots

Write \(p=1/N\), so \(0<p\leq1\). If \(m=0\), assigning mass \(1\) to the empty bundle proves feasibility. Henceforth assume \(m\geq1\).

Order the goods as \(g_1,\ldots,g_m\) so that \(v(g_1)\geq\cdots\geq v(g_m)\). Define

\[ \mu=mp,\qquad K=\lceil\mu\rceil,\qquad r=K-\mu. \]

If \(r>0\), introduce one auxiliary dummy good of value \(0\), placed after all real goods in this ordering. The dummy will be removed from every output bundle. Give each real good interval length \(p\), and the dummy, when present, interval length \(r\). Place these intervals consecutively, partitioning \([0,K]\).

There are \(K\) slots: slot \(j\) is the interval \([j-1,j]\). For each slot \(j\) and real or dummy good \(g\), let \(a_{jg}\) be the length of their intersection. Thus

\[ \sum_g a_{jg}=1,\qquad \sum_j a_{jg}=p\quad\text{for every real good }g, \]

and the dummy’s column sum, when present, is \(r<1\). Every column sum is at most \(1\). Each good’s interval has length at most \(1\), so it intersects at most two slots in positive length. Consequently, there are at most \(2(m+1)\) positive entries.

The ordering supplies the essential fairness property:

If \(a_{jg}>0\) and \(a_{j+1,h}>0\), then \(v(g)\geq v(h)\).

Indeed, if \(g\) occurred strictly after \(h\) in the interval ordering, its left endpoint would be at least the right endpoint of \(h\). Positive intersection with slot \(j\) requires the former endpoint to be smaller than \(j\), whereas positive intersection with slot \(j+1\) requires the latter endpoint to be larger than \(j\). These requirements contradict each other. If \(g=h\), the claimed valuation inequality is equality.

2. Every supported integral matching gives a compatible bundle

Consider any integral matrix \(A\) supported on the positive entries of \(a\), with each slot sum equal to \(1\) and every good’s column sum at most \(1\). Such a matrix selects exactly one good per slot, with no good selected twice.

Let its selected goods be \(b_1,\ldots,b_K\), indexed by slot, and remove the dummy to obtain a real bundle \(B\). Its size is \(K\), or \(K-1\) if the dummy was selected. Therefore all bundles obtained in this way differ in cardinality by at most \(1\).

Now take any two such matchings, producing bundles \(B,D\), with selected goods \(b_j,d_j\). The property established above gives

\[ v(b_j)\geq v(d_{j+1})\qquad(1\leq j<K). \]

Since all valuations are nonnegative and removing the dummy does not change value,

\[ v(B)=\sum_{j=1}^K v(b_j) \geq \sum_{j=2}^K v(d_j) =v(D)-v(d_1). \]

If \(D\) is nonempty, choose a real good \(h\in D\) of maximum value. Whether \(d_1\) is real or dummy, \(v(h)\geq v(d_1)\). Hence

\[ v(B)\geq v(D)-v(h)=v(D\setminus\{h\}). \]

If \(D\) is empty, the stipulated EF1 condition is satisfied automatically. Thus every ordered pair of bundles obtainable from these matchings satisfies goods EF1.

It remains to find a rational distribution over the matchings that preserves the real-good marginals and controls conflicts.

3. A rounding step that preserves marginals and controls conflicts

For any feasible fractional matrix \(z\) supported on the original positive entries, define the inclusion amount of a real good by

\[ q_g(z)=\sum_j z_{jg}, \]

and define the potential

\[ F(z)=\sum_{\{g,h\}\in E}q_g(z)q_h(z). \]

Initially, \(q_g(a)=p\) for every real good, so

\[ F(a)=|E|p^2. \]

We describe a two-outcome rounding step for every nonintegral feasible matrix \(z\).

Form the bipartite graph of its strictly fractional entries: one side consists of slots, the other of goods, and an edge represents an entry strictly between \(0\) and \(1\).

A slot incident to fractional edges has at least two such edges. To see this, an incident entry equal to \(1\) would preclude any positive fractional entry, while one fractional entry alone could not give slot sum \(1\).

There are two possibilities:

  • If the fractional graph contains a cycle, select a simple cycle. Its length is even because the graph is bipartite.
  • Otherwise, select a path between two leaves of a nontrivial tree component. Both leaves are goods, by the preceding slot-degree observation, so this path also has even length.

Assign alternating signs \(+1,-1\) to the selected edges, giving a direction \(d\). Along a cycle, every column sum remains fixed. Along the selected path, only the two endpoint goods’ column sums change, in opposite directions. Every slot sum remains fixed.

Let \(\alpha>0\) and \(\beta>0\) be the largest amounts for which respectively \(z+\alpha d\) and \(z-\beta d\) keep the selected entries in \([0,1]\). These changes also preserve the column upper bounds: internal column sums are unchanged, and each path endpoint has just one fractional incident entry and no incident entry equal to \(1\).

Both resulting matrices are therefore feasible. Each has at least one fewer strictly fractional entry.

Choose the two outcomes with probabilities

\[ z+\alpha d:\quad \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}, \qquad z-\beta d:\quad \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}. \]

Equivalently, the random displacement \(\theta\) satisfies

\[ \mathbb E[\theta]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\theta^2]=\alpha\beta. \]

Every matrix entry, and hence every real-good inclusion amount, preserves its expectation.

The potential also behaves as required. On a cycle, every inclusion amount is unchanged, so \(F\) is unchanged. On a path, only its endpoint inclusion amounts change. If both endpoints are real goods \(u,w\), orient the path so that these amounts become \(q_u+\theta\) and \(q_w-\theta\). All terms in \(F\) change linearly in \(\theta\), except their mutual edge, if present, whose product changes as

\[ (q_u+\theta)(q_w-\theta) =q_uq_w+(q_w-q_u)\theta-\theta^2. \]

If an endpoint is dummy, only one real-good inclusion amount changes, so every affected term is linear. Consequently, in all cases,

\[ \mathbb E[F(\text{new matrix})]\leq F(z). \]

This proves the rounding step’s three properties: feasibility is preserved, real-good marginals are preserved in expectation, and expected potential does not increase.

4. A feasible rational mass allocation

Repeat the rounding step until the matrix is integral. Each step makes at least one fractional entry integral and never changes an integral entry. Therefore every branch terminates after at most \(2(m+1)\) steps.

All initial entries are rational. The step lengths and branching probabilities are rational as well. Thus the finite rounding tree defines a rational distribution over integral matchings.

Remove the dummy from each outcome and combine outcomes giving the same real bundle. Let the resulting masses be \(x_B\). They are nonnegative rationals satisfying

\[ \sum_B x_B=1,\qquad \sum_{B\ni g}x_B=p=\frac1N \quad\text{for every real good }g. \]

Section 2 proves pairwise EF1 and balance for their entire support.

At an integral matching, \(q_g\) is the indicator that \(g\) belongs to its real bundle \(B\). Therefore \(F=c(B)\). Repeatedly applying the expected-potential inequality yields

\[ \sum_B x_Bc(B)\leq F(a)=|E|p^2. \]

Multiplying by \(N\) gives exactly the required conflict bound:

\[ N\sum_B x_Bc(B)\leq N|E|p^2=\frac{|E|}{N}. \]

This already proves feasibility for every valid instance. The following compression makes the construction polynomial in size and time.

5. Polynomial support and deterministic construction

Maintain a weighted list of fractional matrices. For each matrix \(z\), associate the vector

\[ Y(z)=\bigl(1,q_{g_1}(z),\ldots,q_{g_m}(z),F(z)\bigr) \in\mathbb Q^{m+2}. \]

Initially the list contains only \(a\), with weight \(1\).

In each stage, replace every nonintegral matrix by both rounding outcomes with their prescribed weights; retain integral matrices unchanged. This preserves the list’s total weight and every real-good marginal, and does not increase its weighted potential.

Then compress the list to at most \(m+2\) matrices while preserving its weighted \(Y\)-vector. Here is an explicit compression procedure.

If the list contains more than \(m+2\) matrices, their \(Y\)-vectors are linearly dependent. Find a nonzero rational vector \(\lambda\) with

\[ \sum_i\lambda_iY(z_i)=0. \]

Its coordinates sum to zero because the first coordinate of every \(Y(z_i)\) is \(1\). Thus \(\lambda\) has both positive and negative coordinates. For current positive weights \(w_i\), set

\[ t=\min_{\lambda_i>0}\frac{w_i}{\lambda_i}, \qquad w'_i=w_i-t\lambda_i. \]

The new weights are nonnegative, at least one is zero, and their weighted \(Y\)-vector is unchanged. Remove zero weights and repeat.

After each stage the list contains at most \(m+2\) matrices. Every surviving nonintegral descendant has undergone another rounding step, and compression retains only existing matrices. Hence after at most \(2(m+1)\) stages every matrix is integral.

The resulting list deterministically describes the required allocation, with at most \(m+2\) positive-mass bundles after dummy removal and merging duplicates.

6. Running time and binary arithmetic

There is no enumeration of a number of agents proportional to the numerical value of \(N\).

Write \(N=A/D\), where \(A,D\) are positive integers and \(A\geq D\). All initial matrix entries are multiples of \(1/A\). Every rounding step length is also a multiple of \(1/A\), so this property persists. Each state has at most \(2(m+1)\) nonzero entries.

Consequently, coordinates of \(Y(z)\) have denominator dividing \(A^2\), and \(0\leq F(z)\leq |E|\). After multiplication by \(A^2\), these vectors have integer coordinates of bit length \(O(L+\log(m+2))\).

For completeness, the compression weights also have polynomial bit length. With \(d=m+2\), an integer linear dependence can be chosen using determinants of submatrices of the scaled vectors. The determinant bound gives dependence coefficients of bit length

\[ O\bigl(d(L+\log(m+2))\bigr). \]

Maintain all weights over a common denominator. A branching stage increases its bit length by at most \(O(d(L+1))\). Each compression step increases it by at most the bit length of one dependence coefficient. There are \(O(d)\) compression steps per stage and \(O(m+1)\) stages. Thus all weight numerators and denominators have bit length

\[ O\bigl((m+1)^3(L+\log(m+2))\bigr). \]

Sorting requires \(O(m\log(m+1))\) rational comparisons. Finding rounding paths and evaluating potentials across all stages requires \(O((m+1)^3+(m+1)^2|E|)\) arithmetic operations. Computing the linear dependences by exact Gaussian elimination gives a conservative total bound of

\[ O\bigl(m\log(m+1)+(m+1)^5+(m+1)^2|E|\bigr) \]

exact arithmetic operations, all on polynomial-length integers. Therefore the explicit allocation is computable in polynomial bit time in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(\tau=1\) and \(|E|\leq\binom m2\).

On the promised domain of valid inputs, the feasibility decision itself takes \(O(L)\) time to read the input and return YES.
