2. Forts and their activation

Write
$$ k_t=\sum_u a_{tu}, \qquad h_t=\left\lceil\frac{k_t}{2}\right\rceil. $$

If $\delta=1/2$, every private signal is red. Starting from uncolored announcements, every update produces red. Every vertex is eventually selected almost surely, so $p_n=q=1$. Henceforth assume
$$ 0<\delta<\frac12, \qquad \beta=\frac12-\delta>0. $$

For a graph $G$, call a nonempty vertex set $S$ a fort if
$$ |N_G(v)\cap S|\geq \left\lceil\frac{\deg_G(v)}2\right\rceil \qquad(v\in S). $$
Write
$$ \partial_G S=\{w\notin S:N_G(w)\cap S\neq\varnothing\}, \qquad C_G(S)=S\cup\partial_G S. $$

Lemma 2.1: permanence. Suppose every vertex of $S$ currently announces blue. If, for each $v\in S$, either
$$ |N_G(v)\cap S|>\frac{\deg_G(v)}2 $$
or $v$ has a blue private signal, then every vertex of $S$ announces blue forever.

Proof. At an update of $v$, its internal blue neighbors outnumber all possible external red neighbors in the strict-inequality case. Otherwise, the fort inequality guarantees that blue cannot lose: a possible tie is resolved by the blue private signal. Uncolored external neighbors only reduce the competing red count. Induction over updates proves permanence. $\square$

In particular, blue private signals at every vertex of a fort suffice.

We represent updates by independent rate-one Poisson clocks, one per vertex. The superposed process has independent uniform vertex labels, so its embedded update sequence has exactly the distribution required in the question.

For a fort $S$, consider the event that:

  • every private signal in $S$ is blue;
  • every vertex of $S$ rings before time one;
  • no vertex of $\partial_G S$ rings before time one.

Until all vertices of $S$ have rung, their external neighbors remain uncolored. Inductively, every update within $S$ produces blue: colored internal neighbors are blue, and an empty colored neighborhood is resolved by a blue private signal. Thus the event activates a permanent blue fort. Its probability is
$$ \beta^{|S|}(1-e^{-1})^{|S|}e^{-|\partial_G S|}. $$

Consequently, if $|S|\leq K$ and the maximum degree is at most $d$, its activation probability is at least
$$ \theta_K=\beta^K(1-e^{-1})^K e^{-dK}>0. $$

Lemma 2.2: independent activation. If $S_1,\ldots,S_r$ are forts of size at most $K$ whose sets $C_G(S_i)$ are pairwise disjoint, then
$$ \Pr(\text{eventual red consensus}\mid G) \leq(1-\theta_K)^r \leq e^{-\theta_Kr}. $$

Proof. The activation events depend on disjoint sets of signals and clocks, and therefore are independent. Any one activation prevents eventual red consensus. $\square$

3. Polynomial-time recognition of tree forts

A compatible typed fort pattern is a finite nonempty simple graph $F$ with a role assignment $\phi:V(F)\to T$ such that, for every vertex $x$ and every role $u$,
$$ \deg_F(x)\geq h_{\phi(x)} $$
and
$$ |\{y\in N_F(x):\phi(y)=u\}| \leq a_{\phi(x),u}. $$

A tree pattern is such a pattern whose underlying graph is a tree.

For every pair $(t,u)$ with $a_{tu}>0$, introduce a Boolean state $x_{t,u}$. Its intended meaning is that there exists a finite feasible branch rooted at role $t$, with one additional parent edge to role $u$. The parent edge counts toward the root’s threshold and consumes one role-$u$ neighbor slot.

Start with every state false and iterate
$$ x^{(j+1)}_{t,u} = \mathbf 1\!\left\{ \sum_v \bigl(a_{tv}-\mathbf 1_{\{v=u\}}\bigr) x^{(j)}_{v,t} \geq h_t-1 \right\}. $$
Undefined states are interpreted as zero. The balance promise and positive masses imply
$$ a_{tu}>0\iff a_{ut}>0, $$
so the branch states respect undirected role edges.

The iteration increases monotonically from zero. With at most $\tau^2$ states, it stabilizes after at most $\tau^2$ rounds that make a strict change. Denote its fixed point by $x^*$.

Accept the tree test if some role $t$ satisfies
$$ \sum_v a_{tv}x^*_{v,t}\geq h_t. $$

Proposition 3.1. The tree test accepts exactly when a compatible tree fort pattern exists. It runs in deterministic time polynomial in $\tau$ and $L$.

Proof. A branch with no children is feasible exactly when its parent edge meets the root’s threshold, that is, when $h_t\leq1$. These are precisely the states accepted in the first round.

Inductively, the recurrence accepts a state when at least $h_t-1$ available child slots can be filled with already feasible branches. Attach disjoint copies of those branches, respecting the remaining capacities. This constructs a finite feasible branch.

Conversely, induction on branch height shows that every finite feasible branch is eventually accepted: its child branches are accepted first, and their number meets the root’s threshold after counting the parent edge.

The same construction and induction, now without a parent edge, prove the final root test. A role with $k_t=0$ supplies a singleton tree pattern.

There are polynomially many rounds, states, and summands. All capacities and sums have polynomial bit length. The algorithm need not expand a witnessing tree. $\square$

We also need the implication from this test to $q=0$, proved here rather than assumed from the notes.

Proposition 3.2. If the tree test accepts, then there is a constant $c>0$, depending on the fixed input, such that
$$ p_n\leq e^{-cn} $$
for every admissible $n$. Hence $q=0$.

Proof. Choose a witnessing tree $F$, rooted at a role-$t$ vertex. Let $K=|V(F)|$ and let $H$ be its height.

For every role-$t$ vertex $v$ of every admissible graph $G$, construct a role-preserving map $f:F\to G$ with $f(\text{root})=v$. At each tree vertex, map its children to distinct neighbors of its image with the required roles, excluding its parent’s image. The capacity inequalities guarantee enough choices.

This map is injective on the neighbors of each tree vertex, although different branches may collide globally. Its image $S(v)$ is nevertheless a fort. Indeed, for $w=f(x)$, the distinct images of the neighbors of $x$ give
$$ |N_G(w)\cap S(v)|\geq\deg_F(x)\geq h_{\phi(x)}. $$
Moreover,
$$ |S(v)|\leq K, \qquad C_G(S(v))\subseteq B_G(v,H+1). $$

Greedily choose role-$t$ roots at pairwise distances greater than $2H+2$. A ball of radius $2H+2$ contains at most
$$ B=\sum_{j=0}^{2H+2}d^j $$
vertices, where $d^0=1$. Thus at least $n\mu_t/B$ roots can be chosen, and their fort neighborhoods are disjoint. Lemma 2.2 gives
$$ \Pr(\text{eventual red consensus}\mid G) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{\theta_K\mu_t}{B}n\right). $$
This holds for every admissible graph, so averaging preserves it. $\square$

4. A polynomial-time test for unicyclic forts

A unicyclic pattern is a connected compatible typed fort pattern having exactly one cycle. For a connected graph this is equivalent to
$$ |E(F)|=|V(F)|. $$

Use the branch fixed point $x^*$ from Section 3. Construct a directed graph with states $(u,t)$ for which $a_{tu}>0$, interpreted as a cycle edge entering role $t$ from role $u$.

Include a transition
$$ (u,t)\longrightarrow(t,v) $$
exactly when, for every role $w$,
$$ r_w=a_{tw}-\mathbf 1_{\{w=u\}}-\mathbf 1_{\{w=v\}}\geq0, $$
and
$$ \sum_w r_w x^*_{w,t}\geq h_t-2. $$

The two subtracted slots reserve the cycle neighbors. When $u=v$, they reserve two distinct neighbors of that same role.

Accept the unicyclic test if this directed graph contains a directed cycle, including a self-loop.

Proposition 4.1. The unicyclic test accepts exactly when a compatible unicyclic fort pattern exists. It runs in deterministic time polynomial in $\tau$ and $L$.

Proof. Suppose the test accepts. The directed cycle describes a cyclic role sequence with feasible transitions at every position. If its length is less than three, repeat the sequence until the length is at least three. Realize it as a simple cycle on distinct abstract vertices carrying those roles.

At a cycle vertex of role $t$, the transition inequality supplies at least
$$ \max\{0,h_t-2\} $$
available slots for feasible child branches. Attach disjoint copies of their witnesses. The resulting graph is connected and unicyclic, satisfies every role capacity, and meets every threshold.

Conversely, let $F$ be a compatible unicyclic pattern. Its unique cycle has length at least three. The parts attached to that cycle are trees. Each branch attached to a cycle vertex is feasible and therefore has an accepted branch state by Proposition 3.1.

At a cycle vertex of role $t$ whose cycle neighbors have roles $u,v$, the attached branches use only the capacities $r_w$ and supply at least $h_t-2$ children. Hence the corresponding transition exists. Traversing the cycle gives a directed closed walk, which contains a directed cycle.

The directed graph has at most $\tau^2$ states and $\tau^3$ possible transitions. Transition checks use polynomial-bit-length arithmetic, and directed-cycle detection takes polynomial time. $\square$

This recognition result alone does not determine $q$. The next section establishes the required occurrence probability in the actual graph ensemble.

5. Finite patterns in the role-constrained ensemble

5.1 Configuration representation and simplicity

Let
$$ N_t=n\mu_t. $$
At each role-$t$ vertex place $a_{tu}$ labeled stubs designated for role $u$.

For $t<u$, independently match the role-$t$ stubs designated for $u$ bijectively to the role-$u$ stubs designated for $t$. Their counts agree by the balance promise. Within each role $t$, independently pair the $N_ta_{tt}$ internal stubs uniformly. Admissibility ensures that this number is even.

Conditioned on simplicity, this construction gives the uniform distribution on $\mathcal G_n$. Indeed, every simple admissible graph has exactly
$$ \prod_{t\in T} \left(\prod_{u\in T}a_{tu}!\right)^{N_t} $$
stub realizations: at each vertex, assign its designated stubs bijectively to the appropriate neighbors.

We use the following standard bounded-degree simplicity facts: an ordinary configuration model with bounded maximum degree and total degree tending to infinity has simplicity probability bounded away from zero; the corresponding conclusion holds for a bipartite configuration model when both maximum degrees are bounded and the common stub total tends to infinity. These are specializations of [Janson, Theorems 1.1 and 5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.6344).

Their hypotheses hold here. Every nonempty block has a stub total proportional to $n$, and all degrees are fixed. In the bipartite theorem, bounded degrees give its quadratic-sum condition, while maximum degree is negligible compared with the stub total. Blocks with no stubs cause no obstruction.

There are finitely many independent blocks. Consequently, for all sufficiently large admissible $n$,
$$ \Pr(\text{the full configuration is simple})\geq s_0>0, $$
where $s_0$ depends only on the fixed role data.

5.2 Expected pattern counts

Fix a finite compatible simple typed graph $F$. Let:

  • $v_t$ be its number of role-$t$ vertices;
  • $e_{tu}$ be its number of edges between roles $t,u$, with $t\leq u$;
  • $c_{xu}$ be the number of role-$u$ neighbors of a vertex $x$ in $F$.

Define the falling factorial
$$ (z)_r=z(z-1)\cdots(z-r+1), \qquad (z)_0=1. $$

In the configuration model, let $X_F$ count injective role-preserving embeddings of $F$, including the choice of stubs realizing its edges. In a simple configuration this equals the usual number of injective role-preserving graph embeddings.

Choosing vertices, choosing stubs, and prescribing their pairings gives
$$ \mathbb E X_F = \frac{ \displaystyle \prod_t(N_t)_{v_t} \prod_{x\in V(F)}\prod_u (a_{\phi(x),u})_{c_{xu}} }{ \displaystyle \prod_t\prod_{j=0}^{e_{tt}-1} (N_ta_{tt}-2j-1) \prod_{t<u}(N_ta_{tu})_{e_{tu}} }. $$
Products for unused edge classes are empty and equal one.

To justify the denominators, prescribing $r$ disjoint pairs in a uniform pairing of $M$ stubs has probability
$$ \frac1{(M-1)(M-3)\cdots(M-2r+1)}. $$
Prescribing $r$ matches in a uniform bijection between two sets of size $M$ has probability $1/(M)_r$.

All factors involving $F$ are fixed. Every used stub total is proportional to $n$, and compatibility makes the stub-choice factors positive. Thus
$$ \mathbb E X_F = \Theta\!\left(n^{|V(F)|-|E(F)|}\right). $$
In particular, when $F$ is unicyclic,
$$ \mathbb E X_F\longrightarrow c_F>0. $$

The same counting argument gives the upper bound
$$ \mathbb E X_H=O\!\left(n^{|V(H)|-|E(H)|}\right) $$
for every fixed typed multigraph pattern $H$ arising from prescribed stub pairings.

5.3 Unicyclic patterns survive conditioning

Lemma 5.1. If a compatible unicyclic pattern $F$ exists, then there is $\rho_F>0$ such that, for every sufficiently large admissible $n$,
$$ \Pr(F\text{ occurs in }G_n)\geq\rho_F. $$

Proof. First,
$$ \mathbb E X_F^2=O(1). $$
To see this, classify pairs of embedded copies by their vertex and stub identifications. There are finitely many identification patterns because $F$ is fixed.

Let $H$ be the union pattern for one such pair. If the copies are disjoint, each component of $H$ contains a cycle. If they overlap, their union is connected and still contains a cycle. Hence every component contains a cycle and
$$ |E(H)|\geq|V(H)|. $$
The pattern-count bound from Section 5.2 makes each contribution $O(1)$. Summing finitely many contributions proves the second-moment bound.

It remains to ensure a positive first moment after conditioning on simplicity. Fix an injective vertex embedding and a compatible choice of stubs for $F$, and condition on those prescribed edges.

The remaining stubs form independent configuration blocks. Their degree sequences differ from the original ones at only finitely many vertices and remain bounded. Their nonempty stub totals still tend to infinity. The simplicity facts from Section 5.1 therefore give a positive lower bound for the probability that all remaining blocks are simple.

Additionally, the remaining configuration must avoid creating a second edge along one of the finitely many prescribed edges of $F$. For any such pair of endpoints, the expected number of remaining edges joining them is $O(1/n)$: endpoint stub counts are bounded and the relevant block has linearly many stubs. A union bound gives total forbidden-edge probability $O(1/n)$.

Thus, conditioned on any prescribed realization of $F$, the full configuration is simple with probability at least $s_F>0$ for all sufficiently large admissible $n$. The bound is uniform over the vertex embeddings and stub choices, by symmetry within roles.

Summing over these realizations yields
$$ \mathbb E[X_F\mathbf 1_{\{\mathrm{simple}\}}] \geq s_F\mathbb E X_F. $$
Consequently, for some constants $c_1,C_1>0$,
$$ \mathbb E[X_F\mid\mathrm{simple}]\geq c_1, \qquad \mathbb E[X_F^2\mid\mathrm{simple}]\leq C_1. $$
The second inequality uses the lower bound $s_0$ on simplicity probability.

Finally, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$ \Pr(X_F>0\mid\mathrm{simple}) \geq \frac{\mathbb E[X_F\mid\mathrm{simple}]^2} {\mathbb E[X_F^2\mid\mathrm{simple}]} \geq\frac{c_1^2}{C_1}>0. $$
Conditioning gives exactly the required uniform simple-graph ensemble. $\square$

6. Consequences for bounded forts and for $q$

Theorem 6.1: unicyclic certificate. If the unicyclic test accepts and $0<\delta<1/2$, then
$$ q<1. $$

Proof. Fix a witnessing pattern $F$ with $K$ vertices. By Lemma 5.1, its occurrence probability is at least $\rho_F>0$ for all sufficiently large admissible sizes.

When it occurs, choose one embedding by a fixed deterministic rule depending only on the graph. Its image is a fort of size $K$. Conditional on the graph, its activation probability is at least $\theta_K$ from Section 2. Therefore
$$ p_n\leq1-\rho_F\theta_K $$
for all sufficiently large admissible $n$, and
$$ q\leq1-\rho_F\theta_K<1. $$
$\square$

The following converse concerns bounded forts, rather than the consensus probability.

Theorem 6.2: absence of bounded forts. If both the tree and unicyclic tests reject, then for every fixed positive integer $K$,
$$ \Pr\bigl(G_n\text{ has a fort of size at most }K\bigr) =O(1/n) $$
along admissible sizes.

Proof. A connected component of the induced graph on a fort is itself a fort: all its neighbors within the original fort belong to the same component.

Thus a fort of size at most $K$ supplies a connected compatible typed fort pattern $F$ on at most $K$ vertices. Because both tests reject, this pattern is neither a tree nor unicyclic. A connected graph has at least $|V(F)|-1$ edges, so necessarily
$$ |E(F)|\geq|V(F)|+1. $$

Section 5.2 gives $\mathbb E X_F=O(1/n)$ in the configuration model. Conditioning on simplicity increases this upper bound by at most the constant factor $1/s_0$.

There are only finitely many typed simple graphs on at most $K$ vertices. A union bound over them proves the assertion. $\square$

The two tests therefore decide, in polynomial time, the following structural property:

There exists a fixed $K$ for which the probability of a fort of size at most $K$ stays bounded away from zero.

A tree witness establishes this property in every admissible graph; a unicyclic witness establishes it by Lemma 5.1. If both tests reject, Theorem 6.2 excludes it.

For example, with one role and degree $k\geq5$, every fort pattern must have minimum degree at least three. A tree has average degree less than two, and a connected unicyclic graph has average degree exactly two. Neither can be a fort pattern. Hence all bounded forts disappear with high probability in this regime.

This does not establish $q=1$. Forts whose sizes grow with $n$ remain uncontrolled.

7. Explicit bounds for cubic and quartic graphs

For one role with $a_{11}=k$, the ensemble is uniform on labeled simple $k$-regular graphs.

We use the fixed-degree short-cycle theorem: for fixed $k\geq3$, the triangle count $X_n$ converges in distribution to a Poisson variable of mean
$$ \lambda_k=\frac{(k-1)^3}{6} $$
along parity-compatible sizes. This is a specialization of [McKay–Wormald–Wysocka, Theorem 1](https://users.monash.edu/~nwormald/papers/girth.pdf). Its condition on the largest considered cycle length holds because that length and $k$ are fixed.

We also need independence of triangle activation events. With probability $1-O(1/n)$, distinct triangles have disjoint closed neighborhoods. Indeed, two distinct triangles at distance at most two give a connected union pattern containing at least two independent cycles, so
$$ |E(H)|\geq|V(H)|+1. $$
That pattern has at most seven vertices. The pattern-count bound and conditioning argument from Section 5 give occurrence probability $O(1/n)$; only finitely many union patterns are possible.

Fix a triangle $S$. Its boundary has size
$$ b\leq3(k-2). $$
Let $T_v$ denote the first ring time at $v$. These times are independent identically distributed continuous random variables. Thus
$$ \Pr\!\left( \max_{v\in S}T_v< \min_{w\in\partial_GS}T_w \right) = \frac1{\binom{3+b}{3}} \geq \frac1{\binom{3(k-1)}3}. $$

7.1 Cubic graphs

When $k=3$, two internal blue neighbors give a strict majority. Therefore a blue triangle is permanent regardless of its private signals.

Require the first triangle vertex selected to have a blue private signal, and require all three triangle first rings to precede every boundary first ring. Before any boundary vertex rings, the first triangle update is blue. Every subsequently selected triangle vertex sees a blue triangle neighbor and no red neighbor, so it announces blue. Repeated triangle updates also preserve blue.

This activates the triangle with probability at least
$$ \theta_3=\frac{\beta}{\binom63}=\frac{\beta}{20}. $$

7.2 Quartic graphs

When $k=4$, a blue triangle can face a tie against its two external neighbors. Require all three triangle private signals to be blue, as well as the same first-ring ordering. The triangle becomes blue and remains blue by Lemma 2.1.

Its activation probability is at least
$$ \theta_4=\frac{\beta^3}{\binom93}=\frac{\beta^3}{84}. $$

7.3 Averaging the independent obstructions

On the event that triangle neighborhoods are disjoint, their activation events are independent. Hence
$$ p_n\leq \mathbb E[(1-\theta_k)^{X_n}]+O(1/n). $$
Distributional convergence implies convergence of this expectation because the function is bounded. For a Poisson variable $X$ of mean $\lambda$,
$$ \mathbb E[(1-\theta)^X]=e^{-\lambda\theta}. $$

Taking the admissible-size limsup gives
$$ q\leq e^{-\lambda_k\theta_k}. $$
Substituting the constants proves
$$ \boxed{q\leq e^{-\beta/15}\quad(k=3)} $$
and
$$ \boxed{q\leq e^{-3\beta^3/56}\quad(k=4)}. $$

Both are strictly below one for every allowed $\delta<1/2$. Neither argument asserts that $p_n$ has a limit.

8. A higher-degree case missed by the tree certificate

Consider two roles, core and leaf, with
$$ \mu_C=\mu_L=\frac12, \qquad a_{CC}=4,\quad a_{CL}=a_{LC}=1,\quad a_{LL}=0. $$
Every core vertex has degree five and one pendant leaf. The core is a uniform simple $4$-regular graph on $n/2$ vertices: each possible core graph has the same number of perfect matchings assigning the labeled leaves.

There is no compatible tree fort pattern. If such a pattern contained a core vertex, that vertex would need at least three internal neighbors and could have at most one leaf neighbor. Thus every core vertex would have at least two core neighbors in the pattern. The induced graph on core vertices would be a nonempty finite forest of minimum degree at least two, which is impossible. A pattern containing only leaves also cannot meet their threshold, since leaf-to-leaf edges are forbidden.

However, a core triangle together with its three pendant leaves is a compatible unicyclic fort. Each core vertex has three internal neighbors, a strict majority of its five neighbors; each leaf has its sole neighbor inside the fort.

An explicit activation event gives a sharper conclusion. Require that:

  • all three core first rings precede all three leaf first rings;
  • all three leaf first rings precede every external boundary first ring;
  • the first core vertex to ring has a blue private signal.

The first core vertex becomes blue; the other two copy blue through the triangle. The leaves subsequently copy their blue core neighbors. Before the boundary rings, all announcements in the fort are blue, and strict internal majorities make them permanent.

There are at most six external boundary vertices. Uniform first-ring ordering gives activation probability at least
$$ \theta= \beta\,\frac{3!\,3!\,6!}{12!} =\frac{\beta}{18480}. $$
If the actual boundary is smaller, the ordering probability is larger.

Core triangles have asymptotic Poisson mean $27/6=9/2$, and their fort neighborhoods are pairwise disjoint with probability tending to one, by the same pattern-count argument as in Section 7. Therefore
$$ \boxed{q\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{3\beta}{12320}\right)<1.} $$

Thus the unicyclic certificate applies beyond maximum degree four, including inputs on which the tree certificate rejects.

9. What remains open

The polynomial-time tests above recognize structural obstructions; they do not approximate $q$ on all promised inputs.

In particular:

  • A unicyclic witness supplies an upper bound strictly below one, but no matching lower bound.
  • Rejecting both tests excludes only forts of fixed bounded size.
  • No argument here controls growing forts or the general admissible-size limsup well enough to obtain an additive approximation algorithm.
  • No reduction establishes an additive gap sufficient for $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

Consequently, the requested general complexity classification remains unresolved.
