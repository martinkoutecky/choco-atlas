The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(p\) parties, finite type sets \(T_r\), rational type masses \(\mu_{r,t}\) summing to \(1\) within each party, strict rankings of other-party types, and a directed tree on the parties, decide whether a stable mass matching exists and output one if it does. A matching is an array \(x_{\mathbf t}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\mathbf t:t_r=t}x_{\mathbf t}=\mu_{r,t}\); stability excludes any positive-mass regrouping in which every participant weakly prefers each proposed partner type to the incumbent and each strictly prefers at least one. What is the complexity of this search problem in \(p\), \(\tau=\sum_r|T_r|\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given \(p\ge2\) parties with nonempty, disjoint type sets \(T_r\), each party's society is specified by its support: its positive-mass types and their binary rational masses \(\mu_{r,t}\), with \(\sum_{t\in T_r}\mu_{r,t}=1\). Let \(\tau=\sum_r|T_r|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\in T_r\) has a complete strict ranking of \(T_s\) for every \(s\ne r\); write \(\succeq_{r,t}\) for its weak extension. Copies of the same type are indifferent. The input also includes an orientation of a tree on the parties; stability compares partners from every other party, irrespective of tree edges.
A mass matching is a nonnegative array \(x\) on \(\prod_rT_r\) satisfying \(\sum_{\mathbf a:a_r=t}x_{\mathbf a}=\mu_{r,t}\).
A positive-mass block consists of a proposed tuple \(\mathbf t\) and incumbent tuples \(\mathbf a^{(r)}\) with \(x_{\mathbf a^{(r)}}>0\) and \(a^{(r)}_r=t_r\), such that \(t_s\succeq_{r,t_r}a^{(r)}_s\) for every \(r\ne s\), and for every \(r\) at least one of these comparisons is strict. Participants may leave their incumbent families; abandoned relatives need not consent. A matching is stable if no such block exists.
The computational problem is to decide whether a stable mass matching exists and, if so, output one as a list of positive binary rational entries, with omitted entries zero.

Is this decision-and-construction problem solvable in time polynomial in \(p,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For the discrete version with \(n\) individuals per party and strict individual rankings, every directed-tree elemental algorithm produces a stable matching in \(O(pn^2)\) time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02972).
For fractional pair matchings, ordinally stable matchings always exist, including with ties; this concerns pairwise stability rather than the blocking families defined here. [Source](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf). No result settling exactly the mass problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required at least two parties and nonempty type supports, retaining the discrete domain.
  • Interpreted rankings as complete strict lists for each other party separately; copies of a type are indifferent.
  • Made positive-mass regrouping precise through incumbent tuples of positive mass, with componentwise weak improvement and a strict improvement for every participant.
  • Allowed participants to abandon incumbent relatives without their consent, as in the discrete blocking-family definition.
  • Retained the directed tree as input without imposing edge-only stability or additional feasibility constraints; the discrete tree specifies an algorithm rather than admissible families.
  • Specified sparse binary rational output so the implicit array's potentially exponential size does not itself force exponential output.

Partial results

Every valid instance has a stable mass matching. I do not settle the complexity of construction for unrestricted $p,\tau,L$.

The additional construction results proved below are:

  • An exact algorithm with running time

$$ 2^{O((\tau-p+1)^2)}\operatorname{poly}(p,\tau,L). $$
Thus construction is polynomial when $\tau-p$ is bounded.

  • A polynomial algorithm for arbitrary masses and arbitrarily many parties when all evaluators share the same ranking of each target party.
  • A polynomial procedure that compresses any explicitly supplied stable rational matching to at most $\tau-p+1$ positive entries.

I also give an explicit obstruction to extending the discrete tree algorithm by breaking ties between copies.

1. Existence and the relevant dimension

Put
$$ d=\tau-p,\qquad E=\prod_{r=1}^p T_r. $$
Thus $d$ counts the types beyond one reference type per party.

Let $A$ be the type–tuple incidence matrix:
$$ A_{(r,t),\mathbf a}= \begin{cases} 1,&a_r=t,\\ 0,&a_r\ne t. \end{cases} $$
A mass matching satisfies $Ax=\mu$ and $x\ge0$.

1.1 Existence of a stable matching

We use the following capacitated form of Scarf’s lemma. If $Q$ is a finite nonnegative matrix with no zero column, $b$ is strictly positive, and every row strictly orders its incident columns, then there is an extreme point of
$$ \{y\ge0:Qy\le b\} $$
that dominates every column. Domination of a column $e$ means that some incident row $i$ is saturated and ranks every positive incident column at least as highly as $e$. This is the statement of [Lemma 2.1 in *Core-Stable Kidney Exchange via Altruistic Donors*](https://www.ac.tuwien.ac.at/comsoc2025/comsoc2025-papers/64.pdf).

For $t\in T_r$, write $\operatorname{rank}_{r,t,s}(u)$ for the position of $u$ in its ranking of $T_s$, with position $1$ best. On tuples containing $t$, define
$$ U_{r,t}(\mathbf a) = -\sum_{s\ne r}\operatorname{rank}_{r,t,s}(a_s). $$
Order these tuples by decreasing $U_{r,t}$, breaking equal scores by a fixed strict order.

If $\mathbf t$ weakly improves every partner coordinate over $\mathbf a$ for type $t_r$, and strictly improves at least one, then
$$ U_{r,t_r}(\mathbf t)>U_{r,t_r}(\mathbf a). $$
Indeed, every rank weakly decreases and at least one strictly decreases.

Apply Scarf’s lemma to
$$ P=\{y\ge0:Ay\le\mu\}. $$
Its hypotheses hold: every column has exactly $p$ nonzero entries, all listed masses are positive, and the incident orders are strict. Moreover, $P$ contains zero and is bounded, since
$$ y_{\mathbf a}\le\min_r\mu_{r,a_r}\le1. $$
Let $y$ be the dominating extreme point supplied by the lemma.

Set
$$ q=\sum_{\mathbf a\in E}y_{\mathbf a}. $$
For every party $r$,
$$ \sum_{t\in T_r}(Ay)_{r,t}=q\le1. $$
If $q<1$, every party has an unsaturated type. Choosing one such type from each party produces a tuple with no saturated incident row, contradicting domination. Consequently $q=1$.

Within each party, the nonnegative marginal deficits now sum to zero:
$$ \sum_{t\in T_r}\bigl(\mu_{r,t}-(Ay)_{r,t}\bigr)=1-q=0. $$
Hence $Ay=\mu$.

Suppose a tuple $\mathbf t$ blocked $y$, with positive incumbent witnesses $\mathbf a^{(r)}$. For every $r$,
$$ U_{r,t_r}(\mathbf t)>U_{r,t_r}(\mathbf a^{(r)}). $$
Thus every row incident to $\mathbf t$ has a positive incumbent column ranked strictly below $\mathbf t$. No incident row dominates $\mathbf t$, a contradiction.

Therefore $y$ is stable under exactly the blocking definition in the question. In particular, the existence decision always answers yes on valid inputs.

1.2 A reduced marginal matrix

Choose a reference type $b_r\in T_r$ for each party. Define a matrix $B$ with $d+1$ rows:

  • Row $0$ is the all-ones row.
  • For each nonreference type $t\in T_r\setminus\{b_r\}$, its row indicates whether $a_r=t$.

Define the corresponding right-hand side by
$$ \beta_0=1,\qquad \beta_{r,t}=\mu_{r,t}. $$
For a nonnegative array $x$,
$$ Ax=\mu\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Bx=\beta. $$
To check the reverse implication, the reference-type marginal in party $r$ is
$$ \sum_{\mathbf a:a_r=b_r}x_{\mathbf a} = 1-\sum_{t\in T_r\setminus\{b_r\}}\mu_{r,t} = \mu_{r,b_r}. $$

The matrices $A$ and $B$ have the same row span. Each nonreference row of $A$ is a row of $B$, while
$$ A_{(r,b_r),\cdot} = B_{0,\cdot} - \sum_{t\in T_r\setminus\{b_r\}}B_{(r,t),\cdot}. $$
Conversely, $B_{0,\cdot}$ is the sum of the rows of $A$ belonging to any fixed party.

Finally, $B$ has rank $d+1$. The reference tuple $\mathbf b$ gives column
$$ (1,0,\ldots,0)^{\mathsf T}. $$
Replacing its coordinate $r$ by a nonreference type $t$ changes that column by the corresponding standard basis vector in the remaining $d$ coordinates. These differences, together with the reference column, span all $d+1$ coordinates. Therefore
$$ \operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(B)=d+1=\tau-p+1. $$

2. Stable supports can be compressed efficiently

Two facts make sparsification useful here.

First, stability is preserved when positive entries are removed, provided feasibility is retained. More precisely, if $x$ is stable and $z$ is feasible with
$$ \operatorname{supp}(z)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(x), $$
then $z$ is stable. Any block of $z$ would use incumbent tuples positive in $x$, and would therefore also block $x$.

Second, dependent support columns allow such a removal.

Let $S=\operatorname{supp}(x)$ for a feasible rational matching. If the columns of $B_S$ are dependent, choose a nonzero rational vector $h$, supported on $S$, such that
$$ Bh=0. $$
The all-ones row gives
$$ \sum_{\mathbf a\in S}h_{\mathbf a}=0, $$
so $h$ has both positive and negative entries. Set
$$ \varepsilon = \min_{\mathbf a:h_{\mathbf a}>0} \frac{x_{\mathbf a}}{h_{\mathbf a}}, \qquad x'=x-\varepsilon h. $$
Then $x'\ge0$, $Bx'=\beta$, and at least one positive entry becomes zero. No new positive entry appears outside $S$.

Starting from a stable matching, this operation preserves stability. Repeating it until the support columns are independent gives
$$ |\operatorname{supp}(x')|\le d+1. $$

2.1 Polynomial running time for an explicit matching

The preceding compression can be implemented with polynomial bit complexity.

At each step, choose a minimal dependent set of columns. It has at most $d+2$ columns. If its rank is $q$, select $q$ independent rows on those columns. Signed $q\times q$ minors give a nonzero integer kernel vector $h$. The selected rows span the row space on this column set, so this vector also satisfies $Bh=0$.

Because the matrix entries are binary, Hadamard’s inequality bounds every such minor by
$$ q^{q/2}. $$
Thus each entry of $h$ has bit length
$$ O((d+1)\log(d+2)). $$

Suppose the current entries have common denominator $D_x$, and an index $\mathbf i$ attains the minimum defining $\varepsilon$. Writing $x_{\mathbf a}=n_{\mathbf a}/D_x$, the update is
$$ x'_{\mathbf a} = \frac{n_{\mathbf a}h_{\mathbf i}-n_{\mathbf i}h_{\mathbf a}} {D_xh_{\mathbf i}}. $$
A common denominator therefore gains only
$$ O((d+1)\log(d+2)) $$
bits per step. There are at most as many steps as initially positive entries. Kernel computations and updates consequently take polynomial time in the explicit matching’s encoding length and the original input length.

This compression does not require an algorithm for verifying stability; it preserves stability whenever the supplied matching has it.

2.2 Final encoding length

Let the final independent support have size $k\le d+1$. Choose $k$ rows forming a nonsingular square matrix $C$ on these support columns. Its weights $w$ satisfy
$$ Cw=\beta_R. $$
Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the input masses. Its bit length is $O(L)$, and $D\beta_R$ is integral.

Cramer’s rule gives denominators dividing
$$ D|\det C|. $$
Hadamard’s inequality gives
$$ |\det C|\le k^{k/2}. $$
Since every feasible weight lies in $[0,1]$, each output weight has bit length
$$ O\bigl(L+(d+1)\log(d+2)\bigr). $$
Together with at most $d+1$ tuple labels, this is a polynomial-size rational representation.

Applying this argument to the stable matching from Section 1 proves that such a representation always exists.

3. An exact algorithm parameterized by $\tau-p$

The sparse-support bound gives a finite construction algorithm whose running time depends exponentially on $d=\tau-p$, rather than on the magnitudes of denominators.

3.1 Bounding the number of tuples

For every integer $m\ge1$,
$$ m\le2^{m-1}. $$
For example, this follows from the first two terms of the binomial expansion of $2^{m-1}$.

Therefore, writing $N=|E|$,
$$ N=\prod_r|T_r| \le \prod_r2^{|T_r|-1} = 2^d. $$

3.2 Enumerating candidate matchings

The algorithm performs the following exhaustive search.

  • Enumerate each nonempty subset $S\subseteq E$ with

$$ |S|\le d+1. $$

  • Put $k=|S|$. Enumerate every selection $R$ of $k$ rows of $B$.
  • If $C=B_{R,S}$ is nonsingular, solve exactly

$$ Cw=\beta_R. $$

  • Check

$$ w\ge0,\qquad B_Sw=\beta. $$
If either condition fails, discard this candidate.

  • Delete zero weights and check stability of the resulting matching. If it is stable, output its positive entries.

All linear algebra uses binary rational arithmetic.

The stability check is also explicit. For each proposed tuple $\mathbf t\in E$ and each party $r$, search the positive support for an incumbent $\mathbf a$ satisfying
$$ a_r=t_r, $$
$$ t_s\succeq_{r,t_r}a_s\quad\text{for every }s\ne r, $$
and at least one strict comparison.

The tuple blocks precisely when such an incumbent exists for every party. The witnesses can be searched for independently because the definition imposes no compatibility or distinctness requirement on them. With at most $k$ positive entries, this check takes
$$ O(Np^2k) $$
ranking comparisons.

3.3 Correctness

Every accepted candidate satisfies all prescribed marginals by the equivalence $Bx=\beta\Longleftrightarrow Ax=\mu$. The explicit check verifies the exact blocking definition, so every output is stable.

For termination with an output, Section 2 supplies a stable matching whose positive support $S^*$ has at most $d+1$ independent columns of $B$. Some selection of $|S^*|$ rows forms a nonsingular square matrix on these columns.

When the algorithm reaches this support and row selection, the square system uniquely determines the weights of that stable matching. It passes both feasibility and stability checks. Hence the algorithm succeeds.

3.4 Running time

There are at most
$$ (d+1)N^{d+1} $$
candidate supports and at most $2^{d+1}$ row selections for each support.

For each candidate, rational linear algebra takes polynomial time in $d$ and $L$. Stability checking contributes at most $N$ times a polynomial in $p$ and $d$. Since $N\le2^d$, the total running time is
$$ 2^{O((d+1)^2)}\operatorname{poly}(p,\tau,L). $$

Thus the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in $\tau-p$. This does not give a polynomial algorithm for unrestricted $\tau-p$.

4. Polynomial construction under shared target-party rankings

Here is a separate polynomial case with no restriction on $d$ or on the denominators of the masses.

Hypothesis. For each party $s$, every type outside $T_s$ gives the same strict ranking of $T_s$. Write that common order as
$$ u_{s,1}\succ u_{s,2}\succ\cdots\succ u_{s,m_s}. $$

4.1 Construction

Define cumulative masses
$$ c_{s,0}=0,\qquad c_{s,j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^j\mu_{s,u_{s,\ell}}, $$
and intervals
$$ I_{s,j}=[c_{s,j-1},c_{s,j}). $$
For each party, these intervals partition $[0,1)$.

Collect all their endpoints and sort the distinct values:
$$ 0=v_0<v_1<\cdots<v_K=1. $$
On each interval
$$ J_i=[v_{i-1},v_i), $$
every party has a unique type whose interval contains $J_i$. Let $\mathbf a^i$ be the tuple of these types and assign mass
$$ x_{\mathbf a^i}=v_i-v_{i-1}. $$
If a tuple is emitted more than once, combine its masses.

For each type, the total emitted mass containing it is the length of its interval, which is its prescribed mass. The matching is therefore feasible.

There are at most
$$ 1+\sum_s(m_s-1)=d+1 $$
emitted intervals. Computing and sorting rational endpoints, then forming the tuples, takes polynomial time in $p,\tau,L$.

4.2 Stability proof

Suppose $\mathbf t$ blocked the constructed matching. Let $h_s$ be the right endpoint of the interval belonging to $t_s$.

For every party $r$, select its positive incumbent witness $\mathbf a^{(r)}$. That witness was emitted on some positive-length interval. Choose a point $z_r$ in the interior of such an interval. Then
$$ z_r\in I_{r,t_r}, \qquad z_r\in I_{s,a^{(r)}_s}\quad\text{for every }s, $$
and in particular
$$ z_r<h_r. $$

Participant $t_r$ must strictly improve some partner coordinate $s$. By the shared-ranking hypothesis, $t_s$ precedes $a^{(r)}_s$ in party $s$’s common order. Consequently the entire interval of $t_s$ lies before the interval of $a^{(r)}_s$, and
$$ h_s \le \inf I_{s,a^{(r)}_s} \le z_r <h_r. $$

Thus every party $r$ has another party $s$ with $h_s<h_r$. Choose a party minimizing $h_r$. The required strict inequality is impossible for that party.

Therefore no block exists, and the construction is stable.

5. Breaking copy ties does not transfer discrete stability

The following instance shows that even a coherent, uniform way of breaking copy ties can make the discrete tree algorithm project to an unstable mass matching.

Take
$$ T_A=\{a_1,a_2\},\qquad T_B=\{b\},\qquad T_C=\{c_1,c_2\}. $$
Give each $A$- and $C$-type mass $1/2$, and give $b$ mass $1$. Use the party tree $A-B-C$, with any orientation.

The nonsingleton rankings are
$$ a_1:c_2\succ c_1,\qquad a_2:c_1\succ c_2, $$
$$ c_1:a_2\succ a_1,\qquad c_2:a_1\succ a_2, $$
and
$$ b:a_1\succ a_2,\qquad b:c_1\succ c_2. $$

Represent $b$ by two individuals $b_1,b_2$. Both inherit $b$’s rankings. Every $A$- and $C$-individual breaks its indifference between these copies as
$$ b_1\succ b_2. $$
This is a strict individual-ranking instance with two individuals per party.

On edge $AB$, the unique stable bijection is
$$ a_1-b_1,\qquad a_2-b_2. $$
Indeed, the other bijection is blocked by $(a_1,b_1)$.

On edge $BC$, the unique stable bijection is
$$ b_1-c_1,\qquad b_2-c_2, $$
because the other bijection is blocked by $(b_1,c_1)$.

Therefore every orientation of the tree gives the same discrete families. Projecting the copies back to types produces
$$ x_{(a_1,b,c_1)}=\frac12,\qquad x_{(a_2,b,c_2)}=\frac12. $$

This mass matching is blocked by
$$ (a_1,b,c_2). $$
Use incumbent $(a_1,b,c_1)$ for $a_1$, and incumbent $(a_2,b,c_2)$ for both $b$ and $c_2$:

  • $a_1$ strictly improves its $C$-partner and retains the same $B$-type.
  • $b$ strictly improves its $A$-partner and retains $c_2$.
  • $c_2$ strictly improves its $A$-partner and retains the same $B$-type.

All comparisons required by the mass definition hold.

Hence strict stability after this copy refinement does not imply stability after projection. The example does not exclude a more sophisticated construction using copies; it identifies a gap that such a construction must overcome.

A hardness result must concern finding a stable matching: nonexistence cannot encode a hard decision problem here, because every valid instance admits a stable matching.
