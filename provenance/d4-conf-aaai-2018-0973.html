The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a qualifying scoring vector \(\alpha\), a target \(p\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) rankings, compute a minimum-mass bribery plan \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\) that moves mass from each initial ranking \(t\) to a new ranking \(\pi\ne t\). Require \(\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}\le\mu_t\) for each \(t\), and require \(p\) to be a co-winner under the resulting distribution.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{p,c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), with \(m\ge1\), and let rankings be strict total orders. Use positional scoring with a nonnegative integer vector \(\alpha_0\le\cdots\le\alpha_m\), where \(0<\alpha_m\le(m+1)^d\) for a fixed constant \(d\). A qualifying vector satisfies either \(\alpha_m\le A\) or \(\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{j=0}^m\alpha_j\le(1-\epsilon)\alpha_m\), for fixed constants \(A>0\) and \(0<\epsilon<1\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) rankings with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A bribery plan splits mass arbitrarily: \(x_{t,\pi}\ge0\) for \(t\in T\) and any ranking \(\pi\ne t\), with \(\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}\le\mu_t\). Unmoved mass retains its ranking; moved mass adopts \(\pi\), at unit cost per unit mass. Plans are represented by their nonzero rational transfers.
Scores are integrated against the resulting distribution, and feasibility requires \(p\) to have score at least that of every other candidate; ties count as wins. Let \(K\) be the minimum feasible cost \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}\).

Does a randomized algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) always produce a feasible plan of cost at most \(K+\widetilde O(\sqrt K)\), with failure probability at most \(\exp(-\Omega(L))\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard? Here \(\widetilde O\) hides only polylogarithmic factors in \(2+m+\tau+L\), with constants allowed to depend on \(A,\epsilon,d\).

For discrete unit-cost bribery under these scoring conditions, a polynomial-time randomized algorithm uses at most \(k+\widetilde O(\sqrt k)\) voters, where \(k\) is optimal, with exponentially small failure probability. [Source](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/11476/11335).
An extension treats discrete weighted and priced voters; the search identified no result establishing or excluding the stated guarantee for this exact divisible-mass problem. [Source](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/11538).

  • Interpreted 'qualifying' as the union of constant and non-concentrated scoring families, with their bounds fixed independently of the instance.
  • Specified nonnegative integer scores and a fixed polynomial bound on the maximum score, following the score bounds and unit-gap assumptions used in the discrete analysis.
  • Interpreted 'distribution' as total mass one; no additional population-size parameter is supplied.
  • Specified strict complete rankings, arbitrary replacement rankings, unit cost per moved mass, and co-winner feasibility.
  • Used a sparse representation by nonzero rational transfers to avoid requiring an explicitly exponential array of zero entries.
  • Replaced the finite population input-size quantity in the approximation and failure bounds by the continuous input parameters m, τ, and L.

Exact polynomial-time solvability

The requested guarantee holds. In fact, a deterministic algorithm computes a feasible rational plan of cost exactly \(K\) in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time. The proof applies to every scoring vector in the question, without requiring either qualifying condition.

The essential point is that replacement mass can realize a fractional assignment of candidates to positions exactly.

1. A polynomial-size linear program

Write \(n=m+1\), and index candidates by \(i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\), with \(c_0=p\). Index positions by \(j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\), so that position \(j\) awards score \(\alpha_j\).

For a ranking \(t\), let \(s_t(i)\) denote the score it awards candidate \(c_i\). The initial score is

\(S_i=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s_t(i).\)

Introduce variables:

  • \(y_t\): mass removed from source ranking \(t\);
  • \(b\): total removed mass;
  • \(B_{i,j}\): replacement mass placing candidate \(c_i\) in position \(j\).

Impose the following linear constraints:

\(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\qquad b=\sum_{t\in T}y_t,\qquad B_{i,j}\ge0.\)

\(\sum_{j=0}^{m}B_{i,j}=b\quad(i=0,\ldots,m).\)

\(\sum_{i=0}^{m}B_{i,j}=b\quad(j=0,\ldots,m).\)

Define the proposed final scores by the linear expressions

\(F_i=S_i-\sum_{t\in T}y_t s_t(i)+\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_j B_{i,j}.\)

Require

\(F_0\ge F_i\quad(i=1,\ldots,m),\)

and minimize \(b\). Denote the optimum by \(v\).

This LP is feasible: take \(y_t=\mu_t\), \(b=1\), and let \(B\) be the permutation matrix of any ranking placing \(p\) first. Then \(p\) receives \(\alpha_m\), and every opponent receives at most \(\alpha_m\).

Its feasible region is compact. Indeed, \(0\le b\le1\), and the nonnegative row-sum constraints imply \(0\le B_{i,j}\le1\). Thus the optimum is attained.

2. Every bribery plan gives an LP solution of the same cost

Consider any feasible plan \(x\). Set

\(y_t=\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi},\qquad b=\sum_{t\in T}y_t,\)

and

\(B_{i,j}=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}\mathbf{1}\{\pi\text{ places }c_i\text{ in position }j\}.\)

Every ranking places each candidate in exactly one position and assigns exactly one candidate to each position. Consequently, every row and every column of \(B\) sums to \(b\).

The expression \(F_i\) equals candidate \(c_i\)'s actual final score: it subtracts the score contributed by removed mass and adds the score contributed by replacement mass. Thus the winner constraints hold, and the objective \(b\) equals the plan's cost.

Therefore every feasible plan has cost at least \(v\). In particular,

\(v\le K.\)

We next construct a feasible rational plan of cost at most \(v\), proving equality.

3. Constructively decomposing replacement mass into rankings

Take a rational optimal LP solution \((y,b,B)\).

If \(b=0\), nonnegativity and the row-sum constraints give \(B=0\). The winner constraints then say that \(p\) already wins. The empty plan is optimal.

Suppose \(b>0\). We prove constructively that

\(B=\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\lambda_\ell P_{\pi_\ell},\qquad \lambda_\ell>0,\qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\lambda_\ell=b,\qquad q\le n^2,\)

where \(P_{\pi_\ell}\) is the candidate–position permutation matrix of a strict ranking \(\pi_\ell\).

Start with residual matrix \(R=B\). Its rows and columns have a common sum \(\rho=b\). Whenever \(\rho>0\), form the bipartite graph whose vertices are candidates and positions, with edge \((i,j)\) exactly when \(R_{i,j}>0\).

For any candidate subset \(U\), let \(N(U)\) be its neighboring positions. Since entries outside those neighboring columns vanish,

\(\rho|U|=\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{j\in N(U)}R_{i,j}\le\sum_{j\in N(U)}\sum_{i=0}^{m}R_{i,j}=\rho|N(U)|.\)

Dividing by \(\rho>0\) verifies Hall's condition. Hall's marriage theorem therefore gives a perfect matching, corresponding to a ranking \(\pi\).

Set

\(\lambda=\min\{R_{i,j}:(i,j)\text{ belongs to this matching}\}>0,\)

and replace \(R\) by \(R-\lambda P_\pi\). This preserves nonnegativity, reduces every row and column sum to \(\rho-\lambda\), and makes at least one previously positive entry zero. No zero entry becomes positive.

There are initially at most \(n^2\) positive entries, so this process terminates after at most \(n^2\) iterations. At termination the residual is zero. Summing the subtractions gives the claimed decomposition; tracking any row sum gives \(\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell=b\).

All operations preserve rationality.

4. Realizing the decomposition as legal transfers

Merge repeated rankings in the decomposition if necessary. Let \(\lambda_\pi\) be their combined weights, assigning \(\lambda_\pi=0\) to rankings absent from the decomposition. Thus

\(\sum_\pi\lambda_\pi=b,\qquad B=\sum_\pi\lambda_\pi P_\pi.\)

For every source \(t\) and every decomposed ranking \(\pi\ne t\), output the transfer

\(x_{t,\pi}=\dfrac{y_t\lambda_\pi}{b},\)

omitting zero transfers.

These transfers are nonnegative rational numbers. For each source,

\(\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}=\dfrac{y_t}{b}(b-\lambda_t)\le y_t\le\mu_t.\)

Thus the source-capacity constraints hold, and every output transfer changes the ranking.

To verify the resulting scores, temporarily consider assigning \(y_t\lambda_\pi/b\) from source \(t\) to every decomposed ranking, including \(\pi=t\). The assignment back to \(t\) simply retains that mass, so deleting this self-assignment from the transfer list leaves the resulting distribution unchanged.

Under this bookkeeping, source \(t\) contributes

\((\mu_t-y_t)s_t(i)+\sum_\pi\dfrac{y_t\lambda_\pi}{b}s_\pi(i)\)

to candidate \(c_i\)'s score. Summing over sources and using \(\sum_t y_t=b\) gives

\(S_i-\sum_t y_t s_t(i)+\sum_\pi\lambda_\pi s_\pi(i) =S_i-\sum_t y_t s_t(i)+\sum_j\alpha_j B_{i,j}=F_i.\)

Hence the constructed plan makes \(p\) a co-winner.

Its cost satisfies

\(\operatorname{cost}(x)=\sum_t\sum_{\pi\ne t}x_{t,\pi}\le\sum_t y_t=b=v.\)

Section 2 showed that every feasible plan has cost at least \(v\). Applying that bound to this constructed plan proves

\(\operatorname{cost}(x)=v=K.\)

This also proves that a rational optimum exists for the original problem.

5. Running time and encoding length

The LP has \(\tau+n^2+1\) variables and \(O(\tau+n^2)\) constraints, including nonnegativity constraints.

Its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The scores \(s_t(i)\) have \(O(1+d\log n)\) bits. Each \(S_i\) is a sum of the input rational masses multiplied by these integers, so its exact rational encoding also has polynomial length.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: an explicitly encoded rational LP can be optimized exactly in time polynomial in its dimensions and encoding length, with a rational optimal solution of polynomial encoding length when its optimum is attained. The feasibility, boundedness, and attainment hypotheses were verified above.

The decomposition requires at most \(n^2\) perfect-matchings computations. Using an elementary \(O(n^3)\) bipartite matching algorithm, it takes \(O(n^5)\) combinatorial operations.

Exact arithmetic during decomposition also remains polynomial in bit complexity. To see this, choose a common denominator \(Q\) for the entries of the returned rational matrix \(B\). Its bit length is polynomial because the total encoding length of \(B\) is polynomial. Every residual entry and every extracted weight remains an integer multiple of \(1/Q\), between \(0\) and \(1\). Thus repeated subtraction cannot cause uncontrolled denominator growth.

There are at most

\(\tau q\le\tau(m+1)^2\)

nonzero transfers. Each has polynomial encoding length because it is obtained through the rational operations \(y_t\lambda_\pi/b\). Writing the rankings and checking which transfers are self-assignments takes at most \(O(\tau n^3)\) elementary operations.

Consequently, the complete algorithm, including exact optimization and explicit output, runs in

\(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

6. Answer to the approximation question

The algorithm is deterministic and always returns cost exactly \(K\). Its additive error and failure probability are both zero, so it satisfies the requested \(K+\widetilde O(\sqrt K)\) guarantee.

The cited discrete algorithm also uses a Birkhoff decomposition, but samples rankings to assign indivisible voters. Here the decomposition weights themselves are implementable as divisible mass, which makes exact realization possible. See [Keller, Hassidim, and Hazon, Theorem 7 and Algorithm 1](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/11476/11335).
