The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many daycare centers \(D\) with mass capacities \(q_d\), finitely many complete family types \(F\), rational type masses \(\mu_f\), joint preferences over daycare tuples, child acceptability, daycare priority types, and an order \(\pi\) over sibling types, run Mass-ESDA: output either a mass assignment \(x_{f,a}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_a x_{f,a}=\mu_f\), \(\sum_{f,a}|\{i:a_i=d\}|x_{f,a}\le q_d\), individual rationality, and absence of any positive mass-blocking move, or output \(\mathrm{Unsuccess}\) with its rejection-cycle or order-history certificate.

Individual rationality is family-based. Let \(K=\max_f k_f\), let \(B\) be the total number of listed tuples, and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Copies of the same slot type tie: incumbent mass precedes incoming mass, and incumbent cohorts at better family tuples precede those at worse tuples.
A positive mass-blocking move chooses \(f,a,b\) and \(0<\varepsilon\leq x_{f,a}\) with \(b\succ_f a\). Keep all sibling types at their outside options, and process them in the supplied permutation \(\pi\), recording attempted permutations.

At a tuple, tentatively insert the largest amount for which every sibling's offered mass is retained by daycare choice; discard unsuccessful tentative proposals. Repeat at the same tuple until no positive insertion is possible, then advance; residual mass eventually stays outside.

1. Scope and interpretation

I prove finite termination and stability whenever Mass-ESDA succeeds. I also prove a new acceleration result: one permutation attempt can be evaluated in polynomial time under the type-mass scheduling convention specified below. This gives polynomial-time terminal-outcome computation when the number of sibling types is fixed.

The general complexity classification remains open: I do not prove polynomial-time computability or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness when the number of sibling types is unrestricted.

For the acceleration result, I make the singleton-event convention explicit:

Pending singleton mass is aggregated by its type and next tuple. An event processes all mass currently pending at the smallest such pair in the prescribed type-index and tuple-rank order. Newly rejected mass becomes pending for subsequent events.

This is the type-mass interpretation of mass deferred acceptance. If separate cohorts at the same pair are instead scheduled individually, the acceleration theorem below is conditional on the aggregation convention. Its single-pending-cohort case requires no such convention.

I distinguish two output representations:

  • An expanded history lists every proposal and rejection event.
  • A compressed history may describe repeated event cycles together with their binary-encoded repetition counts.

These representations have different output-size bounds.

The discrete termination and stability guarantee is stated in Theorem 2 of [Sun, Yokoyama and Yokoo](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/0453.pdf). The mass proofs below are independent of that theorem.

2. Exact insertion and blocking inequalities

Write $s=(f,i)$ for a slot type. For an acceptable slot $s$ at real daycare $d$, define
$$ H_{d,s}(x) = \sum_{g,c} \sum_{\substack{j:c_j=d\\(g,j)\succeq_d s}} x_{g,c}. $$
Here $\succeq_d$ is the reflexive closure of daycare priority. Thus $H_{d,s}(x)$ is the incumbent mass in the priority prefix through $s$.

For a tuple $c$ of type $f$, define
$$ N_{d,s}(c) = \bigl|\{j:c_j=d,\ (f,j)\succeq_d s\}\bigr|. $$
This counts children of one cohort, rather than all cohorts of type $f$.

2.1. Blocking criterion

Fix $x_{f,a}>0$ and $b\succ_f a$. If an offered real slot is daycare-unacceptable, the move cannot block.

Otherwise, a move of size $0<\varepsilon\leq x_{f,a}$ blocks exactly when, for every offered real slot $s=(f,i)$ with $b_i=d$,
$$ H_{d,s}(x) + \bigl(N_{d,s}(b)-N_{d,s}(a)\bigr)\varepsilon \leq q_d. $$

Indeed, removing the departing cohort subtracts exactly $N_{d,s}(a)\varepsilon$ from the incumbent prefix. Offering its new tuple adds $N_{d,s}(b)\varepsilon$ to the prefix that must fit for its entire slot-$s$ offer to survive. Remaining incumbent copies of $s$ precede incoming copies. Consequently, greedy choice retains that offer fully precisely when the displayed inequality holds.

This calculation removes only the departing cohort’s children, including seats transferred between its siblings.

Set
$$ S_{d,s}=q_d-H_{d,s}(x), \qquad C_{d,s}=N_{d,s}(b)-N_{d,s}(a). $$
Feasibility gives $S_{d,s}\geq0$. Constraints with $C_{d,s}\leq0$ impose no additional restriction. Hence the largest admissible blocking-move size is
$$ \varepsilon_{\max} = \min\left( x_{f,a}, \min_{\substack{d,i:b_i=d\neq d_0\\C_{d,(f,i)}>0}} \frac{S_{d,(f,i)}}{C_{d,(f,i)}} \right), $$
with an empty inner minimum interpreted as $+\infty$.

Thus a positive blocking move exists exactly when all offered slots are acceptable and $\varepsilon_{\max}>0$.

2.2. Largest insertion from outside

If uninserted mass $r>0$ proposes tuple $b$, its old outside cohort occupies no real seats. Provided every offered real slot is acceptable, the largest insertion is therefore
$$ \delta = \min\left( r, \min_{\substack{d,i:b_i=d\neq d_0}} \frac{q_d-H_{d,(f,i)}(x)} {N_{d,(f,i)}(b)} \right). $$
Every denominator in the inner minimum is an integer between $1$ and $K$. An unacceptable offer gives $\delta=0$; the all-outside tuple gives $\delta=r$.

Both insertion calculations and blocking checks require only the listed tuples and supplied priority orders. Their number of rational arithmetic operations is polynomial in the input parameters.

3. Finite termination

3.1. Priority-prefix monotonicity

Consider an attempt before any sibling displacement terminates it.

Every committed daycare update adds proposed mass and applies greedy choice. Rejected singleton mass then advances; retained singleton mass never voluntarily leaves. Uninserted sibling mass arrives from outside.

For any acceptable priority prefix whose old mass is $H$, if a proposal adds prefix mass $u$, its new retained mass is
$$ \min(q_d,H+u)\geq H, $$
because $H\leq q_d$.

Thus every $H_{d,s}$ is nondecreasing while an attempt continues. Discarded tentative proposals do not alter this conclusion.

3.2. At most one positive insertion per tuple

Suppose an insertion at $b$ has size $0<\delta<r$. Some real-daycare constraint attains the minimum, so for an offered slot $s$,
$$ H_{d,s}(x)+N_{d,s}(b)\delta=q_d. $$
After insertion, this prefix is full.

If the resulting chain does not terminate the attempt, prefix monotonicity preserves its fullness. Another positive insertion at the same tuple would add positive mass to that prefix and is therefore impossible.

If $\delta=r$, no uninserted mass remains. Hence each listed sibling tuple receives at most one positive insertion in an attempt.

3.3. Singleton rejection chains are finite

At the beginning of a singleton chain, choose a common denominator $D$ for its rational state and capacities.

Singleton greedy choice uses addition, subtraction and minima. All subsequent masses remain multiples of $1/D$. Every positive rejection consequently has size at least $1/D$.

Number a singleton type’s choices consecutively, including its final outside choice. Let
$$ \Phi = \sum_{\text{singleton }f} \int_{\text{mass of }f} \text{current choice index}\,d\text{mass}, $$
where pending mass is indexed by its next proposal.

A rejection advances the rejected portion by one choice, increasing $\Phi$ by its mass. Acceptance does not decrease $\Phi$. If $b_f$ is the number of listed nonoutside choices, then
$$ \Phi\leq \sum_{\text{singleton }f}\mu_f(b_f+1). $$
Thus there are finitely many positive rejections. Every proposal after the finitely many initially pending proposals is generated by a rejection, so the chain has finitely many proposal events.

The same argument applies to initial singleton deferred acceptance.

3.4. Attempts and restarts are finite

Each sibling insertion is rational, and each ensuing singleton chain is finite. There are finitely many tuple scans and blocking checks, and at most one positive insertion per listed tuple. Every attempt therefore terminates.

Let $s$ be the number of sibling types. A restart either enters a previously unattempted permutation or returns $\mathrm{Unsuccess}$. Since there are $s!$ permutations, the whole process terminates finitely.

4. Stability whenever Mass-ESDA succeeds

Consider the final attempt of a successful run.

4.1. Singleton types cannot block

Suppose positive singleton mass of type $f$ ends at choice $a$, and $b\succ_f a$.

Every portion ending at $a$ has previously been rejected at $b$. If $b$ finds its slot unacceptable, it cannot block. Otherwise, immediately after a positive rejection of slot $(f,1)$ at $b$, greedy choice has a full prefix:
$$ H_{b,(f,1)}=q_b. $$
This holds for both rejected incoming mass and displaced incumbent mass. Prefix monotonicity preserves fullness to the final assignment.

Since $a$ and $b$ are distinct singleton choices, the departing cohort removes no seat at $b$. A blocking move would require
$$ H_{b,(f,1)}+\varepsilon\leq q_b $$
for some $\varepsilon>0$, a contradiction.

4.2. Previously checked sibling types remain unblocked

Fix sibling type $f$, and let $x^{\mathrm{check}}$ be the state when its blocking-improvement check passed.

Its tuple masses remain unchanged afterward in a successful attempt: any subsequent sibling displacement would terminate that attempt. Thus
$$ x^{\mathrm{final}}_{f,a}=x^{\mathrm{check}}_{f,a}. $$
Meanwhile,
$$ H_{d,s}(x^{\mathrm{final}}) \geq H_{d,s}(x^{\mathrm{check}}). $$

If $f,a,b,\varepsilon$ blocked the final assignment, the same departing mass would have been available at the earlier check, and every blocking inequality would also have held there:
$$ H_{d,s}(x^{\mathrm{check}}) + \bigl(N_{d,s}(b)-N_{d,s}(a)\bigr)\varepsilon \leq q_d. $$
This contradicts the passed check.

All retained tuples and slots are acceptable, family mass is conserved, and daycare capacities are respected. The successful output is therefore feasible, individually rational and stable.

5. Polynomial bit lengths

Let $D_0$ be the product of the encoded input denominators. Its binary length is $O(L)$.

Singleton processing introduces no new denominator factors. A sibling insertion introduces at most one additional factor $t$, with $1\leq t\leq K$, because its size is one of the quantities in the insertion minimum.

There are at most $B$ positive real-tuple insertions per attempt. Hence every state in an attempt has a common denominator dividing some $D$ satisfying
$$ D\leq D_0K^B. $$

Masses and retained daycare totals are bounded by input masses and total child mass. Their numerators therefore also have polynomial binary length. This proves that each individual greedy-choice event, insertion calculation and blocking check takes polynomial time.

6. New result: accelerating one permutation attempt

This section uses the aggregate pending-mass convention from Section 1.

Let
$$ V=\sum_{\text{singleton }f}(b_f+1) $$
be the number of singleton type-choice pairs, including outside choices. Then $V\leq B+\tau$.

6.1. Daycare phases

During a singleton chain, sibling incumbents are fixed until a sibling displacement stops the attempt.

At a real daycare of positive capacity, use the following phase descriptions:

  • Slack phase: retained mass is strictly below capacity.
  • Full phase: retained mass equals capacity, and the lowest-priority retained slot type is fixed.

A daycare becomes full at most once during the chain. Once full, it remains full: accepted incoming mass replaces rejected mass.

In a full phase, incoming mass of the cutoff slot type is rejected because its incumbent copies precede incoming copies. A higher-priority offer decreases cutoff mass. When that cutoff mass is exhausted, the cutoff improves strictly.

An exhausted cutoff cannot return. At exhaustion, retained mass strictly above it fills capacity; the corresponding prefix remains full by monotonicity.

Writing
$$ T=\sum_f k_f\leq\tau K, $$
each daycare therefore has at most one fullness transition and at most $T$ cutoff changes. There are at most $m(T+1)$ phase-boundary events in a chain. A single proposal can cross several boundaries; this only reduces the number of such events.

6.2. A fixed functional graph within a phase

For every pending singleton pair $v=(f,j)$, its next proposal has one of the following forms while the phase remains unchanged.

  • Rejected offer. It transfers all pending mass to $(f,j+1)$.
  • Accepted into slack. It removes that pending mass from the queue.
  • Accepted above a singleton cutoff. It displaces the same amount of cutoff singleton mass, transferring pending mass to that singleton’s next choice.
  • Accepted above a sibling cutoff. It immediately causes the prescribed failure or restart.
  • Outside offer. It removes pending mass from the queue.

For case 2, the phase remains unchanged only when the offer is smaller than the remaining slack. For case 3, it remains unchanged only when the offer is smaller than the cutoff mass.

Consequently, within a phase each vertex has at most one successor. Cases 2, 4 and 5 are absorbing events. The successor graph is fixed until a phase boundary or termination.

Its resources are the positive daycare slacks and positive cutoff masses. An ordinary event changes each resource only by decreasing it or leaving it unchanged.

6.3. A scheduling lemma

Lemma. In a fixed functional graph with $V$ vertices, process the smallest vertex having positive pending mass. Aggregate mass when a transfer reaches an already pending vertex.

After at most $V^2$ events, either all pending mass is absorbed or a vertex is selected that is the largest vertex on its directed cycle. Once such a vertex is selected, the same mass traverses that cycle repeatedly, with all other pending vertices inactive.

Proof. For every directed cycle, mark its largest vertex. For a vertex $v$, let $\ell(v)$ be the number of firings along its successor path up to and including absorption or the first marked vertex. Before reaching either, the path has no repeated vertex, so
$$ 1\leq\ell(v)\leq V. $$

Define
$$ \Psi=\sum_{\text{pending }v}\ell(v). $$
Initially $\Psi\leq V^2$.

Before a marked vertex is selected, a transfer to an empty successor decreases $\Psi$ by one. A transfer to an already pending successor decreases it by $\ell(v)$, because the two pending amounts aggregate. Absorption also decreases it. Thus there can be at most $V^2$ such events.

Now suppose marked vertex $p$ is selected. Every other pending vertex is larger than $p$, because $p$ was the smallest pending vertex. All vertices on its cycle are at most $p$. No other vertex on that cycle is pending.

The mass at $p$ therefore follows the cycle without encountering or yielding to any other pending mass. On returning to $p$, the queue is exactly as before. This repeats until a resource boundary occurs. $\square$

6.4. Skipping a repeated cycle exactly

Suppose the circulating mass is $\alpha>0$. For each resource $R_r$, let $c_r$ count the cycle’s events that decrease it. One full cycle decreases that resource by
$$ c_r\alpha. $$

At least one $c_r$ is positive. Otherwise every cycle edge would be an immediate rejection. Such an edge preserves singleton type and strictly increases its choice rank, so those edges cannot form a cycle.

The largest number of complete cycles that can be skipped while keeping all resources strictly positive is
$$ k= \min_{r:c_r>0} \left( \left\lceil\frac{R_r}{c_r\alpha}\right\rceil-1 \right). $$
This is a nonnegative integer.

For each skipped cycle, every intermediate resource consumption is at most the full-cycle consumption. Hence
$$ k c_r\alpha<R_r $$
guarantees that every skipped event has the same successor, acceptance decision and event ordering.

Apply the cycle’s net retained-mass changes multiplied by $k$. The pending queue returns to its original state. For a resource attaining the minimum, the next traversal reaches a boundary within at most $V$ events. Those events are processed literally.

The repetition count has polynomial binary length. Indeed, every positive circulating mass is at least $1/D$, and each cycle includes positive singleton rejection. The bounded potential from Section 3 bounds the total number of repetitions by a quantity whose binary length is polynomial in the input parameters.

Thus skipping uses exact rational and integer arithmetic; it introduces no approximation.

6.5. Polynomial evaluation of an attempt

Each phase requires:

  • polynomial work to construct its successor graph;
  • at most $V^2$ ordinary events before absorption or cycle detection;
  • one exact cycle skip;
  • at most $V$ further events before a boundary.

There are polynomially many boundaries per chain. There are at most $B$ sibling insertion chains, together with initial singleton deferred acceptance. Insertions, direct displacement decisions and blocking checks are polynomial-time operations.

Therefore:

Under the stated aggregate scheduling convention, the exact outcome of one permutation attempt is computable in polynomial time.

The acceleration preserves the first phase-boundary event and processes it literally. It consequently preserves the first sibling displacement and the resulting restart or failure.

A compressed history for one attempt can list ordinary events and cycle blocks containing the cycle, circulating mass and repetition count. There are polynomially many blocks, each of polynomial encoding length. The scheduling lemma and resource inequalities provide polynomial-time verification of those blocks.

If a chain always has exactly one pending singleton cohort, aggregation and queue-order choices never arise. The same acceleration argument then applies directly to that chain.

7. Consequences for terminal-outcome computation

Again, the algorithmic consequences in this section use the aggregate scheduling convention.

7.1. Fixed sibling count

Let $P(m,\tau,K,B,L)$ bound the time to evaluate one attempt.

There are at most $s!$ attempts. Recording attempted permutations gives a terminal-outcome algorithm with running time
$$ s!\,P(m,\tau,K,B,L) $$
up to polynomial bookkeeping factors.

Thus terminal-outcome computation is fixed-parameter tractable in the number $s$ of sibling types. In particular, it is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed $s$, when expanded histories are not required.

This removes numerical precision as a source of exponential outcome-computation time within an attempt.

7.2. Polynomial working space

The entire attempted-permutation set need not be stored.

Define a deterministic transition procedure $F$ that evaluates one attempt. It returns either a terminal record or its proposed restart permutation, without checking whether that permutation was previously attempted.

Maintain the current permutation $p_t$ and the attempt index $t$. If $F(p_t)$ proposes $r$, replay the transition sequence from the initial permutation, comparing $r$ with each of
$$ p_0,\ldots,p_t. $$
A match gives precisely the repeated-permutation failure. Otherwise continue from $r$.

Determinism makes replay valid: before the first repeated permutation, the actual run and the replay have the same transition sequence.

The index has binary length at most
$$ \log_2(s!)+O(1)=O(s\log(s+1)). $$
Only a constant number of permutations and one polynomial-size attempt state are stored. This gives polynomial working space and a running-time bound of
$$ (s!)^2\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,K,B,L). $$

In particular, the success-versus-failure decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{PSPACE}$ under this convention. This upper bound is not a hardness result.

8. Expanded histories can be exponentially long

The output-size obstruction remains even with one sibling type.

Fix $n\geq1$ and set
$$ \delta=2^{-n}, \qquad N=2^n. $$
There are daycares $A,B,C$ with capacities
$$ q_A=q_B=1, \qquad q_C=\delta. $$
There are singleton types $f,g$ and a two-child type $h$, with masses
$$ \mu_f=\mu_g=1, \qquad \mu_h=\delta. $$
Preferences are
$$ (A)\succ_f(B)\succ_f(d_0), $$
$$ (B)\succ_g(A)\succ_g(d_0), $$
$$ (A,C)\succ_h(d_0,d_0). $$

Use these complete daycare priorities:

| Daycare | Priority |
|---|---|
| $A$ | $g_1\succ h_1\succ f_1\succ\varnothing\succ h_2$ |
| $B$ | $f_1\succ g_1\succ\varnothing\succ h_1\succ h_2$ |
| $C$ | $h_2\succ\varnothing\succ g_1\succ f_1\succ h_1$ |

Initial singleton deferred acceptance assigns all $f$-mass to $A$ and all $g$-mass to $B$.

Type $h$ inserts its whole mass at $(A,C)$, displacing $\delta$ of $f$ from $A$. There is exactly one pending singleton cohort throughout the resulting chain.

After $j$ complete rounds, for $0\leq j\leq N-1$, the state is
$$ A:\quad g_1:j\delta,\quad h_1:\delta,\quad f_1:1-(j+1)\delta, $$
$$ B:\quad f_1:j\delta,\quad g_1:1-j\delta, $$
with pending $f$-mass $\delta$.

For $j<N-1$, the pending $f$-mass enters $B$, displacing $\delta$ of $g$. That $g$-mass enters $A$, displacing $\delta$ of $f$. This proves the displayed recurrence by induction.

In the final round, the arriving $g$-mass displaces $h_1$, causing self-displacement failure. There are exactly
$$ 2N=2^{n+1} $$
singleton proposals after the sibling insertion. All structural parameters are constant, while $L=\Theta(n)$.

An expanded chronological history therefore has exponential output length. Polynomial-time production of that representation is impossible.

The cycle acceleration describes the long middle portion using a binary repetition count. Thus the example does not obstruct polynomial-time computation of its terminal outcome.

Moreover, a stable assignment exists: assign all $f$-mass to $B$, all $g$-mass to $A$, and all $h$-mass outside. Each singleton’s preferred daycare is occupied by a higher-priority singleton. Type $h$ cannot enter $A$ against $g$. This also shows why procedural failure cannot certify nonexistence.
