Results

The continuous problem is exactly solvable in deterministic polynomial time. Thus a factor-\(2\) approximation is attainable.

Write \(P=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}\), \(I=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{int}}\), \(Z=C(z)\), and \(c_{\max}=\max_t c_t\). For integral capacities, the tight guarantees uniformly over all continuous optima are:

| Benchmark | Additive guarantee | Multiplicative guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| \(P\) | \(0\le Z-P\le \tau c_{\max}\); coefficient \(\tau\) is sharp | Unbounded, even for \(\tau=1\) |
| \(I\), with \(\tau=1\) | \(Z-I=0\) | \(Z=I\) |
| \(I\), with \(\tau\ge2\) | \(0\le Z-I\le \tau c_{\max}\); coefficient \(\tau\) is sharp | \(Z\le(3\tau-2)I\); factor \(3\tau-2\) is sharp |

The additive upper bounds are strict when \(c_{\max}>0\). Likewise, \(Z<(3\tau-2)I\) when \(\tau\ge2\) and \(I>0\). Sharpness means these constants are suprema and cannot be decreased.

A more precise additive bound is proved below.

1. Exact continuous optimization

Since \(q_t\in(2/3,1]\), we have \(a_t\in(1/3,1]\). Define the cost per unit of contribution by \(\rho_t=c_t/a_t\).

Sort the types so that \(\rho_1\le\cdots\le\rho_\tau\), breaking ties deterministically. Put \(A_j=\sum_{t=1}^j a_tb_t\) and \(A_0=0\). Feasibility guarantees an index \(k\) with \(A_{k-1}<M\le A_k\). Choose

\(\displaystyle x_t=\begin{cases}b_t,&t<k,\\ (M-A_{k-1})/a_k,&t=k,\\0,&t>k.\end{cases}\)

This selection satisfies the bounds and has contribution exactly \(M\).

To prove optimality, let \(\lambda=\rho_k\), and let \(y\) be any continuous feasible selection. Then

\(\displaystyle C(y)-C(x)=\lambda\sum_t a_t(y_t-x_t)+\sum_t(c_t-\lambda a_t)(y_t-x_t).\)

The first term is nonnegative because \(\lambda\ge0\) and \(\sum_t a_ty_t\ge M=\sum_t a_tx_t\). Every summand in the second term is also nonnegative:

  • For \(t<k\), both \(c_t-\lambda a_t\) and \(y_t-b_t\) are nonpositive.
  • For \(t>k\), both \(c_t-\lambda a_t\) and \(y_t\) are nonnegative.
  • For \(t=k\), the coefficient vanishes.

Thus \(C(y)\ge C(x)\).

The algorithm uses \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) exact rational comparisons and \(O(\tau)\) additional rational operations. Comparisons use cross multiplication. Computing \(b_t=N\mu_t\), prefix sums, and the single division produces rational numbers of polynomial bit length: products of input denominators have bit length bounded by their total encoded length. Consequently, the bit running time is polynomial in \(\tau,L,\log N\). No expansion into \(N\) workers is required.

2. Rounding feasibility and the exact additive loss

Assume now that every \(b_t\) is integral. For any continuous optimum \(x^*\), its ceiling \(z_t=\lceil x_t^*\rceil\) satisfies \(0\le z_t\le b_t\). Since all \(a_t>0\), rounding upward preserves feasibility. Therefore

\(\displaystyle P\le I\le Z.\)

Define the set of positive-cost fractional coordinates by

\(\displaystyle F_+=\{t:x_t^*\notin\mathbb Z,\ c_t>0\}.\)

The rounding loss relative to the continuous optimum is exactly

\(\displaystyle \Delta:=Z-P=\sum_{t\in F_+}c_t\bigl(\lceil x_t^*\rceil-x_t^*\bigr).\)

If \(F_+\) is empty, \(\Delta=0\). Otherwise, each rounding increment lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), so

\(\displaystyle 0\le Z-I\le Z-P=\Delta<\sum_{t\in F_+}c_t\le\tau c_{\max}.\)

In particular, \(\sum_t c_t\) is also a valid additive upper bound. Both that bound and the coefficient \(\tau\) multiplying \(c_{\max}\) are sharp, as shown in Section 5.

If \(P=0\), nonnegativity of costs implies that every positive-cost coordinate of \(x^*\) is zero. Hence \(Z=0\), and feasibility gives \(I=0\). All subsequent ratios are therefore considered only for positive optima.

3. Structure of arbitrary continuous optima

Suppose \(P>0\). Every continuous optimum satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_t a_tx_t^*=M.\)

Indeed, if the constraint had slack, some positive-cost coordinate would be positive, and decreasing it sufficiently slightly would preserve feasibility while strictly decreasing cost.

We also need an exchange property. If \(x_s^*>0\) and \(x_t^*<b_t\), then \(\rho_s\le\rho_t\). Otherwise, choose

\(\displaystyle 0<\eta\le\min\{a_sx_s^*,\,a_t(b_t-x_t^*)\}.\)

Decreasing coordinate \(s\) by \(\eta/a_s\) and increasing coordinate \(t\) by \(\eta/a_t\) preserves contribution and bounds, but changes cost by \(\eta(\rho_t-\rho_s)<0\), contradicting optimality.

Consequently, all fractional coordinates have the same ratio. If \(\Delta>0\), this common ratio is some \(\lambda>0\), and every fractional coordinate has positive cost. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \rho_t<\lambda\ \Longrightarrow\ x_t^*=b_t,\qquad \rho_t>\lambda\ \Longrightarrow\ x_t^*=0.\)

Let \(D=\{t:\rho_t<\lambda\}\) and \(A_D=\sum_{t\in D}a_tb_t\). Because a fractional coordinate with ratio \(\lambda\) is positive, tightness of the constraint gives \(M>A_D\).

Every integral feasible selection must therefore select at least one worker outside \(D\). Such a worker has cost

\(\displaystyle c_t=\rho_ta_t\ge\lambda a_t>\lambda/3.\)

Thus the useful lower bound

\(\displaystyle I>\lambda/3\)

holds whenever \(\Delta>0\).

4. Sharp multiplicative bound relative to the integer optimum

For \(\tau=1\), if the cost is positive, the continuous optimum is \(x_1^*=M/a_1\), and the least feasible integer is precisely \(\lceil M/a_1\rceil\). If the cost is zero, all costs are zero. Hence \(Z=I\).

Now let \(\tau\ge2\). If \(\Delta=0\), then \(P=I=Z\), so the claimed bound follows. Assume \(\Delta>0\), and let \(k\) be the number of fractional coordinates.

4.1. Fewer than \(\tau\) fractional coordinates

If \(k\le\tau-1\), the common ratio \(\lambda\) from Section 3 gives

\(\displaystyle \Delta=\lambda\sum_{\text{fractional }t}a_t(\lceil x_t^*\rceil-x_t^*)<k\lambda.\)

Using \(P\le I\) and \(I>\lambda/3\),

\(\displaystyle Z=P+\Delta<I+k\lambda<(1+3k)I\le(3\tau-2)I.\)

4.2. Every coordinate is fractional

Here every type has ratio \(\lambda\), so \(c_t=\lambda a_t\).

Let \(n_t=\lfloor x_t^*\rfloor\), \(B=\sum_t a_tn_t\), and \(S=\sum_t a_t\). All fractional parts are positive, so \(B<M\), and

\(\displaystyle Z=\lambda(B+S).\)

Let \(y\) be an integral optimum and put \(T=\sum_t a_ty_t\). Then \(I=\lambda T\), \(T\ge M>B\), and

\(\displaystyle \frac ZI=\frac{B+S}{T}.\)

There are two cases.

If \(T\ge2/3\): since \(S\le\tau\),

\(\displaystyle \frac{B+S}{T}<1+\frac{3\tau}{2}\le3\tau-2,\)

where the last inequality follows from \(\tau\ge2\).

If \(T<2/3\): each worker contributes strictly more than \(1/3\). Thus \(y\) selects exactly one worker, say of type \(j\), and \(T=a_j\). Similarly, \(B<T<2/3\) implies that \(n\) selects either zero workers or exactly one worker.

If \(n=0\), then

\(\displaystyle \frac{B+S}{T}=1+\sum_{t\ne j}\frac{a_t}{a_j}\le1+\frac{\tau-1}{a_j}<3\tau-2.\)

Otherwise \(n\) selects one worker of type \(i\). Since \(a_i=B<T=a_j\), we have \(i\ne j\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \frac{B+S}{T}=1+2\frac{a_i}{a_j}+\sum_{t\notin\{i,j\}}\frac{a_t}{a_j}<3+3(\tau-2)=3\tau-3.\)

The empty sum when \(\tau=2\) causes no difficulty: \(a_i<a_j\) already makes the expression strictly less than \(3\).

This completes the uniform bound \(Z\le(3\tau-2)I\).

5. Matching examples

All examples below satisfy the binary-rational and population requirements. Choose positive integer capacities meeting the stated bounds and increase one capacity until their sum \(N\) is a power of two. Then \(\mu_t=b_t/N\) are positive dyadic rationals summing to \(1\). Set \(q_t=(1+a_t)/2\), which is dyadic whenever \(a_t\) is dyadic.

5.1. Unbounded ratio to the continuous optimum

Take \(\tau=1\), \(b_1=a_1=c_1=1\), and \(M=\varepsilon\), where \(0<\varepsilon<1\) is dyadic.

Then \(x_1^*=\varepsilon\), \(P=\varepsilon\), and \(Z=I=1\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle Z/P=1/\varepsilon\longrightarrow\infty.\)

Also \(Z-P=1-\varepsilon\to c_{\max}\), proving sharpness of the continuous additive coefficient for \(\tau=1\).

5.2. Additive loss approaching \(\tau c_{\max}\) for both benchmarks

Fix \(\tau\ge2\). Let \(\varepsilon>0\) be dyadic and sufficiently small, and set

\(\displaystyle a_t=c_t=1-(t-1)\varepsilon,\qquad M=1,\qquad S=\sum_t a_t.\)

Require \(b_2\ge2\) and all other capacities at least \(1\). Define \(h=\varepsilon/S\) and

\(\displaystyle x_2^*=1+h,\qquad x_t^*=h\quad(t\ne2).\)

For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), \(0<h<1\), so this selection respects the capacities. Its contribution and cost are

\(\displaystyle a_2+hS=(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon=1.\)

Because \(c_t=a_t\), every feasible selection costs at least \(1\). One integral worker of type \(1\) has contribution and cost exactly \(1\). Thus \(P=I=1\).

Ceiling rounding selects two workers of type \(2\) and one of every other type. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle Z=S+a_2,\qquad Z-P=Z-I=S-\varepsilon=\tau-\left(\frac{\tau(\tau-1)}2+1\right)\varepsilon.\)

Since \(c_{\max}=1\), both additive losses approach \(\tau c_{\max}\). Furthermore,

\(\displaystyle \frac{Z-I}{\sum_t c_t}=\frac{S-\varepsilon}{S}\longrightarrow1,\)

so the additive bound by the sum of type costs is sharp as well.

5.3. Integer approximation ratio approaching \(3\tau-2\)

Fix \(\tau\ge2\). Choose dyadic \(\alpha_h>1/3\) tending to \(1/3\), for example

\(\displaystyle \alpha_h=\frac{\lfloor2^h/3\rfloor+1}{2^h}.\)

For sufficiently large \(h\), set

\(\displaystyle a_1=c_1=\alpha_h,\qquad a_t=c_t=1-(t-2)2^{-h}\quad(2\le t\le\tau),\qquad M=\alpha_h.\)

All these contributions lie in \((1/3,1]\), and \(a_t\ge\alpha_h\). With capacities at least \(1\), choose

\(\displaystyle x_t^*=\frac{\alpha_h}{\tau a_t}.\)

Every coordinate lies in \((0,1)\), and total contribution and cost equal \(\alpha_h\). Thus this is a continuous optimum. One worker of type \(1\) proves \(I=\alpha_h\).

Rounding selects one worker of every type, so

\(\displaystyle \frac ZI=\frac{\sum_t a_t}{\alpha_h}\longrightarrow\frac{1/3+(\tau-1)}{1/3}=3\tau-2.\)

Therefore no smaller factor can hold uniformly over all continuous optima.

6. Relation to the finite-worker guarantee

The cited paper’s Section 6.2.1 states a factor-\(2\) guarantee for discrete finite-worker selection. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1406.7157v3)

The continuous formulation here admits exact optimization on the support. Its ceiling-rounding guarantees are different: an arbitrary optimum can distribute mass across many tied types. The sorting algorithm selects an optimum with at most one fractional coordinate, giving additive loss below \(c_{\max}\); that stronger bound does not hold uniformly over all continuous optima.
