The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{|T|}\) summing to \(1\), an initial bundle \(B_t\), additive nonnegative valuation \(v_t\), and a finite catalogue \(R\) of unlimited-supply goods, decide whether integer bundles \(q_t\in\mathbb{N}^{R}\) exist such that, for every \(t,u\in T\), \(v_t(B_t)+\sum_{r\in R}q_{t,r}v_t(r)\ge v_t(B_u)+\sum_{r\in R}q_{u,r}v_t(r)\); if so, output the bundles \(q_t\) and their aggregate mass-weighted quantities.

Definition

Let \(B,u_1,\ldots,u_s\in\mathbb N_{>0}\), \(\mu_b,\mu_e,\kappa_1,\ldots,\kappa_s\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), and \(\mu_b+\mu_e=1\). Type \(b\) initially holds one good of value \(B\); type \(e\) holds none; each good type \(r_j\) has value \(u_j\).
A configuration is \(q\in\mathbb N^s\). A mass allocation has \(x_{t,q}\ge 0\), \(\sum_qx_{t,q}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,q}x_{t,q}q_j\le\kappa_j\). It is envy-free if \(B\mathbf1_{t=b}+\sum_j u_jq_j\ge B\mathbf1_{t'=b}+\sum_j u_jq'_j\) for all supported \((t,q)\) and \((t',q')\).
Restrict to \(\sum_j\kappa_ju_j=\mu_eB\). Equivalently, ask for \(y_q\ge0\) with \(\sum_qy_q=\mu_e\), \(\sum_qy_qq_j=\kappa_j\), and \(\sum_ju_jq_j=B\) whenever \(y_q>0\).

Complexity of the Balanced Mass Problem

Result

The balanced mass problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete under binary encoding. Nevertheless, it is fixed-parameter tractable parameterized by the number \(s\) of additional-good types. More precisely, if \(L\) denotes the input length, it can be decided in time \(s^{O(s)}L^{O(1)}\).

The hardness reduction fixes \(\mu_b=\mu_e=1/2\) and uses the same capacity \(\kappa_j=1/4\) for every good type.

1. Integer-hull reformulation

Let

\[ S(u,B)=\{q\in\mathbb Z_{\geq 0}^s:u\cdot q=B\} \]

and let

\[ Q(u,B)=\operatorname{conv}(S(u,B)). \]

Define \(a=\kappa/\mu_e\), coordinatewise.

Lemma 1

A balanced mass allocation exists if and only if \(a\in Q(u,B)\).

Proof

Suppose \(y_q\) is feasible. Set \(p_q=y_q/\mu_e\). Then \(p_q\geq 0\),

\[ \sum_qp_q=1, \qquad \sum_qp_qq_j=\frac{\kappa_j}{\mu_e}=a_j, \]

and every \(q\) with \(p_q>0\) belongs to \(S(u,B)\). Hence \(a\) is a convex combination of points in \(S(u,B)\).

Conversely, if \(a=\sum_qp_qq\) for a probability distribution supported on \(S(u,B)\), then \(y_q=\mu_ep_q\) satisfies

\[ \sum_qy_q=\mu_e, \qquad \sum_qy_qq_j=\mu_ea_j=\kappa_j. \]

Thus the two formulations are equivalent. The balance identity gives \(u\cdot a=B\), but membership in the continuous hyperplane \(u\cdot x=B\) is not sufficient: the relevant object is its integer hull \(Q(u,B)\). ∎

2. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Proposition 2

The balanced mass problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Proof

Every \(q\in S(u,B)\) satisfies

\[ 0\leq q_j\leq \frac{B}{u_j}\leq B. \]

Thus every configuration has polynomial binary encoding length.

If \(a\in Q(u,B)\), Carathéodory’s theorem gives configurations \(q^1,\ldots,q^m\in S(u,B)\), with \(m\leq s+1\), and coefficients \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\geq0\) such that

\[ \sum_{r=1}^m\lambda_r=1, \qquad a=\sum_{r=1}^m\lambda_rq^r. \]

The configurations may be chosen affinely independent. The coefficients are then solutions of a nonsingular rational linear system of order at most \(s+1\). Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound show that their numerators and denominators have bit length polynomial in the input length.

A certificate therefore consists of the configurations \(q^r\) and rational coefficients \(\lambda_r\). Exact rational arithmetic verifies all equalities and inequalities in polynomial time. ∎

3. NP-hardness

We reduce from \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\). It is convenient to use the equivalent restricted form in which positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) satisfy

\[ \sum_{i=1}^na_i=2T, \]

and the question is whether some subset sums to \(T\). This restriction remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete: multiplying every integer of an arbitrary \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) instance by \(2\) produces such an instance.

Construction

Set \(D=n+1\), and define

\[ r_i=D^{i-1}, \qquad R=\sum_{i=1}^nr_i. \]

For every \(i\), introduce two good types, indexed by \((i,0)\) and \((i,1)\). Their auxiliary codes are

\[ c_{i,0}=r_i, \qquad c_{i,1}=r_i+D^na_i. \]

Let

\[ A=R+D^nT, \qquad C=\max_{i,\varepsilon}c_{i,\varepsilon}, \qquad H=1+A+nC. \]

Define the actual values by

\[ u_{i,\varepsilon}=H+c_{i,\varepsilon}, \]

and define the target bundle value by

\[ B^\star=nH+A. \]

Finally, take

\[ \mu_b=\mu_e=\frac12, \qquad \kappa_{i,\varepsilon}=\frac14 \]

for every \(i\) and \(\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}\). The number of additional-good types is \(s=2n\).

All constructed integers have polynomial encoding length because \(D^n\) has \(O(n\log n)\) bits.

Lemma 3

The instance is balanced.

Proof

For each \(i\),

\[ c_{i,0}+c_{i,1}=2r_i+D^na_i. \]

Consequently,

\[ \sum_{i,\varepsilon}u_{i,\varepsilon} =2nH+2R+D^n\sum_i a_i =2nH+2R+2D^nT =2B^\star. \]

Therefore

\[ \sum_{i,\varepsilon}\kappa_{i,\varepsilon}u_{i,\varepsilon} =\frac14\cdot 2B^\star =\frac12B^\star =\mu_eB^\star. \]

Thus the required balance identity holds. ∎

Lemma 4

Integer configurations \(q\) satisfying \(u\cdot q=B^\star\) are in bijection with subsets \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfying \(\sum_{i\in I}a_i=T\).

Proof

Let

\[ N=\sum_{i,\varepsilon}q_{i,\varepsilon}. \]

Because every value is \(H+c_{i,\varepsilon}\), if \(N\geq n+1\), then

\[ u\cdot q\geq(n+1)H>nH+A=B^\star, \]

where the strict inequality follows from \(H>A\).

If \(N\leq n-1\), then

\[ u\cdot q \leq (n-1)H+(n-1)C <nH \leq B^\star, \]

because \((n-1)C<nC<H\). Hence necessarily \(N=n\).

After cancelling \(nH\), the configuration equation becomes

\[ \sum_{i,\varepsilon}c_{i,\varepsilon}q_{i,\varepsilon}=A. \]

Put

\[ m_i=q_{i,0}+q_{i,1}. \]

The preceding equality is

\[ \sum_{i=1}^nm_iD^{i-1} +D^n\sum_{i=1}^na_iq_{i,1} = R+D^nT. \]

Reducing modulo \(D^n\) gives

\[ \sum_{i=1}^nm_iD^{i-1}\equiv R\pmod{D^n}. \]

Since \(\sum_i m_i=N=n\), every \(m_i\) lies in \(\{0,\ldots,n\}=\{0,\ldots,D-1\}\). Moreover,

\[ 0\leq\sum_i m_iD^{i-1}\leq nD^{n-1}<D^n, \]

and \(0<R<D^n\). Thus the congruence is an equality. Uniqueness of base-\(D\) representation gives

\[ m_i=1 \]

for every \(i\). Hence exactly one of \(q_{i,0}\) and \(q_{i,1}\) equals \(1\), while the other equals \(0\). The remaining part of the equation yields

\[ \sum_{i=1}^na_iq_{i,1}=T. \]

Thus \(I=\{i:q_{i,1}=1\}\) is a solution to the \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) instance.

Conversely, any subset \(I\) summing to \(T\) defines a configuration by taking \(q_{i,1}=1\) for \(i\in I\), \(q_{i,0}=1\) for \(i\notin I\), and all complementary coordinates equal to \(0\). Substitution gives \(u\cdot q=B^\star\). ∎

Lemma 5

The constructed balanced mass instance is feasible if and only if the \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) instance is positive.

Proof

Suppose \(I\) is a partition solution, and let \(q^I\) be its configuration from Lemma 4. Because \(\sum_i a_i=2T\), the complement \(\overline I\) also sums to \(T\). Its configuration is obtained by swapping \(q_{i,0}\) and \(q_{i,1}\). Therefore

\[ q^I+q^{\overline I}=\mathbf1 \]

in all \(2n\) coordinates.

Assign mass \(1/4\) to each of these two configurations. The total mass is \(1/2=\mu_e\), and every coordinate has total expected supply

\[ \frac14q^I_{i,\varepsilon} +\frac14q^{\overline I}_{i,\varepsilon} =\frac14 =\kappa_{i,\varepsilon}. \]

Thus the balanced mass instance is feasible.

Conversely, any feasible mass allocation has positive total mass and therefore supports at least one configuration satisfying \(u\cdot q=B^\star\). Lemma 4 then produces a partition solution. ∎

Theorem 6

The balanced mass problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Proof

Lemma 5 gives a polynomial-time reduction from \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\), establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Proposition 2 establishes membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). ∎

The reduction establishes ordinary binary-encoded, and hence weak, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. It does not establish strong or unary \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Fixed-parameter tractability in \(s\)

Theorem 7

The balanced mass problem is fixed-parameter tractable parameterized by \(s\).

Proof

By Lemma 1, it suffices to decide whether \(a\in Q(u,B)\).

For an arbitrary rational objective vector \(c\), consider

\[ \max\{c\cdot q:q\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^s,\ u\cdot q=B\}. \]

After clearing denominators in \(c\), this is integer linear optimization in \(s\) integer variables. Optimization can also be obtained by binary search on the objective value, using feasibility systems containing

\[ q\geq0, \qquad u\cdot q=B, \qquad c\cdot q\geq z. \]

Kannan’s fixed-dimensional integer-programming algorithm solves these systems in time \(s^{O(s)}L^{O(1)}\); see [Kannan, 1987](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.12.3.415). Because \(q_j\leq B\), the objective range has polynomial bit length, so the binary search makes only polynomially many oracle calls.

This gives a strong linear-optimization oracle for \(Q(u,B)\), since maximizing over a convex hull is the same as maximizing over its generating configurations.

It remains to verify the rationality hypothesis needed for exact optimization–separation equivalence. Every vertex of \(Q(u,B)\) is an integer vector in \([0,B]^s\), and therefore has \(O(s\log(B+1))\) encoding length. Facet and affine-hull equations obtained from affinely independent vertices have polynomial encoding length by Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s bound. Hence \(Q(u,B)\) has a polynomial rationality guarantee.

The exact optimization–separation equivalence of Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver therefore turns the preceding optimization oracle into an exact strong-separation oracle for \(Q(u,B)\); see [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, 1981](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf). Applying it to \(a\) either certifies \(a\in Q(u,B)\) or returns a separating hyperplane.

The resulting running time is

\[ s^{O(s)}L^{O(1)}, \]

which is fixed-parameter tractable in \(s\). ∎

Conclusion

The balanced mass problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, but it is \(\mathrm{FPT}\) parameterized by \(s\). In particular, for every fixed number of additional-good types—including \(s=3\)—the problem is polynomial-time solvable. Thus the fixed-three-type hardness of the discrete finite-supply problem cannot transfer directly to this mass formulation.

Verification report

Overall assessment

The NP-membership and NP-hardness arguments are correct and rigorous. In particular, the reduction from \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) establishes binary-encoded NP-completeness.

The claimed FPT result is plausible, but Theorem 7 has a major verification gap: the cited 1981 optimization–separation theorem is applied to a necessarily non-full-dimensional polytope without establishing that the cited version covers that setting. Consequently, the prover has not fully established the second part of the target as written.

Step-by-step audit

1. Integer-hull reformulation

Lemma 1 is correct.

Because \(u_j>0\) and \(u\cdot q=B\), every feasible configuration satisfies \(0\le q_j\le B/u_j\). Thus \(S(u,B)\) is finite, so there is no issue concerning infinite convex combinations.

Scaling \(y_q\) by \(\mu_e>0\) gives a probability distribution with expectation \(a=\kappa/\mu_e\), and the converse scaling recovers the required masses. The balance identity indeed implies

\[ u\cdot a=\frac{\sum_j\kappa_ju_j}{\mu_e}=B. \]

No gap is present here.

2. Membership in NP

Proposition 2 is correct.

Each configuration has \(O(s\log(B+1))\) bits. Carathéodory’s theorem supplies at most \(s+1\) configurations. After choosing an affinely independent subfamily, the convex coefficients solve an integer-coefficient linear system whose entries are bounded by \(B\), with rational right-hand side \(a\). Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s inequality give polynomial bit complexity for the coefficients.

The proposed certificate can therefore be checked with exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time.

3. Reduction from PARTITION

The restricted source formulation is valid. Doubling every item ensures an even total and preserves whether an equal partition exists.

The construction has polynomial output length: \(D^n\) has \(O(n\log n)\) bits, and all subsequent quantities are obtained using polynomially many arithmetic operations on polynomial-bit integers.

Balance calculation

Lemma 3 is correct. The calculation gives

\[ \sum_{i,\varepsilon}u_{i,\varepsilon} =2nH+2R+2D^nT =2B^\star, \]

and hence the capacity-weighted value is \(\mu_eB^\star\).

Characterization of configurations

Lemma 4 is correct.

The argument forcing \(N=n\) checks out:

  • If \(N\ge n+1\), then \(u\cdot q\ge(n+1)H>nH+A\), since \(H>A\).
  • If \(N\le n-1\), then \(u\cdot q\le(n-1)(H+C)<nH\), since \((n-1)C<nC<H\).

After reducing modulo \(D^n\), the digit sum lies strictly below \(D^n\), and each \(m_i\) lies in \(\{0,\ldots,D-1\}\). The congruence is therefore equality, and uniqueness of base-\(D\) representation forces \(m_i=1\) for every \(i\). The remaining equation is exactly the subset-sum condition.

The converse substitution is also correct.

Equivalence of feasibility

Lemma 5 is correct.

A partition solution and its complement produce configurations whose coordinatewise sum is the all-ones vector. Assigning mass \(1/4\) to each gives total mass \(1/2\) and coordinate moments \(1/4\).

Conversely, any feasible allocation has positive mass and hence at least one supported configuration. Lemma 4 then produces a partition solution.

Thus Theorem 6 correctly establishes NP-completeness under binary encoding.

4. Fixed-parameter tractability

The fixed-dimensional integer-optimization component is sound. Maximizing a rational linear objective over \(S(u,B)\) reduces, after clearing denominators, to integer programming in \(s\) variables. Kannan’s result does provide an \(s^{O(s)}\) dependence times a polynomial in the encoding length [Kannan, 1987](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.12.3.415).

The objective range has polynomial bit length because \(0\le q_j\le B\), so exact optimization can be recovered using polynomially many feasibility calls.

The claimed vertex-size bound is also correct for nonempty \(Q(u,B)\): every vertex is one of the bounded integer configurations. Polynomial bounds on equations determined by affinely independent bounded vertices are likewise reasonable.

The final optimization-to-separation step, however, is not justified by the cited result as written. Every nonempty \(Q(u,B)\) satisfies

\[ Q(u,B)\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb R^s:u\cdot x=B\}, \]

so it is not full-dimensional in \(\mathbb R^s\). The cited 1981 paper defines the relevant “rational polytope” as full-dimensional and equipped with a rational interior point before stating its strong optimization–separation theorem [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, 1981](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf). The prover neither supplies such an interior point nor explicitly transfers the problem to the affine hull.

Merely observing that affine-hull equations have polynomial encoding length does not establish that the cited theorem applies. The prover must state an exact version covering possibly lower-dimensional well-described rational polyhedra and verify all of its hypotheses, or explicitly formulate the affine-hull reduction. The empty and zero-dimensional cases must also be accounted for.

This is a major gap because the FPT conclusion depends entirely on obtaining exact membership or separation from the optimization oracle.

5. Terminology concerning weak hardness

The sentence saying that the reduction establishes “ordinary binary-encoded, and hence weak, NP-hardness” is imprecise. A reduction from \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) proves binary NP-hardness and does not prove strong or unary hardness. By itself it does not establish that the target problem is weakly NP-hard in the stronger classificatory sense, which commonly also requires a pseudopolynomial algorithm or an exclusion of strong NP-hardness. This does not affect Theorem 6.

Minimum clarification required

To make the claimed complete resolution verifiable, the prover must:

  • State the exact optimization–separation theorem being invoked, including its treatment of lower-dimensional and empty rational polytopes.
  • Verify its hypotheses for \(Q(u,B)\), including the required encoding bounds and oracle behavior.
  • Explain how membership of \(a\) follows within \(s^{O(s)}L^{O(1)}\) time under that theorem.
