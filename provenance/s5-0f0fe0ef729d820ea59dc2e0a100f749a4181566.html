Theorem 1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), binary rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and a strict partner-type order \(\succ_t\) for each \(t\in T\), partition the unit interval into type blocks of those masses and let \(\theta(a)\) denote agent \(a\)'s type. Decide whether there exists a measurable, measure-preserving involution \(\pi\) with \(\pi(a)\ne a\) almost everywhere such that, for every feasible rival pairing \(\sigma\), \(\lambda\{a:\theta(\pi(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\sigma(a))\}\geq\lambda\{a:\theta(\sigma(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\pi(a))\}\). Agents of the same type are indifferent between partners of that type.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\) has a strict total order \(\succ_t\) on all of \(T\), including \(t\). Every distinct agent is an acceptable partner, including agents of one's own type; partners of the same type are indifferent.
Partition \([0,1]\), with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\), into consecutive type intervals of lengths \(\mu_t\), and write \(\theta(a)\) for the type of agent \(a\).
A feasible pairing is a measurable, measure-preserving map \(\pi:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) satisfying \(\pi(\pi(a))=a\) and \(\pi(a)\ne a\) almost everywhere. Rival pairings satisfy exactly these same conditions.
Call \(\pi\) popular if, for every feasible rival \(\sigma\),
\(\lambda\{a:\theta(\pi(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\sigma(a))\}\ge\lambda\{a:\theta(\sigma(a))\succ_{\theta(a)}\theta(\pi(a))\}\).
Is deciding whether a popular feasible pairing exists solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
For finite complete roommates instances with strict preferences and an even number of agents, popular-matching existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. [Cseh–Kavitha](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-020-00791-7)
Popular fractional roommates matchings can be computed in polynomial time under a comparison that multiplies the two partner distributions at each vertex; this differs from comparing the actual partners of each agent here. [Huang–Kavitha](https://www.di.ens.fr/~cchuang/work/mixed_popular.pdf)
No result settling this exact atomless pairing problem was identified in the literature search.

1. Profiles and the exact popularity certificate

For types $t,u,s$, define the vote favoring a change from partner type $s$ to partner type $u$ by
$$ v_t(u,s)= \begin{cases} 1,&u\succ_t s,\\ 0,&u=s,\\ -1,&s\succ_t u. \end{cases} $$

For a feasible pairing $\pi$, let
$$ C_{ts}=\{a:\theta(a)=t,\ \theta(\pi(a))=s\}, \qquad x_{ts}=\lambda(C_{ts}). $$
Its profile satisfies
$$ x_{ts}\geq 0,\qquad x_{ts}=x_{st}, \qquad \sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t. $$
The row equations follow because the cells partition each type interval. Symmetry follows because $\pi$ exchanges $C_{ts}$ and $C_{st}$ modulo null sets and preserves measure.

Conversely, every matrix satisfying these conditions is realizable. Partition the interval of type $t$ into subintervals of lengths $x_{ts}$. For $t\neq s$, exchange the corresponding equal-length subintervals by translations. For a diagonal subinterval, exchange its two halves. The resulting map is a measure-preserving involution without fixed points almost everywhere.

We also need the corresponding construction on arbitrary measurable cells. A measurable set $E\subseteq[0,1]$ of positive measure $m$ is measure-isomorphic, modulo null sets, to an interval of length $m$. To justify this, put
$$ F_E(z)=\lambda(E\cap[0,z]). $$
This continuous nondecreasing function pushes restricted Lebesgue measure on $E$ to Lebesgue measure on $[0,m]$. Indeed, a level set of $F_E$ contains zero restricted measure, and the restricted measure below level $r$ is $r$. A generalized inverse provides an inverse almost everywhere: distinct points with the same value lie in a nontrivial interval on which $F_E$ is constant. The interiors of the maximal such intervals form a countable collection, each having zero restricted measure; their endpoints are also a null set.

Consequently, finitely many measurable cells can be subdivided into pieces of prescribed masses, equal-mass pieces can be exchanged, and an internal piece can be paired by exchanging halves. All these constructions are modulo null sets, which suffices for feasibility.

Fix a profile $x$, and retain its positive cells
$$ \mathcal C=\{(t,s):x_{ts}>0\}. $$
For $c=(t,s)$ and $d=(u,r)$, put
$$ w_{cd}=v_t(u,s)+v_u(t,r). $$
These are the two endpoint votes when a rival pairs agents from these cells.

For distinct cells, let $q_{cd}=q_{dc}$ be the mass paired between them. Let $q_{cc}$ be half the agent mass paired internally in cell $c$. The maximum voting margin favoring a rival is exactly the optimum of
$$ \max_{q\geq0} \left( \sum_{c<d}w_{cd}q_{cd} +\sum_c w_{cc}q_{cc} \right) $$
subject to
$$ \sum_{d\neq c}q_{cd}+2q_{cc}=x_c \qquad(c\in\mathcal C). $$

Every feasible rival induces these variables and this objective. Conversely, the measurable-cell construction realizes every feasible solution as a rival. Thus the program optimizes over exactly the rivals in the question.

Internal pairings establish feasibility. Summing the supply equations shows that the total paired mass is $\tfrac12$, so the program is bounded. Its dual is
$$ \min_\alpha \sum_{(t,s)\in\mathcal C}x_{ts}\alpha_{ts} $$
subject to
$$ \alpha_{ts}+\alpha_{ur} \geq v_t(u,s)+v_u(t,r) \qquad((t,s),(u,r)\in\mathcal C). $$
The case of equal cells is included, and the dual variables are unrestricted.

The incumbent itself gives a rival of margin zero. Popularity is therefore equivalent to the primal optimum being zero.

$$ \alpha_{ts}+\alpha_{st}=0 \qquad(x_{ts}>0) $$

$$ \alpha_{ts}+\alpha_{ur} \geq v_t(u,s)+v_u(t,r) \qquad((t,s),(u,r)\in\mathcal C). $$

$$ -1\leq\alpha_{ts}\leq1. $$

Proof. If $x$ is popular, finite-dimensional linear-programming strong duality gives a dual optimum of objective zero. The constraints for reciprocal incumbent cells give
$$ \alpha_{ts}+\alpha_{st}\geq0, $$
and those for diagonal cells give $\alpha_{tt}\geq0$. Using symmetry,
$$ \sum_{(t,s)\in\mathcal C}x_{ts}\alpha_{ts} = \sum_{t<s}x_{ts}(\alpha_{ts}+\alpha_{st}) +\sum_t x_{tt}\alpha_{tt}. $$
Every term on the right is nonnegative. Since every active coefficient is positive and the sum is zero, all the asserted equalities follow.

Conversely, the asserted equalities make the objective of a feasible dual solution zero. Weak duality then bounds every rival’s margin by zero.

2. The two-partner bound

Suppose $x_{ts}>0$ and $x_{tr}>0$, where $s\neq r$. Apply the certificate inequality to cells $(t,r)$ and $(s,t)$:
$$ \alpha_{tr}+\alpha_{st}\geq v_t(s,r). $$
The second endpoint’s vote is zero because its proposed and current partner types are both $t$. Antisymmetry gives
$$ \alpha_{tr}-\alpha_{ts}\geq v_t(s,r). $$
Interchanging $s$ and $r$ gives the reverse inequality. Hence
$$ \alpha_{tr}-\alpha_{ts}=v_t(s,r). $$

If three positively assigned partner types satisfy
$$ s_1\succ_t s_2\succ_t s_3, $$
this identity gives
$$ \alpha_{ts_2}-\alpha_{ts_1}=1, \qquad \alpha_{ts_3}-\alpha_{ts_2}=1, \qquad \alpha_{ts_3}-\alpha_{ts_1}=1. $$
The first two equations contradict the third.

Thus every type has at most two positively assigned partner types. When there are two, the worse-partner potential is exactly one greater than the better-partner potential.

3. A stronger support normal form

Represent a profile by an undirected graph on $T$: an off-diagonal positive entry gives an edge, and a positive diagonal entry gives a loop. For this graph, a loop counts as one assigned partner type.

Normal-form theorem. If a popular profile exists, a popular profile exists whose connected components are precisely of the following forms:

  • an isolated vertex with a loop;
  • a path with no loops;
  • a path with exactly one loop, at an endpoint;
  • an odd cycle of length at least three, with no loops.

Proof. Start with a popular profile $x$. Among feasible profiles supported within the support of $x$, choose one, $y$, with the fewest positive unordered entries. This choice exists because only finitely many support subsets are possible. Support monotonicity makes $y$ popular.

By the two-partner bound, the graph of off-diagonal edges has maximum degree two. Its components are paths, cycles, or isolated vertices. A cycle vertex cannot have a loop, and an internal path vertex cannot have a loop.

An even cycle cannot occur. Alternately add and subtract a common amount from its edge masses. Each row sum is preserved. Increase the amount until an edge receiving a subtraction reaches zero; all other masses remain nonnegative. This produces a feasible profile with smaller support, a contradiction.

Nor can a path have loops at both endpoints. Write its vertices as $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ and edge masses as $e_1,\ldots,e_{k-1}$. Perturb them by
$$ \delta e_i=(-1)^{i-1}\varepsilon. $$
Perturb the endpoint loops by
$$ \delta x_{v_1v_1}=-\varepsilon, \qquad \delta x_{v_kv_k}=(-1)^{k-1}\varepsilon. $$
Every row sum is unchanged. The perturbation has entries of both signs. Since all support masses are positive, increasing a sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$ until the first decreasing mass becomes zero again reduces the support.

An isolated vertex must have a loop because its prescribed mass is positive. These observations establish the stated list. $\square$

Explicit mass formulas

The masses on every normal-form component are uniquely determined.

For a path $v_1,\ldots,v_k$, label it so that $v_1$ has no loop. Write $\mu_i=\mu_{v_i}$ and let $e_i$ be the mass on edge $v_iv_{i+1}$. The row equations give
$$ e_1=\mu_1,\qquad e_i=\mu_i-e_{i-1}, $$
and therefore
$$ e_i=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(-1)^{i-j}\mu_j. $$
If neither endpoint has a loop, the final row equation requires
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\mu_j=0. $$
If $v_k$ has a loop, its mass is
$$ x_{v_kv_k} =\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\mu_j. $$

For an odd cycle $v_1,\ldots,v_k$, use cyclic indices and put
$$ e_i=x_{v_i v_{i+1}}. $$
The equations are
$$ e_i=\mu_i-e_{i-1}. $$
Iterating around the odd cycle gives
$$ e_i=\frac12\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^j\mu_{i-j}. $$

In each case, all masses on the claimed support must be strictly positive.

These formulas show that a popular profile, when one exists, can be chosen rational with polynomial encoding length. More specifically, every positive entry can be chosen as an alternating sum of input masses, or half such a sum.

4. Certificates can be chosen half-integral

Half-integral certificate theorem. Every popular profile has a certificate with
$$ \alpha_{ts}\in \left\{-1,-\frac12,0,\frac12,1\right\}. $$

Proof. Introduce one variable $z_e$ for each active off-diagonal edge, with its two reciprocal cell potentials equal to $z_e$ and $-z_e$. Set diagonal potentials to zero.

The certificate conditions, together with the already proved bounds, define a nonempty bounded polyhedron. Every nonconstant inequality has one of the forms
$$ \varepsilon_i z_i+\varepsilon_j z_j\geq b,\qquad \varepsilon_i z_i\geq b,\qquad 2\varepsilon_i z_i\geq b, $$
where $\varepsilon_i,\varepsilon_j\in\{-1,1\}$ and $b$ is an integer. Here the first form uses distinct variables; repeated-variable expressions either cancel or give the third form.

Choose a vertex of this polyhedron and a full-rank collection of active equations determining it. Build a graph whose vertices are the variables and whose edges are the equations involving two distinct variables.

Within a connected component, propagation along a spanning tree expresses every variable as
$$ z_i=\delta_i z_r+k_i, \qquad \delta_i\in\{-1,1\},\quad k_i\in\mathbb Z, $$
for a chosen root variable $z_r$.

An active one-variable equation fixes $z_r$ to an integer or a half-integer. Otherwise, substituting the displayed expressions into an additional edge equation either gives an identity or an equation
$$ 2z_r=h $$
up to sign, with $h\in\mathbb Z$. If every additional equation were an identity and there were no one-variable equation, the component would retain a free parameter, contradicting full rank.

Thus every root, and consequently every variable, is a half-integer. The bounds restrict the values to the five stated possibilities. $\square$

This supplies a constant-size alphabet for certificate values; large rational potentials are unnecessary.

5. Odd cycles have a forced preference structure

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ be an odd-cycle component of a popular profile, and put
$$ p_i=\alpha_{v_i v_{i+1}}. $$
The potential at $v_i$ for its predecessor is $-p_{i-1}$. The two-partner identity gives
$$ p_i+p_{i-1}=\varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}, $$
where $\varepsilon_i=-1$ exactly when $v_i$ prefers its successor to its predecessor.

Iterating around the odd cycle yields
$$ 2p_i=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^j\varepsilon_{i-j}. $$
The right side is an odd integer. Since $|p_i|\leq1$, this forces
$$ p_i\in\left\{-\frac12,\frac12\right\}. $$
Two adjacent values must have the same sign because their sum is $\pm1$. Therefore all $p_i$ have the same sign.

Reverse the cycle orientation if necessary. Then every vertex prefers its successor to its predecessor, with potentials
$$ \alpha_{v_i v_{i+1}}=-\frac12, \qquad \alpha_{v_i v_{i-1}}=\frac12. $$

Own type must lie below both assigned partners. Indeed, the internal-cell constraint for the worse-partner cell gives
$$ \frac12\geq v_{v_i}(v_i,v_{i-1}). $$
The right side is either $1$ or $-1$, so it must be $-1$. Thus the predecessor, and hence also the successor, is preferred to own type.

There is a stronger restriction across all odd cycles.

First-choice property. Let $U$ be the set of vertices belonging to odd-cycle components. Each $t\in U$ ranks its successor first among all types in $U$.

Proof. Write $b(t)$ for the successor. Own type and the predecessor lie below $b(t)$ by the preceding argument.

Now take $u\in U$ that is neither $t$ nor a cycle neighbor of $t$. Apply the certificate inequality to the two better-partner cells:
$$ -1 \geq v_t(u,b(t))+v_u(t,b(u)). $$
Neither vote is zero: $u\neq b(t)$ and $t\neq b(u)$. Hence their sum belongs to $\{-2,0,2\}$, and must equal $-2$. In particular,
$$ b(t)\succ_t u. $$
This covers all remaining types in $U$. $\square$

Consequently, once $U$ is specified, the odd-cycle components are uniquely determined: they must be the directed cycles of the first-choice map restricted to $U$, and every such cycle must have odd length at least three.

Equal-mass consequence

Suppose every type has mass $m=1/\tau$. A normal-form path beginning at an unlooped endpoint has
$$ e_1=m,\qquad e_2=0 $$
if it contains at least three vertices. It therefore cannot be a positive-support path of that length.

A two-vertex path with an endpoint loop would have loop mass $m-m=0$, so it cannot occur either.

Thus, for equal masses, a popular profile can always be chosen to consist only of:

  • isolated loops of mass $m$;
  • disjoint two-type edges of mass $m$;
  • odd cycles whose edges all have mass $m/2$.

6. Equal masses do not guarantee existence

Consider five types, each of mass $1/5$, with preferences
$$ \begin{array}{c|l} A&B\succ_A C\succ_A D\succ_A E\succ_A A\\ B&A\succ_B C\succ_B D\succ_B E\succ_B B\\ C&A\succ_C B\succ_C D\succ_C E\succ_C C\\ D&E\succ_D C\succ_D A\succ_D B\succ_D D\\ E&C\succ_E D\succ_E A\succ_E B\succ_E E. \end{array} $$
Every own type is ranked last.

Suppose a popular profile existed, and replace it by an equal-mass normal-form profile.

Case 1: there is a loop

There cannot be two loop types $\ell,h$: their potentials are zero, whereas pairing their cells has weight $2$, contradicting the certificate inequality.

A loop also cannot coexist with an odd cycle. Pair its cell with any better-partner cell $(t,b(t))$ of the cycle. The loop type prefers $t$ to its own type, so the weight is
$$ 1+v_t(\ell,b(t))\in\{0,2\}. $$
The potential sum is $-\tfrac12$, a contradiction.

Thus exactly one type $\ell$ has a loop, and the other four types form two disjoint edges.

For any nonloop type $t$ paired with $s$, comparison with the loop cell gives
$$ \alpha_{ts}\geq1+v_t(\ell,s). $$
The upper bound $\alpha_{ts}\leq1$ forces
$$ s\succ_t\ell,\qquad \alpha_{ts}\geq0. $$
Both endpoints of every edge have nonnegative potentials, whose sum is zero. Hence all nonloop potentials are zero.

Types $A$ and $B$ must be paired. If one were the loop type, the other would prefer it to its assigned partner, contradicting the preceding condition. If neither were the loop type but they were not paired together, their cells would have weight $2$ and potential sum zero.

Among $C,D,E$, one type must therefore have a loop and the other two must be paired. Each possibility violates the condition that every paired type prefers its partner to the loop type:

  • If $C$ and $D$ are paired, then $D$ prefers the loop type $E$.
  • If $D$ and $E$ are paired, then $E$ prefers the loop type $C$.
  • If $E$ and $C$ are paired, then $C$ prefers the loop type $D$.

This excludes the loop case.

Case 2: there are no loops

The normal form must be either one five-cycle, or one three-cycle and one disjoint edge.

A five-cycle is impossible by the first-choice property: $A$’s first choice is $B$ and $B$’s first choice is $A$, producing a directed two-cycle rather than a directed five-cycle.

The only possible three-cycle is on $\{C,D,E\}$. To check this exhaustively:

  • A triple containing both $A$ and $B$ has their mutual first-choice pair.
  • A triple containing exactly one of $A,B$ and containing $C$ has a mutual first-choice pair between $C$ and that type.
  • A triple containing exactly one of $A,B$ and excluding $C$ has the mutual first-choice pair $D,E$.
  • The remaining triple is $\{C,D,E\}$, whose cyclic order is $C\to D\to E\to C$.

Thus the profile must use the three-cycle $CDE$ and edge $AB$. Write
$$ \alpha_{AB}=p,\qquad\alpha_{BA}=-p. $$
The better-partner cell $(C,D)$ has potential $-\tfrac12$.

Pairing cells $(C,D)$ and $(A,B)$ has weight zero: $C$ improves and $A$ worsens. Therefore
$$ p-\frac12\geq0. $$
Pairing cells $(C,D)$ and $(B,A)$ also has weight zero. Therefore
$$ -p-\frac12\geq0. $$
These inequalities are incompatible.

All cases have been excluded. This society has no popular feasible pairing.

There is also an equal-mass counterexample with even support size. Add a sixth type $F$, give all six types mass $1/6$, append $F$ last to each displayed order, and let $F$ rank itself first. An active edge $tF$ would force
$$ \alpha_{tF}\geq1,\qquad\alpha_{Ft}\geq1, $$
because both endpoints prefer own type to the other endpoint’s type. This contradicts antisymmetry. Hence $F$ must pair internally. Restricting the certificate to the other five types and multiplying their profile by $6/5$ would produce a popular profile for the five-type society just proved impossible.

7. Complexity consequences and the remaining question

The normal form gives a particularly explicit $\mathrm{NP}$ certificate.

Guess a spanning support graph of the permitted forms. It has at most $\tau$ unordered edges, counting loops. Compute all its masses using the formulas in Section 3, and verify positivity and the row equations. Then guess the off-diagonal potentials from
$$ \left\{-1,-\frac12,0,\frac12,1\right\}, $$
set reciprocal potentials to their negatives and diagonal potentials to zero, and check every certificate inequality.

All calculations have polynomial bit complexity. For example, the product of the input mass denominators has bit length at most $L$; twice that product is a common denominator for every mass in the normal form. There are only $O(\tau^2)$ certificate inequalities.

Thus existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. Exhaustively guessing at most two partner types per type and then these constant-size potentials gives an exact algorithm with running time
$$ 2^{O(\tau\log\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$

The remaining obstacle is discrete support selection. The new restrictions eliminate even cycles, determine masses explicitly, and determine all odd cycles once their vertex set is chosen. They do not yet provide a polynomial-time method for choosing the remaining paths or edges, nor an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.
