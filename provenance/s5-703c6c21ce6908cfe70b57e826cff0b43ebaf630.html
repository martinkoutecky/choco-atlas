The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\), rational masses \(\mu,\nu\), strict rankings of opposite-side types, and a target \((a^*,b^*)\), decide whether \(\max x_{a^*b^*}>0\) over mass-matching matrices \(x\geq 0\) with row sums \(\mu\), column sums \(\nu\), and, for every \((a,b)\), \(R_{ab}(x)=0\) or \(S_{ab}(x)=0\). Here \(R_{ab}(x)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(S_{ab}(x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\), so stability rules out positive mass on both sides preferring each other to their assigned types.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\) be nonempty, disjoint finite sets of types. Each type has a strict, complete ranking of all opposite-side types; every pair is acceptable. Write \(b\succ_a b'\) when \(a\) prefers \(b\) to \(b'\), and analogously for \(\succ_b\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. The masses satisfy \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\). Let \(\tau=|\mathcal A|+|\mathcal B|\) be the total support size, not the number of possible types, \(m=\max\{|\mathcal A|,|\mathcal B|\}\), and \(L\) the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A mass matching is a real matrix \(x\geq0\) with \(\sum_bx_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_ax_{ab}=\nu_b\). Define \(R_{ab}(x)=\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(S_{ab}(x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\). It is stable exactly when \(R_{ab}(x)=0\) or \(S_{ab}(x)=0\) for every pair \((a,b)\), including pairs receiving positive mass.
Given a target \((a^*,b^*)\in\mathcal A\times\mathcal B\), is deciding whether some stable mass matching satisfies \(x_{a^*b^*}>0\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
Nearest known results: discrete stable-pair membership has complexity \(\Theta(n^2)\), with unit-time access to preference and ranking matrix entries ([Ng–Hirschberg](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/82n213qg)). For the broader capacitated stable-allocation model, minimum-cost stable allocation is polynomial-time solvable ([Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)); this is an optimization result rather than a separately stated positive-entry membership result.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each side's positive rational masses to total one, preserving the equal-total-mass requirement corresponding to complete discrete matchings.
  • Required nonempty supports, complete strict rankings, and universal acceptability, matching the discrete domain.
  • Specified real allocation entries and applied the stability condition to every pair, including positively allocated pairs.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size, m as the larger side's support size, and L as total input bit length.
  • Expressed the positive maximum question equivalently as existence of a stable matching with a positive target entry.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). More specifically, it can be solved using \(O(m^2\tau)\) arithmetic operations, with a conservative bit-complexity bound of

\(O\!\left(\tau L^2+m^2\tau(L+\log\tau)\right).\)

The proof uses the rotation representation of stable allocations. All allocations constructed by the algorithm have the original support size; no expansion into individual agents is required.

1. Equivalence with capacitated stable allocation

Put an edge between every \(a\in\mathcal A\) and \(b\in\mathcal B\), give the vertices quotas \(\mu_a,\nu_b\), and give edge \((a,b)\) capacity

\(u_{ab}=\min\{\mu_a,\nu_b\}.\)

These edge capacities impose no additional restriction on a mass matching: its row and column sums already imply \(x_{ab}\leq u_{ab}\).

In the stable-allocation model, an edge blocks precisely when it is unsaturated and both endpoints have positive allocation to strictly worse partners. Thus its blocking condition is

\(x_{ab}<u_{ab},\qquad R_{ab}(x)>0,\qquad S_{ab}(x)>0.\)

This is the definition in Section 2 of [Dean–Munshi, *Faster Algorithms For Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

The saturation qualification does not change stability in our instance. Indeed, suppose \(x_{ab}=u_{ab}\).

  • If \(\mu_a\leq\nu_b\), then \(x_{ab}=\mu_a\). Nonnegativity and the row-sum constraint force every other entry in row \(a\) to be zero, so \(R_{ab}(x)=0\).
  • If \(\nu_b\leq\mu_a\), the column-sum constraint similarly gives \(S_{ab}(x)=0\).

Consequently, any pair with both \(R_{ab}(x)>0\) and \(S_{ab}(x)>0\) is automatically unsaturated. The two stability definitions therefore agree, including on pairs receiving positive mass.

2. Checking the dummy-vertex convention

For completeness, the reduction also works with the dummy vertices used in Dean–Munshi.

Add a left dummy \(a_0\) and a right dummy \(b_0\), each of quota \(1\). Every original vertex ranks its opposite dummy last. Each dummy ranks all original opposite vertices in an arbitrary strict order, followed by the other dummy. Give the new edges capacities

\(u_{a b_0}=\mu_a,\qquad u_{a_0 b}=\nu_b,\qquad u_{a_0b_0}=1.\)

We claim that stable allocations of this augmented instance correspond exactly to stable mass matchings.

Let \(z\) be an augmented stable allocation and write

\(t=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A,\ b\in\mathcal B}z_{ab}.\)

The original vertex quotas imply

\(\sum_a z_{a b_0}=1-t=\sum_b z_{a_0 b}.\)

If \(t<1\), there are original vertices \(a,b\) with \(z_{a b_0}>0\) and \(z_{a_0 b}>0\). Their quota constraints imply

\(z_{ab}<\mu_a,\qquad z_{ab}<\nu_b,\)

so their edge is unsaturated. Moreover, each prefers the other to its dummy partner. Hence \((a,b)\) blocks, a contradiction.

Thus \(t=1\). All original-to-dummy allocations vanish, and \(z_{a_0b_0}=1\). Restricting \(z\) to the original vertices gives a stable mass matching.

Conversely, extend any stable mass matching by assigning mass \(1\) to \((a_0,b_0)\). Original edges cannot block by Section 1. An edge joining an original vertex to a dummy cannot block because that original vertex ranks the dummy last and has no allocation to a worse partner. The dummy-to-dummy edge is saturated. The extension is therefore stable.

3. The rotation theorem used

We use the following consequence of Dean–Munshi’s Lemmas 10 and 12 and their algorithms in Sections 3–4.

For a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences, \(N\) vertices and \(E\) edges, one can construct stable allocations

\(x^{(0)},x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(k)},\qquad k\leq 2E,\)

and vectors \(d_1,\ldots,d_k\), with positive numbers \(r_1,\ldots,r_k\), such that

\(x^{(i)}=x^{(i-1)}+r_i d_i.\)

Each \(d_i\) describes an alternating cycle, increasing its forward edges and decreasing its reverse edges. Every stable allocation \(x\) has a representation

\(x=x^{(0)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_i d_i,\qquad 0\leq y_i\leq r_i.\)

There are additional precedence restrictions on the coefficients, which we will not need. The whole sequence can be generated in \(O(EN)\) arithmetic operations using the implementation without dynamic trees. [Dean–Munshi, Sections 3–4](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Our augmented instance satisfies the hypotheses: it is finite and bipartite, its preferences are strict, and its quotas and capacities are positive rationals. Section 2 checks precisely the dummy convention. In particular, the theorem also supplies existence of a stable mass matching.

4. A direct positive-entry test

Lemma. For any edge \(e\), some stable allocation has \(x_e>0\) if and only if \(x^{(i)}_e>0\) for at least one allocation in the sequence above.

Proof. If \(x^{(i)}_e>0\), that allocation itself is a witness.

For the converse, suppose \(x^{(i)}_e=0\) for every \(i\). For each rotation,

\(0=x^{(i)}_e-x^{(i-1)}_e=r_i(d_i)_e.\)

Since \(r_i>0\), it follows that \((d_i)_e=0\). Every stable allocation \(x\) consequently satisfies

\(x_e=x^{(0)}_e+\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_i(d_i)_e=0.\)

This proves the converse. \(\square\)

The algorithm is now explicit: construct the sequence for the augmented instance and accept exactly when the target entry is positive in one of its allocations. By Sections 1–2 and the lemma, this answers the question correctly.

5. Binary encoding and running time

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Because their encodings are included in \(L\), the binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Multiply every quota and edge capacity by \(D\).

This scaling preserves feasibility, stability, and positivity. The scaled instance has integer data and total mass \(D\) on each original side.

The sequence construction uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of allocations and residual capacities. Starting with integer data, these operations produce integer allocations and integer rotation amounts. Every allocation or residual capacity lies between \(0\) and \(D\); sums are bounded by the augmented total \(2D\). Thus numerical quantities have \(O(L)\) bits. Indices and counters need \(O(\log\tau)\) bits.

The augmented graph has

\(N=\tau+2,\qquad E=(|\mathcal A|+1)(|\mathcal B|+1)=O(m^2).\)

Its construction and sequence generation therefore require \(O(m^2\tau)\) arithmetic operations. Testing the target entry during the sequence adds only constant work per step.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, computing \(D\) and scaling all masses costs at most \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations. Constructing capacities and generating the sequence then costs

\(O\!\left(m^2\tau(L+\log\tau)\right)\)

bit operations. Reading and restricting preference lists to supported types is covered by this bound and the preprocessing term.

These bounds depend on the support and input bit length, not on the numerical value of \(D\). Exact integer comparisons decide positivity. Indeed, whenever the answer is affirmative, the algorithm produces a witness whose original target entry is at least \(1/D\).

The decision problem is therefore polynomial-time solvable.
