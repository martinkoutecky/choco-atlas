The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational internal opinions \(s_t\in[0,1]\), a symmetric nonnegative rational interaction kernel \(q_{tu}\), a leader-mass budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), and accuracy \(\eta>0\), choose \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\). Define the ordinary-agent equilibrium by \(z_t(x)=\frac{s_t+\sum_{u\in T}q_{tu}(\mu_u-x_u)z_u(x)}{1+\sum_{u\in T}q_{tu}\mu_u}\), and let \(H_\mu(x)=\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)z_t(x)\). Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|T|\), the input bit length, and \(1/\eta\) that returns a feasible rational \(x\) with \(g(x)\ge(1-1/e)(g^*-\eta)\), where \(g(x)=H_\mu(0)-H_\mu(x)\) and \(g^*=\max_{\sum_t y_t=\kappa,\ 0\le y_t\le\mu_t}g(y)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a rational internal opinion \(s_t\in[0,1]\). Interactions are specified by symmetric nonnegative rational intensities \(q_{tu}\); diagonal entries describe interactions within a type. The graph of positive off-diagonal interactions is connected. Let \(m\) count nonzero unordered interaction pairs, including diagonal pairs, and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Given rational \(\kappa\in[0,1]\) and \(\eta>0\), a feasible allocation satisfies \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\). Mass \(x_t\) consists of leaders whose expressed opinion is fixed at \(0\); the remaining mass retains internal opinion \(s_t\).
For every feasible allocation, define ordinary-agent opinions by the unique solution \(z_t(x)=\frac{s_t+\sum_{u\in T}q_{tu}(\mu_u-x_u)z_u(x)}{1+\sum_{u\in T}q_{tu}\mu_u}\). The denominator includes leader mass. When \(x_t=\mu_t\), its auxiliary opinion contributes zero to the objective.
Set \(H_\mu(x)=\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)z_t(x)\), \(g(x)=H_\mu(0)-H_\mu(x)\), and \(g^*=\max\{g(y):0\le y_t\le\mu_t,\ \sum_t y_t=\kappa\}\).

Does an algorithm exist that, in expected time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\eta\), returns a feasible rational \(x\) satisfying \(g(x)\ge(1-1/e)(g^*-\eta)\) on every output?

Result

Yes. A deterministic algorithm suffices. It returns a feasible rational allocation satisfying \(g(x)\ge(1-1/e)(g^*-\eta)\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\eta\).

The proof uses a nonnegative walk expansion of the objective. A small initial allocation then bounds curvature independently of the interaction intensities and the smallest type mass.

1. The objective on the entire allocation box

Index the types by \(i\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Write
\(d_i=1+\sum_j q_{ij}\mu_j\), \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_i)\), and \(Q=(q_{ij})\).

Extend the opinion equation to every \(x\) in the box \(0\le x\le\mu\). Put
\(r=\mu-x\) and \(M(x)=D^{-1}Q\operatorname{diag}(r)\).
Then the equation is
\(z(x)=D^{-1}s+M(x)z(x)\).

Every row sum of \(M(x)\) is at most
\[ \]
\( \displaystyle \rho:=\max_i\frac{d_i-1}{d_i}<1. \)

Consequently, its unique solution is
\( \displaystyle z(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}M(x)^\ell D^{-1}s. \)
In particular, the inverse of \(I-M(x)\) is entrywise nonnegative.

Moreover,
\( \displaystyle (I-M(x))\mathbf 1=D^{-1}(\mathbf 1+Qx)\ge D^{-1}s. \)
Multiplying by the nonnegative inverse gives \(0\le z(x)\le\mathbf 1\). Thus
\(0\le H_\mu(x)\le1\), and \(H_\mu(0)\le1\).

The inverse varies continuously on the box, so \(g\) is continuous there. The feasible set is nonempty and compact; hence the maximum defining \(g^*\) is attained.

2. Walk expansion and diminishing returns

Let \(P=D^{-1}Q\operatorname{diag}(\mu)\). For a finite type sequence
\(\gamma=(i_0,\ldots,i_\ell)\), define
\( \displaystyle A_\gamma=\mu_{i_0}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{\ell}P_{i_{a-1},i_a}\right)\frac{s_{i_\ell}}{d_{i_\ell}}. \)
All these coefficients are nonnegative.

Expanding the preceding Neumann series gives
\( \displaystyle H_\mu(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\ \sum_{\gamma=(i_0,\ldots,i_\ell)} A_\gamma\prod_{a=0}^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{x_{i_a}}{\mu_{i_a}}\right). \)

In particular,
\( \displaystyle \sum_\gamma A_\gamma=H_\mu(0)\le1. \)
Repeated visits are retained in this expansion, so diagonal interactions are included.

Consider positive scales \(c_i\le\mu_i\), and define
\(a_i=c_i/\mu_i\) and \(F(w)=g(c_1w_1,\ldots,c_\tau w_\tau)\) on \(0\le w\le\mathbf 1\).
If \(N_i(\gamma)\) counts occurrences of type \(i\) in \(\gamma\), then
\( \displaystyle F(w)=\sum_\gamma A_\gamma\left[1-\prod_i(1-a_iw_i)^{N_i(\gamma)}\right]. \)

This series can be differentiated twice term by term. Indeed, the total coefficient mass of paths with \(\ell\) transitions is at most \(\rho^\ell\). Each first or second derivative of a product has absolute value at most \((\ell+1)^2\), because \(a_i\le1\). The series
\(\sum_{\ell\ge0}(\ell+1)^2\rho^\ell\) converges, giving uniform convergence of the derivative series on the cube.

It follows directly that
\( \displaystyle \partial_iF(w)\ge0,\qquad \partial_{ij}F(w)\le0 \quad\text{for all }i,j. \)
Thus \(F\) is monotone, and every coordinate marginal decreases when any coordinate increases. In particular,
\(0\le g(x)\le H_\mu(0)\le1\), so \(0\le g^*\le1\).

We will use the following consequence. For any \(w,u\) in the cube,
\( \displaystyle \nabla F(w)\cdot u\ge F(u)-F(w). \)

To prove it, let \(b=(u-w)_+\), coordinatewise. Then \(w+b=w\vee u\), so monotonicity gives \(F(w+b)\ge F(u)\). The signs of the second derivatives imply that the gradient decreases along the nonnegative direction \(b\). Therefore
\( \displaystyle F(u)-F(w)\le\int_0^1\nabla F(w+tb)\cdot b\,dt\le\nabla F(w)\cdot b\le\nabla F(w)\cdot u. \)
The last inequality uses \(0\le b\le u\) and \(\nabla F(w)\ge0\).

3. A curvature bound independent of the intensities

Suppose every \(w_i>0\). For one walk term, put \(p_i=a_iw_i\).

For \(i\ne j\), the absolute mixed second derivative of its product is
\( \displaystyle a_i a_jN_iN_j(1-p_i)^{N_i-1}(1-p_j)^{N_j-1}\prod_{h\ne i,j}(1-p_h)^{N_h}. \)

For any integer \(n\ge1\) and \(p\in[0,1]\),
\(np(1-p)^{n-1}\le1\): the expression is the probability of exactly one success in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials. Consequently, this mixed derivative is at most \(1/(w_iw_j)\).

Similarly, for \(n\ge2\),
\(n(n-1)p^2(1-p)^{n-2}\le2\), because half this expression is the probability of exactly two successes. Hence the absolute second derivative in coordinate \(i\) is at most \(2/w_i^2\).

These inequalities also cover endpoints by evaluating the corresponding polynomials; derivatives with insufficient occurrences are zero. Summing with the coefficients \(A_\gamma\), whose total is at most \(1\), proves
\( \displaystyle |\partial_{ij}F(w)|\le\frac{2}{w_iw_j}. \)

In particular, throughout the region \(w_i\ge\alpha>0\), every direction \(v\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfies
\( \displaystyle v^\mathsf T\nabla^2F(w)v\ge-\frac{2\tau^2}{\alpha^2}. \)

This bound contains no dependence on \(\rho\), the interaction intensities, or the minimum mass.

4. The rational algorithm

If \(\kappa=0\), return \(x=0\). If \(\eta\ge1\), return any feasible rational allocation: monotonicity gives \(g(x)\ge0\), while \(g^*\le1\), so the required inequality holds.

Henceforth assume \(0<\kappa\le1\) and \(0<\eta<1\).

Define
\( \displaystyle c_i=\min\{\mu_i,\kappa\},\quad \delta=\frac{\eta}{4},\quad \alpha=\frac{\delta}{\tau},\quad N=\left\lceil\frac{64\tau^4}{\eta^3}\right\rceil,\quad h=\frac{1-\delta}{N}. \)

Every feasible allocation has \(x_i\le c_i\). Also,
\(\kappa\le\sum_i c_i\le\tau\kappa\):
if some \(\mu_i\ge\kappa\), its corresponding \(c_i\) equals \(\kappa\); otherwise \(\sum_i c_i=\sum_i\mu_i=1\ge\kappa\).

Let
\( \displaystyle \mathcal P=\left\{v\in[0,1]^\tau:\sum_i c_iv_i=\kappa\right\},\qquad F(w)=g(cw). \)
The polytope \(\mathcal P\) is nonempty, and an optimal allocation corresponds to some \(w^*\in\mathcal P\).

Initialize \(w^{(0)}=\alpha\mathbf 1\). For \(j=0,\ldots,N-1\):

  • Compute \(\nabla F(w^{(j)})\) exactly.
  • Choose a maximizer \(v^{(j)}\in\mathcal P\) of \(\nabla F(w^{(j)})\cdot v\).
  • Set \(w^{(j+1)}=w^{(j)}+hv^{(j)}\).

The linear optimization in step 2 is fractional knapsack: sort the ratios
\(\partial_iF(w^{(j)})/c_i\), fill coordinates in that order, and use at most one partially filled coordinate. Break ties by index.

Every intermediate iterate satisfies
\( \displaystyle \alpha\le w_i^{(j)}\le\alpha+jh\le\alpha+1-\delta\le1 \)
and
\( \displaystyle \sum_i c_iw_i^{(j)}=\alpha\sum_i c_i+jh\kappa\le\delta\kappa+(1-\delta)\kappa=\kappa. \)

Finally, start with \(x=cw^{(N)}\) and add mass coordinatewise, respecting \(x_i\le\mu_i\), until \(\sum_i x_i=\kappa\). This is possible because the remaining capacity is \(1-\sum_i x_i\), at least the required additional mass. This final operation preserves rationality and can only increase \(g\).

5. Approximation guarantee

Write \(F_j=F(w^{(j)})\). Since \(w^*\in\mathcal P\), the maximizing direction satisfies
\( \displaystyle \nabla F(w^{(j)})\cdot v^{(j)}\ge\nabla F(w^{(j)})\cdot w^*\ge g^*-F_j. \)

Let \(C=\tau^2/\alpha^2\). The entire update segment has coordinates at least \(\alpha\). The curvature bound and Taylor’s formula with integral remainder therefore give
\( \displaystyle F_{j+1}\ge F_j+h\nabla F(w^{(j)})\cdot v^{(j)}-Ch^2\ge(1-h)F_j+hg^*-Ch^2. \)

Iterating this recurrence, using \(F_0\ge0\), yields
\( \displaystyle F_N\ge[1-(1-h)^N]g^*-Ch. \)
Since \((1-h)^N\le e^{-Nh}=e^{-(1-\delta)}\) and \(h\le1/N\),
\( \displaystyle F_N\ge(1-e^{-(1-\delta)})g^*-\frac{C}{N}. \)

Put \(a=1-1/e\). Because \(0<\delta<1\),
\( \displaystyle e^{-1+\delta}-e^{-1}=\int_0^\delta e^{-1+t}\,dt\le\delta. \)
Using \(g^*\le1\), we obtain
\( \displaystyle F_N\ge ag^*-\delta-\frac{C}{N}. \)

Here
\( \displaystyle C=\frac{\tau^2}{\alpha^2}=\frac{16\tau^4}{\eta^2},\qquad \frac{C}{N}\le\frac{\eta}{4}. \)
Consequently,
\( \displaystyle F_N\ge ag^*-\frac{\eta}{2}\ge a(g^*-\eta), \)
where the last inequality uses \(a>1/2\).

The final budget-filling operation only increases the objective. Thus the returned feasible allocation satisfies the requested guarantee.

6. Exact evaluation and polynomial bit complexity

The infinite walk series is used only in the proof. Gradients are computed by rational linear algebra.

At a current allocation \(x\), set \(r=\mu-x\) and
\(A=D-Q\operatorname{diag}(r)=D(I-M(x))\).
The preceding contraction argument proves that \(A\) is nonsingular. Solve
\(Az=s\) and \(A^\mathsf T b=r\).

Differentiating \(Az=s\), and then \(H_\mu(x)=r^\mathsf Tz\), gives
\( \displaystyle \frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}=z_i\left[1+(Q^\mathsf Tb)_i\right],\qquad \partial_iF(w)=c_i z_i\left[1+(Q^\mathsf Tb)_i\right]. \)
Thus each iteration requires two exact rational linear solves and rational comparisons.

It remains to exclude uncontrolled growth of iterate denominators. Let \(B\) be the product of the input denominators of the masses and \(\kappa\). Its bit length is at most \(L\). Every \(c_i\) is an integer multiple of \(1/B\).

The chosen knapsack direction, in mass coordinates \(y_i=c_iv_i\), has each coordinate equal to \(0\), \(c_i\), or, for the single partial coordinate, \(\kappa\) minus a sum of selected \(c_i\). Hence every \(y_i\) is an integer multiple of \(1/B\).

Write the rational tolerance as \(\eta=p/q\). The mass iterates obey
\( \displaystyle x^{(0)}=\frac{p}{4\tau q}c,\qquad x^{(j+1)}=x^{(j)}+\frac{4q-p}{4qN}y^{(j)}. \)
Therefore all their coordinates have a common denominator dividing \(4\tau qNB\). Their numerators are bounded by this denominator because the iterates lie in the allocation box. The final budget filling preserves this polynomial bit bound.

All matrix entries consequently have bit lengths polynomial in \(\tau,L,\log N\). A nonsingular rational linear system with such entries can be solved exactly in polynomial bit complexity: after clearing denominators, determinant and minor bit lengths are polynomial by Hadamard’s bound, and exact fraction-free elimination provides a polynomial-time implementation.

There are \(N=O(\tau^4/\eta^3)\) iterations. Matrix construction, linear solves, knapsack sorting, and final filling therefore take total time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\eta\).

The algorithm is deterministic, so its expected running time has the same bound, and the guarantee holds on every output.
