A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(P\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For distinct candidates in different parties, \(a\) beats \(b\) exactly when \(\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t>1/2\). Promise that every such comparison is tie-free.
Each party simultaneously chooses one nominee \(p_i\in C_i\) before play and retains that nominee throughout. Match winners advance up the tree; party \(i\) has utility \(1\) if its nominee wins the tournament and \(0\) otherwise. Only parties make strategic choices; the society is fixed.
Compute a pure Nash equilibrium profile \((p_1,\ldots,p_\ell)\), or report that none exists. Unilateral deviations may replace a party's nominee by any other member of that party.

The new result proved below is a parameterized algorithm. Extend the cross-party comparison table to a tournament on all candidates by orienting within-party pairs arbitrarily. If deleting $k$ candidates makes this tournament transitive, equilibrium computation is fixed-parameter tractable in $k$. In particular, it is polynomial-time solvable when $k=O(\log m)$, even for unrestricted $h$.

The proof also simplifies the general dynamic program: its states need not explicitly identify the party that will win the entire tournament.

1. Exact comparisons and representation equivalence

Write $a\triangleright b$ when $a$ beats $b$. Initially, this relation is defined only between different parties.

Suppose the rational masses are supplied as $\mu_t=a_t/b_t$. Set
$$ Q=\prod_{t\in T}b_t, \qquad A_t=a_t(Q/b_t). $$
Normalization gives $\sum_t A_t=Q$. Hence
$$ a\triangleright b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}A_t>Q. $$
The integers involved have $O(L)$ bits: the bit length of $Q$ is bounded by the sum of the denominator bit lengths, and $A_t=\mu_tQ\leq Q$.

After constructing candidate positions in each ranking, all cross-party comparisons can therefore be computed exactly in the conservative bound
$$ B=O(m^2\tau L^2) $$
bit operations, using schoolbook arithmetic. The tie-free promise ensures that every required pair has exactly one winner.

Conversely, every deterministic cross-party table admits a polynomial-size supported representation. Here is a proof, because this equivalence explains why the supported formulation alone does not settle the classification.

Extend the table to all pairs arbitrarily. For each intended orientation $a\triangleright b$, list the remaining candidates as $r_1,\ldots,r_{m-2}$ and introduce the rankings
$$ a\succ b\succ r_1\succ\cdots\succ r_{m-2}, $$
$$ r_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ r_1\succ a\succ b. $$
These two rankings give margin $2$ to $a$ against $b$. They give margin zero to every other pair: their orders disagree on pairs of remaining candidates and on pairs involving one remaining candidate and either $a$ or $b$.

Applying this construction to all $\binom m2$ pairs produces
$$ N_0=m(m-1) $$
ranking occurrences, with margin $2$ in every intended direction. Let $N$ be the least power of two with $N\geq N_0$. Both numbers are even. Pad with $(N-N_0)/2$ ranking-and-reverse pairs, each of which has zero margins.

Giving every occurrence mass $1/N$ yields winning support
$$ \frac{N+2}{2N}=\frac12+\frac1N. $$
Merge identical rankings by adding their masses. The resulting support consists of distinct rankings with positive dyadic masses. Since $N<2m(m-1)$, it has fewer than $2m(m-1)$ rankings and encoding length $O(m^3\log m)$.

The construction preserves parties, seeding, and all cross-party outcomes. Consequently, it preserves every profile’s winner and every unilateral deviation’s winner, and hence preserves equilibrium profiles exactly.

Thus the supported and explicit-table formulations are polynomially equivalent.

2. Recognition and the role of outside opponents

For a fixed nomination profile, consider party $i$’s leaf-to-root path. At each of its $h$ rounds, let $o_{i,r}$ be the winner of the sibling subtree.

These sibling subtrees exclude party $i$, so their winners do not change when only party $i$ changes its nominee. Therefore a replacement $q\in C_i$ wins the tournament exactly when
$$ q\triangleright o_{i,r} \qquad\text{for every }r\in\{1,\ldots,h\}. $$
Indeed, conditional on surviving the preceding rounds, $q$ faces precisely $o_{i,r}$ at round $r$.

If $g$ is the winning party, its utility is already $1$. Every other party has a profitable deviation precisely when some replacement beats every opponent on its path. Hence the profile is an equilibrium exactly when
$$ \forall i\ne g\;\forall q\in C_i\;\exists r: \quad o_{i,r}\triangleright q. $$
Compute all subtree winners once, then check this condition. Recognition takes
$$ O(\ell+mh) $$
table operations after preprocessing. Equilibrium existence therefore belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$: a profile is a polynomial-length certificate.

The algorithm below uses the following observation. For a collection $S$ of outside opponents, define its blocked set
$$ D(S)=\bigcup_{s\in S}N^+(s), \qquad N^+(s)=\{q\in P:s\triangleright q\}. $$
Here and subsequently, orient within-party pairs arbitrarily, producing a tournament on $P$; set $q\notin N^+(q)$.

Whenever $S$ consists of actual outside opponents of a subtree, every candidate in that subtree belongs to a different party from every member of $S$. Thus the added within-party orientations do not affect the interpretation:
$$ q\in D(S) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\text{ loses to at least one opponent in }S. $$
Only the blocked set matters, rather than the identities or order of all outside opponents separately.

3. A dynamic program with two exemption states

For a tree node $v$, let $P_v$ be the candidates belonging to parties below $v$. For $w\in P_v$, let $i(w)$ denote its party.

For any set $D\subseteq P$, define two predicates.

  • $F_0(v,w,D)$ holds if some profile below $v$ has winner $w$, and no party below $v$ can unilaterally nominate a candidate $q\notin D$ that wins subtree $v$.
  • $F_1(v,w,D)$ has the same meaning, except that party $i(w)$ is exempt from the deviation condition.

These are mathematical definitions for arbitrary $D$. They do not require $D$ to arise from actual outside opponents.

At the root $r$, equilibrium existence is equivalent to
$$ \exists w\in P:\ F_1(r,w,\varnothing). $$
To justify this, $q\notin\varnothing$ imposes no restriction. Thus $F_1$ forbids every winning deviation by every party other than the actual winning party. The latter already has utility $1$.

Leaf conditions

At the leaf of party $i$, every $q\in C_i$ wins the one-leaf subtree when nominated. Therefore, for every $w\in C_i$,
$$ F_0(v,w,D)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_i\subseteq D, $$
whereas
$$ F_1(v,w,D)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{true}. $$

Internal recurrence

Let $u,z$ be the children of $v$. For prospective child champions $a\in P_u$ and $b\in P_z$, put
$$ D_a=D\cup N^+(a), \qquad D_b=D\cup N^+(b). $$

If $a\triangleright b$, this pair witnesses $F_0(v,a,D)$ exactly when
$$ F_0(u,a,D_b)\ \land\ F_0(z,b,D_a), $$
and it witnesses $F_1(v,a,D)$ exactly when
$$ F_1(u,a,D_b)\ \land\ F_0(z,b,D_a). $$
If $b\triangleright a$, use the symmetric conditions:
$$ F_0(u,a,D_b)\ \land\ F_0(z,b,D_a) $$
for $F_0(v,b,D)$, and
$$ F_0(u,a,D_b)\ \land\ F_1(z,b,D_a) $$
for $F_1(v,b,D)$.

Each parent entry is true if at least one child-champion pair witnesses it.

Proof. Fix a party below $u$ and a replacement candidate $q$. The other child’s champion remains $b$. Thus $q$ wins subtree $v$ and lies outside $D$ exactly when it wins subtree $u$ and satisfies
$$ q\notin D \quad\text{and}\quad q\triangleright b. $$
The comparison is between different parties. Because it is tie-free, these conditions are equivalent to
$$ q\notin D\cup N^+(b)=D_b. $$
The analogous equivalence holds for parties below $z$.

For $F_0$, every party must be checked, giving two $F_0$ child states. For $F_1$ when $a$ wins the parent match, only party $i(a)$ is exempt. It belongs to $u$, giving an $F_1$ state in $u$ and an $F_0$ state in $z$.

Restricting a parent witness proves necessity. Conversely, combining witnessing child profiles proves sufficiency: their party sets are disjoint, their champions are $a,b$, and the equivalences above establish every required deviation condition. The symmetric case follows identically. $\square$

Storing a witnessing pair for each true internal entry permits reconstruction of a complete profile. If every eligible root entry is false, the recurrence proves nonexistence.

4. The general height-dependent bound

The recurrence recovers the unrestricted quasipolynomial algorithm without a global winning-party index.

At a node of depth $d$, represent its context by a set
$$ S\subseteq P\setminus P_v, \qquad |S|\leq d, $$
and interpret the state through $D(S)$. Moving to a child adds the sibling champion to $S$. The resulting context remains outside that child and has size at most $d+1$.

There are at most
$$ \sum_{j=0}^d\binom mj\leq(m+1)^d\leq(m+1)^h $$
contexts per node.

Two counting identities control the work:
$$ \sum_v |P_v|=m(h+1), $$
because each candidate occurs in one node per depth, and
$$ \sum_{\substack{v\text{ internal}\\u,z\text{ children of }v}} |P_u|\,|P_z| \leq \binom m2. $$
For the second identity, a pair of candidates in different leaves is counted exactly at the lowest common ancestor of their leaves.

For each context and child-champion pair, the recurrence updates both exemption states and the uniquely determined parent winner. Context copying and leaf membership checks cost $O(h+1)$. Including initialization, this gives the conservative bound
$$ O\!\left((h+1)m^2(m+1)^h\right) $$
table operations.

With exact-majority preprocessing, the overall bit-time bound is
$$ O(m^2\tau L^2)+m^{O(h)}\operatorname{poly}(\log m). $$
Since nonempty parties imply $\ell\leq m$ and $h=\log_2\ell$, this is polynomial for fixed $h$ and quasipolynomial in general. The corresponding unrestricted upper bound is also Theorem 1 of [Lisowski, Ramanujan, and Turrini](https://ifmas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2022/pdfs/p807.pdf).

5. New result: deleting few candidates to obtain transitivity

Theorem. Fix any extension of the cross-party outcomes to a tournament on $P$. Suppose $X\subseteq P$, with $|X|=k$, is supplied such that the tournament on
$$ G=P\setminus X $$
is transitive. Then equilibrium computation, including a correct nonexistence report, takes
$$ O\!\left( 4^k(m+1)\bigl[m^2(k+1)+m(h+1)\bigr] \right) $$
table operations after preprocessing.

Consequently, its bit complexity is bounded by
$$ O(m^2\tau L^2) + 4^{O(k)}\operatorname{poly}(m,h,\log m). $$

Compact blocked-set representation

Transitivity means that $G$ has a strict total order $\prec$ satisfying
$$ a\triangleright b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\prec b \qquad(a,b\in G,\ a\ne b). $$
Earlier candidates are stronger.

For any opponent set $S\subseteq P$, record:

  • $s$, the earliest member of $S\cap G$, or $\bot$ if there is none;
  • $U=S\cap X$;
  • $V=D(S)\cap X$.

For $q\in G$, transitivity gives
$$ q\in D(S) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigl(s\ne\bot\text{ and }s\prec q\bigr) \ \lor\ \exists x\in U:\ x\triangleright q. $$
Indeed, among the good opponents $S\cap G$, their earliest member defeats every candidate defeated by any later member. For $q\in X$, membership in $D(S)$ is recorded directly by $V$.

Thus the triple $(s,U,V)$ determines the entire blocked set.

For all triples, including ones not realized by an opponent set, define
$$ \mathcal D(s,U,V) = V \ \cup\ \{q\in G:s\ne\bot,\ s\prec q\} \ \cup\ \{q\in G:\exists x\in U,\ x\triangleright q\}. $$
There are at most
$$ R=(|G|+1)4^k\leq(m+1)4^k $$
such signatures. Distinct signatures may describe the same set; this causes no difficulty.

Closure under adding a sibling champion

Given a signature $(s,U,V)$ and candidate $b$, define the updated signature as follows.

  • If $b\in G$, replace $s$ by the earlier of $s$ and $b$, treating $\bot$ as absent; leave $U$ unchanged.
  • If $b\in X$, leave $s$ unchanged and replace $U$ by $U\cup\{b\}$.
  • In both cases, replace $V$ by

$$ V\cup\bigl(N^+(b)\cap X\bigr). $$

This update satisfies
$$ \mathcal D(\text{updated signature}) = \mathcal D(s,U,V)\cup N^+(b). $$
For candidates in $X$, this follows from the update to $V$. For candidates in $G$, adding a good candidate adds a suffix of the total order, while adding an exceptional candidate adds exactly its defeated good candidates.

Importantly, the equality holds for every signature, not merely realizable ones. Hence the signature family is closed under every context update required by the recurrence.

The empty blocked set is represented by
$$ (\bot,\varnothing,\varnothing). $$

Correctness and running time

Run the two-state dynamic program using signatures instead of explicit blocked sets. Their exact set semantics and closure identity make every recurrence from Section 3 applicable. Induction on subtree height therefore proves that every computed entry has precisely its stated meaning.

Membership tests and signature updates take $O(k+1)$ elementary operations using $k$-bit vectors and the order on $G$. Updates can also be precomputed for every signature-candidate pair.

There are $R$ signatures. The counting identities from Section 4 give:

  • $O(Rm(h+1))$ state initialization work;
  • $O(Rm(k+1))$ leaf-condition work;
  • $O(Rm^2(k+1))$ recurrence work;
  • $O(Rm(k+1))$ work to precompute signature updates.

Substituting $R\leq(m+1)4^k$ gives the theorem’s stated table-operation bound. State indices and signatures have polynomial bit length in $k$, $h$, and $\log m$, yielding the displayed fixed-parameter bit bound. Backtracking takes polynomial additional time. $\square$

6. Finding the deletion set

The set $X$ need not be supplied. For the fixed tournament extension, one can find a suitable set of size at most $k$, or prove that none exists, in
$$ O(3^k m^3) $$
table operations.

First, a tournament is transitive exactly when it has no directed triangle.

To prove the nontrivial direction, suppose a tournament has a directed cycle and choose one of shortest length. If its length exceeds three, write its first three vertices as $v_1,v_2,v_3$. If $v_3\triangleright v_1$, they form a directed triangle. Otherwise $v_1\triangleright v_3$, and bypassing $v_2$ produces a shorter directed cycle. Thus every cyclic tournament has a directed triangle. An acyclic tournament has a topological order, which is a transitive order because every pair is adjacent.

The deletion algorithm finds a directed triangle. Any deletion set producing a transitive tournament must delete at least one of its three vertices. Branch on these three choices and decrease the remaining budget by one.

Each node searches for a triangle in $O(m^3)$ time. The search tree has $O(3^k)$ nodes. A triangle-free residual tournament supplies the required transitive order. Trying budgets successively up to the minimum successful value retains the same asymptotic bound.

Combining this search with Section 5 gives a fixed-parameter algorithm where $k$ is the minimum deletion-set size for the chosen extension.

In particular, if $k\leq c\log_2m$ for a fixed constant $c$, then
$$ 4^k\leq m^{2c}, $$
so the resulting algorithm is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, even when $h$ varies.

The unrestricted hypotheses impose no bound on this deletion parameter. Therefore the new algorithm does not establish unrestricted polynomial-time solvability.

The following conclusions are proved:

  • The supported and explicit-table formulations are polynomially equivalent.
  • Recognition is polynomial, and equilibrium existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.
  • General computation takes $O(m^2\tau L^2)+m^{O(h)}\operatorname{poly}(\log m)$ time.
  • Computation is fixed-parameter tractable in the number of candidates whose deletion makes a chosen tournament extension transitive.

Neither an unrestricted polynomial algorithm nor an NP-hardness reduction is established here. A polynomial many-one NP-hardness proof for existence, combined with the general quasipolynomial algorithm, would imply deterministic quasipolynomial algorithms for every language in $\mathrm{NP}$; that consequence is not a contradiction and does not rule out hardness.
