The continuous mirror question, as graded

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here masses are sought, rather than supplied: \(\mu_A\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\), \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T}\mu_A=1\), and \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subseteq\mathcal T\). Thus \(\tau\) is the actual support size, not the number of possible approval sets.
For a size-\(k\) committee \(W\), define \(u_\mu(W)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal T}\mu_A|A\cap W|\) and \(r_\mu(W)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal T}\mu_A\mathbf1[A\cap W\neq\varnothing]\).
A PAV-winning committee maximizes \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T}\mu_A H(|A\cap W|)\) over all size-\(k\) committees, where \(H(0)=0\) and \(H(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}1/i\); every tied maximizer is eligible.
Write \(R_g(\mu,W)=g_\mu(W)/\max_{|V|=k}g_\mu(V)\), considering only societies with positive denominator. The ratio-threshold question asks whether some such rational society and PAV-winning \(W\) satisfy \(R_g(\mu,W)\leq q\).
The support-restricted approximation guarantee is \(\inf_{\mu}\min_{W\text{ PAV-winning}}R_g(\mu,W)\), with the infimum over these societies. State any running times in \(m,|\mathcal T|,\tau,k,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.

Exact guarantees for a prescribed approval family

For every supplied family \(\mathcal T\), both guarantees are rational, exactly computable, and attained by rational masses. In particular, the ratio-threshold question has answer yes exactly when the supplied threshold is at least the corresponding guarantee, including equality.

The values depend on \(\mathcal T\). Below is an explicit linear-program formula computing them. I also prove the sharp unrestricted representation guarantee
\(\frac{k}{2k-1}\), and give a nontrivial family for which both support-restricted guarantees have explicit formulas.

1. An exact linear-program formula

Remove the empty type and write
\(\mathcal T_+=\mathcal T\setminus\{\varnothing\}\),
\(t=|\mathcal T_+|\), and
\(\mathcal K=\{X\subseteq C:|X|=k\}\).
By hypothesis, \(t\geq1\). Let \(N=|\mathcal K|=\binom{m}{k}\).

For \(A\in\mathcal T_+\) and \(X\in\mathcal K\), define
\(h_{A,X}=H(|A\cap X|)\),
\(b^u_{A,X}=|A\cap X|\), and
\(b^r_{A,X}=\mathbf1[A\cap X\neq\varnothing]\).

Fix \(g\in\{u,r\}\). For each ordered pair \(W,V\in\mathcal K\), form the following rational linear program, with variables \(x_A\), \(A\in\mathcal T_+\):

Objective: minimize
\(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T_+}b^g_{A,W}x_A\).

Constraints:

  • \(x_A\geq0\) for every \(A\in\mathcal T_+\);
  • \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T_+}b^g_{A,V}x_A=1\);
  • \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T_+}(h_{A,W}-h_{A,X})x_A\geq0\) for every \(X\in\mathcal K\);
  • \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T_+}(b^g_{A,V}-b^g_{A,X})x_A\geq0\) for every \(X\in\mathcal K\).

Let \(\lambda_g(W,V)\) be its optimum, assigning value \(+\infty\) to an infeasible program. Then the exact support-restricted guarantee is

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_g(C,\mathcal T,k)=\min_{W,V\in\mathcal K}\lambda_g(W,V).\)

Thus this is an exact formula for both requested guarantees, using only finitely many ordinary rational linear programs. No strict inequality or limiting optimization is needed.

Proof of the formula

First, removing the empty type preserves every relevant ratio. Indeed, positive denominator is equivalent to positive total mass
\(\alpha=\sum_{A\neq\varnothing}\mu_A\).
For such a society, set \(\widehat\mu_A=\mu_A/\alpha\) on nonempty types and set its empty-type mass to zero. Every committee’s PAV score and both objective values are divided by \(\alpha\). Consequently, the winners and ratios remain unchanged.

Now take a normalized society on \(\mathcal T_+\), a PAV winner \(W\), and a committee \(V\) maximizing \(g_\mu\). Its optimum \(d=g_\mu(V)\) is positive. Setting \(x_A=\mu_A/d\) satisfies all the displayed constraints, and the linear-program objective is exactly \(R_g(\mu,W)\).

Conversely, let \(x\) be feasible and put \(s=\sum_Ax_A\). The normalization constraint implies \(s>0\). Define \(\mu_A=x_A/s\). The committee inequalities make \(W\) PAV-winning and \(V\) optimal for \(g\), with \(g_\mu(V)=1/s>0\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle R_g(\mu,W)=\sum_A b^g_{A,W}x_A.\)

These two constructions establish the formula, provided the linear-program minima are attained. That follows from boundedness, as shown next.

Boundedness and nonemptiness

For any nonempty \(A\), a uniformly random \(X\in\mathcal K\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E|A\cap X|=\frac{k|A|}{m}\geq\frac{k}{m}.\)

Also, fixing any \(a\in A\),

\(\displaystyle \Pr(A\cap X\neq\varnothing)\geq\Pr(a\in X)=\frac{k}{m}.\)

Hence, for either objective and any nonnegative vector \(x\),

\(\displaystyle \frac1N\sum_{X\in\mathcal K}\sum_A b^g_{A,X}x_A\geq\frac{k}{m}\sum_Ax_A.\)

In a feasible program, every committee’s \(g\)-value is at most the normalized value \(1\) of \(V\). Thus \(\sum_Ax_A\leq m/k\). Each feasible set is therefore closed and bounded.

At least one program is feasible: put all society mass on any nonempty allowed type, choose a PAV winner and a \(g\)-optimal committee, and apply the preceding construction. Every feasible objective value lies in \((0,1]\), since \(W\) has positive PAV score and \(V\) is \(g\)-optimal.

2. Rational attainment and actual support size

The preceding formula already proves attainment over real masses. The following argument proves rational attainment and gives a stronger bound on the support of a worst-case society.

For a committee \(W\), define its winner polytope

\(\displaystyle P_W=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\mathcal T_+}:\sum_A\mu_A=1,\ \sum_A(h_{A,W}-h_{A,X})\mu_A\geq0\text{ for every }X\in\mathcal K\right\}.\)

Every nonempty \(P_W\) is a bounded rational polyhedron. We use the elementary polytope theorem: such a polyhedron is the convex hull of finitely many vertices, and every vertex is the unique solution of a full-rank subsystem of active constraints. Rational coefficients therefore give rational vertices.

For every normalized society on \(\mathcal T_+\), the averaging calculation above gives
\(\max_Xg_\mu(X)\geq k/m>0\).
If \(\mu\in P_W\), then \(W\) also has positive \(g\)-value: some committee has positive PAV score, so its PAV-winning score is positive.

Let \(Z_W\) denote the vertex set of a nonempty \(P_W\), and put
\(\gamma_W=\min_{z\in Z_W}R_g(z,W)\).
For every vertex \(z\) and every committee \(V\),

\(\displaystyle g_z(W)\geq\gamma_W\max_Xg_z(X)\geq\gamma_Wg_z(V).\)

Write any \(\mu\in P_W\) as a convex combination
\(\mu=\sum_{z\in Z_W}\theta_z z\).
Both committee values are linear in the masses, so summing the preceding inequality gives
\(g_\mu(W)\geq\gamma_Wg_\mu(V)\) for every \(V\).
Taking a maximizing \(V\) proves \(R_g(\mu,W)\geq\gamma_W\).

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_g(C,\mathcal T,k)=\min_{\substack{W\in\mathcal K\\P_W\neq\varnothing}}\ \min_{z\in Z_W}R_g(z,W).\)

This is a finite minimum of rational numbers, attained at a rational society.

Support and bit-length bounds

Suppose a vertex \(z\in P_W\) has actual support size \(\tau\). Restrict its active equations to its positive coordinates. They must have rank \(\tau\); otherwise a sufficiently small nonzero perturbation in their nullspace would preserve positivity and all inactive strict inequalities, contradicting extremality.

There is one normalization equation and at most \(N-1\) nontrivial winner inequalities. Thus

\(\displaystyle \tau\leq\min\{t,N\}.\)

For an explicit bit bound, let \(D=k!\). Every \(DH(j)\), \(0\leq j\leq k\), is an integer. Clearing denominators makes the winner coefficients integers of absolute value at most \(Dk\).

The normalization row is independent of the active homogeneous winner rows: its scalar product with \(z\) is \(1\), whereas theirs is zero. Hence one can determine the positive coordinates using normalization and \(\tau-1\) independent active winner equations. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound
\(|\det M|\leq\tau!(Dk)^\tau\)
show that each mass has numerator and denominator bit lengths

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(\tau\bigl(k\log(k+1)+\log(\tau+1)\bigr)\right).\)

The same order of bit length suffices for the resulting ratio. In particular, unrestricted rational denominators are unnecessary: a worst-case witness has polynomially bounded encoding length in \(t\) and \(k\).

3. Exact algorithms and running times

Enumerating committees and their coefficients takes
\(O(mt+Nt\,k)\) elementary operations when approval types are stored as membership vectors.

Each linear program in Section 1 has \(t\) variables, at most \(2N-2\) nontrivial committee inequalities, one normalization equation, and nonnegativity constraints. Its coefficient bit lengths are \(O(k\log(k+1))\).

Applying the standard polynomial-bit-complexity theorem for rational linear programming therefore computes the exact guarantee in time

\(\displaystyle N^2\operatorname{poly}(N,m,|\mathcal T|,k,L),\qquad N=\binom{m}{k}.\)

The theorem applies because the programs have explicitly listed rational coefficients and, when feasible, bounded feasible sets. For fixed \(k\), this running time is polynomial in \(m,|\mathcal T|,L\); the displayed algorithm is exponential in general.

One can alternatively enumerate vertices of each \(P_W\). Choosing \(t-1\) active inequalities, together with normalization, gives the bound

\(\displaystyle N\binom{t+N-1}{t-1}\operatorname{poly}(N,m,t,k,L).\)

Singular systems are discarded, and each remaining solution is checked against every constraint.

For a proposed society with actual support \(\tau\) and maximum mass bit length \(B\), exhaustive verification of PAV optimality and the ratio takes

\(\displaystyle O\!\left((m\tau+N\tau k)\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau B+k\log(k+1))\right)\)

bit operations. Here \(\tau\) is the support actually supplied in the witness, not the number of allowable types.

4. The sharp unrestricted representation guarantee

For every allowed family,

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_r(C,\mathcal T,k)\geq\frac{k}{2k-1}.\)

This bound is attained by a rational society when the required types are allowed. Thus the unrestricted representation guarantee is exactly \(\frac{k}{2k-1}\).

Exchange proof

Fix a society, a PAV winner \(W\), and a representation-optimal committee \(V\). Choose a bijection \(f:W\to V\) fixing every member of \(W\cap V\). Each nonidentity pair defines a valid size-\(k\) swap; identity pairs leave \(W\) unchanged. PAV optimality implies that the sum of the score changes of these \(k\) swaps is nonpositive.

For one approval type \(A\), write
\(w=|A\cap W|\),
\(v=|A\cap V|\), and
\(a=|\{c\in W:c\in A,\ f(c)\in A\}|\).
Let \(\delta_A\) be its summed contribution to the swaps.

If \(w=0\), then \(\delta_A=v\).

If \(w>0\), there are \(v-a\) pairs adding an approved candidate without removing one, and \(w-a\) pairs removing an approved candidate without adding one. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \delta_A=\frac{v-a}{w+1}-\frac{w-a}{w}.\)

Three bounds follow:

  • If \(w=0\) and \(v>0\), then \(\delta_A\geq1\).
  • If \(w>0\) and \(v=0\), then \(\delta_A=-1\).
  • If \(w>0\) and \(v>0\), then \(\delta_A\geq-(k-1)/k\).

For the third bound, when \(w<k\), the displayed expression equals
\(v/(w+1)-1+a/(w(w+1))\), which is at least
\(-w/(w+1)\geq-(k-1)/k\).
When \(w=k\), every outgoing candidate is approved, so \(a=v\), and the expression is
\(-(k-v)/k\geq-(k-1)/k\).

Let \(\rho\) be the mass represented by \(W\), let \(\eta\) be the mass represented by both \(W\) and \(V\), and let \(\xi\) be the mass represented by \(V\) but not \(W\). Summing the bounds and using PAV optimality gives

\(\displaystyle 0\geq\sum_A\mu_A\delta_A\geq\xi-(\rho-\eta)-\frac{k-1}{k}\eta=\xi-\rho+\frac{\eta}{k}.\)

Hence \(\xi+\eta/k\leq\rho\). Because \(\eta\leq\rho\),

\(\displaystyle r_\mu(V)=\xi+\eta\leq\rho+\frac{k-1}{k}\eta\leq\frac{2k-1}{k}\rho.\)

Dividing by the positive optimum proves the bound.

Rational equality example

Let \(C=X\cup Y\), with disjoint sets \(X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}\) and \(Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_k\}\). Allow \(X\) and all singleton types \(\{y_i\}\). Assign
\(\mu_X=1/2\) and \(\mu_{\{y_i\}}=1/(2k)\).

A committee containing \(j\) candidates from \(X\) has PAV score
\(F(j)=H(j)/2+(k-j)/(2k)\).
Its increments satisfy
\(F(j+1)-F(j)=1/(2(j+1))-1/(2k)\geq0\),
so \(W=X\) is a winner.

Its representation value is \(1/2\). A representation-optimal committee contains one candidate from \(X\) and \(k-1\) from \(Y\), giving optimum \((2k-1)/(2k)\). Thus the ratio is \(k/(2k-1)\). This equality construction also appears in the [cited paper](https://www.dbai.tuwien.ac.at/proj/sudema/papers/aij-guarantees.pdf).

In particular, for every fixed finite \(k\), the threshold \(1/2\) is unattainable.

5. Utilitarian guarantees and an explicit exact family

A general elementary lower bound is

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_u(C,\mathcal T,k)\geq\frac{H(k)}{k}.\)

Indeed, \(H(j)/j\) is nonincreasing for positive integers \(j\): the newly added term \(1/(j+1)\) is at most the preceding average \(H(j)/j\). Consequently,
\(H(j)\geq jH(k)/k\) for \(0\leq j\leq k\), while \(H(j)\leq j\).

Writing \(p_\mu\) for PAV score, a PAV winner \(W\) and utilitarian optimum \(V\) therefore satisfy

\(\displaystyle u_\mu(W)\geq p_\mu(W)\geq p_\mu(V)\geq\frac{H(k)}{k}u_\mu(V).\)

The cited finite-electorate lower bound also transfers exactly to rational masses. Clearing their denominators produces a finite electorate whose PAV scores and objective values are all multiplied by the same integer. Thus

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_u(C,\mathcal T,k)\geq\max\left\{\frac{H(k)}{k},\frac1{2+\sqrt{k}}\right\}.\)

The second bound is [Theorem 2 of the cited paper](https://www.dbai.tuwien.ac.at/proj/sudema/papers/aij-guarantees.pdf).

Exact guarantees for a party-and-singletons family

For the family
\(\mathcal T=\{X,\{y_1\},\ldots,\{y_k\}\}\)
used above, both exact guarantees are

\(\displaystyle \Gamma_r=\frac{k}{2k-1},\qquad \Gamma_u=\min_{0\leq p\leq k-1}\frac{p^2+k}{k(p+1)}.\)

The representation equality is already proved. Here is a proof of the utilitarian formula.

Write \(a=\mu_X\) and \(b_i=\mu_{\{y_i\}}\). Every candidate in \(X\) has approval score \(a\); candidate \(y_i\) has approval score \(b_i\).

Suppose a PAV winner \(W\) contains \(p\) candidates from \(X\).

If \(a=0\), PAV and utilitarian scores coincide. If \(p=0\), then \(W=Y\); swapping any \(y_i\) for an \(X\)-candidate implies \(b_i\geq a\), so \(W\) is utilitarian-optimal. If \(p=k\), swapping an \(X\)-candidate for any \(y_i\) implies \(b_i\leq a/k\leq a\), again making \(W\) utilitarian-optimal.

It remains to consider \(a>0\) and \(1\leq p\leq k-1\). Let \(S\) be the selected singleton candidates. Single-swap optimality gives

\(\displaystyle b_i\geq\frac{a}{p+1}\quad(i\in S),\qquad b_j\leq\frac{a}{p}\leq a\quad(j\notin S).\)

Let \(h\) be the number of singleton candidates with \(b_i>a\). All belong to \(S\). Write their total mass as \(ha+d\), where \(d\geq0\). The utilitarian optimum is exactly \(ka+d\): select these \(h\) candidates and fill the other positions with candidates from \(X\).

Meanwhile,

\(\displaystyle u_\mu(W)\geq pa+ha+d+\frac{k-p-h}{p+1}a =\frac{p^2+k}{p+1}a+\frac{hp}{p+1}a+d \geq\frac{p^2+k}{p+1}a+d.\)

Since \((p^2+k)/(p+1)\leq k\), adding \(d\geq0\) to numerator and denominator cannot decrease the ratio below

\(\displaystyle \frac{p^2+k}{k(p+1)}.\)

This proves the required lower bound for every society on this family.

For attainment, fix \(0\leq p\leq k-1\). Give \(X\) mass \((p+1)/(k+1)\), give \(k-p\) singleton types mass \(1/(k+1)\) each, and give the remaining singleton types mass zero.

A committee containing \(p\) candidates from \(X\) and all positive-mass singleton candidates is PAV-winning. To check this, committees with fewer than \(p\) \(X\)-candidates can replace a zero-mass singleton candidate by an \(X\)-candidate and improve. Starting at \(p\) \(X\)-candidates, the score increment from increasing their number from \(j\) to \(j+1\) is

\(\displaystyle \frac{p+1}{(k+1)(j+1)}-\frac1{k+1}.\)

It is zero at \(j=p\) and negative thereafter. Thus the stated committee is a global maximizer.

The utilitarian optimum consists of \(k\) candidates from \(X\), and the resulting ratio is exactly \((p^2+k)/(k(p+1))\). Its actual support has size \(k-p+1\). Choosing a minimizing \(p\) proves attainment of the formula.

6. Boundary thresholds

For either objective, the complete threshold characterization is

\(\displaystyle \text{a qualifying rational society exists}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\geq\Gamma_g(C,\mathcal T,k).\)

Equality is included because the exact guarantee has a rational attaining society. Thus no boundary gap arises from requiring rational masses.

Also, \(0<\Gamma_g\leq1\). Hence every \(q\leq0\) is a no-instance, and every \(q\geq1\) is a yes-instance. When \(k=1\), when \(k=m\), or when all nonempty allowable types are singletons, both guarantees equal \(1\).

The prescribed-family question is therefore settled by the exact formulas above; no closed formula for the unrestricted utilitarian worst case is asserted.
