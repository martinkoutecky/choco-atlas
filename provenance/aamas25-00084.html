Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a candidate set with \(m\geq1\), and let \(1\leq t\leq m\) be an input integer. A society is given by its support \(S\): the list of the \(\tau\) approval types \(A\subseteq C\) with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_A\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_{A\in S}\mu_A=1\). Here \(\tau=|S|\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible model consists of a partition \(C=C_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\dot\cup}C_t\) into nonempty groups and probabilities \(p_j\in[0,1]\); probabilities for different groups may coincide. Each candidate in \(C_j\) is approved independently with probability \(p_j\).
The optimization problem returns a partition and parameter vector maximizing \(F=\sum_{A\in S}\mu_A\sum_{j=1}^{t}\bigl(|A\cap C_j|\ln p_j+|C_j\setminus A|\ln(1-p_j)\bigr)\). Use \(0\ln0=0\), and interpret a positive coefficient times \(\ln0\) as \(-\infty\). Any maximizer is acceptable; optimal probabilities are returned exactly as binary rationals.

Is this exact optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,t,L\) in the Turing bit model, or is it NP-hard?

For an explicitly listed finite electorate, maximum-likelihood fitting of a single independent approval model with at most \(t\) approval probabilities is polynomial-time solvable. [Faliszewski et al.](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p704.pdf)
Rationally weighted fitting is known to be equivalent to fitting an induced finite electorate; the verified result does not establish a polynomial bound in the binary weight length. [Faliszewski et al., extended version](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18651v1)

2. Reduction to candidate marginals

For each candidate $c$, define its marginal approval probability by
$$ q_c=\sum_{\substack{A\in S\\c\in A}}\mu_A. $$
Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Its binary length is at most $L+1$, and every $Dq_c$ is an integer. Thus $D$ and all marginals can be computed using polynomial-time rational arithmetic.

Interchanging the finite sums in the objective gives
$$ F=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{c\in C_j} \bigl(q_c\ln p_j+(1-q_c)\ln(1-p_j)\bigr). $$
This identity respects the endpoint conventions: the coefficient of $\ln 0$ is positive in the original expression exactly when its aggregated coefficient is positive.

For a nonempty group $B$, write
$$ n_B=|B|, \qquad s_B=\sum_{c\in B}q_c. $$
Its contribution at parameter $p$ is
$$ s_B\ln p+(n_B-s_B)\ln(1-p). $$
If $0<s_B<n_B$, its derivative is
$$ \frac{s_B-n_Bp}{p(1-p)}. $$
Consequently, its unique maximizer is $p=s_B/n_B$. If $s_B=0$, the contribution is $n_B\ln(1-p)$ and uniquely maximized at $p=0$. If $s_B=n_B$, it is uniquely maximized at $p=1$.

Define
$$ f(x)=x\ln x+(1-x)\ln(1-x), \qquad f(0)=f(1)=0. $$
For a partition $\mathcal P$, its optimized score is therefore
$$ W(\mathcal P)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal P}w(B), \qquad w(B)=n_Bf\!\left(\frac{s_B}{n_B}\right). $$
Every partition has an attained finite optimized score. Since there are finitely many partitions, a global optimum exists.

Moreover, if
$$ a_B=\sum_{c\in B}Dq_c, $$
then its optimal parameter is
$$ p_B=\frac{a_B}{n_BD}. $$
Both numerator and denominator have binary length $O(L+\log m)$. Thus the exact output parameters always have polynomial-size encodings.

3. Sorted intervals and the dynamic program

Order the candidates so that
$$ q_1\leq q_2\leq\cdots\leq q_m. $$

Lemma. Some optimal partition consists of exactly $t$ nonempty intervals of this order.

Proof. Take an optimal partition, and order its groups by their means. Suppose groups $B,E$ have sizes $n,r$ and means $u\leq v$, but contain marginals $x\in B$ and $y\in E$ with $x>y$.

Put $\delta=x-y>0$. Swapping these candidates changes the means to
$$ u-\frac{\delta}{n}, \qquad v+\frac{\delta}{r}. $$
Both remain in $[0,1]$, since they are means of actual groups.

Consider
$$ g(z)=nf\!\left(u-\frac zn\right) +rf\!\left(v+\frac zr\right), \qquad 0\leq z\leq\delta. $$
The initial means are interior: $x>0$ implies $u>0$, and $y<1$ implies $v<1$; together with $u\leq v$, this gives $0<u\leq v<1$. For $0<z<\delta$, the interpolated means are also interior.

On $(0,1)$,
$$ f'(x)=\ln\frac{x}{1-x}, \qquad f''(x)=\frac{1}{x(1-x)}>0. $$
Thus
$$ g'(z)= f'\!\left(v+\frac zr\right) - f'\!\left(u-\frac zn\right)>0, $$
because the difference between the two arguments is
$$ (v-u)+z\left(\frac1r+\frac1n\right)>0. $$
Continuity at the endpoints gives $g(\delta)>g(0)$, contradicting optimality.

Hence an earlier group cannot contain a marginal strictly larger than one in a later group. Candidates with equal marginals can be reassigned among their tied positions without changing group sizes or sums. This represents the groups as intervals in the fixed sorted order. $\square$

For an interval, define
$$ w(a,b)=(b-a+1)f\!\left( \frac{\sum_{i=a}^{b}q_i}{b-a+1} \right). $$
Let $V(i,j)$ be the best score for the first $i$ candidates partitioned into $j$ nonempty intervals. Then
$$ V(i,j)= \max_{j-1\leq h<i} \bigl(V(h,j-1)+w(h+1,i)\bigr), $$
with $V(0,0)=0$ and invalid states assigned $-\infty$.

Every interval partition has a last interval, so every feasible solution occurs in a transition. Conversely, every valid transition constructs a feasible partition. Additivity proves optimal substructure.

There are $O(tm)$ states and $O(tm^2)$ transitions. This is the same combinatorial recurrence underlying the finite-electorate algorithm in [Faliszewski et al., Theorem 4.9](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18651v1). Its transition count alone does not establish polynomial bit complexity.

4. Exact score representation and polynomial-time equality testing

For a group $B$, put
$$ b_B=n_BD-a_B. $$
Direct substitution gives
$$ Dw(B)= a_B\ln a_B+b_B\ln b_B-n_BD\ln(n_BD), $$
where a term with zero coefficient is omitted. Therefore
$$ \exp(DW(\mathcal P)) = \prod_{B\in\mathcal P} \frac{a_B^{a_B}b_B^{b_B}} {(n_BD)^{n_BD}}, $$
again omitting zero-exponent factors.

Comparing two partition scores is equivalent to comparing two products of positive integer powers after cross-multiplication. There are at most $6t$ factors in total, and every base and exponent is at most $mD$.

This proves a polynomial-time Turing reduction of exact optimization to general product-of-powers comparison. It proves no reduction in the reverse direction.

Polynomial-time equality testing

Proposition. Given two partitions, whether their optimized scores are equal can be decided in polynomial time in the original input length and the partition encodings.

Proof. It suffices to decide whether
$$ X=\prod_i x_i^{u_i} \quad\text{and}\quad Y=\prod_j y_j^{v_j} $$
are equal, where all bases and exponents are positive binary integers. Discard bases equal to $1$.

If a left base $a$ and a right base $c$ have
$$ g=\gcd(a,c)>1, $$
and corresponding exponents $u,v$, use
$$ \frac{a^u}{c^v} = \frac{(a/g)^u}{(c/g)^v}g^{u-v}. $$
Replace the two bases by $a/g,c/g$, discarding unit bases. If $u>v$, append $g^{u-v}$ on the left; if $v>u$, append $g^{v-u}$ on the right. If $u=v$, append nothing. This preserves $X/Y$.

To bound the number of steps, consider the product of all current bases on both sides, without their exponents. At each step this product decreases by a factor $g$ if $u\neq v$, and by $g^2$ if $u=v$. It therefore decreases by at least a factor $2$.

Consequently, the number of steps is at most the sum of the binary lengths of the initial bases. Each step adds at most one factor. No base exceeds the initial maximum base, and no exponent exceeds the initial maximum exponent: new exponents are differences of existing positive exponents. All intermediate encodings therefore have polynomial size, and each gcd and update takes polynomial time.

At termination, every left base is coprime to every right base. Hence the represented integers $X,Y$ are coprime. They are equal exactly when both equal $1$, equivalently when both factor lists are empty. $\square$

Thus exact ties can be recognized efficiently. The unresolved arithmetic operation is determining the order of unequal scores.

5. Uniform polynomial-time additive approximation

Theorem. Given the society, $t$, and a rational $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, one can compute a feasible partition and its exact rational group means satisfying
$$ F\geq F^*-\varepsilon $$
in time polynomial in $m,\tau,t,L$ and the encoding length of $\varepsilon$. The precision required depends logarithmically on $1/\varepsilon$.

Here $F^*$ denotes the exact optimum.

Computing logarithms with certified error

First, let $x\in(0,1]$ be rational, with numerator and denominator at most $2^B$. Choose an integer $0\leq k\leq B$ such that
$$ 1\leq z=2^kx<2. $$
Then
$$ \ln x=\ln z-k\ln2. $$
For $1\leq z\leq2$, set
$$ r=\frac{z-1}{z+1}, \qquad 0\leq r\leq\frac13. $$
The convergent series is
$$ \ln z= 2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1}. $$
After $N$ terms, its remainder satisfies
$$ 0\leq 2\sum_{\ell=N}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1} \leq \frac{2r^{2N+1}}{1-r^2} \leq \frac34\,9^{-N}. $$
The same formula computes $\ln2$, using $r=1/3$. Thus the resulting approximation to $\ln x$ has error at most
$$ (k+1)9^{-N}. $$
Choosing
$$ N=O\!\left(\log(B+1)+\log\frac1\eta\right) $$
gives error at most $\eta$.

All truncated expressions are rational. Their powers, numerators, and denominators have bit lengths polynomial in $B,N$: even multiplying the denominators of all summands gives length $O(N^2B+N\log(N+1))$. Hence exact evaluation of the truncated series takes polynomial bit time.

Approximate interval weights and exact rational dynamic programming

Set
$$ \eta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2m}. $$
For each interval $B$ with fitted probability $p_B\in(0,1)$, approximate $\ln p_B$ and $\ln(1-p_B)$ to error at most $\eta$, and define
$$ \widehat w(B)= s_B\,\widehat{\ln p_B} + (n_B-s_B)\,\widehat{\ln(1-p_B)}. $$
For $p_B\in\{0,1\}$, set $\widehat w(B)=0$, its exact score.

Since the nonnegative coefficients sum to $n_B$,
$$ |\widehat w(B)-w(B)|\leq n_B\eta. $$
For every full interval partition $\mathcal P$, this implies
$$ |\widehat W(\mathcal P)-W(\mathcal P)| \leq m\eta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$

Run the dynamic program using the rational weights $\widehat w$. Rational additions and comparisons are exact, and all resulting encodings have polynomial size. Let $\widehat{\mathcal P}$ maximize $\widehat W$, and let $\mathcal P^*$ be an exact optimal interval partition. Then
$$ \begin{aligned} W(\widehat{\mathcal P}) &\geq \widehat W(\widehat{\mathcal P})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\ &\geq \widehat W(\mathcal P^*)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\ &\geq W(\mathcal P^*)-\varepsilon. \end{aligned} $$
Return $\widehat{\mathcal P}$ with its exact rational group means. This proves the theorem.

In particular, error $2^{-K}$ is achievable in time polynomial in the original input parameters and $K$.

6. A quantitative loss bound and relative approximation

Define the fully individualized score
$$ F_{\mathrm{full}}=\sum_{c\in C}f(q_c), $$
and the clustering loss of a fitted partition by
$$ \mathcal L(\mathcal P)=F_{\mathrm{full}}-W(\mathcal P). $$
Let $d$ be the number of distinct candidate marginals.

Proposition.

  • If $t\geq d$, the optimal clustering loss is $0$, and an exact optimum is constructible in polynomial time.
  • If $t<d$, every feasible partition has clustering loss at least $D^{-2}$.

Proof. On $(0,1)$,
$$ f''(x)=\frac1{x(1-x)}\geq4. $$
Consequently, $f(x)-2x^2$ is convex there. Its continuous extension to $[0,1]$ remains convex, by taking endpoint limits in the defining convexity inequality.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to a group $B$ with mean $u$ gives
$$ \sum_{c\in B}f(q_c)-n_Bf(u) \geq 2\sum_{c\in B}(q_c-u)^2. $$
Thus every group has nonnegative loss.

If $B$ contains distinct marginals $x,y$, then
$$ 2\bigl((x-u)^2+(y-u)^2\bigr)\geq(x-y)^2. $$
Because $Dx,Dy$ are distinct integers,
$$ |x-y|\geq\frac1D. $$
Therefore this group has loss at least $D^{-2}$.

If $t<d$, some group must contain two distinct marginals: otherwise each group contains only one marginal value, requiring at least $d$ groups. This proves the second assertion.

If $t\geq d$, group candidates by equal marginals and assign each group that marginal as its probability. Every group then has zero loss. If fewer than $t$ groups result, repeatedly split a nonsingleton group while retaining its probability. Such a group exists whenever the current group count is less than $t\leq m$. This constructs exactly $t$ groups with zero loss. $\square$

Corollary. Given rational $0<\delta\leq1$, a feasible model with
$$ \mathcal L(\mathcal P)\leq(1+\delta)\mathcal L^* $$
can be computed in polynomial time in the input and the encoding length of $\delta$, where $\mathcal L^*$ is the minimum clustering loss.

Proof. If $t\geq d$, return the zero-loss construction. Otherwise, use the additive approximation theorem with
$$ \varepsilon=\frac{\delta}{D^2}. $$
Its precision requirement is polynomial, because
$$ \log\frac1\varepsilon = 2\log D+\log\frac1\delta. $$
The returned partition satisfies
$$ \mathcal L(\mathcal P) \leq\mathcal L^*+\frac{\delta}{D^2} \leq(1+\delta)\mathcal L^*, $$
using $\mathcal L^*\geq D^{-2}$. $\square$

This gives a uniform approximation result for unrestricted binary weights. It does not determine the exact maximizing partition.

7. Arbitrary marginals are realizable by nested support

Theorem. Given arbitrary rational numbers
$$ 0\leq q_1\leq\cdots\leq q_m\leq1, $$
one can construct, in polynomial time, a society having these candidate marginals and at most $m+1$ positive-mass approval types. Its support is totally ordered by inclusion.

Proof. Set $q_0=0$. For each $1\leq i\leq m$, define
$$ A_i=\{c_i,c_{i+1},\ldots,c_m\}, \qquad \mu_{A_i}=q_i-q_{i-1}. $$
Also assign
$$ \mu_{\varnothing}=1-q_m. $$
Delete every zero-mass type.

All retained masses are positive, and their sum is
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{m}(q_i-q_{i-1})+(1-q_m)=1. $$
Candidate $c_k$ belongs exactly to the listed suffixes with $i\leq k$. Its marginal is therefore
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{k}(q_i-q_{i-1})=q_k. $$
The suffixes and the empty set form a chain under inclusion.

Sorting and taking rational differences are polynomial-time operations. The approval sets require at most $O(m^2)$ bits, and the rational differences have polynomial encoding length. $\square$

Combining this construction with Section 2 proves polynomial-time equivalence between the society problem and optimization from an arbitrary rational marginal list. This equivalence already holds for societies whose support is nested and has size at most $m+1$.

Consequently, consistency constraints on marginals imposed by a society do not simplify the unrestricted problem.

8. Exact special cases and a conditional unrestricted result

Unconditional exact special cases

The following known arithmetic theorem is applicable: products of positive binary integers raised to positive binary integer powers can be compared in polynomial time when their total number of factors is bounded by a fixed constant. See [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis, Proposition 3.7(2)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.5429).

Fixed $t$. Each dynamic-programming comparison has at most $6t$ factors, all with polynomial binary length. For fixed $t$, the theorem supplies polynomial-time comparisons, and hence exact optimization.

Fixed support size $\tau$. A candidate’s marginal is determined by its incidence pattern across the $\tau$ support types, so
$$ d\leq2^\tau. $$
If $t\geq d$, Section 6 constructs an exact optimum. Otherwise $t<2^\tau$ is bounded by a constant, and the fixed-$t$ algorithm applies.

Fixed $k=m-t$. An interval partition is specified by omitting exactly $k$ of the $m-1$ boundaries, giving
$$ \binom{m-1}{k} $$
possibilities.

For such a partition, let $\mathcal B$ be its nonsingleton blocks. Then
$$ \sum_{B\in\mathcal B}(|B|-1)=k. $$
Hence
$$ |\mathcal B|\leq k, \qquad \sum_{B\in\mathcal B}|B|\leq2k. $$
Subtracting the common singleton score, the partition’s score difference is
$$ \sum_{B\in\mathcal B} \left(w(B)-\sum_{i\in B}f(q_i)\right). $$
It contains at most $k$ block contributions and $2k$ singleton contributions. Each contribution has at most three logarithmic terms. Comparing two differences therefore requires at most $18k$ product factors. For fixed $k$, enumeration and exact comparison both take polynomial time.

These are polynomial-time results for fixed parameter values; they are not uniform polynomial bounds for unrestricted inputs.

Conditional unrestricted polynomial time

Here is an explicit arithmetic assumption sufficient to settle the problem positively.

Assumption. There is an absolute constant $\kappa>0$ such that, for every $r\geq1$, integers $a_i\geq2$, and nonzero integers $b_i$, if
$$ \Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_i\ln a_i\neq0, $$
then
$$ |\Lambda| \geq \frac{\kappa} {rB\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}a_i|b_i|\right)^2}, \qquad B=\max(3,|b_1|,\ldots,|b_r|). $$
This is the parameter-$1$ case of the Lang–Waldschmidt conjecture as formulated in [Etessami, Stewart, and Yannakakis, Conjecture 3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.5429). It is an assumption, not a proved bound.

Conditional theorem. Under this assumption, unrestricted exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable.

Proof. For two interval partitions $\mathcal P,\mathcal Q$, Section 4 represents
$$ D\bigl(W(\mathcal P)-W(\mathcal Q)\bigr) $$
as a linear form having at most $6t$ terms. Omit zero coefficients and bases equal to $1$. Every remaining base and absolute coefficient is at most
$$ M=\max(3,mD). $$
If the scores differ, the assumption gives, for some $1\leq r\leq6t$,
$$ |W(\mathcal P)-W(\mathcal Q)| \geq \frac{\kappa}{DrM^{4r+1}} \geq \frac{\kappa}{D(6t)M^{24t+1}}. $$
The binary logarithm of the reciprocal of this lower bound is
$$ O\bigl(L+\log(t+1)+t(L+\log(m+1))\bigr), $$
with an additive constant depending only on $\kappa$.

Choose additive approximation error smaller than this lower bound. Section 5 then runs in polynomial time. Its returned interval partition must be exactly optimal: a strictly suboptimal interval partition would have a deficit at least the displayed lower bound. Ties between optimal partitions cause no difficulty, since any maximizer is acceptable. $\square$

In particular, an NP-hardness reduction from satisfiability to this optimization problem, under polynomial-time Turing reductions, would imply $\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}$ if this arithmetic conjecture holds. This is a conditional implication, not a hardness obstruction proved unconditionally.
