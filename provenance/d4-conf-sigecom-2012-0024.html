1. Exact characterization for zero costs

Assume:

  • every $F_j$ is concentrated at cost $0$;
  • $N$ and $\delta$ are dyadic rationals;
  • $0<K\le\delta/2$.

The dyadic assumption ensures that the profiles used in the necessity proof satisfy the mass restriction even if “binary rational” means dyadic rational.

Define
$$ n=\left\lfloor\frac N\delta\right\rfloor+1, \qquad c=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor, $$
and, for $a>1$, define
$$ S_n(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a^{-|j-c|}. $$

Theorem. Under these assumptions, a contract satisfying all the requirements exists if and only if
$$ \boxed{S_n(e^\epsilon)\le\frac32.} $$
Whenever one exists, payment objective $0$ is achievable. Consequently, because the task assumes $B\ge0$, this inequality exactly characterizes feasibility for this subclass.

The proof requires a finite-path estimator construction, which is established next.

2. A finite-path privacy lemma

For each integer $n\ge2$, there is a unique $a_n>1$ satisfying
$$ S_n(a_n)=\frac32. $$
Indeed, $S_n$ is continuous and strictly decreasing, its limit at $1$ is $n$, and its limit at infinity is $1$.

The central row has the largest geometric sum. More explicitly,
$$ S_{2s}(a) = 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}a^{-k}+a^{-s}, $$
and
$$ S_{2s+1}(a) = 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{s}a^{-k}. $$
To verify maximality, write
$$ T_i(a)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a^{-|i-j|}. $$
Then
$$ T_{i+1}(a)-T_i(a) = a^{-(i+1)}-a^{-(n-1-i)}. $$
Thus the sums increase towards the middle and decrease afterwards.

These formulas also show that $a_n$ increases with $n$, that $a_2=2$, and that $a_n<5$: at $a=5$, every finite sum is strictly smaller than
$$ 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}5^{-k}=\frac32. $$

Finite-path lemma. There is an $n$-by-$n$ stochastic matrix $Q$ such that
$$ Q_{ii}=\frac23 $$
and, for every adjacent pair of rows and every column,
$$ Q_{ij}\le a_n Q_{i+1,j}, \qquad Q_{i+1,j}\le a_n Q_{ij}. $$

Here rows represent inputs $0,\ldots,n-1$, and columns represent outputs with the same indices.

Construction

Set
$$ u=\frac23,\qquad a=a_n,\qquad b=a^{-1}, $$
and begin with
$$ H_{ij}=u b^{|i-j|}. $$
Because the central geometric sum is $1/u$ and is maximal, every row sum of $H$ is at most $1$. Define its deficit by
$$ r_i=1-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}H_{ij}\ge0. $$

If $n=2s+1$, add the entire deficit in each row to column $s$:
$$ Q_{ij}=H_{ij}+r_i\mathbf 1_{\{j=s\}}. $$

If $n=2s$, add left-half deficits to column $s$ and right-half deficits to column $s-1$:
$$ Q_{ij} = H_{ij} + \begin{cases} r_i\mathbf 1_{\{j=s\}},&i<s,\\ r_i\mathbf 1_{\{j=s-1\}},&i\ge s. \end{cases} $$

Every row now sums to $1$. In the odd case, the central deficit is zero. In the even case, both central deficits are zero. No diagonal entry therefore receives a positive deficit, so every diagonal entry equals $u$.

It remains to prove the adjacent-row inequalities for the modified columns. All unmodified columns already satisfy them, because their adjacent ratios are $a$ or $a^{-1}$.

Adjacent-row inequalities: odd case

Let $n=2s+1$. Write the modified central-column entry as
$$ q_i=Q_{i,s}=1-\sum_{j\ne s}H_{ij}. $$
For $0\le i<n-1$, direct subtraction gives
$$ a q_{i+1}-q_i = (a-1)\left[ 1-(a+1)\sum_{\substack{j\ge i+1\\j\ne s}}H_{ij} \right], $$
and
$$ a q_i-q_{i+1} = (a-1)\left[ 1-(a+1)\sum_{\substack{j\le i\\j\ne s}}H_{i+1,j} \right]. $$

Thus it suffices to bound both sums by $1/(a+1)$.

The threshold equation gives
$$ u\sum_{k=1}^{s}b^k=\frac16. $$
For $i\ge s$, the first sum is at most this quantity.

For $i<s$, put $\ell=s-i$. The first sum is
$$ R_i = \frac{u}{a-1}(1-b^{s+\ell})-u b^\ell. $$
It increases with $\ell$, so its largest value on this side occurs at $i=0$. Comparing with the value at $i=s$ gives
$$ R_0-R_s = u b^s\left[ \frac{1-b^s}{a-1}-1 \right] \le0, $$
because $a\ge2$. Hence the first sum is always at most $1/6$. Reflection gives the same bound for the second sum.

Finally, $a<5$ implies
$$ \frac16\le\frac1{a+1}. $$
Both required inequalities follow.

Adjacent-row inequalities: even case

Let $n=2s$. When $s=1$, there are no deficits, and the claim follows directly from $H$.

Suppose $s\ge2$. Consider column $s$. Its entries differ from $H$ only on the left side. For $i\le s-1$, write
$$ q_i=Q_{i,s}=1-\sum_{j\ne s}H_{ij}. $$
For $i\ge s-1$, the column equals $H$: row $s-1$ has zero deficit, and subsequent rows send their deficits to the other central column.

We therefore only need to check adjacent pairs with $0\le i\le s-2$. The same subtraction identities reduce the proof to bounding
$$ R_i=\sum_{\substack{j\ge i+1\\j\ne s}}H_{ij}, \qquad L_i=\sum_{j\le i}H_{i+1,j}. $$

The threshold equation is
$$ u\frac{a+1}{a-1}(1-b^s)=1. $$
Consequently,
$$ L_i = \frac{u}{a-1}(1-b^{i+1}) \le \frac{u}{a-1}(1-b^{s-1}) \le \frac1{a+1}. $$

For $R_i$, putting $\ell=s-i$ gives
$$ R_i = \frac{u}{a-1}(1-b^{s-1+\ell})-u b^\ell. $$
This increases with $\ell$, so $R_i\le R_0$. Using the threshold equation,
$$ R_0 = \frac{1-b^{2s-1}-(a-1)b^s} {(a+1)(1-b^s)} \le \frac1{a+1}. $$
The last inequality is equivalent to
$$ b^{2s-1}+(a-2)b^s\ge0, $$
which holds because $a\ge2$.

This proves the inequalities for column $s$. Reflection proves them for column $s-1$, completing the finite-path lemma.

3. Proof of the zero-cost characterization

Necessity

Let
$$ q=\left\lfloor\frac N\delta\right\rfloor, $$
so $n=q+1$. Partition a fixed labelled population into $q$ tagged blocks of mass $\delta$ and a possible remainder.

For $i=0,\ldots,q$, let $D_i$ place the first $i$ blocks in category $1$ and all remaining mass in category $2$. These are admissible dyadic profiles, and consecutive profiles are adjacent. Their category-$1$ masses are $i\delta$.

Let $P_i$ be the estimate distribution under the prescribed equilibrium at $D_i$, and define
$$ A_i=\{y:|y-i\delta|<K\}. $$
Because $2K\le\delta$, the events $A_i$ are pairwise disjoint. This includes equality: the success intervals are open.

Accuracy gives $P_i(A_i)\ge2/3$. Iterating differential privacy along the path gives
$$ P_c(A_i) \ge e^{-\epsilon|i-c|}P_i(A_i) \ge \frac23 e^{-\epsilon|i-c|}. $$
Summing disjoint-event probabilities yields
$$ 1 \ge \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P_c(A_i) \ge \frac23 S_n(e^\epsilon). $$
Hence the displayed condition is necessary. This argument uses only the estimate marginal, so it applies to every contract satisfying the stronger joint-output privacy requirement.

Sufficiency

Offer payment density $0$ for both acceptance and rejection, and prescribe acceptance at indifference. Since every cost is zero, every action has utility zero. Acceptance is therefore a Bayesian equilibrium, EIIR holds, and perfect-data-privacy compensation holds regardless of posterior conventions.

Under the prescribed equilibrium, all participants consent, allowing the mechanism to compute
$$ t=\theta_1. $$
Write uniquely
$$ t=r+i\delta, \qquad 0\le r<\delta, \qquad i=\left\lfloor\frac t\delta\right\rfloor. $$
Define
$$ \ell(r)=\left\lfloor\frac{N-r}{\delta}\right\rfloor+1. $$
The possible scalar query values with this residue form the finite path
$$ r,\ r+\delta,\ \ldots,\ r+(\ell(r)-1)\delta. $$

If $\ell(r)=1$, release $t$. Otherwise use row $i$ of the finite-path matrix for $\ell(r)$, releasing $r+j\delta$ when column $j$ is selected.

An adjacency changes $t$ by $0$ or by $\delta$. In the latter case, the two inputs have the same residue, use the same matrix, and occupy adjacent rows. Also,
$$ \ell(r)\le n, \qquad a_{\ell(r)}\le a_n\le e^\epsilon. $$
The matrix inequalities therefore prove $\epsilon$-differential privacy.

The true value is released with probability $2/3$ when $\ell(r)\ge2$, and with probability $1$ otherwise. Thus
$$ \Pr[|\hat\theta_1-\theta_1|\ge K]\le\frac13. $$
All labelled payments are identically zero, so the joint estimate-and-payment output has the same privacy guarantee.

To complete the contract off equilibrium, release $0$ if the consenting mass is less than $N$; otherwise run the estimator on the consenting population. All transfers remain zero. These histories do not affect the equilibrium proof because every participant’s cost and utility are zero.

This establishes the theorem.

For illustration, the first thresholds are:

| $n$ | $a_n$ |
|---|---|
| $2$ | $2$ |
| $3$ | $4$ |
| $4$ | $2+\sqrt6$ |
| $5$ | $2+2\sqrt2$ |

Thus the exact privacy threshold in this subclass is $\epsilon\ge\log a_n$.

4. A repaired contract for arbitrary bounded costs

Let
$$ V=\max_j\max\operatorname{supp}(F_j), \qquad P=\epsilon V. $$

Offer every category the following deterministic payment densities:

  • acceptance: $2P$;
  • rejection: $P$.

Prescribe acceptance at indifference. Verify categories only after consent.

For every completed transcript, every binary indicator $x$, and every admissible cost $v\le V$, acceptance utility satisfies
$$ 2P-\epsilon vx\ge2P-\epsilon v\ge P. $$
Rejection utility satisfies
$$ P-\epsilon vx\le P. $$
These bounds concern the respective completed transcripts; their indicators need not agree.

Acceptance therefore weakly dominates rejection under every hidden profile and every conditional cost belief. Moreover, accepting utility is nonnegative, and either action pays at least
$$ P\ge\epsilon v. $$
Thus EIIR and perfect-data-privacy compensation hold.

This construction avoids the verifier’s objection: it never computes a rejection transfer from a posterior indicator at an earlier transcript stage. Its incentive and compensation proofs hold for every binary assignment of the completed-transcript indicators.

Under the prescribed equilibrium, the labelled payment field is constant at $2P$, and total payment is
$$ 2N\epsilon V. $$

A simple estimator gives a rationally checkable feasibility certificate. Suppose $\epsilon\ge2$. Draw an integer $Z$ with
$$ \Pr[Z=z]=\frac23\,5^{-|z|}, \qquad z\in\mathbb Z, $$
and release
$$ \hat\theta_1=\theta_1+\delta Z. $$
The distribution is normalized because
$$ \frac23\left(1+2\sum_{z=1}^{\infty}5^{-z}\right)=1. $$

Adjacent query values differ by $0$ or $\delta$. Their output supports coincide, and corresponding probability ratios are at most $5$. Since
$$ e^2>1+2+2+\frac43>5, $$
this proves $\epsilon$-differential privacy. Constant equilibrium payments preserve joint-output privacy.

For every $K>0$, the event $Z=0$ guarantees success. Therefore failure probability is at most $1/3$.

After rejection, use the same completion rule as above: release $0$ if consenting mass is below $N$; otherwise apply this estimator to the consenting category-$1$ mass.

We obtain the valid certificate
$$ \boxed{\epsilon\ge2,\qquad B\ge2N\epsilon V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{feasible}.} $$
It applies to arbitrary finite conditional cost laws and arbitrary supplied priors.

5. Payment lower bounds with signed transfers

Fix an admissible profile. EIIR, conditional on a participant’s type, gives
$$ \mathbb E[p_i]\ge\epsilon v_i\Pr[x_i=1]. $$
Population-level absolute integrability permits Fubini’s theorem. Hence
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[\int_I p_i\,d\mu(i)\right] \ge \epsilon\int_I v_i\Pr[x_i=1]\,d\mu(i) \ge0. $$
Signed transfers therefore cannot make the payment objective negative.

A positive lower bound survives from the two-profile example, with a complete proof as follows.

Assume two categories, both costs constantly $c>0$, and prior probability $1/2$ on each of:

  • $D_0$: every label is in category $2$;
  • $D_1$: every label is in category $1$.

Let $P_0,P_1$ be the full-transcript laws, and put
$$ M=\frac{P_0+P_1}{2}, \qquad h_r=\frac{dP_r}{dM}. $$
Almost surely under $M$, $h_0+h_1=2$, and Bayes’ rule gives posterior probability $h_0/2$ for $D_0$.

Define
$$ U=\{h_0=h_1=1\}. $$
On $U$, every label’s posterior category distribution equals its prior. Outside $U$, every label’s posterior changes. The restricted measures agree:
$$ P_0(\,\cdot\cap U)=P_1(\,\cdot\cap U). $$
Write their common mass as $a$.

The success events
$$ A_0=\{|\hat\theta_1|<K\}, \qquad A_1=\{|\hat\theta_1-N|<K\} $$
are disjoint because $K<N/2$. Their contributions on $U$ sum to at most $a$, while their contributions outside $U$ sum to at most $2(1-a)$. Accuracy therefore implies
$$ \frac43 \le P_0(A_0)+P_1(A_1) \le2-a. $$
Thus $a\le2/3$, and under either profile every participant has posterior-change probability at least $1/3$. Integrating EIIR gives
$$ \boxed{\sup_\theta\mathbb E[\text{total payment}] \ge\frac{\epsilon cN}{3}.} $$
This proof uses only prior-supported profiles and requires no off-prior posterior convention.

This is the public number of possible types, whereas the private realized support size is $\tau\le s$.

$$ \Pr[Z=z] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac45\right)^2 5^{-(k+z)}5^{-k} = \frac23\,5^{-z}; $$
