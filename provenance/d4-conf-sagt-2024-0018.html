The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a nonatomic society with a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings \(r_i\) of partner types, and a rational bound \(K\), decide whether there exist masses \(q_{i,j,k}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{j,k}q_{i,j,k}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_{a,k}q_{a,i,k}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_{a,j}q_{a,j,i}=\mu_i\), and \(\sum_k q_{i,j,k}=\sum_\ell q_{j,\ell,i}\) for every \(i,j\in T\). Require \(q_{i,j,k}=0\) whenever \(r_i(j)>r_i(k)\); define \(b_{i,h}(q)=\sum_{j,k:\,r_i(h)<r_i(k)}q_{i,j,k}\), and require \(b_{i,h}(q)=0\) or \(b_{h,i}(q)=0\) for distinct \(i,h\), and \(b_{i,i}(q)=0\). The decision question is whether such a stable assignment has \(\frac12\sum_{i,j,k}q_{i,j,k}(r_i(j)+r_i(k))\le K\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A nonatomic society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(T\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; the number of partner types is \(m=\tau\ge2\).
Each type has a complete strict ranking \(r_i:T\to\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with smaller ranks preferred and \(r_i(i)=m\). Same-type partnerships involve distinct individuals and are allowed; individuals of the same type are indifferent as partners. The input also contains \(K\in\mathbb Q\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An assignment consists of real masses \(q_{i,j,k}\ge0\), representing type \(i\) individuals with successor type \(j\) and predecessor type \(k\). All indices and sums below range over \(T\).
Require \(\sum_{j,k}q_{i,j,k}=\sum_{a,k}q_{a,i,k}=\sum_{a,j}q_{a,j,i}=\mu_i\) for every \(i\), and \(\sum_kq_{i,j,k}=\sum_\ell q_{j,\ell,i}\) for every \(i,j\).
Require \(q_{i,j,k}=0\) whenever \(r_i(j)>r_i(k)\). Define \(b_{i,h}(q)=\sum_{j,k:\,r_i(h)<r_i(k)}q_{i,j,k}\); stability requires \(b_{i,h}(q)=0\) or \(b_{h,i}(q)=0\) for every distinct \(i,h\), and \(b_{i,i}(q)=0\) for every \(i\).
The decision problem asks whether such a stable assignment satisfies \(\frac12\sum_{i,j,k}q_{i,j,k}(r_i(j)+r_i(k))\le K\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite even societies with complete strict preferences, deciding whether a stable partition has egalitarian cost at most a given bound is NP-complete, even under either a solvability or an unsolvability promise ([Glitzner–Manlove](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00437v4)).
Minimum-cost non-bipartite stable allocation with general edge costs is NP-hard ([Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)); this concerns a different allocation formulation and cost domain. No result settling the exact mass problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made rankings complete, numbered from 1, and placed the own type last, following the discrete ranking convention.
  • Interpreted same-type partners as distinct individuals of that type, allowing these partnerships and charging their rank in the stated objective.
  • Set m equal to the support size tau, since every supported type is a partner type, and required at least two types.
  • Did not impose parity on the support size: finite-agent parity has no corresponding restriction on nonatomic masses.
  • Specified real assignment masses and defined L as the total binary input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(m=\tau=2^s\) and every mass is \(\mu_i=2^{-s}\). Consequently, a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The essential step is to prove that, for equal masses, allowing arbitrary real assignments does not improve the minimum cost over finite stable partitions.

1. Equivalent symmetric pair allocations

Write the assignment cost as

\(\displaystyle C(q)=\frac12\sum_{i,j,k}q_{i,j,k}\bigl(r_i(j)+r_i(k)\bigr).\)

A symmetric pair allocation is a matrix \(y\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle y_{i,j}=y_{j,i}\geq0,\qquad \sum_j y_{i,j}=\mu_i.\)

Define

\(\displaystyle D_{i,h}(y)=\sum_{j:\,r_i(h)<r_i(j)}y_{i,j},\qquad F(y)=\sum_{i,j}r_i(j)y_{i,j}.\)

Call \(y\) stable if, for every distinct \(i,h\), either \(D_{i,h}(y)=0\) or \(D_{h,i}(y)=0\). The self condition is automatic: since \(r_i(i)=m\), we always have \(D_{i,i}(y)=b_{i,i}(q)=0\).

Lemma 1. The original formulation and the symmetric formulation have exactly the same attainable costs.

Proof. Given a feasible \(q\), put

\(\displaystyle x_{i,j}=\sum_k q_{i,j,k},\qquad y_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i,j}+x_{j,i}}2.\)

The marginal constraints give row and column sums \(\mu_i\) for \(x\), so \(y\) has the required row sums. Edge consistency gives

\(\displaystyle x_{k,i}=\sum_j q_{i,j,k}.\)

For a fixed \(i,h\), define

\(\displaystyle U_{i,h}=\sum_{j:\,r_i(h)<r_i(j)}x_{i,j},\qquad B_{i,h}=\sum_{k:\,r_i(h)<r_i(k)}x_{k,i}=b_{i,h}(q).\)

Every positive triple satisfies \(r_i(j)\leq r_i(k)\). Thus every triple counted in \(U_{i,h}\) is also counted in \(B_{i,h}\), and

\(\displaystyle 0\leq U_{i,h}\leq B_{i,h},\qquad D_{i,h}(y)=\frac{U_{i,h}+B_{i,h}}2.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle D_{i,h}(y)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b_{i,h}(q)=0.\)

This proves preservation of stability. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle C(q)=\frac12\sum_{i,j}r_i(j)x_{i,j}+\frac12\sum_{i,k}r_i(k)x_{k,i}=F(y).\)

Conversely, given a stable symmetric allocation \(y\), set

\(\displaystyle q_{i,j,k}=\begin{cases}y_{i,j},&k=j,\\0,&k\neq j.\end{cases}\)

Its three marginals equal \(\mu_i\), by symmetry and the row sums of \(y\). Edge consistency reads \(y_{i,j}=y_{j,i}\), and the ranking constraint holds because successor and predecessor types coincide. Finally, \(b_{i,h}(q)=D_{i,h}(y)\) and \(C(q)=F(y)\). This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

We will repeatedly use the following consequence.

Support preservation. If \(y\) is stable and another feasible symmetric allocation \(y'\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle y_{i,j}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ y'_{i,j}=0,\)

then \(y'\) is stable. Indeed, each equality \(D_{i,h}(y)=0\) means that every corresponding worse-partner coordinate is zero; those coordinates remain zero in \(y'\).

2. Equal masses can be rounded to stable partitions

A finite stable partition is a permutation \(\Pi\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle r_i(\Pi(i))\leq r_i(\Pi^{-1}(i))\)

for every \(i\), with no distinct \(i,h\) satisfying both

\(\displaystyle r_i(h)<r_i(\Pi^{-1}(i)),\qquad r_h(i)<r_h(\Pi^{-1}(h)).\)

For this section, include self ranks in its cost:

\(\displaystyle c^*(\Pi)=\frac12\sum_i\bigl(r_i(\Pi(i))+r_i(\Pi^{-1}(i))\bigr).\)

Lemma 2. Suppose all masses equal \(1/m\). From any stable assignment \(q\), there exists a finite stable partition \(\Pi\) such that

\(\displaystyle c^*(\Pi)\leq mC(q).\)

Conversely, every finite stable partition \(\Pi\) gives a stable assignment of cost \(c^*(\Pi)/m\).

Proof. Apply Lemma 1 and normalize by setting \(z=my\). Then \(z\) is symmetric and stable, every row sums to \(1\), and \(F(z)=mC(q)\).

2.1. The positive-support graph

Form a graph whose positive off-diagonal coordinates are edges and whose positive diagonal coordinates are loops. Count a loop twice in graph degree, although its variable occurs once in its row-sum equation.

Every vertex has a unique worst positive partner, because rankings of types are strict.

For a positive off-diagonal edge \(\{i,j\}\), stability implies that this edge is the worst positive edge at at least one endpoint. Otherwise both endpoints have a positive allocation to a partner worse than each other, giving \(D_{i,j}(z)>0\) and \(D_{j,i}(z)>0\).

Every positive loop is also its vertex’s worst positive edge, since self is ranked last. Therefore every edge is selected as the worst edge by at least one vertex. In each connected component,

\(\displaystyle |E|\leq |V|.\)

If a vertex \(i\) has graph degree \(1\), its only incident edge is an off-diagonal edge \(\{i,j\}\), and the row sum forces \(z_{i,j}=1\). The row sum at \(j\) then excludes every other positive incident coordinate. Hence this component is an isolated pair.

Every other component has minimum graph degree at least \(2\). Thus

\(\displaystyle 2|V|\leq\sum_{i\in V}\deg(i)=2|E|\leq2|V|.\)

All its degrees therefore equal \(2\). Such a connected component is either a single loop or a simple cycle.

A loop has weight \(1\). On a cycle, consecutive edge weights sum to \(1\); consequently, an odd cycle has all weights \(1/2\), while an even cycle has alternating weights \(\theta,1-\theta\).

2.2. Remove even cycles without increasing cost

On an even cycle, the allocation is a convex combination of its two alternating perfect matchings, with coefficients \(\theta\) and \(1-\theta\).

Both matching allocations satisfy the same row sums and use only edges already in the support. They preserve stability by support preservation. Since the cost is linear, at least one has cost no greater than the cycle allocation.

Replace every even cycle by such a matching. The resulting stable allocation \(\widehat z\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle F(\widehat z)\leq F(z),\)

and its components are isolated pairs, single loops, and odd cycles of weight \(1/2\).

2.3. Orient the odd cycles

At each vertex of an odd cycle, designate its better neighbor. An edge cannot be designated by both endpoints: each endpoint would then prefer the other to its remaining positive partner, contradicting stability.

There are exactly as many designations as cycle edges. Since each edge receives at most one designation, every edge receives exactly one.

Orient each edge from the endpoint designating it. Every vertex has one outgoing and one incoming edge, so the cycle becomes a directed cycle. Its successor is its better neighbor, and its predecessor is its worse neighbor.

Use these directed cycles, transpositions for isolated pairs, and fixed points for loops to define \(\Pi\). The successor-predecessor ranking condition holds. Furthermore, in every component the predecessor is the worst positive partner, so

\(\displaystyle D_{i,h}(\widehat z)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_i(h)<r_i(\Pi^{-1}(i)).\)

Stability of \(\widehat z\) therefore proves stability of \(\Pi\). The component weights give

\(\displaystyle c^*(\Pi)=F(\widehat z)\leq mC(q).\)

For the converse, define

\(\displaystyle q_{i,\Pi(i),\Pi^{-1}(i)}=\frac1m\)

and set every other coordinate to zero. Since \(\Pi\) is a permutation, all three marginals and edge-consistency equations hold. Its ranking and stability conditions are precisely those required for \(q\), and its cost is \(c^*(\Pi)/m\). \(\square\)

3. Polynomial reduction with dyadic masses

We use the following established finite results:

  • Deciding whether a finite instance admits a stable partition of egalitarian cost at most an integer \(k\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even under the promise that the instance admits a complete stable matching: Glitzner–Manlove, Theorem 4.16.
  • If an instance admits a complete stable matching, no stable partition contains an odd cycle, including a singleton: the Tan theorem stated there as Theorem 2.10.

Their cost omits self ranks. Under the solvability promise there are no singleton cycles, so their cost equals \(c^*\). [Source: Glitzner–Manlove](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00437v4).

Let \((I,k)\) be an instance of this promised hard problem, with an even number \(n\geq2\) of agents.

Choose the smallest power of two \(N\geq n\), and write \(d=N-n\). Since \(n\) and \(N\) are even, \(d\) is even.

Construct the mass instance as follows:

  • Retain one type for each original agent.
  • Add \(d\) dummy types, grouped into designated pairs.
  • Each original type ranks the other original types in their original order, followed by all dummy types, and finally itself.
  • Each dummy ranks its designated partner first, all other types in any strict order, and itself last.
  • Set every mass to \(1/N\), and set \(K=(k+d)/N\).

These masses are dyadic, positive, and sum to \(1\). Also \(m=\tau=N<2n\), so the construction has polynomial size.

3.1. Forward implication

Suppose \(I\) has a stable partition \(\Pi\) of cost at most \(k\). By the solvability promise, it has no singleton cycles.

Extend \(\Pi\) by adding a transposition for every designated dummy pair. Original successor and predecessor ranks are unchanged. Dummy types have their first choices as predecessors and therefore cannot participate in blocking pairs. Thus the extended partition is stable.

Each dummy contributes \(1\) to the finite cost, so the extended cost is at most \(k+d\). Lemma 2 gives a stable mass assignment of cost at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{k+d}{N}=K.\)

3.2. Reverse implication

Suppose the constructed mass instance has a stable assignment \(q\) with \(C(q)\leq K\). Convert it to \(y\) using Lemma 1.

Every designated dummy pair \(\{a,b\}\) must satisfy \(y_{a,b}=1/N\). Indeed, if \(y_{a,b}<1/N\), both row sums leave positive mass assigned elsewhere. Since \(a,b\) are mutual first choices, this gives \(D_{a,b}(y)>0\) and \(D_{b,a}(y)>0\), contradicting stability.

Thus every dummy pair is an isolated component of the positive support. Apply the construction in Lemma 2 to the full instance. It preserves these pairs and produces a stable partition \(\Pi'\) with

\(\displaystyle c^*(\Pi')\leq NC(q)\leq k+d.\)

Remove the dummy transpositions. The remaining permutation \(\Pi_I\) is a stable partition of \(I\): comparisons between original types, including comparisons with self, have the same order before and after padding.

Since \(I\) is solvable, \(\Pi_I\) has no singleton cycles. All ranks appearing in its cost are therefore unchanged by padding. The dummy transpositions contribute exactly \(d\), yielding

\(\displaystyle c_I(\Pi_I)=c^*(\Pi')-d\leq k.\)

This proves the reverse implication and establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

It remains to justify polynomial certificates despite allowing real masses in assignments.

Suppose a yes-instance has a stable symmetric allocation \(y\), and let \(S\) be its positive support, represented by unordered pairs including loops.

Consider the polytope \(P_S\) of all nonnegative symmetric allocations with the required row sums and zero coordinates outside \(S\). It is nonempty and compact: every coordinate lies between \(0\) and \(1\). Every point in \(P_S\) is stable by support preservation.

A linear function on a nonempty compact polytope has an extreme-point minimizer. Choose such a minimizer \(y^*\) of \(F\) on \(P_S\). Then

\(\displaystyle F(y^*)\leq F(y)\leq K.\)

The row-sum matrix has column \(e_i+e_j\) for an off-diagonal coordinate and column \(e_i\) for a loop. Its positive columns at \(y^*\) are linearly independent. Otherwise a nonzero dependence would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, preserving the row sums and nonnegativity, contradicting extremality.

Consequently \(y^*\) has at most \(m\) positive coordinates. Let their number be \(p\). Select \(p\) rows making the corresponding square matrix \(B\) nonsingular.

Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the input masses, chosen as the product of their encoded denominators. Its bit length is \(O(L)\), and the selected right-hand side \(b=D\mu\) is integral with entries at most \(D\). Cramer’s rule gives each positive coordinate as

\(\displaystyle y^*_e=\frac{\det(B_e)}{D\det(B)},\)

where \(B_e\) replaces one column of \(B\) by \(b\). Hadamard’s determinant bound yields

\(\displaystyle |\det(B)|\leq p^{p/2},\qquad |\det(B_e)|\leq Dp^{p/2}.\)

Each coordinate therefore has bit length \(O(L+m\log(m+1))\).

The positive coordinates of \(y^*\) form a polynomial-size rational certificate. Symmetry, row sums, all stability disjunctions, and the cost bound can be checked using exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time. Lemma 1 converts any accepted certificate into a valid original assignment.

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with the reduction, this proves the claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
