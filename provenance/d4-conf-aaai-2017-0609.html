The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given discrete time slots and a finite support \(Q\) of types \(q=(A_q,B_q)\), each with rational mass \(\mu_q\), where \(B_q\le A_q\in\{0,1\}^m\) is its current report and true availability, decide whether a positive mass can coordinate on new reports. Choose masses \(x_{q,D}\ge 0\) for reports \(D\le A_q\), with \(\sum_D x_{q,D}\le\mu_q\); all remaining mass keeps report \(B_q\). Let \(S_j\) be the resulting average reported attendance at slot \(j\), let \(W=\arg\max_j S_j\), and let \(u_q\) be type \(q\)'s current payoff, computed from \(B_q\) and the current co-winners by the paper's maximin rule. Does there exist such an allocation with positive total deviating mass for which every report \(D\) used by positive mass of type \(q\) yields \(\min_{j\in W}D_j/S_j>u_q\), with winning-slot denominators positive?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge1\) time slots. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(q=(A_q,B_q)\) with positive masses \(\mu_q\) encoded as binary rationals, where \(A_q,B_q\in\{0,1\}^m\) and \(B_q\le A_q\) coordinatewise. Require \(\sum_q\mu_q=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Set \(S_j^0=\sum_q\mu_q B_{qj}\), require \(\max_j S_j^0>0\), and define \(W^0=\arg\max_j S_j^0\) and \(u_q=\min_{j\in W^0}B_{qj}/S_j^0\). Ties are evaluated adversarially through this minimum; attendance equals the report, rather than true availability.
For each type, let \(\mathcal D_q=\{D\in\{0,1\}^m:D\le A_q\}\). A coalition allocation consists of real masses \(x_{q,D}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_q}x_{q,D}\le\mu_q\); remaining mass keeps \(B_q\). Reports may increase coordinates relative to \(B_q\), and coalition members may retain \(D=B_q\).
The resulting attendance is \(S_j=\sum_q[(\mu_q-\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_q}x_{q,D})B_{qj}+\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_q}x_{q,D}D_j]\), with \(W=\arg\max_j S_j\).
Call the allocation improving if \(\sum_{q,D}x_{q,D}>0\), \(\max_j S_j>0\), and every pair with \(x_{q,D}>0\) satisfies \(\min_{j\in W}D_j/S_j>u_q\). Coalition mass has no additional bound.

Is deciding whether an improving allocation exists solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies of teams of size one, existence of an improving coalition of unrestricted size is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete; bounding coalition size by \(t\) gives \(\mathsf{W}[2]\)-hardness parameterized by \(t\). [Bredereck et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.08892)
No result settling this exact mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, interpreting the society as a distribution and attendance as an average.
  • Excluded current profiles with zero maximum reported attendance, because their baseline payoff ratios are undefined.
  • Specified binary individual reports and real allocation masses: divisibility applies to the society, not to an individual's report.
  • Explicitly allowed restoring availability relative to the current report and retaining the current report within the coalition, as permitted by the discrete strategy and coalition definitions.
  • Used unrestricted coalition mass, matching the unrestricted-coalition NP-completeness clause of the anchor.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Theorem. The decision problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It suffices to solve two rational linear programs per time slot.

1. Initial utilities. Write \(c=\max_j S_j^0>0\). Every initial winning slot has attendance \(c\), and reports are binary. Therefore

\[ u_q= \begin{cases} 1/c,& B_{qj}=1\text{ for every }j\in W^0,\\ 0,& B_{qj}=0\text{ for some }j\in W^0. \end{cases} \]

Thus there are only two initial utility levels. Define \(Z=\{q:u_q=0\}\).

2. Reduction to a unique winning slot. We prove that every improving allocation can be replaced by one in which every coalition member reports the same unit vector \(e_k\), and \(k\) is the unique winner.

Take an improving allocation \(x\), and put \(s=\max_j S_j>0\) and \(C=\sum_{q,D}x_{q,D}>0\). For every pair with \(x_{q,D}>0\), improvement and \(u_q\ge0\) imply \(D_j=1\) at every \(j\in W\): otherwise that pair’s new utility would be zero.

Choose any \(k\in W\), and define \(y_q=\sum_Dx_{q,D}\). Replace every participating report by \(e_k\), keeping the same participating masses \(y_q\). This is feasible: whenever \(y_q>0\), some participating report has \(D_k=1\), so \(A_{qk}=1\) and hence \(e_k\le A_q\). Also \(0\le y_q\le\mu_q\).

Let \(S'_j\) denote attendance after this replacement. Attendance at \(k\) is unchanged, since all participating reports already had coordinate \(k\) equal to one. Thus \(S'_k=s\). For \(j\ne k\),

\[ S'_j=S_j-\sum_{q,D}x_{q,D}D_j. \]

If \(j\in W\setminus\{k\}\), the subtracted mass is exactly \(C\), so \(S'_j=s-C<s\). If \(j\notin W\), then \(S'_j\le S_j<s\). Consequently \(k\) is the unique winner.

Each participating pair previously had utility \(1/s\), because its report was one at every original winning slot. After replacement it still has utility \(1/s\). Thus the replacement remains improving.

This argument permits a participant’s replacement report to equal its initial report, as allowed in the question.

3. The linear programs. Fix a candidate slot \(k\). For masses \(y_q\) participating with report \(e_k\), attendance is

\[ T_k(y)=S_k^0+\sum_q(1-B_{qk})y_q, \]

and, for \(j\ne k\),

\[ T_j(y)=S_j^0-\sum_qB_{qj}y_q. \]

Both expressions are affine functions of the participating masses.

For each \(k\), construct two programs. Both maximize a variable \(\varepsilon\), subject to the common constraints

\[ 0\le y_q\le\mu_q\quad\text{for every }q,\qquad 0\le\varepsilon\le1, \]

\[ \sum_qy_q\ge\varepsilon,\qquad T_k(y)\ge\varepsilon, \]

\[ T_k(y)-T_j(y)\ge\varepsilon \quad\text{for every }j\ne k. \]

The additional constraints distinguish the two programs:

  • Zero-utility program: impose \(y_q=0\) whenever \(q\notin Z\) or \(A_{qk}=0\).
  • Lower-attendance program: impose \(y_q=0\) whenever \(A_{qk}=0\), and impose \(T_k(y)+\varepsilon\le c\).

Accept if any of these programs has a strictly positive optimum; reject otherwise. An infeasible program does not cause acceptance.

The variable \(\varepsilon\) handles strict inequalities exactly. A positive feasible value guarantees positive coalition mass, positive winning attendance, and a unique winner. In the second program it additionally guarantees winning attendance strictly below \(c\).

4. Correctness. First suppose a program has a feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\). Set \(x_{q,e_k}=y_q\) for the permitted types and set all other allocation variables to zero. The bounds and availability restrictions make this allocation feasible. The common constraints give positive coalition mass, \(T_k(y)>0\), and \(W=\{k\}\).

In the zero-utility program, every participant has initial utility zero and receives \(1/T_k(y)>0\), so every participant strictly improves.

In the lower-attendance program, \(0<T_k(y)<c\). Every participant therefore receives

\[ \frac1{T_k(y)}>\frac1c\ge u_q, \]

so this allocation also improves. This proves soundness.

Conversely, suppose an improving allocation exists. Apply the reduction in part 2, obtaining participating masses \(y_q\), unique winner \(k\), and winning attendance \(s>0\).

If every participating type belongs to \(Z\), these masses satisfy the restrictions of the zero-utility program. If some participating type has positive initial utility, its improvement requires \(1/s>1/c\); since \(s,c>0\), this implies \(s<c\). The masses then satisfy the restrictions of the lower-attendance program.

In either case, all quantities that must be strictly positive are indeed positive: coalition mass, winning attendance, and every gap between the winning attendance and a rival’s attendance. In the second case, \(c-s\) is positive as well. Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) no larger than one and no larger than any of these finitely many positive quantities. The corresponding program has a positive feasible objective value, and hence a positive optimum. This proves completeness.

5. Bit complexity. Computing \(S_j^0\), \(c\), \(W^0\), and \(Z\) requires polynomially many rational arithmetic operations. Their bit lengths are polynomially bounded by \(L\): for example, a common denominator for the input masses can be taken as the product of their denominators, whose bit length is at most the sum of their encoded bit lengths.

Each program has \(\tau+1\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints, all with rational coefficients of polynomial bit length. Its feasible region is bounded by the explicit bounds on \(y_q\) and \(\varepsilon\).

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and the exact optimum of a bounded rational linear program can be computed in time polynomial in its encoded size. All hypotheses hold here. In particular, comparing the exact optimum with zero requires no numerical tolerance or assumed minimum improvement.

There are \(2m\) programs, so the complete decision procedure runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). A positive optimum also supplies an improving allocation using at most one coalition report per type.

The finite-society hardness result concerns indivisible participants. Here the participating masses are real variables, and the preceding proof reduces their selection to linear programming. [Bredereck et al., Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.08892)
