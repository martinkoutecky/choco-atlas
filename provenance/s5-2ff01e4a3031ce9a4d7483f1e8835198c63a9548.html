The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), target \(w\), and complete ranking types \(\rho\) with binary rational registered masses \(\mu_\rho\) summing to \(1\) and available masses \(\nu_\rho\), is there a recruitment vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_\rho\le\nu_\rho\) and \(\sum_\rho x_\rho\le\kappa\) such that \(w\) is the unique Bucklin winner after the type masses become \((\mu_\rho+x_\rho)/(1+\sum_\rho x_\rho)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(w\in C\). Types \(\rho\) are strict complete rankings of \(C\).
A society is given by its support: types with positive mass and their binary rational masses. Here the input lists the \(\tau\) types with \(\mu_\rho+\nu_\rho>0\), giving registered masses \(\mu_\rho\ge0\) and available masses \(\nu_\rho\ge0\), with \(\sum_\rho\mu_\rho=1\); omitted masses are zero. Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The recruitment budget \(\kappa\ge0\) is a binary rational; available masses need not sum to one. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Recruitment chooses real masses \(0\le x_\rho\le\nu_\rho\) with \(\sum_\rho x_\rho\le\kappa\), yielding normalized masses \(p_\rho=(\mu_\rho+x_\rho)/(1+\sum_\sigma x_\sigma)\).
For this mass version of Bucklin, the level \(i\) score is \(s_i(c)=\sum_{\rho:\operatorname{rank}_\rho(c)\le i}p_\rho\). At the first level \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) where any score exceeds \(1/2\), all candidates attaining the largest level score win; ties remain ties.

Is deciding whether some feasible recruitment makes \(w\) the unique Bucklin winner solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of indivisible voters, constructive control by adding at most a specified number of voters is NP-hard; later work also proves W[2]-hardness parameterized by the number added. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.2230)
No result settling this exact divisible-mass recruitment problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified the continuous majority threshold as strictly greater than one half, preserving the discrete strict-majority convention.
  • Specified that equal maximal scores produce multiple winners, with no additional tie-breaking.
  • Made the budget a nonnegative binary rational and available masses nonnegative binary rationals without normalization.
  • Defined support as the union of registered and available supports, allowing either mass to be zero for a listed type and setting omitted masses to zero.
  • Allowed arbitrary real recruitment amounts, expressing divisibility of mass.
  • Allowed any nonempty candidate set, including a singleton, as the discrete problem imposes no minimum of two candidates.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). It suffices to solve at most \(m\) rational linear programs, each with \(\tau+1\) variables and \(O(\tau+m)\) constraints. The strict inequalities can be tested exactly.

  • Remove the normalization.

Let \(R\) be the explicitly listed support, so \(|R|=\tau\). For a feasible recruitment vector \(x\), define
\(T(x)=1+\sum_{\rho\in R}x_\rho\)
and, for \(j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\), define
\(A_{j,c}(x)=\sum_{\rho\in R:\operatorname{rank}_\rho(c)\le j}(\mu_\rho+x_\rho)\).

In particular, \(A_{0,c}(x)=0\). Because \(T(x)\ge1\), the normalized score satisfies
\(s_j(c)=A_{j,c}(x)/T(x)\).
Consequently,
\(s_j(c)>1/2\) if and only if \(2A_{j,c}(x)>T(x)\), and
\(s_j(w)>s_j(c)\) if and only if \(A_{j,w}(x)>A_{j,c}(x)\).

For each candidate, these unnormalized scores are nondecreasing in \(j\): the defining sets of types are nested, and all masses are nonnegative. Also, \(A_{m,c}(x)=T(x)\), so a first majority level always exists.

  • Characterize success at a fixed level.

Fix \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). A feasible \(x\) makes \(w\) the unique winner at first majority level \(i\) if and only if all three conditions hold:

\(2A_{i-1,c}(x)\le T(x)\quad\text{for every }c\in C;\)

\(2A_{i,w}(x)>T(x);\)

\(A_{i,w}(x)>A_{i,c}(x)\quad\text{for every }c\in C\setminus\{w\}.\)

To prove necessity, no candidate exceeds half at level \(i-1\), since \(i\) is the first majority level. At level \(i\), uniqueness means that \(w\)'s score strictly exceeds every rival's score. Some candidate exceeds half at that level, so \(w\) does too.

Conversely, the first condition and monotonicity imply that no candidate exceeds half at any level before \(i\). The second condition supplies a majority at level \(i\). The third makes \(w\) the unique candidate attaining the largest score there. These are precisely the required winning conditions.

  • Test the strict inequalities with a linear program.

For each level \(i\), introduce a variable \(\varepsilon\), and maximize \(\varepsilon\) subject to

\(0\le x_\rho\le\nu_\rho\quad(\rho\in R),\qquad \sum_{\rho\in R}x_\rho\le\kappa;\)

\(0\le\varepsilon\le1;\)

\(2A_{i-1,c}(x)\le T(x)\quad(c\in C);\)

\(2A_{i,w}(x)-T(x)\ge\varepsilon;\)

\(A_{i,w}(x)-A_{i,c}(x)\ge\varepsilon\quad(c\in C\setminus\{w\}).\)

All these constraints are linear. If the program is feasible, its feasible region is closed and bounded: each recruitment variable has a finite upper bound, and \(\varepsilon\in[0,1]\). Thus its maximum is attained.

This program has positive optimal value if and only if recruitment can make \(w\) the unique winner at first majority level \(i\).

Indeed, any feasible point with \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfies all the conditions in step 2. Conversely, suppose \(x\) satisfies those conditions. The finite set
\(\{1,\;2A_{i,w}(x)-T(x)\}\cup\{A_{i,w}(x)-A_{i,c}(x):c\ne w\}\)
consists entirely of positive numbers. Its minimum is therefore positive. Choosing that minimum as \(\varepsilon\) gives a feasible point with positive objective value. This argument also covers \(m=1\), when there are no rival inequalities.

The algorithm accepts if some level's program is feasible with positive optimum, and rejects otherwise. Step 2 proves that this algorithm is correct in both directions.

  • Verify polynomial bit complexity.

Each program has \(\tau+1\) variables and \(2\tau+2m+3\) inequalities. Only the listed types appear; no enumeration of the \(m!\) possible rankings is needed.

The coefficients of the recruitment variables are small integers. The rational constants are input masses, the budget, and sums or differences of registered masses. Their encoding lengths are polynomially bounded by \(L\). More explicitly, a common denominator can be taken as the product of the denominators of the listed registered masses. Its binary length is at most the sum of their denominator lengths, hence \(O(L)\). Since the registered masses are nonnegative and sum to one, every subset sum has numerator at most this common denominator. The resulting constants therefore also have \(O(L)\) bits.

The linear programs can consequently be constructed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and, for a feasible program with finite optimum, exact optimization are computable in time polynomial in the program's binary encoding length. See [Khachiyan, *Polynomial algorithms in linear programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5239). Its hypotheses hold here: the data are rational, the constraints are non-strict, and every feasible program is bounded. Exact optimization permits an exact comparison of the optimal value with zero, even when a positive value is very small.

There are only \(m\) programs, so the total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The cited indivisible-voter model requires choosing a sublist of voters, as specified in [Section 2.2.4 of the source paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.2230). Here recruitment variables range over real intervals, which makes the fixed-level conditions linear programs.
