The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant and employer type sets with rational masses summing to one on each side, a compatibility graph, and strict rankings of counterpart types, does there exist a popular measurable one-to-one matching \(M\) with minimum matched mass \(q(M)\) among all popular matchings, for which a positive mass of agents can be paired with mutually preferred partners? To specify strict rankings within counterpart types, give each type a normalized coordinate in \([0,1]\) and use that shared coordinate as the within-type priority order; matchings are measure-preserving partial bijections, and popularity compares \(M\) with every such matching by integrating each agent’s \(+1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) vote.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite applicant and employer type sets, with compatibility graph \(E\subseteq A\times B\); each type strictly ranks its compatible counterpart types. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\) on each side. Write \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
A type \(t\) of mass \(w_t\) consists of agents \((t,x)\), \(x\in[0,1]\), with measure \(w_t\,dx\). Preferences compare counterpart types first, then prefer smaller counterpart coordinates within a type; every compatible partner is preferred to being unmatched.
A matching \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving partial bijection between the two sides, with measurable inverse, using only compatible type pairs. Maps and conditions are interpreted up to null sets. Its matched mass \(q(M)\) is the measure of its applicant domain.
For matchings \(M,N\), each agent votes \(+1\) if it prefers its assignment in \(M\), \(-1\) if it prefers its assignment in \(N\), and \(0\) if the assignments coincide. Let \(\Delta(M,N)\) be the sum of the vote integrals over both sides; \(M\) is popular if \(\Delta(M,N)\geq0\) for every matching \(N\).
Say \(M\) admits positive blocking mass if there is a compatible measurable, measure-preserving partial bijection \(P\) with applicant domain of positive measure such that almost every paired applicant and employer strictly prefers its partner in \(P\) to its assignment in \(M\); participants may abandon their existing partners.

Is deciding whether there exists a popular matching \(M\) with \(q(M)\leq q(N)\) for every popular matching \(N\), and admitting positive blocking mass, in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

For finite bipartite instances with strict preferences, existence of an unstable minimum-size popular matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, while existence of an unstable popular matching without the minimum-size requirement is decidable in \(O(|E|^2)\) time ([Cseh–Faenza–Kavitha–Powers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.06897)). No result settling the precise continuum problem above was located.

1. Stable matchings determine the minimum popular mass

Let $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$ denote the measures on the applicant and employer spaces. For a matching $M$, write $C_A(M)$ and $C_B(M)$ for its matched sets. Both have measure $q(M)$.

Throughout, call a matching stable if it admits no positive blocking mass, precisely as defined in the question.

Proposition 1

If $S$ is stable, then every matching $N$ satisfies
$$ \Delta(S,N)\geq \mu_A\bigl(C_A(S)\setminus C_A(N)\bigr) + \mu_B\bigl(C_B(S)\setminus C_B(N)\bigr). $$

Consequently, $S$ is popular. Every popular matching contains both matched sets of $S$, modulo null sets. In particular, $S$ has minimum matched mass among popular matchings, and every minimum-mass popular matching has exactly the same matched sets as $S$.

Proof

Write $v_z(N,S)$ for agent $z$'s vote for $N$ against $S$, and set
$$ r= \mu_A\bigl(C_A(S)\setminus C_A(N)\bigr) + \mu_B\bigl(C_B(S)\setminus C_B(N)\bigr). $$

Measure preservation gives
$$ \Delta(N,S) = \int_{C_A(N)} \bigl(v_a(N,S)+v_{N(a)}(N,S)\bigr)\,d\mu_A(a)-r. $$

The final term accounts for agents unmatched by $N$ but matched by $S$.

The measurable set of $N$-pairs whose endpoints both vote positively has measure zero. Otherwise, restricting $N$ to that set would give positive blocking mass for $S$.

For any other $N$-pair, its two votes sum to at most zero. Indeed, if one endpoint keeps its assignment, bijectivity forces the other endpoint to keep its assignment too. Otherwise both votes are nonzero, and they cannot both be positive.

Thus
$$ \Delta(N,S)\leq-r. $$

Antisymmetry proves the displayed inequality and popularity of $S$. If $N$ is popular, then
$$ 0\leq\Delta(N,S)\leq-r, $$
so $r=0$. Both matched-set containments follow. Equality of matched masses turns these containments into equalities. $\square$

A polynomial construction, with explicit dummy augmentation

For each compatible type pair $ab$, introduce an allocation $f_{ab}$ satisfying
$$ f_{ab}\geq0,\qquad \sum_b f_{ab}\leq w_a,\qquad \sum_a f_{ab}\leq w_b. $$

Define unused masses
$$ u_a=w_a-\sum_b f_{ab}, \qquad u_b=w_b-\sum_a f_{ab}, $$
and the masses that would improve by obtaining a specified counterpart type:
$$ d_a(b)=u_a+\sum_{c:\,b\succ_a c}f_{ac}, \qquad d_b(a)=u_b+\sum_{c:\,a\succ_b c}f_{cb}. $$

We need an allocation satisfying
$$ d_a(b)d_b(a)=0 \qquad\text{for every }ab\in E. $$

Use the bipartite stable-allocation theorem: finite instances with strict preferences, vertex quotas and edge capacities admit stable allocations; an implementation without dynamic trees takes $O(ne)$ arithmetic operations. Here allocation stability excludes an unsaturated edge for which both endpoints allocate positive mass to worse neighbors. This is the formulation and bound in [Dean–Munshi, Sections 2–3](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

The following augmentation makes the fully allocated formulation applicable.

  • Add an applicant dummy $a_0$ and an employer dummy $b_0$, each with quota $1$.
  • Give every real type $t$ quota $w_t$.
  • Give real edge $ab$ capacity $\min\{w_a,w_b\}$.
  • Add edges $ab_0$ of capacity $w_a$, edges $a_0b$ of capacity $w_b$, and $a_0b_0$ of capacity $1$.
  • Every real type ranks its dummy last. Each dummy ranks all real neighbors in an arbitrary strict order, followed by the other dummy.

The augmented total quota is $2$ on each side. Feasibility holds: allocate each real applicant entirely to $b_0$, and each real employer entirely to $a_0$.

Apply the theorem and retain its real-edge allocations $f$. Dummy allocation represents unused real mass. Hence an unsaturated real edge cannot have both $d$-values positive. If
$$ f_{ab}=\min\{w_a,w_b\}, $$
an endpoint of minimum quota allocates its entire quota to this edge, so its $d$-value is zero. The required condition therefore holds on every real edge.

To realize $f$, use mass coordinates
$$ s=w_ax,\qquad t=w_by. $$

These coordinates carry ordinary Lebesgue measure. Within every type, put its allocated intervals consecutively in preference order, followed by its unmatched interval. For edge $ab$, let its two intervals be
$$ I_{ab}=[\alpha_{ab},\alpha_{ab}+f_{ab}), \qquad J_{ab}=[\beta_{ab},\beta_{ab}+f_{ab}). $$

Match them by translation:
$$ t=\beta_{ab}+s-\alpha_{ab}. $$

This defines a measurable measure-preserving partial bijection $S$.

Here is a pointwise stability check away from the finitely many interval endpoints.

  • If neither endpoint currently has the proposed counterpart type, mutual improvement requires both endpoints to occupy unmatched or worse-type regions. Those regions have positive length, implying both relevant $d$-values are positive, a contradiction.
  • If exactly one endpoint currently has the proposed counterpart type, the other endpoint can improve only from a region lying after its allocation interval for this edge. Its coordinate is therefore larger than the first endpoint's current partner coordinate, so the first endpoint rejects it.
  • If both endpoints currently have the proposed counterpart type, mutual improvement would require

$$ t<\beta_{ab}+s-\alpha_{ab}, \qquad s<\alpha_{ab}+t-\beta_{ab}. $$
These strict inequalities contradict each other.

A measure-preserving partial bijection cannot use a null exceptional set for positive mass. Thus $S$ is stable.

By Proposition 1,
$$ q_*:=\min\{q(M):M\text{ is popular}\} =\sum_{ab\in E}f_{ab}. $$

Writing
$$ s_t=\sum_{e\ni t}f_e, $$
every minimum-mass popular matching matches exactly
$$ [0,s_t/w_t) $$
in type $t$, modulo null sets.

The construction takes
$$ O\bigl((m+\tau)\tau\bigr) $$
arithmetic operations. A conservative bit bound is
$$ O\!\left( L^3+(m+\tau)\tau\bigl(L+\log(\tau+2)\bigr)^2 \right). $$

For justification, scale by the product of input denominators, whose binary length is at most $L$. The augmentations use additions, subtractions, comparisons and minima on integers of $O(L)$ bits. The resulting interval descriptions also have $O(L)$-bit rational coordinates.

This computes $q_*$ and the mandatory matched sets; it does not decide the general existence question.

2. A new obstruction: an unmatched reserve on one side

We use the following elementary measure fact. Two measurable subsets of finite unions of weighted intervals, of equal positive measure, admit a measurable measure-preserving bijection modulo null sets. To see this, first use mass coordinates and concatenate the intervals. For a measurable subset $X$, its cumulative-measure function
$$ F_X(t)=\lambda\bigl(X\cap(-\infty,t]\bigr) $$
maps its restricted measure to Lebesgue measure on $[0,\lambda(X)]$. Its generalized inverse is an inverse modulo null sets. Flat intervals only introduce endpoint exceptions of measure zero. Composing the two cumulative-measure parametrizations gives the required bijection.

Proposition 2

Let $M$ be popular. Suppose there is a measurable set $T$ of unmatched applicants, with
$$ \mu_A(T)=\varepsilon>0, $$
such that every type represented in $T$ is compatible with every matched employer type.

Then $M$ admits no positive blocking mass.

The same conclusion holds with the two sides exchanged.

Proof

First, a blocking partial bijection for a popular matching cannot involve positive mass of unmatched participants.

For example, suppose a restriction $P$ has applicant domain $D$ consisting entirely of unmatched applicants, of mass $p>0$, and range $R$. Use $P$, retain all $M$-pairs whose employer is outside $R$, and leave the displaced applicants unmatched. The blocking participants contribute $2p$ votes. The displaced applicants have mass at most $p$ and contribute at worst $-p$. The resulting matching therefore defeats $M$ by at least $p$. The employer-side argument is identical.

Consequently, if $M$ admitted positive blocking mass, we could discard null sets and take a blocking partial bijection $P$ whose participants are all matched by $M$. By restriction, choose its mass $p$ to satisfy
$$ 0<p\leq\varepsilon. $$

Let $D$ and $R$ be its domain and range. Define the abandoned partners outside the participant sets:
$$ U=M^{-1}(R)\setminus D, \qquad V=M(D)\setminus R. $$

Since $M$ preserves measure,
$$ \mu_A(U)=\mu_B(V) =p-\mu_B\bigl(R\cap M(D)\bigr)=:r, \qquad 0\leq r\leq p. $$

Choose $T'\subseteq T$ of mass $r$. Construct $N$ by:

  • using $P$ on $D$;
  • retaining every $M$-pair with neither endpoint in $D\cup R$;
  • matching $T'$ measure-preservingly to $V$;
  • leaving $U$ unmatched.

Compatibility in the third step follows from the reserve hypothesis, since $V\subseteq C_B(M)$. The domains and ranges of these three sets of pairs are disjoint.

The vote contributions satisfy:

  • blocking participants: $2p$;
  • applicants in $U$: $-r$;
  • employers in $V$: at least $-r$;
  • applicants in $T'$: $r$.

Thus
$$ \Delta(N,M)\geq2p-r\geq p>0, $$
contradicting popularity. $\square$

A polynomially checkable sufficient NO condition

Apply Proposition 1 to the constructed stable matching. Suppose an applicant type $a$ satisfies
$$ u_a>0 $$
and is compatible with every employer type $b$ satisfying $s_b>0$.

Every minimum-mass popular matching then has the same positive-mass unmatched suffix in type $a$, and the same matched employer types. Proposition 2 proves that no such matching can admit positive blocking mass.

This criterion also applies separately within a compatibility component. Popularity decomposes over components: vote totals add, and an opponent may change its matching in just one component. If every component containing an edge has a reserve of the stated kind on at least one side, the whole instance is NO.

After computing the stable allocation, these sufficient NO conditions can be checked in $O(m+\tau)$ graph operations by counting each type's neighbors among the matched opposite-side types.

3. A complete classification for a substantial subclass

Consider instances in which every component containing an edge satisfies both conditions:

  • its compatibility graph is complete bipartite;
  • all applicant types in the component share one strict order of its employer types, and all employer types share one strict order of its applicant types.

For a component $C$, define
$$ W_A(C)=\sum_{a\in A\cap C}w_a, \qquad W_B(C)=\sum_{b\in B\cap C}w_b. $$

Theorem 3

For this subclass, the answer is YES if and only if some component containing an edge satisfies
$$ W_A(C)=W_B(C). $$

In particular, this completely classifies all instances whose compatibility components are stars or isolated vertices.

Proof: reducing a component to two ordered intervals

Concatenate the applicant types in their common order as ranked by employers. Within each type use mass coordinates. This produces an applicant coordinate
$$ s\in[0,W_A(C)]. $$

Likewise, concatenate employer types in their common order as ranked by applicants, producing
$$ t\in[0,W_B(C)]. $$

Every applicant prefers smaller $t$, and every employer prefers smaller $s$. These order comparisons agree exactly with the given type-first, coordinate-second preferences. Complete compatibility permits every pair of these coordinates.

Set
$$ Q=\min\{W_A(C),W_B(C)\}. $$

The identity matching on $[0,Q]$ is stable. Two matched participants could both improve only if
$$ t<s,\qquad s<t. $$

An unmatched participant, if present, has coordinate at least $Q$ on the excess side. Every matched agent on the opposite side already has a partner of coordinate below $Q$, and rejects that unmatched participant. There are no unmatched agents on both sides.

Proposition 1 therefore shows that every minimum-mass popular matching in this component has matched mass $Q$, and matches exactly the first $Q$ mass units on each side.

Unbalanced components cannot supply blocking mass

If $W_A(C)\ne W_B(C)$, the excess side has unmatched mass
$$ |W_A(C)-W_B(C)|>0. $$

Every agent on that side is compatible with every matched agent on the other side. Proposition 2 rules out positive blocking mass for a minimum-mass popular matching.

This particular conclusion does not need the common-order hypothesis: in any complete bipartite component, stability forces matched mass $Q$, since otherwise both sides have positive unmatched mass that can be paired to block.

Balanced components supply an unstable minimum-mass popular matching

Suppose
$$ W_A(C)=W_B(C)=W>0. $$

On the two ordered intervals define
$$ h(s)= \begin{cases} s+W/2,&0\leq s<W/2,\\ s-W/2,&W/2\leq s<W. \end{cases} $$

Match $s$ to $h(s)$. This is a measure-preserving involution. Define on both sides
$$ \alpha(s)= \begin{cases} 1,&s<W/2,\\ -1,&s\geq W/2. \end{cases} $$

For a proposed pair $(s,t)$, the vote sum against this matching is
$$ c(s,t) = \operatorname{sgn}\bigl(h(s)-t\bigr) + \operatorname{sgn}\bigl(h(t)-s\bigr). $$

It satisfies
$$ c(s,t)=\alpha(s)+\alpha(t). $$

Indeed, both signs are positive in the lower-half pair, both are negative in the upper-half pair, and their arguments are negatives of one another when the coordinates lie in opposite halves.

Let $N$ be any partial matching in this component, with unmatched sets $U_A,U_B$. Since the constructed matching matches everyone and the integral of $\alpha$ over each entire side is zero,
$$ \Delta(N,M) = -\int_{U_A}(\alpha+1)\,ds -\int_{U_B}(\alpha+1)\,dt \leq0. $$

Thus $M$ is popular. Its matched mass is $W=Q$, so it is minimum-mass popular.

The partial identity matching
$$ s\longmapsto s, \qquad 0\leq s<W/2, $$
blocks: both participants currently have partner coordinate $s+W/2$ and strictly improve. Its blocking mass is $W/2$.

Use this matching in one balanced component and stable matchings in all other components. Componentwise popularity and the minimum-mass property give the required global matching.

Conversely, if all edge-containing components are unbalanced, none can supply positive blocking mass. Since there are finitely many components, any positive blocking partial bijection would have positive mass in at least one of them, a contradiction. $\square$

Why stars satisfy the hypotheses

A star is complete bipartite with one type on one side. That type's preference list gives the common order on the leaf types. Every leaf has just one compatible counterpart type, so the other common order is automatic.

Running time

Recognizing the subclass takes $O(m+\tau)$ graph operations: find components, check completeness, and compare the relevant preference lists. The decision then consists of computing and comparing component mass totals.

Using a common denominator and elementary integer arithmetic gives the conservative bit bound
$$ O(m+\tau+L^3). $$

This is a decision running time for the stated subclass.

4. Exact verification of finite interval matchings

The following provides a finite characterization for a restricted representation of a proposed matching.

Definition

A finite interval matching partitions every type's mass-coordinate interval into finitely many positive-length cells. Each matched applicant cell is paired with one employer cell of equal length by increasing translation. The remaining cells are unmatched.

Let $K$ be the total number of cells on both sides. For algorithmic statements, assume the endpoints are rational, and let $H$ be the combined binary length of the instance and this matching description.

Write $I_i$ for applicant cells, $J_j$ for employer cells, and $\ell_i,r_j$ for their lengths. Let $d_i,e_j$ indicate whether the corresponding cells are matched by $M$.

Lemma 4: vote sums are constant on cell pairs

For each compatible cell pair $I_i,J_j$, the sum of the two votes against $M$ is constant, with value
$$ c_{ij}\in\{-2,0,2\}. $$

Proof

If these are cells paired by $M$, write its translation as $t=s+\delta$. The two votes for a proposed pair sum to
$$ \operatorname{sgn}(s+\delta-t) + \operatorname{sgn}(t-\delta-s)=0. $$

For cells not paired by $M$, an endpoint's vote is determined either by a strict type comparison, by being currently unmatched, or by the order of two disjoint counterpart cells within one type. In each case its vote is constant and nonzero, away from endpoints. Their sum is therefore one of the three stated values. $\square$

Theorem 5: a finite characterization of popularity

A finite interval matching $M$ is popular if and only if there are cell potentials
$$ \alpha_i,\beta_j\in\{-1,0,1\} $$
such that:

  • unmatched cells have potential zero;
  • every pair of cells matched by $M$ has potentials summing to zero;
  • every compatible cell pair satisfies

$$ \alpha_i+\beta_j\geq c_{ij}. $$

Proof: the comparison linear program is exact

For any opponent $N$, let $z_{ij}$ be its matched mass between $I_i$ and $J_j$. These values satisfy
$$ z_{ij}\geq0,\qquad \sum_jz_{ij}\leq\ell_i,\qquad \sum_iz_{ij}\leq r_j. $$

Conversely, every vector satisfying these constraints can be realized by a measurable matching: split each cell into subintervals of the prescribed lengths and pair corresponding subintervals by translation.

Lemma 4 gives
$$ \Delta(N,M) = \sum_{ij}c_{ij}z_{ij} -\sum_i d_i\left(\ell_i-\sum_jz_{ij}\right) -\sum_j e_j\left(r_j-\sum_iz_{ij}\right). $$

Equivalently, the greatest possible vote total against $M$ is the optimum of
$$ \max_z \left[ \sum_{ij}(c_{ij}+d_i+e_j)z_{ij}-2q(M) \right] $$
under the displayed constraints. This finite program is feasible and compact. Its optimum is at least zero, because $N=M$ is feasible. Thus popularity is equivalent to optimum zero.

Finite linear-programming duality shows that this happens exactly when real potentials exist satisfying
$$ \alpha_i\geq-d_i,\qquad \beta_j\geq-e_j,\qquad \alpha_i+\beta_j\geq c_{ij}, $$
and
$$ \sum_i\ell_i\alpha_i+\sum_jr_j\beta_j=0. $$

For completeness, these are obtained from the usual nonnegative capacity multipliers $\lambda_i,\nu_j$ by setting
$$ \alpha_i=\lambda_i-d_i,\qquad \beta_j=\nu_j-e_j. $$

Now decompose the zero weighted sum according to $M$. A matched cell pair contributes its common length times $\alpha_i+\beta_j$, which is nonnegative because $c_{ij}=0$. An unmatched cell contributes its positive length times its nonnegative potential. Every term must therefore vanish.

Consequently, unmatched potentials are zero, and matched partners have opposite potentials. The lower bounds imply that every matched potential lies in $[-1,1]$.

Replace each potential by its sign, with $\operatorname{sgn}(0)=0$. All constraints remain valid:

  • if $c_{ij}=2$, the original potentials must both equal $1$;
  • if $c_{ij}=0$, a negative potential requires the other potential to be positive, so the sum of signs is nonnegative;
  • if $c_{ij}=-2$, the constraint holds automatically after rounding.

Matched partners retain opposite signs; unmatched cells retain zero. This proves necessity of the stated ternary potentials.

Conversely, given those potentials, their weighted sum is zero because matched cell lengths agree. For any $N$, integrate the inequalities over its pairs. Its agents left unmatched contribute at most their potentials: a previously matched agent votes $-1$, and an originally unmatched agent votes zero and has potential zero. Hence
$$ \Delta(N,M)\leq \sum_i\ell_i\alpha_i+\sum_jr_j\beta_j=0. $$

Thus $M$ is popular. $\square$

A directed-graph test

The characterization can be tested without solving a linear program.

Associate a variable $x_i$ with each matched applicant cell. Set its potential to $x_i$ and its matched employer cell's potential to $-x_i$. Initially impose
$$ -1\leq x_i\leq1. $$

For a compatible cell pair, let $k$ be the applicant cell matched to its employer cell, when that employer cell is matched.

The constraints become:

| Cell statuses | Vote sum | Required condition |
|---|---:|---|
| Both matched | $2$ | $x_i=1$, $x_k=-1$ |
| Both matched | $0$ | $x_i\geq x_k$ |
| Both matched | $-2$ | No additional condition |
| Applicant unmatched, employer matched | $2$ | Infeasible |
| Applicant unmatched, employer matched | $0$ | $x_k\leq0$ |
| Applicant matched, employer unmatched | $2$ | Infeasible |
| Applicant matched, employer unmatched | $0$ | $x_i\geq0$ |
| Both unmatched | $2$ | Infeasible |

All remaining constraints with vote sum $-2$ hold automatically; a zero constraint between two unmatched cells also holds.

For every inequality $x_i\geq x_k$, add a directed edge $k\to i$. Collapse strongly connected components, whose variables must be equal. Each component inherits the maximum of its lower bounds and the minimum of its upper bounds. In topological order, propagate lower bounds forward by taking maxima.

The system is feasible exactly when no propagated lower bound exceeds its component's upper bound. Indeed, when this holds, assigning every component its propagated lower bound satisfies all constraints. These bounds belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$.

If $R$ is the number of compatible cell pairs, the graph test uses
$$ O(K+R) $$
operations, and $R\leq K^2$.

Including input parsing and rational-description checks, a conservative bit bound is
$$ O\bigl((m+\tau+K^2)H^2\bigr). $$

Blocking mass and the minimum requirement

A finite interval matching admits positive blocking mass exactly when some compatible cell pair satisfies
$$ c_{ij}=2. $$

If such a pair exists, select equal positive-length subintervals and match them by translation; both endpoints improve throughout. Conversely, every blocking pair must belong to a cell pair with vote sum $2$, and a positive blocking partial bijection uses at least one such cell pair with positive mass.

Thus a proposed rational finite interval matching can be checked for all three required properties:

  • popularity, by the graph test;
  • positive blocking mass, by detecting a vote sum of $2$;
  • minimum popular mass, by comparing $q(M)$ with the value $q_*$ computed in Section 1.

5. What remains open

The interval characterization is a verification theorem for a proposed representation. It does not establish that every YES instance possesses such a representation, or that its number of cells and binary length can be bounded polynomially in $m,\tau,L$. Consequently, it does not establish membership of the general problem in $\mathrm{NP}$.

The direct finite-to-continuum replacement still fails: one compatible finite applicant–employer pair has no unstable matching, while its balanced continuum replacement has the popular blocking construction in Section 3. A hardness reduction must control these coordinate-based possibilities.

The general classification therefore remains open. The proved advances are the unmatched-reserve obstruction, the complete classification of the common-order complete-component subclass, and the exact finite interval verification theorem.
