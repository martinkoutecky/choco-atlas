Asymptotic existence of fair divisions for groups (d4-journals-mss-2017-0052)

NEW RESULT — continuous: polynomial-time · adversarial review: proved

Answer: Yes: report-independent uniform assignment succeeds above a sufficient threshold of \(O(g\log(\tau g/\varepsilon)/(\mu_{\min}(1-\alpha)^2))\). With unrestricted support size, admissible societies with arbitrarily many items can have no successful assignment.

Theorem 4 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(g\) equal-mass groups, \(m\) indivisible items, \(\alpha\in[0,1)\), \(\varepsilon>0\), and a finite-support society \(\mu\) over complete types \(t=(k,v)\), where \(k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}\), \(v\in[0,1]^m\), \(\sum_v\mu_{k,v}=1/g\), and \(\sum_{j=1}^m v_j\ge m\mu_{\min}\) for every \(t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), construct a report-independent randomized assignment \(a:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\{1,\ldots,g\}\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\), every supported type satisfies \(\sum_{j:a(j)=k}v_j\ge\alpha\sum_{j:a(j)=\ell}v_j\) for every \(\ell\), and determine the sufficient threshold in \(m\) and implementation complexity as functions of \(g,\tau,\alpha,\mu_{\min},\varepsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(g\ge2\) and \(m\ge1\) be integers, with rational parameters \(\alpha\in[0,1)\), \(\mu_{\min}\in(0,1]\), and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) complete types \(t=(k,v)\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_{k,v}\), where \(k\in\{1,\ldots,g\}\) and \(v\in([0,1]\cap\mathbb Q)^m\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Require \(\sum_v\mu_{k,v}=1/g\) for every group and \(\sum_{j=1}^m v_j\ge m\mu_{\min}\) for every supported type. Valuations are additive, and all members of a group share its entire bundle.
An assignment \(a:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\{1,\ldots,g\}\) allocates each indivisible item exactly once; empty bundles are allowed. It is \(\alpha\)-approximately envy-free when \(\sum_{j:a(j)=k}v_j\ge\alpha\sum_{j:a(j)=\ell}v_j\) for every supported \((k,v)\) and every \(\ell\), with equality accepted.
A randomized mechanism is report-independent if its output law depends only on the public parameters \(m,g,\tau,\alpha,\mu_{\min},\varepsilon\), and not on reported valuation vectors or type masses. The society is fixed, and probability refers only to mechanism randomness.
Can a report-independent randomized mechanism guarantee simultaneous \(\alpha\)-approximate envy-freeness with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\) for every such society once \(m\) is sufficiently large, or can this guarantee fail? What sufficient threshold and implementation complexity are attainable in \(m,g,\tau,L,\alpha,\mu_{\min},\varepsilon\)?
For finite societies of \(n\) agents in equal-sized groups, independent random item values in \([0,1]\) with each mean at least \(\mu_{\min}>0\) permit report-independent uniform random assignment to achieve \(\alpha\)-approximate envy-freeness with high probability when \(m>Cg\log n\), where \(C\) depends only on \(\alpha,\mu_{\min}\). [Verified result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.08219).
No result settling the stated fixed, finite-support society model was located in the literature search.

  • Restricted epsilon to (0,1), the nonvacuous failure-probability range.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

A report-independent guarantee with a support-dependent threshold

For every fixed admissible society, independent uniform assignment succeeds with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\) whenever
\[ m\ge \frac{g}{\mu_{\min}(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2} \log\frac{\tau(g-1)}{\varepsilon}, \qquad 0<\alpha<1. \]
In particular, the simpler sufficient threshold
\[ m\ge \frac{4g}{\mu_{\min}(1-\alpha)^2} \log\frac{\tau(g-1)}{\varepsilon} \]
is valid.

Thus the fixed finite-support model admits a guarantee without any randomness assumptions on valuations. However, a threshold based on the number of items alone can fail when support size is unrestricted: there are admissible societies with arbitrarily many items and no approximately envy-free assignment.

When \(\alpha=0\), every assignment satisfies the requirement, so no threshold is needed.

1. Mechanism and probability bound

Assign each item independently and uniformly to one of the \(g\) groups. This mechanism depends only on \(m\) and \(g\), and hence is report-independent.

Fix a supported type \(t=(k,v)\), and write
\[ S_t=\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_j\ge m\mu_{\min}. \]
For another group \(\ell\ne k\), define
\[ D_{t,\ell} = \sum_{j=1}^{m} v_j\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=k\}} -\alpha\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=\ell\}}\bigr). \]
The envy constraint against \(\ell\) fails exactly when \(D_{t,\ell}<0\). Equality therefore counts as success, as required.

We will prove
\[ \Pr(D_{t,\ell}<0) \le \exp\left( -\frac{(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2S_t}{g} \right). \]
Taking a union bound over the \(\tau(g-1)\) relevant constraints then gives
\[ \Pr(\text{some envy constraint fails}) \le \tau(g-1) \exp\left( -\frac{m\mu_{\min}(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2}{g} \right). \]
This establishes the first threshold. The second follows from
\[ (1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2 = \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{(1+\sqrt{\alpha})^2} \ge \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{4}. \]

The probability statement holds for each fixed society, uniformly over all admissible valuation vectors and masses. It does not assert that one random assignment simultaneously works for every possible society.

2. Proof of the individual constraint bound

Assume \(0<\alpha<1\), and set
\[ s=\frac12\log\frac1\alpha>0, \qquad q=(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2. \]
For the contribution of item \(j\), independence and uniform assignment give
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ e^{-sv_j(\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=k\}} -\alpha\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=\ell\}})} \right] = 1+\frac{e^{-sv_j}+e^{\alpha sv_j}-2}{g}. \]

Consider the function
\[ h(x)=e^{-sx}+e^{\alpha sx}-2. \]
It is convex because
\[ h''(x)=s^2e^{-sx}+\alpha^2s^2e^{\alpha sx}\ge0, \]
and \(h(0)=0\). Consequently, for \(0\le x\le1\), the chord inequality gives
\[ h(x)\le xh(1). \]

Convexity of the exponential function also gives
\[ e^{\alpha s} = e^{(1-\alpha)0+\alpha s} \le (1-\alpha)+\alpha e^s. \]
Since \(e^{-s}=\sqrt{\alpha}\) and \(\alpha e^s=\sqrt{\alpha}\), we obtain
\[ h(1) \le e^{-s}+\alpha e^s-1-\alpha = 2\sqrt{\alpha}-1-\alpha = -q. \]
Thus
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ e^{-sv_j(\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=k\}} -\alpha\mathbf 1_{\{a(j)=\ell\}})} \right] \le 1-\frac{qv_j}{g} \le e^{-qv_j/g}. \]

The assignment labels of different items are independent. Multiplying these bounds yields
\[ \mathbb E[e^{-sD_{t,\ell}}] \le \prod_{j=1}^{m}e^{-qv_j/g} = e^{-qS_t/g}. \]
Finally, \(D_{t,\ell}<0\) implies \(e^{-sD_{t,\ell}}>1\). Markov’s inequality therefore gives
\[ \Pr(D_{t,\ell}<0) \le \mathbb E[e^{-sD_{t,\ell}}] \le e^{-qS_t/g}, \]
as claimed.

No independence between different types or different envy constraints was used. The type masses play no role because success is required for every supported type.

3. Implementation complexity

Exact uniform sampling

Let \(b=\lceil\log_2 g\rceil\). For each item, generate a uniform \(b\)-bit integer; accept it if it is less than \(g\), otherwise repeat. An accepted integer specifies the recipient group.

The acceptance probability is \(g/2^b>1/2\), unless \(g\) is a power of two, in which case it is one. Thus exact sampling uses

  • \(O(m)\) expected word operations;
  • \(O(m\log g)\) expected random bits and bit operations;
  • \(O(m\log g)\) bits to store the explicit assignment.

Its running time is finite almost surely but has no deterministic upper bound.

The mechanism need not inspect the valuation vectors or masses. If the input representation requires scanning the complete society, that adds \(O(L)\) input-processing time.

A bounded-time implementation

A deterministic running-time bound is also attainable, using part of the error budget.

For each item, allow at most
\[ R=\left\lceil\log_2\frac{2m}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil \]
sampling attempts. If all attempts reject, assign that item to group \(1\).

Couple this implementation with exact rejection sampling using the same random bits. For any item, the probability that all \(R\) attempts reject is at most \(2^{-R}\). Hence
\[ \Pr(\text{the two assignments differ}) \le m2^{-R}\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \]
Applying the earlier probability bound with error budget \(\varepsilon/2\), the bounded-time mechanism succeeds with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\) whenever
\[ m\ge \frac{g}{\mu_{\min}(1-\sqrt{\alpha})^2} \log\frac{2\tau(g-1)}{\varepsilon}. \]
Its worst-case sampling complexity is
\[ O\!\left(m\log g\log\frac{m}{\varepsilon}\right) \]
bit operations and random bits. Its output law remains report-independent.

If desired, an assignment can separately be verified by calculating all bundle values for each supported type. This takes \(O(\tau(m+g))\) rational arithmetic operations and polynomial time in \(L\): denominators and numerators of the required sums have polynomially bounded bit length. Verification is not needed by the mechanism.

4. Arbitrarily large societies with no successful assignment

We now show why unrestricted support prevents a general item-count-only guarantee.

Fix
\[ g=2,\qquad 0<\alpha<1,\qquad 0<\mu_{\min}<\frac{1+\alpha}{2}. \]
For every sufficiently large \(m\), let
\[ r=\lceil m\mu_{\min}\rceil. \]
Because the inequality on \(\mu_{\min}\) is strict, sufficiently large \(m\) satisfies
\[ r<\frac{1+\alpha}{2}m. \]

In each group, include the valuation vector \(\mathbf 1_S\) for every subset \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\) of cardinality \(r\). Here \(\mathbf 1_S\) values an item at one precisely when it belongs to \(S\). Every supported type satisfies
\[ \sum_j(\mathbf 1_S)_j=r\ge m\mu_{\min}. \]
The support size is
\[ \tau=2\binom mr. \]

These types can receive positive dyadic masses summing to \(1/2\) in each group. Explicitly, put \(N=\binom mr\) and choose
\[ d=\lceil\log_2N\rceil+2. \]
Give the first \(N-1\) types mass \(2^{-d}\), and the last type mass
\[ \frac12-(N-1)2^{-d}>0. \]
Thus the construction satisfies even the interpretation of binary-rational masses as dyadic rationals.

Consider any assignment. Choose a group with the smaller bundle, of cardinality \(b\le m/2\). The other bundle has cardinality
\[ c=m-b\ge m/2. \]

There are two cases.

  • If \(r\le c\): choose an \(r\)-element subset \(S\) of the other bundle. The supported type \(\mathbf 1_S\) in the smaller group values its own bundle at zero and the other bundle at \(r\). Therefore

\[ 0<\alpha r, \]
so its envy constraint fails.

  • If \(r>c\): choose \(S\) to contain the entire other bundle and \(r-c\) items of the smaller bundle. This is possible because \(r\le m\). The corresponding supported type values its own bundle at \(r-c\) and the other bundle at \(c\). But

\[ r<\frac{1+\alpha}{2}m\le(1+\alpha)c, \]
implying
\[ r-c<\alpha c. \]
Its envy constraint again fails.

Every assignment therefore fails for this fixed society. Consequently, every randomized mechanism has success probability zero, regardless of report independence.

For a particularly transparent subfamily, take \(\mu_{\min}=1/2\) and even \(m\). Then \(r=m/2\), and the support is exponential:
\[ \tau=2\binom m{m/2}\ge\frac{2^{m+1}}{m+1}. \]
The inequality follows because the central binomial coefficient is the largest of the \(m+1\) coefficients whose sum is \(2^m\).

This does not contradict the sufficient threshold: these societies have support growing with the number of items.

5. A dense regime where support size is irrelevant

Large support does not always obstruct a guarantee.

Assign items deterministically so that bundle sizes differ by at most one. Put
\[ b_-=\left\lfloor\frac mg\right\rfloor, \qquad b_+=\left\lceil\frac mg\right\rceil. \]
For every supported type, the value of its own bundle is at least
\[ m\mu_{\min}-(m-b_-), \]
because each item outside that bundle has value at most one. Every other bundle has value at most \(b_+\).

Thus this report-independent assignment succeeds for every admissible society whenever
\[ m\mu_{\min}-m+b_-\ge\alpha b_+. \]

In particular, if
\[ \eta=\mu_{\min}-1+\frac{1-\alpha}{g}>0, \]
it suffices that
\[ m\eta\ge1+\alpha. \]
Indeed, \(b_-\ge m/g-1\) and \(b_+\le m/g+1\) imply the preceding condition. At the boundary \(\eta=0\), it also holds whenever \(g\) divides \(m\).

6. Relation to the supplied result

[Theorem 4 of Manurangsi and Suksompong](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.08219) analyzes uniform random assignment under independent random valuations. The proof above instead fixes every valuation vector and uses independence only in the assignment labels.

The stated deterministic lower bound on each type’s total value is sufficient. No sampling of agents, probabilistic assumptions on preferences, or dependence on the smallest positive type mass is required.
