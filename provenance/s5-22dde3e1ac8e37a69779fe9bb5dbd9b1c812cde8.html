The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a set of alternatives \(A\), distinct complete rankings \(\succ_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\), decide whether there is a linear order \(\succ\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\succ_t,\succ)\le B\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(\succ_t\) of a finite nonempty alternative set \(A\), with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t=1\). Here \(m=|A|\), and \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings.
Given a binary rational threshold \(B\in\mathbb{Q}\), decide whether a linear order \(\succ\) of \(A\) satisfies \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\succ_t,\succ)\le B\), where \(\mathrm{KT}\) is the unnormalized Kendall–tau distance. The output is one linear order. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

Discrete Kemeny score is NP-complete even for three voters, both for the total Kendall–tau objective and for the majority-margin disagreement objective, which differ by a profile-dependent additive constant.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Take the alternative set to be finite and nonempty.
  • Use unnormalized Kendall–tau distance, as in the discrete definition.
  • Define L as the total bit length of the rankings, masses, and threshold.

Conclusion and interpretation

The intended decision problem is NP-complete, and finding a feasible order or reporting that none exists is NP-hard. Hardness holds even when the support consists of exactly three distinct rankings, each of mass $1/3$.

I interpret the question as asking whether there exists an order meeting the threshold. If an order is already supplied and only its score must be checked, verification takes polynomial time.

Polynomial-time verification

For rankings $R,S$, define
$$ d(R,S)=\mathrm{KT}(R,S) $$
as the number of unordered pairs of alternatives whose relative order differs between $R$ and $S$. Consequently,
$$ 0\le d(R,S)\le \binom m2. $$

A certificate is a permutation $R$ of the alternatives. After checking that it contains each alternative exactly once, we can compute all distances $d(\succ_t,R)$ by comparing the relative order of every pair in every support ranking.

The rational comparison can also be performed exactly in polynomial time. Write
$$ \mu_t=\frac{p_t}{q_t}, \qquad B=\frac bc, $$
with positive denominators, and put
$$ D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t. $$
Then feasibility is equivalent to the integer inequality
$$ c\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}p_t\frac{D}{q_t}\,d(\succ_t,R)\le bD. $$
The bit length of $D$ is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths, hence is $O(L)$. Since the masses are positive and sum to one,
$$ 0\le \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}p_t\frac{D}{q_t}\,d(\succ_t,R) \le D\binom m2. $$
Thus all integers used in the comparison have $O(L+\log m)=O(L)$ bits. Using elementary integer arithmetic gives the conservative verification bound
$$ O(\tau m^2L^2) $$
bit operations. The certificate has polynomial length, so the existential decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

Reduction from three-voter Kemeny score

Use the three-voter NP-completeness theorem supplied in the task, for the total Kendall–tau objective.

An instance consists of three rankings $R_1,R_2,R_3$ and an integer threshold $K$, and asks whether some ranking $R$ satisfies
$$ d(R_1,R)+d(R_2,R)+d(R_3,R)\le K. $$

Let $S_1,\ldots,S_\tau$ be the distinct rankings among these three, and let $c_t$ be the multiplicity of $S_t$. Construct the society with masses
$$ \mu_t=\frac{c_t}{3} $$
and threshold
$$ B=\frac K3. $$
The support rankings are distinct, the masses are positive binary-encoded rationals, and their sum is one. For every candidate $R$,
$$ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,d(S_t,R) = \frac13\bigl(d(R_1,R)+d(R_2,R)+d(R_3,R)\bigr). $$
Therefore the constructed instance is feasible exactly when the original instance is feasible.

The construction has polynomial running time and output length; $O(m^2L^2)$ bit operations is a conservative bound. It already establishes NP-hardness with support size at most three.

Exactly three distinct rankings suffice

For completeness, repeated rankings in the source instances can be handled without weakening the restriction to exactly three distinct support rankings.

First, Kendall–tau distance satisfies the triangle inequality:
$$ d(X,Z)\le d(X,Y)+d(Y,Z). $$
Indeed, for each unordered pair, disagreement between $X$ and $Z$ implies disagreement between $X$ and $Y$ or between $Y$ and $Z$. Summing this pairwise inequality proves the claim.

If a three-voter profile has repeated rankings, write it as $X,X,Y$, allowing $X=Y$. Every candidate $R$ satisfies
$$ \begin{aligned} 2d(X,R)+d(Y,R) &\ge d(X,R)+d(X,Y)\\ &\ge d(X,Y). \end{aligned} $$
The first inequality uses the triangle inequality, and the second uses nonnegativity. Equality is attained at $R=X$. Thus the optimum is exactly $d(X,Y)$, which can be computed in polynomial time.

Modify the reduction as follows:

  • If the three source rankings are distinct, use masses $1/3$ and threshold $K/3$.
  • If a ranking repeats, compute the optimum above and output a fixed yes-instance or a fixed no-instance according to whether it is at most $K$.

For both fixed instances, take the three distinct rankings
$$ a\succ b\succ c,\qquad a\succ c\succ b,\qquad b\succ a\succ c, $$
each with mass $1/3$. Threshold $3$ gives a yes-instance because every distance on three alternatives is at most $3$. Threshold $0$ gives a no-instance: a weighted sum of nonnegative distances with positive weights can equal zero only if the candidate equals all three support rankings, which is impossible.

This is a polynomial-time reduction whose every output has exactly three distinct rankings of equal mass. Hence the decision problem remains NP-complete under that restriction.

Running-time implications

An exhaustive algorithm enumerates all $m!$ orders and verifies each, taking
$$ O(m!\,\tau m^2L^2) $$
bit operations. A polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,L$ exists if and only if $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.

For the requested output, a polynomial-time algorithm that returns a feasible order or reports infeasibility would decide this NP-complete problem. Conversely, if $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$, a feasible order can be constructed in polynomial time by extending a prefix one alternative at a time: the question whether a prefix has a feasible completion belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, and at most $m^2$ such queries suffice.
