The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many worker types \(T\) with rational shares \(\mu_t\), chore classes \(J\), and additive costs \(c_t(j)\), an online adversary reveals chore-class mass increments whose total supply per worker is \(q_j\). A causal policy assigns each increment by moving worker mass from states \((t,B)\) to \((t,B+e_j)\), so each worker has an integral bundle. Determine the least \(\alpha\) for which a deterministic policy guarantees, for every type with positive mass, \(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\mid t}\sum_{j\in J}c_t(j)B_j\leq \alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)\), where \(\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)=\inf_{\pi:\,\mathbb E_{B\sim\pi}[B_j]=q_j\ \forall j}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi}\sum_{j\in J}c_t(j)B_j\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A nonatomic society has total mass \(1\), given by its support: \(\tau\) worker types \(T\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Initially a deterministic policy knows only this support and its masses. An adaptive adversary reveals a finite sequence of arrivals \((j,\delta)\), with positive binary-rational chore mass \(\delta\). On a class's first arrival, its nonnegative binary-rational cost column \((c_t(j))_{t\in T}\) is revealed and thereafter fixed. Future classes, costs, supplies, and the stopping time are unknown.
Workers initially have empty bundles. Bundles are \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{J}\), with additive cost \(c_t\cdot B\). Each arrival must be assigned completely and irrevocably before the next arrival, by moving available worker mass along \((t,B)\to(t,B+e_j)\), where \(e_j\) is the unit vector for class \(j\); moving mass \(x\) assigns chore mass \(x\). Mass may be split, and a worker may receive multiple copies during an arrival. Assignments preserve each type's mass and use no randomness.
Let \(J\) be the classes eventually revealed and \(q_j\) their total arriving mass. Define \(\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)=\inf_{\pi:\,\mathbb E_{\pi}B_j=q_j\ \forall j}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi}c_t\cdot B\), where \(\pi\) ranges over probability distributions on \(\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{J}\). A policy is \(\alpha\)-competitive if, at every possible termination, its conditional bundle distribution \(\rho_t\) satisfies \(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\rho_t}c_t\cdot B\leq\alpha\,\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)\) for every \(t\in T\), including types with zero benchmark.
For running times, use \(m=|J|\), support size \(\tau\), arrival count \(K\), and total binary input length \(L\).

For each support-mass vector \(\mu\), what is the optimal deterministic competitive factor \(\alpha^*(\mu)=\inf\{\alpha\geq0:\text{an }\alpha\text{-competitive causal policy exists}\}\), uniformly over all finite admissible arrival sequences?

For discrete workers with unknown future costs and stopping time, the optimal deterministic factor is exactly \(n\): the lower bound matches the trivial upper bound ([Seddighin–Seddighin](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.12984v1)). An independent result also excludes every factor \(n-\varepsilon\) for \(\varepsilon>0\) ([Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14039)).
No result settling this exact nonatomic-worker, integral-bundle online model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the nonatomic society to total mass 1, so total arriving class mass equals supply per worker.
  • Made costs nonnegative binary rationals, arrival masses positive binary rationals, and bundles nonnegative integer count vectors allowing repeated copies of a class.
  • Restored the discrete online information restriction: only the society is known initially; cost columns are revealed upon first arrival, and future classes, total supplies, and termination are unknown.
  • Required complete immediate irreversible assignment, with initially empty bundles; allowed splitting worker mass and repeated unit assignments within one arrival.
  • Specified that the benchmark ranges over probability distributions on integral bundles and that zero benchmarks are handled by the stated inequality.
  • Interpreted the least factor as an infimum uniform over all finite adaptive arrival sequences for a fixed support-mass vector, without imposing a computational restriction on policies.

1. Benchmark properties and rational optimal witnesses

Fix a finite instance and one cost vector $c$. Write
$$ S=\sum_{j\in J}c(j)q_j, \qquad P=\max_{j\in J}c(j), $$
with $P=0$ when $J$ is empty.

Every feasible benchmark distribution $\pi$ satisfies
$$ \mathbb E_\pi[c\cdot B] =\sum_{j\in J}c(j)\mathbb E_\pi B_j =S. $$
Its essential supremum is therefore at least $S$.

Also, every revealed class has $q_j>0$. Because $B_j$ is a nonnegative integer, $\mathbb E_\pi B_j>0$ implies $\pi(B_j\geq1)>0$. Nonnegative costs then imply that the essential supremum is at least $c(j)$. Consequently,
$$ \mathrm{MMS}(q)\geq\max\{S,P\}. $$

The benchmark is finite. Independently rounding each $q_j$ to its floor or ceiling, with probabilities giving mean $q_j$, produces a feasible distribution supported on finitely many bundles. Every supported bundle has cost at most
$$ H_0=\sum_{j\in J}c(j)\lceil q_j\rceil. $$

These observations give the exact zero case:
$$ \mathrm{MMS}(q)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c(j)=0\text{ for every revealed class }j. $$

Lemma 1: rational attainment

For rational costs and supplies, the benchmark is attained by a finite-support distribution with rational probabilities. In particular, $\mathrm{MMS}(q)$ is rational.

Proof. First suppose every revealed cost is positive. The set
$$ \mathcal B(H_0) =\{B\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{J}:c\cdot B\leq H_0\} $$
is finite: each coordinate satisfies $B_j\leq H_0/c(j)$.

Every distribution with essential supremum at most $H_0$ is supported on this finite set. Its essential supremum is thus one of the finitely many rational bundle costs in this set. Since a feasible distribution with cap $H_0$ exists, there is a smallest feasible cap among those costs. This cap equals the defining infimum.

At that cap, the probabilities solve
$$ \sum_B\lambda_B=1, \qquad \sum_B\lambda_B B_j=q_j\quad(j\in J), \qquad \lambda_B\geq0. $$
This system has a rational solution. Here is a direct justification. Choose a feasible solution with the fewest positive entries. The corresponding columns $(1,B)$ must be linearly independent: otherwise a nonzero dependence, whose coefficients sum to zero, allows a perturbation preserving all equations and nonnegativity until a positive entry becomes zero. Linear independence implies that the remaining probabilities are uniquely determined by a rational linear system, so they are rational.

If some costs are zero, delete those coordinates. Projection shows that the reduced benchmark cannot exceed the original benchmark. Conversely, extend any reduced feasible distribution by independently rounding the deleted coordinates to their prescribed means. This changes no costs, so the two benchmarks are equal. Applying the positive-cost argument and this finite rational extension proves the claim. The all-zero case follows directly from rounding. $\square$

2. Exact independence from the support masses

Let $A=\alpha^*((1))$. In fact, every support-mass vector has exactly the same set of achievable factors.

Suppose first that a one-type policy is $\alpha$-competitive. For each actual type $t$, run a separate virtual copy of that policy on a population of mass $1$, feeding it every actual arrival $(j,\delta)$ and the scalar cost $c_t(j)$.

Implement a virtual movement of mass $x$ inside type $t$ as an actual movement of mass $\mu_t x$. The arrival assigns mass $\mu_t\delta$ to type $t$, and therefore assigns total mass
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\delta=\delta. $$
Each actual conditional bundle distribution equals its virtual distribution. The virtual benchmark is precisely $\mathrm{MMS}_t(q)$, so the desired guarantee holds for every type. This construction is deterministic and causal.

Conversely, suppose an $\alpha$-competitive policy exists for a fixed vector $\mu$. In a one-type population, maintain virtual subpopulations of masses $\mu_t$. On revelation of a scalar cost $c(j)$, give the simulated policy the admissible column
$$ c_t(j)=c(j)\quad(t\in T). $$
Execute its movements within the corresponding subpopulations. If their conditional distributions are $\rho_t$, the one-type population has distribution
$$ \rho=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\rho_t. $$
All components have the same benchmark and satisfy the same almost-sure cost bound. Their finite mixture satisfies that bound too. The virtual labels are deterministic internal state.

Thus the achievable-factor sets coincide, and
$$ \alpha^*(\mu)=A $$
for every admissible $\mu$.

It consequently suffices to study one type.

3. A constructive factor-$2$ policy, including zero-cost arrivals

Maintain finitely many positive-mass cohorts, each with a common bundle and a permanent identifier. On an arrival, repeatedly give one copy to a minimum-cost cohort, breaking ties by identifier. Move the entire cohort unless only a smaller mass remains; then split it and finish the arrival. Full movements retain the cohort identifier.

Before the final partial movement, cohort masses remain unchanged. Each full movement therefore consumes at least the smallest cohort mass present at the start of the arrival. Since the arriving mass is finite, execution terminates. Only the final movement can split a cohort, so there are at most $K+1$ cohort records after $K$ arrivals.

Immediately before a movement of a copy costing $p$, let $a$ be the chosen cohort’s cost and $s$ the current average cost. Minimum-cost selection gives $a\leq s$. At any later termination, nonnegative costs give $s\leq S$, where $S$ is the final average cost. Hence the movement produces cost at most
$$ a+p\leq S+P. $$
Apply this inequality to each worker’s last positive-cost movement. Subsequent zero-cost movements leave its cost unchanged; workers without positive-cost movements have cost zero. Thus
$$ \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\rho}c\cdot B \leq S+P \leq2\,\mathrm{MMS}(q). $$
This includes the zero-benchmark case.

Bulk processing when the arriving cost is positive

Let the current cohort masses and loads be $w_i,\ell_i$, and let the arriving cost be $p>0$. Cohort $i$ generates completion events
$$ \ell_i+p,\ \ell_i+2p,\ \ell_i+3p,\ldots, $$
each accounting for chore mass $w_i$. Greedy processes these events in increasing order: comparing current loads is equivalent to comparing the next completion loads.

Define
$$ F(h)=\sum_i w_i \max\left\{0,\left\lfloor\frac{h-\ell_i}{p}\right\rfloor\right\}. $$
Let $H$ be the smallest event value satisfying $F(H)\geq\delta$.

For each cohort $i$, binary-search the first event index satisfying this inequality in the interval
$$ 1\leq k\leq\left\lceil\frac{\delta}{w_i}\right\rceil. $$
The upper endpoint succeeds because that cohort alone contributes at least $\delta$. The smallest successful event value over all cohorts is $H$.

Process all events below $H$, then events equal to $H$ in identifier order until the arrival is exhausted. At most one cohort is partially moved. Counts of repeated copies are stored in binary.

Bulk processing when the arriving cost is zero

Choose a minimum-cost cohort of mass $w$. Write
$$ k=\left\lfloor\frac{\delta}{w}\right\rfloor, \qquad r=\delta-kw, \qquad 0\leq r<w. $$
Give $k$ copies to the entire cohort, and, if $r>0$, give one additional copy to a subcohort of mass $r$.

These are exactly the greedy movements: the selected cohort’s cost and identifier stay unchanged through every full movement. This uses two bulk records and at most one split.

Running time

For a normalized one-type simulation, every cohort mass has denominator dividing the product $D$ of the arrival denominators. Indeed, splitting subtracts an integer combination of existing masses from an arriving mass. Since the binary length of $D$ is $O(L)$, every positive cohort mass is at least $1/D$.

Total arriving supply is at most $2^{O(L)}$. A positive-mass cohort’s total copy count is therefore at most
$$ D\sum_{k=1}^{K}\delta_k=2^{O(L)}. $$
Masses, copy counts, loads, and event indices consequently have polynomial binary length. There are at most $K+1$ cohorts, and the binary searches use polynomially many rational arithmetic operations.

Running the simulations from Section 2 therefore gives a factor-$2$ policy computable in time polynomial in $\tau,K,m,L$, when repeated unit transitions are represented by bulk records. Expanding those transitions individually can take exponential time.

4. Finite arrival trees and compactness

We now prove a principle that permits better existence bounds.

Removing zero-cost arrivals

For one type, erase all zero-cost arrivals from the input supplied to a positive-cost policy. Assign each erased arrival separately, using the zero-cost bulk procedure above.

Such assignments leave the distribution of positive-cost bundles unchanged. When the positive-cost policy subsequently requests a movement from a positive-cost bundle, collect the required mass from its finitely many cohorts, splitting deterministically as necessary.

By Lemma 1’s projection-and-extension argument, erasing zero-cost coordinates also leaves the benchmark unchanged. Thus a policy for strictly positive costs extends to the full one-type model with the same factor.

For the remainder of this section, all revealed costs are positive.

Finite-tree linear programming formulation

A history consists of all arrivals and revelations up to a prefix. Normalize class names by their order of first appearance. Since inputs are finite binary strings, the set of admissible histories is countable.

For a history $h$, write its benchmark as $M_h$. Fix the finite configuration set
$$ \mathcal B_h =\{B\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{J(h)}:c_h\cdot B\leq2M_h\}. $$
At the empty history, use only the empty bundle.

Let $\mathcal F$ be any finite collection of histories containing the empty history and closed under taking prefixes. Fix a proposed factor $r\in[0,2]$. Introduce probabilities $\rho_h(B)$ for $B\in\mathcal B_h$.

For an edge corresponding to an arrival $(j,\delta)$ from parent $h^-$ to child $h$, introduce variables
$$ x_h(B,k)\geq0 $$
for every parent bundle $B$ and nonnegative integer $k$ whose endpoint $B+ke_j$ belongs to $\mathcal B_h$. If $j$ is new, extend $B$ by a zero coordinate.

The constraints are
$$ \sum_k x_h(B,k)=\rho_{h^-}(B), $$
$$ \rho_h(C) =\sum_{\substack{B,k\\B+ke_j=C}}x_h(B,k), $$
$$ \sum_{B,k}k\,x_h(B,k)=\delta, $$
together with $\rho_{\varnothing}(0)=1$ and
$$ \rho_h(B)=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad c_h\cdot B>rM_h. $$

All index sets are finite because every cost is positive.

These constraints characterize deterministic causal strategies on $\mathcal F$:

  • A strategy induces $x_h(B,k)$ as the mass starting the arrival with bundle $B$ and receiving exactly $k$ copies.
  • Conversely, feasible variables prescribe a deterministic partition of each parent cohort into masses $x_h(B,k)$. Give each such subcohort $k$ successive copies. The equations preserve population mass, produce the child distribution, and assign exactly $\delta$ chore mass.

The outgoing constraints for different children use the same parent distribution. This enforces causality at shared prefixes.

Lemma 2: compactness of causal strategies

If every finite prefix-closed arrival tree admits an $r$-competitive strategy, where $r\in[0,2]$, then a single $r$-competitive policy exists for all finite admissible arrival sequences.

Proof. Enumerate the histories and let $\mathcal F_n$ contain the first $n$ histories and all their prefixes. Choose a feasible table on each $\mathcal F_n$.

Across all histories, there are countably many probability and movement coordinates, each in $[0,1]$. Extend coordinates outside $\mathcal F_n$ arbitrarily by zero. Repeated subsequence extraction, followed by a diagonal selection, gives a subsequence along which every coordinate converges.

Every constraint at a fixed history involves finitely many coordinates and holds once that history belongs to $\mathcal F_n$. Passing to the limit preserves all mass equations, supply equations, nonnegativity constraints, and forbidden-support conditions.

The limiting tables therefore define a causal policy by the cohort-partition construction above. Every arrival uses finitely many subcohorts and finitely many unit transitions. The task restricts the input data to binary rationals but places no rationality restriction on split masses, so real limiting masses are admissible. Finally, extend the policy to zero-cost arrivals as described above. $\square$

Corollary: the optimal factor is attained

For a finite tree $\mathcal F$, let $a(\mathcal F)$ be its smallest achievable factor.

This minimum exists and is rational. Within $[0,2]$, feasibility changes only when $r$ crosses one of the finitely many rational values
$$ \frac{c_h\cdot B}{M_h}, \qquad h\in\mathcal F,\quad B\in\mathcal B_h, $$
with the empty history omitted. Eligibility includes equality, and feasibility at $2$ follows from Section 3. Hence the first feasible threshold is attained.

Set
$$ \gamma=\sup_{\mathcal F}a(\mathcal F). $$
Any globally competitive factor must be at least $\gamma$. Conversely, every finite tree admits a $\gamma$-competitive strategy, since $a(\mathcal F)\leq\gamma\leq2$. Lemma 2 gives a global $\gamma$-competitive policy. Therefore
$$ A=\gamma=\sup_{\mathcal F}a(\mathcal F), $$
and an $A$-competitive policy exists.

This also gives a finite-obstruction principle: if a factor below $2$ is impossible globally, it is already impossible on some finite arrival tree.

5. Transferring the scheduling upper bound

The following is the sole external theorem used.

Fleischer–Wahl scheduling theorem. Let
$$ C=1+\sqrt{\frac{1+\ln2}{2}}. $$
For every $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently large numbers $N$ of identical machines admit a deterministic online algorithm with competitive ratio at most $C+\varepsilon$ for makespan minimization. Jobs have known positive processing times when they arrive and must be assigned immediately without knowledge of future jobs or the stopping time. This is the quantified asymptotic guarantee of Theorem 1 in [*Online Scheduling Revisited*](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bor/2421f19/papers/fleischer-best-makespan.pdf).

Lemma 3: exact finite simulations

For any finite positive-cost arrival tree $\mathcal F$, there is an integer $D>0$ such that, for every positive multiple $N$ of $D$:

  • every arrival mass $\delta$ becomes an integer job count $N\delta$;
  • at every history $h\in\mathcal F$, the resulting $N$-machine offline optimum is exactly $M_h$.

Proof. By Lemma 1, choose a rational optimal benchmark distribution $\pi_h$ at every history. Choose $D$ divisible by all arrival denominators and all probability denominators in these finitely many distributions.

Replace an arrival $(j,\delta)$ by $N\delta$ individual jobs of processing time $c(j)$.

At history $h$, any schedule of these jobs on $N$ machines produces an empirical bundle distribution with mean $q_j$ in each coordinate: the total number of class-$j$ jobs is $Nq_j$. Its makespan is therefore at least $M_h$.

Conversely, if $\pi_h(B)$ is the chosen witness probability, use exactly $N\pi_h(B)$ machines with bundle $B$. These numbers are integers and sum to $N$. The coordinate means ensure exactly the required number of jobs of every class, and every machine has load at most $M_h$. This gives an offline schedule of makespan at most $M_h$. $\square$

The improved bound

Fix $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small that $C+\varepsilon<2$, and fix a finite arrival tree $\mathcal F$.

Choose a multiple $N$ of the integer in Lemma 3 large enough for the Fleischer–Wahl theorem. Run its deterministic scheduling algorithm, replacing each arrival by its $N\delta$ jobs in a fixed order.

Interpret every machine as a worker cohort of mass $1/N$. Each job assignment is then a legal unit bundle movement assigning chore mass $1/N$. An entire arrival assigns exactly $\delta$.

This is causal on $\mathcal F$: shared arrival prefixes generate identical job prefixes and hence identical scheduling decisions. The theorem’s hypotheses hold—there are $N$ identical machines, processing times are positive and revealed upon arrival, assignments are irrevocable, and the algorithm has no future or stopping-time information.

At every history, Lemma 3 identifies the offline scheduling optimum with $M_h$. The induced strategy is therefore $(C+\varepsilon)$-competitive.

Since $\mathcal F$ was arbitrary, Lemma 2 gives a global $(C+\varepsilon)$-competitive policy. Letting $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero proves
$$ A\leq C. $$
Because $A$ is attained and competitive guarantees are monotone in the factor, a global $C$-competitive policy exists as well.

The common simulation size $N$ is selected using a finite tree only inside the existence proof. Lemma 2 supplies the single policy that works without knowing the realized future tree.

6. The lower bound $1+1/\sqrt2$

For completeness, here is a self-contained proof of the lower bound from the notes.

Fix any proposed factor $\alpha<\beta$. Define
$$ r_1(a)=\frac{1+2a}{1+a}, \qquad r_2(a)=\frac{1+3a}{2a}. $$
At $a_*=1+\sqrt2$,
$$ r_1(a_*)=r_2(a_*)=\beta. $$
By continuity and density of dyadic rationals, choose a dyadic rational $a\geq2$ such that
$$ \alpha<r_1(a), \qquad \alpha<r_2(a). $$

In the one-type problem, reveal three distinct classes:

| Arrival | Class | Mass | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | $u$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| Second | $v$ | $1$ | $a$ |
| Third | $w$ | $1/4$ | $2a$ |

All input data are admissible.

Suppose a policy satisfies the proposed guarantee at every prefix.

At the first prefix, the benchmark is exactly $1$: average cost gives the lower bound, and one copy per worker gives equality. Since $\alpha<2$, the policy cannot give two copies to positive worker mass. Conservation of the assigned supply then forces
$$ B_u=1 $$
almost surely.

At the second prefix, the benchmark is exactly $1+a$, again by average cost and the constant bundle $(1,1)$. A worker receiving two copies of $v$ would have cost at least $1+2a$, and
$$ 1+2a>\alpha(1+a). $$
Thus $B_v\leq1$ almost surely. Its mean is $1$, so
$$ B_v=1 $$
almost surely. Every worker now has cost $1+a$.

At the final prefix, the benchmark is exactly $2a$. The positive supply of a class costing $2a$ gives the lower bound. The following distribution gives the matching upper bound:

| Probability | Bundle $(B_u,B_v,B_w)$ | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| $1/4$ | $(4,0,0)$ | $4$ |
| $1/2$ | $(0,2,0)$ | $2a$ |
| $1/4$ | $(0,0,1)$ | $2a$ |

Its means are $(1,1,1/4)$, and $4\leq2a$.

Positive arriving mass of $w$ must be assigned to positive worker mass. Every recipient already has cost $1+a$, so its final cost is at least $1+3a$. But
$$ 1+3a>\alpha(2a), $$
contradicting competitiveness.

If the policy fails at an earlier prefix, the adversary simply stops there. Therefore no $\alpha<\beta$ is achievable, and
$$ A\geq1+\frac1{\sqrt2}. $$
Section 2 transfers this bound to every support-mass vector.

7. What is proved and what remains open

The proved interval is
$$ 1+\frac1{\sqrt2} \leq \alpha^*(\mu)=A \leq 1+\sqrt{\frac{1+\ln2}{2}} <1.9201. $$

The achievable-factor sets coincide for all support masses, and an optimal policy exists. The factor-$2$ policy has a polynomial-time bulk implementation covering zero costs.

The smaller upper bound is established through compactness. Its effective implementation and running time are not established here. Most importantly, these results do not determine the exact value of $A$.
