The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant types \(I\), position types \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), and their weak preferences and acceptability, choose an assignment-mass matrix \(x\) with \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), \(x_{ij}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, and row and column sums bounded by \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\); maximize \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\), then minimize \(B(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\text{ mutually acceptable}} A_{ij}(x)C_{ij}(x)\), where \(A_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{j'}x_{ij'}+\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\) and \(C_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{i'}x_{i'j}+\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j i}x_{i'j}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I\) and \(J\) be nonempty, disjoint sets of applicant and position types. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Masses need not sum to one, and the two sides may have unequal total mass.
Each type has a weak preference order over acceptable opposite-side types; individuals within a type are indistinguishable. Let \(E\subseteq I\times J\) contain the mutually acceptable pairs, with every type incident to at least one pair. Every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched; ties are retained, and \(\succ\) denotes strict preference.
A feasible assignment is a real matrix \(x\geq0\) with \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E\), \(\sum_{j\in J}x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\), and \(\sum_{i\in I}x_{ij}\leq\nu_j\). Write \(S^*=\max_x\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\).
For \((i,j)\in E\), define \(A_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{j'\in J}x_{ij'}+\sum_{j'\in J:\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}\) and \(C_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{i'\in I}x_{i'j}+\sum_{i'\in I:\,i\succ_j i'}x_{i'j}\); preference-restricted sums range over acceptable types.
Define \(B(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}A_{ij}(x)C_{ij}(x)\). Given a binary-rational threshold \(b\geq0\), decide whether a feasible \(x\) satisfies \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}=S^*\) and \(B(x)\leq b\).
Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\) or NP-hard? If NP-hard, is it FPT parameterised by \(\tau\)? State running times in \(m=|J|\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), including the threshold.
The discrete maximum-cardinality, minimum-blocking-pairs problem is FPT parameterised by the number of types ([Meeks–Rastegari](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/222769/7/222769.pdf)), but NP-hard even with strict preferences ([Biró–Manlove–Mittal](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/5930/1/minbp.pdf)).
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was located.

Classification

The stated decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete and fixed-parameter tractable parameterised by \(\tau\).

Writing \(d=|E|\), an exact algorithm runs in
\[ 2^{d+\tau}L^{O(1)} \;\leq\; 2^{m(\tau-m)+\tau}L^{O(1)} \;=\; 2^{O(\tau^2)}L^{O(1)}. \]
The exponent hidden in \(L^{O(1)}\) is an absolute constant. All running times below count bit operations, and \(L\) includes the threshold.

The hardness result already holds when every mass equals \(1\), the two sides have equal size, the acceptability graph has a perfect matching, and \(b=0\). The reduction uses ties.

1. Nonnegativity and zero blocking mass

For every feasible assignment \(x\), each \(A_{ij}(x)\) is the sum of unmatched applicant mass and applicant mass assigned to partners strictly worse than \(j\). Thus \(A_{ij}(x)\geq0\). Similarly, \(C_{ij}(x)\geq0\).

Consequently,
\[ B(x)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{for every }(i,j)\in E,\quad A_{ij}(x)=0\ \text{or}\ C_{ij}(x)=0. \]

This equivalence will allow a zero-blocking fractional assignment to be rounded without introducing a blocking pair.

2. A rounding lemma for unit masses

Lemma. Suppose \(|I|=|J|=n\), all masses equal \(1\), and \(x\) is feasible with total assigned mass \(n\). If \(B(x)=0\), then the positive support of \(x\) contains a weakly stable perfect matching.

Here, weak stability means that no mutually acceptable pair strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners.

Proof. The total row capacity and total column capacity both equal \(n\). Since \(x\) assigns total mass \(n\), every row and every column sums to \(1\).

Let
\[ H=\{(i,j)\in E:x_{ij}>0\}. \]
For any \(U\subseteq I\), let \(N_H(U)\) be its neighbourhood in \(H\). Then
\[ |U| = \sum_{i\in U}\sum_jx_{ij} = \sum_{j\in N_H(U)}\sum_{i\in U}x_{ij} \leq \sum_{j\in N_H(U)}\sum_{i\in I}x_{ij} = |N_H(U)|. \]
Hence Hall’s condition holds. Hall’s theorem gives a matching \(M\subseteq H\) covering \(I\); because the sides have equal size, \(M\) is perfect.

Consider any \((i,j)\in E\). Since \(B(x)=0\), at least one of \(A_{ij}(x)\) and \(C_{ij}(x)\) vanishes.

If \(A_{ij}(x)=0\), row saturation gives
\[ 0=A_{ij}(x) =\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i j'}x_{ij'}. \]
Every summand is nonnegative. Therefore no supported partner of \(i\) is strictly worse than \(j\). In particular, \(x_{i,M(i)}>0\) implies that \(i\) does not strictly prefer \(j\) to \(M(i)\).

Likewise, if \(C_{ij}(x)=0\), then \(j\) does not strictly prefer \(i\) to \(M(j)\).

Thus \((i,j)\) cannot block \(M\). This applies to every acceptable pair, proving weak stability. \(\square\)

Conversely, the incidence matrix of any weakly stable perfect matching has \(B=0\): for each acceptable pair, at least one endpoint has no strict improvement, so its corresponding dissatisfaction factor vanishes.

3. NP-hardness

We use the following established theorem: deciding whether an instance of stable marriage with ties and incomplete lists has a weakly stable perfect matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is Theorem 1 of [Iwama–Manlove–Miyazaki–Morita](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/1066/1/ICALP99.pdf). Their convention explicitly requires a matching to cover everyone, and their stability definition requires strict improvement at both endpoints.

Take such an instance with \(n\) agents on each side. Compute whether its mutually acceptable graph has a perfect matching.

If it does, create one listed type for each agent, preserve all preferences and acceptable pairs, set every mass to \(1\), and set \(b=0\). Every type is incident to an acceptable pair, and
\[ S^*=n: \]
an ordinary perfect matching attains \(n\), while total applicant capacity gives the upper bound \(n\).

The equivalence needed for the reduction is now exact:

  • A weakly stable perfect matching gives a feasible assignment of total mass \(S^*=n\) with \(B=0\).
  • A feasible assignment of total mass \(S^*=n\) with \(B\leq0\) has \(B=0\), and the rounding lemma gives a weakly stable perfect matching.

If the source graph has no perfect matching, the source instance is necessarily a no-instance. Map it to the following fixed no-instance, which satisfies all the task’s promises.

Take
\[ I=\{u,v\},\qquad J=\{p,q\},\qquad E=\{(u,p),(u,q),(v,p)\}, \]
with all masses equal to \(1\), threshold \(b=0\), and preferences
\[ u:\ p\succ q,\qquad v:\ p,\qquad p:\ u\succ v,\qquad q:\ u. \]
Here \(S^*=2\). Any assignment attaining \(2\) must saturate every type, so necessarily
\[ x_{uq}=x_{vp}=1,\qquad x_{up}=0. \]
For the pair \((u,p)\), both dissatisfaction factors equal \(1\). The other products vanish, so \(B(x)=1\). Thus this is a no-instance.

The construction and preliminary matching computation take polynomial time. Therefore the continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

4. An exact algorithm for quadratic optimisation

We prove a general lemma that supplies both the parameterised algorithm and the rational certificates needed for membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Lemma. Let
\[ P=\{z\in\mathbb R^d:Gz\leq h,\ Hz=k\} \]
be a nonempty bounded rational polyhedron, with \(r\) inequality rows. Let
\[ q(z)=z^{\mathsf T}Qz+c^{\mathsf T}z+\gamma, \qquad Q=Q^{\mathsf T}, \]
have rational coefficients. If the total encoding length is \(N\), then:

  • the minimum of \(q\) over \(P\) can be computed exactly in \(2^rN^{O(1)}\) bit operations;
  • some global minimiser has rational coordinates of total encoding length \(N^{O(1)}\).

No convexity assumption is required.

4.1 The algorithm

For every subset \(F\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\), form
\[ H_F= \begin{pmatrix} H\\G_F \end{pmatrix}, \qquad k_F= \begin{pmatrix} k\\h_F \end{pmatrix}. \]
Compute a rational matrix \(D_F\) whose columns form a basis of \(\ker H_F\).

Using rational linear programming, test feasibility of the linear system
\[ z\in P,\qquad H_Fz=k_F,\qquad D_F^{\mathsf T}(2Qz+c)=0. \]
If feasible, obtain a rational feasible point \(z_F\) and evaluate \(q(z_F)\) exactly. Return the smallest recorded value.

All the displayed constraints are linear in \(z\).

4.2 The objective is constant on each candidate set

Suppose \(z\) and \(w\) satisfy the system for the same \(F\), and put \(v=w-z\). Their common equality constraints imply
\[ H_Fv=0. \]
Because \(v\) belongs to the span of the columns of \(D_F\), stationarity at both points gives
\[ v^{\mathsf T}(2Qz+c)=0, \qquad v^{\mathsf T}(2Qw+c)=0. \]
Subtracting yields \(2v^{\mathsf T}Qv=0\). Therefore
\[ q(w)-q(z) =(2Qz+c)^{\mathsf T}v+v^{\mathsf T}Qv =0. \]
Thus any feasible representative has the same objective value as every other point in that candidate set.

4.3 Some candidate set contains a global minimiser

Since \(P\) is nonempty and compact, the continuous function \(q\) has a global minimiser \(z^*\).

Let \(F^*\) contain exactly the inequalities tight at \(z^*\). For any \(v\in\ker H_{F^*}\), all equalities and tight inequalities remain equalities along \(z^*+tv\).

Every other inequality has positive slack at \(z^*\). Since there are finitely many inequalities, there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that
\[ z^*+tv\in P\qquad\text{whenever }|t|<\varepsilon. \]
The function \(t\mapsto q(z^*+tv)\) therefore has a local minimum at \(0\), giving
\[ (2Qz^*+c)^{\mathsf T}v=0. \]
This holds for every \(v\in\ker H_{F^*}\), so
\[ D_{F^*}^{\mathsf T}(2Qz^*+c)=0. \]
Hence \(z^*\) belongs to the candidate set for \(F^*\). By the preceding calculation, the algorithm’s representative of that set is also a global minimiser.

Every representative considered by the algorithm lies in \(P\). Thus no recorded value is below the global minimum, and at least one equals it. The algorithm is correct.

4.4 Bit complexity and rational minimisers

Rational Gaussian elimination computes each \(D_F\) in polynomial bit complexity, with output encoding length polynomial in \(N\). Consequently, each candidate system has polynomial encoding length.

Exact rational linear programming decides feasibility and returns a polynomial-size rational feasible point in polynomial bit time. There are \(2^r\) subsets \(F\), proving the running-time bound.

For completeness, the existence of a polynomial-size rational representative also follows from boundedness. A nonempty candidate set is a bounded rational polyhedron, so it has a vertex. A vertex is determined by \(d\) linearly independent tight constraints. After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule expresses its coordinates as ratios of determinants whose encoding lengths are polynomial in \(N\). For \(F^*\), such a vertex is a global minimiser by Section 4.2. \(\square\)

5. Application to the assignment problem

Use one variable per acceptable pair, so the dimension is \(d=|E|\).

First compute \(S^*\) by rational linear programming: maximise the sum of assignments subject to the given capacity and nonnegativity constraints. The feasible region is nonempty because it contains zero, and bounded because every variable is bounded by its incident capacities. Thus \(S^*\) is computable exactly in polynomial bit time and has polynomial encoding length.

The maximum-cardinality feasible region is
\[ P^*= \left\{ x\in\mathbb R^d: x\geq0,\ \sum_jx_{ij}\leq\mu_i,\ \sum_ix_{ij}\leq\nu_j,\ \sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}=S^* \right\}. \]
It is nonempty and compact.

There are exactly \(d+\tau\) displayed inequalities: \(d\) nonnegativity constraints and \(\tau\) capacity constraints. The cardinality condition is an equality.

Each \(A_{ij}\) and \(C_{ij}\) is affine with rational coefficients, so \(B\) is a rational quadratic polynomial. Expanding these \(d\) products produces a polynomial-size encoding; symmetrising its quadratic coefficient matrix gives the form required by the lemma.

Apply the lemma to minimise \(B\) over \(P^*\), and compare the exact minimum with \(b\). The resulting bit complexity is
\[ 2^{d+\tau}L^{O(1)}. \]
Since
\[ d\leq |I||J|=m(\tau-m)\leq\frac{\tau^2}{4}, \]
this gives the claimed bounds
\[ 2^{m(\tau-m)+\tau}L^{O(1)} \quad\text{and}\quad 2^{O(\tau^2)}L^{O(1)}. \]

Hence the problem is FPT parameterised by the number of listed types, without assumptions on mass normalisation, equal side totals, or bounded mass magnitudes.

6. Membership in NP and conclusion

If an instance is a yes-instance, the minimum of \(B\) over \(P^*\) is at most \(b\). The quadratic-optimisation lemma guarantees a global minimiser with polynomial-size rational coordinates.

Use those coordinates as the certificate. A verifier computes \(S^*\) in polynomial time and checks, using exact rational arithmetic:

  • nonnegativity and all capacity constraints;
  • total assigned mass equal to \(S^*\);
  • \(B(x)\leq b\).

These checks take polynomial bit time, so the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with Section 3, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

The zero-threshold rounding argument establishes hardness for the continuous model itself. The active-constraint algorithm establishes the parameterised bound for arbitrary rational thresholds.
