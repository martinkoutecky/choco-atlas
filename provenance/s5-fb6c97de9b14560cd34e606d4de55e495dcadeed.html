The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two candidates \(a,b\), finitely many ordered stages, rational masses of cohorts \(t=(r,p,w,\sigma)\) with \(\sigma\in\{a,b\}\), a revealed history, and budget \(B\), where future ballot masses are adversarially revealed subject to known stage, price, and weight masses: does there exist a nonanticipating policy that, after each revelation, transfers mass between ballots, pays \(p\) per transferred unit while preserving its weight \(w\), and guarantees that \(a\) is a weighted-Plurality co-winner for every future revelation?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are two candidates \(a,b\) and explicitly listed ordered stages \(1,\ldots,R\). A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here the publicly known types are \((r,p,w)\), specifying stage, price per unit mass, and weight per unit mass; ballot labels \(\sigma\in\{a,b\}\) are revealed separately. Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\); prices and weights are binary nonnegative integers.
The input includes a current stage \(r_0\), the finalized ballot masses and total expenditure \(E\) at earlier stages, the rational ballot split of every type at \(r_0\), and a binary rational total budget \(B\geq E\geq0\).
At each later stage, an adaptive adversary reveals, for every type at that stage, any nonnegative real split of its known mass between \(a\) and \(b\); there are no additional constraints on future ballot totals. The whole stage is revealed before its bribery decision.
At the current and each subsequent stage, a policy may transfer any real amount \(x\) of currently revealed ballot mass to the other candidate, bounded by the available source mass. A transfer costs \(px\) and preserves \(r,p,w\); decisions are irrevocable, and mass can be changed only at its own stage. Total expenditure, including \(E\), must stay within \(B\) on every history.
A policy is nonanticipating if its decisions depend only on revelations and actions through the current stage. Use weighted Plurality, with mass \(x\) of weight \(w\) contributing \(wx\) to its chosen candidate; ties count as co-wins.

Is deciding whether such a policy guarantees that \(a\) is a final co-winner for every future revelation in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m=2\), \(\tau\), \(R\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

Discrete priced, weighted online Plurality bribery is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with two candidates, for both constructive and destructive goals ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.08308)). No result settling exactly the divisible-mass policy problem above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Future ballot labels cannot be part of publicly known support without removing the intended uncertainty. Public support therefore consists of stage–price–weight types, with ballot splits revealed separately.
  • Masses are normalized to sum to one; prices and weights retain the discrete definition's binary nonnegative integer domains, including zero. The total budget is a nonnegative binary rational.
  • The revealed history includes finalized earlier ballots and expenditure; the current stage's ballot splits are revealed but remain eligible for bribery. The budget includes past expenditure.
  • Each stage is revealed as a whole before its decision, and its ballots become irrevocable afterward. Transfers affect only mass belonging to the current stage and preserve its price and weight.
  • Adversarial revelations and transfer amounts range over real splits of the supplied rational masses. Only stage–price–weight totals constrain future revelations; the adversary may adapt to earlier actions.
  • The constructive goal is a final co-win for the single desired candidate, with ties accepted and no separate bound on the number of transfers.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\) for \(m=2\). A feasible policy can be constructed by solving one fractional knapsack problem with at most \(\tau\) variables.

The main point requiring proof is that a plan computed under the revelation “every future ballot supports \(b\)” extends to a nonanticipating policy against arbitrary, adaptive, real-valued revelations.

1. A finite optimization problem

Index the support types by \(i\), with stage \(r_i\), mass \(\mu_i\), price \(p_i\), and weight \(w_i\). Let \(I_0\) contain the current-stage types and \(I_+\) the future types.

For \(i\in I_0\), write the given split as \(\alpha_i\) mass supporting \(a\) and \(\beta_i\) mass supporting \(b\), so \(\alpha_i+\beta_i=\mu_i\).

Let \(D_-\) be the finalized weighted score of \(a\) minus that of \(b\) at earlier stages. Define the remaining budget \(K=B-E\) and the baseline final score difference
\[ H=D_-+\sum_{i\in I_0}w_i(\alpha_i-\beta_i) -\sum_{i\in I_+}w_i\mu_i. \]
Thus \(H\) is the final difference without further bribery when every future ballot supports \(b\).

Define available transfer capacities by \(c_i=\beta_i\) for \(i\in I_0\) and \(c_i=\mu_i\) for \(i\in I_+\).

Consider the following feasibility conditions:
\[ 0\leq x_i\leq c_i,\qquad \sum_{i\in I_0\cup I_+}p_ix_i\leq K,\qquad H+2\sum_{i\in I_0\cup I_+}w_ix_i\geq0. \tag{1} \]
The factor \(2\) appears because transferring mass \(x_i\) from \(b\) to \(a\) increases their score difference by \(2w_ix_i\).

Claim. A guaranteeing nonanticipating policy exists if and only if (1) is feasible.

2. Proof of necessity

Suppose a guaranteeing policy exists. Follow the legal revelation history in which every future type puts all its mass on \(b\). The policy must succeed on this history.

For each current or future type, let \(q_i\) be its mass supporting \(a\) after the policy acts. Set
\[ x_i=\max\{q_i-\alpha_i,0\}\quad(i\in I_0), \qquad x_i=q_i\quad(i\in I_+). \]

For a current type, \(q_i\leq\mu_i\) implies \(x_i\leq\mu_i-\alpha_i=\beta_i\). For a future type, \(0\leq q_i\leq\mu_i\). Hence all capacity constraints hold.

Increasing a type’s \(a\)-mass by a positive amount \(d\) requires transferring at least \(d\) mass from \(b\) to \(a\), costing at least \(p_id\). Consequently, the cost of the vector just defined is no greater than the policy’s expenditure on this history:
\[ \sum_i p_ix_i\leq K. \]
This remains true if a policy performs transfers in both directions: total transferred mass is at least the positive net increase in \(a\)-mass.

Under the vector \(x\), a current type’s final \(a\)-mass would be
\[ \alpha_i+x_i=\max\{\alpha_i,q_i\}\geq q_i, \]
and a future type’s final \(a\)-mass would be exactly \(q_i\). Because every \(w_i\) is nonnegative, this vector yields a final score difference at least as large as the successful policy’s difference. Therefore the last inequality of (1) holds.

3. Proof of sufficiency: an explicit policy

Suppose \(x\) satisfies (1).

At the current stage, transfer \(x_i\) mass from \(b\) to \(a\) for every \(i\in I_0\). This is feasible because \(x_i\leq\beta_i\).

At a future stage, suppose type \(i\) reveals \(u_i\) mass supporting \(a\), leaving \(\mu_i-u_i\) supporting \(b\). Transfer
\[ y_i=\min\{x_i,\mu_i-u_i\} \]
from \(b\) to \(a\).

This rule uses only the fixed vector \(x\) and the currently revealed split, so it is nonanticipating. Moreover, \(0\leq y_i\leq x_i\). Since prices are nonnegative, expenditure on every history, including every prefix, is at most
\[ E+\sum_i p_ix_i\leq B. \]

The resulting \(a\)-mass of a future type is
\[ u_i+y_i =u_i+\min\{x_i,\mu_i-u_i\} =\min\{u_i+x_i,\mu_i\} \geq x_i. \]
The last inequality uses \(u_i\geq0\) and \(x_i\leq\mu_i\). Its contribution to the score difference is therefore at least \(w_i(2x_i-\mu_i)\).

Summing these bounds and the exact current-stage contributions shows that the final score difference on every history is at least
\[ H+2\sum_i w_ix_i\geq0. \]
Thus \(a\) is always a co-winner. The argument is pathwise and applies equally to adaptive adversaries and irrational revelations.

This proves the claim.

4. Exact greedy solution

Set \(T=\max\{0,-H/2\}\). Conditions (1) ask whether weighted transfer gain at least \(T\) can be purchased within budget \(K\).

If \(T=0\), accept without further transfers. Otherwise, discard types with \(w_i=0\) or \(c_i=0\); these cannot help achieve positive gain.

For each remaining type define
\[ d_i=w_ic_i,\qquad \rho_i=\frac{p_i}{w_i}. \]
Here \(d_i\) is its available gain and \(\rho_i\) its cost per unit gain. Sort the types so that \(\rho_1\leq\cdots\leq\rho_n\).

If \(T>\sum_i d_i\), reject. Otherwise, let \(k\) be the first index satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^k d_i\geq T\). Choose gains
\[ z_i^*=d_i\ (i<k),\qquad z_k^*=T-\sum_{i<k}d_i,\qquad z_i^*=0\ (i>k), \]
and transfers \(x_i^*=z_i^*/w_i\). Accept exactly when their cost \(C^*=\sum_i\rho_i z_i^*\) satisfies \(C^*\leq K\).

Proof of optimality. Any feasible gain vector with total gain exceeding \(T\) can be scaled down to total gain exactly \(T\), preserving capacities and weakly decreasing cost because all prices are nonnegative.

Now consider any vector \(z\) with \(0\leq z_i\leq d_i\) and \(\sum_i z_i=T\). Since \(\sum_i z_i^*=T\),
\[ \sum_i\rho_i(z_i-z_i^*) =\sum_i(\rho_i-\rho_k)(z_i-z_i^*)\geq0. \]
Indeed, for \(i<k\), both factors are nonpositive; for \(i>k\), both are nonnegative; and the \(i=k\) term is zero. Hence \(C^*\) is the minimum cost of meeting the target.

This proof includes zero prices and equal ratios. The construction has at most one partially used type.

5. Running time and encoding

For \(m=2\), the algorithm uses
\[ O\!\left(R+\tau\log(\tau+1)+1\right) \]
exact arithmetic operations and comparisons, besides reading the input. Ratios are compared by integer cross multiplication; no numerical approximation is required.

To bound bit complexity, let \(Q\) be twice the product of all input rational denominators. Its bit length is \(O(L)\), and it is a common denominator for the capacities, baseline, budget, and target.

Using this common denominator, weighted sums and full-type costs have \(O(L)\)-bit integer representations. The sole potentially partial transfer introduces only the additional denominator \(w_k\), which also has \(O(L)\) bits. Thus all numbers needed by the algorithm have \(O(L)\) bits.

With ordinary quadratic-time integer arithmetic, a valid bound is
\[ O\!\left( L+\bigl(R+\tau\log(\tau+1)+1\bigr)L^2 \right) \]
bit operations. Because the stages and support are explicitly listed, \(R,\tau=O(L)\), giving the coarser bound \(O(L^3\log(L+1))\).

The policy has a finite representation consisting of at most \(\tau\) rational thresholds \(x_i\) and the clipping rule from Section 3. Arbitrary real revelations create no additional decision variables in the algorithm.

The discrete hardness result in [Theorem 5.4 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.08308) concerns indivisible voters. Here divisibility permits the fractional greedy solution, while the policy construction above rigorously handles the online uncertainty.
