The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), target \(c^*\), rational \(w>1\), and fixed sincere rankings \(\rho_1,\rho_2\), consider sincere cohort masses \(w/(w+3)\) and \(1/(w+3)\), plus a coordinated manipulator cohort of mass \(2/(w+3)\). Does there exist a rational allocation \(x_\pi\ge0\) over rankings \(\pi\in\Pi(C)\), with \(\sum_\pi x_\pi=2/(w+3)\), such that \(c^*\) has at least as many total Borda points as every candidate after the manipulator mass reports according to that allocation?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), \(c^*\in C\), and \(\Pi(C)\) the strict total rankings of \(C\). Input consists of a binary rational \(w>1\) and two sincere rankings \(\rho_1,\rho_2\), fixed during manipulation.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here the types are sincere ranking types and a freely reporting manipulator type; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Equal sincere rankings are merged.
The sincere masses are \(w/(w+3)\) and \(1/(w+3)\); the coordinated manipulator mass is \(2/(w+3)\), so total mass is \(1\). Manipulators may divide their mass arbitrarily among all rankings.
Write \(b_\pi(c)\) for the standard Borda score of \(c\) in \(\pi\). A feasible allocation has \(x_\pi\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) and \(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(C)}x_\pi=2/(w+3)\), giving total score \(S(c)=\frac{w}{w+3}b_{\rho_1}(c)+\frac{1}{w+3}b_{\rho_2}(c)+\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(C)}x_\pi b_\pi(c)\). Ties are broken uniformly among highest-scoring candidates; success means \(S(c^*)\ge S(c)\) for every \(c\in C\).

Is deciding whether a successful rational allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total binary input length \(L\), including the encoding of \(w\).

Discretely, with two indivisible unit-weight manipulators and two sincere voters of weights \(w\) and \(1\), manipulation is NP-hard for every fixed rational \(1<w<3\); hardness also holds for fixed rational \(w\ge3\). [Shen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03372)
Continuously, priced bribery for scoring protocols is polynomial-time solvable, a related attack model; no result explicitly treating the restricted coalitional problem above was located. [Koutecký et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444v1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified rankings as strict total orders and required a nonempty candidate set.
  • Specified uniform tie-breaking and interpreted success as positive winning probability, equivalently co-winning.
  • Defined input types by sincere ranking or manipulator eligibility, merging identical sincere rankings; the manipulator type needs no sincere ranking because its reports are unrestricted.
  • Treated w and the sincere rankings as input, with w encoded in binary and included in L; fixed sincere rankings means they cannot change during manipulation.
  • Made explicit that total society mass is one and scores are weighted by mass without applying w a second time.

Result

The problem is in \(\mathrm P\), including when \(w\) is part of the input and encoded in binary.

More precisely, it admits an exact algorithm using \(O(\tau m+m\log(m+1))\) integer operations on integers of bit length \(O(L+\log(m+1))\). Using ordinary quadratic-time integer arithmetic, its bit running time is

\(O\!\left(L+(\tau m+m\log(m+1))(L+\log(m+1))^2\right)\).

Here \(\tau\in\{2,3\}\), according to whether the two sincere rankings coincide. No enumeration of all rankings is required.

1. Normalize the scores and put the target first

If \(m=1\), success is automatic. Henceforth let \(n=m-1\ge1\), and write \(D=C\setminus\{c^*\}\).

Multiply every score by \(w+3\), which preserves all score comparisons. Define the sincere score

\(a(c)=w\,b_{\rho_1}(c)+b_{\rho_2}(c)\).

The normalized manipulator masses \(z_\pi=(w+3)x_\pi\) sum to \(2\).

We may require every manipulator ranking to put \(c^*\) first. Indeed, moving \(c^*\) to the top increases its Borda score, decreases by one the score of each candidate previously above it, and leaves every other candidate’s score unchanged. Applying this operation to every reported ranking therefore preserves success. Rational masses remain rational when identical resulting rankings are merged.

Consequently, the target’s normalized total score is

\(T=a(c^*)+2n\).

For each rival \(c\in D\), define its allowable manipulator score

\(u_c=T-a(c)=a(c^*)+2n-a(c)\).

A successful allocation exists precisely when the rivals’ manipulator score vector \(y\) can satisfy \(y_c\le u_c\) for every \(c\in D\).

In a ranking with \(c^*\) first, the rivals receive a permutation of \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\). Since the total normalized manipulator mass is \(2\), the attainable real score vectors are exactly

\(P_n=\operatorname{conv}\{\text{permutations of }(0,2,\ldots,2(n-1))\}\).

We first characterize real feasibility, then prove that it implies rational feasibility.

2. Exact feasibility criterion

Let \(u_{(1)}\le\cdots\le u_{(n)}\) be the allowable scores sorted increasingly.

Proposition. There exists \(y\in P_n\) with \(y_c\le u_c\) for every rival if and only if

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^k u_{(i)}\ge k(k-1)\qquad\text{for every }k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.\)

Necessity

For any subset \(A\subseteq D\) of size \(k\), every permutation of \(0,2,\ldots,2(n-1)\) assigns to \(A\) a sum at least

\(\displaystyle 0+2+\cdots+2(k-1)=k(k-1).\)

Convex combinations preserve this inequality. Thus, if \(y\in P_n\) and \(y\le u\) coordinatewise, then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{c\in A}u_c\ge\sum_{c\in A}y_c\ge k(k-1).\)

Taking \(A\) to be the \(k\) smallest coordinates of \(u\) proves necessity.

Sufficiency

Assume all the stated prefix inequalities hold. Because the sum of the \(k\) smallest coordinates is the minimum sum over all \(k\)-element subsets, we have

\(\displaystyle \sum_{c\in A}u_c\ge |A|(|A|-1)\qquad\text{for every }A\subseteq D.\)

Define the upward closure

\(K=P_n+\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\,n}\).

It is convex and closed. For closedness, if \(p_j+d_j\) converges, where \(p_j\in P_n\) and \(d_j\ge0\), compactness of \(P_n\) gives a subsequence with \(p_j\to p\in P_n\). Along that subsequence, \(d_j\) converges to a nonnegative vector, so the limit belongs to \(K\).

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \(u\notin K\). Choose a point \(v\in K\) minimizing its Euclidean distance to \(u\), and put \(t=v-u\). Such a minimizer exists because \(K\) is nonempty and closed, and the distance tends to infinity outside bounded sets.

For every \(z\in K\), convexity and minimality give

\(t\cdot(z-v)\ge0.\)

Indeed, the derivative at zero of the squared distance along \(v+\varepsilon(z-v)\), for \(\varepsilon\ge0\), must be nonnegative.

Taking \(z=v+e_c\) shows \(t_c\ge0\) for every coordinate. Moreover,

\(t\cdot u=t\cdot v-\|t\|^2<t\cdot v=\min_{z\in K}t\cdot z=\min_{y\in P_n}t\cdot y.\)

The last equality holds because \(t\ge0\): adding a nonnegative vector cannot decrease its scalar product with \(t\).

Order the candidates as \(c_1,\ldots,c_n\) so that

\(t_{c_1}\ge\cdots\ge t_{c_n}\ge0\),

and set \(t_{c_{n+1}}=0\). Telescoping gives

\(\displaystyle t\cdot u =\sum_{k=1}^n(t_{c_k}-t_{c_{k+1}}) \sum_{j=1}^k u_{c_j} \ge \sum_{k=1}^n(t_{c_k}-t_{c_{k+1}})k(k-1) =\sum_{j=1}^n2(j-1)t_{c_j}. \)

On the other hand,

\(\displaystyle \min_{y\in P_n}t\cdot y=\sum_{j=1}^n2(j-1)t_{c_j}.\)

To justify this minimum, a linear functional attains its minimum over a convex hull at one of its generating vectors. Among permutations, assigning smaller scores to larger coefficients minimizes the scalar product: swapping scores \(s_i>s_j\) assigned to coefficients \(t_i\ge t_j\) decreases it by \((t_i-t_j)(s_i-s_j)\ge0\).

We have therefore proved \(t\cdot u\ge\min_{y\in P_n}t\cdot y\), contradicting the strict inequality above. Hence \(u\in K\), which is equivalent to the required existence of \(y\le u\). This proves the proposition.

3. Real feasibility implies rational feasibility

The distinction matters because the question requires rational allocation masses.

Index the finitely many permutations of \(0,2,\ldots,2(n-1)\) by \(\sigma\), and denote their vectors by \(v^\sigma\). A feasible real convex combination belongs to the polytope

\(\displaystyle Q=\left\{\lambda: \lambda_\sigma\ge0,\quad \sum_\sigma\lambda_\sigma=1,\quad \sum_\sigma\lambda_\sigma v^\sigma_c\le u_c \text{ for every }c\in D \right\}. \)

All coefficients and right-hand sides are rational. If nonempty, \(Q\) is compact and has an extreme point. For example, successively minimizing each coordinate over the previous minimizer set produces a singleton; that point is extreme.

At an extreme point, the active constraint equations have full rank. Otherwise a nonzero vector annihilating all active equations would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, preserving the active equations and all inactive inequalities, contradicting extremality. Selecting a full-rank square subsystem and applying Cramer’s rule shows that every coordinate of this extreme point is rational.

Thus a feasible real combination yields rational coefficients \(\lambda_\sigma\). For the corresponding rankings with \(c^*\) first, set

\(\displaystyle x_\sigma=\frac{2\lambda_\sigma}{w+3}.\)

These masses are rational, nonnegative, sum to \(2/(w+3)\), and satisfy all winning inequalities.

In fact, an extreme point has at most \(n+1=m\) positive coefficients. If it had more, the vectors \((1,v^\sigma)\) on its positive support would be linearly dependent. A sufficiently small perturbation along that dependence would preserve the total mass and every score while maintaining nonnegativity, again contradicting extremality.

The polytope \(Q\) is used only in this existence proof; the decision algorithm does not construct its factorially many variables.

4. Algorithm and binary running time

Write the input weight as \(w=p/q\), where \(p,q\) are positive binary integers and \(p>q\). Reduction to lowest terms is unnecessary.

For every rival \(c\), compute the integer

\(\displaystyle H_c =p\bigl(b_{\rho_1}(c^*)-b_{\rho_1}(c)\bigr) +q\bigl(b_{\rho_2}(c^*)-b_{\rho_2}(c)\bigr) +2nq. \)

Then \(u_c=H_c/q\). Sort these integers increasingly as \(H_{(1)},\ldots,H_{(n)}\). Accept exactly when

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^k H_{(i)}\ge q\,k(k-1) \qquad\text{for every }k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}. \)

These comparisons are exact, including equality, as required by the specified treatment of ties.

Reading the supported sincere rankings and computing Borda scores takes \(O(\tau m)\) operations. Sorting takes \(O(m\log(m+1))\) comparisons, and all prefix tests take \(O(m)\) additional operations.

Every integer used has bit length \(O(L+\log(m+1))\): Borda scores have magnitude at most \(m-1\), and prefix sums introduce at most another factor of \(m\). The bit running time stated at the beginning follows. In particular, it depends polynomially on the binary encoding length of \(w\), rather than on its numerical value.

5. Why two indivisible ballots differ

The discrete hardness studied by [Shen, Tang, and Deng](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03372) concerns a restriction to two indivisible unit-weight manipulator ballots. That restriction can change feasibility, as the following explicit example shows.

Take \(w=5/3\), candidates \(c^*,a,b,d\), and sincere rankings

\(\rho_1:\ d\succ b\succ a\succ c^*,\qquad \rho_2:\ a\succ b\succ c^*\succ d.\)

The normalized sincere scores are

\(\displaystyle a(c^*)=1,\quad a(a)=14/3,\quad a(b)=16/3,\quad a(d)=5. \)

Putting the target first gives \(T=7\), so the rivals’ allowable scores are

\(\displaystyle u_a=7/3,\qquad u_b=5/3,\qquad u_d=2.\)

A fractional manipulation uses normalized masses \(7/6\) and \(5/6\) on

\(c^*\succ a\succ d\succ b,\qquad c^*\succ b\succ d\succ a,\)

respectively. The rivals receive \(7/3,5/3,2\), and all four candidates finish with normalized score \(7\). The original allocation masses are \(1/4\) and \(5/28\).

With two indivisible unit ballots, we may again put \(c^*\) first. The rivals’ combined manipulator scores are integers summing to \(6\). Their upper bounds force these integers to be at most \(2,1,2\), respectively, whose sum is only \(5\). Thus discrete manipulation fails.

The continuous problem is therefore completely resolved by the prefix criterion above.
