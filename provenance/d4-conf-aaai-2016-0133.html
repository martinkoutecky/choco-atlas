The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a partial tournament \(P\) on candidates \(C\) and a target \(c^\star\), does there exist a rational distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(C\) such that every specified pairwise direction in \(P\) agrees with its margin under \(\mu\), every unspecified pair has nonzero margin, and \(c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T_\mu)\), where \(T_\mu\) orients each pair by the sign of its margin?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(c^\star\in C\). The input is a partial tournament \(P\): each unordered pair of distinct candidates has either one specified direction or no specified direction; missing directions are unrestricted, not ties.
A society is represented by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings of \(C\) with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings.
For a society \(\mu\), define \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{r:a\succ_r b}\mu(r)-\sum_{r:b\succ_r a}\mu(r)\). Require \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)>0\) for every specified edge \(a\to b\) and nonzero margins for all unspecified pairs. Define \(T_\mu\) by \(a\to b\) exactly when \(\Delta_\mu(a,b)>0\).
For a tournament \(T\), its tournament game is the symmetric zero-sum game with both action sets \(C\), row payoff \(1\) when the row candidate beats the column candidate, \(-1\) when it loses, and \(0\) on the diagonal. The bipartisan set \(\mathrm{BP}(T)\) comprises candidates played with positive probability in some Nash equilibrium.
The distribution is existentially chosen; neither its support nor its masses are supplied or restricted by the input. Let \(L\) denote the input bit length. State decision running times in \(m,L\), and any bounds or running times involving explicitly represented societies in \(m,\tau,L_\mu\), where \(L_\mu\) is their encoding length.

Is deciding whether such a rational society \(\mu\) exists with \(c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T_\mu)\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete partial tournaments, deciding whether some completion has the target in its bipartisan set is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete ([Brill, Freeman, and Conitzer](https://arxiv.org/html/2104.14317v1)).
No result explicitly treating the rational-distribution formulation above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the candidate domain explicit: a nonempty finite set with the target among its members; singleton tournaments are allowed.
  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and represented only distinct rankings with positive binary-rational masses.
  • Interpreted agreement with a specified direction as a strictly positive margin in that direction; missing directions permit either orientation but no zero margin.
  • Kept the support and masses existentially unrestricted, since the stated input supplies only a partial tournament and a target.
  • Distinguished the input encoding length from the encoding length and support size of an existential society, so witness dimensions do not become extra input parameters.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Consequently, it is polynomial-time solvable if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The key fact is that every tournament admits a rational society with polynomial support size and encoding length. Thus the existential society formulation has exactly the same yes-instances as the discrete completion problem.

For \(\,m=1\,\), the answer is trivially yes. The construction below handles every \(\,m\geq 2\,\).

1. Realizing every tournament by a small rational society

Fix a tournament \(\,T\,\) on \(\,C\,\), and write \(\,q=\binom{m}{2}\,\). Identify candidates with indices so that each ranking has encoding length \(\,O(m\log(m+1))\,\).

For each edge \(\,a\to b\,\) of \(\,T\,\), list the remaining candidates as \(\,x_1,\ldots,x_{m-2}\,\), and introduce the two rankings

\[ a\succ b\succ x_1\succ\cdots\succ x_{m-2}, \qquad x_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ x_1\succ a\succ b. \]

Here and below, these rankings are initially a multiset; repeated rankings will be merged.

Consider their combined, unnormalized pairwise margins.

  • Both rankings place \(\,a\,\) above \(\,b\,\), contributing \(\,2\,\) to that margin.
  • For a pair of remaining candidates, their orders in the two rankings are opposite.
  • For a pair containing exactly one of \(\,a,b\,\), that candidate is above the remaining candidate in the first ranking and below it in the second.

Therefore this pair of rankings contributes \(\,2\,\) precisely on the chosen edge and \(\,0\,\) on every other unordered pair.

Applying this construction to all \(\,q\,\) edges gives \(\,2q\,\) rankings. Their unnormalized margin is \(\,2\,\) on every edge of \(\,T\,\).

To obtain dyadic masses, let \(\,N\,\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(\,N\geq 2q\,\). Both \(\,N\,\) and \(\,2q\,\) are even. Add \(\,(N-2q)/2\,\) copies of an arbitrary ranking together with the same number of copies of its reverse. Every added ranking–reverse pair contributes zero to every margin.

Give each of the resulting \(\,N\,\) ranking occurrences mass \(\,1/N\,\). Merge identical rankings: if ranking \(\,r\,\) occurs \(\,f_r\,\) times, give it mass \(\,\mu(r)=f_r/N\,\). These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to \(\,1\,\). Merging preserves all margins, so

\[ \Delta_\mu(a,b)=\frac{2}{N}>0 \quad\text{for every edge }a\to b\text{ of }T. \]

Hence \(\,T_\mu=T\,\), and no pair has zero margin.

The final support consists of at most the original \(\,2q\,\) rankings and two padding rankings. Thus

\[ \tau\leq 2q+2=m(m-1)+2. \]

Because \(\,2q\leq N<4q\,\), each mass needs \(\,O(\log(m+1))\,\) bits. Under the usual explicit encoding,

\[ L_\mu=O\!\left(\tau m\log(m+1)\right) =O\!\left(m^3\log(m+1)\right). \]

The construction takes polynomial time in \(\,m\,\). For example, generating the rankings, sorting their binary encodings, and merging repetitions takes \(\,O(m^3\log^2(m+1))\,\) bit operations.

This proves realizability even under the stronger interpretation that “binary-rational” requires dyadic masses.

2. Exact equivalence with tournament completion

Let \(\,\mathcal C(P)\,\) denote the tournaments extending every specified edge of \(\,P\,\). We prove

\[ \exists\mu\text{ satisfying the input constraints with } c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T_\mu) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists T\in\mathcal C(P)\text{ with } c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T). \]

For the forward direction, antisymmetry gives \(\,\Delta_\mu(b,a)=-\Delta_\mu(a,b)\,\). Every pair has nonzero margin, so exactly one direction is selected. Every specified edge has positive margin in its specified direction. Consequently, \(\,T_\mu\in\mathcal C(P)\,\), and the assumed bipartisan membership supplies the required completion.

For the reverse direction, take a completion \(\,T\in\mathcal C(P)\,\) with \(\,c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T)\,\). Section 1 constructs a society with \(\,T_\mu=T\,\). Its margins satisfy every specified edge and are nonzero on every unspecified pair. It is therefore an admissible witness.

The equivalence preserves the entire input \(\,(P,c^\star)\,\).

3. Polynomial verification of a fixed completion

For completeness, bipartisan membership can be checked exactly by linear programming.

Let \(\,A\,\) be the tournament payoff matrix. It satisfies \(\,A^{\mathsf T}=-A\,\). Define

\[ K=\left\{p\in\mathbb R^m: p\geq 0,\quad \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}p=1,\quad Ap\leq 0\right\}. \]

The finite zero-sum minimax theorem applies because both action sets are finite and nonempty. The game has value zero: for every probability vector \(\,p\,\), skew-symmetry gives \(\,p^{\mathsf T}Ap=0\,\), so

\[ \max_p\min_q p^{\mathsf T}Aq\leq 0, \qquad \min_q\max_p p^{\mathsf T}Aq\geq 0. \]

Minimax equates these quantities. In particular, an optimal column strategy exists and satisfies \(\,Ap\leq 0\,\), establishing that \(\,K\,\) is nonempty.

If \(\,p,q\in K\,\), then \(\,p^{\mathsf T}A=-(Ap)^{\mathsf T}\geq 0\,\), while \(\,Aq\leq 0\,\). Therefore

\[ 0\leq p^{\mathsf T}Aq\leq 0. \]

Every row deviation against \(\,q\,\) yields payoff at most zero, and every column deviation against \(\,p\,\) yields row payoff at least zero. Thus \(\,(p,q)\,\) is a Nash equilibrium.

Conversely, suppose \(\,(p,q)\,\) is a Nash equilibrium with row payoff \(\,u\,\). The row player can deviate to \(\,q\,\), obtaining \(\,q^{\mathsf T}Aq=0\,\), so \(\,u\geq 0\,\). The column player can deviate to \(\,p\,\), giving row payoff \(\,p^{\mathsf T}Ap=0\,\), so \(\,u\leq 0\,\). Hence \(\,u=0\,\). The pure-deviation inequalities imply \(\,Aq\leq 0\,\) and \(\,p^{\mathsf T}A\geq 0\,\), the latter being equivalent to \(\,Ap\leq 0\,\). Thus both strategies lie in \(\,K\,\).

It follows that

\[ c^\star\in\mathrm{BP}(T) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \max_{p\in K}p_{c^\star}>0. \]

This is a bounded rational linear program with \(\,m\,\) variables, \(\,2m\,\) inequalities, one equality, and coefficients in \(\,\{-1,0,1\}\,\). Polynomial-time exact rational linear programming therefore decides the strict positivity of its optimum in \(\,\operatorname{poly}(m)\,\) bit operations; no numerical tolerance is needed.

4. Complexity conclusion and running times

Theorem 8 of [Brill, Freeman, and Conitzer](https://arxiv.org/html/2104.14317v1) states that possible bipartisan membership for an incomplete tournament is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Their unspecified pairs permit either direction and exclude ties, matching the present input model.

By Section 2, the identity map \(\,(P,c^\star)\mapsto(P,c^\star)\,\) is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the rational-society problem. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

For membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), guess the directions of all unspecified pairs, check that the resulting tournament extends \(\,P\,\), and use Section 3 to check bipartisan membership. Verification takes \(\,\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\,\) time. Section 2 proves that this certificate is equivalent to an admissible rational society. Hence the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

If \(\,k\,\) pairs are unspecified, enumerating their orientations gives a deterministic decision bound of

\[ 2^k\operatorname{poly}(m,L), \qquad k\leq\binom{m}{2}. \]

For an explicitly represented society, computing all margins requires \(\,O(\tau m^2)\,\) exact rational additions after forming the rankings’ position tables. A common denominator can be chosen as the product of the encoded denominators; its bit length is \(\,O(L_\mu)\,\). Using elementary integer arithmetic gives the conservative bit-time bound

\[ O\!\left(\tau m^2(L_\mu+1)^2\right)+\operatorname{poly}(m) \]

for computing the induced tournament and checking target membership, provided all margins are nonzero. Checking compatibility with an additional partial tournament costs \(\,O(L+m^2)\,\).

Thus evaluation of a supplied society is polynomial in its explicit size, while existential choice of the society is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.
