The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \( \mu_t \) with \( \sum_t \mu_t=1 \), rational per-capita supplies \( 0\le \sigma_j\le1 \), and additive \( k \)-ary values \( v_{tj} \) for types \( t\in T=[\tau] \) and goods \( j\in[m] \), find masses \( z_{t,B}\ge0 \) for integral bundles \( B\subseteq[m] \) satisfying \( \sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t \) and \( \sum_{t,B:j\in B}z_{t,B}=\sigma_j \), such that every pair of positive-mass cohorts satisfies \( \mathrm{EF1} \) under \( v_t(B)=\sum_{j\in B}v_{tj} \), and no capacity-feasible fractional reallocation weakly improves every supported cohort while strictly improving one. Determine whether such an allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \( m,\tau,k \) and the input bit length, independently of the common population denominator.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) goods, binary-rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\in[0,1]\), and nonnegative binary-rational additive values \(v_{tj}\). Every good has positive value for some supported type. Each type has at most \(k\) distinct values across goods; these values may differ between types, and \(k\) is an input parameter. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of rational masses \(z_{t,B}\ge0\), for integral bundles \(B\subseteq[m]\), satisfying \(\sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:j\in B}z_{t,B}=\sigma_j\). Represent it by listing its positive entries; empty bundles are permitted.
A supported cohort is a pair \((t,B)\) with \(z_{t,B}>0\). Require EF1 for every ordered pair of supported cohorts, comparing their bundles using the first cohort's valuation; removal of at most one good is allowed.
A fractional reallocation assigns each supported cohort per-capita quantities \(x_{t,B,j}\in[0,1]\), subject to \(\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}x_{t,B,j}\le\sigma_j\) for every good. Require that no such reallocation satisfies \(\sum_jv_{tj}x_{t,B,j}\ge v_t(B)\) for every supported cohort, with strict inequality for at least one.

Is computing such an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal allocation, or reporting that none exists, solvable in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L)\), independently of the common population denominator, or is this problem NP-hard?

For discrete agents and indivisible goods, EF1 and fractional Pareto optimality can be achieved in strongly polynomial time for every fixed \(k\); this does not give a polynomial bound uniform in \(k\). The general discrete search problem belongs to \(\mathrm{PLS}\). [Garg–Murhekar](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.14229v2)
No result settling the exact support-and-supply problem above was located.

Status and scope

The general complexity question remains unresolved here. I prove two additional results:

  • A denominator-independent polynomial-time algorithm exists when every type has at most one distinct positive value: its values lie in $\{0,a_t\}$ for some $a_t>0$.
  • Cohort-level fractional Pareto optimality has an exact characterization by multiplicative inequalities on a directed graph, giving a polynomial-time test using rational arithmetic.

I also reprove the conditional compact-support result needed for the general problem. The verifier’s correction is essential: compact support follows if an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal allocation exists. It does not establish unconditional existence.

The first result covers arbitrary personalized binary valuations and the entire $k=1$ case. It does not cover types having two distinct positive values.

1. A certificate and graph characterization of fractional Pareto optimality

Fix a feasible allocation. Index its supported cohorts by $C$. For $c\in C$, write $t(c)$ for its type, $B_c$ for its bundle, $z_c>0$ for its mass, and
$$ b_{cj}=\mathbf 1_{\{j\in B_c\}}, \qquad v_{cj}=v_{t(c)j}. $$

1.1. The price certificate

Lemma 1. The allocation is fractionally Pareto optimal if and only if there are $\alpha_c>0$ and $p_j\geq0$ satisfying
$$ j\in B_c\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_cv_{cj}\geq p_j, \qquad j\notin B_c\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_cv_{cj}\leq p_j. $$

Proof of sufficiency. For any feasible fractional reallocation $x$, an owned good satisfies $x_{cj}-b_{cj}\leq0$, while an unowned good satisfies $x_{cj}-b_{cj}\geq0$. In either case the certificate implies
$$ \alpha_cv_{cj}(x_{cj}-b_{cj}) \leq p_j(x_{cj}-b_{cj}). $$
Multiplying by $z_c$ and summing gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_c z_c\alpha_c \bigl(v_{t(c)}(x_c)-v_{t(c)}(B_c)\bigr) &\leq \sum_j p_j \left(\sum_c z_cx_{cj}-\sigma_j\right)\\ &\leq0. \end{aligned} $$
A Pareto improvement would make the left side strictly positive, because every $z_c\alpha_c$ is positive. Thus no such improvement exists.

Proof of necessity. Put $U_c=v_{t(c)}(B_c)$ and use total quantities $y_{cj}=z_cx_{cj}$. Consider the linear program
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize}\quad& \sum_{c,j}v_{cj}y_{cj}\\ \text{subject to}\quad& 0\leq y_{cj}\leq z_c,\\ &\sum_c y_{cj}\leq\sigma_j,\\ &\sum_jv_{cj}y_{cj}\geq z_cU_c. \end{aligned} $$
The original assignment $y^0_{cj}=z_cb_{cj}$ is feasible, and the feasible region is bounded. Fractional Pareto optimality is equivalent to $y^0$ being optimal: every feasible point weakly improves all cohort utilities, and the objective increases precisely when some cohort improves strictly.

Assign nonnegative dual variables $p_j$ to the supply constraints, $\lambda_c$ to the utility constraints written with negative signs, and $q_{cj}$ to the upper bounds. The dual inequalities are
$$ p_j+q_{cj}\geq(1+\lambda_c)v_{cj}. $$
The primal is feasible with finite optimum, so linear-programming duality supplies an optimal dual solution. Set $\alpha_c=1+\lambda_c$.

Complementary slackness with $y^0$ gives the required inequalities. If $j\in B_c$, then $y^0_{cj}=z_c>0$, so
$$ p_j+q_{cj}=\alpha_cv_{cj}, $$
and therefore $p_j\leq\alpha_cv_{cj}$. If $j\notin B_c$, the upper bound has positive slack, so $q_{cj}=0$, and consequently $p_j\geq\alpha_cv_{cj}$. This proves necessity. $\square$

In particular, the certificate depends on the supported types and bundles, rather than their masses. Restricting it to a subset of cohorts preserves fractional Pareto optimality whenever the new masses remain feasible.

1.2. Eliminating the prices

For each good $j$, let
$$ O_j=\{c:j\in B_c\}, \qquad N_j=\{c:j\notin B_c\}. $$

Construct a directed multigraph on $C$. For every good $j$ and every pair $c\in N_j$, $d\in O_j$ with $v_{cj},v_{dj}>0$, introduce an edge
$$ c\longrightarrow d \quad\text{of weight}\quad w_{cdj}=\frac{v_{cj}}{v_{dj}}. $$

Theorem 2. The allocation is fractionally Pareto optimal if and only if both conditions hold:

  • There is no good $j$ having an owner $d$ with $v_{dj}=0$ and a nonowner $c$ with $v_{cj}>0$.
  • Every directed cycle has edge-weight product at most $1$.

Proof. A certificate from Lemma 1 immediately excludes the first obstruction: it would require
$$ 0<\alpha_cv_{cj}\leq p_j\leq\alpha_dv_{dj}=0. $$
It also implies, for every graph edge,
$$ \alpha_d\geq w_{cdj}\alpha_c. $$
Multiplying these inequalities around a cycle shows that its weight product cannot exceed $1$.

Conversely, suppose both conditions hold. For each vertex $d$, define $\alpha_d$ as the maximum product of edge weights along a simple directed path ending at $d$, allowing the length-zero path of product $1$.

These maxima exist because there are finitely many simple paths. Moreover, deleting a directed cycle from a walk cannot decrease its weight product. Hence any walk can be shortened to a simple path without decreasing its product. Appending an edge $c\longrightarrow d$ to a maximizing path for $c$ therefore proves
$$ \alpha_d\geq w_{cdj}\alpha_c. $$

Define
$$ p_j=\max_{c\in N_j}\alpha_cv_{cj}, $$
with the maximum equal to $0$ when $N_j$ is empty. The nonowner inequalities in Lemma 1 hold by definition.

For a positive-valued owner $d$, every positive-valued nonowner $c$ supplies a graph edge, so
$$ \alpha_cv_{cj}\leq\alpha_dv_{dj}. $$
Zero-valued nonowners contribute $0$ to the maximum. Thus $p_j\leq\alpha_dv_{dj}$. For a zero-valued owner, the first condition says that every nonowner has value zero, so $p_j=0$. All certificate inequalities hold, and Lemma 1 applies. $\square$

1.3. Exact polynomial-time verification

Let $n=|C|$. Initialize $a_c^{(0)}=1$ and perform $n-1$ synchronous rounds
$$ a_d^{(h)} = \max\left\{ a_d^{(h-1)}, \max_{c\longrightarrow d}w_{cdj}a_c^{(h-1)} \right\}. $$
The resulting value is the maximum product over paths of length at most $n-1$ ending at $d$.

After separately checking the zero-value obstruction, accept exactly when
$$ a_d^{(n-1)}\geq w_{cdj}a_c^{(n-1)} $$
for every edge.

If every cycle has product at most $1$, the simple-path argument proves these inequalities. Conversely, if they all hold, multiplying them around any cycle excludes a product greater than $1$.

There are at most $mn^2$ edges. Every intermediate number is a product of at most $n-1$ input valuation ratios, so it has polynomial bit length. Thus this is a polynomial-time test using exact rational arithmetic, without logarithmic approximations.

2. A polynomial algorithm for personalized binary valuations

Assume that, for each supported type $t$, there is a positive rational $a_t$ such that
$$ v_{tj}=a_t b_{tj}, \qquad b_{tj}\in\{0,1\}. $$
For an identically zero-valued type, take $a_t=1$ and $b_{tj}=0$.

Say that type $t$ *likes* good $j$ when $b_{tj}=1$, and define the total liking mass
$$ M_j=\sum_t\mu_tb_{tj}. $$
The premise that every good has positive value somewhere gives $M_j>0$.

Theorem 3. Under this hypothesis, an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal allocation can always be computed in time $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$, independently of the common population denominator.

2.1. Separate scarce and surplus goods

Partition the goods into
$$ S=\{j:\sigma_j<M_j\}, \qquad H=\{j:\sigma_j\geq M_j\}. $$

We first allocate each good in $S$ entirely to people who like it. Subsequently, for every $j\in H$, we give the good to every person who likes it and assign the remaining mass $\sigma_j-M_j$ to people who dislike it.

The scarce-good allocation will minimize
$$ \Phi=\sum_c z_c|B_c|^2, $$
where, at this stage, $B_c\subseteq S$ and every cohort likes every good it receives.

2.2. A polynomial-size linear program

Write $s=|S|$. If $s=0$, start each type with an empty bundle and proceed to Section 2.5.

For $s>0$, introduce aggregate quantities $y_{tj}$ for $j\in S$, and slot quantities $r_{t\ell}$ for $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,s\}$. Solve
$$ \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad& \sum_t\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(2\ell-1)r_{t\ell}\\ \text{subject to}\quad& \sum_t y_{tj}=\sigma_j &&\text{for every }j\in S,\\ &\sum_{j\in S}y_{tj}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}r_{t\ell} &&\text{for every }t,\\ &0\leq y_{tj}\leq\mu_t &&\text{if }b_{tj}=1,\\ &y_{tj}=0 &&\text{if }b_{tj}=0,\\ &0\leq r_{t\ell}\leq\mu_t. \end{aligned} $$

This linear program is feasible. For each scarce good, the eligible capacities sum to $M_j>\sigma_j$, so its supply can be assigned independently among liking types. Each type’s resulting total quantity is at most $s\mu_t$, and therefore fits its slots.

Let
$$ d_t=\sum_{j\in S}y_{tj}, \qquad u_t=\frac{d_t}{\mu_t}=q_t+\varepsilon_t, $$
where $q_t=\lfloor u_t\rfloor$ and $0\leq\varepsilon_t<1$.

For fixed $y$, the strictly increasing slot costs force the cheapest slots to be filled first: moving a positive quantity from a used expensive slot to an unfilled cheaper slot strictly reduces the objective. Consequently, in an optimal solution the slot cost for type $t$ is
$$ \mu_t\left(q_t^2+(2q_t+1)\varepsilon_t\right). $$
When $u_t=s$, this means all slots are full and the cost is $\mu_ts^2$.

2.3. An explicit decomposition into integral bundles

We now realize the optimal aggregate quantities using polynomially many cohorts.

Fix a type $t$, enumerate $S$ as $j_1,\ldots,j_s$, and put
$$ x_i=\frac{y_{t j_i}}{\mu_t}, \qquad c_0=0, \qquad c_i=\sum_{h=1}^{i}x_h. $$
Each $x_i$ lies in $[0,1]$. For $u\in[0,1)$, define
$$ B_t(u)= \left\{ j_i: \text{there exists }\ell\in\mathbb Z \text{ with }c_{i-1}\leq u+\ell<c_i \right\}. $$

The intervals $[c_{i-1},c_i)$ partition $[0,u_t)$. Each has length at most $1$, so it contains at most one point of the translated integer lattice $u+\mathbb Z$. Therefore:

  • Good $j_i$ belongs to $B_t(u)$ for a set of $u$ of total length $x_i$.
  • The bundle cardinality is the number of lattice points in $[0,u_t)$: it is $q_t+1$ when $0\leq u<\varepsilon_t$, and $q_t$ otherwise.

The first assertion follows by cutting $[c_{i-1},c_i)$ at integer boundaries and translating the pieces into $[0,1)$; their total length remains $x_i$, and their interiors do not overlap.

Partition $[0,1)$ at the fractional parts of $c_0,\ldots,c_s$, together with the endpoint $1$. On every resulting open interval, $B_t(u)$ is constant. Give that bundle a cohort mass equal to $\mu_t$ times the interval length, using a rational midpoint to identify the bundle.

There are at most $s+1$ such intervals. This produces rational cohort masses, assigns aggregate quantity $y_{tj}$ of every scarce good, and gives type $t$ a squared-cardinality contribution
$$ \mu_t\left((1-\varepsilon_t)q_t^2+ \varepsilon_t(q_t+1)^2\right) = \mu_t\left(q_t^2+(2q_t+1)\varepsilon_t\right). $$
Thus the resulting allocation attains the linear-program objective.

2.4. Why this allocation globally minimizes squared cardinalities

Consider any feasible allocation of the scarce goods in which every received good is liked by its recipient. Define
$$ y_{tj}=\sum_{c:t(c)=t,\ j\in B_c}z_c, \qquad r_{t\ell}=\sum_{c:t(c)=t,\ |B_c|\geq\ell}z_c. $$
These variables satisfy all constraints of the linear program. Moreover,
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_t\sum_{\ell=1}^{s}(2\ell-1)r_{t\ell} &= \sum_c z_c\sum_{\ell=1}^{|B_c|}(2\ell-1)\\ &=\sum_c z_c|B_c|^2. \end{aligned} $$
Hence the linear-program optimum is a lower bound on $\Phi$ for every eligible integral-bundle allocation. Section 2.3 attains that bound. Its allocation therefore globally minimizes $\Phi$.

2.5. EF1 for scarce goods, and extension to surplus goods

First consider the scarce-good allocation.

Suppose cohorts $c,d$ violate EF1, with the comparison using type $t=t(c)$. An identically zero-valued type cannot violate EF1, so divide its valuation by $a_t$.

Put
$$ a=|B_c|, \qquad r=|B_d|, \qquad e=\sum_{j\in B_d}b_{tj}. $$
Cohort $c$ likes every good in its own bundle, so its normalized utility is $a$. If $e=0$, there is no envy. Otherwise removing a liked good from $B_d$ leaves normalized value $e-1$. Failure of EF1 therefore implies
$$ a<e-1, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad r\geq e\geq a+2. $$

There exists a good $j\in B_d\setminus B_c$ liked by $t$, since $B_d$ contains at least $a+2$ such goods and $B_c$ contains only $a$ goods. Cohort $d$ also likes $j$, because every scarce good was assigned only to a liker.

Choose rational $\delta$ with
$$ 0<\delta\leq\min(z_c,z_d). $$
Split mass $\delta$ from each cohort, adding $j$ to the first bundle and removing it from the second. Type masses and supplies remain unchanged; the recipient did not already own $j$; and all received goods remain liked.

The change in $\Phi$ is
$$ \begin{aligned} \delta\left((a+1)^2+(r-1)^2-a^2-r^2\right) &=2\delta(a-r+1)\\ &\leq-2\delta<0, \end{aligned} $$
contradicting global minimality. Thus the scarce-good allocation is EF1.

Now process each $j\in H$. Give it to every liking cohort. Assign its remaining mass
$$ \sigma_j-M_j\leq1-M_j $$
to disliking cohorts, using a fixed ordering and splitting a boundary cohort if necessary. This assigns the exact supply and creates at most one additional cohort per good.

Every person of type $t$ now receives every good in $H$ that $t$ likes. Thus, for any two final cohorts $c,d$,
$$ v_{t(c)}(B_c\cap H)\geq v_{t(c)}(B_d\cap H). $$
Their projections onto $S$ already satisfy EF1. Adding the displayed inequality to that comparison preserves its removal witness, or preserves the comparison without removal when none was needed. The final allocation is EF1.

2.6. Fractional Pareto optimality

For any feasible fractional reallocation $x$, consider normalized aggregate utility
$$ F(x)=\sum_c\frac{z_c}{a_{t(c)}}v_{t(c)}(x_c) = \sum_j\sum_{c:b_{t(c)j}=1}z_cx_{cj}. $$
For each good, the quantity assigned to likers is at most its supply $\sigma_j$ and at most their total mass $M_j$. Therefore
$$ F(x)\leq\sum_j\min(\sigma_j,M_j). $$

Our allocation attains this bound good by good. Scarce goods are assigned entirely to likers, and every liker receives each surplus good.

Every coefficient $z_c/a_{t(c)}$ is positive. Hence a cohort-level Pareto improvement would strictly increase $F$, contradicting this upper bound. The allocation is fractionally Pareto optimal.

2.7. Complexity

The linear program has $O(\tau m)$ variables and constraints, with polynomial-size rational data. An optimal rational solution can be computed in polynomial time and has polynomial encoding length.

The decomposition uses at most $s+1$ cohorts per type. Its endpoints, interval lengths, and bundle tests require only polynomially many rational operations on numbers of polynomial bit length. Processing surplus goods adds at most $|H|$ cohorts.

Thus the complete construction runs in $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ time. It uses no population replication and no number of operations proportional to a common denominator. The support can further be reduced to at most $m+\tau$ cohorts by the next theorem.

3. Conditional compact witnesses for general valuations

Theorem 4. If a general instance admits an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal allocation, it admits one with at most $m+\tau$ supported cohorts and polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Start with such an allocation and fix its support $C$. Consider the polytope of nonnegative masses on these cohorts satisfying all type-mass and supply equalities.

This polytope is nonempty. It is bounded because its masses sum to $1$. Choose an extreme point.

The equality matrix has $m+\tau$ rows. Its columns are zero-one vectors recording the cohort’s type and owned goods. At an extreme point, the columns corresponding to positive variables must be linearly independent: a nonzero dependence would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving nonnegativity and all equalities. Thus at most $m+\tau$ variables are positive.

The new support is a subset of the old support, so EF1 persists. Fractional Pareto optimality persists by restricting the certificate of Lemma 1.

For the encoding bound, let $r\leq m+\tau$ be the number of positive variables. Choose $r$ independent rows of their columns, obtaining a nonsingular zero-one matrix $A$. Let $Q$ be the product of the input denominators of the masses and supplies. Its bit length is $O(L)$.

Cramer’s rule gives denominators dividing $Q\det A$. Hadamard’s inequality yields
$$ |\det A|\leq r^{r/2}. $$
For a numerator determinant, the replaced column has entries between $0$ and $Q$, hence Euclidean norm at most $Q\sqrt r$. The other columns have norms at most $\sqrt r$. Consequently, each numerator determinant has absolute value at most $Qr^{r/2}$.

Every mass therefore has bit length
$$ O\!\left(L+r\log(r+1)\right). $$
Listing the bundles also takes polynomial space. $\square$

Applied to Theorem 3, this gives unconditional existence with at most $m+\tau$ cohorts within the personalized binary class.

3.1. Existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$

For a general yes-instance, Theorem 4 supplies a certificate with polynomially many cohorts and polynomial-size rational masses.

Check the type and supply equalities by exact arithmetic. Check EF1 through
$$ v_{t(c)}(B_c) \geq v_{t(c)}(B_d)-\max_{j\in B_d}v_{t(c)j} $$
for every ordered pair, defining the maximum as $0$ for an empty bundle. Finally, check fractional Pareto optimality by Theorem 2.

All checks take polynomial time. Thus the existence decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

3.2. An exact denominator-independent finite algorithm

There are
$$ N=\tau2^m $$
possible cohorts. Put $d=m+\tau$ and enumerate supports of size at most $d$.

For each candidate support:

  • Check EF1.
  • Check fractional Pareto optimality by Theorem 2.
  • Maximize $\delta$ subject to the type and supply equalities and

$$ z_c\geq\delta\geq0. $$
Accept precisely when the optimum is positive.

The third test is equivalent to the existence of strictly positive feasible masses on that support. Theorem 4 guarantees completeness of the enumeration. Its running time is bounded by
$$ (d+1)(\tau2^m)^d\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
This removes any replication factor involving the population denominator, but is exponential in the structural parameters.

4. What remains open

The new polynomial algorithm handles arbitrary liking patterns, arbitrary supported masses, arbitrary supplies, and type-dependent positive values $a_t$. Its proof relies on the fact that transferring a liked good preserves normalized aggregate welfare. With distinct positive values within a type, that argument no longer applies: the recipient and donor can value the transferred good differently.

The general compact-witness theorem remains conditional. Neither it nor the graph test proves existence for every general instance.

Population replication also does not directly transfer the ordinary discrete result: it introduces multiple copies of each good, while this task permits each individual to receive at most one copy. The ordinary discrete allocation model does not impose that additional restriction. [Garg–Murhekar, problem setting and allocation definitions](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.14229v2)

Accordingly, I have not established either a polynomial-time algorithm uniform in $k$ or an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction for the general support-and-supply problem.
