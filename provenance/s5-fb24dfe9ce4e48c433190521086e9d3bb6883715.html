The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given goods \(M\), a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), explicit OR bid lists \(B_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), decide whether a feasible mass allocation reaches revenue \(K\). For each type, let \(\mathcal C_t\) be its collections of pairwise-disjoint atomic bids, including the empty collection; choose masses \(y_{t,Q}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\), total use of each good \(j\) at most \(q_j\), and revenue at least \(K\). In the anchor restriction, \(T=\{t\}\), \(\mu_t=1\), \(q_j=1\), and all bids have value \(1\) and size at most \(3\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) be a set of \(m\ge 1\) goods. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an explicit OR bid list \(B_t\) of nonempty bundles \(S\subseteq M\) with nonnegative binary-rational values \(v_t(S)\), satisfying \(v_t(S)\le v_t(S')\) whenever listed bundles satisfy \(S\subseteq S'\). The empty bundle has value zero.
Let \(\mathcal C_t\) contain all collections of pairwise-disjoint bids from \(B_t\), including the empty collection. Write \(U(Q)=\bigcup_{S\in Q}S\) and \(V_t(Q)=\sum_{S\in Q}v_t(S)\). These collections are implicit, not part of the input.
Given nonnegative binary-rational supplies \(q_j\) and threshold \(K\), a feasible mass allocation consists of real \(y_{t,Q}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{Q:j\in U(Q)}y_{t,Q}\le q_j\) for every \(j\in M\). Unused supply is allowed; revenue is \(\sum_t\sum_Q V_t(Q)y_{t,Q}\).
The decision problem asks whether feasible revenue is at least \(K\). Its anchor restriction is \(T=\{t\}\), \(\mu_t=1\), \(q_j=1\), and every listed bid has value \(1\) and bundle size at most \(3\). Let \(b=\sum_t|B_t|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,b,L\).

Is the mass OR winner-determination decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete OR winner determination is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even when all bid values equal \(1\) and bundles have size at most \(3\) (Lehmann–Müller–Sandholm, *The Winner Determination Problem*).
[Sandholm–Suri](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/sideconstraints.geb06.pdf) establish polynomial-time solvability when atomic package bids may be accepted independently and fractionally; that model does not impose the type-wise distributions over disjoint collections defined here. No result settling this exact mass model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, interpreting the society as a distribution and supplies as per-capita quantities.
  • Specified nonnegative binary-rational bid values, supplies, and revenue threshold.
  • Carried over monotonicity of values on nested listed bundles and free disposal.
  • Represented the zero-valued empty bid through the empty collection; substantive atomic bids use nonempty bundles, including the full goods set.
  • Specified real allocation masses and implicit collections, so input length measures explicit bid lists rather than an enumerated configuration list.

Result

The mass OR winner-determination decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness already holds under the stated anchor restriction. It also admits an exact algorithm running in \(2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,b,L)\) time.

1. At the anchor, the mass and discrete optima coincide

Assume \(T=\{t\}\), \(\mu_t=1\), and \(q_j=1\) for every good \(j\). Define
\[ \]
\(W=\max_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}V_t(Q)\).

The maximum exists because the explicit bid list is finite.

Every nonnegative vector satisfying \(\sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=1\) satisfies the supply constraints: for each \(j\in M\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{Q:j\in U(Q)}y_{t,Q}\le \sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=1=q_j.\)

Furthermore, its revenue satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}V_t(Q)y_{t,Q}\le W\sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=W.\)

Conversely, choose a maximizing collection \(Q^\star\), set \(y_{t,Q^\star}=1\), and set all other variables to zero. This allocation is feasible and has revenue \(W\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=\max_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}V_t(Q).\)

This identity holds for arbitrary nonnegative bid values at the anchor. In particular, if every bid has value \(1\), the optimum is the maximum number of pairwise-disjoint listed bundles.

2. Polynomial reduction and anchor hardness

Use the restricted discrete OR winner-determination problem supplied in the question: all bids have value \(1\), all bundles have size at most \(3\), and the decision asks whether at least \(k\) bids can be accepted with pairwise-disjoint bundles. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is established in the [Lehmann–Müller–Sandholm chapter](https://academic.oup.com/mit-press-scholarship-online/book/29406/chapter-abstract/244789405).

Construct a mass instance using the same goods and bundles, placing all bids in one type. Set \(\mu_t=1\), every supply to \(1\), and \(K=k\). Under OR semantics, bidder identities impose no additional restriction on accepting disjoint bids, so pooling the bid lists preserves the feasible discrete collections. Duplicate bundles, if present, may be removed: two copies of the same nonempty bundle cannot belong to a disjoint collection.

All constructed bids have value \(1\), so the required monotonicity condition holds whenever two listed bundles are nested. The construction takes polynomial time and has polynomial output length.

By Section 1, the constructed mass instance has revenue at least \(k\) exactly when the discrete instance contains at least \(k\) pairwise-disjoint bids. Thus the anchor restriction, and consequently the general problem, is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

3. Polynomial-size certificates for the general problem

The implicit number of collections does not prevent membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Introduce a nonnegative slack variable \(s_j\) for each supply constraint, giving equations

\(\displaystyle \sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_t}y_{t,Q}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{Q:j\in U(Q)}y_{t,Q}+s_j=q_j\quad(j\in M).\)

Write these equations as \(Az=r\), where \(z\) comprises the allocation and slack variables. Let \(d=\tau+m\). The matrix \(A\) has \(d\) rows and entries in \(\{0,1\}\).

The feasible set is nonempty: assign each type entirely to its empty collection. It is also compact, since

\(0\le y_{t,Q}\le\mu_t\quad\text{and}\quad 0\le s_j\le q_j.\)

Hence the linear revenue objective attains a maximum.

Sparse optimal solutions

Choose an optimal solution \(z^\star\) having the smallest possible number of positive coordinates. Its corresponding columns of \(A\) are linearly independent.

Indeed, suppose these columns were dependent. There would be a nonzero vector \(h\), supported on those coordinates, with \(Ah=0\). For sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), both \(z^\star+\varepsilon h\) and \(z^\star-\varepsilon h\) would be feasible. Optimality then forces \(c^\mathsf{T}h=0\), where \(c\) is the revenue coefficient vector. Move in one of these directions until a positive coordinate first becomes zero. This preserves feasibility and revenue while decreasing the support, a contradiction.

Consequently, an optimal solution has at most \(d\) positive coordinates, including slacks.

Rational encoding length

The matrix \(A\) has full row rank: the empty-collection column for each type is its type-row unit vector, and the slack columns are the supply-row unit vectors. Extend the independent support columns of \(z^\star\) to a square nonsingular matrix \(H\) of order \(d\), assigning zero to added coordinates.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators appearing in \(r\). Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and \(Dr\) is an integer vector whose entries have binary length \(O(L)\). Cramer’s rule gives each basis coordinate as

\(\displaystyle z_i^\star=\frac{\det H_i(Dr)}{D\det H},\)

where \(H_i(Dr)\) replaces column \(i\) of \(H\) by \(Dr\). Since \(H\) has entries in \(\{0,1\}\),

\(\displaystyle |\det H|\le d!,\qquad |\det H_i(Dr)|\le d!\max_\ell |(Dr)_\ell|.\)

Thus every positive coordinate has rational encoding length \(O(L+d\log d)\).

A yes-certificate therefore lists at most \(m+\tau\) allocation variables, each specified by its type, its selected bid indices, and its rational mass. Each collection contains at most \(m\) bids because its bundles are nonempty and disjoint. The certificate has polynomial length in \(m,\tau,b,L\).

A verifier checks disjointness, nonnegativity, all mass and supply constraints, and the revenue threshold using exact rational arithmetic in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,b,L)\) time. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), and completes the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness proof.

4. An exact algorithm parameterized by the number of goods

For each type \(t\) and subset \(A\subseteq M\), define

\(\displaystyle w_t(A)=\max\{V_t(Q):Q\in\mathcal C_t,\ U(Q)=A\},\)

omitting subsets for which no such collection exists.

These values can be computed without enumerating collections. Write the bids of type \(t\) as \(S_1,\ldots,S_{b_t}\), where \(b_t=|B_t|\). Let \(D_i(A)\) be the maximum value of a disjoint collection drawn from the first \(i\) bids with union exactly \(A\), using \(-\infty\) for unattainable states. Initialize

\(D_0(\varnothing)=0,\qquad D_0(A)=-\infty\ \text{for }A\ne\varnothing.\)

For each bid \(S_i\), use

\(\displaystyle D_i(A)= \begin{cases} \max\{D_{i-1}(A),\,v_t(S_i)+D_{i-1}(A\setminus S_i)\},&S_i\subseteq A,\\ D_{i-1}(A),&S_i\not\subseteq A. \end{cases}\)

The recurrence is exact: a collection either excludes \(S_i\), or includes it and has remaining union \(A\setminus S_i\), which guarantees disjointness. Hence \(w_t(A)=D_{b_t}(A)\). Across all types, the computation takes \((b+\tau)2^m\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\) time.

Now solve the explicit rational linear program

\(\displaystyle \max \sum_t\sum_A w_t(A)x_{t,A}\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle \sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\sum_{A:j\in A}x_{t,A}\le q_j,\qquad x_{t,A}\ge0.\)

There are at most \(\tau2^m\) variables and \(\tau+m\) constraints.

This program has exactly the original optimum. Given an original allocation, aggregate its variables by their unions; consumption is unchanged, and replacing collection values by \(w_t(A)\) can only increase revenue. Conversely, every variable \(x_{t,A}\) can be assigned to a collection attaining \(w_t(A)\), preserving consumption and revenue.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for explicitly encoded rational linear programming applies: the program is feasible and bounded, and all coefficient lengths are polynomial in \(L\). Its encoding length is \(2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,b,L)\). Thus the total exact running time is

\(\displaystyle 2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,b,L).\)

In particular, the problem is polynomial-time solvable for fixed \(m\), with \(\tau\) treated as the actual input support size.

5. Why independent fractional bids give a different problem

[Sandholm–Suri](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~sandholm/sideconstraints.geb06.pdf) obtain polynomial-time solvability for independently fractional package bids. Their feasible region can strictly exceed the feasible region here.

For example, take one type of mass \(1\), three goods with supply \(1\) each, and value-\(1\) bids on \(\{1,2\}\), \(\{2,3\}\), and \(\{1,3\}\). Every two bids intersect, so every disjoint collection contains at most one bid. The mass optimum is therefore \(1\).

Independent fractional acceptance permits accepting half of each bid. Each good then has total consumption \(1\), while revenue is \(3/2\). This acceptance vector cannot arise from a distribution over disjoint collections of total mass \(1\).

The distinction is therefore substantive: the mass model remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard despite allowing real allocation masses.
