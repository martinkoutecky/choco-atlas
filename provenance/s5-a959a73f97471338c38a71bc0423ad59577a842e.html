The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) goods, population scale \(N\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and value oracles for W-MRS valuations \(v_t\), choose \(x_t\in[0,1]^m\) subject to \(\sum_t N\mu_t x_{tj}\le 1\). With each type-\(t\) agent independently receiving good \(j\) with probability \(1-e^{-x_{tj}}\), can one compute an \(x\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N,1/\epsilon\) such that its expected welfare is at least \(1-\epsilon\) times the best welfare in this product-distribution range and at least \(1-1/e-\epsilon\) times the optimum over all feasible masses assigned to integral bundles?

A support-size polynomial algorithm

The answer is yes. The key is to rescale each type’s coordinates by its maximum feasible per-capita allocation. After this rescaling, gradient samples and curvature admit bounds independent of the numerical population size.

The local-search structure resembles Section 2 of the [cited discrete paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1109.1053). The proof below establishes the required bounds directly for divisible populations, without expanding the population into bidders.

1. Concavity of the exponential extension

For a normalized valuation \(v\), define
\( G_v(x)=\mathbb E[v(R(x))], \)
where goods are included independently with probabilities \(1-e^{-x_j}\).

We first prove that \(G_v\) is concave when \(v\) is a weighted matroid rank sum.

Consider a single matroid rank function \(r\). For a fixed set \(S\), define the symmetric matrix \(D^S\) by
\( D^S_{jj}=-\bigl(r(S\cup\{j\})-r(S)\bigr) \)
and, for distinct \(i,j\), by
\( D^S_{ij}=r(S\cup\{i,j\})-r(S\cup\{i\})-r(S\cup\{j\})+r(S). \)

The function
\( r_S(A)=r(S\cup A)-r(S) \)
is the rank function of the contraction by \(S\), with elements of \(S\) treated as loops. Its nonloops partition into parallel classes. For completeness, the relation between distinct nonloops given by \(r_S(\{i,j\})=1\) is transitive: if \(r_S(\{i,j\})=r_S(\{j,k\})=1\), rank submodularity gives
\( r_S(\{i,j,k\})+r_S(\{j\})\le r_S(\{i,j\})+r_S(\{j,k\})=2. \)
Since \(j\) is a nonloop, this implies \(r_S(\{i,j,k\})=1\), and hence \(r_S(\{i,k\})=1\).

Consequently, \(D^S\) has diagonal entry \(-1\) at every nonloop and off-diagonal entry \(-1\) exactly between nonloops in the same parallel class. All other entries are zero. Thus
\( D^S=-\sum_C\mathbf 1_C\mathbf 1_C^{\mathsf T}, \)
where the sum ranges over the nonloop parallel classes. In particular, \(D^S\) is negative semidefinite.

Differentiating the finite expectation defining \(G_r\) gives
\( \nabla^2G_r(x)=\mathbb E[D^{R(x)}]. \)
Indeed, conditioning on all coordinates except \(j\) gives
\( \frac{\partial^2G_r}{\partial x_j^2} =-e^{-x_j}\mathbb E[r(T\cup\{j\})-r(T)], \)
where \(T\) excludes \(j\). This equals \(\mathbb E[D^{R(x)}_{jj}]\). For distinct \(i,j\), conditioning on their absence gives the corresponding mixed-derivative identity; the discrete second difference is zero whenever either element is already present.

An expectation of negative semidefinite matrices is negative semidefinite. Therefore \(G_r\) is concave on the nonnegative orthant. A finite nonnegative weighted sum preserves this property, proving concavity of \(G_v\) for every valuation in the question.

We will also use the following consequences of normalized, monotone submodularity:
\( 0\le v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\le v(\{j\}), \qquad v(S)\le\sum_{j\in S}v(\{j\}). \)
Matroid ranks satisfy these properties, and their nonnegative weighted sums inherit them.

2. Comparison with the integral-bundle mass benchmark

Write \(a_t=N\mu_t>0\). We prove
\( W_{\mathrm{range}}\ge(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}. \)

Let \(z\) be any feasible benchmark solution. For each type \(t\), let \(S_t\) have distribution
\( \Pr(S_t=S)=z_{tS}/a_t, \)
and define its marginal vector by
\( p_{tj}=\Pr(j\in S_t). \)
Then \(p\in P\), because
\( \sum_ta_tp_{tj} =\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}z_{tS}\le1. \)

By the concavity proved above,
\( G_{v_t}(p_t) \ge\mathbb E[G_{v_t}(\mathbf 1_{S_t})]. \)

For any fixed bundle \(S\), the random set defining \(G_v(\mathbf 1_S)\) retains each element of \(S\) independently with probability \(q=1-1/e\). We claim that its expected value is at least \(qv(S)\).

To prove this, order \(S=\{j_1,\ldots,j_s\}\), and put \(A_i=\{j_1,\ldots,j_{i-1}\}\). For the retained set \(T\), telescoping and diminishing marginal values imply
\( v(T) =\sum_{i:j_i\in T}\bigl(v((T\cap A_i)\cup\{j_i\})-v(T\cap A_i)\bigr) \ge \sum_{i:j_i\in T}\bigl(v(A_i\cup\{j_i\})-v(A_i)\bigr). \)
Taking expectations yields
\( \mathbb E[v(T)] \ge q\sum_{i=1}^s\bigl(v(A_i\cup\{j_i\})-v(A_i)\bigr) =qv(S). \)

Therefore
\( W(p) \ge(1-1/e)\sum_t\sum_Sz_{tS}v_t(S). \)
The benchmark feasible set is compact: it has finitely many coordinates, and \(0\le z_{tS}\le a_t\). Applying this inequality to an optimal benchmark solution proves the claimed comparison.

3. Rescaling removes numerical population dependence

Define the positive rational numbers
\( c_t=\min\{1,a_t^{-1}\}, \qquad b_t=a_tc_t=\min\{a_t,1\}. \)
Introduce coordinates \(u_{tj}\) through \(x_{tj}=c_tu_{tj}\), and define
\( Q=\left\{u\in[0,1]^{\tau\times m}: \sum_tb_tu_{tj}\le1\ \text{for every }j\right\}. \)

This transformation is a bijection between \(Q\) and \(P\). One direction follows by substitution. Conversely, feasibility of \(x\) implies both \(x_{tj}\le1\) and \(a_tx_{tj}\le1\), so \(x_{tj}\le c_t\). Thus \(u_{tj}=x_{tj}/c_t\) belongs to \(Q\).

Let
\( d=\tau m, \qquad H(u)=W(cu)=\sum_ta_tG_{v_t}(c_tu_t), \qquad B=\max_{t,j}b_tv_t(\{j\}). \)
The algorithm obtains \(B\) using \(d\) singleton queries.

If \(B=0\), all singleton values vanish. The submodular bound from Section 1 then implies that every valuation is identically zero. Returning \(x=0\) settles this case. Henceforth assume \(B>0\).

The objective \(H\) is concave. Moreover, throughout \(Q\),
\( 0\le H(u) \le\sum_{t,j}a_t(1-e^{-c_tu_{tj}})v_t(\{j\}) \le\sum_{t,j}b_tu_{tj}v_t(\{j\}) \le dB. \)

There is also a lower bound on its optimum. Choose a coordinate attaining \(B\), and let \(u\) equal one there and zero elsewhere. This point belongs to \(Q\), since \(b_t\le1\). Its value is
\( a_t(1-e^{-c_t})v_t(\{j\}) \ge(1-1/e)b_tv_t(\{j\}) \ge B/2. \)
Here \(1-e^{-c}\ge c(1-e^{-1})\) for \(0\le c\le1\), by concavity of \(1-e^{-c}\), and \(1-e^{-1}>1/2\).

Writing \(H^*=\max_{u\in Q}H(u)=W_{\mathrm{range}}\), we have proved
\( B/2\le H^*\le dB. \)

Gradient and curvature bounds

For a coordinate \((t,j)\), put \(x_t=c_tu_t\). Conditioning on all goods except \(j\) gives
\( \frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{tj}} =b_te^{-x_{tj}} \mathbb E[v_t(T\cup\{j\})-v_t(T)], \)
where each \(k\ne j\) belongs to \(T\) independently with probability \(1-e^{-x_{tk}}\). Consequently,
\( 0\le\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{tj}} \le b_tv_t(\{j\})\le B. \)

Every entry of the Hessian of \(G_{v_t}\) has absolute value at most \(v_t(\{j\})\), when \(j\) is either its diagonal coordinate or one of its two mixed coordinates. For diagonal entries, this follows from the first-marginal formula. For a mixed entry, the relevant discrete difference is
\( [v_t(T\cup\{i,j\})-v_t(T\cup\{i\})] -[v_t(T\cup\{j\})-v_t(T)], \)
which lies between \(-v_t(\{j\})\) and zero by monotonicity and submodularity.

The rescaling multiplies a within-type Hessian entry by \(a_tc_t^2\). Since \(c_t\le1\),
\( a_tc_t^2v_t(\{j\})\le b_tv_t(\{j\})\le B. \)
Cross-type entries are zero. Thus every Hessian entry of \(H\) has absolute value at most \(B\).

For any \(u,y\in Q\) and \(0\le\eta\le1\), Taylor’s formula along their segment now gives
\( H(u+\eta(y-u)) \ge H(u)+\eta\nabla H(u)\cdot(y-u) -\frac{\eta^2}{2}d^2B. \)
Indeed, every coordinate of \(y-u\) has absolute value at most one, so
\( |(y-u)^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2H(w)(y-u)| \le B\|y-u\|_1^2\le d^2B \)
at every point \(w\) on the segment.

4. Polynomial-query rational gradient estimates

We construct rational estimates \(g\) satisfying, simultaneously,
\( \|g-\nabla H(u)\|_\infty\le\frac{\epsilon B}{8d}. \)

Let \(T_{\max}\) be the iteration bound specified in Section 5, and set
\( \rho=\frac{\epsilon}{16dm}. \)
For each coordinate, compute a dyadic rational \(q_{tj}\in[0,1]\) satisfying
\( |q_{tj}-e^{-x_{tj}}|\le\rho. \)

For a fixed coordinate \((t,j)\), sample a set \(\widehat T\) excluding \(j\), with independent inclusion probabilities \(1-q_{tk}\), and form
\( Z=b_tq_{tj}\bigl(v_t(\widehat T\cup\{j\})-v_t(\widehat T)\bigr). \)
This uses two exact value queries, and \(0\le Z\le B\).

Couple \(\widehat T\) to the set with the exact exponential probabilities using common independent uniform random variables. The probability of any discrepancy is at most \((m-1)\rho\). The marginal lies in \([0,v_t(\{j\})]\); accounting also for the prefactor error gives
\( \left|\mathbb E[Z]-\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{tj}}\right| \le m\rho B=\frac{\epsilon B}{16d}. \)

Average \(K\) independent copies, where
\( K=\left\lceil \frac{128d^2}{\epsilon^2} \left\lceil\log_2(6dT_{\max})\right\rceil \right\rceil. \)
Hoeffding’s inequality gives
\( \Pr\left( |\overline Z-\mathbb E[Z]|>\frac{\epsilon B}{16d} \right) \le 2\exp\left(-\frac{K\epsilon^2}{128d^2}\right) \le\frac{1}{3dT_{\max}}. \)
Combining sampling error and bias proves the desired coordinate accuracy except with this probability.

These bounds hold conditionally on every preceding algorithmic history. Therefore a union bound over all coordinates and at most \(T_{\max}\) rounds shows that all gradient estimates are accurate with probability at least \(2/3\).

Everything here uses finite rational arithmetic and finitely many random bits. In particular, the exponential approximations are computable in polynomial time: for rational \(s\in[0,1]\), truncating the Taylor series for \(e^{-s}\) after degree \(r\) has error at most \(1/(r+1)!\le2^{-r}\). Taking \(r=O(\log(1/\rho))\), clipping to \([0,1]\), and rounding to a sufficiently fine dyadic grid gives the required approximation. Sampling a dyadic Bernoulli variable uses its denominator’s bit length in fair random bits.

5. The algorithm and its guarantee

Set
\( \eta=\frac{\epsilon}{8d^2}, \qquad T_{\max}=\left\lceil\frac{128d^3}{\epsilon^2}\right\rceil+1. \)

Initialize \(u=0\). In each of at most \(T_{\max}\) rounds:

  • Construct the rational gradient estimate \(g\) from Section 4.
  • Find a rational maximizer \(y\in Q\) of \(g\cdot y\).
  • If \((y-u)\cdot g\le\epsilon B/4\), return \(x=cu\).
  • Otherwise update \(u\leftarrow u+\eta(y-u)\).

If the iteration cap is reached, return the current \(x=cu\).

The linear maximization in step 2 separates across goods. For each good, it is a fractional knapsack problem with weights \(b_t>0\), upper bounds one, and coefficients \(g_{tj}\ge0\). Sort types by \(g_{tj}/b_t\), fill coordinates in that order, and partially fill the last coordinate if necessary. This is optimal because transferring any available capacity from a lower ratio to a higher ratio cannot decrease the objective.

All iterates are rational and remain in \(Q\), since \(0<\eta<1\) and updates are convex combinations of feasible points. Hence the output always belongs to \(P\).

For the approximation analysis, condition on the event that every gradient estimate is accurate.

Stopping implies approximate optimality

Let \(u^*\) maximize \(H\) over \(Q\). At a stopping round, concavity and linear optimality of \(y\) imply
\( H^*-H(u) \le(u^*-u)\cdot\nabla H(u) \le(y-u)\cdot g+\frac{\epsilon B}{8} \le\frac{3\epsilon B}{8}. \)
The middle inequality uses
\( \|u^*-u\|_1\le d \)
and the coordinate estimation bound. Since \(H^*\ge B/2\),
\( H^*-H(u)\le\frac{3\epsilon}{4}H^*\le\epsilon H^*. \)
Thus the returned point satisfies
\( W(x)=H(u)\ge(1-\epsilon)W_{\mathrm{range}}. \)

Every update makes sufficient progress

When the stopping test fails, gradient accuracy gives
\( (y-u)\cdot\nabla H(u) >(y-u)\cdot g-\frac{\epsilon B}{8} >\frac{\epsilon B}{8}. \)
The curvature bound consequently yields
\( H(u+\eta(y-u))-H(u) > \eta\frac{\epsilon B}{8}-\frac{\eta^2d^2B}{2} =\frac{\epsilon^2B}{128d^2}. \)

Starting from zero, \(T_{\max}\) such updates would produce a value greater than \(dB\), contradicting Section 3. Therefore the algorithm stops within its iteration cap on the accurate-estimation event.

That event has probability at least \(2/3\), proving the first requested guarantee. Section 2 then gives, on the same event,
\( W(x)\ge(1-\epsilon)(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} \ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}. \)

6. Bit complexity and population dependence

The rational numbers \(a_t,b_t,c_t\) have bit lengths polynomial in \(L+\log N\). No step creates individual bidders.

The number of gradient rounds is \(O(d^3/\epsilon^2)\), and each coordinate estimate uses
\( O\!\left(d^2\epsilon^{-2}\log(2d/\epsilon)\right) \)
samples. Thus the total number of value queries is
\( O\!\left(d^6\epsilon^{-4}\log(2d/\epsilon)\right), \)
in addition to the singleton queries.

The arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity:

  • Each knapsack maximizer has coordinates equal to zero, one, or a remaining rational capacity divided by some \(b_t\). Its bit length is polynomial in the input length.
  • Each update increases the iterate’s bit length by at most a polynomial amount: its denominator can be taken to divide the product of the previous denominator, the step denominator, and the relevant maximizer denominator.
  • Each sampled marginal uses two oracle answers of bit length at most \(L\). Summing polynomially many such rationals has polynomial bit length.
  • Exponential approximation and dyadic sampling require precision \(O(\log(dm/\epsilon))\).

Consequently, the running time, number of queries, number of random bits, and output bit length are all polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N,1/\epsilon\), with the encoding of the supplied rational accuracy included in the total explicit input length.

This proves the requested affirmative result with exact rational feasibility and simultaneous success probability at least \(2/3\).
