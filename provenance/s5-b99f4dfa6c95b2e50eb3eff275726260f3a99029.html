The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), initial opinions \(b_t\in\{0,1\}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t<1/2\), and a rational symmetric interaction-density matrix \(P\), choose \(q_t\in[0,1]\), the final fraction of type \(t\) holding opinion \(1\), to minimize \(\sum_t\mu_t|q_t-b_t|\), subject for every \(t\) to \(\sum_{t'}\mu_{t'}P_{tt'}q_{t'}\le\sum_{t'}\mu_{t'}P_{tt'}(1-q_{t'})\).

The optimization problem minimizes \(C(q)\) over these states; its decision version receives a binary rational budget \(K\in[0,1]\) and asks whether a feasible state has \(C(q)\le K\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including the budget; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous elimination problem solvable exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is its decision version NP-hard?

For finite simple undirected networks with a strict initial majority of opinion \(0\), minimizing the number of opinion changes subject to no neighborhood having a strict majority of opinion \(1\) is NP-hard, even on bipartite graphs of diameter at most \(6\), and is \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by the change budget. Result

The continuous elimination problem is exactly polynomial-time solvable. For the stipulated \(\,m=2\,\), an optimal rational state and its exact cost can be computed in \((\tau L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations, hence in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Its decision version belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

The proof uses precisely the given support and permits arbitrary real fractions; it imposes no integrality restriction.

1. An equivalent linear program

For every \(t\in T\), define the final mass holding opinion \(1\) by
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle x_t=\mu_tq_t.\)

Because \(\mu_t>0\), this is a bijection between the permitted states and vectors satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), with inverse \(q_t=x_t/\mu_t\).

Define the rational constants
\(\displaystyle B=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tb_t,\qquad r_t=\sum_{s\in T}\mu_sP_{ts},\qquad c_t=1-2b_t.\)

The elimination constraint for row \(t\) is equivalent to
\(\displaystyle \sum_{s\in T}P_{ts}x_s\le r_t-\sum_{s\in T}P_{ts}x_s,\)
and therefore to
\(\displaystyle 2\sum_{s\in T}P_{ts}x_s\le r_t.\)

The objective is also affine. Indeed, if \(b_t=0\), then \(\mu_t|q_t-b_t|=x_t\); if \(b_t=1\), then it equals \(\mu_t-x_t\). Consequently,
\(\displaystyle C(q)=B+\sum_{t\in T}c_tx_t.\)

Thus the required optimization is exactly the following rational linear program:

  • Minimize \(\displaystyle B+\sum_{t\in T}c_tx_t\).
  • Require \(\displaystyle 2\sum_{s\in T}P_{ts}x_s\le r_t\) for each \(t\in T\).
  • Require \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) for each \(t\in T\).

It has \(\tau\) variables and \(3\tau\) inequalities. Both directions of equivalence follow from the bijection above and the displayed identities, so neither feasibility nor cost changes under this formulation.

The feasible region is nonempty: \(x=0\) satisfies every inequality because \(r_t\ge0\). It is closed and bounded, being contained in \(\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\). Hence the continuous objective attains its minimum.

Positive diagonal entries are simply coefficients in these inequalities. A zero interaction row yields \(0\le0\), and local ties satisfy the non-strict inequalities exactly.

2. Polynomial encoding size and exact computation

We verify that forming this linear program does not introduce numbers of excessive bit length.

Let \(D\) be the product of the positive denominators appearing in the input rational numbers, including the budget. Since their binary encodings are included in \(L\), the binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Each input denominator divides \(D\), so the denominator of each product \(\mu_sP_{ts}\) divides \(D^2\). In particular,
\(\displaystyle D^2r_t=\sum_{s\in T}(D\mu_s)(DP_{ts})\)
is an integer.

Multiplying every constraint by \(D^2\) therefore gives an integer system. Since
\(0\le P_{ts}\le1\), \(0<\mu_t\le1\), and
\(\displaystyle 0\le r_t\le\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s=1,\)
all its coefficients and right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(2D^2\). Each consequently has \(O(L)\) bits.

The dense constraint system has \(O(\tau^2)\) entries, so its total encoding length is \(O(\tau^2L)\). Computing \(D\), the row sums, and the scaled constraints requires only polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial bit length.

We now apply the standard exact rational linear-programming theorem: a nonempty bounded polyhedron given by rational inequalities, together with a rational linear objective, admits an exact optimal rational solution computable in time polynomial in the number of variables, number of inequalities, and their binary encoding length. An ellipsoid-method formulation returning an optimal vertex is stated in [Cai, Theorem 1](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/cai/publication/thesis/thesis.pdf#page=38).

All hypotheses hold here: the inequalities and objective are rational and explicitly listed, and the feasible region is nonempty and bounded. Thus the running time is \((\tau L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations for \(m=2\).

For completeness, exact output also has polynomial length. A linear objective on a nonempty bounded polyhedron attains its minimum at a vertex. At any vertex, the active constraint normals span all \(\tau\) dimensions: otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to them would allow sufficiently small displacements in both directions, preserving active constraints and all inactive inequalities, contradicting extremality.

Choose \(\tau\) linearly independent active constraints in the scaled integer system. They uniquely determine that vertex. By Cramer’s rule, every coordinate is a ratio of integer determinants. The Leibniz determinant formula bounds their absolute values by
\(\displaystyle \tau!(2D^2)^\tau.\)
Their bit lengths are therefore \(O(\tau(L+\log\tau))\). Dividing by the positive rational masses to recover \(q_t=x_t/\mu_t\) preserves polynomial output length.

The decision version can either compare the exact optimum with \(K\), or append the single rational inequality
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}c_tx_t\le K-B\)
and test linear-program feasibility. Both procedures run in polynomial time, including when the optimum equals the budget exactly.

3. Why the finite-network hardness does not transfer

The cited [finite-network hardness theorem](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p749.pdf) requires each vertex’s final opinion to be binary. Giving each vertex its own type with equal mass and taking \(P\) to be the adjacency matrix reproduces the neighborhood inequalities, but the present formulation allows fractional final opinions.

That difference can strictly change the optimum, even on a small bipartite graph.

Consider the star with one center and three leaves. Give all four types mass \(1/4\), take \(P\) to be its adjacency matrix, and initially assign opinion \(1\) only to the center. The initial mass holding opinion \(1\) is \(1/4<1/2\).

Each leaf’s constraint forces
\(\displaystyle \tfrac14q_{\mathrm{center}}\le\tfrac14(1-q_{\mathrm{center}}),\)
so \(q_{\mathrm{center}}\le1/2\). Every feasible state therefore costs at least
\(\displaystyle \tfrac14(1-q_{\mathrm{center}})\ge\tfrac18.\)

Setting the center fraction to \(1/2\) and all leaf fractions to zero is feasible: each leaf sees a tie, and the center sees only opinion \(0\). Its cost is \(1/8\), proving the continuous optimum.

With binary final opinions, the same leaf constraints force the center to opinion \(0\). Changing only the center is feasible and costs \(1/4\). Thus the binary optimum is strictly larger.

The continuous problem therefore admits the exact linear-programming algorithm proved above. Its decision version could be NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
