The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\), \(k\), voter types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu\), a fixed Thiele utility \(f\), and an explicitly represented known rational distribution \(P\) over signatures \(a\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}\), each of \(m\) candidates is revealed sequentially with a signature drawn from \(P\). Find an irrevocable safe policy selecting exactly \(k\) candidates that maximizes \(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f(\sum_{q\in W}a_t^q)]\), with complexity studied especially for fixed \(\tau\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval patterns.
The input includes integers \(1\leq k\leq m\), with \(m\) encoded in unary, and a distribution \(P\) represented by a list of distinct signatures \(a\in\{0,1\}^{\tau}\) and positive binary-rational probabilities summing to \(1\). Let \(s=|\operatorname{supp}(P)|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Fix a utility \(f:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}\to\mathbb{Q}\) whose values are exactly computable in time polynomial in the argument; no sign, monotonicity, or concavity restriction is imposed.
There are \(m\) distinct candidate arrivals with signatures drawn independently from \(P\); coordinate \(a_t\) specifies whether every member of type \(t\) approves that candidate. Equal signatures may recur.
A deterministic policy observes the current signature and past observations and decisions, then immediately and irrevocably accepts or rejects the candidate. It is safe if it selects exactly \(k\) candidates on every sequence of signatures from the support of \(P\); ties between optimal actions may be resolved arbitrarily.
For the selected arrival positions \(W\), maximize \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f(\sum_{q\in W}a_t^q)]\). A policy may be represented by an algorithm computing its next decision; running time includes preprocessing and execution over the horizon.

For each fixed \(f\), is exact optimal safe-policy computation polynomial-time in \(m,k,\tau,s,L\), or is it NP-hard? Does the classification change when \(\tau\) is fixed?

For discrete voters under independent approvals with one common approval probability, an optimal safe policy for every Thiele utility is computable in polynomial time when the voter count is constant ([Online Approval Committee Elections](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2022/0036.pdf)).
No result settling exactly the rational-mass, explicitly represented joint-signature model was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one because the society is a distribution over types.
  • Specified independent, identically distributed candidate signatures across arrivals; correlations among type coordinates remain permitted by the given joint distribution.
  • Required a nonempty society and integers satisfying 1 ≤ k ≤ m.
  • Restricted the fixed real-valued utility to exactly computable rational values with polynomial evaluation time, making bit complexity well-defined; retained the unrestricted signs and shape of the stated discrete utility definition.
  • Encoded the horizon m in unary so polynomial time in m is polynomial time in the input representation.
  • Allowed repeated signatures for distinct candidates and required safety on every supported arrival sequence.
  • Specified deterministic policies, arbitrary resolution of optimal-action ties, and algorithmic policy representation, counting preprocessing and execution time.

1. An exact dynamic program for arbitrary utilities

Let $\mathcal A=\operatorname{supp}(P)$, and write $p_a=P(a)$. Define
$$ U(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f(x_t). $$

Before an arrival, record:

  • $r$: the number of arrivals remaining;
  • $b$: the number of candidates still required;
  • $x_t$: the number of selected candidates approved by type $t$.

A feasible state satisfies $0\leq b\leq r$ and $0\leq x_t\leq k-b$. Let $V(r,b,x)$ be the maximum expected terminal utility of a safe continuation from this state.

The state is sufficient: past selections affect the objective only through $x$, and future signatures are independent of the complete history.

The boundary rules are
$$ V(r,0,x)=U(x) $$
and, when $b=r>0$,
$$ V(r,r,x)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}p_aV(r-1,r-1,x+a). $$
For $0<b<r$,
$$ V(r,b,x)= \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}p_a \max\left\{ V(r-1,b,x),\, V(r-1,b-1,x+a) \right\}. $$

These equations follow by induction on $r$. After observing $a$, rejection and acceptance lead to the two displayed successor states. Both actions are feasible exactly when $0<b<r$. The induction hypothesis identifies their optimal continuation values, so taking their maximum after observing $a$, and then averaging, gives the optimal value.

Choosing a maximizing action yields a deterministic policy. Rejecting when $b=0$ and accepting when $b=r$ preserves feasibility and guarantees exactly $k$ selections on every support sequence.

State count and exact arithmetic

There are at most
$$ (m+1)(k+1)^{\tau+1} $$
states, with at most $s$ transitions per state.

For bit complexity, let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all probabilities, and let $B$ be the product of the denominators of all masses and of $f(0),\ldots,f(k)$. Inductively, every value $V(r,b,x)$ has denominator dividing $BD^r$. Taking a maximum selects one of two rational numbers and introduces no new denominator.

Furthermore,
$$ |V(r,b,x)|\leq \max_{0\leq j\leq k}|f(j)|, $$
because terminal utilities are mass-weighted averages of these values, and continuation values are expectations of terminal utilities.

The input denominators contribute polynomially many bits. Exact polynomial-time computability of the fixed $f$ implies polynomial output length for all its values through $k$. Since $r\leq m$ and $m$ is unary encoded, all arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity.

Proved conclusion. For every fixed $\tau$, preprocessing and execution of an exact optimal safe policy take polynomial time.

2. Affine utilities are tractable for variable $\tau$

Suppose
$$ f(j)=\alpha j+\beta. $$
Define
$$ w(a)=\alpha\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t. $$
For every selected committee,
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f\left(\sum_{q\in W}a_t^q\right) = \beta+\sum_{q\in W}w(a^q). $$

Thus previously accumulated utility is an additive constant, and approval-count vectors are unnecessary. Let $G(r,b)$ be the optimal expected sum of values from the remaining selections. Then
$$ G(r,0)=0,\qquad G(r,r)=r\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}p_aw(a), $$
and, for $0<b<r$,
$$ G(r,b)= \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}p_a \max\left\{ G(r-1,b),\, w(a)+G(r-1,b-1) \right\}. $$

There are $O(mk)$ states and $O(mks)$ transitions. The exact-arithmetic argument from Section 1 applies. Negative slopes cause no difficulty because the boundary rule still forces acceptance when $b=r$.

3. A device for proving hardness of the first decision

All subsequent reductions use perfect three-dimensional matching. Its input consists of disjoint sets $X,Y,Z$, each of size $q$, and distinct triples $E\subseteq X\times Y\times Z$. The question is whether $q$ triples cover every element exactly once. This problem is NP-complete. [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We may impose any fixed lower bound on $q$: add fresh elements and their unique disjoint triples. Every perfect matching must contain those added triples, so this preserves the answer.

We may also require every element of $X$ to occur in a triple. A missing element immediately certifies a NO instance, which can be mapped to a fixed NO instance satisfying this restriction.

First-decision lemma

Suppose a polynomial-time construction supplies:

  • a committee size $k$;
  • a distinguished signature $a^\star$;
  • ordinary signatures, including a dummy signature;
  • positive rational numbers $g,\Omega$ and a rational benchmark $H$;

with the following properties.

  • Every size-$k$ committee containing $a^\star$ has utility at most $H$.
  • In every instance, some committee containing exactly one $a^\star$ has utility $H$.
  • In a YES instance, some committee omitting $a^\star$ has utility $H+g$.
  • In a NO instance, every committee omitting $a^\star$ has utility at most $H-g$.
  • All terminal utilities lie in an interval of length at most $\Omega$.

Assume all numbers and signatures have polynomial encoding length, and the target committees in properties 2 and 3 use only ordinary signatures besides the one specified $a^\star$.

Then exact optimal safe-policy computation is NP-hard.

Proof

Let $N$ be the number of ordinary signatures other than the dummy. Choose the least power of two $h$ with
$$ h\geq4N. $$
Give each of those $N$ signatures probability $1/h$.

Choose the least positive integer $R$ satisfying
$$ 2^R\geq \frac{8k\Omega}{g}, $$
and set
$$ B=hR,\qquad m=1+kB. $$
Choose the least integer $e$ with $2^e\geq8m$, and put
$$ P(a^\star)=\delta=2^{-e},\qquad P(a^{\mathrm{dummy}})=1-\frac Nh-\delta. $$

All probabilities are positive dyadic rationals. The dummy probability is at least $5/8$, so every ordinary signature has probability at least $1/h$. Because $\Omega$ and $g$ have polynomial encoding length, $R$ is polynomially bounded. Hence the unary horizon and the entire construction have polynomial size.

Condition on the first signature being $a^\star$. Partition the remaining $kB$ arrivals into $k$ blocks of length $B$.

For any ordinary target signature, its probability of being absent from an assigned block is at most
$$ (1-1/h)^{hR}\leq2^{-R}. $$
Indeed,
$$ (1-1/h)^{-h} = \left(1+\frac1{h-1}\right)^h \geq1+\frac h{h-1}>2. $$

Assign each still-required selection to a separate block and accept the first occurrence of its target there. The number of scheduled targets equals the number of selections still required: $k$ after rejecting the initial signature, and $k-1$ after accepting it.

Make this policy safe by accepting whenever the remaining arrivals equal the remaining selection requirement, and rejecting after filling the committee. On the event that all assigned targets occur, this override does not change the intended selections. At any unintended arrival, all still-required target positions lie later, so the number of arrivals remaining is strictly greater than the number of selections required.

The union bound gives failure probability at most
$$ k2^{-R}\leq \frac{g}{8\Omega}. $$
Consequently, a target committee of utility $J$ is attained by a safe policy with expected utility at least
$$ J-\frac g8. $$
This follows because both $J$ and every possible failure utility lie in an interval of length at most $\Omega$.

Let $A$ and $Q$ denote the optimal conditional values after accepting and rejecting the initial $a^\star$. Properties 1 and 2 give
$$ H-\frac g8\leq A\leq H. $$

In a YES instance, property 3 gives
$$ Q\geq H+g-\frac g8>H\geq A. $$
Thus every optimal policy rejects.

In a NO instance, another distinguished arrival occurs with probability at most
$$ (m-1)\delta\leq\frac18. $$
Without such an arrival, property 4 bounds every terminal utility by $H-g$. If another distinguished arrival occurs, every terminal utility is at most $H$, by properties 1 and 4. Therefore
$$ Q\leq H-g+\frac g8 =H-\frac{7g}{8} <H-\frac g8\leq A. $$
Thus every optimal policy accepts.

The initial distinguished signature has positive probability. A globally optimal policy must therefore take a conditionally optimal action there; replacing a strictly inferior action and its continuation would improve its unconditional expectation.

A single call to an exact optimal-policy algorithm, followed by inspecting its first decision on $a^\star$, decides perfect three-dimensional matching. The inequalities are strict, so tie-breaking is irrelevant. This proves the lemma.

4. Coverage hardness, with explicit parameter choices

Fix
$$ f(j)=\min\{1,j\}. $$
Take a three-dimensional matching instance with $q\geq2$ and every element of $X$ occurring in some triple.

Choose the least integer $d$ with
$$ 2^d\geq2(10q+1), $$
and write $u=2^{-d}$. Give each element of $X$ mass $2u$, each element of $Y\cup Z$ mass $4u$, one private type mass $u$, and one never-approving filler type mass
$$ 1-(10q+1)u. $$
All masses are positive dyadic rationals.

Each triple signature approves precisely its three element types. The distinguished signature approves every type in $Y\cup Z$ and the private type. The dummy approves no type.

Set
$$ k=q,\qquad H=(10q-1)u,\qquad g=u. $$

A committee containing the distinguished signature covers at most all of $Y\cup Z$, the private type, and $q-1$ elements of $X$. Its utility is therefore at most
$$ 8qu+u+2(q-1)u=H. $$
This bound is attained by choosing the distinguished signature and $q-1$ triples with distinct $X$-coordinates.

A perfect matching has utility
$$ 10qu=H+g. $$

If no perfect matching exists, a committee omitting the distinguished signature cannot cover all $3q$ elements. Otherwise, at most $q$ triple positions would cover all $3q$ elements, forcing exactly $q$ triples with no repeated incidence—a perfect matching. Thus some element of mass at least $2u$ is uncovered, giving utility at most
$$ 10qu-2u=H-g. $$

All utilities lie in $[0,1]$, so take $\Omega=1$. Section 3 supplies explicit polynomial-size choices of the distribution and horizon and proves NP-hardness.

Positive affine transformations preserve optimal policies. Hence the same result holds for
$$ f(j)=\beta+\alpha\min\{1,j\},\qquad \alpha>0. $$

5. New hardness result: strictly increasing concave utilities

Theorem. Suppose the fixed utility is strictly increasing and discretely concave, and satisfies
$$ f(1)-f(0)>f(2)-f(1). $$
Then exact optimal safe-policy computation is NP-hard when $\tau$ varies.

Here discrete concavity means that
$$ \Delta_j=f(j+1)-f(j) $$
is nonincreasing in $j$. Strict increase means $\Delta_j>0$ for every $j$.

5.1. Constructing a unique maximum at one approval

Set $k=q\geq2$, and define
$$ r_-=\frac{\Delta_{q-1}}{\Delta_0}, \qquad r_+=\frac{\Delta_{q-2}}{\Delta_1}. $$
Since $\Delta_{q-2}\geq\Delta_{q-1}>0$ and $\Delta_0>\Delta_1>0$,
$$ r_-<r_+. $$
Choose
$$ w=\frac{r_-+r_+}{2}, \qquad h(x)=wf(x)+f(q-x) \quad(0\leq x\leq q). $$

Its successive differences are
$$ h(x+1)-h(x)=w\Delta_x-\Delta_{q-x-1}. $$
They are nonincreasing: as $x$ increases, the first term does not increase, while the subtracted marginal does not decrease.

Moreover,
$$ h(1)-h(0)=w\Delta_0-\Delta_{q-1}>0, $$
and
$$ h(2)-h(1)=w\Delta_1-\Delta_{q-2}<0. $$
Thus $h$ has a unique maximum at $1$. Define
$$ \eta=\min\{h(1)-h(0),\,h(1)-h(2)\}>0. $$
The nonincreasing successive differences imply
$$ h(1)-h(x)\geq\eta \qquad\text{whenever }x\neq1. $$

5.2. Types and signatures

For each of the $3q$ elements, introduce two complementary types with unnormalized weights $w$ and $1$.

The first type approves exactly those signatures said to contain that element; the second approves every other signature. Consequently, a size-$q$ committee containing the element $x$ times contributes $h(x)$ from this pair.

Introduce a private type of weight
$$ p=\frac{\eta}{2\Delta_{q-1}}, $$
approving every signature except the distinguished signature.

The signatures are:

  • one signature for each triple, containing its three elements;
  • a distinguished signature containing all $3q$ elements;
  • a dummy containing none of the elements.

The complementary types and private type make these signatures distinct.

To obtain positive dyadic masses, choose a positive integer $D$ clearing the denominators of $w$ and $p$. The resulting weights $Dw,D,Dp$ are positive integers. Let $S$ be their total over all types, choose the least integer $\ell$ with $2^\ell\geq2S$, and multiply every weight by $u=2^{-\ell}$. Add a never-approving filler type of mass $1-uS$.

Write $\sigma=uD$.

5.3. Terminal-score separation

For a committee, let $z$ be its number of distinguished signatures and let $x_e$ be its number of selected signatures containing element $e$. Its utility is
$$ U=C+\sigma\sum_e\bigl(h(x_e)-h(1)\bigr) +\sigma p\bigl(f(q-z)-f(q)\bigr), $$
where the committee-independent constant is
$$ C=\sigma\bigl(3qh(1)+pf(q)\bigr)+(1-uS)f(0). $$
Set
$$ g=\frac{\sigma\eta}{2}, \qquad H=C-g. $$

Every element term is nonpositive. If $z\geq1$, strict increase gives
$$ f(q-z)-f(q)\leq-\Delta_{q-1}. $$
Therefore every committee containing the distinguished signature has utility at most
$$ C-\sigma p\Delta_{q-1}=H. $$
The distinguished signature together with $q-1$ dummies attains $H$: every element count equals $1$, and $z=1$.

A perfect matching has $z=0$ and every element count equal to $1$, so its utility is
$$ C=H+g. $$

In a NO instance, a committee omitting the distinguished signature must have some element count different from $1$. Indeed, if every count were $1$, the total $3q$ incidences would require exactly $q$ triples, with no repeated incidence. Therefore its utility is at most
$$ C-\sigma\eta=H-g. $$

These are precisely the terminal bounds required by Section 3.

5.4. Encoding and horizon bounds

All quantities are obtained from $f(0),\ldots,f(q)$ using polynomially many rational operations, and have polynomial bit length. Clearing denominators and dyadic normalization retain polynomial bit length.

Take
$$ \Omega=\max_{0\leq j\leq q}f(j)-\min_{0\leq j\leq q}f(j)>0. $$
Every terminal utility lies between these extrema, because the masses sum to one. Although $g$ may be very small, its encoding length is polynomial. Hence the least $R$ satisfying the inequality in Section 3 is polynomially bounded, and the unary horizon is polynomially bounded.

The first-decision lemma proves the theorem.

For example, this settles the harmonic utility
$$ f(0)=0,\qquad f(j)=\sum_{i=1}^j\frac1i, $$
whose marginals are $\Delta_j=1/(j+1)$.

6. New hardness result: every strictly concave quadratic

Theorem. For every fixed rational quadratic
$$ f(j)=\alpha j^2+\beta j+\gamma,\qquad \alpha<0, $$
exact optimal safe-policy computation is NP-hard when $\tau$ varies.

This theorem allows arbitrary signs and sizes of $\beta$ and $\gamma$.

6.1. Normalize the utility

Subtracting a constant and multiplying by a positive constant preserve all optimal policies. Clearing the fixed coefficient denominators therefore reduces the proof to
$$ \phi(j)=-aj^2+bj, $$
where $a$ is a positive integer and $b$ is an integer.

Choose a fixed integer $d\geq1$ with
$$ 2ad>b. $$
Pad the matching instance, if necessary, so that $q\geq2$ and
$$ 2a(q-1)>b. $$
Set
$$ k=q+d,\qquad M=2ak-2b,\qquad R_0=aM. $$

For $t\in\{d,d+1\}$, define
$$ W_t=2a(k-t)-b,\qquad V_t=2at-b. $$
All these weights are positive integers by the choices of $d$ and $q$.

For
$$ h_t(x)=W_t\phi(x)+V_t\phi(k-x), $$
direct expansion gives
$$ h_t(x)=h_t(t)-R_0(x-t)^2. $$
Indeed, $W_t+V_t=M$, so the quadratic coefficient is $-aM=-R_0$, and the linear coefficient is
$$ b(W_t-V_t)+2akV_t = 4a(ak-b)t = 2R_0t. $$

6.2. Weights, types, and signatures

Define
$$ \lambda=2ad+a-b>0, $$
$$ F=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}|\phi(j)-\phi(d)|, \qquad K=F+2\lambda+1. $$

Use the following positive integer weights:

  • a complementary baseline pair with weights $KW_d,KV_d$;
  • for each element, a complementary pair with weights $2\lambda W_{d+1},2\lambda V_{d+1}$;
  • a private type of weight $R_0$.

There are four kinds of signatures.

  • A baseline signature approves the first baseline type, every first element type, and the private type.
  • Each triple signature approves its three first element types.
  • The distinguished signature approves every first element type and the private type.
  • A dummy approves none of the first element types or the private type.

The first baseline type approves only the baseline signature. Each second type in a complementary pair approves precisely the signatures its first type does not approve.

These signatures are distinct. In particular, the baseline pair distinguishes the baseline signature from the distinguished signature.

Let $S$ be the sum of all integer weights. Choose the least $\ell$ with $2^\ell\geq2S$, put $u=2^{-\ell}$, and multiply all weights by $u$. Add a never-approving filler type of mass $1-uS$. All masses are positive dyadic rationals.

6.3. Exact terminal-utility identity

For a committee, let:

  • $z$ be the number of baseline signatures;
  • $h$ be the number of distinguished signatures;
  • $x_e$ be the number of selected triple signatures containing element $e$.

The first element-type count is $z+h+x_e$, and the private-type count is $z+h$.

Using the identity from Section 6.1, the utility is exactly
$$ U=C-uKR_0(z-d)^2 -2u\lambda R_0\sum_e\bigl(z+h+x_e-(d+1)\bigr)^2 +uR_0\bigl(\phi(z+h)-\phi(d)\bigr), $$
where
$$ C=u\left( Kh_d(d)+6q\lambda h_{d+1}(d+1)+R_0\phi(d) \right). $$
The filler contributes zero because $\phi(0)=0$.

Set
$$ g=u\lambda R_0,\qquad H=C-g. $$

6.4. Terminal-score bounds

Incorrect baseline count. If $z\neq d$, the baseline square costs at least $uKR_0$. The private term is at most $uR_0F$, and the element squares are nonpositive. Hence
$$ U-C\leq-uR_0(K-F) =-uR_0(2\lambda+1)<-2g. $$

A committee containing the distinguished signature. If $z=d$ and $h\geq1$, then
$$ \phi(d+h)-\phi(d) =-(2ad-b)h-ah^2 \leq-\lambda. $$
Thus $U\leq C-g=H$. If $z\neq d$, the preceding bound is stronger. Therefore every committee containing the distinguished signature has utility at most $H$.

This bound is attained by selecting $d$ baseline signatures, one distinguished signature, and $q-1$ dummies. Every element count is $d+1$, and the private difference is exactly $-\lambda$.

A YES instance. Select $d$ baseline signatures and the $q$ triples of a perfect matching. Then $z=d$, $h=0$, and every $x_e=1$. Every penalty vanishes, so the utility is
$$ C=H+g. $$

A NO instance without the distinguished signature. The case $z\neq d$ was already bounded by $C-2g$. If $z=d$ and $h=0$, there are $q$ remaining positions. Some $x_e$ differs from $1$; otherwise those positions contain a perfect matching. The corresponding integer square is at least $1$, yielding
$$ U\leq C-2g=H-g. $$

Again, all hypotheses of the first-decision lemma hold.

6.5. Polynomial construction size

The constants $a,b,d,\lambda$ depend only on the fixed utility. We have
$$ M=O(k),\qquad R_0=O(k),\qquad F=O(k^2),\qquad K=O(k^2), $$
and consequently $S=O(k^3)$. All masses and benchmark values have polynomial encoding length.

Take
$$ \Omega=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}\phi(j)-\min_{0\leq j\leq k}\phi(j)=O(k^2). $$
The least dyadic normalization satisfies $u>1/(4S)$, so $g$ is bounded below by an inverse polynomial in $k$. Therefore the block exponent in Section 3 is $O(\log k)$, and its unary horizon is polynomial in the matching input size.

This proves NP-hardness for $\phi$, and hence for the original quadratic $f$.

7. Other tractable restrictions and remaining open points

For completeness, every permitted fixed $f$ is also tractable when either $s$ or $k$ is fixed.

Index the signatures by $a^1,\ldots,a^s$, and record their selected multiplicities $c_1,\ldots,c_s$. These determine all approval counts:
$$ x_t=\sum_{i=1}^s c_i a_t^i. $$
Their total determines the remaining requirement:
$$ b=k-\sum_i c_i. $$

There are
$$ \binom{k+s}{s} $$
nonnegative multiplicity vectors with total at most $k$. Replacing $x$ by this vector in the dynamic program gives at most
$$ (m+1)\binom{k+s}{s} $$
states. This is polynomial for fixed $s$, and, by
$$ \binom{k+s}{s}=\binom{k+s}{k}, $$
also for fixed $k$. The bit-complexity proof from Section 1 applies unchanged.

The unresolved part is the unrestricted classification for each fixed utility. The proofs above do not settle, for example, $f(j)=j^2$, arbitrary oscillating utilities, or all concave utilities whose first strict decrease in marginal utility occurs later. No universal non-affine hardness claim is made.

  • Dynamic program for arbitrary utilities: verified.

The state $(r,b,x)$ contains all information needed for continuation optimization: $x$ determines the effect of prior selections, and independence makes future arrivals independent of the remaining history.

The boundary conditions correctly enforce safety:

  • When $b=0$, every subsequent candidate must be rejected.
  • When $b=r>0$, every subsequent candidate must be accepted.
  • When $0<b<r$, both actions admit safe continuations.

Both successor states satisfy the stated count bounds. In particular, acceptance changes the requirement to $b-1$, and
$$ x_t+a_t\leq k-b+1=k-(b-1). $$
