The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and additive goods utilities \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_+^m\), with one unit of each good per unit population, compute the unique optimal utility profile \(U\) and a supporting allocation \(z_t\) and price vector \(p\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t=\mathbf{1}\), \(U_t=u_t\cdot z_t\), \(\sum_a p_a=1\), and \(z_t\in\arg\max\{u_t\cdot y:p\cdot y\leq 1\}\); equivalently, solve \(\max\sum_t\mu_t\log(u_t\cdot z_t)\) over feasible allocations.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), with \(m\geq2\) divisible goods, one unit of each per unit population, and utilities \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^m\). Every utility vector is nonzero, and every good has positive utility for some supported type. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation specifies a common per capita bundle \(z_t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^m\) for each type, with \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=\mathbf1\). Define \(U_t=u_t\cdot z_t\). The computational problem is to compute exactly the unique utility profile maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t\), together with any supporting allocation and prices \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^m\) satisfying \(\sum_a p_a=1\) and \(z_t\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^m,\ p\cdot y\leq1}u_t\cdot y\). Ties in demand permit any maximizing bundle.

Is this exact computation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a finite list of agents with equal incomes, competitive utilities uniquely maximize the Nash product. [Orlin](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/DagSemProc.10171.2) gives an exact strongly polynomial algorithm for linear Fisher markets with arbitrary positive integer budgets; rational inputs follow by scaling. This general market result also covers the stated mass problem, so its polynomial-time complexity is already settled.

Result

The exact computation problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). The stated Fisher-market theorem applies using exactly \(\tau\) buyers. Below is the reduction and a direct proof that its output has the required properties.

1. Construct an integral Fisher market

Index the supported types by \(t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the masses, and set
\(b_t=D\mu_t\).
Then every \(b_t\) is a positive integer and
\(\sum_t b_t=D\).

For each type \(t\), let \(C_t\) be the product of the denominators of its utility coefficients, and define
\(v_{ta}=C_tu_{ta}\).
These coefficients are nonnegative integers. Multiplying a buyer’s utility by the positive constant \(C_t\) preserves all its maximizing bundles.

Construct a Fisher market with:

  • one buyer for each supported type \(t\), with budget \(b_t\) and utility coefficients \(v_{ta}\);
  • \(m\) goods, each with supply one.

Every buyer values some good positively, and every good is valued positively by some buyer. These are precisely the nondegeneracy assumptions in Orlin’s model. His exact strongly polynomial algorithm permits arbitrary positive integral budgets and nonnegative integral utility coefficients. [Orlin, *Improved Algorithms for Computing Fisher’s Market Clearing Prices*](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/16dagstuhl-seminar-proceedings/dsp-vol10171/DagSemProc.10171.2/DagSemProc.10171.2.pdf).

Apply this algorithm to obtain equilibrium prices \(q\) and quantity allocations \(x_t\), satisfying
\(\sum_t x_t=\mathbf1\),
where \(x_t\) maximizes \(v_t\cdot x\) subject to \(q\cdot x\leq b_t\).

2. Recover normalized prices and per capita bundles

First, every price \(q_a\) is positive. Otherwise, a buyer with \(v_{ta}>0\) could obtain arbitrarily large utility by adding unlimited quantities of the free good \(a\). Its demand problem would have no maximizing bundle, contradicting equilibrium.

Every buyer spends its entire budget. Indeed, if \(q\cdot x_t<b_t\), it can purchase an additional positive quantity of a good it values, strictly increasing its utility. Consequently,

\(q\cdot x_t=b_t\),

and market clearing implies

\(\sum_a q_a=\sum_t q\cdot x_t=\sum_t b_t=D\).

Define

\(p_a=q_a/D,\qquad z_t=x_t/\mu_t,\qquad U_t=u_t\cdot z_t.\)

Then \(p_a>0\), \(\sum_a p_a=1\), and

\(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=\sum_t x_t=\mathbf1.\)

To verify the required demand condition, fix \(t\). The correspondence \(x=\mu_ty\) is a bijection between nonnegative bundles, and

\(q\cdot x=D\mu_t(p\cdot y)=b_t(p\cdot y).\)

Thus \(q\cdot x\leq b_t\) holds exactly when \(p\cdot y\leq1\). Moreover,

\(v_t\cdot x=C_t\mu_t(u_t\cdot y).\)

The multiplier \(C_t\mu_t\) is positive. Therefore maximizing the Fisher buyer’s utility is equivalent to maximizing \(u_t\cdot y\) under the required unit budget. In particular,

\(z_t\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^m,\ p\cdot y\leq1}u_t\cdot y.\)

This argument allows all demand ties.

3. Prove optimality and uniqueness of the utility profile

Define the positive number

\(\beta_t=\max_a u_{ta}/p_a.\)

For every nonnegative bundle \(y\),

\(u_t\cdot y\leq\beta_t(p\cdot y).\)

If a good \(a\) attains the maximum defining \(\beta_t\), buying \(1/p_a\) units of that good achieves utility \(\beta_t\) at expenditure one. Hence the maximum utility under the unit budget is exactly \(\beta_t\). The demand condition just established therefore gives

\(U_t=\beta_t>0.\)

Now consider any feasible allocation \(w_t\), and write \(V_t=u_t\cdot w_t\). If some \(V_t=0\), its logarithmic objective, interpreted as \(-\infty\), is strictly below the finite objective of \(z\).

Otherwise all \(V_t>0\). The preceding utility bound gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_t\frac{V_t}{U_t}\leq\sum_t\mu_t(p\cdot w_t)=p\cdot\mathbf1=1.\)

For every \(s>0\), the inequality \(\log s\leq s-1\) holds, with equality exactly when \(s=1\). Applying it to each \(V_t/U_t\) yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_t\log V_t-\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t=\sum_t\mu_t\log\frac{V_t}{U_t}\leq\sum_t\mu_t\left(\frac{V_t}{U_t}-1\right)\leq0.\)

Thus the recovered allocation maximizes the stated objective.

If \(V_t\neq U_t\) for at least one type, the corresponding logarithm inequality is strict. Since every \(\mu_t>0\), the objective inequality is then strict as well. Consequently, every maximizing allocation has exactly the utility profile \(U\), proving uniqueness.

4. Exactness and binary complexity

The binary length of a product of positive integer denominators is at most the sum of their binary lengths plus a constant. Thus \(D\) and every \(C_t\) have bit length \(O(L)\). The integral Fisher instance has polynomial encoding length and exactly \(\tau\) buyers and \(m\) goods; the numerical value of \(D\) never determines the number of buyers.

Orlin’s exact strongly polynomial algorithm has a polynomial number of arithmetic operations and polynomially bounded intermediate encoding lengths, giving polynomial binary running time on this integral instance. Its prices and allocations are rational. If allocations are represented as expenditures \(f_{ta}\), recover quantities by \(x_{ta}=f_{ta}/q_a\), which is valid because every \(q_a>0\).

Finally, computing \(p_a=q_a/D\), \(z_t=x_t/\mu_t\), and \(U_t=\sum_a u_{ta}z_{ta}\) requires only polynomially many exact rational operations. These transformations preserve polynomial encoding length.

Therefore the algorithm outputs the unique maximizing utility profile, a supporting allocation, and normalized supporting prices exactly in polynomial time.
