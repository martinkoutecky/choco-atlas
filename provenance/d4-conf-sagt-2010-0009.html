The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two types with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), linear per-person utilities \(u_{ij}\), disagreement utilities \(c_i\), and one unit of each good per unit of population, find a feasible per-type allocation \(q_{ij}\) maximizing \(\sum_{i=1}^2\mu_i\log(\sum_j u_{ij}q_{ij}-c_i)\), subject to \(\sum_i\mu_i q_{ij}\le1\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=2\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) divisible goods, each with total supply one. Inputs are binary rational utilities \(u_{ij}\ge0\) and disagreement utilities \(c_i\ge0\); every type values some good positively, and every good is valued positively by some type. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation specifies a common per-person bundle \(q_i\) for each type, with \(q_{ij}\ge0\), \(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_iq_{ij}\le1\) for every good, and per-person utility \(v_i(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{ij}q_{ij}\).
Restrict to instances admitting an allocation with \(v_i(q)>c_i\) for both types. Compute any exact maximizing allocation for \(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_i\log(v_i(q)-c_i)\), over allocations with strictly positive gains; no tie-breaking among maximizing allocations is prescribed.

Is this exact two-type allocation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For two discrete players, nonsymmetric linear Arrow–Debreu Nash bargaining with positive integer clouts admits a combinatorial strongly polynomial algorithm; more generally, two-player bargaining with rational linear constraints admits an exact rational solution computable in polynomial time. [Vazirani](https://ics.uci.edu/~vazirani/LNB2.pdf)
No separate result for exactly the population-mass formulation above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified nonnegative binary rational utilities and disagreement utilities; rational utilities retain the continuous question's input convention, while nonnegativity follows the goods and bargaining setting.
  • Carried over the conditions that every type desires some good and every good is desired by some type.
  • Restored nonnegative allocation quantities and strictly positive utility gains, and restricted inputs to strictly feasible bargaining instances.
  • Interpreted each allocation quantity as a common per-person bundle within its type, with normalized population and total supply one of each good.
  • Specified exact allocation optimization rather than approximation or exact evaluation of the logarithmic objective; any maximizing allocation is acceptable.
  • Defined the number of goods and total binary input bit length explicitly.

Exact two-type allocation is polynomial-time solvable

Theorem. On the promised instances with \(\tau=2\), an exact rational maximizing allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\).

We prove this directly by enumerating at most \(2m+1\) rational candidates and checking their optimality using rational arithmetic.

1. Rescaling the population allocation

Define aggregate allocations, aggregate utilities, and aggregate disagreement utilities by

\(x_{ij}=\mu_iq_{ij},\qquad U_i(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{ij}x_{ij},\qquad d_i=\mu_ic_i.\)

Because each \(\mu_i>0\), this is an invertible transformation. The allocation constraints become

\(x_{ij}\ge0,\qquad x_{1j}+x_{2j}\le1\quad\text{for every }j.\)

Moreover,

\(v_i(q)-c_i=\dfrac{U_i(x)-d_i}{\mu_i}.\)

Consequently, the original objective equals

\(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_i\log(U_i(x)-d_i)-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_i\log\mu_i.\)

The second term is constant. Thus it suffices to maximize

\(F(x)=\mu_1\log(U_1(x)-d_1)+\mu_2\log(U_2(x)-d_2)\)

over aggregate allocations with \(U_i(x)>d_i\). The promise guarantees that such an allocation exists.

2. Existence and an exact optimality certificate

Existence

Let \(X\) be the aggregate allocation polytope, and define

\(K=\{x\in X:U_1(x)\ge d_1,\ U_2(x)\ge d_2\}.\)

The set \(X\) is compact: every coordinate lies in \([0,1]\), and its defining constraints are closed. Hence \(K\) is compact.

On \(K\), the function

\(H(x)=(U_1(x)-d_1)^{\mu_1}(U_2(x)-d_2)^{\mu_2}\)

is continuous, where a zero gain contributes a zero factor. By the promise, \(H\) is positive somewhere. Therefore its maximum exists and is positive. Every maximizer consequently has both gains strictly positive. Since \(F=\log H\) there, a maximizer of \(F\) exists.

Necessary and sufficient certificate

For any allocation \(x\) with positive gains, define

\(p_i=\dfrac{\mu_i}{U_i(x)-d_i}>0,\qquad M_j=\max\{p_1u_{1j},p_2u_{2j}\}.\)

The following conditions certify optimality:

  • \(x_{1j}+x_{2j}=1\) for every good \(j\).
  • Whenever \(x_{ij}>0\), one has \(p_iu_{ij}=M_j\).

We prove both necessity and sufficiency.

Necessity. Let \(x^*\) maximize \(F\), and compute \(p_i\) at \(x^*\). For any \(y\in X\), the allocation

\(x(s)=(1-s)x^*+sy\)

has positive gains for all sufficiently small nonnegative \(s\). Optimality therefore implies

\(\left.\dfrac{d}{ds}F(x(s))\right|_{s=0} =\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_i\bigl(U_i(y)-U_i(x^*)\bigr)\le0.\)

Thus \(x^*\) maximizes the linear functional

\(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_iu_{ij}x_{ij}\)

over \(X\). This maximization separates across goods. Every good is positively valued by some type, so \(M_j>0\). For each good, a linear maximizer must use its entire supply and assign positive quantities only to types attaining \(M_j\). These are precisely the certificate conditions.

Sufficiency. Suppose \(x\) satisfies the certificate. For every \(y\in X\),

\(\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_iU_i(y) \le\sum_{j=1}^{m}M_j =\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_iU_i(x).\)

If \(y\) also has positive gains, the tangent inequality for the logarithm gives

\(F(y)-F(x) \le\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_i\bigl(U_i(y)-U_i(x)\bigr) \le0.\)

Therefore \(x\) is a global maximizer.

All quantities in this certificate are rational when \(x\) is rational. Checking it requires no logarithm evaluations.

3. A maximizer with at most one shared good

Partition the goods into

\(I_1=\{j:u_{1j}>0,\ u_{2j}=0\},\qquad I_2=\{j:u_{1j}=0,\ u_{2j}>0\},\)

and

\(C=\{j:u_{1j}>0,\ u_{2j}>0\}.\)

These sets partition all goods by the assumption that every good is positively valued by some type.

Write \(C=\{j_1,\ldots,j_k\}\), ordered so that

\(\dfrac{u_{1j_1}}{u_{2j_1}}\ge\cdots\ge\dfrac{u_{1j_k}}{u_{2j_k}}.\)

Fix any order among equal ratios.

Let \(x^*\) be a maximizer and let \(p_i\) be its certificate coefficients. Goods in \(I_i\) go entirely to type \(i\). For a common good, the certificate implies:

  • If \(u_{1j}/u_{2j}>p_2/p_1\), it goes entirely to type \(1\).
  • If \(u_{1j}/u_{2j}<p_2/p_1\), it goes entirely to type \(2\).
  • Sharing is possible only when \(u_{1j}/u_{2j}=p_2/p_1\).

The goods with equality form a contiguous block in the sorted order. We can rearrange allocations within this block without changing either utility.

Indeed, let their common ratio be \(r\), and write \(z_j=x^*_{1j}\). Their total allocation to type \(1\), measured using type \(2\)'s utility coefficients, is

\(T=\sum_{\text{block }j}u_{2j}z_j.\)

Their contributions to the two utilities are respectively

\(rT\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{\text{block }j}u_{2j}-T.\)

Assign the block to type \(1\) in its fixed order until the quantity \(T\) is reached: fill successive goods completely, split the next good if necessary, and assign the remainder to type \(2\). Since every \(u_{2j}\) in the block is positive and

\(0\le T\le\sum_{\text{block }j}u_{2j},\)

this construction is possible and preserves both utilities.

Hence some maximizer has a prefix of common goods assigned to type \(1\), a suffix assigned to type \(2\), and at most one shared good.

4. Enumerating all necessary candidates

Throughout, assign goods in \(I_i\) entirely to type \(i\).

Candidates with no shared good

For every \(\ell\in\{0,\ldots,k\}\), include the allocation assigning the first \(\ell\) common goods to type \(1\) and all remaining common goods to type \(2\).

There are \(k+1\) such candidates.

Candidates with one shared good

For each \(h\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), consider allocations that assign:

  • \(j_1,\ldots,j_{h-1}\) entirely to type \(1\);
  • a fraction \(t\) of \(j_h\) to type \(1\), and \(1-t\) to type \(2\);
  • \(j_{h+1},\ldots,j_k\) entirely to type \(2\).

Set

\(a=u_{1j_h}>0,\qquad b=u_{2j_h}>0,\)

\(P_h=\sum_{j\in I_1}u_{1j}+\sum_{\ell<h}u_{1j_\ell}-d_1,\)

and

\(Q_h=\sum_{j\in I_2}u_{2j}+\sum_{\ell\ge h}u_{2j_\ell}-d_2.\)

The gains along this segment are

\(U_1-d_1=P_h+at,\qquad U_2-d_2=Q_h-bt.\)

Where both gains are positive, the derivative of the objective is

\(\dfrac{dF}{dt} =\dfrac{\mu_1a}{P_h+at}-\dfrac{\mu_2b}{Q_h-bt}.\)

An optimum with \(0<t<1\) must have zero derivative, because sufficiently small changes in either direction remain feasible and preserve positive gains. Clearing the positive denominators gives

\(\mu_1aQ_h-\mu_2bP_h-ab(\mu_1+\mu_2)t=0.\)

Using \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), the only possible interior optimum is

\(t_h=\dfrac{\mu_1Q_h}{b}-\dfrac{\mu_2P_h}{a}.\)

This is rational. Include this candidate whenever \(0<t_h<1\).

There are at most \(k\) additional candidates.

Algorithm and correctness

Enumerate these at most \(2k+1\) candidates. Discard any candidate whose gains are not both strictly positive. Check the optimality certificate from Section 2 on every remaining candidate, and return any candidate that passes.

The algorithm always finds one:

  • Section 3 provides a maximizing allocation in the enumerated prefix form.
  • If it has no shared good, it is one of the first \(k+1\) candidates.
  • If it has a genuinely shared good, its fraction must equal the corresponding \(t_h\), so it is among the additional candidates.
  • Every maximizing allocation passes the certificate.

Conversely, the certificate proves that every returned candidate is globally maximizing.

When \(k=0\), the enumeration contains just the allocation assigning each exclusive good to its valuing type. The same argument applies.

Finally, return the per-person allocation

\(q_{ij}=\dfrac{x_{ij}}{\mu_i}.\)

5. Polynomial bit complexity

Sorting the common goods takes \(O(m\log m)\) rational comparisons. Ratios can be compared by cross multiplication because their denominators are positive.

There are at most \(2m+1\) candidates. Computing utilities and checking the certificate takes \(O(m)\) rational operations per candidate. Thus the algorithm uses

\(O(m^2+m\log m)\)

rational arithmetic operations and comparisons.

All intermediate rational numbers have polynomial encoding length:

  • Each \(d_i\) is a product of two input rationals.
  • Each \(P_h\) and \(Q_h\) is a sum of at most \(m\) input utilities and one transformed disagreement utility.
  • Each \(t_h\) uses a constant number of products, quotients, and differences of these quantities.
  • Candidate utilities, certificate coefficients, and output coordinates likewise use sums and a constant number of arithmetic operations on such quantities.

For completeness, a sum of input rationals can be represented over the product of their denominators. The bit length of that denominator is at most the sum of their denominator bit lengths; the numerator also has polynomial bit length. The subsequent constant-depth operations preserve a polynomial bound. Standard exact rational arithmetic therefore gives polynomial total running time in \(m\) and \(L\), with \(\tau=2\).

6. Relation to the cited bargaining result

The rescaling also places the problem within two-player nonsymmetric linear bargaining. Its feasible utility set is a compact rational polytope. The transformed disagreement point belongs to that set: starting from a strictly feasible allocation, scale each type's aggregate bundle by \(d_i/U_i\), which lies in \([0,1)\), to obtain utilities exactly \(d_i\).

To obtain positive integer clouts, multiply both rational masses by a common denominator. This only scales the objective by a positive constant, and the clouts have polynomial binary encoding length. Thus the hypotheses of [Vazirani’s Theorem 13](https://ics.uci.edu/~vazirani/LNB2.pdf) are satisfied. The direct algorithm above additionally supplies an explicit allocation and bit-complexity proof for this population formulation.
