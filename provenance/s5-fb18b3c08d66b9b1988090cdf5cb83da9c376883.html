The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite agent types \(A\) and item types \(O\), masses \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), common weak preferences and priorities, and a mass matching \(p\) with row sums \(\alpha\) and column sums \(\beta\), decide whether \(p\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty)\), where \(\mathcal S_\infty\) consists of type-incidence limits of weakly stable perfect matchings in compatible \(H\)-copy markets as \(H\to\infty\); if yes, produce the finite lottery decomposition.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Each society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Let \(A,O\) be nonempty agent and item supports, \(\tau=|A|\), \(m=|O|\); these count supported types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Masses satisfy \(\alpha_a,\beta_o>0\) and \(\sum_a\alpha_a=\sum_o\beta_o=1\). Each type has a weak order on its acceptable opposite-side types, with at most three listed types and at most two indifference classes. Acceptability is mutual; unlisted pairs are forbidden.
Input \(p\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{A\times O}\) has row sums \(\alpha\), column sums \(\beta\), and zero entries on forbidden pairs.
For any positive integer \(H\) with all \(H\alpha_a,H\beta_o\) integral, the compatible copy market has exactly those numbers of copies. Copies inherit their type's orders and are indifferent among copies of the same opposite-side type; no ties are broken.
For a perfect matching \(M\) using acceptable pairs, define \(x^H_{ao}(M)\) as the number of matched pairs of types \((a,o)\), divided by \(H\). Use standard weak stability: a blocking pair requires strict improvement on both sides, including acceptable pairs whose entry in \(p\) is zero.
Let \(\mathcal S_\infty\) contain every coordinatewise limit \(\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{H_k}(M_k)\), where compatible \(H_k\to\infty\) and each \(M_k\) is weakly stable and perfect. A finite lottery decomposition means \(p=\sum_{j=1}^r\lambda_jq^j\), with \(q^j\in\mathcal S_\infty\), \(\lambda_j\ge0\), and \(\sum_j\lambda_j=1\).

Is testing \(p\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty)\), including constructing a finite lottery decomposition on yes instances, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the membership decision NP-hard?

For finite individual markets, ex-post weak-stability membership is NP-complete even with dichotomous, incomplete lists of length at most three. [Aziz, Biró, Csáji and Pourmiri](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00453-026-01388-2)
No result settling this exact copy-limit membership problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized both sides' positive rational masses to total one and required rational input matching entries.
  • Restored the anchor's incomplete, dichotomous lists of length at most three; length counts opposite-side types, since replication expands individual lists.
  • Made acceptability mutual, forbade assignments to unlisted pairs, and retained acceptable zero-probability pairs as potential blocking pairs.
  • Defined compatibility by exact integral copy counts, inherited preferences without tie-breaking, normalized incidence by H, and allowed limits along arbitrary compatible sequences.
  • Defined the requested lottery as a finite convex combination of limit incidence matrices, rather than of matchings from any fixed finite copy market.

Result

The membership decision is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when all supported types have the same mass. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm for membership and decomposition would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The key is an exact convex-hull identity for equal masses. This identity accounts for arbitrary copy multiplicities, unchanged ties, and limits.

1. Stability imposes a condition on the support of every limit

Let \(E\subseteq A\times O\) be the set of acceptable pairs. Write \(o\succ_a o'\) when agent type \(a\) strictly prefers \(o\) to \(o'\), and \(a\succ_o a'\) for the corresponding strict preference of item type \(o\).

Lemma 1. Every \(q\in\mathcal S_\infty\) satisfies the following condition: for every \((a,o)\in E\), there cannot exist types \(o',a'\) such that

\[ o\succ_a o',\qquad a\succ_o a',\qquad q_{ao'}>0,\qquad q_{a'o}>0. \]

Proof. By definition, there are compatible sizes \(H_k\to\infty\) and weakly stable perfect matchings \(M_k\) whose normalized type matrices converge to \(q\).

Suppose the displayed conditions hold. Coordinatewise convergence implies that, for all sufficiently large \(k\), both \(x^{H_k}_{ao'}(M_k)\) and \(x^{H_k}_{a'o}(M_k)\) are positive. Thus \(M_k\) contains an agent copy of type \(a\) matched to an item copy of type \(o'\), and an item copy of type \(o\) matched to an agent copy of type \(a'\).

These two copies strictly prefer one another to their current partners. They are not currently matched together, because \(o\succ_a o'\) implies \(o\ne o'\). Their pair is acceptable because \((a,o)\in E\). Hence they block \(M_k\), contradicting weak stability. \(\square\)

The condition applies to every acceptable pair, including pairs whose entry in the input \(p\) is zero.

2. Equal masses give an exact convex-hull identity

Consider a finite individual market with \(n\) agents and \(n\) items, mutual acceptability, and weak orders. Let \(\mathcal W\) denote its set of weakly stable perfect matchings, represented by their incidence matrices.

Make each individual a labeled type and assign every type mass \(1/n\).

Theorem 2. For this equal-mass society,

\[ \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty) = \left\{\frac1n R:R\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W)\right\}. \]

Proof.

First, let \(B\in\mathcal W\). At size \(H=kn\), there are exactly \(k\) copies of each type. For every edge in \(B\), match the copies of its endpoints bijectively. This produces a perfect matching with normalized matrix \(B/n\).

If two copies blocked this matching, their types would strictly prefer one another to their partners in \(B\). That would be a blocking pair in the original market. Thus every constructed matching is weakly stable. Taking \(k\to\infty\) proves

\[ \frac1n B\in\mathcal S_\infty. \]

Taking convex combinations gives one inclusion.

For the reverse inclusion, fix \(q\in\mathcal S_\infty\) and put \(R=nq\). Limits preserve the row sums, column sums, nonnegativity, and forbidden-pair zeros. Therefore \(R\) is a doubly stochastic matrix supported on acceptable pairs.

We use the support-preserving form of Birkhoff decomposition: every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of perfect-matching matrices using only its positive entries. Here is a proof of the required form.

For any set \(U\) of rows, let \(N(U)\) be its neighboring columns in the positive support. Then

\[ |U| = \sum_{a\in U}\sum_{o\in N(U)}R_{ao} \le \sum_a\sum_{o\in N(U)}R_{ao} = |N(U)|. \]

Hall’s marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching in the positive support. Subtract the minimum entry on that matching, multiplied by its incidence matrix. The residual matrix has a common row and column sum. If that sum is positive, the same Hall argument applies after dividing by it. Each subtraction eliminates at least one positive entry and introduces none. Hence the procedure terminates, expressing \(R\) as a convex combination of support-contained perfect matchings.

Every such perfect matching is weakly stable. Indeed, if one were blocked by an acceptable pair \((a,o)\), and its current partners were \(o'\) and \(a'\), then

\[ o\succ_a o',\qquad a\succ_o a',\qquad R_{ao'}>0,\qquad R_{a'o}>0. \]

Since \(R=nq\), this contradicts Lemma 1.

Consequently \(R\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W)\), so \(q\in\frac1n\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W)\). The latter set is convex, proving the reverse inclusion after taking convex hulls. \(\square\)

Thus copies may split across types, but this does not enlarge the relevant convex hull when masses are equal.

3. Polynomial reduction from finite ex-post stability

We use the following established result: deciding whether a rational random matching is a convex combination of weakly stable perfect matchings is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with incomplete, dichotomous preference and priority lists of length at most three. These restrictions hold simultaneously in [Aziz, Biró, Csáji and Pourmiri, Theorem 3.4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-026-01388-2#Sec5).

Let \(P\) be an instance of that finite problem, with \(n\) individuals on each side. Its row and column sums are one, and its positive entries use acceptable pairs.

For robustness to the interpretation of “binary rational,” pad the finite market to a size \(N\) that is a power of two: choose the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\ge n\). For each added agent, add one corresponding item; make these two individuals acceptable only to one another. Give their pair probability one, and give every other entry involving a new individual probability zero. Denote the resulting matrix by \(P^\ast\).

Every perfect matching of the padded market contains all added pairs. Those pairs cannot block or interact with the original market. Therefore

\[ P^\ast\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W^\ast) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W), \]

where \(\mathcal W^\ast\) is the set of weakly stable perfect matchings of the padded market.

Now construct the requested society by using these \(N\) individuals on each side as types and setting

\[ \alpha_a=\beta_o=\frac1N,\qquad p_{ao}=\frac{P^\ast_{ao}}N. \]

This construction satisfies every input requirement:

  • Every supported mass is positive, and the masses sum to one on each side.
  • The masses are dyadic rationals because \(N\) is a power of two.
  • The matrix \(p\) has the specified margins and vanishes on forbidden pairs.
  • Original lists retain their orders; added lists have one entry. Thus every list has at most three entries and at most two indifference classes.
  • Since \(N<2n\), padding and scaling increase the encoding length only polynomially.

By Theorem 2,

\[ p\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P^\ast\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W^\ast) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal W). \]

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Consequence for constructing lotteries

The reduction also respects finite decompositions. A finite lottery over stable perfect matchings of the padded market becomes a valid copy-limit lottery by replacing each matching matrix \(B\) with \(B/N\).

Conversely, any finite copy-limit lottery for the constructed society can be converted into a lottery over stable perfect matchings: multiply each component by \(N\), apply the support-preserving decomposition from Theorem 2, and combine the resulting weights.

The requested decision-and-construction task therefore contains the finite ex-post stability problem. Its membership decision is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under exactly the stated list restrictions.
