The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(l\) lanes with initial queue masses \(Q_j\) and \(\tau\) ordered cohorts with rational masses \(\mu_t\), values of time \(\theta_t\), and accessible lane sets \(A_t\), compute a feasible routing \(x_{tj}\) minimizing \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\theta_t\sum_{j\in A_t}\left[x_{tj}\left(Q_j+\sum_{s<t}x_{sj}\right)+\frac{x_{tj}^{2}}{2}\right]\), subject to \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), \(x_{tj}=0\) for \(j\notin A_t\), and \(\sum_{j\in A_t}x_{tj}=\mu_t\). What is the exact computational complexity in \(l\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(l\geq1\) parallel queues with initial masses \(Q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\), each serving one unit of mass per unit of time. A society is given by its support: an ordered list of \(\tau\geq1\) positive-mass cohort types, each with binary-rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), value of time \(\theta_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), and a nonempty accessible set \(A_t\subseteq\{1,\ldots,l\}\). The arrival index is part of the type; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Total mass is arbitrary, without normalization.
Cohorts arrive in the given order, with no restriction on the order of their values of time. Mass may split among accessible queues; within each queue, all earlier cohorts precede later cohorts, and mass cannot change queues after joining.
A feasible routing has \(x_{tj}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}\), \(x_{tj}=0\) for \(j\notin A_t\), and \(\sum_{j\in A_t}x_{tj}=\mu_t\). Its total cost is \(C(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\theta_t\sum_{j\in A_t}\left[x_{tj}\left(Q_j+\sum_{s<t}x_{sj}\right)+\frac{x_{tj}^{2}}{2}\right]\).
The optimization problem is to compute a routing minimizing \(C\); its decision version additionally receives a binary-rational threshold \(B\) and asks whether a feasible routing satisfies \(C(x)\leq B\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including the accessible sets and threshold.

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard when \(l\) is part of the input, and what is the complexity of computing an exact optimal routing, with running times stated in \(l\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) (the candidate-count parameter \(m\) is inapplicable)?

For discrete ordered unit agents with unrestricted lane access, the vertical-queue recursion has a stated bound \(O(n(n+l)^l)\) for a fixed coalition partition, including the grand coalition; this is an XP bound in \(l\), polynomial for fixed \(l\). Result

The decision problem is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete when \(l\) is part of the input. This holds even when all initial queues are empty, all masses are integers, every accessible set has size at most three, and values of time belong to \(\{1,2,3\}\).

Computing an exact optimal routing is consequently \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Every instance nevertheless has a rational optimal routing of polynomial encoding length. Writing \(N=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}|A_t|\), an exact deterministic algorithm runs in

\(\displaystyle \left[\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}(2^{|A_t|}-1)\right]\operatorname{poly}(N,L)\ \leq\ 2^{l\tau}\operatorname{poly}(l,\tau,L).\)

Exact optimization also belongs to \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\). Thus it is polynomial-time computable if and only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proofs follow.

1. Polynomial-size rational optimal routings

Retain only the \(N\) accessible coordinates. Let \(E\) be the cohort incidence matrix, so feasibility means \(Ex=\mu\) and \(x\geq0\). The objective is

\(\displaystyle C(x)=\frac12x^{\mathsf T}Hx+c^{\mathsf T}x,\)

where \(c_{tj}=\theta_tQ_j\), and

\(\displaystyle H_{(t,j),(s,k)}= \begin{cases} \theta_{\max(t,s)},&j=k,\\ 0,&j\neq k. \end{cases}\)

These identities follow by expanding the given objective: the coefficient of \(x_{tj}x_{sj}\), for \(s<t\), is \(\theta_t\).

The feasible set is nonempty because every \(A_t\) is nonempty. It is compact because \(0\leq x_{tj}\leq\mu_t\). Hence an optimum exists.

Choose an optimum \(x^*\) with the smallest possible number of positive coordinates. Let \(S\) be its positive support, and write \(H_S,E_S,c_S\) for the corresponding restrictions. Each cohort has a positive coordinate, so \(E_S\) has full row rank.

For every \(d\) satisfying \(E_Sd=0\), both sufficiently small positive and negative perturbations \(x^*_S+hd\) remain feasible. Therefore

\(\displaystyle d^{\mathsf T}(H_Sx^*_S+c_S)=0,\qquad d^{\mathsf T}H_Sd\geq0.\)

In fact, \(d^{\mathsf T}H_Sd>0\) for every nonzero such \(d\). Otherwise the quadratic expansion would give

\(\displaystyle C(x^*_S+hd)=C(x^*_S)\)

for every \(h\). A nonzero vector with \(E_Sd=0\) has a negative coordinate. Increasing \(h\) until the first coordinate becomes zero would produce another optimum with fewer positive coordinates, contradicting the choice of \(x^*\).

The first-order condition consequently gives a multiplier \(\lambda\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} H_S&E_S^{\mathsf T}\\ E_S&0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x^*_S\\ \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -c_S\\ \mu \end{pmatrix}. \)

This matrix is nonsingular. Indeed, a vector \((d,\eta)\) in its kernel satisfies \(E_Sd=0\) and \(H_Sd+E_S^{\mathsf T}\eta=0\). Multiplying the latter equation by \(d^{\mathsf T}\) gives \(d^{\mathsf T}H_Sd=0\), hence \(d=0\). Full row rank of \(E_S\) then gives \(\eta=0\).

Thus \(x^*_S\) solves a nonsingular rational linear system. Clearing input denominators produces an integer system of dimension at most \(N+\tau\leq2N\), with coefficients of \(O(L)\) bits. Cramer’s rule and the determinant bound show that each coordinate has \(O(N(L+\log(N+1)))\) bits. In particular, the total encoding length is polynomial.

This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\): a yes-instance has a polynomial-size rational optimal routing as a certificate, and feasibility and \(C(x)\leq B\) can be checked by exact rational arithmetic.

2. Strong hardness reduction

We reduce from \(3\)-SAT. Let the formula have \(n\geq1\) variables and \(k\geq1\) nonempty clauses, each containing at most three literals.

Choose the smallest power of two \(D\) satisfying \(D>2k^2\). Thus \(D\leq4k^2\).

For each variable \(i\), introduce two queues \(i^+\) and \(i^-\), representing its positive and negative literals. All initial masses are zero. Introduce three consecutive blocks of cohorts:

| Block | Cohorts | Mass | Value of time | Accessible queues |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Variable | One for each variable \(i\) | \(D\) | \(1\) | \(\{i^+,i^-\}\) |
| Clause | One for each clause | \(1\) | \(3\) | Its literal queues |
| Balancing | One for each variable \(i\) | \(2D\) | \(2\) | \(\{i^+,i^-\}\) |

The order within each block is arbitrary but fixed. Thus \(l=2n\) and \(\tau=2n+k\).

Let \(r_{i^+},r_{i^-}\) denote the variable cohort’s allocations, so

\(\displaystyle r_{i^+}+r_{i^-}=D.\)

Let \(y_j\) be the total clause mass routed to queue \(j\), and put \(u_i=y_{i^+}+y_{i^-}\). Then

\(\displaystyle \sum_jy_j=\sum_iu_i=k,\qquad 0\leq u_i\leq k<D.\)

Eliminating the balancing cohorts

Immediately before balancing, the two loads for variable \(i\) are \(a_i=r_{i^+}+y_{i^+}\) and \(b_i=r_{i^-}+y_{i^-}\).

If its balancing cohort puts \(z_+\) and \(z_-\) into those queues, its cost is

\(\displaystyle 2\left(a_iz_++b_iz_-+\frac{z_+^2+z_-^2}{2}\right) =(a_i+z_+)^2+(b_i+z_-)^2-a_i^2-b_i^2.\)

The final loads have sum \(3D+u_i\). Their squared sum is minimized by making both equal to

\(\displaystyle h_i=\frac{3D+u_i}{2}.\)

This equalization is feasible for every preceding routing: each preceding load is at most \(D+u_i\), and

\(\displaystyle D+u_i\leq\frac{3D+u_i}{2}\)

because \(u_i\leq D\).

The minimum balancing cost for variable \(i\) is therefore

\(\displaystyle \frac{(3D+u_i)^2}{2}-(r_{i^+}+y_{i^+})^2-(r_{i^-}+y_{i^-})^2.\)

The variable block costs \(\frac12\sum_jr_j^2\). Because every clause cohort has value of time \(3\), its block telescopes queue by queue and costs

\(\displaystyle \frac32\sum_j\bigl((r_j+y_j)^2-r_j^2\bigr).\)

Consequently, after optimizing the balancing block, the total cost is exactly

\[\displaystyle F(r,y)= -\frac12\sum_jr_j^2+\sum_jr_jy_j +\frac12\sum_jy_j^2 +\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}(3D+u_i)^2. \tag{1}\]

Binary variable choices suffice

Fix the complete clause routing, hence fix \(y\). Write \(r_{i^+}=p_i\) and \(r_{i^-}=D-p_i\), with \(0\leq p_i\leq D\).

The terms of \(F\) depending on \(p_i\) are

\(\displaystyle -\frac12\bigl(p_i^2+(D-p_i)^2\bigr) +p_iy_{i^+}+(D-p_i)y_{i^-}. \)

Their second derivative is \(-2\), so this function is strictly concave. Its minimum over \([0,D]\) occurs at an endpoint.

Thus any feasible routing can be transformed, without increasing cost, into one with

\(\displaystyle (r_{i^+},r_{i^-})\in\{(D,0),(0,D)\}\)

for every variable. The clause routing remains feasible, and the balancing block can still be equalized.

Interpret these binary choices by declaring the empty literal queue to represent a true literal:

  • \(r_{i^+}=0,\ r_{i^-}=D\) means variable \(i\) is true.
  • \(r_{i^+}=D,\ r_{i^-}=0\) means variable \(i\) is false.

At binary choices, \(\sum_jr_j^2=nD^2\). Expanding (1) gives

\[\displaystyle F(r,y)=K+\sum_jr_jy_j+\frac12\sum_jy_j^2+\frac12\sum_iu_i^2,\qquad K=4nD^2+3Dk. \tag{2}\]

Set the decision threshold to \(B=K+D/2\).

Satisfiable formulas give yes-instances

For a satisfying assignment, use its binary variable routing. Route each clause’s unit mass entirely to a queue representing one of its true literals. Those queues have \(r_j=0\), so \(\sum_jr_jy_j=0\).

Nonnegativity and \(\sum_jy_j=\sum_iu_i=k\) imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_jy_j^2\leq k^2,\qquad \sum_iu_i^2\leq k^2.\)

Equation (2) yields

\(\displaystyle C\leq K+k^2<K+\frac D2=B.\)

Unsatisfiable formulas give no-instances

Consider any binary variable routing. Its assignment leaves some clause unsatisfied. Every accessible queue of that clause consequently has \(r_j=D\). Regardless of how the clause splits its unit mass, it contributes \(D\) to \(\sum_jr_jy_j\).

All remaining terms beyond \(K\) in (2) are nonnegative. Thus every binary variable routing has cost at least \(K+D>B\).

The preceding endpoint transformation converts any routing to a binary variable routing of no greater cost. Therefore every routing in an unsatisfiable instance also has cost at least \(K+D\).

This proves the reduction.

All numerical inputs are integers: masses are \(1,D,2D\), values of time are \(1,2,3\), and \(B\) is an integer. Their magnitudes are polynomial in \(n+k\), because \(D\leq4k^2\). The reduction therefore proves strong \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Together with Section 1, the decision problem is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

3. An exact deterministic algorithm

Enumerate every tuple of nonempty subsets \(S_t\subseteq A_t\). For each tuple:

  • Set all unselected coordinates to zero.
  • Form the stationarity system from Section 1 on the selected coordinates.
  • If its matrix is nonsingular, solve it exactly.
  • Retain the solution if its routing coordinates are nonnegative, and evaluate its cost.

Return the retained routing with minimum cost.

Every retained routing is feasible. Section 1 proves that an optimum with minimum positive support has a nonsingular stationarity system, so an optimal routing necessarily appears. Singular systems can therefore be skipped.

There are exactly \(\prod_t(2^{|A_t|}-1)\) support tuples. Each system has dimension at most \(2N\), and exact linear algebra and cost comparisons take polynomial bit time. Hence the running time is

\(\displaystyle \left[\prod_t(2^{|A_t|}-1)\right]\operatorname{poly}(N,L) \leq2^{l\tau}\operatorname{poly}(l,\tau,L).\)

An exact optimal-routing algorithm running in polynomial time would decide the strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete decision problem by evaluating its output. Thus exact optimization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

4. Polynomial time with an NP oracle

For completeness, exact optimization belongs to \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\), even if thresholds are required to be dyadic.

The determinant argument gives a computable bound \(V=2^{\operatorname{poly}(N,L)}\) on the denominator of an optimal value. The same bound applies uniformly to all instances obtained by deleting accessible coordinates.

Two distinct rational values with denominators at most \(V\) differ by at least \(1/V^2\).

Let \(C^*\) be the original optimum. A computable upper bound is

\(\displaystyle C^*\leq\theta_{\max}M(Q_{\max}+M),\qquad M=\sum_t\mu_t.\)

Using the decision oracle, dyadic binary search produces an upper endpoint \(\beta\) with

\(\displaystyle C^*\leq\beta,\qquad \beta-C^*<1/V^2\)

after polynomially many queries.

Now consider deleting an accessible coordinate, while keeping every accessible set nonempty. The restricted optimum is at least \(C^*\). By the denominator separation bound, the oracle answers yes at threshold \(\beta\) exactly when the restriction preserves \(C^*\).

Try each coordinate once, retaining every deletion that preserves the optimum. A failed deletion remains impossible after further restrictions. At termination, every remaining coordinate is positive in every optimum: a zero coordinate would permit another optimum-preserving deletion.

The boundary argument in Section 1 now shows that the stationarity matrix on the remaining coordinates is nonsingular. Solve that single rational system to obtain an exact optimum. The search uses polynomially many oracle queries and polynomial additional bit time.

5. Relation to the discrete recursion

The cited paper states the discrete vertical-queue bound \(O(n(n+l)^l)\) in Proposition 1. It is polynomial for fixed \(l\), rather than uniformly polynomial when \(l\) varies. [Lloret-Batlle and Jayakrishnan](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3zr5d4d5/qt3zr5d4d5.pdf)

The reduction above directly handles the continuous model and explicitly accounts for fractional routing. It uses restricted accessible sets and growing \(l\). The exact upper bound proved here is exponential in \(\tau\) even for fixed \(l\); it does not establish an XP bound in \(l\) for the continuous problem.
