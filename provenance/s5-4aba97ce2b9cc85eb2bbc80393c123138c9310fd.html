The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\), community label \(c(t)\), and original assignment marginals \(p_{ts}\), along with two school capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), determine the complexity of finding a lottery over feasible mass assignments \(x_{ts}\). Each realization must satisfy \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}=q_s\), and the lottery must satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[x_{ts}]=\mu_t p_{ts}\). Its objective is to maximize \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{c,s}X_{cs}^{2}]\), where \(X_{cs}=\sum_{t:c(t)=c}x_{ts}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a community label \(c(t)\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), with every community nonempty.
There are \(m=2\) schools, both acceptable to all types, with positive rational capacities \(q_1+q_2=1\). Given rational marginals \(p_{ts}\in[0,1]\), require \(\sum_s p_{ts}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{ts}=q_s\).
A feasible realization is a divisible mass assignment \(x_{ts}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}=q_s\). A lottery is a probability distribution over feasible realizations satisfying \(\mathbb E[x_{ts}]=\mu_t p_{ts}\).
Define \(X_{cs}=\sum_{t:c(t)=c}x_{ts}\) and expected cohesion \(F=\mathbb E[\sum_{c=1}^k\sum_{s=1}^2 X_{cs}^{2}]\). Given a binary-rational threshold \(b\), ask whether some such lottery has \(F\geq b\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete maximum-cohesion correlated-lottery implementation is NP-hard even with two schools and admits no FPTAS unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). [Ashlagi–Shi](https://web.stanford.edu/~iashlagi/papers/correlated-lottery.pdf)
The same source explicitly claims that hardness persists with finitely many communities and infinitely many students per community; its exact continuum formulas instead concern a continuum of communities with homogeneous school cutoffs within each community. A separate complexity theorem for the precise rational-support formulation above was not verified. [Ashlagi–Shi](https://web.stanford.edu/~iashlagi/papers/correlated-lottery.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, consistently with capacities summing to one.
  • Specified nonnegative divisible realization masses, rational marginals in [0,1], row normalization, and aggregate capacity consistency.
  • Required positive school capacities and nonempty communities, excluding vacuous school and community entries.
  • Kept arbitrary consistent marginals as stated in the graded question; no additional preference or priority restrictions are imposed.
  • Formalized exact optimization through its rational-threshold decision problem and included the threshold in the total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when:

  • \(m=2\) and \(q_1=q_2=\tfrac12\);
  • every type has marginals \(p_{t1}=p_{t2}=\tfrac12\);
  • every community contains exactly one supported type, so \(k=\tau\);
  • all masses and the threshold are dyadic rationals.

Here \(k\) grows with the input. The proof below concerns the stated divisible-mass model directly.

1. A universal upper bound and its equality condition

For each community, define its total mass by
\[ \]
\(M_c=\sum_{t:c(t)=c}\mu_t.\)

In every feasible realization, \(X_{c1}+X_{c2}=M_c\). Therefore
\[ \]
\(X_{c1}^2+X_{c2}^2=M_c^2-2X_{c1}X_{c2}\leq M_c^2.\)

Consequently, every lottery satisfies
\[ \]
\(F\leq U:=\sum_{c=1}^k M_c^2.\)

Moreover,
\[ \]
\(U-F=2\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{c=1}^k X_{c1}X_{c2}\right].\)

The random variable inside this expectation is nonnegative. Its expectation is zero if and only if it is zero almost surely. Since its summands are nonnegative, equality \(F=U\) holds if and only if, almost surely, every community satisfies \(X_{c1}X_{c2}=0\).

Thus attaining \(U\) forces every community to be assigned entirely to one school in almost every realization, despite the permission to divide mass.

When each community consists of one type, this condition becomes \(x_{t1}\in\{0,\mu_t\}\) for every type.

2. Polynomial reduction from equal partition

Use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\): given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), determine whether some subset has half their total sum.

We may assume the total is even, say
\[ \]
\(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i=2B.\)

Indeed, doubling every integer preserves the existence of an equal partition and ensures an even total.

Dyadic normalization

Choose a power of two \(M\) satisfying
\[ \]
\(2B<M\leq4B,\)

and set \(D=M-B\). Introduce the following \(n+2\) positive integer weights:
\[ \]
\(w_1=a_1,\ldots,w_n=a_n,\qquad w_{n+1}=w_{n+2}=D.\)

Their total is \(2B+2D=2M\), a power of two. Define the society by
\[ \]
\(\tau=k=n+2,\qquad c(t)=t,\qquad \mu_t=\frac{w_t}{2M}.\)

These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. Set
\[ \]
\(q_1=q_2=\frac12,\qquad p_{t1}=p_{t2}=\frac12,\qquad b=\sum_{t=1}^{n+2}\mu_t^2.\)

The marginal constraints hold because \(\sum_t\mu_t p_{ts}=\tfrac12=q_s\). The threshold is also dyadic: explicitly,
\[ \]
\(b=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2+2D^2}{4M^2}.\)

If the source instance has bit length \(\ell\), then \(M\), \(D\), and this numerator have \(O(\ell)\) bits. The output has \(L=O(n\ell)\) bits and is constructed in polynomial time.

The padding preserves equal partition

We claim that the padded weights have a subset summing to \(M\) if and only if the original weights have a subset summing to \(B\).

Since \(M>2B\), we have \(D=M-B>M/2\). A subset summing to \(M\):

  • cannot contain both padding weights, because \(2D>M\);
  • cannot contain neither padding weight, because all original weights together sum to \(2B<M\).

It therefore contains exactly one padding weight. Its original weights must sum to \(M-D=B\).

Conversely, an original subset summing to \(B\), together with one padding weight, sums to \(M\). This proves the claim.

3. Correctness of the lottery reduction

A partition produces a qualifying lottery

Suppose a padded subset \(S\) has weight \(M\). Its mass is therefore \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=\tfrac12\).

Construct a realization assigning all types in \(S\) entirely to school one and all remaining types entirely to school two. Both capacities are satisfied. Swapping the schools gives another feasible realization.

Give these two realizations probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Every type then satisfies
\[ \]
\(\mathbb E[x_{t1}]=\mathbb E[x_{t2}]=\frac{\mu_t}{2}=\mu_t p_{ts}.\)

Every community remains intact in both realizations. Their cohesion, and hence the lottery’s expected cohesion, equals \(b=\sum_t\mu_t^2\).

A qualifying lottery produces a partition

Conversely, suppose a feasible lottery satisfies \(F\geq b\).

Here each community has mass \(\mu_t\), so Section 1 gives \(F\leq\sum_t\mu_t^2=b\). Thus \(F=b\), and almost every realization assigns every type entirely to one school.

Choose such a realization. The types assigned to school one have total mass \(\tfrac12\), so their padded weights sum to \(M\). By the padding claim, the original instance has a subset summing to \(B\).

We have therefore proved the equivalence
\[ \]
\(\text{original instance has an equal partition}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{constructed society admits a lottery with }F\geq b.\)

This establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the precise rational-support formulation.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

We now show that arbitrary probability distributions do not prevent polynomial certificates.

The realization polytope

For two schools, write \(y_t=x_{t1}\); then \(x_{t2}=\mu_t-y_t\). Feasible realizations correspond exactly to the nonempty compact polytope
\[ \]
\(P=\left\{y\in\mathbb R^\tau:0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t,\ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}y_t=q_1\right\}.\)

It is nonempty because the prescribed mean \(h_t=\mu_t p_{t1}\) belongs to \(P\).

The cohesion of a realization is
\[ \]
\(g(y)=\sum_{c=1}^k\left[\left(\sum_{t:c(t)=c}y_t\right)^2+\left(M_c-\sum_{t:c(t)=c}y_t\right)^2\right].\)

This is continuous and convex: each summand is a square of an affine function.

A point of \(P\) is a vertex if and only if at most one coordinate lies strictly between its bounds. For necessity, two strictly interior coordinates can be perturbed by equal and opposite sufficiently small amounts, producing a nontrivial feasible segment. For sufficiency, all coordinates at bounds must remain fixed in any feasible segment through the point, and the sum constraint fixes the remaining coordinate.

Thus every vertex is rational with encoding length polynomial in \(L\): its coordinates are bounds, except possibly for one coordinate of the form \(q_1-\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\).

Vertex lotteries suffice

Consider any feasible lottery, with mean \(h\) and expected cohesion \(F\). The graph
\[ \]
\(H=\{(y,g(y)):y\in P\}\subseteq\mathbb R^{\tau+1}\)

is compact. By Carathéodory’s theorem its convex hull is compact, and the expectation \((h,F)\) belongs to that convex hull. Indeed, otherwise a strictly separating linear functional would contradict the definition of expectation.

Carathéodory’s theorem therefore represents \((h,F)\) using at most \(\tau+2\) points of \(H\). This gives a finite lottery with the same mean and expected cohesion.

Every point of \(P\) is a convex combination of its vertices. Replacing each realization of this finite lottery by such a combination preserves the mean. Convexity gives
\[ \]
\(g\!\left(\sum_j\alpha_jv^j\right)\leq\sum_j\alpha_jg(v^j),\)

so the replacement weakly increases expected cohesion. Hence a qualifying lottery exists if and only if a qualifying lottery supported on vertices exists.

Polynomial certificates

Let \(V\) denote the finite vertex set. The maximum attainable cohesion is the optimum of the rational linear program
\[ \]
\(\max\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v g(v)\)

subject to
\[ \]
\(\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v=1,\qquad \sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v v=h,\qquad \lambda_v\geq0.\)

Its feasible region is nonempty and compact, so an optimum exists at an extreme point. There are \(\tau+1\) equality rows, including normalization. Every extreme point therefore has at most \(\tau+1\) positive probabilities: otherwise the corresponding columns would be linearly dependent, allowing a nonzero feasible perturbation in both directions.

All coefficients have polynomial bit length. For an extreme point, the positive probabilities are determined by an invertible subsystem of at most \(\tau+1\) equations. Cramer’s rule gives polynomial bounds on their bit lengths.

A yes certificate can consequently list at most \(\tau+1\) rational realizations and their rational probabilities. Exact rational arithmetic verifies feasibility, marginals, normalization, and expected cohesion at least \(b\), in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L)\), with \(m=2\). Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

5. Running time and scope

Each vertex can be generated by choosing its potentially interior coordinate and choosing lower or upper bounds for all other coordinates. Consequently,
\[ \]
\(|V|\leq\tau\,2^{\tau-1}.\)

Enumerating these candidates and solving the resulting rational linear program gives an exact deterministic algorithm with running time
\[ \]
\(2^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L),\qquad m=2.\)

Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness and excludes a general \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L)\)-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The reduction supplies a direct proof for finite supported types with divisible masses. It does not rely on transferring the continuum-of-communities formulas in Section 4 of [Ashlagi–Shi](https://web.stanford.edu/~iashlagi/papers/correlated-lottery.pdf) to this model. It also makes no claim of hardness for a fixed constant \(k\), strong hardness, or absence of an FPTAS.
