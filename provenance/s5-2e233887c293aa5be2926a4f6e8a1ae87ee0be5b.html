The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite outcome set \(O\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the honest team’s ranking types with total mass \(1-\rho\), a coalition mass \(\rho>0\), the other party’s ranking \(p_o\), and a target outcome \(p\), does there exist a rational distribution \(x\) over rankings with total mass \(\rho\) such that \(p\) is the VAOV SPE result for both possible initiators? The team order is obtained by Borda-scoring the honest mass and coalition reports together, with the paper’s lexicographic tie-break.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(O\) be a labeled outcome set with \(m\ge 2\), and let \(\mathcal R(O)\) be its strict total rankings. Input consists of an honest mass distribution \(\mu\), a binary rational \(0<\rho\le 1\) with \(\sum_r\mu(r)=1-\rho\), the other party’s strict ranking \(p_o\), and a target \(p\in O\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\), not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
The coalition may choose any \(x:\mathcal R(O)\to\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) with \(\sum_r x(r)=\rho\), without a prescribed support bound. The team ranking \(p_t(x)\) orders outcomes by decreasing mass-weighted Borda score under \(\mu+x\), breaking equal scores by a fixed public lexicographic order of outcome labels.
In Voting by Alternating Offers and Vetoes (VAOV), the initiator offers an available outcome; acceptance ends negotiation at that outcome, while rejection permanently removes it and makes the rejecting party the next proposer. The final remaining outcome is accepted automatically. Both parties have complete information, rank terminal outcomes by \(p_t(x)\) and \(p_o\), and use subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE); for each initiator the SPE outcome is unique. Write these outcomes as \(N_t(p_t(x),p_o)\) and \(N_o(p_t(x),p_o)\).

Is deciding whether such a rational coalition distribution \(x\) exists with \(N_t(p_t(x),p_o)=N_o(p_t(x),p_o)=p\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite, unweighted voters, this constructive coalitional manipulation problem with Borda is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with two manipulators; a polynomial-time algorithm succeeds with at most one additional manipulator whenever manipulation is possible. [Discrete results](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.14097v2).
No complexity result for exactly this rational-mass variant was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made all ranking types and the other party’s preferences strict total orders, matching the discrete definitions.
  • Required at least two outcomes, consistent with the nonconstant scoring-vector domain.
  • Made the coalition mass a binary rational with 0 < rho <= 1; allowed an empty honest support when rho = 1 because the discrete problem definition does not require honest voters.
  • Specified a fixed public lexicographic priority on outcome labels for all Borda score ties.
  • Allowed nonnegative rational coalition mass on any ranking, without a prescribed support bound or denominator restriction.
  • Defined tau as the honest input support size and L as the total input bit length.
  • Expanded VAOV to include permanent removal after rejection, alternating proposer roles, automatic acceptance of the final outcome, and complete information.

Result

The rational-mass problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). An exact algorithm solves at most \(m\) rational linear programs, each with \(m^2+1\) variables and \(O(m^2)\) constraints. A successful coalition can also be constructed with support size at most \(m^2\).

The proof has two main ingredients: a canonical choice of the team’s top outcomes, and an exact matrix representation of fractional Borda contributions.

1. Reducing negotiation to a prefix condition

For a strict ranking \(r\), let \(A_k(r)\) be its \(k\) highest-ranked outcomes. Define

\( k_*(r,p_o)=\min\{k:A_k(r)\cap A_k(p_o)\ne\varnothing\}, \)

and let \(C(r,p_o)=A_{k_*}(r)\cap A_{k_*}(p_o)\).

We use the following established VAOV characterization. For strict preferences and complete information, the SPE outcome belongs to \(C(r,p_o)\). If this set is a singleton, its member is selected for either initiator. If it contains two outcomes, the initiator’s preferred member is selected when \(m\) is odd, and the responder’s preferred member when \(m\) is even. This is the characterization supplied by Theorems 11–12 of [Erlich, Hazon, and Kraus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00913). Its hypotheses match this problem: strict terminal preferences, alternating offers, removal after rejection, and automatic acceptance of the final outcome.

At the first nonempty intersection, there are at most two outcomes: every member must be one of the two newly admitted outcomes. If there are two, the parties prefer opposite members, because their preceding prefixes were disjoint. Thus changing the initiator changes the SPE outcome in this case. Consequently,

\( N_t(r,p_o)=N_o(r,p_o)=p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C(r,p_o)=\{p\}. \)

Let \(q\) be the position of \(p\) in \(p_o\), counting from the top starting at \(1\). A singleton first intersection at size \(k\) requires

\( q\le k\le \left\lceil m/2\right\rceil. \)

Indeed, \(p\) must belong to \(A_k(p_o)\), and the singleton intersection gives

\( |A_k(r)\cup A_k(p_o)|=2k-1\le m. \)

For such a \(k\), put

\( G_k=O\setminus A_k(p_o). \)

The first intersection is exactly \(\{p\}\) at size \(k\) if and only if:

  • \(A_k(r)=\{p\}\cup H\) for some \(H\subseteq G_k\) with \(|H|=k-1\);
  • if \(q<k\), every member of \(H\) ranks above \(p\).

To verify sufficiency, all smaller team prefixes contain only members of \(H\), except possibly \(p\). Members of \(H\) are outside \(A_k(p_o)\). When \(q=k\), \(p\) is outside every smaller opponent prefix; when \(q<k\), condition 2 places \(p\) exactly \(k\)-th in the team ranking. Hence all earlier intersections are empty.

Necessity follows because, when \(q<k\), including \(p\) in the team’s preceding prefix would already produce an intersection.

2. Exact representation of rational Borda contributions

Let \(b_r(a)\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\) be the Borda score of outcome \(a\) in ranking \(r\). Compute its honest score

\( h_a=\sum_{r\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu(r)b_r(a). \)

Introduce variables \(Y_{a,j}\), where \(a\in O\) and \(j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), subject to

\( Y_{a,j}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}Y_{a,j}=\rho\ \text{for every }a,\qquad \sum_{a\in O}Y_{a,j}=\rho\ \text{for every }j. \)

The resulting total score is

\( S_a=h_a+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}jY_{a,j}. \)

Lemma. Rational matrices satisfying these constraints represent exactly the rational coalition distributions of total mass \(\rho\).

Proof. Given a coalition distribution, set

\( Y_{a,j}=\sum_{r:b_r(a)=j}x(r). \)

Every ranking assigns exactly one score to each outcome and exactly one outcome to each score, so the row and column sums are \(\rho\).

Conversely, consider a rational nonnegative matrix whose row and column sums are a common positive value \(t\). Form its bipartite support graph, with outcomes on one side and scores on the other. For any set \(U\) of outcomes, its neighbor set satisfies

\( |U|t =\sum_{a\in U}\sum_jY_{a,j} \le\sum_{j\in N(U)}\sum_aY_{a,j} =|N(U)|t. \)

Thus Hall’s condition holds, and there is a perfect matching using positive entries. Let \(\delta\) be the minimum matrix entry on this matching. Subtract \(\delta\) from every matched entry. The residual matrix is nonnegative and all its row and column sums become \(t-\delta\).

Repeat until the residual matrix is zero. Each step eliminates at least one positive entry and creates none, so there are at most \(m^2\) steps. Each matching assigns every Borda score exactly once and therefore defines a strict ranking. Assign that ranking mass \(\delta\).

All masses are rational, their sum is \(\rho\), and their aggregate score-assignment matrix is the original matrix. This proves the lemma and the support bound. \(\square\)

3. A canonical choice of the other top outcomes

Let \(\ell(a)\) be the position of outcome \(a\) in the public lexicographic priority order, with smaller values receiving priority.

Order outcomes by the honest key

\( (h_a,-\ell(a)), \)

using decreasing lexicographic order. For each admissible \(k\), let \(H_k\) contain the first \(k-1\) members of \(G_k\) in this order, and put \(T_k=\{p\}\cup H_k\). These sets exist because \(m-k\ge k-1\).

Lemma. If a successful coalition has its first singleton intersection at size \(k\), then another rational coalition does so with team top set exactly \(T_k\).

Proof. Write the original team top set as \(\{p\}\cup H\). If \(H\ne H_k\), choose

\( a\in H_k\setminus H,\qquad b\in H\setminus H_k. \)

By the definition of \(H_k\),

\( (h_a,-\ell(a))\ge_{\mathrm{lex}}(h_b,-\ell(b)). \)

Let \(z_c\) denote the coalition’s Borda contribution to outcome \(c\). Swap the labels \(a,b\) in every coalition ranking, leaving the honest distribution fixed. This gives another rational coalition of the same mass, with

\( z'_a=z_b,\qquad z'_b=z_a, \)

and every other contribution unchanged.

Define the original and new final keys by

\( K_c=(h_c+z_c,-\ell(c)),\qquad K'_c=(h_c+z'_c,-\ell(c)). \)

The honest-key inequality implies

\( K'_a\ge_{\mathrm{lex}}K_b, \qquad K'_b\le_{\mathrm{lex}}K_a. \)

Originally, \(b\) was inside the team top set and \(a\) outside it. Therefore:

  • \(K'_a\) exceeds every unchanged outside key, since \(K_b\) did;
  • \(K'_b\) is below every unchanged inside key, since \(K_a\) was;
  • \(K'_a>_{\mathrm{lex}}K'_b\), because

\(K'_a\ge_{\mathrm{lex}}K_b>_{\mathrm{lex}}K_a\ge_{\mathrm{lex}}K'_b\).

Hence the new top set is precisely \(\{p\}\cup(H\setminus\{b\})\cup\{a\}\).

If \(q<k\), originally \(b\) ranked above \(p\). Since \(K'_a\ge_{\mathrm{lex}}K_b\), the replacement \(a\) also ranks above \(p\). All other members of the new \(H\) retain that property. Thus the conditions in Section 1 remain satisfied.

Each exchange increases \(|H\cap H_k|\). After at most \(k-1\) exchanges, \(H=H_k\). \(\square\)

This removes the need to search exponentially many possible top sets.

4. Linear programs with exact tie-breaking

Fix an admissible \(k\). The required ordered pairs are

\( \mathcal P_k =(T_k\times(O\setminus T_k)) \ \cup\ \begin{cases} \{(a,p):a\in H_k\},&q<k,\\ \varnothing,&q=k. \end{cases} \)

Every pair \((a,b)\in\mathcal P_k\) requires \(a\) to rank above \(b\). Under the public tie-breaking rule, this means

\( S_a-S_b\ge0\quad\text{if }\ell(a)<\ell(b), \)

and

\( S_a-S_b>0\quad\text{if }\ell(a)>\ell(b). \)

To handle strict inequalities exactly, introduce \(\varepsilon\) and solve the rational linear program that maximizes \(\varepsilon\), subject to the matrix constraints from Section 2,

\( 0\le\varepsilon\le1, \)

and, for every required pair,

\( S_a-S_b\ge \begin{cases} 0,&\ell(a)<\ell(b),\\ \varepsilon,&\ell(a)>\ell(b). \end{cases} \)

Accept this case exactly when the program is feasible and its optimum is positive.

This test is equivalent to the required comparisons. If they hold, the finitely many required strict score differences have a positive minimum; choose \(\varepsilon\) as the smaller of that minimum and \(1\). If no strict comparisons are required, choose \(\varepsilon=1\). Conversely, a feasible solution with positive \(\varepsilon\) satisfies every strict comparison.

All coefficients are rational. The feasible region is bounded, since every matrix entry lies between \(0\) and \(\rho\). Therefore, whenever the optimum is positive, an exact rational optimal solution exists and can be found by polynomial-time rational linear programming. Section 2 converts its matrix into a rational coalition.

5. Correctness and running time

The algorithm computes the honest scores and then tests the linear program above for every

\( k\in\{q,\ldots,\lceil m/2\rceil\}. \)

If this range is empty, it returns no.

Soundness. An accepted program yields a rational coalition whose team top set is \(T_k\), with \(p\) below every member of \(H_k\) when \(q<k\). Section 1 then gives first intersection \(\{p\}\), so both initiators select \(p\).

Completeness. Any successful rational coalition has first intersection \(\{p\}\) at some admissible \(k\). Section 3 transforms it into a coalition with top set \(T_k\), preserving the necessary position of \(p\). Its matrix satisfies that case’s required comparisons, so the corresponding program has positive optimum.

For complexity, computing the honest scores takes \(O(m\tau)\) exact rational operations. Their bit lengths are \(O(L+\log m)\): a common denominator can be obtained by multiplying the input mass denominators, whose total encoding length is at most \(L\).

Each program has \(m^2+1\) variables, \(O(m^2)\) constraints, and coefficient bit length \(B=O(L+\log m)\). In sparse form, assembling all programs takes \(O(m^4)\) rational operations.

If \(T_{\mathrm{LP}}(n,c,B)\) denotes a polynomial bit-time bound for exact rational linear programming with \(n\) variables, \(c\) constraints, and coefficient bit length at most \(B\), the total decision time is bounded by

\( O\!\left((m\tau+m^4)\operatorname{poly}(B) +m\,T_{\mathrm{LP}}(m^2+1,O(m^2),B)\right). \)

Thus the running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), with no enumeration of the \(m!\) possible rankings. The rational matching decomposition also runs in polynomial time and constructs a successful coalition with support size at most \(m^2\).
