Instance: a finite set of complete student types \(T\), schools \(S\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\), weak school priorities \(\succsim_s\), and a rational assignment \(p\). Each assignment \(x\) has variables \(x_{ta}\geq 0\) for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\). Call \(x\) weakly stable if, for every type \(t\) and school \(s\), positive mass \(\sum_{a:s\succ_t a}x_{ta}>0\) implies that \(s\) is full and no type \(u\) with \(t\succ_s u\) has \(x_{us}>0\). Let \(\mathcal W(\mu)\) be the weakly stable mass assignments and \(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}\mathcal W(\mu)\). Given \(p\in\mathcal X(\mu)\), decide whether there is no \(q\in\mathcal X(\mu)\) such that \(\sum_{s':s'\succeq_t s}q_{ts'}\geq\sum_{s':s'\succeq_t s}p_{ts'}\) for every type \(t\) and acceptable school \(s\), with strict inequality for at least one such pair.

Each type specifies a strict ranking of its acceptable schools \(A(t)\), all preferred to being unmatched \(\emptyset\), and its priority class at each acceptable school. Each school has a weak priority order over the types accepting it; members of a type are tied, and \(t\succ_s u\) denotes strictly higher priority. Priorities are not refined by tie-breaking.
A feasible mass assignment has real entries \(x_{ta}\geq0\) for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\kappa_s\), and \(x_{ts}=0\) when \(s\notin A(t)\).
Call \(x\) weakly stable if, for every \(t\in T\) and \(s\in A(t)\), \(\sum_{a\in A(t)\cup\{\emptyset\}:s\succ_t a}x_{ta}>0\) implies both \(\sum_u x_{us}=\kappa_s\) and \(x_{us}=0\) for every accepting type \(u\) with \(t\succ_s u\).
Let \(\mathcal W(\mu)\) be these assignments and \(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}\mathcal W(\mu)\). The input includes a binary rational assignment \(p\), promised to belong to \(\mathcal X(\mu)\); no decomposition is supplied.

Theorem. The promised problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. More precisely, it is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete on the promised domain. Hardness already holds when all type masses and all school capacities equal \(1/\tau\), and every type accepts at most four schools.

The essential point is that, for suitably scaled integral supplies and capacities, taking the convex hull of weakly stable mass assignments gives exactly the convex hull of weakly stable finite matchings. Divisibility therefore does not invalidate the finite-student hardness reduction.

1. Weak stability describes a union of faces. Let \(P\) be the polytope of feasible mass assignments, including the unmatched coordinates. Write
\[ \]
For clarity, define \(K=\sum_{t\in T}|A(t)|\leq m\tau\), and, for each acceptable pair, define
\(B_{ts}(x)=\sum_{a\in A(t)\cup\{\emptyset\}:\,s\succ_t a}x_{ta}\).

For each acceptable pair \((t,s)\), consider these two sets of additional constraints:

  • Branch \(0\): \(B_{ts}(x)=0\).
  • Branch \(1\): \(\sum_u x_{us}=\kappa_s\), and \(x_{us}=0\) for every accepting type \(u\) satisfying \(t\succ_s u\).

A branch choice \(b\in\{0,1\}^{K}\) specifies one branch for every acceptable pair. Let \(F_b\) be its intersection with \(P\).

Every nonempty \(F_b\) is a face of \(P\). Indeed, branch \(0\) fixes certain nonnegative coordinates to zero, because a sum of nonnegative coordinates vanishes exactly when each summand vanishes. Branch \(1\) makes a school-capacity inequality tight and fixes other nonnegative coordinates to zero. Each operation defines a face, and a nonempty intersection of faces is a face.

Moreover,
\(\mathcal W(\mu)=\bigcup_{b\in\{0,1\}^{K}}F_b\).

To verify this equality, first take \(x\in F_b\). Whenever \(B_{ts}(x)>0\), branch \(0\) cannot have been chosen for that pair. Branch \(1\) therefore supplies precisely the fullness and priority conditions required by weak stability.

Conversely, take \(x\in\mathcal W(\mu)\). Choose branch \(0\) whenever \(B_{ts}(x)=0\), and branch \(1\) otherwise. Weak stability ensures that all chosen constraints hold.

Consequently, every weakly stable assignment is a convex combination of vertices of \(P\) that are themselves weakly stable: decompose it into vertices of a face \(F_b\) containing it. Those vertices belong to \(F_b\subseteq\mathcal W(\mu)\), and vertices of a face are vertices of the original polytope.

2. Integral supplies and capacities give integral vertices. We prove the needed integrality statement directly.

Suppose temporarily that type supplies and school capacities are nonnegative integers, without requiring supplies to sum to one. Introduce a nonnegative slack \(z_s\) for each capacity constraint. The feasible assignments then satisfy
\(\sum_{a\in A(t)\cup\{\emptyset\}}x_{ta}=\mu_t\)
and
\(\sum_{t:\,s\in A(t)}x_{ts}+z_s=\kappa_s\).

Multiply every school equation by \(-1\). In the resulting coefficient matrix:

  • an acceptable-school assignment column has one entry \(+1\) and one entry \(-1\);
  • an unmatched column has just one entry \(+1\);
  • a school-slack column has just one entry \(-1\).

This matrix is totally unimodular. Here is a determinant proof. In any square submatrix, if some column has at most one nonzero entry, expansion along that column reduces its determinant to zero or, up to sign, a smaller such determinant. Otherwise every column has exactly two nonzero entries of opposite signs, so its column sum is zero. The rows are then linearly dependent, and its determinant is zero. Induction proves that every square determinant belongs to \(\{0,1,-1\}\).

Thus every vertex is integral. Explicitly, the positive-coordinate columns at a vertex must be linearly independent; otherwise a sufficiently small perturbation in both directions would preserve the equations and nonnegativity. Select a nonsingular square submatrix on those columns. Its determinant is \(+1\) or \(-1\), so its inverse applied to the integral right-hand side gives integral coordinates. The slack representation is affinely equivalent to the original feasible-assignment polytope.

Now consider \(n\) distinct types with \(\mu_t=1/n\), and schools with \(\kappa_s=1/n\). Scaling by \(n\) gives integral row supplies \(1\) and integral capacities \(1\). Therefore every scaled vertex is a deterministic matching, with each type assigned to one acceptable school or left unmatched.

Combining this observation with the face argument proves
\(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}\{M/n:\ M\text{ is a weakly stable deterministic matching}\}\).

Indeed, every weakly stable mass assignment decomposes into scaled deterministic vertices that remain weakly stable. Conversely, every weakly stable deterministic matching becomes a weakly stable mass assignment after division by \(n\): positive worse-assignment mass corresponds exactly to a student preferring the school to their assignment, and school fullness and strict-priority exclusions scale identically.

This equality is the required justification for transferring finite hardness.

3. A promise-preserving reduction with distinct complete types. We use the finite-student theorem supplied in the question: deciding constrained-SD-efficiency is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hard with unit capacities and at most three acceptable schools per student. Its hardness construction always supplies an ex-post stable assignment; Appendix A, Claim 1, verifies this promise. [Aziz, Biró, Csáji and Demeulemeester, Theorem 2 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10679v1#A1.SS1).

Take such a finite instance with \(n\geq1\) students, \(m_0\) schools, and promised ex-post stable assignment \(p^{\mathrm{fin}}\). Include unmatched probabilities as
\(p^{\mathrm{fin}}_{i\emptyset}=1-\sum_{s\in A(i)}p^{\mathrm{fin}}_{is}\).

Different students could have identical complete types. To prevent their merger in the support representation, add a private school \(d_i\) for each student \(i\):

  • \(d_i\) has unit capacity and is acceptable only to \(i\);
  • student \(i\) ranks \(d_i\) below every originally acceptable school;
  • all original preferences and priorities are preserved.

Every student now has a different acceptable-school set, hence a distinct complete type. Each accepts at most four schools.

There is a bijection between weakly stable deterministic matchings before and after this modification: replace an unmatched student \(i\) by an assignment to \(d_i\).

To prove the forward direction, observe that comparisons with original schools are unchanged when unmatched status becomes the least acceptable private school. The private school cannot block: its sole accepting student either occupies it or receives a better school.

For the reverse direction, every weakly stable matching in the augmented instance matches every student. Otherwise an unmatched student \(i\) and their vacant private school \(d_i\) form a blocking pair. Replacing assignments to private schools by unmatched status then preserves all blocking-pair comparisons involving original schools.

This bijection extends affinely to the convex hulls. In particular, extend the input assignment by setting
\(p'_{is}=p^{\mathrm{fin}}_{is}\) for original schools,
\(p'_{i d_i}=p^{\mathrm{fin}}_{i\emptyset}\), and
\(p'_{i\emptyset}=0\).

The extension preserves SD dominance exactly. Prefix probabilities at original schools are unchanged. The additional prefix ending at \(d_i\) equals \(1\) in every augmented ex-post stable assignment, so it cannot provide an additional strict improvement.

Finally, form the mass instance with
\(\tau=n\), \(m=m_0+n\), \(\mu_i=1/n\), and \(\kappa_s=1/n\) for every original or private school. Supply the assignment \(\widehat p=p'/n\).

By the equality proved in Section 2, the augmented finite ex-post stable polytope, divided by \(n\), is exactly \(\mathcal X(\mu)\). Thus \(\widehat p\) satisfies the promise, and
\(p^{\mathrm{fin}}\text{ is constrained-SD-efficient}\)
if and only if
\(\widehat p\text{ is constrained-SD-efficient}\).

All dominance inequalities and strict improvements are simply multiplied by \(1/n\). No decomposition needs to be supplied.

The construction takes polynomial time and has polynomial output length: it adds \(n\) schools and one acceptable pair per student, and division by \(n\) increases each rational encoding by at most \(O(\log n)\) bits.

This proves \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hardness by a promise-preserving many-one reduction. It also proves the requested \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions: the complement of the source problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and one efficiency-oracle call followed by reversal of its answer decides that complement.

4. Polynomial certificates for arbitrary rational masses. The preceding hardness argument settles the question. We additionally prove the claimed upper complexity classification.

Let \(d=K+\tau\) count acceptable-school and unmatched coordinates. Let \(V\) be the union of the vertex sets of all nonempty faces \(F_b\). Section 1 gives
\(\mathcal X(\mu)=\operatorname{conv}V\),
and every member of \(V\) is weakly stable.

Every vertex in \(V\) has polynomial encoding length. To see this without expanding the population, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all masses and capacities. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Scaling \(P\) by \(D\) gives integral supplies and capacities, so Section 2 implies that every vertex coordinate is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). Assignment coordinates lie between zero and one, hence each needs only \(O(L)\) bits.

Consider the finite linear program with variables \(\lambda_v\geq0\) for \(v\in V\), and improvement slacks \(h_{ts}\geq0\):
\(\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v=1\),
\(\sum_{v\in V}\lambda_v C_{ts}(v)-h_{ts}=C_{ts}(p)\)
for every acceptable pair. Maximize \(\sum_{t,s}h_{ts}\).

The promise ensures feasibility. The feasible region is bounded: the weights lie in a simplex, and each improvement slack is determined by a prefix probability bounded by one. The optimum is positive exactly when \(p\) is inefficient.

If the optimum is positive, choose an optimal basic feasible solution. There are \(K+1\) equality rows, so at most \(K+1\) variables are positive. In particular, at most \(K+1\) stable vertices receive positive weight. All coefficients have polynomial bit length, and Cramer’s rule on a basis of size at most \(K+1\) gives polynomial bit length for the weights.

An inefficiency certificate therefore consists of at most \(K+1\) rational weakly stable assignments and their rational convex weights, of total size \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). A verifier checks feasibility, weak stability, the weights, and exact SD improvement in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time. Checking strict-priority exclusions requires at most \(O(m\tau^2)\) coordinate comparisons, with polynomial bit arithmetic.

Thus inefficiency has polynomially verifiable certificates on promised inputs. Together with the reduction, this establishes \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness on the promised domain.

5. A deterministic running-time bound. The face representation also yields an exact algorithm running in
\(2^{O(m\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).

Enumerate the \(2^K\) branch choices. For each \(b\), introduce a weight \(\lambda_b\geq0\) and assignment variables \(y^b\). Homogenize the constraints defining \(F_b\): row supplies become \(\lambda_b\mu_t\), capacities become \(\lambda_b\kappa_s\), and the chosen zero-coordinate and fullness constraints are imposed. Add \(\sum_b\lambda_b=1\) and set \(q=\sum_b y^b\).

If \(\lambda_b>0\), then \(y^b/\lambda_b\in F_b\). If \(\lambda_b=0\), nonnegativity and the row equations force \(y^b=0\). Hence the possible aggregates \(q\) are exactly \(\mathcal X(\mu)\).

Impose \(C_{ts}(q)\geq C_{ts}(p)\) and maximize the sum of these prefix improvements. Exact rational linear programming determines whether its optimum is positive. The formulation has \(2^K\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau)\) variables and constraints, giving the stated running time.
