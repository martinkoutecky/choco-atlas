The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite schools \(C\), finite complete types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), type-specific school preferences, school priority classes with boundary-mass splitting, rational \(\alpha\), and cyclic order \(\sigma\), compute exactly the event-driven mass-QRDA outcome, including \(x_{\theta,c}\), the quota-breakpoint sequence, and the first ratio-feasible stage.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) complete types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), normalized by \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge2\) schools; each type strictly ranks all schools, all schools and types are mutually acceptable, and each school specifies an ordered partition of the types into priority classes. The input also contains rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and a cyclic permutation \(\sigma\) of the schools; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For quotas \(q_C\), mass deferred acceptance uses synchronous student-proposing rounds: rejected mass applies to its next school, and schools reconsider newly applying and tentatively held mass together. Each school retains higher priority classes first; if its remaining quota is smaller than the offered mass of the boundary class, it retains the same fraction of every type's offered mass in that class. Define the outcome by the limit of these rounds, writing \(x_{\theta,c}\) for assigned mass and \(y_c=\sum_\theta x_{\theta,c}\).
Define continuous balanced mass-QRDA as follows. Initialize every quota at \(Q=1/(1+(m-1)\alpha)\), and decrease their common value \(q\) continuously toward \(1/m\), evaluating mass deferred acceptance at each value. This is the continuous limit of equal-size round-robin reductions; simultaneous school events are ordered by \(\sigma\). Stop at the first point satisfying \(\min_c y_c\ge\alpha\max_c y_c\), including the initial point.
A quota breakpoint is a value where the deferred-acceptance outcome changes regime: the set of positive type-school assignments, the set of binding quotas, or a school's partially retained priority class changes. Coalesce simultaneous breakpoints, include the initial and stopping quota vectors, and define the first feasible stage by its breakpoint interval and exact stopping value. Exact numerical outputs use real-algebraic representations.
Is computing this mass-QRDA outcome—its \(x_{\theta,c}\), complete quota-breakpoint sequence, and first ratio-feasible stage—possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the problem NP-hard?
Discrete QRDA has running time \(O(nm)\), assuming constant-time school-feasibility checks ([verified full version](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10458-020-09448-9)). No result settling this precisely specified continuous computation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the unspecified total mass to one and required at least two schools, positive support masses, strict complete student preferences, mutual acceptability, and alpha in [0,1].
  • Replaced the integer initial-quota inequality by its mass counterpart, giving Q = 1/(1+(m-1)alpha).
  • Resolved the unspecified continuous quota-reduction path as the continuous limit of balanced equal-size round-robin reductions: all quotas decrease together, with sigma ordering simultaneous events.
  • Resolved boundary-class splitting as proportional retention of offered mass, using synchronous mass deferred acceptance and its limiting outcome.
  • Defined quota breakpoints through changes in assignment support, binding quotas, or partially retained priority classes; included initialization and termination and specified the stopping point within its breakpoint interval.
  • Allowed exact real-algebraic numerical representations rather than assuming that rational inputs necessarily produce rational outputs.

Partial resolution: polynomial time with strict school priorities

The general problem with priority ties remains unresolved. I prove a stronger special-case result than those in the notes:

Theorem. If every school’s priority classes are singletons, then all the requested outputs—assignments, the complete quota-breakpoint sequence, and the first ratio-feasible stage—are computable in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. All numerical outputs in this restriction are rational. There are at most $m\tau$ interior quota breakpoints.

The schools may use different strict priority orders.

I also give strict-priority examples showing that individual assignments can be discontinuous in the quota and that direct synchronous simulation can require exponentially many rounds. Neither phenomenon implies hardness of the exact computation.

1. Well-defined outcomes and an attained stopping quota

These preliminary facts apply even when priority classes contain multiple types. They are needed below, so I prove them here.

Write type $\theta$’s ranking as
$$ c_{\theta,1},\ldots,c_{\theta,m}. $$
Fix a common quota $q\ge 1/m$. Let $A_{\theta,k}(t)$ and $R_{\theta,k}(t)$ be cumulative applications and cumulative rejections at the type’s $k$th school through round $t$. Before the first round both are zero. Synchronous applications satisfy
$$ A_{\theta,1}(t)=\mu_\theta,\qquad A_{\theta,k}(t)=R_{\theta,k-1}(t-1)\quad(k\ge 2). $$
The mass held after round $t$ is
$$ H_{\theta,k}(t)=A_{\theta,k}(t)-R_{\theta,k}(t). $$

Cumulative applications and rejections are nondecreasing in $t$, and
$$ 0\le R_{\theta,k}(t)\le A_{\theta,k}(t)\le\mu_\theta. $$
The last bound follows inductively along the ranking. Thus both cumulative quantities converge, and every held coordinate has a limit.

Let $Y_c(t)$ be the total held by school $c$, and let $a_c(t)$ be its newly applying mass. Mutual acceptability gives
$$ Y_c(t)=\min\{q,Y_c(t-1)+a_c(t)\}. $$
Hence school totals are nondecreasing in time, and any school that rejects positive mass remains full thereafter.

There can be no positive last-school rejection in a finite round. Such rejection for a type would entail earlier positive rejection from every other school in its complete ranking. All schools would therefore be full, holding total mass $mq\ge 1$, while positive mass remained unheld. This contradicts conservation of the unit total mass.

Finally, convergence of cumulative rejection implies
$$ R_{\theta,k}(t)-R_{\theta,k}(t-1)\longrightarrow 0. $$
After a round, unheld mass consists precisely of rejection awaiting its next application. There is no exhausted mass, and there are finitely many coordinates. Consequently all pending mass vanishes. The limit therefore satisfies
$$ \sum_c x_{\theta,c}=\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_c y_c=1,\qquad 0\le y_c\le q. $$

School totals are continuous in $q$. To see this, a type offered mass $a_\theta$ in a priority class of total offer $A$, with higher-class offer $B$, is retained in amount
$$ a_\theta\min\left\{1,\max\left\{0,\frac{q-B}{A}\right\}\right\} $$
when $A>0$. At $A=0$, continuity follows from the bound between zero and $a_\theta$. Thus every finite-round state is continuous in $q$.

Since $Y_c(t,q)$ increases to $y_c(q)$, each $y_c$ is lower semicontinuous. Full assignment also gives
$$ y_c(q)=1-\sum_{d\ne c}y_d(q), $$
so it is upper semicontinuous as well. Therefore it is continuous.

Put
$$ I=[1/m,Q],\qquad Q=\frac{1}{1+(m-1)\alpha}, $$
and define
$$ g(q)=\min_c y_c(q)-\alpha\max_c y_c(q). $$
This function is continuous. At $q=1/m$, all school totals equal $1/m$, so the feasible set
$$ F=\{q\in I:g(q)\ge 0\} $$
is nonempty and compact. The first stopping quota is therefore the attained value
$$ q_*=\max F. $$
This argument does not assume that feasibility is monotone.

2. Strict priorities give a least fixed-point description

From this section through Section 5, assume that every school strictly orders the supported types.

2.1 Path independence of strict-priority choice

Let $C_q(a)$ denote a school’s retained vector from offered vector $a$. For a priority prefix $P$,
$$ \sum_{\theta\in P}C_q(a)_\theta = \min\left\{q,\sum_{\theta\in P}a_\theta\right\}. $$
For nonnegative offer vectors $a,b$, it follows that
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\theta\in P}C_q(C_q(a)+b)_\theta &= \min\left\{q,\min\left\{q,\sum_{\theta\in P}a_\theta\right\} +\sum_{\theta\in P}b_\theta\right\}\\ &= \min\left\{q,\sum_{\theta\in P}(a_\theta+b_\theta)\right\}. \end{aligned} $$
Because every priority class is a singleton, prefix totals determine all coordinates. Hence
$$ C_q(C_q(a)+b)=C_q(a+b). $$

Applying this identity inductively to the specified held-plus-new process shows that the held vector at a school after every round equals its strict-priority choice from cumulative applications.

2.2 Rejection equations

Write $r_{\theta,c}$ for limiting cumulative rejection and define the corresponding cumulative application by
$$ a_{\theta,c}(r)= \begin{cases} \mu_\theta,&c=c_{\theta,1},\\ r_{\theta,c_{\theta,k-1}},&c=c_{\theta,k},\ k\ge 2. \end{cases} $$
Let
$$ s_{\theta,c}(r)= \sum_{\eta:\,\eta\text{ has higher priority than }\theta\text{ at }c} a_{\eta,c}(r). $$
Strict-priority rejection is
$$ T_q(r)_{\theta,c} = \min\left\{ a_{\theta,c}(r), \max\{0,a_{\theta,c}(r)+s_{\theta,c}(r)-q\} \right\}. $$

The map $T_q$ is continuous and coordinatewise nondecreasing in $r$. It is also coordinatewise nonincreasing in $q$. It maps
$$ \mathcal B=\prod_{\theta,c}[0,\mu_\theta] $$
into itself.

By path independence, actual cumulative rejection after round $t$ is exactly
$$ r^{(t)}=T_q(r^{(t-1)}),\qquad r^{(0)}=0. $$
Its limit $r(q)$ is a fixed point by continuity.

It is the coordinatewise least fixed point. Indeed, if $\widehat r=T_q(\widehat r)$, induction gives
$$ r^{(t)}\le\widehat r $$
for every $t$, and hence $r(q)\le\widehat r$.

Quota monotonicity follows from the same iteration:
$$ q_1<q_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_{\theta,c}(q_1)\ge r_{\theta,c}(q_2) \quad\text{for every }\theta,c. $$
Thus cumulative applications $a_{\theta,c}(q)$ are also nonincreasing in $q$.

2.3 A polynomial-time exact oracle at rational quotas

I verify that standard strict stable-allocation algorithms compute this particular fixed point.

Call an allocation stable if it assigns every type’s mass, respects school quotas, and has no pair $(\theta,c)$ such that $\theta$ has positive mass at a less preferred school while $c$ either has unused capacity or holds positive mass of a lower-priority type.

For a fully assigned allocation $x$, define
$$ a_{\theta,c} = \mu_\theta-\sum_{d\succ_\theta c}x_{\theta,d}. $$
Then $a_{\theta,c}-x_{\theta,c}$ is exactly the mass of $\theta$ assigned below $c$.

Stability is equivalent to
$$ C_q(a_{\cdot,c})=x_{\cdot,c} \quad\text{at every school }c. $$
For the nontrivial direction, suppose choice differs from $x$, and take the highest-priority type where they differ. Earlier coordinates agree. Choice must retain more of this type than $x$ does, because $x_{\theta,c}\le a_{\theta,c}$ and $x$ respects the remaining quota. The type has mass below $c$, and the remaining capacity beyond $x_{\theta,c}$ is either unused or occupied by lower-priority types. This is a blocking pair.

Conversely, greedy choice rejects a type only when the school is full with types of at least that priority, so choice admits no blocking pair.

Fixed points of $T_q$ also give fully assigned allocations. Their rejection coordinates satisfy
$$ r_{\theta,c_{\theta,k}}\le r_{\theta,c_{\theta,k-1}}. $$
If a last-school rejection were positive, rejection would be positive at every school for that type. Every school would then be full, whereas telescoping the allocation equations gives held total
$$ 1-\sum_\theta r_{\theta,c_{\theta,m}}<1, $$
contradicting $mq\ge 1$.

Thus fixed points correspond to stable allocations. Moreover,
$$ \sum_{j=1}^k x_{\theta,c_{\theta,j}} = \mu_\theta-r_{\theta,c_{\theta,k}}. $$
The least fixed point therefore simultaneously maximizes every type’s assigned mass in every preference prefix. Its allocation is the proposer-optimal stable allocation.

The known strict bipartite stable-allocation algorithm computes the proposer-optimal allocation in polynomial time; see Dean and Munshi, Section 3 and Lemma 5. Here the bipartition consists of types and schools, with quotas $\mu_\theta$ and $q$, strict orders on both sides, and edge capacities $\min\{\mu_\theta,q\}$. These edge bounds impose no additional restriction. Unused capacity is handled by the standard dummy construction. For rational data, scaling by a common denominator gives integral data of polynomial bit length; the augmenting algorithm uses additions, subtractions, comparisons and minima. Thus it supplies a polynomial-time exact oracle at every rational $q$. [Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

3. At most $m\tau$ interior quota breakpoints

At school $c$, list types in strict priority order as
$$ \theta_{c,1},\ldots,\theta_{c,\tau}. $$
Define cumulative prefix offers
$$ S_{c,j}(q)=\sum_{\ell=1}^j a_{\theta_{c,\ell},c}(q) $$
and predicates
$$ P_{c,j}(q):\quad S_{c,j}(q)\ge q. $$

Each $S_{c,j}$ is nonincreasing in $q$. Consequently each predicate changes at most once as $q$ decreases. More explicitly, for $q_1<q_2$,
$$ S_{c,j}(q_1)\ge S_{c,j}(q_2), $$
so
$$ S_{c,j}(q_2)\ge q_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{c,j}(q_1)>q_1. $$
Equality $S_{c,j}(q)=q$ can occur at only one quota.

Let $E$ contain the interior transition values of all these predicates. Then
$$ |E|\le m\tau. $$

I next prove that there are no outcome-regime breakpoints outside $E$.

3.1 Fixed cutoff patterns give linear equations

Let $J$ be a nonempty open interval between consecutive values in $E$, including the intervals adjacent to the endpoints of $I$. All predicates are constant on $J$, and no prefix offer equals $q$ there.

A school is either:

  • undersubscribed, with $S_{c,\tau}<q$; or
  • full with a fixed boundary type $\theta_{c,j}$ satisfying

$$ S_{c,j-1}<q<S_{c,j}. $$

In the latter case, higher-priority types have rejection zero, the boundary type has
$$ r_{\theta_{c,j},c}=S_{c,j}-q, $$
and lower-priority types have rejection equal to their entire cumulative application.

Set $N=m\tau$. These equations have the form
$$ r=M r+b(q), $$
where $M$ has entries in $\{0,1\}$ and $b(q)$ is affine. A boundary equation contributes coefficient $-1$ for $q$; other equations contribute coefficient zero.

3.2 The dependency graph is acyclic

Draw an edge from coordinate $u$ to coordinate $v$ when $M_{v,u}=1$.

Choose $q_1<q_2$ in $J$ and put
$$ \Delta=r(q_1)-r(q_2)\ge 0,\qquad \delta=q_2-q_1>0. $$
Subtracting the equations gives
$$ \Delta=M\Delta+\delta e, $$
where $e_v=1$ at boundary coordinates and $e_v=0$ elsewhere.

Along every dependency edge $u\to v$,
$$ \Delta_v\ge\Delta_u. $$
If $v$ is a boundary coordinate, the stronger inequality is
$$ \Delta_v\ge\Delta_u+\delta. $$
Therefore a directed cycle containing a boundary coordinate is impossible.

A zero-rejection coordinate has no incoming edges. A remaining coordinate rejects its entire application, so its only dependency is the same type’s rejection at the immediately preceding school in its ranking. A cycle consisting solely of these coordinates would strictly advance that type’s ranking position at every edge, which is also impossible.

The dependency graph is thus acyclic. In particular, $M^N=0$, and
$$ r(q)=(I+M+\cdots+M^{N-1})b(q) \quad(q\in J). $$
All rejection coordinates, applications and assignments are affine functions of $q$ on $J$.

3.3 Supports and priority regimes are constant on $J$

A lower-priority type below the boundary has assignment zero. The boundary type has strictly positive assignment and strictly positive rejection throughout $J$, so its singleton class is partially retained.

An assignment above the boundary, or at an undersubscribed school, equals its application. It is a nonnegative affine function throughout $J$. Such a function either vanishes identically or is positive everywhere on $J$: an interior zero of a nonconstant affine function would force a negative value on one side.

Thus the positive assignment set, binding schools and partially retained classes are all constant on $J$.

Every interior transition in $E$ changes a school’s cutoff, or changes it between undersubscribed and full. Hence it changes the binding status or partially retained class on the adjacent open intervals. Therefore $E$ is precisely the set of interior quota breakpoints, after coalescing simultaneous transitions.

4. Rational breakpoints have polynomial bit length

The previous section bounds their number. Exact polynomial computation also needs a uniform bound on their representation lengths.

4.1 A finite family of candidate affine solutions

For each rejection coordinate, the fixed-point equation has three closed linear branches:
$$ \begin{array}{ll} r_{\theta,c}=0, & S_{\theta,c}\le q,\\[2mm] r_{\theta,c}=a_{\theta,c}, & s_{\theta,c}\ge q,\\[2mm] r_{\theta,c}=S_{\theta,c}-q, & s_{\theta,c}\le q\le S_{\theta,c}, \end{array} $$
where $S_{\theta,c}=s_{\theta,c}+a_{\theta,c}$.

Selecting branches for all coordinates, and imposing $0\le r_{\theta,c}\le\mu_\theta$, describes a bounded polytope at each fixed $q$. The fixed-point set is the union of finitely many such polytopes.

The least fixed point uniquely minimizes
$$ \sum_{\theta,c}r_{\theta,c} $$
over this union. Indeed, every other fixed point is coordinatewise at least the least one, with some coordinate strictly larger.

In any branch polytope containing it, this unique minimizer must be a vertex. Otherwise it would be a nontrivial convex combination of two points of the polytope, and linearity of the objective would contradict uniqueness.

A vertex is determined by $N$ independent active constraints. Their coefficients in $r$ belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$, their coefficients in $q$ have absolute value at most one, and their constants are signed sums of input masses.

Cramer’s rule therefore produces a finite family of rational affine candidate vectors. At each quota, $r(q)$ is a feasible member with minimum objective value.

4.2 An explicit size bound

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the input masses. Then
$$ D\le 2^L. $$
Set
$$ K=(N+1)^{N+1}. $$
Expanding determinants bounds their absolute values by $K$. Each candidate coordinate can consequently be written as
$$ v_i(q)=\frac{p_i+D s_iq}{D d}, $$
with integers satisfying
$$ 1\le |d|\le K,\qquad |p_i|\le DK,\qquad |s_i|\le K. $$

A candidate is feasible on an interval, possibly empty or a singleton, because its feasibility conditions are linear in $q$. A finite endpoint of this interval has numerator and denominator bounded in absolute value by
$$ (N+1)DK. $$
An intersection of two nonidentical candidate objective functions has numerator and denominator bounded by
$$ 2NDK^2. $$
The same feasibility-size bound applies to a candidate’s prefix equation $S_{c,j}(q)=q$.

Between these finitely many event values, candidate feasibility and objective ordering are constant. Uniqueness of the minimizing vector then makes $r(q)$ one affine candidate throughout each resulting interval. A predicate transition must occur either at an event value or at a candidate prefix-equation root.

Thus all interior quota breakpoints are rational. Their reduced numerators and denominators are bounded by, for example,
$$ H=4(N+1)m\,2^L K^2. $$
The endpoints $1/m$ and $Q$ satisfy this bound as well. Crucially,
$$ \log_2 H=O\bigl(L+N\log(N+1)+\log m\bigr), $$
which is polynomial in the input parameters.

5. Polynomial-time construction of all requested outputs

5.1 Locate every predicate transition

For each of the $m\tau$ predicates $P_{c,j}$, evaluate it at $1/m$ and $Q$ using the rational-quota oracle.

If its endpoint values agree, monotonicity excludes an interior transition. Otherwise, binary search brackets its unique transition. Predicate membership at the transition itself need not be known during this search.

Continue until the bracket has width less than
$$ \frac{1}{4H^2}. $$
This takes polynomially many oracle calls, all at rationals of polynomial bit length.

Distinct reduced fractions with denominators at most $H$ are separated by at least $1/H^2$. Hence the bracket contains exactly one possible transition rational of denominator at most $H$.

It can be recovered in polynomial time by continued fractions. Specifically, the bracket midpoint differs from the transition by less than $1/(8H^2)$. For its reduced denominator $b\le H$, this is less than $1/(2b^2)$, so the standard continued-fraction approximation criterion makes the transition a convergent of the midpoint, unless they are equal. Listing the convergents and testing bracket membership recovers the unique rational.

Sort and coalesce the recovered values. This gives the complete breakpoint partition of $I$.

5.2 Compute each interval’s exact formulas

Choose one rational sample in every open breakpoint interval. The oracle determines the school cutoffs there. Section 3 proves that the corresponding dependency graph is acyclic.

Topological substitution in
$$ r=M r+b(q) $$
computes all affine formulas exactly. Assignments follow from
$$ x_{\theta,c}(q)=a_{\theta,c}(q)-r_{\theta,c}(q). $$
The determinant bounds above, or direct substitution along the acyclic graph, show that their rational coefficients have polynomial bit length.

At breakpoint values, use the oracle itself. This is necessary because assignments can jump, as shown below.

5.3 Find the first feasible stage

On each open breakpoint interval, every school total is affine. Ratio feasibility is equivalent to the collection of linear inequalities
$$ y_c(q)-\alpha y_d(q)\ge 0 \qquad\text{for all schools }c,d. $$
Indeed, these inequalities hold exactly when the smallest total is at least $\alpha$ times the largest.

The affine school-total formulas extend to the interval endpoints with their actual values, by the continuity proved in Section 1. Intersecting the inequalities with each closed interval therefore determines its feasible quotas exactly.

Take the largest feasible quota over these intervals. It is $q_*$, is rational, and has polynomial bit length. Its location identifies the first feasible breakpoint interval, including endpoint cases. Evaluate the exact assignments at $q_*$ with the oracle.

Finally, retain the breakpoints encountered from $Q$ down to $q_*$ and include the initial and stopping quota vectors, coalescing coincidences. Simultaneous school events can be listed in the supplied cyclic order $\sigma$ before coalescing.

There are at most $m\tau$ interior breakpoints, and every step uses polynomially many operations on numbers of polynomial bit length. This proves the strict-priority theorem.

Rational outputs satisfy the requested real-algebraic representation requirement using degree-one polynomials.

6. Two strict-priority obstructions to direct simulation arguments

6.1 Individual assignments need not be continuous

Take three schools $A,B,C$ and two types of mass $1/2$, with preferences
$$ u:A\succ B\succ C,\qquad v:B\succ A\succ C. $$
At $A$, put $v$ above $u$; at $B$, put $u$ above $v$. School $C$ has either strict order.

For $q\ge 1/2$, each type receives its first choice completely.

Now let $1/3\le q<1/2$, and write
$$ \varepsilon=\frac12-q>0. $$
Initially, each of $A,B$ holds native mass $q$ and rejects native mass $\varepsilon$. These rejections apply to the other school, where they have higher priority than the native type.

As long as at least $\varepsilon$ native mass remains, the next round displaces exactly $\varepsilon$ native mass at each school. When less remains, all of it is displaced. Thus after finitely many rounds the native holdings vanish. Each of $A,B$ holds quota $q$ entirely of the other type.

School $C$ never rejects: once $A,B$ are full, its held-plus-new offer is at most
$$ 1-2q\le q. $$
The outcome is therefore
$$ \begin{array}{c|ccc} & A&B&C\\ \hline u&0&q&1/2-q\\ v&q&0&1/2-q \end{array} \qquad(1/3\le q<1/2). $$
In particular,
$$ \lim_{q\uparrow 1/2}x_{u,A}(q)=0, \qquad x_{u,A}(1/2)=\frac12. $$
School totals remain continuous.

This jump occurs on an actual QRDA path. For $\alpha=1/4$,
$$ Q=\frac23,\qquad q_*=\frac49, $$
because below $1/2$ the ratio condition is exactly
$$ 1-2q\ge\frac14q. $$

6.2 A fixed-size instance requires exponentially many rounds

Take four schools $A,B,C,D$ and set $\alpha=1$. Initialization and stopping both occur at
$$ q=Q=\frac14. $$
For an integer $b\ge 4$, let
$$ \varepsilon=2^{-b}. $$
Use four types with dyadic masses
$$ \mu_u=\mu_v=q+\varepsilon,\qquad \mu_w=q,\qquad \mu_z=q-2\varepsilon. $$
These are positive and sum to one.

Their preferences are
$$ \begin{array}{c|l} u&A\succ B\succ D\succ C\\ v&B\succ A\succ D\succ C\\ w&C\succ D\succ A\succ B\\ z&D\succ C\succ A\succ B. \end{array} $$
At $A$, use $v\succ u\succ w\succ z$; at $B$, use $u\succ v\succ w\succ z$. The other schools use arbitrary strict orders.

Put
$$ N=\frac{q}{\varepsilon}=2^{b-2}. $$
After round one, $A,B$ each hold native mass $q$ and reject native mass $\varepsilon$. School $C$ holds $w$ of mass $q$, and $D$ holds $z$ of mass $q-2\varepsilon$.

For every round
$$ 1\le t\le N+1, $$
school $A$ holds
$$ q-(t-1)\varepsilon \quad\text{of }u,\qquad (t-1)\varepsilon \quad\text{of }v, $$
and $B$ has the symmetric holdings. Each round rejects native mass $\varepsilon$ from both schools. This follows inductively because arriving nonnative mass has higher priority and displaces the same amount of native mass.

After round $N+1$, native holdings are zero. In round $N+2$, the final incoming nonnative mass $\varepsilon$ is rejected from each already full school. In round $N+3$, this total mass $2\varepsilon$ reaches $D$, exactly filling its remaining capacity. No further rejection occurs.

Thus synchronous DA takes $N+3$ rounds. Here $m=\tau=4$ and the input length is $O(b)$, while
$$ N=2^{b-2}. $$
The number of rounds is exponential in input length.

The final allocation is nevertheless simple:
$$ x_{u,B}=q,\quad x_{u,D}=\varepsilon,\quad x_{v,A}=q,\quad x_{v,D}=\varepsilon,\quad x_{w,C}=q,\quad x_{z,D}=q-2\varepsilon, $$
with all other coordinates zero. Section 5 computes it in polynomial time.

7. What remains open with priority ties

The strict-priority proof depends essentially on path independence. Proportional retention within a nonsingleton class does not have that property.

For example, take one tied class with two types and quota $q=2/5$. Let
$$ a=(1/2,0),\qquad b=(0,3/10). $$
Then
$$ C_q(a)=(2/5,0), $$
and
$$ C_q(C_q(a)+b)=\left(\frac8{35},\frac6{35}\right), $$
whereas
$$ C_q(a+b)=\left(\frac14,\frac3{20}\right). $$
These vectors differ.

Consequently, the fixed-point equations, quota monotonicity and breakpoint bound proved above cannot be transferred to the specified tied-priority process by replacing held-plus-new offers with cumulative offers.

The general polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question therefore remains open in this write-up. The strict-priority theorem isolates nonsingleton priority classes as the remaining source of difficulty; it does not establish their computational hardness.
