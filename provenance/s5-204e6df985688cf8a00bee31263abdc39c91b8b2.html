The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a loopless undirected acceptance graph \(G=(T,E)\), and strict rankings of acceptable partner types that are acyclic in the paper’s sense, does every instance have exactly one stable mass matching, and can it be computed? A matching has variables \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge 0\) for edges \(\{t,u\}\in E\), with \(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\le\mu_t\). Let \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_u x_{tu}\) and \(w_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:\{t,v\}\in E,\ u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\); stability requires, for each edge \(\{t,u\}\), that \(w_{t,u}=0\) or \(w_{u,t}=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support \(T\): its \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The acceptance graph \(G=(T,E)\) is simple and undirected, with \(m=|E|\). Each type strictly ranks all its neighbors, and every acceptable partner is preferred to remaining unmatched.
Preferences are acyclic if there are no distinct types \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\), with \(k\ge3\), such that \(t_{i+1}\succ_{t_i}t_{i-1}\) for every \(i\), with indices modulo \(k\).
A mass matching assigns real values \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge0\) to edges, with \(\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\le\mu_t\). There are no additional edge capacities.
Define \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{u:\{t,u\}\in E}x_{tu}\) and \(w_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:\{t,v\}\in E,\ u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\). Stability means \(w_{t,u}=0\) or \(w_{u,t}=0\) for every edge \(\{t,u\}\in E\).

Does every such acyclic instance have exactly one stable mass matching, or can existence or uniqueness fail? Is finding a stable mass matching polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

Discrete acyclic instances with integer vertex quotas and at most one partnership per pair have a unique stable configuration ([Lebedev et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0612108)).
For real-valued stable allocations on nonbipartite graphs, Biró and Fleiner already state uniqueness and an algorithm under acyclic preferences ([original manuscript](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e265a098dc8b1a0c77f40bd8784e4da0794205ff&repid=rep1&type=pdf)); this covers the mass model above, so the target is already settled.

  • Defined m as the number of acceptance edges and L as the total input bit length.
  • Made explicit that matching values are real, acceptable partners outrank being unmatched, and there are no additional edge capacities.

Result

Every acyclic instance in the question has exactly one stable mass matching, and that matching can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Existence and uniqueness hold even for arbitrary positive real masses; rationality is needed only for the computational statement.

This is an established result: Section 4 of [Biró and Fleiner’s manuscript](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e265a098dc8b1a0c77f40bd8784e4da0794205ff&repid=rep1&type=pdf) states uniqueness and a greedy algorithm for acyclic stable allocations. Their model covers this question by taking vertex bounds \(b(t)=\mu_t\) and edge capacities \(c(e)=2\). Every feasible mass matching satisfies \(x(e)\le 1<2\), so edge capacities never bind. Their requirement that an edge be dominated at an endpoint is exactly \(w_{t,u}=0\).

Here is a self-contained proof for the stated definition of acyclicity.

1. Acyclicity guarantees a mutually first-choice edge

Call an edge \(\{a,b\}\) mutually first-choice if each endpoint ranks the other first among its current neighbors.

Lemma. Every nonempty finite graph with acyclic strict preferences has a mutually first-choice edge. Moreover, preferences remain acyclic after deleting vertices and restricting preference lists.

Proof. On the nonisolated vertices, let \(f(t)\) be the first-choice neighbor of \(t\). Because the graph is undirected, \(f(t)\) is also nonisolated. Iterating \(f\) therefore eventually reaches a directed cycle with distinct vertices.

A cycle cannot have length one because the graph has no loops. If its length is two, its edge is mutually first-choice. If its length is \(k\ge 3\), write its vertices as \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\), with \(f(t_i)=t_{i+1}\), indices modulo \(k\). The successor and predecessor are distinct neighbors, so strict preferences imply

\(t_{i+1}\succ_{t_i}t_{i-1}\quad\text{for every }i.\)

This contradicts acyclicity.

Finally, any forbidden cycle in a restricted instance would also be a forbidden cycle in the original instance. Thus deletion preserves acyclicity. \(\square\)

2. A mutually first-choice allocation is forced

For the recursive argument, allow arbitrary positive vertex quotas \(q_t\), without requiring their sum to be one. Define feasibility, remainders, and stability exactly as in the question, replacing \(\mu_t\) by \(q_t\).

Suppose \(\{a,b\}\) is mutually first-choice. Since every other neighbor of \(a\) is worse than \(b\),

\(w_{a,b}=r_a+\sum_{v\ne b}x_{av}=q_a-x_{ab}.\)

Similarly, \(w_{b,a}=q_b-x_{ab}\). Feasibility gives \(x_{ab}\le\min\{q_a,q_b\}\). Stability requires at least one of these two nonnegative quantities to vanish. Consequently, every stable matching satisfies

\(x_{ab}=\min\{q_a,q_b\}.\)

Relabel the endpoints so that \(q_a\le q_b\). Then \(x_{ab}=q_a\), and feasibility forces every other edge incident with \(a\) to have allocation zero.

Form a reduced instance as follows:

  • Delete \(a\).
  • Replace \(q_b\) by \(q_b-q_a\).
  • If this new quota is zero, delete \(b\) as well.
  • Keep all other quotas and restrict preferences to the remaining graph.

All retained quotas are positive, and the reduced preferences are acyclic by the lemma.

3. Reduction preserves exactly the stable matchings

We prove that restriction and extension give a bijection between the stable matchings of the original and reduced instances.

Restriction

Let \(x\) be any stable matching of the original instance. The preceding calculation forces \(x_{ab}=q_a\). All other edges incident with \(a\) have zero allocation; if \(q_a=q_b\), the same is true for \(b\).

Restrict \(x\) to the remaining edges, obtaining \(y\). This is feasible for the reduced quotas. If \(b\) survives, its remainder is unchanged because

\((q_b-q_a)-\sum_{v\text{ retained}}y_{bv} =q_b-\sum_v x_{bv}=r_b.\)

Every other retained vertex also has unchanged remainder: all its allocations to deleted vertices were zero.

For a surviving edge \(\{b,v\}\), the deleted allocation \(x_{ba}=q_a\) does not enter \(w_{b,v}\), because \(a\) was \(b\)’s first choice and hence was preferred to \(v\). All other deleted incident allocations are zero. Thus, for every surviving edge \(\{t,u\}\),

\(w^{\mathrm{reduced}}_{t,u}(y)=w^{\mathrm{original}}_{t,u}(x).\)

Stability of \(x\) therefore implies stability of \(y\).

Extension

Conversely, let \(y\) be a stable matching of the reduced instance. Extend it by setting \(x_{ab}=q_a\), setting all other edges incident with deleted vertices to zero, and retaining \(y\) elsewhere.

The extension is feasible. The same calculation shows that all willingness quantities on surviving edges are unchanged, so those edges remain stable.

Vertex \(a\) has zero remainder and allocates its entire quota to its first-choice neighbor \(b\). Consequently, \(w_{a,u}=0\) for every neighbor \(u\): its positive allocation is never to a neighbor worse than \(u\). Thus every edge incident with \(a\) is stable.

If \(b\) was also deleted, it likewise allocates its entire quota to its first-choice neighbor \(a\), so every edge incident with \(b\) is stable.

This proves stability of the extension. The forced values on deleted edges make restriction and extension inverse maps. \(\square\)

4. Existence, uniqueness, and the algorithm

We now induct on the number of vertices.

If the graph has no edges, the unique allocation is the empty allocation, and stability is vacuous. Otherwise, Section 1 supplies a mutually first-choice edge. Its reduction deletes at least one vertex and preserves acyclicity. By induction, the reduced instance has exactly one stable matching. The bijection in Section 3 then gives exactly one stable matching of the original instance.

This proves both existence and uniqueness, including uniqueness among all real-valued feasible allocations.

The proof gives the following algorithm:

  • Initialize every edge allocation to zero and every remaining quota to \(\mu_t\).
  • While the remaining graph has an edge, find a mutually first-choice edge \(\{a,b\}\).
  • Assign it \(\delta=\min\{q_a,q_b\}\), subtract \(\delta\) from both quotas, and delete every endpoint whose quota becomes zero.
  • Leave any final isolated vertices’ remaining mass unmatched.

Every iteration deletes at least one vertex, so there are at most \(\tau\) iterations. Each selected edge disappears immediately, so its allocation is assigned only once. The reduction proof establishes that the algorithm returns the unique stable matching, regardless of which mutually first-choice edge is selected.

5. Polynomial bit complexity

Write the input masses as \(\mu_t=p_t/d_t\), with positive integers \(p_t,d_t\), and let

\(D=\prod_{t\in T}d_t,\qquad Q_t=p_t(D/d_t).\)

The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because the denominator bit lengths together are at most \(L\). Since \(\mu_t\le 1\), each integer \(Q_t\) satisfies \(Q_t\le D\).

Run the algorithm on the integer quotas \(Q_t\). Its numerical operations are comparisons, minima, and subtractions. Every remaining quota and assigned edge amount stays between zero and \(D\), hence has \(O(L)\) bits. Divide the resulting edge amounts by \(D\) to obtain the mass matching.

A straightforward implementation scans all current preference lists and vertices to find a mutually first-choice edge in \(O(m+\tau)\) combinatorial operations per iteration. Thus it uses \(O(\tau(m+\tau))\) such operations, together with polynomial-time common-denominator preprocessing and arithmetic on \(O(L)\)-bit integers. The output also has polynomial bit length.

Therefore finding the stable mass matching is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), without expanding the rational masses into individual agents.
