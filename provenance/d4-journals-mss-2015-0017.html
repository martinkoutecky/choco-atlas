The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite state space \(S\) with rational probabilities, risks \(k\), types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and nonnegative rational per-capita endowments \(e_t^k(s)\), determine the exact set of individually rational Pareto-optimal type-homogeneous allocations \(x_t^k(s)\geq 0\). Require \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t^k(s)=\sum_t \mu_t e_t^k(s)\) for each risk and state, and require \(x_t^k\succeq_{SSD}e_t^k\) for every type and risk; Pareto comparisons range over feasible type-homogeneous allocations, with strict improvement for at least one positive-mass type on at least one risk. Represent the resulting set by its extreme points and their convex hull.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) have \(m\geq1\) states with positive binary-rational probabilities \(p_s\) summing to one, and let there be \(r\geq1\) risks.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has nonnegative binary-rational per-capita endowments \(e_t^k(s)\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A type-homogeneous allocation assigns a real per-capita amount \(x_t^k(s)\geq0\) to every member of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t^k(s)=\sum_t\mu_t e_t^k(s)\) for every risk \(k\) and state \(s\).
Use increasing-concave second-order stochastic dominance under the common state law. Strict dominance means weak dominance without reverse dominance. An allocation is individually rational when \(x_t^k\succeq_{SSD}e_t^k\) for every type and risk.
An allocation is Pareto optimal when no feasible type-homogeneous allocation \(y\) satisfies \(y_t^k\succeq_{SSD}x_t^k\) for every type and risk, with strict dominance for at least one pair. Let \(A\) be the set of individually rational Pareto-optimal allocations.

Can \(A\) be represented exactly by enumerating all its extreme points and taking their convex hull in total time polynomial in \(m,\tau,r,L,B\), where \(B\) is the total binary length of the vertex list, or would such an algorithm imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\)?

For finite lists of agents with rational state probabilities, the individually rational Pareto-optimal set is a nonempty polytope, hence the convex hull of finitely many extreme points; vertex enumeration is proposed without a polynomial-time bound. [Chateauneuf, Mostoufi and Vyncke](https://faculty-research.ipag.edu/wp-content/uploads/recherche/WP/IPAG_WP_2014_074.pdf).
The same structural result appears in a [later author version](https://theses.fr/2015PA010044/abes). No result settling the exact mass-version enumeration complexity was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required positive state probabilities summing to one, as in the discrete framework, and nonempty state, risk and type sets.
  • Specified increasing-concave SSD under the common state law and its strict part, so distributionally equivalent allocations do not count as strict improvements.
  • Made allocations real-valued per-capita quantities shared by all members of each type.
  • Specified binary encoding for all rational input data and total input bit length L.
  • Used total output-sensitive time for the requested full vertex enumeration, with B measuring the binary length of all distinct vertices; the anchor supplies no decision problem or complexity bound.

The following bound holds for an exact enumeration algorithm:
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,r,L)\, (B+1)^{6\min\{\tau,D-1\}+4}. $$

The polynomial factor has a fixed degree independent of $\tau$ and $D$. Thus the algorithm is output-polynomial for fixed $\tau$, or for fixed $D$, and in particular for fixed $m$. Its exponent does not settle the requested bound when both $\tau$ and $D$ vary.

The proof has two main ingredients:

  • Individual rationality requires quantile comparisons only at aggregate cumulative probabilities. Endowment-specific knots are unnecessary.
  • After introducing suitable nonnegative variables, the constraint matrix admits a column decomposition of width at most

$$ \min\{3\tau+1,\,2(d_k-1)\}. $$

I first establish the structural facts needed for these assertions directly in the support-and-mass model.

1. Finite-state increasing-concave dominance

For a vector $Z=(Z_s)_{s\in S}$, define
$$ Q_Z(q)= \min\left\{ \sum_s a_sZ_s: 0\leq a_s\leq p_s,\quad \sum_s a_s=q \right\}, \qquad 0\leq q\leq1. $$

The minimum selects outcomes in increasing order, splitting the probability of the last selected state if necessary. Consequently, $Q_Z$ is continuous, piecewise linear, and convex, with
$$ Q_Z(0)=0,\qquad Q_Z(1)=\mathbb EZ. $$

Under the increasing-concave convention,
$$ Z\succeq_{SSD}W \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_Z(q)\geq Q_W(q) \quad\text{for every }q\in[0,1]. $$

To justify this characterization, put
$$ \phi_Z(h)=\mathbb E\min(Z,h). $$
Selecting probability below a threshold gives the identities
$$ Q_Z(q)= \sup_{h\in\mathbb R} \bigl(\phi_Z(h)-(1-q)h\bigr), $$
$$ \phi_Z(h)= \inf_{q\in[0,1]} \bigl(Q_Z(q)+(1-q)h\bigr). $$

For the first identity, every admissible selection $a$ satisfies
$$ \sum_s a_sZ_s\geq \phi_Z(h)-(1-q)h. $$
Equality is obtained by choosing $h$ at the selection cutoff. For the second identity, the same inequality supplies one direction, and selecting exactly the probability strictly below $h$ supplies equality.

Thus comparison of all $Q$-values is equivalent to comparison of all $\phi$-values. Each function $z\mapsto\min(z,h)$ is increasing and concave, so increasing-concave dominance implies the latter comparison.

Conversely, interpolate any increasing concave utility on the finite union of the outcomes of $Z$ and $W$. On the relevant interval, its piecewise-linear interpolation is a constant plus a nonnegative multiple of $z$ and nonnegative multiples of functions $\min(z,h)$. Indeed, its final slope is nonnegative, and each downward change in slope supplies a nonnegative coefficient. Comparison of $\phi$-values, including expectations obtained by taking $h$ above all outcomes, therefore proves comparison for every such utility.

It follows that strict dominance is equivalent to weak quantile comparison with a strict inequality somewhere.

Two further facts will be used:
$$ Q_{cZ}(q)=cQ_Z(q)\qquad(c>0), $$
and
$$ Q_{\sum_t c_tZ_t}(q)\geq \sum_t c_tQ_{Z_t}(q)\qquad(c_t>0). $$

The second inequality follows by minimizing a sum over the common selection set. Equality holds if every $Z_t$ is nondecreasing in one common state ordering, because one selection minimizes every summand.

2. Pareto optimality and nonemptiness in the mass model

Feasibility and dominance separate across risks. An allocation is Pareto optimal precisely when each risk component is Pareto optimal: an improvement of one component extends to an improvement of the entire allocation by retaining all other components, and any improvement of the entire allocation strictly improves some component.

Consider one risk, and write
$$ w(s)=\sum_t\mu_te_t(s),\qquad z_t(s)=\mu_tx_t(s). $$
Feasibility becomes
$$ \sum_tz_t(s)=w(s). $$
Positive scaling preserves both weak and strict SSD, so this is a bijective reformulation of the mass model.

Call an allocation comonotone if
$$ (z_i(a)-z_i(b))(z_j(a)-z_j(b))\geq0 $$
for every pair of types and states.

Proposition: Pareto optimality is equivalent to comonotonicity

Suppose comonotonicity fails. Choose types and states with
$$ z_i(a)>z_i(b),\qquad z_j(a)<z_j(b). $$
Put $\lambda=p_a/p_b$ and choose
$$ 0<\delta< \frac{\min\{z_i(a)-z_i(b),\,z_j(b)-z_j(a)\}}{1+\lambda}. $$
Replace the four entries by
$$ z'_i(a)=z_i(a)-\delta,\qquad z'_i(b)=z_i(b)+\lambda\delta, $$
$$ z'_j(a)=z_j(a)+\delta,\qquad z'_j(b)=z_j(b)-\lambda\delta. $$

This preserves statewise totals and each type’s expectation. For type $i$, the modified two-state vector equals
$$ (1-\theta_i)(z_i(a),z_i(b))+\theta_i(c_i,c_i), $$
where
$$ c_i=\frac{p_az_i(a)+p_bz_i(b)}{p_a+p_b}, \qquad \theta_i= \frac{(1+\lambda)\delta}{z_i(a)-z_i(b)} \in(0,1). $$
For type $j$, the same representation holds with its conditional mean and
$$ \theta_j= \frac{(1+\lambda)\delta}{z_j(b)-z_j(a)} \in(0,1). $$

Both modified pairs remain between their original endpoints, hence remain nonnegative. Concavity, followed by Jensen’s inequality at the conditional mean, proves weak improvement for every increasing concave utility. The improvement is strict for the strictly increasing, strictly concave utility $u(v)=-e^{-v}$. Thus both types strictly improve in SSD, disproving Pareto optimality.

For the converse, order states by increasing $w$. A comonotone allocation must be nondecreasing in this ordering. Otherwise, if $w(a)\leq w(b)$ but $z_i(a)>z_i(b)$, comonotonicity forces
$$ z_j(a)\geq z_j(b) $$
for every other type, making the aggregate decrease strictly—a contradiction.

All types therefore have a common ordering, so
$$ Q_w(q)=\sum_tQ_{z_t}(q). $$
If a feasible allocation $v$ dominates every $z_t$ and strictly dominates one, some $q_0$ satisfies
$$ Q_w(q_0) \geq\sum_tQ_{v_t}(q_0) >\sum_tQ_{z_t}(q_0) =Q_w(q_0), $$
a contradiction. This proves the proposition.

Moreover, when two states have equal aggregate endowments, every type receives equal allocations in those states: all typewise differences have the same weak sign and their sum is zero.

Nonemptiness

Let $C$ be the feasible individually rational set in the $z$-variables. It contains the scaled endowment allocation. Feasibility gives
$$ 0\leq z_t(s)\leq w(s). $$
The SSD threshold characterization makes $C$ closed, so $C$ is compact.

Minimize
$$ F(z)=\sum_t\mathbb E[z_t^2] $$
over $C$. A crossing would permit the contraction constructed above, preserving individual rationality by transitivity of SSD. For each modified type, its conditional mean stays fixed and its conditional variance is multiplied by $(1-\theta_t)^2<1$. Hence $F$ strictly decreases, contradicting minimality.

The minimizer is comonotone and therefore Pareto optimal. Applying this argument independently to all risks proves
$$ A\neq\varnothing. $$

Finally, every feasible individually rational allocation preserves each type’s expectation. SSD gives
$$ \mathbb Ex_t^k\geq\mathbb Ee_t^k. $$
Feasibility implies
$$ \sum_t\mu_t \bigl(\mathbb Ex_t^k-\mathbb Ee_t^k\bigr)=0. $$
Every summand is nonnegative and every mass is positive; therefore
$$ \mathbb Ex_t^k=\mathbb Ee_t^k \qquad\text{for every }t,k. $$

3. A smaller exact inequality description

Fix one risk. Let its distinct aggregate endowment levels be
$$ w_1<\cdots<w_d. $$
Combine states having the same aggregate level, and define
$$ \pi_j=\sum_{\{s:w(s)=w_j\}}p_s, \qquad q_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^j\pi_\ell, \qquad q_0=0. $$

All $\pi_j$ are positive. Pareto-optimal allocations are constant within each combined group and nondecreasing across groups. Denote their compressed outcomes by $x_{tj}$.

Set
$$ h_t=\mathbb Ee_t,\qquad c_{tj}=Q_{e_t}(q_j), $$
and
$$ H_{tj}=\sum_{\ell=1}^j\pi_\ell x_{t\ell}. $$
The endpoint values are
$$ H_{t0}=c_{t0}=0,\qquad H_{td}=c_{td}=h_t. $$

Lemma: only aggregate knots are needed

For an aggregate-ordered allocation,
$$ x_t\succeq_{SSD}e_t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_{tj}\geq c_{tj} \quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,d-1. $$

Necessity follows from quantile dominance at $q_j$.

For sufficiency, take $q\in[q_{j-1},q_j]$ and write
$$ \theta=\frac{q-q_{j-1}}{q_j-q_{j-1}}. $$
The allocation’s integrated quantile is linear on this interval, whereas $Q_{e_t}$ is convex. Hence
$$ Q_{x_t}(q) =(1-\theta)H_{t,j-1}+\theta H_{tj} $$
$$ \geq (1-\theta)c_{t,j-1}+\theta c_{tj} \geq Q_{e_t}(q). $$
The endpoint comparisons hold by the prescribed values at $0$ and $1$. This proves the lemma.

In particular, the endowment-specific knots used in the notes can be omitted.

Define
$$ W_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^j\pi_\ell w_\ell. $$
The exact one-risk polytope is described by
$$ H_{tj}\geq c_{tj} \qquad(1\leq j<d), $$
$$ \frac{H_{t,j+1}-H_{tj}}{\pi_{j+1}} - \frac{H_{tj}-H_{t,j-1}}{\pi_j} \geq0 \qquad(1\leq j<d), $$
$$ \sum_t\mu_tH_{tj}=W_j \qquad(1\leq j<d), $$
together with the fixed endpoint values.

Indeed, recover outcomes by
$$ x_{tj}=\frac{H_{tj}-H_{t,j-1}}{\pi_j}. $$
The second family makes these outcomes nondecreasing. The first outcome is nonnegative because
$$ H_{t1}\geq c_{t1}\geq0, $$
so every outcome is nonnegative. Differencing the aggregate equations gives statewise feasibility. The final aggregate equation follows from
$$ \sum_t\mu_th_t=W_d. $$

Conversely, every individually rational Pareto-optimal allocation satisfies all these constraints by Section 2 and the lemma. For $d=1$, the unique allocation is $x_{t1}=h_t$.

All constants are computable exactly in polynomial time: sort each endowment’s outcomes, evaluate its integrated quantile at the rational $q_j$, and perform rational arithmetic. No expansion of masses into lists of agents occurs.

This proves that $A$ is a nonempty rational polytope with a polynomial-size description.

Its vertices also have polynomial binary encoding length. At a vertex, the equality rows and active inequality rows span the ambient coordinate space; otherwise, a nonzero vector annihilating them would permit small feasible perturbations in both directions. A full-rank subsystem therefore determines the vertex. Clearing rational denominators and applying Cramer’s rule gives coordinates whose numerator and denominator lengths are polynomially bounded: a determinant of an $N$-dimensional integer matrix with entries of binary length at most $H$ has binary length at most
$$ O(NH+N\log N). $$

4. A bounded standard-form formulation

Assume $d\geq2$ and put $n=d-1$. Introduce nonnegative variables
$$ u_{tj}=H_{tj}-c_{tj}, \qquad v_{tj}= \frac{H_{t,j+1}-H_{tj}}{\pi_{j+1}} - \frac{H_{tj}-H_{t,j-1}}{\pi_j} $$
for $1\leq j\leq n$.

Set $u_{t0}=u_{td}=0$, and define
$$ g_{tj}= \frac{c_{t,j+1}-c_{tj}}{\pi_{j+1}} - \frac{c_{tj}-c_{t,j-1}}{\pi_j}, $$
$$ b_j=W_j-\sum_t\mu_tc_{tj}. $$
Convexity of $Q_{e_t}$ gives $g_{tj}\geq0$.

The constraints become
$$ \frac{u_{t,j-1}}{\pi_j} - \left(\frac1{\pi_j}+\frac1{\pi_{j+1}}\right)u_{tj} + \frac{u_{t,j+1}}{\pi_{j+1}} -v_{tj} =-g_{tj}, $$
$$ \sum_t\mu_tu_{tj}=b_j, \qquad u_{tj}\geq0,\quad v_{tj}\geq0. $$

Write this polytope as
$$ \mathcal P=\{z:Mz=a,\ z\geq0\}, $$
where $z$ comprises the $u$- and $v$-variables.

The transformation is an affine bijection: $H=c+u$, and $v$ is uniquely determined by $H$. It therefore preserves vertices.

Importantly, $\mathcal P$ is bounded. A convex $H$ with endpoints $0$ and $h_t$ satisfies
$$ c_{tj}\leq H_{tj}\leq q_jh_t. $$
Thus $u$ is bounded, and $v$ is bounded because it is a fixed linear expression in $H$. Section 2 supplies nonemptiness.

5. Two explicit bounds on the column decomposition width

For a subset $K$ of the columns of $M$, define
$$ \lambda(K)= \operatorname{rank}(M_K) +\operatorname{rank}(M_{\overline K}) -\operatorname{rank}(M). $$
Equivalently,
$$ \lambda(K)= \dim\bigl( \operatorname{span}(M_K) \cap \operatorname{span}(M_{\overline K}) \bigr). $$

A rooted binary column decomposition assigns columns to leaves. Its width is the maximum $\lambda(K)$ over column sets below its nodes.

We use the following elementary bound. If only $h$ rows contain nonzero entries on both sides of a column partition, then
$$ \lambda(K)\leq h. $$
Indeed, every vector in the intersection of the two column spans is supported on those shared rows.

Call the local equation for $(t,j)$ row $R_{tj}$, and the aggregate equation for $j$ row $C_j$. A column $u_{tj}$ occurs only in
$$ R_{t,j-1},\ R_{tj},\ R_{t,j+1},\ C_j, $$
with nonexistent endpoint rows omitted. A column $v_{tj}$ occurs only in $R_{tj}$.

Ordering columns by aggregate level

Order columns in layers $j=1,\ldots,n$, placing all columns $u_{tj},v_{tj}$ of one layer consecutively.

Consider a prefix cut partway through layer $j$. A shared local row must have index
$$ j-1,\quad j,\quad\text{or }j+1. $$
There are at most $3\tau$ such rows. Only aggregate row $C_j$ can be shared. Therefore every prefix cut satisfies
$$ \lambda(K)\leq3\tau+1. $$

Construct a binary decomposition by repeatedly splitting the final column from the remaining prefix. Every non-leaf proper node is a prefix, and a singleton cut has $\lambda\leq1$. This gives width at most $3\tau+1$.

Ordering columns by type

Instead, place all columns of each type consecutively.

At a prefix cut partway through one type, shared local rows belong only to that type, contributing at most $n$ rows. There are at most $n$ shared aggregate rows. Hence
$$ \lambda(K)\leq2n. $$

The same prefix decomposition gives width at most $2n$. Choosing the better ordering yields a constructible decomposition of width
$$ k\leq\min\{3\tau+1,\,2(d-1)\}. $$

These arguments allow redundant equations, zero constants, and degenerate polytopes.

6. Consequences for exact vertex enumeration

The bounded-branch-width enumeration theorem of Reimers and Stougie applies to rational, bounded standard-form polytopes supplied with a column decomposition of bounded width. Its support-merging algorithm enumerates all vertices in total polynomial time for fixed width. A conservative version of its quantitative bound is
$$ \operatorname{poly}(\operatorname{size}(M,a))\, (V+1)^{2k+2}, $$
where $V$ is the number of vertices. The polynomial work includes rational linear-programming and rank tests. See their [Theorems 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.5584).

All hypotheses have been verified here:

  • The coefficients are rational and have polynomial encoding length.
  • The standard-form polytope is nonempty and bounded.
  • An appropriate decomposition is explicitly constructible.
  • The affine formulation preserves the desired vertices.

If preprocessing removes constant coordinates, the width bound persists. For any remaining column partition, the two new column spans are subspaces of the corresponding old spans, so their intersection dimension cannot increase. Removing constant coordinates is an affine bijection after retaining their values.

For one risk, the resulting bound is
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\, (V+1)^{2\min\{3\tau+1,\,2(d-1)\}+2}. $$
Since
$$ \min\{3\tau+1,\,2(d-1)\} \leq3\min\{\tau,d-1\}+1, $$
this is at most
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\, (V+1)^{6\min\{\tau,d-1\}+4}. $$

For multiple risks, their polytopes form a Cartesian product. A product point is extreme precisely when each component is extreme: a nontrivial decomposition in a factor gives one in the product, while a decomposition of a product of extreme points must be constant in every factor.

Enumerate each factor’s vertices, then output their Cartesian product. If the factor counts are $V_k$, all are positive, and
$$ V=\prod_kV_k,\qquad V_k\leq V. $$
Moreover, $V\leq B$, because every listed vertex occupies at least one bit. Exact coordinate recovery and product formation take polynomial work per output vertex.

Consequently, the total running time is bounded by
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,r,L)\, (B+1)^{6\min\{\tau,D-1\}+4}. $$

This proves output-polynomial enumeration for fixed support size, even with arbitrarily many states and arbitrary positive rational masses. It also proves output-polynomial enumeration for fixed numbers of aggregate levels, even with arbitrarily many supported types.

7. What remains open

The established exponent depends on $\min\{\tau,D-1\}$. Thus this argument supplies no polynomial bound of fixed degree when both parameters vary.

Nor does it establish that the requested algorithm would imply $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. The society polytopes are bounded. The general-polyhedron hardness result cited in the notes explicitly leaves the bounded case open; see the [Khachiyan–Boros–Borys–Elbassioni–Gurvich abstract](https://archive.dimacs.rutgers.edu/archive/TechnicalReports/abstracts/2005/2005-21.html). Applying that hardness result to the present bounded class would require an additional reduction.

The unrestricted target therefore still needs either a uniform output-polynomial algorithm or a hardness reduction with the necessary output-length control.
