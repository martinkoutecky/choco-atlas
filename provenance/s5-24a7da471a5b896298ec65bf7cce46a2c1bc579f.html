The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(p\le -1\), take sequentially arriving divisible items with aggregate supplies \(s_i\) and a society of total mass \(N\), represented by \(\tau\) binary valuation profiles \(t\) with shares \(\mu_t\). Each profile has unit monopolist utility, \(\sum_i s_i v_{ti}=1\). Values are revealed item by item; online allocations must depend only on revealed histories, assigning equal per-member allocations to profiles with identical revealed histories. What is the infimum worst-case competitive ratio for maximizing mass-weighted \(p\)-mean welfare, \(\bigl(\sum_t\mu_t U_t^p\bigr)^{1/p}\), against the clairvoyant offline optimum, as a function of \(\tau\), \(N\), and input bit length?

Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible profiles.
Items arrive in adversarial order with positive binary-rational supplies \(s_i\), satisfying \(\sum_i s_i v_{ti}=1\) for every type. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

State algorithmic running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any additional parameters, with \(N\) encoded in binary.

What is the optimal worst-case competitive ratio \(R_p(\tau,N,L)\) for this mass-society online allocation problem?

For finite societies with \(n\) agents and unit monopolist utilities, the verified bounds for \(p\leq-1\) are a competitive-ratio lower bound \(n^{1/2-o(1)}\), already for binary valuations, and an upper bound \(O(\sqrt{n\log n})\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13430v1)

Let $\ell(N)$ denote the binary encoding length of $N$.

The upper bound is

1. Normalization and benchmark bounds

Define
$$ y_{ti}=Nx_{ti}, \qquad u_t=NU_t, \qquad \mu_t=\frac{w_t}{N}. $$
Feasibility becomes
$$ \sum_t\mu_t y_{ti}\leq s_i. $$
Write normalized welfare as
$$ \mathcal W_q(u) = \left(\sum_t\mu_tu_t^{-q}\right)^{-1/q}, $$
with value zero if any utility is zero. Homogeneity gives
$$ \mathcal W_q(u)=N W_p(U), $$
so normalization preserves competitive ratios. Let $B$ be normalized optimal welfare.

Let $J$ be the set of items valued by at least one type, and put
$$ h=|J|, \qquad M=\sum_{i\in J}s_i. $$
Every valued item has supply at most $1$, because it occurs in some type’s monopolist-utility sum. Thus
$$ M\leq h\leq m. $$
Also,
$$ M \leq \sum_t\sum_i s_i v_{ti} =\tau. $$

For a feasible allocation with positive utilities, Jensen’s inequality for the convex function $z\mapsto z^{-q}$ gives
$$ \mathcal W_q(u)\leq\sum_t\mu_tu_t. $$
Moreover,
$$ \sum_t\mu_tu_t = \sum_i\sum_t\mu_t v_{ti}y_{ti} \leq M. $$
The same welfare bound holds when some utility is zero. Therefore
$$ B\leq M\leq\min\{\tau,m\}. $$

The allocation $y_{ti}=s_i$ to every type is feasible and gives every type utility $1$. Hence $B\geq1$.

An optimal allocation exists: the feasible allocation set is compact, since
$$ 0\leq y_{ti}\leq\frac{s_i}{\mu_t}. $$
Welfare extends continuously to zero-utility vectors. Indeed, on positive vectors,
$$ \mathcal W_q(u)\leq\mu_t^{-1/q}u_t $$
for each $t$, so welfare tends to zero whenever one coordinate tends to zero. Because the baseline allocation has welfare $1$, every optimizer has positive utilities.

There is also a useful bound depending on the masses. Feasibility and the monopolist promise imply
$$ u_t\leq\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_i s_i v_{ti} =\frac1{\mu_t}. $$
Consequently,
$$ B\leq \left(\sum_t\mu_t^{q+1}\right)^{-1/q}. $$

2. A supply bound from the number of distinct profiles

The following estimate improves $M\leq m$:
$$ M\leq\frac{h-\log_2\tau+1}{\gamma} \leq\frac{m-\log_2\tau+1}{\gamma}. $$

To prove it, restrict every profile to the valued coordinates $J$. The resulting $\tau$ subsets of $J$ remain distinct, and each has total supply exactly $1$. Thus
$$ \frac{\tau}{2} \leq \sum_{S\subseteq J}2^{-\sum_{i\in S}s_i} = \prod_{i\in J}(1+2^{-s_i}). $$

For $0\leq s\leq1$, define
$$ f(s)=\log_2(1+2^{-s}). $$
Its second derivative is
$$ f''(s) = (\ln2)\frac{2^{-s}}{(1+2^{-s})^2}>0. $$
A convex function lies below the chord joining its endpoint values. Since
$$ f(0)=1, \qquad f(1)=\log_2(3/2)=1-\gamma, $$
we obtain
$$ f(s)\leq1-\gamma s. $$
Taking logarithms in the product inequality now gives
$$ \log_2\tau-1 \leq \sum_{i\in J}f(s_i) \leq h-\gamma M. $$
Rearrangement proves the claim.

Therefore
$$ B\leq \min\left\{ \tau,m,\frac{m-\log_2\tau+1}{\gamma} \right\}. $$

3. An admissible online algorithm

The algorithm maintains a virtual utility $G_C$ for every revealed history class $C$, initially equal to $q$. When a class splits, its children inherit its virtual utility.

On arrival of item $i$, let $\alpha_C$ be the normalized mass of class $C$. For the classes valuing the item, perform weighted water filling:
$$ z_C=(\lambda-G_C)_+, \qquad \sum_{C:\,v_{Ci}=1}\alpha_C(\lambda-G_C)_+=s_i. $$
When at least one class values the item, this equation has a unique water-filling level: its left side is continuous, eventually strictly increasing, and unbounded. If nobody values the item, set every $z_C=0$.

Increase each valuing class’s virtual utility by $z_C$. The actual normalized allocation is
$$ y_{Ci}=\frac{q s_i+z_C}{q+1}. $$
It is feasible, since
$$ \sum_C\alpha_C y_{Ci} = \frac{q s_i+\sum_C\alpha_Cz_C}{q+1} \leq s_i. $$
All decisions use only currently revealed classes, their masses, and maintained virtual utilities. Allocations are equal within each revealed history class.

At termination, each latent type $t$ has
$$ u_t = \frac{q\sum_i s_i v_{ti}+\sum_i v_{ti}z_{ti}}{q+1} = \frac{G_t^{\mathrm{fin}}}{q+1}. $$
The last equality uses both the initial virtual utility $q$ and the unit-monopolist promise.

Competitive analysis

Define
$$ P=\frac1q\sum_t\mu_t\left(q^{-q}-G_t^{-q}\right). $$
Initially $P=0$, and always
$$ 0\leq P\leq q^{-(q+1)}. $$
A history refinement does not change $P$, because children inherit their parent’s utility and their masses sum to the parent’s mass.

During a valued item, parameterize the virtual allocation by the supply already allocated, $\xi\in[0,s_i]$. Water filling raises precisely the currently smallest virtual utilities among valuing types. Since
$$ \frac{d}{dG}\left(-\frac{G^{-q}}q\right)=G^{-(q+1)}, $$
we have, except at the finitely many water-filling breakpoints,
$$ \frac{dP}{d\xi} = \max_t\frac{v_{ti}}{G_t(\xi)^{q+1}}. $$
For an unvalued item both sides are zero.

Choose an optimal normalized allocation $y^*$, with positive utilities $u^*$. During item $i$, put
$$ b_t^*=\frac{y_{ti}^*}{s_i}. $$
Feasibility gives $\sum_t\mu_t b_t^*\leq1$. Because virtual utilities never decrease,
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_t\frac{\mu_t v_{ti}b_t^*}{(G_t^{\mathrm{fin}})^{q+1}} &\leq \max_t\frac{v_{ti}}{G_t(\xi)^{q+1}} \sum_t\mu_t b_t^*\\ &\leq\frac{dP}{d\xi}. \end{aligned} $$
Integrating over all items yields
$$ \sum_t\frac{\mu_tu_t^*}{(G_t^{\mathrm{fin}})^{q+1}} \leq q^{-(q+1)}. $$

Apply weighted Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents $(q+1)/q$ and $q+1$:
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_t\mu_t(G_t^{\mathrm{fin}})^{-q} &= \sum_t\mu_t \left(\frac{u_t^*}{(G_t^{\mathrm{fin}})^{q+1}}\right)^{q/(q+1)} \left((u_t^*)^{-q}\right)^{1/(q+1)}\\ &\leq \left( \sum_t\frac{\mu_tu_t^*}{(G_t^{\mathrm{fin}})^{q+1}} \right)^{q/(q+1)} \left(\sum_t\mu_t(u_t^*)^{-q}\right)^{1/(q+1)}\\ &\leq q^{-q}B^{-q/(q+1)}. \end{aligned} $$
All factors are finite and positive, as required. Taking the negative $1/q$ power gives
$$ \mathcal W_q(G^{\mathrm{fin}}) \geq qB^{1/(q+1)}. $$
Thus normalized algorithmic welfare satisfies
$$ \mathrm{ALG}_0 \geq\frac{q}{q+1}B^{1/(q+1)}, $$
and the competitive ratio is at most
$$ \frac{B}{\mathrm{ALG}_0} \leq\frac{q+1}{q}B^a. $$

Combining this with the benchmark bounds proves the instance-wise guarantee
$$ \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{W_p(A)} \leq \frac{q+1}{q} \min\left\{ M^a, \left(\sum_t\mu_t^{q+1}\right)^{-1/(q+1)} \right\}. $$
In particular,
$$ \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{W_p(A)} \leq \frac{q+1}{q} \left[ \min\left\{ \tau,m,\frac{m-\log_2\tau+1}{\gamma} \right\} \right]^a. $$
Under explicit profile encoding, $\tau m\leq L$. Substitution gives the announced upper bound for $R_p(\tau,N,L)$.

Running time

At each arrival, sort the at most $\tau$ valuing classes by virtual utility and determine the water-filling level using weighted prefix sums. This uses
$$ O\bigl(m\tau\log(\tau+1)\bigr) $$
exact rational operations. The algorithm does not evaluate the powers appearing in the analysis.

The maintained rationals have $O_p(mL)$ bits. Here is a denominator-growth justification. Normalized class masses admit a common denominator of $O(L)$ bits; supplies do as well. If utilities currently have common denominator $H$, a water-filling level has the form
$$ \lambda = \frac{s_i+\sum_{C\in S}\alpha_CG_C} {\sum_{C\in S}\alpha_C}. $$
Writing the masses over their common denominator shows that a common utility denominator can be enlarged by a factor with $O(L)$ bits per item. After $m$ items its length is $O_p(mL)$. Numerators satisfy the same bound because
$$ G_t\leq q+\frac1{\mu_t}. $$

Using elementary reduced-rational arithmetic gives the conservative bit-operation bound
$$ O_p\!\left( m\tau\log(\tau+1)(mL)^3 \right). $$
This is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, with no dependence on the numerical magnitude of $N$.

4. A lower-bound core

I first construct a hard instance with a controlled support size, then increase that support using few additional items.

Fix an integer $k\geq1$, and define
$$ \eta=1+\frac1{2q}, \qquad \delta=\frac1{2(q+1)}, \qquad H_k=\left\lceil\frac{k(q+1)}q\right\rceil. $$
For $1\leq\ell\leq k$, put
$$ c_\ell=2^{H_k-\lceil\delta\ell\rceil}. $$
These are positive integers: $H_k\geq k$ and $\lceil\delta\ell\rceil\leq\ell\leq k$.

Group $G_\ell$ contains $c_\ell$ types, each of normalized mass
$$ \frac{2^{-\ell}}{c_\ell}. $$
There is one distinguished type of mass
$$ b=2^{-k}. $$
The masses sum to $1$. The support size is
$$ T_k=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^k c_\ell. $$

Set
$$ r_0=1, \qquad r_\ell=2^{-\lceil\eta\ell\rceil}, \qquad \sigma_\ell=r_{\ell-1}-r_\ell. $$
Since $\eta>1$, the sequence $r_\ell$ strictly decreases, so every $\sigma_\ell$ is positive.

The first $k$ items have supplies $\sigma_\ell$. Item $\ell$ is valued by the distinguished type and all groups $G_j$ with $j\geq\ell$.

After these items, each good type in $G_\ell$ receives a distinct private item of supply $r_\ell$. Finally, a private item of supply $r_k$ arrives for the distinguished type.

Each good type in $G_\ell$ has monopolist utility
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sigma_j+r_\ell=1. $$
The distinguished type has monopolist utility
$$ \sum_{j=1}^k\sigma_j+r_k=1. $$
Private items make all profiles distinct.

Online welfare bound

At arrival $\ell$ of the first stage, the distinguished type and groups $G_\ell,\ldots,G_k$ have identical revealed histories. Their combined normalized mass is
$$ \sum_{j=\ell}^k2^{-j}+2^{-k} =2^{-(\ell-1)}. $$
History equality and feasibility therefore bound the distinguished type’s utility increment by
$$ \frac{\sigma_\ell}{2^{-(\ell-1)}} \leq 2^{-(\eta-1)(\ell-1)}. $$
Its final private item contributes at most
$$ \frac{r_k}{b}\leq1. $$
Hence its normalized online utility is at most
$$ D_q = 1+\frac1{1-2^{-(\eta-1)}}. $$
Its contribution alone to inverse welfare gives
$$ \mathrm{ALG}_0\leq D_q\,2^{k/q}. $$
This also holds if algorithmic welfare is zero.

Offline welfare bound

Allocate every first-stage item and the final private item to the distinguished type. Allocate every good private item to its unique valuing type.

The distinguished type obtains utility $2^k$. A good type in $G_\ell$ obtains
$$ u_\ell^* = 2^{\ell-\lceil\eta\ell\rceil}c_\ell. $$
The distinguished type’s inverse-welfare contribution is $2^{-k(q+1)}$. Group $G_\ell$ contributes
$$ \begin{aligned} 2^{-\ell}(u_\ell^*)^{-q} &= 2^{-qH_k-(q+1)\ell +q\lceil\eta\ell\rceil+q\lceil\delta\ell\rceil}\\ &\leq 2^{2q}2^{-qH_k}2^{-\delta\ell}. \end{aligned} $$
The inequality uses $\lceil z\rceil\leq z+1$ and the exact identity
$$ q(\eta+\delta)-(q+1)=-\delta. $$
Since $qH_k\geq k(q+1)$, summation gives
$$ \sum_t\mu_t(u_t^*)^{-q} \leq E_q\,2^{-k(q+1)}, \qquad E_q=1+\frac{2^{2q}}{2^\delta-1}. $$
Therefore
$$ B\geq E_q^{-1/q}2^{k(q+1)/q}, $$
and every admissible online algorithm has ratio at least
$$ \frac{B}{\mathrm{ALG}_0} \geq \frac{2^k}{D_qE_q^{1/q}}. $$

Relating the core support to the lower bound

The support sizes satisfy
$$ T_k \leq 1+\frac{2^{H_k}}{2^\delta-1} \leq A_q2^{k/a}, \qquad A_q=1+\frac2{2^\delta-1}. $$
Also, $T_1=3$, and $T_k$ strictly increases: increasing $k$ increases $H_k$ by at least one, increases every existing multiplicity, and adds another group.

For any integer $n\geq3$, choose the largest $k$ with $T_k\leq n$. Then
$$ n<T_{k+1}\leq A_q2^{(k+1)/a}, $$
so
$$ 2^k\geq\frac{n^a}{2A_q^a}. $$
The resulting core thus forces a ratio of at least $c_p n^a$, for a positive constant depending only on $p$.

5. Increasing the support using few items

The next lemma is the main improvement in the lower bound.

Support-padding lemma

Suppose a good type of normalized mass $\alpha$ has a private item of supply $r$. It can be replaced by $D\geq2$ distinct types, with masses summing to $\alpha$, using only
$$ \log_2D+O(\log\log(D+1)) $$
replacement items. This preserves:

  • every first-stage history-class mass;
  • every type’s monopolist utility;
  • the inverse-welfare value of the offline witness above.

Construction of distinct profiles

Put
$$ e=\lceil\log_2D\rceil, \qquad h=e+\lceil\log_2(e+1)\rceil+2, \qquad d=\lfloor h/2\rfloor. $$
There are at least $D$ distinct $d$-subsets of an $h$-element set. Indeed, the largest binomial coefficient is at least the average:
$$ \binom hd\geq\frac{2^h}{h+1}. $$
By the definition of $h$,
$$ 2^h\geq4D(e+1). $$
Furthermore, $\lceil\log_2(e+1)\rceil\leq e$ for $e\geq1$, so
$$ h+1\leq2e+3\leq4(e+1). $$
Thus $\binom hd\geq D$.

Choose $D$ distinct $d$-subsets as the replacement types’ code profiles. All replacement types inherit the old type’s valuations on the unchanged items.

Let
$$ P=2^{\lceil\log_2d\rceil}. $$
Introduce $h$ code items, each of supply $r/P$, valued according to the chosen subsets. If $d<P$, introduce one further item, valued by all replacement types, of supply
$$ r\left(1-\frac dP\right). $$
If $d=P$, omit this item.

Every replacement type receives monopolist utility exactly $r$ from these items. All supplies are positive dyadic rationals, and distinct code subsets give distinct profiles.

Masses

Let $H=2^e$. Split the old mass into $D$ fractions: use $H-D$ fractions equal to $2/H$, and $2D-H$ fractions equal to $1/H$. Their number is $D$, and their sum is
$$ \frac{2(H-D)+(2D-H)}H=1. $$
These are positive dyadic fractions with $O(\log D)$ encoding bits.

Preservation of the offline witness

On each replacement item of supply $s$, allocate per-member normalized quantity $s/\alpha$ to every replacement type. This is feasible because their total mass is $\alpha$.

Each replacement type then obtains utility $r/\alpha$, exactly the old type’s private-item utility. Its inherited items receive zero in the offline witness. Hence their combined inverse-welfare contribution is
$$ \sum_{j=1}^D\alpha_j(r/\alpha)^{-q} = \alpha(r/\alpha)^{-q}, $$
which equals the old contribution.

All replacement items are unvalued by the distinguished type. Moreover, the replacement types remain indistinguishable throughout the first stage. Thus the online bound for the distinguished type is unchanged. This proves the lemma.

6. Encoding bound and the resulting lower bound

Fix $3\leq n\leq\tau$. Choose the core from Section 4 with $T_k\leq n$. If $T_k<\tau$, replace one good type by
$$ D=\tau-T_k+1 $$
types using the support-padding lemma.

The resulting instance has exactly $\tau$ distinct profiles and still forces ratio at least $c_p n^a$.

The core has $T_k+k$ items. Padding adds $O(\log(\tau+1))$ items, so
$$ m=O_p\bigl(n+\log(\tau+1)\bigr). $$
Here $k=O_p(\log(n+1))$, because $T_k\geq2^{H_k-1}$.

Every normalized mass is dyadic with an exponent of
$$ O_p(\log(\tau+1)). $$
Actual masses $w_t=N\mu_t$ therefore each require
$$ O_p\bigl(\ell(N)+\log(\tau+1)\bigr) $$
bits. Every supply also has $O_p(\log(\tau+1))$ bits: the core exponents are $O_p(k)$, and the code construction introduces only a power-of-two divisor of size $O(\log\tau)$.

The profile matrix uses $\tau m$ bits. Masses, supplies, and encoding overhead consequently give a total length at most
$$ K_p\tau\bigl(n+\log_2(\tau+1)+\ell(N)+1\bigr). $$
This proves the lower-bound statement in the opening section.

Matching bounds above the new threshold

Let
$$ b_\tau=\log_2(\tau+1)+\ell(N)+1. $$
Enlarge $K_p$ if necessary so that $K_p\geq1$. Suppose
$$ L\geq2K_p\tau b_\tau. $$
For $\tau\geq3$, choose
$$ n=\min\left\{\tau,\left\lfloor\frac{L}{2K_p\tau}\right\rfloor\right\}. $$
Then $n\geq3$, and
$$ K_p\tau(n+b_\tau)\leq L. $$
Also,
$$ n\geq\frac1{4K_p}\min\{\tau,L/\tau\}. $$
The constructed lower bound therefore yields
$$ R_p(\tau,N,L) \geq c'_p\min\{\tau,L/\tau\}^{\,a}. $$
The upper bound from Section 3 gives the reverse inequality up to a constant depending only on $p$.

For $\tau=1,2$, the same asymptotic statement follows by adjusting constants and using $R_p\geq1$. For $\tau=1$ the exact ratio is $1$: allocating every valued item entirely to the sole type attains the benchmark.

7. Why the smallest budgets remain delicate

The unit-monopolist promise imposes more structure than mere profile distinctness.

Identify each profile with its valued-item subset. These subsets form an antichain: if $S\subsetneq T$, positivity of supplies gives
$$ \sum_{i\in S}s_i<\sum_{i\in T}s_i, $$
so both sums cannot equal $1$.

For completeness, an antichain $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $[m]$ satisfies
$$ \sum_{S\in\mathcal F}\frac1{\binom m{|S|}}\leq1. $$
To prove this, take a uniformly random permutation of $[m]$. The probability that its first $|S|$ entries form $S$ is $1/\binom m{|S|}$. For distinct antichain members these events are disjoint, since initial segments of a permutation are nested. Summing their probabilities proves the inequality. In particular,
$$ \tau\leq\binom m{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}. $$

There is a stronger consequence at maximum support. Suppose
$$ m=2d, \qquad \tau=\binom{2d}{d}. $$
The central binomial coefficient is the unique largest coefficient. Equality in the preceding bound therefore forces every profile to have size $d$, and the support must consist of all $d$-subsets.

For any two coordinates $i,j$, choose a $(d-1)$-subset $T$ avoiding both. Applying the promise to $T\cup\{i\}$ and $T\cup\{j\}$ gives $s_i=s_j$. Hence
$$ s_i=\frac1d \quad\text{for every }i, \qquad M=2. $$
Whenever such an instance is valid, the online algorithm above has competitive ratio at most
$$ \frac{q+1}{q}2^a, $$
independently of $\tau$.

Thus support size alone does not describe the behavior when the item budget is nearly minimal. The proved bound involving $m-\log_2\tau$ captures part of this restriction, but a matching characterization throughout the remaining encoding-budget regime has not been established.
