The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), capacities \(k_i\le 4\), an undirected acceptability graph on types, and strict rankings of acceptable partner types, decide whether a complete mass matching exists that is Pareto-optimal among all feasible mass matchings. A mass \(x_{ij}\ge0\) represents partnerships between types \(i\) and \(j\), with within-type partnerships allowed only when acceptable; completeness requires \(\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}+2x_{ii}=k_i\mu_i\) for every \(i\). Type \(i\)'s average partner vector has coordinate \(q_{ij}(x)=x_{ij}/\mu_i\) for \(j\ne i\) and \(q_{ii}(x)=2x_{ii}/\mu_i\). Ask whether some complete \(x\) has no feasible \(y\) with \(q_i(y)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}q_i(x)\) for every type and strict improvement for at least one.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an integer capacity \(k_i\in\{1,2,3,4\}\). An undirected acceptability graph, possibly with loops, specifies acceptable partner types; each type strictly ranks all its acceptable partner types, including itself when a loop is present.
A feasible mass matching consists of real numbers \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\geq0\), zero on unacceptable pairs, satisfying \(\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}+2x_{ii}\leq k_i\mu_i\) for every \(i\). There are no additional bounds on individual pair masses. A loop represents partnerships between distinct members of the same type.
A matching is complete when every capacity inequality is an equality. Its average partner vector for type \(i\) has coordinates \(q_{ij}(x)=x_{ij}/\mu_i\) for \(j\ne i\) and \(q_{ii}(x)=2x_{ii}/\mu_i\), ordered from best to worst acceptable partner type.
A feasible matching \(x\) is Pareto-optimal if no feasible matching \(y\), possibly incomplete, satisfies \(q_i(y)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}q_i(x)\) for every type, with strict inequality for at least one type. Larger coordinates are preferred.
Let \(m\) be the number of acceptable unordered type pairs, including loops, and \(L\) the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding whether a complete Pareto-optimal mass matching exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete stable fixtures with strict lexicographic preferences, existence of a complete Pareto-optimal matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with capacities at most four; maximum-size Pareto-optimal matching is polynomial-time solvable on bipartite graphs. [Biró–Csáji (2024)](https://unipub.lib.uni-corvinus.hu/9818/1/1-s2.0-S0899825624000411-main.pdf)
For a related fractional graph model with edge capacities, a fractional strong-core matching can be computed in polynomial time, without a completeness guarantee. No result settling the mass-model completeness question above was located. [Biró–Csáji (2024)](https://unipub.lib.uni-corvinus.hu/9818/1/1-s2.0-S0899825624000411-main.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified capacities as positive integers from one through four, following the discrete capacity domain.
  • Defined feasibility using weak capacity inequalities, so Pareto comparisons include incomplete alternatives.
  • Specified mass variables as nonnegative reals, with symmetric cross-type masses and zero mass on unacceptable pairs.
  • Retained the graded question's aggregate mass model without additional pair bounds; the discrete fractional model's unit edge bounds concern individual agents rather than aggregate type pairs.
  • Specified that acceptable within-type partnerships involve distinct members of that type and that the own-type coordinate appears in its strict ranking.
  • Defined m as the number of acceptable unordered type pairs, including loops, and L as total binary input length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, a stronger statement holds:

\[ \text{A complete Pareto-optimal mass matching exists} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{a complete feasible mass matching exists}. \]

The reverse implication is proved below by a construction using at most \(\tau\) optimization linear programs. Consequently, deciding existence requires only one feasibility linear program.

1. Linear formulation.

Write \(E\) for the acceptable unordered pairs, including loops, and put \(b_i=k_i\mu_i\). Define the incidence matrix \(A\) by giving a nonloop \(ij\) the column with entries one at \(i,j\), and giving a loop \(ii\) the column with entry two at \(i\). Thus feasibility and completeness are respectively

\[ x\geq 0,\qquad Ax\leq b, \]

and

\[ x\geq 0,\qquad Ax=b. \]

For each type, every preference coordinate is a positive multiple of its corresponding edge variable. Therefore, the sign of the first changed coordinate is exactly the sign of the change in that edge variable, including for loops.

The complete-matching polytope is bounded: a nonloop variable is at most either endpoint’s capacity, and a loop variable is at most half its endpoint’s capacity.

2. A lemma about edges that are somebody’s favorite.

Consider any residual instance with strictly positive rational capacities \(c_i\), and suppose its complete-matching polytope is nonempty. Preferences are restricted to its remaining acceptable pairs.

Let \(U\) consist of the edges that are the favorite remaining edge of at least one endpoint. Every vertex is incident to an edge of \(U\): an isolated vertex with positive capacity would contradict complete feasibility.

Choose an optimal solution \(z\) of

\[ \max\left\{\sum_{e\in U}z_e:Az=c,\ z\geq 0\right\}. \]

Lemma. Some vertex \(v\) has \(z_e=0\) for every incident edge \(e\notin U\).

*Proof.* Set \(w_e=1\) for \(e\in U\), and \(w_e=0\) otherwise. The dual linear program is

\[ \min\{c^{\mathsf T}p:A^{\mathsf T}p\geq w\}, \]

where the vertex variables \(p_i\) are unrestricted in sign. The primal is feasible and bounded, so strong duality supplies an optimal dual solution \(p\).

For an edge \(ij\), the dual constraint is \(p_i+p_j\geq w_{ij}\); for a loop this means \(2p_i\geq w_{ii}\). Complementary slackness gives

\[ z_{ij}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_i+p_j=w_{ij}. \]

Suppose, contrary to the lemma, that every vertex is incident to a positive-mass edge outside \(U\). For each vertex \(i\), such an edge supplies an endpoint \(j\) with \(p_j=-p_i\). This also covers a loop: then \(p_i=0\).

Hence the finite set of vertex prices contains the negative of each of its elements. Writing

\[ a=\min_i p_i,\qquad M=\max_i p_i, \]

we obtain \(a=-M\).

Choose a vertex \(i\) attaining \(a\). It is incident to an edge \(ij\in U\), but

\[ p_i+p_j\leq a+M=0, \]

contradicting its dual constraint \(p_i+p_j\geq 1\). The same inequality applies when \(ij\) is a loop. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

The absence of individual pair bounds is essential here: the dual has only vertex prices.

3. Construction of a complete matching.

Initially all types are active, with residual capacities \(c_i=b_i\). When a type is removed, every acceptable edge incident to it is permanently fixed.

First solve \(Ax=b,\ x\geq 0\). If it is infeasible, return “no.” Otherwise repeat the following steps.

  • If an active type has residual capacity zero, fix all its remaining incident edges to zero and remove it.
  • When all active residual capacities are positive, form \(U\) from the favorite remaining edges.
  • Solve the optimization problem in the lemma, choosing an optimal basic feasible solution \(z\).
  • Choose the vertex \(v\) supplied by the lemma. Fix every remaining edge incident to \(v\) to its value in \(z\), and remove \(v\).
  • For every remaining vertex \(i\), subtract the fixed nonloop mass \(z_{iv}\) from \(c_i\).

The residual instance stays completely feasible. Indeed, after removing \(v\), the restriction of \(z\) to the remaining edges satisfies precisely the updated capacity equalities. Its residual capacities are nonnegative. If a residual capacity becomes zero, nonnegativity forces all its remaining incident variables to be zero, justifying step 1.

Every removal saturates the removed type, counting its loop twice. At least one type is removed per iteration, so the process terminates and produces a complete matching \(x\).

4. Proof of Pareto-optimality.

Suppose a feasible matching \(y\), possibly incomplete, weakly improves every type relative to the constructed \(x\). We prove, in removal order, that every fixed edge has the same mass in \(y\) and \(x\).

Assume this has already been proved for edges incident to previously removed types. Consequently, all preference coordinates involving those types are unchanged.

For every currently active type \(i\), let \(t(i)\) be its favorite remaining edge. Its coordinate cannot decrease: all better coordinates, if any, involve previously removed types and are already equal. Thus

\[ y_{t(i)}-x_{t(i)}\geq 0. \]

It follows that \(y_e-x_e\geq 0\) for every edge \(e\in U\), since each such edge is \(t(i)\) for some endpoint.

Now consider the next removed type \(v\).

If its residual capacity was zero, its remaining incident masses in \(x\) are zero. Previously fixed masses already consume its entire original capacity, so feasibility forces those remaining masses in \(y\) to be zero as well.

Otherwise, \(v\) was selected using the lemma. Every remaining incident edge outside \(U\) was fixed to zero in \(x\); nonnegativity therefore gives \(y_e-x_e\geq 0\) on those edges too. Hence every remaining incident mass of \(v\) weakly increases.

But \(x\) saturates \(v\), the previously fixed incident masses agree, and \(y\) is feasible. Therefore

\[ \sum_{\substack{e\ni v\\ e\text{ remaining}}} A_{ve}(y_e-x_e)\leq 0. \]

Every summand is nonnegative, with coefficient one or two. Each summand must consequently be zero. All edges fixed when removing \(v\) agree in \(x\) and \(y\).

This completes the induction. Every acceptable edge eventually gets fixed, so \(y=x\). No feasible matching can weakly improve all types and strictly improve one. Thus \(x\) is Pareto-optimal. \(\square\)

5. Rational arithmetic and running time.

The decision algorithm simply tests feasibility of \(Ax=b,\ x\geq 0\). It has \(m\) variables, \(\tau\) equality constraints, and \(m\) nonnegativity constraints. Its binary encoding length is \(O(L+m\tau)\), using a dense matrix representation. Rational linear programming therefore decides the problem in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations.

For completeness, the construction also has polynomial bit complexity; repeated fixing does not create exponentially long rational numbers.

Initially, the capacities share a denominator whose bit length is \(O(L)\). At an optimal basic feasible solution, the columns corresponding to positive variables are linearly independent. If there are \(s\leq\tau\) such columns, choose a nonsingular square submatrix \(B\) of them. Each column has Euclidean norm at most two, including loop columns. Hadamard’s inequality gives

\[ |\det B|\leq 2^s\leq 2^\tau. \]

Cramer’s rule shows that, if the residual capacities have common denominator \(D\), the chosen basic solution has common denominator dividing \(D|\det B|\). The updated residual capacities can use that same denominator.

There are at most \(\tau\) optimization rounds. Consequently, all constructed masses and residual capacities have bit length \(O(L+\tau^2)\): their magnitudes stay bounded by the original capacities, which are at most four.

Let \(\mathsf{LP}(n,r,B)\) denote the polynomial bit complexity of obtaining an optimal basic solution of a rational linear program with \(n\) variables, at most \(r\) constraints, and total encoding length \(B\). Including the initial feasibility test, construction uses at most \(\tau+1\) such programs. A running-time bound is

\[ O\!\left( (\tau+1)\mathsf{LP}\!\left(m,m+\tau,O(m\tau+\tau L+\tau^3)\right) +\tau(m+\tau)\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau^2) \right). \]

This is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

6. Relation to the discrete result.

The cited discrete model restricts a pair to at most one partnership; its fractional extension likewise imposes edge capacities. Those restrictions are absent here. [Biró–Csáji, definitions in Section 2 and Theorem 3.6](https://unipub.lib.uni-corvinus.hu/9818/1/1-s2.0-S0899825624000411-main.pdf)

With additional edge bounds, the dual acquires edge-bound multipliers. A positive edge outside \(U\) then need not force opposite endpoint prices, so the key lemma above does not follow. The discrete hardness result therefore does not conflict with this polynomial-time construction.
