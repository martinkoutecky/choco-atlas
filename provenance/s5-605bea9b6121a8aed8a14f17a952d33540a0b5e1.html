Theorem 16 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_a\) of worker types \(a\), each with a strict employer ranking and common prior employer \(h_a\), responsive firms with quotas \(q_f\) and strict priorities over types, and a rational starting allocation \(x^0_{a,h_a}=\mu_a\) that is individually rational and firm-quasi-stable, compute the worker-worst mass-stable allocation in which no worker mass is worse off than under \(x^0\). Determine whether it is obtained by assigning to each type the mass that the firm-optimal stable allocation \(x^F\) sends to employers preferred to \(h_a\), and leaving the remaining mass at \(h_a\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(F\) contain \(m\) firms. A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_a\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses need not sum to one. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each type has a strict ranking \(\succ_a\) of \(F\cup\{\varnothing\}\) and a common prior employer \(h_a\in F\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Each firm has a positive binary-rational quota \(q_f\) and a strict priority ranking of types and \(\varnothing\); types above \(\varnothing\) are acceptable. A pair is mutually acceptable when both sides rank the other above \(\varnothing\).
An allocation has divisible real masses \(x_{ag}\geq0\), with \(\sum_{g\in F\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{ag}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{af}\leq q_f\). Individual rationality requires positive employment mass only on mutually acceptable pairs. Responsive choice \(C_f(v)\) fills up to \(q_f\) from offered masses \(v\), taking acceptable types in priority order and splitting the last type if necessary; within a type, incumbents are retained before newcomers.
A mutually acceptable pair \((a,f)\) mass-blocks \(x\) if some \(x_{ag}>0\) has \(f\succ_a g\), and either \(\sum_bx_{bf}<q_f\) or \(x_{bf}>0\) for some type \(b\) ranked below \(a\) by \(f\). Mass stability means individual rationality and absence of mass-blocking pairs; equal-priority mass cannot displace itself.
The input allocation is \(x^0_{a,h_a}=\mu_a\), with all other entries zero, and is individually rational and firm-quasi-stable: for every firm and every offer vector satisfying \(0\leq z_a\leq\mu_a\mathbf{1}[f\succ_a h_a]\), require \(C_f(x^0_{\cdot f}+z)\geq x^0_{\cdot f}\) componentwise.
Write \(x\succeq_W y\) when, for every type and every initial segment of its ranking, the mass assigned there under \(x\) is at least that under \(y\). Let \(\mathcal S_0\) be the mass-stable allocations assigning no mass below \(h_a\). Its worker-worst allocation is a least element under \(\succeq_W\).
Let \(x^F\) denote the firm-optimal mass-stable allocation: simultaneously for every firm, its assigned-mass vector in descending priority order is lexicographically maximal among mass-stable allocations. Define \(y_{af}=x^F_{af}\) for \(f\succ_a h_a\), \(y_{a,h_a}=\mu_a-\sum_{f\succ_a h_a}x^F_{af}\), and all remaining entries zero.
Does \(\mathcal S_0\) always have a worker-worst allocation equal to \(y\), or can this representation fail?
Is computing its worker-worst allocation, or reporting that no least element exists, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?
For finite workers with substitutable, quota-separable firm preferences, the worker-worst stable improvement of any firm-quasi-stable matching assigns each worker the better of its prior employer and its firm-optimal stable employer. [Discrete representation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12452).

Conclusion

Under the stated hypotheses, the representation always holds: \(\mathcal S_0\) has a unique worker-worst allocation, and it equals \(y\). Computing it is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The main step is stronger than the requested representation: moving all mass below its type’s prior employer back to that employer preserves stability for every stable allocation.

1. A consequence of firm-quasi-stability

Write \(b\succ_f a\) when firm \(f\) ranks type \(b\) above type \(a\). Define

\( I_f=\{a:h_a=f\}, \qquad E_f=\{a:f\succ_a h_a\text{ and }(a,f)\text{ is mutually acceptable}\}. \)

For every \(i\in I_f\), firm-quasi-stability implies

\[ B_{fi}:= \sum_{\substack{b\in I_f\\b=i\text{ or }b\succ_f i}}\mu_b + \sum_{\substack{b\in E_f\\b\succ_f i}}\mu_b \leq q_f. \tag{1} \]

Indeed, offer firm \(f\) the full mass of every type in \(E_f\). This offer is permitted by the hypothesis. Responsive choice processes all acceptable mass above \(i\), then the incumbent mass \(\mu_i\). Retaining all of \(\mu_i\) therefore requires exactly inequality (1). Individual rationality of \(x^0\) ensures that every incumbent in \(I_f\) is acceptable.

Only this necessary consequence of quasi-stability will be needed.

2. Moving mass to prior employers preserves stability

For any allocation \(x\), define \(T(x)\) by

\( T(x)_{af}=x_{af}\quad\text{if }f\succ_a h_a, \qquad T(x)_{a,h_a}=\mu_a-\sum_{f\succ_a h_a}x_{af}, \)

with every other entry zero. Thus \(T\) moves precisely the mass assigned below \(h_a\) to \(h_a\).

Lemma. If \(x\) is mass-stable, then \(z=T(x)\) is mass-stable and belongs to \(\mathcal S_0\). Moreover, every firm has the same total assigned mass in \(z\) and \(x\).

Feasibility

Row sums and nonnegativity follow directly from the definition. Every employment pair retained from \(x\) is mutually acceptable; every newly increased employment pair is a prior-employer pair and is mutually acceptable by individual rationality of \(x^0\).

Fix a firm \(f\). An entry can increase only for a type in \(I_f\).

If no entry increases, its quota remains satisfied. Otherwise choose the lowest-priority type \(i\in I_f\) whose entry increases:

\( z_{if}>x_{if}. \)

This increase means that \(x\) assigns some mass of \(i\) below \(f=h_i\). Stability of \(x\) consequently implies that \(f\) is full and assigns no positive mass to a type below \(i\).

Every positive entry of \(z_{\cdot f}\) either was positive in \(x_{\cdot f}\), or belongs to an incumbent whose entry increased. By the choice of \(i\), all such types are ranked at least as high as \(i\).

Furthermore, each positive nonincumbent entry of \(z_{\cdot f}\) belongs to \(E_f\), and is strictly above \(i\). Since every entry is at most its type’s mass, (1) gives

\( \sum_a z_{af}\leq B_{fi}\leq q_f. \)

Thus \(z\) is feasible.

Firm totals are preserved

Let \(X_f=\sum_a x_{af}\) and \(Z_f=\sum_a z_{af}\).

If \(X_f=q_f\), feasibility gives \(Z_f\leq X_f\). If \(X_f<q_f\), no incumbent’s entry can increase: such an increase would mean that this mutually acceptable incumbent has mass below \(f\) in \(x\), and hence blocks with the vacancy. Therefore \(Z_f\leq X_f\) also in this case.

On the other hand, \(T\) cannot decrease total employment. When \(h_a\in F\), mass moved to \(h_a\) remains employed, or becomes employed if previously unmatched. When \(h_a=\varnothing\), individual rationality implies that \(x\) has no positive mass below \(\varnothing\), so the row is unchanged. Hence

\( \sum_f Z_f\geq\sum_f X_f. \)

Together with the componentwise inequalities \(Z_f\leq X_f\), this proves

\[ Z_f=X_f\quad\text{for every }f. \tag{2} \]

Absence of blocking pairs

Suppose, for contradiction, that \((a,f)\) mass-blocks \(z\). There is positive mass of \(a\) below \(f\) in \(z\). Because every assignment in \(z\) is at least as good as \(h_a\), necessarily \(f\succ_a h_a\), and thus \(a\in E_f\).

Since \(T\) only moves mass upward, \(x\) also has positive mass of \(a\) below \(f\). Stability of \(x\) therefore implies that \(X_f=q_f\) and that \(x_{\cdot f}\) has no positive mass below \(a\). By (2), \(Z_f=q_f\), so a vacancy cannot explain the alleged block.

Consequently, some positive entry of \(z_{\cdot f}\) belongs to a type below \(a\). Choose the lowest-priority type \(b\) with \(z_{bf}>0\). Then \(a\succ_f b\), while \(x_{bf}=0\). Since only incumbent entries can increase, \(b\in I_f\).

All positive entries of \(z_{\cdot f}\) are ranked at least as high as \(b\). Their masses are therefore bounded by the corresponding terms in \(B_{fb}\). The term for \(a\in E_f\) contributes the full \(\mu_a\), whereas \(z\) assigns only \(z_{af}\) to \(f\). Positive mass below \(f\) implies \(\mu_a-z_{af}>0\). Thus

\( B_{fb} \geq \sum_c z_{cf}+(\mu_a-z_{af}) > q_f, \)

contradicting (1).

This proves the lemma.

3. Firm optimality implies worker-worstness in the required order

We use the following established stable-allocation facts. Side-optimal allocations exist. Every stable allocation can be transformed into the firm-optimal allocation through finitely many rotations, each moving a participating worker’s mass to a strictly worse partner. A side-optimal allocation can be computed using \(O(EN)\) arithmetic operations by the direct implementation, where \(N\) and \(E\) are the numbers of vertices and edges. See [Dean–Munshi, Sections 2–4, particularly Lemmas 6–8 and 12](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Here their hypotheses apply without expanding types into individual workers:

  • A worker vertex represents each supported type \(a\), with capacity \(\mu_a\).
  • A firm vertex has capacity \(q_f\).
  • Edges are exactly mutually acceptable pairs, with capacity \(\min(\mu_a,q_f)\).
  • Rankings at every vertex are strict.
  • Unmatched worker mass and unused firm capacity are represented by least-preferred dummy partners.

The edge-capacity condition introduces no discrepancy in blocking. If \((a,f)\) mass-blocks, positive mass below \(f\) gives \(x_{af}<\mu_a\); a vacancy or positive mass of a lower-priority type gives \(x_{af}<q_f\). Hence \(x_{af}<\min(\mu_a,q_f)\). Conversely, a blocking pair in the augmented model supplies precisely the worker-side condition and either a firm vacancy or a lower-priority real type.

The firm-optimal allocation in this model is exactly the \(x^F\) defined in the question: dummy mass comes last, and each firm’s vector of real type masses is simultaneously lexicographically maximal.

The rotation property establishes the stronger cumulative-mass comparison needed here. Moving an amount \(\delta\) from a better partner to a worse partner never increases the mass in any initial ranking segment: a segment contains both partners, neither, or only the better partner. Applying this observation along a rotation sequence gives

\[ s\succeq_W x^F \quad\text{for every mass-stable allocation }s. \tag{3} \]

Thus the conclusion concerns the stated stochastic-dominance order, rather than merely lexicographic worker comparisons.

4. Identification of the least element

First, \(T\) preserves the worker order. For a type \(a\), an initial segment that excludes \(h_a\) consists entirely of alternatives above \(h_a\), so its mass is unchanged by \(T\). An initial segment containing \(h_a\) has mass \(\mu_a\) after applying \(T\). Therefore

\[ u\succeq_W v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T(u)\succeq_W T(v). \tag{4} \]

By definition, \(y=T(x^F)\). The lemma proves \(y\in\mathcal S_0\), so this set is nonempty.

Now take any \(s\in\mathcal S_0\). Since it assigns no mass below prior employers, \(T(s)=s\). Combining (3) and (4),

\( s=T(s)\succeq_W T(x^F)=y. \)

Hence \(y\) is a least element of \(\mathcal S_0\). It is unique because initial-segment masses determine every row entry by taking consecutive differences.

5. Polynomial-time computation

Compute \(x^F\) by a side-optimal stable-allocation algorithm, then return \(T(x^F)\). No promised input requires reporting that a least element does not exist.

Including dummy vertices and edges, we may take

\( N=m+\tau+2, \qquad E\leq m\tau+m+\tau+1. \)

The direct implementation uses \(O(EN)\) arithmetic operations; constructing the instance and applying \(T\) take \(O(m\tau+m+\tau)\) additional operations.

To obtain a bit-complexity bound, multiply all masses and quotas by the product \(D\) of their input denominators. The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). All scaled capacities are integers, and the algorithm uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima of integer quantities bounded by the total scaled capacity. These quantities have \(O(L)\) bits. Schoolbook preprocessing gives the conservative overall bound

\( O\!\left( L^3+ (m\tau+m+\tau+1)(m+\tau+2)L \right) \)

bit operations, including conversion of the output back to rational masses. Empty sides can be handled directly in \(O(L)\) time.

Thus the representation and the computational question are both resolved.
