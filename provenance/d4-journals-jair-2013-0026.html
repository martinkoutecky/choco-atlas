The continuous mirror question, as graded

Fix a finite atom set \(U\), choose CO or ASO semantics, and let each type \(\theta\) be a complete module \(P_\theta=(G_\theta,S_\theta)\). Given finite-support rational distributions \(\mu,\nu\) over types, form \(P_\mu=(\bigcup_{\mu_\theta>0}G_\theta,\bigcup_{\mu_\theta>0}S_\theta)\) and \(P_\nu\) analogously. Decide whether \(\operatorname{Opt}(P_\mu\cup(\emptyset,S_R))=\operatorname{Opt}(P_\nu\cup(\emptyset,S_R))\) for every ranked selector \(S_R\), where \(\operatorname{Opt}\) is the set of preferred outcomes under the chosen semantics.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(U\) be an input atom set with \(m=|U|\), and choose CO or ASO semantics. A type is a module \(P_\theta=(G_\theta,S_\theta)\), with a finite propositional theory \(G_\theta\) and a finite set \(S_\theta\) of preference rules over \(U\); empty components and inconsistent generators are allowed.
A rule \(r\) has the form \(\phi_1>\cdots>\phi_k\xleftarrow{j}\psi\), where \(k,j\geq1\), ranks are binary integers, and all formulas are explicitly represented propositional formulas. For \(I\subseteq U\), set \(v_I(r)=\min\{\ell:I\models\phi_\ell\}\) when \(I\models\psi\) and some head formula holds; otherwise set \(v_I(r)=1\). Selector formulas are evaluated classically under both semantics.
For a selector \(S\), \(I>^S J\) holds when, at the smallest rank containing any rule with unequal satisfaction degrees, every rule weakly favors \(I\) and at least one strictly favors \(I\). If no degree differs, neither interpretation strictly dominates the other.
For \(P=(G,S)\), outcomes are the classical models of \(G\) under CO and its equilibrium models under ASO. Define \(\operatorname{Opt}(P)\) as the outcomes undominated by another outcome under \(>^S\). Module union is componentwise set union.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; masses sum to \(1\). Support size counts listed types, not possible types. For two societies, let \(\tau\) be the larger support size and \(L\) the total input bit length, including modules, ranks, and masses; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Given societies \(\mu,\nu\), define \(P_\mu=(\bigcup_{\mu_\theta>0}G_\theta,\bigcup_{\mu_\theta>0}S_\theta)\) and analogously \(P_\nu\). Decide whether \(\operatorname{Opt}(P_\mu\cup(\emptyset,S_R))=\operatorname{Opt}(P_\nu\cup(\emptyset,S_R))\) for every finite selector \(S_R\) over \(U\), with arbitrary positive integer ranks.

For each of CO and ASO semantics, is this distribution decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For explicit module pairs, this selector-extension equivalence problem is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete under CO and \(\Pi_2^P\)-complete under ASO, already with simple input selectors. These results use exactly the module semantics and unrestricted selector contexts defined above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted the finite atom set as part of the input, rather than a globally fixed constant, consistently with the anchor's complexity setting.
  • Normalized each distribution to total mass one and defined the joint support-size parameter as the larger of the two support sizes.
  • Interpreted a complete module as an explicitly supplied generator-selector pair, without imposing consistency, completeness of preferences, or nonempty components.
  • Specified arbitrary propositional generators, classical evaluation of selector formulas, positive integer ranks encoded in binary, and unrestricted finite selector contexts over the same atom set.
  • Defined L as the total encoding length, including the module descriptions as well as rational masses.

Exact classifications

Under polynomial-time many-one reductions, the distribution problems are:

| Semantics | Complexity | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| CO | \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete | It is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard if and only if \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\). |
| ASO | \(\Pi_2^P\)-complete | It is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. |

Thus the proposed “in \(\mathrm P\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard” dichotomy needs qualification for CO. Under polynomial-time Turing reductions, both problems are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

I prove these classifications and an explicit deterministic running-time bound below. The classifications are complete; the requested unconditional CO dichotomy under many-one reductions remains dependent on open complexity-class equalities.

1. Societies and explicit modules are polynomially interreducible

Given societies \(\mu,\nu\), form their aggregate modules \(P_\mu,P_\nu\) by taking the prescribed unions. Their combined representation has length \(O(L)\), because every constituent formula and rule already occurs in the input. Duplicate elimination, if needed, takes \(O(L^2)\) time by direct comparisons.

The resulting explicit-module instance asks exactly the same question as the society instance. The atom set remains \(U\), and masses have no further role once the positive support is specified.

Conversely, given any explicit modules \(P,Q\), construct two singleton societies:

  • \(\mu\) has the single type \(P\), with mass \(1\);
  • \(\nu\) has the single type \(Q\), with mass \(1\).

These are valid societies, their aggregates are \(P,Q\), and their support sizes are both \(1\). The transformation has linear output length and preserves the answer.

Consequently, for each semantics, the society problem and the supplied explicit-module problem are polynomial-time many-one equivalent. Applying the completeness results stated in the question proves:

  • CO is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete.
  • ASO is \(\Pi_2^P\)-complete.

Both hardness results already hold for \(\tau=1\), with the same simple input selectors as in the supplied results.

2. A finite characterization of all selector extensions

This section supplies a direct algorithm without enumerating selector contexts.

For a selector \(S\) and distinct interpretations \(I,J\), define \(d_S(I,J)\) as the smallest rank at which some rule has unequal degrees on \(I,J\). Set \(d_S(I,J)=\infty\) if no such rule exists.

When \(d_S(I,J)\) is finite, define \(T_S(I,J)\) to contain:

  • \(+\), if some rule at that rank satisfies \(v_I(r)<v_J(r)\);
  • \(-\), if some rule at that rank satisfies \(v_I(r)>v_J(r)\).

Thus \(T_S(I,J)\) is one of \(\{+\},\{-\},\{+,-\}\). When \(d_S(I,J)=\infty\), set \(T_S(I,J)=\emptyset\).

Write \(F_S(I,J)=(d_S(I,J),T_S(I,J))\).

Proposition. Let \(P=(G_P,S_P)\) and \(Q=(G_Q,S_Q)\), using either CO or ASO semantics. They have equal optimal outcomes under every selector extension if and only if:

  • their outcome sets are equal, say to \(D\);
  • \(F_{S_P}(I,J)=F_{S_Q}(I,J)\) for every distinct \(I,J\in D\).

Sufficiency

Fix any extension \(R\) and any distinct \(I,J\in D\).

At each rank, adjoining rules combines the signs contributed by the original selector and by \(R\). Let \(d\) be the original first differing rank, and let \(e\) be the extension’s first differing rank.

If \(e<d\), the extension determines the comparison. If \(d<e\), the original selector determines it. If \(d=e\), their sign sets are united. The same reasoning applies when either rank is \(\infty\).

Therefore the original fingerprint and \(R\) completely determine the extended comparison. Equal fingerprints imply identical strict-dominance relations on \(D\). An interpretation is optimal precisely when no member of \(D\) strictly dominates it, so the optimal sets are equal.

Necessity of equal outcome sets

For every atom \(a\in U\), put both rules \(a>\neg a\xleftarrow{1}\top\) and \(\neg a>a\xleftarrow{1}\top\) into an extension \(R_0\).

Any distinct interpretations differ on some atom. Its two rules contribute opposite signs at rank \(1\), making the interpretations incomparable regardless of the original selector. Hence every outcome is optimal after adding \(R_0\).

Equality under this extension forces equality of the outcome sets. When \(U=\emptyset\), there is at most one interpretation, so every outcome is already optimal and the same conclusion follows.

Necessity of equal fingerprints

Suppose the common outcome set is \(D\), and fix distinct \(I,J\in D\).

For \(K\subseteq U\), let \(\delta_K\) be the conjunction specifying exactly \(K\): it contains \(a\) for \(a\in K\) and \(\neg a\) for \(a\notin K\). Set \(A=\delta_I\lor\delta_J\).

Add the rank-\(1\) rules \(A>\neg A\xleftarrow{1}\top\) and \(\neg A>A\xleftarrow{1}\top\).

These rules give identical degrees to \(I,J\), so they do not alter their comparison. Between either of them and any interpretation outside \(\{I,J\}\), however, they contribute opposite signs. Consequently, no outside outcome can dominate \(I\) or \(J\), even after further rules are added. Their optimality is therefore determined solely by their mutual comparison.

Suppose their fingerprints differ.

If their first differing ranks coincide, they are finite and their sign sets differ. The three sign sets yield, respectively, the optimal subsets \(\{I\}\), \(\{J\}\), and \(\{I,J\}\) within this isolated pair. Thus the extended optimal sets differ.

Otherwise, interchange \(P,Q\) if necessary so that \(d_{S_P}(I,J)<d_{S_Q}(I,J)\). Write \(d=d_{S_P}(I,J)\).

  • If \(T_{S_P}(I,J)=\{+\}\), add \(\delta_J>\neg\delta_J\xleftarrow{d}\top\).
  • If \(T_{S_P}(I,J)=\{-\}\) or \(\{+,-\}\), add \(\delta_I>\neg\delta_I\xleftarrow{d}\top\).

In the first case, the added rule contributes \(-\); in the other cases it contributes \(+\). Under \(P\), the resulting first-rank sign set is \(\{+,-\}\), so both interpretations are optimal within the pair. Under \(Q\), the added rule is the first differing rule and creates strict dominance, so only one is optimal.

The isolating rules exclude interference from other outcomes. Therefore differing fingerprints contradict equivalence under every selector extension. This proves the proposition.

3. Explicit running times in \(m,\tau,L\)

The proposition gives a deterministic algorithm with running time \(O(\tau+4^mL^2)\) under either semantics. Here is a conservative bit-operation accounting.

First aggregate the modules in \(O(\tau+L^2)\) time.

Under CO, enumerate the \(2^m\) interpretations and evaluate the generators. This takes \(O(2^mL^2)\) time.

Under ASO, use the defining equilibrium-model test: \(I\) must satisfy the theory at the total here-and-there interpretation \((I,I)\), and no \((H,I)\) with \(H\subsetneq I\) may satisfy it. There are \(3^m\) pairs with \(H\subseteq I\), because each atom is absent from \(I\), belongs to \(I\setminus H\), or belongs to \(H\). Evaluating an explicitly represented formula at its two worlds takes polynomial time, giving the conservative bound \(O(3^mL^2)\).

Compare the resulting outcome sets. If they agree, examine all ordered pairs of distinct outcomes. There are fewer than \(4^m\) such pairs. For each, scan the input selectors to compute and compare their fingerprints, in \(O(L^2)\) time.

Ranks are compared as binary integers; the algorithm never iterates through every integer up to a rank. Thus their numerical magnitude does not introduce an additional exponential factor.

The resulting bound is polynomial in the input length for fixed \(m\), with no exponential dependence on \(\tau\).

4. What “\(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard” implies here

Many-one reductions

For CO, suppose the problem were \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Because it belongs to \(\mathrm{coNP}\), closure of \(\mathrm{coNP}\) under polynomial-time many-one reductions would imply \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{coNP}\). Taking complements gives the reverse inclusion. Hence \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

Conversely, if \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\), its proved \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-completeness makes it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Therefore CO is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard if and only if \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

Likewise, its membership in \(\mathrm P\) is equivalent to \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\): membership would put every \(\mathrm{coNP}\) language, and therefore every \(\mathrm{NP}\) language by complementation, in \(\mathrm P\); the converse follows immediately from \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

For ASO, \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\Pi_2^P\): an \(\mathrm{NP}\) existential predicate can be written with a preceding universal quantifier whose variable is ignored. Its \(\Pi_2^P\)-completeness therefore implies \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. A polynomial-time algorithm would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Turing reductions

CO is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. Given an \(\mathrm{NP}\) language \(B\), its complement belongs to \(\mathrm{coNP}\) and reduces many-one to the CO problem. Query that instance and negate the answer to decide membership in \(B\).

ASO is already hard under many-one reductions, hence also under Turing reductions.

The remaining open issue concerns only the requested unconditional CO classification under the many-one convention; the society encoding itself introduces no additional complexity.
