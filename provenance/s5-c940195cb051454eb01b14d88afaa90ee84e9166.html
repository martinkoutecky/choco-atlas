The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) for \(\tau\) types, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), a symmetric matrix \(A\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{\tau\times\tau}\), and a rational threshold \(r\), partition an atomless population into teams with type-mass vectors \(x^k\geq 0\), allowing leftover mass to be dispersed into infinitesimal teams. A team’s welfare is \(\frac12\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_i^kx_j^k\); decide whether the supremum of total welfare is at least \(r\). Determine the exact and additive-\(\epsilon\) computational complexity in \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(\epsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Thus \(\tau\) counts supported types, not all possible types.
Input also includes a symmetric matrix \(A\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{\tau\times\tau}\) and a binary-rational threshold \(r\). Diagonal entries describe interactions between distinct individuals of the same type and may have any of the three values.
Types are atomless and may be split freely. A feasible allocation consists of any finite number \(K\geq0\) of teams with real vectors \(x^k\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{\tau}\) satisfying \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}x_i^k\leq\mu_i\) for every type. Residual mass is dispersed into infinitesimal teams and contributes zero welfare.
Define \(\operatorname{OPT}=\sup_{K,x}\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\tau}A_{ij}x_i^kx_j^k\). Exact decision asks whether \(\operatorname{OPT}\geq r\); additive approximation, given binary-rational \(0<\epsilon<1\), computes a rational \(\widehat v\) with \(|\widehat v-\operatorname{OPT}|\leq\epsilon\).
Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including \(\epsilon\) when present. There is no separate ground-set parameter; the interaction dimension is \(m=\tau\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and \(1/\epsilon\).

Is exact decision in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
Does additive approximation admit an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even when \(1/\epsilon\) is polynomially bounded in \(L\)?

For finite agents on an undirected graph with edge weights in \(\{-1,+1\}\), deciding whether a partition's total internal-edge weight can reach a rational threshold is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.5248)
No result settling this exact atomless formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Zero-mass entries are discarded so that tau is precisely the positive-mass support size.
  • Infinitesimal leftover teams are formalized as residual mass contributing zero welfare; the supremum ranges over arbitrarily many finite teams, with no bound on their number.
  • Team masses may be real, rather than being restricted to binary rationals.
  • Diagonal entries retain the graded question's domain and represent interactions between distinct individuals sharing a type.
  • Additive approximation means absolute error in the optimal value, with binary-rational epsilon strictly between zero and one; its polynomial-time target includes dependence on 1/epsilon.
  • There is no independent parameter m in this model, so m denotes the interaction dimension and equals tau.

Result

Exact decision is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Additive approximation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even when \(\epsilon^{-1}=O(L)\). Consequently, neither problem admits the requested polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Both statements hold with equal positive dyadic masses and every diagonal entry equal to \(+1\). The key is to prove that free splitting cannot improve welfare on this subclass.

1. An unsplitting lemma

Lemma. Suppose \(A_{ii}=+1\) for every supported type. Then \(\operatorname{OPT}\) is attained by an allocation that assigns each type’s entire mass to one team and disperses no mass. In particular,

\[ \operatorname{OPT} = \max_{\mathcal P} \left[ \frac12\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i^2 + \sum_{C\in\mathcal P} \sum_{\substack{i<j\\i,j\in C}} A_{ij}\mu_i\mu_j \right], \]

where \(\mathcal P\) ranges over partitions of the supported types.

Proof. Start with any feasible finite allocation having \(K\geq1\) teams. Fix a type \(i\), hold all other types’ allocations fixed, and write \(z_k=x_i^k\). Its feasible choices form the simplex

\[ D_i=\left\{z\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{K}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_k\leq\mu_i\right\}. \]

Because \(A\) is symmetric and \(A_{ii}=1\), welfare as a function of \(z\) is

\[ F_i(z)=C+\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_k^2+\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_kz_k, \qquad b_k=\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij}x_j^k, \]

with \(C\) independent of \(z\). Thus \(F_i\) is convex.

Every \(z\in D_i\) is a convex combination of the vertices \(0,\mu_i e_1,\ldots,\mu_i e_K\): its coefficient at \(\mu_i e_k\) is \(z_k/\mu_i\), and its coefficient at \(0\) is \(1-\sum_kz_k/\mu_i\). Convexity therefore implies that some vertex has welfare at least \(F_i(z)\).

Replace type \(i\)’s allocation by such a vertex. This preserves feasibility and does not decrease welfare. Repeat for every type. Afterwards, each type is either wholly assigned to one team or wholly dispersed.

For each dispersed type, create a separate singleton-type team. Its welfare contribution is \(\mu_i^2/2>0\), and it introduces no interactions with existing teams. Hence the resulting allocation assigns all masses without splitting and has welfare at least that of the original allocation. If \(K=0\), singleton-type teams directly provide the same conclusion.

Conversely, every partition allocation is feasible. There are finitely many partitions, and each uses at most \(\tau\) teams. Their maximum therefore equals the supremum over all feasible finite allocations. Expanding their welfare gives the displayed formula. \(\square\)

2. Exact-decision hardness

We use the stated finite-agent problem: given a simple undirected graph with edge weights in \(\{-1,+1\}\) and a rational threshold, decide whether some partition has internal-edge weight at least that threshold. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is also stated as Theorem 4 of [Bachrach, Kohli, Kolmogorov, and Zadimoghaddam](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.5248).

Let the source graph have \(n\geq1\) vertices. For a partition \(\mathcal Q\), define

\[ W(\mathcal Q)= \sum_{C\in\mathcal Q} \sum_{\substack{\{i,j\}\in E\\i,j\in C}}w_{ij}, \qquad W^*=\max_{\mathcal Q}W(\mathcal Q). \]

Every \(W(\mathcal Q)\) is an integer. Consequently, a rational source threshold \(s\) can be replaced by the integer \(q=\lceil s\rceil\).

Let \(\tau\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(\tau\geq n\). Construct the society as follows:

  • There is one supported type for each graph vertex and \(\tau-n\) additional supported types.
  • Every type has mass \(\mu_i=1/\tau\).
  • Every diagonal entry is \(A_{ii}=+1\).
  • Between distinct original types, set \(A_{ij}=w_{ij}\) for graph edges and \(A_{ij}=0\) for nonedges.
  • All off-diagonal entries involving an additional type are zero.

These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. Every additional type is therefore counted in the support parameter \(\tau\), and the interaction dimension is exactly \(m=\tau\).

By the lemma, it suffices to consider partitions of types. Their diagonal contribution is the constant

\[ c=\frac12\tau\left(\frac1\tau\right)^2=\frac1{2\tau}. \]

Their off-diagonal contribution is \(W(\mathcal Q)/\tau^2\). Restricting a type partition to original vertices gives a source partition; conversely, any source partition extends by placing additional types in singleton teams. Hence

\[ \operatorname{OPT}=\frac1{2\tau}+\frac{W^*}{\tau^2}. \]

Choose the target threshold

\[ r=\frac1{2\tau}+\frac q{\tau^2}. \]

It is dyadic, and the identity above proves

\[ \operatorname{OPT}\geq r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W^*\geq q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad W^*\geq s. \]

Since \(\tau<2n\), this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. Thus exact decision is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

Indeed, the restriction with all diagonal entries equal to \(+1\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete: the lemma supplies a partition certificate with polynomial encoding length, whose rational welfare can be checked in polynomial time. No membership claim for unrestricted diagonals is needed here.

3. Hardness of additive approximation

Use the same constructed society and set

\[ \epsilon=\frac1{4\tau^2}. \]

This is a positive dyadic rational smaller than one. Suppose an approximation algorithm returns a rational \(\widehat v\) satisfying \(|\widehat v-\operatorname{OPT}|\leq\epsilon\). Define

\[ \widehat W=\tau^2\widehat v-\frac{\tau}{2}. \]

The formula for \(\operatorname{OPT}\) implies

\[ |\widehat W-W^*| = \tau^2|\widehat v-\operatorname{OPT}| \leq\frac14. \]

Since \(W^*\) is an integer, it is the unique nearest integer to \(\widehat W\). Thus one approximation call, followed by exact rational arithmetic and rounding, recovers \(W^*\) and decides the source instance.

Moreover,

\[ \epsilon^{-1}=4\tau^2=4m^2. \]

The input explicitly contains the \(\tau\times\tau\) matrix, so its bit length satisfies \(L=\Omega(\tau^2)\). Therefore \(\epsilon^{-1}=O(L)\) on these instances. This proves approximation hardness even under the requested polynomial bound on inverse accuracy.

4. Running-time consequences

The reductions construct a matrix with \(m=\tau<2n\), and take polynomial time in the source input length. The approximation reduction uses one oracle call and polynomial-time rational postprocessing.

An exact algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or an additive approximation algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\epsilon^{-1}\), would therefore yield a polynomial-time algorithm for the finite-agent \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem. On the approximation instances, both \(m=\tau\) and \(\epsilon^{-1}=O(L)\), so the requested polynomial bound collapses to a polynomial in \(L\).
