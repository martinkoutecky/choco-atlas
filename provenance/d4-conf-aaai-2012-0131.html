The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given old alternatives \(C\), possible additions \(C'\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\cup C'\), a favored alternative \(c\in C\), a quota \(k\), and the rule and control mode, is there a set \(S\subseteq C'\) with \(|S|\le k\) such that, after restricting each ranking to \(C\cup S\), \(c\) is the unique winner in the constructive case or is no longer the unique winner in the destructive case?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given disjoint finite sets of old alternatives \(C\ne\varnothing\) and possible additions \(C'\), let \(m=|C\cup C'|\). The input includes \(c\in C\), an integer quota \(0\le k\le |C'|\), a rule (Schulze or ranked pairs), and a mode (constructive or destructive).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) strict complete rankings of \(C\cup C'\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
For distinct alternatives define \(D(a,b)=\mu(a\succ b)-\mu(b\succ a)\). On any retained set \(A\), use the complete directed graph with edge weights \(D(a,b)\), including zero and negative weights.
For Schulze, let \(B_A(a,b)\) be the maximum, over simple directed paths from \(a\) to \(b\) in \(A\), of the minimum edge weight. Its winner set is \(W(A)=\{a\in A: B_A(a,b)\ge B_A(b,a)\text{ for every }b\in A\setminus\{a\}\}\).
For ranked pairs, process all ordered pairs of distinct retained alternatives in decreasing order of \(D(a,b)\), breaking ties lexicographically by fixed alternative labels. Starting with an empty directed graph, lock each edge unless it creates a directed cycle; \(W(A)\) is the singleton containing the final graph's unique source. The tie order is restricted from the full alternative set and cannot be changed by the controller.
A feasible action selects \(S\subseteq C'\) with \(|S|\le k\), restricts every ranking to \(A=C\cup S\), and preserves its mass. Success means \(W(A)=\{c\}\) constructively and \(W(A)\ne\{c\}\) destructively.

For each of the four rule–mode combinations, is deciding whether a successful action exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

With explicitly listed unweighted voters, constructive addition control is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for both rules, and destructive addition control is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for ranked pairs ([Parkes–Xia, 2012](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/8258/8258-13-11786-1-2-20201228.pdf)).
Destructive addition control for discrete Schulze elections is still listed as open in [Maushagen et al., 2024](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0326.pdf). No result for exactly the rational-mass support model above was located.

The main result proved below is the polynomial-time algorithm for destructive Schulze addition. Its essential step is to replace an initially witnessing opponent by the last vertex on a witnessing path that is unreachable from $c$ above a chosen threshold.

The 2024 paper lists destructive Schulze addition as open in both winner models. The argument below supplies an algorithm for the particular unique-winner destruction objective specified here. [Maushagen et al., Section 6](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0326.pdf)

All arguments include zero and negative margins. If $m=1$, the sole alternative is always the unique winner, so constructive control succeeds and destructive control fails.

2. Exact preprocessing and verification

Put $U=C\cup C'$ and
$$ h=\left\lceil\log_2(m+1)\right\rceil. $$

Index alternatives in their fixed label order. This preserves the prescribed lexicographic tie-breaking.

2.1. Computing margins

Write the $\tau$ input masses as $p_i/q_i$. Define
$$ Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i, \qquad w_i=p_i(Q/q_i). $$

Then $w_i$ are positive integers and
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i=Q. $$

For distinct $a,b\in U$, let $\varepsilon_i(a,b)$ equal $1$ if ranking $i$ places $a$ above $b$, and $-1$ otherwise. Thus
$$ M(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\varepsilon_i(a,b), \qquad D(a,b)=\frac{M(a,b)}{Q}. $$

The integers $Q,w_i,M(a,b)$ have $O(L)$ bits: the bit length of $Q$ is bounded by the sum of the denominator bit lengths, while $w_i\le Q$ and $|M(a,b)|\le Q$.

Restricting a ranking does not change a retained pairwise comparison. Therefore this matrix serves every feasible action.

Using schoolbook arithmetic, a conservative preprocessing bound, including sorting margin values, is
$$ T_{\mathrm{pre}} = O\!\left( \tau L^2+\tau m^2L+m^2Lh \right). $$

2.2. Compressing weights without changing outcomes

Let the distinct positive margin magnitudes be
$$ 0<\delta_1<\cdots<\delta_q. $$
There are at most $\binom m2$ of them. On the occurring margin values, define
$$ f(0)=0, \qquad f(\delta_j)=2j, \qquad f(-\delta_j)=-2j. $$

This is strictly increasing and preserves antisymmetry. Put
$$ F(a,b)=f(D(a,b)). $$

For every simple path $P$,
$$ \min_{e\in P}F(e) = f\!\left(\min_{e\in P}D(e)\right). $$
Because there are finitely many simple paths between distinct vertices,
$$ B_A^F(a,b)=f(B_A^D(a,b)). $$
Consequently, all Schulze winner comparisons, including equalities, are preserved on every retained set.

For ranked pairs, the transformation preserves the ordering and equality classes of edge weights. Labels remain unchanged, so the full processing order is unchanged. Induction over that order shows that every locking decision is unchanged.

Thus, after preprocessing, both rules can use integer weights with $O(h)$ bits.

2.3. Verifying an action

For Schulze, max–min Floyd–Warshall computes strongest-path values. Initialize off-diagonal entries with the edge weights, diagonal entries with a formal $+\infty$, and apply
$$ B(a,b)\leftarrow \max\{B(a,b),\min\{B(a,z),B(z,b)\}\}. $$

The recurrence is valid with negative weights. Concatenation produces walks, and deleting a cycle cannot decrease a walk’s minimum edge weight. Hence allowing walks gives the same maximum bottleneck as allowing simple paths between distinct endpoints.

Verification takes
$$ O(m^3h) $$
bit operations after preprocessing.

For ranked pairs, filter the precomputed edge order to the retained alternatives and maintain the locked graph’s transitive closure. An edge $a\to b$ creates a cycle exactly when $b$ already reaches $a$. An accepted insertion updates the closure in $O(m^2)$ table operations, giving the conservative verification bound
$$ O(m^4h). $$

The final acyclic graph has a unique source. For every pair of distinct vertices, processing an edge either establishes reachability in its direction or rejects it because reverse reachability already exists. Thus every pair is comparable by final reachability. Two distinct sources would contradict this comparability, and every nonempty finite acyclic graph has a source.

A certificate lists at most $k$ additions and uses $O(kh)$ bits. Therefore all four problems belong to $\mathrm{NP}$.

3. Unique Schulze victory and strict path dominance

We first prove the criterion needed by the destructive algorithm.

Lemma. For every retained set $A$ containing $c$,
$$ W(A)=\{c\} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B_A(c,d)>B_A(d,c) \text{ for every }d\in A\setminus\{c\}. $$

Proof. For distinct $a,b,z\in A$, concatenating strongest paths and removing cycles gives
$$ B_A(a,z)\ge \min\{B_A(a,b),B_A(b,z)\}. $$

Define $a\triangleright b$ when
$$ B_A(a,b)>B_A(b,a). $$
This relation is asymmetric. We show it is transitive.

Suppose $a\triangleright b$ and $b\triangleright z$. These assumptions exclude $z=a$. Write
$$ x=B_A(a,b), \qquad y=B_A(b,z), \qquad t=B_A(z,a). $$

If $t\ge\min\{x,y\}$, then:

  • When $x\le y$,

$$ B_A(b,a)\ge\min\{y,t\}\ge x, $$
contradicting $a\triangleright b$.

  • When $y\le x$,

$$ B_A(z,b)\ge\min\{t,x\}\ge y, $$
contradicting $b\triangleright z$.

Hence
$$ B_A(z,a)<\min\{x,y\}\le B_A(a,z), $$
which proves transitivity.

By definition, Schulze winners are exactly the vertices having no incoming $\triangleright$ relation. In a finite transitive asymmetric relation, every dominated vertex is dominated by an undominated vertex. Indeed, repeatedly choose an incoming dominator. Repetition would, by transitivity, contradict asymmetry, so the process terminates; transitivity makes its terminal vertex dominate the initial vertex.

If $c$ is the sole undominated vertex, this observation shows that $c\triangleright d$ for every $d\ne c$. Conversely, if $c\triangleright d$ for every $d\ne c$, then $c$ is undominated and every other vertex is dominated. This proves the lemma. $\square$

Negating its conclusion gives
$$ W(A)\ne\{c\} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists d\in A\setminus\{c\}: B_A(d,c)\ge B_A(c,d). $$

4. A polynomial algorithm for destructive Schulze addition

4.1. Threshold graphs

For a vertex set $X$ and a threshold $t$, define:

  • $G_{>t}(X)$ to contain precisely the edges with $D(a,b)>t$;
  • $G_{\ge t}(X)$ to contain precisely the edges with $D(a,b)\ge t$.

Reachability includes a length-zero path from a vertex to itself.

For distinct endpoints, finiteness of the simple-path set gives
$$ B_X(a,b)>t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\text{ is reachable from }a\text{ in }G_{>t}(X), $$
and likewise with $\ge t$.

Let $\mathcal T$ be the set of all occurring edge weights:
$$ \mathcal T=\{D(a,b):a,b\in U,\ a\ne b\}. $$
Its size is at most $m(m-1)$.

4.2. Safe sets for a threshold and opponent

Fix $t\in\mathcal T$ and $d\in U\setminus\{c\}$. Define
$$ V_0=C\cup\{d\} $$
and
$$ R_{t,d} = \{r\in V_0: d\text{ is reachable from }r \text{ in }G_{>t}(V_0)\}. $$
In particular, $d\in R_{t,d}$.

If $c\in R_{t,d}$, discard this pair $(t,d)$.

Otherwise, define the allowable additional alternatives
$$ E_{t,d} = \left\{ x\in C'\setminus\{d\}: D(x,r)\le t \text{ for every }r\in R_{t,d} \right\}, $$
and put
$$ V_{t,d}=V_0\cup E_{t,d}. $$

Safe-set property. There is no path from $c$ to $d$ in $G_{>t}(V_{t,d})$.

Proof. No vertex of $V_0\setminus R_{t,d}$ has an edge of weight greater than $t$ into $R_{t,d}$. Such an edge, followed by a path to $d$, would put that vertex in $R_{t,d}$.

By definition, no vertex of $E_{t,d}$ has an edge of weight greater than $t$ into $R_{t,d}$ either.

Thus $G_{>t}(V_{t,d})$ has no edge entering $R_{t,d}$ from its complement. Since $c\notin R_{t,d}$ and $d\in R_{t,d}$, the claimed path cannot exist. $\square$

4.3. The algorithm

For every pair $(t,d)$ not discarded above, find a minimum-cost path from $d$ to $c$ in
$$ G_{\ge t}(V_{t,d}). $$

Assign vertex cost $0$ to old alternatives and $1$ to possible additions. Specifically, initialize the distance at $d$ as
$$ \mathbf 1_{\{d\in C'\}}, $$
and assign an edge $a\to b$ cost
$$ \mathbf 1_{\{b\in C'\}}. $$

A simple path’s cost is exactly its number of additional alternatives. All costs are nonnegative, so a minimum-cost walk can be made simple by deleting cycles without increasing cost. Standard shortest-path algorithms therefore compute the required minimum.

Return yes if any such minimum is at most $k$; otherwise return no.

4.4. Soundness

Suppose the algorithm finds a simple path $P$ of cost at most $k$ for $(t,d)$. Select
$$ S=V(P)\cap C', \qquad A=C\cup S. $$
Then $|S|\le k$, and $P$ proves
$$ B_A(d,c)\ge t. $$

Also $A\subseteq V_{t,d}$. By the safe-set property, there is no path from $c$ to $d$ in $G_{>t}(A)$. Therefore
$$ B_A(c,d)\le t. $$

Consequently,
$$ B_A(d,c)\ge t\ge B_A(c,d), $$
and the lemma in Section 3 shows that $W(A)\ne\{c\}$. Thus every accepted instance has a successful feasible action.

4.5. Completeness: changing the witnessing opponent

Suppose a feasible action $S$ succeeds, and write
$$ A=C\cup S. $$
By Section 3, some $d_0\in A\setminus\{c\}$ satisfies
$$ B_A(d_0,c)\ge B_A(c,d_0). $$

Set
$$ t=B_A(d_0,c). $$
Choose a simple path $P$ from $d_0$ to $c$ attaining this value. Its minimum edge weight is $t$, so $t\in\mathcal T$, and every edge of $P$ has weight at least $t$.

Let
$$ H= \{v\in A: v\text{ is reachable from }c \text{ in }G_{>t}(A)\}. $$

We have $c\in H$. On the other hand, $d_0\notin H$, because otherwise
$$ B_A(c,d_0)>t=B_A(d_0,c), $$
contradicting the choice of $d_0$.

Travel along $P$ from $d_0$ toward $c$, and let $d$ be the last vertex outside $H$. Let $P^\star$ be the suffix of $P$ beginning at $d$. Every vertex of $P^\star$ after $d$ belongs to $H$.

We prove that the algorithm accepts at the pair $(t,d)$.

First, $C\cup\{d\}\subseteq A$. If $c$ belonged to $R_{t,d}$, the corresponding path within $C\cup\{d\}$ would also be present in $A$, putting $d$ in $H$. Therefore
$$ c\notin R_{t,d}. $$
This pair is not discarded.

Next, consider any additional alternative $x$ on $P^\star$ other than $d$. Then $x\in H$. Suppose there were $r\in R_{t,d}$ with
$$ D(x,r)>t. $$
There would then be:

  • a path from $c$ to $x$ in $G_{>t}(A)$;
  • the edge $x\to r$ of weight greater than $t$;
  • a path from $r$ to $d$ in $G_{>t}(C\cup\{d\})$.

Their concatenation establishes reachability from $c$ to $d$ in $G_{>t}(A)$, contradicting $d\notin H$. Hence
$$ D(x,r)\le t \text{ for every }r\in R_{t,d}, $$
so $x\in E_{t,d}$.

All old alternatives are included in $V_{t,d}$, as is $d$. Thus the entire suffix $P^\star$ lies in $V_{t,d}$. Its edges have weight at least $t$, and its number of additional alternatives is at most
$$ |S|\le k. $$
The shortest-path calculation therefore finds cost at most $k$.

This proves completeness.

5. Running times in $m,\tau,L,k$

After the preprocessing of Section 2, each threshold and opponent pair requires:

  • reverse reachability within $C\cup\{d\}$;
  • checking the defining inequalities for allowable additions;
  • a shortest-path computation on at most $m$ vertices.

Each operation takes $O(m^2h)$ bit operations. For the shortest-path computation, ordinary Dijkstra with linear scanning suffices; finite relevant distances are at most $m$.

There are $O(m^2)$ thresholds and at most $m-1$ opponents. Hence destructive Schulze addition is decidable in
$$ O\!\left( \tau L^2+\tau m^2L+m^2Lh+m^5h \right) $$
bit operations.

There is no exponential dependence on $k$. Indeed, minimizing the path cost over all processed pairs computes the minimum number of additions needed for destructive success; the algorithm then compares that minimum with $k$.

For the other three combinations, exact enumeration gives explicit upper bounds. Put
$$ H_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{|C'|}{j}. $$
Then the running times are
$$ O(T_{\mathrm{pre}}+H_km^3h) $$
for constructive Schulze addition, and
$$ O(T_{\mathrm{pre}}+H_km^4h) $$
for either ranked-pairs mode.

Since
$$ H_k\le(k+1)m^k, $$
all combinations are polynomial-time solvable for fixed $k$. The hardness classifications concern unrestricted input quota.

6. Transferring the three hardness results

The known theorem used here is that, with explicitly listed unweighted strict-ranking voters, constructive alternative addition is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard for Schulze and ranked pairs, and destructive alternative addition is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard for ranked pairs. Its Schulze objective is unique victory; its ranked-pairs results apply to any fixed tie-breaking mechanism. The paper explicitly permits negative Schulze path weights and defines the same addition quota and destructive objective used here. [Parkes–Xia, Theorem 3 and definitions](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/8258/8258-13-11786-1-2-20201228.pdf)

To transfer this theorem, take an explicitly listed electorate with $n\ge1$ voters. Group identical rankings. If ranking $R$ occurs $t_R$ times, give it mass
$$ \mu(R)=\frac{t_R}{n}. $$

This transformation is polynomial-time: there are at most $n$ support rankings, and each numerator and denominator has $O(\log(n+1))$ bits. For every pair,
$$ D_{\mathrm{support}}(a,b) = \frac{D_{\mathrm{unweighted}}(a,b)}{n}. $$

For Schulze, positive scaling multiplies every path strength and strongest-path value by the same factor, preserving the entire winner set.

For ranked pairs, positive scaling preserves the complete edge order, including ties. Labels are unchanged. Induction over the processed edges therefore preserves the locked graph and its source.

Both statements hold for every retained set $C\cup S$. Consequently, the transformation preserves success of every feasible action, in either mode.

The three cited hardness results therefore apply to the rational-support model. Together with the $\mathrm{NP}$ membership proved in Section 2, they establish the three $\mathrm{NP}$-completeness entries in the table.

7. Equivalence with polynomially sized unweighted electorates

For completeness, the reverse transformation from the notes also holds, with the all-zero case made explicit.

Use the compressed even antisymmetric matrix $F$ from Section 2. For an unordered pair oriented so that
$$ F(a,b)=2j>0, $$
list the remaining alternatives as $r_1,\ldots,r_{m-2}$. Introduce $j$ copies of each ranking
$$ a\succ b\succ r_1\succ\cdots\succ r_{m-2} $$
and
$$ r_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ r_1\succ a\succ b. $$

One pair of these rankings contributes margin $2$ to $a$ against $b$. Every other contribution cancels: the remaining alternatives reverse their mutual order, and each is below $a,b$ in one ranking and above them in the other.

Summing these gadgets realizes exactly $F$. Write $u=\binom m2$. If $F$ is nonzero, the actual number of constructed voters is
$$ n_F= \sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq U}|F(a,b)| \le 2u^2. $$

If $F$ is identically zero, instead use an arbitrary ranking and its reverse, and explicitly set
$$ n_F=2. $$
This includes $m=1$.

Thus the electorate has polynomial size, with encoding length
$$ O(m^5h), $$
and construction time
$$ O(T_{\mathrm{pre}}+m^5h). $$

Section 2 proved that $F$ preserves both rules on every retained set. Keeping $C,C',c,k$, the rule, and the mode unchanged therefore gives a polynomial-time many-one reduction back to explicitly listed unweighted elections.

Accordingly, the classification above also applies to the corresponding unweighted problems. In particular, the destructive Schulze algorithm does not rely on rational masses or support compression.
