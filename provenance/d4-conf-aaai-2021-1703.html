The Price of Connectivity in Fair Division (d4-conf-aaai-2021-1703)

Answer: \(\mathrm{NP\text{-}hard}\)

The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 3.16 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a star \(S_m\), a finite set of additive valuation types \(T\), a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and per-location capacities \(q_v\), find the maximum \(\alpha\) for which there is a mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) over connected integral bundles \(B\subseteq V(S_m)\), satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B\ni v}x_{t,B}=q_v\), and \(u_t(B)\ge \alpha\,\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(q)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S_m\) be a star, \(T\) a finite set of types with nonnegative additive valuations \(u_t\), and \(\mu\) a probability distribution on \(T\). All input numbers are rational. Let \(q_v\) be the required mass using vertex \(v\); the empty bundle is permitted.
Define \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(q)\) as the largest \(z\) for which masses \(y_B\geq 0\) over all subsets \(B\subseteq V(S_m)\) satisfy \(\sum_B y_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni v}y_B=q_v\), and \(u_t(B)\geq z\) whenever \(y_B>0\). These benchmark bundles need not be connected.
A feasible allocation uses masses \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\) over connected bundles \(B\), with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B\ni v}x_{t,B}=q_v\), and \(u_t(B)\geq\alpha\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(q)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\).
Is deciding, for a rational \(a\), whether the maximum feasible \(\alpha\) is at least \(a\) in P, or is it NP-hard?
For finite agents on a star, a polynomial-time algorithm guarantees each agent \(1/(m-n+1)\) of her unrestricted MMS when \(m\geq n\), and this worst-case factor is tight. That guarantee does not classify the exact mass-allocation problem above.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is NP-hard, even with two equally likely types, center mass \(1\), every leaf mass \(1/2\), and the fixed threshold \(a=3/2\). The reduction uses explicitly known benchmarks.

This uses the definition as stated, which does not restrict \(\alpha\) to be at most \(1\).

1. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the standard NP-complete problem PARTITION: given positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\), encoded in binary, decide whether some \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in I}w_i=W/2\), where \(W=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\).

Construct a star with center \(c\), item leaves \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\), and two additional leaves \(p_1,p_2\). Set \(q_c=1\) and \(q_{v_i}=q_{p_1}=q_{p_2}=1/2\).

There are two types, with \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\). Their additive valuations are defined by:

  • \(u_t(c)=0\) for both types.
  • \(u_t(v_i)=w_i\) for both types and every item leaf.
  • \(u_1(p_1)=u_2(p_2)=W\).
  • \(u_1(p_2)=u_2(p_1)=0\).

Set \(a=3/2\). The construction has polynomial size and uses nonnegative integer valuations and rational masses.

2. Both benchmarks equal \(W\)

Fix a type \(t\in\{1,2\}\).

For a lower bound, give mass \(1/2\) to each of the bundles \(\{c,p_1,p_2\}\) and \(\{c,v_1,\ldots,v_n\}\). These masses have exactly the prescribed vertex marginals. Both bundles have utility \(W\) for type \(t\). Consequently, \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(q)\geq W\).

For the upper bound, consider any distribution with the prescribed marginals. Since \(q_{p_t}=1/2\), bundles omitting \(p_t\) have total mass \(1/2\). Every such bundle has utility at most \(W\): the item leaves have total value \(W\), and all other available vertices have value zero for type \(t\). Thus no distribution can give every positive-mass bundle utility strictly greater than \(W\).

Therefore, \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_1(q)=\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_2(q)=W\).

3. A partition gives a feasible allocation at \(3/2\)

Suppose \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in I}w_i=W/2\).

Allocate mass \(1/2\) of type \(1\) to \(\{c,p_1\}\cup\{v_i:i\in I\}\), and mass \(1/2\) of type \(2\) to \(\{c,p_2\}\cup\{v_i:i\notin I\}\).

Both bundles are connected because they contain the center. Each leaf is used with mass \(1/2\), the center is used with mass \(1\), and each type receives its required mass.

Each type’s bundle has utility \(W+W/2=3W/2\), exactly \(3/2\) times its benchmark. Hence the allocation is feasible at \(\alpha=3/2\).

4. Feasibility at \(3/2\) gives a partition

Conversely, suppose an allocation is feasible at \(\alpha=3/2\). Every positive-mass bundle assigned to type \(t\) must have utility at least \(3W/2\).

A bundle omitting \(p_t\) has utility at most \(W\), so every positive-mass bundle of type \(t\) must contain \(p_t\).

Define the item value of a bundle by \(s(B)=\sum_{i:v_i\in B}w_i\). Since the only additional positive value for type \(t\) is \(W\) from \(p_t\), every positive-mass bundle satisfies \(s(B)\geq W/2\).

On the other hand, the item marginal constraints give
\(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}s(B)=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\sum_{t,B\ni v_i}x_{t,B}=\sum_{i=1}^n w_iq_{v_i}=W/2\).

The total allocation mass is \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}=\mu_1+\mu_2=1\). Therefore,
\(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}(s(B)-W/2)=0\).

Every summand is nonnegative. It follows that every positive-mass bundle has \(s(B)=W/2\). At least one positive-mass bundle exists, and its item leaves give a solution to PARTITION.

5. The maximum and the complexity conclusion

For completeness, every feasible allocation in this construction satisfies \(\alpha\leq3/2\). Indeed, its total utility is
\(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)=W/2+W\sum_{B\ni p_1}x_{1,B}+W\sum_{B\ni p_2}x_{2,B}\leq3W/2\),
because each private leaf has total usage \(1/2\). Feasibility also makes this total at least \(\alpha W\).

The maximum is attained. Feasibility at \(\alpha=1\) follows by giving each type its mass times the benchmark distribution from Section 2. Moreover, because there are finitely many types and bundles and both benchmarks equal the positive number \(W\), the largest factor supported by any fixed feasible allocation is the minimum of finitely many ratios \(u_t(B)/W\). Across all allocations, these minima belong to a finite set. Thus the nonempty set of attainable factors has a maximum.

We have proved that the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance if and only if the constructed instance has maximum feasible factor at least \(3/2\). This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, establishing NP-hardness. It does not require computing an unknown benchmark.

  • Source problem and construction.

The stated binary-encoded PARTITION problem is a standard NP-complete problem. Its positive integer inputs give \(W>0\), as required by the later strict comparison and division by \(W\).

The constructed star has \(n+3\) vertices and two types. Computing \(W\) and writing the valuations requires polynomial time and polynomial encoding length. All valuations are nonnegative integers; all prescribed masses and the decision threshold are rational.

The use of \(a=3/2\) is admissible: the task places no upper restriction of \(1\) on \(\alpha\). The result establishes NP-hardness, without claiming strong NP-hardness or membership in NP.

  • Benchmark calculation.

The proposed two-bundle distribution is valid. The center occurs in both bundles, and each leaf occurs in exactly one, giving center marginal \(1\) and leaf marginals \(1/2\). For either type, the private-leaf bundle has utility \(W\), and the item-leaf bundle also has utility \(W\). Thus the lower bound is established.

For the upper bound, the marginal of \(p_t\) forces total mass \(1/2\) on bundles omitting \(p_t\). Every such bundle has utility at most \(W\). Since the bundle family is finite and this mass is positive, at least one such bundle has positive mass. Consequently, no supported-utility threshold exceeding \(W\) is possible.

The conclusion \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(q)=W\) is therefore exact for both types. This argument correctly concerns unrestricted benchmark bundles.

  • Forward implication.

Given a partition, each of the two allocated bundles contains the center and is connected. The complementary item sets ensure that every item leaf has total usage \(1/2\); each private leaf also has usage \(1/2\). The center and type-mass constraints hold.

Both types receive utility \(3W/2\) on their positive-mass bundle. With benchmark \(W\), this proves feasibility at \(\alpha=3/2\).

  • Reverse implication.

Because \(W>0\), a bundle omitting its type’s private leaf has utility at most \(W<3W/2\). Thus every positive-mass bundle must contain that private leaf.

Its utility is then precisely \(W+s(B)\), even if it also contains the other private leaf, whose value to this type is zero. Hence \(s(B)\geq W/2\).

The displayed marginal calculation is correct: finite sums can be interchanged, and each item contributes its value multiplied by its prescribed usage \(1/2\). The resulting total item value is \(W/2\), while total allocation mass is \(1\).

Therefore the weighted deviations from \(W/2\) sum to zero. Each positive-mass term is nonnegative; zero-mass terms vanish. It follows that every positive-mass bundle has item value exactly \(W/2\). Such a bundle exists because total mass is \(1\), and its item indices solve PARTITION.

This implication does not need connectivity beyond the assumed feasibility of the allocation, which causes no gap.

  • Upper bound and attainment of the maximum.

The total-utility identity is correct. Item leaves contribute \(W/2\) in aggregate. Each private leaf contributes only when assigned to its corresponding type, and that assigned mass cannot exceed its total marginal \(1/2\). Thus aggregate utility is at most \(3W/2\).

Feasibility gives aggregate utility at least \(\alpha W\), proving \(\alpha\leq3/2\).

Feasibility at \(\alpha=1\) is also correctly established. Scaling the benchmark distribution by each type’s mass preserves the required aggregate marginals, and both benchmark bundles contain the center, so they are connected.

For any allocation satisfying the mass constraints, its largest supported factor is the minimum ratio over its positive-mass support. This support is nonempty and finite, and all denominators equal the positive number \(W\). These minima range over a finite set of ratios. Together with the exhibited feasible allocation, this establishes that an optimal factor is attained. The proof therefore addresses the task’s use of a maximum rather than merely a supremum.

  • Complexity conclusion and feedback.

Both directions of the polynomial-time many-one reduction are proved. Exact benchmark computation creates no unresolved dependency because the benchmarks are explicitly established as \(W\).

The stated finite-agent guarantee is not used and does not interfere with this reduction. No additional clarification or repair is needed.
