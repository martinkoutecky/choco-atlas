The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}\), type utilities \(u_t\in\{0,1\}^{G}\), type endowments \(e_t\), aggregate supplies \(s_g=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tg}\), and rational \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\), compute prices \(p\), type budgets \(b_t\), and unit bundles \(x_t\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tg}=s_g\), each \(x_t\) is a cheapest utility-maximizing bundle at budget \(b_t\), and \( (1-\epsilon)p\cdot e_t\le b_t\le\epsilon+p\cdot e_t \) for every type \(t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).
There are \(m\geq1\) divisible goods \(G\). Type \(t\) has linear utility \(u_t\in\{0,1\}^{G}\), with at least one liked good, and a binary-rational endowment \(e_t\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_g e_{tg}=1\). Supplies are \(s_g=\sum_t\mu_t e_{tg}>0\).
Given binary-rational \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\), compute rational prices \(p\geq0\), budgets \(b_t\geq0\), and bundles \(x_t\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_gx_{tg}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tg}=s_g\).
Each \(x_t\) must maximize \(u_t\cdot y\) over \(y\geq0\) with \(\sum_g y_g=1\) and \(p\cdot y\leq b_t\), and minimize expenditure among these maximizers.
Require \((1-\epsilon)p\cdot e_t\leq b_t\leq\epsilon+p\cdot e_t\), and \(e_t=e_{t'}\Rightarrow b_t=b_{t'}\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Can such an equilibrium be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), or is computing such an equilibrium NP-hard?

For explicitly listed agents with unit supplies and fractional-perfect-matching endowments, a dichotomous-utility equilibrium with these budget tolerances is computable in time polynomial in the number of agents and \(1/\epsilon\). [Garg–Tröbst–Vazirani](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.10320)
No computational result for this exact support-encoded mass version was located in the literature search.

  • Require nonnegative binary-rational endowments of total size one per type, carrying over the discrete unit-endowment condition.
  • Require positive supply for every listed good, carrying over the discrete model's positive unit supplies.
  • Require every type to like at least one good, as assumed for the anchor's algorithm.
  • Specify nonnegative prices, budgets, and allocations, unit-size consumption, and rational outputs.

Polynomial-time computation for support-encoded societies

The equilibrium can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). The population need not be expanded into explicitly listed agents.

The proof below gives a weighted price procedure and controls both its number of iterations and the binary lengths of its rational outputs. The budget update follows the approach of [Garg–Tröbst–Vazirani, Theorem 22](https://arxiv.org/html/2009.10320); the required support-level price computation is proved here.

1. Notation and a flow criterion

Let \(T\) be the set of supported types, and let \(H_t=\{g:u_{tg}=1\}\) be the nonempty set of goods liked by type \(t\). For subsets, write

\(\mu(I)=\sum_{t\in I}\mu_t\) and \(s(S)=\sum_{g\in S}s_g\).

It is convenient to allocate aggregate quantities \(z_{tg}=\mu_t x_{tg}\). Their row sums must be \(\mu_t\), and their column sums must be \(s_g\). Since every supported mass is positive, recovering \(x_{tg}=z_{tg}/\mu_t\) is well-defined.

We use the following capacitated version of Hall’s condition.

Flow criterion. Consider a bipartite graph between types \(I\) and goods \(C\), type capacities \(c_t\geq0\), and required good quantities \(s_g\). There exists a flow filling every good without exceeding any type capacity if and only if

\(s(S)\leq\sum_{t\in N_I(S)}c_t\quad\text{for every }S\subseteq C,\)

where \(N_I(S)\) denotes the types adjacent to at least one good in \(S\).

To prove this, construct a network with source-to-type capacities \(c_t\), unbounded capacities on bipartite edges, and good-to-sink capacities \(s_g\). A finite cut whose sink-side goods are \(S\) has minimum possible capacity

\(\sum_{t\in N_I(S)}c_t+s(C\setminus S).\)

Thus every cut has capacity at least \(s(C)\) precisely when the displayed inequalities hold. The max-flow min-cut theorem proves the criterion. Unbounded arcs can be replaced by a rational capacity exceeding \(\sum_t c_t+s(C)\).

All networks used below have polynomially many vertices and arcs. We use standard polynomial-time algorithms for exact rational maximum flow and rational linear programming.

2. A fixed decomposition into free and positive-price goods

Construct the liked-edge flow network with source-to-type capacities \(\mu_t\) and good-to-sink capacities \(s_g\), and compute a maximum flow \(f\).

If its value is \(1\), set \(z=f\), \(p=0\), and \(b=0\). Every type receives one unit entirely of liked goods, hence achieves utility \(1\) at minimum expenditure \(0\). All requested conditions hold. This settles the case in which the liked-edge graph admits a complete allocation.

Suppose henceforth that the maximum-flow value is less than \(1\). In its residual network, let \(A_1\) and \(G_1\) be the type and good vertices reachable from the source. Put \(A_2=T\setminus A_1\) and \(G_0=G\setminus G_1\).

The residual-network definition gives four properties.

  • Every liked good of a type in \(A_1\) belongs to \(G_1\). Otherwise a residual liked edge would make that good reachable.
  • Every good in \(G_1\) is fully filled by \(f\). Otherwise the sink would be reachable.
  • Every type in \(A_2\) sends its entire mass \(\mu_t\). Otherwise its source arc would make it reachable.
  • No positive flow goes from \(A_2\) to \(G_1\). Such a flow would create a residual reverse edge making the type reachable.

Consequently, \(f\) restricted to \(A_1\times G_1\) fills all goods in \(G_1\) with type capacities \(\mu_t\). Its restriction to \(A_2\times G_0\) allocates each type in \(A_2\) entirely to liked goods.

Moreover, if the flow value is \(F<1\), then

\(F=\mu(A_2)+s(G_1),\qquad \mu(A_1)-s(G_1)=1-F>0.\)

Both \(G_1\) and \(G_0\) are therefore nonempty. We keep this decomposition throughout the algorithm, always assigning price \(0\) to \(G_0\).

3. The positive-price allocation problem

Fix strictly positive budgets \(b_t\). Suppose the goods in \(G_1\) have positive prices. Define, for each \(t\in A_1\),

\(q_t=\min_{g\in H_t}p_g,\qquad d_t=\mu_t\min\{1,b_t/q_t\}.\)

We seek an allocation that fills every good in \(G_1\), assigns type \(t\) exactly aggregate quantity \(d_t\), and uses only its cheapest liked goods.

Such an allocation produces an equilibrium for the fixed budgets:

  • Keep the original liked allocation of types in \(A_2\) to the free goods.
  • Complete the unfilled rows of types in \(A_1\) using the remaining free-good supply.

The completion is possible because its total row deficit is

\(\mu(A_1)-s(G_1),\)

equal to the free-good supply remaining after allocating \(A_2\). A greedy transportation allocation fills these rows and columns exactly.

For \(t\in A_1\), the resulting per-capita bundle has utility

\(v_t=\min\{1,b_t/q_t\}\)

and expenditure \(q_t v_t\). Indeed, its positive-price goods are cheapest liked goods, while its fillers are free and disliked.

For any competing bundle \(y\), nonnegative prices imply

\(p\cdot y\geq q_t(u_t\cdot y).\)

Therefore every affordable bundle has utility at most \(\min\{1,b_t/q_t\}\), and every bundle attaining utility \(v_t\) costs at least \(q_t v_t\). The constructed bundle attains both bounds. It is thus utility-maximizing and expenditure-minimizing among maximizers.

Types in \(A_2\) attain utility \(1\) at expenditure \(0\), so their bundles also satisfy both optimality requirements.

It remains to compute the positive prices and their liked allocation efficiently, while permitting only price increases.

4. A polynomial-time ascending price procedure

4.1 The maintained feasibility condition

For a positive price vector on \(G_1\), call the vector admissible for budgets \(b\) if each price class can be filled using its cheapest-liked-edge graph, with type capacities

\(\mu_t\min\{1,b_t/q_t\}.\)

The flows in this definition need not exhaust all type capacities.

Two observations supply the inputs needed later.

  • If every price and every budget equals a positive number \(\delta\), the vector is admissible: all type capacities are \(\mu_t\), and the flow from Section 2 fills \(G_1\).
  • If prices already have the exact liked allocation from Section 3, increasing budgets preserves admissibility, because all relevant type capacities weakly increase.

We now prove that any admissible vector can be raised to one having the exact allocation from Section 3.

4.2 Raising the lowest remaining price class

Initially, all goods in \(G_1\) and types in \(A_1\) are active. During the procedure, frozen goods receive their final allocations, and the participating types are removed.

Maintain the following invariant: no active type likes a frozen good, and the active goods satisfy the admissibility condition on their cheapest-edge graph.

Let \(a\) be the smallest active price, let \(C\) be its price class, and let \(I_C\) be the active types liking at least one good in \(C\). Since \(C\) is the lowest class, it consists of cheapest liked goods for all these types.

Let \(c>a\) be the next active price level, if one exists. Raise all prices in \(C\) together by solving this linear program. Its variables are a common price \(q\) and monetary flows \(w_{tg}\) on liked edges between \(I_C\) and \(C\):

\(\text{maximize }q,\)

subject to

\(\sum_{t\in I_C}w_{tg}=q s_g\quad(g\in C),\)

\(\sum_{g\in C}w_{tg}\leq\mu_t b_t\quad(t\in I_C),\)

\(\sum_{g\in C}w_{tg}\leq\mu_t q\quad(t\in I_C),\)

\(w_{tg}\geq0,\qquad q\geq a,\)

and, when the next level exists, \(q\leq c\).

This is a rational linear program with polynomially many variables and constraints. Admissibility makes it feasible at \(q=a\). It is bounded because

\(q s(C)\leq\sum_{t\in I_C}\mu_t b_t.\)

Let \(q^*\) be its optimum.

If \(q^*=c\), raise \(C\) to \(c\), merge the two price classes, and continue. Their separate filling flows remain feasible in the merged cheapest-edge graph; merging only adds available edges.

Otherwise, raise \(C\) to \(q^*\) and freeze a nonempty tight subset, as follows.

4.3 Finding and freezing a tight subset

At price \(q>0\), feasibility of the class is equivalent, by the flow criterion, to

\(q s(S)\leq\sum_{t\in N_{I_C}(S)}\mu_t\min\{b_t,q\}\quad(S\subseteq C).\)

At an optimum below the next price level, some nonempty \(S\subseteq C\) must satisfy equality. Otherwise all nonempty-subset inequalities would be strict, and continuity, together with finiteness of the subsets, would allow a small further increase of \(q\).

A tight set can be found in polynomial time. Use the physical-quantity network with type capacities \(\mu_t\min\{1,b_t/q^*\}\) and good capacities \(s_g\). For each good in \(C\), compute a minimum cut constrained to put that good on the sink side. At least one such cut has capacity \(s(C)\). Its sink-side goods form a nonempty tight set \(S\). The constraint can be implemented with one sufficiently large-capacity arc.

Freeze the goods in \(S\) at price \(q^*\), and let \(N=N_{I_C}(S)\). Tightness says

\(s(S)=\sum_{t\in N}\mu_t\min\{1,b_t/q^*\}.\)

In any flow filling \(C\), all flow into \(S\) comes from \(N\). The equality forces every type in \(N\) to exhaust its capacity on \(S\), with none of its flow going elsewhere. Thus:

  • a maximum flow allocates \(S\) fully and gives every type in \(N\) its exact required quantity;
  • removing \(S\) and \(N\) preserves feasibility for the remaining goods.

No remaining type likes \(S\), because every active neighbour of \(S\) was removed. The invariant is preserved.

4.4 Termination and the resulting allocation

Each freezing event removes at least one good, so there are at most \(m\) freezing events.

Each merging event reduces the number of active price classes. Raising and partially freezing a lowest class does not increase that number. Hence there are at most \(m-1\) merging events.

The procedure therefore uses polynomially many linear programs and flow computations.

Every type in \(A_1\) is eventually removed: it has a liked good, and it is removed when the first such good is frozen. At that event, it receives exactly its required quantity on cheapest liked goods. Later price changes cannot make another liked good cheaper, because only the lowest active class is processed and prices only rise.

We have proved:

Monotone price lemma. From any admissible positive prices and strictly positive budgets, one can compute, in polynomial time in their binary encoding length, weakly higher prices and an exact liked allocation of the form required in Section 3.

In particular, after increasing budgets, an equilibrium produced by this procedure can be updated using only price increases. Throughout, the prices of \(G_0\) remain zero.

5. The budget iteration

Set

\(\delta=\epsilon/2,\qquad r=\frac{1-\delta}{1-\epsilon}>1.\)

Initialize \(b_t^{(1)}=\delta\). Starting with price \(\delta\) on every good in \(G_1\), apply the monotone price lemma and complete the allocation using free goods. Denote the resulting equilibrium by \((x^{(1)},p^{(1)})\).

For each \(k\geq1\), define

\(b_t^{(k+1)}=\delta+(1-\delta)\,p^{(k)}\cdot e_t.\)

Apply the monotone price lemma to obtain an equilibrium for these budgets with

\(p^{(k+1)}\geq p^{(k)}.\)

The first budget update weakly increases every budget because \(p^{(1)}\geq0\). Subsequently, price monotonicity and nonnegative endowments imply

\(b^{(k+1)}\geq b^{(k)}.\)

Thus every update is well-defined.

Stop at the first \(K\geq2\) for which

\(p^{(K)}\leq r p^{(K-1)}\)

coordinatewise. This condition is checked using exact rational comparisons.

5.1 Correctness at termination

Nonnegative endowments and the stopping condition give

\((1-\epsilon)p^{(K)}\cdot e_t\leq(1-\epsilon)r p^{(K-1)}\cdot e_t=(1-\delta)p^{(K-1)}\cdot e_t\leq b_t^{(K)}.\)

Price monotonicity also gives

\(b_t^{(K)}=\delta+(1-\delta)p^{(K-1)}\cdot e_t\leq\epsilon+p^{(K)}\cdot e_t.\)

Hence both budget tolerances hold.

The budget formula depends only on the endowment vector. Therefore

\(e_t=e_{t'}\ \Longrightarrow\ b_t^{(K)}=b_{t'}^{(K)}.\)

By Sections 3 and 4, the final bundles have the required row sums, clear every good, and satisfy exact utility maximization and expenditure minimization.

6. A polynomial bound on the number of updates

Define weighted total budgets and revenue by

\(B_k=\sum_t\mu_t b_t^{(k)},\qquad R_k=\sum_g s_g p_g^{(k)}.\)

Market clearing and budget feasibility imply \(R_k\leq B_k\). Using the supply definition and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), the budget update gives

\(B_{k+1}=\delta+(1-\delta)R_k\leq\delta+(1-\delta)B_k.\)

Since \(B_1=\delta<1\), induction yields \(B_k\leq1\). Consequently,

\(p_g^{(k)}\leq1/s_g\quad\text{for every }g.\)

Meanwhile, every price in \(G_1\) starts at least at \(\delta\) and never decreases. Let \(s_{\min}=\min_g s_g>0\), and define the potential

\(\Phi_k=\sum_{g\in G_1}\log\!\left(p_g^{(k)}/\delta\right).\)

It satisfies

\(0\leq\Phi_k\leq |G_1|\log\!\left(1/(\delta s_{\min})\right).\)

Whenever the stopping condition fails, some positive-price coordinate increases by a factor greater than \(r\). All other coordinates weakly increase, so the potential increases by more than \(\log r\).

Finally,

\(\log r\geq1-1/r=\frac{\epsilon}{2-\epsilon}\geq\epsilon/2.\)

It follows that

\(K=O\!\left(1+\frac{m}{\epsilon}\log\!\frac{1}{\epsilon s_{\min}}\right).\)

The supplies have polynomial binary encoding length, so \(\log(1/s_{\min})=O(L)\). Thus the number of updates is polynomial in the requested parameters. No factor proportional to an expanded population appears.

7. Polynomial binary lengths

A bound on the number of exact linear programs alone would not rule out excessive growth of intermediate rational encodings. We therefore give an explicit denominator bound.

Choose positive integers:

  • \(Q_e\), a common denominator of all endowment entries;
  • \(Q\), a common denominator of all masses and supplies;
  • \(Q_\delta\), a denominator of \(\delta\).

Products of the relevant input denominators suffice, and all three integers have binary length \(O(L)\).

7.1 The form of a newly frozen price

Consider a freezing price \(q>0\). Because this event occurs below the next price level, \(q\) is the maximum feasible class price without that upper bound.

For a subset \(S\) of the class, put

\(h_S(v)=\sum_{t\in N(S)}\mu_t\min\{b_t,v\}-v s(S).\)

At \(q\), all \(h_S(q)\) are nonnegative. Some tight subset must have negative right derivative. Indeed, if every tight subset had nonnegative right derivative, all subset inequalities would remain valid on a common sufficiently short interval to the right of \(q\), contradicting maximality.

For such a subset, define

\(J=\{t\in N(S):b_t\leq q\},\qquad H=N(S)\setminus J.\)

The right derivative is \(\mu(H)-s(S)<0\). Tightness therefore gives

\(q=\frac{\sum_{t\in J}\mu_t b_t}{s(S)-\mu(H)},\qquad s(S)-\mu(H)>0.\)

Set \(a_t=Q\mu_t\) and \(d=Q(s(S)-\mu(H))\). These are integers, with \(1\leq d\leq Q\), and

\(q=\frac{\sum_{t\in J}a_t b_t}{d}.\)

Thus, if the current budgets have common denominator \(D_b\), each frozen price has a denominator dividing \(D_b d\) for an integer \(d\leq Q\).

There are at most \(m\) freezing events per call. Every final positive price is a frozen price. Hence their common denominator can be chosen with binary length at most

\(\log D_b+m\log Q.\)

7.2 Growth across budget updates

If the preceding prices have common denominator \(D_{k-1}\), the new budgets have common denominator

\(D_b=D_{k-1}Q_eQ_\delta.\)

Therefore a common price denominator after the update satisfies

\(\log D_k\leq\log D_{k-1}+\log Q_e+\log Q_\delta+m\log Q.\)

Starting with budgets of denominator \(Q_\delta\), the denominator lengths after \(K\) iterations are consequently \(O(K(m+1)L)\).

Prices are bounded by \(1/s_{\min}\), and budgets by \(\delta+(1-\delta)/s_{\min}\). Their numerators therefore also have polynomial binary length.

Every linear program and flow network consequently has polynomial input length. Exact rational solutions and the final transportation completion have polynomial output length. Dividing aggregate allocations by the positive input masses preserves polynomial encoding length.

This establishes polynomial bit complexity, as well as a polynomial number of arithmetic and optimization steps.

8. Conclusion

The algorithm returns rational prices, budgets, and bundles satisfying every condition in the task, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\).

The essential change from equal-mass agents is to bound weighted revenue by \(1\), giving \(p_g\leq1/s_g\). Although a small supply can permit a numerically large price, only its logarithm enters the iteration bound, and that logarithm is controlled by the binary input length.
