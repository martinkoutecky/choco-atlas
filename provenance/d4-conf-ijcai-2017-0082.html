The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a fixed linear tie-break, and finitely many delegated voter blocs \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), shared truthful rankings \(r_t\), and current ballots \(b_t\), set \(P_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_{b_t} b}\mu_t\) and \(\mathrm{mm}_\mu(c)=\min_{d\ne c}P_\mu(c,d)\). Start from \(b_t=r_t\). At each step, one bloc changes its whole ballot to a best response: the new winner is strictly preferred under \(r_t\) to the current winner, and no alternative ballot gives that bloc a more-preferred winner. Decide whether an infinite legal sequence is reachable from the truthful state, equivalently whether a directed cycle is reachable among the finitely many ballot profiles.

Partial results and scope

I do not resolve whether the unrestricted problem is in $\mathrm{P}$ or is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.

I prove two additional structural results:

  • Two equal-mass blocs can cycle from truthful ballots with four candidates. For two blocs and at most three candidates, no cycle is reachable from truthfulness.
  • Cycles do not require equality between distinct coalition masses or ties between winning scores. A four-bloc, four-candidate cycle exists throughout an open region of mass vectors, with a unique winner at every visited profile.

I also give self-contained proofs of the polynomial-time best-response oracle, the $\mathrm{PSPACE}$ upper bound, and the polynomial-time algorithm when both the number of candidates and the number of distinct masses are fixed.

Throughout, “earlier” refers to the fixed tie-break. A string such as $\mathrm{BCAD}$ denotes the strict ballot $B\succ C\succ A\succ D$.

1. Two-bloc dynamics

1.1 A bloc with mass greater than one half

Proposition. If some bloc has mass greater than $1/2$, the truthful profile has no legal move. From an arbitrary initial profile, every legal sequence has length at most one.

Proof. Let the bloc have mass $\mu>1/2$, and let $p$ be the first candidate in its current ballot. For every $x\ne p$,

$$ P_\mu(p,x)\ge \mu>\frac12, \qquad P_\mu(x,p)\le 1-\mu<\frac12. $$

Thus $p$ has score greater than $1/2$, while every rival has score less than $1/2$. The winner is always the first candidate in this bloc’s ballot, independently of all other ballots.

Consequently, no other bloc can change the winner. The dominant bloc can attain its truthful first choice by placing it first. If a legal move occurs, it must elect that candidate, after which no further legal move is possible. At truthfulness, this candidate already wins. $\square$

In particular, two blocs with unequal masses have no legal move from truthfulness.

1.2 Attainable winners for two equal-mass blocs

Suppose the two masses are $1/2$. Fix one bloc’s ballot, whose first candidate is $q$.

Lemma. The other bloc can elect exactly the candidates that are no later than $q$ in the tie-break.

Proof. The fixed ballot contributes $1/2$ to every support $P_\mu(q,x)$, so $q$ always has score at least $1/2$. For any $p\ne q$,

$$ \operatorname{score}(p)\le P_\mu(p,q)\le \frac12. $$

If $p$ is later than $q$, it therefore cannot win.

Conversely, submitting a ballot with $q$ first elects $q$: both ballots then put $q$ first, giving it score $1$.

Let $p$ be earlier than $q$. Submit a ballot beginning with $p\succ q$, followed by all other candidates. Both $p$ and $q$ have score $1/2$. Every other candidate $x$ lies below $q$ in both ballots, so

$$ P_\mu(x,q)=0. $$

Thus all other candidates have score $0$, and $p$ wins the tie against $q$. $\square$

The best attainable winner is therefore the bloc’s truthfully most preferred candidate in this initial segment of the tie-break. This characterization permits every ballot attaining that winner.

1.3 No truthful cycle for two blocs and three candidates

Proposition. With two blocs and three candidates, every legal sequence from truthfulness has length at most two.

Proof. Unequal masses are covered by Proposition 1.1. Assume equal masses, and name the candidates so that the tie-break is $A$ before $B$ before $C$.

If a truthful ballot has $A$ first, then $A$ wins regardless of the other ballot: its score is at least $1/2$, every rival’s score is at most $1/2$, and $A$ wins all ties. That bloc already obtains its truthful first choice, and the other bloc cannot change the winner. Hence the truthful profile is terminal.

If both truthful ballots have the same first candidate, that candidate wins and both blocs obtain their first choice, again making the profile terminal.

The remaining case has truthful first candidates $B$ and $C$. Call these blocs $1$ and $2$, respectively. Their possible truthful rankings are

$$ r_1\in\{\mathrm{BAC},\mathrm{BCA}\}, \qquad r_2\in\{\mathrm{CAB},\mathrm{CBA}\}. $$

We examine both possibilities for $r_2$.

  • If $r_2=\mathrm{CBA}$, both ballots put $B$ above $A$. Hence $A$ has score $0$, while $B$ has score $1/2$ and wins. Bloc $1$ obtains its first choice. By Lemma 1.2, bloc $2$ can attain only $A$ or $B$ against a ballot with $B$ first. It prefers $B$ to $A$, so it cannot improve. The profile is terminal.
  • Suppose $r_2=\mathrm{CAB}$.

If $r_1=\mathrm{BAC}$, all three candidates have score $1/2$, and $A$ wins. Bloc $2$ already obtains its best attainable winner against a ballot with $B$ first. Bloc $1$ can improve only by electing its first choice $B$.

Any replacement ballot electing $B$ must have $B$ first. If it instead has $A$ first, $A$ necessarily wins; if it has $C$ first, both ballots have $C$ first and $C$ wins. Of the two ballots with $B$ first, $\mathrm{BAC}$ elects $A$, while $\mathrm{BCA}$ elects $B$. Thus the only legal move leads to $(\mathrm{BCA},\mathrm{CAB})$.

If $r_1=\mathrm{BCA}$, this same profile is already truthful.

At $(\mathrm{BCA},\mathrm{CAB})$, the winner is $B$. Only bloc $2$ can improve, and its best attainable winner is $A$. Any ballot electing $A$ must have $A$ first: a ballot with $B$ first makes $B$ unanimously preferred to $A$, and a ballot with $C$ first makes $C$ unanimously preferred to $A$.

After this move, the ballot with $A$ first fixes the winner at $A$ against every possible ballot of bloc $1$. Bloc $2$ still faces a ballot with $B$ first, so its best attainable winner remains $A$. The resulting profile is terminal.

This exhausts all truthful profiles. There are at most two legal moves. $\square$

For two candidates and any number of blocs, truthfulness is also terminal: a bloc’s truthful ballot already maximizes the support for its preferred candidate and minimizes the opponent’s support. Reversing that ballot cannot improve its winner.

1.4 A truthful cycle with two blocs and four candidates

Use tie-break $A$ before $B$ before $C$ before $D$, equal masses $1/2$, and truthful rankings

$$ r_1=\mathrm{BCAD}, \qquad r_2=\mathrm{CABD}. $$

Consider the following profiles. Scores are listed in candidate order $(A,B,C,D)$.

| State | Bloc $1$ | Bloc $2$ | Scores | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\mathrm{BCAD}$ | $\mathrm{CABD}$ | $(0,1/2,1/2,0)$ | $B$ |
| $1$ | $\mathrm{BCAD}$ | $\mathrm{DABC}$ | $(1/2,1/2,0,1/2)$ | $A$ |
| $2$ | $\mathrm{BDAC}$ | $\mathrm{DABC}$ | $(0,1/2,0,1/2)$ | $B$ |
| $3$ | $\mathrm{BDAC}$ | $\mathrm{CABD}$ | $(1/2,1/2,1/2,0)$ | $A$ |

The sequence is

$$ 0\longrightarrow 1\longrightarrow 2\longrightarrow 3\longrightarrow 0. $$

The table follows directly from counting the two ballots. For example, at state $0$, both ballots put $C$ above $A$, so $A$ has score $0$. Candidate $B$ has supports $1/2,1/2,1$ against $A,C,D$, respectively; candidate $C$ has supports $1,1/2,1$ against $A,B,D$. Thus $B$ and $C$ tie at $1/2$, and $B$ wins.

To verify legality:

  • On $0\to1$ and $2\to3$, bloc $2$ changes the winner from $B$ to $A$. The other ballot has $B$ first, so Lemma 1.2 says that its attainable winners are exactly $A$ and $B$. Since its truthful ranking is $\mathrm{CABD}$, $A$ is its best attainable winner and is strictly preferred to $B$.
  • On $1\to2$ and $3\to0$, bloc $1$ changes the winner from $A$ to its truthful first choice $B$. Such a move is necessarily a best response.

Each move changes exactly one indivisible bloc ballot. State $0$ is truthful, so this is a reachable cycle.

Consequently, the minimum support size permitting a truthful cycle is two. For two blocs, four is the minimum candidate count permitting such a cycle. Appending additional candidates below $A,B,C,D$ in every displayed ballot and truthful ranking gives examples for every $m\ge4$.

2. A cycle with unique winners and unequal coalition masses

The preceding two-bloc example uses score ties. The following construction shows that ties are not necessary.

2.1 A family of masses

Use four candidates with tie-break $A$ before $B$ before $C$ before $D$. Let the four bloc masses be $a,b,c,d$, where

$$ a,b,c,d>0, \qquad a+b+c+d=1, $$

and assume

$$ a<b, \qquad b+c-a<d<b+c. $$

These inequalities imply

$$ d>c, \qquad a+c<b+c<a+d, \qquad d<b+c. $$

Choose truthful rankings

$$ r_1=\mathrm{ABDC}, \qquad r_2=\mathrm{BDAC}, \qquad r_3=\mathrm{BADC}, \qquad r_4=\mathrm{DCAB}. $$

Blocs $1$ and $3$ retain their truthful ballots throughout the proposed sequence.

2.2 Winner calculations

Consider five states, where state $0$ is truthful:

| State | Bloc $2$ | Bloc $4$ | $(P_\mu(A,B),P_\mu(A,C),P_\mu(A,D))$ | Score of $A$ | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $\mathrm{BDAC}$ | $\mathrm{DCAB}$ | $(a+d,a+b+c,a+c)$ | $a+c$ | $B$ |
| $1$ | $\mathrm{BDAC}$ | $\mathrm{CABD}$ | $(a+d,a+b+c,a+c+d)$ | $a+d$ | $A$ |
| $2$ | $\mathrm{BCAD}$ | $\mathrm{CABD}$ | $(a+d,a+c,1)$ | $a+c$ | $B$ |
| $3$ | $\mathrm{BCAD}$ | $\mathrm{DABC}$ | $(a+d,a+c+d,a+b+c)$ | $a+d$ | $A$ |
| $4$ | $\mathrm{BDAC}$ | $\mathrm{DABC}$ | $(a+d,1,a+c)$ | $a+c$ | $B$ |

The supports in the table are obtained by summing precisely the masses whose displayed ballots put $A$ above its opponent. The stated minima use $d>c$ and $d<b+c$.

At every displayed state, blocs $1$ and $4$ put $A$ above $B$, while blocs $2$ and $3$ put $B$ above $A$. Hence

$$ P_\mu(B,A)=b+c. $$

Moreover, blocs $1,2,3$ all put $B$ above both $C$ and $D$, so

$$ P_\mu(B,C)\ge a+b+c, \qquad P_\mu(B,D)\ge a+b+c. $$

Therefore $B$ has score exactly $b+c$.

The same three blocs put $B$ above $C$ and $D$, giving

$$ \operatorname{score}(C)\le P_\mu(C,B)\le d, \qquad \operatorname{score}(D)\le P_\mu(D,B)\le d. $$

Since $d<b+c$, neither $C$ nor $D$ can match $B$’s score. The strict inequalities

$$ a+c<b+c<a+d $$

now prove every winner in the table. Every displayed winner is unique.

2.3 Best-response verification

Follow the sequence

$$ 0\longrightarrow1\longrightarrow2\longrightarrow3\longrightarrow4 \longrightarrow1. $$

The last four states form a cycle.

When bloc $4$ moves, the other ballots are $\mathrm{ABDC}$, $\mathrm{BADC}$, and either $\mathrm{BDAC}$ or $\mathrm{BCAD}$. For every replacement ballot of bloc $4$,

$$ \operatorname{score}(B)\ge b+c, $$

because the unchanged blocs already supply $b+c$ against $A$ and $a+b+c$ against $C,D$. Meanwhile,

$$ \operatorname{score}(C)\le d<b+c, \qquad \operatorname{score}(D)\le d<b+c. $$

Thus bloc $4$ cannot elect either $C$ or $D$. Its truthful ranking is $D\succ C\succ A\succ B$, so whenever $A$ is attainable, it is the best attainable winner. The table shows that its moves $0\to1$, $2\to3$, and $4\to1$ attain $A$ and strictly improve upon $B$.

Bloc $2$ makes moves $1\to2$ and $3\to4$. Both elect its truthful first choice $B$ instead of $A$, and therefore are strictly improving best responses.

This proves a reachable cycle under all the required move conditions.

2.4 An explicit instance with no equal distinct coalition masses

Take

$$ (a,b,c,d)=\frac1{84}(13,17,23,31). $$

The conditions hold because

$$ 13<17, \qquad 17+23-13=27<31<40=17+23. $$

The winner scores along states $0,1,2,3,4$ are respectively

$$ \frac{40}{84},\quad \frac{44}{84},\quad \frac{40}{84},\quad \frac{44}{84},\quad \frac{40}{84}. $$

All $16$ subset sums of the integer weights are distinct. In increasing order, they are

$$ 0,\ 13,\ 17,\ 23,\ 30,\ 31,\ 36,\ 40,\ 44,\ 48,\ 53,\ 54,\ 61,\ 67,\ 71,\ 84. $$

Thus distinct sets of blocs have different total masses. In particular, no coalition has mass $1/2$.

The inequalities defining the construction are strict, so the same cycle persists under sufficiently small perturbations preserving positivity and total mass $1$. Distinctness of the finitely many coalition masses also persists under sufficiently small perturbations.

Therefore there is an open region of mass vectors exhibiting this cycle, with unique winners throughout and no equality between distinct coalition masses. Any proposed unrestricted convergence argument based solely on excluding such equalities would fail.

3. Polynomial-time computation of a best attainable winner

This section establishes the local oracle without enumerating all $m!$ ballots.

3.1 Exact integer arithmetic

Write each mass as $\mu_t=a_t/d_t$, and set

$$ D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}d_t, \qquad v_t=D\mu_t. $$

Every $v_t$ is a positive integer, and

$$ \sum_t v_t=D. $$

The bit lengths of $D$ and each $v_t$ are $\mathrm{O}(L)$. Elementary integer arithmetic computes them in $\mathrm{O}(\tau L^2)$ bit operations.

Multiplying supports by $D$ multiplies all Maximin scores by $D$, preserving comparisons, ties, winners, and legal moves. Hence we may use integer weights.

Fix a bloc of weight $v$ and let

$$ Q(x,y)=\sum_{s\ne t:\,x\succ_{b_s}y}v_s $$

be the support supplied by all other blocs.

3.2 Placing a proposed winner first

If a ballot elects $p$, raise $p$ to the first position while preserving the order of the remaining candidates.

Every support entering $p$’s score weakly increases. For each rival, its support against $p$ weakly decreases, while its supports against other rivals remain unchanged. Thus $p$’s score cannot decrease and no rival’s score can increase. All comparisons required by the fixed tie-break remain valid.

Consequently, $p$ is attainable if and only if it is attainable with $p$ first. This is an attainability argument; it does not restrict the ballots allowed in legal dynamics.

3.3 A graph characterization

Let $R=C\setminus\{p\}$. When $p$ is first, its score is fixed at

$$ \sigma_p=\min_{y\in R}\bigl(Q(p,y)+v\bigr). $$

For $x\in R$, define the predicate

$$ G_p(x,z)= \begin{cases} z<\sigma_p,&\text{if }x\text{ is earlier than }p,\\ z\le \sigma_p,&\text{if }p\text{ is earlier than }x. \end{cases} $$

Candidate $p$ wins exactly when every rival $x$ has some opponent against whom its support satisfies this predicate.

Call $x$ a root if either

$$ G_p(x,Q(x,p)) $$

holds, or some $y\in R\setminus\{x\}$ satisfies

$$ G_p(x,Q(x,y)+v). $$

On vertex set $R$, insert an arc $y\to x$ whenever $x\ne y$ and

$$ G_p(x,Q(x,y)). $$

Lemma. Candidate $p$ is attainable if and only if every vertex is reachable from the root set.

Proof of sufficiency. Order the roots first, followed by the other vertices in nondecreasing shortest-path distance from the roots. Put this order after $p$.

For a root, its defining witness remains valid regardless of the remaining order: its support against $p$ is $Q(x,p)$, and its support against any rival $y$ is at most $Q(x,y)+v$. Both predicates are preserved when their numerical argument decreases.

For a nonroot $x$, choose a predecessor $y$ on a shortest path from the roots. Candidate $y$ is above $x$ in the ballot, so

$$ P(x,y)=Q(x,y). $$

The arc $y\to x$ supplies the required witness. Hence every rival loses to $p$ under the score comparison and tie-break. $\square$

Proof of necessity. Suppose a ballot with $p$ first elects $p$. If some vertices are unreachable from the roots, choose the highest such vertex $x$ in this ballot.

Because $p$ wins, there is an opponent $y$ satisfying $G_p(x,P(x,y))$.

If $y=p$, then $x$ is a root. If $x$ is above $y$, then

$$ P(x,y)=Q(x,y)+v, $$

which also makes $x$ a root. Both are contradictions.

Otherwise $y\in R$ is above $x$, and

$$ P(x,y)=Q(x,y). $$

There is therefore an arc $y\to x$. By the choice of $x$, the higher vertex $y$ is reachable, making $x$ reachable as well. This is the final contradiction. $\square$

For one proposed winner, constructing this graph and testing reachability takes $\mathrm{O}(m^2L)$ bit operations. Testing every candidate takes $\mathrm{O}(m^3L)$. The bloc’s best attainable winner is the truthfully highest candidate among those passing the test.

Including integer normalization and construction of the support matrix, one best-response computation takes

$$ \mathrm{O}\!\left( \tau L^2+(\tau m^2+m^3)L \right). $$

A proposed one-bloc replacement is legal exactly when its actual winner is strictly preferred to the current winner and equals the best attainable winner just computed. Thus legal-edge verification is polynomial-time computable.

4. Unrestricted upper bounds

4.1 Explicit finite-state algorithm

Set

$$ q=m!, \qquad N=q^\tau. $$

There are exactly $N$ labelled ballot profiles. Each has at most $\tau(q-1)$ possible one-bloc replacements.

Enumerate all profiles and replacements, retain precisely the legal edges using the oracle above, and search the graph reachable from truthfulness for a directed cycle. The resulting running time is

$$ (m!)^{\tau+1}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

This is an exact algorithm, but not a polynomial-time classification.

4.2 Polynomial space

A profile has an encoding of length

$$ S=\tau m\lceil\log_2m\rceil, $$

and $\log_2N\le S$.

For profiles $u,v$, let $\operatorname{Reach}(u,v,k)$ mean that there is a directed walk of length at most $2^k$ from $u$ to $v$. At $k=0$, test equality or a legal edge. For $k>0$, enumerate all profiles $z$ and test

$$ \operatorname{Reach}(u,z,k-1) \quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{Reach}(z,v,k-1). $$

This recurrence is correct: a walk of length at most $2^k$ can be split into two walks of length at most $2^{k-1}$, and conversely such walks concatenate. For a walk shorter than $2^{k-1}$, one part may be empty.

Every reachable vertex has a simple reaching path of length at most $N-1$, so $k=\lceil\log_2N\rceil$ suffices. The two recursive calls can be evaluated sequentially. Each level stores only a constant number of profiles and enumeration counters, and the depth is $\mathrm{O}(S)$. Thus the recursion uses $\mathrm{O}(S^2)$ space.

Enumerate legal edges $u\to v$ and accept if truthfulness reaches $u$ and $v$ reaches $u$. Acceptance gives a reachable nonempty closed walk, which contains a cycle. Conversely, a reachable directed cycle supplies such an edge.

Including the local oracle, this gives deterministic space

$$ \mathrm{O}\!\left( S^2+(\tau+m^2)L \right). $$

Hence the unrestricted problem belongs to $\mathrm{PSPACE}$.

Finally, infinite legal play and reachable-cycle existence are equivalent here. An infinite walk in a finite graph repeats a vertex. Conversely, a reachable cycle can be repeated indefinitely because legality depends only on the current profile and no fairness condition is imposed.

5. Polynomial time with fixed candidate count and fixed number of masses

Let $k$ be the number of distinct masses, and again put $q=m!$.

Partition blocs into classes according to the pair consisting of their mass and truthful ranking. There are at most $kq$ classes. Let class $g$ contain $n_g$ blocs.

Represent a profile by counts $h_{g,b}$, recording the number of blocs in class $g$ submitting ballot $b$. They satisfy

$$ \sum_b h_{g,b}=n_g. $$

The number of count states is

$$ H=\prod_g\binom{n_g+q-1}{q-1} \le (\tau+1)^{kq(q-1)}. $$

The inequality follows because each class’s first $q-1$ counts determines its last count, and each of those counts has at most $\tau+1$ possible values.

A count move removes one bloc from a positive cell $(g,b)$ and places it in $(g,b')$. It changes one bloc of the original mass; it does not split that bloc.

Lemma. The count graph preserves existence of an infinite legal sequence from truthfulness.

Proof. Counts determine every pairwise support. For a bloc in cell $(g,b)$, subtracting its mass contribution under ballot $b$ determines the other blocs’ support matrix. Its truthful preferences are also determined by $g$. Therefore its best attainable winner and the legality of any replacement depend only on the count state and the source cell.

Every labelled legal move projects to a legal count move. Conversely, from any labelled representative of a count state, choose an actual bloc in the positive source cell of a legal count move. Changing its ballot realizes that count move, with exactly the same best-response test.

Thus every infinite labelled sequence projects to an infinite count sequence, and every infinite count sequence can be lifted step by step to an infinite labelled sequence. Finiteness gives the corresponding equivalence for reachable cycles. $\square$

Enumerating this graph and checking its edges takes

$$ (\tau+1)^{k(m!)(m!-1)} (m!)^{\mathrm{O}(1)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$

time. This is polynomial in $\tau,L$ when both $m$ and $k$ are fixed.

The quotient preserves separate bloc controllers and all ballots attaining the best winner. It does not require a count cycle to return immediately to the same labelled profile.

6. What remains unresolved

The local move condition is polynomial-time decidable, but that alone does not bound the length or complexity of global reachability.

The two-bloc example shows that very small support does not guarantee convergence when all best-winner ballots are permitted. The four-bloc construction shows that unequal coalition masses and unique winners along a trajectory do not guarantee convergence either.

These results establish no unrestricted polynomial-time algorithm and no $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction. In particular, the mass inequalities in the cycle construction are not a hardness reduction: they do not provide a legal preparation phase encoding arbitrary subset choices, nor do they exclude unintended reachable cycles in negative instances.
