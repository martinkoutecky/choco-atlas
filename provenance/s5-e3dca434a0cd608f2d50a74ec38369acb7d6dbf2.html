$$ q=1+\lceil\log_2 Q\rceil. $$
Using elementary integer arithmetic, its bit complexity is
$$ O\!\left(L^3+d\tau^2S^2+\tau q^2S\right). $$

There is also an upper bound polynomial in the numerical value of $Q$. Writing $R=T+1$, the algorithm takes
$$ O\!\left(L^3+d\tau^2R[L+\tau Rq]^2\right), $$
where
$$ R=O(1+Q^{10}d^2). $$

A further support-sensitive bound can be proved in one dimension. If
$$ \beta=\min_{1\leq i\leq\tau}\mu_i, $$
then
$$ T=O\!\left(\frac{\tau}{\beta^2}\right) =O(\tau Q^2). $$
Thus, in one dimension, one may use

2. An exponential output with three types

Let $m=2^k$, with $k\geq1$, and choose
$$ p_-=-1,\qquad p_0=0,\qquad p_+=1, $$
with masses
$$ \mu_-=\mu_+=\frac{m-1}{2m}, \qquad \mu_0=\frac1m. $$
These masses are positive and sum to $1$. Since $m-1$ is odd, the endpoint mass fractions are reduced. Consequently,
$$ Q=2m,\qquad L=\Theta(\log m). $$

Define
$$ r=\frac{m-1}{m+1}, \qquad K=\min\{t\geq0:r^t\leq\tfrac12\}. $$

Exact trajectory and stopping time

For every $0\leq t\leq K$,
$$ x_-(t)=-r^t,\qquad x_0(t)=0,\qquad x_+(t)=r^t. $$

Indeed, the assertion holds at $t=0$. Suppose it holds at $t<K$, and write $s=r^t$. Then
$$ \frac12<s\leq1. $$
Each endpoint neighbors itself and the center. The endpoints do not neighbor each other, because their distance is $2s>1$. Therefore
$$ x_+(t+1) =\frac{\mu_+s}{\mu_++\mu_0} =\frac{m-1}{m+1}s =rs. $$
The negative endpoint evolves symmetrically, and the center remains at $0$ because its weighted average is $0$. This proves the induction.

At round $K$, every pairwise distance is at most $1$. Hence every neighborhood contains all three labels, and
$$ x_-(K+1)=x_0(K+1)=x_+(K+1)=0. $$
Every update before this changes an endpoint, and the configuration at round $K+1$ is stationary. Thus
$$ T=K+1, \qquad K=\left\lceil \frac{\log 2}{\log((m+1)/(m-1))} \right\rceil. $$
The argument includes the case of equality at the confidence boundary.

For $u>0$, integration of $1/(1+s)$ over $[0,u]$ gives
$$ \frac{u}{1+u}\leq\log(1+u)\leq u. $$
Taking $u=2/(m-1)$ yields
$$ \frac{2}{m+1} \leq \log\frac{m+1}{m-1} \leq \frac{2}{m-1}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \frac{(m-1)\log2}{2} \leq K \leq \frac{(m+1)\log2}{2}+1, $$
so $T=\Theta(m)=\Theta(Q)$.

Output length

Because $m$ is even,
$$ \gcd(m-1,m+1)=1. $$
The nonzero endpoint coordinates are therefore the reduced fractions
$$ \pm\frac{(m-1)^t}{(m+1)^t}. $$
Each has encoding length $\Theta(1+t\log m)$. Summing over $0\leq t\leq K$ gives
$$ S=\Theta(K^2\log m) =\Theta(Q^2\log Q) =2^{\Theta(L)}. $$

3. Finite convergence and the replication bound

$$ a_i=Q\mu_i. $$

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}a_i=Q. $$
The update becomes
$$ x_i(t+1) = \frac{\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_jx_j(t)}{m_i(t)}, \qquad m_i(t)=\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_j, $$
with
$$ 1\leq m_i(t)\leq Q. $$
In particular, $\tau\leq Q$.

Replace initial type $i$ by $a_i$ equal-weight agents at $p_i$. There are $Q$ agents altogether. Coincident agents have identical neighborhoods and updates, so copies of a type remain coincident. Inductively, their update counts each neighboring type $j$ exactly $a_j$ times and therefore agrees with the displayed weighted update. This argument remains valid when different initial types later coincide. Thus the expanded system has exactly the same stopping time.

The equal-weight convergence theorem applies to synchronous Euclidean updates with closed unit-radius neighborhoods, permits coincident agents, and uses equality of consecutive configurations as convergence. It gives $O(n^{10}d^2)$ rounds in general and $O(n^3)$ in one dimension. Applying it with $n=Q$ proves
$$ T=O(Q^{10}d^2), \qquad T=O(Q^3)\quad\text{if }d=1. $$

4. A stronger support-sensitive bound in one dimension

This section proves independently that
$$ T=O(\tau/\beta^2), \qquad \beta=\min_i\mu_i. $$

Order, persistent gaps, and permanent coincidences

Order the labels initially so that
$$ x_1(0)<\cdots<x_\tau(0). $$
Their order remains nondecreasing at every round.

To verify this, suppose the current positions are ordered. Each neighborhood is an interval of indices. For $i<j$, both endpoints of the neighborhood interval of $i$ are at most the corresponding endpoints for $j$: the confidence interval shifts to the right as its center increases.

If these neighborhoods overlap, pass from the first to the second by removing its lowest positions and then adding higher positions. Removing values no larger than every retained value cannot decrease a positive weighted average; adding values no smaller than every existing value cannot decrease it either. If the neighborhoods are disjoint, every position in the first lies to the left of every position in the second, so their averages are ordered. This proves order preservation.

A gap greater than $1$ between consecutive positions persists forever. There are no interactions across such a gap, and convex averaging keeps all positions on either side within their respective current intervals. The gap therefore cannot decrease at the next round; induction proves persistence.

Call a label frozen if every neighbor has its own current position. Its coincident cluster has gaps greater than $1$ to all other positions, so the preceding argument shows that it remains frozen forever.

Finally, coincident labels remain coincident, since their neighborhoods and updates are identical. Thus mergers permanently decrease the number of distinct positions.

A two-round progress lemma

Consider a subsystem not yet stationary. Let $\ell$ be its leftmost nonfrozen label at round $t$, and set
$$ y=x_\ell(t). $$
There is no neighbor strictly to its left: such a neighbor would itself be nonfrozen, contradicting the choice of $\ell$.

Let $r$ be the first label strictly to its right. Because $\ell$ is nonfrozen,
$$ 0<x_r(t)-y\leq1. $$
The two neighborhoods satisfy
$$ N_\ell(t)\subseteq N_r(t). $$
Indeed, every neighbor of $\ell$ lies in $[y,y+1]$, and hence is within distance $1$ of $x_r(t)$. Also, $r$ has no neighbor below $y$: any such position would be within distance $1$ of $y$, contradicting the preceding paragraph.

If the neighborhoods are equal, then $\ell$ and $r$ merge at round $t+1$.

Otherwise, there is some
$$ s\in N_r(t)\setminus N_\ell(t). $$
Necessarily $x_s(t)>y+1$. Every neighbor of $r$ is at least $y$, and the averaging coefficient of $s$ is at least $\beta$, since the total neighborhood mass is at most $1$. Therefore
$$ x_r(t+1)>y+\beta. $$

If
$$ x_\ell(t+1)-y\geq\frac{\beta}{2}, $$
then $\ell$ has already advanced by at least $\beta^2/2$, because $\beta\leq1$. It cannot move left during the next update: labels formerly to its left are permanently frozen and separated from it, and all remaining labels stay to its right by order preservation.

In the remaining case,
$$ x_r(t+1)-x_\ell(t+1)>\frac{\beta}{2}. $$
Order preservation shows that no position lies strictly between these two positions at round $t+1$.

If their gap exceeds $1$, the cluster containing $\ell$ freezes. Otherwise, $r$ remains a neighbor of $\ell$, with averaging coefficient at least $\beta$. Since $\ell$ has no neighbor strictly to its left,
$$ x_\ell(t+2)-x_\ell(t+1) \geq \beta\bigl(x_r(t+1)-x_\ell(t+1)\bigr) > \frac{\beta^2}{2}. $$

We have proved: within two rounds, either two previously distinct positions merge, the leftmost active cluster freezes, or that leftmost active position advances by at least $\beta^2/2$.

Counting progress

Partition the initial positions into consecutive components at gaps greater than $1$. These components evolve independently forever.

Consider one component with $s$ initial labels. Every consecutive initial gap inside it is at most $1$, so its initial diameter is at most $s-1$. Convexity keeps all its future positions in that initial interval.

Apply the lemma in disjoint two-round blocks while the component is active.

There are at most $s-1$ blocks charged to mergers, because coincidences are permanent. There are at most $s$ blocks charged to freezing, because each such event permanently freezes a previously active label.

The leftmost active position is nondecreasing: labels to its left are already permanently frozen, while it averages only positions at or to its right. If it ceases to be active, the next active label is farther right by order preservation. Consequently, each displacement block increases this quantity by at least $\beta^2/2$. Its total increase is at most $s-1$, so there are at most
$$ \frac{2(s-1)}{\beta^2} $$
such blocks. At most one final block ends with no active labels.

These bounds also prove termination: infinitely many active blocks would contradict the finite event counts and bounded total displacement. The component therefore stops within
$$ O\!\left(s+\frac{s-1}{\beta^2}\right) =O(s/\beta^2) $$
rounds. Taking the maximum over independent components gives
$$ T=O(\tau/\beta^2). $$
Since $\mu_i=a_i/Q\geq1/Q$, this implies
$$ T=O(\tau Q^2). $$

5. Rational size growth

Let $D_0$ be the least common multiple of all reduced initial coordinate denominators. Define auxiliary denominators by
$$ \widehat D_0=D_0, \qquad \widehat D_{t+1} = \widehat D_t\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}m_i(t). $$

Every reduced coordinate denominator at round $t$ divides $\widehat D_t$. Initially this follows from the definition of $D_0$. For the induction step, write
$$ x_{jc}(t)=\frac{z_{jc}}{\widehat D_t} $$
with integer $z_{jc}$. Then
$$ x_{ic}(t+1) = \frac{\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_jz_{jc}} {m_i(t)\widehat D_t}, $$
whose reduced denominator divides $\widehat D_{t+1}$.

Since $m_i(t)\leq Q$ and $\log_2D_0\leq L$,
$$ \log_2\widehat D_t \leq L+t\tau\log_2Q. $$

Every coordinate update is a convex combination. Thus each coordinate stays between its initial minimum and maximum, including when coordinates have either sign. Its absolute value is at most $2^L$. Combining this magnitude bound with the denominator bound shows that every reduced numerator and denominator at round $t$ has
$$ O(L+t\tau q) $$
bits.

Summing over $R=T+1$ output rounds gives
$$ S=O\!\left(d\tau[RL+\tau qR^2]\right). $$
This also accommodates conventional labels, round numbers, and the encoding of $T$: their logarithmic lengths are absorbed by $O(L)$ using the convergence bound.

Consequently, both the output length and the bounds below are polynomial in $d,\tau,L,Q$.

6. Exact simulation and bit complexity

Algorithm and correctness

Maintain reduced fractions for every labeled coordinate. At round $t$:

  • Record the current state.
  • Compute its positive least common coordinate denominator $D$, and write

$$ x_{ic}(t)=z_{ic}/D $$
with integer $z_{ic}$.

  • Determine all neighborhoods using

$$ j\in N_i(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{c=1}^{d}(z_{ic}-z_{jc})^2\leq D^2. $$

  • Compute $m_i(t)=\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_j$ and reduce each candidate fraction

$$ \frac{\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_jz_{jc}}{m_i(t)D}. $$

  • Compare the candidates with the current reduced fractions. If all agree, return $T=t$; otherwise advance to the candidates.

The distance test is exactly equivalent to the prescribed Euclidean test because $D>0$. It includes self-neighbors and boundary neighbors. Self-neighbors also ensure $m_i(t)>0$. The candidate formula is exactly the required weighted average.

Induction therefore proves that every recorded state is correct. Testing after each state detects the first stationary round and records the terminal state without recording a duplicate round $T+1$. The value of $T$ can be prepended after simulation or written together with the stored trajectory; this does not change the bounds.

Output-sensitive bound

Let $h_t$ be the total bit length of the reduced coordinate fractions at round $t$, excluding record overhead. Then
$$ \sum_{t=0}^{T}h_t\leq S, \qquad d\tau(T+1)\leq S. $$

The common denominator $D$ has $O(h_t)$ bits, because its bit length is bounded by the sum of the coordinate-denominator lengths. Each scaled numerator $z_{ic}$ likewise has $O(h_t)$ bits.

The squared-distance tests use integers of $O(h_t)$ bits. For weighted sums,
$$ \left|\sum_{j\in N_i(t)}a_jz_{jc}\right| \leq Q\max_j|z_{jc}|, $$
so both candidate numerators and candidate denominators have $O(h_t+q)$ bits.

Schoolbook multiplication and division, and binary greatest-common-divisor computation, take $O(b^2)$ bit operations on $b$-bit operands. Common-denominator conversion, neighborhood construction, averaging, reduction, and comparison require $O(d\tau^2)$ such operations per round. Thus the round cost is
$$ O\!\left(d\tau^2(h_t+q)^2\right). $$

Preprocessing the input fractions, computing $Q$, and computing the integer masses costs $O(L^3)$: there are at most $O(L)$ operations on integers of $O(L)$ bits.

Using
$$ (h_t+q)^2\leq2h_t^2+2q^2 $$
and
$$ \sum_{t=0}^{T}h_t^2 \leq \left(\sum_{t=0}^{T}h_t\right)^2 \leq S^2, $$
the total arithmetic cost is
$$ O\!\left(L^3+d\tau^2[S^2+Rq^2]\right). $$
Output writing and round bookkeeping fit within this bound. Finally, $d\tau R\leq S$ gives
$$ \boxed{ O\!\left(L^3+d\tau^2S^2+\tau q^2S\right). } $$

Apart from its contribution to $S$, the dependence on $Q$ here is only through $\log Q$.

Numerical-$Q$ bound

The current common denominator divides $\widehat D_t$. The preceding denominator and magnitude estimates therefore bound all intermediate integers at round $t$ by
$$ O(L+(t+1)\tau q) $$
bits. Summing the round costs gives
$$ \boxed{ O\!\left(L^3+d\tau^2R[L+\tau Rq]^2\right), \qquad R=T+1. } $$

Use
$$ R=O(1+Q^{10}d^2) $$
in general, or
$$ R=O(1+\tau Q^2) $$
in one dimension. Both substitutions yield bounds polynomial in $d,\tau,L,Q$.

In particular, polynomially bounded $Q$ suffices for input-polynomial exact computation. Equal masses have $Q=\tau$.

In one dimension, the stronger bound $R=O(1+\tau/\beta^2)$ gives a further conclusion: if $1/\beta$ is polynomially bounded in $L$, then exact computation is input-polynomial, since $q=O(L)$, regardless of the numerical size of $Q$.

7. What this establishes about hardness

The explicit-output problem has an unconditional exponential-output obstruction and a polynomial output-sensitive algorithm. Neither fact supplies an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

One tractable class of associated decision problems can also be identified. If a round bound $u$ is encoded in unary, exact simulation through round $u$ takes at most
$$ O\!\left( L^3+d\tau^2(u+1)[L+\tau(u+1)q]^2 \right) $$
bit operations. Hence deciding whether $T\leq u$ is polynomial-time for unary $u$. Comparing a specified coordinate at unary round $u$ with an input rational threshold is also polynomial-time: the simulated coordinate has polynomial bit length, and comparison uses exact cross multiplication. After convergence, the state remains constant.

This does not settle corresponding questions with binary round bounds, nor hardness reductions to information about the complete trajectory.

The remaining classification requires a specified reduction model for the trajectory function, or a specified decision problem with polynomial-size answers, followed by a hardness proof or an exclusion. No such argument is established here. The displayed arithmetic bounds are valid upper bounds; their optimality is not claimed.
