The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C=\{p,c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), rational nonmanipulator masses \(\mu_\pi\) on \(\tau\) rankings summing to \(1-\alpha\), and a coordinated bloc of mass \(\alpha\), compute the minimum possible maximum final Borda score among candidates \(c\ne p\). Each unit of bloc mass must report a complete ranking with \(p\) first. Also determine whether the minimum is at most \(b_p+m\alpha\), where \(b_p\) is the nonmanipulators’ Borda score mass for \(p\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{p,c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), with \(m\geq1\), use Borda, and count ties as wins for \(p\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
Here the types are strict complete rankings, the nonmanipulator masses satisfy \(\sum_\pi\mu_\pi=1-\alpha\), and the coordinated bloc has binary rational mass \(0<\alpha\leq1\); the total electorate has mass one. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Write \(B_\pi(c)\) for the Borda score and \(b_c=\sum_\pi\mu_\pi B_\pi(c)\).
A bloc allocation assigns nonnegative masses \(x_\rho\) summing to \(\alpha\) to strict complete rankings \(\rho\) with \(p\) first; its mass may be split arbitrarily among such rankings.
Define \(T(x)=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}(b_{c_i}+\sum_\rho x_\rho B_\rho(c_i))\) and \(T^*=\min_x T(x)\). A winning allocation exists exactly when \(T^*\leq b_p+m\alpha\).

Does this problem admit a randomized algorithm, polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), returning a bloc allocation with \(T(x)\leq T^*+O(\alpha\sqrt{m\log(m+1)})\) and failure probability \(\exp(-\Omega(m))\), or is achieving this additive guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete unweighted blocs of \(k\) voters, a polynomial-time randomized algorithm achieves additive error \(O(k\sqrt{m\log m})\) with exponentially small failure probability ([source](https://www.ariel.ac.il/wp/noam-hazon/wp-content/uploads/sites/317/2021/02/17.aamas_.new_approx_borda.pdf)).
For divisible votes, exact co-winner feasibility and minimum required coalition weight are polynomial-time solvable for positional scoring rules; these use different objectives from the fixed-mass maximum rival score ([source](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified strict complete rankings and arbitrary splitting of the bloc mass among reports.
  • Specified a positive binary rational bloc mass at most one, allowing an empty nonmanipulator support when the bloc has mass one.
  • Required at least one rival so that the maximum rival score is defined.
  • Counted score ties as wins for the preferred candidate, matching the stated weak threshold.
  • Normalized the discrete additive error by total electorate size, replacing coalition count by coalition mass.
  • Used log(m+1) to define the asymptotically equivalent approximation bound also when there is one rival.

Result

The problem is exactly solvable in deterministic polynomial time. Consequently, the requested additive guarantee holds with zero error and zero failure probability.

The proof directly optimizes the fixed-mass maximum rival score. It also constructs an optimal allocation supported on at most \(m^2\) strict rankings.

1. A polynomial-size linear program

With \(p\) first, every bloc ranking assigns the scores \(0,1,\ldots,m-1\) bijectively to the rivals.

For \(1\leq i\leq m\) and \(0\leq j\leq m-1\), introduce a variable \(y_{ij}\), representing the bloc mass that assigns score \(j\) to candidate \(c_i\). Consider the linear program

\( \displaystyle \begin{aligned} \text{minimize}\quad &t\\ \text{subject to}\quad &y_{ij}\geq0 && (1\leq i\leq m,\ 0\leq j\leq m-1),\\ &\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}y_{ij}=\alpha && (1\leq i\leq m),\\ &\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{ij}=\alpha && (0\leq j\leq m-1),\\ &b_{c_i}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j\,y_{ij}\leq t && (1\leq i\leq m),\\ &0\leq t\leq m. \end{aligned} \)

The bounds on \(t\) do not restrict the original optimization. Indeed, nonnegativity of Borda scores gives \(T(x)\geq0\), while

\( \displaystyle b_{c_i}+\sum_\rho x_\rho B_\rho(c_i) \leq m(1-\alpha)+(m-1)\alpha =m-\alpha\leq m. \)

The linear program is nonempty: set \(y_{ij}=\alpha/m\) and \(t=m\). It is bounded because every \(y_{ij}\) lies in \([0,\alpha]\), and \(t\) lies in \([0,m]\). Thus it attains its minimum.

Every bloc allocation induces a feasible matrix by setting

\( \displaystyle y_{ij}=\sum_{\rho:\,B_\rho(c_i)=j}x_\rho. \)

Each candidate receives exactly one score in each ranking, giving the row equations. Each score is assigned to exactly one rival in each ranking, giving the column equations. Moreover,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j\,y_{ij} =\sum_\rho x_\rho B_\rho(c_i). \)

Hence \(t=T(x)\) makes this matrix a feasible linear-program solution.

The essential converse is proved next.

2. Every feasible matrix gives an allocation

Lemma. Let \(Y\) be a nonnegative \(m\)-by-\(m\) matrix whose row and column sums all equal \(\alpha>0\). In polynomial time, one can construct positive rational masses \(\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_q\) and permutation matrices \(P_1,\ldots,P_q\) satisfying

\( \displaystyle Y=\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\delta_\ell P_\ell, \qquad \sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\delta_\ell=\alpha, \qquad q\leq m^2, \)

provided the entries of \(Y\) are rational.

Proof. Maintain a nonnegative residual matrix \(R\) whose row and column sums have a common value \(r\). Initially \(R=Y\) and \(r=\alpha\).

If \(r=0\), nonnegativity and the row equations imply \(R=0\), so the procedure terminates.

Suppose \(r>0\). Form a bipartite graph with candidates on one side and score positions on the other, placing an edge \((i,j)\) precisely when \(R_{ij}>0\). For a set \(S\) of candidate vertices, let \(N(S)\) be its neighboring score vertices. Then

\( \displaystyle r|S| =\sum_{i\in S}\sum_jR_{ij} =\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_{i\in S}R_{ij} \leq\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_iR_{ij} =r|N(S)|. \)

Dividing by \(r>0\) gives Hall’s condition. Hall’s marriage theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching. Such a matching can be found in polynomial time.

Let \(P\) be its permutation matrix and define

\( \displaystyle \delta=\min\{R_{ij}:P_{ij}=1\}>0. \)

Record the pair \((\delta,P)\), and replace \(R\) by \(R-\delta P\). All entries remain nonnegative, and every row and column sum becomes \(r-\delta\). At least one positive entry becomes zero, and no zero entry becomes positive.

There are at most \(m^2\) initially positive entries, so there are at most \(m^2\) iterations. At termination the residual is zero. Summing the updates gives the claimed matrix decomposition; summing the decreases in the common row sum gives \(\sum_\ell\delta_\ell=\alpha\). The arithmetic bit complexity is addressed below. \(\square\)

For each permutation matrix \(P_\ell\), construct a strict ranking \(\rho_\ell\): put \(p\) first, followed by the rivals in decreasing order of their assigned scores. This is well-defined because the matching assigns every score exactly once.

Give \(\rho_\ell\) mass \(\delta_\ell\), adding masses if the same ranking occurs more than once. The resulting allocation has total mass \(\alpha\), and for each rival,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\delta_\ell B_{\rho_\ell}(c_i) =\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j\,y_{ij}. \)

Thus the decomposition preserves every rival’s score exactly.

3. The linear-program optimum equals the target optimum

Let \(v\) be the linear-program optimum.

Section 1 shows that every allocation gives a feasible solution with objective value \(T(x)\). Therefore \(v\leq T^*\).

Conversely, apply Section 2 to the matrix in an optimal linear-program solution. The resulting allocation satisfies

\( \displaystyle T(x)= \max_i\left(b_{c_i}+\sum_jj\,y_{ij}\right) \leq v. \)

By the definition of \(T^*\),

\( \displaystyle T^*\leq T(x)\leq v\leq T^*. \)

Consequently, \(T(x)=v=T^*\).

This proves exact equivalence for the stated fixed-mass objective, including recovery of an optimal allocation.

4. Polynomial bit complexity and output size

First compute all baseline scores from the supplied support. This requires reading only the \(\tau\) listed rankings and performing \(O(\tau m)\) rational arithmetic operations. A common denominator for the input masses can be obtained by multiplying their denominators; its bit length is at most the sum of their encoded denominator lengths. Since Borda scores are integers bounded by \(m\), the resulting baseline scores have bit length polynomial in \(L+\log(m+1)\).

The linear program has \(m^2+1\) variables and \(O(m^2)\) constraints, with rational data of polynomial encoding length. We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: a nonempty bounded linear program with binary-encoded rational coefficients admits an exact rational optimizer computable in polynomial time, with polynomial output bit length. Its hypotheses were verified in Section 1.

The decomposition also uses polynomial-size numbers. Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all entries of the optimal matrix and of \(\alpha\). Its bit length is polynomial because there are polynomially many entries, each of polynomial bit length. Initially every matrix entry is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). Every subsequent subtraction and every extracted mass retains this property. All residual entries and extracted masses lie in \([0,1]\), so their numerators over denominator \(D\) remain bounded by \(D\).

There are at most \(m^2\) matching computations and matrix updates. Thus decomposition takes polynomial bit time. The output consists of at most \(m^2\) rankings, each of length \(m+1\), together with polynomial-bit rational masses.

The complete algorithm therefore runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). It never enumerates the possible rankings.

5. Requested guarantee and relation to the cited results

The algorithm returns \(x\) satisfying \(T(x)=T^*\). In particular,

\( \displaystyle T(x)\leq T^*+O\!\left(\alpha\sqrt{m\log(m+1)}\right). \)

Its failure probability is zero, which satisfies the requested exponentially small bound. A deterministic algorithm is also a randomized algorithm that ignores its random bits.

The cited scheduling paper establishes exact polynomial-time solvability for divisible-vote co-winner feasibility and minimum coalition weight in its Theorem 3.4. [Xia, Conitzer, and Procaccia](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~conitzer/schedulingEC10.pdf). The linear program above separately establishes exact solvability of the fixed-mass maximum rival score asked here.

Finally, since ties count as wins and \(p\)’s final score is always \(b_p+m\alpha\), the optimal allocation makes \(p\) a winner exactly when \(T^*\leq b_p+m\alpha\); this comparison is also exact in polynomial time.
