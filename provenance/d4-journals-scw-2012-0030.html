The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a candidate \(p\), determine whether \(p\) minimizes \(\delta_\mu(c)\) over \(c\in C\), where \(\delta_\mu(c)\) is the infimum total mass whose ranking is changed so that \(c\) becomes a strict Condorcet winner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(p\in C\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Let \(\mathcal R(C)\) denote all strict rankings over \(C\). A replacement assigns nonnegative real masses \(x_{t,r}\) satisfying \(\sum_{r\in\mathcal R(C)}x_{t,r}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\); the resulting mass of ranking \(r\) is \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,r}\). Its cost is \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r\ne t}x_{t,r}\).
For each \(c\in C\), define \(\delta_\mu(c)\) as the infimum replacement cost over resulting societies in which \(c\) is a strict Condorcet winner. All candidates attaining the smallest infimum count as winners, even when an infimum is not attained.

Is deciding whether \(\delta_\mu(p)\le\delta_\mu(c)\) for every \(c\in C\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of indivisible voters, deciding membership in the voter replacement winner set is NP-hard ([Elkind–Faliszewski–Slinko](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.0300)).
A homogeneous analogue on the vote-distribution simplex has been described ([Hadjibeyli–Wilson](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~mcw/Research/Outputs/HaWi2016b.pdf)); no complexity classification for the exact support-input problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made mass divisibility explicit: each input type may be split into arbitrary nonnegative real portions.
  • Allowed replacement portions to receive any strict ranking over the same candidate set, including rankings outside the input support.
  • Required replacement to preserve total mass and charged unit cost per mass receiving a different ranking.
  • Included every candidate tied for the smallest infimum, following the discrete winner-set convention.
  • Made the candidate domain explicit as nonempty, with the designated candidate belonging to it.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, each infimum \(\delta_\mu(c)\) is the exact value of a rational linear program with \(\tau\) variables.

1. The linear program. Fix \(c\in C\). Write \(d\succ_t c\) when ranking \(t\) places \(d\) above \(c\). Let \(u_t\) denote mass of ranking \(t\) that we retain unchanged.

Define \(U_\mu(c)\) as the optimum of the following linear program:

Maximize \( \sum_{t\in T}u_t \), subject to

  • \(0\le u_t\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\);
  • \(\sum_{t\in T:\,d\succ_t c}u_t\le \frac12\) for every \(d\in C\setminus\{c\}\).

The feasible set is nonempty because \(u=0\) is feasible. It is closed and bounded by the coordinate bounds, so the maximum is attained. Moreover, \(0\le U_\mu(c)\le\sum_t\mu_t=1\).

We will prove the exact identity
\( \delta_\mu(c)=1-U_\mu(c) \).

For \(m=1\), the opponent constraints are absent, giving \(U_\mu(c)=1\) and \(\delta_\mu(c)=0\), as required by the vacuous Condorcet condition. The proof below also covers this case.

2. Every successful replacement gives the lower bound. Consider any replacement \(x\) whose resulting society has \(c\) as a strict Condorcet winner. Set \(u_t=x_{t,t}\). The row-sum condition and nonnegativity give \(0\le u_t\le\mu_t\).

For an opponent \(d\), let \(B_x(d,c)\) be the resulting mass ranking \(d\) above \(c\). Every unchanged portion of an original ranking with \(d\succ_t c\) contributes to this mass. Consequently,
\( B_x(d,c)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r:\,d\succ_r c}x_{t,r}\ge\sum_{t:\,d\succ_t c}u_t \).

The resulting society has total mass \(1\), and every ranking is strict. Thus \(c\) strictly defeats \(d\) precisely when \(B_x(d,c)<\frac12\). Hence \(u\) satisfies all constraints of the linear program.

The replacement cost is exactly
\( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r\ne t}x_{t,r}=\sum_{t\in T}(\mu_t-x_{t,t})=1-\sum_{t\in T}u_t\ge1-U_\mu(c) \).

This applies to every successful replacement, so taking the infimum gives
\( \delta_\mu(c)\ge1-U_\mu(c) \).

3. Approaching the lower bound with strict majorities. Let \(u^*\) be an optimal solution of the linear program. Choose any ranking \(r^c\) that places \(c\) first.

For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), retain mass \((1-\varepsilon)u_t^*\) of each original ranking \(t\), and assign all its remaining mass to \(r^c\). Formally, define
\( x^\varepsilon_{t,r}=(1-\varepsilon)u_t^*\,\mathbf 1_{\{r=t\}}+\bigl(\mu_t-(1-\varepsilon)u_t^*\bigr)\mathbf 1_{\{r=r^c\}} \).
If \(t=r^c\), the two terms add in the same entry.

These masses are nonnegative and sum to \(\mu_t\) in each row. Because \(r^c\) places \(c\) above every opponent, the resulting opposition mass is
\( B_{x^\varepsilon}(d,c)=(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{t:\,d\succ_t c}u_t^*\le\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}<\frac12 \).

Thus \(c\) is a strict Condorcet winner. Its replacement cost is at most the total mass assigned to \(r^c\):
\( \operatorname{cost}(x^\varepsilon)\le1-(1-\varepsilon)\sum_tu_t^*=1-U_\mu(c)+\varepsilon U_\mu(c)\le1-U_\mu(c)+\varepsilon \).

For every positive tolerance, we can choose a smaller positive \(\varepsilon\). Therefore
\( \delta_\mu(c)\le1-U_\mu(c) \).
Together with the lower bound, this proves the claimed identity.

This argument explicitly accommodates unattained infima. For example, with two candidates and equal masses on the two opposite rankings, both scores are \(0\), although neither candidate can become a strict Condorcet winner at cost exactly \(0\).

4. Polynomial time and exact comparisons. Each candidate’s program has \(\tau\) variables and \(2\tau+m-1\) inequalities. Its matrix entries belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and its right-hand sides are the input masses, \(0\), and \(\frac12\). Its binary encoding length is therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The rankings determine every matrix entry by a direct position comparison.

We apply the standard rational linear-programming theorem: an explicitly given rational linear program can be solved exactly in time polynomial in its binary encoding length, including detection of infeasibility or unboundedness. Here those exceptional cases are excluded by the nonempty, compact feasible set. Polynomial-time solvability follows from the ellipsoid method; see [Gács and Lovász, *Khachiyan’s linear programming algorithm*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0196677480900024).

For completeness, exact optimum values have polynomial encoding length here. Write each input mass with positive denominator \(b_t\), and set \(Q=2\prod_t b_t\). All right-hand sides have denominators dividing \(Q\), and \(\log Q=O(L)\). At any vertex, choose \(\tau\) linearly independent active constraints, with coefficient matrix \(A\). By Cramer’s rule, all coordinates have denominators dividing \(Q|\det A|\). Since the entries of \(A\) have absolute value at most \(1\), Hadamard’s inequality gives \(|\det A|\le\tau^{\tau/2}\). A linear objective on a nonempty compact polytope attains its maximum at a vertex. Consequently, \(U_\mu(c)\in[0,1]\) has numerator and denominator bit lengths bounded by \(O(L+\tau\log(\tau+1))\).

The algorithm therefore solves these \(m\) linear programs exactly and accepts precisely when
\( U_\mu(p)\ge U_\mu(c) \)
for every \(c\in C\). By the proved identity, this is equivalent to the requested comparisons of \(\delta_\mu\). Rational cross multiplication performs these comparisons in polynomial time and preserves ties.

The discrete hardness result concerns indivisible voters. Here arbitrary portions of each supported ranking may be replaced, and the proof above shows that these continuous choices are captured exactly by the linear programs.
