The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(T_A,T_B\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and each type's incomplete ranking of acceptable opposite-side types with ties of size at most \(L\), find a stable allocation \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\), subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). Stability means that for every acceptable pair \((i,j)\), either zero mass of type \(i\) strictly prefers \(j\) to its assigned partner (counting unmatched mass as worse than acceptable partners), or zero mass of type \(j\) strictly prefers \(i\) to its assigned partner. Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in the support size, input bit length, and \(L\) that returns a stable allocation of mass at least \(\frac{2L-1}{3L-2}\OPT_\infty\), where \(\OPT_\infty\) is the maximum mass in any stable allocation?

A society is given by its support: finite nonempty sets \(T_A,T_B\) of positive-mass types, with binary rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side. Let \(\tau=|T_A|+|T_B|\) be the support size, not the number of possible preference types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Let \(E\subseteq T_A\times T_B\) specify mutually acceptable pairs and \(m=|E|\). Each type has a weak order over its acceptable opposite-side types, with every indifference class containing at most \(\ell\) types, where \(\ell\ge1\) is an integer. All acceptable partners are strictly preferred to being unmatched; ties retain their indifference.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{ij}\ge0\), zero outside \(E\), satisfying \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\nu_j\).
Writing \(\succeq_i\) and \(\succeq_j\) for the respective weak preferences, stability requires, for every \((i,j)\in E\), either \(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}=\mu_i\) or \(\sum_{h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}=\nu_j\). Thus at least one endpoint has zero mass assigned to strictly worse partners or left unmatched.
Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) over stable allocations.

Does an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\ell\) always return a stable allocation of mass at least \(\frac{2\ell-1}{3\ell-2}\mathrm{OPT}_\infty\), or is achieving this guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete agents with incomplete lists and ties of size at most \(\ell\), a polynomial-time algorithm achieves the fraction \(\frac{2\ell-1}{3\ell-2}\) of maximum stable matching cardinality. [Könemann–Pashkovich–Tofigzade](https://arxiv.org/html/2005.05228)

1. Definitions and a strict-preference subroutine

For a vertex $v$, let $c_v$ denote its mass: $c_i=\mu_i$ on side $A$ and $c_j=\nu_j$ on side $B$. For an allocation $z$, write
$$ d_z(v)=\sum_{e\ni v}z_e. $$

A vertex is saturated if $d_z(v)=c_v$. It protects an incident edge $e$ if it is saturated and every edge receiving positive allocation at that vertex is weakly preferred to $e$. Feasibility shows that this is equivalent to the upper-contour equality in the question.

The auxiliary instances below have parallel edges, and preferences order individual edges.

Strict-preference subroutine. Given a finite bipartite multigraph, positive rational vertex capacities, and strict orders over incident edges, one can compute, in polynomial time, a feasible allocation under which every edge is protected by at least one endpoint.

Here is a precise justification that includes parallel edges. We use the stable-allocation theorem for two totally ordered polymatroids: a stable common independent vector can be computed in strongly polynomial time given saturation and exchange-capacity oracles; integer-valued rank functions yield an integral output. See [Iwata–Yokoi, Theorems 5.9 and 6.6](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf).

Let $U$ be the multigraph’s edge set and $\delta(v)$ its incident-edge set. Define
$$ h(S)=\sum_{i\in T_A}c_i\,\mathbf 1_{\{S\cap\delta(i)\ne\varnothing\}}, \qquad g(S)=\sum_{j\in T_B}c_j\,\mathbf 1_{\{S\cap\delta(j)\ne\varnothing\}}. $$

These functions are normalized, nondecreasing, and submodular. To verify submodularity, for a fixed set $D$, the indicator of intersecting $D$ satisfies
$$ \mathbf 1_{\{S\cap D\ne\varnothing\}} + \mathbf 1_{\{R\cap D\ne\varnothing\}} \ge \mathbf 1_{\{(S\cup R)\cap D\ne\varnothing\}} + \mathbf 1_{\{(S\cap R)\cap D\ne\varnothing\}}. $$
Taking nonnegative weighted sums gives the assertion.

The polymatroid polytope for $h$ is exactly
$$ P(h)= \left\{ z\ge0: \sum_{e\in\delta(i)}z_e\le c_i \text{ for every }i\in T_A \right\}. $$
Indeed, its subset inequalities follow from the displayed capacity inequalities; conversely, apply the subset inequality to $\delta(i)$. The analogous assertion holds for $g$.

Concatenate the local strict orders on side $A$ into one total order on $U$, and do likewise on side $B$. This is possible because the incident-edge sets on each side form a partition.

For either polymatroid, let $v$ be the corresponding endpoint of $e$. Its saturation capacity is $c_v-d_z(v)$. For distinct edges $e,e'$, the maximum feasible transfer from $e'$ to $e$ is
$$ \begin{cases} z_{e'},&\text{if they have the same endpoint on that side},\\[2mm] \min\{z_{e'},c_v-d_z(v)\},&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
Thus the required oracles are polynomial-time computable.

When $v$ is saturated, a transfer into $e$ from a distinct positive coordinate is possible exactly when that coordinate has the same endpoint $v$. Consequently, the theorem’s stability condition says precisely that at least one endpoint is saturated and has no positive allocation to an edge ranked below $e$. This is protection as defined above.

Finally, a common denominator $Q$ for the capacities has binary length bounded by the sum of the input denominator lengths. Scaling by $Q$ makes the rank functions integral without introducing additional edges or vertices. The strongly polynomial subroutine therefore gives polynomial running time and polynomial output encoding length for the rational input.

2. The auxiliary instance

For each original edge $e$, introduce three parallel edges
$$ e^0,\quad e^1,\quad e^2. $$
Retain every original vertex capacity.

The preference construction is adapted from the three-copy auxiliary instance of [Yokoi, Section 3](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol212-isaac2021/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2021.71/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2021.71.pdf). The continuous approximation proof below is given directly.

Order the members of each original indifference class arbitrarily. If the successive classes at a vertex are $C_1,\ldots,C_r$, write, for an ordered class $C=(e_1,\ldots,e_k)$,
$$ X(C)=e_1^0,\ldots,e_k^0,e_1^1,\ldots,e_k^1, $$
$$ Z(C)=e_1^2,\ldots,e_k^2,e_1^1,\ldots,e_k^1. $$

At an $A$-vertex, use the strict list
$$ X(C_1),\ldots,X(C_r), \quad \text{all }e^2\text{ copies in original class order}. $$
At a $B$-vertex, use
$$ Z(C_1),\ldots,Z(C_r), \quad \text{all }e^0\text{ copies in original class order}. $$

Compute a stable auxiliary allocation $f$ using Section 1, and return
$$ x_e=f_{e^0}+f_{e^1}+f_{e^2}. $$

Aggregation preserves all vertex totals, so $x$ is feasible. The auxiliary instance has $3m$ edges and $\tau$ vertices. Its construction and solution take time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, hence also in the parameters requested in the question.

3. The projected allocation is stable

Lemma. The allocation $x$ is stable under the original weak orders.

Proof. Suppose an original edge $e$ is protected by neither endpoint in $x$.

At each endpoint, either the vertex is unsaturated or it has positive allocation to some original edge $g$ strictly worse than $e$.

At an $A$-vertex, all three copies of such a strictly worse edge $g$ occur below $e^1$: its $g^0,g^1$ copies belong to a later class, and its $g^2$ copy belongs to the final tail. At a $B$-vertex, the same statement holds with $g^2,g^1$ in a later class and $g^0$ in the final tail.

Positive aggregate allocation to $g$ means that at least one of its copies receives positive auxiliary allocation. Therefore neither endpoint protects $e^1$ in $f$. Unsaturation gives the same conclusion because aggregation preserves vertex totals.

This contradicts auxiliary stability. Hence every original edge is protected by at least one endpoint. $\square$

4. Excluding short augmenting paths

Fix any stable original allocation $y$.

An alternating path relative to $x,y$ has forward edges on which $y_e>x_e$ and backward edges on which $x_e>y_e$. The paths considered here start at an $A$-vertex unsaturated in $x$ and end at a $B$-vertex unsaturated in $x$.

Lemma. No such simple path contains one or three acceptable edges.

Proof for one edge. Both endpoints would be unsaturated in $x$, contradicting its stability.

Proof for three edges. Suppose there is a path
$$ a_0,b_1,a_1,b_2 $$
with
$$ y_{a_0b_1}>x_{a_0b_1}, \qquad x_{a_1b_1}>y_{a_1b_1}, \qquad y_{a_1b_2}>x_{a_1b_2}, $$
where $a_0,b_2$ are unsaturated in $x$. Denote these three original edges by $e,h,g$, respectively.

Because $a_0$ is unsaturated in $f$, auxiliary stability forces $b_1$ to protect $e^2$. Thus every positive auxiliary coordinate incident to $b_1$ is ranked at least as high as $e^2$.

Likewise, $b_2$ is unsaturated in $f$, so $a_1$ must protect $g^0$. Every positive auxiliary coordinate incident to $a_1$ is ranked at least as high as $g^0$.

Now $x_h>y_h\ge0$, so some copy of $h$ receives positive allocation.

At $b_1$, every $0$-copy lies below $e^2$, ruling out positive allocation to $h^0$. At $a_1$, every $2$-copy lies below $g^0$, ruling out positive allocation to $h^2$. Consequently,
$$ f_{h^1}>0. $$

Since $b_1$ protects $e^2$, it ranks $h^1$ above $e^2$. Within an original indifference class, every $2$-copy precedes every $1$-copy. Therefore $h$ and $e$ cannot be tied, and the auxiliary ordering implies
$$ a_1\succ_{b_1}a_0. $$

Similarly, protection of $g^0$ by $a_1$, together with $f_{h^1}>0$, implies that $h^1$ precedes $g^0$. Within a class, all $0$-copies precede all $1$-copies. Hence
$$ b_1\succ_{a_1}b_2. $$

But $y_e>0$ and $y_g>0$. Thus, in $y$, vertex $b_1$ has positive allocation to a partner strictly worse than $a_1$, while $a_1$ has positive allocation to a partner strictly worse than $b_1$. Neither endpoint protects $h$, contradicting stability of $y$. $\square$

5. The two-thirds mass bound

Proposition. For every stable original allocation $y$,
$$ M(y)\le\frac32M(x). $$

Proof. Assume
$$ \Delta=M(y)-M(x)>0; $$
otherwise the conclusion is immediate.

Represent each allocation as a flow on the network
$$ s\longrightarrow T_A\longrightarrow T_B\longrightarrow t, $$
with source and sink arc flows equal to the corresponding vertex totals.

Form the signed difference of the flows for $y$ and $x$. A positive difference gives an arc in its original direction, and a negative difference gives a reversed arc; assign its absolute value as weight. Internal vertices have zero divergence, while $s$ has divergence $\Delta$ and $t$ has divergence $-\Delta$.

Add an artificial arc $t\to s$ of weight $\Delta$. The resulting weighted directed graph is balanced at every vertex and decomposes into weighted simple directed cycles. For completeness, this follows by repeatedly finding a directed cycle among positive-weight arcs and subtracting its minimum arc weight. Each subtraction eliminates at least one positive-weight arc; balance is preserved, so the procedure terminates.

The cycles containing the artificial arc yield simple directed $s$-to-$t$ paths in the original difference graph. Their weights sum to $\Delta$.

Each such path starts at an $A$-vertex whose total allocation is larger in $y$ than in $x$. Feasibility of $y$ implies that this vertex is unsaturated in $x$. The same argument applies to the final $B$-vertex.

Between these endpoints, the path alternates between forward acceptable edges with $y_e>x_e$ and backward acceptable edges with $x_e>y_e$. Its number of acceptable edges is odd. By Section 4, that number is at least five. It therefore contains at least two backward acceptable edges.

A path of weight $w$ consequently consumes at least $2w$ units of backward acceptable-edge weight. The cycle decomposition never uses more than the available weight of any arc. Hence
$$ 2\Delta \le \sum_{e:x_e>y_e}(x_e-y_e) \le \sum_e x_e = M(x). $$
Rearranging gives
$$ M(y)=M(x)+\Delta\le\frac32M(x). $$
$\square$

Taking the supremum over stable $y$ proves
$$ M(x)\ge\frac23\mathrm{OPT}_\infty. $$

This argument applies even when a comparison allocation has irrational coordinates.

6. A tight example for this construction with ties of size two

The following example shows that every stable auxiliary allocation produced by this construction can have mass only two-thirds of the original optimum.

There are three types on each side, each of mass $1/3$. Label the acceptable edges
$$ e=(a_0,b_1),\quad f=(a_1,b_1),\quad g=(a_1,b_2),\quad h=(a_2,b_2),\quad k=(a_2,b_0). $$

The original preferences are:

| Type | Preference list |
|---|---|
| $a_0$ | $e$ |
| $a_1$ | $f\sim g$ |
| $a_2$ | $h\sim k$ |
| $b_0$ | $k$ |
| $b_1$ | $f\succ e$ |
| $b_2$ | $h\succ g$ |

The allocation assigning mass $1/3$ to each of $e,g,k$ is stable: its used edges have saturated endpoints, and the unused edges $f,h$ are protected by $a_1,a_2$, respectively, using their ties. It has mass $1$, so
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_\infty=1. $$

Order the tied edges as $f,g$ and $h,k$. The auxiliary lists are:

| Type | Strict auxiliary list |
|---|---|
| $a_0$ | $e^0,e^1,e^2$ |
| $a_1$ | $f^0,g^0,f^1,g^1,f^2,g^2$ |
| $a_2$ | $h^0,k^0,h^1,k^1,h^2,k^2$ |
| $b_0$ | $k^2,k^1,k^0$ |
| $b_1$ | $f^2,f^1,e^2,e^1,f^0,e^0$ |
| $b_2$ | $h^2,h^1,g^2,g^1,h^0,g^0$ |

The auxiliary allocation
$$ F_{f^1}=F_{h^0}=\frac13, \qquad F_q=0\text{ for every other copied edge }q, $$
is stable. Explicitly:

  • $b_1$ protects all copies of $e$ and also $f^0$.
  • $a_1$ protects $f^1,f^2,g^1,g^2$.
  • $b_2$ protects $g^0$.
  • $a_2$ protects all copies of $h$ and $k$.

These groups cover every copied edge. Its projected mass is $2/3$.

To show that a different stable auxiliary allocation cannot improve the mass, we need the following rounding fact.

Rounding fact

Suppose all vertex capacities of a bipartite multigraph become integral after multiplication by an integer $Q$. Every stable allocation $z$ admits a stable allocation $z'$ with
$$ M(z')\ge M(z), \qquad Qz'_q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$

Proof. Choose one protecting endpoint for every edge of $z$. Within the feasible allocation polytope, requiring that endpoint to protect its edge is equivalent to requiring its capacity inequality to be tight and all coordinates ranked strictly below that edge to be zero.

These are tight inequalities of the feasible polytope. Their intersection is therefore a nonempty face containing $z$, and every point in it remains stable.

After scaling by $Q$, the feasible polytope has integral vertex capacities. Its bipartite incidence matrix is totally unimodular: changing the signs of the rows on one side gives a directed incidence matrix. Parallel edges simply give repeated columns and preserve this property. Thus the scaled polytope, and every face of it, has integral vertices.

Maximize mass over the chosen face and select an optimal vertex. Scaling back proves the claim. $\square$

Apply this fact with $Q=3$. A stable auxiliary allocation of mass greater than $2/3$ would yield a stable integral allocation after scaling, with mass greater than $2$. Each scaled vertex has capacity one, so this would be a stable perfect matching of the auxiliary multigraph.

No such matching exists. Indeed, a perfect matching must use one copy each of $e,g,k$: vertices $a_0,b_0$ force $e,k$, and then $a_1$ must use $g$.

Vertex $b_1$, matched through a copy of $e$, cannot protect $f^1$, which it ranks above every copy of $e$. Therefore $a_1$ must protect $f^1$. Among copies of $g$, only $g^0$ precedes $f^1$ in $a_1$’s list, so the matching must use $g^0$.

Now $b_2$, matched through $g^0$, cannot protect $h^0$. Vertex $a_2$, matched through a copy of $k$, also cannot protect $h^0$, which it ranks above every copy of $k$. This contradicts stability.

Hence every stable auxiliary allocation has mass at most $2/3$. The general bound already proved supplies the reverse inequality on this instance, so every possible auxiliary output has mass exactly $2/3$.
