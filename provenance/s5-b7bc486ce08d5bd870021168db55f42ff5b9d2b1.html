The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given fixed spatial clusters and finite-support distributions over requester and quality-qualified executor types, with each type specified by its role, task capabilities, capacity, and gross-substitutes value or cost schedule, compute clearing prices and a feasible allocation maximizing total gain from trade. The allocation assigns mass \(x_{abY}\) of requester type \(a\) and executor type \(b\) to trade bundle \(Y\), respects type-mass and capacity limits, and gives each unit of mass an integral bundle; type-level allocated mass may be fractional.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\) across both roles. Each type belongs to one of \(k\) fixed clusters; executors are already quality-qualified, and trade is restricted to a cluster.
There are \(m\geq1\) task kinds and a per-kind bound \(\beta\geq1\); both are fixed constants. A bundle is an integral vector \(Y\in\{0,\ldots,\beta\}^{m}\). Requester type \(a\) has a task-availability vector \(q_a\), and executor type \(b\) has a capability vector \(e_b\) with \(\sum_j(e_b)_j\leq\Delta_b\), its execution capacity; both vectors lie in this bundle domain. Their feasible bundles satisfy \(0\leq Y\leq q_a\) and \(0\leq Y\leq e_b\), respectively.
Requesters have unlimited budgets and normalized, nonnegative, monotone gross-substitutes valuations \(v_a\). Executor costs are opportunity costs \(c_b(Y)=w_b(e_b)-w_b(e_b-Y)\), where \(w_b\) is a normalized, nonnegative, monotone gross-substitutes valuation of retained capacity. All valuation tables are explicitly supplied as binary rationals; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For each compatible pair and nonempty common feasible bundle, \(x_{abY}\geq0\) is the mass trading that bundle. Require \(\sum_{b,Y}x_{abY}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_{a,Y}x_{abY}\leq\mu_b\). Each unit of participating mass trades one integral bundle with one counterparty; unused mass receives the empty bundle. Gain from trade is \(\sum_{a,b,Y}x_{abY}(v_a(Y)-c_b(Y))\).
Clearing prices are nonnegative rational vectors \(p^{h}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}^{m}\), one per cluster. Every allocated bundle must maximize \(v_a(Y)-p^{h}\cdot Y\) for its requester and \(p^{h}\cdot Y-c_b(Y)\) for its executor over their respective feasible domains; positive unused mass requires the empty bundle to maximize utility. Ties may be split arbitrarily. Each trade transfers \(p^{h}\cdot Y\), so demand and supply balance coordinatewise.

Is computing a feasible allocation of maximum gain from trade together with supporting clearing prices, or determining that no such prices support any optimum, solvable in polynomial time or NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k,\beta\), and the capacities \(\Delta_b\), retaining the fixed-constant assumptions on \(m\) and \(\beta\).

Discrete: TRUST-SC claims polynomial-time computation of its allocation and payments, but does not establish exact gain-from-trade maximization ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22241)).
Nearest known: MIDA studies finite gross-substitutes markets and establishes truthful, budget-balanced, asymptotically near-optimal trade under additional conditions; this does not settle the specified mass-and-bilateral-bundle problem ([source](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2018/0068.pdf)). No result for precisely this continuous model was located.

  • Normalized the combined requester and executor masses to one; the graded question did not specify normalization.
  • Used m for the number of task kinds and retained the discrete model's fixed-constant assumptions on the number of kinds and the per-kind bound beta.
  • Specified explicit binary-rational valuation tables because neither the anchor nor the graded question defines a computational representation.
  • Encoded requester task availability and executor capabilities by bounded integral vectors, with executor total capacity imposed on its capability vector.
  • Interpreted the executor's intended opportunity cost as w(e)-w(e-Y), correcting the reversed difference in the displayed utility; gross substitutes applies to retained-capacity valuations rather than directly to execution costs.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable under the stated fixed-constant assumptions on \(m\) and \(\beta\).

An exact algorithm computes a maximum-gain feasible allocation and then either produces nonnegative rational clearing prices supporting it or proves that no optimum has such prices. Gross substitutes are not needed for this algorithm; explicitly supplied finite valuation tables suffice.

Supporting prices need not exist, even for additive valuations.

1. Compute the exact bilateral optimum

Let \(A\) and \(B\) denote the requester and executor types. Write \(h(t)\) for the cluster of type \(t\), and define their feasible domains by

\( \displaystyle \mathcal F_a=\{Y\in\{0,\ldots,\beta\}^{m}:Y\leq q_a\},\qquad \mathcal F_b=\{Y\in\{0,\ldots,\beta\}^{m}:Y\leq e_b\}. \)

Both domains contain \(0\). Normalization gives \(v_a(0)=c_b(0)=0\).

Let \(\mathcal E\) contain every allowed triple \((a,b,Y)\), where the pair is compatible and \(Y\neq0\) belongs to both feasible domains. Set

\( \displaystyle g_{abY}=v_a(Y)-c_b(Y). \)

Solve the rational linear program

\( \displaystyle V^*=\max_{x\geq0}\sum_{(a,b,Y)\in\mathcal E}g_{abY}x_{abY} \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle \sum_{b,Y:(a,b,Y)\in\mathcal E}x_{abY}\leq\mu_a\quad(a\in A), \)

\( \displaystyle \sum_{a,Y:(a,b,Y)\in\mathcal E}x_{abY}\leq\mu_b\quad(b\in B). \)

This is exactly the specified allocation problem: each variable represents mass assigned one integral bundle and one counterparty. No relaxation of the bilateral restriction has been introduced.

The feasible region is nonempty because \(x=0\) is feasible. It is bounded because every variable satisfies \(x_{abY}\leq\min(\mu_a,\mu_b)\). Consequently, an optimum \(x^*\) exists, and rational linear programming computes \(x^*\) and \(V^*\) exactly.

2. Test for supporting prices with another linear system

Introduce nonnegative price vectors \(p^h\), requester utility bounds \(\alpha_a\), and executor utility bounds \(\gamma_b\). Consider the following rational linear feasibility problem:

\( \displaystyle p^h_j\geq0,\qquad \alpha_a\geq0,\qquad\gamma_b\geq0, \)

\( \displaystyle \alpha_a\geq v_a(Y)-p^{h(a)}\cdot Y\quad(a\in A,\;Y\in\mathcal F_a), \)

\( \displaystyle \gamma_b\geq p^{h(b)}\cdot Y-c_b(Y)\quad(b\in B,\;Y\in\mathcal F_b), \)

and

\[ \displaystyle \sum_{a\in A}\mu_a\alpha_a+\sum_{b\in B}\mu_b\gamma_b=V^*. \tag{1} \]

The constraints involving \(Y=0\) already imply the nonnegativity of \(\alpha_a\) and \(\gamma_b\). Most importantly, this system is linear: \(V^*\) is a previously computed rational constant.

Claim. This system is feasible if and only if some optimal bilateral allocation has supporting clearing prices. If it is feasible, its prices support the already computed allocation \(x^*\).

Proof of the claim

For any feasible allocation \(x\), define its unused masses by

\( \displaystyle s_a=\mu_a-\sum_{b,Y}x_{abY},\qquad s_b=\mu_b-\sum_{a,Y}x_{abY}. \)

Define the utility gaps

\( \displaystyle d_a(Y)=\alpha_a-v_a(Y)+p^{h(a)}\cdot Y, \)

\( \displaystyle d_b(Y)=\gamma_b-p^{h(b)}\cdot Y+c_b(Y). \)

All unused masses and utility gaps are nonnegative. Since every trade occurs within one cluster, its requester and executor face the same price vector. Expanding and cancelling the price terms therefore gives the exact identity

\[ \displaystyle \sum_a\mu_a\alpha_a+\sum_b\mu_b\gamma_b-G(x) =\sum_a s_a\alpha_a+\sum_b s_b\gamma_b+\sum_{(a,b,Y)\in\mathcal E}x_{abY}\bigl(d_a(Y)+d_b(Y)\bigr). \tag{2} \]

Every term on the right is nonnegative.

Sufficiency. Suppose the price system is feasible. Apply (2) to \(x^*\). By (1) and optimality, the left side is \(V^*-G(x^*)=0\). Thus every summand on the right vanishes.

If \(x^*_{abY}>0\), then \(d_a(Y)=d_b(Y)=0\). Hence

\( \displaystyle v_a(Y)-p^{h(a)}\cdot Y=\alpha_a,\qquad p^{h(b)}\cdot Y-c_b(Y)=\gamma_b. \)

The inequalities defining the utility bounds show that these bundles maximize utility over the respective feasible domains.

If \(s_a>0\), then \(\alpha_a=0\). Every feasible requester utility is at most zero, while the empty bundle has utility zero; hence the empty bundle maximizes utility. The executor argument is identical.

Finally, coordinatewise clearing holds automatically: each variable \(x^*_{abY}\) contributes the same vector \(x^*_{abY}Y\) to demand and supply. Thus all required conditions hold.

Necessity. Suppose an optimum \(\widehat x\) has supporting nonnegative rational prices \(p\). Choose the bounds to be the actual maximum utilities:

\( \displaystyle \alpha_a=\max_{Y\in\mathcal F_a}\bigl(v_a(Y)-p^{h(a)}\cdot Y\bigr), \)

\( \displaystyle \gamma_b=\max_{Y\in\mathcal F_b}\bigl(p^{h(b)}\cdot Y-c_b(Y)\bigr). \)

These maxima are rational because their domains are finite. They are nonnegative because the empty bundle is feasible.

Every allocated bundle has zero utility gap, and positive unused mass requires maximum utility zero. The right side of (2), evaluated at \(\widehat x\), is therefore zero. Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \sum_a\mu_a\alpha_a+\sum_b\mu_b\gamma_b=G(\widehat x)=V^*, \)

so the price system is feasible. This proves the claim.

The same identity also proves that every allocation with supporting clearing prices is globally optimal: those prices supply an upper bound on every feasible allocation’s gain, attained by the supported allocation.

3. Algorithm and correctness

The complete algorithm is:

  • Solve the bilateral allocation LP, obtaining \(x^*\) and \(V^*\).
  • Solve the price-feasibility system above.
  • If feasible, return \(x^*\), the resulting prices, and payments \(p^{h(a)}\cdot Y\).
  • If infeasible, report that no optimum has supporting nonnegative clearing prices.

The proof establishes that choosing an arbitrary optimum in the first step is safe: whenever prices support one optimum, those same prices support every optimum.

The only algorithmic theorem needed is that explicitly given rational linear programs admit exact optimization and feasibility testing in polynomial bit complexity. Its hypotheses hold here because both constructed systems have rational coefficients and polynomial encoding size under the fixed bounds. See [Khachiyan’s polynomial-time linear-programming result](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

4. Running time and capacity dependence

Let \(D=(\beta+1)^m\), and let \(r_h,s_h\) be the numbers of requester and executor types in cluster \(h\).

The allocation LP has \(n\) variables, where

\( \displaystyle n\leq(D-1)\sum_{h=1}^{k}r_hs_h\leq(D-1)\frac{\tau^2}{4}. \)

It has \(\tau\) mass constraints, besides variable nonnegativity.

The price system has \(\tau+km\) variables, at most \(\tau D\) bundle-utility inequalities, nonnegativity constraints, and one equality. Its coefficients are input rationals or differences of input rationals, together with integer bundle coordinates and the polynomial-bit-length optimum \(V^*\).

Exact rational LP algorithms therefore give the bit-operation bound

\( \displaystyle \bigl(L+km+m\tau^2(\beta+1)^m\bigr)^{O(1)}. \)

For fixed \(m\) and \(\beta\), this becomes

\( \displaystyle (L+\tau+k)^{O(1)}. \)

Empty clusters can receive zero prices; writing all \(k\) price vectors accounts for the \(km\) output term.

There is no dependence on the numerical magnitudes of the capacities \(\Delta_b\), beyond their input encoding lengths already included in \(L\). Indeed, every executor bundle satisfies

\( \displaystyle \sum_jY_j\leq\sum_j(e_b)_j\leq\Delta_b,\qquad\sum_j(e_b)_j\leq m\beta. \)

Thus the capacity bound requires no additional enumeration or iteration up to \(\Delta_b\).

5. Supporting prices can genuinely fail

Consider one cluster with \(m=2\), \(\beta=1\), and three types:

| Type | Mass | Availability or capability | Valuation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Requester \(a_1\) | \(3/8\) | \(q_{a_1}=(1,0)\) | \(v_{a_1}(Y)=Y_1\) |
| Requester \(a_2\) | \(3/8\) | \(q_{a_2}=(0,1)\) | \(v_{a_2}(Y)=Y_2\) |
| Executor \(b\) | \(1/4\) | \(e_b=(1,1)\), \(\Delta_b=2\) | \(w_b(Y)=0\) |

All masses are dyadic rationals and sum to one. The valuations are additive, hence gross substitutes: utility maximization separates by coordinate, so changing another coordinate’s price does not change the preferred quantity of an unchanged-price coordinate. Executor costs are identically zero.

Every allowed nonempty bilateral bundle is a singleton with gain one. Total trading mass is at most \(1/4\), and trading that mass attains the bound. Hence \(V^*=1/4\).

In every feasible allocation, each requester uses at most \(1/4\) of its mass \(3/8\). Both therefore have positive unused mass. Supporting prices would require the empty bundle to maximize their utilities, implying

\( \displaystyle p_1\geq1,\qquad p_2\geq1. \)

At these prices, the executor strictly prefers selling \((1,1)\) to either singleton or the empty bundle. But neither requester can receive \((1,1)\), whereas every optimum contains positive singleton trading mass. Thus no optimum has supporting prices.

This example verifies that the algorithm’s infeasibility outcome is necessary even within the promised gross-substitutes class.
