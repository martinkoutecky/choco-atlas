Theorem 9 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses of students by preference and priority-class type, partitioned into round-one matched students, leftover students \(L\), and arrivals \(N\), a stable round-one assignment, and initial school capacities, choose round-two assignments \(x_{b,h}\) of each type’s mass to schools or \(\emptyset\), and expanded capacities \(c'_h \ge c_h\). Preserve every round-one matched student’s school and require stability: no positive mass that prefers a school to its assignment may face either spare capacity there or assigned mass from a strictly lower priority class. Among feasible outcomes, maximize the mass from \(L\) assigned to schools, then minimize the mass from \(N\) assigned to schools.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) be a set of \(m\geq 1\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive masses \(\mu_b\), each a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Total mass is finite and need not equal \(1\).
Each type has a strict complete preference order \(\succ_b\) on \(H\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Each school orders priority classes and places \(\emptyset\) strictly between acceptable and unacceptable classes; members of one class tie, with no tie-breaking. A pair is mutually acceptable when both sides rank the other above \(\emptyset\).
Types also record membership in the round-one matched population \(F\), leftovers \(\mathcal L\), or arrivals \(\mathcal N\); types in \(F\) record their original school \(a_b\). The supplied round-one assignment sends all \(\mu_b\) of each \(b\in F\) to \(a_b\) and all leftovers to \(\emptyset\), and is stable at nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(c_h\). Preferences and priorities among original students remain unchanged when arrivals enter.
Choose divisible allocations \(x_{b,a}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{a\in H\cup\{\emptyset\}}x_{b,a}=\mu_b\), assigning mass only to mutually acceptable schools and requiring \(x_{b,a_b}=\mu_b\) for \(b\in F\). Write \(q_h=\sum_b x_{b,h}\) and use expanded capacities \(c'_h=\max\{c_h,q_h\}\), with no expansion budget.
Stability means that, for every mutually acceptable pair \((b,h)\), if \(\sum_{a:\,h\succ_b a}x_{b,a}>0\), then \(q_h=c'_h\) and \(x_{d,h}=0\) for every type \(d\) in a strictly lower priority class at \(h\). Apply the same definition to round one, excluding arrivals and using \(c_h\).
Among stable allocations, maximize \(A=\sum_{b\in\mathcal L,h\in H}x_{b,h}\), then minimize \(B=\sum_{b\in\mathcal N,h\in H}x_{b,h}\) subject to maximal \(A\). Let \(L\) denote the total input bit length.

Is this exact lexicographic optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For indivisible students with strict preferences and school priorities, this objective is NP-hard; reversing the objective order admits a polynomial-time algorithm ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.04431)).
No result settling exactly the divisible problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified arbitrary positive binary-rational masses without unit normalization, and nonnegative binary-rational initial capacities.
  • Restored strict complete student preferences including the outside option and mutual acceptability, allowing schools to reject priority classes.
  • Interpreted priority classes as school-specific ties without tie-breaking; only strictly higher priority creates a displacement block, as required by the graded question.
  • Included population status and original school in the support types so that preservation applies to every originally matched student's mass.
  • Preserved original preferences and relative school priorities when arrivals enter.
  • Used the explicit stable-extension capacity convention from both available versions: expanded capacity is the maximum of original capacity and assigned mass, retaining unused original seats.
  • Retained the graded question's promise of a stable round-one assignment rather than imposing student optimality.

Result

The exact lexicographic optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when every mass and original capacity equals \(1\), every school has distinct priority classes for distinct types, and every leftover type has exactly two mutually acceptable schools.

The crucial point is that admitting any positive leftover mass can force an entire unit of arrival mass to be admitted. Divisibility therefore does not eliminate the discrete selection cost.

1. Reduction from Vertex Cover

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of Vertex Cover: given a finite simple undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether there exists \(C\subseteq V\) with \(|C|\leq k\) such that every edge has an endpoint in \(C\). See [Karp, *Reducibility among Combinatorial Problems*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

We may restrict to graphs with at least one edge, since instances without edges are immediately decidable. Write \(\operatorname{vc}(G)\) for the minimum vertex-cover size.

For each vertex \(v\), create a school \(h_v\) with original capacity \(c_{h_v}=1\). Create the following types, all with mass \(1\):

  • An incumbent \(f_v\in F\), originally assigned to \(h_v\), who accepts only \(h_v\).
  • An arrival \(n_v\in\mathcal N\), who accepts only \(h_v\).
  • For every edge \(e=\{u,v\}\), a leftover \(\ell_e\in\mathcal L\), who accepts exactly \(h_u\) and \(h_v\), in either fixed strict order.

To obtain strict complete student preferences, place the stated acceptable schools above \(\emptyset\), and all other schools below \(\emptyset\), in arbitrary strict order.

At school \(h_v\), use the following priority order:

\[ \text{} \]

\(f_v\succ_{h_v}n_v\succ_{h_v}\text{all }\ell_e\text{ with }v\in e\succ_{h_v}\emptyset\succ_{h_v}\text{all remaining types}.\)

Order the incident leftover types strictly, and likewise the unacceptable types. Every type occupies its own priority class.

The supplied round-one assignment gives \(f_v\) its entire mass at \(h_v\) and leaves every \(\ell_e\) unmatched. Arrivals are excluded from round one.

There are \(m=|V|\) schools and \(\tau=2|V|+|E|\) types. All complete orders and numerical data can be written in polynomial time and have polynomial total bit length.

2. The supplied round-one assignment is stable

Each school has occupancy \(q_{h_v}=1=c_{h_v}\).

An incumbent has no mass assigned below its only acceptable school, so its stability antecedent is false.

Each leftover wants its two endpoint schools. At either endpoint school, the only assigned type is the incumbent, which has strictly higher priority than the leftover. Thus that school is full and assigns no mass to any type strictly below the leftover.

These checks cover all mutually acceptable pairs in round one. Hence the supplied assignment satisfies the promised stability condition. Inserting each arrival between its incumbent and the incident leftovers preserves all priorities among original types.

3. Positive leftover mass forces full arrival admission

Consider any feasible stable round-two allocation. Suppose that, for some vertex \(v\) and incident edge \(e\),

\(x_{\ell_e,h_v}>0.\)

The arrival \(n_v\) can be assigned only to \(h_v\) or to \(\emptyset\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{a:\,h_v\succ_{n_v}a}x_{n_v,a}=x_{n_v,\emptyset}=1-x_{n_v,h_v}.\)

If \(x_{n_v,h_v}<1\), this sum is positive. Stability for \((n_v,h_v)\) would then require every type strictly below \(n_v\) at \(h_v\) to receive zero mass there. But \(\ell_e\) is strictly below \(n_v\), contradicting \(x_{\ell_e,h_v}>0\).

Therefore,

\(x_{\ell_e,h_v}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{n_v,h_v}=1.\)

This implication holds however small the positive leftover mass is.

4. Every vertex cover gives a stable allocation

Let \(C\subseteq V\) be any vertex cover. Construct an allocation as follows:

  • Keep every incumbent at its original school.
  • Assign every arrival \(n_v\) with \(v\in C\) entirely to \(h_v\); leave all other arrivals unmatched.
  • Assign every leftover \(\ell_e\) entirely to its preferred endpoint school among those whose vertices belong to \(C\).

The last choice exists because \(C\) covers every edge. This allocation is feasible and respects mutual acceptability.

Every school retains its unit incumbent mass, so \(q_{h_v}\geq1=c_{h_v}\). Under the specified expansion rule, therefore,

\(c'_{h_v}=\max\{1,q_{h_v}\}=q_{h_v}.\)

Thus every school is full relative to its expanded capacity. We now check the priority condition for every possible stability antecedent.

Incumbents. Each incumbent receives its entire mass at its only acceptable school, so no antecedent is positive.

Arrivals at vertices in \(C\). These likewise receive their entire mass at their only acceptable school.

Arrivals at vertices outside \(C\). Each is unmatched and wants its own school. That school receives only its incumbent: no arrival is assigned there, and leftovers are assigned only to schools indexed by \(C\). The incumbent has higher priority than the arrival, so there is no assigned mass of lower priority.

Leftovers. Each leftover receives its entire mass at its preferred endpoint school indexed by \(C\). If it prefers its other endpoint school to its assignment, that other endpoint must lie outside \(C\). As just observed, such a school contains only its incumbent, whose priority is above the leftover.

All stability conditions hold. The resulting objective values are

\(A=|E|,\qquad B=|C|.\)

Taking \(C=V\) proves that all leftover mass can be admitted. Since its total mass is \(|E|\), the first-stage optimum is exactly \(A^\star=|E|\).

5. Every allocation with maximal leftover admission yields a vertex cover

Let \(x\) be any stable allocation with \(A=|E|\), allowing arbitrary splitting of mass. Define

\(\displaystyle C_x=\left\{v\in V:\sum_{e:\,v\in e}x_{\ell_e,h_v}>0\right\}.\)

Every leftover has mass \(1\), so its admitted mass is at most \(1\). Their admitted masses sum to \(|E|\); hence every leftover is admitted in full.

For \(e=\{u,v\}\), mutual acceptability then gives

\(x_{\ell_e,h_u}+x_{\ell_e,h_v}=1.\)

At least one summand is positive. Therefore \(u\in C_x\) or \(v\in C_x\), proving that \(C_x\) is a vertex cover.

By Section 3, each \(v\in C_x\) forces \(x_{n_v,h_v}=1\). Since each arrival accepts only its own school,

\(\displaystyle B=\sum_{v\in V}x_{n_v,h_v}\geq |C_x|\geq\operatorname{vc}(G).\)

Conversely, Section 4 applied to a minimum vertex cover gives a stable allocation with \(A=|E|\) and \(B=\operatorname{vc}(G)\). Thus the exact second-stage optimum is

\(B^\star=\operatorname{vc}(G).\)

6. Complexity conclusion

A polynomial-time exact lexicographic optimizer would compute \(B^\star\) for the constructed instance. Comparing it with \(k\) would decide Vertex Cover, because

\(B^\star\leq k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{vc}(G)\leq k.\)

The reduction satisfies every input promise and permits fully divisible allocations throughout. It proves that the stated optimization problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
