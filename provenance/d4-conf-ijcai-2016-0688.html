The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite worker-type and job-type sets \(H,D\), strict preferences over opposite-side types and \(\varnothing\), rational masses \(a_h,b_d\) with equal total mass on each side, and rational edge weights \(w_{hd}\), compute an allocation \(x_{hd}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{h,d} w_{hd}x_{hd}\). Require \(\sum_d x_{hd}\le a_h\), \(\sum_h x_{hd}\le b_d\), and \(x_{hd}=0\) unless both types prefer each other to \(\varnothing\). For every mutually acceptable pair, define \(u_h=a_h-\sum_d x_{hd}\), \(v_d=b_d-\sum_h x_{hd}\), \(A_{hd}=\sum_{d':\,d\succ_h d'}x_{hd'}+u_h\mathbf{1}[d\succ_h\varnothing]\), and \(B_{hd}=\sum_{h':\,h\succ_d h'}x_{h'd}+v_d\mathbf{1}[h\succ_d\varnothing]\); require \(A_{hd}B_{hd}=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H,D\) be nonempty finite sets of worker and job types. Each type has a strict total preference order over opposite-side types and \(\varnothing\), representing being unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|H|+|D|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Require \(\sum_h a_h=\sum_d b_d=T>0\), without normalizing \(T\). Let \(E=\{(h,d):d\succ_h\varnothing,\ h\succ_d\varnothing\}\), \(m=|E|\), and \(L\) be the total input bit length, including preferences and arbitrary signed rational weights \(w_{hd}\).
An allocation has real entries \(x_{hd}\ge0\), satisfies \(\sum_d x_{hd}\le a_h\), \(\sum_h x_{hd}\le b_d\), and has \(x_{hd}=0\) outside \(E\). Define unmatched masses \(u_h=a_h-\sum_d x_{hd}\) and \(v_d=b_d-\sum_h x_{hd}\).
For \((h,d)\in E\), define \(A_{hd}=u_h+\sum_{d':\,d\succ_h d'}x_{hd'}\) and \(B_{hd}=v_d+\sum_{h':\,h\succ_d h'}x_{h'd}\). Call the allocation stable when \(A_{hd}B_{hd}=0\) for every \((h,d)\in E\).
The optimization problem is to maximize \(\sum_{(h,d)\in E}w_{hd}x_{hd}\) over stable allocations; any maximizer is permitted.

Is this optimization problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is its threshold decision version, with binary rational threshold \(K\), \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete one-to-one matching with strict preferences, stable matchings are precisely the extreme points of the stable marriage polytope, yielding polynomial-time weighted optimization ([Roth–Rothblum–Vande Vate](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/moor.18.4.803)).
The continuous optimization problem above is already covered by polynomial-time minimum-cost bipartite stable allocation with real quotas and strict preferences ([Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)); consequently, this target is not open.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified nonempty supports and strictly positive masses, retaining arbitrary equal total mass rather than imposing probability normalization.
  • Defined tau as the combined support size, m as the number of mutually acceptable pairs, and L as the total binary input length.
  • Made explicit that weights may have either sign, allocations are real-valued, and any optimum is allowed.
  • Specified the rational-threshold decision version to make the hardness alternative precise.
  • Removed the unmatched-mass indicators because both equal one on every mutually acceptable pair.

Conclusion

The optimization problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Its threshold decision version therefore belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

We prove this by reducing it to minimum-cost bipartite stable allocation, including a check of stability equivalence and binary encoding complexity.

1. The theorem used

Dean–Munshi establish polynomial-time minimum-cost stable allocation on a bipartite graph with strict preferences, real vertex quotas, and real edge capacities. In their formulation, every vertex receives exactly its quota. An edge blocks precisely when it is unsaturated and both endpoints have positive allocation on less-preferred edges. See Sections 2 and 4 of [Dean–Munshi, *Faster Algorithms For Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

We use this theorem as a known result. The construction below verifies its hypotheses and its applicability to the stability definition in the question.

2. Reduction to exact vertex quotas

Introduce a dummy worker \(h_*\) and a dummy job \(d_*\). The augmented bipartite graph has edge set

\(E'=E\cup\{(h,d_*):h\in H\}\cup\{(h_*,d):d\in D\}\cup\{(h_*,d_*)\}\).

Give the original vertices quotas \(a_h\) and \(b_d\), and give both dummy vertices quota \(T\). Thus each side has total quota \(2T\).

Give every edge capacity equal to the smaller of its endpoint quotas. In particular, the capacities are:

  • \(\min(a_h,b_d)\) on \((h,d)\in E\);
  • \(a_h\) on \((h,d_*)\);
  • \(b_d\) on \((h_*,d)\);
  • \(T\) on \((h_*,d_*)\).

At every original vertex, retain the original order restricted to incident edges in \(E\), and put its dummy neighbor last. This is valid because every retained real neighbor is preferred to being unmatched. Each dummy vertex ranks all its real neighbors in any strict order, followed by the other dummy vertex.

All quotas and capacities are positive, and all preference lists are strict.

Claim. Feasible allocations in the question correspond bijectively to feasible allocations with exact quotas in the augmented instance.

To prove this, let \(x\) be an original feasible allocation and set \(s=\sum_{(h,d)\in E}x_{hd}\). Define its extension \(y\) by

\(y_{hd}=x_{hd},\qquad y_{h d_*}=u_h,\qquad y_{h_*d}=v_d,\qquad y_{h_*d_*}=s.\)

Every original vertex receives exactly its quota. Moreover,

\(\sum_h u_h=T-s,\qquad \sum_d v_d=T-s.\)

Consequently, the dummy worker receives total allocation \((T-s)+s=T\), and the dummy job does likewise.

The capacity bounds hold: \(x_{hd}\le\min(a_h,b_d)\), \(u_h\le a_h\), \(v_d\le b_d\), and \(s\le T\).

Conversely, restricting any augmented feasible allocation \(y\) to \(E\) gives an original feasible allocation \(x\). The exact-quota equations force \(y_{h d_*}=u_h\) and \(y_{h_*d}=v_d\). The dummy worker’s quota then forces \(y_{h_*d_*}=s\). Hence the extension is unique.

3. Exact equivalence of stability

Fix a real edge \((h,d)\in E\). In the extension \(y\), the total allocation at \(h\) on edges less preferred than \((h,d)\) is exactly

\(y_{h d_*}+\sum_{d':\,d\succ_h d'}y_{hd'}=A_{hd}.\)

The corresponding quantity at \(d\) is exactly \(B_{hd}\). Edges outside \(E\) contribute zero to the original sums, so deleting them does not change these identities.

The Dean–Munshi blocking condition for this edge is therefore

\(x_{hd}<\min(a_h,b_d),\qquad A_{hd}>0,\qquad B_{hd}>0.\)

The capacity condition is automatic whenever the other two conditions hold. Indeed, feasibility gives

\(A_{hd}\le a_h-x_{hd},\qquad B_{hd}\le b_d-x_{hd}.\)

Thus \(A_{hd}>0\) and \(B_{hd}>0\) imply \(x_{hd}<a_h\) and \(x_{hd}<b_d\).

Since \(A_{hd}\) and \(B_{hd}\) are nonnegative, we conclude that a real edge blocks in the augmented instance exactly when \(A_{hd}B_{hd}>0\).

No dummy edge can block:

  • For \((h,d_*)\), the worker \(h\) has no less-preferred incident edge.
  • For \((h_*,d)\), the job \(d\) has no less-preferred incident edge.
  • For \((h_*,d_*)\), neither endpoint has a less-preferred incident edge.

Therefore, \(x\) satisfies every condition \(A_{hd}B_{hd}=0\) if and only if its extension \(y\) is a stable allocation in the Dean–Munshi model.

4. Objective and binary complexity

Assign cost \(c_{hd}=-w_{hd}\) to every real edge and cost zero to every dummy edge. Then

\(\sum_{e\in E'}c_e y_e=-\sum_{(h,d)\in E}w_{hd}x_{hd}.\)

The established bijection preserves stability, so restricting a minimum-cost augmented stable allocation produces a maximizer for the original problem.

The augmented instance has \(N=\tau+2\) vertices and \(M=m+\tau+1\) edges. Its encoding length is polynomial in \(L,m,\tau\): computing \(T\), taking minima, and adding dummy preference entries require only polynomially many operations on binary rationals.

We also check that real-quota arithmetic does not conceal dependence on quota magnitudes. The Dean–Munshi construction uses polynomially many additions, subtractions, and minimum operations to obtain allocations and rotation multiplicities, then solves a minimum-cost closure problem. A rotation’s cost is its multiplicity times the signed sum of its edge costs. These are the algorithmic ingredients described in Sections 3–4 of the [paper](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, and \(R\) the product of the denominators of all weights. Both have bit length at most \(O(L)\). Scaling all augmented quotas and capacities by \(Q\), and all costs by \(R\), produces integral data of polynomial bit length.

This scaling preserves the problem: allocations scale by \(Q\), stability is unchanged, and the objective scales by the positive factor \(QR\).

On these integral data, additions, subtractions, and minimum operations preserve integrality. After a polynomial number \(p\) of additions or subtractions, integer bit length grows by at most \(p\), beyond the maximum starting bit length. Rotation-cost multiplication and summation also have polynomial bit length. The resulting closure problem has polynomial size and integer costs of polynomial bit length, so an exact polynomial-time minimum-cut algorithm solves it in the binary model.

Reconstructing the chosen allocation and dividing by \(Q\) consequently returns an exact rational optimizer in polynomial time. No expansion into individual workers or jobs is required, and \(T\) need not be normalized.

Finally, evaluate its rational objective and compare it exactly with the input threshold \(K\). This establishes polynomial-time solvability of the threshold version as well.
