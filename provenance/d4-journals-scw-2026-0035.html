The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(M\) group types with rational masses \(\alpha_\lambda>0\), \(\sum_\lambda\alpha_\lambda=1\), and rational \(J_0,\bar J\) satisfying \(0\leq\bar J\leq J_0\) and \(J_0+(M-1)\bar J<1\), compute the ray of council weights minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[(S/\sqrt{N}-\sum_\lambda w_\lambda\chi_\lambda)^2]\) as group populations grow with \(N_\lambda/N\to\alpha_\lambda\), under the paper's balanced mean-field coupling.

Input also includes binary rationals \(0\leq\bar J\leq J_0\) with \(J_0+(M-1)\bar J<1\), and a precision parameter \(k\geq1\); let \(L\) be the total rational input bit length.

1. Classification

Computing the optimal ray is deterministic polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L,k\) on the stated domain, where \(m=2\) and \(\tau=M\).

The key is an explicit normalized formula together with a uniform precision bound. Although the coupling can approach the boundary arbitrarily closely, its rational encoding limits the number of additional bits needed.

The hypotheses match the “Friendly World” regime in [Kirsch and Toth, Section 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.08636v2). Below, I derive the ray and prove the bit-complexity bound directly.

If \(M=1\), the answer is exactly \(u_1=1\). Henceforth assume \(M\geq2\).

2. The limiting quadratic optimization problem

Write \(b=\bar J\), and define

\(d=1-J_0-(M-1)b>0,\qquad a=1-J_0+b=d+Mb.\)

Let \(\mathbf 1\) denote the all-ones column vector. Then

\(J=(J_0-b)I+b\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}.\)

Its eigenvalues are \(J_0-b\), on the subspace orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\), and \(J_0+(M-1)b\), on its span. Thus \(J\) is positive semidefinite and its largest eigenvalue \(h=J_0+(M-1)b\) satisfies \(h<1\).

I first justify the Gaussian limit, including the moment convergence needed for \(F\).

Under independent uniform votes, put \(Y_{N,\lambda}=S_\lambda/\sqrt{N_\lambda}\). Since every \(\alpha_\lambda>0\), each \(N_\lambda\) tends to infinity. The classical central limit theorem, and independence between groups, give convergence of \(Y_N\) to a standard \(M\)-dimensional Gaussian.

For every real vector \(v\), independence and \(\cosh t\leq e^{t^2/2}\) give

\(\mathbb E_0 e^{v^{\mathsf T}Y_N}\leq e^{\|v\|_2^2/2}.\)

If \(G\) is an independent standard Gaussian vector, Gaussian integration and this inequality imply, for \(0<t<1\),

\(\mathbb E_0 e^{t\|Y_N\|_2^2/2} =\mathbb E_G\mathbb E_0 e^{\sqrt t\,G^{\mathsf T}Y_N} \leq\mathbb E_G e^{t\|G\|_2^2/2} =(1-t)^{-M/2}.\)

The voting distribution in the question is precisely this independent distribution tilted by \(e^{Y_N^{\mathsf T}JY_N/2}\). Choose \(h<t<1\). For any function bounded in absolute value by a constant times \(1+\|y\|_2^2\), its tilted tail outside \(\|y\|_2>R\) tends uniformly to zero: indeed,

\((1+\|y\|_2^2)e^{h\|y\|_2^2/2} \leq C_{h,t}e^{-(t-h)R^2/4}e^{t\|y\|_2^2/2}\)

on that set. The preceding exponential-moment bound applies.

Consequently, truncation and weak convergence justify convergence of the tilted normalization and all the moments below. The limiting density is proportional to

\(e^{-\|y\|_2^2/2}e^{y^{\mathsf T}Jy/2} =e^{-y^{\mathsf T}(I-J)y/2}.\)

Thus the limiting vector \(Y\) is centered Gaussian with covariance

\(C=(I-J)^{-1} =\frac1a I+\frac{b}{ad}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}.\)

Functions involving signs have discontinuities only on coordinate hyperplanes. The limiting Gaussian assigns these hyperplanes probability zero, so the same truncation argument applies. In particular, the tie convention has no effect on the limit.

Define \(s_\lambda=\sqrt{\alpha_\lambda}\), \(s=(s_\lambda)_\lambda\), and \(T=\sum_\lambda s_\lambda\). Because \(S/\sqrt N=\sum_\lambda\sqrt{N_\lambda/N}\,Y_{N,\lambda}\), we obtain

\(F(w)=\mathbb E\left[\left(s^{\mathsf T}Y-\sum_\lambda w_\lambda\operatorname{sgn}(Y_\lambda)\right)^2\right].\)

Put

\(A_{\lambda\nu}=\mathbb E[\operatorname{sgn}(Y_\lambda)\operatorname{sgn}(Y_\nu)],\qquad \beta_\lambda=\mathbb E[\operatorname{sgn}(Y_\lambda)s^{\mathsf T}Y].\)

Then

\(F(w)=s^{\mathsf T}Cs-2\beta^{\mathsf T}w+w^{\mathsf T}Aw.\)

3. Explicit formula and positivity

All diagonal entries of \(C\) equal \(c=(d+b)/(ad)\), and all distinct-coordinate correlations equal

\(q=\frac{b}{d+b}\in[0,1).\)

Define

\(r=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin q.\)

For two standard Gaussian variables with correlation \(q\), write them as \(U\) and \(qU+\sqrt{1-q^2}V\), with \(U,V\) independent standard Gaussians. Their rotationally invariant joint density shows that the probability of opposite signs is \(\arccos(q)/\pi\). Therefore their sign-product expectation is \(r\), and

\(A=(1-r)I+r\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}.\)

Its eigenvalues are \(1-r>0\) and \(1+(M-1)r>0\). Hence \(F\) has a unique minimizer.

Gaussian conditional expectation gives

\(\mathbb E[Y_\nu\mid Y_\lambda]=(C_{\nu\lambda}/c)Y_\lambda.\)

Using \(\mathbb E|Y_\lambda|=\sqrt{2c/\pi}\), it follows that

\(\beta_\lambda=\gamma\big((1-q)s_\lambda+qT\big),\qquad \gamma=\sqrt{2c/\pi}>0.\)

The inverse of \(A\) is

\(A^{-1}=\frac1{1-r}\left(I-\frac{r}{1+(M-1)r}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\right).\)

Multiplying gives the actual minimizer

\(w_\lambda^*=\frac{\gamma}{1-r} \left((1-q)s_\lambda+\frac{q-r}{1+(M-1)r}T\right).\)

To check positivity, \(\arcsin x\) is convex on \([0,1]\): its second derivative is \(x/(1-x^2)^{3/2}\geq0\) in the interior. A convex function lies below the chord joining its endpoints, so

\(\arcsin q\leq\frac{\pi q}{2},\qquad 0\leq r\leq q<1.\)

Every \(s_\lambda\) is positive. Hence every \(w_\lambda^*\) is positive.

Set \(\delta=1-q\). Normalizing the preceding formula gives

\(\displaystyle u_\lambda=\theta\frac{s_\lambda}{T}+\frac{1-\theta}{M},\qquad \theta=\frac{\delta\big(1+(M-1)r\big)} {(1-r)\big(1+(M-1)q\big)}.\)

Indeed, the algebraic identity

\(\delta\big(1+(M-1)r\big)+M(q-r) =(1-r)\big(1+(M-1)q\big)\)

verifies both the normalization and this expression.

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle 1-\theta=\frac{M(q-r)} {(1-r)\big(1+(M-1)q\big)}\geq0.\)

Thus \(0<\theta\leq1\): the normalized ray is a convex mixture of the normalized square-root masses and the uniform vector.

4. Uniform precision bound

All rational calculations in this section can be performed exactly.

Let \(Q_0,Q_b\) be the denominators in the input representations of \(J_0,b\). Because \(d>0\) and \(Q_0Q_b d\) is an integer,

\(d\geq\frac1{Q_0Q_b}\geq2^{-L}.\)

Since \(M\geq2\),

\(0<d+b=1-J_0-(M-2)b\leq1.\)

Consequently,

\(\delta=\frac{d}{d+b}\geq d\geq2^{-L}.\)

This is the required quantitative bound near the coupling boundary.

Choose the working precision

\(p=k+L+\lceil\log_2(8M)\rceil,\qquad \varepsilon=2^{-p}.\)

Compute rational approximations satisfying

\(|\widehat s_\lambda-s_\lambda|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad |\widehat r-r|\leq\varepsilon.\)

Clip \(\widehat s_\lambda\) to \([0,1]\) and \(\widehat r\) to \([0,q]\). These operations cannot increase the errors, because the true values belong to those intervals.

Define, using exact rational arithmetic,

\(\displaystyle \widehat T=\sum_\lambda\widehat s_\lambda,\qquad \widehat\theta= \frac{\delta\big(1+(M-1)\widehat r\big)} {(1-\widehat r)\big(1+(M-1)q\big)},\qquad \widehat u_\lambda= \widehat\theta\frac{\widehat s_\lambda}{\widehat T} +\frac{1-\widehat\theta}{M}.\)

Here are explicit error bounds.

First, \(T\geq1\), because \(T^2\geq\sum_\lambda s_\lambda^2=1\). Also,

\(|\widehat T-T|\leq M\varepsilon.\)

Our precision choice gives \(M\varepsilon\leq1/2\), so \(\widehat T\geq1/2\). Since \(s_\lambda\leq1\),

\(\displaystyle \left|\frac{\widehat s_\lambda}{\widehat T} -\frac{s_\lambda}{T}\right| \leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\widehat T} +\frac{s_\lambda|\widehat T-T|}{T\widehat T} \leq2\varepsilon+2M\varepsilon \leq4M\varepsilon.\)

Second, consider \(\theta\) as a function of its argument \(r\), keeping \(q\) fixed. Differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle \theta'(r)= \frac{M\delta} {\big(1+(M-1)q\big)(1-r)^2}.\)

Throughout \(0\leq r\leq q\), we have \(1-r\geq\delta\). Therefore

\(0\leq\theta'(r)\leq M/\delta,\qquad |\widehat\theta-\theta|\leq M\varepsilon/\delta.\)

The same positivity calculation from Section 3 gives \(0\leq\widehat\theta\leq1\). Since \(s_\lambda/T\) and \(1/M\) belong to \([0,1]\),

\(\displaystyle |\widehat u_\lambda-u_\lambda| \leq4M\varepsilon+\frac{M\varepsilon}{\delta} \leq\frac{5M\varepsilon}{\delta}.\)

Finally, \(\varepsilon\leq2^{-k}2^{-L}/(8M)\) and \(\delta\geq2^{-L}\), so

\(\displaystyle \|\widehat u-u\|_\infty \leq\frac58\,2^{-k}<2^{-k}.\)

The output also satisfies \(\sum_\lambda\widehat u_\lambda=1\) exactly.

5. Polynomial-time evaluation of the elementary functions

It remains to justify that the approximations used above have polynomial bit complexity. This requires a uniformly convergent procedure for \(\arcsin q\), rather than its slowly converging Taylor series near \(q=1\).

Square roots

For a nonnegative rational \(x\leq4\), bisection on \([0,2]\), comparing the square of each rational midpoint with \(x\), approximates \(\sqrt x\) to error \(2^{-n}\) after \(O(n)\) steps. Every comparison uses integers with bit length polynomial in the input length and \(n\).

This computes each \(\widehat s_\lambda\) with polynomial cost.

The inverse-trigonometric term

Use two half-angle reductions:

\(\displaystyle z=\frac{q}{1+\sqrt{1-q^2}},\qquad v=\frac{z}{1+\sqrt{1+z^2}},\qquad \arcsin q=4\arctan v.\)

The identities follow from the tangent half-angle formulas. Since \(0\leq q<1\), we have \(0\leq z<1\), and therefore

\(0\leq v\leq\sqrt2-1<1/2.\)

On \([0,1/2]\), the alternating series

\(\displaystyle \arctan x=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^j x^{2j+1}}{2j+1}\)

has remainder after \(n\) terms at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}\leq2^{-(2n+1)}.\)

Thus \(O(p)\) terms suffice, uniformly over the input.

For completeness, \(\pi\) can be computed by the same series using Machin’s identity,

\(\pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239).\)

To verify the identity, if \(a=\arctan(1/5)\), then the tangent addition formula gives \(\tan(4a)=120/119\), and hence \(\tan(4a-\arctan(1/239))=1\). This angle lies in \((0,\pi/2)\), so it equals \(\pi/4\).

Here is an explicit error budget. Put \(\zeta=\varepsilon/64\).

  • Approximate \(\sqrt{1-q^2}\) within \(\zeta\), keeping the approximation in \([0,1]\). The resulting \(\widehat z\) differs from \(z\) by at most \(\zeta\).
  • Approximate \(\sqrt{1+\widehat z^2}\) within \(\zeta\), keeping it in \([1,2]\). The derivative of \(x\mapsto\sqrt{1+x^2}\) has absolute value at most \(1\) on \([0,1]\), so this approximation differs from \(\sqrt{1+z^2}\) by at most \(2\zeta\).
  • The resulting approximation to \(v\) differs by at most \(3\zeta\); it belongs to \([0,1/2]\).
  • Approximate its arctangent by the series with remainder at most \(\zeta\). Since \(\arctan\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz, the resulting approximation \(\widehat A\) to \(\arcsin q\) has error at most \(16\zeta\).
  • Approximate both arctangents in Machin’s identity within \(\zeta\). This gives a \(\pi\)-approximation with error at most \(20\zeta\); clipping it to \([3,4]\) preserves that bound.

Because \(\arcsin q\leq2\) and both the true and approximated \(\pi\) are at least \(3\), exact rational division then gives

\(\displaystyle \left|\frac{2\widehat A}{\widehat\pi} -\frac{2\arcsin q}{\pi}\right| \leq\frac23(16\zeta)+\frac49(20\zeta) <20\zeta<\varepsilon.\)

Clipping this result to \([0,q]\) produces the required \(\widehat r\).

All square-root approximations require \(O(p)\) bisection steps, and all series require \(O(p)\) terms. Exact rational powers and sums have polynomial bit length: powers raise denominator bit lengths by \(O(p)\), and combining \(O(p)\) rational terms still produces only polynomially many bits. Hence these procedures run in polynomial time in \(L,p\).

6. Complexity conclusion

The working precision is

\(p=O(k+L+\log M).\)

The algorithm computes \(M\) square roots, one inverse-trigonometric value, and then polynomially many exact rational operations. Its running time and output bit length are polynomial in \(M,L,k\). Since \(M=\tau\) is the number of explicitly listed supported types, no enumeration of unsupported types is required.

This settles the requested approximation problem on the specified domain. An NP-hardness result under standard polynomial-time reductions would therefore imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
