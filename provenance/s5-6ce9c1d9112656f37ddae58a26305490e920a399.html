The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given fixed \(K\), a finite set of patient-donor types with compatibility and rankings, and rational masses \(\mu_t\), maximize the matched mass over cycle-template allocations satisfying \(\sum_r N_t(r)z_r\leq\mu_t\), subject to there being no feasible template that can form a positive-mass local block using unmatched agents or agents who strictly prefer the proposed donor type. Is this optimization problem tractable, and how does its complexity depend on \(K\) and the number of types?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) patient–donor types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\) has a donor category \(d(t)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), a set of compatible donor categories excluding \(d(t)\), and a weak preference order over that set. Compatibility and preferences depend only on these types and categories; every compatible donor is strictly preferred to remaining unmatched. Ties are retained.
Fix an integer \(K\ge2\). A cycle template \(r=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), with \(2\le\ell\le K\), is feasible when \(d(t_i)\) is compatible with patient type \(t_{i+1}\), with cyclic indices. Types may repeat, using distinct individuals at different positions. Let \(N_t(r)\) count occurrences of \(t\).
An allocation consists of divisible masses \(z_r\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_rN_t(r)z_r\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\). Each position of template \(r\) receives mass \(z_r\) of its predecessor's donor category; the remaining mass is unmatched. Its matched mass is \(\sum_r\ell(r)z_r\).
A positive-mass local block is a feasible template whose positions can each recruit mass \(\varepsilon>0\) from mutually disjoint portions of the society, each recruited individual being unmatched or strictly preferring the proposed donor category to the currently assigned one. A positive total mass of recruits must already be matched. An allocation is locally stable if no such block exists; the zero allocation is allowed.

For each fixed \(K\ge2\), is exact maximum-matched-mass locally stable exchange solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard? How does the complexity depend on \(K\) and \(\tau\)?

Discrete: deciding whether an arbitrary digraph has a nonempty local kernel is NP-complete, so maximizing its size is NP-hard; a local kernel is an independent set whose every out-neighbor has an arc back to the set. This does not establish hardness for kidney-exchange blocking digraphs. [Baratto et al.](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/304478)
Discrete maximum locally stable exchange with \(K=2\) and strict preferences is polynomial-time solvable. [Vandomme et al.](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/330279/1/Local_stability_Complexity_ORBI.pdf)
No result settling the continuous problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, as the continuous society is a distribution.
  • Defined m as the number of donor categories and specified category-based compatibility and preferences for each supported patient–donor type.
  • Excluded compatibility with a type's own donor category, reflecting the population of incompatible patient–donor pairs; used cycle lengths from two through K.
  • Allowed weak preference orders, as in the discrete definition, retaining ties and requiring strict improvement by matched recruits.
  • Allowed repeated types in templates while requiring distinct individuals at different positions, since distinct discrete agents may have identical types.
  • Made allocation masses divisible nonnegative real quantities and defined positive-mass recruitment through disjoint portions of the current assignment.
  • Restored the local-intersection condition: a block must recruit positive mass of already matched individuals, rather than consist entirely of unmatched individuals.
  • Allowed the zero allocation, consistent with the discrete empty exchange being locally stable.

1. Result

For every fixed \(K\ge 2\), exact maximum-matched-mass locally stable exchange is NP-hard under the stated assumptions, which retain ties. Its rational-threshold decision version is NP-complete.

The problem is nevertheless fixed-parameter tractable in the support size \(\tau\). An exact algorithm runs in

\(2^{O(\tau\log(\tau+1))}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+\log K).\)

Consequently, it is polynomial-time solvable for fixed \(\tau\), even when \(K\) is part of the input. Moreover, replacing \(K\) by \(\min\{K,\tau\}\) does not change the optimum.

The proof uses a reduction within the specified exchange model. It does not rely on hardness for arbitrary blocking digraphs.

2. Characterizing positive-mass local blocks

Write \(c\succ_t a\) when type \(t\) strictly prefers donor category \(c\) to category \(a\).

For an allocation, let \(x_{t,a}\) denote the mass of type \(t\) assigned category \(a\), and let

\(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_a x_{t,a}\)

be its unmatched mass. For each compatible category \(c\), define

\(B_t(c)=\sum_{a:\,c\succ_t a}x_{t,a},\qquad W_t(c)=u_t+B_t(c).\)

Thus \(B_t(c)\) is the already matched mass that strictly prefers \(c\), while \(W_t(c)\) is all mass willing to receive \(c\).

Blocking criterion. A feasible template \(r=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), with proposed categories \(c_i=d(t_{i-1})\), locally blocks precisely when

\(W_{t_i}(c_i)>0\quad\text{for every }i,\qquad B_{t_j}(c_j)>0\quad\text{for some }j.\)

Proof. Necessity follows directly from the recruitment requirements.

For sufficiency, designate a position \(j\) with positive \(B_{t_j}(c_j)\). Choose \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfying

\(\ell\varepsilon<\min\bigl(\{W_{t_i}(c_i):1\le i\le\ell\}\cup\{B_{t_j}(c_j)\}\bigr).\)

First recruit mass \(\varepsilon\) from the already matched, strictly improving pool at position \(j\). Recruit the other positions sequentially. Before each recruitment, previous recruits have removed at most \((\ell-1)\varepsilon\) from that position’s eligible pool, leaving more than \(\varepsilon\). Divisibility permits selecting the required portions.

This construction handles repeated types and overlapping eligible pools, gives mutually disjoint recruits, and includes positive already matched mass. ∎

Simple type cycles suffice

Form the directed compatibility graph on types: an arc \(s\to t\) exists when \(d(s)\) is compatible with patient type \(t\). There are no self-loops, because \(d(t)\) is excluded from type \(t\)’s compatibility set.

Every template is a closed directed walk. Repeatedly splitting a closed walk at repeated vertices decomposes its edge occurrences into simple directed cycles. Each component has length at least two and no greater than the original length.

Replacing mass on a template by equal mass on its component cycles preserves every \(x_{t,a}\): these quantities count incoming edge occurrences with the corresponding donor category. It therefore preserves feasibility, unmatched masses, matched mass, and local stability.

Likewise, a blocking closed walk decomposes into simple cycles whose edges all satisfy \(W>0\). At least one component contains an edge satisfying \(B>0\), so that component blocks by the criterion above.

Hence allocations and blocks may both be restricted to simple type cycles of length at most \(\min\{K,\tau\}\).

3. NP-hardness for every fixed cycle bound

Source problem

We use the following known NP-complete problem:

Given equally sized sets \(A,B\), a bipartite graph of mutually acceptable pairs, and weak preference lists, decide whether there is a perfect weakly stable matching. Weak stability means that no acceptable pair strictly prefers each other to their assigned partners.

This perfect-matching restriction follows from Manlove’s Theorem 2.1 and its reduction: the constructed instance has \(3n-k\) agents on each side, and the target matching size is exactly \(3n-k\). Hardness holds even when ties occur on only one side. [Manlove, *Stable Marriage with Ties and Unacceptable Partners*, Section 2](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~davidm/pubs/6497.pdf)

Let \(G=(A\cup B,E)\) be such an instance, and put \(N=|A|+|B|\).

Construction

Fix \(K\ge2\), and set

\(s=\lfloor K/2\rfloor.\)

For every original agent \(v\), introduce \(s\) types

\((v,1),\ldots,(v,s).\)

Give every introduced type its own distinct donor category. Type \((v,1)\) is the main type for \(v\).

Define compatibility and preferences as follows:

  • The main type \((v,1)\) accepts precisely the categories \(d(u,s)\) for acceptable original partners \(u\). Its weak preferences reproduce \(v\)’s preferences.
  • For each \(j\ge2\), type \((v,j)\) accepts only category \(d(v,j-1)\).

These compatibility sets exclude each type’s own donor category.

Choose a positive dyadic rational \(a=2^{-p}\) small enough that

\(N(2s-1)a\le\tfrac12.\)

Give each main type mass \(a\), and each intermediate type mass \(2a\). Add one inert type, with empty compatibility set, of mass

\(1-N(2s-1)a.\)

All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. For fixed \(K\), the construction has polynomial size.

The only feasible templates represent original pairs

The compatibility arcs are exactly

\((v,j)\to(v,j+1)\quad(j<s),\)

and

\((u,s)\to(v,1)\quad\text{when }uv\in E.\)

Every closed walk traverses complete chains between successive main-type positions. Its length is therefore \(qs\), where \(q\) counts main-type positions.

At each transition between chains, the original agent moves between \(A\) and \(B\). Thus \(q\) is positive and even. Since

\(2s\le K<4s,\)

a template of length at most \(K\) must have \(q=2\). Consequently, up to cyclic rotation, every feasible template is

\(R_{uv}=((u,1),\ldots,(u,s),(v,1),\ldots,(v,s))\)

for some \(uv\in E\).

This argument covers repeated-type templates as well: a template traversing four or more chains exceeds the length bound.

Aggregate allocation variables for cyclic rotations into \(z_{uv}\). Define

\(x_v=\sum_{u:\,uv\in E}z_{uv}.\)

Every type in \(v\)’s chain has matched mass \(x_v\). Feasibility is equivalent to \(x_v\le a\), since the intermediate capacities are \(2a\).

Therefore every allocation has matched mass

\(\sum_{uv\in E}2s\,z_{uv}=s\sum_v x_v\le sNa.\)

Use the decision threshold

\(Q=sNa.\)

Equality holds exactly when \(x_v=a\) for every original agent \(v\).

A perfect weakly stable matching attains the threshold

Suppose the source instance has a perfect weakly stable matching \(M\). Set

\(z_{uv}=a\quad\text{for }uv\in M,\qquad z_{uv}=0\quad\text{otherwise}.\)

Every main type is fully matched, and every intermediate type has matched mass \(a\) and unmatched mass \(a\). The matched mass is \(Q\).

Suppose some \(R_{uv}\) locally blocks. Its two main positions cannot recruit unmatched individuals, because the main types are fully matched. Thus both original agents \(u,v\) must strictly prefer each other to their partners in \(M\). This contradicts weak stability.

Hence the allocation is locally stable and attains \(Q\).

A locally stable allocation attaining the threshold yields a perfect weakly stable matching

Conversely, suppose a locally stable allocation attains \(Q\). Then every main type is fully matched: \(x_v=a\).

Set \(y_{uv}=z_{uv}/a\). These values satisfy

\(y_{uv}\ge0,\qquad \sum_{u:\,uv\in E}y_{uv}=1\quad\text{for every }v.\)

Consider the bipartite support graph consisting of edges with \(y_{uv}>0\). For every \(S\subseteq A\),

\(|S|=\sum_{u\in S}\sum_v y_{uv}\le\sum_{v\in N(S)}\sum_u y_{uv}=|N(S)|.\)

Hall’s theorem therefore gives a matching covering \(A\). Because \(|A|=|B|\), this matching \(M\) is perfect, and every edge of \(M\) has positive \(z\)-mass.

Suppose \(uv\) blocks \(M\) in the original instance. Let \(p=M(u)\) and \(q=M(v)\). Since the matching uses support edges, positive mass of main type \((u,1)\) receives \(d(p,s)\), and positive mass of main type \((v,1)\) receives \(d(q,s)\).

The alleged blocking pair implies that these positive matched masses strictly prefer \(d(v,s)\) and \(d(u,s)\), respectively.

Every intermediate position in \(R_{uv}\) has unmatched mass

\(2a-x_v=a>0,\)

and accepts its proposed predecessor category. Thus every position has positive willing mass, and the main positions have positive already matched, strictly improving mass. The blocking criterion makes \(R_{uv}\) a local block, a contradiction.

Therefore \(M\) is weakly stable.

We have proved

\(\text{source instance has a perfect weakly stable matching}\iff\text{constructed society has a locally stable allocation of mass at least }Q.\)

This proves NP-hardness for every fixed \(K\ge2\). The intermediate unmatched masses are essential: they allow the chain positions to participate in blocks even though their preferences have only one compatible category.

4. An exact algorithm parameterized by the support size

Only donor categories occurring among the supported types can be assigned or proposed. There are at most \(\tau\) such categories.

For each type \(t\), restrict its preference list to these categories and number its indifference classes from best to worst:

\(1,\ldots,q_t,\qquad q_t\le\tau.\)

Write \(\rho_t(c)\) for the rank of category \(c\).

Enumerating states

For each type, enumerate a state \((h_t,e_t)\), where

\(h_t\in\{0,\ldots,q_t\},\qquad e_t\in\{0,1\}.\)

Its interpretation is:

  • \(h_t=0\) permits no matched mass.
  • \(h_t>0\) permits assigned categories only at ranks at most \(h_t\).
  • \(e_t=0\) requires zero unmatched mass; \(e_t=1\) permits unmatched mass.

For a compatible proposed category \(c\), define Boolean flags

\(\widehat B_t(c)=[h_t>\rho_t(c)],\qquad \widehat W_t(c)=[e_t=1\ \text{or}\ h_t>\rho_t(c)].\)

Declare a state safe when there is no feasible simple cycle of permitted length for which every position has \(\widehat W=1\) and some position has \(\widehat B=1\).

There are at most

\(\prod_t2(q_t+1)\le(2\tau+2)^\tau\)

states.

The linear program for a safe state

Enumerate feasible simple type cycles of length at most \(\min\{K,\tau\}\). For a given safe state, retain only cycles whose proposed category at every position \(t\) has rank at most \(h_t\).

Solve the rational linear program maximizing matched mass, subject to

\(\sum_rN_t(r)z_r\le\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t,\)

\(\sum_rN_t(r)z_r=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t\text{ with }e_t=0,\)

and \(z_r\ge0\).

For \(h_t=0\), no retained cycle contains \(t\).

Every feasible point of this program is locally stable. Indeed, positive unmatched mass implies \(e_t=1\). Positive matched mass strictly preferring a proposal of rank \(\rho_t(c)\) requires an assigned rank larger than \(\rho_t(c)\), hence \(h_t>\rho_t(c)\). Thus

\(W_t(c)>0\implies\widehat W_t(c)=1,\qquad B_t(c)>0\implies\widehat B_t(c)=1.\)

Any actual block would contradict safety.

Every locally stable allocation belongs to the program of some safe state. After decomposing its templates into simple cycles, set \(e_t=1\) precisely when \(u_t>0\). Set \(h_t=0\) if \(t\) has no matched mass; otherwise let \(h_t\) be the worst assigned rank carrying positive mass.

For these choices,

\(\widehat W_t(c)=1\iff W_t(c)>0,\qquad \widehat B_t(c)=1\iff B_t(c)>0.\)

The state is therefore safe, and the allocation satisfies its linear program.

It follows that maximizing over all safe-state programs gives exactly the requested optimum.

Running time and attainment

The number of ordered simple cycles is at most

\(\sum_{\ell=2}^{\tau}\tau^\ell\le\tau^{\tau+1}\quad(\tau\ge2).\)

Enumerating states and cycles, checking safety, and solving the resulting explicitly described rational linear programs therefore takes

\(2^{O(\tau\log(\tau+1))}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L+\log K)\)

time.

Every state program is a closed, bounded rational polytope: each variable uses positive mass of at least one type and is bounded by that type’s capacity. There are finitely many states, and the zero allocation is locally stable. Hence the maximum exists and the algorithm computes it exactly.

This proves fixed-parameter tractability in \(\tau\), uniformly in \(K\), and polynomial-time solvability for every fixed support size.

5. NP membership

The threshold problem belongs to NP even when \(K\) is part of the input. Two facts give a polynomial-size certificate and a polynomial-time verifier.

A short rational certificate exists

Suppose some locally stable allocation attains a rational threshold. Its associated safe-state program has an optimum at least that threshold.

Choose an optimal extreme point of this program. Let \(A\) be its type-incidence matrix. Each column corresponds to a simple cycle, so its entries are zero or one.

An extreme point has at most \(\tau\) positive cycle variables. Otherwise the corresponding columns of \(A\) are linearly dependent. A nonzero dependence permits sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, preserving every type’s total consumption and nonnegativity. This would express the point as the midpoint of two distinct feasible points.

The positive variables are determined by linearly independent active capacity equations. Cramer’s rule, together with the determinant bound for zero-one matrices and the binary encoding of the masses, gives polynomial bit length for their rational values.

Thus a certificate needs at most \(\tau\) simple cycles, each of length at most \(\tau\), and polynomial-length rational masses.

Stability is polynomial-time checkable

From the certificate, compute \(u_t\), \(B_t(c)\), and \(W_t(c)\).

Build a directed graph with an arc \(s\to t\) precisely when \(d(s)\) is compatible with \(t\) and

\(W_t(d(s))>0.\)

Mark that arc when also \(B_t(d(s))>0\).

By the blocking criterion and the simple-cycle reduction, a local block exists exactly when this graph contains a directed cycle of length at most \(K\) containing a marked arc.

For each marked arc \(s\to t\), find the shortest directed path from \(t\) to \(s\). Such an arc belongs to a cycle of length at most \(K\) exactly when

\(1+\operatorname{dist}(t,s)\le K.\)

A shortest path is simple, so adding the marked arc gives a feasible simple cycle. Conversely, any cycle containing that arc supplies a return path of the required length.

Directed unweighted shortest paths, feasibility checks, and rational arithmetic all take polynomial time.

Together with the reduction, this proves NP-completeness of the threshold problem for every fixed \(K\ge2\), and also when \(K\) is supplied as input.
