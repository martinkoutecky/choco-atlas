The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type supports \(P\) and \(R\), rational masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, strict type-level rankings, utilities, benchmarks, and bottom-type exemptions, choose thresholds \(L_P,L_R\). Retain a pair \((i,j)\) when each non-exempt type’s utility meets its benchmark minus its side’s threshold; run mass DA and find thresholds minimizing \(\sum_{i\in P}p_i\lvert\{j\in R:(i,j)\text{ is retained}\}\rvert\), subject to its assignment \(x(E)\) equaling the full-graph assignment \(x(E_{\mathrm{all}})\). Return the thresholds and common assignment.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P,R\) be nonempty finite type supports. A society is given by its support: the list of its types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational; \(\tau=|P|+|R|\) is the total support size, not the number of possible types. Masses satisfy \(\sum_i p_i=\sum_j q_j=1\). Set \(m=\max\{|P|,|R|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a complete strict ranking of opposite-side types, induced by its distinct rational utilities \(u_{ij},v_{ij}\in[0,1]\); every listed type is preferred to being unmatched. Rational benchmarks \(b_i,c_j\in[0,1]\) and fixed bottom-type exemption sets \(B_P\subseteq P,B_R\subseteq R\) are supplied as input.
For rational thresholds \(L_P,L_R\in[0,1]\), retain exactly \(E=\{(i,j):(i\in B_P\lor u_{ij}\ge b_i-L_P)\land(j\in B_R\lor v_{ij}\ge c_j-L_R)\}\).
An assignment has divisible masses \(x_{ij}\ge0\), zero outside \(E\), with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le p_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le q_j\). Mass DA is \(R\)-proposing deferred acceptance for stable allocation: free mass proposes down its retained ranking, each receiving type holds its best incoming mass up to its quota, and rejected mass continues down its list; exhausted mass remains unmatched. There is no additional pair capacity. Write \(x(E)\) for its terminal assignment and \(E_{\mathrm{all}}=P\times R\).
Minimize \(C(E)=\sum_{i\in P}p_i\lvert\{j\in R:(i,j)\in E\}\rvert\) subject to entrywise equality \(x(E)=x(E_{\mathrm{all}})\), returning minimizing thresholds and the common assignment.

Is this exact threshold optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents, bilateral utility-threshold truncation preserves the proposing-side DA outcome when the retained graph contains every full-market stable matching. [Agarwal–Cole](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol261-icalp2023/LIPIcs.ICALP.2023.8/LIPIcs.ICALP.2023.8.pdf)
For real-valued bipartite stable allocation, polynomial algorithms are known for finding a stable allocation and minimizing linear assignment costs; these results concern assignment selection, rather than threshold selection with the objective above. [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

Result

The exact threshold optimization problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

There are only polynomially many graphs induced by the two global thresholds. Each graph’s exact \(R\)-proposing DA outcome can also be computed in polynomial time, including with binary-encoded rational masses. The latter statement requires a polynomial stable-allocation algorithm: a direct simulation of batch DA does not itself supply a polynomial running-time bound.

1. A finite grid represents every threshold outcome

Write \(N=|P||R|\), so \(N\le m^2\). For each pair, define
\[ \]
\(a_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&i\in B_P,\\ \max\{0,b_i-u_{ij}\},&i\notin B_P,\end{cases}\qquad d_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&j\in B_R,\\ \max\{0,c_j-v_{ij}\},&j\notin B_R.\end{cases}\)

All these numbers belong to \([0,1]\). The definition of retention is equivalent to
\[ \]
\((i,j)\in E(L_P,L_R)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_{ij}\le L_P\ \text{and}\ d_{ij}\le L_R.\)

Indeed, for a nonexempt \(P\)-type, the original inequality is \(L_P\ge b_i-u_{ij}\). Since \(L_P\ge0\), replacing its right-hand side by its maximum with zero changes nothing. Exemptions and the other side work identically.

Define the rational candidate sets
\[ \]
\(A=\{0,1\}\cup\{a_{ij}:(i,j)\in P\times R\},\qquad D_R=\{0,1\}\cup\{d_{ij}:(i,j)\in P\times R\}.\)

Each set has at most \(N+2\) elements.

Grid lemma. Every admissible threshold pair induces the same graph as some pair in \(A\times D_R\).

Proof. Given \(L_P,L_R\), set
\[ \]
\(\widehat L_P=\max\{a\in A:a\le L_P\},\qquad \widehat L_R=\max\{d\in D_R:d\le L_R\}.\)

Both maxima exist because the sets contain zero. For every pair, \(a_{ij}\in A\), so
\[ \]
\(a_{ij}\le L_P\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_{ij}\le\widehat L_P.\)

The forward implication follows from the definition of the maximum; the reverse follows from \(\widehat L_P\le L_P\). The same argument applies to \(d_{ij}\). Consequently,
\[ \]
\(E(L_P,L_R)=E(\widehat L_P,\widehat L_R).\)

The two pairs therefore have identical costs and identical DA outcomes. This proves the lemma.

Thus it suffices to examine at most \((N+2)^2\) threshold pairs. Equality at a breakpoint is handled exactly because retention uses weak inequalities.

2. Computing the specified DA outcome

The relevant known result is stronger than merely finding some stable allocation:

On a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences, vertex quotas, and edge capacities, proposing-side batch Gale–Shapley returns the unique proposing-side lexicographically optimal stable allocation. Dean–Munshi’s polynomial augmenting algorithm returns that same allocation.

These statements are Lemmas 1 and 5 and Section 3 of [Dean–Munshi, *Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf). Their algorithm accommodates real capacities; its augmentations use additions, subtractions, and minima. These facts let us apply it to exact rational input.

Here are the checks connecting that model to this problem.

Redundant edge capacities

For a retained real pair, introduce the capacity
\[ \]
\(h_{ij}=\min\{p_i,q_j\}.\)

This imposes no additional restriction: every assignment satisfying the vertex quotas already satisfies \(x_{ij}\le h_{ij}\).

It also does not alter the rejection process. If \(x_{ij}=q_j\), proposer \(j\) has no free mass. If \(x_{ij}=p_i\), receiver \(i\) already holds its entire quota from \(j\), and any additional mass from that same type must be rejected. Thus these capacities merely express restrictions already enforced by the quotas.

Unmatched mass

To put the instance into the fully assigned formulation, add a dummy proposer \(r_*\) and dummy receiver \(p_*\), each with quota \(1\).

Every real proposer ranks \(p_*\) last, and every real receiver ranks \(r_*\) last. Each dummy ranks all real types on the opposite side before the other dummy, using an arbitrary strict order among real types. Add all dummy-incident edges, with capacity equal to the minimum of their endpoint quotas.

The resulting graph has \(\tau+2\) vertices and at most \(N+\tau+1\) edges, and all preference lists are strict.

To verify that its DA outcome projects to the required outcome, consider the following valid proposal schedule. Run real proposers first, with \(p_*\) as their final fallback. The dummy receiver can hold all such fallback mass: the total real proposing mass is \(1\). Consequently, proposals to real receivers evolve exactly as in the original process, and originally unmatched mass is held by \(p_*\).

Let \(T\) be the total assignment on real pairs after this stage. Real receivers have total vacancies \(1-T\), while \(p_*\) holds \(1-T\). Now let \(r_*\) propose. Real receivers hold its mass precisely in their remaining vacancies; because they rank it last, it displaces no real mass. After those vacancies are filled, its remaining mass is \(T\), which fits exactly in the remaining capacity of \(p_*\).

Thus the real-pair projection is precisely \(x(E)\). Proposal-order independence and the cited equivalence with the polynomial augmenting algorithm establish that this projection can be computed polynomially. This argument applies separately to every retained graph and to \(E_{\mathrm{all}}\).

3. Exact optimization algorithm

Perform the following steps:

  • Compute \(x^*=x(E_{\mathrm{all}})\) using the preceding algorithm.
  • Construct \(A\) and \(D_R\).
  • For each \((a,d)\in A\times D_R\), construct \(E(a,d)\) and compute \(x(E(a,d))\).
  • Keep precisely the candidates satisfying \(x(E(a,d))=x^*\) entrywise, putting zero on absent edges.
  • Among these candidates, minimize the exact rational cost

\[ \]
\(C(E(a,d))=\sum_{i\in P}p_i\,|\{j\in R:(i,j)\in E(a,d)\}|.\)

  • Return a minimizing threshold pair and \(x^*\).

At least one candidate is feasible. Specifically, \((1,1)\in A\times D_R\), and it retains every pair: \(b_i-1\le0\le u_{ij}\), with the analogous inequality on the other side.

Optimality proof. Take any feasible rational threshold pair. By the grid lemma, some enumerated pair induces exactly its graph. That enumerated pair has the same DA assignment and cost, so it is feasible and is considered by the algorithm. Hence the algorithm’s chosen cost is no larger than the cost of any feasible threshold pair. Its chosen pair is itself feasible, proving global optimality.

This proof directly checks the required DA equality; it needs no characterization involving all full-market stable matchings.

4. Bit complexity

It remains to ensure that the allocation computations and comparisons are polynomial in binary input length.

Let \(H\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, written as integer fractions. Then
\[ \]
\(\log_2 H\le L.\)

Scale every vertex quota and edge capacity by \(H\). All become integers: real quotas are \(Hp_i,Hq_j\), dummy quotas are \(H\), and real edge capacities are \(\min\{Hp_i,Hq_j\}\). Positive scaling preserves the DA process, since it preserves quota comparisons and scales every proposed or rejected amount uniformly.

The augmenting algorithm chooses amounts by taking minima of residual capacities and deficits, then adds or subtracts those amounts. Therefore, on the scaled instance, its allocation and residual quantities remain integers. Quotas and individual allocations are bounded by \(H\); explicitly maintained sums have bit length \(O(L+\log(N+\tau+2))\).

For a conservative implementation, one may maintain the augmenting algorithm’s vectors explicitly. Its polynomially many augmentations and pointer advances can each be implemented by scanning the current graph. This gives a polynomial number of operations on integers of the preceding bit length, without relying on unit-cost arithmetic.

Equality comparisons are exact integer comparisons after scaling. Likewise, the scaled objective is
\[ \]
\(H C(E)=\sum_{i\in P}(Hp_i)\deg_E(i),\)

an integer bounded by \(H|R|\), because \(\sum_i Hp_i=H\). Objective comparisons therefore have polynomial bit complexity as well.

Each breakpoint is either zero, one, or a difference of two input rationals, so its encoding length is \(O(L)\). The candidate sets and their comparisons are consequently computable in polynomial time.

There are \(O((N+2)^2)\) candidates, each requiring polynomial work in \(N,\tau,L\). Since \(N\le m^2\), the complete algorithm runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), and its returned thresholds and assignment have polynomial encoding length. No expansion into individual agents is used.
