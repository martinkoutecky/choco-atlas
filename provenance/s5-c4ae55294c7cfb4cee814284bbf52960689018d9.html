A \(2\)-competitive policy is achievable in polynomial time

There is a deterministic causal, type-aggregated policy satisfying \(J\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}\). It has an exact implementation using polynomially many bit operations in \(\tau\) and \(L\), with no additional dependence on the numerical value of \(1/\kappa\). Here \(L\) includes the binary encoding of \(\kappa\).

The implementation uses compressed periodic instructions. Explicitly listing every subcohort completion can require exponentially many entries, even for one type.

1. The policy

Give types decreasing priority according to their original density \(\rho_t=v_t/d_t\). Break equal-density ties by a fixed lexicographic order on the revealed parameter triples.

For the mathematical description, order the labels within each cohort increasingly. At every time, process at rate \(1\) the highest-priority unfinished mass, up to total mass \(\kappa\). Process every other job at rate \(0\).

Thus, if unfinished released mass exceeds \(\kappa\), the active mass is exactly \(\kappa\). If it does not, every unfinished released job runs at rate \(1\).

This is the fluid counterpart of P-WSPT, the discrete policy studied by [Megow and Schulz](https://web.mit.edu/schulz/www/epapers/ms-orl-2004.pdf). The fluid guarantee and compressed implementation are proved below.

The policy is causal: its decisions use only revealed types and their current remaining processing requirements.

Within a cohort, remaining processing is nondecreasing in label order. Indeed, whenever a larger unfinished label is processed, every smaller unfinished label is processed too. Consequently, the policy can operate on the cohort’s ordered mass distribution of remaining processing. Labels provide a measurable realization of tie-breaking; individual jobs need not be enumerated.

If \(\kappa\geq1\), all released jobs can run immediately at rate \(1\), giving \(J=\mathrm{OPT}\). Henceforth assume \(0<\kappa<1\).

2. Lower bounds on the fluid optimum

Index the complete stream in the priority order just defined. Introduce

\(P_t=q_t d_t,\qquad D=\sum_t q_t v_t d_t,\qquad A=\sum_t q_t v_t(r_t+d_t)\),

and

\(B=\frac1\kappa\left(\sum_t q_t v_t\sum_{h<t}q_h d_h+\frac12\sum_t q_t^2v_t d_t\right)\).

We prove \(\mathrm{OPT}\geq A\) and, more strongly, \(\mathrm{OPT}\geq B+D/2\).

The release-time and individual-speed bound

Since each job has rate at most \(1\), its completion time satisfies \(C_t(a)\geq r_t+d_t\). Integrating gives \(J\geq A\) for every feasible schedule, and therefore \(\mathrm{OPT}\geq A\).

A processing-moment bound

For an arbitrary feasible schedule, let \(w_t(s)=\int_0^{q_t}u_t(a,s)\,da\) be type \(t\)’s aggregate processing rate. Its total processing is \(P_t\). Define

\(M=\sum_t \rho_t\int_0^\infty s\,w_t(s)\,ds\).

First we show \(M\geq B\). Let \(W_i=\sum_{t\leq i}P_t\), and let \(Z_i(s)\) be the unfinished processing volume of the first \(i\) types at time \(s\). Capacity implies

\(Z_i(s)\geq (W_i-\kappa s)_+\),

where \(x_+=\max\{x,0\}\). Set \(\rho_{\tau+1}=0\). Tonelli’s theorem and telescoping over priority prefixes give

\(M=\int_0^\infty\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(\rho_i-\rho_{i+1})Z_i(s)\,ds\).

All coefficients are nonnegative. Thus

\(M\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}(\rho_i-\rho_{i+1})\frac{W_i^2}{2\kappa}\).

Expanding this expression yields

\(\frac1\kappa\sum_t\rho_t\left(P_t\sum_{h<t}P_h+\frac{P_t^2}{2}\right)=B\).

Release constraints were simply omitted from this lower-bound calculation.

Next, the individual rate bound implies \(J\geq M+D/2\). To see this, consider one job of processing requirement \(d\) and finite completion time \(C\). Put \(f(x)=u(C-x)\), extending the rate by zero outside its processing interval. Then \(0\leq f\leq1\) and \(\int_0^\infty f(x)\,dx=d\). Pointwise,

\((x-d)\bigl(f(x)-\mathbf1_{[0,d]}(x)\bigr)\geq0\).

Integrating, and using the equality of the two masses, gives

\(\int_0^\infty x f(x)\,dx\geq d^2/2\).

Equivalently,

\(C\geq \frac1d\int_0^\infty s\,u(s)\,ds+\frac d2\).

Multiplying by the job’s weight and integrating over all cohorts proves \(J\geq M+D/2\). Schedules with infinite objective satisfy the lower bounds trivially. Hence

\(\mathrm{OPT}\geq B+D/2\).

3. Competitive guarantee

Fix an unfinished job with label \(a\) in type \(t\).

Whenever this job is not running, the policy uses all capacity \(\kappa\) on strictly higher-priority jobs. Their total processing volume over the entire stream is at most

\(\sum_{h<t}q_h d_h+a d_t\).

The job itself receives exactly \(d_t\) units of rate-\(1\) processing. Its total waiting time is therefore at most the preceding volume divided by \(\kappa\), so

\(C_t(a)\leq r_t+d_t+\frac1\kappa\left(\sum_{h<t}q_h d_h+a d_t\right)\).

Integrating over \(a\in[0,q_t]\), multiplying by \(v_t\), and summing gives

\(J_{\mathrm{policy}}\leq A+B\).

Combining the two proved lower bounds,

\(J_{\mathrm{policy}}\leq A+B\leq2\mathrm{OPT}-D/2\leq2\mathrm{OPT}\).

The remaining sections establish that this policy has an exact, polynomial-time aggregate implementation.

4. Only linearly many distribution atoms are needed

Represent each cohort by ordered atoms: each atom is a positive mass of jobs having the same remaining processing requirement. Keep active and inactive pieces separate when the capacity boundary splits an atom.

After \(k\) cohorts have arrived, at most \(2k\) atoms are needed:

  • A new cohort introduces one atom. Recomputing the active priority prefix splits at most one atom, so an arrival increases the count by at most two.
  • At a completion event, at least one active atom disappears. Selecting the new active prefix splits at most one remaining atom, so the count does not increase.

This proves the bound inductively, including simultaneous arrivals and completions.

The ordinary event construction is finite. For this existence argument only, let \(E\) be a common denominator of all cohort masses and \(\kappa\). Every atom mass is an integer multiple of \(1/E\): selecting a capacity boundary uses only addition and subtraction of such masses. Every completion therefore removes at least \(1/E\) mass. There can be only finitely many completion events.

Importantly, the implementation below never enumerates these \(1/E\)-sized pieces.

5. Compressing repeated completions

Consider a state at time \(s\), immediately after dispatch. Suppose some released mass is waiting.

Write the active atoms as \(1,\ldots,K\), with masses \(b_i\) and remaining requirements \(x_i>0\). Their total mass is \(\kappa\). Let the first waiting atom have mass \(Q>0\), remaining requirement \(p>0\), and cohort type \(t\).

Until this waiting atom is exhausted or a new cohort arrives, every vacated portion of active capacity is filled from this atom.

Consequently, active group \(i\) first completes after \(x_i\) time units. Its replacement from the waiting atom then needs \(p\) units, and subsequent replacements also need \(p\). Its refill times, relative to \(s\), are therefore

\(x_i,\ x_i+p,\ x_i+2p,\ldots\).

For \(z\geq0\), define

\(n_i(z)=0\) if \(z<x_i\), and \(n_i(z)=1+\lfloor(z-x_i)/p\rfloor\) otherwise.

The mass requested from the waiting atom by relative time \(z\), including refills at that time, is

\(S(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}b_i n_i(z)\).

Let \(T\) be the first time at which \(S(T)\geq Q\). Before \(T\), all these renewals are valid. At \(T\), the waiting atom is exhausted. If the simultaneous refill demand exceeds its remaining mass, ordinary priority dispatch fills the remaining vacancies from subsequent waiting atoms.

Thus one instruction describes an entire interval:

  • the active groups \((b_i,x_i)\);
  • the waiting atom’s type, mass \(Q\), and remaining requirement \(p\);
  • the periodic refill rule;
  • the stopping time \(s+T\).

This is an exact representation of the policy, not a discretization.

Interruptions and exact state recovery

If a new cohort arrives before \(s+T\), recover the current state using integer division.

For each group, before its first completion the remaining requirement is \(x_i-z\). Afterwards, its current replacement’s remaining requirement follows from the remainder of \(z-x_i\) upon division by \(p\). The waiting mass is \(Q\) minus the mass already allocated at preceding refill times.

At a time coinciding with a completion, first remove completed jobs, then reveal arrivals, then dispatch. Counting refills strictly before that time implements this convention exactly.

Measurable subcohorts can also be recovered without enumeration. Assign successive portions of the waiting atom in chronological refill order, breaking simultaneous ties by a fixed group order. The beginning of any allocated portion is obtained by evaluating the same refill-count formula at its start time. This specifies its label interval using polynomially many arithmetic operations.

Polynomially many instructions

Between consecutive arrival times, inactive atoms never receive processing until they are selected, and an active job is never preempted.

Every periodic instruction ends by exhausting at least one waiting atom. During such an interval, no new waiting pool is created: dispatch only shortens the first existing waiting pool and then advances to the next.

There are at most \(2k\) atoms at the beginning of an interval following \(k\) arrivals. Hence that interval needs at most \(2k\) waiting-pool exhaustion instructions.

Once no mass is waiting, all remaining atoms run at rate \(1\); one further instruction specifies their completions. Summing over all arrival intervals gives \(O(\tau^2)\) instructions, each containing \(O(\tau)\) groups.

An unanticipated arrival simply interrupts the current instruction. No future release time is used.

6. Exact bit complexity

Two rational lattices suffice:

  • Masses lie on the lattice whose denominator is the least common multiple of the denominators of the revealed masses and \(\kappa\).
  • Event times and remaining requirements lie on the lattice whose denominator is the least common multiple of the denominators of revealed release times and processing requirements.

Both denominators have \(O(L)\) bits. They are updated when cohorts arrive.

To find \(T\), binary-search the time lattice using the monotone predicate \(S(z)\geq Q\). With \(b_{\min}=\min_i b_i\), an upper search bound is

\(U=\max_i x_i+p\lceil Q/b_{\min}\rceil\).

Indeed, by time \(U\), a group of mass at least \(b_{\min}\) has requested enough replacements to account for mass \(Q\).

Since \(Q\leq1\), positive masses are at least the reciprocal of the mass-lattice denominator, and \(x_i,p\leq d_{\max}\), the lattice index of \(U\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Binary search therefore uses \(O(L)\) predicate evaluations, each involving \(O(\tau)\) integer divisions and additions.

All actual times also have polynomial bit length. After the last release, full-capacity processing lasts at most \((\sum_tq_t d_t)/\kappa\); once unfinished mass falls below \(\kappa\), every remaining job finishes within \(d_{\max}\). Thus the final completion time is at most

\(r_{\max}+\frac{\sum_tq_t d_t}{\kappa}+d_{\max}\).

State recovery, interval reconstruction, and redispatch use at most \(O(\tau^2+\tau L)\) arithmetic operations per instruction, on \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Together with \(O(\tau^2)\) instructions, elementary exact integer arithmetic gives a conservative total bound of

\(O(\tau^4L^3)\)

bit operations. The working distribution has \(O(\tau)\) atoms.

There is no separate numerical factor involving \(1/\kappa\). Large repetition counts are binary integers manipulated by division and comparison.

For example, one cohort with mass \(1\), processing requirement \(1\), and \(\kappa=2^{-b}\) produces \(2^b\) completion waves under this policy. Its periodic instruction nevertheless has polynomial size in \(b\). The polynomial guarantee concerns this exact compressed policy representation, rather than an explicit list of every wave.
