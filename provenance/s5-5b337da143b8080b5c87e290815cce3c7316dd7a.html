Proposition 4 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(s\) seats and two voter types with masses \(\mu_A=f\) and \(\mu_B=1-f\), where each type approves every candidate on its own party's \(s\)-candidate slate and none of the other, determine how many seats party \(A\) receives under Jefferson (\(h=1\)) or Webster (\(h=\tfrac12\)); ties may be excluded as in Proposition 4.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not all possible approval sets. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
There are \(s\geq1\) integer seats and \(m=2s\) explicitly listed candidates, divided into disjoint party slates \(A\) and \(B\), each of size \(s\). The two supported types have masses \(\mu_A=f\) and \(\mu_B=1-f\), with rational \(0<f<1\), and approve exactly their own party's slate; thus \(\tau=2\).
The input selects Jefferson, \(h=1\), or Webster, \(h=\tfrac12\), and either the sequential or nonsequential version. Seats remain indivisible.
Sequentially, start with \(a=b=0\); award each next seat to the party with larger priority \(f/(h+a)\) or \((1-f)/(h+b)\), then increment its seat count. Promise that no cross-party tie occurs during these \(s\) rounds.
Nonsequentially, choose the integer \(k\in\{0,\ldots,s\}\) maximizing \(F_h(k)=f\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(h+j)^{-1}+(1-f)\sum_{j=0}^{s-k-1}(h+j)^{-1}\), with empty sums equal to zero; promise a unique maximizing seat count.

Is computing party \(A\)'s seat count for each specified version solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,s\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite electorates with these two exclusive approval types, Brams, Kilgour, and Potthoff establish explicit seat thresholds for both versions, expressed in the normalized vote fraction \(f\). [Source](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77931/1/MPRA_paper_77931.pdf)

Result

All four specified versions are polynomial-time solvable. Under the applicable promise, party \(A\)’s seat count is

  • Jefferson: \(k=\lfloor(s+1)f\rfloor\).
  • Webster: \(k=\lfloor sf+\tfrac12\rfloor\).

These formulas hold for both the sequential and nonsequential versions. The proof below derives them directly from the given rules; it does not require a complexity result from the [cited paper](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/77931/).

1. Nonsequential optimization

Fix \(h\in\{1,\tfrac12\}\). For each integer \(k\in\{0,\ldots,s-1\}\), define the consecutive difference \(D_h(k)=F_h(k+1)-F_h(k)\). Cancelling the common summands gives

\(D_h(k)=\dfrac{f}{h+k}-\dfrac{1-f}{h+s-k-1}.\)

Both denominators are positive. Combining the fractions therefore gives

\(D_h(k)=\dfrac{(s+2h-1)f-h-k}{(h+k)(h+s-k-1)}.\)

Write \(C=s+2h-1\), which is positive for both allowed values of \(h\). Consequently,

\(D_h(k)>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f>\dfrac{h+k}{C}.\)

The thresholds \((h+k)/C\) increase strictly with \(k\). Thus the positive differences form an initial segment, followed by at most one zero difference, followed by negative differences.

If a zero difference occurs at index \(k\), all preceding differences are positive and all subsequent differences are negative. Hence precisely \(k\) and \(k+1\) maximize \(F_h\). The unique-maximizer promise therefore excludes every zero difference.

Let \(r\) be the number of positive differences. Then \(F_h\) increases strictly up to \(r\) and decreases strictly thereafter, with the evident interpretation when \(r=0\) or \(r=s\). Its unique maximizer is consequently

\(k=r=\bigl|\{j\in\{0,\ldots,s-1\}:h+j<Cf\}\bigr|.\)

For Jefferson, \(h=1\) and \(C=s+1\). The promise excludes \((s+1)f\in\{1,\ldots,s\}\). Since \(0<(s+1)f<s+1\), counting the integers \(j+1<(s+1)f\) gives

\(k=\lfloor(s+1)f\rfloor.\)

For Webster, \(h=\tfrac12\) and \(C=s\). The promise excludes \(sf\in\{\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\ldots,s-\tfrac12\}\). Counting the integers \(j\) satisfying \(j+\tfrac12<sf\) gives

\(k=\lfloor sf+\tfrac12\rfloor.\)

In particular, these formulas include the boundary allocations \(k=0\) and \(k=s\).

2. The sequential rule maximizes the same objective

Consider the following two lists of labeled marginal scores:

\(u_j=\dfrac{f}{h+j},\qquad v_j=\dfrac{1-f}{h+j},\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,s-1\}.\)

Each list is strictly decreasing because its numerator is positive and its denominator increases.

After the sequential rule has assigned \(a\) seats to \(A\) and \(b\) seats to \(B\), its selected scores are precisely the prefixes \(u_0,\ldots,u_{a-1}\) and \(v_0,\ldots,v_{b-1}\). Before any of the \(s\) rounds, \(a+b<s\), so both next scores \(u_a\) and \(v_b\) exist.

Every remaining score in the first list is at most \(u_a\), and every remaining score in the second is at most \(v_b\). The sequential comparison therefore selects a largest score among all remaining scores. Under the no-cross-party-tie promise, this largest score is unique: the two heads differ, and later entries within either list are strictly smaller than its head.

By induction over the rounds, the rule selects the \(s\) largest scores from the two lists. Moreover, every selected score is strictly larger than every score left unselected: when a selected score was chosen, every finally unselected score was still available.

Let the resulting count for \(A\) be \(k\). The sum of the selected scores is exactly \(F_h(k)\). For any other count \(\ell\), the prefixes of lengths \(\ell\) and \(s-\ell\) constitute another subset of \(s\) labeled scores, with sum \(F_h(\ell)\).

If \(\ell\ne k\), this subset differs from the greedy subset. It omits and adds equally many scores, and every omitted greedy score is strictly larger than every added unselected score. Thus

\(F_h(\ell)<F_h(k).\)

The sequential count is therefore the unique nonsequential maximizer. The formulas from Section 1 apply to every input satisfying the sequential promise as well.

3. Exact arithmetic and running time

Write the input rational as \(f=p/q\), where \(p,q\) are binary integers satisfying \(0<p<q\). Reduction to lowest terms is unnecessary.

The two formulas become exact integer divisions:

  • Jefferson: \(k=\left\lfloor\dfrac{(s+1)p}{q}\right\rfloor\).
  • Webster: \(k=\left\lfloor\dfrac{2sp+q}{2q}\right\rfloor\).

Every integer in these calculations has bit length \(O(L+\log(s+1))\). A constant number of additions, multiplications, and integer divisions suffices. Using elementary binary arithmetic, the running time, including reading the input, is

\(O\!\left(L+(L+\log(s+1))^2\right).\)

This is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,s\), with \(m=2s\) and \(\tau=2\). No expansion into individual voters and no evaluation of the harmonic sums is needed. Consequently, all four promised problems admit deterministic polynomial-time algorithms.
