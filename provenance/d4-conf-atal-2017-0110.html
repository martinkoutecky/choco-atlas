The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an agenda Φ, integrity constraint Γ, desired conclusions L, and a rational distribution μ over complete, Γ-consistent judgment sets, find the minimum total mass to transfer from types J to reports K ≠ J, subject to ∑ₖ xᵢₖ ≤ μᵢ for each J, such that every Kemeny winner under the resulting distribution ν contains L. Here Kemeny winners minimize ∑ᵢ νᵢ d(J, J⁺) over complete, Γ-consistent judgment sets J⁺.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\Phi=\{\varphi_1,\neg\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_m,\neg\varphi_m\}\) be a nonempty propositional agenda closed under complementation, with double negations identified, and let \(\Gamma\) be a propositional integrity constraint; auxiliary variables outside the agenda are allowed.
Let \(\mathcal J\) comprise the complete judgment sets choosing exactly one formula from each complementary pair whose conjunction with \(\Gamma\) is satisfiable. Input also includes desired conclusions \(\mathcal L\subseteq\Phi\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) distinct types \(J_i\in\mathcal J\) with positive masses \(\mu_i\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
A transfer chooses real amounts \(x_{iK}\geq0\) for every \(K\in\mathcal J\setminus\{J_i\}\), subject to \(\sum_{K\ne J_i}x_{iK}\leq\mu_i\). Any feasible report is allowed, including reports outside the input support; transferred mass costs one per unit.
The resulting mass of \(K\in\mathcal J\) is \(\nu_K=\sum_{i:J_i=K}(\mu_i-\sum_{H\ne J_i}x_{iH})+\sum_{i:J_i\ne K}x_{iK}\), and total cost is \(c(x)=\sum_i\sum_{K\ne J_i}x_{iK}\).
Kemeny winners are all members of \(\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{A\in\mathcal J}\sum_{K\in\mathcal J}\nu_Kd(K,A)\), where \(d\) is Hamming distance on the \(m\) issues, counting each complementary pair once. Success requires every winner to contain \(\mathcal L\); no tie-breaking is applied.
Given a binary rational budget \(b\in[0,1]\), decide whether a successful transfer with \(c(x)\leq b\) exists. This asks for an attained feasible cost, rather than comparing an infimum with the budget. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous Kemeny bribery decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete unit-cost bribery requiring every Kemeny winner to contain the desired conclusions is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete, even for unidimensionally aligned input profiles and a single desired conclusion. [Source](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2017/pdfs/p1151.pdf)
A full treatment also establishes \(\Sigma_2^p\)-completeness for this discrete requirement under the name cautious exact bribery. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.02406) No result settling the mass-transfer problem above was located in the searches.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the rational distribution to total mass one and restricted the budget to the meaningful range [0,1].
  • Specified arbitrary nonnegative real transfers, allowing each input type's mass to split among any complete, Gamma-consistent reports, including reports absent from the input support.
  • Used the formula-based framework, allowing auxiliary propositional variables in the integrity constraint and counting Hamming disagreements once per issue.
  • Replaced the potentially unattained minimum with the budget decision question: whether an actual successful transfer of cost at most the given budget exists.
  • Retained all tied Kemeny minimizers and allowed any subset of the agenda as desired conclusions, as in the discrete bribery definition.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds even when:

  • \(\tau=3\), with all three masses equal to \(\frac13\);
  • \(b=\frac1{16}\);
  • the agenda consists entirely of propositional variables and their complements;
  • there are no auxiliary variables;
  • \(\mathcal L\) contains a single positive literal;
  • a feasible judgment set containing \(\mathcal L\) always exists;
  • the initial society is always unsuccessful.

Thus, a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. A bound valid for arbitrary fractional transfers

For a distribution \(\nu\), write its Kemeny score as

\(S_\nu(A)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal J}\nu_Kd(K,A)\).

Let \(\mu\) denote the initial distribution. From the definition of a transfer,

\(S_\nu(A)-S_\mu(A)=\sum_i\sum_{K\ne J_i}x_{iK}\bigl(d(K,A)-d(J_i,A)\bigr)\).

Every Hamming distance lies between \(0\) and \(m\). Consequently,

\(\lvert S_\nu(A)-S_\mu(A)\rvert\le m c(x)\).

Applying this bound to two reports \(A,B\) gives

\(S_\nu(A)-S_\nu(B)\ge S_\mu(A)-S_\mu(B)-2m c(x)\).

This bound applies to every allowed destination, including reports outside the input support, and to arbitrary real transfer amounts.

2. Reduction from satisfiability

We reduce from propositional satisfiability, whose \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is the Cook–Levin theorem. [Cook’s original paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/800157.805047).

Let \(F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) be the input formula. We may assume \(n\ge1\), adding an unused variable if necessary. Set

\(q=3(n+1)\).

The agenda has the following atomic issues, each together with its complement:

  • \(p\);
  • \(x_j,y_j\), for \(1\le j\le n\);
  • \(z_{r,k}\), for \(1\le r\le3\) and \(1\le k\le q\).

Thus,

\(m=1+2n+3q=11n+10\).

We describe complete judgment sets by their corresponding Boolean vectors.

Feasible reports

The integrity constraint permits precisely the following reports.

Three baseline reports \(B_1,B_2,B_3\). In \(B_i\),

\(p=0,\qquad x_j=y_j=0\ \text{for every }j,\)

and

\(z_{r,k}=1\ \text{if }r=i,\qquad z_{r,k}=0\ \text{if }r\ne i.\)

One fallback report \(T\). In \(T\),

\(p=1,\qquad x_j=y_j=0\ \text{for every }j,\qquad z_{r,k}=1\ \text{for every }r,k.\)

Solution reports \(C(a)\). For each assignment \(a\in\{0,1\}^n\) satisfying \(F\), there is a report with

\(p=1,\qquad x_j=a_j,\qquad y_j=1-a_j,\qquad z_{r,k}=0\ \text{for every }r,k.\)

These conditions have a polynomial-size propositional encoding. Explicitly, let

\(Z=\bigwedge_{j=1}^n(\neg x_j\land\neg y_j)\),

let \(H_i\) assert that precisely block \(i\) of the \(z\)-variables is all \(1\), with the other blocks all \(0\), and let

\(H_*=\bigwedge_{r=1}^3\bigwedge_{k=1}^q z_{r,k}\).

Take

\(\Gamma=(\neg p\land Z\land(H_1\lor H_2\lor H_3))\lor(p\land Z\land H_*)\lor\left(p\land\bigwedge_{r,k}\neg z_{r,k}\land\bigwedge_{j=1}^n(y_j\leftrightarrow\neg x_j)\land F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\right)\).

Because every variable belongs to the agenda, the three disjuncts characterize exactly the reports just described. In particular, checking whether a given complete report is feasible requires only evaluating \(\Gamma\), rather than solving an auxiliary satisfiability problem.

Society, desired conclusion, and budget

Give each \(B_i\) mass \(\frac13\), and set

\(\mathcal L=\{p\},\qquad b=\frac1{16}\).

The three support reports are distinct and feasible. The fallback \(T\) is always feasible and contains \(p\), independently of whether \(F\) is satisfiable.

3. Initial Kemeny scores

Two different baseline reports disagree on precisely two blocks of \(q\) selector issues. Hence,

\(d(B_i,B_j)=2q\quad\text{for }i\ne j\),

and therefore

\(S_\mu(B_i)=\frac{4q}{3}=4(n+1)\).

Write this common score as \(D=4(n+1)\).

Every solution report \(C(a)\) differs from each \(B_i\) on:

  • the issue \(p\);
  • exactly one issue in each pair \(x_j,y_j\);
  • the \(q\) selector issues in block \(i\).

Thus,

\(d(C(a),B_i)=1+n+q\),

so

\(S_\mu(C(a))=1+n+q=4(n+1)=D\).

Finally, \(T\) differs from each \(B_i\) on \(p\) and the two selector blocks other than block \(i\). Therefore,

\(S_\mu(T)=1+2q=6n+7=D+(2n+3)\).

It follows that every \(B_i\) is initially a Kemeny winner, whether or not solution reports exist. Since none contains \(p\), the initial society is always unsuccessful.

4. Correctness of the reduction

Satisfiable formulas yield successful attained transfers

Suppose \(F\) has a satisfying assignment \(a\), and let \(A=C(a)\).

From each baseline report, transfer mass \(\frac{b}{3}\) to \(A\). This is feasible because \(\frac{b}{3}\le\frac13\), and \(A\) is distinct from every baseline report. Its total cost is exactly

\(c(x)=b=\frac1{16}\).

The resulting distribution is

\(\nu=(1-b)\mu+b\delta_A\).

For every feasible report \(Y\),

\(S_\nu(Y)-S_\nu(A)=(1-b)\bigl(S_\mu(Y)-S_\mu(A)\bigr)+b\,d(A,Y)\).

Section 3 shows that \(S_\mu(A)=D\) is a minimum initial score. Hence the first term is nonnegative. If \(Y\ne A\), then \(d(A,Y)\ge1\), making the second term strictly positive.

Consequently, \(A\) is the unique Kemeny winner. It contains \(p\), so the transfer succeeds at an attained cost equal to the budget.

Unsatisfiable formulas admit no successful transfer within budget

Suppose \(F\) is unsatisfiable. Then

\(\mathcal J=\{B_1,B_2,B_3,T\}\),

and \(T\) is the only feasible report containing \(p\).

Consider any allowed transfer with \(c(x)\le b\). Applying Section 1 to \(T\) and \(B_1\),

\(S_\nu(T)-S_\nu(B_1)\ge (2n+3)-2mb\).

Using \(m=11n+10\) and \(b=\frac1{16}\), the right-hand side equals

\(2n+3-\frac{11n+10}{8}=\frac{5n+14}{8}>0\).

Thus \(T\) has strictly greater score than \(B_1\) after every transfer within budget. It cannot be a winner. Every winner therefore belongs to \(\{B_1,B_2,B_3\}\), and fails to contain \(p\).

We have proved the equivalence

\(F\text{ is satisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the constructed continuous bribery instance is successful within budget}.\)

5. Encoding and running-time consequences

If the input formula has bit length \(s\), the construction has

\(m=11n+10,\qquad \tau=3,\qquad L=O\bigl(s+n\log(n+2)\bigr)\),

using binary indices for variables. The constraint, agenda, and three support reports can all be constructed in polynomial time.

Therefore, an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) would solve satisfiability in polynomial time. The hardness uses constant masses and a constant positive budget; no small rational perturbation is hidden in the input.

6. Exact handling of strict success and attainment

For completeness, there is an exact finite LP characterization of the general problem. It explains how to handle the attained-budget requirement once the feasible reports are enumerated.

Let \(N=\lvert\mathcal J\rvert\le2^m\), and partition the reports into

\(\mathcal G=\{A\in\mathcal J:\mathcal L\subseteq A\},\qquad \mathcal B=\mathcal J\setminus\mathcal G\).

For each fixed \(A\in\mathcal G\), introduce variables \(y_{iK}\ge0\), representing the mass from original type \(i\) ultimately reporting \(K\). Impose

\(\sum_{K\in\mathcal J}y_{iK}=\mu_i\quad\text{for every }i,\)

and

\(\sum_i\sum_{K\ne J_i}y_{iK}\le b.\)

Introduce \(0\le\epsilon\le m+1\), impose

\(\sum_i\sum_{K\in\mathcal J}y_{iK}\bigl(d(K,B)-d(K,A)\bigr)\ge\epsilon\quad\text{for every }B\in\mathcal B,\)

and maximize \(\epsilon\).

The original instance is successful exactly when one of these LPs is feasible and has optimum strictly greater than \(0\):

  • A positive \(\epsilon\) makes \(A\) strictly better than every bad report, so no bad report can win.
  • Conversely, after any successful transfer, choose a good winner \(A\). Every bad report has strictly greater score. Since there are finitely many bad reports, their positive gaps have a positive minimum, providing a feasible \(\epsilon>0\). If there are no bad reports, the inequalities are vacuous.

The LP feasible region is closed and bounded: the mass equalities bound every \(y_{iK}\), and \(\epsilon\) has explicit bounds. Thus any feasible LP attains its optimum. All coefficients are rational, so standard exact rational linear programming decides whether the optimum is positive in polynomial time in the explicit LP encoding length.

Enumerating feasible reports by examining assignments to all propositional variables takes \(2^{O(L)}\) time, including auxiliary variables. There are at most \(2^m\) LPs, each with \(O(\tau2^m)\) variables and \(O(\tau+2^m)\) constraints. This gives an exact running-time bound

\(2^{O(L)}+2^m\operatorname{poly}(\tau2^m,m,L)\).

Finally, the reduction itself exhibits nonattainment of an infimum: for satisfiable \(F\), the same construction succeeds with every transfer proportion \(\epsilon>0\), while cost \(0\) leaves bad winners. Its successful-cost infimum is therefore \(0\), but \(0\) is not attained. The proof above uses an explicit successful transfer at the positive budget.
