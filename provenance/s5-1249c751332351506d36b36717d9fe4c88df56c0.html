$$ M(x)\ge \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}. $$

The following results hold for the specified mass dynamics.

  • Polynomial-time approximation. For every rational $0<\varepsilon<1$, there is a deterministic algorithm achieving

$$ \boxed{\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(1-1/e)^2}{8\tau}}. $$
Its running time is
$$ O\!\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^8+m\tau^2\right) $$
rational arithmetic operations, and
$$ O\!\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\tau^8+m\tau^2\right) \operatorname{poly}\!\left(L,\tau,\log(1/\varepsilon)\right) $$
bit operations. This improves the limiting constant in the notes by a factor of four.

  • Conditional impossibility. Under deterministic Gap-ETH, no deterministic algorithm polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ achieves $\tau^{-f(\tau)}$ for any nonnegative $f(\tau)=o(1)$. Under randomized Gap-ETH, the same exclusion holds for randomized algorithms succeeding with any fixed positive probability.
  • Approximation for fixed support size. A deterministic $(1-\varepsilon)$-approximation is attainable using

2. Activation: least fixed points, scaling, and exact evaluation

Define normalized variables
$$ p_t=\frac{x_t}{\mu_t}, \qquad y_t^r=\frac{a_t^r}{\mu_t}, \qquad W_{ts}=\mu_sK_{st}. $$
The influence assumption says that $W$ is row substochastic:
$$ W_{ts}\ge0, \qquad \sum_sW_{ts}\le1. $$

Whenever $0\le a_s^r\le\mu_s$,
$$ z_t(a^r)\le\sum_s\mu_sK_{st}\le1. $$
The update preserves these bounds. Thus the cap never changes the recursion, which becomes
$$ y^0=p, \qquad y^{r+1}=p+D_{1-p}Wy^r. $$
Here $D_{1-p}$ is diagonal with entries $1-p_t$.

The update maps the unit cube into itself and is monotone in its previous iterate. Also $y^1\ge y^0$. Therefore the iterates increase coordinatewise to a limit $y\in[0,1]^\tau$, and
$$ y=p+D_{1-p}Wy. $$
This is the least nonnegative fixed point: every nonnegative fixed point dominates $p$, and induction makes it dominate every iterate.

Monotonicity and scaling

For a fixed $v\in[0,1]^\tau$, the update
$$ p_t+(1-p_t)(Wv)_t $$
is nondecreasing in $p_t$, because $(Wv)_t\le1$. Comparing the iterations consequently proves that $a(x)$ and $z(a(x))$ are coordinatewise nondecreasing in $x$.

For every $0\le\lambda\le1$,
$$ a(\lambda x)\ge\lambda a(x), \qquad z_t(a(\lambda x))\ge\lambda z_t(a(x)). $$
Indeed, if $y^r(\lambda p)\ge\lambda y^r(p)$, then
$$ \begin{aligned} y_t^{r+1}(\lambda p) &\ge \lambda p_t+(1-\lambda p_t)\lambda(Wy^r(p))_t\\ &\ge \lambda p_t+\lambda(1-p_t)(Wy^r(p))_t\\ &=\lambda y_t^{r+1}(p). \end{aligned} $$
The initial comparison is equality, so induction and passage to the limit prove the claim.

Exact rational evaluation

For rational seeds, put $A=D_{1-p}W$. Activation can be computed exactly in polynomial time, even when $I-A$ is singular.

In the directed graph of $A$, remove every closed strongly connected component for which all internal row sums equal one. For each vertex in such a component, $p_t=0$: indeed,
$$ \sum_sA_{ts}=(1-p_t)\sum_sW_{ts}\le1-p_t. $$
The component has no outgoing dependencies, so its activation iterates remain zero.

Let $R$ be the matrix on the remaining vertices. Every remaining vertex can reach a row with a deficit in $R$. Otherwise it could reach a terminal strongly connected component with all internal row sums one, which would have been removed.

To justify nonsingularity explicitly, choose, from each remaining vertex, a positive-probability path to a deficient row. There are finitely many vertices. Thus there are an integer $h$ and $\eta>0$ such that the substochastic chain loses mass within $h$ transitions with probability at least $\eta$, from every starting vertex. Hence
$$ \|R^h\|_\infty\le1-\eta, \qquad \rho(R)<1. $$
The remaining activation coordinates are therefore the unique solution of
$$ (I-R)y=p. $$

Strongly connected components and rational linear algebra give an implementation using $O(\tau^3)$ arithmetic operations. Common-denominator conversion and determinant bounds give polynomial binary lengths; fraction-free elimination implements the computation in polynomial bit time.

No continuity assumption is involved.

3. Expressing the margin through nonnegative gains

Write
$$ h(z)=\frac{z}{1+z}, \qquad g_t(x)=a_t(x)h(z_t(x)), \qquad u_t=1-\pi_t(c^\star). $$
Direct substitution into the voting update gives the target's score gain
$$ G(x)=\sum_tu_tg_t(x) $$
and each opponent's score loss
$$ D_c(x)=\sum_t\pi_t(c)g_t(x). $$

Let
$$ B=\max_{c\ne c^\star}F_c^0, \qquad \delta_c=B-F_c^0\ge0. $$
Then
$$ \boxed{ M(x)=G(x)+\min_{c\ne c^\star}\bigl(\delta_c+D_c(x)\bigr). } $$
This identity uses all opponents and handles ties directly.

Since $\pi_t(c)\le u_t$ for every opponent,
$$ 0\le D_c(x)\le G(x). $$
Moreover, at least one opponent has $\delta_c=0$. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{G(x)\le M(x)\le2G(x).} $$

The identity also shows that $M$ is nondecreasing in the seeds. Furthermore,
$$ h(\lambda z)\ge\lambda h(z) \qquad (0\le\lambda\le1), $$
so activation scaling gives
$$ g_t(\lambda x)\ge\lambda^2g_t(x). $$
Thus $G(\lambda x)\ge\lambda^2G(x)$ and $D_c(\lambda x)\ge\lambda^2D_c(x)$. Since $\delta_c\ge\lambda^2\delta_c$,
$$ \boxed{M(\lambda x)\ge\lambda^2M(x).} $$

4. Approximating linear activation objectives

Consider
$$ H(x)=\sum_tw_ta_t(x), $$
where the weights $w_t$ are nonnegative encoded rationals. Their encoding length is included in the complexity of this auxiliary problem.

We first prove diminishing returns and then exploit a grid whose individual costs are small.

Diminishing returns

Interpret $W$ as the transition matrix of a substochastic Markov chain. On visiting state $i$, a success trial succeeds with probability $p_i$. After failure, the chain transitions according to $W$; missing transition probability terminates it without success.

The probability of success within the first $r+1$ visited states is exactly $y_t^r$ when starting at $t$. This follows inductively from
$$ y_t^{r+1}=p_t+(1-p_t)(Wy^r)_t. $$

Pre-sample the underlying chain path independently of the success trials. For a finite path portion visiting state $i$ exactly $N_i$ times, the conditional success probability is
$$ 1-\prod_i(1-p_i)^{N_i}. $$
For $N_i>0$, its derivative with respect to $p_i$ is
$$ N_i(1-p_i)^{N_i-1} \prod_{j\ne i}(1-p_j)^{N_j}. $$
It is nonnegative and nonincreasing in every coordinate, including $p_i$. For $N_i=0$, the derivative is zero.

Integrating these derivative comparisons proves diminishing returns for coordinate increments in the interior. Continuity of the finite-path expression extends the inequalities to the boundary. Expectations preserve the inequalities, as does the pointwise increasing limit over finite horizons.

Therefore, for $b\le b'$ and an increment $\xi\ge0$ for which both augmented vectors respect their coordinate bounds,
$$ H(b+\xi e_i)-H(b) \ge H(b'+\xi e_i)-H(b'). $$
The rescaling from $p_i$ to $x_i=\mu_ip_i$ preserves this property.

Grid construction

Fix a positive integer $q$, and define
$$ v_t=\min\{\mu_t,\kappa\}, \qquad N=q\tau, \qquad d_t=\frac{v_t}{N}, \qquad B'=\kappa+\sum_td_t. $$
Then
$$ B'\le(1+1/q)\kappa, \qquad d_t\le\frac{\kappa}{N}. $$

Create $N$ distinct items of cost $d_t$ for each type $t$. Selecting an item adds $d_t$ seed mass to that type. There are $q\tau^2$ items. The resulting set function is normalized, monotone, and submodular, by the diminishing-returns property.

Every originally feasible seed vector can be rounded upward coordinatewise to this grid. Its coordinates remain at most $v_t$, and its total cost becomes at most $B'$. Its objective does not decrease. Thus, if $V$ is the maximum grid value under budget $B'$,
$$ V\ge\sup_{\text{originally feasible }x}H(x). $$

Greedy bound using small item costs

Starting with the empty set, repeatedly choose a remaining item of largest marginal gain divided by cost. Stop immediately before the first chosen item that would exceed $B'$. Return the accepted prefix $S$. If all items are accepted, return them all.

Let $O$ be an optimal grid solution. At any feasible prefix $S$, submodularity gives
$$ V-H(S) \le \sum_{i\in O\setminus S} \bigl(H(S\cup\{i\})-H(S)\bigr). $$
The total cost of these items is at most $B'$. Hence the maximum marginal density is at least
$$ \frac{V-H(S)}{B'}. $$
For an accepted item of cost $d$, this implies
$$ V-H(S_{\mathrm{new}}) \le \left(1-\frac d{B'}\right)(V-H(S)). $$
Iterating and using $1-u\le e^{-u}$ yields
$$ H(S)\ge \left(1-e^{-\operatorname{cost}(S)/B'}\right)V. $$

If an item is rejected, its cost is at most $\kappa/N$, so
$$ \operatorname{cost}(S)>B'-\frac{\kappa}{N}. $$
Since $B'\ge\kappa$,
$$ H(S)\ge \left(1-e^{-(1-1/N)}\right)V. $$
If all items fit, their value is $V$, so the same bound holds.

Finally, scale the selected seeds by $\lambda=\kappa/B'$. The output is originally feasible, and linear activation scaling proves
$$ H(\lambda x)\ge\lambda H(x). $$
Consequently, the subroutine achieves
$$ \boxed{ c_{q,\tau} = \frac{1-e^{-(1-1/(q\tau))}}{1+1/q}. } $$

The improvement over the notes comes from the small upper bound on each item cost: the accepted prefix nearly exhausts the enlarged budget, so a best-singleton comparison and its factor-of-two loss are unnecessary.

5. Polynomial-time approximation for the margin

For every type $t$, apply the preceding subroutine to
$$ H^{A,t}(x)=a_t(x), \qquad H^{Z,t}(x)=z_t(x)=\sum_sK_{st}a_s(x). $$
These objectives have nonnegative rational weights already encoded in the input.

Let the outputs be $x^{A,t}$ and $x^{Z,t}$, and set
$$ x^{(t)}=\frac{x^{A,t}+x^{Z,t}}2. $$
This vector is feasible.

Put $c=c_{q,\tau}$. For every feasible comparison vector $x$, monotonicity and scaling imply
$$ \begin{aligned} a_t(x^{(t)}) &\ge a_t(x^{A,t}/2) \ge\frac12a_t(x^{A,t}) \ge\frac c2a_t(x),\\ z_t(x^{(t)}) &\ge z_t(x^{Z,t}/2) \ge\frac12z_t(x^{Z,t}) \ge\frac c2z_t(x). \end{aligned} $$
Using the scaling inequality for $h$,
$$ g_t(x^{(t)})\ge\frac{c^2}{4}g_t(x). $$

Return the candidate $x^{(t)}$ with the largest exact margin. Its value satisfies, for every feasible $x$,
$$ \begin{aligned} M(x^{\mathrm{out}}) &\ge\max_tM(x^{(t)})\\ &\ge\max_tu_tg_t(x^{(t)})\\ &\ge\frac{c^2}{4}\max_tu_tg_t(x)\\ &\ge\frac{c^2}{4\tau}G(x)\\ &\ge\frac{c^2}{8\tau}M(x). \end{aligned} $$
Taking the supremum over comparison vectors gives the guarantee
$$ \boxed{\frac{c_{q,\tau}^2}{8\tau}}. $$

Choosing the approximation parameter

Let $s=1-1/e$. For $\tau\ge2$ and $q\ge1$, put $u=1/(q\tau)\le1/2$. The power series gives
$$ e^u\le\frac1{1-u}\le1+2u. $$
Therefore,
$$ 1-e^{-(1-u)} \ge s-\frac{2u}{e} \ge s-\frac1{eq}. $$
It follows that
$$ c_{q,\tau} \ge \frac{s-1/(eq)}{1+1/q} \ge s-\frac1q. $$
For the last inequality, subtracting $s-1/q$ leaves
$$ \frac{1/q^2}{1+1/q}\ge0, $$
because $s+1/e=1$.

Choose
$$ q=\left\lceil4/\varepsilon\right\rceil. $$
Since $s>1/2$,
$$ c_{q,\tau} \ge s-\varepsilon/4 \ge s(1-\varepsilon/2). $$
Thus
$$ c_{q,\tau}^2\ge(1-\varepsilon)s^2, $$
which proves the stated ratio.

Running time

Each linear-objective call has $q\tau^2$ items and uses $O(q^2\tau^4)$ exact activation evaluations by scanning remaining items at every step. There are $2\tau$ calls, hence $O(q^2\tau^5)$ evaluations.

Each evaluation uses $O(\tau^3)$ arithmetic operations. Evaluating all final margins costs $O(m\tau^2)$ additional operations. The total is
$$ O(q^2\tau^8+m\tau^2). $$
Grid seeds, scaled seeds, linear-system solutions, and objective comparisons have binary lengths polynomial in $L,\tau,\log q$. Substituting the chosen $q$ gives the running times in Section 1.

This algorithm does not require the lower bounds on nonzero probabilities or influences.

6. Approximation for fixed support and a nonattainment example

A grid approximation scheme

Use the same grid and enlarged budget, but enumerate all grid vectors of cost at most $B'$. There are at most
$$ (q\tau+1)^\tau $$
vectors.

Choose a vector with maximum exact margin, and scale it by $\lambda=\kappa/B'$. Upward rounding and margin monotonicity show that the chosen grid value dominates every originally feasible value. The quadratic margin-scaling bound then gives
$$ M(x^{\mathrm{out}}) \ge (1+1/q)^{-2}\,\mathrm{OPT}. $$
With $q=\lceil2/\varepsilon\rceil$,
$$ (1+1/q)^{-2}\ge1-2/q\ge1-\varepsilon. $$

The arithmetic running time is
$$ O\!\left( (q\tau+1)^\tau(\tau^3+m\tau) \right), $$
with operand lengths polynomial in $L,\tau,\log q$.

Both this argument and the polynomial-time algorithm compare against every feasible vector before taking a supremum. Neither requires an attained optimum.

An instance whose supremum is not attained

Take two types, two candidates, and
$$ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12, \qquad \kappa=\frac14, \qquad \pi_t(c^\star)=0, \qquad \pi_t(c)=1. $$
Set
$$ W= \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\\ 0&1/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K= \begin{pmatrix} 2&0\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}. $$
Every promise holds: the normalized influence row sums are at most one, and their nonzero entries are at least $2^{-\gamma}$.

For type $1$, the least fixed point is
$$ y_1= \begin{cases} 0,&x_1=0,\\ 1,&x_1>0. \end{cases} $$
For type $2$, writing $p_2=2x_2$ gives
$$ y_2=p_2+(1-p_2)y_2/2, \qquad y_2=\frac{2p_2}{1+p_2}. $$
The margin is therefore
$$ M(x)= \frac12\,\mathbf 1_{\{x_1>0\}} +\frac{y_2^2}{2+y_2}. $$

The second term is strictly increasing in $x_2>0$. At $x_2=1/4$, one has $y_2=2/3$, and this term equals $1/6$.

If $x_1=0$, the margin is at most $1/6$. If $x_1>0$, feasibility forces $x_2<1/4$, so the margin is strictly less than
$$ \frac12+\frac16=\frac23. $$
Conversely, choosing
$$ x_1=\eta,\qquad x_2=\frac14-\eta $$
and letting $\eta$ decrease to zero approaches $2/3$. Hence
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=\frac23, $$
but no feasible seeds attain it.

7. A direct reduction from Densest $k$-Subgraph

The discrete PLTR result does not itself establish hardness for this recursion. The following reduction works directly with the mass dynamics.

Let a simple undirected graph have $n_0$ vertices, with $2\le k\le n_0$, and let $E^\star\ge1$ be the maximum number of edges induced by $k$ vertices. The cases $k=1$ and an edgeless graph are trivial.

Pad with isolated vertices until the graph has order $n$, a power of two. Then
$$ n_0\le n<2n_0, $$
and $E^\star$ is unchanged. Write $A$ for its adjacency matrix.

Construct a society with
$$ \tau=n,\qquad m=2,\qquad \mu_i=\frac1n,\qquad \kappa=\frac kn, $$
and deterministic initial preferences entirely for the opponent:
$$ \pi_i(c^\star)=0,\qquad \pi_i(c)=1. $$
Set
$$ \xi=n^{-4}, \qquad K_{st}=n\xi A_{ts}. $$
Every nonzero normalized influence equals
$$ \mu_sK_{st}=\xi=n^{-4}\ge n^{-\gamma}. $$
Its recipient row sum is at most $(n-1)\xi\le1$. All numbers are dyadic, and the input length is $O(n^2\log n)$.

Writing $p=nx$ and $y=na$, feasibility becomes
$$ 0\le p_i\le1, \qquad \sum_ip_i\le k. $$
The dynamics and margin are
$$ y=p+D_{1-p}\xi Ay, \qquad M(x)=\frac{2\xi}{n} \sum_i\frac{y_i(Ay)_i}{1+\xi(Ay)_i}. $$

Lower bound from an integral seed set

Put $d=(n-1)\xi$. Seed an optimal $k$-vertex set integrally. Then $y\ge p$ and $\xi(Ay)_i\le d$, giving
$$ \mathrm{OPT} \ge \frac{2\xi}{n(1+d)}p^{\mathsf T}Ap = \frac{4\xi E^\star}{n(1+d)}. $$

Bounding activation beyond fractional seeds

For arbitrary feasible fractional $p$, put $\delta=y-p\ge0$. The fixed-point equation and the column-sum bound on $A$ imply
$$ \sum_i\delta_i \le d\sum_i y_i \le d\left(k+\sum_i\delta_i\right). $$
Thus
$$ \sum_i\delta_i\le\frac{dk}{1-d}. $$

Symmetry gives
$$ y^{\mathsf T}Ay-p^{\mathsf T}Ap = \delta^{\mathsf T}A(y+p). $$
Since $p,y\in[0,1]^n$,
$$ y^{\mathsf T}Ay-p^{\mathsf T}Ap \le2n\sum_i\delta_i \le\frac{2ndk}{1-d}. $$
Using $d\le n^{-3}$, $k\le n$, and $n\ge2$,
$$ \frac{2ndk}{1-d} \le\frac{2}{n(1-n^{-3})} \le\frac{16}{7n} <\frac3n. $$
Dropping the margin denominators consequently yields
$$ M(x)\le \frac{2\xi}{n} \left(p^{\mathsf T}Ap+\frac3n\right). $$

Suppose an $\alpha$-approximation returns these fractional seeds. Combining the preceding bounds,
$$ p^{\mathsf T}Ap \ge \frac{2\alpha E^\star}{1+d}-\frac3n \ge \alpha E^\star-\frac3n. $$
If $\alpha\ge6/n$, then $E^\star\ge1$ implies
$$ \boxed{ p^{\mathsf T}Ap\ge\frac{\alpha E^\star}{2}. } $$

Deterministic rounding to at most $k$ vertices

Make $k$ independent draws. Each draw selects vertex $i$ with probability $p_i/k$, or selects a dummy outcome with the remaining probability. Let $S$ contain the distinct selected vertices, so $|S|\le k$.

Split the draws into groups of sizes
$$ r=\lfloor k/2\rfloor, \qquad s=\lceil k/2\rceil. $$
For $0\le u\le1$ and $hu\le1$, the two-term inclusion–exclusion inequality gives
$$ 1-(1-u)^h \ge hu-\binom h2u^2 \ge\frac{hu}{2}. $$
Apply this with $u=p_i/k$ and $h=r$, or with $u=p_j/k$ and $h=s$. For each edge $\{i,j\}$, the probability that $i$ occurs in the first group and $j$ in the second is at least
$$ \frac{rp_i}{2k}\frac{sp_j}{2k} \ge\frac{p_ip_j}{24}, $$
because $r/k\ge1/3$ and $s/k\ge1/2$. Hence
$$ \mathbb E[|E(S)|] \ge \frac1{24}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}p_ip_j = \frac{p^{\mathsf T}Ap}{48}. $$

This can be derandomized exactly. Let $T$ be the vertices already selected, let $h$ draws remain, and write $q_i=p_i/k$. An edge's probability of eventual inclusion is:

  • $1$, if both endpoints are in $T$;
  • $1-(1-q_j)^h$, if only endpoint $j$ is missing;
  • $1-(1-q_i)^h-(1-q_j)^h+(1-q_i-q_j)^h$, if both endpoints are missing.

Summing these expressions computes the conditional expected edge count. Enumerate the possible next outcomes and choose one whose conditional expectation is at least the current expectation; such an outcome exists because their weighted average is the current expectation. Repeating this procedure gives a deterministic set with at least the original expected edge count.

Delete any padding vertices and complete the set to size $k$ using original vertices. Deleting isolated vertices loses no edges, and completion cannot decrease the edge count. Therefore,
$$ \boxed{ |E(S)|\ge\frac{\alpha E^\star}{96} } \qquad \text{whenever }\alpha\ge6/n. $$

If the mass algorithm takes time $T(m,\tau,L)$ and its output length is $\ell$, the transfer takes
$$ T(2,n,O(n^2\log n))+\operatorname{poly}(n,\ell) $$
bit time.

8. Applying Gap-ETH

Use the following known theorem: under Gap-ETH, for every nonnegative $g(N)=o(1)$, no polynomial-time algorithm distinguishes a graph containing a $k$-clique from a graph whose every induced $k$-vertex subgraph has density at most $N^{-g(N)}$. Density here is the edge count divided by $\binom{k}{2}$. This is [Manurangsi’s Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.05991).

The theorem excludes Densest $k$-Subgraph approximation guarantees $N^{-o(1)}$. Indeed, for a proposed guarantee $\rho(N)=N^{-h(N)}$ with $h=o(1)$, choose soundness
$$ \frac{\rho(N)}2 = N^{-\left(h(N)+\log 2/\log N\right)}. $$
