The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings, and a Borda winner \(w\), choose rational masses \(x_{t,p}\ge 0\) for partial rankings \(p\subseteq t\), with \(\sum_{p\subseteq t}x_{t,p}=\mu_t\). Require that for every opponent \(c\), \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\underline{s}_p(w)\ge\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\overline{s}_p(c)\), where the score bounds range over completions of \(p\); ties count as wins. Restrict each partial ballot to have either no hidden entries or at least two, and minimize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}|p|\), the average number of disclosed ranking entries per person.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge2\) candidates. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(t\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The parameter \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(w\in C\) be a Borda winner of this society; ties count as wins.
A partial ballot \(p\subseteq t\) discloses selected candidate–position entries of \(t\), retaining their absolute positions. Its other entries are hidden; a completion is any complete ranking agreeing with all disclosed entries. Require either zero or at least two hidden entries, and let \(|p|\) count disclosed entries.
Write \(s_q(c)\) for the Borda score of \(c\) in complete ranking \(q\), and define \(\underline{s}_p(c)=\min_{q\text{ completing }p}s_q(c)\) and \(\overline{s}_p(c)=\max_{q\text{ completing }p}s_q(c)\).
Choose rational masses \(x_{t,p}\ge0\) over admissible partial ballots, satisfying \(\sum_{p\subseteq t}x_{t,p}=\mu_t\) and, for every \(c\in C\setminus\{w\}\), \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\underline{s}_p(w)\ge\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\overline{s}_p(c)\). The disclosure cost is \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}|p|\).
Given a binary rational budget \(B\in[0,m]\), is deciding whether feasible masses of disclosure cost at most \(B\) exist solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), including the budget.
For a finite society of \(n\) voters, one inclusion-minimal contrastive explanation can be found in \(\Theta(nm^2)\), and one inclusion-minimal abductive explanation subject to the same restriction on hidden entries in \(\Theta(nm^3)\). These algorithms find one minimal explanation, without guaranteeing minimum disclosure cost.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted partial rankings as disclosed candidate–position entries with absolute positions, following the discrete rank-matrix representation.
  • Made distribution normalization explicit: positive support masses sum to one.
  • Restricted to at least two candidates, consistent with the nonconstant voting classification setting.
  • Introduced a binary rational budget in [0,m] to state the decision version of the given minimization problem, and included it in the total input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Proven result and running time. The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable. An equivalent rational linear program has \(O(\tau m^3)\) variables and constraints. Its construction and solution take

\( \bigl(L+\tau m^3\log(2\tau m)\bigr)^{O(1)} \)

bit operations. The input length \(L\) includes the budget. The same construction also permits computation of the minimum disclosure cost.

The essential point is that, after fixing the two extreme hidden positions and whether \(w\) is hidden, all remaining hiding choices can be represented exactly by their marginal masses.

1. Score bounds from hidden positions.

Use Borda scores \(m-1,\ldots,0\). For a ranking \(t\), let \(r_t(c)\) be the position of candidate \(c\), and write \(a_t(c)=m-r_t(c)\).

A partial ballot is determined by its hidden candidate set \(H\): every candidate outside \(H\) retains its position from \(t\). If \(H\ne\varnothing\), put

\( i=\min_{c\in H}r_t(c),\qquad j=\max_{c\in H}r_t(c). \)

The admissibility restriction gives \(i<j\). The unfilled positions are precisely the original positions of candidates in \(H\). Any hidden candidate can occupy any unfilled position, because the other hidden candidates can be assigned bijectively to the remaining positions. Consequently,

\( \underline{s}_p(w)= \begin{cases} a_t(w),&w\notin H,\\ m-j,&w\in H, \end{cases} \)

and, for every rival \(c\),

\( \overline{s}_p(c)= \begin{cases} a_t(c),&c\notin H,\\ m-i,&c\in H. \end{cases} \)

Thus these bounds depend on the extreme hidden positions and on which individual candidates are hidden.

2. Partitioning partial ballots into polynomially many groups.

Fix \(t\), and let \(c_{t,k}\) denote its candidate at position \(k\). Apart from the fully disclosed ballot, group admissible partial ballots by a triple \(\rho=(i,j,e)\), where \(i<j\) and \(e\in\{0,1\}\) records whether \(w\) is hidden.

Include precisely the triples satisfying the following conditions:

  • If \(e=1\), require \(i\le r_t(w)\le j\).
  • If \(e=0\), require \(r_t(w)\notin\{i,j\}\).

For an included triple, define the mandatory hidden set

\( F_{t,\rho}=\{c_{t,i},c_{t,j}\}\cup \begin{cases} \{w\},&e=1,\\ \varnothing,&e=0, \end{cases} \)

and the optional hidden set

\( U_{t,\rho}=\{c\in C\setminus\{w\}:i<r_t(c)<j\}. \)

The hidden sets in this group are exactly

\( H=F_{t,\rho}\cup K,\qquad K\subseteq U_{t,\rho}. \)

Indeed, the endpoints must be hidden; candidates outside the interval must be disclosed; and the status of \(w\) is fixed by \(e\). Every other interior candidate can independently be hidden or disclosed. Conversely, every such choice has extreme hidden positions \(i,j\), has the prescribed status of \(w\), and hides at least two entries.

Therefore every admissible partial ballot belongs to exactly one group, or is fully disclosed. There are at most \(2\binom m2\) groups per ranking.

Write

\( h_{t,\rho}=m-i,\qquad \ell_{t,\rho}= \begin{cases} a_t(w),&e=0,\\ m-j,&e=1. \end{cases} \)

Within this group, the lower score of \(w\) is always \(\ell_{t,\rho}\).

3. The compact linear program.

Introduce the following variables for each support ranking \(t\):

  • \(z_{t,0}\): mass assigned to the fully disclosed ballot;
  • \(z_{t,\rho}\): total mass assigned to group \(\rho\);
  • \(y_{t,\rho,u}\), for \(u\in U_{t,\rho}\): mass in that group on which \(u\) is hidden.

Impose

\( z_{t,0}\ge0,\qquad z_{t,\rho}\ge0,\qquad 0\le y_{t,\rho,u}\le z_{t,\rho}, \)

and the mass-conservation equations

\( z_{t,0}+\sum_\rho z_{t,\rho}=\mu_t. \)

For each candidate \(c\), define the following linear expression for its hidden mass within a group:

\( v_{t,\rho,c}= \begin{cases} z_{t,\rho},&c\in F_{t,\rho},\\ y_{t,\rho,c},&c\in U_{t,\rho},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \)

The contribution of this group to the aggregate upper score of a rival \(c\) is

\( a_t(c)z_{t,\rho} +\bigl(h_{t,\rho}-a_t(c)\bigr)v_{t,\rho,c}. \)

To verify this expression, the mass \(z_{t,\rho}-v_{t,\rho,c}\) discloses \(c\), giving upper score \(a_t(c)\), while the mass \(v_{t,\rho,c}\) hides \(c\), giving upper score \(h_{t,\rho}\).

For every \(c\ne w\), impose the linear inequality

\( \sum_t\left[ a_t(w)z_{t,0}+\sum_\rho\ell_{t,\rho}z_{t,\rho} \right] \ge \sum_t\left[ a_t(c)z_{t,0} +\sum_\rho\left( a_t(c)z_{t,\rho} +\bigl(h_{t,\rho}-a_t(c)\bigr)v_{t,\rho,c} \right) \right]. \)

Within a group, every ballot hides all of \(F_{t,\rho}\), and each optional candidate contributes one further hidden entry exactly when it is hidden. Hence the aggregate disclosure cost is the linear expression

\( D=\sum_t\left[ m z_{t,0} +\sum_\rho\left( (m-|F_{t,\rho}|)z_{t,\rho} -\sum_{u\in U_{t,\rho}}y_{t,\rho,u} \right) \right]. \)

Finally, impose \(D\le B\).

4. Proof of exact equivalence.

First suppose rational masses \(x_{t,p}\) satisfy the original problem. Set \(z_{t,0}=x_{t,t}\). For every group, let \(z_{t,\rho}\) be the sum of its ballot masses, and let \(y_{t,\rho,u}\) be the sum over its ballots hiding \(u\).

These variables are rational and satisfy the bounds and mass-conservation equations. The formulas above show that their aggregate score bounds and cost equal those of the original masses. Thus they satisfy the compact linear program.

For the converse, suppose the compact program has a rational feasible solution. We explicitly realize every group by admissible partial ballots.

Fix a group and abbreviate its mass by \(z\). List its optional candidates as \(u_1,\ldots,u_k\), ordered so that

\( 0\le y_1\le\cdots\le y_k\le z,\qquad y_a=y_{t,\rho,u_a}. \)

Set \(y_0=0\), \(y_{k+1}=z\), and define

\( \beta_r=y_{r+1}-y_r,\qquad 0\le r\le k. \)

Assign mass \(\beta_r\) to the partial ballot with hidden set

\( F_{t,\rho}\cup\{u_{r+1},\ldots,u_k\}. \)

For \(r=k\), the optional set is empty. For \(k=0\), this construction simply assigns mass \(z\) to the ballot hiding \(F_{t,\rho}\).

All these masses are nonnegative rational numbers. They sum to

\( \sum_{r=0}^{k}\beta_r=y_{k+1}-y_0=z. \)

Moreover, \(u_a\) is hidden precisely in the ballots indexed by \(r<a\). Its total hidden mass is therefore

\( \sum_{r=0}^{a-1}\beta_r=y_a-y_0=y_a. \)

Thus the construction realizes exactly every prescribed optional hidden mass. Every resulting ballot is admissible because its mandatory hidden set contains the two distinct endpoint candidates.

Apply this construction to all groups and assign \(z_{t,0}\) to the fully disclosed ballot. The reconstructed masses sum to \(\mu_t\) for each ranking. Their score bounds and disclosure cost equal the corresponding linear expressions, so all original constraints hold.

This proves equivalence for rational solutions. Standard rational linear-programming algorithms return a rational feasible point whenever this rational system is feasible, so rationality introduces no additional obstacle.

5. Size and bit complexity.

There are \(O(m^2)\) groups per support ranking, each with at most \(m\) optional candidates. Consequently, the program has \(O(\tau m^3)\) variables and constraints.

Its sparse coefficient count is also \(O(\tau m^3)\):

  • Each group variable occurs in at most \(m-1\) winner inequalities, its mass equation, its cost term, and its optional-variable bounds.
  • Each optional variable occurs in its bound, its own candidate’s winner inequality, and the cost inequality.

All coefficients introduced by the construction are integers of magnitude at most \(m\). The remaining rational data are the original masses and budget. Including coefficient indices, a sparse encoding therefore has length

\( S=O\bigl(L+\tau m^3\log(2\tau m)\bigr). \)

The program can be constructed in polynomial time in this bound. The polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming then decides feasibility in \(S^{O(1)}\) bit operations: all its hypotheses hold because the program has finitely many variables and constraints and explicitly encoded rational coefficients. Converting the sparse representation to a dense one, if needed, adds only polynomial overhead.

Since the rankings are explicitly supplied, this is polynomial in the total input length. The reconstruction above uses only \(O(\tau m^3)\) partial ballots and is likewise polynomial-time.

The given inclusion-minimal explanation algorithms do not establish minimum-cost optimality. Here, minimum cost follows by minimizing \(D\) over the compact program. Its feasible region is nonempty before imposing the budget: full disclosure is feasible because the supplied \(w\) is a Borda winner. All variables are bounded by their ranking’s mass, so the minimum is attained.
