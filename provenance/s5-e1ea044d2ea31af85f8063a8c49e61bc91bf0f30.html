The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support society with rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s vote between status quo \(r\) and proposal \(p\), an accepted-threshold set \(A_t\) encoded as a finite union of rational intervals, and an accepted-outcome set \(B_t\subseteq\{r,p\}\), find a threshold \(q\in[1/2,1)\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[q\in A_t\ \text{or}\ y_q\in B_t]\), where \(y_q=p\) exactly when the proposal-vote mass exceeds \(q\), and return \((q,y_q)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are two distinct feasible outcomes, status quo \(r\) and proposal \(p\), so \(m=2\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type reports a vote \(v_t\in\{r,p\}\), an accepted-threshold set \(A_t\subseteq[1/2,1)\), and an accepted-outcome set \(B_t\subseteq\{r,p\}\). Each \(A_t\) is an explicitly listed finite union of intervals with rational endpoints and specified endpoint inclusion; singleton intervals and empty sets are allowed. No relationship between votes and acceptance sets is required.
Every threshold \(q\in[1/2,1)\) is feasible. Put \(s=\sum_{t:v_t=p}\mu_t\); its outcome is \(y_q=p\) if \(s>q\), and \(y_q=r\) otherwise. Type \(t\) accepts exactly when \(q\in A_t\) or \(y_q\in B_t\).
The optimization problem is to return a rational threshold and its outcome \((q,y_q)\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[q\in A_t\text{ or }y_q\in B_t]\); ties between maximizers may be broken arbitrarily. Let \(K\) be the total number of listed intervals and \(L\) the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,K,L\).

Is this exact acceptance-maximization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents, an acceptance-maximizing mechanism for absolute disjunctivists is established; with explicitly enumerated feasible rules and outcomes and polynomial-time rule evaluation, it runs in polynomial time. [Abramowitz and Mattei](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.08501v2)
No result settling the exact finite-support, rational-interval problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to sum to one, so proposal-vote mass is the vote fraction compared with the threshold.
  • Specified interval endpoint inclusion explicitly, allowing open, closed, and half-open intervals, singleton intervals, and empty unions.
  • Restricted accepted-threshold sets to the feasible threshold domain; acceptance of infeasible thresholds has no effect.
  • Required a rational output threshold and allowed arbitrary tie-breaking between maximizers.
  • Made both distinct outcomes and every threshold in the stated domain feasible, with no required relationship between votes and acceptance sets.
  • Defined the outcome count as m=2, the interval count as K, and L as total binary input length.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. For the stipulated $m=2$, an exact algorithm uses
$$ O\!\left((K+1)(\tau+K+1)L^2\right) $$
bit operations and returns a maximizing rational threshold of binary length $O(L)$.

The key is that the objective can change only at listed interval endpoints or at the outcome-switching threshold $s$. Endpoint inclusion requires checking these points separately.

1. A finite set of candidate thresholds

Write the acceptance objective as
$$ F(q)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t \mathbf{1}\!\left[q\in A_t\ \text{or}\ y_q\in B_t\right], \qquad q\in[1/2,1). $$

Let $E$ contain:

  • the two boundary points $1/2$ and $1$;
  • every listed interval endpoint lying in $[1/2,1]$;
  • $s$, if $1/2<s<1$.

Remove duplicates and sort:
$$ \frac12=e_0<e_1<\cdots<e_{h-1}=1. $$

There are at most two endpoints per listed interval, so
$$ h\le 2K+3. $$

Define the candidate set
$$ C= \{e_i:0\le i<h-1\} \ \cup\ \left\{\frac{e_i+e_{i+1}}2:0\le i<h-1\right\}. $$

Every candidate is rational and belongs to $[1/2,1)$. Moreover,
$$ |C|\le 2h-2\le 4K+4. $$

Lemma: constancy between consecutive breakpoints

For every $i$ with $0\le i<h-1$, the outcome and every type’s acceptance indicator are constant on $(e_i,e_{i+1})$.

Proof. Consider one listed interval. No endpoint of that interval lies strictly inside $(e_i,e_{i+1})$, by construction of $E$. Thus membership in that interval is constant throughout this open gap: the comparisons with its endpoints cannot change there. Endpoint inclusion flags do not affect this conclusion because no point in the gap equals an endpoint. Empty intervals contribute nothing.

Taking the union of the listed intervals shows that membership in each $A_t$ is constant on the gap. This remains valid when intervals overlap or when some are singletons.

The outcome predicate is $q<s$. If $1/2<s<1$, then $s\in E$, so this predicate is constant on every gap. If $s\le1/2$, the outcome is always $r$ on the feasible domain; if $s=1$, it is always $p$. These cases exhaust the possibilities because the positive masses sum to $1$, hence $0\le s\le1$.

Consequently, both $q\in A_t$ and $y_q\in B_t$ are constant on the gap, and so is their disjunction. Therefore $F$ is constant there. $\square$

Proposition: the candidate set contains a global maximizer

For every feasible threshold $q$, there is a candidate $c\in C$ such that
$$ y_c=y_q \qquad\text{and}\qquad F(c)=F(q). $$

Proof. If $q$ equals a breakpoint, then $q\ne1$ by feasibility, so $q\in C$ and we take $c=q$.

Otherwise, $q$ lies in a unique gap $(e_i,e_{i+1})$. Take
$$ c=\frac{e_i+e_{i+1}}2. $$
The lemma gives the stated equalities. $\square$

Because $C$ is finite and nonempty, its maximum is attained. The proposition therefore proves
$$ \max_{q\in[1/2,1)}F(q)=\max_{c\in C}F(c). $$

This also proves existence of a rational maximizer, despite the feasible domain being half-open.

2. Exact algorithm and correctness

Perform the following steps:

  • Compute $s$ exactly from the masses of types voting for $p$.
  • Construct and sort $E$, and construct $C$.
  • For each $c\in C$, compute $y_c$ using the strict comparison $s>c$.
  • For every type, test whether $y_c\in B_t$ or whether at least one listed interval of $A_t$ contains $c$. Apply each interval’s specified endpoint inclusion flags.
  • Sum the masses of the accepting types and return a candidate with maximum score, together with its outcome.

Each type contributes its mass at most once, even if several of its intervals contain the candidate. The proposition proves that the returned pair is globally optimal.

In particular, the algorithm evaluates $q=s$ separately whenever it is feasible. Thus it respects the prescribed outcome $y_s=r$. Evaluating every feasible breakpoint separately also captures acceptance occurring only at a singleton.

3. Bit complexity

Write each mass in its input representation as
$$ \mu_t=\frac{a_t}{b_t}, \qquad b_t>0. $$

Define a common denominator and integer weights by
$$ D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t, \qquad w_t=a_t\frac{D}{b_t}. $$

These are integers. Since $\mu_t>0$ and the masses sum to $1$,
$$ 0<w_t\le D, \qquad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t=D. $$

The sum of the binary lengths of the input denominators is at most $L$, so $D$ and every $w_t$ have $O(L)$ bits. Compute
$$ W_p=\sum_{t:v_t=p}w_t, \qquad s=\frac{W_p}{D}. $$

The score of a candidate can now be compared using the integer
$$ Z(c)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t \mathbf{1}\!\left[c\in A_t\ \text{or}\ y_c\in B_t\right], $$
because $F(c)=Z(c)/D$. Every such score lies between $0$ and $D$.

All input endpoints and $s$ have rational representations of $O(L)$ bits. If two consecutive breakpoints are represented as $a/b$ and $c/d$, their midpoint is
$$ \frac{ad+bc}{2bd}, $$
which also has $O(L)$ bits. Fractions need not be reduced for exact comparisons or output.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, multiplication, division, and rational comparison on these representations cost $O(L^2)$ bit operations. Hence:

  • Computing $D$, the weights, and $s$ costs $O(\tau L^2)$.
  • Sorting the breakpoints costs $O((K+1)\log(K+2)L^2)$.
  • Constructing the candidates costs $O((K+1)L^2)$.
  • Evaluating one candidate costs $O((\tau+K+1)L^2)$: scan the types and their listed intervals, then add the accepting weights.

Since there are $O(K+1)$ candidates, the total is
$$ O\!\left((K+1)(\tau+K+1)L^2\right). $$

This bound is for $m=2$, as required by the problem. It depends on the explicitly listed support size $\tau$; the algorithm never expands rational masses into individual agents.

4. Relation to the cited result and conclusion

The paper’s definition of absolute disjunctivism uses the same logical condition: acceptance of the rule or acceptance of the outcome. [Abramowitz and Mattei, Definition 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.08501v2). The proof above directly handles the present infinite threshold domain through a polynomial-size candidate set, without assuming an extension of the finite-agent result.

Thus the exact optimization problem is polynomial-time solvable. Under standard polynomial-time reductions, an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness classification for its decision version would therefore imply $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
