The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), binary valuations \(v_{tI},v_{tj}\) for one pool of identical indivisible items and \(J\) divisible resource categories, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_I,q_j\), find feasible measures \(P_t\) over bundles \((k,d)\), where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) and \(d\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^J\). Require \(P_t\) to have mass \(\mu_t\), require \(k=0\) or \(d_j=0\) whenever the type does not value that resource, and impose \(\sum_t\int k\,dP_t=q_I\) and \(\sum_t\int d_j\,dP_t=q_j\) for each \(j\). Minimize \(\sum_t\int (v_{tI}k+\sum_j v_{tj}d_j)^2\,dP_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge 1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There is one pool of identical indivisible items and \(J\ge 1\) divisible resource categories; write \(m=J+1\). Input valuations satisfy \(v_{tI},v_{tj}\in\{0,1\}\), and per-capita supplies \(q_I,q_j\) are positive binary rationals. Each resource is valued by at least one supported type.
An allocation consists of nonnegative finite Borel measures \(P_t\) on \(\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\times\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{J}\), with \(P_t\) of total mass \(\mu_t\). A type may be split among different bundles.
For \(P_t\)-almost every bundle \((k,d)\), require \(k=0\) if \(v_{tI}=0\), and \(d_j=0\) if \(v_{tj}=0\). Require full allocation: \(\sum_t\int k\,dP_t=q_I\) and \(\sum_t\int d_j\,dP_t=q_j\) for every \(j\). These are aggregate mass constraints; bundles have no additional upper bounds.
Define \(u_t(k,d)=v_{tI}k+\sum_{j=1}^{J}v_{tj}d_j\) and minimize \(F(P)=\sum_t\int u_t(k,d)^2\,dP_t\).

Is deciding whether some feasible allocation satisfies \(F(P)\le B\), for an input binary rational \(B\ge 0\), in P or NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length, including \(B\).

For finite lists of agents, square-sum minimization is NP-hard even with identical indivisible items; it is polynomial-time solvable when all divisible goods have the same valuation profile. [Verified results](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05986v2).
No result settling this exact distribution-over-types problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Supply signs and empty resource categories were unspecified: retained the mixed setting with positive supplies and at least one divisible category; zero-supply categories can be omitted.
  • Carried over the assumption that every supplied resource is valued by at least one supported type.
  • Specified nonnegative finite Borel measures and interpreted bundle restrictions almost everywhere; measures may split a type among different bundles.
  • Kept the graded question's aggregate supply constraints and unbounded bundle domains, without adding individual supply caps.
  • Defined m as the number of resource categories, J+1, since the continuum input has no finite item count.
  • Used the rational-threshold decision version of the stated square-sum optimization problem to make the P-versus-NP-hard target precise; included the threshold in L.

Result

The decision problem is in $\mathrm P$. It can be decided deterministically in
$$ (m\tau+L)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations.

The main issue is exact comparison: convexity alone only gives approximation algorithms. We resolve this by proving that the optimal value is algebraic of degree at most three, with a polynomial bound on its coefficient lengths. Consequently, a polynomial number of bits of approximation suffices to compare it with the rational threshold, including equality.

1. Reducing allocations to a finite convex problem

For finite nonnegative $a,b$, define
$$ \phi(a,b)=\inf \mathbb E[(K+D)^2], $$
where $K$ is a nonnegative integer-valued random variable, $D$ is nonnegative, and
$$ \mathbb E K=a,\qquad \mathbb E D=b. $$

Write
$$ n=\lfloor a\rfloor,\qquad p=a-n,\qquad z=a+b. $$

Then
$$ \phi(a,b)= \begin{cases} z^2,&z\ge n+1,\\[2mm] (1-p)\left(n+\dfrac{b}{1-p}\right)^2+p(n+1)^2,&z<n+1. \end{cases} $$

In both cases, an attaining distribution uses at most two bundles.

Proof of the formula

Jensen’s inequality gives $\phi(a,b)\ge z^2$.

If $z\ge n+1$, give probabilities $1-p,p$ to item counts $n,n+1$, respectively, and set $D=z-K$. This is nonnegative, has the prescribed means, and gives constant utility $z$.

Suppose instead that $z<n+1$. Set
$$ s=\frac{b}{1-p},\qquad r=n+s. $$
Here $0\le s<1$. The proposed distribution gives probabilities $1-p,p$ to
$$ (K,D)=(n,s),\qquad (K,D)=(n+1,0). $$

To prove optimality, put
$$ \alpha=2n+1+s^2,\qquad \beta=2r,\qquad \gamma=(n+1)^2-\alpha(n+1). $$
For every integer $k\ge0$ and every $d\ge0$,
$$ (k+d)^2\ge \alpha k+\beta d+\gamma. $$

Indeed, for $k\le n$, minimization over $d\ge0$ gives
$$ -r^2+(2r-\alpha)k, $$
which is smallest at $k=n$ because
$$ 2r-\alpha=-(1-s)^2\le0. $$
Its value there equals $\gamma$.

For $k\ge n+1$, the minimizing divisible amount is zero, and
$$ k^2-\alpha k-\gamma =(k-n-1)(k+n+1-\alpha)\ge0, $$
since $\alpha\le2n+2$. Equality holds at both proposed bundles. Integrating proves the formula, including against distributions of infinite cost.

For several divisible categories with prescribed means $b_j$, let $b=\sum_jb_j$. When $b>0$, replace each scalar $D$ by coordinates
$$ d_j=\frac{b_j}{b}D. $$
This preserves every category mean and total utility. When $b=0$, all coordinates are zero.

Aggregate formulation

Let $A_t$ be the aggregate item amount assigned to type $t$, let $D_{tj}$ be its aggregate amount of divisible category $j$, and put
$$ C_t=\sum_jD_{tj}. $$
Let $\mathcal A$ be the rational polytope specified by nonnegativity, the valuation restrictions, and
$$ \sum_tA_t=q_I,\qquad \sum_tD_{tj}=q_j. $$

The optimal value of the original problem is exactly
$$ F_*=\min_{(A,D)\in\mathcal A} f(A,D), \qquad f(A,D)=\sum_t\mu_t\phi\left(\frac{A_t}{\mu_t},\frac{C_t}{\mu_t}\right). $$

The formula supplies the lower bound for any allocation after normalizing each type’s measure by $\mu_t$. Conversely, its two-bundle construction realizes every aggregate point, including every category total. For a type not valuing items, $A_t=0$ and its contribution is $C_t^2/\mu_t$.

The polytope is nonempty because each resource has a supported valuer. It is compact because each coordinate is bounded by its resource supply.

The function $\phi$ is convex: mixing attaining distributions mixes their means and costs. It is continuous. Away from integer $a$, this follows from the formulas and their agreement at $z=n+1$; at integer $a$, use
$$ z^2\le\phi(a,b)\le z^2+p(1-p), $$
whose upper bound comes from adjacent item counts with constant divisible amount $b$.

Thus $F_*$ is attained. In particular, the decision question is exactly whether $F_*\le B$.

2. Polynomial-time approximation

Define
$$ Q=q_I+\sum_jq_j,\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t. $$

For every feasible aggregate point,
$$ 0\le f(A,D)\le \frac{Q^2}{\mu_{\min}}+\frac14. $$
Indeed, writing $Y_t=A_t+C_t$, the preceding estimate gives
$$ f(A,D)\le\sum_t\frac{Y_t^2}{\mu_t}+\frac14 \le\frac{(\sum_tY_t)^2}{\mu_{\min}}+\frac14. $$
Here $\sum_tY_t=Q$.

There is an exact rational value and subgradient oracle. In the region $z\ge n+1$, a subgradient of $\phi$ is
$$ (2z,2z). $$
In the other region, the supporting inequality proved above gives the subgradient
$$ (2n+1+s^2,\;2n+2s), \qquad s=\frac{b}{1-p}. $$
These formulas remain valid at integer $a$. Each component is bounded on feasible means by
$$ g=2Q/\mu_{\min}+2. $$

For each resource, eliminate one valued recipient coordinate using its supply equality. The resulting feasible polytope is a product of sets of the form
$$ \left\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{h-1}:\sum_i x_i\le q_r\right\}, $$
where $h$ is that resource’s number of supported valuers. Its dimension $N$ satisfies $N\le m\tau$.

If $N=0$, the aggregate point is unique and its rational objective can be evaluated exactly. Assume $N>0$. In the remaining coordinates, $f$ has a Lipschitz bound
$$ K=2Ng. $$
This follows because eliminating a coordinate replaces each derivative by a difference of two aggregate derivatives, each bounded in magnitude by $g$.

We apply the standard ellipsoid weak optimization theorem: a bounded convex body with a polynomial-time separation oracle and explicitly supplied inner and outer balls admits rational weak optimization in time polynomial in the dimension, the encoding lengths of those balls, and the requested accuracy length. This is the separation–optimization theorem of [Grötschel, Lovász and Schrijver, Theorem 3.1](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf).

Here all its hypotheses can be checked explicitly. Put
$$ V=Q^2/\mu_{\min}+1 $$
and use the bounded epigraph
$$ \mathcal E=\{(x,z):x\in\mathcal A,\ f(x)\le z\le V\} $$
in the eliminated coordinates.

Its separation oracle uses either a violated linear constraint or the rational subgradient of $f$. Give every valued recipient of resource $r$ the aggregate amount $q_r/h$. In the eliminated coordinates this point has an inner ball of radius
$$ r_0=\frac{\min_rq_r}{2\tau^2}. $$
To check this, a perturbation of Euclidean norm at most $r_0$ changes an individual coordinate by at most $r_0$, and the eliminated coordinate by at most $\tau r_0$; both remain nonnegative.

At height $V-\tfrac12$, an inner ball for $\mathcal E$ therefore has radius
$$ r_{\mathcal E}=\min\left\{r_0,\frac1{8(K+1)},\frac18\right\}. $$
The objective bound above leaves a vertical margin of at least $\tfrac14$, which accommodates the changes in $f$ and height. An outer radius is, for example,
$$ R_{\mathcal E}=(N+1)(Q+V+1). $$
All these numbers have polynomial encoding length.

Weak optimization with accuracy $\eta$ gives a rational $(y,z)$ with distance at most $\eta$ from $\mathcal E$ and $z\le F_*+\eta$. Project $y$ onto the product of simplices. This projection is computable exactly with rational arithmetic: each factor uses
$$ x_i=\max\{y_i-\lambda,0\}, $$
where either $\lambda=0$, or sorting the coordinates identifies a rational $\lambda$ making their sum equal the cap.

If $\bar x$ is the projection, proximity to $\mathcal E$ and the Lipschitz bound give
$$ F_*\le f(\bar x)\le F_*+(2+2K)\eta. $$
Consequently, for any positive rational $\varepsilon$, we can compute a rational $U$ satisfying
$$ F_*\le U\le F_*+\varepsilon $$
in
$$ \operatorname{poly}\bigl(m\tau,L+\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr) $$
bit operations.

It remains to bound the accuracy needed for exact decision.

3. A dual formulation

For nonnegative prices $\alpha$ for items and $\beta_j$ for divisible categories, define
$$ \beta_t=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{\beta_j:v_{tj}=1\}\bigr). $$

For a type not valuing items, the minimum bundle cost minus its resource-price payment is
$$ \gamma_t=-\frac{\beta_t^2}{4}. $$
For an item-valuing type, it is
$$ \gamma_t=g(\alpha,\beta_t), \qquad g(\alpha,\beta)= \min_{\substack{k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\\d\ge0}} \bigl((k+d)^2-\alpha k-\beta d\bigr). $$

These scalar expressions are exact because a fixed total divisible amount is assigned to a valued category of largest price. If a type values no divisible category, then $\beta_t=0$, and allowing scalar $d\ge0$ does not change the minimum: $d=0$ minimizes it.

The dual objective is
$$ D(\alpha,\beta) =\alpha q_I+\sum_j\beta_jq_j+\sum_t\mu_t\gamma_t. $$
Integrating the bundle inequalities gives $D(\alpha,\beta)\le F_*$.

We will also use strong duality, justified as follows. With divisible supplies fixed, let $f_I(x)$ be the optimal mixed cost when item supply is $x\ge0$. It is finite, convex, and nondecreasing:

  • Finiteness follows from the aggregate formulation.
  • Convexity follows by mixing allocations.
  • To reduce item supply, mix an allocation with its image under $(k,d)\mapsto(0,d)$; costs decrease and divisible supplies are preserved.

Because $q_I>0$, this finite convex function has a subgradient $\alpha\ge0$ at $q_I$. Its supporting inequality extends to $x=0$: apply it to a convex combination of $0$ and $q_I$ and use convexity. Hence, defining
$$ H(\alpha)=\min\left\{F(P)-\alpha\sum_t\int k\,dP_t: \text{the divisible supplies are }q_j\right\}, $$
we obtain
$$ F_*=\max_{\alpha\ge0}\bigl(H(\alpha)+\alpha q_I\bigr). $$

The minimum defining $H$ exists. In aggregate coordinates, divisible variables are bounded and
$$ \mu_t\phi(A_t/\mu_t,C_t/\mu_t)-\alpha A_t \ge A_t^2/\mu_t-\alpha A_t, $$
which makes its sublevel sets bounded in the free item variables.

For fixed $\alpha$, the same supporting-hyperplane argument applies to $H$ as a function of the positive divisible supplies. It is finite, convex, and nondecreasing in each supply: removing divisible amounts reduces cost without changing the item penalty. Thus its supporting prices are nonnegative. Minimizing the corresponding Lagrangian separates over types and bundles. This proves
$$ H(\alpha)=\max_{\beta\ge0} \left(\sum_j\beta_jq_j+\sum_t\mu_t\gamma_t\right), $$
with attainment. Therefore
$$ F_*=\max_{\alpha,\beta\ge0}D(\alpha,\beta). $$

4. Restricting the item price to an interval of length two

Consider the relaxation in which the item quantity is also divisible:
$$ G=\min_{(A,D)\in\mathcal A} \sum_t\frac{(A_t+C_t)^2}{\mu_t}. $$
Choose an optimizer and write
$$ z_t=\frac{A_t+C_t}{\mu_t}. $$

For every resource $r$, any type receiving a positive amount must minimize $2z_t$ among its supported valuers. Otherwise, shifting a sufficiently small amount to a type with smaller $2z_t$ would decrease the quadratic objective. Define its price by
$$ \pi_r=\min_{t:v_{tr}=1}2z_t, \qquad \alpha_0=\pi_I. $$
Thus $2z_t\ge\pi_r$ on every valued incidence, with equality on every positive allocation.

Let $H_c(\alpha)$ denote the analogue of $H(\alpha)$ for this divisible-item relaxation. The preceding inequalities and the supporting inequality for a convex quadratic show that the chosen relaxation optimizer minimizes $H_c(\alpha_0)$; its item total is $q_I$.

Because some type receives positive item quantity,
$$ \alpha_0\le 2Q/\mu_{\min}. $$
Set
$$ n=\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_0}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad a=2n,\qquad b=2n+2. $$
In particular,
$$ 0\le n\le Q/\mu_{\min}. $$

Equality at even prices

For every nonnegative integer $\ell$,
$$ H(2\ell)=H_c(2\ell). $$

To prove this, fix the divisible aggregate allocation in a minimizer of $H_c(2\ell)$. Minimizing its quadratic expression over $A_t$ gives, for an item-valuing type,
$$ \frac{A_t}{\mu_t} =\max\left\{\ell-\frac{C_t}{\mu_t},0\right\}. $$
If this item mean is positive, its total utility is $\ell$. It can be realized using item counts $0,\ell$, with scalar divisible amount $\ell-K$, while preserving both means and attaining Jensen equality. Individual category means are preserved by proportional division. A type with zero item mean uses a constant divisible bundle.

This realizes the relaxation minimizer in the mixed problem with the same item total and cost. The reverse inequality follows from the relaxation.

Bracketing the maximizing price

Item totals of minimizers of $H_c(\alpha)$ are monotone in $\alpha$. Indeed, if $x_1,x_2$ are minimizing item totals at $\alpha_1<\alpha_2$, adding the two optimality inequalities gives
$$ (\alpha_2-\alpha_1)(x_2-x_1)\ge0. $$

Since $\alpha_0\in[a,b]$, choose minimizers at $a,b$ with item totals
$$ x_-\le q_I\le x_+. $$
At an endpoint equal to $\alpha_0$, choose the original relaxation optimizer. The even-price construction gives mixed minimizers of $H(a),H(b)$ with these same totals.

Using those allocations as competitors,
$$ H(\alpha)+\alpha q_I \le H(a)+a q_I+(\alpha-a)(q_I-x_-) \quad\text{if }\alpha<a, $$
and
$$ H(\alpha)+\alpha q_I \le H(b)+b q_I+(\alpha-b)(q_I-x_+) \quad\text{if }\alpha>b. $$
The final terms are nonpositive. Consequently,
$$ F_*=\max_{\substack{2n\le\alpha\le2n+2\\\beta\ge0}} D(\alpha,\beta). $$

The integer $n$ is used to prove a uniform algebraic bound; the decision algorithm need not compute it.

5. Explicit bundle dual costs

We now calculate $g(\alpha,\beta)$ on this interval.

Put $r=\beta/2$. Minimization over $d$ at fixed $k$ gives
$$ g_k= \begin{cases} -r^2+(\beta-\alpha)k,&k\le r,\\ k^2-\alpha k,&k\ge r. \end{cases} $$

If $\beta\ge\alpha$, writing $u=k+d$ gives
$$ u^2-\beta u+(\beta-\alpha)k\ge-\beta^2/4, $$
attained at $k=0$, $d=\beta/2$.

Suppose $\beta<\alpha$ and $2n\le\alpha\le2n+2$. Only $k=n,n+1$ can minimize.

For $k\ge n+2$, both $k$ and $n+1$ exceed $r$, and
$$ g_k-g_{n+1} =(k-n-1)(k+n+1-\alpha)>0. $$
For $k<n$, if $r\ge n$, the first expression is strictly decreasing in $k$, so $g_k>g_n$. If $r<n$ and $k\ge r$, then
$$ g_k-g_n=(n-k)(\alpha-n-k)\ge(n-k)^2>0. $$
If $k<r<n$, then
$$ g_k-g_n =(n-k)(\alpha-n-k)-(r-k)^2 \ge(n-k)^2-(r-k)^2>0. $$

Comparing $g_n$ and $g_{n+1}$ now gives the following formulas.

For $2n\le\alpha\le2n+1$,
$$ g(\alpha,\beta)= \begin{cases} n^2-n\alpha,&0\le\beta\le2n,\\ -\beta^2/4+n\beta-n\alpha,&2n\le\beta\le\alpha,\\ -\beta^2/4,&\beta\ge\alpha. \end{cases} $$

For $2n+1\le\alpha\le2n+2$, write
$$ \alpha=2n+1+s^2,\qquad 0\le s\le1,\qquad h(s)=2n+2s. $$
Since $\alpha-h(s)=(1-s)^2\ge0$,
$$ g(\alpha,\beta)= \begin{cases} (n+1)^2-(n+1)\alpha,&0\le\beta\le h(s),\\ -\beta^2/4+n\beta-n\alpha,&h(s)\le\beta\le\alpha,\\ -\beta^2/4,&\beta\ge\alpha. \end{cases} $$
For the comparison underlying the middle boundary,
$$ g_n-g_{n+1}=s^2-(\beta/2-n)^2. $$
All formulas agree on their shared boundaries.

A bounded price box

Let $M=\max_j\beta_j$. Some supported type values a category with price $M$, so its bundle dual cost is at most $-M^2/4$. All other bundle dual costs are at most zero. Thus, throughout the restricted item-price interval,
$$ D(\alpha,\beta) \le (2n+2)q_I+\left(\sum_jq_j\right)M-\mu_{\min}M^2/4. $$

Set
$$ R=\frac{8\bigl((2n+2)q_I+\sum_jq_j+1\bigr)}{\mu_{\min}}. $$
The displayed upper bound is negative for $M\ge R$: at $R$ the negative quadratic term dominates, and the bound is decreasing thereafter. Since $F_*\ge0$, a maximizing dual point lies in
$$ 0\le\beta_j\le R. $$

We therefore have a compact dual domain.

6. Why the optimal value has degree at most three

Choose a total ordering of the category prices. On its closed ordering region, every $\beta_t$ is either zero or a specified coordinate $\beta_j$. Further specify which of the three displayed branches applies to each item-valuing type. There are finitely many resulting closed regions, and they cover the dual domain.

In the lower item-price interval, each region is a rational polytope in $(\alpha,\beta)$, and the dual objective is a concave rational quadratic. Its maximum is rational; the linear-system argument below, with no parameter, proves this and bounds its bit length.

In the upper interval, each region has the form
$$ 0\le s\le1,\qquad A\beta\le b(s), $$
where $A$ is constant rational and every coordinate of $b(s)$ is a polynomial of degree at most two. The bounds $\beta_j\in[0,R]$ are included. Its objective has the form
$$ c_0+c_2s^2+d^{\mathsf T}\beta-\frac12\beta^{\mathsf T}C\beta, $$
where $C$ is a constant rational positive semidefinite matrix. This form follows directly from the branch formulas: the only quadratic price terms are negative multiples of $\beta_j^2$, and the dependence on $\alpha$ is linear.

The following lemma handles degeneracies.

Parameterized quadratic maximization lemma

Suppose a compact maximization problem has the form
$$ \max_{\substack{0\le s\le1\\Ax\le b(s)}} \left(c(s)+d^{\mathsf T}x-\frac12x^{\mathsf T}Cx\right), $$
where $C$ is constant rational positive semidefinite, $A,d$ are constant rational, and $b,c$ have rational coefficients and degree at most two. Then its optimal value is algebraic of degree at most three. Its defining polynomial has coefficient lengths bounded polynomially in the dimensions and the input coefficient lengths.

Proof. Choose a maximizing parameter $s_*$ and, among the maximizers at $s_*$, choose a vertex $x_*$ of their optimal set.

That optimal set is a polytope. Indeed, if two feasible points maximize the concave quadratic, the quadratic’s value along their segment forces their difference to lie in $\ker C$. Their quadratic and linear objective values then agree individually. The optimal set is consequently the feasible polytope intersected with affine equations.

At $x_*$, quadratic-program optimality gives
$$ Cx_*-d+A_{\mathrm{act}}^{\mathsf T}\lambda=0, \qquad \lambda\ge0, $$
using active constraint rows. This is the polyhedral normal-cone characterization; it requires no strict feasibility assumption.

Moreover,
$$ \ker C\cap\ker A_{\mathrm{act}}=\{0\}. $$
Otherwise a nonzero vector in this intersection permits sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions. Stationarity makes their linear objective changes zero, and membership in $\ker C$ makes their quadratic changes zero. This contradicts the chosen vertex.

A nonnegative conic combination of active row vectors can be reduced to one using linearly independent rows: eliminate dependencies while maintaining nonnegative coefficients. Extend these rows to a basis $A_I$ of the active row space, assigning zero multipliers to added rows. Then
$$ Cx_*-d+A_I^{\mathsf T}\lambda_I=0,\qquad A_Ix_*=b_I(s_*), $$
with $\lambda_I\ge0$.

The matrix
$$ \begin{pmatrix} C&A_I^{\mathsf T}\\ A_I&0 \end{pmatrix} $$
is nonsingular. For a vector in its kernel, multiplication of the first equation by $x^{\mathsf T}$ gives $x^{\mathsf T}Cx=0$, hence $Cx=0$. The kernel-intersection property gives $x=0$, and row independence then gives $\lambda=0$.

Solving this same system symbolically defines rational polynomials $x(s),\lambda_I(s)$ of degree at most two. Define
$$ S_I=\{s\in[0,1]:Ax(s)\le b(s),\ \lambda_I(s)\ge0\}. $$
It contains $s_*$. For every $s\in S_I$, the equations and nonnegative multipliers certify quadratic-program optimality at that parameter.

Substitution gives a rational polynomial
$$ W(s)=c(s)+d^{\mathsf T}x(s)-\frac12x(s)^{\mathsf T}Cx(s) $$
of degree at most four. Its maximum over $S_I$ is the original global optimum.

The set $S_I$ is defined by univariate polynomial inequalities of degree at most two. It is a finite union of closed intervals and isolated points. Every boundary point is either $0$, $1$, or a root of one of its nonzero defining polynomials.

If $W$ is nonconstant, a maximum occurs at such a boundary point or at a root of $W'$, which has degree at most three. If $W$ is constant, choose a boundary point of the nonempty compact set $S_I$. Thus some maximizing parameter has algebraic degree at most three. Its value under $W$ has no larger algebraic degree.

Cramer’s rule bounds the coefficient lengths of $x(s)$ and $\lambda_I(s)$ polynomially. The defining inequalities of $S_I$ and the coefficients of $W$ inherit polynomial bounds. A polynomial for the optimal value is obtained by eliminating $s$ between its degree-at-most-three equation and $X-W(s)=0$. Their resultant uses a matrix of size at most seven, so its coefficient lengths are also polynomially bounded. $\square$

The lemma applies to a region containing a global dual maximizer. Therefore
$$ [\mathbb Q(F_*):\mathbb Q]\le3. $$
The possibly exponential number of regions does not affect the degree or the uniform coefficient bound. We will not enumerate them.

7. A uniform coefficient bound and a rational-threshold gap

For completeness, we give a coarse explicit bound sufficient for the algorithm. Set
$$ S=m+\tau+L+2,\qquad H=10^6S^4. $$
The primitive minimal polynomial of $F_*$ has degree at most three and every coefficient has absolute value at most $2^H$.

Here are bounds verifying this assertion.

Binary encoding gives
$$ q_r\le2^L,\qquad \mu_{\min}\ge2^{-L}, \qquad Q\le m2^L,\qquad n\le m2^{2L}. $$
Let $D_0$ be the product of the input rational denominators, and let $p_{\min}$ be the numerator of the listed rational $\mu_{\min}$. Then
$$ E=4D_0p_{\min} $$
is a common denominator for all coefficients in the dual regions, including $R$.

After multiplication by $E$, those coefficients are integers with at most $20S$ bits. This follows from the bounds just displayed: the coefficients consist of supplies, sums of masses, integer multiples involving $n$ or $n^2$, and the bound $R$.

The linear systems in the lemma, or in the lower-interval quadratic problem, have size at most $2m$. Clearing their denominators using $E$ gives integer systems whose entry lengths are at most $20S$. Hadamard’s determinant bound gives at most $50S^2$ bits for each determinant used by Cramer’s rule.

All coordinates of the resulting polynomial solutions share the same determinant denominator. Consequently:

  • The substituted quartic objective can be written with integer numerator coefficients and a common integer denominator of at most $200S^2$ bits.
  • Cleared defining polynomials for feasible parameter boundaries have at most $100S^2$ bits.
  • The derivative of that quartic, after clearing its denominator, has coefficient lengths at most $210S^2$.

The resultant of the degree-at-most-three parameter equation and the quartic value equation has size at most seven. Its coefficients have at most $2000S^2$ bits, and its degree in the value variable is at most three. It is nonzero: the coefficient of its highest value power contains a nonzero power of the quartic’s denominator.

Passing to a primitive irreducible factor retains a polynomial bound. $$ |F_*-B| \ge2^{-(H+20S+3L+3)} \quad\text{whenever }F_*\ne B. $$

In particular, with
$$ p=H+30S+10,\qquad \delta=2^{-p}, $$
we have the uniform implication
$$ F_*\ne B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |F_*-B|\ge\delta. $$
This bound is computable directly from $m,\tau,L$; no optimal polynomial or dual region needs to be found.

and use Section 2 to obtain a rational $U$ with
