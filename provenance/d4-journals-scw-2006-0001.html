The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite-support distributions of ideal-point types λ ∈ [0,1] with rational masses for EP and CM, simple majority in EP, a quota θ in CM, and \(q\) uniform on \([0,1]\), compute each chamber’s exact expected marginal pass-through. For each \(q\), use the direction-appropriate pivotal quantiles \(p_h(q)\); if both pivots favor change on the same side of \(q\), set \(x^*(q)\) to the pivot nearer \(q\), and otherwise set \(x^*(q)=q\), as in Proposition 1. Return \(ξ_h=∫_0^1 ∂x^*(q)/∂p_h(q)\,dq\) for each chamber.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Each chamber \(h\in\{\mathrm{EP},\mathrm{CM}\}\) is a society given by its support \(S_h\): a nonempty list of rational ideal-point types \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), each with positive binary-rational mass, summing to \(1\). Types have utility \(u_\lambda(x)=-|x-\lambda|\). Let \(\tau=|S_{\mathrm{EP}}|+|S_{\mathrm{CM}}|\) be the supplied support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total binary input length and the policy dimension is \(m=1\).
Write \(F_h(t)\) for mass at or below \(t\), and \(T_h(t)\) for mass at or above \(t\). EP uses strict simple majority; CM requires mass at least an input rational quota \(\theta\in(1/2,1]\).
The rightward and leftward pivots are \(r_{\mathrm{EP}}=\max\{t\in S_{\mathrm{EP}}:T_{\mathrm{EP}}(t)>1/2\}\), \(\ell_{\mathrm{EP}}=\min\{t\in S_{\mathrm{EP}}:F_{\mathrm{EP}}(t)>1/2\}\), \(r_{\mathrm{CM}}=\max\{t\in S_{\mathrm{CM}}:T_{\mathrm{CM}}(t)\ge\theta\}\), and \(\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}=\min\{t\in S_{\mathrm{CM}}:F_{\mathrm{CM}}(t)\ge\theta\}\).
For status quo \(q\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]\), set \(x^*(q)=\min\{r_{\mathrm{EP}},r_{\mathrm{CM}}\}\) if \(q<\min\{r_{\mathrm{EP}},r_{\mathrm{CM}}\}\); set \(x^*(q)=\max\{\ell_{\mathrm{EP}},\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}\}\) if \(q>\max\{\ell_{\mathrm{EP}},\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}\}\); otherwise set \(x^*(q)=q\).
During change, \(p_h(q)\) denotes the pivot for that direction. Define \(D_h(q)\) as the partial derivative of the outcome with respect to this pivot, holding the other pivot and \(q\) fixed; at coincident chamber pivots use the arithmetic mean of the two one-sided partial derivatives, applying the same outcome rule to local real perturbations. Set \(D_h(q)=0\) when there is no change; values at \(q\) equal to a pivot are immaterial.
The computational problem is to return the exact rational pair \(\xi_h=\int_0^1D_h(q)\,dq\).

Can this pair be computed in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is its exact computation NP-hard?

For finite chambers with linear distance utilities, the symmetric Nash bargain selects the ideal point nearer the status quo when both chambers favor change in the same direction. [Verified bargaining result](https://www.vwl4.uni-bayreuth.de/pool/Publikationen_Napel/2006_The-Inter-Institutional-Distribution_Council_vs_Parliament37.pdf).
That analysis also expresses expected marginal influence through directional order statistics under independently uniform individual ideal points; it provides no complexity classification for the mass-input problem. No classification for exactly this continuous problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Ideal-point coordinates are binary rationals, making the computational input finite; each chamber's masses are normalized to one.
  • The support parameter is the sum of the two supplied support sizes; m denotes the fixed policy dimension, and L includes coordinates, masses, and quota.
  • CM's quota lies strictly above one half and at most one, retaining qualified-majority voting and preventing conflicting directions of change.
  • Directional quantiles use strict majority for EP and an inclusive quota for CM, reproducing the discrete lower/upper parliamentary medians and council quota pivots.
  • The supplied societies are fixed mass profiles; only the status quo is random, rather than independently resampling individual ideal points.
  • Coincident chamber pivots receive the mean of the two one-sided partial derivatives, with local real perturbations also defining endpoint sensitivities; no-change situations receive zero sensitivity.

Result

The exact pair is polynomial-time computable for the specified problem with \(m=1\).

Define
\[ a=\min\{r_{\mathrm{EP}},r_{\mathrm{CM}}\}, \qquad b=\max\{\ell_{\mathrm{EP}},\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}\}. \]
For each chamber \(h\), let \(k\) denote the other chamber, and define
\[ c_h^R= \begin{cases} 1,&r_h<r_k,\\ \frac12,&r_h=r_k,\\ 0,&r_h>r_k, \end{cases} \qquad c_h^L= \begin{cases} 1,&\ell_h>\ell_k,\\ \frac12,&\ell_h=\ell_k,\\ 0,&\ell_h<\ell_k. \end{cases} \]
Then the exact answer is
\[ \boxed{\xi_h=a\,c_h^R+(1-b)\,c_h^L.} \]

The following proof derives this formula and verifies its bit complexity.

1. Existence and ordering of the pivots

All four pivots exist. In either chamber, the smallest supported ideal point has tail mass \(1\), and the largest supported ideal point has cumulative mass \(1\). These satisfy the respective chamber’s threshold.

We next prove \(r_h\leq\ell_h\) for both chambers.

Suppose \(r_{\mathrm{EP}}>\ell_{\mathrm{EP}}\). The sets of types at or below \(\ell_{\mathrm{EP}}\) and at or above \(r_{\mathrm{EP}}\) are disjoint. Consequently,
\[ F_{\mathrm{EP}}(\ell_{\mathrm{EP}}) + T_{\mathrm{EP}}(r_{\mathrm{EP}}) \leq 1. \]
But each summand is strictly greater than \(1/2\), a contradiction.

Similarly, if \(r_{\mathrm{CM}}>\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}\), disjointness gives
\[ F_{\mathrm{CM}}(\ell_{\mathrm{CM}}) + T_{\mathrm{CM}}(r_{\mathrm{CM}}) \leq 1, \]
whereas the pivot definitions give a sum at least \(2\theta>1\). This is again a contradiction.

Thus \(r_h\leq\ell_h\). In particular,
\[ 0\leq a\leq r_{\mathrm{EP}}\leq\ell_{\mathrm{EP}}\leq b\leq 1. \]
The outcome rule therefore divides the status-quo interval into rightward change on \(q<a\), no change on \(a\leq q\leq b\), and leftward change on \(q>b\).

2. Directional derivatives

Fix \(q<a\). The gap \(a-q\) is positive, so sufficiently small perturbations of either rightward pivot preserve the rightward-change condition. Locally the outcome is therefore
\[ x^*(q)=\min\{r_h,r_k\}. \]

If \(r_h<r_k\), sufficiently small perturbations preserve this strict inequality, and the outcome equals the perturbed \(r_h\). Hence \(D_h(q)=1\).

If \(r_h>r_k\), sufficiently small perturbations leave the outcome equal to \(r_k\). Hence \(D_h(q)=0\).

If \(r_h=r_k=s\), perturbing \(r_h\) by \(\varepsilon\) gives
\[ \min\{s+\varepsilon,s\}= \begin{cases} s+\varepsilon,&\varepsilon<0,\\ s,&\varepsilon>0. \end{cases} \]
The two one-sided partial derivatives are \(1\) and \(0\). Their prescribed arithmetic mean is \(1/2\). Thus, throughout the rightward-change interval, \(D_h(q)=c_h^R\).

Now fix \(q>b\). Sufficiently small perturbations of either leftward pivot preserve the leftward-change condition, and locally
\[ x^*(q)=\max\{\ell_h,\ell_k\}. \]
The same argument gives derivative \(1\) when \(\ell_h>\ell_k\), and \(0\) when \(\ell_h<\ell_k\). At equality \(\ell_h=\ell_k=s\),
\[ \max\{s+\varepsilon,s\}= \begin{cases} s,&\varepsilon<0,\\ s+\varepsilon,&\varepsilon>0, \end{cases} \]
so the prescribed mean is again \(1/2\). Hence \(D_h(q)=c_h^L\) throughout the leftward-change interval.

These calculations use local real perturbations, as stipulated, including when a pivot lies at an endpoint of the policy interval.

On the no-change interval, \(D_h(q)=0\) by definition. Values at the finitely many endpoints do not affect the integral.

3. Exact integration

Because the status quo is uniform on the unit interval, its density is \(1\). The derivative is constant on each change interval, whose lengths are \(a\) and \(1-b\). Therefore,
\[ \xi_h = \int_0^a c_h^R\,dq + \int_a^b 0\,dq + \int_b^1 c_h^L\,dq = a\,c_h^R+(1-b)\,c_h^L. \]
This also covers empty change intervals and coincident pivots.

As a consistency check, the coefficient definitions imply
\[ c_{\mathrm{EP}}^R+c_{\mathrm{CM}}^R=1, \qquad c_{\mathrm{EP}}^L+c_{\mathrm{CM}}^L=1. \]
Consequently,
\[ \xi_{\mathrm{EP}}+\xi_{\mathrm{CM}} = a+1-b = \Pr[x^*(q)\neq q]. \]

4. Algorithm and bit complexity

For each chamber, sort its supplied entries by ideal point and combine any duplicate ideal points by adding their masses. Then compute cumulative and tail masses.

The pivots are obtained by scanning these arrays:

  • For EP, select the smallest ideal point with cumulative mass strictly greater than \(1/2\), and the largest ideal point with tail mass strictly greater than \(1/2\).
  • For CM, use the same selections with the comparison “at least \(\theta\).”

Next compute \(a,b\), compare the corresponding chamber pivots to obtain the four coefficients, and evaluate the proved formula.

There are \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational comparisons for sorting and \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations and threshold comparisons afterward. These operations have polynomial bit cost, as follows.

Under standard binary encoding of rationals, write the supplied masses in a chamber as \(n_i/d_i\). A common denominator is
\[ D=\prod_i d_i. \]
Its bit length is at most a constant plus the sum of the denominator bit lengths, and therefore is \(O(L)\). Each mass can be represented over \(D\). Since masses are positive and sum to \(1\), every cumulative or tail mass has a numerator between \(0\) and \(D\), hence also has \(O(L)\) bits.

Coordinate comparisons and comparisons with the quota can be performed by integer cross multiplication, using integers with \(O(L)\) bits. Integer arithmetic on these numbers takes polynomial time.

Finally, each output uses at most two input coordinates and coefficients from \(0,1/2,1\). Its numerator and denominator therefore have \(O(L)\) bits; exact evaluation and reduction by the Euclidean algorithm take polynomial time.

Thus both the computation and the output size are polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Since this problem fixes \(m=1\), this establishes the requested polynomial bound in \(m,\tau,L\) on its stated domain. It does not assert a classification for higher-dimensional variants.
