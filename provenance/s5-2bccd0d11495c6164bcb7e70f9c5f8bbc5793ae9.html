The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, student course rankings and eligibility classes for each type, course priority tiers over types, and rational per-capita course and laminar class quotas, assign each unit of type mass to an acceptable course or leave it unmatched. Compare two allocations on the same population: student votes follow course rankings, while each course compares the mass assigned to it by pairing opposite differences within the most refined classes in priority order, with unpaired mass contributing the enrollment difference. Return a popular allocation with maximum matched mass, or report that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) courses, mutually acceptable type–course pairs, strict student rankings, and course priority tiers over acceptable types. Every acceptable course is preferred to being unmatched, denoted \(\bot\).
Each course \(h\) has an explicitly listed laminar family \(\mathcal C_h\) of nonempty subsets of its acceptable types, including the root containing all acceptable types. Classes have nonnegative binary-rational upper quotas \(q_{h,C}\); the root quota equals the positive course quota \(q_h\). There are no lower quotas. Write \(K=\sum_h|\mathcal C_h|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,a}\geq0\), where \(a\) is an acceptable course or \(\bot\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in C}x_{t,h}\leq q_{h,C}\). Its matched mass is \(\sum_{t,h}x_{t,h}\).
To compare a challenger \(y\) with \(x\) on the same atomless population, specify joint masses \(z_{t,a,b}\geq0\) having marginals \(\sum_bz_{t,a,b}=x_{t,a}\) and \(\sum_az_{t,a,b}=y_{t,b}\). Let \(v_t(b,a)\) be the student's signed preference vote; its total contribution is \(\sum_{t,a,b}z_{t,a,b}v_t(b,a)\).
For course \(h\), cancel the common mass \(z_{t,h,h}\). The old and new residual masses of type \(t\) are respectively \(x_{t,h}-z_{t,h,h}\) and \(y_{t,h}-z_{t,h,h}\). Process classes from descendants to ancestors; within each class pair the remaining old and new mass in priority order, repeatedly pairing the queue heads by the smaller remaining mass. Use input type order within a priority tier and input class order among incomparable classes.
A paired amount \(d\) of new type \(t\) and old type \(s\) contributes \(d\,v_h(t,s)\), where \(v_h\) is positive for better priority, negative for worse priority, and zero within a tier. Add the enrollment difference \(\sum_t(y_{t,h}-x_{t,h})\) to obtain the course's vote. Let \(\Delta(y,x;z)\) be the sum of student and course votes.
An allocation \(x\) is popular if \(\Delta(y,x;z)\leq0\) for every feasible real allocation \(y\) and every joint assignment \(z\) with these marginals.

Is computing a popular allocation of maximum matched mass, or reporting that none exists, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite residents with strict preferences and laminar upper quotas, a maximum-cardinality popular matching under this class-based correspondence rule is computable in \(O(en)\) time, where \(e\) counts acceptable pairs and \(n\) counts residents and hospitals. [Nasre–Rawat](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.07650v3)
Popular mixed matchings under one-sided preferences and expected voting always exist and are polynomial-time computable; that model differs from the same-population correspondence voting defined here. [Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://cse.iitm.ac.in/~meghana/papers/TCS11-2.pdf) No result settling this precise mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the population atomless and allowed real allocation masses, with positive rational type masses normalized to one.
  • Specified mutual acceptability, strict student rankings, and unmatched status below every acceptable course.
  • Included each course's full acceptable-type set as a root class with quota equal to its positive course capacity; retained only upper quotas and allowed zero class quotas.

NP-hardness of the mass problem

The problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard. In fact, deciding whether any popular allocation exists is already $\mathrm{NP}$-hard when every course has only its root class.

The main issue in transferring finite hardness is that real allocations might admit popular solutions even when no finite popular matching exists. The proof below excludes that possibility for the constructed instances.

Throughout, signed votes have values $+1$, $0$, and $-1$.

1. The finite hardness theorem used

We use the following established result of Cseh–Huang–Kavitha:

Given a bipartite graph $G=(A\cup B,E)$, with strict preferences at every vertex of $A$ and possibly tied preferences at vertices of $B$, deciding whether a popular matching exists is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete. This remains true when each vertex of $B$ either has strict preferences or places all its neighbors in a single tie.

Here every vertex prefers an acceptable partner to being unmatched, and popularity compares the total signed votes of all vertices against every matching. This is [Theorem 2 of *Popular matchings with two-sided preferences and one-sided ties*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.07168).

The course priority tiers permitted in the present task satisfy exactly these preference hypotheses. We will prove that real masses introduce no additional popular solutions in our reduction.

2. Popularity passes to components of a feasible convex decomposition

Let $\mathcal X$ denote the feasible-allocation polytope of any instance in the task.

Lemma 1. Suppose $u,v\in\mathcal X$, $0<\alpha\leq1$, and
$$ x=\alpha u+(1-\alpha)v. $$
If $x$ is popular, then $u$ is popular.

Proof. We prove the contrapositive. Suppose a feasible allocation $y$ and a joint assignment $z$ defeat $u$:
$$ \Delta(y,u;z)>0. $$
Define
$$ \widehat y=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)v $$
and, for every type $t$ and outcomes $a,b$, define
$$ \widehat z_{t,a,b} = \alpha z_{t,a,b} + (1-\alpha)v_{t,a}\mathbf 1_{\{a=b\}}. $$
Convexity of $\mathcal X$ makes $\widehat y$ feasible. The old and new marginals of $\widehat z$ are respectively $x$ and $\widehat y$.

The added diagonal mass contributes zero student vote. Hence the student vote is multiplied by $\alpha$.

At any course $h$, cancellation leaves old residual mass
$$ x_{t,h}-\widehat z_{t,h,h} = \alpha\bigl(u_{t,h}-z_{t,h,h}\bigr) $$
and new residual mass
$$ \widehat y_{t,h}-\widehat z_{t,h,h} = \alpha\bigl(y_{t,h}-z_{t,h,h}\bigr). $$
Thus both residual vectors are precisely $\alpha$ times those in the challenge against $u$.

The correspondence algorithm is positively homogeneous. Indeed, multiplying all queue masses by $\alpha>0$ preserves their prescribed ordering and multiplies each head-pairing amount by $\alpha$, because
$$ \min\{\alpha r,\alpha s\}=\alpha\min\{r,s\}. $$
Induction through the pairings and then through the prescribed class order shows that every paired amount and every remaining residual amount is multiplied by $\alpha$. This also respects the fixed orders within priority tiers and among incomparable classes.

Consequently, every priority vote is multiplied by $\alpha$. The enrollment difference is likewise multiplied by $\alpha$. Therefore
$$ \Delta(\widehat y,x;\widehat z) = \alpha\Delta(y,u;z)>0. $$
This defeats $x$, proving the lemma. $\square$

In particular, if a popular allocation is expressed as a finite convex combination of feasible allocations, every component with positive coefficient must be popular. This statement does not assert that mixtures of popular allocations are popular.

3. Construction of the mass instance

Take an instance $G=(A\cup B,E)$ of the finite hardness theorem, and write $n=|A|\geq1$.

Delete isolated vertices of $B$. Such vertices are unmatched in every matching and always contribute zero votes, so deletion preserves existence of a popular matching. Every remaining course will therefore have a nonempty root class.

Let
$$ N=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}, \qquad \delta=\frac1N. $$
Construct the society as follows.

  • For every applicant $t\in A$, create a type of mass $\mu_t=\delta$.
  • If $n<N$, add one type of mass $(N-n)\delta$ that accepts no course.
  • For every post $h\in B$, create a course with quota $q_h=\delta$.
  • Copy acceptable pairs and applicant preferences from $G$.
  • Copy each post’s preferences as the corresponding course’s priority tiers.
  • Give each course only its root class, containing all its acceptable types, with root quota $\delta$.

Every acceptable course is ranked above $\bot$. The additional type, when present, is necessarily unmatched.

The masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to
$$ n\delta+(N-n)\delta=1. $$
All quotas are positive dyadic rationals. Each class family is laminar because it contains only its root.

The support size satisfies $\tau\leq n+1$, and $K=m=|B|$. All numerical entries have $O(\log n)$ bits, and all lists are copied from $G$. Thus the construction has polynomial input length.

For a finite matching $M$ in $G$, let $x^M$ assign the entire mass $\delta$ of each original type to its partner in $M$, or to $\bot$ if unmatched. The additional type remains unmatched.

4. Every feasible allocation decomposes into finite matchings

For an arbitrary feasible allocation $x$, define
$$ p_{t,h}=\frac{x_{t,h}}{\delta} \qquad ((t,h)\in E). $$
The feasible course coordinates correspond exactly to the polytope
$$ Q= \left\{ p\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}: \sum_{h:(t,h)\in E}p_{t,h}\leq1\ \text{for every }t\in A,\quad \sum_{t:(t,h)\in E}p_{t,h}\leq1\ \text{for every }h\in B \right\}. $$
The unmatched coordinates are then determined by
$$ x_{t,\bot} = \delta\left(1-\sum_h p_{t,h}\right). $$
The additional type’s allocation is fixed. Thus this correspondence between $Q$ and $\mathcal X$ is an affine bijection.

Lemma 2. Every vertex of $Q$ is the incidence vector of a matching.

Proof. Every coordinate of a feasible $p$ lies in $[0,1]$. Suppose some coordinate lies strictly between $0$ and $1$, and let $H$ be the bipartite graph consisting of those fractional edges.

If $H$ contains a cycle, the cycle has even length. Alternately increasing and decreasing its coordinates by a sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$ preserves every incident vertex sum. Both choices of sign remain feasible and give distinct points whose midpoint is $p$. Hence $p$ is not a vertex.

Otherwise $H$ is a forest. Choose a component containing an edge and a path between two of its leaves. A leaf has exactly one incident fractional edge. It cannot have an incident edge of value $1$, since that would violate its capacity constraint. Thus its total incident mass is strictly less than $1$.

Alternately increasing and decreasing coordinates along the chosen path preserves the sums at its internal vertices. At its endpoints there is positive capacity slack. Choosing $\varepsilon$ smaller than the endpoint slacks and all affected fractional coordinates ensures that both perturbations are feasible. Again, $p$ is the midpoint of two distinct feasible points.

Therefore every vertex has only $0$ and $1$ coordinates. The vertex-sum constraints then make its positive edges a matching. $\square$

The polytope $Q$ is nonempty and bounded, so the standard finite-dimensional polytope theorem expresses every point as a convex combination of its vertices. Consequently, every feasible real allocation has a finite decomposition
$$ x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_i x^{M_i}, \qquad \lambda_i>0, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_i=1, $$
where each $M_i$ is a finite matching in $G$.

5. Popularity of a matching survives all real challengers

For finite matchings $M$ and $P$, let
$$ D(P,M) = \sum_{t\in A}v_t(P(t),M(t)) + \sum_{h\in B}v_h(P(h),M(h)), $$
where an unmatched partner is denoted by $\bot$, and every acceptable partner is preferred to $\bot$.

We now establish the precise voting correspondence.

Lemma 3. A finite matching $M$ is popular in $G$ if and only if $x^M$ is popular against all feasible real allocations and all joint assignments in the constructed society.

Proof. Fix $x^M$, and let $y$ be any feasible real challenger.

Each original type has only one old outcome, namely $M(t)$. Therefore its joint assignment is forced:
$$ z_{t,M(t),b}=y_{t,b}, $$
with all other old rows zero. The additional type is fixed at $\bot$ and contributes no vote.

The student contribution is consequently
$$ \sum_{t\in A}\sum_b y_{t,b}v_t(b,M(t)), $$
which is linear in $y$.

Write
$$ \nu_h(y)=\sum_t y_{t,h}. $$

If $h$ is unmatched in $M$, it has no old residual mass. Its priority contribution is zero, and its total vote is
$$ V_h(y)=\nu_h(y). $$

If $h$ is matched in $M$ to type $s$, cancellation removes $y_{s,h}$. The remaining old mass is
$$ \delta-y_{s,h}, $$
all of type $s$. The new residual consists of masses $y_{t,h}$ for $t\neq s$. Feasibility gives
$$ \sum_{t\neq s}y_{t,h} = \nu_h(y)-y_{s,h} \leq \delta-y_{s,h}. $$
Hence the root correspondence pairs every new residual amount against old type $s$, regardless of its queue position. The course vote is exactly
$$ V_h(y) = \nu_h(y)-\delta + \sum_{t\neq s}y_{t,h}v_h(t,s). $$

Thus the total vote against $x^M$ is an affine function of $y$.

For a pure challenger $x^P$, these formulas give
$$ \Delta(x^P,x^M;z)=\delta D(P,M). $$
Indeed, a course matched in both matchings contributes $\delta$ times its partner-comparison vote; a course newly matched contributes $+\delta$; and a course becoming unmatched contributes $-\delta$. Student votes are also scaled by $\delta$.

By Lemma 2, write an arbitrary real challenger as
$$ y=\sum_i\lambda_i x^{P_i}. $$
Affineness and $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$ imply
$$ \Delta(y,x^M;z) = \delta\sum_i\lambda_i D(P_i,M). $$
If $M$ is popular, every summand is nonpositive, so no real challenger defeats $x^M$.

Conversely, if $M$ is not popular, some finite matching $P$ has $D(P,M)>0$, and $x^P$ defeats $x^M$. $\square$

6. Equivalence of existence and of optimal matched mass

We have proved
$$ G\text{ has a popular matching} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed society has a popular real allocation}. $$

The forward implication is Lemma 3.

For the reverse implication, suppose $x$ is a popular real allocation. Decompose it as
$$ x=\sum_i\lambda_i x^{M_i}. $$
For any component with $\lambda_i<1$, the normalized combination of the other components is a feasible allocation, so Lemma 1 implies that $x^{M_i}$ is popular. If $\lambda_i=1$, this is immediate. Lemma 3 then implies that $M_i$ is a popular finite matching.

The same argument identifies the optimum matched mass. Every component of a popular allocation’s decomposition is popular, and
$$ \operatorname{mass}(x) = \delta\sum_i\lambda_i|M_i|. $$
If $k$ is the maximum cardinality of a popular finite matching, this quantity is at most $\delta k$. Conversely, a popular matching of size $k$ yields a popular allocation of matched mass $\delta k$. Therefore
$$ \max_{x\text{ popular}}\operatorname{mass}(x) = \delta\max_{M\text{ popular}}|M| $$
whenever either side has a feasible popular solution.

The construction is thus a polynomial-time many-one reduction from the cited $\mathrm{NP}$-complete existence problem to existence of a popular real allocation. Any algorithm computing a maximum-matched-mass popular allocation, or correctly reporting nonexistence, would decide that finite problem.

Therefore the requested computational problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, already with root-only class families. The reduction uses course ties allowed by the task; the finite strict-preference algorithm does not cover these instances.
