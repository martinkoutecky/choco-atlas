The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given binary issues, a target outcome \(p\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete unconditional approval-ballot types, and a rational deletion budget \(\beta\), is there a vector of deletion masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\) such that \(p\) uniquely minimizes the remaining population’s total dissatisfaction?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge 1\) binary issues with outcome space \(\{0,1\}^m\). A complete unconditional ballot type \(t\) specifies an approval set \(A_{tj}\subseteq\{0,1\}\) for every issue \(j\); empty sets and approval of both values are allowed. Its dissatisfaction at outcome \(s\) is \(\delta_t(s)=|\{j:s_j\notin A_{tj}\}|\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) distinct types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
The input also includes a target outcome \(p\in\{0,1\}^m\) and a binary rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\). Deletion may remove any real mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type, at unit cost per mass, with \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\). The remaining dissatisfaction is \(D_x(s)=\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)\delta_t(s)\). Deletion succeeds exactly when \(D_x(p)<D_x(s)\) for every \(s\ne p\); ties do not count as success.

Is deciding whether a successful deletion exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite electorates, making a prescribed outcome the unique Unconditional Minisum winner by deleting at most a given number of voters is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with binary issues. [Markakis and Papasotiropoulos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.01660v3)
No result settling this exact divisible-mass deletion problem was found in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The mass normalization was unspecified; initial masses sum to one, and remaining masses are used directly without renormalization.
  • The budget domain was unspecified; use a nonnegative binary rational budget between zero and one, since larger budgets permit the same deletions as one.
  • Complete ballots specify an approval set on every issue; empty sets and sets containing both binary values are allowed, as in the discrete definition.
  • Deletion masses range over real numbers, with unit cost per deleted mass and no indivisibility restriction.
  • Use at least one binary issue and positive initial support; deleting the entire society is permitted but fails the strict uniqueness condition.

Result

The divisible-mass deletion problem is solvable in polynomial time, with running time \((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations. A successful deletion, when one exists, can also be computed with rational coordinates of polynomial bit length.

The proof reduces uniqueness to one strict inequality per issue, then handles strictness by maximizing a common margin in a rational linear program.

1. Uniqueness is equivalent to positive margins on every issue

Write \(y_t=\mu_t-x_t\) for the retained mass of type \(t\). The deletion bounds and budget are equivalent to

\[ 0\le y_t\le\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}y_t\ge 1-\beta. \]

For each issue \(j\) and value \(b\in\{0,1\}\), define its remaining dissatisfaction contribution by

\[ C_j(b;y)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}y_t\,\mathbf 1[b\notin A_{tj}]. \]

Unconditionality gives the decomposition

\[ D_x(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}C_j(s_j;y). \]

Define coefficients and margins by

\[ a_{jt} =\mathbf 1[1-p_j\notin A_{tj}] -\mathbf 1[p_j\notin A_{tj}] \in\{-1,0,1\}, \qquad g_j(y)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_{jt}y_t. \]

Thus \(g_j(y)=C_j(1-p_j;y)-C_j(p_j;y)\). Empty approval sets and approval of both values yield \(a_{jt}=0\), so both are covered.

Lemma. The target \(p\) is the unique minimum-dissatisfaction outcome exactly when \(g_j(y)>0\) for every issue \(j\).

Proof. Suppose \(p\) is uniquely optimal. The outcome obtained by flipping only issue \(j\) has dissatisfaction difference \(g_j(y)\) from \(p\). Uniqueness therefore implies \(g_j(y)>0\).

Conversely, suppose all these margins are positive. For any \(s\ne p\), let \(J(s)=\{j:s_j\ne p_j\}\). Binary domains imply \(s_j=1-p_j\) whenever \(j\in J(s)\). Consequently,

\[ D_x(s)-D_x(p)=\sum_{j\in J(s)}g_j(y)>0, \]

because \(J(s)\) is nonempty. This proves uniqueness. \(\square\)

2. A linear program handles all strict inequalities

Consider the following linear program in the retained masses and one additional variable \(z\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize}\quad &z\\ \text{subject to}\quad &0\le y_t\le\mu_t &&(t=1,\ldots,\tau),\\ &\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}y_t\ge 1-\beta,\\ &\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_{jt}y_t\ge z &&(j=1,\ldots,m),\\ &-1\le z\le1. \end{aligned} \]

Let its optimum be \(z^\star\).

The feasible region is nonempty: take \(y_t=\mu_t\) and \(z=-1\). Indeed,

\[ g_j(\mu)\ge-\sum_t\mu_t=-1. \]

It is also closed and bounded, so its optimum is attained.

Proposition. Successful deletion exists exactly when \(z^\star>0\).

Proof. A successful deletion gives feasible retained masses with every margin positive. Since \(m\) is finite,

\[ \varepsilon=\min_{1\le j\le m}g_j(y)>0. \]

Moreover, \(|g_j(y)|\le\sum_ty_t\le1\). Hence \((y,\varepsilon)\) is feasible in the linear program, and \(z^\star\ge\varepsilon>0\).

Conversely, if \(z^\star>0\), an optimal solution satisfies \(g_j(y)\ge z^\star>0\) for every issue. The lemma establishes uniqueness, and \(x_t=\mu_t-y_t\) satisfies all deletion bounds and the budget. \(\square\)

In particular, an optimum of zero means failure: ties are not accepted.

3. Exactness and bit complexity

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a linear program with binary-encoded rational data can be solved exactly in time polynomial in its encoding length, including deciding feasibility and computing an attained finite optimum. See [the INRIA lecture notes, Chapter 10](https://www-sop.inria.fr/members/Frederic.Giroire/teaching/ubinet/pdfs/ubinet.pdf).

Here the program has \(\tau+1\) variables and \(m+2\tau+3\) inequalities. Its constraint matrix has entries only in \(\{-1,0,1\}\); its right-hand sides are the input masses, \(1-\beta\), zero, and unit constants. Constructing the margin coefficients takes \(O(m\tau)\) ballot inspections. The resulting encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The linear-programming theorem therefore applies with all its hypotheses satisfied.

For completeness, the following bound shows explicitly why arbitrarily small strict margins do not require unbounded precision.

Let \(n=\tau+1\), and let \(Q\) be the product of the positive denominators appearing in the input masses and budget. Then \(Q\) has \(O(L)\) bits, and multiplying every right-hand side by \(Q\) makes it integral.

A linear objective on a nonempty bounded polyhedron has an optimal vertex. At such a vertex, choose \(n\) linearly independent tight constraints, giving

\[ Bv=b, \]

where \(B\) is an invertible integer matrix with entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and \(Qb\) is integral. Cramer’s rule shows that every coordinate of \(v\) is an integer divided by \(Q\det B\).

Each row of \(B\) has Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt n\). Hadamard’s determinant inequality therefore gives

\[ |\det B|\le n^{n/2}. \]

Consequently, if \(z^\star>0\), then

\[ z^\star\ge \frac{1}{Q|\det B|} \ge \frac{1}{Qn^n}. \]

The final bound has \(O(L+\tau\log(\tau+1))\) bits. Thus strict positivity can alternatively be decided by adding the rational constraint \(z\ge1/(Qn^n)\) and testing ordinary non-strict feasibility. This also proves that a rational successful deletion of polynomial bit length exists whenever any real successful deletion exists.

The total decision time is therefore \((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

4. Relation to finite-electorate hardness

The cited finite-electorate result concerns deleting whole voters; its binary unconditional hardness appears as Theorem 8 of [Markakis and Papasotiropoulos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.01660v3). Whole-voter deletion imposes integrality restrictions on deletion counts. Those restrictions are absent here, allowing the linear program above to decide the exact problem.

The target question is therefore completely resolved for the stated divisible-mass model.
