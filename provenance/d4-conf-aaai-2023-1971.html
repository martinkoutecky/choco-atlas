The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given household types \(a\in[\tau]\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\) summing to \(1\), resource categories \(g\in[r]\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), rational utilities \(u_{ag}\) normalized so \(\max_g u_{ag}=1\), positive rational type weights \(w_a\), and a smoothing parameter \(\omega\in(0,1]\), each household must receive an integral bundle \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^r\). Choose masses \(x_{a,k}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_k x_{a,k}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_{a,k} k_gx_{a,k}\le q_g\) for each category. With \(U_a(k)=\sum_g u_{ag}k_g\) and \(W=\sum_a\mu_aw_a\), maximize \(F_\omega(x)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{W}\sum_{a,k}x_{a,k}w_a\ln(U_a(k)+\omega)\right)\). Does there exist an algorithm returning a feasible \(x\) with value at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/O\!\left(e^\epsilon/(\omega\ln(1+1/\omega))\right)\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), the input bit length, \(1/\epsilon\), and \(1/\omega\)?

Resolution: the guarantee holds with \(C=1\)

There is a deterministic algorithm satisfying the requested guarantee. In fact, its running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\); dependence on \(\omega\) through its binary encoding suffices.

The proof uses a fractional relaxation followed by a distributional rounding procedure. The latter preserves each type’s expected consumption exactly and incurs a bounded loss in logarithmic welfare. A final conversion enforces binary-rational output masses.

1. A fractional upper bound

Define the normalized type weights
\( p_a=\mu_aw_a/W. \)
They are positive rational numbers and satisfy \(\sum_a p_a=1\).

Let \(z_{ag}\) denote the aggregate mass of category \(g\) assigned to type \(a\). Consider the compact rational polytope
\( \mathcal Z=\{z\ge0:\sum_a z_{ag}\le q_g\text{ for every }g\}. \)
For \(z\in\mathcal Z\), put
\( t_a(z)=\mu_a^{-1}\sum_g u_{ag}z_{ag}, \)
and define
\( J(z)=\sum_a p_a\ln(t_a(z)+\omega),\qquad V=\max_{z\in\mathcal Z}J(z). \)

Claim. \(\ln(\mathrm{OPT})\le V\).

Indeed, for any feasible bundle distribution \(x\), set
\( z_{ag}=\sum_k k_gx_{a,k}. \)
These quantities are finite and \(z\in\mathcal Z\). Under the probability distribution \(x_{a,k}/\mu_a\), the expected utility of type \(a\) is \(t_a(z)\). Concavity of the logarithm gives
\( \mu_a^{-1}\sum_k x_{a,k}\ln(U_a(k)+\omega) \le \ln(t_a(z)+\omega). \)
Multiplying by \(p_a\) and summing proves
\( \ln F_\omega(x)\le J(z)\le V. \)

This also applies to distributions of infinite support. Their expected utilities are finite, and
\( 0\le\ln(1+U_a(k)/\omega)\le U_a(k)/\omega, \)
so the logarithmic expectations are well defined and finite. Taking the supremum proves the claim.

2. Computing a rational fractional allocation

We first construct, in polynomial time, a rational \(\widehat z\in\mathcal Z\) satisfying
\( J(\widehat z)\ge V-\epsilon/2. \)

Write
\( Q=\sum_gq_g,\qquad \mu_{\min}=\min_a\mu_a. \)
If \(Q=0\), assigning every household the empty bundle is optimal, so assume \(Q>0\). Every feasible fractional utility satisfies
\( 0\le t_a(z)\le Q/\mu_{\min}. \)
Set
\( S=\omega+Q/\mu_{\min},\qquad \delta=\min\{1,\epsilon/4\}. \)

Construct the rational geometric grid
\( b_i=\omega(1+\delta)^i,\qquad 0\le i\le N, \)
where \(N\) is the smallest index with \(b_N\ge S\). Its size satisfies
\( N=O\!\left(1+\delta^{-1}\ln(S/\omega)\right). \)
Because \(S/\omega\) has polynomial binary encoding length, \(N\), and the encoding lengths of all grid points, are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\).

For each grid point, compute a rational number \(c_i\) with
\( \ln b_i-\delta\le c_i\le\ln b_i. \)
Define
\( \ell_i(s)=c_i+(s-b_i)/b_i,\qquad H(s)=\min_{0\le i\le N}\ell_i(s). \)

For every \(s\in[\omega,S]\),
\( \ln s-\delta\le H(s)\le\ln s+\delta. \)

For the lower bound, the tangent inequality gives
\( \ln b_i+(s-b_i)/b_i\ge\ln s, \)
and replacing \(\ln b_i\) by \(c_i\) decreases its value by at most \(\delta\).

For the upper bound, choose \(b_i\le s\le(1+\delta)b_i\). With \(r=s/b_i\),
\( \ell_i(s)-\ln s \le r-1-\ln r \le r-1 \le\delta. \)

Now solve the rational linear program maximizing \(\sum_a p_ah_a\), subject to
\( z\in\mathcal Z,\qquad h_a\le\ell_i(\omega+t_a(z)) \quad\text{for every }a,i, \)
and the additional lower bounds \(h_a\ge c_0-1\).

These lower bounds do not exclude any maximizing choice: since \(\delta\le1\),
\( H(\omega+t_a(z)) \ge\ln\omega-\delta \ge c_0-1. \)
The resulting feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded. Every coefficient is rational with polynomial encoding length. We apply the standard theorem that rational linear programs can be optimized exactly in polynomial time, returning a rational optimum of polynomial encoding length. See the discussion of rational LP complexity in [Adler and Beling](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf).

Because every \(p_a\) is positive, the LP optimizes
\( \widetilde J(z)=\sum_a p_aH(\omega+t_a(z)). \)
The pointwise estimates imply
\( J(z)-\delta\le\widetilde J(z)\le J(z)+\delta. \)
Consequently, its rational optimizer \(\widehat z\) satisfies
\( J(\widehat z)\ge V-2\delta\ge V-\epsilon/2. \)

For completeness, computing the \(c_i\) needs no exact logarithm oracle. For a positive rational \(b\), write \(b=2^kr\) with \(1\le r<2\). With \(v=(r-1)/(r+1)\), we have
\( \ln r=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad 0\le v<1/3. \)
The tail after \(n\) terms is at most
\( \frac{2v^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)(1-v^2)}. \)
The same series with \(v=1/3\) computes \(\ln2\). Combining certified rational intervals for \(\ln r\) and \(\ln2\) gives a lower approximation to \(\ln b=k\ln2+\ln r\) within \(\delta\), using polynomially many rational operations of polynomial bit length.

3. A polynomial-size rounding lemma

Lemma. Given rational vectors \(y\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\) and \(u\in\mathbb Q^m\cap[0,1]^m\), one can compute a rational probability distribution on at most \(2(m+1)\) integral bundles \(K\) such that
\( \mathbb E[K]=y \)
and
\( \mathbb E[\ln(u\cdot K+\omega)] \ge\ln(u\cdot y+\omega)-A_\omega, \)
where
\( s_\omega=\ln(1+1/\omega),\qquad A_\omega=\ln\!\left(\frac1{\omega s_\omega}\right)-1+\omega s_\omega. \)
The computation and output encoding lengths are polynomial in the input encoding length and \(m\).

3.1 Decomposition preserving utility

Write
\( y=h+f,\qquad h=\lfloor y\rfloor,\qquad f\in[0,1]^m. \)
Consider
\( P=\{v\in[0,1]^m:u\cdot v=u\cdot f\}. \)

Every vertex of \(P\) has at most one fractional coordinate. To prove this, if two coordinates \(i,j\) are strictly between their bounds, there is a nonzero direction supported on them with \(u\cdot d=0\). Small moves in both directions remain feasible, contradicting extremality. A single fractional coordinate with zero utility coefficient likewise permits a feasible move in both directions.

We next give a constructive decomposition
\( f=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_jv^{(j)},\qquad s\le m+1, \)
where the \(v^{(j)}\) are vertices of \(P\), the coefficients are nonnegative rationals, and \(\sum_j\lambda_j=1\).

Start with a residual point \(x=f\). Find a vertex \(v\) while preserving every coordinate already at a bound:

  • Set any fractional coordinate with zero utility coefficient to a bound.
  • If two fractional coordinates have positive coefficients, move in direction \((u_j,-u_i)\) on those coordinates until one reaches a bound.

Each move fixes another coordinate, so at most \(m\) moves are needed.

If \(x=v\), terminate. Otherwise choose the largest \(\lambda\) such that
\( x'=(x-\lambda v)/(1-\lambda)\in[0,1]^m. \)
Explicitly, \(\lambda\) is the minimum of
\( x_i/v_i\quad(v_i>0),\qquad (1-x_i)/(1-v_i)\quad(v_i<1). \)
Because \(v\) shares the coordinates of \(x\) already at bounds and \(x\ne v\), this minimum satisfies \(0<\lambda<1\). The residual \(x'\) lies in \(P\), preserves all previously fixed coordinates, and fixes at least one additional coordinate. Record the term \(\lambda v\) and continue with \(x'\), scaling subsequent coefficients by \(1-\lambda\). There are at most \(m\) peeling steps followed by one final vertex.

Here is also an explicit bit-complexity justification. A vertex with one fractional coordinate \(i\) has that coordinate equal to
\( \frac{u\cdot f-\sum_{g\ne i}u_gv_g}{u_i}, \)
with every other coordinate integral. Thus every vertex has a common denominator of polynomial bit length, uniformly over all steps.

If the current residual has common denominator \(D\), and the chosen vertex has common denominator \(E\), either active-ratio formula above gives a new residual with common denominator at most \(DE\), before reduction. For example, when \(\lambda=x_i/v_i\), writing \(x_j=X_j/D\) and \(v_j=V_j/E\) gives
\( x'_j=\frac{X_jV_i-X_iV_j}{DV_i-X_iE}. \)
The upper-bound case has the same denominator bound. Consequently, denominator bit lengths grow additively over at most \(m\) steps. The decomposition coefficients also have polynomial bit length.

3.2 Rounding the last fractional coordinate

At an integral vertex \(v\), use the single bundle \(h+v\).

At a vertex whose only fractional coordinate is \(i\), write \(v_i=p\). Round that coordinate to zero with probability \(1-p\), and to one with probability \(p\). This preserves the mean vector \(h+v\).

The resulting utilities are two endpoints \(U_0,U_0+d\), where
\( 0<d=u_i\le1,\qquad U_0+pd=u\cdot y. \)
Therefore the loss from this rounding is
\( \ln(U_0+pd+\omega) -(1-p)\ln(U_0+\omega) -p\ln(U_0+d+\omega). \)
Setting \(b=U_0+\omega\) and \(t=d/b\), this becomes
\( G_t(p)=\ln(1+pt)-p\ln(1+t). \)
Since \(b\ge\omega\) and \(d\le1\), we have \(0<t\le1/\omega\). Moreover,
\( \frac{\partial G_t(p)}{\partial t} =\frac{pt(1-p)}{(1+pt)(1+t)} \ge0. \)
Hence the loss is at most \(\max_{0\le p\le1}G_{1/\omega}(p)\).

For any \(t>0\), differentiation in \(p\) gives the maximizer
\( p^*=\frac1{\ln(1+t)}-\frac1t. \)
It lies strictly between zero and one because
\( t/(1+t)<\ln(1+t)<t. \)
Substitution yields
\( \max_{0\le p\le1}G_t(p) =\ln\!\left(\frac{t}{\ln(1+t)}\right)-1+\frac{\ln(1+t)}t. \)
At \(t=1/\omega\), this is exactly \(A_\omega\).

Every vertex has the same unrounded utility \(u\cdot y\). Mixing the rounded distributions according to the decomposition coefficients thus preserves \(\mathbb E[K]=y\) and proves the stated logarithmic bound. At most two bundles arise per vertex, completing the lemma.

4. Applying the rounding lemma

For each type \(a\), set
\( y_{ag}=\widehat z_{ag}/\mu_a. \)
Apply the lemma with utility vector \(u_a\), and multiply its probability masses by \(\mu_a\). Denote the resulting allocation by \(x^\circ\).

Its type masses are exactly \(\mu_a\). Its consumption satisfies
\( \sum_{a,k}k_gx^\circ_{a,k} =\sum_a\mu_a y_{ag} =\sum_a\widehat z_{ag} \le q_g. \)
Thus it is feasible, with at most \(2\tau(m+1)\) listed bundles.

Its normalized logarithmic welfare satisfies
\( \ln F_\omega(x^\circ) \ge J(\widehat z)-\sum_a p_aA_\omega =J(\widehat z)-A_\omega. \)
By Section 2,
\( \ln F_\omega(x^\circ)\ge V-\epsilon/2-A_\omega. \)

This construction is deterministic: the distribution itself is the output allocation; no random sampling is performed.

5. Enforcing binary-rational output masses

All intermediate masses are rational with polynomial encoding length. We now also ensure terminating binary masses whenever “binary-rational” has that stricter, dyadic meaning.

Every constructed bundle satisfies
\( k_g\le y_{ag}+1. \)
Consequently,
\( U_a(k)\le\sum_gk_g \le Q/\mu_{\min}+m=:B_*. \)
Choose a positive integer \(R\), of polynomial size, satisfying
\( R\ge\ln(1+B_*/\omega). \)
Such an \(R\) can be obtained directly from the bit length of the numerator of the rational number \(1+B_*/\omega\).

Let
\( N_0=2\tau(m+1),\qquad w_{\max}=\max_aw_a. \)
Choose an integer precision \(B\) such that
\( 2^{-B}N_0\frac{w_{\max}}W R\le\epsilon/2. \)
If the input type masses are dyadic, also choose \(B\) so that every \(2^B\mu_a\) is an integer. These requirements give a \(B\) polynomial in the input encoding length and \(\log^+(1/\epsilon)\).

For every nonempty listed bundle, replace its mass \(\rho\) by
\( \rho'=2^{-B}\lfloor2^B\rho\rfloor. \)
For each type, assign all remaining mass to the empty bundle. Remove zero entries and merge duplicate bundles.

Type masses remain exact, and consumption can only decrease. All nonempty masses are dyadic. The empty mass is \(\mu_a\) minus a dyadic number, so it is dyadic when the input masses are dyadic; under the ordinary binary-encoded rational interpretation, it also meets the required representation.

Moving mass \(d\) from a bundle \(k\) of type \(a\) to the empty bundle decreases normalized logarithmic welfare by
\( \frac{w_a}W d\ln\!\left(1+\frac{U_a(k)}\omega\right) \le\frac{w_{\max}}W dR. \)
Each listed nonempty mass loses less than \(2^{-B}\). Therefore the final feasible allocation \(x\) satisfies
\( \ln F_\omega(x) \ge\ln F_\omega(x^\circ)-\epsilon/2 \ge V-\epsilon-A_\omega. \)

It has at most \(\tau(2m+3)\) entries and polynomial total encoding length.

6. The approximation guarantee and running time

Since \(\ln(\mathrm{OPT})\le V\),
\( F_\omega(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}\,e^{-\epsilon-A_\omega}. \)
The explicit loss formula gives
\( e^{-A_\omega} =\omega\ln(1+1/\omega)\, e^{\,1-\omega\ln(1+1/\omega)}. \)
The inequality \(\ln(1+t)\le t\), applied at \(t=1/\omega\), shows
\( \omega\ln(1+1/\omega)\le1. \)
Thus
\( e^{-A_\omega}\ge\omega\ln(1+1/\omega), \)
and finally
\( \boxed{ F_\omega(x)\ge \mathrm{OPT}\, \frac{\omega\ln(1+1/\omega)}{e^\epsilon} }. \)

The rational LP has polynomial size; all rounding decompositions, bundle coordinates, and masses have polynomial encoding length; and the final dyadic conversion is polynomial time. No step enumerates individual households or resource units. The requested algorithm therefore exists with the absolute constant \(C=1\).
