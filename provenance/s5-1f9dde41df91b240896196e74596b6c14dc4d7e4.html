The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) of complete extended-preference and priority-tier types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita capacities for both periods, find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,a,b}\) assigning each unit of mass an integral bundle \((a,b)\). Require individual rationality and no positive mass that can block under the paper’s blocking tests, with schools applying priority tiers and splitting a cutoff tier symmetrically; report if no such allocation exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools, and let \(0\) denote the outside option. A bundle \((a,b)\in(S\cup\{0\})^2\) gives a first-period seat and a second-period priority entitlement, respectively; these consume separate capacities \(q_s^1,q_s^2\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a strict complete school ranking \(P_t\), including \(0\), and a strict complete bundle ranking \(\succ_t\). A priority-only type ranks \((0,s)\) according to \(P_t\), with every bundle having a nonzero first coordinate below \((0,0)\). A willingness-to-remain type ranks diagonal bundles according to \(P_t\), places every unequal-coordinate bundle below \((0,0)\), and satisfies \((s,s)\succ_t(s,r)\) whenever \(s\ne r\). Every type belongs to one of these two domains.
Each school specifies acceptable types and ordered priority tiers, identical across periods. Its choice from an applicant mass pool rejects unacceptable types, fills capacity from higher tiers first, and retains the same proportion of every applicant subgroup in a partially admitted cutoff tier; lower tiers are rejected.
An allocation consists of rational masses \(x_{t,a,b}\geq0\) summing to \(\mu_t\) for each type. School occupancy in each coordinate must respect its capacity. Individual rationality requires every positive-mass bundle to be weakly preferred to \((0,0)\), every nonzero coordinate to be acceptable under \(P_t\), and its type to be acceptable to the corresponding school. Each unit receives one integral bundle, although a type's mass may be split among bundles.
A block consists of \(0<\delta\leq x_{t,a,b}\) seeking a strictly preferred individually rational bundle \((c,d)\), changing either one coordinate or both. For every changed nonzero coordinate, the destination school's choice from its current occupants plus these \(\delta\) applicants must retain all \(\delta\) applicants. Unchanged coordinates need no new admission; moving to \(0\) needs no consent. Stability means individual rationality and absence of such a block.

Can a stable allocation be found, or nonexistence correctly reported, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computational problem NP-hard?

For finite societies with strict school priorities and the stated preference domain, a stable matching exists. [Atay and Romero-Medina](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.14689v1)
Continuum many-to-one matching admits stable outcomes under convexity and continuity conditions on firms' choices; this is a different model from two-period bundle allocation. [Che, Kim, and Kojima](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA13547) No result settling the exact computational problem stated here was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question omitted the preference restriction: impose the anchor's priority-only or willingness-to-remain domain.
  • Normalize total mass to one and interpret capacities as nonnegative binary-rational capacities per unit of population.
  • Complete the willingness-to-remain definition at bundles containing the outside option: all unequal-coordinate bundles are below the all-outside bundle, consistent with the stated unwillingness to attend unless both objects concern the same school.
  • Align bundle acceptability with the underlying school ranking and retain school-side acceptability, which the responsive-priority definition mentions.
  • Interpret the ambiguous coordinate preference notation through strict improvement of the proposed bundle, retaining both one-coordinate and simultaneous two-coordinate deviations.
  • Define symmetric cutoff splitting as proportional selection within the tested applicant pool; a positive-mass block requires the entire proposed submass to be admitted at every changed destination.
  • Use rational allocation masses so that exact allocations have a finite computational representation.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses has a stable rational allocation.

The proof reduces the problem to bipartite stable allocation with rational quotas and strict preferences. Each school is replaced by two auxiliary pools. The main step is proving that stability in this auxiliary market implies stability under the original proportional-choice rule.

1. Admissible bundles and the exact blocking condition

Let \(T_P\) and \(T_W\) denote the priority-only and willingness-to-remain types, respectively. Define the mutually acceptable schools of type \(t\) by

\[ A_t=\{s\in S:sP_t0\text{ and school }s\text{ accepts }t\}. \]

Individual rationality and the preference restrictions imply that the only possible positive-mass bundles are:

  • \((0,0)\), for either domain;
  • \((0,s)\), with \(s\in A_t\), for \(t\in T_P\);
  • \((s,s)\), with \(s\in A_t\), for \(t\in T_W\).

Thus write \(z_{t,s}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving its domain’s bundle at school \(s\), and \(z_{t,0}\) for its outside-option mass. These masses satisfy \(\sum_{s\in A_t\cup\{0\}}z_{t,s}=\mu_t\).

If a type satisfies both domain definitions, it has no school above \(0\) in \(P_t\): otherwise its bundle \((0,s)\) would have to be both above and below \((0,0)\). Such a type receives only the outside option and can be placed in either class.

Number each school’s priority tiers from highest to lowest, and let \(\pi_s(t)\) be the tier of acceptable type \(t\). For an allocation \(z\), define the masses occupying school \(s\) with priority at least that of \(t\):

\[ B_s^1(t)= \sum_{\substack{u\in T_W\\ \pi_s(u)\leq\pi_s(t)}}z_{u,s}, \qquad B_s^2(t)= \sum_{\substack{u\\ \pi_s(u)\leq\pi_s(t)}}z_{u,s}. \]

Sums here and below include only types acceptable to the relevant school.

Admission lemma. In coordinate \(k\), school \(s\) retains all of an additional mass \(\delta>0\) of type \(t\) if and only if
\[ B_s^k(t)+\delta\leq q_s^k. \]

Indeed, if this inequality holds, every applicant in \(t\)’s tier or a higher tier is admitted. If it fails, either the capacity is exhausted above \(t\)’s tier, or \(t\)’s tier is only partially admitted. In the latter case, proportional choice retains strictly less than the whole new applicant subgroup. This proves both directions.

Say that \(t\) wants \(s\) if \(s\in A_t\) and \(z_{t,a}>0\) for some \(a\) with \(sP_ta\). All possible blocks are moves of such mass to its admissible bundle at \(s\). A priority-only move changes only the second coordinate; a willingness-to-remain move changes both coordinates.

Consequently, stability is equivalent to the following conditions for every \(t\) that wants \(s\):

  • If \(t\in T_P\), then \(B_s^2(t)=q_s^2\).
  • If \(t\in T_W\), then \(B_s^1(t)=q_s^1\) or \(B_s^2(t)=q_s^2\).

To justify necessity, if every required prefix mass is strictly below its capacity, choose \(\delta>0\) smaller than the positive source mass and every required capacity slack. The admission lemma gives a block. Sufficiency follows because equality at any required coordinate prevents full admission of every positive \(\delta\). Prefix masses never exceed the corresponding capacities, so these exhaust the possibilities.

2. Construction of the auxiliary market

For every school \(s\), put
\[ \kappa_s=\min\{q_s^1,q_s^2\}, \qquad \lambda_s=q_s^2-\kappa_s. \]

Create two auxiliary pools:

  • a shared pool \(C_s\), with capacity \(\kappa_s\), accepting both domains;
  • a priority-only pool \(R_s\), with capacity \(\lambda_s\), accepting only \(T_P\).

Omit pools with zero capacity.

Each type is a left-side vertex with quota \(\mu_t\). Its acceptable auxiliary pools and preferences are as follows:

  • A willingness-to-remain type lists \(C_s\) for \(s\in A_t\), in its school order.
  • A priority-only type replaces each \(s\in A_t\) by consecutive entries \(R_s,C_s\), in that order, while preserving its order between schools. Omitted pools are simply deleted.

Every listed pool is preferred to unused quota, which represents the outside option.

At each school, refine its priority tiers into an arbitrary strict order of acceptable types. Use the same refinement for both auxiliary pools. Let \(\rho_s(t)\) be a type’s position in this order, with smaller positions better.

The refinements are computational devices. The proof below verifies stability against the original tiers and proportional choice.

3. The stable-allocation theorem and a consequence

We use the following established theorem.

Bipartite stable-allocation theorem. For a finite bipartite graph with strict preferences at each vertex, nonnegative rational vertex quotas and edge capacities, a stable allocation exists and can be computed by a strongly polynomial algorithm. See the [corrected Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Here an edge \((t,v)\) blocks a feasible allocation \(y\) precisely when all three inequalities hold:
\[ y_{t,v}<u_{t,v}, \]
\[ \sum_{w:\,w\succeq_t v}y_{t,w}<\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{a:\,a\succeq_v t}y_{a,v}<c_v, \]
where \(u_{t,v}\) is its edge capacity and \(c_v\) is the pool quota. Thus both endpoints can increase that edge by using unused quota or reducing inferior assignments.

All theorem hypotheses hold for the constructed market: it is finite and bipartite, its incident-edge preferences are strict, and its quotas are rational. Set each edge capacity to
\[ u_{t,v}=\min\{\mu_t,c_v\}. \]
This imposes no restriction beyond the vertex quotas. Let \(y\) be a stable auxiliary allocation supplied by the theorem.

We will repeatedly use the following consequence.

Prefix-saturation lemma. If type \(t\) has unused quota or positive allocation to a pool inferior to an acceptable pool \(v\), then
\[ \sum_{a:\,a\succeq_v t}y_{a,v}=c_v. \]

Proof. Suppose that the displayed sum were strictly below \(c_v\). Then \(y_{t,v}<c_v\). The type’s unused or inferior allocation also implies \(y_{t,v}<\mu_t\) and
\[ \sum_{w:\,w\succeq_t v}y_{t,w}<\mu_t. \]
Therefore \(y_{t,v}<\min\{\mu_t,c_v\}=u_{t,v}\), and all three blocking inequalities hold, contradicting stability. ∎

For an omitted zero-capacity pool, define its allocations and prefix sums to be zero. Its prefix-saturation equality holds automatically.

4. Projection to integral bundles

Project the auxiliary allocation by setting
\[ z_{t,s}=y_{t,C_s} \quad\text{if }t\in T_W, \]
and
\[ z_{t,s}=y_{t,C_s}+y_{t,R_s} \quad\text{if }t\in T_P. \]
Set
\[ z_{t,0}=\mu_t-\sum_{s\in A_t}z_{t,s}. \]

These numbers are nonnegative. They define original bundle masses by assigning \(z_{t,s}\) to \((s,s)\) for willingness-to-remain types, to \((0,s)\) for priority-only types, and \(z_{t,0}\) to \((0,0)\).

All assigned bundles are individually rational. First-period occupancy satisfies
\[ \sum_{t\in T_W}z_{t,s} \leq\sum_t y_{t,C_s} \leq\kappa_s \leq q_s^1. \]
Second-period occupancy satisfies
\[ \sum_t z_{t,s} =\sum_t y_{t,C_s}+\sum_t y_{t,R_s} \leq\kappa_s+\lambda_s =q_s^2. \]

This construction splits a type’s mass into portions receiving single integral bundles. It does not assign fractional bundles to individual units.

5. Proof of stability after projection

For each acceptable type \(t\), define the refined-order prefix masses
\[ H_s^C(t)= \sum_{\rho_s(u)\leq\rho_s(t)}y_{u,C_s}, \qquad H_s^R(t)= \sum_{\rho_s(u)\leq\rho_s(t)}y_{u,R_s}, \]
and
\[ H_s^W(t)= \sum_{\substack{u\in T_W\\\rho_s(u)\leq\rho_s(t)}}y_{u,C_s}. \]

Their capacity bounds are
\[ H_s^C(t)\leq\kappa_s,\qquad H_s^R(t)\leq\lambda_s,\qquad H_s^W(t)\leq q_s^1. \]

Because the strict order refines the original tiers, every type counted in these prefixes belongs to \(t\)’s tier or a higher tier. Hence
\[ B_s^1(t)\geq H_s^W(t), \qquad B_s^2(t)\geq H_s^C(t)+H_s^R(t). \]

It therefore suffices to establish the required saturation certificates using refined prefixes.

Priority-only types

Suppose \(t\in T_P\) wants \(s\) in the projected allocation.

Positive outside mass corresponds to unused auxiliary quota. Positive mass at a worse original school corresponds to an auxiliary allocation below both copies of \(s\). The prefix-saturation lemma therefore gives
\[ H_s^C(t)=\kappa_s, \qquad H_s^R(t)=\lambda_s. \]

Consequently,
\[ B_s^2(t)\geq H_s^C(t)+H_s^R(t) =\kappa_s+\lambda_s=q_s^2. \]
Feasibility supplies the reverse inequality, so \(B_s^2(t)=q_s^2\). By Section 1, this type cannot block at \(s\).

Willingness-to-remain types

Suppose \(t\in T_W\) wants \(s\). Its unused or inferior auxiliary allocation and the prefix-saturation lemma give
\[ H_s^C(t)=\kappa_s. \]

There are two capacity cases.

Case 1: \(q_s^1\geq q_s^2\). Then \(\kappa_s=q_s^2\), so
\[ B_s^2(t)\geq H_s^C(t)=q_s^2. \]
Thus \(B_s^2(t)=q_s^2\), preventing a block.

Case 2: \(q_s^1<q_s^2\). Then \(\kappa_s=q_s^1\) and \(\lambda_s=q_s^2-q_s^1\).

If no priority-only mass occurs in the shared-pool prefix of \(t\), then
\[ H_s^W(t)=H_s^C(t)=q_s^1. \]
It follows that \(B_s^1(t)=q_s^1\), preventing a block.

Otherwise, choose a priority-only type \(u\) satisfying
\[ \rho_s(u)\leq\rho_s(t), \qquad y_{u,C_s}>0. \]
Type \(u\) prefers \(R_s\) to \(C_s\). Its positive shared-pool allocation therefore implies, by the prefix-saturation lemma,
\[ H_s^R(u)=\lambda_s. \]
The identical priority order in the two pools now gives prefix inclusion:
\[ H_s^R(t)\geq H_s^R(u)=\lambda_s. \]
The reserve-pool capacity bound implies equality. Hence
\[ B_s^2(t)\geq H_s^C(t)+H_s^R(t) =q_s^1+\lambda_s=q_s^2. \]
Thus \(B_s^2(t)=q_s^2\), again preventing a block.

These cases include zero first-period capacity: then the first-period saturation certificate is simply \(B_s^1(t)=q_s^1=0\).

Every type wanting every acceptable school consequently has a saturation certificate from Section 1. The projected allocation is stable under the original choice rule. ∎

6. Polynomial bit complexity

The auxiliary instance has at most \(\tau+2m\) vertices and \(2m\tau\) edges. Its construction, including preference lists and priority refinements, takes polynomial time.

Rational input does not require expanding masses into individual applicants. To make the bit-complexity argument explicit, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all original masses and capacities. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Multiplication by \(D\) makes all auxiliary quotas and edge capacities integral, including the differences defining \(\lambda_s\).

One may use the bipartite path-and-cycle augmentation algorithm of [Dean and Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf), which performs polynomially many augmentations using additions, subtractions and minima. With integral input these operations preserve integrality. An elementary implementation uses quantities bounded by sums of the scaled quotas, whose bit lengths are polynomial in \(L,m,\tau\). Thus its arithmetic can be performed in polynomial bit time.

Dividing the computed allocation by \(D\) preserves every feasibility and stability condition. Projection then produces rational bundle masses of polynomial encoding length.

Therefore a stable allocation can always be found in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\); nonexistence never occurs for an instance satisfying the hypotheses.
