$$ f_i(y+s\mathbf e_{j_i})=f_i(y)+a_i s. $$

We construct $n$ distinct dyadic masses. Their distinctness ensures that the resulting per-person budgets distinguish all supported types, even if some finite buyers have identical utilities.

For $n\ge2$, let $N$ be the smallest power of two satisfying $N\ge n$, and put $k=N-n$. Define

The baseline masses sum to one because
$$ \sum_i\beta_i=\frac{2k+n-k}{N}=1. $$
The perturbations sum to zero, so $\sum_i\mu_i=1$. Every mass is dyadic.

Moreover,

For $n\ge3$, using $N<2n$ gives

For $n=2$, the masses are $7/16$ and $9/16$, which also satisfy the required bound.

Within either baseline group, the masses are strictly increasing. Across groups, the baseline difference is $1/N$, whereas the difference between perturbations has magnitude less than $1/(2N)$. Thus all masses are distinct. For $n=1$, use mass one.

Then

Positivity follows from the chosen uncapped component; the upper bound follows from monotonicity and $\mu_i\le1$.

3. Converting every society solution into a finite-market solution

$$ Y_i=\mu_i x_i,\qquad c_i=p^\top Y_i,\qquad Q=\sum_jp_j. $$
Its guarantees become
$$ f_i(Y_i)\ge U_i(p)-\epsilon H_i, $$
$$ c_i\le1+\mu_i\epsilon n, $$
$$ \sum_iY_{ij}\le1, $$
and
$$ S:=\sum_jp_j\left(1-\sum_iY_{ij}\right)\le\epsilon n. $$

Since $f_i(Y_i)$ is finite, the first inequality forces $U_i(p)$ to be finite. In particular,
$$ p_{j_i}>0; $$
otherwise unlimited free consumption of the uncapped component would give $U_i(p)=+\infty$.

3.1. Bounding the total price

Supply feasibility and the definition of $S$ give
$$ Q=\sum_i c_i+S. $$
Summing the budget bounds yields
$$ \sum_i c_i \le n+\epsilon n\sum_i\mu_i =n(1+\epsilon). $$
Therefore,
$$ Q\le n(1+2\epsilon). $$

Define the rational constant
$$ D=\sum_i\frac{H_i}{a_i}. $$
We also obtain a lower bound.

If $c_i\le1$, the bundle
$$ Y_i+\frac{1-c_i}{p_{j_i}}\mathbf e_{j_i} $$
is affordable at budget one. Its utility is
$$ f_i(Y_i)+a_i\frac{1-c_i}{p_{j_i}}. $$
The utility approximation consequently implies
$$ a_i\frac{1-c_i}{p_{j_i}}\le\epsilon H_i. $$
Since $p_{j_i}\le Q$,
$$ c_i\ge1-\epsilon\frac{H_i}{a_i}Q. $$
When $c_i>1$, this last inequality holds immediately.

Summing and using $Q\ge\sum_i c_i$ gives
$$ Q\ge n-\epsilon DQ, $$
hence
$$ \frac{n}{1+\epsilon D}\le Q\le n(1+2\epsilon). $$

$$ \alpha=\frac nQ,\qquad p'=\alpha p. $$
Then
$$ \sum_jp'_j=n, \qquad \frac1{1+2\epsilon}\le\alpha\le1+\epsilon D. $$

Because $c_i\le1+\epsilon n$,
$$ (p')^\top v_i=\alpha\kappa c_i\le1. $$

These rational bundles satisfy exact clearing:
$$ \sum_i Z_{ij} = \sum_i v_{ij}+\ell_j =1. $$
They also satisfy exact budgets:
$$ (p')^\top Z_i = (p')^\top v_i+ \frac{r_i}{R}\sum_jp'_j\ell_j = (p')^\top v_i+r_i =1. $$
This calculation remains valid for zero-price goods.

where the last inequality uses $f_i(0)=0$.

$$ \theta=\min\{1,1/Q\}, $$
gives, by concavity,
$$ U_i(p)\ge f_i(\theta\mathbf1)\ge\theta H_i. $$

$$ U_i(p)\ge\frac{H_i}{3n}. $$

$$ f_i(Y_i) \ge U_i(p)-\epsilon H_i \ge(1-3n\epsilon)U_i(p). $$

$$ U_i(p')\le(1+2\epsilon)U_i(p). $$

$$ f_i(\alpha y)\ge\alpha f_i(y). $$
Taking suprema yields
$$ U_i(p')\le\frac{U_i(p)}{\alpha} \le(1+2\epsilon)U_i(p). $$

$$ f_i(Z_i) \ge \frac{\kappa(1-3n\epsilon)}{1+2\epsilon}U_i(p'). $$

$$ K=D+4n+2. $$

$$ \frac1{1+s}\ge1-s \qquad(s\ge0) $$
and the elementary product bound

then imply

The product bound follows inductively from
$$ (1-a)(1-b)=1-a-b+ab\ge1-a-b. $$

Therefore the repaired solution satisfies
$$ f_i(Z_i)\ge(1-\epsilon K)U_i(p') $$
for every buyer, with exact budgets and exact clearing.

5.1. Regularity of the given utilities

Fix one type and omit its subscript. The feasible domain
$$ K=\{x\ge0:\exists z,\ Ax+Bz\le b\} $$
is a projection of a polyhedron, hence is itself a closed polyhedron.

Because the utility LP at the origin is feasible and has finite optimum, its dual supplies a multiplier in the nonempty polyhedron
$$ \Lambda=\{\lambda\ge0:B^\top\lambda=s\}. $$
For any feasible $(x,z)$ and any $\lambda\in\Lambda$,
$$ q^\top x+s^\top z \le (q-A^\top\lambda)^\top x+b^\top\lambda. $$
Thus the utility is bounded above at every fixed feasible $x$.

The polyhedron $\Lambda$ is pointed: a line contained in it would also be contained in the nonnegative orthant, which is impossible. Consequently, every finite optimum of a linear objective over $\Lambda$ is attained at a vertex. Let its finitely many vertices be $\lambda^1,\ldots,\lambda^s$. Strong duality gives, for $x\in K$,
$$ u(x)= \min_h \left\{ (q-A^\top\lambda^h)^\top x+b^\top\lambda^h \right\}. $$
This finite minimum of affine functions is concave and Lipschitz on $K$. Indeed, each affine function has Lipschitz constant equal to the Euclidean norm of its slope, and their minimum has Lipschitz constant at most the largest of those norms. Hence $u$ is regular: its domain is closed, it is concave and Lipschitz there, and $u(0)=0$.

I use the established existence theorem that Fisher markets with positive budgets and regular concave utilities satisfying these origin conditions admit an exact equilibrium; see [Garg–Tao–Végh, Theorem 2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.05700v1#S2.Thmtheorem1).

To apply it to supported types, put
$$ h=\min_t\mu_t,\qquad Y_t=\mu_t x_t,\qquad e_t=\mu_tw_t,\qquad f_t(Y)=h\,u_t(Y/\mu_t). $$
These utilities remain regular. They also satisfy the theorem’s cube normalization. If $Y\in[0,1]^m$ and $Y/\mu_t$ is feasible, concavity gives
$$ u_t(Y)\ge\mu_tu_t(Y/\mu_t), $$
so
$$ f_t(Y)\le h/\mu_t\le1. $$
Their cube maxima are positive: if $u_t(a)>0$ for some $a\in[0,1]^m$, then
$$ f_t(\mu_ta)=h\,u_t(a)>0. $$

An exact equilibrium in this finite market therefore yields an exact society equilibrium $(p^*,x^*)$. Write
$$ X_j^*=\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}^*. $$
Exact clearing and budgets imply
$$ \sum_jp_j^* = \sum_jp_j^*X_j^* = \sum_t\mu_t(p^*)^\top x_t^* \le W. $$
Supply feasibility also implies
$$ x_{tj}^*\le1/\mu_t. $$
