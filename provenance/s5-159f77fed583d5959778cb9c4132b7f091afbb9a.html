Result and interpretation

A polynomial-time mechanism exists. The mass model admits both required \(o(H)\) regrets and the stated, unnormalized type-average individual rationality.

For finite populations, the following stronger statements hold, with the known input and unknown value function fixed while \(H\to\infty\):

  • A polynomial-time implementation has \(o(H)\) single-user reporting regret for every feasible joint scaling \(n=n(H)\).
  • If \(n(H)\to\infty\), the same implementation also retains both per-capita \(o(H)\) regrets against the specified mass benchmark.
  • In the worst case, \(n(H)\to\infty\) is necessary for retaining those mass-benchmark guarantees. Bounded populations have an unavoidable welfare gap caused by random requests.

The construction uses tabular estimates. The linear feature assumption is therefore unnecessary for these conclusions.

The mechanism knows \(H\), as permitted by the running-time requirement. Its asymptotic guarantees are pointwise in the fixed input; no uniform polynomial sample-complexity claim in the inverse of the smallest cell probability is needed.

1. Learning from the permitted feedback

Let \(\mathcal C=\{(t,w):w\in W_t\}\), let \(N=|\mathcal C|\le \tau m\), and write \(v_j\) and \(a_j\) for the value and mass of cell \(j\).

For a truthful report \(Y=\mathbf 1[U\ge c]\), independence and uniformity of \(c\) give

\[ \mathbb E[Y\mid U]=\int_0^1\mathbf 1[U\ge z]\,dz=U. \]

Consequently,

\[ \mathbb E[Y\mid j]=v_j. \]

Thus one sampled report supplies an unbiased bounded observation of its cell mean. Neither realized values nor aggregate feedback are necessary.

A concentration lemma

Suppose \(T\) independent exploration observations have labels in a set of size \(J\). Every label has probability at least \(f>0\), and conditional on its label, an observed bounded variable has a fixed mean. Define its empirical mean to be zero if the label has no observations.

Put

\[ \ell=\left\lceil\log_2(8JH^4)\right\rceil. \]

Choose a rational radius \(\rho=\rho_H(f,J)\) as follows:

  • if \(\sqrt{16\ell/(Tf)}\ge 1\), take \(\rho=1\);
  • otherwise choose a dyadic rational between \(\sqrt{16\ell/(Tf)}\) and \(\min\{1,2\sqrt{16\ell/(Tf)}\}\).

Then, for \(H\ge 2\), all empirical means are within \(\rho\) of their means except on an event of probability at most \(H^{-3}\). The same bound holds for two bounded variables observed with each label.

Here is the verification. If \(\rho=1\), the assertion is deterministic. Otherwise, for a label with probability \(f_j\), the probability that its count is below \(Tf_j/2\) is at most \(\exp(-Tf_j/8)\). Conditional on a count at least \(Tf_j/2\), Hoeffding’s inequality bounds its estimation failure probability by

\[ 2\exp(-Tf_j\rho^2). \]

A union bound over two variables and \(J\) labels gives at most

\[ 2J\left(\exp(-Tf/8)+2\exp(-Tf\rho^2)\right). \]

Since \(\rho<1\) implies \(Tf>16\ell\), this is at most

\[ 2J\left(\exp(-2\ell)+2\exp(-16\ell)\right)\le H^{-3}. \]

We will use

\[ T=\min\left\{H,\left\lceil H^{2/3}\right\rceil\right\}. \]

For every fixed positive \(f\) and fixed \(J\), the resulting radius tends to zero.

2. Clearing prices and allocations

For any score vector \(s\in[0,1]^{\mathcal C}\), define

\[ p(s)=\inf\left\{z\in[0,1]:\sum_j a_j\mathbf 1[s_j>z]\le q\right\}. \]

Let \(x(s)\) serve all cells strictly above \(p(s)\), no cells strictly below it, and split the cutoff tie proportionally. Explicitly, on the cutoff tie its served fraction is

\[ \frac{q-\sum_{j:s_j>p(s)}a_j}{\sum_{j:s_j=p(s)}a_j}. \]

The denominator is positive: for finite support and \(q<1\), the cutoff is an attained score. Thus \(\sum_j a_jx_j(s)=q\).

Three properties will be used.

Optimality. The allocation \(x(s)\) maximizes \(\sum_j a_jx_js_j\) over feasible fractional allocations. To verify this, exchanging served mass from a lower score to an unserved higher score increases the objective. The displayed allocation has no such exchange and fills capacity.

Cutoff properties. Every served cell has \(s_j\ge p(s)\), and every cell with \(s_j>p(s)\) is fully served.

Price stability. If \(\|s-v\|_\infty\le r\), then

\[ |p(s)-p(v)|\le r. \]

Indeed, scores exceeding \(p(v)+r\) must have true values exceeding \(p(v)\), so their mass is at most \(q\). This proves \(p(s)\le p(v)+r\). Interchanging \(s\) and \(v\) proves the reverse bound; clipping at the endpoints causes no difficulty.

Also, optimality gives

\[ 0\le W^*-\sum_j a_jx_j(s)v_j\le 2qr. \]

For the upper bound, insert the score objective between the two value objectives. Each score-to-value replacement changes an allocation’s objective by at most \(qr\), and the difference between the score objectives is nonpositive.

These facts apply to the benchmark in the question, including the case where the mass strictly above its cutoff equals \(q\).

3. The mass mechanism

Construction

During the first \(T\) rounds, serve fraction \(q\) of every cell and charge zero. The uniformly sampled served user then has cell distribution \(a_j\). Store only these exploration reports.

Let \(\widehat v_j\) be the resulting empirical means. Set

\[ a_{\min}=\min_j a_j,\qquad \rho_P=\rho_H(a_{\min},N). \]

In every remaining round:

  • use allocation \(x(\widehat v)\);
  • charge every served user the price

\[ p_0=\max\{0,p(\widehat v)-\rho_P\}; \]

  • ignore that round’s feedback for future decisions.

This is an online mechanism: every allocation and price uses only previously collected data.

Welfare and revenue guarantees

Let \(G\) be the event that all estimates are within \(\rho_P\) of their true means. By the concentration lemma,

\[ \Pr(G^c)\le H^{-3}. \]

On \(G\), exploitation welfare loss is at most \(2q\rho_P\). Moreover, price stability implies

\[ p^*-2\rho_P\le p_0\le p^*. \]

Since exploitation serves exactly mass \(q\), its revenue differs from \(R^*=qp^*\) by at most \(2q\rho_P\).

Every round’s welfare and revenue belong to \([0,1]\). Therefore both absolute expected cumulative regrets are bounded by

\[ T+2qH\rho_P+H^{-2}=o(H). \]

The absolute-value requirement on revenue is thus satisfied, rather than merely a one-sided revenue bound.

The stated individual rationality

On \(G\), every served exploitation cell satisfies

\[ v_j\ge \widehat v_j-\rho_P \ge p(\widehat v)-\rho_P. \]

If the final expression is negative, the charged price is zero; otherwise it is exactly \(p_0\). In either case, \(v_j\ge p_0\). Exploration also gives nonnegative expected utility because its price is zero.

For any type, the cumulative expected utility is consequently at least

\[ -H\Pr(G^c)\ge -H^{-2}. \]

Its limit inferior is nonnegative. This establishes the question’s unnormalized individual-rationality condition, not just an \(o(H)\) bound on participation losses.

4. Finite implementation when the population grows

Write

\[ \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t,\qquad \nu_{\min}=\min_{t,w}\nu_t(w). \]

Exploration uses exactly the permitted access

During exploration, choose one persistent user uniformly, serve only that user, and charge zero. This is feasible because \(k\ge1\).

The mechanism knows the chosen recipient from its own allocation decision. Thus individual estimates used below require no additional identity information from the feedback channel. With a single recipient, the uniformly sampled served user is that recipient.

A sampled user-request pair has probability \(\nu_t(w)/n\); its cell has probability \(a_{tw}\). All exploitation feedback is ignored.

Pooled implementation

First consider using the pooled cell estimates from Section 3.

In an exploitation round, independently mark user \(i\), whose current cell is \(j\), for service with probability \(x_j(\widehat v)\). If more than \(k\) users are marked, retain a uniformly chosen \(k\) of them. Charge retained users \(p_0\).

This allocation is indivisible and always respects capacity.

Effect of one arbitrary reporting strategy

Fix a deviating user of type \(t\). Couple its exploration path with a shadow path in which it reports truthfully. Exploration recipients, requests, values and thresholds are identical in the two paths because exploration ignores reports.

For a cell of type \(t\), the conditional probability that its sampled user is the deviator equals

\[ b_t=\frac{1}{n\mu_t}. \]

For other types it is zero. Apply the concentration lemma to the shadow truthful report and the indicator of sampling the deviator. Except on an event of probability at most \(H^{-3}\), simultaneously,

\[ \|\widehat v^{\mathrm{truth}}-v\|_\infty\le\rho_P, \]

and the fraction of observations changed in any cell is at most \(b_t+\rho_P\).

Changing binary observations changes their empirical mean by at most the fraction changed. Therefore, under every adaptive reporting strategy,

\[ \|\widehat v^\sigma-v\|_\infty \le 2\rho_P+\frac{1}{n\mu_{\min}}. \]

The good event depends only on the exogenous exploration data and shadow truthful reports. Hence this bound is uniform over strategies; it does not presume that the deviator’s reports are independent.

Capacity trimming

Conditional on the learned allocation, let \(S\) denote the number marked. Independence of users’ requests and marking coins gives

\[ \mathbb ES=nq,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S)\le n/4. \]

The expected number removed is at most

\[ \mathbb E(S-k)_+\le \sqrt n/2+1. \]

Define

\[ B_n=\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}+\frac1n. \]

Thus trimming changes expected per-capita welfare or revenue by at most \(B_n\).

For an individual, condition on its current request and on being marked. The conditional mean of \(S\) is at most \(nq+1\), and its variance is at most \(n/4\). Uniform trimming therefore removes it with probability at most

\[ D_n=\min\left\{1,\frac{\sqrt n/2+2}{k}\right\}. \]

Indeed, given \(S\), its removal probability is \((S-k)_+/S\), which is at most \((S-k)_+/k\). Taking expectations gives the displayed bound. For fixed \(q\),

\[ D_n=O_q(n^{-1/2}). \]

Reporting-regret bound

For a user whose current cell value is \(v_j\), define

\[ g_j=(v_j-p^*)_+. \]

This is an upper bound on its expected utility from any allocation charging price \(p^*\).

On the good event, truthful price satisfies \(p_0\le p^*\). If

\[ v_j>p^*+2\rho_P, \]

then \(\widehat v_j>p(\widehat v)\), so that user is marked with probability one. Otherwise \(g_j\le2\rho_P\), unless \(g_j=0\). Truthful marked users have nonnegative expected utility by the same argument as in Section 3. After allowing for trimming, truthful expected utility is therefore at least

\[ g_j-2\rho_P-D_n. \]

Under a deviation, price stability gives

\[ p_0^\sigma\ge p^*-3\rho_P-\frac{1}{n\mu_{\min}}. \]

Whatever allocation results, the user’s conditional expected utility is at most

\[ g_j+3\rho_P+\frac{1}{n\mu_{\min}}. \]

Current realized values have the specified mean conditional on the request and are independent of the past used to set allocations and prices. These conditional utility comparisons are therefore valid.

Exploration utility is identical under the two strategies. Cumulative utilities lie in \([-H,H]\), so the exceptional event contributes at most \(2H^{-2}\). We obtain

\[ \operatorname{Reg}_{\mathrm{report}}(n,H) \le H\left( 5\rho_P+\frac{1}{n\mu_{\min}}+D_n \right)+2H^{-2}. \]

Consequently, this pooled implementation has \(o(H)\) reporting regret whenever \(n(H)\to\infty\), with no further relation between \(n\) and \(H\).

Under truthful feedback, each per-capita mass-benchmark regret is at most

\[ T+H(2q\rho_P+B_n)+H^{-2}. \]

It therefore retains both guarantees whenever \(n(H)\to\infty\). Its truthful cumulative expected utility for every user is at least \(-H^{-2}\).

5. Handling small populations

The preceding reporting bound need not vanish for fixed \(n\). We now supply a finite implementation that handles those populations too.

For individual estimates, use the same exploration data but maintain a separate empirical mean for each user-request pair. Let

\[ J_I=\sum_t n\mu_t|W_t|\le nm,\qquad \rho_I=\rho_H(\nu_{\min}/n,J_I). \]

There are two cases.

Case A: every type has at least three users

For each cell \((t,w)\), take the median of the corresponding individual estimates among users of type \(t\).

Under a single deviation, only one individual estimator is corrupted. On the event that all shadow truthful individual estimates are within \(\rho_I\), strictly more than half of each type’s estimates remain within \(\rho_I\) of \(v_t(w)\). Therefore every cell median lies within that same interval, under every deviation.

We must handle exact ties carefully. Sort the cell medians and join consecutive cells into a cluster whenever their consecutive score gap is at most \(2\rho_I\). Give every cell in a cluster the minimum median in that cluster. Use these clustered scores in the mass allocation \(x(\cdot)\), with price equal to their clearing price minus \(\rho_I\), clipped at zero. Implement the fractions by marking and trimming as before.

Let \(\Delta\) be the smallest positive difference between distinct true cell values; put \(\Delta=+\infty\) if all values coincide. Because there are finitely many cells, \(\Delta>0\) whenever it is finite.

If

\[ 4\rho_I<\Delta, \]

then the clusters are exactly the true value classes:

  • estimates within one true class span an interval of length at most \(2\rho_I\), so consecutive estimates in that class are joined;
  • estimates from consecutive distinct true classes have gap at least \(\Delta-2\rho_I>2\rho_I\), so they are separated.

The ordered classes and their known masses then equal the benchmark’s ordered classes and masses. Hence the resulting fractions are exactly the benchmark fractions, regardless of the deviator’s reports.

Couple truthful and deviating marking and trimming coins. Their allocations are identical on the good event. Both prices belong to

\[ [\max\{0,p^*-2\rho_I\},p^*], \]

so their cumulative expected utility difference is at most

\[ 2H\rho_I+2H^{-2}. \]

This reporting bound applies once \(4\rho_I<\Delta\). The mechanism does not need to know \(\Delta\).

For completeness, individual rationality holds on the good event even before the clustering becomes exact. A cluster’s score is no larger than any member’s raw median. Thus a served cell’s true value is at least its cluster score minus \(\rho_I\), and hence at least the charged price.

Case B: some type has fewer than three users

Use the individual estimates directly. In each exploitation round, serve the \(k\) highest estimated current values, breaking ties by a fixed rule.

For a served user \(i\), let \(\widehat\theta_i\) be the \(k\)-th largest estimated current value among the other users. Charge

\[ p_i=\max\{0,\widehat\theta_i-\rho_I\}. \]

This is well-defined because \(k<n\).

Fix a deviator \(i\). Its exploration reports affect only its own estimator. All opponents’ estimators and requests are unaffected. Let \(\theta_i\) be the \(k\)-th largest true current value among opponents. On the good event, order-statistic stability gives

\[ |\widehat\theta_i-\theta_i|\le\rho_I. \]

To verify stability, moving every coordinate by at most \(\rho_I\) moves each ordered coordinate by at most \(\rho_I\), by coordinatewise monotonicity of order statistics.

Let

\[ g_i=(v_i-\theta_i)_+. \]

Under truthful reporting, if user \(i\) loses, then its estimated value is at most \(\widehat\theta_i\), so \(v_i-\theta_i\le2\rho_I\). If it wins, its charged price is at most \(\theta_i\), and its expected utility is nonnegative. Its truthful expected utility is consequently at least \(g_i-2\rho_I\).

Under any deviation, its price, if served, is at least \(\max\{0,\theta_i-2\rho_I\}\). Its expected utility is therefore at most \(g_i+2\rho_I\).

It follows that

\[ \operatorname{Reg}_{\mathrm{report}}(n,H) \le4H\rho_I+2H^{-2}. \]

A truthful winner has estimated value at least \(\widehat\theta_i\), so its true value is at least the discounted price. The same exceptional-event argument again gives cumulative expected utility at least \(-H^{-2}\).

6. One implementation for all joint scalings

Choose the exploitation rule using the known population:

  • if \(n^6\ge H\), use the pooled implementation of Section 4;
  • if \(n^6<H\) and every type has at least three users, use median estimates and tie clustering;
  • otherwise use the individual critical-price implementation.

All branches use the same free, singleton exploration phase.

For fixed input parameters, in the small-population branches,

\[ \rho_I = O\left( \sqrt{\frac{n\log(nmH)}{T\nu_{\min}}} \right) = O\left(H^{-1/4}\sqrt{\log H}\right). \]

Thus \(\rho_I\to0\) uniformly over \(n^6<H\). Eventually the median branch satisfies \(4\rho_I<\Delta\). Both small-population reporting bounds are then \(o(H)\).

In the large-population branch, \(n\ge H^{1/6}\), so

\[ \frac{1}{n\mu_{\min}}\to0,\qquad D_n\to0,\qquad \rho_P\to0. \]

Its reporting bound is also \(o(H)\).

This proves \(o(H)\) single-user reporting regret for every feasible sequence \(n(H)\), including fixed populations, diverging populations, and oscillating sequences.

If additionally \(n(H)\to\infty\), eventually every type has at least three users, because \(\mu_{\min}>0\). The individual critical-price branch is then absent. In either remaining branch, truthful per-capita mass-benchmark regrets vanish after division by \(H\): the pooled branch has the bound in Section 4, while the median branch eventually uses the exact benchmark fractions, with price error at most \(2\rho_I\) and trimming loss at most \(B_n\).

Why growing populations are necessary for the mass benchmark

Consider one type with two requests, whose probabilities are \(q\) and \(1-q\), and values \(1\) and \(0\). This satisfies the linear assumption using one feature, respectively \(1\) and \(0\), and coefficient \(1\).

The mass benchmark has

\[ W^*=q,\qquad p^*=0,\qquad R^*=0. \]

For \(n\) finite users, the number of value-one requests is \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,q)\). No feasible allocation can obtain more than \(\min\{X,k\}\) welfare. Therefore its expected per-capita welfare is at most

\[ \frac{\mathbb E\min\{X,k\}}{n}. \]

Since \(\mathbb EX=nq\),

\[ q-\frac{\mathbb E\min\{X,k\}}{n} = \frac{\mathbb E(X-k)_+}{n}>0. \]

The strict inequality holds because \(\Pr(X=n)=q^n>0\) and \(k<n\).

If \(n(H)\) fails to tend to infinity, it has a bounded infinite subsequence. There are only finitely many population sizes on that subsequence, and each has a strictly positive welfare gap above. Their minimum gap is positive. Along that subsequence, expected cumulative per-capita welfare regret is therefore bounded below by a positive constant times \(H\).

Thus, in the worst case, retaining the mass-benchmark guarantees requires \(n(H)\to\infty\).

7. Running time and relation to the cited paper

The mass mechanism stores at most \(\tau m\) counters and sorts at most that many cell scores. Individual estimation stores at most \(nm\) counters. Medians, clustering, sorting users, and marking and trimming all take polynomial time. Every price, fraction and confidence radius can be represented with polynomially many bits. No unknown coefficient is recovered or optimized over.

For a strict bounded-time random-bit implementation, approximate each internal rational random choice to sufficiently small total variation error that the probability of any discrepancy over the entire run is at most \(H^{-5}\). There are only polynomially many such choices, so polynomially many random bits suffice. Coupling with the ideal implementation changes cumulative expected utilities and regrets by at most \(2H^{-4}\); all established limits survive. The stipulated feedback threshold remains the feedback-access operation in the question.

Consequently, total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,d,L,H\), and polynomial also in \(n\) for finite populations.

The cited paper uses a learned second-price mechanism for a single allocation, with free random exploration; its exploitation comparison threshold is the learned price. Those details appear in Algorithm 1 and Section 5. Its theorem therefore does not directly establish the mass result here. The construction above instead proves the guarantees under the question’s independently uniform sampled-feedback access. [Robertson–Koyejo, *No Bidding, No Regret*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01860)
