The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses, stand-alone payoffs, a rational piecewise-affine coalition-worth function, and a finite rational step allocation, with nonempty core, does a primitive-recursive algorithm output a finite rational step-encoded path to a core allocation? Each block selects positive mass, weakly improves every selected agent and strictly improves a positive-mass subset, and allocates exactly the coalition's worth.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(p_i\), where \(\sum_i p_i=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Realize type \(i\) as a disjoint interval \(I_i=[0,p_i)\), with Lebesgue measure \(\mu\), and write \(\Omega=\bigsqcup_i I_i\).
Input also includes stand-alone payoff densities \(a_i\in\mathbb Q\) and a continuous piecewise-affine function \(f:\prod_i[0,p_i]\to\mathbb R\), specified by a rational polyhedral subdivision with rational affine formulas and satisfying \(f(0)=0\). Payoffs and worths may have either sign. A measurable coalition \(S\) has type-mass vector \(q(S)_i=\mu(S\cap I_i)\) and worth \(f(q(S))\).
An imputation is an integrable payoff density \(x\) satisfying \(x\geq a_i\) almost everywhere on \(I_i\) and \(\int_\Omega x\,d\mu=f(p)\). Its core consists of imputations satisfying \(\int_S x\,d\mu\geq f(q(S))\) for every measurable coalition \(S\); this core is promised nonempty.
The initial imputation \(x^0\) is a finite rational step function. A rational step encoding lists rational interval endpoints and rational payoff values within each type; an encoded coalition is a finite union of such intervals. Partitions may be refined during the path.
A block \(x\to y\) selects an encoded coalition \(S\) with \(\mu(S)>0\), requires \(y\geq x\) almost everywhere on \(S\), \(\mu(\{u\in S:y(u)>x(u)\})>0\), and \(\int_S y\,d\mu=f(q(S))\). Both allocations must be imputations; outsiders' payoffs may change subject to these requirements. A path has finitely many blocks and may have length zero.
Let \(m\) count the affine pieces, \(k\) the initial step intervals, and \(L\) the total binary input length; state running-time bounds in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

Does a uniform primitive-recursive algorithm output, for every promised input, a finite rational step-encoded blocking path from \(x^0\) to a core imputation, or is no such algorithm possible?

For finite TU games with nonempty core, a primitive-recursive algorithm constructs such a path through imputations, with path length bounded solely by the number of players ([Kóczy](https://real.mtak.hu/74945/1/d968f198969e3abfcaa2f13f521776bce463.pdf)). No result settling the precise nonatomic problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one and realized each supported type as an atomless interval, allowing coalitions to select fractions of types.
  • Interpreted stand-alone payoffs as type-specific per-unit-mass lower bounds; allowed signed rational payoffs and signed coalition worths.
  • Made coalition worth depend only on the selected type-mass vector, with empty-coalition worth zero.
  • Specified piecewise-affine input as a continuous function on an explicitly supplied rational polyhedral subdivision.
  • Required the initial allocation and every intermediate allocation to be efficient and individually rational, as in the discrete definition.
  • Allowed outsiders' payoffs to change during a block, subject to imputation constraints.
  • Defined the core against all measurable coalitions, rather than only coalitions admitting rational encodings.
  • Specified rational step encodings using interval endpoints and payoff values, allowing arbitrary finite refinements during the path.
  • Interpreted agentwise inequalities almost everywhere and permitted a zero-length path when the initial allocation already belongs to the core.
  • Used the number of affine pieces as the size parameter m, since this problem has no candidate or alternative count.

1. Type averages of core allocations

Write
$$ p=(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau),\qquad V=f(p),\qquad Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}[0,p_i]. $$

A vector $c\in\mathbb R^\tau$ will be called a core density vector if
$$ c_i\geq a_i,\qquad c\cdot p=V,\qquad c\cdot q\geq f(q)\quad(q\in Q). $$

Such a vector defines a core allocation by assigning density $c_i$ throughout type $i$.

Lemma 1. Under the nonempty-core promise, a core density vector exists. Moreover, one exists with rational coordinates.

Proof. Let $x$ be a core allocation and define
$$ c_i=\frac{1}{p_i}\int_{I_i}x\,d\mu. $$
Individual rationality and efficiency give $c_i\geq a_i$ and $c\cdot p=V$.

Fix $q\in Q$. Regard $I_i$ as a circle of circumference $p_i$, and translate an interval of length $q_i$ uniformly around that circle. Each point is selected with probability $q_i/p_i$. Taking these selections independently across types produces coalitions of mass vector $q$. Integrability permits Fubini’s theorem, and therefore
$$ \mathbb E\!\left[\int_S x\,d\mu\right] =\sum_i\frac{q_i}{p_i}\int_{I_i}x\,d\mu =c\cdot q. $$
Every selected coalition satisfies its core inequality. Averaging gives $c\cdot q\geq f(q)$.

For rationality, let $\mathcal V$ contain all vertices of the subdivision cells. On each cell, $c\cdot q-f(q)$ is affine, so its nonnegativity at the vertices implies its nonnegativity throughout the cell. Consequently the core density vectors are exactly the solutions of the finite rational system
$$ c_i\geq a_i,\qquad c\cdot p=V,\qquad c\cdot v\geq f(v)\quad(v\in\mathcal V). $$
This system defines a nonempty bounded polyhedron: efficiency and the lower bounds imply
$$ c_i\leq \frac{V-\sum_{j\ne i}p_ja_j}{p_i}. $$
A nonempty bounded rational polyhedron has a rational vertex, obtained by solving a nonsingular rational subsystem of active constraints. Hence a rational core density vector exists. $\square$

2. A finite reduction that preserves every block

Choose an integer $D\geq2$ such that all type masses and initial interval endpoints are multiples of
$$ h=\frac1D. $$
Partition the society into intervals of length $h$. There are exactly $N=D$ such intervals. In this section these intervals are the players of a finite auxiliary game; they remain nonatomic intervals in the original problem.

For a grid interval $j$ of type $i(j)$, put
$$ b_j=\max\{a_{i(j)}h,\ f(he_{i(j)})\}. $$
For a set $A$ of grid intervals, let $q(A)$ be its type-mass vector. Define the finite game by
$$ w(\varnothing)=0,\qquad w(\{j\})=b_j,\qquad w(A)=f(q(A))\quad(|A|\geq2). $$
Because $N\geq2$, its grand-coalition worth is $V$.

2.1 Nonemptiness of the finite core

Let $c$ be supplied by Lemma 1, and pay grid interval $j$ the total $hc_{i(j)}$. These payments are efficient and satisfy
$$ hc_{i(j)}\geq a_{i(j)}h,\qquad hc_{i(j)}\geq f(he_{i(j)}). $$
Every larger coalition receives $c\cdot q(A)\geq f(q(A))$. Thus the finite game has nonempty core. In particular,
$$ \sum_j b_j\leq V. $$

2.2 Repairing the starting allocation

Let $z_j$ be the total payment currently assigned to grid interval $j$. Initially, the allocation is constant on each such interval.

If $z_j<b_j$, original individual rationality implies
$$ b_j=f(he_{i(j)}). $$
Raise $z_j$ to $b_j$, financing the increase by decreasing payments to other intervals only while their payments remain at least their respective $b_\ell$.

There is sufficient surplus, since
$$ \sum_\ell(z_\ell-b_\ell)^+ = V-\sum_\ell b_\ell+\sum_\ell(b_\ell-z_\ell)^+ \geq b_j-z_j. $$
A greedy withdrawal from donors uses rational arithmetic.

This is an original block by interval $j$: its density strictly increases, and its new total payment equals its worth. Outsiders remain individually rational, and total payment remains $V$. Once an interval has been repaired, it never becomes deficient again. At most $N$ blocks therefore produce a finite-game imputation.

2.3 Applying the finite theorem and obtaining rational output

We use the finite-game accessibility theorem stated in the question. It applies to weak improvement on the blocking coalition, with at least one strict improvement, coalition payment equal to worth, and intermediate allocations satisfying finite-game individual rationality.

A conservative player-only path bound is
$$ B(N)=2^{\,N+5^N N^N}. $$
Indeed, the classification in Appendix A uses at most $5^NN^N$ Boolean comparisons: each player has five category choices, and the relevant category has at most $N^N$ partitions. Combining their truth patterns with the gaining sets gives at most $B(N)$ classes; the paper’s shortening argument bounds path length by this class count. These are the finite results being invoked. [Kóczy, Theorem 3.3 and Appendix A](https://real.mtak.hu/74945/1/d968f198969e3abfcaa2f13f521776bce463.pdf)

For completeness, rational intermediate allocations can be obtained without assuming that the theorem’s original construction is rational. Enumerate coalition sequences $A_1,\ldots,A_r$ with $r\leq B(N)$.

Fix $z^0$ to the repaired starting vector. For a prescribed sequence, introduce payment variables $z_j^t$ and a variable $0\leq\eta\leq1$. Impose, for $1\leq t\leq r$,
$$ z_j^t\geq b_j,\qquad \sum_jz_j^t=V, $$
$$ z_j^t\geq z_j^{t-1}\quad(j\in A_t),\qquad \sum_{j\in A_t}z_j^t=w(A_t), $$
$$ \sum_{j\in A_t}(z_j^t-z_j^{t-1})\geq\eta. $$
Impose every finite-core inequality on $z^r$, and maximize $\eta$.

For $r>0$, a path with this coalition sequence exists exactly when the optimum is positive. The forward implication follows by taking the minimum of its finitely many positive coalition gains, capped at $1$. The reverse implication follows directly from the constraints. Payment variables are bounded by their lower bounds and efficiency, so a positive optimum has a rational optimizing solution. Length zero is checked separately.

Every finite block lifts to an original block by assigning constant densities on grid intervals. A singleton cannot block a finite imputation: its old payment is already at least $b_j$, whereas its new payment would have to equal $b_j$. Thus every actual finite block uses an original coalition worth $f(q(A_t))$.

We have proved:

Proposition 2. For every such grid, a uniform primitive-recursive algorithm constructs a rational original blocking path, of length at most
$$ N+B(N), $$
whose endpoint belongs to the finite-grid core.

This proposition alone does not put the endpoint in the nonatomic core.

3. Uniform approximate accessibility for arbitrary subdivisions

For $\varepsilon>0$, call an original imputation an $\varepsilon$-core imputation if
$$ \int_Sx\,d\mu\geq f(q(S))-\varepsilon $$
for every measurable coalition $S$.

Write the affine formula on subdivision cell $P$ as
$$ f(q)=\alpha^P\cdot q+\beta^P, $$
and define the rational number
$$ K=\max_{P,i}|\alpha_i^P|. $$

Lemma 3. For all $q,q'\in Q$,
$$ |f(q)-f(q')|\leq K\sum_i|q_i-q_i'|. $$

Proof. First vary one coordinate while holding the others fixed. Along this segment, continuity and the finite subdivision make $f$ a continuous piecewise-affine function whose slopes have absolute value at most $K$. Its change is therefore at most $K$ times the segment length. Vary the coordinates successively and apply the triangle inequality. $\square$

Now let $x$ be constant on grid intervals and belong to the finite-grid core. For each type, sort its grid intervals by nondecreasing density. Let $g_i(t)$ be the payment obtained by selecting type-$i$ mass $t$ from the cheapest intervals first, allowing a partial final interval. Then
$$ G(q)=\sum_i g_i(q_i) $$
is the minimum payment among coalitions of mass vector $q$.

To justify this minimum, if selected mass lies in a more expensive interval while an equally large amount remains unselected in a cheaper one, exchanging these amounts cannot increase payment. Repeating these exchanges yields the stated selection.

Each $g_i$ has breakpoints at multiples of $h$. Thus $G$ is affine on each elementary grid box. At every grid vertex $v$, a cheapest coalition is a union of whole grid intervals, and therefore
$$ G(v)\geq f(v). $$
For a singleton vertex this follows from $b_j\geq f(he_{i(j)})$; the empty vertex follows from $f(0)=0$.

Fix a point $q$ in an elementary grid box. Independently round coordinate $q_i$ to the lower or upper endpoint of its grid interval, choosing probabilities so that the resulting random vertex $W$ satisfies $\mathbb E W_i=q_i$. Affinity of $G$ gives
$$ G(q)=\mathbb E G(W)\geq\mathbb E f(W). $$
Write $q_i$ as the lower endpoint plus $\theta_i h$, where $0\leq\theta_i\leq1$. Then
$$ \mathbb E|W_i-q_i| =2h\theta_i(1-\theta_i)\leq\frac h2. $$
Lemma 3 consequently gives
$$ G(q)\geq f(q)-K\sum_i\mathbb E|W_i-q_i| \geq f(q)-\frac{K\tau}{2D}. $$
Every coalition receives at least $G$ at its mass vector.

Theorem 4. For every promised input and every positive rational $\varepsilon$, a uniform primitive-recursive algorithm outputs a finite rational blocking path to an $\varepsilon$-core imputation.

Proof. Choose a grid aligning the initial endpoints and type masses, with
$$ D\geq2,\qquad D\geq\frac{K\tau}{2\varepsilon}. $$
Apply Proposition 2 and the preceding estimate. $\square$

All steps of this path are exact blocks. Only the terminal core condition is approximate.

4. A new exact sufficient condition

Consider the following condition on $f$:

There exist mass vectors $q^1,\ldots,q^s\in Q$ and positive weights $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_s$ such that
$$ \sum_{r=1}^{s}\lambda_rq^r=p,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^{s}\lambda_rf(q^r)=V, $$
and, for every supported type $i$, at least one vector satisfies
$$ 0<q_i^r<p_i. $$

The weights need not sum to $1$. The first equality is a balancing equality, rather than a convex-combination requirement.

4.1 Why this condition makes the finite reduction exact

First suppose the vectors and weights are rational. Choose $D$ aligning:

  • the type masses and initial endpoints;
  • all coordinates of the $q^r$;
  • all subdivision vertices.

Let $z$ be any finite-grid core allocation, and put
$$ Z_i=\sum_{j:\,i(j)=i}z_j,\qquad n_i=Dp_i. $$

For each $r$, consider all coalitions choosing exactly $Dq_i^r$ grid intervals from type $i$. Every such coalition receives at least $f(q^r)$. Its uniformly averaged payment is
$$ M_r=\sum_i\frac{q_i^r}{p_i}Z_i, $$
because a type-$i$ interval is selected with probability $q_i^r/p_i$.

The balancing equalities imply
$$ \sum_r\lambda_rM_r = \sum_i\frac{Z_i}{p_i}\sum_r\lambda_rq_i^r = \sum_iZ_i = V = \sum_r\lambda_rf(q^r). $$
Each $M_r-f(q^r)$ is nonnegative and each $\lambda_r$ is positive. Hence
$$ M_r=f(q^r)\quad\text{for every }r. $$
Every coalition in the finite averaging family must therefore receive exactly $f(q^r)$.

Fix a type $i$, and choose $r$ with $0<q_i^r<p_i$. Its selected interval count lies strictly between $0$ and $n_i$. Given two intervals of this type, one can choose a coalition in the averaging family containing the first and excluding the second. Swapping those two intervals preserves its mass vector. Both coalitions have equal payment, so the two intervals have equal payment.

Thus $z$ is constant within every type. Write its lifted densities as $c_i$. Every subdivision vertex is a grid mass vector, and its finite-core inequality gives
$$ c\cdot v\geq f(v). $$
Individual rationality and efficiency also hold. Affine interpolation on each subdivision cell now proves $c\cdot q\geq f(q)$ throughout $Q$.

Theorem 5. Under the nonempty-core promise, the balanced condition above implies a uniform primitive-recursive algorithm producing a finite rational blocking path to the exact nonatomic core.

Proof. Apply Proposition 2 on the aligned grid. The preceding argument proves that its endpoint belongs to the nonatomic core. $\square$

The half-mass condition is a special case: take $q^1=p/2$ and $\lambda_1=2$.

4.2 The condition is effectively decidable

For every nonempty face $F$ of every subdivision cell, let $b_F$ be the arithmetic mean of its vertices. Form the finite set
$$ \mathcal B=\{b_F\}\setminus\{0\}. $$
Every candidate is rational and belongs to the relative interior of its face.

Solve the following rational linear program, with variables $\gamma_b$ and $\eta$:
$$ \gamma_b\geq0,\qquad 0\leq\eta\leq1, $$
$$ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\gamma_b b=p,\qquad \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\gamma_b f(b)=V, $$
$$ \sum_{\substack{b\in\mathcal B\\0<b_i<p_i}}\gamma_b\geq\eta \quad(1\leq i\leq\tau), $$
maximizing $\eta$.

The variables are bounded. Indeed, summing the coordinate balancing equalities yields
$$ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\gamma_b\sum_i b_i=1, $$
and every retained $b$ has positive total mass. Hence
$$ \gamma_b\leq\frac1{\sum_i b_i}. $$
The program is feasible with $\eta=0$, by using the candidate $p$ alone.

A positive optimum supplies the required rational family: retain the candidates having positive weight.

Conversely, suppose any finite balanced family satisfies the condition, even with real coordinates and weights. Delete zero vectors, which contribute neither mass nor worth. For each remaining vector $q$, choose a subdivision cell containing it and its minimal face $F$ containing $q$. Then $q$ lies in the relative interior of $F$.

If $F$ is not a singleton, there is an $\alpha>0$ such that
$$ q=\alpha b_F+(1-\alpha)q',\qquad q'\in F. $$
This follows because a sufficiently small displacement from $q$ away from $b_F$ remains in $F$. Express $q'$ as a convex combination of vertices of $F$. Since $f$ is affine on $F$, this replacement preserves both the contributed mass vector and the contributed worth.

If $0<q_i<p_i$, then $0<(b_F)_i<p_i$. For example, if $(b_F)_i=0$, every vertex has coordinate zero, forcing $q_i=0$; the argument at $p_i$ is identical. Thus the replacement preserves positive partial coverage of every relevant type.

Replacing all vectors produces feasible candidate weights with strictly positive coverage for every type, so the optimum is positive. Rational linear programming then yields a rational certificate.

This proves that the condition is decidable by a finite rational linear program. A zero optimum means only that this sufficient condition fails; it does not establish failure of accessibility.

4.3 An example beyond the previous conditions

Take
$$ \tau=2,\qquad p_1=p_2=\frac12,\qquad a_1=a_2=0, $$
and define
$$ f(q)=\max\left\{ 0,\ q_1+q_2-\left|q_1-\frac14\right|-\left|q_2-\frac12\right|,\ q_1+q_2-\left|q_1-\frac12\right|-\left|q_2-\frac14\right|,\ 2(q_1+q_2)-1 \right\}. $$
This is continuous rational piecewise affine. Every displayed term is at most $q_1+q_2$ on the domain, and
$$ f(0)=0,\qquad f(p)=1. $$
Thus density $(1,1)$ is a core allocation.

Set
$$ q^1=\left(\frac14,\frac12\right),\qquad q^2=\left(\frac12,\frac14\right),\qquad \lambda_1=\lambda_2=\frac23. $$
Direct substitution gives
$$ f(q^1)=f(q^2)=\frac34, $$
so both balancing equalities hold, and each type is partially selected by one vector.

The previous half-mass condition fails:
$$ f(p/2)=\frac14\ne\frac12. $$
The function is not concave, by this same inequality between $0$ and $p$. It is not convex either, since
$$ f(0,1/2)=\frac14,\qquad f(1/2,1/2)=1,\qquad f(1/4,1/2)=\frac34>\frac58. $$
Finally, on the open square $(1/4,3/8)^2$,
$$ f(q)=\max\{2q_1-1/4,\ 2q_2-1/4\}. $$
Its kink along $q_1=q_2$ prevents affinity on any finite subdivision solely by coordinate hyperplanes. Theorem 5 therefore covers inputs beyond the previously established classes.

5. Running-time bounds

Define
$$ \operatorname{exp}_1(t)=2^t,\qquad \operatorname{exp}_{r+1}(t)=2^{\operatorname{exp}_r(t)}, \qquad n=m+\tau+k+L. $$

For a specified grid with $N=D$, coalition-sequence enumeration and rational linear programming give the bound
$$ (B(N)+1)\,2^{NB(N)} \operatorname{poly}\!\left(2^N,NB(N),m,\tau,k,L,\log D\right). $$
There are at most $(B(N)+1)2^{NB(N)}$ sequences. Each program has $O(NB(N))$ variables and $O(NB(N)+2^N)$ constraints. Rational arithmetic in the preliminary repairs has polynomial bit complexity in $N$ and the grid-game encoding length.

For Theorem 4, let $E$ be the binary length of the positive rational tolerance $\varepsilon$. The product $D_0$ of the input endpoint and mass denominators satisfies $D_0\leq2^L$. Take
$$ D=D_0\max\left\{2,\ \left\lceil\frac{K\tau}{2D_0\varepsilon}\right\rceil \right\}. $$
The encoded coefficients give $K\leq2^L$, and $\varepsilon^{-1}\leq2^E$. Consequently
$$ \log D=O(L+E+\log(\tau+1)). $$
For a sufficiently large absolute constant $C$, the complete approximate-accessibility algorithm runs in time at most
$$ \operatorname{exp}_4((n+E)^C). $$

For Theorem 5 and its recognition procedure, face and vertex enumeration also have primitive-recursive bounds. With inequality descriptions, a cell with $H$ inequalities has at most $H^\tau$ candidate vertices and at most $2^H$ faces. Vertex coordinates have polynomial bit length by determinant bounds. A face centroid can be computed by summing its vertices.

Since the explicitly encoded inequality count is bounded by $L+2\tau$, these estimates bound both the number of candidates and their bit lengths by $\operatorname{exp}_1(n^C)$. A common grid denominator containing all candidates and subdivision vertices then has logarithm bounded by $\operatorname{exp}_1(n^C)$. Substituting into the displayed path-search bound gives
$$ \operatorname{exp}_5(n^C). $$
For explicitly listed vertex descriptions, facet enumeration can add an exponential level. A common conservative bound covering either standard description is therefore
$$ \operatorname{exp}_6(n^C). $$
These bounds include recognition of the balanced condition and construction of the exact path when recognition succeeds.

6. The obstruction persists at arbitrarily fine grid scales

Use the fixed input data
$$ \tau=2,\qquad p_1=p_2=\frac12,\qquad a_1=a_2=0, $$
and
$$ f(q_1,q_2)= \max\left\{\min(q_1,2q_2),\ 2(q_1+q_2)-\frac23\right\}. $$
Comparisons between the finitely many rational affine expressions produce a rational polyhedral subdivision. The function is continuous, $f(0)=0$, and $V=4/3$.

The density vector
$$ c=\left(\frac53,1\right) $$
is a core allocation. It dominates the first expression because
$$ \min(q_1,2q_2)\leq q_1\leq\frac53q_1+q_2. $$
Its difference from the second expression is
$$ \frac23-\frac13q_1-q_2\geq0 $$
throughout the domain, and its total payment is $4/3$.

Let $D\geq12$ be any even integer and set $h=1/D$. Define
$$ d_h=\frac{5/3}{1-2h}. $$
Then $5/3\leq d_h\leq2$. Assign density zero to type-1 mass $h$, density $d_h$ to the rest of type 1, and density $1$ throughout type 2. This rational three-interval allocation is individually rational and efficient:
$$ d_h(1/2-h)+1/2=\frac56+\frac12=\frac43. $$
Its cheapest-payment function is
$$ G_h(q)=d_h\max(q_1-h,0)+q_2. $$

At a grid point $q=(rh,sh)$,
$$ \frac{G_h(q)}h=d_h\max(r-1,0)+s. $$
If $r=0$ or $s=0$, this is at least $\min(r,2s)$. If $r,s\geq1$, then
$$ d_h(r-1)+s\geq(r-1)+1=r\geq\min(r,2s). $$
Thus every grid point satisfies the inequality against the first expression defining $f$.

For $q_1\geq h$, the difference against the second expression is
$$ (d_h-2)q_1-d_hh-q_2+\frac23. $$
Since $d_h\leq2$, its minimum occurs at $q_1=q_2=1/2$, where it equals
$$ d_h(1/2-h)-\frac56=0. $$
For $q_1\leq h$, the difference is
$$ \frac23-2q_1-q_2\geq\frac16-2h\geq0. $$
Therefore every union of whole grid intervals satisfies its coalition inequality. The modified singleton worths are zero, so this allocation is also a finite-grid core imputation.

Nevertheless, at the rational mass vector $(h,h/2)$,
$$ G_h(h,h/2)=\frac h2,\qquad f(h,h/2)=h. $$
The coalition consisting of the zero-paid type-1 interval and type-2 mass $h/2$ violates its core inequality by $h/2$.

This proves that, for one fixed worth function, every even grid denominator $D\geq12$ admits a finite-grid core allocation outside the nonatomic core. In particular, one may take arbitrarily large denominators divisible by all subdivision-vertex denominators.

It also shows that the error in Theorem 4 cannot be replaced by $o(h)$ uniformly over finite-grid core allocations, even for this fixed function.

7. Remaining gap

Approximate accessibility does not establish exact accessibility: the preceding family has a strictly positive missed deficit at every grid scale.

The balanced condition supplies a decidable exact subclass, but the argument does not show that every promised worth function satisfies it. When its linear program has optimum zero, neither the existence of a finite exact path nor a primitive-recursive bound has been established.
