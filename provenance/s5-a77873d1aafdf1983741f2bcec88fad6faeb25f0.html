The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) sites, a piece limit \(k\), rational population mass \(N\ge 1\), and \(\tau\) types with masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(N\) and rational per-unit values \(v_{tj}\), assign each type’s mass to bundles \(z\in[0,N]^m\) with at most \(k\) positive entries. Each assigned bundle must give type \(t\) value at least \(\rho N\sum_j v_{tj}\), and total assigned land at each site must not exceed \(N\). What is the largest \(\rho\) guaranteed for every such instance—is it \(\min\{1/N,k/(m+N-1)\}\)—and can an attaining assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k\) and the input bit length, independent of the numeric value of \(N\)?

Partial resolution: the proposed formula is false

The distribution-over-types model has a different sharp guarantee from the finite-agent model. I prove the complete formula
$$ r(4,3,N)= \begin{cases} 1-N/4,&1\le N\le2,\\ 1/N,&N\ge2. \end{cases} $$
For these parameters, an assignment attaining the guarantee can be computed in polynomial time, independently of the numeric value of $N$.

In particular,
$$ r(4,3,5/4)=\frac{11}{16} < \frac{12}{17} = \min\left\{\frac{1}{5/4},\frac{3}{4+5/4-1}\right\}. $$
Thus the proposed general expression is refuted. The general formula and the complexity of attaining its actual sharp guarantee remain unresolved here.

1. Normalization and reduction to one valuation vector

Write
$$ x_{t\ell j}=\frac{z_{t\ell j}}{N}, \qquad V_t=\sum_jv_{tj}. $$
The assignment conditions become
$$ x_{t\ell}\in[0,1]^m, \qquad |\{j:x_{t\ell j}>0\}|\le k, \qquad v_t\cdot x_{t\ell}\ge\rho V_t, $$
and
$$ \sum_{t,\ell}a_{t\ell}x_{t\ell j}\le1 \quad\text{for every }j. $$
Thus normalized site capacity is $1$, and population mass remains $N$.

A useful sufficient condition is the following. Suppose that for each nonzero valuation vector $v$ there are bundles $x^\ell$ and weights $\alpha_\ell>0$ satisfying
$$ \sum_\ell\alpha_\ell=N, \qquad v\cdot x^\ell\ge\rho\sum_jv_j, \qquad \sum_\ell\alpha_\ell x^\ell_j\le1. $$
For a type of mass $\mu_t$, use these bundles with submasses
$$ a_{t\ell}=\frac{\mu_t}{N}\alpha_\ell. $$
Their submasses sum to $\mu_t$, and their consumption at each normalized site is at most $\mu_t/N$. Summing over types gives capacity at most $1$.

This construction preserves fairness for every bundle. A type whose valuation vector is zero can receive one zero bundle.

We will first establish existence of such templates over the reals, and subsequently obtain rational assignments through a finite rational linear program.

2. A family of upper bounds using two valuable sites

The following obstruction extends beyond the special case solved below.

Proposition. Suppose $k\ge2$, $m\ge k+1$, and $1\le N<2$. Then
$$ r(m,k,N)\le \frac{N+(k-1)(2-N)} {2N+(m-2)(2-N)}. $$

Proof. Consider one type of mass $N$ with valuation vector
$$ v=(B,B,1,\ldots,1), \qquad B=\frac{N}{2-N}\ge1. $$
Its total value is
$$ V=2B+m-2. $$
Set
$$ q_0=B+k-1. $$

Suppose an assignment gives every normalized bundle utility at least $q>q_0$.

A bundle using at most one of the first two sites has utility at most $B+k-1$: it uses at most $k$ coordinates, all remaining coordinates have value $1$, and $B\ge1$. Therefore every assigned bundle must use both first sites.

Such a bundle has at most $k-2$ other positive coordinates. Hence
$$ B(x_1+x_2)+(k-2)\ge q>B+k-1, $$
which implies
$$ x_1+x_2>1+\frac1B=\frac2N. $$
Because the assignment has finitely many bundles with positive submasses summing to $N$,
$$ \sum_\ell a_\ell(x_{\ell1}+x_{\ell2})>2. $$
This contradicts the combined normalized capacity $2$ of the first two sites.

Consequently no fraction greater than $q_0/V$ is attainable for this society. Substituting the value of $B$ gives the stated bound. All masses and valuations used here are permitted rational inputs. $\square$

For $m=4$ and $k=3$, this simplifies to
$$ r(4,3,N)\le1-\frac N4 \qquad(1\le N<2). $$

There is also a universal upper bound
$$ r(m,k,N)\le\frac1N. $$
Indeed, for one type of mass $N$ valuing only site $1$, every fair normalized bundle satisfies $x_1\ge\rho$. Capacity then forces $N\rho\le1$.

These observations prove the upper bounds needed for the claimed formula for $r(4,3,N)$.

For a concrete counterexample, take $N=5/4$ and
$$ v=(5,5,3,3). $$
A bundle omitting either of the first two sites has utility at most $11$. Any bundle of utility greater than $11$ therefore uses both first sites and satisfies
$$ 5(x_1+x_2)+3>11, \qquad x_1+x_2>\frac85. $$
Integrating over population mass $5/4$ violates their combined capacity $2$. Thus no fraction greater than $11/16$ is attainable.

3. An auxiliary price inequality on three sites

We need an elementary consequence of a two-bundle construction.

Lemma. Let $c,w\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^3$, and let
$$ Q=\sum_jc_j\ge2. $$
Suppose
$$ w\cdot y\le a $$
for every $y\in[0,1]^3$ with at most two positive coordinates and $c\cdot y\le1$. Then
$$ \sum_jw_j\le Qa. $$

Proof. The conclusion is immediate if $w=0$. Otherwise reorder coordinates so that
$$ w_1\ge w_2\ge w_3, \qquad W=w_1+w_2+w_3>0. $$
Both $w_2$ and $w_3$ are at most $W/2$. Define
$$ y^A= \left(\frac{W/2-w_2}{w_1},1,0\right), \qquad y^B= \left(\frac{W/2-w_3}{w_1},0,1\right). $$
The first coordinates are nonnegative and sum to $1$, so both bundles lie in the box. They use at most two sites and satisfy
$$ w\cdot y^A=w\cdot y^B=\frac W2, \qquad y^A+y^B=(1,1,1). $$
Their prices sum to $Q$. At least one, say $y$, has price at most $Q/2$.

The bundle $(2/Q)y$ still lies in the box, uses at most two sites, and has price at most $1$. Its utility is $W/Q$. The hypothesis therefore gives $W/Q\le a$. $\square$

4. The four-site price inequality

This is the main lower-bound lemma.

Lemma. Let $p,u\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^4$, with
$$ P=\sum_jp_j\in[1,2]. $$
Suppose
$$ u\cdot x\le1 $$
for every $x\in[0,1]^4$ with at most three positive coordinates and $p\cdot x\le1$. Then
$$ U:=\sum_ju_j\le\frac4{4-P}. $$

Proof. Put
$$ H=\{j:p_j\ge P-1\}. $$
We distinguish the four possible ranges of $|H|$.

Case 1: $|H|=0$

For every $i$, the three-site bundle with coordinates
$$ x_j=\frac1{P-p_i}\quad(j\ne i), \qquad x_i=0 $$
is admissible: $P-p_i>1$, and its price is $1$. Therefore
$$ U-u_i\le P-p_i. $$
Summing over all four indices gives
$$ 3U\le3P, \qquad U\le P. $$

Case 2: $|H|\ge3$

For every $i\in H$, the bundle consisting of all three other coordinates at amount $1$ has price $P-p_i\le1$. Hence
$$ u_i\ge U-1. $$
Choose three distinct indices in $H$. The bundle allocating amount $1/P$ to these three coordinates is admissible, so their utilities sum to at most $P$. Consequently
$$ 3(U-1)\le P, \qquad U\le1+\frac P3. $$

Case 3: $|H|=1$

Relabel so that $H=\{1\}$, and write
$$ P_S=p_2+p_3+p_4, \qquad s=u_2+u_3+u_4, \qquad \lambda=\frac{u_1}{p_1}. $$
Here $p_1>0$. Since $P_S=P-p_1\le1$, the full bundle on sites $2,3,4$ is admissible. Thus
$$ s\le1, \qquad u_1\ge U-1. $$

For any pair $i,j$ of these three sites, use the bundle
$$ x_i=x_j=1, \qquad x_1=\frac{1-p_i-p_j}{p_1}. $$
It is admissible. Its first coordinate is nonnegative because $p_i+p_j\le P_S\le1$, and is less than $1$ because the omitted low-price coordinate has price less than $P-1$.

The utility inequality is
$$ u_i+u_j+\lambda(1-p_i-p_j)\le1. $$
Summing the three pair inequalities yields
$$ 2s+\lambda(3-2P_S)\le3. $$
Therefore
$$ U=\lambda p_1+s \le\frac32+\lambda\left(P-\frac32\right). $$

First suppose $P\le3/2$, and put $a=3/2-P\ge0$. Using $u_1\ge U-1$,
$$ U\le\frac32-a\frac{U-1}{p_1}. $$
Hence
$$ U\le\frac{(3/2)p_1+a}{p_1+a} \le1+\frac P3. $$
The last inequality follows by multiplying by $p_1+a>0$: the difference between its right and left numerators is $a(P-p_1)/3\ge0$.

Now suppose $P\ge3/2$. If $\lambda\le1$, the preceding bound gives $U\le P$.

If $\lambda>1$, the admissible one-site bundle implies $p_1<1$. Define
$$ \delta=1-p_1>0, \qquad \varepsilon=1-u_1\ge0. $$
Since $u_1>p_1$, we have $\varepsilon<\delta$.

For any bundle $y$ on the other three sites using at most two coordinates and having price at most $\delta$, the bundle $(1,y)$ is admissible in the original four-site problem. Thus
$$ (u_2,u_3,u_4)\cdot y\le\varepsilon. $$
Rescale those prices by $1/\delta$. Their total price is
$$ Q=\frac{P_S}{\delta}\ge2. $$
Indeed,
$$ P_S-2\delta=P+p_1-2 \ge2P-3\ge0. $$
The auxiliary lemma gives
$$ s\le\varepsilon\frac{P_S}{\delta}. $$
Consequently
$$ U\le1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon\frac{P_S}{\delta} =1+\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}(P-1) \le P. $$

Case 4: $|H|=2$

Relabel so that $H=\{1,2\}$, and put
$$ P_H=p_1+p_2, \qquad P_S=p_3+p_4, \qquad h=u_1+u_2, \qquad s=u_3+u_4. $$
The full bundles on $\{1,3,4\}$ and $\{2,3,4\}$ are admissible. Hence
$$ u_1+s\le1, \qquad u_2+s\le1, \qquad h\le2(1-s). $$
It follows that
$$ U=h+s\le1+\frac h2. $$

For each $j\in\{3,4\}$, use amount $1$ at site $j$ and amount
$$ \frac{1-p_j}{P_H} $$
at each high-price site. This amount lies in $(0,1)$: the other low-price coordinate has price less than $P-1$, so $P_H+p_j>1$. The bundle has price $1$, giving
$$ u_j+\frac{1-p_j}{P_H}h\le1. $$
Summing these two inequalities gives
$$ s+\frac{2-P_S}{P_H}h\le2, $$
and therefore
$$ U\le2-\frac{2-P}{P_H}h. $$

The first bound on $U$ is increasing in $h$, and the second is nonincreasing. Their intersection gives
$$ U\le1+\frac{P_H}{P_H+4-2P} \le1+\frac P{4-P} =\frac4{4-P}. $$
For the second inequality, use $P_H\le P$ and $4-2P\ge0$.

Finally, the bounds from the other cases also imply the claimed conclusion, since
$$ \frac4{4-P}-P=\frac{(P-2)^2}{4-P}\ge0 $$
and
$$ \frac4{4-P}-\left(1+\frac P3\right) =\frac{P(P-1)}{3(4-P)}\ge0. $$
This completes the proof. $\square$

5. Attainment for four sites when $1\le N\le2$

Fix a nonzero valuation vector $v\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^4$, and write
$$ V=\sum_jv_j, \qquad \rho=1-\frac N4, \qquad C=\frac1\rho=\frac4{4-N}. $$

5.1. Every nonnegative price vector admits an inexpensive fair bundle

Let $p\ge0$ be nonzero, and put $P_0=\sum_jp_j$. Rescale it to
$$ c=\frac N{P_0}p, \qquad \sum_jc_j=N. $$
Let $A$ be the maximum of $v\cdot x$ over box bundles using at most three sites and satisfying $c\cdot x\le1$.

The feasible set is a finite union of compact sets, so the maximum exists. Also $A>0$, since a sufficiently small positive amount of a positively valued coordinate is feasible.

Apply the four-site price lemma to $c$ and $u=v/A$. Its hypothesis holds by the definition of $A$. Therefore
$$ \frac VA\le C, \qquad A\ge\rho V. $$
A maximizing bundle consequently satisfies
$$ v\cdot x\ge\rho V, \qquad p\cdot x\le\frac{P_0}{N}. $$

5.2. Separation produces a distribution of individually fair bundles

Let
$$ F=\{x\in[0,1]^4: |\{j:x_j>0\}|\le3,\ v\cdot x\ge\rho V\}, $$
and let $K=\operatorname{conv}(F)$.

The set $F$ is compact and nonempty. For nonemptiness, the three largest valuation coordinates sum to at least $3V/4$, while $\rho\le3/4$.

We claim that some $y\in K$ satisfies
$$ y_j\le\frac1N \quad\text{for every }j. $$
Suppose otherwise. The set
$$ D=K+\mathbb R_{\ge0}^4 $$
is closed and convex; closedness follows from compactness of $K$. The point
$$ b=(1/N,1/N,1/N,1/N) $$
does not belong to $D$.

Strong separation gives a nonzero vector $p$ such that
$$ p\cdot b<\inf_{x\in D}p\cdot x. $$
Necessarily $p\ge0$: a negative coordinate would make the infimum over the corresponding upward ray equal to negative infinity. Thus separation says
$$ \frac{\sum_jp_j}{N}<\min_{x\in K}p\cdot x. $$
This contradicts Section 5.1, which supplies a point of $F\subseteq K$ with price at most the left side.

Therefore $y\in K$ with $y\le b$ exists. Express it as a finite convex combination
$$ y=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell x^\ell, \qquad \lambda_\ell>0, \qquad \sum_\ell\lambda_\ell=1, \qquad x^\ell\in F. $$
The weights $\alpha_\ell=N\lambda_\ell$ form the template from Section 1. Every bundle is individually fair, and every normalized site has load at most $1$.

5.3. Rational output and polynomial running time

For each nonzero type $t$, put $q_t=\rho V_t$. For every support $S$ of size at most three, form the rational polytope
$$ P_{tS}= \{x\in[0,1]^4: x_j=0\text{ for }j\notin S,\ v_t\cdot x\ge q_t\}. $$

Its vertices are among:

  • feasible zero-one points;
  • points with exactly one fractional coordinate, obtained by setting utility equal to $q_t$ and fixing all other coordinates to $0$ or $1$.

To justify this enumeration, a point with two fractional coordinates admits a nonzero perturbation preserving utility equality and all active box constraints, so it cannot be a vertex. If utility inequality is inactive, even one fractional coordinate admits a small perturbation.

A fractional coordinate with valuation coefficient zero also admits an independent perturbation. Accordingly, fractional-coordinate equations are solved only for strictly positive valuation coefficients. This handles zero valuations without division by zero or undetermined candidates.

There are only constantly many vertices per support, all with polynomial rational bit length. Let $X_t$ be their union. For a zero-valued type, set $X_t=\{0\}$.

Solve the rational linear program
$$ a_{tx}\ge0, \qquad \sum_{x\in X_t}a_{tx}=\mu_t, $$
and
$$ \sum_t\sum_{x\in X_t}a_{tx}x_j\le1 \quad(j=1,2,3,4). $$
It is feasible: the real templates just proved can be combined as in Section 1, and every bundle can then be replaced by a convex combination of vertices of its support polytope, preserving its coordinate consumption.

The program has polynomial size and coefficient bit length. Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore produces rational submasses. Discard zero variables and output
$$ z=Nx $$
for every remaining variable.

Each output bundle is individually fair and uses at most three sites. The running time and output length are polynomial in the input bit length and support size.

Together with Section 2, this proves
$$ r(4,3,N)=1-\frac N4 \qquad(1\le N\le2). $$

6. Attainment for four sites when $N\ge2$

Here there is a direct construction.

For a nonzero type, reorder its valuations so that
$$ v_1\ge v_2\ge v_3\ge v_4, \qquad V=\sum_jv_j. $$
We have
$$ v_2\le V/2, \qquad v_3+v_4\le V/2. $$
Define
$$ x^A= \left(\frac{V/2-v_2}{v_1},1,0,0\right), $$
and
$$ x^B= \left(\frac{V/2-v_3-v_4}{v_1},0,1,1\right). $$
Their first coordinates are nonnegative and sum to $1$, so both bundles lie in the box. They satisfy
$$ v\cdot x^A=v\cdot x^B=V/2, \qquad x^A+x^B=(1,1,1,1). $$
Their support sizes are at most two and three.

Give each bundle original size
$$ z^A=2x^A, \qquad z^B=2x^B, $$
with submass $\mu_t/2$. Because $N\ge2$, these bundles lie in $[0,N]^4$. Each has utility $V$, exactly the required utility for fraction $1/N$:
$$ V=\frac1N\,NV. $$
The type consumes $\mu_t$ units at every site. Summing over types gives capacity $N$; zero-valued types may instead receive zero bundles.

This uses two rational bundles per nonzero type and polynomially many rational operations. Combined with the upper bound $1/N$, it proves
$$ r(4,3,N)=\frac1N \qquad(N\ge2). $$

7. Consequences and remaining questions

For every $1<N<2$, the sharp guarantee just proved is strictly smaller than both terms in the proposed expression:
$$ \frac1N-\left(1-\frac N4\right) =\frac{(N-2)^2}{4N}>0, $$
and
$$ \frac3{N+3}-\left(1-\frac N4\right) =\frac{N(N-1)}{4(N+3)}>0. $$
Thus the discrepancy holds throughout a nontrivial parameter interval.

The general two-valuable-site upper bound in Section 2 supplies further obstructions that any replacement formula must accommodate.

What remains unresolved is the exact universal guarantee for general parameters, and whether an assignment attaining that actual guarantee always admits an algorithm polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L$. The polynomial algorithm proved here settles that computational question for $m=4$, $k=3$ only; no general hardness conclusion follows from it.
