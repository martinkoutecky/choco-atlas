The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given colleges \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_u\), each type specifies a preference list and integer score \(s_{tu}\) at each college. For a nonnegative integer cutoff vector \(\ell\), all mass of type \(t\) goes to its first listed college \(u\) with \(s_{tu}\ge\ell_u\), or remains unmatched. Compute the applicant-best and applicant-worst H-stable cutoff vectors \(\ell_A^H,\ell_C^H\), where H-feasibility requires assigned mass \(x_u(\ell)\le\kappa_u\) and H-stability requires lowering any positive \(\ell_u\) by one to violate that college’s capacity; certify that every H-stable vector \(\ell\) satisfies \(\ell_A^H\le\ell\le\ell_C^H\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\ge1\) colleges with nonnegative binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_u\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Each type has a strict, possibly incomplete preference list of acceptable colleges and a positive integer score \(s_{tu}\) at each listed college, encoded in binary; being unmatched is worse than every listed college. Scores may tie across types, and ties are never broken.
For \(\ell\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{C}\), all mass of type \(t\) is assigned to its first listed college \(u\) satisfying \(s_{tu}\ge\ell_u\), or remains unmatched. Write \(x_u(\ell)\) for the total mass assigned to \(u\).
The vector \(\ell\) is H-stable if \(x_u(\ell)\le\kappa_u\) for every \(u\), and whenever \(\ell_u>0\), replacing only \(\ell_u\) by \(\ell_u-1\) gives assigned mass strictly greater than \(\kappa_u\) at \(u\). All vector inequalities below are coordinatewise.

Does every such society admit H-stable vectors \(\ell_A^H,\ell_C^H\) satisfying \(\ell_A^H\le\ell\le\ell_C^H\) for every H-stable \(\ell\)? Is computing these applicant-best and applicant-worst vectors polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite applicants with integer quotas, applicant-best and applicant-worst H-stable cutoff vectors exist and bound every H-stable vector coordinatewise. [Biró–Kiselgof](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10100-013-0320-9).
For continuum societies with zero mass at every score level, stable matchings correspond to market-clearing cutoffs; that assumption excludes the positive-mass score ties allowed here. [Azevedo–Leshno](https://eduardomazevedo.github.io/papers/Azevedo-Leshno-Supply-and-Demand-Matching-WP.pdf).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified positive, binary-encoded integer scores on acceptable applications, following the discrete score domain.
  • Interpreted complete applicant types as complete descriptions, allowing strict, possibly incomplete preference lists as in the discrete model; every listed college is preferred to being unmatched.
  • Specified nonnegative rational capacities without an upper bound or a restriction on their sum; the supplied discrete definition states no positive-quota condition.
  • Defined L as total binary input length, including masses, capacities, scores, and preference lists.
  • Made existence and coordinatewise extremality part of the question rather than assuming the discrete structural conclusion carries over.

Result

Every society in the question has a coordinatewise least H-stable vector \(\ell_A^H\) and a coordinatewise greatest H-stable vector \(\ell_C^H\). Both can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof uses a monotone map on a finite product of chains. Its iteration bound depends on the number of listed scores, rather than their numerical magnitudes or the denominators of the masses.

1. The smallest feasible cutoff at one college

Fix a college \(u\). For a cutoff vector \(q\), define
\[ P_u(q)=\{t\in T: u\text{ is listed by }t,\text{ and }s_{tv}<q_v \text{ for every }v\text{ preceding }u\text{ on }t\text{’s list}\}. \]

Thus \(P_u(q)\) consists precisely of the types that would choose \(u\) if they qualified there. It depends only on the coordinates other than \(u\).

For a nonnegative integer \(h\), define
\[ d_u(h;q)=\sum_{\substack{t\in P_u(q)\\s_{tu}\ge h}}\mu_t. \]
By the assignment rule, this is the mass assigned to \(u\) when its cutoff is \(h\) and all other cutoffs are as in \(q\). In particular, \(x_u(q)=d_u(q_u;q)\).

The function \(d_u(h;q)\) is nonincreasing in \(h\). Define
\[ F_u(q)=\min\{h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:d_u(h;q)\le\kappa_u\}. \]
This minimum exists: a cutoff exceeding every listed score at \(u\) gives demand zero, and \(\kappa_u\ge0\).

Let \(F(q)=(F_u(q))_{u\in C}\).

Fixed-point characterization. A vector \(\ell\) is H-stable if and only if \(F(\ell)=\ell\).

Proof. Suppose \(\ell\) is H-stable. Feasibility gives \(d_u(\ell_u;\ell)\le\kappa_u\). If \(\ell_u=0\), it is therefore the smallest feasible nonnegative cutoff. If \(\ell_u>0\), H-stability gives \(d_u(\ell_u-1;\ell)>\kappa_u\). Since demand is nonincreasing in the cutoff, every smaller nonnegative cutoff is also infeasible. Hence \(F_u(\ell)=\ell_u\).

Conversely, suppose \(F(\ell)=\ell\). Every coordinate is feasible by the definition of \(F_u\). Whenever \(\ell_u>0\), minimality implies \(d_u(\ell_u-1;\ell)>\kappa_u\), which is exactly the required unilateral-decrease condition. This proves the equivalence. \(\square\)

2. Monotonicity and a finite domain

Monotonicity. If \(q\le r\), then \(F(q)\le F(r)\).

Proof. A type rejected by every college preceding \(u\) under \(q\) is also rejected by those colleges under \(r\). Thus \(P_u(q)\subseteq P_u(r)\), giving \(d_u(h;q)\le d_u(h;r)\) for every \(h\).

Consequently, any cutoff feasible at \(u\) with other cutoffs \(r\) is also feasible with other cutoffs \(q\). Taking the smallest feasible cutoff gives \(F_u(q)\le F_u(r)\). This holds for every \(u\). \(\square\)

For each college define the finite ordered set
\[ D_u=\{0\}\cup\{s_{tu}+1:t\text{ lists }u\}. \]
Repeated values occur only once. Let \(D=\prod_{u\in C}D_u\), and let \(B_u=\max D_u\).

Finite-domain claim. For every \(q\), \(F_u(q)\in D_u\). Consequently, every H-stable vector belongs to \(D\).

Proof. Write \(h=F_u(q)\). If \(h=0\), the claim holds. If \(h>0\), minimality gives \(d_u(h-1;q)>\kappa_u\), whereas \(d_u(h;q)\le\kappa_u\). These demands differ only through types whose score at \(u\) is exactly \(h-1\). Their difference is positive, so at least one such type exists. Hence \(h=s_{tu}+1\in D_u\).

The assertion about H-stable vectors follows from the fixed-point characterization. \(\square\)

In particular, \(F\) maps \(D\) into itself, and every H-stable vector lies between \(0\) and \(B\).

3. Construction of both extrema

Compute the two sequences
\[ a^{(0)}=0,\qquad a^{(k+1)}=F(a^{(k)}), \]
and
\[ c^{(0)}=B,\qquad c^{(k+1)}=F(c^{(k)}). \]
Each update computes all coordinates from the preceding vector.

Because \(F\) takes nonnegative values, \(a^{(0)}\le a^{(1)}\). Monotonicity then proves inductively that \(a^{(k)}\le a^{(k+1)}\) for every \(k\).

Similarly, \(F(B)\le B\), since \(F(B)\in D\). Monotonicity proves inductively that \(c^{(k+1)}\le c^{(k)}\).

Both sequences therefore stabilize in the finite set \(D\). Denote their terminal vectors by \(\ell_A^H\) and \(\ell_C^H\). They are fixed points of \(F\), hence H-stable.

Now take any H-stable vector \(\ell\). We have \(0\le\ell\le B\) and \(F(\ell)=\ell\). Induction using monotonicity gives
\[ a^{(k)}\le\ell\le c^{(k)} \]
for every \(k\). Passing to the terminal vectors proves
\[ \ell_A^H\le\ell\le\ell_C^H. \]

Thus these vectors are respectively the least and greatest H-stable vectors.

Their applicant interpretation follows directly: decreasing cutoffs expands each type’s set of qualifying colleges, so its first qualifying college can only weakly improve. Therefore every type weakly prefers its assignment under \(\ell_A^H\) to its assignment under any H-stable vector, and weakly prefers any H-stable assignment to its assignment under \(\ell_C^H\).

4. Polynomial running time, including bit complexity

Let \(E\) be the total number of preference-list entries. Then \(E\le m\tau\), and
\[ \sum_{u\in C}(|D_u|-1)\le E. \]

Assign each element of \(D_u\) its rank starting at zero. Every nonstationary update of the increasing sequence strictly increases the sum of coordinate ranks. That sum can increase by at most \(E\). The decreasing sequence has the analogous bound. Thus each sequence requires at most \(E\) nonstationary updates and one additional evaluation to detect termination.

To evaluate \(F(q)\), consider each college \(u\):

  • Scan the types’ preference lists to determine \(P_u(q)\).
  • Examine the candidates \(h\in D_u\) in increasing order.
  • Compute \(d_u(h;q)\) and return the first candidate satisfying \(d_u(h;q)\le\kappa_u\).

The finite-domain claim proves that this procedure returns exactly \(F_u(q)\). Across all colleges there are at most \(E+m\) candidates. Even computing each candidate’s demand by a fresh scan uses only polynomially many operations; a crude bound per evaluation of \(F\) is \(O((E+m)(E+\tau))\).

All comparisons can be performed exactly with polynomial bit complexity. Write the input rationals as binary-encoded numerator-denominator pairs, and let \(Q\) be the product of all mass and capacity denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Scaling gives integer weights \(w_t=Q\mu_t\) and integer capacities \(K_u=Q\kappa_u\), each with \(O(L)\) bits. Every demand comparison becomes
\[ \sum_{\substack{t\in P_u(q)\\s_{tu}\ge h}}w_t\le K_u. \]
Since the masses sum to one, \(\sum_t w_t=Q\), so demand sums also have \(O(L)\) bits. Constructing and sorting the candidates \(s_{tu}+1\) likewise takes polynomial time.

Combining these bounds, both extrema are computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No expansion into individual applicants is required.

Verdict

Step-by-step verification

1. Demand at a single college

The definition of \(P_u(q)\) correctly identifies the types for which every acceptable college preceding \(u\) rejects them. Membership depends only on cutoffs at those preceding colleges, so it is independent of \(q_u\).

Consequently, when only the cutoff at \(u\) is replaced by \(h\), a type is assigned to \(u\) exactly when it belongs to \(P_u(q)\) and satisfies \(s_{tu}\ge h\). Thus the stated identity between assigned mass and \(d_u(h;q)\) is correct, including for incomplete preference lists.

The demand function is nonincreasing in \(h\). The minimum defining \(F_u(q)\) exists because there are finitely many listed scores, a sufficiently large cutoff produces zero demand, and capacities are nonnegative. If no type lists \(u\), demand is identically zero and the minimum is zero.

2. Fixed-point characterization

Both directions are valid.

For an H-stable vector \(\ell\), feasibility makes \(\ell_u\) a feasible cutoff. If \(\ell_u=0\), it is automatically the smallest admissible cutoff. If \(\ell_u>0\), the unilateral-decrease condition makes \(\ell_u-1\) infeasible. Monotonicity in the cutoff makes every smaller cutoff infeasible as well. Hence \(F_u(\ell)=\ell_u\).

Conversely, if \(F(\ell)=\ell\), each coordinate is feasible. When \(\ell_u>0\), minimality makes \(\ell_u-1\) infeasible, which means its demand is strictly greater than capacity.

The argument correctly uses the fact that changing the cutoff at \(u\) does not change \(P_u(\ell)\). It therefore establishes exactly the task’s unilateral-decrease condition.

3. Monotonicity of the map

For \(q\le r\), rejection at every college preceding \(u\) under \(q\) implies rejection there under \(r\). Hence
\[ P_u(q)\subseteq P_u(r). \]

Nonnegative masses then imply \(d_u(h;q)\le d_u(h;r)\) for every \(h\). Every cutoff feasible under \(r\) is therefore feasible under \(q\), so the minimum feasible cutoff under \(q\) cannot exceed the minimum under \(r\).

The direction \(F(q)\le F(r)\) is correct. This step does not require breaking score ties.

4. Finite domain

If \(h=F_u(q)>0\), minimality and feasibility give
\[ d_u(h-1;q)>\kappa_u\ge d_u(h;q). \]

Because scores and cutoffs are integers, the difference between these two demands consists exactly of mass with score \(h-1\) at \(u\), among types in \(P_u(q)\). The strict inequality implies that at least one such type exists. Thus \(h=s_{tu}+1\), proving membership in \(D_u\).

The zero case is explicitly included. Repeated scores cause no problem because \(D_u\) is a set.

The fixed-point characterization consequently places every H-stable vector in \(D\), and therefore between \(0\) and \(B\). No possible H-stable vector outside this finite domain is omitted.

5. Construction and extremality

The lower sequence starts with \(0\le F(0)\); monotonicity propagates this inequality through all subsequent updates.

For the upper sequence, \(F(B)\in D\) implies \(F(B)\le B\); monotonicity likewise propagates the decreasing inequalities.

Both sequences are monotone in a finite product of chains, so each eventually has consecutive equal vectors. Such a terminal vector is a fixed point and hence H-stable.

For any H-stable vector \(\ell\), the initial inequalities \(0\le\ell\le B\) hold. Applying monotonicity and \(F(\ell)=\ell\) establishes the stated bounds at each iteration. The terminal vectors therefore bound every H-stable vector coordinatewise.

This proves existence and identifies actual least and greatest elements, not merely minimal and maximal elements.

6. Applicant interpretation

Lowering cutoffs enlarges each type’s set of qualifying acceptable colleges. With its strict preference list held fixed, its first qualifying college can only weakly improve. Becoming matched from an unmatched state is also an improvement under the task’s convention.

Thus the coordinatewise extrema have the claimed applicant-best and applicant-worst interpretations.

7. Polynomial running time and exact arithmetic

The candidate-count bound is correct:
\[ \sum_{u\in C}(|D_u|-1)\le E. \]

Every nonstationary update of a monotone sequence changes at least one coordinate rank strictly in the appropriate direction. The total available rank change is at most \(E\). Hence each sequence has at most \(E\) nonstationary updates, followed by at most one additional evaluation to detect termination. This bound depends on the number of listed entries, not score magnitudes.

Searching the candidates in increasing order returns the true minimum feasible cutoff because that minimum was proved to belong to \(D_u\). Determining the relevant type sets and computing candidate demands by repeated scans takes polynomially many operations. The stated crude bound accommodates those scans, including empty preference lists.

The bit-complexity argument is also valid:

  • The product \(Q\) of the input denominators has bit length bounded by their aggregate encoded length, hence \(O(L)\).
  • Each scaled mass and capacity is an integer with \(O(L)\) bits.
  • Since total mass is one, the sum of all scaled masses is \(Q\); every demand sum consequently has \(O(L)\) bits.
  • Adding one to a binary score and sorting the resulting candidates requires polynomial time.

Thus the arithmetic-operation count is supported by polynomial bit costs. The procedure is polynomial in the specified input parameters.

Edge cases and repair feedback

The proof handles zero capacities, colleges appearing on no list, types with empty lists, tied scores, exact equality between demand and capacity, and the absence of any preference-list entries. Arbitrarily large binary scores and rational denominators do not invalidate the iteration or bit-length bounds.

No external theorem is used critically; the existence and extremality argument is established directly.
