The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite rational provider and requester type sets \(P\) and \(R\), skills \(S\), type-level compatibility, capacities, workloads, time slots, deadlines, bid orders, and complete current-state descriptors including residual capacity, clock, and ordered service history, does the deterministic mass-RPA process reach a fixed point within \(K\) rounds, and what mass schedule and utility result?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: disjoint nonempty provider and requester type lists \(P,R\), each type with positive binary-rational mass \(a_p,b_r\). Let \(\tau=|P|+|R|\) and \(m=|S|\); masses need not sum to one. Types are pooled: capacities and demands are \(a_pc_{ps}\) and \(b_rd_{rs}\), and workload is divisible.
The input specifies compatible triples \((p,r,s)\), positive per-unit capacities, demands and processing rates \(v_{ps}\), finite lists of nonnegative rational admissible start times, positive rational deadlines \(D_{rs}\), and nonnegative rational travel times depending on the provider's last destination. Serving aggregate workload \(x\) takes time \(x/(a_pv_{ps})\). A provider pool performs services sequentially; each triple occurs at most once in its trial schedule.
Proposal-quality functions \(Q_{rs}(w,t)\), bid functions \(B_{rs}(t)\), and requester schedule-utility functions \(U_r\) are given as explicit rational piecewise-affine circuits; utilities and qualities are nonnegative on feasible inputs. Require \(B_{rs}(t)>B_{rs}(t')\) exactly when \(t<t'\) on admissible times. Fixed input orders break every tie. Global utility is \(\sum_{r\in R}b_rU_r(\sigma_r/b_r)\), where normalization divides workloads and concurrent provider masses, but leaves times unchanged.
The supplied current state contains proposals, requests, residual workloads, clocks, last destinations and ordered trial schedules. A proposal records a compatible triple, offered workload and earliest admissible start time after the provider's clock plus travel; omit zero-workload proposals and triples with no such time. Deadlines constrain completion.
One synchronous round first resets requester demands. For each requester-skill pair, process previous proposals in descending \(Q_{rs}(w,t)\); request \(x=\min\{w,\text{remaining demand}\}\) at the proposed time, with bid \(B_{rs}(t)\), subtracting \(x\) from demand. Omit requests whose completion would miss the deadline.
Next reset each provider's capacities, clock and trial schedule to their original capacities, zero and empty. Process the new requests in descending bid order: accept a request only if its workload fits, its earliest admissible start is at most its requested start, and completion at the requested start meets the deadline; otherwise stop processing that provider's requests immediately. An accepted request is scheduled at its requested start, updates residual capacity and clock, and receives a new proposal recording capacity immediately before acceptance and the earliest admissible start computed for it.
For every compatible triple not already proposed during this provider phase, propose its final residual capacity and earliest admissible start after the final trial schedule. These proposals and the new requests and schedules form the next state. Schedules are rebuilt each round rather than executed cumulatively. A fixed point means exact equality of the entire state after one round.
Given binary-encoded \(K\geq0\), is deciding whether this deterministic mass-RPA process reaches a fixed point within \(K\) rounds in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard? What is the complexity of computing its schedule and utility at the first fixed point, or after \(K\) rounds if none has occurred, with running times stated in \(m,\tau,L\) and \(\log(K+1)\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
Discrete RPA has a stated convergence bound of \(2n^2m^2\) iterations under earlier-start bid preference and the service-repeat restriction; this is an iteration bound, without a general binary-bit complexity guarantee for arbitrary utility functions. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.14507). No complexity result for the mass process specified here was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question does not define the mass lifting. Provider and requester types are treated as pools with mass-scaled capacities and demands, divisible workloads, and processing rates proportional to provider mass; individual replicas and state-cohort splitting are not used.
  • Mass normalization is unspecified. Positive rational masses are left unnormalized so provider/requester population ratios remain part of the input.
  • Work-time functions and temporal feasibility lack finite encodings. Processing is linear with positive rational rates; admissible start times are explicit finite rational lists, and travel depends on the last destination.
  • Proposal quality, bids and utility lack computational representations. They are explicit rational piecewise-affine circuits, with the anchor's strict earlier-start bid preference imposed; global utility is the mass-weighted sum of normalized requester utilities.
  • Deadline handling is not specified in the RPA pseudocode. Requests that cannot complete by their deadline are omitted, and provider acceptance also checks completion.
  • The current-state descriptors omit messages needed to determine the next round. Pending proposals and requests are included, alongside residual capacities, clocks, last destinations and ordered schedules.
  • A round is one requester phase followed by one provider phase. Trial capacities, demands and schedules reset each round; provider scheduling stops at the first failed request.
  • The pseudocode and prose differ about the proposal sent after acceptance. The pseudocode is followed: it reports pre-acceptance residual capacity and the computed earliest start; the accepted service itself uses the requested start.
  • The repeat restriction is interpreted as at most one occurrence of each provider-requester-skill triple within a trial schedule, rather than a prohibition on reconsidering that service in later rounds.
  • K is binary-encoded; round zero is the supplied state, and fixed-point equality includes all messages and state descriptors. The requested output is the first fixed-point schedule, or the schedule after K rounds if no fixed point occurs.

Result

The bounded-round fixed-point problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with one skill, two provider types, two requester types, unit masses, and zero travel times.

Computing the requested schedule or its exact utility is also \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Both tasks admit exact algorithms using polynomial space and time

\[ (K+1)\operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\right). \]

Thus a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Below, I prove the reduction and the bit-complexity bounds. The bounds do not assert a matching completeness classification.

1. Conventions

Let \(F\) denote the specified synchronous transition, and let \(s_j=F^j(s_0)\). I interpret “reaches a fixed point within \(K\) rounds” as asking whether \(F(s_j)=s_j\) for some \(0\leq j\leq K\).

The literal requester rule can produce zero-workload requests after demand is exhausted. I retain these requests. The reduction also works if zero requests are omitted: the only difference is the removal of a zero-workload service from its absorbing schedule.

All start-time lists in the construction are the same two-element list. Consequently, the proof does not require provider-specific admissible-time domains.

2. A piecewise-affine circuit that tests an encoded assignment

Start with a Boolean CNF formula \(\varphi\) on \(n\geq1\) variables. Put \(M=2^n\). Boolean CNF satisfiability is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Define the rational piecewise-affine function

\[ \operatorname{clip}(u)=\min\{1,\max\{0,u\}\}. \]

We construct a circuit that, at integer workloads \(w\in\{2,\ldots,M+1\}\), extracts the bits of \(w-2\).

Set \(u_n=w-2\). For \(j=n-1,\ldots,0\), define

\[ \beta_j(w)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(u_{j+1}-(2^j-1)\right), \qquad u_j=u_{j+1}-2^j\beta_j(w). \]

To verify this construction, suppose \(u_{j+1}\) is an integer satisfying \(0\leq u_{j+1}<2^{j+1}\).

  • If \(u_{j+1}<2^j\), the argument of \(\operatorname{clip}\) is nonpositive, so \(\beta_j=0\).
  • If \(u_{j+1}\geq2^j\), that argument is at least one, so \(\beta_j=1\).

In either case, \(u_j\) is the remainder after removing the most significant bit and satisfies \(0\leq u_j<2^j\). Induction proves that the \(\beta_j\) are exactly the bits of \(w-2\).

Evaluate \(\varphi\) using these bits, replacing Boolean conjunction by minimum, disjunction by maximum, and negation by \(1-\beta_j\). Denote the resulting circuit by \(E(w)\). Then:

  • \(0\leq E(w)\leq1\) for every real \(w\).
  • For \(w=2+u\), where \(0\leq u<M\) is an integer, \(E(w)=1\) exactly when assignment \(u\) satisfies \(\varphi\).
  • For every integer \(w\), all extracted bits are Boolean, even outside the intended encoding interval. Hence, if \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, \(E(w)=0\) at every integer \(w\).

Define the halt predicate

\[ H(w)=\max\{\operatorname{clip}(2-w),E(w)\}. \]

Thus \(H(1)=1\), while \(H(w)=E(w)\) at integers \(w\geq2\).

These are explicit rational piecewise-affine circuits. Their total encoding length is polynomial in \(n+|\varphi|\): the bit extraction uses \(O(n)\) gates and constants with total bit length \(O(n^2)\).

3. The society and its initial state

There is one skill, suppressed from the notation. Let

\[ P=\{A,B\},\qquad R=\{X,Y\}. \]

All four provider-requester pairs are compatible. All masses and processing rates equal one.

Set

\[ N=M+10,\qquad T=N+1,\qquad K=2M+2. \]

Both providers have capacity \(N\). Requester demands are \(N\) for \(X\) and \(N-1\) for \(Y\). Every admissible-time list is \(\{0,T\}\), every deadline is \(2T\), and every travel time is zero.

Use the same bid function for both requesters:

\[ B_X(t)=B_Y(t)=2T-t. \]

It satisfies the required earlier-start preference. In particular, every request at time \(0\) precedes every request at time \(T\).

Put \(c(t)=\operatorname{clip}(t/T)\), and define qualities by

\[ Q_X(w,t)=1+c(t)-2\min\{c(t),H(w)\}, \qquad Q_Y(w,t)=c(t). \]

These qualities are nonnegative: since \(0\leq c,H\leq1\),

\[ Q_X(w,t)\geq1-c(t)\geq0. \]

At the admissible times:

\[ Q_X(w,0)=1,\qquad Q_X(w,T)=2-2H(w), \]

and

\[ Q_Y(w,0)=0,\qquad Q_Y(w,T)=1. \]

The initial proposals are:

| Triple | Offered workload | Proposed time |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(A,X\) | \(2\) | \(T\) |
| \(A,Y\) | \(N\) | \(0\) |
| \(B,X\) | \(N\) | \(0\) |
| \(B,Y\) | \(1\) | \(T\) |

To specify the rest of the supplied state concretely, use these ordered trial schedules:

| Provider | First service | Second service |
|---|---|---|
| \(A\) | \(Y\), workload \(N-2\), start \(0\) | \(X\), workload \(1\), start \(T\) |
| \(B\) | \(X\), workload \(N-1\), start \(0\) | \(Y\), workload \(1\), start \(T\) |

The requests are precisely these scheduled services, with their prescribed bids. Provider residual capacities are \(1\) and \(0\), respectively; both clocks are \(T+1\); last destinations are \(X\) and \(Y\). Both requester residual demands are zero.

The schedules are feasible. Their proposal records also have the correct capacities before acceptance: \(N,2\) for \(A\), and \(N,1\) for \(B\). The second service’s earliest admissible time is \(T\), because its first service completes strictly between \(0\) and \(T\).

The problem supplies the current state rather than requiring it to arise from a prescribed initialization. This construction uses that stated input convention.

4. Proven counter dynamics

Consider a state whose proposals have the form

\[ (A,Y,N,0),\quad(A,X,z,T),\quad(B,X,N,0),\quad(B,Y,y,T), \]

where \(z,y\) are positive integers satisfying

\[ y\leq z\leq y+1,\qquad z,y<N-1. \]

Suppose \(H(z)=0\).

Requester \(X\) ranks \(A\)'s proposal above \(B\)'s, because their qualities are \(2\) and \(1\). It requests:

\[ x_{AX}=z,\qquad x_{BX}=N-z. \]

Requester \(Y\) ranks \(B\)'s proposal first, because its proposed time is \(T\). It requests:

\[ x_{BY}=y,\qquad x_{AY}=N-1-y. \]

All these workloads are positive in the stated range.

Provider \(A\) processes \(Y\) at time \(0\), followed by \(X\) at time \(T\). Its capacity before the second acceptance is

\[ N-(N-1-y)=y+1. \]

Thus \(X\)'s request fits because \(z\leq y+1\).

Provider \(B\) processes \(X\) at time \(0\), followed by \(Y\) at time \(T\). Its capacity before the second acceptance is

\[ N-(N-z)=z. \]

Thus \(Y\)'s request fits because \(y\leq z\).

Each first service has duration strictly between \(0\) and \(N<T\), so the second service’s earliest admissible start is exactly \(T\). Every completion meets its deadline, since

\[ T+N<2T. \]

Therefore all four requests are accepted, and the new proposals have the same form with

\[ z'=y+1,\qquad y'=z. \]

Starting from \((z_0,y_0)=(2,1)\), induction gives, for as long as the halt predicate remains zero,

\[ z_j=2+\lfloor j/2\rfloor,\qquad y_j=1+\lceil j/2\rceil. \]

Every transition changes at least one proposal workload. Hence none of these states is fixed.

An additional property is essential: assignment \(u\) is first tested at round \(j=2u\), when

\[ z_j=2+u,\qquad y_j=1+u=z_j-1. \]

5. Proven absorbing behavior

Suppose a satisfying assignment exists, and let \(u\) be its smallest integer encoding. By the preceding calculation, its first test occurs at round \(j=2u\), with

\[ z=2+u,\qquad y=z-1,\qquad H(z)=1. \]

Requester \(X\) now ranks \(B\) first: \(B\)'s quality is \(1\), whereas \(A\)'s is \(0\). Its requests are

\[ x_{BX}=N,\qquad x_{AX}=0. \]

Requester \(Y\)'s requests remain

\[ x_{BY}=y,\qquad x_{AY}=N-1-y=N-z. \]

Provider \(B\) accepts its full-capacity \(X\) request, then rejects \(Y\)'s positive request and stops. Its residual capacity is zero, so its \(Y\) proposal disappears.

Provider \(A\) accepts \(Y\)'s workload \(N-z\), leaving capacity \(z\), then accepts the zero-workload \(X\) request at time \(T\). Consequently, its \(X\) proposal remains exactly \((z,T)\).

This is why the first satisfying test must occur with \(z=y+1\): the tested workload remains unchanged for the next round.

In that next round, \(H(z)=1\) still holds. Requester \(X\) again requests all \(N\) units from \(B\). Requester \(Y\), having no proposal from \(B\), requests all \(N-1\) units from \(A\).

The resulting state has proposals

\[ (A,Y,N,0),\qquad(A,X,1,T),\qquad(B,X,N,0), \]

and schedules

\[ \sigma_A=((Y,N-1,0),(X,0,T)), \qquad \sigma_B=((X,N,0)). \]

Its provider residual capacities are \(1,0\), clocks are \(T,N\), and last destinations are both \(X\). Its requester residual demands are zero.

Because \(H(1)=1\), applying another round reproduces every proposal, request, schedule, residual, clock, and destination. This entire state is a fixed point.

It is reached at round

\[ 2u+2\leq2(M-1)+2=2M<K. \]

If zero requests are omitted, the zero service disappears from \(A\)'s schedule; its clock becomes \(N-1\) and last destination becomes \(Y\). That version is likewise an exact fixed point.

6. NP-hardness of reachability, schedule computation, and utility computation

If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, Section 5 proves that a fixed point is reached within \(K\) rounds.

If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, \(H(w)=0\) at every integer \(w\geq2\). Thus the counter dynamics continue through round \(K+1\). Indeed, throughout this interval,

\[ z_j,y_j\leq M+3<N-1, \]

so every hypothesis used in Section 4 remains valid. Since every transition changes a proposal workload, no state through round \(K\) is fixed.

We have therefore proved

\[ \varphi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the supplied process reaches a fixed point within }K\text{ rounds}. \]

All numbers have \(O(n)\) bits, and the circuits have polynomial encoding length. The construction is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. It proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness with \(m=1\) and \(\tau=4\).

For schedule and utility computation, set \(U_Y=0\), and let \(U_X\) be the workload scheduled for \(X\) by provider \(A\). This is a nonnegative linear function of the schedule; all masses equal one, so it is also the global utility.

  • If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, the first fixed-point schedule gives \(A\)'s \(X\) service workload zero.
  • If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, the schedule after \(K\) rounds gives that service positive workload.

Thus examining the returned schedule, or testing whether its exact utility is zero, decides satisfiability. Both requested computations are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

7. Exact bit complexity and upper bounds

Let \(h\) be the number of compatible triples. Then

\[ h\leq m|P||R|\leq m\tau^2/4. \]

7.1 Workload bit lengths remain bounded

Let \(q\) be the square of the product of denominators of all rational constants in the input, including the supplied state. Then \(\log q=O(L)\).

Every initial offered workload, pooled capacity \(a_pc_{ps}\), and pooled demand \(b_rd_{rs}\) belongs to the lattice

\[ q^{-1}\mathbb Z. \]

All subsequent workload operations are resets to these constants, subtraction, and minimum. Consequently, all workload fields remain on this same lattice.

Generated workloads are bounded by pooled capacities or demands. These bounds are at most \(2^{O(L)}\). Therefore every workload can be stored as an integer numerator over \(q\), using \(O(L)\) bits, independently of the number of rounds.

Quality and bid circuit outputs influence ordering; they are never substituted into workload arithmetic.

7.2 Times and circuit evaluation

Every generated proposed or requested start belongs to an explicit input list. After an acceptance, a provider’s clock is

\[ t+\frac{x}{a_pv_{ps}}, \]

where \(t\) is an input start time and \(x\) has the preceding \(O(L)\)-bit representation. Hence generated clocks, completion times, and travel comparisons also have \(O(L)\)-bit rational representations.

An explicit rational piecewise-affine circuit can be evaluated in polynomial time on rational inputs of polynomial bit length. For affine gates, rational addition and multiplication by encoded constants preserve polynomial bit length over the circuit’s polynomially many gates; minimum, maximum, and branch selection select existing rational values.

A round requires polynomially many circuit evaluations, comparisons, list scans, and sorting operations. Therefore one exact round takes

\[ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \]

bit operations.

For the selected output schedule, dividing workloads and provider masses by the fixed requester masses preserves polynomial bit length. Concurrent masses, if required as circuit inputs, are obtained by summing fixed rational masses over the schedule’s polynomially many service intervals. Exact normalized utility evaluation is therefore polynomial-time as well.

7.3 Direct simulation

Store the current state, its successor, and a binary round counter. Compare the complete states after each transition. An extra successor calculation checks whether the state at round \(K\) itself is fixed.

This yields decision and output algorithms with time

\[ (K+1)\operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\right) \]

and space

\[ \operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\right). \]

Equivalently, for \(\ell=\lceil\log_2(K+1)\rceil\), the time bound is

\[ 2^{O(\ell+1)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \]

The decision problem thus belongs to \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\). Exact schedule and utility computation belong to \(\mathrm{FPSPACE}\), with polynomial output length.

7.4 Optional cycle acceleration

There is also a finite-state bound independent of the numerical value of \(K\).

Bid values can be represented implicitly by their requester-skill pair and requested-time index, because they are determined by those data. With this representation, a generated state has a binary description of length

\[ O\!\left((h+m\tau+\tau+1)L\right). \]

This accounts for workload fields, time indices, message presence and order, schedules, residuals, clocks, and destinations. Therefore the number of generated states is at most

\[ N_{\mathrm{st}}=2^{O((m\tau^2+\tau+1)L)}. \]

Standard deterministic cycle finding uses polynomial space and \(O(N_{\mathrm{st}})\) transitions to find the orbit’s preperiod and period.

A period-one cycle is a fixed point. A longer cycle cannot contain a fixed point, since a deterministic orbit cannot leave a fixed point. Once the preperiod and period are known, modular arithmetic determines the state at round \(K\).

Combining direct simulation and cycle finding gives the additional upper bound

\[ \min\{K+1,N_{\mathrm{st}}\} \operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,L,\log(K+1)\right). \]

8. Why the discrete iteration bound does not transfer

The cited paper states its discrete RPA convergence bound in Proposition 3.2 under earlier-start bid preference and a service-repeat restriction. That is an iteration claim for its process. [Lavie et al., Section 3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.14507)

The transition specified here admits the counter of Section 4. Taking \(H(w)=\operatorname{clip}(2-w)\) removes the assignment-testing circuit entirely. For arbitrarily large binary-encoded \(N\), the same four types undergo \(\Omega(N)\) changing rounds, with input length \(O(\log N)\).

Thus the present rules admit exponentially many changing rounds even with fixed \(m,\tau\). This alone does not prove computational hardness—such a counter might be accelerated—but the SAT-testing quality circuit supplies the rigorous hardness reduction.
