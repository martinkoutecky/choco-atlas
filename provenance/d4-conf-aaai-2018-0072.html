The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite signal set \(S\), a finite rational family of possible common signal models with prior \(\pi\), a finite grid of payment parameters, and \(H\) atomless reporting cohorts, does there exist an adaptive payment policy and a Bayesian–Nash equilibrium whose expected dishonest strategic mass is at most a given rational threshold \(q\)? The mechanism observes each cohort’s exact report-mass histogram, but not the true model.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) be a signal set of size \(m\geq2\). The input contains \(R\) possible common signal models, a positive rational prior \(\pi\) on them, a nonempty grid \(G\) of \(K\) fully mixed rational distributions on \(S\), a rational noise rate \(0\leq\epsilon<1\), and a unary horizon \(H\geq1\).
Each model \(\theta\) specifies a finite latent-state distribution with positive rational weights and fully mixed rational conditional signal distributions \(p_\theta(x\mid\omega)\). Peer signals are conditionally independent given the state; their posterior distributions given distinct signals must differ. Let \(J\) be the total number of listed latent states.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here types are signal–behavior pairs, and the input lists their masses conditional on each model and state.
Every cohort has total mass one. Conditional on its state, strategic signal-\(x\) mass is \((1-\epsilon)p_\theta(x\mid\omega)\), and nonstrategic signal-\(x\) mass is \(\epsilon p_\theta(x\mid\omega)\); nonstrategic mass reports uniformly on \(S\). The model is drawn once from \(\pi\), and cohort states are independently drawn from that model.
Agents know the model but not the realized state; the mechanism knows only the input. Before each cohort reports, a committed adaptive policy chooses and announces \(P\in G\), possibly randomly, using previous exact report-mass histograms. Past histograms and chosen payments are public; each agent participates in only one cohort.
An agent reporting \(r\) is paired uniformly with a peer in that cohort and receives \(u_P(r,z)=\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{\{r=z\}}-\epsilon/m\bigr)/P(r)\) when the peer reports \(z\). Agents maximize expected payment; an individual has zero effect on histograms.
Promise that for every model some \(P\in G\) makes honest reporting a strict Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, but no single one-peer payment matrix makes honesty a strict Bayesian–Nash equilibrium for every listed model.
Allow Bayesian–Nash equilibria with arbitrary report fractions within each strategic signal type, depending on the model, public history, and announced payment; ties may be split arbitrarily. Dishonesty is the total strategic mass reporting a value different from its signal, summed over cohorts, with expectation over the model, states, and policy randomization.
For rational \(0\leq q\leq H(1-\epsilon)\), is deciding whether some adaptive payment policy and Bayesian–Nash equilibrium have expected dishonest strategic mass at most \(q\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,R,J,K,H\) and the total input bit length \(L\).
For finite-agent minimal peer prediction with limited knowledge, the reported dishonesty limit is \(\Theta(\log n)\), with AdaPTS attaining the upper bound in a strict equilibrium ([Radanovic–Faltings](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/11511/11511-13-15039-1-2-20201228.pdf)). This is an asymptotic dishonesty bound, not a polynomial-time classification of the decision problem.
No result settling this exact atomless, exact-histogram policy-existence problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Represent rational common signal models by finite latent-state mixtures, retaining conditional independence, full mixing, and stochastic relevance.
  • Normalize each cohort to unit mass and sum dishonest mass over cohorts, rather than averaging it.
  • Draw the unknown model once and use independent latent-state draws across cohorts; the graded question did not specify dependence across cohorts.
  • Agents know the true common model, while the mechanism has the prior over models; histories and announced payments are public.
  • Interpret the payment grid as fully mixed PTS distributions, use the noise-corrected payment, and normalize its positive scale to one.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already with
\(H=1\), \(R=3\), \(K=3\), \(\epsilon=0\), \(\tau=m\), and \(J=3m\).

The reduction below allows arbitrary mixed reporting fractions and satisfies both promises in the question. Thus, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), there is no algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,R,J,K,H,L\).

The one-cohort restriction is also in \(\mathrm{NP}\). For general horizons, a finite enumeration using linear programming gives an explicit, though much larger, upper bound.

1. A realizable family of signal models

We first construct models whose equilibria can be analyzed directly.

Let \(d\) be a fully mixed rational distribution on \(S\), and let \(b\) be rational with \(0<b<1\). Define a model \(M(d,b)\) with one latent state for each signal. State \(i\) has probability \(d_i\), and its conditional signal distribution is

\[ p(x\mid i)=(1-b)d_x+b\mathbf{1}_{\{x=i\}}. \]

Every state weight and conditional signal probability is positive. Conditional on the state, peer signals are independent.

The marginal signal distribution is \(d\). Writing \(t=b^2\), the joint signal probabilities are

\[ \Pr(X=x,Y=y)=(1-t)d_xd_y+t\,d_x\mathbf{1}_{\{x=y\}}. \]

Consequently, the peer-signal posterior is

\[ Q(y\mid x)=(1-t)d_y+t\mathbf{1}_{\{x=y\}}. \]

Distinct signals have distinct peer-signal posteriors, since their posterior vectors differ by \(t(e_x-e_{x'})\).

For the reduction, set \(\epsilon=0\). Let \(a_{xr}\) be the fraction of strategic signal-\(x\) agents reporting \(r\), so \(a_{xr}\geq0\) and \(\sum_r a_{xr}=1\). Define the marginal report mass

\[ c_r=\sum_x d_xa_{xr}. \]

Under payment distribution \(P\), the expected payoff to signal \(x\) from reporting \(r\) is

\[ U_x(r)=\frac{(1-t)c_r+t a_{xr}}{P_r}. \]

Thus \(a\) is an equilibrium exactly when every report with positive fraction in a row maximizes that row’s expected payoff. This characterization permits arbitrary splitting at ties.

Honest reporting is a strict equilibrium when \(P=d\): under \(a_{xr}=\mathbf{1}_{\{x=r\}}\),

\[ U_x(x)=1-t+\frac{t}{d_x}, \qquad U_x(r)=1-t\quad(r\neq x). \]

2. Two equilibrium lemmas

2.1. An upper bound on honest mass in every equilibrium

For a nonempty report set \(U\subseteq S\), define

\[ T_P(U)=\sum_{r\in U}P_r, \qquad F_{d,P}(U)=\sum_{r\in U}\min\left\{d_r,\frac{P_r}{T_P(U)}\right\}. \]

Also define

\[ \Delta(t,P)=\frac{t}{(1-t)\min_r P_r}. \]

Lemma 1. Every equilibrium of \(M(d,b)\) under \(P\), including mixed equilibria, has honest mass at most

\[ \max_{\varnothing\neq U\subseteq S}F_{d,P}(U)+\Delta(t,P). \]

Proof. Let \(U=\{r:c_r>0\}\), which is nonempty. Set

\[ M=\max_s\frac{c_s}{P_s}. \]

For each \(r\in U\), some signal \(x\) has \(a_{xr}>0\). Equilibrium therefore implies, for every \(s\),

\[ \frac{(1-t)c_r+t a_{xr}}{P_r} \geq \frac{(1-t)c_s+t a_{xs}}{P_s} \geq \frac{(1-t)c_s}{P_s}. \]

Using \(a_{xr}\leq1\), we obtain

\[ M-\Delta(t,P)\leq\frac{c_r}{P_r}\leq M. \]

The \(P\)-weighted average of these ratios over \(U\) is

\[ \frac{\sum_{r\in U}c_r}{T_P(U)}=\frac{1}{T_P(U)}. \]

Hence \(M\leq1/T_P(U)+\Delta(t,P)\), and therefore

\[ c_r\leq\frac{P_r}{T_P(U)}+P_r\Delta(t,P). \]

The honest mass reporting \(r\) is \(d_ra_{rr}\), which is at most both \(d_r\) and \(c_r\). Consequently,

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_r d_ra_{rr} &\leq \sum_{r\in U}\min\{d_r,c_r\}\\ &\leq F_{d,P}(U)+T_P(U)\Delta(t,P)\\ &\leq F_{d,P}(U)+\Delta(t,P). \end{aligned} \]

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

The proof also establishes the following fact for every used report \(r\):

\[ \frac{c_r}{P_r}\geq\frac{1}{T_P(U)}-\Delta(t,P). \tag{1} \]

2.2. An equilibrium achieving full honesty on a chosen signal set

Lemma 2. Suppose \(\varnothing\neq U\subseteq S\), write \(T=T_P(U)\), and assume

\[ (1-t)d_r\leq\frac{P_r}{T}\qquad(r\in U). \]

Then there is an equilibrium in which every signal in \(U\) reports honestly and every signal outside \(U\) reports within \(U\). Its honest mass is exactly \(\sum_{r\in U}d_r\).

Proof. Let

\[ d_D=1-\sum_{r\in U}d_r, \qquad C=(1-t)d_D+t, \]

and define

\[ v_r= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{P_r/T-(1-t)d_r}{C},&r\in U,\\ 0,&r\notin U. \end{cases} \]

The assumed inequalities give \(v_r\geq0\). Moreover,

\[ \sum_r v_r = \frac{1-(1-t)(1-d_D)}{C}=1. \]

Have each signal \(x\in U\) report \(x\), and each signal outside \(U\) use report fractions \(v\). For \(r\in U\), the marginal report mass is \(c_r=d_r+d_Dv_r\). By construction,

\[ \frac{(1-t)c_r}{P_r} = \frac1T-\frac{t v_r}{P_r}. \]

A signal outside \(U\) therefore receives payoff \(1/T\) from every report in \(U\), and zero from every report outside \(U\).

A signal \(x\in U\) receives from its honest report

\[ U_x(x)=\frac1T+\frac{t(1-v_x)}{P_x}\geq\frac1T. \]

Every other report in \(U\) gives payoff at most \(1/T\), and every report outside \(U\) gives zero. All prescribed reports are therefore best responses. The claimed honest mass follows immediately. \(\square\)

3. Encoding PARTITION into payment supports

We reduce from binary-encoded PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), determine whether some subset sums to half their total. This is an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem; see [Karp’s original reduction framework](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let \(A=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\), and use signals \(S=\{0,1,\ldots,n,\ast\}\), so \(m=n+2\). Define a payment distribution \(P\) and signal marginal \(d\) by

\[ P_0=\frac14,\qquad P_i=\frac{3a_i}{10A}\quad(1\leq i\leq n),\qquad P_\ast=\frac9{20}, \]

and

\[ d_0=\frac58,\qquad d_r=\frac{P_r}{2}\quad(r\neq0). \]

Both distributions are fully mixed and sum to one. Define

\[ \gamma=\frac{3}{160A}. \]

Lemma 3.

  • If the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance, some report set \(U\) has \(T_P(U)=2/5\) and \(F_{d,P}(U)=7/10\).
  • If it is a no-instance, every nonempty report set \(U\) satisfies \(F_{d,P}(U)\leq7/10-\gamma\).

Proof. If \(0\notin U\), then \(P_r/T_P(U)\geq P_r>d_r\) for every \(r\in U\). Thus

\[ F_{d,P}(U)=\frac{T_P(U)}2\leq\frac38. \tag{2} \]

Now suppose \(0\in U\), and write \(T=T_P(U)\). The same calculation for the other signals gives

\[ F_{d,P}(U) = f(T):= \min\left\{\frac58,\frac{1}{4T}\right\} +\frac12\left(T-\frac14\right). \]

At \(T=2/5\), this equals \(7/10\).

For \(T\leq2/5\),

\[ \frac7{10}-f(T)=\frac12\left(\frac25-T\right). \]

For \(2/5\leq T\leq1\),

\[ \frac7{10}-f(T) = \frac{(T-2/5)(5/4-T)}{2T} \geq\frac{T-2/5}{8}. \]

Every attainable \(T\) with \(0\in U\) has the form

\[ T=\frac14+\frac{3B}{20A}, \]

where

\[ B=2\sum_{i\in U\cap\{1,\ldots,n\}}a_i +3A\mathbf{1}_{\{\ast\in U\}} \]

is an integer. Therefore \(T=2/5\) exactly when \(B=A\). Since the dummy contribution \(3A\) exceeds \(A\), this happens exactly when \(\ast\notin U\) and \(2\sum_{i\in U}a_i=A\).

If PARTITION has no solution, every such \(T\) differs from \(2/5\) by at least \(3/(20A)\). The preceding bounds then give a loss of at least \(\gamma\). Sets omitting \(0\) satisfy the same bound by (2), since \(3/8<7/10-\gamma\). \(\square\)

4. Constructing the promised three-model instance

Let \(p_{\min}=\min_r P_r\), and choose

\[ b=\frac{\gamma p_{\min}}{100}, \qquad t=b^2, \qquad b_g=\frac{p_{\min}}{100}. \]

Define a shifted marginal

\[ d'=d+\frac{t}{1-t}(e_0-e_1). \]

Since \(b<1/100\),

\[ \frac{t}{1-t}\leq2b^2<\frac{p_{\min}}4. \]

Thus every coordinate of \(d'\) is positive. In particular,

\[ \min_r d_r\geq\frac{p_{\min}}2, \qquad \min_r d'_r\geq\frac{p_{\min}}4. \tag{3} \]

Use the following models and payment grid.

| Model | Signal model | A payment giving strict honesty |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | \(M(d,b)\) | \(d\) |
| Gate | \(M(P,b_g)\) | \(P\) |
| Witness | \(M(d',b)\) | \(d'\) |

Thus \(G=\{P,d,d'\}\). Set \(H=1\) and \(\epsilon=0\). There is one strategic type for each signal, with its conditional mass equal to the corresponding conditional signal probability.

This gives \(R=3\), \(K=3\), \(\tau=m\), and \(J=3m\).

4.1. Both promises hold

Each model has a grid payment giving strict honesty, by the calculation in Section 1.

There is also no single one-peer payment matrix giving strict honesty in every model. Indeed, the hard model’s posterior after signal \(0\) is identical to the witness model’s posterior after signal \(1\):

\[ (1-t)d+t e_0 = (1-t)d'+t e_1. \]

For any common payment matrix, these two agents have exactly the same expected payoff from each report. Strict honesty would require report \(0\) to beat report \(1\) in the first model, and report \(1\) to beat report \(0\) in the second. These requirements contradict each other.

4.2. The gate penalizes the other grid payments

Lemma 4. Every equilibrium of the gate model under either payment \(d\) or payment \(d'\) has dishonest mass at least \(1/4\).

Proof. Write \(B\in\{d,d'\}\) for the chosen payment and \(t_g=b_g^2\). By (3),

\[ \Delta(t_g,B) \leq \frac{8p_{\min}}{10000} < \frac18. \]

The gate’s signal-\(0\) mass is \(P_0=1/4\).

If report \(0\) is unused, all that mass is dishonest. Otherwise, (1) gives

\[ c_0 \geq B_0\left(\frac1{T_B(U)}-\Delta(t_g,B)\right) \geq B_0-\Delta(t_g,B) \geq\frac12, \]

where we used \(T_B(U)\leq1\) and \(B_0\geq5/8\).

At most \(1/4\) of the mass reporting \(0\) can be honest. Therefore dishonest mass is at least \(c_0-1/4\geq1/4\). \(\square\)

5. Correctness of the reduction

Set

\[ w=\frac18, \qquad \eta=\frac{w\gamma}{8}, \]

and choose the positive model prior

\[ \pi_{\mathrm{hard}}=w,\qquad \pi_{\mathrm{witness}}=\eta,\qquad \pi_{\mathrm{gate}}=1-w-\eta. \]

Finally, set

\[ q=w\left(\frac3{10}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right). \]

This is a rational threshold satisfying \(0<q<1\).

5.1. A partition gives a feasible policy and equilibrium

Suppose PARTITION has a solution. Choose payment \(P\).

By Lemma 3, there is a set \(U\) containing \(0\), omitting \(\ast\), and satisfying \(T_P(U)=2/5\). For every \(r\in U\),

\[ d_r\leq\frac{P_r}{T_P(U)}. \]

Thus Lemma 2 applies to the hard model. Its equilibrium has honest mass

\[ \sum_{r\in U}d_r = \frac58+\frac12\left(\frac25-\frac14\right) = \frac7{10}. \]

Its dishonest mass is therefore \(3/10\).

Use the strict honest equilibrium in the gate model. In the witness model, use the equilibrium in which everyone reports \(0\): report \(0\) pays positively and every other report pays zero. Its dishonest mass is at most one.

The resulting expected dishonest mass is at most

\[ w\frac3{10}+\eta = w\left(\frac3{10}+\frac{\gamma}{8}\right) <q. \]

5.2. Without a partition, every policy fails

Suppose PARTITION has no solution.

First consider payment \(P\). Its equilibrium error satisfies

\[ \Delta(t,P) = \frac{b^2}{(1-b^2)p_{\min}} \leq \frac{2\gamma^2p_{\min}}{10000} < \frac{\gamma}{4}. \]

Lemmas 1 and 3 therefore imply that every hard-model equilibrium has honest mass at most

\[ \frac7{10}-\gamma+\Delta(t,P) < \frac7{10}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}. \]

Hence expected dishonest mass under payment \(P\), even ignoring the other models, exceeds

\[ w\left(\frac3{10}+\frac{3\gamma}{4}\right)>q. \]

Now consider either other grid payment. Lemma 4 gives expected dishonest mass at least

\[ \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{gate}}}{4}. \]

Since \(\eta<1/8\), we have \(\pi_{\mathrm{gate}}>3/4\). Consequently,

\[ \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{gate}}}{4}>\frac3{16}>q. \]

With \(H=1\), the mechanism has no previous histogram. Its randomized payment is therefore independent of the model. Its expected dishonesty is a convex combination of the conditional expected dishonesty for the three payment choices. Every choice exceeds \(q\), for every admissible equilibrium, so randomization cannot succeed.

This proves the equivalence between PARTITION and the constructed instance.

5.3. Encoding size

All constructed rationals have bit length \(O(\log(A+1))\). There are \(O(m^2)\) conditional signal and type-mass entries. Hence the constructed input has

\[ L=O\!\left(m^2\log(A+1)\right), \]

and it can be constructed in \(O(m^2\operatorname{poly}(\log(A+1)))\) bit operations.

Both \(m\) and \(\log(A+1)\) are polynomially bounded by the source input length. The reduction is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction. It proves ordinary \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness; no strong-hardness claim is needed.

6. Running-time upper bounds

6.1. One cohort

For a fixed model and payment, expected payoffs are affine functions of the reporting fractions.

Enumerate, for each strategic reporting row, a nonempty allowed report set. There are at most \((2^m-1)^\tau\) choices. For each choice, impose:

  • Nonnegative fractions summing to one, with zero fractions outside the allowed set.
  • Every allowed report has payoff at least that of every alternative report.
  • The desired linear objective or dishonest-mass constraint.

These are rational linear-programming constraints. Allowing an allowed report to have zero fraction is harmless: every report actually used is still a best response. Conversely, every equilibrium appears by choosing its actual positive supports.

For each payment, minimize dishonest mass separately in each model and average using \(\pi\). Agents know the model, so these equilibrium choices are independent. Randomization over payments cannot improve the minimum.

This gives a running time bounded by

\[ KR\,2^{m\tau}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,J,L). \]

Furthermore, a feasible support-constrained linear program has a rational feasible solution of polynomial encoding length. A payment and polynomial-size rational equilibrium certificate therefore establish membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Thus the one-cohort restriction, including the promised family above, is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete as a promise problem.

6.2. General unary horizon

A larger finite enumeration also handles adaptive policies.

First, policy randomization can be removed. For any fixed policy and equilibrium, there are finitely many reached public histories: each announced payment has at most \(J\) possible state-conditioned histograms. At each history, choose an action minimizing the conditional expected remaining dishonesty, and proceed backward. This preserves the current cohort’s equilibrium because agents participate only once and their individual reports have zero effect on future histograms. The resulting deterministic policy has no greater expected dishonesty.

For a deterministic policy, let

\[ N=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}J^h \]

bound the number of cohort-decision nodes.

At each node, enumerate:

  • The payment, with \(K\) choices.
  • The reporting supports for compatible models, with at most \(2^{R\tau m}\) choices.
  • A partition of the at most \(J\) model-state outcomes according to equality of their exact histograms.
  • For outcomes in different blocks, a coordinate and sign witnessing unequal histograms.

Histograms are affine in reporting fractions. Equality within a block is therefore linear; a selected coordinate and sign gives a strict linear inequality between blocks.

Once these partitions are fixed, each node’s model-conditioned reach probability is a product of sums of the listed state weights. These probabilities are rational constants. Consequently, expected total dishonesty is linear in the reporting fractions.

Every enumerated case is thus a rational linear feasibility problem, possibly with strict inequalities. Strict inequalities can be checked by introducing one common slack variable, bounding it between zero and one, and maximizing it. The original strict system is feasible exactly when the maximum slack is positive.

A conservative running-time bound is

\[ 2^{O\!\left( N\left[ R\tau m+\log(K+1)+J\log(J+1)+J^2\log(2m) \right]\right)} \operatorname{poly}(N,m,\tau,R,J,K,H,L). \]

This bound explicitly accounts for equality of exact histograms. No discretization of reporting fractions or observations is required.

7. Relation to the asymptotic dishonesty bound

The finite-agent \(\Theta(\log n)\) dishonesty result concerns an asymptotic equilibrium-performance bound, as stated in [Radanovic–Faltings](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/11511/11511-13-15039-1-2-20201228.pdf). The reduction here instead establishes computational hardness for the specified atomless decision problem, already before adaptivity arises.
