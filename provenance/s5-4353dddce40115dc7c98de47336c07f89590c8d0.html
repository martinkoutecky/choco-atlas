The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite tournament \(G\) on entrant types \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a distinguished singleton favorite \(f\) with mass \(\mu_f=2^{-R}\), and \(R\) rounds, can the current type masses be paired in equal blocks each round so that the favorite's entire block advances every round and wins? A match between distinct types produces the winner specified by \(G\); a same-type match produces that type. Restrict to instances where the subset feedback arc number of \(G\) at \(f\) is \(1\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) entrant types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The input includes a tournament \(G=(\mathcal T,E)\) on this support, with \(m=|E|\), masses satisfying \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t=1\), an integer \(R\geq1\) encoded in binary, and a favorite \(f\in\mathcal T\) of mass \(\mu_f=2^{-R}\).
An arc \((s,t)\) means that \(s\) defeats \(t\). The subset feedback arc number at \(f\) is the minimum number of arcs whose reversal leaves no directed cycle containing \(f\); promise that this number equals \(1\).
Initially the current masses are \(\mu_t\). In each of exactly \(R\) rounds, partition all current mass into finitely many homogeneous blocks of arbitrary positive real masses and pair the blocks, with equal masses within each pair. There are no byes.
Each pair retains its winning block, with its mass unchanged, and eliminates the other block. Distinct types have the winner specified by \(G\); a same-type pair retains one block of that type.
The favorite represents one entrant: its block is indivisible and must survive every round. All other masses may be split and regrouped freely between rounds. A successful schedule leaves only the favorite's block after round \(R\).

Is deciding whether such a successful schedule exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,R,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete entrants in a balanced knockout tournament, deciding whether a favorite can win is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even when its subset feedback arc number equals \(1\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13422v1)

1. Mass accounting and a general necessary condition

Write
$$ q=2^{-R},\qquad U=\mathcal T\setminus\{f\},\qquad A=\{t\in U:f\to t\}. $$

Every pair consumes twice its retained mass. Hence the total mass after round $r$ is $2^{-r}$. The favorite’s surviving block always has mass $q$, so survival through all $R$ rounds automatically leaves only that block.

In every round, the favorite must defeat one homogeneous block of mass $q$, belonging to a type in $A$. Let $n_t$ count its matches against type $t$. A type’s mass cannot increase: victories retain existing mass, losses remove mass, and same-type matches halve the participating mass. Consequently,
$$ n_t\leq \left\lfloor\frac{\mu_t}{q}\right\rfloor, \qquad \sum_{t\in A}n_t=R. $$

Call a nonempty set $S\subseteq U$ dominating if every type in $S$ defeats every type in $U\setminus S$. Define
$$ \mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t, \qquad K(S)=\min\left\{ R,\; \sum_{t\in A\cap S} \left\lfloor\frac{\mu_t}{q}\right\rfloor \right\}. $$

Proposition. Every successful schedule satisfies
$$ \boxed{\mu(S)\leq 1-2^{-K(S)}} $$
for every dominating set $S$.

Proof. Let $\nu_r$ be the mass of $S$ after round $r$, and let $z_r$ indicate whether the favorite’s round-$r$ opponent belongs to $S$.

In that round, let $I_r$ be the retained mass from pairs internal to $S$, and let $W_r$ be the retained mass from matches in which a type in $S$ defeats a type outside $S$. No type in $S$ can lose to a nonfavorite outside $S$. Thus
$$ \nu_{r-1}=2I_r+W_r+qz_r, \qquad \nu_r=I_r+W_r, $$
and therefore
$$ 2\nu_r=\nu_{r-1}-qz_r+W_r \geq \nu_{r-1}-qz_r. $$

Multiplying by $2^{r-1}$ and summing over the rounds gives, because $\nu_R=0$,
$$ \mu(S)\leq q\sum_{r=1}^{R}2^{r-1}z_r. $$

There are at most $K(S)$ rounds with $z_r=1$. The sum of at most $K(S)$ distinct weights from $1,2,\ldots,2^{R-1}$ is maximized by taking the largest ones. Hence
$$ \mu(S) \leq q\left(2^R-2^{R-K(S)}\right) =1-2^{-K(S)}. $$
This proves the proposition.

These conditions can be checked in polynomial time. Indeed, the strongly connected components of a tournament have a total order in which every earlier component defeats every later component. To justify this, arcs in both directions between two components would make their union strongly connected; the component tournament is therefore acyclic and hence transitive.

A dominating set cannot contain only part of a strongly connected component: its vertices would receive no arc from the omitted vertices, preventing an internal path from an omitted vertex to an included one. Thus the dominating sets are precisely the nonempty prefixes of the ordered components. There are at most $\tau-1$ such sets.

2. An exact polynomial algorithm for transitive nonfavorite tournaments

Assume that $G[U]$ is transitive. Order its types as
$$ t_1,t_2,\ldots,t_n,\qquad n=\tau-1, $$
so that $t_i\to t_j$ whenever $i<j$.

Define
$$ c_i= \begin{cases} \left\lfloor\mu_{t_i}/q\right\rfloor,&t_i\in A,\\ 0,&t_i\notin A, \end{cases} \qquad k_j=\min\left\{R,\sum_{i=1}^{j}c_i\right\}. $$

Theorem. A successful schedule exists if and only if
$$ \boxed{ \sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{t_i}\leq 1-2^{-k_j} \quad\text{for every }j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}. } $$

Necessity

Every prefix $\{t_1,\ldots,t_j\}$ is dominating. Its value of $K(S)$ is $k_j$, so all the inequalities follow from the proposition.

Sufficiency: choosing the favorite’s opponents

Measure mass in units of $q$. Put
$$ x_i=\frac{\mu_{t_i}}q, \qquad M_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}x_i. $$
The favorite has mass $1$, and the total nonfavorite mass is $2^R-1$. The assumed inequalities become
$$ M_j\leq 2^R-2^{R-k_j}. $$

For $j=n$, they imply $k_n=R$: if $k_n<R$, the right-hand side is at most $2^R-2$, whereas $M_n=2^R-1$.

Choose counts $n_i$ greedily from strongest to weakest:
$$ n_i=\min\left\{c_i,\;R-\sum_{h<i}n_h\right\}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_i n_i=R,\qquad n_i\leq x_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{j}n_i=k_j. $$

Use these opponents in order from weakest to strongest. Let $a_r$ be the chosen opponent in round $r$. For each prefix, its selected opponents occupy exactly the last $k_j$ rounds. Therefore
$$ \sum_{\{r:a_r\in\{t_1,\ldots,t_j\}\}}2^{r-1} =2^R-2^{R-k_j}. $$

Sufficiency: allocating initial mass to opponent brackets

For each round $r$, create an opponent component of initial mass
$$ w_r=2^{r-1}. $$
It will undergo $r-1$ internal rounds and then supply a unit block of type $a_r$ to the favorite.

Reserve one unit of type $a_r$ in each component. Type $t_i$ consequently has remaining mass
$$ d_i=x_i-n_i\geq0, $$
and component $r$ has remaining capacity
$$ b_r=w_r-1. $$

Assign mass of type $t_i$ only to components whose roots are at least as strong as $t_i$. For each prefix, the available capacity for its remaining mass is
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\{r:a_r\in\{t_1,\ldots,t_j\}\}}b_r &=2^R-2^{R-k_j}-k_j\\ &\geq M_j-k_j\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{j}d_i. \end{aligned} $$

These nested capacity inequalities suffice for an allocation. Process types in the order $t_1,\ldots,t_n$. When assigning $d_i$, all mass assigned earlier lies in components eligible for $t_i$. Thus the remaining eligible capacity is
$$ \sum_{\{r:a_r\in\{t_1,\ldots,t_i\}\}}b_r -\sum_{h<i}d_h \geq d_i. $$
Split $d_i$ among those capacities.

Finally,
$$ \sum_i d_i =2^R-1-R =\sum_{r=1}^{R}b_r, $$
so every component is filled exactly. Each contains at least one unit of its root type, and every other type in it loses to that root.

Sufficiency: collapsing each component

The following elementary construction completes the proof.

Lemma. Suppose a divisible component has total mass $2^h$, and a type $s$ that defeats every other type present has mass at least $1$. Then the component can become exactly one unit of type $s$ after $h$ rounds.

Proof. At the start of a round, let $x$ be the mass of $s$ and $y$ the remaining mass. Match
$$ z=\min\{x,y\} $$
units of $s$ against $z$ units of other types, distributing these matches among the finitely many types present. Self-pair the remaining mass of every type.

The next mass of $s$ is
$$ x'=\frac{x-z}{2}+z =\frac{x+z}{2} =\min\left\{x,\frac{x+y}{2}\right\}. $$

The total mass halves. After $h$ rounds, the mass of $s$ is therefore
$$ \min\{x_{\mathrm{initial}},1\}=1. $$
Since the total mass is also $1$, all other types have disappeared.

Apply the lemma to component $r$ with $h=r-1$. Run these components simultaneously, and in round $r$ pair their unit root block against the favorite.

Every active component performs an internal round until its scheduled favorite match. The favorite plays exactly once per round and remains indivisible. Thus there are no byes, and the construction is a legal successful schedule.

Complexity

Transitivity can be checked and its order obtained in polynomial time. The theorem then requires only floor computations, prefix sums, and comparisons.

All numbers have bit length polynomial in $R$ and $L$. The mass allocation uses rational subtraction and splitting. The component construction uses $O(\tau R^2)$ pairs, since each component needs at most $O(\tau)$ pairs per internal round.

Thus both decision and construction take polynomial time in $m,\tau,R,L$.

4. Another polynomial subclass: an exchange argument

An exact LP is useful here. Fix any opponent sequence
$$ (a_1,\ldots,a_R)\in A^R, $$
and continue measuring mass in units of $q$.

Let $v_t^r$ be the mass of type $t\in U$ after round $r$. For each nonfavorite arc $s\to t$, introduce $y_{st}^r\geq0$, and introduce $y_{tt}^r\geq0$ for same-type matches. Impose
$$ v_t^{r-1} = \mathbf1_{\{t=a_r\}} +2y_{tt}^r +\sum_{\{s:t\to s\}}y_{ts}^r +\sum_{\{s:s\to t\}}y_{st}^r, $$
$$ v_t^r = y_{tt}^r+\sum_{\{s:t\to s\}}y_{ts}^r, $$
with
$$ v_t^0=\mu_t/q,\qquad v_t^R=0, $$
and all variables nonnegative.

This LP is exact. A schedule gives its variables by aggregating match masses. Conversely, each positive variable specifies one legal pair; the consumption equations partition all current mass, and the survival equations give the next state. Add the favorite’s designated unit opponent in each round. There are finitely many variables, so the constructed schedule uses finitely many positive blocks.

The system has $O(R(m+\tau))$ variables and constraints and polynomial coefficient bit length. Polynomial-time feasibility of rational linear systems therefore decides it, including feasibility over arbitrary real block masses.

Exchange lemma

Suppose $s,t\in A$, and $s$ defeats $t$ and every nonfavorite type defeated by $t$. If a successful schedule uses favorite opponents $s,t$ in consecutive rounds, it can instead use $t,s$ in those rounds while preserving all other states after the second round.

Proof. Trace the unit block of $t$ used in the second favorite match back through the preceding round. Refine its winning pairs as necessary. Its production uses one unit of winning mass of type $t$ and one unit of loser mass. Each loser is either another block of $t$ or a type defeated by $t$.

The original unit block of $s$ facing the favorite is disjoint from these masses: $t$ cannot defeat $s$.

Replace the first favorite match by $f\to t$, using the extracted unit of winning mass of $t$. Split the original unit of $s$ to defeat all the extracted loser blocks. Every such victory is valid by hypothesis. After that round, the extracted unit of $t$ has been replaced by one unit of $s$, and all other surviving masses are unchanged.

In the next round, use $f\to s$. All other matches remain as before. After this round, the state agrees exactly with the original schedule.

Application under the promise

Assume
$$ A=\{a,t\},\qquad a\to t, $$
every type in $B=U\setminus A$ defeats $t$, and $a$ defeats at least one type in $B$. No restriction is imposed on $G[B]$.

These instances satisfy the promise. A triangle through $f$ exists using an arc $a\to b$ with $b\in B$. Reversing $f\to a$ leaves $t$ as the sole outneighbor of $f$, and every other nonfavorite defeats $t$. Thus no cycle through $f$ remains.

Furthermore, $t$ defeats no nonfavorite type: it loses to $a$ and to every type in $B$. The exchange lemma therefore permits every consecutive occurrence $a,t$ to be exchanged.

Repeatedly applying it puts all appearances of $a$ after all appearances of $t$. Hence a successful schedule exists if and only if one of the following $R+1$ sequences has a feasible LP:
$$ (\underbrace{t,\ldots,t}_{R-k}, \underbrace{a,\ldots,a}_{k}), \qquad 0\leq k\leq R. $$

This gives a polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,R,L$, even when $G[B]$ is arbitrary.
