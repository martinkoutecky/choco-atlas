The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many schools and subjects, a rational mass distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) over teacher types, rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_{s,p}\), school rankings over types for each subject, and each type’s ranking of acceptable schools, does there exist a feasible assignment flow \(x_{t,s}\) with no positive-mass blocking deviation? A blocking deviation moves some \(\delta>0\) mass of type \(t\) from an unassigned or less-preferred destination to school \(s\), which can accept it either using slack in both subjects or by one of the paper’s permitted displacement patterns, with capacities feasible after the exchange and the displaced mass ranked below \(t\) in the relevant subject or subjects.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools and \(P\) a set of \(k\geq2\) subjects. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) teacher types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type specifies two distinct subjects \(P(t)\), an acceptable-school set \(A_t\), and a strict ranking of \(A_t\), with every acceptable school preferred to unemployment.
Each school has nonnegative binary rational capacities \(\kappa_{s,p}\) and, for each subject \(p\), a strict ranking \(\succ_{s,p}\) of the types applying there whose specialization contains \(p\). Individuals of the same type are indistinguishable in these rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\).
A feasible flow has real entries \(x_{t,s}\geq0\), zero outside acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}\leq\mu_t\) and loads \(\ell_{s,p}=\sum_{t:p\in P(t)}x_{t,s}\leq\kappa_{s,p}\). Write \(x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_s x_{t,s}\).
A blocking deviation moves \(\delta>0\) mass of type \(t\), with \(P(t)=\{p,q\}\), from a destination \(d\) having \(x_{t,d}\geq\delta\) to an acceptable school \(s\) strictly preferred to \(d\), where \(d\) may be \(\bot\). School \(s\) accepts through one of these four patterns:

  • Remove nothing, with at least \(\delta\) slack in both \(p\) and \(q\).
  • For \((a,b)\in\{(p,q),(q,p)\}\), use at least \(\delta\) slack in \(a\) and remove \(\delta\) mass of a type \(u\) with \(b\in P(u)\) and \(t\succ_{s,b}u\).
  • Remove \(\delta\) mass of one type \(u\) with \(P(u)=\{p,q\}\), \(t\succ_{s,p}u\), and \(t\succ_{s,q}u\).
  • Remove two disjoint portions of mass \(\delta\), of types \(u,v\), with \(p\in P(u)\), \(q\in P(v)\), \(t\succ_{s,p}u\), and \(t\succ_{s,q}v\). These types may coincide, requiring \(x_{u,s}\geq2\delta\) in that case.

All removed mass must currently be assigned to \(s\); it becomes unemployed, and the resulting flow must remain feasible. A flow is stable if it admits no blocking deviation.
Is deciding whether a stable feasible flow exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
Discrete stable-assignment existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even with three subjects, capacities at most one, subject-specific master rankings of teachers, and a common master ranking of schools; a master ranking means each relevant preference list is its restriction. [Source](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/129124/7/129124.pdf). No complexity classification for exactly the mass model above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total teacher mass to one, interpreting capacities in the same per-capita units; allowed nonnegative rational capacities, including zero.
  • Made a distributional type specify its subject pair and school preferences, with schools ranking support types strictly and treating individuals within a type identically.
  • Required two distinct specialization subjects, strict preferences, assignment only to acceptable schools, and unemployment below every acceptable school.
  • Allowed real divisible flows and positive real deviation sizes; a deviation draws its entering mass from one worse destination or unemployment.
  • Specified the four displacement patterns by scaling each displaced applicant to a portion equal to the entering mass, including the pattern that removes twice the entering mass.
  • Interpreted two different displaced applicants as two disjoint mass portions, allowing them to have the same distributional type when enough mass is available; displaced portions become unemployed.
  • Retained general school and teacher rankings from the graded question; the anchor's master-list and capacity restrictions describe its stronger discrete hardness result rather than restricting the continuous input.

Result

The existence decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses admits a stable feasible flow.

The proof uses Scarf’s lemma. The essential step is to verify that its domination condition agrees with the precise blocking deviations in the question.

1. Characterizing blocking deviations

Fix a feasible flow \(x\). For an acceptable pair \((t,s)\), define the mass of type \(t\) at destinations worse than \(s\) by
\[ D_{t,s} = x_{t,\bot} + \sum_{\substack{r\in A_t\\s\succ_t r}}x_{t,r}. \]
Here \(\succ_t\) denotes type \(t\)’s ranking, extended by placing unemployment last. Because the destination set is finite, \(D_{t,s}>0\) holds exactly when some permissible source destination contains positive mass.

For a subject \(a\in P(t)\), define
\[ h_{s,a}=\kappa_{s,a}-\ell_{s,a}, \qquad B_{t,s,a} = \{u:s\in A_u,\ a\in P(u),\ t\succ_{s,a}u,\ x_{u,s}>0\}. \]
Say that subject \(a\) is available for the deviation if \(h_{s,a}>0\) or \(B_{t,s,a}\ne\varnothing\).

Lemma. If \(P(t)=\{p,q\}\), a blocking deviation to \(s\) exists exactly when \(D_{t,s}>0\) and both \(p\) and \(q\) are available.

Proof. Necessity follows from the four permitted patterns. Each requires positive source mass and, for each incoming subject, either positive slack or removal of positive mass ranked below \(t\) in that subject.

For sufficiency, choose a destination \(d\) worse than \(s\) with \(x_{t,d}>0\). There are three cases:

  • If both subjects have positive slack, use the first pattern.
  • If exactly one subject has positive slack, select a type from \(B_{t,s,a}\) for the other subject and use the second pattern.
  • If neither subject has positive slack, select \(u\in B_{t,s,p}\) and \(v\in B_{t,s,q}\), and use the fourth pattern.

Choose \(\delta>0\) no larger than the source mass, every slack used, and every selected removable mass. In the third case, if \(u=v\), additionally require \(\delta\le x_{u,s}/2\). All these bounds are positive, so such a \(\delta\) exists.

The resulting flow is feasible: each incoming subject receives sufficient slack or loses at least \(\delta\) mass before gaining \(\delta\); other subject loads only decrease. The source destination loses mass, and all removed portions become unemployed. Strict school rankings ensure that neither removed type is \(t\). Thus all nonnegativity and type-mass constraints also remain satisfied. \(\square\)

Consequently, stability is equivalent to requiring, for every acceptable \((t,s)\), at least one of
\[ D_{t,s}=0, \tag{1} \]
\[ \ell_{s,p}=\kappa_{s,p} \quad\text{and}\quad B_{t,s,p}=\varnothing, \tag{2} \]
\[ \ell_{s,q}=\kappa_{s,q} \quad\text{and}\quad B_{t,s,q}=\varnothing. \tag{3} \]

2. The theorem used

We use the following inequality form of Scarf’s lemma.

Let \(Q\) be a finite nonnegative matrix with no zero columns, let \(b\) have strictly positive coordinates, and suppose
\[ F=\{z:z\ge0,\ Qz\le b\} \]
is bounded. Each row \(i\) has a strict preference order on the columns \(j\) with \(Q_{ij}>0\).

Then there exists \(z\in F\) such that every column \(j\) is dominated by some incident row \(i\): that row is tight, \((Qz)_i=b_i\), and every column \(h\) with \(Q_{ih}z_h>0\) is ranked at least as highly as \(j\) by row \(i\).

This formulation, including the definition of domination, appears in Definition 3.1 and Lemma 3.1 of [Nguyen and Vohra, *Near-Feasible Stable Matchings with Couples*](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~nguye161/e2sided.pdf#page=9).

3. Applying Scarf’s lemma

First remove every acceptable pair \((t,s)\) for which \(\kappa_{s,a}=0\) for some \(a\in P(t)\). Such a pair necessarily has \(x_{t,s}=0\) in every feasible flow. Moreover, it cannot receive a blocking deviation: its zero-capacity subject has zero slack and no positive assigned mass to remove.

Let \(E\) be the remaining acceptable pairs. If \(E=\varnothing\), the all-unemployed flow is stable, so assume \(E\ne\varnothing\).

Construct a matrix \(Q\) with columns indexed by \(E\). Its rows are indexed by teacher types and school-subject pairs, with zero rows omitted. For a column \(e=(t,s)\), put a \(1\) in precisely these three rows:

  • the type row \(t\);
  • the school-subject row \((s,p)\);
  • the school-subject row \((s,q)\), where \(P(t)=\{p,q\}\).

All other entries are zero. Set the corresponding bounds to
\[ b_t=\mu_t, \qquad b_{(s,a)}=\kappa_{s,a}. \]

Give type row \(t\) its strict ranking of the schools represented in its columns. Give row \((s,a)\) the strict ranking \(\succ_{s,a}\) of the types represented there.

We now check the hypotheses:

  • Every column is nonzero; indeed, it has exactly three \(1\) entries.
  • Every retained bound is positive. Type masses are positive by assumption, and columns incident with zero-capacity subject rows were removed.
  • The prescribed row orders are strict restrictions of the input rankings.
  • The feasible set is nonempty because it contains zero.
  • It is bounded because the type constraints imply \(0\le z_{t,s}\le\mu_t\le1\) for every column.

Scarf’s lemma therefore supplies a feasible flow \(x\) dominating every retained column. Extend it by zero on the removed pairs.

4. Domination implies stability

Fix a retained column \((t,s)\), where \(P(t)=\{p,q\}\). Its dominating row must be one of its three incident rows.

If the dominating row is \(t\), then
\[ \sum_{r\in A_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t, \]
and every school receiving positive type-\(t\) mass is ranked at least as highly as \(s\). Hence \(x_{t,\bot}=0\), all worse destinations have zero mass, and \(D_{t,s}=0\). Condition (1) holds.

If the dominating row is \((s,p)\), then \(\ell_{s,p}=\kappa_{s,p}\), and every positive assigned type containing \(p\) is ranked at least as highly as \(t\) in \(\succ_{s,p}\). Thus \(B_{t,s,p}=\varnothing\), giving condition (2).

If the dominating row is \((s,q)\), the same reasoning gives condition (3).

Therefore every retained pair satisfies the stability characterization. Removed pairs cannot block, as proved above. The extended flow is stable. This proves universal existence for the exact mass model in the question.

5. Decision complexity

On inputs satisfying the stated hypotheses, the decision algorithm simply returns YES. Its running time is \(O(1)\), or \(O(L)\) if the complete input is read. Thus it is polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

This running-time statement concerns the requested existence decision. The proof establishes existence through Scarf’s lemma; it does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for constructing the flow. The discrete hardness result presents no contradiction, because this argument imposes no integrality requirement.
