Partial result: new two-center bounds

I do not establish a polynomial-time algorithm or an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction for the full decision problem. I prove the following additional results:

  • For every admissible mass parameter $\alpha$,

$$ N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)=2 \qquad\text{whenever}\qquad \frac{5}{16}\le\epsilon<\frac12. $$

  • If $\alpha\ne 1/2$, any cover with two centers has radius at least $33/128$. Consequently,

$$ \epsilon<\frac{33}{128} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)\ge3. $$

  • For any prescribed finite family of step-function centers, every target has a step-function replacement with a bounded number of pieces preserving its distances to all centers. This gives an exact reduction of verification of such a cover to finitely many systems of polynomial inequalities.

All arguments below are independent of the notes.

1. Reduction to monotone threshold functions

1.1. Every area class has an admissible representative

Write $a=\alpha$ and $b=1-\alpha$. For any measurable upward-closed set $A\subseteq[0,a]\times[0,b]$, define
$$ B(A)= \bigl(A\cap((0,a)\times(0,b))\bigr) \cup\bigl(\{a\}\times[0,b]\bigr) \cup\bigl((0,a]\times\{b\}\bigr). $$
This changes $A$ only on the boundary of the rectangle, so
$$ \lambda_2(A\triangle B(A))=0. $$

The set $B(A)$ is measurable and upward-closed. Indeed, increasing a winning interior point either leaves it in the interior of $A$ or places it on a prescribed winning edge. Increasing a point on either prescribed edge leaves it on a winning edge.

The origin is losing and the upper-right corner is winning. The pairs
$$ (0,0),(a,0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (a/2,0),(a/2,b) $$
witness relevance of the first and second coordinates, respectively.

Distinctness of the types also holds:

  • If $a\le b$, use $\delta=a$ and $z=(0,0)$. The point $(a,0)$ wins and $(0,a)$ loses.
  • If $a>b$, use $\delta=b$ and $z=((a-b)/2,0)$. The point $((a+b)/2,0)$ loses and $((a-b)/2,b)$ wins.

These witnesses satisfy the bounds required in the question. Thus every area-equivalence class, including the empty and full area classes, has an admissible representative.

This boundary observation does not trivialize the covering problem: it removes the pointwise restrictions from the metric classes that must be covered.

1.2. Normalization and threshold representation

The map
$$ T_a(x,y)=(x/a,y/b) $$
preserves coordinate order and satisfies
$$ d(W,V)=\lambda_2(T_aW\triangle T_aV). $$
Hence, for unequal masses, the covering number is independent of $\alpha$.

Let $\mathscr M$ be the space of nonincreasing functions $f:[0,1]\to[0,1]$, identified when equal almost everywhere, with distance
$$ \Delta(f,g)=\int_0^1|f(x)-g(x)|\,dx. $$

For a measurable upward-closed set $A\subseteq[0,1]^2$, its vertical section $A_x$ is an upper interval. Define
$$ f_A(x)=1-\lambda_1(A_x). $$
Upward closure makes $f_A$ nonincreasing. Each section differs from $\{y:y>f_A(x)\}$ by at most an endpoint. Conversely, every $f\in\mathscr M$ defines the upward-closed region
$$ A_f=\{(x,y):y>f(x)\}. $$
Fubini’s theorem therefore gives
$$ \lambda_2(A_f\triangle A_g)=\Delta(f,g). $$

Let $C(\epsilon)$ be the covering number of $\mathscr M$. Let $Q(\epsilon)$ be the covering number after identifying the corresponding regions under coordinate interchange. The admissible-representative construction proves
$$ N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)= \begin{cases} C(\epsilon),&\alpha\ne1/2,\\ Q(\epsilon),&\alpha=1/2. \end{cases} $$
For the quotient, projecting any cover proves
$$ Q(\epsilon)\le C(\epsilon). $$

We will use the functional
$$ \nu(f)=\int_0^1f(x)\,dx. $$
It is the normalized losing area and satisfies
$$ |\nu(f)-\nu(g)|\le\Delta(f,g). $$
Coordinate interchange preserves losing area, so the same inequality holds for quotient distance.

1.3. Positive radii have finite covers

For completeness, fix a positive integer $n$. Consider step functions taking values $q_i/n$ on the intervals $((i-1)/n,i/n)$, where
$$ n\ge q_1\ge\cdots\ge q_n\ge0 $$
and all $q_i$ are integers. There are $\binom{2n}{n}$ such functions.

For a pointwise nonincreasing representative $f$, put
$$ c_i=\frac{f((i-1)/n)+f(i/n)}2. $$
Monotonicity implies
$$ \int_{(i-1)/n}^{i/n}|f(x)-c_i|\,dx \le \frac{f((i-1)/n)-f(i/n)}{2n}. $$
Summation gives total error at most $1/(2n)$. Rounding each $c_i$ to the nearest multiple of $1/n$, with a consistent tie rule, preserves their order and adds at most $1/(2n)$ to the integrated error.

Thus this finite family is a $1/n$-net. Taking $n=\lceil1/\epsilon\rceil$ proves finiteness of the covering number for every positive $\epsilon$.

2. Basic lower bounds and the one-center case

Define constant and step thresholds by
$$ c_t(x)=t, \qquad s_t(x)=\mathbf1_{[0,t)}(x). $$
Write
$$ c=c_{1/2}, \qquad s=s_{1/2}. $$

The four functions
$$ 0,\quad 1,\quad c,\quad s $$
have pairwise distance at least $1/2$: the distance between $0$ and $1$ is $1$, and all other distances are $1/2$. A closed ball of radius less than $1/4$ contains at most one of them. Hence
$$ C(\epsilon)\ge4 \qquad\text{if}\qquad \epsilon<1/4. $$

In the coordinate-interchange quotient, $c$ and $s$ represent the same orbit. The three orbits represented by $0,1,c$ still have pairwise distance at least $1/2$. Consequently,
$$ Q(\epsilon)\ge3 \qquad\text{if}\qquad \epsilon<1/4. $$

A single center $f$ has distances $\nu(f)$ and $1-\nu(f)$ from $0$ and $1$, respectively. This remains true in the quotient. Thus one center requires radius at least $1/2$.

Conversely,
$$ \Delta(f,c)\le\frac12 $$
for every $f\in\mathscr M$, because the integrand is pointwise at most $1/2$. Therefore
$$ N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \epsilon\ge\frac12. $$

3. An explicit two-center cover of radius $5/16$

Define
$$ l_*=\min(s,c), \qquad u_*=\max(s,c). $$
Thus $l_*$ equals $1/2$ on the left half and $0$ on the right half, while $u_*$ equals $1$ on the left half and $1/2$ on the right half. Their integrals are
$$ \nu(l_*)=\frac14, \qquad \nu(u_*)=\frac34. $$

We prove
$$ \min\{\Delta(f,l_*),\Delta(f,u_*)\}\le\frac{5}{16} \qquad\text{for every }f\in\mathscr M. $$

3.1. An order dichotomy

Every nonincreasing $f$ satisfies at least one of
$$ f\ge l_*\quad\text{almost everywhere}, \qquad f\le u_*\quad\text{almost everywhere}. $$
Indeed, if $f(x)\ge1/2$ throughout the left half, the first inequality holds. Otherwise, some point of the left half has value below $1/2$, and monotonicity makes all subsequent values below $1/2$. This implies the second inequality.

Define the isometry
$$ (Jf)(x)=1-f(1-x). $$
It preserves $\mathscr M$ and exchanges $l_*$ and $u_*$. Consequently, it suffices to treat the case $f\le u_*$.

3.2. Estimating the distances

Assume $f\le u_*$ and put $v=\nu(f)$. Then
$$ \Delta(f,u_*)=\frac34-v. $$
If $v\ge7/16$, this is at most $5/16$.

Suppose instead that $v<7/16$. Set
$$ t=\lambda_1\{x:f(x)>1/2\}, \qquad P=\int_0^t f(x)\,dx. $$
Monotonicity and $f\le u_*$ imply
$$ 0\le t\le\frac12, \qquad 0\le P\le t. $$
Splitting the integral at $t$ and $1/2$ gives
$$ \Delta(f,l_*) = \frac14+v-t-2\int_t^{1/2}f(x)\,dx. $$

The average of a nonincreasing function over an initial portion of an interval is at least its average over the entire interval. Applied to $(t,1)$, this gives
$$ \int_t^{1/2}f(x)\,dx \ge \frac{1/2-t}{1-t}(v-P). $$
For $t=1/2$, both sides are zero. For $t<1/2$, the inequality follows by integrating $f(x)\ge f(y)$ over $t<x<1/2<y<1$ and rearranging.

Using $P\le t$ and $1-2t\ge0$, we obtain
$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta(f,l_*) &\le \frac14+v-t-\frac{1-2t}{1-t}(v-P)\\ &\le \frac14+\frac{t(v-t)}{1-t}. \end{aligned} $$

For $0\le v<1$, the identity
$$ \left(1-\sqrt{1-v}\right)^2 -\frac{t(v-t)}{1-t} = \frac{\left(t-(1-\sqrt{1-v})\right)^2}{1-t} $$
shows that
$$ \frac{t(v-t)}{1-t} \le \left(1-\sqrt{1-v}\right)^2. $$
Since $v<7/16$,
$$ \left(1-\sqrt{1-v}\right)^2\le\frac1{16}. $$
Therefore $\Delta(f,l_*)\le5/16$. Applying $J$ handles the other branch of the order dichotomy.

This proves
$$ C(5/16)\le2, \qquad Q(5/16)\le2. $$

3.3. Exactness for this particular pair

Define
$$ F(x)= \begin{cases} 1,&0\le x<1/4,\\ 1/4,&1/4\le x\le1. \end{cases} $$
Direct calculation yields
$$ \Delta(F,l_*) = \frac14\cdot\frac12 +\frac14\cdot\frac14 +\frac12\cdot\frac14 = \frac5{16}, $$
and
$$ \Delta(F,u_*) = \frac14\cdot0 +\frac14\cdot\frac34 +\frac12\cdot\frac14 = \frac5{16}. $$

The regions associated with $l_*$ and $u_*$ are, up to null boundaries,
$$ \{x>1/2\text{ or }y>1/2\}, \qquad \{x>1/2\text{ and }y>1/2\}. $$
Both are invariant under coordinate interchange. Thus their quotient distances from every target equal their ordinary distances, and this pair also has covering radius exactly $5/16$ in the quotient.

Combining the cover with the one-center lower bound proves the exact result
$$ \boxed{ N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)=2 \quad\text{for every }\alpha \text{ and every }\frac5{16}\le\epsilon<\frac12. } $$
The optimal choice of two centers at smaller radii remains open.

4. A universal two-center lower bound of $33/128$ for unequal masses

We now work in $\mathscr M$ without the coordinate-interchange quotient.

Suppose two centers cover $\mathscr M$ at radius $r$. The packing in Section 2 implies $r\ge1/4$. If $r\ge1/2$, the desired lower bound already holds. Hence assume
$$ \frac14\le r<\frac12, \qquad \eta=r-\frac14. $$

The functions $0$ and $1$ cannot be covered by the same center. Relabel the centers as $l,u$ so that
$$ \nu(l)\le r, \qquad \nu(u)\ge1-r. $$

4.1. Both centers must approach two canonical targets

Choose any $h$ satisfying
$$ 2\eta<h<\frac12. $$
The four targets
$$ c_{1/2-h},\quad c_{1/2+h}, \quad s_{1/2-h},\quad s_{1/2+h} $$
have integrals $1/2-h$ or $1/2+h$.

The center $u$ cannot cover either target of integral $1/2-h$, because its distance from such a target is at least
$$ (1-r)-(1/2-h) = \frac12-r+h > r. $$
Therefore $l$ covers both of those targets. Similarly, $l$ cannot cover either target of integral $1/2+h$, so $u$ covers both of them.

Since
$$ \Delta(c_{1/2\pm h},c)=h, \qquad \Delta(s_{1/2\pm h},s)=h, $$
the triangle inequality gives
$$ \Delta(l,c),\Delta(l,s), \Delta(u,c),\Delta(u,s) \le r+h. $$
Letting $h$ decrease to $2\eta$, we conclude
$$ \Delta(l,c),\Delta(l,s), \Delta(u,c),\Delta(u,s) \le r+2\eta. $$

4.2. A quantitative median inequality

For every function $g:[0,1]\to[0,1]$,
$$ \nu(g)+\Delta(g,s)+\Delta(g,c) \ge \frac34+\Delta(g,l_*). $$

To verify this pointwise, write $z=g(x)$. On the left half,
$$ z+|z-1|+|z-1/2| = 1+|z-1/2|. $$
On the right half,
$$ z+|z|+|z-1/2| = 2z+|z-1/2| \ge \frac12+z. $$
Integration proves the inequality.

Applying it to $l$ gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta(l,l_*) &\le \nu(l)+\Delta(l,s)+\Delta(l,c)-\frac34\\ &\le r+2(r+2\eta)-\frac34\\ &=7\eta. \end{aligned} $$

The isometry $J$ fixes $c$ and $s$, exchanges $l_*$ and $u_*$, and satisfies $\nu(Ju)=1-\nu(u)$. Applying the same inequality to $Ju$ therefore gives
$$ \Delta(u,u_*)\le7\eta. $$

4.3. A target forcing a larger radius

Use the function $F$ from Section 3.3, whose distances from $l_*$ and $u_*$ are both $5/16$. The reverse triangle inequality gives
$$ \Delta(F,l)\ge\frac5{16}-7\eta, \qquad \Delta(F,u)\ge\frac5{16}-7\eta. $$
Since one center must cover $F$,
$$ r\ge\frac5{16}-7\left(r-\frac14\right). $$
Rearranging,
$$ 8r\ge\frac{33}{16}, \qquad r\ge\frac{33}{128}. $$

Thus
$$ \boxed{ \alpha\ne\frac12,\quad \epsilon<\frac{33}{128} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)\ge3. } $$

The proof uses ordinary distances from both $c$ and $s$. In the equal-mass quotient those targets represent the same orbit, so this argument does not establish the same lower bound there.

5. Finite witnesses for prescribed step-function centers

The next result addresses exact verification of a proposed cover, rather than optimization over all possible centers.

Lemma. Suppose $g_1,\ldots,g_k\in\mathscr M$ are step functions constant on a common partition of $[0,1]$ into $p$ intervals. Let
$$ V=\{0,1\}\cup \{\text{all values taken by the centers}\} = \{v_0<\cdots<v_q\}. $$
For every $f\in\mathscr M$, there is a nonincreasing step function $\widehat f$ with at most $p+q-1$ pieces such that
$$ \Delta(\widehat f,g_i)=\Delta(f,g_i) \qquad\text{for every }i. $$

Proof. For each interior level $v_j$, define
$$ t_j=\lambda_1\{x:f(x)>v_j\}. $$
Because $f$ is nonincreasing, this superlevel set is an initial interval up to its endpoint.

Partition $[0,1]$ using the centers’ $p-1$ interior breakpoints and these $q-1$ crossing points. There are at most $p+q-1$ resulting intervals.

On each positive-length interval $I$, all centers are constant, and $f$ takes values almost everywhere within some adjacent-level interval $[v_j,v_{j+1}]$. No center value lies strictly between these adjacent levels. Therefore, for every center value $z$, the function
$$ w\longmapsto|w-z| $$
is affine on $[v_j,v_{j+1}]$.

Replace $f$ on $I$ by its average
$$ \widehat f_I=\frac1{\lambda_1(I)}\int_I f(x)\,dx. $$
Affineness gives
$$ \int_I|f(x)-z|\,dx = \lambda_1(I)|\widehat f_I-z|. $$
The averages on successive intervals are nonincreasing, because $f$ is nonincreasing. Thus the replacement belongs to $\mathscr M$ and preserves every center distance. $\square$

For quotient covers, apply the lemma to both each center region and its transpose. The transpose of a region defined by a step threshold again has a step threshold: the region is a finite union of rectangles, and transposition preserves that property. Preserving all distances to these representatives preserves their minima defining quotient distances.

5.1. Exact reduction to polynomial inequalities

Suppose the prescribed centers have rational values and breakpoints. Put $s=p+q-1$. Parameterize a target with at most $s$ pieces by
$$ 0=t_0\le t_1\le\cdots\le t_s=1, \qquad 1\ge h_1\ge\cdots\ge h_s\ge0. $$
Zero-length pieces permit fewer than $s$ pieces.

If the common center breakpoints are $b_0,\ldots,b_p$, and center $i$ has value $z_{i,j}$ on its $j$th interval, its distance from this target is
$$ \sum_{a=1}^s\sum_{j=1}^p \max\!\left\{ 0,\, \min(t_a,b_j)-\max(t_{a-1},b_{j-1}) \right\} |h_a-z_{i,j}|. $$

After finitely many case distinctions concerning the orders of breakpoints and heights, each summand is a product of two affine expressions. Thus the distances are polynomial expressions of degree at most two on each case.

By the lemma, the prescribed family fails to cover at radius $\epsilon$ exactly when one of these finite systems has a solution satisfying
$$ \Delta(f,g_i)>\epsilon \qquad\text{for every }i. $$
For the quotient, require this inequality for both representatives of every center orbit.

This reduction is exact, including closed-ball boundary cases. It does not establish a bound on the number of pieces needed for optimal centers, or a polynomial running-time bound.

6. Consequences for the decision problem and remaining gaps

Since $m=\tau=2$, polynomial time here means polynomial in the total binary input length $L$. The proved regimes can be decided by rational comparisons in polynomial time:

| Input condition | Exact decision consequence |
|---|---|
| $\epsilon\ge1/2$ | Affirmative for every $k\ge1$. |
| $k=1$ and $\epsilon<1/2$ | Negative. |
| $5/16\le\epsilon<1/2$ | Affirmative exactly when $k\ge2$. |
