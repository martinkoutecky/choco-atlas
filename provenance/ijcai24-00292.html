Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), competence values \(q_t\), an allowed typed delegation graph \(H\), and \(\rho>0\), choose real masses \(r_t\) and \(y_{t,u}\) satisfying \(r_t+\sum_{u\in N_H(t)}y_{t,u}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t r_t=\rho\), nonnegativity, and acyclic positive support, maximizing atomless weighted-majority accuracy: \(1\) if \(\sum_t q_t r_t>\rho/2\), \(0\) if it is below \(\rho/2\), and \(1/2\) at equality.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. Let \(T\) be a finite type set, \(H\) an undirected allowed-delegation graph, \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and competence \(q_t\in[0,1]\). A policy has \(x_{tt}\) as type \(t\)'s direct-voting fraction and \(x_{tu}\) as its fraction delegating to neighbor \(u\), with \(\sum_{u\in N_H(t)\cup\{t\}}x_{tu}=1\); the positive non-self arcs must be acyclic. For \(\alpha\in\{1/m,1\}\), let \(W_r(x)\) be the total mass reaching direct-voting type \(r\), with each delegation hop discounted by \(\alpha\). Define atomless-limit correctness as the fixed-tie-broken majority predicate of \(B(x)=\sum_{r\in T}W_r(x)(2q_r-1)\).

Target. Is deciding whether an acyclic mass-delegation policy \(x\) with atomless-limit correctness at least \( heta\) exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Nearest known results. The discrete Optimal Delegation Graph problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for \(\alpha=1/m\); its \(\alpha=1\) restriction is also \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and \(1/16\)-inapproximable.

Prover output (gpt-5.6-sol / xhigh, Bolzano prover prompt)

Complexity Classification

1. Finite-input convention

A Turing-complexity classification requires finitely encoded inputs. Accordingly, assume that every \(q_t\), \(\mu_t\), and \(\theta\) is rational and binary encoded, and that \(m\) is either fixed or binary encoded.

Let \(a_t=2q_t-1\). Write \(\kappa(B)\in\{0,1\}\) for the fixed-tie-broken majority predicate: it is \(1\) when \(B>0\), \(0\) when \(B<0\), and has the prescribed value at \(B=0\).

The nontrivial case is \(0<\theta\le 1\), when the question is whether some policy has \(\kappa(B)=1\).

2. Fractional policies can be rounded

For a policy \(x\), define \(V_t(x)\) as the signed contribution to \(B(x)\) produced by one unit of mass entering type \(t\).

Because the positive non-self support is acyclic, every delegation trajectory terminates after at most \(|T|-1\) hops. Consequently,

\(V_t(x)=x_{tt}a_t+\alpha\sum_{u\in N_H(t)}x_{tu}V_u(x)\),

and

\(B(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tV_t(x)\).

Lemma 1: deterministic rounding

For every feasible fractional acyclic policy \(x\), there is a deterministic acyclic policy \(f\), where each type either votes directly or delegates all its mass to one neighbor, such that \(B(f)\ge B(x)\).

Proof

Take a topological ordering of the positive non-self support of \(x\), with every arc directed forward, and process the vertices in reverse topological order.

At a currently processed vertex \(t\), the quantities \(a_t\) and \(\alpha V_u(x)\) for its positive outgoing choices \(u\) are already determined. The recurrence expresses \(V_t(x)\) as a convex combination of these quantities. Hence one choice having positive coefficient has value at least \(V_t(x)\).

Replace the row of \(t\) by the corresponding pure choice. This does not create a non-self arc outside the original support, so acyclicity is preserved. It weakly increases \(V_t\). Every continuation value affected upstream is a nonnegative linear combination of downstream continuation values, so no upstream value decreases. Since all \(\mu_t\) are nonnegative, \(B\) does not decrease.

After processing every vertex, all rows are deterministic. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

Thus the optimum over fractional policies equals the optimum over deterministic rooted forests.

For such a deterministic policy, let \(r_f(t)\) be the direct-voting root reached from \(t\), and let \(d_f(t)\) be the number of delegation hops. Then

\(V_t(f)=\alpha^{d_f(t)}a_{r_f(t)}\).

This also proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\): a deterministic map \(f\) is a polynomial-size certificate, acyclicity is checkable in polynomial time, and \(B(f)\) can be computed exactly using rational arithmetic.

3. The undiscounted case \(\alpha=1\)

For every connected component \(C\) of \(H\), define \(\mu(C)=\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t\) and \(a(C)=\max_{r\in C}a_r\).

Theorem 2

When \(\alpha=1\), the maximum attainable signed margin is

\(\operatorname{OPT}=\sum_C\mu(C)a(C)\).

It and an optimal policy can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof

Consider a deterministic policy. Every \(t\in C\) reaches some root \(r_f(t)\in C\), and therefore

\(V_t(f)=a_{r_f(t)}\le a(C)\).

It follows that the contribution from \(C\) is at most \(\mu(C)a(C)\).

For attainability, choose \(r_C\in C\) with \(a_{r_C}=a(C)\), choose a spanning tree of \(C\) rooted at \(r_C\), and let every other vertex delegate to its parent. Delegation strictly decreases tree distance to \(r_C\), so the resulting orientation is acyclic. Every type in \(C\) then has continuation value \(a(C)\).

Summing over components proves the formula. Connected components, maximizing roots, and rooted spanning trees can all be found in polynomial time. \(\square\)

Therefore, for \(0<\theta\le1\), the instance is accepted precisely when \(\operatorname{OPT}>0\), or when \(\operatorname{OPT}\ge0\) if ties are broken toward correctness. Hence the atomless problem with \(\alpha=1\) belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

4. The discounted case

Theorem 3

For every \(0<\alpha<1\), and in particular for \(\alpha=1/m\) with \(m\ge2\), the decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds with \(\theta=1\), rational competences, and strictly positive type masses.

Proof

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) follows from Lemma 1. It remains to prove hardness.

Reduce from undirected Hamiltonian Path. Given an undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\), add a new vertex \(s\) adjacent to every vertex of \(G\). Call the resulting graph \(K\), and put \(N=|V|+1\).

There is a Hamiltonian path in \(G\) if and only if \(K\) has a Hamiltonian path starting at \(s\). Indeed, a Hamiltonian path of \(G\) can be prefixed by \(s\); conversely, deleting the initial \(s\) from an \(s\)-starting Hamiltonian path in \(K\) leaves a Hamiltonian path using only edges of \(G\).

Let the allowed-delegation graph \(H\) be the disjoint union of \(K\) and one isolated vertex \(p\). Define

\(c=\bigl(\alpha^{N-1}+\alpha^{N-2}\bigr)/2\),

\(g=\bigl(\alpha^{N-2}-\alpha^{N-1}\bigr)/2>0\),

and \(\delta=g/8\).

Set \(a_t=-1\), equivalently \(q_t=0\), for every \(t\in V(K)\). Set \(a_p=c\), equivalently \(q_p=(1+c)/2\). Because \(0<c<1\), this is a valid competence.

Assign masses

\(\mu_s=\mu_p=(1-\delta)/2\)

and

\(\mu_v=\delta/(N-1)\)

for every \(v\in V\). These masses are strictly positive and sum to \(1\).

When \(\alpha=1/m\), the relevant numbers are

\(c=(m+1)/(2m^{N-1})\),

\(g=(m-1)/(2m^{N-1})\),

and

\(\delta=(m-1)/(16m^{N-1})\),

so the construction has encoding length polynomial in \(N\) and \(\log m\).

Suppose first that \(G\) has a Hamiltonian path. Orient the corresponding Hamiltonian path of \(K\) from \(s\) toward its last vertex, which votes directly. Then \(d_f(s)=N-1\), so the combined contribution of \(s\) and \(p\) is

\((1-\delta)\bigl(c-\alpha^{N-1}\bigr)/2=(1-\delta)g/2\).

Every remaining type has continuation value at least \(-1\), so their total contribution is at least \(-\delta\). Therefore

\(B(f)\ge (1-\delta)g/2-\delta=g(3/8-g/16)>0\).

Thus the atomless majority is correct.

Conversely, suppose \(G\) has no Hamiltonian path. Consider any deterministic feasible policy. The orbit of \(s\) is a simple path in \(K\). Since there is no Hamiltonian path starting at \(s\), its length \(d_f(s)\) satisfies \(d_f(s)\le N-2\). Hence

\(\alpha^{d_f(s)}\ge\alpha^{N-2}\).

All types in \(K\) have signed competence \(-1\), so all their continuation values are negative. Since \(\mu_s=\mu_p\),

\(B(f)\le\mu_s\bigl(c-\alpha^{d_f(s)}\bigr)\le\mu_s\bigl(c-\alpha^{N-2}\bigr)=-\mu_sg<0\).

By Lemma 1, no fractional policy can do better than every deterministic policy. Thus every fractional policy also has negative margin.

The reduction produces strict positive or negative margins, so its correctness is independent of the prescribed tie-breaking rule. This proves NP-hardness, and therefore NP-completeness. \(\square\)

5. Conclusion

The two discount regimes have different complexity:

  • For \(\alpha=1\), the problem is polynomial-time solvable by choosing, in each connected component, a maximum-competence root.
  • For \(\alpha=1/m\) with \(m\ge2\), the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Thus the known discrete \(\alpha=1\) hardness does not survive this atomless-limit objective. The collapse is caused by replacing finite-majority correctness with the sign of the deterministic aggregate margin. Fractional delegation itself is not responsible, since Lemma 1 shows that an optimal deterministic policy always exists.

Verification report

Scope and input convention

The prover correctly observes that a Turing-complexity classification requires finite encodings. Its conclusion is explicitly conditional on \(q_t\), \(\mu_t\), and \(\theta\) being binary-encoded rationals, with \(m\) fixed or binary encoded. Since the original task does not specify encodings for \(q_t\) or \(\theta\), this qualification is necessary.

Because the correctness predicate is binary, the reduction to deciding whether \(\kappa(B)=1\) for \(0<\theta\le 1\) is correct. The trivial case \(\theta>1\) is not mentioned, while \(\theta\le 0\) is only implicit.

Lemma 1: deterministic rounding

The underlying rounding claim is sound.

For a fixed acyclic support, the recurrence

\[ V_t=x_{tt}a_t+\alpha\sum_u x_{tu}V_u \]

is correct. Processing vertices in reverse topological order permits each row to be replaced by a pure choice of value at least its current convex-combination value. The chosen non-self arc belongs to the original support, so acyclicity is preserved. Upstream continuation values depend on the modified downstream value with nonnegative coefficients, so the objective cannot decrease.

There is a minor notation defect: the proof continues to write \(V_u(x)\) after downstream rows have already been replaced. These are generally continuation values of the current partially rounded policy, not of the original \(x\). A precise invariant distinguishing the original policy from the current policy is needed. This is an expositional gap rather than a substantive failure.

The conclusion that the fractional and deterministic optima coincide is correct: every fractional policy is dominated by a deterministic one, while deterministic policies form a subset of fractional policies.

The claimed membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) is also correct under the stated rational-input convention. A deterministic policy is polynomially encodable, acyclicity is efficiently checkable, and for \(\alpha=1/m\), each value \(\alpha^d a_r\) has polynomial encoding length.

The undiscounted case

The upper bound

\[ \sum_C \mu(C)a(C) \]

is correct because every type in a component reaches a root in the same component whose signed competence is at most \(a(C)\).

The construction using a spanning tree rooted at a maximum-competence vertex is feasible and acyclic. With \(\alpha=1\), every vertex routed to this root has continuation value \(a(C)\), so the upper bound is attained.

Thus the polynomial-time classification for \(\alpha=1\) is fully established.

The discounted reduction

The graph transformation is correct. A Hamiltonian path in \(G\) can be prefixed by the universal vertex \(s\), and deleting the initial \(s\) from an \(s\)-starting Hamiltonian path in \(K\) leaves a Hamiltonian path in \(G\).

For \(\alpha=1/m\), the constructed quantities are rational and have polynomial encoding length:

\[ c=\frac{m+1}{2m^{N-1}},\qquad g=\frac{m-1}{2m^{N-1}},\qquad \delta=\frac{m-1}{16m^{N-1}}. \]

The masses are strictly positive and sum to \(1\), and the competence assigned to \(p\) lies in the permitted interval.

In the yes case, the contribution from \(s\) and \(p\) is correctly computed as

\[ \frac{1-\delta}{2}\bigl(c-\alpha^{N-1}\bigr) =\frac{(1-\delta)g}{2}. \]

The remaining mass is \(\delta\), and each remaining continuation value is at least \(-1\). Therefore

\[ B(f)\ge \frac{(1-\delta)g}{2}-\delta =g\left(\frac38-\frac{g}{16}\right)>0. \]

This calculation is correct.

In the no case, the deterministic orbit of \(s\) is a simple path because the non-self support is acyclic. If it had \(N-1\) delegation hops, it would be an \(s\)-starting Hamiltonian path in \(K\); hence its length is at most \(N-2\). Since all contributions from the other vertices of \(K\) are negative, discarding them gives a valid upper bound:

\[ B(f)\le \mu_s\bigl(c-\alpha^{d_f(s)}\bigr) \le \mu_s\bigl(c-\alpha^{N-2}\bigr) =-\mu_sg<0. \]

Lemma 1 then excludes every fractional policy as well: each fractional policy is dominated by some deterministic policy, and every deterministic policy has negative margin.

Consequently, \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, and together with membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for \(\alpha=1/m\), \(m\ge2\), are established.

Unsupported overstatement

Theorem 3 is stated for every real \(0<\alpha<1\), but the finite-input convention and encoding-length argument only cover the required reciprocal-integer regime. For an arbitrary irrational \(\alpha\), the constructed \(c\), \(g\), and \(q_p\) need not be rational, and exact membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) is not justified. This does not affect the requested \(\alpha=1/m\) result, but the broader theorem statement must be restricted or supplied with an explicit finite representation model for \(\alpha\).

Verdict

The requested classification is correct under the explicitly stated rational-input convention. The proof has only minor presentational and scope defects: ambiguity in the rounding invariant, omission of the trivial \(\theta>1\) case, and an unsupported extension beyond the reciprocal-integer discount regime.
