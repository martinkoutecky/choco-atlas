The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of house types \(\mathcal H\), a finite support \(T\) of complete types \(t=(e_t,\succ_t)\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with supply \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\), determine whether a strict-core allocation exists and output one if it does. An allocation is \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=q_h\). A blocking coalition selects assigned masses and feasibly reallocates their endowed supply so all selected mass weakly prefers its reassignment and positive mass strictly prefers it. Does the paper's existence characterization, construction, and uniqueness result hold for this population model, and with what running time in \(|\mathcal H|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

Let \(\mathcal H\) be the nonempty set of endowed house types, with \(m=|\mathcal H|\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct complete types \(t=(e_t,\succ_t)\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(e_t\in\mathcal H\), and \(\succ_t\) is a strict total order on \(\mathcal H\); copies of the same house type are indifferent. The support size \(\tau\) is not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length and \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t>0\).
An allocation is a real matrix \(x\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_hx_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{t,h}=q_h\). Its entries describe masses receiving individual houses, rather than lotteries evaluated by agents.
A blocking coalition is represented by real masses \(z_{t,h,k}\geq0\), selecting agents of type \(t\) currently receiving \(h\) and reassigning them to \(k\), with \(\sum_kz_{t,h,k}\leq x_{t,h}\). For every \(k\in\mathcal H\), feasibility requires \(\sum_{t,h}z_{t,h,k}=\sum_{t:e_t=k}\sum_{h,j}z_{t,h,j}\). Require \(z_{t,h,k}=0\) unless \(k=h\) or \(k\succ_t h\), and \(\sum_{t,h,k:k\succ_t h}z_{t,h,k}>0\). An allocation is in the strict core exactly when no such coalition exists.
Define the mass HTTS procedure as follows. Initially \(R=\mathcal H\). Let \(b_t(R)\) be type \(t\)'s favorite remaining house type; draw \(e_t\to b_t(R)\) for every \(t\) with \(e_t\in R\), and select any sink strongly connected component \(S\). Assign all mass of every type endowed in \(S\) to \(b_t(R)\). Stop with failure unless \(\sum_{t:e_t\in S,\ b_t(R)=h}\mu_t=q_h\) for every \(h\in S\); otherwise remove \(S\) and its owners and repeat, succeeding when \(R\) is empty.

Is strict-core existence for this mass model in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can an allocation be computed, with what running time in \(m,\tau,L\)?

Result

Strict-core existence for the stated mass model is in \(\mathrm P\). Mass HTTS succeeds exactly when the strict core is nonempty, independently of sink choices. Whenever it succeeds, it produces the unique allocation matrix.

An implementation uses \(O(m^2+m\tau)\) graph and arithmetic operations after exact integer preprocessing. A conservative bit-complexity bound, including preprocessing, is
\(\displaystyle O\!\left(L^2+(m^2+m\tau)L\right)\).

The characterization below holds even for positive real masses. Rationality is needed only for the computational bound.

The cited [Sandholtz–Tai paper](https://taiandrew.github.io/research/files/Tai_HouseSwapwObjIndiff.pdf) establishes the finite-society characterization. The following proof works directly with the mass-blocking definition.

1. Blocking coalitions are circulations

Write \(k\succeq_t h\) when either \(k=h\) or \(k\succ_t h\).

For a feasible allocation \(x\), construct a directed multigraph \(G_x\) on the house types. For each triple \((t,h,k)\) satisfying \(x_{t,h}>0\) and \(k\succeq_t h\), include an arc
\(\displaystyle e_t\longrightarrow k\),
labelled by \((t,h,k)\). Mark it strict when \(k\succ_t h\).

Lemma 1. An allocation \(x\) is blocked if and only if \(G_x\) contains a directed cycle with at least one strict arc. Self-loops count as cycles.

Proof. Suppose such a cycle exists, and choose the labelled arc used at each step. Take a common positive mass \(\varepsilon\) no larger than any corresponding \(x_{t,h}\). Set \(z_{t,h,k}=\varepsilon\) on these labels and zero elsewhere.

We may take the cycle to have distinct vertices except for its repeated endpoint. Its arcs therefore have distinct endowment vertices, so no type, and hence no capacity \(x_{t,h}\), is used twice. At each cycle vertex, selected endowment mass and received mass both equal \(\varepsilon\). Thus the coalition is feasible. Every selected agent weakly improves, and a positive mass strictly improves.

Conversely, let \(z\) be a blocking coalition. Give each labelled arc weight \(z_{t,h,k}\), omitting zero weights. Coalition feasibility says exactly that incoming weight equals outgoing weight at every vertex:
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,h}z_{t,h,k} = \sum_{t:e_t=k}\sum_{h,j}z_{t,h,j}. \)
Thus these weights form a nonnegative circulation.

Every finite nonnegative circulation decomposes into directed cycles: follow positive-weight arcs until a vertex repeats, subtract the smallest weight on the resulting cycle, and repeat. Conservation is preserved, and each subtraction removes at least one positive-weight arc, so the process terminates. Because the original circulation has positive weight on strict arcs, at least one cycle in its decomposition contains a strict arc. \(\square\)

This lemma applies to arbitrary real entries of \(x\) and \(z\).

2. A sink component forces its owners’ assignments

Consider a market on a nonempty remaining house set \(R\), retaining precisely the types endowed in \(R\), with preferences restricted to \(R\). Its total mass need not equal one; all allocation and blocking equations remain unchanged.

Let \(D_R\) contain the arcs \(e_t\to b_t(R)\), and let \(S\) be a sink strongly connected component of \(D_R\).

Lemma 2. If \(x\) is a strict-core allocation of this remaining market, then every type endowed in \(S\) receives its favorite remaining house with its entire mass:
\(\displaystyle x_{t,b_t(R)}=\mu_t \qquad(e_t\in S). \)
Moreover,
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t:e_t\in S,\ b_t(R)=h}\mu_t=q_h \qquad(h\in S), \)
and types endowed outside \(S\) receive no mass of houses in \(S\).

Proof. Since \(S\) is a sink, \(b_t(R)\in S\) whenever \(e_t\in S\).

Suppose some such type has \(x_{t,h}>0\) for \(h\ne b_t(R)\). Its favorite is strictly preferred to \(h\), so \(G_x\) contains a strict arc \(e_t\to b_t(R)\).

Strong connectivity supplies a directed path in \(D_R\) from \(b_t(R)\) back to \(e_t\), entirely inside \(S\). Every arc \(e_u\to b_u(R)\) on this path is also available as a weak arc in \(G_x\): choose any \(h_u\) with \(x_{u,h_u}>0\), which exists because \(\mu_u>0\), and use \(b_u(R)\succeq_u h_u\). Taking a simple return path gives a cycle containing the designated strict arc. If \(e_t=b_t(R)\), that arc itself is a strict self-loop.

Lemma 1 now gives a block, a contradiction. Hence every owner in \(S\) receives only \(b_t(R)\).

Define its resulting demand for each \(h\in S\) by
\(\displaystyle d_h=\sum_{t:e_t\in S,\ b_t(R)=h}\mu_t.\)
Column feasibility gives \(d_h\le q_h\). Since all these favorites belong to \(S\),
\(\displaystyle \sum_{h\in S}d_h = \sum_{t:e_t\in S}\mu_t = \sum_{h\in S}q_h. \)
The nonnegative differences \(q_h-d_h\) therefore all vanish. Thus \(d_h=q_h\) for every \(h\in S\), and those owners exhaust every house supply in \(S\). No outside type can receive houses in \(S\). \(\square\)

Removal consequence. After deleting \(S\) and its owners, the restriction of \(x\) is feasible and remains in the strict core of the reduced market. Indeed, a blocking coalition in that reduced market would extend to a blocking coalition in the original market by setting all other coalition variables to zero.

3. Necessity and uniqueness

Suppose the original market has a strict-core allocation \(x\), and run mass HTTS with arbitrary sink choices.

At its first step, Lemma 2 forces exactly the assignments prescribed by HTTS and proves every required supply-demand equality. The step therefore succeeds. The removal consequence supplies a strict-core allocation of the remaining market.

Repeating this argument proves that every step succeeds and that \(x\) agrees with the algorithm’s assignment at every step. Since each step removes at least one house type, the procedure terminates successfully.

Consequently:

  • Strict-core existence implies success for every sequence of sink choices.
  • Any failed balance check certifies that the original strict core is empty.
  • Every strict-core allocation must equal the output of any fixed successful run, so there is at most one such allocation matrix.

It remains to prove that successful output is actually in the strict core.

4. Successful output cannot be blocked

Suppose HTTS succeeds, removing components \(S_1,\ldots,S_r\) in that order. Define
\(\displaystyle R_i=\bigcup_{j=i}^rS_j,\)
and let \(d(h)\) be the unique index with \(h\in S_{d(h)}\).

For each type \(t\), put
\(\displaystyle a_t=b_t(R_{d(e_t)}).\)
The output is
\(\displaystyle x^*_{t,h}= \begin{cases} \mu_t,&h=a_t,\\ 0,&h\ne a_t. \end{cases} \)
Each \(a_t\) belongs to its owner’s removal component. The successful balance checks establish all column sums, while the displayed definition establishes all row sums. Thus \(x^*\) is feasible.

Consider any arc \(e_t\to k\) of \(G_{x^*}\). Its current-house label must be \(a_t\), so \(k\succeq_t a_t\).

If \(d(k)\ge d(e_t)\), then \(k\in R_{d(e_t)}\). Since \(a_t\) is the unique favorite in that set, weak improvement requires \(k=a_t\). Such an arc is non-strict, and its endpoints have equal removal indices.

It follows that every arc satisfies
\(\displaystyle d(k)\le d(e_t),\)
and every strict arc satisfies
\(\displaystyle d(k)<d(e_t).\)
Along a directed cycle, removal indices cannot weakly decrease with at least one strict decrease and return to their starting value. Hence \(G_{x^*}\) has no cycle containing a strict arc. Lemma 1 proves that \(x^*\) is in the strict core.

Together with Section 3, this proves the characterization, uniqueness, and independence of sink choices. In particular, if one run succeeds, all runs succeed and return the same matrix; if the strict core is empty, every run fails.

5. Exact computation and running time

Assume preferences are supplied as explicit ordered lists.

Maintain a Boolean array indicating remaining houses and, for each active type, a pointer to its favorite remaining house. Whenever that favorite is removed, advance the pointer until it reaches a remaining house. Each pointer advances at most \(m\) times, so all pointer advancement costs \(O(m\tau)\).

At each iteration:

  • Build the graph on remaining houses, using at most one arc per active type.
  • Compute strongly connected components and select a sink.
  • Sum the masses demanding each house in the selected component and check equality with its supply.
  • Record assignments and remove the component and its owners.

A standard strongly connected component algorithm takes linear time in the number of vertices and arcs, here \(O(m+\tau)\) per iteration. There are at most \(m\) iterations. Including preference-pointer maintenance, the combinatorial operation count is therefore
\(\displaystyle O(m^2+m\tau).\)

To perform the balance checks exactly, write each input mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/d_t\), with positive integers represented in binary, and set
\(\displaystyle D=\prod_{t\in T}d_t, \qquad w_t=p_t(D/d_t), \qquad Q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}w_t. \)
For dyadic inputs, a common power-of-two denominator also suffices.

The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because denominator bit lengths sum to at most \(L\). Since \(\mu_t\le1\), each \(w_t\le D\). All supply and demand sums are also at most \(D\), since total mass is one.

Using schoolbook arithmetic, this integer conversion can be done in \(O(L^2)\) bit operations. To see the bound explicitly, write \(\ell_t\) for the bit length of \(d_t\) and \(a_t'\) for that of \(p_t\). Multiplying the denominators costs at most \(O(L\sum_t\ell_t)\); computing the divisions costs at most the same order; and multiplying the quotients by the numerators costs at most \(O(L\sum_t a_t')\). Both sums are bounded by \(L\).

Thereafter the checks use additions and comparisons of \(O(L)\)-bit integers. Charging \(O(L)\) bit operations for each combinatorial or arithmetic operation gives the conservative bound
\(\displaystyle O\!\left(L^2+(m^2+m\tau)L\right). \)
Thus the decision problem and allocation construction are polynomial in the input bit length. There is no need to expand the masses into a potentially exponentially large finite society.

The allocation has exactly one nonzero entry per support type, equal to its original mass. It can therefore be returned sparsely as the assignments \(t\mapsto a_t\), or explicitly as the full matrix.
