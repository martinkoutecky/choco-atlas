Theorem 4.3 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given item types \(G\), an independence-system oracle \(\mathcal F\), strict rankings for \(\tau\) agent types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), find a polynomial-bit rational assignment \(x_{t,S}\geq 0\), or certify none exists. Require \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal F}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\leq b_g\), so each agent receives a whole feasible bundle. Every pair of bundles assigned positive mass must satisfy EF1 under the first bundle’s type ranking; the assignment must also admit no feasible reassignment of the same agents that weakly improves every agent and strictly improves a positive mass. Is this solvable in time and oracle calls polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) contain \(m\geq1\) item types. A common independence system \(\mathcal F\subseteq2^G\), containing \(\emptyset\) and closed under subsets, is given by a membership oracle.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Each type supplies a strict ranking of \(G\); all items are desirable, and bundles are compared lexicographically by the highest-ranked item in their symmetric difference. Write \(\succeq_t\) for weak preference.
Supplies \(b_g\geq0\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total binary input length excluding the oracle. Each agent receives a whole bundle \(S\in\mathcal F\), hence at most one item of each type; unused supply is allowed.
An assignment consists of masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal F}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\leq b_g\).
Require EF1: whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\) and \(x_{u,T}>0\), some \(Z\subseteq T\) with \(|Z|\leq1\) satisfies \(S\succeq_t T\setminus Z\).
Require Pareto optimality: no reassignment may split the mass of each current group \((t,S)\) among feasible bundles weakly preferred to \(S\), respect the same supplies, and strictly improve a positive total mass. Reassignments may use real masses and need not satisfy EF1.
The computational problem is to find an assignment as a sparse list of nonzero rational entries with polynomial total encoding length, or report that no EF1 and Pareto-optimal assignment exists.

Can this problem be solved with time and oracle calls polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete: a polynomial-time round-robin algorithm is known to produce EF1 and Pareto-optimal allocations under a common independence system and lexicographic preferences. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01462v2)
Continuous resources: an efficient envy-free and Pareto-efficient mechanism is known for divisible goods with lexicographic preferences; that model does not require whole feasible bundles. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4366) No result settling the mass-of-agents problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted strict item rankings as lexicographic bundle preferences with every item strictly desirable, excluding a zero-valued last item that would make its presence indifferent.
  • Set supplies to nonnegative binary rationals and retained incomplete allocations with unused supply; bundles contain at most one item of each type.
  • Made Pareto comparisons individual: each current type-and-bundle group may be split among weakly preferred bundles, with positive mass strictly improving; competing reassignments may have real masses and need not satisfy EF1.
  • Interpreted polynomial-bit output as a sparse rational list with polynomial total encoding length, and defined L as the explicit input length excluding the membership oracle.
  • Required at least one item type; no stronger nondegeneracy restriction is imposed.

1. Elementary improvements

Write $g\succ_t h$ when type $t$ ranks item $g$ above item $h$. For an assignment $x$, define
$$ u_g(x)=\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}. $$

Its positive support is
$$ J(x)=\{(t,S):x_{t,S}>0\}. $$

For a supported group $(t,S)$ and an item $g\notin S$, define
$$ P_{t,g}(S)=\{h\in S:h\succ_t g\}, $$
$$ D_{t,g}(S)=\{h\in S:g\succ_t h\}, $$
and
$$ E_{t,g}(S)=P_{t,g}(S)\cup\{g\}. $$

Thus $E_{t,g}(S)$ gains $g$, preserves every item of $S$ ranked above $g$, and discards every item of $S$ ranked below $g$. Whenever $E_{t,g}(S)\in\mathcal F$, call the replacement
$$ S\longrightarrow E_{t,g}(S) $$
an elementary improvement. Its highest-ranked changed item is the gained item $g$, so it strictly improves type $t$.

Lemma 1. An assignment admits a Pareto improvement if and only if it admits one using only elementary improvements and unchanged bundles.

Proof. The reverse implication follows immediately from the definition of an elementary improvement.

For the forward implication, represent a Pareto improvement by transfer masses from supported groups $(t,S)$ to feasible destinations $R\succeq_t S$. There are finitely many possible transfer coordinates, because both the type set and $\mathcal F$ are finite.

Consider a positive transfer with $R\ne S$. Lexicographic preferences are a strict total order on distinct bundles, so $R\succ_t S$. Let $g$ be the highest-ranked item in $R\triangle S$, according to type $t$. Because the transfer is an improvement,
$$ g\in R\setminus S. $$
Every item of $S$ ranked above $g$ also belongs to $R$. Consequently,
$$ E_{t,g}(S)\subseteq R. $$
Heredity implies $E_{t,g}(S)\in\mathcal F$.

Replace this transfer’s destination by $E_{t,g}(S)$, keeping its mass unchanged. The replacement remains a strict improvement from $S$. Moreover, it consumes no more of any item than destination $R$.

Perform this replacement for every changed positive transfer and leave unchanged transfers intact. Source-group masses are preserved, aggregate consumption can only decrease relative to the proposed reassignment, and a positive mass remains strictly improved. The resulting reassignment is therefore a feasible Pareto improvement of the required form. $\square$

2. An exact graph characterization of Pareto optimality

For an assignment $x$, define its set of elementary gain items by
$$ A(x)= \left\{ g\in G: \begin{array}{l} \text{there exists }(t,S)\in J(x)\text{ with }g\notin S\\ \text{and }E_{t,g}(S)\in\mathcal F \end{array} \right\}. $$

Define a directed graph $\Gamma(x)$ on vertex set $G$. It has an edge
$$ g\longrightarrow h $$
if some supported group $(t,S)$ admits the elementary improvement gaining $g$ and
$$ h\in D_{t,g}(S). $$

An edge records an elementary improvement that gains its initial vertex and releases its terminal vertex. Each edge can be stored together with one witnessing group and elementary improvement.

Theorem 2. An assignment $x$ is Pareto optimal if and only if both conditions hold:

  • Every elementary gain item has exhausted supply:

$$ u_g(x)=b_g\qquad\text{for every }g\in A(x). $$

  • The graph $\Gamma(x)$ is acyclic.

2.1. Necessity of exhausted supply

Suppose $g\in A(x)$ and $u_g(x)<b_g$. Choose a witnessing supported group $(t,S)$. For
$$ 0<\delta\leq\min\{x_{t,S},\,b_g-u_g(x)\}, $$
move mass $\delta$ from $S$ to $E_{t,g}(S)$.

The move gains only $g$ and releases the items in $D_{t,g}(S)$. It respects all supplies and strictly improves positive mass. Thus $x$ is not Pareto optimal.

2.2. Necessity of acyclicity

Suppose $\Gamma(x)$ has a directed cycle with distinct vertices
$$ g_1\longrightarrow g_2\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow g_\ell\longrightarrow g_1. $$

For each edge $g_i\longrightarrow g_{i+1}$, with indices interpreted cyclically, choose a witnessing elementary improvement from a group $j_i$. This improvement gains $g_i$ and releases $g_{i+1}$, possibly releasing other items as well.

A group may witness several cycle edges. Let $c_j$ be the number of selected moves whose source is group $j$, and choose
$$ \delta=\frac12\min_{j:c_j>0}\frac{x_j}{c_j}. $$
All groups in this minimum have positive mass, so $\delta>0$. Split off mass $\delta$ for each selected move. Their total source mass from group $j$ is $c_j\delta\leq x_j$.

Each cycle item is gained once, by mass $\delta$, and released at least once, by its predecessor’s move. Items outside the cycle are never gained. Thus aggregate consumption of every item weakly decreases.

All selected transfers strictly improve their recipients, so this is a Pareto improvement. Therefore a Pareto-optimal assignment cannot have a directed cycle.

This construction uses precisely the permission to split a current group among multiple destinations.

2.3. Sufficiency

Assume all items in $A(x)$ have exhausted supply and $\Gamma(x)$ is acyclic. Suppose, for contradiction, that a Pareto improvement exists.

By Lemma 1, replace it by a reassignment using elementary improvements and unchanged bundles. Let $B$ be the nonempty set of items gained by elementary improvements carrying positive mass.

Since $\Gamma(x)$ is acyclic, it has a topological ordering $\pi$ such that
$$ g\longrightarrow h\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi(g)<\pi(h). $$
Choose $g\in B$ minimizing $\pi(g)$.

No positive elementary transfer can release $g$. Indeed, such a transfer would gain some $a\in B$ and create an edge $a\longrightarrow g$, giving
$$ \pi(a)<\pi(g), $$
contrary to the choice of $g$.

At least one positive transfer gains $g$, and none releases it. Its aggregate consumption therefore strictly increases. But $g\in A(x)$ and $u_g(x)=b_g$, so the reassignment violates its supply constraint. This contradiction proves sufficiency. $\square$

2.4. Polynomial verification

Suppose a rational candidate assignment has $k$ positive entries.

First verify bundle feasibility, type totals, and supply constraints. Next, for each supported group and each missing item $g$, query whether $E_{t,g}(S)\in\mathcal F$. These require at most $km$ additional oracle calls. The answers determine $A(x)$ and $\Gamma(x)$.

The graph has $m$ vertices and at most $m(m-1)$ distinct edges. Its construction, its acyclicity test, and the exact supply-equality checks all take polynomial time in $m$, $k$, and the rational encoding length.

Thus Pareto optimality is polynomially verifiable even though its definition quantifies over reassignments with real masses and potentially exponentially many destinations.

When Pareto optimality fails, the proof supplies an explicit improvement: either one elementary move using slack supply, or at most $m$ elementary moves witnessing a directed cycle.

3. Supporting item prices

The characterization also gives a succinct certificate excluding all Pareto improvements.

Corollary 3. If $x$ is Pareto optimal, there are nonnegative integer item prices $p_g$, each using $O(m\log(m+1))$ bits, such that:

  • $p_g=0$ whenever $u_g(x)<b_g$;
  • for every supported group $(t,S)$ and every feasible $R\succ_t S$,

$$ \sum_{g\in R}p_g>\sum_{g\in S}p_g. $$

Proof. Let $\pi$ be a topological ordering of $\Gamma(x)$, indexed from $1$ to $m$, and put
$$ p_g= \begin{cases} (m+1)^{m-\pi(g)},&g\in A(x),\\ 0,&g\notin A(x). \end{cases} $$
Theorem 2 implies that every positive-price item has exhausted supply. The asserted bit bound follows directly from this formula.

For any elementary improvement gaining $g$, every released item $h$ has an edge $g\longrightarrow h$. If $p_h>0$, then
$$ p_h\leq\frac{p_g}{m+1}. $$
There are at most $m$ released items, and hence
$$ \sum_{h\in D_{t,g}(S)}p_h \leq\frac{m}{m+1}p_g<p_g. $$
Therefore
$$ \sum_{h\in E_{t,g}(S)}p_h-\sum_{h\in S}p_h = p_g-\sum_{h\in D_{t,g}(S)}p_h>0. $$

For an arbitrary feasible $R\succ_t S$, Lemma 1’s construction gives an elementary destination $E_{t,g}(S)\subseteq R$. Nonnegativity of prices yields
$$ \sum_{h\in R}p_h \geq\sum_{h\in E_{t,g}(S)}p_h >\sum_{h\in S}p_h. $$
This proves the claim. $\square$

For completeness, these properties directly exclude a Pareto improvement. Every unchanged transfer preserves price expenditure, and every strictly improving transfer increases it. A positive strictly improved mass would therefore increase aggregate expenditure. Yet the original assignment already spends
$$ \sum_g p_g u_g(x)=\sum_g p_g b_g, $$
which is the maximum expenditure compatible with the supplies.

4. Sparse rational certificates for existence

I now reprove the sparse witness result using Theorem 2.

Theorem 4. If an EF1 and Pareto-optimal assignment exists with real masses, then one exists with at most $\tau+m$ positive rational entries. Each mass has encoding length
$$ O\bigl(L+(\tau+m)\log(\tau+m+1)\bigr). $$

4.1. A support-restricted polytope

Let $x$ be a real-valued EF1 and Pareto-optimal assignment. Put
$$ J=J(x),\qquad A=A(x). $$

Consider the polytope $K$ of assignments $y$ satisfying:

  • $y$ uses only coordinates in $J$;
  • every type has its prescribed total mass;
  • all supply inequalities hold;
  • every item in $A$ remains exhausted:

$$ u_g(y)=b_g\qquad(g\in A). $$

The polytope is nonempty because $x\in K$, by Theorem 2. It is bounded because each coordinate is nonnegative and at most its type’s mass.

Every $y\in K$ is EF1: its positive support is a subset of $J$, so all its required comparisons were already required and satisfied for $x$.

Every $y\in K$ is also Pareto optimal. Indeed,
$$ A(y)\subseteq A(x),\qquad \Gamma(y)\subseteq\Gamma(x). $$
The second inclusion means edge inclusion on the common vertex set. Thus $\Gamma(y)$ is acyclic, and every item in $A(y)$ has exhausted supply in $y$. Theorem 2 applies.

4.2. A vertex has small support

A nonempty bounded polytope in a finite-dimensional space has a vertex. Choose a vertex $y$ of $K$, and let $k$ be its number of positive coordinates.

Restrict the active type-total and supply equations to these $k$ coordinates. Their coefficient matrix must have column rank $k$. Otherwise, there would be a nonzero perturbation supported on the positive coordinates that preserves all these equations.

For sufficiently small perturbations in either direction:

  • positive coordinates remain nonnegative;
  • inactive supply inequalities remain satisfied, because they have positive slack;
  • coordinates outside the support remain zero.

Both perturbations would lie in $K$, contradicting that $y$ is a vertex.

There are only $\tau+m$ type-total and supply rows. Consequently,
$$ k\leq\tau+m. $$

4.3. Rationality and encoding length

Select $k$ independent active equations determining the positive coordinates. Their coefficient matrix $M$ is a nonsingular $k\times k$ matrix with entries in $\{0,1\}$. Their right-hand sides are input masses or supplies.

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all input masses and supplies. Its binary length is $O(L)$. Multiplying the right-hand sides by $D$ gives integers of binary length $O(L)$.

By Cramer’s rule, each positive coordinate can be written with denominator $D\det M$. The determinant expansion gives
$$ |\det M|\leq k!. $$
If $M_i$ is obtained by replacing one column of $M$ by the integer right-hand side vector $c$, then
$$ |\det M_i|\leq k!\max_j|c_j|. $$
Thus each numerator and denominator has binary length
$$ O\bigl(L+k\log(k+1)\bigr). $$

The selected vertex is EF1 and Pareto optimal, as proved above. Encoding its at most $\tau+m$ bundles and type indices also takes polynomial space. $\square$

4.4. Consequence for complexity

EF1 is polynomially checkable on a sparse support. For a nonempty target bundle $T$, let $h_t(T)$ be its highest-ranked item according to the comparing type $t$. Among all permitted deletions, deleting $h_t(T)$ gives the least-preferred remainder: deleting any other item retains $h_t(T)$, which is the highest-ranked difference between the two remainders.

Therefore the required comparison is equivalent to
$$ S\succeq_t T\setminus\{h_t(T)\}. $$
For an empty target bundle the comparison holds automatically, because every item is desirable.

There are at most $k^2$ support comparisons, each computable from the explicit rankings and bundles. Together with Theorems 2 and 4, this proves:

Corollary 5. The decision problem asking whether an EF1 and Pareto-optimal assignment exists belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ relative to the membership oracle. For any representation of $\mathcal F$ with polynomial-time membership testing, it belongs to ordinary $\mathrm{NP}$.

This is a certificate result. It does not provide a polynomial algorithm for discovering a suitable support.

5. A polynomial algorithm with one partially supplied item

Call an item partially supplied if
$$ 0<b_g<1. $$

The threshold $1$ is the population mass. Since a bundle contains at most one copy of each item,
$$ u_g(x)\leq\sum_t\mu_t=1 $$
for every assignment. Thus supplies at least $1$ never restrict feasibility.

Theorem 6. Suppose at most one item is partially supplied. Then an EF1 and Pareto-optimal assignment exists and can be computed in polynomial time, using at most $2\tau m$ membership-oracle calls. It has at most $\tau+1$ positive entries.

5.1. Greedy computation of a lexicographically best feasible bundle

We first record the greedy fact used by the algorithm.

Fix a type, an available item set $H$, and a mandatory feasible set $Q\subseteq H$. Start with $Q$, inspect the items of $H\setminus Q$ in the type’s ranking order, and add an item whenever the resulting set belongs to $\mathcal F$.

The output is the lexicographically best feasible subset of $H$ containing $Q$.

To prove this, suppose a feasible candidate containing $Q$ were better than the greedy output. Let $h$ be their highest-ranked differing item. The candidate contains $h$, while the greedy output does not. At the step when $h$ was rejected, the current set consisted of $Q$ and the previously accepted items. The candidate contains all those items, because it agrees with the output above $h$ and contains $Q$. The candidate also contains $h$.

Heredity would therefore make the rejected extension feasible, a contradiction.

This algorithm uses at most $m$ membership queries, including a feasibility check of $Q$ if needed.

5.2. No partially supplied item

Let
$$ H=\{h:b_h\geq1\}. $$
Every positively allocated bundle must avoid zero-supply items. Give each type its lexicographically best feasible subset of $H$, computed greedily.

This assignment is feasible because each item’s usage is at most $1$. It is envy-free: every target bundle is a feasible subset of $H$, and each type receives its best such bundle. It is Pareto optimal because no positive group has any strictly preferred feasible destination using available items.

5.3. One partially supplied item

Let $g$ be the unique partially supplied item and write
$$ q=b_g,\qquad 0<q<1. $$
Put
$$ H=\{h:b_h\geq1\}. $$

For each type $t$, compute:

  • $U_t$: its best feasible bundle contained in $H$;
  • $V_t$: its best feasible bundle contained in $H\cup\{g\}$ and containing $g$, if such a bundle exists.

Compute $U_t$ with mandatory set $\emptyset$, and $V_t$ with mandatory set $\{g\}$. If $\{g\}\notin\mathcal F$, heredity implies that no feasible bundle contains $g$.

Call type $t$ favorable if $V_t$ exists and
$$ V_t\succ_t U_t. $$
Let its total favorable mass be
$$ d=\sum_{t:\,V_t\succ_t U_t}\mu_t. $$

Choose any fixed order of favorable types. Give $V_t$ to their mass in that order, up to total mass $\min\{q,d\}$. Give $U_t$ to everyone else.

At most one type is split between the two bundles. Thus the output has at most $\tau+1$ positive entries. It is feasible: the usage of $g$ is $\min\{q,d\}$, every other positively used item has supply at least $1$, and zero-supply items are absent.

5.4. EF1

Every recipient of $V_t$ is favorable. Its bundle is its global best feasible bundle within $H\cup\{g\}$, because $U_t$ and $V_t$ are respectively the best bundles excluding and including $g$. Such a recipient does not envy any allocated bundle.

A nonfavorable type receiving $U_t$ likewise receives its global best bundle within $H\cup\{g\}$.

It remains to consider an unserved favorable group receiving $U_t$. Let $T$ be any positively allocated target bundle.

If $g\notin T$, then $T$ is a feasible subset of $H$, and hence
$$ U_t\succeq_t T. $$
If $g\in T$, heredity gives
$$ T\setminus\{g\}\in\mathcal F, $$
and this remainder is contained in $H$. Therefore
$$ U_t\succeq_t T\setminus\{g\}. $$
Deleting at most $g$ establishes EF1 in every case.

5.5. Pareto optimality

If $d\leq q$, every type receives its global best feasible bundle using the positive-supply items. No Pareto improvement exists.

Suppose $d>q$. Then the recipients of $V_t$ consume exactly mass $q$ of item $g$.

Each such recipient holds its unique global best feasible bundle within $H\cup\{g\}$. In any weakly improving reassignment, all of that recipient’s mass must therefore retain exactly $V_t$. This fixes consumption of all supply $q$ of item $g$.

Consequently, no other positive source group can receive $g$ in a supply-feasible reassignment. All remaining groups hold $U_t$, their best feasible bundles excluding $g$. Their only weakly preferred destinations excluding $g$ are those same bundles, by strictness of the bundle order.

Zero-supply items cannot appear in any positive transfer. Hence every positive source group must retain its bundle, even when splitting among destinations is permitted. A strict improvement of positive mass is impossible.

5.6. Complexity and rational output

Computing both candidate bundles for every type uses at most $2\tau m$ oracle calls. Preference comparisons and allocation in the fixed type order take polynomial time.

Only one cutoff can split a type. The resulting masses are obtained from input masses and $q$ by additions, subtractions, comparisons, and minima. Using the common denominator $D$ from Theorem 4, all output masses are integer multiples of $1/D$ and have polynomial encoding length. This completes the algorithmic proof. $\square$
