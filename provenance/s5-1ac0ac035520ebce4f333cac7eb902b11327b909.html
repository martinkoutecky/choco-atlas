The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses for finitely many applicant and position types, their preferences over counterpart types, acceptable type pairs, a feasible baseline mass matching \(M\), and \(\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 1}\cup\{\infty\}\), decide whether there is a feasible mass matching \(N\) whose mass of agents strictly preferring their assignment in \(N\) is at least \(\alpha\) times the mass strictly preferring their assignment in \(M\), and return such an \(N\) when it exists. Use the paper’s convention that the unchanged comparison counts as \(1\)-more-popular, and require positive improvement when \(\alpha>1\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Applicant types \(X\) and position types \(Y\) form an atomless society given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|X|+|Y|\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(s_v\), normalized by \(\sum_v s_v=1\). Support size counts listed types, not possible types. Let \(m=|E|\) for the supplied acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq X\times Y\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a weak preference order, allowing ties, over its acceptable counterpart types and the unmatched outcome \(\bot\); every acceptable counterpart is strictly preferred to \(\bot\). Both sides vote, and indifferent agents abstain.
A feasible mass matching \(Q\) assigns nonnegative mass \(Q_{xy}\) to each acceptable pair, with \(\sum_y Q_{xy}\le s_x\) and \(\sum_x Q_{xy}\le s_y\). Write \(Q_v(a)\) for type \(v\)'s mass assigned to outcome \(a\), including the unmatched residual.
The input includes a feasible binary-rational baseline \(M\) and \(\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge1}\cup\{\infty\}\). To preserve individual comparisons, a proposed matching \(N\) includes nonnegative transition masses \(c^v_{ab}\): mass of type \(v\) with old outcome \(a\) and new outcome \(b\), satisfying \(\sum_b c^v_{ab}=M_v(a)\) and \(\sum_a c^v_{ab}=N_v(b)\). These transitions are existentially chosen as part of the reassignment.
Define \(P=\sum_v\sum_{b\succ_v a}c^v_{ab}\) and \(D=\sum_v\sum_{a\succ_v b}c^v_{ab}\). For finite \(\alpha\), require \(P\ge\alpha D\), additionally \(P>0\) when \(\alpha>1\); for \(\alpha=\infty\), require \(P>0\) and \(D=0\). The unchanged comparison is allowed at \(\alpha=1\).

Is this mass reassignment decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can a qualifying matching and its transition masses also be returned in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For finite agents, deciding and constructing an \(\alpha\)-more-popular matching relative to a given matching is polynomial-time solvable, even with preference ties ([source](https://www.dcs.warwick.ac.uk/~u1671158/papers/15-ICALP-C.pdf)).
Existing fractional-popularity results compare fractional challengers with integral baselines ([source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-024-01287-4)); they do not settle the arbitrary mass-baseline problem with the transition comparison defined here. No result settling this exact problem was identified in the search.

  • Defined the infinite threshold as positive improving mass with zero worsening mass.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Whenever a qualifying reassignment exists, a qualifying matching and all its transition masses can be returned as rational numbers in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

At \(\alpha=1\), the unchanged matching is always a qualifying witness. The nontrivial cases, including \(\alpha=\infty\), are solved by a polynomial-size rational linear program.

The proof uses exactly the stated model: transition masses are existentially chosen, and matching and transition masses need not be integral.

1. A linear description of feasible reassignments

For each listed type \(v\), let \(\Gamma(v)\) be its acceptable counterpart types, let \(d_v=|\Gamma(v)|\), and define its outcome set by

\(A_v=\Gamma(v)\cup\{\bot\}.\)

The baseline outcome masses are known rational numbers. For an acceptable pair \((x,y)\), they satisfy \(M_x(y)=M_y(x)=M_{xy}\); the unmatched mass is

\(M_v(\bot)=s_v-\sum_{e\ni v}M_e.\)

These unmatched masses are nonnegative because the supplied baseline is feasible.

Introduce the following nonnegative variables:

  • \(n_{xy}\) for every acceptable pair \((x,y)\in E\);
  • \(r_v\) for every listed type \(v\);
  • \(c^v_{ab}\) for every listed type \(v\) and every \(a,b\in A_v\).

Here \(n_{xy}\) represents the proposed matching mass, and \(r_v\) represents its unmatched residual. Define the linear expressions

\(z_x(y)=z_y(x)=n_{xy},\qquad z_v(\bot)=r_v.\)

Impose the mass-balance equations

\(r_v+\sum_{e\ni v}n_e=s_v\qquad\text{for every }v,\)

and the transition-marginal equations

\(\sum_{b\in A_v}c^v_{ab}=M_v(a)\qquad\text{for every }v,\ a\in A_v,\)

\(\sum_{a\in A_v}c^v_{ab}=z_v(b)\qquad\text{for every }v,\ b\in A_v.\)

Let \(\mathcal F\) denote the set satisfying these equations and nonnegativity.

Claim. Points of \(\mathcal F\) correspond exactly to feasible matchings together with transition masses satisfying the question’s marginal requirements.

Proof. Given a point of \(\mathcal F\), set \(N_{xy}=n_{xy}\). Since \(r_v\) is nonnegative, the mass-balance equation gives \(\sum_{e\ni v}N_e\le s_v\). It also identifies \(r_v\) with the unmatched residual. Consequently, \(z_v(b)=N_v(b)\) for every outcome \(b\). The two transition equations are precisely the required old and new marginals.

Conversely, given a feasible matching \(N\) and permitted transition masses, set \(n_{xy}=N_{xy}\) and \(r_v=N_v(\bot)\). Feasibility and the prescribed marginals give every displayed equation. This proves both directions. \(\square\)

Thus this formulation retains the individual comparison information encoded by the transitions.

2. Voting and strict positivity

Define the linear functions

\(P(c)=\sum_v\sum_{\substack{a,b\in A_v\\ b\succ_v a}}c^v_{ab},\qquad D(c)=\sum_v\sum_{\substack{a,b\in A_v\\ a\succ_v b}}c^v_{ab}.\)

Tied outcomes contribute to neither function, so these definitions include abstention exactly as specified.

The set \(\mathcal F\) is nonempty: take \(N=M\), its unmatched residuals, and the identity transitions

\(c^v_{aa}=M_v(a),\qquad c^v_{ab}=0\quad\text{when }a\ne b.\)

For these transitions, \(P(c)=D(c)=0\).

Furthermore, \(\mathcal F\) is compact. It is closed because it is defined by finitely many linear equalities and nonnegativity constraints. Every matching and residual variable is bounded by its incident type mass. Every transition variable satisfies

\(0\le c^v_{ab}\le M_v(a)\le s_v\le1.\)

Hence all variables are bounded. Also, summing all transition rows gives

\(\sum_v\sum_{a,b\in A_v}c^v_{ab}=\sum_v s_v=1,\)

so \(0\le P(c)\le1\).

Finite thresholds greater than one

For rational \(1<\alpha<\infty\), solve the linear program

\(p_\alpha^*=\max\{P(c):(n,r,c)\in\mathcal F,\ P(c)-\alpha D(c)\ge0\}.\)

Its feasible set is nonempty because it contains the identity reassignment. It is compact, so the maximum is attained.

A qualifying reassignment exists if and only if \(p_\alpha^*>0\):

  • A qualifying reassignment is feasible for this program and has positive objective value; therefore its existence implies \(p_\alpha^*>0\).
  • If \(p_\alpha^*>0\), an optimizer satisfies both \(P(c)>0\) and \(P(c)\ge\alpha D(c)\), so it is a qualifying reassignment.

This handles the strict condition without introducing an arbitrary positive tolerance. In particular, a reassignment with \(P(c)=\alpha D(c)>0\) is correctly accepted.

The infinite threshold

For \(\alpha=\infty\), solve

\(p_\infty^*=\max\{P(c):(n,r,c)\in\mathcal F,\ D(c)=0\}.\)

Again, the feasible set is nonempty and compact. The same two implications show that a qualifying reassignment exists exactly when \(p_\infty^*>0\). Any optimizer with positive objective is the required witness.

Threshold one

For \(\alpha=1\), return \(N=M\) and the identity transitions. They satisfy \(P=D=0\), which is expressly allowed.

3. Polynomial encoding size

Only listed types and supplied acceptable pairs are used.

The number of transition variables is

\(K=\sum_v(d_v+1)^2=\sum_v d_v^2+4m+\tau,\)

because \(\sum_v d_v=2m\). Nonnegativity of the degrees gives

\(\sum_v d_v^2\le\left(\sum_v d_v\right)^2=4m^2.\)

Therefore

\(K\le4m^2+4m+\tau.\)

Including matching and unmatched variables, the total number of variables is \(m+\tau+K\), which is polynomial in \(m,\tau\).

There are \(\tau\) mass-balance equations and \(2(2m+\tau)\) transition-marginal equations, followed by one voting constraint. Thus the number of constraints, including nonnegativity, is polynomial as well.

All numerical data have polynomial bit length:

  • Preference comparisons determine objective coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\).
  • The mass and marginal equations use coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).
  • Baseline unmatched masses are computed by exact rational subtraction. A common denominator can be formed from the denominators of the relevant input numbers; its bit length is bounded by their total input bit length.
  • If \(\alpha=u/w\), with positive integers \(u,w\), its voting constraint is equivalently \(wP(c)-uD(c)\ge0\). The integers \(u,w\) are part of the input.

Consequently, the entire rational linear program can be constructed in time and space polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

4. Exact optimization and witness construction

We apply the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: a finitely encoded rational linear program can be solved in polynomial bit complexity, with an exact rational optimizer returned when the optimum is finite and attained. Equalities and lower-dimensional feasible sets are permitted. Rational coefficients can first be converted to integer coefficients by clearing denominators. See [MIT’s lecture notes on polynomial-time linear programming](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-251j-introduction-to-mathematical-programming-fall-2009/e13f5e6650f7be1e21101a2e7bd0d5b8_MIT6_251JF09_lec18.pdf).

The required hypotheses hold here: the encoding is polynomial, the feasible set is nonempty, and the objective attains a finite maximum.

For completeness, polynomial witness size also follows directly from rational polytope geometry. Let \(n\) be the number of variables. After clearing denominators, let all constraint coefficients and right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(U\), where \(\log U\) is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). An optimal vertex exists because the feasible set is a nonempty bounded polyhedron. At that vertex, choose \(n\) linearly independent active constraints. Cramer’s rule expresses each coordinate as a ratio of integer determinants. Hadamard’s inequality bounds the absolute values of these determinants by

\(n^{n/2}U^n.\)

Their bit lengths are therefore polynomial, specifically \(O(n(\log n+\log U))\).

The algorithm computes the exact optimum and tests whether it is positive. On a positive answer, it returns \(N_{xy}=n_{xy}\) and the optimizer’s transition masses \(c^v_{ab}\). They have polynomial total encoding length and satisfy every required condition by Sections 1 and 2.

Thus both decision and witness construction are polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary feasible mass baselines under the stated transition comparison.
