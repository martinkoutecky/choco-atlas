The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), item supplies \(q_j\), and types \(t=(S_t,v_t,b_t)\) with \(b_t \ge v_t\), compute in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) item prices \(\rho_j\) and masses \(z_{t,X}\) assigned to integral bundles \(X\subseteq S_t\). Require \(\sum_X z_{t,X}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,X:j\in X}z_{t,X}\le q_j\), and every bundle receiving positive mass to maximize that type’s affordable utility at \(\rho\). The revenue must be at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}/4\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}\) is the maximum revenue of a feasible, individually rational, bundle-price envy-free allocation on the same instance.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\ge1\) types \(t=(S_t,v_t,b_t)\), with \(S_t\subseteq J\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(b_t\ge v_t>0\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Supplies \(q_j\ge0\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each individual receives an integral bundle, containing at most one of each label. Utility is \(u_t(X,p)=v_t|X\cap S_t|-p\) when \(0\le p\le b_t\), and \(-\infty\) otherwise; the outside option is \((\varnothing,0)\).
An item-price outcome consists of nonnegative rational prices \(\rho_j\) and nonnegative rational masses \(z_{t,X}\), for \(X\subseteq S_t\), satisfying \(\sum_Xz_{t,X}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,X:j\in X}z_{t,X}\le q_j\). Every positively assigned bundle must maximize \(u_t(Y,\sum_{j\in Y}\rho_j)\) over all \(Y\subseteq J\); any utility tie is allowed. Revenue is \(R=\sum_{t,X}z_{t,X}\sum_{j\in X}\rho_j\). For the bundle-price benchmark, use nonnegative masses \(a_{t,X}\), now allowing \(X\subseteq J\), with the same mass and supply constraints, and a common nonnegative price \(P(X)\) for each assigned bundle. For finite buyers and one copy of each item, a polynomial-time item-price algorithm achieves revenue at least one quarter of the optimal individually rational, bundle-price envy-free revenue when every budget is at least its per-item value. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.09221)

Status and new partial results

The general quarter-approximation question remains unresolved here. I prove the following additional results:

  • For fixed $m$, the supremum of item-price revenue can be computed in polynomial time, and an item-price outcome arbitrarily close to that supremum can be produced in polynomial time.
  • The corresponding statement holds for the bundle-price benchmark when $m$ is fixed.
  • Both revenue suprema are rational numbers of polynomial encoding length. Near-optimal outcomes also have polynomial encoding length and polynomial support.
  • When every type is interested in at most one label, a polynomial-time item-price algorithm achieves $\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}$ exactly, even with heterogeneous values.

These results address price selection and representation, but the running time of the general price enumeration depends exponentially on $m$. They do not establish the requested uniform polynomial-time guarantee.

Throughout, let $\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}$ denote the supremum of revenue over item-price outcomes, and put

$$ V=\max_t v_t, \qquad B=\max_t b_t. $$

Both suprema are finite: individual rationality bounds every type’s average payment by $b_t$, so every outcome has revenue at most $\sum_t\mu_t b_t$.

1. Demand and allocation at fixed item prices

The following facts are needed for the price-selection argument. I give their proofs so that the argument is self-contained.

1.1 Exact demand description

Fix nonnegative rational item prices $\rho$.

Removing labels outside $S_t$ from an affordable bundle preserves its value and weakly reduces its payment. Consequently, maximizing utility over subsets of $S_t$ gives the same maximum as maximizing over all subsets of $J$.

Define

$$ W_t=\{j\in S_t:\rho_j<v_t\}, \qquad Z_t=\{j\in S_t:\rho_j=v_t\}. $$

Suppose first that $\sum_{j\in W_t}\rho_j\le b_t$. Every affordable interested bundle has utility at most

$$ \sum_{j\in W_t}(v_t-\rho_j), $$

and $W_t$ attains this bound. Equality requires including every label in $W_t$ and excluding every label priced above $v_t$. Thus the demanded interested bundles are exactly

$$ W_t\cup H, \qquad H\subseteq Z_t, \qquad |H|\le r_t, $$

where

$$ r_t= \min\left\{ |Z_t|, \left\lfloor \frac{b_t-\sum_{j\in W_t}\rho_j}{v_t} \right\rfloor \right\}. $$

Now suppose that $\sum_{j\in W_t}\rho_j>b_t$. Sort the prices in $S_t$ as

$$ \pi_1\le\cdots\le\pi_n, \qquad C_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\pi_i, \qquad C_0=0. $$

Let $d_t$ be the largest $k$ with $C_k\le b_t$. Because $C_{|W_t|}>b_t$, we have $d_t<|W_t|$. For every $1\le k\le d_t$,

$$ (v_tk-C_k)-(v_t(k-1)-C_{k-1}) = v_t-\pi_k>0. $$

A cheapest $k$-element subset maximizes utility among bundles of cardinality $k$. No cardinality greater than $d_t$ is affordable. Therefore every maximizing interested bundle is a cheapest $d_t$-element subset.

The assumption $b_t\ge v_t$ ensures $d_t\ge1$: a label in $W_t$ has price less than $v_t$ and is affordable. Define

$$ c_t=\pi_{d_t}, \qquad F_t=\{j\in S_t:\rho_j<c_t\}, \qquad E_t=\{j\in S_t:\rho_j=c_t\}, \qquad h_t=d_t-|F_t|. $$

The demanded bundles are exactly $F_t\cup H$ with $H\subseteq E_t$ and $|H|=h_t$.

In both cases, demand therefore has the form

$$ \mathcal D_t = \{F_t\cup H:H\subseteq E_t,\ \ell_t\le |H|\le u_t\}. $$

In the first case, $F_t=W_t$, $E_t=Z_t$, $\ell_t=0$, and $u_t=r_t$. In the second case, $\ell_t=u_t=h_t$.

1.2 A compact allocation linear program

Every member of type $t$ must receive all labels in $F_t$. Define the residual supplies

$$ Q_j=q_j-\sum_{t:j\in F_t}\mu_t. $$

A negative $Q_j$ makes these prices infeasible. Otherwise introduce $x_{tj}$ for $j\in E_t$, representing the mass of type $t$ receiving optional label $j$. The necessary constraints are

$$ 0\le x_{tj}\le\mu_t, $$

$$ \ell_t\mu_t \le \sum_{j\in E_t}x_{tj} \le u_t\mu_t, $$

$$ \sum_{t:j\in E_t}x_{tj}\le Q_j. $$

They are also sufficient.

To prove sufficiency, fix a type and write $y_j=x_{tj}/\mu_t$. Append $u_t-\ell_t$ dummy coordinates in $[0,1]$ so that all coordinates sum to the integer $u_t$. This is possible because the required dummy sum is

$$ u_t-\sum_j y_j\in[0,u_t-\ell_t]. $$

Place consecutive half-open intervals of the coordinate lengths along $[0,u_t)$. For $U$ uniformly distributed on $[0,1)$, select a coordinate if its interval contains a point $U+k$ for an integer $k$.

Apart from finitely many endpoint values of $U$, exactly $u_t$ coordinates are selected. An interval of length at most one contains at most one such point, and its selection probability equals its length. Discarding the dummy coordinates leaves between $\ell_t$ and $u_t$ original labels, with original inclusion probabilities $y_j$.

Partitioning $[0,1)$ at the fractional parts of the interval endpoints produces an explicit rational distribution with at most $2m+1$ pieces. The case $u_t=0$ simply gives the deterministic empty optional set. Adding $F_t$ and multiplying probabilities by $\mu_t$ gives the required allocation.

Revenue at these prices is

$$ \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j\in F_t}\rho_j + \sum_t\sum_{j\in E_t}\rho_jx_{tj}. $$

Thus feasibility and maximum revenue at fixed prices are computable by a rational linear program with at most $\tau m$ variables.

The resulting bundle list can be reduced to at most $\tau+m$ positive entries. Indeed, over that list, preserve each type’s total mass and each label’s total allocated mass. An extreme point of this nonempty bounded polytope has at most $\tau+m$ positive coordinates: otherwise its positive columns are linearly dependent, yielding a nonzero perturbation in both directions that preserves all these equalities. Preserving label loads also preserves revenue.

2. Two elementary facts about price regions

I will use regions specified by rational linear equalities and weak or strict inequalities.

2.1 Optimizing over an open region

Let $C$ be a nonempty bounded set specified by such constraints, and let $\overline C_{\mathrm{weak}}$ be obtained by replacing its strict inequalities by weak ones.

Then $\overline C_{\mathrm{weak}}$ is the closure of $C$. To see this, choose $p^0\in C$. For any $p^*\in\overline C_{\mathrm{weak}}$ and $0<\lambda<1$, the point

$$ p^\lambda=(1-\lambda)p^*+\lambda p^0 $$

satisfies all equalities and weak inequalities, and satisfies every originally strict inequality strictly. Hence $p^\lambda\in C$ and $p^\lambda\to p^*$.

Consequently, the supremum of an affine function on $C$ is its maximum on $\overline C_{\mathrm{weak}}$, computable by rational linear programming.

Strict feasibility can also be checked by linear programming. Write each strict constraint as $f_i(p)>0$, replace it by $f_i(p)\ge\eta$, impose $0\le\eta\le1$, and maximize $\eta$. The original region is nonempty exactly when the optimum is positive.

2.2 Enumerating regions in fixed dimension

For $H$ hyperplanes in dimension $d$, the number of nonempty sign regions, including lower-dimensional regions, is at most

$$ \sum_{i=0}^d 2^i\binom Hi. $$

Here a sign region specifies, for every defining affine function, whether it is negative, zero, or positive.

For completeness, let $F(H,d)$ be the maximum number of regions. On inserting the last hyperplane, an old region that is cut transversely becomes two regions plus its intersection with that hyperplane, increasing the count by two. The distinct intersections are regions of the induced arrangement in dimension $d-1$. Thus

$$ F(H,d)\le F(H-1,d)+2F(H-1,d-1). $$

The displayed bound follows from this recurrence and the initial conditions $F(0,d)=F(H,0)=1$.

An algorithm can insert hyperplanes successively, extend each retained sign pattern by the three possible signs, and test feasibility using the preceding linear program. In fixed dimension, this takes polynomial time in $H$ and the coefficient encoding length.

3. Item-price optimization for fixed $m$

Theorem

For each fixed $m$, there is an algorithm polynomial in $\tau$, $L$, and the encoding length of a rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ that:

  • computes $\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}$ exactly as a rational number; and
  • outputs an item-price outcome with

$$ R\ge(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}. $$

The theorem concerns a supremum; it does not assert that an optimal outcome always exists.

3.1 Prices can be bounded

It suffices to consider prices in $[0,V]^m$.

Indeed, a label priced above $V$ cannot occur in a positively assigned interested bundle: removing it strictly increases utility. Replace every price above $V$ by $V$. Previously assigned bundles keep their payments.

For any type, removing the newly reduced labels from any newly affordable alternative weakly increases its utility, since those labels still have price at least that type’s value. The remaining bundle uses unchanged prices and was an available alternative before the reduction. Therefore no newly affordable alternative has utility greater than the utility of the previously assigned bundle.

This replacement preserves the outcome and its revenue.

3.2 A finite arrangement fixes demand

Inside $[0,V]^m$, use the following hyperplanes:

$$ \rho_i=\rho_j, \qquad \rho_j=v_t, \qquad \sum_{j\in A}\rho_j=b_t \quad (A\subseteq S_t), $$

together with the box-boundary hyperplanes.

There are at most

$$ O(m^2+\tau m+\tau 2^m) $$

hyperplanes.

Within a sign region:

  • all price comparisons and ties are fixed;
  • the comparison of each interested price with $v_t$ is fixed;
  • affordability of every interested subset is fixed.

The demand description from Section 1 is therefore constant throughout the region. In particular, the sets $F_t,E_t$ and integers $\ell_t,u_t$ are fixed.

3.3 Maximum feasible revenue is affine within a region

Partition the types into $\mathcal A$, those in the first demand case, and $\mathcal B$, those in the second.

For $t\in\mathcal A$, every optional label has price exactly $v_t$ throughout the region.

For $t\in\mathcal B$, choose any $e_t\in E_t$. Every optional label has price $\rho_{e_t}$, and the allocation constraints require total optional mass $h_t\mu_t$. Its optional payment is therefore always $h_t\mu_t\rho_{e_t}$, regardless of how those optional labels are distributed.

The compact allocation polytope from Section 1 depends only on the region. Define

$$ C= \max_x \sum_{t\in\mathcal A}\sum_{j\in E_t}v_tx_{tj} $$

over that polytope. If it is infeasible, discard the region. Otherwise, maximum feasible revenue at any price in the region is exactly

$$ F(\rho)= \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j\in F_t}\rho_j + \sum_{t\in\mathcal B}\mu_th_t\rho_{e_t} + C. $$

This is affine in $\rho$. Moreover, one allocation solution attaining $C$ is optimal throughout the region.

3.4 The algorithm and its guarantee

Enumerate all nonempty price regions. For each region with feasible allocation constraints:

  • Compute $C$.
  • Maximize $F$ over the region’s weak closure.
  • Retain the largest resulting value.

Every admissible price belongs to one region, so the largest value is exactly $\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}$.

For a region attaining that supremum, let $\rho^*$ maximize $F$ over its weak closure and let $\rho^0$ be a rational point in the region. Set

$$ \rho^\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon)\rho^*+\varepsilon\rho^0. $$

This price vector lies in the original region. Since $F(\rho^0)$ is revenue from a feasible outcome, it is nonnegative. Thus

$$ F(\rho^\varepsilon) = (1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_{IP} + \varepsilon F(\rho^0) \ge (1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}. $$

Use the region’s optimal allocation solution and the decomposition from Section 1 to produce the outcome.

For fixed $m$, the number of hyperplanes and regions is polynomial in $\tau$. Every linear program has polynomial size and rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. This proves the theorem.

4. Bundle-price optimization for fixed $m$

A related affine structure holds for the benchmark despite the apparent products of allocation masses and bundle prices.

Theorem

For each fixed $m$, $\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}$ can be computed exactly as a rational number in polynomial time. For rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, a bundle-price outcome with revenue at least

$$ (1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}_{BP} $$

can also be produced in polynomial time.

4.1 Enumerating menus and demand regions

An assigned empty bundle must have price zero by individual rationality. Treat it as the free outside option.

There are $N=2^m-1$ nonempty bundles. Enumerate every subset $M$ of them as a possible menu, with price variables $P_X$ for $X\in M$. It suffices to impose $0\le P_X\le B$, because every genuinely assigned bundle must be affordable to some type.

For this intermediate optimization, allow some menu entries to remain unassigned. This does not change the supremum:

  • every outcome in the question is included;
  • from an outcome with unassigned menu entries, delete those entries. Previously assigned bundles remain utility-maximizing against the smaller menu.

For a fixed menu, use hyperplanes for affordability,

$$ P_X=b_t, $$

and utility comparisons, including comparison with the outside option,

$$ P_X-P_Y = v_t\bigl(|X\cap S_t|-|Y\cap S_t|\bigr). $$

For the outside option, take $Y=\varnothing$ and $P_Y=0$. Include the price-box boundaries.

Within each sign region, the set $\mathcal D_t$ of affordable utility-maximizing menu bundles, including the outside option, is fixed.

4.2 Linearizing revenue within a region

Choose a reference bundle $X_t^0\in\mathcal D_t$ for each type. For every $X\in\mathcal D_t$, utility equality gives

$$ P_X-P_{X_t^0} = v_t\bigl(|X\cap S_t|-|X_t^0\cap S_t|\bigr). $$

Consequently, for an allocation supported on these demand sets,

$$ \sum_{t,X}a_{t,X}P_X = \sum_t\mu_tP_{X_t^0} + \sum_{t,X}a_{t,X}v_t \bigl(|X\cap S_t|-|X_t^0\cap S_t|\bigr). $$

The second sum is a linear objective in the allocation masses with coefficients independent of prices. Its maximum $C$ is obtained from the mass and supply linear program over the fixed demand sets.

Maximum revenue in the region is thus the affine function

$$ F(P)=\sum_t\mu_tP_{X_t^0}+C. $$

If a reference bundle is the outside option, its reference price is zero.

Now maximize this affine function over the weak closure of every feasible region and take the largest value over all menus. The same convex-mixture argument as in Section 3 produces an outcome within a factor $1-\varepsilon$ of that value.

For fixed $m$, both the menu size and price-space dimension are constants. There are polynomially many hyperplanes and regions in $\tau$, and every allocation linear program has polynomial size. This proves the theorem.

5. Polynomial support and encoding length

The preceding algorithms have expensive enumeration when $m$ varies. Nevertheless, their underlying optimization has polynomial-size rational representations.

5.1 Bundle-price outcomes can be made sparse

Every bundle-price outcome is dominated in revenue by one with at most $\tau+m$ positive type-bundle entries.

To prove this, fix its offered menu and prices. For each type, retain only its utility-maximizing affordable menu bundles and the outside option when maximizing. Optimize revenue over allocation masses on these demand sets.

The original allocation is feasible. The allocation polytope is bounded because each type’s total mass is fixed. Hence an optimal extreme point exists.

If that extreme point had more than $\tau+m$ positive entries, the corresponding columns of the $\tau$ type-mass rows and $m$ label-load rows would be linearly dependent. A sufficiently small nonzero perturbation in both directions would preserve nonnegativity, all type masses, and all label loads. This contradicts extremality.

Finally, delete every unassigned menu entry. This preserves validity and revenue.

5.2 A linear description for a fixed sparse bundle-price support

Fix at most $\tau+m$ proposed positive pairs $(t,X)$. Let $a_{t,X}>0$ be their masses, $P_X$ the common prices of their distinct nonempty bundles, and $U_t$ the utility received by type $t$.

For an assigned pair, impose

$$ U_t=v_t|X\cap S_t|-P_X, \qquad P_X\le b_t. $$

Also impose $U_t\ge0$, the type-mass equalities, and the supply inequalities.

For each type $t$ and offered bundle $Y$, choose one of two affordability branches:

$$ P_Y\le b_t, \qquad U_t\ge v_t|Y\cap S_t|-P_Y, $$

or

$$ P_Y>b_t. $$

These conditions are exactly the required individual rationality and envy-freeness conditions. Every offered bundle is actually offered because some proposed pair assigns it positive mass.

Revenue becomes a linear function:

$$ R= \sum_{t,X}a_{t,X}v_t|X\cap S_t| - \sum_t\mu_tU_t. $$

Indeed, substitute the assigned-pair utility equalities and use $\sum_Xa_{t,X}=\mu_t$.

All variables can be bounded:

$$ 0\le P_X\le B, \qquad 0\le U_t\le mV, \qquad 0\le a_{t,X}\le\mu_t. $$

Thus, for a fixed support and affordability pattern, optimization is over a bounded rational linear system with some strict inequalities. Its number of variables and coefficient encoding lengths are polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

5.3 Consequences

Standard rational linear programming gives polynomial encoding bounds for vertices: a vertex is obtained by solving a nonsingular subsystem with no more equations than variables, and determinant bounds give polynomial bit length when the dimension and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial.

Applying this fact to the preceding systems yields:

  • $\mathrm{OPT}_{IP}$ and $\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}$ are rational numbers with encoding lengths polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.
  • For rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, each supremum has a corresponding $(1-\varepsilon)$-approximate outcome whose prices, masses, and support have total encoding length polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ and the encoding length of $\varepsilon$.

For item pricing, there are finitely many demand regions. The allocation and price linear programs from Section 3 have polynomial dimension and polynomial coefficient encoding length, even though there may be exponentially many regions.

For bundle pricing, there are finitely many sparse supports and affordability patterns. Sparsification loses no revenue, and each feasible pattern has the bounded linear description above. Its supremum is the optimum over its weak closure. Taking the maximum over this finite collection preserves rationality and the uniform encoding bound.

Near-optimal outcomes follow by mixing a closure optimizer with a strictly feasible rational point. Such a point of polynomial encoding length is obtained by the positive-margin linear program from Section 2. The allocation decompositions and support reductions preserve polynomial encoding length.

There is also a certificate consequence. For a rational threshold $T$, each of the following decision problems belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$:

  • whether an item-price outcome with $R\ge T$ exists;
  • whether a bundle-price outcome with $R\ge T$ exists.

To justify the encoding bound for an exact threshold, add $R\ge T$ to the appropriate linear system before maximizing the strict-inequality margin. If a feasible outcome exists, this system has a positive-margin solution, and a rational vertex supplies a polynomial-size witness. The item-price witness can be checked using the exact demand calculation from Section 1; the bundle-price witness can be checked directly against its polynomial-size offered menu.

This statement concerns existence of an outcome attaining the threshold. It does not replace that condition by a comparison with a possibly unattained supremum.

6. Exact solution when each type wants at most one label

Theorem

Suppose $|S_t|\le1$ for every type. A polynomial-time item-price algorithm achieves $\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}$ exactly.

The values and budgets may be heterogeneous.

6.1 Bundle pricing cannot improve on separate singleton prices

Consider any bundle-price outcome. Types with empty interest sets pay zero by individual rationality.

For each label $j$ occurring in an offered bundle, let

$$ p_j=\min\{P(X):X\text{ is offered and }j\in X\}. $$

Consider a type with $S_t=\{j\}$ that receives a bundle containing $j$ at price $P$. Individual rationality gives $P\le v_t$ and $P\le b_t$. Because $p_j\le P$, a bundle attaining $p_j$ is affordable. Envy-freeness gives

$$ v_t-P\ge v_t-p_j, $$

so $P=p_j$.

Moreover, if $v_t>p_j$, the cheapest offered bundle containing $j$ is affordable because

$$ p_j<v_t\le b_t, $$

and gives strictly positive utility. Every member of this type must therefore receive a bundle containing $j$.

Replace each assigned bundle by its recipient’s interested singleton when it contains that singleton, and otherwise by the outside option. Price singleton $j$ at $p_j$. For a label in no offered bundle, choose a price strictly above $V$.

This replacement weakly reduces every supply load and preserves revenue. It is item-price envy-free:

  • types with $v_t>p_j$ receive $j$ throughout their mass;
  • types with $v_t=p_j$ may receive $j$ or the outside option;
  • types with $v_t<p_j$ receive the outside option;
  • unrelated labels cannot increase utility at nonnegative prices.

Thus every bundle-price outcome is matched in revenue by an item-price outcome.

6.2 Polynomial-time price selection

Each label can now be optimized independently. For every distinct value $p$ among types interested in $j$, define

$$ H_j(p)=\sum_{t:S_t=\{j\},\ v_t>p}\mu_t, $$

$$ E_j(p)=\sum_{t:S_t=\{j\},\ v_t=p}\mu_t. $$

Price $p$ is feasible exactly when

$$ H_j(p)\le q_j. $$

Its maximum revenue is

$$ r_j(p)=p\min\{q_j,H_j(p)+E_j(p)\}. $$

Serve all strictly demanding types, then fill the remaining capacity with indifferent types. The largest interested value is always a feasible candidate.

These candidate prices dominate all other feasible prices with positive revenue. Given such a price $P$, let $p$ be the smallest interested value satisfying $v_t\ge P$. Every buyer served at $P$ remains willing and affordable at $p$, and every type strictly demanding at $p$ already strictly demanded at $P$. Keeping the same allocation therefore remains feasible and weakly increases revenue.

Choose the best candidate independently for every label. Since each type is interested in at most one label, the resulting allocations combine without any additional budget or demand interaction.

The algorithm uses at most $\tau$ candidates per label and polynomial-time rational arithmetic. It dominates every bundle-price outcome. Conversely, restricting an item-price outcome to its assigned bundles gives a valid bundle-price outcome. Hence the algorithm’s revenue equals $\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}$.

7. What remains open

The general question now has a finite rational optimization description, polynomial-size near-optimal outcomes, and polynomial-time price optimization for fixed $m$. The exact singleton-interest result also permits arbitrary heterogeneous values and rational supplies.

What is missing is a bound connecting the two revenue suprema for arbitrary interest sets, together with a uniform polynomial-time construction meeting the exact requested threshold. None of the price-region arguments proves

$$ R\ge\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{BP}}4 $$

for general inputs. The fixed-$m$ enumeration has an exponent depending on $m$, and polynomial-size certificates do not themselves provide polynomial-time search.
