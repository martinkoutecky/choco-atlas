The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), Borda with fixed tie-breaking, a finite-support public-history distribution \(\bar\mu\) over partial orders \(Q_t\), and a queried homogeneous cohort of mass \(\delta\) sharing private \(P_t^{\mathrm{true}}\), current \(P_t\), and \(Q_t\), with \(P_t(c_j,c_k)\), compute either a truthful response or an order \(P_t'\) satisfying \(P_t'\models Q_t\cup\{(c_k,c_j)\}\), never producing a worse Borda winner than \(P_t\) for the cohort's common true ranking over any feasible mass-weighted completion and producing a strictly better winner for at least one completion, while minimizing \(d_{\mathrm{swap}}(P_t,P_t')\). Feasible completions assign variables \(y_{t,P}\ge0\) to complete rankings extending \(Q_t\), with \(\sum_{P\models Q_t}y_{t,P}=\bar\mu_t\); after a strategic response, move mass \(\delta\) from \(Q_t\) to \(\operatorname{cl}(Q_t\cup\{(c_k,c_j)\})\).

A hardness barrier for the exact continuous response problem

I prove that the problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under deterministic polynomial-time Turing reductions, even when the queried reversal has a unique minimum-distance response and only one residual history is uncertain. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,L$ would imply $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$.

The distinction between reductions matters: the proof below does not establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness under polynomial-time many-one reductions. Interpreting the task’s hardness question in that conventional, stronger sense, the result is partial.

1. Definitions and the main reduction

For a strict partial order $Q$ on $n$ candidates, define its Borda score polytope by
$$ K(Q)=\operatorname{conv}\{b_S:S\models Q\}, \qquad b_S(i)=n-\operatorname{pos}_S(i). $$
Thus $K(Q)$ consists exactly of the expected Borda score vectors of distributions over linear extensions of $Q$. Every $x\in K(Q)$ satisfies
$$ 0\leq x_i\leq n-1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=\frac{n(n-1)}2. $$

A dyadic rational is a rational of the form $a/2^b$, with integers $a,b$ and $b\geq0$. All election masses constructed below are dyadic, so the construction also applies if “binary-rational” is interpreted specifically as dyadic.

Proposition 1. Given $Q$ and a dyadic vector $v$ satisfying
$$ 0\leq v_i\leq n-1, \qquad \sum_i v_i=\frac{n(n-1)}2, $$
one can construct, in polynomial time, an instance of the response problem such that:

  • $\mathcal M$ consists of one ranking $R$;
  • $R$ dominates $P_t$ if and only if $v\in K(Q)$;
  • every completion outside the target score vector produces the same winner under $P_t$ and $R$;
  • the instance has $m=n+2$ candidates and $\tau=O(m)$ support types.

The proof requires an explicit way to supply fixed Borda score offsets.

2. Realizing small score offsets with complete histories

Write $\mathbf1$ for the vector whose coordinates are all $1$, and $e_c$ for the coordinate vector of candidate $c$.

Lemma 2. Let there be $m$ candidates. Let $\beta>0$ be dyadic, and let $d$ be the least power of two satisfying $d\geq m-1$. Suppose a dyadic vector $g$ satisfies
$$ \sum_c g_c=0, \qquad |g_c|\leq\frac{\beta}{2d}. $$
Then a profile of total mass $\beta$, supported on $O(m)$ complete rankings, can have Borda score vector
$$ \beta\frac{m-1}{2}\mathbf1+g. $$
Its masses and rankings are computable in polynomial time and have polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Fix a candidate $h$. For each $c\neq h$, choose a complete ranking $T_c$ in which $c$ is immediately above $h$. Let $T_c^{\mathrm{rev}}$ be its reverse.

Assign mass
$$ a=\frac{\beta}{2d} $$
to each of $T_c$ and $T_c^{\mathrm{rev}}$. A ranking and its reverse give every candidate a combined Borda score of $m-1$. Consequently, each such pair contributes $a(m-1)\mathbf1$.

The mass assigned so far is $\beta(m-1)/d$. Allocate the remaining mass
$$ \beta\left(1-\frac{m-1}{d}\right) $$
equally between any ranking and its reverse. The resulting score vector is
$$ \beta\frac{m-1}{2}\mathbf1. $$

For each $c\neq h$, make one further transfer.

  • If $g_c>0$, transfer mass $g_c$ from $T_c^{\mathrm{rev}}$ to the ranking obtained by swapping its adjacent pair $h,c$. This adds $g_c(e_c-e_h)$.
  • If $g_c<0$, transfer mass $-g_c$ from $T_c$ to the ranking obtained by swapping its adjacent pair $c,h$. This again adds $g_c(e_c-e_h)$.

The bound on $|g_c|$ ensures that every source has sufficient mass. These transfers preserve total mass and nonnegativity. Their combined score change is
$$ \sum_{c\neq h}g_c(e_c-e_h)=g, $$
because $\sum_c g_c=0$.

There are at most three rankings per $c\neq h$, plus the final balanced pair. All masses are dyadic. Identical rankings can be merged, and zero masses omitted. This proves the lemma. $\square$

3. Encoding membership as a unique dominating reversal

Let the original candidates be $1,\ldots,n$. Add candidates $j,k$, and put
$$ m=n+2, \qquad c_0=\frac{m-1}{2}. $$

Choose a positive dyadic $\alpha$ satisfying
$$ \alpha\leq\frac{1}{64m^3}, $$
with encoding length $O(\log m)$, and define
$$ \delta=m\alpha, \qquad \beta=1-\alpha-\delta, \qquad H=c_0+\alpha. $$
In particular, $\beta>\frac12$.

3.1 The cohort and the uncertain type

Give the cohort type $t$ mass $\bar\mu_t=\delta$. Its history orders the original candidates as
$$ 1\succ2\succ\cdots\succ n $$
and imposes no comparison involving $j$ or $k$. Set
$$ P=P_t=(1,\ldots,n,j,k), $$
and
$$ P_t^{\mathrm{true}}=(k,1,\ldots,n,j). $$
Both rankings extend the cohort history. The queried pair $j,k$ is incomparable in its transitive closure.

Because the entire type is the cohort,
$$ r_t=0. $$

Give another type $u$ mass $\alpha$. Its history contains $Q$ on the original candidates, places every original candidate above $j$, and places $j$ above $k$. Its extensions are precisely
$$ (S,j,k), \qquad S\models Q. $$
Thus its residual contribution, for some $x\in K(Q)$, is
$$ \alpha(x_i+2)\quad\text{to original candidate }i, $$
and respectively $\alpha$ and $0$ to $j$ and $k$. Conversely, every $x\in K(Q)$ is realizable by a feasible distribution of this type’s mass.

The remaining mass $\beta$ will have complete histories.

3.2 The desired fixed scores

Let $b_P(c)=m-\operatorname{pos}_P(c)$. Prescribe the fixed-history contribution $B$ by
$$ B_i=H-\alpha(v_i+2)-\delta b_P(i) \qquad(1\leq i\leq n), $$
$$ B_j=H-\alpha-\delta, \qquad B_k=H-\delta. $$

We now verify that Lemma 2 realizes these values.

Since
$$ \sum_i(v_i+2)+1=\frac{m(m-1)}2, $$
and
$$ \sum_i b_P(i)+1=\frac{m(m-1)}2, $$
we obtain
$$ \sum_c B_c = mH-\alpha\frac{m(m-1)}2 -\delta\left(\frac{m(m-1)}2+1\right). $$
The identity $\delta=m\alpha$ gives
$$ mH=\frac{m(m-1)}2+\delta. $$
Therefore
$$ \sum_c B_c=\beta\frac{m(m-1)}2. $$

Define
$$ g_c=B_c-\beta c_0. $$
It follows that $\sum_c g_c=0$.

For the coordinate bound, define
$$ a_i=v_i+2,\qquad a_j=1,\qquad a_k=0. $$
Every $a_c$ and $b_P(c)$ lies in $[0,m-1]$, and
$$ g_c=(\alpha+\delta)c_0+\alpha-\alpha a_c -\delta b_P(c)-\delta\,\mathbf1_{\{c=k\}}. $$
Using $\delta=m\alpha$ yields
$$ \begin{aligned} |g_c| &\leq (\alpha+\delta)\frac{m-1}{2} +\alpha+\alpha(m-1)+\delta(m-1)+\delta\\ &= \alpha\left(\frac{3m^2}{2}+m-\frac12\right) \leq2m^2\alpha \leq\frac{1}{32m}. \end{aligned} $$
If $d$ is the least power of two at least $m-1$, then $d<2(m-1)$. Hence
$$ \frac{\beta}{2d}>\frac{1}{8(m-1)}>\frac{1}{32m}. $$
Lemma 2 therefore supplies the required fixed profile.

All its positive masses become supplied complete-history types. Identical complete histories are merged. If the history of $u$ happens to be complete, merging it with an identical fixed history preserves the same fixed score contribution. The total support size remains $O(m)$.

3.3 Minimum distance and the winners

The ranking
$$ R=(1,\ldots,n,k,j) $$
extends the cohort history with the required reversal and has Kendall distance one from $P$. Any reversed ranking must invert the pair $j,k$, so its distance is at least one. A ranking at distance one can invert no other pair, and is therefore exactly $R$. Thus
$$ \mathcal M=\{R\}. $$

Choose tie-breaking priority
$$ j\triangleright k\triangleright1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright n. $$

For a completion represented by $x\in K(Q)$, the total scores under $P$ are
$$ S_P(i)=H+\alpha(x_i-v_i), \qquad S_P(j)=H, \qquad S_P(k)=H-\delta. $$
Under $R$, original candidates’ scores remain the same, while
$$ S_R(j)=H-\delta, \qquad S_R(k)=H. $$

There are exactly two cases.

Case 1: $x=v$. Every original candidate has score $H$. Under $P$, tie-breaking elects $j$. Under $R$, it elects $k$. This is a strict improvement because the true ranking puts $k$ first and $j$ last.

Case 2: $x\neq v$. Both vectors have the same coordinate sum. Therefore some coordinate satisfies $x_i>v_i$: otherwise every coordinate would satisfy $x_i\leq v_i$, with at least one strict inequality, contradicting equality of the sums. This candidate has score strictly above $H$.

Consequently, both ballots elect an original candidate. All original scores and their tie-breaking priorities are unchanged, so they elect the same candidate.

It follows that $R$ never worsens the outcome, and improves it for some completion exactly when $v\in K(Q)$. This proves Proposition 1. $\square$

In particular, this is a reduction to the actual queried-response problem, including its global minimum-distance requirement.

4. A full-dimensional score polytope with an explicit interior ball

To derive a complexity barrier from Proposition 1, we need a membership-to-optimization theorem. Such a theorem requires a full-dimensional convex body and explicit geometric bounds. The following construction provides them.

Let $Q$ be a strict partial order on $n$ jobs. Add a new candidate $z$ incomparable with every job, obtaining $\widetilde Q$ on $N=n+1$ candidates.

Project $K(\widetilde Q)$ onto the original $n$ coordinates:
$$ K'=\{(x_1,\ldots,x_n):x\in K(\widetilde Q)\}. $$
The omitted coordinate is uniquely recovered as
$$ x_z=\frac{N(N-1)}2-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i. $$
Thus membership in $K'$ is equivalent to membership of this reconstructed vector in $K(\widetilde Q)$.

Lemma 3. One can compute a dyadic point $a_0$ and rational radii $r,R$ such that
$$ B_2(a_0,r)\subseteq K'\subseteq B_2(0,R), $$
where
$$ r=\frac{1}{2d}, \qquad R=n^2, $$
and $d$ is the least power of two at least $n$. The construction has polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Compute any linear extension $T$ of $Q$. For each job $i$, insert $z$ immediately before $i$ or immediately after $i$ in $T$. Both rankings extend $\widetilde Q$.

Let their projected Borda vectors be $p_i^-$ and $p_i^+$, with the superscript $+$ corresponding to $z$ immediately after $i$. The two rankings differ only by an adjacent swap, so
$$ p_i^+-p_i^-=e_i. $$

Choose any projected extension vector $q_0\in K'$ and put
$$ a_0= \frac{1}{2d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i^++p_i^-) +\left(1-\frac nd\right)q_0. $$

For any vector $h$ with $|h_i|\leq1/(2d)$, define
$$ q_i(h)= \left(\frac12+dh_i\right)p_i^+ + \left(\frac12-dh_i\right)p_i^-. $$
Its coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one. Moreover,
$$ a_0+h= \frac1d\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_i(h) +\left(1-\frac nd\right)q_0. $$
This is a convex combination of points of $K'$. Thus $K'$ contains the coordinate cube of radius $1/(2d)$ about $a_0$, and consequently contains the Euclidean ball of that radius.

Every coordinate of $K'$ lies in $[0,n]$. Therefore
$$ \|x\|_2\leq n\sqrt n\leq n^2 \qquad(x\in K'). $$
All vectors used have integer coordinates, and $d$ is a power of two, so $a_0$ is dyadic with polynomial encoding length. $\square$

5. A response oracle supplies weak membership

For a compact convex set $K$, define
$$ K_\varepsilon = \{u:\operatorname{dist}_2(u,K)\leq\varepsilon\}, $$
and
$$ K_{-\varepsilon} = \{u:B_2(u,\varepsilon)\subseteq K\}. $$

A weak membership oracle, given rational $u$ and $\varepsilon>0$, may either assert $u\in K_\varepsilon$ or assert $u\notin K_{-\varepsilon}$.

Suppose an oracle decides whether the response problem has a dominating member of $\mathcal M$. It supplies weak membership for $K'$ as follows.

Round a queried rational vector $u$ to a dyadic vector $u'$ satisfying
$$ \|u-u'\|_2\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$
This requires polynomially many bits. Reconstruct the omitted coordinate to obtain a dyadic vector $v$ on $N$ candidates whose sum is $N(N-1)/2$.

If any coordinate of $v$ lies outside $[0,N-1]$, then $v\notin K(\widetilde Q)$. Otherwise, use Proposition 1 to test its membership through one response query.

  • If $u'\in K'$, assert $u\in K'_\varepsilon$, since its distance to $u'$ is at most $\varepsilon/2$.
  • If $u'\notin K'$, assert $u\notin K'_{-\varepsilon}$. Indeed, membership of $u$ in $K'_{-\varepsilon}$ would imply that $u'\in K'$.

Both answers meet the weak membership specification. Every election query has polynomial size and dyadic masses.

6. From membership to an $\mathrm{NP}$-hard scheduling problem

We use two established results, stated precisely here.

Scheduling hardness theorem. Given a precedence partial order $Q$, nonnegative integer weights $w_i$, and an integer bound $B$, deciding whether there is a linear extension $T$ satisfying
$$ \sum_i w_i\operatorname{pos}_T(i)\leq B $$
is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete. This is single-machine precedence-constrained scheduling with unit processing times and weighted completion-time objective. The unit-time hardness is explicitly recalled and used in [Hassin and Levin, Section 2](https://www.math.tau.ac.il/~hassin/minsum_sc.pdf), citing the classical scheduling hardness results.

Membership-to-optimization theorem. Given a compact convex body $K\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, rational data satisfying
$$ B_2(a_0,r)\subseteq K\subseteq B_2(0,R), $$
and a weak membership oracle, a deterministic oracle-polynomial-time algorithm solves weak optimization. For rational $c$ and $\varepsilon>0$, it either finds rational $y$ such that
$$ y\in K_\varepsilon, \qquad c^\top y\geq c^\top x-\varepsilon \quad\text{for every }x\in K_{-\varepsilon}, $$
or asserts that $K_{-\varepsilon}$ is empty. This is [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Corollary 4.3.12](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf).

We now check the application and its precision requirements.

6.1 The scheduling objective is linear on $K'$

Start with an instance of the scheduling hardness theorem and form $\widetilde Q,K'$ as above. Give $z$ weight zero.

For a ranking $S$ extending $\widetilde Q$,
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i\operatorname{pos}_S(i) = N\sum_i w_i-\sum_i w_i b_S(i). $$
Consequently, minimizing weighted completion time is equivalent to maximizing
$$ w^\top x \qquad\text{over }x\in K'. $$

Adding $z$ does not change the minimum scheduling cost. Any extension of $Q$ can be followed by $z$, preserving all original positions. Conversely, removing $z$ from an extension of $\widetilde Q$ cannot increase an original job’s position and therefore cannot increase the cost, because all weights are nonnegative.

Let
$$ V=\max_{x\in K'}w^\top x. $$
This number is an integer: maximizing a linear function over the convex hull of finitely many integer vectors attains its maximum at one of those vectors.

6.2 Weak optimization determines $V$ exactly

Lemma 3 verifies all geometric hypotheses of the optimization theorem. Section 5 supplies the required weak membership oracle.

Write
$$ W=\sum_i w_i. $$
The case $W=0$ is immediate, so assume $W\geq1$. Choose a positive dyadic tolerance satisfying
$$ \varepsilon\leq \frac{1}{16(1+2RW/r+W)}. $$
Its encoding length is polynomial in the scheduling input length. This choice also gives $\varepsilon<r$, so $K'_{-\varepsilon}$ is nonempty.

Let $x^*\in K'$ attain $V$, and define
$$ \lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{r}, \qquad x_\varepsilon=(1-\lambda)x^*+\lambda a_0. $$
Convexity and the inclusion $B_2(a_0,r)\subseteq K'$ imply
$$ B_2(x_\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subseteq K', $$
so $x_\varepsilon\in K'_{-\varepsilon}$. Furthermore,
$$ \|x^*-a_0\|_2\leq2R, \qquad \|w\|_2\leq W. $$
Hence
$$ w^\top x_\varepsilon \geq V-\frac{2RW\varepsilon}{r}. $$

The weak optimizer returns $y$ satisfying
$$ w^\top y \geq V-\varepsilon\left(1+\frac{2RW}{r}\right). $$
Since $y\in K'_\varepsilon$, there is also an $x\in K'$ with $\|y-x\|_2\leq\varepsilon$. Therefore
$$ w^\top y\leq V+W\varepsilon. $$
The chosen tolerance gives
$$ |w^\top y-V|\leq\frac1{16}. $$
Rounding $w^\top y$ to the nearest integer thus recovers $V$ exactly.

The optimum scheduling cost is consequently
$$ N W-V. $$
Comparing this integer with $B$ decides the original $\mathrm{NP}$-complete problem.

The optimization theorem uses polynomially many oracle calls of polynomial encoding length. Each weak membership call uses at most one polynomial-size response instance. Thus the entire procedure is a deterministic polynomial-time Turing reduction.

7. Proven consequence and remaining question

Theorem 4. Deciding whether a dominating ranking exists in $\mathcal M$ is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under deterministic polynomial-time Turing reductions. This remains true on instances with:

  • $\mathcal M$ consisting of the single adjacent reversal of the queried pair;
  • $O(m)$ supplied support types;
  • only one potentially incomplete history having positive residual mass;
  • dyadic masses;
  • a true cohort ranking that extends its public history.

Computing the required response is at least as hard: on these instances, the returned ranking is $R$ exactly for a positive decision answer, and otherwise is the stipulated fallback $P$.

The required history updates preserve this conclusion. Both added query orientations are consistent with the cohort history, and the update can be computed once the response is known by rational mass arithmetic and transitive closure.

Therefore, an unrestricted polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,L$ would imply $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. The outstanding point is whether this can be strengthened to a polynomial-time many-one hardness reduction. The membership-to-optimization theorem provides adaptive oracle access, so the argument above does not establish that stronger claim.
