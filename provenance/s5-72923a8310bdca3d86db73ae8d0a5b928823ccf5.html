The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a connected type graph \(H=(T,F)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and independent aggregate observations \(Z_t\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2/\mu_t)\), compute a type’s exact posterior action at round \(k\) under the paper’s common uniform prior, returning coefficients \(a_{tu}^{(k)}\) such that \(X_t(k)=\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}^{(k)}Z_u\).

Input includes a connected undirected simple graph \(H=(T,F)\), with \(T\) the support and \(m=|F|\), a designated type \(t\), a unary-encoded round \(k\geq0\), and a known positive binary-rational variance scale \(\sigma^2\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

I prove new polynomial-bit-complexity results for arbitrary positive rational masses on distance-regular graphs and on graphs formed by joining cliques at articulation vertices. More generally, I give a polynomial-time, graph-only test characterizing when every estimate is the mass-weighted average over its current graph ball.

These results do not establish a polynomial denominator bound on arbitrary graphs or an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction.

“Binary-rational output” is interpreted as an integer numerator and a positive integer denominator, both written in binary. Even dyadic input masses can produce coefficients without finite binary expansions, as the example below shows.

1. Posterior characterization and locality

Put $n=\tau$, identify coefficient vectors with columns in $\mathbb R^n$, and define
$$ D=\operatorname{diag}(1/\mu_u:u\in T), \qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_u:u\in T). $$
Let $e_v$ denote the coordinate vector associated with vertex $v$. Define its information space at round $j$ by
$$ U_v(j)=\operatorname{span}\bigl( \{e_v\}\cup \{a_u(i):u\in N(v),\ 0\leq i<j\} \bigr). $$

Lemma 1. Every estimate has a rational coefficient vector $a_v(j)$ satisfying $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}a_v(j)=1$. It is the unique minimizer of
$$ V(a)=a^{\mathsf T}Da $$
over
$$ a\in U_v(j), \qquad \mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}a=1. $$
The coefficients do not depend on $\sigma^2$.

Proof. At round $0$, the coefficient vector is $e_v$. Suppose all earlier coefficient vectors are rational and have coordinate sum $1$.

Select linearly independent rows from the coefficient vectors of the observations available to $v$, and place them in a matrix $A$ with $r$ rows. This selection preserves the information: every discarded observation is a known linear combination of the retained observations. We have
$$ A\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1_r. $$
Conditional on $\theta$, the retained observation vector $Y=AZ$ has distribution
$$ Y\mid\theta\sim N(\theta\mathbf 1_r,\sigma^2 C), \qquad C=ADA^{\mathsf T}. $$
The matrix $C$ is positive definite because $D$ is positive definite and $A$ has full row rank.

Set
$$ d=\mathbf 1_r^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\mathbf 1_r>0, \qquad x=\frac{\mathbf 1_r^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}Y}{d}. $$
Completing the square gives
$$ (Y-\theta\mathbf 1_r)^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}(Y-\theta\mathbf 1_r) = d(\theta-x)^2+Y^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}Y-dx^2. $$
Thus normalizing the likelihood against Lebesgue measure gives the proper posterior
$$ N(x,\sigma^2/d). $$
Its mean has coefficient vector
$$ a_v(j)= \frac{A^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\mathbf 1_r} {\mathbf 1_r^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\mathbf 1_r}. $$
This vector is rational, has coordinate sum $1$, and is independent of $\sigma^2$.

Every vector in $U_v(j)$ has the form $A^{\mathsf T}\lambda$. Its coordinate sum is $\mathbf 1_r^{\mathsf T}\lambda$, and its variance objective is $\lambda^{\mathsf T}C\lambda$. Positive definiteness of $C$ makes this objective strictly convex. Its unique constrained minimum occurs at
$$ \lambda= \frac{C^{-1}\mathbf 1_r} {\mathbf 1_r^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}\mathbf 1_r}, $$
which gives the displayed posterior vector. This completes the induction. $\square$

For $S\subseteq T$, write
$$ M(S)=\sum_{u\in S}\mu_u. $$
For a vertex $v$ and integer $j\geq0$, let
$$ B_v(j)=\{u:\operatorname{dist}_H(v,u)\leq j\}. $$

Lemma 2. Every coefficient vector $a_v(j)$ is supported in $B_v(j)$. For every nonempty $S$, the unique minimum-variance unbiased vector supported in $S$ is
$$ g_S(u)= \begin{cases} \mu_u/M(S),&u\in S,\\ 0,&u\notin S. \end{cases} $$

Proof. Locality follows by induction. A neighbor’s report $a_u(i)$ available at round $j$ is supported in $B_u(i)$, and
$$ B_u(i)\subseteq B_v(j) \qquad (u\in N(v),\ i<j). $$
Lemma 1 places $a_v(j)$ in the span of these reports and $e_v$.

For an unbiased vector supported in $S$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$ 1= \left(\sum_{u\in S}a_u\right)^2 \leq M(S)\sum_{u\in S}\frac{a_u^2}{\mu_u}. $$
Equality holds precisely when $a_u$ is proportional to $\mu_u$ on $S$. The normalization determines the proportionality constant as $1/M(S)$. $\square$

Consequently, to prove that an estimate equals the weighted average on its ball, it suffices to prove that this average belongs to its information space.

2. A graph-only characterization of exact ball averaging

Let $b_v(j)\in\{0,1\}^n$ be the indicator vector of $B_v(j)$. Define the proposed ball-average coefficient vector
$$ g_v(j)=\frac{Wb_v(j)}{M(B_v(j))}. $$

Let $\delta$ be the diameter of $H$. For each vertex $v$ and each $1\leq j\leq\delta$, consider the following condition:
$$ b_v(j)\in \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q} \bigl( \{e_v\}\cup \{b_u(i):u\in N(v),\ 0\leq i<j\} \bigr). $$
Call this condition $\mathcal R(v,j)$.

Theorem 3. The following are equivalent:

  • Every condition $\mathcal R(v,j)$ holds.
  • For every choice of positive masses summing to $1$, every vertex and round satisfy

$$ a_v(j)=g_v(j). $$

  • There exists one choice of positive masses summing to $1$ for which the preceding equality holds at every vertex through round $\delta$.

Moreover, these equivalent conditions can be tested in polynomial bit time using only the graph.

Proof. Suppose that all estimates through round $j-1$ are ball averages. Since all normalizing masses are nonzero, their scalar factors do not affect spans. Also, $We_v=\mu_v e_v$. Therefore
$$ U_v(j)=W\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R} \bigl( \{e_v\}\cup \{b_u(i):u\in N(v),\ 0\leq i<j\} \bigr). $$
The matrix $W$ is invertible. Hence
$$ g_v(j)\in U_v(j) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathcal R(v,j). $$
Here membership over $\mathbb R$ and over $\mathbb Q$ is equivalent: both are characterized by equality of the ranks of rational matrices.

If $\mathcal R(v,j)$ holds, Lemma 2 shows that $g_v(j)$ is the unique minimum-variance unbiased vector supported in $B_v(j)$. Locality and Lemma 1 therefore give
$$ a_v(j)=g_v(j). $$
Starting from $g_v(0)=e_v$, induction proves that condition 1 implies ball averaging through round $\delta$, for every positive mass vector.

At round $\delta$, every ball is $T$, so every estimate has coefficient vector $\mu$. This vector is the unique global minimum-variance unbiased vector by Lemma 2. Information spaces are nested, and each type’s previously computed estimate belongs to its retained information space. Thus all later estimates remain $\mu$. This proves condition 2.

Condition 2 implies condition 3 immediately. Conversely, if condition 3 holds, the displayed information-space identity applies at each round through $\delta$. The actual coefficient vector belongs to $U_v(j)$, so $g_v(j)\in U_v(j)$ and $\mathcal R(v,j)$ holds. Thus condition 3 implies condition 1.

For the algorithm, compute all graph distances by breadth-first searches. Each membership test is a rank comparison between a $0$–$1$ matrix and the matrix obtained by appending $b_v(j)^{\mathsf T}$. Each matrix has $n$ columns and at most
$$ 1+\deg(v)j\leq 1+(n-1)^2 $$
rows. There are at most $n(n-1)$ tests.

Exact rank computation has polynomial bit complexity here. During elimination, Schur-complement entries can be represented as ratios of minors of the original integer matrix. Every minor has order at most $n$, and its determinant has absolute value at most $n!$ by the determinant expansion. Thus these numerators and denominators have $O(n\log(n+1))$ bits, and polynomially many exact arithmetic operations suffice. $\square$

There is also a useful consequence for graphs that fail the test.

Corollary 4. The first round at which some type departs from ball averaging is independent of the positive masses.

Proof. Let $q$ be the first round with a failed condition $\mathcal R(v,q)$. All earlier conditions pass, so the proof of Theorem 3 gives ball averaging through round $q-1$ for every positive mass vector. At round $q$, the failed membership makes $g_v(q)$ infeasible for every such vector. Thus the actual estimate differs from the ball average at that vertex. If no condition fails through the diameter, ball averaging holds forever. $\square$

This characterization concerns ball averaging, not polynomial computability in general. Failure of the test is not a hardness certificate.

3. Bit complexity whenever the graph test succeeds

Proposition 5. On every graph satisfying Theorem 3, the requested coefficients can be computed in polynomial bit time. Every coefficient has numerator and denominator bit length $O(L)$.

Proof. Write the input masses as $\mu_u=p_u/q_u$, with positive integer numerators and denominators. Define
$$ Q=\prod_{u\in T}q_u, \qquad w_u=p_uQ/q_u. $$
These are integers, their bit lengths are $O(L)$, and normalization gives
$$ \sum_{u\in T}w_u=Q. $$
For a ball $B=B_t(k)$, every nonzero output coefficient is
$$ a_{tu}^{(k)}=\frac{w_u}{\sum_{x\in B}w_x}. $$
Both numerator and denominator are positive integers at most $Q$. Reduction to lowest terms cannot increase their bit lengths.

A breadth-first search determines the ball. Integer summation and greatest-common-divisor computations then produce the reduced coefficients in polynomial bit time. For $k\geq\delta$, the answer is simply the mass vector. The graph test itself is polynomial by Theorem 3. $\square$

The following two sections prove that substantial graph families pass this test.

4. Distance-regular graphs with arbitrary masses

A connected graph is distance-regular if it is $d$-regular and, for every ordered pair $(x,y)$ at distance $i$, the numbers of neighbors of $y$ at distances $i-1$, $i$, and $i+1$ from $x$ depend only on $i$. Denote these numbers by $c_i,a_i,b_i$, respectively. Thus
$$ c_i+a_i+b_i=d, \qquad c_0=a_0=0, \qquad b_0=d. $$

For $0\leq i<\delta$, we have $c_{i+1}>0$: a shortest path between vertices at distance $i+1$ supplies a neighbor that decreases their distance.

Theorem 6. On a distance-regular graph, for arbitrary positive masses,
$$ X_v(j)= \frac{\sum_{x\in B_v(j)}\mu_xZ_x} {M(B_v(j))} $$
for every vertex and every round. Therefore exact coefficient computation has polynomial bit complexity.

Proof. Define the unnormalized ball totals
$$ P_v(r)=\sum_{x\in B_v(r)}\mu_xZ_x, \qquad P_v(-1)=0. $$
Fix $0\leq r<\delta$. Count the multiplicity of a signal $\mu_xZ_x$ in
$$ \sum_{u\in N(v)}P_u(r). $$

If $\operatorname{dist}(v,x)\leq r-1$, all $d$ neighbors of $v$ have distance at most $r$ from $x$.

If $\operatorname{dist}(v,x)=r$, apply distance-regularity to the ordered pair $(x,v)$. Exactly $c_r+a_r=d-b_r$ neighbors of $v$ have distance at most $r$ from $x$.

If $\operatorname{dist}(v,x)=r+1$, exactly $c_{r+1}$ neighbors of $v$ have distance at most $r$ from $x$.

More distant signals contribute zero. Consequently,
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{u\in N(v)}P_u(r) ={}&dP_v(r-1) +(d-b_r)\bigl(P_v(r)-P_v(r-1)\bigr)\\ &+c_{r+1}\bigl(P_v(r+1)-P_v(r)\bigr). \end{aligned} $$
Rearranging gives the exact identity
$$ P_v(r+1)= \frac{ \sum_{u\in N(v)}P_u(r) -(d-b_r-c_{r+1})P_v(r) -b_rP_v(r-1) }{c_{r+1}}. $$

Now induct on $r$. At round $0$, the asserted formula holds. Assume it holds through round $r$ at every vertex. At round $r+1$, vertex $v$ receives $X_u(r)$ and hence can compute
$$ P_u(r)=M(B_u(r))X_u(r) $$
for each neighbor. Its own earlier totals $P_v(r)$ and $P_v(r-1)$ are linear functions of retained information. The recurrence therefore makes $P_v(r+1)$ available.

Dividing by $M(B_v(r+1))$ gives a feasible ball-average coefficient vector. Lemmas 1 and 2 identify this vector with the actual posterior estimate. Induction reaches round $\delta$, when all estimates equal the global weighted average. They remain there afterward.

The single-vertex case is immediate. Polynomial bit complexity follows from Proposition 5. $\square$

For example, the $h$-dimensional hypercube satisfies
$$ c_i=i,\qquad a_i=0,\qquad b_i=h-i: $$
flipping one of the $i$ differing coordinates decreases distance, and flipping one of the other coordinates increases it. Thus this theorem applies even when its masses are unequal and destroy the symmetry of the observation variances.

The cited paper proves the equal-variance result for distance-transitive graphs. The argument above independently establishes the stated arbitrary-mass result using intersection counts. [Mossel–Olsman–Tamuz](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.0747v3)

5. Trees of cliques with arbitrary masses

Consider a family $\mathcal C$ of vertex subsets, each of size at least $2$, with the following properties:

  • Two distinct vertices are adjacent in $H$ exactly when they belong to a common member of $\mathcal C$.
  • The bipartite incidence graph with vertex classes $T$ and $\mathcal C$, and edges $vC$ whenever $v\in C$, is a tree.

This explicitly defines the class used here; these graphs are commonly called block graphs. It includes trees and complete graphs.

Theorem 7. On every graph in this class, for arbitrary positive masses,
$$ X_v(j)= \frac{\sum_{x\in B_v(j)}\mu_xZ_x} {M(B_v(j))} $$
for every vertex and round. Exact coefficient computation therefore has polynomial bit complexity.

Proof. Retain the notation
$$ P_v(r)=\sum_{x\in B_v(r)}\mu_xZ_x, \qquad P_v(-1)=0. $$

Fix a clique $C\in\mathcal C$. Removing its node from the incidence tree leaves components indexed by $w\in C$. Let $T_{w,C}$ be the set of type vertices in the component containing $w$, and define
$$ R_{w,C}(r)= \sum_{\substack{x\in T_{w,C}\\ \operatorname{dist}_H(w,x)\leq r}}\mu_xZ_x, \qquad R_{w,C}(-1)=0. $$

Graph distance equals half the length of the corresponding path in the incidence tree. Indeed, a graph edge gives a two-edge incidence path, while the unique incidence path gives a graph path of half its length. In particular, for distinct $v,w\in C$ and $x\in T_{w,C}$,
$$ \operatorname{dist}_H(v,x)=1+\operatorname{dist}_H(w,x). $$

For $v\in C$, let $Q_{v,C}(r)$ be the part of $P_v(r)$ lying outside $T_{v,C}$. Then
$$ Q_{v,C}(r)=\sum_{w\in C\setminus\{v\}}R_{w,C}(r-1) \qquad (r\geq1), $$
and set $Q_{v,C}(0)=Q_{v,C}(-1)=0$. We also have
$$ P_v(r)=R_{v,C}(r)+Q_{v,C}(r). $$

Put $h=|C|-1$. For $u\in C\setminus\{v\}$ and $j\geq1$, the component decomposition gives
$$ P_u(j-1) = R_{u,C}(j-1) +\sum_{w\in C\setminus\{u\}}R_{w,C}(j-2). $$
Summing over these $h$ choices of $u$ gives
$$ \sum_{u\in C\setminus\{v\}}P_u(j-1) = Q_{v,C}(j) +(h-1)Q_{v,C}(j-1) +hR_{v,C}(j-2). $$
The multiplicities are explicit: each component indexed by $w\neq v$ appears once in the first sum and $h-1$ times in the second; the component indexed by $v$ appears $h$ times in the second. Hence
$$ \begin{aligned} Q_{v,C}(j) ={}&\sum_{u\in C\setminus\{v\}}P_u(j-1) -(h-1)Q_{v,C}(j-1)\\ &-h\bigl(P_v(j-2)-Q_{v,C}(j-2)\bigr). \end{aligned} $$
This identity also holds at $j=1$ under the stated zero conventions.

Inductively suppose that, through round $j-1$, every estimate is its ball average and every type has reconstructed its earlier directional totals $Q_{v,C}$. At round $j$, each $u\in C\setminus\{v\}$ is a neighbor of $v$, so its report supplies $P_u(j-1)$ after multiplication by the known ball mass. All other terms in the recurrence are retained information. Thus $v$ can reconstruct $Q_{v,C}(j)$ for every incident clique $C$.

The branches through the incident clique nodes partition all vertices other than $v$, so
$$ P_v(j)=\mu_vZ_v+\sum_{\substack{C\in\mathcal C\\v\in C}}Q_{v,C}(j). $$
Therefore its ball total, and hence its normalized ball average, is available. Lemmas 1 and 2 show that the posterior chooses this average.

At round $0$, the ball-average formula holds and every directional total is zero, establishing the induction base. The single-vertex graph is immediate separately. Polynomial bit complexity follows from Proposition 5. $\square$

No clique decomposition needs to be supplied as extra input for the coefficient algorithm: the graph-only test of Theorem 3 already certifies the ball formula on every graph covered by this theorem.

6. A limitation: ball averaging fails even on a small chordal graph

The preceding results do not extend to all graphs with simple clique decompositions.

Consider vertices $0,\ldots,5$ and edges
$$ F= \bigl\{ \{0,1\},\{0,2\},\{1,2\}, \{1,3\},\{1,4\},\{2,4\},\{2,5\} \bigr\}. $$
Take the dyadic masses
$$ \mu=\frac18(1,1,1,1,1,3). $$
The graph is connected and chordal. Vertices $3$ and $5$ are leaves; the remaining four vertices induce two triangles sharing edge $\{1,2\}$, and their only four-cycle has that edge as a chord.

At round $1$,
$$ X_1(1)=\frac{Z_0+Z_1+Z_2+Z_3+Z_4}{5}, $$
and
$$ X_2(1)=\frac{Z_0+Z_1+Z_2+Z_4+3Z_5}{7}. $$
Thus vertex $0$’s round-$2$ information is equivalent to
$$ Z_0,\quad Z_1,\quad Z_2,\quad Z_3+Z_4,\quad Z_4+3Z_5. $$
Its feasible coefficient vectors have the form
$$ (\alpha_0,\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\beta,\beta+\gamma,3\gamma), $$
with unbiasedness constraint
$$ \alpha_0+\alpha_1+\alpha_2+2\beta+4\gamma=1. $$
The objective, divided by $8$, is
$$ \alpha_0^2+\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2 +\beta^2+(\beta+\gamma)^2+3\gamma^2. $$
For fixed $\alpha_0+\alpha_1+\alpha_2$, the first three terms are uniquely minimized when all three coordinates equal some $r$. The remaining strictly convex problem is therefore
$$ \text{minimize }3r^2+2\beta^2+2\beta\gamma+4\gamma^2, \qquad 3r+2\beta+4\gamma=1. $$
The Lagrange equations are
$$ 6r=3\lambda,\qquad 4\beta+2\gamma=2\lambda,\qquad 2\beta+8\gamma=4\lambda. $$
They give
$$ r=\lambda/2,\qquad \beta=2\lambda/7,\qquad \gamma=3\lambda/7. $$
The constraint then gives $\lambda=14/53$, so
$$ a_0(2)=\frac1{53}(7,7,7,4,10,18). $$
Strict convexity proves that this stationary point is the unique optimum.

Here $B_0(2)=T$, but the resulting vector differs from the global weighted average $\mu$. The graph-only obstruction is equally direct: outside coordinates $0,1,2$, the two neighbor-ball indicators restrict to
$$ (1,1,0),\qquad (0,1,1). $$
Their span does not contain $(1,1,1)$, because matching the first and third coordinates forces the middle coordinate to equal $2$. Thus $\mathcal R(0,2)$ fails.

This example limits the ball reconstruction method. Its denominator $53$ is not an asymptotic output-size lower bound.

7. The precise remaining bit-complexity question

For completeness, the denominator characterization from the notes can be established directly.

Proposition 8. The unrestricted problem admits a polynomial-time bit algorithm if and only if all requested reduced coefficient denominators have a uniform polynomial bound on their bit lengths in the input length.

Proof. Necessity follows from explicit output length.

For sufficiency, assume such a bound. Since $e_v$ is always feasible in Lemma 1,
$$ V(a_v(j))\leq 1/\mu_v. $$
Consequently, for every coordinate,
$$ \frac{|a_{v,u}(j)|^2}{\mu_u}\leq 1/\mu_v. $$
Every input mass satisfies $\mu_u\leq1$ and $\mu_v\geq2^{-L}$, so
$$ |a_{v,u}(j)|\leq2^{L/2}. $$
If a coefficient is written in reduced form as $p/q$, a polynomial bound on the bit length of $q$ therefore also bounds the bit length of $p$ polynomially.

Every intermediate vector in a round-by-round simulation is itself the requested output of an instance obtained by changing the designated type and replacing $k$ by some $j\leq k$. These instances have length $O(L)$ because the round is unary encoded. The uniform hypothesis therefore bounds all intermediate coefficient heights polynomially.

At each vertex, retain at most $n$ linearly independent original observation rows as a basis of its memory. To advance one round, append the latest neighbor rows and select an independent subset. Then apply the covariance formula in Lemma 1.

These rank computations and linear solves have polynomial bit complexity when their entries have polynomial height. Explicitly, clearing denominators produces integer matrices whose entry lengths are polynomial in $n$ and the coefficient heights. The determinant expansion bounds every minor’s bit length polynomially in the matrix dimensions and entry lengths; exact elimination uses ratios of such minors. Thus each update takes polynomial bit time.

There are $nk$ updates, and all required intermediate heights are polynomially bounded. The complete simulation is therefore polynomial in $m,n,L,k$. $\square$

The new results prove the required denominator bound, indeed an $O(L)$ bound, on every graph passing the graph-only test. They leave unrestricted coefficient growth unbounded by a polynomial in this argument. No $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness conclusion follows from failure of that test.
