The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(S\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies a strict school preference order and an ordered priority class at each school, run mass-proposing DA, splitting a type’s mass when a cutoff passes through it. For each baseline cohort \((t,r)\), find rational assignment masses \(x_{t,r,s}\) that conserve the cohort, respect capacities, and assign no mass below its DA school. Require a positive improvement whenever DA is inefficient; require priority violations against type mass only where that mass is unimprovable or benefits in the assignment; and require that any justifiable DA improvement Pareto-dominating the output strictly expands its beneficiary mass, allowing relabeling of interchangeable agents within a cohort. Can this be done in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools with positive binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_s\), and let \(\bar S=S\cup\{\varnothing\}\), where \(\varnothing\) has unlimited capacity. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has strict preferences \(\succ_t\) over \(\bar S\) and an ordered priority class at each school. Break equal classes between types by support-list order, for both DA and priority comparisons; members of one type remain interchangeable. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Run student-proposing mass DA with these priorities: schools retain the highest-priority proposing mass up to capacity, splitting a type at a cutoff, and rejected mass proposes to its next acceptable school; schools below \(\varnothing\) are unacceptable. Write \(d_{t,r}\) for the resulting mass assigned to \(r\). Each positive \(d_{t,r}\) defines a baseline cohort \((t,r)\).
An admissible assignment consists of masses \(x_{t,r,s}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{t,r,s}=d_{t,r}\), \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,r,s}\leq\kappa_s\) for \(s\in S\), and \(x_{t,r,s}=0\) unless \(s\succeq_t r\). Require non-wastefulness: a school with unused capacity is preferred to no assigned school carrying positive mass.
Define beneficiary mass \(b_{t,r}(x)=\sum_{s\succ_t r}x_{t,r,s}\) and \(b(x)=\sum_{t,r}b_{t,r}(x)\). A cohort is unimprovable if \(b_{t,r}(z)=0\) in every admissible assignment \(z\); DA is inefficient if some admissible \(z\) has \(b(z)>0\).
A positive portion \(x_{t,r,a}\) suffers a priority violation if some school \(s\succ_t a\) contains positive mass of a type with strictly lower priority than \(t\). Call \(x\) justifiable when every such portion belongs to an unimprovable cohort or has \(a\succ_t r\).
An assignment \(y\) Pareto-dominates \(x\) if, within every baseline cohort, their school distributions admit a coupling moving mass only from a school to a weakly preferred school, with positive total mass moving strictly upward. This permits relabeling only within a cohort; all assignments and couplings in these definitions may have real masses.
The required output is a rational admissible, justifiable \(x\), with \(b(x)>0\) whenever DA is inefficient, such that every admissible justifiable \(y\) Pareto-dominating \(x\) satisfies \(b(y)>b(x)\).
Is this output problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?
For finite students with strict priorities and arbitrary quotas, SJBC+ is stated to compute in polynomial time a justifiable DA improvement that cannot be Pareto-dominated by another justifiable improvement without strictly enlarging its beneficiary set. Polynomial-time solvability

The output problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\).

The construction has two steps: find a positive justifiable improvement whenever DA is inefficient, then fix every cohort’s beneficiary mass and maximize a strictly increasing rank utility over a linear program. The second step gives exactly the required protection against Pareto dominators with unchanged total beneficiary mass.

1. Notation and computation of DA

Let \(C\) be the set of positive baseline cohorts. For \(c=(t,r)\), write \(t_c=t\), \(r_c=r\), and \(d_c=d_{t,r}\). Thus \(n:=|C|\leq\tau(m+1)\). Write \(x_{c,s}\) for \(x_{t,r,s}\), and write \(t\triangleright_s u\) when \(t\) has strictly higher priority than \(u\), after the prescribed support-order tie-breaking.

We first justify that the specified mass DA outcome can be computed in polynomial time.

We use the following established stable-allocation result: for a bipartite instance with strict preferences, rational vertex capacities, and rational bounds on individual edges, a stable allocation and its proposer-optimal stable allocation can be computed in strongly polynomial time. Proposer optimality implies weak dominance of every other stable allocation in each proposer’s distribution. The relevant results are Theorem 2, Theorem 10, and Corollary 4 of the [corrected Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Here the proposer vertices are the support types, with capacities \(\mu_t\); the receiving vertices are the schools, with capacities \(\kappa_s\). Add an outside-option vertex of capacity \(1\), which suffices because total mass is \(1\). Keep acceptable school edges and outside-option edges, each with edge bound \(2\). These bounds never bind. School preferences are the specified strict order on types; outside-option priorities may be arbitrary. Thus all hypotheses hold.

For completeness, the proposer-optimal allocation in this compressed instance is precisely the specified mass DA outcome. To verify this identification, choose an integer \(D\) clearing the input denominators. Conceptually replace each type by \(D\mu_t\) students and each school’s capacity by \(D\kappa_s\) seats. Give members of a type one common internal priority order at every school. Aggregated finite DA then performs exactly the stated mass-retention and rejection operations.

The finite DA theorem applies: preferences and priorities are strict, quotas are integral, and student-proposing DA is student optimal among stable matchings. Any rational stable allocation of the compressed instance can also be lifted to such a finite instance, after clearing its denominators. Within each type, assign students in internal priority order to schools in preference order. This prevents blocking within a type; stability of the compressed allocation prevents blocking between types and blocking through vacancies. Consequently, mass DA weakly dominates every rational stable allocation type by type. The rational proposer-optimal allocation returned by the stable-allocation algorithm weakly dominates mass DA as well, so their distributions coincide.

This expansion is only a correctness argument; the algorithm works on the compressed instance. In particular, \(d\) is computable in polynomial time and has polynomial encoding length.

Two consequences of DA will be used repeatedly:

  • Non-wastefulness: if \(s\succ_t r\) and \(d_{t,r}>0\), then school \(s\) is full.
  • No baseline priority violation: if \(s\succ_t r\), \(d_{t,r}>0\), and \(t\triangleright_s u\), then \(d_{u,s}=0\).

Indeed, the relevant portion of type \(t\) proposed to \(s\) and was rejected. A school that rejects mass remains full, and its retained priority cutoff can only improve. Both statements also follow from stability of the compressed allocation.

2. Non-wastefulness becomes automatic

Let \(P\) be the polytope defined by the linear constraints

\(\displaystyle x_{c,s}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{s\in\bar S}x_{c,s}=d_c,\qquad \sum_{c\in C}x_{c,s}\leq\kappa_s\quad(s\in S),\)

together with \(x_{c,s}=0\) whenever \(s\not\succeq_{t_c}r_c\).

At this point, non-wastefulness is not included explicitly.

Lemma 1. Every \(x\in P\) preserves every school’s DA occupancy and is non-wasteful. Hence \(P\) is exactly the set of admissible assignments.

Proof. Let

\(\displaystyle K_s=\sum_t d_{t,s},\qquad F=\{s\in S:K_s=\kappa_s\}.\)

By baseline non-wastefulness, every strictly improving destination belongs to \(F\). Moreover, every baseline school is weakly preferred to the outside option, so the outside option cannot be a strictly improving destination.

Thus mass initially in \(F\) stays in \(F\). Its total is already \(\sum_{s\in F}\kappa_s\), the entire capacity of \(F\). Capacity feasibility therefore implies that every school in \(F\) remains full and that no mass initially outside \(F\) enters \(F\).

Every cohort initially outside \(F\) must consequently stay at its baseline school: its only possible alternative destinations would be strictly preferred schools in \(F\). All school occupancies are therefore unchanged.

Finally, suppose \(s\notin F\). Baseline non-wastefulness gives \(r_c\succeq_{t_c}s\) for every cohort \(c\). Every positive assigned portion satisfies \(a\succeq_{t_c}r_c\), hence \(a\succeq_{t_c}s\). Thus the schools that remain underfilled cause no waste. ∎

The polytope \(P\) is nonempty, rational, and compact. In particular, for every cohort,

\(\displaystyle b_c(x)=d_c-x_{c,r_c}.\)

3. Identify the improvable cohorts by linear programming

For each \(c\in C\), solve

\(\displaystyle \beta_c=\max_{z\in P}b_c(z).\)

Define

\(\displaystyle I=\{c\in C:\beta_c>0\}.\)

By Lemma 1 and the definition in the question, \(I\) is exactly the set of improvable cohorts. These are ordinary rational linear programs, so exact comparisons with zero are possible in polynomial time.

DA is inefficient exactly when \(I\neq\varnothing\). If \(I=\varnothing\), output the baseline assignment. It is admissible and has no priority violations. A Pareto dominator of this baseline would necessarily give some cohort positive beneficiary mass, contradicting \(I=\varnothing\).

Henceforth assume \(I\neq\varnothing\).

4. Construct a positive justifiable improvement

Define the set of schools desired by improvable cohorts:

\(\displaystyle T=\{s\in S:\text{some }c\in I\text{ satisfies }s\succ_{t_c}r_c\}.\)

This set is nonempty.

Lemma 2. If \(c\in I\), then \(r_c\in T\).

Proof. Choose \(z\in P\) with \(b_c(z)>0\). The proof of Lemma 1 shows that cohorts whose baseline lies outside \(F\) cannot improve. Thus \(r_c\in F\).

Let

\(\displaystyle O_s=\sum_{h:r_h=s}b_h(z),\qquad A_s=\sum_{h:r_h\neq s}z_{h,s}.\)

These are the outgoing and incoming strictly improving masses at school \(s\). Occupancy preservation gives \(A_s=O_s\). For \(s=r_c\), we have \(O_s\geq b_c(z)>0\), so \(A_s>0\).

Consequently, some cohort \(h\) with \(r_h\neq r_c\) has \(z_{h,r_c}>0\). Its support restriction implies \(r_c\succ_{t_h}r_h\), and therefore \(b_h(z)>0\). Thus \(h\in I\) and \(r_c\in T\). ∎

For every \(s\in T\), choose a cohort \(c(s)\in I\) satisfying \(s\succ_{t_{c(s)}}r_{c(s)}\) whose type has highest priority at \(s\) among all such cohorts. Ties between cohorts of the same type can be broken arbitrarily.

By Lemma 2,

\(\displaystyle f(s)=r_{c(s)}\)

defines a map from \(T\) to itself. A finite directed graph with one outgoing edge per vertex contains a directed cycle. Choose one,

\(\displaystyle s_1\longrightarrow s_2\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow s_k\longrightarrow s_1.\)

There are no self-loops, because \(s\succ_{t_{c(s)}}r_{c(s)}\); hence \(k\geq2\). Put \(c_i=c(s_i)\), so \(r_{c_i}=s_{i+1}\), with indices interpreted cyclically. The cohorts \(c_i\) are distinct because their baseline schools are distinct.

Set

\(\displaystyle \delta=\min_{1\leq i\leq k}d_{c_i}>0.\)

Starting from the baseline assignment, move mass \(\delta\) of cohort \(c_i\) from \(s_{i+1}\) to \(s_i\), for every \(i\). Call the resulting assignment \(q\).

Every moved portion strictly improves. Every cycle school loses and gains the same mass, so \(q\in P\). Lemma 1 gives admissibility, and

\(\displaystyle b(q)=k\delta>0.\)

Lemma 3. The assignment \(q\) is justifiable.

Proof. It suffices to rule out violations against baseline portions of improvable cohorts, since all other violations are permitted by the definition.

Suppose an improvable cohort \(h\) retains positive baseline mass at \(r_h\), and suppose \(s\succ_{t_h}r_h\). Consider any positive mass at \(s\).

If that mass was already at \(s\) in DA, its type cannot have strictly lower priority than \(t_h\), by absence of baseline priority violations.

Otherwise, \(s=s_i\) for some cycle school, and the incoming type is \(t_{c(s)}\). But \(h\in I\) and \(s\succ_{t_h}r_h\), so \(h\) was among the candidates considered when choosing \(c(s)\). The highest-priority choice therefore prevents \(t_h\triangleright_s t_{c(s)}\).

Thus no baseline portion of an improvable cohort suffers a violation. ∎

5. Fix beneficiary masses and express justifiability linearly

Fix the baseline residuals of \(q\):

\(\displaystyle h_c=q_{c,r_c}\qquad(c\in C).\)

Define \(P_h\) by starting with \(P\), imposing

\(\displaystyle z_{c,r_c}=h_c\qquad(c\in C),\)

and adding the following zero constraints:

\(\displaystyle z_{e,s}=0\)

whenever there is a cohort \(c\in I\) such that

\(\displaystyle h_c>0,\qquad s\succ_{t_c}r_c,\qquad t_c\triangleright_s t_e.\)

These are linear equalities.

Lemma 4. The polytope \(P_h\) consists exactly of the admissible, justifiable assignments with baseline residuals \(h\).

Proof. Consider a cohort \(c\in I\) with \(h_c>0\). Its positive baseline portion is not a beneficiary portion, so justifiability requires that no preferred school contain positive mass of a strictly lower-priority type. Because all variables are nonnegative, this requirement is exactly the listed zero constraints.

An improvable cohort with \(h_c=0\) has only beneficiary portions, so all its violations are justified. Violations against unimprovable cohorts are also justified. There are no other cases. Admissibility is equivalent to membership in \(P\), by Lemma 1. ∎

By Lemma 3, \(q\in P_h\), so this polytope is nonempty. Every \(z\in P_h\) has the same beneficiary mass in every cohort:

\(\displaystyle b_c(z)=d_c-h_c=b_c(q).\)

6. Optimize rank utility and prove the required maximality

Let \(\operatorname{rank}_t(s)\in\{1,\ldots,m+1\}\) be the position of \(s\) in type \(t\)’s strict preference order, with smaller ranks better. Define

\(\displaystyle u_t(s)=m+2-\operatorname{rank}_t(s).\)

Thus \(u_t\) strictly increases with preference. Solve the linear program

\(\displaystyle \max_{z\in P_h}W(z),\qquad W(z)=\sum_{c\in C}\sum_{s\in\bar S}u_{t_c}(s)z_{c,s},\)

and output a rational optimum \(x\).

By Lemma 4, \(x\) is admissible and justifiable. Also,

\(\displaystyle b(x)=b(q)>0.\)

It remains to prove the requested property against real-mass Pareto dominators.

Suppose an admissible, justifiable \(y\) Pareto-dominates \(x\). For each cohort \(c\), let \(\gamma^c_{a,s}\) be a witnessing coupling, with first marginal \(x_{c,a}\), second marginal \(y_{c,s}\), and positive entries only when \(s\succeq_{t_c}a\).

All assignments in this cohort are weakly preferred to \(r_c\). Therefore mass ending at \(r_c\) can come only from \(r_c\). It follows that

\(\displaystyle y_{c,r_c}=\gamma^c_{r_c,r_c}\leq\sum_s\gamma^c_{r_c,s}=x_{c,r_c}.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle b_c(y)\geq b_c(x)\quad\text{for every }c,\qquad b(y)\geq b(x).\)

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that \(b(y)=b(x)\). The finitely many nonnegative differences \(b_c(y)-b_c(x)\) must then all vanish. Hence

\(\displaystyle y_{c,r_c}=x_{c,r_c}=h_c\quad\text{for every }c.\)

Since \(y\) is admissible and justifiable, Lemma 4 gives \(y\in P_h\).

On the other hand, the couplings give

\(\displaystyle W(y)-W(x)=\sum_{c,a,s}\gamma^c_{a,s}\bigl(u_{t_c}(s)-u_{t_c}(a)\bigr)>0.\)

Every summand is nonnegative. A positive total mass moves strictly upward, and each strict move increases utility by at least \(1\), which proves the strict inequality. This contradicts optimality of \(x\) over \(P_h\).

Therefore every admissible justifiable Pareto dominator \(y\) satisfies \(b(y)>b(x)\), as required.

7. Running time and rational output

The algorithm uses the compressed DA computation, at most \(n\) linear programs to identify \(I\), a functional-graph cycle search, and one final linear program.

Each assignment polytope has at most \(n(m+1)\) variables. Even a direct enumeration of the zero constraints uses only polynomially many constraints. The DA masses have polynomial encoding length; the seed uses only a minimum and additions or subtractions of those masses. Thus the final linear program has polynomial encoding length.

Exact rational linear programming produces an optimal rational solution of polynomial encoding length in polynomial time. Its optimality is over the entire real polytope, so the argument excludes real-mass dominators as well as rational ones.

This proves polynomial-time solvability of the exact output problem.
