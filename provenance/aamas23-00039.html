The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type and action sets \(\Theta\) and \(A\), rational masses \(\mu\), rational \(\Lambda\), \(\varepsilon\), and \(\delta\), and a succinct continuous anonymous congestion payoff representation \(U_\theta(a,x)\) with a simulator producing \(\hat U\) satisfying \(\sup_{\theta,a,x}|\widehat U_\theta(a,x)-U_\theta(a,x)| \le \eta\) with probability \(1-\delta\), compute \(x \in X_\mu=\{x \ge 0:\Sigma_a x_{\theta,a}=\mu_\theta\}\) and \(z\) such that \(W_\mu(x)+\Lambda R_\mu(x) \le \inf_{y \in X_\mu}(W_\mu(y)+\Lambda R_\mu(y))+\varepsilon\) and \(|z-\inf_{y \in X_\mu}(W_\mu(y)+\Lambda R_\mu(y))| \le \varepsilon\), with time and sample complexity as the targets.

A society is given by its support: the list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(A\) be a nonempty list of \(m\) actions and \(X_\mu=\{x\geq0:\sum_{a\in A}x_{\theta,a}=\mu_\theta\}\).
There are \(r\) resources, and each action specifies a resource subset \(E_a\). Write \(\ell_e(x)=\sum_{\theta,a:e\in E_a}x_{\theta,a}\) and \(U_\theta(a,x)=\sum_{e\in E_a}g_{\theta,e}(\ell_e(x))\), where the unknown resource-payoff functions are continuous and utilities lie in \([-1,1]\); no monotonicity is assumed.
An individual is nonatomic: changing action leaves loads fixed. Define \(W_\mu(x)=\sum_{\theta,a}x_{\theta,a}U_\theta(a,x)\) and \(R_\mu(x)=\max_{\theta,a:x_{\theta,a}>0}\bigl(\max_{b\in A}U_\theta(b,x)-U_\theta(a,x)\bigr)\). Ties are allowed, and the current action is an allowed deviation.
The supplied learning routine, requested at rational accuracy \(\eta>0\) and failure probability \(\beta\in(0,1)\), returns resource-payoff circuits using rational constants and \(+, -, \times, \min, \max\), inducing \(\widehat U\) with \(\sup_{\theta,a,x\in X_\mu}|\widehat U_\theta(a,x)-U_\theta(a,x)|\leq\eta\) with probability at least \(1-\beta\). Given rational \(\Lambda\geq0\), \(\epsilon>0\), and \(\delta\in(0,1)\), compute rational \(x\in X_\mu\) and \(z\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), \(F(x)\leq v+\epsilon\) and \(|z-v|\leq\epsilon\), where \(F=W_\mu+\Lambda R_\mu\) and \(v=\inf_{y\in X_\mu}F(y)\).
Is this additive-approximation problem solvable with additional computation polynomial in \(m,\tau,r,L,1/\epsilon,1+\Lambda,\log(1/\delta)\), or is it NP-hard even for exact utilities and inverse-polynomial \(\epsilon\)?
What are its total time and sample complexities in these parameters and the supplied learning costs \(T_{\rm learn}\) and \(N_{\rm learn}\)?

Result

The problem is NP-hard even with exact affine resource-payoffs, one supported type, and inverse-polynomial accuracy. Thus estimation stability does not yield a polynomial-time optimizer.

A general algorithm using the supplied learner and existential real feasibility has the following upper bounds. Set \(\eta=\epsilon/[4(1+2\Lambda)]\). Then

\[ \text{Time}\ \leq\ T_{\rm learn}(\eta,\delta) + 2^{\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,r,L,\log(2+1/\epsilon),\log(2+\Lambda))}, \]

\[ \text{Samples}\ =\ N_{\rm learn}(\eta,\delta). \]

These are upper bounds, not claims of optimal running time. Under the standard explicit binary encoding of the returned rationals, succinct circuits can also force superpolynomial output length, independently of complexity-class assumptions.

1. NP-hardness with one type and affine payoffs

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a simple undirected graph with \(n\) vertices. Construct a society with one type of mass \(1\), and one action for each vertex. Write \(x_i\) for the mass choosing vertex \(i\); thus \(x_i\geq 0\) and \(\sum_i x_i=1\).

For each edge \(\{i,j\}\), introduce a resource used by actions \(i\) and \(j\), with payoff \(g_{\{i,j\}}(t)=-t\). For each vertex \(i\), introduce a private resource used only by action \(i\), with payoff \(g_i(t)=d_i t\), where \(d_i\) is its degree.

All payoffs are affine with integer coefficients. The resulting action utility is

\[ U(i,x) = d_i x_i-\sum_{j:\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i+x_j) = -\sum_{j:\{i,j\}\in E}x_j. \]

Consequently, every action utility lies in \([-1,0]\), as required. The construction has \(m=n\), \(\tau=1\), and \(r=n+|E|\), with polynomial encoding length.

Take \(\Lambda=0\). Then

\[ F(x)=W(x)=-2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}x_i x_j. \]

We now prove its minimum exactly.

Clique identity

Let \(\omega(G)\) denote the maximum clique size, and define \(Q(x)=2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}x_i x_j\). Then

\[ \max_{x\geq0,\ \sum_i x_i=1}Q(x)=1-\frac1{\omega(G)}. \]

To prove the upper bound, suppose two nonadjacent vertices \(i,j\) both have positive mass. With all other coordinates fixed, write

\[ Q(x)=Q_{\rm rest}+2x_i A_i+2x_j A_j, \qquad A_i=\sum_{h:\{i,h\}\in E}x_h, \quad A_j=\sum_{h:\{j,h\}\in E}x_h. \]

Because \(i,j\) are nonadjacent, neither \(A_i\) nor \(A_j\) depends on \(x_i,x_j\). Moving their combined mass to whichever vertex has the larger \(A\)-value cannot decrease \(Q\), and removes a positive coordinate.

Repeating this operation produces a distribution supported on a clique of some size \(k\leq\omega(G)\). On that support,

\[ Q(x)=1-\sum_i x_i^2 \leq 1-\frac1k \leq 1-\frac1{\omega(G)}, \]

where the first inequality follows from \((\sum_i x_i)^2\leq k\sum_i x_i^2\). Since the merging operations never decrease \(Q\), this bounds the original distribution as well.

Conversely, the uniform distribution on a maximum clique achieves \(1-1/\omega(G)\). Hence

\[ v=-1+\frac1{\omega(G)}. \]

Inverse-polynomial approximation distinguishes CLIQUE

Consider CLIQUE with threshold \(2\leq k\leq n\). In a yes-instance,

\[ v\leq a:=-1+\frac1k; \]

in a no-instance,

\[ v\geq b:=-1+\frac1{k-1}. \]

The separation is

\[ b-a=\frac1{k(k-1)}\geq\frac1{n^2}. \]

Choose \(\epsilon=1/(8n^2)\). Any output satisfying \(|z-v|\leq\epsilon\) distinguishes the cases by comparison with \((a+b)/2\).

The restricted CLIQUE problem used here is NP-hard: the usual reduction from a formula with \(q\) clauses creates vertices for literal occurrences and joins occurrences from different clauses exactly when they are not complementary. A clique of size \(q\) selects mutually consistent literals satisfying every clause; a satisfying assignment supplies such a selection.

Thus the requested approximation problem is NP-hard.

This reduction also works with the strictly positive choice \(\Lambda=1/(8n^2)\). Indeed, the constructed utilities give \(0\leq R(x)\leq1\), so its penalized value satisfies

\[ v_0\leq v_\Lambda\leq v_0+\Lambda. \]

The yes/no value intervals remain disjoint after allowing the stated approximation error.

For randomized algorithms, the usual qualification matters. A polynomial-time algorithm satisfying the requested probability guarantee would imply \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{RP}\): run it with \(\delta=1/4\), validate feasibility of \(x\), and accept exactly when its affine-instance welfare is below \((a+b)/2\). No-instance distributions can never pass this test, while a successful output on a yes-instance does pass.

2. Existence of a minimum and estimation stability

Define the occupied support by \(S(x)=\{(\theta,a):x_{\theta,a}>0\}\). Since every type has positive mass and the action list is nonempty, \(S(x)\) is nonempty.

Although regret need not be continuous, it is lower semicontinuous. To see this, let \(x^{(j)}\to x\). Every coordinate positive at \(x\) is eventually positive at \(x^{(j)}\). Therefore, eventually,

\[ R_\mu(x^{(j)}) \geq \max_{\substack{(\theta,a)\in S(x)\\b\in A}} \bigl(U_\theta(b,x^{(j)})-U_\theta(a,x^{(j)})\bigr). \]

The right-hand side converges to \(R_\mu(x)\), because it is a finite maximum of continuous functions. Welfare is continuous, so \(F\) is lower semicontinuous for \(\Lambda\geq0\). The feasible set is compact; consequently, the infimum \(v\) is attained.

Next, suppose two utility systems \(U,V\) differ uniformly by at most \(\eta\). Their welfare values at a fixed distribution differ by at most

\[ \sum_{\theta,a}x_{\theta,a}\eta=\eta. \]

Every deviation difference changes by at most \(2\eta\). Taking maxima over the same occupied support therefore gives \(|R_U(x)-R_V(x)|\leq2\eta\). Writing \(C=1+2\Lambda\), we obtain

\[ |F_U(x)-F_V(x)|\leq C\eta, \qquad |v_U-v_V|\leq C\eta. \]

The second inequality follows by taking infima in both directions. This directly proves stability for the stated nonatomic objective. The [supplied paper](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2023/pdfs/p272.pdf) studies the related utility-stability principle for finite games.

If an optimizer for the estimated objective returns \(x\) with \(F_V(x)\leq v_V+\gamma\), then

\[ F_U(x)\leq v_U+\gamma+2C\eta. \]

The factor \(2C\eta\) accounts separately for estimation at the returned distribution and estimation of the optimal value.

3. A general algorithm with rational output

Call the learner once at accuracy \(\eta=\epsilon/(4C)\) and failure probability \(\delta\). Clip each returned action utility to obtain

\[ \widetilde U_\theta(a,x) = \max\{-1,\min\{1,\widehat U_\theta(a,x)\}\}. \]

Clipping preserves the uniform error guarantee because the true utilities lie in \([-1,1]\). It also ensures bounded estimated utilities on every learner outcome. Let \(\widetilde F\) and \(\widetilde v\) denote the corresponding objective and minimum.

We construct rational \(y,z\) satisfying

\[ \widetilde F(y)\leq\widetilde v+\frac{\epsilon}{2}, \qquad |z-\widetilde v|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4}. \]

3.1 Threshold feasibility is an existential real formula

For rational \(t\), consider the question whether some \(x\in X_\mu\) satisfies \(\widetilde F(x)\leq t\).

Introduce variables for loads, circuit gates, utilities \(u_{\theta,a}\), and a regret bound \(\rho\). A multiplication gate is represented by an equation of degree two. A maximum gate \(h=\max(p,q)\) is represented by

\[ h\geq p,\qquad h\geq q,\qquad (h=p\ \text{or}\ h=q), \]

and a minimum gate is represented analogously.

Add feasibility constraints, \(0\leq\rho\leq2\), and, for every \(\theta,a,b\),

\[ x_{\theta,a}=0 \quad\text{or}\quad u_{\theta,b}-u_{\theta,a}\leq\rho. \]

Finally add

\[ \sum_{\theta,a}x_{\theta,a}u_{\theta,a}+\Lambda\rho\leq t. \]

This existential formula is equivalent to the threshold question. In one direction, its constraints imply \(\rho\geq\widetilde R(x)\), and \(\Lambda\geq0\). In the other direction, choose \(\rho=\widetilde R(x)\).

The formula has polynomially many variables and polynomial predicates, all of degree at most two. Circuit intermediate values remain variables, so expanding high-degree circuit polynomials is unnecessary.

We use the standard existential real decision theorem: a Boolean formula involving \(s\) integer polynomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(k\) variables can be decided in \((sd)^{O(k)}\operatorname{poly}(B)\) bit operations, where \(B\) bounds coefficient bit lengths. Rational coefficients are cleared first. These arithmetic and bit bounds follow from the one-block case of Theorem 2.18 in [Basu’s survey](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf).

All hypotheses hold here: there is one existential block, rational coefficients, and bounded polynomial degree.

3.2 Approximate the estimated value

Because clipped utilities lie in \([-1,1]\),

\[ -1\leq\widetilde F(x)\leq1+2\Lambda. \]

Use threshold feasibility to bisect this interval. Maintain endpoints \(a,b\) with \(a\leq\widetilde v\leq b\) and with the upper threshold \(b\) feasible. A feasible midpoint replaces \(b\); an infeasible midpoint replaces \(a\).

Stop when \(b-a\leq\epsilon/4\). Then

\[ \widetilde v\leq b\leq\widetilde v+\frac{\epsilon}{4}, \]

and the formula \(\widetilde F(x)\leq b\) remains feasible. Set \(z=b\).

3.3 Compute a usable continuity bound

The returned circuits admit an effectively computable common constant \(K\) such that

\[ |\widetilde U_\theta(a,x)-\widetilde U_\theta(a,y)| \leq K\|x-y\|_1. \]

Here is an explicit way to compute it. For each resource circuit on load interval \([0,1]\), propagate an absolute-value bound \(M\) and a Lipschitz bound \(K_g\). Constants have Lipschitz bound zero; the input load has bounds \(M=K_g=1\). Addition and subtraction use sums of bounds. Multiplication uses

\[ M=M_1M_2, \qquad K_g=M_1K_2+M_2K_1. \]

For minimum and maximum gates, use \(M=\max(M_1,M_2)\) and \(K_g=\max(K_1,K_2)\). Loads change by at most \(\|x-y\|_1\); summing the resource bounds for each action and then clipping establishes the claimed \(K\).

These bounds can be large, but their bit lengths are at most singly exponential in circuit encoding length. Indeed, each gate increases the logarithm of the combined magnitude and Lipschitz bound by at most a constant plus twice the preceding maximum.

Let \(H=1+CK\). Whenever \(S(y)\subseteq S(x)\), we have

\[ \widetilde F(y)\leq\widetilde F(x)+H\|y-x\|_1. \]

For welfare, the absolute change is at most \((1+K)\|y-x\|_1\), by splitting the change into distribution weights and utility values. For regret, support containment ensures that every occupied action at \(y\) is also available in the regret maximum at \(x\). Its deviation difference changes by at most \(2K\|y-x\|_1\). Combining these bounds proves the inequality.

3.4 Find rational coordinates while preserving occupied support

Start with the feasible formula \(\widetilde F(x)\leq b\). For each coordinate, test whether adding \(x_{\theta,a}=0\) preserves feasibility. If so, retain that equality; otherwise retain \(x_{\theta,a}>0\). Each step preserves feasibility. At the end, every feasible point of the retained formula has the same occupied support.

If \(m=1\), the distribution is uniquely determined by the masses, and no rounding is needed. Otherwise let \(D=\tau(m-1)\), and choose a positive rational \(h\) with

\[ h\leq\frac{\epsilon}{8DH}. \]

For each positive coordinate, use feasibility queries to bisect its interval \([0,\mu_\theta]\) until the retained interval has width at most \(h\). Always retain a half for which the current formula remains feasible. Thus all final intervals have a common feasible point \(w\), although the algorithm need not calculate it.

For each type, choose an action \(a_\theta^\ast\) in the retained positive support. For every other action, set \(y_{\theta,a}\) to its interval’s rational lower endpoint, using zero for inactive actions. Set the remaining coordinate to

\[ y_{\theta,a_\theta^\ast} = \mu_\theta-\sum_{a\ne a_\theta^\ast}y_{\theta,a}. \]

Each lower endpoint is nonnegative and at most the corresponding coordinate of \(w\). Hence \(y\) is feasible, and its occupied support is contained in that of \(w\).

For each type, the increase in the remaining coordinate equals the sum of decreases in the other coordinates. Therefore

\[ \|y-w\|_1\leq2\tau(m-1)h=2Dh. \]

The preceding support-sensitive bound now gives

\[ \widetilde F(y) \leq\widetilde F(w)+2DHh \leq b+\frac{\epsilon}{4} \leq\widetilde v+\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \]

This proves the claimed estimated-objective guarantees with rational outputs.

3.5 Transfer to the true utilities

On the learner’s uniform-accuracy event,

\[ F(y) \leq \widetilde v+\frac{\epsilon}{2}+C\eta \leq v+\frac{\epsilon}{2}+2C\eta = v+\epsilon. \]

Also,

\[ |z-v| \leq |z-\widetilde v|+|\widetilde v-v| \leq\frac{\epsilon}{4}+C\eta =\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \]

Thus both requested conclusions hold with probability at least \(1-\delta\).

4. Total time and sample bounds

Let \(S=1+L+\tau m^2+\tau mr\). The existential formulas above have \(O(S)\) variables and predicates of degree at most two.

The computed continuity bounds have bit lengths at most \(2^{O(S)}\). Consequently, the coordinate bisections require at most singly exponentially many queries and produce endpoints with at most singly exponential bit length. Each query is decidable in singly exponential time by the stated existential real theorem, whose dependence on coefficient bit length is polynomial.

This establishes the general bounds

\[ T_{\rm total} \leq T_{\rm learn}\!\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4(1+2\Lambda)},\delta\right) + 2^{\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,r,L,\log(2+1/\epsilon),\log(2+\Lambda))}, \]

\[ N_{\rm total} = N_{\rm learn}\!\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4(1+2\Lambda)},\delta\right). \]

The additional computation is deterministic and uses no further samples. The NP-hardness construction itself uses exact circuits and requires no learning samples.

5. Succinct circuits can force long rational outputs

There is also an unconditional obstruction to polynomial total time under explicit binary rational output.

Take one type of mass \(1\), two actions, and two private resources. For an integer \(s\geq2\), circuits of size \(O(s)\) gates generate

\[ C=2^{2^s}, \qquad a=2^{-2^s}, \]

by repeated squaring of \(2\) and \(1/2\). Let action one have utility zero, and let action two’s resource payoff be

\[ g(t)=-\max\{0,\ 1-2C|t-(1-a)|\}, \]

where absolute value is implemented by a maximum. These utilities lie in \([-1,0]\).

Write \(x\) for action one’s mass and put

\[ h(x)=\max\{0,\ 1-2C|x-a|\}. \]

With the fixed choice \(\Lambda=1/2\), the objective is

\[ F(x)=(x-\tfrac12)h(x). \]

For interior distributions this follows because action two has regret \(h(x)\) and action one has regret zero. At both endpoints \(h(x)=0\), so the formula holds there as well.

At \(x=a\), we obtain \(F(a)=a-1/2\leq-7/16\). Choose inverse-polynomial accuracy \(\epsilon=1/(s+8)\). Every successful output must have negative objective, hence \(h(x)>0\). Therefore

\[ \frac a2<x<\frac{3a}{2}. \]

If the returned rational is \(x=p/q>0\), with positive integers \(p,q\), then \(x\geq1/q\). Consequently,

\[ q>\frac{2}{3a}=\frac23\,2^{2^s}. \]

Its denominator requires more than \(2^s-1\) binary digits. The circuit input has encoding length \(O(s\log s)\), while \(1/\epsilon=O(s)\). Thus successful explicit rational outputs can require superpolynomial length even with fixed \(m,\tau,r,\Lambda\).

The NP-hardness proof already applies to simple affine circuits; this separate construction explains why arbitrary succinct circuits introduce an additional output-size obstruction.
