The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many colleges \(C\), student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational affine type and college utilities over school and cohort composition, find a core allocation \(x\), where \(x_{tc}\geq 0\), \(\sum_c x_{tc}\leq\mu_t\), and each college's cohort vector is \(z_c=(x_{tc})_{t\in T}\). A block is a one-college deviation in which the college retains some current student mass and recruits available mass, forming a new cohort; each participating unit weakly prefers its new school and cohort to its current outcome, the college weakly prefers the new cohort, and the college or a positive mass of participants strictly prefers the deviation. Assume the colleges and the unit mass admit a finite ordered decomposition into top-coalition layers, with type mass divisible across layers, as in the continuous analogue of the paper's weak top-coalition property. Does this promise guarantee a unique core allocation, and can an algorithm find it?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\geq1\) colleges. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) student types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Type utilities are \(u_t(c,z)=a_{tc}+b_{tc}^{\mathsf T}z\), and college utilities are \(v_c(z)=d_c^{\mathsf T}z\), with arbitrary signed rational coefficients. Unmatched students have utility \(0\). Numerical utility comparisons define preferences, including indifference; there is no tie-breaking. Cohort coordinates are absolute masses, and there are no capacities.
An allocation has real entries \(x_{tc}\geq0\), with \(\sum_cx_{tc}\leq\mu_t\). Write \(z_c=(x_{tc})_t\), \(x_{t0}=\mu_t-\sum_cx_{tc}\), and \(u_t(0)=0\). Individual rationality requires \(u_t(c,z_c)\geq0\) whenever \(x_{tc}>0\), and \(v_c(z_c)\geq v_c(y)\) for every \(0\leq y\leq z_c\).
A one-college block chooses \(c\) and real masses \(0\leq y_{td}\leq x_{td}\) from origins \(d\in C\cup\{0\}\), forming \(q_t=\sum_dy_{td}\). For every \(y_{td}>0\), require \(u_t(c,q)\geq u_t(d,z_d)\), interpreting origin \(0\) as unmatched; also require \(v_c(q)\geq v_c(z_c)\). At least one comparison is strict, either for the college or for positive participant mass. Origin \(c\) supplies retained mass; other origins supply recruits. Expelled students and colleges losing recruits need not consent, and \(q=0\) is allowed. A core allocation is individually rational and has no such block.
The layer promise is defined on residual colleges \(D\subseteq C\) and residual masses \(r\). A college layer chooses \(c\in D\) and \(0\leq q\leq r\): \(q\) uniquely maximizes \(v_c\) over that box, and, for each \(q_t>0\), \((c,q)\) is the unique best outcome among unmatched status and all \((d,z)\) with \(d\in D\), \(0\leq z\leq r\), \(z_t>0\). Remove \(c\) and subtract \(q\) from \(r\).
An unmatched layer removes a nonzero vector \(0\leq q\leq r\) whose positive-mass types uniquely prefer unmatched status to every residual school outcome just described. Starting from \((C,\mu)\), some finite sequence of these layers exhausts all colleges and mass. College layers may have \(q=0\); types may be divided across layers. The sequence is promised to exist but is not supplied.

Does this promise guarantee a unique core allocation?
Is exact core search on these promised inputs, allowing an emptiness report, polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite students with strict preferences, ordered residual top-coalition layers guarantee a unique core matching, found in at most \(k\) iterations when there are \(k\) layers; this is an iteration bound rather than a bit-complexity bound for affine inputs. Result

The promise guarantees exactly one core allocation, and exact core search is polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\). On promised inputs, the core is therefore never empty.

Moreover, in that unique allocation, every type is either entirely assigned to one college or entirely unmatched. The proof below gives an algorithm that discovers the layers without being supplied their sequence.

1. Consequences of affine college utilities

Write \(d_{ct}\) for coordinate \(t\) of the college vector \(d_c\). At a residual state \((D,r)\), let \(I=\{t:r_t>0\}\).

Because \(v_c(q)=\sum_t d_{ct}q_t\), its maximizer over \(0\leq q\leq r\) is unique if and only if \(d_{ct}\neq0\) for every \(t\in I\). When it is unique, it is necessarily

\[ q^c_t= \begin{cases} r_t,&d_{ct}>0,\\ 0,&d_{ct}<0, \end{cases} \qquad t\in I, \]

with \(q^c_t=0\) outside \(I\).

Indeed, each coordinate is optimized independently. A positive coefficient forces its upper endpoint, a negative coefficient forces zero, and a zero coefficient on a nondegenerate interval prevents uniqueness.

Consequently, a college layer removes all residual mass of every type it includes.

Call an unmatched layer *saturated* if it removes all residual mass of one type. Whenever a type satisfies the unmatched-layer preference condition, such a saturated layer is permitted.

We next show that greedily choosing these layers preserves the promise.

2. Any eligible choice preserves the promise

Throughout this section, “eligible” means satisfying precisely the layer conditions in the question.

Lemma 1: An eligible college layer can be moved first

Suppose \((D,r)\) admits an exhausting layer sequence, and \((c,q)\) is any eligible college layer at this state. Then an exhausting sequence exists whose first layer is \((c,q)\).

Proof. Let \(S=\{t:q_t>0\}\). Consider an exhausting sequence, and its layer removing \(c\).

Before that layer, no mass of any type in \(S\) can be removed. To prove this, suppose there were a first earlier layer removing positive mass of some \(t\in S\). Immediately before this first removal, \(c\) remains available and \(q\) remains feasible.

If the earlier layer is an unmatched layer, its condition gives \(0>u_t(c,q)\). But eligibility of \((c,q)\) gives \(u_t(c,q)>0\), a contradiction.

If it is a college layer \((e,p)\), then \(e\neq c\). Eligibility of the original layer gives \(u_t(c,q)>u_t(e,p)\), because \(p\leq r\) and \(p_t>0\). Eligibility of the earlier layer, with \((c,q)\) still available, gives the reverse strict inequality. This is again a contradiction.

Thus \(q\) remains feasible until \(c\) is removed. Since it uniquely maximizes \(v_c\) over the original box, it also uniquely maximizes over this smaller box. The layer removing \(c\) therefore uses exactly \(q\).

Move this layer to the beginning. Every layer formerly preceding it removes only types outside \(S\), so its cohort remains feasible. Its residual box and set of alternative colleges only shrink. A unique college maximum remains unique when the feasible set shrinks while retaining its maximizer; likewise, a unique best student outcome remains unique when alternatives are removed. Hence all those earlier layers remain valid.

After these reordered layers, the residual state is exactly the state following the original removal of \(c\). The remaining sequence is unchanged. This argument also covers \(q=0\), when \(S\) is empty. ∎

Lemma 2: An eligible unmatched type can be removed completely

Suppose \((D,r)\) admits an exhausting sequence, and an active type \(t\) uniquely prefers unmatched status to every residual school outcome. Then an exhausting sequence exists beginning with removal of all \(r_t\) mass of type \(t\).

Proof. Every later residual school outcome is among the school outcomes available at \((D,r)\). Thus, whenever it contains type \(t\), its utility for that type is strictly negative.

No college layer in the exhausting sequence can include \(t\): its included types must strictly prefer its outcome to unmatched status. Therefore all mass of \(t\) is eventually removed through unmatched layers.

Remove all \(r_t\) first. In the original sequence, replace coordinate \(t\) of every unmatched removal vector by zero, omitting any resulting zero unmatched layer. Every college cohort is unchanged, because none included \(t\).

At every remaining layer, its cohort stays feasible, its residual box only shrinks, and its preference conditions remain valid. The modified sequence exhausts the residual problem. ∎

The greedy construction

At each residual state:

  • Choose any eligible college layer, if one exists.
  • Otherwise, choose an eligible unmatched type and remove all its residual mass.
  • Continue until no colleges or mass remain.

On a promised state, some next layer exists. If that layer is unmatched, any positive-mass type in it is eligible for a saturated unmatched removal. Thus the algorithm always has a choice.

Lemmas 1 and 2 show inductively that every chosen layer leaves a promised residual state.

Each iteration removes a college or eliminates an active type. Hence there are at most \(m+\tau\) iterations. Furthermore, at every state, each residual coordinate is either \(\mu_t\) or zero.

Let \(X^*\) be the allocation constructed by this saturated sequence.

3. Every core allocation must equal \(X^*\)

Fix any core allocation \(X\). We prove, in sequence order, that \(X\) must implement every layer of the greedy construction.

The induction invariant is that every previously removed college has its constructed cohort, and every previously removed type has all its mass at its constructed college or unmatched.

Consequently, each remaining college’s cohort in \(X\) lies in the current residual box \(0\leq z\leq r\). All mass of an active type originates at remaining colleges or unmatched status.

College layers are forced

Consider the next college layer \((c,q)\). If its actual cohort \(z_c\) differs from \(q\), unique maximization gives \(v_c(q)>v_c(z_c)\).

For every \(t\) with \(q_t>0\), we have \(q_t=r_t=\mu_t\). Collect all mass of these types from their current origins, retaining any mass already at \(c\). This forms exactly \(q\).

Every positive participant strictly prefers \((c,q)\) to their current outcome: unmatched status is strictly worse, and any current school outcome is an available residual outcome distinct from \((c,q)\). The college also strictly improves. Thus this is an allowed block.

If \(q=0\), the same strict college improvement produces the permitted empty block.

Therefore \(z_c=q\). Every included type is entirely at \(c\), establishing the induction invariant for this layer.

Saturated unmatched layers are forced

Suppose the next layer removes all residual mass of type \(t\). Any remaining-school outcome containing \(t\) gives that type strictly negative utility.

If \(X\) assigned positive mass of \(t\) to a remaining college, that college’s cohort would lie in the residual box and contain \(t\), contradicting student individual rationality. Previously removed colleges have already been fixed and contain no active type.

Hence all mass of \(t\) is unmatched in \(X\).

The induction exhausts all colleges and types. Thus there is at most one core allocation, namely \(X^*\).

4. The constructed allocation is in the core

Individual rationality

Every type assigned by a college layer strictly prefers its assigned outcome to unmatched status, so its utility is positive.

For a college layer \((c,q)\), every subcohort \(0\leq y\leq q\) also lies in that layer’s residual box. Maximization therefore gives \(v_c(q)\geq v_c(y)\). This is exactly college individual rationality.

Absence of blocks

Suppose a proposed block targets college \(c\), whose college layer has index \(k\), and forms cohort \(q\).

We first show that \(q\) cannot contain any type removed before layer \(k\).

Otherwise, choose a type in \(q\) whose removal layer has the smallest index, say \(j<k\). Because no type in \(q\) was removed before \(j\), the entire cohort \(q\) lies in the residual box at layer \(j\). College \(c\) is still present there.

If that type was removed by an unmatched layer, it strictly prefers its current unmatched status to \((c,q)\).

If it was assigned by a college layer, it strictly prefers its current layer outcome to \((c,q)\), since that earlier layer’s college differs from \(c\).

Either case violates the participant comparison required for a block.

Thus \(q\) lies in the residual box at college \(c\)’s own layer. Write \(q^c\) for its constructed cohort. The blocking college comparison requires \(v_c(q)\geq v_c(q^c)\). Unique maximization over that box forces \(q=q^c\).

Every type in \(q^c\) has all its mass already at \(c\). Hence positive participants can only be retained students, and their outcome is unchanged. The college’s utility is also unchanged. No required comparison is strict, so this is not a block.

This argument includes \(q=0\). Therefore \(X^*\) is a core allocation. Together with Section 3, it proves uniqueness.

5. Exact polynomial-time layer tests

It remains to justify that eligibility can be tested without quantifying computationally over a continuum.

Write \(b_{td,s}\) for coordinate \(s\) of \(b_{td}\). For an active type \(t\) and residual college \(d\), define

\[ M_{td}(r)=a_{td}+\sum_{\substack{s\in I\\ b_{td,s}>0}}b_{td,s}r_s. \]

This is the supremum of \(u_t(d,z)\) over \(0\leq z\leq r\), \(z_t>0\).

To verify this, maximizing over the closed box selects the upper endpoint of each positive-coefficient coordinate and zero for each negative-coefficient coordinate. The restricted domain \(z_t>0\) is dense in that box, so it has the same supremum.

The supremum is attained in the restricted domain if and only if \(b_{td,t}\geq0\):

  • If \(b_{td,t}<0\), every closed-box maximizer has \(z_t=0\).
  • If \(b_{td,t}>0\), a maximizer has \(z_t=r_t>0\).
  • If \(b_{td,t}=0\), a maximizing positive value of \(z_t\) can be chosen.

Consequently, for any rational threshold \(H\), all these school outcomes have utility strictly below \(H\) exactly when

\[ M_{td}(r)<H \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(M_{td}(r)=H\ \text{and}\ b_{td,t}<0\bigr). \]

If the supremum exceeds \(H\), some feasible outcome exceeds \(H\); if it equals \(H\), strict inequality everywhere holds precisely when the supremum is unattained.

Testing an unmatched type

An active type \(t\) is eligible exactly when, for every \(d\in D\),

\[ M_{td}(r)<0 \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(M_{td}(r)=0\ \text{and}\ b_{td,t}<0\bigr). \]

When \(D\) is empty, this condition is vacuous.

Testing a college layer

First require \(d_{cs}\neq0\) for all active \(s\), and form the uniquely maximizing endpoint vector \(q^c\) from Section 1.

For every included type \(t\), set \(U_t=u_t(c,q^c)\). Check:

  • \(U_t>0\).
  • For every active coordinate \(s\), the coefficient \(b_{tc,s}\) has the same strict sign as \(d_{cs}\).
  • For every \(d\in D\setminus\{c\}\), check

\[ M_{td}(r)<U_t \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(M_{td}(r)=U_t\ \text{and}\ b_{td,t}<0\bigr). \]

Condition 2 is exactly the requirement that \(q^c\) uniquely maximize this student’s utility among outcomes at \(c\) containing type \(t\). If a coefficient has the wrong sign, moving that coordinate inward improves utility; if it is zero, such a movement gives a distinct tied outcome. When the varied coordinate is \(t\), the movement can be chosen small enough to keep it positive.

Conversely, the stated strict signs make every coordinate movement away from \(q^c\) weakly decrease utility, with a strict decrease for at least one changed coordinate. Hence every distinct feasible outcome at \(c\) is strictly worse.

Conditions 1 and 3 handle unmatched status and the other colleges. Therefore these tests are necessary and sufficient for college-layer eligibility. When \(q^c=0\), the student tests are vacuous.

6. Bit complexity and conclusion

Every residual mass is either its original input value \(\mu_t\) or zero. Each computed utility or supremum is a sum of at most \(\tau\) products of input rationals. These numbers therefore have polynomial bit length in \(\tau,L\), and all comparisons, including equality, can be performed exactly in polynomial time.

Testing all colleges and unmatched types at one state uses a polynomial number of rational operations; a loose bound is \(O(m^2\tau^2)\). There are at most \(m+\tau\) states requiring a choice. The total bit complexity is consequently polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The output entries are all zero or original masses \(\mu_t\). No approximation or real-number oracle is needed.

Thus the stated promise yields a unique, nonempty core and a polynomial-time exact search algorithm.
