2. An exact algorithm polynomial in the numerical horizon

and write $\rho=p/r$ in lowest terms, with $0<p<r$. Define
$$ Q=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}d_t, \qquad w_t=n_t\frac{Q}{d_t}. $$
Then $w_t$ are positive integers and
$$ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}w_t=Q. $$

For each item, compute
$$ N_i=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau} w_t p^{a_t}r^{A-a_t}\sigma_t(i). $$
The identity
$$ N_i=Qr^A B_\rho(i) $$
shows that sorting the integers $N_i$, with increasing labels breaking ties, gives the required ranking exactly.

The integer $Q$ has $O(L)$ bits. Moreover,
$$ 0<B_\rho(i)\leq m, $$
because masses sum to one, $\rho^{a_t}\leq1$, and $\sigma_t(i)\leq m$. Thus scores, positive partial sums, and the necessary integer powers have at most
$$ K=O\bigl((A+1)L+\log(m+1)\bigr) $$
bits.

Repeated squaring requires $O(\log(A+2))$ multiplications for each needed power. Using schoolbook arithmetic gives the valid bound
$$ O\!\left( \bigl[L+\tau\log(A+2)+m\tau+m\log(m+1)\bigr]K^2 \right). $$
This is polynomial in $m,\tau,L,A$. It is polynomial in the binary input length when $A$ is polynomially bounded in that length.

It does not settle arbitrary binary-encoded ages. Indeed, for $N>2$,
$$ F_N(X)=X^N-4X^2+4X-1 $$
satisfies
$$ F_N(1/2)=2^{-N}. $$
For two items, use masses $1/10,4/10,4/10,1/10$ at ages $0,1,2,N$, choosing the ranking at each age so that its contribution to $B_\rho(2)-B_\rho(1)$ has the corresponding coefficient sign. Then
$$ B_{1/2}(2)-B_{1/2}(1)=\frac{2^{-N}}{10}, $$
while the input length is $O(\log N)$.

This invalidates a general argument requiring only polynomial absolute precision. It is not a hardness proof; Section 5 gives a polynomial-time exact algorithm covering this very example.

3. Equivalence with sparse rational sign evaluation

Define sparse rational sign evaluation as follows. Given
$$ F(X)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}b_\ell X^{e_\ell}, $$
where coefficients are integers, exponents are nonnegative integers encoded in binary, and $x\in(0,1)$ is rational, determine whether $F(x)$ is negative, zero, or positive.

Combine repeated exponents and remove zero coefficients first. The empty polynomial has sign zero and requires no oracle query.

Ranking reduces to sign evaluation

For items $i,j$, form
$$ P_{ij}(X)= \sum_{t=1}^{\tau} w_t\bigl(\sigma_t(i)-\sigma_t(j)\bigr)X^{a_t}. $$
Its sparse representation has polynomial encoding length. In fact, after combining equal exponents, every coefficient has absolute value at most $(m-1)Q$.

Since
$$ P_{ij}(\rho)=Q\bigl(B_\rho(i)-B_\rho(j)\bigr), $$
its sign gives an exact score comparison. An identically zero polynomial gives a tie immediately. Comparison sorting uses $O(m\log(m+1))$ sign queries.

Sign evaluation reduces to ranking with two items

For a nonempty polynomial with nonzero coefficients, put
$$ C=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}|b_\ell|. $$
At age $e_\ell$, assign mass $|b_\ell|/C$ to ranking $(1,2)$ if $b_\ell>0$, and to ranking $(2,1)$ if $b_\ell<0$. Set
$$ \rho=x,\qquad A=\max_\ell e_\ell. $$
These masses are positive, sum to one, and have polynomial encoding length. Their score difference is
$$ B_\rho(2)-B_\rho(1)=\frac{F(x)}{C}. $$

Make a second query after reversing every supported ranking. The second score difference is $-F(x)/C$. The two outputs are:

| Sign of $F(x)$ | First output | Second output |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | $(1,2)$ | $(2,1)$ |
| Negative | $(2,1)$ | $(1,2)$ |
| Zero | $(1,2)$ | $(1,2)$ |

The zero row follows from the prescribed label tie-breaker. Hence these two problems are polynomial-time Turing equivalent.

Restricting the evaluation point to $(0,1)$ does not avoid general rational-point sign evaluation. Negative points are handled by replacing $F(X)$ with $F(-X)$. For $x>1$, letting $D=\deg F$ and
$$ G(Y)=Y^D F(1/Y) $$
gives
$$ F(x)=x^D G(1/x), $$
with a positive multiplier and a polynomial-size sparse representation. Evaluation at $0$, $1$, and $-1$ is immediate.

4. New partial result: exact ties are polynomial-time decidable

Although I do not resolve general sign evaluation, equality to zero admits an elementary polynomial-time algorithm.

Lemma

Given a sparse integer polynomial $F$ and a rational $p/r\in(0,1)$ in lowest terms, one can decide whether
$$ F(p/r)=0 $$
in time polynomial in their binary encoding length.

Proof

Handle the empty polynomial immediately. Otherwise factor out the least power of $X$; this preserves vanishing at the nonzero point $p/r$. Thus assume the constant coefficient is nonzero.

Write
$$ F(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{D}b_kX^k, \qquad H=\max_k|b_k|\geq1, $$
where missing coefficients are zero. A nonzero constant polynomial does not vanish.

Consider division by $rX-p$. Set $q_D=0$ and, descending through $k=D,\ldots,1$, use
$$ q_{k-1}=\frac{b_k+p q_k}{r}. $$
These equations give the identity
$$ F(X) = (rX-p)\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}q_kX^k + (b_0+p q_0). $$
Therefore $F(p/r)=0$ exactly when the final remainder is zero.

If $F(p/r)=0$, every $q_k$ in this recurrence is an integer. To prove this without assuming an integer quotient, multiply the root equation by $r^D$ and reduce modulo $r$:
$$ b_Dp^D\equiv0\pmod r. $$
Because $\gcd(p,r)=1$, we obtain $r\mid b_D$. Subtracting
$$ (rX-p)\frac{b_D}{r}X^{D-1} $$
leaves an integer polynomial of smaller degree with the same root. Repeating establishes integrality of every quotient coefficient.

Consequently, a nonintegral division certifies nonvanishing. If all divisions are integral, the final remainder decides vanishing.

The remaining issue is avoiding $D$ steps. Throughout integral divisions,
$$ |q_k|\leq\frac{H}{r-p}\leq H. $$
Indeed, starting from $q_D=0$, the induction step is
$$ |q_{k-1}| \leq \frac{H+pH/(r-p)}r = \frac{H}{r-p}. $$

Across a gap of $g$ zero coefficients, the recurrence sends an integer $z$ to
$$ \left(\frac pr\right)^g z. $$
All divisions across that gap are integral exactly when $r^g\mid z$, because $\gcd(p,r)=1$.

If $z=0$, skip the entire gap. If $z\ne0$, let
$$ B=\left\lceil\log_2(H+1)\right\rceil. $$
For $g>B$, we have $r^g>|z|$, so divisibility is impossible and the algorithm rejects immediately. Otherwise, perform at most $B$ ordinary divisions.

Thus each gap costs either a binary comparison or at most $B$ arithmetic steps. All manipulated integers have polynomial bit length. Processing the sparse coefficients and these gaps is therefore polynomial-time. This proves the lemma.

Consequence for ranking

Let
$$ S=L+\left\lceil\log_2(m+1)\right\rceil+1. $$
For each comparison polynomial $P_{ij}$, coefficient bit lengths are $O(S)$, and it has at most $\tau$ terms.

After sorting support entries by age and constructing the common-denominator weights, the preceding algorithm detects a particular score tie in
$$ O(\tau S^3) $$
bit operations with schoolbook arithmetic. All pairwise score ties can therefore be detected in polynomial time, with the conservative bound
$$ O\!\left( [L+m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1)]S^2 + m^2\tau S^3 \right). $$

This separates exact tie detection from the unresolved task of ordering unequal scores.

5. New partial result: polynomial-time ranking for reciprocal-integer discounts

Suppose
$$ \rho=\frac1r,\qquad r\geq2 $$
is an integer, itself binary encoded. Then unrestricted ranking is polynomial-time solvable.

It suffices to determine the sign of a sparse integer polynomial at $1/r$. Write
$$ F(X)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}b_\ell X^{e_\ell}, \qquad e_1<\cdots<e_t=D, $$
after removing zero coefficients. Define
$$ G(Y)=Y^D F(1/Y). $$
Because $r^D>0$,
$$ \operatorname{sgn}F(1/r)=\operatorname{sgn}G(r). $$

Sparse Horner evaluation starts at $s=b_1$ and applies
$$ s\longmapsto r^{e_{\ell+1}-e_\ell}s+b_{\ell+1}. $$
The final accumulator equals $G(r)$.

Put
$$ H=\max_\ell|b_\ell|. $$
If at any stage $|s|>H$, every later step preserves its sign and keeps its magnitude above $H$. Indeed, every exponent gap is at least one, so
$$ |r^g s+b| \geq2|s|-H>|s|>H, $$
and $|r^gs|>|b|$. The algorithm can stop immediately.

When $s=0$, the next accumulator is simply the next coefficient, regardless of the gap. Otherwise $1\leq|s|\leq H$. Set
$$ T=2H+1. $$
Compute only
$$ v=\min(T,r^g). $$
Repeated squaring with every product capped at $T$ computes this exactly in $O(\log(g+1))$ operations on integers of $O(\log(H+1))$ bits. This follows from
$$ \min(T,uv) = \min\bigl(T,\min(T,u)\min(T,v)\bigr) $$
for positive integers $u,v$.

If $v=T$, then
$$ |r^g s+b|\geq T-H=H+1, $$
and its sign is the sign of $s$. Stop. Otherwise $v=r^g$ exactly, and compute $vs+b$. This has $O(\log(H+1))$ bits; either its magnitude exceeds $H$, allowing termination, or it remains a bounded accumulator.

The empty polynomial returns zero. All other cases return the exact sign, including cancellation to zero.

For ranking, coefficient construction followed by comparison sorting gives the valid bound
$$ O\!\left( \bigl[ L+m\tau+\tau\log(\tau+1) + m\log(m+1)\,\tau\log(A+2) \bigr]S^2 \right). $$
Only $\log(A+2)$ appears. Thus this is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ even when $A$ is binary encoded and numerically enormous.

6. Strict ordering of Mallows expected positions

For a mode $\pi$, define
$$ \Pr_\pi(\sigma)=\frac{q^{d_K(\sigma,\pi)}}{Z_m(q)}, \qquad 0<q<1. $$
Relabeling each item by its position in $\pi$ preserves Kendall distance and bijects the rankings. Hence there are numbers $e_1,\ldots,e_m$, depending only on $m,q$, such that
$$ \mathbb E_\pi[\sigma(i)]=e_{\pi(i)}. $$

I prove
$$ e_1<e_2<\cdots<e_m. $$

Use the identity mode and fix adjacent modal labels $k,k+1$. Pair each ranking $\sigma$ with $\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)$ with the ranking $T\sigma$ obtained by exchanging those item labels.

Their mutual pair changes from agreement to disagreement. Every third modal label lies before both or after both, so exchanging their positions preserves its total disagreement count with the two labels. Therefore
$$ d_K(T\sigma,\mathrm{id}) = d_K(\sigma,\mathrm{id})+1. $$
Their position differences have opposite signs. Summing the contributions of these pairs gives
$$ e_{k+1}-e_k = \frac{1-q}{Z_m(q)} \sum_{\sigma:\,\sigma(k)<\sigma(k+1)} q^{d_K(\sigma,\mathrm{id})} \bigl(\sigma(k+1)-\sigma(k)\bigr). $$
Every summand is positive, the summation set is nonempty, and $1-q>0$. Thus $e_{k+1}-e_k>0$, as required.

7. Exact Mallows recovery, unequal masses, and ties

Let $x,y$ be the swapped items, listed in their current modal order:
$$ \pi_{\mathrm{cur}}(x)=k, \qquad \pi_{\mathrm{cur}}(y)=k+1. $$
Define
$$ W_{\mathrm{cur}}=\sum_{a=0}^{h-1}c_a\rho^a, \qquad W_{\mathrm{old}}=\sum_{a=h}^{A}c_a\rho^a, $$
with an empty sum interpreted as zero.

The promise and Section 6 imply
$$ B_\rho(x)=W_{\mathrm{cur}}e_k+W_{\mathrm{old}}e_{k+1}, $$
$$ B_\rho(y)=W_{\mathrm{cur}}e_{k+1}+W_{\mathrm{old}}e_k. $$
Consequently,
$$ B_\rho(y)-B_\rho(x) = (W_{\mathrm{cur}}-W_{\mathrm{old}}) (e_{k+1}-e_k). $$
The second factor is strictly positive.

To verify the entire ranking, put
$$ W=W_{\mathrm{cur}}+W_{\mathrm{old}}>0. $$
Every unchanged item at modal position $j$ has score $We_j$. Both swapped scores belong to
$$ [We_k,We_{k+1}]. $$
Thus every item before the pair has strictly smaller score, every item after it has strictly larger score, and unchanged items retain their strict mutual order.

Therefore the complete criterion is
$$ \boxed{ \text{Recovery} \iff W_{\mathrm{cur}}>W_{\mathrm{old}} \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(W_{\mathrm{cur}}=W_{\mathrm{old}}\text{ and }x<y\bigr). } $$

More precisely:

  • If $W_{\mathrm{cur}}>W_{\mathrm{old}}$, the output is $\pi_{\mathrm{cur}}$.
  • If $W_{\mathrm{cur}}<W_{\mathrm{old}}$, the output is $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$.
  • At equality, exactly the swapped pair ties; increasing labels determine its order.

The criterion is independent of $q$ throughout $0<q<1$.

For an explicit tie, take
$$ A=1,\quad h=1,\quad \rho=\frac12,\quad c_0=\frac13,\quad c_1=\frac23. $$
Then
$$ W_{\mathrm{cur}}=W_{\mathrm{old}}=\frac13. $$
Any rational $q\in(0,1)$ gives rational Mallows masses, and recovery holds exactly when $x<y$.

When $h\leq A$, there is no threshold depending only on $h,\rho,A$ that guarantees recovery for all positive unequal cohort masses. To see this, distribute total recent mass $\varepsilon$ and total old mass $1-\varepsilon$ positively within the respective groups. Then
$$ W_{\mathrm{cur}}\leq\varepsilon, \qquad W_{\mathrm{old}}\geq\rho^A(1-\varepsilon). $$
A positive rational
$$ \varepsilon<\frac{\rho^A}{1+\rho^A} $$
forces failure.

Conversely, assigning total recent mass $1-\varepsilon$ and old mass $\varepsilon$ gives
$$ W_{\mathrm{cur}}\geq\rho^{h-1}(1-\varepsilon), \qquad W_{\mathrm{old}}\leq\varepsilon. $$
A positive rational
$$ \varepsilon<\frac{\rho^{h-1}}{1+\rho^{h-1}} $$
forces recovery. Uniform allocations within each group ensure every cohort mass is positive.

If $h=A+1$, the old sum is empty and recovery always occurs.

8. Equal cohort masses and the infinite-stream boundary

Suppose
$$ c_a=\frac1{A+1} $$
for every age. Geometric summation gives
$$ W_{\mathrm{cur}}-W_{\mathrm{old}} = \frac{1-2\rho^h+\rho^{A+1}}{(A+1)(1-\rho)}. $$
Thus strict recovery is equivalent to
$$ \rho^h<\frac{1+\rho^{A+1}}2, $$
or equivalently,
$$ h>\log_\rho\!\left(\frac{1+\rho^{A+1}}2\right). $$
The logarithmic inequality reverses direction because $0<\rho<1$.

For rational $\rho$, equality cannot occur at a finite horizon. It would make $\rho$ a root of
$$ H(X)= \sum_{a=0}^{h-1}X^a-\sum_{a=h}^{A}X^a. $$
If nonconstant, this integer polynomial has constant coefficient $1$ and leading coefficient $1$ or $-1$. The rational-root theorem permits only $1$ and $-1$ as rational roots. If constant, it equals $1$. Neither case allows a root in $(0,1)$.

For the infinite-stream comparison, use one unit of cohort weight at every age, discounted by $\rho^a$. These weights have finite sum $1/(1-\rho)$, and multiplying all scores by $1-\rho$ would normalize them without changing the ranking. The recent-minus-old weight is
$$ \sum_{a=0}^{h-1}\rho^a-\sum_{a=h}^{\infty}\rho^a = \frac{1-2\rho^h}{1-\rho}. $$
Hence strict recovery occurs exactly when
$$ \rho^h<\frac12, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad h>\log_\rho(1/2), $$
matching the expected-score threshold in the supplied [streaming source](https://arxiv.org/html/1910.08795v3). At equality, recovery follows the label criterion.

This is convergence of the cutoff, not necessarily eventual agreement of rankings at the boundary. When $\rho^h=1/2$, every finite equal-mass horizon has a positive numerator $\rho^{A+1}$ and therefore strict recovery, while the infinite stream has a tie. The normalized finite scores themselves tend to zero; rescaling them by $A+1$ gives the unit-cohort convention just used.

9. Polynomial bit complexity under the explicit Mallows promise

Every promised type has positive mass:
$$ \mu_{a,\sigma} = \frac{c_a q^{d_K(\sigma,\pi_a)}}{Z_m(q)}>0. $$
There are $A+1$ positive cohorts, and every cohort supports all $m!$ rankings. Consequently,
$$ \tau=(A+1)m!, \qquad A+1\leq\tau. $$
The horizon-dependent algorithm of Section 2 is therefore polynomial in the explicit promised input length.

Recovery can be decided directly, without computing Mallows expectations or the normalizer. Using the common denominator $Q$, form
$$ C_a=Qc_a=\sum_{t:\,a_t=a}w_t. $$
Compute
$$ U_{\mathrm{cur}} = \sum_{a=0}^{h-1}C_ap^ar^{A-a}, \qquad U_{\mathrm{old}} = \sum_{a=h}^{A}C_ap^ar^{A-a}. $$
They satisfy
$$ U_{\mathrm{cur}}=Qr^A W_{\mathrm{cur}}, \qquad U_{\mathrm{old}}=Qr^A W_{\mathrm{old}}. $$
Compare these integers, applying the label rule at equality.

The age weights can be generated in $O(A+1)$ arithmetic operations: start with $r^A$, and obtain the next weight by dividing by $r$ and multiplying by $p$. The division is exact whenever another age remains.

With the earlier bit bound $K$, a valid recovery running time is
$$ O\!\left( (L+m+\tau+A+1)K^2 \right). $$
The recent and old ranges contain respectively $h$ and $A+1-h$ cohorts, so their combined processing cost is $O(A+1)$. Since $A+1\leq\tau$, the bound is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, and hence in the explicit promised input length. The promise is assumed rather than verified.

The remaining unproved classification concerns unrestricted rational discounts with reduced numerator greater than one and arbitrary binary-encoded ages. Neither a general polynomial-time algorithm nor an $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness proof is established here.
