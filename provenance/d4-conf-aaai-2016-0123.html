Proposition 1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), initial influenced and mapped masses, rational type-level kernels defining cascade transmission and teaching events, a horizon \(H\), intervention budget \(\beta\), and weight \(\lambda\), choose feasible intervention masses \(x_t^r\) to maximize \(\lambda I_H+(1-\lambda)M_H\), where \(I_H\) and \(M_H\) are the resulting expected influenced and mapped masses. For input accuracy \(\epsilon\), find a schedule within \(\epsilon\) of the optimum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Use the following homogeneous Poisson mass dynamics. Inputs are nonnegative rational transmission and teaching intensities \(P_{tu},Q_{tu}\), a unary horizon \(H\ge1\), rational budget \(0<\beta\le1\), weight \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), and accuracy \(0<\epsilon<1\). Let \(m\) count ordered pairs having a nonzero intensity, and \(L\) be the total bit length of the rational input, including \(\epsilon\).
For each type, maintain unselected masses \(U_{t,b,c}\), where \(b,c\in\{0,1\}\) indicate influenced and mapped status, and selected mass \(s_t\), which is always influenced and mapped. Initially \(s_t=0\); the four initial joint masses are supplied as nonnegative binary rationals summing to \(\mu_t\). Initial influence has already finished spreading.
In round \(r\), choose nonnegative intervention masses \(x_{t,b}^r\le U_{t,b,1}\), set \(x_t^r=x_{t,0}^r+x_{t,1}^r\), and require \(\sum_{r,t}x_t^r\le\beta\). Remove these masses from their compartments and add them to \(s_t\). Previously selected mass cannot be selected again; influenced but unselected mapped mass remains eligible.
Teaching then transfers the fraction \(1-\exp(-\sum_tQ_{tu}x_t^r)\) of every \(U_{u,b,0}\) to \(U_{u,b,1}\). Mapping means discovering individuals, and persists; it does not itself cause influence.
Complete a cascade before the next intervention. Start its active cohort at \(a_t=x_{t,0}^r\). In each wave, transfer the fraction \(f_u=1-\exp(-\sum_tP_{tu}a_t)\) of every \(U_{u,0,c}\) to \(U_{u,1,c}\); the transferred mass, summed over \(c\), is the next \(a_u\). Use each cohort once and take the limit of the wave updates. Influence persists and may reach unmapped mass.
All kernels are supplied to the planner; mapping restricts intervention eligibility. A schedule specifies rational intervention masses for these deterministic expected-mass dynamics. Define \(I_H=\sum_t(s_t+\sum_cU_{t,1,c})\), \(M_H=\sum_t(s_t+\sum_bU_{t,b,1})\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) as the supremum of \(\lambda I_H+(1-\lambda)M_H\) over feasible schedules.

Is finding a feasible schedule with value at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,H,L\), or is this computational problem NP-hard?

Discrete simultaneous influencing and mapping is NP-hard, including the fully mapped, empty-teaching-list case; the source labels it NP-complete but its reduction establishes hardness only. [Source](https://amulyayadav.com/Papers/HIAI16.pdf)
Discount-based continuous influence maximization on a finite graph is also NP-hard; its controls change individual seed probabilities rather than population masses. No result settling the mass dynamics specified here was located. [Source](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~jpei/publications/CIM%20SIGMOD16.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The continuum transmission and teaching laws were unspecified. Chose homogeneous Poisson exposure with nonnegative rational intensities and explicitly defined deterministic expected-mass updates; these laws are modeling choices, not identities for finite independent cascades.
  • Normalized total population mass to one and measured both objectives and additive error in that normalization.
  • Initial influenced and mapped marginals do not specify their overlap. Supplied the four joint status masses per type as input, allowing arbitrary overlap.
  • Interventions select only currently mapped mass, with separate amounts for influenced and uninfluenced individuals, so already influenced individuals can still teach.
  • Excluded repeated selection of the same individuals, preserving the fixed teaching-list interpretation rather than allowing repeated fresh teaching opportunities.
  • Interpreted the horizon as intervention rounds, with each cascade completed before the next round and initial influence already exhausted.
  • Used unit cost per selected mass and a total budget across rounds; allowed unused budget and both endpoint objective weights.
  • Treated the supplied kernels as available to the planner and mapping as an eligibility constraint; no prior over unknown kernels was supplied.
  • Chose rational schedules, binary accuracy, and a unary horizon; polynomial time includes the full precision bit length rather than inverse accuracy.
  • Used a supremum to define the optimum without assuming the completed-cascade dynamics attain a maximum.
  • Defined m as the number of ordered type pairs with a nonzero transmission or teaching intensity.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\), even with \(\ H=1\), \(\lambda=1\), no teaching, full initial mapping, and no initial influence.

The proof gives a polynomial reduction from Independent Set. It controls the complete cascade uniformly and explicitly rules out an advantage from fractional intervention masses.

1. A quadratic rounding lemma

Let \(G=([n],E)\) be a simple undirected graph, let \(1\le k\le n\) be an integer, and let \(d\ge2\). Define

\(\displaystyle F(y)=d\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i-2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}y_i y_j,\qquad 0\le y_i\le1,\quad \sum_i y_i\le k.\)

Lemma. Every feasible \(y\) can be replaced by a feasible vector in \(\{0,1\}^{n}\) without decreasing \(F\).

Proof. Suppose two coordinates \(y_i,y_j\) are strictly between zero and one. Vary them as \(y_i+z,y_j-z\) over the maximal interval preserving their bounds. This preserves their sum and hence feasibility.

Along this interval, every term of \(F\) is affine in \(z\), except possibly the term corresponding to \(\{i,j\}\). If that edge exists, its contribution is

\(\displaystyle -2(y_i+z)(y_j-z)=-2y_i y_j+2(y_i-y_j)z+2z^2.\)

Thus \(F\) is convex along the interval. At least one endpoint has value at least its value at \(z=0\). Moving to that endpoint makes at least one of these coordinates integral.

Repeating leaves at most one fractional coordinate. If the other coordinates contain \(\ell\) ones and the remaining coordinate is fractional, feasibility and integrality of \(k\) imply \(\ell\le k-1\). Both rounding choices are therefore feasible. Since \(F\) is affine in that coordinate, one choice does not decrease \(F\). This proves the lemma.

For a set \(S\subseteq[n]\), write \(e(S)\) for the number of edges with both endpoints in \(S\). Then

\(\displaystyle F(\mathbf 1_S)=d|S|-2e(S).\)

Consequently:

  • If \(G\) has an independent set of size \(k\), the maximum of \(F\) is \(dk\).
  • If it has no such independent set, every feasible \(y\) satisfies \(F(y)\le dk-2\).

For the second assertion, apply the lemma. A rounded set of size \(k\) has at least one internal edge. A rounded set of size at most \(k-1\) has value at most \(d(k-1)\le dk-2\). The first assertion follows from an independent-set witness and the universal bound \(F(y)\le d\sum_i y_i\le dk\).

2. Constructing the society

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Independent Set problem: given a simple undirected graph and an integer \(1\le k\le n\), decide whether it has an independent set of size at least \(k\). Its hardness follows from the classical Clique theorem by taking graph complements: a set is independent in a graph exactly when it is a clique in its complement. [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Given such an instance, let \(d_i\) denote vertex \(i\)'s degree and set

\(\displaystyle d=\max\{2,\max_i d_i\}.\)

Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two at least \(n+2\), and let \(D\) be the smallest power of two at least \(d\). Define

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac1N,\qquad \theta=\frac{\eta^2}{32D^2},\qquad \Delta=\theta\eta^2.\)

There are \(n+2\) types: the vertices \(1,\ldots,n\), an auxiliary type \(z\), and a filler type \(w\). Their masses are

\(\displaystyle \mu_i=\mu_z=\eta,\qquad \mu_w=1-(n+1)\eta.\)

Every mass is positive, and their sum is one. Initially everyone is mapped and uninfluenced:

\(\displaystyle U_{t,0,1}=\mu_t,\qquad U_{t,0,0}=U_{t,1,0}=U_{t,1,1}=0.\)

Set

\(\displaystyle H=1,\qquad \lambda=1,\qquad Q_{tu}=0,\qquad \beta=k\eta,\qquad \epsilon=\frac{\Delta}{4}.\)

For every edge \(\{i,j\}\in E\), set \(P_{ij}=P_{ji}=\theta\). Additionally, set

\(\displaystyle P_{iz}=\theta(d-d_i)\)

for each vertex \(i\). All other transmission intensities are zero.

Thus each vertex has total outgoing intensity \(\theta d\), and both auxiliary types have no outgoing transmission. All input quantities are dyadic rationals.

Since there is only one intervention round and no initial influence, write \(x_t=x_{t,0}^{1}\). Feasibility is exactly

\(\displaystyle 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\qquad T:=\sum_t x_t\le\beta.\)

The objective is the final influenced mass, denoted \(I(x)\).

3. A rational surrogate for the complete cascade

Define the nonnegative integer matrix \(W=P/\theta\). Its vertex rows sum to \(d\); its other rows are zero. Every type receiving transmission has mass \(\eta\).

Immediately after intervention, let

\(\displaystyle S_u=\mu_u-x_u,\qquad h_u=\theta\sum_tW_{tu}x_t.\)

The first cascade wave influences mass \(S_u(1-e^{-h_u})\) at type \(u\). Define its linear approximation and the resulting surrogate by

\(\displaystyle A(x)=\sum_u S_u h_u,\qquad J(x)=T+A(x).\)

Writing \(X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\), direct expansion gives

\(\displaystyle A(x)=\theta\left(d\eta X-2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}x_i x_j-x_z\sum_i(d-d_i)x_i\right).\)

Indeed, the graph edges contribute

\(\displaystyle \theta\sum_i d_i\eta x_i-2\theta\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}x_i x_j,\)

and transmission into \(z\) contributes

\(\displaystyle \theta(\eta-x_z)\sum_i(d-d_i)x_i.\)

Their sum is the stated expression. In particular, \(J(x)\) is an exactly computable rational number whenever the intervention masses are rational.

We next prove the uniform bound

\(\displaystyle |I(x)-J(x)|\le\frac{\Delta}{16}\)

for every feasible intervention.

Bounding all later waves

Let \(a^{(0)}=x\), and let \(a^{(\ell)}\) be the newly influenced cohort in wave \(\ell\), for \(\ell\ge1\). Put

\(\displaystyle \rho=\theta\eta d.\)

For any wave, susceptible masses are at most their initial masses. Using \(1-e^{-v}\le v\) for \(v\ge0\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle \|a^{(\ell+1)}\|_1\le\theta\sum_t a_t^{(\ell)}\sum_u\mu_uW_{tu}\le\rho\|a^{(\ell)}\|_1.\)

The last inequality holds because each vertex row has weighted sum \(\eta d\), and the auxiliary rows are zero. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \rho=\frac{\eta^3d}{32D^2}\le\frac1{32d}\le\frac1{64}<\frac12.\)

Induction therefore gives \(\|a^{(\ell)}\|_1\le\rho^\ell T\). In particular, the complete cascade converges, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\ell\ge2}\|a^{(\ell)}\|_1\le\frac{\rho^2T}{1-\rho}\le2\theta^2\eta^2d^2T\le2\theta^2d^2.\)

Here \(T\le1\) and \(\eta\le1\). Since each cohort consists of newly influenced mass,

\(\displaystyle I(x)=T+\sum_{\ell\ge1}\|a^{(\ell)}\|_1.\)

Bounding the first-wave approximation error

For \(h\ge0\),

\(\displaystyle 0\le h-(1-e^{-h})=\int_0^h(1-e^{-v})\,dv\le\frac{h^2}{2}.\)

Every entry of \(W\) is at most \(d\), so \(h_u\le\theta dT\). Also \(A(x)\le\rho T\), by the same weighted-row calculation used above. Hence

\(\displaystyle 0\le A(x)-\|a^{(1)}\|_1\le\frac12\sum_uS_uh_u^2\le\frac{\theta dT}{2}A(x)\le\frac{\theta^2\eta d^2T^2}{2}\le\frac{\theta^2d^2}{2}.\)

Combining the first-wave error with the later-wave bound yields

\(\displaystyle -\frac{\theta^2d^2}{2}\le I(x)-J(x)\le2\theta^2d^2.\)

Finally,

\(\displaystyle 2\theta^2d^2=\Delta\frac{d^2}{16D^2}\le\frac{\Delta}{16},\)

which proves the claimed uniform bound.

4. The independent-set gap survives fractional interventions

For each vertex, set \(y_i=x_i/\eta\). Every feasible intervention satisfies

\(\displaystyle 0\le y_i\le1,\qquad \sum_i y_i=\frac X\eta\le k.\)

The term involving \(x_z\) in \(A(x)\) is nonpositive. Therefore

\(\displaystyle J(x)\le T+\Delta F(y).\)

Define the rational reference value

\(\displaystyle V_0=\beta+\Delta dk.\)

Yes instance. Suppose \(G\) has an independent set \(S\) of size \(k\). Select the whole mass \(\eta\) of each type in \(S\), and select no other mass. This is a rational feasible schedule. Its total selected mass is \(\beta\), its internal-edge term vanishes, and \(x_z=0\). Thus

\(\displaystyle J(x)=V_0,\qquad I(x)\ge V_0-\frac{\Delta}{16}.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\ge V_0-\frac{\Delta}{16}.\)

No instance. Suppose \(G\) has no independent set of size \(k\). The rounding lemma gives \(F(y)\le dk-2\) for every feasible intervention, including every fractional one. Hence

\(\displaystyle J(x)\le\beta+\Delta(dk-2)=V_0-2\Delta.\)

The cascade bound also implies

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\le V_0-\frac{31\Delta}{16}.\)

These bounds concern the supremum over rational schedules directly: the yes bound uses a rational witness, and the no bound holds for every feasible schedule.

5. Recovering the decision from an approximate schedule

Suppose an algorithm returns a rational feasible schedule \(x\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle I(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon.\)

On a yes instance, because \(\epsilon=\Delta/4\),

\(\displaystyle I(x)\ge V_0-\frac{5\Delta}{16},\)

and therefore

\(\displaystyle J(x)\ge I(x)-\frac{\Delta}{16}\ge V_0-\frac{3\Delta}{8}>V_0-\Delta.\)

On a no instance, every feasible output satisfies

\(\displaystyle J(x)\le V_0-2\Delta<V_0-\Delta.\)

Thus a single call to the proposed approximation algorithm, followed by the exact rational comparison

\(\displaystyle J(x)>V_0-\Delta,\)

decides Independent Set. This comparison requires neither exponential evaluation nor numerical simulation of the cascade.

6. Encoding size and conclusion

The constructed society has exactly \(\tau=n+2\) positive-mass types and at most \(m=2|E|+n\) nonzero ordered transmission pairs. Its horizon is the unary constant \(H=1\).

Both \(N\) and \(D\) are polynomially bounded in \(n\). Every rational input has \(O(\log n)\) bits, including

\(\displaystyle \epsilon=\frac1{128D^2N^4}.\)

Hence the entire instance has polynomial encoding length. Any algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,H,L\) also produces a polynomial-length output; evaluating \(J\) and performing the threshold comparison then takes polynomial time.

Such an algorithm would decide an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem in polynomial time. Therefore the requested additive-approximation search problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\), and is not polynomial-time solvable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

1. Quadratic rounding lemma

The lemma is valid on the stated domain.

  • Changing two fractional coordinates by opposite amounts preserves their sum and the budget constraint.
  • Terms involving just one of these coordinates are affine in the change parameter. The term involving both coordinates, when their edge exists, has quadratic coefficient \(2\), so the restriction of \(F\) to the interval is convex.
  • A convex function on this closed interval has an endpoint whose value is at least the value at the original point. At an endpoint, at least one of the two coordinates becomes integral. Thus the procedure terminates with at most one fractional coordinate.
  • If one fractional coordinate remains alongside \(\ell\) coordinates equal to one, then \(\ell\le k-1\). Both endpoint choices for that coordinate are feasible, and affinity ensures that one does not decrease the objective.

The resulting independent-set gap is also correct. A rounded set of size \(k\) in a no instance has at least one internal edge, giving value at most \(dk-2\). A smaller rounded set has value at most \(d(k-1)\le dk-2\), using precisely the hypothesis \(d\ge2\). In a yes instance, an independent set of size \(k\) attains the universal upper bound \(dk\).
