The continuous mirror question, as graded

Input: a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), compatible unordered type pairs, and for each type \(i\) a complete weak ranking \(\succeq_i\) of compatible partner types and unmatched, with compatible partners preferred to unmatched. Decide whether a symmetric assignment \(y_{ij}=y_{ji}\geq 0\) exists, with \(y_{ij}=0\) for incompatible pairs and \(\sum_{j\in T}y_{ij}\leq\mu_i\); \(y_{ii}\) counts endpoints of type \(i\) paired within their type. Let \(u_i=\mu_i-\sum_j y_{ij}\), \(W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}y_{ik}\), and \(S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}y_{ik}\). Require that no compatible pair \(\{i,j\}\) satisfies \(W_i(j)>0\), \(W_j(i)>0\), and \(S_i(j)>0\) or \(S_j(i)>0\); for \(i=j\), positive mass represents distinct nonatomic members.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Input also specifies a set \(C\) of compatible unordered type pairs, allowing within-type pairs, and complete transitive weak rankings \(\succeq_i\) of compatible partner types and unmatched. Every compatible partner is strictly preferred to unmatched; ties between partners are allowed. Write \(\succ_i\) for strict preference and \(m=|C|\).
An assignment consists of nonnegative real masses \(y_{ij}=y_{ji}\), zero on incompatible pairs, with \(\sum_{j\in T}y_{ij}\leq\mu_i\). For distinct types, \(y_{ij}\) is the mass of pairs between them; \(y_{ii}\) counts endpoints paired within type \(i\), so the mass of such pairs is \(y_{ii}/2\).
Define \(u_i=\mu_i-\sum_{j\in T}y_{ij}\), \(W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}y_{ik}\), and \(S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}y_{ik}\), with preference sums restricted to compatible partner types.
Call the assignment strongly stable if no \(\{i,j\}\in C\) satisfies \(W_i(j)>0\), \(W_j(i)>0\), and \((S_i(j)>0\lor S_j(i)>0)\). For within-type pairs, positive mass supplies distinct nonatomic members; pairing oneself is excluded.

Is deciding whether such a strongly stable assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete strongly stable roommates existence with ties and incomplete lists admits a polynomial-time algorithm using linear programming ([Kamiyama](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16385)); a combinatorial algorithm runs in \(O(ne)\) time for \(n\) agents and \(e\) acceptable pairs ([Kunysz](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESA.2016.60)).
No result settling this exact normalized, nonatomic mass problem with the stated blocking condition was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Removed zero-mass types so the supplied type list is exactly the positive-mass support.
  • Defined m as the number of compatible unordered type pairs and L as the total input bit length.
  • Restricted preference sums to compatible partner types, since incompatible types have no defined ranking.
  • Interpreted the blocking condition as requiring both weak-improvement masses to be positive and at least one strict-improvement mass to be positive.
  • Made complete weak rankings explicitly transitive and required every compatible partner to be strictly preferred to unmatched.
  • Allowed within-type compatibility between distinct nonatomic members and made the endpoint convention explicit: y_ii/2 is the mass of within-type pairs.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). More precisely, existence can be decided using \(\mathop{O}(m+1)\) rational linear programs, each of size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). When an assignment exists, the algorithm produces one with polynomial encoding length.

The proof uses a capacity-normalized LP and successive restrictions of its permitted preference classes. LP restrictions also appear in [Kamiyama’s discrete algorithm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16385); the properties needed for the present mass model are proved below, including the treatment of within-type pairs.

1. Strong stability forces a single outcome class per type

Call the positive partners of type \(i\) *flat* if they are all indifferent under \(\succeq_i\).

Lemma 1. In any strongly stable assignment, each type is either entirely unmatched, or entirely matched with flat positive partners.

Proof. Suppose \(y_{ij}>0\). Then \(W_i(j)>0\) and \(W_j(i)>0\), including when \(i=j\). Strong stability therefore implies \(S_i(j)=S_j(i)=0\).

In particular, \(u_i=0\). Moreover, if \(y_{ik}>0\) and \(j\succ_i k\), then \(S_i(j)\geq y_{ik}>0\), a contradiction. Completeness of the weak ranking consequently makes all positive partners of \(i\) indifferent. If \(i\) has no positive partner, it is entirely unmatched. \(\square\)

For an assignment having this structure, let \(r_i\) denote its partner indifference class, or unmatched if the type is entirely unmatched. Regard unmatched as strictly below every compatible partner.

Because \(\mu_i>0\), its blocking condition becomes exactly:

  • both endpoints weakly prefer the proposed partner to their respective \(r_i\);
  • at least one endpoint strictly prefers the proposed partner.

This equivalence follows directly from the definitions of \(W_i\) and \(S_i\): their positive summands are entirely in the class \(r_i\), or entirely unmatched.

2. A compact linear relaxation

Use one variable \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\) for each pair in \(C\), with the original endpoint convention for \(x_{ii}\). All sums below are over compatible partner types. Define

\[ \]
\(d_i(x)=\sum_j x_{ij},\qquad F_i^x(j)=\frac{1}{\mu_i}\sum_{k:\,k\succeq_i j}x_{ik},\qquad G_i^x(j)=\frac{1}{\mu_i}\sum_{k:\,k\succ_i j}x_{ik}.\)

Let \(\mathcal P\) be the polytope defined by nonnegativity, the capacity inequalities \(d_i(x)\leq\mu_i\), and, for every \(\{i,j\}\in C\),

\(F_i^x(j)+G_j^x(i)\geq1,\qquad G_i^x(j)+F_j^x(i)\geq1.\)

When \(i=j\), these two inequalities coincide. There are \(m\) variables and at most \(\tau+2m\) inequalities besides nonnegativity.

Lemma 2.

  • Every strongly stable assignment belongs to \(\mathcal P\).
  • Every element of \(\mathcal P\) whose positive partners are flat at every participating type is strongly stable.

Proof of necessity. By Lemma 1, a strongly stable assignment has one outcome \(r_i\) per type. Consequently,

\(F_i^x(j)=1\) exactly when \(r_i\succeq_i j\), and \(G_i^x(j)=1\) exactly when \(r_i\succ_i j\);

otherwise these quantities are zero.

Failure of \(F_i^x(j)+G_j^x(i)\geq1\) would mean that type \(i\) strictly prefers \(j\) to its outcome, while type \(j\) weakly prefers \(i\) to its outcome. That is a blocking pair. The other inequality handles the opposite orientation. This reasoning also applies to a within-type pair.

Sufficiency follows from the same argument once the saturation property proved next is available. \(\square\)

3. Saturation and complementary identities

The following identity is the main reason the normalization by \(\mu_i\) works.

Let \(E^\to\) contain both orientations of each distinct-type compatible pair and one orientation of each within-type pair. For \(x,z\in\mathcal P\), put

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x,z)=\frac12\sum_{(i,j)\in E^\to} z_{ij}\bigl(F_i^x(j)+G_j^x(i)-1\bigr). \)

Every summand is nonnegative, so \(\Phi(x,z)\geq0\). Symmetry of \(z\) allows the second preference term to be reindexed:

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x,z)=\frac12\sum_i\sum_j z_{ij}\bigl(F_i^x(j)+G_i^x(j)-1\bigr). \)

For any two partner types \(j,k\), completeness and transitivity give

\(\mathbf1_{k\succeq_i j}+\mathbf1_{k\succ_i j} +\mathbf1_{j\succeq_i k}+\mathbf1_{j\succ_i k}=2.\)

Indeed, for tied partners the four terms are \(1,0,1,0\); for strictly ordered partners they are \(1,1,0,0\), in one order or the other. Expanding the preference sums therefore gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_j z_{ij}\bigl(F_i^x(j)+G_i^x(j)\bigr) +\sum_j x_{ij}\bigl(F_i^z(j)+G_i^z(j)\bigr) =\frac{2d_i(x)d_i(z)}{\mu_i}. \)

It follows that

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x,z)+\Phi(z,x) =\sum_i\left( \frac{d_i(x)d_i(z)}{\mu_i} -\frac{d_i(x)+d_i(z)}2 \right). \)

Each term on the right is nonpositive, since it equals

\(\displaystyle -\frac{ d_i(x)(\mu_i-d_i(z)) +d_i(z)(\mu_i-d_i(x)) }{2\mu_i}. \)

The left side is nonnegative. Hence both sides are zero, every displayed nonpositive term is zero, and \(\Phi(x,z)=\Phi(z,x)=0\).

We obtain two consequences.

Lemma 3. For every \(x,z\in\mathcal P\):

  • At each type \(i\), either \(d_i(x)=d_i(z)=0\), or \(d_i(x)=d_i(z)=\mu_i\).
  • If \(z_{ij}>0\), then

\(F_i^x(j)+G_j^x(i)=1,\qquad G_i^x(j)+F_j^x(i)=1.\)

Proof. For the first assertion, equality in the nonpositive term above implies that both products in its numerator vanish. If one degree is positive, the other must equal \(\mu_i\), which then forces the first to equal \(\mu_i\). If one degree is zero, the other must also be zero.

For the second assertion, \(\Phi(x,z)=0\) is a sum of nonnegative terms. Every term with positive weight must vanish. For a distinct-type pair, use both orientations; for a within-type pair, the two asserted equalities coincide. \(\square\)

Thus, whenever \(\mathcal P\) is nonempty, all its elements share the same set \(V\) of participating types. Every type in \(V\) is saturated, and every type outside \(V\) is unmatched.

This proves the sufficiency assertion of Lemma 2: a flat element of \(\mathcal P\) has the required single-outcome structure, and its LP inequalities exclude every blocking pair.

Another useful consequence of Lemma 3 is that, on every pair positive in some \(z\in\mathcal P\),

\(F_i^x(j)-G_i^x(j)=F_j^x(i)-G_j^x(i).\)

The normalized masses in the corresponding indifference classes are therefore equal at both endpoints.

4. Restricting preference intervals preserves existence

For \(x\in\mathcal P\) and \(i\in V\), let \(B_i\) and \(D_i\) denote respectively the best and worst indifference classes among its positive partners.

Define \(T_x\subseteq C\) to contain those compatible pairs whose endpoints belong to \(V\) and whose partner classes lie between these extremes at both endpoints:

\(\displaystyle T_x=\bigl\{\{i,j\}\in C: i,j\in V,\; B_i\succeq_i j\succeq_i D_i,\; B_j\succeq_j i\succeq_j D_j \bigr\}. \)

Comparisons with a class mean comparisons with any representative of that class. Write

\(\mathcal P(T_x)=\{z\in\mathcal P:z_{ij}=0\text{ for }\{i,j\}\notin T_x\}.\)

The support of \(x\) lies in \(T_x\), so this restricted polytope is nonempty.

Lemma 4 — interval preservation. If a strongly stable assignment exists, then a strongly stable assignment exists in \(\mathcal P(T_x)\), for every \(x\in\mathcal P\).

4.1 Partition relative to a stable assignment

Fix a strongly stable assignment \(\sigma\). By Lemmas 1–3, it saturates exactly \(V\) and has one partner class \(r_i\) at every \(i\in V\).

Partition \(V\) as follows:

  • \(P\): \(D_i\succ_i r_i\), or \(D_i\sim_i r_i\) with \(B_i\succ_i D_i\).
  • \(Q\): \(r_i\succ_i B_i\), or \(r_i\sim_i B_i\) with \(B_i\succ_i D_i\).
  • \(R\): \(B_i\sim_i D_i\sim_i r_i\).
  • \(S\): \(B_i\succ_i r_i\succ_i D_i\).

These cases are disjoint and exhaustive.

Set \(f_i=F_i^x(r_i)\) and \(g_i=G_i^x(r_i)\). The partition has the following equivalent description.

| Position | Values |
|---|---|
| \(r_i\) strictly below \(D_i\) | \(f_i=g_i=1\) |
| \(r_i\sim_i D_i\), with nonflat support | \(f_i=1,\;0<g_i<1\) |
| \(r_i\) strictly above \(B_i\) | \(f_i=g_i=0\) |
| \(r_i\sim_i B_i\), with nonflat support | \(g_i=0,\;0<f_i<1\) |
| \(i\in R\) | \(f_i=1,\;g_i=0\) |
| \(i\in S\) | \(0<g_i\leq f_i<1\) |

For example, the last row follows because positive mass lies strictly above \(r_i\) and positive mass lies strictly below it.

If \(\sigma_{ij}>0\), that pair belongs to \(r_i\) and \(r_j\). Lemma 3 consequently gives

\(f_i+g_j=1,\qquad g_i+f_j=1.\)

The table shows explicitly that these equations pair its first row with its third row, its second row with its fourth row, and preserve each of its last two rows. Therefore every positive pair of \(\sigma\) connects \(P\) to \(Q\), lies entirely in \(R\), or lies entirely in \(S\).

4.2 Extremal positive pairs of \(x\)

Suppose \(x_{ij}>0\) and \(j\in D_i\). Put

\(\displaystyle q_i=\frac1{\mu_i}\sum_{k\in D_i}x_{ik}>0.\)

Then \(F_i^x(j)=1\) and \(G_i^x(j)=1-q_i\). Lemma 3 gives

\(G_j^x(i)=0,\qquad F_j^x(i)=q_i.\)

Thus \(i\in B_j\), and the normalized mass of \(B_j\) is \(q_i\). Exchanging the endpoints proves the converse: a positive pair is worst at one endpoint exactly when it is best at the other.

We need two closure properties.

First closure property. If \(i\in P\) and \(x_{ij}>0\) lies in \(D_i\), then \(j\in Q\).

To verify this, apply Lemma 3 with \(x\) there replaced by \(\sigma\), and with the positive pair taken from the present \(x\).

If \(r_i\) is strictly below \(D_i\), then \(F_i^\sigma(j)=G_i^\sigma(j)=0\). The equalities force \(F_j^\sigma(i)=G_j^\sigma(i)=1\), so \(r_j\succ_j i\). Since \(i\in B_j\), this places \(j\) in \(Q\).

If \(r_i\sim_i D_i\), then \(F_i^\sigma(j)=1\) and \(G_i^\sigma(j)=0\), forcing \(r_j\sim_j i\sim_j B_j\). The support at \(j\) cannot be flat: flatness would give \(F_j^x(i)=1,G_j^x(i)=0\), and the equalities for \(x\) would force \(F_i^x(j)=1,G_i^x(j)=0\), contradicting nonflatness at \(i\). Hence again \(j\in Q\).

Exchanging best with worst and exchanging the endpoints proves that every positive best-class pair at a type in \(Q\) connects to a type in \(P\) at its worst class. Concretely, an outcome strictly above that best class forces the other outcome strictly below its worst class; an outcome tied with that best class forces a tie with the other worst class, with both supports nonflat.

Second closure property. If \(i\in R\) and \(x_{ij}>0\), then \(j\in R\).

Here \(F_i^x(j)=1,G_i^x(j)=0\). Lemma 3 forces \(F_j^x(i)=1,G_j^x(i)=0\), so the support of \(x\) at \(j\) is flat at \(i\). Also \(F_i^\sigma(j)=1,G_i^\sigma(j)=0\); applying Lemma 3 again forces \(r_j\sim_j i\). Thus \(j\in R\).

4.3 Constructing the restricted assignment

Let \(\mathcal E\) be the positive pairs of \(x\) connecting worst classes at \(P\) to best classes at \(Q\).

For \(i\in P\), let \(q_i\) be its normalized worst-class mass. For \(i\in Q\), let \(q_i\) be its normalized best-class mass. The extremal identities above show that \(q_i=q_j\) along every pair in \(\mathcal E\). The first closure property also gives

\(\sum_{j:\,\{i,j\}\in\mathcal E}x_{ij}=\mu_iq_i \quad\text{for every }i\in P\cup Q.\)

Define \(\widehat\sigma\) by:

  • on \(\mathcal E\), set \(\widehat\sigma_{ij}=x_{ij}/q_i=x_{ij}/q_j\);
  • on \(R\), retain the restriction of \(x\);
  • on \(S\), retain the restriction of \(\sigma\);
  • set all remaining masses to zero.

The closure properties and the decomposition of the support of \(\sigma\) ensure that these pieces have disjoint endpoint sets. Each type in \(V\) receives exactly \(\mu_i\). Within-type pairs can occur in the \(R\) and \(S\) pieces, where their endpoint masses are retained directly.

The new outcome classes are

\(\displaystyle r'_i= \begin{cases} D_i,&i\in P,\\ B_i,&i\in Q,\\ r_i,&i\in R\cup S. \end{cases} \)

Every class lies in its prescribed interval. Hence the support of \(\widehat\sigma\) is contained in \(T_x\).

4.4 Verifying strong stability

Consider any compatible pair \(\{i,j\}\), including a within-type pair.

If neither endpoint belongs to \(Q\), both outcomes have weakly improved relative to \(\sigma\). A pair blocking the new assignment would therefore also block \(\sigma\): weak improvement by a proposed partner remains weak improvement relative to an inferior old outcome, and strict improvement remains strict. Thus such a pair cannot block.

Otherwise take \(i\in Q\), so its new outcome is \(B_i\). Completeness of the ranking gives three cases.

  • If \(B_i\succ_i j\), type \(i\) rejects the proposed partner.
  • If \(j\succ_i B_i\), then \(F_i^x(j)=0\). The LP inequality forces \(G_j^x(i)=1\). Thus \(j\in V\), and its worst positive class satisfies \(D_j\succ_j i\). Since \(r'_j\succeq_j D_j\), type \(j\) rejects the proposed partner.
  • If \(j\sim_i B_i\), then \(G_i^x(j)=0\). The other LP inequality forces \(F_j^x(i)=1\). Thus \(j\in V\) and \(D_j\succeq_j i\), giving \(r'_j\succeq_j i\). Type \(i\) is indifferent and type \(j\) cannot strictly improve, so the pair does not block.

This proves strong stability of \(\widehat\sigma\). By Lemma 2, it also belongs to \(\mathcal P\), and therefore to \(\mathcal P(T_x)\). Lemma 4 follows. \(\square\)

5. The algorithm

First solve the feasibility problem for \(\mathcal P\). If it is empty, answer No by Lemma 2. Otherwise choose an exact rational feasible point \(x\).

Repeat the following steps.

  • If the positive partners of every participating type are flat, return \(x\). Lemma 2 proves that it is strongly stable.
  • Choose a type \(i\) with nonflat positive partners. Let \(D_i\) be its current worst class, and define the linear objective

\(\displaystyle h(z)=\sum_{j\in D_i}z_{ij}.\)

  • Maximize \(h\) over \(\mathcal P(T_x)\).
  • If its maximum is \(\mu_i\), replace \(x\) by a maximizer and continue.
  • Otherwise minimize \(h\) over \(\mathcal P(T_x)\).
  • If its minimum is zero, replace \(x\) by a minimizer and continue.
  • If its minimum is positive, answer No.

Both optima exist because the restricted polytope is nonempty and compact.

Correctness of rejection. In the rejection case, every \(z\in\mathcal P(T_x)\) satisfies \(0<h(z)<\mu_i\). A strongly stable assignment in this polytope is impossible: it saturates \(i\), and Lemma 1 forces its positive partners into one class. Its mass in \(D_i\) must consequently be either zero or \(\mu_i\).

Lemma 4 says that existence anywhere would imply existence in \(\mathcal P(T_x)\). Thus rejection is correct.

Progress after an update. Let \(z\) be the replacement point. Its support lies in \(T_x\), and its participating types are unchanged by Lemma 3. Consequently its preference intervals are contained in the previous intervals, giving \(T_z\subseteq T_x\).

This inclusion is strict:

  • If \(h(z)=\mu_i\), its interval at \(i\) collapses to \(D_i\). At least one previously positive pair at a strictly better class is removed.
  • If \(h(z)=0\), its worst class at \(i\) strictly improves. At least one previously positive pair in \(D_i\) is removed.

Therefore every successful update strictly decreases \(|T_x|\). There are at most \(m\) successful updates, followed by acceptance or rejection. The algorithm uses \(\mathop{O}(m+1)\) linear programs.

6. Encoding length and running time

Every LP has \(m\) variables, polynomially many constraints, and coefficients obtained from the input masses through reciprocals and addition. Their binary encoding lengths are polynomial in \(L\).

Restricting to \(T_x\) only sets selected variables to zero. The objectives have coefficients zero or one. Thus successive iterations do not introduce growing numerical coefficients.

Standard polynomial-time algorithms for rational linear programming provide exact feasibility decisions, exact optimum values, and rational optimizing points of polynomial encoding length. The comparisons with zero and \(\mu_i\), the support computations, and the preference-interval updates consequently take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The algorithm works directly with the \(\tau\) supported types and the binary-encoded masses. It proves the claimed membership in \(\mathrm{P}\).

5. Algorithm and termination

The rejection criterion is correct. If the maximum is less than \(\mu_i\) and the minimum is positive, every feasible restricted assignment has \(0<h(z)<\mu_i\). A strongly stable assignment saturates \(i\) and uses one partner class, so its mass in \(D_i\) must be either zero or \(\mu_i\). Lemma 4 then rules out existence anywhere.

After an update, support containment and the common participating set imply that every new preference interval is contained in its predecessor.

  • At \(h(z)=\mu_i\), the interval collapses to \(D_i\), excluding a previously positive better-class pair.
  • At \(h(z)=0\), the worst class strictly improves, excluding a previously positive pair in \(D_i\).

Thus every successful update removes at least one permitted pair. At most \(m\) successful updates occur, with at most two optimization LPs per iteration. The claimed \(O(m+1)\) LP bound follows.
