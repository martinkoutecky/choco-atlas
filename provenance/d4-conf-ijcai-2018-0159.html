The continuous mirror question, as graded

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given \(m\geq 2\) candidates \(C\) and a target \(p\in C\), a society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(\rho_t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The society graph \(G=(T,E)\) is simple and undirected, with an edge exactly when the two rankings differ by one adjacent swap. Its vertices, edges, and rankings remain fixed throughout diffusion, including vertices whose mass becomes zero. Write \(N(t)\) for the open neighborhood and \(N[t]=N(t)\cup\{t\}\).
Starting from \(x=\mu\), an asynchronous update selects \(t\). If some \(u\in N(t)\) satisfies \(x_u>\tfrac12\sum_{z\in N[t]}x_z\), simultaneously replace \(x_u\) by \(x_u+x_t\) and \(x_t\) by zero; otherwise nothing changes. Mass cannot be split.
A distribution is stabilized when every update leaves it unchanged. The decision problem asks whether some finite legal update sequence reaches a stabilized distribution in which \(p\) is a Plurality winner, with ties accepted and votes weighted by mass.

Is this decision problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For integer voter counts, choosing an asynchronous update order that makes the target a Plurality winner is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; the corrected full version strengthens this to strong \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness ([verified result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.00651)).

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Normalizing integer voter counts preserves the entire diffusion process, including stabilization and Plurality ties.

Here “binary-rational” may mean rational numbers whose numerators and denominators are encoded in binary. The conclusion also holds if masses are required to be dyadic rationals; this restriction is addressed below.

1. Positive scaling preserves diffusion

For a fixed society graph, let \(U_t(x)\) denote the distribution obtained by selecting vertex \(t\).

First, the absorbing neighbor, when one exists, is unique. Indeed, write \(S=\sum_{z\in N[t]}x_z\). If two distinct neighbors \(u,v\) both satisfied the strict-majority condition, then \(x_u+x_v>S\), contradicting \(x_u+x_v\leq S\).

For every positive scalar \(\alpha\), we have
\(U_t(\alpha x)=\alpha U_t(x)\).
To verify this, observe that
\[ \]
\(x_u>\frac12\sum_{z\in N[t]}x_z\)
holds exactly when
\(\alpha x_u>\frac12\sum_{z\in N[t]}\alpha x_z\).
Thus scaling preserves whether an absorbing neighbor exists and its identity. If absorption occurs, the new recipient mass is \(\alpha x_u+\alpha x_t=\alpha(x_u+x_t)\); all other coordinates also agree with the scaled update.

Induction on the length of an update sequence therefore proves that executing the same sequence from \(x\) and \(\alpha x\) produces distributions related by the factor \(\alpha\) at every step.

Two consequences follow:

  • A distribution \(x\) is stabilized exactly when \(\alpha x\) is stabilized, because \(U_t(x)=x\) is equivalent to \(U_t(\alpha x)=\alpha x\) for every \(t\).
  • Writing \(\operatorname{top}(\rho_t)\) for the first candidate in ranking \(\rho_t\), the Plurality score \(\operatorname{sc}_c(x)=\sum_{\operatorname{top}(\rho_t)=c}x_t\) satisfies \(\operatorname{sc}_c(\alpha x)=\alpha\operatorname{sc}_c(x)\). Every score comparison and tie is preserved.

2. Polynomial reduction from integer counts

We use the established integer-count hardness theorem: deciding whether some asynchronous diffusion order makes the target a Plurality winner in the converged election is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Its corrected construction uses distinct supported rankings with positive integer weights and the graph induced by adjacent swaps. The underlying definitions use the same strict closed-neighborhood majority rule and accept tied winners. Thus this theorem concerns the integer version of the present problem, rather than diffusion on an arbitrary graph. See [Sections 3–4 and Theorem 1 of the corrected full paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.00651).

Take an instance from that hard family, with integer weights \(w_t>0\). Define
\(W=\sum_{t\in T}w_t\)
and
\(\mu_t=w_t/W\).
Keep all candidates, rankings, vertices, and edges unchanged.

These masses are positive and satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). By Section 1, every update sequence from \(w\) corresponds exactly to the same sequence from \(\mu\), with every intermediate distribution divided by \(W\). In particular, the integer instance admits a stabilized distribution where \(p\) wins if and only if the normalized instance does.

The transformation has polynomial encoding length. If \(b\) is the maximum binary length of an input weight, then \(W<\tau 2^b\), so its binary length is at most \(b+\lceil\log_2\tau\rceil+1\). Each output fraction \(w_t/W\) consequently has polynomial length, and all fractions can be computed in polynomial time. Reducing fractions, if required by the encoding, also takes polynomial time.

This is a polynomial many-one reduction and proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A changing update must have a positive source mass. Its recipient has positive mass as well, because it satisfies a strict-majority inequality over nonnegative masses.

Consequently, every changing update decreases the number of positive-mass vertices by exactly one: the source becomes zero, and the already-positive recipient remains positive. Furthermore, a zero-mass vertex can never receive mass, since it cannot satisfy the strict-majority condition.

Starting with \(\tau\) positive vertices, there can therefore be at most \(\tau-1\) changing updates. Any updates that leave the distribution unchanged can be deleted from a sequence without altering subsequent states. Hence every successful instance has a certificate consisting of at most \(\tau-1\) selected vertices.

We can verify this certificate using exact polynomial-time arithmetic. Write each initial mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive binary-encoded integers \(a_t,b_t\), and set
\(D=\prod_{t\in T}b_t\)
and
\(A_t=a_t(D/b_t)\).
The binary length of \(D\) is at most the sum of the denominator lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Since the masses sum to one, \(\sum_t A_t=D\).

Simulate diffusion on these integer weights. At every stage the weights are nonnegative integers summing to \(D\), so every weight has \(O(L)\) bits. A majority test is simply
\(2A_u>\sum_{z\in N[t]}A_z\).

After simulating the certificate, check stabilization explicitly: for every vertex \(t\) with \(A_t>0\) and every \(u\in N(t)\), require
\(2A_u\leq\sum_{z\in N[t]}A_z\).
Updates at zero-mass vertices already leave the distribution unchanged. Finally, compute all Plurality scores and check that the target’s score is at least every other score.

The graph itself can be computed by comparing every pair of listed rankings for a single adjacent swap, in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Thus certificate verification is polynomial, proving membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. If “binary-rational” means dyadic

A direct normalization by \(W\) need not be dyadic. Nevertheless, hardness still follows from the strong integer-count theorem.

Use its hard family, whose total integer weight \(W\) is polynomially bounded in the source-instance length. Let \(Q\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(Q\geq W\), and put \(h=Q-W\). Add \(h\) new candidates \(d_1,\ldots,d_h\) and \(h\) isolated ranking vertices, each of integer weight one.

Here is an explicit realization of the required graph. Append the common suffix \(d_1,\ldots,d_h\) to every original ranking. Fix two distinct original candidates \(a,b\). Give new vertex \(i\) the ranking beginning \(d_i,a,b\), followed by all remaining candidates in any fixed order.

These new vertices are isolated:

  • In an extended original ranking, \(d_i\) occupies position at least three. One adjacent swap cannot move it to first place.
  • Between two new rankings, an adjacent swap below first place preserves their different first candidates. Swapping the first two positions makes \(a\) first, which also cannot produce the other new ranking.

Appending a common suffix preserves exactly the original adjacent-swap edges. It also preserves the original first candidates and therefore their scores. Each new candidate has the constant integer score one.

At any stabilized original distribution, an original winner has positive integer score, hence score at least one. Thus, if \(p\) originally wins, it still wins after adding the new candidates, with ties accepted. Conversely, winning against the enlarged candidate set implies winning against the original candidates. Isolation ensures that the added vertices do not affect diffusion or stabilization.

The augmented total weight is \(Q\). Dividing all weights by \(Q\) now gives positive dyadic masses summing to one. Because \(Q<2W\) and \(W\) is polynomially bounded, the number of added candidates and rankings, and their explicit encodings, are polynomially bounded.

Therefore the dyadic restriction is also \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.
