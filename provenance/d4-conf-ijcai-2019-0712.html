Result

The requested guarantee is achievable in deterministic polynomial time. In particular, a randomized algorithm can achieve it with probability \(1\).

The proof has two essential ingredients:

  • After a small, budget-respecting regularization, the objectives are smooth functions with diminishing returns and polynomially bounded derivatives in appropriately rescaled coordinates.
  • Their values can be computed to arbitrary additive accuracy by weak convex optimization, with running time polynomial in the input bit length and the logarithm of the requested accuracy.

The argument applies to arbitrarily overlapping groups and uses only the \(\tau\) listed types.

1. Notation and existence of the limit

Write
\[ \]
\(A_{ut}=\mu_t\lambda_{tu}\), and define
\(T_x(z)_u=1-(1-x_u)\exp(-(Az)_u)\).

Thus the prescribed iteration is \(z^{(0)}=x\) and \(z^{(q+1)}=T_x(z^{(q)})\). Below, denote its limit by \(Z(x)\).

The map \(T_x\) is coordinatewise increasing on \([0,1]^\tau\), maps this cube into itself, and satisfies \(T_x(x)\geq x\). Consequently, the sequence is increasing and bounded, so \(Z(x)\) exists. Continuity gives \(T_x(Z(x))=Z(x)\).

Let \(F_0(x)=I(x)\) and \(F_g(x)=\Phi_g(x)\) for \(1\leq g\leq m\). Each \(F_j\) is a convex combination of the coordinates of \(Z(x)\); hence \(0\leq F_j(x)\leq1\).

2. A branching-tree representation

For each root type \(u\), construct a multitype branching tree as follows. Independently for every vertex of type \(v\), its number of children of type \(t\) is Poisson with mean \(A_{vt}\). Independently mark every type-\(t\) vertex with probability \(x_t\).

Let \(p_u^{(q)}(x)\) be the probability that the tree rooted at type \(u\) contains a marked vertex within depth \(q\). At depth zero this probability is \(x_u\). The Poisson probability-generating function gives

\(p_u^{(q+1)}(x)=1-(1-x_u)\exp\!\left(-\sum_t A_{ut}p_t^{(q)}(x)\right).\)

Therefore \(p^{(q)}(x)=z^{(q)}(x)\). Taking increasing limits,

\(Z_u(x)=\Pr(\text{the tree rooted at type }u\text{ contains a marked vertex}).\)

For any finite tree with \(N_t\) vertices of type \(t\), its conditional activation probability is

\(h_N(x)=1-\prod_t(1-x_t)^{N_t}.\)

This representation yields two properties needed below.

Monotonicity

Every finite-depth activation probability is coordinatewise increasing in \(x\). Taking limits shows that \(Z_u\), and therefore every \(F_j\), is coordinatewise increasing.

Scaling inequality

For a finite tree, \(s\mapsto h_N(sx)\) is concave on \([0,1]\). Indeed, differentiating twice gives a sum of nonpositive terms. Since \(h_N(0)=0\),

\(h_N(sx)\geq s\,h_N(x)\qquad(0\leq s\leq1).\)

Apply this inequality to each finite-depth tree and then take expectations and increasing limits. For every \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\),

\[Z_u(sx)\geq sZ_u(x),\qquad F_j(sx)\geq sF_j(x). \tag{1}\]

This proof includes seed vectors with zero coordinates.

3. Polynomial-time evaluation at strictly positive seeds

We first prove a value-oracle result.

Lemma. Given rational \(x\in(0,1)^\tau\) and rational \(0<\xi<1\), one can compute rational approximations to all coordinates of \(Z(x)\), each with additive error at most \(\xi\), in time polynomial in the encoding lengths of \(A,x\), in \(\tau\), and in \(\log(1/\xi)\).

3.1 Uniqueness of the fixed point

Suppose \(p,q\in[0,1]^\tau\) are fixed points of \(T_x\). They satisfy \(p,q\geq x>0\).

If \(\theta=\max_u p_u/q_u>1\), then \(p\leq\theta q\). For \(a\geq0\),

\(1-e^{-\theta a}\leq\theta(1-e^{-a}).\)

Extend the formula defining \(T_x\) to nonnegative vectors. Monotonicity and the preceding inequality give

\(p=T_x(p)\leq T_x(\theta q)\leq\theta T_x(q)-(\theta-1)x=\theta q-(\theta-1)x.\)

At a coordinate attaining \(p_u/q_u=\theta\), this is a contradiction. Thus \(p\leq q\). Interchanging them gives \(p=q\).

Furthermore, every \(z\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfying \(z\leq T_x(z)\) is dominated by \(Z(x)\): iteration from \(z\) increases to a fixed point, which must be the unique one.

3.2 A convex set of subsolutions

Fix rational \(0<\kappa<1\), and define

\(K_\kappa=\left\{z\in[0,1-\kappa]^\tau: -\log(1-z_u)-(Az)_u+\log(1-x_u)\leq0\ \text{for every }u\right\}.\)

Each displayed constraint is convex. Moreover, it is equivalent to \(z_u\leq T_x(z)_u\). Thus every \(z\in K_\kappa\) satisfies \(z\leq Z(x)\).

The map \(T_x\) is concave in its argument. Hence

\(T_x((1-\kappa)Z(x))\geq(1-\kappa)T_x(Z(x))+\kappa T_x(0)\geq(1-\kappa)Z(x).\)

Consequently,

\[(1-\kappa)Z(x)\in K_\kappa. \tag{2}\]

We can therefore approximate every coordinate of \(Z(x)\) by approximately maximizing \(\sum_u z_u\) over this convex set.

3.3 Explicit geometric bounds and weak separation

Put

\(x_{\min}=\min_u x_u,\qquad s=\min\{x_{\min},1-\kappa\},\qquad c=(s/2)\mathbf1.\)

The box \([0,s]^\tau\) lies in \(K_\kappa\), because \(z_u\leq x_u\) and \(Az\geq0\) there. In particular, \(K_\kappa\) contains the Euclidean ball of radius \(s/4\) around \(c\). It is contained in the ball of radius \(\tau\) around \(c\). These radii have polynomial encoding length.

Here is an explicit weak separation oracle. Write

\(G_u(z)=-\log(1-z_u)-(Az)_u+\log(1-x_u).\)

At \(c\),

\(G_u(c)\leq\log\!\left(\frac{1-x_u}{1-s/2}\right)\leq-x_u/2\leq-\sigma,\qquad \sigma=x_{\min}/2.\)

For a query point \(y\) outside \([0,1-\kappa]^\tau\), a violated box inequality gives an exact separating hyperplane. Otherwise, for requested distance accuracy \(\theta>0\), approximate every \(G_u(y)\) within

\(\rho=\theta\sigma/(4\tau).\)

If some approximation exceeds \(\rho\), then \(G_u(y)>0\). Its gradient is the rational vector

\(\nabla G_u(y)=\frac{1}{1-y_u}e_u-A_{u\cdot}.\)

By convexity, this vector strictly separates \(y\) from \(K_\kappa\).

If all approximations are at most \(\rho\), then every \(G_u(y)\leq2\rho\). Set

\(a=\frac{2\rho}{\sigma+2\rho},\qquad \widetilde y=(1-a)y+ac.\)

Convexity gives \(G_u(\widetilde y)\leq0\), so \(\widetilde y\in K_\kappa\). Also,

\(\|y-\widetilde y\|_2\leq a\tau\leq2\rho\tau/\sigma=\theta/2.\)

Thus the oracle either returns a rational separating hyperplane or correctly certifies distance at most \(\theta\).

All these computations have polynomial bit complexity. In particular, logarithms of positive rationals can be computed to absolute error \(\rho\) in time polynomial in their encoding length and \(\log(1/\rho)\). One elementary construction uses range reduction and

\(\log v=2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{2k+1},\qquad t=\frac{v-1}{v+1},\qquad 1\leq v\leq2,\)

for which \(0\leq t\leq1/3\) and the truncation error decreases geometrically.

We now use the standard weak-separation/weak-optimization theorem: for a compact convex body with known rational inner and outer balls and a polynomial-time weak separation oracle, linear optimization can return a rational point within distance \(\delta\) of the body and within additive \(\delta\) of the optimal objective, in polynomial time in the relevant encoding lengths and \(\log(1/\delta)\). This is the Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver theorem. [Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

Every hypothesis has been verified above. If the theorem is formulated relative to the inner parallel body, the stated version follows by moving each feasible point toward \(c\): a movement fraction \(a\) creates an interior ball of radius \(a(s/4)\), while changing the objective by at most \(a\tau\).

3.4 Recovering coordinate values

Weakly maximize \(\sum_u z_u\) over \(K_\kappa\), obtaining rational \(y\) with

\(\operatorname{dist}(y,K_\kappa)\leq\delta,\qquad \sum_u y_u\geq\max_{z\in K_\kappa}\sum_u z_u-\delta.\)

Choose \(v\in K_\kappa\) with \(\|y-v\|_2\leq\delta\). Since \(v\leq Z(x)\), equation (2) implies

\(\sum_u(Z_u(x)-v_u)\leq\tau\kappa+(\tau+1)\delta.\)

Every summand is nonnegative, so, coordinatewise,

\(|Z_u(x)-y_u|\leq\tau\kappa+(\tau+2)\delta.\)

Taking \(\kappa=\xi/(2\tau)\) and \(\delta=\xi/(2(\tau+2))\) proves the lemma.

Because each \(F_j\) is a convex combination of these coordinates, the same oracle approximates every objective within \(\xi\), without any dependence on the reciprocal of a group’s mass.

4. Budget-respecting regularization and derivative bounds

Set

\(\eta=\varepsilon/4,\qquad u_t=\min\{1,\beta/\mu_t\},\qquad d=\eta/(2\tau),\qquad b_t=d\,u_t.\)

Every budget-feasible original seed vector satisfies \(x_t\leq u_t\). Introduce the rational polytope

\(Q=\left\{w\in[0,1]^\tau:\sum_t\mu_tu_tw_t\leq\beta\right\},\)

and define the regularized seed vector and objectives by

\(x(w)=b+(1-\eta)(u_t w_t)_t,\qquad f_j(w)=F_j(x(w)).\)

Since \(\mu_tu_t\leq\beta\),

\(\sum_t\mu_tb_t\leq\eta\beta/2.\)

Consequently, every \(w\in Q\) gives a budget-feasible seed vector:

\[\sum_t\mu_tx_t(w)\leq\eta\beta/2+(1-\eta)\beta=(1-\eta/2)\beta\leq\beta. \tag{3}\]

Throughout the entire cube,

\(d\,u_t\leq x_t(w)\leq1-\eta/2.\)

Thus all value-oracle queries use strictly positive seeds strictly below \(1\).

4.1 Smoothness and diminishing returns

On an infinite branching tree, strictly positive seed probabilities make the probability of no marked vertex zero. Its conditional activation probability is therefore the constant \(1\).

On a finite tree, the conditional activation probability remains \(h_N(x)\). For an integer \(n\geq0\) and \(0<p<1\),

\(np(1-p)^{n-1}\leq1,\qquad n(n-1)p^2(1-p)^{n-2}\leq2.\)

These inequalities follow from the probabilities of exactly one and exactly two successes in a binomial distribution.

Differentiating \(h_N(x(w))\), these bounds imply

\(0\leq\partial_i h_N(x(w))\leq1/d,\)

and, for every pair \(i,j\),

\(-2/d^2\leq\partial_{ij}h_N(x(w))\leq0.\)

For example, the mixed derivative for distinct coordinates is the negative product of their two first-derivative factors. A repeated-coordinate derivative uses the second binomial bound.

These bounds are uniform over finite trees. On a neighborhood of the cube, the seed probabilities remain strictly between zero and one, with comparable bounds. Dominated differentiation therefore applies to the branching-tree expectations. It follows that every \(f_j\) is twice continuously differentiable and satisfies

\[\partial_i f_j(w)\geq0,\qquad -H\leq\partial_{ij}f_j(w)\leq0,\qquad H=2/d^2. \tag{4}\]

Thus every gradient decreases coordinatewise when its argument increases. Crucially, \(H\) is polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(1/\varepsilon\), independently of \(\beta\), the masses, and the contact rates.

4.2 Preserving comparison points

For any original feasible \(x^*\), put \(w_t^*=x_t^*/u_t\). Then \(w^*\in Q\), and monotonicity together with (1) gives

\[f_j(w^*)=F_j(b+(1-\eta)x^*)\geq(1-\eta)F_j(x^*). \tag{5}\]

In particular, defining \(U_g=(1-\eta)W_g\), the promise ensures that some \(w^*\in Q\) satisfies every \(f_g(w^*)\geq U_g\).

5. A gradient comparison inequality

For any \(w,w^*\in[0,1]^\tau\), equations (4) imply

\[\nabla f_j(w)\cdot w^*\geq f_j(w^*)-f_j(w). \tag{6}\]

To verify this, let \(r=(w^*-w)_+\). Along the increasing segment from \(w\) to \(w\vee w^*\), diminishing returns gives

\(f_j(w\vee w^*)-f_j(w)\leq\nabla f_j(w)\cdot r.\)

Monotonicity gives \(f_j(w\vee w^*)\geq f_j(w^*)\). Finally, \(r\leq w^*\) and \(\nabla f_j(w)\geq0\), proving (6).

6. A finite-precision algorithm

Choose

\(\gamma=\varepsilon/(64\tau),\qquad \nu=\varepsilon/64,\qquad N=\left\lceil4H\tau^2/\varepsilon\right\rceil.\)

Start with \(w^{(0)}=0\). At iteration \(k\), obtain rational estimates satisfying

\(\|\widehat a_j-\nabla f_j(w^{(k)})\|_\infty\leq\gamma,\qquad |\widehat f_g-f_g(w^{(k)})|\leq\nu.\)

These estimates are computable in polynomial time. For gradients, use a forward or backward coordinate difference whose endpoints remain in the cube. With step \(h\leq\gamma/(2H)\), its truncation error is at most \(Hh/2\). Evaluating each endpoint within \(\gamma h/8\) adds at most \(\gamma/4\) error. The remaining margin permits rounding to a fixed dyadic precision.

Solve the rational linear program

\(\max_{v\in Q}\widehat a_0\cdot v\)

subject to

\[\widehat a_g\cdot v\geq U_g-\widehat f_g-(\tau\gamma+\nu),\qquad 1\leq g\leq m. \tag{7}\]

Choose a rational optimal basic solution \(v^{(k)}\), and update

\(w^{(k+1)}=w^{(k)}+v^{(k)}/N.\)

The linear program is always feasible. Indeed, any comparison point \(w^*\in Q\) satisfying the regularized group targets obeys (6), so

\(\widehat a_g\cdot w^*\geq U_g-f_g(w^{(k)})-\tau\gamma \geq U_g-\widehat f_g-(\tau\gamma+\nu).\)

Moreover,

\(w^{(k)}=\frac1N\sum_{\ell<k}v^{(\ell)}.\)

Since \(Q\) is convex and contains zero, every iterate lies in \(Q\), and the update segments lie in the cube.

Return the rational vector \(x(w^{(N)})\).

7. Approximation guarantees

Write \(w=w^{(k)}\) and \(v=v^{(k)}\).

For each group, equation (7) and the approximation errors give

\[\nabla f_g(w)\cdot v\geq U_g-f_g(w)-2\tau\gamma-2\nu. \tag{8}\]

For the objective, let \(x^*\) be any original seed vector satisfying the budget and all original targets, and let \(w^*\) be its comparison point. This point is feasible in (7). Optimality of \(v\), followed by (6), gives

\[\nabla f_0(w)\cdot v \geq\widehat a_0\cdot v-\tau\gamma \geq\widehat a_0\cdot w^*-\tau\gamma \geq f_0(w^*)-f_0(w)-2\tau\gamma. \tag{9}\]

Taylor’s theorem and (4), using \(0\leq v_i\leq1\), yield

\[f_j(w+v/N)\geq f_j(w)+\frac1N\nabla f_j(w)\cdot v-\frac{H\tau^2}{2N^2}. \tag{10}\]

Let \(\alpha_N=1-(1-1/N)^N\), so \(\alpha_N\geq1-1/e\). Iterating the recurrences obtained from (8)–(10), and using \(f_j(0)\geq0\), gives

\(f_g(w^{(N)})\geq\alpha_N U_g-(2\tau\gamma+2\nu)-\frac{H\tau^2}{2N},\)

and

\(f_0(w^{(N)})\geq\alpha_N f_0(w^*)-2\tau\gamma-\frac{H\tau^2}{2N}.\)

Our choices ensure

\(2\tau\gamma+2\nu=\varepsilon/16,\qquad 2\tau\gamma=\varepsilon/32,\qquad H\tau^2/(2N)\leq\varepsilon/8.\)

Using \(U_g=(1-\eta)W_g\), \(W_g\leq1\), and \(\eta=\varepsilon/4\), we obtain

\(\Phi_g(x(w^{(N)})) \geq(1-1/e)W_g-\frac{7\varepsilon}{16} \geq(1-1/e)W_g-\varepsilon.\)

For the objective, equation (5) applies to every original feasible comparison point. Taking the supremum over these points therefore gives, without assuming that the supremum is attained,

\(I(x(w^{(N)})) \geq\alpha_N(1-\eta)\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon/32-\varepsilon/8 \geq(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}-\frac{13\varepsilon}{32} \geq(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon.\)

Budget feasibility follows exactly from (3).

8. Bit complexity

Here \(H=128\tau^2/\varepsilon^2\), so the number of iterations is

\(N=O(\tau^4/\varepsilon^3).\)

Each iteration uses polynomially many value-oracle calls and one rational linear program with \(\tau\) variables and \(m\) group constraints.

The oracle’s required accuracy for finite differences is inverse-polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(1/\varepsilon\). Its running time depends polynomially on the logarithm of that accuracy. Its geometric bounds also have polynomial encoding length: in particular, \(u_t\geq\beta\), so all queried seed coordinates are at least \(d\beta\).

Round value and gradient estimates to fixed dyadic precisions sufficient for the prescribed errors. By the derivative bound \(1/d\), the resulting linear-program coefficients have uniformly polynomial bit length. An optimal basic solution consequently has polynomial bit length. Summing the \(N\) update vectors increases encoding length by at most a polynomial factor.

Thus every query, linear program, iterate, and final seed vector has polynomial encoding length, and the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\).
