The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite \(B\), term sets \(T_b\), complete type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), ordered priority blocks containing otherwise identical agents, and per-capita capacities \(\bar q_b\), compute an allocation \(x_{\theta,b,t}\) and unmatched masses such that no positive mass can block under the paper’s layered chosen-set rule, while every type’s assignment distribution weakly dominates its assignment under any other stable mass allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

A type \(\theta\in\Theta\) specifies strict preferences over \(A=\{(b,t):b\in B,t\in T_b\}\cup\{\varnothing\}\) and its priority block at each \(b\); it has rational mass \(\mu_\theta\). For each \(b\), terms are \(t_b^1<\cdots<t_b^{k_b}\) and rational layer capacities \(\bar q_b^1,\ldots,\bar q_b^{k_b}\) sum to \(\bar q_b\). An allocation \(x\) assigns nonnegative mass \(x_{\theta,b,t}\), with total at most \(\mu_\theta\) per type and \(\bar q_b\) per object; the remainder is assigned \(\varnothing\).
For offered mass, \(C_b\) fills layers in order: layer \(1\) selects by priority; layer \(r>1\) first selects offers at terms \(t_b^r\) or above by priority, then fills any remainder from lower-term offers by decreasing term and then priority. Ties within a priority block are split fractionally; multiple offers by one infinitesimal agent count once, using the lowest qualifying term in the first pass and the highest term in the fallback pass.
Call \(x\) stable if it is individually acceptable, each \(b\)'s assigned mass is chosen by \(C_b\), and no positive mass of a type can move from \(\varnothing\) or a less-preferred assignment to a contract \((b,t)\) that \(C_b\) would retain. Distributional weak preference means first-order stochastic dominance under that type's ranking.

Target

Is computing a stable mass allocation whose distribution for every type weakly dominates its distribution under every other stable mass allocation in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Nearest known results

For finite individual agents with the same nested layered choice rule, the cumulative-offer algorithm produces a stable allocation weakly preferred by every agent to every other stable allocation.

1. Explicit tie convention and instance.

Consider the proportional interpretation of fractional tie splitting: if a priority block offers masses \(z_1,\ldots,z_m\) competing for capacity \(q\), its selected masses are \(\alpha z_1,\ldots,\alpha z_m\), where
\(\alpha=\min\{1,q/(\sum_i z_i)\}\).
The same selection fraction applies to incumbent and newly offered subpopulations in that block.

This convention is an explicit assumption: “ties within a priority block are split fractionally” does not itself give this formula.

Construct an instance with two types \(\theta,\phi\), each of mass \(1\), and three objects, each having one term, one layer, and capacity \(1\). Abbreviate their contracts by \(b,c,d\). Preferences are
\(\theta:b\succ\varnothing\succ c\succ d\) and
\(\phi:b\succ\varnothing\succ d\succ c\).
The types occupy the same priority block at \(b\); priorities elsewhere are immaterial.

The unacceptable contracts merely ensure that the types have distinct preference rankings. Individual acceptability forces all assigned mass to go to \(b\).

Consequently, every individually acceptable feasible allocation is described by
\(a=x_{\theta,b}\), \(h=x_{\phi,b}\), with
\(a\geq0\), \(h\geq0\), and \(a+h\leq1\).
Its unmatched masses are \(1-a\) and \(1-h\). Its assigned mass is chosen when offered alone, because it does not exceed capacity.

2. Positive retained mass as a blocking deviation: no stable allocation.

Suppose a deviation blocks whenever offering previously unmatched mass causes a positive part of that mass to be retained.

First consider \(a+h<1\). Because \(a<1\), choose
\(0<\delta\leq\min\{1-a,1-a-h\}\).
Offer an additional unmatched mass \(\delta\) of type \(\theta\) to \(b\). The total offered mass remains at most \(1\), so the entire new offer is selected. These agents strictly prefer \(b\) to being unmatched. Thus the allocation is blocked.

Now consider \(a+h=1\). At least one type has assigned mass strictly below \(1\); relabel the types so that \(a<1\). Choose \(0<\delta\leq1-a\) and offer that much unmatched mass of type \(\theta\).

The total offer becomes \(1+\delta\). Proportional selection retains mass
\(\delta/(1+\delta)>0\)
from the new offer. Hence this is a blocking deviation under the stated interpretation.

The example also works if positive retention is measured by a net increase in the proposing type’s assigned mass. Its newly selected total is
\((a+\delta)/(1+\delta)\), and
\(\displaystyle \frac{a+\delta}{1+\delta}-a=\frac{\delta(1-a)}{1+\delta}>0\).

These two cases exhaust all feasible allocations. Therefore, under proportional splitting and either of these positive-retention interpretations, this instance has no stable allocation.

3. Entire offered mass as a blocking deviation: no greatest stable allocation.

Consider instead the alternative definition that a deviation blocks only if the entire newly offered mass is selected in the same choice operation.

Every allocation with \(a+h=1\) is stable under this definition. To verify this, individual acceptability and choice consistency were established above. The only possible improving move is from \(\varnothing\) to \(b\). If any positive mass \(\delta\) makes that offer, proportional selection retains only
\(\delta/(1+\delta)<\delta\)
of the newly offered mass. Thus no such deviation satisfies the full-retention requirement.

In particular, both allocations
\(x^{(1)}=(1,0)\) and \(x^{(2)}=(0,1)\)
are stable.

Suppose a feasible allocation \(x^\star\) weakly dominated every stable allocation for both types. Dominating \(x^{(1)}\) for type \(\theta\) requires
\(x^\star_{\theta,b}\geq1\):
this follows directly from the first-order stochastic dominance inequality at that type’s top-ranked contract \(b\). Likewise, dominating \(x^{(2)}\) for type \(\phi\) requires
\(x^\star_{\phi,b}\geq1\).

Their sum would be at least \(2\), contradicting \(b\)’s capacity \(1\). Therefore, under this alternative blocking definition, stable allocations exist, but no stable allocation dominates every other stable allocation for every type.

4. A concrete obstacle to transferring a cumulative-offer argument.

Proportional fractional choice also fails the following contraction property:
if \(C(z)\leq y\leq z\) coordinatewise, then \(C(y)=C(z)\).
This property would say that deleting offers while keeping all previously chosen mass available leaves the choice unchanged.

For the same unit-capacity priority block, take
\(z=(1,1)\) and \(y=(1/2,1)\).
Then
\(C(z)=(1/2,1/2)\), so \(C(z)\leq y\leq z\), but
\(C(y)=(1/3,2/3)\neq C(z)\).

Thus, retaining a cumulative pool of offers and retaining only the chosen mass can produce different subsequent choices. A transfer of the finite-agent result that identifies these operations would require an additional argument; the displayed calculation rules out that particular identification.

5. What remains unresolved.

The calculations establish an existence obstruction under an explicitly specified fractional convention. They do not prove computational hardness.

If proportional splitting is intended, either blocking interpretation examined above defeats the requested universal output: one gives no stable allocation, and the other gives no greatest stable allocation. An algorithm allowed to report nonexistence would address a different search task.

If another convention is intended—for example, persistent individual tie-breaking or a stability condition evaluated against cumulative demand—the choice and blocking operations must first be specified mathematically. The complexity of that resulting problem remains unresolved here.
