The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), target \(p\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the nonmanipulators’ ranking types, find the infimum \(\lambda^\star=\inf_{\nu\ge 0}\sum_r\nu_r\) such that \(p\) is the unique Maximin winner under the normalized society \(\rho=(\mu+\nu)/(1+\sum_r\nu_r)\), where \(\nu_r\) is manipulator mass reporting ranking \(r\). Determine the computational complexity of this optimization in \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), including whether an \(\epsilon\)-approximate optimum can be computed efficiently.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, with target \(p\in C\), and let \(R\) be all strict linear rankings of \(C\). The nonmanipulators have a distribution \(\mu\) on \(R\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Manipulators may add arbitrary nonnegative real masses \(\nu_r\) on any rankings, including rankings outside the input support. Their cost is \(\lambda=\sum_r\nu_r\), and the resulting society is \(\rho=(\mu+\nu)/(1+\lambda)\).
Apply Maximin using pairwise masses. A manipulation is feasible precisely when \(p\) is the unique winner; ties with \(p\) count as failure. Define \(\lambda^\star=\inf\{\sum_r\nu_r:\nu\text{ is feasible}\}\).
For \(\lambda^\star>0\), an \(a\)-approximation returns a feasible rational manipulation of cost at most \(a\lambda^\star\). An additive \(\epsilon\)-approximation returns one of cost at most \(\lambda^\star+\epsilon\), including when \(\lambda^\star=0\).
Can a polynomial-time \(a\)-approximation for some fixed \(1<a<3/2\) be obtained, or is attaining such a factor NP-hard?
Can additive \(\epsilon\)-approximations be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), for binary rational \(0<\epsilon<1\)?
For discrete unweighted coalitions, every approximation factor below \(3/2\) is impossible in polynomial time unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), while a polynomial-time \(5/3\)-approximation exists ([source](https://tzin.bgu.ac.il/~omerlev/papers/aamas11.pdf)).
No result settling these approximation questions for this precise continuous model was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Require at least two candidates and strict complete rankings, with nonmanipulator masses summing to one.
  • Allow arbitrary nonnegative real manipulator masses on all rankings; computationally returned manipulations have binary rational masses.
  • Interpret epsilon-approximation as additive approximation by a feasible manipulation.
  • Apply multiplicative guarantees only when the infimum is positive; retain zero-infimum instances in the additive question because strict unique-winner inequalities may prevent attainment.

Results

Both questions can be settled for the stated continuous model:

  • A deterministic polynomial-time \(4/3\)-approximation exists. More generally, every fixed factor \(a>1\) is achievable in polynomial time.
  • Computing additive \(\epsilon\)-approximations in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\) is NP-hard, and hence impossible unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The constructions below allow manipulator rankings outside the input support and return explicitly listed rational manipulations.

The discrete \(3/2\) hardness argument uses integer coalition sizes; it therefore does not establish hardness for this continuous model. See [Zuckerman–Lev–Rosenschein, Proposition 3.5](https://tzin.bgu.ac.il/~omerlev/papers/aamas11.pdf).

1. Pairwise formulation and the strict-winner condition

For distinct candidates \(x,y\), define the original pairwise mass

\(A_{xy}=\sum_{r:\,x\succ_r y}\mu_r.\)

Thus \(A_{xy}+A_{yx}=1\). Write \(V=C\setminus\{p\}\), and define

\(s=\min_{q\in V}A_{pq},\qquad S(q)=\min_{y\ne q}A_{qy}.\)

These numbers can be computed exactly in polynomial time from the supplied support.

The normalization by \(1+\lambda\) multiplies every Maximin score by the same positive number. Consequently, all winner comparisons may use the unnormalized pairwise masses.

Lemma 1. Moving \(p\) to the top of every manipulator ranking preserves feasibility and cost.

Proof. This operation increases or preserves every pairwise mass favoring \(p\). For each rival \(q\), it decreases or preserves its pairwise mass against \(p\), while preserving all comparisons between rivals. Hence \(p\)'s score cannot decrease, and no rival's score can increase. \(\square\)

Assume henceforth that every manipulator ranks \(p\) first. Let \(t\) be the total manipulator mass, and let \(x_{jq}\) be the manipulator mass ranking \(j\) above \(q\), for distinct \(j,q\in V\). Then \(p\)'s unnormalized score is exactly \(s+t\).

Consider the weak relaxation in which \(p\) may tie for first place. A rival \(q\) has score at most \(s+t\) precisely when at least one of the following holds:

  • Its comparison against \(p\) witnesses this: \(A_{qp}\le s+t\).
  • Some \(j\in V\setminus\{q\}\) witnesses this: \(A_{qj}+t-x_{jq}\le s+t\), equivalently \(x_{jq}\ge A_{qj}-s\).

Define

\(e_q=A_{qp}-s,\qquad d_{qj}=\max\{0,A_{qj}-s\}.\)

Thus a witness against \(p\) requires \(t\ge e_q\), while a witness against another rival requires \(x_{jq}\ge d_{qj}\).

A candidate with \(S(q)\le s\) needs no imposed ranking constraint for the weak relaxation: choose an original comparison attaining \(S(q)\). Its pairwise mass increases by at most \(t\), so its resulting score remains at most \(s+t\).

Lemma 2. Any rational weak manipulation of mass \(t\) can be converted into a rational strict manipulation of mass \(t+\eta\), for every rational \(\eta>0\).

Proof. Fix any order of \(V\). Add mass \(\eta/2\) on each of the two rankings consisting of \(p\) first, followed by this order and its reverse.

The score of \(p\) increases by \(\eta\). Every pairwise mass between two rivals increases by exactly \(\eta/2\); every rival's pairwise mass against \(p\) stays fixed. Therefore a comparison witnessing a rival's weak defeat now witnesses its strict defeat. \(\square\)

The minimum weak cost exists. Indeed, weak feasibility holds at cost \(1\): \(p\)'s score is \(s+1\), while every rival's comparison against \(p\) is at most \(1\). Restricting to \(p\)-first manipulations of cost at most \(1\) gives a compact finite-dimensional set, and weak feasibility is closed because Maximin scores are continuous.

By Lemmas 1 and 2, this minimum weak cost equals \(\lambda^\star\).

We also obtain an exact test for the zero-infimum case:

\(\lambda^\star=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S(q)\le s\text{ for every }q\in V.\)

The reverse implication follows from Lemma 2 applied to the zero manipulation. For the forward implication, if \(S(q)>s\), adding nonnegative masses leaves \(q\)'s score at least \(S(q)\), whereas \(p\)'s score is at most \(s+t\). Thus every feasible manipulation has \(t>S(q)-s>0\).

2. Realizing witness cycles by rational rankings

A witness choice can be represented by a directed graph on \(V\):

  • A root has no outgoing arc. It is certified either by \(S(q)\le s\), or by its comparison against \(p\).
  • Every other vertex \(q\) chooses one outgoing arc \(q\to j\), requiring manipulator mass at least \(d_{qj}\) to rank \(j\) above \(q\).

A graph with at most one outgoing arc per vertex consists of trees leading to roots or to vertex-disjoint directed cycles.

Lemma 3. Such witness choices can be realized by a rational manipulation of total mass \(T>0\), provided:

  • Every root using its comparison against \(p\) satisfies \(e_q\le T\).
  • Every chosen arc satisfies \(d_{qj}\le T\).
  • Every chosen directed cycle \(Q\), of length \(k\), satisfies

\(\displaystyle\sum_{q\to j\in Q}d_{qj}\le(k-1)T.\)

The manipulation can be constructed in polynomial time with \(O(m)\) rankings.

Proof. Every strict ranking must violate at least one desired comparison on a directed cycle. Thus, for any distribution of total mass \(T\),

\(\displaystyle\sum_{q\to j\in Q}x_{jq}\le(k-1)T.\)

This proves the necessity of the cycle inequality for fixed witnesses.

For sufficiency, consider a cycle with demands \(d_1,\ldots,d_k\). Choose rational numbers \(f_1,\ldots,f_k\) satisfying

\(0\le f_i\le T-d_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^k f_i=T.\)

They exist because the capacities have sum

\(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^k(T-d_i)\ge T.\)

They can be obtained by greedily filling these capacities.

For mass \(f_i\), break only the \(i\)-th cycle arc and satisfy every other cycle comparison. The successful mass for the \(i\)-th comparison is then \(T-f_i\ge d_i\).

To combine different cycles, partition the same interval of length \(T\) according to the masses \(f_i\) for each cycle. Refine all these partitions jointly. There are \(O(m)\) resulting intervals.

For each interval, remove its selected broken arc from every cycle. The remaining witness graph is acyclic. Topologically order its precedence requirements, placing each witness \(j\) before its dependent candidate \(q\), and place \(p\) first. Give this ranking the interval's length as its mass.

Every tree comparison is satisfied on all mass \(T\); each cycle comparison receives the required successful mass. Roots are certified as specified. All masses are rational, and the number of intervals and arithmetic operations is polynomial. \(\square\)

3. A polynomial-time \(4/3\)-approximation

Set \(\gamma=5/4\). We first construct a rational weak manipulation of cost at most \(\gamma\lambda^\star\).

For a rational parameter \(b\ge0\), define the directed graph \(G_b\) on \(V\) by

\(q\to j\in E(G_b)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d_{qj}\le b.\)

Declare the following vertices to be available roots:

\(Z_b=\{q:S(q)\le s\}\cup\{q:e_q\le b\}.\)

A directed cycle \(Q\) of length \(k\) in \(G_b\) is called acceptable if either:

  • \(k\ge5\); or
  • \(2\le k\le4\) and \(\sum_{q\to j\in Q}d_{qj}\le(k-1)\gamma b\).

Every acceptable cycle satisfies Lemma 3 at mass \(T=\gamma b\). For a cycle of length at least five, this follows from

\(\displaystyle\sum_{q\to j\in Q}d_{qj}\le kb\le(k-1)\frac54b.\)

Define a test that accepts \(b\) when every sink strongly connected component of \(G_b\) contains either an available root or an acceptable cycle.

3.1. The test is polynomial-time computable

Cycles of lengths two, three, and four can be enumerated.

A directed cycle of length at least five can also be found in polynomial time by an elementary procedure. Enumerate ordered sequences of five distinct vertices \(v_1,\ldots,v_5\). When their consecutive arcs form a path, search for a path from \(v_5\) to \(v_1\) after deleting \(v_2,v_3,v_4\).

A simple return path produces a simple cycle of length at least five. Conversely, any cycle of length at least five contains a five-vertex prefix whose remaining part supplies such a return path. Thus this procedure detects exactly whether a sufficiently long cycle exists.

Together with strongly connected component computation, this gives a polynomial-time test.

3.2. Acceptance produces a weak manipulation of mass \(\gamma b\)

For every sink component, select an available root or an acceptable cycle.

Every vertex reaches one of the selected objects: the condensation graph is finite and acyclic, and each of its vertices reaches a sink component.

For vertices outside the selected objects, choose one arc decreasing the shortest distance to the selected objects. On each selected cycle, choose its cycle arcs. Selected roots have no outgoing arc. The resulting witness graph has exactly the selected cycles, together with trees leading to those cycles and roots.

All chosen demands are at most \(b\), all roots using \(p\) satisfy \(e_q\le b\), and all selected cycles satisfy the required inequality at mass \(\gamma b\). Lemma 3 constructs the weak manipulation.

3.3. The test accepts \(b=\lambda^\star\)

Take a minimum-cost weak manipulation of mass \(\lambda^\star\).

Consider a sink component of \(G_{\lambda^\star}\) containing no available root. Every vertex \(q\) in this component has \(S(q)>s\), and cannot use \(p\) as its witness. Its weak defeat therefore has some rival witness \(j\) with

\(d_{qj}\le x_{jq}\le\lambda^\star.\)

Consequently \(q\to j\) belongs to \(G_{\lambda^\star}\), and the sink property keeps \(j\) in the same component.

Choose such a witness for each vertex. The resulting functional graph contains a directed cycle \(Q\). If its length is \(k\), the necessity calculation in Lemma 3 gives

\(\displaystyle\sum_{q\to j\in Q}d_{qj}\le(k-1)\lambda^\star.\)

This cycle is acceptable: the displayed inequality suffices when \(k\le4\), and a longer cycle is acceptable by definition. Hence every sink component passes the test.

3.4. Finding a suitable parameter exactly

There is no need to know \(\lambda^\star\), or to approximate it numerically.

Form the following finite set of rational parameters:

  • \(0\) and \(1\);
  • every \(d_{qj}\);
  • every \(\max\{0,e_q\}\);
  • for every simple directed cycle \(Q\) of length \(k\in\{2,3,4\}\) in the complete directed graph on \(V\), the value

\(\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{q\to j\in Q}d_{qj}}{\gamma(k-1)}.\)

The set has polynomial size.

Between successive values in this set, the graph, available roots, and acceptable short cycles remain unchanged. Long-cycle existence depends only on the graph. Because all comparisons are non-strict, the test has the same value throughout each interval beginning at a breakpoint and ending just before the next breakpoint.

The test accepts at \(1\), since every vertex then has its comparison against \(p\) available. Evaluate the test at all listed parameters and let \(b_0\) be the smallest accepted one.

Since the test accepts \(\lambda^\star\), the breakpoint observation implies

\(b_0\le\lambda^\star.\)

If \(\lambda^\star>0\), then \(b_0>0\). Indeed, a vertex with \(S(q)>s\) has every \(d_{qj}>0\) and \(e_q>0\); at \(b=0\) it is an isolated sink without an available root.

Construct the weak manipulation of cost \(5b_0/4\), and apply Lemma 2 with

\(\eta=b_0/12.\)

The resulting rational manipulation makes \(p\) the unique winner and has cost

\(\displaystyle\frac54b_0+\frac1{12}b_0=\frac43b_0\le\frac43\lambda^\star.\)

This proves the requested polynomial-time approximation below \(3/2\).

All rational numbers have polynomial encoding length: pairwise masses are sums of input rationals, breakpoint formulas involve at most four demands, and the ranking construction uses polynomially many rational additions, subtractions, and comparisons. The output has \(O(m)\) rankings.

3.5. Every fixed factor above one

The same proof works with a fixed integer \(K\ge3\) and

\(\gamma=K/(K-1).\)

Enumerate cycles shorter than \(K\), and detect cycles of length at least \(K\) by enumerating \(K\)-vertex prefixes and searching for a return path.

For any fixed \(a>1\), choose \(K\) so that \(\gamma<a\), and add a positive rational perturbation within the remaining budget \((a-\gamma)b_0\). The running time remains polynomial for fixed \(a\), although its exponent depends on \(a\).

4. An inverse-polynomial gap for additive approximation

We now construct societies whose optimum encodes the longest directed cycle.

4.1. The source graph problem

Directed Hamiltonian Cycle is NP-complete; see [Karp's original paper, reprinted here](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

It remains NP-hard on strongly connected oriented graphs, where “oriented” means that opposite arcs never both occur.

Here is the reduction establishing the needed restriction. Non-strongly-connected inputs are immediate NO-instances and can be mapped to a fixed strongly connected NO-instance. For a strongly connected loopless directed graph \(G\), replace every vertex \(v\) by

\(v_{\mathrm{in}}\to v_{\mathrm{mid}}\to v_{\mathrm{out}},\)

and replace every original arc \(v\to w\) by

\(v_{\mathrm{out}}\to w_{\mathrm{in}}.\)

The resulting graph \(H\) is strongly connected and oriented. Every Hamiltonian cycle must traverse each three-vertex block consecutively, because the middle vertex has only its designated predecessor and successor, and the out-vertex has only its designated predecessor. Contracting these blocks gives a Hamiltonian cycle of \(G\). Conversely, expanding a Hamiltonian cycle of \(G\) gives one of \(H\).

Let \(H\) have \(n\ge3\) vertices, and let \(\ell\) be its longest directed cycle length. Strong connectivity and orientation imply \(\ell\ge3\).

4.2. The prescribed pairwise masses

Use one rival for each vertex of \(H\), together with \(p\). Choose

\(b=\lceil\log_2(n(n+3))\rceil,\qquad h=2^{-b},\qquad d=h/2,\qquad s=1/2-h.\)

Prescribe

\(A_{pq}=1/2-h\)

for every rival \(q\). For two rivals, prescribe:

  • If \(q\to j\) is an arc of \(H\), set \(A_{qj}=1/2-h/2\).
  • Its reverse comparison then has \(A_{jq}=1/2+h/2\).
  • If neither direction is an arc, set both pairwise masses to \(1/2\).

Accordingly, the witness demands are

\(A_{qj}-s=h/2\) on arcs of \(H\),

\(A_{qj}-s=3h/2\) on reverse arcs,

and \(A_{qj}-s=h\) on nonadjacent pairs.

Every witness against \(p\) requires mass \(2h\).

4.3. Realization by polynomial support and dyadic masses

We explicitly realize the pairwise matrix as a distribution of strict rankings.

For any pair \(u,v\) and dyadic bias \(\beta>0\), let \(z_1,\ldots,z_{m-2}\) list the remaining candidates. Give mass \(\beta\) to each ranking

\(u\succ v\succ z_1\succ\cdots\succ z_{m-2}\)

and

\(z_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ z_1\succ u\succ v.\)

This block has total mass \(2\beta\). Relative to half its total mass, it contributes bias \(\beta\) favoring \(u\) over \(v\), and zero bias on every other pair.

Use such a block:

  • with bias \(h\) favoring \(q\) over \(p\), for every rival \(q\);
  • with bias \(h/2\) favoring \(j\) over \(q\), for every arc \(q\to j\) of \(H\).

If \(H\) has \(e\) arcs, the total block mass is

\(B=2nh+eh.\)

Since \(H\) is oriented, \(e\le n(n-1)/2\), and hence

\(\displaystyle B\le\frac{h\,n(n+3)}2\le\frac12.\)

Give the remaining mass \(1-B\) equally to any ranking and its reverse. These last two rankings contribute zero pairwise bias. The resulting distribution realizes every prescribed pairwise mass exactly.

There are at most \(2(n+e)+2=O(n^2)\) ranking types before merging duplicates. All input masses are positive dyadic rationals of \(O(\log n)\) bits. Thus \(m,\tau,L\) are polynomially bounded in the graph size.

5. Exact optimum of the constructed society

Lemma 4. For the society constructed from \(H\),

\(\displaystyle\lambda^\star=\frac h2\frac{\ell}{\ell-1}.\)

Proof: lower bound. Move \(p\) first in any feasible manipulation.

The proposed value is at most \(3h/4<h\). Therefore any manipulation of mass at least \(h\) already exceeds it.

Now suppose the manipulation has mass \(t<h\). No rival can use its comparison against \(p\), which requires mass greater than \(2h\) for strict defeat. A nonadjacent pair requires successful witness mass greater than \(h\), and a reverse arc requires more than \(3h/2\); both are impossible because successful mass is at most \(t\).

Consequently, every rival must choose an arc of \(H\) as its witness. These choices contain a directed cycle of some length \(k\le\ell\). Strict defeat requires successful mass greater than \(h/2\) on every one of its \(k\) comparisons. Since every ranking satisfies at most \(k-1\) of them,

\(\displaystyle(k-1)t>k\frac h2.\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle t>\frac h2\frac{k}{k-1}\ge\frac h2\frac{\ell}{\ell-1},\)

where the last inequality uses that \(k/(k-1)\) decreases with \(k\). Taking the infimum proves the lower bound.

Proof: upper bound. Select a longest cycle. Strong connectivity ensures that every other vertex reaches it. Choose tree arcs leading to the cycle, all belonging to \(H\).

Set

\(\displaystyle T=\frac h2\frac{\ell}{\ell-1}.\)

Every selected arc has demand \(h/2\le T\), and the cycle satisfies

\(\displaystyle\ell\frac h2=(\ell-1)T.\)

Lemma 3 supplies a rational weak manipulation of mass \(T\). Lemma 2 supplies strict feasible manipulations of mass \(T+\eta\) for arbitrarily small rational \(\eta>0\). Hence \(\lambda^\star\le T\). \(\square\)

This also handles the unattained-infimum issue explicitly: every feasible manipulation costs strictly more than the displayed optimum, but feasible rational costs approach it.

6. Additive approximation would decide Hamiltonian Cycle

If \(H\) is Hamiltonian, Lemma 4 gives

\(\displaystyle\lambda^\star=Y:=\frac h2\frac n{n-1}.\)

If it is not Hamiltonian, then \(\ell\le n-1\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle\lambda^\star\ge N:=\frac h2\frac{n-1}{n-2}.\)

The gap is

\(\displaystyle\Delta=N-Y=\frac{h}{2(n-1)(n-2)}.\)

Because \(h\) is bounded above and below by constant multiples of \(1/n^2\), this gap is bounded above and below by constant multiples of \(1/n^4\).

Choose a dyadic rational \(\epsilon\) satisfying

\(\Delta/8<\epsilon\le\Delta/4.\)

Such an \(\epsilon\) can be computed in polynomial time, has \(O(\log n)\) bits, and satisfies \(1/\epsilon=O(n^4)\).

Suppose an additive approximation algorithm had running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). Apply it to the constructed society, and let \(t\) be the cost of its returned feasible manipulation.

  • On a Hamiltonian instance, \(t\le Y+\epsilon\le Y+\Delta/4<(Y+N)/2\).
  • On a non-Hamiltonian instance, feasibility implies \(t\ge\lambda^\star\ge N>(Y+N)/2\).

Comparing the rational output cost with \((Y+N)/2\) decides Hamiltonian Cycle in polynomial time. Thus the requested additive approximation algorithm cannot exist unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The hardness already holds on instances with positive optimum, polynomial support, and dyadic input masses. It does not depend on the zero-infimum case.
