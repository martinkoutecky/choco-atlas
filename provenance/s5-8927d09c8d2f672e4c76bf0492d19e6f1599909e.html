The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(m\), \(k\), manipulator mass \(\rho\), and target \(d\), let \(T=S_m\), let \(\mu\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Delta(T))\) be the high-multiplicity limit of IAC, and let the coalition choose \(\nu\in\Delta(T)\); compute \(\nu\) maximizing \(\Pr_{\mu}[d\in W_k((1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu)]\) under the paper's tie-breaking convention.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, let \(d\in A\), and let \(1\le k<m\) be an integer. The coalition's share of total mass is a binary rational \(0<\rho<1\); total mass is normalized to one.
Types are strict rankings \(T=S_m\). Write \(\Delta(T)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}:\sum_{t\in T}x_t=1\}\). The sincere society \(\mu\) is drawn uniformly, with respect to normalized relative Lebesgue measure, from \(\Delta(T)\); the coalition chooses \(\nu\in\Delta(T)\) before observing \(\mu\).
Apply \(k\)-approval with scores weighted by mass to \((1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu\). Ties are resolved against \(d\), so success means that \(d\)'s score strictly exceeds every rival's score.
Compute an exact optimal coalition society \(\nu\) maximizing \(\Pr_{\mu}[d\text{ wins}]\), without requiring the maximum probability itself as output.
A society is encoded by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is support size, not the number of possible rankings. The random \(\mu\) is specified by its law, rather than supplied as an encoded society.
State running times in \(m,k,\tau,L\), where \(\tau\) is the returned society's support size and \(L\) is the binary input length. Polynomial time means polynomial in \(m,L\), including the output cost; the implicitly specified prior does not contribute \(m!\) explicit input entries.

Is computing an exact optimal coalition society for this continuous Bayesian \(k\)-approval problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite electorates under IC or IAC, an optimal strategy has all \(c\) manipulators approve \(d\) and distributes the remaining \(c(k-1)\) approvals among rivals with totals differing by at most one. [Verified result](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~cebly/Papers/LuEtAl_uai12_withproofs.pdf).

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact optimal manipulation is polynomial-time solvable

An exact optimal coalition society has approval scores
\(q_d^*=1\) and \(q_a^*=(k-1)/(m-1)\) for every rival \(a\ne d\).

Below is a complete proof and an explicit construction using at most \(m-1\) rankings. Rational masses are encoded by their numerators and denominators in binary.

1. Approval scores and winning regions

For a ranking \(t\), let \(I_a(t)\) equal \(1\) if \(t\) approves candidate \(a\), and \(0\) otherwise. Define the approval score of candidate \(a\) in a society \(x\) by
\(s_a(x)=\sum_{t\in T}I_a(t)x_t\).

A coalition society \(\nu\) affects the outcome only through its score vector \(q\), where \(q_a=s_a(\nu)\). Every such vector satisfies \(0\le q_a\le1\) and \(\sum_{a\in A}q_a=k\).

Write \(P(q)\) for the coalition’s success probability. Success against rival \(a\) requires
\((1-\rho)(s_d(\mu)-s_a(\mu))+\rho(q_d-q_a)>0\).

Let \(N=m!\), \(D=N-1\), and let
\(H=\{x\in\mathbb R^T:\sum_t x_t=1\}\).
Write \(V_D\) for relative Lebesgue measure on this affine space. Define the closed winning region

\(K(q)=\{x\in\Delta(T):(1-\rho)(s_d(x)-s_a(x))+\rho(q_d-q_a)\ge0\text{ for every }a\ne d\}\).

This is a compact convex subset of \(H\).

Ties have probability zero. Fix \(a\ne d\). Because \(1\le k<m\), there exists a ranking approving \(d\) but not \(a\), and another approving \(a\) but not \(d\). Thus \(s_d-s_a\) takes values \(1\) and \(-1\) at vertices of the simplex. Since \(1-\rho>0\), the corresponding tie equation is nonconstant on \(H\). Its solution set is a proper affine hyperplane and has relative measure zero. The union over the finitely many rivals also has measure zero.

Consequently, adverse tie-breaking is accounted for exactly by
\(P(q)=V_D(K(q))/V_D(\Delta(T))\).

2. Proven lemma: winning probability is log-concave

We prove that, for any score vectors \(q^0,q^1\) and \(0<\lambda<1\),

\(P((1-\lambda)q^0+\lambda q^1)\ge P(q^0)^{1-\lambda}P(q^1)^\lambda\).

Here \(P\) and \(K\) can be defined by the preceding formulas even for vectors not induced by coalition societies.

Set \(q^\lambda=(1-\lambda)q^0+\lambda q^1\). Linearity of every winning inequality gives

\((1-\lambda)K(q^0)+\lambda K(q^1)\subseteq K(q^\lambda)\).

Indeed, if \(x^i\in K(q^i)\), then each winning margin at \(((1-\lambda)x^0+\lambda x^1,q^\lambda)\) is the corresponding convex combination of two nonnegative margins. The combined society also belongs to the simplex.

Suppose first that both endpoint probabilities are positive. Then both endpoint regions have positive \(D\)-dimensional volume. A convex set of positive volume has nonempty interior: otherwise its affine hull would be proper and its volume would be zero. Thus these compact regions satisfy the hypotheses of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality in the Euclidean affine space \(H\). That inequality states that
\(V_D((1-\lambda)B+\lambda C)^{1/D}\ge(1-\lambda)V_D(B)^{1/D}+\lambda V_D(C)^{1/D}\)
for convex bodies \(B,C\). See [Gardner, *The Brunn–Minkowski Inequality*, equation (1)](https://faculty.gardner.wwu.edu/gorizia12.pdf).

Applying it to the endpoint regions, using the inclusion above, and dividing by \(V_D(\Delta(T))^{1/D}\), yields

\(P(q^\lambda)^{1/D}\ge(1-\lambda)P(q^0)^{1/D}+\lambda P(q^1)^{1/D}\).

The weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality bounds the right-hand side below by
\(P(q^0)^{(1-\lambda)/D}P(q^1)^{\lambda/D}\).
Raising to the power \(D\) proves the claim.

If either endpoint probability is zero, the claimed log-concavity inequality follows directly from nonnegativity. This also covers empty winning regions.

3. Rival symmetry forces a balanced optimum

Put \(n=m-1\), and label the rivals \(a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}\).

Any permutation of rival names, fixing \(d\), induces a permutation of the ranking coordinates of \(\Delta(T)\). This transformation preserves relative Lebesgue measure. Moreover, it permutes sincere and coalition approval scores in the same way, preserving the winning conditions. Hence
\(P(gq)=P(q)\)
for every such permutation \(g\).

Let \(G\) consist of the \(n\) cyclic permutations of the rivals. For any coalition score vector \(q\), form
\(\bar q=n^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}gq\).
This vector is feasible: relabel each coalition society and average the resulting societies.

If \(P(q)>0\), repeated application of log-concavity gives

\(P(\bar q)\ge\prod_{g\in G}P(gq)^{1/n}=P(q)\).

If \(P(q)=0\), the same conclusion follows from nonnegativity.

Writing \(p=q_d\), cyclic averaging gives
\(\bar q_d=p\) and \(\bar q_a=(k-p)/n\) for every rival.

Now compare this with the claimed optimum \(q^*\). Its feasibility will be proved in Section 4. For each rival,

\((q_d^*-q_a^*)-(\bar q_d-\bar q_a)=1-\frac{k-1}{n}-p+\frac{k-p}{n}=\frac{m(1-p)}{n}\ge0\),

because \(p\le1\). Thus, for every sincere society, every strict winning margin under \(q^*\) is at least the corresponding margin under \(\bar q\). Success under \(\bar q\) therefore implies success under \(q^*\), so
\(P(q^*)\ge P(\bar q)\ge P(q)\).

Since \(q\) was arbitrary, every society realizing \(q^*\) is exactly optimal. This argument applies to every permitted value of \(\rho\).

4. Explicit society with polynomial support

If \(k=1\), output one ranking placing \(d\) first, with mass \(1\). It realizes the required scores.

Suppose \(k>1\), and put \(r=k-1\). Then \(1\le r<n\). For each \(j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\), define the approved rival set

\(B_j=\{a_{(j+\ell)\bmod n}:\ell\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}\}\).

Construct a ranking \(t_j\) by placing \(d\) first, then the candidates in \(B_j\), then all remaining rivals. Within each of the latter two groups, use increasing candidate index. Give every \(t_j\) mass \(1/n\).

Each ranking approves exactly \(d\) and the \(r\) rivals in \(B_j\). A fixed rival \(a_i\) belongs to exactly \(r\) sets: their starting indices are \(i-\ell\pmod n\), for \(0\le\ell<r\). Its coalition score is therefore \(r/n=(k-1)/(m-1)\), while \(d\)’s score is \(1\).

The rankings are distinct. Each nonempty proper cyclic interval \(B_j\) has exactly one member whose immediate cyclic predecessor lies outside the interval, namely \(a_j\); hence its starting index is uniquely determined.

The returned support size is consequently
\(\tau=1\) if \(k=1\), and \(\tau=m-1\) if \(k>1\).
All masses are positive binary-encoded rationals.

5. Running time and conclusion

With candidates indexed by integers, each ranking uses \(O(m\log m)\) bits, and each mass uses \(O(\log m)\) bits.

For each starting index \(j\), scan the rival indices to emit the approved group, then scan again to emit the remaining group. Membership is determined by whether \((i-j)\bmod n<r\), using \(O(\log m)\)-bit arithmetic. Including reading the input and writing the output, the construction takes

\(O(L+\tau m\log m)\)

bit operations. Since \(\tau\le m-1\), this is polynomial in \(m,L\), including output cost.

The factorial dimension appears only in the optimality proof. The algorithm neither enumerates rankings in the prior nor computes any winning probability.

Thus computing an exact optimal coalition society for the specified continuous Bayesian approval problem is polynomial-time solvable.
