The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) alternatives, a committee bound \(K\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose a committee \(S\) with \(|S|\le K\) and an allocation \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). What is the polynomial-time approximability of minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a))\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a scoring family \(\alpha=(\alpha_m)_{m\ge1}\), where \(\alpha_m:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) is strictly increasing, \(\alpha_m(1)=0\), and \(\alpha_{m+1}(i)=\alpha_m(i)\) for \(1\le i\le m\); its values are computable in time polynomial in \(m\).
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(m\ge2\) alternatives, each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length. The integer committee bound satisfies \(1\le K\le m\), and \(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)\) denotes rank, starting at \(1\).
Choose a committee \(S\) with \(1\le |S|\le K\) and nonnegative rational allocations \(x_{t,a}\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\). There are no capacity or equal-representation constraints. Minimize \(C(S,x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a))\).
A multiplicative \(\beta\)-approximation returns a feasible solution with \(C(S,x)\le\beta\operatorname{OPT}\), including when \(\operatorname{OPT}=0\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L,K\).

For each fixed scoring family \(\alpha\) satisfying these conditions, does this problem admit a polynomial-time multiplicative approximation for some constant \(\beta>1\), or is every such constant-factor approximation impossible unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\)?

For discrete unit-weight voters assigned individually to at most \(K\) alternatives without capacities, every such scoring family admits no polynomial-time constant-factor approximation unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.1661v2)
No result settling the exact rational-mass formulation above was located in the literature search.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

For every fixed scoring family \(\alpha\) satisfying the stated assumptions and every fixed constant \(\beta>1\), a polynomial-time multiplicative \(\beta\)-approximation for the rational-mass problem would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The reduction combines identical discrete voters into one ranking with rational mass. The absence of capacity constraints ensures that fractional allocation does not improve the optimum.

1. Fractional allocations can be eliminated

Let \(A\) be the alternative set. For any nonempty committee \(S\subseteq A\), define
\[ d_t(S)=\min_{a\in S}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)), \qquad F(S)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_t(S). \]

For every feasible allocation \(x\) to \(S\), nonnegativity and the mass constraint give
\[ \sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_t(a)) \ge d_t(S)\sum_{a\in S}x_{t,a} =\mu_t d_t(S). \]
Summing over \(t\) proves \(C(S,x)\ge F(S)\).

Conversely, choose \(a_t\in S\) attaining \(d_t(S)\), and assign all of type \(t\)'s mass to \(a_t\). This allocation is feasible because there are no capacity constraints, and its cost is \(F(S)\). Consequently,
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} =\min_{\substack{S\subseteq A\\1\le |S|\le K}}F(S). \]

Moreover, replacing any returned allocation by this allocation cannot increase its cost. Thus fractional splitting creates no advantage, even for approximate solutions.

2. An approximation-preserving reduction

Consider an explicitly listed discrete instance with \(n\ge1\) unit-weight voters, rankings \(r_1,\ldots,r_n\), \(m\) alternatives, and committee bound \(K\). Its objective is
\[ D(\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_m(\operatorname{pos}_{r_i}(\phi(i))), \]
where the assignment \(\phi\) uses at most \(K\) alternatives.

Let \(T\) be the set of distinct rankings appearing in this list. For each \(t\in T\), let \(c_t\) be its number of occurrences and set
\[ \mu_t=\frac{c_t}{n}. \]
These are positive rational masses encoded in binary, and their sum is \(1\). The resulting society satisfies the distinct-support requirement.

For a fixed committee \(S\), assigning every discrete voter to its highest-ranked member of \(S\) minimizes the discrete cost. Its value is
\[ D_{\min}(S) =\sum_{t\in T}c_t d_t(S) =nF(S). \]
Minimizing over the same feasible committees therefore yields
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}} =n\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}. \]

Now suppose an algorithm returns a feasible rational-mass solution \((S,x)\) with
\[ C(S,x)\le\beta\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}. \]
Assign each original discrete voter to its highest-ranked member of \(S\). The resulting assignment uses at most \(K\) alternatives, and
\[ D(\phi) =nF(S) \le nC(S,x) \le n\beta\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}} =\beta\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}}. \]

Thus a rational-mass \(\beta\)-approximation gives a discrete \(\beta\)-approximation with exactly the same approximation factor. This argument also holds when either optimum is zero.

3. Polynomial size and running time

The reduction never enumerates all possible rankings. Its support size is \(\tau\le n\).

Using alternative labels of \(O(\log m)\) bits, the constructed input has length
\[ L_{\mathrm{mass}} =O\!\left(\tau m\log m+\tau\log(n+1)+\log(K+1)\right). \]
Each numerator \(c_t\) and denominator \(n\) requires \(O(\log(n+1))\) bits. Since the source lists its \(n\) voters explicitly, this output length is polynomial in the source input length.

Identical rankings can be grouped by lexicographic sorting in \(O(nm\log(n+1))\) symbol operations. Recovering the discrete assignment takes \(O(nm)\) symbol operations by scanning each ranking for its first member of \(S\).

If the hypothetical approximation algorithm runs in time \(q(m,\tau,L,K)\), where \(q\) is polynomial, the resulting discrete algorithm runs in time
\[ q(m,\tau,L_{\mathrm{mass}},K) +\operatorname{poly}(n,m,\log(n+1)). \]
It is therefore polynomial in the explicit discrete input length.

For the rational-mass problem itself, evaluating a committee and constructing its optimal allocation takes \(O(\tau m)\) symbol operations to scan the rankings, together with polynomial-time score evaluation and rational arithmetic. The score bit lengths are polynomial in \(m\) by the computability assumption; exact weighted sums have polynomial bit length in \(L\) and those score lengths.

4. Applying the discrete hardness theorem

Theorem 3 of Skowron, Faliszewski, and Slinko states that, for every normal increasing positional scoring family and every constant \(\beta>1\), the discrete Chamberlin–Courant total-dissatisfaction problem has no polynomial-time \(\beta\)-approximation unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). Their definition of a normal family requires precisely strict increase, value zero at rank one, prefix consistency, and polynomial-time computability. Their Chamberlin–Courant formulation uses unit-weight voters, at most \(K\) alternatives, and no binding capacities. [Source: definitions and Theorem 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.1661v2)

All hypotheses therefore match the discrete problem used in Section 2. Trivial source cases outside \(m\ge2\) and \(1\le K\le m\) can be handled directly.

A polynomial-time rational-mass \(\beta\)-approximation would, by Sections 2–3, contradict this theorem unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). This proves the claimed inapproximability for every fixed admissible \(\alpha\).

5. Zero cost and fixed committee bounds

The zero-cost condition is easy to recognize. Let \(H\) be the set of alternatives ranked first by at least one supported ranking. Strict increase and \(\alpha_m(1)=0\) imply that rank one is the unique zero-cost rank. Because every mass is positive,
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |H|\le K. \]
Indeed, zero cost requires every supported ranking's top alternative to belong to the committee; selecting \(H\) also suffices. Reading the input and checking this condition takes \(O(\tau m)\) symbol operations. Hence the hardness conclusion does not rely on difficult zero-cost detection.

For completeness, enlarging a committee cannot increase \(F(S)\). An optimal committee of size exactly \(K\) therefore exists. Enumerating all such committees gives an exact algorithm in time
\[ \binom{m}{K}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,K). \]
This is polynomial for every fixed \(K\); the inapproximability result concerns unrestricted input \(K\).
