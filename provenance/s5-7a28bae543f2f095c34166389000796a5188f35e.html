The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a directed type-approval relation, and a rational threshold \(\alpha\), decide whether an atomless society of those types admits two matchings whose per-capita PAV score is at least \(\alpha\). Each person may be paired at most once in each matching, and approval between types applies uniformly to their members.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Input also includes a nonempty directed approval relation \(R\subseteq T\times T\) and a binary rational threshold \(\alpha\). Let \(m=|R|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Realize the society as disjoint intervals \(X_t\) of Lebesgue measure \(\mu_t\), with union \(X\) and measure \(\lambda\). A person of type \(t\) approves every distinct person of type \(s\) exactly when \((t,s)\in R\); diagonal entries permit approval of distinct people of the same type.
A matching is a measurable, measure-preserving, fixed-point-free involution \(p:D\to D\) on a measurable subset \(D\subseteq X\); people outside \(D\) are unmatched. All conditions are interpreted modulo null sets. Write \(N_p\) for the people whose assigned partner they approve.
Each selected matching must be minimal: almost every pair has at least one approving endpoint. It must also be Pareto optimal: no matching \(q\) satisfies \(\lambda(N_p\setminus N_q)=0\) and \(\lambda(N_q\setminus N_p)>0\).
Select exactly two matchings \(p_1,p_2\), allowing repetitions and repeated partners. Their per-capita PAV score is \(\int_X H_{h(x)}\,d\lambda(x)\), where \(h(x)=\mathbf{1}_{N_{p_1}}(x)+\mathbf{1}_{N_{p_2}}(x)\), \(H_0=0\), \(H_1=1\), and \(H_2=3/2\).

Is deciding whether such two matchings have per-capita PAV score at least \(\alpha\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies, this threshold problem is NP-complete even for two matchings and at most three approved partners per person; PAV winner determination is polynomial-time solvable for bipartite or symmetric approvals ([published results](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-023-01453-7)).
No result settling exactly the atomless problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified atomless matchings as measurable, measure-preserving partial involutions on intervals representing the type masses.
  • Restored the discrete outcome restrictions of minimality and Pareto optimality, interpreting domination and feasibility modulo null sets.
  • Required a nonempty approval relation, carrying over the exclusion of elections with no approvals.
  • Allowed approval between distinct members of the same type through diagonal relation entries; excluded pairing a person with themselves.
  • Explicitly allowed repeated matchings and repeated partners, as in the discrete multiset convention.
  • Defined m as the number of approval arcs and L as the total input bit length because this setting has no separately listed candidate set.

Result

The threshold problem is polynomial-time solvable. An exact linear program with \(O(m+\tau)\) variables and constraints computes the maximum score. Its optimum is attained by two minimal, Pareto-optimal measurable matchings.

The proof uses atomlessness in two places: realizing arbitrary feasible pair masses, and arranging the two approval sets to have minimum overlap within every type.

1. Type-level pair masses

Fix an ordering of \(T\), and define
\[ \]
\(E=\{(t,s):t\le s,\ (t,s)\in R\text{ or }(s,t)\in R\}\).

Thus \(E\) records unordered permissible type pairs, including loops, and \(|E|\le m\).

For each \(e=(t,s)\in E\), introduce a nonnegative variable \(x_e\). Its interpretation is:

  • If \(t<s\), then \(x_e\) is the mass of type-\(t\) people paired with type-\(s\) people. Measure preservation requires the same mass at the other endpoint.
  • If \(t=s\), then \(x_e\) is the total mass of people participating in pairs within type \(t\).

Let \(I_t\) be the edges incident to \(t\), counting a loop once. Define \(b_{te}=1\) if \(e\in I_t\) and type \(t\) approves its partner type on \(e\); otherwise define \(b_{te}=0\).

The capacity constraints and approval masses are
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle x_e\ge0,\qquad \sum_{e\in I_t}x_e\le\mu_t,\qquad a_t=\sum_{e\in I_t}b_{te}x_e.\)

In particular, \(0\le a_t\le\mu_t\).

Every minimal matching gives such a vector. Indeed, for \(t<s\), the involution maps the people of type \(t\) paired with type \(s\) bijectively and measure-preservingly onto the corresponding people of type \(s\). The incident portions are disjoint, giving the capacity constraints. Minimality excludes pairs outside \(E\), and the definition of \(b_{te}\) gives
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle a_t=\lambda(N_p\cap X_t).\)

2. Realizing a mass vector with prescribed approvers

Realization lemma. Suppose \(x\) satisfies the capacity constraints. For each type \(t\), prescribe a set \(A_t\subseteq X_t\) that is a finite union of intervals and has measure \(a_t\). Then there is a minimal measurable matching realizing \(x\) whose approval set within \(X_t\) is exactly \(A_t\), modulo null sets.

Proof. For each type \(t\), partition \(A_t\) into portions \(B_{te}\), one for each incident edge with \(b_{te}=1\), having measures \(x_e\). This is possible because their required measures sum to \(a_t\).

For the incident edges with \(b_{te}=0\), select disjoint portions \(B_{te}\) from \(X_t\setminus A_t\), again having measures \(x_e\). The total required measure is
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \sum_{\substack{e\in I_t\\b_{te}=0}}x_e =\sum_{e\in I_t}x_e-a_t \le\mu_t-a_t.\)

All these portions can be chosen as finite unions of intervals by cutting the available intervals successively. Any remaining people are unmatched.

For a cross-type edge \(e=(t,s)\), pair \(B_{te}\) with \(B_{se}\). Both have measure \(x_e\). Two finite unions of intervals with equal measure admit a measure-preserving bijection consisting of finitely many translations: order their component intervals, refine both at the combined cumulative-length breakpoints, and translate corresponding pieces. Use this bijection in one direction and its inverse in the other.

For a loop \(e=(t,t)\), split \(B_{te}\) into two disjoint portions of measure \(x_e/2\), and pair these portions by the same construction.

Together these maps form a measurable, measure-preserving involution. They have no fixed points because each paired pair of portions is disjoint. Interval endpoints may be discarded as null sets.

Every used edge belongs to \(E\), so every pair has at least one approving endpoint. A type-\(t\) person approves precisely when placed in a portion with \(b_{te}=1\). These portions constitute \(A_t\), proving the lemma. \(\square\)

3. An upper bound for any two minimal matchings

Write \(S(p_1,p_2)\) for the PAV score and \(A_i=N_{p_i}\). Pointwise,
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle H_{\mathbf1_{A_1}+\mathbf1_{A_2}} =\mathbf1_{A_1}+\mathbf1_{A_2}-\frac12\mathbf1_{A_1\cap A_2}.\)

Let \(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}\) be the mass vectors of the two matchings, and let
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle a_t^{(i)}=\lambda(A_i\cap X_t).\)

Since both approval sets lie within \(X_t\),
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \lambda(A_1\cap A_2\cap X_t) \ge\max\{0,a_t^{(1)}+a_t^{(2)}-\mu_t\}.\)

The average vector
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle x=\frac{x^{(1)}+x^{(2)}}2\)

satisfies the capacity constraints, since those constraints are linear. Its approval masses satisfy
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle a_t=\frac{a_t^{(1)}+a_t^{(2)}}2.\)

Consequently,
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle S(p_1,p_2) \le\sum_{t\in T}\left(2a_t-\frac12\max\{0,2a_t-\mu_t\}\right).\)

This bounds every pair of minimal matchings using only one feasible mass vector.

4. Every value in the bound is attainable

Conversely, fix any feasible mass vector \(x\), with approval masses \(a_t\).

In each interval \(X_t\), let \(A_t^{(1)}\) be its initial portion of measure \(a_t\), and let \(A_t^{(2)}\) be its terminal portion of measure \(a_t\). These sets satisfy
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \lambda(A_t^{(1)}\cap A_t^{(2)}) =\max\{0,2a_t-\mu_t\}.\)

Apply the realization lemma separately to these two families of sets, using the same vector \(x\). This produces two minimal matchings with score
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\left(2a_t-\frac12\max\{0,2a_t-\mu_t\}\right).\)

The constructions are independent. This is permitted because the problem allows repeated partners and repetitions of matchings.

Together with the upper bound, this proves that the maximum score over two minimal matchings is exactly
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle V=\max_{\substack{x_e\ge0\\\sum_{e\in I_t}x_e\le\mu_t}} \sum_{t\in T}\left(2a_t-\frac12\max\{0,2a_t-\mu_t\}\right), \qquad a_t=\sum_{e\in I_t}b_{te}x_e.\)

5. The exact linear program

Introduce variables \(z_t\). Consider the following rational linear program:

Maximize
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\left(2a_t-\frac12z_t\right)\)

subject to
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle x_e\ge0\quad(e\in E),\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{e\in I_t}x_e\le\mu_t\quad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle a_t=\sum_{e\in I_t}b_{te}x_e\quad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle 0\le z_t\le\mu_t,\qquad z_t\ge2a_t-\mu_t\quad(t\in T).\)

For fixed feasible \(x,a\), the objective is maximized by taking
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle z_t=\max\{0,2a_t-\mu_t\}.\)

This choice respects \(z_t\le\mu_t\), because \(a_t\le\mu_t\). Therefore the linear program has optimum \(V\).

Its feasible region is nonempty: take all variables equal to zero. It is also compact. Every edge variable is bounded by the mass of an incident type, and \(a_t,z_t\) lie between zero and \(\mu_t\). Hence an optimum exists, and Section 4 constructs two minimal matchings attaining it.

6. Pareto optimality follows at the maximum

It remains to prove that imposing Pareto optimality does not reduce \(V\).

First, any matching \(q\) can be made minimal without changing \(N_q\): delete every pair for which neither endpoint approves. The deleted subset of its domain is measurable and invariant under the involution. Restricting \(q\) to its complement therefore remains a measurable, measure-preserving, fixed-point-free involution.

Now let \(p_1,p_2\) be minimal matchings attaining \(V\). Suppose \(p_1\) is not Pareto optimal. Then some matching \(q\) satisfies
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \lambda(N_{p_1}\setminus N_q)=0,\qquad \delta:=\lambda(N_q\setminus N_{p_1})>0.\)

Make \(q\) minimal by the preceding deletion operation, which preserves its approval set.

Replacing \(p_1\) with \(q\) loses approval only on a null set. Every newly approving person gains either \(H_1-H_0=1\) or \(H_2-H_1=1/2\), depending on approval of \(p_2\). Thus
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle S(q,p_2)-S(p_1,p_2)\ge\frac12\delta>0.\)

This contradicts the definition of \(V\) as the maximum over pairs of minimal matchings. Therefore \(p_1\) is Pareto optimal. The same argument applies to \(p_2\).

Hence \(V\) is also the maximum under both required restrictions.

7. Complexity and the finite-model distinction

The linear program has at most \(m+2\tau\) variables and \(O(m+\tau)\) constraints. Its coefficients, apart from the input masses, belong to a fixed finite set of rational numbers. Its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore computes \(V\) exactly, or directly decides feasibility after adding the objective constraint
\[ \]
\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\left(2a_t-\frac12z_t\right)\ge\alpha.\)

The answer to the original problem is yes exactly when \(V\ge\alpha\).

The distinction from the [published finite-society hardness result, Theorem 3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-023-01453-7) is substantive: finite people cannot be divided among different incident edges. Here every feasible mass vector is realizable, and the two approval sets can be positioned independently within each type. These two proved properties make the linear program exact.
