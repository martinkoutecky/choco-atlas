The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational metric \(d\), a common destination \(D\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}>0\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over rider types \(t=(p_t,\alpha_t)\), find an ordering \(\pi\) of the support minimizing its route length to \(D\), subject at every cohort pickup \(j\ge2\) to \(\delta_j\sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k}\le\mu_{\pi_j}\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D)\), where \(\delta_j=d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})+d(p_{\pi_j},D)-d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},D)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(X\) be a finite set of \(m\) locations, with an explicitly given rational metric \(d\), destination \(D\in X\), and binary rational \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}>0\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) distinct types \(t=(p_t,\alpha_t)\), with \(p_t\in X\), binary rational \(\alpha_t>0\), and positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; different types may share a location.
A route is a permutation \(\pi\) of the entire support, picking up each type's whole mass once and ending at \(D\); its starting pickup is unrestricted, and masses cannot be split.
Its length is \(\ell(\pi)=\sum_{j=2}^{\tau}d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})+d(p_{\pi_\tau},D)\).
Define \(\delta_j=d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})+d(p_{\pi_j},D)-d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},D)\). A route is feasible exactly when \(\delta_j\sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k}\le\mu_{\pi_j}\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D)\) for every \(2\le j\le\tau\).
Given also a nonnegative binary rational bound \(B\), the decision problem asks whether a feasible route has length at most \(B\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this continuous route-length decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finitely many individual riders, minimizing route length under the constraints \(\delta_j(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}+\sum_{k<j}\alpha_{\pi_k})\le\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D)\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with identical positive sensitivities ([discrete hardness result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.07306v2)).
No result settling the exact mass-based problem defined above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass 1 and required a nonempty support.
  • Required positive binary rational rider sensitivities, following the discrete optimization problem's domain.
  • Specified an explicit metric distance matrix on m locations and defined L as the total binary input length.
  • Defined route length from the first pickup to the destination, with unrestricted first pickup and no depot travel.
  • Made each support type an indivisible cohort visited exactly once; distinct types may share a pickup location, including the destination.
  • Introduced a nonnegative binary rational length bound B to express the optimization question as a decision problem.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}=1\), every sensitivity equals \(1\), masses are equal, pickup locations are distinct, and every permutation is feasible.

The proof below uses the exact mass-based constraints in the question.

1. Reduction from Hamiltonian path

We use the known \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness of undirected Hamiltonian path: given a finite simple undirected graph, decide whether some ordering of all its vertices has adjacent consecutive vertices. This unrestricted-endpoint version is explicitly recalled in footnote 9 of the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.07306v2).

Let \(G=(V,E)\), with \(|V|=n\ge2\), be an instance. Restricting to \(n\ge2\) discards only trivial instances and preserves hardness.

Create one location \(p_v\) for each \(v\in V\), together with a new destination \(D\). Thus \(m=n+1\). Define the symmetric distance function, with zero diagonal, by

\[ d(p_u,p_v)= \begin{cases} 1,&\{u,v\}\in E,\\ 2,&\{u,v\}\notin E, \end{cases} \qquad (u\ne v), \qquad d(p_v,D)=2n. \]

For each vertex, introduce exactly one type \(t_v=(p_v,1)\), of mass \(\mu_{t_v}=1/n\). Set

\[ \tau=n,\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{op}}=1,\qquad B=3n-1. \]

The types are distinct because their locations are distinct. All masses and sensitivities are positive rationals, and the masses sum to \(1\).

The constructed distances form a metric

Symmetry and positivity away from the diagonal hold by construction.

For three distinct pickup locations, each distance is at most \(2\), while the sum of any two distances is at least \(2\). Hence every triangle inequality holds.

For a triangle containing \(D\) and two distinct pickup locations, the necessary inequalities are

\[ d(p_u,p_v)\le 2\le 4n=d(p_u,D)+d(D,p_v) \]

and

\[ d(p_u,D)=2n\le d(p_u,p_v)+2n =d(p_u,p_v)+d(p_v,D). \]

The remaining inequality follows by exchanging \(u\) and \(v\). Triangles with repeated locations satisfy the inequalities automatically. Thus this is a valid rational metric.

2. Every permutation satisfies the mass-based constraints

Consider any permutation \(\pi\) of the support. Every pickup is at distance \(2n\) from \(D\), so, for \(2\le j\le n\),

\[ \delta_j =d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})+2n-2n =d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})\le2. \]

Because masses equal \(1/n\) and sensitivities equal \(1\),

\[ \sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k}=\frac{j-1}{n}. \]

Consequently, the left side of the required feasibility inequality satisfies

\[ \delta_j\sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k} \le \frac{2(j-1)}n \le\frac{2(n-1)}n<2. \]

Its right side is exactly

\[ \mu_{\pi_j}\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D) =\frac1n\cdot1\cdot2n=2. \]

Every constraint therefore holds, for every permutation.

3. The length bound detects a Hamiltonian path

For a permutation \(\pi\), let \(q(\pi)\) be the number of consecutive pickup pairs whose corresponding vertices are nonadjacent in \(G\).

There are \(n-1\) consecutive pickup pairs. Each contributes \(1\), with an additional \(1\) precisely when it is a nonedge. The final journey to \(D\) contributes \(2n\). Hence

\[ \ell(\pi)=(n-1)+q(\pi)+2n=3n-1+q(\pi). \]

It follows that

\[ \ell(\pi)\le B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q(\pi)=0. \]

If \(G\) has a Hamiltonian path, picking up the types in its vertex order gives a feasible route of length \(B\).

Conversely, any route of length at most \(B\) has \(q(\pi)=0\). Its corresponding vertex ordering visits every vertex exactly once and uses only graph edges between consecutive vertices, so it is a Hamiltonian path.

Thus the construction is a many-one reduction. The unrestricted starting pickup agrees exactly with the unrestricted endpoints of the source problem.

Encoding and construction time

The construction has \(m=n+1\) and \(\tau=n\). Every numerical entry has \(O(\log n)\) bits, so its total binary length is \(L=O(n^2\log n)\). Given an adjacency matrix for \(G\), the construction takes \(O(n^2\log n)=O(m^2\log m)\) bit operations.

The reduction is therefore polynomial, proving \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and running times

A certificate is a permutation of the \(\tau\) support indices, requiring \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) bits.

Exact verification is polynomial despite the binary rational inputs. To see this explicitly, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input rational numbers. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Write each rational input \(z\) as \(Z/Q\); all resulting integers have \(O(L)\) bits.

In particular, write

\[ \mu_t=M_t/Q,\qquad \alpha_t=A_t/Q,\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{op}}=A_{\mathrm{op}}/Q,\qquad d(x,y)=C_{xy}/Q,\qquad B=K/Q. \]

For each pickup step, define

\[ \Delta_j=C_{p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j}} +C_{p_{\pi_j},D}-C_{p_{\pi_{j-1}},D}. \]

Multiplying the feasibility inequality by the positive integer \(Q^3\) gives the equivalent integer comparison

\[ \Delta_j\sum_{k<j}M_{\pi_k}A_{\pi_k} \le M_{\pi_j}A_{\mathrm{op}}C_{p_{\pi_j},D}. \]

The prefix sums can be updated after each pickup. The length bound becomes

\[ \sum_{j=2}^{\tau}C_{p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j}} +C_{p_{\pi_\tau},D}\le K. \]

All integers in these calculations have \(O(L)\) bits: multiplication involves only a constant number of scaled inputs, and summing at most \(\tau\) terms adds \(O(\log\tau)=O(L)\) bits.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, preparing the common denominator and scaled inputs takes \(O((m^2+\tau)L^2)\) bit operations. Checking the permutation and all inequalities then takes \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

For completeness, exhaustive enumeration gives a deterministic decision algorithm taking \(O((m^2+\tau\,\tau!)L^2)\) bit operations, reusing the scaled inputs across permutations.

Therefore the exact problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, and a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
