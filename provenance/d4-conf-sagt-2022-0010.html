The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite resident and hospital type sets with positive rational masses \(\rho_i,\eta_j\), tied and incomplete rankings over types, mutual acceptability, and a common quota pair \(1\le\ell\le u\), find a maximum-score stable flow \(x_{ij}\ge0\). Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\rho_i\), \(q_j=\sum_i x_{ij}\le u\eta_j\), and \(x_{ij}=0\) for unacceptable pairs; for each acceptable pair define \(D_{ij}=\rho_i-\sum_{j':j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\) and \(A_{ij}=u\eta_j-q_j+\sum_{k:i\succ_j k}x_{kj}\), and require \(D_{ij}=0\) or \(A_{ij}=0\). Maximize \(\sum_j\eta_j\min\{1,q_j/(\ell\eta_j)\}\), with complexity measured in the type support and input bit length.

Result

Both inapproximability bounds remain valid. Divisible mass does not improve the optimum on the unit-mass instances used for the discrete hardness results.

The key is a rounding theorem: when resident masses, hospital capacities, and lower-quota thresholds are integers, every stable fractional flow can be converted in polynomial time into a stable integral flow with at least the same score.

1. Preserving stability by preserving support and saturated margins

Let \(E\) be the acceptable pairs. Write \(c_j=u\eta_j\), \(b_j=\ell\eta_j\), and \(d_i(x)=\sum_jx_{ij}\). The objective satisfies

\(\ell S(x)=\sum_j\min\{b_j,q_j(x)\}\).

Fix a stable flow \(x\). Define \(F_x\) to consist of feasible flows \(y\) satisfying:

  • \(y_{ij}=0\) whenever \(x_{ij}=0\).
  • \(d_i(y)=\rho_i\) whenever \(d_i(x)=\rho_i\).
  • \(q_j(y)=c_j\) whenever \(q_j(x)=c_j\).

This set is nonempty because it contains \(x\).

Lemma. Every flow in \(F_x\) is stable.

For an acceptable pair \((i,j)\), the resident deficit can be written as

\(D_{ij}(x)=\rho_i-d_i(x)+\sum_{h:\,j\succ_i h}x_{ih}\),

where \(\succ_i\) denotes the strict part of the resident’s weak ranking. Both terms are nonnegative. Consequently, \(D_{ij}(x)=0\) implies that \(d_i(x)=\rho_i\) and every assignment strictly worse than \(j\) has zero mass. The defining restrictions of \(F_x\) preserve both properties, so \(D_{ij}(y)=0\).

Likewise,

\(A_{ij}(x)=c_j-q_j(x)+\sum_{k:\,i\succ_j k}x_{kj}\).

Its terms are nonnegative. Thus \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) implies that hospital \(j\) is full and every assignment to a resident strictly worse than \(i\) has zero mass. These properties are also preserved in \(F_x\), giving \(A_{ij}(y)=0\).

Stability of \(x\) supplies at least one of these zero deficits for every acceptable pair. The same deficit remains zero in \(y\). This proves the lemma.

2. Rounding without decreasing the soft-quota score

Assume now that all \(\rho_i\), \(c_j\), and \(b_j\) are integers. We optimize the score over \(F_x\) using a minimum-cost circulation.

Create nodes \(s,t\), one node for each resident type \(i\), and two nodes \(j,v_j\) for each hospital type. Put \(R=\sum_i\rho_i\). Use the following arcs; all unspecified costs are zero.

| Arc | Lower bound | Upper bound | Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(s\to i\) | \(\rho_i\) if \(d_i(x)=\rho_i\), otherwise \(0\) | \(\rho_i\) | \(0\) |
| \(i\to j\), only when \(x_{ij}>0\) | \(0\) | \(\rho_i\) | \(0\) |
| \(j\to v_j\) | \(c_j\) if \(q_j(x)=c_j\), otherwise \(0\) | \(c_j\) | \(0\) |
| First arc \(v_j\to t\) | \(0\) | \(b_j\) | \(-1\) |
| Second arc \(v_j\to t\) | \(0\) | \(c_j-b_j\) | \(0\) |
| \(t\to s\) | \(0\) | \(R\) | \(0\) |

The two \(v_j\to t\) arcs are parallel arcs. Their capacities are nonnegative because \(\ell\le u\).

For any \(y\in F_x\), assign flow \(y_{ij}\) to \(i\to j\). The other arc flows are determined by its row and column totals, except that the two outgoing hospital arcs receive respectively

\(\min\{b_j,q_j(y)\}\quad\text{and}\quad q_j(y)-\min\{b_j,q_j(y)\}\).

This produces a feasible circulation of cost \(-\ell S(y)\). In particular, the circulation problem is feasible.

Conversely, every feasible circulation defines a flow in \(F_x\) through its \(i\to j\) arc values. At an optimal circulation, the cost-\(-1\) hospital arc must carry \(\min\{b_j,q_j\}\): if its capacity were unused while the parallel cost-zero arc carried positive flow, transferring flow between them would strictly decrease the cost. Hence an optimal circulation has cost exactly \(-\ell S(y)\).

The circulation has integral lower and upper bounds. The network-flow integrality theorem therefore gives an integral optimal circulation. Its hypotheses hold here: the node-arc incidence matrix is totally unimodular, the bounds and node balances are integral, and the feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded. Thus an optimal vertex is integral.

Extract its resident-hospital flows \(y\). They are integral, belong to \(F_x\), and are stable by the preceding lemma. Comparing the optimal circulation with the circulation induced by \(x\) gives

\(-\ell S(y)\le-\ell S(x)\),

so \(S(y)\ge S(x)\).

This proves the rounding theorem. The essential integrality condition includes the thresholds \(b_j\), so the argument explicitly accounts for the objective’s truncation.

3. An approximation-preserving embedding of discrete instances

Take a discrete uniform-quota instance. Make each resident and each hospital a supported type of mass one:

\(\rho_i=1,\qquad\eta_j=1\).

Copy the preference lists, including ties and unacceptable pairs, and retain the common quotas \(\ell,u\). Then \(c_j=u\) and \(b_j=\ell\) are integers.

Integral feasible flows in this instance are precisely discrete matchings: each resident row has sum at most one, so its entries are zero or one.

For such a matching \(M\):

  • \(D_{ij}(M)>0\) precisely when resident \(i\) is unmatched or strictly prefers \(j\) to its assigned hospital.
  • \(A_{ij}(M)>0\) precisely when hospital \(j\) has a vacant seat or contains a resident strictly worse than \(i\).

Thus the supplied stability equations coincide with discrete weak stability. The objectives also coincide:

\(S(M)=\sum_j\min\{1,|M(j)|/\ell\}\).

Every discrete stable matching is therefore a stable mass flow with the same score. Conversely, the rounding theorem converts every stable fractional flow into a discrete stable matching with at least its score. Consequently,

\(\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}=\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}\)

on these embedded instances.

Moreover, this is an approximation-preserving conversion of solutions. If a mass algorithm returns \(x\) with \(S(x)\ge\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}/\alpha\), rounding returns a discrete stable matching \(M\) satisfying

\(S(M)\ge S(x)\ge\operatorname{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}/\alpha\).

4. Transferring the two hardness bounds

The discrete result states that, for uniform quotas with \(\theta=u/\ell\), approximation within

\(\alpha_{\mathrm{NP}}=1+(2-\sqrt2)\theta/3-\varepsilon\theta^2\)

is NP-hard, and approximation within

\(\alpha_{\mathrm{UGC}}=1+\theta/3-\varepsilon\theta^2\)

is hard under UGC. Proposition 16 supplies the reduction for every fixed pair of positive integer quotas \(\ell\le u\). [Makino–Miyazaki–Yokoi, Theorem 13 and Proposition 16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06660v3).

If a polynomial-time mass algorithm attained either corresponding factor, apply it to the unit-mass embedding and then perform the polynomial-time rounding above. This would attain the same factor for the discrete problem, contradicting the corresponding hardness theorem.

Therefore, whenever the stated factor exceeds one:

  • The first factor remains NP-hard to attain; a polynomial-time algorithm would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
  • The second factor remains hard to attain under UGC.

5. Running times and parameter dependence

Let \(B\) be the binary encoding length of a supplied rational flow \(x\). The rounding network has \(r+2m+2\) vertices and at most \(|E|+r+3m+1\) arcs, with \(|E|\le rm\). Support and saturation can be determined by exact rational arithmetic in polynomial time.

On the embedded instances, all capacities are at most \(\max\{r,u\}\), and costs belong to \(\{-1,0\}\). Standard exact minimum-cost circulation algorithms therefore make the conversion in time

\(\operatorname{poly}\bigl(rm+\tau,\ L+B+\log(u+1)+\log(\ell+1)\bigr)\).

It has no dependence on \(\varepsilon\) and requires no expansion into individual capacity slots.

The embedding adds only unit masses and copies the original supported agents and lists. In particular, \(\tau=r+m\) counts the listed types throughout. The cited graph construction has \(r=5uN/2\), \(m=(u+3/2)N\), and \(|E|=2u|F|+9uN/2\), where \(N\) and \(|F|\) are its graph sizes; hence it is polynomial for each fixed \(u\). [Appendix B.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06660v3).

Because hardness holds for each fixed admissible \(\ell,u,\varepsilon\), it also excludes running times of the form \(f(\ell,u,\varepsilon)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), with polynomial degree independent of these parameters, under the respective assumptions.
