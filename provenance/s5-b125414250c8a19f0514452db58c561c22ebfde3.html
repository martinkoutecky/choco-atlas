The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and tertiary utility vectors \(u_t\in\{-1,0,1\}^m\), determine whether there is a finite-support allocation \(x_{t,k}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^m\) satisfying sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and sum_{t,k} k_j x_{t,k}=q_j\) for every item category \(j\), and output one if so. Require EFX3: for each pair of positive-mass cohorts \((t,k)\), \((t',\ell)\), and each \(S\in\{[m],G,B\}\), apply the paper's EFX inequalities to the bundles restricted to \(S\), evaluated using \(u_t\), where \(G=\{j:\exists t,\ u_{tj}>0\}\) and \(B=\{j:\forall t,\ u_{tj}<0\}\). Require Pareto optimality under type-preserving couplings from current to new integral bundles that preserve each type's mass and all supplies: every positively coupled reassignment must weakly improve that type's utility, and a positive mass must improve strictly. Analyze the computation in \(m\), tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau=|T|\ge1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\ge1\) item categories, binary rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\ge0\), and additive utilities \(u_t\in\{-1,0,1\}^m\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A bundle is \(k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), with unrestricted multiplicities. An allocation consists of finitely many nonzero weights \(x_{t,k}\ge0\), satisfying \(\sum_kx_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,k}k_jx_{t,k}=q_j\) for every category \(j\).
Define \(G=\{j:\exists t\in T,\ u_{tj}>0\}\), \(B=\{j:\forall t\in T,\ u_{tj}<0\}\), and \(U_t^S(k)=\sum_{j\in S}u_{tj}k_j\).
EFX3 requires, for every ordered pair of cohorts \((t,k),(t',\ell)\) with \(x_{t,k},x_{t',\ell}>0\), and every \(S\in\{[m],G,B\}\): \(U_t^S(k)-u_{tj}\ge U_t^S(\ell)\) whenever \(j\in S\), \(k_j>0\), and \(u_{tj}<0\); and \(U_t^S(k)\ge U_t^S(\ell)-u_{tj}\) whenever \(j\in S\), \(\ell_j>0\), and \(u_{tj}>0\). Empty families of inequalities are vacuous.
Write \(U_t=U_t^{[m]}\). An allocation is Pareto optimal if there is no nonnegative coupling \(\gamma_{t,k,h}\), over current and new integral bundles, satisfying \(\sum_h\gamma_{t,k,h}=x_{t,k}\), \(\sum_{t,k,h}h_j\gamma_{t,k,h}=q_j\), and \(\gamma_{t,k,h}>0\Rightarrow U_t(h)\ge U_t(k)\), with \(\sum_{t,k,h:\,U_t(h)>U_t(k)}\gamma_{t,k,h}>0\). The coupling may have countably infinite support, and the improved allocation need not satisfy EFX3.
Is deciding whether an EFX3 and Pareto-optimal allocation exists in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can a finite allocation also be output in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?
For finitely many agents and explicitly listed indivisible items, tertiary additive utilities admit a polynomial-time algorithm constructing an EFX3 and Pareto-optimal allocation. [Aleksandrov–Walsh](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.11005v2)
No result settling this exact continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Result

An EFX3 and Pareto-optimal allocation exists for every valid input. Moreover, a finite allocation with rational weights and at most \(m+\tau\) positive cohorts can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The construction balances fractional goods assignments, rounds them into integral bundles, and assigns extra chores to cohorts with larger goods counts.

1. A rounding lemma

Lemma. Given a nonnegative rational vector \(v\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^n\), one can compute, in polynomial time, a rational probability distribution on at most \(n+2\) integral vectors \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n\) such that

\(\mathbb E[z]=v,\qquad \sum_jz_j\in\{\lfloor s\rfloor,\lceil s\rceil\},\qquad s=\sum_jv_j.\)

Every coordinate with \(v_j=0\) is zero in every output vector. The running time is polynomial in the binary encoding length, regardless of the magnitude of \(s\).

Proof. If \(s=0\), output the zero vector. Otherwise, put \(r=\lceil s\rceil\), and append a dummy coordinate of length \(r-s\). Partition \([0,r)\) into consecutive half-open intervals of these lengths.

For \(\alpha\in[0,1)\), count how many points of

\(\{\alpha,\alpha+1,\ldots,\alpha+r-1\}\)

fall in each interval. Delete the dummy coordinate from the resulting vector.

The dummy interval has length less than one, so its count is either zero or one. Consequently, the remaining total count is either \(\lfloor s\rfloor\) or \(\lceil s\rceil\). If \(s\) is integral, the dummy interval is empty and the total count is exactly \(s\).

For any interval \(I\subseteq[0,r)\), integration over \(\alpha\) gives

\(\displaystyle \int_0^1\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\mathbf1_I(\alpha+i)\,d\alpha=|I|.\)

Thus each coordinate has the required expectation.

The vector changes only when \(\alpha\) passes the fractional part of an interval endpoint. Partitioning \([0,1)\) at these rational breakpoints therefore gives at most \(n+2\) vectors, weighted by the lengths of the corresponding subintervals.

This construction does not enumerate the \(r\) points. For an interval \([a,b)\), its count is

\(\lceil b-\alpha\rceil-\lceil a-\alpha\rceil.\)

All operations are rational arithmetic, comparisons, and integer rounding on polynomial-length numbers. This proves the algorithmic assertions. \(\square\)

2. Balance the goods before rounding

Write \(D=[m]\setminus(G\cup B)\). Every category in \(D\) has maximum utility zero and has at least one type valuing it at zero.

For \(j\in G\), let \(f_{tj}\) denote the aggregate amount assigned to type \(t\). Consider the rational convex quadratic program

\(\displaystyle \min_f\ \Phi(f)=\sum_{t\in T}\frac{\left(\sum_{j\in G}f_{tj}\right)^2}{\mu_t}\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle f_{tj}\ge0,\qquad \sum_t f_{tj}=q_j\quad(j\in G),\qquad f_{tj}=0\quad\text{if }u_{tj}\ne1.\)

The feasible set is nonempty: each goods category has a positively valuing type. It is compact because \(0\le f_{tj}\le q_j\). The objective has a rational positive semidefinite Hessian, since its quadratic form is a sum of nonnegative squares divided by positive \(\mu_t\).

We use the standard exact polynomial-time solvability theorem for rational convex quadratic programming: a rational minimizer can be computed in polynomial time when the rational quadratic objective is convex, the constraints are linear, and a minimum exists. These hypotheses hold here. See [Kozlov–Tarasov–Khachiyan, *The polynomial solvability of convex quadratic programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5189).

Choose such a minimizer and define the per-capita goods totals

\(\displaystyle y_t=\frac{\sum_{j\in G}f_{tj}}{\mu_t}.\)

The following comparison is the key property:

\(\boxed{f_{sj}>0\ \text{and}\ u_{tj}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_s\le y_t.}\)

To prove it, suppose instead that \(y_s>y_t\). Transfer an amount \(\varepsilon>0\) of category \(j\) from type \(s\) to type \(t\). For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\), the modified assignment remains feasible. Its objective change is

\(\displaystyle 2\varepsilon(y_t-y_s)+\varepsilon^2\left(\frac1{\mu_t}+\frac1{\mu_s}\right),\)

which is negative for sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), contradicting optimality.

If \(G\) is empty, use \(y_t=0\) and empty goods bundles throughout.

Apply the rounding lemma separately to each type’s vector

\(\displaystyle v^t_j=\frac{f_{tj}}{\mu_t}\quad(j\in G).\)

Multiply its probability weights by \(\mu_t\). This gives goods cohorts with the correct type masses and supplies. Every goods item is held by a positively valuing type, and a type-\(t\) cohort’s goods count \(a\) belongs to

\(\{\lfloor y_t\rfloor,\lceil y_t\rceil\}.\)

For two goods cohorts \((t,k)\) and \((s,\ell)\), let their goods counts be \(a\) and \(a'\). If \(\ell_j>0\) for some \(j\in G\) with \(u_{tj}=1\), then \(f_{sj}>0\), so \(y_s\le y_t\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle a'\le\lceil y_s\rceil\le\lfloor y_t\rfloor+1\le a+1.\)

If \(\ell\) contains no goods positively valued by \(t\), then \(U_t^G(\ell)\le0\). In either case,

\(\boxed{U_t^G(\ell)\le a+1=U_t^G(k)+1.}\)

3. Assign chores and zero-valued categories

Let

\(\displaystyle Q_B=\sum_{j\in B}q_j,\qquad d=\lfloor Q_B\rfloor,\qquad \theta=Q_B-d.\)

The population has total mass one. Sort the goods cohorts by decreasing goods count. Assign a chore count of \(d+1\) to a prefix of total mass \(\theta\), and a count of \(d\) to the remaining population. Split the cohort crossing the prefix boundary if necessary.

Writing \(b_i\) for cohort \(i\)’s chore count and \(a_i\) for its goods count, this ensures

\(\displaystyle b_i\in\{d,d+1\},\qquad \sum_i w_i b_i=Q_B,\)

and, crucially,

\(\boxed{b_i=d+1,\ b_h=d\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_i\ge a_h.}\)

If \(Q_B>0\), refine a cohort with prescribed count \(b_i\) by applying the rounding lemma to

\(\displaystyle v^i_j=b_i\frac{q_j}{Q_B}\quad(j\in B).\)

Its total is the integer \(b_i\), so every resulting bundle has exactly that chore count. Aggregate category supplies are correct because

\(\displaystyle \sum_i w_i v^i_j=\frac{q_j}{Q_B}\sum_iw_i b_i=q_j.\)

If \(Q_B=0\), all chore coordinates are zero.

Finally, for each \(j\in D\), choose a type \(t(j)\) with \(u_{t(j)j}=0\). For every existing cohort of type \(t\), round the vector

\(\displaystyle v^t_j=\begin{cases}q_j/\mu_t,&t=t(j),\\0,&t\ne t(j),\end{cases}\qquad j\in D.\)

Refine that cohort using the resulting probability distribution. The aggregate supply of category \(j\) is \(\mu_{t(j)}q_j/\mu_{t(j)}=q_j\). All assigned categories in \(D\) have utility zero for their recipients.

These refinements preserve goods and chore counts. We now have a finite feasible allocation.

4. Verify EFX3

We prove the stronger statement that for every ordered pair of positive cohorts and each required set,

\(\boxed{U_t^S(k)+1\ge U_t^S(\ell)\qquad(S\in\{[m],G,B\}).}\)

Because every nonzero utility coefficient is either \(1\) or \(-1\), this statement implies every triggered EFX3 inequality.

Fix cohorts \((t,k)\) and \((s,\ell)\). Let their goods counts be \(a,a'\), and their chore counts be \(b,b'\). Put \(e=U_t^G(\ell)\).

By construction,

\(\displaystyle U_t^G(k)=a,\qquad e\le a',\qquad e\le a+1.\)

The goods inequality was proved in Section 2.

For chores, every type values every category in \(B\) at \(-1\), so

\(\displaystyle U_t^B(k)=-b,\qquad U_t^B(\ell)=-b'.\)

Since the two counts differ by at most one, \(-b+1\ge-b'\).

For all categories together, the recipient’s categories in \(D\) contribute zero, whereas another cohort’s categories in \(D\) contribute at most zero. Hence

\(\displaystyle U_t(k)=a-b,\qquad U_t(\ell)\le e-b'.\)

There are two cases.

  • If \(b\le b'\), then \(U_t(\ell)\le a+1-b'\le a-b+1\).
  • If \(b>b'\), then \(b=b'+1\). The prefix rule gives \(a\ge a'\), so \(U_t(\ell)\le a'-b'\le a-b+1\).

Thus all three utility-gap inequalities hold, proving EFX3.

5. Verify Pareto optimality, including countable couplings

Define \(s_j=\max_{t\in T}u_{tj}\). Thus \(s_j=1\) on \(G\), \(s_j=-1\) on \(B\), and \(s_j=0\) on \(D\).

Every assigned item attains its category’s maximum utility. Consequently, the allocation’s total utility is

\(\displaystyle W=\sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}U_t(k)=\sum_j s_jq_j.\)

Consider any coupling satisfying the row and supply constraints in the question. Since \(u_{tj}\le s_j\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,k,h}\gamma_{t,k,h}U_t(h)\le\sum_j s_jq_j=W.\)

This calculation remains valid for countably supported couplings. Indeed,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,k,h}\gamma_{t,k,h}|U_t(h)|\le\sum_{t,k,h}\gamma_{t,k,h}\sum_jh_j=\sum_jq_j<\infty,\)

so the utility sums are absolutely convergent. The row constraints also give

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,k,h}\gamma_{t,k,h}U_t(k)=W.\)

If the coupling weakly improved every individual, every difference \(U_t(h)-U_t(k)\) on its support would be nonnegative. A strictly positive difference is an integer and therefore at least one. Positive mass of strict improvements would consequently imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,k,h}\gamma_{t,k,h}\bigl(U_t(h)-U_t(k)\bigr)>0,\)

contradicting the preceding upper bound. The constructed allocation is Pareto optimal under the exact definition in the question.

6. Polynomial running time and sparse output

The quadratic program has at most \(\tau m\) variables and polynomial encoding length. Its exact rational optimizer has polynomial encoding length.

Goods rounding creates at most \(\tau(m+2)\) cohorts. The chore-prefix step splits at most one cohort. Chore and zero-valued-category rounding each multiply the cohort count by at most \(m+2\). Thus the total number of generated cohorts is polynomial.

All rounding operations use endpoint arithmetic and sorting; they never enumerate a binary-encoded number of items. The rational vectors, breakpoint weights, bundle coordinates, and products of refinement weights all have polynomial encoding length.

One can further reduce the output to at most \(m+\tau\) cohorts. On the finite set of constructed cohorts, solve the linear feasibility system consisting of the \(\tau\) type-mass equations and the \(m\) supply equations, and compute a basic feasible solution. It has at most \(m+\tau\) positive variables. This is a rational linear-programming computation of polynomial size.

Removing cohorts preserves EFX3. Every retained cohort still assigns items only to types attaining their category’s maximum utility, so the Pareto-optimality proof also remains valid.

Therefore existence is always answered affirmatively, and a finite rational EFX3 and Pareto-optimal allocation can be output in polynomial time.
