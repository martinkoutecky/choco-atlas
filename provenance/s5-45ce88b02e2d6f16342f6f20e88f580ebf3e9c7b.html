Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supports \(T_F\) and \(T_V\) of function-slot and value-demand types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) and \(\nu_s\), and complete weak preference rankings that include being unmatched, find a rational assignment \(x_{ts}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\nu_s\), or report that none exists. Require individual rationality: \(x_{ts}>0\) only when each type weakly prefers its match to being unmatched. For each pair \((t,s)\), let \(A_{ts}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to types it strictly prefers less than \(s\), plus its unmatched mass if it strictly prefers \(s\) to being unmatched; define \(B_{ts}\) symmetrically for type \(s\). Require \(A_{ts}B_{ts}=0\) for every pair, so no positive mass on both sides can form a blocking match.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T_F,T_V\) be disjoint finite supports of function-slot and value-demand types. Each type has a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) or \(\nu_s\); totals need not coincide or equal one. A society is given by its support, and \(\tau=|T_F|+|T_V|\) counts positive-mass types, not possible types. Let \(m=\max\{|T_F|,|T_V|\}\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has an explicitly given complete weak ranking of all opposite-side types and the unmatched outcome \(\bot\); ties, including ties with \(\bot\), are allowed.
Find a rational assignment \(x_{ts}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\nu_s\), or report nonexistence. Require \(x_{ts}>0\) only if \(s\succeq_t\bot\) and \(t\succeq_s\bot\).
Write \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_s x_{ts}\), \(w_s=\nu_s-\sum_t x_{ts}\), and define
\(A_{ts}=\sum_{r\in T_V:\,s\succ_t r}x_{tr}+\mathbf1_{s\succ_t\bot}u_t\), \(B_{ts}=\sum_{q\in T_F:\,t\succ_s q}x_{qs}+\mathbf1_{t\succ_s\bot}w_s\).
Require \(A_{ts}B_{ts}=0\) for every \((t,s)\in T_F\times T_V\), including pairs already assigned positive mass.

Is finding such an assignment, or reporting nonexistence, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite one-to-one markets with weak preferences, extended TTC is proved to terminate and return an individually rational matching with no pair that strictly improves both partners; no polynomial running-time bound is stated. [Discrete stability result](https://doi.org/10.1299/transjsme.23-00149)
For divisible allocations with strict preferences and real capacities, a strongly polynomial algorithm is known; this result does not explicitly treat the weak rankings specified here. [Stable allocation result, corrected version](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302)

  • The size parameters were unspecified: tau is the combined positive-mass support size, m is the larger side's support size, and L is the total encoded input length.

  • No tie-breaking rule is imposed: blocking requires strict improvement on both sides, exactly as in the discrete stability definition.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Moreover, a rational assignment always exists.

The proof uses the corrected strict-preference stable-allocation algorithm after breaking ties. Two details require care: pairs involving an outcome tied with \(\bot\), and the requirement to check stability even on pairs carrying positive mass.

1. Pairs that can be omitted

Define the set of mutually strictly acceptable pairs by

\( \displaystyle E=\{(t,s):s\succ_t\bot\text{ and }t\succ_s\bot\}. \)

We will construct an assignment supported entirely on \(E\). This satisfies individual rationality, which only requires weak acceptability.

For any feasible assignment supported on \(E\), consider a pair outside \(E\). Suppose first that \(s\not\succ_t\bot\). Completeness of the weak ranking gives \(\bot\succeq_t s\). Every \(r\) with \(x_{tr}>0\) satisfies \(r\succ_t\bot\), and consequently \(r\succ_t s\). Thus no positive term appears in the sum defining \(A_{ts}\). Its unmatched term also vanishes because \(s\not\succ_t\bot\). Therefore \(A_{ts}=0\).

If instead \(t\not\succ_s\bot\), the symmetric argument gives \(B_{ts}=0\).

Hence:

Every feasible assignment supported on \(E\) already satisfies \(A_{ts}B_{ts}=0\) for every pair outside \(E\), including pairs tied with \(\bot\).

If either support is empty, the zero assignment solves the problem. Below assume both supports are nonempty.

2. The strict-preference theorem used

We use the following form of the Baïou–Balinski stable-allocation result.

Let \(I,J\) be finite sets, with strict rankings of opposite-side agents, positive vertex capacities \(a_i,b_j\), and nonnegative edge capacities \(c_{ij}\). Their inductive algorithm computes, using polynomially many arithmetic operations and comparisons, an allocation \(y\) satisfying the vertex and edge bounds and, for every pair,

\( \displaystyle y_{ij}<c_{ij} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{k:\,k\succeq'_i j}y_{ik}=a_i \ \text{or}\ \sum_{h:\,h\succeq'_j i}y_{hj}=b_j. \)

Here \(\succeq'\) is the reflexive closure of the strict ranking. Zero edge capacities are permitted, and the implication applies also when \(y_{ij}>0\). This is condition (4), together with the inductive algorithm and Theorem 2, in the [corrected paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

3. Construction

Write the input masses as binary-encoded fractions. Let \(D\) be the product of their denominators, and set

\( \displaystyle a_t=D\mu_t,\qquad b_s=D\nu_s. \)

These are positive integers. Define

\( \displaystyle C=1+\sum_t a_t+\sum_s b_s. \)

For each type, break ties among opposite-side types arbitrarily, preserving every original strict comparison. Denote the resulting strict rankings by \(\succ'\).

Construct a strict stable-allocation instance on the original supports, with vertex capacities \(a_t,b_s\) and edge capacities

\( \displaystyle c_{ts}= \begin{cases} C,&(t,s)\in E,\\ 0,&(t,s)\notin E. \end{cases} \)

All hypotheses of the theorem hold: the rankings are strict and complete, vertex capacities are positive, and edge capacities are nonnegative.

Run its inductive algorithm, obtaining \(y\), and return

\( \displaystyle x_{ts}=y_{ts}/D. \)

The capacity inequalities hold after division by \(D\). Zero-capacity edges ensure that \(x\) is supported on \(E\), so individual rationality holds.

For every \((t,s)\in E\), feasibility gives

\( \displaystyle y_{ts}\leq a_t<C=c_{ts}. \)

Consequently, the theorem’s stability implication applies to every retained pair, regardless of whether it carries positive mass. After dividing its conclusion by \(D\), we obtain

\[ \displaystyle \sum_{r:\,r\succeq'_t s}x_{tr}=\mu_t \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_{q:\,q\succeq'_s t}x_{qs}=\nu_s. \tag{1} \]

4. Proof of the required blocking condition

Fix a retained pair \((t,s)\in E\). Define the corresponding quantities for the refined rankings by

\( \displaystyle \widetilde A_{ts} =u_t+\sum_{r:\,s\succ'_t r}x_{tr}, \qquad \widetilde B_{ts} =w_s+\sum_{q:\,t\succ'_s q}x_{qs}. \)

Because each refined ranking is strict and complete, its upper and lower sets partition the opposite support. Therefore

\( \displaystyle \widetilde A_{ts} =\mu_t-\sum_{r:\,r\succeq'_t s}x_{tr}, \qquad \widetilde B_{ts} =\nu_s-\sum_{q:\,q\succeq'_s t}x_{qs}. \)

Equation (1) implies that \(\widetilde A_{ts}=0\) or \(\widetilde B_{ts}=0\).

Tie-breaking preserves every strict comparison in the original ranking. Thus

\( \displaystyle \{r:s\succ_t r\}\subseteq\{r:s\succ'_t r\}, \qquad \{q:t\succ_s q\}\subseteq\{q:t\succ'_s q\}. \)

Furthermore, both unmatched indicators in the original definitions equal one because \((t,s)\in E\). Nonnegativity now gives

\( \displaystyle 0\leq A_{ts}\leq\widetilde A_{ts}, \qquad 0\leq B_{ts}\leq\widetilde B_{ts}. \)

Hence \(A_{ts}=0\) or \(B_{ts}=0\), proving \(A_{ts}B_{ts}=0\).

Section 1 proves the same condition for every omitted pair. The returned assignment therefore satisfies all requirements.

5. Rational output and bit complexity

The binary length of \(D\) is bounded by the sum of the input denominator lengths. Thus \(D\), all scaled vertex capacities, and \(C\) have \(O(L)\) bits. Constructing the rankings and edge capacities takes polynomial time, and the instance has \(\tau\) vertices and at most \(m^2\) edges.

The inductive algorithm’s numerical updates use sums, differences, minima, and comparisons, starting from zero allocations. Consequently, integer data produce integer allocations. These operations are explicit in the algorithm on pages 668–670 of the [corrected paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

For completeness, let \(Q\) bound the number of elementary numerical operations; \(Q\) is polynomial in the instance dimensions. Each addition or subtraction increases the maximum integer bit length by at most one, while minima and comparisons do not increase it. Starting with \(O(L)\)-bit integers, all intermediate numbers therefore have \(O(L+Q)\) bits. Exact execution has polynomial bit complexity.

Finally, \(x_{ts}=y_{ts}/D\) is rational with polynomial encoding length. No expansion into mass-sized populations is required, and unequal total masses cause no difficulty because the vertex constraints are inequalities.

Thus the construction is a polynomial-time search algorithm, and nonexistence never occurs under the stated hypotheses.
