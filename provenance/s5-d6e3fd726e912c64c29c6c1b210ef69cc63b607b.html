Theorem 3.6 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite item-class set \(M\), graph-valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita supply \(\lambda\) for each class, take compatible populations of \(N\) buyers and \(\lambda N\) copies of each class. Order the copies uniformly at random and assign each to a uniformly selected eligible buyer with maximum current marginal value, where a buyer may receive at most one copy of each class. Compute the limiting expected type-bundle masses \(x_{t,S}\) and normalized welfare as \(N\to\infty\), and determine the sharp worst-case ratio to the mass-allocation optimum \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\), including whether welfare is always at least \(4/7\,\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\); give the running time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) be a finite set of \(m\) item classes. Each type \(t\) has an explicitly given simple undirected unweighted graph \(G_t=(M,E_t)\), with valuation \(v_t(S)=|\{e\in E_t:e\cap S\ne\varnothing\}|\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Fix a positive binary-rational supply \(\lambda\), common to all classes. For each compatible integer \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) buyers of type \(t\) and \(\lambda N\) distinguishable copies of each class; compatibility means these quantities are integers.
Start all buyers with empty bundles and process a uniformly random permutation of all copies. For a copy of class \(j\), eligible buyers are those whose bundle \(S\) excludes \(j\); choose uniformly among eligible buyers maximizing \(v_t(S\cup\{j\})-v_t(S)\), including when this maximum is zero. Discard the copy only if no buyer is eligible.
Let \(X^{(N)}_{t,S}\) count type-\(t\) buyers ending with exactly \(S\subseteq M\). Define the candidate limits \(x_{t,S}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E[X^{(N)}_{t,S}]/N\) along compatible integers, and \(W=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).
Define \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda=\max_y\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(y_{t,S}\ge0\), \(\sum_Sy_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}y_{t,S}\le\lambda\) for every \(j\in M\). Ratios are taken only when \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda>0\).

Do the limits \(x_{t,S}\) exist, and how can they and \(W\) be computed to additive accuracy \(\varepsilon\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1)\), with running time stated in \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon\)?
What is the sharp worst-case ratio \(\inf W/\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\) over these inputs; in particular, is \(W\ge\frac47\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\) always true?

For finite buyers and distinct single-copy items, random-order greedy with vertex-cover valuations guarantees expected welfare at least \(\frac47\mathrm{OPT}\). A discrete family with priority tie-breaking has ratio at most \(\frac{177}{240}+o(1)\). [Dobzinski–Mor](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.02178)
No result settling this exact mass model was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted graph valuations as explicit simple undirected unweighted graphs on the item classes, with value equal to the number of incident edges covered.
  • Normalized positive type masses to sum to one, so N is the total buyer population and bundle masses and welfare are per buyer.
  • Allowed any positive rational common supply lambda; discarded copies only when no eligible buyer exists, covering supplies exceeding one.
  • Specified that zero-marginal copies are still allocated when eligible buyers exist, and that uniform tie-breaking is over individual buyers.
  • Defined compatible populations by integrality of all type counts and copy counts, and took limits along those populations.
  • Defined the mass-allocation optimum using bundle masses, type-mass equalities, and per-class supply inequalities; restricted welfare ratios to positive optimum.
  • Made numerical computation precise through an additive rational accuracy parameter epsilon, without presuming the limits have rational exact representations.

1. Result

The limits exist. Moreover, the sharp worst-case ratio in this mass model is
\(1-e^{-1}\).

In particular,
\(W\ge (1-e^{-1})\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda>\frac47\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\)
whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda>0\).

Below I prove existence, give a certified approximation algorithm with an explicit—very conservative—running-time bound, and construct instances approaching the stated ratio.

The proof is independent of the distinct-item theorem of [Dobzinski–Mor](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.02178). That theorem does not directly apply after duplicating classes: an original graph edge then corresponds to a coverage set containing every copy of both endpoint classes.

2. The fluid system

Assume first that \(m\ge1\) and \(0<\lambda<1\). Use time \(u\in[0,\lambda]\), scaled so that each class arrives at rate \(1\).

For an eligible state, define its marginal by
\(d_t(j,S)=|\{k:\{j,k\}\in E_t,\ k\notin S\}|\), for \(j\notin S\).
Set \(d_t(j,S)=0\) when \(j\in S\).

The important monotonicity property is the following: when a bundle grows, its marginal for any still-eligible class cannot increase.

Let \(z_{t,S}(u)\) denote fluid buyer masses, initially
\(z_{t,\varnothing}(0)=\mu_t\), with all other coordinates zero.

At a state \(z\), let
\(h_j=\max\{d_t(j,S):z_{t,S}>0,\ j\notin S\}\).
Write
\(B_j(h_j)=\{(t,S):j\notin S,\ d_t(j,S)=h_j\}\)
and
\(q_j(z)=\sum_{(t,S)\in B_j(h_j)}z_{t,S}\).

On an interval where the vector \(h=(h_j)_{j\in M}\) is fixed, the system is

\(z'(u)=\sum_{j\in M}F_j(z(u))\),

where

\(F_j(z)=\sum_{(t,S)\in B_j(h_j)}\frac{z_{t,S}}{q_j(z)} \bigl(\mathbf e_{t,S\cup\{j\}}-\mathbf e_{t,S}\bigr)\).

Here \(\mathbf e_{t,S}\) is the coordinate unit vector. Thus class \(j\)'s unit arrival rate is distributed proportionally among the maximizing buyer states, exactly as required by uniform buyer tie-breaking.

Existence through phase boundaries

While all denominators are positive, the vector field is locally Lipschitz. Its solution preserves nonnegativity and each type's total mass. It also satisfies
\(\|z'(u)\|_1\le2m\).

Fix a current maximizing band \(B_j(h_j)\). No transition from outside that band can enter it:

  • assigning \(j\) makes the buyer ineligible for \(j\);
  • assigning another class cannot increase the marginal for \(j\);
  • states with a greater marginal have zero mass throughout the phase.

Consequently,

\(-m\le q_j'(u)\le-1\).

The contribution \(-1\) comes from class \(j\)'s own assignments; other assignments can only remove further mass from its maximizing band.

The solution therefore extends continuously to the first vanishing denominator. At that time, recompute the maximizing levels from the positive coordinates and continue. Every vanishing band causes its class's maximizing level to decrease strictly, and an exhausted level can never reappear.

Each level belongs to \(\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\). Hence there are at most
\(P=m(m+1)+1\)
phases; this is a convenient loose bound.

There is always an eligible buyer mass before time \(1\). Indeed, class \(j\)'s assignments increase the mass holding \(j\) at rate \(1\), so its eligible mass is \(1-u\). Thus the construction is well defined on all of \([0,\lambda]\).

These arguments also prove uniqueness: each phase has a unique ordinary differential equation solution, and the continuation rule at its endpoint is determined by that endpoint.

3. Convergence of the finite populations

Let \(Z^{(N)}(u)\) be the normalized finite-population state vector after \(\lfloor mNu\rfloor\) copies, with linear interpolation between consecutive copies. Let \(A_j^{(N)}(u)\) be the number of processed class-\(j\) copies divided by \(N\), interpolated in the same way.

For each fixed class,

\(\sup_{0\le u\le\lambda}|A_j^{(N)}(u)-u|\longrightarrow0\)

in probability. To justify this directly, at any processing index the count is hypergeometric, has the correct mean, and has variance at most \(mN\). After normalization its variance is at most \(m/N\). Apply Chebyshev's inequality on a fixed finite time grid and then use the \(m\)-Lipschitz interpolation to obtain uniform convergence.

Conditional on the current state and the next class, the expected normalized state increment is \(F_j(Z^{(N)})/N\), using the finite population's current maximizing band. Subtracting these conditional expectations gives a martingale. Every coordinate increment has absolute value at most \(2/N\), and there are at most \(mN\) increments. Doob's inequality therefore gives, for each coordinate, a supremum second-moment bound of \(16m/N\). Since the number of coordinates \(\tau2^m\) is fixed for a fixed input, the vector martingale converges uniformly to zero in probability.

It remains to justify passage through the singular phase boundaries.

Phase-boundary argument

Suppose convergence has been established at the beginning \(a\) of a fluid phase with maximizing levels \(h_j\).

For each class \(j\), the finite mass with marginal greater than \(h_j\) tends to zero: that mass is zero in the fluid state at \(a\). Its membership can only decrease. Whenever a class-\(j\) copy arrives while this mass is positive, the copy removes one buyer from it. The uniform convergence of \(A_j^{(N)}\) therefore implies that all such exceptional higher bands disappear after a time tending to zero.

After this initial clearing interval, fix \(\eta>0\) smaller than every current fluid denominator at \(a\). Up to the first time a fluid denominator reaches \(\eta\), the relevant vector fields are Lipschitz on denominators bounded below by \(\eta/2\). The arrival-clock convergence, the vanishing martingale, and the interpolation error then give convergence by Grönwall's inequality. A finite maximizing band cannot disappear earlier on this interval: that would require a discrepancy of at least \(\eta\) from the fluid state.

If a fluid denominator reaches \(\eta\), the fluid phase ends within a further time \(\eta\), because its derivative is at most \(-1\). Both interpolated state trajectories are \(2m\)-Lipschitz. Their discrepancy at the phase endpoint is consequently at most \(4m\eta+o_{\mathbb P}(1)\).

Letting \(\eta\) decrease to zero establishes convergence at that endpoint. Induction over the finitely many phases proves

\(Z^{(N)}(\lambda)\longrightarrow z(\lambda)\)

in probability. The coordinates are bounded by \(1\), so their expectations converge as well. Therefore,

\(x_{t,S}=z_{t,S}(\lambda)\).

If \(\lambda\ge1\), the answer is simpler: each class has at least \(N\) copies, and its first \(N\) copies go to distinct eligible buyers. Every buyer finishes with \(M\), exactly, for every compatible \(N\). Thus \(x_{t,M}=\mu_t\), with all other coordinates zero. If \(m=0\), every buyer retains the empty bundle.

4. Certified computation and running time

Here is an entirely rational approximation algorithm. Its purpose is to give a rigorous computability and complexity bound; the bound is not intended to be practical.

Let

\(D=\tau2^m,\qquad H=\max\{1,\binom m2\},\qquad p=4m+2,\qquad P=m(m+1)+1\),

and set

\(B=2^{100(m+1)^2},\qquad A=4B^2,\qquad C=p^P\).

Let \(H_0\) be the smallest power of two at least \(H\), and let
\(r=\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\rceil\).
Choose

\(R=(A H_0\,2^r)^C,\qquad h=\lambda/R\).

For \(\lambda<1\), perform \(R\) rounds. In each round, process every class once, giving it allocation amount \(h\):

  • Find its highest marginal among positive eligible state masses.
  • Let \(q\) be the total mass in that band.
  • Transfer amount \(\min\{h_{\mathrm{remaining}},q\}\) proportionally from those states to their bundles with this class added.
  • If the band is exhausted and allocation amount remains, continue with the next band.

All operations are exact rational operations. Denote the resulting vector by \(\widehat x\), and output
\(\widehat W=\sum_{t,S}\widehat x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

Quantitative stability

I supply the error estimate, including its treatment of vanishing bands.

Interpret the algorithm as a continuous curve \(y(u)\): during each class's subinterval, that class's clock increases at rate \(m\), and the others are stationary. Its clocks \(A_j(u)\) satisfy
\(\sup_u|A_j(u)-u|\le h\).
Both \(y\) and \(z\) are \(2m\)-Lipschitz.

Suppose their distance at the beginning of a fluid phase is \(e\). The approximate mass in bands above the fluid levels is at most \(e\) for each class. These bands are cleared within
\(\Delta=e+2h\),
because each class's clock advances by at least the elapsed time minus \(2h\). During clearing, the discrepancy grows to at most

\(E\le e+4m\Delta\le(1+12m)(e+h)\).

If the phase ends during clearing, this already bounds its endpoint discrepancy.

Otherwise, compare the two trajectories after clearing, while every fluid denominator is at least \(\eta\) and their modes agree. Normalizing a band gives

\(\|F_j(y)-F_j(z)\|_1\le\frac{4\|y-z\|_1}{q_j(z)}\).

Furthermore, because \(-m\le q_j'\le-1\),

\(\int\frac{du}{q_j(z(u))}\le\log(1/\eta)\).

Along the fluid solution, the total variation of \(F_j(z(u))\) on this interval is at most \(6m\log(1/\eta)\): differentiate the normalized band vector, using \(\|z'\|_1\le2m\) and \(|q_j'|\le m\), and then use the preceding integral bound.

Integration by parts against \(A_j(u)-u\) therefore bounds the total clock error by

\(4mh+12m^2h\log(1/\eta)\).

The same integration by parts, applied to the increasing function \(1/q_j(z(u))\), gives

\(\int\frac{dA_j(u)}{q_j(z(u))} \le\log(1/\eta)+4h/\eta\).

Grönwall's inequality consequently bounds the discrepancy before any fluid denominator reaches \(\eta\) by

\(\bigl(E+4mh+12m^2h\log(1/\eta)\bigr) \eta^{-4m}e^{16mh/\eta}\).

Put
\(K_m=100(m+1)^2e^{16(m+1)}\).
When \(\eta\le1\) and \(\eta\ge h\), the last bound is at most

\(K_m(e+h)\eta^{-(4m+1)}\).

Choose \(\eta=(2K_m(e+h))^{1/p}\), when this is below \(1\). The discrepancy before reaching that threshold is at most \(\eta/2\). This also justifies the assumed agreement of modes: an approximate band could not vanish while the corresponding fluid band had mass at least \(\eta\).

The remaining fluid phase lasts at most \(\eta\). Hence its endpoint discrepancy is at most \((4m+1)\eta\). If the chosen threshold is at least \(1\), use the trivial distance bound \(2\) instead. The explicit constant \(B\) above dominates these constants in both cases. Thus, phase by phase,

\(e_{\mathrm{next}}\le B(e+h)^{1/p}\).

Starting with zero error and iterating over at most \(P\) phases gives

\(\|\widehat x-x\|_1\le A h^{1/C}\).

Indeed, \(A=4B^2\) satisfies \(B(A+1)^{1/p}\le A\), so the estimate follows inductively. Since \(\lambda<1\) and \(h\le1/R\), our choice of \(R\) yields

\(\|\widehat x-x\|_1\le\frac{2^{-r}}{H_0}\le\frac{\varepsilon}{H}\).

Every coordinate is therefore accurate to \(\varepsilon\), and, since \(v_t(S)\le H\),

\(|\widehat W-W|\le\varepsilon\).

Bit complexity

A class block crosses at most \(m\) marginal levels, so there are at most
\(T=m(m+1)R\)
band transfers.

Store all masses with a common integer denominator. One transfer increases its bit length by at most a factor \(4\), plus \(O(L+\log R+\log D)\): the maximizing-band mass has numerator no larger than the current common denominator, and the remaining allocation amount has a denominator dividing the product of that denominator and the fixed denominator of \(h\).

Thus ordinary exact integer arithmetic gives the explicit bound

\(\operatorname{poly}(\tau2^m,m,L+\ell_\varepsilon+\log R)\, 2^{O(m(m+1)R)}\)

bit operations, where \(\ell_\varepsilon\) is the encoding length of \(\varepsilon\). If the accuracy input is included in \(L\), omit the separate \(\ell_\varepsilon\).

For \(\lambda\ge1\), the exact answer described above requires only reading the graphs and writing the output.

5. The universal welfare bound

Write
\(w(u)=\sum_{t,S}z_{t,S}(u)v_t(S)\).
Within every phase,

\(w'(u)=\sum_{j\in M}h_j(u)\).

Let \(y\) be an optimal solution to the configuration program. Graph-cover valuations are monotone and submodular. Therefore, for every pair of bundles \(S,B\),

\(v_t(B)\le v_t(S\cup B) \le v_t(S)+\sum_{j\in B}d_t(j,S)\).

For every positive fluid state, \(d_t(j,S)\le h_j(u)\). This includes buyers already holding \(j\), whose marginal is zero.

For each type, average the preceding inequality over \(S\) with probabilities \(z_{t,S}(u)/\mu_t\), multiply by \(y_{t,B}\), and sum over types and optimal bundles. The supply constraints give

\(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda \le w(u)+\sum_j h_j(u)\sum_{t,B:j\in B}y_{t,B} \le w(u)+\lambda\sum_jh_j(u)\).

Consequently, almost everywhere,

\(w'(u)\ge\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda-w(u)}{\lambda}\).

Multiplying by \(e^{u/\lambda}\), integrating from \(0\) to \(\lambda\), and using \(w(0)=0\), gives

\(W=w(\lambda)\ge(1-e^{-1})\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\).

For \(\lambda\ge1\), every buyer receives every class, so \(W=\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\). This completes the lower bound for all supplies.

6. A family approaching the bound

The construction uses only simple unweighted graphs, uniform ties, and dyadic masses and supply.

Fix an integer \(k\ge16\), and put \(D_k=k-2\). Define integers recursively by

\(r_1=kD_k,\qquad d_i=\lceil r_i/k\rceil+1,\qquad r_{i+1}=r_i-d_i\),

for \(i=1,\ldots,k\).

Write \(a_k=1-1/k\). Then

\(r_{i+1}=a_k r_i-\theta_i,\qquad 1\le\theta_i<2\).

In particular,

\(r_i\ge a_k^{i-1}kD_k-2(i-1) \ge k(k-10)/4>0\).

Here \(a_k^k\ge1/4\) for \(k\ge2\). For example, this follows because \(x\log(1-1/x)\) increases for \(x>1\): its derivative is
\(\log(1-1/x)+1/(x-1)>0\).

It follows that all the degrees used below satisfy
\(2\le d_i\le k-1\), and the sequence \(d_i\) is nonincreasing.

The graph

Create \(k\) good vertices \(g_0,\ldots,g_{k-1}\) and \(k\) bad vertices \(b_1,\ldots,b_k\).

Let \(C_i=\sum_{\ell=1}^i d_\ell\), with \(C_0=0\). Connect \(b_i\) to the \(d_i\) good vertices whose indices are

\(C_{i-1},C_{i-1}+1,\ldots,C_i-1\pmod k\).

These neighbors are distinct because \(d_i<k\). After any prefix of bad vertices, the numbers of incident selected bad vertices at the good vertices differ by at most one.

Finally, attach private leaves to each good vertex until every good vertex has degree exactly \(D_k\). This is possible because
\(C_k=kD_k-r_{k+1}<kD_k\):
the balanced prefix counts are at most \(D_k\).

The resulting graph has:

  • good-vertex degrees \(D_k\);
  • bad-vertex degrees \(d_i\);
  • leaf degrees \(1\);
  • exactly \(kD_k\) edges.

Pad with isolated vertices so the total number \(m\) of vertices is a power of two, and identify the classes with the cyclic group \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\).

Take every cyclic translate of this graph as a type, each with mass \(1/m\), and set \(\lambda=k/m\). Identical translated graphs, if any, can be merged by adding their masses. All masses and the supply remain dyadic.

Why greedy chooses the bad vertices

Partition the bad vertices into consecutive groups of equal degree.

Suppose all previous groups have been selected, and the current group has degree \(d\). At its beginning, the maximum remaining marginal of a good vertex is

\(D_k-\lfloor C/k\rfloor=\lceil(kD_k-C)/k\rceil=d-1\),

where \(C\) is the number of edges incident to previously selected bad vertices. This equality uses the balanced prefix counts and the definition of the first degree in the current group.

Selecting any subset of the current bad group can only decrease good-vertex marginals. Unselected bad vertices in that group retain marginal \(d\), since no two bad vertices are adjacent. Later bad groups have smaller degrees, and leaves have degree \(1<d\).

Thus the only maximizing positive states during this phase assign vertices from the current bad group.

If the group has \(b\) vertices, then at the phase's start every class has maximizing eligible mass \(b/m\): across the cyclic translates, that class represents a vertex of the group in exactly \(b\) types.

Assignments of other classes do not change membership in this pool, because current-group bad vertices are mutually nonadjacent. Therefore every maximizing pool decreases at rate exactly \(1\), and the phase lasts \(b/m\). At its endpoint, every buyer has acquired the entire group.

Induction over the groups shows that at time

\(\lambda=k/m\),

every buyer's bundle consists precisely of the \(k\) bad vertices in that buyer's translated graph. Uniform tie-breaking does not affect this conclusion.

Hence

\(W=C_k=kD_k-r_{k+1}\).

The optimum and limiting ratio

Allocate all \(k\) good vertices to every type. Cyclic translation makes the load of every class exactly \(k/m=\lambda\). This allocation covers every graph edge, so

\(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda=kD_k\).

The recurrence gives

\(r_{k+1} =a_k^k kD_k-\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i a_k^{k-i}\).

Consequently,

\(\frac{W}{\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda} =1-a_k^k+ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k\theta_i a_k^{k-i}}{kD_k}\),

and the last term lies between zero and \(2/D_k\). Therefore,

\(\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{W}{\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda}=1-e^{-1}\).

Combined with the universal lower bound, this proves

\(\inf\frac{W}{\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda}=1-e^{-1}\).
