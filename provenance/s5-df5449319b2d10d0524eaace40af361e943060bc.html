The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a type-determined directed compatibility relation \(A\), a fixed cycle cap \(L\), and a rational target \(q\in[0,1]\), let \(\mathcal C_L\) be the directed cycle patterns of lengths \(2\) through \(L\), allowing repeated type labels when positions use distinct pairs. Is there a nonnegative cycle-mass vector \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C_L}a_t(C)x_C\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\) and \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C_L}|C|x_C\ge q\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) patient–donor pair types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input includes an explicitly listed directed compatibility relation \(A\subseteq T\times T\), with \(m=|A|\), and a binary-rational target \(q\in[0,1]\). Compatibility applies between distinct pairs; a diagonal entry permits compatibility between distinct pairs of the same type. There are no chains, and every transplant has unit value.
Fix an integer cycle cap \(\ell\ge3\). A cycle pattern is a cyclic sequence \(C=(t_1,\ldots,t_r)\), where \(2\le r\le\ell\) and \((t_i,t_{i+1})\in A\), with \(t_{r+1}=t_1\). Type labels may repeat, but positions represent distinct pairs. Patterns are identified up to cyclic rotation.
Let \(\mathcal C_\ell\) be all such patterns, \(|C|=r\), and \(a_t(C)=|\{i:t_i=t\}|\). A feasible allocation consists of real cycle masses \(x_C\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C_\ell}a_t(C)x_C\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\); unused mass is permitted.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and the fixed cap \(\ell\).

For each fixed \(\ell\ge3\), is deciding whether a feasible allocation satisfies \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C_\ell}|C|x_C\ge q\) solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For discrete pair populations, [Lin et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/a12020050) give a randomized algorithm with running time \(O^*(2^{k+\ell}(k+\ell)^2\ell^3n^\ell)\) for covering at least \(k\) of \(n\) vertices by disjoint cycles, hence randomized FPT in \(k\) for fixed \(\ell\).
[Biró, Manlove, and Rizzi](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25732/1/25732.pdf) prove discrete maximum-cardinality cycle packing APX-complete for every fixed \(\ell\ge3\), implying NP-hardness of its threshold decision problem. Result

For every fixed \(\ell\ge3\), the continuous threshold problem is deterministically solvable in polynomial time. The allocation variables are real, and the fixed cap permits explicit enumeration of all cycle patterns followed by rational linear-programming feasibility.

1. Enumerating all patterns

Index the explicitly supplied support as \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). For each \(r\in\{2,\ldots,\ell\}\), enumerate every tuple \((t_1,\ldots,t_r)\in T^r\), allowing repeated labels.

Retain a tuple precisely when all required arcs belong to \(A\), including \((t_r,t_1)\). To identify patterns up to cyclic rotation, retain only the lexicographically least tuple among its rotations. This rule produces exactly one representative of each pattern: every rotation class has a unique least tuple, even when several rotations produce that same tuple.

Consequently, writing \(N=|\mathcal C_\ell|\), we have

\[ N\le\sum_{r=2}^{\ell}\tau^r\le(\ell-1)\tau^\ell. \]

Thus \(N\) is polynomial in the support size for fixed \(\ell\). No population is expanded according to the denominators of its masses.

An adjacency table takes \(O(\tau^2+m)\) elementary operations to construct. Testing a tuple and comparing its rotations takes \(O(\ell^2)\) operations, so enumeration takes \(O(\tau^2+m+\ell^2\tau^\ell)\) elementary operations. The indices manipulated have polynomial bit length.

2. Exact linear-programming formulation

For each enumerated pattern \(C\), introduce one real variable \(x_C\). Consider the following feasibility problem:

\[ x_C\ge0\qquad(C\in\mathcal C_\ell), \]

\[ \sum_{C\in\mathcal C_\ell}a_t(C)x_C\le\mu_t \qquad(t\in T), \]

\[ -\sum_{C\in\mathcal C_\ell}|C|x_C\le-q. \]

This system is equivalent to the requested threshold decision:

  • A feasible allocation meeting the target satisfies all its inequalities.
  • A solution to these inequalities assigns nonnegative masses to exactly the allowed patterns, respects every type capacity, and meets the target.

Repeated labels are handled by their multiplicities \(a_t(C)\). In particular, if a pattern uses a type several times, its capacity constraint charges that many units per unit of cycle mass. Diagonal compatibility entries require no additional restriction under the stated model.

If \(N=0\), the answer is yes exactly when \(q=0\); hence this case is also decided directly.

3. Encoding length and running time

Every matrix coefficient in the capacity constraints is an integer between \(0\) and \(\ell\). Every coefficient in the threshold constraint has absolute value at most \(\ell\). Their binary encoding lengths are therefore \(O(\log(\ell+1))\).

The right-hand sides are the input rationals \(\mu_t\) and \(-q\), whose total encoding length is at most \(L\). With nonnegativity recorded as variable sign restrictions, the LP has \(N\) variables, \(\tau+1\) other inequalities, and dense encoding length bounded by

\[ S=O\!\left(L+(\tau+1)(N+1)\log(\ell+1)\right). \]

The rational linear-programming feasibility theorem provides a deterministic algorithm deciding feasibility over the reals in time polynomial in the total binary encoding length. It applies to arbitrary rational systems, including systems whose feasible region has empty interior. See the [MIT notes on polynomial-time linear programming](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

Its hypotheses hold here: the entire system is explicitly constructed, all coefficients are rational, and its encoding length is bounded as above. Thus the comparison with \(q\) is exact, including equality at the optimum.

Combining enumeration, construction, and LP feasibility yields the conservative bit-time bound

\[ \left(L+m+\ell^2(\tau+1)^{\ell+1}\log(\ell+1)\right)^{O(1)}, \]

where the outer exponent is an absolute constant. For every fixed \(\ell\), this is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

4. Why discrete hardness does not transfer

The discrete APX-completeness theorem concerns vertex-disjoint integral cycle packings. Its integrality requirement is absent here. This is the formulation studied by [Biró, Manlove, and Rizzi](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25732/1/25732.pdf).

The distinction can change the threshold answer for every fixed cap. Choose an odd integer \(s>\ell\) and a power of two \(D>s\). Use \(s\) types arranged in a bidirected ring, together with one isolated type. Give each ring type mass \(1/D\), and the isolated type mass \((D-s)/D\). All masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to \(1\).

For every ring edge, assign mass \(1/(2D)\) to its two-cycle. Each ring type belongs to exactly two such cycles, so its consumed mass is

\[ 2\cdot\frac1{2D}=\frac1D. \]

The resulting transplant mass is

\[ s\cdot2\cdot\frac1{2D}=\frac{s}{D}. \]

This is optimal: the isolated type appears in no pattern, so only the total ring mass \(s/D\) can be used.

Now consider the corresponding discrete population of \(D\) pairs: one of each ring type and \(D-s\) isolated pairs. Every simple directed cycle in the ring has length either \(2\) or \(s\). Indeed, after traversing one ring edge, a simple cycle of length greater than \(2\) cannot immediately reverse direction; each subsequent step is then forced around the entire ring. Since \(s>\ell\), only two-cycles are allowed.

A discrete packing therefore corresponds to a matching in an odd ring. It covers at most \(s-1\) vertices, and alternating edges attain that bound. Hence the continuous target \(q=s/D\) is achievable, while the corresponding discrete target of covering \(s\) pairs is not.

The continuous problem is therefore settled by the polynomial-size LP, independently of the discrete hardness result.
