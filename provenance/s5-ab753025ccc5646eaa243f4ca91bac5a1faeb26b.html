The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a bounded rational polygon \(P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2\), compute the exact Lebesgue area of \(P\cap D\), where \(D=\{(\alpha,q):0<\alpha<1,\ 0\le q<1-\alpha\}\). Here \(\alpha\) is the population mass of veto type \(A\), type \(B\) has mass \(1-\alpha\), and \(q\) is the threshold for \(B\): a coalition wins exactly when \(x_A=\alpha\) and \(x_B\ge q\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, with masses encoded as binary rationals; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Consider the normalized two-type family with masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). A coalition has divisible masses \(0\le x_A\le\alpha\) and \(0\le x_B\le1-\alpha\), and wins exactly when \(x_A=\alpha\) and \(x_B\ge q\).
The admissible parameter domain is \(D=\{(\alpha,q)\in\mathbb R^2:0<\alpha<1,\ 0\le q<1-\alpha\}\); zero threshold is allowed, including a null type \(B\). Area uses all real parameter points, extending the rationally encoded instances.
The input is a bounded simple polygon \(P\subseteq\mathbb R^2\), including its interior, given by its \(m\) boundary vertices in cyclic order with binary rational coordinates. Convexity is not required. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; here \(\tau=2\).

Is computing the exact Lebesgue area \(\lambda_2(P\cap D)\) possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computation NP-hard?

Discrete: up to isomorphism, the number of complete simple games with \(n\ge2\) voters, exactly two voter types, and veto players is \(n(n-1)/2\). [Freixas–Kurz](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.8180v1)

Result. Computing the exact area is possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for the stated family, where \(\tau=2\). The output is a rational number with polynomially bounded bit length. The proof below gives an exact algorithm for arbitrary simple polygons, including nonconvex ones.

1. Replace the domain by a closed triangle.

Define
\(T=\{(\alpha,q):0\le\alpha\le1,\ 0\le q\le1-\alpha\}\).

The given domain satisfies \(D\subseteq T\), and \(T\setminus D\) is contained in the boundary of \(T\). This boundary consists of three line segments, each of two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. Consequently,
\(\lambda_2(P\cap D)=\lambda_2(P\cap T)\).

Thus the strict inequalities do not affect the requested area. The allowed zero threshold causes no difficulty.

For the geometric argument, write \(x=\alpha\) and \(y=q\). The vertical section of \(T\) at \(x\in[0,1]\) is the interval \([0,1-x]\).

2. Construct finitely many vertical slabs.

Construct a set \(S\subseteq[0,1]\) containing:

  • \(0\) and \(1\);
  • every polygon vertex’s \(x\)-coordinate that lies in \([0,1]\);
  • the \(x\)-coordinate of every isolated intersection of a polygon edge with either line \(y=0\) or \(y=1-x\), provided that coordinate lies in \([0,1]\).

An edge contained in one of these lines requires no additional breakpoint: its coincidence with the line persists throughout the edge.

Each edge has at most one isolated intersection with each line. Therefore \(|S|\le3m+2\). Every breakpoint is rational and is computable by a constant number of rational arithmetic operations.

Sort and remove duplicates, obtaining
\(0=s_0<s_1<\cdots<s_r=1\).

We analyze each open slab \(I_k=(s_k,s_{k+1})\).

3. Describe the polygon’s sections within one slab.

Fix \(I_k\), and let \(c_k=(s_k+s_{k+1})/2\).

No polygon vertex has its \(x\)-coordinate in \(I_k\). Therefore:

  • No vertical polygon edge intersects a vertical line whose coordinate lies in \(I_k\).
  • Each nonvertical edge either intersects every such vertical line or intersects none of them.

Indeed, the vertical lines meeting a nonvertical edge have coordinates between its two endpoint coordinates, and neither endpoint coordinate lies inside the slab.

Call the edges in the first class *active*. An active edge with endpoints \((u_1,v_1)\) and \((u_2,v_2)\) has the affine height function
\(f_e(x)=v_1+\frac{v_2-v_1}{u_2-u_1}(x-u_1)\).

Sort the active edges by their heights at \(c_k\). Their order remains unchanged throughout \(I_k\): equality of two heights at an interior point would give an intersection of two polygon edges at their relative interiors, contradicting simplicity.

The number of active edges is even. To justify this and identify the interior intervals, apply the Jordan curve theorem to the polygon boundary, which is a simple closed polygonal curve by hypothesis. Every intersection under consideration occurs in the relative interior of a nonvertical edge, so the vertical line crosses the boundary transversely there. Each crossing switches between the bounded interior and the exterior. The line begins and ends outside the bounded polygon.

Hence, writing the ordered height functions as
\(f_1(x)<f_2(x)<\cdots<f_{2h}(x)\),
the section of \(P\), up to its boundary endpoints, is
\(\bigcup_{j=1}^{h}[f_{2j-1}(x),f_{2j}(x)]\).

This argument allows any number of disjoint section intervals and therefore does not require convexity.

4. Compute each slab’s area exactly.

Intersect each section interval with the triangle’s section \([0,1-x]\). Its resulting length is
\(\ell_j(x)=\max\{0,\ \min(f_{2j}(x),1-x)-\max(f_{2j-1}(x),0)\}\).

We now verify that \(\ell_j\) is affine throughout the open slab, including the possibility that it is identically zero.

Consider all active height functions together with \(0\) and \(1-x\). Their relative order cannot change inside \(I_k\):

  • Two active height functions cannot meet, by polygon simplicity.
  • An active height function can meet either clipping line only at an intersection whose coordinate was included in \(S\), unless the two functions coincide identically.
  • The functions \(0\) and \(1-x\) meet only at \(x=1\), outside every open slab.

Thus every minimum, maximum, and positivity choice in the formula for \(\ell_j\) can be determined at \(c_k\) and used throughout \(I_k\). Identically coincident functions may be chosen arbitrarily because they give the same affine expression.

Let \(g_{k,j}\) be this affine expression, or the zero function when the clipped interval has zero length. Evaluate its affine extension at the slab endpoints. Its exact integral is
\(\int_{s_k}^{s_{k+1}}g_{k,j}(x)\,dx =\frac{s_{k+1}-s_k}{2}\bigl(g_{k,j}(s_k)+g_{k,j}(s_{k+1})\bigr)\).

These endpoint values need not describe the actual polygon section on a breakpoint line. They describe the affine extension from the open slab, which suffices for integration.

Finally compute
\(\lambda_2(P\cap T) =\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{h_k} \frac{s_{k+1}-s_k}{2}\bigl(g_{k,j}(s_k)+g_{k,j}(s_{k+1})\bigr)\).

For completeness, this identity follows from Fubini’s theorem applied to the indicator of the bounded measurable set \(P\cap T\). Within each open slab, the clipped intervals have disjoint interiors, so their lengths add. The finitely many omitted vertical breakpoint lines have area zero.

Every quantity in this formula is rational, and the algorithm uses exact arithmetic throughout.

5. Bound the bit complexity.

There are \(O(m)\) slabs. In each slab, scanning all edges, sorting the active heights, selecting the clipping expressions, and integrating requires \(O(m\log(m+1))\) rational arithmetic operations and comparisons. The total is therefore \(O(m^2\log(m+1))\).

An operation count alone is insufficient for binary input, so we also bound operand sizes.

Every breakpoint, midpoint, affine coefficient, endpoint evaluation, and individual integrated contribution is obtained from a constant number of input rationals by a fixed number of additions, subtractions, multiplications, and divisions by nonzero rationals. Their numerator and denominator lengths are therefore \(O(L+1)\). Exact comparisons use integer cross multiplication on operands of this size.

There are \(N=O(m^2)\) integrated contributions. Write them as \(p_i/q_i\), with positive denominators. A common denominator is
\(Q=\prod_{i=1}^{N}q_i\),
whose bit length is \(O(N(L+1))\). The corresponding summed numerator also has polynomial bit length: it is a sum of \(N\) integers, each with \(O(N(L+1))\) bits. Sequential rational addition consequently keeps all accumulator sizes polynomial in \(m,L\).

Integer arithmetic on these operands takes polynomial time. If a reduced fraction is required, a final greatest-common-divisor computation also takes polynomial time.

This proves polynomial bit complexity, as well as polynomial output length.

6. Conclusion and relation to the cited results.

The exact area belongs to the class of polynomial-time computable functions. Since \(\tau=2\) throughout the specified family, the running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof directly handles fixed-position intersection with the admissible triangle. It does not require discrete game enumeration or the optimization-under-motion results described in [Venkat’s paper](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/ece_fac_articles/26/).

Under standard polynomial-time reductions, an NP-hardness classification for this computation would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
