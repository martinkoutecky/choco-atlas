The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(T\) of complete utility types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(ms\), \(m\) teams of mass \(s\), pairwise utilities \(u_{t,t'}\), and rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), compute an allocation maximizing \(\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\). Here \(x_{t,k}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to team \(k\), each unit of population belongs to one team, and \(R(x)\) is the greatest total uplift of any mass-\(s\) coalition whose members are all weakly better off after leaving their current teams. Determine the exact and approximation complexity of this optimization problem as a function of the input size and parameters.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(m,s\) be positive integers and \(\sum_t\mu_t=ms\); masses are not normalized to one.
Each type specifies its utility for every type: \(u_{t,t'}\) are binary-encoded nonnegative integers, possibly asymmetric, and \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is rational. The population is nonatomic; \(u_{t,t}\) measures utility from other members of the same type, while an individual's self-interaction has measure zero.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{t,k}\ge0\), with \(\sum_{k=1}^m x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,k}=s\). Define current per-unit utility \(v_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{t'}u_{t,t'}x_{t',k}\) and welfare \(W(x)=\sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}v_{t,k}(x)\).
A deviating coalition selects real masses \(0\le y_{t,k}\le x_{t,k}\) satisfying \(\sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}=s\). Write \(z_t=\sum_k y_{t,k}\) and \(b_t(z)=\sum_{t'}u_{t,t'}z_{t'}\); require \(y_{t,k}>0\Rightarrow b_t(z)\ge v_{t,k}(x)\).
Define \(R(x)=\max_y\sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}(b_t(z)-v_{t,k}(x))\) over these coalitions, and \(F(x)=\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\). There are no additional team constraints, transfers, or requirements to reorganize the population left behind.
For approximation, use additive error relative to \(F^*=\sup_x F(x)\), with rational \(\varepsilon>0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including any threshold and accuracy parameter; state running times in \(m,\tau,s,L\) and \(\varepsilon\).

Is deciding, for a rational threshold \(q\), whether a feasible allocation satisfies \(F(x)\ge q\) in P or NP-hard?
What is the polynomial-time additive approximability of maximizing \(F(x)\), as a function of \(\varepsilon\) and the input parameters?

Discrete team formation with this weighted welfare–uplift objective has a valid single-level binary integer formulation; its welfare-only case \(\alpha=1\) is NP-hard ([verified source](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/nav.22084)).
No result settling this exact nonatomic mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Retained positive integer team count and team size, nonnegative integer utilities, and possibly asymmetric utilities from the discrete definition.
  • Interpreted allocations as actual assignments of nonatomic individuals, allowing real mass splits rather than lotteries over an individual's team.
  • Defined utilities by unnormalized sums against teammate masses and welfare by integrating these per-unit utilities.
  • Allowed same-type utility to be nonzero: distinct individuals of the same type can value one another, while individual self-interaction has measure zero.
  • Tracked coalition members by both type and source team, so weak improvement is checked against each selected member's current utility rather than a type-wide average.
  • Used the unconstrained equal-size model and required only the deviating coalition to be feasible, without requiring a feasible rearrangement of those left behind.
  • Specified exact complexity through an attained rational-threshold decision question; used the supremum benchmark for approximation without assuming an optimal allocation exists.
  • Chose additive approximation because the weighted objective can be negative or zero and no approximation convention was supplied.

1. Result and interpretation

I interpret the decision question as asking whether there exists a feasible allocation \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge q\).

For the unrestricted input problem:

  • The decision problem is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with integer masses, utilities in \(\{0,1\}\), and the fixed weight \(\alpha=\tfrac12\).
  • Unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), maximizing \(F\) admits no polynomial-time additive approximation with any fixed positive error. In particular, there is no algorithm with running time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,s,L,1/\varepsilon)\) guaranteeing \(F(x)\ge F^*-\varepsilon\).
  • When both \(m\) and \(\tau\) are fixed, exact decision and additive approximation are polynomial-time solvable by real quantifier elimination. A conservative general running-time bound is given below.

The hardness proof concerns the nonatomic model directly.

2. Coalition feasibility and basic bounds

Let \(\mathcal X\) denote the set of feasible allocations. It is nonempty: \(x_{t,k}=\mu_t/m\) is feasible. It is also compact, because it is closed and every coordinate lies in \([0,\mu_t]\).

For fixed \(x\), define the coalition gain

\(G(x,y)=\sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}\bigl(b_t(z)-v_{t,k}(x)\bigr),\qquad z_t=\sum_k y_{t,k}.\)

Given \(y_{t,k}\ge0\), the individual-rationality condition is equivalent to the polynomial inequality

\(y_{t,k}\bigl(b_t(z)-v_{t,k}(x)\bigr)\ge0.\)

Indeed, when \(y_{t,k}>0\), division by \(y_{t,k}\) gives the required inequality; when \(y_{t,k}=0\), the condition imposes no restriction.

Consequently, the feasible coalition set is closed and bounded. It is nonempty: selecting any entire existing team gives a coalition of mass \(s\) whose members all have gain zero. Therefore the maximum defining \(R(x)\) exists, and

\(R(x)\ge0.\)

Write \(U=\max_{t,t'}u_{t,t'}\). Since every team and every coalition has mass \(s\),

\(0\le v_{t,k}(x)\le Us,\qquad 0\le b_t(z)\le Us.\)

Thus

\(0\le W(x)\le Ums^2,\qquad 0\le R(x)\le Us^2,\)

and hence

\(-(1-\alpha)Us^2\le F(x)\le\alpha Ums^2.\)

These bounds establish that \(F^*\) is finite. They do not assume that the supremum is attained.

3. A direct reduction from \(\mathrm{3\text{-}PARTITION}\)

We use the standard strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem \(\mathrm{3\text{-}PARTITION}\): given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_{3h},B\) satisfying

\(\sum_{i=1}^{3h}a_i=hB,\qquad B/4<a_i<B/2,\)

decide whether the indices can be partitioned into \(h\) triples, each having sum \(B\). Strong \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness means that hardness persists when these integers are polynomially bounded in \(h\). See [Garey–Johnson, problem SP15](https://perso.limos.fr/~palafour/PAPERS/PDF/Garey-Johnson79.pdf).

Construct the society by setting

\(\tau=3h,\qquad m=h,\qquad s=B,\qquad \mu_i=a_i,\)

and

\(u_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1,&i=j,\\0,&i\ne j.\end{cases}\)

All listed types have positive mass, and the required total mass is \(hB=ms\). Define

\(Q=\sum_i a_i^2.\)

For this society,

\(v_{i,k}(x)=x_{i,k},\qquad W(x)=\sum_{i,k}x_{i,k}^2.\)

3.1. The welfare upper bound detects unsplit types

For each type \(i\),

\(a_i^2=\left(\sum_kx_{i,k}\right)^2 =\sum_kx_{i,k}^2+2\sum_{k<\ell}x_{i,k}x_{i,\ell}.\)

Therefore

\(Q-W(x)=2\sum_i\sum_{k<\ell}x_{i,k}x_{i,\ell}\ge0.\)

Equality holds exactly when each type has positive mass in only one team. Call such an allocation unsplit.

An unsplit allocation is feasible exactly when the numbers \(a_i\) can be grouped into sets of sum \(B\). Each such set must contain exactly three indices:

  • Two or fewer indices have total mass strictly less than \(B\).
  • Four or more indices have total mass strictly greater than \(B\).

Thus a feasible unsplit allocation exists exactly when the source instance is a yes-instance.

3.2. Every unsplit allocation has zero uplift

Suppose \(x\) is unsplit and type \(i\) occupies team \(k(i)\). Its current utility is \(a_i\).

For any feasible coalition containing positive mass of type \(i\), individual rationality requires

\(b_i(z)=z_i\ge a_i.\)

On the other hand, the availability constraints imply \(z_i\le a_i\). Hence \(z_i=a_i\), and every participating unit of that type has gain zero.

Every participating type therefore has zero gain. It follows that

\(R(x)=0,\qquad F(x)=\alpha Q.\)

3.3. Decision hardness, including a positive uplift weight

Fix any rational constant \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and choose threshold \(q=\alpha Q\).

If the source instance is a yes-instance, an unsplit allocation satisfies \(F(x)=q\).

Conversely, if \(F(x)\ge q\), then

\(\alpha Q\le F(x)=\alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)R(x)\le\alpha W(x)\le\alpha Q.\)

Since \(\alpha>0\), equality forces \(W(x)=Q\). Section 3.1 then gives a feasible unsplit allocation and hence a solution to the source instance.

This proves decision hardness for every fixed rational \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), including \(\alpha=\tfrac12\). The construction has polynomial encoding length. On the polynomially bounded hard instances of \(\mathrm{3\text{-}PARTITION}\), all its numerical parameters, including \(Q\), are polynomially bounded. The hardness is therefore strong.

4. A uniform gap despite fractional allocations

The preceding reduction proves exact hardness. Additive inapproximability requires a quantitative gap.

4.1. Integer vertices of the allocation polytope

For the constructed instance, every vertex of \(\mathcal X\) has integer coordinates.

To prove this, associate a bipartite support graph to an allocation: its vertices are the types and teams, and edge \((i,k)\) is present when \(x_{i,k}>0\).

If the support graph contains a cycle, alternately increasing and decreasing its edge masses by a sufficiently small positive amount preserves all row sums, column sums, and nonnegativity. Performing both signs of this perturbation expresses the allocation as the midpoint of two distinct feasible allocations. It cannot be a vertex.

A vertex consequently has a forest as its support graph. In any nonempty forest component, take a leaf. Its unique incident edge equals that leaf’s remaining prescribed row or column sum, which is an integer. Remove that edge and leaf, subtracting the edge mass from the neighboring prescribed sum. That sum remains an integer. Repeating this argument proves integrality of every edge mass.

4.2. Convexity bounds every fractional allocation

The function \(W(x)=\sum_{i,k}x_{i,k}^2\) is convex. Every point of the bounded polytope \(\mathcal X\) is a convex combination of its vertices. Convexity therefore implies

\(W(x)\le\max\{W(v):v\text{ is a vertex of }\mathcal X\}.\)

Thus welfare has an integer maximizer.

In a no-instance, every feasible integer allocation splits at least one type between two teams. Those two positive coordinates are integers, so their product is at least one. The identity from Section 3.1 gives

\(Q-W(x)\ge2\)

for every feasible integer allocation. Applying the vertex bound yields the same upper bound for every real feasible allocation:

\(W(x)\le Q-2.\)

Since \(R(x)\ge0\), we have established the gap

\(\text{yes-instance: }F^*=\alpha Q,\qquad \text{no-instance: }F^*\le\alpha Q-2\alpha.\)

5. Consequences for additive approximation

Fix \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) and \(\varepsilon=\tfrac14\). Suppose an algorithm returns a feasible rational allocation satisfying \(F(x)\ge F^*-\varepsilon\).

In a yes-instance,

\(F(x)\ge Q/2-1/4.\)

Because \(F(x)\le W(x)/2\), its output satisfies

\(W(x)\ge Q-1/2.\)

In a no-instance, every feasible output satisfies

\(W(x)\le Q-2.\)

Comparing the output’s welfare with \(Q-1\) therefore decides \(\mathrm{3\text{-}PARTITION}\). Welfare is a rational quadratic expression and can be evaluated in polynomial time in the input and output lengths. The argument also applies to other standard output representations permitting polynomial-time evaluation to fixed absolute precision.

On the strongly hard source instances, \(m,\tau,s,L\) are polynomially bounded in the source length, while \(1/\varepsilon=4\). Consequently, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), no such approximation algorithm runs in

\(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,s,L,1/\varepsilon).\)

The same gap rules out polynomial-time estimation of \(F^*\) to absolute error \(\tfrac14\).

Any fixed positive error is hard

Multiplying every utility by a positive integer \(C\) preserves coalition feasibility: it multiplies both sides of every utility comparison by \(C\). It also multiplies \(W\), \(R\), and \(F\) by \(C\).

The gap becomes \(2\alpha C\). For any fixed error \(\varepsilon_0>0\), choose a fixed integer \(C\) with \(\alpha C>\varepsilon_0\). The resulting gap separates both feasible-solution approximations and two-sided value estimates of error \(\varepsilon_0\).

Thus, under the question’s absolute additive-error convention, there is no additive polynomial-time approximation scheme. This conclusion uses the permitted, unnormalized objective scale; it does not establish hardness for a different error convention normalized by population size or utility magnitude.

6. General algorithms and their running times

There is nevertheless an exact algebraic decision procedure, and it gives polynomial-time algorithms when both \(m\) and \(\tau\) are fixed.

Let \(A(x)\) express allocation feasibility, and let \(C(x,y)\) express

\(0\le y_{t,k}\le x_{t,k},\qquad \sum_{t,k}y_{t,k}=s,\qquad y_{t,k}\bigl(b_t(z)-v_{t,k}(x)\bigr)\ge0\ \text{for all }t,k.\)

The threshold question is exactly the real sentence

\(\exists x\ \forall y\ \left[ A(x)\ \land\ \left(C(x,y)\Longrightarrow \alpha W(x)-(1-\alpha)G(x,y)\ge q \right) \right].\)

For feasible \(x\), requiring the displayed inequality for every feasible coalition is equivalent to requiring it for the coalition attaining \(R(x)\).

Put \(n=m\tau\). This formula has:

  • \(2n\) real variables;
  • \(O(n)\) distinct polynomial constraints;
  • polynomial degrees at most two;
  • integer coefficient bit lengths \(O(L)\), after clearing positive denominators.

Real quantifier elimination therefore applies. A conservative cylindrical-algebraic-decomposition bound, including coefficient arithmetic and witness extraction, is

\((m\tau+L+2)^{\,2^{O(m\tau)}}\)

bit operations. The relevant standard algorithms are described in [Basu–Pollack–Roy, *Algorithms in Real Algebraic Geometry*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-662-05355-3); see also [Basu’s survey, Section 2.1.2](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf). Witnesses can be represented by real algebraic coordinates.

For additive approximation, initialize a threshold interval with endpoints

\(a=-(1-\alpha)Us^2,\qquad b=\alpha Ums^2+1.\)

The lower threshold is feasible, and the upper threshold is infeasible. Binary search using the exact decision procedure preserves

\(a\le F^*\le b,\)

with the lower threshold feasible. After the interval width is at most \(\varepsilon\), extract an allocation satisfying \(F(x)\ge a\). Then

\(F(x)\ge F^*-\varepsilon.\)

This requires

\(O\!\left(1+\log(m+1)+\log(s+1)+\log(U+1)+\log^+(1/\varepsilon)\right)\)

decision calls, where \(\log^+(r)=\max\{0,\log_2 r\}\). Since \(L\) includes the binary accuracy parameter, a conservative total bound remains

\((m\tau+L+2)^{\,2^{O(m\tau)}}.\)

This procedure works with the supremum and requires no attainment assumption. In particular, for fixed \(m\) and fixed \(\tau\), its running time is polynomial in \(L\), including the encoding length of \(\varepsilon\). The general hardness results concern variable support size and variable numbers of teams.
