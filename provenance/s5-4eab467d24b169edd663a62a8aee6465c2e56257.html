Result and interpretation

Yes: every instance satisfying the stated strict gaps admits a computable rational threshold and a finite-state self-enforcing process with uniform random serial dictatorship in every on-path period.

The construction problem, including exact recommendation evaluation, is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already with two firms, one cohort profile, and globally affine input functions.

Worker individual rationality is understood to apply to the recommendation at every history, as in \(M^\circ(r)\). Arbitrary deviations can change the intrinsic utilities of workers retained by other firms; requiring their realized utilities also to remain nonnegative would be an additional condition.

The argument below establishes the mass result directly. It does not assume that the finite-worker existence theorem in [Liu, Wang, and Zhang](https://hanzhezhang.github.io/research/2508SelfEnforcedMatch.pdf) automatically extends to divisible workers.

1. Surplus bounds and the mass minmax formula

Write

\[ Y_f(z)=\left\{y:0\le y\le z,\ \sum_t y_t\le\kappa_f\right\}, \qquad h_f(z,r)=\max_{y\in Y_f(z)}S_f(y,r). \]

Each maximum exists: the feasible set is nonempty and compact, and surplus is continuous. Moreover, \(S_f(0,r)=0\), so \(h_f(z,r)\ge0\).

Although the revenue and intrinsic-utility functions are piecewise linear, surplus is generally piecewise quadratic. On a common refinement of their finitely many polyhedral pieces, its gradient has a computable rational bound. Continuity across pieces then gives a computable rational constant \(H>0\), common to all firms and supported profiles, such that

\[ |S_f(y,r)-S_f(y',r)|\le H\|y-y'\|_1 \]

whenever both workforce vectors are feasible. Indeed, a line segment between them lies in the convex workforce domain, intersects finitely many pieces, and the gradient bound applies on each segment.

Put

\[ K=\sum_f\kappa_f, \qquad c=\max\{0,1-K\}, \qquad Z(r)=\left\{z:0\le z\le r,\ \sum_tz_t\ge c\right\}. \]

Proposition 1. For every firm and supported profile,

\[ b_f(r)=\min_{z\in Z(r)}h_f(z,r). \]

Furthermore, the infimum defining \(b_f(r)\) is attained by a recommendation with finite wages.

Proof. First consider any recommendation in \(M^\circ(r)\), and let

\[ z=r-\sum_gx_g \]

be its unemployed workforce vector. Total employment is at most \(K\), hence \(z\in Z(r)\).

For any \(y\in Y_f(z)\), the firm can recruit these unemployed workers at wages

\[ p_t=-v_{ft}(y,r)+\eta, \qquad \eta>0. \]

Every recruited worker strictly improves, and the firm obtains

\[ S_f(y,r)-\eta\sum_ty_t. \]

Taking the supremum over deviations and letting \(\eta\) decrease to zero proves that the deviation supremum is at least \(h_f(z,r)\). Consequently,

\[ b_f(r)\ge\min_{z\in Z(r)}h_f(z,r). \]

For the reverse inequality, fix \(z\in Z(r)\). The employment vector \(r-z\) has total mass at most \(K\), so it can be distributed among firms within their capacities. Give every employed worker utility exactly \(H\), using wages

\[ p_{gt}=H-v_{gt}(x_g,r). \]

Consider any deviation by firm \(f\), with recruited workforce \(y\). Decompose the selected workers into formerly unemployed workers, of vector \(y^0\le z\), and formerly employed workers, of vector \(y^1\), so \(y=y^0+y^1\).

Every formerly employed recruited worker had utility \(H\), and every formerly unemployed recruited worker had utility zero. Strict improvement therefore implies that total recruited-worker utility is at least \(H\sum_ty_t^1\). This remains true with one wage per type: if a type includes workers with different reservation utilities, the common wage must satisfy the reservation utility of every recruited worker.

Thus the deviation profit is at most

\[ S_f(y,r)-H\sum_ty_t^1 \le S_f(y^0,r) \le h_f(z,r). \]

The middle inequality is the Lipschitz bound. Recruiting only unemployed workers approaches \(h_f(z,r)\), so the deviation supremum equals it.

Finally, \(h_f(\cdot,r)\) is continuous. To see this, take a maximizer \(y\) for \(z\), and clip it coordinatewise to \(z'\). The clipped vector remains capacity feasible and loses at most \(\|z-z'\|_1\) in total mass. Hence

\[ h_f(z,r)-h_f(z',r)\le H\|z-z'\|_1. \]

Interchanging \(z,z'\) proves continuity. Since \(Z(r)\) is compact, its minimum is attained. The finite-wage recommendation just constructed attains the minmax infimum. \(\square\)

We will also use a uniform upper bound on deviation profits. Define

\[ M_f=\max_{r\in\mathcal R}h_f(r,r). \]

At any worker-individually-rational recommendation, every recruited worker must receive positive utility after deviation. Therefore every feasible deviation has profit at most \(S_f(y,r)\le M_f\). Recruiting nobody gives profit zero, also within this bound.

2. Uniform serial dictatorship lies strictly above minmax

Let \(a^0(r,o)\) be the prescribed serial-dictatorship recommendation for profile \(r\) and ordering \(o\). Its wages give every employed worker utility zero, and its firm profits are the selected coalition surpluses.

Immediately before firm \(f\) chooses, the remaining workforce \(z\) satisfies \(z\in Z(r)\): preceding firms have employed at most \(K\) workers in total. Thus, by Proposition 1,

\[ u_f(a^0(r,o),r)=h_f(z,r)\ge b_f(r). \]

If firm \(f\) is first, its profit is \(h_f(r,r)\). Define

\[ q_f=\mathbb E_{r,o}[u_f(a^0(r,o),r)], \qquad \bar b_f=\sum_r\pi_rb_f(r). \]

The probability that \(f\) is first is \(1/m\). Therefore

\[ q_f-\bar b_f \ge \frac1m\sum_r\pi_r\bigl(h_f(r,r)-b_f(r)\bigr)>0. \]

The last inequality follows from the assumed strict gap at some supported profile and its positive probability.

Choose a rational number \(\varepsilon>0\) satisfying

\[ \varepsilon<\min_f(q_f-\bar b_f). \]

We need auxiliary recommendations with expected payoff vectors

\[ q^f=q-\varepsilon e_f. \]

These are feasible. For each \(f\), choose a profile-order event of positive probability on which \(f\)'s serial-dictatorship workforce has positive total mass. Such an event exists because the strict gap implies \(h_f(r,r)>0\) at some supported profile.

Suppose the selected event has probability \(\rho_f>0\) and workforce mass \(a_f>0\). On that event alone, increase every wage paid by \(f\) by

\[ \frac{\varepsilon}{\rho_fa_f}. \]

The expected reduction in \(f\)'s profit is exactly \(\varepsilon\). Other firms' profits are unchanged, and all worker utilities remain nonnegative. Denote this auxiliary recommendation rule by \(a^f\).

3. Minmax recommendations with constant deviation gains

A minmax recommendation can give the punished firm a profit below its minmax value. The construction must make accepting that recommendation preferable to restarting punishment.

We first arrange that its immediate deviation gain is constant across profiles.

Let

\[ E=\min\{1,K\}, \qquad \alpha_f=\min\{\kappa_f,E\}>0. \]

For each \(f,r\), choose a minimizing unemployed vector from Proposition 1 with total mass exactly \(c\). Such a minimizer exists: starting from any minimizer, reduce unemployed coordinates until their sum is \(c\). The function \(h_f\) is coordinatewise nondecreasing, so its value cannot increase and remains the minimum.

Distribute the resulting employed mass \(E\) among firms, assigning total mass exactly \(\alpha_f\) to firm \(f\). This is feasible because

\[ E-\alpha_f\le\sum_{g\ne f}\kappa_g. \]

Call the resulting allocation \(x^{f,r}\). Initially give every employed worker utility \(H\). The punished firm's profit is then

\[ S_f(x_f^{f,r},r)-H\alpha_f. \]

Choose a positive rational \(D_f\) sufficiently large that, for every supported profile,

\[ b_f(r)-D_f \le S_f(x_f^{f,r},r)-H\alpha_f. \]

Increase all of firm \(f\)'s employed workers' utilities by the common nonnegative amount

\[ \lambda_f(r)= \frac{S_f(x_f^{f,r},r)-H\alpha_f-b_f(r)+D_f}{\alpha_f}. \]

This defines a punishment recommendation \(a^{P_f}(r)\) with

\[ u_f(a^{P_f}(r),r)=b_f(r)-D_f. \]

Every employed worker still receives utility at least \(H\). The proof of Proposition 1 therefore still applies: the punished firm's deviation supremum remains exactly \(b_f(r)\).

Consequently, the punished firm's supremal immediate deviation gain is \(D_f\) at every profile.

Write

\[ B_j^f=\sum_r\pi_ru_j(a^{P_f}(r),r). \]

In particular,

\[ B_f^f=\bar b_f-D_f. \]

Define

\[ \Delta_f=q_f^f-B_f^f =q_f-\varepsilon-\bar b_f+D_f. \]

Because \(q_f-\varepsilon>\bar b_f\), we have

\[ \Delta_f>D_f>0. \]

4. A finite-state process and its incentive inequalities

The process has the following states:

  • \(C_0\): recommend \(a^0\), the exact prescribed uniform serial dictatorship.
  • \(C_f\): recommend the auxiliary rule \(a^f\).
  • \(P_f(n)\), for \(1\le n\le N\): recommend \(a^{P_f}\), with \(n\) punishment periods remaining.

It starts in \(C_0\). Without a deviation, each \(C\)-state persists; punishment counts down, with \(P_f(1)\) followed by \(C_f\). Any identified deviation by firm \(j\), from any state, leads next period to \(P_j(N)\).

Thus every on-path period uses \(a^0\).

Choose a finite bound

\[ C=\max_{\substack{j,\ h\in\{0\}\cup F\\r,o}} \bigl(M_j-u_j(a^h(r,o),r)\bigr). \]

This maximum is finite because there are finitely many profile-order events and finitely many auxiliary recommendation rules. Choose an integer \(N\ge1\) such that

\[ N\Delta_j>C \qquad\text{for every }j. \]

For a fixed discount factor, let \(V_j(S)\) be firm \(j\)'s normalized discounted payoff when all recommendations are followed, evaluated before the next profile is drawn. Then

\[ V_j(C_0)=q_j, \qquad V_j(C_f)=q_j^f, \]

and

\[ V_j(P_f(n)) =(1-\delta^n)B_j^f+\delta^nq_j^f. \]

For convenience, set \(P_f(0)=C_f\).

4.1 The punished firm's incentives

Suppose the current state is \(P_f(n)\). Its immediate deviation gain is at most \(D_f\). Following leads to \(P_f(n-1)\), while deviating restarts \(P_f(N)\).

The continuation advantage of following is

\[ V_f(P_f(n-1))-V_f(P_f(N)) =(\delta^{n-1}-\delta^N)\Delta_f. \]

Since \(n\le N\),

\[ \delta\bigl(V_f(P_f(n-1))-V_f(P_f(N))\bigr) \ge(1-\delta)\delta^N\Delta_f. \]

Therefore the sufficient incentive inequality is

\[ D_f\le\delta^N\Delta_f. \]

It holds for every sufficiently large \(\delta<1\), because \(\Delta_f>D_f\).

4.2 Firm \(j\)'s incentives in its own auxiliary state

In state \(C_j\), following preserves payoff \(q_j^j\). Deviating leads to \(P_j(N)\). The sufficient inequality is

\[ (1-\delta)C \le \delta(1-\delta^N)\Delta_j. \]

Dividing by \(1-\delta>0\), this becomes

\[ C\le\delta(1+\delta+\cdots+\delta^{N-1})\Delta_j. \]

The right side tends to \(N\Delta_j>C\), so this too holds sufficiently close to one.

4.3 All other firm-state pairs

In \(C_0\), or in \(C_f\) with \(j\ne f\), firm \(j\)'s continuation payoff from following is \(q_j\). Its deviation continuation payoff satisfies

\[ V_j(P_j(N))\longrightarrow q_j-\varepsilon \qquad\text{as }\delta\longrightarrow1. \]

The continuation advantage thus tends to \(\varepsilon>0\), whereas the normalized immediate deviation gain is at most \((1-\delta)C\), which tends to zero.

Similarly, in \(P_f(n)\) with \(j\ne f\),

\[ V_j(P_f(n-1))-V_j(P_j(N)) \longrightarrow q_j-(q_j-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon. \]

For each profile, the immediate gain is bounded above by

\[ M_j-u_j(a^{P_f}(r),r), \]

a finite number. Hence all these inequalities hold sufficiently close to one.

There are only finitely many firms, states, profiles, and recommendation events. All their incentive inequalities therefore hold simultaneously on some interval ending at one.

4.4 History-dependent deviation plans

The preceding inequalities compare every feasible one-period deviation with following, using the continuation state that the deviation triggers. They hold at every state and every observed profile-randomness event.

Applying them successively to any history-dependent plan yields, from an ex ante state \(S_0\),

\[ \mathbb E\left[ (1-\delta)\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\delta^tu_{jt} +\delta^kV_j(S_k) \right]\le V_j(S_0). \]

All state values are bounded, so the terminal term tends to zero.

Deviation profits are uniformly bounded above by \(M_j\). Thus the infinite payoff can be handled through the nonnegative shortfalls \(M_j-u_{jt}\), even if a deviation plan has an unbounded negative tail. Passing to the limit proves that no feasible history-dependent plan improves the firm's normalized discounted profit. The same argument applies conditional on any current recommendation event.

All recommendations give workers nonnegative utility. This proves self-enforcement at every history.

5. Effective construction and a rational threshold

All quantities used above are computable with real algebraic encodings.

The relevant sets and functions are semialgebraic over the rationals: workforce feasibility is polyhedral, and surplus is piecewise quadratic. Effective quantifier elimination over real closed fields can decide the required formulas and extract algebraic witnesses. The underlying decidability and algebraic-number framework is described in [Mahboubi and Cohen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.3731).

Concretely:

  • Each surplus maximum and minmax minimum is attained and can be computed algebraically.
  • A lexicographically least surplus maximizer is obtained by successively minimizing each coordinate over the compact set of maximizers. Its coordinates are algebraic and computable.
  • Enumerating the finitely many profiles and firm orderings computes \(q\), all auxiliary recommendations, and the finite payoff bounds.
  • Rational choices of \(D_f\) and \(\varepsilon\), and an integer choice of \(N\), can be found by exact comparisons.
  • The sufficient incentive inequalities are polynomial inequalities in \(\delta\), with algebraic coefficients.

To obtain a rational threshold, enumerate

\[ d_k=1-2^{-k}, \qquad k=1,2,\ldots. \]

For each candidate, use quantifier elimination to test whether all sufficient incentive inequalities hold for every real \(\delta\) satisfying

\[ d_k<\delta<1. \]

The existence argument proves that this search terminates. Its accepted value is a computable rational \(\delta_0<1\).

The finite-state process already constructed works for every rational \(\delta\in(\delta_0,1)\). Recommendations can be stored as finite tables of algebraic numbers indexed by the state, profile, and ordering. Uniform sampling among the finitely many orderings and the finite transition rules have finite algorithmic encodings.

This establishes computability, without asserting a polynomial running time or polynomial table size.

6. Hardness with two firms and affine inputs

We reduce maximum clique to the mandatory on-path recommendation evaluation.

Let \(G\) be a simple graph. Pad it with isolated vertices to obtain \(n\ge2\) vertices, where \(n\) is a power of two and is less than twice the original vertex count, apart from the harmless minimum-size adjustment. Padding does not change its clique number \(\omega(G)\).

Use one cohort profile, with one type per vertex and masses

\[ r_i=\frac1n. \]

These masses are dyadic. There are two firms, with capacities

\[ \kappa_1=\frac1n, \qquad \kappa_2=1. \]

Let \(A\) be the graph's adjacency matrix. Define globally affine inputs by

\[ R_1(y)=n\sum_i y_i, \qquad v_{1i}(y)=n^2\sum_jA_{ij}y_j, \]

and

\[ R_2(y)=\sum_i y_i, \qquad v_{2i}(y)=0. \]

Thus

\[ S_1(y)=n\sum_i y_i+n^2y^{\mathsf T}Ay, \qquad S_2(y)=\sum_i y_i. \]

The representation has polynomial length.

6.1 These instances satisfy the strict-gap promise

Total capacity exceeds one. Therefore \(c=0\), and Proposition 1 gives

\[ b_1=b_2=0 \]

by taking \(z=0\).

Firm 2's maximum surplus is one. Firm 1 can hire mass \(1/n\) of any type and obtain surplus one. Both strict gaps therefore hold.

6.2 Firm 1's first choice encodes maximum clique

When firm 1 is first, substitute \(z=ny\). Its optimization becomes

\[ \max\left\{ \sum_i z_i+z^{\mathsf T}Az: z\ge0,\ \sum_i z_i\le1 \right\}. \]

The individual upper bounds \(z_i\le1\) are redundant.

For \(z=tw\), where \(\sum_iw_i=1\), the objective is

\[ t+t^2w^{\mathsf T}Aw. \]

Since \(A\) is nonnegative, this is strictly increasing in \(t\in[0,1]\). Hence every maximizer has total mass one.

We now prove the needed quadratic identity:

\[ \max_{\substack{z\ge0\\\sum_i z_i=1}}z^{\mathsf T}Az =1-\frac1{\omega(G)}. \]

Choose a maximizer with minimum support size. If two positive coordinates \(z_i,z_j\) correspond to nonadjacent vertices, keep their sum fixed and vary only these coordinates. Because there is no edge between them, the objective is affine in this redistribution. One endpoint is at least as good and eliminates a positive coordinate, contradicting minimality. Thus the support is a clique.

For a clique support of size \(k\),

\[ z^{\mathsf T}Az =\left(\sum_i z_i\right)^2-\sum_i z_i^2 =1-\sum_i z_i^2 \le1-\frac1k \le1-\frac1{\omega(G)}. \]

The first inequality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz. Uniform weights on a maximum clique attain equality.

Consequently, firm 1's maximum surplus is

\[ 2-\frac1{\omega(G)}. \]

We can recover a maximum clique directly from the prescribed tie-breaking. Suppose the lexicographically least maximizer had positive mass on two nonadjacent vertices \(i<j\). The objective is affine when redistributing their fixed total mass. Since the current point is an interior maximizer of that interval, the affine function must be constant. Moving all their mass to coordinate \(j\) preserves optimality and decreases the earlier coordinate \(i\), contradicting lexicographic minimality.

Its support is therefore a clique. Equality in the preceding bound forces that clique to have size \(\omega(G)\), with equal positive coordinates. The selected workforce consequently satisfies

\[ y_i\in\left\{0,\frac1{n\omega(G)}\right\}, \]

and its positive coordinates identify a maximum clique.

6.3 Implication for the requested computational problem

Exact evaluation of the prescribed recommendation for the public ordering \((1,2)\) therefore solves maximum clique. This evaluation task is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, despite all functions having a single affine piece.

For the combined threshold-construction-evaluation task, suppose a polynomial-time algorithm existed. Apply it to obtain its rational threshold \(\delta_0\), and choose

\[ \delta=\frac{1+\delta_0}{2}. \]

A polynomial-time threshold algorithm necessarily outputs \(\delta_0\) with polynomial encoding length, so this admissible rational discount also has polynomial encoding length. Construct the process and evaluate its first on-path recommendation for ordering \((1,2)\). The returned workforce identifies a maximum clique in polynomial time.

Thus the construction problem including recommendation evaluation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions. In particular, a polynomial-time solution would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The hardness concerns implementing the required recommendations; it does not concern deciding existence on the promised input class, for which the existence result above is affirmative.
