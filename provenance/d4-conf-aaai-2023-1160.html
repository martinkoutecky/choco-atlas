Proven results and remaining question

Additive approximation is polynomial-time solvable in $m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)$.

The general exact-optimization question remains unresolved here. I prove uniqueness and algebraicity of the optimum, a bound on its algebraic degree, and polynomial-time exact solvability for fixed $m$.

I also resolve a family with variable $m$: for two equally weighted opposite rankings, the maximizing worths have an explicit sine formula and polynomial-size algebraic representations. Thus this family does not supply an exponential-output obstruction.

All arguments below are self-contained apart from precisely stated algorithmic theorems.

1. Normalization and uniqueness

Discard rankings with $q_\pi=0$, and define

$$ a_\pi=\mu_\pi q_\pi,\qquad W=\sum_\pi a_\pi,\qquad \alpha_\pi=\frac{a_\pi}{W},\qquad \alpha_{\min}=\min_\pi\alpha_\pi. $$

The hypotheses give $W>0$. All remaining $\alpha_\pi$ are positive, and their sum is $1$. These rational numbers have polynomial bit length and are computable in polynomial time.

Write

$$ A_{\pi,r}=\{\pi_r,\ldots,\pi_m\}, $$

and define the normalized negative log-likelihood

$$ g(\theta) = -\frac{F(\theta)}{W} = \sum_\pi\alpha_\pi \sum_{r=1}^{m-1} \left[ \log\left(\sum_{j\in A_{\pi,r}}e^{\theta_j}\right) -\theta_{\pi_r} \right]. $$

Every stage loss is nonnegative because its denominator includes its numerator.

For a nonempty set $A$, put

$$ s_j=\frac{e^{\theta_j}}{\sum_{k\in A}e^{\theta_k}} \qquad(j\in A). $$

Direct differentiation gives

$$ v^{\mathsf T} \nabla^2\log\left(\sum_{j\in A}e^{\theta_j}\right)v = \sum_{j\in A}s_jv_j^2 - \left(\sum_{j\in A}s_jv_j\right)^2. $$

This is a variance, so it is nonnegative. Since every $s_j>0$, it vanishes exactly when $v$ is constant on $A$.

Every ranking contributes a first-stage term with $A=\{1,\ldots,m\}$, and the normalized weights of these terms sum to $1$. Consequently, $\nabla^2g$ is positive definite on

$$ V=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb R^m:\sum_i\theta_i=0\right\}. $$

Indeed, a nonzero vector in $V$ cannot be constant on all items. Thus $g$ is strictly convex on $V$.

Under the finite-maximizer promise, there is therefore exactly one optimal normalized utility vector $\theta^*$. The correspondence

$$ p_i=\frac{e^{\theta_i}}{\sum_j e^{\theta_j}}, \qquad \theta_i=\log p_i-\frac1m\sum_j\log p_j $$

is a bijection between $V$ and the positive simplex. Hence the maximizing worth vector $p^*$ is also unique.

2. Existence and a polynomial bound on optimal utilities

2.1. An existence criterion

Consider the following condition:

For every nonempty proper subset $H$ of the items, some positive-weight ranking places an item outside $H$ before an item in $H$.

This condition is necessary for a finite maximizer.

To prove necessity, suppose it fails for $H$. Every active ranking then places all items of $H$ before all items outside $H$. Increase the utilities in $H$ together. At a stage whose winner belongs to $H$, the derivative of the log choice probability is

$$ 1- \frac{\sum_{j\in A\cap H}e^{\theta_j}} {\sum_{j\in A}e^{\theta_j}}. $$

It is nonnegative, and the first stage makes it strictly positive. Once the winner is outside $H$, no item of $H$ remains, so the derivative is zero. Thus $F$ has a strictly positive derivative in this direction at every finite point. Subtracting the direction's mean preserves normalization and preserves the derivative, because $F$ is invariant under common utility shifts. A finite maximizer is impossible.

The condition is also sufficient. Let

$$ R_\theta=\max_i\theta_i-\min_i\theta_i. $$

If $R_\theta=0$, the inequality below follows from $g\ge0$. Otherwise, sort the utilities and choose an adjacent gap of size at least $R_\theta/(m-1)$. Define the lower and upper groups by their positions in this sorted order. The selected gap is positive, so every utility in the upper group exceeds every utility in the lower group by at least that gap.

The condition supplies an active ranking with a lower-group winner while an upper-group item remains. This is a modeled stage, since that winner cannot be last. Its loss is at least the selected gap. Consequently,

$$ g(\theta)\ge \frac{\alpha_{\min}}{m-1}R_\theta. $$

On $V$, bounded range implies bounded coordinates: zero lies between the smallest and largest coordinates, so $|\theta_i|\le R_\theta$. The displayed inequality therefore makes every sublevel set bounded. These sets are closed by continuity, and minimizing over a nonempty compact sublevel set gives a finite minimum.

Thus the condition characterizes finite existence. It can also be checked in polynomial time: form the directed graph containing an edge from every earlier-ranked item to every later-ranked item in each active ranking. The condition says that every nonempty proper vertex subset has an incoming edge, which is equivalent to strong connectivity.

2.2. Bounding every optimal adjacent gap

Sort $\theta^*$ and consider a positive adjacent gap $d$. Let $H$ be the items above that gap and $C$ its complement.

At a stage with remaining set $A$, define

$$ s_H= \frac{\sum_{j\in A\cap H}e^{\theta_j^*}} {\sum_{j\in A}e^{\theta_j^*}}. $$

When utilities in $H$ increase together, the derivative of the log choice probability is $1-s_H$ if the winner belongs to $H$, and $-s_H$ otherwise.

For a winner in $H$, comparison with that winner's worth gives

$$ 1-s_H\le m e^{-d}. $$

The existence condition supplies a stage with a winner in $C$ and an item of $H$ remaining. At that stage,

$$ s_H\ge\frac{1}{1+m e^{-d}}. $$

Stationarity makes the total weighted derivative zero. There are $m-1$ stages per ranking, and the ranking weights sum to $1$, so the total positive contribution is at most $m(m-1)e^{-d}$. The distinguished negative contribution has magnitude at least $\alpha_{\min}/(1+m e^{-d})$. Therefore

$$ \frac{\alpha_{\min}}{1+m e^{-d}} \le m(m-1)e^{-d}. $$

If $d\ge\log m$, the denominator on the left is at most $2$, yielding

$$ d\le \log\left(\frac{2m(m-1)}{\alpha_{\min}}\right). $$

If $d<\log m$, this bound also holds because $2m(m-1)/\alpha_{\min}\ge m$. A zero adjacent gap satisfies the bound immediately. Summing all adjacent gaps gives

$$ \max_i\theta_i^*-\min_i\theta_i^* \le (m-1)\log\left(\frac{2m(m-1)}{\alpha_{\min}}\right). $$

Write $\alpha_{\min}=u/v$ with positive integers $u,v$, and let $b$ be the binary length of $v$. Then $\alpha_{\min}>2^{-b}$. Since

$$ \log(2m(m-1))<2m, $$

the integer

$$ B=(m-1)(b+2m)+1 $$

strictly exceeds the optimal utility range. Its numerical value is polynomially bounded in the input parameters.

3. Polynomial-time additive approximation

3.1. A bounded rational-coordinate problem

Set $d=m-1$, and use coordinates

$$ t(x)=(x_1,\ldots,x_d,0), \qquad f(x)=g(t(x)). $$

The optimum is represented by

$$ x_i^*=\theta_i^*-\theta_m^*, $$

so $|x_i^*|<B$. Consider the box

$$ D=[-2B,2B]^d. $$

The optimum has distance greater than $B$ from every face.

A stage gradient in full utility coordinates is a probability vector minus the winner's coordinate vector. Its Euclidean norm is at most $2$. Restricting to the first $d$ coordinates cannot increase that norm. Thus

$$ \|\nabla f(x)\|_2\le 2(m-1)\le M, \qquad M=2m. $$

In particular, $f$ is globally $M$-Lipschitz. The box lies in a ball centered at zero with rational radius

$$ R=2Bm. $$

3.2. Polynomial-time finite-precision evaluation

At rational $x\in D$, both $f(x)$ and $\nabla f(x)$ can be approximated to absolute error $\zeta$ in time polynomial in the input parameters, the bit length of $x$, and $\log(1/\zeta)$.

Here is an explicit construction. For each stage, let

$$ u=\max_{j\in A}t_j(x), \qquad Z=\sum_{j\in A}e^{t_j(x)-u}. $$

Its loss is

$$ u-t_{\pi_r}(x)+\log Z. $$

The exponential arguments belong to $[-4B,0]$, and $1\le Z\le m$.

For $z\in[-4B,0]$, Taylor's theorem bounds the degree-$n$ exponential remainder by

$$ \frac{|z|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}. $$

Using $k!\ge(k/e)^k$, a degree bounded by a constant times

$$ B+\log(1/\rho)+1 $$

suffices for absolute error $\rho$. Rational evaluation has polynomial bit complexity. Clamp each approximation to $[0,1]$, which cannot increase its error. The term with argument zero is evaluated exactly as $1$, so the resulting rational sum $\widetilde Z$ lies in $[1,m]$ and satisfies

$$ |Z-\widetilde Z|\le m\rho. $$

For the logarithm, use

$$ \log\widetilde Z = 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2k+1}}{2k+1}, \qquad w=\frac{\widetilde Z-1}{\widetilde Z+1} \le\frac{m-1}{m+1}. $$

After $n$ terms the tail is at most

$$ \frac{2w^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)(1-w^2)}. $$

Polynomially many terms in $m$ and $\log(1/\rho)$ make this at most $\rho$. Since both sums are at least $1$,

$$ |\log Z-\log\widetilde Z|\le m\rho. $$

Thus each stage-value error is at most $(m+1)\rho$, and the total value error is at most $m^2\rho$.

Gradient probabilities are computed by rational division. If $y,\widetilde y\in[0,1]$ are corresponding exponential terms, then

$$ \left|\frac{y}{Z}-\frac{\widetilde y}{\widetilde Z}\right| \le |y-\widetilde y| + |\widetilde y|\frac{|Z-\widetilde Z|}{Z\widetilde Z} \le (m+1)\rho. $$

Summing stage errors and using $\sum_\pi\alpha_\pi=1$ bounds the Euclidean gradient error by $2m^3\rho$. Choosing sufficiently small $\rho$, for example

$$ \rho=\min\left\{1,\frac{\zeta}{4m^3}\right\}, $$

proves the oracle claim. Only the listed rankings and their stages are evaluated.

3.3. A finite-precision ellipsoid algorithm

I use the following standard finite-precision ellipsoid theorem. Starting from a rational ball of radius $R$, rational central cuts can be processed so that the next ellipsoid contains the intersection of the previous ellipsoid with the supplied halfspace, and after $T$ cuts the volume is at most

$$ e^{-T/[4(d+1)]}\operatorname{vol}(B_d(0,R)). $$

The implementation uses polynomial time and rational encoding lengths in the dimension, number of cuts, initial encoding length, and cut encoding lengths. Dimension one can instead use rational interval bisection. These are bit-complexity properties of the ellipsoid method, as treated by [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4_4).

Set

$$ \delta=\min\{1,\varepsilon/W\}, \qquad \xi=\delta/16, \qquad \eta=\frac{\delta}{16R}, \qquad r=\frac{\delta}{4M}. $$

At each ellipsoid center $x$:

  • If $x\notin D$, use a central cut from a violated box constraint.
  • If $x\in D$, compute a rational $\widetilde f(x)$ with error at most $\xi$, and a rational vector $h$ with

$$ \|h-\nabla f(x)\|_2\le\eta. $$
Record the point and approximate value.

  • If $h\ne0$, supply the central cut

$$ h^{\mathsf T}(z-x)\le0. $$
If $h=0$, return $x$.

In the zero-vector case, convexity gives

$$ f(x)-f(x^*) \le \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(x-x^*) \le 2R\eta = \delta/8. $$

For the remaining case, define

$$ K=B_d(x^*,r). $$

This ball lies in $D$, because $r<B$. Lipschitz continuity gives

$$ f(z)\le f(x^*)+\delta/4 \qquad(z\in K). $$

Suppose every recorded center satisfies

$$ f(x)>f(x^*)+\delta/2. $$

Convexity then implies, for every $z\in K$,

$$ \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(z-x) \le f(z)-f(x) <-\delta/4. $$

Both points belong to $D$, so $\|z-x\|_2\le2R$. Therefore

$$ h^{\mathsf T}(z-x) < -\delta/4+2R\eta = -\delta/8. $$

Every objective cut consequently preserves $K$. Every box cut preserves $D$, and hence also preserves $K$.

Choose an integer $T$ satisfying

$$ T>4d(d+1)\log(R/r). $$

If every recorded center were more than $\delta/2$ suboptimal, the final ellipsoid would contain $K$ while having volume smaller than that of $K$, a contradiction. Hence a recorded center has value at most $f(x^*)+\delta/2$.

Return the recorded point $\widehat x$ with the smallest approximate value. Accounting for the two value errors gives

$$ f(\widehat x) \le f(x^*)+\delta/2+2\xi = f(x^*)+5\delta/8 < f(x^*)+\delta. $$

3.4. Output and complexity

Define rational normalized utilities by

$$ \widehat\theta = t(\widehat x) - \frac1m\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\widehat x_i\right)\mathbf1. $$

Their sum is zero, and shift invariance preserves the objective. Thus

$$ F(\widehat\theta) \ge F(\theta^*)-W\delta \ge F(\theta^*)-\varepsilon. $$

The bound $B$ is polynomial in the input parameters, and

$$ \log(1/\delta) \le \log(1/\varepsilon)+\log\max\{1,W\}. $$

The latter additional term is polynomially bounded by the input length. Therefore the iteration count, oracle precision requirements, and rational encoding lengths are polynomially bounded in

$$ m,\tau,L,\log(1/\varepsilon). $$

This proves polynomial-time additive approximation.

4. General exact optimization: algebraicity and degree bounds

4.1. A compressed polynomial system

Let $\mathcal A$ be the family of distinct proper remaining sets occurring at modeled stages:

$$ \mathcal A = \{A_{\pi,r}:2\le r\le m-1\}. $$

Put

$$ K=|\mathcal A|\le\tau(m-2), \qquad \beta_A = \sum_{\substack{\pi,r\\A_{\pi,r}=A}}\alpha_\pi, \qquad s_A(p)=\sum_{j\in A}p_j. $$

Also define the weighted number of modeled selections of item $i$:

$$ c_i = \sum_\pi\alpha_\pi \sum_{r=1}^{m-1}\mathbf1_{\{\pi_r=i\}}. $$

Each ranking has $m-1$ modeled selections and $m-2$ proper remaining sets, so

$$ \sum_i c_i=m-1, \qquad \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\beta_A=m-2. $$

On the positive simplex, the utility score equations are

$$ E_i(p) = c_i-p_i - \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A\\i\in A}} \beta_A\frac{p_i}{s_A(p)} = 0. $$

The term $p_i$ collects the first stages, whose total normalized weight is $1$. Summing the equations gives the identity

$$ \sum_iE_i(p)=0. $$

Let

$$ Q(p)=\prod_{A\in\mathcal A}s_A(p), $$

with $Q=1$ if $\mathcal A$ is empty. On the positive simplex, $Q>0$. Hence the first $m-1$ equations are equivalent to

$$ P_i(p) = (c_i-p_i)Q(p) - \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A\\i\in A}} \beta_Ap_i \prod_{\substack{B\in\mathcal A\\B\ne A}}s_B(p) = 0. $$

Their degrees are at most

$$ D=K+1\le1+\tau(m-2). $$

After substituting

$$ p_m=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}p_i $$

and clearing rational coefficient denominators, these are integer polynomial equations in $m-1$ variables. Together with positivity, they have exactly one solution: the score equations express stationarity in utility-ratio coordinates, and strict convexity makes every stationary point the unique optimum.

4.2. Algebraicity and computability

The admissible solution set is a singleton defined by rational polynomial equalities and inequalities. By real quantifier elimination, each coordinate projection is a singleton defined by a Boolean combination of signs of rational univariate polynomials.

Some nonzero defining polynomial must vanish at the projected coordinate. Otherwise all defining signs would be constant in a neighborhood, and that singleton would contain a neighborhood. This is impossible.

Thus every $p_i^*$ is algebraic. Effective elimination followed by univariate root isolation computes the required integer polynomials and rational isolating intervals.

For fixed $m$, this computation is polynomial-time. Indeed:

  • The degree bound $D$ is polynomial in the input parameters.
  • There are at most $\binom{D+m-1}{m-1}$ monomials, polynomially many for fixed $m$.
  • After substitution, each linear factor $s_A$ has coefficient absolute sum at most $m$. Products of $K$ factors therefore have coefficient bit length bounded by a polynomial in $K\log m$ and the rational input length.

Real quantifier elimination has polynomial bit complexity when the number of variables is fixed; the requisite degree and intermediate-bit-length bounds are stated in [Basu’s survey, Theorem 2.18](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf). Polynomial-time univariate root isolation and refinement then supply the requested output; see [Sagraloff’s root-isolation analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0344).

The exponent of this fixed-dimensional algorithm depends on $m$.

4.3. The optimum is a nonsingular algebraic root

There is also a quantitative algebraic-degree bound.

Use simplex coordinates $y_i=p_i$ for $i<m$, and utility-ratio coordinates

$$ x_i=\log(p_i/p_m). $$

Their Jacobian is

$$ \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial y_j} = \frac{\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}}}{p_i}+\frac1{p_m}. $$

This matrix is positive definite, since for every nonzero real vector $v$,

$$ v^{\mathsf T}\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}v = \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{v_i^2}{p_i} + \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}v_i\right)^2}{p_m} >0. $$

Recall $f(x)=g(x_1,\ldots,x_{m-1},0)$. Its Hessian is positive definite: a nonzero direction $(v,0)$ is nonconstant on all $m$ items. The score relation is

$$ E_i(p(x))=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}. $$

At the optimum, differentiation therefore gives

$$ D_yE(p^*) = -\nabla^2f(x^*)\,D_yx(p^*), $$

which is nonsingular. Since $E_i(p^*)=0$,

$$ D_y(QE)(p^*)=Q(p^*)D_yE(p^*) $$

is also nonsingular. Clearing coefficient denominators preserves nonsingularity.

Thus the optimum is an isolated nonsingular complex zero of the polynomial system, not merely an isolated real solution.

Let

$$ \mathbb K=\mathbb Q(p_1^*,\ldots,p_{m-1}^*). $$

Each embedding of $\mathbb K$ into $\mathbb C$ produces a distinct solution tuple of the same rational polynomial equations. Its Jacobian determinant remains nonzero under the embedding, so every such tuple is an isolated complex zero.

The affine Bézout theorem bounds the number of isolated zeros of $m-1$ polynomials of degrees at most $D$ by $D^{m-1}$, even if other components have positive dimension. A statement covering isolated solutions appears in [Mondal’s treatment, Theorem I.2](https://pinakimondal.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/p1_to_16.pdf). Since number fields in characteristic zero have as many embeddings as their degrees,

$$ [\mathbb K:\mathbb Q] \le D^{m-1} \le \bigl(1+\tau(m-2)\bigr)^{m-1}. $$

Every worth coordinate therefore has algebraic degree bounded by the same quantity. This is an upper bound, not an output-size lower bound.

5. A variable-dimensional exact family: opposite rankings

Suppose the two active rankings are

$$ (1,2,\ldots,m) \quad\text{and}\quad (m,m-1,\ldots,1), $$

with equal effective weights. Equivalently, their normalized weights are both $1/2$.

5.1. Explicit maximizing worths

Define

$$ \phi=\frac{\pi}{m+1}, \qquad t=1-\cos\phi, \qquad p_i=\frac{t\sin(i\phi)}{\sin\phi} \quad(1\le i\le m). $$

I prove that these are the maximizing worths.

Every $p_i$ is positive because $0<i\phi<\pi$. Let

$$ s_k=\sum_{i=1}^k p_i, \qquad s_0=0. $$

The cosine-difference identity

$$ \cos((i-\tfrac12)\phi)-\cos((i+\tfrac12)\phi) = 2\sin(i\phi)\sin(\phi/2) $$

telescopes to give

$$ s_k = \frac12 \left( 1-\frac{\cos((k+\tfrac12)\phi)}{\cos(\phi/2)} \right). $$

At $k=m$, the cosine in the numerator is $-\cos(\phi/2)$, so $s_m=1$. The worths are normalized. They are also palindromic:

$$ p_i=p_{m+1-i}. $$

Two identities will verify stationarity:

$$ p_{k+1}-p_k=t(1-2s_k), $$

and

$$ p_kp_{k+1}=2t\,s_k(1-s_k) \qquad(1\le k\le m-1). $$

The first follows from the sine-difference formula and the displayed expression for $s_k$. For the second, put $z=(k+\tfrac12)\phi$ and use

$$ \sin(k\phi)\sin((k+1)\phi) = \cos^2(\phi/2)-\cos^2z. $$

Then

$$ s_k(1-s_k) = \frac{\cos^2(\phi/2)-\cos^2z}{4\cos^2(\phi/2)}, $$

while the corresponding expression for $p_kp_{k+1}$ has multiplier $t^2/\sin^2\phi$. Their ratio is

$$ \frac{4t^2\cos^2(\phi/2)}{\sin^2\phi} = \frac{t^2}{\sin^2(\phi/2)} = 2t. $$

For $m=2$, the formula gives $p_1=p_2=1/2$, which directly maximizes the likelihood. Assume henceforth $m\ge3$.

For arbitrary positive worths, write $T=\sum_i p_i$. The normalized log-likelihood is $H(p)-\log T$, where

$$ H(p) = \sum_{i=1}^m c_i\log p_i -\frac12\sum_{k=2}^{m-1}\log s_k -\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{m-2}\log(T-s_k), $$

with

$$ c_1=c_m=\frac12, \qquad c_i=1\quad(2\le i\le m-1). $$

On the simplex, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to maximizing $H$.

Let $G_i=\partial H/\partial p_i$. For an interior index $2\le i\le m-2$, differentiation and cancellation of common terms gives

$$ G_i-G_{i+1} = \frac1{p_i}-\frac1{p_{i+1}} + \frac12\left(\frac1{1-s_i}-\frac1{s_i}\right). $$

The two proved identities imply

$$ \frac1{p_i}-\frac1{p_{i+1}} = \frac{p_{i+1}-p_i}{p_ip_{i+1}} = \frac{1-2s_i}{2s_i(1-s_i)}. $$

The other term is its negative. Hence $G_i=G_{i+1}$.

At the first endpoint,

$$ G_1-G_2 = \frac1{2p_1}-\frac1{p_2}+\frac1{2(1-p_1)}. $$

Here $p_1=t$ and $p_2=2t(1-t)$, so this difference is zero.

The function $H$ is invariant under reversal of the item labels. At a palindromic worth vector, its gradient is consequently palindromic as well. Thus the last endpoint difference also vanishes. All coordinates of the gradient are equal, proving stationarity on the simplex.

The transformation from normalized utilities to positive normalized worths is a smooth bijection. This simplex stationarity therefore gives utility stationarity. Strict concavity of the utility log-likelihood, proved in Section 1, establishes the unique global maximum:

$$ \boxed{ p_i^* = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2(m+1)}\right) \sin\left(\frac{i\pi}{m+1}\right) }. $$

In particular, the existence promise holds for this family.

5.2. Polynomial-size exact output

Define integer polynomials recursively by

$$ U_0(C)=1,\qquad U_1(C)=2C,\qquad U_{k+1}(C)=2C\,U_k(C)-U_{k-1}(C). $$

Induction using the sine addition formula gives

$$ U_k(\cos\phi) = \frac{\sin((k+1)\phi)}{\sin\phi}. $$

Consequently,

$$ c=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{m+1}\right) $$

is the largest real root of $U_m$, whose $m$ distinct roots are

$$ \cos\left(\frac{j\pi}{m+1}\right), \qquad 1\le j\le m. $$

The maximizing worths satisfy

$$ p_i^*=(1-c)U_{i-1}(c). $$

For each coordinate, form the integer resultant

$$ R_i(X) = \operatorname{Res}_C \left( U_m(C),\, X-(1-C)U_{i-1}(C) \right). $$

It is nonzero: the product formula for the resultant expresses it, up to a nonzero constant, as the product over the roots of $U_m$ of the corresponding linear factors in $X$. It has degree at most $m$ and vanishes at $p_i^*$.

The sum of absolute coefficients of $U_k$ is at most $3^k$. This follows by induction from the recurrence, because

$$ 2\cdot3^k+3^{k-1}<3^{k+1}. $$

The Sylvester matrix defining $R_i$ has order at most $2m$ and entries of coefficient absolute sum at most $1+2\cdot3^m$. Its determinant therefore has coefficient absolute sum at most

$$ H=(2m)!\,(1+2\cdot3^m)^{2m}. $$

Thus $R_i$ has degree at most $m$ and coefficient bit length

$$ O(m^2+m\log m). $$

Fraction-free computation of these Sylvester determinants takes polynomial time, since the matrix order, polynomial degrees, and coefficient bit lengths are polynomially bounded.

For completeness, identifying the intended root also requires only polynomial precision. Let $Q_i$ be the primitive squarefree part of $R_i$. It divides $R_i$. By the Cauchy root bound, every root of $R_i$ has absolute value at most $1+H$. Vieta's formulas therefore bound the coefficients of $Q_i$ by

$$ H_0=H(2+H)^m. $$

For a squarefree integer polynomial of degree $e\ge2$, its nonzero integer discriminant satisfies

$$ 1 \le |a_e|^{2e-2} \prod_{j<k}|z_j-z_k|^2. $$

Using $|a_e|\le H_0$ and $|z_j-z_k|\le4H_0$ shows that any two distinct roots are separated by at least

$$ \Delta=(4H_0)^{-m^2}. $$

For a degree-one polynomial there is only one root, so no separation requirement is needed. The bit length of $\Delta$ is polynomial in $m$.

On $[0,1]$, the derivative of $(1-C)U_{i-1}(C)$ has absolute value at most

$$ A=2m3^{m-1}. $$

Isolate and refine the largest root $c$ of $U_m$ to a rational $\bar c\in[0,1]$ with

$$ |\bar c-c|<\frac{\Delta}{16A}. $$

The polynomial-time root-isolation and refinement theorem applies because $U_m$ has degree $m$, polynomial coefficient bit length, and simple roots; the requested refinement precision also has polynomial bit length.

Put

$$ a_i=(1-\bar c)U_{i-1}(\bar c). $$

The mean value theorem gives $|a_i-p_i^*|<\Delta/16$. Therefore

$$ \left(a_i-\frac{\Delta}{4},\, a_i+\frac{\Delta}{4}\right) $$

contains $p_i^*$ and no other distinct root of $Q_i$. These rational isolating intervals and integer polynomials supply exactly the required representation in polynomial time.

5.3. Algebraic degree can grow even with two rankings

This family also demonstrates growing algebraic degree, although only linearly.

Let $r=m+1$ be an odd prime and set

$$ \zeta=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/r}, \qquad a=\frac{r+1}{2}. $$

The polynomial

$$ \Phi_r(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{r-1} $$

is irreducible: $\Phi_r(X+1)$ satisfies Eisenstein's criterion at $r$. Hence

$$ [\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=r-1. $$

Since $a$ is coprime to $r$, $\mathbb Q(\zeta^a)=\mathbb Q(\zeta)$. Moreover,

$$ c=\cos(\pi/r) = -\frac{\zeta^a+\zeta^{-a}}2. $$

The nonreal number $\zeta^a$ satisfies

$$ Z^2+2cZ+1=0. $$

Thus $\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ has degree exactly $2$ over the real field $\mathbb Q(c)$: the degree is at most $2$ by this equation and cannot be $1$ because $\zeta^a$ is nonreal. Therefore

$$ [\mathbb Q(c):\mathbb Q] = \frac{r-1}{2}. $$

Since $p_1^*=1-c$,

$$ \deg_{\mathbb Q}(p_1^*)=\frac m2. $$

This establishes unbounded exact algebraic degree with support size $2$, while remaining compatible with the polynomial-time exact algorithm for this family.
