The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many complete advertiser types \(a\), each with rational mass \(\mu_a\), values \(v_{aj}\), and maximum prices \(m_{aj}\), and slot classes \(j\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) and reserves \(r_j\), does a feasible envy-free mass assignment and price vector always exist, and can one be computed in time polynomial in the number of types, slot classes, and input bit length? The variables are assignment masses \(x_{aj}\ge 0\) and class prices \(p_j\), with \(\sum_j x_{aj}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{aj}\le q_j\), and unlimited dummy supply; each positive assignment must be to a reserve-feasible class giving its type maximum utility, with utility \(v_{aj}-p_j\) when \(p_j<m_{aj}\) and \(-\infty\) otherwise. For the bidder-optimal version, require each type's utility to be at least its utility in every other feasible envy-free mass outcome.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) complete advertiser types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_a\), normalized by \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. There are \(m\) real slot classes, with nonnegative rational values \(v_{aj}\), maximum prices \(m_{aj}\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and reserves \(r_j\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Add a dummy class \(0\) with unlimited supply, \(v_{a0}=r_0=p_0=0\), and \(m_{a0}=\infty\). Outcomes have real masses \(x_{aj}\ge0\) and real prices \(p_j\ge0\), with \(\sum_{j=0}^{m}x_{aj}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{aj}\le q_j\) for real classes.
Define \(u_{aj}(p_j)=v_{aj}-p_j\) when \(p_j<m_{aj}\), and \(u_{aj}(p_j)=-\infty\) otherwise; set \(U_a(p)=\max_{0\le j\le m}u_{aj}(p_j)\). An outcome is feasible and envy free when every \(x_{aj}>0\) satisfies \(p_j\ge r_j\) and \(u_{aj}(p_j)=U_a(p)\). Ties permit arbitrary splitting. The maximum includes all classes, even those priced below reserve; unused supply imposes no additional price condition.
An outcome is bidder optimal if \(U_a(p)\ge U_a(p')\) for every type \(a\) and every feasible envy-free outcome \((x',p')\).

Does every instance admit a feasible envy-free outcome and a bidder-optimal one?
For each requirement, is finding such an outcome or determining nonexistence polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite bidders and unit-capacity items, a feasible envy-free bidder-optimal outcome always exists and is computable in \(O(nk^3)\) arithmetic operations, where \(n\) counts bidders and \(k\) items. [Source](https://eprints.cs.univie.ac.at/3594/1/0912.1934v6.pdf)
For a different continuum unit-demand model with structured demand, a revenue approximation of \(1.88\) is known; it does not settle this bidder-optimal mass problem. [Source](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/~sekars/envyfree_teac.pdf) No result settling this exact mass model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total advertiser mass to one, interpreting supplies as per-capita quantities.
  • Specified nonnegative rational values, finite maximum prices, supplies, and reserves; zero is allowed because no strict positivity condition is stated.
  • Restored nonnegative prices and the dummy class's zero price, zero value, zero reserve, and infinite maximum price.
  • Specified that envy compares against every class at its posted price, including classes below reserve; reserve feasibility applies only to positive assignments.
  • Defined type utility as the common maximum utility of its positively assigned classes, allowed splitting among ties, and imposed no market-clearing condition on unused supply.
  • Allowed real outcomes while retaining binary-rational input and measuring computational complexity in the total input bit length.

1. Result

Every instance admits a feasible envy-free outcome and a bidder-optimal outcome. Both can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof uses transportation linear programs directly on the listed types. The crucial point is that the auxiliary programs produce prices below those of every feasible envy-free outcome, including outcomes with unused supply.

2. Reserve normalization

First, a feasible envy-free outcome is immediate: set

\(\displaystyle p_j=\max_a v_{aj}\)

and allocate every type entirely to the dummy. Every affordable real class gives nonpositive utility, so \(U_a(p)=0\).

We will compute a bidder-optimal outcome with \(p_j\ge r_j\) for every real class. This restriction preserves all attainable utility vectors.

Indeed, given any feasible envy-free outcome \((x,y)\), define

\(\displaystyle \bar y_j=\max\{y_j,r_j\}.\)

Every class whose price changes was unused, because positive allocation requires its price to meet its reserve. Thus every positively allocated class retains its price and utility. Increasing other prices cannot increase their utilities. Consequently, \((x,\bar y)\) remains feasible and envy free, and

\(\displaystyle U_a(\bar y)=U_a(y)\quad\text{for every }a.\)

Call an outcome with all real prices at least their reserves reserve-normalized.

3. A least-price lemma for a continuous auxiliary market

Consider the following auxiliary market:

  • Type masses are the given positive numbers \(\mu_a\).
  • Real-class supplies are \(q_j\).
  • The allowed type–class pairs form an arbitrary set \(E\).
  • Real prices must satisfy \(z_j\ge\ell_j\).
  • Each type has an unlimited outside option of utility \(w_a\).
  • On an allowed real pair, utility is \(v_{aj}-z_j\), with no affordability cutoff.

Here \(\ell_j,w_a\ge0\). Feasibility and envy freeness have the same allocation interpretation as in the question, including arbitrary splitting and no requirement that unused supply have a particular price.

Lemma. This auxiliary market has a feasible envy-free outcome whose real price vector \(z^*\) is componentwise below the price vector of every auxiliary feasible envy-free outcome. For rational data, it can be computed using two rational linear-program optimizations.

Proof. Write \(z_j=\ell_j+t_j\), and define

\(\displaystyle s_{aj}=v_{aj}-\ell_j-w_a.\)

Consider the transportation primal

\(\displaystyle W=\max_{x\ge0}\sum_{(a,j)\in E}s_{aj}x_{aj},\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j:(a,j)\in E}x_{aj}\le\mu_a,\qquad \sum_{a:(a,j)\in E}x_{aj}\le q_j.\)

Unallocated type mass takes its outside option. The primal is feasible and bounded: the zero allocation is feasible, and every variable is bounded by its type’s mass.

Its dual is

\(\displaystyle \min_{h,t\ge0}\left(\sum_a\mu_a h_a+\sum_jq_jt_j\right),\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle h_a+t_j\ge s_{aj}\qquad((a,j)\in E).\)

The dual is feasible, for example by taking \(t=0\) and sufficiently large \(h\). Strong linear-programming duality therefore gives optimal primal and dual solutions.

Among optimal dual solutions, minimize \(\sum_jt_j\). This secondary optimum exists: after bounding that sum by its value at any optimal dual solution, all \(t_j\) are bounded; the positive coefficients \(\mu_a\) also bound all \(h_a\).

Because every \(\mu_a>0\), an optimal dual solution necessarily has

\(\displaystyle h_a(t)=\max\left\{0,\max_{j:(a,j)\in E}(s_{aj}-t_j)\right\}.\)

Define the dual objective after eliminating \(h\):

\(\displaystyle F(t)=\sum_a\mu_a h_a(t)+\sum_jq_jt_j,\qquad t\ge0.\)

For componentwise minimum and maximum, denoted by \(\wedge,\vee\),

\(\displaystyle h_a(t\wedge t')=\max\{h_a(t),h_a(t')\},\)

and

\(\displaystyle h_a(t\vee t')\le\min\{h_a(t),h_a(t')\}.\)

Consequently,

\[\displaystyle F(t\wedge t')+F(t\vee t')\le F(t)+F(t').\tag{1}\]

If \(t,t'\) both minimize \(F\), each term on the left is at least its minimum value. Thus both \(t\wedge t'\) and \(t\vee t'\) minimize \(F\).

Let \(t^*\) be the selected minimizer of \(\sum_jt_j\). For any other minimizer \(t\), the vector \(t^*\wedge t\) is a minimizer. If any coordinate of \(t\) were below the corresponding coordinate of \(t^*\), this would strictly reduce the secondary objective. Hence

\[\displaystyle t^*\le t\quad\text{for every minimizer }t.\tag{2}\]

Any primal optimum paired with this dual optimum gives an auxiliary feasible envy-free outcome. To verify this explicitly, the primal–dual gap is

\(\displaystyle \sum_a h_a\left(\mu_a-\sum_jx_{aj}\right) +\sum_jt_j\left(q_j-\sum_ax_{aj}\right) +\sum_{(a,j)\in E}x_{aj}(h_a+t_j-s_{aj})=0. \)

All terms are nonnegative. Therefore every positively allocated real pair satisfies \(s_{aj}-t_j=h_a\), and positive outside-option allocation implies \(h_a=0\). These are exactly the first-choice conditions, with utility \(w_a+h_a\).

It remains to compare \(t^*\) with every auxiliary envy-free price vector, rather than only dual optima.

Let \(\ell+t'\) support an auxiliary feasible envy-free allocation \(x'\), and put

\(\displaystyle d'_j=\sum_ax'_{aj}\le q_j.\)

The first-choice conditions imply, for every \(t\ge0\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_a\mu_a h_a(t) \ge \sum_a\mu_a h_a(t')-\sum_jd'_j(t_j-t'_j). \)

This inequality follows by evaluating each type’s allocated first-choice alternatives at \(t\), including the outside option’s adjusted utility \(0\), and averaging with its allocation masses. Adding the price term gives

\[\displaystyle F(t)\ge F(t')+\sum_j(q_j-d'_j)(t_j-t'_j).\tag{3}\]

Since \(q_j-d'_j\ge0\), equation (3) yields

\(\displaystyle F(t^*\vee t')\ge F(t').\)

Together with (1), this implies

\(\displaystyle F(t^*\wedge t')\le F(t^*).\)

Thus \(t^*\wedge t'\) is a minimizer. By (2), \(t^*\le t'\). Therefore \(z^*=\ell+t^*\) is below every auxiliary feasible envy-free price vector. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

4. The algorithm

Initialize the current price lower bounds by

\(\displaystyle \ell_j=r_j.\)

At each stage, perform the following steps.

  • Form the currently affordable pairs:

\(\displaystyle E=\{(a,j):\ell_j<m_{aj}\}.\)

  • For each class having an incident pair in \(E\), define its next affordability threshold:

\(\displaystyle B_j=\min_{a:(a,j)\in E}m_{aj}.\)

For a class with no incident pair, set \(B_j=\ell_j\). Thus \(B_j\ge\ell_j\), with strict inequality for every class incident to \(E\).

  • Define artificial outside utilities:

\(\displaystyle w_a=\max\left\{0,\max_{j:(a,j)\in E}(v_{aj}-B_j)\right\}. \)

  • Apply the lemma to \(E,\ell,w\), obtaining its least-price outcome \((x,z)\).

At prices \(B\), every allowed real utility is at most its type’s outside utility. Hence allocating everyone outside is an auxiliary feasible envy-free outcome. The lemma therefore gives

\[\displaystyle \ell\le z\le B.\tag{4}\]

  • If some pair in \(E\) satisfies \(z_j\ge m_{aj}\), replace \(\ell\) by \(z\) and repeat.

Otherwise, return \(z\), the auxiliary real allocations \(x\), and dummy allocations

\(\displaystyle x_{a0}=\mu_a-\sum_{j:(a,j)\in E}x_{aj}.\)

By (4), a pair becomes unaffordable only when \(z_j=B_j=m_{aj}\). Every repeating stage permanently removes at least one pair: lower bounds never decrease, so removed pairs never return. There are at most \(\tau m\) repeating stages and one final stage.

5. Correctness and bidder optimality

We first prove the invariant

\[\displaystyle \ell_j\le y_j \quad\text{for every reserve-normalized original feasible envy-free outcome }(x',y). \tag{5}\]

It holds initially because \(\ell=r\).

Suppose it holds at the start of a stage. Fix any such original outcome \((x',y)\), and define capped prices

\(\displaystyle c_j=\min\{y_j,B_j\}.\)

By the invariant, \(c\ge\ell\). We will show that \(c\) supports an auxiliary feasible envy-free outcome. The lemma will then imply \(z\le c\le y\), preserving (5).

Pairs outside \(E\) are unaffordable in the original outcome: they satisfy \(m_{aj}\le\ell_j\le y_j\).

For a pair in \(E\):

  • If \(y_j<B_j\), it is affordable at \(y\), and capping leaves its utility unchanged.
  • If \(y_j\ge B_j\), its capped auxiliary utility is \(v_{aj}-B_j\le w_a\). Its original utility, whenever affordable, is also at most \(w_a\).

Therefore the auxiliary maximum utility at \(c\) equals

\[\displaystyle U_a^{\mathrm{aux}}(c)=\max\{w_a,U_a(y)\}.\tag{6}\]

Construct an auxiliary allocation type by type.

If \(U_a(y)\le w_a\), allocate its entire mass outside.

If \(U_a(y)>w_a\), retain its original allocation. Every positively allocated pair must have \(y_j<B_j\): otherwise its utility would be at most \(w_a\). Thus all retained allocations have unchanged utility \(U_a(y)\), which is the auxiliary maximum by (6).

This construction only removes real allocations, so it preserves supply feasibility. It proves that \(c\) supports an auxiliary feasible envy-free outcome, and hence establishes the invariant.

Now consider the final stage. Its stopping condition and (4) imply

\[\displaystyle z_j<B_j\quad\text{for every class incident to }E.\tag{7}\]

Indeed, equality would make a pair attaining the minimum defining \(B_j\) unaffordable and trigger another stage.

If \(w_a>0\), some allowed pair attains its defining maximum. By (7), that pair gives

\(\displaystyle v_{aj}-z_j>v_{aj}-B_j=w_a.\)

Thus this type strictly prefers a real class to the artificial outside option, and receives no outside allocation. If \(w_a=0\), its outside option coincides with the original dummy.

Every pair in \(E\) remains affordable at the final prices; every pair outside \(E\) remains unaffordable because \(z\ge\ell\). Consequently, the returned auxiliary allocation, with its outside allocations interpreted as dummy allocations, is feasible and envy free in the original model. Its prices satisfy \(z\ge r\).

Finally, the invariant gives

\(\displaystyle z\le y\)

for every reserve-normalized original feasible envy-free price vector \(y\). Each original utility function is nonincreasing in its price, including at the strict affordability cutoff. Therefore

\(\displaystyle U_a(z)\ge U_a(y)\quad\text{for every }a.\)

Reserve normalization preserves the utilities of every original feasible envy-free outcome. The returned outcome thus dominates every such outcome type by type, and is bidder optimal.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

The stage count alone does not establish polynomial time: we must also bound the encoding lengths of intermediate prices.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all original rational inputs. Its binary length is at most their total encoding length. Initially, every \(\ell_j\) is a multiple of \(1/D\).

Assume this holds at the start of a stage. Then every \(B_j,w_a,s_{aj}\) is also a multiple of \(1/D\).

The auxiliary dual’s constraint matrix consists of the pair constraints \(h_a+t_j\ge s_{aj}\) and nonnegativity constraints. It is totally unimodular. To check this directly, negate the columns belonging to the \(h_a\). Every resulting row has either one nonzero entry, or two opposite entries. For any square submatrix, expand along a row with at most one nonzero entry; if all rows have two entries, their row sums vanish and the submatrix is singular. Induction gives determinants only \(0,1,-1\).

The selected dual solution is a vertex of this original dual polyhedron. Its price vector is unique by the least-minimizer argument, and its \(h\)-vector is uniquely determined because all \(\mu_a>0\). If it were a nontrivial convex combination of feasible dual points, both points would have to attain both optimization objectives, contradicting uniqueness.

Total unimodularity therefore implies that its \(h_a,t_j\) are multiples of \(1/D\). Hence the updated prices \(z_j=\ell_j+t_j\) remain on the same grid.

Their magnitudes are bounded by original input values:

\(\displaystyle r_j\le\ell_j\le H_j, \qquad H_j=\max\{r_j,\max_a m_{aj}\}. \)

For an active class this follows from \(z_j\le B_j\); for an inactive class the least auxiliary price is unchanged. Also,

\(\displaystyle 0\le w_a\le\max_jv_{aj},\qquad 0\le h_a\le\max_jv_{aj}.\)

Thus every stage has polynomial encoding length.

Each stage uses two exact rational LP optimizations, with \(\tau+m\) dual variables and at most \(\tau m\) pair constraints, plus an optimal transportation allocation. Standard rational linear programming computes these in polynomial time and returns allocations of polynomial encoding length. With at most \(\tau m+1\) stages, the entire algorithm is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus nonexistence never occurs for either requirement; both associated search problems are polynomial-time solvable.
