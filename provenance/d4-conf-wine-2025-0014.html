A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass agent types and their masses; \(\tau\) is not the number of possible rankings. Running times are measured in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each \(\succ_p\) is a strict total ranking of \(Q\). Let \(\mathcal D(\succ)\) be all finite-valued nonnegative pseudometrics on \(P\cup Q\) satisfying \(q\succ_p r\Rightarrow d(p,q)\leq d(p,r)\); distinct types may be colocated, and rankings may resolve distance ties arbitrarily.
A feasible transport plan belongs to \(\mathcal X(\mu,\nu)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{P\times Q}:\sum_qx_{pq}=\mu_p,\ \sum_px_{pq}=\nu_q\}\). Mass may split, all mass must be assigned, and a deterministic mechanism observes only \(I\) and returns one such plan.
Define \(\Phi_\infty(x,d)=\max\{d(p,q):x_{pq}>0\}\), \(\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)=\min_{y\in\mathcal X(\mu,\nu)}\Phi_\infty(y,d)\), and \(V(I)=\inf_{\mathcal M}\sup_{d\in\mathcal D(\succ)}\Phi_\infty(\mathcal M(I),d)/\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)\). Interpret \(0/0=1\) and a positive numerator divided by zero as \(+\infty\).
Define \(I_8\) with \(P=\{a_1,\ldots,a_8\}\), \(Q=\{b_1,\ldots,b_8\}\), and every mass \(1/8\). Exact characterization and the eight-type profile

For the profile specified in the question,
\[ \boxed{V(I_8)=5.} \]
Thus a deterministic transport mechanism attains a worst-case ratio strictly smaller than \(7\), including under every uniform replication.

For arbitrary supported societies, there is an exact finite characterization of \(V(I)\) by linear programs and a bottleneck transport problem. It yields an optimal deterministic mechanism in time \(m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). In particular, \(V(I)\) is always finite and rational.

1. Threshold feasibility

For a bipartite graph \(E\subseteq P\times Q\), write
\[ N_E(A)=\{q\in Q:\text{some }p\in A\text{ satisfies }(p,q)\in E\}, \]
and let \(\mu(A)=\sum_{p\in A}\mu_p\), with analogous notation for \(\nu\).

A feasible transport plan supported on \(E\) exists if and only if
\[ \mu(A)\leq \nu(N_E(A))\qquad\text{for every }A\subseteq P. \tag{1} \]

Indeed, necessity follows by summing the transported mass from \(A\). For sufficiency, construct a flow network with capacities \(\mu_p\) from the source to \(p\), capacities \(2\) on the edges of \(E\), and capacities \(\nu_q\) from \(q\) to the sink. A cut crossing an edge of \(E\) has capacity at least \(2\). Every other cut, whose source-side agent set is \(A\), has capacity at least
\[ 1-\mu(A)+\nu(N_E(A)). \]
Condition (1) therefore makes every cut have capacity at least \(1\). The max-flow min-cut theorem gives a flow of value \(1\), which is the required transport plan.

For \(d\in\mathcal D(\succ)\) and \(t\geq0\), define
\[ E_t(d)=\{(p,q):d(p,q)\leq t\}. \]
Consequently,
\[ \operatorname{OPT}(I,d)\leq t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_t(d)\text{ supports a feasible transport plan}. \tag{2} \]
The optimum is attained: feasibility changes only at the finitely many agent–item distances.

2. An exact characterization for arbitrary masses

For each edge \((p,q)\), define its normalized worst-case distance
\[ W_{pq} = \sup\{d(p,q):d\in\mathcal D(\succ),\ \operatorname{OPT}(I,d)\leq1\}. \tag{3} \]
Allow \(W_{pq}=+\infty\).

These quantities satisfy \(W_{pq}\geq1\). To see this, take the pseudometric with distance \(1\) between every two distinct types. It is consistent with every ranking and has optimum \(1\).

Edgewise characterization of a plan’s distortion

For every feasible plan \(x\),
\[ \sup_{d\in\mathcal D(\succ)} \frac{\Phi_\infty(x,d)}{\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)} = \max_{x_{pq}>0}W_{pq}. \tag{4} \]

To prove the upper bound, first suppose \(T=\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)>0\). Scaling \(d\) by \(1/T\) gives a consistent pseudometric with optimum \(1\), so
\[ \frac{\Phi_\infty(x,d)}{T} \leq \max_{x_{pq}>0}W_{pq}. \]
If \(T=0\) and all supported \(W_{pq}\) are finite, every supported distance must be zero. Otherwise, arbitrary scaling of \(d\) would make one such \(W_{pq}\) infinite. The ratio is then \(1\), which is also bounded by the right-hand side.

For the reverse bound, fix a supported edge and a metric appearing in (3) with \(d(p,q)>0\). If its optimum is positive, the plan’s ratio is at least \(d(p,q)\), because that optimum is at most \(1\). If its optimum is zero, the ratio is infinite. Taking suprema gives the reverse inequality, including when \(W_{pq}\) is infinite.

Now put
\[ E_\lambda=\{(p,q):W_{pq}\leq\lambda\}. \]
Equation (4) and threshold feasibility give the exact formula
\[ \boxed{ V(I)= \min\left\{\lambda\geq1: \mu(A)\leq\nu(N_{E_\lambda}(A)) \text{ for every }A\subseteq P \right\}. } \tag{5} \]
The minimum is finite, as proved in Section 3. Any feasible flow on \(E_{V(I)}\) attains \(V(I)\).

There is no additional restriction hidden in the infimum over mechanisms: at a fixed instance, any feasible plan can be selected by a deterministic mechanism, using an arbitrary fixed feasible rule on other instances.

Computing the edge weights

Write the ranking of \(p\) as
\[ q_{p,1}\succ_p q_{p,2}\succ_p\cdots\succ_p q_{p,m}. \]
For \(k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^P\), define the prefix graph
\[ H_k=\{(p,q_{p,j}):j\leq k_p\}. \]
Keep precisely those \(k\) for which \(H_k\) supports a feasible transport plan.

For each retained \(k\) and each edge \((p,q)\), solve the following linear program:

  • maximize \(d(p,q)\);
  • impose nonnegativity, symmetry, zero diagonal, and every triangle inequality;
  • impose

\[ d(p,q_{p,j})\leq d(p,q_{p,j+1}) \qquad\text{for every }p,\ j<m; \]

  • impose

\[ d(p,q_{p,j})\leq1 \qquad\text{whenever }j\leq k_p. \]

Let \(U_{pq}(k)\) denote its optimum, with value \(+\infty\) if unbounded. Then
\[ W_{pq}=\max_{\substack{k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^P\\H_k\text{ feasible}}}U_{pq}(k). \tag{6} \]

For justification, every feasible point of one of these programs has optimum at most \(1\), because \(H_k\) is feasible. Conversely, if a consistent metric has optimum at most \(1\), each row of \(E_1(d)\) is a nonempty ranking prefix. Thus \(E_1(d)=H_k\) for a retained \(k\), and that metric satisfies the corresponding program’s constraints.

There are at most \(m^\tau\) prefix vectors. Each program has \(\binom{m+\tau}{2}\) distance variables and polynomially many constraints. Exact rational linear programming computes its optimum or detects unboundedness in polynomial time. After computing the weights, sort their finite values and use max-flow feasibility to find the first admissible threshold in (5).

The total running time is therefore
\[ m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \]
All finite weights are rational, so (5) also proves rationality of \(V(I)\).

3. A finite guarantee depending only on the supported agent types

For completeness, a simple deterministic mechanism establishes
\[ 1\leq V(I)\leq3^{\tau-1}. \tag{7} \]
This is a valid universal upper bound, without a claim of sharpness.

Order the agent types arbitrarily. Process them sequentially, assigning each its mass greedily to its highest-ranked items with remaining capacity.

Fix a consistent metric and let \(T=\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)\). Start with an optimal transport plan. Suppose the current residual plan \(z\) uses only edges of distance at most \(C\), and the next type is \(p\). Let \(g\) be its greedy row.

Call an item \(q\) a deficit item if \(g_q>z_{pq}\), and an item \(r\) a surplus item if \(z_{pr}>g_r\). Every deficit item precedes every surplus item in \(p\)’s ranking. Indeed, for a surplus item \(r\), the greedy row leaves some of its available capacity unused; hence it has already exhausted \(p\)’s mass by that position. Any item on which the greedy row increases its assignment must occur earlier.

Thus, for every deficit \(q\) and surplus \(r\),
\[ d(p,q)\leq d(p,r)\leq C. \]
In each deficit column \(q\), take \(g_q-z_{pq}\) mass from the other agents’ original assignments. Enough mass is available because \(g_q\) does not exceed the remaining column capacity. Redistribute those donor amounts to the surplus columns, whose total released capacity equals the total deficit.

If agent \(s\)’s mass is moved from \(q\) to \(r\), its new distance satisfies
\[ d(s,r) \leq d(s,q)+d(q,p)+d(p,r) \leq3C. \]
All edges of the newly fixed greedy row have distance at most \(C\): existing positive edges already satisfy this, and increased edges are deficit edges.

This produces a feasible residual plan with distances at most \(3C\). Repeating the argument fixes the first \(\tau-1\) rows; the final row is forced by the remaining capacities. The resulting plan has bottleneck at most \(3^{\tau-1}T\).

The same reasoning applies when \(T=0\), yielding bottleneck zero and ratio \(1\). This proves (7), and hence finiteness in (5).

4. Equal masses: splitting cannot improve the instance-wise value

Suppose there are \(n\) agent types and \(n\) item types, all of mass \(1/n\). The support of every feasible transport plan contains a perfect matching.

Indeed, for any agent set \(A\), feasibility implies
\[ \frac{|A|}{n}\leq\frac{|N(A)|}{n}. \]
Hall’s theorem therefore supplies a perfect matching in the support.

For every metric, that matching’s bottleneck is no larger than the original plan’s bottleneck. Applying this to an optimal plan also shows that the transport optimum equals the perfect-matching optimum. Consequently,
\[ V(I) = \min_{\text{perfect matchings }M} \sup_{d\in\mathcal D(\succ)} \frac{\max_p d(p,M(p))}{\operatorname{OPT}(I,d)}. \tag{8} \]

Thus splitting mass does not improve the value of a fixed equal-mass profile. The cited discrete lower bound is an existential statement over profiles; it does not by itself establish the same lower bound for the particular fixed rankings in this question. [Hastings–Ramakrishnan, Theorem 4 and Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05460v1#A3)

6. A deterministic mechanism with ratio at most \(5\)

Use the perfect matching
\[ \begin{aligned} &a_1\mapsto b_8,\qquad a_2\mapsto b_1,\qquad a_3\mapsto b_2,\qquad a_4\mapsto b_3,\\ &a_5\mapsto b_4,\qquad a_6\mapsto b_5,\qquad a_7\mapsto b_6,\qquad a_8\mapsto b_7. \end{aligned} \tag{9} \]
Assign mass \(1/8\) on each of these edges.

Fix any consistent pseudometric. By Section 4, choose an optimal perfect matching \(M^\star\), and put
\[ T=\max_i d(a_i,M^\star(a_i)). \]

Favorite items have distance at most \(T\)

Every agent prefers its favorite item to its assigned item in \(M^\star\). Therefore its favorite-item distance is at most \(T\).

Second-ranked items have distance at most \(3T\)

Consider either pair sharing a favorite item \(f\), and let \(h\) be their common second-ranked item. At least one agent \(z\) in the pair is assigned an item other than \(f\). Consequently,
\[ d(z,h)\leq T,\qquad d(z,f)\leq T, \]
so \(d(f,h)\leq2T\). For either agent \(p\) in that pair,
\[ d(p,h)\leq d(p,f)+d(f,h)\leq3T. \tag{10} \]

The common third-ranked item \(b_4\) has distance at most \(5T\)

Consider one half of four agents. Its three internal items are its two favorite leaves and their common parent \(h\). At least one agent \(z\) in that half is assigned outside these three items. The rankings imply
\[ d(z,b_4)\leq T,\qquad d(z,h)\leq T. \]
For any agent \(p\) in the same half, (10) gives
\[ d(p,b_4) \leq d(p,h)+d(h,z)+d(z,b_4) \leq5T. \tag{11} \]

The edge \((a_1,b_8)\) has distance at most \(5T\)

Let \(r\) be the agent assigned \(b_8\) in \(M^\star\).

If \(r\) is in the left half, it ranks \(b_1\) before \(b_8\), whence
\[ d(b_1,b_8)\leq d(b_1,r)+d(r,b_8)\leq2T. \]
It follows that \(d(a_1,b_8)\leq3T\).

Now suppose \(r\) is in the right half. It satisfies
\[ d(r,b_4)\leq T,\qquad d(r,b_1)\leq d(r,b_3). \tag{12} \]
Among the four left-half agents, some agent \(z\) is assigned outside \(\{b_1,b_2,b_3\}\). Hence
\[ d(z,b_4)\leq T. \]
Let \(f\in\{b_1,b_3\}\) be \(z\)’s favorite item. Then \(d(z,f)\leq T\), so
\[ d(r,f)\leq d(r,b_4)+d(b_4,z)+d(z,f)\leq3T. \]
If \(f=b_1\), this directly bounds \(d(r,b_1)\). If \(f=b_3\), comparison (12) gives the same bound. Therefore
\[ d(b_1,b_8)\leq d(b_1,r)+d(r,b_8)\leq4T, \]
and
\[ d(a_1,b_8)\leq d(a_1,b_1)+d(b_1,b_8)\leq5T. \tag{13} \]

The edges of (9) are now bounded respectively by
\[ 5T,\ T,\ 3T,\ T,\ 5T,\ T,\ 3T,\ T. \]
Thus the mechanism has ratio at most \(5\). When \(T=0\), these inequalities give numerator zero, so the prescribed ratio is \(1\). We have proved
\[ V(I_8)\leq5. \tag{14} \]

7. Explicit witnesses proving the lower bound \(5\)

For every agent \(p=a_i\), construct a consistent tree pseudometric \(d^{(i)}\) with
\[ d^{(i)}(a_i,b_8)=5, \qquad \operatorname{OPT}(I_8,d^{(i)})=1. \tag{15} \]

Let \(f\) be \(a_i\)’s favorite leaf item, \(g\) its sibling leaf item, and \(h\in\{b_2,b_6\}\) their common parent. Take three branches \(A,B,C\) emanating from a center \(o\), with the following placements:

| Branch or center | Placements |
|---|---|
| Center \(o\) | \(h\) and \(b_4\) |
| Branch \(A\) | The other agent sharing \(f\), at distance \(1\); \(f\), at distance \(2\); \(a_i\), at distance \(3\) |
| Branch \(B\) | Both agents sharing \(g\), at distance \(1\); \(g\), at distance \(2\) |
| Branch \(C\) | All four agents in the other half, at distance \(1\); its three internal items and \(b_8\), at distance \(2\) |

Use tree-path distance, restricted to the sixteen types. Colocations are permitted by the question.

Consistency

In each agent’s prescribed ranking order, its distances are:

  • for \(a_i\):

\[ 1,3,3,5,5,5,5,5; \]

  • for the other agent sharing \(f\), and for the two agents sharing \(g\):

\[ 1,1,1,3,3,3,3,3; \]

  • for each agent in the other half:

\[ 1,1,1,1,1,1,3,3. \]

Every sequence is nondecreasing. Thus \(d^{(i)}\in\mathcal D(\succ)\), including all the specified tie-breaking comparisons.

Optimum

There is a perfect matching of bottleneck \(1\):

  • assign \(a_i\) to \(f\), and its paired agent to \(h\);
  • assign the two agents sharing \(g\) to \(g\) and \(b_4\);
  • assign the four remaining agents to their half’s three internal items and \(b_8\).

Every one of these distances is exactly \(1\). Conversely, \(a_i\)’s distance to every item is at least \(1\), so every feasible transport plan has bottleneck at least \(1\). Hence the optimum is exactly \(1\).

Finally, \(a_i\) and \(b_8\) lie on different branches at distances \(3\) and \(2\) from the center. Their distance is \(5\), proving (15).

Now take any feasible transport plan \(x\). Since \(b_8\) has positive demand, some \(i\) satisfies \(x_{a_i b_8}>0\). For the corresponding witness,
\[ \frac{\Phi_\infty(x,d^{(i)})} {\operatorname{OPT}(I_8,d^{(i)})} \geq5. \]
Therefore \(V(I_8)\geq5\). Together with (14), this proves
\[ \boxed{V(I_8)=5.} \]

8. Uniform replication

Under the definition in the question, multiplying every mass by a positive integer \(k\) and then normalizing returns the original masses:
\[ \frac{k\mu_p}{k\sum_r\mu_r}=\mu_p, \qquad \frac{k\nu_q}{k\sum_s\nu_s}=\nu_q. \]
The support and rankings also remain unchanged. Thus replication leaves \(I\), and therefore \(V(I)\), unchanged.

In particular, every specified uniform replication of \(I_8\) has value \(5\).
