Partial solution

The following statements are proved below:

  • Recovery has deterministic bit complexity

$$2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,b),$$
without a conditioning promise.

  • A deterministic polynomial-time recovery algorithm would imply $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{RP}$.
  • A nontrivial subclass is polynomial-time solvable: every non-anchor pair and triple has uniform choices, and the mixing weight differs from $1/2$.
  • Inverse sensitivity can be exponential in $L$, even with fixed $m$, fixed $\epsilon$, uniformly separated components, and a fixed positive margin above the coordinate bound.
  • The dependence on $b$ cannot be eliminated: some fixed promised inputs require output length $\Omega(b)$.

These results do not establish deterministic, promise-preserving $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness or a tight worst-case complexity bound.

1. An unconditional deterministic upper bound

1.1 Polynomial equations describing feasibility

Write
$$ x_i=\theta_i^{(1)},\qquad y_i=\theta_i^{(2)}, \qquad X_S=\sum_{a_i\in S}x_i,\qquad Y_S=\sum_{a_i\in S}y_i. $$
For each available type, the observation equation is equivalent to
$$ \mu_{S,a_i}X_SY_S -\phi_S\bigl(\alpha x_iY_S+(1-\alpha)y_iX_S\bigr)=0. $$
The equivalence follows from
$$ X_S,Y_S\ge |S|\epsilon>0. $$
These equations have degree at most three.

The other constraints are
$$ \sum_i x_i=\sum_i y_i=1,\qquad x_i,y_i\ge\epsilon,\qquad \alpha>0,\qquad 1-\alpha>0, $$
and
$$ D:=\sum_i(x_i-y_i)^2>0. $$
The last constraint is equivalent to distinctness of the components.

On a promised input, every possible type has positive mass: each mixture choice probability is positive, and every menu has positive mass. A four-element group contributes
$$ 6\cdot2+4\cdot3+1\cdot4=28 $$
types. Identifying duplicate menus can only decrease this number, so
$$ \tau\le 28C=O(m). $$

1.2 Exact feasibility in a rational box

For a polynomial $g$, the following encodings are equivalent over the reals:
$$ g\ge0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists u:\ g-u^2=0, $$
and
$$ g>0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists u:\ gu^2-1=0. $$
Apply these encodings to all inequalities, including any imposed rational box bounds.

There are $O(m)$ original and auxiliary variables. The resulting equations have degree at most four; the highest degree comes from $Du^2-1=0$. Clear denominators in each equation, obtaining integer polynomials $E_1,\ldots,E_s$, and form
$$ P=\sum_{\ell=1}^s E_\ell^2. $$
Over the reals, $P=0$ is equivalent to all the encoded constraints. The degree of $P$ is at most eight.

If the box bounds have bit length at most $H_{\mathrm{box}}$, this construction has polynomial description length in $m,\tau,L,H_{\mathrm{box}}$: constant-degree polynomials in $O(m)$ variables have only polynomially many possible monomials, and clearing the rational denominators increases coefficient lengths polynomially.

The one-block specialization of the real quantifier-elimination bound in [Basu, Theorem 2.18](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf) decides whether an integer polynomial of degree $d$ in $k$ variables has a real zero using $d^{O(k)}$ arithmetic operations, with intermediate integer lengths bounded by $H d^{O(k)}$, where $H$ bounds coefficient lengths. Consequently, with $d\le8$ and $k=O(m)$, box feasibility has bit complexity
$$ 2^{O(m)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,H_{\mathrm{box}}). $$
This theorem permits singular and unbounded zero sets; no regularity or separation hypothesis is required.

1.3 Recovery by exact bisection

Let $N=2m+1$. Start with the box $[0,1]^N$, which contains a feasible parameter by the promise.

For each coordinate, bisect its current interval at a rational midpoint $h$. Decide whether the current box contains a feasible parameter with that coordinate at most $h$. Retain the lower closed half if the answer is yes, and the upper closed half otherwise.

The box remains feasible after each operation. In the second case, no feasible point has coordinate at most $h$, so every feasible point in the previous box belongs to the retained upper half.

Bisect every coordinate
$$ k=b+\lceil\log_2N\rceil+1 $$
times. The midpoint vector $\widehat p$ of the resulting box satisfies, for every point $q$ in that box,
$$ \|\widehat p-q\|_2\le \sqrt N\,2^{-k-1}\le2^{-b}. $$
There is a feasible $q$ in the box, and the uniqueness promise gives $q=p$ or $q=\sigma p$. Thus
$$ d(\widehat p,p)\le2^{-b}. $$

There are $O(m(b+\log m))$ feasibility calls, and their box bounds have bit length $O(b+\log m)$. Therefore
$$ \boxed{ T_{\mathrm{recovery}} \le 2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,b). } $$
In particular, recovery is polynomial-time solvable for fixed $m$. Conditioning contributes no additional parameter to this exact-arithmetic bound.

2. A local identification lemma

The following lemma will be used for the hardness construction, the polynomial-time subclass, and the conditioning example.

Lemma. Consider an anchor and three other alternatives. Suppose the observations are uniform on every pair and on the triple of non-anchor alternatives. Suppose also that, on a menu containing the anchor and $k$ non-anchor alternatives, its probability is
$$ F(k)=\frac{\lambda}{1+kx}+\frac{1-\lambda}{1+ky}, \qquad k=1,2,3, $$
where $0<\lambda<1$ and $x,y>0$ are distinct.

Every positive two-component representation has equal non-anchor weights within each component. After scaling each component’s anchor weight to one, its common non-anchor ratios and mixing weight are either
$$ (\lambda,x,y) \quad\text{or}\quad (1-\lambda,y,x). $$

Proof. Let a candidate representation have mixing weight $\beta\in(0,1)$. Normalize its component weights on the non-anchor triple, obtaining vectors $u,v$ whose coordinate sums are one. Uniform triple choices give
$$ \beta u_i+(1-\beta)v_i=\frac13. $$
For distinct indices $i,j,k$ in this triple, uniform pair choices give
$$ \beta\frac{u_i-u_j}{1-u_k} +(1-\beta)\frac{v_i-v_j}{1-v_k}=0. $$
All denominators are positive.

Subtracting the triple equations for $i$ and $j$ yields
$$ v_i-v_j=-\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}(u_i-u_j). $$
Substitution in the pair equation gives
$$ (u_i-u_j)(u_k-v_k)=0. $$
The triple equation also gives
$$ u_k-v_k=\frac{u_k-1/3}{1-\beta}. $$
Hence
$$ (u_i-u_j)(u_k-1/3)=0. $$

If $u$ were nonuniform, at least two coordinates would differ from $1/3$, because its coordinates sum to one. Call these coordinates $u_1,u_2$. Taking $k=1$ forces $u_2=u_3$, and taking $k=2$ forces $u_1=u_3$. This contradicts nonuniformity. Therefore
$$ u=v=(1/3,1/3,1/3). $$
Thus the original non-anchor weights are equal within each component.

Scale each component’s anchor weight to one, and let its common non-anchor ratio be $z$ or $w$. Its anchor probabilities are
$$ R(t)=\frac{\beta}{1+tz}+\frac{1-\beta}{1+tw}. $$
Both $R$ and $F$ have numerator degree at most one and denominator degree at most two. They agree at $t=0,1,2,3$. Cross-multiplication produces a polynomial of degree at most three with four distinct zeros, so the rational functions are identical.

The two poles of $F$, at $-1/x$ and $-1/y$, are distinct and uncancelled, because both mixture coefficients are positive. Thus $z\ne w$, and matching poles gives
$$ \{z,w\}=\{x,y\}. $$
Matching the coefficients of the two simple fractions then gives the stated alternatives for $\beta$. ∎

The same proof gives a useful degenerate case: if the anchor probabilities are $1/(1+kr)$, then both component ratios must equal $r$. A representation with two distinct positive ratios would have two uncancelled poles and could not equal this single-pole rational function.

3. Unique partition and randomized hardness

3.1 A deterministic encoding of partition

Let $w_1,\ldots,w_n$ be positive binary integers, and define
$$ W=\sum_{t=1}^n w_t,\qquad m=3n+1,\qquad K=m+\frac32W,\qquad \epsilon=\frac1{K+1}. $$
Then $0<\epsilon<1/m$.

For this value of $m$, the prescribed groups have a common anchor and disjoint non-anchor triples. In group $t$, generate the choice probabilities from the equal mixture of local weights
$$ (1,1,1,1) \quad\text{and}\quad (1,1+w_t,1+w_t,1+w_t), $$
with the anchor first.

There are $11n$ distinct menus. Give each menu mass $1/(11n)$ and multiply this mass by its conditional choice probabilities to obtain the type masses. These masses are positive rationals, sum to one, and have polynomial total encoding length in the partition instance. There are $28n$ supported types.

By the lemma, every feasible representation has mixing weight $1/2$. For each group, one component’s non-anchor ratio to its anchor is $1+w_t$, and the other’s ratio is one.

Let $I$ index the groups assigned the larger ratio in component one. Scaling each component’s anchor ratio to one, its normalization denominator is
$$ T_1=m+3\sum_{t\in I}w_t, \qquad T_2=m+3\sum_{t\notin I}w_t. $$
The anchor is a minimum coordinate in each component. Consequently, the coordinate constraints are equivalent to
$$ T_1,T_2\le K+1. $$
Since $T_1+T_2=2K$, these inequalities are equivalent to
$$ \left|3\left(\sum_{t\in I}w_t-\frac W2\right)\right|\le1. $$

If $W$ is even, the quantity inside the absolute value is a multiple of three; only zero satisfies the inequality. If $W$ is odd, its absolute value is at least $3/2$, so the inequality is impossible. Thus feasibility is equivalent to
$$ \sum_{t\in I}w_t=\frac W2. $$

Conversely, every equal-sum partition gives a feasible representation reproducing all observations. Its normalization denominators are both $K$, so every coordinate is at least
$$ \frac1K>\frac1{K+1}=\epsilon. $$
The components are distinct because every $w_t$ is positive.

We have therefore proved a bijection between feasible parameter orbits and unordered equal-sum partitions $\{I,I^c\}$. In particular, a uniquely solvable unordered partition instance gives a promised recovery instance. All its feasible coordinates are strictly above $\epsilon$.

This also proves $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness of feasibility without the uniqueness promise, by the standard $\mathsf{NP}$-completeness of binary positive-integer PARTITION. It does not prove hardness of the promised recovery problem.

3.2 Decoding an approximate parameter

For a feasible partition, a non-anchor coordinate in group $t$ is either
$$ \frac1K \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{1+w_t}{K}. $$
Request
$$ b=\lceil\log_2(4K)\rceil, \qquad \eta=2^{-b}\le\frac1{4K}. $$
The threshold
$$ \frac{1+w_t/2}{K} $$
is at distance $w_t/(2K)\ge1/(2K)$ from both possible coordinate values.

Euclidean approximation error at most $\eta$ bounds each coordinate error by $\eta$. Thresholding one recovered component therefore yields either $I$ or $I^c$. The requested precision and the constructed input length are polynomial in the partition input length.

Thus uniquely solvable partition search reduces deterministically to promised recovery.

3.3 A self-contained isolation argument

We now prove the randomized consequence, including the endpoint handling.

Start with positive binary integers $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ and a nonnegative integer target $t$. Set $U=\sum_i u_i$. The cases $t=0$ and $t=U$ are immediately satisfiable; $t>U$ is immediately unsatisfiable. Hence only
$$ 0<t<U $$
needs reduction. Positive-integer binary Subset Sum remains $\mathsf{NP}$-complete with these trivial cases handled separately.

Let
$$ \mathcal F=\left\{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}:\sum_{i\in S}u_i=t\right\}. $$
Choose independent random integers $r_i$ uniformly from $\{1,\ldots,2n\}$.

If $\mathcal F$ is nonempty, with probability at least $1/2$ it has a unique subset minimizing $\sum_{i\in S}r_i$. To prove this, fix an index $i$ and condition on all $r_j$ for $j\ne i$. Define
$$ A_i=\min_{\substack{S\in\mathcal F\\i\in S}} \sum_{j\in S\setminus\{i\}}r_j, \qquad B_i=\min_{\substack{S\in\mathcal F\\i\notin S}} \sum_{j\in S}r_j, $$
when both families are nonempty.

A minimum-weight subset containing $i$ ties a minimum-weight subset omitting $i$ only when
$$ r_i=B_i-A_i. $$
Conditionally, this has probability at most $1/(2n)$. If the overall minimum is attained by two distinct subsets, those subsets differ at some index $i$, and such a tie occurs for that index. A union bound over the indices gives tie probability at most $1/2$.

Now define
$$ M=4n^2,\qquad v_i=Mu_i+r_i, $$
and consider the $2n^2$ Subset Sum instances with entries $v_i$ and targets
$$ t_\ell=Mt+\ell,\qquad 1\le\ell\le2n^2. $$
If a subset satisfies $\sum_{i\in S}v_i=t_\ell$, then
$$ M\left(\sum_{i\in S}u_i-t\right) =\ell-\sum_{i\in S}r_i. $$
The right-hand side has absolute value at most $2n^2<M$. The parenthesized quantity is an integer, so it must be zero. Thus every solution to a generated instance solves the original instance.

If the original instance is unsatisfiable, all generated instances are unsatisfiable. If it is satisfiable and the random weights isolate a unique minimum subset, the instance indexed by that subset’s random weight has exactly one solution.

Every generated entry is positive. Moreover, writing $V=\sum_i v_i$,
$$ V-t_\ell=M(U-t)+\sum_i r_i-\ell>0. $$
Thus every generated target satisfies
$$ 0<t_\ell<V, $$
as required for the next conversion.

3.4 Conversion to partition and the consequence

For positive entries with total $V$ and target $t'$ strictly between zero and $V$, append the positive integer
$$ |V-2t'| $$
when it is nonzero.

If $V>2t'$, the new half-total is $V-t'$. The side of a partition omitting the appended entry has this sum; its complement among the original entries sums to $t'$.

If $V<2t'$, the new half-total is $t'$, and the side omitting the appended entry is itself a target subset.

In both cases these operations are reversible, giving a bijection between target subsets and unordered partitions. If $V=2t'$, omit the appended entry. Existence is still equivalent, but a unique target subset is impossible because every solution has a distinct complementary solution.

Consequently, the isolated unique Subset Sum instance yields a uniquely solvable unordered partition instance and hence a promised society.

Suppose a deterministic polynomial-time promised recovery algorithm exists. Run it on all generated societies at the precision in Section 3.2, with a polynomial timeout. Decode its output and verify the resulting integer subset directly.

An unsatisfiable original instance is never accepted, regardless of the algorithm’s behavior outside its promise. On a satisfiable instance, with probability at least $1/2$, one call satisfies the recovery promise and supplies a verified witness. The timeout preserves that successful call and controls all other calls.

This is an $\mathsf{RP}$ algorithm for an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem. Since $\mathsf{RP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$,
$$ \boxed{ \text{Deterministic polynomial-time promised recovery} \ \Longrightarrow\ \mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{RP}. } $$
The reduction is randomized; it does not supply a deterministic promise-preserving $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness proof.

4. A polynomial-time subclass with unknown mixing weight

Proposition. Suppose a promised input additionally satisfies:

  • In every group, choices on all non-anchor pairs and on the non-anchor triple are uniform.
  • Its mixing weight is different from $1/2$.

Then recovery is possible in deterministic time
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,b). $$
No quantitative separation from $1/2$ is needed.

Proof. The first part of the lemma forces equal non-anchor weights within each component of each group. Let $f_{t,k}$ be the observed anchor probability in group $t$ on a menu with $k$ non-anchor alternatives. It is independent of which such alternatives are used.

First test whether the group has a single common ratio. Set
$$ r_t=f_{t,1}^{-1}-1, $$
and test exactly whether
$$ f_{t,2}=\frac1{1+2r_t}, \qquad f_{t,3}=\frac1{1+3r_t}. $$
If these equalities hold, the degenerate case of the lemma forces both component ratios to equal $r_t$.

Otherwise, the group has two distinct ratios. Write its anchor-probability rational function as
$$ F_t(z)=\frac{1+c_tz}{1+s_tz+v_tz^2}. $$
The coefficients are obtained from the three linear equations
$$ f_{t,k}(1+s_tk+v_tk^2)=1+c_tk, \qquad k=1,2,3. $$

These equations have a unique solution. Indeed, any two solutions yield a cross-product difference of degree at most three vanishing at $0,1,2,3$. Thus their rational functions agree. The true function has two distinct uncancelled poles, so its reduced denominator has degree two. Any other denominator of degree at most two, normalized to have constant coefficient one, must equal it; the numerator must then also agree.

Let
$$ \Delta_t=s_t^2-4v_t>0. $$
The two ratios are
$$ \frac{s_t-\sqrt{\Delta_t}}2, \qquad \frac{s_t+\sqrt{\Delta_t}}2. $$
If component one has ratio $z_t$ and component two has ratio $w_t$, then
$$ c_t=\alpha w_t+(1-\alpha)z_t, $$
so
$$ c_t-\frac{s_t}2 =\left(\alpha-\frac12\right)(w_t-z_t). $$

Choose the global labeling with $\alpha>1/2$. For every nondegenerate group,
$$ \alpha =\frac12+\frac{|c_t-s_t/2|}{\sqrt{\Delta_t}}, $$
and its oriented ratios are
$$ z_t= \frac{s_t-\operatorname{sgn}(c_t-s_t/2)\sqrt{\Delta_t}}2, $$
$$ w_t= \frac{s_t+\operatorname{sgn}(c_t-s_t/2)\sqrt{\Delta_t}}2. $$
The sign is determined by exact rational arithmetic. It is nonzero under the proposition’s hypothesis.

At least one group is nondegenerate. Otherwise, every alternative would have the same ratio to the anchor in both components; global normalization would make the components identical, contradicting feasibility.

Thus a nondegenerate group determines the mixing weight, and all groups determine consistently oriented ratios. Consistency on overlaps follows from the promised representation. Scaling the anchor ratio to one and normalizing the resulting global vectors recovers both components.

It remains to verify polynomial bit complexity. All rational coefficients above are produced by linear systems of fixed size. Let $H$, polynomial in the input length, bound their binary lengths, the lengths of the $\Delta_t$, and the length of $\epsilon$. Then
$$ \sqrt{\Delta_t}\ge2^{-H}, $$
and every true ratio to the anchor is at most
$$ \frac1\epsilon\le2^H. $$
The first inequality follows because a positive rational of encoding length at most $H$ is at least $2^{-H}$; this also implies the weaker displayed bound for its square root.

Approximate each required square root within $\rho$, where a dyadic $\rho$ is chosen to satisfy
$$ \rho\le \min\left\{ 2^{-H-1},\frac1m, \frac{\eta}{4m2^H\sqrt{2m+1}} \right\}. $$
Such approximations can be computed by rational bisection in polynomial time, since
$$ \log_2(1/\rho)=O(b+H+\log m). $$

Each ratio error is at most $\rho/2$. If $T$ is a component’s true normalization denominator and $\widehat T$ its approximation, then
$$ T\ge1,\qquad |T-\widehat T|\le m\rho/2,\qquad \widehat T\ge1/2. $$
For a ratio $r_i\le2^H$,
$$ \left| \frac{\widehat r_i}{\widehat T}-\frac{r_i}{T} \right| \le 2|\widehat r_i-r_i| +\frac{2r_i}{T}|\widehat T-T| \le(1+m2^H)\rho \le2m2^H\rho. $$

For the mixing weight, put $a=|c_t-s_t/2|$. Since $a/\sqrt{\Delta_t}=\alpha-1/2\le1/2$, the square-root approximation gives
$$ \left| \frac a{\widehat{\sqrt{\Delta_t}}} -\frac a{\sqrt{\Delta_t}} \right| \le2^H\rho. $$
Our choice of $\rho$ therefore bounds every parameter-coordinate error by $\eta/(2\sqrt{2m+1})$. The Euclidean error is at most $\eta/2$.

All operations and all rational lengths are polynomial in $m,\tau,L,b$, proving the proposition. ∎

This proposition applies only to the stated uniform non-anchor subclass. It does not establish polynomial-time recovery for general unequal-weight inputs.

5. Exponential sensitivity with separated components and strict coordinate margins

The preceding subclass also supplies a stronger conditioning example.

Fix
$$ m=13,\qquad \epsilon=\frac1{20}. $$
There are four groups with disjoint non-anchor triples. For a two-element set $I\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}$, define components $x_I,y_I$ by giving the anchor coordinate $1/19$ in both components. In group $t$, give each non-anchor coordinate the values
$$ (x_I)_i= \begin{cases} 2/19,&t\in I,\\ 1/19,&t\notin I, \end{cases} \qquad (y_I)_i= \begin{cases} 1/19,&t\in I,\\ 2/19,&t\notin I. \end{cases} $$
Each component has six non-anchor coordinates of each value, so its coordinates sum to
$$ \frac{1+6\cdot2+6\cdot1}{19}=1. $$
Every coordinate exceeds $\epsilon$ by at least
$$ \frac1{19}-\frac1{20}=\frac1{380}. $$
The component separation is fixed:
$$ \|x_I-y_I\|_2=\frac{\sqrt{12}}{19}. $$

Let
$$ 0<\delta\le\frac18,\qquad \alpha_\delta=\frac12+\delta, $$
and define
$$ p_{I,\delta}=(\alpha_\delta,x_I,y_I). $$
Both mixture weights are at least $3/8$.

Give each of the $44$ menus mass $1/44$, and generate its type masses from this parameter. Every type mass is positive.

5.1 Each input has exactly one feasible orbit

The non-anchor pairs and triples are uniform. In every group, the two anchor-relative ratios are one and two. The lemma forces any representation to have mixing weight $\alpha_\delta$ or $1-\alpha_\delta$.

After globally choosing the labeling with mixing weight greater than $1/2$, the lemma fixes the orientation of every group. Normalization then fixes both global components. Thus every constructed input satisfies the uniqueness promise.

5.2 Close observations and distant parameters

Take
$$ I=\{1,2\},\qquad J=\{1,3\}. $$
Without swapping, the parameters differ in six coordinates of each component, each by $1/19$. Hence
$$ \|p_{I,\delta}-p_{J,\delta}\|_2^2=\frac{12}{19^2}. $$
Swapping the second parameter gives the same squared component-coordinate difference and adds the squared mixing-weight difference $4\delta^2$. Therefore
$$ d(p_{I,\delta},p_{J,\delta})=\frac{\sqrt{12}}{19}. $$

The observations differ only in groups two and three. On an anchored menu with $k$ non-anchor alternatives, exchanging which component has ratio two changes the anchor probability by
$$ 2\delta\left| \frac1{1+2k}-\frac1{1+k} \right| = \frac{2\delta k}{(1+k)(1+2k)}. $$
For $k=1,2,3$, the maximum is $\delta/3$, attained at $k=1$. Each non-anchor probability is $(1-F(k))/k$, so its change is no larger. Non-anchor-only menus remain uniform.

Writing $\mu_{I,\delta},\mu_{J,\delta}$ for the full type-mass vectors, it follows that
$$ \|\mu_{I,\delta}-\mu_{J,\delta}\|_\infty =\frac{\delta}{132}. $$
Thus the two-point inverse-sensitivity ratio is exactly
$$ \frac{d(p_{I,\delta},p_{J,\delta})} {\|\mu_{I,\delta}-\mu_{J,\delta}\|_\infty} = \frac{132\sqrt{12}}{19\delta}. $$
