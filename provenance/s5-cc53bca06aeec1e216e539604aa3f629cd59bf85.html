The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given worker-type masses \(\mu_a>0\), uniform task arrivals \(s_j=1/m\), shared unknown values \(v_{a,j}\), bounded sub-Gaussian feedback, and horizon \(T\), assign each arriving indivisible task to a worker using past observations and pool feedback by type. For type-level allocation fractions \(x_{a,j}\) with \(\sum_a x_{a,j}=1\), define \(u_a(x)=\mu_a^{-1}\sum_j s_jv_{a,j}x_{a,j}\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\max_x\exp(\sum_a\mu_a\log u_a(x))\). Establish a regret bound for a type-pooled DA-EtC policy against this weighted population benchmark; in particular, determine when balanced masses permit \(\widetilde O(T^{2/3}(\tau m)^{1/3})\) regret.

Result

For balanced masses and fixed \(l,h,H,\sigma,\delta,c,C\), the proposed leading rate does hold. A bound proved directly for the stated policy is

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E R_\mu(T)=\widetilde O\!\left(T_0+T\sqrt{\frac{\tau m}{T_0}}+\sqrt{\tau T}+\tau\right),\)

provided exploration is sufficiently long. Consequently, choosing \(T_0\) appropriately gives \(\widetilde O(T^{2/3}(\tau m)^{1/3})\). The dimension-dependent additional terms in this bound are \(\widetilde O(\sqrt{\tau T}+\tau)\).

For general masses, dependence on small masses is essential: there are two-type instances with \(\mu_{\min}^{-1}\) polynomial in \(T\) for which this policy has linear expected regret, for every exploration length.

The proof below is independent of the constants in the [cited finite-agent analysis](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/yamada24a/yamada24a.pdf).

1. An explicit bound for every exploration length

Write

\(\displaystyle N=T-T_0,\qquad \mu_*=\min_a\mu_a,\qquad s=\min\{\sigma,H/2\},\qquad B=\frac{1+\delta}{l}.\)

The use of \(s\) is valid because conditional Hoeffding’s lemma makes the centered reward conditionally \(H^2/4\)-sub-Gaussian.

Define the following constants, which depend only on the value bounds and projection parameter:

\(\displaystyle \gamma=\frac{\delta}{2(1+\delta)},\qquad g=\frac{\delta}{2}-\log(1+\delta/2)>0,\qquad J=\frac{2}{\gamma}\max\{1,g^{-1}\}.\)

For any \(r>0\), put

\(\displaystyle K_N=Bh\,\tau\bigl(1+\log(Nh/l)\bigr)+\log N,\)

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=2s\sqrt{\frac{mr}{T_0\mu_*}},\qquad \theta=\frac{s}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{2Bh\,r}{N\mu_*}},\)

\(\displaystyle p_{\mathrm e}=\min\!\left\{1,\ \tau m\exp\!\left(-\frac{T_0\mu_*}{8m}\right)+2\tau mT_0e^{-r}\right\},\qquad p_{\mathrm n}=\min\{1,\tau Ne^{-r}\}.\)

Then the stated policy satisfies

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E R_\mu(T)\le \min\{hT/2,\mathcal B\},\)

where

\(\displaystyle \mathcal B=\frac h2T_0+\frac{5h}{4l}N\varepsilon+hN\theta+hK_N\bigl[1+(2+\log(h/l))J\bigr]+hN\bigl(\tfrac32p_{\mathrm e}+p_{\mathrm n}\bigr).\)

This bound requires neither \(\varepsilon<l\) nor \(\theta<1\); when these quantities are large, the bound simply becomes uninformative and the cap \(hT/2\) applies.

A convenient confidence-parameter version follows. For \(0<\alpha<1\), choose

\(\displaystyle r=\log\frac{8\tau mT}{\alpha}.\)

If

\(\displaystyle T_0\ge \frac{8m}{\mu_*}\log\frac{8\tau mT}{\alpha},\)

then \(p_{\mathrm e}\le\alpha\) and \(p_{\mathrm n}\le\alpha\). Thus the last term can be replaced by \((5h/2)N\alpha\).

The proof occupies the next four sections.

2. The estimated market and its dual

Condition on the exploration history, and write \(w_{a,j}=\hat v_{a,j}\). These numbers are now fixed and satisfy \(l\le w_{a,j}\le h\).

For aggregate utility vectors \(q\), define

\(\displaystyle G(q)=\prod_a(q_a/\mu_a)^{\mu_a},\)

with value zero if any coordinate is zero. Let \(q^*\) be an optimal aggregate utility vector for the estimated market, and let \(G^*=G(q^*)\).

The proportional allocation \(x_{a,j}=\mu_a\) proves \(G^*\ge l\). Weighted arithmetic–geometric mean gives \(G(q)\le\sum_aq_a\), so \(G^*\le h\).

Define

\(\displaystyle \beta_a^*=\frac{\mu_a}{q_a^*},\qquad P(\beta)=\frac1m\sum_j\max_a\beta_aw_{a,j},\qquad D(\beta)=P(\beta)-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a.\)

Here every \(q_a^*>0\), since a feasible allocation has positive welfare.

At an optimum, every positive \(x_{a,j}^*\) must maximize \(\beta_a^*w_{a,j}\). Otherwise shifting a sufficiently small amount of task \(j\) to a type with larger \(\beta_aw_{a,j}\) would increase the logarithmic objective. Therefore

\(\displaystyle P(\beta^*)=\sum_a\beta_a^*q_a^*=1.\)

For each type,

\(\displaystyle \beta_a^*\frac1m\sum_jw_{a,j}\le P(\beta^*)=1,\)

which gives \(\beta_a^*\le1/l\). Also \(q_a^*\le h\), hence \(\beta_a^*\ge\mu_a/h\). Thus \(\beta^*\) lies inside the policy’s projection box.

For every positive \(\beta\),

\(\displaystyle D(\beta)\ge\sum_a\bigl(\beta_aq_a^*-\mu_a\log\beta_a\bigr)\ge1+\log G^*.\)

The second inequality follows by minimizing each scalar expression over \(\beta_a>0\). Equality holds at \(\beta^*\), so the unrestricted dual optimum is

\(\displaystyle D^*=D(\beta^*)=1+\log G^*.\)

Finally, define the concave function

\(\displaystyle \Phi(q)=\min_{\beta\ \text{in the projection box}}\left\{\langle q,\beta\rangle-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\right\}.\)

Whenever \(q_a\le h\), its scalar summands are

\(\displaystyle \Phi_a(q_a)= \begin{cases} Bq_a-\mu_a\log B,&q_a\le\mu_a/B,\\ \mu_a+\mu_a\log(q_a/\mu_a),&q_a\ge\mu_a/B. \end{cases}\)

Indeed, the lower projection endpoint cannot bind: \(\mu_a/q_a\ge\mu_a/h\). In particular,

\(\displaystyle \Phi(q)=1+\log G(q)\quad\text{if every }q_a\ge\mu_a/B,\qquad \Phi(q^*)=D^*.\)

3. Direct control of the dual-averaging error

Let \(z_k=w_{a_k,j_k}e_{a_k}\), let \(S_k=\sum_{t\le k}z_t\), and set \(\Psi(\beta)=-\sum_a\mu_a\log\beta_a\).

The policy’s update is exactly

\(\displaystyle \beta(k)\in\arg\min_\beta\{\langle S_{k-1},\beta\rangle+(k-1)\Psi(\beta)\}.\)

For \(k=1\), it chooses the upper endpoint \(B\) in every coordinate. Define

\(\displaystyle L_k(\beta)=\langle z_k,\beta\rangle+\Psi(\beta).\)

The elementary “be-the-leader” inequality gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^NL_k(\beta(k+1))\le\min_\beta\sum_{k=1}^NL_k(\beta)=N\Phi(S_N/N).\)

For completeness, if \(F_k=\min_\beta\sum_{t\le k}L_t(\beta)\), then

\(\displaystyle F_k-F_{k-1}\ge L_k(\beta(k+1));\)

summing proves the inequality.

We next bound the difference between consecutive leaders. Suppose type \(a\) receives estimated reward \(z\), and its previous cumulative estimated utility is \(S\). The update implies

\(\displaystyle \beta_a(k+1)\ge\beta_a(k)\frac{S}{S+z}.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle z\bigl(\beta_a(k)-\beta_a(k+1)\bigr)_+\le Bh\,\frac{z}{S+z}.\)

For \(k\ge2\), every coordinate also satisfies

\(\displaystyle \frac{\beta_a(k+1)}{\beta_a(k)}\le\frac{k}{k-1}.\)

For the first round these ratios are at most one. Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^N\bigl[L_k(\beta(k))-L_k(\beta(k+1))\bigr]\le Bh\sum_a\sum_{\substack{k:\,a_k=a}}\frac{z_k}{S_a(k)}+\log N.\)

If a type receives successive positive rewards \(z_1,\ldots,z_b\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^b\frac{z_i}{z_1+\cdots+z_i}\le1+\log\frac{z_1+\cdots+z_b}{z_1}\le1+\log(Nh/l).\)

The first inequality follows by comparing each term after the first with the integral of \(1/x\) over its corresponding cumulative-utility interval. Summing over supported types proves the pathwise bound

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^NL_k(\beta(k))\le N\Phi(S_N/N)+K_N.\)

Because each new task is uniform and independent of the past,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[L_k(\beta(k))\mid\text{past}]=D(\beta(k)).\)

Moreover, \(\mathbb E[S_N/N]\) is feasible for the estimated market. Concavity of \(\Phi\), followed by evaluation at \(\beta^*\), gives

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E\Phi(S_N/N)\le\Phi(\mathbb E[S_N/N])\le D^*.\)

Combining these facts yields both

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^N\mathbb E[D(\beta(k))-D^*]\le K_N\)

and

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[D^*-\Phi(S_N/N)]\le K_N/N.\)

These expectations remain conditional on the exploration history.

4. Controlling small virtual utilities

Let \(\mathcal A\) be the event that some final virtual average satisfies

\(\displaystyle S_a(N)/N<\mu_a/B.\)

A useful consequence of positivity is that this event can be controlled without a factor \(\mu_*^{-1}\).

First, scaling any positive \(\beta\) by \(P(\beta)\) gives

\(\displaystyle D(\beta)-D^*\ge P(\beta)-1-\log P(\beta).\)

This follows from \(D(\beta/P(\beta))\ge D^*\) and the scaling identity for \(D\).

On \(\mathcal A\), choose a deficient type \(a\). For every \(k\),

\(\displaystyle \beta_a(k)\ge B\frac{k-1}{N},\)

because \(S_a(k-1)\le S_a(N)<\mu_aN/B\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle P(\beta(k))\ge l\beta_a(k)\ge(1+\delta)\frac{k-1}{N}.\)

Whenever \(k-1\ge(1-\gamma)N\), this price is at least \(1+\delta/2\), and hence

\(\displaystyle D(\beta(k))-D^*\ge g.\)

There are at least \(\gamma N-1\) such rounds. If \(N\ge2/\gamma\), the previous section gives

\(\displaystyle \Pr(\mathcal A)\le\frac{2K_N}{\gamma gN}.\)

For smaller \(N\), use \(\Pr(\mathcal A)\le1\) and \(K_N\ge1\). Thus, for every \(N\ge1\),

\(\displaystyle \Pr(\mathcal A)\le\min\{1,JK_N/N\}.\)

We can now convert the logarithmic potential bound to welfare. Set \(q=S_N/N\) and \(d=D^*-\Phi(q)\). On \(\mathcal A^c\),

\(\displaystyle G^*-G(q)=G^*(1-e^{-d})\le G^*d.\)

For every possible \(q\), evaluation of \(\Phi(q)\) at the feasible vector \(\beta_a=1/h\) gives

\(\displaystyle \Phi(q)\le1+\log h.\)

Since \(D^*\ge1+\log l\), we have \(d\ge-\log(h/l)\). Therefore, on \(\mathcal A\),

\(\displaystyle G^*-G(q)\le G^*d+G^*(1+\log(h/l)).\)

Taking expectations proves

\(\displaystyle N\mathbb E[G^*-G(S_N/N)]\le hK_N[1+(1+\log(h/l))J].\)

This proves the stated logarithmic optimization cost, uniformly over all estimated matrices in \([l,h]\).

5. Estimation error and realized rewards

The following concentration argument accommodates the stipulated conditional noise; independence of rewards is unnecessary.

For any pair or type, its selected centered rewards form a martingale sum \(M\). If its number of observations is \(n\), exponential-supermartingale bounds imply

\(\displaystyle \Pr\{M\ge s\sqrt{2nr},\ \text{count}=n\}\le e^{-r},\)

and likewise for the lower tail. Taking a union over possible positive counts gives the factors \(T_0\) and \(N\) appearing in \(p_{\mathrm e}\) and \(p_{\mathrm n}\).

During exploration, the count of pair \((a,j)\) is binomial with mean \(T_0\mu_a/m\). The binomial lower-tail bound

\(\displaystyle \Pr\{\text{count}<T_0\mu_a/(2m)\}\le\exp(-T_0\mu_a/(8m))\)

and the martingale bounds show that, except with probability at most \(p_{\mathrm e}\),

\(\displaystyle \max_{a,j}|w_{a,j}-v_{a,j}|\le\varepsilon.\)

Projection onto \([l,h]\) cannot increase this error because every true value belongs to that interval.

On this estimation event, when \(\varepsilon\le2l\),

\(\displaystyle w_{a,j}\ge(1-\varepsilon/(2l))v_{a,j}.\)

Monotonicity and homogeneity of \(G\) therefore imply

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}_\mu-G^*\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2l}\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\le\frac{h\varepsilon}{4l}.\)

For \(\varepsilon>2l\), the same upper bound follows from \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu-G^*\le\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\). Allowing for estimation failure gives

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\mathrm{OPT}_\mu-G^*]\le\frac{h\varepsilon}{4l}+\frac h2p_{\mathrm e}.\)

Next, let \(n_a\) be the exploitation allocation count, and let

\(\displaystyle V_a=\sum_{\substack{k\le N\\a_k=a}}v_{a,j_k},\qquad W_a^{\mathrm{ex}}=\sum_{\substack{k\le N\\a_k=a}}Y_{T_0+k}.\)

On \(\mathcal A^c\),

\(\displaystyle n_a\ge S_a(N)/h\ge N\mu_a/(Bh).\)

Except with probability at most \(p_{\mathrm n}\),

\(\displaystyle W_a^{\mathrm{ex}}\ge V_a-s\sqrt{2n_ar}.\)

Since \(V_a\ge2ln_a\), on these two events,

\(\displaystyle W_a^{\mathrm{ex}}\ge(1-\theta)V_a.\)

On the estimation event, if \(\eta=\varepsilon/l\le1\), we also have \(V_a\ge(1-\eta)S_a(N)\). Thus, when \(\eta,\theta<1\), monotonicity and homogeneity give

\(\displaystyle G(W^{\mathrm{ex}})\ge(1-\eta)(1-\theta)G(S_N)\ge(1-\eta-\theta)G(S_N).\)

If either parameter is at least one, the last inequality holds simply because its right-hand side is nonpositive. Since \(G(S_N)\le hN\),

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[G(S_N)-G(W^{\mathrm{ex}})]\le hN(\eta+\theta)+hN\bigl(p_{\mathrm e}+p_{\mathrm n}+\Pr(\mathcal A)\bigr).\)

Exploration rewards are nonnegative, so total realized welfare is at least \(G(W^{\mathrm{ex}})\). Decompose the remaining loss into

\(\displaystyle N(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu-G^*)+N(G^*-G(S_N/N))+G(S_N)-G(W^{\mathrm{ex}}).\)

Adding the bounds above, and using \(T_0\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\le hT_0/2\), proves \(\mathcal B\). The cap follows directly from nonnegative realized welfare and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\le h/2\).

6. Balanced masses, horizon conditions, and lower-order terms

Assume \(c/\tau\le\mu_a\le C/\tau\), with fixed \(c,C\), and take \(\alpha=T^{-2}\). Write

\(\displaystyle r=\log(8\tau mT^3).\)

For every exploration length satisfying

\(\displaystyle T_0\ge8\tau mr/c,\)

the explicit bound implies

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E R_\mu(T)=O\!\left( T_0+T\sqrt{\frac{\tau mr}{cT_0}}+\sqrt{\frac{\tau Tr}{c}}+\tau(1+\log(Th/l)) \right).\)

Here the hidden constant depends only on \(l,h,s,\delta\). In particular, this is the previously stated

\(\displaystyle \widetilde O\!\left(T_0+T\sqrt{\tau m/T_0}+\sqrt{\tau T}+\tau\right).\)

A concrete sufficient horizon condition is

\(\displaystyle T\ge64\tau mr/c.\)

Under this condition choose

\(\displaystyle T_0=\left\lceil T^{2/3}(\tau mr/c)^{1/3}\right\rceil.\)

Then \(8\tau mr/c\le T_0\le T/2\). The exploration and estimation terms have order \(T^{2/3}(\tau mr/c)^{1/3}\).

The reward-noise term is smaller: its ratio to that scale, apart from fixed constants, is

\(\displaystyle \frac{\sqrt{\tau Tr/c}}{T^{2/3}(\tau mr/c)^{1/3}} =\left(\frac{\tau r}{cTm^2}\right)^{1/6},\)

which is bounded under the stated horizon condition. The logarithmic optimization term is also absorbed because the leading scale is at least \(\tau mr/c\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E R_\mu(T)=O_{l,h,H,\sigma,\delta,c,C}\!\left(T^{2/3}(\tau mr)^{1/3}\right) =\widetilde O(T^{2/3}(\tau m)^{1/3}).\)

These are sufficient horizon conditions, not claimed to be necessary.

For shorter horizons, the trivial bound supplies the same order up to logarithms: if \(T<64\tau mr/c\), then \(T\le(64r/c)^{1/3}T^{2/3}(\tau m)^{1/3}\). Thus the balanced rate can be stated uniformly, although it becomes informative as a sublinear guarantee only for sufficiently long horizons.

A basic obstruction remains: if \(\tau>T\), some supported type necessarily receives no task, so realized welfare is zero and expected regret is linear. This does not contradict the rate, since then \(\tau m>T\).

7. An unbalanced instance with linear regret

Here is a counterexample to replacing the general-mass dependence by \(\tau m\) alone.

Take \(\tau=2\), \(m=1\), and

\(\displaystyle l=1/4,\quad h=4,\quad H=2,\quad \delta=1,\quad v_{1,1}=v_{2,1}=1.\)

Type \(2\) always returns reward \(1\). Type \(1\) independently returns \(0\) or \(2\), each with probability \(1/2\). These rewards satisfy all assumptions with \(\sigma=1\).

Let

\(\displaystyle \mu_1=\epsilon=2^{-\lceil3\log_2T\rceil},\qquad \mu_2=1-\epsilon.\)

The optimum is exactly \(1\): proportional allocation achieves it, and weighted arithmetic–geometric mean bounds every feasible welfare by \(1\).

For any \(T_0<T\), let \(E\) be the event that exploration never selects type \(1\). Then

\(\displaystyle \Pr(E^c)\le T_0\epsilon\le T\epsilon.\)

On \(E\), the fixed estimates are \(w_1=1/4\), \(w_2=1\), and \(B=8\). After type \(1\) receives its first exploitation task, its subsequent bid at round \(k\) is at most \(\epsilon(k-1)\).

Type \(2\)’s bid is always at least \(1-\epsilon\): if its allocation count is positive, its bid is

\(\displaystyle \min\{8,(1-\epsilon)(k-1)/n_2\}\ge1-\epsilon,\)

and if its count is zero its bid is \(8\). Since \(\epsilon T<1-\epsilon\), type \(1\) receives at most one exploitation task.

Consequently, on \(E\), welfare is zero with probability at least \(1/2\), and otherwise is at most

\(\displaystyle A_T=(2/\epsilon)^\epsilon\bigl(T/(1-\epsilon)\bigr)^{1-\epsilon}.\)

On \(E^c\), welfare is at most total reward, hence at most \(2T\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E G(W(T))\le\tfrac12A_T+2T^2\epsilon.\)

Now

\(\displaystyle \frac{A_T}{T}=\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon T}\right)^\epsilon(1-\epsilon)^{-(1-\epsilon)}\longrightarrow1,\qquad T\epsilon\longrightarrow0.\)

It follows that

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E R_\mu(T)\ge(1/2-o(1))T,\)

uniformly over all choices of \(T_0\). The rational masses have bit length \(O(\log T)\). Thus polynomial input length alone does not ensure a useful statistical horizon for arbitrary masses.

8. Running time and numerical representation

No convex-program solver is required by the policy. Store cumulative estimated utilities instead of recursively represented averages. This gives \(O(T\tau+\tau m)\) arithmetic operations and \(O(\tau m+\tau)\) storage locations.

For bit complexity, an observation representation must also be specified. If rewards are supplied as dyadic rationals with at most \(p\) bits and the known rational parameters have bit length \(L\), exact states and comparisons can be represented with

\(\displaystyle b=O(m(L+p+\log T))\)

bits. Each row contains at most \(m\) fixed empirical estimates; their denominators and subsequent integer allocation counts give this bound.

Using exact rational arithmetic and exact sampling from the rational mass distribution gives the conservative expected bit-operation bound

\(\displaystyle \widetilde O\!\left((T\tau+\tau m)b^2+T(\tau L)^2\right).\)

The second term allows sampling through a common-denominator integer distribution and rejection of excess random integers. Exact comparisons implement the specified tie rule.

For arbitrary real observations, the arithmetic-operation count remains valid; a bit-operation bound cannot depend on \(L\) alone without specifying how those observations are represented.
