The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational role masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), direct-choice probabilities \(q_{r\ell}\), role-to-role adoption probabilities \(\rho_{rs}\), a target \(j\), a budget \(\kappa\), and a threshold \(z\), decide whether \(\max F(x)\ge z\), where \(0\le x_r\le\mu_r\), \(\sum_r x_r\le\kappa\), \(p_{rj}=q_{rj}+\sum_s\rho_{rs}\left[x_s/\mu_s+(1-x_s/\mu_s)p_{sj}\right]\), and \(F(x)=\sum_r[x_r+(\mu_r-x_r)p_{rj}]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) roles with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_r\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(C\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) choices and \(j\in C\) the target.
For each role, binary rational probabilities \(q_{r\ell},\rho_{rs}\in[0,1]\) satisfy \(\sum_{\ell\in C}q_{r\ell}+\sum_s\rho_{rs}=1\). Every role has a directed path of positive adoption probabilities to a role with \(\sum_\ell q_{r\ell}>0\). Adoption selects a uniformly sampled individual of the destination role; \(\rho_{rr}\) may be positive, since another individual can have the same role.
Choose real ambassador masses \(0\le x_r\le\mu_r\), with \(\sum_r x_r\le\kappa\), where the binary rational budget satisfies \(0\le\kappa\le1\). Ambassadors choose and recommend \(j\) with certainty; remaining individuals retain their original behavior. Recruitment costs one per unit mass, which also measures choice share.
Let \(p_{rj}(x)\) be the unique nonambassador choice probabilities satisfying \(p_{rj}=q_{rj}+\sum_s\rho_{rs}[x_s/\mu_s+(1-x_s/\mu_s)p_{sj}]\), and set \(F(x)=\sum_r[x_r+(\mu_r-x_r)p_{rj}(x)]\). Given a binary rational threshold \(z\in[0,1]\), the decision problem asks whether \(\max_x F(x)\ge z\).

Is this continuous ambassador-mass decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length, or is it NP-hard?

Discrete ambassador selection under a cardinality budget is NP-hard, even with two choices and equal endowments, and admits a \(1-1/e\) greedy approximation. Finite splitting into separately recruitable subagents also has that approximation guarantee. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.3138v2)

Result

The continuous ambassador-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\), even with two choices, equal role masses, and no self-adoption.

The key is an exact upper bound: attaining it forces the fully recruited roles to form a vertex cover. Fractional recruitment therefore cannot turn a negative instance into a positive one.

1. Reduction from Vertex Cover

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Vertex Cover problem: given a simple undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether some set \(S\subseteq V\), with \(|S|\le k\), meets every edge.

We may restrict to graphs without isolated vertices, with \(n=|V|\ge2\) and \(0\le k\le n\). Indeed, deleting isolated vertices preserves the answer, and instances outside these restrictions are immediately decidable.

Construct a society with one role per vertex and choices \(C=\{j,b\}\). Set

\(\displaystyle \mu_v=\frac1n,\qquad q_{vj}=0,\qquad q_{vb}=\frac12.\)

Writing \(d_v\ge1\) for the degree of vertex \(v\), set

\(\displaystyle \rho_{vu}= \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2d_v},&\{v,u\}\in E,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \)

Thus every adoption row sums to \(1/2\), so the required normalization holds. Every role has positive direct-choice probability; moreover, each has a positive adoption edge to another such role. There are no self-adoption edges.

Set the budget and threshold to

\(\displaystyle \kappa=\frac{k}{n},\qquad z=\frac{n+k}{2n}.\)

This is the graph construction underlying the discrete hardness proof in [Chen, proof of Proposition 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.3138v2). The following argument establishes its validity for continuous recruitment.

2. Choice probabilities and attainment

For any feasible recruitment vector, define the recruited fraction and total target-choice probability of role \(v\) by

\(\displaystyle a_v=nx_v,\qquad y_v=a_v+(1-a_v)p_v,\)

where \(p_v=p_{vj}(x)\). Feasibility gives

\(\displaystyle 0\le a_v\le1,\qquad \sum_v a_v\le k.\)

Because the direct target-choice probabilities are zero, the defining equations become

\(\displaystyle p_v=\sum_u\rho_{vu}\bigl[a_u+(1-a_u)p_u\bigr] =\sum_u\rho_{vu}y_u.\)

For fixed \(a\), the map on the right sends \([0,1]^n\) into \([0,1/2]^n\). Its contraction constant in the maximum norm is at most

\(\displaystyle \max_v\sum_u\rho_{vu}(1-a_u)\le\frac12.\)

Consequently, there is a unique fixed point, and it satisfies

\(\displaystyle 0\le p_v\le\frac12,\qquad 0\le y_v\le1.\)

The objective is

\(\displaystyle F(x)=\frac1n\sum_v y_v.\)

For completeness, this objective attains its maximum. Let \(R=(\rho_{vu})\) and \(D(a)=\operatorname{diag}(1-a_v)\). The fixed point satisfies

\(\displaystyle p=(I-RD(a))^{-1}Ra.\)

The inverse exists for every \(a\in[0,1]^n\), since \(\|RD(a)\|_\infty\le1/2\). Hence \(p\), and therefore \(F\), depends continuously on \(a\). The feasible set is nonempty and compact, so a maximum exists.

3. A tight upper bound and its equality condition

The probability bound above yields

\(\displaystyle y_v=a_v+(1-a_v)p_v\le\frac{1+a_v}{2}.\)

Summing gives

\(\displaystyle F(x)\le\frac{n+\sum_v a_v}{2n} \le\frac{n+k}{2n}=z.\)

We now characterize equality. The exact difference is

\(\displaystyle z-F(x) =\frac{k-\sum_v a_v}{2n} +\frac1n\sum_v(1-a_v)\left(\frac12-p_v\right). \)

Every term on the right is nonnegative. Thus, if \(F(x)=z\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_v a_v=k,\)

and every role with \(a_v<1\) satisfies \(p_v=1/2\).

For such a role \(v\), the adoption row sum gives

\(\displaystyle 0=\frac12-p_v =\sum_u\rho_{vu}(1-y_u). \)

Again, every summand is nonnegative. Since \(\rho_{vu}>0\) for every neighbor \(u\), each neighbor must satisfy \(y_u=1\). But

\(\displaystyle 1-y_u=(1-a_u)(1-p_u),\)

and \(1-p_u\ge1/2>0\). Therefore \(y_u=1\) forces \(a_u=1\).

It follows that every vertex which is not fully recruited has only fully recruited neighbors. Equivalently,

\(\displaystyle S=\{v\in V:a_v=1\}\)

is a vertex cover. Its size satisfies

\(\displaystyle |S|\le\sum_v a_v=k.\)

This is the step that rules out fractional recruitment as a way around Vertex Cover.

4. Equivalence of the instances

If the graph has a vertex cover of size at most \(k\): enlarge it, if necessary, to a vertex cover \(S\) of size exactly \(k\). Recruit precisely these roles fully:

\(\displaystyle a_v= \begin{cases} 1,&v\in S,\\ 0,&v\notin S. \end{cases} \)

This costs \(k/n=\kappa\). Every vertex outside \(S\) has all its neighbors in \(S\), where \(y_u=1\). Hence each such vertex satisfies

\(\displaystyle p_v=\sum_u\rho_{vu}=\frac12.\)

Therefore

\(\displaystyle F(x)=\frac{k+(n-k)/2}{n}=z.\)

If the continuous instance satisfies \(\max_x F(x)\ge z\): the universal upper bound and maximum attainment imply that some feasible vector satisfies \(F(x)=z\). Section 3 then produces a vertex cover of size at most \(k\).

Thus,

\(\displaystyle G\text{ has a vertex cover of size at most }k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \max_xF(x)\ge z. \)

5. Encoding and complexity

All constructed fractions have binary numerators and denominators of length \(O(\log n)\). There are \(n\) roles and two choices; even a dense representation of all adoption probabilities has polynomial length. The construction is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

The hardness also holds if “binary rational” specifically means a finite binary expansion. Here is a modification ensuring that interpretation:

  • Choose a power of two \(N\) with \(2n\le N<4n\). Pad the graph to \(N\) vertices using disjoint edges and, if \(N-n\) is odd, one disjoint triangle. This is possible because \(N-n\ge2\). Increase \(k\) by the minimum vertex-cover size of these added components. Vertex Cover answers are preserved because covers of disjoint components decompose.
  • Give every role mass \(1/N\). For a vertex of degree \(d\), designate one neighbor; give every other neighbor adoption probability \(1/(4N)\), and the designated neighbor probability \(1/2-(d-1)/(4N)\).

These adoption probabilities are positive dyadic rationals and still sum to \(1/2\). The budget and threshold are dyadic as well. The proof above uses only the adoption row sums and their positive graph support, so it applies unchanged.

Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) for the stated continuous problem would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
