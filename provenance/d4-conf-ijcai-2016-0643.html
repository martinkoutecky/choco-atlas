The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), a finite set \(T\) of complete ranking types, rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta^{|T|}\), and decomposable scores \(f_{m,k}(i_1,\ldots,i_k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma^{(j)}_{m,k}(i_j)\) satisfying the paper's adjacent-layer condition, compute a committee \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=k\) such that \(F_\mu(S)\ge (1-1/e)\max_{|S'|=k}F_\mu(S')\), where \(F_\mu(S)=\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_r(S))\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(|C|=m\geq1\) and an integer \(1\leq k\leq m\), a society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete ranking types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_r\), where \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The input also contains binary-encoded nonnegative integer scoring tables \(\gamma^{(j)}_{m,k}:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\), nonincreasing in position, satisfying \(\gamma^{(j)}_{m,k}(p)\geq\gamma^{(j+1)}_{m,k}(p)\) for every \(1\leq j<k\) and \(1\leq p\leq m\). Let \(L\) denote the total input bit length.
For a committee \(S\subseteq C\) of size \(k\), let \(\operatorname{pos}_r(S)=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)\) list its members' positions in ranking \(r\) in increasing order, with position \(1\) best. Define \(f_{m,k}(i_1,\ldots,i_k)=\sum_{j=1}^k\gamma^{(j)}_{m,k}(i_j)\) and \(F_\mu(S)=\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_r(S))\). Any qualifying committee may be returned; ties impose no additional requirement.

Is there an algorithm, polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), that always returns a committee \(S\) with \(|S|=k\) and \(F_\mu(S)\geq(1-1/e)\max_{S'\subseteq C,\,|S'|=k}F_\mu(S')\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

Consequently, a uniform approximation algorithm for explicitly listed voters transfers to binary-rational masses with an arbitrarily small loss. All unconditional claims below are proved independently. The transfer statements explicitly identify the discrete algorithm they assume.

1. Definitions and elementary cases

Write $\gamma_j$ for $\gamma^{(j)}_{m,k}$, and let

$$ \operatorname{OPT} = \max_{\substack{S\subseteq C\\ |S|=k}}F_\mu(S). $$

For any $S\subseteq C$, let $i_1<\cdots<i_{|S|}$ be its selected positions in ranking $r$. Define

$$ G_r(S) = \sum_{j=1}^{\min(k,|S|)}\gamma_j(i_j), \qquad G(S) = \sum_{r\in T}\mu_rG_r(S). $$

Thus $G(S)=F_\mu(S)$ when $|S|=k$, and $G(\varnothing)=0$.

Each $G_r$ is monotone. When a candidate is added, every retained order statistic weakly decreases in position, so its contribution weakly increases. If the number of retained order statistics increases, the new contribution is nonnegative. Positive weighted sums preserve monotonicity, so $G$ is monotone.

The cases $k=1$ and $k=m$ are exactly solvable: evaluate every singleton in the first case, and return $C$ in the second. The arguments below address the general input class.

2. An unconditional bound relating individual scores to the optimum

Put

$$ A=\gamma_1(1). $$

Layer dominance and position monotonicity imply

$$ 0\leq\gamma_j(p)\leq A $$

for every $j$ and $p$. If $A=0$, every score is zero and any size-$k$ committee qualifies. Hence assume $A>0$.

For each candidate $c$, define its first-place mass by

$$ q_c = \sum_{\substack{r\in T\\ \operatorname{pos}_r(c)=1}}\mu_r. $$

Every ranking has exactly one first-place candidate, so

$$ \sum_{c\in C}q_c=1. $$

Let $S_0$ contain the $k$ candidates with largest $q_c$. Their total first-place mass is at least $k/m$. Indeed, the average of the $k$ largest numbers in a list is at least the average of the entire list.

Whenever a ranking's first-place candidate belongs to $S_0$, its committee score is at least $A$. All other contributions are nonnegative. Consequently,

$$ \operatorname{OPT} \geq F_\mu(S_0) \geq \frac{k}{m}A. $$

On the other hand, every size-$k$ committee has individual ranking score at most $kA$. Thus, for every ranking $r$ and every size-$k$ committee $S$,

$$ 0 \leq f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_r(S)) \leq kA \leq m\operatorname{OPT}. $$

This bound is independent of the smallest mass and of the numerical magnitude of the scoring entries.

3. Polynomial-size mass compression

Lemma

Let $N>\tau$ be an integer. Define

$$ n_r=\lfloor N\mu_r\rfloor, \qquad N'=\sum_{r\in T}n_r, $$

and define the unnormalized rounded objective

$$ \widetilde F(S) = \frac1N \sum_{r\in T}n_r f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_r(S)). $$

Then $N'>0$, $N'\leq N$, and every size-$k$ committee satisfies

$$ 0 \leq F_\mu(S)-\widetilde F(S) \leq \frac{m\tau}{N}\operatorname{OPT}. $$

In particular,

$$ \max_{|S|=k}\widetilde F(S) \geq \left(1-\frac{m\tau}{N}\right)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

Proof

Because $\sum_rN\mu_r=N$ and $\lfloor x\rfloor>x-1$,

$$ N' > N-\tau > 0. $$

Also, $N'\leq N$.

For every ranking,

$$ 0\leq \mu_r-\frac{n_r}{N}<\frac1N. $$

The scoring entries are nonnegative, so

$$ 0 \leq F_\mu(S)-\widetilde F(S) = \sum_{r\in T} \left(\mu_r-\frac{n_r}{N}\right) f_{m,k}(\operatorname{pos}_r(S)). $$

Applying the bound from Section 2 gives

$$ F_\mu(S)-\widetilde F(S) \leq \frac{\tau}{N}m\operatorname{OPT}. $$

Apply this inequality to an optimal committee to obtain the final assertion. $\square$

The rounded electorate consists of $n_r$ explicitly listed copies of ranking $r$, omitting types with $n_r=0$. Its ordinary total-score objective is $N\widetilde F$, so multiplying by $N$ does not change approximation ratios.

For rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, choose

$$ N=\left\lceil\frac{m\tau}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil. $$

The rounded electorate then has at most $N$ voters, and the error is at most $\varepsilon\operatorname{OPT}$ for every committee.

Each $n_r$ can be computed exactly from the binary-rational input. Explicitly writing the rounded electorate takes time polynomial in $m,\tau,L,$ and $1/\varepsilon$. The scoring tables are unchanged.

4. What transfers from a discrete approximation algorithm

The precise algorithmic premise

Assume an algorithm $\mathcal A$ has the following properties:

  • Its input contains explicitly listed voters, the scoring tables, and $k$.
  • It runs in time polynomial in that input's bit length.
  • It always returns a size-$k$ committee whose score is at least $\rho$ times the optimum, where $0<\rho\leq1$.

This is a uniform premise concerning algorithms with scoring tables supplied as input. A theorem stated separately for each fixed rule family does not automatically establish this uniform premise.

The cited [IJCAI paper, Theorem 15](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/043.pdf) asserts the discrete $1-1/e$ guarantee but does not supply its algorithm or proof. The following statements are implications from the precise premise above.

Transfer with an arbitrarily small loss

Run $\mathcal A$ on the rounded electorate from Section 3, and let $S$ be its output. Since its total-score objective is $N\widetilde F$,

$$ \widetilde F(S) \geq \rho\max_{|W|=k}\widetilde F(W). $$

Rounding only decreases scores. Therefore,

$$ F_\mu(S) \geq \widetilde F(S) \geq \rho\left(1-\frac{m\tau}{N}\right)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

With $N=\lceil m\tau/\varepsilon\rceil$, this implies

$$ F_\mu(S) \geq \rho(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT} \geq (\rho-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

Consequently, if the supplied discrete assertion admits the uniform formulation above, then for every rational $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ there is an algorithm, polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L,$ and $1/\varepsilon$, returning a committee satisfying

$$ F_\mu(S) \geq \left(1-\frac1e-\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

This does not establish the exact requested factor.

An inverse-polynomial excess would give the exact factor

Put

$$ \alpha=1-\frac1e. $$

Suppose the discrete algorithm instead guarantees

$$ \rho\geq\alpha+\eta, $$

where $\eta>0$ is an explicitly available rational number and $1/\eta$ is polynomially bounded in the input parameters. Choose

$$ N=\left\lceil\frac{2m\tau}{\eta}\right\rceil. $$

Then

$$ F_\mu(S) \geq \rho\left(1-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\operatorname{OPT} \geq \left(\rho-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\operatorname{OPT} \geq \left(\alpha+\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

Here the second inequality uses $\rho\leq1$. This gives the exact requested guarantee in polynomial time.

One particularly useful possible discrete guarantee is

$$ \beta_k = 1-\left(1-\frac1k\right)^k. $$

It satisfies

$$ \beta_k-\alpha>\frac{1}{12k}. $$

To verify this, first take $k\geq2$. The inequality

$$ \log(1-x)\leq-x-\frac{x^2}{2} $$

for $0<x<1$ follows by integrating $1/(1-t)\geq1+t$. Hence

$$ \left(1-\frac1k\right)^k \leq e^{-1}e^{-1/(2k)}. $$

For $0\leq x\leq1$,

$$ 1-e^{-x} = \int_0^x e^{-t}\,dt \geq \int_0^x(1-t)\,dt \geq \frac{x}{2}. $$

It follows that

$$ \beta_k-\alpha \geq e^{-1}\left(1-e^{-1/(2k)}\right) \geq \frac{1}{4ek} > \frac{1}{12k}. $$

The last inequality uses $e<3$, which follows from the series for $e$ and $n!\geq2^{n-1}$, with strict inequality for some terms. For $k=1$, the assertion follows from $\beta_1-\alpha=1/e>1/3$.

Thus a uniform discrete $\beta_k$-approximation would settle the weighted target: taking

$$ N=24m\tau k $$

suffices. Such a discrete guarantee has not been established here for the full scoring-table class.

5. An exact polynomial-time submodularity criterion

The next result is unconditional.

Because every ranking is a relabeling of the canonical order $1,\ldots,m$, it suffices to examine the function

$$ g(X) = \sum_{j=1}^{\min(k,|X|)}\gamma_j(p_j), $$

where $p_1<\cdots<p_{|X|}$ are the elements of $X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$.

Define auxiliary zero tables and layer differences by

$$ \gamma_{k+1}(p)=\gamma_{k+2}(p)=0, $$

$$ \delta_j(p)=\gamma_j(p)-\gamma_{j+1}(p) \quad (1\leq j\leq k), \qquad \delta_{k+1}(p)=0. $$

All $\delta_j(p)$ are nonnegative.

Pairwise marginal calculation

A set function is submodular exactly when, for every $X$ and distinct $a,b\notin X$,

$$ g(X\cup\{a\})+g(X\cup\{b\}) - g(X)-g(X\cup\{a,b\}) \geq0. $$

Indeed, this inequality says that adding one element cannot increase another element's marginal. Applying it successively along a chain proves the general diminishing-returns inequality.

Assume $a<b$, and put

$$ \ell=1+|X\cap\{1,\ldots,b-1\}|. $$

If $\ell>k$, adding $b$ changes neither $g(X)$ nor $g(X\cup\{a\})$, so the displayed expression is zero.

Suppose $\ell\leq k$. Put $s=\min(k,|X|)$, and denote the retained selected positions after $b$ by

$$ b<p_\ell<\cdots<p_s. $$

The list is empty when $s=\ell-1$.

Adding $b$ to $X$ gives it layer $\ell$ and shifts each retained position $p_i$ from layer $i$ to layer $i+1$. Thus its marginal is

$$ \gamma_\ell(b) + \sum_{i=\ell}^{s} \bigl(\gamma_{i+1}(p_i)-\gamma_i(p_i)\bigr). $$

After $a$ has also been added, $b$ receives layer $\ell+1$, and the same positions have their layer indices increased by one. The auxiliary zero tables account for positions dropped by truncation at $k$. Subtracting the two marginals gives

$$ D(X;a,b) = \delta_\ell(b) + \sum_{i=\ell}^{s} \bigl(\delta_{i+1}(p_i)-\delta_i(p_i)\bigr). $$

This calculation depends on the selected positions before $b$ only through their number.

For such a configuration to exist, $b-1$ positions must accommodate the $\ell-1$ selected positions before $b$ and the unselected position $a$. Equivalently,

$$ b\geq\ell+1. $$

Conversely, whenever this inequality holds, any increasing tail of length at most $k-\ell+1$ is realizable: choose $a=1$ and choose $\ell-1$ positions from $\{2,\ldots,b-1\}$.

Sets larger than $k$ introduce no additional expressions, because positions below the first $k$ contribute zero throughout the calculation.

Dynamic program

For $q\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$, define

$$ V_{k+1}(q)=0. $$

For $i=k,k-1,\ldots,1$, define

$$ V_i(q) = \min\left( 0,\; \min_{p>q} \left[ \delta_{i+1}(p)-\delta_i(p)+V_{i+1}(p) \right] \right). $$

An empty inner minimum is ignored.

The value $V_i(q)$ is the minimum sum obtainable from an increasing tail after $q$, beginning at layer $i$. Choosing zero means taking an empty tail. Otherwise, choose its first position $p$, pay the corresponding summand, and optimize the remaining tail using $V_{i+1}(p)$. This interpretation follows by backward induction on $i$.

Therefore, for fixed $\ell,b$, the minimum possible pairwise expression is

$$ \delta_\ell(b)+V_\ell(b). $$

We obtain the following exact criterion:

$$ \boxed{ g\text{ is submodular} \iff \delta_\ell(b)+V_\ell(b)\geq0 \text{ for every } 1\leq\ell\leq k,\quad \ell+1\leq b\leq m. } $$

Necessity follows because every minimizing tail is realizable. Sufficiency follows because every pairwise marginal expression is either zero or one of the expressions bounded by the criterion.

The direct implementation takes $O(km^2)$ integer operations. It can be reduced to $O(km)$ by computing suffix minima of

$$ \delta_{i+1}(p)-\delta_i(p)+V_{i+1}(p) $$

for each row $i$. Every tail contains at most $k$ terms of absolute value at most $A$, so all intermediate integers have polynomial bit length.

This is consequently a polynomial-time recognition algorithm.

6. The requested guarantee on every recognized input

If the criterion in Section 5 holds, every $G_r$ is submodular, because it is a relabeling of $g$. Positive weighted sums preserve submodularity. Thus $G$ is normalized, monotone, and submodular.

Run greedy for $k$ additions, choosing at each step a candidate of maximum marginal value. Let $S_t$ be the committee after $t$ additions, and let $O$ be an optimal size-$k$ committee.

Monotonicity and diminishing returns give

$$ G(O)-G(S_t) \leq G(O\cup S_t)-G(S_t) \leq \sum_{c\in O\setminus S_t} \bigl(G(S_t\cup\{c\})-G(S_t)\bigr). $$

There are at most $k$ summands. The greedy marginal is therefore at least

$$ \frac{G(O)-G(S_t)}{k}. $$

Starting from $G(\varnothing)=0$ and iterating gives

$$ F_\mu(S_k) = G(S_k) \geq \left(1-\left(1-\frac1k\right)^k\right)G(O) \geq \left(1-\frac1e\right)\operatorname{OPT}. $$

All evaluations and comparisons can be exact. Write $\mu_r=a_r/b_r$, and put

$$ D=\prod_{r\in T}b_r, \qquad w_r=\frac{a_rD}{b_r}. $$

Then $w_r$ are positive integers, $\sum_rw_r=D$, and

$$ DG(S)=\sum_{r\in T}w_rG_r(S). $$

The bit length of $D$ is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths, up to an additive constant. Moreover,

$$ 0\leq DG(S)\leq DkA. $$

Thus these integer calculations have polynomial bit length. Greedy uses at most $km$ score evaluations, each computable directly from the $\tau$ rankings. Its running time is polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L$.

The recognized subclass strictly enlarges the earlier sufficient subclass

The position-drop condition from NOTES is

$$ \gamma_j(p)-\gamma_j(p+1) \geq \gamma_{j+1}(p)-\gamma_{j+1}(p+1) $$

for $j<k$ and $p<m$.

This condition says that each $\delta_j$ with $j<k$ is nonincreasing in position. The same is true of $\delta_k=\gamma_k$ by the original hypotheses.

For a nonempty tail, the expression from Section 5 telescopes as

$$ D(X;a,b) = \delta_\ell(b)-\delta_\ell(p_\ell) + \sum_{i=\ell+1}^{s} \bigl(\delta_i(p_{i-1})-\delta_i(p_i)\bigr) + \delta_{s+1}(p_s). $$

Every term is nonnegative. An empty tail gives $D(X;a,b)=\delta_\ell(b)\geq0$. Thus the earlier condition implies the exact criterion.

The converse fails. For every $m\geq3$, take $k=2$ and

$$ \gamma_1(p)=3 \quad\text{for all }p, $$

$$ \gamma_2(p)= \begin{cases} 2,&p<m,\\ 1,&p=m. \end{cases} $$

These tables satisfy all original hypotheses, but violate the position-drop condition at $p=m-1$.

Let $z$ be the last-ranked candidate, and call the other candidates good. The extended score is:

  • $0$ for the empty set;
  • $3$ for every singleton;
  • $4$ for a pair containing $z$;
  • $5$ for every other set of size at least two.

Adding $z$ to a set containing respectively zero, one, or at least two good candidates has marginal $3,1,$ or $0$.

For an added good candidate, the marginal depends on the existing number $h$ of good candidates and whether $z$ is present:

$$ \begin{array}{c|cc} & z\text{ absent} & z\text{ present}\\ \hline h=0 & 3 & 1\\ h=1 & 2 & 1\\ h\geq2 & 0 & 0 \end{array} $$

As a set grows, $h$ can only increase and $z$ can only change from absent to present. The displayed marginals never increase. Hence this extension is submodular, proving strict enlargement.

The criterion does not cover all admissible inputs

For example, take $m=4$, $k=3$, and

$$ \gamma_1=(1,1,1,1), \qquad \gamma_2=(1,1,0,0), \qquad \gamma_3=(0,0,0,0). $$

All original hypotheses hold. In the canonical ranking, let $X=\{3\}$ and add positions $1$ and $2$. Then

$$ g(\{3\})=g(\{1,3\})=g(\{2,3\})=1, \qquad g(\{1,2,3\})=2. $$

The pairwise submodularity expression is therefore $-1$. Thus recognition followed by greedy does not solve the full class.

7. Remaining target

The mass-compression lemma isolates a concrete route to a complete solution: a uniform discrete guarantee of $\beta_k$, or any inverse-polynomial excess over $1-1/e$, would yield the exact weighted guarantee with only polynomially many rounded voters.

A uniform discrete guarantee of exactly $1-1/e$ yields arbitrarily close guarantees by this argument, but the proof loses a positive amount and does not establish the exact factor.

Independently, the submodularity criterion settles the requested guarantee for a larger, polynomially recognizable subclass. Inputs outside that subclass still require another approximation argument or an approximation-hardness reduction.
