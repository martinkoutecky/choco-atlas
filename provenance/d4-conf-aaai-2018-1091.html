The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many buyer and seller types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), capacity at most \(M\), and explicit rational DMR valuation tables over integral quantities \(q\in\{0,\ldots,M\}\), compute a price \(p\) and allocation masses \(x_{t,q}\). Require \(\sum_q x_{t,q}=\mu_t\), equal total quantities bought and sold, and that every assigned quantity maximizes its type’s gain at \(p\); among such allocations, maximize total GFT.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) buyer and seller types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; masses need not sum to one. There is one good, so \(m=1\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each buyer has an integral capacity \(c_t\le M\), each seller an integral endowment \(c_t\le M\), and \(M\ge1\). Each type has an explicit rational valuation table on \(\{0,\ldots,c_t\}\), normalized by \(v_t(0)=0\), with weakly decreasing marginal values; values and prices may have either sign. Buyers have no monetary budget constraint.
For integral \(q\in\{0,\ldots,c_t\}\), define buyer gain \(g_t(q,p)=v_t(q)-pq\) and seller gain \(g_t(q,p)=pq+v_t(c_t-q)-v_t(c_t)\).
An admissible output consists of a rational price \(p\) and nonnegative rational allocation masses \(x_{t,q}\), satisfying \(\sum_qx_{t,q}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in B,q}qx_{t,q}=\sum_{t\in S,q}qx_{t,q}\), and \(x_{t,q}>0\Rightarrow q\in\arg\max_r g_t(r,p)\). Ties may be resolved centrally, including splitting a type's mass among maximizing quantities.
Total GFT is \(G(x)=\sum_{t\in B,q}x_{t,q}v_t(q)+\sum_{t\in S,q}x_{t,q}[v_t(c_t-q)-v_t(c_t)]\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) maximize this expression subject to mass conservation and material balance; let \(k\) be the largest traded quantity among its maximizers.
Can an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,M,L\) always produce an admissible output, and, whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\), guarantee expected GFT at least \((1-O(M\sqrt{\ln k/k}))\mathrm{OPT}\) as \(k\to\infty\), with a universal implied constant, or is this approximation guarantee unattainable in polynomial time?
For finite traders, Lottery-MUDA and Vickrey-MUDA attain this expected approximation guarantee while being truthful, individually rational, and budget balanced; their allocations need not give every trader a maximizing quantity at their posted price.   • Each seller's initial endowment is explicit; its valuation concerns units retained, rather than units sold.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact optimal GFT is achievable in polynomial time

Under the stated mass-splitting and centralized tie-breaking rules, a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm produces an admissible output with GFT exactly equal to \(\mathrm{OPT}\). Thus the requested approximation guarantee holds with no loss.

The proof below constructs the price and allocation directly.

1. Maximizing quantities at a given price

For each buyer type \(t\), define its marginal values by

\(b_{t,j}=v_t(j)-v_t(j-1),\qquad 1\le j\le c_t.\)

By hypothesis, these are weakly decreasing. Moreover,

\(g_t(q,p)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}(b_{t,j}-p).\)

Define

\(\ell_t(p)=|\{j:b_{t,j}>p\}|,\qquad u_t(p)=|\{j:b_{t,j}\ge p\}|.\)

The increments of buyer gain are first positive, then possibly zero, then negative. Consequently, its maximizing quantities are exactly

\(\arg\max_q g_t(q,p)=\{\ell_t(p),\ell_t(p)+1,\ldots,u_t(p)\}.\)

For each seller type \(t\), define the opportunity cost of its \(j\)-th unit sold by

\(s_{t,j}=v_t(c_t-j+1)-v_t(c_t-j),\qquad 1\le j\le c_t.\)

These costs are weakly increasing: they list the weakly decreasing valuation marginals in reverse order. Telescoping gives

\(g_t(q,p)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}(p-s_{t,j}).\)

For sellers, define

\(\ell_t(p)=|\{j:s_{t,j}<p\}|,\qquad u_t(p)=|\{j:s_{t,j}\le p\}|.\)

Again, gain increments are first positive, then possibly zero, then negative. Thus the seller’s maximizing quantities are exactly

\(\arg\max_q g_t(q,p)=\{\ell_t(p),\ell_t(p)+1,\ldots,u_t(p)\}.\)

These conclusions also cover zero capacities and signed valuations and prices.

2. Finding a price that permits material balance

Define the smallest and largest aggregate maximizing demands and supplies:

\(D_{\min}(p)=\sum_{t\in B}\mu_t\ell_t(p),\qquad D_{\max}(p)=\sum_{t\in B}\mu_tu_t(p),\)

\(S_{\min}(p)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\ell_t(p),\qquad S_{\max}(p)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_tu_t(p).\)

The smallest and largest excess demands obtainable from maximizing quantities are

\(E_-(p)=D_{\min}(p)-S_{\max}(p),\qquad E_+(p)=D_{\max}(p)-S_{\min}(p).\)

We will find a rational price satisfying

\(E_-(p)\le 0\le E_+(p).\)

Let \(A\) be the set of all buyer marginal values and seller opportunity costs. If \(A\) is empty, every capacity is zero; output \(p=0\) and \(x_{t,0}=\mu_t\). This is admissible and optimal.

Otherwise, sort its distinct elements as

\(a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_h.\)

Choose the smallest index \(i\) for which \(E_-(a_i)\le 0\), and set \(p=a_i\).

Such an index exists. At the largest breakpoint, no buyer marginal strictly exceeds the price, and every seller opportunity cost is at most the price. Therefore

\(E_-(a_h)=-\sum_{t\in S}\mu_tc_t\le 0.\)

The selected price also satisfies \(E_+(p)\ge 0\). If \(i=1\), every buyer marginal is at least \(a_1\), while no seller opportunity cost is strictly below \(a_1\). Hence

\(E_+(a_1)=\sum_{t\in B}\mu_tc_t\ge 0.\)

If \(i>1\), minimality gives \(E_-(a_{i-1})>0\). There are no marginal values or opportunity costs strictly between \(a_{i-1}\) and \(a_i\). It follows that

\(D_{\max}(a_i)=D_{\min}(a_{i-1}),\qquad S_{\min}(a_i)=S_{\max}(a_{i-1}).\)

Indeed, a buyer marginal is at least \(a_i\) exactly when it strictly exceeds \(a_{i-1}\); a seller opportunity cost is strictly below \(a_i\) exactly when it is at most \(a_{i-1}\). Thus

\(E_+(a_i)=E_-(a_{i-1})>0.\)

This proves the required pair of inequalities.

3. Constructing rational allocation masses

Fix the price from Section 2. Define two quantities for each type:

  • In configuration \(-\), buyers receive \(\ell_t(p)\) and sellers sell \(u_t(p)\).
  • In configuration \(+\), buyers receive \(u_t(p)\) and sellers sell \(\ell_t(p)\).

Write these quantities as \(q_t^-\) and \(q_t^+\), respectively. Every chosen quantity maximizes the corresponding type’s gain at \(p\). The two configurations have excess demands \(E_-(p)\) and \(E_+(p)\).

If \(E_+(p)=E_-(p)\), the inequalities already proved force both to be zero. Set \(\lambda=0\).

Otherwise, set

\(\lambda=\dfrac{-E_-(p)}{E_+(p)-E_-(p)}.\)

Because \(E_-(p)\le 0\le E_+(p)\), we have \(0\le\lambda\le 1\). Define

\(x_{t,q}=\mu_t\bigl((1-\lambda)\mathbf 1_{\{q=q_t^-\}}+\lambda\mathbf 1_{\{q=q_t^+\}}\bigr).\)

When the two quantities coincide, the two contributions are added.

All these masses are nonnegative rational numbers, and each type’s masses sum to \(\mu_t\). Their aggregate excess demand is

\((1-\lambda)E_-(p)+\lambda E_+(p)=0.\)

Thus material balance holds. Every quantity receiving positive mass is maximizing at the common price, so the output is admissible. Each type uses at most two quantities.

4. Every admissible output is welfare-optimal

This establishes optimality without invoking an equilibrium theorem.

For each type, let

\(H_t(p)=\max_{0\le q\le c_t}g_t(q,p).\)

Consider any nonnegative allocation \(y\) satisfying mass conservation and material balance, whether or not it is admissible. Summing the gain definitions gives

\(\sum_{t,q}y_{t,q}g_t(q,p)=G(y)-p\bigl(\sum_{t\in B,q}qy_{t,q}-\sum_{t\in S,q}qy_{t,q}\bigr)=G(y).\)

The price terms cancel by material balance. Since \(g_t(q,p)\le H_t(p)\), mass conservation implies

\(G(y)\le\sum_t\mu_tH_t(p).\)

For the constructed allocation \(x\), every quantity receiving positive mass attains \(H_t(p)\). Consequently,

\(G(x)=\sum_t\mu_tH_t(p).\)

Therefore \(G(y)\le G(x)\) for every feasible allocation \(y\), proving

\(G(x)=\mathrm{OPT}.\)

The feasible set is nonempty, since assigning every type quantity zero is feasible. It is also closed and bounded: each allocation coordinate lies between zero and its type’s mass. Hence the maximum defining \(\mathrm{OPT}\) exists. The same compactness argument ensures that the largest traded quantity \(k\) among optimal allocations exists.

5. Polynomial running time and encoding length

Let \(N=\sum_t c_t\), so \(N\le\tau M\). The algorithm:

  • Computes the \(N\) marginal values and opportunity costs.
  • Sorts their distinct values.
  • Scans these candidate prices, computing the four aggregate quantities at each.
  • Computes \(\lambda\) and outputs the allocation masses.

A direct implementation takes \(O(N\log N+N^2)\) rational arithmetic operations: evaluating a candidate price requires inspecting at most \(N\) marginal entries.

These operations have polynomial bit complexity. Every breakpoint is a difference of two input rational values, so its numerator and denominator have bit lengths bounded polynomially in \(L\). Every aggregate quantity is a sum of input masses multiplied by integers of magnitude at most \(M\). A common denominator can be taken to be the product of the input mass denominators, whose bit length is at most their total input encoding length. The resulting numerators have polynomial bit length in \(L\), \(\log M\), and \(\log\tau\).

The formula for \(\lambda\) uses one subtraction and one division of these aggregates. Each nonzero output mass is then a product involving \(\lambda\) and an input mass. Hence the price and all output masses have polynomial encoding length. There are at most \(2\tau\) nonzero allocation entries; even a dense output has at most \(\tau(M+1)\) entries.

Thus the algorithm runs in polynomial time in the requested parameters. It never expands a type into individual traders, and unnormalized masses cause no difficulty.

6. The requested approximation guarantee

The algorithm is deterministic and satisfies

\(\mathbb E[G(x)]=G(x)=\mathrm{OPT}.\)

In particular, whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\) and \(k>1\),

\(\mathbb E[G(x)]\ge\bigl(1-M\sqrt{\ln k/k}\bigr)\mathrm{OPT}.\)

This supplies a universal implied constant and proves a stronger guarantee than requested. Computing \(k\) is unnecessary.

The distinction from truthful finite-trader mechanisms matters: the task does not require incentive compatibility. The cited [MUDA paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/11450/11309) itself distinguishes welfare-maximizing Walrasian pricing from truthful mechanisms. The argument above directly establishes exact optimization for the specified mass-allocation problem.
