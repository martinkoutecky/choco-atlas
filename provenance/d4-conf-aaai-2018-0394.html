The continuous mirror question, as graded

For the paper's fixed-variance Gaussian location-family RUM, given \(m\) alternatives, \(k\), rational \(C,\epsilon>0\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) of mass over \(\tau\) complete rankings, find rational mixture weights \(\alpha\) and component means \(\theta^{(r)}\) with \(\alpha\) in the simplex, \(\sum_i\theta_i^{(r)}=0\), and \(\theta_i^{(r)}\in[-C,C]\), whose objective is within \(\epsilon\) of the global minimum of \(G_\mu(\alpha,\theta)=\sum_{i<j}\left(\sum_{V:a_i\succ_V a_j}\mu_V-\sum_{r=1}^k\alpha_r\Phi((\theta_i^{(r)}-\theta_j^{(r)})/\sqrt{2})\right)^2\). What is the complexity of this task in \(m,k,\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), in the correctly specified, pairwise-identifying regime?

Let \(m\ge2\), \(k\ge1\), and let \(C,\epsilon>0\) be binary rationals. A society \(\mu\) is given by its support: \(\tau\) strict complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An admissible parameter \(x=(\alpha,\theta)\) satisfies \(\alpha_r\ge0\), \(\sum_{r=1}^k\alpha_r=1\), \(\sum_{i=1}^m\theta_i^{(r)}=0\), and \(\theta_i^{(r)}\in[-C,C]\). Its ranking law selects component \(r\) with probability \(\alpha_r\), independently draws utilities \(U_i\sim\mathcal N(\theta_i^{(r)},1)\), and ranks alternatives by decreasing utility; utility ties have probability zero.
Write \(\Phi\) for the standard normal CDF, \(b_{ij}=\sum_{V:a_i\succ_V a_j}\mu_V\), \(p_{ij}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^k\alpha_r\Phi((\theta_i^{(r)}-\theta_j^{(r)})/\sqrt2)\), and \(G_\mu(x)=\sum_{i<j}(b_{ij}-p_{ij}(x))^2\).

The computational task is to return an admissible rational \(\widehat x\) satisfying \(G_\mu(\widehat x)\le\min_{x\text{ admissible real}}G_\mu(x)+\epsilon\).

Is this promised approximation task solvable in time polynomial in \(m,k,\tau,L\), and \(\max\{0,\log(1/\epsilon)\}\), or is it NP-hard?

I prove an additional structural restriction on the promise:

If $m\ge3$ and $k=\binom m2$, every component mean vector of the promised parameter lies on the boundary of the allowed mean set.

The argument establishes a stronger geometric fact: every attainable pairwise-moment vector has a representation using at most $\binom m2$ boundary mean vectors. I also give identifiable boundary mixtures showing that pairwise uniqueness alone does permit multiple components. These examples do not establish the exact rational-input promise.

For completeness, I supply self-contained proofs of the quasipolynomial upper bound, polynomial-time solvability for $k=1$, and a restriction on promise-preserving hardness reductions. The restriction extends to a precisely defined class of adaptive reductions.

1. Basic consequences of the promise

Set
$$ n=\binom m2,\qquad H=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb R^m:\sum_i\theta_i=0\right\}, \qquad D_C=H\cap[-C,C]^m. $$
Throughout, the interior and boundary of $D_C$ are taken relative to $H$.

Define
$$ h(d)=\Phi(d/\sqrt2), \qquad F(\theta)=\bigl(h(\theta_i-\theta_j)\bigr)_{i<j}. $$
The pairwise-moment vector of a parameter is
$$ p(x)=\sum_{r=1}^k\alpha_rF(\theta^{(r)}). $$

1.1. Full support and zero optimum

Every component assigns positive probability to every strict ranking. Indeed, its joint Gaussian utility density is positive everywhere, and the utility region defining any fixed strict ranking is a nonempty open subset of $\mathbb R^m$.

Consequently, every promised input satisfies
$$ \tau=m!, \qquad 0<b_{ij}<1. $$
Exact realizability also gives
$$ G_\mu(x_*)=0. $$
Since $G_\mu$ is nonnegative, its minimum is zero. Thus the promised task is to produce an admissible rational parameter with
$$ G_\mu(\widehat x)\le\epsilon. $$

1.2. Pairwise uniqueness implies $k\le n$

Write $v_r=F(\theta_*^{(r)})$.

First, $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are affinely independent. Otherwise there would be nonzero real coefficients $c_r$ with
$$ \sum_rc_r=0,\qquad \sum_rc_rv_r=0. $$
For sufficiently small nonzero $t$, all weights $\alpha_{*,r}+tc_r$ remain positive. Changing the weights this way preserves their sum and all pairwise moments. Because the mean vectors are distinct and unchanged, the resulting parameter is not a permutation of $x_*$. This contradicts uniqueness.

Hence $k\le n+1$. To exclude $k=n+1$, choose $\gamma\in D_C$ different from $\theta_*^{(1)}$ and replace the first mean vector by
$$ \theta^{(1)}(t)=(1-t)\theta_*^{(1)}+t\gamma. $$
Let $M(t)$ have columns
$$ (1,F(\theta^{(1)}(t))),\quad (1,v_2),\ldots,(1,v_k). $$
When $k=n+1$, affine independence makes $M(0)$ invertible. For sufficiently small $t$, the vector
$$ \beta(t)=M(t)^{-1}(1,b) $$
is well-defined and has positive coordinates, by continuity and positivity of $\alpha_*$. Its defining equation gives weights summing to one and exactly the original moments.

For sufficiently small positive $t$, the perturbed mean differs from every original mean vector. This again contradicts uniqueness. Therefore
$$ k\le n. $$

2. An elliptic identity for Gaussian pairwise moments

The following property is specific to the Gaussian comparison function and yields the new boundary restriction.

2.1. No nonzero linear functional of $F$ is constant

The comparison function has the expansion
$$ h(d)=\frac12+\frac1{2\sqrt\pi} \left(d-\frac{d^3}{12}+\cdots\right). $$

Suppose, for coefficients $c_{ij}$, that
$$ f(\theta)=\sum_{i<j}c_{ij}h(\theta_i-\theta_j) $$
is constant on $H$. Its homogeneous cubic Taylor term must vanish, so
$$ \sum_{i<j}c_{ij}(\theta_i-\theta_j)^3=0 \qquad(\theta\in H). $$
This polynomial depends only on coordinate differences. Subtracting the average coordinate therefore shows that it vanishes on all of $\mathbb R^m$.

For $i<j$, the coefficient of the monomial $\theta_i^2\theta_j$ is $-3c_{ij}$. Thus every $c_{ij}$ is zero.

In particular, the affine hull of $F(D_C)$ is $\mathbb R^n$: an affine relation holding on $D_C$ would hold on a neighborhood of zero in $H$, and its cubic term gives the same conclusion.

2.2. The differential identity

Direct differentiation gives
$$ h'(d)=\frac1{2\sqrt\pi}e^{-d^2/4}, \qquad h''(d)=-\frac d2h'(d). $$
For $d=\theta_i-\theta_j$,
$$ \Delta_{\mathbb R^m}h(d)=2h''(d)=-dh'(d), $$
whereas
$$ \theta\cdot\nabla h(d)=dh'(d). $$
Hence every linear functional $f$ as above satisfies
$$ \Delta_Hf+\theta\cdot\nabla_Hf=0. $$
The restriction to $H$ is valid because $f$ is invariant under adding a common constant to all coordinates. Its first and second derivatives in the direction orthogonal to $H$ therefore vanish.

2.3. No interior local extrema

Lemma. For $m\ge3$, every nonzero linear functional of $F$ has no local maximum or local minimum anywhere in $H$.

Proof. The function $f$ is real analytic and nonconstant by Section 2.1. At any $\theta_0\in H$, its Taylor expansion therefore has a first nonzero homogeneous term after its constant term.

Suppose $\theta_0$ were a local extremum. Its gradient would vanish, so this first term, denoted $Q_\ell$, would have degree $\ell\ge2$. In coordinates $y$ centered at $\theta_0$, the differential identity becomes
$$ \Delta_Hf(\theta_0+y)+(\theta_0+y)\cdot\nabla_Hf(\theta_0+y)=0. $$
The term of degree $\ell-2$ is exactly $\Delta_HQ_\ell$. Thus
$$ \Delta_HQ_\ell=0. $$

Let $S$ be the unit sphere in $H$. The divergence theorem and homogeneity imply
$$ 0=\int_{\{\|y\|\le1\}}\Delta_HQ_\ell(y)\,dy =\int_S\nabla_HQ_\ell(y)\cdot y\,dS(y) =\ell\int_SQ_\ell(y)\,dS(y). $$
At a local minimum, the Taylor expansion along every ray would give $Q_\ell(y)\ge0$ on $S$. Since $Q_\ell$ is nonzero and continuous, its integral would then be strictly positive. At a local maximum it would be strictly negative. Both contradict the displayed identity. $\square$

3. Boundary representations and the new uniqueness restriction

3.1. The boundary generates the entire convex hull

Proposition. If $m\ge3$, then
$$ \operatorname{conv}F(D_C) = \operatorname{conv}F(\partial D_C). $$
Moreover,
$$ F(\operatorname{int}D_C) \subset \operatorname{int}\bigl(\operatorname{conv}F(D_C)\bigr), $$
where the second interior is in $\mathbb R^n$.

Proof. Every nonzero linear functional of $F$ attains its maximum on the compact set $D_C$. By Section 2.3, no maximizer can lie in its interior. Its maximum is therefore attained on $\partial D_C$.

The convex hull of $F(\partial D_C)$ is compact. To justify this, the usual affine-dependence elimination shows that every point in a convex hull in $\mathbb R^n$ uses at most $n+1$ points. The hull in question is consequently the continuous image of a compact set consisting of $n+1$ boundary points and their simplex weights.

If some $F(\theta)$ lay outside this compact convex hull, a separating linear functional would give
$$ c\cdot F(\theta)> \max_{\eta\in\partial D_C}c\cdot F(\eta), $$
contradicting the preceding maximum property. This proves equality of the hulls.

The hull has full dimension by Section 2.1. If $\theta$ is interior but $F(\theta)$ lies on its boundary, a supporting hyperplane supplies a nonzero $c$ for which $\theta$ maximizes $c\cdot F$ over $D_C$. This contradicts Section 2.3. $\square$

3.2. At most $n$ boundary components suffice

I use the following elementary refinement of the convex-hull representation bound.

Lemma. If $S\subset\mathbb R^n$ is path connected, every point in $\operatorname{conv}S$ is a convex combination of at most $n$ points of $S$.

Proof. Affine-dependence elimination first gives a representation using at most $n+1$ points. The only remaining case consists of affinely independent points $s_0,\ldots,s_n$ with strictly positive weights.

Choose a continuous path $\gamma:[0,1]\to S$ from $s_0$ to $s_1$. Put
$$ T=\left\{t:b\in\operatorname{conv} \{\gamma(t),s_1,\ldots,s_n\}\right\}. $$
The set $T$ is closed: for convergent sequences of parameters, a convergent subsequence of the simplex weights supplies a representation at the limit.

Let $t_*$ be the largest number such that $[0,t_*]\subseteq T$. If $t_*<1$, affine independence and strict positivity of the barycentric weights at $t_*$ would persist in a neighborhood of $t_*$, contradicting maximality. Thus, at $t_*$, either a weight is zero or the points are affinely dependent. If $t_*=1$, the points are dependent because $\gamma(1)=s_1$.

A zero weight removes a point. In the dependent case, choose a nonzero affine dependence and adjust the weights along it until a weight first reaches zero. This preserves nonnegativity, total weight, and the represented point. Either way, at most $n$ points remain. $\square$

The boundary $\partial D_C$ is path connected for $m\ge3$. Indeed, radial projection gives a homeomorphism from the unit sphere of $H$ onto $\partial D_C$:
$$ u\longmapsto \frac{C}{\max_i|u_i|}u. $$
That sphere has dimension $m-2\ge1$ and is path connected. Its continuous image $F(\partial D_C)$ is therefore path connected.

Combining this fact with Section 3.1 proves:

Boundary representation theorem. Every pairwise-moment vector of an admissible mixture has a representation using at most $n$ means in $\partial D_C$.

3.3. Consequence for promised uniqueness

Suppose $k=n$ and some component mean of $x_*$ is interior. By the boundary representation theorem, $b$ has a representation using at most $n$ boundary means. Pad it with zero-weight components if necessary to obtain an admissible parameter with exactly $n$ components.

This parameter cannot be a permutation of $x_*$: the latter has positive weights and includes an interior mean. It nevertheless has exactly the same pairwise moments, contradicting the promise.

Thus, on promised inputs,
$$ m\ge3,\quad k=n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_*^{(r)}\in\partial D_C \quad\text{for every }r. $$
Equivalently, if any promised component mean is interior, then
$$ k\le n-1. $$

A further consequence is useful: if $b$ lies on the boundary of $\operatorname{conv}F(D_C)$, a supporting functional $c$ satisfies
$$ c\cdot b=\max_{\theta\in D_C}c\cdot F(\theta). $$
Positive weights force every component to attain that same maximum. Thus every component lies on $\partial D_C$ and in the same exposed moment face.

4. Identifiable boundary mixtures exist over real ranking laws

This section concerns real ranking masses. It does not construct promised binary-rational inputs.

Take $m=3$ and any $C>0$, and consider
$$ \vartheta^{(1)}=(C,0,-C),\qquad \vartheta^{(2)}=(-C,C,0),\qquad \vartheta^{(3)}=(0,-C,C). $$
Define the cyclic comparison functional
$$ f(\theta)= h(\theta_1-\theta_2)+ h(\theta_2-\theta_3)+ h(\theta_3-\theta_1). $$

Claim. Its maximum on $D_C$ is
$$ M=2h(C)+h(-2C), $$
and its maximizers are exactly the three vectors above.

Proof. Write $q(d)=h(d)-1/2$. This function is odd, and on $(0,\infty)$ it is strictly increasing and strictly concave.

The three cyclic differences sum to zero. If two are positive, write them as $a,b>0$, so the third is $-(a+b)$. Then
$$ f(\theta)-\frac32=q(a)+q(b)-q(a+b). $$
Their sum $s=a+b$ is the range of the three mean coordinates, hence $s\le2C$. Strict concavity gives
$$ q(a)+q(b)-q(s)\le2q(s/2)-q(s), $$
with equality precisely when $a=b=s/2$.

The function on the right is strictly increasing for $s>0$, since its derivative is
$$ q'(s/2)-q'(s)>0. $$
It is positive: strict decrease of $q'$ implies $q(a)+q(b)>q(a+b)$.

If instead two cyclic differences are negative, oddness gives a value below $3/2$. If a difference is zero, the value is $3/2$. Therefore the maximum occurs exactly when the two positive cyclic differences equal $C$ and the negative one equals $-2C$. Centering gives exactly the three listed vectors. $\square$

Use the equivalent moment coordinates $(p_{12},p_{23},p_{31})$. Put
$$ u=h(C),\qquad v=h(-2C). $$
The three moment vectors are
$$ (u,u,v),\qquad(v,u,u),\qquad(u,v,u). $$
They are affinely independent because $u\ne v$.

A positive mixture of all three is therefore globally identifiable among admissible three-component parameters from pairwise moments. Any competing representation must have every positive-weight mean among the three maximizers, since its cyclic functional equals $M$. Uniqueness of barycentric coordinates then forces the original weights and means.

Likewise, a positive mixture of any two is identifiable among two-component parameters: its moment vector lies in the open edge joining their two moment vectors, and its barycentric coordinate at the third vertex is zero.

These examples show that boundary configurations genuinely matter. However, no argument here shows that their complete ranking probabilities are binary rational. They therefore do not settle existence or complexity under the exact input promise.

5. Numerical tools with polynomial precision dependence

The following tools justify the computational upper bounds without inverse-conditioning assumptions.

5.1. Forward continuity

Since $h'(d)\le1/2$, admissible parameters $x=(\alpha,\theta)$ and $y=(\beta,\eta)$ satisfy
$$ |p_{ij}(x)-p_{ij}(y)| \le \|\alpha-\beta\|_1+ \max_{r,\ell}|\theta_\ell^{(r)}-\eta_\ell^{(r)}|. $$
The weight contribution is bounded by the first term because component probabilities lie in $[0,1]$. A coordinate perturbation of size $a$ changes each mean difference by at most $2a$, hence its comparison probability by at most $a$.

5.2. Rational polynomial approximation of $h$

Lemma. Given integers $R,s\ge1$, one can construct a rational polynomial $P$ with
$$ \sup_{|d|\le R}|P(d)-h(d)|\le2^{-s} $$
in time polynomial in $R+s$. Its degree and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in $R+s$.

Proof. Define
$$ T_M(d)= \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \frac{(-1)^jd^{2j+1}}{4^jj!(2j+1)}. $$
Taylor’s remainder for $e^{-t^2/4}$ gives
$$ \left|T_M(d)-\int_0^d e^{-t^2/4}\,dt\right| \le R\frac{(R^2/4)^M}{M!}. $$
Using $M!\ge(M/e)^M$ and $e<3$, this is at most $R2^{-M}$ whenever $M\ge3R^2/2$. Taking also
$$ M\ge s+\lceil\log_2(R+1)\rceil+4 $$
makes the error at most $2^{-s-4}$.

The constant $A=1/(2\sqrt\pi)$ has polynomial-time rational approximations to any requested binary precision. Here is a construction using the same polynomials. Gaussian normalization gives
$$ \sqrt\pi=\int_0^\infty e^{-t^2/4}\,dt, $$
and
$$ \int_T^\infty e^{-t^2/4}\,dt \le \frac2T e^{-T^2/4}. $$
For precision $2^{-t}$, choose an integer cutoff proportional to $t+1$ and approximate the truncated integral with the preceding Taylor construction. Its degree and rational bit lengths are polynomial in $t$. Taking a reciprocal preserves this complexity because $\sqrt\pi>1$ and the approximations can be kept above $1/2$.

Choose rational $a$ with
$$ |a-A|\le\frac{2^{-s}}{4(R+1)}. $$
Then $P(d)=1/2+aT_M(d)$ has the required error: the integral approximation contributes at most $2^{-s-4}/2$, and $|T_M(d)|\le R+1$ bounds the constant-approximation contribution by $2^{-s}/4$. The explicit coefficients also give the asserted bit bounds. $\square$

5.3. Compressing large mean gaps

Set
$$ \epsilon_0=\min\{1,\epsilon\},\qquad q=\frac{\epsilon_0}{64n},\qquad s=\left\lceil\log_2(1/q)\right\rceil,\qquad B=2(s+1). $$
The Gaussian Chernoff bound yields
$$ 1-h(B)\le e^{-B^2/4}\le2^{-s}\le q. $$

For a component mean vector, sort its coordinates and let $g_j$ be the adjacent gaps. Replace each gap by $\min\{g_j,B\}$ and center the resulting coordinates. This preserves the bound $C$: the centered extremes are
$$ \theta_{(m)}=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}jg_j, \qquad \theta_{(1)}=-\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(m-j)g_j. $$
Decreasing gaps lowers the maximum and raises the minimum.

The resulting vector has coordinates bounded by $mB$. For any pair, either its difference is unchanged, or both old and new differences have the same sign and absolute value at least $B$. Its comparison probability consequently changes by at most $q$.

Applying this operation to $x_*$ gives an admissible parameter $x^c$ with mean bound
$$ C'=\min\{C,mB\} $$
and
$$ |p_{ij}(x^c)-b_{ij}|\le q. $$

6. Computational upper bounds

Define
$$ N= 2+m+k+\tau+L+ \left\lceil\max\{0,\log_2(1/\epsilon)\}\right\rceil. $$

6.1. A deterministic algorithm in time $N^{O(km)}$

Construct $P$ from Section 5.2 on $[-2mB,2mB]$ with error at most $q$. Define
$$ \widetilde p_{ij}(x)= \sum_r\alpha_rP(\theta_i^{(r)}-\theta_j^{(r)}). $$
Consider the conjunction defining $K$:

  • the simplex constraints on the weights;
  • the centering constraints on every component;
  • the mean bounds $|\theta_i^{(r)}|\le C'$;
  • the inequalities $|\widetilde p_{ij}(x)-b_{ij}|\le2q$.

This is a nonempty semialgebraic set because $x^c\in K$. Every $y\in K$ satisfies
$$ |p_{ij}(y)-b_{ij}|\le3q. $$

The system has $v=k(m+1)$ variables and $S=O(km+n)$ polynomial constraints. Its degree $D$ and, after clearing denominators, coefficient bit length $H$ are polynomial in $N$.

For a conjunction of $S$ integer polynomial sign conditions in $v$ variables, degrees at most $D$ and coefficient bit lengths at most $H$, existential feasibility is decidable in bit time
$$ (2HSD)^{O(v)}. $$
This is the one-block, no-free-variable specialization of [Basu’s Theorem 2.18](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf), including its intermediate bit-length bound. All formulas used here have length $O(S+v)$; thus there is no omitted arbitrary Boolean-formula length. The theorem requires no nonsingularity hypothesis.

To obtain a rational output, repeatedly bisect coordinate intervals, retaining a closed half whose intersection with $K$ and the previously retained intervals is nonempty. Such a half always exists.

Put
$$ \eta=\frac q{2k+4}. $$
After polynomially many bisections, every interval has width at most $2\eta$. Its rational midpoint vector $u$ is within coordinate distance $\eta$ of some $y=(\alpha,\theta)\in K$.

Repair $u$ as follows. Normalize its nonnegative weight coordinates by their sum. That sum differs from one by at most $k\eta<1$, so normalization is defined and gives
$$ \|\widehat\alpha-\alpha\|_1\le2k\eta. $$
For each mean vector, subtract its coordinate average. This creates coordinate error at most $2\eta$. If its maximum absolute coordinate $M$ exceeds $C'$, scale it by $C'/M$. Since $M\le C'+2\eta$, scaling adds error at most $2\eta$. Thus
$$ \max_{r,i}|\widehat\theta_i^{(r)}-\theta_i^{(r)}| \le4\eta. $$
The output is rational and exactly admissible. Forward continuity gives
$$ |p_{ij}(\widehat x)-p_{ij}(y)| \le(2k+4)\eta=q. $$
Therefore
$$ G_\mu(\widehat x) \le16nq^2 =\frac{\epsilon_0^2}{256n} \le\epsilon. $$

There are polynomially many feasibility calls, with coefficient and endpoint bit lengths polynomial in $N$. Dense polynomial encoding, if used, has size $N^{O(v)}$ and fits the same bound. The resulting total time is $N^{O(km)}$.

On promised inputs, $\tau=m!$ implies
$$ m=O\left(\frac{\log N}{\log\log N}\right). $$
Together with $k\le n=O(m^2)$, this gives the quasipolynomial bound
$$ 2^{\,O\left((\log N)^4/(\log\log N)^3\right)}. $$
Its exponent still prevents a uniform polynomial-time conclusion.

6.2. Polynomial time for $k=1$

For one component, define $d_m=0$ and
$$ d_i=h^{-1}(b_{im})\quad(i<m). $$
Strict monotonicity of $h$ and exact realizability give
$$ d_i=\theta_{*,i}-\theta_{*,m}, \qquad \theta_{*,i}=d_i-\frac1m\sum_jd_j. $$

Each marginal’s denominator is at most the product of the input mass denominators, hence at most $2^L$. Since it is strictly between zero and one,
$$ 2^{-L}\le b_{im}\le1-2^{-L}. $$
The tail bound places all $d_i$ in $[-T,T]$ for $T=2(L+1)$. On that interval,
$$ h'(d)\ge\lambda, \qquad \lambda=2^{-2(L+1)^2-2}. $$
Indeed, $1/(2\sqrt\pi)>1/4$ and $e<4$.

To compute an inverse to coordinate error $a$, bisect this interval and evaluate $h$ with absolute error at most $\rho=\lambda a/4$, using Section 5.2. At a midpoint $t$:

  • an approximation below $b_{im}-\rho$ puts the inverse to the right;
  • one above $b_{im}+\rho$ puts it to the left;
  • otherwise $|h(t)-b_{im}|\le2\rho$, so $|t-d_i|\le a/2$.

Alternatively, stop when the interval width is at most $2a$. The evaluation precision has bit length
$$ O\bigl(L^2+\log(1/a)\bigr), $$
and the number of bisections is polynomial.

Take $a=\epsilon_0/(16n)$. Center the resulting rational approximations, and scale if needed to enforce $C$. The repair calculation gives coordinate error at most $4a$, hence
$$ G_\mu(\widehat x)\le16na^2 =\frac{\epsilon_0^2}{16n}\le\epsilon. $$
All computations are polynomial in the requested input and precision measures. Since $m=2$ forces $k=1$, that promised case is also settled.

7. Polynomial witnesses and an adaptive hardness restriction

7.1. A polynomial-time certificate relation

The algorithm in Section 6.1 produces outputs of polynomial bit length. Its approximation tools give a polynomial-time verifier:

  • Check exact rational admissibility with mean bound $C'$.
  • Check

$$ |\widetilde p_{ij}(x)-b_{ij}|\le5q \qquad(i<j). $$

The constructed output is accepted: its true moment error is at most $4q$, and polynomial approximation adds at most $q$.

Conversely, every accepted output has true moment error at most $6q$, giving
$$ G_\mu(x)\le36nq^2 =\frac{9\epsilon_0^2}{1024n}<\epsilon. $$
Thus acceptance is sound. A fixed polynomial bound on witness length can be imposed, since all bisection endpoints and repaired rational coordinates have polynomial bit length.

No inverse-conditioning bound is used. Consequently, under $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$, accepted witnesses can be found in polynomial time by Boolean-circuit encoding and satisfiability self-reduction.

7.2. Robust adaptive reductions would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$

Consider a deterministic polynomial-time oracle procedure deciding satisfiability and using this search task. Assume:

  • Every query satisfies the promise after every history of valid oracle answers.
  • The final satisfiability answer is correct after every such history.

These are explicit robustness requirements; the conclusion below is restricted to this reduction notion.

For an unsatisfiable formula, guess a complete oracle transcript in which each answer is an accepted polynomial-size witness from Section 7.1. Verify the answers and simulate the procedure, accepting the certificate only if its final answer is “unsatisfiable.”

Such a transcript exists: each query is promised, so it has an accepted witness. The transcript has polynomial length.

For a satisfiable formula, every verified answer is a valid approximation output. Inductively, the simulated history is therefore a valid oracle history. The reduction’s correctness excludes an “unsatisfiable” final answer.

Unsatisfiability would consequently belong to $\mathrm{NP}$, implying
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

This extends the analogous one-query obstruction to robust adaptive reductions. It does not prove a polynomial-time algorithm and does not address reductions that fail these requirements.

8. What remains open
