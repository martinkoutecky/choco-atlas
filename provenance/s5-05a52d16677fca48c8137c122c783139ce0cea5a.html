The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational row-stochastic mixing matrix \(K\), reference opinions \(r_q\in\mathbb{Q}^D\), fixed per-capita allocations \(u_{q,t}\) for \(q\ne p\), a per-capita budget \(\beta\), and a rational threshold \(a\), decide whether there are allocations \(u_{p,t}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tu_{p,t}\le\beta\) and stationary opinions \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^D\) satisfying \(\left(1+\sum_q u_{q,t}\right)x_t-\sum_{s\in T}K_{ts}x_s=\sum_q u_{q,t}r_q\) for every \(t\), such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t r_p^\top x_t\ge a\). Assume \(K\) is irreducible and the fixed players exert positive influence on at least one type, ensuring a unique stationary state.

Let \(m=D\ge1\) be the opinion dimension, \(P\ge2\) the number of players, and \(L\) the total input bit length.
The input specifies a player \(p\), an irreducible nonnegative rational row-stochastic matrix \(K\in\mathbb Q^{\tau\times\tau}\), pairwise distinct rational reference opinions \(r_q\in\mathbb Q^m\) with \(\|r_q\|_2=1\), a rational common per-capita budget \(\beta>0\), and a threshold \(a\in\mathbb Q\).
For each \(q\ne p\), fixed rational allocations satisfy \(u_{q,t}\ge0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tu_{q,t}\le\beta\); assume \(\sum_t\sum_{q\ne p}u_{q,t}>0\). The entry \(K_{ts}\) is the total averaging weight type \(t\) places on type \(s\).
Feasibility means that real allocations \(u_{p,t}\ge0\) satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_tu_{p,t}\le\beta\), and the unique stationary opinions \(x_t\in\mathbb R^m\) satisfy \((1+\sum_{q=1}^P u_{q,t})x_t-\sum_sK_{ts}x_s=\sum_{q=1}^P u_{q,t}r_q\) for every \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t r_p^\top x_t\ge a\).

1. Scalar reduction and uniqueness

Index the supported types by $i=1,\ldots,\tau$. Define
$$ u_i=u_{p,i},\qquad v_i=\sum_{q\ne p}u_{q,i},\qquad c_i=\sum_{q\ne p}u_{q,i}(1-r_p^\top r_q). $$
Because the reference opinions are distinct unit vectors,
$$ 1-r_p^\top r_q =\frac12\|r_p-r_q\|_2^2\in(0,2] \qquad(q\ne p). $$
Thus
$$ 0\le c_i\le 2v_i, \qquad c\ne0, \qquad v\ne0. $$
The last two conclusions use the stipulated positive total rival allocation.

Put
$$ h_i=1+v_i,\qquad d_i=h_i+u_i,\qquad H(u)=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_\tau), \qquad A(u)=H(u)-K. $$

Lemma 1. The matrix $A(u)$ has a strictly positive inverse for every $u\ge0$

Let
$$ Q=H(u)^{-1}K. $$
This matrix is nonnegative and irreducible. Its row sums are $1/d_i\le1$, with at least one strictly smaller than one because $v\ne0$.

Define
$$ e=\mathbf1-Q\mathbf1\ge0 $$
and choose $j$ with $e_j>0$. For every $i$, irreducibility supplies a positive directed path from $i$ to $j$ of length at most $\tau-1$. Consequently,
$$ \mathbf1-Q^\tau\mathbf1 =\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}Q^ke>\mathbf0. $$
Since $Q^\tau$ is nonnegative, this implies
$$ \|Q^\tau\|_\infty<1. $$
The series $\sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k$ therefore converges: group its terms according to the exponent modulo $\tau$ and use the geometric bound on powers of $Q^\tau$.

The factorization $A(u)=H(u)(I-Q)$ gives
$$ A(u)^{-1} =\sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^kH(u)^{-1}. $$
Every term is nonnegative. For any $i,j$, a positive directed path from $i$ to $j$ supplies a strictly positive contribution to the corresponding inverse entry; for $i=j$, the term with $k=0$ already does so. Hence $A(u)^{-1}>0$ entrywise. $\square$

The original stationary opinion equations consequently have a unique solution in every coordinate.

Let
$$ z_i=r_p^\top x_i,\qquad y_i=1-z_i. $$
Projection of the stationary equations gives
$$ A(u)z =u+\left(\sum_{q\ne p}u_{q,i}r_p^\top r_q\right)_{i=1}^{\tau}. $$
Because $K\mathbf1=\mathbf1$,
$$ A(u)\mathbf1=u+v. $$
Subtracting yields
$$ A(u)y=c. $$
Thus
$$ y=A(u)^{-1}c>0, \qquad G(u):=\sum_i\mu_i r_p^\top x_i =1-f(u), $$
where
$$ f(u)=\mu^\top A(u)^{-1}c. $$

Writing $b=1-a$, exact feasibility is therefore equivalent to
$$ u\ge0,\qquad \mu^\top u\le\beta,\qquad f(u)\le b. $$
All coefficients in this scalar formulation are rational and can be computed in polynomial time and with polynomial encoding length from the original input.

For completeness,
$$ A(u)(2\mathbf1-y)=2(u+v)-c\ge0. $$
Lemma 1 implies $y\le2\mathbf1$. Hence $-1\le G(u)<1$: thresholds $a\le-1$ are feasible, while thresholds $a\ge1$ are infeasible. The substantive range is $-1<a<1$.

2. Convexity and exact optimality conditions

Lemma 2. The deficit $f$ is strictly convex and strictly decreasing in every allocation coordinate

The series in Lemma 1 expands $f$ into nonnegative path terms. A path $i_0,\ldots,i_k$ contributes
$$ \mu_{i_0}c_{i_k} \left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}K_{i_\ell i_{\ell+1}}\right) \left(\prod_{\ell=0}^{k}d_{i_\ell}^{-1}\right). $$
Every nonzero term has the form
$$ g(u)=C\prod_i(h_i+u_i)^{-n_i}, $$
where $C>0$ and the $n_i$ are nonnegative integers.

Consider a segment $u(t)=u+t(w-u)$, with $0\le t\le1$, and write $\Delta_i=w_i-u_i$. Then
$$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log g(u(t)) =\sum_i\frac{n_i\Delta_i^2}{(h_i+u_i+t\Delta_i)^2}\ge0. $$
Moreover,
$$ \frac{d^2}{dt^2}g(u(t)) =g(u(t)) \left( \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\log g(u(t)) + \left(\frac{d}{dt}\log g(u(t))\right)^2 \right)\ge0. $$
Thus each term is convex. Finite partial sums are convex, and passage to their finite pointwise limit preserves the convexity inequality.

If $u\ne w$, choose $i$ with $\Delta_i\ne0$ and $j$ with $c_j>0$. Irreducibility supplies a positive path from $i$ to $j$. Its term has $n_i>0$, so its second derivative along the segment is strictly positive. The sum of the remaining terms is convex, proving strict convexity of $f$.

For monotonicity, define
$$ y=A(u)^{-1}c,\qquad z=A(u)^{-\top}\mu. $$
Both vectors are strictly positive by Lemma 1. Differentiating the matrix inverse gives
$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial u_i}=-z_i y_i<0. $$
$\square$

The allocation set
$$ \mathcal U=\{u\ge0:\mu^\top u\le\beta\} $$
is nonempty, compact, and convex. Indeed, each coordinate satisfies $u_i\le\beta/\mu_i$. Continuity gives a minimizer $u^*$ of $f$; strict convexity makes it unique. Strict coordinate monotonicity implies
$$ \mu^\top u^*=\beta. $$

The following optimality characterization will give an existential description of infeasibility.

Lemma 3. Exact first-order characterization

A vector $u\in\mathcal U$ minimizes $f$ if and only if it uses the full budget and there exist $\lambda\ge0$ and $\nu\ge0$ such that
$$ z_i y_i+\nu_i=\lambda\mu_i, \qquad u_i\nu_i=0 \qquad(i=1,\ldots,\tau), $$
where $y=A(u)^{-1}c$ and $z=A(u)^{-\top}\mu$.

Necessity. At the minimizer, the full budget is used, so some coordinate is positive. Write
$$ g_i=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_i}=-z_i y_i. $$
If $u_i,u_j>0$, both signs of a sufficiently small move in direction
$$ \frac{e_i}{\mu_i}-\frac{e_j}{\mu_j} $$
are feasible. Differentiability and optimality therefore imply
$$ \frac{g_i}{\mu_i}=\frac{g_j}{\mu_j}. $$
Fix any positive coordinate $i$ and set
$$ \lambda=-\frac{g_i}{\mu_i}>0. $$
For a zero coordinate $j$, a sufficiently small positive move in direction
$$ \frac{e_j}{\mu_j}-\frac{e_i}{\mu_i} $$
is feasible. Its directional derivative must be nonnegative, giving
$$ \frac{g_j}{\mu_j}-\frac{g_i}{\mu_i}\ge0. $$
Consequently, the quantities
$$ \nu_j=\lambda\mu_j-z_jy_j $$
are nonnegative, and they vanish at every positive coordinate. These are the claimed conditions.

Sufficiency. Convexity and differentiability imply, for every $w\in\mathcal U$,
$$ f(w)\ge f(u)+\nabla f(u)^\top(w-u). $$
Using the displayed conditions and $\mu^\top u=\beta$,
$$ \begin{aligned} \nabla f(u)^\top(w-u) &=(-\lambda\mu+\nu)^\top(w-u)\\ &=\lambda(\beta-\mu^\top w)+\nu^\top w\\ &\ge0. \end{aligned} $$
Here $\nu^\top u=0$ follows from coordinatewise complementarity. Thus $f(w)\ge f(u)$ for every feasible $w$. $\square$

3. A stronger general upper bound: $\exists\mathbb R\cap\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R$

The class $\exists\mathbb R$ consists of decision problems polynomial-time reducible to deciding whether a finite system of polynomial equalities and inequalities with integer coefficients has a real solution. Strict inequalities are permitted. The class $\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R$ consists of complements of such problems.

Proposition 4. Feasibility has a polynomial-size quadratic existential description

Introduce real vectors $u,y$ and impose
$$ u_i\ge0,\qquad \mu^\top u\le\beta, $$
$$ (1+v_i+u_i)y_i-\sum_jK_{ij}y_j=c_i \qquad(i=1,\ldots,\tau), $$
$$ \mu^\top y\le b. $$
The equations determine $y=A(u)^{-1}c$ uniquely by Lemma 1. Hence this system is equivalent to the original feasibility problem.

There are $2\tau$ variables and polynomially many constraints, all of degree at most two. Clearing rational denominators uses positive integer multipliers and preserves polynomial encoding length. Thus feasibility belongs to $\exists\mathbb R$.

Proposition 5. Infeasibility also has a polynomial-size quadratic existential description

Introduce real vectors $u,y,z,\nu$ and a real scalar $\lambda$. Impose
$$ u_i\ge0,\qquad \mu^\top u=\beta, $$
$$ A(u)y=c,\qquad A(u)^\top z=\mu, $$
$$ \lambda\ge0,\qquad \nu_i\ge0, $$
$$ z_i y_i+\nu_i=\lambda\mu_i,\qquad u_i\nu_i=0 \qquad(i=1,\ldots,\tau), $$
and finally
$$ \mu^\top y>b. $$

If this system has a solution, Lemma 1 identifies $y$ and $z$ as the inverse expressions used in Lemma 3. That lemma makes $u$ a global minimizer of $f$. Since its value exceeds $b$, the original instance is infeasible.

Conversely, if the instance is infeasible, its attained minimum satisfies
$$ f(u^*)>b. $$
Lemma 3 supplies $\lambda,\nu$ completing a solution of the displayed system.

This description has $4\tau+1$ variables, polynomially many constraints, and degree at most two. Its coefficients again have polynomial encoding length. Therefore
$$ \boxed{ \text{Exact society feasibility belongs to } \exists\mathbb R\cap\operatorname{co}\exists\mathbb R. } $$

This is an upper bound using real witnesses. It does not establish membership in $\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}$: no polynomial-size binary encoding of these witnesses has been proved.

4. Exact solution of the mass-matched rank-one subclass using $\operatorname{SQRTSUM}$

In this section, assume additionally that
$$ K_{ij}=\mu_j\qquad\text{for all }i,j. $$
Every entry of $K$ is positive because every supported mass is positive, so this subclass satisfies irreducibility.

Thresholds with $b\le0$ are infeasible by Section 1. Hence assume $b>0$ and define
$$ w_i=c_i+b>0,\qquad B=\beta+\sum_i\mu_i h_i. $$

4.1 Reduction to a separable reciprocal objective

For any allocation, set $d_i=h_i+u_i$ and $f=\mu^\top y$. Since $Ky=f\mathbf1$, the deficit equations become
$$ d_i y_i-f=c_i. $$
Define
$$ S(d)=\sum_i\frac{\mu_i}{d_i}, \qquad C(d)=\sum_i\frac{\mu_i c_i}{d_i}. $$
Averaging $y_i=(c_i+f)/d_i$ gives
$$ f=C(d)+fS(d). $$
Every $d_i\ge h_i\ge1$, and some $h_i>1$ because $v\ne0$. Consequently $S(d)<1$, and
$$ f=\frac{C(d)}{1-S(d)}. $$
It follows that
$$ f\le b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_b(d):=\sum_i\frac{\mu_i w_i}{d_i}\le b. $$
The allocation constraints are exactly
$$ d_i\ge h_i,\qquad \sum_i\mu_i d_i\le B. $$

Thus this subclass requires exact comparison of the minimum of a separable reciprocal objective with the rational number $b$.

4.2 The unique minimizing allocation

Set
$$ t_i=\frac{h_i}{\sqrt{w_i}}. $$
Consider
$$ \Phi(\alpha)=\sum_i\mu_i\max(h_i,\alpha\sqrt{w_i}) \qquad(\alpha\ge0). $$
This function is continuous, equals $\sum_i\mu_i h_i<B$ at $\alpha=0$, and tends to infinity. For $\alpha>\min_i t_i$, a coordinate with minimum $t_i$ increases strictly with $\alpha$, while every other coordinate is nondecreasing. Hence $\Phi$ is strictly increasing on that interval.

There is therefore a unique $\alpha>\min_i t_i$ satisfying $\Phi(\alpha)=B$. Define
$$ d_i^*=\max(h_i,\alpha\sqrt{w_i}). $$

To prove optimality, set $\lambda=\alpha^{-2}$. At every coordinate with $t_i\le\alpha$,
$$ \frac{\partial F_b}{\partial d_i}(d^*)=-\lambda\mu_i. $$
At every coordinate with $t_i>\alpha$,
$$ d_i^*=h_i,\qquad \frac{\partial F_b}{\partial d_i}(d^*) =-\frac{\mu_i w_i}{h_i^2}>-\lambda\mu_i. $$
The function $F_b$ is strictly convex on the positive orthant, because its Hessian is diagonal with positive entries
$$ \frac{\partial^2 F_b}{\partial d_i^2} =\frac{2\mu_i w_i}{d_i^3}>0. $$

For any feasible $d$, the supporting inequality gives
$$ F_b(d)\ge F_b(d^*)+\nabla F_b(d^*)^\top(d-d^*). $$
At coordinates with $t_i>\alpha$, we have $d_i-d_i^*\ge0$. Thus
$$ \nabla F_b(d^*)^\top(d-d^*) \ge-\lambda\sum_i\mu_i(d_i-d_i^*)\ge0, $$
where the last inequality uses $\sum_i\mu_i d_i\le B=\sum_i\mu_i d_i^*$. This proves that $d^*$ is the unique minimizer.

4.3 Finding the active coordinates by exact root comparisons

Sort the indices so that
$$ t_1\le t_2\le\cdots\le t_\tau. $$
This requires only rational comparisons, because
$$ t_i^2=\frac{h_i^2}{w_i}. $$

Let $k$ be the largest index with $t_k\le\alpha$. In particular, all indices tied at $\alpha$ are included. For each candidate $k$, define
$$ H_k=\sum_{i>k}\mu_i h_i,\qquad C_k=B-H_k, $$
$$ R_k=\sum_{i\le k}\mu_i\sqrt{w_i}, \qquad J_k=\sum_{i>k}\frac{\mu_i w_i}{h_i}. $$
For the correct $k$, the budget equation gives
$$ \alpha=\frac{C_k}{R_k}. $$
A candidate is correct precisely when
$$ R_k\le\frac{C_k}{t_k} $$
and, if $k<\tau$,
$$ R_k>\frac{C_k}{t_{k+1}}. $$
These conditions are exactly $\alpha\ge t_k$ and $\alpha<t_{k+1}$. Conversely, if they hold, the resulting allocation satisfies $\Phi(\alpha)=B$, so uniqueness of $\alpha$ proves correctness of the candidate.

Each comparison can be submitted to $\operatorname{SQRTSUM}$. For example,
$$ R_k\le\frac{C_k}{t_j} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i\le k}\mu_i\sqrt{w_iw_j} \le\frac{C_kw_j}{h_j}. $$
All radicands and the right-hand side are positive rationals. The strict comparison needed for the first inactive coordinate is obtained by negating the answer to the corresponding non-strict comparison.

Trying all $k$ uses at most $2\tau$ root-comparison queries and finds the unique correct prefix, including when some coordinates lie exactly at their lower bounds.

4.4 Comparing the minimum with the threshold

For the correct prefix,
$$ F_b(d^*)=\frac{R_k^2}{C_k}+J_k. $$
The quantities $C_k$ and $R_k$ are positive. If $b-J_k\le0$, the threshold is infeasible. Otherwise put
$$ Q_k=C_k(b-J_k)>0. $$
Then
$$ F_b(d^*)\le b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R_k\le\sqrt{Q_k} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i\le k}\mu_i\sqrt{w_iQ_k}\le Q_k. $$
This requires one further root-comparison query.

To justify the conversion to the stated integer-input oracle, consider any comparison
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\gamma_i\sqrt{\eta_i}\le r $$
with positive rational coefficients, radicands, and threshold. Absorb the coefficients into the radicands:
$$ \gamma_i\sqrt{\eta_i}=\sqrt{\gamma_i^2\eta_i}. $$
Choose a positive integer $D_0$ divisible by every denominator of $\gamma_i^2\eta_i$ and by the denominator of $r$. Then
$$ z_i=D_0^2\gamma_i^2\eta_i $$
are positive integers, $s=D_0r$ is an integer, and the comparison is equivalent to
$$ \sum_i\sqrt{z_i}\le s. $$
One may choose $D_0$ as the product of the relevant denominators. Its bit length is at most their total bit length. All arithmetic, oracle inputs, and the number of queries are consequently polynomially bounded in the original input length.

We have proved a polynomial-time Turing reduction from this society subclass to $\operatorname{SQRTSUM}$.

5. A converse reduction satisfying every society promise

I now prove the converse reduction, including the common-budget requirement and dyadic masses.

Given positive integers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ and an integer $s$, pad the list with entries equal to one until its length $N$ is a power of two. Increase $s$ by the number of appended entries. This preserves the comparison, and $N\le2n$. Use the padded notation below.

If $s<N$, the comparison is false. Map this case to the fixed society
$$ \tau=m=1,\quad P=2,\quad K=[1],\quad \mu_1=1,\quad r_1=1,\quad r_2=-1, $$
with rival allocation one, budget one, and threshold $a=1/2$. Its stationary opinion is
$$ x_1=\frac{u_1-1}{u_1+1}, \qquad 0\le u_1\le1, $$
whose maximum is zero. This society is infeasible.

Assume henceforth $s\ge N$. Define
$$ M=\max_i z_i,\qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{4M^2},\qquad B=\frac{s^2}{N^2\varepsilon}. $$
Construct
$$ \tau=N,\qquad m=1,\qquad P=2,\qquad p=1, $$
$$ r_1=1,\qquad r_2=-1,\qquad \mu_i=\frac1N,\qquad K_{ij}=\frac1N. $$
Set
$$ v_i=u_{2,i}=\frac{z_i-\varepsilon}{2}, \qquad h_i=1+v_i, $$
$$ \beta=B-\frac1N\sum_i h_i, \qquad a=1-\varepsilon. $$

5.1 Verification of the input promises

The masses are positive dyadic rationals because $N$ is a power of two. The matrix is strictly positive and row-stochastic, hence irreducible. The references are distinct rational unit vectors.

Every rival allocation is positive, since $z_i\ge1$ and $\varepsilon\le1/4$. Also
$$ B\ge\frac1\varepsilon=4M^2, \qquad v_i\le\frac M2, \qquad h_i\le1+\frac M2. $$
Therefore
$$ \beta\ge4M^2-1-\frac M2\ge\frac M2>0. $$
The middle inequality is equivalent to $4M^2-M-1\ge0$, which holds for every integer $M\ge1$. It follows that
$$ \sum_i\mu_i u_{2,i}\le\frac M2\le\beta. $$
Thus the rival satisfies the required common per-capita budget.

All constructed rationals have polynomial encoding length. The explicitly supplied matrix has $N^2$ entries, with $N\le2n$, so the construction is polynomial in the source input length.

5.2 Exact threshold equivalence

Here
$$ c_i=2v_i=z_i-\varepsilon,\qquad b=1-a=\varepsilon. $$
Section 4.1 therefore shows that feasibility is equivalent to
$$ F(d):=\frac1N\sum_i\frac{z_i}{d_i}\le\varepsilon, $$
subject to
$$ d_i\ge h_i,\qquad \frac1N\sum_i d_i\le B. $$

Write
$$ R=\sum_i\sqrt{z_i}. $$
For every feasible $d$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$ R^2 = \left(\sum_i\sqrt{\frac{z_i}{d_i}}\sqrt{d_i}\right)^2 \le \left(\sum_i\frac{z_i}{d_i}\right) \left(\sum_i d_i\right) \le N^2BF(d). $$
Thus
$$ F(d)\ge\frac{R^2}{N^2B}. $$

Equality is attained by
$$ d_i^*=\frac{NB\sqrt{z_i}}{R}. $$
These values have average $B$. They satisfy the lower bounds because
$$ R\le N\sqrt M $$
and hence
$$ d_i^* \ge\frac B{\sqrt M} \ge\frac BM \ge4M >1+\frac M2 \ge h_i. $$
Consequently,
$$ \min F(d)=\frac{R^2}{N^2B}. $$
The constructed society is feasible exactly when
$$ \frac{R^2}{N^2B}\le\varepsilon. $$
Substitution of $B=s^2/(N^2\varepsilon)$ gives
$$ R^2\le s^2. $$
Both $R$ and $s$ are positive in this branch, so this is equivalent to
$$ \sum_i\sqrt{z_i}\le s. $$
The reduction therefore preserves equality as well as strict comparisons. Its witness allocations may be irrational, which is permitted by the task.

Combining Sections 4 and 5 proves
$$ \boxed{ \text{Exact feasibility with }K_{ij}=\mu_j \text{ is polynomial-time Turing equivalent to } \operatorname{SQRTSUM}. } $$
The converse direction is already a polynomial-time many-one reduction, even with $m=1$, $P=2$, uniform dyadic masses, and strictly positive $K$.

6. What remains unresolved

The general problem has both existential feasibility certificates and existential infeasibility certificates. The mass-matched rank-one subclass has an exact characterization in terms of square-root-sum comparison.

Neither conclusion supplies the requested polynomial-time algorithm or an NP-hardness proof. Square-root-sum comparison is a standard exact-comparison benchmark discussed by [Allender et al., *On the Complexity of Numerical Analysis*](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2005/037/revision/1/download); related separation difficulties are studied by [Eisenbrand, Haeberle, and Singer](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.54). The reduction above is not an NP-hardness theorem.

A complete classification still requires additional mathematics: either an exact algorithm with polynomial binary bit complexity, including equality at the optimum, or a valid NP-hardness reduction.
