The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two populations with binary-rational type masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\), acceptable pairs, and rankings over counterpart types with ties of size at most two, does there exist an algorithm polynomial in the number of types, acceptable pairs, and mass bit length that returns a weakly stable mass matching \(x\) with \(\sum_{ij}x_{ij}\ge 3\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}/4\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\) is the maximum matched mass among weakly stable assignments? Is the \(4/3\) approximation factor tight for this model?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two nonempty populations of types, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\). Each type weakly ranks its acceptable counterpart types; every indifference class contains at most two types, and every acceptable counterpart is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Masses \(p_i,q_j\) need not sum to one or have equal population totals. Set \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; running times are measured in \(m,\tau,L\).
A mass matching has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq p_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq q_j\); types may split their mass among partners. Write \(k\succeq_i j\) when type \(i\) weakly prefers \(k\) to \(j\), and analogously \(h\succeq_j i\).
Call \(x\) weakly stable if, for every \((i,j)\in E\), either \(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}=p_i\) or \(\sum_{h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}=q_j\). Thus a pair blocks precisely when both sides have positive mass unmatched or assigned to strictly worse counterparts; indifference alone does not block.
Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\) over weakly stable mass matchings.

Does an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) return a rational weakly stable mass matching of mass at least \(3\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}/4\)? Is \(4/3\) the optimal polynomial-time approximation factor, or can a strictly smaller constant factor be achieved?

For finite individual agents with ties of size at most two, a polynomial-time \(4/3\)-approximation is known, and its worst-case bound is attained ([Chiang–Pashkovich](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04510v2)).
For unit-capacity fractional bipartite matchings, ordinal stability implies that every integral matching in a decomposition is stable; maximizing the number of fully matched agents with ties is NP-complete ([Chen–Roy–Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)). No result settling the approximation question above for arbitrary rational type masses was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The undefined mass-stability notion is settled by prohibiting positive mutually improving mass transfers, including transfers from unmatched mass; this gives the stated saturation disjunction.
  • Masses are positive binary rationals without normalization or equal-total requirements, preserving the discrete model's allowance for unequal population sizes.
  • Assignments may split type masses and use real entries; the algorithm must return rational entries for finite binary output.
  • Acceptability is mutual, rankings are complete weak orders on acceptable neighbors, and acceptable partners strictly outrank being unmatched. Ties of size two count counterpart types.
  • The undefined tightness question concerns the best polynomial-time approximation factor for the model, rather than the performance of an unspecified continuous adaptation of the discrete algorithm.
  • The size parameters are fixed as total support size tau, number of acceptable pairs m, and total input bit length L.

$$ |x|\geq \frac{2}{3}\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. $$
This theorem allows indifference classes of arbitrary size.

The construction uses three auxiliary copies of each acceptable edge. Its analysis below works directly with capacities; the algorithm never expands binary-encoded masses into individual agents. A six-type example shows that the guarantee for arbitrary stable outputs of this construction is tight, even with ties of size at most two.

I also prove that the conditional $4/3$ lower bound transfers to fractional outputs. Thus, under the Unique Games Conjecture, the optimal attainable factor for the full mass model lies between $4/3$ and $3/2$. Whether $4/3$ is attainable remains open here.

1. Definitions and rational attainment

Write $V=A\cup B$, and denote the mass of a vertex $v$ by $c_v$. For a feasible allocation $z$, put
$$ d_z(v)=\sum_{e\ni v}z_e, \qquad |z|=\sum_{e\in E}z_e. $$
An endpoint *protects* an edge when its protecting equality in the question holds.

If $E$ is empty, the zero allocation is optimal. Henceforth the constructions concern the nontrivial case.

We first establish rational attainment, which will justify clearing denominators in the approximation proof.

Lemma 1. For integral masses, every weakly stable allocation is a convex combination of integral weakly stable allocations. For rational masses, a rational optimum exists with polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Consider the bounded transportation polytope
$$ \mathcal P(c)= \left\{ z\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E}: d_z(v)\leq c_v\text{ for every }v \right\}. $$
For each edge, choose one endpoint required to protect it. The resulting set $F$ imposes equalities of the form
$$ \sum_{k\succeq_i j}z_{ik}=c_i. $$
Each is the equality case of a valid inequality on $\mathcal P(c)$, so every nonempty such $F$ is a face. Every point of $F$ is stable. Conversely, every stable point belongs to at least one such face.

The incidence matrix of a bipartite graph is totally unimodular: after changing the signs of the rows indexed by $B$, each column has one positive and one negative entry. To check a square minor, expand along a column with at most one nonzero entry; if every column has two nonzero entries, the rows sum to zero. Induction therefore gives determinants in $\{0,1,-1\}$. Adding the nonnegativity constraints preserves this property.

Consequently, for integral $c$, all vertices of $\mathcal P(c)$ are integral. The vertices of a face $F$ are vertices of $\mathcal P(c)$, and all are stable. Decomposing a stable point into vertices of its chosen face proves the first assertion.

The stable set is a finite union of closed faces of a bounded polytope. Once nonemptiness is established below, it is compact and the objective attains its maximum.

For rational masses, let $D$ be the product of their denominators. Its bit length is at most the sum of those denominator bit lengths. Scaling by $D$ preserves feasibility and stability. The integral-mass conclusion gives an optimum satisfying
$$ Dz_e\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0} \quad\text{for every }e. $$
Since $z_e\leq\min(c_i,c_j)$, these entries have polynomial total encoding length. $\square$

2. The strict stable-allocation subroutine

We use the following known result: a bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences, rational vertex quotas, and rational edge capacities can be solved in polynomial time. An edge blocks when it has unused capacity and both endpoints are underfilled or hold positive allocation on a worse incident edge. See [Dean–Munshi, Sections 2–3](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Preferences here rank incident edges. Parallel edges are distinct list entries. The proposal, rejection, and augmentation operations use these entries individually; allowing parallel edges, including alternating cycles of length two, does not change their correctness or polynomial bounds.

For completeness, we explain how to implement underfilled vertices through the full-quota formulation of that subroutine. Let
$$ P=\sum_{i\in A}c_i, \qquad Q=\sum_{j\in B}c_j. $$
Add vertices $a_0,b_0$ of quotas $Q,P$, respectively. Give the new edges capacities
$$ u_{i b_0}=c_i,\qquad u_{a_0 j}=c_j,\qquad u_{a_0b_0}=\min(P,Q). $$
Real vertices rank their dummy edge last. Each dummy ranks all real vertices above the other dummy.

Every feasible allocation $f$ on the real graph has a full-quota extension:
$$ f_{i b_0}=c_i-d_f(i),\qquad f_{a_0 j}=c_j-d_f(j),\qquad f_{a_0b_0}=|f|. $$
The dummy totals are $P$ and $Q$, and $|f|\leq\min(P,Q)$ ensures feasibility.

Conversely, restricting a full-quota stable allocation gives stability under the stated underfilling convention: an underfilled real vertex has positive allocation on its worst dummy edge.

The augmented instance has only linearly many additional vertices and edges. Clearing input denominators gives integer data of polynomial bit length. The stable-allocation algorithm performs polynomially many arithmetic operations on capacities and residual capacities, yielding polynomial bit complexity and rational output.

Breaking the original ties arbitrarily and applying this subroutine also establishes nonemptiness of the original stable set. Indeed, if an original edge blocked, both endpoints would be underfilled or hold strictly worse original partners. They would still desire that edge after tie breaking. Moreover, its allocation would be below $\min(c_i,c_j)$, because equality would fill one endpoint entirely with that counterpart. It would therefore block the strict allocation, a contradiction. This completes the existence assertion used in Lemma 1.

3. The three-copy algorithm

The preference pattern below adapts the three-copy construction of [Yokoi, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03076). We prove the capacitated guarantee independently, rather than invoking the finite-contract approximation theorem.

For every original edge $e=(i,j)$, create three parallel auxiliary edges
$$ X_e,\quad Y_e,\quad Z_e. $$
Each has capacity
$$ u_e=\min(c_i,c_j). $$
The vertex quotas remain $c_v$.

At each vertex, choose an arbitrary order within every original indifference class.

For $i\in A$, replace a class containing edges $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ by
$$ X_{e_1}\succ\cdots\succ X_{e_k} \succ Y_{e_1}\succ\cdots\succ Y_{e_k}. $$
Concatenate these blocks in the original class order. Then append all $Z$ copies, ordered by the original classes and the chosen within-class orders.

For $j\in B$, use the symmetric construction:
$$ Z_{e_1}\succ\cdots\succ Z_{e_k} \succ Y_{e_1}\succ\cdots\succ Y_{e_k}, $$
and append all $X$ copies at the end.

Find a strict stable auxiliary allocation $f$ using Section 2, and return
$$ x_e=f_{X_e}+f_{Y_e}+f_{Z_e}. $$
Because auxiliary vertex quotas are unchanged,
$$ d_x(v)=d_f(v)\leq c_v. $$
Thus $x$ is feasible. No separate capacity constraint on the three copies together is needed: feasibility already gives $x_e\leq u_e$.

There are $3m$ auxiliary edges, so this construction and the subroutine run in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

4. The projection is weakly stable

Lemma 2. The projected allocation $x$ is weakly stable.

Proof. Suppose an original edge $e=(i,j)$ blocks $x$. Each endpoint then has positive mass unmatched or assigned to counterparts strictly worse than the other endpoint.

At either endpoint, $Y_e$ is preferred to every auxiliary copy of an original edge strictly worse than $e$. This follows directly from the preference construction: the first two layers respect the original class order, and the final layer is below every $Y$ copy.

Therefore both endpoints desire additional allocation on $Y_e$.

Also,
$$ f_{Y_e}\leq x_e<u_e. $$
The strict inequality holds because $x_e=u_e$ would fill a minimum-mass endpoint entirely on $e$, which would protect the original edge.

Thus $Y_e$ has unused capacity and both endpoints desire it. This contradicts auxiliary stability. $\square$

5. Proof of the $2/3$ guarantee

Fix a rational optimal original allocation $y$, whose existence follows from Lemma 1.

For the proof only, multiply all masses and all entries of $f,x,y$ by a common denominator. They are now integers. Scaling preserves stability and every ratio under consideration. The following unit copies are mathematical bookkeeping; the algorithm does not construct them.

5.1 Alternating decomposition

For each original edge $e$, cancel $\min(x_e,y_e)$ common units.

Represent the remaining units of $y$ by red edges and the remaining units of $x$ by blue edges. Label each blue unit by an auxiliary layer from which it originates. Such labels can be assigned because
$$ x_e=f_{X_e}+f_{Y_e}+f_{Z_e}. $$

At each vertex, pair red and blue incident units until one color is exhausted. These pairings partition the units into alternating paths and cycles.

An unpaired red endpoint at a vertex $v$ implies
$$ d_y(v)>d_x(v). $$
Since $d_y(v)\leq c_v$, it follows that
$$ d_x(v)<c_v. $$

Let $C$ be the canceled mass, and let $R,B$ be the total remaining red and blue masses. Then
$$ |y|=C+R,\qquad |x|=C+B. $$

An alternating cycle has equal red and blue counts. A path with different-colored ends also has equal counts. A blue-ended path has fewer red edges than blue edges.

A red-ended path has $b+1$ red edges and $b$ blue edges. If every such path has $b\geq2$, then
$$ b+1\leq\frac32b. $$
Summing over components gives
$$ R\leq\frac32B, $$
and therefore
$$ |y|=C+R\leq C+\frac32B \leq\frac32(C+B)=\frac32|x|. $$

It remains to exclude red-ended paths containing zero or one blue edge.

5.2 A single red edge is impossible

Such a path consists of one original edge $(i,j)$ whose endpoints satisfy
$$ d_x(i)<c_i,\qquad d_x(j)<c_j. $$
Neither endpoint could protect that edge in $x$, contradicting Lemma 2.

5.3 A red–blue–red path is impossible

After orienting the path from its $A$ endpoint, write its edges as
$$ (i,j),\qquad (h,j),\qquad (h,k), $$
where the first and last edges are red and the middle edge is blue.

Thus
$$ y_{ij}>0,\qquad x_{hj}>0,\qquad y_{hk}>0, $$
and the endpoints satisfy
$$ d_x(i)<c_i,\qquad d_x(k)<c_k. $$
Cancellation ensures that an original pair cannot carry both red and blue units. Hence $i\neq h$ and $j\neq k$.

Let $\lambda\in\{X,Y,Z\}$ label the middle blue unit, so
$$ f_{\lambda_{(h,j)}}>0. $$

Consider $Z_{(i,j)}$. Its capacity is not filled: indeed,
$$ f_{Z_{(i,j)}}\leq x_{ij}\leq d_x(i)<c_i $$
and, because $x_{hj}>0$ with $h\neq i$,
$$ f_{Z_{(i,j)}}\leq x_{ij}\leq d_x(j)-x_{hj}<c_j. $$
Thus it has unused capacity. Its endpoint $i$ is underfilled, so desires it. Auxiliary stability forces $j$ to hold no allocation worse than this edge. In particular,
$$ \lambda_{(h,j)}\succ_j Z_{(i,j)}. $$
From the $B$-side auxiliary order, this implies one of:
$$ \lambda=Z\ \text{ and }\ h\succeq_j i, \qquad \lambda=Y\ \text{ and }\ h\succ_j i. $$
The possibility $\lambda=X$ is excluded because all $X$ copies come after all $Z$ copies.

Apply the symmetric argument to $X_{(h,k)}$. Its capacity is not filled because $k$ is underfilled and $h$ has positive allocation on $(h,j)$. Stability therefore gives
$$ \lambda_{(h,j)}\succ_h X_{(h,k)}. $$
The $A$-side order implies one of:
$$ \lambda=X\ \text{ and }\ j\succeq_h k, \qquad \lambda=Y\ \text{ and }\ j\succ_h k. $$

The same layer $\lambda$ must satisfy both sets of possibilities. Their only common possibility is
$$ \lambda=Y,\qquad h\succ_j i,\qquad j\succ_h k. $$

These strict preferences make $(h,j)$ block $y$. Explicitly,
$$ \sum_{\ell\succeq_h j}y_{h\ell} \leq d_y(h)-y_{hk}<c_h $$
and
$$ \sum_{r\succeq_j h}y_{rj} \leq d_y(j)-y_{ij}<c_j. $$
This contradicts the stability of $y$.

Both short possibilities are excluded. The counting argument therefore proves
$$ |x|\geq\frac23|y|=\frac23\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. $$
Dividing out the proof’s common denominator completes the rational-mass case.

6. The construction’s guarantee is tight with size-two ties

Give all six types unit mass, with
$$ A=\{a,h,t\},\qquad B=\{b,c,d\}, $$
and preferences
$$ \begin{aligned} a &: c\sim d\succ b,\\ h &: d\succ b,\\ t &: c,\\ b &: a\sim h,\\ c &: a\succ t,\\ d &: a\sim h. \end{aligned} $$

The matching
$$ \{(a,d),(h,b),(t,c)\} $$
is stable. Its unused acceptable pairs are protected as follows: $a$ protects $(a,c)$ through indifference and $(a,b)$ through strict preference; $d$ protects $(h,d)$ through indifference. Its mass is $3$, which is optimal because it fills both populations.

In the auxiliary construction, order $c$ before $d$ in $a$’s tie, and $a$ before $h$ in the ties of $b,d$. Consider
$$ f_{Y_{(a,c)}}=f_{Y_{(h,d)}}=1, $$
with every other auxiliary allocation zero.

This auxiliary allocation is stable:

  • On either allocated original pair, the unused $X$ copy is rejected by the $B$ endpoint, and the unused $Z$ copy is rejected by the $A$ endpoint.
  • All copies of $(a,b)$ are below $a$’s assigned copy.
  • All copies of $(h,b)$ are below $h$’s assigned copy.
  • All copies of $(t,c)$ are below $c$’s assigned copy.
  • For $(a,d)$, its $X$ copy is below $d$’s assigned copy, while its $Y$ and $Z$ copies are below $a$’s assigned copy.

The projection has mass $2$, exactly
$$ \frac23\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. $$

Consequently, a $3/4$ guarantee cannot hold for every stable output of this auxiliary construction. This is a limitation of the construction, not an impossibility result for other algorithms.

7. The conditional $4/3$ lower bound survives fractional outputs

We supply the rounding argument needed to transfer finite-agent hardness.

Lemma 3. With unit masses, any rational weakly stable allocation $z$ can be converted in polynomial time into an integral weakly stable matching of cardinality at least $|z|$.

Proof. Choose a protecting endpoint for each edge by testing the rational equalities exactly. This places $z$ in a face $F$ from Lemma 1.

Each selected protecting equality is equivalent to requiring that its endpoint be saturated and that every strictly worse incident edge have allocation zero. Indeed, its protecting sum cannot exceed the endpoint’s total allocation, which cannot exceed its capacity.

Delete the forced-zero edges. Optimize over the remaining graph using a flow network with arcs
$$ s\to i,\qquad i\to j,\qquad j\to t. $$
All endpoint upper capacities are one. Give a source or sink arc lower capacity one precisely when its vertex must be saturated; other lower capacities are zero. Give surviving middle arcs capacity one.

Flows correspond exactly to points of $F$. Polynomial-time maximum flow with lower bounds returns an integral optimum. It is stable because it belongs to $F$, and its cardinality is at least $|z|$ because $z$ was feasible for $F$. $\square$

In particular,
$$ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{int}} $$
for unit masses.

A polynomial-time mass approximation with factor $\alpha$ would therefore give a finite-agent approximation with the same factor: represent each agent as a unit-mass type, run the mass algorithm, and apply Lemma 3. Acceptability and tie sizes remain unchanged.

The finite-agent inapproximability result reported by [Chiang–Pashkovich](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04510v2) consequently implies:

Conditional conclusion. Under the Unique Games Conjecture, no polynomial-time algorithm for this mass problem has a fixed approximation factor strictly smaller than $4/3$, even when every mass is one and every tie has size at most two.

This establishes a conditional lower bound, not the arbitrary-mass $4/3$ upper bound.

8. Equal masses and the remaining target

When all types have the same positive rational mass $r$, divide allocations by $r$. This produces a unit-mass instance without changing stability. Lemma 3 identifies its fractional optimum with its integral stable optimum.

Applying the finite-agent $4/3$ approximation of [Chiang–Pashkovich](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04510v2), and multiplying its matching by $r$, gives
$$ |x|\geq\frac34\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. $$
Thus the equal-mass case is settled.

For arbitrary unequal rational masses, the theorem proved here improves the established polynomial-time guarantee to $2/3$ of optimum. The requested $3/4$ guarantee remains unproved. Under the Unique Games Conjecture, a factor below $4/3$ is impossible; without that assumption, the question of a strictly smaller factor is also unresolved here.
