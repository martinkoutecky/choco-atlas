Inputs also include directed nonnegative rational contact rates \(\lambda_{tu}\), a rational seed share \(0<\kappa<1\), and rational tolerances \(0<\delta<1\) and \(0<\varepsilon<1/2\). Contacts within and between groups are unrestricted. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Result

The requested approximation is computable in polynomial time.

More precisely, there is a deterministic algorithm that returns a feasible rational allocation \(x\) satisfying
\[ F(x)\ge \frac{1}{m}(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}-\delta, \]
in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta)\).

The proof has three ingredients:

  • Each group’s influence function has diminishing returns in the seed masses, including at boundary allocations.
  • At allocations giving every type positive seed mass, influence can be evaluated to additive accuracy \(\rho\) in time polynomial in \(\log(1/\rho)\).
  • A greedy algorithm using geometrically increasing seed masses approximates each group’s objective. Averaging the resulting allocations gives the factor \(1/m\).

1. Normalization and fixed points

Write
\[ p_t=\frac{x_t}{\mu_t}, \qquad q_t=\frac{z_t}{\mu_t}, \qquad B_{ut}=\frac{\lambda_{tu}\mu_t}{\mu_u}. \]
Thus \(B\) is a nonnegative rational matrix. The normalized recurrence is
\[ q^{(0)}=p, \qquad q^{(j+1)}=T_p(q^{(j)}), \]
where
\[ (T_p(q))_u = 1-(1-p_u)\exp\!\left(-\sum_t B_{ut}q_t\right). \]

The map \(T_p\) is coordinatewise monotone, maps \([0,1]^\tau\) into itself, and satisfies \(T_p(p)\ge p\). Consequently, the iterates increase coordinatewise and converge. Continuity of \(T_p\) shows that their limit is a fixed point.

Every fixed point \(r\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfies \(r=T_p(r)\ge p\). Induction therefore gives \(q^{(j)}\le r\) for every \(j\), proving that the limit is the specified least fixed point.

Define the individual group objective
\[ f_g(x)=\frac{\sum_{t\in g}\mu_tq_t(x)}{\mu_g}. \]
Then \(F(x)=\min_g f_g(x)\), and \(0\le f_g(x)\le1\).

Uniqueness with positive seeds

Suppose \(p_t>0\) for every type. Then \(T_p\) has exactly one fixed point in \([0,1]^\tau\).

To prove this, let \(q,r\) be fixed points. Both are strictly positive. Each coordinate of \(T_p\) is concave on the nonnegative orthant. Hence, for \(a>1\),
\[ T_p(aq) \le aT_p(q)-(a-1)T_p(0) = aq-(a-1)p < aq. \]
If \(a=\max_t r_t/q_t>1\), then monotonicity gives
\[ r=T_p(r)\le T_p(aq)<aq, \]
contradicting equality in a coordinate attaining that maximum. Thus \(r\le q\). Interchanging \(q,r\) gives equality.

This uniqueness result will allow convex optimization to evaluate the least fixed point.

2. Diminishing returns, including boundary allocations

We establish properties of \(f_g\) without assuming differentiability of the limiting fixed point.

Consider a multitype Poisson branching tree. A vertex of type \(u\) independently has a Poisson number of children of type \(t\), with mean \(B_{ut}\). Independently mark each vertex of type \(t\) with probability \(p_t\).

For a root of type \(u\), the probability that some vertex within depth \(j\) is marked is exactly \(q_u^{(j)}\). Indeed, depth zero gives \(p_u\), and the Poisson generating function gives
\[ \Pr(\text{no mark within depth }j+1) = (1-p_u)\exp\!\left(-\sum_tB_{ut}q_t^{(j)}\right). \]

Every finite-depth tree is finite almost surely. Conditional on such a tree having \(N_t\) vertices of type \(t\), its probability of containing a mark is
\[ H_N(p)=1-\prod_t(1-p_t)^{N_t}. \]
This polynomial is monotone. Its diagonal second derivatives are nonpositive, and its mixed second derivatives are nonpositive. Thus it is concave in each coordinate and has diminishing returns: increasing any coordinates cannot increase the marginal gain from a further increase in one coordinate.

It is also concave along every ray in a nonnegative direction. This follows because its Hessian entries are nonpositive, so the second derivative along such a ray is nonpositive.

These inequalities persist under expectation, positive weighted sums, and pointwise limits. The diagonal change of variables \(p_t=x_t/\mu_t\) also preserves them. Therefore every \(f_g\) has the following properties on
\[ \mathcal B=\{x:0\le x_t\le\mu_t\}. \]

  • Monotonicity: \(x\le y\) implies \(f_g(x)\le f_g(y)\).
  • Coordinate concavity: its restriction to each coordinate interval is concave.
  • Diminishing returns: if \(x\le y\) and both increments are feasible, then

\[ f_g(x+a e_t)-f_g(x) \ge f_g(y+a e_t)-f_g(y). \]

  • Ray concavity: for \(0\le c\le1\),

\[ f_g(cx)\ge c f_g(x). \]

For the last property, use \(f_g(0)=0\), which follows directly from the specified least-fixed-point iteration.

These statements remain valid when the limiting influence function is discontinuous at a boundary allocation. No continuity at zero is being assumed.

3. Polynomial-time evaluation at positive allocations

We next prove the numerical result needed by the algorithm.

Evaluation lemma. Given a rational allocation \(x\) with \(x_t>0\) for every type, and rational \(0<a<1\), every \(f_g(x)\) can be approximated to absolute error at most \(a\) in time polynomial in the encoding length of \(x\), \(\tau,L,\log(1/a)\).

3.1 A convex set of subsolutions

Types with \(p_t=1\) have \(q_t=1\); eliminate these coordinates. Let \(I\) be the remaining types and \(n=|I|\). If \(n=0\), evaluation is immediate.

For \(u\in I\), put
\[ c_u=\sum_{t\notin I}B_{ut}. \]
The reduced map is
\[ \widetilde T_u(v) = 1-(1-p_u)\exp\!\left(-c_u-\sum_{t\in I}B_{ut}v_t\right). \]
It is monotone and coordinatewise concave, and \(\widetilde T(0)\ge p>0\). The uniqueness proof from Section 1 applies to this map as well. Write its unique fixed point as \(q^*\).

Choose rational \(0<h\le1/4\), and define
\[ K_h= \left\{ v\in[0,1-h]^n: H_u(v)\le0\text{ for every }u\in I \right\}, \]
where
\[ H_u(v) = -\log(1-v_u)+\log(1-p_u)-c_u-\sum_{t\in I}B_{ut}v_t. \]
Each \(H_u\) is convex, so \(K_h\) is compact and convex.

The logarithmic inequality is equivalent to
\[ H_u(v)\le0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_u\le\widetilde T_u(v). \]
Thus every \(v\in K_h\) is a subsolution. Iterating \(\widetilde T\) from \(v\) gives an increasing sequence converging to its unique fixed point. Consequently,
\[ v\le q^* \qquad\text{for every }v\in K_h. \]

Conversely, concavity gives
\[ \widetilde T((1-h)q^*) \ge (1-h)\widetilde T(q^*)+h\widetilde T(0) \ge (1-h)q^*. \]
Therefore \((1-h)q^*\in K_h\). In particular,
\[ \sum_uq_u^*-nh \le \max_{v\in K_h}\sum_uv_u \le \sum_uq_u^*. \]

3.2 Explicit interior and separation bounds

Let
\[ p_{\min}=\min_{u\in I}p_u, \qquad v^0=p/2, \qquad G=3+\max_{u\in I}\sum_{t\in I}B_{ut}. \]
For each \(u\),
\[ H_u(v^0) \le \log\!\left(\frac{1-p_u}{1-p_u/2}\right) \le -\frac{p_u}{2}. \]
The last inequality follows from \(\log(1-s)\le-s\), applied to \(s=p_u/(2-p_u)\).

Set
\[ r= \min\left\{ \frac18,\frac{p_{\min}}4,\frac{p_{\min}}{4G} \right\}. \]
The Euclidean ball of radius \(r\) around \(v^0\) lies in the box \([0,1-h]^n\). Within this ball, every coordinate is at most \(5/8\), and
\[ \|\nabla H_u(v)\|_2 \le \frac{1}{1-v_u}+\sum_tB_{ut} \le G. \]
Hence \(H_u(v)\le-p_{\min}/4\) throughout the ball. We have an explicit inscribed ball, while \(K_h\) lies in a ball of radius \(n+1\).

There is also a polynomial-time weak separation oracle. Here are the precision details.

Given a rational point \(y\) and a distance tolerance \(\xi>0\), first separate any violation of the box constraints. Otherwise approximate each \(H_u(y)\) to error at most
\[ b=\frac{\xi p_{\min}}{8n}. \]
If an approximation exceeds \(b\), then \(H_u(y)>0\). Convexity yields a separating hyperplane with rational normal
\[ \nabla H_u(y) = \frac{e_u}{1-y_u}-(B_{ut})_{t\in I}. \]
This normal is nonzero because \(H_u\) is negative at \(v^0\).

If no approximation exceeds \(b\), all \(H_u(y)\le2b\). Define
\[ \theta=\frac{2b}{2b+p_{\min}/2}. \]
Convexity shows that \((1-\theta)y+\theta v^0\in K_h\), and its distance from \(y\) is at most
\[ \theta\sqrt n \le \frac{4b\sqrt n}{p_{\min}} \le\xi. \]
Thus the oracle either separates \(y\) or correctly certifies distance at most \(\xi\).

The logarithms used here are polynomial-time computable to the required precision. For example, scale a positive rational into \([1,2]\) by a power of two, then use
\[ \log s = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{((s-1)/(s+1))^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. \]
On \([1,2]\), the absolute value of the series parameter is at most \(1/3\), giving geometric convergence. The same series computes \(\log2\).

3.3 Recovering the fixed point

We use the standard weak-separation-to-weak-optimization theorem: for a bounded convex body with a known inscribed ball, a polynomial-time weak separation oracle permits linear optimization to additive accuracy \(\xi\), returning a rational point within distance \(\xi\) of the body, in polynomial time in the dimension, encoding lengths, logarithmic radius bounds, and \(\log(1/\xi)\). This is the convex-body optimization theorem of [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, Theorem 3.1](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf).

All its hypotheses have been checked above.

Apply it to the objective \(\sum_uv_u\), taking
\[ h=\frac{a}{4n}, \qquad \xi=\frac{a}{8n}. \]
It returns a rational \(v\) satisfying
\[ \operatorname{dist}(v,K_h)\le\xi, \qquad \sum_uv_u\ge\max_{s\in K_h}\sum_us_u-\xi. \]
Choose \(s\in K_h\) with \(\|v-s\|_2\le\xi\). Since \(s\le q^*\),
\[ 0\le\sum_u(q_u^*-s_u) \le nh+(\sqrt n+1)\xi. \]
For every coordinate,
\[ |v_u-q_u^*| \le nh+(\sqrt n+2)\xi <a. \]
Restoring the eliminated coordinates, and optionally clipping to \([0,1]\), gives rational approximations to all \(q_t\) with error at most \(a\). The group weights \(\mu_t/\mu_g\) sum to one, so the same error bound holds for every \(f_g(x)\).

Finally, all logarithmic bounds are polynomial in the rational input lengths: \(p_{\min}\), \(r\), \(h\), and the positive gaps \(1-p_u\) are rational numbers of polynomial encoding length. This proves the evaluation lemma.

4. Geometric greedy optimization for one group

Fix a group, abbreviate its objective by \(f\), and put
\[ M=\sup\left\{ f(y):y\in\mathcal B,\ \sum_ty_t=\kappa \right\}. \]
We will compute a feasible rational allocation satisfying
\[ f(x)\ge(1-1/e-\varepsilon/2)M-\delta. \]

Set
\[ \eta=\varepsilon/4, \qquad x_t^{(0)}=\eta\kappa\mu_t. \]
The initial budget is \(\eta\kappa\), and every type has strictly positive seed mass.

At a current allocation \(x\), let the remaining budget be
\[ R=\kappa-\sum_tx_t. \]
If \(R>0\), for every coordinate with \(x_t<\mu_t\), consider the increment
\[ d_t=\min\{\eta x_t,\mu_t-x_t,R\} \]
and its gain per unit budget
\[ D_t=\frac{f(x+d_te_t)-f(x)}{d_t}. \]

Let \(d_{\min}\) be the minimum positive candidate increment. Evaluate the relevant function values to absolute error
\[ a_{\mathrm{eval}}=\frac{\delta d_{\min}}{4\kappa}. \]
Each estimated ratio then has error at most \(\delta/(2\kappa)\). Choosing a coordinate with maximum estimated ratio guarantees that its true ratio satisfies
\[ D_t\ge\max_sD_s-\frac{\delta}{\kappa}. \]
Allocate \(d_t\) to that coordinate and repeat until the budget is exactly \(\kappa\).

All evaluation points retain positive seed mass in every type, so Section 3 applies.

4.1 The comparison inequality

At any iteration, define
\[ w_t=\min\{(1+\eta)x_t,\mu_t\}, \qquad a_t=w_t-x_t. \]
We claim that for every feasible comparison allocation \(y\),
\[ f(y)\le(1+\eta)f(x)+\kappa\max_tD_t. \tag{1} \]

First, \(w/(1+\eta)\le x\). Ray concavity and monotonicity give
\[ f(w)\le(1+\eta)f(w/(1+\eta)) \le(1+\eta)f(x). \]

Next, increase \(w\) coordinate by coordinate to \(w\vee y\). Let
\[ h_t=(y_t-w_t)_+. \]
If \(h_t>0\), then \(w_t<\mu_t\), so \(a_t=\eta x_t>0\).

Coordinate concavity orders the slopes of consecutive coordinate intervals. Diminishing returns bounds the slope of the interval ending at \(w_t\), with the other coordinates already increased, by the slope of the interval from \(x_t\) to \(x_t+a_t\). Consequently, that coordinate’s gain is at most
\[ \frac{h_t}{a_t}\bigl(f(x+a_te_t)-f(x)\bigr). \]
Summing the gains and using monotonicity yields
\[ f(y) \le f(w)+ \sum_{t:a_t>0} h_t\frac{f(x+a_te_t)-f(x)}{a_t}. \]
Because \(d_t\le a_t\), coordinate concavity gives
\[ \frac{f(x+a_te_t)-f(x)}{a_t} \le D_t. \]
Also \(\sum_th_t\le\sum_ty_t=\kappa\). These facts prove (1).

Taking the supremum over \(y\) in (1),
\[ \max_tD_t\ge\frac{M-(1+\eta)f(x)}{\kappa}. \]

4.2 Approximation guarantee

Write \(v_i=f(x^{(i)})\), and let \(d_i\) be the chosen increment. The approximate ratio selection gives
\[ v_{i+1} \ge \left(1-\frac{(1+\eta)d_i}{\kappa}\right)v_i + \frac{d_i}{\kappa}(M-\delta). \tag{2} \]
Here \(d_i\le\eta\kappa\), so all coefficients multiplying \(v_i\) are nonnegative.

Suppose first that \(M>\delta\). Iterating (2), using \(v_0\ge0\), gives
\[ v_{\mathrm{final}} \ge \frac{M-\delta}{1+\eta} \left( 1-\prod_i\left(1-\frac{(1+\eta)d_i}{\kappa}\right) \right). \]
The total added budget is \((1-\eta)\kappa\), so
\[ \prod_i\left(1-\frac{(1+\eta)d_i}{\kappa}\right) \le \exp(-(1+\eta)(1-\eta)). \]
Therefore
\[ v_{\mathrm{final}}\ge A(M-\delta), \qquad A=\frac{1-\exp(-(1+\eta)(1-\eta))}{1+\eta}. \]

Using
\[ A=\int_0^{1-\eta}e^{-(1+\eta)t}\,dt, \qquad 1-1/e=\int_0^1e^{-t}\,dt, \]
and \(1-e^{-\eta t}\le\eta t\), we obtain
\[ (1-1/e)-A \le \eta+\eta\int_0^1t\,dt \le2\eta = \varepsilon/2. \]
Since \(A\le1\),
\[ f(x^{\mathrm{final}}) \ge(1-1/e-\varepsilon/2)M-\delta. \]
If \(M\le\delta\), this same inequality follows from \(f\ge0\). Thus it holds in all cases.

4.3 Number of steps and rational encoding length

Every nonfinal step either:

  • multiplies the chosen coordinate by \(1+\eta\); or
  • fills that coordinate to its capacity.

A coordinate starts at \(\eta\kappa\mu_t\). Hence the total number of iterations is at most
\[ 1+\tau\left( 1+ \left\lceil \frac{\log(1/(\eta\kappa))}{\log(1+\eta)} \right\rceil \right). \]
This is polynomial in \(\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), since \(\log(1/\kappa)\) is bounded by the binary input length.

The allocations and candidate increments are obtained by polynomially many rational operations, involving repeated multiplication by \(1+\eta\), capacity assignments, and budget subtraction. Their encoding lengths remain polynomial. In particular, every positive \(d_{\min}\) satisfies
\[ \log(1/d_{\min})\le\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L,1/\varepsilon). \]
Thus
\[ \log(1/a_{\mathrm{eval}}) \le \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L,1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta). \]
The evaluation lemma therefore makes the entire single-group algorithm polynomial-time with the required precision dependence.

5. Combining the groups

Run the preceding algorithm for each group \(g\), obtaining a feasible rational allocation \(x^{[g]}\). Define
\[ M_g=\sup\left\{ f_g(y):y\in\mathcal B,\ \sum_ty_t=\kappa \right\}. \]
The guarantee is
\[ f_g(x^{[g]}) \ge (1-1/e-\varepsilon/2)M_g-\delta. \]
Because \(f_g(y)\ge F(y)\) for every feasible \(y\),
\[ M_g\ge\mathrm{OPT}. \]
This argument uses suprema and does not require an optimum to be attained.

Return the average
\[ \bar x=\frac1m\sum_gx^{[g]}. \]
It is rational and feasible. For each group \(g\), coordinatewise nonnegativity gives \(\bar x\ge x^{[g]}/m\). Monotonicity and ray concavity therefore imply
\[ f_g(\bar x) \ge f_g(x^{[g]}/m) \ge \frac1m f_g(x^{[g]}). \]
Consequently,
\[ F(\bar x) \ge \frac{1-1/e-\varepsilon/2}{m}\mathrm{OPT} -\frac{\delta}{m} \ge \frac{1-1/e-\varepsilon}{m}\mathrm{OPT}-\delta. \]

There are \(m\) single-group runs, each with polynomially many evaluations of polynomial precision. Averaging adds only polynomial rational arithmetic. The overall running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta)\), as required.
