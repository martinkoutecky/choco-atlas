Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This remains true when all input masses are dyadic rationals and the input Plurality lottery has exactly two tied winners.

Every yes-instance has a rational witness supported on at most \(1+\binom m2\) rankings, with encoding length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Nevertheless, a polynomial-time algorithm that finds a witness or correctly reports nonexistence exists if and only if \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof below gives both the certificate bound and a polynomial-time reduction from Boolean satisfiability.

1. Polynomial-size certificates

Label the candidates and retain one pairwise coordinate for each unordered pair. Put \(q=1+\binom m2\). For a ranking \(r\), let \(v(r)\in\{0,1\}^{q}\) contain a normalization coordinate equal to \(1\), followed by its pairwise indicators.

Write \(B\) for the matrix whose columns are the vectors \(v(r)\), over all rankings. The distributions pairwise equivalent to \(\mu\) form the nonempty compact polytope

\(\mathcal F=\{x\in\mathbb R^{m!}:x\geq0,\ Bx=B\mu\}\).

The omitted reverse pairwise coordinates are determined by normalization, since every strict ranking orders exactly one of \(a,b\) above the other.

For a candidate \(a\), write \(p_a(x)\) for its first-place mass. Let \(W\) be the Plurality winner set of \(\mu\), and fix \(w_0\in W\).

A distribution \(x\in\mathcal F\) changes the winner set if and only if at least one of the following holds:

  • For some \(w\in W\setminus\{w_0\}\), \(p_w(x)\neq p_{w_0}(x)\).
  • For some \(b\notin W\), \(p_b(x)\geq p_{w_0}(x)\).

Indeed, unequal scores of two original winners prevent both from remaining winners. For the second condition, either \(w_0\) ceases to be a winner, or \(b\) becomes a winner. Conversely, if neither condition holds, all original winners tie and every other candidate has strictly smaller mass.

Thus existence can be checked using the maxima and minima of \(p_w-p_{w_0}\), and the maxima of \(p_b-p_{w_0}\), over \(\mathcal F\). These are linear objectives. Whenever one of the conditions is attainable, it is attainable at a vertex of \(\mathcal F\).

At such a vertex, the columns indexed by its positive coordinates must be linearly independent. Otherwise a nonzero dependence permits sufficiently small positive and negative perturbations of those coordinates, contradicting extremality. Consequently, its support has size at most \(q\).

To bound its rational encoding length, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators in the input masses. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and every entry of \(B\mu\) has denominator dividing \(D\). For a vertex with support size \(k\leq q\), select a nonsingular \(k\)-by-\(k\) submatrix of its support columns. Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound show that each weight has binary encoding length

\(O(L+q\log q)\).

Hence a witness has total encoding length

\(O\!\left(q\bigl(m\log m+L+q\log q\bigr)\right)\).

Its rankings, normalization, pairwise probabilities and winner set can all be checked exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. A Boolean ranking encoding

We use the explicit Boolean-quadric face construction from [Maksimenko’s theorem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.06170), stated here in the coordinates needed below.

For a positive integer \(N\), introduce candidates \(o_i,e_i\), for \(1\leq i\leq N\). Given \(b\in\{0,1\}^N\), define \(r(b)\) by concatenating:

  • the candidates \(o_i\) with \(b_i=1\), in decreasing index order;
  • the pairs \(e_i,o_i\) with \(b_i=0\), in decreasing index order;
  • the candidates \(e_i\) with \(b_i=1\), in decreasing index order.

Their pairwise vectors are exactly the vertices of a face of the linear-ordering polytope. Writing \(y_{ac}\) for the indicator that \(a\) precedes \(c\), their coordinates satisfy

\(y_{o_i e_i}=b_i\),

and, for \(i<j\),

\(y_{e_i o_j}=0,\qquad y_{e_i e_j}=(1-b_i)b_j,\)

\(y_{o_i o_j}=b_i(1-b_j),\qquad y_{o_i e_j}=b_i+b_j-b_ib_j.\)

This is the construction in that theorem for \(N\) Boolean variables and \(2N\) ordered elements; our positive integer \(N\) and strict-ranking coordinates satisfy its hypotheses.

Two consequences will be used.

First, pairwise equivalence preserves this encoding. Suppose a society is supported on the rankings \(r(b)\). Its pairwise vector lies in the stated face. Every pairwise-equivalent society is also supported there: a supporting linear functional attains its face value at the common average, so every positive-mass ranking must attain that value individually.

Thus every ranking in a pairwise-equivalent society also equals \(r(b)\) for some Boolean vector.

Second, pairwise equivalence determines all first and second Boolean moments. The displayed identities give

\(\mathbb E[b_i]=\mathbb E[y_{o_i e_i}],\qquad \mathbb E[b_ib_j]=\mathbb E[b_j]-\mathbb E[y_{e_i e_j}]\quad(i<j).\)

Conversely, these moments determine every pairwise coordinate of a distribution on the encoded rankings.

Finally, if at least one bit is \(1\), the first candidate of \(r(b)\) is \(o_i\) for the greatest index \(i\) with \(b_i=1\).

3. Enforcing Boolean gates through moments

The encoding lets us enforce Boolean gate relations using pairwise probabilities.

For Boolean inputs \(s,t\) and output \(v\), define

\(Q_{\mathrm{AND}}(s,t,v)=st-2sv-2tv+3v.\)

Its values are:

| Inputs \((s,t)\) | \(v=0\) | \(v=1\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| \((0,0)\) | \(0\) | \(3\) |
| \((0,1)\) | \(0\) | \(1\) |
| \((1,0)\) | \(0\) | \(1\) |
| \((1,1)\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |

Therefore it is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when \(v=st\).

Likewise,

\(Q_{\mathrm{NOT}}(s,v)=(s+v-1)^2=1-s-v+2sv\)

is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when \(v=1-s\).

Both expectations depend only on normalization and first and second moments. Consequently, if the input society satisfies a specified collection of AND and NOT gates in every support ranking, every pairwise-equivalent society satisfies the same gates in every positive-mass ranking. The expectations of their nonnegative penalties remain zero, forcing each penalty to vanish individually.

The same argument enforces a constant bit \(w=1\) through \(\mathbb E[w]=1\), and mutual exclusion of two bits \(u,z\) through \(\mathbb E[uz]=0\).

4. The satisfiability reduction

4.1. Formula and gate variables

Reduce from satisfiability of a CNF formula whose clauses have at most three literals, an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem; see, for example, the [Cook–Levin lecture notes](https://zoo.cs.yale.edu/classes/cs468/previous-years/spr16/lectures/Cook.pdf).

Given such a formula \(\Phi\), append seven fresh variables and seven unit clauses requiring those variables to be true. Call the resulting formula \(\Psi\).

Let \(n\) be its number of input variables and \(s\) its number of clauses. Then:

  • \(\Psi\) is satisfiable exactly when \(\Phi\) is satisfiable;
  • every assignment of Hamming weight at most \(6\) violates \(\Psi\).

Introduce Boolean variables consisting of:

  • the input variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) and their complements;
  • a bypass bit \(z\) and its complement;
  • intermediate AND-gate variables computing each clause’s failure indicator \(t_j(x)\);
  • failure bits \(h_j=(1-z)t_j(x)\), for \(1\leq j\leq s\);
  • a constant bit \(w=1\);
  • a marker bit \(u\).

For a clause with at most three literals, its failure indicator is the conjunction of the falsity indicators of those literals. At most two intermediate AND gates suffice. All these relations are enforced as in Section 3. Also enforce \(uz=0\).

There are \(N\leq 2n+3s+4\) Boolean variables altogether. Assign their indices in the ranking encoding in this order:

  • all variables other than \(w,h_1,\ldots,h_s,u\);
  • \(w\);
  • all failure bits \(h_j\);
  • \(u\), with the greatest index.

Let \(a\) be the odd candidate associated with \(u\), and let \(c\) be the odd candidate associated with \(w\).

For every valid encoded ranking:

  • \(a\) is first exactly when \(u=1\);
  • \(c\) is first exactly when \(u=0\) and every failure bit is zero.

Because \(uz=0\), setting \(z=1\) forces \(u=0\), and all failure bits then vanish. If \(z=0\), all failure bits vanish exactly when \(x\) satisfies \(\Psi\). Thus, for every distribution on valid encoded rankings,

\(p_a=\mathbb E[u],\)

\(p_c=\mathbb E[z]+\Pr(u=0,\ z=0,\ x\models\Psi).\)

4.2. An explicit society of polynomial support

Let

\(\mathcal B=\{T\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}:|T|\leq6\},\qquad K=|\mathcal B|,\)

and let \(H\) be the smallest power of two with \(H\geq K\). For \(T\in\mathcal B\), write \(x^T\) for the assignment whose true variables are exactly those in \(T\).

Construct \(\mu\) from the following valid encoded rankings:

  • mass \(3/8\) on the assignment \(u=1,z=0,x=0\);
  • mass \(3/8\) on the assignment \(u=0,z=1,x=0\);
  • mass \(\lambda_T\) on each assignment \(u=0,z=0,x=x^T\), where

\(\lambda_T=\frac1{4H}\quad(T\neq\varnothing),\qquad \lambda_\varnothing=\frac{H-K+1}{4H}.\)

All gate variables receive their computed values, and \(w=1\).

Every weight is positive and dyadic, and

\(\sum_{T\in\mathcal B}\lambda_T=\frac14.\)

The support rankings are distinct because their Boolean vectors are distinct. Therefore

\(m=2N=O(n+s),\qquad \tau=K+2=O(n^6).\)

Each ranking and weight can be produced directly. The total encoding length satisfies

\(L=O\!\left((K+2)(m\log m+\log H)\right),\)

so the reduction runs in polynomial time in the formula’s encoding length.

No assignment \(x^T\) in the third group satisfies \(\Psi\). Consequently,

\(p_a(\mu)=p_c(\mu)=\frac38.\)

All other candidates together receive first-place mass \(1/4\). Thus the original Plurality winner set is exactly \(\{a,c\}\).

4.3. Unsatisfiable formulas yield no witness

Suppose \(\Psi\) is unsatisfiable, and let \(\nu\) be any society pairwise equivalent to \(\mu\).

Section 2 forces every support ranking of \(\nu\) to be Boolean encoded. Section 3 forces all gate relations, \(w=1\), and \(uz=0\). Moreover,

\(\mathbb E_\nu[u]=\mathbb E_\nu[z]=\frac38.\)

Unsatisfiability makes the additional probability in the formula for \(p_c\) zero. Hence

\(p_a(\nu)=p_c(\nu)=\frac38.\)

All other candidates together have first-place mass \(1/4\), so none can tie these two candidates. Every pairwise-equivalent society therefore has the same Plurality lottery as \(\mu\).

This proves the no-direction of the reduction.

4.4. Satisfiable formulas yield an explicit witness

Suppose \(\Psi\) is satisfiable. Let \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) be the set of true input variables in a satisfying assignment. The seven appended unit clauses imply \(|S|\geq7\).

Fix \(u=z=0\) and compute all other bits from \(x\). Each computed bit is a multilinear polynomial in \(x\) of degree at most \(3\): clause failure indicators involve at most three literals, and the bypass multiplier is now the constant \(1\).

Let \(V(x)\) be the augmented pairwise vector of the resulting ranking, including its normalization coordinate. By the formulas in Section 2, every coordinate of \(V(x)\) is a multilinear polynomial of degree at most \(6\). Replacing repeated powers using \(x_i^2=x_i\) does not increase the degree.

We now establish an affine interpolation identity.

For any multilinear polynomial \(P\) of degree at most \(6\), write

\(P(x)=\sum_{|U|\leq6}\alpha_U\prod_{i\in U}x_i.\)

Evaluating on subset assignments gives \(P(x^T)=\sum_{U\subseteq T}\alpha_U\). Inclusion–exclusion therefore yields

\(\alpha_U=\sum_{T\subseteq U}(-1)^{|U|-|T|}P(x^T).\)

Indeed, after substituting the evaluation formula, the coefficient of \(\alpha_J\) is \(0\) unless \(J=U\), when it is \(1\).

Consequently,

\(P(x^S)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal B}c_TP(x^T),\)

where

\(c_T=\sum_{\substack{T\subseteq U\subseteq S\\|U|\leq6}}(-1)^{|U|-|T|}.\)

An empty sum is zero. These coefficients are integers, and \(|c_T|\leq K\leq H\), since each sum contains at most \(K\) terms. Applying the identity to the constant polynomial gives \(\sum_Tc_T=1\). Applying it coordinatewise to \(V\) gives

\(V(x^S)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal B}c_TV(x^T).\)

Set

\(\varepsilon=\frac1{8H^2}.\)

Keep the two calibration rankings at mass \(3/8\). Replace each active weight \(\lambda_T\) by \(\lambda_T-\varepsilon c_T\), and add mass \(\varepsilon\) on the valid encoded ranking for \(u=z=0,x=x^S\).

All resulting weights are positive, because

\(\lambda_T-\varepsilon c_T\geq\frac1{4H}-\frac{H}{8H^2}=\frac1{8H}.\)

The identity \(\sum_Tc_T=1\) preserves total mass. The vector interpolation identity preserves every pairwise probability. Thus this construction produces a rational society \(\nu\) with

\(A_{\mathrm{WT}}\nu=A_{\mathrm{WT}}\mu.\)

Every original active ranking still violates \(\Psi\), whereas the newly added ranking places \(c\) first. Hence

\(p_a(\nu)=\frac38,\qquad p_c(\nu)=\frac38+\varepsilon.\)

All other candidates together receive mass \(1/4-\varepsilon\). Therefore \(c\) is the unique Plurality winner, and its lottery differs from the original uniform lottery over \(\{a,c\}\).

This proves the yes-direction. All input and constructed witness masses in this reduction are dyadic.

5. Complexity and witness construction

Sections 1 and 4 prove \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

The polynomial certificate bound also gives the exact relationship between decision and construction:

  • If a polynomial-time algorithm returns a witness or correctly reports nonexistence, it decides this \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem. Therefore \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
  • If \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), a witness can be constructed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Use the polynomial certificate-length bound from Section 1 and determine its bits successively: whether a prescribed prefix extends to a valid certificate is itself an \(\mathrm{NP}\) question.

Unconditionally, enumerating all rankings and solving the linear programs from Section 1 gives decision and construction in time

\(2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

Here \(m!\) is an explicitly enumerated search space, not the input support size. The linear programs have rational data; polynomial-time rational linear programming applies, as established by [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

For fixed \(m\), this is polynomial in \(\tau,L\). For \(m=1\) or \(m=2\), no witness exists: normalization determines the first-place mass in the first case, and the pairwise probabilities determine both first-place masses in the second.
