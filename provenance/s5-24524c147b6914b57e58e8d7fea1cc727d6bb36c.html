Theorem 17 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), type disutilities \(v_{t,j}<0\), budgets \(b_t<0\), and per-capita chore supplies \(s_j>0\), enumerate all competitive type-utility profiles \(u_t=v_t\cdot z_t\), together with one representative allocation \(z_t\) and price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}^m\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t z_{t,j}=s_j\) and each type's budget-constrained utility maximization.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. All input numbers are rational. Let \(T\) be a finite type set, with \(\mu_t\geq0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), \(v_{t,j}<0\), \(b_t<0\), and \(s_j>0\).
A competitive type equilibrium is \(z\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^{T\times[m]}\) and \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}^m\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tz_{t,j}=s_j\) for every \(j\), and \(v_t\cdot z_t=\max\{v_t\cdot x:x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^m,\ p\cdot x\leq b_t\}\) for every \(t\).
Its type-utility profile is \(u=(v_t\cdot z_t)_{t\in T}\). Enumerate all distinct such profiles, with one witnessing pair \((z,p)\) per profile.

Target.
With \(|T|\) and \(m\) unbounded, is exact enumeration of all competitive type-utility profiles solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

Nearest known results.
For finite-agent divisible chores with strictly negative additive values and budgets, all competitive utility profiles can be enumerated in strongly polynomial time when the number of agents or chores is fixed.
A nearby finite-agent variant that additionally forbids assigning a chore whose disutility reaches a specified threshold is strongly NP-hard; this is not the problem above.
For standard finite-agent CEEI with linear chore disutilities, an FPTAS for approximate equilibrium is known.

This output-size obstruction does not establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, and I do not settle whether output-polynomial enumeration is possible.

I also prove that every instance has finitely many profiles, all rational, and give an exact exponential-time enumeration procedure that handles zero-mass types.

1. Individual optimality. Write \(d_{t,j}=-v_{t,j}>0\), \(B_t=-b_t>0\), and \(q_j=-p_j>0\). A type’s optimization problem becomes minimizing \(d_t\cdot x\) subject to \(x\geq0\) and \(q\cdot x\geq B_t\).

Define \(\alpha_t(q)=\min_j d_{t,j}/q_j\). Every feasible bundle satisfies

\(d_t\cdot x\geq\alpha_t(q)\,q\cdot x\geq\alpha_t(q)B_t.\)

Equality is attainable: choose a minimizing chore \(j\) and take \(x_j=B_t/q_j\), with all other coordinates zero. Consequently, a bundle is optimal exactly when

\(q\cdot x=B_t,\qquad x_j>0\Longrightarrow d_{t,j}/q_j=\alpha_t(q).\)

Indeed, both inequalities above must be equalities, and their simultaneous equality is sufficient. In particular, the utility at prices \(q\) is uniquely determined:

\(u_t=-B_t\alpha_t(q).\)

These statements also apply to types with \(\mu_t=0\).

2. A family with exponentially many profiles. Fix a positive integer \(k\). There are \(2k\) types and \(2k\) chores, partitioned into corresponding pairs. Write types as \((r,a)\) and chores as \((r,b)\), where \(r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and \(a,b\in\{1,2\}\).

Set

\(\mu_{(r,a)}=\frac1{2k},\qquad B_{(r,a)}=1,\qquad s_{(r,b)}=\frac1{2k}.\)

Within a block, the disutilities are

\(d_{(r,1),(r,1)}=d_{(r,2),(r,2)}=1,\qquad d_{(r,1),(r,2)}=d_{(r,2),(r,1)}=2.\)

Between different blocks, set every disutility equal to \(4\). Thus all original valuations and budgets are strictly negative, and all supplies are strictly positive.

For each block, independently choose one of the following three states. Each allocation row records only that type’s two coordinates within its own block; all allocations outside its block are zero.

| State | Prices \((q_1,q_2)\) | Allocation \(z_1\) | Allocation \(z_2\) | Disutilities \((d_1\cdot z_1,d_2\cdot z_2)\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | \((1,1)\) | \((1,0)\) | \((0,1)\) | \((1,1)\) |
| B | \((4/3,2/3)\) | \((3/4,0)\) | \((1/4,1)\) | \((3/4,3/2)\) |
| C | \((2/3,4/3)\) | \((1,1/4)\) | \((0,3/4)\) | \((3/2,3/4)\) |

Here is a complete check of equilibrium.

First, each allocation column in the table sums to \(1\). Therefore its population-weighted sum is \(1/(2k)\), exactly the corresponding supply.

Second, every allocation row earns its required amount \(B_t=1\). In state B, for example,

\((4/3)(3/4)=1,\qquad (4/3)(1/4)+(2/3)(1)=1.\)

State A is immediate, and state C exchanges the two types and chores of state B.

Third, the within-block ratios \(d_{t,j}/q_j\) are:

| State | Type \(1\) ratios | Type \(2\) ratios |
|---|---|---|
| A | \((1,2)\) | \((2,1)\) |
| B | \((3/4,3)\) | \((3/2,3/2)\) |
| C | \((3/2,3/2)\) | \((3,3/4)\) |

Every positive allocation is on a minimizing ratio. Moreover, every price in every block is at most \(4/3\). Thus, for a chore outside a type’s block,

\(d_{t,j}/q_j\geq 4/(4/3)=3.\)

Each type’s minimum within-block ratio is at most \(3/2\). Hence no outside chore improves its minimum ratio. The optimality characterization in part 1 now proves that every assembled allocation is an equilibrium, with original prices \(p=-q\).

Finally, the three utility pairs are

\((-1,-1),\qquad(-3/4,-3/2),\qquad(-3/2,-3/4).\)

They are distinct. Two different choices of block states differ in the utility coordinates of at least one block. Therefore the instance has at least \(\boldsymbol{3^k}\) distinct competitive type-utility profiles.

The dense rational input has bit length \(L_k=O(k^2)\): its valuation matrix contains \(4k^2\) constant-size entries, and the masses and supplies require \(O(k\log k)\) additional bits. Explicitly listing the profiles requires at least \(3^k\) output records. For every fixed exponent \(C\), this eventually exceeds \(L_k^C\).

Thus total running time polynomial in input length is impossible, independently of any complexity-theoretic assumption. This conclusion already holds with equal positive type masses, equal budgets, equal supplies, and disutilities drawn from \(\{1,2,4\}\).

3. Finiteness and exact enumeration. Let \(I=\{t:\mu_t>0\}\) and \(n=|I|\). Because the masses sum to \(1\), \(I\) is nonempty.

I prove that there are at most \(2^{nm}\) distinct profiles and that all can be enumerated with rational witnesses in time \(2^{nm}\operatorname{poly}(L)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. The support-and-flow approach also appears in [Brânzei and Sandomirskiy’s work on competitive chore division](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01766); the argument here includes the weighted-type and zero-mass details.

For an equilibrium, form its bipartite support graph \(G\) on vertices \(I\cup[m]\), with edge \(tj\) exactly when \(z_{t,j}>0\). This graph has no isolated vertices:

  • Every positive-mass type earns \(B_t>0\), so its bundle has a positive coordinate.
  • Every chore has positive supply, so some positive-mass type receives a positive amount of it.

Set \(\beta_t=1/\alpha_t(q)>0\). Optimality gives

\(q_j=d_{t,j}\beta_t\quad\text{on every edge of }G,\)

and

\(q_j\leq d_{t,j}\beta_t\quad\text{for every }t\in I,\ j\in[m].\)

Fix a connected component \(C\) of \(G\). Its edge equalities determine all its prices and \(\beta\)-values up to one positive common factor. To see this explicitly, assign one chore a provisional price \(1\). Across an edge, compute \(\beta_t=q_j/d_{t,j}\), or \(q_j=d_{t,j}\beta_t\), depending on which endpoint is already assigned. Connectivity reaches every vertex. Any remaining edge either confirms these assignments or proves inconsistency.

For a consistent component, write the resulting positive rational values as \(w_j\) and \(h_t\). Every positive solution of the edge equalities has the form

\(q_j=\rho_Cw_j,\qquad\beta_t=\rho_Ch_t.\)

The scale is uniquely fixed by market clearing and budget exhaustion:

\(\sum_{j\in C\cap[m]}s_jq_j=\sum_{t\in C\cap I}\mu_tB_t.\)

Indeed, only types in this component consume its chores, so summing their weighted expenditures proves the equality. Therefore

\(\displaystyle \rho_C=\frac{\sum_{t\in C\cap I}\mu_tB_t}{\sum_{j\in C\cap[m]}s_jw_j}.\)

Both numerator and denominator are strictly positive. Thus each support graph determines at most one equilibrium price vector. Prices determine every type’s utility, including zero-mass types, by part 1. Since there are at most \(2^{nm}\) support graphs, the asserted profile bound follows.

The same reasoning gives an algorithm. Enumerate all such graphs, discarding graphs with isolated vertices or inconsistent edge equalities. For each remaining graph:

  • Compute its uniquely determined candidate \(q\) and \(\beta\).
  • Check every inequality \(q_j\leq d_{t,j}\beta_t\) for \(t\in I\).
  • Test for nonnegative payments \(f_{t,j}\), supported on the graph, satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_j f_{t,j}=\mu_tB_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in I}f_{t,j}=s_jq_j.\)

The last step is a rational maximum-flow problem. Use source-to-type capacities \(\mu_tB_t\), chore-to-sink capacities \(s_jq_j\), and capacity \(M=\sum_{t\in I}\mu_tB_t\) on every graph edge directed from type to chore. The two sides have equal total capacity \(M\), by the component scaling equations. A flow of value \(M\) is therefore equivalent to the required payment matrix.

For a successful candidate, define

\(z_{t,j}=f_{t,j}/(\mu_tq_j)\qquad(t\in I).\)

The flow equations give market clearing and individual budget exhaustion. Edge equalities and the checked inequalities ensure that every positive allocation minimizes \(d_{t,j}/q_j\), proving individual optimality.

For each zero-mass type, choose a minimizing chore \(j\), assign it \(B_t/q_j\), and assign zero elsewhere. This is optimal and does not affect market clearing.

Every genuine equilibrium’s support is considered, so the procedure finds every profile. Duplicate profiles can be removed by exact rational comparison.

Finally, all computed numbers have polynomial bit length. Propagation uses products and quotients along paths with at most \(n+m-1\) edges, and scaling uses sums of polynomially many such rationals. Exact maximum flow with rational capacities runs in polynomial bit complexity and supplies rational payments of polynomial bit length. Consequently, each graph requires polynomial time, and every output has a rational witness of polynomial size.

4. What remains unsettled. The proved output-size bound rules out polynomial total time. It does not rule out time polynomial in the combined input and output lengths, nor polynomial delay between successive profiles.

No reduction establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for the stated unrestricted model has been proved here. In particular, neither the large number of profiles nor hardness of the threshold-forbidden variant supplies such a reduction. The requested hardness classification therefore remains incomplete.
