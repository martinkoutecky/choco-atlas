The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\) summing to \(1\), and rational costs ordered by type as \(c_{t1}\le\cdots\le c_{tk}\), compute a feasible mass assignment \(x_{tj}\) that lexicographically minimizes the descending multiset of assigned costs, and separately of regrets \(d_{tj}=c_{tj}-\min_\ell c_{t\ell}\). Prove or refute that filling interventions \(k,k-1,\ldots,1\) with the lowest-cost remaining mass, splitting at a cutoff when needed, returns these optima.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=k\ge1\) interventions with positive binary-rational capacities \(q_j\) satisfying \(\sum_jq_j=1\). Costs are binary rationals satisfying \(0\le c_{t1}\le\cdots\le c_{tk}\le1\); define regrets \(d_{tj}=c_{tj}-c_{t1}\).
Assume the entries of \(C\) are pairwise distinct and the entries \(d_{tj}\) with \(j\ge2\) are pairwise distinct; the unavoidable equalities \(d_{t1}=0\) are permitted.
A feasible assignment consists of divisible masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) with \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{tj}=q_j\). Each portion incurs its assigned cost or regret, rather than a type's average outcome.
For either array \(a=C\) or \(a=D\), list its distinct values as \(v_1>\cdots>v_s\). The descending mass profile is \(h_a(x)=(\sum_{t,j:a_{tj}=v_r}x_{tj})_{r=1}^s\); lexicographically minimize this vector, separately for costs and regrets.
The proposed greedy procedure visits \(j=k,k-1,\ldots,1\), filling capacity \(q_j\) from remaining types in increasing order of \(a_{tj}\), splitting a type's remaining mass at the cutoff; all mass left for intervention \(1\) is assigned there.

Is computing an optimal assignment for each objective polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total binary input length? Does the specified greedy procedure always return an optimum for each objective?

For finite unit-demand agents with row-sorted costs and the stated absence of relevant ties, lowest-cost-first filling from the last intervention backward computes both objectives in \(O(nk)\) arithmetic operations. Result

Both objectives are polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). The specified greedy procedure always returns the unique optimal assignment for each objective. The two objectives may have different optimal assignments.

The proof below directly handles divisible masses. The corresponding unit-demand rule appears in Proposition 2 and Algorithm 1 of [Nguyen, Das, and Garnett (2021)](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/16707/16707-13-20201-1-2-20210518.pdf).

1. Properties of the two arrays

Fix either \(a=C\) or \(a=D\), and write \(h_a\) for its descending mass profile.

Row sorting and pairwise distinctness of the entries of \(C\) imply

\(c_{t1}<c_{t2}<\cdots<c_{tk}\).

Subtracting \(c_{t1}\) throughout row \(t\) therefore gives

\(0=d_{t1}<d_{t2}<\cdots<d_{tk}\).

Consequently, either chosen array satisfies:

  • Every row is strictly increasing.
  • Within each column \(j\ge2\), all entries are distinct.

The equal regrets in column \(1\) will not cause a problem.

An optimal assignment exists. Indeed, the feasible set

\(P=\{x\ge0:\ \sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t,\ \sum_tx_{tj}=q_j\}\)

is nonempty because \(x_{tj}=\mu_tq_j\) is feasible. It is closed and bounded, since \(0\le x_{tj}\le\mu_t\), and hence compact.

Each coordinate of \(h_a\) is a continuous linear function. Minimize its first coordinate over \(P\), then its second over the minimizers of the first, and continue through all coordinates. At each step, the minimizer set is nonempty and compact. Any assignment in the final set is lexicographically optimal: a lexicographically smaller profile would contradict the minimum at its first differing coordinate.

2. The greedy cutoff property

Let \(g\) be the greedy assignment for \(a\). Before filling column \(j\), define the remaining mass of type \(t\) by

\(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{\ell>j}g_{t\ell}\).

Its total is \(\sum_t r_t=\sum_{\ell\le j}q_\ell\), so enough mass remains to fill column \(j\). Thus the procedure produces a feasible assignment.

For \(j\ge2\), order the types with positive remaining mass by their distinct values \(a_{tj}\). Greedy filling assigns all remaining mass of every type preceding the cutoff, possibly part of the cutoff type, and no mass of any type following it.

It follows that, for distinct types \(t,u\),

\(g_{tj}>0\ \text{and}\ g_{uj}<r_u\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_{tj}<a_{uj}.\)

To justify this implication, \(g_{tj}>0\) places \(t\) at or before the cutoff, while \(g_{uj}<r_u\) places \(u\) at or after it. Since the types are distinct, they cannot both be the cutoff type, so \(t\) precedes \(u\) strictly.

3. Every assignment differing from greedy can be improved

Let \(x\) be a feasible assignment with \(x\ne g\). Choose the largest column index \(j\) at which they differ. Hence

\(x_{t\ell}=g_{t\ell}\quad\text{for all }t\text{ and }\ell>j.\)

Necessarily \(j\ge2\): if every column above \(1\) agrees, the row-sum constraints force column \(1\) to agree as well.

Both assignments fill column \(j\) to the same capacity. Since their column vectors differ, there are distinct types \(t,u\) such that

\(x_{tj}<g_{tj},\qquad x_{uj}>g_{uj}.\)

Because the higher columns agree, both assignments have the same remaining masses \(r_t\) before column \(j\). In particular, \(x_{uj}\le r_u\). Therefore

\(g_{tj}>0,\qquad g_{uj}<r_u,\)

and the cutoff property gives \(a_{tj}<a_{uj}\).

Also, some mass of type \(t\) lies in a column \(i<j\), since

\(\sum_{i<j}x_{ti}=r_t-x_{tj}\ge g_{tj}-x_{tj}>0.\)

Choose such an \(i\), and set

\(\varepsilon=\min\{g_{tj}-x_{tj},\ x_{uj}-g_{uj},\ x_{ti}\}>0.\)

Define \(y\) by changing only four entries:

\[ \]
\(y_{tj}=x_{tj}+\varepsilon,\qquad y_{uj}=x_{uj}-\varepsilon,\)

\(y_{ti}=x_{ti}-\varepsilon,\qquad y_{ui}=x_{ui}+\varepsilon.\)

Both decreased entries remain nonnegative: \(\varepsilon\le x_{ti}\) and \(\varepsilon\le x_{uj}-g_{uj}\le x_{uj}\). Every affected row and column receives one increase and one decrease of the same size. Thus \(y\) is feasible.

Let \(v=a_{uj}\). Strict row sorting and the cutoff inequality give

\(a_{ti}<a_{tj}<v,\qquad a_{ui}<v.\)

Hence all profile coordinates corresponding to values greater than \(v\) are unchanged. At value \(v\), the total assigned mass decreases by exactly \(\varepsilon\): the only modified entry with value \(v\) is \((u,j)\). All other changes occur at smaller values. Consequently,

\(h_a(y)<_{\mathrm{lex}}h_a(x).\)

Thus every feasible assignment different from \(g\) fails to be optimal.

Since an optimal assignment exists, it must equal \(g\). This proves optimality and uniqueness for either choice of \(a\). For \(k=1\), the row constraints already force \(x_{t1}=\mu_t\), so the conclusion also holds.

4. Polynomial bit complexity

A simple implementation sorts the \(\tau\) types by \(a_{tj}\) separately for each column \(j\ge2\), then scans that order to fill the column. Column \(1\) receives the remaining masses. This requires

\(O\!\left(m\tau\log(\tau+1)\right)\)

rational comparisons and arithmetic operations. Computing \(D\) first requires \(O(m\tau)\) additional subtractions.

To bound the size of the mass arithmetic, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and capacities. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the binary lengths of those denominators is at most \(L\).

Represent masses and capacities as integer multiples of \(1/Q\). Every greedy step uses only comparisons, minima, and subtraction. Therefore all residual masses, unfilled capacities, and output entries remain multiples of \(1/Q\). Their values lie between \(0\) and \(1\), so their integer numerators lie between \(0\) and \(Q\) and have \(O(L)\) bits.

The cost and regret entries also have \(O(L)\)-bit rational representations; taking a difference of two input rationals preserves this bound. Thus every required arithmetic operation has polynomial bit complexity, and the entire computation is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). No expansion into individual mass units is needed.
