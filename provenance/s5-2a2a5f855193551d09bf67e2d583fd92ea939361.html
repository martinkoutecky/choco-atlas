The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given ordered men’s sites \(M_1,\dots,M_p\) and women’s sites \(W_1,\dots,W_q\), each with strict rankings of acceptable opposite-bank sites and rational supplies \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to \(1/2\) on each bank, find a rational assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row and column sums bounded by supplies and no positive \(x_{ij},x_{i'j'}\) when \(i<i'\) and \(j>j'\). For each mutually acceptable pair \(i,j\) whose site edge crosses no edge in the support of \(x\), define \(A_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(B_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_h x_{hj}+\sum_{h:\,i\succ_j h}x_{hj}\), the masses unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred site; require \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\). Does every such instance admit a construction algorithm running in time polynomial in support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here a type specifies its bank, ordered site, and strict preference list. There are ordered sites \(M_1,\ldots,M_p\) and \(W_1,\ldots,W_q\), with \(m=p+q=\tau\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Supplies satisfy \(\mu_i,\nu_j>0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1/2\). Preference lists may be empty; discard nonmutual entries, and rank every acceptable site above being unmatched. Write \(j\succ_i k\) for \(M_i\)'s preference and \(i\succ_j h\) for \(W_j\)'s preference.
An assignment has rational entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\mu_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\nu_j\). Mass may split among partners.
Edges \((i,j)\) and \((h,k)\) cross exactly when \((i-h)(j-k)<0\); shared endpoints do not count as crossings. Require the support \(\{(i,j):x_{ij}>0\}\) to be noncrossing.
Define \(A_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_kx_{ik}+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(B_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_hx_{hj}+\sum_{h:\,i\succ_j h}x_{hj}\).
Call the assignment weakly stable if every acceptable pair whose edge crosses no support edge satisfies \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\), including pairs with \(x_{ij}>0\).

Does every instance admit such an assignment? Is finding one, or reporting nonexistence, solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For discrete agents with strict incomplete lists, a weakly stable noncrossing matching always exists and can be found in \(O(n^2)\) time for \(n\) agents per bank, or \(O(n_1n_2)\) for unequal banks ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.02185)).
The discrete maximum-cardinality variant is solvable in \(O(n^4)\) time ([source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-021-00832-9)). No result settling this exact rational-mass formulation was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required zero assignment on unacceptable pairs and discarded nonmutual preference entries, following the discrete acceptability convention.
  • Specified that every acceptable partner is preferred to being unmatched and allowed empty preference lists.
  • Identified types by bank, ordered site, and preferences; retained only positive-mass sites and set m=p+q=tau.
  • Specified that crossing means strict order inversion, so shared endpoints do not cross.
  • Applied the stated mass stability condition even to pairs carrying positive mass, since both sites can still have dissatisfied residual mass.
  • Separated the unresolved existence question from construction complexity and allowed reporting nonexistence without assuming that the discrete existence guarantee transfers.

1. Scaling and the stability condition

Let $E$$ be the set of mutually acceptable pairs. For an acceptable partner, write $\succeq_i$ and $\succeq_j$ for the reflexive closures of the strict preferences.

Choose
$$ Q=2\prod_{\ell} d_\ell, $$
where the $d_\ell$ are the denominators of the input masses. Define
$$ C_i=Q\mu_i,\qquad D_j=Q\nu_j,\qquad N=\sum_i C_i=\sum_jD_j=Q/2. $$
These are integers, and $Q$ has $O(L)$ bits.

For a feasible integer assignment $y$, define
$$ a_{ij}(y)=C_i-\sum_{k:\,k\succeq_i j}y_{ik}, \qquad b_{ij}(y)=D_j-\sum_{h:\,h\succeq_j i}y_{hj}. $$
Equivalently,
$$ a_{ij}(y) = \left(C_i-\sum_k y_{ik}\right) +\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}y_{ik}, $$
with the analogous identity for $b_{ij}$. Consequently, both quantities are nonnegative, and
$$ A_{ij}(y/Q)=a_{ij}(y)/Q,\qquad B_{ij}(y/Q)=b_{ij}(y)/Q. $$

Call an acceptable pair eligible if its edge crosses no support edge and
$$ a_{ij}(y)>0,\qquad b_{ij}(y)>0. $$
Absence of eligible pairs is exactly the required stability condition, including its application to positive assignment entries.

2. An integral transfer algorithm

Start with $y=0$. While an eligible pair exists:

  • Choose the smallest row index $i$ having an eligible partner. Choose its most preferred eligible partner $j$.
  • If row $i$ has unused capacity, use one unit of it. Otherwise remove one unit from its least preferred positive partner $k$.
  • If column $j$ has unused capacity, use one unit of it. Otherwise remove one unit from its least preferred positive partner $h$.
  • Add one unit to $y_{ij}$.

When row $i$ is full, $a_{ij}>0$ guarantees positive mass at a partner strictly worse than $j$. Thus the row donor satisfies $j\succ_i k$. Similarly, a column donor satisfies $i\succ_j h$.

Neither donor is the target edge, and the two donors are distinct. Removing the row donor leaves column $j$ unchanged, so the column operation is well defined.

Each update preserves nonnegativity and capacity constraints. It preserves noncrossing because the target was compatible with the entire previous support, and the other changes only remove mass.

The termination proof depends on the following direction lemma.

3. Direction lemma

Lemma. Every row transfer from $(i,k)$ to $(i,j)$ satisfies $j<k$. Every column replacement of $(h,j)$ by $(i,j)$ satisfies $i>h$.

3.1. Persistent southwest obstructions

Suppose that step $s$ is the first violation of either assertion. All preceding transfers therefore replace a disappearing donor edge by an edge with weakly larger row coordinate and weakly smaller column coordinate.

For a prospective target $(i,j)$, define
$$ R_{ij}=\{(u,v):u>i,\ v<j\}. $$
Before step $s$, once the support contains an edge in $R_{ij}$, it continues to contain an edge there. Indeed, either that edge remains positive, or its replacement still belongs to $R_{ij}$.

Every edge in $R_{ij}$ crosses $(i,j)$. Thus such an earlier obstruction would contradict eligibility of $(i,j)$ at step $s$.

3.2. A first row-direction violation is impossible

Suppose step $s$ transfers mass from $(i,k)$ to $(i,j)$ with $k<j$. Necessarily $j\succ_i k$.

Let $t<s$ be the last step that introduced $(i,k)$ into the support. That edge stays positive from immediately after $t$ until immediately before $s$.

Immediately before $t$,
$$ a_{ij}\geq a_{ik}>0. $$
Since the algorithm selected $k$ instead of the more preferred $j$, either $(i,j)$ was incompatible with the support or $b_{ij}=0$.

First case: incompatibility. A crossing witness $(u,v)$ for $(i,j)$ that does not cross the eligible edge $(i,k)$ must satisfy
$$ u>i,\qquad k\leq v<j. $$
Indeed, $u<i$ would require $v>j$, also crossing $(i,k)$; when $u>i$, compatibility with $(i,k)$ requires $v\geq k$.

This witness survives step $t$. If $v>k$, neither donor operation touches it. If $v=k$, its disappearance would require column $k$ to replace $u>i$ by $i$, an earlier column-direction violation.

The surviving witness belongs to $R_{ij}$, so persistence contradicts eligibility at $s$.

Second case: $b_{ij}=0$. Column $j$ is full, with every positive partner weakly preferred to $i$. Step $t$ leaves that column unchanged: its target column is $k$, and $j$ cannot be the worst row donor because $j\succ_i k$.

Until a row voluntarily removes mass from column $j$, replacements there preserve fullness and improve its partners. Hence they preserve $b_{ij}=0$.

Consider the first voluntary departure from column $j$ before step $s$, if there is one. Write it as
$$ (u,j)\longrightarrow(u,v). $$
The still-positive edge $(i,k)$, with $k<j$, forces $u\geq i$ by noncrossing. Equality is impossible: row $i$ still holds $k$, which it prefers less than $j$, so $j$ cannot be its worst positive partner. Thus $u>i$.

Since this departure precedes the first violation, $v<j$. It creates an edge in $R_{ij}$, contradicting eventual eligibility at $s$.

If no departure occurs, $b_{ij}=0$ persists until $s$, again contradicting eligibility. This excludes a first row-direction violation.

3.3. A first column-direction violation is impossible

Suppose step $s$ replaces $(h,j)$ by $(i,j)$ with $i<h$. Necessarily $i\succ_j h$.

Let $t<s$ be the last introduction of $(h,j)$ into the support. It remains positive throughout the intervening states.

The target $(i,j)$ must be compatible immediately after step $t$. A crossing witness with row coordinate below $i$ would also cross $(h,j)$, contradicting noncrossing. Every other crossing witness lies in $R_{ij}$ and would persist until $s$.

Compatibility also held immediately before $t$:

  • the column donor shares column $j$;
  • any row donor is $(h,v)$ with $v>j$, by the earlier row-direction property, and does not cross $(i,j)$;
  • the new edge $(h,j)$ shares column $j$.

Immediately before $t$,
$$ b_{ij}\geq b_{hj}>0. $$
Because proposer $h>i$ was selected, row $i$ had no eligible partner. Compatibility and positive column slack therefore imply
$$ a_{ij}=0. $$
Row $i$ is unchanged at $t$: column $j$ cannot reject the preferred partner $i$ when accepting the less preferred $h$.

Thus, immediately after $t$, row $i$ is full and every positive partner is weakly preferred to $j$.

During the subsequent interval, the positive edge $(h,j)$ forces every positive partner $k$ of row $i$ to satisfy $k\leq j$. A rejection of row $i$ at $k<j$ would replace it by some $u>i$, using the earlier column-direction property. The new edge $(u,k)$ would belong to $R_{ij}$, contradicting eventual eligibility. A rejection at $k=j$ cannot choose $i$ as the worst partner while the strictly less preferred $h$ remains positive.

Hence row $i$ undergoes no rejections before $s$. Its own transfers preserve fullness and improve its worst partner, preserving $a_{ij}=0$. This contradicts eligibility at $s$ and proves the lemma.

4. Termination and universal existence

Define
$$ r(i,j)=i+q-j,\qquad \Phi(y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}3^{r(i,j)}y_{ij}. $$
The levels satisfy
$$ 1\leq r(i,j)\leq m-1. $$

By the direction lemma, every donor edge has strictly smaller level than the target. An update with target level $r$ removes at most two units of weight at most $3^{r-1}$ and adds one unit of weight $3^r$. Therefore
$$ \Delta\Phi\geq 3^r-2\cdot3^{r-1}=3^{r-1}\geq1. $$
Since total assigned mass is at most $N$,
$$ 0\leq\Phi(y)\leq N3^{m-1}. $$
The integral potential strictly increases, so the algorithm terminates.

At termination there is no eligible pair. Thus $x=y/Q$ is rational, feasible, noncrossing, and weakly stable.

This proof also establishes an important fact used below:

There always exists a solution with denominator $Q$ and integer numerators.

The transfer algorithm itself has only the denominator-dependent bound
$$ N3^{m-1}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

5. A combinatorial certificate for a fixed support

Fix a noncrossing set $S\subseteq E$, and require it to be the exact support.

For a positive assignment on $S$, the nonnegative-sum expression for $A_{ij}$ shows that
$$ A_{ij}(x)=0 $$
holds precisely when:

  • row $i$ is saturated; and
  • every $(i,k)\in S$ satisfies $k\succeq_i j$.

Similarly,
$$ B_{ij}(x)=0 $$
holds precisely when column $j$ is saturated and every $(h,j)\in S$ satisfies $h\succeq_j i$.

Now choose a set $U$ of sites that must be saturated. For every acceptable pair $(i,j)$ compatible with $S$, perform the following combinatorial test:
$$ \begin{aligned} &\left[ M_i\in U \ \text{and}\ k\succeq_i j\ \text{for every }(i,k)\in S \right]\\ &\qquad\text{or}\\ &\left[ W_j\in U \ \text{and}\ h\succeq_j i\ \text{for every }(h,j)\in S \right]. \end{aligned} $$

If this test passes, every feasible assignment with exact support $S$ saturating $U$ is weakly stable. Additional saturated sites cause no difficulty.

Conversely, if an assignment is weakly stable, choosing $S$ as its support and $U$ as all its saturated sites makes every test pass. This follows directly from the equivalences just proved.

The tests take polynomial time. What remains is feasibility with exact support and specified saturated sites.

6. Checking a candidate by integral circulation

For a candidate $(S,U)$, seek integers satisfying
$$ y_{ij}\geq1\quad\text{for }(i,j)\in S, $$
with entries zero elsewhere, row and column capacity bounds, and equality at every site in $U$.

Subtract the required unit from each support edge:
$$ z_{ij}=y_{ij}-1\quad\text{for }(i,j)\in S. $$
Define residual capacities
$$ c_i=C_i-\deg_S(M_i),\qquad d_j=D_j-\deg_S(W_j). $$
Reject the candidate if a residual capacity is negative.

Otherwise build a directed circulation network with vertices $s,t$, all row sites, and all column sites. Its arcs and lower–upper bounds are:

| Arc | Bounds |
|---|---|
| $s\to M_i$, with $M_i\in U$ | $[c_i,c_i]$ |
| $s\to M_i$, with $M_i\notin U$ | $[0,c_i]$ |
| $M_i\to W_j$, for $(i,j)\in S$ | $[0,N]$ |
| $W_j\to t$, with $W_j\in U$ | $[d_j,d_j]$ |
| $W_j\to t$, with $W_j\notin U$ | $[0,d_j]$ |
| $t\to s$ | $[0,N]$ |

A feasible circulation gives the desired $z$ through its row-to-column arc flows. Conservation imposes the row and column sums, and the lower bounds impose the required equalities.

Conversely, every feasible $z$ extends to a circulation by putting its row sums, column sums, and total sum on the remaining arcs. The total sum is at most $N$, so the stated upper bounds suffice.

For completeness, lower-bound circulation reduces to maximum flow as follows. For an arc $a$, write its lower and upper bounds as $\ell_a,u_a$, subtract $\ell_a$, and give the residual arc capacity $u_a-\ell_a$. At each vertex define
$$ b(v)=\sum_{a\text{ entering }v}\ell_a -\sum_{a\text{ leaving }v}\ell_a. $$
Add an auxiliary source with an arc to $v$ of capacity $b(v)$ when $b(v)>0$, and an arc from $v$ to an auxiliary sink of capacity $-b(v)$ when $b(v)<0$. A maximum flow saturating all auxiliary-source arcs exists precisely when the original circulation is feasible: conservation at $v$ then imposes residual outflow minus residual inflow equal to $b(v)$.

The standard integral maximum-flow theorem applies because every capacity is a nonnegative integer. A polynomial-time maximum-flow algorithm either rejects the candidate or returns an integral circulation.

The network has $O(m+|S|)$ arcs, and all bounds have $O(L)$ bits. Thus this candidate check takes polynomial time in $m,\tau,L$. Reconstructing $y=z+1$ gives exact support $S$, and the combinatorial tests certify stability of $y/Q$.

The restriction $y_{ij}\geq1$ might exclude some other rational assignments. It does not jeopardize the search: Section 4 guarantees at least one solution satisfying this restriction.

7. Enumerating all candidates in single-exponential time

A noncrossing support is a chain in coordinatewise order. To see this, sort its edges lexicographically. Whenever the row coordinate increases, the column coordinate cannot decrease, because that would create a crossing. Within a fixed row, the column coordinate increases by the sorting rule.

Every such chain can be embedded in a grid path from $(1,1)$ to $(p,q)$ using unit steps that increase exactly one coordinate. Connect successive chain elements by such steps and extend at both ends.

There are
$$ \binom{m-2}{p-1}\leq2^{m-2} $$
grid paths. Each path has $m-1$ cells and therefore at most $2^{m-1}$ subsets. Enumerating those subsets, and discarding subsets containing unacceptable pairs, covers every possible noncrossing support. Repeated enumeration of the same support is harmless.

For each support, enumerate the $2^m$ possible choices of $U$. The total number of candidates is at most
$$ 2^{m-2}\,2^{m-1}\,2^m = 2^{3m-3} < 8^m. $$

For every candidate, apply the combinatorial tests and the circulation check. Universal existence guarantees that some candidate succeeds. Hence finding an assignment takes
$$ 8^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time.

The returned assignment has at most $m-1$ positive entries. Each numerator is at most $N$, and its denominator is $Q$, so its encoding length is
$$ O\!\left(m(L+\log m)\right). $$

8. Why a simple batching argument does not finish the problem

One tempting improvement is to transfer the largest possible amount at each step, hoping that each edge is introduced only once. That latter property fails.

Consider three sites per bank, with scaled capacities
$$ C=(1,7,4),\qquad D=(5,2,5). $$
Divide all capacities by $24$ to obtain the required bank totals. Let every pair be acceptable, with preferences:

| Site | Preference order |
|---|---|
| $M_1$ | $W_2\succ W_1\succ W_3$ |
| $M_2$ | $W_3\succ W_1\succ W_2$ |
| $M_3$ | $W_1\succ W_3\succ W_2$ |
| $W_1$ | $M_3\succ M_1\succ M_2$ |
| $W_2$ | $M_2\succ M_1\succ M_3$ |
| $W_3$ | $M_1\succ M_3\succ M_2$ |

Use the same target-selection rule, but transfer the minimum of the available row resource and column resource, where a resource is unused capacity or the mass of the specified worst donor edge. The successive updates are:

| Step | Target | Amount | Donor edge |
|---|---|---:|---|
| $1$ | $(1,2)$ | $1$ | none |
| $2$ | $(2,3)$ | $5$ | none |
| $3$ | $(2,2)$ | $1$ | none |
| $4$ | $(2,2)$ | $1$ | $(1,2)$ |
| $5$ | $(1,1)$ | $1$ | none |
| $6$ | $(2,1)$ | $2$ | $(2,2)$ |
| $7$ | $(3,3)$ | $4$ | $(2,3)$ |
| $8$ | $(2,1)$ | $2$ | none |
| $9$ | $(2,2)$ | $2$ | none |

Here are the target-selection checks. Initially $M_1$ selects $W_2$. While $(1,2)$ is positive, $(2,1)$ is inaccessible, so $M_2$ fills $W_3$ and then $W_2$. After $W_2$ rejects $M_1$, row $1$ selects $W_1$. Row $2$ then replaces its mass at its worst partner $W_2$ by mass at $W_1$. At this point rows $1$ and $2$ are stable: $(1,2)$ is obstructed by $(2,1)$, and $W_3$ has no available capacity for row $2$.

Row $3$ cannot reach $W_1$ because of $(2,3)$, so it selects $W_3$, which prefers it to row $2$. This frees four units in row $2$. Two units fill the remaining capacity at $W_1$; the other two go to $W_2$, since $W_3$ is unavailable and $W_1$ is now full.

Thus $(2,2)$ disappears at step $6$ and returns at step $9$. This refutes the proposed once-per-edge bound. It does not establish a superpolynomial lower bound for batching.

9. The remaining complexity issue

The existence decision problem is constant on valid inputs: its answer is always yes. The substantive unresolved issue is the complexity of finding a solution.

The integral outputs above also define a total polynomially verifiable search relation: every valid instance has a polynomial-length witness, and feasibility, noncrossing, and stability can all be checked exactly in polynomial time.

This places a qualification on the meaning of an NP-hardness claim. Suppose a polynomial-time procedure decided SAT using an oracle for this total search relation, and correctness were required for every valid oracle output. A polynomial-length transcript of valid oracle answers yielding “unsatisfiable” would then be an NP certificate for unsatisfiability: simulate the procedure and verify every answer. Totality supplies such transcripts, and the assumed correctness supplies soundness. Consequently, such a hardness result would imply
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$
This observation concerns that explicit oracle-reduction notion; it is not a hardness classification.

The proved results therefore settle existence and give a denominator-independent single-exponential search algorithm. They leave open whether unrestricted search admits a polynomial-time algorithm.

3. Direction lemma

This is the central argument, and its first-violation structure is consistent.

If an edge in that region remains positive, it remains an obstruction directly. If it disappears, its replacement remains in the region. Thus the persistence assertion follows from the preceding transfer directions without assuming the lemma at the violating step.

First row-direction violation

For a putative transfer with $k<j$, the donor preference condition gives $j\succ_i k$. The inequality
$$ a_{ij}\geq a_{ik}>0 $$
immediately before the introduction of $(i,k)$ has the correct direction.

The witness survives the introduction step: when $v>k$, neither donor touches it; when $v=k$, its disappearance would itself constitute an earlier column-direction violation. Persistence then contradicts eligibility at the proposed violating step.

In the zero-slack case, $b_{ij}=0$ correctly implies that column $j$ is full and all its positive partners are weakly preferred to $i$. The introduction of $(i,k)$ leaves column $j$ unchanged.

Subsequent column replacements preserve this condition unless a row voluntarily leaves column $j$. The first such departure must have $u>i$: noncrossing excludes $u<i$, and the still-positive, less-preferred partner $k$ excludes $u=i$ as a departure from the worst positive partner. The earlier row-direction property then places the replacement in $R_{ij}$.

Both alternatives yield the stated contradiction.

$$ b_{ij}\geq b_{hj}>0 $$

Thus the direction lemma is established as written.
