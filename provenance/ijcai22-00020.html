The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an undirected community graph H=(B,F) with no isolated communities, candidates C, a common single-peaked axis \triangleright, rational masses \mu=(\mu_{b,r})_{b\in B,r\in L^{\triangleright}_{\mathrm{sp}}(C)}, and an extreme ranking r^\star\in\{r^\uparrow,r^\downarrow\}, form the complete-bipartite community blow-up. For each community b, let \nu_{b,r}(\mu)=\frac{\sum_{b':\{b,b'\}\in F}\mu_{b',r}}{\sum_{b':\{b,b'\}\in F}\sum_s\mu_{b',s}} and let U_b(r;\mu) be the paper’s tie-broken single-peaked Kemeny update for a type currently holding r. A legal move chooses (b,r) with \mu_{b,r}>0 and rational \delta\in(0,\mu_{b,r}], transfers \delta from r to U_b(r;\mu), and updates the state. Find a finite legal sequence ending in a stable state that maximizes \sum_b\mu^{\mathrm{final}}_{b,r^\star}.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H=(B,F)\) be a finite simple undirected graph without isolated vertices; communities have no internal edges, and adjacent communities interact completely. Let \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), \(m\geq 2\), with common single-peaked axis \(c_1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright c_m\); write \(S\) for its strict single-peaked rankings.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \((b,r)\in B\times S\) with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Unlisted types have zero mass; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Require \(\sum_{b,r}\mu_{b,r}=1\) and \(M_b:=\sum_r\mu_{b,r}>0\) for every community. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
At state \(\mu\), define \(\nu_{b,s}(\mu)=\frac{\sum_{b':\{b,b'\}\in F}\mu_{b',s}}{\sum_{b':\{b,b'\}\in F}M_{b'}}\). Let \(U_b(r;\mu)\) be the weighted Kemeny ranking for \(\nu_b(\mu)\), restricted to single-peaked winners, then minimizing Kendall tau distance to \(r\), with remaining ties broken lexicographically using candidate order \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\).
A legal move selects \((b,r)\) with \(\mu_{b,r}>0\) and \(U_b(r;\mu)\ne r\), and transfers any rational mass \(0<\delta\leq\mu_{b,r}\) from \((b,r)\) to \((b,U_b(r;\mu))\). Recompute updates after every move; newly appearing rankings need not belong to the initial support. A state is stable when \(U_b(r;\mu)=r\) for every positive-mass type.
Given \(r^\star\in\{c_1\succ\cdots\succ c_m,c_m\succ\cdots\succ c_1\}\), optimize \(\sum_b\mu^{\mathrm{final}}_{b,r^\star}\) over finite legal sequences ending in a stable state, including the empty sequence.

Is finding a maximizing finite legal sequence solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,|B|,|F|,L\), or is this optimization problem NP-hard?

For finite individual-agent networks, a maximizing Kemeny update sequence ending in a stable state can be computed in \(O(n^3m^4)\) time, where \(n\) is the number of agents ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.14094)). No result settling this exact rational-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one while allowing unequal positive community masses; the graded question left normalization and community nonemptiness unspecified.
  • Interpreted the community blow-up as having no internal community edges and complete interaction exactly between adjacent communities of a simple graph.
  • Specified strict rankings and at least two candidates so that the two extreme rankings are distinct.
  • Fixed remaining update ties lexicographically by candidate indices, a choice explicitly permitted in the full version.
  • Excluded moves that leave the ranking unchanged, consistently with counting actual opinion changes in the discrete process.
  • Specified stability for every positive-mass type, allowed rankings outside the initial support to appear, and allowed the empty sequence.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. A maximizing finite legal sequence can be constructed using at most \(2\tau\binom{m}{2}\) moves. Every transfer can use the mass of one initially listed type.

The proof works directly with the support and binary-rational masses. It extends the greedy approach underlying the [cited finite-agent result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.14094), with a separate procedure for stabilizing all pairwise comparisons.

1. Encoding single-peaked rankings by order ideals

Let \(P=\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq m\}\), ordered by
\[ \]
\( (a,b)\leq_P(i,j)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\leq i\text{ and }b\leq j. \)

For a ranking \(r\), define its inversion set by
\( I(r)=\{(i,j)\in P:c_j\succ_r c_i\}. \)

An order ideal is a subset \(I\subseteq P\) such that \(q\in I\) and \(p\leq_P q\) imply \(p\in I\).

Lemma 1. The map \(r\mapsto I(r)\) is a bijection between the strict single-peaked rankings on the given axis and the order ideals of \(P\). Moreover,
\( d_{\mathrm{Kt}}(r,s)=|I(r)\mathbin{\triangle}I(s)|. \)

Proof. Recall that a strict ranking is single-peaked precisely when no axis triple \(a<b<c\) has both \(c_a\) and \(c_c\) ranked above \(c_b\).

Suppose \(r\) is single-peaked and \((i,j)\in I(r)\), so \(c_j\succ_r c_i\).

If \(a<i\), then \(c_j\succ_r c_a\): otherwise \(c_a\succ_r c_j\succ_r c_i\), giving a forbidden valley at \(i\). If also \(a<b<j\), then \(c_b\succ_r c_a\): otherwise \(c_j\succ_r c_a\succ_r c_b\), giving a forbidden valley at \(b\). Consequently, every \((a,b)\leq_P(i,j)\) belongs to \(I(r)\).

Conversely, let \(I\) be an order ideal. Orient each pair by declaring \(c_j\succ c_i\) exactly when \((i,j)\in I\). This tournament has no directed triangle. Indeed, for \(i<j<k\), its two possible cyclic orientations would require either

  • \((j,k)\in I\) and \((i,k)\notin I\), or
  • \((i,k)\in I\) and \((i,j)\notin I\).

Both violate downward closure. Absence of directed triangles implies transitivity, so these comparisons define a unique strict ranking.

That ranking has no valley: \(c_i\succ c_j\) and \(c_k\succ c_j\), for \(i<j<k\), would mean \((i,j)\notin I\) and \((j,k)\in I\), again violating downward closure. Thus the ranking is single-peaked.

Finally, Kendall distance counts exactly the pairwise comparisons on which two rankings disagree. ∎

Unions and intersections of order ideals are order ideals.

2. The Kemeny update acts coordinate by coordinate

Write \(N(b)=\{b':\{b,b'\}\in F\}\) and
\( D_b=\sum_{b'\in N(b)}M_{b'}. \)

Community masses are invariant under moves. Because there are no isolated vertices and every community has positive mass, \(D_b>0\).

For \(p\in P\), let
\( t_{b,p}=\frac{1}{D_b}\sum_{b'\in N(b)}\sum_{s:p\in I(s)}\mu_{b',s}. \)

Thus \(t_{b,p}\) is the neighborhood mass fraction having inversion \(p\). Define
\( A_b=\{p:t_{b,p}>\tfrac12\} \)
and
\( E_b=\{p:t_{b,p}\geq\tfrac12\}. \)

Both are order ideals. Indeed, \(p\leq_P q\) implies that every ranking containing \(q\) contains \(p\), hence \(t_{b,p}\geq t_{b,q}\).

Lemma 2. For every current single-peaked ranking \(r\),
\( I(U_b(r;\mu))=(I(r)\cup A_b)\cap E_b. \)

Equivalently, each pair follows its strict neighborhood majority and retains its current orientation on a tie.

Proof. The weighted Kemeny score of a ranking with inversion set \(J\) is
\( K_b(J)=\sum_{p\in J}(1-t_{b,p})+\sum_{p\notin J}t_{b,p}. \)

Each summand is minimized by including \(p\) when \(t_{b,p}>\tfrac12\), excluding it when \(t_{b,p}<\tfrac12\), and either choice on a tie.

The ideal \(A_b\) attains all these individual minima. By Lemma 1 it represents a single-peaked ranking, so it also attains the unrestricted Kemeny minimum. Therefore the single-peaked Kemeny winners are exactly the ideals \(J\) satisfying
\( A_b\subseteq J\subseteq E_b. \)

The set
\( J_r=(I(r)\cup A_b)\cap E_b \)
is an ideal satisfying these inclusions. On every tied coordinate it agrees with \(I(r)\). All winners have the same orientations on the strict-majority coordinates; therefore \(J_r\) uniquely minimizes Kendall distance to \(r\). The remaining lexicographic tie-breaker is never needed. ∎

The two target rankings correspond to single coordinates:

  • \(r^\star=c_1\succ\cdots\succ c_m\) holds exactly when \((1,2)\notin I(r)\), because \((1,2)\) is the least element of \(P\).
  • \(r^\star=c_m\succ\cdots\succ c_1\) holds exactly when \((m-1,m)\in I(r)\), because \((m-1,m)\) is the greatest element of \(P\).

Thus the objective is the mass having a specified bit value on one pair.

3. A two-phase procedure for one coordinate

Maintain one labeled cohort for every initially supported type. Cohort \(a\) has fixed community \(b_a\), fixed positive mass \(w_a\), and current ranking \(r_a\). The represented state is
\( \mu_{b,r}=\sum_{a:b_a=b,\ r_a=r}w_a. \)

Updating a cohort transfers exactly \(w_a\) to \(U_{b_a}(r_a;\mu)\). This is legal whenever its ranking changes: even if several cohorts have merged into the same type, that type has mass at least \(w_a\). The labels are only bookkeeping.

Fix a coordinate \(p\in P\) and a preferred bit \(\alpha\in\{0,1\}\). Let \(x_b\) denote the mass in community \(b\) having bit \(\alpha\) on \(p\).

By Lemma 2:

  • an opposite-bit cohort changes to \(\alpha\) exactly when \(\sum_{b'\in N(b)}x_{b'}>D_b/2\);
  • an \(\alpha\)-bit cohort changes to the opposite bit exactly when \(\sum_{b'\in N(b)}x_{b'}<D_b/2\).

Perform the following procedure, always making the full Kemeny update of the selected cohort.

  • Growth phase: repeatedly update an opposite-bit cohort whose update gives it bit \(\alpha\).
  • Removal phase: repeatedly update an \(\alpha\)-bit cohort whose update gives it the opposite bit.

Other coordinates may change during these updates.

Lemma 3. The procedure uses at most \(2\tau\) legal moves and finishes with coordinate \(p\) stable for every positive-mass type.

Proof. In the growth phase every selected cohort changes its \(p\)-bit to \(\alpha\), so no cohort is selected twice. Similarly, no cohort is selected twice during removal.

At the end of growth, every opposite-bit cohort sees preferred neighborhood mass at most \(D_b/2\). Preferred mass only decreases during removal, so these cohorts remain stable on \(p\).

A cohort changed during removal sees preferred neighborhood mass strictly below \(D_b/2\) at that move. Subsequent decreases keep it stable with its new opposite bit. When removal terminates, every remaining preferred-bit cohort sees preferred neighborhood mass at least \(D_b/2\). Thus all cohorts are stable on \(p\). ∎

4. Optimality against arbitrary rational splitting

We now prove that this procedure maximizes preferred-coordinate mass even among sequences that split mass arbitrarily.

Let \(X_b\) be preferred mass after growth, and \(Y_b\) preferred mass after removal.

Lemma 4. For every stable state reachable from the procedure’s starting state by any finite legal sequence, its preferred masses \(z_b\) satisfy \(z_b\leq Y_b\) for every community.

Proof. Growth never decreases preferred mass. Its termination implies
\( X_b<M_b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{b'\in N(b)}X_{b'}\leq D_b/2. \)
Indeed, if \(X_b<M_b\), an opposite-bit cohort remains in \(b\), and it is ineligible for growth.

First, \(X\) bounds preferred masses at every state of every legal sequence starting from the same initial state. Initially the bound holds. Suppose it holds before a move.

A move decreasing or preserving preferred mass preserves the bound. A move increasing preferred mass in \(b\) also preserves it if \(X_b=M_b\). Otherwise, the bound on neighboring communities gives
\( \sum_{b'\in N(b)}x_{b'}\leq\sum_{b'\in N(b)}X_{b'}\leq D_b/2, \)
so Lemma 2 forbids a move increasing preferred mass in \(b\). Induction establishes the bound, including for arbitrary rational transfer amounts. In particular, \(z\leq X\).

Stability further implies
\( z_b>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{b'\in N(b)}z_{b'}\geq D_b/2. \)
Otherwise every positive-mass preferred-bit type in \(b\) would change that bit.

Follow the removal phase, writing \(y\) for its current preferred-mass vector. We prove that \(z\leq y\) remains true. It holds initially because \(y=X\).

If removal updates a preferred-bit cohort in \(b\), then
\( \sum_{b'\in N(b)}y_{b'}<D_b/2. \)
The inductive bound gives
\( \sum_{b'\in N(b)}z_{b'}<D_b/2. \)
The stability implication above forces \(z_b=0\). Removing the cohort therefore preserves \(z_b\leq y_b\); other communities are unchanged.

At termination, \(y=Y\), proving \(z\leq Y\). ∎

This is a componentwise bound, stronger than a bound on total preferred mass.

5. Stabilizing the entire state

Call a coordinate stable if every positive-mass type’s update preserves that coordinate.

Lemma 5. Once a coordinate is stable, it remains stable and unchanged under all subsequent legal moves.

Proof. Any legal move preserves that coordinate in its source and destination rankings. Consequently, its mass in every community, and hence all neighborhood tallies for it, remain unchanged. The destination has the same coordinate bit as the source, which was stable under those tallies. Every other type is unchanged. Induction proves the claim. ∎

Construct the complete sequence as follows.

First process the target coordinate:

  • use \(p^\star=(1,2)\) and \(\alpha=0\) for the left extreme;
  • use \(p^\star=(m-1,m)\) and \(\alpha=1\) for the right extreme.

Then process every other coordinate using the two-phase procedure, with preferred bit \(0\), for example.

By Lemmas 3 and 5, each processed coordinate becomes stable and stays stable. After all \(\binom{m}{2}\) coordinates have been processed, every positive-mass ranking agrees with its update on every pair. By Lemma 1, it therefore equals its update: the final state is stable.

The target-coordinate masses remain equal to \(Y_b\) throughout the subsequent stabilization. These are exactly the final masses of \(r^\star\). Lemma 4 bounds the corresponding masses in every other reachable stable state by \(Y_b\). Hence the constructed sequence maximizes the stated objective.

The total number of moves is at most \(2\tau\binom{m}{2}\). If the initial state is stable, the construction makes no moves.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the initially supplied masses. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Scale each cohort mass to the integer \(W_a=Qw_a\). Since total mass is one, \(\sum_a W_a=Q\).

Maintain, for each community and coordinate, the scaled neighborhood inversion mass. All such totals, and the scaled \(D_b\), lie between \(0\) and \(Q\). Lemma 2 computes an update using exact integer comparisons with half the neighborhood mass.

The resulting inversion ideal determines its ranking in polynomial time: form its transitive pairwise comparison relation and order candidates by the number of candidates ranked above them.

There are exactly \(\tau\) tracked cohorts throughout. Each move:

  • finds an eligible cohort by scanning at most \(\tau\) cohorts;
  • computes its update using \(\binom{m}{2}\) comparisons;
  • adjusts neighborhood totals along edges incident with its community.

Together with the polynomial move bound, this gives polynomial running time in \(m,\tau,|B|,|F|,L\). Every output transfer equals an original cohort mass, so the sequence also has polynomial encoding length.

Therefore a maximum is attained by a finite legal sequence, and such a maximizing sequence is computable in polynomial time.
