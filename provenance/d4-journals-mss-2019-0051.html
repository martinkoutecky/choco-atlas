The continuous mirror question, as graded

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Fix binary-rational wealth \(x>0\) and horizon \(h>0\). The market has one riskless asset and \(m\ge1\) risky assets driven by an \(m\)-dimensional Brownian motion with its augmented natural filtration; adapted coefficients \(r,b,\sigma\) give unique strong asset-price solutions, \(\sigma\) is invertible, and the market is complete. Set \(D_s=\exp(-\int_0^s r_u\,du)\), let \(Z_s\) be the density process of its unique equivalent martingale measure, assumed a true martingale, and set \(q_s=D_sZ_s\).
Each type has utility \(U_t\) and a bounded adapted discount rate \(\beta_t\); terminal wealth has utility \(U_3\) and bounded adapted discount rate \(\beta_3\). Discount rates may have either sign. Write \(B_{i,s}=\exp(-\int_0^s\beta_{i,u}\,du)\). Every utility is strictly increasing and strictly concave, belongs to \(C^2((0,\infty))\), has nondecreasing second derivative and marginal utility tending to zero at infinity, and is extended at zero by its limit, possibly \(-\infty\).
For \(i\in T\cup\{3\}\), let \(I_i\) be inverse marginal utility, extended by zero above \(U_i'(0+)\). For every \(y>0\), assume finiteness of \(H_t(y)=\mathbb E\int_0^h q_s I_t(yq_s/B_{t,s})\,ds\) and \(H_3(y)=\mathbb E[q_hI_3(yq_h/B_{3,h})]\), absolute integrability of the corresponding discounted utilities, and differentiability of their expectations with differentiation permitted under expectation and integration.
An admissible plan is self-financing apart from its nonnegative, progressively measurable, pathwise integrable consumption; it receives no subsequent endowment and keeps wealth nonnegative almost surely throughout the horizon. Short positions and borrowing are allowed subject to that wealth condition. Only plans with finite expected utility are considered.
Define \(V_t(a)=\sup\mathbb E\int_0^h B_{t,s}U_t(c_s)\,ds\) over admissible plans starting from \(a\), and \(V_3(a)=\sup\mathbb E[B_{3,h}U_3(X_h)]\) over admissible plans starting from \(a\) with zero consumption. At zero wealth use their extended-real limiting values.
The optimization is to choose \(a_t,a_3\ge0\) and implementing plans maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tV_t(a_t)+V_3(a_3)\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t+a_3=x\); aggregate consumption and investment are weighted by the same masses.
For a finite computational input, utilities and market and discount coefficient functionals are supplied as programs in the standard computable-analysis representation, with computable nonanticipating coefficient functionals; all stated assumptions are promises. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including programs and a rational threshold \(K\). The exact threshold problem asks whether the optimal value is at least \(K\).

Is this exact threshold problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For a finite household with two consumption streams and shared terminal wealth, an optimal sharing rule and implementing plans are analytically characterized by inverse aggregate dual-budget functions; extension to more consumption streams is explicitly discussed ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1052v3)). Result

The exact threshold problem is NP-hard and undecidable, even with \(m=\tau=1\), fixed logarithmic utilities, zero utility discount rates, and constant market coefficients supplied as computable programs. Consequently, it has no polynomial-time decision algorithm under the stated representation.

The proof computes the optimal value explicitly on a restricted family and then gives reductions whose outputs satisfy every promise.

1. A restricted family satisfying the promises

Fix a computable constant \(\alpha\in[0,1/2]\). Take \(T=\{1\}\), \(\mu_1=1\), \(x=2\), \(h=1\), and \(K=0\). Set

\(U_1(z)=U_3(z)=\log z,\qquad \beta_1=\beta_3=0.\)

Use one risky asset, with initial price \(1\), and constant coefficients

\(r=b=-\alpha,\qquad \sigma=1.\)

The bank account and risky asset have the unique strong solutions

\(S^0_s=e^{-\alpha s},\qquad S_s=e^{(-\alpha-1/2)s+W_s}.\)

Thus \(D_s=e^{\alpha s}\), and the discounted risky price is

\(\widetilde S_s=D_sS_s=e^{W_s-s/2},\qquad d\widetilde S_s=\widetilde S_s\,dW_s.\)

The physical measure is an equivalent martingale measure. Completeness follows from Brownian martingale representation: in the augmented natural Brownian filtration, every integrable discounted payoff has a conditional-expectation martingale representable as a stochastic integral against \(W\). Since \(\widetilde S_s>0\), that integral can be implemented by trading \(\widetilde S\).

For uniqueness, any equivalent measure has a positive density martingale locally representable as \(dZ_s=Z_s\theta_s\,dW_s\). Girsanov’s theorem makes the drift of \(\widetilde S\) under that measure equal to \(\widetilde S_s\theta_s\). The martingale requirement forces \(\theta=0\) almost everywhere. Hence the unique density is \(Z_s=1\), a true martingale, and

\(q_s=e^{\alpha s}.\)

All coefficients are computable nonanticipating functionals: they ignore both time and the observed path.

The utilities satisfy the required conditions because

\(U'(z)=1/z,\qquad U''(z)=-1/z^2,\qquad \frac{d}{dz}U''(z)=2/z^3>0.\)

Their extension at zero is \(-\infty\), and their inverse marginal utility is \(I(y)=1/y\). Consequently,

\(H_1(y)=\int_0^1 e^{\alpha s}\frac{1}{ye^{\alpha s}}\,ds=\frac1y,\qquad H_3(y)=e^\alpha\frac{1}{ye^\alpha}=\frac1y.\)

The corresponding utility expectations are

\(\int_0^1\log I(ye^{\alpha s})\,ds=-\log y-\frac{\alpha}{2},\qquad \log I(ye^\alpha)=-\log y-\alpha.\)

They are absolutely integrable. Differentiation under integration is valid: on every compact interval of positive \(y\), the deterministic integrands and their derivatives are uniformly bounded. Thus every analytic promise holds.

2. Exact value and implementing plans

First establish the budget bound without assuming optimality.

For any admissible plan starting from \(a\), discounted self-financing gives

\(M_s:=D_sX_s+\int_0^sD_uc_u\,du=a+\int_0^s\varphi_u\,dW_u\)

for a stochastically integrable trading process \(\varphi\). Nonnegative wealth and consumption imply \(M_s\geq0\). A nonnegative local martingale is a supermartingale; explicitly, localization followed by Fatou’s lemma gives

\(\mathbb E\!\left[e^\alpha X_1+\int_0^1e^{\alpha s}c_s\,ds\right]\leq a.\)

Consumption value

For \(a>0\), the supporting-line inequality for logarithmic utility gives

\(\log c_s\leq\log(ae^{-\alpha s})+\frac{e^{\alpha s}c_s}{a}-1.\)

The inequality also holds in the extended-real sense when \(c_s=0\). Taking expectations and integrating, the budget bound yields

\(\mathbb E\int_0^1\log c_s\,ds\leq\log a-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac1a\mathbb E\int_0^1e^{\alpha s}c_s\,ds-1\leq\log a-\frac{\alpha}{2}.\)

Equality is attained by holding only the bank account and choosing

\(c_s=ae^{-\alpha s},\qquad X_s=a(1-s)e^{-\alpha s}.\)

Indeed, this nonnegative wealth satisfies \(dX_s=-\alpha X_s\,ds-c_s\,ds\), and the utility is finite. Therefore

\(V_1(a)=\log a-\frac{\alpha}{2}.\)

Terminal-wealth value

For zero consumption, the budget bound gives \(\mathbb E[e^\alpha X_1]\leq a\). Applying the supporting-line inequality at \(ae^{-\alpha}\),

\(\mathbb E\log X_1\leq\log a-\alpha+\frac{\mathbb E[e^\alpha X_1]}{a}-1\leq\log a-\alpha.\)

Holding only the bank account, with \(X_s=ae^{-\alpha s}\), attains equality. Hence

\(V_3(a)=\log a-\alpha.\)

Both value functions tend to \(-\infty\) at zero, as prescribed.

Optimal allocation

For positive allocations satisfying \(a_1+a_3=2\), the objective is

\(\log a_1+\log a_3-\frac{3\alpha}{2}.\)

Because \(a_1a_3\leq((a_1+a_3)/2)^2=1\), its maximum is attained at \(a_1=a_3=1\). Boundary allocations have value \(-\infty\). Thus the optimal value is exactly

\(\operatorname{OPT}(\alpha)=-\frac{3\alpha}{2}.\)

The two bank-account plans above implement this allocation; their aggregate wealth is \(e^{-\alpha s}(2-s)\geq0\). In particular,

\(\operatorname{OPT}(\alpha)\geq K=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha=0.\)

3. Uniformly encoding nonhalting

Given a machine \(M\) and input \(w\), count execution steps starting at \(1\), and define

\(\alpha_{M,w}=\begin{cases}2^{-s},&\text{if $M(w)\) first halts at step \(s\)},\\0,&\text{if \(M(w)\) never halts}.\end{cases}\)

This is a computable real uniformly in \(M,w\). To approximate it within \(2^{-n}\), simulate the first \(n\) steps. Return \(2^{-s}\) if halting occurs at step \(s\leq n\); otherwise return \(0\).

If halting occurs later, its step satisfies \(s\geq n+1\), so the approximation error is at most \(2^{-(n+1)}\). If it never occurs, the returned value is exact. Every approximation request therefore terminates.

A fixed wrapper containing \(M,w\) supplies the constant coefficient programs \(r=b=-\alpha_{M,w}\). Constructing this wrapper requires no determination of whether the machine halts. Its description length is polynomial in the descriptions of \(M,w\).

Every resulting instance belongs to the promised family above, and

\(\operatorname{OPT}(\alpha_{M,w})\geq0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M(w)\text{ does not halt}.\)

A threshold algorithm terminating correctly on all promised inputs would therefore decide nonhalting, contradicting undecidability of the halting problem. The threshold problem is undecidable even on this restricted family.

4. Polynomial-time reduction from satisfiability

Undecidability alone does not supply the required polynomial-time reduction, so we give one explicitly.

Given a Boolean formula \(\phi\), construct a machine \(M_\phi\) that exhaustively checks all assignments. If it finds a satisfying assignment, it loops forever. If it exhausts the assignments without finding one, it halts. Thus

\(M_\phi\text{ does not halt}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi\text{ is satisfiable}.\)

Its description consists of a fixed search routine and the embedded formula. Producing this description, the computable-real wrapper, and the remaining fixed market data takes polynomial time in the formula length. The potentially exponential search is performed inside the encoded program, not during the reduction.

The preceding value calculation gives

\(\phi\text{ is satisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{OPT}(\alpha_{M_\phi})\geq0.\)

Since Boolean satisfiability is NP-complete, this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction proving NP-hardness. Every output satisfies all promises, and \(m,\tau,x,h,K\) remain fixed.

5. Why analytic characterization does not decide the threshold

The cited paper characterizes allocation through inverse aggregate budget functions ([Theorem 4.4](https://arxiv.org/html/1402.1052v3)). Our family admits precisely such an elementary characterization: \(H_1(y)+H_3(y)=2/y\), so wealth \(2\) gives multiplier \(y=1\) and allocation \(a_1=a_3=1\).

The unresolved computational comparison is exact equality of a program-represented computable constant to zero. Here the optimal value is itself uniformly computable, yet deciding whether it reaches the threshold is undecidable. Thus the hardness persists with fixed normalized utilities and an explicitly solved allocation problem.
