The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) commodities, bid types \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), bundle vectors \(q(t)\), prices \(p_t\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\), choose accepted masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t q_j(t)x_t\le\kappa_j\) for each commodity \(j\), to maximize \(\sum_t p_t x_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge 2\) commodities. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge 1\) bid types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Type \(t\) requests a bundle \(q(t)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\) and offers a nonnegative binary rational price \(p_t\) for that entire bundle. Per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\) are positive binary rationals. Bundle coordinates need not be bounded by per-capita supplies.
An allocation chooses real accepted masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t q_j(t)x_t\le\kappa_j\) for every commodity. These are the only feasibility constraints; there are no mutually exclusive bids.
Its revenue is \(V(x)=\sum_t p_tx_t\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the maximum feasible revenue. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

For each fixed \(0<\varepsilon<\tfrac12\), is there an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) that returns a feasible allocation with revenue at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/m^{1/2-\varepsilon}\), or is achieving this approximation impossible unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{ZPP}\)?

For discrete weighted multi-set packing, polynomial-time approximation within \(m^{1/2-\varepsilon}\) is impossible unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{ZPP}\), for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0202032).
No separate approximation theorem for the distribution-over-types formulation was verified.

Conclusion

The stated problem is exactly solvable in deterministic polynomial time. Consequently, the requested approximation exists for every fixed \(0<\varepsilon<\tfrac12\), without any complexity assumption.

The decisive premise is that accepted masses \(x_t\) may be real numbers. Under the given feasibility constraints, the problem is a rational linear program.

1. Exact linear programming formulation

Define \(Q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{m\times\tau}\) by \(Q_{jt}=q_j(t)\), and let \(p\), \(\mu\), and \(\kappa\) be the corresponding input vectors. Consider the linear program

\(\displaystyle \text{maximize }p^\mathsf T x\quad\text{subject to }Qx\le\kappa,\quad 0\le x\le\mu.\)

Its variables are precisely the accepted masses. Its inequalities are precisely the feasibility constraints in the question, and its objective is precisely the revenue. Thus its optimal value equals \(\mathrm{OPT}\); no relaxation or rounding is involved.

The feasible region is nonempty because \(x=0\) is feasible. It is closed and contained in the bounded box \(\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_t]\), hence compact. The continuous linear objective therefore attains its maximum. Moreover, nonnegativity of the prices gives the finite bound

\(\displaystyle 0\le\mathrm{OPT}\le\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}p_t\mu_t.\)

2. Polynomial time and exact output

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: an explicitly represented linear program with rational coefficients can be solved in time polynomial in its binary encoding length; when its feasible region is nonempty and its optimum is finite, an exact rational optimal solution can be returned. This is the classical consequence of the ellipsoid method. See [Khachiyan, *Polynomial algorithms in linear programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=zvmmf&option_lang=eng&paperid=5239&wshow=paper).

All hypotheses hold here:

  • There are \(\tau\) variables and \(m+2\tau\) inequalities.
  • Every coefficient is an input integer or rational, or one of the constants \(0,1,-1\).
  • Writing the constraint matrix, even densely, takes polynomial space and time in \(m,\tau,L\).
  • Feasibility and boundedness were established above.

Large bundle coordinates cause no difficulty: the algorithm uses their binary representations and never expands a coordinate into individual units.

For completeness, an exact optimal allocation also has polynomial binary output length. Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input rationals. Then \(\log_2D\le L\). Multiplying the constraints by \(D\) gives integer coefficients whose absolute values are at most \(H=2^{2L}\).

A nonempty compact polytope has an optimal vertex. At a vertex, \(\tau\) linearly independent active constraints determine the coordinates. By Cramer's rule, each coordinate is a ratio of determinants of integer matrices of order \(\tau\). The determinant expansion bounds the absolute values of these determinants by \(\tau!H^\tau\). Hence each coordinate has binary encoding length \(O(\tau(L+\log\tau))\). This confirms that an exact rational allocation can be represented with polynomially many bits.

The algorithm is therefore simply to construct this linear program, solve it exactly, and return its optimal solution \(x^*\).

Since \(m\ge2\), \(1/2-\varepsilon>0\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge0\),

\(\displaystyle V(x^*)=\mathrm{OPT}\ge\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{m^{1/2-\varepsilon}}.\)

3. Why the discrete packing hardness does not apply

Discrete packing requires choosing whole bids. That restriction is absent here. Indeed, setting \(y_t=x_t/\mu_t\) transforms the given problem into

\(\displaystyle \text{maximize }\sum_t p_t\mu_t y_t,\quad \text{subject to }\sum_t q_j(t)\mu_t y_t\le\kappa_j,\quad 0\le y_t\le1.\)

Requiring \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\) would introduce a different problem. The supplied paper explicitly distinguishes binary bid selection from its linear programming relaxation in Section 3.1. [Gonen and Lehmann, *Optimal Solutions for Multi-Unit Combinatorial Auctions*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0202032).

The distinction can affect the optimum even in a small instance. Take \(m=\tau=3\), masses \(\mu_t=1/3\), supplies \(\kappa_j=1/3\), prices \(p_t=3\), and bundles

\(\displaystyle q(1)=(1,1,0),\quad q(2)=(1,0,1),\quad q(3)=(0,1,1).\)

Accepting each type at mass \(x_t=1/6\) uses exactly \(1/3\) of each commodity and earns revenue \(3/2\). Summing the three supply constraints gives \(2(x_1+x_2+x_3)\le1\), so this revenue is optimal.

If each type instead had to be accepted wholly or rejected, any two accepted types would exceed their shared commodity's supply. The optimal revenue under that additional restriction would be \(1\).

Thus the discrete hardness theorem does not settle the stated continuous problem. The exact linear programming algorithm does.
