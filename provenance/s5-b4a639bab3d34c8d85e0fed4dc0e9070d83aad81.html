The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), mass distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), symmetric compatibility weights \(w\), and an adversarial ordered arrival process \(\eta\) of total mass \(\mu\) revealed online, determine the best competitive ratio of policies maintaining individual matchings under free dissolution. Policies may leave arriving mass unmatched, match it to unmatched mass, or dissolve an existing pair and match the newcomer to one endpoint. The offline benchmark is \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,w)=\max\{\sum_{t,t'\in T}w(t,t')x_{tt'}:x=x^\top\ge0,\sum_{t'}x_{tt'}\le\mu_t\}\); determine the ratio of the threshold policy with \(\theta=1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) and whether any policy can exceed \(\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt{2}}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; there is no separate parameter \(m\), so take \(m=\tau\). Compatibility weights \(w:T\times T\to\mathbb Q\) are symmetric and may have either sign.
The society is nonatomic: each individual has one type and at most one partner; distinct individuals of the same type may pair. An arrival order is a piecewise constant measurable map \(\eta:[0,1]\to T\), with rational breakpoints and \(\lambda(\eta^{-1}(t))=\mu_t\), streamed in increasing order of its argument. Let \(K\) be its number of constant intervals and \(L\) the total binary encoding length; express any running times in \(m,\tau,K,L\).
Policies are deterministic and measurable. They observe only the arrival prefix and compatibility weights between individuals already present; the support, final type masses, future weights, and future arrival intervals are not disclosed in advance.
At arrival, each individual may remain unmatched, pair with an earlier unmatched individual, or replace one endpoint of an existing pair, leaving the displaced partner unmatched. These moves may be applied to arbitrary measurable portions of arriving mass; no move may create a pair between two earlier individuals. Dissolution removes the old pair's entire contribution, with no additional cost.
For a matching, \(x_{tt'}\) denotes the mass of type \(t\) individuals paired with type \(t'\) individuals; thus \(x_{tt}\) counts both endpoints of same-type pairs. Terminal welfare is \(V(x)=\sum_{t,t'}w(t,t')x_{tt'}\), and \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,w)=\max\{V(x):x=x^\top\ge0,\ \sum_{t'}x_{tt'}\le\mu_t\}\).
The threshold policy with \(\theta=1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\) admits replacement of a pair of weight \(v\) by a pair of weight \(u\) only when \(u\ge\theta v\). As mass arrives, it selects an eligible move of greatest positive welfare gain per unit arriving mass, leaving mass unmatched if none exists; ties use a fixed ordering of revealed endpoint types and their current partner types.
For a policy \(A\), define \(c_A=\inf_{\mu,w,\eta:\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,w)>0}V(A(\eta))/\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,w)\), and \(c^*=\sup_A c_A\).
What are the threshold policy's competitive ratio and \(c^*\)? Does \(c^*=\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt2}\), or can a deterministic policy exceed this value?
For finite individual arrivals, the threshold policy achieves \(\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt2}\), and no deterministic policy exceeds it under the same dissolution moves. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16965v2).
For fractional unweighted matching under edge arrival, a competitive-ratio upper bound of \(0.5661\) is known; that model differs in its arrivals and objective. [Source](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol374-icalp2026/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2026.128/LIPIcs.ICALP.2026.128.html). No result settling the specified mass model was located.

  • Used signed symmetric rational compatibility weights and normalized total mass to one.
  • Made the society nonatomic, allowed distinct same-type individuals to pair, and interpreted diagonal matching entries as endpoint mass.

  • Excluded zero-optimum instances from the ratio infimum and used terminal welfare rather than accumulated historical welfare.

2. Normalization and deterministic labeling

Count each pair once, and write
$$ W=\frac V2. $$
This applies to same-type pairs as well: their diagonal entry in $x$ counts both endpoints. Competitive ratios are unchanged.

Fix an integer $N\ge1$, a rational $q\in(0,1)$, and a real rounding base $\beta>1$. These are fixed parameters of a policy, independent of the input.

Give every original type $t$ the internal labels
$$ (t,j,h),\qquad 0\le j<N,\qquad h\in\{0,1\}. $$
The index $j$ specifies a lane. Pairings between different lanes are forbidden. Within a lane, pairings between distinct effective types are permitted; an effective type cannot pair with itself. In particular, individuals of one original type can pair across its two different $h$-labels.

Labels are assigned using only the cumulative arrived mass $s$ of the original type. Partition $(0,1]$ into
$$ (q^{n+1},q^n],\qquad n\ge0, $$
and divide each such interval into $2N$ consecutive equal pieces, one for each label, in a fixed order.

For every $s>0$, each label has received mass at least
$$ \frac{qs}{2N}. $$
Indeed, choose $n$ with $q^{n+1}<s\le q^n$. All the smaller intervals, of total length $q^{n+1}$, have arrived. Each label receives exactly a $1/(2N)$ share of them, and
$$ \frac{q^{n+1}}{2N}\ge\frac{qs}{2N}. $$
Consequently, every effective type has terminal mass at least
$$ \frac{q\mu_t}{2N}. $$

This assignment is measurable and uses neither the final masses nor the future arrival intervals.

3. The rounded policy and its finite-interval dynamics

For positive weights, define the lane-$j$ score
$$ R_j(u)= \beta^{\,j/N+\lfloor\log_\beta u-j/N\rfloor}. $$
Then
$$ R_j(u)\le u<\beta R_j(u). $$
Nonpositive weights are ignored.

On an arrival in lane $j$, the policy considers only that lane. It may pair with an earlier unmatched individual, or replace an existing pair when the new score is strictly greater than the old score. Among eligible moves, it maximizes the new score.

Ties are resolved by a fixed ordering of target effective types, followed by their partner types. An unmatched target has a distinguished sentinel partner, ordered before all actual partner types. Original types are numbered by their order of first revelation, so this ordering requires no advance knowledge of the support.

Every replacement increases actual welfare. If its old and new scores are $r$ and $r'$, respectively, then
$$ r'\ge\beta r, \qquad u_{\mathrm{new}}\ge r'\ge\beta r>u_{\mathrm{old}}. $$

Finite dynamics on a constant effective-type interval

Suppose effective type $c$ is arriving throughout an interval.

A newly created pair cannot be replaced again during this interval. Targeting its other endpoint offers exactly the score already on that pair; targeting its $c$-endpoint would require the forbidden effective diagonal. Thus every replacement source is a pair present at the interval’s beginning.

Order target types by decreasing new score, followed by the fixed type ordering. A replacement retaining endpoint type $a$ and displacing endpoint type $b$ cannot release an unmatched endpoint of higher target priority. Otherwise, targeting $b$ would itself be eligible and preferred: its new score would be at least the chosen score and hence strictly greater than the old pair’s score.

The interval can therefore be processed by a finite list:

  • Visit target types in priority order.
  • For each target, visit its eligible unmatched or paired sources in the fixed partner order.
  • Consume the minimum of the available source mass and remaining arriving mass.

A replacement replenishes unmatched stock only at a later target priority. No previously processed source needs to be revisited. After eligible sources are exhausted, any remaining arrival mass stays unmatched.

Every operation is a minimum followed by affine stock updates. Thus, for fixed revealed weights, the resulting trajectory is continuous in the initial stock vector and interval length. It consists of finitely many pure operation intervals, on each of which every arrival performs the same kind of move.

4. Constructing aggregate entrance trajectories

The labeling intervals accumulate at cumulative type mass zero. This section constructs aggregate trajectories there; the next section realizes them by actual individuals.

An aggregate state consists of unmatched masses and unordered pair masses for the finitely many effective types currently revealed.

At the first appearance of an original type $t$, fix the revealed state and consider a hypothetical continuation consisting entirely of type $t$, for cumulative duration $1$. For each positive integer $\ell$, define a truncated trajectory that leaves the first $q^\ell$ mass of $t$ unmatched and then follows the rounded rule.

Above $q^\ell$, there are only finitely many label boundaries. Section 3 therefore defines each truncated trajectory.

In the sum norm on stock coordinates, each trajectory has Lipschitz constant at most $3$: a replacement changes one old pair stock, one new pair stock, and one unmatched stock. All coordinates are bounded by $2$, because the hypothetical continuation adds at most one unit of mass to the initial state. The trajectories lie in a common compact metric space under uniform convergence. Compactness follows by taking a diagonal subsequence on a countable dense set of times and using the common Lipschitz bound.

Every uniform subsequential limit satisfies the rounded rule on every open constant effective-type interval at positive cumulative coordinate. To verify this, fix such an interval with left endpoint $a>0$. For sufficiently large $\ell$, the truncation ends before $a$. Its evolution on this interval is the continuous finite-operation evolution from Section 3, started at its state at $a$. Uniform convergence and continuity preserve that evolution.

These intervals cover all positive coordinates except countably many boundaries.

A deterministic measurable selection

A measurable subsequential limit can be selected without invoking an additional selection theorem.

For fixed state dimension and rational revealed-weight configuration, choose a countable dense set of centers in the common compact trajectory space. Start with all truncation indices. At stage $n$, choose the first open ball of radius $2^{-n}$ containing infinitely many of the retained trajectories, and retain those indices.

Such a ball exists because compactness supplies a finite subcover of the balls of that radius. Choose increasingly large truncation indices from the successive retained sets. The resulting subsequence is Cauchy: after stage $n$, all its remaining terms lie in one ball of radius $2^{-n}$.

For each fixed ball, the assertion that infinitely many retained indices lie in it is a countable intersection of countable unions of measurable conditions. The selection is therefore measurable in the initial stock vector. The truncated trajectories depend continuously on that vector, as shown in Section 3.

The parameter space is the countable disjoint union, over revealed support sizes and rational revealed-weight configurations, of the corresponding Euclidean stock polytopes, each with its Borel sigma-algebra. Applying the preceding construction on each component gives one deterministic Borel selection.

The actual policy uses the selected hypothetical trajectory until the original type changes, then continues from the current stock state. On a later appearance of the same original type, its cumulative coordinate is positive, so only finitely many label boundaries remain and ordinary finite-interval evolution applies.

All weights used at positive coordinates are already revealed. In particular, a positive mass of the current original type has already arrived, so its diagonal weight is revealed too. The isolated first-arrival instant can be assigned any action without changing any mass.

This construction produces a deterministic aggregate trajectory from prefix information. The remaining issue is its realization by consistent endpoint identities.

5. Measurable realization by actual individuals

Here is the repair of the gap identified in the verifier report. It constructs individual identities directly from cumulative stock flows, rather than taking limits of individual matchings.

5.1 FIFO transfers from cumulative supply and demand

The aggregate execution is covered, up to a null set, by countably many pure operation intervals from Section 3.

Create two kinds of queues:

  • For each effective type, an unmatched queue. Its supplies are arrivals left unmatched and endpoints displaced by replacements. Its demands are formations of new pairs using unmatched targets.
  • For each unordered effective-type pair, a pair queue. Its supplies are pair creations. Its demands are retirements by replacement.

For any queue, let $I(s)$ and $D(s)$ be its cumulative supplied and demanded masses. Stock feasibility gives
$$ 0\le D(s)\le I(s). $$
Both functions are absolutely continuous.

Match supply and demand by FIFO rank. A demanded rank $r$ is assigned the supply at the generalized inverse time
$$ \inf\{s:I(s)>r\}, $$
with the usual immaterial choices at exceptional ranks. The same rank parametrizes its demand time through the generalized inverse of $D$.

These maps preserve mass and are invertible between the used supply and demand portions, modulo null sets. This follows directly from cumulative ranks: rank Lebesgue measure is pushed forward to the corresponding supply or demand measure.

Crucially, every demanded item was supplied strictly earlier:

  • During an unmatched-target formation interval, that target’s unmatched queue has no inflow. The arriving effective type differs from the target, and formation displaces nobody.
  • During a replacement interval, the source pair queue has no new births. Newly created pairs cannot be replacement sources on the surrounding constant effective-type interval.

Thus a demanded rank at an interior time of such an interval was supplied no later than its left endpoint. FIFO never uses a same-time supply on a positive-mass set.

These cumulative-rank definitions start at time zero. They do not require a first positive-coordinate labeling interval.

5.2 Endpoint records and predecessor transfers

Let $C\subseteq[0,1]$ be the measurable set of arrivals creating a pair. For each creation time $s\in C$, introduce two endpoint records: its incoming endpoint and its target endpoint.

Their space is
$$ X=C\times\{0,1\}, $$
with measure $\nu$ equal to arrival mass on each copy. In particular,
$$ \nu(X)=2\lambda(C)\le2. $$
Each record has birth time $b(x)$ equal to its pair’s creation time.

FIFO assigns each retired pair to an earlier pair creation. Its two endpoint records are consequently determined.

Now define a predecessor for a new target record whenever its individual was previously paired:

  • In a replacement, its predecessor is the retained endpoint record of the retired pair.
  • In a formation using an unmatched individual supplied by displacement, its predecessor is the displaced endpoint record of that earlier retired pair.

Incoming records have no predecessor. A target drawn from an arrival that originally stayed unmatched also has no predecessor; its actual arrival identity is supplied directly by the unmatched FIFO queue.

These transfers define a measurable partial map
$$ P:D_P\longrightarrow X. $$
It is injective and preserves measure on every measurable subset of its domain. Indeed, each old endpoint can feed at most one next endpoint record. At retirement, one endpoint is retained once; the other enters an unmatched queue and is used at most once before receiving a new endpoint record. FIFO maps preserve the mass of every such transfer.

For every predecessor transfer,
$$ b(Px)<b(x). $$
For retained endpoints, FIFO assigns a strictly earlier pair birth. For displaced endpoints, the old birth precedes displacement, which strictly precedes the unmatched queue’s subsequent demand.

5.3 Infinite backward histories have measure zero

Let $S_\infty$ be the set of records having infinitely many predecessors. It is measurable, being the intersection of the sets on which the first $n$ predecessor iterations exist.

We have
$$ P(S_\infty)\subseteq S_\infty. $$
Since $P$ preserves measure and $\nu(X)<\infty$,
$$ \nu(P(S_\infty))=\nu(S_\infty). $$
Hence $P(S_\infty)$ equals $S_\infty$ modulo a null set.

Measure preservation then gives
$$ \int_{S_\infty}b(Px)\,d\nu(x) = \int_{P(S_\infty)}b(y)\,d\nu(y) = \int_{S_\infty}b(y)\,d\nu(y). $$
But $b(Px)<b(x)$ throughout $S_\infty$. If $\nu(S_\infty)>0$, the integral of the positive function $b(x)-b(Px)$ would be positive, contradicting the displayed equality. Therefore
$$ \nu(S_\infty)=0. $$

This is the step that makes the entrance accumulation harmless: almost every endpoint record reaches a concrete arrival identity after finitely many predecessor transfers.

5.4 Identity assignment and matching consistency

Assign each root record its concrete arrival identity:

  • An incoming record receives its creation-time arrival.
  • A root target record receives the earlier unmatched arrival selected by FIFO.

These root assignments preserve mass and use distinct arrival identities. Incoming roots come from $C$; unmatched-arrival roots come from its complement and are consumed at most once.

For every record with finitely many predecessors, follow them to its root and inherit that arrival identity. This assignment is measurable: partition the records by predecessor depth and use a finite composition on each part.

Injectivity of predecessor transfers means an individual’s records form a single chain. At retirement, its old record stops being active. Its next record begins either immediately as the retained endpoint, or strictly later after a period unmatched. Thus the individual cannot occupy two active pair records simultaneously.

At creation, the incoming endpoint has that creation time as its arrival identity. The target identity arrived strictly earlier, by the FIFO chronology and predecessor chronology. Therefore every created pair has an arriving endpoint and an earlier endpoint.

Pairing the two identities attached to each active pair’s records gives the required actual matching. It has the prescribed stock masses and performs exactly the aggregate formations and replacements. Discarding the null exceptional records and their countable saturation under the transfer maps and endpoint exchange changes no masses.

Finally, if $n(a)$ counts the number of pair creations involving individual $a$, mass preservation and Tonelli’s theorem give
$$ \int_0^1 n(a)\,da=2\lambda(C)\le2. $$
Thus almost every individual has only finitely many incidences.

5.5 Measurable dependence and online information

The cumulative queue functions are measurable in the selected trajectory and time. Their generalized inverses are measurable as well; inverse-time inequalities can be tested using countably many rational times. All record transfers and finite predecessor compositions are consequently measurable in the revealed state.

At a demand time, FIFO uses only preceding supply. Every predecessor lies earlier in the history. Thus the identity assigned to an action uses only the arrival prefix. The hypothetical entrance selector uses only the initial revealed state and weights, and assumes a continuation of the currently arriving type; it does not consult the actual future.

This completes the construction of an admissible deterministic measurable policy, denoted $A_{\beta,N,q}$.

6. A charging lemma for rounded scores

Fix one lane. Its positive scores belong to
$$ \{\beta^{z+k}:k\in\mathbb Z\} $$
for its fixed offset $z$.

For an individual $a$, let $p(a)$ be the largest score of an algorithmic pair ever incident to it, or zero if there was none. This maximum exists almost everywhere by Section 5.

Offline edge coverage

For almost every positive offline pair $\{a,b\}$ of score $r$,
$$ r\le p(a)+p(b). $$

To prove this, suppose $a$ arrives before $b$. If $p(a)\ge r$, the assertion is immediate.

Otherwise, consider the individuals of $a$’s effective type with final historical price exactly $p(a)$. There are finitely many effective types and realized scores. Within each such measurable class, almost every member has a positive mass of that class preceding it. Indeed, the members before the class’s first positive cumulative mass form a null set.

Hence, for almost every relevant $a$, positive mass of its price class precedes $b$. Every such individual is currently unmatched or belongs to an edge of score at most $p(a)<r$. Targeting that class is therefore eligible at $b$’s arrival. The rounded policy selects a new score at least $r$, giving $p(b)\ge r$.

A feasible type-level offline matching can be realized by splitting measurable type portions and pairing equal-mass portions with measure-preserving bijections. Since effective diagonals are forbidden, its endpoints are distinct. Integrating the coverage inequality over its pairs gives
$$ W_{\mathrm{opt},R}\le\int p(a)\,da. $$

Historical-price accounting

Let $W_{\mathrm{ret},R}$ be the total score of retired pair portions, counting each retirement.

Assign each individual’s price to its last incident edge attaining that price. A terminal edge can receive assignments from both endpoints. A retired edge can receive an assignment only from its displaced endpoint: its retained endpoint immediately receives a strictly higher score.

Therefore
$$ \int p(a)\,da \le 2W_{\mathrm{term},R}+W_{\mathrm{ret},R}. $$

Every replacement increases score by a factor of at least $\beta$. Follow the pair-replacement ancestry, splitting portions as necessary. A terminal pair of score $r$ has retired ancestor scores summing to at most
$$ \frac r\beta+\frac r{\beta^2}+\cdots = \frac r{\beta-1}. $$
An instance has finitely many scores, so these ancestries have bounded depth. Integrating gives
$$ W_{\mathrm{ret},R} \le \frac{W_{\mathrm{term},R}}{\beta-1}. $$

Combining the inequalities proves
$$ \boxed{ W_{\mathrm{term},R} \ge \frac{\beta-1}{2\beta-1}W_{\mathrm{opt},R}. } $$

7. Competitive guarantees and strict separation

Choose an optimal original type matching $x^*$ and remove its nonpositive-weight entries. Feasibility is preserved and welfare does not decrease.

For each lane $j$, construct an effective-type comparison matching. For distinct original types $t,u$, put
$$ x^{(j)}_{(t,j,h),(u,j,k)} = \frac{q}{4N}x^*_{tu}, \qquad h,k\in\{0,1\}. $$
For an original diagonal entry, put
$$ x^{(j)}_{(t,j,0),(t,j,1)} = x^{(j)}_{(t,j,1),(t,j,0)} = \frac{q}{2N}x^*_{tt}. $$

Each effective-type row uses mass
$$ \frac{q}{2N}\sum_u x^*_{tu} \le \frac{q\mu_t}{2N}, $$
so the comparison is feasible by Section 2. Its lane-$j$ rounded welfare is
$$ \frac qN V_{R_j}(x^*). $$

Actual welfare is at least rounded welfare. Applying Section 6 in each lane yields
$$ V(A_{\beta,N,q}) \ge \frac{q(\beta-1)}{N(2\beta-1)} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V_{R_j}(x^*). $$

For every $u>0$,
$$ \frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}R_j(u) \ge \frac{(\beta-1)u} {\beta N(\beta^{1/N}-1)}. $$
For verification, let $y$ be the fractional part of $N\log_\beta u$. The fractional parts of $\log_\beta u-j/N$, in some order, are
$$ \frac yN,\frac{y+1}N,\ldots,\frac{y+N-1}N. $$
Thus
$$ \begin{aligned} \frac1N\sum_j\frac{R_j(u)}u &= \frac{\beta^{-y/N}}N \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\beta^{-k/N}\\ &\ge \frac{\beta^{-1/N}}N \frac{1-\beta^{-1}}{1-\beta^{-1/N}}\\ &= \frac{\beta-1}{\beta N(\beta^{1/N}-1)}. \end{aligned} $$

Applying this entrywise to $x^*$ proves
$$ \boxed{ c_{A_{\beta,N,q}} \ge \frac{q(\beta-1)^2} {\beta(2\beta-1)N(\beta^{1/N}-1)}. } $$

Letting $N$ increase and rational $q$ approach $1$, using
$$ N(\beta^{1/N}-1)\longrightarrow\log\beta, $$
gives
$$ c^* \ge \frac{(\beta-1)^2} {\beta(2\beta-1)\log\beta}. $$
Taking $\beta=5/2$ proves
$$ c^*\ge\frac{9}{40\log(5/2)}. $$
No policy is claimed to attain this limiting guarantee exactly.

For an explicit single policy, take
$$ \beta=2,\qquad N=2,\qquad q=\frac9{10}. $$
Its guarantee is
$$ c_{A_{2,2,9/10}} \ge \frac{3(1+\sqrt2)}{40}. $$
Since
$$ \frac{1}{3+2\sqrt2}=3-2\sqrt2, $$
strict separation is equivalent to $83\sqrt2>117$. Both sides are positive, and squaring gives
$$ 13778>13689. $$
Thus a deterministic admissible policy strictly exceeds the finite-individual constant.

8. A universal upper bound

For any deterministic policy, give four types $a,b,c,d$ mass
$$ \delta=\frac14 $$
each, arriving in that order. Initially specify
$$ w(a,c)=w(b,c)=1. $$
All other weights among the first three types are zero.

After the $c$-interval, let $r_a,r_b$ be the masses of its positive pairs with $a,b$. Necessarily
$$ r_a+r_b\le\delta. $$
Choose $e\in\{a,b\}$ with $r_e\ge r_f$, where $f$ is the other type, and complete the instance with
$$ w(d,e)=1,\qquad w(d,f)=w(d,c)=w(d,d)=0. $$

This is a legitimate deterministic adversary: the two completions have identical observable prefixes through the $c$-interval.

During the $d$-interval, no additional $c$-$f$ pair can be created, because both endpoints would be earlier individuals. Their terminal pair mass is at most $r_f$. Every other positive terminal pair uses type $e$, limiting their combined pair mass to $\delta$. Hence
$$ W_{\mathrm{term}} \le \delta+r_f \le \frac32\delta. $$

Offline, pair all of $c$ with $f$ and all of $d$ with $e$, obtaining pair welfare $2\delta$. This is optimal: every positive pair uses one endpoint from $\{a,b\}$, whose total mass is $2\delta$.

Therefore every deterministic policy has competitive ratio at most $3/4$, and
$$ \boxed{c^*\le\frac34.} $$
This instance has $m=\tau=K=4$ and dyadic masses.

9. The threshold rule: a sharp conditional guarantee

Set
$$ \theta=1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}. $$
Suppose an admissible measurable execution satisfies the stated replacement threshold and greatest-positive-gain rule.

Define $p(a)$ as the largest actual weight ever incident to individual $a$, or zero. Every created pair has positive weight, and total creation mass is at most $1$. Thus almost every individual has finitely many incidences, and $p$ is well-defined almost everywhere.

For almost every positive offline pair $\{a,b\}$ of weight $u$,
$$ u\le\theta\bigl(p(a)+p(b)\bigr). $$
Suppose $a$ is earlier. If $p(a)\ge u/\theta$, this is immediate.

Otherwise, the price-class argument from Section 6 supplies positive earlier mass of $a$’s type with historical price $p(a)$. Such a target is unmatched or paired at weight $v\le p(a)<u/\theta$. It offers an eligible move of gain $u-v$.

If the selected move creates weight $z$ and retires weight $v'$, with $v'=0$ for a formation, maximal gain implies
$$ z-v'\ge u-v. $$
Since $v'\ge0$,
$$ p(b)\ge z\ge u-p(a). $$
This proves the coverage inequality.

The historical-price accounting gives
$$ W_{\mathrm{opt}} \le \theta\int p(a)\,da \le \theta\left(2W_{\mathrm{term}}+W_{\mathrm{ret}}\right). $$
Replacement ancestry grows by at least $\theta$, so
$$ W_{\mathrm{ret}} \le \frac{W_{\mathrm{term}}}{\theta-1}. $$
Consequently,
$$ W_{\mathrm{opt}} \le \theta\left(2+\frac1{\theta-1}\right)W_{\mathrm{term}} = (3+2\sqrt2)W_{\mathrm{term}}. $$

Tight rational examples

Choose rational $r>\theta$ and $0<s<\theta$. Give equal mass $\delta$ to
$$ a_0,\ldots,a_{k+1},b_0,\ldots,b_{k+1}. $$
The positive weights are
$$ w(a_i,a_{i+1})=r^i, \qquad w(a_i,b_i)=sr^i \quad(0\le i\le k), $$
and
$$ w(a_{k+1},b_{k+1})=sr^k. $$
All other weights, including diagonals, are zero.

Use the order
$$ a_0,a_1,b_0,a_2,b_1,\ldots,a_{k+1},b_k,b_{k+1}. $$
Each chain arrival replaces the previous chain pair on its entire mass because $r>\theta$. Each leaf arrival is rejected because its sole positive target remains paired at a weight whose $\theta$ multiple exceeds the leaf weight.

The terminal pair welfare is $\delta r^k$. Pairing each $a_i$ with $b_i$ is feasible offline and gives
$$ \delta s\left(\sum_{i=0}^k r^i+r^k\right). $$
Thus the ratio is at most
$$ \frac{r^k}{s\left(\sum_{i=0}^k r^i+r^k\right)}. $$
As $k$ increases, this approaches
$$ \frac{r-1}{s(2r-1)}. $$
