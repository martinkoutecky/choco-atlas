The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many weighted rooted network templates \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_{h,a}\) of rooted roles \((h,a)\), regression parameters \(k,\varepsilon\), and \(W_h=L_h+I\), choose per-agent sample intensities \(r_{h,a}\ge k\) minimizing \(S_\infty=\sum_{h,a}\mu_{h,a}r_{h,a}\), subject to \(k\sum_{b\in V_h}[(W_h^{-1})_{ab}]^2/r_{h,b}\le\varepsilon\) for every rooted role \((h,a)\), and output the optimum and an allocation attaining it.

Partial classification

I do not settle whether the general exact threshold problem is in $\mathrm{P}$ or is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard. The following results are proved below.

  • For $m\le2$, exact threshold decision is polynomial-time many-one equivalent to square-root-sum comparison. This strengthens the one-way reduction in the notes.
  • For arbitrary templates, the decision problem belongs to $\exists\mathbb R\cap\mathrm{co}\text{-}\exists\mathbb R$, and consequently to $\mathrm{PSPACE}$.
  • The optimum is attained by a unique allocation. Exact decision, and exact computation of the value and allocation using algebraic-number representations, are possible in time

$$ (\tau+1)^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(m,L,\log(k+1)). $$

  • Edgeless templates are solvable in polynomial time.

These statements concern exact comparison, including equality at $B$.

1. Normalization, attainment, and uniqueness

Enumerate all supported roles by $i=1,\ldots,\tau$. Since every vertex of every listed template is supported, all variables have positive objective coefficients, and
$$ \sum_h |V_h|=\tau. $$
Empty templates, if permitted, can be discarded.

Write
$$ P_h=W_h^{-1}, \qquad C^h_{ab}=(P_h)_{ab}^{2}, $$
and let $C$ be the block diagonal matrix with blocks $C^h$.

1.1. Properties of the influence matrices

For every nonzero real vector $z$ indexed by $V_h$,
$$ z^{\mathsf T}W_hz = \sum_a z_a^2+ \sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq V_h}v^h_{ab}(z_a-z_b)^2 >0. $$
Thus $W_h$ is positive definite and invertible. Its inverse has rational entries.

Moreover, $P_h$ is entrywise nonnegative. To prove this, take any $b\ge0$ and write $z=W_h^{-1}b$. If some coordinate of $z$ were negative, choose a coordinate $a$ attaining its minimum. Then
$$ b_a=(W_hz)_a = z_a+\sum_{c\ne a}v^h_{ac}(z_a-z_c) \le z_a<0, $$
contradicting $b\ge0$. Applying this argument to each coordinate vector $b$ proves $P_h\ge0$.

Since $W_h\mathbf1=\mathbf1$, we also have $P_h\mathbf1=\mathbf1$. Therefore
$$ \sum_b C^h_{ab} = \sum_b(P_h)_{ab}^2 \le \left(\sum_b(P_h)_{ab}\right)^2 =1. $$

1.2. Scaling and attainment

Set $r_i=kx_i$. The normalized problem is
$$ F^*= \min\left\{ \sum_i\mu_i x_i: x_i\ge1,\quad C(1/x)\le\varepsilon\mathbf1 \right\}, \qquad S_\infty^*=kF^*, $$
where $1/x$ denotes coordinatewise reciprocation.

Let
$$ R=\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}. $$
The uniform allocation $x_i=R$ is feasible because every row sum of $C$ is at most $1$. Its objective is $R$, using $\sum_i\mu_i=1$.

Every allocation whose normalized objective is at most $R$ satisfies
$$ 1\le x_i\le R/\mu_i. $$
All masses are positive. Hence the feasible set intersected with this objective sublevel is nonempty, closed, and bounded. The objective is continuous, so its minimum is attained. In particular,
$$ k\le S_\infty^*\le kR. $$

1.3. Uniqueness

Set $y_i=1/x_i$. The normalized problem becomes
$$ \min_{y\in D} f(y), \qquad f(y)=\sum_i\frac{\mu_i}{y_i}, \qquad D=\{y:0<y_i\le1,\ Cy\le\varepsilon\mathbf1\}. $$
The domain $D$ is convex. On the positive orthant,
$$ \nabla^2f(y) = \operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{2\mu_i}{y_i^3}\right) $$
is positive definite, because every $\mu_i>0$. Thus $f$ is strictly convex.

If two distinct feasible points both minimized $f$, their midpoint would be feasible and would have strictly smaller objective. This is impossible. Therefore the minimizing $y$, and consequently the minimizing allocation $r_i=k/y_i$, are unique.

2. Exact solution of every two-vertex template

Consider a template with two vertices $a,b$ and influence weight $w\ge0$. Let its masses be $u,v>0$. These are their actual society masses; they need not sum to $1$.

When $w=0$, both vertices are isolated. Their optimal normalized intensities are
$$ x_a=x_b=\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}, $$
with contribution
$$ (u+v)\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}. $$

Assume henceforth $w>0$. Direct inversion gives
$$ P= \begin{pmatrix} p&q\\ q&p \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p=\frac{1+w}{1+2w}, \qquad q=\frac{w}{1+2w}. $$
Thus $p>q>0$ and $p+q=1$. Define
$$ A=p^2,\qquad D=q^2,\qquad \rho=A+D. $$
The normalized reciprocal constraints are
$$ \frac{A}{x_a}+\frac{D}{x_b}\le\varepsilon, \qquad \frac{D}{x_a}+\frac{A}{x_b}\le\varepsilon. $$

If $\varepsilon\ge\rho$, the allocation $x_a=x_b=1$ is feasible and optimal, with contribution $u+v$.

For the remaining case, assume
$$ 0<\varepsilon<\rho. $$
Interchange the vertex labels if necessary so that $u\ge v$, and define
$$ \gamma=A+pq\sqrt{v/u}. $$

The exact solution is as follows.

| Condition | Optimal $(x_a,x_b)$ | Optimal normalized contribution |
|---|---|---|
| $uD\le vA$ | $\left(\rho/\varepsilon,\rho/\varepsilon\right)$ | $(u+v)\rho/\varepsilon$ |
| $uD>vA$ and $\varepsilon\le\gamma$ | $\left((A+pq\sqrt{v/u})/\varepsilon,(D+pq\sqrt{u/v})/\varepsilon\right)$ | $(Au+Dv+2pq\sqrt{uv})/\varepsilon$ |
| $uD>vA$ and $\varepsilon>\gamma$ | $\left(1,D/(\varepsilon-A)\right)$ | $u+vD/(\varepsilon-A)$ |

The original intensities are $r_a=kx_a$ and $r_b=kx_b$.

2.1. Proof of the formulas

Use reciprocal variables $y_a=1/x_a$ and $y_b=1/x_b$.

First, the optimizer satisfies $y_a\ge y_b$. When $u>v$, if $y_a<y_b$, swapping the coordinates preserves feasibility and decreases the objective, since
$$ \left(\frac{u}{y_a}+\frac{v}{y_b}\right) - \left(\frac{u}{y_b}+\frac{v}{y_a}\right) = (u-v)\left(\frac1{y_a}-\frac1{y_b}\right)>0. $$
When $u=v$, swapping preserves the objective, and uniqueness forces $y_a=y_b$.

For $y_a\ge y_b$, the first constraint is the stronger one, because
$$ (Ay_a+Dy_b)-(Dy_a+Ay_b) = (A-D)(y_a-y_b)\ge0. $$

At the optimizer, this first constraint is tight. Indeed, suppose
$$ Ay_a+Dy_b<\varepsilon. $$
Move along the segment from $(y_a,y_b)$ toward $(1,1)$. This preserves the coordinate bounds and ordering. The first constraint increases continuously to $\rho>\varepsilon$, so it becomes tight before reaching $(1,1)$. Both coordinates weakly increase, and at least one strictly increases. Consequently the objective strictly decreases, contradicting optimality.

Put $z=y_a$. Tightness gives
$$ y_b=\frac{\varepsilon-Az}{D}. $$
The ordering and positivity conditions become
$$ z\ge\frac{\varepsilon}{\rho}, \qquad z\le1, \qquad z<\frac{\varepsilon}{A}. $$
Conversely, these conditions ensure $0<y_b\le z\le1$, and hence feasibility for both constraints.

We therefore minimize
$$ \phi(z)=\frac{u}{z}+\frac{vD}{\varepsilon-Az} $$
on this interval. Its derivatives are
$$ \phi'(z) = -\frac{u}{z^2} + \frac{vDA}{(\varepsilon-Az)^2}, $$
$$ \phi''(z) = \frac{2u}{z^3} + \frac{2vDA^2}{(\varepsilon-Az)^3} >0. $$
The unique unconstrained critical point is
$$ z_0 = \frac{\varepsilon}{A+\sqrt{AD}\sqrt{v/u}} = \frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma}. $$
It always satisfies $z_0<\varepsilon/A$.

The position of $z_0$ relative to the remaining endpoints determines the optimizer:

  • We have

$$ z_0\le\frac{\varepsilon}{\rho} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A+pq\sqrt{v/u}\ge A+D \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad vA\ge uD. $$
Strict convexity then makes the left endpoint optimal, giving the first row of the table.

  • If $uD>vA$, then $z_0>\varepsilon/\rho$. When $\varepsilon\le\gamma$, we also have $z_0\le1$, so $z_0$ is feasible and optimal. Reciprocation gives the second row. Its cost is

$$ u\frac{A+pq\sqrt{v/u}}{\varepsilon} + v\frac{D+pq\sqrt{u/v}}{\varepsilon} = \frac{Au+Dv+2pq\sqrt{uv}}{\varepsilon}. $$

  • If $uD>vA$ and $\varepsilon>\gamma$, then $z_0>1$, so the right endpoint $z=1$ is optimal. Here $\varepsilon>\gamma>A$, making that endpoint feasible. The inequality $\varepsilon<\rho=A+D$ also implies

$$ 0<\frac{\varepsilon-A}{D}<1. $$
This gives the third row.

This proves every case, including the equality boundaries.

2.2. Selecting the cases requires only rational comparisons

All parameters except $\gamma$ are rational. Comparing $\varepsilon$ with $\gamma$ also reduces to rational arithmetic.

If $\varepsilon\le A$, then automatically $\varepsilon\le\gamma$. If $\varepsilon>A$, both sides of
$$ \varepsilon-A\le pq\sqrt{v/u} $$
are nonnegative, so squaring is reversible:
$$ \varepsilon\le\gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u(\varepsilon-A)^2\le ADv. $$

Thus the applicable formula can be selected in polynomial time using exact rational arithmetic, without approximate square-root comparisons.

3. Equivalence with square-root-sum for $m\le2$

Define the square-root-sum decision problem, abbreviated $\mathrm{SRS}$, as follows: given positive binary integers $d_1,\ldots,d_n$ and a nonnegative binary integer $K$, decide whether
$$ \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{d_i}\le K. $$
The exact comparison issue is discussed in [Cheng et al., *On the Sum of Square Roots of Polynomials and Related Problems*](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Sum20of20Square20Roots20ToCT.pdf).

3.1. Reduction from allocation decision to $\mathrm{SRS}$

Different templates have disjoint variables and constraints. Every allocation costs at least the sum of the individual template minima, and combining their individual minimizers attains this sum. Therefore their optimal contributions add exactly.

For $m\le2$, the formulas in Section 2 express the normalized optimum as
$$ F^*=R_0+\sum_{j=1}^J\sqrt{z_j}, $$
where $R_0$ is rational, every $z_j$ is a positive rational, and $J\le\tau$.

Specifically, each interior two-vertex case contributes
$$ \frac{Au+Dv}{\varepsilon} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{2pq}{\varepsilon}\right)^2uv}. $$
All other cases contribute only rational terms. Singleton templates contribute
$$ \mu_i\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}. $$

The case selection and construction of $R_0,z_1,\ldots,z_J$ take
$$ \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L,\log(k+1)) $$
bit operations. Rational additions and products preserve polynomial encoding length: the bit length of a product is at most the sum of its factors’ bit lengths, and common denominators can be formed by products.

Set
$$ t=B/k-R_0. $$
If $t<0$, the answer is no. If $J=0$, rational comparison decides the answer. In these cases, output a fixed yes or no instance of $\mathrm{SRS}$, respectively $(d_1,K)=(1,1)$ or $(1,0)$.

Otherwise, write
$$ z_j=a_j/b_j, \qquad t=c/e, $$
with positive integers $a_j,b_j,e$ and a nonnegative integer $c$. Define
$$ Q=e\prod_{j=1}^J b_j, \qquad d_j=a_jb_j(Q/b_j)^2, \qquad K=Qt. $$
All $d_j$ are positive integers, and $K$ is a nonnegative integer. Moreover,
$$ \sqrt{d_j}=Q\sqrt{z_j}. $$
Since $Q>0$,
$$ S_\infty^*\le B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{j=1}^J\sqrt{d_j}\le K. $$

The binary length of $Q$ is the sum of polynomially bounded denominator lengths. The output therefore has polynomial length and is computable in polynomial time. This proves a polynomial-time many-one reduction to $\mathrm{SRS}$.

The same computation outputs the unique allocation as polynomial-length radical expressions. This symbolic output does not itself decide their sum’s comparison with a rational threshold.

3.2. Reduction from $\mathrm{SRS}$ to allocation decision

Here is a complete reduction in the other direction, including the construction from the notes.

Take an $\mathrm{SRS}$ instance $d_1,\ldots,d_n,K$, with $n\ge1$. Set
$$ D_0=\sum_{i=1}^n d_i, \qquad N=9D_0+n, $$
and choose a power of two $T>N$.

For each $i$, introduce a separately labelled two-vertex template with edge weight $1/2$, assigning masses
$$ u_i=\frac{9d_i}{T}, \qquad v_i=\frac1T. $$
Add a singleton template with mass
$$ \frac{T-N}{T}. $$
Every mass is positive, and their sum is $1$. The template labels distinguish the roles even though the two-vertex structures are identical.

Set
$$ k=1, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac14. $$
For every pair,
$$ p=\frac34,\qquad q=\frac14, \qquad A=\frac9{16},\qquad D=\frac1{16}, \qquad \rho=\frac58. $$

If $d_i=1$, then $u_iD=v_iA$, so the first case of Section 2 applies. If $d_i>1$, then $u_iD>v_iA$, and $\varepsilon<A<\gamma$, so the interior case applies. In both cases the pair’s exact contribution is
$$ \frac{81d_i+1+18\sqrt{d_i}}{4T}. $$
For $d_i=1$, this expression equals $25/T$, matching the uniform-case contribution.

The corresponding normalized intensities, valid for every $d_i\ge1$, are
$$ x_{i,a}=\frac94+\frac1{4\sqrt{d_i}}, \qquad x_{i,b}=\frac{9\sqrt{d_i}+1}{4}. $$
Their feasibility and optimality follow from the proved cases in Section 2.

The singleton’s optimal intensity is $4$. Adding contributions gives
$$ S_\infty^* = \frac{ 16(T-N)+81D_0+n+18\sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{d_i} }{4T}. $$
Choose
$$ B= \frac{16(T-N)+81D_0+n+18K}{4T}. $$
Then
$$ S_\infty^*\le B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{d_i}\le K. $$

All masses and the threshold are dyadic, so the reduction remains valid if “binary-rational” is restricted to finite binary expansions. It has
$$ m=2,\qquad \tau=2n+1. $$
If the source input length is $\ell$, the quantities $D_0,N,T$ have $O(\ell)$ bits, and the constructed input has $O(\ell^2)$ length. The reduction is therefore polynomial-time.

Proved conclusion: exact allocation decision restricted to $m\le2$ is polynomial-time many-one equivalent to $\mathrm{SRS}$. Neither direction establishes $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

4. General existential-real formulations

4.1. Polynomial-size rational preprocessing

For a template of size $n_h$, clear the denominators of its influence weights. A common denominator obtained by multiplying their denominators has polynomial binary length. The resulting integer matrix representing $W_h$ has entries of polynomial binary length.

The determinant expansion bounds determinant and cofactor lengths by a polynomial in $n_h,L$: a determinant has at most $n_h!$ terms, each a product of $n_h$ entries. The adjugate formula therefore bounds the rational entry lengths of $W_h^{-1}$ by a polynomial in $m,L$.

The inverses can be computed using exact elimination in
$$ O\left(\sum_h n_h^3\right) \le O(\tau m^2) $$
rational arithmetic operations. Fraction-free elimination gives polynomial bit complexity. Squaring inverse entries to obtain $C$, and clearing positive denominators in the formulas below, preserves polynomial encoding length.

4.2. A formula for the threshold condition

The condition $S_\infty^*\le B$ is equivalent to the existence of real vectors $x,t$ satisfying
$$ x_i\ge1,\qquad t_i\ge0,\qquad x_it_i\ge1, $$
$$ Ct\le\varepsilon\mathbf1, \qquad k\sum_i\mu_i x_i\le B. $$

Indeed, an original feasible allocation meeting the threshold gives such a point by taking $x_i=r_i/k$ and $t_i=1/x_i$.

Conversely, these inequalities imply $t_i\ge1/x_i$. Since $C\ge0$,
$$ C(1/x)\le Ct\le\varepsilon\mathbf1. $$
Thus $r_i=kx_i$ is feasible and meets the threshold. Attainment from Section 1 ensures equivalence with the optimum comparison, including equality.

After denominator clearing, this formula has $2\tau$ variables, $4\tau+1$ inequalities, degree at most two, and polynomially bounded integer coefficient lengths. Hence the decision problem belongs to $\exists\mathbb R$.

4.3. Polynomial optimality conditions

There is also a polynomial-size existential formula certifying the opposite answer.

Consider again
$$ f(y)=\sum_i\frac{\mu_i}{y_i}, \qquad 0<y_i\le1, \qquad Cy\le\varepsilon\mathbf1. $$
This is a differentiable convex program on the open positive orthant. It attains its minimum.

It has a strictly feasible point. Set
$$ \delta=\frac12\min\{1,\varepsilon\}, \qquad y=\delta\mathbf1. $$
Then $0<\delta<1$ and, using the row-sum bound,
$$ Cy\le\delta\mathbf1<\varepsilon\mathbf1. $$

The convex KKT theorem states that, for such a differentiable convex program with an attained minimum and a strictly feasible point, nonnegative multipliers satisfying stationarity and complementary slackness exist at every minimizer. Its hypotheses have just been checked. See [Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, Section 5.5.3](https://www.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf).

Thus a feasible $y$ is optimal precisely when there exist $\lambda_a,\nu_i\ge0$ satisfying
$$ \mu_i = y_i^2\left(\sum_a C_{ai}\lambda_a+\nu_i\right), $$
$$ \lambda_a\bigl((Cy)_a-\varepsilon\bigr)=0, \qquad \nu_i(y_i-1)=0. $$
Here $\lambda_a$ corresponds to a row constraint, and $\nu_i$ to $y_i\le1$.

For completeness, sufficiency follows directly. Stationarity says
$$ \nabla f(y)=-C^{\mathsf T}\lambda-\nu. $$
For any feasible $z$, convexity gives
$$ f(z)\ge f(y)+\nabla f(y)^{\mathsf T}(z-y). $$
Complementary slackness and feasibility imply
$$ -\lambda^{\mathsf T}C(z-y)\ge0, \qquad -\nu^{\mathsf T}(z-y)\ge0. $$
For example, whenever $\lambda_a>0$, complementary slackness gives $(Cy)_a=\varepsilon$, while $(Cz)_a\le\varepsilon$. The coordinate inequality is justified identically. Hence $f(z)\ge f(y)$.

Introduce $x_i$ with $x_iy_i=1$. Let $\mathcal M(x,y,\lambda,\nu)$ denote the following system:
$$ 0<y_i\le1,\qquad x_iy_i=1, \qquad Cy\le\varepsilon\mathbf1, $$
$$ \lambda_a\ge0,\qquad \nu_i\ge0, $$
$$ \mu_i=y_i^2\left(\sum_a C_{ai}\lambda_a+\nu_i\right), $$
$$ \lambda_a\bigl((Cy)_a-\varepsilon\bigr)=0, \qquad \nu_i(y_i-1)=0. $$
Its solutions have exactly the optimal $x,y$, though the multipliers need not be unique.

Consequently,
$$ S_\infty^*>B $$
is equivalent to
$$ \exists x,y,\lambda,\nu: \quad \mathcal M(x,y,\lambda,\nu) \quad\text{and}\quad k\sum_i\mu_i x_i>B. $$
This formula has $4\tau$ variables, $O(\tau)$ polynomial conditions, degree at most three, and polynomial coefficient lengths.

Therefore both the decision problem and its complement reduce to existential real feasibility:
$$ \boxed{\text{Exact threshold decision belongs to } \exists\mathbb R\cap\mathrm{co}\text{-}\exists\mathbb R.} $$
This does not assert membership in $\mathrm{NP}$: the existential witnesses are real numbers, without a proved polynomial-size discrete certificate.

5. Exact algorithms and output representations

5.1. Decision complexity

Existential real formulas with integer polynomial coefficients are decidable in polynomial space. This yields membership in $\mathrm{PSPACE}$; see [Canny, *Some Algebraic and Geometric Computations in PSPACE*](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1988/6041.html).

The one-block real quantifier-elimination bounds give decision time
$$ (sd)^{O(q)}\operatorname{poly}(H) $$
for $q$ quantified variables, $s$ polynomial conditions, degree bound $d$, and integer coefficient bit bound $H$. Applying these bounds to Section 4.2, with
$$ q=2\tau,\qquad s=4\tau+1,\qquad d=2, $$
gives
$$ (\tau+1)^{O(\tau)} \operatorname{poly}(m,L,\log(k+1)). $$
The relevant quantifier-elimination and intermediate coefficient bounds are stated in [Basu, *Algorithms in Real Algebraic Geometry: A Survey*, Theorem 2.27](https://arxiv.org/html/1409.1534#S2.SS5.SSS2).

5.2. Computing the exact optimum and allocation

An exact output representation is necessary because optimal coordinates need not be rational. Use the following representation for each output number:

  • an integer univariate polynomial;
  • a rational interval containing exactly one distinct real root of that polynomial.

The represented number is that root. The polynomial need not be minimal.

For an allocation coordinate, keep $x_i$ free in $\mathcal M$ and existentially quantify all other variables. By existence and uniqueness, the resulting formula defines exactly the singleton
$$ \{x_i^*\}. $$
For the optimum value, introduce a free variable $s$, impose
$$ s=k\sum_i\mu_i x_i, $$
and quantify all variables of $\mathcal M$. This defines exactly
$$ \{S_\infty^*\}. $$

Each formula has one free variable, $O(\tau)$ quantified variables and conditions, degree at most three, and polynomial coefficient lengths. One-block quantifier elimination therefore computes an equivalent univariate formula within
$$ (\tau+1)^{O(\tau)} \operatorname{poly}(m,L,\log(k+1)) $$
time, with output degree and coefficient lengths bounded within the same asymptotic bound.

To see why this yields algebraic numbers, consider any such singleton formula after quantifier elimination. Some nonzero polynomial in the formula must vanish at the singleton point. Otherwise every polynomial sign would be constant on a sufficiently small neighborhood of that point, making the Boolean formula true on an interval. That would contradict its defining a singleton.

Univariate real-root isolation and sign evaluation select the unique root satisfying the formula. These operations take polynomial time in the univariate formula’s total length, degrees, and coefficient lengths. Performing this for all coordinates and for the value preserves the displayed singly exponential bound. Multiplication by $k$ produces the original intensities $r_i^*=kx_i^*$.

Thus the general optimization problem is exactly computable, with the stated representation and running time. In particular, both exact decision and exact optimization are polynomial-time solvable for fixed $\tau$.

6. Polynomial special cases and the SOCP distinction

For edgeless templates, $W_h=P_h=I$, so every role independently requires
$$ r_i\ge k, \qquad r_i\ge k/\varepsilon. $$
Because every mass is positive, its smallest feasible intensity is optimal:
$$ r_i^*=k\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}, \qquad S_\infty^*=k\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}. $$
The allocation and rational threshold comparison take
$$ \operatorname{poly}(\tau,L,\log(k+1)) $$
time. This includes $m=1$.

The general rational SOCP formulation is also valid. Under $x_i\ge1$ and $t_i\ge0$,
$$ x_it_i\ge1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \left\|(2,x_i-t_i)\right\|_2\le x_i+t_i. $$
Indeed, the right-hand side is positive, and squaring gives exactly
$$ 4+(x_i-t_i)^2\le(x_i+t_i)^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_it_i\ge1. $$
This matches the reciprocal-variable formulation in [Liu et al., Lemma 20](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.02349v3#Sx2.SS2).

It does not prove a polynomial-time exact threshold algorithm. Such an algorithm would, by Section 3.2, decide square-root-sum comparison in polynomial time.

7. What remains unresolved

The strengthened partial result identifies the exact comparison problem for all two-vertex templates: it is polynomial-time equivalent to square-root-sum comparison, and its optimum and allocation have explicit radical formulas.

For larger templates, both threshold answers have polynomial-size existential-real formulations, and exact algebraic output is available in singly exponential time.

None of these arguments supplies a polynomial-time exact algorithm for unrestricted $\tau$, or a reduction establishing $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. The requested general classification therefore remains unresolved here.
