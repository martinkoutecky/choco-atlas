The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(\tau\) applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational weights \(w_t\), and rankings over acceptable job categories, together with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), decide whether a popular assignment exists and construct one. An assignment \(x\) allocates each type's mass across whole-job categories and its outside option, respecting supplies; it is popular if every feasible reassignment coupling \(z_{tjj'}\), with current-assignment marginals \(x_{tj}\) and challenger totals within the supplies, has nonpositive weighted preference margin \(\sum_{t,j,j'} w_t z_{tjj'}(\mathbf{1}[j'\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t j'])\). Ask whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of job categories, and the input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) job categories with binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\geq0\). Each type has a positive binary-rational weight \(w_t\) and a total preorder, allowing ties, on its acceptable subset \(A_t\) of categories. Append an outside option \(\bot\), strictly worse than every acceptable category and without a supply constraint; unacceptable categories cannot be assigned. Let \(B_t=A_t\cup\{\bot\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An assignment consists of real masses \(x_{tj}\geq0\), indexed by \(j\in B_t\), satisfying \(\sum_{j\in B_t}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:j\in A_t}x_{tj}\leq q_j\) for every job category. Each individual receives one whole job or the outside option; mass may split across these outcomes.
A feasible reassignment coupling consists of real \(z_{tjj'}\geq0\), indexed by \(j,j'\in B_t\), with \(\sum_{j'\in B_t}z_{tjj'}=x_{tj}\) and \(\sum_{t:j'\in A_t}\sum_{j\in B_t}z_{tjj'}\leq q_{j'}\) for every job category \(j'\).
The assignment is popular if every such coupling satisfies \(\sum_t\sum_{j,j'\in B_t}w_tz_{tjj'}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[j'\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t j']\bigr)\leq0\); tied outcomes contribute zero.

Is deciding whether a popular assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with one constructible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the decision problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete weighted popular matching with ties admits an \(O(\min\{k\sqrt{n},n\}e)\) algorithm, where \(n\) counts vertices, \(e\) acceptable pairs, and \(k\) distinct applicant weights ([Mestre](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0707.0546)).
Weighted popular mixed matchings always exist and are polynomial-time computable, but compare independently drawn outcomes rather than arbitrary reassignment couplings ([Kavitha–Mestre–Nasre](https://cse.iitm.ac.in/~meghana/papers/TCS11-2.pdf)). No result settling the coupling-based problem above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive masses to sum to one, consistent with a distribution over types and per-capita supplies.
  • Required strictly positive weights, as in the discrete definition.
  • Allowed ties through total preorders, matching the anchor's preference domain; ties contribute zero without tie-breaking.
  • Made the outside option unconstrained and strictly worse than every acceptable category, corresponding to private last-resort jobs.
  • Excluded unacceptable categories from both assignments and reassignment couplings; allowed empty acceptable subsets.
  • Took supplies to be nonnegative binary rationals with no required total, and assignment and coupling masses to be nonnegative reals.

Polynomial-time solvability

The decision problem is in $\mathrm{P}$. When a popular assignment exists, one can construct one in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

The proof combines an exact reduction to discrete weighted popularity with a compressed implementation of Mestre’s algorithm. Explicitly creating the discrete instance would generally take exponential time; the compression is therefore essential.

1. Scaling and a popular-vertex lemma

We may replace each $q_j$ by $\min\{q_j,1\}$, because the total applicant mass is $1$. We may also delete categories with zero supply. Both operations preserve feasible assignments, feasible couplings, and popularity.

Let $D$ be a common denominator of the remaining masses and supplies, and put
$$ N=D,\qquad U_t=D\mu_t,\qquad V_j=Dq_j. $$
These are integers, with
$$ \sum_t U_t=N,\qquad 1\le V_j\le N. $$
Taking the product of the input denominators suffices, so $\log D=O(L)$.

Let $P$ denote the assignment polytope. For $x\in P$, define
$$ \Phi(x)= \max_z \sum_t\sum_{j,j'\in B_t} w_tz_{tjj'} \bigl(\mathbf 1[j'\succ_t j]-\mathbf 1[j\succ_t j']\bigr), $$
where the maximum is over the feasible reassignment couplings with source assignment $x$. The feasible set is nonempty and compact: the diagonal coupling is feasible, and all coupling entries are nonnegative with total sum $1$. Consequently, the maximum exists, and
$$ \Phi(x)\ge 0. $$
Popularity is exactly the condition $\Phi(x)=0$.

Lemma 1. If $P$ contains a popular assignment, it contains a popular vertex.

Proof. The function $\Phi$ is concave. Indeed, if $z^{(1)}$ and $z^{(2)}$ are maximizing couplings for $x^{(1)}$ and $x^{(2)}$, their convex combination is feasible for the corresponding convex combination of assignments. Thus
$$ \Phi\bigl(\theta x^{(1)}+(1-\theta)x^{(2)}\bigr) \ge \theta\Phi(x^{(1)})+(1-\theta)\Phi(x^{(2)}) $$
for $0\le\theta\le1$.

Write a popular assignment as a convex combination of vertices:
$$ x=\sum_r\alpha_r v^{(r)},\qquad \alpha_r>0,\qquad \sum_r\alpha_r=1. $$
Concavity and nonnegativity give
$$ 0=\Phi(x)\ge\sum_r\alpha_r\Phi(v^{(r)})\ge0. $$
Hence every vertex occurring in this combination is popular. $\square$

Lemma 2. Every vertex $x$ of $P$ satisfies $Dx_{tj}\in\mathbb Z$.

Proof. After scaling by $D$, the assignment constraints form a network-flow polytope. There is a type node of supply $U_t$, real category nodes of capacities $V_j$, and one outside node per type. Assignment entries are the flows from types to their acceptable categories or their outside nodes. An outside node may be given capacity $U_t$, which is redundant.

All capacities and supplies are integral. The integral-flow theorem states that every vertex of a network-flow polytope with integral capacities and supplies is integral. Here the other arc flows are uniquely determined by the assignment entries, so the correspondence preserves vertices. $\square$

Together, these lemmas show that existence over arbitrary real masses is equivalent to existence on the grid with denominator $D$.

2. Exact equivalence with a discrete instance

Define a virtual discrete instance $H$ as follows:

  • Type $t$ has $U_t$ distinct applicants, each of weight $w_t$.
  • Category $j$ has $V_j$ distinct job slots.
  • Every type-$t$ applicant accepts every slot of each category in $A_t$, with slots of the same category tied.
  • Each applicant has a private last-resort job, ranked strictly below all acceptable real jobs.

Interpret a private last-resort job as the outside option. Every feasible integral scaled assignment lifts to an applicant-complete matching in $H$.

Lemma 3. A grid assignment $x$ is popular under the coupling definition if and only if any matching lifting $Dx$ is discretely weighted popular.

Proof. Fix such a lifting $M$. Scale a coupling by $D$, writing $Z=Dz$. Its source constraints become
$$ \sum_{j'}Z_{tjj'}=Dx_{tj}, $$
and its real destination capacities are the integers $V_{j'}$.

Maximizing the vote over $Z$ is another network-flow problem: source nodes are the pairs $(t,j)$, and destination nodes are the real categories and type-specific outside nodes. Its capacities and source supplies are integral. Therefore it has an integral optimum.

Every integral feasible $Z$ can be realized by a competing matching: partition the applicants currently receiving outcome $j$ according to their prescribed destinations, allocate distinct real slots, and give each applicant sent outside their own private last-resort job. Conversely, every competing matching gives an integral feasible $Z$.

The discrete vote is exactly $D$ times the coupling vote. The integral optimum therefore equals the largest discrete vote against $M$. This proves the equivalence. $\square$

By Lemmas 1–3,
$$ \text{a popular assignment exists} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H\text{ has a weighted popular matching}. $$

The virtual instance can have exponentially many vertices. We next show how to run the relevant discrete algorithm without constructing it.

3. Maximum matchings and criticality without cloning

Partition types into weight classes, with distinct weights
$$ \omega_1>\omega_2>\cdots>\omega_k>0, \qquad k\le\tau. $$

We use Mestre’s algorithm in Sections 3–3.1. Its data comprise first and second edge sets $F(a),S(a)$, prefix first-edge graphs $G_i$, their maximum matching sizes $R_i$, critical vertices, and job labels. First and second sets are selected by preference rank and criticality across prefixes. After pruning, Theorem 4 characterizes popular matchings as precisely the applicant-complete matchings using surviving first/second edges whose restriction to every $G_i$ has size $R_i$. The scalar pruning rules are given in Figure 3. [Mestre, *Weighted Popular Matchings*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0707.0546).

A vertex is critical in a graph if every maximum matching covers it.

We establish inductively that first and second sets are uniform within each type, apart from the identity of private last-resort jobs. Real slots within a category are interchangeable, as are applicants within a type together with their private jobs. Thus criticality is uniform within each such group. Selecting outcomes by rank and these criticality predicates preserves this uniformity.

Represent $G_i$ by a flow network having:

  • one applicant node per included type, with capacity $U_t$;
  • one node per real category, with capacity $V_j$;
  • an arc for each real category in that type’s first set.

If a type’s first set consists of its private last-resort jobs, represent their disjoint single-edge components by one outside node of capacity $U_t$. This preserves maximum matching size: that type has no real first edges, and all its private edges can be matched simultaneously.

Every integral flow lifts to a matching, and every matching aggregates to a flow. Hence $R_i$ is computable on a network with $O(\tau+m)$ vertices and $O(\tau m+\tau+m)$ arcs.

Criticality also requires only maximum-flow queries. For a vertex $v$ in a graph with maximum matching size $R$,
$$ v\text{ is critical} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \nu(G-v)=R-1. $$
Indeed, a maximum matching avoiding $v$ exists exactly when deletion preserves size $R$; deleting the edge covering $v$ always preserves at least $R-1$ edges.

Deleting one applicant clone is represented by decreasing $U_t$ by $1$. Deleting one real slot is represented by decreasing $V_j$ by $1$. Thus all required criticality tests are compressed exactly. Private last-resort vertices are either isolated or belong to disjoint single-edge components, so their criticality is immediate.

4. Compressing the alternating-path queries

The remaining matching-dependent operation is label propagation through alternating paths.

Lemma 4. Let $G$ have maximum matching size $R$, and let $p$ be a critical job and $a$ a critical applicant. For any maximum matching $M$, an alternating path from $p$ to $a$ beginning and ending with edges of $M$ exists if and only if
$$ \nu(G-\{p,a\})=R-1. $$

Proof. If such a path exists, toggling its edges leaves $p$ and $a$ unmatched and reduces matching size by exactly $1$. This gives an $(R-1)$-matching after deleting both endpoints. Criticality of $p$ rules out size $R$ after deletion.

Conversely, let $K$ be an $(R-1)$-matching avoiding $p$ and $a$. Consider $M\triangle K$. No component can contain more $K$-edges than $M$-edges, since that would augment $M$. Because $|M|-|K|=1$, exactly one component contains one more $M$-edge.

Both $p$ and $a$ are unmatched in $K$ and matched in $M$. Neither can lie on an equal-size exchange component: toggling that component would produce a maximum matching leaving that critical endpoint unmatched. They therefore lie on the unique component with one extra $M$-edge, and must be its two endpoints. This is the required path. $\square$

For a real slot in category $j$ and an applicant of type $t$, the deletion test in Lemma 4 is represented by simultaneously decreasing $V_j$ and $U_t$ by $1$. It is therefore another compressed maximum-flow query.

In particular, for critical endpoints, reachability is independent of the chosen maximum matching and is uniform across clone groups.

We must also check why noncritical applicant endpoints do not obstruct this compression. In Mestre’s labeling step at class $i$, write $\delta(a)$ for the minimum earlier label strictly better than $F(a)$, and $\eta(a)$ for the minimum earlier label tied with, but outside, $F(a)$. Empty minima are $+\infty$. The pruning step ensures that a surviving noncritical endpoint satisfies
$$ \delta(a)\ge w(a)+\omega_i,\qquad \eta(a)\ge\omega_i. $$
Its label candidate is consequently
$$ \min\{\omega_i,\delta(a)-w(a),\eta(a)\}=\omega_i, $$
which cannot lower the existing cap $\omega_i$. Only critical applicant endpoints need be queried; this is also the reduction used in the paper’s labeling analysis.

We can therefore implement each labeling round as follows:

  • Compute the scalar candidate once per applicant type.
  • For each newly critical real category and each critical applicant type, use Lemma 4 to test endpoint reachability.
  • Take the minimum of the reachable candidates and the class cap.

For a critical private last-resort job, its only reachable applicant endpoint in $G_i$ is its owner. These labels are likewise computable once per type.

Starting with the uniform initial labels, this gives an induction showing that all real slots within a category receive the same label. All subsequent comparisons and first/second-edge deletions are therefore uniform within types and categories. Thus Figure 3 can be executed exactly on representatives.

This accounts for every operation preceding Mestre’s final matching-construction step: preference scans, maximum matching sizes, criticality, scalar label operations, alternating-path queries, and pruning.

5. Constructing the final matching by one flow optimization

After pruning, retain only the surviving first and second assignment arcs. Let $g(t)$ be the weight-class index of type $t$.

Construct a network for a full assignment of all $N$ applicants. Real category capacities are $V_j$; a type’s outside arc, when retained, has capacity $U_t$. Give each retained first arc of type $t$ profit
$$ k-g(t)+1, $$
and each retained second arc profit $0$.

For a feasible flow $X$, define its prefix first-edge counts by
$$ p_i(X)= \sum_{\substack{t:g(t)\le i}} \sum_{j\in F_t}X_{tj}. $$
Outside first edges are included in this sum. The profit is exactly
$$ \sum_{i=1}^k p_i(X). $$

For every feasible full flow,
$$ p_i(X)\le R_i. $$
To see this, restrict $X$ to the first arcs belonging to classes at most $i$. This is a feasible fractional matching flow in $G_i$, whose maximum value is $R_i$ by flow integrality.

First check whether a full flow of value $N$ exists. If it does, compute an integral maximum-profit full flow. Integral maximum-cost flow with integral capacities and integer costs is polynomial-time solvable in the network size and input bit length.

Its profit is at most $\sum_iR_i$. Moreover,
$$ \text{profit}=\sum_iR_i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_i(X)=R_i\text{ for every }i, $$
because each individual deficiency $R_i-p_i(X)$ is nonnegative.

Thus:

  • If no full flow exists, or its maximum profit is smaller than $\sum_iR_i$, no popular matching exists.
  • Otherwise, lift the integral optimal flow to an applicant-complete matching. It uses surviving first/second edges and is maximum in every $G_i$, so Mestre’s characterization makes it popular.

Return the assignment
$$ x_{tj}=\frac{X_{tj}}D. $$
Lemma 3 proves its popularity against all real feasible reassignment couplings, including couplings outside the rational grid.

6. Bit complexity and conclusion

There are at most $\tau$ weight classes. Each prefix network has polynomial size. Criticality requires $O(\tau+m)$ maximum-flow queries per class, and labeling requires at most $O(\tau m)$ additional queries per class. All capacities have $O(L)$ bits.

The label arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity. One may first multiply all weights by a common positive denominator, preserving popularity and every comparison. Labels are selected by minima and subtractions from labels of earlier classes; hence each finite label is a signed sum of at most $k+1$ scaled input weights. Their bit lengths are polynomial in $L$ and $\tau$.

The final maximum-cost flow uses integer profits at most $k$, and the output has denominator $D$. No applicant or job clone is explicitly generated.

Therefore the coupling-based existence problem is polynomial-time decidable in $m,\tau,L$, with a popular assignment constructible within the same polynomial-time bound.
