The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given positive rational type masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), a rational query state \(x\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\), rational polynomial vector fields \(f_t\) on that box, and rational \(\epsilon>0\), compute each type’s allocation \(u_t(x)-u_t(0)\) to additive error at most \(\epsilon\). Here \(u_t\) is the mean-zero solution of \(-\Delta u_t=-\nabla\cdot f_t\) with Neumann boundary condition and the compatibility condition \(\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot f_t=0\); the aggregate allocation to type \(t\) is \(\mu_t(u_t(x)-u_t(0))\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), encoded in binary and summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(\Omega=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_t]\). Input also includes \(x\in\Omega\cap\mathbb Q^\tau\), \(\epsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), and polynomial vector fields \(f_t:\Omega\to\mathbb R^\tau\) with rational coefficients of unrestricted sign. Each component is an explicit list of monomials with binary rational coefficients and binary nonnegative integer exponents; let \(m\) count all listed monomials and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For every \(t\), promise \(\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot f_t\,dy=0\). Define \(u_t\) as the mean-zero weak solution of \(-\Delta u_t=-\nabla\cdot f_t\), with homogeneous Neumann condition \(\partial_nu_t=0\) on every face and \(\int_\Omega u_t\,dy=0\); point values use its continuous representative on the closed box.
The computational problem is to approximate every \(a_t(x)=u_t(x)-u_t(0)\) within additive error \(\epsilon\). The aggregate allocation is \(\mu_t a_t(x)\).

Is this promised allocation problem solvable in deterministic time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), including the binary encoding of \(\epsilon\), or is it NP-hard?

For explicitly represented finite connected weighted graphs with reversible walks, expected antisymmetric-flow allocations equal the unique graph Poisson solution normalized at the initial vertex; rational instances therefore admit polynomial-time computation in the explicit graph input size. Result

The promised problem is solvable in deterministic time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This includes binary-encoded exponents and binary-encoded accuracy.

The proof uses the tensor factorization of the Neumann heat semigroup. The main technical point is that its one-dimensional monomial averages can be evaluated in polynomial time even when the degree is exponentially large.

1. Bounds and the heat representation

Write \(d=\tau\), and, for each requested allocation, set
\[ g_t=-\nabla\cdot f_t. \]

Here and below, displayed formulas are written using the requested delimiters:

\(g_t(y)=\sum_{\ell}c_{t\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{d}y_j^{r_{t\ell j}}.\)

Differentiating a listed monomial in its component’s coordinate produces at most one monomial. Thus all the \(g_t\) together have at most \(m\) listed terms. Their exponents and coefficients have polynomial bit length.

Because the masses are positive and sum to \(1\), every coordinate satisfies \(0\leq y_j\leq\mu_j\leq1\). Each original coefficient and exponent has magnitude at most \(2^L\), so every differentiated coefficient has magnitude at most \(2^{2L}\). Consequently, the computable bound

\(B=2^{2L+m}\)

satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,\ell}|c_{t\ell}|\leq B,\qquad \|g_t\|_\infty\leq B.\)

The promise gives \(\int_\Omega g_t=0\).

Let \(P_s\) denote the Neumann heat semigroup on \(\Omega\), with generator \(\Delta\). We will use

\[\displaystyle u_t(z)=\int_0^\infty(P_sg_t)(z)\,ds,\qquad a_t(x)=\int_0^\infty\bigl((P_sg_t)(x)-(P_sg_t)(0)\bigr)\,ds. \tag{1}\]

We first justify this representation and its uniform convergence.

Let \(V=\prod_j\mu_j\). The one-dimensional Neumann kernel on \([0,\mu_j]\) is

\(\displaystyle K_s^{(j)}(z,w)=\frac1{\mu_j} \left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\pi^2n^2s/\mu_j^2} \cos\frac{n\pi z}{\mu_j}\cos\frac{n\pi w}{\mu_j}\right).\)

For \(s\geq1\), since \(\mu_j\leq1\),

\(\displaystyle \left|\mu_jK_s^{(j)}(z,w)-1\right| \leq2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\pi^2n^2s} \leq3e^{-\pi^2s}=:\rho(s).\)

The last inequality follows from \(n^2\geq1+3(n-1)\) and a geometric-series bound. Also \(\rho(s)\leq1\).

The box kernel is the product of the coordinate kernels. Hence

\(\displaystyle |VK_s^\Omega(z,w)-1| \leq(1+\rho(s))^d-1 \leq d\rho(s)e^{d\rho(s)} \leq3de^d e^{-\pi^2s}. \)

Using the zero mean of \(g_t\), we obtain

\[\displaystyle \|P_sg_t\|_\infty\leq3Bde^d e^{-\pi^2s},\qquad s\geq1. \tag{2}\]

For all real \(s>0\), positivity and preservation of constants give \(\|P_sg_t\|_\infty\leq B\). These bounds prove uniform convergence of the integral in (1). Its value is continuous on the closed box: integrals over compact subintervals of positive time are continuous, and the omitted small-time and large-time integrals converge uniformly to zero.

For completeness, the normalized tensor cosine eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis, with eigenvalues

\(\displaystyle \lambda_{\mathbf n}=\pi^2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{n_j^2}{\mu_j^2}, \qquad \mathbf n\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{d}.\)

The zero eigenfunction is constant, and every nonzero eigenvalue is at least \(\pi^2\). Integrating each heat coefficient in (1) therefore gives the coefficient \(\widehat g_t(\mathbf n)/\lambda_{\mathbf n}\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\mathbf n\ne0}\lambda_{\mathbf n} \left|\frac{\widehat g_t(\mathbf n)}{\lambda_{\mathbf n}}\right|^2 \leq\frac1{\pi^2}\|g_t\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2. \)

Thus the integral belongs to \(H^1(\Omega)\) and satisfies the weak Neumann equation. This uses the usual cosine-series identity for the \(H^1\) quadratic form, obtained by even reflection and Parseval’s identity. Its mean is zero. Uniqueness follows by testing the difference of two weak solutions against itself: its gradient vanishes, and its zero mean then makes it zero. Accordingly, (1) gives precisely the continuous representative specified in the question.

2. An elementary quadrature rule with controlled bit complexity

We will repeatedly use the following lemma.

Quadrature lemma. Suppose a real-valued function \(F\) on an interval \(I\) of length \(\ell\) extends analytically to every complex disk of radius \(R\) centered on \(I\), and has magnitude at most \(M\) there. Assume \(\ell\leq R/4\). Interpolate \(F\) at \(n\geq2\) equally spaced nodes, and integrate the interpolating polynomial exactly. The quadrature error is at most

\[\displaystyle \ell M4^{-n}. \tag{3}\]

The quadrature weights are rational when the interval endpoints are rational. They have polynomial bit length in \(n\) and the endpoint bit lengths, and their total absolute value is at most \(\ell n^n\).

Proof. Cauchy’s estimate gives

\(\displaystyle \sup_{y\in I}|F^{(n)}(y)|\leq n!MR^{-n}.\)

The real interpolation remainder is bounded by \(M(\ell/R)^n\), since each of its \(n\) distance factors is at most \(\ell\). Integrating proves (3).

After rescaling \(I\) to \([0,1]\), the nodes are \(j/(n-1)\). Each Lagrange basis polynomial has magnitude at most \((n-1)^{n-1}\) on \([0,1]\). Therefore the sum of the absolute integrated weights is at most \(n(n-1)^{n-1}\leq n^n\).

The weights can be constructed by rational polynomial multiplication and integration. The factors, coefficients, and denominators have polynomial bit length. Finally, errors of at most \(\eta\) in the sampled function values contribute at most \(\ell n^n\eta\). Thus only \(O(n\log n)\) additional accuracy bits are needed. \(\square\)

3. Polynomial-time evaluation of one-dimensional heat moments

Define, on the unit interval,

\(\displaystyle H_r(z,v)=\int_0^1K_v(z,w)w^r\,dw, \qquad r\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0},\quad z\in[0,1],\quad v>0.\)

Heat-moment lemma. Given binary-encoded \(r\), rational \(z,v\), and an integer accuracy parameter \(p\geq1\), one can approximate \(H_r(z,v)\) within \(2^{-p}\) in deterministic time polynomial in \(p\) and the input bit length.

Let \(b\) bound the bit lengths of \(r,z,v\); in particular, \(2^{-b}\leq v\leq2^b\). We prove the lemma separately for large and small \(v\).

3.1. The case \(v\geq1\)

The cosine expansion gives

\[\displaystyle H_r(z,v)=\frac1{r+1} +2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-\pi^2n^2v}\cos(n\pi z)\,C_{r,n}, \qquad C_{r,n}=\int_0^1w^r\cos(n\pi w)\,dw. \tag{4} \]

Since \(|C_{r,n}|\leq1\), truncating this series after \(J\) terms has error at most

\(\displaystyle 2\sum_{n>J}e^{-\pi^2n^2}\leq4e^{-(J+1)^2}.\)

It suffices to take \(J=O(p+b+1)\).

To compute \(C_{r,n}\), choose a positive integer \(D\) and restrict integration to

\(\displaystyle I_r=\left[\max\left\{0,1-\frac D{r+1}\right\},1\right].\)

If this discards a nonempty interval, then \(r\geq1\), and the discarded integral has absolute value at most

\[\displaystyle \left(1-\frac D{r+1}\right)^r \leq e^{-rD/(r+1)} \leq e^{-D/2}. \tag{5} \]

For a real center \(w\in[0,1]\), on a complex disk of radius

\(\displaystyle R=\min\left\{\frac1{r+1},\frac1{n+1}\right\}\)

we have

\(\displaystyle |w_{\mathbb C}^{\,r}|\leq e,\qquad |\cos(n\pi w_{\mathbb C})|\leq e^\pi.\)

The interval \(I_r\) has length at most \(D/(r+1)\). Dividing it into pieces of length at most \(R/4\) takes at most

\(\displaystyle 4D(n+1)+1\)

pieces. Applying the quadrature lemma on each piece gives exponentially decreasing error in the interpolation order.

For example, take \(J,D\), and the interpolation order all equal to \(16(p+b+10)\). Equations (3)–(5), summed over the retained terms of (4), make all truncation and interpolation errors smaller than a fixed fraction of \(2^{-p}\). The quadrature lemma supplies polynomially many guard bits for evaluating the nodes.

3.2. The case \(0<v<1\)

Write

\(\displaystyle G_v(a)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi v}}e^{-a^2/(4v)}.\)

Even reflection and periodic extension give the image formula

\[\displaystyle K_v(z,w)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z} \bigl(G_v(z-w+2k)+G_v(z+w+2k)\bigr). \tag{6} \]

This formula follows by convolving the even, period-\(2\) extension with the real-line Gaussian. Reflection gives the Neumann conditions; preservation of constants gives kernel mass \(1\). Its Fourier expansion is the cosine kernel used in (4).

For \(|k|\geq2\), the absolute values of both Gaussian arguments in (6) are at least \(|k|\). Thus omitting \(|k|>J\), for \(J\geq2\), contributes at most

\[\displaystyle 4v^{-1/2}e^{-(J+1)^2/4}. \tag{7} \]

Here we bounded the resulting Gaussian series by a geometric series and used \(v<1\). Because \(v^{-1/2}\leq2^{b/2}\), taking \(J=O(p+b+1)\) suffices.

Each retained image requires an integral of the form

\(\displaystyle I_{r,c,v}=\int_0^1w^r e^{-(w-c)^2/(4v)}\,dw,\)

where \(c=z+2k\) or \(c=-z-2k\).

Choose a rational dyadic number \(h\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle h^2\leq v<4h^2.\)

Such an \(h\) is found by integer comparisons and has \(O(b)\) bits. Restrict the integral to

\(\displaystyle I=I_r\cap[c-Dh,c+Dh]\cap[0,1].\)

The polynomial cutoff discards at most \(e^{-D/2}\), by (5). Outside the Gaussian cutoff,

\(\displaystyle e^{-(w-c)^2/(4v)}\leq e^{-D^2/16}.\)

Consequently, the total discarded integral is at most

\[\displaystyle e^{-D/2}+e^{-D^2/16}. \tag{8}\]

Now put

\(\displaystyle R=\min\left\{\frac1{r+1},h\right\}.\)

On every complex disk of radius \(R\) centered on \(I\),

\(\displaystyle |w_{\mathbb C}^{\,r}|\leq e,\qquad \left|e^{-(w_{\mathbb C}-c)^2/(4v)}\right| \leq e^{R^2/(4v)}\leq e^{1/4}. \)

The second bound holds because the real part of the Gaussian exponent is at most the square of the imaginary part divided by \(4v\).

Crucially, the retained interval has length

\(\displaystyle |I|\leq \min\left\{\frac D{r+1},2Dh\right\}\leq2DR.\)

It therefore takes at most \(8D+1\) pieces of length at most \(R/4\), irrespective of how large \(r\) is or how small \(v\) is. The quadrature lemma applies with \(M=e^{5/4}\).

Taking \(J=D\) and the interpolation order equal to \(16(p+b+10)\) makes (7), and the errors in (8) and (3) after multiplication by all retained Gaussian prefactors, smaller than a fixed fraction of \(2^{-p}\). Indeed, there are \(4J+2\) retained images, and each prefactor is at most \(2^{b/2}\). These factors have logarithms polynomial in \(p+b\).

3.3. Arithmetic and guard bits

Every quadrature node above is rational with polynomial bit length. Computing \(w^r\) needs only \(O(\log(r+1))\) rounded multiplications by repeated squaring.

To justify the accuracy dependence, clamp intermediate power approximations to \([0,1]\). Multiplication on this square is Lipschitz with constant \(1\) in each argument. Expanding the powering computation into its multiplication tree therefore bounds the propagated error by \(O(r)\) times the per-operation error. Thus \(O(p+\log(r+1))\) fractional bits suffice for a \(2^{-p}\) power approximation, before the polynomially many quadrature guard bits are added.

The remaining evaluations are polynomial-time elementary arithmetic:

  • The cosine arguments have polynomial magnitude, so Taylor series with polynomially many terms and guard bits suffice.
  • For a nonnegative Gaussian exponent \(A\), return zero when \(A\) exceeds the requested accuracy threshold; otherwise \(A\) has polynomial magnitude and a Taylor series suffices for \(e^{-A}\).
  • Square roots and inverse square roots of positive rationals of \(b\) bits need polynomially many Newton iterations and \(O(b+p)\) accuracy bits.
  • \(\pi\) can be computed to the necessary precision by, for example, the geometrically convergent arctangent series in Machin’s formula.

All threshold comparisons and roundoff allowances can be made with rational interval bounds. The quadrature weights require \(O(n\log n)\) guard bits by Section 2. The Gaussian prefactors require only \(O(b)\) more bits. There are polynomially many terms, panels, and nodes.

This proves the heat-moment lemma, including its bit-complexity assertion. \(\square\)

4. Evaluating the box semigroup

For a monomial \(q(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}y_j^{r_j}\), tensor factorization and coordinate rescaling give

\[\displaystyle (P_sq)(z)= \prod_{j=1}^{d} \left[ \mu_j^{r_j} H_{r_j}\left(\frac{z_j}{\mu_j},\frac{s}{\mu_j^2}\right) \right]. \tag{9} \]

Each bracket lies in \([0,1]\). The powers \(\mu_j^{r_j}\) are evaluated by the same rounded-powering procedure, and the heat-moment lemma evaluates the other factors.

If each bracket is approximated within \(\theta\), with its approximation clamped to \([0,1]\), telescoping the product gives error at most \(d\theta\). Summing over the sparse monomials therefore gives error at most \(Bd\theta\) in \(P_sg_t(z)\), and at most \(2Bd\theta\) in

\(\displaystyle F_t(s)=(P_sg_t)(x)-(P_sg_t)(0).\)

Thus \(F_t(s)\) can be evaluated within any accuracy \(\eta>0\) in time polynomial in the original input length, the rational time’s bit length, and \(\log^+(1/\eta)\): choose, for example, \(\theta\leq\eta/(4Bd)\).

Computing all fields at one time uses at most \(2md\) one-dimensional moments, together with polynomially many rational arithmetic operations.

5. Polynomial-time quadrature over time

It remains to integrate \(F_t\) without dependence polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\).

Let

\(\displaystyle \epsilon_*=\min\{\epsilon,1\},\qquad N=10(L+m+d+1),\qquad \delta=2^{-N},\qquad T=N.\)

Since \(\epsilon\) is an explicit positive binary rational, \(\epsilon_*\geq2^{-L}\).

5.1. Truncation errors

The small-time error is at most

\[\displaystyle \left|\int_0^\delta F_t(s)\,ds\right| \leq2B\delta. \tag{10} \]

By (2), the large-time error is at most

\[\displaystyle \left|\int_T^\infty F_t(s)\,ds\right| \leq\frac{6Bde^d}{\pi^2}e^{-\pi^2T}. \tag{11} \]

Both are at most \(\epsilon_*/8\). For (10), use \(B=2^{2L+m}\). For (11), the elementary inequalities \(e^d\leq2^{2d}\), \(d\leq2^d\), \(e^{-T}\leq2^{-T}\), and \(\pi^2>1\) give the upper bound \(2^{2L+m+3d+3-N}\). The stated choice of \(N\) makes both bounds smaller than \(2^{-L}/8\).

5.2. Analyticity supplies exponentially accurate quadrature

We need a complex-time bound independent of the monomial degrees.

For complex \(\zeta\) with \(\operatorname{Re}\zeta>0\), put \(s'=|\zeta|^2/\operatorname{Re}\zeta\). A complex Gaussian satisfies

\(\displaystyle |G_\zeta(a)|= \sqrt{\frac{|\zeta|}{\operatorname{Re}\zeta}}\;G_{s'}(a). \)

Applying this identity term by term to the image kernel shows that its integral absolute value on an interval is at most \(\sqrt{|\zeta|/\operatorname{Re}\zeta}\). Tensor factorization therefore gives

\[\displaystyle |(P_\zeta g_t)(z)| \leq B\left(\frac{|\zeta|}{\operatorname{Re}\zeta}\right)^{d/2}. \tag{12} \]

The image series converges locally uniformly in the right half-plane, which also proves analyticity there.

Partition \([\delta,T]\) into successive intervals \([s,2s]\), clipping the last interval at \(T\), and divide each into \(16\) equal panels. There are \(O(N+\log N)\) parent intervals.

For any real center in \([s,2s]\), the disk of radius \(s/4\) lies in the right half-plane and satisfies

\(\displaystyle \frac{|\zeta|}{\operatorname{Re}\zeta}\leq3.\)

Thus (12) bounds the analytic extension of \(F_t\) on every such disk by

\(\displaystyle M=2B3^{d/2}\leq2B2^d.\)

Each panel has length at most \(s/16\), so Section 2 applies with \(R=s/4\). Use \(N\) interpolation nodes per panel. Summing the interpolation errors gives at most

\[\displaystyle TM4^{-N}\leq\epsilon_*/8. \tag{13}\]

For an explicit check, \(\log_2T=\log_2N\leq N\), so the left-hand side is at most \(2^{1+2L+m+d-N}\), which is smaller than \(2^{-L}/8\).

5.3. Evaluation accuracy and total complexity

Let \(W_N=N^N\). Across all time panels, the sum of absolute quadrature weights is at most \(TW_N\). Evaluate each \(F_t\) within

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac{\epsilon_*}{8TW_N}.\)

Section 4 then bounds the total evaluation error in the time quadrature by \(\epsilon_*/8\).

The required number of accuracy bits is polynomial:

\(\displaystyle \log_2(1/\eta) =O\bigl(L+N\log N\bigr). \)

All time nodes are rational with \(O(N+\log N)\) bits. The arguments \(x_j/\mu_j\) and \(s/\mu_j^2\) consequently have polynomial bit length. There are polynomially many time nodes, and each uses polynomially many one-dimensional moment computations at polynomial precision. The rational quadrature weights, sampled approximations, and their final sums also have polynomial bit length.

Combining (10), (11), (13), and the evaluation error gives

\(\displaystyle |\widehat a_t-a_t(x)|\leq\epsilon_*/2\leq\epsilon.\)

The construction is deterministic and computes all requested allocations in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

6. Aggregate allocation

Return \(\mu_t\widehat a_t\) for the aggregate allocation. Exact rational multiplication preserves polynomial bit complexity, and

\(\displaystyle |\mu_t\widehat a_t-\mu_ta_t(x)| \leq\mu_t\epsilon\leq\epsilon. \)

Thus the stated promised allocation problem is polynomial-time solvable, including unrestricted coefficient signs, variable support size, binary exponents, and binary accuracy.
