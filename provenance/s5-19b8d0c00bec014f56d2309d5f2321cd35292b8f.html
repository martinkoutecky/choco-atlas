The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\ge3\) and binary-rational scoring vectors \(s\ne s_B\) and \(s'\), find alternatives \(x,y\) and a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings such that \(x\) loses pairwise to every other alternative, \(x\) uniquely maximizes \(s\)'s score, and \(y\)'s \(s'\)-score strictly exceeds \(x\)'s.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(X=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m\ge3\). Input scoring vectors \(s,s'\in\mathbb Q^m\) satisfy \(1=s(1)\ge\cdots\ge s(m)=0\) and the same normalization for \(s'\); assume \(s\ne s_B\) and \(s'\ne s\), where \(s_B\) is normalized Borda.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings.
For a scoring vector \(v\), write \(S_v(a)=\sum_R\mu(R)v(r_R(a))\), where \(r_R(a)\) is the position of \(a\) in ranking \(R\), with first position numbered \(1\).
Construct distinct alternatives \(x,y\) and such a society \(\mu\) satisfying \(\mu\{R:a\succ_R x\}>1/2\) and \(S_s(x)>S_s(a)\) for every \(a\ne x\), together with \(S_{s'}(y)>S_{s'}(x)\). All inequalities are strict; no tie-breaking is used.
Let \(L\) be the input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), including the cost of writing the output support and masses.

Can this search problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m\) and \(L\), including the complete output, or is it NP-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions?

For distinct rules with the first non-Borda, finite-voter witnesses with these strict properties are known to exist, without a stated polynomial-time bound ([Doi–Nakamura](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05916v1)). Rational-distribution existence follows from finite-voter existence; the search found no complexity classification for this exact construction problem.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Excluded s'=s: the literal proposition omits distinctness, but its proof assumes it, and identical vectors cannot satisfy the required opposing strict score inequalities.
  • Specified both scoring vectors as weakly decreasing rational vectors normalized to first score 1 and last score 0.
  • Specified strict rankings, positive supported masses totaling 1, strict pairwise losses, and no tie-breaking.
  • Treated the society as output, with no supplied support restriction or support-size bound; polynomial time includes writing its full representation.
  • Used polynomial-time Turing reductions to specify NP-hardness for the search problem.

Result

The search problem is polynomial-time solvable, including the complete output. There is an algorithm producing a witness with support size \(\tau\leq 5(m-2)\), with each mass having \(O(L+\log m)\) bits. The masses can even be chosen dyadic.

The essential reduction is to symmetrize all alternatives except the designated \(x,y\). This leaves only five distinct strict inequalities.

1. Symmetry reduces the construction to five margins

Fix \(x=1\), \(y=2\), and write \(A=X\setminus\{x,y\}\), \(n=m-2\), and \(T_s=\sum_{r=1}^{m}s(r)\).

We use the existence result supplied in the question: under the stated hypotheses, there is a finite-voter society in which \(x\) is a strict Condorcet loser and the unique winner under \(s\), while some \(y\neq x\) satisfies \(S_{s'}(y)>S_{s'}(x)\). These are precisely the properties furnished by [Doi–Nakamura, Proposition 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05916v1#S3). Dividing voter counts by the electorate size gives rational masses. Relabeling alternatives lets us take \(x=1,y=2\).

Average this society over all permutations of \(A\), keeping \(x,y\) fixed. The resulting society still satisfies every required strict inequality:

  • The scores of \(x,y\), and their pairwise comparison, are unchanged.
  • Each alternative in \(A\) receives the average of the original \(s\)-scores of alternatives in \(A\), which is strictly below \(S_s(x)\).
  • Each alternative in \(A\) has the average of the original probabilities of beating \(x\), which is strictly above \(1/2\).

For distinct positions \(i,j\), let \(p_{ij}\) be the probability that \(x\) occupies position \(i\) and \(y\) position \(j\). Conditional on these positions, each fixed alternative in \(A\) is uniformly distributed among the other \(n\) positions.

Define five coefficients for every \(i\neq j\):

  • \(d^{(1)}_{ij}=s(i)-s(j)\).
  • \(d^{(2)}_{ij}=s(i)-\dfrac{T_s-s(i)-s(j)}{n}\).
  • \(d^{(3)}_{ij}=s'(j)-s'(i)\).
  • \(d^{(4)}_{ij}=\mathbf 1_{\{j<i\}}-\dfrac12\).
  • \(d^{(5)}_{ij}=\dfrac{i-1-\mathbf 1_{\{j<i\}}}{n}-\dfrac12\).

Their expectations are, respectively:

  • \(S_s(x)-S_s(y)\).
  • \(S_s(x)-S_s(a)\), for every \(a\in A\).
  • \(S_{s'}(y)-S_{s'}(x)\).
  • \(\Pr(y\succ x)-1/2\).
  • \(\Pr(a\succ x)-1/2\), for every \(a\in A\).

For the last formula, there are \(i-1\) positions above \(x\), of which \(\mathbf 1_{\{j<i\}}\) are occupied by \(y\). Uniformity over the remaining positions gives the stated probability.

Consequently, the supplied existence theorem implies the existence of a probability vector \(p\) for which all five expectations are strictly positive.

2. A polynomial-size linear program

Introduce a common margin \(t\). Solve the rational linear program:

  • Maximize \(t\).
  • Require \(\sum_{i\neq j}p_{ij}=1\).
  • Require \(p_{ij}\geq 0\) and \(t\geq 0\).
  • For each \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,5\}\), require

\(\sum_{i\neq j}d^{(\ell)}_{ij}p_{ij}\geq t\).

There are \(m(m-1)+1\) variables and five margin inequalities.

The preceding symmetrization argument gives a feasible point with \(t>0\). Thus the optimum \(t^*\) is positive. The feasible set is bounded: \(p\) lies in a simplex, and the fourth margin inequality gives \(t\leq 1/2\). It is closed, so the optimum is attained.

We apply the standard rational linear-programming theorem: a rational linear program can be optimized exactly in polynomial time in its binary encoding length; when its feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded, an optimal vertex can also be obtained in polynomial time. All these hypotheses have just been verified.

Choose an optimal vertex \((p^*,t^*)\).

Support bound. Let \(k\) be the number of positive coordinates of \(p^*\). After fixing its zero coordinates, there are \(k+1\) variables: these positive coordinates and \(t\). The constraint \(t\geq0\) is inactive because \(t^*>0\). The remaining active constraints consist of the normalization equality and at most five margin equalities.

If \(k+1>6\), their coefficient matrix has a nonzero null direction. A sufficiently small displacement in either direction preserves the active constraints, positivity of the positive coordinates and \(t\), and every inactive inequality. This contradicts being a vertex. Therefore \(k\leq5\).

3. Realizing each position pair with explicit rankings

For each supported pair \((i,j)\), construct a block of \(n\) rankings:

  • Place \(x\) at position \(i\) and \(y\) at position \(j\).
  • List the unused positions in increasing order.
  • Fill those positions with each of the \(n\) cyclic rotations of the list \(3,4,\ldots,m\).

Assign each ranking in this block mass \(p^*_{ij}/n\).

Within the block, every \(a\in A\) occupies each unused position exactly once. Hence its average score under \(s\) is \((T_s-s(i)-s(j))/n\), and its probability of appearing above \(x\) is \((i-1-\mathbf 1_{\{j<i\}})/n\).

Thus these blocks realize all five margins from the linear program exactly. Every required score difference and majority excess is at least \(t^*>0\).

The rankings are distinct: different pairs give different positions for \(x,y\), and different rotations within a block give different rankings. Their masses are positive and sum to \(1\). The support size is therefore \(\tau_0=kn\leq5(m-2)\).

4. Bit lengths and dyadic masses

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all entries of \(s,s'\), as encoded in the input, and put \(D=2nQ\). Then \(\log D=O(L+\log m)\).

Every coefficient \(d^{(\ell)}_{ij}\) has denominator dividing \(D\) and absolute value at most \(1\). At the chosen vertex, the \(k\) positive weights and \(t^*\) are determined by \(k+1\leq6\) independent active equations.

Multiplying those equations by \(D\) gives an integer system whose entries and right-hand side have absolute value at most \(D\). By Cramer’s rule, its solution coordinates have numerators and denominators bounded in absolute value by \(6!D^6\). In particular:

  • Each positive \(p^*_{ij}\) and \(t^*\) has \(O(L+\log m)\) bits.
  • Each output mass \(p^*_{ij}/n\) has \(O(L+\log m)\) bits.
  • Since \(t^*>0\), we have \(t^*\geq(6!D^6)^{-1}\).

This already provides masses encoded as binary rationals. To cover the stronger interpretation requiring dyadic masses, the following rounding completes the construction.

Write the block-expanded masses as \(w_1,\ldots,w_{\tau_0}\). Choose an integer \(b\) such that \(2^b\geq4\tau_0/t^*\). The preceding lower bound implies \(b=O(L+\log m)\).

For \(h<\tau_0\), set \(\widehat w_h=2^{-b}\lfloor2^bw_h\rfloor\), and set \(\widehat w_{\tau_0}=1-\sum_{h<\tau_0}\widehat w_h\). Delete rankings whose rounded mass is zero.

All retained masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to \(1\). Moreover,

\(\sum_h|\widehat w_h-w_h|\leq2(\tau_0-1)2^{-b}<t^*/2\).

Each required score difference is the expectation of a per-ranking quantity in \([-1,1]\). Each majority excess is the expectation of an indicator minus \(1/2\), also in \([-1,1]\). Therefore changing the masses alters every required margin by less than \(t^*/2\).

All margins remain strictly positive, even though rounding may destroy the symmetry. The final support size still satisfies \(\tau\leq5(m-2)\), and every mass has \(O(L+\log m)\) bits.

5. Running time, including output

Put \(B=L+\lceil\log_2m\rceil+1\).

The linear program has encoding length \(O(m^2B)\). Constructing it, finding an exact optimal vertex, and performing the rounding take \((m^2B)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

Generating the supported rankings takes \(O(\tau m)\) elementary index operations. The complete output has

\(O\!\left(\tau(m\log m+B)\right)\)

bits: each ranking requires \(O(m\log m)\) bits, and each mass requires \(O(B)\) bits. Writing that output takes time linear in its length.

Thus a valid total running-time bound is

\((m^2B)^{O(1)}+O\!\left(\tau(m\log m+B)\right)\), with \(\tau\leq5(m-2)\).

This is polynomial in \(m,L\), including the complete support and masses. Consequently, NP-hardness under polynomial-time Turing reductions would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
