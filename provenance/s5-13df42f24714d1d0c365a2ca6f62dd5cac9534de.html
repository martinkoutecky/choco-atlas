Result

Under the usual binary encoding of rational inputs, the requested approximation is polynomial-time computable: for every rational \(\varepsilon>0\), one can produce a rational feasible \(x\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x)\ge (1-1/e-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT},\)

in time polynomial in the input length and \(1/\varepsilon\).

Exact optimization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when the supplied graph is obtained by replacing every edge of a simple cubic graph by two opposite arcs, all masses are equal, and every nonzero interaction coefficient equals \(1/100\). There is no fully polynomial-time approximation scheme unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

There is one necessary qualification: a maximum need not exist. Accordingly, define

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}:=\sup\left\{F_\infty(x):0\le x\le\mu,\ \sum_t x_t\le\kappa\right\}.\)

The approximation guarantee holds against this supremum. The hardness construction has an attained maximum. A nonattainment example is proved below.

1. Fixed-point formulation

Write \(n=|T|\), \(y_t^r=\mu_t a_t^r\), and define the nonnegative matrix \(B\) by \(B_{ts}=\lambda_{st}\). For fixed \(x\), put

\(\displaystyle \Phi_{x,t}(y)=\mu_t-(\mu_t-x_t)\exp(-(By)_t).\)

The given recurrence implies

\(\displaystyle \mu_t-y_t^{r+1}=(\mu_t-y_t^r)\exp(-(B(y^r-y^{r-1}))_t).\)

Multiplying these identities, and using \(y^{-1}=0\) and \(y^0=x\), gives

\(\displaystyle y^{r+1}=\Phi_x(y^r).\)

The map \(\Phi_x\) is coordinatewise nondecreasing in both \(x\) and \(y\). Moreover, \(\Phi_x(0)=x\), and it maps the box \([0,\mu]\) into itself. Thus the iteration starting from \(0\) increases to a limit \(y(x)\), which satisfies

\(\displaystyle y(x)=\Phi_x(y(x)),\qquad F_\infty(x)=\sum_t y_t(x).\)

It is the least fixed point in the box: if \(z=\Phi_x(z)\), induction gives every iterate at most \(z\).

Consequently, \(F_\infty\) is coordinatewise nondecreasing, and

\(\displaystyle \sum_t x_t\le F_\infty(x)\le1.\)

We henceforth consider \(0<\kappa<1\). If \(\kappa=0\), the only feasible point is \(0\), with value \(0\); if \(\kappa\ge1\), choosing \(x=\mu\) attains value \(1\). Negative \(\kappa\) makes the feasible set empty.

2. Diminishing returns

We prove that whenever \(x\le z\) and both increments remain in the box,

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x+h e_i)-F_\infty(x)\ge F_\infty(z+h e_i)-F_\infty(z)\qquad(h\ge0).\)

This is the continuous diminishing-returns property.

For a finite iterate, abbreviate \(u_t=(By^r)_t\). Differentiation gives

\(\displaystyle \partial_i y_t^{r+1}=e^{-u_t}\left[\delta_{ti}+(\mu_t-x_t)\partial_i u_t\right]\)

and

\(\displaystyle \partial_{ij}y_t^{r+1} =e^{-u_t}\left[-\delta_{ti}\partial_j u_t-\delta_{tj}\partial_i u_t +(\mu_t-x_t)\bigl(\partial_{ij}u_t-\partial_i u_t\,\partial_j u_t\bigr)\right].\)

At \(r=0\), every first derivative of \(y^0=x\) is nonnegative and every second derivative is zero. Since \(B\) has nonnegative entries, the displayed identities inductively imply

\(\displaystyle \partial_i y_t^r\ge0,\qquad \partial_{ij}y_t^r\le0\)

for every \(i,j,t,r\), including \(i=j\).

Therefore each first derivative decreases when any coordinate increases. Integrating the relevant first derivative over an increment of length \(h\) proves diminishing returns for every finite iterate. Taking limits proves the asserted inequality for \(F_\infty\).

This argument does not assume that the limit is continuous at the boundary.

3. Polynomial-time evaluation at strictly positive seed vectors

The approximation algorithm will query only rational vectors satisfying \(x_t>0\) for every \(t\). At these vectors, \(F_\infty(x)\) can be evaluated to additive error \(\tau\) in time polynomial in the input length and \(\log(1/\tau)\).

3.1 Uniqueness of the fixed point

Each coordinate of \(\Phi_x\), considered on the nonnegative orthant, is concave. Hence, for \(\alpha\ge1\),

\(\displaystyle \Phi_x(\alpha w)\le\alpha\Phi_x(w)-(\alpha-1)x.\)

Indeed, apply concavity to \(w=\alpha^{-1}(\alpha w)+(1-\alpha^{-1})0\).

Suppose \(z,w\) are fixed points in the box. Both are at least \(x>0\). If

\(\displaystyle \alpha=\max_t z_t/w_t>1,\)

choose \(i\) attaining that maximum. Monotonicity and the preceding inequality yield

\(\displaystyle z_i=\Phi_{x,i}(z) \le\Phi_{x,i}(\alpha w) \le\alpha w_i-(\alpha-1)x_i <\alpha w_i=z_i,\)

a contradiction. Thus \(z\le w\); interchanging them proves equality.

3.2 A convex formulation

Define

\(\displaystyle K_x=\{y\in[0,\mu]:y\le\Phi_x(y)\}.\)

This is compact and convex, because its nonlinear constraints are

\(\displaystyle g_t(y):=y_t-\mu_t+(\mu_t-x_t)e^{-(By)_t}\le0,\)

and each \(g_t\) is convex.

If \(y\in K_x\), iteration of \(\Phi_x\) starting at \(y\) increases to a fixed point. By uniqueness, that fixed point is \(y(x)\). Hence \(y\le y(x)\). Since \(y(x)\in K_x\),

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x)=\max_{y\in K_x}\sum_t y_t.\)

Thus evaluation is a convex optimization problem, even though optimization over the seed vector \(x\) is not.

3.3 Bit complexity of evaluation

We verify the hypotheses of the standard weak-membership-to-weak-optimization theorem: a compact convex body with a polynomial-time weak membership oracle and explicitly given rational inner and outer balls admits polynomial-time weak linear optimization. The running time is polynomial in the encoding lengths of those balls and the requested accuracy. This is the convex-body oracle theorem of Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver; see [Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

Let

\(\displaystyle q=\min_t x_t>0,\qquad c=x/2,\qquad L=1+\max_t\mu_t\sum_s B_{ts}.\)

On the box, the Euclidean norm of each \(\nabla g_t\) is at most \(L\). Also, \(\Phi_x(c)\ge x\), so

\(\displaystyle g_t(c)\le-x_t/2\le-q/2.\)

The distance from \(c\) to every face of the box is at least \(q/2\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \mathbb B(c,q/(4L))\subseteq K_x\subseteq\mathbb B(c,1).\)

The outer inclusion follows because the box has Euclidean diameter at most \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). These rational ball parameters have polynomial encoding length.

For completeness, a weak membership oracle follows directly from approximate evaluation of the constraints. Set \(m=q/2\), and let the requested membership tolerance be \(0<\eta\le1\).

  • A point outside the box is outside \(K_x\).
  • For \(v\) inside the box, evaluate every \(g_t(v)\) with error at most \(\delta=m\eta/4\).
  • If some approximation exceeds \(\delta\), its true constraint value is positive, so \(v\notin K_x\).
  • Otherwise all true constraint values are at most \(2\delta\). With

\(\theta=2\delta/(m+2\delta)\), convexity gives
\(\displaystyle g_t((1-\theta)v+\theta c)\le(1-\theta)2\delta-\theta m=0.\)
The resulting point belongs to \(K_x\) and is within distance
\(\theta\le2\delta/m\le\eta\) of \(v\).

These are valid weak membership answers: acceptance certifies distance at most \(\eta\), and rejection certifies exclusion from \(K_x\), hence also from its \(\eta\)-interior.

All required exponentials have nonpositive arguments. They can be approximated to \(p\) bits in polynomial time: sufficiently negative arguments can be truncated to zero, while the remaining bounded range admits the usual Taylor-series evaluation with range reduction.

The convex-body theorem therefore applies. A weak optimizer within distance \(\eta\) of \(K_x\), whose objective is at least the optimum minus \(\eta\), has objective at most the optimum plus \(\sqrt n\,\eta\). Taking \(\eta=\tau/(n+1)\) gives an additive-\(\tau\) estimate of \(F_\infty(x)\).

4. The approximation algorithm

Assume \(0<\varepsilon<1\), and define

\(\displaystyle K=\left\lceil\frac{2n}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,\qquad \Delta=\frac{\kappa}{K},\qquad M=K-n.\)

Reserve the strictly positive baseline

\(\displaystyle b_t=\min\{\Delta,\mu_t\}.\)

For a nonnegative integer vector \(z\), define

\(\displaystyle X_t(z)=\min\{\mu_t,b_t+\Delta z_t\},\qquad H(z)=F_\infty(X(z)).\)

The function \(H\) is nondecreasing and has diminishing returns on the integer lattice. To verify the latter, suppose \(z\le w\). An increment in coordinate \(i\) produces a physical increment at \(X(w)\) no larger than the one at \(X(z)\), because of the cap. Monotonicity first permits shortening the latter increment; the diminishing-returns inequality from Section 2 then compares the equal-length increments.

Start with \(z^0=0\). For \(M\) steps, evaluate all \(H(z^j+e_i)\) to additive accuracy

\(\displaystyle \tau=\frac{\varepsilon\kappa}{4K}\)

and choose a coordinate with maximum estimated value.

Every query has strictly positive seeds, so Section 3 applies. The output is feasible because

\(\displaystyle \sum_t X_t(z^M)\le\sum_t b_t+\Delta M \le n\Delta+(K-n)\Delta=\kappa.\)

4.1 Comparison with an arbitrary feasible solution

Fix any feasible \(x\), and put

\(\displaystyle z_t^*=\left\lfloor x_t/\Delta\right\rfloor.\)

Then \(\sum_t z_t^*\le K\) and \(X(z^*)\ge x\). For the latter assertion:

  • if \(\mu_t<\Delta\), the baseline already equals \(\mu_t\);
  • otherwise \(b_t=\Delta\), and \(\Delta(1+\lfloor x_t/\Delta\rfloor)\ge x_t\), before applying the cap.

For any current vector \(z\), monotonicity and diminishing returns imply

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x)-H(z) \le H(z+z^*)-H(z) \le\sum_t z_t^*\bigl(H(z+e_t)-H(z)\bigr).\)

Thus the best one-step gain is at least \((F_\infty(x)-H(z))/K\). Taking the supremum over feasible \(x\), it is at least

\(\displaystyle \frac{\operatorname{OPT}-H(z)}{K}.\)

No attainment assumption is used.

4.2 Guarantee with evaluation errors

Choosing the largest estimated successor value loses at most \(2\tau\) relative to the best true successor. Writing \(H_j=H(z^j)\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle H_{j+1}\ge H_j+\frac{\operatorname{OPT}-H_j}{K}-2\tau.\)

Iteration, using \(H_0\ge0\), gives

\(\displaystyle H_M\ge \left(1-\left(1-\frac1K\right)^M\right)\operatorname{OPT}-2K\tau \ge(1-e^{-M/K})\operatorname{OPT}-2K\tau.\)

Let \(a=n/K\le\varepsilon/2\). Since the derivative of \(e^{-1+a}\) is at most \(1\) for \(0\le a\le1\),

\(\displaystyle 1-e^{-M/K}=1-e^{-1+a}\ge1-e^{-1}-a \ge1-e^{-1}-\varepsilon/2.\)

A feasible vector of total mass \(\kappa\) exists, and its influence is at least \(\kappa\), so \(\operatorname{OPT}\ge\kappa\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle 2K\tau=\varepsilon\kappa/2\le\varepsilon\operatorname{OPT}/2.\)

Combining the inequalities proves

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(X(z^M))\ge(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\operatorname{OPT}.\)

There are \(nM\) value queries. All query vectors and precision parameters have polynomial encoding length, and \(K\) is polynomial in \(n\) and \(1/\varepsilon\). This proves the claimed polynomial running time.

5. Exact optimization is hard on bidirected cubic graphs

We reduce from Independent Set on simple cubic graphs, which is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The restriction to regular degree three is covered by [Independence and Irredundance in \(k\)-Regular Graphs](https://combinatorialpress.com/ars-articles/volume-049-ars-articles/independence-and-irredundance-in-k-regular-graphs/).

Let \(G\) have \(n\) vertices, adjacency matrix \(A\), and independence number \(\alpha(G)\). Given an integer \(2\le k\le n\), construct

\(\displaystyle \mu_i=\frac1n,\qquad \kappa=\frac kn,\qquad B=\gamma A,\qquad \gamma=\frac1{100}. \)

The supplied graph has two opposite arcs for each edge of \(G\), and no self-loops.

5.1 A graph quadratic inequality

For every \(v\ge0\),

\(\displaystyle v^\mathsf T(I+A)v\ge \frac{(\sum_i v_i)^2}{\alpha(G)}.\)

Here is a proof. Normalize \(\sum_i v_i=1\), and select a minimizer of \(v^\mathsf T(I+A)v\) having minimum support.

If adjacent vertices \(i,j\) both have positive mass, redistribute their combined mass between them. Their internal quadratic contribution is

\(\displaystyle v_i^2+v_j^2+2v_iv_j=(v_i+v_j)^2,\)

which stays fixed. All terms involving other vertices are linear in the redistributed mass. An endpoint of the redistribution interval therefore does not increase the objective and removes a support vertex, contradicting minimality of the support.

The support is consequently independent. On an independent support of size at most \(\alpha(G)\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\(\displaystyle v^\mathsf T(I+A)v=\sum_i v_i^2\ge1/\alpha(G).\)

Rescaling proves the inequality.

Now suppose \(0\le x_i\le1/n\) and \(\sum_i x_i=k/n\). Since \(\sum_i x_i^2\le k/n^2\),

\(\displaystyle x^\mathsf T A x \ge\frac{k^2}{n^2\alpha(G)}-\frac{k}{n^2}.\)

In particular:

  • If \(\alpha(G)\ge k\), setting \(x_i=1/n\) on an independent set of size \(k\), and zero elsewhere, gives \(x^\mathsf T A x=0\).
  • If \(\alpha(G)\le k-1\), every such \(x\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle x^\mathsf T A x\ge \frac{k}{n^2(k-1)}\ge\frac1{n^2}.\)

5.2 A uniform expansion with a certified error

Let \(y=y(x)\), \(d=y-x\), and \(h=\gamma Ay\). The fixed-point equation gives

\(\displaystyle d_i=(1/n-x_i)(1-e^{-h_i}).\)

Since \(y_i\le1/n\) and every vertex has degree three,

\(\displaystyle 0\le h_i\le3\gamma/n,\qquad \sum_i d_i\le\frac{\gamma}{n}\sum_i(Ay)_i =\frac{3\gamma}{n}F_\infty(x)\le\frac{3\gamma}{n}.\)

Using \(0\le h-(1-e^{-h})\le h^2/2\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x) =\sum_i x_i+\gamma\sum_i(1/n-x_i)(Ax)_i+R(x). \)

To bound the remainder, replacing \(Ay\) by \(Ax\) introduces the nonnegative term

\(\displaystyle P=\gamma\sum_i(1/n-x_i)(Ad)_i \le\frac{3\gamma}{n}\sum_i d_i \le\frac{9\gamma^2}{n^2}. \)

The exponential approximation subtracts a nonnegative term bounded by

\(\displaystyle N\le\frac12\sum_i(1/n-x_i)h_i^2 \le\frac{9\gamma^2}{2n^2}. \)

Thus \(R=P-N\), and

\(\displaystyle |R(x)|\le\frac{9\gamma^2}{n^2} \le\frac{\gamma}{10n^2}.\)

For full-budget vectors, cubic regularity gives

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(x)=C-\gamma x^\mathsf T A x+R(x), \qquad C=\frac kn+\frac{3\gamma k}{n^2}. \)

By monotonicity, every feasible vector can be extended to a full-budget vector without decreasing its objective. It therefore suffices to optimize over full-budget vectors.

5.3 The separating gap

The preceding bounds imply

\(\displaystyle \alpha(G)\ge k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{OPT}\ge C-\frac{\gamma}{10n^2}, \)

whereas

\(\displaystyle \alpha(G)<k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{OPT}\le C-\frac{9\gamma}{10n^2}. \)

Hence the rational threshold

\(\displaystyle Q=C-\frac{\gamma}{2n^2}\)

separates the two cases, with an inverse-polynomial gap. Deciding whether the optimal value exceeds \(Q\) is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

There is no nonattainment or numerical-evaluation obstruction in this reduction. For every \(x\), the map \(\Phi_x\) is a contraction in the maximum norm with factor at most \(3\gamma/n<1\). Its fixed point is unique and continuous in \(x\): directly,

\(\displaystyle \|y(x)-y(z)\|_\infty \le\|x-z\|_\infty+\frac{3\gamma}{n}\|y(x)-y(z)\|_\infty. \)

The maximum is thus attained on the compact feasible set. The contraction also permits polynomial-time evaluation to inverse-polynomial accuracy, including at seed vectors with zero coordinates.

In particular, an algorithm producing a feasible point within additive error \(\gamma/(10n^2)\) of optimum would decide the source problem, using objective evaluation to the same accuracy.

Since \(F_\infty\le1\), a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme with accuracy parameter \(\gamma/(10n^2)\) would provide that additive guarantee. Therefore no such scheme exists unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

6. Why a maximum need not exist

Consider two types with

\(\displaystyle \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad \kappa=\frac14,\qquad \lambda_{11}=8, \)

and every other interaction coefficient zero.

Write \(u=x_1\). Type two has no interactions, so its final infected mass equals \(x_2\). For \(u>0\), let \(f(u)\) be type one’s final mass. By the uniqueness argument above, it is the unique solution of

\(\displaystyle f(u)=\frac12-\left(\frac12-u\right)e^{-8f(u)}.\)

For \(h=7/16\),

\(\displaystyle \frac12-\left(\frac12-u\right)e^{-8h} \ge\frac12-\frac12e^{-7/2}>\frac7{16}, \)

because \(e^{7/2}>8\). Iteration from \(h\) and uniqueness therefore show \(f(u)>7/16\).

Implicit differentiation is valid because

\(\displaystyle 1-8(1/2-f(u))>1/2.\)

It yields

\(\displaystyle f'(u)=\frac{e^{-8f(u)}}{1-8(1/2-f(u))}<\frac14. \)

Any maximizer would use the full budget: otherwise increasing \(x_2\) increases the objective. On that budget line, for \(u>0\),

\(\displaystyle F_\infty(u,1/4-u)=f(u)+1/4-u,\)

whose derivative is strictly negative. Thus every feasible point with \(u>0\) and full budget is improved by slightly decreasing \(u\) and increasing \(x_2\).

At \(u=0\), the original iteration remains identically zero in type one, so the full-budget value is only \(1/4\). Every positive-\(u\) full-budget point has value greater than \(7/16\).

There is consequently no maximizing vector. This explains why the supremum formulation is necessary.

7. Scope of the conclusion

The requested \((1-1/e-\varepsilon)\) guarantee is achievable in polynomial time, while exact optimal-value computation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even on the restricted graphs above. These statements follow from the continuous model itself.

The proof does not establish that \(1-1/e\) is the best possible constant approximation factor. Hardness of beating that factor in discrete Independent Cascade does not, without a further reduction, establish such hardness here.
