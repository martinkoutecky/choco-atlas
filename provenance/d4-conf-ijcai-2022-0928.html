The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite goods \(G\) with common values \(v(g)\), restricted-additive interest types \(I_t\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\), find masses \(z_{t,B}\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(B\) satisfying \(\sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}B_gz_{t,B}=q_g\), such that every positive-mass type-\(t\) bundle \(B\) weakly prefers its bundle to every positive-mass bundle \(B'\) after deleting any valued copy of one good, and no feasible reassignment of mass to integral bundles Pareto-improves the allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) contain \(m\) good categories. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) interest types \(I_t\subseteq G\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Common values \(v(g)\) and per-capita supplies \(q_g\geq0\) are binary rationals; \(v(g)>0\) for goods valued by some type, and \(v(g)=0\) otherwise. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An integral bundle is \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{G}\), allowing multiple copies without a per-person cap; its utility to type \(t\) is \(u_t(B)=\sum_{g\in I_t}v(g)B_g\).
An allocation is a finitely supported family of nonnegative rational masses \(z_{t,B}\) satisfying \(\sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}B_gz_{t,B}=q_g\) for every \(g\).
It satisfies \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\) if, whenever \(z_{t,B}>0\) and \(z_{s,B'}>0\), one has \(u_t(B)\geq u_t(B'-e_g)\) for every \(g\in I_t\) with \(v(g)>0\) and \(B'_g\geq1\), where \(e_g\) denotes one copy of \(g\). Comparisons include different bundles held by the same type.
Pareto efficiency means that no reassignment of individuals to integral bundles, preserving types and aggregate supplies, weakly increases utility almost everywhere and strictly increases it on positive mass; reassignment may split each original bundle class and need not satisfy fairness.

Can an allocation satisfying \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\) and Pareto efficiency be found, or infeasibility reported, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computational problem NP-hard?

For finitely many agents and individually listed goods, a complete \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\), Pareto-efficient allocation can be computed in polynomial time. [Akrami–Rezvan–Seddighin](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.13676v2)
No result settling this exact mass-allocation problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one so that supplies are per capita.
  • Interpreted goods as categories with integral, unrestricted copy counts in each bundle, following the continuous question's copy terminology.
  • Specified binary-rational common values and nonnegative binary-rational supplies; retained positive values for valued goods and the anchor's zero-value convention for universally unvalued goods.
  • Specified finite support and rational masses for the allocation's computational representation.
  • Interpreted Pareto improvement at the individual level, allowing original bundle classes to split, requiring no utility loss almost everywhere and strict improvement on positive mass.
  • Included comparisons between distinct bundle classes of the same type and made deletion depend on positive value to the comparing type.
  • Included reporting infeasibility because the continuous question supplies no existence promise.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable, and every instance satisfying the stated hypotheses is feasible.

More precisely, an allocation satisfying \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\) and Pareto efficiency can be constructed with at most \(m+\tau\) positive type–bundle classes. Its entire explicit binary encoding has polynomial length.

The algorithm compresses the descending-value greedy rule from [Akrami–Rezvan–Seddighin, Algorithm 4 and Theorem 7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.13676v2). The key is to process all copies of one category in a single batch, without expanding the population or the supplies.

1. Exact scaling to an implicit finite instance

Write every input rational in numerator–denominator form. Let \(N\) be the product of the denominators appearing in the type masses and supplies. Then

\[ \]
\(n_t=N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\qquad K_g=Nq_g\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0},\qquad \sum_t n_t=N.\)

Thus we may consider an implicit finite instance with \(N\) individuals, including \(n_t\) individuals of type \(t\), and \(K_g\) copies of category \(g\). These numbers may be exponentially large, but their binary lengths are polynomial in \(L\).

Likewise, let \(P\) be the product of the denominators of the common values, and put \(w_g=Pv(g)\). Every \(w_g\) is a nonnegative integer. Multiplying all utilities by the positive number \(P\) preserves the fairness inequalities.

We represent the implicit individuals by cohorts. A cohort is a triple \((t,B,n)\): it consists of \(n\) individuals of type \(t\), all holding bundle \(B\). Initially there are \(\tau\) cohorts, namely \((t,0,n_t)\).

After constructing the finite allocation implicitly, a cohort \((t,B,n)\) contributes mass \(n/N\) to \(z_{t,B}\). Identical type–bundle pairs can be merged.

2. Nonwaste implies the required Pareto efficiency

Call an allocation *nonwasteful* if every copy with positive common value is assigned to an interested type. Categories with zero common value impose no restriction.

For any feasible mass allocation, total utility satisfies

\( \displaystyle \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}u_t(B)\leq \sum_g v(g)q_g=:W. \)

Indeed, each copy contributes either its common value or zero to its holder’s utility. A nonwasteful allocation attains equality.

This proves Pareto efficiency even under the reassignment notion in the question. Any supply-preserving reassignment still has total utility at most \(W\). If it weakly improved utility almost everywhere and strictly improved it on positive mass, its total utility would exceed \(W\).

To justify that last inference explicitly, let \(\Delta\) be the individual utility gain. Under such a reassignment, \(\Delta\) is nonnegative almost everywhere, and \(\{\Delta>0\}\) has positive mass. Since

\( \displaystyle \{\Delta>0\}=\bigcup_{k\geq1}\{\Delta\geq 1/k\}, \)

one of these sets has positive mass, so the integral of \(\Delta\) is positive. All utilities are integrable because supplies and common values are finite and nonnegative.

Consequently, it suffices to construct a nonwasteful \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\) allocation.

3. The finite greedy rule preserves fairness

Process copies in nonincreasing order of their common values. Give each positive-valued copy to a minimum-utility individual among those interested in its category. Zero-valued copies may be assigned arbitrarily.

Here is the fairness argument, including repeated copies. Use scaled utilities, and write \(V(B)=\sum_g w_gB_g\).

Suppose a copy of \(g\) is added to individual \(i\)’s bundle. Before this addition, nonwastefulness gives \(u_i(B_i)=V(B_i)\). Only comparisons whose target is the updated bundle need checking, because nobody’s own utility decreases.

If observer \(j\) does not value \(g\), the required inequalities follow from the previous allocation. If \(j\) values \(g\), greedy choice gives \(u_j(B_j)\geq u_i(B_i)\). For every copy \(h\) valued by \(j\) in the updated bundle, processing order gives \(w_h\geq w_g\). Therefore

\( \displaystyle u_j(B_i+e_g-e_h)\leq V(B_i)+w_g-w_h\leq V(B_i)=u_i(B_i)\leq u_j(B_j). \)

The observer’s utility after the addition is at least the last expression, including when \(j=i\). Zero-valued additions affect no utilities or required comparisons.

Starting from empty bundles, induction proves \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\). The rule also preserves nonwastefulness.

The remaining issue is implementing this rule without performing one iteration per copy.

4. Processing one category in polynomial time

Fix a positive-valued category \(g\), with scaled value \(w=w_g>0\) and supply \(K=K_g>0\). Consider the current eligible cohorts, indexed by \(j\). Let their sizes and current utilities be \(n_j\) and \(U_j\).

4.1. Marginal priorities

For each individual initially having utility \(U_j\), the priorities of successive copies of this category are

\( U_j,\ U_j+w,\ U_j+2w,\ldots. \)

The greedy rule selects the first \(K\) entries from the union of these sequences, counting every cohort sequence with multiplicity \(n_j\).

This characterization is exact: an individual’s successive priorities strictly increase. Hence ordering the marginal entries by priority always selects that individual’s preceding entries first, and each selected entry corresponds to a currently available greedy choice.

Write

\( U_j=wh_j+f_j,\qquad h_j=\lfloor U_j/w\rfloor,\qquad 0\leq f_j<w. \)

For an integer \(a\geq0\), define

\( \displaystyle F(a)=\sum_j n_j\max\{0,a-h_j\}. \)

This is precisely the number of marginal entries with priority strictly below \(aw\). For one individual in cohort \(j\), those entries have indices \(0,\ldots,a-h_j-1\) when \(a>h_j\), and there are none otherwise.

4.2. Find the cutoff interval

Find an integer \(a\) satisfying

\( F(a)\leq K<F(a+1). \)

This takes polynomial time by binary search. We have \(F(0)=0\), and the integer

\( H=\max_j h_j+K+1 \)

satisfies \(F(H)>K\). Thus binary search on the integer interval from zero to \(H\) yields the desired adjacent cutoff values.

Give every individual in eligible cohort \(j\) the base number

\( k_j=\max\{0,a-h_j\} \)

of copies. This assigns exactly \(F(a)\) copies. Put \(R=K-F(a)\).

The remaining relevant priorities lie in the interval from \(aw\), inclusive, to \((a+1)w\), exclusive. Each cohort with \(h_j\leq a\) contributes exactly one such entry per individual, with priority \(aw+f_j\). Cohorts with \(h_j>a\) contribute none. Consequently,

\( \displaystyle 0\leq R<F(a+1)-F(a)=\sum_{j:h_j\leq a}n_j. \)

Sort the cohorts with \(h_j\leq a\) by \(f_j\), breaking ties by any fixed cohort order. Assign one additional copy to individuals in this order until exactly \(R\) additional copies have been assigned.

4.3. At most one cohort splits

Each eligible cohort receives its base number of copies uniformly. In the additional-copy step, each cohort receives either:

  • one extra copy for every individual;
  • no extra copies; or
  • one extra copy for only some of its individuals.

Only the cohort at the stopping point can fall into the third case. If it has size \(n_j\) and exactly \(s\) individuals receive the extra copy, replace it by cohorts

\( (t_j,B_j+(k_j+1)e_g,s) \)

and

\( (t_j,B_j+k_je_g,n_j-s), \)

omitting any zero-size cohort. Thus processing one category increases the number of cohorts by at most one.

The batch selects all priorities below \(aw\), followed by the first \(R\) priorities in the next interval. It therefore implements a valid sequence of individual greedy choices. The fairness proof in Section 3 applies exactly.

4.4. Zero-valued categories

For a category with zero common value, write

\( K_g=bN+R,\qquad 0\leq R<N. \)

Give every individual \(b\) copies. Then give one additional copy to \(R\) individuals, following any fixed order of the current cohorts. Again, at most one cohort splits. Utilities and fairness remain unchanged.

Categories with zero supply require no operation.

5. Support size and bit complexity

Initially there are \(\tau\) cohorts. Each of the \(m\) categories increases this number by at most one. Hence the final allocation has at most \(m+\tau\) positive type–bundle classes.

We now check the sizes of all numbers used.

The integers \(N\), \(P\), \(n_t\), \(K_g\), and \(w_g\) have bit lengths bounded by a constant multiple of \(L\): their defining products involve input denominators whose total binary length is at most \(L\).

Throughout the algorithm:

  • cohort sizes are positive integers at most \(N\);
  • every bundle coordinate satisfies \(B_g\leq K_g\);
  • every cohort utility satisfies \(U_j\leq\sum_g w_gK_g\).

Therefore these numbers, and the quotients and remainders used in batching, have polynomial bit length. The binary-search upper bound \(H\) also has polynomial bit length, so binary search takes polynomially many iterations.

Each evaluation of \(F\) uses at most \(m+\tau\) terms. Sorting, updating bundle vectors, and splitting one cohort also take polynomial time. There are only \(m\) category batches.

Finally, every output mass is \(n/N\), and every output bundle has \(m\) polynomial-length coordinates. Thus the complete explicit output has polynomial length.

6. Correctness of the mass allocation

For each type \(t\), the final cohort sizes sum to \(n_t\). For each category \(g\), the final bundles contain exactly \(K_g\) copies in aggregate. Dividing cohort sizes by \(N\) therefore gives

\( \displaystyle \sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B}B_gz_{t,B}=q_g. \)

Every positive output class contains at least one implicit individual. The individual greedy allocation satisfies all required \(\mathsf{EFX}^{+}\) comparisons, so the mass allocation satisfies the support-wide comparisons as well. Comparison of a bundle with itself is immediate.

Every positive-valued copy is assigned to an interested type. Section 2 therefore proves Pareto efficiency against arbitrary permitted mass-splitting reassignments.

This establishes a polynomial-time construction for every valid input; no infeasible instance arises under the stated premises.
