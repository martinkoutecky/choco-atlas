The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), predictor scores \(f\), and \(c\in(0,1)\), choose a rule \(\Pi\) that sees \((T,\mu,f)\) and returns a lottery \(q^\Pi\) over types. For unknown type utilities \(u_t\in[0,1]\) with \(\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s>0\), define \(C_\mu(f,u)=\frac{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s(\mathbf{1}\{f_t>f_s\}+\frac12\mathbf{1}\{f_t=f_s\})}{\sum_{u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s}\). What is the maximum, over such rules, of the worst-case advantage \(\inf_{T,\mu,f,u:\,C_\mu(f,u)\ge c}\left(\sum_t q_t^\Pi u_t-\sum_t\mu_tu_t\right)\) over uniform selection from the population?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge2\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The support size \(\tau\) counts listed types, not all possible types.
Each type has an observed binary-rational predictor score \(f_t\ge0\) and an unknown utility \(u_t\in[0,1]\), shared by its entire mass; higher scores predict higher utility. There is one item to allocate, with no censoring.
Fix \(c\in(0,1)\). A rule \(\Pi\), allowed to depend on \(c\), observes only \((T,\mu,f)\) and returns a lottery \(q^\Pi\in\Delta(T)\). Rules have no computational restriction; running-time parameters, if relevant, are \(\tau\) and the total input bit length \(L\), with no separate parameter \(m\).
Define \(D_\mu(u)=\sum_{t,s:\,u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s\), and admit only utilities with \(D_\mu(u)>0\).
Define \(C_\mu(f,u)=D_\mu(u)^{-1}\sum_{t,s:\,u_t>u_s}\mu_t\mu_s\bigl(\mathbf1\{f_t>f_s\}+\tfrac12\mathbf1\{f_t=f_s\}\bigr)\). Utility ties are excluded; predictor ties receive half credit.
Uniform population selection has type lottery \(\mu\), so the advantage of \(\Pi\) is \(\sum_t(q_t^\Pi-\mu_t)u_t\).

What is the exact value, for every \(c\in(0,1)\), of \(V(c)=\sup_\Pi\inf_{T,\mu,f,u:\,D_\mu(u)>0,\ C_\mu(f,u)\ge c}\sum_t(q_t^\Pi-\mu_t)u_t\), where the infimum ranges over all finite societies satisfying the stated domains?

For finite lists of candidates, any randomized selection algorithm using a predictor with C-index at least \(c<1\) can fail to guarantee greater expected utility than uniform selection. [Verified discrete statement](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02671v1).
No result settling this precise mass formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized masses to sum to one so that the stated population baseline is an expected utility.
  • Specified nonnegative binary-rational predictor scores, with larger values predicting larger utility, to give finite input encodings consistent with survival prediction.
  • Treated c as a fixed known threshold and allowed the rule to depend on it, without imposing a computational restriction.
  • Interpreted each type as homogeneous in both predictor score and unknown utility, and allowed any lottery over the positive-mass support.
  • Used a supremum over rules rather than assuming that a maximizing rule exists.
  • Made explicit that support sizes are unbounded across the adversary's instances and that there is no censoring or separate parameter m.

Exact value

For every \(c\in(0,1)\),
\[ V(c)=0. \]

Population selection, defined by \(q^\Pi=\mu\) on every observed society, has advantage exactly \(0\) for every admissible utility vector. Hence \(V(c)\ge0\).

The literal upper bound follows from scaling: fix two types with \(\mu_1=\mu_2=\tfrac12\), scores \(f_1=0,f_2=1\), and utilities \(u_1=0,u_2=\varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\in(0,1]\). Then \(D_\mu(u)=\tfrac14\) and \(C_\mu(f,u)=1\). For any rule, its lottery on this fixed observed input has advantage \(\varepsilon(q^\Pi_2-\tfrac12)\), which tends to \(0\) as \(\varepsilon\) tends to \(0\). Thus every rule has worst-case advantage at most \(0\).

This settles the stated minimax value. The more substantive allocation question is whether the impossibility survives a fixed utility range. It does, as proved next.

Stronger result with utility range fixed

Proven claim. For every \(c\in(0,1)\), there is a single observed society such that every lottery different from population selection has strictly negative advantage for some admissible utility vector satisfying \(\min_t u_t=0\) and \(\max_t u_t=1\). The utilities and predictor scores can both be strictly ordered.

This gives a direct proof under the mass formulation of the related impossibility stated in [Proposition 2 of the linked paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02671v1#S3.SS1).

Construction of the observed society

Choose a power of two \(n\) satisfying \(n>2/(1-c)\). In particular, \(n\ge4\). Set
\[ T=\{1,\ldots,n\},\qquad \mu_j=\frac1n,\qquad f_j=j. \]

These are exactly \(n\) listed types. Their masses are positive dyadic rationals summing to \(1\), and their scores are nonnegative binary rationals.

Fix any lottery \(q\in\Delta(T)\) on this observed society. If \(q=\mu\), its advantage is identically \(0\).

Suppose instead that \(q\ne\mu\). Since \(\sum_j(q_j-\tfrac1n)=0\), there is an index \(i\) such that
\[ \delta=q_i-\frac1n>0. \]
Set \(\varepsilon=\delta/2\).

List the remaining indices in increasing score order as \(j_1<\cdots<j_{n-1}\). Define
\[ u_i=0,\qquad u_{j_r}=1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon\frac{r-1}{n-2} \quad\text{for }r\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}. \]

Because \(0<\varepsilon<1\), all utilities lie in \([0,1]\) and are distinct. Their minimum is \(0\), their maximum is \(1\), and all types other than \(i\) have utilities increasing with their scores.

Verification of concordance

Since utilities are distinct, every unordered pair contributes exactly one ordered pair to the definition of \(D_\mu(u)\). Therefore
\[ D_\mu(u)=\frac{\binom n2}{n^2}=\frac{n-1}{2n}>0. \]

Every pair excluding \(i\) is concordant. For a pair involving \(i\), the utility of \(i\) is lower than that of the other type. Such a pair is discordant precisely when the other index is smaller than \(i\). Consequently there are exactly \(i-1\) discordant unordered pairs. Predictor ties do not occur, so
\[ C_\mu(f,u) =1-\frac{i-1}{\binom n2} \ge1-\frac{n-1}{\binom n2} =1-\frac2n >c. \]

Thus the constructed utility vector is admissible.

Verification of negative advantage

The selected type \(i\) has utility \(0\), and every other utility is at most \(1\). Hence
\[ \sum_j q_j u_j\le1-q_i. \]

Every type other than \(i\) has utility at least \(1-\varepsilon\). Population selection therefore has expected utility at least
\[ \sum_j\mu_j u_j\ge(1-\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac1n\right). \]

Subtracting gives
\[ \sum_j(q_j-\mu_j)u_j \le-\delta+\varepsilon\left(1-\frac1n\right) \le-\delta+\varepsilon =-\frac{\delta}{2}<0. \]

This proves the stronger claim.

Conclusion

For each \(c\in(0,1)\), every rule encounters an admissible society on which its advantage is nonpositive, even when utilities have minimum \(0\) and maximum \(1\). Population selection attains advantage \(0\) everywhere. Therefore the exact value is \(V(c)=0\), with the supremum attained by population selection.
