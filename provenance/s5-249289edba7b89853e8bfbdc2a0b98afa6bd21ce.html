The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational claims \(v_t\), and a rational supply \(\kappa\) of positions per recipient, compute an allocation maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\ge0}x_{t,j}v_t/(2j+1)\), subject to \(0\le x_{t,j}\le\mu_t\), \(x_{t,j+1}\le x_{t,j}\), and \(\sum_{t,j}x_{t,j}=\kappa\). Here \(x_{t,j}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) recipients receiving at least \(j+1\) positions, so each recipient receives an integral bundle; return an optimal allocation compactly, including any mass split at a tied cutoff.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|T|\ge1\) types with positive mass, each mass encoded as a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Masses satisfy \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each type has a positive binary-rational claim \(v_t\), and the supply per recipient is a positive binary rational \(\kappa\).
Recipients receive bundles in \(\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), with no minimum entitlement or upper bound. A type may split its mass among different bundle sizes.
Let \(x_{t,j}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving at least \(j+1\) positions, for \(j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). Feasibility requires \(0\le x_{t,j}\le\mu_t\), \(x_{t,j+1}\le x_{t,j}\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\ge0}x_{t,j}=\kappa\).
Compute an allocation maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\ge0}x_{t,j}v_t/(2j+1)\). Represent the allocation compactly by listing, for each type, its occupied bundle sizes as binary integers and their masses as binary rationals. Any optimal split among equal marginal priorities is allowed.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including claims, masses, and supply. State running times in \(\tau\) and \(L\); there is no separate candidate parameter \(m\).

Can an exact optimal allocation for this problem be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is computing one NP-hard?

For discrete recipients, selection of the \(k\)-th smallest element in a union of \(n\) increasing arithmetic sequences takes \(O(n)\) time under unit-cost arithmetic, yielding discrete Sainte-Laguë apportionment within that bound ([Cheng–Eppstein](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2603v1)).
A subsequent discrete divisor-apportionment algorithm also achieves linear time ([Reitzig–Wild](https://www.wild-inter.net/publications/reitzig-wild-2023.pdf)). No result settling this exact mass allocation problem was located in the search.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. An exact optimal allocation is computable in time polynomial in $\tau$ and $L$. More specifically, the algorithm below uses $O(\tau L)$ arithmetic operations on integers with $O(L)$ bits, hence $O(\tau L^3)$ bit operations with schoolbook arithmetic. Its output contains at most two bundle sizes per type, and at most one type is split.

The proof handles the rational masses directly; it does not assume a unit-cost arithmetic model.

1. Exact weighted counting

Write each claim as $v_t=a_t/b_t$, where $a_t,b_t$ are positive integers given in binary. Define the inverse marginal priorities
$$ d_{t,j}=\frac{2j+1}{v_t},\qquad j\ge0. $$
Smaller inverse priority means larger objective coefficient. For each type, this sequence is strictly increasing.

For a rational $d\ge0$, the number of inverse priorities of type $t$ at most $d$ is
$$ N_t(d) =\#\{j\ge0:d_{t,j}\le d\} =\left\lfloor\frac{v_td+1}{2}\right\rfloor. $$
Indeed, $d_{t,j}\le d$ is equivalent to $2j+1\le v_td$; the displayed floor counts exactly the nonnegative integers satisfying this inequality.

Define the weighted count
$$ F(d)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tN_t(d). $$
Thus $F(d)$ is the total supply consumed by assigning full type mass to every marginal position whose inverse priority is at most $d$. It is nondecreasing and can be evaluated exactly using $O(\tau)$ arithmetic operations.

To implement comparisons without rational summation, let $Q$ be the product of the input denominators of all masses and of $\kappa$. Set
$$ W_t=Q\mu_t,\qquad K=Q\kappa. $$
These are integers, and
$$ F(d)<\kappa \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_tW_tN_t(d)<K. $$
Because the total denominator encoding length is at most $L$, the integer $Q$ has $O(L)$ bits.

2. A bounded search interval and separated breakpoints

Let
$$ \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t,\qquad v_{\max}=\max_tv_t,\qquad B=\left\lceil\frac{\kappa}{\mu_{\min}}\right\rceil, \qquad U=\frac{2B-1}{v_{\max}}. $$
Since $\kappa>0$, we have $B\ge1$.

Choose a type $s$ with $v_s=v_{\max}$. Then
$$ N_s(U)=B, $$
so
$$ F(U)\ge\mu_sB\ge\mu_{\min}B\ge\kappa. $$
Also $F(0)=0<\kappa$. Consequently, the required cutoff lies in $(0,U]$.

For every type and every $d\in[0,U]$,
$$ v_td\le v_{\max}U=2B-1, $$
and therefore $N_t(d)\le B$. In particular, only finitely many breakpoints occur in this search interval.

Now set
$$ A=\max_ta_t. $$
Any two distinct breakpoints satisfy
$$ \left| \frac{(2j+1)b_t}{a_t} - \frac{(2i+1)b_s}{a_s} \right| = \frac{ \left|(2j+1)b_ta_s-(2i+1)b_sa_t\right| }{a_ta_s} \ge\frac1{A^2}. $$
The numerator is a nonzero integer. This separation bound applies even when the supplied fractions are unreduced.

3. Finding the exact cutoff

Initialize $\ell=0$ and $h=U$, maintaining
$$ F(\ell)<\kappa\le F(h). $$
At each step, compute $d=(\ell+h)/2$. If $F(d)<\kappa$, replace $\ell$ by $d$; otherwise replace $h$ by $d$. Stop when
$$ h-\ell<\frac1{A^2}. $$

The invariant implies that $(\ell,h]$ contains a breakpoint: otherwise all the counts, and hence $F$, would agree at its endpoints. The separation bound implies that this interval contains exactly one distinct breakpoint.

Compute
$$ n_t=N_t(\ell),\qquad r_t=\frac{2n_t+1}{v_t}. $$
For each type, $r_t$ is its first breakpoint strictly larger than $\ell$. Therefore
$$ r=\min_tr_t $$
is the unique breakpoint in $(\ell,h]$.

Let
$$ E=\{t:r_t=r\},\qquad M=\sum_t\mu_tn_t=F(\ell). $$
There are no breakpoints between $\ell$ and $r$, or between $r$ and $h$. At $r$, exactly the types in $E$ contribute one new marginal position. Hence
$$ M<\kappa\le M+\sum_{t\in E}\mu_t. $$
This identifies the cutoff and its entire tie group exactly.

4. Constructing the compact allocation

Set $R=\kappa-M$. Process the types in $E$ in any fixed order, assigning
$$ \alpha_t=\min\{\mu_t,R\} $$
and then replacing $R$ by $R-\alpha_t$. Set $\alpha_t=0$ for types outside $E$.

The preceding inequality guarantees that this process ends with $R=0$. Thus
$$ 0\le\alpha_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\alpha_t=\kappa-M. $$
All these quantities are exact rationals. Equivalently, the process can use integer residuals and the weights $W_t$, with denominator $Q$ throughout.

For type $t$, output:

  • bundle size $n_t$ with mass $\mu_t-\alpha_t$;
  • bundle size $n_t+1$ with mass $\alpha_t$.

Omit entries having zero mass. The corresponding tail allocation is
$$ x^*_{t,j}= \begin{cases} \mu_t,&0\le j<n_t,\\ \alpha_t,&j=n_t,\\ 0,&j>n_t. \end{cases} $$
It satisfies the bounds and monotonicity constraints. Its supply consumption is
$$ \sum_t\sum_{j\ge0}x^*_{t,j} =\sum_t(\mu_tn_t+\alpha_t) =M+(\kappa-M) =\kappa. $$

The sequential filling rule partially fills at most one tied type: a partial fill exhausts the remaining residual. Thus at most one type occupies two bundle sizes, and the entire output has at most $\tau+1$ entries.

5. Proof of global optimality

Define
$$ p_{t,j}=\frac{v_t}{2j+1}, \qquad \lambda=\frac1r>0. $$
The cutoff construction gives:

  • If $p_{t,j}>\lambda$, then $d_{t,j}<r$, so $j<n_t$ and $x^*_{t,j}=\mu_t$.
  • If $p_{t,j}<\lambda$, then $d_{t,j}>r$, so $x^*_{t,j}=0$.
  • If $p_{t,j}=\lambda$, then the position belongs to the cutoff tie group.

Let $y$ be any feasible allocation, including one with infinitely many nonzero tail entries. Since $0\le y_{t,j}\le\mu_t$, each coordinate satisfies
$$ (p_{t,j}-\lambda)(x^*_{t,j}-y_{t,j})\ge0. $$
For priorities above the cutoff, both factors are nonnegative. For priorities below it, both are nonpositive. At the cutoff, the first factor is zero.

All relevant infinite sums are absolutely convergent. Indeed,
$$ 0\le\sum_{t,j}p_{t,j}y_{t,j}\le v_{\max}\kappa, $$
and
$$ \sum_{t,j} \left|(p_{t,j}-\lambda)(x^*_{t,j}-y_{t,j})\right| \le (v_{\max}+\lambda)\sum_{t,j}(x^*_{t,j}+y_{t,j}) = 2(v_{\max}+\lambda)\kappa. $$
We may therefore sum the coordinate inequalities and separate the sums:
$$ \begin{aligned} 0 &\le \sum_{t,j}(p_{t,j}-\lambda)(x^*_{t,j}-y_{t,j})\\ &= \sum_{t,j}p_{t,j}x^*_{t,j} - \sum_{t,j}p_{t,j}y_{t,j} - \lambda\left(\sum_{t,j}x^*_{t,j}-\sum_{t,j}y_{t,j}\right)\\ &= \sum_{t,j}p_{t,j}x^*_{t,j} - \sum_{t,j}p_{t,j}y_{t,j}. \end{aligned} $$
The last equality uses equal total supply. Hence $x^*$ is globally optimal.

6. Bit complexity

Every positive input rational lies between $2^{-L}$ and $2^L$. Consequently,
$$ B\le 2^{2L}+1,\qquad U<2^{3L+2},\qquad A<2^L. $$
It follows that $O(L)$ halvings make the interval width less than $1/A^2$.

Every bisection point has the form
$$ d=U\frac{z}{2^i}, \qquad 0\le z\le2^i,\qquad i=O(L). $$
Using this representation, its numerator and denominator have $O(L)$ bits. Computing each $N_t(d)$ therefore requires multiplication and integer division on $O(L)$-bit integers.

The weighted sums also have $O(L)$ bits. In fact, throughout the search,
$$ 0\le\sum_tW_tN_t(d)\le B\sum_tW_t=BQ, $$
where $\sum_tW_t=Q$ follows from $\sum_t\mu_t=1$.

Thus each of the $O(L)$ search iterations costs $O(\tau L^2)$ bit operations with schoolbook arithmetic. Preprocessing, cutoff recovery, and output construction fit within the same overall bound:
$$ O(\tau L^3). $$
The output bundle sizes are at most $B+1$, and its masses can be written with denominator $Q$. Each entry therefore uses $O(L)$ bits, giving output length $O(\tau L)$.

The cited [Cheng–Eppstein](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2603v1) and [Reitzig–Wild](https://www.wild-inter.net/publications/reitzig-wild-2023.pdf) papers address discrete apportionment. The argument above supplies an exact polynomial bit-complexity bound for the rational-mass formulation itself.
