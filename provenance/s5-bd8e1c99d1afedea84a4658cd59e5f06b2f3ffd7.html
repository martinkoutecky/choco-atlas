The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant and position type sets \(I,J\), rational type masses, compatible pairs, strict rankings over counterpart types, and nonnegative rational rewards \(w_{ij}\), represent a matching by masses \(x_{ij}\) that can be realized by assigning each individual unit mass to one partner. Agents are indifferent among members of the same counterpart type and compare matchings by their assigned partner type, with being unmatched last. A matching is popular if, against every measurable re-matching of the same population, the total mass preferring it is at least the total mass preferring the challenger. Compute a popular matching maximizing \(\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}x_{ij}\), or give an approximation algorithm, with complexity measured in the finite type input and rational bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be disjoint nonempty applicant and position type sets, with compatibility relation \(E\subseteq I\times J\), strict rankings of compatible counterpart types on both sides, and rewards \(w_{ij}\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive binary rational masses \(a_i,b_j\), normalized by \(\sum_i a_i+\sum_j b_j=1\). Support size counts listed types, not possible types; let \(m=|E|\) and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Represent each type by a disjoint Lebesgue interval of its specified mass. A matching \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving bijection between measurable subsets of the applicant and position populations, using only compatible pairs; everyone outside those subsets is unmatched.
Individuals rank partners by type, are indifferent within a counterpart type, and rank every compatible partner above being unmatched. Both sides vote with their Lebesgue mass; indifference contributes no vote.
Write \(\phi(M,N)\) for the total mass strictly preferring its assignment in \(M\) to its assignment in \(N\). A matching is popular when \(\phi(M,N)\geq\phi(N,M)\) for every feasible measurable matching \(N\) of the same individuals, including challengers that match previously unmatched individuals.
The finite representation is \(x_{ij}\), the mass paired between types \(i,j\), satisfying \(x_{ij}\geq0\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq a_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq b_j\). It is popular if it admits a popular realization. Its reward is \(W(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\); let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be the supremum reward over popular realizations.
A \(\rho\)-approximation returns a rational popular mass matrix with reward at least \(\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

Can a \(\tfrac12\)-approximation be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)? Can any fixed ratio \(\rho\in(\tfrac12,1]\) be achieved in polynomial time, or is achieving such an improvement NP-hard?

For finite bipartite populations with strict individual preferences and nonnegative rewards, a polynomial-time \(\tfrac12\)-approximation exists, and any better ratio is impossible unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). [Faenza et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.11473)
Popular fractional matchings under expected pairwise voting admit polynomial-time minimum-cost optimization; their voting definition differs from comparisons against measurable reassignments of the same individuals here. [Kavitha](https://d-nb.info/1365619575/34)

Status and proved results

The general unequal-mass problem remains partially resolved. I interpret the substantive approximation question as restricted to instances that admit a popular realization: without that promise, a required popular output need not exist.

The following results are proved below:

  • Every fixed approximation ratio $\rho>\tfrac12$ is NP-hard, even for equal dyadic masses and instances admitting popular realizations.
  • Equal-mass instances admit a polynomial-time $\tfrac12$-approximation.
  • If the compatibility graph is a forest, arbitrary unequal rational masses admit polynomial-time exact optimization, including detection of nonexistence.
  • Every popular matrix admits a certificate whose category potentials belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$.

The forest algorithm advances the unequal-mass question, but does not settle it for general compatibility graphs.

1. Exact finite certificates for measurable popularity

Write $V=I\cup J$, and let $\mu_v$ denote the specified mass of type $v$. Write $\bot$ for being unmatched. For an assignment type $p$ and an assignment type $q$, define
$$ \operatorname{vote}_v(p,q)= \begin{cases} 1,&v\text{ strictly prefers }p\text{ to }q,\\ 0,&p=q,\\ -1,&v\text{ strictly prefers }q\text{ to }p. \end{cases} $$
Every compatible assignment is ranked above $\bot$.

For matchings $M,N$, set
$$ \Delta(N,M)=\phi(N,M)-\phi(M,N). $$
Thus $M$ is popular precisely when every feasible challenger has $\Delta(N,M)\leq0$.

1.1 Assignment categories and the challenger linear program

Fix a realization $M$ of a feasible matrix $x$. Partition each type into its incumbent assignment categories. An applicant category $(i,k)$ contains the type-$i$ individuals assigned to type $k$; its mass is $x_{ik}$. The unmatched category $(i,\bot)$ has mass
$$ r_i=a_i-\sum_jx_{ij}. $$
Position categories $(j,\ell)$ are defined symmetrically, with unmatched mass
$$ r_j=b_j-\sum_ix_{ij}. $$
Retain only positive-mass categories. Let their sets be $S,T$, and their masses be $h_s,h_t$.

A challenger can pair $s=(i,k)$ with $t=(j,\ell)$ exactly when $(i,j)\in E$. Denote this category-pair set by $H$. Define
$$ c_{st}=\operatorname{vote}_i(j,k)+\operatorname{vote}_j(i,\ell), $$
and
$$ d_s=\operatorname{vote}_i(\bot,k),\qquad d_t=\operatorname{vote}_j(\bot,\ell). $$
A matched category has $d=-1$, and an unmatched category has $d=0$.

The maximum challenger margin equals the optimum of
$$ \max\left( \sum_{(s,t)\in H}c_{st}y_{st} +\sum_{s\in S}d_su_s +\sum_{t\in T}d_tv_t \right) $$
subject to
$$ \sum_{t:(s,t)\in H}y_{st}+u_s=h_s\qquad(s\in S), $$
$$ \sum_{s:(s,t)\in H}y_{st}+v_t=h_t\qquad(t\in T), $$
$$ y_{st},u_s,v_t\geq0. $$

Every measurable challenger induces these variables by recording its category-pair masses and unmatched masses. Its votes give exactly the displayed objective.

Conversely, every feasible solution can be realized. Each category is an atomless Lebesgue space modulo null sets, so it can be partitioned into pieces of the prescribed masses. Equal-measure pieces admit measurable measure-preserving bijections modulo null sets. Pair the corresponding pieces and leave discarded null sets unmatched. The resulting matching has the prescribed margin.

There are at most $2m+\tau$ categories and polynomially many category pairs. For rational $x$, this is a polynomial-size rational linear program.

Consequently, popularity depends only on $x$, rather than on its particular realization, and rational matrices can be checked for popularity in polynomial time.

1.2 Dual certificate

The dual minimizes
$$ \sum_{s\in S}h_s\alpha_s+\sum_{t\in T}h_t\beta_t $$
subject to
$$ \alpha_s+\beta_t\geq c_{st}\qquad((s,t)\in H), $$
$$ \alpha_s\geq d_s,\qquad \beta_t\geq d_t. $$

The primal is feasible and bounded. In particular, the incumbent itself is a feasible challenger of margin zero. The dual is feasible, for example with every potential equal to $2$. Strong duality therefore applies.

If $x$ is popular, both optimal values are zero. Complementary slackness with the incumbent challenger gives
$$ \alpha_{i,j}+\beta_{j,i}=0 \qquad\text{whenever }x_{ij}>0, $$
and
$$ \alpha_{i,\bot}=0\quad\text{if }r_i>0,\qquad \beta_{j,\bot}=0\quad\text{if }r_j>0. $$

Conversely, dual-feasible potentials satisfying these identities have objective zero: the two potentials associated with each incumbent edge cancel, and unmatched categories contribute zero. Weak duality then excludes every positive challenger margin.

These inequalities and identities are therefore an exact popularity certificate.

1.3 Three-valued certificates

Proposition. Every popular matrix has a certificate in which every category potential belongs to $\{-1,0,1\}$, and every unmatched-category potential is zero.

Proof. Start with a certificate from Section 1.2. For each positive edge $e=(i,j)$, put
$$ p_e=\alpha_{i,j}=-\beta_{j,i}. $$
The lower bounds on both matched-category potentials imply
$$ -1\leq p_e\leq1. $$

Introduce a fixed symbol $p_0=0$ for unmatched categories. Every category-pair inequality becomes
$$ p_e-p_f\geq c $$
for some integer $c$, where either index may be $0$: applicant potentials are $p_e$, and position potentials are $-p_f$.

Replace every $p_e$ by $\lfloor p_e\rfloor$, keeping $p_0=0$. If $p_e-p_f\geq c$ and $c$ is integral, then
$$ p_e\geq p_f+c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lfloor p_e\rfloor\geq\lfloor p_f\rfloor+c. $$
Hence every inequality survives.

The new edge potentials belong to $\{-1,0,1\}$. Assign their negatives to the corresponding position categories. The matched-category lower bounds, unmatched zero potentials, and edge cancellation identities all remain valid. This is the required certificate. $\square$

2. Structural restrictions and attainment

2.1 At most two assignment categories per type

Lemma. In a popular realization, every type has positive mass in at most two assignment categories, counting $\bot$ as a category.

Proof. Suppose a type $v$ has three positive categories, with assignments
$$ p_1\succ_v p_2\succ_v p_3. $$
Choose equal positive masses $\varepsilon$ from these categories. Give the first group the assignments previously held by the third, the second those held by the first, and the third those held by the second.

This reassignment is feasible by atomlessness and measure-preserving bijections between the selected groups. If $p_3=\bot$, the first group becomes unmatched.

Two groups improve and one worsens, giving challenger margin $\varepsilon$ on type $v$. Every affected counterpart still receives a partner of type $v$, and is therefore indifferent. Everyone else keeps the same assignment.

Thus the challenger defeats the incumbent. $\square$

2.2 Popularity is inherited by the smallest containing face

Let $P$ be the feasible mass-matching polytope. For $x\in P$, its smallest containing face $F(x)$ consists of the feasible matrices $z$ satisfying
$$ z_{ij}=0\qquad\text{whenever }x_{ij}=0, $$
together with every type-capacity equality that is tight at $x$.

These are exactly the defining inequalities of $P$ tight at $x$, imposed as equalities.

Lemma. If $x$ is popular, every $z\in F(x)$ is popular.

Proof. A positive matched category of $z$ was already present at $x$, because $z$ introduces no new positive edges. A positive unmatched category of $z$ was also present at $x$, because every type saturated at $x$ remains saturated.

Restrict a certificate for $x$ to the categories present at $z$. Its inequalities remain valid: their coefficients depend only on category labels. Edge cancellations and unmatched zero potentials remain valid as well. Section 1.2 proves popularity of $z$. $\square$

There are finitely many faces of $P$. The popular set is consequently a union of finitely many faces, hence is compact. When it is nonempty, reward attains its maximum.

Moreover, an optimal popular matrix can be chosen to be a vertex of $P$: maximize reward over the face containing an optimal popular point, and choose an optimal vertex of that face. Face inheritance preserves popularity.

Such vertices have polynomial encoding length. Indeed, let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the input masses. Its bit length is bounded by the total input encoding length. After scaling by $D$, all capacities are integral. The bipartite vertex-edge incidence matrix is totally unimodular: negating the position rows converts it into an oriented incidence matrix. Thus scaled vertices are integral. Since every unscaled coordinate lies in $[0,1]$, each is an integer divided by $D$, with polynomial bit length.

2.3 Positive support at an optimal vertex

Lemma. The positive-edge support of any popular vertex consists of disjoint paths. Every internal path vertex is saturated, and each nonempty path has at least one saturated endpoint.

Proof. The two-category lemma bounds every positive-support degree by two.

A support cycle would admit an alternating perturbation by $\varepsilon$ and $-\varepsilon$ around the cycle. Both signs preserve every type sum and remain nonnegative for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$. This contradicts extremality. Thus every nonempty support component is a path.

An internal path vertex has two positive matched categories. If it had positive unmatched mass, it would have three categories, contradicting Section 2.1. It is therefore saturated.

If both endpoints of a support path had slack, an alternating perturbation along the path would preserve internal sums. Both signs would remain feasible for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, using the endpoint slacks and positive edge masses. This again contradicts extremality. $\square$

3. Exact polynomial-time optimization on forests

Theorem. If the compatibility graph $G=(V,E)$ is a forest, one can determine in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$ whether a popular realization exists and, if so, return a rational popular matrix of maximum reward.

The proof gives an explicit finite-state dynamic program.

3.1 Polynomially many candidate edge masses

Fix an edge $e=uv$. Removing $e$ separates its tree component into two parts.

For every vertex $v_0$ in the part containing $u$, consider the unique path
$$ v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1}=u,v_k=v. $$
Define
$$ q_1=\mu_{v_0},\qquad q_{t+1}=\mu_{v_t}-q_t\qquad(1\leq t<k). $$
Include $q_k$ in a candidate set $K_e$ if
$$ 0<q_k\leq\min(\mu_u,\mu_v). $$
Do the same for vertices on the other side, reversing the orientation.

There are at most $\tau$ candidate values per edge. They can be computed in polynomial time. Each is an alternating sum of input masses, so its bit length is polynomial in $L$.

Lemma. Every positive edge mass of a popular vertex belongs to its corresponding $K_e$.

Proof. By Section 2.3, the edge lies on a support path with a saturated endpoint $v_0$. Orient the support path from that endpoint toward $e$.

The first edge has mass $\mu_{v_0}$. Every intermediate vertex is saturated and has exactly the preceding and following positive edges, so the next edge mass is its type mass minus the preceding edge mass. This is precisely the recurrence above.

Because $G$ is a forest, this support-path prefix is the unique path from $v_0$ through $e$. Its final value is therefore included in $K_e$. $\square$

The candidate sets may contain values that cannot occur in a popular matrix. This causes no difficulty: the dynamic program checks all remaining requirements.

3.2 Local states

For each type $v$, enumerate the following local states.

Choose a set $B_v$ of at most two incident edges declared positive. For every $e\in B_v$, choose a mass
$$ q_{v,e}\in K_e. $$
Declare all other incident masses zero, and retain only choices satisfying
$$ \sum_{e\in B_v}q_{v,e}\leq\mu_v. $$

Let
$$ r_v=\mu_v-\sum_{e\in B_v}q_{v,e}. $$
The categories of this state are the counterpart types on $B_v$, together with $\bot$ exactly when $r_v>0$. Retain only states with at most two categories.

Assign a potential
$$ \lambda_{v,k}\in\{-1,0,1\} $$
to each matched category, and potential zero to an unmatched category.

All matched-category lower bounds are automatically satisfied because their required bound is $-1$.

If $d_v$ is the compatibility degree of $v$, the number of states is at most
$$ \sum_{r=0}^{2}\binom{d_v}{r}(3\tau)^r, $$
before discarding infeasible choices. Thus it is polynomial in $\tau$.

3.3 Compatibility of neighboring states

For every compatibility edge $ij$, a state at $i$ and a state at $j$ are compatible exactly when the following conditions hold.

First, they assign the same incumbent mass to $ij$:
$$ q_{i,ij}=q_{j,ij}, $$
where an undeclared edge has mass zero.

Second, if this mass is positive, they satisfy cancellation:
$$ \lambda_{i,j}+\lambda_{j,i}=0. $$

Third, for every category $k$ present at $i$ and every category $\ell$ present at $j$, they satisfy
$$ \lambda_{i,k}+\lambda_{j,\ell} \geq \operatorname{vote}_i(j,k)+\operatorname{vote}_j(i,\ell). $$

There are at most four inequalities in this last check. They must be checked even when the incumbent mass on $ij$ is zero, because challengers may use any compatible edge.

Lemma. A globally compatible selection of local states defines a popular matrix.

Proof. Endpoint agreement defines one mass per edge. The local capacity checks establish feasibility.

The selected potentials satisfy every category-pair dual inequality, because every such pair arises across some compatibility edge. They also satisfy all lower bounds, edge cancellations, and unmatched zero identities. Section 1.2 therefore proves popularity. $\square$

Lemma. If a popular matrix exists, an optimal popular matrix is represented by a globally compatible selection of these states.

Proof. Choose an optimal popular vertex using Section 2.2. Its edge masses lie in the candidate sets by Section 3.1, and every type has at most two categories by Section 2.1.

Choose its three-valued certificate from Section 1.3. These masses, categories, and potentials give enumerated local states. All neighboring checks hold because they are precisely the feasibility and certificate conditions. $\square$

3.4 Dynamic programming and running time

Root each tree component arbitrarily. For a state $s$ at $v$, define its local reward by
$$ R_v(s)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\sum_{e\in B_v}w_e q_{v,e},&v\in I,\\ 0,&v\in J. \end{cases} $$
Each edge reward is then counted exactly once.

Let $F_v(s)$ be the maximum total local reward in the rooted subtree at $v$, conditional on state $s$. For children $u$ of $v$, use the recurrence
$$ F_v(s)=R_v(s)+ \sum_{u\text{ child of }v} \max_{\substack{t\text{ a state at }u\\ s,t\text{ compatible across }vu}} F_u(t). $$
An empty feasible maximum has value $-\infty$.

This recurrence is valid because, in a tree, distinct child subtrees have no compatibility edges between them. Once the parent state is fixed, their choices are independent.

Maximize at each root and add the component optima. If some component has no feasible state selection, no popular matrix exists. Otherwise, backtracking returns a globally compatible selection.

There are polynomially many states, and each neighboring state pair can be checked in polynomial time. Candidate masses, rewards, and dynamic-programming values all have polynomial rational encoding length. The algorithm therefore runs in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

Every selected solution is popular, and an optimal popular vertex is among the represented solutions. Hence the returned matrix is globally optimal. This proves the theorem.

4. Nonexistence on the unrestricted domain

Consider
$$ I=\{i_1,i_2,i_3\},\qquad J=\{j\},\qquad E=I\times J, $$
with masses
$$ a_{i_1}=\frac14,\qquad a_{i_2}=a_{i_3}=\frac18,\qquad b_j=\frac12. $$
Every applicant has its sole compatible assignment, and
$$ i_1\succ_j i_2\succ_j i_3. $$
Take all rewards equal to $1$.

The two population totals both equal $\tfrac12$. If positive applicant mass remains unmatched, equally much position mass remains unmatched. Matching small equal portions of these populations produces challenger margin $2\varepsilon>0$. Therefore a popular matching would have to match everyone.

But full matching gives $j$ three positive assignment categories, contradicting Section 2.1.

Thus this valid instance admits no popular realization. It also shows why the forest theorem must include nonexistence detection.

The literal requirement of returning a popular matrix on every permitted input is consequently impossible. This does not settle the approximation question under an existence promise.

5. Equal masses and hardness transfer

Suppose every listed type has mass
$$ c=\frac1\tau. $$
Regard the type graph as a finite bipartite matching instance with strict rankings.

5.1 Lifting finite matchings

For a finite matching $M$, let $\chi_M$ be its incidence vector.

Lemma. $M$ is finite-popular if and only if $c\chi_M$ is popular in the continuous model.

Proof. A defeating finite challenger lifts directly to a continuous challenger with margin multiplied by $c$.

For the converse, suppose $M$ is finite-popular. Every type in a realization of $c\chi_M$ has a single incumbent assignment $t_v$, possibly $\bot$.

Aggregate any continuous challenger into edge masses and divide them by $c$, obtaining $z$ in the unit-capacity bipartite matching polytope. Its margin divided by $c$ is
$$ \sum_{ij\in E} \bigl( \operatorname{vote}_i(j,t_i)+ \operatorname{vote}_j(i,t_j) \bigr)z_{ij} + \sum_{v\in V} \operatorname{vote}_v(\bot,t_v) \left(1-\sum_{e\ni v}z_e\right). $$
This expression is affine in $z$.

The unit-capacity bipartite matching polytope has integral vertices by total unimodularity. Thus $z$ is a convex combination of incidence vectors of finite matchings. The displayed margin is the same convex combination of their margins against $M$. All are nonpositive, so the continuous challenger cannot defeat the incumbent. $\square$

5.2 Rounding, including real matrices

Lemma. For every real continuous popular matrix $x$, there exists a finite popular matching $M$ such that
$$ c\,w(M)\geq W(x). $$
For rational input $x$, such an $M$ is computable in polynomial time.

Proof. Maximize reward over $F(x)$. Dividing coordinates by $c$ gives a face of the integral unit-capacity bipartite matching polytope. Choose an integral optimal vertex $\chi_M$. Then
$$ W(c\chi_M)\geq W(x). $$
Face inheritance proves continuous popularity of $c\chi_M$, and Section 5.1 proves finite popularity of $M$.

This existence argument applies to real $x$: the face is determined by its zero coordinates and saturated types.

When $x$ is rational, these conditions can be identified exactly in polynomial time. The face has a polynomial-size rational linear-programming description, and an optimal vertex can be obtained in polynomial time. Integrality gives the required finite matching. $\square$

Writing $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}$ for the finite optimal popular reward, lifting and rounding establish
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=c\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}, $$
including optimization over continuous matrices with arbitrary real coordinates.

5.3 Approximation and inapproximability

The finite theorem used here states that maximum-reward popular matching in bipartite graphs with strict rankings and nonnegative rewards admits a polynomial-time $\tfrac12$-approximation, whereas every fixed better ratio is impossible in polynomial time unless $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. The hardness construction uses zero-one rewards. These are the conclusions of [Faenza et al., Theorem 14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.11473).

All hypotheses hold for the equal-mass type graph. Apply its finite $\tfrac12$-approximation and lift the returned matching $M$. Then
$$ W(c\chi_M) =c\,w(M) \geq\frac12c\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}} =\frac12\,\mathrm{OPT}. $$

Now fix $\rho\in(\tfrac12,1]$. Suppose a polynomial-time continuous $\rho$-approximation existed on equal-mass instances admitting popular realizations.

Given a finite instance with $n$ vertices, pad it with isolated vertices to a power-of-two support size $\tau$, with
$$ n\leq\tau<2n. $$
Give every type mass $c=1/\tau$. Padding preserves finite popularity and reward, since isolated vertices remain unmatched in every matching. The resulting masses are dyadic and have polynomial encoding length. These instances admit popular realizations by finite existence and Section 5.1.

Run the hypothetical continuous algorithm and round its rational output $x$ using Section 5.2. The resulting finite popular matching satisfies
$$ w(M)\geq\frac{W(x)}c \geq\rho\,\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}c =\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{fin}}. $$
This contradicts the finite inapproximability theorem unless $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$.

Therefore every fixed improvement over $\tfrac12$ is NP-hard in the continuous model, even under an existence promise and with equal dyadic masses.

6. Remaining open question

The unresolved target is whether a polynomial-time $\tfrac12$-approximation exists for arbitrary unequal rational masses, promised to admit a popular realization.

The forest proof does not supply such an algorithm. Its polynomial candidate bound uses uniqueness of paths in the compatibility graph. In a general graph, optimal positive supports still consist of paths, but a fixed edge may lie on exponentially many possible simple paths, with different alternating mass sums. Moreover, the compatibility constraints between local states need not form a tree, so the stated dynamic-programming recurrence no longer applies.

These are limitations of the proved method, not proofs of hardness at ratio $\tfrac12$.
