The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite tuple catalog \(X\) and a finite support of user types \(T'\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and a complete revision profile \((P_t,Q_t)\) of compatible SPOs, at least one having the single-chain property, find an SPO refinement \(R_t\) for each type that minimizes \(\sum_{t\in T'}\mu_t|R_t\setminus P_t|\), subject to \(P_t\cup Q_t\subseteq R_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(X\) be a finite tuple catalog with \(m=|X|\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T'\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type supplies two strict partial orders (SPOs) \(P_t,Q_t\subseteq X\times X\), explicitly represented by their complete Boolean matrices; “complete profile” means fully specified relations, not total orders.
Compatibility means there are no \(x,y\in X\) with \((x,y)\in Q_t\) and \((y,x)\in P_t\). For every type, the orders are compatible and at least one has the single-chain property: at most one maximal totally ordered subset has at least two elements.
Choose one SPO \(R_t\subseteq X\times X\) for each type, with \(P_t\cup Q_t\subseteq R_t\), minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T'}\mu_t|R_t\setminus P_t|\). Each added ordered pair has unit cost, and choices for different types have no shared constraints.
The threshold decision version additionally receives a nonnegative binary-rational budget \(b\) and asks whether this cost can be at most \(b\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is the threshold decision version of this mass-weighted SPO refinement problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For one finite preference profile under these promises, a least SPO refinement under inclusion exists ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0503092)); a later result also establishes existence and uniqueness when one compatible order is an interval order ([source](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~chomicki/papers-amai07.pdf)).
No complexity result for exactly the stated mass-weighted problem was located in the search.

  • The relation encoding was unspecified; use explicit complete Boolean matrices over the finite catalog.
  • Interpret 'complete revision profile' as fully specified SPOs, allowing incomparability rather than requiring total orders.
  • Apply the compatibility and single-chain promises separately to every type, with the catalog as the entire preference domain.
  • Retain one ordinary SPO per type and independent choices across types, as stated in the graded question.
  • Introduce a nonnegative binary-rational threshold budget to formulate the two-sided decision complexity target, and define L as total input bit length.

Result

The threshold decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Its exact optimum is

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}=\sum_{t\in T'}\mu_t\left|\operatorname{TC}(P_t\cup Q_t)\setminus P_t\right|,\)

where \(\operatorname{TC}\) denotes transitive closure using paths of positive length.

We prove that these closures are feasible under the promises, that they minimize the objective, and that the weighted threshold comparison takes polynomial time.

1. Transitive closure is forced in every refinement

For any relation \(U\subseteq X\times X\), define \(\operatorname{TC}(U)\) to contain \((x,y)\) precisely when there is a directed path of positive length from \(x\) to \(y\) using edges of \(U\).

Suppose \(R\) is transitive and \(U\subseteq R\). For every path \(x=x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_k=y\) in \(U\), repeated application of transitivity gives \((x,y)\in R\). Consequently,

\(\operatorname{TC}(U)\subseteq R.\)

Transitive closure is transitive: concatenating two paths gives a path. It is irreflexive exactly when \(U\) has no directed cycle. Thus:

Lemma 1. If \(U\) is acyclic, then \(\operatorname{TC}(U)\) is the least SPO containing \(U\). If \(U\) has a directed cycle, no SPO contains \(U\).

2. The promises guarantee acyclicity

We first identify the structure imposed by the single-chain property.

Lemma 2. Let \(A\) be an SPO on finite \(X\) satisfying the single-chain property. Either \(A\) is empty, or there is a chain \(C\subseteq X\) such that every ordered pair of \(A\) has both endpoints in \(C\). Every element outside \(C\) is incomparable with every other element.

Proof. If \((x,y)\in A\), then \(\{x,y\}\) is a chain. Finiteness allows it to be extended to an inclusion-maximal chain. That chain has at least two elements, so the single-chain property forces it to be the unique such maximal chain \(C\). Applying this argument to every ordered pair proves the assertion. The restriction of \(A\) to \(C\) totally orders \(C\), by the definition of a chain. ∎

Lemma 3. If compatible SPOs \(A,B\) satisfy the single-chain property for \(A\), then \(A\cup B\) is acyclic.

Proof. If \(A\) is empty, the union is \(B\), which is acyclic because it is an SPO.

Otherwise, let \(C\) be the chain from Lemma 2. Suppose that \(A\cup B\) has a directed cycle, and choose one of minimum length \(r\), with distinct vertices apart from the repeated starting vertex.

Irreflexivity excludes \(r=1\). A cycle of length \(2\) cannot have both edges in one SPO, by transitivity and irreflexivity. If its edges belong to different SPOs, they violate compatibility. Hence \(r\geq 3\).

Label each edge of the cycle by one relation containing it, choosing arbitrarily if it belongs to both. Two consecutive edges cannot have the same label: transitivity of that relation would supply a shortcut and produce a shorter directed cycle. The labels therefore alternate between \(A\) and \(B\). Every cycle vertex is consequently an endpoint of an \(A\)-edge, and hence belongs to \(C\).

For any \(B\)-edge \((x,y)\) on the cycle, total ordering on \(C\) gives either \((x,y)\in A\) or \((y,x)\in A\). Compatibility excludes the latter. Thus every cycle edge belongs to \(A\). Transitivity around the cycle then contradicts irreflexivity of \(A\). ∎

Compatibility is symmetric in the two relations, so Lemma 3 applies whichever of \(P_t,Q_t\) has the single-chain property. Therefore every \(P_t\cup Q_t\) is acyclic.

This agrees with the broader interval-order result in Theorem 1 of [Chomicki’s later paper](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~chomicki/papers-amai07.pdf); the argument above proves the required special case directly.

3. Exact optimality of the closures

For each supported type, set

\(R_t^*=\operatorname{TC}(P_t\cup Q_t).\)

By Lemmas 1 and 3, \(R_t^*\) is a feasible SPO. For any other feasible \(R_t\), Lemma 1 gives \(R_t^*\subseteq R_t\). Since both relations contain \(P_t\), we obtain

\(R_t^*\setminus P_t\subseteq R_t\setminus P_t,\)

and therefore

\(\left|R_t^*\setminus P_t\right|\leq\left|R_t\setminus P_t\right|.\)

Multiplying by the positive mass \(\mu_t\) and summing proves the stated formula for \(\operatorname{OPT}\). The closures attain this bound simultaneously because different types have no shared constraints.

Indeed, the optimizer is unique. Any distinct feasible family has \(R_t^*\subsetneq R_t\) for some type. Every extra pair lies outside \(P_t\), so it strictly increases that type’s cost and, because \(\mu_t>0\), the total cost.

4. Algorithm and running time

For each of the \(\tau\) supported types:

  • Form the Boolean matrix of \(P_t\cup Q_t\).
  • Compute its transitive closure by Boolean Warshall’s algorithm.
  • Count \(k_t=\left|\operatorname{TC}(P_t\cup Q_t)\setminus P_t\right|\).

The relation computations take \(O(\tau(m+1)^3)\) operations on the usual RAM model. The counts satisfy \(0\leq k_t\leq m^2\).

It remains to compare the weighted sum with the budget exactly. Write the input rationals as \(\mu_t=a_t/d_t\) and \(b=c/e\), with positive denominators. Define

\(D=e\prod_{t\in T'}d_t.\)

Then the answer is yes exactly when the following integer inequality holds:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T'}a_tk_t(D/d_t)\leq c(D/e).\)

All quotients here are exact integers. The bit length of \(D\) is at most the sum of the encoded denominator lengths, hence \(O(L)\). Since \(k_t\leq m^2\), all integers used in this comparison have \(O(L+\log(m+2))\) bits. Using elementary integer multiplication and division, the arithmetic takes

\(O\!\left((\tau+1)(L+\log(m+2))^2\right)\)

bit operations. Including input reading, a conservative RAM running-time bound accounting for this arithmetic is

\(O\!\left(L+\tau(m+1)^3+(\tau+1)(L+\log(m+2))^2\right).\)

The complete matrices are explicit input, so this bound is polynomial in \(L\). Only the listed support of size \(\tau\) is processed; no enumeration of possible types is needed.

Thus the exact optimization problem, and consequently its threshold decision version, are polynomial-time solvable.

3. Acyclicity of the union

Lemma 3 is valid. Each step in its shortest-cycle argument checks out.

Their endpoints are distinct, and replacing the two edges by the shortcut produces a shorter directed cycle. The argument applies also across the cyclic boundary.
