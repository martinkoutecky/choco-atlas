The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) item categories, a public set \(T\) of possible demand types \(t\subseteq[m]\), rational supplies \(N\nu_j\), and an unknown rational mass vector \(\mu\) with support size at most \(\tau\), determine the smallest worst-case approximation factor \(\alpha\) achievable by an \(r\)-round protocol. Each unit of type mass knows its type and sends at most \(b\) bits per round; the platform observes the exact mass \(q_h\) of each message-history group \(h\), then chooses allocation fractions \(p_{h,j}\) with \(\sum_j p_{h,j}\le1\) and \(\sum_h q_h p_{h,j}\le N\nu_j\). Welfare is evaluated against the full-information fractional matching optimum for \(\mu\), with randomized protocols evaluated in expectation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Public data are integers \(m,r\ge1\), \(b\ge0\), a support bound \(\tau\ge1\), an explicitly listed nonempty set \(T\subseteq2^{[m]}\), a positive rational population scale \(N\), and nonnegative rational supplies \(N\nu_j\); no restriction is imposed on \(\sum_j\nu_j\).
A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Its support size is at most \(\tau\); support means positive-mass types, not all possible types. This list is hidden from the platform and participants; each participant knows only its own type. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state any running times in \(m,\tau,L,|T|,r,b\).
Type \(t\) has physical mass \(N\mu_t\) and unit-demand value one for an item in \(t\), zero otherwise; empty demand sets are allowed. Let \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)\) be the full-information fractional matching optimum with these type masses and supplies.
In each of \(r\) simultaneous rounds, each participant sends a message from an alphabet of size \(2^b\), depending only on its type, its own message history, previous public observations, and randomness. After each round, the exact masses of all message-history groups are publicly revealed without a precision or communication charge.
Protocols are anonymous within types. Private randomization is modeled by stochastic kernels: conditional on public randomness, each type-history mass splits exactly in the kernel's probabilities. Public randomness is shared by participants and the platform; local computation is unrestricted.
At the end, let \(x_{h,t}\) be the latent physical mass of type \(t\) with history \(h\), and \(q_h=\sum_t x_{h,t}\). Using only the public transcript and public randomness, the platform chooses \(p_{h,j}\ge0\) with \(\sum_jp_{h,j}\le1\) and \(\sum_hq_hp_{h,j}\le N\nu_j\).
Welfare is \(W_\pi(\mu)=\sum_{h,t}x_{h,t}\sum_{j\in t}p_{h,j}\). Allocations to unwanted items consume supply and contribute zero welfare. Expectations include protocol randomness; the guarantee is required separately for every admissible rational society.

For fixed public data, what is the infimum \(\alpha\ge1\) achievable by an \(r\)-round protocol satisfying \(\mathbb E[W_\pi(\mu)]\ge\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)/\alpha\) for every society with support size at most \(\tau\), allowing \(\alpha=\infty\) if no finite guarantee exists?

For discrete matching, every randomized \(r\)-round protocol has approximation factor \(\Omega(n^{1/5^{r+1}})\) on a family with perfect matchings when per-player bandwidth is at most \(n^{1/5^{r+1}}\), for sufficiently large bandwidth ([source](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~nalon/PDFS/dismatch2.pdf)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored simultaneous rounds and public access to previous observations from the shared-blackboard model.

An exact characterization of the infimum

The infimum is always finite. For zero-bit protocols, it is the reciprocal of an explicitly described linear-programming value. For positive bandwidth, it is the reciprocal of a limit of finite real-algebraic optimization values, with an explicit error bound. Thus the characterization below determines the requested infimum to any prescribed accuracy.

I use the intended reading that every society’s types belong to the public set \(T\). The constructions use algebraic splitting probabilities, which the stated stochastic-kernel model permits.

1. Normalization and elementary bounds

Enumerate \(T=\{t_1,\ldots,t_s\}\), where \(s=|T|\). Divide physical masses and welfare by \(N\). Write \(v(\mu)=\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)/N\). Then \(v(\mu)\) is the value of the fractional matching program

\( \displaystyle v(\mu)=\max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in t}f_{t,j}, \)

subject to

  • \(f_{t,j}\ge 0\);
  • \(\sum_{j\in t}f_{t,j}\le \mu_t\) for every \(t\);
  • \(\sum_{t:j\in t}f_{t,j}\le \nu_j\) for every \(j\).

Let

\( \displaystyle J=\{j:\nu_j>0\},\qquad G=\{t\in T:t\cap J\ne\varnothing\}. \)

If \(G=\varnothing\), every society has zero optimum, so the answer is \(\alpha_*=1\). Assume henceforth that \(G\ne\varnothing\), and define

\( \displaystyle c=\min_{j\in J}\nu_j>0,\qquad d=\min\{1,c\},\qquad a(\mu)=\sum_{t\in G}\mu_t. \)

We will use the bounds

\( \displaystyle d\,a(\mu)\le v(\mu)\le a(\mu). \)

For the upper bound, only types in \(G\) can receive positive welfare. For the lower bound, choose one item \(j(t)\in t\cap J\) for each \(t\in G\), and match mass \(d\mu_t\) to that item. Each item receives at most \(d\,a(\mu)\le d\le\nu_j\), and each type’s matched mass is at most its mass.

Every realized protocol allocation also gives a feasible matching after allocations to unwanted items are discarded. Consequently its welfare is at most \(Nv(\mu)\).

A finite guarantee requires no communication: set

\( \displaystyle p_j= \begin{cases} d/m,&j\in J,\\ 0,&j\notin J. \end{cases} \)

This allocation is feasible, and every type in \(G\) receives welfare at least \(d/m\) per unit mass. Therefore

\( \displaystyle 1\le\alpha_*\le m/d. \)

In particular, \(\alpha_*=\infty\) never occurs.

2. The zero-bit answer is an explicit linear program

When \(b=0\), every participant has the same history, and every observation is independent of the society. Averaging any public randomization gives a single feasible vector \(p\), with

\( \displaystyle p_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_jp_j\le1,\qquad p_j\le\nu_j. \)

Define \(u_t(p)=\sum_{j\in t}p_j\).

For each nonempty \(S\subseteq T\) with \(|S|\le\tau\), introduce variables

\( \displaystyle \lambda_S\in\mathbb R,\qquad a_{S,t}\ge0\quad(t\in S),\qquad e_{S,j}\ge0\quad(j\in[m]). \)

Let \(\beta_0\) be the maximum of \(\beta\), subject to \(0\le\beta\le1\), the feasibility constraints on \(p\), and, for every such \(S\),

\( \displaystyle \lambda_S-\sum_j\nu_je_{S,j}\ge0, \)

\( \displaystyle u_t(p)\ge\lambda_S+a_{S,t}\qquad(t\in S), \)

\( \displaystyle a_{S,t}+e_{S,j}\ge\beta \qquad(t\in S,\ j\in t). \)

The exact zero-bit answer is

\( \displaystyle \boxed{\alpha_*=\frac1{\beta_0}.} \)

Proof

Fix \(S\). The required guarantee for societies supported in \(S\) is equivalent to

\( \displaystyle \sum_{t\in S}\mu_tu_t(p)-\beta\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{j\in t}f_{t,j}\ge0 \)

for every feasible pair \((\mu,f)\), where

\( \displaystyle \mu_t\ge0,\qquad \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1, \)

and \(f\) satisfies the matching constraints.

Indeed, maximizing the second sum over \(f\) gives \(v(\mu)\). Rational societies are dense in each support simplex, and this inequality is continuous, so requiring it for rational societies is equivalent to requiring it throughout that simplex.

Minimize its left-hand side over \((\mu,f)\). This is a feasible, bounded linear program. Its dual maximizes

\( \displaystyle \lambda_S-\sum_j\nu_je_{S,j} \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle u_t(p)-\lambda_S-a_{S,t}\ge0,\qquad a_{S,t}+e_{S,j}-\beta\ge0, \)

with \(a_{S,t},e_{S,j}\ge0\). These constraints follow directly by collecting coefficients in the Lagrangian

\( \displaystyle \lambda_S-\sum_j\nu_je_{S,j} +\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\bigl(u_t(p)-\lambda_S-a_{S,t}\bigr) +\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{j\in t}f_{t,j} \bigl(a_{S,t}+e_{S,j}-\beta\bigr). \)

Linear-programming strong duality shows that the minimum is nonnegative exactly when the displayed dual constraints admit a solution with nonnegative objective. Enforcing this for all \(S\) proves the claim.

Writing

\( \displaystyle B=\sum_{k=1}^{\min\{\tau,s\}}\binom{s}{k}, \)

the LP has description size polynomial in \(L+m+s+B\,m(\tau+1)\). It can therefore be solved in time polynomial in that quantity.

3. One binary round can reveal the entire rational society

This is the feature that makes the present model substantially different from ordinary discrete communication models.

Let \(K=2^b\), and suppose \(K\ge2\). Let \(A\) be any rational column-stochastic \(K\times s\) matrix: \(A_{i,t}\) is the probability that type \(t\) sends first-round message \(i\).

Choose a rational \(0<\varepsilon<1\), and put

\( \displaystyle \theta=2^{-1/(s+1)}. \)

The polynomial \(x^{s+1}-2\) is irreducible by Eisenstein’s criterion. Since \(\theta\) is the reciprocal of one of its roots, \(\theta\) has degree \(s+1\). Hence

\( \displaystyle 1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^s \)

are linearly independent over \(\mathbb Q\).

Define a column-stochastic matrix \(D\) by

\( \displaystyle D_{1,t_i}=\theta^i,\qquad D_{2,t_i}=1-\theta^i,\qquad D_{k,t_i}=0\quad(k>2), \)

and use the perturbed first-round matrix

\( \displaystyle A^\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)A+\varepsilon D. \)

The normalized mass of first-round message \(1\) is

\( \displaystyle y=(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^s A_{1,t_i}\mu_{t_i} +\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^s\mu_{t_i}\theta^i. \)

For a rational society, its coordinates in the basis \(1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^s\) have coefficient \(\varepsilon\mu_{t_i}\) at \(\theta^i\). Thus \(y\) uniquely determines every \(\mu_{t_i}\).

This decoder uses the stipulated exact observation. Equivalently, it is the inverse of an injective map on rational societies. No finite-precision decoding assertion is needed.

Moreover,

\( \displaystyle \max_t\sum_i|A^\varepsilon_{i,t}-A_{i,t}|\le2\varepsilon. \)

Thus full discovery can be added to any rational first-round rule by an arbitrarily small perturbation. The remaining issue is whether that perturbation can uniformly preserve approximation ratios. Section 5 proves that it can.

4. A finite-dimensional continuation problem

Set

\( \displaystyle H=K^{r-1}. \)

For a first-round matrix \(A\) and a society \(\mu\), define \(\Phi_H(A,\mu)\) as follows. Its variables are normalized latent masses

\( \displaystyle z_{i,\ell,t}\ge0 \qquad(i\in[K],\ \ell\in[H],\ t\in T) \)

and allocation probabilities \(p_{i,\ell,j}\ge0\). Maximize

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i,\ell,t}z_{i,\ell,t}\sum_{j\in t}p_{i,\ell,j}, \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle \sum_{\ell=1}^H z_{i,\ell,t}=\mu_tA_{i,t}, \)

\( \displaystyle \sum_jp_{i,\ell,j}\le1, \)

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i,\ell}\left(\sum_tz_{i,\ell,t}\right)p_{i,\ell,j} \le\nu_j. \)

This is a compact optimization problem with a continuous polynomial objective, so its maximum exists. Also,

\( \displaystyle 0\le\Phi_H(A,\mu)\le v(\mu). \)

Its interpretation is precise: the first-round splitting rule is fixed, but the continuation knows the society and may create at most \(H\) suffix histories below each first-round message.

Once the society is known, every feasible solution is implementable in the remaining rounds. Conditional on type \(t\) and first message \(i\), select suffix \(\ell\) with probability

\( \displaystyle \frac{z_{i,\ell,t}}{\mu_tA_{i,t}}, \)

whenever the denominator is positive. Send the suffix’s base-\(K\) code during the remaining rounds. Equivalently, use the successive conditional probabilities of its prefixes. Zero-mass cases can be assigned arbitrary kernels.

When \(r=1\), \(H=1\), and the \(z\)-variables are fixed by \(A\) and \(\mu\).

5. A uniform perturbation bound

For two column-stochastic matrices \(A,B\), put

\( \displaystyle \delta=\max_t\sum_i|A_{i,t}-B_{i,t}|,\qquad C=\frac12\left(\frac1c+\frac1d\right). \)

For every society with \(v(\mu)>0\),

\( \displaystyle \boxed{ \left| \frac{\Phi_H(A,\mu)}{v(\mu)} -\frac{\Phi_H(B,\mu)}{v(\mu)} \right|\le C\delta. } \)

The bound is uniform over supports and over arbitrarily small positive masses.

Proof

Take an optimizer \(z,p\) for \(A\). We may set \(p_{i,\ell,j}=0\) for all zero-supply items without reducing welfare.

For each \(i,t\), retain the proportion

\( \displaystyle \frac{\min\{A_{i,t},B_{i,t}\}}{A_{i,t}} \)

of every suffix mass below that pair, with retained mass zero when \(A_{i,t}=0\). Distribute the additional mass \(\mu_t(B_{i,t}-A_{i,t})_+\) arbitrarily among suffixes.

For type \(t\), the removed mass equals

\( \displaystyle \frac{\mu_t}{2}\sum_i|A_{i,t}-B_{i,t}| \le\frac{\mu_t\delta}{2}. \)

The same bound holds for its added mass. Consequently:

  • removed welfare is at most \(\delta a(\mu)/2\);
  • each item’s load increases by at most \(\delta/2\).

Scale all allocation probabilities by

\( \displaystyle \lambda=\frac{c}{c+\delta/2}. \)

For every positive-supply item,

\( \displaystyle \lambda(\nu_j+\delta/2)\le\nu_j, \)

because \(c\le\nu_j\). The modified solution is therefore feasible for \(B\), and

\( \displaystyle \Phi_H(B,\mu)\ge \lambda\left(\Phi_H(A,\mu)-\frac{\delta a(\mu)}2\right). \)

Using \(v(\mu)\ge d a(\mu)\) and \(\Phi_H(A,\mu)\le v(\mu)\), we obtain

\( \displaystyle \frac{\Phi_H(A,\mu)-\Phi_H(B,\mu)}{v(\mu)} \le (1-\lambda)+\frac{\lambda\delta}{2d} \le \frac{\delta}{2c}+\frac{\delta}{2d}. \)

Interchanging \(A\) and \(B\) proves the assertion.

Continuity in the society

We also need that \(\Phi_H(A,\mu)\) is continuous in \(\mu\), including at zero coordinates.

Fix \(A\), and express an optimal \(z\) using conditional suffix probabilities for each type and first message. Reuse those probabilities at \(\mu'\). If \(\eta=\|\mu-\mu'\|_1\), the resulting latent arrays differ in total absolute mass by at most \(\eta\). Welfare decreases by at most \(\eta\), and every item load increases by at most \(\eta\). Scaling allocations by \(c/(c+\eta)\) restores feasibility. Reversing the roles of \(\mu,\mu'\) proves continuity.

The full-information value \(v\) is continuous as well. Given a matching at \(\mu\), reduce any type row exceeding its new mass at \(\mu'\). This removes at most \(\|\mu-\mu'\|_1\) welfare and preserves item capacities. Applying the argument in both directions proves continuity.

6. The positive-bandwidth answer

Let \(\mathcal M_\tau\) be the set of real probability vectors on \(T\) with at most \(\tau\) positive coordinates. It is a finite union of closed simplex faces.

For a positive integer \(Q\), let \(\mathcal A_Q\) consist of all column-stochastic \(K\times s\) matrices whose entries are integer multiples of \(1/Q\). Its cardinality is

\( \displaystyle M_Q=\binom{Q+K-1}{K-1}^{s}. \)

Define \(\beta_Q\) by the following finite-dimensional optimization:

\( \displaystyle \beta_Q=\max\left\{ \beta\in[0,1]: \begin{array}{l} \text{there are }w_A\ge0,\ \sum_{A\in\mathcal A_Q}w_A=1,\\ \sum_{A\in\mathcal A_Q}w_A\Phi_H(A,\mu) \ge\beta v(\mu) \quad\text{for every }\mu\in\mathcal M_\tau \end{array} \right\}. \)

Here the notation inside braces specifies ordinary real variables and constraints. The maximum exists: the weight simplex and \([0,1]\) are compact, and each fixed society imposes a closed linear inequality on the weights and \(\beta\).

For \(b\ge1\), the exact answer is

\( \displaystyle \boxed{ \alpha_*= \frac1{\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{2^n}}. } \)

The values along these grids are nondecreasing. More precisely, if \(\beta_*=1/\alpha_*\), then for every \(Q\),

\( \displaystyle \boxed{ \beta_Q\le\beta_* \le \min\left\{1,\, \beta_Q+\frac{K-1}{Q}\left(\frac1c+\frac1d\right) \right\}. } \)

These statements characterize the infimum, without asserting that an optimal protocol always attains it.

Upper bound: every protocol is bounded by a grid value

Condition on the protocol’s entire public random tape. Its first-round kernels define a column-stochastic matrix \(A\), independent of the society: before that round, participants know only their types and public data.

For a fixed society, the final histories below each first message number at most \(H\). Their latent masses and the final allocation are feasible for the continuation problem. Hence realized normalized welfare is at most \(\Phi_H(A,\mu)\).

Round each column of \(A\) to the \(1/Q\)-grid by rounding its first \(K-1\) entries downward and placing the residual in its last entry. The resulting \(A_Q\) satisfies

\( \displaystyle \max_t\sum_i|A_{i,t}-(A_Q)_{i,t}| \le\frac{2(K-1)}Q. \)

The perturbation bound therefore gives, for every society of positive optimum,

\( \displaystyle \frac{\mathbb E[W_\pi(\mu)]}{Nv(\mu)} \le \mathbb E\left[\frac{\Phi_H(A_Q,\mu)}{v(\mu)}\right] +\frac{K-1}{Q}\left(\frac1c+\frac1d\right). \)

The distribution of \(A_Q\) is a distribution on the finite set \(\mathcal A_Q\). Its worst-case ratio is at most \(\beta_Q\).

To justify using real societies in this last statement, inequalities of the form

\( \displaystyle \sum_Aw_A\Phi_H(A,\mu)\ge\beta v(\mu) \)

are continuous in \(\mu\). Rational societies are dense in every support face, so their validity for admissible rational societies is equivalent to validity throughout \(\mathcal M_\tau\).

Taking the worst-case society and then the supremum over protocols proves the upper bound on \(\beta_*\).

Lower bound: grid values can be approached by protocols

Take maximizing weights \(w_A\) for \(\beta_Q\). Publicly sample \(A\) according to these weights, and use its perturbed matrix \(A^\varepsilon\) from Section 3.

After the first observation, all participants and the platform can reconstruct the rational society. They then implement an optimizer of \(\Phi_H(A^\varepsilon,\mu)\).

Because the matrix distance is at most \(2\varepsilon\), the expected welfare ratio is at least

\( \displaystyle \beta_Q-2C\varepsilon. \)

Letting positive rational \(\varepsilon\) tend to zero proves \(\beta_*\ge\beta_Q\).

Finally, \(\mathcal A_{2^n}\subseteq\mathcal A_{2^{n+1}}\), so the corresponding values are nondecreasing. The upper error bound tends to zero, proving the exact limit formula.

7. Effective computation and certified accuracy

The definition of \(\beta_Q\) is a finite real-algebraic optimization problem. Here is an explicit polynomial formulation.

Introduce weights \(w_A\), and universally quantify over \(\mu\) and a feasible full-information matching \(f\). Require that there exist feasible continuation variables \(z^A,p^A\), one collection for each \(A\in\mathcal A_Q\), such that

\( \displaystyle \sum_Aw_A \sum_{i,\ell,t}z^A_{i,\ell,t}\sum_{j\in t}p^A_{i,\ell,j} \ge \beta\sum_t\sum_{j\in t}f_{t,j}. \)

This is equivalent to the definition:

  • if the definition holds, select maximizing continuations;
  • conversely, take \(f\) to be a full-information optimizer and bound each feasible continuation by \(\Phi_H(A,\mu)\).

The support condition is polynomially expressible using indicators \(u_t\) satisfying

\( \displaystyle u_t(u_t-1)=0,\qquad \mu_t(1-u_t)=0,\qquad \sum_tu_t\le\tau. \)

All other constraints have degree at most three. Coefficients are rational. Thus real quantifier elimination computes \(\beta_Q\) exactly as a real algebraic number.

For completeness, a conservative description-length bound for this formula is

\( \displaystyle S_Q= O\left( \bigl[M_QK^r(s+1)(m+1)+(s+1)(m+1)\bigr] \bigl[L+\log Q+\log(M_QK^r(s+m+1))+\log(\tau+1)\bigr] \right). \)

Standard cylindrical algebraic decomposition gives a conservative bit-running-time bound of

\( \displaystyle 2^{\,2^{\operatorname{poly}(S_Q)}}. \)

This bound is deliberately loose; it establishes effectiveness, not efficiency. The applicable real quantifier-elimination and cylindrical algebraic decomposition bounds are described in [Basu’s survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.1534).

Let

\( \displaystyle e_Q=\frac{K-1}{Q}\left(\frac1c+\frac1d\right). \)

The elementary protocol in Section 1 gives \(\beta_Q\ge d/m\). Therefore

\( \displaystyle \frac1{\min\{1,\beta_Q+e_Q\}} \le\alpha_*\le\frac1{\beta_Q}, \)

and this certified interval has width at most

\( \displaystyle e_Q\,\frac{m^2}{d^2}. \)

Given an additive accuracy \(\eta>0\), choose a power of two satisfying

\( \displaystyle Q\ge \frac{(K-1)(1/c+1/d)m^2}{d^2\eta}. \)

Computing \(\beta_Q\) then determines \(\alpha_*\) within an interval of width at most \(\eta\). Every quantity involved is determined by the public data.

8. Checks and exact special cases

A support of size one

If \(\tau=1\) and \(b\ge1\), then \(\alpha_*=1\).

The first-round encoding identifies the sole type. The platform uses the same optimal allocation vector in every history group. Since all groups contain that type, their splitting causes no welfare loss.

Enough histories to identify types

If \(K^r\ge |T|\), then \(\alpha_*=1\).

Assign every type a distinct length-\(r\) code. Its final history identifies its type, and the revealed masses supply the full matching instance.

Enough suffixes after discovery

If \(b\ge1\) and \(K^{r-1}\ge |J|+1\), then \(\alpha_*=1\).

Use the first round to reveal \(\mu\). Compute an optimal matching \(f\). For each type \(t\), choose a suffix identifying item \(j\) with probability \(f_{t,j}/\mu_t\), and choose a dummy suffix with the remaining probability. Use one suffix for each positive-supply item and one for the dummy outcome.

The platform allocates the identified item with probability one, and allocates nothing to dummy histories. The item-\(j\) history mass is exactly \(\sum_t f_{t,j}\le\nu_j\), and every allocated item is wanted. Welfare equals the optimum.

Exact observations do not remove the history constraint

Take \(T=\{\{1\},\ldots,\{m\}\}\), \(\tau\ge m\), \(\mu_{\{j\}}=1/m\), and \(\nu_j=1/m\). One may take \(m\) to be a power of two if “binary-rational” is interpreted as dyadic.

The normalized optimum is one. For any realized protocol, let \(a_{h,j}\) be the fraction of type \(\{j\}\) ending in history \(h\). Then

\( \displaystyle W/N =\frac1m\sum_h\sum_j a_{h,j}p_{h,j} \le\frac1m\sum_h\max_j a_{h,j} \le\frac{K^r}{m}. \)

The first inequality uses \(\sum_jp_{h,j}\le1\); the second uses \(a_{h,j}\le1\) and at most \(K^r\) histories. It holds for every realization and hence in expectation. Thus this family satisfies

\( \displaystyle \alpha_*\ge\max\{1,m/K^r\}. \)

Small supplies can matter even with one item

Let \(T=\{\varnothing,\{1\}\}\), \(\tau\ge2\), \(b=0\), and \(0<\nu_1<1\). Then \(\alpha_*=1/\nu_1\).

A zero-bit allocation has \(p_1\le\nu_1\). In a rational society whose demanding mass is \(0<\mu_{\{1\}}\le\nu_1\), its welfare ratio is exactly \(p_1\). Conversely, \(p_1=\nu_1\) gives ratio at least \(\nu_1\) for every society.

These cases also explain why the cited discrete-matching lower bound cannot simply be transferred. Its communication model uses individual finite messages; the present model additionally reveals exact stochastic split masses. The rational-society decoder above exploits that additional observation directly. See the communication model in [Alon, Nisan, Raz, and Weinstein](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~nalon/PDFS/dismatch2.pdf).
