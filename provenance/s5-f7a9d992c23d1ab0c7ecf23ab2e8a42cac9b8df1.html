The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(I,J\), rational type masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), rankings with ties, acceptable pairs, and a designated pair \((i^*,j^*)\), choose flows \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\beta_j\). For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(S_i(j)\) be the mass of type \(i\) unmatched or strictly preferring \(j\) to its assignment, and \(W_i(j)\) the mass unmatched or weakly preferring \(j\). Require \(S_i(j)W_j(i)=0\) and \(W_i(j)S_j(i)=0\). Find the maximum \(x_{i^*j^*}\) over such strongly stable flows, returning an attaining flow or reporting that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(I,J\) be disjoint finite type sets, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\) and a designated pair \((i^*,j^*)\in E\). Each type ranks its acceptable neighbours by a total preorder; ties are preserved, and every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. The masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) need not sum to one or have equal totals. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; put \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A flow has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), zero entries elsewhere, and satisfies \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\), \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\beta_j\). Set \(u_i=\alpha_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=\beta_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\).
For \((i,j)\in E\), define \(S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}\).
Similarly define \(S_j(i)=v_j+\sum_{h:i\succ_j h}x_{hj}\) and \(W_j(i)=v_j+\sum_{h:i\succeq_j h}x_{hj}\). All sums range over acceptable neighbours; weak comparisons include the neighbour itself.
A flow is strongly stable precisely when \(S_i(j)W_j(i)=0\) and \(W_i(j)S_j(i)=0\) for every \((i,j)\in E\), including pairs carrying positive flow.
The computational problem is to maximize \(x_{i^*j^*}\), returning an attaining flow or reporting that no strongly stable flow exists.

Is this problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard, with hardness measured by deciding whether a strongly stable flow satisfies \(x_{i^*j^*}\geq q\) for an input binary rational \(q\geq0\)?

For discrete agents, deciding whether a designated acceptable pair belongs to a strongly stable matching, and finding an \(I\)-optimal such matching, takes \(O(\tau m)\) time ([Ghosal–Kunysz–Paluch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.00677)).
For unit-capacity random matchings under a different, linear definition of fractional strong stability, testing stability and decomposing into strongly stable integral matchings are polynomial-time solvable ([Aziz–Csáji–Cseh](https://www.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/expost_stability.pdf)); this does not settle the mass problem defined above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified mutually acceptable pairs, zero flow on unacceptable pairs, and a designated pair belonging to the acceptable set, as required by the discrete anchor.
  • Specified total preorders on acceptable neighbours, preserved ties, and strict preference for every acceptable partner over being unmatched.
  • Required strictly positive rational support masses, with arbitrary and potentially unequal side totals; the discrete model imposes neither normalization nor balanced sides.
  • Made unmatched mass and both preference sums explicit, including the current neighbour in weak comparisons and imposing stability even on pairs carrying positive flow.
  • Defined m as the number of acceptable type pairs, tau as the combined support size, and L as total input bit length; introduced a rational threshold q to specify the hardness decision problem.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). This includes deciding the threshold question, detecting nonexistence, and returning an exact maximizing flow.

The proof has three parts: a polynomial feasibility algorithm working directly with masses, a reduction enforcing a positive designated-flow threshold, and an integrality argument enabling exact optimization.

1. A structural consequence of strong stability

Write \(j\sim_i k\) when \(i\) is indifferent between \(j\) and \(k\).

Lemma 1. In a strongly stable flow, every type is either entirely unmatched or fully matched to partners belonging to a single tie class.

Proof. If \(x_{ij}>0\), then \(W_i(j)\geq x_{ij}>0\) and \(W_j(i)\geq x_{ij}>0\). The two stability equations therefore imply \(S_i(j)=S_j(i)=0\). In particular, \(u_i=v_j=0\).

If \(x_{ij}>0\), \(x_{ik}>0\), and \(j\succ_i k\), then \(S_i(j)\geq x_{ik}>0\), a contradiction. Since preferences are total preorders, all positive-flow partners of \(i\) are tied. The argument for \(j\) is identical. ∎

Thus partial matching of a type is impossible, although its mass can be divided among partners within one tie.

2. A polynomial algorithm for existence

Discrete strong stability has polynomial proposal-and-deletion algorithms; see [Kavitha–Mehlhorn–Michail–Paluch](https://d-michail.github.io/assets/papers/strong-journal.pdf). Below we give a procedure on weighted graphs and prove its correctness directly for real masses.

All types considered in this section have positive mass. For a set of types, write \(\alpha(H)=\sum_{i\in H}\alpha_i\) and \(\beta(U)=\sum_{j\in U}\beta_j\).

Maintain a remaining edge set \(R\), initially \(E\), and a set \(T\subseteq J\), initially empty. Repeat the following steps.

  • Proposals. Each \(i\) with a nonempty remaining list proposes along every edge in its best remaining tie. Let \(A\subseteq R\) be all these proposal edges, and add their \(J\)-endpoints to \(T\).
  • Strict deletions. Delete every \((h,j)\in R\) for which some proposal \((i,j)\in A\) satisfies \(i\succ_j h\). If any edge was deleted, restart.
  • Maximum flow. Let \(P\subseteq I\) be the types incident to \(A\). Compute a maximum flow \(f\) in the network with:
  • an arc \(s\to i\) of capacity \(\alpha_i\) for each \(i\in P\);
  • an arc \(i\to j\) for each \((i,j)\in A\), of capacity \(M=1+\sum_i\alpha_i+\sum_j\beta_j\);
  • an arc \(j\to t\) of capacity \(\beta_j\) for each \(j\in J\).
  • Critical deletions. If some \(s\to i\), \(i\in P\), is unsaturated, let \(Z\subseteq P\) and \(B\subseteq J\) be the type vertices reachable from \(s\) in the residual network. For every \(j\in B\), delete its worst remaining tie. Restart.
  • Termination. Otherwise \(f\) saturates every type in \(P\). If it also saturates every type in \(T\), return \(f\), extended by zero outside \(A\). If it does not, report nonexistence.

After step 2 produces no deletion, all proposals received by any particular \(j\) are tied. Moreover, that tie is \(j\)'s worst remaining tie: a worse remaining edge would have been deleted in step 2.

Each restart deletes at least one edge. For step 4, an unsaturated active \(I\)-type has a proposal neighbor reachable in the residual network, so \(B\neq\varnothing\), and each \(j\in B\) has a proposal edge. Consequently there are at most \(m\) restarts and \(m+1\) maximum-flow computations.

2.1. Strict deletions preserve every strongly stable flow

We prove inductively that no deleted edge carries positive flow in any strongly stable flow of the original instance.

Suppose this holds for all previous deletions, and let \(y\) be any original strongly stable flow. Then \(y\) is supported on the current \(R\).

For a proposal \((i,j)\), no positive-flow partner of \(i\) is strictly preferred to \(j\), because \(j\) belongs to \(i\)'s best remaining tie. Hence \(W_i(j)=\alpha_i>0\). Stability gives \(S_j(i)=0\). Therefore:

  • \(j\) is fully matched in \(y\);
  • \(y\) assigns \(j\) no partner strictly worse than \(i\).

Every strict deletion caused by this proposal consequently removes an edge of zero \(y\)-flow.

This also proves an invariant needed later:

Every type in \(T\) is fully matched in every original strongly stable flow.

2.2. Critical deletions preserve every strongly stable flow

Consider step 4, after strict deletions have finished. The residual network has the following properties:

  • every forward proposal arc has positive residual capacity, since its capacity \(M\) exceeds the total possible flow;
  • every proposal neighbor of a type in \(Z\) belongs to \(B\);
  • every \(j\in B\) is saturated by \(f\), since otherwise \(t\) would be reachable;
  • if \(f_{ij}>0\) and \(j\in B\), then \(i\in Z\), by the reverse residual arc.

Let \(y\) again be an original strongly stable flow supported on \(R\). For each \(j\in B\), denote its proposal tie by \(r_j\). This is precisely the tie being deleted.

Define \(U\subseteq B\) to consist of those \(j\) receiving positive \(y\)-flow from tie \(r_j\). By Lemma 1, each \(j\in U\) is fully matched, entirely within \(r_j\).

Define \(H\subseteq Z\) to consist of those \(i\) fully matched by \(y\) within their best remaining tie.

If \(j\in U\), \(i\in Z\), and \((i,j)\in A\), then \(W_j(i)=\beta_j>0\). Stability gives \(S_i(j)=0\). Since \(j\) is in \(i\)'s best remaining tie and \(y\) is supported on \(R\), this forces \(i\in H\). Thus no proposal edge joins \(Z\setminus H\) to \(U\).

Conversely, every positive \(y\)-flow from \(H\) goes to \(U\). Indeed, it uses proposal edges; all proposal neighbors of \(H\subseteq Z\) lie in \(B\); and a positive-flow proposal edge puts its \(J\)-endpoint in \(U\). Therefore \(\alpha(H)\leq\beta(U)\).

The maximum flow \(f\) saturates \(U\). Every positive \(f\)-flow into \(U\) originates in \(Z\), and hence, by the preceding argument, in \(H\). Consequently

\(\displaystyle \beta(U)=\sum_{i\in H,\ j\in U}f_{ij}\leq\sum_{i\in H,\ j}f_{ij}\leq\alpha(H).\)

All these inequalities are equalities. It follows that \(f\) saturates every \(i\in H\), and every positive \(f\)-flow from \(H\) goes to \(U\).

If \(U\neq\varnothing\), take a residual path from \(s\) to a vertex of \(U\), and consider its first entry into \(H\cup U\). That entry is impossible:

  • an arc \(s\to i\), \(i\in H\), has zero residual capacity;
  • a forward proposal arc from \(Z\setminus H\) into \(U\) does not exist;
  • a reverse flow arc from \(B\setminus U\) into \(H\) does not exist, because all positive flow from \(H\) goes to \(U\).

These exhaust the possibilities for first entry. Thus \(U=\varnothing\), so every critically deleted edge has zero \(y\)-flow. The deletion invariant is proved.

2.3. Correctness at termination

Suppose first that the algorithm returns \(f\). It saturates \(P\) and \(T\).

For every deleted edge \((i,j)\), immediately after its deletion all remaining neighbors of \(j\) are strictly preferred to \(i\). This holds for strict deletions and for deletion of the entire worst remaining tie. Also \(j\in T\). Thus its final partners are all strictly preferred to \(i\), and \(S_j(i)=W_j(i)=0\).

For a remaining edge outside \(A\), its \(I\)-endpoint is fully matched within a strictly better tie, so \(S_i(j)=W_i(j)=0\).

Finally, for an edge in \(A\), both endpoints are fully matched within the respective proposal ties, so \(S_i(j)=S_j(i)=0\). Every original edge therefore satisfies both product equations.

Now suppose the final maximum flow saturates \(P\) but fails to saturate \(T\). Its value is \(\alpha(P)\), and all its \(J\)-endpoints lie in \(T\). Hence \(\alpha(P)<\beta(T)\).

Any original strongly stable flow \(y\) must be supported on the final \(R\), so its total matched mass is at most \(\alpha(P)\). But every type in \(T\) must be fully matched, so that same total is at least \(\beta(T)\). This is a contradiction.

We have proved:

Theorem 2. Existence of a strongly stable flow can be decided using at most \(m+1\) maximum-flow computations on networks with at most \(\tau+2\) vertices and \(m+\tau\) arcs. An attaining flow is returned whenever one exists.

2.4. Invariance of the matched types

A further consequence will support the threshold reduction.

Corollary 3. For a fixed instance, all strongly stable flows match exactly the same types.

Proof. If the algorithm succeeds, its final flow satisfies \(\alpha(P)=\beta(T)\). Every original strongly stable flow \(y\) is supported on \(R\), and every type in \(T\) is fully matched. Thus its total matched mass is simultaneously at most \(\alpha(P)\) and at least \(\beta(T)\).

Equality forces every type in \(P\) to be fully matched and every type outside \(P\) to be unmatched. It also forces every type in \(T\) to be fully matched and every type outside \(T\) to be unmatched. ∎

3. Enforcing the designated-flow threshold

Let \(a=i^*\) and \(b=j^*\).

For \(q=0\), the threshold question is just existence. If \(q>\min\{\alpha_a,\beta_b\}\), it is infeasible. Assume henceforth that \(0<q\leq\min\{\alpha_a,\beta_b\}\).

Reserve flow \(q\) on \((a,b)\), and reduce the corresponding masses to \(\alpha'_a=\alpha_a-q\) and \(\beta'_b=\beta_b-q\). All other masses remain unchanged.

Because the reserved edge has positive flow, Lemma 1 requires \(a\) and \(b\) to be fully matched within their respective ties containing that edge. Construct a reduced edge set by imposing the following restrictions.

Endpoint restrictions.

  • A remaining edge \((a,j)\) must satisfy \(j\sim_a b\).
  • A remaining edge \((i,b)\) must satisfy \(i\sim_b a\).

Restrictions on neighbors of \(a\). For every acceptable \(j\neq b\):

| Comparison at \(a\) | Requirement on \(j\) |
|---|---|
| \(j\succ_a b\) | Fully matched, with every partner \(h\succ_j a\). |
| \(j\sim_a b\) | Fully matched, with every partner \(h\succeq_j a\). |
| \(b\succ_a j\) | No additional requirement. |

Restrictions on neighbors of \(b\). Symmetrically, for every acceptable \(i\neq a\):

| Comparison at \(b\) | Requirement on \(i\) |
|---|---|
| \(i\succ_b a\) | Fully matched, with every partner \(k\succ_i b\). |
| \(i\sim_b a\) | Fully matched, with every partner \(k\succeq_i b\). |
| \(a\succ_b i\) | No additional requirement. |

Delete every edge violating any partner restriction. Mark \(a,b\), and all neighbors required to be fully matched, as required types. Remove zero-mass types from the reduced instance; their required saturation is vacuous.

Apply Theorem 2 to this reduced instance. If it reports nonexistence, reject. Otherwise, obtain a strongly stable flow \(y\), and check whether every required type is saturated at its reduced mass. Reject if this fails; otherwise return \(x=y+q\,\mathbf{1}_{(a,b)}\).

3.1. Necessity of the reduction

Suppose an original strongly stable flow \(x\) satisfies \(x_{ab}\geq q\).

Lemma 1 gives the endpoint restrictions and endpoint saturation. If \(j\succ_a b\), then \(S_a(j)=W_a(j)=\alpha_a>0\), so stability forces \(W_j(a)=0\). Thus \(j\) is fully matched exclusively to partners strictly preferred to \(a\).

If \(j\sim_a b\), then \(S_a(j)=0\) and \(W_a(j)=\alpha_a>0\), so stability forces \(S_j(a)=0\). Thus \(j\) is fully matched exclusively to partners weakly preferred to \(a\). The restrictions on neighbors of \(b\) follow identically.

Consequently all deleted edges carry zero \(x\)-flow. Subtracting \(q\) from \(x_{ab}\) gives a feasible reduced flow \(y\), saturating every required type. The unmatched masses remain unchanged. Each stability factor on a surviving edge can only decrease, so all reduced product equations remain zero. Hence \(y\) is strongly stable in the reduced instance.

3.2. Sufficiency of the reduction

Conversely, suppose \(y\) is strongly stable in the reduced instance and saturates every required type. Set \(x=y+q\,\mathbf{1}_{(a,b)}\).

The endpoints \(a,b\) are fully matched within the required ties. Consider an original edge incident to \(a\):

  • If \(b\succ_a j\), then \(S_a(j)=W_a(j)=0\).
  • If \(j\succ_a b\), the restriction on \(j\) gives \(S_j(a)=W_j(a)=0\).
  • If \(j\sim_a b\), then \(S_a(j)=0\), and the restriction on \(j\) gives \(S_j(a)=0\).

Thus both stability equations hold. Edges incident to \(b\) are handled symmetrically.

For a surviving edge with neither endpoint designated, the stability factors are unchanged from \(y\). For a deleted edge with neither endpoint designated, some required endpoint excluded that partner through a strict or weak preference cutoff. Every allowed partner is strictly preferred to the excluded partner, and the endpoint is fully matched. Its \(S\)- and \(W\)-factors are therefore both zero.

Hence \(x\) is strongly stable and \(x_{ab}\geq q\).

3.3. Why one reduced flow suffices

The reduction needs a reduced strongly stable flow saturating all required types. By Corollary 3, all strongly stable flows of the reduced instance match the same types. By Lemma 1, a matched type is fully matched.

Therefore, if the flow returned by Theorem 2 fails to saturate a required positive-mass type, no other reduced strongly stable flow can saturate it.

We have established:

Theorem 4. The threshold question is decidable in polynomial time, and a witnessing flow can be returned whenever the answer is affirmative.

4. Exact optimization with binary rational masses

It remains to show that polynomially many threshold calls suffice for exact maximization.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Then every \(D\alpha_i\) and \(D\beta_j\) is integral, and \(D\) has bit length \(O(L)\).

4.1. An optimum lies on the rational grid

Lemma 5. If a strongly stable flow exists, the maximum designated flow is attained at a multiple of \(1/D\).

Proof. The strongly stable feasible set is bounded and closed: feasibility gives boundedness, and the product equations are continuous equalities. Hence an optimum \(x\) exists.

Fix its matched types and the tie class used at each matched type. Consider the transportation polytope consisting of flows that:

  • use only edges belonging to the prescribed tie at both endpoints;
  • fully match every prescribed matched type;
  • leave every other type unmatched.

This polytope contains \(x\). Every flow in it has the same stability factors as \(x\): at a matched \(I\)-type, the pair \((S_i(j),W_i(j))\) is

  • \((\alpha_i,\alpha_i)\) if \(j\) is strictly better than the prescribed tie;
  • \((0,\alpha_i)\) if \(j\) belongs to that tie;
  • \((0,0)\) if \(j\) is strictly worse.

At an unmatched type it is \((\alpha_i,\alpha_i)\). The analogous formulas hold on the \(J\)-side. Thus every flow in this polytope is strongly stable.

After scaling by \(D\), the polytope has integral row and column margins. Its vertices are integral. For completeness, a vertex's positive support cannot contain a cycle: alternating positive and negative perturbations around a bipartite cycle preserve all margins and contradict extremality. Its positive support is therefore a forest. At a leaf, the incident edge equals that leaf's integral remaining margin; removing the leaf and subtracting this value from the neighboring margin proves integrality inductively.

A linear objective attains its maximum at a vertex of this nonempty bounded polytope. Such a vertex has integral scaled entries, and its designated-flow value equals the global optimum because the polytope contains the globally optimal \(x\). ∎

4.2. Binary search and running time

First run the existence algorithm. If it reports nonexistence, return that answer.

Otherwise set \(K=\min\{D\alpha_a,D\beta_b\}\). By Lemma 5, the optimum is \(k/D\) for some integer \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\).

Binary-search for the largest feasible \(k\), using Theorem 4 with threshold \(q=k/D\). Feasibility is monotone in \(k\). Since \(K\) has bit length \(O(L)\), this takes \(O(L+1)\) threshold calls.

For positive optimal \(k\), the successful threshold call returns an optimal flow. If the optimal \(k\) is zero, the initial existence flow is optimal.

Each call uses at most \(m+1\) maximum-flow computations on networks with at most \(\tau+2\) vertices and \(m+\tau\) arcs. All capacities and thresholds have polynomial bit length. Standard exact maximum-flow algorithms therefore give polynomial bit complexity; filtering edges and checking saturation also take polynomial time.

Thus the optimization problem, including returning an attaining flow or reporting nonexistence, is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).
