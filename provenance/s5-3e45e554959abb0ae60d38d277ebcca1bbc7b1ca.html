Result

For the directed stability notion in the question, the proposed limit is correct:
$$ R_q=\frac12\sqrt q\left(1+O\left(q^{-1/2}\log q\right)\right) $$
with high probability. Consequently,
$$ \frac{R_q}{\sqrt q}\xrightarrow{\mathbb P}\frac12. $$
Moreover, infeasibility has probability exactly zero:
$$ \mathbb P(R_q=+\infty)=0 \qquad\text{for every }q\geq2. $$

The key is an exact deterministic reduction from stable couplings to stable permutations.

1. The relevant permutation stability condition

Fix any strict preference profile satisfying the question’s assumptions. For a permutation $\sigma$ of $[q]$, define
$$ \pi^\sigma_{ab}=\frac1q\,\mathbf 1_{\{b=\sigma(a)\}}. $$
This matrix has every row and column sum $1/q$.

Its positive arcs are precisely $(a,\sigma(a))$. Substituting these arcs into the coupling stability condition shows that $\pi^\sigma$ is stable if and only if
$$ a\notin\{d,\sigma(d)\},\quad \sigma(d)\succ_a\sigma(a) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\succ_{\sigma(d)}a. $$
This is exactly the stable-permutation definition in Section 2 of [Pittel’s paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.06691), including both exclusions. Let $\mathcal S$ denote the permutations satisfying this condition, and define their total successor rank by
$$ S(\sigma)=\sum_{a=1}^q r_a(\sigma(a)). $$

2. Fractional mass cannot lower the optimum

Claim. Whenever $\mathcal S$ is nonempty,
$$ R_q=\frac1q\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal S}S(\sigma). $$

Proof. Let $\pi$ be any stable coupling. The matrix $X=q\pi$ is doubly stochastic: its entries are nonnegative, and every row and column sums to $1$.

The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem states that every such matrix is a finite convex combination of permutation matrices. Hence
$$ X=\sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_jP^{\sigma_j}, \qquad \lambda_j>0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_j=1, $$
where $P^\sigma_{ab}=\mathbf 1_{\{b=\sigma(a)\}}$.

For every $j$ and $a$,
$$ X_{a,\sigma_j(a)}\geq\lambda_j>0. $$
Thus every arc of $\sigma_j$ belongs to the support of $\pi$.

Stability is inherited when arcs are removed: every pair of arcs remaining in a smaller support already satisfied the required implication in the original support. Therefore each $\pi^{\sigma_j}$ is stable, so $\sigma_j\in\mathcal S$.

By linearity,
$$ \sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}r_a(b) =\frac1q\sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_j S(\sigma_j) \geq\frac1q\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal S}S(\sigma). $$
Conversely, a permutation attaining the finite minimum on the right gives a stable coupling attaining that value. This proves the claim.

More generally, every stable coupling satisfies
$$ \frac1q\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal S}S(\sigma) \leq \sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}r_a(b) \leq \frac1q\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal S}S(\sigma). $$

3. Feasibility holds for every preference profile

Pittel’s Theorem 1.1 states that the extended one-sided proposal algorithm produces a stable permutation for every strict preference profile, with the agent itself placed last as “no partner.” These are exactly our deterministic hypotheses, and Section 1 verifies that the stability definitions agree. Thus $\mathcal S$ is always nonempty. [Pittel, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.06691)

Its permutation coupling is feasible and stable. Therefore $R_q$ is finite for every realization of the preferences, proving
$$ \mathbb P(R_q=+\infty)=0. $$
This applies to both even and odd $q$, since directed permutation cycles are allowed.

4. Transfer of the random rank theorem

We now use the discrete rank theorem supplied in the question, also stated as Pittel’s Theorem 1.2(b). Its hypotheses are independent uniform strict rankings of the other agents, followed by the agent itself last. Our random model satisfies these hypotheses exactly. [Pittel, Theorem 1.2(b)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.06691)

Precisely, there are a constant $C$ and events $E_q$ with $\mathbb P(E_q)\to1$ such that, on $E_q$, every $\sigma\in\mathcal S$ satisfies
$$ \left| \frac{S(\sigma)}{\frac12q^{3/2}}-1 \right| \leq Cq^{-1/2}\log q. $$

Because $\mathcal S$ is nonempty, the same bounds hold for its minimum. The deterministic identity from Section 2 therefore gives, on $E_q$,
$$ \left|R_q-\frac12\sqrt q\right| \leq\frac C2\log q. $$
Dividing by $\sqrt q$, and using $\log q/\sqrt q\to0$, proves convergence in probability to $1/2$.

In fact, the same estimate holds simultaneously for the rank cost of every stable coupling, by the final inequality in Section 2.

5. Computation and input size

Pittel’s Theorem 1.1 also gives a permutation $\sigma_0$ whose successor is best for each agent among all stable permutations. Consequently,
$$ R_q=\frac1q\sum_a r_a(\sigma_0(a)). $$
[Pittel, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.06691)

With inverse-rank tables, each proposal takes constant word-RAM time, and each ordered pair of distinct agents receives at most one proposal. Including input reading and preprocessing, the running time is
$$ O(L+m\tau)=O(L+q^2), $$
using $O(m\tau)=O(q^2)$ words of storage, with words large enough for input addresses. Here $m=\tau=q$ counts the explicitly supported agents; no enumeration of possible rankings is involved.
