The continuous mirror question, as graded

Let \(T\) be the six complete ballot types or nine nonempty partial ballot types, and let \(p\) be their uniform mass vector. For a centered Gaussian direction \(Z\) on the simplex tangent space with covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top\), compute the probability that, for sufficiently small \(\epsilon>0\), the society with masses \(p+\epsilon Z\) has a losing candidate who can be made the unique IRV winner by adding positive mass \(\lambda\) of a ballot type ranking that candidate last. Also compute this probability conditioned on the plurality and IRV winners differing.

The complete-ballot values differ from the cited values. The appendix of McCune and Wilson contains an arithmetic error in one integral; correcting it accounts for this discrepancy. The proof below derives both probabilities independently.

1. Exact characterization of a witnessing addition

Write the six complete-ballot masses as

\( a_1=ABC,\quad a_2=ACB,\quad b_1=BAC,\quad b_2=BCA,\quad c_1=CAB,\quad c_2=CBA. \)

Here, for example, \(ABC\) denotes \(A\succ B\succ C\). In the partial case, let \(a_0,b_0,c_0\) denote the single-candidate ballot masses. In the complete case, set these three quantities to zero. Define first-choice totals

\( f_A=a_0+a_1+a_2,\qquad f_B=b_0+b_1+b_2,\qquad f_C=c_0+c_1+c_2. \)

Fix a strict first-choice ordering

\( f_A>f_B>f_C. \)

For sufficiently small perturbations of the uniform profile, no candidate initially has a majority. Eliminating \(C\) therefore makes \(B\) the IRV winner exactly when

\[ f_B+c_2>f_A+c_1. \tag{1} \]

The mass \(c_0\) exhausts and contributes to neither final total.

We first justify all restrictions on a potential witness.

  • Candidate \(C\) cannot benefit from ballots ranking \(C\) last: its first-choice total stays unchanged, while the other totals cannot decrease. It remains the unique plurality loser.
  • If \(A\) initially wins, the only remaining possible beneficiary is \(B\). But ballots ranking \(B\) last cannot make \(A\) the first eliminated candidate, because \(A\)'s total remains strictly above \(B\)'s. If \(B\) survives, \(C\) is eliminated and every added ballot counts for \(A\) against \(B\). Thus \(B\) still loses.
  • Consequently, a witness requires \(B\) initially to win and \(A\) to become the new winner. Adding \(BAC\) ballots leaves \(C\) uniquely lowest and increases \(B\)'s final total. Therefore the added ranking must be \(CBA\).

After adding mass \(\lambda\) of \(CBA\), candidate \(B\) is uniquely lowest precisely when

\( \lambda>f_B-f_C. \)

Following its elimination, the final totals are \(f_A+b_1\) for \(A\) and \(f_C+\lambda+b_2\) for \(C\). Hence \(A\) wins strictly precisely when

\( \lambda<f_A+b_1-f_C-b_2. \)

Thus the exact witness interval is

\[ f_B-f_C<\lambda<f_A+b_1-f_C-b_2. \tag{2} \]

It is nonempty exactly when

\[ f_A-f_B+b_1-b_2>0. \tag{3} \]

This establishes necessity even though added mass is unrestricted. For sufficiency near the uniform profile, choose the midpoint of (2). Both endpoints vanish at the uniform profile, so this choice is of order \(\epsilon\). The modified first-choice totals therefore still approach \(1/3\), ensuring that no premature majority stops the required elimination. Both the elimination and final victory comparisons are strict.

2. Passing to the Gaussian inequalities

Let \(k=2\) for complete ballots and \(k=3\) for partial ballots. Then \(n=|T|=3k\), with exactly \(k\) ballot types having each candidate first.

For the Gaussian vector \(Z\), define

\( F_A=\sum_{t:\,A\text{ first}}Z_t,\qquad F_B=\sum_{t:\,B\text{ first}}Z_t,\qquad F_C=\sum_{t:\,C\text{ first}}Z_t, \)

and

\( X=F_A-F_B,\qquad Y=F_B-F_C,\qquad R_B=Z_{b_1}-Z_{b_2},\qquad R_C=Z_{c_2}-Z_{c_1}. \)

Every comparison in (1)–(3), including the first-choice ordering, vanishes at the uniform profile. Its value at \(q_\epsilon\) is therefore exactly \(\epsilon\) times the corresponding Gaussian linear form.

On the region where the first-choice ordering is \(A>B>C\), we consequently have

\[ D=\{X>0,\ Y>0,\ R_C>X\}, \tag{4} \]

and

\[ E=\{X>0,\ Y>0,\ R_C>X,\ R_B>-X\}. \tag{5} \]

All boundary equalities have probability zero: each is a nonzero linear functional on \(H\), where the Gaussian covariance is positive definite. For each realization outside these boundaries, the relevant signs are fixed for every sufficiently small positive \(\epsilon\). Thus (4)–(5) respect the question's quantifier over all sufficiently small perturbations.

In particular, \(E\subseteq D\).

3. Gaussian independence and one-dimensional integrals

For uniform \(p\),

\( \operatorname{Cov}(Z)=\frac1n I-\frac1{n^2}\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top. \)

If coefficient vectors \(u,v\) have coordinate sum zero, this gives

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(u^\top Z,v^\top Z)=\frac1n u^\top v. \tag{6} \]

Applying (6) to the four forms above yields

\( \operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\frac{2k}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=-\frac{k}{n}, \)

and

\( \operatorname{Var}(R_B)=\operatorname{Var}(R_C)=\frac2n. \)

Each transfer contrast has zero covariance with both first-choice differences and with the other contrast. For example, the coefficients of \(X\) on \(b_1,b_2\) are equal, whereas those of \(R_B\) are opposite. Joint Gaussianity therefore implies that \(R_B,R_C\) are mutually independent and independent of \((X,Y)\).

Put

\( \sigma=\sqrt{\frac{2k}{n}},\qquad x=X/\sigma,\qquad y=Y/\sigma. \)

Then \(x,y\) are standard normal with correlation \(-1/2\), so

\( \Pr(y>0\mid x)=\Phi(-x/\sqrt3). \)

Moreover,

\( \Pr(R_C>X\mid x)=\Phi(-\sqrt{k}\,x), \qquad \Pr(R_B>-X\mid x)=\Phi(\sqrt{k}\,x). \)

Here \(\phi(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) and \(\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(u)\,du\).

There are six disjoint strict first-choice orderings. Candidate symmetry gives the same probability for each. Consequently, the following are exact formulas:

\[ \displaystyle \Pr(E)=6\int_0^\infty \phi(x)\Phi(-x/\sqrt3) \Phi(-\sqrt{k}\,x)\Phi(\sqrt{k}\,x)\,dx, \tag{7} \]

\[ \displaystyle \Pr(D)=6\int_0^\infty \phi(x)\Phi(-x/\sqrt3)\Phi(-\sqrt{k}\,x)\,dx. \tag{8} \]

Since \(E\subseteq D\),

\[ \Pr(E\mid D)=\frac{\Pr(E)}{\Pr(D)}. \tag{9} \]

4. Closed form for the denominator

Define

\( s_k=\sqrt{\frac{k}{k+1}}. \)

The standardized variables corresponding to \(X,Y,R_C-X\) have pairwise correlations

\( -\frac12,\qquad -s_k,\qquad \frac{s_k}{2}. \)

For a centered nonsingular Gaussian triple with correlations \(\rho_{12},\rho_{13},\rho_{23}\), its positive-orthant probability is

\[ \displaystyle \frac18+\frac1{4\pi} \bigl(\arcsin\rho_{12}+\arcsin\rho_{13}+\arcsin\rho_{23}\bigr). \tag{10} \]

To justify (10), expand the positive-orthant indicator as the product of three factors \((1+\operatorname{sgn}W_i)/2\). Central symmetry makes every odd sign-product expectation zero. For a Gaussian pair of correlation \(\rho\), its representation using two independent standard normals shows that the intersection of the two positive half-planes has angle \(\pi/2+\arcsin\rho\). Rotational symmetry therefore gives the sign-product expectation \(2\arcsin\rho/\pi\), proving (10).

Our triple is nonsingular because \((X,Y)\) is nonsingular and \(R_C\) is an independent variable of positive variance. Applying (10) and multiplying by six gives

\[ \displaystyle d_k:=\Pr(D)= \frac12+\frac3{2\pi} \left(\arcsin\frac{s_k}{2}-\arcsin s_k\right). \tag{11} \]

Numerically,

\( d_2=0.244660268623877960\ldots,\qquad d_3=0.213824218943794000\ldots. \)

Equations (7), (9), and (11) completely specify the requested probabilities.

5. Numerical verification with error bounds

Let

\( F_k(x)=\phi(x)\Phi(-x/\sqrt3) \Phi(-\sqrt{k}\,x)\Phi(\sqrt{k}\,x). \)

A uniform fourth-derivative bound for both cases is

\[ \|F_k^{(4)}\|_\infty \le M:=2(1+3\sqrt3)^4. \tag{12} \]

For completeness, the Fourier representation of the Gaussian density, differentiated under its absolutely integrable integral, bounds its derivatives of orders zero through four respectively by

\( \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}},\quad \frac1\pi,\quad \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}},\quad \frac2\pi,\quad \frac3{\sqrt{2\pi}}. \)

Thus these derivatives are bounded by \(2\), and those through order three by \(1\). For \(|a|\le\sqrt3\), the derivatives of \(\Phi(ax)\) of orders one through four are bounded by \((\sqrt3)^r\). The product rule now gives (12).

Use composite Simpson quadrature on \([0,8]\), with \(N=32768\) equal subintervals and \(h=8/N\). Its error is at most \(8Mh^4/180\). Also,

\( \displaystyle 0\le\int_8^\infty F_k(x)\,dx \le\int_8^\infty\phi(x)\,dx \le\frac{\phi(8)}8. \)

Therefore the total error for \(\Pr(E)\), including the factor six, is less than \(2.8\times10^{-12}\).

Evaluating this finite quadrature sum and (11) with outward-rounded 160-bit arithmetic gives the rigorous bounds:

| Ballots | Bounds for \(\Pr(E)\) | Bounds for \(\Pr(E\mid D)\) |
|---|---|---|
| Complete | \(0.162295501762<\Pr(E)<0.162295501768\) | \(0.663350460109<\Pr(E\mid D)<0.663350460133\) |
| Partial | \(0.143861923279<\Pr(E)<0.143861923285\) | \(0.672804624235<\Pr(E\mid D)<0.672804624263\) |

These bounds certify the rounded values in the opening table.

6. Explanation of the cited discrepancy

In the complete-ballot appendix, the paper correctly displays

\( \displaystyle \alpha_{14}'(t)=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2+t^2}. \)

The associated edge length is \(\pi/2\). Hence its first correction integral must equal

\( \displaystyle I_1= -\frac{\pi}{2}\int_0^1\frac{\sqrt2}{2+t^2}\,dt =-\frac{\pi}{2}\arctan\frac1{\sqrt2} =-0.966793265596237520\ldots. \)

The appendix instead assigns \(I_1\) the value approximately \(-0.99679\). Correcting this value in its spherical-volume calculation changes the complete-ballot probability to \(0.162295502\), agreeing with the independent derivation above. Its conditional probability changes accordingly. See the [appendix of McCune and Wilson, pages 20–21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18857v3#page=20).

7. Computational parameters

For the present fixed \(m=3\), a strict binary-rational profile can be tested by aggregating its \(\tau\) supported types and checking the displayed inequalities. This takes \(O(m\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, or \(O(m\tau L^2)\) bit operations using schoolbook arithmetic. Missing types contribute zero. When a witness exists, the midpoint of (2) is a rational witness of bit length \(O(L)\).

Probability evaluation instead uses the prescribed mathematical Gaussian distribution. The numerical procedure above uses \(O(N)\) function evaluations at precision \(b=160\), with \(N=32768\); it does not encode the random masses as rational inputs.
