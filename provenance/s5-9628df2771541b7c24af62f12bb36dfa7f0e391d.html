The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite project catalogue \(P\), lecturers \(L\), and a list of \(\tau\) student preference types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), together with rational per-capita project and lecturer capacities, compute an allocation \(q_{t,r}\geq 0\), where \(r\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}\), that maximizes \(\sum_{t\in T,p\in A_t}q_{t,p}\). Require each type’s assigned mass to sum to \(\mu_t\), all capacities to hold, and no positive mass to form a blocking pair under the paper’s lecturer conditions. Also require the envy graph on occupied categories \((t,r)\), with an edge to \((u,s)\) when type \(t\) prefers \(s\) to \(r\), to be acyclic. Equivalently, decide for a rational threshold \(z\) whether such an allocation assigning mass at least \(z\) exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P\) be a nonempty catalogue of \(m\) projects and \(\mathcal L\) a set of lecturers whose nonempty offered sets \(P_k\) partition \(P\). Lecturer \(k\) strictly ranks \(P_k\) and is indifferent among students assigned to the same project. Project capacities \(c_p\) and lecturer capacities \(d_k\) are positive binary rationals.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Each type strictly ranks an acceptable set \(A_t\subseteq P\), possibly empty, above being unassigned, denoted \(\bot\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of real masses \(q_{t,r}\geq0\) for \(r\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}\), with \(\sum_r q_{t,r}=\mu_t\). Set \(q_{t,p}=0\) for unacceptable projects, \(x_p=\sum_t q_{t,p}\), and \(y_k=\sum_{p\in P_k}x_p\); require \(x_p\leq c_p\) and \(y_k\leq d_k\).
An occupied category \((t,r)\), meaning \(q_{t,r}>0\), blocks with \(p\in A_t\) offered by \(k\) exactly when \(p\succ_t r\), \(x_p<c_p\), and either: \(r\in P_k\) and \(p\succ_k r\); or \(r\notin P_k\) and \(y_k<d_k\); or \(r\notin P_k\) and some \(w\in P_k\) satisfies \(x_w>0\) and \(p\succ_k w\). Take \(\bot\notin P_k\).
The envy graph has all occupied categories as vertices and an edge \((t,r)\to(u,s)\) exactly when \(s\in A_t\) and \(s\succ_t r\). Call an allocation stable if it has no blocking category–project pair and this graph is acyclic.
Given a binary rational threshold \(z\), the decision problem asks whether a stable allocation exists with \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{p\in A_t}q_{t,p}\geq z\).

Is this continuous decision problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard, with running times stated in \(m,\tau,|\mathcal L|,L\)?

For finite students, an [exact integer programming formulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.09993v1) has optimal solutions corresponding precisely to maximum-cardinality stable matchings. The discrete problem is [NP-hard even with two lecturers, and polynomial-time solvable with one lecturer](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/222006/1/222006.pdf).
No result settling the exact continuous problem defined above was located in the literature search.

  • Interpreted lecturer membership separately for each occupied assignment category, rather than for an entire type whose mass may be split among lecturers.
  • Defined positive-mass blocking by applying all three discrete lecturer conditions to occupied categories, with undersubscription measured by strict inequalities.

Classification

The continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-complete}\), even with \(|\mathcal L|=2\). Thus a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|\mathcal L|,L\) is impossible unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

For \(|\mathcal L|=1\), it is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, some stable allocation attains the maximum assigned mass among all feasible allocations.

The crucial step is an integrality lemma that preserves stability while rounding. Simply allowing fractional variables in the cited integer programme would not establish this conclusion.

1. A sufficient condition for preserving stability

Write

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{supp}(q)=\{(t,r):q_{t,r}>0\},\qquad M(q)=\sum_t\sum_{p\in A_t}q_{t,p}.\)

Call a project or lecturer *saturated* when its load equals its capacity.

Lemma 1. Let \(q\) be stable, and let \(q'\) be feasible for the same instance. Suppose:

  • \(\operatorname{supp}(q')\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(q)\);
  • every project saturated under \(q\) remains saturated under \(q'\);
  • every lecturer saturated under \(q\) remains saturated under \(q'\).

Then \(q'\) is stable.

Proof. The envy graph of \(q'\) is the subgraph of the envy graph of \(q\) induced by its remaining occupied categories. Consequently, it is acyclic.

Suppose that an occupied category \((t,r)\) blocks with project \(p\), offered by lecturer \(k\), under \(q'\). Then \((t,r)\) was occupied under \(q\), and \(p\succ_t r\).

Moreover, \(x'_p<c_p\). By condition 2, \(p\) could not have been saturated under \(q\); hence \(x_p<c_p\).

Consider the lecturer-side condition causing the block.

  • If \(r\in P_k\) and \(p\succ_k r\), this same condition holds under \(q\).
  • If \(r\notin P_k\) and \(y'_k<d_k\), condition 3 implies \(y_k<d_k\).
  • If \(r\notin P_k\) and \(x'_w>0\) for some \(w\in P_k\) with \(p\succ_k w\), support containment implies \(x_w>0\).

In every case, \((t,r)\) also blocks with \(p\) under \(q\), contradicting its stability. \(\square\)

Notice that previously unsaturated projects or lecturers may become saturated. Previously occupied categories may disappear. Both changes are harmless.

2. Rounding stable allocations through an integral flow

For this section, temporarily allow arbitrary positive type masses, without requiring their sum to equal \(1\).

Lemma 2. Suppose all type masses, project capacities, and lecturer capacities are integers. For every stable real allocation \(q\), there is a stable integral allocation \(Q\) satisfying \(M(Q)\geq M(q)\).

Proof. Let \(a_t\) denote the integral mass of type \(t\), and let \(H=\sum_t a_t\).

Construct a directed network with source \(\sigma\), sink \(\omega\), a node for each type, a node for each project, and a node for each lecturer. Its arcs and bounds are:

| Arc | Lower bound | Upper bound |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(\sigma\to t\) | \(a_t\) | \(a_t\) |
| \(t\to p\), when \(q_{t,p}>0\) | \(0\) | \(a_t\) |
| \(t\to\omega\), when \(q_{t,\bot}>0\) | \(0\) | \(a_t\) |
| \(p\to k\), where \(p\in P_k\) | \(c_p\) if \(x_p=c_p\), otherwise \(0\) | \(c_p\) |
| \(k\to\omega\) | \(d_k\) if \(y_k=d_k\), otherwise \(0\) | \(d_k\) |
| \(\omega\to\sigma\) | \(H\) | \(H\) |

Impose flow conservation at every node. The original allocation \(q\) supplies a feasible circulation.

Maximize the total flow on type-to-project arcs. The feasible circulation polytope is nonempty and bounded, so this linear objective attains an optimum at a vertex.

Every vertex is integral. Here is a direct justification. If a feasible circulation has nonintegral arc values, consider the undirected graph consisting of those arcs. No vertex can have exactly one incident nonintegral arc: conservation would equate that nonintegral value, with a sign, to a sum of integers. Therefore this graph contains an undirected cycle.

Each cycle arc lies strictly between its integral bounds. A sufficiently small signed perturbation around the cycle preserves conservation and all bounds, in both directions. Thus the circulation is the midpoint of two distinct feasible circulations and is not a vertex. This proves vertex integrality.

Choose an integral optimal circulation and read its type-to-project and type-to-sink flows as \(Q_{t,p}\) and \(Q_{t,\bot}\). Flow conservation gives the required type masses and loads. Optimality gives \(M(Q)\geq M(q)\).

Only originally occupied categories were permitted, and every originally saturated project and lecturer was forced to remain saturated. Lemma 1 therefore proves that \(Q\) is stable. \(\square\)

The rounding lemma preserves the exact blocking definition, including its dependence on strict slack and positive project occupancy.

3. Polynomial-size witnesses and membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and capacities. Its binary length is at most the total input bit length, up to a constant.

Multiplying masses, capacities, and an allocation by \(D\) preserves every preference comparison, positivity condition, saturation condition, and envy edge. The scaled instance has integral masses and capacities.

Apply Lemma 2 to any stable allocation meeting the threshold, then divide the resulting integral allocation by \(D\). We obtain a stable allocation \(q'\) meeting the threshold with

\(\displaystyle q'_{t,r}\in \left\{0,\frac1D,\frac2D,\ldots,1\right\}.\)

Each coordinate therefore has \(O(L)\) bits. Define

\(\displaystyle a=\sum_t|A_t|,\qquad N=a+\tau\leq\tau(m+1).\)

There are \(N\) allocation coordinates, so a certificate has size \(O(NL)\).

Given such a certificate, a verifier can:

  • check row sums, capacities, and the threshold by exact rational arithmetic;
  • compute saturation and occupancy;
  • check every occupied category–project pair for blocking;
  • construct the envy graph and test it for acyclicity.

The graph has at most \(N\) vertices and \(N^2\) edges. All these checks take \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|\mathcal L|,L)\) bit operations. Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

4. Transfer of discrete hardness

4.1 The finite source problem

We use the finite-student cardinality-threshold version of the same stability notion: no blocking pair and no coalition of students cyclically improving their assigned projects.

The cited paper’s Section 3.2 establishes two-lecturer hardness and preserves optimal cardinality from the original hardness construction. In that construction, the optimum is \(2u+v-\beta(G)\), where \(\beta(G)\) is the minimum size of a maximal matching in the source graph. Thus its hardness applies to cardinality thresholds, not merely to outputting an optimal matching. See [Manlove, Milne and Olaosebikan, Section 3.2 and Theorem 4](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/222006/1/222006.pdf) and [Manlove and O’Malley, Section 3](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25739/1/25739.pdf).

The finite blocking definition agrees with the question’s definition. In particular, preferring the worst nonempty project is equivalent to preferring some nonempty project. Also, a coalition is exactly a directed cycle in the finite envy graph. Unassigned vertices cannot belong to a cycle because no envy edge targets an unassigned student.

4.2 Construction of the continuous instance

Take a finite instance with \(n\geq1\) students, integer capacities \(C_p,D_k\), and cardinality threshold \(B\).

Let \(h\) be the smallest power of \(2\) satisfying \(h\geq n\). Add \(h-n\) dummy students with empty acceptable sets. Group all students with identical preference lists into types, and let \(n_t\) be the number of students of type \(t\).

Define

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=\frac{n_t}{h},\qquad c_p=\frac{C_p}{h},\qquad d_k=\frac{D_k}{h},\qquad z=\frac{B}{h}.\)

Retain the project partition and all preference orders.

Every listed mass is positive, and the masses sum to \(1\). All numbers are binary-encoded rationals; indeed, they are dyadic. The construction takes polynomial time and has \(\tau\leq n+1\).

Dummy students cannot be assigned or block, so adding them does not change the finite optimum.

4.3 Grouping preserves stability

For any finite matching of the enlarged instance, define \(Q_{t,r}\) to count the students of type \(t\) assigned to category \(r\), and set \(q_{t,r}=Q_{t,r}/h\).

Feasibility and blocking comparisons are preserved exactly.

We must also check acyclicity after grouping.

A finite envy cycle maps to a directed closed walk in the category graph. Consecutive vertices cannot collapse to the same category, because that would require strict preference for its project over itself. Every directed closed walk contains a directed cycle.

Conversely, from a simple category cycle, select one student realizing each occupied category. Distinct categories contain disjoint sets of students, so these representatives are distinct. The same preference comparisons give a finite envy cycle.

Thus the finite matching is coalition-free exactly when the grouped category graph is acyclic.

4.4 Equivalence of the decision instances

If the finite instance has a stable matching of size at least \(B\), grouping and scaling gives a stable allocation with assigned mass at least \(B/h=z\).

Conversely, suppose the continuous instance has a stable allocation of assigned mass at least \(B/h\). Multiply it by \(h\). The resulting masses and capacities are integers. Lemma 2 gives a stable integral allocation \(Q\) with \(M(Q)\geq B\).

For each type, distribute its \(n_t\) individual students among categories according to the integral counts \(Q_{t,r}\). The grouping argument proves that the resulting finite matching is stable.

We have therefore proved

\(\displaystyle \text{finite stable matching of size at least }B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{continuous stable allocation of mass at least }B/h.\)

The reduction retains the lecturers. It consequently proves hardness even when \(|\mathcal L|=2\). Combined with Section 3, this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-completeness}\).

5. One lecturer: a polynomial-time algorithm

Assume \(|\mathcal L|=1\), so every project belongs to the same lecturer.

Choose integer scores \(b_p\) strictly decreasing along the lecturer’s project ranking. For each type, choose integer scores \(a_{t,p}\) strictly decreasing along its acceptable-project ranking, with \(a_{t,\bot}=0\). All scores can lie between \(0\) and \(m\).

Over the ordinary feasible allocation polytope, successively maximize:

\(\displaystyle A(q)=M(q),\qquad B(q)=\sum_p b_p x_p,\qquad C(q)=\sum_t\sum_{r\in A_t\cup\{\bot\}}a_{t,r}q_{t,r}.\)

Each maximization is restricted to the optima of the preceding objectives. The polytope is nonempty and compact, so these three linear programmes have solutions.

Proposition. Every resulting allocation is stable.

Proof: absence of blocks. Suppose \((t,r)\) blocks with \(p\).

If \(r\) is a project, the one-lecturer blocking condition requires \(p\succ_k r\). Moving a sufficiently small positive mass from \((t,r)\) to \((t,p)\) is feasible because \(q_{t,r}>0\) and \(x_p<c_p\). It preserves \(A\) and increases \(B\), a contradiction.

Otherwise \(r=\bot\).

If the lecturer has slack, moving a sufficiently small positive mass from \((t,\bot)\) to \((t,p)\) increases \(A\), a contradiction.

If the lecturer is saturated, blocking requires an occupied project \(w\) with \(p\succ_k w\). Choose a type \(u\) with \(q_{u,w}>0\). Assign a sufficiently small mass of type \(t\) from \(\bot\) to \(p\), and move the same mass of type \(u\) from \(w\) to \(\bot\). This is feasible, preserves \(A\), and increases \(B\) by \(\varepsilon(b_p-b_w)>0\), again a contradiction. These changes remain valid when \(u=t\).

Proof: absence of envy cycles. Suppose a simple envy cycle consists of categories

\(\displaystyle (t_1,r_1)\to(t_2,r_2)\to\cdots\to(t_s,r_s)\to(t_1,r_1).\)

No \(r_i\) is \(\bot\), since no edge targets an unassigned category. For every \(i\), move a sufficiently small common mass \(\varepsilon>0\) of type \(t_i\) from \(r_i\) to \(r_{i+1}\), with indices interpreted cyclically.

All destination projects are acceptable by the edge definition. Type masses are preserved, and project loads are unchanged because the destination projects cyclically permute the source projects. Thus feasibility, \(A\), and \(B\) are preserved.

The change in \(C\) is

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^s\bigl(a_{t_i,r_{i+1}}-a_{t_i,r_i}\bigr)>0,\)

because every envy edge represents strict improvement. This contradicts optimality of \(C\). \(\square\)

Therefore the maximum stable assigned mass equals the maximum feasible assigned mass. Three rational linear programmes construct a maximizing stable allocation in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time.

6. Running times

For one lecturer, the decision itself needs only an ordinary maximum flow. Use source-to-type capacities \(\mu_t\), acceptable type-to-project arcs, project-to-lecturer capacities \(c_p\), and lecturer-to-sink capacity \(d_k\).

This network has

\(\displaystyle V=\tau+m+3,\qquad E=a+\tau+m+1.\)

After clearing denominators, capacities can be capped at the total mass \(D\) for computing the maximum-flow value. Each capacity then has \(O(L)\) bits. Edmonds–Karp uses \(O(VE^2)\) additions and comparisons, giving the decision bound

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{poly}(L)+O\!\left((\tau+m+3)(a+\tau+m+1)^2L\right).\)

The proposition above guarantees that this maximum-flow value is attainable by a stable allocation.

For arbitrary lecturers, an exact deterministic algorithm has running time

\(\displaystyle 2^{\,N+m+|\mathcal L|}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|\mathcal L|,L) \ \leq\ 2^{\,\tau(m+1)+m+|\mathcal L|}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,|\mathcal L|,L).\)

To obtain this bound, enumerate a permitted category support, a set of projects forced to be saturated, and a set of lecturers forced to be saturated. In each branch, maximize assigned mass using the corresponding bounded circulation, then verify stability.

Soundness follows from the verification. For completeness, any stable witness supplies a branch containing its actual support and saturated sets. Every feasible allocation in that branch satisfies Lemma 1 relative to the witness, so an optimum in that branch is stable and has at least the witness’s assigned mass.

These algorithms operate directly on the listed support. None expands a rational mass into a number of students depending on its denominator.
