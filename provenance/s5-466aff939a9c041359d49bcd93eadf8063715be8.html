The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with distribution \(\mu\), item classes \(O\) with per-capita supplies \(q_o\), additive utilities \(u_t(o)\), and normalized type weights \(\beta_t\), find a measurable assignment of integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{O}\) with mass \(\lambda_{t,B}\), satisfying \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_o=q_o\), such that almost every type-\(t\) agent satisfies weighted PROP1 relative to \(P_t=\beta_t\sum_o q_o u_t(o)\), and the assignment is fPO against fractional reallocations preserving the aggregate supplies.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

For rational input \(T,\mu,O,q,u,\beta\), let \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_t\beta_t=1\), \(\mu_t,\beta_t>0\), and \(U_t(B)=\sum_o B_o u_t(o)\) for \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{O}\). Let \(P_t=\beta_t\sum_o q_o u_t(o)\).
A feasible assignment is masses \(\lambda_{t,B}\ge0\) with \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_o=q_o\). It is weighted PROP1 if, whenever \(\lambda_{t,B}>0\), either \(U_t(B)\ge P_t\), or \(U_t(B)+u_t(o)\ge P_t\) for some \(o\) with \(q_o>0\), or \(B_o\ge1\) and \(U_t(B)-u_t(o)\ge P_t\) for some \(o\).
It is fPO if no feasible fractional reallocation makes almost every individual weakly better off and a positive-mass set strictly better off.

Is this finite-type distributional problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many individual agents with additive mixed utilities and asymmetric weights, an integral weighted-PROP1 and fPO allocation always exists.
Moreover, such a discrete allocation is computable in strongly polynomial time.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Deciding whether a feasible weighted-PROP1 and fPO assignment exists is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with two equally sized types, equal weights, and identical strictly negative utilities. The reduction below accounts explicitly for the fact that bundles may contain arbitrarily many copies of an object.

1. Source problem and numerical encoding.

We reduce from PARTITION: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) with \(\sum_i a_i=2K\), decide whether some subset sums to \(K\). This remains \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete with \(n\ge 2\) and even total sum: doubling all input numbers ensures an even total without changing the answer.

We first construct positive integer costs \(c_{i,0},c_{i,1}\) and an integer \(M\) such that

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n(c_{i,0}+c_{i,1})=2M, \]

and every nonnegative integer solution of

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(c_{i,0}z_{i,0}+c_{i,1}z_{i,1}\bigr)=M \]

necessarily satisfies \(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}=1\) for every \(i\), and \(\sum_i a_i z_{i,1}=K\).

Here and below, the displayed equations can equivalently be read as inline formulas. Define \(A=2K\), \(b=n+1\), \(R=nA+1\), and \(D=\sum_{i=1}^n b^{i-1}\). Set

\(L=Rb^{n-1}+A,\qquad H=nL+1,\)

\(c_{i,0}=H+Rb^{i-1},\qquad c_{i,1}=H+Rb^{i-1}+a_i,\)

and \(M=nH+RD+K\).

Summing the paired costs gives \(\sum_i(c_{i,0}+c_{i,1})=2nH+2RD+A=2M\).

We now prove the claimed restriction on integer solutions. Each cost belongs to \([H,H+L]\). Also,

\(RD+K\le nRb^{n-1}+A/2<H,\)

so \(nH\le M<(n+1)H\).

Let \(k=\sum_{i,j}z_{i,j}\) be the total number of objects in a solution of cost \(M\).

  • If \(k\ge n+1\), its cost is at least \((n+1)H>M\).
  • If \(k\le n-1\), its cost is at most \((n-1)(H+L)<nH\le M\), since \((n-1)L<H\).

Consequently, \(k=n\). Subtracting \(nH\) from the cost equation gives

\(R\sum_i b^{i-1}(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1})+\sum_i a_i z_{i,1}=RD+K.\)

Write \(S=\sum_i a_i z_{i,1}\). Because there are exactly \(n\) objects, \(0\le S\le nA<R\); also \(0\le K<R\). Reduction modulo \(R\) therefore yields \(S=K\). The remaining equality is

\(\sum_i b^{i-1}(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1})=D.\)

Each coefficient \(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}\) lies between \(0\) and \(n=b-1\). Uniqueness of base-\(b\) representation implies \(z_{i,0}+z_{i,1}=1\) for every \(i\). Thus the indices with \(z_{i,1}=1\) form a partition of sum \(K\).

Conversely, any partition of sum \(K\) selects exactly one cost from each pair and has total cost \(M\). Its complementary selection also has total cost \(M\).

Finally, each \(c_{i,j}<M\): indeed, \(c_{i,j}\le H+L<2H\le nH\le M\).

2. The distributional instance.

Use two types \(T=\{1,2\}\), with \(\mu_1=\mu_2=\beta_1=\beta_2=1/2\).

There is one distinguished object \(d\), together with the objects \((i,0),(i,1)\) constructed above. Their supplies are

\(q_d=1,\qquad q_{i,0}=q_{i,1}=1/2.\)

Both types have identical utilities:

\(u_t(d)=-M,\qquad u_t(i,j)=-c_{i,j}.\)

For a bundle \(B\), define its positive cost by

\(C(B)=MB_d+\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}B_{i,j}.\)

Thus \(U_t(B)=-C(B)\). The total supplied cost is

\(Mq_d+\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}q_{i,j}=M+\frac12(2M)=2M.\)

Hence the proportional benchmark of either type is \(P_t=-M\).

Every object is a chore. Adding one therefore cannot repair a violation of proportionality. Removing an object of cost \(c\) succeeds precisely when \(C(B)-c\le M\). Since every object costs at most \(M\), every weighted-PROP1 bundle satisfies

\(C(B)\le 2M.\)

Moreover, if \(C(B)=2M\), a successful removal must remove an object costing at least \(M\). The only such object is \(d\). Thus every weighted-PROP1 bundle of cost \(2M\) contains \(d\).

3. Every feasible weighted-PROP1 assignment yields a partition.

Suppose an assignment satisfies feasibility and weighted PROP1. Its total mass is \(1\), and feasibility gives

\(\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}C(B)=2M.\)

Every supported bundle has cost at most \(2M\). Therefore every supported bundle must have cost exactly \(2M\): any positive mass at a strictly smaller cost would make the average strictly smaller than \(2M\).

By the preceding argument, every supported bundle contains at least one copy of \(d\). Feasibility for \(d\) gives

\(\sum_{t,B}\lambda_{t,B}B_d=q_d=1.\)

Since total mass is \(1\), every supported bundle must contain exactly one copy of \(d\). Consequently, its remaining objects satisfy

\(\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}B_{i,j}=M.\)

The numerical encoding proved in Section 1 now produces a subset of the original numbers summing to \(K\).

This implication uses no efficiency assumption: feasibility and weighted PROP1 alone already force a solution of PARTITION. It also applies to assignments with infinite support, because the arguments use only sums of nonnegative quantities and the feasibility equalities.

4. Every partition yields a feasible weighted-PROP1 assignment.

Conversely, suppose \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in I}a_i=K\).

Construct two bundles \(B^+\) and \(B^-\). Each contains exactly one \(d\). For each index \(i\):

  • \(B^+\) contains \((i,1)\) if \(i\in I\), and \((i,0)\) otherwise.
  • \(B^-\) contains the other object of that pair.

Both selections outside \(d\) have cost \(M\), so both bundles have cost \(2M\). Removing \(d\) leaves utility \(-M=P_t\); hence both bundles satisfy weighted PROP1 for either type.

For each type, assign mass \(1/4\) to each bundle:

\(\lambda_{t,B^+}=\lambda_{t,B^-}=1/4.\)

Each type receives total mass \(1/2\). The aggregate consumption of \(d\) is \(1\), and each other object occurs in exactly one of the two bundles, giving aggregate consumption \(1/2\). Thus all feasibility equalities hold.

5. Fractional Pareto optimality is automatic here.

All individuals have the same additive utility function. Under every feasible allocation, including every feasible fractional reallocation, integrated utility equals the utility of total supply, namely \(-2M\).

A reallocation that weakly improves almost every individual and strictly improves a positive-mass set would strictly increase integrated utility. To justify the strictness, the utility gain would be a nonnegative measurable function positive on a positive-mass set, and hence have strictly positive integral.

All utilities involved are integrable: there are finitely many objects, their costs are finite, and their aggregate quantities are fixed and finite. The strict increase therefore contradicts the invariant integrated utility.

Consequently, every feasible assignment in the constructed instance is fPO. In particular, the assignment in Section 4 satisfies both requested properties.

6. Polynomial size and the discrete theorem.

The construction uses two types and \(2n+1\) object types. The largest numerical quantities have binary length \(O(\log A+n\log n)\). Thus the reduction has polynomial size and can be computed in polynomial time.

We have proved the equivalence:

\(\text{PARTITION is a yes-instance}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the constructed instance admits a feasible weighted-PROP1 and fPO assignment}.\)

There is no contradiction with the supplied discrete existence theorem. Its individual weights must sum to \(1\). If each individual in a discrete expansion represents mass \(1/N\), the expansion has \(N\mu_t\) individuals of type \(t\) and \(Nq_o\) copies of object \(o\). Matching the question’s benchmark would require each such individual to have weight \(\beta_t/N\). These individual weights sum to

\(\sum_t(N\mu_t)(\beta_t/N)=\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t,\)

which equals \(1/2\) in this reduction, rather than \(1\). Thus the normalization required by the discrete theorem fails.

The target feasibility problem is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; a polynomial-time algorithm that always constructs a valid assignment or correctly reports infeasibility would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
