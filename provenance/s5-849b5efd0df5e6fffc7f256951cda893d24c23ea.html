The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational unit-mass distributions \(\mu_B\) and \(\mu_S\) over finitely many buyer and seller types, where each type specifies a valuation, feedback-noise law, and UCB or LCB parameter, run the paper’s decentralized bidding protocol in a continuum Average auction. Each type carries a measure over agents’ private feedback histories; the auction clears by bid and ask mass quantiles, splits ties proportionally, and sets the price to the average marginal bid and ask. Does its expected aggregate welfare regret relative to the oracle allocation satisfy \(T W^* - \mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^T W_t] \le P(\tau_B,\tau_S,\alpha_{\max},1/\Delta)\log T + O(1)\), with no dependence on a finite number of agents representing those masses, or is there a counterexample?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Consider one indistinguishable good, with buyer and seller societies each of total mass \(1\). Each society is given by its support: its list of positive-mass types and their binary rational masses. Write \(\tau_B,\tau_S\) for support sizes and \(\tau=\tau_B+\tau_S\), not the number of possible types; any running times are measured in \(m=1,\tau,L,T\) and other stated parameters, where \(L\) is the input bit length.
Each type has a nonnegative rational valuation \(v\), a rational confidence parameter \(\alpha\ge4\), and a finitely specified, mean-zero, \(1\)-sub-Gaussian noise law. Valuations are unknown to agents. Each agent can trade one unit per round and observes \(Y=v+\nu\) only when trading; fresh noise draws are independent across observations, with the law determined by type. Agents retain their own histories and never pool observations.
Initially all agents trade at price \(0\), receiving one observation. In round \(t\ge2\), an agent with \(n\) previous trades and empirical valuation \(\widehat v\) submits \(b=\widehat v+\sqrt{\alpha\log(t-1)/n}\) as a buyer or \(s=\widehat v-\sqrt{\alpha\log(t-1)/n}\) as a seller; bids and asks are not clipped.
Represent each type by a measure on private histories. Let \(b_t(u)\) and \(s_t(u)\), for \(0<u\le1\), be respectively the decreasing bid and increasing ask mass quantiles, using their left limits at endpoints. Set \(q_t=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{u:b_t(u)\ge s_t(u)\})\); select the highest-bidding buyer mass and lowest-asking seller mass of size \(q_t\), splitting boundary ties proportionally across all tied histories and types. For \(q_t>0\), set \(p_t=(b_t(q_t)+s_t(q_t))/2\); otherwise there is no trade and \(p_t=0\).
A selected history fraction trades fully, increments its sample count by one, and branches according to its type's fresh feedback law; the unselected fraction retains its history. This measure recursion defines the continuum process and its aggregate expectations.
Apply the same clearing rule to true valuations to define oracle volume \(q^*>0\), allocation, and price \(p^*\). Assume \(\Delta=\min_{\text{supported types}}|v-p^*|>0\), and let \(\alpha_{\max}=\max\alpha\). Define \(W_t\) as the integral of buyer valuations over traded buyer mass plus seller valuations over untraded seller mass; define \(W^*\) analogously for the oracle.

Does there exist a universal polynomial \(P\) such that every admissible instance satisfies \(TW^*-\mathbb E[\sum_{t=1}^T W_t]\le P(\tau_B,\tau_S,\alpha_{\max},1/\Delta)\log T+C\) for every \(T\ge2\), where \(C\) may depend on the instance but not on \(T\) or any finite population representing its masses, or is there a counterexample?

Result

No such universal polynomial exists. We construct a family with \(\tau_B=\tau_S=2\), \(\alpha_{\max}=4\), and \(\Delta=1\), whose logarithmic regret coefficient is unbounded. The construction includes nonzero noise and branching of private histories.

The obstruction is that distance from the oracle Average price does not control the valuation gap between competing sellers.

1. An admissible family

Fix a dyadic rational \(\varepsilon\) satisfying \(0<\varepsilon<1/2\). Every type has mass \(1/2\) and confidence parameter \(\alpha=4\).

| Society | Type | Valuation | Noise law |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Buyer | High | \(3+2\varepsilon\) | Identically zero |
| Buyer | Low | \(0\) | Identically zero |
| Seller | Good | \(1\) | Identically zero |
| Seller | Costly | \(1+\varepsilon\) | \(\varepsilon\) or \(-\varepsilon\), each with probability \(1/2\) |

Fresh draws are independent as required. All laws are finitely specified and mean-zero. For the nonzero law,

\(\mathbb E[e^{\lambda\nu}]=\cosh(\lambda\varepsilon)\le e^{\lambda^2\varepsilon^2/2}\le e^{\lambda^2/2}\).

The first inequality follows by comparing power-series coefficients, using \((2k)!\ge 2^k k!\). Thus every law is \(1\)-sub-Gaussian.

Under true valuations, precisely the high buyers and good sellers trade. Indeed, for \(0<u\le1/2\), the buyer valuation exceeds the seller valuation; for \(u>1/2\), they are respectively \(0\) and \(1+\varepsilon\). Consequently,

\(q^*=\frac12,\qquad p^*=\frac{(3+2\varepsilon)+1}{2}=2+\varepsilon.\)

The four distances from \(p^*\) are \(1+\varepsilon,\ 2+\varepsilon,\ 1+\varepsilon,\ 1\). Hence

\(\tau_B=\tau_S=2,\qquad \alpha_{\max}=4,\qquad \Delta=1.\)

These parameters are independent of \(\varepsilon\).

All quantities below are integrals over the stipulated private-history measures. Feedback randomness is incorporated through their branching.

2. Three properties of the history measures

Let \(a_t\) and \(d_t\) denote the traded masses of good and costly sellers in round \(t\). Include the forced initial round, so \(a_1=d_1=1/2\).

Let \(N_G(T)\) and \(N_D(T)\) be the mean sample counts after round \(T\), conditional on belonging to the respective seller type. Because each type has mass \(1/2\), conservation of history mass and the sample-count update give

\(N_G(T)=2\sum_{t=1}^T a_t,\qquad N_D(T)=2\sum_{t=1}^T d_t.\)

Both means are nondecreasing.

Trade volume is always at least one half

Every high buyer bids at least \(3+2\varepsilon\), since its empirical valuation is exact and its confidence bonus is nonnegative.

Every seller asks at most \(1+2\varepsilon\): good sellers have empirical valuation \(1\), while every observation of a costly seller lies in \(\{1,1+2\varepsilon\}\).

Thus, for every \(t\ge2\),

\(b_t(1/2)\ge3+2\varepsilon>1+2\varepsilon\ge s_t(1/2),\)

and therefore \(q_t\ge1/2\). The initial volume is \(1\). Since \(a_t+d_t=q_t\), it follows that

\[N_G(T)+N_D(T)=2\sum_{t=1}^Tq_t\ge T.\tag{1}\]

Good sellers’ sample counts differ by at most one

After every round, all positive-mass good-seller histories have sample counts in two consecutive integers.

Initially every count is \(1\). In round \(2\), any selection produces counts \(1\) and \(2\).

For a later round \(t\ge3\), a good seller with count \(n\) asks

\(1-\sqrt{\frac{4\log(t-1)}{n}},\)

which is strictly increasing in \(n\). Hence lower-count good sellers are selected before higher-count good sellers.

Suppose the current counts are \(k\) and \(k+1\). If any count-\(k\) mass remains unselected, no count-\(k+1\) mass can be selected, so the updated counts remain \(k,k+1\). Otherwise all count-\(k\) mass increments, and the updated counts are \(k+1,k+2\). This also covers proportional splitting at the selection boundary.

In particular, immediately before round \(t\), every good-seller history has count at least \(N_G(t-1)-1\). By (1), for \(t\le T\), this is at least

\[N_G(t-1)-1\ge t-2-N_D(T).\tag{2}\]

Costly sellers’ empirical valuations are uniformly bounded

Every costly-seller observation lies in \(\{1,1+2\varepsilon\}\). Therefore, for every private history,

\[\widehat v_D\le1+2\varepsilon.\tag{3}\]

No concentration estimate or pooling of observations is used here.

3. Costly sellers necessarily accumulate logarithmically many trades

We prove

\[\displaystyle \liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_D(T)}{\log T}\ge\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^2}.\tag{4}\]

Fix any \(c\) satisfying \(0<c<1/(2\varepsilon^2)\). Suppose, for some large integer \(T\), that

\[N_D(T)\le c\log T.\tag{5}\]

Consider every round \(t\) with \(\lceil T/2\rceil\le t\le T\).

By (2), each good seller’s previous sample count is at least \(T/2-2-c\log T\). For sufficiently large \(T\), this is positive, and every good seller asks at least \(1-\eta_T\), where

\(\displaystyle \eta_T=\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{T/2-2-c\log T}}\longrightarrow0.\)

Meanwhile, monotonicity and (5) imply \(N_D(t-1)\le c\log T\). Markov’s inequality, applied to sample counts under the conditional costly-seller history measure, shows that at least half that type has count

\(n\le2c\log T.\)

This is society mass at least \(1/4\). For each such history, (3) bounds its ask above by \(1+2\varepsilon-\kappa_T\), where

\(\displaystyle \kappa_T=\sqrt{\frac{4\log(T/2-1)}{2c\log T}}\longrightarrow\sqrt{\frac2c}>2\varepsilon.\)

Consequently, for sufficiently large \(T\),

\(1+2\varepsilon-\kappa_T<1-\eta_T.\)

Every one of these costly-seller histories therefore asks strictly less than every good seller.

Each such history must trade: all sellers ranked ahead of it belong to the costly type, whose entire mass is \(1/2\), while the selected seller mass is at least \(1/2\). Boundary ties cannot exclude any of this mass.

Thus \(d_t\ge1/4\) throughout these at least \(T/2\) rounds, giving

\(\displaystyle N_D(T)=2\sum_{t=1}^T d_t\ge\frac T4.\)

This contradicts (5) for sufficiently large \(T\). Hence \(N_D(T)>c\log T\) eventually. Since this holds for every \(c<1/(2\varepsilon^2)\), (4) follows.

4. A welfare-regret lower bound

Let \(h_t\) and \(\ell_t\) be the traded masses of high and low buyers. Feasibility gives

\(h_t+\ell_t=a_t+d_t,\qquad h_t\le1/2,\qquad \ell_t\ge0.\)

The total seller valuation before trade is \(S_0=1+\varepsilon/2\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle W_t=S_0+(3+2\varepsilon)h_t-a_t-(1+\varepsilon)d_t =S_0+(2+2\varepsilon)h_t-\ell_t-\varepsilon d_t.\)

The oracle welfare is \(W^*=S_0+(2+2\varepsilon)/2\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle W^*-W_t=(2+2\varepsilon)(1/2-h_t)+\ell_t+\varepsilon d_t\ge\varepsilon d_t.\)

Writing \(R(T)=TW^*-\mathbb E[\sum_{t=1}^T W_t]\), integration and summation yield

\(\displaystyle R(T)\ge\varepsilon\sum_{t=1}^T d_t=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}N_D(T).\)

Using (4),

\[\displaystyle \boxed{\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{R(T)}{\log T}\ge\frac{1}{4\varepsilon}.}\tag{6}\]

5. Contradiction to a universal polynomial

Suppose the proposed polynomial \(P\) existed, and set \(K=P(2,2,4,1)\).

For every instance in this family, its asserted bound would imply

\(\displaystyle \limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{R(T)}{\log T}\le K,\)

because its instance-dependent additive constant divided by \(\log T\) tends to zero.

Choose a dyadic \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)\) sufficiently small that \(1/(4\varepsilon)>K\). Equation (6) contradicts that upper bound.

Thus even an arbitrary finite coefficient depending only on the four stated parameters cannot suffice.

The explicit finite-population bound in Theorem 1 of [Basu–Sankararaman](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/0bcfb525c8f8f07ae10a93d0b2a40e00-Paper-Conference.pdf) contains inverse seller valuation gaps. Here that gap is \(\varepsilon\), although \(\Delta=1\). The counterexample establishes precisely why those gaps cannot be replaced uniformly by the stated distance from the Average price.
