The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a road network, routing costs, fleet and service constraints, and a rational distribution μ over complete customer types (including exact location, demand, schedule, channel, and contract), form the routing-cost cooperative game \(v_n\) with \(nμ_t\) customers of each type, scaling delivery capacity with the repeated operation. For a target type \(t^\star\), let \(\bar{\phi}^{(n)}_{t^\star}=(nμ_{t^\star})^{-1}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t^\star}\mathrm{Sh}_i(v_n)\), for replication factors \(n\) such that \(nμ_t\) is integral. Does \(\bar{\phi}^{(n)}_{t^\star}\) converge as \(n\) grows, and when it does, can its limit be approximated within a fixed factor in time polynomial in the network description, τ, input bit length, and fleet parameters?

Proven result

Every promised input has a finite replication limit. Approximating the limit is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every fixed factor \(\alpha>1\), unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The hardness holds with two vehicle classes and zero traversed-edge costs. It also holds when the target limit is promised to be strictly positive.

1. Reduction to finitely many route patterns

Use the usual routing interpretation of service duration as nonnegative.

Let \(a_j\) be the original availability of class \(j\). After replication, that class has \(na_j\ge n\) vehicles. A routing solution for a coalition of at most \(n\) customers uses at most \(n\) nonempty routes: every nonempty route serves at least one customer, and customers are served separately by one vehicle each. Empty routes can be discarded because costs are nonnegative.

Consequently, vehicle availability never binds in any replicated coalition.

Deleting customers from a feasible route preserves feasibility. Keep its road walk, replace deleted services by waiting, and discard any route that becomes empty. Demands and stop counts decrease, eligibility remains valid, and costs do not increase. This uses the absence of obligations to customers outside the coalition.

In particular, the grand-coalition feasibility promise implies that every supported type can be served alone.

Let \(\mathcal A\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}\setminus\{0\}\) consist of all count vectors that one feasible route can serve. The customer-stop bounds make \(\mathcal A\) finite. For \(a\in\mathcal A\), let \(c_a\) be the minimum cost of a route serving that pattern, minimized over eligible classes.

These minima are attained. Between consecutive service events, and between the depot and the first or last event, delete cycles from the road walk. Nonnegative edge times and costs make this weakly cheaper and earlier; waiting preserves the original service times. Each resulting leg is a simple path. There are finitely many resulting service sequences, class choices, and path choices, and each has a fixed rational cost.

Write \(e_t\) for the unit vector of type \(t\), and put \(c_t=c_{e_t}\).

Define the routing cost with unlimited vehicles by

\(\displaystyle V(x)=\min\left\{\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}c_a z_a:\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}a z_a=x,\quad z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\right\}.\)

For every replicated coalition with count vector \(x\), its cost is exactly \(V(x)\). Singleton routes make \(V\) finite on all integer nonnegative vectors. Deletion and addition of a singleton route give

\[\displaystyle 0\le V(x+e_t)-V(x)\le c_t. \tag{1}\]

A fractional relaxation with uniformly bounded error

For real \(x\ge0\), define

\(\displaystyle F(x)=\min\left\{\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}c_a z_a:\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}a z_a\ge x,\quad z\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\right\}.\)

The covering inequality is useful: surplus services can be deleted.

Set \(C=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}c_a\). For an integer vector \(x\), round every coordinate of an optimal fractional solution upward. This increases cost by at most \(C\) and covers at least \(x\). Select exactly \(x_t\) occurrences of each type and delete all other services. The resulting routes serve exactly \(x\), without increasing cost. Thus

\[\displaystyle 0\le V(x)-F(x)\le C \qquad(x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}). \tag{2}\]

Finite-dimensional linear programming duality gives

\[\displaystyle F(x)=\max_{\lambda\in P}\lambda\cdot x,\qquad P=\{\lambda\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\tau}:a\cdot\lambda\le c_a\text{ for every }a\in\mathcal A\}. \tag{3}\]

The primal is feasible by singleton routes and bounded below by zero. The dual contains zero. Its singleton constraints imply \(0\le\lambda_t\le c_t\), so \(P\) is compact. Therefore it has finitely many distinct vertices \(q^1,\ldots,q^J\), and

\[\displaystyle F(x)=\max_{1\le j\le J}q^j\cdot x. \tag{4}\]

In particular,

\[\displaystyle 0\le F(x+e_t)-F(x)\le c_t. \tag{5}\]

2. Convergence of the replicated Shapley values

Fix a supported type \(t\), and write \(N_s=n\mu_s\). Customers of the same type have equal Shapley values: exchanging their identities preserves every coalition cost and bijects the permutations in the Shapley formula. Hence their common value equals \(\bar\phi_t^{(n)}\).

Assign every customer an independent uniform priority in \([0,1]\). Their priority order is a uniform random permutation. Conditional on the priority of a tagged type-\(t\) customer being \(u\), its predecessor counts are independent, with

\(\displaystyle X_{n,s}(u)\sim\operatorname{Bin}(N_s-\mathbf1_{\{s=t\}},u).\)

Therefore the ordinary Shapley formula becomes

\[\displaystyle \bar\phi_t^{(n)}=\int_0^1\mathbb E\!\left[V(X_n(u)+e_t)-V(X_n(u))\right]\,du. \tag{6}\]

2.1. Binomial smoothing removes the integer error

Let \(R(x)=V(x)-F(x)\). By (2), \(0\le R(x)\le C\).

Fix \(u\in(0,1)\). Let \(M=N_t-1\), and let \(p_j\) be the probabilities of \(\operatorname{Bin}(M,u)\), extended by zero outside its support. Independence of the coordinates gives

\[\displaystyle \left|\mathbb E[R(X_n(u)+e_t)-R(X_n(u))]\right|\le C\sum_j|p_{j-1}-p_j|. \tag{7}\]

The binomial probabilities are unimodal: the consecutive ratio is

\(\displaystyle \frac{p_{j+1}}{p_j}=\frac{M-j}{j+1}\frac{u}{1-u},\)

which decreases with \(j\). Summing the increases and decreases of a unimodal sequence yields

\(\displaystyle \sum_j|p_{j-1}-p_j|=2\max_j p_j.\)

For fixed \(u\in(0,1)\), its mode is within a bounded distance of \(Mu\); Stirling’s formula gives

\(\displaystyle \max_j p_j=O\!\left((Mu(1-u))^{-1/2}\right).\)

Since \(\mu_t>0\), \(M\to\infty\). Consequently,

\[\displaystyle \mathbb E[\Delta_t V(X_n(u))]-\mathbb E[\Delta_t F(X_n(u))]\longrightarrow0, \tag{8}\]

where \(\Delta_t H(x)=H(x+e_t)-H(x)\).

The bounded error in total cost thus disappears after averaging marginal costs.

2.2. The fractional marginal has a limit even at a breakpoint

Let

\(\displaystyle I=\{j:q^j\cdot\mu=F(\mu)\}\)

be the vertices optimal in direction \(\mu\). Let

\(\displaystyle Z\sim\mathcal N(0,\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau)).\)

Define \(\widehat q(Z)\) to be the vertex maximizing \(q^j\cdot Z\) over \(j\in I\). This maximizer is unique almost surely. Indeed, for distinct vertices \(q^j,q^\ell\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{Var}((q^j-q^\ell)\cdot Z)=\sum_s\mu_s(q_s^j-q_s^\ell)^2>0,\)

because all supported masses are positive. Each pairwise tie therefore has probability zero.

For fixed \(u\in(0,1)\), put

\(\displaystyle W_n=\frac{X_n(u)-nu\mu}{\sqrt{nu(1-u)}}.\)

The independent binomial central limit theorem gives \(W_n\Rightarrow Z\). Its hypotheses hold because every coordinate contains a number of bounded independent Bernoulli variables tending to infinity. The omitted tagged customer changes the centered mean by the bounded vector \(-ue_t\), which vanishes after this normalization.

The score of vertex \(q^j\) at \(X_n(u)\) is

\[\displaystyle nu\,q^j\cdot\mu+\sqrt{nu(1-u)}\,q^j\cdot W_n. \tag{9}\]

Vertices outside \(I\) have a strictly smaller first term, by an amount proportional to \(n\). On bounded sets of \(W_n\), they therefore cannot maximize for sufficiently large \(n\).

Among vertices in \(I\), the common first term cancels. Away from their finitely many tie hyperplanes, the winning normalized score has a positive gap. Multiplying that gap by \(\sqrt{nu(1-u)}\) eventually makes it larger than every possible score change \(q_t^j\) caused by adding \(e_t\). Thus the same vertex maximizes at \(X_n(u)\) and \(X_n(u)+e_t\), and the marginal equals its type-\(t\) coordinate.

For completeness, this argument can be made uniform on any bounded set whose distance from the tie hyperplanes is positive. The central limit theorem transfers probabilities of these regions because their boundaries have Gaussian measure zero. Letting the bound grow and the excluded distance shrink makes the omitted probability tend to zero. Hence

\(\displaystyle \Delta_t F(X_n(u))\Rightarrow\widehat q_t(Z).\)

By (5), these random variables are uniformly bounded. Therefore

\[\displaystyle \mathbb E[\Delta_t F(X_n(u))]\longrightarrow\mathbb E[\widehat q_t(Z)]. \tag{10}\]

The right-hand side is independent of \(u\).

Combining (8) and (10), and then applying dominated convergence to (6), using (1), proves

\[\displaystyle \boxed{\ell_t=\lim_{\substack{n\to\infty\\ n\mu\ \mathrm{integral}}}\bar\phi_t^{(n)}=\mathbb E[\widehat q_t(Z)].} \tag{11}\]

This establishes convergence for every promised input, including directions where \(F\) is nondifferentiable. Moreover, \(0\le\ell_t\le c_t\), so every limit is finite.

3. A direct hardness construction

We reduce from directed Hamiltonian path, an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem. See [Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer, *Some simplified NP-complete problems*](https://doi.org/10.1145/800119.803884).

Let \(G\) have vertices \(v_1,\ldots,v_h\), with \(h\ge2\). Put

\(\displaystyle b=h+1,\qquad w_i=b^{i-1},\qquad B=\sum_{i=1}^h w_i,\qquad W=B+1,\qquad H=h+2.\)

The road network is the complete directed graph on these vertices together with a depot \(d\) and an additional vertex \(r\).

There are \(h\) graph-customer types and one target type, with the following requirements:

| Type | Location | Weight | Volume | Duration | Window | Eligible classes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Graph type \(i\) | \(v_i\) | \(w_i\) | \(W-w_i\) | \(0\) | \([0,H]\) | Both |
| Target | \(r\) | \(1/2\) | \(1/2\) | \(1\) | \([h+1,H]\) | Second only |

All fees are zero. The masses are

\(\displaystyle \mu_\star=\frac1{2h+1},\qquad \mu_i=\frac2{2h+1}\quad(1\le i\le h).\)

There are two vehicle classes:

| Class | Availability | Weight capacity | Volume capacity | Stop bound | Activation cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | \(1\) | \(W\) | \(W\) | \(1\) | \(1\) |
| Second | \(1\) | \(B+1/2\) | \(hW-B+1/2\) | \(h+1\) | \(h\) |

All edge costs are zero. First-class travel times are zero. Second-class travel times are:

  • \(0\) on \(d\to r\) and \(r\to d\);
  • \(1\) between a graph vertex and either \(d\) or \(r\), in either direction;
  • \(1\) on \(v_i\to v_j\) when that arc belongs to \(G\);
  • \(h+3\) on other arcs between distinct graph vertices.

Routes execute within \([0,H]\).

Every demand and capacity is positive. The numbers \(w_i\) have \(O(h\log h)\) bits, so the entire construction has polynomial binary length. It has \(\tau=h+1\), \(k=2\), and \(m=O(h^2)\).

The feasibility promise holds: serve each graph customer on a first-class singleton route and each target on a second-class singleton route. A target route reaches \(r\) at time zero, waits until \(h+1\), performs its service, and returns at time \(H\). After replication, each class has \(n\) vehicles, which suffices.

3.1. Capacity and time-window properties

A second-class route serves at most one target. Any target service starts no earlier than \(h+1\), lasts one time unit, and must finish by \(H=h+2\). Two separate target services cannot fit. This reasoning also applies if a window must contain the whole service interval.

Every second-class route serves at most \(h\) graph customers. Each graph customer has combined weight and volume \(W\), while the combined capacities equal \(hW+1\). Since \(W>1\), even \(h+1\) graph customers exceed the combined capacities.

Suppose a second-class route serves one target and exactly \(h\) graph customers. If it serves \(c_i\) customers of graph type \(i\), then the two capacity constraints imply

\(\displaystyle \sum_i c_i w_i\le B,\qquad hW-\sum_i c_i w_i\le hW-B.\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle \sum_i c_i w_i=B=\sum_i b^{i-1}.\)

Here \(\sum_i c_i=h\), so \(0\le c_i\le h<b\). Uniqueness of base-\(b\) representation therefore forces \(c_i=1\) for every \(i\).

Consequently, a route containing one target and \(h\) graph customers must serve exactly one customer of every graph type.

Such a route exists if and only if \(G\) has a directed Hamiltonian path:

  • Given a Hamiltonian path, travel from \(d\) through its \(h\) graph vertices and then to \(r\). Travel takes \(h+1\) time units. Serve the target for one unit and return to \(d\) on the zero-time edge.
  • Conversely, target service must begin at \(h+1\) and finish at \(H\). All graph services must precede it, since traveling from \(r\) to any graph vertex takes positive time. Reaching the first graph vertex, transitioning between the \(h\) distinct graph vertices, and reaching \(r\) requires at least \(1+(h-1)+1=h+1\) time units. The budget forces every transition between consecutive graph vertices to take exactly one unit. A road walk between distinct graph vertices takes one unit only when it is a direct arc of \(G\): any intermediate graph vertex or passage through \(d\) or \(r\) costs at least two units. The service order is therefore a Hamiltonian path.

4. The limit separates Hamiltonian-path instances

4.1. A Hamiltonian path gives target limit zero

Every route costs at least the number of graph customers it serves:

  • A first-class route serves one graph customer and costs one.
  • A second-class route serves at most \(h\) graph customers and costs \(h\).

Hence the fractional relaxation satisfies

\[\displaystyle F(x)\ge\sum_{i=1}^h x_i. \tag{12}\]

If \(G\) has a Hamiltonian path, the full pattern containing one target and one customer of each graph type is feasible and costs \(h\). Whenever \(x_i\ge x_\star\) for every graph type, use \(x_\star\) copies of this pattern fractionally and \(x_i-x_\star\) first-class singleton routes of type \(i\). Their total cost is

\(\displaystyle hx_\star+\sum_{i=1}^h(x_i-x_\star)=\sum_{i=1}^h x_i.\)

Together with (12), this proves equality.

Since \(\mu_i>\mu_\star\) for every \(i\), there is an \(\varepsilon>0\) such that

\(\displaystyle F(\mu+\varepsilon e_\star)=F(\mu).\)

For any dual price \(\lambda\in P\) optimal at \(\mu\), (3) gives

\(\displaystyle F(\mu)=F(\mu+\varepsilon e_\star)\ge\lambda\cdot(\mu+\varepsilon e_\star)=F(\mu)+\varepsilon\lambda_\star.\)

Nonnegativity of dual prices forces \(\lambda_\star=0\). Every vertex selected in (11) therefore has zero target coordinate, and

\[\displaystyle \ell_\star=0. \tag{13}\]

4.2. No Hamiltonian path gives every target marginal at least one

Suppose \(G\) has no Hamiltonian path. Every second-class route serving a target then serves at most \(h-1\) graph customers.

Take any coalition \(S\) and an additional target customer \(p\). In an optimal solution for \(S\cup\{p\}\), the route serving \(p\) contains no other target. Let it contain \(g\le h-1\) graph customers.

Delete this entire route and serve those \(g\) graph customers on first-class singleton routes. This serves exactly \(S\), and changes cost by \(-h+g\). Availability does not bind, as established in Section 1. Therefore

\(\displaystyle v_n(S)\le v_n(S\cup\{p\})-h+g\le v_n(S\cup\{p\})-1.\)

Every target marginal is at least one, so every finite target Shapley value is at least one. The convergence theorem yields

\[\displaystyle \ell_\star\ge1. \tag{14}\]

5. Approximation hardness

Fix any \(\alpha>1\). Apply a purported polynomial-time multiplicative approximation algorithm to the constructed input.

If \(G\) has a Hamiltonian path, (13) and the required approximation guarantee force its output to be exactly zero. Otherwise, (14) forces its output to be at least \(1/\alpha\). Thus one invocation decides directed Hamiltonian path.

The construction has polynomial \(m,\tau,k,L\), so approximation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for every fixed \(\alpha>1\).

This hardness also survives a restriction to positive limits. Choose a fixed integer \(\beta>\alpha^2\), and add target fee \(\varepsilon=1/\beta\). Every coalition cost increases by exactly \(\varepsilon\) times its number of targets, so every target marginal and Shapley value increases by \(\varepsilon\). The two cases become

\(\displaystyle \ell_\star=\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_\star\ge1+\varepsilon.\)

Their allowed approximation intervals are disjoint, because

\(\displaystyle \alpha\varepsilon<\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\alpha},\)

equivalently \(\alpha^2<\beta+1\). Thus the hardness is not dependent on allowing a zero target limit.
