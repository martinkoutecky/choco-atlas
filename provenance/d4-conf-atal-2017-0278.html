The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given complete student types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), one college with per-capita budget \(b\), type-specific wages \(w_\theta\), additive utilities \(u_\theta\), acceptability, and a current mass matching \(y_\theta\in[0,\mu_\theta]\), decide whether there exist incoming home masses \(z_\theta\in[0,\mu_\theta-y_\theta]\) and rejected matched masses \(r_\theta\in[0,y_\theta]\) such that incoming agents strictly prefer their contract to home, \(\sum_\theta w_\theta(y_\theta-r_\theta+z_\theta)\le b\), and \(\sum_\theta u_\theta(z_\theta-r_\theta)>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\Theta\) of \(\tau\geq 1\) complete student types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There is one college, so \(m=1\), with positive rational per-capita budget \(b\). Each type specifies whether its sole college contract exists, its nonnegative rational wage \(w_\theta\), its rational college utility \(u_\theta\), and a strict student preference between that contract and home with zero wage.
College utility is additive in assigned mass and does not subtract wages; home contributes zero utility. The college weakly accepts a type exactly when \(u_\theta\geq 0\). Let \(E\) contain the types whose contract exists, whose students strictly prefer it to home, and which the college weakly accepts.
The input includes a feasible rational current matching: \(0\leq y_\theta\leq\mu_\theta\), \(y_\theta=0\) outside \(E\), and \(\sum_\theta w_\theta y_\theta\leq b\). All remaining mass is at home.
A blocking deviation consists of real incoming masses \(0\leq z_\theta\leq\mu_\theta-y_\theta\) and rejected masses \(0\leq r_\theta\leq y_\theta\), with \(z_\theta=0\) outside \(E\) and \(\sum_\theta z_\theta>0\). Rejected students need not consent.
It must satisfy \(\sum_\theta w_\theta(y_\theta-r_\theta+z_\theta)\leq b\) and \(\sum_\theta u_\theta(z_\theta-r_\theta)>0\).

Is deciding whether a blocking deviation exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, where \(L\) is the total binary input length?

For finite students, checking coalitional stability is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-complete, even with one college, one contract per student, additive college utility, and ordered wage types; equivalently, detecting a blocking coalition is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. [Source](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/11582/26504)
No result settling this precise mass decision problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified nonnegative wages, a positive budget, signed college utilities, zero utility for home, and weak college acceptability exactly at nonnegative utility.
  • Used binary rational wages, utilities, budget, and current matching; this extends the integer numerical domains to the rational mass setting.
  • Required the current matching to be individually acceptable and budget-feasible, as in the discrete verification problem.
  • Interpreted complete types as determining contract availability, wage, college utility, and strict student preference; each student has at most one college contract.
  • Made deviations divisible real masses, required positive incoming mass, and retained the college's ability to reject any matched mass without its consent.
  • Fixed the number of colleges at one and interpreted the normalized budget as expenditure per unit of total population mass.

Result

Deciding whether a blocking deviation exists is in \(\mathrm{P}\) for the stated model with \(m=1\). An exact algorithm optimizes a fractional knapsack problem and compares its optimum with the current college utility.

The proof covers real deviations, zero wages, zero utilities, and the strict requirement of positive incoming mass.

1. Equivalence with improving the final assignment

Restrict attention to \(E\). By definition, \(u_\theta\geq 0\) for every \(\theta\in E\), and the current assignment is zero outside \(E\).

Define the set of feasible final assignments by

\(\displaystyle \mathcal F=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^E:0\leq x_\theta\leq\mu_\theta\text{ for all }\theta\in E,\quad \sum_{\theta\in E}w_\theta x_\theta\leq b\right\},\)

and define college utility by

\(\displaystyle U(x)=\sum_{\theta\in E}u_\theta x_\theta.\)

Lemma. A blocking deviation exists if and only if some \(x\in\mathcal F\) satisfies \(U(x)>U(y)\).

Proof. Suppose first that \((z,r)\) is a blocking deviation. Set \(x_\theta=y_\theta-r_\theta+z_\theta\). Its bounds imply

\(\displaystyle x_\theta\geq y_\theta-r_\theta\geq 0,\qquad x_\theta\leq y_\theta+z_\theta\leq\mu_\theta.\)

The deviation’s budget condition gives \(x\in\mathcal F\), and its utility condition gives

\(\displaystyle U(x)-U(y)=\sum_{\theta\in E}u_\theta(z_\theta-r_\theta)>0.\)

Conversely, suppose \(x\in\mathcal F\) and \(U(x)>U(y)\). Define

\(\displaystyle z_\theta=\max\{x_\theta-y_\theta,0\},\qquad r_\theta=\max\{y_\theta-x_\theta,0\},\)

and set both quantities to zero outside \(E\). Since \(0\leq x_\theta\leq\mu_\theta\),

\(\displaystyle 0\leq z_\theta\leq\mu_\theta-y_\theta,\qquad 0\leq r_\theta\leq y_\theta.\)

Also \(y_\theta-r_\theta+z_\theta=x_\theta\), so the budget condition holds and the utility gain is strictly positive.

It remains to check positive incoming mass. If \(\sum_{\theta\in E}z_\theta=0\), every \(z_\theta\) is zero, so \(x_\theta\leq y_\theta\) for every eligible type. Because all their utilities are nonnegative, this implies \(U(x)\leq U(y)\), a contradiction. Thus the constructed deviation satisfies every required condition. \(\square\)

Consequently, it suffices to compute an optimal feasible assignment \(g\) and answer yes exactly when \(U(g)>U(y)\).

2. Constructing an optimal assignment

For every eligible type with \(w_\theta=0\), set \(g_\theta=\mu_\theta\). This costs nothing and cannot reduce utility.

Index the remaining eligible types by \(1,\ldots,n\), so each \(w_i>0\). Sort them in nonincreasing order of utility per unit wage:

\(\displaystyle \frac{u_1}{w_1}\geq\frac{u_2}{w_2}\geq\cdots\geq\frac{u_n}{w_n}.\)

Ties may be resolved arbitrarily. Define \(S_0=0\) and

\(\displaystyle S_j=\sum_{i=1}^j w_i\mu_i\qquad (1\leq j\leq n).\)

If \(S_n\leq b\), set \(g_i=\mu_i\) for every positive-wage type. This includes the case \(n=0\).

Otherwise, let \(k\) be the smallest index with \(S_k>b\). Set

  • \(g_i=\mu_i\) for \(i<k\);
  • \(g_k=(b-S_{k-1})/w_k\);
  • \(g_i=0\) for \(i>k\).

Minimality of \(k\) gives \(S_{k-1}\leq b<S_k\), hence \(0\leq g_k<\mu_k\). The constructed assignment spends exactly \(b\). In particular, it is feasible. If \(S_{k-1}=b\), then \(g_k=0\).

3. Proof of optimality

If \(S_n\leq b\), the algorithm assigns the full eligible mass of every type. For any \(x\in\mathcal F\), each \(x_\theta\leq g_\theta\); nonnegative utilities therefore give \(U(x)\leq U(g)\).

Now suppose \(S_n>b\), and define

\(\displaystyle \lambda=\frac{u_k}{w_k}\geq 0.\)

For any \(x\in\mathcal F\), the following identity holds:

\(\displaystyle U(x)-U(g)=\lambda\sum_{\theta\in E}w_\theta(x_\theta-g_\theta)+\sum_{\theta\in E}(u_\theta-\lambda w_\theta)(x_\theta-g_\theta).\)

The first term is nonpositive: \(x\) spends at most \(b\), \(g\) spends exactly \(b\), and \(\lambda\geq 0\).

Every summand in the second term is also nonpositive:

  • For a zero-wage type, \(u_\theta-\lambda w_\theta=u_\theta\geq 0\), whereas \(x_\theta-g_\theta=x_\theta-\mu_\theta\leq 0\).
  • For \(i<k\), the sorted order gives \(u_i-\lambda w_i\geq 0\), whereas \(x_i-g_i=x_i-\mu_i\leq 0\).
  • For \(i=k\), the coefficient \(u_k-\lambda w_k\) equals zero.
  • For \(i>k\), the sorted order gives \(u_i-\lambda w_i\leq 0\), whereas \(x_i-g_i=x_i\geq 0\).

Thus \(U(x)\leq U(g)\) for every feasible \(x\). This proves optimality, including when some utilities or the threshold \(\lambda\) equal zero.

Together with the lemma, this proves the exact decision criterion

\(\displaystyle \text{a blocking deviation exists}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U(g)>U(y).\)

When the answer is yes, the lemma’s formulas construct a rational blocking deviation.

4. Polynomial binary running time

Determining \(E\) requires inspecting the explicitly listed \(\tau\) types. No enumeration of other possible types is needed.

The sorting step uses \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) exact comparisons. Since positive-wage denominators are positive, a ratio comparison can be performed by comparing \(u_iw_j\) and \(u_jw_i\), using rational arithmetic.

The remaining computation consists of prefix sums of the products \(w_i\mu_i\), at most one division to construct a fractional coordinate, and the sums defining \(U(g)\) and \(U(y)\).

These operations have polynomial bit complexity. To see why denominator sizes do not cause an exponential encoding:

  • Each product of input rationals has numerator and denominator bit lengths bounded by a constant multiple of \(L\).
  • A sum of at most \(\tau\) such products can use the product of their denominators as a common denominator. Its bit length is bounded by the sum of their denominator bit lengths, hence is polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). The numerator also has polynomial bit length.
  • The single fractional coordinate \((b-S_{k-1})/w_k\), and the subsequent utility computations, preserve polynomial bit length.

Standard exact integer arithmetic therefore implements all operations in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). The strict comparison \(U(g)>U(y)\) is exact; it requires neither a numerical tolerance nor a prescribed minimum gain. Since \(m=1\), this is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) on the stated input class.

5. Why finite-student hardness does not transfer

The cited finite-student work establishes hardness of checking coalitional stability. [Ismaili et al., *Weighted Matching Markets with Budget Constraints*](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/weighted-matching-markets-with-budget-constraints/). Here, however, the specified deviations allow arbitrary fractions of each type’s mass. The proof above uses that divisibility directly.

For example, take two eligible types with \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\), wages \(w_1=1,w_2=2\), utilities \(u_1=1,u_2=3\), budget \(b=1/2\), and current assignment \(y_1=1/2,y_2=0\). Both types’ students prefer their contract to home.

Rejecting \(r_1=1/2\) and admitting \(z_2=1/4\) spends \(2(1/4)=1/2\) and yields utility gain

\(\displaystyle 3(1/4)-1(1/2)=1/4>0.\)

If each type instead represented one indivisible student of mass \(1/2\), admitting the second student would cost \(2(1/2)=1>b\). No blocking deviation would then exist. Thus allowing fractional mass can change the answer, and it permits the polynomial optimization proved above.
