The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a baseline attack relation \(R\subseteq T\times T\), a target \(C\subseteq T\), and \(\sigma\in\{st,pr,co,ad\}\), choose a relation \(R'\) and a \(\sigma\)-extension \(E\) of \((T,R')\) with \(C\subseteq E\) minimizing \(\sum_{(t,u)\in R\triangle R'}\mu_t\mu_u\). Each type-pair edit applies uniformly to all ordered argument-instance pairs in that block.

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) argument types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; there is no separate candidate universe, so \(m=\tau\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The input also specifies an attack relation \(R\subseteq T\times T\), a target \(C\subseteq T\), a binary-rational budget \(B\geq0\), and \(\sigma\in\{st,pr,co,ad\}\), denoting standard Dung stable, preferred, complete, and admissible semantics.
An edit chooses any \(R'\subseteq T\times T\), allowing attack additions and deletions, including self-attacks, while keeping \(T\) and its masses fixed. Each ordered type-pair edit applies uniformly to the entire corresponding block of argument-instance pairs.
Extensions are subsets \(E\subseteq T\) evaluated under the named semantics on \((T,R')\); membership includes an entire type, and masses affect only edit costs. Define \(w(R,R')=\sum_{(t,u)\in R\triangle R'}\mu_t\mu_u\). Decide whether some such \(R'\) has a \(\sigma\)-extension \(E\) with \(C\subseteq E\) and \(w(R,R')\leq B\).

For each fixed \(\sigma\in\{st,pr,co,ad\}\), is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

For each fixed \(\sigma\in\{st,pr,co,ad\}\), the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even with equal dyadic masses, a target of size three, and a positive budget permitting exactly one ordered-pair edit.

An exact deterministic algorithm runs in
\[ O\!\left(2^{\tau-|C|}\tau^2L^2\right) = O\!\left(2^{m-|C|}m^2L^2\right) \]
bit operations using elementary integer arithmetic. Here \(m=\tau\), as stipulated; there is no additional candidate-universe parameter.

I prove the complexity classification and the algorithm below.

Definitions and semantic equivalence

For an attack relation \(S\subseteq T\times T\), a set \(E\subseteq T\) is conflict-free if \(S\cap(E\times E)=\varnothing\). It defends \(x\) if every attacker of \(x\) is attacked by some member of \(E\).

The four semantics are:

  • Admissible: \(E\) is conflict-free and defends every member of \(E\).
  • Complete: \(E\) is admissible and contains every argument it defends.
  • Preferred: \(E\) is inclusion-maximal among admissible sets.
  • Stable: \(E\) is conflict-free and attacks every member of \(T\setminus E\).

A stable set is admissible: every attacker of a member of \(E\) lies outside \(E\), and stability supplies a counterattack.

Lemma. Every admissible set in a finite framework is contained in a preferred extension, and every preferred extension is complete.

Proof. Finiteness gives an inclusion-maximal admissible superset \(P\) of any admissible set.

To prove that \(P\) is complete, suppose that \(P\) defends \(x\notin P\). First, no member \(p\in P\) can attack \(x\): defense of \(x\) would require a member of \(P\) attacking \(p\), contradicting conflict-freeness.

Also, \(x\) cannot attack a member \(p\in P\). Defense of \(p\) would then require a member of \(P\) attacking \(x\), which was just excluded. Finally, \(x\) cannot attack itself, because defense of \(x\) would again require an attack from \(P\) to \(x\).

Thus \(P\cup\{x\}\) is conflict-free. It defends its members because \(P\) already defends both its own members and \(x\). This contradicts maximality of \(P\). Consequently, \(P\) contains every argument it defends and is complete. \(\square\)

It follows, for every fixed edited relation \(R'\), that
\[ \exists E\in ad(T,R'):\ C\subseteq E \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists E\in co(T,R'):\ C\subseteq E \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists E\in pr(T,R'):\ C\subseteq E. \]
Therefore, the feasible edited relations—and hence their minimum costs—coincide for admissible, complete, and preferred non-strict enforcement. This conclusion holds for the given weighted costs.

The degenerate case \(C=\varnothing\) is always a yes-instance for these three semantics, even at budget zero. The hardness construction below uses a nonempty target and a positive budget.

Exact local costs and running time

Fix a nonempty candidate set \(E\subseteq T\). Define
\[ D(E)=\sum_{(t,u)\in R\cap(E\times E)}\mu_t\mu_u, \qquad a(E)=\min_{t\in E}\mu_t. \]
For each \(v\notin E\), define
\[ I_E(v)=\sum_{\substack{u\in E\\(v,u)\in R}}\mu_u, \qquad H_E(v)= \begin{cases} 1,&\text{if some }t\in E\text{ satisfies }(t,v)\in R,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

The minimum costs of making this particular \(E\) admissible or stable are respectively
\[ \Phi_{ad}(E) = D(E)+ \sum_{\substack{v\notin E\\H_E(v)=0}} \mu_v\min\{I_E(v),a(E)\}, \]
and
\[ \Phi_{st}(E) = D(E)+ \sum_{\substack{v\notin E\\H_E(v)=0}} \mu_v a(E). \]

Proof. Every original attack inside \(E\times E\), including every self-attack on a member of \(E\), must be deleted. This costs exactly \(D(E)\).

Consider \(v\notin E\). If \(H_E(v)=1\), retaining an existing attack from \(E\) to \(v\) provides the required counterattack, at no additional cost.

Suppose \(H_E(v)=0\). To make \(E\) admissible, there are precisely two possibilities:

  • Leave \(v\) unattacked by \(E\). Then every original attack from \(v\) to \(E\) must be deleted, costing \(\mu_v I_E(v)\).
  • Make \(v\) attacked by \(E\). At least one attack from \(E\) to \(v\) must be added. The cheapest such addition costs \(\mu_v a(E)\), and that one attack defends every member of \(E\) against \(v\).

For stability, the second possibility is mandatory.

The attack pairs involved for different outside vertices are disjoint, and they are disjoint from \(E\times E\). Attacks with both endpoints outside \(E\) affect neither condition and can be retained. Thus the local lower bounds add, and the described edits attain them. \(\square\)

Set \(\Phi_{ad}(\varnothing)=0\) and \(\Phi_{st}(\varnothing)=+\infty\), since \(T\) is nonempty. The global optimum is therefore
\[ \operatorname{OPT}_{\sigma} = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\min_{C\subseteq E\subseteq T}\Phi_{ad}(E), &\sigma\in\{ad,co,pr\},\\[6pt] \displaystyle\min_{C\subseteq E\subseteq T}\Phi_{st}(E), &\sigma=st. \end{cases} \]

There are \(2^{\tau-|C|}\) candidate sets. Each formula takes \(O(\tau^2)\) arithmetic operations.

For exact bit complexity, write \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), and let
\[ Q=\prod_{t\in T}q_t, \qquad A_t=Q\mu_t. \]
The integers \(Q\) and \(A_t\) have \(O(L)\) bits. Multiplying costs by \(Q^2\) makes the formulas integral: for example,
\[ Q^2\Phi_{ad}(E) = \sum_{(t,u)\in R\cap(E\times E)}A_tA_u + \sum_{\substack{v\notin E\\H_E(v)=0}} A_v\min\!\left\{ \sum_{\substack{u\in E\\(v,u)\in R}}A_u,\ \min_{t\in E}A_t \right\}. \]
Budget comparison uses integer cross-multiplication. All these integers have \(O(L)\) bits, so elementary arithmetic gives the claimed
\[ O\!\left(2^{\tau-|C|}\tau^2L^2\right) \]
bit-operation bound, including preprocessing.

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For stable semantics, guess \(R'\) and \(E\). Check \(C\subseteq E\), conflict-freeness, and whether \(E\) attacks every outside type.

For admissible semantics, instead check whether every attacker of a member of \(E\) is attacked by \(E\). Both semantic checks take \(O(\tau^2)\) Boolean operations.

Compute the edit cost exactly using the integer scaling above. Its verification takes \(O(\tau^2L^2)\) bit operations. The certificate contains \(O(\tau^2)\) bits for \(R'\) and \(O(\tau)\) bits for \(E\).

For complete and preferred semantics, an admissible witness containing \(C\) suffices by the lemma. Thus all four decision problems belong to \(\mathrm{NP}\); checking preferred maximality is unnecessary.

Hardness with a positive budget permitting one edit

A framework encoding satisfiability

Let \(\varphi\) have variables \(x_1,\ldots,x_d\) and clauses \(K_1,\ldots,K_r\). Construct a framework \(F_\varphi\) with:

  • a distinguished argument \(q\);
  • two literal arguments \(x_i,\overline{x_i}\) for each variable;
  • a clause argument \(c_j\) for each clause.

Its attacks are exactly:

  • \(x_i\to\overline{x_i}\) and \(\overline{x_i}\to x_i\);
  • \(c_j\to c_j\) and \(c_j\to q\);
  • \(\ell\to c_j\) whenever literal \(\ell\) occurs in \(K_j\).

Claim. If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, \(F_\varphi\) has a stable extension containing \(q\). Conversely, if \(F_\varphi\) has an admissible set containing \(q\), then \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

Proof. Given a satisfying assignment, take \(q\) together with the one true literal argument for each variable. This set is conflict-free. It attacks every omitted literal through the mutual literal attacks, and every clause argument through a true literal in that clause. Hence it is stable.

Conversely, let \(E\) be admissible and contain \(q\). Clause arguments cannot belong to \(E\), because they self-attack. Since each \(c_j\) attacks \(q\), defense of \(q\) requires some member of \(E\) attacking \(c_j\). Such a member must be a literal occurring in \(K_j\).

Conflict-freeness prevents \(E\) from containing both literals of any variable. Assign each selected literal to be true, and assign unrepresented variables arbitrarily. Every clause has a selected true literal, so this assignment satisfies \(\varphi\). \(\square\)

Three copies and normalized dyadic masses

Let \(n\) be the number of arguments in \(F_\varphi\). Take three disjoint copies, with distinguished arguments \(q_1,q_2,q_3\). Add isolated arguments until the total number \(N\) is a power of two. Choosing the least such power gives
\[ 3n\leq N<6n. \]

Use these \(N\) arguments as the support and assign
\[ \mu_t=\frac1N \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad C=\{q_1,q_2,q_3\}, \qquad B=\frac1{N^2}. \]
These masses are positive dyadic rationals summing to one. Every ordered-pair edit costs exactly \(1/N^2\), so the budget allows at most one edit, including an addition, deletion, or self-attack edit. The construction has polynomial encoding length.

If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, choose a stable extension containing \(q_i\) in each copy and include every isolated padding argument. Their union is stable in the original relation. It is also preferred and complete: stability prevents any conflict-free proper superset, and the lemma makes every preferred extension complete. Thus all four enforcement instances are yes-instances with zero edits.

Conversely, suppose an edited relation within budget has a \(\sigma\)-extension \(E\) containing \(C\). At most one ordered pair was edited. Its two endpoints belong to at most two of the three copies. Therefore, some copy has no edited pair incident to any of its vertices.

That copy retains its original internal attacks and has no attacks to or from other components. Every extension under any of the four semantics is admissible. Restrict \(E\) to the untouched copy:

  • It remains conflict-free.
  • If a vertex in that restriction is attacked, admissibility of \(E\) supplies a defender in \(E\).
  • Every attack entering the untouched copy originates inside it, so that defender also belongs to the restriction.

The restriction is consequently an admissible set containing that copy’s distinguished argument. The preceding claim implies that \(\varphi\) is satisfiable.

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for each fixed semantics under the stated positive-budget restriction.

The classification agrees with Proposition 2 of [Wallner, Niskanen, and Järvisalo](https://www.cs.helsinki.fi/u/mjarvisa/papers/wallner-niskanen-jarvisalo.aaai16.pdf), which establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for unit-cost non-strict enforcement. The reduction above directly verifies hardness under normalized dyadic mass-product costs.

3. Local admissible and stable costs

The formulas correctly account for all necessary modifications for a fixed nonempty \(E\).

  • If \(v\) remains unattacked by \(E\), all its attacks into \(E\) must be deleted.

Their minimum costs are respectively \(\mu_v I_E(v)\) and \(\mu_v a(E)\). A single added counterattack handles every attack from \(v\) into \(E\). For stability, an attack from \(E\) to \(v\) is mandatory.

The decomposition is valid because the relevant ordered pairs for distinct outside vertices are disjoint and do not overlap \(E\times E\). Attacks entirely outside \(E\) do not affect whether this particular \(E\) is admissible or stable.
