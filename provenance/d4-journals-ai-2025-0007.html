The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given equal-sized candidate sets \(C,D\) and finitely supported rational societies μ and ν over complete rankings of \(C\) and \(D\), compute an optimal candidate bijection δ and position-transport plans minimizing \(∑_{c∈C}\operatorname{EMD}(p^μ_c,p^ν_{δ(c)})\), where \(p^μ_{c,i}=∑_{π:\operatorname{pos}_π(c)=i}μ_π\); equivalently, decide whether the optimum is at most a given rational threshold.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C,D\) have equal size \(m\). The input consists of explicitly listed complete rankings with nonnegative, binary-encoded rational masses \(\mu_\pi,\nu_\rho\), each society having total mass \(1\). For \(c\in C\), set \(p^\mu_{c,i}=\sum_{\pi:\operatorname{pos}_\pi(c)=i}\mu_\pi\); define \(p^\nu_{d,i}\) analogously. Here position \(1\) is top-ranked, and \(\operatorname{EMD}(x,y)\) moves mass between positions \(i,j\) at cost \(|i-j|\) per unit.
Define \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\delta:C\to D\text{ bijective}}\sum_{c\in C}\operatorname{EMD}(p^\mu_c,p^\nu_{\delta(c)})\). An output includes an optimal bijection and optimal position-transport plans.

Is deciding whether \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,\nu)\le t\), for a rational threshold \(t\), in P or NP-hard?

For finite voter lists, positionwise distance is computable in polynomial time ([Szufa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03809)). Frequency matrices and the same positionwise distance have also been defined for vote distributions ([Boehmer et al.](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/cb1a0e1bd8b2f20f7500c7886e1fbc60-Paper-Conference.pdf)).

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). An optimal bijection and optimal position-transport plans can also be computed in time polynomial in the binary input length.

The objective agrees with the raw positionwise distance between frequency matrices defined by [Boehmer et al.](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/cb1a0e1bd8b2f20f7500c7886e1fbc60-Paper-Conference.pdf). The proof below explicitly handles rational masses and their encoding lengths.

Exact computation of the marginals. Let \(L\) denote the total binary input length. Assume first that \(m\ge 1\). Let \(Q\) be the product of all denominators appearing in the masses of both societies, with positive denominators. Then every mass is an integer multiple of \(1/Q\), and \(\log_2 Q\) is at most the sum of the input denominator bit lengths, hence is \(O(L)\).

Define integer marginals
\(a_{c,i}=Qp^\mu_{c,i}\) and \(b_{d,i}=Qp^\nu_{d,i}\).
They can be computed by scanning the explicitly listed rankings and adding their scaled masses to the appropriate entries. Because each ranking contains every candidate exactly once and each society has total mass \(1\),
\(\sum_{i=1}^m a_{c,i}=\sum_{i=1}^m b_{d,i}=Q\)
for every \(c,d\). Every entry lies between \(0\) and \(Q\), so these computations have polynomial bit complexity.

Optimal transport on positions. Consider any two nonnegative integer vectors \(a,b\) of length \(m\), each summing to \(Q\). Write
\(A_k=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\) and \(B_k=\sum_{i=1}^k b_i\), with \(A_0=B_0=0\).
We prove
\(\operatorname{EMD}(a/Q,b/Q)=Q^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}|A_k-B_k|\),
and construct a plan attaining this value.

A scaled transport plan is a nonnegative matrix \(T\) with row sums \(a_i\) and column sums \(b_j\); its cost before division by \(Q\) is \(\sum_{i,j}|i-j|T_{ij}\).

For each cut between positions \(k\) and \(k+1\), define the mass crossing rightward and leftward by
\(R_k=\sum_{i\le k<j}T_{ij}\) and
\(S_k=\sum_{j\le k<i}T_{ij}\).
The marginal constraints imply \(R_k-S_k=A_k-B_k\): subtracting total demand on the first \(k\) positions from total supply there cancels all mass transported within those positions. Consequently,
\(R_k+S_k\ge |A_k-B_k|\).

A shipment from \(i\) to \(j\) crosses exactly \(|i-j|\) cuts. Summing its contribution over those cuts therefore gives
\(\sum_{i,j}|i-j|T_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(R_k+S_k)\ge\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}|A_k-B_k|\).

To attain this bound, partition the interval \((0,Q]\) into intervals
\(I_i=(A_{i-1},A_i]\) and, separately, into intervals
\(J_j=(B_{j-1},B_j]\).
Define
\(T_{ij}=\operatorname{length}(I_i\cap J_j)=\max\{0,\min(A_i,B_j)-\max(A_{i-1},B_{j-1})\}\).

The two partitions show that the row sums are \(a_i\) and the column sums are \(b_j\), so this is a feasible integer plan. At cut \(k\), its rightward crossing mass is the length of \((0,A_k]\cap(B_k,Q]\), namely \(\max\{A_k-B_k,0\}\). Its leftward crossing mass is similarly \(\max\{B_k-A_k,0\}\). Thus \(R_k+S_k=|A_k-B_k|\) at every cut, establishing equality in the lower bound.

Dividing this plan by \(Q\) gives the required optimal transport plan for the probability vectors. The construction also covers zero entries and, with an empty cut sum, \(m=1\).

Optimal candidate matching. For every \(c\in C\) and \(d\in D\), compute the integer weight
\(W_{cd}=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left|\sum_{i=1}^k a_{c,i}-\sum_{i=1}^k b_{d,i}\right|\).
The transport formula proves that
\(W_{cd}=Q\,\operatorname{EMD}(p^\mu_c,p^\nu_d)\).

Construct the complete bipartite graph with vertex classes \(C,D\) and edge weights \(W_{cd}\). A perfect matching selects exactly one partner for each candidate on both sides, so perfect matchings correspond bijectively to the allowed bijections. For the matching associated with \(\delta\), its weight is exactly
\(Q\sum_{c\in C}\operatorname{EMD}(p^\mu_c,p^\nu_{\delta(c)})\).

Apply the standard polynomial-time algorithm for minimum-weight bipartite perfect matching with binary-encoded integer weights, such as the Hungarian algorithm. Its hypotheses hold: the graph is finite and bipartite, both sides have size \(m\), every edge is present, and all weights are integers. In particular, a perfect matching exists.

If its minimum weight is \(K\), then
\(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu,\nu)=K/Q\).
The matching supplies an optimal bijection. For each selected pair, the interval-intersection construction supplies an optimal position-transport plan. These plans can be selected independently because the stated objective imposes only the individual marginal constraints for each matched pair.

Bit complexity and decision. Each prefix sum lies between \(0\) and \(Q\). Hence
\(0\le W_{cd}\le(m-1)Q\) and \(0\le K\le m(m-1)Q\).
All these integers have \(O(L+\log m)\) bits.

Computing all pair weights uses \(O(m^3)\) arithmetic operations after the marginals are available. The matching algorithm runs in polynomial time in the graph size and weight bit lengths. Constructing all selected transport matrices uses \(O(m^3)\) further arithmetic operations; each output entry has the form \(T_{ij}/Q\), with \(0\le T_{ij}\le Q\), and therefore has polynomial encoding length. Since rankings are explicitly listed, \(m\) is bounded by the input length.

Finally, write the threshold as \(t=u/v\), where \(v>0\). The exact decision is obtained by testing \(vK\le uQ\), an integer comparison of polynomial bit complexity. If \(m=0\) is allowed, the empty bijection has cost \(0\), and the decision is simply \(0\le t\).

Thus both the decision problem and the requested optimal-output problem are polynomial-time solvable. No expansion into a number of voters proportional to \(Q\) is needed.
