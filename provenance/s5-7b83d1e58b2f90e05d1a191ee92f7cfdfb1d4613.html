The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) candidates, \(\tau\) ranking types \(\sigma_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and shared nonnegative rational transposition costs \(\varphi\), compute a ranking \(\pi\) whose expected distance \(C_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\,\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma_t)\) is at most \(4\) times optimum. The paper's method minimizes the expected surrogate \(D\), where \(D(\pi,\sigma)\) sums shortest-path costs between each candidate's positions; the guarantee is \(2\) for metric costs and weighted Kendall costs.

Conclusion

Yes. The assignment-based approximation algorithm extends directly to binary rational masses. It achieves factors \(4\), \(2\), and \(2\) for general costs, metric costs, and weighted Kendall costs, respectively, without expanding the support into individual voters.

A conservative bound on its running time is
\(O\!\left((m^3+\tau m^2+L)(L+\log(m+1))^2\right)\)
bit operations.

1. A pairwise comparison theorem

For a candidate \(x\), let \(p_\pi(x)=\pi^{-1}(x)\) denote its position in ranking \(\pi\).

Define a distance \(\delta\) on positions as follows.

| Cost class | Definition of \(\delta(a,b)\) |
|---|---|
| General costs | Shortest-path distance in the complete undirected graph whose edge costs are \(\varphi\) |
| Metric costs | \(\delta(a,b)=\varphi(a,b)\) |
| Weighted Kendall costs | \(\delta(a,b)=\sum_{j=\min(a,b)}^{\max(a,b)-1}w_j\) |

In every case, \(\delta(a,a)=0\). For metric costs, the shortest-path distance equals \(\varphi\): repeated application of the triangle inequality bounds every path from below by its direct edge cost.

Define the surrogate
\(D(\pi,\sigma)=\sum_{x=1}^{m}\delta(p_\pi(x),p_\sigma(x))\).

We use the following known pairwise bounds:

| Cost class | Bounds |
|---|---|
| General costs | \(\frac12D(\pi,\sigma)\leq\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\leq2D(\pi,\sigma)\) |
| Metric costs | \(\frac12D(\pi,\sigma)\leq\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\leq D(\pi,\sigma)\) |
| Weighted Kendall costs | \(\frac12D(\pi,\sigma)\leq\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\leq D(\pi,\sigma)\) |

These are Theorem 28 and Lemma 31 of the [full treatment](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.1471). Their hypotheses match this problem: nonnegative shared position costs; the triangle inequality for metric costs; and an extended path, meaning only consecutive positions can be swapped, for weighted Kendall costs. Neither strict positivity nor monotonicity of the adjacent costs is required.

For clarity, the lower bound also has a direct proof. Follow each candidate through any swap sequence transforming \(\pi\) into \(\sigma\). Its trajectory is a walk between its initial and final positions, so its trajectory cost is at least \(\delta(p_\pi(x),p_\sigma(x))\). Every swap contributes its cost to exactly two candidate trajectories. Consequently,
\(D(\pi,\sigma)\leq2\,\operatorname{cost}(\text{sequence})\).
Taking the minimum over sequences proves the lower bound, including when costs vanish.

All distances are finite: the allowed swaps generate every permutation. A minimum exists because the graph of ranking states is finite and a walk with repeated states can have its cycles removed without increasing cost.

2. Exact minimization of the weighted surrogate

Set
\(F_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_tD(\pi,\sigma_t)\).

Construct a complete bipartite graph with positions on one side and candidates on the other. Give edge \((a,x)\) cost
\(A_{a,x}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\delta(a,p_{\sigma_t}(x))\).

Compute a minimum-cost perfect matching and return the strict ranking \(\widehat\pi\) that places each candidate in its matched position.

Every perfect matching corresponds to exactly one strict ranking. Its cost is
\(\sum_{a=1}^{m}A_{a,\pi(a)} =\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{x=1}^{m}\delta(p_\pi(x),p_{\sigma_t}(x)) =F_\mu(\pi)\).

Thus the matching algorithm returns an exact minimizer of \(F_\mu\). It uses the masses directly.

3. Approximation guarantees

Let \(\rho^\star\) minimize \(C_\mu\); such a ranking exists because \(S_m\) is finite. Write \(\beta=2\) for general costs and \(\beta=1\) for either other subclass.

The pairwise bounds give
\(\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\leq\beta D(\pi,\sigma)\)
and
\(D(\pi,\sigma)\leq2\mathsf d_\varphi(\pi,\sigma)\).

Multiplying by each nonnegative mass and summing, then using the optimality of \(\widehat\pi\) for the surrogate, yields
\(C_\mu(\widehat\pi) \leq\beta F_\mu(\widehat\pi) \leq\beta F_\mu(\rho^\star) \leq2\beta C_\mu(\rho^\star) =2\beta\,\mathrm{OPT}\).

This proves all three requested factors. The argument also covers \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\), in which case the returned ranking has zero objective value.

4. Bit complexity

We give an implementation using integer arithmetic throughout.

Write each input cost as a numerator divided by a positive denominator, and let \(Q\) be the product of all cost denominators. For weighted Kendall costs, use the denominators of the \(w_a\). Every scaled allowed-swap cost is then an integer.

Likewise, write \(\mu_t=u_t/v_t\), let \(R=\prod_t v_t\), and define
\(M_t=u_t(R/v_t)\).
These are positive integers satisfying \(\sum_t M_t=R\).

Both \(Q\) and \(R\) have \(O(L)\) bits: the logarithm of a product of denominators is bounded by their total input bit length. Their numerical values can be large, but no computation enumerates that many objects.

Compute the scaled position distances
\(\Delta(a,b)=Q\delta(a,b)\).
For general costs, Floyd–Warshall takes \(O(m^3)\) integer additions and comparisons. For metric costs, \(\Delta(a,b)=Q\varphi(a,b)\). For weighted Kendall costs, prefix sums compute the path distances.

The integer assignment costs are
\(W_{a,x}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}M_t\Delta(a,p_{\sigma_t}(x)) =RQ\,A_{a,x}\).

Since \(RQ>0\), minimizing these integer costs gives exactly the same matchings as minimizing \(A\).

To bound their sizes, let \(H\) be the largest scaled allowed-swap cost, taking \(H=0\) if all costs vanish. Nonnegative costs ensure that a shortest position path can be chosen simple, so
\(\Delta(a,b)\leq(m-1)H\).
Therefore
\(W_{a,x}\leq R(m-1)H\).
All these integers have \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) bits.

Building the assignment matrix uses \(O(\tau m^2)\) integer arithmetic operations. The Hungarian algorithm solves the complete integer assignment problem in \(O(m^3)\) additions, subtractions, and comparisons; its intermediate integers also have \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) bits. Constructing the denominator products and scaled inputs requires at most \(O(L)\) further arithmetic operations.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, a conservative total bound is therefore
\(O\!\left((m^3+\tau m^2+L)(L+\log(m+1))^2\right)\)
bit operations.

For \(m=1\), returning the unique ranking is immediate. Thus the algorithms are polynomial in the actual support representation and total input bit length.
