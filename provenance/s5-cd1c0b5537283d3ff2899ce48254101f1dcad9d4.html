Lemma 5 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_i\) for \(\tau\) ordered voter types and a finite rational polyhedral description of a monotone complete coalition rule \(g\) on \(Q=\prod_i[0,\mu_i]\), find the infimum \(\alpha\ge1\) over ordered nonnegative per-mass weights \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_\tau\ge0\) satisfying \(w\cdot x\ge1\) for every winning allocation \(x\) and \(w\cdot x\le\alpha\) for every allocation in the closure of the losing region; report if no finite feasible weights exist.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) ordered types with positive masses \(\mu_i\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Allocations are real vectors in \(Q=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_i]\). Define \(x\preceq y\) when \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i\le\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_i\) for every \(1\le k\le\tau\).
A complete coalition rule is a function \(g:Q\to\{0,1\}\) satisfying \(g(0)=0\), \(g(\mu)=1\), and \(x\preceq y\Rightarrow g(x)\le g(y)\).
The input explicitly partitions \(Q\) into finitely many labeled cells, each described by rational linear equalities and strict or weak inequalities; the labels specify \(g\), including every boundary point. Let \(m\) count the listed cells and constraints, and \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Write \(W=g^{-1}(1)\) and \(D=g^{-1}(0)\), and take \(\overline D\) relative to \(Q\). The continuous critical threshold is the infimum of \(\alpha\ge1\) for which real per-mass weights \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_\tau\ge0\) satisfy \(w\cdot x\ge1\) for every \(x\in W\) and \(w\cdot x\le\alpha\) for every \(x\in\overline D\).

Is computing this infimum exactly, or reporting that no finite feasible weights exist, polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete complete simple games, the critical threshold equals the optimum of an LP using ordered nonnegative type weights and explicitly listed shift-minimal winning and shift-maximal losing vectors, meaning the minimal and maximal vectors under \(\preceq\). [Freixas–Kurz](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.2861v2).
For graph-represented discrete simple games, computing the threshold without the lower bound \(\alpha\ge1\) is NP-hard, and polynomial-time solvable for bipartite graphs. [Hof et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08841). No result settling exactly the continuous problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one because the society is a distribution; required positive support masses and at least one type.
  • Defined continuous completeness by monotonicity under prefix-sum dominance, extending the discrete ordering of coalition vectors.
  • Required the empty allocation to lose and the grand allocation to win, as for discrete simple games.
  • Made the polyhedral encoding an explicit labeled partition using rational equalities and strict or weak inequalities, so boundary outcomes and representation size are unambiguous.
  • Interpreted losing-region closure relative to the allocation box and weights as real constants per unit mass.
  • Defined m as the total number of listed cells and constraints and L as the total binary input length.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). There is a polynomial-size rational linear program whose feasible pairs \((w,\alpha)\) are exactly those in the question. If feasible, its optimum is attained and is a rational number computable exactly in polynomial time.

The proof handles strict inequalities, cells of lower dimension, and every assigned boundary label.

1. Computing the closure of each cell

Interpret each listed cell as the intersection of its defining constraints with \(Q\). After reversing inequalities where necessary, write a cell as

\(C=\{x\in Q:Ax\le b,\ Ex=f,\ Bx<d\}\).

All matrices and vectors are rational. Let its weak relaxation be

\(P=\{x\in Q:Ax\le b,\ Ex=f,\ Bx\le d\}\).

Lemma 1. If \(C\) is nonempty, its closure relative to \(Q\) is \(P\).

Proof. The set \(P\) is closed and contains \(C\), so \(\overline C\subseteq P\).

Conversely, fix \(z\in C\) and any \(y\in P\). For \(0<t\le1\), put

\(y_t=(1-t)y+tz\).

Convexity preserves the box constraints, weak inequalities, and equalities. For every strict row,

\(B_\ell y_t=(1-t)B_\ell y+tB_\ell z<(1-t)d_\ell+td_\ell=d_\ell\).

Thus \(y_t\in C\), and \(y_t\to y\) as \(t\to0\). Hence \(P\subseteq\overline C\). This also proves the assertion for cells of lower dimension. \(\square\)

Nonemptiness can be tested exactly in polynomial time. If there are strict constraints, solve the rational LP

\(\max t\)

subject to

\(x\in Q,\quad Ax\le b,\quad Ex=f,\quad Bx+t\mathbf1\le d,\quad 0\le t\le1\).

The cell is nonempty exactly when this LP is feasible and its optimum is positive:

  • A feasible point with \(t>0\) belongs to \(C\).
  • Given \(x\in C\), the finitely many strict slacks are positive. Their minimum, capped at \(1\), supplies a feasible positive \(t\).

The feasible region is compact, so a feasible LP attains its optimum. If there are no strict constraints, ordinary LP feasibility suffices.

Discard any empty cells. For every remaining cell, replace strict inequalities by weak ones and equalities by pairs of weak inequalities. Include the box constraints explicitly. Its closure now has the form

\(P_j=\{x\in\mathbb R^\tau:H_jx\le h_j\}\).

Each \(P_j\) is a nonempty compact rational polytope.

2. Passing from labeled cells to closed polytopes

Let \(J_W\) and \(J_D\) index the nonempty winning and losing cells. Because these unions are finite, Lemma 1 gives

\(\overline W=\bigcup_{j\in J_W}P_j,\qquad \overline D=\bigcup_{j\in J_D}P_j\).

For fixed finite \(w\), the map \(x\mapsto w^\mathsf Tx\) is continuous. Consequently,

\(w^\mathsf Tx\ge1\text{ for every }x\in W\)

is equivalent to

\(w^\mathsf Tx\ge1\text{ for every }x\in\overline W\).

Indeed, the forward implication follows by taking limits of winning points; the reverse follows from \(W\subseteq\overline W\).

Thus the original constraints are exactly

\(w^\mathsf Tx\ge1\quad\text{for every }x\in P_j,\ j\in J_W,\)

and

\(w^\mathsf Tx\le\alpha\quad\text{for every }x\in P_j,\ j\in J_D.\)

This passage preserves the effect of boundary labels: each closure is taken from the actual cell carrying that label.

3. Linear certificates for the universal inequalities

We use the following standard consequence of LP strong duality. If

\(P=\{x\in\mathbb R^\tau:Hx\le h\}\)

is nonempty and compact, then, for any real vector \(c\) and real number \(\beta\),

\(c^\mathsf Tx\le\beta\text{ for every }x\in P\)

holds if and only if there exists \(\lambda\) satisfying

\(\lambda\ge0,\qquad H^\mathsf T\lambda=c,\qquad h^\mathsf T\lambda\le\beta.\)

To verify this precisely, consider the primal-dual pair

\(\max\{c^\mathsf Tx:Hx\le h\},\qquad \min\{h^\mathsf T\lambda:H^\mathsf T\lambda=c,\ \lambda\ge0\}.\)

The primal is feasible and has a finite attained optimum because \(P\) is nonempty and compact. Strong duality supplies an attained dual optimum with the same value. Conversely, any displayed certificate gives

\(c^\mathsf Tx=\lambda^\mathsf THx\le\lambda^\mathsf Th\le\beta\)

for every \(x\in P\). These are the usual [LP duality statements](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n12-duality.html); all their hypotheses have been checked here.

Apply this equivalence separately to every cell:

  • For a winning cell, use \(c=-w\) and \(\beta=-1\).
  • For a losing cell, use \(c=w\) and \(\beta=\alpha\).

4. The exact finite linear program

Introduce one vector \(\lambda_j\), with one coordinate per row of \(H_j\), for each winning cell, and one analogous vector \(\eta_j\) for each losing cell.

Minimize \(\alpha\), subject to

\(\alpha\ge1,\qquad w_i-w_{i+1}\ge0\ (1\le i<\tau),\qquad w_\tau\ge0,\)

and, for every \(j\in J_W\),

\(\lambda_j\ge0,\qquad H_j^\mathsf T\lambda_j=-w,\qquad h_j^\mathsf T\lambda_j\le-1,\)

and, for every \(j\in J_D\),

\(\eta_j\ge0,\qquad H_j^\mathsf T\eta_j=w,\qquad h_j^\mathsf T\eta_j\le\alpha.\)

Proposition 2. A pair \((w,\alpha)\) is feasible for the original problem if and only if it extends to a feasible point of this LP.

Proof. The weight-order constraints give exactly \(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_\tau\ge0\). Section 2 identifies the original universal constraints with those over the polytopes \(P_j\). Section 3 supplies certificates for each of these constraints in both directions. There are finitely many cells, so the certificates can all be included simultaneously. \(\square\)

In particular, every constraint in this formulation is linear. The coefficients multiplying the certificate variables are fixed input coefficients.

5. Characterizing the case of no finite weights

There is also a simple geometric characterization.

Proposition 3. Finite feasible weights exist if and only if \(0\notin\overline W\).

Proof. If \(0\in\overline W\), the winning constraint and continuity would imply

\(w^\mathsf T0\ge1,\)

which is impossible for any finite vector \(w\).

Conversely, suppose \(0\notin\overline W\). The set \(\overline W\) is nonempty because \(g(\mu)=1\), and it is compact. Since every coordinate of every point in \(Q\) is nonnegative, the function \(\sum_i x_i\) vanishes on \(Q\) exactly at \(0\). Therefore

\(\delta=\min_{x\in\overline W}\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}x_i>0.\)

Also \(\delta\le1\), because \(\mu\in W\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\).

Choose

\(w_1=\cdots=w_\tau=\delta^{-1},\qquad \alpha=\delta^{-1}.\)

These weights are ordered and nonnegative, and \(\alpha\ge1\). Every winning point satisfies

\(w^\mathsf Tx=\delta^{-1}\sum_i x_i\ge1.\)

Every point of \(\overline D\subseteq Q\) satisfies

\(w^\mathsf Tx\le\delta^{-1}\sum_i\mu_i=\alpha.\)

Hence these are finite feasible weights. \(\square\)

The condition is computable exactly: solve

\(\delta_j=\min\{\mathbf1^\mathsf Tx:H_jx\le h_j\}\)

for each winning cell and take \(\delta=\min_{j\in J_W}\delta_j\). Finite feasibility is equivalent to \(\delta>0\).

The impossible case can occur even for a valid complete rule: take \(\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=1\), with the losing cell \(\{0\}\) and winning cell \((0,1]\).

6. Polynomial size, exactness, and conclusion

Let \(s\) be the number of retained cells, and let \(r_j\) be the number of rows of \(H_j\). Converting equalities into pairs and adding the box to each cell gives

\(\sum_j r_j=O(m+s\tau)=O(m\tau).\)

The final LP has

\(\tau+1+\sum_j r_j\)

variables. It has \(s\tau\) cell-certificate equalities, together with \(O(s+\tau+\sum_j r_j)\) inequalities, counting nonnegativity constraints. Both quantities are polynomial in \(m,\tau\).

Its coefficients come from the original rational data, their negatives, and \(0,1,-1\). Even if written as dense matrices, its total binary encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The preprocessing LPs likewise have polynomial encoding length, and there are only polynomially many of them.

We now apply the standard bit-model theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and optimization are solvable in polynomial time in the binary encoding length; a feasible LP with a finite optimum has a rational optimal solution of polynomial encoding length. Polynomial-time algorithms and the rational-solution bit bounds are described in [MIT’s LP algorithm notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/18/Scribe/s14-lpAlgorithms/s14-lpAlgorithms.html) and [LP structure notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/21/Notes/n11-lp.html).

If the final LP is infeasible, Proposition 2 proves that no finite feasible weights exist. If it is feasible, its objective is bounded below by \(1\). The finite-optimum theorem therefore guarantees an attained rational optimum, which Proposition 2 identifies exactly with the requested infimum.

Thus the target question is resolved: exact computation, including reporting impossibility, is polynomial-time solvable under the stated explicit cell representation. The graph representation in [Hof et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08841) is a different input representation; its hardness result does not establish hardness for this explicitly listed polyhedral partition.
