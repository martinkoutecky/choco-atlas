The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), \(k\) initial districts, a target \(c\in C\), a rational movement budget \(\beta\), and rational masses \(\mu_{i,a}\) over supported types \((i,a)\) (initial district and top-ranked candidate), decide whether there are assignments \(x_{i,a,j}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{i,a,j}=\mu_{i,a}\) and \(\sum_{i,a}\sum_{j\ne i}x_{i,a,j}\le\beta\), such that \(c\) is the unique overall winner. For \(q_{j,a}=\sum_i x_{i,a,j}\) and \(W_j=\arg\max_{a\in C}q_{j,a}\), this means \(\sum_j\mathbf{1}[c\in W_j]>\sum_j\mathbf{1}[a\in W_j]\) for every \(a\ne c\), counting every district-level tied winner as in the paper.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(m\ge 1\), \(k\ge 1\) labeled districts, a target \(c\in C\), and a binary rational budget \(\beta\ge 0\), consider the following decision problem.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \((i,a)\) with positive mass \(\mu_{i,a}\), each mass a binary rational; \(i\) is the initial district and \(a\) the unique top-ranked candidate. Unsupported types have zero mass. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_{i,a}\mu_{i,a}=1\), and require \(\sum_a\mu_{i,a}>0\) for every initial district.
Mass is divisible and may move between any districts without changing its top-ranked candidate. Staying costs zero; moving costs one per unit mass. Seek real assignments \(x_{i,a,j}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_jx_{i,a,j}=\mu_{i,a}\) and \(\sum_{i,a}\sum_{j\ne i}x_{i,a,j}\le\beta\).
Set \(q_{j,a}=\sum_i x_{i,a,j}\), require \(\sum_a q_{j,a}>0\) for every final district, and let \(W_j=\arg\max_{a\in C}q_{j,a}\). Every tied plurality winner receives one district win. The assignment succeeds exactly when \(\sum_j\mathbf{1}[c\in W_j]>\sum_j\mathbf{1}[a\in W_j]\) for every \(a\ne c\).

Is this continuous movement-budget decision problem in \(\mathsf{P}\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,k,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.

The indivisible-voter version with unit movement costs is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete; it admits polynomial-time algorithms when the budget, the number of districts, or the number of candidates is fixed ([source](https://ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2022/pdfs/p1575.pdf)). No result settling this exact divisible-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The mass normalization was unspecified; total mass is set to one, with the budget measured in the same mass units.
  • The graded question omitted district nonemptiness; initial and final districts must have positive mass, carrying over the partition into nonempty districts.
  • The budget domain was unspecified beyond rationality; it is nonnegative and encoded in binary.
  • Assignments may use nonnegative real values; unsupported types have zero mass, and the positive integer candidate and district counts remain unrestricted input parameters.

1. Result

The decision problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete when $m$ and $k$ are unbounded.

The hardness reduction below applies even when:

  • each initial district supports at most two candidates;
  • exactly one initial district contains any mass supporting $c$;
  • every initial district has a unique plurality winner.

The proof allows arbitrary real assignments and counts every tied winner. In particular, it does not rely on indivisibility.

There is also an exact algorithm with running time
$$ r^k\operatorname{poly}(m,k,\tau,L), \qquad r=\left|\left\{a\in C:\sum_i\mu_{i,a}>0\right\}\right| \leq \min\{m,\tau\}. $$
Thus fixed $k$ remains polynomial-time solvable.

2. A discrete source problem

We use the standard $\mathsf{NP}$-completeness of positive-integer SUBSET SUM: given positive integers $v_1,\ldots,v_N$ and a positive integer $b$, all encoded in binary, decide whether some subset sums to $b$. This is the equality version of the KNAPSACK problem in [Karp’s paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

We first reduce it to the following cardinality-constrained problem:

Given positive integers $w_1,\ldots,w_n$, a positive integer $B$, and $1\leq s\leq n$, decide whether there is a set $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ satisfying
$$ > |J|=s, > \qquad > \sum_{j\in J}w_j=B. > $$

For a SUBSET SUM instance, take
$$ n=2N,\qquad s=N,\qquad B=b+N, $$
and weights
$$ w_i=v_i+1\quad (1\leq i\leq N), \qquad w_{N+i}=1\quad (1\leq i\leq N). $$

Any selection of exactly $N$ weights has sum $N$ plus the sum of the original $v_i$ corresponding to its selected weights among the first $N$. Conversely, a subset of the original weights can be extended to a selection of exactly $N$ weights by using the added weights of value $1$. Therefore the two instances are equivalent. This proves that the cardinality-constrained problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard.

3. Construction of the society

Consider an instance $w_1,\ldots,w_n,B,s$ of the cardinality-constrained problem. Define
$$ W=\max_j w_j, \qquad A=2W+B+1, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac14, $$
and
$$ d_j=A-\frac{w_j}{2}, \qquad K=sA-\frac B2, \qquad M=2sA+1. $$

These quantities satisfy
$$ d_j>w_j>0,\qquad K>0,\qquad M>K. $$
Indeed, $A>3W/2$, $A>B$, and
$$ M-K=sA+\frac B2+1>0. $$

The candidates are
$$ C=\{c,z\}\cup\{a_j,b_j:1\leq j\leq n\}. $$
There are three kinds of labeled districts. Initially, their masses are as follows; every unlisted mass is zero.

| District | Initial positive masses |
|---|---|
| One reservoir district $R$ | $B+\varepsilon$ for $c$ |
| Item district $D_j$, for each $1\leq j\leq n$ | $d_j$ for $a_j$ and $w_j$ for $b_j$ |
| Protected district $H_t$, for each $1\leq t\leq s$ | $M$ for $z$ |

For now these are unnormalized masses, and the movement budget is $K$. Their total is
$$ Z=B+\varepsilon+nA+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^n w_j+sM. $$
The actual instance divides every mass and the budget by $Z$, so its budget is
$$ \beta=\frac KZ. $$

Multiplying an assignment for the normalized instance by $Z$ preserves all winner sets and multiplies its movement cost by $Z$. Consequently, we may prove equivalence using the unnormalized units.

The construction has
$$ m=2n+2,\qquad k=n+s+1,\qquad \tau=2n+s+1. $$
Every initial district is positive and has a unique winner.

4. Any successful assignment yields a discrete solution

Let an arbitrary real assignment have total movement $\mathcal M\leq K$. Write its final masses in unnormalized units.

4.1. The protected districts retain their winners

Fix a protected district $H_t$. Let $O_z$ denote the mass of $z$ moved out of this district. For a candidate $a\ne z$, let $I_a$ denote its mass moved into this district.

Initially, the district contains $M$ units of $z$ and no mass of $a$. Hence
$$ q_{H_t,z}\geq M-O_z, \qquad q_{H_t,a}=I_a. $$
The movements counted by $O_z$ and $I_a$ belong to different candidates. They are therefore disjoint portions of the total movement, giving
$$ O_z+I_a\leq\mathcal M. $$
It follows that
$$ q_{H_t,z}-q_{H_t,a} \geq M-O_z-I_a \geq M-\mathcal M \geq M-K >0. $$

Thus $z$ remains the unique winner of every protected district. In particular, $z$ has at least $s$ district wins, and $c$ cannot win a protected district.

If the assignment succeeds, $c$ must therefore have at least $s+1$ wins. The reservoir accounts for at most one of them, so $c$ must win at least $s$ item districts.

Choose any set $J$ of exactly $s$ such item districts, and put
$$ S=\sum_{j\in J}w_j. $$

4.2. A movement lower bound

For each $j\in J$, set
$$ y_j=q_{D_j,c}, \qquad Y=\sum_{j\in J}y_j. $$
All initial $c$-mass lies in the reservoir. Consequently, the $Y$ units of $c$ in these item districts have moved, and conservation gives
$$ Y\leq B+\varepsilon. $$

Let $O_{j,a_j}$ and $O_{j,b_j}$ denote the masses moved out of $D_j$ for its two private candidates. Each private candidate initially occurs only in its own item district, so
$$ q_{D_j,a_j}=d_j-O_{j,a_j}, \qquad q_{D_j,b_j}=w_j-O_{j,b_j}. $$

Because $c$ is a winner of $D_j$, including when it ties,
$$ y_j\geq q_{D_j,a_j}, \qquad y_j\geq q_{D_j,b_j}. $$
Writing $t_+=\max\{t,0\}$, these inequalities imply
$$ O_{j,a_j}\geq(d_j-y_j)_+, \qquad O_{j,b_j}\geq(w_j-y_j)_+. $$

All outgoing private-candidate masses counted here are disjoint from one another and from the incoming $c$-mass. Therefore
$$ \mathcal M \geq \sum_{j\in J} \left[y_j+(d_j-y_j)_++(w_j-y_j)_+\right]. $$
For every real $y_j\geq0$,
$$ y_j+(d_j-y_j)_+\geq d_j. $$
Also,
$$ \sum_{j\in J}(w_j-y_j)_+ \geq \max\left\{\sum_{j\in J}(w_j-y_j),0\right\} =\max\{S-Y,0\}. $$
Combining these facts gives the key bound
$$ \mathcal M \geq sA-\frac S2+\max\{S-Y,0\} \geq sA-\frac S2+\max\{S-B-\varepsilon,0\}. $$

This bound holds for arbitrary real movements and arbitrary ties.

4.3. The budget forces exact equality

Both $S$ and $B$ are integers.

If $S<B$, then $S\leq B-1$, and the lower bound gives
$$ \mathcal M \geq sA-\frac S2 =K+\frac{B-S}{2} \geq K+\frac12, $$
contradicting $\mathcal M\leq K$.

If $S>B$, then $S\geq B+1$. Since $\varepsilon=1/4$, the positive-part term in the bound is strictly positive, and
$$ \mathcal M \geq sA-\frac S2+S-B-\varepsilon = K+\frac{S-B}{2}-\varepsilon \geq K+\frac14. $$
This is again a contradiction.

Hence
$$ S=B. $$
The chosen set $J$ has exactly $s$ members and weights summing to $B$. Thus every successful assignment yields a solution to the discrete source instance.

5. A discrete solution yields a successful assignment

Conversely, suppose there is a set $J$ satisfying
$$ |J|=s, \qquad \sum_{j\in J}w_j=B. $$

For every $j\in J$, perform precisely these movements:

  • Move $w_j$ units of $c$ from the reservoir to $D_j$.
  • Move $d_j-w_j$ units of $a_j$ from $D_j$ to $H_1$.

These movements are feasible. The reservoir exports exactly $B$ units and retains $\varepsilon>0$. Moreover,
$$ d_j-w_j=A-\frac{3w_j}{2}>0. $$

In each selected item district, the final masses are
$$ q_{D_j,c}=q_{D_j,a_j}=q_{D_j,b_j}=w_j>0. $$
Thus all three candidates win that district. In every unselected item district, nothing changes, and $a_j$ remains its unique winner because $d_j>w_j$.

The total movement is exactly
$$ \sum_{j\in J}w_j+\sum_{j\in J}(d_j-w_j) = \sum_{j\in J}d_j = sA-\frac B2 = K. $$

All protected districts still have $z$ as unique winner. To check $H_1$, its initial $z$-mass is $M>K$, whereas every other candidate’s incoming mass is at most the total movement $K$. No $z$-mass moves.

Every final district is positive. The district scores are:

  • $c$ has $s+1$ wins: the reservoir and the selected item districts;
  • $z$ has exactly $s$ wins;
  • each $a_j$ has exactly one win;
  • each $b_j$ has at most one win.

Since $s\geq1$, the target strictly beats every rival. After normalization, the cost is exactly $\beta=K/Z$.

Together with Section 4, this proves the equivalence required for the reduction.

6. Encoding size and hardness

Let
$$ h=\left\lceil\log_2(1+B+W)\right\rceil. $$
The integers $A$ and the numerators of the $d_j$ have $O(h)$ bits. The integer $M$ and the integer $4Z$ have
$$ O\bigl(h+\log(n+s)\bigr) $$
bits.

Every normalized mass and the budget can be represented using the common denominator $4Z$. Their respective numerators are among
$$ 4B+1,\qquad 4A-2w_j,\qquad 4w_j,\qquad 4M, \qquad 4sA-2B. $$
Thus the output length satisfies
$$ L_{\mathrm{out}} = O\bigl((n+s)(h+\log(n+s))\bigr). $$
Computing the construction takes
$$ (n+s)\operatorname{poly}\bigl(h+\log(n+s)\bigr) $$
bit operations.

For the padding reduction from a SUBSET SUM instance with $N$ weights, the society has
$$ m=4N+2,\qquad k=3N+1,\qquad \tau=5N+1. $$
Hence the entire reduction is polynomial-time in the binary source length, and the movement problem is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard.

7. Membership in $\mathsf{NP}$ and an exact running-time bound

For completeness, we prove the upper bounds directly, including the treatment of strict district positivity and tied winners.

7.1. Eliminating the transportation variables

Extend the initial matrix by setting unsupported masses to zero. Define
$$ T_a=\sum_i\mu_{i,a}. $$
A proposed final matrix $q$ must satisfy
$$ q_{j,a}\geq0, \qquad \sum_jq_{j,a}=T_a. $$

Its minimum realizable movement cost is
$$ D(q)=\sum_{j,a}(\mu_{j,a}-q_{j,a})_+ =\frac12\sum_{j,a}|\mu_{j,a}-q_{j,a}|. $$

To prove the first equality, at most $\min\{\mu_{j,a},q_{j,a}\}$ of an initial type can stay, giving the stated lower bound. For attainability, retain exactly this amount. For each candidate separately, send the remaining mass from districts with $\mu_{j,a}>q_{j,a}$ to those with $\mu_{j,a}<q_{j,a}$. Candidate-wise conservation equates total supply and demand, and these source and destination sets are disjoint. The resulting cost is exactly the sum of the positive parts.

Finally,
$$ \sum_j(\mu_{j,a}-q_{j,a})=0 $$
for each candidate, so the positive and negative differences have equal total absolute mass. This proves the second equality.

The budget condition is therefore equivalent to the existence of variables $u_{j,a}$ satisfying
$$ u_{j,a}\geq0, \qquad u_{j,a}\geq\mu_{j,a}-q_{j,a}, \qquad \sum_{j,a}u_{j,a}\leq\beta. $$
We may replace $\beta$ by $\min\{\beta,1\}$, because total mass is one.

7.2. Verification of a designated-winner choice

Let
$$ \mathcal A=\{a:T_a>0\}, \qquad r=|\mathcal A|. $$
An inactive candidate cannot win a positive district: it has zero mass there, while some candidate has positive mass.

If $m=1$, staying succeeds. If $m\geq2$ and $T_c=0$, success is impossible. Hence suppose $c\in\mathcal A$.

Choose a designated winner $w_j\in\mathcal A$ for every district. For this choice, let $P_w$ be the rational polytope defined by the conservation and budget constraints above, restricted to active candidates, together with
$$ q_{j,w_j}\geq q_{j,a} \qquad (j=1,\ldots,k,\ a\in\mathcal A). $$
Empty districts are temporarily permitted.

The polytope is compact: every $q_{j,a}\leq T_a\leq1$ and every $u_{j,a}\leq\beta\leq1$.

First maximize $\delta$ subject to $P_w$ and
$$ 0\leq\delta\leq1, \qquad \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}q_{j,a}\geq\delta \quad (j=1,\ldots,k). $$
A point with all districts positive exists exactly when this LP is feasible and has optimum strictly greater than zero. Indeed, finitely many positive district totals have a positive minimum.

If this test passes, for every district and active candidate compute
$$ G_{j,a} = \max_{(q,u)\in P_w} \left(q_{j,w_j}-q_{j,a}\right), $$
and define
$$ F_j=\{a\in\mathcal A:G_{j,a}=0\}. $$

All these gaps are nonnegative throughout $P_w$. Consequently, $a\in F_j$ exactly when it ties with $w_j$ throughout $P_w$. In particular, $F_j$ is contained in the winner set of every feasible point of $P_w$.

There is also a feasible point with positive districts whose winner sets are exactly the $F_j$. To construct one, take:

  • a feasible point with all districts positive;
  • for every pair with $G_{j,a}>0$, a maximizing point for that gap.

Average these finitely many points with positive weights. Convexity preserves feasibility, and the first point preserves district positivity. Every identically zero gap stays zero. Every other gap becomes positive because its own maximizing point contributes positively and all other contributions are nonnegative.

Accept the designation exactly when the winner sets $F_j$ give $c$ strictly more wins than every rival. Inactive candidates have score zero.

7.3. Correctness

Acceptance supplies positive districts with precisely the accepted winner sets. The transportation result realizes their matrix within budget.

Conversely, suppose a successful assignment exists, with winner sets $W_j$. Designate
$$ w_j=c\quad\text{whenever }c\in W_j, $$
and choose any $w_j\in W_j$ otherwise.

The successful matrix lies in $P_w$, so its positivity test passes. We have $F_j\subseteq W_j$. Furthermore, whenever $c\in W_j$, the designation $w_j=c$ implies $c\in F_j$; whenever $c\notin W_j$, containment implies $c\notin F_j$.

Thus the sets $F_j$ preserve exactly the target’s score and can only decrease rivals’ scores. This designation is accepted.

7.4. Bit complexity and certificates

Each designation requires at most $kr+1$ rational LPs. Each LP has $O(kr)$ variables and constraints. All coefficients have bit length polynomial in $L$: in particular, forming candidate totals from the input rationals gives quantities with $O(L)$ bits.

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and a finite optimum can be determined exactly in time polynomial in the LP encoding length. This permits exact comparisons of the optima with zero.

Enumerating the $r^k$ designations therefore gives running time
$$ r^k\operatorname{poly}(m,k,\tau,L), \qquad r\leq\min\{m,\tau\}. $$

Alternatively, a certificate consists of one designation, encoded using
$$ O\bigl(k\log(r+1)\bigr) $$
bits, and the verifier performs the polynomial-time procedure above. Because every initial district has support,
$$ k\leq\tau, \qquad r\leq\tau. $$
Hence the certificate length and all LP dimensions are polynomial in the input length. This proves membership in $\mathsf{NP}$.

Combining membership with the reduction proves $\mathsf{NP}$-completeness. Consequently, a running time polynomial in $m,k,\tau,L$ for unrestricted instances would imply $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$.
