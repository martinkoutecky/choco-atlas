The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given recipient types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), priorities \(\rho_t\), per-capita envy limits \(\Lambda_t\), and sample access to an item-valuation distribution \(\alpha\) on \([0,\bar{x}]^{|T|}\), compute an \(\epsilon\)-optimal welfare-maximizing allocation policy \(q_t(x)\) subject to the type quotas and per-capita envy constraints; characterize the algorithm's dependence on \(|T|\), \(\epsilon\), and the input representation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge 2\) recipient types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has binary-rational per-capita priority \(\rho_t>0\) and envy limit \(\Lambda_t\ge0\); set \(p_t=\mu_t\rho_t/\sum_s\mu_s\rho_s\).
Items have valuation vectors distributed according to an arbitrary probability measure \(\alpha\) on \([0,\bar x]^m\), where \(m=\tau\) and \(\bar x>0\) is binary rational. Access to \(\alpha\) consists solely of independent exact samples; sampling and arithmetic on sampled coordinates have unit cost. Let \(L\) be the total bit length of the supplied rational data.
An allocation policy is a measurable vector \(q(x)\) with \(q_t(x)\ge0\) and \(\sum_tq_t(x)=1\); it assigns an item to type \(t\) with probability \(q_t(x)\), distributed uniformly within that type. Randomization, including at ties, is allowed.
Feasibility requires the exact population quotas \(\mathbb E_\alpha[q_t(X)]=p_t\) and per-capita priority-adjusted envy bounds \(\max_{s\in T}\{\frac{\rho_t}{\rho_s\mu_s}\mathbb E_\alpha[X_tq_s(X)]-\frac{1}{\mu_t}\mathbb E_\alpha[X_tq_t(X)]\}\le\Lambda_t\) for every \(t\).
Welfare is \(W(q)=\mathbb E_\alpha[\sum_tX_tq_t(X)]\), and \(W^\star\) is its supremum over feasible policies. For rational \(0<\epsilon<\bar x\) and \(0<\delta<1/2\), the problem is to compute, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), a feasible policy satisfying \(W(q)\ge W^\star-\epsilon\); constraint violations are not included in the approximation allowance.

Can this problem be solved with time and sample complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\bar x/\epsilon,\log(1/\delta)\), or must at least one be superpolynomial in this sample-oracle model?

For finitely many recipients and continuously distributed item values, a published structural theorem asserts strong duality and an optimal allocation described by generalized Laguerre cells. [Yin–Kroer](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/a488aa1a0c00d76db8a922ef7815a786-Paper-Conference.pdf)
The same work reports stochastic convergence for the dual objective and, under a bounded-coefficient assumption, sample guarantees for empirical dual optimization; these statements do not establish exact population feasibility of a policy learned from samples. [Yin–Kroer](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/a488aa1a0c00d76db8a922ef7815a786-Paper-Conference.pdf)

  • Normalized recipient masses to sum to one and retained at least two positive-mass types.

  • Interpreted a type policy as an aggregate assignment probability, with uniform allocation within each type; translated priority-adjusted envy into per-capita units.
  • Required positive rational priorities, nonnegative rational envy limits, and bounded nonnegative valuations.

Conclusion

Polynomial time and sample complexity are impossible. There is a family with \(m=\tau=2\), zero envy limits, constant \(\bar x/\epsilon\) and constant \(\delta\), for which the required sample complexity is \(2^{\Omega(L)}\).

For the usual interpretation—computing a measurable policy fixed after training—exact quotas give an even stronger obstruction: no uniform finite sample bound exists in the listed parameters.

Allocation randomization is allowed throughout: the argument concerns the probabilities \(q_t(x)\), not deterministic assignments.

1. An explicit family with exponentially rare items

Fix an integer \(k\ge 4\), and set \(\eta=2^{-k}\). Supply the following rational data:

\[ \mu_1=\eta,\qquad \mu_2=1-\eta,\qquad \rho_1=1-\eta,\qquad \rho_2=\eta,\qquad \Lambda_1=\Lambda_2=0. \]

Set \(\bar x=1\), \(\epsilon=1/8\), and \(\delta=1/4\). The supplied bit length is \(L=\Theta(k)\). Moreover,

\[ \mu_1\rho_1=\mu_2\rho_2=\eta(1-\eta), \]

so the quotas are \(p_1=p_2=1/2\).

Write \(q(x)=q_1(x)\), so \(q_2(x)=1-q(x)\). Substituting the supplied masses and priorities into the envy inequalities shows that feasibility is equivalent to

\[ \mathbb E[q(X)]=\frac12,\qquad \mathbb E[X_1(1-2q(X))]\le 0,\qquad \mathbb E[X_2(2q(X)-1)]\le 0. \]

Indeed, the first cross-type envy expression is \(1/\eta\) times the first displayed envy numerator, and the second is \(1/(1-\eta)\) times the second numerator. Both factors are positive; self-envy is zero.

Define three valuation vectors:

\[ A=(3/4,0),\qquad B=(3/4+\eta,1),\qquad C=(0,0). \]

Consider two possible item laws, with identical supplied data:

  • Under \(\alpha_0\), each of \(A,B\) has probability \(1/2\).
  • Under \(\alpha_1\), each of \(A,B\) has probability \((1-\eta)/2\), and \(C\) has probability \(\eta\).

These are admissible probability measures with dyadic rational coordinates and probabilities.

2. Feasibility under \(\alpha_0\) forces equal allocation probabilities

Let \(u=q(A)\) and \(v=q(B)\). The quota under \(\alpha_0\) gives \(u+v=1\).

Using this equality, the first envy numerator becomes

\[ \frac12\left[\frac34(1-2u)+\left(\frac34+\eta\right)(1-2v)\right] =\frac{\eta}{2}(1-2v). \]

Thus the first envy inequality requires \(v\ge 1/2\). The second numerator is \((2v-1)/2\), so the second inequality requires \(v\le 1/2\). Consequently, every policy feasible under \(\alpha_0\) satisfies

\[ q(A)=q(B)=\frac12. \]

The constant policy \(q(x)=1/2\) is feasible, so \(\alpha_0\) is not an infeasible instance. Its optimal welfare is

\[ W_0^\star=\frac58+\frac{\eta}{4}. \]

If a policy feasible under \(\alpha_0\) is evaluated under \(\alpha_1\), its value at \(C\) contributes no welfare. Hence its welfare is necessarily

\[ W_1(q)=(1-\eta)\left(\frac58+\frac{\eta}{4}\right). \]

3. Under \(\alpha_1\), a feasible policy improves welfare by a constant

Consider the measurable policy specified on the item support by

\[ q(A)=1,\qquad q(B)=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta},\qquad q(C)=0. \]

Its values elsewhere can be set to \(1/2\). Since \(\eta\le 1/16\), all allocation probabilities lie in \([0,1]\).

Its quota is exactly

\[ \mathbb E_{\alpha_1}[q(X)] =\frac{1-\eta}{2}\left(1+\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\right) =\frac12. \]

Direct calculation gives the two envy numerators:

\[ \mathbb E_{\alpha_1}[X_1(1-2q(X))] =-\frac{\eta}{4}-\frac{3\eta^2}{2}<0, \]

\[ \mathbb E_{\alpha_1}[X_2(2q(X)-1)] =\frac{3\eta-1}{2}<0. \]

Thus this policy is exactly feasible. Its welfare is

\[ W_{\mathrm{cand}}=\frac78-\eta+\frac{\eta^2}{2}, \]

and therefore \(W_1^\star\ge W_{\mathrm{cand}}\). No assertion that this candidate is optimal is needed.

For every policy feasible under \(\alpha_0\), the difference between the candidate’s welfare and that policy’s welfare under \(\alpha_1\) is

\[ W_{\mathrm{cand}}-W_1(q) =\frac14-\frac{5\eta}{8}+\frac{3\eta^2}{4} \ge \frac{27}{128} >\frac18. \]

Consequently, no policy feasible under \(\alpha_0\) can meet the required welfare guarantee under \(\alpha_1\). In particular, the sets of successful output policies for the two laws are disjoint.

4. Coupling proves the exponential sample lower bound

Suppose an algorithm uses at most \(N\) samples and succeeds with probability at least \(1-\delta\) under each law.

Couple its executions as follows. Use the same internal random bits. For each oracle call, generate a uniformly chosen element of \(\{A,B\}\). Return it under \(\alpha_0\); under \(\alpha_1\), independently replace it by \(C\) with probability \(\eta\).

This gives the correct marginal sample laws. If no replacement occurs during the first \(N\) calls, the complete transcripts and output policies coincide. Adaptive stopping causes no difficulty: unused calls can be padded.

The probability of no replacement is \((1-\eta)^N\). On this event, the common output cannot succeed under both laws, by Section 3. Hence

\[ (1-\eta)^N \le \Pr(\text{failure under }\alpha_0) + \Pr(\text{failure under }\alpha_1) \le 2\delta. \]

It follows that

\[ N\ge \frac{\log(1/(2\delta))}{-\log(1-\eta)} \ge \frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\log\frac1{2\delta}. \]

The last inequality uses \(-\log(1-\eta)\le \eta/(1-\eta)\), obtained by integrating \(1/(1-t)\) over \(0\le t\le\eta\).

For \(\delta=1/4\) and \(\eta=2^{-k}\), this yields \(N=\Omega(2^k)=2^{\Omega(L)}\). All other requested complexity parameters are constant.

The same conclusion holds for an expected sample bound. If \(M_0\) is the number of calls under \(\alpha_0\), the coupling gives

\[ 2\delta\ge \mathbb E[(1-\eta)^{M_0}] \ge (1-\eta)^{\mathbb E[M_0]}, \]

where the second inequality is Jensen’s inequality for the convex function \(z\mapsto(1-\eta)^z\). Thus \(\mathbb E[M_0]\) satisfies the same lower bound.

Since each sample has unit cost, this also excludes the proposed polynomial running-time guarantee.

5. Exact quotas give a stronger obstruction for policies fixed after training

Here all supplied parameters can remain constant. Take

\[ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad \rho_1=\rho_2=1,\qquad \Lambda_1=\Lambda_2=0,\qquad \bar x=1,\qquad \epsilon=\frac18,\qquad\delta=\frac14. \]

For \(r\in[1/3,2/3]\), let \(\beta_r\) assign probability \(r\) to \(D=(1,0)\) and probability \(1-r\) to \(E=(0,1)\).

Writing \(a=q(D)\) and \(b=q(E)\), the quota is

\[ ra+(1-r)b=\frac12. \]

The optimum is

\[ W_r^\star=1-\left|r-\frac12\right|. \]

To verify this explicitly, welfare is \(ra+(1-r)(1-b)\). The quota and the bounds \(a\le1\), \(b\ge0\) give the upper bounds \(W\le 1/2+r\) and \(W\le 3/2-r\). Their minimum is attained by

\[ (a,b)= \begin{cases} \left(1,\dfrac{1/2-r}{1-r}\right),&r\le1/2,\\[6pt] \left(\dfrac1{2r},0\right),&r\ge1/2. \end{cases} \]

These policies also satisfy both envy inequalities: \(a\ge1/2\) and \(b\le1/2\).

If one policy satisfies the exact quota for two distinct parameters \(r,r'\), subtracting their quota equalities gives

\[ (r-r')(a-b)=0. \]

Therefore \(a=b=1/2\), and its welfare is \(1/2\). But throughout the parameter interval, \(W_r^\star\ge5/6\). Thus successful-policy sets for any two distinct parameters are disjoint.

Now suppose there were a uniform finite sample bound \(N\). Couple samples from \(\beta_r,\beta_{r'}\) so each pair differs with probability \(h=|r-r'|\). Their first \(N\) samples coincide with probability \((1-h)^N\). The preceding argument again requires

\[ (1-h)^N\le 2\delta=\frac12. \]

For every finite \(N\), one can choose distinct dyadic rational \(r,r'\) sufficiently close that this inequality fails. Their item probabilities are unknown oracle-law parameters, not part of the supplied bit length \(L\).

Therefore no uniform finite sample bound in the listed parameters exists for an output policy fixed after training.

6. Allowing additional samples during policy evaluation does not restore polynomial complexity

The exponential family in Sections 1–4 also handles an interpretation in which the returned policy program retains the sampling oracle, provided evaluation costs are counted.

Suppose evaluation on an item uses at most \(K\) additional samples. For a fixed returned program \(H\), let \(q_i^H(x)\) denote its probability of allocating to type \(1\) when its evaluation oracle has law \(\alpha_i\).

Coupling the evaluation calls as before gives

\[ |q_1^H(x)-q_0^H(x)|\le K\eta. \]

If \(H\) is feasible under \(\alpha_0\), Section 2 implies \(q_0^H(A)=q_0^H(B)=1/2\). Its welfare under \(\alpha_1\) is therefore at most

\[ (1-\eta)\left(\frac58+\frac{\eta}{4}\right) +\frac{(1-\eta)^2K\eta}{2} \le (1-\eta)\left(\frac58+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)+\frac{K\eta}{2}. \]

If \(K\eta\le1/8\), this additional term is at most \(1/16\). The welfare gap proved in Section 3 then remains greater than \(\epsilon=1/8\). Thus a program successful under \(\alpha_0\) cannot also succeed under \(\alpha_1\), and the training lower bound applies.

If \(K\eta>1/8\), evaluation itself requires \(K>1/(8\eta)\) samples. Hence either training or evaluation has exponential sample complexity.

7. Relation to the cited structural theorem

The cited paper’s Theorem 1 describes optimal policies using population-dependent dual parameters under its continuity hypothesis. Its Section 6 and Theorem 2 concern approximation of the population dual objective, under a bounded-coefficient assumption. These conclusions do not supply exact population quotas for a policy computed from finite samples. [Yin–Kroer, Theorems 1–2](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/a488aa1a0c00d76db8a922ef7815a786-Paper-Conference.pdf)

The lower bound above is independent of those structural results. Its atomic item laws are permitted by the question’s arbitrary-measure hypothesis, and both instances have explicitly verified feasible policies.
