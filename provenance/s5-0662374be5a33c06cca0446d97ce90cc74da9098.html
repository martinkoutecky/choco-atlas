The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 5.9 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite report domain \(D\), a population support \(T\subseteq D\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and sample access to \(Z_{tu}=v(t,O)\) for \(O\sim\mathcal A(u)\), where \(u\in T\), can one compute and sample lotteries \(q_t\in\Delta(T)\) for every report \(t\in D\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t(u)=\mu_u\) for each \(u\), the lotteries form a truthful common-price menu, and population welfare is within \(\epsilon\) per unit mass of the best balanced coupling, in expected time and sample complexity polynomial in \(|D|,\ |T|,\ 1/\epsilon\), and the input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(D\) be an explicitly listed finite report domain, with \(m=|D|\). A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|T|\) types \(T\subseteq D\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the explicit input bit length and \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\) a rational tolerance.
For every \(t\in D,u\in T\), an oracle supplies independent samples of \(Z_{tu}=v(t,O)\in[0,1]\), where \(O\sim\mathcal A(u)\); write \(w_{tu}=\mathbb E[Z_{tu}]\). Expectations are unknown; oracle sampling and value operations have unit cost.
An allocation consists of lotteries \(q_t\in\Delta(T)\), represented by exact sampling procedures, with probabilities taken over all their internal and oracle randomness. Balance means \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t(u)=\mu_u\) for every \(u\in T\).
A truthful common-price menu means a single function \(c:Q\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\), where \(Q=\{q_s:s\in D\}\), such that \(q_t\in\arg\max_{q\in Q}\{\sum_{u\in T}w_{tu}q(u)-c(q)\}\) for every \(t\in D\). Agents have risk-neutral quasi-linear utility; any maximizing tie is allowed. Prices are expected payments, and their existence is required.
Define \(W(q)=\sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_tw_{tu}q_t(u)\) and \(W^*=\max\{\sum_{t,u\in T}w_{tu}x_{tu}:x\geq0,\ \sum_u x_{tu}=\mu_t,\ \sum_t x_{tu}=\mu_u\}\).

Is constructing balanced, truthful common-price lotteries satisfying \(W(q)\geq W^*-\epsilon\) possible with expected construction time, time per sample, and oracle sample complexity polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite replica–surrogate markets, a polynomial-time sampling algorithm achieves additive \(\epsilon\) welfare loss per replica; its associated surrogate-selection mechanism is exactly truthful and stationary ([Dughmi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04143)).
Finite-support fractional-assignment reductions also obtain approximate Bayesian truthfulness with small welfare loss ([Bei and Huang](https://arxiv.org/abs/1005.4244)); these results do not directly establish the exact mass-coupling guarantee above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the valuation domain [0,1], independent sample access, and risk-neutral quasi-linear utilities.
  • Interpreted common-price menu as a report-independent price function on offered lotteries, allowing nonlinear lottery prices as in maximal-in-range truthfulness.
  • Required nonnegative expected menu prices to exist; did not add a requirement to explicitly compute or sample payments.
  • Interpreted computing lotteries as constructing exact samplers, with balance and truthfulness imposed on unconditional allocation probabilities.
  • Defined the benchmark as the maximum welfare coupling with both marginals equal to the population masses.
  • Set m to the report-domain size, defined L as the explicit input bit length, and restricted the rational tolerance to (0,1).
  • Used the source's unit-cost sample and value-operation model for oracle data; imposed no lower bound on positive population masses.
  • Allowed arbitrary maximizing ties and imposed truthfulness on every report in D, including reports outside the population support.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The construction is possible with polynomial expected time and oracle use. The key is to randomize the finite market afresh whenever a lottery is sampled. Exact balance then follows from saturation of the market; an empirical transportation bound gives welfare close to the optimal mass coupling.

Prices need only exist, as stipulated in the question. Their numerical computation is unnecessary.

1. The finite-market primitive

Use the finite replica–surrogate algorithm of Dughmi et al., with market size denoted here by \(n\). For accuracy \(\eta>0\), it chooses an integer \(k=\operatorname{poly}(n,1/\eta)\), matches all \(N=kn\) replicas, and uses each surrogate exactly \(k\) times. Its expected welfare satisfies

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\operatorname{ALG}]\geq\mathbb E[\operatorname{OPT}]-\eta N.\)

Its expected running time and oracle use are polynomial in \(n,1/\eta\). The algorithm processes replicas sequentially in a random order. At a replica’s arrival, its conditional selection probabilities are

\(\displaystyle r_t^H(j)=\frac{\exp((w_{tS_j}-a_j)/\delta)}{\sum_{\ell\in J}\exp((w_{tS_\ell}-a_\ell)/\delta)},\qquad j\in J,\)

where \(H\) is the pre-arrival state, \(J\) the available surrogates, and \(a_j,\delta>0\) report-independent parameters. Preprocessing uses independent auxiliary replicas. These are the guarantees and structure of Algorithm 4, its preprocessing, and Theorems 5.8–5.9. [Dughmi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04143)

The hypotheses hold here: every edge has independent samples supported in \([0,1]\), obtained by querying \(Z_{R_iS_j}\). Sampling replica and surrogate types from the rational distribution \(\mu\) is also available.

2. Construction of the lotteries

Set

\(\displaystyle \eta=\epsilon/2,\qquad n=\max\left\{2,\left\lceil\frac{4\tau}{\epsilon^2}\right\rceil\right\},\qquad N=kn.\)

For each report \(t\in D\), define \(q_t\) by this exact sampling procedure:

  • Choose \(I\) uniformly from \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\).
  • Set \(R_I=t\), and independently draw the other \(N-1\) replica types from \(\mu\).
  • Independently draw surrogate types \(S_1,\ldots,S_n\) from \(\mu\).
  • Run the finite-market primitive, including its independent preprocessing.
  • Output the type of the surrogate matched to replica \(I\).

The matching algorithm receives the replica and surrogate profiles, but does not receive the designation \(I\). All randomness, including preprocessing and oracle samples, is generated afresh on each invocation.

This procedure defines a lottery in \(\Delta(T)\), including when \(t\notin T\).

3. Exact balance

Draw the designated report itself from \(\mu\). The resulting joint experiment has an equivalent description:

  • draw all \(N\) replica types independently from \(\mu\);
  • independently choose a uniform designated index \(I\);
  • generate the surrogates and matching without using \(I\).

Thus, conditional on the complete matching, \(I\) remains uniform. Each surrogate has exactly \(k\) matched replicas, so its conditional probability of selection is \(k/N=1/n\).

Consequently, for every \(u\in T\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t(u) =\mathbb E\left[\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf 1_{\{S_j=u\}}\right] =\mu_u. \)

This is exact balance for the original masses. No approximation or rounding of those masses occurs.

4. Exact truthfulness and a common price function

We prove truthfulness on all of \(D\), including unsupported reports.

Fix the designated replica and consider the state \(H\) immediately before its assignment. Its distribution is independent of the designated report: preprocessing uses independent auxiliary replicas, and earlier assignments depend only on the other replicas. Write \(S_j\) for the surrogate type at position \(j\), and \(J\) for the nonempty available set.

For a vector \(z\in[0,1]^T\), define

\(\displaystyle F_H(z)=\delta\log\left(\sum_{j\in J}\exp((z_{S_j}-a_j)/\delta)\right).\)

This is convex. Its gradient, aggregated by surrogate type, is precisely the conditional selection lottery at value vector \(z\). Put \(w_t=(w_{tu})_{u\in T}\). Convexity gives, for every \(t,s\in D\),

\(\displaystyle F_H(w_t)-F_H(w_s) \geq \sum_{j\in J}r_s^H(j)(w_{tS_j}-w_{sS_j}). \)

Define the finite quantities

\(\displaystyle f_t=\mathbb E_H[F_H(w_t)-F_H(0)].\)

Indeed, because every coordinate of \(w_t\) lies in \([0,1]\),

\(\displaystyle 0\leq F_H(w_t)-F_H(0)\leq1.\)

Averaging the convexity inequality, and using the definition of the sampling procedures, yields

\(\displaystyle f_t-f_s\geq(w_t-w_s)\cdot q_s.\)

Now define a report payment

\(\displaystyle p_t=w_t\cdot q_t-f_t.\)

Then

\(\displaystyle w_t\cdot q_t-p_t =f_t \geq w_t\cdot q_s-p_s \qquad(t,s\in D). \)

Thus all reports satisfy exact incentive compatibility.

It remains to make prices functions of lotteries rather than reports. If \(q_t=q_s\), the incentive inequalities in both directions imply \(p_t=p_s\). Hence the payment is constant on every class of reports producing the same lottery.

Since \(D\) is finite, let \(b=\min_{s\in D}p_s\), and define

\(\displaystyle c(q_t)=p_t-b.\)

This defines a single function \(c:Q\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\). Subtracting the same constant from every payment preserves all incentive inequalities. Therefore

\(\displaystyle q_t\in\arg\max_{q\in Q}\{w_t\cdot q-c(q)\} \qquad(t\in D). \)

This proves the required truthful common-price menu.

5. Comparing empirical markets with the mass optimum

For probability vectors \(a,b\) on \(T\), define

\(\displaystyle \Phi(a,b)= \max\left\{ \sum_{t,u\in T}w_{tu}x_{tu}: x\geq0,\ \sum_u x_{tu}=a_t,\ \sum_t x_{tu}=b_u \right\}. \)

In particular, \(W^*=\Phi(\mu,\mu)\). Define total variation by

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{TV}(a,b)=\frac12\sum_{t\in T}|a_t-b_t|.\)

Lemma: stability under changes in the marginals

For any probability vectors \(a,b\),

\(\displaystyle \Phi(a,b)\geq W^* -\operatorname{TV}(a,\mu) -\operatorname{TV}(b,\mu). \)

Proof. Start with an optimal coupling for \((\mu,\mu)\).

First, scale down every row whose mass exceeds \(a_t\). The total removed mass is

\(\displaystyle \sum_t(\mu_t-a_t)_+=\operatorname{TV}(a,\mu).\)

The remaining column masses are at most \(\mu_u\). Next, scale down every column whose remaining mass exceeds \(b_u\). This removes at most

\(\displaystyle \sum_u(\mu_u-b_u)_+=\operatorname{TV}(b,\mu).\)

The remaining matrix has row masses at most \(a\) and column masses at most \(b\). Its row and column deficits have the same total mass, say \(d\). If \(d>0\), fill the deficits by adding the outer product of the deficit vectors divided by \(d\); if \(d=0\), nothing needs filling.

Removing mass \(h\) loses at most \(h\) welfare because \(w_{tu}\leq1\). Filling deficits cannot decrease welfare because \(w_{tu}\geq0\). The resulting feasible coupling proves the inequality. \(\square\)

Empirical marginals

When the designated report is drawn from \(\mu\), define

\(\displaystyle \widehat a_t=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf1_{\{R_i=t\}}, \qquad \widehat b_u=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{S_j=u\}}. \)

For each realized market,

\(\displaystyle \frac{\operatorname{OPT}}N=\Phi(\widehat a,\widehat b).\)

To justify this equality, aggregate a matching by replica and surrogate types. Conversely, multiply a feasible transportation matrix by \(N\). Its margins are integer replica counts and integer surrogate-slot counts, the latter being \(k\) times the surrogate counts.

A transportation polytope with integer margins has an integral optimum. One direct justification is that an extreme point’s positive-support graph cannot contain a cycle: alternating perturbations around a cycle would contradict extremality. Its support is therefore a forest, and successively removing leaves shows that every entry is integral. Such an integral type-level assignment can be distributed among individual replicas and surrogate slots. This proves the equality.

For an empirical distribution \(\widehat\mu\) from \(h\) independent samples,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\operatorname{TV}(\widehat\mu,\mu)] \leq \frac12\sum_t\sqrt{\frac{\mu_t(1-\mu_t)}h} \leq\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{h}}. \)

The first inequality follows from the binomial variance and the bound \(\mathbb E|X-\mathbb EX|\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\). The second follows from Cauchy–Schwarz and \(\sum_t\mu_t(1-\mu_t)\leq1\).

Applying the lemma and using \(N\geq n\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E\left[\frac{\operatorname{OPT}}N\right] \geq W^*-\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{N}} -\frac12\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}} \geq W^*-\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{n}} \geq W^*-\epsilon/2. \)

6. Welfare and computational bounds

By the uniform-designation argument from Section 3, the designated replica’s expected welfare equals the matching’s expected welfare per replica. Therefore

\(\displaystyle W(q)=\mathbb E\left[\frac{\operatorname{ALG}}N\right] \geq \mathbb E\left[\frac{\operatorname{OPT}}N\right]-\eta \geq W^*-\epsilon. \)

This inequality concerns the lotteries’ full distributions; it is not a high-probability guarantee conditional on some estimated weight matrix.

Exact sampling from \(\mu\) takes expected polynomial time in \(L,\tau\). For example, take the product of its rational denominators as a common denominator. Its bit length is at most the sum of the input denominator bit lengths. Rejection sampling a uniform integer below that denominator takes fewer than two trials in expectation; integer intervals then implement the masses exactly.

Here \(n\), \(k\), and \(N\) are polynomial in \(\tau,1/\epsilon\). The finite-market primitive consequently has polynomial expected sampling time and oracle use, including for unsupported reports, whose edge samples still lie in \([0,1]\).

Construction consists of emitting \(m\) sampling procedures sharing the input data and parameters, so its time and representation size are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). Each lottery invocation has polynomial expected time and expected oracle sample complexity. As with the cited exact-sampling primitive, these are expected bounds; stopping times need not be deterministically bounded.

No bound depends on the reciprocal of the smallest supported mass.
