The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational polygonal jurisdiction \(\Omega\), a finite rational polygonal subdivision with nonnegative rational party densities \(\rho_A,\rho_B\) normalized so their total integral is \(1\), and integers \(d,q,r\), is there a partition of \(\Omega\) into \(d\) connected polygonal districts, each with at most \(r\) edges and total population \(1/d\), such that at least \(q\) districts have \(\int_{R_j}\rho_A > 1/(2d)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: a bounded rational polygonal jurisdiction \(\Omega\) with connected interior, a rational polygonal subdivision into positive-area cells, and binary integers \(d\geq1\), \(0\leq q\leq d\), \(r\geq3\). Holes are allowed.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here a type \((C,I)\) specifies a subdivision cell and party \(I\in\{A,B\}\); its mass is uniformly distributed over \(C\). Omitted types have zero mass, and all masses sum to \(1\). Thus the party densities \(\rho_A,\rho_B\) are nonnegative rational constants on each cell; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
A district is a closed polygonal region with connected interior, arbitrary real vertices, and at most \(r\) maximal straight boundary edges, counting all hole boundaries. Districts may cut subdivision cells; their interiors must be disjoint and their union must be \(\Omega\).
An admissible partition has \(d\) districts satisfying \(\int_{R_j}(\rho_A+\rho_B)=1/d\). Promise that at least one admissible partition exists. Party \(A\) wins precisely when \(\int_{R_j}\rho_A>1/(2d)\); ties do not count as wins.
Let \(m\) be the total number of vertices in the input geometry and \(L\) the total binary input length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L,d,r\), distinguishing dependence on numeric parameters from their bit lengths.

Is deciding whether an admissible partition gives party \(A\) at least \(q\) wins in \(\mathrm{P}\) with respect to the total binary input length, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite voters, this decision problem is NP-complete when allowable districts are explicitly listed equal-sized voter sets of odd size at least five, promised to admit a partition ([Puppe–Tasnádi](https://unipub.lib.uni-corvinus.hu/275/1/Puppe_Tasnadi_EL_2009_v105_p93.pdf)).
A continuous density model on an interval has an existence theorem for simultaneous partisan fairness, but concerns a different feasible family and objective ([Benadè–Procaccia–Tucker-Foltz](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2023.0443)). No result settling the exact polygonal decision problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted rational party densities as constant on each subdivision cell and represented the society by positive cell–party masses, uniformly distributed within each cell.
  • Specified a bounded jurisdiction with connected interior and positive-area subdivision cells; allowed holes in both the jurisdiction and districts.
  • Required connected district interiors to reflect adjacency through shared edges, excluding connectivity solely through point contacts or zero-width appendages.
  • Allowed arbitrary real district vertices and cuts through subdivision cells; counted maximal straight edges on every boundary component.
  • Interpreted partition as exact coverage with disjoint interiors, permitting shared boundaries of zero population mass.
  • Carried over the guaranteed existence of an admissible districting as an input promise.
  • Set the integer domains and binary encoding explicitly; retained strict majority and treated ties as no win for party A.
  • Defined the geometry size and total input bit length, including the binary integer parameters.

1. Definitions and triangular districts

Write
$$ M(S)=\int_S(\rho_A+\rho_B),\qquad a(S)=\int_S\rho_A. $$
A district is admissible when $M(S)=1/d$, and it is won by $A$ when
$$ a(S)>\frac{1}{2d}. $$

I use the usual convention that a polygonal region is the closure of its interior. All input and district boundaries have planar area zero, so boundary overlaps do not affect either integral.

The densities are bounded: they are constants on finitely many positive-area cells. Consequently, a district of mass $1/d>0$ has positive area.

A positive-area polygonal region with connected interior and at most three maximal boundary edges is a triangle. Indeed, its outer polygonal boundary needs at least three edges; a hole would require at least three additional edges. With only three edges available, the outer boundary is a nondegenerate three-edge cycle and there are no holes.

Thus, when $r=3$, every district is a nondegenerate closed triangle.

2. An angle identity that includes T-junctions

Let $P=\Omega$ be a convex polygon with extreme vertices
$$ v_1,\ldots,v_n $$
in counterclockwise order. Consider any partition of $P$ into $d$ nondegenerate triangles with disjoint interiors.

Let $V$ be the set of all triangle vertices. Every $v_i$ belongs to $V$: because $v_i$ is an extreme point of $P$, it cannot lie in a triangle contained in $P$ without being a vertex of that triangle.

Classify the additional vertices in $V\setminus\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ as follows:

  • $b$ vertices lie on $\partial P$.
  • $t$ vertices lie in $\operatorname{int}P$ and in the relative interior of an edge of some triangle.
  • $u$ vertices lie in $\operatorname{int}P$ and do not lie in the relative interior of any triangle edge.

Lemma.
$$ d=n-2+b+t+2u. $$

Proof. Group the angles of all triangles according to their vertex locations.

At an original corner $v_i$, every triangle containing $v_i$ has it as a vertex. In a sufficiently small neighborhood, the triangle sectors cover the polygon sector and have disjoint interiors. Their angles therefore sum to the interior angle of $P$ at $v_i$. Summed over the original corners, the contribution is
$$ (n-2)\pi. $$

Now let $z\in V\setminus\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ lie on $\partial P$. Locally, $P$ is a halfplane. No triangle can contain $z$ in the relative interior of its edge: such a triangle would occupy the entire local halfplane, leaving no positive-angle sector for a triangle having $z$ as a vertex. All incident triangles consequently have a corner at $z$, and their corner angles sum to $\pi$.

Finally, let $z\in V\cap\operatorname{int}P$.

No triangle can contain $z$ in its interior, because another triangle has a corner at $z$, which would force interior overlap.

There can be at most one triangle containing $z$ in the relative interior of an edge. To see this, two such triangles would occupy local open halfplanes. Disjointness forces those halfplanes to be opposite halfplanes with the same boundary line. Together they would occupy all local directions except that line, again leaving no positive-angle sector for a triangle corner at $z$.

If exactly one triangle contains $z$ in the relative interior of an edge, that triangle occupies angle $\pi$. The angles of the triangles having corners at $z$ sum to the remaining $\pi$.

Otherwise, every incident triangle has a corner at $z$, and their angles sum to $2\pi$.

Each triangle contributes total corner angle $\pi$. Hence
$$ d\pi=(n-2)\pi+b\pi+t\pi+2u\pi. $$
Dividing by $\pi$ proves the identity. $\square$

In particular,
$$ d\geq n-2, $$
and the total number of additional triangle vertices satisfies
$$ b+t+u\leq d-n+2. $$

This argument does not assume that the partition is a conforming triangulation.

3. Exact integration over candidate triangles

Both algorithms below need exact values of $M(T)$ and $a(T)$ for candidate triangles, sometimes with one moving vertex.

First, refine the input subdivision into $O(m^2)$ rational convex polygons having at most four sides, on each of which both densities are constant.

Here is an explicit construction. Draw vertical lines through every input vertex. In each open slab between consecutive vertical lines, the input boundary segments have a fixed vertical order: a change of order would require an intersection inside the slab, contrary to validity of the subdivision. Consecutive boundary segments enclose trapezoids or triangles. Retain those lying in $\Omega$ and label each by its subdivision cell.

There are $O(m)$ slabs and $O(m)$ retained pieces per slab. Their vertices are rational intersections of input supporting lines with rational vertical lines, so their coordinate bit lengths are polynomial in $L$. Labels can be determined using rational interior sample points and exact point-in-polygon tests. A straightforward implementation takes
$$ (m^3+\tau)(L+1)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations.

For a supported type of mass $p_{C,I}$, its density is
$$ \frac{p_{C,I}}{\operatorname{area}(C)}. $$
Cell areas are positive rationals computed by signed shoelace sums, including hole boundaries. These densities have polynomial bit length. Omitted types receive density zero.

Denote the retained convex pieces by $K_\ell$, with constant densities $\mu_\ell$ and $\gamma_\ell$ for total population and party $A$, respectively. Then
$$ M(T)=\sum_\ell\mu_\ell\operatorname{area}(T\cap K_\ell), \qquad a(T)=\sum_\ell\gamma_\ell\operatorname{area}(T\cap K_\ell). $$

For a rational triangle $T$, each intersection $T\cap K_\ell$ is the intersection of at most seven halfplanes. Its vertices and area are computable with a constant number of exact rational operations. Therefore, computing both masses of one rational candidate triangle takes
$$ m^2(L+1)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations.

This construction uses the supported types directly; it does not introduce fictitious positive-mass types for omitted cell-party pairs.

4. A triangulation dynamic program

The following dynamic program applies to a convex polygon whose boundary vertices are given in cyclic order. A collinear boundary vertex is allowed, but all triangles must have positive area and every listed boundary vertex must be used.

Let the ordered vertices be $w_1,\ldots,w_h$. For $i<j$, let
$$ P_{ij}=\operatorname{conv}\{w_i,\ldots,w_j\}. $$

For every positive-area candidate triangle $T$, define
$$ \operatorname{legal}(T)\iff M(T)=\frac1d, $$
and
$$ \operatorname{win}(T)= \begin{cases} 1,&a(T)>1/(2d),\\ 0,&a(T)\leq1/(2d). \end{cases} $$

Let $F(i,j)$ be the maximum number of wins in a triangulation of $P_{ij}$ using all its listed vertices and only legal triangles. Use $-\infty$ for infeasibility.

Set
$$ F(i,i+1)=0. $$
For $j\geq i+2$, use
$$ F(i,j)= \max_{\substack{i<k<j\\ T_{ikj}\text{ has positive area}\\ \operatorname{legal}(T_{ikj})}} \left( F(i,k)+F(k,j)+\operatorname{win}(T_{ikj}) \right), $$
where
$$ T_{ikj}=\operatorname{conv}\{w_i,w_k,w_j\}. $$
A maximum over an empty set is $-\infty$.

Correctness. In any conforming triangulation, the closing edge $w_iw_j$ is incident to one triangle $T_{ikj}$. Removing that triangle leaves the two subpolygons $P_{ik}$ and $P_{kj}$. Conversely, triangulations of those subpolygons, together with $T_{ikj}$, partition $P_{ij}$ by convexity.

The positive-area requirement handles collinear boundary vertices. An interval consisting of at least three collinear listed vertices is infeasible, so the recurrence cannot discard a collinear vertex through a degenerate triangle. Induction on $j-i$ therefore proves both completeness and soundness.

The table has $O(h^2)$ entries and $O(h^3)$ transitions.

6.1. The real-algebraic fact used

For integer univariate polynomials of maximum degree $\Delta$ and coefficient bit length $B$, feasibility of a conjunction of specified polynomial signs is decidable in
$$ (s\Delta+1)^{O(1)}(B+1)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations. This is the one-variable specialization of the one-block quantifier-elimination bound, including its integer bit-size bound, in [Basu’s survey, Theorem 2.27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.1534).

All applications below have one real variable, integer coefficients after positive denominator clearing, and polynomially bounded degrees and coefficient sizes.

There are at most $s\Delta$ distinct real roots among the nonzero polynomials. Their roots and intervening intervals give at most $2s\Delta+1$ sign patterns. Thus all realizable sign patterns can also be enumerated in polynomial time: extend feasible sign prefixes by each of the three signs, testing feasibility at each extension. At every depth, distinct feasible prefixes have disjoint full extensions, so their number is bounded by the number of full realizable patterns.

Include the restrictions $\lambda>0$ and $1-\lambda>0$ in every feasibility test.

6.2. Fixing the intersection geometry

For each candidate triangle $T(\lambda)$ and each fixed piece $K_\ell$, consider the vertices of
$$ T(\lambda)\cap K_\ell. $$

Every such vertex is either a vertex of one of the two polygons or an intersection of their supporting lines. There are at most nineteen candidates: seven polygon vertices and twelve pairs of supporting lines.

Represent candidates homogeneously as
$$ H=(X,Y,Z). $$
Their coordinates are polynomials in $\lambda$ of degree at most one. Indeed, a moving triangle edge has one fixed endpoint and the affine endpoint $z(\lambda)$, so its supporting-line coefficients are affine in $\lambda$; the lines of $K_\ell$ are fixed.

Include the following polynomial signs:

  • $Z$, to identify finite candidates;
  • evaluations of every relevant supporting line at every candidate;
  • coordinate-comparison numerators

$$ X_aZ_b-X_bZ_a,\qquad Y_aZ_b-Y_bZ_a; $$

  • orientation determinants

$$ \det(H_a,H_b,H_c); $$

  • each candidate triangle’s orientation determinant.

These polynomials have degree at most three. Their number, over all triangle-piece pairs, is
$$ O(h^3m^2). $$

On a realizable sign pattern, these signs determine which finite candidates satisfy both polygons’ halfplane inequalities, which candidates coincide, and their coordinate and orientation comparisons. A convex-hull computation therefore has the same result throughout the pattern. This determines the cyclic list of intersection vertices, or determines that the intersection has zero area.

Parallel or coincident supporting lines cause no omission: endpoints of a collinear overlap are already among the polygon-vertex candidates. Zero signs are retained.

6.3. Masses and their signs

Fix one realizable geometric sign pattern. Discard degenerate candidate triangles.

For every remaining candidate triangle, the shoelace formula gives each intersection area as a sum of terms
$$ \frac{X_aY_b-Y_aX_b}{2Z_aZ_b}. $$
The denominators of the selected finite vertices are nonzero throughout the pattern.

For a candidate triangle $T$, let $D_T$ be the product of these denominators over all its intersections with the pieces $K_\ell$. There are $O(m^2)$ factors. Multiplication by $D_T^2$ clears all variable denominators, and
$$ D_T^2>0 $$
throughout the pattern.

Consequently, rational polynomials $P_T,Q_T$ satisfy
$$ M(T(\lambda))=\frac{P_T(\lambda)}{D_T(\lambda)^2}, \qquad a(T(\lambda))=\frac{Q_T(\lambda)}{D_T(\lambda)^2}. $$
Their degrees are $O(m^2)$ and their coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in $m$ and $L$.

These polynomials can be expanded in polynomial time: they are univariate and have polynomial degree. Products of polynomially many rational factors increase coefficient bit lengths only polynomially.

Admissibility is equivalent to
$$ E_T(\lambda):=dP_T(\lambda)-D_T(\lambda)^2=0. $$
Victory is equivalent to
$$ V_T(\lambda):=2dQ_T(\lambda)-D_T(\lambda)^2>0. $$

Enumerate the realizable refinements obtained by specifying the signs of all $E_T,V_T$, while retaining the geometric sign pattern. The univariate sign-enumeration argument gives polynomially many refinements and polynomial total running time.

On each refinement, all three statuses in the lemma are fixed. Equalities are tested exactly, and $V_T=0$ is nonwinning. This proves the lemma. $\square$

7.2. One interior T-junction

Here
$$ b=0,\qquad t=1,\qquad u=0. $$

Write that triangle as
$$ T=\operatorname{conv}\{p,q,a\}, $$
with $z$ on the edge $pq$. All three vertices $p,q,a$ are original corners, since $z$ is the only additional vertex. Because $z$ is interior to $P$, $pq$ is an original-vertex diagonal.

The triangle $T$ separates $P$ into three possibly degenerate convex subpolygons. Each uses the boundary arc between two of $p,q,a$ that does not contain the third vertex.

The subpolygon opposite $T$ across $pq$ has $z$ inserted on its closing edge. Its remaining districts form a conforming triangulation using this augmented boundary list. The other two subpolygons have conforming triangulations using only original corners.

Here is why no further district can cross between these subpolygons. At every point in the relative interior of an edge of $T$, the side toward $T$ is locally occupied by its interior. A different triangle crossing that edge would overlap $T$ in its interior. The original corner endpoints cannot be crossed inside $P$ either. Thus every other triangle lies in one of the three subpolygons. Within those subpolygons, there is no additional interior T-junction, and a boundary edge cannot pass straight through the listed corner $z$.

Enumerate the $O(n^3)$ choices of the original-corner triangle $T$ and its distinguished edge $pq$. For each choice:

  • Require $M(T)=1/d$.
  • Parameterize $z$ on the relative interior of $pq$.
  • Apply Section 6 to the candidate triangles.
  • Run the dynamic program on the three subpolygons, inserting $z$ in the one opposite $T$ across $pq$.
  • Add their optimum scores and $\operatorname{win}(T)$.

A two-vertex degenerate subpolygon contributes zero districts and zero wins.

To check the district count, let the three original boundary arcs have respectively $s_1,s_2,s_3$ edges, with the first opposite the distinguished edge. Then
$$ s_1+s_2+s_3=n. $$
The augmented first subpolygon has $s_1+2$ listed vertices and hence $s_1$ triangles. The other two have $s_2-1$ and $s_3-1$ triangles. Including $T$, the total is
$$ s_1+(s_2-1)+(s_3-1)+1=n-1=d. $$

Accept if any realizable class gives total score at least $q$.
