For finitely many buyers, proportional seller-budget dynamics has competitive equilibria as fixed points, converges to equilibrium, and has an ergodic Eisenberg–Gale objective gap of \(O(T^{-1})\). [Li–Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11872v1)

Proven results and remaining classification

The prescribed process converges in the sense requested, and
$$ T=\left\lceil\frac{2L}{\epsilon}\right\rceil $$
exact rounds suffice for the objective guarantee.

There is also a stronger computational obstruction than the one in NOTES: the exact round average itself can require exponentially many output bits, even with four types and two singleton sellers. Thus, under the standard representation of the output by explicit binary rationals, obtaining the exact prescribed average is not polynomial-time in the stated parameters. This conclusion applies to arbitrary algorithms, rather than only to algorithms explicitly storing every split.

This is an output-size lower bound, not an NP-hardness proof. Consequently, the proposed polynomial-time-versus-NP-hard dichotomy is not established.

For the numerical interpretation, I additionally prove that a polynomial-bit rounded implementation produces an exactly feasible allocation whose average utilities approximate those of the prescribed trajectory and whose objective gap is at most $\epsilon$. Its output need not equal an exact prescribed round average.

All logarithms below are natural unless explicitly written as $\log_2$.

1. Reduction using the support, without expanding the population

Define
$$ w_t=\frac{\mu_tb_t}{B},\qquad q_{tk}^r=\frac{\mu_t\beta_{tk}^r}{B},\qquad y_{tj}^r=\frac{\mu_tx_{tj}^r}{s_j},\qquad a_{tj}=\frac{s_jv_{tj}}{\mu_t}. $$
Then
$$ \sum_t w_t=1,\qquad \sum_kq_{tk}^r=w_t,\qquad \sum_ty_{tj}^r=1. $$
The utility transformation is exact:
$$ U_{tk}(y):=\sum_{j\in J_k}a_{tj}y_{tj} =\sum_{j\in J_k}v_{tj}x_{tj}=u_{tk}. $$
Write $U_t=\sum_kU_{tk}$. The objective becomes
$$ F(y):=\sum_tw_t\log U_t(y)=G(x). $$

For prices and multipliers, define
$$ \pi_j^r=\frac{s_jp_j^r}{B}, \qquad A_{tk}^r=\frac{\mu_t\alpha_{tk}^r}{B}. $$
For $j\in J_k$, the seller conditions become
$$ \pi_j^r=\max_tA_{tk}^ra_{tj},\qquad y_{tj}^r>0\Longrightarrow A_{tk}^ra_{tj}=\pi_j^r, $$
and
$$ \sum_{j\in J_k}\pi_j^ry_{tj}^r=q_{tk}^r. $$
These are precisely the equilibrium conditions of a unit-supply linear Fisher market with seller-specific budgets $q_{tk}^r$. Summing payments gives
$$ \sum_j\pi_j^r=\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^r=1. $$

Every item has positive price. Every type has positive spending at every seller, so its seller utility is positive. The tight-edge condition therefore gives
$$ A_{tk}^r=\frac{q_{tk}^r}{U_{tk}^r}, \qquad q_{tk}^{r+1}=w_t\frac{U_{tk}^r}{U_t^r}. $$
In particular, all finite-round splits remain positive.

The transformed market has exactly $\tau$ buyers. Its rational input length is polynomial in the original input length. The finite-market convergence results of [Li–Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11872v1) are therefore relevant, but the required convergence and rate statements are proved below.

2. Equilibrium characterization

Let
$$ \mathcal Y=\left\{y\geq0:\sum_ty_{tj}=1\text{ for every }j\right\}. $$
This set is compact and convex. The allocation $y_{tj}=1/\tau$ gives every type positive utility. The extended objective $F$, with $\log0=-\infty$, is upper semicontinuous and bounded above, so it attains its maximum. Every maximizer has positive total utilities.

The maximizing total utility vector $U^*$ is unique. Indeed, the feasible utility vectors form a convex set, and
$$ U\longmapsto\sum_tw_t\log U_t $$
is strictly concave on positive utility vectors.

Fix a maximizing allocation $y^*$. Define
$$ \lambda_t^*=\frac{w_t}{U_t^*}, \qquad \pi_j^*=\max_t\lambda_t^*a_{tj}. $$
For every feasible $z$, the directional derivative at $y^*$ gives
$$ \sum_{t,j}\lambda_t^*a_{tj}(z_{tj}-y_{tj}^*)\leq0. $$
Hence $y^*$ maximizes this linear functional over $\mathcal Y$. Since the allocation of each item can be varied independently,
$$ y_{tj}^*>0\Longrightarrow\lambda_t^*a_{tj}=\pi_j^*. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_j\pi_j^*y_{tj}^* =\lambda_t^*U_t^*=w_t, \qquad \sum_j\pi_j^*=1. $$
For any bundle $z_t$ costing at most $w_t$,
$$ \lambda_t^*\sum_ja_{tj}z_{tj} \leq\sum_j\pi_j^*z_{tj}\leq w_t. $$
Thus $y_t^*$ is utility maximizing at prices $\pi^*$.

Conversely, consider an arbitrary Fisher equilibrium $(\widetilde y,\widetilde\pi)$, and write its utilities as $\widetilde U_t$. Every price is positive: a zero-priced item valued positively by some buyer would make that buyer's utility-maximization problem unbounded. Every buyer spends its budget because it values some item positively.

Set
$$ \widetilde\lambda_t=\frac{w_t}{\widetilde U_t}. $$
Individual optimality and full spending imply
$$ \widetilde\lambda_ta_{tj}\leq\widetilde\pi_j, \qquad \widetilde y_{tj}>0 \Longrightarrow \widetilde\lambda_ta_{tj}=\widetilde\pi_j. $$
Therefore, for any feasible $y$ with positive utilities,
$$ \begin{aligned} F(y)-F(\widetilde y) &\leq\sum_t\widetilde\lambda_t \bigl(U_t(y)-\widetilde U_t\bigr)\\ &\leq\sum_j\widetilde\pi_j \left(\sum_ty_{tj}-\sum_t\widetilde y_{tj}\right) =0. \end{aligned} $$
An allocation with a zero utility has objective $-\infty$, so the same optimality conclusion holds for all feasible allocations.

Thus equilibrium allocations are exactly the maximizers of $F$. Their total utilities are $U^*$, and their prices are uniquely
$$ \pi_j^*=\max_t\frac{w_t}{U_t^*}a_{tj}. $$

Define the split associated with $y^*$ by
$$ q_{tk}^* =\sum_{j\in J_k}\pi_j^*y_{tj}^* =\lambda_t^*U_{tk}^*. $$
These reference splits may have zero coordinates, and their row sums are $w_t$.

3. A telescoping identity and the exact-round bound

For the fixed equilibrium split $q^*$, define
$$ D_r=\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^*\log\frac{q_{tk}^*}{q_{tk}^r}, $$
interpreting zero-weight terms as zero. Both arrays have total mass one, so $D_r\geq0$.

Set
$$ M_{tk}^r=A_{tk}^rU_{tk}^*, \qquad h_r=\sum_{t,k}M_{tk}^r. $$
The maximum-bid condition implies
$$ h_r =\sum_{t,j}A_{tk(j)}^ra_{tj}y_{tj}^* \leq\sum_j\pi_j^r=1. $$
Moreover, $h_r>0$, and $q_{tk}^*>0$ implies $M_{tk}^r>0$.

Define
$$ H_r=\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^* \log\frac{q_{tk}^*}{M_{tk}^r}. $$
Writing $D(p\|z)=\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/z_i)$ for probability vectors,
$$ H_r=D(q^*\|M^r/h_r)-\log h_r\geq0. $$

Using the update and $q_{tk}^r=A_{tk}^rU_{tk}^r$,
$$ \begin{aligned} D_r-D_{r+1} &=\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^* \log\frac{q_{tk}^{r+1}}{q_{tk}^r}\\ &=\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^* \log\frac{w_t/U_t^r}{A_{tk}^r}\\ &=\sum_tw_t\log\frac{U_t^*}{U_t^r} +\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^* \log\frac{\lambda_t^*}{A_{tk}^r}\\ &=F^*-F(y^r)+H_r. \end{aligned} $$
The last equality uses $q_{tk}^*=\lambda_t^*U_{tk}^*$ on every positive reference coordinate. Therefore
$$ D_r-D_{r+1}\geq F^*-F(y^r)\geq0. $$

Summing over the first $T$ rounds,
$$ \frac1T\sum_{r=0}^{T-1}\bigl(F^*-F(y^r)\bigr) \leq\frac{D_0}{T}. $$
The average allocation $\hat y$ is feasible. By concavity,
$$ G^*-G(\hat x)=F^*-F(\hat y)\leq\frac{D_0}{T}. $$

Since $q_{tk}^*\leq w_t$,
$$ D_0 \leq\sum_{t,k}q_{tk}^*\log\frac{w_t}{q_{tk}^0} \leq\max_{t,k}\log\frac{b_t}{\beta_{tk}^0}. $$
Every positive rational in an input of total length $L$ lies between $2^{-L}$ and $2^L$. Hence
$$ D_0\leq2L\log2<2L. $$
This proves the sufficient round bound
$$ \boxed{ T=\left\lceil\frac{2L}{\epsilon}\right\rceil \Longrightarrow G^*-G(\hat x)\leq\epsilon. } $$
The bound holds for every equilibrium-consistent choice of ties.

4. Convergence of the prescribed process

4.1 Utilities and allocation accumulation points

The telescoping inequality gives
$$ \sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\bigl(F^*-F(y^r)\bigr)<\infty, $$
so $F(y^r)\to F^*$.

Every allocation subsequence has a convergent further subsequence because $\mathcal Y$ is compact. An accumulation point cannot have a zero total utility: that logarithmic term would tend to $-\infty$, while the remaining terms have fixed finite upper bounds.

At a positive-utility accumulation point, $F$ is continuous. Every such point consequently maximizes $F$, and is an equilibrium allocation. Uniqueness of $U^*$ then gives
$$ U_t^r\longrightarrow U_t^* \quad\text{for every }t. $$

4.2 Prices

Since $D_r$ decreases to a finite limit, $D_r-D_{r+1}\to0$. The identity in Section 3 implies $H_r\to0$. Its decomposition therefore yields
$$ h_r\to1,\qquad D(q^*\|M^r/h_r)\to0. $$

We use the probability-vector form of Pinsker's inequality, with natural logarithms:
$$ D(p\|z)\geq\frac12\|p-z\|_1^2. $$
Here both vectors are normalized, and every positive reference coordinate has positive denominator. Thus
$$ M^r/h_r\to q^*, \qquad M^r\to q^*. $$
Whenever $U_{tk}^*>0$,
$$ A_{tk}^r =\frac{M_{tk}^r}{U_{tk}^*} \longrightarrow\lambda_t^*. $$

For each item $j$, choose a type $t$ with $y_{tj}^*>0$. Its reference seller utility is positive, so
$$ \liminf_r\pi_j^r \geq\lim_rA_{tk(j)}^ra_{tj} =\pi_j^*. $$
All current and limiting price vectors sum to one. For any fixed item $j$,
$$ \limsup_r\pi_j^r \leq1-\sum_{\ell\ne j}\liminf_r\pi_\ell^r \leq\pi_j^*. $$
Hence $\pi^r\to\pi^*$.

4.3 Splits

Let
$$ a_{\min}=\min\{a_{tj}:a_{tj}>0\}. $$
Every type values some item of every seller positively. Since $\pi_j^r\leq1$, the maximum-bid condition gives
$$ 0<A_{tk}^r\leq\frac1{a_{\min}}. $$

Take a subsequence along which $(q^r,y^r)\to(\bar q,\bar y)$. Its allocation limit is an equilibrium allocation.

If $\bar U_{tk}=0$, the identity $q_{tk}^r=A_{tk}^rU_{tk}^r$ and the multiplier bound imply $\bar q_{tk}=0$.

If $\bar U_{tk}>0$, some item $j\in J_k$ has $a_{tj}>0$ and $\bar y_{tj}>0$. Eventually $y_{tj}^r>0$ along this subsequence, so
$$ A_{tk}^r=\frac{\pi_j^r}{a_{tj}} \longrightarrow\frac{\pi_j^*}{a_{tj}} =\lambda_t^*. $$
Thus in both cases
$$ \bar q_{tk}=\lambda_t^*\bar U_{tk}. $$
Every split accumulation point is therefore an equilibrium split.

Use $\bar q$ as the reference split in Section 3. The potential $D(\bar q\|q^r)$ is nonincreasing. Along the chosen subsequence it tends to zero, because every positive coordinate of $\bar q$ has a positive limiting denominator. Hence it tends to zero along the entire sequence. Pinsker's inequality gives $q^r\to\bar q$.

The inverse changes of variables are fixed. This proves convergence of the original splits, prices, and total utilities, with every allocation accumulation point a continuum competitive-equilibrium allocation.

5. Sufficient bounds for exact rational computation

An exact rational linear Fisher equilibrium is computable in polynomial bit complexity. One applicable result is Orlin's exact algorithm: with $N$ buyers plus goods and $E$ positive valuation incidences, its strongly polynomial arithmetic bound is
$$ O\!\left(N^2\log N\,(E+N\log N)\right). $$
The bit-operation consequence uses polynomially bounded intermediate encodings, not unit-cost arithmetic on arbitrary-length numbers. Rational inputs reduce to integral inputs by clearing budget denominators globally and valuation denominators separately for each buyer. The former scales prices; the latter scales each buyer's utility and preserves its preferences. Positive budgets and positive valuation incidence for every buyer and good hold here. See [Orlin, Theorem 4.1 and Section 1.1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/16dagstuhl-seminar-proceedings/dsp-vol10171/DagSemProc.10171.2/DagSemProc.10171.2.pdf).

The later-round budget lengths must nevertheless be bounded.

Consider a seller's tight-edge graph, with edges satisfying
$$ \pi_j=A_ta_{tj}. $$
Every buyer and item lies in this graph. Within a connected component, spanning-tree paths express
$$ A_t=c_t\ell,\qquad \pi_j=d_j\ell, $$
where the rational coefficients depend only on the fixed valuations. Their bit lengths are polynomial in the original input length and market dimensions. Summing spending within the component gives
$$ \ell= \frac{\sum_{t\text{ in the component}}q_t} {\sum_{j\text{ in the component}}d_j}. $$
Thus prices and multipliers have rational encodings whose lengths are linear in the current budget encoding length, up to polynomial factors.

Let $S_r$ bound the bit length of a common denominator and all numerators of the split array. Seller utilities are $q_{tk}^r/A_{tk}^r$. Summing these utilities and applying the proportional update uses polynomially many rational operations. Taking a common denominator for the resulting split array consequently gives
$$ S_{r+1}\leq P(S_r+1), $$
for some polynomial $P\geq2$ in $m,\tau,|K|,L$. Enlarging $P$ to cover the initial encoding yields
$$ S_r\leq P^{r+1}. $$

A rational equilibrium allocation can be selected by the exact algorithm. Equivalently, once prices and tight edges are known, rational payments satisfying the buyer and item totals can be found by a transportation-flow computation, and quantities are payments divided by prices.

Consequently, for a computably chosen rational tie division:

  • Round $r$ requires at most $P^{O(r+1)}$ bit operations.
  • Computing the first $T$ rounds and their exact average requires at most $P^{O(T)}$ bit operations.

Combined with Section 3, this is a sufficient singly exponential exact-computation bound. It does not bound the encoding of arbitrary externally supplied irrational tie divisions.

6. New result: the exact average has exponential output length

The following example strengthens the simulation obstruction in NOTES to an obstruction for every algorithm producing an explicit exact average.

6.1 Four types and two singleton sellers

Take
$$ \tau=4,\qquad \mu_t=\frac14,\qquad b_t=1,\qquad s_1=s_2=1,\qquad v_{t1}=v_{t2}=1. $$
Each seller owns one item. Let
$$ f_t(z)=\frac{tz}{1+tz},\qquad z_0=24, $$
and initialize
$$ \beta_{t1}^0=f_t(z_0),\qquad \beta_{t2}^0=1-f_t(z_0). $$
All task hypotheses hold.

Write $S(z)=\sum_{t=1}^4f_t(z)$. If the current splits have this form, seller prices and per-capita allocations are uniquely
$$ p_1=\frac{S(z)}4,\qquad p_2=\frac{4-S(z)}4, $$
$$ x_{t1}=\frac{4f_t(z)}{S(z)},\qquad x_{t2}=\frac{4(1-f_t(z))}{4-S(z)}. $$
Uniqueness follows directly from the single-item payment equations.

The update satisfies
$$ \frac{f_t^{\,\mathrm{next}}}{1-f_t^{\,\mathrm{next}}} =tz\,\frac{4-S(z)}{S(z)}. $$
Hence the form is preserved, with
$$ z_{r+1}=R(z_r):=z_r\frac{4-S(z_r)}{S(z_r)}. $$

Define
$$ P(z)=\prod_{t=1}^4(1+tz) =1+10z+35z^2+50z^3+24z^4. $$
Since $S(z)=zP'(z)/P(z)$,
$$ R(z)=\frac{4P(z)-zP'(z)}{P'(z)} =\frac{50z^3+70z^2+30z+4} {96z^3+150z^2+70z+10}. $$

6.2 Uniform bounds on cancellation

Write $z_r=a_r/b_r$ in lowest positive terms. Define the homogeneous polynomials
$$ \begin{aligned} N(a,b)&=50a^3+70a^2b+30ab^2+4b^3,\\ D(a,b)&=96a^3+150a^2b+70ab^2+10b^3,\\ C(a,b)&=4(b+2a)(b+3a)(b+4a). \end{aligned} $$
The next unreduced split parameter is $N(a_r,b_r)/D(a_r,b_r)$, and
$$ x_{11}^r=\frac{C(a_r,b_r)}{D(a_r,b_r)}. $$

The following polynomial identities are verified by expansion:
$$ \begin{aligned} &(-27120a^2-33255ab-8741b^2)N\\ &\quad +(14125a^2+15025ab+3500b^2)D=36b^5,\\ &(210a^2+225ab+50b^2)N\\ &\quad +(-109a^2-100ab-20b^2)D=36a^5, \end{aligned} $$
and
$$ \begin{aligned} &(-6000a^2-7407ab-1972b^2)C\\ &\quad +(6000a^2+4532ab+790b^2)D=12b^5,\\ &(285a^2+325ab+75b^2)C\\ &\quad +(-284a^2-190ab-30b^2)D=96a^5. \end{aligned} $$

Because $\gcd(a,b)=1$, the first pair implies
$$ \gcd(N(a,b),D(a,b))\mid36. $$
Indeed, a common divisor divides both $36a^5$ and $36b^5$, whose greatest common divisor is $36$.

The second pair similarly implies
$$ \gcd(C(a,b),D(a,b))\mid96. $$
For each prime, at least one of $a,b$ has zero valuation, and $12$ divides $96$.

Let $d_r$ be the reduced denominator of $x_{11}^r$. Reduction of the recurrence and of the allocation therefore gives
$$ \boxed{ \frac{b_{r+1}}{96}\leq d_r\leq36b_{r+1}. } $$

6.3 Denominator growth survives averaging

The coefficient ratios of the numerator and denominator of $R$ lie between $2/5$ and $25/48$. Since all coefficients are positive,
$$ \frac25\leq z_r\leq\frac{25}{48}<1 \quad\text{for every }r\geq1. $$
In particular,
$$ D(a_r,b_r)\geq10b_r^3. $$
The cancellation bound yields
$$ b_{r+1}\geq\frac5{18}b_r^3 \quad(r\geq1). $$

Set
$$ h_r=\log_2b_r,\qquad c=\log_2(18/5). $$
Then
$$ h_{r+1}\geq3h_r-c. $$
The first iterate is
$$ z_1=\frac{366122}{707597}, $$
so $h_1>c/2$. Induction gives
$$ h_r\geq3^{r-1}(h_1-c/2)+c/2. $$

We now bound the denominator of the sum, allowing arbitrary cancellation. Fix $r\geq1$, and let $H$ be the reduced denominator of
$$ \sum_{i=0}^r x_{11}^i. $$
Let $E$ be the reduced denominator of the preceding sum. Then
$$ E\leq\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}d_i. $$
Subtracting that preceding sum from the full sum shows that $d_r$ divides $HE$. Therefore
$$ H\geq\frac{d_r}{E} \geq\frac{d_r}{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}d_i}. $$

The recurrence also implies, for $1\leq k\leq r$,
$$ h_k\leq3^{-(r-k)}h_r+c/2. $$
Consequently,
$$ \sum_{k=1}^rh_k\leq\frac32h_r+\frac{rc}{2}. $$
Using the bounds on $d_i$,
$$ \begin{aligned} \log_2H &\geq h_{r+1}-\log_2 96 -\sum_{k=1}^rh_k-r\log_2 36\\ &\geq\frac32h_r-c-\log_2 96 -r\bigl(c/2+\log_2 36\bigr). \end{aligned} $$
The right-hand side is $\Omega(3^r)$.

Dividing a reduced rational by a positive integer cannot decrease its reduced denominator: if the rational is $n/H$, the new denominator is
$$ \frac{HT}{\gcd(n,T)}\geq H. $$
Taking $r=T-1$, we conclude
$$ \boxed{ \text{The exact coordinate }\hat x_{11} \text{ requires }\Omega(3^T)\text{ binary bits.} } $$
This proof does not assume that an algorithm constructs the intermediate splits.

6.4 Accuracy forces many rounds

The function $S$ increases continuously from $0$ to $4$, so there is a unique $z_*>0$ with $S(z_*)=2$. Moreover,
$$ zS'(z)=\sum_tf_t(z)(1-f_t(z)). $$
Differentiating $R=z(4-S)/S$ gives
$$ R'(z) =\frac{4\sum_tf_t(z)^2-S(z)^2}{S(z)^2}>0. $$
The strict inequality follows because the four $f_t(z)$ are distinct for $z>0$.

Since $S(24)>2$, we have $z_0>z_*$. For $z>z_*$,
$$ R(z)<z,\qquad R(z)>R(z_*)=z_*. $$
Thus $z_*<z_{r+1}<z_r$ for every round.

The per-capita total utility is
$$ U_t(z)=\frac{4f_t(z)}{S(z)} +\frac{4(1-f_t(z))}{4-S(z)}, $$
and direct simplification gives
$$ U_t(z)-2 =\frac{(4-2S(z))(4f_t(z)-S(z))} {S(z)(4-S(z))}. $$
For type $1$, both numerator factors are negative when $z>z_*$, because $f_1(z)$ is strictly smaller than the average of the four coordinates. Hence
$$ U_1^r>2 \quad\text{for every }r. $$
Define the fixed positive constant
$$ d=U_1^0-2. $$
The average utility satisfies
$$ \hat U_1-2\geq\frac dT. $$

Every feasible allocation has
$$ \sum_{t=1}^4U_t=8,\qquad U_t\leq8. $$
The allocation $x_{t1}=x_{t2}=1$ is feasible and gives utilities $(2,2,2,2)$. Jensen's inequality shows that this vector maximizes
$$ F(U)=\frac14\sum_t\log U_t. $$
On positive utilities bounded by $8$,
$$ \nabla^2F(U)\preceq-\frac1{256}I. $$
Taylor's inequality from $(2,2,2,2)$ therefore gives
$$ F^*-F(\hat U) \geq\frac1{512}\sum_t(\hat U_t-2)^2. $$
The linear term vanishes because the total utility is fixed. Thus
$$ G^*-G(\hat x)\geq\frac{d^2}{512T^2}. $$
Achieving gap at most $\epsilon$ requires
$$ T\geq\frac d{\sqrt{512\epsilon}}. $$

Combining this with the exact-average encoding bound proves
$$ \boxed{ \text{Every accurate explicit exact average in this example needs } \exp\!\bigl(\Omega(\epsilon^{-1/2})\bigr) \text{ output bits.} } $$

6.5 The obstruction occurs at inverse-polynomial accuracy

For an integer $n\geq2$, replace every budget by
$$ b_t=1+2^{-n} $$
and multiply every initial split by the same factor. Uniformly scaling all budgets scales prices and preserves every round allocation and split proportion.

The resulting total input length is $L_n=\Theta(n)$. Choose
$$ \epsilon_n=\frac1{n^2}. $$
This is inverse-polynomial in $L_n$, and the required output length is
$$ \exp(\Omega(n))=\exp(\Omega(L_n)). $$
Therefore explicit exact-average computation is not polynomial-time even at inverse-polynomial objective accuracy.

7. Polynomial-bit numerical approximation of the trajectory

This section concerns a feasible numerical substitute for the exact average. It does not remove the output obstruction above.

7.1 Stability of seller utilities

Consider two equilibria of the same seller market, with positive budget vectors $q,q'$ and multipliers $A,A'$. Define
$$ e=\max_t\left|\log\frac{q'_t}{q_t}\right|. $$
I claim
$$ \max_t\left|\log\frac{A'_t}{A_t}\right|\leq e. $$

Let $h=\max_t A'_t/A_t$, and let $I$ be the buyers attaining this maximum. Consider every item allocated positively to some buyer in $I$ in the first equilibrium. Its second price is exactly $h$ times its first price: an old tight bidder in $I$ supplies the lower bound, while all multiplier ratios are at most $h$.

Every second-equilibrium winner of such an item belongs to $I$. A buyer with smaller multiplier ratio bids strictly below that new price. Therefore
$$ \sum_{t\in I}q'_t \geq h\sum_{t\in I}q_t. $$
On the other hand,
$$ \sum_{t\in I}q'_t \leq\left(\max_t\frac{q'_t}{q_t}\right) \sum_{t\in I}q_t. $$
Thus $h\leq\max_tq'_t/q_t$. Reversing the roles of the equilibria gives the lower multiplier-ratio bound.

Since seller utilities satisfy $U_t=q_t/A_t$,
$$ \left|\log\frac{U'_t}{U_t}\right|\leq2e. $$
In particular, equal budgets imply equal seller utilities, independently of tie divisions.

For full split arrays, define the same maximum logarithmic distance over all type–seller pairs. Local seller utilities and their sums differ multiplicatively by at most $\exp(2e)$. Consequently the proportional update map $\Phi$ satisfies
$$ \boxed{ \max_{t,k}\left| \log\frac{\Phi(q')_{tk}}{\Phi(q)_{tk}} \right|\leq4e. } $$

7.2 Rounding with polynomial precision

Write $f_{tk}=q_{tk}/w_t$, so every row sums to one. Set
$$ \rho_0=\min_{t,k}f_{tk}^0,\qquad a_{\max}=\max_{t,j}a_{tj},\qquad w_{\min}=\min_tw_t, $$
and
$$ c_0=\frac{w_{\min}a_{\min}}{ma_{\max}}\in(0,1]. $$
The multiplier bound gives
$$ U_{tk}\geq a_{\min}q_{tk}, \qquad U_t\leq ma_{\max}. $$
Thus every ideal next proportion satisfies
$$ f_{tk}^{\mathrm{ideal}} =\frac{U_{tk}}{U_t}\geq c_0f_{tk}. $$

Choose
$$ T=\left\lceil\frac{4L}{\epsilon}\right\rceil, \qquad \eta=\frac{\epsilon}{32\,4^T}. $$
Choose a dyadic grid spacing $\delta=2^{-H}$ satisfying
$$ |K|\delta \leq\eta\rho_0(c_0/2)^T. $$

At each round, compute an exact seller equilibrium at the current rounded budgets. Round the first $|K|-1$ ideal proportions in each row downward to the grid, and assign the residual to the last coordinate.

Each coordinate changes by at most $|K|\delta$. Induction gives
$$ f_{tk}^r\geq\rho_0(c_0/2)^r, $$
and the relative rounding error is at most $\eta$. Indeed, for $r<T$, the ideal proportion is at least $\rho_0(c_0/2)^T$, and multiplication by $1-\eta\geq1/2$ preserves the inductive lower bound.

The logarithmic rounding error is at most $2\eta$. Let $e_r$ be the logarithmic distance between the rounded and prescribed splits at round $r$. The stability lemma gives
$$ e_0=0,\qquad e_{r+1}\leq4e_r+2\eta. $$
Therefore
$$ e_r\leq\frac{2\eta(4^r-1)}3 \leq\frac{\epsilon}{48} \quad(r\leq T). $$
Their corresponding total utilities differ multiplicatively by at most $\exp(\epsilon/24)$. The same bound holds for averaged total utilities, because averaging preserves coordinatewise multiplicative inequalities.

Let $\hat y_{\mathrm{round}}$ be the average of the rounded-round allocations, and $\hat y_{\mathrm{exact}}$ the prescribed average. Then
$$ \left|F(\hat y_{\mathrm{round}}) -F(\hat y_{\mathrm{exact}})\right| \leq\frac{\epsilon}{24}. $$
Section 3 gives
$$ F^*-F(\hat y_{\mathrm{exact}}) \leq\frac{2L}{T}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}. $$
Consequently,
$$ F^*-F(\hat y_{\mathrm{round}}) \leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{24}<\epsilon. $$
Every rounded-round allocation, and hence its rational average, is exactly feasible.

The rational magnitude bounds imply
$$ H=O\!\left(T(L+1)+\log(|K|/\epsilon)\right). $$
The grid can be selected using rational comparisons with polynomial-length encodings. Each round's budgets have polynomial encoding length in $L+H$. Exact seller computation and rational averaging therefore take polynomially many bit operations in
$$ m,\tau,|K|,L,1/\epsilon. $$

8. Scope of the conclusions

The convergence question is settled affirmatively. The prescribed trajectory has the exact-round objective bound $2L/T$, and exact rational computation has a sufficient singly exponential bit-operation bound.

The new four-type example settles a stronger negative computational statement: an explicit exact average of the prescribed rounds cannot always be obtained in polynomial time, because the required output itself is too long. This remains true at inverse-polynomial accuracy.

The numerical substitute in Section 7 has polynomial bit complexity and closely tracks the prescribed average utilities. It is nevertheless an average of rounded-process allocations, so it does not satisfy the strict exact-average output requirement.

No NP-hardness reduction is established, and the complexity of producing succinct representations of exact prescribed averages remains open.
