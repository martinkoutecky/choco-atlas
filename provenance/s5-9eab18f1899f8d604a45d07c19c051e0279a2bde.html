2. A gap lemma for exact equality at a rational point

The following lemma accommodates binary-encoded exponents whose numerical values may be exponentially large.

Lemma. Let
$$ P(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_iX^{e_i}, \qquad 0\leq e_1<\cdots<e_n, $$
where the $c_i$ are nonzero integers and the $e_i$ are binary-encoded integers. Let $t=A/B>1$ be reduced, with positive integers $A>B$. Deciding whether $P(t)=0$ takes polynomial time in the encoded input length.

Proof. Put
$$ H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_i|, $$
and choose the integer $T$ such that
$$ 2^{T-1}\leq H<2^T. $$
Partition the terms into consecutive blocks, starting a new block whenever two consecutive exponents differ by more than $T$.

First, I prove that
$$ P(t)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{every block vanishes at }t. $$

The reverse implication follows by addition. For the forward implication, suppose $P(A/B)=0$. The primitive polynomial $BX-A$ divides $P$ over the rationals. Gauss’s lemma therefore gives
$$ P(X)=(BX-A)Q(X), \qquad Q\in\mathbb Z[X]. $$
Here Gauss’s lemma applies because $\gcd(A,B)=1$: a primitive integer polynomial dividing an integer polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ has an integer quotient.

Write $c_j$ for the coefficient of $X^j$ in $P$, including zero coefficients, and $q_j$ for the coefficient of $X^j$ in $Q$. Set $q_{-1}=0$. Comparing coefficients gives
$$ c_j=Bq_{j-1}-Aq_j. $$
Iteration yields
$$ q_j = -\frac1A\sum_{h=0}^{j}c_h \left(\frac BA\right)^{j-h}. $$
Since $0<B/A<1$,
$$ |q_j| \leq \frac1A\sum_{h=0}^{j}|c_h| \leq H. $$

Consider a block boundary between exponents $e<f$, so $f-e>T$ and all coefficients strictly between them vanish. The recurrence gives
$$ q_{f-1} = \left(\frac BA\right)^{f-e-1}q_e. $$
Both quotient coefficients are integers. Because $A$ and $B$ are coprime,
$$ A^{f-e-1}\mid q_e. $$
But
$$ A^{f-e-1}\geq2^T>H\geq|q_e|. $$
Thus $q_e=0$, and hence $q_{f-1}=0$.

Consequently, the portion of $P$ consisting of terms of exponent at most $e$ is itself divisible by $BX-A$: it equals that factor times the truncation of $Q$ to exponents below $e$. This holds at every block boundary. Differences of consecutive such portions show that every block vanishes at $A/B$, as claimed.

It remains to implement the test. If a block starts at exponent $s$, factor it symbolically as $X^sR(X)$. Its vanishing at $t>0$ is equivalent to $R(t)=0$. Within a block, consecutive exponent gaps are at most $T$, so
$$ \deg R\leq(n-1)T. $$
This degree is polynomial in the input length. Evaluate $R(A/B)$ by exact rational arithmetic. For example, if $w=\deg R$, multiply by $B^w$ and compute the integer
$$ B^wR(A/B) = \sum_{e_i\text{ in the block}} c_iA^{e_i-s}B^{w-(e_i-s)}. $$
All powers here have polynomially bounded numerical exponents, and all resulting integers have polynomial bit length.

The possibly enormous block starts $s$ are never exponentiated. Sorting exponents, finding boundaries, and testing every block therefore take polynomial time. $\square$

3. Exact equality for arbitrary rational powers of one base

Theorem. Given binary-encoded rationals
$$ b>1,\qquad a_i,\qquad r_i, $$
one can decide in polynomial time whether
$$ F=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b^{r_i}=0. $$
The exponents and coefficients may have either sign.

3.1 Computing the maximal rational-power exponent

Write $b=A/B$ in lowest terms, with $A>B\geq1$. Define
$$ g=\gcd\bigl\{|\operatorname{ord}_p(b)|: \operatorname{ord}_p(b)\neq0\bigr\}, $$
where $\operatorname{ord}_p$ is the usual prime valuation.

Prime factorization is used only to justify this definition, not as an algorithmic operation. A positive integer $j$ satisfies $b=\gamma^j$ for a positive rational $\gamma$ exactly when both $A$ and $B$ are perfect $j$-th powers. Equivalently, $j$ divides every nonzero prime valuation of $b$. Thus the largest successful $j$ is $g$.

Because $A\geq2$, any such $j$ satisfies
$$ j\leq\lfloor\log_2 A\rfloor. $$
Test every integer in this range by exact integer-root computation. Binary search and integer exponentiation suffice, with polynomially many operations on polynomial-length integers. This computes $g$ and
$$ \beta=b^{1/g}\in\mathbb Q_{>1} $$
in polynomial time.

3.2 Proving the required linear independence

Let $D$ be the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of the $r_i$, and put
$$ h=\gcd(D,g),\qquad d=D/h,\qquad a=g/h. $$
Define positive real numbers
$$ \theta=b^{1/D}, \qquad t=\beta^a. $$
Then $t$ is rational and
$$ \theta^d=t. $$
Its rational encoding has polynomial length: the exponent $a$ is at most $g$, and $\beta$ was obtained by taking exact roots of the input numerator and denominator.

I claim that
$$ [\mathbb Q(\theta):\mathbb Q]=d. $$
Let this degree be $s$. Since $\theta$ satisfies $X^d-t$, one has $s\leq d$.

For the reverse inequality, take the field norm from $\mathbb Q(\theta)$ to $\mathbb Q$. Multiplicativity of the norm and $\theta^D=b$ imply
$$ \operatorname{Norm}(\theta)^D=b^s. $$
The norm is a nonzero rational number. Taking prime valuations gives, for every prime $p$,
$$ D\,\operatorname{ord}_p\bigl(\operatorname{Norm}(\theta)\bigr) = s\,\operatorname{ord}_p(b). $$
Thus $D$ divides every $s\operatorname{ord}_p(b)$. By the defining greatest-common-divisor property of $g$, it follows that $D\mid sg$. Since
$$ D=hd,\qquad g=ha,\qquad \gcd(d,a)=1, $$
we obtain $d\mid s$. Together with $1\leq s\leq d$, this proves $s=d$.

Therefore
$$ 1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{d-1} $$
are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. This argument does not require explicitly constructing a field of dimension $d$.

3.3 Reduction to sparse rational equality tests

Each
$$ s_i=Dr_i $$
is an integer. Perform Euclidean division, including when $s_i$ is negative:
$$ s_i=dz_i+e_i, \qquad z_i\in\mathbb Z,\qquad 0\leq e_i<d. $$
Then
$$ b^{r_i} = \theta^{s_i} = t^{z_i}\theta^{e_i}. $$
Grouping by the observed residues gives
$$ F = \sum_e\theta^e \left(\sum_{i:e_i=e}a_i t^{z_i}\right). $$
The coefficients in parentheses are rational, although their expanded representations might be very large. Linear independence proves
$$ F=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i:e_i=e}a_i t^{z_i}=0 \quad\text{for every observed residue }e. $$

There are at most $n$ observed residues, regardless of the numerical size of $d$.

For each group, clear the coefficient denominators and subtract the least integer exponent from every exponent. These operations multiply its value by nonzero rational factors and preserve whether it is zero. The result is an integer-coefficient sparse polynomial evaluated at the rational point $t>1$. Apply the lemma from Section 2.

All intermediate encodings have polynomial length: the bit length of $D$ is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths; all exponent arithmetic is performed on binary integers; coefficient denominators can be cleared by their product. Hence all groups are tested in polynomial time. This proves the theorem.
