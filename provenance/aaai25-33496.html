The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational o and k, and a finite rational distribution over location types on either side of o, compute a pathway (a,b) minimizing expected agent travel cost, and determine whether the weighted threshold rule is optimal and group-strategyproof under a specified positive-mass or clone-stable reporting semantics.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given rational \(o\in(0,1)\) and \(k\in[0,1)\), let \(D=[0,o)\cup(o,1]\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct rational location types \(x_i\in D\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(w_i\), with \(\sum_i w_i=1\) and positive mass in both regions. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(L\) is the total input bit length and \(m=2\) is the fixed number of regions.
A feasible pathway satisfies \(0\le a<o<b\le1\). Its individual cost is \(c(a,b,x)=|x-a|+k(b-a)+1-b\) for \(x<o\), and \(c(a,b,x)=|x-b|+k(b-a)+a\) for \(x>o\); minimize \(C_\mu(a,b)=\sum_i w_i c(a,b,x_i)\).
For a reported distribution \(\nu\), write \(p=\nu([0,o))\), \(q=\nu((o,1])\), and define \(X_L=\{t\in[0,o):(\nu([0,t])+q)(1-k)<\nu((t,o))(1+k)\}\) and \(X_R=\{t\in(o,1]:(\nu([t,1])+p)(1-k)<\nu((o,t))(1+k)\}\).
The weighted threshold rule \(f(\nu)\) returns \(a=\sup X_L\), or \(a=0\) if empty, and \(b=\inf X_R\), or \(b=1\) if empty; the strict inequalities fix its tie-breaking.
Use positive-mass reporting semantics: a coalition selects finitely many positive rational portions of the true type masses, with positive total mass, and assigns each portion a rational reported location in its true region; unselected mass reports truthfully, and mass cannot be created or deleted. Group-strategyproofness means no such deviation strictly decreases \(c(f(\nu),x)\), evaluated at the true location \(x\), for every participating portion.

Is exact expected-cost minimization polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard (for its decision version, given rational \(T\), does a feasible pathway satisfy \(C_\mu(a,b)\le T\))?
Is the weighted threshold rule always optimal and group-strategyproof under the specified reporting semantics?

For finite equally weighted agents, the threshold rule is polynomial-time computable, social-cost optimal, and group-strategyproof with within-region reports. [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18626v1#S4).

Result and computational interpretation

Exact expected-cost minimization is polynomial-time solvable for the stated model with \(m=2\). The weighted threshold rule is always optimal and group-strategyproof under the specified reporting semantics.

For the bit-complexity claim, I interpret \(k\) as a binary-encoded rational, or a fixed rational constant. An arbitrary real \(k\) without an effective representation does not define a bit-complexity problem. The optimality and group-strategyproofness proofs below hold for every real \(k\in[0,1)\).

These conclusions extend the discrete statement in [Theorem 2 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18626v1#S4). The proofs below work directly with masses.

1. Exact characterization of the threshold rule

Let \(\rho\) be any finitely supported probability distribution on \(D\), with positive mass in both regions. Define

\[ p=\rho([0,o)),\qquad q=\rho((o,1]),\qquad s=\frac{1-k}{2},\qquad \alpha=p-s,\qquad \beta=q-s. \]

Here and below, display equations use the same mathematical notation as inline equations. Since \(k<1\), we have \(s>0\), so \(\alpha<p\) and \(\beta<q\).

For \(t\in[0,o)\), put \(F(t)=\rho([0,t])\). The left threshold inequality is equivalent to

\[ (F(t)+q)(1-k)<(p-F(t))(1+k) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F(t)<\alpha. \]

For \(t\in(o,1]\), put \(G(t)=\rho([t,1])\). Likewise, the right threshold inequality is equivalent to \(G(t)<\beta\).

Consequently, the rule has the following finite characterization:

  • If \(\alpha\le0\), it returns \(a=0\). If \(\alpha>0\), it returns the smallest left support location \(a\) satisfying \(F(a)\ge\alpha\).
  • If \(\beta\le0\), it returns \(b=1\). If \(\beta>0\), it returns the largest right support location \(b\) satisfying \(G(b)\ge\beta\).

To verify the left characterization when \(\alpha>0\), such a support location exists because the cumulative mass at the largest left support location is \(p>\alpha\). Before the first qualifying location, \(F(t)<\alpha\); at and after it, \(F(t)\ge\alpha\). Thus \(X_L=[0,a)\) if \(a>0\), and \(X_L\) is empty if \(a=0\).

The right characterization follows identically using descending cumulative mass: \(X_R=(b,1]\) if \(b<1\), and it is empty if \(b=1\).

In particular, the returned pathway is feasible. Every nondefault endpoint belongs to its corresponding support, hence remains strictly outside the obstacle.

Two strict inequalities will be essential later. Whenever \(\alpha>0\) and \(a>0\),

\[ \rho([0,a))<\alpha\le\rho([0,a]). \]

Whenever \(\beta>0\) and \(b<1\),

\[ \rho((b,1])<\beta\le\rho([b,1]). \]

2. Proof of optimality

Expanding the expected cost gives

\[ C_\rho(a,b)=p+A(a)+B(b), \]

where

\[ A(a)=\sum_{x_i<o}w_i|x_i-a|+(q-k)a, \qquad B(b)=\sum_{x_i>o}w_i|x_i-b|+(k-p)b. \]

Thus the two endpoint choices are independent minimization problems.

The function \(A\) is continuous and piecewise linear. Its right derivative at every \(t\in[0,o)\) is

\[ A'_+(t) =\rho([0,t])-\rho((t,o))+q-k =2(F(t)-\alpha). \]

The derivative formula includes atoms at \(t\): their right derivative contribution is their positive mass.

If \(\alpha\le0\), every slope is nonnegative, so \(a=0\) minimizes \(A\). If \(\alpha>0\), the endpoint characterized in Section 1 has negative slopes everywhere before it and nonnegative slopes everywhere after it. Summing slope times interval length over the finitely many linear pieces proves

\[ A(a)\le A(u)\qquad\text{for every }u\in[0,o). \]

Similarly, the left derivative of \(B\) at \(t\in(o,1]\) is

\[ B'_-(t) =\rho((o,t))-\rho([t,1])+k-p =2(\beta-G(t)). \]

If \(\beta\le0\), every slope is nonpositive, so \(b=1\) minimizes \(B\). If \(\beta>0\), the endpoint characterized in Section 1 has nonpositive slopes before it and positive slopes after it. Therefore

\[ B(b)\le B(v)\qquad\text{for every }v\in(o,1]. \]

Adding these inequalities proves that \(f(\rho)\) minimizes \(C_\rho\) over every feasible pathway.

This also resolves attainment despite the open boundaries at \(o\): a feasible minimizer actually exists. When there are flat intervals of minimizers, the rule selects the smallest minimizing left endpoint and the largest minimizing right endpoint.

3. Polynomial-time exact computation

Partition the support by region and sort each part. Compute \(p,q,\alpha,\beta\), then scan cumulative masses in increasing order on the left and decreasing order on the right. Section 1 identifies the returned endpoints through exact comparisons.

This takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) rational comparisons and \(O(\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. Evaluate the cost of the resulting pathway using the original cost formula.

All these operations have polynomial bit complexity in \(L\). For example, a common denominator obtained by multiplying the input denominators has bit length bounded by their total encoded length. Cumulative masses, threshold comparisons, and objective evaluation therefore involve rational numbers with polynomially bounded bit length.

The algorithm never expands masses into equally weighted agents.

For the decision problem, compute the attained minimum \(C^*=C_\mu(f(\mu))\) and answer yes exactly when \(C^*\le T\). Hence the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\). This establishes polynomial-time solvability for the specified fixed value \(m=2\); it makes no assertion about an unspecified extension to more regions.

4. Proof of group-strategyproofness

Let a coalition select finitely many portions indexed by \(j\), with positive masses \(v_j\), true locations \(x_j\), and reported locations \(y_j\) in their true regions. Write \(\nu\) for the resulting distribution.

For every set \(S\subseteq D\),

\[ \nu(S)-\mu(S) =\sum_j v_j\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{y_j\in S\}}-\mathbf 1_{\{x_j\in S\}}\bigr). \]

Therefore, a strict increase of mass in \(S\) requires at least one participating positive portion to enter \(S\); a strict decrease requires one to leave.

Within-region reporting preserves \(p\) and \(q\), and consequently preserves \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Let

\[ f(\mu)=(a,b),\qquad f(\nu)=(a',b'), \qquad d_a=|a'-a|,\qquad d_b=|b'-b|. \]

If both displacements vanish, nobody strictly benefits. Otherwise, consider an endpoint whose displacement is largest.

4.1. The left endpoint moves at least as far

Suppose \(d_a\ge d_b\) and \(d_a>0\). Necessarily \(\alpha>0\), since otherwise the left endpoint is constantly zero.

Case \(a'>a\). The quantile characterization gives

\[ \mu([0,a])\ge\alpha>\nu([0,a]). \]

Thus some participating portion has true location \(x\le a\) and reports beyond \(a\). For this portion, both endpoints \(a,a'\) lie weakly to its right, so its cost change is exactly

\[ c(a',b',x)-c(a,b,x) =(1-k)(a'-a)-(1-k)(b'-b) \ge(1-k)(d_a-d_b)\ge0. \]

This participant does not strictly benefit.

Case \(a'<a\). Here \(a>0\), and

\[ \mu([0,a))<\alpha \le\nu([0,a']) \le\nu([0,a)). \]

Some participating portion therefore has true location \(x\ge a\) and reports below \(a\). Both left endpoints lie weakly to its left. Its cost change is

\[ c(a',b',x)-c(a,b,x) =(1+k)(a-a')-(1-k)(b'-b) \ge(1+k)d_a-(1-k)d_b \ge2k\,d_a\ge0. \]

Again, this participant does not strictly benefit.

4.2. The right endpoint moves farther

Suppose \(d_b>d_a\). Necessarily \(\beta>0\).

Case \(b'>b\). Since \(b<1\),

\[ \mu((b,1])<\beta \le\nu([b',1]) \le\nu((b,1]). \]

Some participating portion has true location \(x\le b\) and reports above \(b\). Both right endpoints lie weakly to its right. Its cost change is

\[ c(a',b',x)-c(a,b,x) =(1+k)(b'-b)+(1-k)(a'-a) \ge(1+k)d_b-(1-k)d_a \ge0. \]

Case \(b'<b\). The quantile characterization gives

\[ \mu([b,1])\ge\beta>\nu([b,1]). \]

Some participating portion has true location \(x\ge b\) and reports below \(b\). Both right endpoints lie weakly to its left. Its cost change is

\[ c(a',b',x)-c(a,b,x) =(1-k)(b-b')+(1-k)(a'-a) \ge(1-k)(d_b-d_a)>0. \]

Thus every possible changed outcome has a participating positive portion whose true-location cost does not strictly decrease. This proves group-strategyproofness under exactly the stated semantics. The proof includes \(k=0\), threshold equalities, and default endpoints.
