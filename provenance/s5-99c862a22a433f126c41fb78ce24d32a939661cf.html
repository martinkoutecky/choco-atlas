The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) institutions, \(K\) applicant profiles, piecewise-constant rational queue densities \(f_k(q)\) on \(q\in[0,1]\), and per-capita institution capacities \(\kappa_d\), compute the continuous Rank-Maximal allocation. Each type \((k,q)\) receives at most one acceptable institution; first maximize matched mass, then lexicographically maximize assigned masses in the baseline institution order, and then assign the highest-priority available profile and queue mass at each institution while preserving completion. Return the exact allocation as finitely many queue intervals with rational endpoints.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq1\) institutions \(D\), a baseline permutation \(\pi\) of \(D\), and \(K\geq1\) applicant profiles. Profile \(k\) specifies an acceptable set \(A_k\subseteq D\); applicants are indifferent among acceptable institutions and prefer any of them to being unmatched. Institutions accept all applicants.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=K\) profiles with positive mass, each mass \(p_k\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible profiles. Each profile additionally has a nonatomic queue coordinate \(q\in[0,1]\), with mass measure \(f_k(q)\,dq\), where \(f_k\geq0\) is piecewise constant with binary-rational values and breakpoints, \(\int_0^1 f_k(q)\,dq=p_k\), and \(\sum_k p_k=1\).
Each institution has a binary-rational capacity \(\kappa_d>0\) and an input strict ordering of all profiles; within a profile, smaller \(q\) has higher priority. Let \(B\) be the total number of density pieces and \(L\) the total input bit length.
An allocation is a measurable assignment of each \((k,q)\) to at most one institution in \(A_k\), or to unemployment, with assigned mass at \(d\) at most \(\kappa_d\); assignments are identified up to sets of zero mass.
The continuous Rank-Maximal rule first maximizes total matched mass, then lexicographically maximizes the institution masses \((x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(m)})\). Holding these masses fixed, it fixes institutions' assignments successively in order \(\pi\).
At institution \(d\), among allocations admitting a completion with those institution masses and all previously fixed assignments, select its assignment maximizing assigned mass in every priority prefix simultaneously. A priority prefix consists of all profiles preceding some \(k\) in \(d\)'s ordering, together with \(\{(k,q):0\leq q\leq t\}\), for \(t\in[0,1]\).
Compute the resulting allocation as finitely many labeled queue intervals with rational endpoints, ignoring zero-mass boundary choices.

Is exact computation of this continuous Rank-Maximal allocation possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,B,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite applicants with dichotomous acceptability and strict institutional priorities, the corresponding SAFE/Rank-Maximal outcome is computable in polynomial time, including selection of institution assignments subject to completion. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09962v1)
No result settling this exact nonatomic queue problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted the finite support as applicant profiles, with the queue coordinate an additional nonatomic coordinate rather than an uncountable list of support types.
  • Normalized total applicant mass to one, making capacities per capita; required positive rational profile masses and nonnegative rational densities with rational breakpoints.
  • Specified each profile's acceptable institution set, permitted empty acceptable sets, and retained the requirement that institutions accept all applicants.
  • Supplied the missing baseline institution permutation and institutional priority inputs: each institution strictly orders profiles, then prioritizes smaller queue coordinates within a profile.
  • Required positive rational institutional capacities, with unused capacity allowed.
  • Made the continuous highest-priority selection precise through simultaneous maximization of mass in every priority prefix, preserving the fixed institution masses and all earlier institutional assignments.
  • Used deterministic measurable assignments and equality up to zero-mass sets; interval labels specify the assigned institution or unemployment.
  • Included the total density-piece count and total binary input length among the running-time parameters.

1. Result and reduction to profile masses

Exact computation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,B,L\). Moreover, the rule is well-defined up to zero-mass sets, and its allocation has a representation using at most \(\tau(m+1)\) labeled queue intervals.

Write \(0\) for unemployment, and define the measure and cumulative mass of profile \(k\) by
\(\mu_k(E)=\int_E f_k(q)\,dq\) and \(F_k(t)=\mu_k([0,t])\).
The function \(F_k\) is continuous and nondecreasing, with \(F_k(0)=0\) and \(F_k(1)=p_k\).

For an allocation, let \(z_{kd}\) be the mass of profile \(k\) assigned to institution \(d\), and let \(z_{k0}\) be its unemployed mass. These numbers satisfy:

  • \(z_{kd}\geq 0\), with \(z_{kd}=0\) whenever \(d\notin A_k\);
  • \(\sum_{d\in D\cup\{0\}}z_{kd}=p_k\) for every profile \(k\);
  • \(\sum_k z_{kd}\leq\kappa_d\) for every institution \(d\).

Conversely, every matrix satisfying these constraints is realizable by a measurable allocation. Indeed, for each profile, partition its queue into consecutive intervals having the prescribed masses, using continuity of \(F_k\). Eligibility depends only on the profile, so every resulting assignment is acceptable.

The same equivalence holds after some institutions’ assignments have been fixed: replace \(p_k\) by the remaining mass \(r_k\). To justify this even for an arbitrary measurable remaining set \(E_k\), its cumulative mass function \(t\mapsto\mu_k(E_k\cap[0,t])\) is continuous. It therefore permits a partition of \(E_k\) into any prescribed finite list of nonnegative masses summing to \(r_k\).

Thus completion feasibility depends on remaining profile masses, rather than on the locations of remaining applicants within their queues.

2. Exact flow optimization and the institution masses

Use a circulation network with vertices \(s,t\), profile vertices \(v_k\), institution vertices \(w_d\), and an unemployment vertex \(w_0\). Its edges and bounds are:

  • \(s\to v_k\), with lower and upper bounds both \(p_k\);
  • \(v_k\to w_d\) for \(d\in A_k\), with bounds \(0\) and \(1\);
  • \(v_k\to w_0\), with bounds \(0\) and \(1\);
  • \(w_d\to t\), with bounds \(0\) and \(\min\{\kappa_d,1\}\);
  • \(w_0\to t\), with bounds \(0\) and \(1\);
  • \(t\to s\), with lower and upper bounds both \(1\).

Capping an institution’s upper bound at \(1\) changes nothing, because the total applicant mass is \(1\). By the preceding reduction, feasible circulations correspond exactly to feasible profile-mass matrices. Assigning everyone to unemployment gives an initial feasible circulation.

We will repeatedly optimize one edge and then fix its flow by setting its lower and upper bounds equal to the optimum.

Here is an explicit max-flow implementation of this operation. Suppose \(f\) is a feasible circulation and we want to maximize the flow on an edge \(e=(a,b)\), whose upper bound is \(h_e\). Build the residual graph using every original edge except \(e\): an edge \(g=(u,v)\) contributes forward capacity \(h_g-f_g\) and backward capacity \(f_g-l_g\). Add a new source with an edge to \(b\) of capacity \(h_e-f_e\), and compute a maximum flow to sink \(a\), of value \(\delta\).

Apply the residual changes to the other edges and increase \(f_e\) by \(\delta\). Conservation holds because the residual flow travels from \(b\) to \(a\), compensating for the increase on \(a\to b\). Every edge remains within its bounds.

This increase is optimal. If another feasible circulation increased \(e\) by \(\delta'>0\), its differences on all other edges would form a feasible residual flow from \(b\) to \(a\) of value \(\delta'\). Including the new source edge would therefore give a flow of value \(\delta'\) in our auxiliary network. Hence \(\delta'\leq\delta\). Minimizing an edge is symmetric: use a residual flow from \(a\) to \(b\), bounded by \(f_e-l_e\).

Apply this operation as follows:

  • Minimize the flow on \(w_0\to t\). Let its optimum be \(U\), and fix this edge at \(U\). The maximum matched mass is \(M=1-U\).
  • In baseline order \(\pi\), maximize the flow on \(w_d\to t\), fixing each optimum before proceeding. Denote the resulting institution masses by \(x_d\).

The first step maximizes total matched mass. The second lexicographically maximizes the institution masses while retaining that maximum. Every fixation preserves a feasible circulation.

3. Exchange lemma: lexicographic optimization maximizes all full-profile prefixes

The following lemma is the essential point.

Lemma. Consider a nonempty set of feasible nonnegative matrices with prescribed row sums \(r_k\), prescribed column sums, and a prescribed set of allowed entries. Fix a column \(d\) and an ordering \(k_1,\ldots,k_\tau\) of the rows. Let \(z^*\) lexicographically maximize
\((z_{k_1d},\ldots,z_{k_\tau d})\).

Then, for every \(\ell\), the matrix \(z^*\) also maximizes
\(S_\ell(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}z_{k_i d}\)
over all feasible matrices.

Proof. The feasible set is compact: entries are nonnegative and bounded by their prescribed row sums. Consequently the lexicographic optimum exists, by successive maximization of coordinates.

Suppose a feasible matrix \(z'\) satisfies \(S_\ell(z')>S_\ell(z^*)\). Let \(\Delta=z'-z^*\). Construct a weighted directed bipartite graph:

  • if \(\Delta_{kj}>0\), put an edge from row \(k\) to column \(j\), of weight \(\Delta_{kj}\);
  • if \(\Delta_{kj}<0\), put an edge from column \(j\) to row \(k\), of weight \(-\Delta_{kj}\).

At every vertex, incoming weight equals outgoing weight, because both matrices have identical row and column sums.

This balanced weighted graph decomposes into positively weighted simple directed cycles. For completeness, start at a positive edge and follow positive outgoing edges. Balance ensures that such an outgoing edge exists whenever a vertex is reached. A repeated vertex produces a simple cycle. Subtract its minimum edge weight and repeat; each subtraction eliminates at least one positive edge, so the process terminates.

A simple cycle containing column \(d\) enters it from one row and leaves it toward another row. Its contribution to \(S_\ell\) is positive precisely when it enters from one of \(k_1,\ldots,k_\ell\) and leaves toward a row outside that set. Since the total change in \(S_\ell\) is positive, at least one cycle has this property.

Modify \(z^*\) by a sufficiently small positive amount along that cycle, increasing entries on row-to-column edges and decreasing entries on column-to-row edges. Row and column sums remain unchanged. Decreased entries remain nonnegative: each was positive in \(z^*\), since its corresponding difference was negative. All increased entries are allowed.

At column \(d\), this modification increases a coordinate among the first \(\ell\) rows and decreases a coordinate after them; all other coordinates at \(d\) remain unchanged. It therefore strictly improves the lexicographic vector, contradicting its optimality. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

The unemployment column is included in this lemma whenever applicants may remain unmatched. Its prescribed column sum makes every row sum exact.

4. Institution assignments and prefixes ending inside a queue

Process institutions in order \(\pi\). Suppose all assignments before the current institution \(d\) have been fixed, and let \(E_k\) be the remaining part of profile \(k\), of mass \(r_k\).

The completion matrices have:

  • row sums \(r_k\);
  • column sums \(x_j\) for all remaining institutions \(j\);
  • unemployment column sum \(U\);
  • the original acceptability restrictions.

This set is nonempty by induction. Let \(k_1,\ldots,k_\tau\) be \(d\)’s priority ordering of profiles. Successively maximize and fix the entries \(z_{k_i d}\) in that order. These are edge optimizations in the circulation network; previously fixed institutions’ profile entries remain fixed. Removing those institutions gives exactly the completion matrices just described.

Write the resulting vector as \(y_i=z_{k_i d}\), and put \(S_\ell^*=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_i\), with \(S_0^*=0\). Assign to \(d\) the earliest remaining mass \(y_i\) of each profile \(k_i\).

This assignment admits a completion. A feasible optimizing matrix exists. After removing \(d\)’s chosen applicants, profile \(k_i\) has remaining mass \(r_{k_i}-y_i\). Its entries for the other columns partition exactly this mass, and the realization argument in Section 1 supplies a completion. The particular queue locations chosen for \(d\) do not affect eligibility.

We now prove that this assignment maximizes every priority prefix, including those ending partway through a profile.

Fix \(i\) and a threshold \(t\in[0,1]\). The relevant priority prefix contains all profiles preceding \(k_i\), and the part of profile \(k_i\) with queue coordinate at most \(t\). Define the available mass in that part by
\(a_i(t)=\mu_{k_i}(E_{k_i}\cap[0,t])\).

For any competing assignment \(W\) admitting a completion, let \(w_j\) be its mass from profile \(k_j\), and put \(S_\ell(W)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}w_j\). Its assigned mass in the chosen prefix is at most
\(\min\{S_{i-1}(W)+a_i(t),\,S_i(W)\}\).
The first bound limits its current-profile contribution by available prefix mass; the second limits it by its entire current-profile assignment.

By the exchange lemma,
\(S_{i-1}(W)\leq S_{i-1}^*\) and \(S_i(W)\leq S_i^*\).
Consequently its prefix mass is at most
\(\min\{S_{i-1}^*+a_i(t),\,S_i^*\} =S_{i-1}^*+\min\{a_i(t),y_i\}\).

Our assignment attains this bound: it takes the earliest remaining mass \(y_i\), whose intersection with the queue prefix has mass \(\min\{a_i(t),y_i\}\). Thus one assignment simultaneously maximizes every priority prefix, exactly as required by the rule.

This assignment is unique up to zero-mass sets. Indeed, any other simultaneous maximizer has the same mass in every priority prefix. Subtracting the common masses of preceding profiles shows, for each profile \(k\), equality of its assigned cumulative masses for every threshold \(t\). If the two assigned sets are \(G_k\) and \(H_k\), then
\(\int_0^t f_k(q)(\mathbf{1}_{G_k}(q)-\mathbf{1}_{H_k}(q))\,dq=0\)
for every \(t\). By the fundamental theorem for indefinite Lebesgue integrals, the integrand is zero almost everywhere. Hence \(\mu_k(G_k\mathbin{\triangle}H_k)=0\).

Starting with no fixed assignments and applying this argument inductively proves that the algorithm computes the stated continuous Rank-Maximal allocation.

5. Rational interval output

For each profile \(k\), define a nondecreasing quantile function \(Q_k\) by:

  • \(Q_k(0)=0\);
  • \(Q_k(p_k)=1\);
  • for \(0<a<p_k\), \(Q_k(a)=\inf\{t\in[0,1]:F_k(t)\geq a\}\).

Continuity gives \(F_k(Q_k(a))=a\). The endpoint conventions can include zero-density portions, which are irrelevant under the problem’s identification of allocations.

Let \(y_{k,\pi(j)}\) be the profile masses computed for institution \(\pi(j)\), and define
\(s_{k,0}=0\) and \(s_{k,j}=\sum_{h=1}^{j}y_{k,\pi(h)}\).

For profile \(k\), output the interval
\([Q_k(s_{k,j-1}),Q_k(s_{k,j}))\)
with label \(\pi(j)\), for each \(j\), omitting empty intervals. Label the remaining interval
\([Q_k(s_{k,m}),1]\)
as unemployment.

Each institution receives the earliest remaining part of every profile, because all institutions use smaller queue coordinates first within that profile. Thus these intervals realize exactly the assignments proved correct above. There are at most \(m+1\) intervals per profile.

All endpoints are rational. On a density piece beginning at \(b\), with positive constant density \(c\), inversion of a cumulative mass \(a\) gives
\(Q_k(a)=b+(a-F_k(b))/c\).
If the mass is attained at a piece boundary, that rational boundary supplies the quantile. Zero-density pieces require no division.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators occurring in the input masses \(p_k\) and capacities \(\kappa_d\). Then \(\log_2 Q=O(L)\), and every original circulation bound is a multiple of \(1/Q\).

The initial all-unemployed circulation lies on this same grid. Inductively, every residual capacity and every auxiliary source capacity does too. After multiplying capacities by \(Q\), each max-flow call has nonnegative integer capacities. An augmenting-path implementation preserves integrality, so every updated circulation and every subsequently fixed optimum remains a multiple of \(1/Q\).

All original circulation flows and bounds lie between \(0\) and \(1\). Their scaled representations therefore use \(O(L)\) bits. In particular, the algorithm never expands a profile into \(Qp_k\) separate applicants.

The network has \(O(m+\tau)\) vertices and \(O(m\tau+m+\tau)\) edges. There are at most \(1+m+m\tau\) edge optimizations. The standard [Edmonds–Karp max-flow algorithm](https://ftp.eecs.umich.edu/~pettie/matching/Edmonds-Karp-network-flow.pdf) uses \(O(VE^2)\) arithmetic operations on a finite directed network with nonnegative capacities, independently of their magnitudes. Here its integer arithmetic has polynomial bit length by the preceding grid argument. Thus all flow computations take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Finally, cumulative masses at the \(B\) density-piece boundaries, and the quantile inversions above, require polynomially many rational arithmetic operations. Their operands have polynomial bit length: they arise from the input rationals and the computed grid masses by finite sums, products, and a single division for each inversion. The at most \(\tau(m+1)\) output intervals consequently have polynomial total encoding length.

This completes both the correctness proof and the polynomial-time bound.
