The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete preference values \(a_{tj}\), position weights \(v\), and parameters \(\lambda,\eta,\epsilon\), return a lottery \(q_t\) over integral rankings for each type whose welfare is within \(\epsilon\) of optimum. Here \(r_{tj}(q)=\sum_{\sigma}q_{t\sigma}v_{\sigma(j)}\), \(U_t(q)=\sum_j a_{tj}r_{tj}(q)\), \(E_j(q)=\sum_t\mu_t r_{tj}(q)\), and the welfare is \(F(q)=(1-\lambda)\sum_t\mu_t\log(U_t(q)+\eta)+\lambda\sum_j\log(E_j(q)+\eta)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq1\) items. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Inputs also include complete cardinal values \(a_{tj}\in\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\), position weights \(v_1\geq\cdots\geq v_m\geq0\), and binary rational parameters \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), \(\eta>0\), and \(\epsilon>0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
For each type, choose a lottery \(q_t\) over permutations \(\sigma\) of the items, where \(\sigma(j)\) is the position of item \(j\). Output lotteries as explicit finite lists of permutations and nonnegative rational probabilities summing to \(1\); ties in values or weights impose no additional restrictions.
Define \(r_{tj}(q)=\sum_\sigma q_{t\sigma}v_{\sigma(j)}\), \(U_t(q)=\sum_{j=1}^m a_{tj}r_{tj}(q)\), and \(E_j(q)=\sum_{t=1}^\tau\mu_t r_{tj}(q)\).
Using natural logarithms, define \(F(q)=(1-\lambda)\sum_{t=1}^\tau\mu_t\log(U_t(q)+\eta)+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^m\log(E_j(q)+\eta)\). The computational problem is to return \(q\) satisfying \(F(q)\geq\max_{q'}F(q')-\epsilon\).

Is this additive approximation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), including the encoded accuracy and smoothing parameters, or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of users, a verified algorithmic result gives welfare error \(O(BH/s)\) after \(s\) iterations, where \(B\) bounds the absolute second derivatives of the scalar welfare terms and \(H\) is the maximum squared Euclidean norm of a feasible utility profile; each iteration uses polynomially many arithmetic operations.   • Used the welfare parameter domain lambda in [0,1] and positive rational smoothing and accuracy parameters; retained zero values and zero position weights, which the definitions permit.

1. Answer and input interpretation

The additive approximation problem is solvable in polynomial time in the rational-input bit model. In particular, the running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), including the binary encodings of \(\epsilon\) and \(\eta\). The output consists of at most \(m^2\) permutations per type, with rational probabilities of polynomial encoding length.

I interpret the position weights \(v_k\) as binary-encoded rationals, as intended by the rational-input formulation. Arbitrary real weights would require a specified representation model.

If \(m=1\), the unique permutation is optimal. Henceforth assume \(m\geq2\).

2. Replace lotteries by a polynomial-size polytope

For every type \(t\), introduce an \(m\times m\) matrix \(P_t\), where \(P_{tjk}\) is the probability that item \(j\) occupies position \(k\). Every lottery induces a doubly stochastic matrix:

\[ P_{tjk}\geq0,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{tjk}=1,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{tjk}=1. \]

Conversely, every doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices. Section 6 proves this constructively and gives the required bit bounds.

Define

\[ r_{tj}(P)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{tjk}v_k,\qquad U_t(P)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}a_{tj}P_{tjk}v_k,\qquad E_j(P)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{tjk}v_k. \]

These expressions agree exactly with the quantities induced by the corresponding lotteries. Thus maximizing the given objective over lotteries is equivalent to maximizing it over the product of \(\tau\) doubly stochastic matrix polytopes.

We now remove the row and column equalities. Let

\[ n=\tau(m-1)^2 \]

and use coordinates \(x_{tjk}=P_{tjk}\) for \(j,k<m\). Recover the remaining entries by

\[ P_{tj m}=1-\sum_{k<m}x_{tjk},\qquad P_{t m k}=1-\sum_{j<m}x_{tjk},\qquad P_{tmm}=\sum_{j,k<m}x_{tjk}-(m-2). \]

These formulas enforce every row and column sum. Let \(K\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) be the polytope defined by nonnegativity of all reconstructed entries. It has \(\tau m^2\) explicit rational linear inequalities. Membership and a violated inequality can therefore be determined exactly in polynomial time.

Let \(c\) have every coordinate equal to \(1/m\). Its reconstructed matrices have every entry equal to \(1/m\). Set

\[ \rho_0=\frac{1}{2m^2},\qquad R=n+1. \]

Then

\[ B(c,\rho_0)\subseteq K\subseteq B(c,R), \]

where \(B(c,r)\) denotes the closed Euclidean ball.

To verify the first inclusion, perturb \(c\) by \(h\) with \(\|h\|_2\leq\rho_0\). The coefficient vectors defining the changes in reconstructed entries have Euclidean norms \(1\), \(\sqrt{m-1}\), or \(m-1\). Consequently, every entry changes by at most

\[ (m-1)\rho_0<\frac{1}{2m}. \]

Every reconstructed entry remains positive. For the second inclusion, \(K\subseteq[0,1]^n\), so every point of \(K\) is within distance \(\sqrt n\leq n<R\) of \(c\).

3. Concavity, exact rational gradients, and a Lipschitz bound

Write \(f(x)=F(P(x))\). All logarithm arguments are at least \(\eta>0\). Since they are affine functions of \(x\), and their coefficients in the objective are nonnegative, \(f\) is concave and differentiable on a neighborhood of \(K\). It is continuous on the compact set \(K\), so its maximum \(f^*\) is attained.

Let

\[ A=\max_{t,j}a_{tj},\qquad V=v_1. \]

For \(j,k<m\), direct differentiation gives

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{tjk}} = (1-\lambda)\mu_t \frac{(a_{tj}-a_{tm})(v_k-v_m)}{U_t+\eta} + \lambda\mu_t(v_k-v_m) \left(\frac{1}{E_j+\eta}-\frac{1}{E_m+\eta}\right). \]

This gradient is rational and exactly computable at every rational feasible point. No logarithm evaluation is needed to compute it.

Using \(0<\mu_t\leq1\), \(|a_{tj}-a_{tm}|\leq A\), and \(0\leq v_k-v_m\leq V\), we obtain

\[ \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{tjk}}\right| \leq \frac{V(A+2)}{\eta}. \]

Define the rational number

\[ G=\max\left\{1,\frac{nV(A+2)}{\eta}\right\}. \]

Then \(\|\nabla f(x)\|_2\leq G\) throughout \(K\). Integration along the line segment between any \(x,y\in K\) gives

\[ |f(y)-f(x)|\leq G\|y-x\|_2. \]

The encoding length of \(G\) is polynomial in the input length. Its numerical value may be exponentially large; the argument below depends logarithmically on that value.

4. A polynomial-bit ellipsoid argument

The standard algorithmic fact used

We use the rational central-cut ellipsoid procedure in the following form. Given rational \(c\), \(R\geq r>0\), it queries rational centers \(x\). At each query, supply a nonzero rational normal \(a\) and retain the halfspace \(\{y:a^\mathsf T(y-x)\geq0\}\). After \(O(n^2(1+\log(R/r)))\) queries, the intersection of the initial ball and retained halfspaces cannot contain a Euclidean ball of radius \(r\). The query points have polynomial encoding length; polynomial-time cut computations give polynomial total bit complexity. In dimension one, interval bisection supplies the same property.

This is the finite-volume form of the ellipsoid method, with its rational rounding implementation. The rounding qualification is essential here. See [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, *The ellipsoid method and its consequences in combinatorial optimization*, Section 2](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf).

A near-optimal ball

Set

\[ e=\min\{\epsilon,1\},\qquad \theta=\frac{e}{8Gn},\qquad r=\theta\rho_0=\frac{e}{16Gn m^2}. \]

All these numbers are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length. Also, \(0<\theta\leq1/8\).

Choose a maximizer \(x^*\in K\), and define

\[ z=(1-\theta)x^*+\theta c. \]

I claim that

\[ B(z,r)\subseteq\{y\in K:f(y)\geq f^*-e/4\}. \]

Indeed, if \(\|h\|_2\leq r\), then \(\|h/\theta\|_2\leq\rho_0\), so \(c+h/\theta\in K\). Convexity of \(K\) gives

\[ z+h=(1-\theta)x^*+\theta(c+h/\theta)\in K. \]

Furthermore, \(\|c-x^*\|_2\leq n\) and \(\rho_0\leq n\). Hence

\[ \|z+h-x^*\|_2 \leq\theta n+\theta\rho_0 \leq2\theta n. \]

The Lipschitz bound therefore yields

\[ f(z+h)\geq f^*-2G\theta n=f^*-e/4. \]

This proves the claim. The ball is needed only for the proof; the algorithm does not need to know \(x^*\) or \(z\).

The cuts and their correctness

Start the ellipsoid procedure with \(B(c,R)\), using the radius \(r\) above.

At each rational query point \(x\), proceed as follows.

  • If \(x\notin K\): find a violated inequality \(\ell(y)=a^\mathsf Ty+b\geq0\). Since \(\ell(x)<0\), every \(y\in K\) satisfies

\[ a^\mathsf T(y-x)=\ell(y)-\ell(x)>0. \]
Retain the corresponding central halfspace.

  • If \(x\in K\): save \(x\) and compute \(g=\nabla f(x)\) exactly. If \(g=0\), return \(x\): concavity implies \(f(y)\leq f(x)\) for all \(y\in K\). Otherwise retain

\[ \{y:g^\mathsf T(y-x)\geq0\}. \]

After the prescribed number of queries, at least one saved point satisfies

\[ f(x)\geq f^*-e/2. \]

For a contradiction, suppose every queried feasible point has \(f(x)<f^*-e/2\). Every infeasibility cut preserves \(K\), hence preserves \(B(z,r)\). At a feasible query point, concavity gives

\[ g^\mathsf T(y-x)\geq f(y)-f(x). \]

For every \(y\in B(z,r)\), the right-hand side is greater than \(e/4\). Thus every objective cut also preserves \(B(z,r)\). The intersection of all retained halfspaces with the initial ball would still contain that radius-\(r\) ball, contradicting the ellipsoid guarantee.

Bit-complexity checks

The iteration count depends on

\[ \log(R/r)= \log\left(\frac{16Gn m^2(n+1)}{e}\right). \]

This is polynomially bounded in the input length and in \(m,\tau\). Each infeasibility cut uses an explicit rational linear inequality. Each objective cut uses the rational gradient from Section 3, whose encoding length and computation time are polynomial in the input length and query-point length.

All hypotheses of the rational ellipsoid procedure are therefore satisfied. It produces a polynomial-size list of exactly feasible rational points, one of which has error at most \(e/2\).

5. Select a good point using certified logarithm approximations

For each saved point \(x\), compute a rational approximation \(\widehat f(x)\) satisfying

\[ |\widehat f(x)-f(x)|\leq e/4. \]

It suffices to approximate each individual logarithm to absolute error \(e/(4m)\), because the sum of their nonnegative coefficients is

\[ (1-\lambda)\sum_t\mu_t+\lambda m =(1-\lambda)+\lambda m\leq m. \]

For completeness, these approximations can be computed in polynomial bit complexity by an elementary series. Given a positive rational \(b\), write \(b=2^k u\) with integer \(k\) and \(1\leq u<2\). Then

\[ \log b=k\log2+\log u,\qquad \log u=2\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1},\qquad w=\frac{u-1}{u+1}\in[0,1/3). \]

The analogous series for \(\log2\) uses \(w=1/3\). After \(N\) terms, either series has error at most

\[ \frac{2(1/3)^{2N+1}}{1-1/9}. \]

Thus, to approximate \(\log b\) within \(\delta>0\), it suffices to approximate both \(\log u\) and \(\log2\) within \(\delta/(|k|+1)\). The required number of terms is polynomial in the encoding length of \(b\) and \(\log(1/\delta)\). Exact rational evaluation of those partial sums also has polynomial bit complexity.

Here the logarithm arguments are positive rationals of polynomial encoding length, so this construction applies.

Choose a saved point \(\bar x\) maximizing \(\widehat f(x)\). If \(x_0\) is a saved point with \(f(x_0)\geq f^*-e/2\), then

\[ f(\bar x) \geq\widehat f(\bar x)-e/4 \geq\widehat f(x_0)-e/4 \geq f(x_0)-e/2 \geq f^*-e \geq f^*-\epsilon. \]

Thus \(\bar x\) is exactly feasible and has the required additive guarantee.

6. Convert the matrices into explicit rational lotteries

Fix a rational doubly stochastic matrix \(P\). Initialize a residual matrix \(D=P\); all its row and column sums equal \(s=1\).

Whenever \(s>0\), form the bipartite graph whose edges are the positive entries of \(D\). This graph has a perfect matching. To check Hall’s condition, let \(S\) be any set of rows and \(N(S)\) its neighboring columns. Then

\[ s|S| =\sum_{j\in S,k}D_{jk} \leq\sum_{k\in N(S),j}D_{jk} =s|N(S)|. \]

Since \(s>0\), this gives \(|N(S)|\geq|S|\). A perfect matching can be found in polynomial time.

Let \(M\) be its permutation matrix, and set

\[ p=\min\{D_{jk}:M_{jk}=1\}>0. \]

Record \(M\) with probability \(p\), and replace \(D\) by \(D-pM\). The residual remains nonnegative, and every row and column sum becomes \(s-p\). At least one positive entry becomes zero, and no zero entry becomes positive.

Consequently, the procedure terminates after at most \(m^2\) iterations. At termination the residual is zero, and the recorded probabilities sum to \(1\). They give an exact decomposition of \(P\).

For the bit bound, let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the entries of \(P\). Its encoding length is at most the sum of their denominator encoding lengths, hence polynomial. Every residual entry and every recorded probability is an integer multiple of \(1/Q\), lying in \([0,1]\). All have polynomial encoding length.

Applying this procedure to every \(P_t(\bar x)\) gives the requested explicit lotteries. Their marginals equal these matrices exactly, so their objective value equals \(f(\bar x)\).

7. Why the supplied iteration bound does not settle the bit question

These numerical quantities can be exponentially large in their binary encoding lengths, so that rate alone does not establish the requested polynomial bit complexity. The proof above instead uses exact rational cuts, logarithmic dependence on the geometric accuracy parameters, and an exact polynomial-size lottery decomposition. It works directly with the \(\tau\) support types and rational masses; no expansion into a finite list of equally weighted users is required.
