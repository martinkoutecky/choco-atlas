The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m=2^k\) candidates, a target \(c^\star\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) supported complete rankings with binary rational masses, assume every candidate pair has a strict majority and the induced tournament makes \(c^\star\) a king with out-degree \(m/4\). Decide whether there is a balanced single-elimination seeding in which \(c^\star\) wins. The input is the ranking support and masses, with complexity measured in \(m\), \(\tau\), and their bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m=2^k\) candidates, with integer \(k\geq 2\), and let \(c^\star\in C\).
A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings \(R_i\) of \(C\), each with positive binary rational mass \(p_i\), where \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Define \(x\to y\) when \(\sum_{i:x\succ_{R_i}y}p_i>1/2\). Promise that every pair has a strict majority, so these edges form a tournament.
Promise that \(c^\star\) has exactly \(m/4\) outgoing edges and is a king: for every \(y\neq c^\star\), either \(c^\star\to y\) or there exists \(z\) with \(c^\star\to z\to y\).
A seeding assigns each candidate exactly once to the leaves of a complete binary tree of height \(k\); at each internal node, the candidate that beats the other child’s winner advances. The society and match outcomes remain fixed.

Is deciding whether some seeding makes \(c^\star\) win solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard, under these promises?

For an explicitly supplied tournament, this decision problem is NP-hard even when the target is a king with out-degree \(m/4\). [Kim and Williams](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/085.pdf)

Classification

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete under the stated promises. The key is that every explicitly supplied tournament can be represented by a rational society with support size \(\tau=O(m^2)\) and total encoding length \(L=O(m^3\log m)\). This representation preserves every match outcome, and therefore both promises and every seeding’s winner.

The construction below uses dyadic masses, so it works whether “binary rational” means a rational encoded in binary or a rational with a finite binary expansion.

1. Representing a tournament by a polynomial-size society

Let \(T\) be any tournament on the candidate set \(C\), with \(|C|=m\). Fix an ordering of \(C\).

For each edge \(a\to b\) of \(T\), list the remaining candidates in the fixed order as \(d_1,\ldots,d_{m-2}\), and introduce these two rankings:

\[ a\succ b\succ d_1\succ\cdots\succ d_{m-2}, \qquad d_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ d_1\succ a\succ b. \]

For an ordered pair \((x,y)\), define its *count margin* in a multiset of rankings as the number ranking \(x\) above \(y\), minus the number ranking \(y\) above \(x\).

The two rankings associated with \(a\to b\) contribute:

  • Margin \(2\) to \((a,b)\), because both rank \(a\) above \(b\).
  • Margin \(0\) between two remaining candidates, because their orders are reversed.
  • Margin \(0\) between either \(a\) or \(b\) and a remaining candidate, because the remaining candidate is below both in the first ranking and above both in the second.

Thus this pair of rankings contributes only to its designated edge.

There are \(q=2\binom{m}{2}=m(m-1)\) rankings, counted with multiplicity. For every edge \(x\to y\) of \(T\), the resulting count margin is exactly \(2\): its own gadget contributes \(2\), and every other gadget contributes \(0\).

Dyadic normalization

Let \(M\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(M\geq q\). Then \(q\leq M<2q\). Since \(m\geq4\), both \(q\) and \(M\) are even.

Choose any ranking \(Q\), and append \((M-q)/2\) copies of the pair consisting of \(Q\) and its reverse. Each appended pair contributes margin \(0\) to every candidate pair. The multiset now contains exactly \(M\) rankings, while every tournament edge still has count margin \(2\).

Give each occurrence mass \(1/M\). For any edge \(x\to y\) of \(T\), let \(N_{xy}\) count occurrences ranking \(x\) above \(y\). Completeness of the rankings and the margin calculation give

\[ N_{xy}+N_{yx}=M, \qquad N_{xy}-N_{yx}=2. \]

Consequently,

\[ \Pr[x\succ y]=\frac{N_{xy}}{M} =\frac12+\frac1M>\frac12. \]

The induced majority tournament is therefore exactly \(T\), with no ties.

Distinct support and encoding bounds

Merge identical rankings. If a distinct ranking \(R\) occurs \(n_R\) times, assign it mass \(p_R=n_R/M\). These masses are positive dyadic rationals, and

\[ \sum_R p_R=\frac{\sum_R n_R}{M}=1. \]

Merging preserves all pairwise probabilities. The output therefore has distinct support and satisfies

\[ \tau\leq M<2m(m-1)=O(m^2). \]

Using candidate labels of \(O(\log m)\) bits, each ranking needs \(O(m\log m)\) bits. Each mass needs \(O(\log m)\) bits because both its numerator and denominator are at most \(M=O(m^2)\). Hence

\[ L=O(\tau m\log m)=O(m^3\log m). \]

Generating the rankings and merging duplicates by comparison sorting takes \(O(m^3(\log m)^2)\) bit operations. Indeed, there are \(O(m^2)\) strings of length \(O(m\log m)\), and sorting uses \(O(m^2\log m)\) comparisons.

This proves a polynomial-time representation lemma without assuming that all possible rankings are supplied.

Use the stated explicit-tournament hardness theorem: deciding whether a target can win a balanced single-elimination tournament is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even when the target is a king of out-degree exactly \(m/4\). Kim and Williams state this as Theorem 1, and their preliminaries specify tournament sizes that are powers of two. [Kim and Williams, *Fixing Tournaments for Kings, Chokers, and More*](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/085.pdf)

Take such an instance \((T,c^\star)\), and apply the representation construction from Section 1. Keep the candidate set and target unchanged.

Because the constructed society induces exactly \(T\):

  • Every pair has a strict majority.
  • The target still has exactly \(m/4\) outgoing edges.
  • The target remains a king: every witnessing edge or two-edge path in \(T\) remains present.

For any particular seeding, its winner is also unchanged. To prove this, induct upward through the bracket. Each leaf contains the same candidate in both formulations. At an internal node, the induction hypothesis gives the same two child winners, and the preserved tournament edge gives the same match winner. Thus the root winner agrees.

It follows that

\[ c^\star\text{ wins some seeding in }T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c^\star\text{ wins some seeding for the constructed society}. \]

The construction has polynomial running time and output length, and always produces an instance satisfying the promises. It is therefore a polynomial-time reduction establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and verification time

A certificate lists the candidates at the leaves in their fixed left-to-right order. Its length is \(O(m\log m)\).

Pairwise majorities can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Write each input mass as \(p_i=a_i/b_i\), with positive integers encoded in binary, and set

\[ D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}b_i, \qquad w_i=a_i(D/b_i). \]

The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the denominator encoding lengths is at most \(L\). For a valid society, \(0<w_i\leq D\) and \(\sum_iw_i=D\).

After recording each candidate’s position in each ranking, determine every majority by the exact comparison

\[ 2\sum_{i:x\succ_{R_i}y}w_i>D. \]

Using elementary integer arithmetic, forming the common denominator and weights takes \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations. Computing all pairwise comparisons takes \(O(\tau m^2L)\) bit operations. Thus a sufficient preprocessing bound is

\[ O(\tau L^2+\tau m^2L). \]

The verifier then checks that the certificate is a permutation and evaluates the bracket using \(m-1\) tournament-table lookups.

The input validity conditions are also polynomial-time checkable. In particular, after computing the tournament, the degree and king promises require \(O(m^2)\) table lookups: for each candidate not beaten directly by \(c^\star\), inspect the out-neighbors of \(c^\star\) for a two-edge witness.

Hence the language that rejects invalid inputs and accepts valid promised inputs exactly when a winning seeding exists belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with Section 2, this proves the claimed completeness result. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
