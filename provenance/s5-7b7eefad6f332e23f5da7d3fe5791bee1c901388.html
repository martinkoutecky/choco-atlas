The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(p\), rational arrival shares \(a_0=1-p\lambda\) and \(a_j=\lambda\), density \(d\), horizon \(H\), and precision \(\varepsilon\), compute \(\varepsilon\)-approximations to each type’s and the total perished fraction under the Patient rule, starting from an empty pool in the sparse mass limit \(m\to\infty\), \(\alpha=d/m\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here the types are \(A_0,\ldots,A_p\), with \(p\geq1\), \(0<\lambda<1/p\), masses \(a_0=1-p\lambda\) and \(a_j=\lambda\), and \(\tau=p+1\); masses sum to \(1\).
Inputs also include binary rationals \(d>0\), \(H>0\), and \(0<\varepsilon<1\); \(L\) is the total input bit length. Time is measured in units of mean critical waiting time. In the finite model, arrivals are Poisson at rate \(m\), critical clocks are independent exponential clocks of rate \(1\), and permitted pairs have independent compatibility probability \(\alpha=d/m\).
Permitted pairs are \(A_0\) with any type and \(A_j\) with itself; distinct hard types are incompatible. Patient matches only when an agent becomes critical, choosing uniformly among compatible hard agents if any, otherwise uniformly among compatible easy agents; without a partner it perishes. Both matched agents leave.
Define the sparse continuous mass model by the following initial-value problem, where \(x\) is easy pool mass and \(y\) is pool mass of each hard type, normalized by \(m\): \(x(0)=y(0)=0\).
\(\dot x=a_0-x-x(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dpy}-py(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dy},\qquad \dot y=\lambda-y-y(1-e^{-dy})-\frac{x}{p}(1-e^{-dpy}).\)
The finite-horizon perished fractions are \(\ell_0=(a_0H)^{-1}\int_0^H x(t)e^{-d(x(t)+py(t))}\,dt\), \(\ell_j=(\lambda H)^{-1}\int_0^H y(t)e^{-d(x(t)+y(t))}\,dt\) for \(1\leq j\leq p\), and \(\ell=a_0\ell_0+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^p\ell_j\). Agents still waiting at \(H\) do not count as perished.
Compute rational approximations to all these fractions with absolute error at most \(\varepsilon\).

Is this approximation problem solvable deterministically in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it NP-hard? Here \(d\), \(H\), and \(\varepsilon\) are binary encoded; \(m\) occurs only in the limiting normalization and is not a finite input parameter.

For finite stochastic pools, Patient drift equations have been derived, without a computational complexity classification. Continuous ODE analysis gives uniqueness, preservation of hard-type symmetry, and stationary loss asymptotics, but no bit-complexity bound for this finite-horizon approximation problem. [Dai and He](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.09757v2).
No result settling this exact approximation problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted to p at least 1 and 0<lambda<1/p, retaining positive mass for every named type and avoiding the undefined bound 1/p at p=0.
  • Specified positive binary-rational density and horizon, binary-rational precision between 0 and 1, and absolute rather than relative approximation error.
  • Normalized arrivals to total mass rate 1 and pool sizes by m; fixed independent critical clocks of rate 1.
  • Defined the continuous object explicitly by the sparse limit of the symmetric Patient ODE, rather than requiring an unproved finite-horizon stochastic-to-fluid correspondence.
  • Used uniform selection among compatible agents as specified by the Patient algorithm. The unequal-pool formula elsewhere uses uniform selection among represented types; the explicit symmetric ODE avoids that discrepancy.
  • Defined perished fractions relative to arrivals through H and excluded agents still waiting at H, following the finite-horizon loss definition.
  • Required polynomial bit complexity with binary-encoded d and H; m is a limiting scale rather than an input to the continuous computation.

Result

The approximation problem is deterministically solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\).

The proof uses two different regimes. When \(d\) is moderately sized, a quantitative contraction bound allows a polynomial-length horizon truncation. When \(d\) is large, losses become negligible after an initial interval that has polynomial length under the time change \(u=dt\). In both regimes, a high-order Taylor method requires only polynomially many precision bits.

1. Aggregate variables and invariant bounds

Write
\[ a=1-p\lambda,\qquad b=p\lambda,\qquad z=py,\qquad \rho=\min(a,b). \]
Thus \(a,b,\rho>0\) and \(a+b=1\). The given IVP becomes
\[ \dot x=F(x,z) =a-x-x(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dz}-z(1-e^{-dx})e^{-dz/p}, \]
\[ \dot z=G(x,z) =b-z-z(1-e^{-dz/p})-x(1-e^{-dz}), \qquad x(0)=z(0)=0. \]

Define the normalized instantaneous loss rates
\[ g_0(x,z)=\frac{x}{a}e^{-d(x+z)}, \qquad g_1(x,z)=\frac{z}{b}e^{-d(x+z/p)}. \]
Then
\[ \ell_0=\frac1H\int_0^H g_0(x(t),z(t))\,dt, \qquad \ell_j=\ell_1=\frac1H\int_0^H g_1(x(t),z(t))\,dt \]
for every \(j\geq1\), and \(\ell=a\ell_0+b\ell_1\).

The rectangle
\[ \mathcal R=[0,a]\times[0,b] \]
is positively invariant. Indeed, \(F(0,z)=a>0\), \(G(x,0)=b>0\), \(F(a,z)\leq0\), and \(G(x,b)\leq0\). Since the vector field is analytic, local existence and uniqueness, together with this compact invariant rectangle, give a unique solution for every nonnegative time.

Moreover, \(F(x,z)\leq a-x\) and \(G(x,z)\leq b-z\), so
\[ x(t)\leq a(1-e^{-t}), \qquad z(t)\leq b(1-e^{-t}). \]
In particular, both normalized loss rates lie in \([0,1]\).

Let \(S=x+z\). Adding the equations gives the exact identity
\[ \dot S=1-2S+x e^{-dS}+z e^{-d(x+z/p)}. \]
Consequently, \(\dot S\geq1-2S\), and integration yields
\[ S(t)\geq\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2. \tag{1} \]

2. A quantitative contraction bound

We prove a contraction estimate valid for every \(d>0\). Its dependence on \(d\) will be used only when \(d\) has polynomial magnitude.

Put
\[ q=dx,\quad r=dz,\quad s=r/p,\quad A=1-e^{-q},\quad B=1-e^{-r}, \]
and
\[ k=q e^{-r},\qquad h=(1-s)e^{-s}, \qquad w=1-e^{-s}+s e^{-s}. \]
Direct differentiation gives
\[ F_x=-1-(A+q e^{-q})e^{-r}-r e^{-q}e^{-s}, \qquad F_z=A(k-h), \]
\[ G_x=-B,\qquad G_z=-1-w-k. \]

Define the unweighted column margins
\[ \delta_x=-F_x-|G_x|, \qquad \delta_z=-G_z-|F_z|. \]
For the first column,
\[ \delta_x=(1+A+q e^{-q})e^{-r}+r e^{-q}e^{-s} \geq e^{-r}=1-B. \tag{2} \]

We next show
\[ \delta_z\geq\frac1{4p}. \tag{3} \]

If \(k\geq h\), then
\[ \delta_z=1+w+(1-A)k+Ah. \]
Since \(w,k\geq0\), \(0\leq A\leq1\), and the minimum of \((1-s)e^{-s}\) on \(s\geq0\) is \(-e^{-2}\), this gives
\[ \delta_z\geq1-e^{-2}>\frac12. \]

If \(k<h\), necessarily \(0\leq s<1\), and
\[ \delta_z=1+w-Ah+(1+A)k. \]
When \(r\leq1\), using \(h\leq1\) gives
\[ \delta_z\geq1-A+k=e^{-q}+q e^{-r}. \]
For \(q\leq1\), the first term is at least \(e^{-1}\); for \(q\geq1\), the second term is at least \(e^{-1}\). Thus \(\delta_z\geq e^{-1}\).

When \(r\geq1\), instead use
\[ \delta_z\geq1-h\geq1-e^{-s} \geq1-e^{-1/p}\geq\frac1{2p}. \]
The last inequality follows from \(1-e^{-v}\geq v/2\) for \(0\leq v\leq1\). These cases prove (3).

On \(\mathcal R\), we also have
\[ |F_z|\leq k+|h|\leq q+1\leq d+1. \tag{4} \]

Now set
\[ \eta=\frac1{16p(d+1)},\qquad \theta=1-\eta, \]
and use the weighted norm
\[ \|(u,v)\|_\theta=|u|+\theta|v|. \]
Notice that \(\theta\geq15/16\).

The two weighted Jacobian column sums satisfy
\[ F_x+\theta|G_x| =-\delta_x-\eta B \leq-(1-B)-\eta B\leq-\eta, \]
and, by (3)–(4),
\[ G_z+\theta^{-1}|F_z| =-\delta_z+\frac{\eta}{\theta}|F_z| \leq-\frac1{4p}+\frac1{15p} =-\frac{11}{60p}\leq-\eta. \]

It follows that the flow \(\Phi_t\) contracts:
\[ \|\Phi_t(u)-\Phi_t(v)\|_\theta \leq e^{-\eta t}\|u-v\|_\theta \qquad (u,v\in\mathcal R). \tag{5} \]
For completeness, apply the mean-value formula to the difference of two solutions. The resulting averaged Jacobian retains the preceding column inequalities, because taking absolute values of its off-diagonal entries is bounded by averaging their absolute values. The upper right derivative of the weighted norm is therefore at most \(-\eta\) times that norm, and integration proves (5).

The diameter of \(\mathcal R\) in this norm is at most \(a+\theta b\leq1\). Writing \(X(t)=(x(t),z(t))\), autonomy and (5) consequently give
\[ \|X(t)-X(T)\|_\theta =\|\Phi_T(X(t-T))-\Phi_T(0)\|_\theta \leq e^{-\eta T} \qquad(t\geq T). \tag{6} \]

We also need a loss-rate Lipschitz bound. Differentiation gives
\[ |\partial_x g_0|\leq1/a+d,\quad |\partial_z g_0|\leq d,\quad |\partial_x g_1|\leq d,\quad |\partial_z g_1|\leq1/b+d/p. \]
Thus both functions are Lipschitz on \(\mathcal R\), in the weighted norm, with constant
\[ C=\frac{2(1+d)}{\rho}. \tag{7} \]
Combining (6)–(7),
\[ |g_i(X(t))-g_i(X(T))|\leq C e^{-\eta T} \qquad(t\geq T,\ i\in\{0,1\}). \tag{8} \]

3. Large density makes late losses negligible

Since \(p\geq1\),
\[ x+z/p\geq S/p, \]
and therefore, for both loss rates,
\[ g_i(X(t))\leq e^{-dS(t)/p}. \tag{9} \]

Let
\[ n=\left\lceil\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\right\rceil, \qquad K=n+4. \]
Both integers are computable by rational comparisons, and \(K=O(L)\).

Suppose
\[ d\geq4pK, \]
and define
\[ T_{\mathrm{large}}=\frac{2pK}{d}\leq\frac12. \]
For \(0\leq t\leq1/2\), (1) and \(1-e^{-2t}\geq t\) give \(S(t)\geq t/2\). The lower bound in (1) is increasing, so for every \(t\geq T_{\mathrm{large}}\),
\[ S(t)\geq T_{\mathrm{large}}/2=pK/d. \]
Equation (9) now implies
\[ g_i(X(t))\leq e^{-K}\leq2^{-K}\leq\varepsilon/16. \tag{10} \]

Thus discarding all loss contributions after \(T_{\mathrm{large}}\) causes an error at most \(\varepsilon/16\) in each finite-horizon fraction, regardless of how large \(H\) is.

4. The numerical step has polynomial bit complexity

We record the numerical fact needed below, including why it provides a bit-complexity bound.

Consider a fixed-dimensional IVP whose vector field is a fixed finite sum of products of polynomials and exponentials of affine functions, with rational coefficients. Suppose:

  • the integration interval and the coordinates of the real solution have polynomially bounded magnitude;
  • the coefficients have polynomial bit length;
  • on a complex neighborhood of radius \(r\) around a known box containing the solution, the vector field has norm at most \(M\) and its Jacobian has norm at most \(\Lambda\);
  • \(r^{-1}\), \(M\), and \(\Lambda\) have polynomial magnitude.

Then the terminal state can be approximated to coordinatewise error \(\delta\) in deterministic polynomial time, provided \(\log(1/\delta)\) is polynomially bounded.

Here is a justification. Complex Picard iteration, with Cauchy derivative bounds, provides a uniform analytic time radius
\[ R=\frac{r}{cM}, \]
where \(c\) is a fixed constant depending only on the dimension. Centers within distance \(r/4\) of the real solution box have the same guarantee. Using steps of length at most \(R/2\), the tail after Taylor order \(k\) is bounded by a fixed constant times \(r2^{-k}\).

Let \(N_{\mathrm{step}}\) be the number of steps and let
\[ \delta_0=\min(\delta,r/8). \]
Propagation of local errors is bounded by \(e^{\Lambda T}\). Hence it suffices to choose
\[ k=O\!\left( \Lambda T+ \log_2\!\frac{(N_{\mathrm{step}}+1)(1+r)}{\delta_0} +1\right) \]
and to make each rounding error smaller than
\[ \frac{\delta_0 e^{-\Lambda T}}{4N_{\mathrm{step}}}. \]
The required order and precision are polynomial. The resulting error bound also keeps all numerical centers inside the neighborhood used in the argument, closing the induction.

Taylor coefficients can be computed by polynomial-length arithmetic recurrences. In particular, if
\[ V(s)=\sum_{j\geq0}v_js^j,\qquad e^{V(s)}=\sum_{j\geq0}w_js^j, \]
then
\[ w_0=e^{v_0},\qquad w_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n jv_jw_{n-j}. \]
For the IVP itself, the coefficient of order \(n+1\) is the vector-field coefficient of order \(n\), divided by \(n+1\). A fixed number of such recurrences uses polynomially many arithmetic operations.

Cauchy bounds give coefficient magnitudes with polynomially bounded binary logarithms. The recurrences therefore also give polynomial bounds on the logarithms of intermediate magnitudes. A polynomial-length arithmetic circuit with those bounds can be evaluated with polynomially many guard bits: error amplification is bounded by the product of polynomially many factors, each having polynomially bounded logarithm. The constant exponentials can likewise be computed to the required precision by their Taylor series, since their arguments have polynomial magnitude.

This proves the stated numerical fact without a dependence polynomial in \(1/\delta\).

We check its hypotheses explicitly for the two IVPs used next.

For the original variables, augment the IVP with
\[ \dot J_0=x e^{-d(x+z)},\qquad \dot J_1=z e^{-d(x+z/p)},\qquad J_0(0)=J_1(0)=0. \tag{11} \]
Over an interval of length \(U\), the real solution lies in
\[ [0,a]\times[0,b]\times[0,U]^2. \]
Take the complex neighborhood radius
\[ r_{\mathrm{small}}=\frac1{8(1+d)}. \]
Every exponential appearing in the expanded vector field has modulus at most \(e^{1/4}<2\). Direct bounds on the terms and their derivatives give, in the maximum norm,
\[ M_{\mathrm{small}}\leq32,\qquad \Lambda_{\mathrm{small}}\leq128(1+d). \]
Consequently, if \(d\) and \(U\) have polynomial magnitude, the numerical fact applies.

For large \(d\), use \(u=dt\) and set
\[ Q(u)=d\,x(u/d),\qquad Z(u)=d\,z(u/d). \]
Their equations are
\[ Q'=a-\frac Qd-\frac Qd(1-e^{-Q})e^{-Z} -\frac Zd(1-e^{-Q})e^{-Z/p}, \]
\[ Z'=b-\frac Zd-\frac Zd(1-e^{-Z/p}) -\frac Qd(1-e^{-Z}). \]
Augment these with
\[ \widehat J_0'=Qe^{-(Q+Z)},\qquad \widehat J_1'=Ze^{-(Q+Z/p)}, \tag{12} \]
starting from zero.

For a rescaled interval of length \(V\), we have \(0\leq Q,Z\leq V\) and \(0\leq\widehat J_i\leq V^2\). On a complex neighborhood of radius \(1/8\), the exponentials again have modulus less than \(2\). Since the large-density regime has \(d>1\), direct termwise bounds give
\[ M_{\mathrm{large}}\leq32(V+1),\qquad \Lambda_{\mathrm{large}}\leq128(V+1). \]
Thus the numerical fact applies whenever \(V\) has polynomial magnitude, even if \(d\) has exponentially large magnitude.

5. The approximation algorithm

Use the common numerical tolerance
\[ \delta=\frac{\varepsilon\rho\min(H,1)}{256(1+d)}. \tag{13} \]
Its required precision is polynomial in the input length. Indeed, each positive input rational is at least \(2^{-O(L)}\), \(b=p\lambda\geq\lambda\), and \(d\leq2^{O(L)}\). Hence
\[ \log_2(1/\delta)=O(L+\log p). \]

Moderate density

Suppose \(d<4pK\). Choose an integer \(N\) satisfying
\[ C2^{-N}\leq\varepsilon/16, \]
and define
\[ T_{\mathrm{small}}=\frac N\eta=16p(d+1)N, \qquad U=\min(H,T_{\mathrm{small}}). \]
One can choose \(N=O(L+\log p)\) using exact rational comparisons. Moreover,
\[ T_{\mathrm{small}} \leq16p(4pK+1)N, \]
so \(d\) and the integration horizon \(U\) both have polynomial magnitude.

Integrate the augmented IVP (11) to time \(U\), approximating every terminal coordinate to error at most \(\delta\).

Set \(c_0=a\), \(c_1=b\). With exact terminal values, form
\[ R_i=\frac{J_i(U)}{c_iH} +\left(1-\frac UH\right)g_i(X(U)). \]
If \(U=H\), this is exactly \(\ell_i\). Otherwise, (8), together with \(e^{-N}\leq2^{-N}\), shows
\[ |R_i-\ell_i|\leq\varepsilon/16. \tag{14} \]

For numerical evaluation, project the approximated \(x,z\) coordinates onto \(\mathcal R\). Coordinatewise clipping does not increase their errors. Evaluate the resulting \(g_i\) to additional absolute error at most \(\varepsilon/64\).

The integral-coordinate error contributes at most
\[ \frac{\delta}{\rho H}\leq\varepsilon/256. \]
By (7), the state error contributes at most
\[ 2C\delta\leq\varepsilon/64. \]
Finally, round each output to a rational with rounding error at most \(\varepsilon/64\). Including (14) and the scalar evaluation error, the total error is less than \(\varepsilon/2\).

Large density

Suppose \(d\geq4pK\). Let
\[ U=\min(H,T_{\mathrm{large}}), \qquad V=dU=\min(dH,2pK). \]
Crucially,
\[ V\leq2pK, \]
which has polynomial magnitude.

Integrate the rescaled augmented IVP (12) to time \(V\), with coordinatewise error at most \(\delta\). The change of variables gives
\[ \int_0^U x(t)e^{-d(x(t)+z(t))}\,dt =\frac{\widehat J_0(V)}{d^2}, \]
and
\[ \int_0^U z(t)e^{-d(x(t)+z(t)/p)}\,dt =\frac{\widehat J_1(V)}{d^2}. \]
Therefore form
\[ R_i=\frac{\widehat J_i(V)}{c_iHd^2}. \]

The omitted tail contributes at most \(\varepsilon/16\), by (10). The numerical integral error contributes at most
\[ \frac{\delta}{\rho Hd^2}\leq\varepsilon/256. \]
Rounding to a rational with error at most \(\varepsilon/64\) again gives total error less than \(\varepsilon/2\).

6. Outputs and running time

Let the resulting approximations be \(\widetilde\ell_0,\widetilde\ell_1\), clipped to \([0,1]\). Clipping cannot increase their errors. Return \(\widetilde\ell_1\) for each of the \(p\) hard types, and return
\[ \widetilde\ell=a\widetilde\ell_0+b\widetilde\ell_1 \]
for the overall fraction. Because \(a+b=1\),
\[ |\widetilde\ell-\ell| \leq a|\widetilde\ell_0-\ell_0| +b|\widetilde\ell_1-\ell_1| <\varepsilon. \]

All rational comparisons and arithmetic have polynomial bit complexity. In the moderate-density regime, both \(d\) and the truncated horizon have polynomial magnitude. In the large-density regime, the rescaled horizon has polynomial magnitude and the large parameter appears only through the bounded coefficient \(1/d\). The numerical precision is polynomial in \(L+\log p\), and the numerical argument above establishes polynomial running time in both regimes.

Producing the repeated hard-type outputs adds only polynomial work in \(p=\tau-1\). Thus all requested fractions are approximable deterministically in \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) time. The parameter \(m\) plays no role in this algorithm.

There is one minor issue: the numerical lemma is stated more generally than its justification supports. The two IVPs actually used satisfy the additional bounds needed by that justification.

1. Reduction and invariant bounds

The substitution \(z=py\), \(a=1-p\lambda\), and \(b=p\lambda\) correctly produces the displayed aggregate equations. The loss-rate normalizations are also correct: \(y/\lambda=z/b\), and the overall fraction is \(a\ell_0+b\ell_1\).

Adding the equations gives the stated identity for \(S=x+z\). Thus \(S'\geq1-2S\), and the lower bound \(S(t)\geq(1-e^{-2t})/2\) is correct.

All four Jacobian entries are correct. In particular, differentiation of the last term of \(F\) produces the factor \(h=(1-s)e^{-s}\), including its possible negative sign.

The estimate \(|F_z|\leq d+1\), the choice of \(\theta\), and both weighted column inequalities check out. The supplied averaged-Jacobian argument justifies the contraction inequality, including at points where the norm is nondifferentiable.

The autonomy argument establishing (6) is valid for every \(t\geq T\); it does not require an unstated equilibrium result.

The loss-rate derivative bounds and the weighted Lipschitz constant \(C=2(1+d)/\rho\) are sufficient. Thus the tail variation bound (8) follows.

3. Large-density truncation

Because \(p\geq1\), both loss exponents are bounded below by \(dS/p\), while their prefactors are at most one. This proves (9).

The inequality \(1-e^{-2t}\geq t\) holds throughout \(0\leq t\leq1/2\). With \(T_{\mathrm{large}}=2pK/d\leq1/2\), the increasing lower bound on \(S\) therefore gives \(S(t)\geq pK/d\) for every later time.

The constants in (10) are correct: \(e^{-K}\leq2^{-K}\leq\varepsilon/16\). Since the omitted interval has length at most \(H\), its contribution to the normalized average is at most \(\varepsilon/16\), even when \(H\) is very large.

4. Numerical bit complexity

The local analytic-radius argument, geometric Taylor-tail bound, and global error amplification estimate have the required polynomial dependence in the two applications. The Taylor order absorbs \(\Lambda T\), so the argument does not incorrectly assume that \(e^{\Lambda T}\) has polynomial magnitude.

The displayed exponential coefficient recurrence is correct. With polynomial Taylor order, bounded intermediate magnitudes, and polynomial precision, it yields polynomially many bit operations.

The explicit application bounds are adequate:

  • For the original augmented IVP, the chosen complex neighborhood bounds every exponential individually. The constants \(32\) and \(128(1+d)\) comfortably bound the vector field and its Jacobian, respectively. The integral coordinates do not enter the vector field, so their potentially larger values do not invalidate these bounds.
  • For the rescaled IVP, \(Q,Z\leq V\), and the integral coordinates satisfy the stated \(V^2\) bound. The complex-neighborhood estimates have polynomial magnitude when \(V\) does. The large parameter enters through \(1/d\), whose magnitude is bounded and whose encoding length remains polynomial.

Minor issue — the general numerical lemma is overgeneralized. Its stated hypotheses do not imply that all constant exponential arguments have polynomial magnitude, or that intermediate expression values have polynomially bounded logarithms.

For example, the fixed expression \(e^D-e^D\), with binary-encoded \(D=2^k\), defines the zero vector field and satisfies polynomial bounds on the vector field and Jacobian. Nevertheless, its individual exponential arguments have exponential magnitude, and the individual intermediate values have binary logarithm proportional to \(D\). Thus the blanket claims about direct Taylor evaluation and intermediate magnitudes do not follow from the abstract hypotheses.

This does not invalidate the algorithm presented here: in both concrete applications, the exponential arguments have polynomial magnitude and the individual exponential factors are explicitly bounded.

Minimum clarification: restrict the numerical lemma to expressions satisfying those additional bounds, or explicitly limit its justification to the two augmented IVPs used in this proof. No new mathematical argument is needed for those applications.

5. Algorithm and error accounting

The tolerance \(\delta\) requires only polynomially many precision bits. Positivity of the rational inputs, including \(a=1-p\lambda\), supplies the required lower bounds despite possible subtraction in computing \(a\).

In the moderate-density regime, \(N\), \(d\), and the truncated horizon have polynomial magnitude. The replacement of the tail by the terminal loss rate has error at most \(\varepsilon/16\). Clipping the numerical state preserves its coordinatewise error bounds and permits application of the Lipschitz estimate.

The integral-coordinate, state, scalar-evaluation, and rounding errors satisfy the displayed bounds. Their sum is below \(\varepsilon/2\).

In the large-density regime, \(V\leq2pK\) has polynomial magnitude. Both change-of-variable identities contain the correct factor \(d^{-2}\). The integral error bound is valid because this regime has \(d>1\); together with truncation and rounding, it is below \(\varepsilon/2\).

These arguments cover very small \(H\), very small positive masses, \(p=1\), and equality at the density threshold.

6. Outputs and classification

Repeating the hard-type approximation produces every requested type fraction. Clipping preserves accuracy, and the convex combination with weights \(a,b\) preserves the error bound for the overall fraction.
