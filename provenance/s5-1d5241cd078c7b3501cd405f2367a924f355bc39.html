The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a fixed tie-breaking order, a rational society \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq C\), and a rational budget \(b\), decide whether there are nonnegative transfers \(x_{A,B}\) for \(A\) in the support of \(\mu\) and \(B\supseteq A\), with \(\sum_{B\supseteq A}x_{A,B}=\mu_A\), such that \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}|B\setminus A|\le b\) and the resulting society \(\nu_D=\sum_{A\subseteq D}x_{A,D}\) has \(R(\nu,k)\ne R(\mu,k)\), for \(R\in\{\text{GreedyCC},\text{GreedyPAV},\text{Phragmén}\}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge2\) candidates, let \(1\le k\le m\), and fix an input total order for breaking all ties. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) approval types \(A\subseteq C\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with \(\sum_A\mu_A=1\); empty approval types are allowed. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(b\ge0\) be a binary rational and \(L\) the total input bit length.
For any society \(\rho\), GreedyCC and GreedyPAV start with \(W=\varnothing\) and repeatedly add an unselected candidate maximizing \(\sum_{D\ni c}\rho_D\lambda(|D\cap W|+1)\), until \(|W|=k\); use \(\lambda(j)=\mathbf{1}_{j=1}\) and \(\lambda(j)=1/j\), respectively.
Sequential Phragmén starts with zero money; every unit of voter mass earns money at rate one. Advance time until an unselected candidate's approving mass jointly holds one unit of money, select that candidate, and reset all its approving mass's money to zero. Resolve simultaneous purchases by the fixed order, recomputing affordability after each purchase; if every remaining candidate has zero approving mass, fill the remaining seats by that order.
For each fixed \(R\in\{\mathrm{GreedyCC},\mathrm{GreedyPAV},\mathrm{Phragm\acute{e}n}\}\), define the decision problem by asking whether nonnegative real transfers \(x_{A,B}\), indexed by \(A\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) and \(A\subseteq B\subseteq C\), satisfy \(\sum_{B\supseteq A}x_{A,B}=\mu_A\) and \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}|B\setminus A|\le b\), while the resulting society \(\nu_D=\sum_{A\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu),\,A\subseteq D}x_{A,D}\) satisfies \(R(\nu,k)\ne R(\mu,k)\). The outcome is an unordered committee, and the same tie-breaking order is used throughout.

For each fixed rule \(R\), is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite lists of voters, deciding whether at most a given number of added approvals changes the committee is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for GreedyCC, GreedyPAV, and sequential Phragmén. [Verified discrete results](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10058-025-00385-1).
No result settling exactly the divisible-mass decision problem above was located in the literature search.

Result

For each of the three rules, the divisible-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. This holds even when \(k=m-1\) and all input masses and the budget are dyadic rationals.

The cases \(b=0\) and \(k=m\) always have answer no. The hardness concerns variable committee size.

The proof uses the exact-cover steering idea underlying the discrete reductions in [Faliszewski, Gawron, and Kusek](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10058-025-00385-1), but supplies explicit bounds against arbitrary real transfers. In particular, no integrality assumption about the transfers is used.

1. Preliminaries for the reductions

1.1. Source problem

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem Exact Cover by 3-Sets. An instance consists of a universe \(U\), with \(|U|=3n\), and a list \(S_1,\ldots,S_N\) of three-element subsets of \(U\). The question is whether some \(n\) sets partition \(U\).

We may assume \(n\ge1\) and \(N\ge n\). Instances with \(N<n\) can immediately be mapped to a fixed no-instance.

Introduce candidates

\(C=\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,p,d\}\),

where \(s_i\) corresponds to \(S_i\), and set

\(k=N+1=m-1\).

Fix the order \(s_1\succ\cdots\succ s_N\succ p\succ d\). Our decisive comparisons will be strict, so the relative order of \(p\) and \(d\) will not matter.

Write

\(E_u=\{s_i:u\in S_i\}\)

for each \(u\in U\).

1.2. Unnormalized masses

We describe societies using unnormalized dyadic weights \(w_A\). Once the nonempty types have been specified, choose a power of two \(Z\) exceeding their total weight by at least one, and assign the remaining weight to the empty type. Thus the total weight is \(Z\), and the empty type has weight at least one.

The actual input masses are \(\mu_A=w_A/Z\). If the unnormalized modification budget is \(\beta\), the input budget is \(b=\beta/Z\).

This normalization preserves the selected committee:

  • Greedy scores are all divided by \(Z\).
  • For Phragmén, multiplying every purchase time by \(Z\) gives precisely the process for the normalized masses.

Consequently, we can analyze weights \(w_A\), earning money at rate one per unit of weight, with purchase price one and modification budget \(\beta\).

Coincident approval types are merged by summing their weights. They may still be viewed as separate labeled blocks in the proof: any transfer from a merged type can be disaggregated among its blocks without changing the resulting society or its cost.

1.3. What the budget controls

Let \(y_{A,B}=Zx_{A,B}\) denote an unnormalized transfer. Every changed portion has \(B\ne A\), and therefore \(|B\setminus A|\ge1\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{A,B:\,B\ne A}y_{A,B}\le \sum_{A,B}y_{A,B}|B\setminus A|\le\beta.\)

Thus the total weight whose approval type changes is at most \(\beta\), even when transfers are arbitrary nonnegative reals.

We use two consequences.

First, for either greedy rule, at every fixed selected set \(W\), each candidate’s marginal score changes by at most \(\beta\). Indeed, every portion’s marginal-score coefficient lies in \([0,1]\), so

\(\displaystyle |\operatorname{score}_{\nu}(c\mid W)-\operatorname{score}_{w}(c\mid W)|\le\beta.\)

This compares the two societies at the same \(W\); their actual selection histories need not agree.

Second, for Phragmén:

  • A candidate’s total approving weight increases by at most \(\beta\).
  • At least \(a-\beta\) weight of an original block of weight \(a\) remains unchanged.
  • Every original approval is retained, including on changed portions.
  • At time \(t\), each unit of weight holds at most \(t\) money.

These statements follow directly from the transfer restrictions.

2. GreedyCC and GreedyPAV

We give one construction with parameters distinguishing the two rules.

Set

\(T=100(n+1)^2,\qquad \delta=\frac18,\qquad \beta=\frac1{128}.\)

For GreedyCC, set \(\alpha=1\) and \(\ell=0\). For GreedyPAV, set \(\alpha=\frac12\) and \(\ell=\frac{3n}{2}\).

Define

\(H_r=(N-\alpha r)T,\qquad Q=H_n+4n.\)

The nonempty approval blocks are:

| Approval type | Weight |
|---|---:|
| \(\{s_i\}\), for each \(i\) | \(T\) |
| \(\{s_i,s_j\}\), for each \(i<j\) | \(T\) |
| \(\{p,d\}\) | \(Q\) |
| \(E_u\cup\{d\}\), for each \(u\in U\) | \(1\) |
| \(\{p\}\) | \(\ell+\delta\) |

Complete the society with empty weight as in Section 1.2.

2.1. The first \(n\) choices are set candidates

Suppose that exactly \(r\) set candidates, and neither \(p\) nor \(d\), have been selected.

For an unselected set candidate, the singleton and pair blocks contribute exactly \(H_r\):

  • Under GreedyCC, its \(r\) pair blocks involving selected candidates contribute zero.
  • Under GreedyPAV, those \(r\) blocks contribute \(T/2\) each, while the other singleton and pair blocks contribute \(T\).

Its element blocks contribute between zero and three. Its reference score therefore lies in \([H_r,H_r+3]\).

Meanwhile, each of \(p,d\) has reference score at most \(Q+3n+\delta\). The score perturbation bound gives, for \(r<n\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{score}_{\nu}(s_i\mid W)\ge H_{n-1}-\beta=H_n+\alpha T-\beta,\)

whereas

\(\displaystyle \max\{\operatorname{score}_{\nu}(p\mid W),\operatorname{score}_{\nu}(d\mid W)\}\le H_n+7n+\delta+\beta.\)

The comparison is strict because

\(\alpha T\ge T/2=50(n+1)^2>7n+\delta+2\beta.\)

Thus every budget-feasible modification makes the first \(n\) choices set candidates.

Immediately afterwards, every remaining set candidate has score at most \(H_n+3+\beta\). Each of \(p,d\) has score at least \(Q-\beta\), and

\(Q-\beta-(H_n+3+\beta)=4n-3-2\beta>0.\)

Therefore the next choice is \(p\) or \(d\). If no set candidates remain, this conclusion is immediate.

2.2. A noncover forces the choice of \(d\)

Let \(\mathcal T\) be the first \(n\) selected sets, and define

\(r_u=|\{S_i\in\mathcal T:u\in S_i\}|.\)

Because each selected set has three elements,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{u\in U}r_u=3n.\)

At this point, the reference score of \(p\) is \(Q+\ell+\delta\).

For GreedyCC, the reference score of \(d\) is

\(Q+|\{u:r_u=0\}|.\)

If \(\mathcal T\) is not an exact cover, some element is uncovered: otherwise \(3n\) incidences covering \(3n\) elements would cover each element exactly once. Thus \(d\)’s element contribution is at least one.

For GreedyPAV, that contribution is

\(\displaystyle g=\sum_{u\in U}\frac1{r_u+1}.\)

We claim that \(g=3n/2\) for an exact cover and \(g\ge3n/2+1/3\) otherwise.

To prove the latter bound, let

\(\displaystyle a=\sum_{u\in U}\max(r_u-1,0).\)

There are \(3n-a\) covered elements. Starting from contribution one per element, the first incidence on an element decreases its contribution by \(1/2\), while every subsequent incidence decreases it by at most \(1/6\), since

\(\displaystyle \frac1j-\frac1{j+1}=\frac1{j(j+1)}\le\frac16\quad(j\ge2).\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle 3n-g\le\frac{3n-a}{2}+\frac a6=\frac{3n}{2}-\frac a3.\)

For a noncover, \(a\ge1\), proving the claim.

Thus, under either rule, a noncover gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{score}_{w}(d\mid W)-\operatorname{score}_{w}(p\mid W)\ge\frac13-\delta.\)

After arbitrary feasible transfers, the difference remains positive because

\(\frac13-\delta-2\beta>0.\)

Hence a noncover forces \(d\).

2.3. The remaining choices are all remaining set candidates

Once \(p\) or \(d\) is chosen, the other candidate’s reference score is bounded by

\(\displaystyle 3n+\delta\)

for GreedyCC, and by

\(\displaystyle Q/2+3n+\delta\)

for GreedyPAV.

These bounds remain valid after further set selections, because greedy marginal scores are nonincreasing as the selected set grows.

For an unselected set candidate, its singleton and pair blocks always contribute at least \(H_{N-1}\). Therefore its modified score is at least \(H_{N-1}-\beta\).

For GreedyCC,

\(H_{N-1}=T>3n+\delta+2\beta.\)

For GreedyPAV,

\(\displaystyle H_{N-1}-(Q/2+3n+\delta)=\left(\frac n4+\frac12\right)T-5n-\delta>2\beta.\)

Thus the loser among \(p,d\) cannot enter before all remaining set candidates. Every feasible outcome is consequently either

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,p\}\)

or

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,d\}.\)

If the source instance has no exact cover, Section 2.2 forces the latter outcome under every feasible transfer.

2.4. An exact cover can be steered into the first \(n\) positions

Suppose that \(\mathcal H\) is an exact cover. Put \(\varepsilon=\beta/n\). For every set candidate in \(\mathcal H\), transfer \(\varepsilon\) empty weight to its singleton approval type.

The total cost is \(n\varepsilon=\beta\).

Inductively, suppose that all choices so far are members of \(\mathcal H\). Every unselected member of \(\mathcal H\) has three elements uncovered by those choices. Its element contribution is therefore three under either rule, and its added singleton weight contributes \(\varepsilon\).

Every other set candidate has element contribution at most three and receives no added weight. Their singleton and pair contributions are the same \(H_r\). Thus an unselected cover candidate strictly beats every noncover candidate. Section 2.1 ensures that no special candidate intervenes.

The first \(n\) selected sets are therefore exactly \(\mathcal H\). At the next step, the scores of \(d,p\) are respectively \(Q+\ell\) and \(Q+\ell+\delta\): these transfers do not affect either special candidate’s score. Hence \(p\) is selected, and Section 2.3 gives the committee

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,p\}.\)

This proves the required separation for both greedy rules.

3. Phragmén

Here we use a different society, with no pair blocks.

Let \(z\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(z\ge1000n\), and define

\(\displaystyle \gamma=\frac1z,\quad h=3n\gamma,\quad \eta=\frac\gamma2,\quad \beta=\frac\gamma{128}.\)

Also set

\(T=100(n+1)^2,\qquad M=T+3,\qquad Q=M-2h.\)

In particular,

\(\displaystyle 0<h\le\frac3{1000},\qquad 0<\beta\le\frac1{128000},\qquad T\ge400.\)

The nonempty approval blocks are:

| Approval type | Weight |
|---|---:|
| \(\{s_i\}\), for each \(i\) | \(T\) |
| \(E_u\), for each \(u\in U\) | \(1-\gamma\) |
| \(E_u\cup\{d\}\), for each \(u\in U\) | \(\gamma\) |
| \(\{p,d\}\) | \(Q\) |
| \(\{p\}\) | \(\eta\) |

Again, add empty weight and normalize.

Every set candidate has original approving weight \(M\). Candidates \(d,p\) have original approving weights \(Q+h=M-h\) and \(Q+\eta\), respectively.

For an arbitrary feasible transfer, define the time bounds

\(\displaystyle a_0=\frac1{M+\beta},\qquad B_0=\frac1{Q-\beta},\qquad C_0=a_0+\frac1{(M+\beta)^2},\qquad D_0=\frac1{T-\beta}.\)

3.1. An early set purchase cannot overlap an earlier set purchase

All candidates have approving weight at most \(M+\beta\), and money holdings are at most time times approving weight. Thus the first purchase occurs no earlier than \(a_0\).

Before either \(p\) or \(d\) is purchased, at least \(Q-\beta\) unchanged weight approving exactly \(\{p,d\}\) keeps all its money. Therefore the first purchase of \(p\) or \(d\) occurs by \(B_0\). The process cannot already have filled the committee using only set candidates, because there are only \(N\) of them and \(k=N+1\).

Suppose that \(s_j\) is purchased after an overlapping set candidate \(s_i\). Choose \(u\in S_i\cap S_j\). The entire original element block for \(u\), of weight one, still approves both candidates, even after transfers. It was therefore reset at the purchase of \(s_i\), at a time at least \(a_0\).

At any subsequent time \(t\), candidate \(s_j\)’s approving money is consequently at most

\((M+\beta)t-a_0.\)

It cannot be purchased before \(C_0\).

We now verify \(B_0<C_0\). This inequality is equivalent to

\((2h+2\beta)(M+\beta)<M-2h-\beta.\)

Our parameter bounds give \(2h+2\beta<1/100\), \(\beta<1\), and \(2h+\beta<1\). Since \(M\ge403\),

\(\displaystyle (2h+2\beta)(M+\beta)<\frac{M+1}{100}<M-1<M-2h-\beta.\)

Also \(B_0<D_0\), because \(Q-\beta=T+3-2h-\beta>T-\beta\).

It follows that all set candidates selected before the first purchase of \(p\) or \(d\) correspond to pairwise disjoint sets. In particular, there are at most \(n\) such purchases.

3.2. Without an exact cover, \(d\) is purchased first

Assume the source instance has no exact cover. By Section 3.1, the sets selected before the first special candidate are pairwise disjoint and leave some element \(u\) uncovered.

Suppose, for contradiction, that \(p\) is the first special candidate purchased, at time \(t\). Its approving weight is at most \(Q+\eta+\beta\), so

\(\displaystyle t\ge P_0:=\frac1{Q+\eta+\beta}.\)

At least \(Q-\beta\) unchanged weight from the shared \(\{p,d\}\) block has never been reset.

Moreover, at least \(\gamma-\beta\) unchanged weight from the \(d\)-approving block for the uncovered element \(u\) has never been reset: none of its original set candidates has been selected, and \(d\) has not been selected.

Thus, just before the proposed purchase of \(p\), candidate \(d\)’s approving money is at least

\(\displaystyle (Q+\gamma-2\beta)t\ge\frac{Q+\gamma-2\beta}{Q+\eta+\beta}>1.\)

The strict inequality follows from

\(\gamma-\eta-3\beta=\gamma(1/2-3/128)>0.\)

This is impossible under the specified Phragmén process. Immediately before any purchase, every remaining candidate holds at most one unit of approving money: time advances only to the first affordability event, and purchases can only decrease other candidates’ holdings. This invariant also covers simultaneous purchases with recomputation.

Therefore \(d\) is the first special candidate.

3.3. The other special candidate cannot enter the committee

This claim holds for every feasible transfer, regardless of whether an exact cover exists.

Let the first special purchase occur at time \(s\), where \(a_0\le s\le B_0<D_0\). The entire original shared block of weight \(Q\) approves both special candidates and is reset at \(s\), including its changed portions.

The other special candidate has approving weight at most \(Q+h+\beta\), because \(\eta\le h\). Its money at any subsequent time \(t\le D_0\) is therefore at most

\(\displaystyle (Q+h+\beta)D_0-Qa_0.\)

Using \(Q=M-2h\) and \(M=T+3\), this is at most

\(\displaystyle \frac{3+2\beta}{T-\beta}+\frac{2h+\beta}{M+\beta}<\frac4{T-1}+\frac1M\le\frac4{399}+\frac1{403}<1.\)

Thus the other special candidate cannot be purchased by \(D_0\).

Meanwhile, each unselected set candidate has at least \(T-\beta\) unchanged singleton weight. This weight has never been reset and holds at least one unit at \(D_0\). Consequently, every set candidate is purchased by \(D_0\), unless the committee has already filled.

There is room for precisely all \(N\) set candidates and one special candidate. Hence every feasible outcome is

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,p\}\)

or

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,d\},\)

according to which special candidate was purchased first.

Together with Section 3.2, this proves that a no-instance of exact cover permits no committee change.

3.4. An exact cover makes \(p\) the first special candidate

Suppose \(\mathcal H\) is an exact cover. As before, transfer \(\varepsilon=\beta/n\) empty weight to the singleton type of each cover candidate.

Each cover candidate now has approving weight \(M+\varepsilon\); all other set candidates have approving weight \(M\). The two special candidates have approving weights strictly below \(M\).

The first purchases therefore occur at

\(\displaystyle A_\varepsilon=\frac1{M+\varepsilon}.\)

The cover candidates have disjoint approving populations: their sets are disjoint, their original singleton blocks are separate, and their added singleton portions are separate. All \(n\) cover candidates are thus purchased at \(A_\varepsilon\), with each remaining cover candidate still affordable after every preceding purchase.

Every other set overlaps a purchased cover candidate. Section 3.1 prevents its purchase before \(C_0>B_0\).

Put

\(\displaystyle B_p=\frac1{Q+\eta}<B_0.\)

Until a special purchase, \(p\) holds exactly \((Q+\eta)t\) money, so it reaches affordability at \(B_p\).

All element blocks were reset at \(A_\varepsilon\). Candidate \(d\)’s money at \(B_p\), provided neither special candidate has yet been selected, is

\(\displaystyle QB_p+h(B_p-A_\varepsilon).\)

This is less than one precisely when

\(\displaystyle h\left(1-\frac{A_\varepsilon}{B_p}\right)<\eta.\)

The left side satisfies

\(\displaystyle h\left(1-\frac{A_\varepsilon}{B_p}\right)=h\frac{2h-\eta+\varepsilon}{M+\varepsilon}<\frac{3h^2}{M},\)

because \(\varepsilon\le\beta<h\).

Since \(\eta=h/(6n)\), it suffices that \(18nh<M\). This holds because

\(\displaystyle 18nh\le\frac{54n}{1000}<100(n+1)^2<M.\)

Therefore \(d\) remains unaffordable through \(B_p\). No other set candidate can intervene, and \(p\) is purchased at \(B_p\). Section 3.3 then gives the committee

\(\{s_1,\ldots,s_N,p\}.\)

4. Completing the many-one reductions

For each fixed rule, construct its society and compute its unmodified committee.

The proofs above show that this committee contains all set candidates and exactly one of \(p,d\).

If it contains \(p\), the source instance has an exact cover:

  • For the greedy rules, Section 2.2 implies that the first \(n\) selected sets form an exact cover.
  • For Phragmén, Sections 3.2–3.3 exclude an outcome containing \(p\) when no exact cover exists.

In this case output a fixed yes-instance: two candidates \(a,z\), committee size one, order \(a\succ z\), empty approval mass one, and budget \(1/2\). Transferring mass \(1/2\) from the empty type to \(\{z\}\) changes the winner from \(a\) to \(z\) under every rule.

Otherwise, output the constructed normalized society and budget.

If the source instance has an exact cover, the transfers in Section 2.4 or Section 3.4 change its committee from all set candidates plus \(d\) to all set candidates plus \(p\).

If the source instance has no exact cover, every feasible real transfer leaves the committee equal to all set candidates plus \(d\).

Thus each reduction preserves both yes- and no-instances.

5. Encoding and running time of the reductions

The greedy constructions have at most \(O(N^2+n)\) approval types. The Phragmén construction has at most \(O(N+n)\) types. Each type is explicitly represented as a subset of the \(N+2\) candidates.

All specified weights are dyadic rationals with polynomial bit length. The normalization factor \(Z\) can be chosen with polynomial bit length, and both the normalized masses and the budget remain dyadic rationals. Merging coincident types only decreases support size.

The unmodified committee is computable in polynomial time. For the greedy rules this follows by directly evaluating their rational marginal scores.

For Phragmén, if approval type \(A\) was last reset at time \(r_A\), a positive-support candidate \(c\) next reaches affordability at

\(\displaystyle t_c=\frac{1+\sum_{A\ni c}w_A r_A}{\sum_{A\ni c}w_A}.\)

Compute these times, select the earliest candidate using the fixed order, and update reset times. There are at most \(k\) purchases. The weights and candidate approval totals are fixed rationals of polynomial bit length; at each purchase, a common denominator for the reset times can be updated by multiplication by integers of polynomial bit length. Therefore the bit length grows by only a polynomial amount over the \(k\) steps, and exact simulation is polynomial-time.

Hence all three constructions are polynomial-time many-one reductions.

Conclusion

The stated divisible-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for each of GreedyCC, GreedyPAV, and sequential Phragmén, already for \(k=m-1\) and dyadic input data. The no-instance arguments cover all nonnegative real transfers, including arbitrary splitting and additions of multiple approvals to the same portion of mass.

1. Scope and source problem

The output addresses the exact divisible-mass problem: its soundness arguments quantify over arbitrary nonnegative real transfers, and its successful modifications are admissible transfers from the empty type.

The source problem is stated precisely enough: Exact Cover by 3-Sets asks whether \(n\) members of a listed family of three-element sets partition a universe of size \(3n\). Its standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is the only external complexity theorem needed.

The cited [discrete robustness paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10058-025-00385-1) provides context, but the output does not rely on its discrete hardness theorem to establish continuous hardness.

Restricting to \(n\ge1\) is harmless. Instances with \(N<n\) cannot have an exact cover, and mapping them to a fixed no-instance is legitimate. The preliminary observation that budget zero always gives a no-instance supplies such instances.

2. Normalization and control of arbitrary transfers

The normalization argument is correct:

  • Dividing all weights by \(Z\) divides greedy scores uniformly.
  • Multiplying Phragmén purchase times by \(Z\) preserves every approving population’s money and all simultaneous affordability events.
  • The correspondence \(y_{A,B}=Zx_{A,B}\) scales both mass constraints and approval-addition costs correctly.

Disaggregation of merged approval types is valid because their constituent blocks have identical original approvals. This bookkeeping imposes no restriction on the resulting society.

The changed-weight bound is correct. Every changed portion incurs at least one added approval, so its total weight is at most \(\beta\). This applies even when a portion receives multiple approvals.

For either greedy rule, at a fixed selected set \(W\), the marginal contribution per unit of mass lies in \([0,1]\). Consequently, the absolute score perturbation is at most \(\beta\). Importantly, the output correctly compares scores at the same \(W\), rather than assuming identical election histories.

The Phragmén consequences also follow from the transfer constraints: original approvals remain, unchanged weight in a block is at least its original weight minus \(\beta\), and money per unit of weight never exceeds elapsed time.

3. GreedyCC and GreedyPAV: forced selection phases

The singleton and pair-block calculation is correct. After \(r\) set candidates have been selected, their contribution to each remaining set candidate is exactly

\[ H_r=(N-\alpha r)T. \]

Each set candidate belongs to exactly three element blocks, so its additional reference contribution lies between zero and three.

The bound \(Q+3n+\delta\) on both special candidates is valid for both rules. The stated inequality

\[ \alpha T>7n+\delta+2\beta \]

holds throughout the specified range \(n\ge1\). Thus, even after arbitrary feasible transfers, the first \(n\) selections must be set candidates.

At that point, the separation between special candidates and remaining set candidates is also correct:

\[ 4n-3-2\beta>0. \]

This covers \(n=1\). When \(N=n\), no set candidates remain, so the stated exception is immediate.

4. Greedy rules: exact-cover discrimination
