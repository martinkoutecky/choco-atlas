The continuous mirror question, as graded

For rational \(d,\rho\) in Proposition 2’s parameter regime and \(\tau\) policy cohorts at \(z_j=(j-\tfrac12)/\tau\), with mass \(\rho/\tau\) on type \((z_j,+d)\) and \((1-\rho)/\tau\) on type \((z_j,-d)\), characterize all pure- and mixed-strategy Nash equilibria. Candidates choose platforms in \([0,1]\); each type votes using the paper’s utility rule, and a candidate’s payoff is \(1\), \(0\), or \(1/2\) according to whether their vote mass is above, below, or exactly half.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(d,\rho\) be binary rationals with \(0<d\leq\tfrac14\) and \(\tfrac12+2d\leq\rho\leq1\), and let \(k\geq1\) be an integer. At \(z_j=(j-\tfrac12)/k\), assign mass \(\rho/k\) to type \((z_j,d)\) and mass \((1-\rho)/k\) to type \((z_j,-d)\), for \(1\leq j\leq k\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\); omit zero-mass types. Thus \(\tau=2k\) when \(\rho<1\), and \(\tau=k\) when \(\rho=1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length and \(m=2\) the number of candidates.
The society is common knowledge. Candidates \(A,B\) simultaneously choose \(x,y\in[0,1]\). Type \((z,s)\) has utilities \(s-(z-x)^2\) for \(A\) and \(-(z-y)^2\) for \(B\); its mass votes for the higher-utility candidate and splits equally upon indifference.
Each candidate receives payoff \(1\), \(0\), or \(\tfrac12\) when its vote mass is respectively greater than, less than, or equal to \(\tfrac12\). Mixed strategies are arbitrary Borel probability measures on \([0,1]\), independently sampled, with expected payoffs. There is no sampled-electorate size parameter.

What are all pure- and mixed-strategy Nash equilibria of this game? Can their exact characterization be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this characterization problem NP-hard?

For an odd finite electorate with independent uniform ideal points and independent image signs, the known result states that, when \(0<d<\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac12+2d<\rho\leq1\), and \(n\geq[4d(2\rho-1)]^{-1}\), the unique equilibrium has \(A\) at \(\tfrac12\) and \(B\) mixing equally between \(\tfrac12\pm\sqrt{d(2\rho-1)}\). Partial solution

The full characterization remains open. The following results are proved below:

  • All pure equilibria, and all mixed equilibria whenever pure equilibria exist, admit a polynomial-time exact characterization.
  • When $\rho=1$, all mixed equilibria admit an explicit characterization, including in the regime without pure equilibria.
  • A further regime with $\rho<1$ is resolved: if

$$ k=2,\qquad \frac{3-2\sqrt2}{4}\leq d<\frac1{16}, $$
the value to $A$ is $3/4$, and each candidate has an equilibrium strategy with two locations. Explicit measure inequalities characterize all equilibria in this regime.

  • In every remaining regime without pure equilibria, any equilibrium must have both candidates randomizing and must give $A$ a payoff strictly greater than $1/2$ and at most $3/4$.

Explicitly listing a finite equilibrium can require exponentially many locations. This establishes an output-size obstruction, but does not establish NP-hardness or exclude a polynomial-time succinct characterization.

1. Vote masses and a midpoint guarantee

Define
$$ \phi(t)= \begin{cases} 0,&t<0,\\ \frac12,&t=0,\\ 1,&t>0. \end{cases} $$

The utility difference for type $(z,s)$ is
$$ D_{z,s}(x,y)=s+(y-z)^2-(x-z)^2 =s+(y-x)(x+y-2z). $$

Thus $A$’s vote mass is
$$ V(x,y)=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^k \left[ \rho\,\phi(D_{z_j,d}(x,y)) +(1-\rho)\,\phi(D_{z_j,-d}(x,y)) \right]. $$
The second term may be retained formally when $\rho=1$; it then has zero coefficient.

Write $u(x,y)$ for $A$’s election payoff:
$$ u(x,y)= \begin{cases} 1,&V(x,y)>\frac12,\\ \frac12,&V(x,y)=\frac12,\\ 0,&V(x,y)<\frac12. \end{cases} $$
These functions are Borel measurable. Candidate $B$’s payoff is $1-u(x,y)$, so the game is constant sum.

Since $d>0$ and $\rho>1/2$,
$$ V(x,x)=\rho>\frac12,\qquad u(x,x)=1. $$

Define the grid distribution function with half weight at equality:
$$ C_k(t)=\frac{|\{j:z_j<t\}|}{k} +\frac{|\{j:z_j=t\}|}{2k}. $$
Reflection of the grid gives
$$ C_k(1-t)=1-C_k(t). $$

For $y>x$, put $t=y-x$. The positive- and negative-image voting thresholds are
$$ \alpha=\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{d}{2t}, \qquad \beta=\frac{x+y}{2}-\frac{d}{2t}, $$
and
$$ V(x,y)=\rho C_k(\alpha)+(1-\rho)C_k(\beta). $$

Proposition 1: the midpoint never loses

For every admissible instance,
$$ V\left(\frac12,y\right)\geq\frac12 \qquad\text{for all }y\in[0,1]. $$
If $k$ is odd, the inequality is always strict.

Proof. The case $y=1/2$ was established above. Simultaneous reflection of both locations preserves vote masses, so it suffices to consider $y>1/2$.

Writing $t=y-1/2>0$ gives
$$ \alpha=\frac12+\frac{t+d/t}{2}, \qquad \beta=\frac12+\frac{t-d/t}{2}. $$
Consequently, $\alpha>1/2$ and $\alpha+\beta=1+t>1$. Hence
$$ C_k(\alpha)\geq C_k(1-\beta)=1-C_k(\beta). $$

If $C_k(\beta)<1/2$, then
$$ V\left(\frac12,y\right) \geq \rho-(2\rho-1)C_k(\beta) =\frac12+(2\rho-1)\left(\frac12-C_k(\beta)\right) >\frac12. $$
If $C_k(\beta)\geq1/2$, monotonicity and $\alpha>\beta$ imply
$$ V\left(\frac12,y\right)\geq\frac12. $$
Equality in this second case requires
$$ C_k(\alpha)=C_k(\beta)=\frac12. $$
Conversely, these two equalities give $V=1/2$.

For odd $k$, $\alpha>1/2$ implies $C_k(\alpha)>1/2$, excluding equality. $\square$

For even $k$, set
$$ h=\frac1{2k},\qquad a=\frac12-h,\qquad b=\frac12+h. $$
The distribution function equals $1/2$ exactly on the open interval $(a,b)$. Thus midpoint ties occur exactly when
$$ t+\frac dt<\frac1k, $$
or equivalently,
$$ t^2-\frac tk+d<0. $$

It follows that the midpoint wins against every location if
$$ d\geq\frac1{4k^2}. $$
If $d<1/(4k^2)$, define
$$ t_\pm=\frac{1/k\pm\sqrt{1/k^2-4d}}2. $$
The midpoint ties exactly when
$$ t_-<\left|y-\frac12\right|<t_+, $$
and wins otherwise.

2. All pure equilibria and the regimes with value one

Define the universally winning set
$$ W=\{x\in[0,1]:u(x,y)=1\text{ for every }y\in[0,1]\}. $$

Proposition 2: complete pure-equilibrium characterization

The pure equilibria are exactly
$$ W\times[0,1]. $$
Furthermore,
$$ W\neq\varnothing \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\text{ is odd, or }d\geq\frac1{4k^2}. $$

Proof. At a pure equilibrium $(x,y)$, candidate $A$ can deviate to $y$ and obtain payoff $1$. Thus its equilibrium payoff must be $1$. Candidate $B$ then has no profitable deviation exactly when $u(x,y')=1$ for every $y'$, which means $x\in W$. Conversely, any $x\in W$ gives $A$ its maximum payoff and $B$ zero against every deviation.

Proposition 1 proves nonemptiness in the stated cases.

Suppose $k$ is even and $d<h^2$. If $x\geq1/2$, let $B$ choose $a$. At the central ideal point $a$,
$$ D_{a,d}(x,a)=d-(x-a)^2\leq d-h^2<0. $$
Since $x>a$, the function $D_{z,d}(x,a)$ increases with $z$. Therefore every positive-image type with $z\leq a$ strictly votes for $B$. Its negative-image counterpart also does, because its utility difference is smaller. These ideal points carry total mass $1/2$, so
$$ u(x,a)\leq\frac12. $$
For $x\leq1/2$, reflection gives $u(x,b)\leq1/2$. Hence $W$ is empty. $\square$

Proposition 3: polynomial-time computation of $W$

The set $W$ is a union of intervals and isolated points with rational or quadratic algebraic endpoints. It can be computed exactly in time polynomial in $\tau,L$.

Proof. For each positive-mass type $i$, use its indifference curve
$$ D_i(x,y)=s_i+(y-z_i)^2-(x-z_i)^2=0. $$

Construct a finite set $E$ of critical $x$-coordinates, including $0,1$ and:

  • Multiple roots in $y$, satisfying

$$ y=z_i,\qquad (x-z_i)^2=s_i. $$

  • Roots at a domain endpoint $e\in\{0,1\}$, satisfying

$$ (x-z_i)^2=s_i+(e-z_i)^2. $$

  • Intersections of distinct indifference curves.

For intersections,
$$ D_i-D_\ell=s_i-s_\ell+2(z_i-z_\ell)(x-y). $$
Equal signs at distinct ideal points would force $x=y$, where neither curve vanishes. Opposite signs at the same ideal point cannot intersect.

In the remaining case, define
$$ c=-\frac{s_i-s_\ell}{2(z_i-z_\ell)}\neq0. $$
The intersection has
$$ x-y=c,\qquad x=z_i+\frac{s_i+c^2}{2c}, $$
so its coordinates are rational.

There are $O(\tau^2)$ critical coordinates. On each open interval between consecutive coordinates:

  • roots in $y$ are simple and distinct;
  • none crosses $0$ or $1$;
  • their order is fixed;
  • voting signs in every intervening cell are fixed.

Consequently, membership in $W$ is constant on each such interval.

Test one sample from each open interval and every critical coordinate. For each sample, partition $[0,1]$ using all indifference roots. Evaluate every root, both domain endpoints, and one point in every nonempty open cell, including the outer cells. If there are no roots, test the whole open interval and its endpoints. At coincident roots, evaluate all indifferent types together.

These tests determine whether every payoff is $1$.

All algebraic numbers involved have bounded degree and polynomial coefficient bit length. The standard fixed-degree real-algebraic algorithms isolate and compare such numbers in polynomial bit time. Their hypotheses apply here: critical coordinates have degree at most two, subsequent quadratic roots have degree at most four, and the number of operations is polynomial. Root separation also permits rational interval samples with polynomial bit length. Thus the entire computation is polynomial. $\square$

This testing procedure includes the outer cells omitted in the previous algorithm.

Proposition 4: all mixed equilibria when $W\neq\varnothing$

For arbitrary Borel probability measures $P,Q$,
$$ (P,Q)\text{ is an equilibrium} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(W)=1. $$
There is no restriction on $Q$.

Proof. A location in $W$ guarantees payoff $1$. Hence every equilibrium has payoff $1$ to $A$, and
$$ \int u(x,y)\,dP(x)=1 \qquad\text{for every }y. $$

We prove that this forces $P(W)=1$, taking care of the uncountably many possible deviations.

Consider an unsafe point $x\notin E$, where $E$ is the finite critical set above. Choose a witness $y$ with $V(x,y)\leq1/2$.

If no type is indifferent, voting signs persist on a neighborhood of $x$. Thus there is an open neighborhood $O$ and a fixed witness $y$ for which $u(x',y)\leq1/2$ throughout $O$.

Otherwise, exactly one type is indifferent. Its root is simple, lies inside the domain, and is isolated. The vote mass at the root is the average of the vote masses on its two sides. At least one side therefore has vote mass at most $1/2$. Move the witness slightly into that side, then apply the preceding neighborhood argument.

Each such neighborhood has $P$-mass zero, since positive mass would make the expected payoff against its fixed witness less than $1$. Second countability supplies a countable subcover of the unsafe points outside $E$, so their total mass is zero. Every unsafe point of $E$ also has zero mass, by its individual witness. Therefore $P(W)=1$.

Conversely, $P(W)=1$ gives payoff $1$ against every pure or mixed strategy of $B$, proving equilibrium. $\square$

Thus all equilibria are characterized in polynomial time when $k$ is odd or $d\geq1/(4k^2)$.

3. All mixed equilibria when $\rho=1$

Only even $k$ with $d<h^2$ remains to consider here. Put
$$ C=[a,b],\qquad D=b-a=\frac1k,\qquad q=\sqrt d. $$

3.1 Central voters and projection

When $\rho=1$, for $x<y$:

  • $A$ wins if $D_{b,d}(x,y)\geq0$;
  • $A$ loses if $D_{a,d}(x,y)\leq0$;
  • otherwise the election ties.

Indeed, the utility difference decreases with the ideal point. At indifference of the voter at $b$, the left half votes strictly for $A$, with an additional half-voter mass at $b$; this is a strict majority. At indifference of the voter at $a$, $A$ has less than half the mass. For $x>y$, exchange $a$ and $b$.

Let $\pi$ be projection onto $C$. Then
$$ u(\pi(x),y)\geq u(x,y), \qquad u(x,\pi(y))\leq u(x,y). $$

To verify projection to $a$, suppose first that $x<a$. Replacing $x$ by $a$ gives a win whenever $y\leq a$. For $y>a$, it guarantees at least a tie and increases the utility difference at $b$, preserving every previous win.

Next suppose $y<a$. If $x\leq y$ or $x\geq a$, moving $y$ to $a$ decreases both central utility differences and preserves their ordering. If $y<x<a$, the original payoff is already $1$, so the replacement cannot increase it. Reflection verifies projection to $b$.

These inequalities allow both security guarantees and pure-deviation checks to be restricted to $C$.

3.2 An interval-covering game

For $x\in C$, define
$$ l(x)=a+\sqrt{\max\{(x-a)^2-d,0\}}, $$
$$ r(x)=b-\sqrt{\max\{(b-x)^2-d,0\}}, \qquad I(x)=[l(x),r(x)]. $$

For $x,y\in C$,
$$ u(x,y)=\frac12+\frac12\mathbf1_{\{y\in I(x)\}}. $$
For example, when $y<x$, a win is equivalent to
$$ d+(y-a)^2-(x-a)^2\geq0, $$
which is $y\geq l(x)$. The other direction gives $y\leq r(x)$. Within $C$ there are no losses.

Both endpoints are continuous and nondecreasing, and
$$ l(x)\leq x\leq r(x). $$

Define
$$ F(t)=r\left(\min\{b,a+\sqrt{(t-a)^2+d}\}\right) =\max\{r(x):l(x)\leq t,\ x\in C\}. $$
Starting from $t_0=a$, let
$$ t_i=F(t_{i-1}),\qquad N=\min\{i\geq1:t_i=b\}. $$

The integer $N$ is finite. For $t<b$, the maximizing location exceeds $t$, so $F(t)>t$. Moreover, $F$ is continuous and equals $b$ throughout a neighborhood of $b$. If the increasing iterates never reached $b$, their limit would be a fixed point. The only possible fixed point is $b$, but convergence to $b$ would eventually place an iterate in the terminal neighborhood, a contradiction.

Theorem 5: value and finite equilibrium construction

The value to $A$ is
$$ v=\frac12+\frac1{2N}. $$
Each candidate has an equilibrium strategy with $N$ locations.

Proof. For $i=1,\ldots,N$, choose
$$ x_i=\min\{b,a+\sqrt{(t_{i-1}-a)^2+d}\}. $$
Then $l(x_i)\leq t_{i-1}$ and $r(x_i)=t_i$. The intervals $I(x_i)$ cover $C$, so uniform mixing over the $x_i$ guarantees winning probability at least $1/N$.

For a matching upper bound, choose
$$ a=y_1<y_2<\cdots<y_N<b, \qquad y_{i+1}>F(y_i). $$
Such points exist. Set recursively $y_{i+1}=F(y_i)+\varepsilon$. At $\varepsilon=0$, the last point is $t_{N-1}<b$; continuity permits a sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$.

No interval $I(x)$ contains two selected points. If it contained $y_i$ and a later point, then
$$ r(x)\leq F(y_i)<y_{i+1}, $$
a contradiction. Uniform mixing over the $y_i$ therefore holds winning probability to at most $1/N$.

Projection extends both guarantees to the original action space. $\square$

3.3 Characterization of every Borel equilibrium

For $x\in C$, set
$$ \alpha(x)=\sqrt{\max\{(x-a)^2-d,0\}}, \qquad \beta(x)=\sqrt{\max\{(b-x)^2-d,0\}}, $$
and
$$ H(x)= \{y\in[0,1]: y\leq a-\alpha(x) \text{ or } a+\alpha(x)\leq y\leq b-\beta(x) \text{ or } y\geq b+\beta(x)\}. $$
The central-voter conditions give
$$ u(x,y)=\frac12+\frac12\mathbf1_{H(x)}(y) \qquad(x\in C). $$

For $y\in C$, define
$$ p(y)=\sqrt{d+(y-a)^2}, \qquad q(y)=\sqrt{d+(b-y)^2}, $$
$$ J(y)=[b-q(y),a+p(y)]\cap[0,1], $$
$$ R(y)=\{x\in[0,1]:x\leq a-p(y)\text{ or }x\geq b+q(y)\}. $$

These are respectively the winning and losing locations for $A$. For $x<y$, the winning condition is $D_{b,d}\geq0$, yielding $x\geq b-q(y)$; for $x>y$, it is $D_{a,d}\geq0$, yielding $x\leq a+p(y)$. The losing conditions give the two outer branches of $R(y)$. Hence
$$ u(x,y)=\frac12+\frac12\mathbf1_{J(y)}(x) -\frac12\mathbf1_{R(y)}(x). $$

Therefore arbitrary Borel strategies $P,Q$ form an equilibrium exactly when
$$ P(J(y))-P(R(y))\geq\frac1N \qquad\text{for every }y\in C, $$
and
$$ Q(H(x))\leq\frac1N \qquad\text{for every }x\in C. $$

Necessity follows from the equilibrium security guarantees at the established value. Conversely, these inequalities give matching guarantees, and projection makes the checks on $C$ sufficient for every deviation.

3.4 Exponentially large finite supports

Every finitely supported equilibrium strategy of either candidate has at least $N$ locations, and
$$ N\geq\frac{D}{2\sqrt d}=\frac1{2k\sqrt d}. $$

Indeed,
$$ x-l(x)\leq\sqrt d,\qquad r(x)-x\leq\sqrt d. $$
Thus each covering interval has length at most $2\sqrt d$, proving the displayed lower bound.

For $A$, each of the $N$ packing points must have positive coverage probability. Since one interval cannot cover two packing points, at least $N$ support locations are necessary.

For $B$, optimality gives $Q(I(x_i))\leq1/N$ for the $N$ covering intervals. Since they cover $C$,
$$ 1\leq\sum_{i=1}^N Q(I(x_i))\leq1. $$
Every interval consequently has mass $1/N$, and their overlaps have zero mass. A finite support therefore requires at least $N$ points. Projection preserves optimality and cannot increase finite support size, so these conclusions hold for strategies on the full action space.

Taking
$$ k=2,\qquad \rho=1,\qquad d=2^{-2r},\qquad r\geq3, $$
gives $\tau=2$, input length $O(r)$, and
$$ N\geq2^{r-2}. $$
An explicit finite-support equilibrium list therefore cannot always be produced in polynomial time.

4. A further resolved mixed regime with $\rho<1$

Theorem 6: two-location equilibria for a parameter band

Suppose
$$ k=2,\qquad \frac{3-2\sqrt2}{4}\leq d<\frac1{16}. $$
For every admissible $\rho$, including $\rho<1$, the value to $A$ is $3/4$.

An equilibrium is
$$ P_0=\frac12\delta_{\,1/4+\sqrt d} +\frac12\delta_{\,3/4-\sqrt d}, \qquad Q_0=\frac12\delta_{\,1/4} +\frac12\delta_{\,3/4}. $$

Proof. Write
$$ a=\frac14,\qquad b=\frac34,\qquad D=b-a=\frac12,\qquad c=\frac12,\qquad q=\sqrt d. $$

We first prove that every location in
$$ S=\left[c-\frac{d}{2D},c+\frac{d}{2D}\right] =[c-d,c+d] $$
never loses, even when negative-image types are present.

Take $x\in S$ and $x<y\leq b$. Put
$$ t=y-x,\qquad u=x-a,\qquad v=b-x. $$
The positive-image type at $a$ strictly votes for $A$, and the negative-image type at $b$ strictly votes for $B$. The other two utility differences are
$$ D_{b,d}=d-t(2v-t), \qquad D_{a,-d}=-d+t(2u+t). $$

If $D_{b,d}>0$, both positive-image types vote for $A$, so $V\geq\rho>1/2$.

If $D_{a,-d}>0$, both types at $a$ vote for $A$, so $V\geq1/2$.

It remains to examine the possibility that both displayed differences are nonpositive. Their inequalities imply
$$ t(2v-t)\geq d\geq t(2u+t), $$
hence
$$ t\leq v-u=2(c-x). $$
This is impossible if $x\geq c$. Otherwise write $\varepsilon=c-x$, so
$$ 0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{d}{2D}. $$
Since $t\leq2\varepsilon\leq v$ and $t(2v-t)$ increases on $[0,v]$,
$$ d\leq t(2v-t) \leq2\varepsilon(2v-2\varepsilon) =2D\varepsilon \leq d. $$
Every inequality is therefore an equality. Both types are indifferent, and
$$ V=\frac{3\rho}{4}+\frac{1-\rho}{4} =\frac14+\frac{\rho}{2}>\frac12. $$
Thus $A$ never loses for $x<y\leq b$. Reflection proves the corresponding assertion for $a\leq y<x$.

For $y\leq a$, both types at $b$ strictly vote for $A$. To check the negative-image type, use $d<D^2/4$ and
$$ b-x\leq\frac D2+\frac{d}{2D}<\frac{5D}{8}. $$
Then
$$ D_{b,-d}(x,y) \geq-d+D^2-(b-x)^2 > D^2-\frac{D^2}{4}-\frac{25D^2}{64} =\frac{23D^2}{64}>0. $$
The positive-image type has a larger difference. Hence $V\geq1/2$. Reflection handles $y\geq b$. This proves the nonlosing assertion on the full action space.

For $x\in S$ and $y\in[a,b]$, the preceding calculation also shows that the winning set is exactly
$$ I(x)= \left[ a+\sqrt{\max\{(x-a)^2-d,0\}}, \ b-\sqrt{\max\{(b-x)^2-d,0\}} \right]. $$
At an endpoint of this interval, the election is a strict win: either the other central type strictly votes for $A$, or the simultaneous-indifference calculation above applies.

Now let
$$ x_L=a+q,\qquad x_R=b-q. $$
The condition that both belong to $S$ is
$$ \frac D2-q\leq\frac{q^2}{2D}, $$
equivalently,
$$ q\geq D(\sqrt2-1). $$
Its square is precisely the assumed lower bound on $d$.

Their winning intervals inside $[a,b]$ are
$$ I(x_L)= \left[a,b-\sqrt{D^2-2Dq}\right], $$
$$ I(x_R)= \left[a+\sqrt{D^2-2Dq},b\right]. $$
They cover $[a,b]$ if
$$ q\geq\frac{3D}{8}. $$
This follows from $q\geq D(\sqrt2-1)$, since $\sqrt2-1>3/8$.

For $y\leq a$, location $x_L$ wins: both types at $b$ strictly vote for $A$, and
$$ D_{a,d}(x_L,y)=(y-a)^2\geq0. $$
Its vote mass is therefore at least $1/2+\rho/4$. Similarly, $x_R$ wins for $y\geq b$.

Against every $y$, both locations are nonlosing and at least one wins. Thus $P_0$ guarantees payoff at least $3/4$.

Conversely, the proof of Proposition 2 shows that
$$ x\geq c\Longrightarrow u(x,a)\leq\frac12, \qquad x\leq c\Longrightarrow u(x,b)\leq\frac12. $$
The other payoff is at most $1$. Hence
$$ \frac{u(x,a)+u(x,b)}2\leq\frac34 \qquad\text{for every }x. $$
So $Q_0$ guarantees the matching upper bound. The two strategies form an equilibrium. $\square$

All equilibria in this band

Here is an explicit measure characterization that includes strategies placing mass outside the central interval.

For $z\in\{1/4,3/4\}$, put
$$ T_z(x,y)=(y-z)^2-(x-z)^2, $$
and define
$$ G(x,y)= \rho\sum_z\phi(d+T_z(x,y)) +(1-\rho)\sum_z\phi(-d+T_z(x,y)). $$
Thus $V(x,y)=G(x,y)/2$.

Define
$$ X_+(y)=\{x:G(x,y)>1\},\qquad X_-(y)=\{x:G(x,y)<1\}, $$
$$ Y_+(x)=\{y:G(x,y)>1\},\qquad Y_-(x)=\{y:G(x,y)<1\}. $$
For each fixed argument, these are finite unions of intervals and points, obtained by evaluating the four quadratic indifference equations.

Arbitrary Borel strategies $P,Q$ form an equilibrium exactly when
$$ P(X_+(y))-P(X_-(y))\geq\frac12 \qquad\text{for every }y\in[0,1], $$
and
$$ Q(Y_+(x))-Q(Y_-(x))\leq\frac12 \qquad\text{for every }x\in[0,1]. $$

To verify this, use
$$ u(x,y)=\frac12+ \frac12\mathbf1_{\{G(x,y)>1\}} -\frac12\mathbf1_{\{G(x,y)<1\}}. $$
The inequalities say exactly that $P$ guarantees at least $3/4$ and $Q$ guarantees at most $3/4$. Their necessity follows from the established value, and their sufficiency follows from the matching guarantees. No unknown value or equilibrium parameter appears in this characterization.

5. Bounds throughout the unresolved regime

The following conclusions do not assume a minimax theorem or equilibrium existence.

Proposition 7: a reciprocal payoff inequality

For all locations,
$$ u(x,y)+u(y,x)\geq1. $$
Moreover, if $|x-y|\leq d/2$, both payoffs equal $1$.

Proof. For a fixed ideal point $z$, write
$$ T=(y-z)^2-(x-z)^2. $$
Then
$$ H=\phi(d+T)+\phi(d-T)\geq1. $$
Using $\phi(-r)=1-\phi(r)$, the sum of this ideal point’s contributions to $V(x,y)$ and $V(y,x)$ is
$$ \frac1k\left[2(1-\rho)+(2\rho-1)H\right]. $$
Summing gives $V(x,y)+V(y,x)\geq1$. Two election payoffs whose vote masses sum to at least one have payoff sum at least one.

If $|x-y|\leq d/2$, then, since every ideal point lies strictly between $0$ and $1$,
$$ |T|=|x-y|\,|x+y-2z|<d. $$
Every positive-image type votes strictly for $A$ and every negative-image type votes strictly for $B$. Thus $V(x,y)=\rho>1/2$, and the same holds after exchanging the locations. $\square$

Proposition 8: equilibrium payoff bounds and necessary randomization

Suppose $k$ is even and $d<1/(4k^2)$. Let
$$ R=\left\lceil\frac2d\right\rceil. $$
If an equilibrium exists, its payoff $v$ to $A$ satisfies
$$ \frac12+\frac1{2R}\leq v\leq\frac34. $$
In particular, neither candidate can use a pure strategy.

Proof. The equal mixture over $a,b$ used in Theorem 6 gives the upper bound for every even $k$: the entire left half votes for $B$ at $y=a$ when $x\geq1/2$, and the reflected statement holds at $y=b$ when $x\leq1/2$.

For the lower bound, let $Q$ be any Borel probability measure. Partition $[0,1]$ into $R$ Borel intervals of diameter at most $1/R\leq d/2$, and denote their masses by $q_1,\ldots,q_R$.

Independent draws from $Q$ fall in the same interval with probability
$$ \sum_{i=1}^R q_i^2\geq\frac1R, $$
by Cauchy–Schwarz. Proposition 7 therefore gives
$$ \int\!\!\int u(x,y)\,dQ(x)\,dQ(y) =\frac12\int\!\!\int [u(x,y)+u(y,x)]\,dQ(x)\,dQ(y) \geq\frac12+\frac1{2R}. $$

At equilibrium, candidate $A$ may deviate to the measure $Q$ used by $B$, proving the lower bound.

If $A$ were pure, the appropriate central deviation of $B$ would hold its payoff to at most $1/2$, contradicting this lower bound. If $B$ were pure, $A$ could choose the same location and obtain $1$, contradicting the upper bound. $\square$

The lower-bound argument also proves that every $Q$ admits an $A$ deviation with payoff at least $1/2+1/(2R)$. It does not by itself produce one $A$ strategy guaranteeing that amount against every $Q$; such an interchange would require an additional argument.

6. Further computational facts

Only polynomially many payoff phases in $\rho$

For fixed $k,d,x,y$, define
$$ p=2\sum_j\phi(D_{z_j,d}(x,y)), \qquad q=2\sum_j\phi(D_{z_j,-d}(x,y)). $$
These are integers between $0$ and $2k$, with $p\geq q$, because $D_{z_j,d}>D_{z_j,-d}$.

The majority comparison is
$$ V(x,y)\mathrel{\gtrless}\frac12 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q+(p-q)\rho\mathrel{\gtrless}k. $$
When $p=q$, it is independent of $\rho$. Otherwise its only threshold is
$$ \rho=\frac{k-q}{p-q}. $$

There are $O(k^2)$ possible thresholds. Partitioning $(1/2,1]$ into these threshold points and the intervening open intervals gives $O(k^2)$ phases. Throughout each open phase, the entire payoff function is identical, including its tie outcomes. The thresholds have $O(\log k)$ bit length and can be generated and sorted in polynomial time.

This reduces dependence on $\rho$, but does not solve the mixed-strategy problem within a phase.

Exact verification of a listed rational equilibrium is polynomial

Suppose finite strategies with rational locations and rational probabilities are supplied.

For each location $y_j$ of $B$ and each type $i$, possible changes in $A$’s payoff occur at
$$ x=z_i\pm\sqrt{s_i+(y_j-z_i)^2}, $$
when the radicand is nonnegative. Their union partitions $[0,1]$ into polynomially many cells relative to the listed support size. The expected payoff against the supplied $Q$ is constant on each open cell. Testing every root, every open cell and both domain endpoints computes the maximum deviation payoff exactly.

Similarly, deviations of $B$ are partitioned by
$$ y=z_i\pm\sqrt{(x_j-z_i)^2-s_i}. $$
Testing all cells computes the minimum expected payoff against $P$.

The algebraic degrees are bounded and the coefficient sizes are polynomial in the supplied description. Thus verification is polynomial in that description’s bit length. This does not bound the length of an equilibrium description produced from the original input.

The pure-equilibrium question is settled completely. Mixed equilibria are characterized when $W\neq\varnothing$, when $\rho=1$, and in the additional $k=2$ band of Theorem 6.
