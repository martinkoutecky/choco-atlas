The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), an explicitly supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings \(\mathcal L(C)\), target \(p\), and rational \(\beta\in[0,1]\), decide whether there is a mass-transfer vector \(x_{\rho,\sigma}\ge0\) with \(\sum_\sigma x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\mu_\rho\) and \(\sum_{\rho,\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\beta\) such that the extended \(E_1\) rule makes \(p\) a winner; in the search version, output such an \(x\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a polynomial-time recognizable set \(B\subseteq\mathrm{SAT}\) for which no polynomial-time function finds satisfying assignments for every member. Such sets exist if \(\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}\). Fix a formula encoding with at most \(\max\{0,\lfloor |F|/2\rfloor-1\}\) distinct variables in any encoded formula \(F\), ordered by first occurrence.
Candidates \(C\) have distinct binary-string names; let \(m=|C|\ge1\). Define \(F(C)\) by sorting names lexicographically and concatenating the last bit of each nonempty name, omitting the empty name.
Assign bit \(1\) to candidates of odd position in that sorting and bit \(0\) to those of even position. For a strict ranking \(\sigma\in\mathcal L(C)\), read its \(k=\max\{0,\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1\}\) least-preferred candidates from worst upward to obtain a bitstring \(a(\sigma)\).
A ranking qualifies if \(F(C)\in B\) and the first \(d\) bits of \(a(\sigma)\) satisfy \(F(C)\), where \(d\) is its number of variables. Extended \(E_1\) makes a candidate a winner exactly when some qualifying ranking with positive mass ranks that candidate first; multiple winners and an empty winner set are allowed.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) rankings with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\sum_\rho\mu_\rho=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including candidate names.
Given this society, \(p\in C\), and binary rational \(\beta\in[0,1]\), allow rational transfers \(x_{\rho,\sigma}\ge0\) from original support rankings to arbitrary \(\sigma\in\mathcal L(C)\), with \(\sum_\sigma x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\mu_\rho\) and \(\sum_{\rho,\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le\beta\).
Set \(\nu_\sigma=\mu_\sigma-\sum_\eta x_{\sigma,\eta}+\sum_\rho x_{\rho,\sigma}\), extending missing masses and transfer rows by zero. The decision problem asks whether \(p\) wins under \(E_1(C,\nu)\); the search problem seeks such transfers, represented by their nonzero entries.

Is this continuous constructive-bribery decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete constructive bribery for \(E_1\) has polynomial-time winner and decision algorithms, but its search problem is outside \(\mathrm{FP}\) under \(\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}\); this is conditional search hardness, not decision \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. [Verified source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.6641v2).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The unspecified extension of E1 treats a ranking as an effective ballot exactly when its mass is strictly positive, preserving the discrete existential winner condition.
  • The distribution is normalized to total mass one; the budget measures total mass whose ranking is reset.
  • Candidate names are distinct binary strings, including the possible empty string, and their lengths count toward L.
  • The ballot encoding is read from least preferred upward, keeping the most-preferred candidate outside the encoding region as intended by the discrete construction.
  • The set B and formula encoding are fixed parts of the rule, rather than instance inputs; variables are ordered by first occurrence.
  • Transfers are rational, may create rankings absent from the original support, and search witnesses list only nonzero transfer entries.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The continuous constructive-bribery decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

More precisely, writing \(W(C,\mu,p)\) for the assertion that \(p\) already wins, an instance is feasible exactly when
\[ F(C)\in B \quad\text{and}\quad \bigl(\beta>0\ \text{or}\ W(C,\mu,p)\bigr). \]
The existence of a qualifying target ranking can be proved without finding its encoded satisfying assignment.

1. Every assignment can be encoded with \(p\) first

Let \(c_1,\ldots,c_m\) be the candidates in lexicographic order, and let \(b(c_i)\) be \(1\) for odd \(i\) and \(0\) for even \(i\). Set \(k=\max\{0,\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1\}\).

Lemma. For every \(p\in C\) and every bitstring \(u\in\{0,1\}^k\), there is a ranking \(\sigma\) that ranks \(p\) first and has \(a(\sigma)=u\).

Proof. If \(k=0\), any ranking with \(p\) first works.

Suppose \(k>0\). Before removing \(p\), there are \(\lceil m/2\rceil\) candidates labeled \(1\) and \(\lfloor m/2\rfloor\) candidates labeled \(0\). Removing \(p\) removes at most one candidate of either label. Thus \(C\setminus\{p\}\) contains at least \(k\) candidates of each label, because
\[ \lceil m/2\rceil-1\ge k, \qquad \lfloor m/2\rfloor-1=k. \]

The string \(u\) requires at most \(k\) candidates of either label. We can therefore choose distinct candidates \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\) from \(C\setminus\{p\}\) with \(b(z_i)=u_i\).

Rank \(p\) first, then all remaining unchosen candidates in any order, then \(z_k,\ldots,z_1\). Reading the bottom \(k\) candidates from worst upward gives \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\), and hence \(a(\sigma)=u\). This proves the lemma.

Corollary. A qualifying ranking with \(p\) first exists if and only if \(F(C)\in B\).

Proof. Qualification requires \(F(C)\in B\), proving necessity.

For sufficiency, suppose \(F(C)\in B\). Since \(B\subseteq\mathrm{SAT}\), the formula has a satisfying assignment \(s\). Let \(n=|F(C)|\), and let \(d\) be its number of distinct variables. Each candidate contributes at most one bit, so \(n\le m\). The stipulated encoding bound gives
\[ d\le \max\{0,\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\} \le \max\{0,\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1\}=k. \]

Write \(s\) in the prescribed first-occurrence variable order and append arbitrary bits to obtain a string \(u\) of length \(k\). By the lemma, some ranking \(\sigma\) has \(p\) first and \(a(\sigma)=u\). Its first \(d\) bits satisfy \(F(C)\), so it qualifies. This also covers \(d=0\): the satisfying assignment is then the empty string.

2. Exact feasibility criterion

Theorem. Successful transfers exist if and only if \(F(C)\in B\) and either \(\beta>0\) or \(p\) already wins.

Proof.

If \(F(C)\notin B\), no ranking qualifies. Transfers change masses but leave \(C\), and therefore \(F(C)\), unchanged. Consequently, \(p\) cannot become a winner.

If \(\beta=0\), nonnegativity together with \(\sum_{\rho,\sigma}x_{\rho,\sigma}\le0\) forces every transfer to be zero. The resulting society equals the original society, so success is equivalent to \(p\) already winning.

It remains to prove sufficiency when \(F(C)\in B\) and \(\beta>0\). If \(p\) already wins, use the empty transfer list. Otherwise, the corollary supplies a qualifying ranking \(\sigma^\star\) with \(p\) first. Its original mass is zero: positive original mass would already make \(p\) a winner.

The original support is nonempty because its masses sum to \(1\). Choose any support ranking \(\rho\), and set
\[ \delta=\tfrac12\min\{\beta,\mu_\rho\}. \]
This is a positive rational number. Since \(\sigma^\star\) is outside the original support, \(\rho\ne\sigma^\star\). Use the single transfer \(x_{\rho,\sigma^\star}=\delta\), with all other entries zero.

The source constraint holds because \(\delta\le\mu_\rho\), and the budget constraint holds because \(\delta\le\beta\). The resulting masses are \(\mu_\rho-\delta\) at \(\rho\), \(\delta\) at \(\sigma^\star\), and the original masses elsewhere. They are nonnegative and sum to \(1\). Since \(\sigma^\star\) qualifies, has positive mass, and ranks \(p\) first, \(p\) wins.

This proves both directions.

3. Polynomial-time decision algorithm

Use the following algorithm:

  • Sort candidate names, compute their assigned bits, and construct \(F(C)\).
  • Test membership in \(B\). If the test fails, reject.
  • If \(\beta>0\), accept.
  • Otherwise, scan the original support. Accept exactly when some listed ranking ranks \(p\) first and its first \(d\) bottom-read bits satisfy \(F(C)\).

Correctness follows from the theorem.

For the running time, sorting and processing the candidate names take time polynomial in \(m,L\). The membership test takes polynomial time because \(B\) is fixed and polynomial-time recognizable, and \(|F(C)|\le m\). Testing whether the binary rational \(\beta\) is positive takes polynomial time in its encoding length.

In the zero-budget branch, each of the \(\tau\) explicit rankings can be scanned in polynomial time in \(m,L\). Evaluating an encoded Boolean formula on a given assignment takes polynomial time in its length. Thus the entire algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), without enumerating possible rankings.

Accordingly, polynomial-time many-one \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

4. The continuous search problem still requires finding assignments

For completeness, the assumed hardness of finding assignments for members of \(B\) also rules out an \(\mathrm{FP}\) algorithm that outputs successful transfers on every feasible instance.

Suppose such an algorithm exists. Given \(F=f_1\cdots f_n\in B\), first handle \(n<2\) directly: the variable bound forces \(d=0\), so the empty assignment satisfies \(F\).

For \(n\ge2\), let \(\ell=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil\). For each \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), make a candidate whose name is the \(\ell\)-bit binary encoding of \(i-1\), followed by \(f_i\). These names are distinct, nonempty, and sort in index order, since their equal-length prefixes are distinct and ordered. Hence their last bits concatenate to exactly \(F\).

Choose distinct candidates \(p,q\). Give mass \(1\) to a single ranking \(\rho\) with \(q\) first, and set \(\beta=1\). This construction has polynomial size. Initially \(p\) does not win, because the only positive-mass ranking has \(q\) first. Nevertheless, the instance is feasible by the theorem.

Run the presumed search algorithm. Its successful output must create positive mass at some qualifying ranking \(\sigma\) with \(p\) first. Such a ranking differs from \(\rho\), has zero original mass, and has no outgoing transfer row because it is outside the original support. Therefore its positive mass comes from a nonzero listed transfer into it.

Scan the explicitly listed destinations and find one that qualifies and ranks \(p\) first. Qualification can be checked in polynomial time. Its first \(d\) bottom-read bits give a satisfying assignment for \(F\). A polynomial-time search algorithm has polynomial output length, so this scan is polynomial as well.

This would find satisfying assignments for every member of \(B\), contradicting the fixed premise. Thus the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable while its transfer-search problem is outside \(\mathrm{FP}\) under that premise.

The prover’s decision result is correct. The feasibility criterion, polynomial-time algorithm, and additional search-hardness argument all follow from the stated premises. No major or minor mathematical issues were found.

  • Encoding every assignment with \(p\) first.

The lemma correctly handles \(k=0\): the bottom-read string is empty, and any ranking placing \(p\) first realizes it.

When \(k>0\), the definition gives \(k=\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1\). Removing \(p\) leaves at least \(\lceil m/2\rceil-1\) candidates labeled \(1\) and at least \(\lfloor m/2\rfloor-1\) candidates labeled \(0\). Both quantities are at least \(k\).

Any length-\(k\) string requires at most \(k\) candidates of each label. Thus the distinct candidates \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\) can be chosen as asserted. Placing them at the bottom in the order \(z_k,\ldots,z_1\) makes the worst-upward reading exactly \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\). Since none equals \(p\), the construction also preserves \(p\)’s first position.

The lemma establishes existence uniformly for every candidate \(p\) and every length-\(k\) bitstring. It does not presume an algorithm for finding a satisfying assignment.

  • Existence of a qualifying target ranking.

Necessity follows directly from the definition of qualification.

For sufficiency, membership in \(B\subseteq\mathrm{SAT}\) supplies a satisfying assignment. Each nonempty candidate name contributes exactly one bit, while the empty name contributes none, so \(n=|F(C)|\le m\). The function \(t\mapsto\max\{0,\lfloor t/2\rfloor-1\}\) is nondecreasing on nonnegative integers. Consequently, the stipulated variable bound implies \(d\le k\).

Padding the satisfying assignment to length \(k\) and applying the lemma therefore produces the required ranking. The prescribed variable order is respected. The argument also covers \(d=0\), including cases with \(k=0\): because the formula is satisfiable and has no variables, the empty assignment satisfies it.

  • Necessity of the feasibility criterion.

If \(F(C)\notin B\), every ranking fails qualification, regardless of its mass. Transfers leave \(C\) unchanged, so they cannot change this obstruction.

If \(\beta=0\), the nonnegative transfer entries have total at most zero, forcing every entry to vanish. Hence success is equivalent to \(p\) already winning.

These cases establish necessity: any successful instance must have \(F(C)\in B\), and must either have positive budget or already make \(p\) a winner.

  • Sufficiency of the feasibility criterion.

If \(p\) already wins, the empty transfer list is permitted and succeeds.

Otherwise, the corollary provides a qualifying ranking \(\sigma^\star\) placing \(p\) first. Its original mass must be zero, since positive mass would contradict the assumption that \(p\) does not already win.

The original support is nonempty because its positive masses sum to \(1\). For any support ranking \(\rho\), both \(\mu_\rho\) and \(\beta\) are positive rationals, so
\[ \delta=\tfrac12\min\{\beta,\mu_\rho\} \]
is a positive rational satisfying both required bounds. The source and target are distinct because \(\sigma^\star\) is outside the support.

The single transfer leaves every mass nonnegative, preserves total mass, and gives \(\sigma^\star\) positive mass. This is sufficient under the stated winner rule. No minimum positive transfer size is imposed by the task.

  • Polynomial-time decision algorithm.

The algorithm implements the proved criterion exactly. After accepting membership in \(B\), positive budget permits immediate acceptance without constructing a qualifying ranking.

In the zero-budget branch, scanning the explicit support correctly tests whether \(p\) already wins. Candidate labels and the first-occurrence variable order can be computed from the input; the required assignment bits can be read from each ranking and checked by Boolean-formula evaluation.

Sorting binary-string names takes polynomial time in their total encoded length. The fixed membership test for \(B\) takes polynomial time in \(|F(C)|\le m\). Rational positivity testing, support scanning, and formula evaluation also take polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). No enumeration of all rankings or search for satisfying assignments is used.

The statement about polynomial-time many-one NP-hardness is correctly qualified: such hardness, together with this polynomial-time algorithm, would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

This argument is also valid, although it exceeds the decision question.
