Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(J\) be finite, nonempty sets of candidate and job types. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A|+|J|\) types with positive binary-rational masses, not the number of possible types. Put \(m=|J|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Masses satisfy \(\sum_a\mu_a=1+\alpha\) and \(\sum_j\nu_j=1\), where \(\alpha\geq0\) is rational. Each type strictly orders a possibly empty subset of opposite-side types; a pair is acceptable only when mutually listed, and every acceptable partner is preferred to being unmatched.
A feasible allocation has divisible real masses \(x_{aj}\geq0\), vanishes on unacceptable pairs, and satisfies \(\sum_jx_{aj}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_ax_{aj}\leq\nu_j\). Define unmatched masses \(u_a=\mu_a-\sum_jx_{aj}\) and \(v_j=\nu_j-\sum_ax_{aj}\).
An acceptable pair \((a,j)\) blocks precisely when both \(u_a+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_a k}x_{ak}>0\) and \(v_j+\sum_{b:\,a\succ_j b}x_{bj}>0\); an allocation is stable when no pair blocks.
The candidate-proposing allocation means a candidate-optimal stable allocation: for every stable allocation \(y\), every candidate \(a\), and every prefix \(P\) of its acceptable list, \(\sum_{j\in P}x_{aj}\geq\sum_{j\in P}y_{aj}\).

Is computing this candidate-optimal stable allocation and all unmatched masses possible in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete candidate-proposing deferred acceptance computes the candidate-optimal, job-pessimal stable matching in polynomial time, independently of proposal order; unmatched agents are invariant across stable matchings ([source](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.125/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.125.pdf)).
The continuous problem is already covered by Baïou–Balinski's stable allocation model, including missing acceptable pairs: they give strongly polynomial algorithms for a stable allocation and its candidate-optimal extremum ([corrected primary source](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302)). Thus this target is already settled in polynomial time.

1. The applicable theorem

Baïou–Balinski consider strict preferences, positive row supplies \(s_i\), positive column demands \(d_j\), and nonnegative pair capacities \(c_{ij}\). Feasible allocations satisfy row, column, and pair capacities. Stability requires, for every pair,

\(x_{ij}<c_{ij}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_{k:\,k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}=s_i \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_{h:\,h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}=d_j.\)

Their inductive algorithm finds a stable allocation in strongly polynomial time. Their row-optimal algorithm then finds \(x^\star\) such that, for every stable \(y\),

\(x^\star_{ij}<y_{ij}\ \Longrightarrow\ x^\star_{ik}=0\quad\text{for every }k\text{ less preferred than }j.\)

Moreover, every row and column has the same total allocation in all stable allocations. These statements hold without balance or nondegeneracy assumptions. See Theorem 2, Lemma 8, Theorem 10, and Corollary 4 of the [corrected paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

2. Exact correspondence with the given problem

Let \(E\subseteq A\times J\) be the mutually acceptable pairs. Construct an instance with rows \(A\), columns \(J\), and

\(s_a=\mu_a,\qquad d_j=\nu_j,\qquad c_{aj}= \begin{cases} 2,&(a,j)\in E,\\ 0,&(a,j)\notin E. \end{cases}\)

For each type, preserve the order of its mutually acceptable partners and append all remaining opposite-side types in any strict order. Zero pair capacities force all unacceptable entries to vanish, so this extension has no effect on allocations or blocking.

Every allocation feasible in the original problem satisfies

\(x_{aj}\leq\nu_j\leq1<2.\)

Consequently, the positive pair capacities impose no additional restriction. Conversely, feasibility in the constructed instance gives exactly the original row, column, and acceptability constraints.

For an acceptable pair, define

\(R_{aj}(x)=\sum_{k:\,k\succeq_a j}x_{ak}, \qquad C_{aj}(x)=\sum_{b:\,b\succeq_j a}x_{bj}.\)

The definitions of unmatched masses give the identities

\(\mu_a-R_{aj}(x) =u_a+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_a k}x_{ak},\)

\(\nu_j-C_{aj}(x) =v_j+\sum_{b:\,a\succ_j b}x_{bj}.\)

Both quantities are nonnegative by feasibility. Thus the pair blocks in the original problem precisely when

\(R_{aj}(x)<\mu_a \quad\text{and}\quad C_{aj}(x)<\nu_j.\)

Because \(x_{aj}<c_{aj}\) always holds on acceptable pairs, this is exactly a violation of Baïou–Balinski stability. On unacceptable pairs, \(x_{aj}=c_{aj}=0\), so their stability implication imposes no condition.

Therefore the two instances have exactly the same stable allocations. Empty lists and isolated types are included in this correspondence.

3. Row optimality implies every required prefix inequality

Let \(x^\star\) be the row-optimal allocation returned by the theorem, and let \(y\) be any stable allocation. Fix a candidate \(a\), whose acceptable partners in decreasing preference order are

\(j_1\succ_a j_2\succ_a\cdots\succ_a j_d.\)

Fix a prefix \(P=\{j_1,\ldots,j_t\}\).

If \(x^\star_{aj_r}\geq y_{aj_r}\) for every \(r\leq t\), summing proves the desired inequality.

Otherwise, choose \(r\leq t\) with \(x^\star_{aj_r}<y_{aj_r}\). The row-optimality property implies

\(x^\star_{aj_q}=0\qquad\text{for every }q>r.\)

Hence all of candidate \(a\)'s allocation under \(x^\star\) lies in \(P\). The invariant row-total property then gives

\(\sum_{j\in P}x^\star_{aj} =\sum_{j\in J}x^\star_{aj} =\sum_{j\in J}y_{aj} \geq\sum_{j\in P}y_{aj}.\)

This proves candidate optimality exactly as defined in the task. For an empty list the condition is vacuous.

It also proves uniqueness under that definition: two candidate-optimal allocations dominate one another on every prefix, so their prefix sums agree; subtracting consecutive prefix sums shows that every acceptable entry agrees.

4. Polynomial time with binary-rational masses

The constructed instance has \(\tau\) vertices and at most

\(|A||J|=(\tau-m)m\leq\tau m\)

pairs. Constructing capacities and extended preference lists therefore takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

To justify exact bit complexity, write all input masses as binary-encoded rational numbers and let \(D\) be the product of their denominators. The bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Multiply supplies, demands, and pair capacities by \(D\). This produces integer data of bit length \(O(L)\).

Positive scaling preserves feasibility, blocking, and prefix comparisons. Run the two algorithms on the scaled instance, then divide their output by \(D\).

The numerical updates in these algorithms use addition, subtraction, minima, and comparisons. This follows directly from their displayed update rules in [Sections 2 and 4](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302). Thus the scaled computations remain integral. If there are \(q\) elementary arithmetic operations, each addition or subtraction increases the maximum operand bit length by at most one; minima and comparisons do not increase it. Every intermediate integer therefore has bit length \(O(L+q)\). Strong polynomiality makes \(q\) polynomial in the constructed instance size, so exact execution has polynomial bit complexity.

Finally, compute

\(u_a=\mu_a-\sum_jx^\star_{aj}, \qquad v_j=\nu_j-\sum_ax^\star_{aj}.\)

These require only polynomially many rational additions and subtractions. The invariant row and column totals also show that these unmatched masses are the same in every stable allocation.

The computation operates directly on the listed support types; it never expands rational masses into individual agents.
