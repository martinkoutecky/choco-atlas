The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given branches \(B\), service terms \(T\), and a finite support \(R\) of cadet types with rational masses \(\mu_r\), strict preferences over branch-term pairs and the outside option, and base-priority classes \(\rho_b(r)\), compute an exact rational mass allocation \(x_{r,b,t}\). Branch \(b\) has rational per-capita capacity \(q_b\) and uses BfYC priorities: allocate the first \(\lambda q_b\) by base priority, choosing the lowest-cost contract among a type's contracts, then allocate the remaining capacity by descending service term with base priority breaking ties, choosing the highest-cost contract among a type's contracts. Split mass at cutoffs among tied types. Require individual rationality and mass stability, meaning no positive mass of a type prefers a contract that the branch would accept; among stable allocations, return one that is cadet-optimal for every type under its ranking over outcomes.

Each branch has base-priority classes \(\rho_b(r)\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), with smaller values better. To specify cutoff splitting, represent type \(r\) by individuals \((r,u)\), \(u\in[0,1]\), with mass measure \(\mu_r\,du\); resolve equal classes by smaller \(u\), using the same fixed lottery rank at every branch and throughout all deviations. Partial result for unrestricted numbers of branches

I do not settle whether the computation is polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard. I prove the following results for the specified model, with arbitrary $m,\tau,k$.

  • Every stable mass vector has a stable realization in which each type’s assignment weakly worsens as its lottery rank increases.
  • A cadet-optimal mass-stable allocation always exists.
  • Such an allocation has an exact rational description of polynomial bit length, using at most $mk+1$ intervals per type.
  • Exact computation belongs to $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$. In particular, an exact algorithm with exponential running time follows.

Thus existence and succinct rational output do not obstruct an exact polynomial-time algorithm. The remaining issue is computational complexity.

1. A stability characterization

Write $z=(r,u)$ and let $\nu$ be population mass:
$$ \nu(D)=\sum_r\mu_r\int_0^1\mathbf 1\{(r,u)\in D\}\,du. $$

For branch $b$, put
$$ \alpha_b=\lambda q_b,\qquad \beta_b=(1-\lambda)q_b, $$
and define the priority keys
$$ p_b(r,u)=(\rho_b(r),u),\qquad d_b(r,u,t)=(-t,\rho_b(r),u). $$
Smaller keys are better.

Fix a feasible, acceptable allocation $a$. Let $A_b$ be its incumbent individuals at $b$. Let $S_b$ be the first $\min\{\alpha_b,\nu(A_b)\}$ mass of $A_b$ in base-priority order, and put $Q_b=A_b\setminus S_b$.

For an external individual $z\notin A_b$, define
$$ G_b(z)=\nu\{w\in A_b:p_b(w)<p_b(z)\}, $$
and
$$ K_b(z,t)= \nu\{w\in Q_b:d_b(w,t_a(w))<d_b(z,t)\}, $$
where $t_a(w)$ is the incumbent’s assigned term.

All equalities between measurable sets below are understood modulo null sets.

Lemma 1. The allocation is mass-stable if and only if:

  • every individual in $S_b$ receives $(b,t_1)$;
  • for every $r,b,t$, almost every external individual $(r,u)$ strictly preferring $(b,t)$ satisfies

$$ G_b(r,u)\ge\alpha_b,\qquad K_b(r,u,t)\ge\beta_b. $$

Proof. Incumbents are automatically retained: phase two receives mass
$$ \max\{0,\nu(A_b)-\alpha_b\}\le\beta_b. $$

If positive phase-one mass receives a longer term, some type has positive mass among those individuals. Their additional offers of $t_1$ leave the offering individuals unchanged, and phase one selects the shorter offers. This blocks.

Suppose a positive-mass set of external proposers satisfies $G_b(z)<\alpha_b$. Some positive-mass subset has a uniform slack $\varepsilon>0$. Choose a positive-mass proposing subset $D$ with $\nu(D)<\varepsilon$. Every member has less than $\alpha_b$ offering mass ahead after the additions, so phase one selects its offer.

If instead a positive-mass set satisfies $G_b(z)\ge\alpha_b$ and $K_b(z,t)<\beta_b$, choose a subset with uniform slack and sufficiently small positive mass. Its members cannot enter phase one, so $S_b$ remains unchanged. Each has less than $\beta_b$ mass ahead in phase two after the additions, and its offer is selected.

Conversely, consider an allowed proposing subpopulation. An internal proposer must propose a shorter term. It cannot belong to $S_b$, whose members receive $t_1$. Adding external individuals cannot promote it into phase one. In phase two its retained, longer incumbent offer prevents selection of its shorter offer.

Almost every external proposer satisfies both displayed inequalities. The first excludes phase-one entry and leaves $S_b$ unchanged. The second places at least $\beta_b$ incumbent mass ahead in phase two. Hence no positive mass of added offers is selected. $\square$

For fixed $r,b,t$, both $G_b(r,u)$ and $K_b(r,u,t)$ are continuous, nondecreasing functions of $u$. Indeed, their variable parts are weighted measures of measurable subsets of $[0,u)$.

2. Canonical realization of every stable mass vector

For each type $r$, list all $s=mk+1$ outcomes in preference order:
$$ c_{r,1}\ P_r\ c_{r,2}\ P_r\ \cdots\ P_r\ c_{r,s}. $$
This list includes the outside outcome.

Given an allocation’s outcome masses, define
$$ y_{r,0}=0,\qquad y_{r,j}=\frac{1}{\mu_r}\sum_{\ell=1}^j x_{r,c_{r,\ell}}, \qquad y_{r,s}=1. $$
Its canonical allocation assigns $c_{r,j}$ on
$$ (y_{r,j-1},y_{r,j}). $$
It preserves every outcome mass and weakly worsens each type’s outcome as $u$ increases.

The difficulty is proving that this rearrangement preserves stability.

2.1. Cross-branch inversions are impossible

Lemma 2. In a stable allocation, for each type $r$, the following set of pairs has two-dimensional Lebesgue measure zero:
$$ \{(u,v):u<v,\ a(r,v)\ P_r\ a(r,u),\ a(r,u)\text{ and }a(r,v)\text{ have different branch labels}\}. $$
Here the outside outcome has its own label.

Proof. Acceptability excludes inversions whose better outcome is outside. Fix the remaining two outcomes, with the later, better outcome $(b,t)$ and the earlier outcome external to $b$.

If the relevant pair set has positive measure, Fubini’s theorem gives a positive-measure set of earlier ranks $u$ having positive incumbent mass of their own type at $(b,t)$ behind them.

If positive such mass belongs to $S_b$, then $G_b(r,u)<\alpha_b$: the individual precedes positive phase-one mass, so all incumbent mass ahead belongs to a proper initial portion of $S_b$.

Otherwise positive such mass belongs to $Q_b$. Those incumbents have the same class and term as the proposed offer and larger lottery ranks. Therefore
$$ K_b(r,u,t)<\nu(Q_b)\le\beta_b. $$
Either case contradicts Lemma 1. There are finitely many outcome pairs, so their union also has measure zero. $\square$

2.2. Sorting preserves branch membership

Lemma 3. The canonical allocation of a stable allocation assigns almost every individual to the same branch as before, including the outside label.

Proof. Fix a type. Sample independent uniform ranks $U_1,U_2,\ldots$. By Lemma 2, almost surely every finite sample, ordered by lottery rank, has no preference inversion involving different branch labels.

Such a finite outcome list can be sorted into preference order using adjacent swaps of inverted outcomes. Every swapped pair has the same branch label. Moreover, an adjacent swap of equal branch labels leaves all cross-label relative orders unchanged. Thus the branch label at each sample position remains unchanged throughout sorting.

Let $J_n$ be the position of $U_1$ among the first $n$ sampled ranks. The strong law gives
$$ \frac{J_n}{n}\longrightarrow U_1 $$
and convergence of every empirical outcome frequency to its population frequency. Except when $U_1$ equals one of the finitely many canonical boundaries, the outcome at position $J_n$ in the sorted list eventually equals the canonical outcome at $U_1$.

Its branch label equals the original label at $U_1$ for every $n$. Hence the canonical and original branch labels agree at a uniform random rank with probability one, proving the assertion. $\square$

Consequently, canonicalization sorts terms from shortest to longest within each type’s fixed branch-membership set.

2.3. Sorting preserves stability

Theorem 4. The canonical allocation of every mass-stable allocation is mass-stable.

Proof. Denote the canonical allocation by $a'$. By Lemma 3, every $A_b$ remains unchanged. Therefore every $S_b$, $Q_b$, and $G_b$ remains unchanged.

For a fixed type, its portion of $S_b$ is an initial portion of its branch-membership set in lottery order. Originally this portion receives $t_1$. Sorting terms within that membership set still assigns $t_1$ to the entire portion. Thus $a'$ satisfies the first condition of Lemma 1.

It follows also that, for every type and term, the mass assigned that term within $Q_b$ remains unchanged: total branch-term masses are preserved, and $S_b$ receives $t_1$ both before and after sorting.

Fix $r,b,t$, and let
$$ D=\{u:(r,u)\notin A_b,\ (b,t)\ P_r\ a(r,u)\}. $$
Define $D'$ similarly using $a'$. These sets have the same measure, because canonicalization preserves the mass at every outcome and preserves branch membership.

If their common measure is zero, there is nothing to check. Otherwise let
$$ \ell=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}D. $$

Within any other branch, outcomes worse than $(b,t)$ form a suffix of its terms. Sorting terms moves that suffix to the end of its membership set in lottery order. Since the original $D$ has no mass below $\ell$, neither does $D'$. The outside-membership set is unchanged. Hence
$$ D'\subseteq[\ell,1] $$
up to a null set.

Original stability and continuity imply
$$ G_b(r,\ell)\ge\alpha_b,\qquad K_b(r,\ell,t)\ge\beta_b: $$
there are ranks of $D$, outside the exceptional null set, arbitrarily close to $\ell$ from above.

The base inequality therefore holds throughout $D'$.

If $\beta_b=0$, the bidding inequality is automatic. Suppose $\beta_b>0$. Since $D$ has positive measure and $K_b\le\nu(Q_b)\le\beta_b$, original stability forces
$$ \nu(Q_b)=\beta_b. $$
It also forces zero phase-two mass at terms shorter than $t$. Otherwise all offers of $t$ from $D$ would precede that positive incumbent mass, giving $K_b<\beta_b$.

Thus $t$ is a shortest term present in $Q_b$, if it is present at all. After sorting, each type’s phase-two incumbents receiving $t$ form an initial portion of that type’s $Q_b$-membership set. For every cutoff $u$, this maximizes their mass below $u$, among subsets of the same total mass.

Longer-term masses and better-class masses at term $t$ remain unchanged. It follows that the new bidding count satisfies
$$ K'_b(r,u,t)\ge K_b(r,u,t) $$
for every $u$. Therefore, throughout $D'$,
$$ K'_b(r,u,t)\ge K_b(r,\ell,t)\ge\beta_b. $$
Lemma 1 proves stability. $\square$

Finite analogue. The same result holds for equal-mass discrete individuals when both phase quotas are integer multiples of the individual mass. Cross-branch inversions are excluded by a single-individual deviation; branch preservation follows directly from adjacent swaps; and the preceding suffix and initial-portion arguments use counting instead of integration.

In particular, a finite cadet-optimal allocation is canonical within each type. Its canonical rearrangement is stable and preserves each type’s outcome multiset. Cadet optimality makes every individual weakly prefer the original allocation to that rearrangement. The sum of preference positions is unchanged, so no individual can strictly prefer it. The two allocations therefore coincide.

3. A finite union of rational polytopes

Theorem 5. The set of stable mass vectors is the affine image of a finite union of compact rational polytopes. Each polytope has polynomially many variables and constraints, with coefficients of polynomial bit length.

Proof. By Theorem 4, it suffices to describe canonical stable allocations using their variables $y_{r,j}$.

First impose
$$ 0=y_{r,0}\le y_{r,1}\le\cdots\le y_{r,s}=1. $$
Unacceptable outcomes must have zero interval length. Branch capacities give linear inequalities, since
$$ x_{r,c_{r,j}}=\mu_r(y_{r,j}-y_{r,j-1}). $$

We describe phase-one selection by finitely many cases.

  • If $\alpha_b=0$, set $S_b=\varnothing$.
  • Otherwise, one case has $\nu(A_b)\le\alpha_b$ and $S_b=A_b$.
  • The remaining cases choose a cutoff class $c_b\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}$ and a variable $h_b\in[0,1]$. Define $S_b$ to consist of incumbents in better classes, together with class-$c_b$ incumbents below $h_b$, and impose

$$ \nu(S_b)=\alpha_b. $$

Every actual phase-one selection has one of these descriptions.

Next enumerate an ordering of all outcome endpoints, phase-one cutoff variables, and the constants $0,1$, using weak inequalities. There are
$$ D=O(\tau mk+m) $$
variables and endpoints. Consecutive ordered endpoints determine elementary cells. On the interior of each cell, every type’s assigned outcome and its phase-one membership are fixed by the chosen case and ordering.

All masses of these cells are linear expressions of the form
$$ \mu_r(e_{i+1}-e_i). $$
Hence capacities and phase-one quota equalities are linear.

For a cell on which type $r$ is external to $b$ and prefers $(b,t)$, Lemma 1 requires
$$ G_b(r,u)\ge\alpha_b,\qquad K_b(r,u,t)\ge\beta_b. $$
Both functions are continuous and nondecreasing. If the cell has positive length, these conditions are equivalent to
$$ G_b(r,e_i)\ge\alpha_b,\qquad K_b(r,e_i,t)\ge\beta_b. $$
The two left-hand sides are linear expressions: all contributing incumbent intervals are either wholly before the endpoint, wholly after it, or in better classes or longer terms.

An empty cell imposes no stability condition. Thus the exact requirement is the finite disjunction
$$ e_i=e_{i+1} \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(G_b(r,e_i)\ge\alpha_b \ \text{and}\ K_b(r,e_i,t)\ge\beta_b\bigr). $$
Enumerate its alternatives. A phase-one cell receiving a term longer than $t_1$ is required to have zero length.

Each fully selected set of alternatives is therefore a system of rational linear equalities and non-strict inequalities. Its variables lie in $[0,1]$, so its feasible set is a compact rational polytope. There are polynomially many constraints in each system; their coefficients are sums of input masses and quota parameters and have polynomial bit length.

Every feasible system describes a stable canonical allocation by Lemma 1. Conversely, every stable allocation can be canonicalized and then described by one of the systems. $\square$

Two consequences will be useful.

Rational density. Rational feasible points are dense in every component polytope. To see this at a feasible point, impose as equalities all constraints tight there. Gaussian elimination gives a rational particular solution and a rational basis for the resulting affine space. Approximate its free coordinates by rationals. Sufficiently close approximations preserve every remaining, strictly satisfied inequality.

Vertex bit bound. Every vertex has polynomial bit length. At a vertex, choose a full-rank collection of tight constraints. Clear denominators and use Cramer’s rule. For a matrix of dimension $D$ whose integer entries have at most $H$ bits, its determinants have
$$ O(DH+D\log D) $$
bits. Here both $D$ and $H$ are polynomially bounded in the input parameters.

These statements apply also when additional rational linear constraints of polynomial description length are imposed.

4. Existence of a cadet-optimal continuum allocation

The proof uses the finite theorem supplied in the task: with strict base priorities, integer phase quotas, and the specified two-phase choice, cumulative offers produce a stable allocation weakly preferred by every individual to every stable allocation. The finite stability test retains incumbent offers. Branch-specific priorities are covered by the stated extension of the rule. See [Sönmez, Theorem 1, Lemma 2, and Remark 2](https://www.tayfunsonmez.net/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Sonmez-JPE2013.pdf).

The following argument establishes the continuum passage; it does not infer a polynomial running time from finite expansion.

4.1. Finite grids and their stable limits

Choose an integer $D_0$ clearing the denominators of the masses and phase quotas, and set
$$ N_j=D_0j!. $$
For each $N=N_j$, type $r$ has $n_r=N\mu_r$ individuals, each of mass $1/N$. Give its individuals representative ranks
$$ u_{r,i}=\frac{i-\tfrac12}{n_r}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n_r. $$
Resolve coincident representative ranks by a fixed type order, common across all branches. Thus finite base priorities are strict.

Branch capacities and phase quotas are $Nq_b$, $N\alpha_b$, and $N\beta_b$. They are integers, with exactly the same common $\lambda$. Preferences and terms are unchanged, so the finite theorem applies.

Lift each finite individual to its type’s interval
$$ \left(\frac{i-1}{n_r},\frac{i}{n_r}\right). $$
Let $a_N$ be the lifted finite cadet-optimal allocation. By the finite analogue of Theorem 4, it is canonical within each type. Its finitely many canonical endpoints therefore have a convergent subsequence. Write $a$ for the allocation defined by the limiting endpoints.

I verify that $a$ is stable.

Put
$$ h_N=\frac{1}{2N\min_r\mu_r}. $$
Every actual rank in a lifted cell differs from its representative rank by at most $h_N$.

Let $\widehat S_{b,N}$ be the lift of the finite phase-one set, and let $S_{b,N}$ be the actual continuum phase-one set of $a_N$. If offers are exhausted, these sets coincide. Otherwise let $(c,v)$ be the class and representative rank at the finite cutoff. They agree outside the rank band $[v-h_N,v+h_N]$ in class $c$: lower individuals are selected in both, and higher individuals are rejected in both. Both selections have mass $\alpha_b$. Since population density in the lottery coordinate is at most $1$,
$$ \nu(\widehat S_{b,N}\mathbin{\triangle}S_{b,N})\le 2h_N. $$

For a lifted proposer, comparison of actual and representative base keys can differ only for individuals with actual ranks within $2h_N$ of its rank. That band has mass at most $4h_N$. Consequently, finite and continuum base-ahead counts differ by at most $4h_N$. Bidding counts differ by at most $6h_N$, including the preceding phase-one discrepancy.

The finite stability characterization thus gives, for every external improving offer throughout its lifted cell,
$$ G_{b,N}(r,u)\ge\alpha_b-4h_N, \qquad K_{b,N}(r,u,t)\ge\beta_b-6h_N. $$
Furthermore, the mass of actual continuum phase-one incumbents receiving a term longer than $t_1$ is at most $2h_N$.

Endpoint convergence implies that $a_N$ converges to $a$ in population measure. Phase-one selection is continuous under this convergence. Explicitly, if incumbent sets $A,A'$ differ by mass $\delta$, their initial-priority selections differ by at most $3\delta$: compare their cutoff positions; the earlier selection’s difference from the later is contained in $A\mathbin{\triangle}A'$, and their selected masses differ by at most $\delta$.

Thus $S_{b,N}$ and the term-labelled $Q_{b,N}$ converge in measure to those of $a$. Their cumulative counting functions $G_{b,N}$ and $K_{b,N}$ converge uniformly: the difference of any such count is bounded by the mass on which the underlying incumbent sets or term labels differ.

Outside the finitely many limiting endpoints, an individual’s assignment is eventually constant. Passing to the limit in the approximate inequalities therefore gives Lemma 1’s exact inequalities for almost every external improving proposer. The phase-one term violation tends to zero as well. Feasibility and acceptability also pass to the limit.

Hence $a$ is mass-stable.

4.2. Exact embedding of rational stable allocations

Lemma 6. Every rational canonical stable allocation embeds exactly as a stable allocation in all sufficiently divisible finite grids.

Proof. Its outcome endpoints are rational. Its phase-one cutoffs can also be chosen rational: within each priority class, incumbent density is piecewise constant with rational breakpoints and values; solving for the desired selected mass uses rational arithmetic.

Choose $N$ so that every outcome endpoint and every phase-one cutoff is a grid boundary for every type, and so that masses and phase quotas scale to integers. Such an $N$ exists because all relevant numbers are rational.

Each grid cell now has a constant assignment and phase-one status. The finite phase-one set lifts exactly to $S_b$.

Consider an external individual proposing an improvement at its representative rank $u$. Its grid cell is a positive-length set of external improving individuals. Continuity and Lemma 1 give
$$ G_b(r,u)\ge\alpha_b,\qquad K_b(r,u,t)\ge\beta_b. $$

For a positive phase-one quota, the first inequality means that the entire $\alpha_b$ mass of $S_b$ precedes the proposer. A same-class incumbent cell contained in that preceding set has its midpoint strictly before $u$. Thus all corresponding finite phase-one incumbents precede the proposer.

For a positive phase-two quota, the second inequality, together with $\nu(Q_b)\le\beta_b$, means that $\nu(Q_b)=\beta_b$ and almost every phase-two incumbent offer precedes the proposal. Each incumbent cell has fixed term and class. If these tie the proposal, the whole cell lies before $u$, so its midpoint also precedes $u$. Hence all finite phase-two incumbents precede the proposal.

Zero quotas require no check. Internal phase-one incumbents receive $t_1$, while internal phase-two improvements are shorter and ineffective because their incumbent offers remain. The finite stability characterization proves stability. $\square$

4.3. The limit dominates every stable mass vector

Fix a rational canonical stable allocation. Its finitely many denominators divide $N_j$ in the required scaled sense for every sufficiently large $j$, because every fixed positive integer divides $j!$ eventually.

By Lemma 6, it is then a stable allocation in the same finite market used to construct $a_{N_j}$. Finite cadet optimality implies, for every type and ranking prefix,
$$ \text{prefix mass under }a_{N_j} \ \ge\ \text{prefix mass under the fixed rational allocation}. $$
Passing along the convergent subsequence shows that $a$ dominates every rational canonical stable allocation.

Now take any measurable stable allocation. Theorem 4 canonicalizes it without changing its mass vector. By Theorem 5 and rational density, this canonical mass vector is a limit of rational canonical stable mass vectors. Prefix masses are continuous linear functions of the mass vector. Hence $a$ dominates this vector too.

Therefore $a$ is cadet-optimal under exactly the comparison required in the task, including prefixes containing the outside outcome.

5. Rational output and complexity upper bounds

5.1. Polynomial-size exact output

For a canonical allocation define
$$ F(y)=\sum_r\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\mu_r y_{r,j}. $$
This is the sum of all nontrivial type-prefix masses, and
$$ 0\le F(y)\le s-1. $$

The cadet-optimal vector proved above dominates every stable vector in every summand. If another stable mass vector differs from it, some prefix mass differs and is strictly smaller. Consequently, maximizing $F$ over stable allocations selects exactly the cadet-optimal mass vector.

By Theorem 5, this maximization is over a finite union of compact rational polytopes. A linear objective attains its maximum at a vertex of one of them. The vertex bit bound gives rational endpoints and cutoffs of polynomial bit length.

Thus an exact rational cadet-optimal allocation exists, using at most $s=mk+1$ outcome intervals per type. Its mass vector is unique: two cadet-optimal vectors dominate one another in every prefix, so their prefix masses, and hence individual outcome masses, coincide.

Enumerating the polyhedral cases and their vertices gives an exact algorithm. The number of cases and candidate vertices is at most
$$ 2^{\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L)}. $$
This establishes an exponential upper bound, not a polynomial one.

5.2. An $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ upper bound

Consider the decision problem with an additional rational input $c$:

Does there exist a mass-stable allocation satisfying $F\ge c$?

It belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. A certificate specifies one polyhedral case and rational feasible endpoints and cutoffs. The case has polynomial description length and can be checked by rational arithmetic. If it is feasible with $F\ge c$, the resulting compact rational polytope has a vertex whose bit length is polynomial in the original input and the bit length of $c$.

The determinant bound also supplies a computable polynomial $p$ such that the reduced denominator of the optimum $F^*$ is at most $2^p$. Binary search with this $\mathrm{NP}$ predicate locates $F^*$ in an interval of length less than
$$ 2^{-2p-2} $$
using polynomially many queries.

There is at most one rational of denominator at most $2^p$ in that interval: distinct such rationals differ by at least $2^{-2p}$. Exact rational reconstruction is polynomial-time. For example, the interval’s midpoint approximates the desired reduced fraction $a/b$ with error less than $1/(2b^2)$; the standard continued-fraction criterion therefore places $a/b$ among the midpoint’s convergents. Check those convergents against the interval and denominator bound.

Finally, the relation
$$ \text{“this polynomial-size certificate describes a stable allocation with }F=F^*\text{”} $$
is polynomial-time verifiable and has a certificate. Polynomially many $\mathrm{NP}$ queries fixing successive certificate bits recover one. Its mass vector is cadet-optimal by the uniqueness argument above.

Therefore exact computation belongs to
