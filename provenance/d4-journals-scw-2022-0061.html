The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set \(T\) of rational piecewise-constant valuation types \(u_t\) on \([0,1]\), a rational type-mass vector \(\mu\), and market size \(r\), compute a finite-support distribution \(\Pi\) over \(r\)-agent configurations \(a=((t_1,X_1),\ldots,(t_r,X_r))\), with \((X_1,\ldots,X_r)\) partitioning \([0,1]\) and type marginal \(\mu\), maximizing \(\int \frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log u_{t_j}(X_j)\,d\Pi(a)\); determine exact or \(\varepsilon\)-optimal algorithms and, separately, whether a positive-mass bloc analogue of NOM exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\).
Each type has an additive valuation \(u_t(X)=\int_X f_t(x)\,dx\), where \(f_t\) is nonnegative and piecewise constant with rational breakpoints and heights, and \(u_t([0,1])=1\). Let \(m\) count intervals in the common breakpoint refinement, \(L\) be the total input bit length, and \(r\geq2\) be an integer market size.
A configuration \(a=((t_1,X_1),\ldots,(t_r,X_r))\) allows repeated types and partitions \([0,1]\) into pieces that are finite unions of intervals; disconnected pieces are allowed and boundaries are immaterial.
A finite-support probability distribution \(\Pi\) is feasible when \(\mathbb E_\Pi[r^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^r\mathbf1\{t_j=t\}]=\mu_t\) for every type. There is no independence requirement or restriction on each configuration's type counts.
Maximize \(F(\Pi)=\mathbb E_\Pi[r^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^r\log u_{t_j}(X_j)]\), with \(\log0=-\infty\). Algorithms must produce explicit finite mixtures with rational probabilities and interval endpoints; additive \(\varepsilon\)-optimality means \(F(\Pi)\geq\sup_{\Pi'}F(\Pi')-\varepsilon\), for rational \(0<\varepsilon<1\).
For incentives, a direct rule \(M\) chooses a maximizing distribution for every reported society, with one fixed selection among ties; members with identical reports are treated exchangeably.
A homogeneous bloc of rational mass \(b\in(0,1)\), true type \(t\), reports type \(s\). The remaining society is any finite-support rational probability distribution \(\nu\) over normalized nonnegative rational piecewise-constant types, not restricted to the original support. Set \(\rho_s=b\delta_s+(1-b)\nu\) and define its expected utility per member by \(U(s,\nu)=\mathbb E_{M(\rho_s)}[\sum_j\mathbf1\{t_j=s\}u_t(X_j)]/(r\rho_s(s))\).
Call \(M\) bloc-NOM if, for every \(r,b,t,s\) with \(U(s,\nu)>U(t,\nu)\) for some \(\nu\), both \(\inf_\nu U(s,\nu)\leq\inf_\nu U(t,\nu)\) and \(\sup_\nu U(s,\nu)\leq\sup_\nu U(t,\nu)\) hold.

Is exact maximization polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,r,L\), or is it NP-hard?
Is additive \(\varepsilon\)-optimal maximization polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,r,L,\log(1/\varepsilon)\), or is it NP-hard?
Does a maximizing direct rule satisfying bloc-NOM exist?

For a fixed finite agent profile, the Nash-optimal direct mechanism is NOM, envy-free and Pareto-optimal ([Ortega–Segal-Halevi](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00355-022-01416-4)); Nash-optimal allocations for piecewise-constant valuations are computable in polynomial time ([Aziz–Ye](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2908)).
No result settling this configuration-marginal optimization problem or the stated bloc-NOM condition was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted piecewise-constant valuations as additive measures with nonnegative rational densities and rational breakpoints, normalized to value the entire cake at one, following the discrete domain.
  • Normalized society mass to one and interpreted the type marginal as the expected fraction of configuration slots; repeated types and correlated configuration compositions are allowed.
  • Took market size to be an integer at least two and measured running time in its numerical value, alongside support size, interval count and input bit length.
  • Allowed disconnected finite unions of intervals and required complete allocation of the cake, following the discrete outcome domain.
  • Defined zero utility to contribute negative infinity and approximation to mean additive error in expected log welfare; used explicit rational mixture and cut representations for computational output.
  • Interpreted positive-mass bloc manipulation as a homogeneous bloc of mass strictly between zero and one jointly submitting one alternative normalized valuation.
  • Defined bloc payoff as expected true utility per member, with exchangeable treatment of identical reports, including when bloc reports coincide with residual reports.
  • Allowed residual societies and alternative reports to range over the entire rational piecewise-constant valuation domain, rather than only the input support, preserving the unrestricted-opponent meaning of NOM.
  • Made the incentive question concern a fixed selection of exact maximizing distributions, with best and worst cases taken over residual societies and lottery randomness averaged in bloc utility.

Results

All three questions can be settled:

  • Exact maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.
  • Additive \(\varepsilon\)-optimal maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(\varepsilon\) is inverse-polynomial in the size of the reduction.
  • A maximizing direct rule satisfying bloc-NOM exists. In fact, every maximizing direct rule with the stipulated exchangeability satisfies bloc-NOM, regardless of its selection among ties.

The hardness concerns choosing a distribution over configurations. The allocation problem for any fixed configuration of types remains the familiar Nash allocation problem treated by [Aziz–Ye](https://arxiv.org/html/1307.2908).

1. Fixed-profile facts and existence of rational maximizers

Let the common refinement intervals have lengths \(\ell_i\), and let \(a_{ji}\) be agent \(j\)'s density on interval \(i\). An allocation is represented by lengths \(x_{ji}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle x_{ji}\geq 0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^r x_{ji}=\ell_i,\qquad v_j=\sum_i a_{ji}x_{ji}.\)

Every such matrix is realizable by interval pieces: subdivide each refinement interval consecutively into the prescribed lengths.

For a fixed type profile, allocating each agent a fraction \(1/r\) of every refinement interval gives \(v_j=1/r\). The Nash product is continuous on the compact allocation polytope and therefore attains a positive maximum. Consequently, the sum of logarithms also attains a finite maximum.

1.1 Properties of every fixed-profile maximizer

Fix a maximizing allocation and its positive utilities \(v_j\). Define

\(\displaystyle \pi_i=\max_j\frac{a_{ji}}{v_j}.\)

If \(x_{ji}>0\), then

\(\displaystyle \frac{a_{ji}}{v_j}=\pi_i.\)

Indeed, otherwise transferring a sufficiently small length of interval \(i\) from agent \(j\) to an agent attaining the maximum would have positive directional derivative for the sum of logarithms.

It follows that every agent has expenditure exactly one:

\(\displaystyle \sum_i\pi_i x_{ji}=\sum_i\frac{a_{ji}x_{ji}}{v_j}=1.\)

Summing over agents gives \(\sum_i\pi_i\ell_i=r\). Since \(a_{ji}\leq v_j\pi_i\), normalization implies

\(\displaystyle 1=\sum_i a_{ji}\ell_i\leq v_j\sum_i\pi_i\ell_i=rv_j.\)

Thus every agent receives utility at least \(1/r\).

The same inequalities establish envy-freeness:

\(\displaystyle u_j(X_h)=\sum_i a_{ji}x_{hi}\leq v_j\sum_i\pi_i x_{hi}=v_j.\)

Finally, every maximizer is Pareto-optimal: weakly increasing all positive utilities and strictly increasing one would strictly increase the sum of logarithms.

1.2 Rational optimal allocations exist

Here is an explicit rationality argument, so exact maximization is not obstructed by the requested output representation.

Form the bipartite graph whose vertices are agents and intervals with \(\pi_i>0\), with an edge whenever \(x_{ji}>0\). Put \(\beta_j=1/v_j\). Along every edge,

\(\displaystyle \pi_i=a_{ji}\beta_j,\)

where \(a_{ji}>0\) is rational. Hence, within each connected component, all prices and all \(\beta_j\) are rational multiples of one common positive scalar.

If a component contains agent set \(A\) and interval set \(B\), its total expenditure satisfies

\(\displaystyle |A|=\sum_{i\in B}\pi_i\ell_i.\)

Every interval in \(B\) is fully allocated within the component. The displayed identity therefore determines the common scalar rationally. All \(\beta_j\), and hence all \(v_j\), are rational.

The allocation constraints together with these rational utility equalities define a nonempty rational polytope. It has a rational vertex, yielding a maximizing allocation with rational interval endpoints.

1.3 The distribution problem is a finite linear program

Let \(c=(c_t)_t\) range over nonnegative integer count vectors with \(\sum_t c_t=r\). Let \(V_c\) be the maximum average log utility for profile \(c\). The society problem is exactly

\(\displaystyle \max_{\lambda\geq0}\sum_c\lambda_cV_c,\qquad \sum_c\lambda_c\frac{c_t}{r}=\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t.\)

The marginal equations imply \(\sum_c\lambda_c=1\).

This feasible polytope is nonempty: choose an all-\(t\) configuration with probability \(\mu_t\), allocating equal shares within it. Its objective is \(-\log r\). An optimal vertex therefore exists. Its probabilities are rational because the constraints are rational, even though the objective coefficients involve logarithms. It uses at most \(\tau\) count profiles.

Combining this with Section 1.2 gives an exact maximizing finite mixture with rational probabilities and endpoints.

Moreover, every positive-probability configuration in any maximizing distribution is fixed-profile Nash-optimal. Replacing a nonoptimal allocation by an optimal allocation with the same type counts would preserve every marginal and strictly increase the objective.

2. Hardness of exact and additive maximization

We reduce from Independent Set: given a graph \(G_0\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether it has an independent set of size at least \(k\). This is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, equivalently by taking complements in the classical Clique problem from [Karp’s reductions](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9). We may restrict to \(2\leq k\leq |V(G_0)|\).

2.1 A graph encoded by valuation overlaps

Choose \(r\) to be the smallest power of two satisfying \(r\geq k+1\). Add \(r-1-k\) isolated vertices to \(G_0\), producing \(G\), and put \(n=|V(G)|\). Then

\(\displaystyle \alpha(G)\geq r-1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha(G_0)\geq k.\)

Construct a graph \(H\) with vertices

\(\displaystyle V(G)\cup\{z\}\cup D,\qquad D=\{d_0,\ldots,d_{r-1}\}.\)

Its edges are those of \(G\), together with every edge \(\{z,d_i\}\). There are no other edges. Put \(N=n+r+1\).

Create one equal-length cake interval for each vertex of \(H\), and one for each edge of \(H\). Thus the number of intervals is

\(\displaystyle m=N+|E(H)|.\)

For each vertex \(h\), define a type that values its private vertex interval and its incident edge intervals, with constant density

\(\displaystyle \frac{m}{\deg_H(h)+1}\)

on those intervals and density zero elsewhere. Each valued interval has utility \(1/(\deg_H(h)+1)\), so the type is normalized.

Two distinct types have overlapping positive supports exactly when their vertices are adjacent. The types are distinct because each has a private interval.

Therefore an \(r\)-agent configuration can give every agent utility one if and only if its types are distinct vertices forming an independent set in \(H\).

2.2 A uniform loss for conflicting configurations

Call a pair conflicting if its types are equal or adjacent in \(H\).

For an adjacent pair, use their shared edge interval; for an equal pair, use their shared private interval. On this interval each agent's value is at least \(1/N\).

If the two agents receive fractions \(q_1,q_2\) of this interval, then \(q_1+q_2\leq1\). Writing their full-interval values as \(w_1,w_2\), their combined utility deficit is at least

\(\displaystyle w_1(1-q_1)+w_2(1-q_2) \geq \min\{w_1,w_2\}(2-q_1-q_2) \geq \frac1N.\)

For every positive utility \(v\leq1\), \(\log v\leq v-1\). Hence every configuration containing a conflicting pair has average log utility at most

\(\displaystyle -\frac1{rN}.\)

A zero utility only strengthens this bound.

2.3 Fixed positive marginals

Let \(P\) be the smallest power of two with \(P\geq n\), and define

\(\displaystyle \eta=\frac1{4P},\qquad \delta=\frac1{8P}.\)

Prescribe expected counts \(y_h=r\mu_h\) by

\(\displaystyle y_z=\delta,\qquad y_v=\eta\quad(v\in V(G)),\)

\(\displaystyle y_{d_0}=1-n\eta+(r-2)\delta,\qquad y_{d_i}=1-\delta\quad(1\leq i\leq r-1).\)

All are positive, and

\(\displaystyle \delta+n\eta+[1-n\eta+(r-2)\delta]+(r-1)(1-\delta)=r.\)

Thus \(\mu_h=y_h/r\) is a valid society. Since \(r\) and \(P\) are powers of two, its masses are even dyadic rationals.

These marginals are specified without knowing an independent set.

2.4 Yes-instances have optimum zero

Suppose \(G\) has an independent set \(S\) of size \(r-1\). Consider the independent configurations

\(\displaystyle C_0=D,\qquad C_v=\{v\}\cup(D\setminus\{d_0\}),\qquad C_S=\{z\}\cup S.\)

Give them probabilities

\(\displaystyle \lambda(C_S)=\delta,\)

\(\displaystyle \lambda(C_v)= \begin{cases} \eta-\delta,&v\in S,\\ \eta,&v\notin S, \end{cases}\)

and

\(\displaystyle \lambda(C_0)=1-n\eta+(r-2)\delta.\)

They are positive and sum to one. Their expected counts are exactly the prescribed \(y_h\):

  • Each vertex in \(S\) appears with probability \((\eta-\delta)+\delta=\eta\); every other vertex of \(G\) appears with probability \(\eta\).
  • Type \(z\) appears with probability \(\delta\).
  • Type \(d_0\) appears only in \(C_0\).
  • Every other filler appears in every configuration except \(C_S\), hence with probability \(1-\delta\).

In each configuration the positive supports are disjoint. Allocate each agent its entire positive support and distribute unvalued intervals arbitrarily. All utilities are one, using rational endpoints.

The objective is zero. Since every normalized utility is at most one, zero is the global upper bound.

2.5 No-instances have an inverse-polynomial gap

Suppose \(G\) has no independent set of size \(r-1\). Every configuration containing \(z\) has a conflicting pair: a conflict-free such configuration would exclude all fillers and contain \(r-1\) independent vertices of \(G\).

Let \(Z\) denote the event that \(z\) appears. Feasibility gives expected count \(\delta\), while its count is at most \(r\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \Pr(Z)\geq\frac{\delta}{r}.\)

All configuration objectives are nonpositive, and those in \(Z\) are at most \(-1/(rN)\). Therefore every feasible distribution satisfies

\(\displaystyle F(\Pi)\leq-\frac{\Pr(Z)}{rN} \leq-\frac{\delta}{r^2N}.\)

Put \(\Delta=\delta/(r^2N)\). We have proved the gap

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}=0\ \text{in yes-instances},\qquad \operatorname{OPT}\leq-\Delta\ \text{in no-instances}.\)

The parameters \(m,\tau,r,L\) are polynomially bounded in the graph input size, and \(\Delta\) is inverse-polynomial.

This proves exact maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

2.6 Hardness with explicit approximate outputs

Set \(\varepsilon=\Delta/2\). In a yes-instance, an additive \(\varepsilon\)-optimal output must have \(F(\Pi)\geq-\Delta/2\).

If every positive-probability configuration containing \(z\) were conflicting, the calculation in Section 2.5 would instead give \(F(\Pi)\leq-\Delta\). Thus the output must contain a conflict-free configuration containing \(z\).

Inspecting its types reveals \(r-1\) independent vertices of \(G\). At most \(r-1-k\) are padding vertices, so at least \(k\) belong to \(G_0\).

In a no-instance, no such configuration exists. We can therefore decide Independent Set simply by inspecting the explicitly returned mixture, without computing or comparing logarithms.

Here \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\) is polynomially bounded. Hence additive \(\varepsilon\)-optimal maximization in the requested running time is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

3. Every maximizing exchangeable rule is bloc-NOM

Fix \(r,b,t\), and let \(M\) be any maximizing rule. We prove

\(\displaystyle \inf_\nu U(t,\nu)=\frac1r,\qquad \sup_\nu U(t,\nu)=\min\left\{1,\frac1{rb}\right\}.\)

We then bound the corresponding extremes of every alternative report.

3.1 A universal upper bound for every report

For any reported type \(s\), write

\(\displaystyle N_s(a)=\sum_j\mathbf1\{t_j=s\},\qquad S_s(a)=\sum_{j:t_j=s}u_t(X_j).\)

Because the pieces are disjoint and the true valuation is normalized,

\(\displaystyle S_s(a)\leq1,\qquad S_s(a)\leq N_s(a).\)

Feasibility gives \(\mathbb E N_s=r\rho_s(s)\), and \(\rho_s(s)\geq b\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle U(s,\nu)\leq \min\left\{1,\frac1{r\rho_s(s)}\right\} \leq \min\left\{1,\frac1{rb}\right\}.\)

3.2 Truthful worst case and the misreporting worst-case bound

Every positive-probability configuration selected by \(M\) is fixed-profile Nash-optimal. By Section 1.1, each truthfully reporting \(t\)-agent receives utility at least \(1/r\). Averaging gives

\(\displaystyle U(t,\nu)\geq\frac1r.\)

For \(\nu=\delta_t\), the truthful society is homogeneous. Its pieces have total \(t\)-utility one, so the defined per-member utility is exactly \(1/r\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \inf_\nu U(t,\nu)=\frac1r.\)

Now suppose \(s\neq t\), and again choose \(\nu=\delta_t\). In every selected configuration, each of its \(r-N_s\) agents reporting \(t\) receives true utility at least \(1/r\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle S_s(a)\leq1-\frac{r-N_s(a)}r=\frac{N_s(a)}r.\)

Taking expectations and dividing by \(r\rho_s(s)\) yields

\(\displaystyle U(s,\delta_t)\leq\frac1r.\)

Hence

\(\displaystyle \inf_\nu U(s,\nu)\leq\frac1r=\inf_\nu U(t,\nu).\)

For \(s=t\), the inequality is equality.

3.3 Truthful best case: concentration of opponents

It remains to show that truthful reporting approaches the universal upper bound, independently of tie selection.

Choose a proper interval \(I\) with rational endpoints and arbitrarily small

\(\displaystyle \theta=u_t(I)<1.\)

Such intervals exist because the piecewise-constant density of \(t\) is bounded. Let \(q\) be the normalized uniform type supported on \(I\), and choose \(\nu=\delta_q\). This is an admissible rational opponent society. Since \(u_t(I)<1\), the types \(q\) and \(t\) differ, and the truthful society has masses \(b\) and \(1-b\).

Let \(W(k)\) be the maximum sum of logarithms for a profile containing \(k\) agents of type \(t\) and \(r-k\) of type \(q\). Define

\(\displaystyle g(k)=-k\log k-(r-k)\log(r-k),\)

with \(0\log0=0\).

Within each nonempty identical-type group, total own-type utility is at most one. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality therefore gives

\(\displaystyle W(k)\leq g(k).\)

For \(0<k<r\), give the \(t\)-agents equal shares of the cake outside \(I\), and the \(q\)-agents equal shares inside \(I\). This yields

\(\displaystyle W(k)\geq g(k)+k\log(1-\theta).\)

At the endpoints, \(W(0)=g(0)\) and \(W(r)=g(r)\). Thus, uniformly in \(k\),

\(\displaystyle g(k)-d\leq W(k)\leq g(k),\qquad d=-r\log(1-\theta).\)

3.4 Every maximizing distribution balances the truthful count

The function \(g\) is strictly concave on \([0,r]\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \gamma=\min_{1\leq k\leq r-1} [\,2g(k)-g(k-1)-g(k+1)\,]>0.\)

Choose \(I\) sufficiently small that \(2d<\gamma\). Then

\(\displaystyle 2W(k)-W(k-1)-W(k+1)\geq\gamma-2d>0.\)

Thus the successive slopes \(W(k+1)-W(k)\) strictly decrease.

Put \(\xi=rb\). Feasibility requires \(\mathbb E N_t=\xi\). The decreasing-slope property implies that every distribution maximizing \(\mathbb E W(N_t)\) is supported on the two integers adjacent to \(\xi\), or only on \(\xi\) when it is integral.

For completeness, when \(\xi\) is nonintegral, the affine line through \(W(\lfloor\xi\rfloor)\) and \(W(\lceil\xi\rceil)\) strictly dominates every other integer point. Taking expectations proves the assertion and its equality condition. When \(\xi\) is integral, choose a supporting-line slope strictly between its left and right slopes; equality then holds only at that integer.

Since \(M\) maximizes the original objective and uses fixed-profile optimal allocations, this count conclusion applies to every selection made by \(M\). In particular,

\(\displaystyle \Pr(N_t>0)=\min\{1,rb\}.\)

3.5 Pareto optimality gives almost all outside value to truthful agents

Whenever \(N_t>0\), fixed-profile Pareto optimality requires the \(t\)-agents collectively to receive all \(t\)-valued cake outside \(I\). Otherwise some such cake could be transferred from a \(q\)-agent to a \(t\)-agent, strictly increasing the latter's utility without decreasing the former's utility.

Thus, in every configuration with \(N_t>0\),

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j:t_j=t}u_t(X_j)\geq1-\theta.\)

Since \(\rho_t(t)=b\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle U(t,\delta_q)\geq \frac{(1-\theta)\Pr(N_t>0)}{rb} =(1-\theta)\min\left\{1,\frac1{rb}\right\}.\)

Taking rational intervals with \(\theta\) tending to zero, and using Section 3.1, proves

\(\displaystyle \sup_\nu U(t,\nu)=\min\left\{1,\frac1{rb}\right\}.\)

Consequently, for every report \(s\),

\(\displaystyle \sup_\nu U(s,\nu)\leq\sup_\nu U(t,\nu).\)

Together with Section 3.2, both required bloc-NOM inequalities hold for every report, whether or not it is profitable somewhere.

4. Existence of a fixed exchangeable selection

Section 1 supplies a rational maximizing finite mixture for every rational reported society. Fix one selection among these maximizers for each society. Symmetrize each selected configuration over permutations of the agent positions.

This produces another finite maximizing distribution, preserves all type marginals, and treats identical reports exchangeably. Fixing this symmetrized selection defines a direct rule satisfying the stipulated tie convention.

Section 3 applies to every such selection. Therefore a maximizing bloc-NOM direct rule exists, and its bloc-NOM property requires no particular choice among maximizing distributions.
