Conclusions

Computing the specified approximate equilibrium is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, rational prices can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\) and \(\log(1/\delta)\), with exact optimal demands represented implicitly as requested.

Using the finite-buyer convergence theorem stipulated in the question, the stated synchronous convergence guarantee transfers exactly to the mass formulation. However, this process does not have a uniform polynomial bound in the binary input length: even with two goods, one supported type, and constant accuracy, step sizes covered by such a guarantee can require exponentially many rounds.

The polynomial-time algorithm below is independent of the convergence theorem.

1. Exact aggregation and the demand formula

Write \(w_t=\mu_tB_t\), so that \(w_t>0\) and \(\sum_t w_t=1\).

Each utility is homogeneous of degree one: \(u_t(\lambda x)=\lambda u_t(x)\) for \(\lambda>0\), including its continuous extension. Consequently, replacing type \(t\) by one finite buyer with budget \(w_t\) and the same utility gives aggregate purchases

\(X_{t,j}^{J}(p)=\mu_t x_{t,j}^{J}(p)\).

Indeed, multiplication by \(\mu_t\) maps the original budget-feasible set bijectively onto the new one, and multiplies utility by the positive constant \(\mu_t\). Thus it preserves optimizers. This construction uses exactly \(\tau\) buyers and has polynomial encoding length; no replication according to mass denominators is involved.

We next derive demands explicitly. Put

\(r=\dfrac{1}{1-\rho}>0,\qquad s=r-1=\dfrac{\rho}{1-\rho}\ne0,\)

and, setting coefficients to zero outside their bundles, define

\(v_{t,j}^{J}=\dfrac{a_{t,j}^{J}}{c_t^{J}},\qquad d_t^{J}(p)=\sum_jv_{t,j}^{J}p_j.\)

If \(y_t^{J}=c_t^{J}\min_{j\in J}x_{t,j}^{J}/a_{t,j}^{J}\), achieving this bundle quantity costs at least \(d_t^{J}(p)y_t^{J}\). Equality is achieved by \(x_{t,j}^{J}=v_{t,j}^{J}y_t^{J}\).

For a buyer with budget \(b>0\), consider the reduced problem with constraint \(\sum_Jd_t^{J}y_t^{J}\le b\). Its unique optimal bundle quantities are

\(y_t^{J}=\dfrac{b(d_t^{J})^{-r}}{\sum_H(d_t^{H})^{1-r}}.\)

Here is a verification covering both exponent ranges.

  • If \(0<\rho<1\), maximizing utility is equivalent to maximizing the strictly concave function \(\sum_J(y_t^{J})^\rho\). The displayed positive vector spends exactly \(b\), and its derivatives satisfy

\(\rho(y_t^{J})^{\rho-1}=\lambda d_t^{J}\)
for one positive \(\lambda\). The concavity inequality therefore proves global optimality and uniqueness.

  • If \(\rho<0\), boundary vectors have zero utility, whereas positive feasible vectors have positive utility. On positive vectors, maximizing utility is equivalent to minimizing the strictly convex function \(\sum_J(y_t^{J})^\rho\). The displayed vector satisfies

\(\rho(y_t^{J})^{\rho-1}+\lambda d_t^{J}=0\)
for one positive \(\lambda\). The convexity inequality proves global optimality and uniqueness.

The reduced optimum spends the entire budget. Hence any optimal original purchase must attain the minimum cost of every bundle: positive prices leave no room for additional quantities. Thus original purchases are also unique.

Define

\(\alpha_t^{J}(p)=\dfrac{(d_t^{J}(p))^{-s}}{\sum_H(d_t^{H}(p))^{-s}},\qquad h_t^{J}(p)=\dfrac{v_t^{J}}{d_t^{J}(p)}.\)

The exact aggregate demands are consequently

\(D(p)=\sum_t w_t\sum_{J\in\mathcal J_t}\alpha_t^{J}(p)h_t^{J}(p).\)

They satisfy the expenditure identity

\(\sum_jp_jD_j(p)=\sum_t w_t=1.\)

In particular, \(D_j(p)\le 1/p_j\) at every positive price vector.

2. A convex objective whose gradient is minus excess demand

Let \(k_t=|\mathcal J_t|\). On positive prices define

\(F(p)=\sum_jp_j+\sum_t\dfrac{w_t}{s}\log\left(\dfrac{1}{k_t}\sum_{J\in\mathcal J_t}(d_t^{J}(p))^{-s}\right).\)

The factors \(1/k_t\) change the objective only by a price-independent constant. Differentiation gives

\(\nabla F(p)=\mathbf1-D(p)=-z(p).\)

We need convexity and a quantitative smoothness bound. For each type, let expectation and covariance refer to the probabilities \(\alpha_t^{J}(p)\), and put \(\bar h_t=\mathbb E_t[h_t^{J}]\). Direct differentiation yields

\(\nabla^2F(p)=\sum_t w_t\left(r\,\operatorname{Cov}_t(h_t^{J})+\bar h_t\bar h_t^{\mathsf T}\right).\)

For completeness, the identities underlying this calculation are

\(\nabla\alpha_t^{J}=-s\alpha_t^{J}(h_t^{J}-\bar h_t),\qquad \nabla h_t^{J}=-h_t^{J}(h_t^{J})^{\mathsf T}.\)

Thus the Hessian contribution is
\(\mathbb E_t[hh^{\mathsf T}]+s\operatorname{Cov}_t(h) =r\operatorname{Cov}_t(h)+\bar h_t\bar h_t^{\mathsf T}\).
Both terms in the final expression are positive semidefinite, proving convexity without any rank assumption on bundle incidence vectors.

Suppose every coordinate of \(p\) is at least \(\eta>0\). Nonnegativity of \(v_t^{J}\) gives

\(\|h_t^{J}\|_2\le\|h_t^{J}\|_1 =\dfrac{\sum_jv_{t,j}^{J}}{d_t^{J}(p)}\le\dfrac1\eta.\)

For \(r\ge1\),

\(r\operatorname{Cov}(h)+\bar h\bar h^{\mathsf T} =r\mathbb E[hh^{\mathsf T}]-(r-1)\bar h\bar h^{\mathsf T} \preceq r\mathbb E[hh^{\mathsf T}].\)

For \(0<r\le1\), the same matrix equals
\(r\mathbb E[hh^{\mathsf T}]+(1-r)\bar h\bar h^{\mathsf T} \preceq\mathbb E[hh^{\mathsf T}]\), because covariance is positive semidefinite.

Since the weights sum to one, we obtain

\(0\preceq\nabla^2F(p)\preceq M I,\qquad M=\dfrac{\max\{1,r\}}{\eta^2}.\)

Hence the gradient is \(M\)-Lipschitz on every convex region with prices bounded below by \(\eta\).

3. Approximate minimization produces the required equilibrium

Set

\(\eta=\delta/4,\qquad C=[\eta,2]^m,\qquad M=\dfrac{\max\{1,r\}}{\eta^2},\qquad \gamma=\dfrac{\delta^2}{8M}.\)

The compact box \(C\) contains a minimizer of \(F\). We prove that every \(q\in C\) satisfying

\(F(q)\le\min_{p\in C}F(p)+\gamma\)

is a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium.

Write \(g=\nabla F(q)\) and \(h=\delta/M\). Notice that

\(h\le\delta^3/16<\delta/16.\)

Excess demand. Suppose \(z_j(q)>\delta\), equivalently \(g_j<-\delta\). Then \(D_j(q)>1\), so the expenditure identity implies \(q_j<1\). Therefore \(q+he_j\in C\). The Hessian bound gives

\(F(q+he_j)\le F(q)+hg_j+\dfrac M2h^2 <F(q)-\dfrac{\delta^2}{2M}.\)

This contradicts the assumed objective gap \(\gamma=\delta^2/(8M)\). Consequently \(z_j(q)\le\delta\).

Undersupply at a large price. Suppose \(z_j(q)<-\delta\) and \(q_j>\delta\). Then \(g_j>\delta\). Because \(q_j-\eta>3\delta/4>h\), we have \(q-he_j\in C\), and similarly

\(F(q-he_j)\le F(q)-hg_j+\dfrac M2h^2 <F(q)-\dfrac{\delta^2}{2M},\)

again a contradiction. Thus \(z_j(q)<-\delta\) implies \(q_j\le\delta\).

These are exactly the two required conditions. Demands remain the exact optimizers defined in Section 1. The definition does not require output prices to sum to one.

4. Binary-input complexity of the convex algorithm

The preceding reduction becomes a polynomial-time algorithm once we verify numerical access to \(F\).

Polynomial-precision evaluation

At a rational positive query \(p\), each \(d_t^{J}(p)\) is computed by rational arithmetic. Evaluate the probabilities using

\(u_t^{J}=-s\log d_t^{J},\qquad \alpha_t^{J}=\dfrac{\exp(u_t^{J}-u_{\max})}{\sum_H\exp(u_t^{H}-u_{\max})}.\)

Subtracting the maximum avoids exponentiating large positive numbers. To obtain a prescribed absolute error, sufficiently negative arguments can be discarded: their total omitted contribution is bounded by the number of bundles times the corresponding exponential cutoff.

The objective uses the same calculation:

\(\log\left(\dfrac1{k_t}\sum_J e^{u_t^{J}}\right) =u_{\max}+\log\sum_J e^{u_t^{J}-u_{\max}}-\log k_t.\)

Both \(s\) and \(1/s\) have polynomial binary encoding length. Multiplication by \(s\), or division by it, therefore requires only polynomially many additional precision bits—even when their numerical magnitudes are exponential.

Logarithms of positive rationals and exponentials on a bounded argument interval admit polynomial-time rational approximations. One elementary construction uses binary range reduction followed by the convergent series for \(\log\) and \(\exp\). After the exponential cutoff above, the retained argument interval has length polynomial in the requested precision and the input length.

It follows that \(F(p)\) and \(\nabla F(p)\) can be approximated to absolute error \(2^{-P}\) in time polynomial in \(L\), the query encoding length, \(P\), and the explicitly listed incidences. Their number is at most \(mK\).

Applying weak convex optimization

We use the standard weak ellipsoid optimization theorem: a convex differentiable function on a rational box can be minimized to additive error \(\gamma\), with rational feasible output, in polynomial time when its value and gradient admit polynomial-time arbitrary-precision approximation, and bounds on its magnitude and Lipschitz constant have polynomial encoding length. The dependence on accuracy and these numerical bounds is logarithmic. This is the weak-oracle framework of [Grötschel, Lovász and Schrijver](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-78240-4).

Here all hypotheses hold:

  • The box \(C=[\delta/4,2]^m\) has rational endpoints, an explicit interior ball, and polynomial encoding length.
  • Convexity and approximate value and gradient oracles were established above.
  • The expenditure identity gives

\(\sum_jD_j(p)\le1/\eta\), and therefore
\(\|\nabla F(p)\|_2\le\sqrt m+1/\eta\).

  • If \(d_{\min}\) and \(d_{\max}\) bound all bundle costs on \(C\), then

\[ \]
\( \left|\dfrac1s\log\left(\dfrac1{k_t}\sum_J(d_t^{J})^{-s}\right)\right| \le\max\{|\log d_{\min}|,|\log d_{\max}|\}. \)
This follows because the average lies between the minimum and maximum of its summands, with inequality reversal handled when dividing by negative \(s\). Positive input coefficients and the box bounds provide
\(|\log d_{\min}|+|\log d_{\max}|=O(L+\log m+\log(1/\delta))\).

  • Finally,

\(\log(1/\gamma)=O(\log(1/\delta)+\log\max\{1,r\})\),
and \(\log\max\{1,r\}=O(L)\).

To explain the role of inexact gradients, suppose a query is more than \(\gamma\) above the minimum. A gradient approximation with error at most \(\gamma/(4\operatorname{diam}C)\) still separates that query from the sublevel set \(F\le\min_C F+\gamma/2\). Convexity proves this directly. That sublevel set contains an explicitly bounded ball: move a minimizer slightly toward the box center, then use the Lipschitz bound. The ball radius has polynomial logarithmic encoding length. These are precisely the geometric and precision bounds needed by the weak ellipsoid theorem.

Thus the algorithm outputs the rational \(q\) of Section 3 in polynomial bit time. In particular, it is polynomial in all parameters requested in the question.

5. Transfer of the synchronous convergence theorem

This section uses the finite-buyer convergence result supplied as a premise in the question, stated as [Theorem 22 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1401.6637v3#Thm22).

Apply that theorem to the \(\tau\)-buyer market of Section 1, with budgets \(w_t=\mu_tB_t\). Its utilities have exactly the required nested CES–Leontief form; all supported budgets are positive, their sum is one, and every good occurs in a bundle. Its aggregate demands coincide with the society’s demands at every positive price.

Therefore the two markets have identical synchronous trajectories. The finite-buyer theorem supplies effective constants depending on \(a,c,w,\rho\), hence on the type data alone, giving the asserted guarantee:

\(N\ge\dfrac{\kappa_2\log(1/p_{\min}^0)}{\epsilon^2\delta^3},\qquad \epsilon\le1/\kappa_1.\)

The constants are independent of \(\delta\) and \(p^0\).

Positivity and normalization are preserved directly:

\(p_j^{k+1}\ge(1-\epsilon)p_j^k>0,\)

and

\(\sum_jp_j^{k+1} =(1-\epsilon)\sum_jp_j^k+\epsilon\sum_jp_j^kD_j(p^k)=1.\)

For \(m=1\), the initial price is already \(1\) and demand is exactly the unit supply.

6. Exponentially many rounds can be necessary

Here is a binary-input obstruction to a uniform polynomial-round guarantee.

Take two goods, one type with mass and budget \(1\), two singleton bundles, and all coefficients equal to \(1\). For an integer \(n\ge3\), set

\(r=2^n,\qquad \rho=1-1/r,\qquad s=r-1.\)

This instance has encoding length \(O(n)\).

Write the normalized prices as \((x,1-x)\). The expenditure share on good \(1\) is

\(Q(x)=\dfrac{x^{-s}}{x^{-s}+(1-x)^{-s}},\)

so the update is

\(T_\epsilon(x)=(1-\epsilon)x+\epsilon Q(x).\)

Any uniform step-size threshold must shrink exponentially

Let \(x_*=\tfrac12-\tfrac1{4r}\). Then

\(\log\dfrac{1-x_*}{x_*} =\log\dfrac{1+1/(2r)}{1-1/(2r)}\ge1/r.\)

Hence

\(\left(\dfrac{1-x_*}{x_*}\right)^s \ge\exp((r-1)/r)\ge\exp(7/8)>2,\)

which implies \(Q(x_*)>2/3\).

Define

\(\epsilon_c=\dfrac{1-2x_*}{Q(x_*)-x_*}.\)

All quantities are rational, since \(s\) is an integer. Moreover,

\(0<\epsilon_c<\dfrac{1/(2r)}{1/6}=\dfrac3r<\dfrac12.\)

By construction, \(T_{\epsilon_c}(x_*)=1-x_*\). The symmetry \(Q(1-x)=1-Q(x)\) gives \(T_{\epsilon_c}(1-x_*)=x_*\). Thus these rational initial prices generate an exact two-cycle.

At \(x_*\),

\(D_1=\dfrac{Q(x_*)}{x_*}>\dfrac43,\)

and at \(1-x_*\) the same inequality holds for good \(2\). Neither cycle point is a \(1/4\)-approximate equilibrium.

Consequently, any constants satisfying the question’s guarantee for every permitted initial price and every \(\epsilon\le1/\kappa_1\) must exclude \(\epsilon_c\). Therefore

\(1/\kappa_1<\epsilon_c<3/r,\qquad \kappa_1>r/3.\)

Allowed step sizes require exponentially many rounds

Now start at \(x^0=1/4\) and fix \(\delta=1/4\). Whenever \(x\le2/5\),

\(\left(\dfrac{1-x}{x}\right)^s\ge(3/2)^7>2,\)

so \(Q(x)>2/3\) and

\(D_1(x)=Q(x)/x>5/3.\)

Such a price is not a \(\delta\)-approximate equilibrium. The process must therefore reach \(x>2/5\).

Each round changes \(x\) by at most \(\epsilon\), because \(x,Q(x)\in[0,1]\). Reaching \(2/5\) from \(1/4\) consequently requires more than \(3/(20\epsilon)\) rounds. For every step size covered by the uniform guarantee,

\(N>\dfrac3{20\epsilon}>\dfrac r{20}=\dfrac{2^n}{20}.\)

Thus the guaranteed process can require exponentially many rounds in the market’s encoding length, with \(m,\tau,K\) and \(1/\delta\) all constant.

7. Numerical complexity of implementing the process

Exact trajectories need not be rational. For example, take the same singleton market with \(\rho=-1\), initial prices \((1/4,3/4)\), and rational nonzero \(\epsilon\). The expenditure share on good \(1\) is \(1/(1+\sqrt3)\), so

\(p_1^1=(1-\epsilon)/4+\epsilon/(1+\sqrt3)\)

is irrational. Exact rational iteration is therefore not a Turing implementation of the stated process.

Nevertheless, a rational approximation to an \(N\)-round trajectory can be computed in time polynomial in \(N\) and the binary inputs. Here is an explicit precision argument.

Set

\(a_0=p_{\min}^0\,2^{-N}.\)

Every exact iterate through time \(N\) has all prices at least \(a_0\). On the box \([a_0/2,2]^m\), the earlier Hessian bound gives

\(M_N=4\max\{1,r\}/a_0^2.\)

For the update map \(T(p)=(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon\,\operatorname{diag}(p)D(p)\), differentiation gives

\(DT(p)=(1-\epsilon)I+\epsilon\operatorname{diag}(D(p)) -\epsilon\operatorname{diag}(p)\nabla^2F(p).\)

Using \(D_j(p)\le2/a_0\), an upper bound on its operator norm is

\(A=2+2/a_0+8\max\{1,r\}/a_0^2.\)

If each numerical update has Euclidean error at most \(\nu\), trajectory errors satisfy \(e_{k+1}\le Ae_k+\nu\). Choose

\(b_*=\min\{a_0/4,\delta/4,\delta/(4M_N)\},\qquad \nu=b_*/(NA^N).\)

Then \(e_k\le b_*\) through time \(N\), by induction. This also keeps all approximate iterates inside the box used for the derivative bound. The required precision satisfies

\(\log(1/\nu)=O(N(N+L)+\log N+\log(1/\delta)),\)

so each demand evaluation and the complete simulation take polynomial bit time in \(N\) and the input length. For a computational implementation, \(\epsilon\) must itself be rational or efficiently approximable; its encoding length is included when necessary.

Finally, run long enough that the exact endpoint is a \(\delta/2\)-approximate equilibrium. The computed endpoint \(q\) then satisfies

\(\|D(q)-D(p^N)\|_\infty\le M_Nb_*\le\delta/4.\)

Thus \(z_j(q)\le3\delta/4\). If \(z_j(q)<-\delta\), then \(z_j(p^N)<-3\delta/4\), so \(p_j^N\le\delta/2\); the price approximation implies \(q_j\le3\delta/4\). Hence the rational endpoint meets the requested definition with its exact implicit optimal demands.

The process is therefore implementable with polynomial bit overhead per numerical horizon, but its horizon can be exponential in the binary market input. The convex algorithm of Sections 2–4 avoids that limitation.
