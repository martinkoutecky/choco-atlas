The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite employer types \(E\) and applicant types \(A\) with rational masses \(w\) and \(v\), partial orders, admissible type-level strict refinements, a realized refinement profile \(\rho\), batch interview costs \(c_{ea}\), and budget \(B\), does there exist an interview-mass allocation \(q\) of cost \(\sum_{e,a} c_{ea}q_{ea}\le B\) whose revealed comparisons certify that the employer-optimal stable mass matching \(x^\rho\) remains employer-optimal for every compatible refinement? In the diligent variant, every positive edge of \(x^\rho\) must be covered by an interview.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: nonempty finite sets of employer types \(E\) and applicant types \(A\), each with positive mass \(w_e\) or \(v_a\) encoded as a binary rational. Masses need not sum to one or have equal totals across sides. Let \(\tau=|E|+|A|\) be the support size, not the number of possible preference types, let \(m=\max\{|E|,|A|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a fixed acceptable subset of opposite-side types and a strict partial order on that subset. Admissible refinements are all independently chosen strict linear extensions, with every acceptable type preferred to being unmatched and every unacceptable type ranked below it. All members of a type share its refinement; a realized admissible profile \(\rho\) is supplied.
A mass matching is a real matrix \(x\ge0\) with \(\sum_a x_{ea}\le w_e\), \(\sum_e x_{ea}\le v_a\), and \(x_{ea}=0\) unless the pair is mutually acceptable. Unassigned mass is unmatched.
Under a refinement \(\sigma\), a mutually acceptable pair \((e,a)\) blocks precisely when both \(w_e-\sum_{b\succeq_e^\sigma a}x_{eb}>0\) and \(v_a-\sum_{f\succeq_a^\sigma e}x_{fa}>0\); stability means that no pair blocks. A stable matching is employer-optimal if each employer’s assigned distribution first-order stochastically dominates its distribution in every other stable matching, with unmatched mass included. Write \(x^\rho\) for the employer-optimal stable mass matching under \(\rho\).
An interview allocation is a real matrix satisfying \(0\le q_{ea}\le\min\{w_e,v_a\}\). A type may interview several opposite-side types, so there are no aggregate row or column bounds. Interviews with unacceptable types are permitted but reveal comparisons only among acceptable interviewed types.
For each employer \(e\), all comparisons under \(\rho_e\) between acceptable types \(a,b\) with \(q_{ea}>0\) and \(q_{eb}>0\) are revealed; symmetrically, each applicant type learns comparisons between its positively interviewed acceptable employer types. These comparisons are shared throughout the type. A compatible refinement extends the initial partial orders and all revealed comparisons.
Given nonnegative binary-rational costs \(c_{ea}\) and budget \(B\), decide whether some allocation satisfies \(\sum_{e,a}c_{ea}q_{ea}\le B\) and makes \(x^\rho\) employer-optimal under every compatible refinement. In the diligent variant also require \(x^\rho_{ea}>0\Rightarrow q_{ea}>0\).

Is this continuous optimality-certificate decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard? What is the classification of its diligent variant?

The discrete minimum-interview optimality-certificate problem is NP-hard even for ranked equivalence classes; the diligent version is also NP-hard ([source](https://users.eecs.northwestern.edu/~nickle/pubs/interviews.pdf)).
For discrete societies, existence of a matching that is employer-optimal under every refinement of given partial orders is decidable in polynomial time ([source](https://immorlica.com/pubs/matchingInference.pdf)). No result settling the mass-allocation problem defined above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Mass normalization was unspecified: allow arbitrary positive rational masses and unequal side totals, preserving the discrete model’s potentially unequal market sizes.
  • Admissible refinements were unspecified: use all independent strict linear extensions of the supplied partial orders, preserving fixed acceptability.
  • Stable mass matching and employer optimality were undefined: use blocking by positive masses on both sides assigned to worse partners or unmatched, and employer optimality by first-order stochastic dominance.
  • Interview allocation feasibility was unspecified: allow real amounts bounded by the smaller endpoint mass on each edge, without aggregate bounds because agents can conduct several interviews.
  • The connection between interview amounts and revealed comparisons was unspecified: positive interview amounts activate type-level interviews, and comparisons among positively interviewed acceptable types are shared throughout each homogeneous type.
  • Interviews involving unacceptable candidates were unspecified: permit them, but only comparisons among acceptable interviewed candidates are informative.
  • Cost and budget domains were unspecified: use nonnegative binary rationals, allowing a zero budget.
  • Diligent coverage was unspecified quantitatively: interpret coverage of a positive matching edge as a positive interview allocation on that edge.

Classification

Under polynomial many-one reductions, the ordinary problem is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete, even when all applicant masses equal $1$, all employer masses belong to $\{1,2\}$, and every pair is mutually acceptable. The diligent variant is in $\mathrm{P}$, with running time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

Thus the proposed $\mathrm{P}$-versus-$\mathrm{NP}$-hard dichotomy misses a third classification. The ordinary problem is in $\mathrm{P}$ exactly if $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$, and it is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard exactly if $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

Positive budgets have an immediate certificate because arbitrarily small positive interviews reveal comparisons. The substantive complexity occurs at budget zero.

1. Eliminating the continuous allocation choice

Write $x=x^\rho$.

If $B<0$, feasibility is impossible because interview costs are nonnegative. Hence assume $B\geq 0$.

Positive budgets

Suppose $B>0$. Define
$$ h=\min_{e,a}\min\{w_e,v_a\}, \qquad C=\sum_{e,a}c_{ea}, \qquad \varepsilon=\min\left\{h,\frac{B}{2(1+C)}\right\}. $$
Every mass is positive, so $\varepsilon>0$. Set $q_{ea}=\varepsilon$ for every pair. This allocation satisfies the individual bounds, and
$$ \sum_{e,a}c_{ea}q_{ea} =C\varepsilon \leq \frac{BC}{2(1+C)} <B. $$
Every type interviews every acceptable opposite-side type. Consequently, all relevant comparisons under $\rho$ are revealed, and every compatible refinement agrees with $\rho$ on its acceptable types. This certifies $x$, and the allocation is diligent.

The rational number $\varepsilon$ has polynomial encoding length.

Zero budget

Suppose $B=0$, and put
$$ F=\{(e,a):c_{ea}=0\}. $$
Nonnegativity gives
$$ q_{ea}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (e,a)\in F. $$
Consider the maximal free allocation
$$ q^F_{ea}= \begin{cases} \min\{w_e,v_a\},&(e,a)\in F,\\ 0,&(e,a)\notin F. \end{cases} $$
Every zero-budget allocation reveals a subset of its comparisons. Adding comparisons only restricts compatible refinements. Therefore:

  • The ordinary instance is YES exactly when $x$ is employer-optimal under every refinement compatible with $q^F$.
  • The diligent instance is YES exactly when the preceding condition holds and

$$ x_{ea}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{ea}=0. $$

Let $P_e,P_a$ be the transitive closures of the initial orders together with the comparisons revealed by $q^F$. They are strict partial orders because every added comparison agrees with $\rho$.

2. Fixed-profile facts and universal stability

For a fixed strict profile, say that one distribution dominates another if every preference prefix contains at least as much mass, with unmatched mass placed below all acceptable types.

We use the following stable-allocation facts: the employer-optimal allocation exists uniquely; it dominates every stable allocation for employers and is dominated by every stable allocation for applicants; and every stable allocation assigns the same total mass to each individual type. These follow from Lemma 8 and Theorems 3, 5, and 10 of the [corrected Baïou–Balinski paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302). Its comparison relation transfers mass from worse positions to better positions and hence implies prefix dominance.

The hypotheses apply here. Retain mutually acceptable edges and give edge $(e,a)$ capacity $\min\{w_e,v_a\}$. At this capacity, one endpoint has its entire quota on the edge, so the pair cannot block. Below capacity, their blocking condition coincides with the question’s condition. Arbitrary positive quotas, unequal side totals, and unmatched mass are allowed.

The preferred-side optimum is computable in polynomial time by the algorithm of [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf), with unmatched mass represented by dummy vertices.

For bit complexity, multiply all rational quotas by the product of their denominators. This product has polynomial bit length. The resulting integral allocation algorithm uses polynomially many additions, subtractions, minima, and comparisons; allocation values are bounded by the total scaled quota. Scaling back therefore computes exact rational allocations in polynomial bit complexity.

Universal-stability criterion

For every mutually acceptable pair $(e,a)$, define
$$ G_e(a)=\{a\}\cup\{b:b\succ_{P_e}a\}, \qquad G_a(e)=\{e\}\cup\{f:f\succ_{P_a}e\}, $$
and
$$ D_e(a)=w_e-\sum_{b\in G_e(a)}x_{eb}, \qquad D_a(e)=v_a-\sum_{f\in G_a(e)}x_{fa}. $$

Lemma 1. The allocation $x$ is stable under every compatible refinement exactly when every mutually acceptable pair satisfies
$$ D_e(a)=0\quad\text{or}\quad D_a(e)=0. $$

Proof. Every compatible extension puts the forced predecessors of $a$ before $a$, so its employer deficit at $(e,a)$ is at most $D_e(a)$.

This bound is attainable. The forced predecessors form a predecessor-closed set. Order them consistently first, then place $a$, then order the remaining types consistently. No remaining type must precede $a$ or one of its forced predecessors. The resulting order is a linear extension whose prefix through $a$ is exactly $G_e(a)$.

The analogous construction attains $D_a(e)$. Refinements of different types are independent, so both positive deficits can be attained simultaneously. Conversely, if one bound is zero, that endpoint’s deficit is zero in every extension. $\square$

This test uses transitive closure and exact rational arithmetic, hence runs in polynomial time.

3. A graph criterion for fixed-profile optimality

Fix a strict profile $\sigma$ under which $x$ is stable.

An employer is full if its assigned total equals its quota. For every full employer $e$, let $t_e$ be its worst positively assigned applicant.

Call $e$ willing at $a$ if $(e,a)$ is mutually acceptable and
$$ w_e-\sum_{b\succeq_e^\sigma a}x_{eb}>0. $$
An underfull employer is willing at every mutually acceptable applicant. A full employer is willing exactly when
$$ a\succ_e^\sigma t_e. $$

For each applicant $a$, let $h_a$ be its most preferred willing employer, when one exists. Define a directed graph $G_\sigma(x)$ on applicants: if $h_a$ exists and is full, insert
$$ a\longrightarrow t_{h_a}. $$

Lemma 2. A stable allocation $x$ is employer-optimal under $\sigma$ exactly when $G_\sigma(x)$ is acyclic.

A cycle yields a stable improvement

Suppose there is a simple directed cycle
$$ a_i\longrightarrow a_{i+1}, \qquad h_{a_i}=e_i, \qquad t_{e_i}=a_{i+1}, $$
with indices interpreted cyclically. The employers $e_i$ are distinct: repeated employers would have identical successor applicants.

Choose
$$ 0<\delta<\min_i x_{e_i,a_{i+1}}. $$
Increase $x_{e_i,a_i}$ by $\delta$ and decrease $x_{e_i,a_{i+1}}$ by $\delta$. Let $z$ be the resulting allocation.

Each affected row and column has unchanged total, so $z$ is feasible. Each affected employer replaces tail mass by strictly preferred mass, and therefore strictly improves.

To check stability, consider a mutually acceptable pair $(f,a)$.

If its employer deficit was zero in $x$, it remains zero in $z$, because every employer’s prefixes weakly increase.

Otherwise $f$ was willing at $a$, and stability of $x$ implies that the applicant deficit was zero. An unchanged applicant retains zero deficit. For a changed applicant $a$, its deficit can become positive only if its newly received employer $h_a$ is worse than $f$: otherwise the added mass remains inside the prefix through $f$ and offsets any removed mass. But $f$ was willing, contradicting the definition of $h_a$.

Thus $z$ is stable and strictly improves some employers. Therefore $x$ is not employer-optimal.

Failure of optimality yields a cycle

Suppose $x$ is not employer-optimal, and let $y\neq x$ be the employer-optimal stable allocation. Employers’ distributions in $y$ dominate those in $x$, applicants’ distributions in $x$ dominate those in $y$, and corresponding assigned totals agree.

We use an elementary prefix fact: if $u$ dominates $v$, their totals agree, and $u$ has strictly more mass at one position, then $u$ has strictly less mass at some worse position. Indeed, the cumulative difference becomes positive at that position and must return to zero by the final position.

First consider a removed entry
$$ x_{eb}>y_{eb}. $$
Applicant $b$’s dominance implies that $y$ assigns positive mass to an employer worse than $e$. Its deficit at $(e,b)$ in $y$ is consequently positive. Stability forces the employer deficit there to be zero: $e$ is full in $y$ and has no positive allocation worse than $b$.

Equal row totals make $e$ full in $x$ too. Its original tail entry decreases: if $t_e=b$, this is the assumed decrease; if $t_e$ is worse than $b$, then
$$ y_{e,t_e}=0<x_{e,t_e}. $$
Every changed employer has a removed entry because its total is unchanged. Thus every changed employer is full and loses original tail mass.

Let $T$ be the set of applicants losing mass at some employer’s original tail. It is nonempty. For $a\in T$, equal column totals give an employer $e'$ such that
$$ y_{e'a}>x_{e'a}. $$
Employer dominance implies that $e'$ loses mass at a worse applicant, so $e'$ was willing at $a$ in $x$. Hence $h_a$ exists.

If $h_a=e'$, it is a changed employer and loses its original tail. If $a$ prefers $h_a$ to $e'$, then $y_{e'a}>0$ makes the applicant deficit at $(h_a,a)$ positive in $y$. Stability forces $h_a$ to be full and to have no positive allocation worse than $a$. Equal totals make it full in $x$. Since it was willing there, its original tail is worse than $a$, and that tail entry decreases to zero.

Therefore
$$ a\longmapsto t_{h_a} $$
maps the nonempty finite set $T$ into itself. Iteration gives a directed cycle in $G_\sigma(x)$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

4. The ordinary problem is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete

Membership in $\mathrm{coNP}$

Section 1 handles positive budgets and eliminates allocation search at zero budget.

For a zero-budget NO-instance, a certificate is a compatible strict profile $\sigma$ under which $x^\rho$ is not employer-optimal. Its encoding length is polynomial. A verifier checks compatibility, computes $x^\rho$ and $x^\sigma$, and checks
$$ x^\rho\neq x^\sigma. $$
Uniqueness of the employer-optimal allocation makes this inequality equivalent to failure of the certificate requirement.

Thus the ordinary problem belongs to $\mathrm{coNP}$.

An auxiliary colored-cycle problem

A rainbow directed cycle is a simple directed cycle whose vertices have pairwise distinct colors.

We first prove that detecting such a cycle is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete even when every color occurs on at most two vertices and no edge joins equally colored vertices.

Membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ is immediate. For hardness, start with a formula having variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ and clauses $C_1,\ldots,C_r$, each containing three literal occurrences. Give each occurrence $(j,k)$ its own color $\chi_{jk}$.

Construct a directed acyclic graph with consecutive variable stages followed by consecutive clause stages.

For variable $X_i$, create two internally disjoint directed chains between the stage’s entrance and exit, representing its two truth values. Each chain contains:

  • a private marker vertex;
  • one vertex of color $\chi_{jk}$ for every occurrence involving $X_i$ that is false under that chain’s truth value.

For clause $C_j$, create three parallel paths of length two between its entrance and exit. Their middle vertices have colors
$$ \chi_{j1},\chi_{j2},\chi_{j3}. $$
Every marker and stage boundary has a fresh color. Let $s$ and $t$ be the first and last boundaries.

A directed $s$-to-$t$ path selects a truth value for every variable and one literal occurrence per clause. During the variable stages it visits exactly the occurrence colors of false literals. Therefore the path has distinct colors exactly when each selected clause literal is true.

Hence a rainbow $s$-to-$t$ path exists exactly when the formula is satisfiable.

Add the edge $t\to s$. Every directed cycle must use this edge because the original graph was acyclic. A rainbow cycle therefore exists exactly when a rainbow $s$-to-$t$ path exists.

Every occurrence color occurs exactly twice: once on the branch falsifying that occurrence and once in its clause stage. All other colors occur once. Consecutive vertices always have distinct colors. The construction has polynomial size, proving the auxiliary hardness claim.

Encoding a colored graph as a society

Let $D=(V,R)$ be a graph from this construction, with coloring $\kappa$. For each color $e$, let
$$ S_e=\{a\in V:\kappa(a)=e\}. $$

Construct the society as follows:

  • Applicant types are the vertices $a\in V$, each of mass $v_a=1$.
  • Employer types are the colors $e$, with mass

$$ w_e=|S_e|\in\{1,2\}. $$

  • Every pair is mutually acceptable.
  • Applicant $a$ initially ranks its owner $\kappa(a)$ above every other employer; other employers are incomparable.
  • Employer $e$ has exactly the comparisons

$$ b\succ_{P_e}a \quad\text{whenever}\quad b\in S_e,\ a\notin S_e,\ (a,b)\notin R. $$

  • All interview costs equal $1$, and $B=0$.

The employer relations are strict partial orders. Every comparison starts in $S_e$ and ends outside $S_e$, so there are no directed paths of length two and no cycles.

Supply a realized refinement $\rho$ in which every employer places all of $S_e$ before all applicants outside $S_e$. Supply any applicant refinement placing its owner first.

Define
$$ x_{ea}= \begin{cases} 1,&a\in S_e,\\ 0,&a\notin S_e. \end{cases} $$

This allocation is feasible and stable under every compatible refinement. Indeed, each applicant is fully assigned to its first-choice owner, making its deficit zero at every pair.

Moreover, $x=x^\rho$. To see this directly, under $\rho_e$ the first $|S_e|$ applicants are precisely $S_e$. For a prefix of $k\leq |S_e|$ such applicants, $x$ assigns mass $k$, while any feasible allocation assigns at most $k$ because each applicant has mass one. Every later prefix already contains the full employer quota under $x$. Thus $x$ dominates every feasible allocation for every employer.

At zero budget, all costs are positive, so the only feasible interview allocation is the zero matrix. Compatibility therefore means extending just these initial partial orders.

We prove
$$ \exists \sigma\text{ compatible with }x\text{ not employer-optimal} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ has a rainbow directed cycle}. $$

A bad refinement gives a rainbow cycle

Suppose $x$ is not employer-optimal under a compatible profile $\sigma$. It is stable, so Lemma 2 gives a cycle in $G_\sigma(x)$.

Every arc in that graph ends at an employer’s tail. Therefore every cycle vertex is some employer’s tail. Since $x$ assigns each applicant only to its owner, that employer is the vertex’s owner. An employer has only one tail, so distinct cycle vertices have distinct owners, hence distinct colors.

For a cycle arc
$$ a\longrightarrow b, $$
the willing employer is $\kappa(b)$, and $b$ is its tail. Since the cycle colors are distinct, $a\notin S_{\kappa(b)}$. Willingness means
$$ a\succ^\sigma_{\kappa(b)}b. $$
If $(a,b)\notin R$, the initial order requires the opposite comparison. Consequently $(a,b)\in R$.

Thus the graph cycle is a rainbow directed cycle in $D$.

A rainbow cycle gives a bad refinement

Conversely, suppose
$$ a_0\longrightarrow a_1\longrightarrow\cdots \longrightarrow a_{\ell-1}\longrightarrow a_0 $$
is a rainbow cycle in $D$, with cyclic indices.

For employer $e=\kappa(a_{i+1})$, choose the following order:

  • all applicants in $S_e\setminus\{a_{i+1}\}$;
  • applicant $a_i$;
  • applicant $a_{i+1}$;
  • all remaining applicants.

The rainbow condition makes $a_i$ external to $S_e$. This order extends $P_e$: comparisons starting at another member of $S_e$ are respected because that member comes first; comparisons starting at $a_{i+1}$ are respected because all external applicants other than $a_i$ come later. The comparison $a_{i+1}\succ_{P_e}a_i$ is absent because $(a_i,a_{i+1})\in R$.

These employers are distinct, so their choices are independent. For every employer outside the cycle, put all owned applicants before all external applicants. Choose any compatible applicant orders.

The cycle employer $\kappa(a_{i+1})$ now has tail $a_{i+1}$ and is willing at exactly one external applicant, namely $a_i$.

Applicant $a_i$ is its owner’s tail, so its owner is unwilling there. Every other employer except $\kappa(a_{i+1})$ is also unwilling there. Thus
$$ h_{a_i}=\kappa(a_{i+1}), $$
and $G_\sigma(x)$ contains the displayed cycle. Lemma 2 shows that $x$ is not employer-optimal.

This proves the equivalence.

Consequence

Combining the formula construction and society construction gives
$$ \text{formula unsatisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{ordinary certificate instance is YES}. $$
All constructions have polynomial size. The supplied realized profile and its employer-optimal allocation were explicitly verified, and all masses and costs have constant-size encodings.

Thus the ordinary problem is $\mathrm{coNP}$-hard. Together with membership,
$$ \boxed{\text{The ordinary problem is }\mathrm{coNP}\text{-complete}.} $$

5. Universal optimality when employer tails are fixed

We now prove the polynomial criterion needed for diligence.

Assume:

  • $x$ is stable under every compatible refinement;
  • every full employer $e$ has the same tail $t_e$ in every compatible refinement.

The second condition means that every other positively assigned applicant satisfies
$$ b\succ_{P_e}t_e. $$
Indeed, a strict comparison holds in every linear extension exactly when it belongs to the transitive partial order.

For a mutually acceptable pair, define
$$ \operatorname{Def}(e,a)= \begin{cases} \text{true},&e\text{ is underfull},\\ a\succ_{P_e}t_e,&e\text{ is full}, \end{cases} $$
and
$$ \operatorname{Poss}(e,a)= \begin{cases} \text{true},&e\text{ is underfull},\\ a\neq t_e\text{ and }t_e\not\succ_{P_e}a,&e\text{ is full}. \end{cases} $$
These respectively mean that $e$ is willing at $a$ in every compatible refinement, or in at least one.

Construct a graph $H$ on applicants. For every full employer $e$, insert
$$ a\longrightarrow t_e $$
if:

  • $\operatorname{Poss}(e,a)$ holds; and
  • there is no mutually acceptable employer $f$ such that

$$ \operatorname{Def}(f,a) \quad\text{and}\quad f\succ_{P_a}e. $$

Lemma 3. Under the two assumptions, $x$ is employer-optimal under every compatible refinement exactly when $H$ is acyclic.

Acyclicity suffices

Every arc of every $G_\sigma(x)$ belongs to $H$. If $h_a=e$, then $e$ is possibly willing. A definitely willing employer forced above $e$ would contradict $h_a=e$.

Thus acyclicity of $H$ implies acyclicity of every $G_\sigma(x)$. Lemma 2 proves universal employer optimality.

A shortest cycle can be realized

Suppose $H$ contains a shortest directed cycle
$$ a_i\longrightarrow a_{i+1}=t_{e_i}, $$
where $e_i$ witnesses the indicated arc. The employers $e_i$ are distinct because their tails are distinct cycle successors.

For a full employer outside the cycle, choose an extension placing its tail immediately after its forced predecessors. It is then willing exactly at its definitely willing applicants.

For cycle employer $e_i$, add
$$ a_i\succ_{e_i}t_{e_i}. $$
This is consistent by $\operatorname{Poss}(e_i,a_i)$. In the augmented order, place the tail immediately after its predecessors. The willing applicants are exactly those $b$ satisfying at least one of
$$ \operatorname{Def}(e_i,b), \qquad b=a_i, \qquad b\succ_{P_{e_i}}a_i. $$
This follows because the added predecessors of the tail are precisely $a_i$ and its original predecessors.

Underfull employers may choose arbitrary compatible extensions.

We claim that no employer willing at a cycle applicant $a_i$ is forced above $e_i$ in $P_{a_i}$.

Suppose such an employer $f$ exists. The condition defining the original arc excludes $\operatorname{Def}(f,a_i)$. Therefore $f$ must be another cycle employer, say $f=e_j$ with $j\neq i$, whose added willingness gives
$$ a_i\succ_{P_f}a_j. $$
Because $\operatorname{Poss}(f,a_j)$ holds, this implies
$$ a_i\neq t_f, \qquad t_f\not\succ_{P_f}a_i. $$
Either contrary possibility would force $t_f\succ_{P_f}a_j$. Hence $\operatorname{Poss}(f,a_i)$ holds.

Also, no definitely willing employer $g$ is forced above $f$ at $a_i$. Otherwise transitivity with $f\succ_{P_{a_i}}e_i$ would put $g$ above $e_i$, contradicting the original arc’s condition.

Consequently $H$ contains the shortcut
$$ a_i\longrightarrow t_f=a_{j+1}. $$
Its endpoint is neither $a_i$, by $\operatorname{Poss}(f,a_i)$, nor the original successor $a_{i+1}$, because $j\neq i$ and the cycle vertices are distinct. Together with the appropriate segment of the original cycle, it gives a strictly shorter directed cycle. This contradiction proves the claim.

For each cycle applicant $a_i$, now choose an extension placing $e_i$ above every other willing employer. Such an extension exists: none of those employers is forced above $e_i$. More explicitly, adding comparisons from $e_i$ to all those employers cannot create a cycle, since a resulting cycle would require an original path from one of them back to $e_i$.

Thus $h_{a_i}=e_i$ for every cycle applicant. The resulting $G_\sigma(x)$ contains the chosen cycle. Universal stability ensures that $x$ is stable under this profile, and Lemma 2 proves that it is not employer-optimal. $\square$

All predicates and graph operations in this lemma are polynomial-time computable.

6. The diligent variant is in $\mathrm{P}$

The algorithm is:

  • If $B<0$, return NO. If $B>0$, return YES.
  • For $B=0$, compute $x=x^\rho$.
  • If some positive entry $x_{ea}$ has $c_{ea}>0$, return NO.
  • Reveal all zero-cost interviews and compute $P_e,P_a$.
  • Apply Lemma 1. If universal stability fails, return NO.
  • Construct $H$ from Section 5 and return YES exactly when it is acyclic.

It remains to verify the fixed-tail hypothesis in step 6.

After step 3 succeeds, every positively assigned pair is zero-cost and is therefore positively interviewed by $q^F$. For each employer, all comparisons among its positively assigned applicants are revealed. In particular, its realized worst positively assigned applicant remains worst under every compatible refinement. Every full employer consequently has a fixed tail.

Lemma 3 now applies. Section 1 proves that using maximal free revelation loses no feasible diligent certificate. Hence all steps are correct.

Fixed-profile allocation computation, transitive closure, rational deficit calculations, construction of $H$, and cycle detection have polynomial bit complexity in $m,\tau,L$. Therefore
$$ \boxed{\text{The diligent variant belongs to }\mathrm{P}.} $$

7. Interpretation of the requested dichotomy

The ordinary problem is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete under polynomial many-one reductions.

If it were in $\mathrm{P}$, then every $\mathrm{coNP}$ problem would be in $\mathrm{P}$; taking complements gives $\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{P}$. Conversely, $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$ puts it in $\mathrm{P}$.

If it were $\mathrm{NP}$-hard, its membership in $\mathrm{coNP}$ would imply
$$ \mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{coNP}, $$
hence $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$. Conversely, under that equality, its $\mathrm{coNP}$-completeness makes it $\mathrm{NP}$-hard.

The unconditional classifications are therefore $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete for the ordinary variant and $\mathrm{P}$ for the diligent variant.
