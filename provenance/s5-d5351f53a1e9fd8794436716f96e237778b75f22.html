The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of \(A\)-types and \(B\)-types with rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), rankings with ties, acceptable pairs \(E\), and nonnegative rational weights \(w_{ij}\), choose matched masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\), maximizing \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}x_{ij}\). For each acceptable pair, define \(S_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(W_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}\), with \(S_j(i),W_j(i)\) defined symmetrically; require that it is not the case that \(S_i(j)>0\) and \(W_j(i)>0\), or that \(W_i(j)>0\) and \(S_j(i)>0\). Find an exact maximum-weight feasible assignment or report that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A,B\) be finite, disjoint sets of types with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\). Each type has a weak order on its acceptable opposite-side types; every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched, and ties are preserved.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Write \(m=|E|\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\) need not sum to one or have equal totals across sides; weights \(w_{ij}\) are nonnegative binary rationals.
An assignment consists of real masses \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(E\), satisfying \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq\beta_j\).
For \((i,j)\in E\), define \(S_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_kx_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(W_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_kx_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}\); symmetrically, \(S_j(i)=\beta_j-\sum_kx_{kj}+\sum_{k:i\succ_j k}x_{kj}\) and \(W_j(i)=\beta_j-\sum_kx_{kj}+\sum_{k:i\succeq_j k}x_{kj}\). All sums range over acceptable partners.
Call an assignment strongly stable if every \((i,j)\in E\) satisfies \(\neg(S_i(j)>0\land W_j(i)>0)\land\neg(W_i(j)>0\land S_j(i)>0)\).
The computational problem is to maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\) exactly over strongly stable assignments, or report infeasibility.

Is this exact maximum-weight problem polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For discrete bipartite matching with incomplete lists and ties, the strongly stable matching polytope is the convex hull of strongly stable matchings, implying polynomial-time maximum-weight optimization ([Kunysz, 2018](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.42)).
For unit-capacity fractional bipartite matching with strict preferences, maximum-welfare ordinally stable matching is polynomial-time solvable ([Chen, Roy and Sorge, 2021](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)); this covers a restricted case of the condition above. No result settling the exact problem with arbitrary rational masses and ties was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Mass normalization was unspecified; masses are arbitrary positive binary rationals, with neither normalization nor equality of side totals required, reflecting the unrestricted sizes of the discrete sides.
  • Assignments on unacceptable pairs were unspecified; they are fixed to zero, as discrete matchings use only graph edges.
  • The treatment of unmatched mass was implicit; every acceptable partner is strictly preferred to being unmatched.
  • Rankings are weak orders on exactly the acceptable partners, with no tie-breaking.
  • Defined the size parameters as the total support size, the number of acceptable pairs, and the total input bit length.

Result

The exact maximum-weight problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof uses a mass-normalized analogue of the inequalities in [Kunysz’s discrete formulation, Section 3](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol123-isaac2018/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.42/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.42.pdf). We prove directly that the resulting polynomial-size polytope is the convex hull of the strongly stable assignments. We also give an explicit rounding procedure that recovers a strongly stable optimum.

1. A compact linear relaxation

For any capacity-feasible assignment \(x\), define the normalized masses assigned to strictly better and weakly better partners:

\[ p_i(j)=\frac{1}{\alpha_i}\sum_{k:k\succ_i j}x_{ik}, \qquad q_i(j)=\frac{1}{\alpha_i}\sum_{k:k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}, \]

and, symmetrically,

\[ p_j(i)=\frac{1}{\beta_j}\sum_{k:k\succ_j i}x_{kj}, \qquad q_j(i)=\frac{1}{\beta_j}\sum_{k:k\succeq_j i}x_{kj}. \]

All supported masses are positive, so these divisions are defined. Capacity feasibility implies that these quantities lie in \([0,1]\).

Partitioning each preference list into better, tied, and worse partners gives

\[ S_i(j)=\alpha_i(1-q_i(j)), \qquad W_i(j)=\alpha_i(1-p_i(j)), \]

with the analogous identities on the other side.

Let \(P\) be the polytope consisting of the capacity-feasible assignments satisfying, for every \((i,j)\in E\),

\[ q_i(j)+p_j(i)\geq 1, \qquad p_i(j)+q_j(i)\geq 1. \tag{1} \]

Every strongly stable assignment belongs to \(P\). Indeed, all the quantities \(S,W\) are nonnegative. Consequently, the first strong-stability condition says that either \(S_i(j)=0\) or \(W_j(i)=0\). Thus either \(q_i(j)=1\) or \(p_j(i)=1\), which implies the first inequality in (1). The second condition similarly implies the second inequality.

The converse does not hold pointwise. We will instead prove that every point of \(P\) is a convex combination of strongly stable assignments.

2. Saturation and tightness identities

Fix \(x\in P\), and write

\[ R_i=\sum_jx_{ij}, \qquad C_j=\sum_ix_{ij}, \qquad T=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}. \]

Define the nonnegative slacks

\[ d_{ij}=q_i(j)+p_j(i)-1, \qquad e_{ij}=p_i(j)+q_j(i)-1. \]

We first establish

\[ \sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}(d_{ij}+e_{ij}) = \sum_i\frac{R_i^2}{\alpha_i} + \sum_j\frac{C_j^2}{\beta_j} -2T. \tag{2} \]

To verify the calculation at an individual vertex, let its capacity be \(c\), and let \(t_1,\ldots,t_d\) be its assigned masses in successive indifference classes, ordered from best to worst. The contribution from its normalized strictly-better and weakly-better quantities is

\[ \frac{1}{c}\sum_{h=1}^{d} t_h\left(2\sum_{\ell<h}t_\ell+t_h\right) = \frac{1}{c}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{d}t_h\right)^2. \]

Summing this identity over both sides proves (2).

Since the slacks are nonnegative, the left side of (2) is nonnegative. But its right side equals

\[ -\sum_i\frac{R_i(\alpha_i-R_i)}{\alpha_i} -\sum_j\frac{C_j(\beta_j-C_j)}{\beta_j}, \tag{3} \]

which is nonpositive by capacity feasibility. Therefore both sides are zero.

Every summand in each relevant nonnegative sum must consequently vanish. We obtain:

  • Every type receiving positive mass is fully matched:

\[ R_i\in\{0,\alpha_i\}, \qquad C_j\in\{0,\beta_j\}. \tag{4} \]

  • Both inequalities are tight on every positive edge:

\[ x_{ij}>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_i(j)+p_j(i)=1, \qquad p_i(j)+q_j(i)=1. \tag{5} \]

Subtracting the equalities in (5), for every positive edge define

\[ f_{ij}=q_i(j)-p_i(j)=q_j(i)-p_j(i)>0. \tag{6} \]

The strict positivity follows because the indifference class being measured contains the positive mass \(x_{ij}\).

3. Threshold rounding

For each positive edge, define the open interval

\[ I_{ij}=(p_i(j),q_i(j)). \]

By (5), it also has the representation

\[ I_{ij}=(1-q_j(i),1-p_j(i)). \tag{7} \]

Its length is \(f_{ij}\).

At any matched type on side \(A\), these intervals are the normalized masses of its positive indifference classes, arranged from best to worst. By (4), they partition \((0,1)\) apart from their endpoints. At a matched type on side \(B\), representation (7) gives the corresponding partition arranged from worst to best.

Let the set of breakpoints be

\[ H=\{0,1\}\cup \{p_i(j),q_i(j),1-p_j(i),1-q_j(i):(i,j)\in E\}. \tag{8} \]

Choose any \(\theta\in(0,1)\setminus H\). Define \(x^{(\theta)}\) by setting

\[ x^{(\theta)}_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}}{f_{ij}} \]

when \(x_{ij}>0\) and \(\theta\in I_{ij}\), and setting \(x^{(\theta)}_{ij}=0\) otherwise.

Capacity feasibility

Consider a type \(i\in A\) with \(R_i>0\). Exactly one of its positive indifference-class intervals contains \(\theta\). Let that class be \(D\), and let its normalized mass be \(f\). Every positive edge from \(i\) into \(D\) has the same interval and the same denominator \(f\). Therefore

\[ \sum_jx^{(\theta)}_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{j\in D}x_{ij}}{f} = \alpha_i. \]

Types with \(R_i=0\) remain unmatched.

The same argument applies on side \(B\), using (6)–(7). Thus every previously matched type remains fully matched, every previously unmatched type remains unmatched, and every matched type receives partners from exactly one of its indifference classes.

Strong stability

In the following implications, \(S^{(\theta)},W^{(\theta)}\) are calculated from the rounded assignment, while \(p,q\) are calculated from the original point \(x\):

\[ S_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\Longrightarrow\theta>q_i(j), \qquad W_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\Longrightarrow\theta>p_i(j), \tag{9} \]

\[ S_j^{(\theta)}(i)>0\Longrightarrow\theta<1-q_j(i), \qquad W_j^{(\theta)}(i)>0\Longrightarrow\theta<1-p_j(i). \tag{10} \]

Here is why these implications hold. A matched type receives its entire capacity within one selected indifference class. Thus \(S_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\) means that its selected class is strictly worse than \(j\), and \(W_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\) means that its selected class is weakly worse than \(j\). The ordering of the intervals on side \(A\) gives (9). Their reverse ordering on side \(B\) gives (10). The inequalities are strict because \(\theta\) avoids all breakpoints.

For an unmatched type, the original incident mass is zero, so its \(p,q\) quantities are zero. Then (9) or (10), as appropriate, follows from \(0<\theta<1\).

Now suppose an edge satisfied \(S_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\) and \(W_j^{(\theta)}(i)>0\). Equations (9)–(10) and the first inequality in (1) would give

\[ \theta>q_i(j)\geq 1-p_j(i)>\theta, \]

a contradiction.

Likewise, \(W_i^{(\theta)}(j)>0\) and \(S_j^{(\theta)}(i)>0\) would imply

\[ \theta>p_i(j)\geq 1-q_j(i)>\theta, \]

using the second inequality in (1), again a contradiction.

Therefore every rounded assignment \(x^{(\theta)}\) is strongly stable.

4. Exact convex-hull characterization

List the distinct breakpoints as

\[ 0=h_0<h_1<\cdots<h_K=1. \]

For each interval, choose its midpoint \(\theta_\ell\), and put

\[ y^\ell=x^{(\theta_\ell)}, \qquad \lambda_\ell=h_\ell-h_{\ell-1}. \]

The rounded assignment is constant as \(\theta\) varies within any one breakpoint interval.

For a positive edge \((i,j)\), the breakpoint intervals contained in \(I_{ij}\) have total length \(f_{ij}\). Hence

\[ \sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\lambda_\ell y^\ell_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{f_{ij}} \sum_{\ell:\theta_\ell\in I_{ij}}\lambda_\ell = x_{ij}. \]

This identity also holds for zero edges. Since all \(\lambda_\ell\) are positive and sum to one, we have proved

\[ x=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\lambda_\ell y^\ell. \tag{11} \]

Every \(y^\ell\) is strongly stable. Conversely, Section 1 showed that every strongly stable assignment belongs to the convex polytope \(P\). Thus, writing \(\mathcal S\) for the strongly stable assignments,

\[ P=\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S). \tag{12} \]

In particular, \(P\) is feasible if and only if a strongly stable assignment exists.

5. Exact optimization and polynomial bit complexity

Solve the rational linear program

\[ \max\left\{ \sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}:x\in P \right\}. \tag{13} \]

If it is infeasible, report infeasibility. Otherwise let \(x^*\) be an exact rational optimum, with objective value \(V\).

Apply the decomposition (11) to \(x^*\). Each rounded assignment \(y^\ell\) is strongly stable and hence belongs to \(P\), so its objective value is at most \(V\). But their positively weighted average has objective value \(V\). Therefore every assignment in this decomposition has objective value \(V\).

Consequently, it suffices to compute the breakpoints of \(x^*\), choose the midpoint of any consecutive pair, and return its rounded assignment.

The bit-complexity requirements are satisfied:

  • The LP has \(m\) variables, \(\tau\) capacity constraints, and \(2m\) stability inequalities, together with nonnegativity constraints.
  • Its coefficients are rational numbers obtained from the input masses by taking reciprocals. Their encoding lengths, and the total explicit LP encoding length, are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).
  • The LP is bounded by the capacity constraints. Standard polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore supplies an exact rational optimum of polynomial encoding length, or reports infeasibility.
  • There are at most \(4m+2\) breakpoints. Computing them, selecting a midpoint, and evaluating the rounding formula requires polynomially many rational operations, with polynomial encoding lengths.

No expansion into individual agents is required. Unequal side totals, isolated supported types, and arbitrary positive rational masses are covered by the argument.
