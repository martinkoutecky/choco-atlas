Theorem 19 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_A)_{A\subseteq C}\) over approval types, \(J\subseteq C\), committee size \(k\), distance bound \(r\), and rational mass budget \(\beta\), decide whether transfers \(x_{A,B}\geq 0\) with \(B\supsetneq A\), \(\lvert B\setminus A\rvert\leq r\), \(\sum_B x_{A,B}\leq\mu_A\), and \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\leq\beta\) can produce \(\mu'\) in which no \(j\in J\) belongs to any winning \(k\)-committee under SAV.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(m=|C|\), a society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) approval types \(A\subseteq C\) with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_A\) a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(2^m\); unlisted types have zero mass, \(\sum_A\mu_A=1\), and \(\sum_{A\neq\emptyset}\mu_A>0\).
Also given are a nonempty set \(J\subseteq C\), integers \(0\leq k\leq m\) and \(r\geq0\), and a binary rational budget \(0\leq\beta\leq1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A transfer plan consists of real masses \(x_{A,B}\geq0\), allowed only when \(A\subsetneq B\subseteq C\) and \(|B\setminus A|\leq r\), satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{A,B}\leq\mu_A\) for every \(A\) and \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\leq\beta\). Each portion of original mass is changed at most once; destinations need not belong to the input support.
The resulting distribution is \(\mu'_T=\mu_T-\sum_Bx_{T,B}+\sum_Ax_{A,T}\). Under satisfaction approval voting (SAV), a committee \(W\) has score \(\sum_{T\neq\emptyset}\mu'_T|T\cap W|/|T|\); every score-maximizing \(k\)-committee wins, with no tie-breaking.
The decision problem asks whether such a plan exists for which every winning \(k\)-committee \(W\) satisfies \(W\cap J=\emptyset\).

Is this continuous destructive addition bribery problem in \(\mathsf{P}\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k,r\).

For finite lists of voters, the corresponding problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even with \(k=1\), for every fixed integer \(r\geq1\). [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2002.00836v3#Thmtheorem14)
No result settling this exact continuous mass-transfer problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and restricted the budget to [0,1], corresponding to the discrete bound that at most all voters may be changed.
  • Required a nonempty distinguished set and positive mass on at least one nonempty approval type, carrying over the discrete assumptions.
  • Used the discrete domains: nonnegative integer distance bound and integer committee size between zero and the number of candidates.
  • Made transfers real-valued, allowed splitting original mass among arbitrary permitted destination types, and specified that incoming mass cannot be transferred again.
  • Defined the resulting distribution and mass-weighted SAV scores, with empty types contributing zero and all tied maximizing committees winning.

Result

The problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete when \(k\) is part of the input, even with \(r=1\), \(\beta=1\), and \(|J|=1\).

However, for every fixed \(k\), it is polynomial-time solvable, with unrestricted \(r\). In particular, the continuous problem with \(k=1\) is in \(\mathsf{P}\).

Writing \(R=\min\{r,m\}\), a deterministic algorithm has running time
\(\binom{m-|J|}{k}\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau m(R+1))\)
for \(1\leq k\leq m-|J|\). The polynomial has an absolute degree, independent of \(k\) and \(r\).

1. Characterizing successful score vectors

For a resulting distribution, define the individual candidate score
\(s_c=\sum_{T\ni c}\mu'_T/|T|\).
The SAV score of a committee \(W\) is then \(\sum_{c\in W}s_c\).

Suppose \(k\geq1\), and put \(t=\max_{j\in J}s_j\). Every winning \(k\)-committee avoids \(J\) if and only if at least \(k\) candidates outside \(J\) have scores strictly greater than \(t\).

Indeed, if at least \(k\) candidates have scores greater than \(t\), a committee containing a member of \(J\) can be improved by replacing that member with an omitted candidate of score greater than \(t\).

Conversely, suppose fewer than \(k\) candidates have scores greater than \(t\). Choose \(j\in J\) with \(s_j=t\). There is a maximizing \(k\)-committee containing \(j\): include all candidates with scores greater than \(t\), include \(j\), and fill the remaining places with the highest-scoring remaining candidates.

Consequently, success is equivalent to the existence of a set \(S\subseteq C\setminus J\), with \(|S|=k\), satisfying
\(s_c>s_j\) for every \(c\in S\) and \(j\in J\).

The boundary cases are immediate: \(k=0\) is a yes-instance, and \(k>m-|J|\) is a no-instance.

2. A polynomial-size description of attainable scores

Let \(\mathcal A\) denote the input support. Only its types can be transfer origins, because every other type has original mass zero.

For \(A\in\mathcal A\), write \(a=|A|\) and \(R_A=\min\{r,m-a\}\). For each \(1\leq h\leq R_A\), introduce:

  • \(y_{A,h}\): original mass of type \(A\) changed by adding exactly \(h\) candidates;
  • \(z_{A,h,c}\), for \(c\notin A\): the portion of that mass whose additions include \(c\).

Impose
\(y_{A,h}\geq0\),
\(0\leq z_{A,h,c}\leq y_{A,h}\), and
\(\sum_{c\notin A}z_{A,h,c}=h\,y_{A,h}\).

Also impose the original-mass and budget constraints
\(\sum_{h=1}^{R_A}y_{A,h}\leq\mu_A\)
and
\(\sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\sum_{h=1}^{R_A}y_{A,h}\leq\beta\).

These constraints describe exactly the possible aggregate additions.

To prove sufficiency, fix \(A,h\) with \(y_{A,h}>0\), and put \(p_c=z_{A,h,c}/y_{A,h}\). Then
\(0\leq p_c\leq1\) and \(\sum_{c\notin A}p_c=h\).
The polytope defined by these inequalities is the convex hull of the incidence vectors of \(h\)-element subsets of \(C\setminus A\).

Here is the integrality argument. A point with two fractional coordinates is not a vertex, since those coordinates can be perturbed in opposite directions while preserving their sum. A point cannot have exactly one fractional coordinate because its coordinate sum is the integer \(h\). Thus every vertex is an incidence vector of an \(h\)-element subset. Since the polytope is bounded, every point is a convex combination of its vertices.

Multiplying such a convex combination by \(y_{A,h}\) supplies transfers to destinations \(A\cup H\), where \(|H|=h\), with exactly the prescribed marginals. Performing this independently for each \(A,h\) respects the original-mass constraints and changes each original portion only once. Necessity follows directly by aggregating any transfer plan.

Set \(z_{A,h,c}=0\) when \(c\in A\). The resulting scores are the linear expressions

\(s_c= \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A\\c\in A}} \frac{\mu_A-\sum_{h=1}^{R_A}y_{A,h}}{|A|} + \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}\sum_{h=1}^{R_A} \frac{\mathbf 1_{\{c\in A\}}y_{A,h}+z_{A,h,c}}{|A|+h}.\)

The first sum never divides by zero, and the second has positive denominators even when \(A=\emptyset\).

3. Algorithm and membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

Fix a proposed set \(S\subseteq C\setminus J\) of size \(k\). Add a variable \(\varepsilon\), impose \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq1\), and maximize it subject to the preceding constraints and
\(s_c\geq s_j+\varepsilon\)
for all \(c\in S\), \(j\in J\).

This linear program accepts \(S\) exactly when its optimum is positive. A successful plan has a positive minimum margin over the finitely many comparisons; conversely, a positive feasible margin yields a successful plan by Section 2.

There are at most \(\tau R(m+1)+1\) variables and
\(O(\tau m(R+1)+k|J|)\) constraints. Its rational encoding length is polynomial in \(L+\tau m(R+1)\). Standard exact rational linear programming therefore solves it in polynomial bit time.

Enumerating \(S\) gives the claimed running time
\(\binom{m-|J|}{k}\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau m(R+1))\).
Thus every fixed \(k\), including \(k=1\), gives polynomial time for arbitrary \(r\). The same bound is at most
\(2^m\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau m^2)\).

The problem also belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\). A certificate consists of \(S\) and rational values for \(y,z,\varepsilon\) satisfying the constraints with \(\varepsilon>0\). These imply the existence of an actual transfer plan by Section 2.

Such a certificate has polynomial length whenever the instance is successful: the fixed-\(S\) linear program is bounded, and a positive optimum has an optimal vertex whose rational coordinates have polynomial encoding length by the determinant bounds for rational linear programs.

When \(r=0\) or \(\beta=0\), no transfer is possible, and the original scores can be checked in polynomial time.

4. Hardness construction

We reduce from Independent Set on simple cubic graphs: given a graph \(G=(V,E)\) in which every vertex has degree three, and an integer \(1\leq K\leq|V|\), decide whether an independent set of size at least \(K\) exists. This restriction is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete; see [Garey–Johnson, problem GT20](https://perso.limos.fr/~palafour/PAPERS/PDF/Garey-Johnson79.pdf).

Write \(n=|V|\), \(M=|E|=3n/2\), and \(a=n-1\). Construct

\(C=V\cup\{d\},\qquad J=\{d\},\qquad k=K,\qquad r=1,\qquad\beta=1.\)

Choose
\(h=\lceil\log_2(2(M+1))\rceil\),
\(q=2^{-h}\), and \(b=q/4\).

The support consists of:

  • \(A_e=C\setminus\{u,v\}\), of mass \(q\), for each edge \(e=\{u,v\}\);
  • \(V\), of mass \(b\);
  • \(C\), of mass \(f=1-Mq-b\).

All masses are positive dyadic rationals. Indeed,
\(Mq+b=(M+1/4)2^{-h}<1/2\),
so \(f>0\). Their sum is one.

Because \(r=1\), mass from \(A_e\) can only add one of its two omitted endpoints. Let \(t_{e,u}\) and \(t_{e,v}\) denote those respective transferred masses. They satisfy
\(t_{e,u}\geq0\), \(t_{e,v}\geq0\), and
\(t_{e,u}+t_{e,v}\leq q\).

The only transfer from \(V\) adds \(d\); denote its mass by \(z\), where \(0\leq z\leq b\). The full type \(C\) cannot be changed.

Exact score differences

Fix \(v\in V\). A nonincident edge type approves both \(v\) and \(d\), before and after every permitted transfer, so it contributes zero to \(s_v-s_d\). The full type contributes zero as well.

For an incident edge \(e=\{v,u\}\), the original contribution to \(s_v-s_d\) is \(-q/a\). Adding \(v\) makes that portion’s contribution zero; adding \(u\) changes its denominator from \(a\) to \(n\). Its resulting contribution is therefore

\(-\frac q a+\frac{t_{e,v}}a+\frac{t_{e,u}}{an}.\)

The type \(V\) contributes \((b-z)/n\). Since \(v\) has exactly three incident edges,

\(s_v-s_d= \frac{b-z}{n}-\frac{3q}{a} +\sum_{e=\{v,u\}\ni v} \left(\frac{t_{e,v}}a+\frac{t_{e,u}}{an}\right).\)

Define

\(D_v=\sum_{e=\{v,u\}\ni v} \left(n(q-t_{e,v})-t_{e,u}\right).\)

Multiplying the preceding score identity by \(an\) gives

\(an(s_v-s_d)=a(b-z)-D_v.\)

Moreover, \(t_{e,u}\leq q-t_{e,v}\), so every summand satisfies

\(n(q-t_{e,v})-t_{e,u} \geq(n-1)(q-t_{e,v}) =a(q-t_{e,v}).\)

Consequently,

\(D_v\geq a\sum_{e\ni v}(q-t_{e,v}).\)

5. Correctness of the reduction

A successful plan implies an independent set

Suppose \(s_v>s_d\). The exact score identity gives

\(D_v<a(b-z)\leq ab.\)

Combining this with the lower bound on \(D_v\), and using \(a>0\), yields

\(\sum_{e\ni v}(q-t_{e,v})<b=q/4.\)

Every term is nonnegative. Hence, for every incident edge,

\(t_{e,v}>3q/4.\)

If adjacent vertices \(u,v\) both had scores greater than \(s_d\), their shared edge would therefore satisfy

\(t_{e,u}+t_{e,v}>3q/2>q,\)

contradicting its mass constraint.

Thus the vertices with scores greater than \(s_d\) form an independent set. By Section 1, a successful plan supplies at least \(K\) such vertices.

This argument applies to arbitrary real transfers, including arbitrary splitting of edge masses.

An independent set implies a successful plan

Conversely, let \(S\subseteq V\) be an independent set of size \(K\). For every edge incident with a vertex \(v\in S\), transfer its entire mass \(q\) by adding \(v\). Leave all other original mass unchanged.

Independence ensures that no edge receives two conflicting instructions. The total transferred mass is \(3Kq\leq Mq<1\), so the budget is respected.

For each \(v\in S\), all three incident transfers satisfy \(t_{e,v}=q\), and \(z=0\). The score formula gives

\(s_v-s_d=\frac bn-\frac{3q}{a}+\frac{3q}{a}=\frac bn>0.\)

There are therefore \(K\) candidates strictly above \(d\), so every winning \(K\)-committee excludes \(d\).

The reduction is correct in both directions.

6. Complexity conclusion

The construction has \(m=n+1\), \(\tau=3n/2+2\), and masses with \(O(\log n)\) encoding length each. Listing its approval types takes polynomial time and space; using incidence bit vectors, their total size is \(O(n^2+n\log n)\).

Together with Section 3, the reduction proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness, already for \(r=1\), \(\beta=1\), and one forbidden candidate. The general problem therefore has no polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).

For fixed \(k\), the established running time is
\(m^k\operatorname{poly}(L+\tau m(R+1))\).
In particular, continuous splitting makes the \(k=1\) case polynomial-time solvable, despite the discrete hardness stated in the question.
