Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\Theta=(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_m)\), with \(m\geq1\) and finite binary rational thresholds \(1\leq\tau_1<\cdots<\tau_m\), and let \(P,Q\) be distinct candidates.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) reported types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses sum to \(1\); absent types have mass zero. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, and state running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any additional input parameters.
A latent completion places an atomless population in an arbitrary metric space with \(P,Q\), allowing different locations within each reported type and coincident locations for different voters; candidate distances are measurable and integrable. A voter reports a preferred candidate consistent with weak distance preference, resolving equal distances to either candidate, and has strength \(s\) equal to distance to the other candidate divided by distance to the preferred candidate, with \(s=\infty\) when the latter distance is zero.
Type \(A_l\) reports \(P\) and \(\tau_l\leq s<\tau_{l+1}\); type \(B_l\) reports \(Q\) with the same interval. The last interval has no upper bound. Type \(U\) has \(s<\tau_1\) and reveals no preferred candidate; necessarily \(\mu(U)=0\) when \(\tau_1=1\). Write \(a_l=\mu(A_l)\), \(b_l=\mu(B_l)\).
For a completion \(K\), define \(SC_K(X)=\int d(v,X)\,d\mu(v)\), \(R_X(\Theta,\mu)=\sup_K SC_K(X)/\min\{SC_K(P),SC_K(Q)\}\), and \(V(\Theta,\mu)=\min_{X\in\{P,Q\}}R_X(\Theta,\mu)\). A zero denominator gives ratio \(1\) if the selected cost is zero and \(\infty\) otherwise.
Define \(D_\Theta=\max\bigl(\{\tau_1,(\tau_m+2)/\tau_m\}\cup\{(\tau_l\tau_{l+1}+2\tau_{l+1}-1)/(\tau_l\tau_{l+1}+1):1\leq l<m\}\bigr)\), and choose \(k\) with \(\tau_k\leq D_\Theta<\tau_{k+1}\), taking \(\tau_{m+1}=\infty\).
Put \(c_l=(D_\Theta\tau_l-1)/(\tau_l+1)\). Put \(h_l=(\tau_{l+1}-D_\Theta)/(\tau_{l+1}+1)\) for \(l<k\), \(h_l=(\tau_{l+1}-D_\Theta)/(\tau_{l+1}-1)\) for \(k\leq l<m\), and \(h_m=1\). The mass admissibility condition permits \(P\) only if \(\sum_l c_la_l\geq\sum_l h_lb_l\), and permits \(Q\) only if \(\sum_l c_lb_l\geq\sum_l h_la_l\).
The weighted rule assigns weight \(c_l+h_l\) to types \(A_l,B_l\), weight zero to \(U\), and selects \(P\) iff \(\sum_l(c_l+h_l)a_l\geq\sum_l(c_l+h_l)b_l\), breaking score ties toward \(P\).
Is deciding \(V(\Theta,\mu)\leq r\), for an input binary rational \(r\geq1\), in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, and can a minimizing deterministic winner and its worst-case distortion be computed in polynomial time?
For each fixed \(\Theta\), is \(\sup_\mu V(\Theta,\mu)=D_\Theta\), do rules satisfying the mass admissibility condition guarantee this bound, and does the displayed weighted rule satisfy that condition and attain the bound?
For finite electorates, the displayed weighted rule satisfies the displayed admissibility condition and achieves the optimal global distortion \(D_\Theta\); its rational weighted scores can be evaluated in polynomial time. [Abramowitz, Anshelevich, and Zhu](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.10562)
No prior result settling precisely this mass-completion problem was located in the literature search.

  • Thresholds were not assigned an encoding; require finite binary rational thresholds and at least one threshold.

  • Interpret a continuous completion as an atomless population whose mass within a reported type may occupy different latent locations; require measurable distances and finite social costs.
  • Use the discrete model's weak distance preferences, resolve equal-distance preference reports to either candidate, and break weighted-score ties toward P.
  • Use lower-inclusive, upper-exclusive strength bins, include infinite strength in the last bin, and require U to be empty when the smallest threshold is one.

1. Results

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). A minimizing deterministic winner and its exact worst-case distortion can be computed in polynomial time.

For every fixed \(\Theta\),

\[ \sup_\mu V(\Theta,\mu)=D_\Theta. \]

Every rule satisfying the mass admissibility condition guarantees distortion at most \(D_\Theta\). The displayed weighted rule satisfies that condition, including its prescribed tie-breaking, and its global worst-case distortion is exactly \(D_\Theta\).

The finite-electorate counterpart appears in [Abramowitz, Anshelevich, and Zhu, Condition 18 and Theorem 19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.10562). We prove the mass-completion statements directly below.

2. Normalization and one-voter optimization

Because \(P,Q\) are distinct points of a metric space, their distance is positive. Rescaling each completion preserves types and distortion, so we normalize \(d(P,Q)=1\).

Write \(x=d(v,P)\) and \(y=d(v,Q)\). The triangle inequalities imply

\[ x,y\geq0,\qquad x+y\geq1,\qquad |x-y|\leq1. \]

For \(r\geq1\), define

\[ C_t(r)=\frac{rt-1}{t+1}, \]

and, for finite \(u>1\),

\[ H_u(r)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{u-r}{u-1},&1\leq r<u,\\[4pt] \dfrac{u-r}{u+1},&r\geq u. \end{cases} \qquad H_\infty(r)=1. \]

At \(r=u\), both expressions give zero.

We first establish the following exact one-voter suprema:

| Reported type | Supremum of \(x-ry\) |
|---|---|
| \(A_l\) | \(-C_{\tau_l}(r)\) |
| \(B_l\) | \(H_{\tau_{l+1}}(r)\) |
| \(U\), when \(\tau_1>1\) | \(H_{\tau_1}(r)\) |

Here and below \(\tau_{m+1}=\infty\).

Types preferring \(P\)

Suppose \(x>0\), and put \(s=y/x\). For type \(A_l\), \(s\geq t=\tau_l\). Since \(x+y\geq1\),

\[ x\geq\frac1{s+1}. \]

The coefficient \(1-rs\) is nonpositive, so

\[ x-ry=x(1-rs) \leq\frac{1-rs}{s+1} \leq\frac{1-rt}{t+1}. \]

The last inequality follows because the derivative of \((1-rs)/(s+1)\) is \(-(r+1)/(s+1)^2<0\).

Equality is attained on the real line with \(P=0,Q=1\), at voter location \(1/(t+1)\). Its strength is exactly \(t\), which belongs to the reported interval.

The remaining possibility is \(x=0\). Then the triangle inequalities force \(y=1\), and the voter can belong only to \(A_m\). Its objective value is \(-r\), which is smaller than \(-C_t(r)\). Thus the stated supremum holds for every \(A_l\).

Types preferring \(Q\)

When \(y>0\), put \(s=x/y\). The triangle inequalities give

\[ y\geq\frac1{s+1}, \qquad y\leq\frac1{s-1}\quad\text{when }s>1. \]

Consequently,

\[ x-ry=y(s-r)\leq g_r(s), \]

where

\[ g_r(s)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{s-r}{s+1},&0<s\leq r,\\[4pt] \dfrac{s-r}{s-1},&s>r. \end{cases} \]

This function is nondecreasing. Its derivatives on the two branches are respectively

\[ \frac{r+1}{(s+1)^2}>0, \qquad \frac{r-1}{(s-1)^2}\geq0. \]

For \(r>1\), the branches meet at zero. For \(r=1\), there is an upward jump from \(g_1(1)=0\) to \(g_1(s)=1\) for \(s>1\), which also preserves monotonicity.

For \(B_l\) with finite upper endpoint \(u=\tau_{l+1}\), taking \(s\uparrow u\) therefore gives the upper bound \(H_u(r)\). This bound is approached by actual voters on the real line:

  • When \(r\geq u\), use location \(s/(s+1)\).
  • When \(r<u\), choose \(s>r\) sufficiently close to \(u\), and use location \(s/(s-1)\).

In both cases choose \(s\in[\tau_l,u)\). Thus the strict upper endpoint causes no discrepancy between the stated supremum and the allowed completions.

For \(B_m\),

\[ x-ry=(x-y)-(r-1)y\leq1. \]

The value \(1\) is attained at \(Q\), where \(x=1,y=0\) and strength is infinite.

Unreported voters

When \(\tau_1>1\), a voter is of type \(U\) exactly when both distances are positive and

\[ \frac1{\tau_1}<\frac{x}{y}<\tau_1. \]

The same bound using \(g_r(x/y)\), together with locations of strength \(s\uparrow\tau_1\), proves that the supremum is \(H_{\tau_1}(r)\).

When \(\tau_1=1\), type \(U\) is absent by the hypotheses.

3. Exact tests for candidate distortion

Put \(\sigma=\mu(U)\), and define

\[ \Phi_P(r)= -\sum_{l=1}^m a_l C_{\tau_l}(r) +\sum_{l=1}^m b_l H_{\tau_{l+1}}(r) +\sigma H_{\tau_1}(r), \]

where the last term is omitted when \(\tau_1=1\). Define \(\Phi_Q(r)\) by exchanging all \(a_l,b_l\).

The one-voter bounds imply that every normalized completion satisfies

\[ SC_K(P)-rSC_K(Q)\leq\Phi_P(r). \]

This bound is also the exact supremum. To see this, use the real-line locations from Section 2, choosing an arbitrarily close approximation to the supremum for each supported type. Partition the atomless probability space \([0,1]\), with Lebesgue measure, into intervals of the prescribed masses, and map each interval to its chosen voter location.

These are valid atomless-population completions: coincident locations are explicitly allowed, and candidate distances are measurable and integrable. There are only finitely many locations. Since the masses sum to one, approximating every one-voter supremum within \(\varepsilon\) approximates their weighted sum within \(\varepsilon\). Hence

\[ \sup_K\bigl(SC_K(P)-rSC_K(Q)\bigr)=\Phi_P(r), \]

with the supremum taken over normalized completions. Reflection gives the corresponding identity for \(Q\).

For every completion,

\[ SC_K(P)+SC_K(Q)\geq1. \]

Thus both costs cannot vanish. For finite \(r\geq1\), the ratio convention in the question gives the exact equivalence

\[ \frac{SC_K(P)}{\min\{SC_K(P),SC_K(Q)\}}\leq r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad SC_K(P)\leq rSC_K(Q). \]

Indeed, if \(P\) has the smaller cost its ratio is one; if \(Q\) has the smaller positive cost, the equivalence is ordinary division; and if \(SC_K(Q)=0\), the selected cost is positive and both conditions fail.

It follows that

\[ R_P(\Theta,\mu)\leq r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Phi_P(r)\leq0, \]

and symmetrically for \(Q\). Therefore

\[ V(\Theta,\mu)\leq r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Phi_P(r)\leq0\ \text{or}\ \Phi_Q(r)\leq0. \]

This already gives an exact polynomial-time decision procedure.

4. Computing an optimal winner and its exact distortion

Each \(\Phi_X\) is continuous and piecewise affine on \([1,\infty)\). Its breakpoints belong to the input threshold list.

For \(P\), every supported type except \(B_m\) contributes a strictly negative slope on every affine piece. Specifically, the slope magnitudes are

\[ \frac{\tau_l}{\tau_l+1} \quad\text{for }A_l, \qquad \frac1{\tau_{l+1}-1}\ \text{or}\ \frac1{\tau_{l+1}+1} \quad\text{for finite-upper-endpoint }B_l, \]

with the analogous expressions for \(U\). Type \(B_m\) contributes the constant \(1\).

Consequently:

  • If \(b_m=1\), then \(\Phi_P(r)=1\) for every finite \(r\), so \(R_P=\infty\).
  • If \(b_m<1\), then \(\Phi_P\) is strictly decreasing and eventually tends to \(-\infty\).
  • In the second case, \(R_P=1\) when \(\Phi_P(1)\leq0\). Otherwise, \(R_P\) is the unique root of \(\Phi_P(r)=0\) above one.

On each affine interval, write

\[ \Phi_P(r)=\alpha-\beta r. \]

When \(b_m<1\), \(\beta>0\), and its candidate root is the rational number \(\alpha/\beta\). Enumerating the at most \(m+1\) intervals and checking which contains this candidate computes \(R_P\) exactly. Apply the same procedure to \(Q\); its exceptional infinite case is \(a_m=1\).

The two infinite cases cannot occur simultaneously. Thus \(V\) is always a finite rational. Comparing the computed candidate risks and returning a candidate with smaller risk gives a minimizing deterministic winner.

Running time and encoding length

Only supported types need to be scanned. The decision procedure uses \(O(m+\tau+1)\) rational operations, including preprocessing. Computing both exact risks by interval enumeration uses

\[ O\bigl((m+1)(\tau+1)\bigr) \]

rational operations.

All arithmetic can be performed with polynomially bounded encodings; in fact \(O(L)\) bits suffice. To verify this, write a threshold as \(p/q\). The affine coefficients involve only mass rationals and factors such as

\[ \frac{p}{p+q},\qquad \frac{q}{p+q},\qquad \frac{p}{p-q},\qquad \frac{q}{p-q}. \]

In a coefficient sum, each input mass occurs once, and each threshold supplies only a constant number of denominator factors. Their product has \(O(L)\) bits, so the reduced sums, roots, and comparison operands do as well.

Using elementary exact rational arithmetic, conservative bit-operation bounds are therefore

\[ O\bigl((m+\tau+1)L^3\bigr) \]

for decision, and

\[ O\bigl((m+1)(\tau+1)L^3\bigr) \]

for computing a minimizing winner and its exact distortion. For decision, \(L\) includes the encoding of \(r\). There is no dependence on the number of possible voters or on a common denominator interpreted as an electorate size.

5. The mass admissibility condition guarantees the bound

Write \(D=D_\Theta\). By definition \(D\geq\tau_1\), and \(D>1\) because \((\tau_m+2)/\tau_m>1\).

The coefficients in the question are precisely

\[ c_l=C_{\tau_l}(D), \qquad h_l=H_{\tau_{l+1}}(D). \]

Indeed, when \(l<k\), \(\tau_{l+1}\leq D\), giving the denominator \(\tau_{l+1}+1\); when \(k\leq l<m\), \(\tau_{l+1}>D\), giving the denominator \(\tau_{l+1}-1\). The last coefficient is \(H_\infty(D)=1\).

Define

\[ F_P=\sum_l c_l a_l-\sum_l h_l b_l, \qquad F_Q=\sum_l c_l b_l-\sum_l h_l a_l. \]

The displayed admissibility condition permits exactly the candidates whose corresponding \(F_X\) is nonnegative.

Because \(D\geq\tau_1\), the unreported contribution satisfies \(H_{\tau_1}(D)\leq0\) whenever it is present. Thus

\[ \Phi_P(D)=-F_P+\sigma H_{\tau_1}(D)\leq-F_P. \]

If \(P\) is permitted, \(\Phi_P(D)\leq0\), hence \(R_P\leq D\). The identical argument proves \(R_Q\leq D\) whenever \(Q\) is permitted.

Every rule satisfying the mass admissibility condition therefore guarantees distortion at most \(D_\Theta\).

6. The weighted rule satisfies admissibility

We first prove

\[ c_l\geq h_l\qquad\text{for every }l. \]

For \(l<m\), put \(t=\tau_l\) and \(u=\tau_{l+1}\). If \(D\geq u\), then \(C_t(D)>0\) and \(H_u(D)\leq0\), so the inequality holds.

If \(D<u\), direct subtraction gives

\[ C_t(D)-H_u(D) = \frac{(tu+1)D-(tu+2u-1)}{(t+1)(u-1)}. \]

The denominator is positive, and the numerator is nonnegative because

\[ D\geq\frac{tu+2u-1}{tu+1}. \]

For the last interval,

\[ c_m-h_m = \frac{D\tau_m-\tau_m-2}{\tau_m+1} \geq0 \]

by the tail term in the definition of \(D\).

Now let \(W\) be the weighted score difference:

\[ W=\sum_l(c_l+h_l)(a_l-b_l). \]

The two admissibility quantities satisfy

\[ F_P-F_Q=W, \qquad F_P+F_Q=\sum_l(c_l-h_l)(a_l+b_l)\geq0. \]

If \(W\geq0\), including a score tie, then

\[ F_P=\frac{(F_P+F_Q)+W}{2}\geq0. \]

Thus the prescribed choice of \(P\) is permitted. If \(W<0\), then

\[ F_Q=\frac{(F_P+F_Q)-W}{2}>0, \]

so the prescribed choice of \(Q\) is permitted.

This also proves that at least one candidate is always admissible.

The weights are positive. When \(D<u\), this follows from \(c_l>0\) and \(h_l>0\). When \(D\geq u\),

\[ c_l+h_l = \frac{(D+1)(tu-1)}{(t+1)(u+1)}>0. \]

The last weight is \(c_m+1>0\). Computing \(D\), the coefficients, and the support-weighted scores takes \(O(m+\tau+1)\) rational operations and polynomial bit time.

7. Matching lower bounds

We exhibit rational-mass profiles for every potentially maximal term defining \(D_\Theta\). Each construction uses an atomless population mapped to finitely many locations on the real line.

The unreported term

Suppose \(\tau_1>1\), and let all mass be of type \(U\).

For any \(1\leq s<\tau_1\), put every voter at \(s/(s+1)\), with \(P=0,Q=1\). Then

\[ \frac{SC(P)}{SC(Q)}=s. \]

Reflection gives the reverse ratio for the same reported profile. Hence both candidate risks are at least \(\tau_1\).

Conversely, every unreported voter satisfies \(x<\tau_1y\) and \(y<\tau_1x\). Integration gives both candidate risks at most \(\tau_1\). Therefore

\[ V(\Theta,\mu)=\tau_1. \]

When \(\tau_1=1\), this profile is unavailable, but its term cannot determine \(D_\Theta\), since \(D_\Theta>1\).

A finite interval

Fix \(l<m\), put \(t=\tau_l,u=\tau_{l+1}\), and take

\[ a_l=b_l=\frac12, \]

with all other masses zero.

Choose \(s\in[t,u)\) with \(s>1\). Put the \(A_l\) population at \(1/(t+1)\), and the \(B_l\) population at \(s/(s-1)\). These strengths are respectively \(t\) and \(s\). Their social-cost ratio is

\[ \frac{SC(P)}{SC(Q)} = \frac{\frac1{t+1}+\frac{s}{s-1}} {\frac{t}{t+1}+\frac1{s-1}} = \frac{ts+2s-1}{ts+1}. \]

Letting \(s\uparrow u\) shows that \(R_P\) is at least

\[ d_l=\frac{tu+2u-1}{tu+1}. \]

Reflection preserves the reported masses, so \(R_Q\geq d_l\) as well.

We can also identify these risks exactly. The calculations

\[ d_l-1=\frac{2(u-1)}{tu+1}>0, \qquad u-d_l=\frac{(u-1)(tu-1)}{tu+1}>0 \]

show that \(1<d_l<u\). Substituting into the coefficient difference from Section 6 yields

\[ C_t(d_l)=H_u(d_l). \]

Thus \(\Phi_P(d_l)=\Phi_Q(d_l)=0\), and Section 3 gives

\[ R_P=R_Q=V=d_l. \]

The upper strength endpoint need not be attained: the definition uses a supremum.

The last interval

Put \(t=\tau_m\), and take \(a_m=b_m=1/2\).

Place the \(A_m\) population at \(1/(t+1)\), and the \(B_m\) population at \(Q\). The strengths are \(t\) and infinity, and

\[ \frac{SC(P)}{SC(Q)} = \frac{\frac1{t+1}+1}{\frac{t}{t+1}} = \frac{t+2}{t}. \]

Reflection again preserves the profile. At \(r=(t+2)/t\), we have \(C_t(r)=H_\infty(r)=1\), so both \(\Phi\)-tests equal zero. Hence

\[ R_P=R_Q=V=\frac{\tau_m+2}{\tau_m}. \]

8. Sharpness for every fixed threshold list

Sections 5–6 provide a permitted weighted-rule winner of risk at most \(D_\Theta\) for every society. Therefore

\[ V(\Theta,\mu)\leq D_\Theta \qquad\text{for every valid }\mu. \]

Section 7 gives a rational-mass society attaining each relevant term in the finite maximum defining \(D_\Theta\). Choosing a society corresponding to a maximal term proves the stronger statement

\[ \max_{\mu\text{ valid rational masses}}V(\Theta,\mu)=D_\Theta. \]

On that society every deterministic choice has worst-case distortion at least \(D_\Theta\). Consequently the displayed weighted rule, and every everywhere-defined rule satisfying the mass admissibility condition, has global worst-case distortion exactly \(D_\Theta\).
