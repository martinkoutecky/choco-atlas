Theorem 9 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(H\) and a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type has a strict preference order over \(H\) and being unmatched, each school has a strict priority order over types, and each acceptable pair has rational weight \(w_{t,h}\), choose \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) and unmatched masses \(u_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}+u_t=\mu_t\). Set each school's capacity to its assigned mass, \(q_h=\sum_t x_{t,h}\), and require stability: for every acceptable \((t,h)\), either \(u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}=0\), or \(\sum_{t':\,t\succ_h t'}x_{t',h}=0\). Maximize \(\sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}x_{t,h}\); what is its complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H\) contain \(m\geq1\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has a strict ranking of \(H\cup\{\emptyset\}\), where \(\emptyset\) means unmatched, and a priority category at each school. Each school strictly orders its categories and specifies which it accepts; equal categories remain tied, with no tie-breaking. A pair is acceptable exactly when both sides accept it.
Input nonnegative binary rational masses \(y_{th},\ell_t,\nu_t\) and capacities \(q_h\), with \(a_t=\sum_h y_{th}+\ell_t+\nu_t>0\) and \(\sum_t a_t=1\). Here \(y\) is the fixed first-round assignment, \(\ell\) is its unmatched mass, and \(\nu\) arrives afterward; rankings and priorities remain unchanged.
Require \(y\) to use only acceptable pairs, satisfy \(\sum_t y_{th}\leq q_h\), and be stable under the mass-stability condition below with capacities \(q\), assignments \(y\), and unmatched masses \(\ell\), excluding arrivals.
Choose divisible real assignments \(x^L_{th},x^N_{th}\geq0\) on acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_h x^L_{th}\leq\ell_t\) and \(\sum_h x^N_{th}\leq\nu_t\); all other entries are zero.
Set \(z_{th}=y_{th}+x^L_{th}+x^N_{th}\), \(u_t=\ell_t+\nu_t-\sum_h(x^L_{th}+x^N_{th})\), and \(q'_h=\max\{q_h,\sum_t z_{th}\}\); capacity expansion has no additional bound or cost.
Mass stability means: for every acceptable pair \((t,h)\), if \(u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}z_{tg}>0\), then \(\sum_s z_{sh}=q'_h\) and every \(s\) with \(z_{sh}>0\) has priority at least as high as \(t\) at \(h\). This includes fixed first-round mass.
Among stable extensions, maximize \(\sum_{t,h}x^L_{th}\), then minimize \(\sum_{t,h}x^N_{th}\) among those optima. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this exact lexicographic mass-extension problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For indivisible students with strict preferences on both sides, maximizing leftover admissions and then minimizing arriving admissions is NP-hard; minimizing arriving admissions first and then maximizing leftover admissions has a polynomial-time algorithm. [Verified results](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.04431v6)
No result settling this exact continuous problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified mutual acceptability using the student's unmatched option and a school acceptance cutoff over ordered priority categories.
  • Retained ties within priority categories without tie-breaking, following the graded condition that only strictly lower priority creates a priority violation.
  • Normalized combined first-round and arriving mass to one; every listed type has positive combined mass.
  • Specified nonnegative binary rational input masses and capacities, divisible real assignments, and separate conservation bounds for leftover and arriving mass.
  • Made first-round stability a promise on the original society, excluding arrivals, and included fixed first-round assignments in final stability.
  • Retained the graded question's promise of a stable first-round assignment rather than adding a continuous student-optimality requirement.
  • Allowed zero capacities and empty acceptable sets, which the discrete definitions do not exclude; required at least one school.
  • Specified unchanged preferences and priorities and unrestricted, cost-free capacity expansion; the full version explicitly preserves the original capacity as a lower bound.

Result

The exact lexicographic mass-extension problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when all capacities are positive, all masses are dyadic rationals, and schools have strict priorities without ties.

The key is that divisibility does not permit fractional payment for opening a school: admitting any positive mass of a lower-priority leftover type can force admission of an entire higher-priority arrival type. We adapt the Set Cover construction in [Gajulapalli et al., Theorem 9](https://arxiv.org/html/1904.04431v6), verifying this implication directly under the stated mass-stability condition.

1. Construction

We reduce from Set Cover. Let the universe be \(U=\{1,\ldots,n\}\), with sets \(S_1,\ldots,S_r\) satisfying \(\bigcup_i S_i=U\), and let \(\kappa\) denote the minimum cover size. Assume \(n,r\geq1\).

Set Cover is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Restricting to families whose union is the universe preserves hardness: an instance with an uncovered element is an immediately recognizable no-instance and can be replaced by a fixed coverable no-instance. See [Karp’s original completeness result](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Write \(W=n+2r\). Choose the smallest power of two \(B>W\), and define

\(\delta=B^{-1},\qquad d=1-W\delta>0.\)

There are \(m=r\) schools, \(h_1,\ldots,h_r\), each with initial capacity \(q_{h_i}=\delta\). Introduce the following types:

  • An incumbent type \(b_i\) for each school, with \(y_{b_i h_i}=\delta\) and no unmatched or arriving mass.
  • An arrival type \(v_i\) for each school, with \(\nu_{v_i}=\delta\) and no other mass.
  • An element type \(e_j\) for each element, with \(\ell_{e_j}=\delta\) and no other mass.
  • A dummy type \(d_0\), with \(\ell_{d_0}=d\) and no other mass.

All unspecified entries are zero. Thus every listed type has positive mass, and

\(\sum_t a_t=r\delta+r\delta+n\delta+d=1.\)

There are \(\tau=n+2r+1\) types.

The preferences are as follows:

  • Both \(b_i\) and \(v_i\) find only \(h_i\) acceptable.
  • Type \(e_j\) finds precisely the schools \(h_i\) with \(j\in S_i\) acceptable, in any fixed strict order.
  • Type \(d_0\) finds no school acceptable.

Complete each ranking strictly by placing the remaining schools below \(\emptyset\).

Every school accepts every priority category. At school \(h_i\), give every type a distinct category and order them with \(b_i\) first, \(v_i\) second, all element types next, and the remaining types afterward. Consequently, priorities are strict, and at \(h_i\) both \(b_i\) and \(v_i\) outrank every element type.

Validity of the first round

The initial assignment uses acceptable pairs and fills each school exactly to capacity.

The only first-round types that can desire an acceptable school are the element types. Each such school contains only its highest-priority incumbent \(b_i\), and is full. Therefore the first-round assignment is stable under the required mass-stability condition.

The dummy has no acceptable pair, and arrivals are excluded from the first round. Thus the construction satisfies every input requirement.

2. Every cover gives a stable extension

Let \(C\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}\) be a cover.

For each element \(j\), let \(h_{i(j)}\) be its most preferred acceptable school whose index belongs to \(C\). Assign

\(x^L_{e_j h_{i(j)}}=\delta.\)

For each \(i\in C\), assign \(x^N_{v_i h_i}=\delta\). Admit no other arriving mass.

This extension admits leftover mass \(n\delta\) and arriving mass \(|C|\delta\). We verify stability school by school.

If \(i\notin C\): school \(h_i\) contains only \(b_i\), of mass \(\delta\). Hence its enrollment equals \(q'_{h_i}=\delta\), and its sole occupant has higher priority than every other type. Every stability implication at this school is satisfied.

If \(i\in C\): every type having an acceptable pair with \(h_i\) has zero mass that desires it:

  • The incumbent \(b_i\) is entirely assigned to its only acceptable school.
  • The arrival \(v_i\) is entirely assigned to its only acceptable school.
  • Each element type is entirely assigned to its most preferred acceptable school in \(C\), so it has no mass assigned below \(h_i\).

Thus every stability antecedent at this school is zero.

The extension is therefore stable.

Only element types have leftover mass that can be admitted; their total mass is \(n\delta\). Taking \(C=\{1,\ldots,r\}\) proves that the first objective has exact optimum

\(\max\sum_{t,h}x^L_{th}=n\delta.\)

3. Positive element admission forces full arrival admission

Activation lemma. In any stable extension, if positive element mass is assigned to \(h_i\), then \(x^N_{v_i h_i}=\delta\).

Proof. Suppose some element type has positive assignment at \(h_i\), but \(x^N_{v_i h_i}<\delta\).

Type \(v_i\) has only one acceptable school and no fixed assignment. Consequently,

\(u_{v_i}=\delta-x^N_{v_i h_i}>0.\)

The stability antecedent for \((v_i,h_i)\) is therefore positive. Stability requires every positive-mass occupant of \(h_i\) to have priority at least as high as \(v_i\).

The admitted element type has strictly lower priority than \(v_i\), contradicting that requirement. Hence \(x^N_{v_i h_i}=\delta\). \(\square\)

This argument applies to arbitrarily small positive element assignments.

4. The second optimum equals the minimum cover size

Consider any stable extension attaining the first optimum \(n\delta\). Each element type has assignment at most \(\delta\), so attaining this total forces every element type to be entirely assigned.

Define

\(C(x)=\left\{i:\sum_{j=1}^{n}x^L_{e_j h_i}>0\right\}.\)

Every element has positive assignment at some acceptable school. Thus \(C(x)\) covers \(U\), and \(|C(x)|\geq\kappa\).

By the activation lemma, every school indexed by \(C(x)\) receives its full arrival mass \(\delta\). Therefore

\(\sum_{t,h}x^N_{th}\geq\delta|C(x)|\geq\delta\kappa.\)

Conversely, Section 2 constructs a stable extension from a minimum cover, attaining leftover admission \(n\delta\) and arriving admission \(\delta\kappa\). The exact lexicographic optimum is consequently

\(\left(n\delta,\ \delta\kappa\right).\)

Given a Set Cover threshold \(k\), the instance has a cover of size at most \(k\) exactly when the second coordinate of this optimum is at most \(k\delta\).

Reduction size and running time

The constructed instance has

\(m=r,\qquad \tau=n+2r+1.\)

All masses and capacities have \(O(\log\tau)\)-bit dyadic representations. Explicitly writing the preference rankings, priority orders, and mass tables takes

\(O\!\left(m\tau\log(m+\tau)\right)\)

bit operations and gives an input length \(L=O\!\left(m\tau\log(m+\tau)\right)\).

Thus an exact algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) would solve Set Cover in polynomial time. The requested problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and has no such polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

5. An exact exponential-time upper bound

For completeness, the general problem admits an exact algorithm running in

\((\tau+1)^m\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

Here is a proof that also addresses exact optimization over real assignments.

For an acceptable pair, define its nonnegative desire mass

\(D_{th}=u_t+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}z_{tg},\)

and write \(Z_h=\sum_t z_{th}\). These are affine expressions in the extension variables.

At each school, enumerate the following alternatives:

  • Zero desire: impose \(D_{th}=0\) for every acceptable type \(t\).
  • A category cutoff \(c\): choose a category represented by an acceptable support type and impose:
  • \(Z_h\geq q_h\);
  • \(z_{sh}=0\) for every acceptable type \(s\) with priority below \(c\);
  • \(D_{th}=0\) for every acceptable type \(t\) with priority above \(c\).

There are at most \(\tau+1\) alternatives per school.

Soundness. The zero-desire alternative makes all stability antecedents vanish. Under a cutoff alternative, any type with positive desire has priority at most \(c\), while every occupant has priority at least \(c\). Also \(Z_h\geq q_h\) implies \(Z_h=q'_h\). Hence stability holds.

Completeness. Take a stable extension. If all desire masses at a school vanish, choose its zero-desire alternative. Otherwise stability gives \(Z_h\geq q_h\). If \(Z_h>0\), choose the lowest-priority occupied category as \(c\). Any type above \(c\) must have zero desire, because otherwise it would object to that occupant. If \(Z_h=0\), then \(q_h=0\); choose the highest-priority acceptable category. All cutoff constraints again hold.

Therefore the stable-extension set is exactly a union of at most \((\tau+1)^m\) rational polytopes.

For each alternative combination, impose the basic extension constraints and solve two linear programs: first maximize leftover admission, then fix that optimum and minimize arriving admission. The variables are bounded by their cohort masses, so every nonempty polytope is compact. The polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming applies: the number of variables, constraints, and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Each nonempty polytope has an exact rational lexicographic optimum of polynomial bit length.

Comparing these optima gives the stated running time, or detects infeasibility.
