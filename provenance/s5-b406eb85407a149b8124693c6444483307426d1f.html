The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite induced square-grid graph \(H=(P,E)\) of \(\tau\) population types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_p\) summing to \(1\), suppose every individual of type \(p\) is connected to every individual in each adjacent type \(q\). Let \(D_p=\sum_{q\in N(p)}\mu_q\). Choose seed masses \(x_p\in[0,\mu_p]\), initially active masses \(A_p^0=x_p\), and update synchronously: an inactive type \(p\) becomes fully active when \(\sum_{q\in N(p)}A_q^r\ge D_p/2\). What is the minimum \(\sum_{p\in P}x_p\) that eventually activates every type (equivalently, given a rational budget \(b\), is there a seed vector of total mass at most \(b\))?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_p\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The types are distinct points \(P\subset\mathbb Z^2\), supplied with binary integer coordinates; \(H=(P,E)\) has an edge exactly between points at Manhattan distance \(1\). Put \(m=|E|\), and let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including coordinates, masses, and budget.
Individuals are adjacent exactly when their types are adjacent in \(H\); there are no connections within a type. Define \(N(p)=\{q:\{p,q\}\in E\}\) and \(D_p=\sum_{q\in N(p)}\mu_q\).
Choose real seed masses \(0\le x_p\le\mu_p\), with \(A_p^0=x_p\). In synchronous rounds, set \(A_p^{r+1}=\mu_p\) if \(\sum_{q\in N(p)}A_q^r\ge D_p/2\), and otherwise set \(A_p^{r+1}=A_p^r\). Thus seeded mass persists and the remaining mass activates together when the threshold is met; isolated types activate in the first round.
Given a binary rational budget \(b\in[0,1]\), decide whether some seed vector with \(\sum_{p\in P}x_p\le b\) yields \(A_p^r=\mu_p\) for every type after finitely many rounds.

Is this continuous majority target-set decision problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete target set selection on induced square-grid graphs, with thresholds \(\lceil\deg(v)/2\rceil\) and seed cost equal to cardinality, is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete ([Dvořák, Knop, and Schierreich, 2024](https://doi.org/10.46298/dmtcs.11591)).
No published complexity result for exactly the continuous problem defined here was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the partial-type update explicit: seeded mass persists, and all remaining mass activates simultaneously when the neighboring active mass reaches the threshold.
  • Allowed real seed masses; only input masses and the budget must be binary rationals.
  • Specified a nonempty support and supplied binary integer grid coordinates, with induced adjacency determined by Manhattan distance one.
  • Restricted the normalized budget to [0,1], since total population mass is one.
  • Retained isolated types and zero thresholds, consistent with the discrete majority definition; they activate without seeds in the first round.
  • Defined m as the number of support-graph edges and L as the total binary input length.

Let
$$ B=\{p\in P:\deg_H(p)\ge 3\}, \qquad k=|B|. $$

Proven result. The continuous problem can be solved in
$$ \tau^{O(k+1)}\operatorname{poly}(L) $$
time. Consequently, it is polynomial-time solvable when $k$ is bounded by a constant. In particular, arbitrary binary rational masses on supports of maximum degree $2$—disjoint unions of paths, cycles, and isolated vertices—admit a polynomial-time algorithm.

The proof gives a finite description of optimal seed values that does not enumerate a common denominator numerically. I also prove membership in $\mathsf{NP}$ and give a counterexample showing that the direct transfer from discrete hardness fails even on supports without leaves.

1. An exact description using acyclic orientations

An orientation $\mathcal O$ assigns a direction to each edge. Write
$$ I_{\mathcal O}(p)=\{q:q\to p\}, \qquad O_{\mathcal O}(p)=\{q:p\to q\}. $$

For $S\subseteq P$ and an acyclic orientation $\mathcal O$, let $F(S,\mathcal O)$ be the polytope defined by
$$ 0\le x_p\le\mu_p \quad(p\in P), $$
$$ x_p=\mu_p \quad(p\in S), $$
and
$$ \sum_{q\in I_{\mathcal O}(p)}\mu_q+ \sum_{q\in O_{\mathcal O}(p)}x_q \ge \frac{D_p}{2} \quad(p\notin S). $$

Lemma 1. A seed vector succeeds if and only if it belongs to $F(S,\mathcal O)$ for some $S$ and some acyclic orientation $\mathcal O$.

Proof. Suppose $x$ succeeds. Let $S$ contain exactly the initially fully active types. Order the vertices by their first round of full activation, breaking ties arbitrarily, and orient each edge from its earlier endpoint to its later endpoint.

For $p\notin S$, let $r\ge 1$ be its first round of full activation. An outgoing neighbor becomes fully active in round $r$ or later, so its mass in round $r-1$ is exactly its seed mass. An incoming neighbor has active mass at most its full mass. Hence
$$ \sum_{q\in I_{\mathcal O}(p)}\mu_q+ \sum_{q\in O_{\mathcal O}(p)}x_q \ge \sum_{q\in N(p)}A_q^{r-1} \ge \frac{D_p}{2}. $$
The orientation is acyclic because it follows a linear order.

Conversely, suppose $x\in F(S,\mathcal O)$, and take a topological ordering $p_1,\ldots,p_\tau$. Inductively, $p_i$ is fully active by round $i$: if $p_i\in S$, it is already fully active; otherwise, by round $i-1$, every incoming neighbor is fully active, while every outgoing neighbor retains at least its seed mass. The defining inequality therefore activates $p_i$ by round $i$. This also handles isolated vertices, whose inequality is $0\ge 0$. $\square$

There are finitely many choices of $S$ and $\mathcal O$. Each $F(S,\mathcal O)$ is closed and bounded. Their union is nonempty, since seeding all types fully succeeds. Therefore the minimum successful seed cost
$$ \operatorname{OPT}=\min\left\{\sum_{p\in P}x_p:x\text{ succeeds}\right\} $$
exists.

The graph itself can be constructed by testing pairwise Manhattan distances using binary arithmetic. This takes polynomial time in $\tau$ and $L$, without enumerating the coordinate bounding box.

Corollary 2. The decision problem belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$.

Proof. A certificate specifies $S$ and an orientation. The verifier checks acyclicity and tests feasibility of $F(S,\mathcal O)$ with the additional inequality
$$ \sum_{p\in P}x_p\le b. $$
This rational linear program has $\tau$ variables, polynomially many constraints, and coefficients of polynomial binary encoding length. Polynomial-time rational linear-programming feasibility applies. Lemma 1 proves that the verifier accepts exactly the successful instances. $\square$

2. A finite description of optimal seed values

Introduce centered variables
$$ u_p=2x_p-\mu_p. $$
Thus
$$ -\mu_p\le u_p\le\mu_p, \qquad x_p=\frac{\mu_p+u_p}{2}. $$

For $p\notin S$, the orientation inequality becomes
$$ \sum_{q\in O_{\mathcal O}(p)}u_q \ge -\sum_{q\in I_{\mathcal O}(p)}\mu_q. $$
Indeed, multiplying the original inequality by $2$ and substituting $2x_q=\mu_q+u_q$ cancels the outgoing full masses.

At vertices of degree at most $2$, the only nontrivial possibilities are:

  • Degree $1$, with its neighbor outgoing:

$$ u_q\ge 0. $$

  • Degree $2$, with one incoming neighbor $r$ and one outgoing neighbor $q$:

$$ u_q\ge-\mu_r. $$

  • Degree $2$, with both neighbors outgoing:

$$ u_q+u_r\ge 0. $$

All remaining cases at these degrees give automatic inequalities.

Define
$$ \mathcal A_0=\{0\}\cup\{\mu_p,-\mu_p:p\in P\}. $$

Lemma 3. There is an optimal seed vector for which, for some $r\le k$ and some rational numbers $t_1,\ldots,t_r$,
$$ u_p\in \mathcal A_0\cup\{t_1,-t_1,\ldots,t_r,-t_r\} \quad(p\in P). $$
Moreover, a family of tuples containing such a tuple can be enumerated in $\tau^{O(k+1)}\operatorname{poly}(L)$ time.

Proof of the structural assertion

Choose a globally optimal successful seed vector, and choose $S,\mathcal O$ supplied by Lemma 1. Minimize seed cost over $F(S,\mathcal O)$ and take an optimal vertex of this polytope. Its cost remains $\operatorname{OPT}$.

Call the following constraints ordinary:

  • All variable bounds and the equalities fixing vertices in $S$.
  • All constraints associated with vertices of degree at most $2$.

The remaining constraints are associated with vertices in $B$; there are at most $k$ of them.

Every nonautomatic ordinary constraint is either a one-variable constraint with constant in $\mathcal A_0$, or
$$ u_q+u_r\ge 0. $$

At the chosen vertex, form a graph on the variable indices: put an edge $qr$ whenever the corresponding ordinary pair constraint is tight. Its equation is
$$ u_q+u_r=0. $$
Duplicate equations may be discarded.

Consider a connected component of this graph.

If it contains an odd cycle, following the equations around that cycle gives $u_q=-u_q$, hence $u_q=0$. Connectivity then forces every variable in that component to be $0$.

Otherwise, the component is bipartite. Choose signs $\varepsilon_q\in\{-1,1\}$ that change sign across every edge. The pair equations imply
$$ u_q=\varepsilon_q t $$
for one parameter $t$.

If this component also has a tight ordinary one-variable constraint $u_q=a$, with $a\in\mathcal A_0$, then $t=\varepsilon_q a$. Since $\mathcal A_0$ is closed under negation, every variable in the component belongs to $\mathcal A_0$.

Thus the tight ordinary constraints leave precisely one free parameter for each bipartite component having no tight one-variable constraint. Let their number be $r$, and denote their parameters by $t_1,\ldots,t_r$.

At a vertex, the full collection of tight constraint normals spans the whole variable space. Otherwise, a nonzero direction orthogonal to all tight normals would allow sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, preserving all constraints and contradicting extremality. Therefore the at most $k$ remaining tight constraints must determine these $r$ parameters. In particular,
$$ r\le k. $$
Choose $r$ of those constraints whose restrictions to the free parameters are linearly independent. They determine $t_1,\ldots,t_r$ uniquely. This proves the asserted form of the variables.

Enumeration of the parameters

For each $r\in\{0,\ldots,k\}$, proceed as follows. For $r=0$, include the empty tuple.

For $r>0$:

  • Choose $r$ distinct vertices of $B$.
  • For each chosen vertex $p$, choose which of its neighbors are outgoing.
  • For each outgoing neighbor, choose a formal expression from

$$ \mathcal A_0\cup\{T_1,-T_1,\ldots,T_r,-T_r\}. $$

  • Form the $r$ equations

$$ \sum_{q\in O(p)}\ell_{pq} = -\sum_{q\in N(p)\setminus O(p)}\mu_q, $$
where $\ell_{pq}$ is the chosen expression.

  • If the coefficient matrix in $T_1,\ldots,T_r$ is nonsingular, solve the system and include its solution tuple.

The independent tight constraints used above appear in this enumeration, with the expressions that describe their actual variable values. Hence the enumerated family contains a suitable tuple. Other enumerated tuples need not describe feasible seeds; feasibility will be checked later.

A grid vertex has at most $4$ neighbors. Thus, for each selected vertex, there are at most $16$ outgoing-neighbor choices and at most
$$ (2\tau+1+2r)^4 $$
expression assignments. The total number of systems is $\tau^{O(k+1)}$.

For bit complexity, let $Q$ be the product of the input denominators. Then $\log Q=O(L)$. Each system has an integer coefficient matrix whose entries have absolute value at most $4$, and a right-hand side consisting of signed sums of input masses. Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s inequality show that its solution coordinates have binary encoding length
$$ O\bigl(L+r\log(r+1)\bigr). $$
Exact solution of each system therefore takes polynomial time in $\tau$ and $L$. $\square$

A useful special case follows immediately.

Corollary 4. If $H$ has maximum degree $2$, an optimal seed vector exists with
$$ x_p\in \left( \left\{\frac{\mu_p}{2}\right\} \cup \left\{ \frac{\mu_p+\mu_q}{2}, \frac{\mu_p-\mu_q}{2}:q\in P \right\} \right) \cap[0,\mu_p]. $$

There are at most $2\tau+1$ candidate values for each type. Their number is independent of the numerical values of the input denominators.

3. Optimizing over a fixed finite set of values

Fix one tuple enumerated in Lemma 3, and put
$$ \mathcal A= \mathcal A_0\cup\{t_1,-t_1,\ldots,t_r,-t_r\}. $$
For each type define its allowed seed values by
$$ \mathcal X_p= \left\{ \frac{\mu_p+a}{2}: a\in\mathcal A,\ -\mu_p\le a\le\mu_p \right\}. $$
Write
$$ K=\max_{p\in P}|\mathcal X_p|. $$
We have $K=O(\tau)$.

For a fixed allowed labeling and orientation, the local condition is
$$ x_p=\mu_p \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_{q\in I_{\mathcal O}(p)}\mu_q+ \sum_{q\in O_{\mathcal O}(p)}x_q \ge\frac{D_p}{2}. $$
By Lemma 1, these local conditions together with acyclicity are exactly what is needed.

Paths

First consider a path $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. For its edge $v_iv_{i+1}$, let $d_i=1$ mean $v_i\to v_{i+1}$, and let $d_i=0$ mean the reverse.

For an internal vertex $v_i$, its threshold condition is
$$ \begin{aligned} &d_{i-1}\mu_{v_{i-1}}+(1-d_{i-1})x_{v_{i-1}}\\ &\qquad +(1-d_i)\mu_{v_{i+1}}+d_i x_{v_{i+1}} \ge \frac{\mu_{v_{i-1}}+\mu_{v_{i+1}}}{2}, \end{aligned} $$
unless $x_{v_i}=\mu_{v_i}$. At endpoints, omit the missing neighbor.

For $i\ge 2$, a dynamic-programming state records
$$ (x_{v_{i-1}},x_{v_i},d_{i-1}). $$
Its value is the minimum cost on $v_1,\ldots,v_i$, subject to these recorded choices and the local conditions at $v_1,\ldots,v_{i-1}$.

Initialize by choosing $x_{v_1},x_{v_2},d_1$, checking the condition at $v_1$, and assigning cost $x_{v_1}+x_{v_2}$. A transition chooses $x_{v_{i+1}}$ and $d_i$, checks the condition at $v_i$, adds $x_{v_{i+1}}$, and shifts the state. At termination, check the condition at $v_n$.

Induction on $i$ proves correctness: a transition checks exactly the newly determined local condition, and every feasible labeling and orientation appears in the recurrence. Every path orientation is acyclic.

There are $O(K^2)$ states and $O(K)$ choices per transition, giving
$$ O(nK^3) $$
arithmetic operations. A single isolated vertex has optimum $0$.

Cycles

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ be a cycle in cyclic order. Fix the first two labels and the direction $d_1$. Use the same recurrence, but defer the condition at $v_1$ until the closing edge is chosen.

Also record whether the directions seen so far contain $0$, contain $1$, or contain both. At termination, choose the closing-edge direction and check the conditions at $v_n$ and $v_1$.

Require both direction values to occur. This is precisely the acyclicity condition: a directed cycle in an orientation of a simple undirected cycle must traverse the entire cycle, so all edge directions must agree in cyclic order.

For each initial choice, the recurrence costs $O(nK^3)$. There are $O(K^2)$ initial label choices and constantly many direction choices, giving
$$ O(nK^5) $$
operations.

Therefore maximum-degree-$2$ supports can be optimized in $O(\tau^6)$ arithmetic operations after constructing the graph.

4. Incorporating the branching types

The preceding dynamic programs extend to graphs with $k$ branching types.

Remove $B$ from $H$. Every remaining vertex has degree at most $2$, so every component of $H-B$ is a path, a cycle, or an isolated vertex.

A cycle component has no neighbors in $B$, because each of its vertices already has two neighbors within the component. A path component has at most two edges to $B$:

  • An internal path vertex already has two neighbors within the path.
  • Each endpoint has at most one additional neighbor.
  • An isolated vertex has at most two neighbors in $B$.

Let
$$ F=\{q\in P\setminus B:N(q)\cap B\ne\varnothing\}. $$
Since every branching vertex has degree at most $4$,
$$ |F|\le 4k. $$

For the fixed sets of allowed seed values, enumerate:

  • The labels on $B\cup F$.
  • The directions of all edges incident with $B$.
  • A linear order of the vertices in $B$.

There are at most
$$ K^{5k}2^{4k}k! $$
such choices.

For each choice, check the local conditions at every vertex in $B$. Reject any edge between branching vertices that points backwards in the chosen order.

Now optimize each component of $H-B$ independently, subject to its fixed endpoint labels and boundary-edge directions.

For a path, include the known contribution of an adjacent branching vertex in the endpoint condition: its contribution is its full mass if its boundary edge points into the path, and its seed mass otherwise. A one-vertex component is checked directly.

One additional condition is needed for a path having two boundary edges:

  • If its two boundary branching vertices are distinct, forbid a directed path from the later branching vertex to the earlier one.
  • If both boundary edges lead to the same branching vertex, forbid a directed cycle through that vertex.

These conditions are checked by recording which direction values occur along the entire path, including its boundary edges. A traversal from one boundary vertex to the other is directed exactly when all directions along that traversal agree. Thus only a constant number of additional states is needed.

Unattached cycles use the cycle algorithm from Section 3.

Why the resulting orientation is acyclic

Suppose an accepted orientation had a directed cycle.

If it avoided $B$, it would lie in a cycle component of $H-B$, which the cycle algorithm forbids.

Otherwise, consider consecutive visits to branching vertices along the directed cycle. Between those visits, the cycle either follows an edge within $B$, or traverses a path component of $H-B$.

A return to the same branching vertex is forbidden. Every traversal between distinct branching vertices goes forward in the chosen order. A directed cycle would therefore give a closed sequence of strict increases in a linear order, which is impossible.

Hence the assembled orientation is acyclic. All its local conditions have been checked, so Lemma 1 proves that its seed vector succeeds.

Why an optimum is retained

Conversely, take any successful labeling using the allowed values. Lemma 1 supplies an acyclic orientation. Restrict a topological order to $B$.

The labels, boundary directions, and restricted order occur in the enumeration. Its directed paths between branching vertices necessarily go forward in that order. Each component labeling is therefore admitted by its dynamic program. Thus the algorithm returns the minimum successful cost among the allowed labelings.

Count the labels in $B$ explicitly, and count every other label in its component cost. In particular, labels in $F$ are fixed by the enumeration but counted only in their component, avoiding double counting.

Running time and exact arithmetic

For a fixed tuple, the interface enumeration and component processing take
$$ \tau^{O(k+1)} $$
arithmetic operations. Lemma 3 supplies $\tau^{O(k+1)}$ tuples; multiplying these bounds preserves the same form.

For each tuple, all candidate values have polynomial binary encoding length. They can be represented over a common denominator obtained from $Q$ and the determinant of the tuple’s coefficient matrix. Dynamic-programming costs are sums of these values, so exact arithmetic and budget comparisons have polynomial bit complexity.

Lemma 3 guarantees that one enumerated tuple contains a globally optimal labeling. Every accepted labeling is genuinely successful. Taking the minimum across tuples therefore computes $\operatorname{OPT}$ exactly.

This proves the stated
$$ \tau^{O(k+1)}\operatorname{poly}(L) $$
decision algorithm.

5. Fractional seeding matters even without leaves

The discrete majority hardness theorem concerns fully seeded vertices and cardinality cost. [Dvořák, Knop, and Schierreich, Corollary 29](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/13889/pdf).

The direct replacement of discrete budget $s$ by continuous budget $s/\tau$ fails even when every type has degree at least $2$.

Take two disjoint copies of the full $3$-by-$3$ grid:
$$ P=\{0,1,2\}^2 \cup \bigl((5,0)+\{0,1,2\}^2\bigr). $$
There are $\tau=18$ types, each of mass
$$ w=\frac1{18}. $$
The coordinate separation ensures that there are no edges between the copies.

In each copy, using coordinates relative to that copy, seed
$$ x_{(1,2)}=w, \qquad x_{(2,0)}=\frac w2, $$
and seed all other types with zero. The total cost over both copies is
$$ 2\left(w+\frac w2\right)=3w=\frac16. $$

Within either copy, the following additional types become fully active:

| Round | Newly fully active types |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $(0,2),(2,2)$ |
| $2$ | $(2,1)$ |
| $3$ | $(2,0),(1,1)$ |
| $4$ | $(1,0),(0,1)$ |
| $5$ | $(0,0)$ |

For the nonintegral step in round $2$, type $(2,1)$ has degree $3$ and receives
$$ w+\frac w2=\frac{3w}{2}, $$
exactly its threshold. In round $3$, the center receives $2w$, exactly its degree-$4$ threshold. Every other listed activation receives either one full neighbor at degree $2$ or two full neighbors at degree $3$, which suffices.

In the discrete process, one seed cannot activate a $3$-by-$3$ grid:

  • A corner seed or center seed activates no other vertex, because all its neighbors require two active neighbors.
  • A side-middle seed activates its two neighboring corners. Afterwards, the center has only one active neighbor, and each other side-middle vertex has at most one active neighbor. The process stops.

Therefore each copy requires at least two discrete seeds. A total discrete budget of $3$ fails, whereas the corresponding continuous budget
$$ b=\frac3{18} $$
succeeds.

Thus the failure of the direct hardness transfer is not confined to degree-$1$ vertices.

6. What remains open in this argument

The algorithm is polynomial for a constant number of branching types, but an unrestricted induced grid support can have $k$ proportional to $\tau$. Its running time therefore does not establish polynomial-time solvability in $m,\tau,L$.

Nor does the counterexample exclude a different hardness reduction. The missing step remains either a general polynomial-time algorithm or a reduction that accounts for fractional seeding.
