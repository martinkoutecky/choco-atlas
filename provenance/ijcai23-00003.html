The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), known valuation templates \(f_\theta\) over public-good classes, prediction values \(\widehat V_\theta\), and parameters \(c_\theta,d_\theta\), an adversary reveals \(L\) current project classes per round without revealing future rounds. The policy irrevocably chooses \(x_{\ell,t}\in[0,1]\) subject to \(\sum_\ell x_{\ell,t}\le1\) and \(\sum_{t,\ell}x_{\ell,t}\le B\). With \(u_\theta(x)=\sum_{t,\ell}f_\theta(p_{\ell,t})x_{\ell,t}\), determine the smallest guarantee \(\alpha\) achievable by an online policy such that \(\max_{w\in\mathcal F}\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta u_\theta(w)/(c_\theta u_\theta(x))\le\alpha\) for every legal arrival sequence.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. Let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of types with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\ge0\) summing to \(1\). In round \(t\in[T]\), values \(v_{\theta,t}\ge0\) are revealed and the policy irrevocably chooses \(x_t\in[0,1]\), with \(\sum_t x_t\le B\). Let \(u_\theta(z)=\sum_t v_{\theta,t}z_t\), \(V_\theta=\sum_t v_{\theta,t}\), and \(\widehat V_\theta\in[V_\theta/d_\theta,c_\theta V_\theta]\), where \(c_\theta,d_\theta\ge1\). A comparator \(w\) satisfies \(w_t\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_t w_t\le B\); use \(0/0=1\) and \(a/0=+\infty\) for \(a>0\).

Target. Is finding an optimal online policy minimizing \(\sup_{w:\sum_t w_t\le B}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\,u_\theta(w)/(c_\theta u_\theta(x))\) polynomial-time solvable, or NP-hard?

Nearest known result. For a finite unweighted agent list with binary values and \(B=1\), every online algorithm has \(\Omega(\log N)\) proportional-fairness loss, even when it knows \(T\) and every agent’s total \(\kappa_i=\sum_t v_{i,t}\). This is an online lower bound, rather than a computational classification of the weighted type-distribution formulation.

Status: the stated dichotomy is not yet well-defined

The displayed expression defines the loss of a completed allocation on one realized value sequence. It does not define how losses on different admissible sequences are aggregated when optimizing over online policies. Consequently, it presently defines neither a decision problem nor a search problem to which “polynomial-time solvable” or “NP-hard” applies.

Nevertheless, two substantial facts can be proved:

  • Under the fixed-sequence offline interpretation, the optimal value has a closed form and weak optimization is polynomial-time solvable by second-order cone programming.
  • There need not be a rational optimal allocation, even when all input data are rational. Thus exact output also requires an explicit representation model.

1. Why the online-policy problem is underspecified

An online policy \( \pi \) maps each revealed history \( (v_{\theta,s})_{\theta\in\Theta,s\le t} \) to a decision \( x_t \). For a completed sequence \( \mathbf v \), let \( x^\pi(\mathbf v) \) be its output and let \( L_{\mathbf v}(\pi) \) denote the displayed proportional-fairness loss.

The collection \( \{L_{\mathbf v}(\pi)\}_{\mathbf v} \) is not a scalar objective. One must additionally specify, for example,

\[ \inf_\pi\sup_{\mathbf v\in\mathcal V(\widehat V,c,d)} \sup_{w\in P_B} \sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta \frac{u_\theta(w)} {c_\theta u_\theta(x^\pi(\mathbf v))}, \]

where \( P_B=\{w\in[0,1]^T:\sum_t w_t\le B\} \), or instead an expectation under a given probability distribution over sequences.

The prediction inequalities would naturally define

\[ \mathcal V(\widehat V,c,d) = \left\{\mathbf v: \frac{\widehat V_\theta}{c_\theta} \le \sum_t v_{\theta,t} \le d_\theta\widehat V_\theta \text{ for every }\theta \right\}, \]

but this outer quantifier is absent from the target.

No policy can be pointwise optimal on every admissible sequence

Take one type of mass \( 1 \), \( T=2 \), \( B=1 \), \( c=1 \), \( \widehat V=1 \), and \( d=M+1 \), where \( M>1 \). Consider the two admissible sequences

\[ (1,0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (1,M). \]

Both have the same first-round observation. Their totals are respectively \( 1 \) and \( M+1 \), and both satisfy \( \widehat V\in[V/d,cV] \).

Let the policy choose \( x_1=a \) after observing the common first value \( 1 \).

For the first sequence, the hindsight comparator obtains utility \( 1 \), while the policy obtains \( a \). Its loss is \( 1/a \), with the stated convention when \( a=0 \). Achieving the offline optimum \( 1 \) therefore requires \( a=1 \).

For the second sequence, even if the policy spends all remaining budget in round two, its utility is at most

\[ a+M(1-a)=M-(M-1)a. \]

The comparator obtains \( M \). Hence the loss is at least

\[ \frac{M}{M-(M-1)a}, \]

which equals \( 1 \) only when \( a=0 \).

Thus no online policy is simultaneously pointwise optimal on both admissible sequences. An outer worst-case, expectation, regret, or other aggregation criterion is indispensable.

This agrees with the source model, where an online algorithm is called \( \alpha \)-proportionally fair when it always produces an \( \alpha \)-proportionally fair allocation; the known \( \Omega(\log N) \) theorem is a worst-case guarantee over arrival sequences, not a computational-hardness statement. [Banerjee et al., *Proportionally Fair Online Allocation of Public Goods with Predictions*](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~emicha/papers/fair-online-public-goods.pdf)

2. Complete solution of the fixed-sequence offline problem

Assume now that the entire rational value matrix \( (v_{\theta,t}) \) is part of the input and that \( B>0 \). Zero-mass types are discarded; this avoids the otherwise undefined product \( 0\cdot+\infty \).

Define

\[ \Theta_+ = \{\theta:\mu_\theta>0,\ V_\theta>0\}, \qquad \Theta_0 = \{\theta:\mu_\theta>0,\ V_\theta=0\}, \]

and

\[ a_\theta=\frac{\mu_\theta}{c_\theta}, \qquad C_0=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_0}\mu_\theta, \qquad A=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+}a_\theta. \]

Theorem

For a fixed revealed sequence and \( B>0 \),

\[ \min_{x\in P_B} \sup_{w\in P_B} \sum_{\theta\in\Theta} \mu_\theta \frac{u_\theta(w)} {c_\theta u_\theta(x)} = C_0+A. \]

Moreover, any maximizer of

\[ \Phi(x)= \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+} a_\theta\log u_\theta(x) \]

over \( P_B \) is optimal.

Proof

First, a feasible allocation giving every active type positive utility exists. Indeed, for

\[ \delta=\min\left\{\frac{B}{2T},\frac12\right\}>0, \]

the allocation \( x_t=\delta \) is feasible and satisfies \( u_\theta(x)=\delta V_\theta>0 \) for every \( \theta\in\Theta_+ \).

If some \( \theta\in\Theta_+ \) has \( u_\theta(x)=0 \), choose a round \( t \) with \( v_{\theta,t}>0 \) and let \( w_t=\min\{1,B\}>0 \), with every other \( w_s=0 \). Then \( u_\theta(w)>0 \), so the objective at \( x \) is \( +\infty \). It therefore suffices to consider allocations giving all active types positive utility.

The feasible region is compact. Since \( \Phi \) tends to \( -\infty \) whenever an active utility tends to zero, \( \Phi \) has a maximizer \( x^* \) with \( u_\theta(x^*)>0 \) for every active type.

For any \( w\in P_B \), consider the feasible segment \( x^*+\lambda(w-x^*) \). Optimality of \( x^* \) gives the one-sided derivative inequality

\[ 0 \ge \left.\frac{d}{d\lambda} \Phi(x^*+\lambda(w-x^*)) \right|_{\lambda=0^+} = \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+} a_\theta \frac{u_\theta(w)-u_\theta(x^*)} {u_\theta(x^*)}. \]

Rearranging,

\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+} a_\theta \frac{u_\theta(w)} {u_\theta(x^*)} \le A. \]

Every type in \( \Theta_0 \) has \( u_\theta(w)=u_\theta(x^*)=0 \), so its ratio is \( 1 \) by convention. Consequently, the full objective at \( x^* \) is at most \( C_0+A \).

Conversely, for any finite-loss allocation \( x \), choosing \( w=x \) yields ratio \( 1/c_\theta \) for every active type and ratio \( 1 \) for every zero-value type. Hence its loss is at least \( C_0+A \). Equality follows.

If \( B=0 \), both \( x \) and \( w \) must be zero, so every ratio is \( 1 \) and the objective equals \( \sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1 \).

3. Polynomial-time weak optimization

For any \( x \) with positive active utilities, define

\[ q_t(x)= \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+} \frac{a_\theta v_{\theta,t}}{u_\theta(x)}. \]

Then the active part of the comparator problem is simply

\[ \max_{w\in P_B}\sum_t q_t(x)w_t. \]

It is evaluated by sorting the \( q_t(x) \): invest fully in the largest coordinates and, if \( B \) is nonintegral, fractionally in the next one. Thus a given allocation can be evaluated in polynomial time.

More strongly, the entire offline minimization is a rational second-order cone program. Introduce variables \( y_\theta,\lambda,s_t\) and minimize

\[ C_0+B\lambda+\sum_t s_t \]

subject to

\[ 0\le x_t\le1, \qquad \sum_t x_t\le B, \qquad \lambda\ge0, \qquad s_t\ge0, \]

\[ \lambda+s_t \ge \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+} a_\theta v_{\theta,t}y_\theta, \]

and, for every active type,

\[ \left\| \left(2,\ y_\theta-u_\theta(x)\right) \right\|_2 \le y_\theta+u_\theta(x). \]

The last constraint is equivalent to \( y_\theta u_\theta(x)\ge1 \), because squaring it gives

\[ 4+\left(y_\theta-u_\theta(x)\right)^2 \le \left(y_\theta+u_\theta(x)\right)^2. \]

For fixed \( x \), monotonicity forces \( y_\theta=1/u_\theta(x) \) at optimum. The remaining variables \( \lambda,s \) are precisely the dual of the comparator linear program. Strong linear-programming duality therefore proves equivalence with the original offline objective.

Consequently, under the standard weak-optimization interpretation, an additive \( \varepsilon \)-optimal fixed-sequence allocation can be computed in time polynomial in the rational input length and \( \log(1/\varepsilon) \).

4. Exact optimal allocations can be irrational

Take \( T=2 \), \( B=1 \), three types of mass \( 1/3 \), and \( c_\theta=1 \). Let their value vectors be

\[ (1,0),\qquad (0,1),\qquad (2,1). \]

Write \( x=(x_1,x_2) \). For finite loss, \( x_1,x_2>0 \). Define

\[ q_1= \frac13 \left( \frac1{x_1} + \frac2{2x_1+x_2} \right), \qquad q_2= \frac13 \left( \frac1{x_2} + \frac1{2x_1+x_2} \right). \]

Since \( B=1 \), the loss is \( \max\{q_1,q_2\} \). Also,

\[ x_1q_1+x_2q_2=1. \]

The theorem shows that the optimal loss is \( 1 \). If \( \max\{q_1,q_2\}=1 \), then

\[ 1=x_1q_1+x_2q_2\le x_1+x_2\le1. \]

Hence \( x_1+x_2=1 \), and positivity of both coordinates implies \( q_1=q_2=1 \).

Writing \( x_1=z \) and \( x_2=1-z \), the equality \( q_1=q_2 \) becomes

\[ \frac1z+\frac1{1+z}-\frac1{1-z}=0. \]

After multiplication by the positive denominator \( z(1-z)(1+z) \), this is

\[ 1-3z^2=0. \]

Thus the unique feasible solution is

\[ x_1=\frac1{\sqrt3}, \qquad x_2=1-\frac1{\sqrt3}. \]

Both coordinates are irrational although all input data are rational. Therefore “find an exact optimal allocation” cannot mean output a rational vector. An algebraic-number or circuit representation would have to be specified before exact computational complexity can be classified.

5. Consequences for the target question

The existing lower bound transfers immediately to the weighted formulation by taking \( \Theta=[N] \), \( \mu_\theta=1/N \), and \( c_\theta=1 \). It proves a lower bound on the best worst-case fairness guarantee, but no reduction from an NP-hard language.

The rigorous classification currently supported is therefore:

  • The fixed-sequence weak optimization problem is polynomial-time solvable.
  • Exact fixed-sequence allocation requires a non-rational output model.
  • The genuine online-policy synthesis problem remains unclassified because its outer sequence criterion, policy representation, randomization semantics, and decision version have not been specified.

1. Underspecification of the online objective

This criticism is correct.

For a policy \(\pi\), each completed sequence \(\mathbf v\) produces a different allocation \(x^\pi(\mathbf v)\) and hence a different loss \(L_{\mathbf v}(\pi)\). Without a supremum, expectation, or other aggregation over \(\mathbf v\), “minimizing” this family of losses has no defined meaning.

The output also correctly observes that a computational classification would require:

  • a finite encoding of admissible inputs and policies;
  • deterministic or randomized policy semantics;
  • an interpretation of randomized loss;
  • and a threshold decision or approximation problem.

These omissions genuinely prevent a polynomial-time versus NP-hard classification.

2. Pointwise-optimality counterexample

The counterexample is correct.

From \(\widehat V\in[V/d,cV]\), the equivalent constraints are \(\widehat V/c\le V\le d\widehat V\). With \(\widehat V=1\), \(c=1\), and \(d=M+1\), both totals \(1\) and \(M+1\) are admissible.

For the sequence \((1,0)\), the loss is \(1/a\), so loss \(1\) requires \(a=1\).

For \((1,M)\), the policy utility is at most \(a+M(1-a)\), while the comparator utility is \(M\). The resulting lower bound

\[ \frac{M}{M-(M-1)a} \]

equals \(1\) exactly when \(a=0\). Therefore a deterministic policy cannot be offline-optimal on both sequences sharing the same first-round history.

This supports the need for an outer aggregation criterion. It does not itself supply such a criterion or classify the resulting optimization problem.

3. Fixed-sequence theorem

Subject to the explicit removal of zero-mass types, the theorem is correct.

Positive-utility feasibility

For \(B>0\) and \(T\ge1\), the proposed constant allocation \(x_t=\delta\), where \(\delta=\min\{B/(2T),1/2\}\), is feasible and gives every type with \(V_\theta>0\) positive utility.

Infinite loss at zero active utility

If \(V_\theta>0\) but \(u_\theta(x)=0\), some coordinate has \(v_{\theta,t}>0\). Because \(B>0\), a comparator can allocate a positive amount there, producing a positive numerator and zero denominator. Thus the supremum is infinite.

Logarithmic maximizer

The compactness argument is adequate: a finite-valued feasible point was constructed, utilities are bounded above on \(P_B\), and any approach to a boundary where an active utility vanishes sends the positively weighted log objective to \(-\infty\).

Directional derivative

For every feasible \(w\), the segment between \(x^*\) and \(w\) is feasible. The one-sided derivative inequality at a maximizer therefore gives

\[ \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+}a_\theta \frac{u_\theta(w)}{u_\theta(x^*)}\le \sum_{\theta\in\Theta_+}a_\theta. \]

After reinstating the \(c_\theta\) factors through \(a_\theta=\mu_\theta/c_\theta\), this is exactly the required active-type upper bound.

Matching lower bound

For every finite-loss \(x\), choosing \(w=x\) contributes \(\mu_\theta/c_\theta\) from each active type and \(\mu_\theta\) from each zero-value type. Hence the lower bound \(C_0+A\) is correct.

The \(B=0\) case is also correct under the stated \(0/0=1\) convention.

Zero-mass caveat

Discarding zero-mass types is a new convention rather than a consequence of the original definition. Literally, a zero mass multiplying an infinite ratio can produce the undefined expression \(0\cdot+\infty\). The conditional theorem is sound, but it must be labeled as applying after defining zero-mass terms to be omitted.

4. SOCP formulation and complexity claim

The comparator linear program and its dual are correctly represented. The rotated-cone constraint is also correct: together with the cone’s implicit nonnegative right-hand side, it enforces \(y_\theta u_\theta(x)\ge1\).

There are nevertheless three issues.

First, the statement that monotonicity “forces” \(y_\theta=1/u_\theta(x)\) is false. For example, take \(B=1\), \(x=(1/10,9/10)\), masses \(9/10\) and \(1/10\), \(c_1=c_2=1\), and value vectors \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). At the componentwise-minimal values, the comparator coefficients are \((9,1/9)\). Increasing the second \(y\)-variable to \(10\) raises the second coefficient only to \(1\), leaving the comparator optimum equal to \(9\). Thus equality is not forced at every optimum. This overstatement does not destroy equality of optimal values, but it is literally incorrect.

Second, the sorting description misses the case \(B\ge T\). If \(B\) is nonintegral but exceeds the number of coordinates, there is no “next” coordinate to receive a fractional allocation. The comparator simply selects every coordinate fully.

Third, the polynomial-time conclusion is not rigorously established. The output assumes only that the value matrix is rational, while rationality and encodings of \(B\) and \(c_\theta\) are also needed for the displayed program to have rational data. Moreover, no precise polynomial-time SOCP theorem is stated, and its feasibility, bit-complexity, approximation, and solution-extraction hypotheses are not verified. Under the verifier’s required standard, this is a major gap in the auxiliary complexity claim.

5. Irrational optimal allocation

This example is correct.

The formulas for \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are correct, as is the identity

\[ x_1q_1+x_2q_2=1. \]

At optimal loss \(1\), positivity and the budget constraint force \(x_1+x_2=1\) and \(q_1=q_2=1\). Substituting \(x_1=z\) gives

\[ \frac1z+\frac1{1+z}-\frac1{1-z}=0, \]

whose numerator is \(1-3z^2\). The unique solution in \(0<z<1\) is \(z=1/\sqrt3\). Hence no rational vector can represent the unique optimum.

This establishes only that rational-coordinate output is insufficient; it does not select a particular exact representation model.

6. Cited online lower bound

The cited paper defines proportional fairness using the normalized expression \((1/N)\sum_i u_i(w)/u_i(x)\), defines an online algorithm’s guarantee uniformly over arrivals, and states the binary-value, unit-budget \(\Omega(\log N)\) lower bound even with exact total-value information. Thus the normalization \(\mu_\theta=1/N\), \(c_\theta=1\) is consistent with that result. [Banerjee et al., *Proportionally Fair Online Allocation of Public Goods with Predictions*](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~emicha/papers/fair-online-public-goods.pdf)

As the prover correctly says, this transfers an online performance lower bound, not an NP-hardness result.
