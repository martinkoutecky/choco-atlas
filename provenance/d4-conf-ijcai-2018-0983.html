The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite package set \(O\), two binary-rational distributions \(\mu^1,\mu^2\) over complete strict rankings of \(O\), and a valid current VAOV history, suppose each distribution induces a strict transitive majority ranking. What move does the Theorem 10 SPE prescribe at that history, and what agreement outcome follows if both representatives continue using its strategies?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(O=\{o_1,\ldots,o_m\}\), with \(m\geq2\). Each of two societies is given by its support: a list of \(\tau_k\) complete strict rankings with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Write \(\tau=\tau_1+\tau_2\) for total support size, not the number of possible rankings, and \(L\) for total input bit length.
Representative \(p_k\) prefers \(x\) to \(y\) exactly when the \(\mu^k\)-mass ranking \(x\) above \(y\) exceeds \(1/2\). These majority relations are promised to be complete, strict and transitive; both representatives know both relations and use them as their preferences.
In VAOV, \(p_1\) offers first; an offer is a single available package. Acceptance ends negotiation; rejection permanently removes that package and switches the proposer. The sole remaining package is accepted automatically. Both representatives prefer every agreement to disagreement; lottery offers are forbidden.
The input history consists of distinct rejected offers \(H\), optionally followed by a pending offer \(x\in S=O\setminus H\); no agreement has occurred. The current proposer is determined by the parity of \(|H|\).
At any state with proposer \(p_i\), responder \(p_j\), and \(n=|S|\geq2\), let \(B_j\) be the responder's bottom \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) packages in \(S\). Let \(B_i\) be the proposer's bottom \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) packages when \(n\) is odd, and bottom \(n/2-1\) when \(n\) is even.
The prescribed offering strategy selects the smallest-index package in \(B_i\cap B_j\) when that intersection is nonempty, and otherwise the responder's least preferred available package.
For a pending offer \(x\), define \(a\) as the sole survivor after rejecting \(x\) and then repeatedly using the prescribed offering strategy and rejecting every subsequent offer until one package remains. The prescribed response accepts exactly when \(x\succeq_j a\), where \(\succeq_j\) includes equality. At a singleton state, offer or accept its package.

Can the prescribed current move and the agreement reached when both representatives subsequently follow these strategies be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computation NP-hard?

For two explicitly given strict preference orders, these strategies form an SPE, and its move at any history is computable in \(O(m)\) time; the equilibrium agreement is unique ([2018 result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00913)).
The [2024 full version](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~krauss/data/articles/AIJ-2024.pdf) retains the discrete result. No result specifically treating the distribution-input problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each distribution to total mass one and defined majority by mass strictly exceeding one half; the strict-ranking promise excludes pairwise ties and cycles.
  • Made each representative's preferences exactly its society's majority ranking, with full information about both rankings.
  • Resolved the offering strategy's arbitrary choice from a nonempty intersection by choosing the smallest package index, consistently at every history.
  • Included both offering and response histories, including histories outside the prescribed play, and determined the proposer from the rejected-offer count.
  • Restored the alternating roles, permanent removal after rejection, prohibition of lottery offers, preference for agreement over disagreement, and compulsory singleton agreement.
  • Used an initial domain with at least two packages and explicitly defined the singleton continuation.
  • Defined the support parameter as the sum of the two society support sizes and included the entire input in the bit-length parameter.

Result

The prescribed current move and the subsequent agreement are computable in deterministic polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof has two parts: recover the representatives’ orders by exact majority calculations, then compute the negotiation using a single sequence of hypothetical rejections.

1. Recovering the majority orders exactly

For society \(k\in\{1,2\}\), write its support as rankings \(R_{k1},\ldots,R_{k\tau_k}\), with masses \(w_{k\ell}=u_{k\ell}/v_{k\ell}\). Numerators and denominators are positive integers encoded in binary; finite binary fractions are included in this representation.

Precompute the position of every package in every supported ranking. Then whether \(R_{k\ell}\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\) can be determined by comparing their positions.

Define

\(D_k=\prod_{\ell=1}^{\tau_k}v_{k\ell}\), and \(W_{k\ell}=u_{k\ell}(D_k/v_{k\ell})\).

These are integers. Because the masses sum to one,

\(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tau_k}W_{k\ell}=D_k\).

For every distinct pair \(x,y\), compute

\(A_k(x,y)=\sum_{\ell:\,R_{k\ell}\text{ ranks }x\text{ above }y}W_{k\ell}\).

The representative’s preference is therefore determined exactly by

\(x\succ_k y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2A_k(x,y)>D_k\).

There is no numerical approximation in this comparison.

Bit complexity. The binary length of \(D_k\) is at most one plus the sum of the binary lengths of the denominators, hence is \(O(L)\). Positivity and normalization imply that every \(W_{k\ell}\) and every \(A_k(x,y)\) lies between zero and \(D_k\), so these integers also have \(O(L)\) bits. All pairwise comparisons require \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic and table operations. Exact integer addition, multiplication, division, and comparison on these lengths take polynomial time.

By the promise, the resulting relation is a strict total order. To turn it into an explicit ordered list, define

\(r_k(x)=1+\bigl|\{y\in O:y\succ_k x\}\bigr|\).

These ranks faithfully represent preference. Indeed, if \(x\succ_k y\), transitivity implies that every package preferred to \(x\) is preferred to \(y\), and \(x\) itself is an additional package preferred to \(y\). Thus \(r_k(x)<r_k(y)\). Completeness makes all ranks distinct; because they lie in \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\), they fill that set exactly.

Consequently,

\(x\succeq_k y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_k(x)\leq r_k(y)\).

Restricting these lists to any available set gives precisely the representatives’ preferences on that set.

2. Computing an offer

Let \(F(T,i)\) denote the prescribed offer when the available set is \(T\) and the proposer is \(p_i\).

For \(|T|\geq2\), scan the two preference lists from worst to best, skipping unavailable packages. This identifies both bottom sets with exactly the cardinalities specified in the question. Mark their members, then scan package indices in increasing order:

  • If their intersection is nonempty, return its smallest-index member.
  • Otherwise, return the responder’s least preferred member of \(T\).

For a singleton, return its sole member.

Each invocation uses \(O(m)\) elementary table operations. In particular, the even case with two available packages correctly gives the proposer an empty bottom set.

The function \(F\) is deterministic and depends only on the available set and proposer. This observation also handles arbitrary histories: previously rejected offers affect future offering decisions through their removal and the proposer’s parity.

3. A single rejection sequence determines all responses

Let \(S=O\setminus H\), let \(n=|S|\), and let \(i_0\) be the current proposer, determined by the parity of \(|H|\). Assume first that \(n\geq2\).

Construct a hypothetical sequence \(q_0,\ldots,q_{n-1}\) as follows. Set \(T_0=S\).

  • If there is a pending offer \(x\), set \(q_0=x\). Otherwise, set \(q_0=F(T_0,i_0)\).
  • For each \(t<n-1\), hypothetically reject \(q_t\), set \(T_{t+1}=T_t\setminus\{q_t\}\), and switch the proposer to \(i_{t+1}=3-i_t\).
  • For \(1\leq t<n-1\), set \(q_t=F(T_t,i_t)\).
  • Let \(q_{n-1}\) be the sole member of \(T_{n-1}\).

Write \(z=q_{n-1}\). The sequence is well defined because each rejection removes one available package.

Lemma. At every nonfinal offer \(q_t\) along this hypothetical sequence, the package \(a\) appearing in the prescribed response rule is \(z\).

Proof. Rejecting \(q_t\) leaves exactly the state \((T_{t+1},i_{t+1})\). The definition of \(a\) then uses the prescribed offering function and rejects every subsequent offer. Since \(F\) is deterministic and depends only on the current available set and proposer, this continuation is exactly the already constructed suffix \(q_{t+1},\ldots,q_{n-1}\). Its sole survivor is \(z\). This also applies at \(t=0\) when the pending offer was arbitrary. ∎

Let \(j_t=3-i_t\) be the responder at position \(t\). By the lemma, the response to each nonfinal offer is acceptance exactly when

\(r_{j_t}(q_t)\leq r_{j_t}(z)\).

4. Extracting the current move and agreement

Scan the constructed sequence from its beginning. Let \(t^\ast\) be the first position satisfying

\(t^\ast<n-1\quad\text{and}\quad r_{j_{t^\ast}}(q_{t^\ast})\leq r_{j_{t^\ast}}(z)\).

If no such position exists, set \(t^\ast=n-1\). Return \(q_{t^\ast}\) as the agreement.

Correctness. Actual play starts with \(q_0\), either because it is pending or because it is the prescribed offer. At every position before \(t^\ast\), the response inequality fails, so the prescribed response rejects. Removal and alternation then produce exactly the next constructed state, whose prescribed offer is the next sequence member. This proves inductively that actual play follows the sequence until \(t^\ast\). There it accepts by the response inequality, or accepts automatically if only one package remains.

The current move is obtained simultaneously:

  • Without a pending offer, offer \(q_0\).
  • With a pending offer \(x\), accept exactly when \(r_{j_0}(x)\leq r_{j_0}(z)\); otherwise reject.

If \(S\) is a singleton, its package is immediately offered or accepted, according to whether an offer is pending, and is the agreement.

5. Complexity and conclusion

Constructing the hypothetical sequence requires at most \(m-1\) calls to \(F\), hence \(O(m^2)\) elementary table operations. Checking the response inequalities requires only \(O(m)\) additional operations.

Together with exact majority preprocessing, the whole computation is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). A conservative bound, charging schoolbook arithmetic and index operations at \(O((L+1)^2)\) each, is

\(O\bigl((\tau+1)m^2(L+1)^2\bigr)\).

The crucial input feature is that \(\tau\) counts explicitly listed support rankings: each majority comparison sums over that list.

For the equilibrium interpretation, the recovered orders satisfy completeness, strictness, and transitivity; both representatives know them; and the protocol matches the explicit-order setting. The specified index choice selects a permitted intersection member. Thus the cited SPE result applies as well. [Erlich, Hazon, and Kraus, Strategies 1–2 and Theorem 10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.00913). The algorithm above directly proves the requested computational conclusion.
