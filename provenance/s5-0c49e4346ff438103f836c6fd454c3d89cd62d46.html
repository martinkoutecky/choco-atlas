Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G\) and \(C\) be nonempty finite sets, with \(m=|G|\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. All input rationals have total bit length \(L\).
Each type has \(A_t\in\mathbb Q^{|C|\times m}\), nonnegative rational requirements \(r_t\) and delays \(d_t\), and a positive rational per-request budget \(b_t\); supplies \(q_j\) are positive rationals. Entries of \(A_t\) may have either sign.
An equilibrium consists of exact prices \(p\ge0\) and per-request bundles \(x_t\ge0\), where each \(x_t\) minimizes \(d_t\cdot x\) subject to \(A_tx\ge r_t\), \(p\cdot x\le b_t\), and \(x\ge0\). Any minimizing bundle is allowed.
Require \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}\le q_j\) for every good, with equality whenever \(p_j>0\).
Define the scaled representative market to have one agent per type, bundle \(z_t\), constraints \(A_tz_t\ge\mu_tr_t\), delay \(d_t\cdot z_t\), budget \(\mu_tb_t\), and supplies \(q_j\). Promise that a supply-respecting allocation satisfying every agent's constraints exists.
For \(S\subseteq T\), an allocation is jointly optimal for \(S\) if it minimizes \(\sum_{t\in S}d_t\cdot z_t\) subject to these agents' constraints, nonnegativity, and \(\sum_{t\in S}z_{tj}\le q_j\), without budget constraints.
Promise extensibility: for every \(S\subsetneq T\), every jointly optimal allocation for \(S\), and every \(u\notin S\), there exists a jointly optimal allocation for \(S\cup\{u\}\) preserving each agent's delay in \(S\).
Promise sufficient demand: for every type \(t\), every minimizer of \(d_t\cdot z\) subject only to \(A_tz\ge\mu_tr_t\) and \(z\ge0\) has \(z_j>q_j\) for some good \(j\).

Can an exact equilibrium for this promised distribution problem be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|C|,L\), or is this search problem NP-hard?

Finite-agent markets satisfying extensibility and sufficient demand admit a polynomial-time exact equilibrium algorithm. [Devanur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1511.08748v2)

Exact equilibrium computation is polynomial-time

The promised problem admits an exact equilibrium algorithm running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|C|,L\). The proof is a reduction to the finite-agent theorem, with one agent per supported type.

1. The finite-agent result used

We use the following established result of Devanur et al.:

Finite-agent equilibrium theorem. For a rational finite-agent market with unit supplies, nonnegative requirements and delays, positive budgets, and constraints of the form \(By\ge s,\ y\ge0\), extensibility and sufficient demand imply that an exact equilibrium can be computed in time polynomial in the market’s bit description.

The paper’s Section 2 explicitly permits arbitrary signs in the constraint matrices. Its Theorem 6 establishes polynomial time in the bit description, so the guarantee applies to exact computation with binary-encoded rational data. [Devanur et al., Section 2 and Theorem 6](https://arxiv.org/html/1511.08748v2)

We now verify the reduction and every hypothesis needed for this application.

2. Equivalence with the scaled representative market

Fix any nonnegative price vector \(p\). For type \(t\), define its affordable feasible set by

\( F_t(p)=\{x\ge0:A_tx\ge r_t,\ p\cdot x\le b_t\}. \)

The corresponding representative agent has affordable feasible set

\( H_t(p)=\{z\ge0:A_tz\ge\mu_tr_t,\ p\cdot z\le\mu_tb_t\}. \)

Because \(\mu_t>0\), the map \(x\mapsto z=\mu_tx\) is a bijection between these sets. Indeed,

\( A_t(\mu_tx)=\mu_tA_tx,\qquad p\cdot(\mu_tx)=\mu_t(p\cdot x). \)

Multiplication or division by \(\mu_t\) preserves each inequality. This argument does not require the entries of \(A_t\) to be nonnegative.

The objectives satisfy

\( d_t\cdot z=\mu_t(d_t\cdot x). \)

Consequently, \(x\) minimizes the original objective over \(F_t(p)\) if and only if \(\mu_tx\) minimizes the representative objective over \(H_t(p)\). To justify this directly, any strictly better feasible representative bundle could be divided by \(\mu_t\) to obtain a strictly better original bundle, and conversely. The equivalence holds for all minimizers, including ties.

For an allocation related by \(z_t=\mu_tx_t\), aggregate consumption satisfies

\( \sum_{t\in T}z_{tj}=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tx_{tj}. \)

Thus supply feasibility and the requirement of equality whenever \(p_j>0\) are identical in the two formulations.

Proved equivalence. Prices \(p\) and bundles \((x_t)_{t\in T}\) form a distribution equilibrium if and only if prices \(p\) and bundles \((\mu_tx_t)_{t\in T}\) form a representative-market equilibrium.

3. Normalize the supplies

Let \(Q\) be the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries \(q_j\). Every \(q_j\) is positive, so \(Q\) is invertible.

Construct a finite-agent market with agent set \(T\), unit supplies, and data

\( \widehat A_t=A_tQ,\qquad \widehat r_t=\mu_tr_t,\qquad \widehat d_{tj}=q_jd_{tj},\qquad \widehat b_t=\mu_tb_t. \)

Relate normalized bundles and prices to representative bundles and prices by

\( z_t=Qy_t,\qquad \pi=Qp. \)

The representative constraints become exactly the normalized constraints:

\( A_tz_t\ge\mu_tr_t \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \widehat A_ty_t\ge\widehat r_t. \)

Objectives and expenditure are preserved:

\( \widehat d_t\cdot y_t=d_t\cdot z_t,\qquad \pi\cdot y_t=p\cdot z_t. \)

Supply feasibility transforms as

\( \sum_tz_{tj}\le q_j \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_ty_{tj}\le1. \)

Finally, \(\pi_j=q_jp_j\), so \(\pi_j>0\) if and only if \(p_j>0\). Hence the positive-price clearing conditions correspond exactly.

It follows that representative-market equilibria and normalized-market equilibria are in bijection.

4. Verification of the promises

Feasibility and numerical hypotheses

The promised supply-respecting feasible representative allocation becomes a feasible normalized allocation through \(y_t=Q^{-1}z_t\).

All normalized data are rational. Requirements and delays remain nonnegative, and budgets remain positive. The matrices may have signed entries, which the cited theorem allows.

For every subset \(S\subseteq T\), restricting the full feasible allocation to \(S\) proves feasibility for that subset. Its normalized supply-respecting feasible set is closed and bounded, since every coordinate lies between \(0\) and \(1\). Therefore a jointly optimal allocation exists for every subset.

Extensibility

Fix \(S\subsetneq T\), any normalized allocation jointly optimal for \(S\), and \(u\notin S\).

The transformation \(z_t=Qy_t\) is a bijection between the supply-respecting feasible allocations for \(S\) in the two markets. It preserves each agent’s delay, and therefore preserves their sum. Thus the transformed allocation is jointly optimal in the representative market.

By promised extensibility, there exists a representative allocation jointly optimal for \(S\cup\{u\}\) preserving every delay in \(S\). Transforming it back by \(y_t=Q^{-1}z_t\) gives a normalized jointly optimal allocation with the same preservation property.

This verifies extensibility with its full universal quantification over jointly optimal allocations.

Sufficient demand

For each agent \(t\), the transformation \(z=Qy\) also gives a bijection between the feasible sets without supply or budget constraints, preserving the objective. Hence their minimizers correspond.

These minimizers exist: the feasible set is nonempty, and its linear objective is bounded below by \(0\), so the linear program attains its finite optimum.

By the sufficient-demand promise, every representative minimizer has some coordinate satisfying \(z_j>q_j\). Its corresponding normalized minimizer satisfies

\( y_j=z_j/q_j>1. \)

Thus every normalized minimizer exceeds the unit supply of some good. This verifies sufficient demand.

All hypotheses of the finite-agent theorem are satisfied.

5. Algorithm and bit complexity

The algorithm is:

  • Construct the normalized market from Section 3.
  • Apply the finite-agent exact equilibrium algorithm to obtain \(\pi\) and \((y_t)_{t\in T}\).
  • Return

\( p_j=\pi_j/q_j,\qquad x_{tj}=q_jy_{tj}/\mu_t. \)

Sections 2–4 prove that the returned prices and bundles constitute an exact distribution equilibrium.

It remains to check the encoding bound. The normalized market has \(\tau\) agents, \(m\) goods, and \(|C|\) constraints per agent. Its number of rational entries is

\( O\bigl(\tau(m|C|+m+|C|+1)+m\bigr). \)

Every new input coefficient is a product of at most two original rationals. If two rationals are represented by integer numerators and denominators, their product can be represented by multiplying the numerators and denominators; the resulting bit lengths are at most the sums of the original bit lengths, up to a constant. Therefore each transformed entry has bit length \(O(L)\), and the entire normalized market has description length polynomial in \(m,\tau,|C|,L\).

The cited theorem consequently runs in polynomial time on this market. Its exact rational output has polynomial length, and the divisions and multiplications in Step 3 also take polynomial time and produce polynomial-length rational output.

In particular, a small mass \(\mu_t\) does not require creating many agents: its reciprocal has bit length comparable to that of \(\mu_t\). The construction always uses exactly \(\tau\) agents.

6. Conclusion

The support-distribution formulation reduces exactly, with polynomial encoding overhead, to a finite-agent market satisfying extensibility and sufficient demand. Thus the promised search problem is polynomial-time solvable.
