The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\), rational type masses \(\mu\), rational per-capita supplies \(\rho\), and a finite type set \(T\) with \(\Gamma_t\subseteq[m]\) and nondecreasing concave \(f_t:\{0,\ldots,m\}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), choose \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) for \(t\in T\) and \(B\subseteq[m]\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:\,j\in B}x_{t,B}=\rho_j\), and compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation maximizing \(\exp(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\log f_t(|B\cap\Gamma_t|))\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions

Input consists of \(m\), a finite set \(T\), rational \(\mu_t,\rho_j\geq0\), sets \(\Gamma_t\subseteq[m]\), and table-encoded nondecreasing concave functions \(f_t:\{0,\ldots,m\}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\). A continuous allocation is \(x_{t,B}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:\,j\in B}x_{t,B}=\rho_j\).
Its Nash welfare is \(U(x)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\log f_t(|B\cap\Gamma_t|)\right)\), with \(U(x)=0\) if a positive-mass term has value \(0\). An \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation has \(U(x)\geq e^{-\varepsilon}\max_yU(y)\).

Target

Is computing an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal continuous allocation solvable in polynomial time (in the input size and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)), or is it NP-hard?

Nearest known results

For the discrete problem in which each indivisible good is assigned to one agent and agent \(i\)'s valuation is \(f_i(|S\cap\Gamma_i|)\), with \(f_i\) nondecreasing and concave, a Nash-optimal allocation is computable in polynomial time.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

For every positive rational \(\varepsilon\), the problem is polynomial-time solvable in the input size and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). A polynomial-size rational allocation can be returned explicitly. The key is a polynomial-size linear formulation that records the mass assigned to each possible cardinality and its item marginals.

1. A polynomial-size formulation.

Write \(J_t=\Gamma_t\), \(n_t=|J_t|\), and \(M=\sum_t\mu_t\). For each \(t\) and \(k\in\{0,\ldots,n_t\}\), introduce:

  • \(z_{t,k}\): the mass of bundles containing exactly \(k\) items of \(J_t\);
  • \(y_{t,k,j}\), for \(j\in J_t\): the mass of those bundles containing \(j\).

For \(j\notin J_t\), introduce \(a_{t,j}\), the total mass of type \(t\) containing \(j\).

Consider the rational polytope \(P\) defined by
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n_t}z_{t,k}=\mu_t,\qquad z_{t,k}\geq0, \]
\[ 0\leq y_{t,k,j}\leq z_{t,k}, \qquad \sum_{j\in J_t}y_{t,k,j}=kz_{t,k}, \]
\[ 0\leq a_{t,j}\leq\mu_t, \]
and, for every \(j\in[m]\),
\[ \sum_{t:\,j\in J_t}\sum_{k=0}^{n_t}y_{t,k,j} + \sum_{t:\,j\notin J_t}a_{t,j} =\rho_j. \]

This formulation has \(O(|T|(m+1)^2)\) variables and constraints. Its rational coefficients have polynomial encoding length.

Every feasible allocation induces a point of \(P\): set
\[ z_{t,k}=\sum_{B:\,|B\cap J_t|=k}x_{t,B}, \]
\[ y_{t,k,j} =\sum_{B:\,|B\cap J_t|=k,\ j\in B}x_{t,B}, \qquad a_{t,j}=\sum_{B:\,j\in B}x_{t,B}. \]
The displayed constraints follow directly from the allocation constraints and counting the \(k\) relevant items in each bundle.

We next prove the converse constructively.

2. Every rational point of \(P\) has a polynomial-size allocation representation.

First fix a finite set \(J\), an integer \(k\in\{0,\ldots,|J|\}\), and rational numbers satisfying
\[ z>0,\qquad 0\leq y_j\leq z,\qquad \sum_{j\in J}y_j=kz. \]
We claim that these are the marginals of a rational distribution of total mass \(z\) on at most \(|J|+1\) subsets of size \(k\).

Choose a set \(S\subseteq J\) of size \(k\) containing every coordinate with \(y_j=z\), and containing only coordinates with \(y_j>0\). Such a choice exists: the sum condition implies that at most \(k\) coordinates equal \(z\), and at least \(k\) coordinates are positive.

Set
\[ \delta= \min\left( \{z\} \cup\{y_j:j\in S\} \cup\{z-y_j:j\notin S\} \right). \]
Every number in this minimum is positive. Assign mass \(\delta\) to \(S\), and replace
\[ z\leftarrow z-\delta,\qquad y_j\leftarrow y_j-\delta\,\mathbf 1_{\{j\in S\}}. \]

The residual quantities satisfy the same constraints. Indeed, selected coordinates remain nonnegative and satisfy
\[ y_j-\delta\leq z-\delta; \]
unselected coordinates satisfy \(y_j\leq z-\delta\) by the definition of \(\delta\). The sum decreases by \(k\delta\).

If the residual mass is positive, at least one previously strict coordinate reaches either zero or the new upper bound. Coordinates already at a bound remain at a bound: a zero coordinate is unselected, and a coordinate equal to \(z\) is selected. Consequently, after at most \(|J|\) such steps, all coordinates are at bounds, and one final step removes the remaining mass. This proves the claim.

Apply this construction separately to every positive \(z_{t,k}\). For each type \(t\), it produces a list of relevant-item subsets, with rational masses summing to \(\mu_t\), realizing all the prescribed \(y_{t,k,j}\).

It remains to incorporate items outside \(J_t\). Represent the list as consecutive half-open intervals partitioning \([0,\mu_t)\), each interval labeled by its relevant-item subset. For every \(j\notin J_t\), include \(j\) precisely on \([0,a_{t,j})\). Cut the partition at every endpoint \(a_{t,j}\). Each resulting interval now specifies one complete bundle; give that bundle mass equal to the interval’s length.

This realizes every required marginal. It also preserves the masses \(z_{t,k}\), because adding items outside \(J_t\) does not change the relevant cardinality.

For each type, the initial list has at most \((n_t+1)^2\) intervals, and adding outside items introduces at most \(m-n_t\) cuts. Thus the allocation has polynomial support.

The construction also has polynomial bit complexity. Its operations are comparisons, additions, and subtractions of rational numbers. A common denominator for the polynomially many input coordinates has polynomial bit length and remains a common denominator throughout.

Therefore \(P\) is an exact formulation, and rational points of \(P\) can be converted into explicit rational allocations in polynomial time.

3. Feasibility and zero welfare.

Rational linear programming decides feasibility of \(P\) in polynomial time and, when feasible, returns a rational feasible point of polynomial encoding length. If \(P\) is infeasible, the original instance is infeasible.

Now define \(P^+\) by adding
\[ z_{t,k}=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad f_t(k)=0. \]

A feasible allocation has positive welfare if and only if it induces a point of \(P^+\):

  • Positive welfare means no positive mass is assigned to a bundle with value zero.
  • Conversely, the construction in Section 2 applied to a point of \(P^+\) uses only bundles with positive value whenever their mass is positive.

Hence, if \(P\) is feasible but \(P^+\) is infeasible, every feasible allocation has welfare zero. Any allocation constructed from a rational feasible point of \(P\) is then optimal.

If \(M=0\), feasibility requires every \(\rho_j=0\). The zero allocation is optimal and has welfare \(1\), since its logarithmic welfare is the empty sum.

It therefore remains to treat \(M>0\) and nonempty \(P^+\).

4. Approximating the logarithmic objective.

On \(P^+\), define
\[ L(z)= \sum_{t,k:\,f_t(k)>0}z_{t,k}\log f_t(k). \]
The formulation and reconstruction preserve this objective exactly. Thus maximizing welfare is equivalent to maximizing \(L\) on \(P^+\).

All variables of \(P^+\) are bounded, so \(P^+\) is compact. The objective \(L\) is a finite linear function there; consequently, a maximizer \(z^*\) exists.

Put
\[ \bar\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon,1\}, \qquad \eta=\frac{\bar\varepsilon}{4\max\{1,M\}}. \]
For every positive table entry used in the objective, compute a rational approximation \(c_{t,k}\) satisfying
\[ |c_{t,k}-\log f_t(k)|\leq\eta. \]
Define the rational objective
\[ \widehat L(z)=\sum_{t,k:\,f_t(k)>0}c_{t,k}z_{t,k}. \]

These logarithm approximations can be computed in polynomial time. For completeness, write a positive rational \(q\) as \(q=2^h r\), with \(1\leq r<2\). The magnitude of \(h\) is bounded by the binary encoding length of \(q\). For \(w=(r-1)/(r+1)\), use
\[ \log r = 2\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}\frac{w^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1} +R_N. \]
Since \(0\leq w\leq1/3\),
\[ |R_N| \leq 2\sum_{\ell=N}^{\infty}(1/3)^{2\ell+1} \leq3^{-2N}. \]
The same formula approximates \(\log 2\), using \(w=1/3\). Combining the two approximations gives an approximation to
\[ \log q=h\log 2+\log r \]
with error at most \((|h|+1)3^{-2N}\). A polynomial number of terms therefore suffices, and the rational partial sums have polynomial encoding length.

Solve the rational linear program maximizing \(\widehat L\) on \(P^+\), and let \(\widehat z\) denote the resulting cardinality masses. For every feasible \(z\),
\[ |L(z)-\widehat L(z)| \leq \eta\sum_{t,k}z_{t,k} =\eta M. \]
Optimality for the approximate objective now gives
\[ L(\widehat z) \geq \widehat L(\widehat z)-\eta M \geq \widehat L(z^*)-\eta M \geq L(z^*)-2\eta M \geq L(z^*)-\varepsilon. \]

Reconstruct an allocation \(\widehat x\) from the complete rational LP solution. Because reconstruction preserves \(L\),
\[ U(\widehat x) =\exp(L(\widehat z)) \geq e^{-\varepsilon}\exp(L(z^*)) =e^{-\varepsilon}\max_x U(x). \]

5. Complexity and conclusion.

The algorithm uses polynomial-size rational linear programs, logarithm approximations of polynomial encoding length, and a polynomial-time reconstruction producing polynomially many nonzero bundle masses. Since
\[ \log(1/\eta) = O\!\left( 1+\log\max\{1,M\} +\log(1/\bar\varepsilon) \right), \]
its running time is polynomial in the input size and \(\max\{0,\log(1/\varepsilon)\}\).

The proof does not require monotonicity or concavity: the same result holds for arbitrary nonnegative rational table-encoded functions of the relevant-item cardinality.
