The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many agent types \(T\) with additive utilities \(u_t(r)\), rational type masses, an initial rational mass distribution over integral bundles, and conserved per-capita supplies of resource classes \(R\), can one compute a utilitarian-optimal final bundle distribution and a finite sequence of equal-mass one-copy gifts reaching it? Each gift moves resource class \(r\) from type \(t\) to type \(s\) only when \(u_s(r)>u_t(r)\), and interaction is complete.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize masses \(p_t\) by \(\sum_t p_t=1\).
There are \(m\geq1\) resource classes and \(\tau\geq1\) types, with additive utilities specified by signed binary rationals \(u_t(r)\). A bundle is \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{m}\); empty bundles and arbitrarily many copies are allowed, with no capacity constraints or payments.
The initial distribution is an explicit finite list of cells \((t,b,x^0_{t,b})\), with positive binary-rational masses and binary-encoded bundle coordinates, satisfying \(\sum_b x^0_{t,b}=p_t\). Define conserved per-capita supplies \(q_r=\sum_{t,b}x^0_{t,b}b_r\).
A feasible final distribution is a finite rational list \(x_{t,b}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=p_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_r=q_r\). Its utilitarian welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\sum_r b_r u_t(r)\).
Interaction is complete. A gift selects distinct types \(t,s\), current bundles \(b,c\), a class \(r\) with \(b_r\geq1\) and \(u_s(r)>u_t(r)\), and rational mass \(0<\alpha\leq\min\{x_{t,b},x_{s,c}\}\). It replaces mass \(\alpha\) in these cells by mass \(\alpha\) in \((t,b-e_r)\) and \((s,c+e_r)\), where \(e_r\) is the unit vector; cells may be split and identical cells merged.
Is computing a globally utilitarian-optimal feasible distribution together with an explicitly listed finite gift sequence reaching it solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this problem NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
For finite agents with additive utilities and complete interaction, strictly utilitarian-improving one-resource gifts converge to a global optimum ([Nongaillard and Mathieu, 2011](https://jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/14/3/5.html)). No result settling this exact mass-distribution problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total society mass to one, making the conserved supplies per-capita quantities.
  • Use signed rational utilities: the discrete utility definition permits real values without a positivity restriction; rational encoding makes the computational input finite.
  • Interpret resource classes and integral bundles as nonnegative integer copy-count vectors, allowing empty bundles and no capacity bounds. This implements the graded question's class-based analogue of uniquely identified discrete resources.
  • Specify the initial allocation as a joint distribution over types and bundles whose type marginals equal the supplied society masses.
  • Define an equal-mass gift by splitting donor and recipient cells and transferring exactly one copy per paired agent, with no payments or individual-rationality requirement.
  • Allow utility ties in the input but forbid gifts between equally valuing types, following the strict utilitarian gift criterion.
  • Use explicit finite rational output distributions and explicitly listed gift sequences; encode integer bundle coordinates in binary.
  • Interpret optimality globally over all feasible distributions with the same type masses and supplies.

Conclusion

The requested output cannot always be computed in polynomial time: even with one resource class and two supported types, every gift sequence reaching an optimum can require exponentially many entries.

This is an unconditional output-length obstruction, independent of whether \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). It does not establish \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. The proposed polynomial-time versus \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard dichotomy overlooks this obstruction.

The underlying optimization is nevertheless tractable: an optimal distribution of polynomial encoding length can be computed in polynomial time. Moreover, such a distribution can always be reached by a finite gift sequence that can be generated with polynomial work per listed gift.

1. Characterization of global optima

For each resource class \(r\), define its maximum utility and its maximizing types by

\( M_r=\max_{1\leq t\leq\tau}u_t(r), \qquad T_r=\{t:u_t(r)=M_r\}. \)

These sets are nonempty because the support is finite and nonempty.

For any feasible distribution \(x\), conservation of resources gives

\( \sum_r q_rM_r-W(x) = \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\sum_r b_r\bigl(M_r-u_t(r)\bigr). \)

Every summand on the right is nonnegative. Consequently,

\( W(x)\leq\sum_r q_rM_r. \)

Equality holds precisely when every positive-mass cell satisfies

\( b_r>0\ \Longrightarrow\ t\in T_r. \)

Thus, once attainability of this bound is proved, a distribution is globally optimal exactly when all copies of every resource class belong to maximizing types. This argument allows signed utilities: even resources with negative maximum utility must be allocated because their supplies are conserved.

There is also a useful local characterization. If a distribution fails this condition, some positive-mass cell \((t,b)\) has \(b_r>0\) and \(t\notin T_r\). Choose \(s\in T_r\). Since \(p_s>0\), there is a positive-mass cell \((s,c)\). The types are distinct, and a gift with

\( \alpha=\min\{x_{t,b},x_{s,c}\}>0 \)

is legal and increases welfare by

\( \alpha\bigl(M_r-u_t(r)\bigr)>0. \)

Conversely, a distribution satisfying the condition admits no legal gift: a type holding a resource already has maximum utility for it. Hence terminal distributions are exactly global optima, provided the bound is attainable. The construction below proves attainability.

2. Exponentially many gifts are sometimes necessary

The case \(\tau=1\) requires no gifts. The obstruction already appears with \(m=1\) and \(\tau=2\).

For an integer \(n\geq1\), put \(N=2^n\). Consider types \(\ell,h\) with

\( p_\ell=p_h=\tfrac12, \qquad u_\ell(1)=0, \qquad u_h(1)=1. \)

The initial distribution consists of the two cells

\( (\ell,N,\tfrac12), \qquad (h,0,\tfrac12). \)

Here bundles are written as scalar coordinates. The conserved supply is \(q_1=N/2\). A feasible distribution with cells

\( (\ell,0,\tfrac12), \qquad (h,N,\tfrac12) \)

has welfare \(N/2\), meeting the upper bound from Section 1. Therefore every optimum must place all resources with type \(h\).

Every legal gift goes from \(\ell\) to \(h\). Define the resource supply held by \(h\) at distribution \(x\) as

\( H(x)=\sum_b x_{h,b}b. \)

Initially \(H(x^0)=0\). A gift of mass \(\alpha\) increases \(H\) by exactly \(\alpha\), because it adds one copy to each member of the receiving mass. Furthermore,

\( \alpha\leq p_h=\tfrac12. \)

If a sequence of \(k\) gifts reaches any optimum, then

\( \tfrac N2=H(x^{\mathrm{final}}) =\sum_{j=1}^k\alpha_j \leq\tfrac k2. \)

Thus \(k\geq N\). This bound holds regardless of how cells are split or merged.

It is attained: for each integer \(j\) with \(0\leq j<N\), give mass \(1/2\) from bundle \(N-j\) of type \(\ell\) to bundle \(j\) of type \(h\). After exactly \(N\) gifts, the displayed optimum is reached.

All input fields except \(N\) have constant encoding length, while the binary encoding of \(N\) has \(n+1\) bits. Therefore \(L=\Theta(n)\), and every valid output gift list has

\( 2^{\Omega(L)} \)

entries. Merely writing that list requires exponential time.

3. A polynomial-size, reachable optimal distribution

The preceding lower bound concerns the sequence. We now prove that a compact reachable optimum can always be computed.

Index the initial cells by \(i=1,\ldots,h\), writing them as

\( (t_i,b_i,x_i), \qquad x_i=\frac{v_i}{d_i}, \)

where \(v_i,d_i\) are positive integers. Define

\( D=\prod_{i=1}^h d_i, \qquad \delta=\frac1D, \qquad a_i=Dx_i. \)

Every \(a_i\) is a positive integer, and normalization implies

\( \sum_i a_i=D. \)

Conceptually, initial cell \(i\) consists of \(a_i\) pieces, each of mass \(\delta\). We will not need to enumerate these pieces when computing the final distribution.

Choose one maximizing type \(s_r\in T_r\) for every resource class. Let

\( S=\{s_r:1\leq r\leq m\}. \)

For every \(s\in S\), choose an initial cell \(i(s)\) of type \(s\), which exists because \(p_s>0\). Reserve one mass-\(\delta\) piece from that cell as a designated recipient. Define \(\varepsilon_i=1\) when \(i=i(s)\) for some \(s\in S\), and \(\varepsilon_i=0\) otherwise.

Retain the coordinates that are already assigned to maximizing types:

\( \widehat b_{i,r} = \begin{cases} b_{i,r},&t_i\in T_r,\\ 0,&t_i\notin T_r. \end{cases} \)

The number of mass-\(\delta\) copies of resource \(r\) initially assigned to nonmaximizing types is the integer

\( A_r=\sum_{i:\,t_i\notin T_r}a_i b_{i,r}. \)

Construct the final list as follows:

  • For each initial cell \(i\), include mass \((a_i-\varepsilon_i)/D\) of type \(t_i\) at bundle \(\widehat b_i\), omitting zero masses.
  • For each \(s\in S\), include mass \(1/D\) at bundle \(c_s\), where

\( c_{s,r}=\widehat b_{i(s),r}+\mathbf 1_{\{s=s_r\}}A_r. \)

Merge identical cells if desired.

Type masses are preserved. For each selected type, precisely one piece was removed from its ordinary cells and reinstated as its designated recipient. Other type masses are unchanged.

Resource supplies are preserved. For each \(r\), the total final supply is

\( \frac1D\left(\sum_i a_i\widehat b_{i,r}+A_r\right) = \frac1D\sum_i a_i b_{i,r} = q_r. \)

The distribution is optimal. Every retained coordinate belongs to a type in \(T_r\). Every added coordinate belongs to \(s_r\in T_r\). Thus the equality condition from Section 1 holds, and

\( W(x^{\mathrm{final}})=\sum_r q_rM_r. \)

The list contains at most \(h+|S|\leq h+\tau\) cells.

4. Polynomial encoding length and computation time

Let \(B\) be the largest initial bundle coordinate, with \(B=0\) if all coordinates vanish.

Because all denominator encodings occur in the input, the bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\). Since \(\sum_i a_i=D\), each \(a_i\leq D\). Moreover,

\( A_r\leq\sum_i a_i B=DB, \qquad c_{s,r}\leq B+DB. \)

The bit length of \(B\) is \(O(L)\), so all constructed bundle coordinates have \(O(L)\) bits. Every output mass has denominator \(D\) and numerator at most \(D\), hence also has \(O(L)\) bits.

Finding the maximizing types, forming \(D\), computing the integers \(a_i,A_r\), and constructing these cells use polynomially many arithmetic operations on integers of polynomial bit length. Consequently, the optimal distribution above is computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

In particular, this also computes the optimum value in polynomial time.

5. A finite gift sequence with polynomial work per gift

We prove that the particular compact optimum from Section 3 is reachable.

Use the conceptual mass-\(\delta\) pieces. For each piece initially belonging to cell \(i\), give each of its original copies of resource \(r\) with \(t_i\notin T_r\) directly to the designated recipient of type \(s_r\).

Each individual transfer is a legal gift:

  • The donor possesses the copy being transferred.
  • The donor and recipient types are distinct, since \(t_i\notin T_r\) and \(s_r\in T_r\).
  • The utility comparison is strict:

\( u_{s_r}(r)=M_r>u_{t_i}(r). \)

  • Both participating pieces have mass \(\delta\), so \(\alpha=\delta\) is permitted.

A designated recipient may itself have original resources to donate. This creates no conflict: every resource it receives is a class for which its type is maximizing, while every resource it donates is a class for which its type is nonmaximizing. In particular, incoming gifts never replenish a coordinate that must be removed.

After all transfers, ordinary pieces have bundles \(\widehat b_i\). The designated recipient for class \(r\) has received exactly \(A_r\) copies. The aggregate final distribution is therefore exactly the one constructed in Section 3.

The number of gifts in this construction is

\( K=\sum_r A_r =\sum_i a_i\sum_{r:\,t_i\notin T_r}b_{i,r} \leq mDB. \)

This is finite and at most \(2^{O(L)}\).

The conceptual expansion into \(D\) pieces need not be stored. First process the designated recipients’ original nonmaximizing coordinates. Then process each remaining initial cell one piece at a time. For each initial cell, retain its unprocessed mass at \(b_i\) and its processed mass at \(\widehat b_i\), together with the currently active piece and the designated recipients.

Thus only polynomially many records are needed. Their coordinates and mass numerators have polynomial bit length. Cells requiring no gifts can be skipped in one operation; for every other cell, each processed piece generates at least one gift. This yields total running time

\( (K+1)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L), \)

including computation of the final distribution and generation of the explicit sequence. It does not seek a shortest sequence.

6. Complexity interpretation

The exact results are:

  • An optimal distribution of polynomial encoding length is polynomial-time computable.
  • A finite gift sequence reaching that distribution always exists and can be generated with polynomial work per listed gift.
  • Every gift sequence can nevertheless require exponential length, even with \(m=1\) and \(\tau=2\).

The finite-agent result cited in the question is consistent with these conclusions: its direct-transfer argument establishes attainability, without giving a polynomial bound in the bit length of binary-encoded multiplicities. See [Nongaillard and Mathieu, Theorem 6](https://jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/14/3/5.html).

Accordingly, the requested polynomial-time algorithm is impossible under the explicit-list output requirement. No \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness assertion is needed or proved here; the obstruction is the unavoidable size of the required output.
