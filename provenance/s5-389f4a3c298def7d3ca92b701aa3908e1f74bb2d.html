The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of household types \(\mathcal{T}\) with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), sizes \(s_t\), ranked acceptable hospitals, and hospital priority tiers, plus per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\), find a feasible assignment \(x_{t,h}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}s_t x_{t,h}\). Each household still receives an integral assignment or remains unmatched; \(x_{t,h}\) records the mass of identical households assigned to \(h\). Call an assignment occupancy-stable if there are no type \(t\), hospital \(h\), and positive mass \(\delta\) of type \(t\) preferring \(h\), together with removable masses \(y_u\) of types ranked below \(t\) at \(h\), such that \(0\leq y_u\leq x_{u,h}\), \(\sum_u s_u y_u\leq \delta s_t\), and \(\sum_u s_u x_{u,h}-\sum_u s_u y_u+\delta s_t\leq q_h\). Common master orders over types may be imposed as a restriction.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) household types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(m\geq1\) be the number of hospitals and \(L\) the total input bit length.
Each type has a positive integer size \(s_t\) and a strict ranking of its mutually acceptable hospitals, all preferred to being unmatched. Each hospital has positive binary-rational capacity \(q_h\), measured in size units per unit household population, and ordered priority tiers of acceptable types; only a strictly lower tier permits displacement, with no tie-breaking.
An assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,h}\geq0\) on acceptable pairs, with \(\sum_hx_{t,h}\leq\mu_t\) and \(O_h:=\sum_ts_tx_{t,h}\leq q_h\). Mass may be divided among hospitals, but every individual household is assigned whole to one hospital or remains unmatched.
Write \(x_{t,\bot}:=\mu_t-\sum_hx_{t,h}\) and \(D_{t,h}:=x_{t,\bot}+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_t g}x_{t,g}\), the mass of type \(t\) that strictly prefers \(h\) to its assignment.
An assignment is occupancy-stable if no acceptable pair \((t,h)\) admits \(0<\delta\leq D_{t,h}\) and removable masses \(0\leq y_u\leq x_{u,h}\), supported only on types strictly below \(t\) at \(h\), such that \(\sum_us_uy_u\leq\delta s_t\) and \(O_h-\sum_us_uy_u+\delta s_t\leq q_h\). Removing no mass is allowed.
The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,h}s_tx_{t,h}\) over occupancy-stable assignments. The restriction to master orders on both sides means that hospital rankings are restrictions of one strict order of types, and household rankings are restrictions of one strict order of hospitals.

Is computing a maximum-occupancy occupancy-stable assignment polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? Does the same classification hold when \(s_t\leq3\) and master orders are imposed on both sides?

For indivisible finite households, maximum-occupancy occupancy-stable matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with sizes at most \(3\) and master orders on both sides; an occupancy-stable matching always exists and can be found in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03638v2)
No result settling the exact mass problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Classification

The general problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when every type has size \(1\).

The classification changes under the stated master-order restriction: there is a unique occupancy-stable mass assignment, computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). This holds for arbitrary positive integer sizes, hence also when \(s_t\leq3\). In fact, only the strict master order of types is needed.

The proofs below use exactly the permitted real-mass assignments and strictly lower-tier displacement rule.

1. An exact characterization of mass stability

For an acceptable pair \((t,h)\), define the occupancy contributed by types strictly below \(t\) at \(h\) by

\[ B_{t,h}:=\sum_{u:\,t\text{ is strictly above }u\text{ at }h}s_ux_{u,h}. \]

Lemma 1. A feasible assignment is occupancy-stable if and only if, for every acceptable pair \((t,h)\),

\[ D_{t,h}=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(O_h=q_h\text{ and }B_{t,h}=0\bigr). \]

Proof. Suppose \(D_{t,h}>0\).

If \(O_h<q_h\), choose

\[ \delta=\min\left\{D_{t,h},\frac{q_h-O_h}{s_t}\right\}>0. \]

Removing no mass accommodates \(\delta\), so the pair blocks.

If \(B_{t,h}>0\), some strictly lower-priority type \(u\) has \(x_{u,h}>0\). Choose

\[ \delta=\min\left\{D_{t,h},\frac{s_ux_{u,h}}{s_t}\right\}>0, \qquad y_u=\frac{\delta s_t}{s_u}, \]

and set all other removable masses to zero. Then \(y_u\leq x_{u,h}\), and the removed occupancy equals \(\delta s_t\). Thus occupancy remains \(O_h\leq q_h\), and again the pair blocks.

Conversely, if \(O_h=q_h\) and \(B_{t,h}=0\), every permitted removable mass is zero. Adding any positive \(\delta\) violates capacity. If \(D_{t,h}=0\), no positive admissible \(\delta\) exists. This proves both directions. \(\square\)

It is useful to change variables to occupancy units:

\[ z_{t,h}:=s_tx_{t,h}, \qquad b_t:=s_t\mu_t. \]

Feasibility becomes

\[ z_{t,h}\geq0,\qquad \sum_hz_{t,h}\leq b_t,\qquad \sum_tz_{t,h}\leq q_h. \]

The occupancy demand for \(h\) is

\[ d_{t,h}:=s_tD_{t,h} =b_t-\sum_{g:\,g=h\text{ or }g\succ_t h}z_{t,g}. \]

Consequently, Lemma 1 says that whenever \(d_{t,h}>0\), hospital \(h\) must be full and contain no occupancy from strictly lower-priority types. Sizes now enter only through the supplies \(b_t\).

2. A rounding lemma for unit supplies and capacities

Consider a bipartite instance with household supplies and hospital capacities both equal to \(1\), strict household preferences, and hospital priority tiers. In this section, \(z\) denotes its occupancy assignment.

An integral feasible assignment is an ordinary matching. By Lemma 1, its occupancy stability is exactly weak stability: there is no acceptable pair for which the household strictly prefers the hospital to its assignment and the hospital is either unmatched or strictly prefers that household to its current partner.

Lemma 2. Every occupancy-stable fractional assignment \(z\) in such an instance admits an occupancy-stable integral assignment \(w\) satisfying

\[ \sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}\geq\sum_{t,h}z_{t,h}. \]

Proof. For each acceptable pair \((t,h)\), select one alternative of Lemma 1 that holds at \(z\):

  • If \(d_{t,h}=0\), impose this equality.
  • Otherwise, impose that \(h\) is full and has zero occupancy from types strictly below \(t\).

Let \(P\) be the feasible-assignment polytope with all these selected conditions imposed. It contains \(z\).

Each additional condition consists only of vertex saturation and edge-zero constraints. Indeed, because supply is \(1\),

\[ d_{t,h}=0 \]

is equivalent to

\[ \sum_gw_{t,g}=1, \qquad w_{t,g}=0\quad\text{for every }g\text{ strictly worse than }h. \]

The other alternative is equivalent to

\[ \sum_uw_{u,h}=1, \qquad w_{u,h}=0\quad\text{for every }u\text{ strictly below }t. \]

Every point of \(P\) is occupancy-stable, since it satisfies a selected alternative of Lemma 1 for each pair.

We next prove that every vertex of \(P\) is integral. Suppose a point \(w\in P\) has fractional coordinates, and form the bipartite graph consisting of edges with \(0<w_{t,h}<1\).

If this graph contains a cycle, the cycle has even length. Alternately increasing and decreasing its coordinates by a sufficiently small positive amount preserves every vertex sum. Both perturbation directions remain in \(P\), so \(w\) is not a vertex.

If the graph contains no cycle, a component containing an edge is a tree and has two leaves. Take the path between two leaves and perturb its coordinates alternately. Every internal vertex sum is preserved. At either endpoint, the unique fractional incident edge is its only positive incident edge: an additional coordinate equal to \(1\) would violate the capacity bound. Thus both endpoints have sum strictly less than \(1\), and neither is subject to a saturation equality. A sufficiently small perturbation in either direction is feasible.

These perturbations never alter a forced-zero edge. Therefore any nonintegral point of \(P\) fails to be a vertex.

Finally, \(P\) is nonempty and bounded, so maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}w_{t,h}\) over \(P\) has an optimal vertex. That vertex is integral, is occupancy-stable, and has objective at least that of \(z\). \(\square\)

This lemma rules out an improvement in the optimum caused by fractional assignments in the unit-supply reduction below.

3. NP-hardness of the general problem

We use the following known theorem: deciding whether a stable-marriage instance with incomplete lists has a weakly stable matching of cardinality at least \(K\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete even when ties occur on only one side. Thus we may take household preferences to be strict and allow ties only in hospital preferences. This is Lemma 2.1 of [Manlove, Irving, Iwama, Miyazaki and Morita, *Hard Variants of Stable Marriage*](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~davidm/pubs/TR-1999-43.pdf).

Take such an instance with \(n\geq1\) households and \(n\) hospitals. Let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Construct the mass instance as follows:

  • For each original household, create a type of size \(1\) and mass \(1/N\).
  • Give each original hospital capacity \(1/N\).
  • Preserve all acceptable pairs, household preferences, and hospital priority tiers.

To normalize the population, if \(N>n\), add one dummy type of size \(1\) and mass \((N-n)/N\), together with a private hospital of capacity \((N-n)/N\). These two find only each other acceptable.

All masses and capacities are positive dyadic rationals. Their encodings, and the entire construction, have polynomial length. All sizes equal \(1\).

The dummy type must be fully assigned to its private hospital in every stable assignment: otherwise that hospital has slack and the type has positive demand, contradicting Lemma 1. Its fixed occupancy contribution is \((N-n)/N\). When \(N=n\), no dummy is needed.

For the original pairs, normalize an assignment by setting

\[ z_{t,h}:=Nx_{t,h}. \]

This gives household supplies and hospital capacities equal to \(1\). Multiplication by \(N\) preserves zero demand, full capacity, and zero lower-priority occupancy, so it preserves the stability characterization.

Let \(k^*\) be the maximum cardinality of a weakly stable matching in the original instance.

Every such matching gives a stable mass assignment by placing mass \(1/N\) on each matched pair. Therefore the maximum mass occupancy is at least

\[ \frac{k^*+N-n}{N}. \]

Conversely, take any stable mass assignment and normalize its original-pair coordinates. Lemma 2 gives a weakly stable integral matching with cardinality at least

\[ N\sum_{\text{original }(t,h)}x_{t,h}. \]

Its cardinality cannot exceed \(k^*\). Adding the fixed dummy contribution therefore shows that every stable mass assignment has occupancy at most

\[ \frac{k^*+N-n}{N}. \]

Hence the exact optimum is

\[ V^*=\frac{k^*+N-n}{N}. \]

In particular, the original instance has a weakly stable matching of cardinality at least \(K\) if and only if the mass instance has a stable assignment with occupancy at least

\[ \frac{K+N-n}{N}. \]

This is a polynomial reduction. Computing a maximum-occupancy occupancy-stable mass assignment is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with \(s_t=1\).

4. Strict master orders give a unique assignment

Assume hospitals rank acceptable types according to restrictions of one strict master order,

\[ t_1\succ t_2\succ\cdots\succ t_\tau. \]

Household hospital rankings may be arbitrary strict rankings; a household master order is unnecessary.

Work in occupancy units, with supply \(b_t=s_t\mu_t\).

The greedy algorithm

Initialize residual capacities \(c_h=q_h\). Process types in master-order sequence. For the current type \(t\), initialize remaining supply \(r=b_t\), and visit its acceptable hospitals in preference order. At each visited hospital \(h\), set

\[ z_{t,h}=\min\{r,c_h\}, \qquad r\leftarrow r-z_{t,h}, \qquad c_h\leftarrow c_h-z_{t,h}. \]

After processing all types, return \(x_{t,h}=z_{t,h}/s_t\).

Stability

Feasibility follows directly from the updates.

Suppose the final assignment has \(d_{t,h}>0\). When type \(t\) visited \(h\), its remaining supply exceeded the amount allocated there: their difference is precisely the final demand \(d_{t,h}\). Since the algorithm allocated the minimum of remaining supply and residual capacity, it exhausted the residual capacity of \(h\).

Thus \(h\) is full. At that moment only \(t\) and types above \(t\) had been processed. No later type can receive positive occupancy at \(h\), because its residual capacity is zero. Hence \(h\) contains no type strictly below \(t\).

Lemma 1 proves stability.

Uniqueness

Let \(z\) be any stable assignment. We show inductively that it equals the greedy assignment.

Suppose allocations of all types above \(t\) have already been fixed. Define the capacity remaining after these higher types by

\[ c_h=q_h-\sum_{u\text{ above }t}z_{u,h}. \]

Write the acceptable hospitals of \(t\) as

\[ h_1\succ_t h_2\succ_t\cdots\succ_t h_k. \]

Proceed inductively through this list. Once allocations to earlier hospitals have been fixed, define

\[ r_j=b_t-\sum_{\ell<j}z_{t,h_\ell}. \]

Feasibility implies

\[ z_{t,h_j}\leq\min\{r_j,c_{h_j}\}. \]

Suppose this inequality were strict. Then \(r_j-z_{t,h_j}>0\), which equals \(d_{t,h_j}\). By Lemma 1, hospital \(h_j\) must be full and contain no type below \(t\).

Because the hospital master order is strict, every other acceptable type is either above or below \(t\). Consequently,

\[ q_{h_j} =\sum_{u\text{ above }t}z_{u,h_j}+z_{t,h_j}, \]

so \(z_{t,h_j}=c_{h_j}\). This contradicts the assumed strict inequality. Therefore

\[ z_{t,h_j}=\min\{r_j,c_{h_j}\}. \]

This is exactly the greedy allocation. Induction over hospitals and then types fixes every coordinate. The stable assignment is unique and therefore maximizes occupancy.

Bit complexity

Let \(E\) be the set of acceptable pairs. The algorithm uses \(O(|E|+\tau+m)\) arithmetic operations once the type master order is available.

If the master order is not supplied, it can be recovered by topologically sorting the precedence constraints from consecutive entries of each hospital’s strict list. The master-order hypothesis guarantees that these constraints are acyclic.

For exact arithmetic, take a common denominator \(Q\) equal to the product of all input denominators. Its bit length is at most the total denominator encoding length, hence at most \(L\). Every supply \(b_t\) and capacity \(q_h\) is an integer multiple of \(1/Q\). Greedy updates use only comparisons and subtraction, so all intermediate quantities retain this denominator and have polynomial bit length. Finally, division by \(s_t\) also produces coordinates of polynomial bit length.

The algorithm thus runs in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

5. Why the finite-household restriction behaves differently

The decisive difference is that removable mass can be arbitrarily small. Any positive occupancy from a lower-priority type can accommodate a sufficiently small equal-occupancy displacement. Likewise, any positive slack can accommodate sufficiently small incoming mass.

Under a strict master order, these facts force the greedy allocation at every step. The finite-household hardness cited in the question therefore does not extend to the stated master-order mass problem.
