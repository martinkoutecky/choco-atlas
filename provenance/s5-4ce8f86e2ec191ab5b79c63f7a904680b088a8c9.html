The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), each type’s ranking of mutually acceptable partner types (with unmatched \(\bot\)), and an incumbent matching described by symmetric pair masses \(x^0_{tu}\) whose rows sum to \(\mu_t\), compute the incumbent’s unpopularity factor against all alternative matchings of the atomless population. A challenger can be represented by \(z_{tuv}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) with incumbent partner type \(u\) and challenger partner type \(v\), subject to \(\sum_v z_{tuv}=x^0_{tu}\) and symmetric challenger pair masses \(y_{tv}=\sum_u z_{tuv}\). Maximize \(A(z)/B(z)\), where \(A(z)=\sum_{t,u,v:\,v\succ_t u}z_{tuv}\) and \(B(z)=\sum_{t,u,v:\,u\succ_t v}z_{tuv}\), using the paper’s \(0/0=1\) and positive-over-zero \(=\infty\) conventions.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is an atomless population given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type \(t\) has a weak ranking of its mutually acceptable partner types \(N(t)\), all strictly preferred to being unmatched \(\bot\); ties are allowed. Acceptability is symmetric, and at least one acceptable pair exists. Same-type partners are allowed when \(t\in N(t)\), but individuals cannot partner themselves.
Write \(S_t=N(t)\cup\{\bot\}\). The incumbent is given by nonnegative binary-rational participant masses \(x^0_{tu}\), with \(\sum_{u\in S_t}x^0_{tu}=\mu_t\) and \(x^0_{tu}=x^0_{ut}\) for actual partner types. Diagonal entries count participants, and \(x^0_{t\bot}\) counts unmatched participants.
A challenger is represented by real masses \(z_{tuv}\geq0\), for \(t\in T\) and \(u,v\in S_t\), satisfying \(\sum_v z_{tuv}=x^0_{tu}\). Its participant masses \(y_{tv}=\sum_u z_{tuv}\) must satisfy \(y_{tv}=y_{vt}\) for actual partner types; unmatched masses have no symmetry constraint.
Define \(A(z)=\sum_{t,u,v:\,v\succ_t u}z_{tuv}\), \(B(z)=\sum_{t,u,v:\,u\succ_t v}z_{tuv}\), and the incumbent’s unpopularity factor \(U(x^0)=\sup_z A(z)/B(z)\), with \(0/0=1\) and \(a/0=\infty\) for \(a>0\). All feasible challenger masses are included.
Is exact computation of \(U(x^0)\), including detection of \(\infty\), polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard? State running times in \(m=\sum_t|N(t)|\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\).
For finite roommates instances, computing a given matching’s unpopularity factor, allowing preference ties and unmatched people, takes \(O(m_d\sqrt{n}\log^2 n)\) time, where \(n\) counts people and \(m_d\) is their total preference-list length ([Ruangwises–Itoh](https://arxiv.org/html/1803.09435v6)). No result settling the exact atomless problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive rational masses to total mass one and defined L as the total input bit length.
  • Allowed preference ties and placed unmatched strictly below every acceptable partner, following the discrete definition.
  • Restricted both matchings to mutually acceptable partners and made unmatched masses exempt from symmetry.
  • Allowed pairing distinct individuals of the same type when that type is acceptable to itself; diagonal pair masses count participants rather than pairs.
  • Required at least one acceptable pair to exclude the instance whose only individual matching is the all-unmatched matching, for which the discrete maximum over other matchings is undefined.
  • Retained all feasible challenger mass arrays as in the graded question: equality of mass arrays does not imply equality of individual matchings. With the nonempty acceptability condition, including the incumbent array preserves the value obtained by excluding the incumbent individual matching.
  • Used a supremum to define the value without presupposing attainment, and specified real challenger masses with binary-rational incumbent data.

Result

Exact computation of $U(x^0)$, including detection of $\infty$, is polynomial-time solvable. An algorithm uses at most two rational linear programs and runs in
$$ \bigl((\tau+1)(m+\tau)+L\bigr)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations. Every finite output is rational and has polynomial encoding length.

The proof uses exactly the challenger constraints in the question, including participant-counted diagonal entries.

1. The challenger polytope

Write $d_t=|N(t)|$ and let
$$ D=\sum_{t\in T}(d_t+1)^2 $$
be the number of challenger variables.

Define $P$ by nonnegativity, the incumbent-group constraints
$$ \sum_{v\in S_t}z_{tuv}=x^0_{tu} \qquad(t\in T,\ u\in S_t), $$
and, for every unordered acceptable pair of distinct types $\{t,v\}$, the balance constraint
$$ \sum_{u\in S_t}z_{tuv} = \sum_{u\in S_v}z_{vut}. $$
For a same-type pair, the symmetry condition is an identity, so no additional constraint is needed.

These are precisely the stated feasibility conditions. In particular, there are no integrality constraints.

The polytope is nonempty: the challenger that preserves the incumbent is
$$ z^{\mathrm{stay}}_{tuv} = \begin{cases} x^0_{tu},&v=u,\\ 0,&v\ne u. \end{cases} $$
Its participant masses equal $x^0$, so it satisfies symmetry. Moreover,
$$ A(z^{\mathrm{stay}})=B(z^{\mathrm{stay}})=0, $$
and hence $U(x^0)\geq1$ by the specified convention.

Summing all incumbent-group constraints gives
$$ \sum_{t,u,v}z_{tuv} = \sum_{t,u}x^0_{tu} = 1. $$
Thus $P$ is bounded; it is also closed, because its constraints are linear. Consequently, linear objectives attain their maxima on $P$.

Define coefficient vectors $a,b\in\{0,1\}^{D}$ by
$$ a_{tuv}=\mathbf{1}[v\succ_t u], \qquad b_{tuv}=\mathbf{1}[u\succ_t v]. $$
Then $A(z)=a^\mathsf{T}z$ and $B(z)=b^\mathsf{T}z$. Tied comparisons have both coefficients zero.

2. First linear program: determine whether positive losing mass is possible

Solve
$$ \beta=\max_{z\in P}b^\mathsf{T}z. $$
Since $B$ is nonnegative, there are two cases.

Case $\beta=0$. Every feasible challenger has $B(z)=0$. Solve a second linear program:
$$ \alpha=\max_{z\in P}a^\mathsf{T}z. $$
If $\alpha>0$, a maximizing challenger has positive gaining mass and zero losing mass, so $U(x^0)=\infty$.

If $\alpha=0$, every challenger has $A(z)=B(z)=0$, so $U(x^0)=1$.

This completely resolves the case $\beta=0$.

3. Second linear program when $\beta>0$

Introduce nonnegative variables $w_{tuv}$ and $s$. Extend $A$ and $B$ to $w$ using the same coefficient vectors.

Consider the linear program maximizing
$$ A(w) $$
subject to
$$ \sum_{v\in S_t}w_{tuv}=s\,x^0_{tu} \qquad(t\in T,\ u\in S_t), $$
$$ \sum_{u\in S_t}w_{tuv} = \sum_{u\in S_v}w_{vut} \qquad(\{t,v\}\text{ acceptable},\ t\ne v), $$
$$ B(w)=1, \qquad w\geq0,\quad s\geq0. $$

We verify that its objective values are exactly the ratios from challengers with positive losing mass.

If $z\in P$ and $B(z)>0$, set
$$ s=\frac{1}{B(z)}, \qquad w=\frac{z}{B(z)}. $$
Every constraint above follows by scaling the corresponding constraint of $P$, and
$$ A(w)=\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}. $$

Conversely, suppose $(w,s)$ satisfies the transformed constraints. Summing its incumbent-group constraints yields
$$ \sum_{t,u,v}w_{tuv}=s. $$
Since $w$ is nonnegative and $B(w)$ sums a subset of its coordinates,
$$ 1=B(w)\leq\sum_{t,u,v}w_{tuv}=s. $$
In particular, $s>0$. Therefore $z=w/s$ belongs to $P$, and
$$ B(z)=\frac{1}{s}>0, \qquad \frac{A(z)}{B(z)}=A(w). $$

Because $\beta>0$, some challenger has positive losing mass, so this transformed program is feasible. Its objective supremum is therefore
$$ \sup_{\substack{z\in P\\B(z)>0}}\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}. $$
The verification above also handles the possible zero denominators without assuming that $B$ is positive everywhere on $P$.

4. Correctness of infinity detection and the finite answer

If the transformed program is unbounded above, the correspondence just proved supplies arbitrarily large challenger ratios. Hence $U(x^0)=\infty$.

It remains to show that a challenger with $B(z)=0<A(z)$ cannot escape this detection.

Suppose such a challenger $z^0$ exists. Since $\beta>0$, choose $z^+\in P$ with $B(z^+)>0$. For $0<\varepsilon<1$, convexity of $P$ gives the feasible challenger
$$ z^\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)z^0+\varepsilon z^+. $$
Linearity gives
$$ B(z^\varepsilon)=\varepsilon B(z^+)>0 $$
and
$$ A(z^\varepsilon)\geq(1-\varepsilon)A(z^0). $$
Consequently,
$$ \frac{A(z^\varepsilon)}{B(z^\varepsilon)} \geq \frac{(1-\varepsilon)A(z^0)} {\varepsilon B(z^+)} \longrightarrow\infty \qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow0. $$
Thus a zero-denominator challenger with positive gaining mass forces the transformed program to be unbounded.

If instead that program has a finite optimum $q$, the preceding argument proves that every challenger with $B(z)=0$ also has $A(z)=0$. Those challengers contribute ratio $1$; all positive-denominator ratios are accounted for by the transformed program. Therefore the exact answer is
$$ U(x^0)=\max\{1,q\}. $$

This proves correctness in every case.

5. Encoding size and running time

Let $e$ be the number of unordered acceptable pairs of distinct types. Symmetric acceptability gives $e\leq m/2$. The original program has $D$ variables and
$$ m+\tau+e $$
equality constraints, besides nonnegativity. The transformed program has $D+1$ variables and
$$ m+\tau+e+1 $$
equality constraints.

Because $d_t\leq\tau$,
$$ D = \sum_t(d_t+1)^2 \leq (\tau+1)\sum_t(d_t+1) = (\tau+1)(m+\tau). $$

All constraint coefficients are $0$, $1$, or $-1$, except for the coefficients $-x^0_{tu}$ of $s$ in the transformed program. Each incumbent entry appears there once. Each challenger variable occurs in one incumbent-group equation, at most one balance equation, and at most one denominator-normalization equation. Thus the programs have $O(D+m+\tau)$ nonzero coefficients. Their sparse encoding length is
$$ O\!\left(L+(D+m+\tau)\log(D+m+\tau+2)\right). $$
Even a dense encoding has polynomial length in $D$, $m+\tau$, and $L$.

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility, unboundedness, and an exact rational optimum can be determined in polynomial bit time. Polynomial bit complexity for rational linear programming and exact rational solution methods are discussed in [Adler–Beling](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf) and [Gärtner–Gleixner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12820). Its hypotheses hold here because all program data are rational and have polynomial encoding length.

Constructing the programs and making at most two exact linear-programming calls therefore takes
$$ \operatorname{poly}(D,m+\tau,L) = \bigl((\tau+1)(m+\tau)+L\bigr)^{O(1)} $$
bit operations. This is a polynomial bound in the supplied support representation; no expansion into individual people is required.
