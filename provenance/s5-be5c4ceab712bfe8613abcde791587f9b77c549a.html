The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), and a complete weak preference order over types for each \(i\in T\), does there exist a stable pairing of the atomless population? Represent a pairing by type-mass variables \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\) for \(i\ne j\); for \(q_i(j)=\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\), require \(q_i(i)=0\) and, for every distinct \(i,j\), \(q_i(j)=0\) or \(q_j(i)=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_{i\in T}\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types. Each type \(i\) has a complete weak preference order \(\succeq_i\) over \(T\), including its own type; all partner types are acceptable, ties are retained, and blocking requires strict improvement by both people.
The population is atomless. A complete pairing is represented by real variables \(x_{ij}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in T}x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\) for distinct \(i,j\). The variable \(x_{ii}\) counts the mass of people paired with distinct people of their own type, counting both endpoints; nobody remains unmatched.
Define \(q_i(j)=\sum_{k\in T:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\). A pairing is stable precisely when \(q_i(i)=0\) for every \(i\), and \(q_i(j)=0\) or \(q_j(i)=0\) for every distinct \(i,j\); these conditions exclude blocking within and between types. Let \(m=|T|=\tau\) be the number of partner alternatives and \(L\) the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether such a stable complete pairing exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents with complete weak preferences, deciding whether a stable arrangement exists on prescribed numbers of isolated vertices, two-vertex paths, and three-vertex paths is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already for arrangements consisting entirely of pairs. [Glitzner](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27102v1)
Ordinally stable fractional matchings with unit capacities, allowing unmatched agents, always exist and can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time even with ties; this result does not classify the problem above with arbitrary binary-rational masses and complete pairing. [Chen, Roy, and Sorge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12259)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified that diagonal mass represents pairings between distinct people of the same type and counts both endpoints, rather than representing self-pairing or unmatched people.
  • Made explicit that all types, including one's own type, are acceptable and that ties are retained; stability uses strict improvements on both sides.
  • Allowed real pairing variables, as appropriate for atomless divisible masses.
  • Defined L as total binary input length and m as the number of partner-type alternatives, which equals tau in this model.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). In fact, every valid instance admits a stable complete pairing, including instances with unequal binary-rational masses and arbitrary ties.

The proof below extends the cited unit-capacity existence theorem by cloning types and taking a limit. It checks both between-type and within-type stability.

1. The finite existence theorem used

We use the following consequence of [Chen, Roy, and Sorge, Lemma 3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12259#page=6):

For a finite graph with complete weak preference orders over each vertex’s neighbors, there exists a symmetric fractional matching \((F_{uv})\) with nonnegative entries and row sums at most one such that, for every edge \(\{u,v\}\), either all of \(u\)’s unit capacity is assigned to neighbors weakly preferred to \(v\), or the analogous statement holds for \(v\).

Unmatched capacity is permitted in this theorem. We will eliminate it through same-type pairing, and then eliminate possible within-type blocking through a limit.

2. Clone the population

Since the masses are rational, choose a positive integer \(D\) such that every \(D\mu_i\) is an integer. For each integer \(n\geq2\), put \(N=nD\) and create a clone set \(C_i\) of size \(N\mu_i\) for every type \(i\). Thus there are \(N\) clones altogether, and every type has at least two clones.

Write \(p(u)\) for the type of clone \(u\). Use the complete graph on the clones. A clone of type \(i\) ranks other clones according to their types under \(\succeq_i\); clones whose types are tied remain tied.

This satisfies the finite theorem’s hypotheses: the graph has no isolated vertices, all partners are acceptable, and every clone has a complete weak order over its neighbors. Apply the theorem to obtain \(F\), setting \(F_{uu}=0\).

For each clone \(u\), define its matched and unmatched capacities by

\(s_u=\sum_v F_{uv},\qquad r_u=1-s_u\geq0.\)

For a clone \(u\) of type \(i\), define

\(B_u(j)=\sum_{v:\,j\succ_i p(v)}F_{uv},\qquad d_u(j)=r_u+B_u(j).\)

Because the preference order is complete, the mass assigned to partners weakly preferred to type \(j\) is exactly \(s_u-B_u(j)\). Consequently, \(d_u(j)\) is its deficit from one.

The finite stability theorem therefore gives, for every two distinct clones \(u,v\),

\(d_u(p(v))\,d_v(p(u))=0.\) \[\tag{1}\]

3. Aggregate and complete the pairing

For distinct types \(i,j\), define

\(x^{(n)}_{ij}=\frac1N\sum_{u\in C_i}\sum_{v\in C_j}F_{uv}.\)

Define the diagonal entries by

\(x^{(n)}_{ii}=\frac1N\left(\sum_{u\in C_i}\sum_{v\in C_i}F_{uv}+\sum_{u\in C_i}r_u\right).\)

The first diagonal sum counts both endpoints of each same-type edge, as required in the question. The second term completes the remaining mass through same-type pairing. This is legitimate in the atomless model: any positive mass assigned to same-type pairing can be divided into two equal parts and paired across those parts.

Nonnegativity and off-diagonal symmetry follow immediately. Moreover,

\(\sum_jx^{(n)}_{ij}=\frac1N\sum_{u\in C_i}(s_u+r_u)=\frac{|C_i|}{N}=\mu_i.\)

Thus \(x^{(n)}\) is a complete pairing.

Let \(q^{(n)}_i(j)\) denote its stability quantities. Directly from the definitions,

\(q^{(n)}_i(j)=\frac1N\left(\sum_{u\in C_i}B_u(j)+\mathbf1_{\{j\succ_i i\}}\sum_{u\in C_i}r_u\right).\) \[\tag{2}\]

In particular,

\(0\leq q^{(n)}_i(j)\leq\frac1N\sum_{u\in C_i}d_u(j).\) \[\tag{3}\]

Between-type stability holds exactly

Fix distinct types \(i,j\). Suppose \(q^{(n)}_i(j)>0\). By (3), some clone \(u\in C_i\) satisfies \(d_u(j)>0\).

For every \(v\in C_j\), equation (1) then implies \(d_v(i)=0\). Since \(d_v(i)=r_v+B_v(i)\) is a sum of nonnegative quantities, both quantities are zero. Equation (2) consequently gives \(q^{(n)}_j(i)=0\).

Hence, for every distinct \(i,j\),

\(q^{(n)}_i(j)=0\quad\text{or}\quad q^{(n)}_j(i)=0.\) \[\tag{4}\]

Within-type blocking mass tends to zero

For any type \(i\), at most one clone \(u\in C_i\) can satisfy \(B_u(i)>0\). Indeed, two such distinct clones \(u,v\) would satisfy \(d_u(i)>0\) and \(d_v(i)>0\), contradicting (1).

Each \(B_u(i)\) is at most \(s_u\leq1\). Since \(i\not\succ_i i\), equation (2) therefore yields

\(0\leq q^{(n)}_i(i)=\frac1N\sum_{u\in C_i}B_u(i)\leq\frac1N.\) \[\tag{5}\]

4. Pass to an exactly stable limit

The set of nonnegative matrices satisfying the prescribed row sums and off-diagonal symmetry is closed and bounded in finite-dimensional Euclidean space: every entry lies between zero and one. It is therefore compact.

Choose a convergent subsequence of \(x^{(n)}\), and denote its limit by \(x\). Nonnegativity, symmetry, and the row-sum equalities pass to the limit, so \(x\) is a complete pairing.

Every \(q_i(j)\) is a linear function of the matrix entries and hence is continuous. Since \(N=nD\) tends to infinity, (5) gives

\(q_i(i)=0\qquad\text{for every }i.\)

For distinct types, (4) is equivalently

\(q^{(n)}_i(j)\,q^{(n)}_j(i)=0.\)

Taking limits gives \(q_i(j)q_j(i)=0\). Both factors are nonnegative, so

\(q_i(j)=0\quad\text{or}\quad q_j(i)=0.\)

These are exactly all the stability conditions stipulated in the question. Thus \(x\) is stable and complete.

5. Decision complexity

On the promised domain of valid inputs, the algorithm simply returns YES. If input validity must also be checked, positivity, rational normalization, and the preference-order conditions can be verified in time polynomial in \(L\), and therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The cloning argument is an existence proof, not an algorithm that must expand binary-encoded masses. Its potentially large intermediate populations do not affect the decision running time.

The finite integral hardness result does not transfer: the proof uses fractional allocations, atomless same-type completion, and a limit to obtain exact stability.

Step-by-step verification

1. The finite existence theorem

The cited result applies. Chen, Roy, and Sorge’s Lemma 3.6 guarantees an ordinally stable fractional matching for finite graphs with preferences allowing ties. Their Definition 2.3 defines ordinal stability by requiring, on every edge, that at least one endpoint receive total weight one from partners weakly preferred to the other endpoint. This is precisely the consequence stated by the prover. [Definitions and Lemma 3.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12259)

Although the paper presents preferences through rational satisfaction values, finite complete weak orders admit such representations. Consequently, this presentation imposes no additional restriction on the clone preferences.

The clone graph satisfies the hypotheses: it is finite, has no isolated vertices, and every vertex has a complete weak order over its neighbors. Symmetry and row sums at most one follow from the definition of a fractional matching.

2. Clone construction and deficits

A common denominator \(D\) exists because all masses are rational. Positivity ensures \(D\mu_i\) is a positive integer, so \(nD\mu_i\geq2\) for every \(n\geq2\). Thus every type has distinct same-type clones, including when \(\tau=1\).

The definitions give
\[ d_u(j)=1-s_u+B_u(j)=1-\bigl(s_u-B_u(j)\bigr). \]
Completeness of the weak order makes the partners weakly preferred to \(j\) exactly the complement, among neighbors, of those strictly worse than \(j\). Therefore \(s_u-B_u(j)\) is the relevant assigned weight.

All deficits are nonnegative. The cited stability condition thus yields equation (1) for every pair of distinct clones. There is no assumption here that every clone is fully matched.

3. Aggregation and completion

The off-diagonal entries aggregate symmetric edge weights with the correct normalization.

The diagonal internal-edge sum counts each same-type edge twice, matching the task’s convention that the diagonal counts endpoints. Adding residual capacity to the diagonal gives exactly the prescribed row sum:
\[ \sum_jx^{(n)}_{ij} =\frac{|C_i|}{N} =\mu_i. \]

The same-type completion is legitimate under the stipulated atomless model and matrix representation. It does not require an integer number of people or a finite perfect matching.

Equation (2) correctly accounts for both contributions to a stability quantity:

  • Existing matched mass contributes through \(B_u(j)\).
  • Newly completed same-type mass contributes precisely when \(j\succ_i i\).

Since the indicator is at most one, inequality (3) follows. No normalization factor is missing.

4. Between-type stability

If \(q^{(n)}_i(j)>0\), inequality (3) implies that at least one clone \(u\in C_i\) has \(d_u(j)>0\).

For every \(v\in C_j\), the clones are distinct because \(i\neq j\). Equation (1) therefore forces \(d_v(i)=0\). Nonnegativity separately forces \(r_v=0\) and \(B_v(i)=0\).

Substitution into equation (2) proves \(q^{(n)}_j(i)=0\), regardless of the comparison between \(i\) and \(j\) in type \(j\)’s order. Hence equation (4) holds exactly for every finite approximation.

5. Within-type estimate

If two distinct same-type clones had positive \(B_u(i)\), their corresponding deficits would both be positive, contradicting equation (1). Thus at most one clone contributes positive mass to this sum.

Each contribution is at most one. Moreover, the strict part of a weak order is irreflexive, so the added diagonal residual mass contributes nothing to \(q^{(n)}_i(i)\). Therefore equation (5), including its constant \(1/N\), is correct.

This step properly identifies why the finite approximations need not yet satisfy exact within-type stability.

The feasible matrices lie in a fixed finite-dimensional space. Nonnegativity, symmetry, and prescribed row sums define a closed set; the row sums bound every entry. Compactness and the existence of a convergent subsequence follow.

Each stability quantity is a fixed linear function of the matrix entries. Consequently:

  • The within-type bound tends to zero along the subsequence.
  • Every between-type product remains zero in the limit.

The limiting factors are nonnegative, so a zero product gives the required disjunction. The resulting matrix satisfies every stability condition in USER_TASK.

The argument also covers singleton support and arbitrary ties without additional assumptions.
