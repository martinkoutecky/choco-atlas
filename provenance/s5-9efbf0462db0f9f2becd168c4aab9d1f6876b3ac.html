The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of applicant types \(A\) and position types \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), an acceptable-pair set \(E\), strict applicant rankings \(\succ_a\) over acceptable position types, and weak position rankings \(\succ_b\) over acceptable applicant types, find a mass assignment \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\), and stability. For each \((a,b)\in E\), define \(D_A(a,b)=\mu_a-\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(D_B(a,b)=\nu_b-\sum_{a'}x_{a'b}+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\); require \(D_A(a,b)=0\) or \(D_B(a,b)=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be disjoint finite sets of applicant and position types, with mutually acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\). Applicants strictly rank their acceptable position types; positions have total preorders over acceptable applicant types. Every acceptable partner is strictly preferable to being unmatched; position ties remain unresolved, and \(\succ_b\) denotes strict preference.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_a\) or \(\nu_b\) encoded as a binary rational. Mass totals need not agree or sum to one. Set \(m=|B|\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
A mass assignment has \(x_{ab}\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) for \((a,b)\in E\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\); all sums range over acceptable pairs.
Define \(D_A(a,b)=\mu_a-\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(D_B(a,b)=\nu_b-\sum_{a'}x_{a'b}+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\).
An assignment is stable if every \((a,b)\in E\) satisfies \(D_A(a,b)=0\) or \(D_B(a,b)=0\). Let \(V(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\), and let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be its maximum over stable assignments.

Can a stable assignment satisfying \(V(x)\geq\frac{9}{13}\mathrm{OPT}\) be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For discrete maximum-cardinality weakly stable matching with one-sided ties and incomplete lists, a combinatorial algorithm achieves factor \(\frac{13}{9}\), tight for that algorithm ([Bauckholt–Pashkovich–Sanità](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05391)); another polynomial-time algorithm achieves \(1+\frac1e\) ([Lam–Plaxton](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1.9781611975482.175)).
Related fractional matching work classifies welfare and fully matched-agent objectives under ordinal and cardinal stability with unit capacities ([Chen–Roy–Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)); no result settling the approximation target above for arbitrary rational masses was located.

  • Every acceptable partner is strictly preferable to being unmatched, as in discrete weak stability.

Result

The requested guarantee is achievable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, the construction below guarantees

\(\displaystyle V(x)\ge \frac{7}{10}\mathrm{OPT}>\frac{9}{13}\mathrm{OPT}\)

whenever \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\).

The main ingredients are an additive bound for the loss caused by rounding masses, and an exact procedure for restoring the rounded-away masses without decreasing the computed matching volume.

1. Two established results used in the proof

We use the following results with their stated hypotheses.

  • Discrete approximation. For bipartite unit-capacity matching with incomplete lists, strict applicant preferences and position ties, Lam–Plaxton’s polynomial-time algorithm returns a weakly stable matching \(M\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |M|\ge \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}},\qquad \alpha=\frac{e}{e+1}.\)
[Lam–Plaxton](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~plaxton/pubs/2019/soda.pdf)

  • Strict stable allocations. For a finite bipartite allocation instance with strict preferences on both sides, positive rational vertex quotas and rational edge upper bounds, a stable allocation can be computed in polynomial time. Furthermore, any two stable allocations assign the same total mass to each vertex, hence have equal volume. These are the algorithmic result and Lemma 8 of the corrected Baïou–Balinski paper. [Baïou–Balinski](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302)

We verify the hypotheses when applying these results. All other reductions and estimates are proved below.

2. Aggregation, cloning, and integrality

2.1. Aggregating refined types preserves stability

Suppose each original type is split into finitely many subtypes whose masses sum to its original mass. Connect every applicant subtype of \(a\) to every position subtype of \(b\) whenever \((a,b)\in E\).

Require the refined preferences to preserve every original strict comparison. Comparisons within an original position type, and original position ties between applicant types, may be refined arbitrarily.

Claim. Every stable refined assignment aggregates to a stable original assignment, with the same volume.

Proof. Suppose its aggregate \(x\) had an acceptable pair \((a,b)\) with both \(D_A(a,b)>0\) and \(D_B(a,b)>0\).

The first inequality means that some applicant subtype \(u\) of \(a\) has positive unmatched mass or positive mass assigned to original position types strictly below \(b\). Consequently, for every position subtype \(v\) of \(b\), its refined demand for \(v\) is positive.

Similarly, the second inequality identifies a position subtype \(v\) of \(b\) with positive unmatched mass or positive mass assigned to original applicant types strictly below \(a\). Its refined demand for every applicant subtype of \(a\), including \(u\), is positive.

The pair \((u,v)\) is acceptable and has positive demand on both sides, contradicting refined stability. Aggregation preserves volume because it merely sums coordinates. \(\square\)

2.2. Integral stable assignments can be lifted to one-sided-ties matchings

Suppose all masses and all coordinates of a stable assignment \(x\) are integers.

Replace each applicant type \(a\) by \(\mu_a\) applicant copies, and each position type \(b\) by \(\nu_b\) position copies. Every acceptable type pair induces all corresponding copy pairs.

Applicant copies strictly rank position types as originally, and rank the copies within each position type by increasing index. Position copies retain the original total preorder over applicant types, tying all copies of originally tied types.

Claim. There is a weakly stable matching containing exactly \(x_{ab}\) pairs between copies of \(a\) and copies of \(b\).

Proof. For each \(b\), arrange its position copies so that copies assigned better applicant types come first, followed by copies assigned worse types, with unmatched copies last. Within a tie class, use any ordering. Assign distinct applicant copies to these slots in the prescribed numbers.

Consider a potential blocking pair consisting of a copy of \(a\) and a copy of \(b\).

If the applicant copy is unmatched or assigned a position type worse than \(b\), then \(D_A(a,b)>0\). Stability of \(x\) implies \(D_B(a,b)=0\): every copy of \(b\) is occupied by an applicant type weakly preferred to \(a\). Thus the position copy cannot strictly prefer the proposed applicant.

If the applicant copy is already assigned to another copy of \(b\), a position copy that strictly prefers it to its current partner must be occupied by a strictly worse applicant type, or be unmatched. By the slot ordering, that position copy has a larger index than the applicant’s current position copy. The applicant therefore prefers its current partner.

Finally, an applicant assigned a better position type does not want the proposed position copy. These cases exclude every blocking pair. \(\square\)

Together with aggregation, this establishes correspondence of stable matching volumes whenever the relevant assignment is integral.

2.3. Optimal stable assignments exist and respect a common mass grid

Let \(P\) be the feasible transportation polytope.

Because every summand in \(D_A(a,b)\) is nonnegative, the equality \(D_A(a,b)=0\) is equivalent to:

  • saturation of applicant \(a\)’s quota;
  • zero assignment to every position type strictly below \(b\).

Likewise, \(D_B(a,b)=0\) is equivalent to saturation of position \(b\)’s quota and zero assignment to all strictly worse applicant types.

For each acceptable edge, choose which of these two equalities will certify stability. The resulting constraints specify a face of \(P\): some quota inequalities are tight and some coordinates are zero. The stable set is exactly the finite union of these faces.

It is nonempty. Indeed, clear the rational mass denominators, form the copy instance, strictly refine all remaining position ties, and compute a strict stable matching. Its aggregate is stable by Section 2.1.

The stable set is therefore nonempty and compact, so volume attains its maximum.

If the masses are integers, \(P\) is integral. To see this, its quota matrix is the bipartite vertex-edge incidence matrix; multiplying the position rows by \(-1\) gives a directed incidence matrix, which is totally unimodular. Every face of an integral polytope is integral. Maximizing volume over a face containing an optimum consequently produces an integral optimum.

Thus:

Grid integrality. If all masses are integer multiples of a positive rational \(\delta\), there is an optimal stable assignment whose coordinates are integer multiples of \(\delta\).

In particular, arbitrary rational masses admit a rational optimal stable assignment.

3. A deletion bound for strict discrete matching

We need the following elementary fact.

Lemma. In a strict bipartite matching instance, deleting \(r\) vertices decreases the cardinality of a stable matching by at most \(r\).

Proof. First, all stable matchings of a fixed strict instance have equal cardinality.

Otherwise, their symmetric difference contains an augmenting path for the smaller matching. Starting at its unmatched applicant, stability forces the first position to prefer its partner in the smaller matching. Stability of the other matching then forces that applicant to prefer its next position along the path. These strict comparisons propagate along the path, until its final edge blocks the smaller matching because the final position is unmatched there. This is a contradiction.

Next, add one applicant to an instance with stable matching \(M\). Start a proposal process from \(M\), with the new applicant free. Positions retain their preferred proposal and reject the other; a displaced applicant continues below the partner that rejected it.

All initially occupied positions remain occupied, and position partners only improve. Initial stability certifies the rejections above each old applicant’s original partner; subsequent rejections remain valid because positions only improve. When the process terminates, every applicant has either a partner or has exhausted its list, and every preferred alternative has rejected it. The result is stable.

The resulting cardinality is either \(|M|\) or \(|M|+1\). Adding a position has the symmetric property.

Now consider deleting one vertex. Add it back to any stable matching of the smaller instance. The preceding construction increases cardinality by at most one. Equal cardinality of all stable matchings in the restored strict instance therefore shows that the deletion lost at most one. Repeat for the \(r\) deletions. \(\square\)

The insertion argument also proves that adding vertices to a strict instance admits a stable matching of at least the previous cardinality.

4. Capping masses at a polynomially computable scale

Write \(c_a=\mu_a\) and \(c_b=\nu_b\). Let

\(\displaystyle F=\max\left\{\sum_{(a,b)\in E}y_{ab}:y\text{ is feasible}\right\}.\)

This is an ordinary rational maximum-flow value, computable in polynomial time.

If \(F=0\), the zero assignment is stable and optimal: with positive supported masses, any acceptable edge would permit positive feasible volume. Hence assume \(F>0\).

4.1. Every stable assignment has volume at least \(F/2\)

For a stable assignment \(x\), let \(C\) contain all saturated applicant and position types.

Every acceptable edge has an endpoint in \(C\). Otherwise both endpoints have positive unmatched mass, making both demands positive.

For any feasible assignment \(y\),

\(\displaystyle V(y)\le \sum_{v\in C}c_v =\sum_{v\in C}\text{mass assigned to }v\text{ under }x \le 2V(x).\)

The first inequality holds because \(C\) covers every edge; the last holds because each assigned unit is counted at most twice. Maximizing over \(y\) gives

\(\displaystyle F\le 2V(x),\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad F\le 2\mathrm{OPT}.\)

4.2. Capping each mass at \(F\) preserves the stable set

Define \(\bar c_v=\min\{c_v,F\}\).

The feasible sets before and after capping are identical: every feasible assignment has total volume at most \(F\), so each individual assigned mass is at most \(F\).

An originally stable assignment remains stable after capping. Each demand can only decrease, remains nonnegative, and hence any demand previously equal to zero remains zero.

Conversely, suppose \(x\) is stable for the capped masses. Consider an edge certified by zero applicant demand.

If \(c_a\le F\), its original applicant demand is also zero. If \(c_a>F\), zero capped demand implies that \(a\) receives mass \(F\). Thus \(V(x)=F\), and all assigned mass comes from \(a\).

Every acceptable neighbor \(b\) of \(a\) must be saturated under its original quota. Otherwise, because \(a\) still has original unused mass, increasing \(x_{ab}\) would produce feasible volume greater than \(F\). Moreover, all mass at \(b\) comes from \(a\). Consequently, its original demand \(D_B(a,b)\) is zero.

An edge certified by zero position demand is handled symmetrically. Thus capped stability implies original stability.

The stable sets, and their optimum volumes, are identical.

5. Rounding causes only an additive loss

Set

\(\displaystyle \delta=\frac{F}{100\tau},\qquad n_v=\left\lfloor\frac{\bar c_v}{\delta}\right\rfloor,\qquad \widehat c_v=\delta n_v.\)

Let the total removed mass be

\(\displaystyle R=\sum_v(\bar c_v-\widehat c_v).\)

Then

\(\displaystyle 0\le R<\tau\delta=\frac{F}{100}\le\frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{50}.\)

Let \(\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\) denote the maximum stable volume for the rounded masses.

Claim.

\(\displaystyle \widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\ge \mathrm{OPT}-R \ge \frac{49}{50}\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Proof. Choose a rational optimal assignment for the capped instance. Take an integer \(Q\) clearing the denominators of its coordinates, the capped masses, and the rounded masses.

Lift this assignment to a weakly stable matching on unit copies after multiplication by \(Q\), using Section 2.2. Its cardinality is \(Q\mathrm{OPT}\).

Strictly refine every position copy’s ties by placing its current partner first within its tie class. This preserves stability: a newly created blocking pair would require a position to prefer a previously tied applicant to its current partner, which this refinement prevents. Unmatched positions may refine their ties arbitrarily.

Delete exactly \(Q(\bar c_v-\widehat c_v)\) copies of every original type \(v\). The total number deleted is \(QR\). Section 3 supplies a strict stable matching of cardinality at least

\(\displaystyle Q\mathrm{OPT}-QR.\)

Its preferences still preserve every original strict comparison. Aggregating it, with each copy representing mass \(1/Q\), gives a stable assignment for the rounded masses with volume at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-R\). \(\square\)

This large copy instance is used only to prove the estimate; the algorithm never constructs it.

6. Computing the rounded approximation with polynomially many copies

Construct the discrete instance with exactly \(n_v\) copies of type \(v\). Omit types with \(n_v=0\). Use the preferences from Section 2.2.

Since \(\bar c_v\le F\),

\(\displaystyle n_v\le \frac{F}{\delta}=100\tau,\qquad N:=\sum_v n_v\le 100\tau^2.\)

The instance has strict applicant preferences, position ties, incomplete lists, and unit capacities. Hence it satisfies Lam–Plaxton’s hypotheses.

Grid integrality and the lifting and aggregation results give the exact identity

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{disc}} =\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}}{\delta}.\)

Run Lam–Plaxton’s algorithm, obtaining a weakly stable matching \(M\). It satisfies

\(\displaystyle \delta|M|\ge \alpha\,\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}, \qquad \alpha=\frac{e}{e+1}.\)

It remains to restore the residual masses while maintaining exact stability.

7. Restoring residual masses without losing volume

7.1. Constructing a strict refined allocation instance

Strictly refine each old position copy’s ties by placing its partner in \(M\), if any, first within its tie class. As in Section 5, \(M\) remains stable.

Now regard each old applicant and position copy as a vertex of quota \(\delta\). Giving mass \(\delta\) to every matched edge of \(M\) defines a stable allocation \(x^0\) in this strict instance.

For each original type \(v\), add one residual vertex of quota

\(\displaystyle r_v=\bar c_v-\widehat c_v\)

if \(r_v>0\). Connect all refined vertices whose original types form an acceptable pair.

Extend the old strict preference lists while preserving their relative order. Place each new vertex within the appropriate original position-type block or original applicant tie class. Give new vertices arbitrary strict preferences that preserve all original strict comparisons.

Thus every refined preference list is strict, and all refined quotas belonging to original type \(v\) sum to \(\bar c_v\).

Assign every acceptable refined edge upper bound

\(\displaystyle U=1+\sum_v\bar c_v.\)

These edge bounds are inactive for every feasible assignment. The refined instance is finite, bipartite, and has strict preferences and positive rational quotas. Baïou–Balinski’s algorithm therefore computes a stable allocation \(y\).

7.2. Proof that restoration does not decrease volume

We prove

\(\displaystyle V(y)\ge V(x^0)=\delta|M|.\)

Choose an integer \(Q\) clearing all refined quota denominators. Expand every refined vertex into unit copies after multiplying its quota by \(Q\). Each copy ranks refined partner vertices by their strict order, then ranks copies within a partner vertex by increasing index. Both sides now have strict preferences.

Initially omit the residual vertices. Every old vertex has \(K=Q\delta\) copies. For each edge of \(M\), match the \(i\)-th applicant copy to the \(i\)-th position copy.

This matching is stable:

  • A blocking pair between different old matched pairs would project to a blocking pair for the strict matching \(M\).
  • Within one old matched pair, index-for-index matching is stable: if an applicant wants a smaller-index position copy, that position copy is matched to a smaller-index applicant whom it prefers.

Its cardinality is \(K|M|\).

Insert all residual unit copies, retaining the fixed strict preference orders. By the insertion result of Section 3, the augmented instance admits a stable matching with cardinality at least \(K|M|\). Aggregate it into a stable allocation \(z\) on the refined vertices, giving each unit copy mass \(1/Q\). Then

\(\displaystyle V(z)\ge \frac{K|M|}{Q}=\delta|M|.\)

Both \(y\) and \(z\) are stable allocations of the same strict refined instance. Equal volume of strict stable allocations consequently gives

\(\displaystyle V(y)=V(z)\ge\delta|M|.\)

Again, the large expansion is only an existence argument. The actual computation uses the polynomial-size refined allocation instance.

7.3. Returning an assignment for the original masses

Aggregate \(y\) by original types. Section 2.1 proves stability for the capped masses, and Section 4.2 proves stability for the original masses. Aggregation preserves volume, so the output \(x\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle V(x)\ge \delta|M|\ge \alpha\,\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}.\)

The temporary strict refinements serve only the computation; the returned aggregate satisfies the task’s original stability condition with unresolved position ties.

8. Approximation guarantee and running time

Combining the estimates gives

\(\displaystyle V(x)\ge \frac{e}{e+1}\frac{49}{50}\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Since

\(\displaystyle e>1+1+\frac12+\frac16>\frac52,\)

we have \(\alpha>5/7\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle V(x)\ge \frac57\frac{49}{50}\mathrm{OPT} =\frac7{10}\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Finally,

\(\displaystyle \frac7{10}>\frac9{13}\)

because \(91>90\). The case \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\) was already handled.

For the running time:

  • Rational maximum flow computes \(F\) in polynomial time.
  • The rounded discrete instance has at most \(100\tau^2\) vertices and polynomially many edges.
  • The restoration instance adds at most \(\tau\) vertices.
  • All constructed rational quotas and edge bounds have bit length polynomial in \(L\) and \(\log\tau\).
  • Both invoked algorithms run in polynomial time on these instances.

No denominator-clearing expansion used in a proof is constructed by the algorithm. Thus the actual running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), even when the original masses would require exponentially many unit copies.
