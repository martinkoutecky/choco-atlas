The continuous mirror question, as graded

Input finite male-type and female-type sets \(P\) and \(Q\), rational masses \(\mu^P\) and \(\mu^Q\) of equal total mass, strict rankings of opposite-side types, a target type matching \(\pi\) with \(\mu^P_p=\mu^Q_{\pi(p)}\), and budget \(B\). Choose female types \(S\subseteq Q\), paying \(\sum_{q\in S}\mu^Q_q\), and arbitrarily reorder every member of each selected type. Decide whether the target transport plan \(\nu^\star\), pairing all mass of \(p\) with \(\pi(p)\), is the unique stable transport plan, where stability forbids \(p,q\) with \(\sum_{q'\succ_p q}\nu_{p,q'}>0\) and \(\sum_{p'\succ_q p}\nu_{p',q}>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(m=|P|=|Q|\), \(\tau=|P|+|Q|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Also given are a bijection \(\pi:P\to Q\) satisfying \(\mu^P_p=\mu^Q_{\pi(p)}\) and a binary rational budget \(B\geq0\). The target is \(\nu^\star_{p,q}=\mu^P_p\mathbf{1}_{\{q=\pi(p)\}}\).
A transport plan is a nonnegative real matrix \(\nu\) with row sums \(\mu^P\) and column sums \(\mu^Q\). It is stable if no \((p,q)\) satisfies both \(\sum_{q':\,q\succ_p q'}\nu_{p,q'}>0\) and \(\sum_{p':\,p\succ_q p'}\nu_{p',q}>0\).
Choose \(S\subseteq Q\) with \(\sum_{q\in S}\mu^Q_q\leq B\), replacing each selected type's ranking by any one strict complete ranking shared by its entire mass. All other rankings remain fixed.
Decide whether such changes make \(\nu^\star\) the unique stable transport plan. For parameterization, define \(k=\lfloor B/\min_{q\in Q}\mu^Q_q\rfloor\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, with running times stated in \(m,\tau,L\)? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, is it FPT parameterized by \(k\), with time \(f(k)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\)?

For finite agents, making a specified matching uniquely stable by reordering preferences is NP-hard and \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard in the number of reordered agents, even with an initially stable target and changes confined to women; an XP algorithm runs in \(O(2^\ell n^{2\ell+2})\). Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete and is \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by \(k\), even when every mass equals \(1\) and the target is initially stable. Consequently, an algorithm running in \(f(k)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) would imply \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[2]\).

For arbitrary input masses, there is an XP algorithm. Writing \(K=\min\{k,m\}\), one valid bit-complexity bound is

\(O\!\left(\left(\sum_{r=0}^{K}\binom mr\right)\left[m^2\log(2m)+(\tau+L)^3\right]\right).\)

The key structural result is that, under the paired-mass hypothesis, uniqueness of the target transport is equivalent to uniqueness of the corresponding finite matching. The proof below also gives a polynomial-time algorithm when the set of women whose rankings may change is specified.

1. Definitions and target stability

Index the types so that \(\pi(p_i)=q_i\), and write \(w_i=\mu^P_{p_i}=\mu^Q_{q_i}>0\). Thus the target transport has entries \(\nu^\star_{i,i}=w_i\), with all other entries zero.

Let \(M=\{(p_i,q_i):1\leq i\leq m\}\) denote the corresponding finite matching.

At the target, the male dissatisfaction in a pair \((p_i,q_j)\) is positive precisely when \(q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i\). The female dissatisfaction is positive precisely when \(p_i\succ_{q_j}p_j\). Positivity of every \(w_i\) is used here. Therefore:

Target-stability equivalence. The transport \(\nu^\star\) is stable if and only if \(M\) is stable as a finite matching.

For the remainder of this section, assume that the target is stable.

Define the male successor \(s_P(i)\) to be the most preferred woman \(q_j\) satisfying

\(q_i\succ_{p_i}q_j\quad\text{and}\quad p_i\succ_{q_j}p_j.\)

If no such woman exists, the successor is undefined. Define the female successor \(s_Q(j)\) symmetrically: it is the most preferred man \(p_i\) satisfying

\(p_j\succ_{q_j}p_i\quad\text{and}\quad q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i.\)

Construct two directed graphs on the indices \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\). The male graph has an arc \(i\to j\) when \(s_P(i)=q_j\); the female graph has an arc \(j\to i\) when \(s_Q(j)=p_i\). Each vertex has at most one outgoing arc.

2. Uniqueness is independent of the paired masses

Proposition. For every choice of positive paired masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_m\), the target transport is uniquely stable if and only if it is stable and both successor graphs are acyclic.

2.1. A successor cycle produces another stable transport

Suppose the male graph contains a cycle \(i_1\to i_2\to\cdots\to i_r\to i_1\). Thus \(s_P(i_a)=q_{i_{a+1}}\), with indices interpreted cyclically.

Choose \(0<\varepsilon<\min_a w_{i_a}\). Starting from \(\nu^\star\), subtract \(\varepsilon\) from each entry \((i_a,i_a)\) and add \(\varepsilon\) to each entry \((i_a,i_{a+1})\). Call the resulting transport \(\widehat\nu\).

Every affected row loses and gains the same amount. Every affected column also loses and gains the same amount. Hence \(\widehat\nu\) is feasible and differs from \(\nu^\star\).

Every woman receives only her target partner and, possibly, a man she prefers to that partner. Every affected man receives his target partner and his male successor, which he likes less.

Suppose a pair \((p_i,q_j)\) could block \(\widehat\nu\). Male dissatisfaction requires \(q_j\) to be preferred to some supported partner of \(p_i\). There are three possible cases:

  • If \(q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i\), target stability implies that \(q_j\) prefers her target partner to \(p_i\). All her supported partners are at least as good as her target partner, so female dissatisfaction is zero.
  • If \(j=i\), all supported partners of \(q_i\) are at least as good as \(p_i\), so female dissatisfaction is zero.
  • Otherwise, \(p_i\) must belong to the cycle, and \(q_j\) must lie strictly between \(q_i\) and \(s_P(i)\) in his ranking. By the definition of the first successor, \(q_j\) does not prefer \(p_i\) to her target partner. Strictness again implies zero female dissatisfaction.

These cases exhaust the possibilities. Thus \(\widehat\nu\) is stable.

A female successor cycle gives another stable transport by the same construction with the two sides exchanged.

2.2. Another stable transport produces a successor cycle

Let \(\nu\neq\nu^\star\) be stable. Define

\(A_P=\{i:\nu_{i,i}<w_i\text{ and every supported partner of }p_i\text{ is weakly preferred to }q_i\},\)

and define \(A_Q\) analogously for women.

At least one of these sets is nonempty. Indeed, choose an off-diagonal positive entry \(\nu_{i,j}>0\).

  • If \(q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i\), target stability gives \(p_j\succ_{q_j}p_i\). Consequently, the female dissatisfaction at \((p_j,q_j)\) is positive in \(\nu\). Stability forces every supported partner of \(p_j\) to be weakly preferred to \(q_j\). Moreover, \(\nu_{j,j}<w_j\), since column \(j\) contains positive off-diagonal mass. Hence \(j\in A_P\).
  • If \(q_i\succ_{p_i}q_j\), male dissatisfaction at \((p_i,q_i)\) is positive. Stability forces every supported partner of \(q_i\) to be weakly preferred to \(p_i\). Since \(\nu_{i,i}<w_i\), we obtain \(i\in A_Q\).

Suppose first that \(A_P\neq\varnothing\).

If \(i\in A_P\) and \(\nu_{i,j}>0\) with \(j\neq i\), strictness gives \(q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i\). The preceding argument then gives \(j\in A_P\). Thus all mass leaving rows indexed by \(A_P\) enters columns indexed by \(A_P\).

The total mass of these rows and columns is the same, namely \(\sum_{i\in A_P}w_i\). Therefore no positive mass enters those columns from rows outside \(A_P\).

Fix \(j\in A_P\). Since \(\nu_{j,j}<w_j\), there is an off-diagonal supported pair \((p_h,q_j)\). Necessarily \(h\in A_P\), and hence \(q_j\succ_{p_h}q_h\). Target stability gives \(p_j\succ_{q_j}p_h\). Thus \(p_h\) is a candidate for \(s_Q(j)\), so that successor exists.

Write \(s_Q(j)=p_t\). If \(t=h\), then \(t\in A_P\). Otherwise, the choice of the most preferred candidate gives \(p_t\succ_{q_j}p_h\). The supported mass at \((p_h,q_j)\) makes female dissatisfaction at \((p_t,q_j)\) positive. Stability of \(\nu\) therefore forces every supported partner of \(p_t\) to be weakly preferred to \(q_j\). Because \(p_t\) is a successor candidate, \(q_j\succ_{p_t}q_t\). Consequently, every supported partner of \(p_t\) is strictly preferred to his target partner, and \(t\in A_P\).

We have proved that every vertex of the nonempty finite set \(A_P\) has a female-successor arc to another vertex of \(A_P\). Following these arcs must eventually repeat a vertex, producing a directed cycle.

If instead \(A_Q\neq\varnothing\), the identical argument with the sides exchanged produces a cycle in the male successor graph. This proves the proposition.

2.3. Equivalence with finite-matching uniqueness

For completeness, stability is inherited when positive support is reduced: a blocking pair for the smaller support would have a worse supported partner on each side, and those same partners would witness blocking for the larger support.

A successor cycle therefore also produces another stable finite matching: rotate the target partners around the cycle. Its edges are contained in the support of the stable transport constructed in Section 2.1.

Conversely, any stable finite matching different from \(M\) is a stable transport when all paired masses equal \(1\). Section 2.2 then produces a successor cycle.

It follows that, for every fixed preference profile,

\(\nu^\star\text{ is uniquely stable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M\text{ is the unique stable finite matching}.\)

This statement concerns uniqueness of the specified target; it does not assert that arbitrary stable transports are integral.

3. Polynomial verification and hardness

Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate specifies the selected women and their replacement rankings. Its size is polynomial in the input length.

The verifier checks the rational budget, checks target stability, constructs both successor graphs, and tests them for directed cycles. Section 2 proves that this verifies uniqueness among all real-valued feasible transports.

The preference checks take \(O(m^2)\) comparisons after rank preprocessing. Exact rational arithmetic is polynomial in \(L\). Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Hardness reduction

Use the women-only restriction of Exact-Unique-Reorder: given a finite stable-marriage instance, a specified complete matching, and budget \(\ell\), decide whether reordering at most \(\ell\) women makes that matching uniquely stable. The cited paper establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness and \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness parameterized by \(\ell\), even with an initially stable target. [Boehmer, Bredereck, Heeger, and Niedermeier, Theorem 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.04948#S5.SS1)

Create one type per agent, preserve the rankings and target bijection, give every type mass \(1\), and set \(B=\ell\). Then \(T=m\), \(\tau=2m\), and \(k=\ell\).

Selecting women has exactly the original cardinality cost. By Section 2.3, every modified profile makes the target finite matching uniquely stable if and only if it makes the target transport uniquely stable.

The construction has polynomial size and preserves the parameter. Hence the transport problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard parameterized by \(k\). Together with membership, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

4. A polynomial algorithm for a specified set of women

Fix a set \(S\subseteq Q\) whose rankings may be replaced. We now decide in polynomial time whether some replacements succeed.

First reject if the target has a blocking pair involving a woman outside \(S\). Such a pair cannot be removed because both relevant rankings remain fixed.

Introduce a sink vertex \(t\). Construct two graphs of allowed successors on \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\cup\{t\}\).

4.1. Allowed female successors

For each woman \(q_j\), let

\(C_j=\{i:q_j\succ_{p_i}q_i\}.\)

These are exactly the men who prefer her to their target partners.

If \(q_j\in S\), target stability requires her to place her own partner above every member of \(C_j\). Therefore:

  • If \(C_j=\varnothing\), add only \(j\to t\).
  • Otherwise, add \(j\to i\) for every \(i\in C_j\).

Each candidate can be made her female successor by placing it immediately after her own partner.

If \(q_j\notin S\), her successor is fixed. Add its arc, or add \(j\to t\) if it is undefined. Call this graph \(H_Q\).

4.2. Allowed male successors

For each man \(p_i\), let \(b_i\) be his most preferred unselected woman satisfying

\(q_i\succ_{p_i}b_i\quad\text{and}\quad p_i\succ_{b_i}\pi^{-1}(b_i).\)

If no such woman exists, set \(b_i=t\). Add the corresponding fixed arc from \(i\).

For every selected woman \(q_j\), also add \(i\to j\) when

\(q_i\succ_{p_i}q_j\)

and either \(b_i=t\) or \(q_j\succ_{p_i}b_i\).

These are exactly the selected women who can become his first successor before the earliest fixed candidate. Call this graph \(H_P\).

4.3. The reachability criterion

In a graph of allowed successors, one can choose one outgoing arc per non-sink vertex and obtain an acyclic graph if and only if every vertex can reach \(t\).

For necessity, following chosen arcs in a finite acyclic graph must terminate at the only vertex without a chosen outgoing arc, namely \(t\). For sufficiency, choose an arc that decreases shortest-path distance to \(t\).

Therefore the fixed-set algorithm accepts exactly when every vertex reaches \(t\) in both \(H_P\) and \(H_Q\).

To prove that the two choices can be realized simultaneously, choose acyclic outgoing arcs in both graphs. For each selected woman \(q_j\), replace her ranking as follows:

  • Put above \(p_j\) exactly those men whose chosen male-successor arc points to \(j\).
  • Put \(p_j\) next.
  • If her chosen female successor is \(p_h\), put \(p_h\) immediately afterward.
  • Put all remaining men afterward in any strict order.

Every man placed above \(p_j\) prefers his own target partner to \(q_j\), by the definition of \(H_P\). Thus these placements cannot block the target. A chosen female successor belongs to \(C_j\), so it cannot conflict with an above-partner placement.

The resulting female successors are exactly the chosen ones. The male successors are also exactly the chosen ones: each man is placed above the target partner of at most his chosen selected successor, and that successor precedes every fixed candidate.

Both actual successor graphs are therefore acyclic, and Section 2 proves uniqueness.

Conversely, any successful replacements supply actual successor arcs contained in \(H_P\) and \(H_Q\). Their acyclicity implies the required reachability. This proves correctness.

Both graphs have \(O(m^2)\) arcs and can be constructed and checked in polynomial time.

5. Running times and parameterized conclusion

Every affordable selection satisfies

\(|S|\min_{q\in Q}\mu^Q_q\leq\sum_{q\in S}\mu^Q_q\leq B,\)

so \(|S|\leq k\).

Enumerate all subsets of at most \(K=\min\{k,m\}\) women, discard those exceeding the rational budget, and apply Section 4 to each remaining subset.

A conservative bit-complexity bound is

\(O\!\left(\left(\sum_{r=0}^{K}\binom mr\right)\left[m^2\log(2m)+(\tau+L)^3\right]\right).\)

Here the cubic term permits straightforward exact arithmetic on the binary rational input. In particular, this is bounded by

\(O\!\left((K+1)m^K\left[m^2\log(2m)+(\tau+L)^3\right]\right),\)

and unrestricted enumeration gives \(2^m(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) time. Recall that this problem has \(\tau=2m\).

Thus the problem is in XP parameterized by \(k\). Its \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hardness rules out an FPT algorithm under the standard assumption \(\mathrm{FPT}\neq\mathrm{W}[2]\); an unconditional separation is not claimed.
