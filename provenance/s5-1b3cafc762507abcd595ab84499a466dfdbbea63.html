The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given three candidates \(p,a,b\), binary-rational masses of fixed-vote types \((r,w)\) and manipulator types \(w\), with total mass \(1\), ask whether the manipulators’ mass can be assigned among the \(15\) nonempty top-truncated ballots so that \(p\) is a co-winner under Borda-down. Let \(x_{w,r}\geq 0\) be the mass of weight-\(w\) manipulators assigned ballot \(r\), constrained by \(\sum_r x_{w,r}=\nu_w\). A complete ballot scores \((2,1,0)\) by rank, a two-candidate ballot scores its listed candidates \(2,1\) and the unlisted candidate \(0\), and a singleton ballot scores its listed candidate \(1\) and the others \(0\). Compute whether an assignment makes each resulting weighted score of \(p\) at least the corresponding score of \(a\) and \(b\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C=\{p,a,b\}\), so \(m=3\). Let \(R\) be the \(15\) strict rankings of nonempty subsets of \(C\); omitted candidates are tied below listed candidates.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational mass. Here types are fixed voters \((r,w)\) with masses \(\mu_{r,w}\), or manipulators \(w\) with masses \(\nu_w\), where \(r\in R\) and \(w\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\) is binary encoded. All masses together sum to \(1\). The support size \(\tau\) counts supplied types, not possible ballots; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Under Borda-down, a complete ballot assigns scores \((2,1,0)\) by rank; a two-candidate ballot assigns \((2,1)\) to its listed candidates and \(0\) to the omitted candidate; a singleton assigns \(1\) to its listed candidate and \(0\) to the others. Write \(s_r(c)\) for this score.
Manipulator mass is divisible: choose \(x_{w,r}\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) with \(\sum_{r\in R}x_{w,r}=\nu_w\) for every manipulator type. Fixed ballots remain unchanged. The resulting score is \(S(c)=\sum_{(r,w)}\mu_{r,w}w s_r(c)+\sum_w\sum_{r\in R}x_{w,r}w s_r(c)\).

Is deciding whether such an assignment satisfies \(S(p)\geq S(a)\) and \(S(p)\geq S(b)\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(m=3\) fixed.

For indivisible voters with nonnegative integer weights, constructive coalitional manipulation under three-candidate Borda-down is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when all fixed ballots are complete ([Menon–Larson](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/papers/MenonLarson17JAAMAS.pdf)). No result settling this exact divisible-mass problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The weight domain was unspecified; weights are binary-encoded nonnegative integers, matching the discrete definition, including zero.
  • Assignment values are nonnegative real masses, making divisibility explicit.
  • Fixed-vote types may use any of the same nonempty top-truncated ballots, matching the discrete manipulation problem.

Result

The divisible-mass problem is in \(\mathrm P\). In fact, three rational inequalities characterize feasibility exactly.

Define the fixed scores, fixed deficits, and total weighted manipulator mass by

\(F_c=\sum_{(r,w)}\mu_{r,w}w s_r(c),\qquad d_a=F_a-F_p,\qquad d_b=F_b-F_p,\qquad W=\sum_w\nu_w w.\)

Then a successful assignment exists if and only if

\(\boxed{d_a\leq 2W,\qquad d_b\leq 2W,\qquad d_a+d_b\leq 3W.}\)

For the specified \(m=3\), these conditions can be checked using \(O(m\tau)=O(\tau)\) exact arithmetic operations and \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations with schoolbook integer arithmetic.

1. Two complete ballots suffice

For a ballot \(r\), define its contribution to the two score margins by

\(\Delta(r)=\bigl(s_r(p)-s_r(a),\,s_r(p)-s_r(b)\bigr).\)

Consider the complete ballots

\(r_A=(p\succ a\succ b),\qquad r_B=(p\succ b\succ a).\)

Their margin vectors are

\(\Delta(r_A)=(1,2),\qquad \Delta(r_B)=(2,1).\)

Every allowed ballot is dominated coordinatewise by one of these two ballots:

  • If \(p\) is first on a ballot listing at least two candidates, its scores are \(2\) for \(p\), \(1\) for one opponent, and \(0\) for the other. Its margin vector therefore equals \(\Delta(r_A)\) or \(\Delta(r_B)\).
  • In every other case, \(s_r(p)\leq 1\). Since both opponents receive nonnegative scores, both coordinates of \(\Delta(r)\) are at most \(1\). Thus \(\Delta(r)\) is dominated by \(\Delta(r_A)=(1,2)\).

Replace each portion of manipulator mass by a dominating ballot, keeping its weight and mass unchanged. Because every weight is nonnegative, neither total score margin decreases. The mass constraint for each manipulator type is preserved.

Consequently, a successful assignment exists exactly when one exists using only \(r_A\) and \(r_B\). This argument covers all \(15\) ballots, including singleton ballots and zero-weight types.

2. Exact feasibility characterization

In an assignment using these two ballots, let

\(t=\sum_w w x_{w,r_A}\)

be the weighted manipulator mass assigned to \(r_A\). The weighted mass assigned to \(r_B\) is \(W-t\), so necessarily \(0\leq t\leq W\).

The manipulators contribute scores

\(S_{\mathrm{man}}(p)=2W,\qquad S_{\mathrm{man}}(a)=t,\qquad S_{\mathrm{man}}(b)=W-t.\)

Therefore the two winning conditions are equivalent to

\(F_p+2W\geq F_a+t,\qquad F_p+2W\geq F_b+W-t,\)

or, after rearrangement,

\(t\leq 2W-d_a,\qquad t\geq d_b-W.\)

Thus a successful assignment requires

\(\max\{0,d_b-W\}\leq t\leq\min\{W,2W-d_a\}.\)

This interval is nonempty exactly when each lower endpoint is at most each upper endpoint. The four comparisons are

\(0\leq W,\qquad 0\leq 2W-d_a,\qquad d_b-W\leq W,\qquad d_b-W\leq 2W-d_a.\)

The first holds because \(W\geq0\). The remaining three are precisely

\(d_a\leq2W,\qquad d_b\leq2W,\qquad d_a+d_b\leq3W.\)

It remains to prove that every point in the interval is realizable under the individual type constraints.

3. Constructing a successful assignment

Suppose the three inequalities hold.

If \(W=0\), they imply \(d_a\leq0\) and \(d_b\leq0\). Moreover, every manipulator type has weight zero: each has positive supplied mass, weights are nonnegative, and their weighted sum is zero. Assigning every manipulator to \(r_A\) therefore leaves the scores unchanged and succeeds.

Now suppose \(W>0\). Choose

\(t=\max\{0,d_b-W\},\qquad \lambda=t/W.\)

By the interval characterization, \(0\leq t\leq W\), hence \(0\leq\lambda\leq1\). For every manipulator type \(w\), set

\(x_{w,r_A}=\lambda\nu_w,\qquad x_{w,r_B}=(1-\lambda)\nu_w,\)

and set all its other ballot masses to zero.

These masses are nonnegative and sum to \(\nu_w\). Their weighted mass on \(r_A\) is exactly

\(\sum_w w x_{w,r_A}=\lambda\sum_w w\nu_w=\lambda W=t.\)

The winning inequalities now follow from the chosen interval. All quantities in this construction are rational, so allowing real-valued assignments introduces no additional feasibility issue.

This proves both necessity and sufficiency.

4. Running time and input encoding

Computing all \(m\) fixed scores and \(W\) requires \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations. For \(m=3\), the final test uses only three additional comparisons.

Here is an explicit polynomial bit bound. Write each supplied mass as \(u_i/v_i\), with binary-encoded integers and \(v_i>0\), and form the common denominator

\(D=\prod_i v_i.\)

The total bit length of these denominators is at most \(L\), so \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits. Using \(D\), compute the integer numerators of \(d_a,d_b,W\) by summing terms of the form

\(u_i w_i\bigl(s_{r_i}(c)-s_{r_i}(p)\bigr)(D/v_i)\)

for fixed types, and \(u_i w_i(D/v_i)\) for manipulator types.

Each intermediate integer has \(O(L)\) bits: the scores are bounded by \(2\), the masses sum to \(1\), and every weight has at most \(L\) bits. Each exact division or multiplication consequently takes \(O(L^2)\) bit operations using schoolbook algorithms. There are \(O(\tau)\) such operations, giving the bound

\(\boxed{O(\tau L^2)\text{ bit operations for }m=3.}\)

The support size remains the number of supplied types. Neither weights nor masses are expanded into individual voters.

5. Why indivisible hardness does not transfer

The cited indivisible weighted-manipulation hardness concerns choosing ballots for whole voters. [Menon–Larson, Theorem 2](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/papers/MenonLarson17JAAMAS.pdf).

Here divisibility permits every weighted split \(t\in[0,W]\), as the construction above proves. An indivisible model can restrict achievable splits to a discrete set. Our algorithm relies exactly on removing that restriction.
