The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of complete types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), groups \(m_t\), scores \(s_t\), per-capita capacity \(q\), increasing \(h\), and rational piecewise-linear concave utilities \(u_m\), compute an allocation \(a_t\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t=q\) that maximizes \(W(a)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_th(s_t)+\sum_{m\in M}u_m(x_m)\), where \(x_m=\sum_{t:m_t=m}\mu_ta_t\), and provide a certificate \(\pi_t\in h(s_t)+\partial u_{m_t}(x_{m_t})\) implementing the allocation by adaptive priorities.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each complete type consists of a group \(m_t\in M\) and a rational score \(s_t\in[0,1]\); supported scores are pairwise distinct, and \(m=|M|\ge1\).
The capacity is rational \(q\in(0,1)\). A continuous, strictly increasing \(h:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) is specified by its binary-rational values at supported scores and both endpoints; only these values are used. Each \(u_j:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is continuous, concave and piecewise linear, explicitly given by rational breakpoints, slopes and intercepts, including its two tails. Slopes and utility values may have either sign; assume \(h(0)+u'_{j,-}(q)\ge0\) for every group. Let \(K\) be the total number of utility pieces and \(L\) the total binary input length.
An allocation splits each type's mass using \(a_t\in[0,1]\), with \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=q\). Write \(x_j=\sum_{t:m_t=j}\mu_ta_t\) and \(W(a)=\sum_t\mu_ta_th(s_t)+\sum_{j\in M}u_j(x_j)\).
An implementing adaptive-priority certificate consists of a common marginal value \(d_j\in\partial u_j(x_j)\) for each group and a cutoff \(\lambda\), giving \(\pi_t=h(s_t)+d_{m_t}\). Here \(\partial\) denotes the concave superdifferential. Implementation means \(a_t>0\Rightarrow\pi_t\ge\lambda\) and \(a_t<1\Rightarrow\pi_t\le\lambda\); mass at the cutoff may be divided arbitrarily.

Can an exact welfare-maximizing allocation together with an implementing adaptive-priority certificate be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For discrete agents, adaptive priorities based on \(h(s)+u_j(k)-u_j(k-1)\), with rejected agents evaluated after their admission, implement only optimal allocations. [Discrete optimality result](https://joelflynn.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/APM_Discrete_Note.pdf)
For atomless score distributions and differentiable concave diversity utilities, optimal adaptive-priority mechanisms are characterized. These results do not establish the binary-input complexity of the finite-support problem stated here. [Continuum characterization](https://joelflynn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Adaptive_Priority_Mechanisms_March2026_Post.pdf)

  • Normalized total society mass to one and restricted capacity to a rational strictly between zero and one, following the single-authority continuum model.
  • Defined complete types as score-group pairs, restricted scores to the unit interval, and retained the discrete model's distinct-score assumption across supported types.
  • Made h continuous, strictly increasing and nonnegative; supplied its relevant values as binary rationals rather than leaving an arbitrary real function without an input encoding.
  • Specified utilities on the real line by explicit rational piecewise-linear descriptions with linear tails; allowed signed values and slopes.
  • Carried over the preference for filling capacity using the left derivative at capacity, the natural boundary convention for piecewise-linear utilities.
  • Interpreted the certificate's derivative notation as the concave superdifferential and required one shared marginal value per group.
  • Defined priority implementation by a cutoff with arbitrary division of tied mass, accommodating positive-mass types and fractional admission.
  • Made computation exact and included the total utility-piece count and binary input length in the running-time parameters.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. An exact welfare-maximizing allocation and a rational implementing adaptive-priority certificate can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\).

The proof uses a rational linear program for welfare and extracts the certificate from its dual.

1. Representing the concave utilities

For group \(j\), let its \(k_j\) utility pieces have affine formulas
\(\ell_{jr}(x)=\alpha_{jr}x+\beta_{jr}\), where \(\sum_j k_j=K\). Include both tails.

Concavity implies that the slopes of successive pieces are nonincreasing. Consequently, every affine piece, extended to the whole real line, is a global upper bound on \(u_j\). Indeed, starting at an interior point of that piece, slopes to its right are at most its slope, and slopes to its left are at least its slope; summing the changes across the intervening pieces proves the upper-bound inequality in either direction. Continuity extends it to breakpoints.

At every point, at least one piece agrees with the utility. Therefore
\(\displaystyle u_j(x)=\min_{1\le r\le k_j}\{\alpha_{jr}x+\beta_{jr}\}\)
for all \(x\in\mathbb R\).

This representation uses the given rational coefficients.

2. An exact welfare linear program

Write \(c_t=h(s_t)\) and use admitted masses \(y_t=\mu_ta_t\). Define
\(\displaystyle x_j(y)=\sum_{t:m_t=j}y_t\).
Because every \(\mu_t\) is positive, allocations and admitted-mass vectors correspond bijectively through \(a_t=y_t/\mu_t\).

Consider the linear program with variables \(y_t\) and unrestricted real variables \(z_j\):

\(\displaystyle \text{maximize}\quad P(y,z)=\sum_t c_ty_t+\sum_j z_j\),

subject to

  • \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\) for every supported type;
  • \(\sum_t y_t=q\);
  • \(z_j\le\alpha_{jr}x_j(y)+\beta_{jr}\) for every group and utility piece.

For fixed feasible \(y\), the largest permitted value of \(z_j\) is exactly \(u_j(x_j(y))\), by Section 1. Since its objective coefficient is positive, every optimal solution satisfies
\(z_j=u_j(x_j(y))\).
Thus the linear program is exactly the welfare-maximization problem.

It is feasible: take \(y_t=q\mu_t\) and \(z_j=u_j(x_j(y))\). The mass constraints hold because \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(0<q<1\).

Its optimum is finite and attained. The feasible admitted-mass vectors form a nonempty compact set, and welfare is continuous on that set.

3. The dual program

Associate nonnegative dual variables \(p_{jr}\) with the utility-piece inequalities, nonnegative variables \(v_t\) with the upper mass bounds, and an unrestricted variable \(\lambda\) with capacity.

The dual is

\(\displaystyle \text{minimize}\quad D(p,v,\lambda)=\sum_{j,r}\beta_{jr}p_{jr}+\sum_t\mu_tv_t+q\lambda\),

subject to

  • \(p_{jr}\ge0\) and \(v_t\ge0\);
  • \(\sum_r p_{jr}=1\) for every group;
  • \(\lambda+v_t\ge c_t+\sum_r\alpha_{m_t r}p_{m_t r}\) for every supported type.

To verify the signs and constraints, relax the utility inequalities, upper bounds, and capacity equality while retaining \(y_t\ge0\) and unrestricted \(z_j\). The resulting Lagrangian is

\(\displaystyle \mathcal L =D(p,v,\lambda) +\sum_j z_j\Bigl(1-\sum_rp_{jr}\Bigr) +\sum_t y_t\Bigl(c_t+\sum_r\alpha_{m_t r}p_{m_t r}-\lambda-v_t\Bigr). \)

Its supremum over these retained domains is finite precisely when the displayed dual constraints hold, and then equals \(D\).

Since the primal is feasible and has a finite optimum, linear-programming strong duality supplies an attained dual optimum with the same value. This is the standard strong-duality theorem; see [Goemans, Theorem 4.4](https://math.mit.edu/~shor/18.310/lpnotes310.pdf).

Choose optimal primal and dual solutions and define
\(\displaystyle d_j=\sum_r\alpha_{jr}p_{jr}\).

We next prove that these values and the dual variable \(\lambda\) give the requested certificate.

4. The group marginals are valid supergradients

For any feasible primal-dual pair, define
\(w_t=\lambda+v_t-c_t-d_{m_t}\), which is nonnegative by dual feasibility. Direct expansion gives the gap identity

\(\displaystyle D-P =\sum_{j,r}p_{jr}\bigl(\alpha_{jr}x_j+\beta_{jr}-z_j\bigr) +\sum_t v_t(\mu_t-y_t) +\sum_t y_tw_t. \)

Every summand is nonnegative. At optimality, strong duality makes their sum zero, so each summand vanishes.

In particular, if \(p_{jr}>0\), then
\(\alpha_{jr}x_j+\beta_{jr}=z_j=u_j(x_j)\).
That affine line globally majorizes \(u_j\), so for every \(\xi\in\mathbb R\),

\(\displaystyle u_j(\xi)\le u_j(x_j)+\alpha_{jr}(\xi-x_j).\)

Multiply these inequalities by their nonnegative weights \(p_{jr}\) and sum. Because \(\sum_rp_{jr}=1\), this yields

\(\displaystyle u_j(\xi)\le u_j(x_j)+d_j(\xi-x_j).\)

By the definition of the concave superdifferential, this proves \(d_j\in\partial u_j(x_j)\). The argument includes breakpoints and groups with no supported types.

5. The cutoff implements the allocation

Set \(a_t=y_t/\mu_t\) and \(\pi_t=c_t+d_{m_t}\).

If \(a_t>0\), then \(y_t>0\). The equality \(y_tw_t=0\) forces \(w_t=0\), and hence
\(\pi_t=\lambda+v_t\ge\lambda\).

If \(a_t<1\), then \(y_t<\mu_t\). The equality \(v_t(\mu_t-y_t)=0\) forces \(v_t=0\). Dual feasibility then gives
\(\pi_t\le\lambda\).

These are exactly the two required implementation implications. In particular, \(0<a_t<1\) implies \(\pi_t=\lambda\).

For completeness, these certificate conditions also directly certify welfare optimality. Let \(\widetilde a\) be any feasible allocation, with admitted masses \(\widetilde y_t\). The supergradient inequalities give

\(\displaystyle W(\widetilde a)-W(a) \le\sum_t\pi_t(\widetilde y_t-y_t) =\sum_t(\pi_t-\lambda)(\widetilde y_t-y_t), \)

where the last equality uses equal total admitted mass. Each summand is nonpositive:

  • when \(y_t=0\), its first factor is nonpositive and its second is nonnegative;
  • when \(y_t=\mu_t\), its first factor is nonnegative and its second is nonpositive;
  • when \(0<y_t<\mu_t\), its first factor is zero.

Thus \(W(\widetilde a)\le W(a)\).

6. Polynomial binary-input complexity

The primal has \(\tau+m\) variables, \(K+2\tau\) inequalities, and one equality. Its coefficients are input rationals or the constants \(0,1,-1\). Even explicitly expanding every group sum creates only polynomially many coefficient entries. The dual likewise has polynomial size.

We use the standard exact rational linear-programming theorem: a rational linear program can be solved in time polynomial in its binary encoding length, returning an exact rational optimum whenever the optimum is finite. Apply it separately to the primal and dual. Exact recovery, rather than only approximation, is part of this theorem; see the [MIT notes on polynomial-time LP algorithms](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.854/18/Notes/n14-LP_algorithms.html).

There is also no output-size obstruction. After conversion to standard form, optimal basic feasible solutions exist. Clearing denominators produces integer coefficients of polynomial bit length. Cramer’s rule expresses each basic coordinate as a ratio of determinants; for an \(n\)-dimensional integer matrix with entries of at most \(B\) bits, each determinant has \(O(nB+n\log n)\) bits. Both \(n\) and \(B\) are polynomially bounded here.

Finally, computing \(a_t=y_t/\mu_t\) and \(d_j=\sum_r\alpha_{jr}p_{jr}\) uses polynomially many exact rational operations and produces polynomial-length outputs. The cutoff \(\lambda\) is already a dual coordinate.

The resulting algorithm therefore computes both requested objects exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L\). No sign restriction on utility slopes or values is needed for this proof, so it applies in particular under the stated additional assumption.
