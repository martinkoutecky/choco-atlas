The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(B,p,\varepsilon>0\) and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(\alpha_t,\delta_t)\), with rational \(0<\alpha_t,\delta_t<1\) and common share success probability \(p\), compute a finite-support rational payout sequence \(x_i\ge0\), \(\sum_i x_i\le B\), whose welfare \(p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{i\ge0}\delta_t^i x_i^{\alpha_t}\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the supremum over feasible payout sequences.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Types are \(t=(\alpha_t,\delta_t)\), with rational \(0<\alpha_t,\delta_t<1\), and masses satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).
Inputs also include binary rationals \(B,\varepsilon>0\) and a common share success probability \(0<p\le1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; there is no candidate-count parameter \(m\).
A fixed payout rule assigns the same amount \(x_i\) to a share whenever a successful share occurs \(i\) submissions later, including its own success at \(i=0\). Successes are independent with probability \(p\); payouts are amounts, and the rule cannot depend on type or history.
Feasible rules are real sequences \(x=(x_i)_{i\ge0}\) with \(x_i\ge0\) and \(\sum_{i\ge0}x_i\le B\). Define \(W(x)=p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{i\ge0}\delta_t^i x_i^{\alpha_t}\), with \(0^{\alpha_t}=0\), and \(W^*=\sup_{x\text{ feasible}}W(x)\).
The computational problem is to output a feasible finite-support rational rule satisfying \(W(x)\ge W^*-\varepsilon\), encoded as a list of nonzero entries with binary indices and binary rational amounts; all omitted entries are zero.

Is this additive approximation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For the original homogeneous model, an optimal geometric fixed payout rule is steady-state optimal among pure pools; this is an optimality characterization, not a bit-complexity bound. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03846)
No result settling the stated heterogeneous, explicitly encoded approximation problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted p to 0<p<=1 because it is a success probability.
  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and required nonempty support.
  • Interpreted payouts as amounts rather than fractions of B, with the current successful share included at index zero.
  • Made the supremum range over all feasible real infinite sequences, while requiring the output to have finite rational support.
  • Specified an explicit sparse output encoding with binary indices, binary rational amounts, and zero omitted entries.
  • Included epsilon and all other rational inputs in L, so polynomial time refers to binary input length.

2. Exponential output is necessary

Consider one type with mass $1$, exponent $\alpha=\tfrac12$, discount $\delta=q\in(0,1)$, and $p=1$. Then
$$ W(x)=\sum_{i\ge0}q^i\sqrt{x_i}. $$

Cauchy–Schwarz applied to finite partial sums, followed by passage to the limit, gives
$$ W(x)\le \left(\sum_{i\ge0}q^{2i}\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_{i\ge0}x_i\right)^{1/2} \le \sqrt{\frac{B}{1-q^2}}. $$
The feasible rule
$$ x_i=B(1-q^2)q^{2i} $$
attains this bound. Therefore
$$ W^*=\sqrt{\frac{B}{1-q^2}}. $$

Suppose a rule has at most $k$ nonzero entries. Order its nonzero indices as
$$ i_0<i_1<\cdots<i_{r-1}, \qquad r\le k. $$
Since $i_j\ge j$,
$$ \sum_{j=0}^{r-1}q^{2i_j} \le \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}q^{2j}. $$
Another application of Cauchy–Schwarz yields
$$ W(x)\le \sqrt{\frac{B(1-q^{2k})}{1-q^2}}. $$
This bound is attained by the rational rule
$$ x_i= \frac{Bq^{2i}}{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}q^{2j}} \quad(0\le i<k), $$
with all remaining entries zero. In particular, large binary indices cannot improve the support-constrained optimum.

Now choose, for each integer $n\ge1$,

$$ W(x)\le\sqrt{1-q_n^{2k}}. $$
An acceptable output must therefore satisfy
$$ q_n^{2k}\le\frac7{16}, $$

$$ k\ge \frac{\log(16/7)}{2[-\log q_n]}. $$

3. A uniformly controlled finite horizon

Define
$$ C=\max\{1,B\}, \qquad d=\min_t(1-\delta_t), \qquad U=\frac{C}{d}. $$
Feasibility implies $x_i\le B$. Since $0<\alpha_t<1$,
$$ B^{\alpha_t}\le C. $$
Using $p\le1$ and $\sum_t\mu_t=1$, we obtain
$$ W(x) \le p\sum_t\mu_t\frac{B^{\alpha_t}}{1-\delta_t} \le U. $$
In particular, $W^*$ is finite. If $\varepsilon\ge U$, the zero rule is acceptable. Henceforth assume $\varepsilon<U$.

Let $H$ be the least positive integer satisfying
$$ U2^{-H}\le\frac{\varepsilon}{8}, $$
and set
$$ R=\left\lceil\frac1d\right\rceil, \qquad N=HR. $$
These quantities are computable by rational arithmetic and comparisons with powers of two.

To bound the tail, observe that
$$ (1-d)^{-R} = \left(1+\frac{d}{1-d}\right)^R \ge 1+Rd \ge2, $$
where the first inequality follows from the binomial expansion. Consequently,
$$ (1-d)^N\le2^{-H}. $$

For every feasible rule,
$$ \begin{aligned} W(x)-p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\delta_t^i x_i^{\alpha_t} &\le p\sum_t\mu_t B^{\alpha_t} \frac{\delta_t^N}{1-\delta_t}\\ &\le U(1-d)^N\\ &\le\frac{\varepsilon}{8}. \end{aligned} $$

Define
$$ f_i(u)=p\sum_t\mu_t\delta_t^i u^{\alpha_t}, \qquad F(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}f_i(x_i). $$
Let $V_N$ denote the maximum of $F$ over nonnegative vectors with total payout at most $B$. This maximum exists because the feasible set is compact and $F$ is continuous.

Every $f_i$ is strictly increasing. Thus a maximizing vector uses the full budget:
$$ \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_i=B. $$
The uniform tail bound proves
$$ V_N\le W^*\le V_N+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}. $$

Under binary rational encoding,
$$ d\ge2^{-O(L)}, \qquad U\le2^{O(L)}, \qquad \varepsilon\ge2^{-O(L)}. $$
Therefore
$$ H=O(L), \qquad N=O\!\left(\frac{L}{d}\right), \qquad \log N=O(L). $$

It remains to solve this finite problem with precision depending polynomially on the input bit length.

4. A positive floor and stable marginal equations

4.1. Enforcing a small positive floor

Set
$$ \gamma=\frac{\varepsilon}{8U}, \qquad a=\frac{\gamma B}{N}. $$
Then $0<\gamma<\tfrac18$ and $Na=\gamma B<B$.

Let $V_a$ be the maximum of $F$ on
$$ \mathcal K_a= \left\{ x\in[a,B]^N: \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_i=B \right\}. $$
This set is nonempty and compact.

Take a vector $v$ attaining $V_N$, and define
$$ y_i=(1-\gamma)v_i+a. $$
Then $y\in\mathcal K_a$. For every type and coordinate,
$$ y_i^{\alpha_t} \ge (1-\gamma)^{\alpha_t}v_i^{\alpha_t} \ge (1-\gamma)v_i^{\alpha_t}. $$
The last inequality holds because $0<1-\gamma<1$ and $\alpha_t<1$. Hence
$$ V_a\ge(1-\gamma)V_N \ge V_N-\frac{\varepsilon}{8}, $$
using $V_N\le U$.

4.2. Adding a small quadratic penalty

Set
$$ \kappa=\frac{\varepsilon}{8B^2}, \qquad G(x)=F(x)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_i^2. $$
Let $z^*$ maximize $G$ on $\mathcal K_a$.

If $v^*$ maximizes $F$ on $\mathcal K_a$, then
$$ G(z^*)\ge G(v^*). $$
Every vector in $\mathcal K_a$ satisfies
$$ \sum_i x_i^2\le\left(\sum_i x_i\right)^2=B^2. $$
It follows that
$$ F(z^*)\ge V_a-\frac{\kappa B^2}{2} = V_a-\frac{\varepsilon}{16}. $$

The penalty makes the marginal equations uniformly stable. Define
$$ D_i(u)=f_i'(u)-\kappa u. $$
For $u>0$,
$$ f_i'(u) = p\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t\delta_t^i u^{\alpha_t-1}, $$
and
$$ f_i''(u) = p\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t(\alpha_t-1)\delta_t^i u^{\alpha_t-2}<0. $$
Therefore
$$ D_i'(u)\le-\kappa. $$

Also, for $u\in[a,B]$,
$$ 0<f_i'(u)\le\max\{1,a^{-1}\}<M, \qquad M=1+a^{-1}. $$
Indeed, $u^{\alpha_t-1}\le1$ when $a\ge1$, and $u^{\alpha_t-1}\le a^{-1}$ when $a<1$.

4.3. Reduction to one scalar multiplier

For a real number $\lambda$, let $z_i(\lambda)$ be the unique maximizer on $[a,B]$ of
$$ f_i(u)-\frac{\kappa}{2}u^2-\lambda u. $$
Equivalently,
$$ z_i(\lambda)= \begin{cases} a,&D_i(a)\le\lambda,\\ B,&D_i(B)\ge\lambda,\\ \text{the unique }u\in(a,B)\text{ with }D_i(u)=\lambda, & D_i(B)<\lambda<D_i(a). \end{cases} $$

These functions are nonincreasing and satisfy
$$ |z_i(\lambda)-z_i(\lambda')| \le\frac{|\lambda-\lambda'|}{\kappa}. $$
Here is a proof including the endpoint cases. Suppose $\lambda<\lambda'$ and write $z=z_i(\lambda)$, $z'=z_i(\lambda')$. Adding the two maximizing inequalities gives
$$ (\lambda'-\lambda)(z-z')\ge0, $$
so $z\ge z'$. If $z>z'$, the first-order inequalities at the two constrained maximizers give
$$ D_i(z)\ge\lambda, \qquad D_i(z')\le\lambda'. $$
Consequently,
$$ \kappa(z-z') \le D_i(z')-D_i(z) \le\lambda'-\lambda. $$

Put
$$ S(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}z_i(\lambda). $$
The preceding bound implies that $S$ is continuous. At
$$ \lambda_-=-\kappa B, \qquad \lambda_+=M, $$
we have
$$ S(\lambda_-)=NB, \qquad S(\lambda_+)=Na<B. $$
The first equality follows from $D_i(B)>-\kappa B$; the second follows from $D_i(a)<M$.

Since $N>1$, the intermediate value theorem supplies $\lambda^*\in[\lambda_-,\lambda_+]$ with
$$ S(\lambda^*)=B. $$
The vector $z(\lambda^*)$ maximizes $G$ on $\mathcal K_a$. To verify this directly, coordinate optimality gives, for every $v_i\in[a,B]$,
$$ \bigl(D_i(z_i(\lambda^*))-\lambda^*\bigr) \bigl(v_i-z_i(\lambda^*)\bigr)\le0. $$
Concavity supplies the supporting-line inequality. Summing it over coordinates, the multiplier contribution vanishes because both vectors have total payout $B$. Thus
$$ G(v)\le G(z(\lambda^*)). $$
We may therefore take $z^*=z(\lambda^*)$.

5. Certified rational bisection

Define the coordinate accuracy
$$ s=\frac{\varepsilon}{64NM}. $$

5.1. Computing a coordinate within error $s$

For rational $\lambda$, one can compute a rational $y_i\in[a,B]$ satisfying
$$ |y_i-z_i(\lambda)|\le s $$
by bisection on $[a,B]$.

At each midpoint $u$, evaluate $D_i(u)$ with absolute error at most
$$ \eta=\frac{\kappa s}{4}. $$
Such evaluations are justified in Section 6.

If the computed value exceeds $\lambda+\eta$, then the true value exceeds $\lambda$, so the maximizer lies to the right. If the computed value is below $\lambda-\eta$, the maximizer lies to the left.

Otherwise,
$$ |D_i(u)-\lambda|\le2\eta=\frac{\kappa s}{2}. $$
The bound $D_i'\le-\kappa$ then implies
$$ |u-z_i(\lambda)|\le\frac{s}{2}, $$
including when the maximizer is an endpoint. For example, if $z_i(\lambda)=a$, then
$$ \lambda-D_i(u) \ge D_i(a)-D_i(u) \ge\kappa(u-a). $$
The case $z_i(\lambda)=B$ is analogous.

If this residual stopping condition never occurs, stop once the enclosing interval has width at most $2s$ and return its midpoint. The number of iterations is
$$ O\!\left(1+\log\max\{1,B/s\}\right). $$

5.2. Computing the multiplier

Maintain a bracket $[\ell,r]$ containing $\lambda^*$, initially $[\lambda_-,\lambda_+]$.

At its midpoint $\lambda$, compute the rational coordinates $y_i$ above and their exact sum
$$ S_y=\sum_i y_i. $$
Since
$$ |S_y-S(\lambda)|\le Ns, $$
the following updates are certified:

  • If $S_y>B+Ns$, then $S(\lambda)>B$, so replace $\ell$ by $\lambda$.
  • If $S_y<B-Ns$, then $S(\lambda)<B$, so replace $r$ by $\lambda$.
  • Otherwise, accept $y$.

If the bracket reaches width at most $\kappa s$, compute $y$ at its midpoint and accept it. Thus the number of outer iterations is at most
$$ O\!\left( 1+\log\max\left\{1,\frac{M+\kappa B}{\kappa s}\right\} \right). $$

5.3. Exact budget correction

Correct the accepted rational vector $y$ to a rational vector $x\in\mathcal K_a$.

If its sum exceeds $B$, decrease coordinates, preserving their lower bound $a$, until the excess is removed. There is enough removable mass because $Na<B$. If its sum is below $B$, increase coordinates, preserving the upper bound $B$, until the deficit is removed. The total available capacity is $NB-S_y\ge B-S_y$.

This can be done sequentially, using exact rational arithmetic, and satisfies
$$ \|x-y\|_1=|S_y-B|. $$

If acceptance occurred because $|S_y-B|\le Ns$, then
$$ |S(\lambda)-B|\le2Ns. $$
All coordinates of $z(\lambda)-z(\lambda^*)$ have the same sign, by monotonicity. Therefore
$$ \|z(\lambda)-z^*\|_1 = |S(\lambda)-B| \le2Ns. $$
Together with $\|y-z(\lambda)\|_1\le Ns$ and the budget correction, this gives
$$ \|x-z^*\|_1\le4Ns. $$

If acceptance occurred because the bracket width is at most $\kappa s$, then
$$ |\lambda-\lambda^*|\le\frac{\kappa s}{2}, $$
so
$$ \|y-z^*\|_1\le\frac32Ns. $$
The budget correction adds at most another $\tfrac32Ns$. Thus the same bound $\|x-z^*\|_1\le4Ns$ holds in both cases.

Since $f_i'$ is bounded by $M$ on $[a,B]$, integration of the derivative gives
$$ |F(x)-F(z^*)| \le M\|x-z^*\|_1 \le4NMs =\frac{\varepsilon}{16}. $$

Combining all losses,
$$ \begin{aligned} F(x) &\ge V_a-\frac{\varepsilon}{16}-\frac{\varepsilon}{16}\\ &\ge V_N-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\\ &\ge W^*-\frac{3\varepsilon}{8}. \end{aligned} $$
Extending $x$ by zeros at indices $i\ge N$ therefore gives an acceptable rule. It is rational, has finite support, and uses exactly the budget $B$.

6. Bit complexity

The bisection procedure uses certified approximations to rational powers. The following details ensure that it does not assume a real-arithmetic oracle.

6.1. Evaluating rational powers

Let $r>0$ have binary encoding length at most $K$, let $\beta\in[-1,0]$ be rational with encoding length at most $K$, and let $Q\ge1$. A rational approximation to $r^\beta$ with absolute error at most $2^{-Q}$ can be computed in time polynomial in $K+Q$.

To justify this, write
$$ r=2^e v, \qquad 1\le v<2, \qquad |e|=O(K). $$
For
$$ w=\frac{v-1}{v+1}, $$
we have $0\le w<\tfrac13$ and
$$ \log v = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. $$
After $m$ terms, the error is at most
$$ 2\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}|w|^{2j+1} \le\frac34\,9^{-m}. $$
The same series with $w=\tfrac13$ evaluates $\log2$. Hence $\log r=e\log2+\log v$ can be approximated to any prescribed binary precision with polynomially many rational operations and polynomial intermediate bit length.

Next use
$$ r^\beta=\exp(\beta\log r). $$
The exponent has magnitude $O(K)$. Its approximation needs only $Q+O(K+\log K)$ bits of absolute precision, because the derivative of the exponential on this range is bounded by $2^{O(K)}$.

To evaluate the exponential, divide its argument by a power of two until its magnitude is at most $1$. Approximate the resulting exponential by its Taylor series, then repeatedly square. The number of squarings is $O(\log(K+1))$, and the Taylor remainder decreases at least geometrically with the number of terms. Using $Q+O(K+\log K)$ bits of initial accuracy controls the final error: for positive $A,B$,
$$ |A^m-B^m| \le m\max\{A,B\}^{m-1}|A-B|. $$
Here $m=O(K+1)$ and the initial values are bounded by a constant. Exact rational evaluation of the finite series and subsequent squarings has polynomial bit complexity.

This argument applies even when the denominator of $\beta$ is exponentially large in its bit length.

6.2. Evaluating discounted coefficients

For $0<\delta<1$ and a binary integer $i$, the value $\delta^i$ can be approximated to absolute error $2^{-Q}$ in time polynomial in
$$ L+\log(i+1)+Q. $$
Use repeated squaring and binary exponentiation, rounding intermediate values to a sufficiently fine dyadic grid.

All factors remain in $[0,1]$. Squaring propagates an error $e$ to at most $2e$ plus the rounding error, while multiplication propagates at most the sum of its two input errors plus the rounding error. Consequently, choosing
$$ Q+O(\log(i+1)) $$
fractional bits controls the final absolute error. Only $O(\log(i+1))$ multiplications are needed.

6.3. Evaluating the marginals

At every coordinate-bisection point $u\in[a,B]$,
$$ u^{\alpha_t-1}\le M. $$
Approximate each $\delta_t^i$ and each $u^{\alpha_t-1}$ to absolute error at most
$$ \zeta= \min\left\{1,\frac{\eta}{4\tau(M+2)}\right\}. $$
The resulting error in each product is at most $(M+2)\zeta$. Since $p\mu_t\alpha_t\le1$, summing over types gives an error at most $\eta/4$, and hence at most $\eta$. The term $-\kappa u$ is rational and can be handled exactly.

Thus every derivative evaluation required by Section 5 has polynomial bit complexity in the input lengths and requested precision.

6.4. Sizes and total running time

The rational quantities
$$ a,\ \kappa,\ M,\ s,\ \lambda_-,\ \lambda_+ $$
have $O(L+\log N)$ bits. The logarithmic iteration bounds in Section 5 are also $O(L+\log N)$.

Coordinate outputs are midpoints obtained by at most $O(L+\log N)$ bisections of $[a,B]$. Their denominators therefore divide one common denominator whose bit length is $O(L+\log N)$. The exact budget correction preserves that denominator. Since every final amount lies in $[a,B]$, each amount has $O(L+\log N)$ bits.

Each outer iteration performs $N$ coordinate computations, each involving polynomially many arithmetic operations and power evaluations. The total running time is consequently
$$ N\,\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L+\log N), $$
and the explicit output length is
$$ O\bigl(N(L+\log N)\bigr). $$

Because $\log N=O(L)$ and $N\le2^{O(L)}$, this proves the claimed $2^{O(L)}$ worst-case upper bound.

If $d\ge1/q(L)$ for a fixed polynomial $q$, then
$$ N=O(Lq(L)), $$
and the same algorithm runs in polynomial time.

7. Remaining classification question

The following conclusions are proved:

  • The required explicit output prevents polynomial-time solvability on unrestricted inputs, already when $\tau=1$.
  • General heterogeneous inputs admit a $2^{O(L)}$-time algorithm with polynomially many bits per listed payout.
  • A polynomial lower bound on the discount gaps suffices for polynomial-time solvability.

These results provide no reduction establishing NP-hardness. Some instances have no polynomial-length valid output, so a hardness claim must specify how a polynomial-time reduction accesses potentially exponentially long oracle answers. Establishing or refuting NP-hardness under such a specified reduction remains open here.
