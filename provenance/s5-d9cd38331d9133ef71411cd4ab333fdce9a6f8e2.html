The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite goods set \([m]\), types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), initial bundles \(S_t\subseteq[m]\), and symmetric competition values \(\beta_{tu}\in(0,1)\), find a finite schedule of pairwise mass exchanges. Track \(\lambda_{t,A}\), the mass of type \(t\) holding bundle \(A\supseteq S_t\), initially \(\lambda_{t,S_t}=\mu_t\). An exchange of mass \(q\) pairs agents in states \((t,A)\) and \((u,B)\), giving each a good initially held by the other type that the recipient does not already hold; each unit of mass retains an integral bundle and cannot pass on received goods. Require every exchange to be feasible for the available mass, with no agent receiving a duplicate good. Return a schedule whose terminal distribution has no positive mass of mutually exchangeable pairs remaining.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The masses satisfy \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\).
Input also includes \(m\geq1\) freely replicable goods, initial bundles \(S_t\subseteq[m]\), and binary rational competition values \(\beta_{tu}=\beta_{ut}\in(0,1)\) for distinct types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each agent retains an integral bundle and may give copies only of goods in its initial bundle. Let \(\lambda_{t,A}\geq0\) be the mass of type \(t\) holding \(A\supseteq S_t\), with \(\sum_{A\supseteq S_t}\lambda_{t,A}=\mu_t\); initially only \(\lambda_{t,S_t}=\mu_t\) is positive.
A schedule is an explicit finite sequence of exchanges \((t,A,u,B,r,s,q)\), where \(t\neq u\), \(r\in S_t\setminus B\), \(s\in S_u\setminus A\), and \(q\) is a binary rational satisfying \(0<q\leq\min\{\lambda_{t,A},\lambda_{u,B}\}\) immediately before the exchange.
The exchange subtracts \(q\) from each source state and adds \(q\) to each of \((t,A\cup\{s\})\) and \((u,B\cup\{r\})\). Copies are retained, and agents never receive a duplicate good.
A distribution is stable if no two positive-mass states \((t,A),(u,B)\), with \(t\neq u\), satisfy both \(S_t\setminus B\neq\varnothing\) and \(S_u\setminus A\neq\varnothing\). The computational problem is to return a feasible schedule ending in a stable distribution.

Is computing such a schedule possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this search problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For \(n\) discrete participating agents, CLEAR constructs a stable allocation under universal acceptance in \(O(n^2m^2\max\{n,m\})\) time; its protocol also satisfies Nash incentive compatibility and individual rationality. [Yang–Kandasamy](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12403v1)

Result

The search problem is polynomial-time solvable. More precisely, a feasible schedule ending in a stable distribution can be constructed with at most \(\tau m(2m+1)\) exchanges. All exchange masses have bit length \(O(L)\).

The competition values \(\beta_{tu}\) do not enter the construction: the specified feasibility and stability conditions depend only on the initial bundles and masses.

1. Assigning aggregate exchange masses

Construct a graph whose vertices are the type–good pairs
\[ V=\{(t,g):t\in T,\ g\in[m]\setminus S_t\}. \]
The vertex \((t,g)\) represents type \(t\) receiving good \(g\), and has capacity \(\mu_t\).

For each unordered pair of distinct types \(t,u\), and each pair of goods
\[ r\in S_t\setminus S_u,\qquad s\in S_u\setminus S_t, \]
create an edge joining \((t,s)\) and \((u,r)\). This edge represents exchanging copies of \(r\) and \(s\).

Initialize residual capacities \(c_{t,g}=\mu_t\). Process every edge once, in any fixed order. For an edge \(e=\{(t,s),(u,r)\}\), assign
\[ w_e=\min\{c_{t,s},c_{u,r}\}, \]
and subtract \(w_e\) from both endpoint capacities. Retain only edges with \(w_e>0\).

This construction has three immediate properties:

  • For every vertex \(v\), \(\sum_{e\ni v}w_e\leq\mu_t\), where \(v=(t,g)\).
  • Every edge has at least one endpoint with final residual capacity zero. Indeed, processing the edge makes at least one endpoint zero, and capacities never increase.
  • There are at most \(|V|\leq\tau m\) retained edges. Each positive assignment makes at least one previously positive capacity zero, so it can be charged to a distinct vertex.

The remaining task is to realize these aggregate exchanges with a polynomial-length explicit schedule.

2. A nested-support invariant

For each type, maintain its positive-mass bundles as a chain under inclusion. Initially this holds because type \(t\) occupies only \(S_t\).

We first establish the update rule that preserves this invariant.

Lemma. Suppose the occupied bundles of one type form an inclusion chain, and some positive mass lacks good \(g\). Let \(A\) be the largest occupied bundle lacking \(g\). Moving any mass \(q\), with \(0<q\leq\lambda_{t,A}\), from \(A\) to \(A\cup\{g\}\) preserves the chain property.

Proof. Every occupied bundle smaller than \(A\) is contained in \(A\cup\{g\}\). Every occupied bundle larger than \(A\) contains \(g\), by the choice of \(A\); it therefore contains \(A\cup\{g\}\). Thus the new bundle is comparable with every occupied bundle. Removing an exhausted source or merging identical bundles also preserves the chain. \(\square\)

Consequently, each type always has at most \(m+1\) occupied bundles: cardinality strictly increases along a strict inclusion chain.

3. Realizing one retained edge

Process the retained edges sequentially. Consider an edge representing type \(t\) receiving \(s\), and type \(u\) receiving \(r\), with assigned mass \(w_e>0\).

Let \(h=w_e\) be its remaining mass. While \(h>0\):

  • Choose the largest occupied bundle \(A\) of type \(t\) lacking \(s\).
  • Choose the largest occupied bundle \(B\) of type \(u\) lacking \(r\).
  • Set \(q=\min\{h,\lambda_{t,A},\lambda_{u,B}\}\).
  • Output and execute \((t,A,u,B,r,s,q)\), then replace \(h\) by \(h-q\).

We verify that these choices always exist and that every exchange is feasible.

Initially, all mass of type \(t\) lacks \(s\). Each previous exchange in which \(t\) receives \(s\) decreases that missing mass by exactly its exchange mass. At every point, the total mass already assigned to \((t,s)\), including the current edge, is at most \(\mu_t\). Hence the mass currently lacking \(s\) is at least \(h\). The same argument applies to type \(u\) and good \(r\). Therefore both source states exist whenever \(h>0\).

Moreover, \(r\in S_t\setminus B\) and \(s\in S_u\setminus A\), and \(q\) satisfies the required source-mass inequalities. Thus each output exchange is feasible. Applying the lemma to both types preserves their nested supports.

We next prove a bound independent of the numerical sizes of the masses.

Lemma. Realizing one retained edge takes at most \(2m+1\) exchanges.

Proof. For a type \(v\) and good \(g\), define the missing mass
\[ z_{v,g}=\sum_{C:\,g\notin C}\lambda_{v,C}. \]

Because the occupied bundles form a chain, arrange their mass intervals consecutively from the smallest bundle to the largest. A good \(g\) is absent precisely on the initial interval of length \(z_{v,g}\).

During realization of the current edge, only \(z_{t,s}\) and \(z_{u,r}\) change. Every \(z_{t,g}\) with \(g\neq s\), and every \(z_{u,g}\) with \(g\neq r\), remains fixed.

Consider a source \(A\), the largest occupied bundle of type \(t\) lacking \(s\). Its upper interval boundary is \(z_{t,s}\). Its lower boundary is either zero or \(z_{t,g}\) for some \(g\neq s\). To see this, if \(A\) has an immediately preceding occupied bundle \(C\), choose \(g\in A\setminus C\). All occupied bundles at or below \(C\) lack \(g\), while all bundles at or above \(A\) contain \(g\). Thus the boundary between them is exactly \(z_{t,g}\).

If an exchange exhausts source \(A\), the new value of \(z_{t,s}\) equals that lower boundary. Since \(z_{t,s}\) strictly decreases at every exchange, it can reach each fixed value in
\[ \{0\}\cup\{z_{t,g}:g\neq s\} \]
at most once. This set has at most \(m\) values. Consequently, at most \(m\) exchanges can exhaust a source of type \(t\). Likewise, at most \(m\) exchanges can exhaust a source of type \(u\).

Every exchange except the final one has \(q<h\). By the definition of \(q\), such an exchange exhausts at least one of its two source states. Charge it to one exhausted source. There are at most \(2m\) such charges, followed by at most one final exchange. \(\square\)

In particular, every retained edge is fully realized after finitely many exchanges.

4. Stability of the final distribution

After every retained edge has been realized, for each initially missing good \(g\),
\[ z_{t,g}=\mu_t-\sum_{e\ni(t,g)}w_e=c_{t,g}, \]
where the last quantity is the final residual capacity from Section 1.

Suppose the resulting distribution were unstable. Then there would be positive-mass states \((t,A)\) and \((u,B)\), with distinct types, and goods
\[ r\in S_t\setminus B,\qquad s\in S_u\setminus A. \]

Because \(B\supseteq S_u\) and \(A\supseteq S_t\), these goods also satisfy
\[ r\in S_t\setminus S_u,\qquad s\in S_u\setminus S_t. \]
Thus the graph contains the edge joining \((t,s)\) and \((u,r)\).

The positive mass at \(A\) lacks \(s\), so \(c_{t,s}=z_{t,s}>0\). Similarly, \(c_{u,r}=z_{u,r}>0\). This contradicts the established property that every graph edge has an endpoint with final residual capacity zero.

Therefore the final distribution is stable.

5. Output length and bit complexity

There are at most \(\tau m\) retained edges, each requiring at most \(2m+1\) exchanges. Hence the schedule length is at most \(\tau m(2m+1)=O(\tau m^2)\).

The graph has at most \(\tau m\) vertices and \(O(\tau^2m^2)\) edges. During realization, each type occupies at most \(m+1\) bundles. Enumerating the graph, choosing source states, and updating these explicit chains therefore require polynomially many operations.

To bound arithmetic sizes, write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), and let \(D=\prod_{t\in T}b_t\). Then \(\log D=O(L)\). Every capacity, assigned mass, state mass, and exchange mass is an integer multiple of \(1/D\): the algorithm uses only comparisons, minima, additions, and subtractions. All these quantities lie between zero and one, so their numerators and denominators require \(O(L)\) bits.

If “binary rational” specifically means a dyadic rational, then \(D\) is a power of two, and every output exchange mass is dyadic as well.

Thus the entire explicit schedule can be computed and written in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The cited [Yang–Kandasamy paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12403v1) establishes a finite-agent CLEAR protocol with additional incentive guarantees. The proof above directly settles the stated mass-exchange search problem; it does not assert those additional guarantees.
