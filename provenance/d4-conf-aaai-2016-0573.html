The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rational belief-matrix types \(P^t\) on signal alphabet \([m]\), compute a shared payment matrix \(M\in[0,1]^{m\times m}\) maximizing \(\delta\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_j P^t_{s,j}(M(s,j)-M(x,j))\ge\delta\) for every \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\), signal \(s\), and alternative report \(x\ne s\). Return whether the optimum is positive, which is exactly whether one strictly truthful mechanism serves every type in the society.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m\ge2\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Each type supplies a complete rational matrix \(P^t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{m\times m}\), with \(\sum_jP^t_{s,j}=1\): its subjective distribution of a distinct peer's signal conditional on observing \(s\). Every own signal has positive probability; each matrix has pairwise distinct rows and no identically zero column, ensuring stochastic relevance and positive peer-signal priors. No common prior or consistency between types is required.
A shared mechanism pays \(M(x,j)\) when an agent reports \(x\) and its peer reports \(j\). Strict truthfulness means that, conditional on every signal, truthful reporting uniquely maximizes expected payment when the peer reports truthfully; ties do not qualify.
Compute \(M\in[0,1]^{m\times m}\) maximizing \(\delta\ge0\), subject to \(\sum_{j=1}^mP^t_{s,j}(M(s,j)-M(x,j))\ge\delta\) for every \(t\in[\tau]\), \(s\in[m]\), and \(x\in[m]\setminus\{s\}\). The decision problem asks whether the optimum is positive.

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Known results: For finite agents, minimal mechanisms' regions of unique optimal reports are precisely strict power-diagram cells, with explicit conversions in both directions ([Frongillo–Witkowski](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~raf/media/papers/geometry.pdf)); this is a characterization, not a complexity classification of the decision problem above.
Power-diagram detection for an explicitly supplied polyhedral decomposition, including a restricted domain, is polynomial-time ([Borgwardt–Frongillo](https://arxiv.org/html/1711.06207)); that input model differs from a finite support of belief matrices. No exact continuous-support complexity classification was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution's positive rational masses to sum to one and required nonempty support.
  • Made belief matrices row-stochastic with nonnegative rational entries and carried over the requirement of at least two signals.
  • Carried over stochastic relevance as pairwise distinct rows within each type.
  • Made every own signal possible and encoded positive peer-signal priors by excluding identically zero columns; individual conditional probabilities may be zero.
  • Interpreted truthfulness strictly, conditional on a distinct peer reporting truthfully, without any tie-breaking or common-prior requirement.
  • Defined L as total binary input length and retained the graded question's unit payment bounds and uniform margin objective.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Moreover, an optimal rational mechanism \(\,M^\star\in[0,1]^{m\times m}\,\) and its exact optimal margin \(\delta^\star\) can be computed in time polynomial in \(\,m,\tau,L\,\).

The proof uses the explicitly listed incentive constraints directly.

1. Strict truthfulness and a positive margin

Write \([k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}\). For each \(\,t\in[\tau]\), \(\,s\in[m]\), and \(\,x\in[m]\setminus\{s\}\), define

\(\displaystyle D_{t,s,x}(M)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P^t_{s,j}\bigl(M(s,j)-M(x,j)\bigr).\)

This is the difference between the expected payment for truthful reporting and that for reporting \(\,x\,\), conditional on type \(\,t\,\) and signal \(\,s\,\).

Consequently, \(\,M\,\) is strictly truthful exactly when every \(\,D_{t,s,x}(M)>0\,\). There are finitely many such comparisons, so strict truthfulness implies

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\min_{t,s,x:\,x\ne s}D_{t,s,x}(M)>0.\)

Thus \(\,(M,\varepsilon)\,\) is feasible for the stated optimization problem. Conversely, feasibility with \(\,\delta>0\,\) implies strict truthfulness for every comparison. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \delta^\star>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{a strictly truthful mechanism in }[0,1]^{m\times m}\text{ exists}.\)

The positive masses ensure that every listed type belongs to the support. Their numerical values do not enter these pointwise incentive constraints.

2. Exact computation by rational linear programming

There are \(\,n=m^2+1\,\) variables: the payment entries and \(\,\delta\,\). The incentive constraints are linear because every coefficient \(\,P^t_{s,j}\,\) is supplied in the input.

The program is feasible: \(\,M=0\,\) and \(\,\delta=0\,\) satisfy every constraint. Also, for any allowed payment matrix,

\(\displaystyle D_{t,s,x}(M)\le \sum_{j=1}^{m}P^t_{s,j}=1,\)

since \(\,M(s,j)-M(x,j)\le1\,\) and the probabilities are nonnegative. Because \(\,m\ge2\,\) and \(\,\tau\ge1\,\), there is at least one incentive constraint, so every feasible solution has \(\,0\le\delta\le1\,\).

We may therefore include the redundant bound \(\,\delta\le1\,\). The feasible set is then a nonempty closed subset of \(\,[0,1]^{m^2+1}\,\), hence compact. The objective \(\,\delta\,\) is continuous, so its maximum is attained.

The resulting rational linear program has

\(\displaystyle \tau m(m-1)+2m^2+2\)

inequalities. Each incentive row contains at most \(\,2m+1\,\) nonzero coefficients, consisting of input probabilities, their negatives, and the coefficient of \(\,\delta\,\). Even a dense encoding has length polynomial in \(\,m,\tau,L\,\): its dimensions are polynomial, and each coefficient requires at most \(\,O(L+1)\,\) bits.

We now apply the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility can be decided, and a finite attained optimum and a rational optimizer can be computed exactly, in time polynomial in the binary encoding length. Polynomial time here is in the bit-complexity model; see the introduction of [Dadush–Huiberts–Natura–Végh](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10107-023-01956-2).

All hypotheses have been checked: the program is explicitly represented, rational, feasible, and has an attained finite optimum. Hence it yields \(\,(M^\star,\delta^\star)\,\) in the required polynomial time. Comparing the exact rational number \(\,\delta^\star\,\) with zero decides the question.

3. A direct feasibility reduction handling strictness

There is also a decision procedure that avoids comparing an optimum with zero. Introduce unrestricted real variables \(\,W(s,j)\,\) and ask whether the following ordinary, non-strict rational linear system is feasible:

\(\displaystyle D_{t,s,x}(W)\ge1\qquad\text{for every }t,s,x\ne s.\)

We prove that this system is feasible exactly when the original optimum is positive.

If \(\,(M,\delta)\,\) is feasible with \(\,\delta>0\,\), set \(\,W=M/\delta\,\). Linearity gives \(\,D_{t,s,x}(W)\ge1\,\).

Conversely, suppose \(\,W\,\) satisfies this system. Set

\(\displaystyle a=\min_{s,j}W(s,j),\qquad b=\max_{s,j}W(s,j),\qquad r=b-a.\)

We have \(\,r>0\,\): otherwise all entries of \(\,W\,\) would coincide and every incentive difference would be zero. Define

\(\displaystyle M(s,j)=\frac{W(s,j)-a}{r}.\)

Then \(\,M\in[0,1]^{m\times m}\,\), and subtracting the same constant from every entry cancels in each report comparison. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle D_{t,s,x}(M)=\frac{D_{t,s,x}(W)}{r}\ge\frac1r>0.\)

Thus \(\,(M,1/r)\,\) is feasible for the original problem. This proves the equivalence and gives a polynomial-time decision procedure using rational linear feasibility alone.

The stochastic-relevance promises impose no additional difficulty for these algorithms. The finite list of beliefs already supplies every required inequality, so a geometric detection theorem is unnecessary.
