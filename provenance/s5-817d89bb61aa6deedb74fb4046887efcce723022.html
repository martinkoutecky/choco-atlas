The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(x\in C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported first-choice-and-approval types \((f,S)\), with \(f\in S\subseteq C\), determine the exact set of \(k\geq 1\) for which \(x\) is a \(\beta(k)\) winner, including ties.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set with \(m=|C|\geq1\), and let \(x\in C\). A type is \((f,S)\), where \(f\in S\subseteq C\) is its designated first choice and approval set.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; masses sum to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
For each real \(k\geq1\), a type contributes score \(k\) to \(f\), score \(1\) to each candidate in \(S\setminus\{f\}\), and score \(0\) to every other candidate. Candidate scores are the mass-weighted sums of these contributions; every candidate attaining the maximum is a winner, including all ties. Types and masses remain fixed as \(k\) varies.
The computational problem is to determine exactly \(K_x=\{k\in\mathbb R:k\geq1\text{ and }x\text{ is a }\beta(k)\text{ winner}\}\).

Is computing \(K_x\) possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite electorates with more than two potential winners whose first-choice totals strictly increase and approval totals strictly decrease, the winning parameter ranges are characterized by neighboring potential winners' totals ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00368)).
No result settling the exact support-encoded rational-mass problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and defined L as the total encoded input length.
  • Specified that k ranges over real numbers and that the supported types and masses remain fixed as k varies.
  • Resolved the literal ballot-definition ambiguity at k=1 by retaining a designated first choice while assigning score one to every approved candidate, consistent with the intended approval equivalence.
  • Retained arbitrary nonempty candidate sets and tied score totals: the anchor's restrictions on the number and ordering of potential winners are conditions for its characterization, not restrictions on valid election inputs.

Result

Computing \(K_x\) is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Its exact output is either the empty set, a closed interval with rational endpoints, or a ray with a rational left endpoint. A closed interval may consist of a single point.

The proof applies to every society in the question, without assumptions about the number or ordering of potential winners.

1. Expressing the scores

Index the supported types by \(i\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), and write their masses as \(\mu_i\). For each candidate \(c\in C\), define its first-choice total and approval total by

\(F_c=\sum_{i:f_i=c}\mu_i,\qquad A_c=\sum_{i:c\in S_i}\mu_i.\)

Because \(f_i\in S_i\), the mass approving \(c\) without designating it first is \(A_c-F_c\). Thus its score at parameter \(k\) is

\(\operatorname{score}_c(k)=kF_c+(A_c-F_c)=A_c+(k-1)F_c.\)

This affine score identity also appears in Definition 2.8 of the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.00368); here it follows directly for rational masses.

Set \(t=k-1\), so the allowed parameter domain becomes \(t\geq0\). Since all maximum-score candidates are winners, \(x\) wins exactly when, for every \(c\in C\),

\(A_x+tF_x\geq A_c+tF_c.\)

Equivalently, define

\(d_c=F_x-F_c,\qquad r_c=A_c-A_x.\)

Then the necessary and sufficient conditions are \(t\geq0\) and \(d_ct\geq r_c\) for every \(c\in C\).

2. Exact characterization and algorithm

Each candidate contributes one of three constraints:

  • If \(d_c>0\), division by \(d_c\) gives \(t\geq r_c/d_c\).
  • If \(d_c<0\), division reverses the inequality and gives \(t\leq r_c/d_c\).
  • If \(d_c=0\), the constraint is \(0\geq r_c\). It holds for every \(t\) when \(r_c\leq0\), and for no \(t\) when \(r_c>0\).

Consequently, if any candidate satisfies \(d_c=0\) and \(r_c>0\), return \(K_x=\varnothing\).

Otherwise, compute

\(\ell=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{r_c/d_c:c\in C,\ d_c>0\}\bigr)\)

and

\(u=\min\{r_c/d_c:c\in C,\ d_c<0\},\)

using the convention \(u=+\infty\) when the latter set is empty.

The exact answer is

\(K_x=\varnothing\quad\text{if }\ell>u;\)

\(K_x=[1+\ell,1+u]\quad\text{if }\ell\leq u<+\infty;\)

\(K_x=[1+\ell,+\infty)\quad\text{if }u=+\infty.\)

Correctness. After the inconsistent zero-coefficient case has been excluded, all constraints with positive coefficients hold exactly when \(t\geq\ell\), including the domain constraint \(t\geq0\). All constraints with negative coefficients hold exactly when \(t\leq u\). The remaining constraints hold identically. Their conjunction therefore describes precisely the interval \([\ell,u]\), or the ray \([\ell,+\infty)\), unless \(\ell>u\). Translating by \(k=1+t\) gives the stated answer.

Every finite endpoint is included because the winner inequalities are non-strict. In particular, \(\ell=u\) gives a valid singleton winning set.

For \(m=1\), all candidate constraints hold identically, so this formula gives \(K_x=[1,+\infty)\).

3. Polynomial bit complexity

It remains to show that exact rational arithmetic takes polynomial time, even when the masses encode an electorate of very large size.

Write each input mass as \(\mu_i=p_i/q_i\), where \(p_i,q_i\) are positive integers encoded in binary. Define

\(D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i,\qquad W_i=p_i(D/q_i).\)

Each \(W_i\) is an integer, and normalization gives

\(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}W_i=D.\)

The binary length of \(D\) is at most \(1+\sum_i\operatorname{bits}(q_i)\), hence is \(O(L)\). Moreover, positivity and normalization imply \(0<W_i\leq D\), so each \(W_i\) also has \(O(L)\) bits.

Compute the integer totals

\(N_c=\sum_{i:f_i=c}W_i,\qquad M_c=\sum_{i:c\in S_i}W_i.\)

They satisfy \(F_c=N_c/D\), \(A_c=M_c/D\), and \(0\leq N_c,M_c\leq D\). Thus every threshold used by the algorithm can be represented as

\(\frac{r_c}{d_c}=\frac{M_c-M_x}{N_x-N_c},\qquad N_x\ne N_c.\)

Its numerator and denominator have \(O(L)\) bits: the common denominator \(D\) cancels. Adding \(1\) to an endpoint preserves this bit bound. Fractions can be compared exactly by making their denominators positive and cross-multiplying; the resulting products still have \(O(L)\) bits.

Constructing \(D\) and the weights requires polynomially many operations on integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Computing all totals requires at most \(O(m\tau)\) additions and membership checks, and finding the bounds requires \(O(m)\) rational comparisons. Standard integer arithmetic therefore yields total running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). If reduced endpoint fractions are desired, greatest-common-divisor computations also take polynomial time.

The algorithm processes only the listed support and outputs at most two rational endpoints plus an empty-set or unbounded-endpoint marker. This establishes polynomial-time exact computation in the stated encoding.
