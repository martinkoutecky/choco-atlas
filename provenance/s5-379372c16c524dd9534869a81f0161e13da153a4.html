The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) chores and \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), per-capita budgets \(b_t>0\) satisfying \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t=1\), and normalized convex CES disutilities \(d_t(x)=(\sum_j(d_{tj}x_j)^\rho)^{1/\rho}\), find prices \(p\) and each type's optimal per-capita bundle \(x_t\) such that \(1-\varepsilon\leq\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq 1/(1-\varepsilon)\) for every chore \(j\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq1\) divisible chores, each with unit supply.
Type \(t\) has a binary-rational per-capita earning requirement \(b_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_tb_t=1\). A common binary-rational exponent \(\rho>1\) and positive binary-rational coefficients \(a_{tj}\) specify \(d_{tj}=a_{tj}/(\sum_k a_{tk}^{\rho})^{1/\rho}\) and \(d_t(x)=(\sum_j(d_{tj}x_j)^{\rho})^{1/\rho}\), so \(d_t(\mathbf1)=1\).
Prices are payments for performing chores. At \(p\in\mathbb R_{>0}^m\), the per-capita demand is the unique bundle \(x_t(p)\in\operatorname*{argmin}_{x\in\mathbb R_{2b}^m}\{d_t(x):p\cdot x\geq b_t\}\); earnings must satisfy \(p\cdot x_t(p)=b_t\) exactly.

Can this approximate-equilibrium problem be solved in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\), uniformly over rational \(\rho>1\), or is it NP-hard?

Partial resolution: an output obstruction and a polynomial algorithm for two chores

1. Scope and proved conclusions

I interpret the bundle domain as $\mathbb R_{\geq 0}^m$. Prices are output as ordinary binary-encoded rational fractions; the permission to use rational-power expressions applies to bundles.

Under this convention, the requested uniform polynomial-time bound is impossible, because some instances require exponentially long price outputs. This does not establish NP-hardness.

The additional result proved here is:

Two-chore algorithm. When $m=2$, the problem can be solved in time polynomial in $\tau$, $L$, $\lceil\rho\rceil$, and $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. The algorithm outputs rational prices and their exact individual demands. Its complexity has no numerical dependence on $1/(\rho-1)$.

Consequently, for two chores and any polynomially bounded numerical value of $\rho$, the requested problem is polynomial-time solvable—even when $\rho-1$ is exponentially small.

For $m=1$, the solution is simply $p_1=1$ and $x_{t1}=b_t$, since normalization gives $d_t(x)=x_1$ and $\sum_t\mu_tb_t=1$.

The classification for an arbitrary number of chores remains incomplete.

2. Exact demands and support aggregation

Put
$$ q=\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}>1, \qquad B_t=\mu_tb_t, \qquad S_t(p)=\sum_{k=1}^m a_{tk}^{-q}p_k^q. $$
Then $B_t>0$ and $\sum_tB_t=1$.

The normalization of the coefficients multiplies each type’s objective by a positive constant. Taking the positive power $\rho$ also preserves minimizers. Thus individual demand can be found by minimizing
$$ H_t(x)=\sum_{j=1}^m a_{tj}^{\rho}x_j^\rho $$
over $x\geq 0$ with $p\cdot x\geq b_t$.

Proposition. For every positive price vector, the unique demand is
$$ x_{tj}(p)= \frac{b_ta_{tj}^{-q}p_j^{q-1}}{S_t(p)}. $$

Proof. The displayed bundle is positive and satisfies
$$ p\cdot x_t(p) = b_t\frac{\sum_j a_{tj}^{-q}p_j^q}{S_t(p)} =b_t. $$
Using $q(\rho-1)=\rho$ and $(q-1)(\rho-1)=1$, we obtain
$$ a_{tj}^{\rho}x_{tj}(p)^{\rho-1} = \left(\frac{b_t}{S_t(p)}\right)^{\rho-1}p_j. $$
Therefore
$$ \nabla H_t(x_t(p)) = \rho\left(\frac{b_t}{S_t(p)}\right)^{\rho-1}p. $$
For any feasible $y$, convexity gives
$$ H_t(y)\geq H_t(x_t(p)) + \rho\left(\frac{b_t}{S_t(p)}\right)^{\rho-1} p\cdot(y-x_t(p)) \geq H_t(x_t(p)). $$
The last inequality follows from $p\cdot y\geq b_t=p\cdot x_t(p)$. Strict convexity of $H_t$ proves uniqueness. $\square$

Aggregate demand is therefore
$$ D_j(p)=\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}(p) = p_j^{q-1}\sum_t\frac{B_ta_{tj}^{-q}}{S_t(p)}. $$
It satisfies the exact earnings identity
$$ \sum_jp_jD_j(p)=1. $$

This also proves an exact reduction to a finite-agent market: give one agent per support type earning requirement $B_t$. Its demand is $\mu_tx_t(p)$. The reduction preserves aggregate demand and does not expand the population according to its masses.

3. Exponentially long rational price outputs

For each positive integer $n$, consider
$$ m=2,\qquad \tau=1,\qquad \mu_1=b_1=1,\qquad a_{11}=1,\qquad a_{12}=2,\qquad \rho=N=2^n,\qquad \varepsilon=\frac14. $$
These instances satisfy all input conditions, and their total input length is $L=\Theta(n)$.

Let $(p_1,p_2)$ be any valid output and $(x_1,x_2)$ its exact demand. The demand formula implies
$$ \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 2^N\left(\frac{x_2}{x_1}\right)^{N-1}. $$
The required bounds give
$$ \frac34\leq x_j\leq\frac43, \qquad \frac{x_2}{x_1}\geq\frac9{16}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \frac{p_2}{p_1} \geq 2^N\left(\frac9{16}\right)^{N-1} = \frac{16}{9}\left(\frac98\right)^N. $$

Write the output fractions as $p_i=u_i/v_i$, with positive integer numerators and denominators. Then
$$ \frac{p_2}{p_1} = \frac{u_2v_1}{v_2u_1} \leq u_2v_1. $$
If $T$ denotes total output length, the binary representations of $u_2$ and $v_1$ alone imply
$$ T>\log_2(u_2v_1) \geq \log_2\frac{p_2}{p_1} \geq N\log_2\frac98+\log_2\frac{16}{9}. $$
Thus every valid output has length
$$ T=\Omega(2^n)=2^{\Omega(L)}. $$

The obstruction is not infeasibility. An exact equilibrium is
$$ p_1=\frac1{1+2^N}, \qquad p_2=\frac{2^N}{1+2^N}, \qquad x_1=x_2=1. $$
The prices sum to one, and substitution into the demand formula verifies the demands.

Conclusion. With explicit binary-fraction prices, no algorithm can meet the proposed uniform polynomial running time, independently of any complexity-theoretic assumption.

4. General existence and polynomial-size outputs for bounded $\rho$

The following results separate the output obstruction from the remaining computational difficulty.

Define
$$ A=\max_t\frac{\max_j a_{tj}}{\min_j a_{tj}}\geq1. $$

4.1. Exact equilibrium existence

Proposition. Every instance has an exact equilibrium $p^*>0$ with
$$ \sum_jp_j^*=1, \qquad p_j^*\geq\frac{A^{-\rho}}m. $$

Proof. On the simplex
$$ \Delta=\left\{p\geq0:\sum_jp_j=1\right\}, $$
consider
$$ F(p)=\frac1q\sum_tB_t\log S_t(p). $$
Every $S_t$ is positive on $\Delta$, because at least one price coordinate is positive and every coefficient is positive. Hence $F$ is continuous on the compact simplex and attains a minimum.

Suppose a minimizer $p$ has $p_j=0$. For $0<h<1$, let $p(h)=(1-h)p+he_j$. Then
$$ S_t(p(h))=(1-h)^qS_t(p)+a_{tj}^{-q}h^q, $$
and therefore
$$ F(p(h))-F(p) = \log(1-h) + \frac1q\sum_tB_t \log\left( 1+\frac{a_{tj}^{-q}}{S_t(p)} \left(\frac h{1-h}\right)^q \right). $$
The first term is $-h+o(h)$. Each remaining term is $O(h^q)=o(h)$, since $q>1$. There are finitely many types, so the whole difference is $-h+o(h)<0$ for sufficiently small $h$. This contradicts minimality. Every minimizer is therefore interior.

At an interior minimizer,
$$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_j}=D_j(p). $$
The first-order condition on $\Delta$ makes all these derivatives equal to some $\lambda$. The earnings identity yields
$$ 1=\sum_jp_jD_j(p)=\lambda\sum_jp_j=\lambda. $$
Thus $D_j(p)=1$ for every chore.

To prove the price bound, put $a_{t,\max}=\max_k a_{tk}$. Convexity of $z\mapsto z^q$ gives
$$ S_t(p^*) \geq a_{t,\max}^{-q}\sum_k(p_k^*)^q \geq a_{t,\max}^{-q}m^{1-q}. $$
Hence
$$ \frac{a_{tj}^{-q}}{S_t(p^*)} \leq A^qm^{q-1}. $$
Since $\sum_tB_t=1$,
$$ 1=D_j(p^*) \leq (p_j^*)^{q-1}A^qm^{q-1}. $$
Taking the power $1/(q-1)$ and using $q/(q-1)=\rho$ proves
$$ p_j^*\geq\frac{A^{-\rho}}m. $$
For $m=1$, the claimed equilibrium is directly $p_1^*=1$. $\square$

4.2. Rational rounding with exact demands

Set $\eta=\varepsilon/(2q)$, and choose an integer $K$ satisfying
$$ 2^{-K}\leq\frac{\eta A^{-\rho}}m. $$
Round an exact equilibrium downward:
$$ \widehat p_j=2^{-K}\left\lfloor2^Kp_j^*\right\rfloor. $$
The price bound gives
$$ 0<(1-\eta)p_j^*\leq\widehat p_j\leq p_j^*, $$
and consequently
$$ (1-\eta)^qS_t(p^*)\leq S_t(\widehat p)\leq S_t(p^*). $$
The exact demand formula implies
$$ (1-\eta)^{q-1} \leq \frac{x_{tj}(\widehat p)}{x_{tj}(p^*)} \leq (1-\eta)^{-q}. $$
Because $q>1$, Bernoulli’s inequality gives
$$ (1-\eta)^q\geq1-q\eta=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$
Also $(1-\eta)^{q-1}\geq(1-\eta)^q$. Aggregating therefore yields
$$ 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \leq D_j(\widehat p) \leq \left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{-1}. $$
These bounds imply the requested bounds. Individual optimality and earnings remain exact.

One can choose
$$ K= O\left( 1+\rho\log_2 A+\log_2m+\log_2q+\log_2(1/\varepsilon) \right). $$
Since $\log_2A=O(L)$ and $\log_2q=O(L)$, polynomial-size rational outputs exist whenever the numerical value of $\rho$ is polynomially bounded. This argument establishes output size, not an algorithm for locating $p^*$.

5. A polynomial algorithm for two chores

Assume throughout this section that $m=2$.

5.1. Reduction to a scalar equation

We seek prices of the form
$$ p=(\alpha,1-\alpha), \qquad 0<\alpha<1. $$
Define type $t$’s fraction of earnings from chore one by
$$ s_t(\alpha)= \frac{a_{t1}^{-q}\alpha^q} {a_{t1}^{-q}\alpha^q+a_{t2}^{-q}(1-\alpha)^q}, $$
and put
$$ s(\alpha)=\sum_tB_ts_t(\alpha), \qquad f(\alpha)=s(\alpha)-\alpha. $$
The demand formula gives
$$ D_1(p)=\frac{s(\alpha)}{\alpha} =1+\frac{f(\alpha)}{\alpha}, $$
$$ D_2(p)=\frac{1-s(\alpha)}{1-\alpha} =1-\frac{f(\alpha)}{1-\alpha}. $$
Thus $f(\alpha)=0$ is exactly the market-clearing equation for normalized prices.

We will find a rational $\alpha$ with sufficiently small $|f(\alpha)|$.

5.2. An explicitly computable bracket

Each input coefficient lies between $2^{-L}$ and $2^L$, so
$$ A\leq2^{2L}. $$
Choose
$$ K=\left\lceil\rho(2L+1)\right\rceil+2, \qquad \delta=2^{-K}. $$
Then
$$ \delta\leq(2A)^{-\rho}, \qquad \delta\leq\frac14. $$

At $\alpha=\delta$, each type satisfies
$$ \frac{s_t(\delta)}{\delta} \leq \left(\frac{a_{t2}}{a_{t1}}\right)^q \frac{\delta^{q-1}}{(1-\delta)^q} = \left( \frac{(a_{t2}/a_{t1})\delta^{1/\rho}} {1-\delta} \right)^q \leq \left(\frac23\right)^q <1. $$
Averaging with weights $B_t$ proves $D_1<1$, hence
$$ f(\delta)<0. $$

Exchanging the two chores gives $D_2<1$ at $\alpha=1-\delta$. Since
$$ f(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)(1-D_2), $$
we obtain
$$ f(1-\delta)>0. $$

Continuity therefore guarantees a root in $[\delta,1-\delta]$.

5.3. A derivative bound

Differentiating the odds ratio defining $s_t$ gives
$$ s_t'(\alpha) = \frac{q\,s_t(\alpha)(1-s_t(\alpha))} {\alpha(1-\alpha)}. $$
Since $s_t(1-s_t)\leq1/4$ and $\sum_tB_t=1$,
$$ |f'(\alpha)| \leq 1+\frac{q}{4\alpha(1-\alpha)} \leq 1+\frac q\delta =:H $$
throughout $[\delta,1-\delta]$.

The rational number $H$ has bit length $O(K+L)$, even when $q$ has exponentially large numerical value.

5.4. Polynomial-time evaluation without enormous powers

We need approximate evaluations of $f$, not exact evaluations of its rational powers.

Evaluation lemma. Given rational $\alpha\in[\delta,1-\delta]$ and rational $0<\zeta<1$, one can compute rational $\widetilde f$ with
$$ |\widetilde f-f(\alpha)|\leq\zeta $$
in time polynomial in the input length, the bit length of $\alpha$, and $\log(1/\zeta)$.

Proof. Write
$$ z_t= q\left( \log\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} + \log\frac{a_{t2}}{a_{t1}} \right). $$
Then
$$ s_t(\alpha)=\sigma(z_t), \qquad \sigma(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}. $$
The derivative satisfies
$$ 0<\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\leq\frac14. $$

First, logarithms of positive rationals can be approximated efficiently by rational arithmetic. For a positive rational $v$ of bit length $h$, find an integer $k$ such that
$$ v=2^ku,\qquad 1\leq u<2. $$
Here $|k|=O(h)$ and $u$ has bit length $O(h)$. With
$$ w=\frac{u-1}{u+1}\in[0,1/3], $$
the convergent expansion is
$$ \log u= 2\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{w^{2i+1}}{2i+1}+R_N, $$
where
$$ 0\leq R_N \leq \frac{2w^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-w^2)}. $$
The same expansion with $w=1/3$ approximates $\log2$. Thus $\log v=k\log2+\log u$ can be approximated to absolute error $2^{-P}$ using $N=O(P+\log(h+1))$ terms. All intermediate rational numbers have polynomial bit length.

Approximate each of the two logarithms defining $z_t$ to error at most $\zeta/(2q)$. This produces a rational $\widetilde z_t$ with
$$ |\widetilde z_t-z_t|\leq\zeta. $$
The required precision is polynomial because $\log_2q=O(L)$. The derivative bound for $\sigma$ gives
$$ |\sigma(\widetilde z_t)-s_t(\alpha)|\leq\frac{\zeta}{4}. $$

Choose an integer $M$ with $2^{-M}\leq\zeta/4$. If $\widetilde z_t\geq M$, approximate $\sigma(\widetilde z_t)$ by $1$. If $\widetilde z_t\leq-M$, approximate it by $0$. In either case the error is at most
$$ e^{-M}\leq2^{-M}\leq\frac{\zeta}{4}. $$

Otherwise $|\widetilde z_t|<M$. Put $r=|\widetilde z_t|$ and approximate $E=e^r$ by its Taylor polynomial. For $n=N+1$, the remainder satisfies
$$ \left|E-\sum_{i=0}^N\frac{r^i}{i!}\right| \leq e^M\frac{M^n}{n!}. $$
The elementary bound $n!\geq(n/e)^n$ follows by integrating $\log x$ from $1$ to $n$. If
$$ n\geq8M, \qquad n\geq2M+\left\lceil\log_2(4/\zeta)\right\rceil, $$
then $e<4$ gives
$$ e^M\frac{M^n}{n!} \leq 2^{2M}\left(\frac12\right)^n \leq\frac{\zeta}{4}. $$
Thus only $O(M+\log(1/\zeta))$ Taylor terms are needed. The rational polynomial is positive and has polynomial bit length.

For nonnegative $\widetilde z_t$, use $E/(1+E)$; for negative $\widetilde z_t$, use $1/(1+E)$. Each expression has derivative in $E$ of absolute value at most one for $E\geq0$, so replacing $E$ by its Taylor approximation adds error at most $\zeta/4$.

We have therefore obtained a rational $\widetilde s_t$ with
$$ |\widetilde s_t-s_t(\alpha)|\leq\frac{\zeta}{2}. $$
Finally compute
$$ \widetilde f=\sum_tB_t\widetilde s_t-\alpha $$
by exact rational arithmetic. Because the positive weights sum to one,
$$ |\widetilde f-f(\alpha)| \leq \sum_tB_t\frac{\zeta}{2} =\frac{\zeta}{2} \leq\zeta. $$
The number of arithmetic operations and all operand lengths are polynomial in the stated parameters. $\square$

5.5. Sign-preserving bisection

Set
$$ \zeta=\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{16}. $$
Initialize
$$ \ell=\delta,\qquad u=1-\delta. $$
The invariant is
$$ f(\ell)<0<f(u). $$

At each iteration, let $\alpha=(\ell+u)/2$ and compute $\widetilde f$ to error at most $\zeta$.

  • If $|\widetilde f|\leq2\zeta$, return $\alpha$.
  • If $\widetilde f>2\zeta$, replace $u$ by $\alpha$.
  • If $\widetilde f<-2\zeta$, replace $\ell$ by $\alpha$.

In the second case $f(\alpha)>\zeta>0$; in the third case $f(\alpha)<-\zeta<0$. Thus the bracket invariant is preserved.

Whenever the bracket has width at most $\zeta/H$, continuity supplies a root $\alpha^*$ inside it, and the derivative bound implies
$$ |f(\alpha)|\leq H|\alpha-\alpha^*|\leq\zeta $$
at its midpoint. Hence $|\widetilde f|\leq2\zeta$, and the algorithm terminates.

The number of iterations is consequently
$$ O\left(\log_2(H/\zeta)\right) = O\left(K+\log_2q+\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\right). $$
Every midpoint is a rational of bit length polynomial in this quantity.

At termination,
$$ |f(\alpha)| \leq |\widetilde f|+\zeta \leq3\zeta. $$
Since both prices are at least $\delta$,
$$ |D_j(p)-1| \leq \frac{3\zeta}{\delta} = \frac{3\varepsilon}{16} $$
for each chore. In particular,
$$ 1-\varepsilon \leq D_j(p) \leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}. $$

Output the rational prices $(\alpha,1-\alpha)$ and the expressions
$$ x_{tj}(p)= \frac{b_ta_{tj}^{-q}p_j^{q-1}} {a_{t1}^{-q}p_1^q+a_{t2}^{-q}p_2^q}. $$
These are exact individual demands, with exactly satisfied earning requirements.

Finally,
$$ K=O(\lceil\rho\rceil L), \qquad \log_2q=O(L). $$
The iteration count, evaluation costs, and output lengths are therefore polynomial in $\tau$, $L$, $\lceil\rho\rceil$, and $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. This proves the two-chore theorem.

6. Multiple equilibria already occur at $\rho=2$

The preceding algorithm deliberately uses continuity rather than monotonicity or uniqueness.

Consider
$$ m=\tau=2,\qquad \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad b_1=b_2=1,\qquad \rho=2, $$
with coefficient vectors
$$ a_1=(1,3), \qquad a_2=(3,1). $$
Here $q=2$.

Any exact equilibrium must have $p_1+p_2=1$, by the earnings identity. Parametrize its price ratio by
$$ r=\frac{p_1}{p_2}>0, \qquad p_1=\frac r{1+r}. $$
The aggregate earnings share for chore one is
$$ s(r)=\frac12\left( \frac{9r^2}{1+9r^2} + \frac{r^2}{9+r^2} \right). $$
Direct common-denominator calculation gives
$$ s(r)-\frac r{1+r} = \frac{ r(r-1)(9r^2-32r+9) }{ (r+1)(r^2+9)(9r^2+1) }. $$
The denominator is positive for $r>0$. Consequently, exactly three positive price ratios clear the market:
$$ r=1, \qquad r=\frac{16+5\sqrt7}{9}, \qquad r=\frac{16-5\sqrt7}{9}. $$
Equivalently, the three first-coordinate prices are
$$ p_1=\frac12, \qquad p_1=\frac{5+\sqrt7}{10}, \qquad p_1=\frac{5-\sqrt7}{10}. $$
Each gives $D_1=D_2=1$ by the scalar characterization.

This example also shows that the existence potential from Section 4 need not be convex. Along normalized prices,
$$ \frac{d}{d\alpha}F(\alpha,1-\alpha) = D_1-D_2 = \frac{f(\alpha)}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}. $$
At $\alpha=1/2$, both types have $s_t(1-s_t)=9/100$. Hence
$$ s'(1/2)=\frac{18}{25}, \qquad f'(1/2)=-\frac7{25}, $$
and, because $f(1/2)=0$,
$$ \left.\frac{d^2}{d\alpha^2}F(\alpha,1-\alpha)\right|_{\alpha=1/2} =-\frac{28}{25}<0. $$
Thus a general convex-minimization argument using this potential is unavailable. Nonconvexity itself proves no computational hardness.

7. Remaining computational questions

The cited multiplicative tâtonnement bound includes an inverse instance-dependent lower-price parameter; its stated iteration bound does not establish the requested uniform bit complexity. [Chaudhury–Kroer–Mehta–Nan, Corollary 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00300v1)

The general homogeneous-disutility FPTAS has a positive linear coordinate-increment assumption. Its unequal-income extension also states a polynomial bound on the ratio of largest to smallest incomes. Neither condition automatically follows from the present inputs. [Boodaghians–Chaudhury–Mehta, Assumption 16 and Section 6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.06649)

For the first issue, take a CES bundle with $x_j=0$ and another coordinate positive. Writing
$$ C=\sum_{k\ne j}(d_{tk}x_k)^\rho>0, $$
we have, as $h\downarrow0$,
$$ d_t(x+he_j)-d_t(x) = (C+d_{tj}^{\rho}h^\rho)^{1/\rho}-C^{1/\rho} = \frac1\rho C^{1/\rho-1}d_{tj}^{\rho}h^\rho +o(h^\rho). $$
Dividing by $h$ gives a limit of zero. Thus no positive uniform linear lower bound applies.

For the second issue, the support reduction produces incomes $B_t=\mu_tb_t$, whose ratio can be exponential in input length. For example, $b_1=b_2=1$ and masses $2^{-n}$ and $1-2^{-n}$ give income ratio $2^n-1$.

The results proved here therefore establish:

  • Failure of the requested uniform polynomial bound for explicit rational prices.
  • Polynomial-size rational solutions when the numerical value of $\rho$ is polynomially bounded.
  • A polynomial algorithm for two chores in that regime, including exact individual demands.
  • Multiple equilibria and a nonconvex existence potential even at $\rho=2$.

They do not establish NP-hardness or settle computation for an arbitrary number of chores after removing the output-length obstruction.
