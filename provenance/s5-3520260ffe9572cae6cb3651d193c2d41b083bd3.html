The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a directed network \(G=(V,E)\), a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), source and destination vertices, integral request sizes \(q_t>0\), rational unit values \(p_t\ge 0\), and rational edge supplies \(b_e\ge 0\), choose accepted mass \(x_{tP}\ge 0\) routed on each available path \(P\) for type \(t\). Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P}p_tq_tx_{tP}\), subject to \(\sum_Px_{tP}\le\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P:e\in P}q_tx_{tP}\le b_e\) for each edge.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite directed graph whose underlying undirected graph is connected, with \(m=|E|\). Each buyer type \(t\in T\) specifies distinct vertices \(s_t,d_t\in V\), a request size \(q_t\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), and a unit value \(p_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\); each edge has supply \(b_e\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|T|\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The parameter \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Let \(\mathcal P_t\) contain all simple directed paths from \(s_t\) to \(d_t\), specified implicitly by the graph and endpoints. Accepted mass may be divided among these paths; unused mass and edge supply are permitted.
Choose real masses \(x_{tP}\ge0\) to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t}p_tq_tx_{tP}\), subject to \(\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t}x_{tP}\le\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t:e\in P}q_tx_{tP}\le b_e\) for every edge.

Is exact optimization of this continuous network allocation problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,|V|,\tau,L\).

The discrete multi-item arc-oriented winner determination problem with all-or-nothing buyer offers has an NP-complete decision version. Continuous multi-item arc-oriented winner determination with arbitrary demand bounds is already known to admit polynomial-time optimization, covering the allocation problem above; consequently this target is already settled. [Kaleta, Network winner determination problem](https://journals.pan.pl/Content/103618/PDF/acsc-28-1-4.pdf).

Result

Exact optimization is polynomial-time solvable, including recovery of an optimal allocation supported on polynomially many simple paths. A deterministic running-time bound in the bit model is
\( \bigl(\tau(m+|V|+1)+L\bigr)^{O(1)} \).

This agrees with Proposition 4 of [Kaleta, *Network winner determination problem*](https://journals.pan.pl/Content/103618/PDF/acsc-28-1-4.pdf), covering continuous multi-item arc-oriented allocation with arbitrary demand bounds. The following proof explicitly handles the implicitly specified simple paths.

1. A polynomial-size flow formulation

Write \(n=|V|\) and define \(D_t=q_t\mu_t\). Introduce variables:

  • \(z_t\): accepted quantity of type \(t\), measured in units of edge supply;
  • \(f_{te}\): quantity of type \(t\) flowing over edge \(e\).

Let \(\delta^+(v)\) and \(\delta^-(v)\) denote the outgoing and incoming edges at \(v\). Consider the linear program

\( \displaystyle \max \sum_{t\in T}p_tz_t \)

subject to

\( \displaystyle \sum_{e\in\delta^+(v)}f_{te}-\sum_{e\in\delta^-(v)}f_{te} =z_t\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{v=s_t\}}-\mathbf 1_{\{v=d_t\}}\bigr) \qquad(t\in T,\ v\in V), \)

\( \displaystyle \sum_{t\in T}f_{te}\le b_e\qquad(e\in E), \)

\( \displaystyle 0\le z_t\le D_t\qquad(t\in T),\qquad f_{te}\ge0\qquad(t\in T,\ e\in E). \)

It has \(\tau(m+1)\) variables, \(\tau n\) balance equations, and \(m+\tau\) further constraints apart from nonnegativity.

The zero solution is feasible. Moreover, \(f_{te}\le b_e\) follows from the shared capacity constraint and nonnegativity, while \(z_t\le D_t\). Hence its feasible region is a nonempty bounded polyhedron, so an optimum exists.

2. Equivalence to the path allocation problem

From paths to flows

Given a feasible path allocation, set

\( \displaystyle z_t=q_t\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t}x_{tP}, \qquad f_{te}=q_t\sum_{\substack{P\in\mathcal P_t\\e\in P}}x_{tP}. \)

A directed source-to-destination path has divergence equal to one at its source, minus one at its destination, and zero elsewhere. Summing these identities proves every balance equation.

The mass and edge constraints imply respectively \(z_t\le q_t\mu_t=D_t\) and \(\sum_t f_{te}\le b_e\). Finally,

\( \displaystyle \sum_t p_tz_t=\sum_t\sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t}p_tq_tx_{tP}. \)

Thus the flow formulation attains at least the path formulation’s optimal value.

From flows to simple paths

Fix a feasible flow solution and a type \(t\). Add a distinct artificial edge from \(d_t\) to \(s_t\), carrying flow \(z_t\). The augmented flow has zero divergence at every vertex: the artificial edge cancels the original divergence at both endpoints.

We prove the required circulation decomposition directly. If a nonnegative circulation has a positive edge, follow positive edges from its tail. Whenever a positive edge enters a vertex, flow conservation guarantees a positive outgoing edge. Since the graph is finite, this walk eventually repeats a vertex and therefore contains a simple directed cycle.

Subtract from that cycle the minimum flow on its edges. This preserves nonnegativity and zero divergence, and makes at least one positive edge zero. Repeating terminates after at most \(m+1\) iterations, because no edge becomes positive again. Consequently, the augmented circulation is a sum of at most \(m+1\) weighted simple cycles.

Discard cycles that do not contain the artificial edge. Removing the artificial edge from any remaining simple cycle gives a simple directed path from \(s_t\) to \(d_t\). Let the resulting paths and weights be \(P_{ti}\) and \(\alpha_{ti}>0\). Since the artificial edge initially carried \(z_t\),

\( \displaystyle \sum_i\alpha_{ti}=z_t. \)

Furthermore, discarding cycles only decreases original edge usage, so

\( \displaystyle \sum_{i:e\in P_{ti}}\alpha_{ti}\le f_{te}\qquad(e\in E). \)

Assign mass \(\alpha_{ti}/q_t\) to each resulting path, combining weights if needed. Then

\( \displaystyle \sum_{P\in\mathcal P_t}x_{tP}=\frac{z_t}{q_t}\le\mu_t, \)

and, for every edge,

\( \displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{\substack{P\in\mathcal P_t\\e\in P}}q_tx_{tP} \le\sum_t f_{te}\le b_e. \)

The objective remains \(\sum_t p_tz_t\). This proves the reverse inequality between the optimal values, and hence their equality.

3. Exact computation and running time

The standard exact rational linear-programming theorem supplies an optimal rational solution in time polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints and the total coefficient encoding length. Exact polynomial-time algorithms, including rational recovery, are discussed in [*Linear Programming using Limited-Precision Oracles*, Section 1.3](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/7507.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • All constraint coefficients are in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).
  • The objective coefficients \(p_t\) and supplies \(b_e\) are rational input data.
  • Computing \(D_t=q_t\mu_t\) increases bit length only additively in the bit lengths of its factors.
  • The feasible region is nonempty and bounded, as proved above.

Consequently, solving the formulation exactly takes

\( \displaystyle \operatorname{poly}\bigl(\tau(m+1),\ \tau n+m+\tau,\ L\bigr) =\bigl(\tau(m+n+1)+L\bigr)^{O(1)} \)

bit operations. In particular, this bound depends on the binary lengths of the request sizes, not their numerical magnitudes.

The subsequent circulation decompositions take
\(O\bigl(\tau(m+1)(m+n)\bigr)\) graph and rational-arithmetic operations and produce at most \(\tau(m+1)\) positive path allocations. These operations also have polynomial bit complexity: a common denominator for the finitely many rational flow coordinates has polynomial bit length; cycle subtraction preserves that denominator and never increases edge flows.

Thus both the exact optimal value and a compact optimal path allocation are computable in polynomial time. Only the \(\tau\) supported types are used. The discrete all-or-nothing problem’s hardness does not carry over, because accepted quantities here are continuous.
