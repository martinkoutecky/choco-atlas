The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), opportunity classes \(J\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), reserve prices \(r_j\), and the Chapter 1 class of strictly decreasing piecewise-linear utility schedules \(u_{tj}(p_j)\) with outside options \(o_t\), compute a bidder-optimal envy-free market-clearing outcome. The outcome has prices \(p_j\ge r_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), including outside-option mass \(x_{t0}\), satisfying \(\sum_{j\in J\cup\{0\}}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}x_{tj}\le q_j\); positive assignment mass goes only to utility-maximizing classes or to the outside option when it is utility-maximizing, and any undersold class has price \(r_j\). Bidder-optimality requires each type’s utility to be at least its utility in every other such equilibrium. Is there an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|J|,|\mathcal T|\), the schedule-description parameters, and input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(t\in\mathcal T\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=|J|\ge1\) opportunity classes, with binary-rational supplies \(q_j>0\), reserve prices \(r_j\ge0\), and outside utilities \(o_t\in\mathbb Q\). Supplies need not sum to one. The outside option \(0\) has unlimited capacity, price zero, and utility \(u_{t0}=o_t\).
Each schedule is explicitly specified on consecutive intervals \([s^{(h)}_{tj},e^{(h)}_{tj})\) partitioning \([r_j,\infty)\), with \(u_{tj}(p)=v^{(h)}_{tj}-c^{(h)}_{tj}p\), rational finite endpoints and coefficients, \(c^{(h)}_{tj}>0\), and unrestricted signs for \(v^{(h)}_{tj}\). Downward jumps are allowed; breakpoint values use the interval beginning there. Each schedule is globally strictly decreasing and reaches a value at most \(o_t\) at some finite price. Let \(S\) be the total number of intervals and \(L\) the total input bit length.
An equilibrium consists of prices \(p_j\ge r_j\) and masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in J\cup\{0\}}x_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tx_{tj}\le q_j\), and \(\sum_tx_{tj}<q_j\Rightarrow p_j=r_j\).
Write \(U_t(p)=\max\{o_t,\max_{j\in J}u_{tj}(p_j)\}\). Require \(x_{tj}>0\Rightarrow u_{tj}(p_j)=U_t(p)\), including \(j=0\); ties may be split arbitrarily. An equilibrium is bidder-optimal if \(U_t(p)\ge U_t(p')\) for every type and every other equilibrium \((x',p')\).
The computational problem is to compute an exact bidder-optimal equilibrium, or determine that none exists; no general-position promise is imposed.

Is this problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,S,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite unit-demand bidders and unit-capacity items, bidder-optimal envy-free outcomes with these schedules are computable in polynomially many arithmetic operations; market clearing is additionally guaranteed for continuous utilities or suitable nondegenerate discontinuous inputs. Partial result: polynomial time for fixed support size or fixed number of classes

The general polynomial-time-versus-$\mathrm{NP}$-hardness question remains unresolved here.

I prove an exact algorithm with running time
$$ (m+\tau+S)^{O(\min\{m,\tau\})}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$
Consequently, the stated problem is polynomial-time solvable whenever either the number of classes or the support size is fixed. The algorithm allows arbitrary rational masses and supplies, downward jumps, and ties.

The reduction using the support size is the main new result: it replaces prices by a vector of target utilities and uses continuous piecewise-affine inverse thresholds. I also give a direct polynomial-time algorithm for a single supported type and a fully specified $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$ upper bound.

1. Relaxed feasibility and the least price vector

A relaxed outcome satisfies all equilibrium conditions except the requirement that a class priced above reserve must be full. Let $\mathcal F$ denote the set of prices admitting a relaxed outcome.

For prices $p$, write
$$ D_t(p)=\{j\in J\cup\{0\}:u_{tj}(p_j)=U_t(p)\}, $$
where $u_{t0}=o_t$.

1.1. Feasibility at fixed rational prices

Construct a network with a source, one vertex per type, one vertex per option, and a sink. Its edges are:

  • Source to type $t$, with capacity $\mu_t$.
  • Type $t$ to option $j$, with capacity $1$, precisely when $j\in D_t(p)$.
  • Real option $j$ to the sink, with capacity $q_j$.
  • Outside option to the sink, with capacity $1$.

A flow of value $1$ is equivalent to a relaxed allocation. Indeed, the source capacities sum to $1$, so every source-to-type edge must be saturated.

To test equilibrium feasibility, impose lower bound $q_j$ on the option-to-sink edge whenever $p_j>r_j$. Such an edge must then carry exactly $q_j$.

These are polynomial-time tests. For completeness, a flow of prescribed value can be represented as a circulation by adding a sink-to-source edge with equal lower and upper bounds. A circulation with lower bounds reduces to ordinary flow by subtracting the lower bounds and introducing vertices that enforce the resulting balance requirements. All capacities and bounds here are finite binary rationals.

1.2. Nonemptiness and closure under minimum

The final interval of each schedule is unbounded and has strictly negative slope. Thus there is a rational vector $B\ge r$ such that
$$ u_{tj}(B_j)<o_t \qquad\text{for every }t,j. $$
Assigning everyone outside shows that $B\in\mathcal F$. Such a vector has polynomial encoding length: choose $B_j$ at least every final-interval starting point and every quantity
$$ \frac{v_{tj}^{\mathrm{last}}-o_t+1} {c_{tj}^{\mathrm{last}}}. $$

Lemma 1. If $a,b\in\mathcal F$, then their componentwise minimum $c=a\wedge b$ belongs to $\mathcal F$.

Proof. Monotonicity gives
$$ U_t(c)=\max\{U_t(a),U_t(b)\}. $$
Choose relaxed allocations $x$ at $a$ and $y$ at $b$. Partition the types into
$$ A=\{t:U_t(a)\ge U_t(b)\}, \qquad A^c=\{t:U_t(a)<U_t(b)\}. $$
Use $x_t$ for types in $A$ and $y_t$ for types in $A^c$.

Suppose $t\in A$ and $x_{tj}>0$ for a real class. If $a_j>b_j$, strict decrease would give
$$ u_{tj}(b_j)>u_{tj}(a_j)=U_t(a)\ge U_t(b), $$
which is impossible. Therefore $a_j\le b_j$, and this allocation remains maximizing at $c$.

If $x_{t0}>0$, then $U_t(a)=o_t$. Since $U_t(b)\ge o_t$ and $t\in A$, both utilities equal $o_t$, so the outside allocation remains maximizing.

Similarly, if $t\in A^c$ and $y_{tj}>0$, then $b_j<a_j$. Such a type cannot receive outside under $y$, because $U_t(b)>U_t(a)\ge o_t$.

Consequently, a class with $a_j\le b_j$ receives only portions of $x$, while a class with $b_j<a_j$ receives only portions of $y$. Capacities are therefore respected, and every type retains its full mass. ∎

Lemma 2. The set $\mathcal F$ has a componentwise least element $p^*$.

Proof. Define
$$ p_j^*=\inf\{p_j:p\in\mathcal F\}. $$
These numbers are finite, since $\mathcal F$ is nonempty and prices are bounded below by reserves.

For every positive integer $n$ and class $j$, choose $p^{n,j}\in\mathcal F$ with
$$ p_j^{n,j}<p_j^*+\frac1n. $$
Taking their componentwise minimum, Lemma 1 produces $p^n\in\mathcal F$ satisfying
$$ p_j^*\le p_j^n<p_j^*+\frac1n \qquad\text{for every }j. $$

Choose relaxed allocations $x^n$. The allocation vectors lie in a compact set: they are nonnegative, have fixed row sums, and satisfy the capacity inequalities. Pass to a subsequence converging to $x^*$.

Every schedule is right-continuous under the stated breakpoint convention. Since $p_j^n\ge p_j^*$ and $p_j^n\to p_j^*$,
$$ u_{tj}(p_j^n)\longrightarrow u_{tj}(p_j^*). $$
The maximum over finitely many options also converges.

If $x_{tj}^*>0$, then $x_{tj}^n>0$ eventually along the subsequence. Utility maximization at $p^n$ therefore passes to the limit. Row sums and capacities also pass to the limit, so $x^*$ is relaxed feasible at $p^*$.

By its definition, $p^*\le p$ for every $p\in\mathcal F$. ∎

1.3. Market clearing can be tested at the least vector

Lemma 3. Suppose $p\in\mathcal F$, $P$ is an equilibrium price vector, and $p\le P$. Then $p$ is also an equilibrium price vector.

Proof. Let $x$ be relaxed feasible at $p$ and $y$ be an equilibrium allocation at $P$. Set
$$ H=\{j:P_j>p_j\}, \qquad W=\{t:U_t(p)>U_t(P)\}. $$

Every class in $H$ is full under $y$, because $P_j>p_j\ge r_j$.

If $y_{tj}>0$ for $j\in H$, strict decrease gives
$$ U_t(p)\ge u_{tj}(p_j)>u_{tj}(P_j)=U_t(P). $$
Thus only types in $W$ receive classes in $H$ under $y$.

Conversely, types in $W$ allocate all their mass to $H$ under $x$. Every option outside $H$, including outside, has unchanged utility. If such an option were assigned under $x$, it would imply $U_t(p)\le U_t(P)$.

Writing
$$ q(H)=\sum_{j\in H}q_j, \qquad \mu(W)=\sum_{t\in W}\mu_t, $$
we obtain
$$ \mu(W)\le q(H)\le\mu(W). $$
The first inequality follows from $x$ and capacities. The second follows because $y$ fills $H$ using only types in $W$.

Hence equality holds. Under $x$, the mass of $W$ fills every class in $H$. Under $y$, all mass of $W$ goes to $H$.

Use $x_t$ for $t\in W$ and $y_t$ otherwise. Types outside $W$ have $U_t(p)=U_t(P)$ and receive only unchanged-price options under $y$, so their allocations remain maximizing. Classes in $H$ are full. Loads outside $H$ equal their loads under $y$; any such class priced above reserve at $p$ was also priced above reserve at $P$ and is full.

This gives an equilibrium at $p$. ∎

It follows that:

  • An equilibrium exists if and only if the equilibrium flow test succeeds at $p^*$.
  • Every equilibrium at $p^*$ is bidder-optimal.

For the second statement, every other equilibrium price vector $P$ lies in $\mathcal F$, so $p^*\le P$. Consequently,
$$ U_t(p^*)\ge U_t(P) \qquad\text{for every }t. $$

Thus computing $p^*$ suffices for the original task.

2. A finite-cell optimization principle

The algorithms below use the following elementary construction.

Consider a compact rational box in $\mathbb R^d$ and a collection of $N$ rational affine hyperplanes. A cell specifies, for every hyperplane, whether its defining affine expression is negative, zero, or positive, and intersects these requirements with the box.

2.1. Enumeration and exact feasibility

All nonempty cells can be enumerated incrementally. When a hyperplane is added, intersect each existing cell with its negative side, the hyperplane itself, and its positive side, retaining nonempty intersections.

Strict feasibility is tested by rational linear programming. Write strict inequalities as $a_i z<b_i$, replace them by
$$ a_i z+\delta\le b_i, \qquad 0\le\delta\le1, $$
and maximize $\delta$, retaining the original weak inequalities and equalities. The cell is nonempty exactly when the optimum is positive. Indeed, any point satisfying finitely many strict inequalities has a positive minimum slack.

I use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility and an attained finite optimum for a linear program with binary-rational coefficients can be computed exactly in polynomial time in its encoding length. The programs above have compact feasible regions, so their optima are attained whenever they are feasible.

The number of cells, including lower-dimensional cells, is at most
$$ \sum_{k=0}^{d}2^k\binom Nk. $$
To justify this bound, adding a hyperplane replaces each crossed old cell by its two sides and its intersection, increasing the count by two. The intersections are distinct cells in an arrangement on the new hyperplane, of dimension at most $d-1$. Thus the maximum count satisfies
$$ F(N,d)\le F(N-1,d)+2F(N-1,d-1), $$
with $F(0,d)=F(N,0)=1$. Induction gives the displayed bound.

Enumeration therefore takes $(N+1)^{O(d)}$ polynomial-time linear-programming calls.

2.2. Closure of a cell

Let $C$ be a nonempty cell and let $Q$ be obtained by replacing all its strict inequalities by weak ones. Then
$$ Q=\overline C. $$
Indeed, fix $z_0\in C$. For any $z\in Q$ and $0<\varepsilon<1$,
$$ (1-\varepsilon)z+\varepsilon z_0\in C. $$
Every originally strict inequality becomes strict, while equalities and weak inequalities remain satisfied.

This argument will allow optimization over $Q$, even when schedules jump on its boundary.

3. An exact algorithm in price dimension

Choose the polynomial-size vector $B\in\mathcal F$ from Section 1 and restrict prices to
$$ r_j\le p_j\le B_j. $$
The least vector belongs to this box.

Construct the following hyperplanes:

  • Every schedule breakpoint, expressed as a coordinate hyperplane.
  • Equality between any two affine pieces belonging to different options of the same type.
  • Equality between every real-option affine piece and the type’s outside utility.

Identically zero equations require no hyperplane; nonzero constant equations have no zero set. There are $O(S^2)$ hyperplanes, with polynomial-size rational coefficients.

On each cell, every schedule has a fixed active interval. All relevant utility comparisons have fixed signs. Therefore each type’s exact demand set is fixed, including all ties.

For every nonempty cell $C$:

  • Obtain a rational representative.
  • Run the relaxed flow test on its demand graph.
  • If feasible, form $Q=\overline C$ and minimize $\sum_j p_j$ over $Q$.
  • Record an optimal price vector.

Finally, select a recorded vector with the smallest objective value.

Proposition 4. The selected vector is exactly $p^*$.

Proof. If a cell passes the relaxed flow test, its demand graph is constant, so every point of $C$ belongs to $\mathcal F$. Hence every point of $C$ is componentwise at least $p^*$. Taking limits, every point of $Q$ is also componentwise at least $p^*$.

Thus every recorded vector has objective at least $\sum_jp_j^*$.

The cell containing $p^*$ passes the flow test. Its closure contains $p^*$, so its minimum objective equals $\sum_jp_j^*$.

A selected vector $z$ therefore satisfies
$$ z\ge p^*, \qquad \sum_jz_j=\sum_jp_j^*. $$
These two conditions imply $z=p^*$. ∎

The allocation associated with a cell need not remain feasible on its boundary. The proof uses the boundary only for its componentwise lower bound; after selecting $p^*$, the algorithm runs a fresh equilibrium flow test there.

By Section 2, the running time is
$$ (m+\tau+S)^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$

4. An exact algorithm in support dimension

This section gives the reduction that makes fixed support size tractable even when the number of classes is unrestricted.

4.1. Inverse thresholds

For a real number $w$, define
$$ \beta_{tj}(w)= \min\{p\ge r_j:u_{tj}(p)\le w\}. $$

The set is nonempty because the final affine piece tends to negative infinity. Its minimum is attained: its infimum can be approached from above, and right-continuity gives the required weak inequality at the infimum.

Strict decrease implies
$$ u_{tj}(p)\le w \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\ge\beta_{tj}(w). $$

Lemma 5. Each $\beta_{tj}$ is continuous, nonincreasing, and piecewise affine, with $O(S_{tj})$ pieces and polynomial-size rational coefficients, where $S_{tj}$ is that schedule’s interval count.

Proof. Consider a finite schedule interval $[s,e)$ with formula $v-cp$. Define
$$ h=v-cs, \qquad \ell=v-ce. $$
For $\ell<w\le h$,
$$ \beta_{tj}(w)=\frac{v-w}{c}. $$
At $w=\ell$, the threshold is $e$: all prices below $e$ have utility greater than $\ell$, and the utility at $e$ is at most $\ell$.

Let $h'$ be the utility at $e$ from the next interval. Global decrease gives $h'\le\ell$. Throughout
$$ h'\le w\le\ell, $$
the threshold is the constant $e$. The neighboring inverse-affine expressions both agree with this constant at their joining endpoints.

Above the utility at reserve, the threshold is the constant $r_j$. The final interval supplies an inverse-affine expression for every sufficiently small $w$.

These formulas prove continuity and the stated piece count. Their coefficients are input rationals or ratios of input rationals, so their encoding lengths are polynomial. ∎

The equality
$$ u_{tj}(\beta_{tj}(w))=w $$
need not hold. On a plateau corresponding to a downward jump, it fails except at the lower utility endpoint. This distinction is retained below.

4.2. Canonical prices determined by target utilities

Set
$$ H_t=\max\{o_t,\max_j u_{tj}(r_j)\}, \qquad \mathcal B=\prod_{t\in\mathcal T}[o_t,H_t]. $$
Every actual utility vector lies in this compact rational box.

For $w\in\mathcal B$, define canonical prices
$$ \pi_j(w)=\max_{t\in\mathcal T}\beta_{tj}(w_t). $$
Then
$$ u_{tj}(\pi_j(w))\le w_t \qquad\text{for every }t,j. $$

Lemma 6. If $p\in\mathcal F$ and $w_t=U_t(p)$, then
$$ \pi(w)\le p, $$
and the same relaxed allocation is feasible at $\pi(w)$, with actual utilities exactly $w$.

Proof. Since $u_{tj}(p_j)\le w_t$, the threshold property gives
$$ p_j\ge\beta_{tj}(w_t) \qquad\text{for every }t. $$
Taking the maximum proves $\pi_j(w)\le p_j$.

Suppose a relaxed allocation assigns positive mass of type $t$ to real class $j$. Then $u_{tj}(p_j)=w_t$. If $\pi_j(w)<p_j$, strict decrease would imply
$$ u_{tj}(\pi_j(w))>w_t, $$
contradicting the canonical-price inequality. Hence $\pi_j(w)=p_j$ for every positively allocated real edge.

A positive outside allocation already implies $w_t=o_t$. Thus every assigned option retains utility $w_t$, while all options have utility at most $w_t$. Since each supported type has positive mass, it receives at least one option, and its actual maximum utility remains $w_t$. Capacities and row sums are unchanged. ∎

For arbitrary $w\in\mathcal B$, define its equality graph by including real edge $(t,j)$ exactly when
$$ u_{tj}(\pi_j(w))=w_t, $$
and outside edge $(t,0)$ exactly when $w_t=o_t$.

A flow of value $1$ in this graph is equivalent to a relaxed outcome at $\pi(w)$ with utilities exactly $w$. The canonical-price inequality bounds every option above by $w_t$, and positive mass on an equality edge proves that the maximum equals $w_t$.

4.3. A polynomial-size hyperplane arrangement

Construct an arrangement in the $\tau$ target-utility coordinates as follows.

First, include every scalar endpoint at which an inverse threshold changes formula. For each schedule, these are the values
$$ u_{tj}(s^{(h)}_{tj}) \quad\text{and}\quad v^{(h)}_{tj}-c^{(h)}_{tj}e^{(h)}_{tj} $$
for finite interval endpoints. Also include the box boundaries.

Second, for every class $j$, every pair of types, and every pair of their candidate inverse-affine or constant expressions, include the hyperplane on which those expressions agree.

The total number of inverse expressions is $O(S)$, so this construction has $O(S^2)$ hyperplanes. All coefficients have polynomial encoding length.

On any nonempty cell $C$:

  • Each $\beta_{tj}(w_t)$ has a fixed affine expression.
  • Comparisons between these expressions have fixed signs.
  • Therefore each $\pi_j(w)$ equals one fixed affine expression, say $A_j(w)$.
  • Whether $u_{tj}(\beta_{tj}(w_t))=w_t$ is fixed.

The last assertion follows directly from Lemma 5’s formulas: inverse-affine interiors attain the target, jump-plateau interiors do not, and every exceptional endpoint is a scalar hyperplane.

Moreover,
$$ u_{tj}(\pi_j(w))=w_t $$
holds exactly when both
$$ \beta_{tj}(w_t)=\pi_j(w) \quad\text{and}\quad u_{tj}(\beta_{tj}(w_t))=w_t $$
hold. If the threshold is strictly below $\pi_j(w)$, strict decrease makes the utility strictly below $w_t$.

Thus the equality graph is constant throughout $C$.

4.4. Optimization over utility cells

For every nonempty utility cell $C$:

  • Compute its equality graph at a rational representative.
  • Test whether that graph supports a flow of value $1$.
  • If so, minimize the affine objective

$$ \sum_j A_j(w) $$
over $Q=\overline C$.

  • Record the vector $A(w)$ at an optimum.

Select a recorded price vector with minimum coordinate sum.

Proposition 7. This procedure returns $p^*$.

Proof. On every cell passing the flow test, $\pi(w)=A(w)$ is relaxed feasible. Therefore
$$ A(w)\ge p^* \qquad\text{for every }w\in C. $$
By continuity of the affine map, the same inequality holds on $Q$.

Now let $w_t^*=U_t(p^*)$. Lemma 6 shows that $\pi(w^*)$ is relaxed feasible and at most $p^*$. Leastness gives the reverse inequality, so
$$ \pi(w^*)=p^*. $$
The cell containing $w^*$ passes the flow test, and its objective minimum equals $\sum_jp_j^*$.

Every recorded vector is componentwise at least $p^*$, and the selected one has the same coordinate sum. It must therefore equal $p^*$. ∎

The running time is
$$ (m+\tau+S)^{O(\tau)}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$

Choosing between the price-dimensional and utility-dimensional algorithms proves the claimed bound
$$ (m+\tau+S)^{O(\min\{m,\tau\})}\operatorname{poly}(L). $$
A final equilibrium flow test supplies a bidder-optimal equilibrium or proves nonexistence by Lemma 3.

5. A direct algorithm for one supported type

Suppose $\tau=1$. Its mass is $1$; omit its type index and write outside utility $o$.

For a utility level $w$, define
$$ E(w)=\{j:\text{some }p\ge r_j\text{ satisfies }u_j(p)=w\}, \qquad K(w)=\sum_{j\in E(w)}q_j. $$

For $w>o$, a relaxed outcome with utility $w$ exists exactly when
$$ K(w)\ge1. $$
Necessity follows because all mass must go to classes attaining $w$. For sufficiency, set $p_j=\beta_j(w)$. Exactly the classes in $E(w)$ attain $w$ at those prices; their total capacity can accommodate the mass.

At $w=o$, canonical prices always admit the all-outside relaxed allocation.

5.1. Finding the maximum feasible utility

The range of a finite schedule interval is
$$ (v^{(h)}-c^{(h)}e^{(h)},\, v^{(h)}-c^{(h)}s^{(h)}], $$
and the final interval’s range is
$$ (-\infty,\, v^{\mathrm{last}}-c^{\mathrm{last}}s^{\mathrm{last}}]. $$

Let $\mathcal H$ be the list of all these upper endpoints. Define
$$ w^*= \max\bigl( \{o\}\cup\{h\in\mathcal H:h>o,\ K(h)\ge1\} \bigr). $$

This is the largest feasible utility. To verify that the finite candidate list suffices, take any feasible $w>o$. For every class in $E(w)$, select the schedule interval whose range contains $w$. Let $b$ be the smallest upper endpoint among those selected ranges. Then $b\ge w$, and every selected range still contains $b$: its lower endpoint is strictly below $w$, and its upper endpoint is at least $b$. Therefore
$$ K(b)\ge K(w)\ge1. $$
The value $b$ belongs to $\mathcal H$.

Set
$$ p_j^*=\beta_j(w^*). $$
These prices are relaxed feasible. For any other relaxed prices $P$, their actual utility $w$ is at most $w^*$. Since thresholds are nonincreasing,
$$ p_j^*=\beta_j(w^*)\le\beta_j(w)\le P_j. $$
Thus these are precisely the least relaxed prices.

There are $O(S)$ candidate levels, and range membership can be checked by scanning the schedules. This gives a direct polynomial-time implementation.

5.2. Exact existence criterion

Define
$$ H=\{j:p_j^*>r_j\}, \qquad E=\{j:u_j(p_j^*)=w^*\}. $$
An equilibrium exists exactly when
$$ H\subseteq E \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{j\in H}q_j\le1. $$

Necessity follows because every class in $H$ must be full and must receive maximizing mass.

For sufficiency, fill every class in $H$. If $w^*=o$, assign the remaining mass outside. If $w^*>o$, then $K(w^*)\ge1$, so the classes in $E\setminus H$ have enough remaining capacity to absorb the residual mass. Every assigned option is maximizing, and every positive-price class is full.

This criterion explicitly detects failure caused by a jump: a raised-price class whose utility falls strictly below $w^*$ cannot clear.

6. Polynomial certificates and exact oracle computation

The geometric argument also gives polynomial-size certificates and repairs the exact-recovery step without requiring an unspecified rational-reconstruction algorithm.

6.1. Polynomial-size rational least prices

Consider the price cell containing $p^*$ and its closed polyhedron $Q$. Every point of $Q$ is componentwise at least $p^*$, so $p^*$ uniquely minimizes $\sum_jp_j$ over $Q$. Hence $p^*$ is a vertex.

Here is an explicit bit bound. Let $b$ bound the binary lengths of every numerator and denominator occurring in the price-arrangement constraints and the price box. This bound is polynomial in the input size.

Set
$$ A=(m+2)(b+1), \qquad K=mA+m\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil+1. $$
Clearing denominators separately in each constraint gives integer coefficients, including its right-hand side, of absolute value at most $2^A$.

At a vertex, the active constraint normals span $\mathbb R^m$. Otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving every constraint, contradicting extremality. Select $m$ independent active constraints.

Cramer’s rule and the determinant expansion bound their determinants by
$$ m!\,2^{mA}<2^K. $$
Thus every reduced numerator and denominator of $p_j^*$ is at most $2^K$.

For allocations, let $D$ be the product of the input denominators of all masses and supplies. Scaling the network by $D$ gives integer capacities. Integral flow, or integral lower-bound circulation, then supplies an allocation whose entries are integer multiples of $1/D$. Since total mass is $1$, each allocation numerator lies between $0$ and $D$. Its encoding length is polynomial.

Therefore equilibrium existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$: whenever an equilibrium exists, Lemma 3 supplies one at $p^*$, and the preceding bounds give a polynomial-length certificate. Direct rational verification checks every equilibrium condition.

6.2. Exact computation in $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$

Put $Q_0=2^K$. For a class $j$ and rational $a$, consider the threshold predicate
$$ \exists p\in\mathcal F:\ p_j\le a. $$
It is equivalent to $p_j^*\le a$.

This predicate belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ even if certificates are restricted to the price bounds just proved and to allocations with denominator $D$. A positive instance has $p^*$ as such a witness; any witnessed relaxed price vector is componentwise at least $p^*$.

This is an $\mathrm{NP}$ predicate: guess the remaining bits and verify the completed certificate. Choose zero if the answer is yes, and one otherwise. A valid completion exists at every step, and the certificate length is polynomial.

Every recovered coordinate must equal $p_j^*$, by the rational-separation bound. The final equilibrium flow test then solves the original task. This proves
$$ \text{exact computation}\in\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}. $$

7. What remains open

The new algorithms settle all instances with fixed $m$ or fixed $\tau$, including arbitrary binary-rational quantities and degenerate discontinuities. Their exponent still grows when both parameters grow.

The finite-bidder result in [Dütting, Henzinger, and Weber](https://ingmarweber.de/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/An_Expressive_Mechanism_for-Auctions_on_the_Web_TEAC.pdf) does not itself remove that dependence. Common-denominator replication can introduce exponentially many bidders; for example, masses $2^{-k}$ and $1-2^{-k}$ require $2^k$ replicas in that construction.

No polynomial-time algorithm for unrestricted $m,\tau$ or $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness reduction is proved here.
