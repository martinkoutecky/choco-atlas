The anchor (named result in the paper)

Theorem 4.8 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite weighted compatibility graph \(H=(R,F)\) on student profiles and rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_r\), consider a mass-one population of identical advisors. Each advisor receives an integral bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{R}\), with matching utility \(u_H(z)\). Does there exist a polynomial-time algorithm, measured in the graph size and binary input length, that outputs a rational finite-support distribution \(x_z\ge 0\), with \(\sum_z x_z=1\) and \(\sum_z x_z z_r=\beta_r\), such that every pair of bundles in its support satisfies EF1 and \(\sum_z x_z u_H(z)\ge (2/3)\mathrm{SW}^*\), where \(\mathrm{SW}^*\) is the maximum welfare without EF1?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H=(R,F,w)\) be a finite simple undirected graph, with \(m=|R|\), nonnegative binary-rational edge weights, and supplies \(\beta_r\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\). Supplies are per advisor and need not sum to one.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here advisors have one identical type of mass one, so \(\tau=1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
For \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{R}\), form a graph with \(z_r\) distinct copies of each profile \(r\); every copy of \(r\) is adjacent to every copy of \(s\) precisely when \(\{r,s\}\in F\), with weight \(w(\{r,s\})\). There are no edges between copies of the same profile. Define \(u_H(z)\) as its maximum-weight matching value.
An allocation is an explicitly listed finite-support rational distribution \(x\), with \(x_z>0\) on its support, \(\sum_zx_z=1\), and \(\sum_zx_zz_r=\beta_r\) for every \(r\); bundle coordinates are encoded in binary.
It is EF1 if, for every ordered pair \((z,z')\) in its support, either \(z'=0\) or some \(r\) with \(z'_r>0\) satisfies \(u_H(z)\ge u_H(z'-e_r)\), where \(e_r\) is the coordinate unit vector.
Let \(\mathrm{SW}^*\) be the supremum of \(\sum_zx_zu_H(z)\) over such allocations without EF1.
Does there exist an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,|F|,\tau,L)\) that, on every input, outputs an EF1 allocation with \(\sum_zx_zu_H(z)\ge\frac23\mathrm{SW}^*\)?
For finite graphs allocated to \(n\) identical agents, a polynomial-time algorithm guarantees EF1 and welfare at least \(\left(\frac23+\frac{2}{9n-3}\right)\mathrm{SW}^*\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.01031).
No result settling this exact distributional approximation question was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined matching utility on integral profile bundles using distinct copies and complete compatibility between copies of adjacent profiles; the discrete model does not specify repeated profiles.
  • Excluded same-profile compatibility because the supplied compatibility graph, following the discrete model, has no loops.
  • Restricted weights and supplies to nonnegative binary rationals; supplies remain arbitrary per-capita quantities rather than a probability distribution.
  • Made comparison with an empty bundle automatically satisfy EF1, correcting the literal existential definition, which otherwise contradicts the intended existence guarantee when agents receive empty bundles.
  • Defined the unconstrained welfare benchmark as a supremum over the same finite-support rational allocations, avoiding an unstated attainment assumption.
  • Specified explicit support output and binary bundle-coordinate encoding, and distinguished output bundle support from the society's single supported advisor type.

Result

Yes. There is a deterministic algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,|F|,L)\) time that outputs an EF1 allocation with welfare at least \(\frac23\mathrm{SW}^*\). Its support has size at most \(|F|+m+1\), and its entire output has polynomial binary length.

The matching partition and one-vertex repair below follow the strategy underlying [Algorithm 6 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.01031). The additional ingredient is an exact batching procedure that handles exponentially many copies and agents without expanding them. We give the proof of the required \(\frac23\) guarantee explicitly.

Multiplying all edge weights by a common positive denominator preserves EF1 and welfare ratios. Hence, throughout the proof, assume the weights are nonnegative integers. This scaling has polynomial binary length.

1. Matching values and the welfare benchmark

Let \(A\) be the vertex-edge incidence matrix of \(H\), so that \((Ay)_r=\sum_{e\ni r}y_e\).

For every integer bundle \(z\),

\[ u_H(z)= \max\left\{\sum_{e\in F}w_e y_e: y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{F},\ Ay\le z\right\}. \]

Indeed, a matching determines integer edge multiplicities satisfying these degree inequalities. Conversely, for any such multiplicities, assign distinct copies of each profile to its incident edge occurrences. The degree inequalities provide enough copies, and the definition of the copy graph supplies every required edge.

Define the rational linear program

\[ V= \max\left\{\sum_{e\in F}w_e q_e: q\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{F},\ Aq\le\beta\right\}. \]

Its feasible region is nonempty and bounded: each coordinate satisfies \(q_{\{r,s\}}\le\min(\beta_r,\beta_s)\). Thus an optimal rational solution \(q\) exists and can be computed with polynomial encoding length by rational linear programming. Set \(q_e=0\) on zero-weight edges; this preserves optimality and feasibility.

Claim. \(\mathrm{SW}^*=V\), and the supremum is attained.

For the upper bound, choose a maximum matching for each supported bundle of any allocation, and let \(y^z\) be its edge-multiplicity vector. Then

\[ A\left(\sum_z x_z y^z\right) \le \sum_z x_z z=\beta. \]

Consequently, its welfare is at most \(V\).

For attainment, choose a positive integer \(n\) clearing all denominators of \(\beta\) and \(q\). Both \(n\beta\) and \(nq\) are integer vectors. The latter gives a matching inside the bundle \(n\beta\), of weight \(nV\). The fractional degree-packing bound gives the reverse inequality, so

\[ u_H(n\beta)=nV. \]

Assign probability \(1/n\) to \(n\beta\) and the remaining probability to the empty bundle, merging coincident bundles and omitting zero probabilities. This allocation has mean \(\beta\) and welfare \(V\).

We fix this \(n\) below. Taking a product of the relevant denominators shows that \(\log n\) is polynomially bounded.

2. Utilities are computable with binary bundle coordinates

We require an exact utility oracle whose running time depends polynomially on the binary length of \(z\), rather than on \(\sum_r z_r\).

The integer optimization above is maximum-weight uncapacitated \(b\)-matching with degree upper bounds. It reduces to the perfect version as follows.

Create two copies of \(R\), denoted \(r\) and \(r^\dagger\). Include:

  • each original edge, with its original weight;
  • its counterpart between the dagger vertices, with weight zero;
  • each edge \(\{r,r^\dagger\}\), with weight zero.

Prescribe degree \(z_r\) at both \(r\) and \(r^\dagger\), allowing integer edge multiplicities.

An original feasible vector \(y\), with degrees \(d=Ay\), extends to a perfect \(b\)-matching by using the same multiplicities in the dagger graph and multiplicity \(z_r-d_r\) on \(\{r,r^\dagger\}\). Conversely, the original-edge multiplicities in any perfect \(b\)-matching satisfy \(Ay\le z\). The objectives agree.

Maximum-weight perfect uncapacitated \(b\)-matching is polynomial-time solvable with binary degree bounds; importantly, the strongly polynomial bound does not expand the degree bounds into copies. See [Gabow, *Data Structures for Weighted Matching and Extensions to \(b\)-matching and \(f\)-factors*, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.07541). The reduction uses at most \(2m\) vertices and \(2|F|+m\) edges. Zero-degree vertices can be omitted.

Therefore \(u_H(z)\), and each \(u_H(z-e_r)\), can be computed exactly in polynomial time from binary-encoded coordinates.

3. An exact batching lemma

We represent a population of \(n\) agents by groups. Group \(i\) contains \(c_i\) agents in an identical state, where each \(c_i\) is a positive integer and \(\sum_i c_i=n\).

Suppose we must distribute \(J>0\) identical objects. For an agent initially in group \(i\), let \(f_i(k)\) be its integer-valued priority after receiving \(k\) objects. Assume \(f_i\) is nondecreasing and can be evaluated in polynomial time.

Batching lemma. We can distribute exactly \(J\) objects, creating at most one additional group, so that some integer threshold \(T\) satisfies:

  • every final priority is at least \(T\);
  • whenever an agent receives \(k>0\) objects, \(f_i(k-1)\le T\).

The procedure uses polynomially many evaluations in the number of groups, \(\log(J+1)\), and the binary length of the priority values.

Proof. Consider, for every agent in group \(i\), the \(J\) possible successive assignment priorities

\[ f_i(0),f_i(1),\ldots,f_i(J-1). \]

For an integer \(T\), define

\[ l_i(T)=\left|\{k:0\le k<J,\ f_i(k)<T\}\right|, \qquad h_i(T)=\left|\{k:0\le k<J,\ f_i(k)\le T\}\right|. \]

Monotonicity permits computing each count by binary search. Find the least integer \(T\) such that

\[ \sum_i c_i h_i(T)\ge J. \]

An upper search bound is \(\max_i f_i(J-1)\). Since the priorities are integers, minimality gives

\[ R:=\sum_i c_i l_i(T)<J. \]

First give every agent in group \(i\) exactly \(l_i(T)\) objects. This uses \(R\) objects. The remaining \(J-R\) objects can be assigned within the capacities \(c_i(h_i(T)-l_i(T))\).

Fill those capacities in a fixed group order. At most one group is partially filled. If that group receives \(d\) additional objects, write \(d=c_i a+b\), with \(0\le b<c_i\). Give \(a\) additional objects to \(c_i-b\) agents and \(a+1\) to the other \(b\). Thus only that group can split.

Every final count \(k_i\) lies between \(l_i(T)\) and \(h_i(T)\). Moreover, \(l_i(T)<J\) for every group: otherwise \(R\ge c_iJ\ge J\), a contradiction. Hence \(f_i(l_i(T))\ge T\), and monotonicity gives \(f_i(k_i)\ge T\).

If \(k_i>0\), then \(k_i-1<h_i(T)\), so \(f_i(k_i-1)\le T\). This proves both properties. \(\square\)

EF1-preserving application

For a family of bundles, let \(a\) be its minimum utility. It is EF1 exactly when every nonempty bundle \(z\) has some coordinate \(r\) satisfying \(u_H(z-e_r)\le a\).

Suppose this family is EF1 and we distribute \(J\) additional copies of profile \(r\). Apply the lemma with

\[ f_i(k)=u_H(z_i+k e_r). \]

These priorities are nondecreasing because adding vertices cannot decrease matching value.

Every priority is at least the old minimum \(a\), so the selected threshold satisfies \(T\ge a\). For an unchanged bundle, its previous deletion witness still has value at most \(a\le T\). For a bundle receiving \(k>0\) copies, deleting one newly added copy gives value \(f_i(k-1)\le T\). Every final bundle has value at least \(T\).

Thus the batch preserves EF1. It also preserves or increases total welfare, since each individual utility is nondecreasing.

This argument permits arbitrary utility plateaus; it does not assume concavity or strictly positive marginal gains.

4. Constructing the initial matching partition

Consider, only conceptually, the finite graph containing \(n\beta_r\) copies of each profile. Let

\[ N=\sum_r n\beta_r. \]

The multiplicities \(nq_e\) realize a matching \(M\) of weight \(nV\). The fractional degree-packing bound proves that \(M\) is maximum-weight in this finite graph.

A useful consequence is that every submatching \(S\subseteq M\) is maximum-weight on its own endpoint set. Otherwise, replacing \(S\) by a better matching on those endpoints would improve \(M\).

We partition the edges of \(M\) among the \(n\) agents. Initially there is one group of \(n\) empty agents. Process positive-weight edge types in nonincreasing weight order.

When processing edge type \(e\), distribute its \(J=nq_e\) occurrences using the batching lemma with priorities

\[ f_i(k)=\ell_i+k w_e, \]

where \(\ell_i\) is the current total weight of the assigned matching in group \(i\). Receiving an edge means receiving both its endpoint copies. Record each group's edge multiplicities and its last assigned edge type.

Every resulting bundle is an endpoint set of a submatching of \(M\), so its utility equals its recorded matching weight.

Let \(a\) be the minimum utility after all edge types have been processed. For every nonempty matching bundle \(i\), with last edge \(e_i\),

\[ \ell_i-w_{e_i}\le a. \tag{1} \]

To verify this invariant, at any batch the lemma gives a threshold \(T\). Every updated bundle satisfies \(\ell_i-w_e\le T\), while every final load is at least \(T\). An unchanged bundle retains its previous inequality, because the minimum load cannot decrease. Induction proves (1).

Also, \(e_i\) is a minimum-weight edge of its bundle, since edge types were processed in nonincreasing weight order.

Each edge-type batch creates at most one new group. Thus there are at most \(|F|+1\) groups at this point.

5. Repairing EF1 while retaining two-thirds of welfare

Mark every nonempty initial bundle \(z_i\) satisfying

\[ u_H(z_i-e_r)>a \quad\text{for every }r\text{ with }(z_i)_r>0. \]

These are exactly the bundles that violate EF1 relative to a minimum-utility agent.

A marked bundle contains at least three matching edges

A bundle with one matching edge becomes a single vertex after deleting an endpoint, giving utility zero. An empty bundle cannot be marked.

Now suppose a bundle consists of two matching edges, with weights \(b\ge c\). Its last edge has weight \(c\), so (1) gives \(b\le a\).

On its four vertices, consider all edges of weight greater than \(b\). Two such edges cannot be disjoint: their combined weight would exceed \(2b\ge b+c\), contradicting optimality of the assigned matching on those four vertices.

A pairwise-intersecting family of graph edges either has a common endpoint or contains a triangle. To see this, take two distinct edges \(uv,ut\). An edge intersecting both while avoiding \(u\) must be \(vt\).

Here a triangle of edges heavier than \(b\) is impossible: every three of the four vertices contain one edge of the assigned perfect matching, whose weight is at most \(b\). Therefore all edges heavier than \(b\) have a common endpoint. Delete that endpoint; if there are no such edges, delete any vertex.

The remaining three vertices have maximum matching size at most one, and every remaining edge has weight at most \(b\le a\). This bundle is consequently unmarked.

Thus every marked bundle contains at least three assigned matching edges.

The repair

For every marked bundle, remove one endpoint of its last assigned edge and put that vertex in the remaining pool.

Let \(Y_i\) be its repaired bundle. Since it was marked, \(u_H(Y_i)>a\). If the removed edge has endpoints of profiles \(r,s\), then deleting its other endpoint gives

\[ u_H(Y_i-e_s)=\ell_i-w_{e_i}\le a. \]

The equality follows from optimality of the remaining submatching on its endpoint set.

Every unmarked nonempty bundle already has a deletion witness of value at most \(a\), and its utility is at least \(a\). Hence the repaired partial allocation is EF1.

For welfare, a marked bundle contains at least three matching edges, and its last edge is a minimum-weight edge. Therefore \(w_{e_i}\le\ell_i/3\). The other matching edges remain feasible after the repair, so

\[ u_H(Y_i)\ge\ell_i-w_{e_i}\ge\frac23\ell_i. \]

Unmarked bundles lose nothing. Summing with group multiplicities, the repaired partial allocation has total welfare at least \(\frac23 nV\).

6. Allocating every remaining vertex

The pool contains both the initially unmatched vertices and the vertices removed during repair. Keep its multiplicity of each profile as a binary integer.

For each profile \(r\), distribute all remaining copies of that profile using the EF1-preserving application of the batching lemma from Section 3.

Each batch:

  • allocates exactly the specified number of copies;
  • preserves EF1;
  • does not decrease welfare;
  • creates at most one additional group.

After at most \(m\) batches, all vertices have been allocated. The final family is EF1 and has total welfare at least \(\frac23 nV\). There are at most \(|F|+m+1\) groups.

For each final group \(i\), output its bundle \(z_i\) with probability \(c_i/n\), merging identical bundles. Conservation of copies gives

\[ \sum_i \frac{c_i}{n}z_i=\beta, \qquad \sum_i \frac{c_i}{n}=1. \]

The allocation is EF1 because every pair of its supported bundles occurs among the final groups. Its welfare satisfies

\[ \sum_i\frac{c_i}{n}u_H(z_i) \ge\frac23 V =\frac23\mathrm{SW}^*. \]

7. Polynomial running time and output length

It remains to check that the construction never performs work proportional to \(n\) or \(N\).

The LP solution \(q\), the integer \(n\), and \(N=n\sum_r\beta_r\) have polynomial binary length. If \(N=0\), output the empty bundle with probability one.

Otherwise, throughout the algorithm:

  • group multiplicities are positive integers at most \(n\);
  • every actual bundle contains at most \(N\) vertices;
  • every batch budget \(J\) is at most \(N\);
  • there are at most \(|F|+m+1\) groups.

Let \(W\) be the maximum scaled edge weight. Utility queries in a vertex batch have the form \(u_H(z_i+k e_r)\), with \(k\le J\le N\). These hypothetical bundles contain at most \(2N\) vertices, and their utilities are at most \(2NW\). All queried numbers therefore have polynomial binary length.

For each batching operation, the outer threshold search requires polynomially many iterations in \(\log(N+1)\) and \(\log(W+1)\). Each threshold count is computed by another binary search over at most \(J\) positions, using the polynomial-time utility oracle. Edge batches use the simpler arithmetic priorities \(\ell_i+k w_e\).

Repair requires at most \(m\) deletion-value queries per group. There are at most \(|F|\) edge batches and \(m\) vertex batches. No step enumerates agents, vertex copies, or individual edge occurrences.

Finally, every output coordinate is at most \(N\), and every probability is \(c_i/n\). Together with the support bound, this proves polynomial output length and total running time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,|F|,L)\). Since \(\tau=1\), this is the requested bound. Returning to the original rational weights preserves the established EF1 condition and welfare ratio.
