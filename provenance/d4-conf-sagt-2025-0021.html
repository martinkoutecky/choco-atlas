The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), integer capacities \(c_t\), strict rankings of partner types, and a rational bound \(B\), decide whether a stable half-matching exists with average rank-weighted cost at most \(B\). A realization assigns mass to finite individual portfolios of full partners and half-match incidences; portfolio masses sum to \(\mu_t\), capacities hold for each individual, and compatible incidences are paired into mass-preserving edges and half-match cycles. Stability uses the paper’s blocking condition interpreted over partner types.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(T\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a binary-encoded integer capacity \(c_t\geq1\) and a strict complete ranking of partner types \(T\). Write \(r_t(s)\) for the rank of \(s\), starting at \(1\), and let \(B\in\mathbb Q\).
Individuals form disjoint atomless spaces \(X_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\). Distinct individuals of the same type may partner; self-partnership is forbidden. Partners of the same type are indifferent, with no additional tie-breaking.
A realization assigns individuals finite portfolios of distinct full partners and either zero or two distinct half-partners. Each type's portfolio masses are nonnegative reals summing to \(\mu_t\). Partner types may repeat within a portfolio.
Incidences must be realized by measurable, mass-preserving pairings between individuals: full edges have weight \(1\), half edges have weight \(\tfrac12\), and the half-edge graph consists of vertex-disjoint finite simple cycles of length at least \(3\). No individual pair has multiple edges.
Writing \(\theta(x)\) for the type of \(x\) and \(w_{xy}\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\) for pair weight, every individual satisfies \(\sum_y w_{xy}\leq c_{\theta(x)}\); an individual on a half-cycle is saturated. Unused capacity contributes zero cost.
Stability means that, after deleting a null set of individuals, every distinct pair \(x,y\) satisfies either \(w_{xy}=1\), or \(\sum_{z:\,\theta(z)\succeq_{\theta(x)}\theta(y)}w_{xz}=c_{\theta(x)}\), or the symmetric equality at \(y\). Thus half-matched pairs are also tested.
The average rank-weighted cost is \(\int_X\sum_y r_{\theta(x)}(\theta(y))w_{xy}\,d\nu(x)\), where \(\nu\) is population mass; both endpoints contribute. Decide whether a stable realization has cost at most \(B\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m=|T|\), \(\tau\), the total input bit length \(L\), and \(C=\max_t c_t\); polynomial time refers to the binary input length.

For finite Stable Fixtures instances, minimizing egalitarian cost over stable half-matchings is NP-hard, already when every capacity is \(1\); an arbitrary stable half-matching is computable in polynomial time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11456v3)
Existence results are known for distributional many-to-many matching with finite portfolios, but concern a different stability model and do not classify this half-cycle cost problem. [Source](https://www2.uibk.ac.at/downloads/c9821000/wpaper/2026-02.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the individual population atomless and specified measurable, mass-preserving incidence pairings rather than mere aggregate incidence balance.
  • Allowed partnerships between distinct individuals of the same type and repeated partner types in portfolios; prohibited self-partnership and multiple edges between the same individuals.
  • Completed rankings over all partner types, including one's own type; individuals of the same partner type are indifferent, and stability uses weak type comparisons without additional tie-breaking.
  • Required positive binary-encoded capacities. The finite-agent upper bound on capacity has no finite population-size counterpart in an atomless society, so capacities have no additional upper bound.
  • Specified the half-edge structure as vertex-disjoint finite simple cycles, with two half-partners and saturation for every participating individual.
  • Specified stability for every distinct pair outside a common null set, including half-matched pairs, using the capacity-prefix equality defining stable half-matchings.
  • Allowed nonnegative real portfolio masses; only the input masses and threshold must be rational.
  • Defined average cost per unit population mass, counted both endpoints with full and half weights, and assigned zero cost to unused capacity.
  • Identified m with the number of partner types and included capacity encodings in the input bit length.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already when \(C=1\) and all type masses are equal dyadic rationals.

A general decision algorithm runs in \(m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(L)\) bit time. Its dependence on capacities is through their binary encodings, rather than through \(C\) itself. In the stated input, \(m=|T|=\tau\).

The proof below interprets the atomless populations in the usual distributional sense: interval populations are permissible representatives of the given support.

1. Atomless stability forces saturation and uniform cutoffs

For an individual \(x\), define

\(A_x(s)=\sum_{z:\,r_{\theta(x)}(\theta(z))\leq r_{\theta(x)}(s)}w_{xz}\).

Thus an individual of type \(t\) rejects an additional partner of type \(s\) precisely when \(A_x(s)=c_t\).

All assertions about individuals below are made outside the null set allowed in the stability definition.

Lemma 1. In every stable realization, almost every individual is saturated.

Proof. Suppose a positive-mass set of individuals is unsaturated. Some type \(t\) then has a positive-mass set \(U\) of unsaturated individuals.

Choose \(x\in U\). Its portfolio is finite, so the set consisting of \(x\) and its full partners has mass zero. Consequently, there exists \(y\in U\) distinct from \(x\) with \(w_{xy}\neq1\).

Both individuals have total incident weight strictly below \(c_t\). Hence neither stability equality can hold for this pair, a contradiction. \(\square\)

For each type \(t\), let \(q_t\) be the largest rank of a partner type occurring with positive aggregate incidence mass among individuals of type \(t\). Saturation and \(\mu_t>0\) ensure that this is defined. There are finitely many partner types, so almost every type-\(t\) individual has all partner ranks at most \(q_t\).

Lemma 2. Every stable realization satisfies, for all \(t,s\in T\),

\[q_t\leq r_t(s)\quad\text{or}\quad q_s\leq r_s(t).\tag{1}\]

This includes \(t=s\), and therefore \(q_t\leq r_t(t)\).

Proof. If \(q_t>r_t(s)\), a positive aggregate incidence mass goes from type \(t\) to types strictly worse than \(s\). Since portfolios are finite and capacities are bounded by \(C\), a positive-mass set of type-\(t\) individuals therefore satisfies \(A_x(s)<c_t\).

If both inequalities in (1) fail, there are positive-mass sets of individuals of types \(t\) and \(s\) that fail their respective stability equalities. Choose one individual from the first set, and then choose an individual from the second set outside its finite set of full partners and outside itself. This gives a blocking pair.

The same argument applies when \(t=s\). \(\square\)

Conversely, if everyone is saturated, all type-\(t\) portfolios use ranks at most \(q_t\), and (1) holds, then the realization is stable. Indeed, for every pair of types, (1) supplies an endpoint whose entire capacity is assigned to partner types at least as good as the other endpoint's type.

2. An exact formulation using cutoffs and linear programs

Let

\(d_t=\mu_t c_t,\qquad y_{ts}=\int_{X_t}\sum_{z\in X_s}w_{xz}\,d\nu(x).\)

Here \(y_{tt}\) counts incidences at both endpoints of same-type edges. Mass-preserving pairings give \(y_{ts}=y_{st}\).

For a fixed cutoff vector \(q\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{T}\) satisfying (1), consider the following rational linear feasibility problem:

\(y_{ts}\geq0,\qquad y_{ts}=y_{st},\)

\[\sum_{s\in T}y_{ts}=d_t\quad(t\in T),\tag{2}\]

\[y_{ts}=0\quad\text{if }r_t(s)>q_t\text{ or }r_s(t)>q_s,\tag{3}\]

\[\sum_{t,s\in T}r_t(s)y_{ts}\leq B.\tag{4}\]

Every stable realization of cost at most \(B\) yields a feasible solution for its cutoff vector, by Lemmas 1 and 2.

We next prove the converse, including the requirement that individual partners be distinct.

Lemma 3. Every feasible instance of (2)–(4) has a valid stable realization of cost at most \(B\), using only full edges.

Proof. A nonempty rational polyhedron contains a rational point. Choose a rational feasible matrix \(y\).

Choose a positive integer \(Q\) such that every \(Q\mu_t\) and \(Qy_{ts}\) is integral and every \(Qy_{tt}\) is even. For type \(t\), create \(Q\mu_t\) temporary vertices, each with \(c_t\) stubs.

By (2), the number of these stubs is

\(Q\mu_t c_t=\sum_s Qy_{ts}.\)

Label exactly \(Qy_{ts}\) type-\(t\) stubs with partner type \(s\). Pair the stubs between distinct types using symmetry, and pair the same-type stubs among themselves using their even counts. This produces a finite multigraph with the required degrees and type incidence counts. It may contain loops and parallel edges.

We remove those defects while preserving degrees and incidence proportions. Put \(k=2C+1\), and replace each temporary vertex \(v\) by copies \(v_a\), indexed by \(a\in\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z\).

For distinct temporary vertices \(u,v\) joined by \(p\) parallel edges, fix an orientation of their unordered pair and replace their edges by

\(\{u_a,v_{a+j}\}\quad(a\in\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z,\ 0\leq j<p).\)

Since \(p\leq C<k\), these edges are distinct.

If a temporary vertex \(v\) has \(\ell\) loops, replace them by

\(\{v_a,v_{a+j}\}\quad(a\in\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z,\ 1\leq j\leq\ell).\)

Each former loop contributes degree two to every copy. Because \(2\ell\leq C\) and \(k=2C+1\), these edges contain neither loops nor repetitions: the shifts \(j\) are nonzero and distinct even up to sign.

The resulting graph is simple, and each copy of a type-\(t\) vertex has degree \(c_t\).

Replace every graph vertex by an interval of mass \(1/(Qk)\). For every graph edge, pair equal coordinates in its two intervals. These pairings are measurable and mass-preserving. Every individual has exactly \(c_t\) distinct full partners, and self-partnership is impossible.

The type masses are \(\mu_t\), and the aggregate incidence matrix is exactly \(y\). Condition (3) places every partner within the prescribed cutoff. Condition (1) consequently proves stability. The cost is the left-hand side of (4).

There are no half edges, so the half-cycle requirement is satisfied vacuously. \(\square\)

Thus the cutoff linear programs characterize the decision problem exactly.

Each program has \(O(\tau^2)\) variables and constraints, with rational encoding length polynomial in \(L\). Products \(\mu_t c_t\) have polynomial bit length even when \(C\) is large. Enumerating the cutoffs and solving the programs therefore takes

\(m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(L)\)

bit time.

Guessing a cutoff vector and checking its linear program is a nondeterministic polynomial-time algorithm. Equivalently, a feasible rational matrix of polynomial encoding length supplies a certificate. Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). The potentially large graph in Lemma 3 is an existence construction; it need not be written into the certificate.

3. A rounding lemma for equal masses and capacity one

The reverse direction of the hardness reduction requires more than lifting a finite instance: arbitrary population mixtures must also be controlled.

Consider \(n\) types corresponding to finite agents. Their rankings of other original types are the finite rankings, and each ranks its own type after all other original types.

Suppose \(p\) is a symmetric nonnegative matrix on these types satisfying

\[\sum_j p_{ij}=1.\tag{5}\]

For each \(i\), define its worst supported rank by

\(W_i=\max\{r_i(j):p_{ij}>0\}.\)

Assume that

\[W_i\leq r_i(j)\quad\text{or}\quad W_j\leq r_j(i)\qquad\text{for all }i,j.\tag{6}\]

Define its rank cost as \(H(p)=\sum_{i,j}r_i(j)p_{ij}\).

Lemma 4. Such a matrix can be replaced, without increasing \(H\), by a matrix whose components are isolated diagonal entries of value \(1\), full pairs, and simple half-cycles.

Proof. Form the undirected support graph, including a loop at \(i\) when \(p_{ii}>0\).

For a positive off-diagonal edge \(\{i,j\}\), the definition of \(W\) gives

\(r_i(j)\leq W_i,\qquad r_j(i)\leq W_j.\)

Together with (6), this implies equality at at least one endpoint. Assign the edge to an endpoint at which it is the worst supported partner. A positive loop is likewise the worst supported partner at its vertex, because own type is ranked last among original types.

Strict rankings imply that each vertex can receive at most one assigned edge. Thus every connected component has at most as many edges as vertices.

A vertex of graph degree one has exactly one off-diagonal entry. Equation (5) forces that entry to be \(1\), and symmetry and (5) force its neighbor to have no other positive entry. Such a component is an isolated full pair.

Every other component has minimum graph degree at least two, counting loops twice. Since its edge count is at most its vertex count, the degree-sum identity forces every vertex to have degree exactly two. Consequently, it is an isolated loop or a simple cycle.

An isolated loop has value \(1\). On a simple cycle, (5) forces successive edge weights to alternate between \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\). On an odd cycle, this forces \(\alpha=\tfrac12\).

On an even cycle, the matrix is a convex combination of the two alternating perfect matchings. Choose the matching with smaller rank cost. Its cost is no greater than the convex combination's cost.

These replacements only remove supported partners. Thus every new worst rank is at most its old value, which preserves (6). This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

Interpret every isolated diagonal entry as an unmatched finite agent, and retain the full pairs and half-cycles.

The result is a finite stable half-matching. To check this carefully, give an unmatched agent worst rank \(n\), corresponding to its diagonal entry before deletion. For a distinct pair \(i,j\), (6) guarantees an endpoint that is matched entirely to agents at least as good as the other endpoint. That endpoint cannot be unmatched, since every distinct original type has rank strictly below \(n\).

If the decoded matching has \(h\) unmatched agents, then

\[H(p')=\operatorname{cost}(M)+nh.\tag{7}\]

4. Polynomial-time hardness reduction

Use the finite capacity-one egalitarian half-matching threshold problem from the question. Its decision version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard; the corresponding stable-partition threshold hardness is stated explicitly in [Glitzner–Manlove, Theorem 4.16](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.00437v1#S4).

We also use two finite results: a stable half-matching can be computed in \(O(n^3)\) combinatorial time, and every agent receives the same total allocation in all stable half-matchings of the same instance. These are [Theorem 4.4 and Theorem 5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11456v3). Their hypotheses apply here: the finite instance has strict complete preferences and integral capacities equal to one.

Let the source instance have \(n\) agents and cost threshold \(K\). Compute any stable half-matching and let \(h\) be its number of unmatched agents. Allocation invariance shows that this same \(h\) occurs in every stable half-matching of the instance.

Put \(N=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}\). Construct a society with \(N\) types, each of mass \(1/N\) and capacity \(1\).

For each original agent \(i\), its type ranks:

  • the other original types in the source order;
  • its own type, at rank \(n\);
  • all added types.

Each of the \(N-n\) added types ranks its own type first, followed by the remaining types in any strict order.

Set

\[B=\dfrac{K+nh+(N-n)}{N}.\tag{8}\]

All masses are dyadic rationals.

Forward implication

Given a finite stable half-matching \(M\):

  • realize each full pair by pairing equal coordinates in its two type intervals;
  • realize each half-cycle by placing equal-coordinate individuals on copies of that finite cycle;
  • give each unmatched original type a full same-type matching;
  • give each added type a full same-type matching.

A same-type matching can be realized on its interval by the fixed-point-free involution that exchanges its two halves.

All portfolios are finite and have distinct partners. The half-edge components are disjoint finite simple cycles.

For distinct original types, finite stability supplies the required endpoint equality. A finite full pair also supplies that equality at both endpoints, since capacities are one. Replacing an unmatched agent by same-type partners changes none of its cumulative allocations through the rank of a distinct original type. Same-type pairs satisfy the equality because everyone is saturated and own type is no better than their assigned original partners. Added types reject every distinct type because their portfolios consist entirely of their first choice.

The realization is therefore stable. Its cost is

\(\dfrac{\operatorname{cost}(M)+nh+(N-n)}{N}.\)

Thus \(\operatorname{cost}(M)\leq K\) implies population cost at most \(B\).

Reverse implication

Suppose the constructed society has a stable realization of cost at most \(B\).

By Lemmas 1 and 2, an added type has cutoff at most its own rank, namely \(1\). It therefore partners only with its own type and contributes exactly \(1/N\) to the cost.

An original type has cutoff at most its own rank \(n\), so it has no partners among added types.

Let \(y\) be the aggregate incidence matrix restricted to original types, and put \(p_{ij}=Ny_{ij}\). This is symmetric and has row sums one. Its worst supported ranks satisfy (6), by Lemma 2. Moreover,

\(\operatorname{cost}(\text{realization})=\dfrac{H(p)+(N-n)}{N}.\)

Apply Lemma 4 and decode the rounded matrix into a finite stable half-matching \(M\). Allocation invariance guarantees that \(M\) has exactly \(h\) unmatched agents. Hence (7) gives

\(\operatorname{cost}(M)+nh=H(p')\leq H(p).\)

Using the assumed cost bound and (8),

\(H(p)\leq NB-(N-n)=K+nh.\)

Therefore \(\operatorname{cost}(M)\leq K\). This proves the reverse implication.

5. Running times and conclusion

The reduction uses \(N<2n\) types, has \(C=1\), and produces encoding length \(O(n^2\log n+\operatorname{bits}(K))\). Its finite preprocessing uses \(O(n^3)\) combinatorial operations; the entire reduction takes polynomial bit time.

For general inputs, the cutoff algorithm takes \(m^\tau\operatorname{poly}(L)\) bit time and requires no expansion proportional to \(C\). In particular, fixed support size gives polynomial time in the binary input length.

When support size varies, the reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness already for \(C=1\). Combined with Section 2, this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
