Theorem 1.3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) on agent types \(t\in T\), rational supplies \(\nu_s\) on object types \(s\in S\), with both totals equal to \(1\), and partial-order preferences for each agent type, is there a feasible target assignment \(x\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\nu_s\) whose mass unpopularity margin is at most \(\kappa\)? Define that margin as \(U(x)=\max_z\sum_{t,s,s'}\sigma_t(s',s)z_{tss'}\), where \(z_{tss'}\ge 0\) couples the target object \(s\) and rival object \(s'\) for the same type-\(t\) mass, satisfies \(\sum_{s'}z_{tss'}=x_{ts}\) and \(\sum_{t,s}z_{tss'}=\nu_{s'}\), and uses only acceptable pairs; \(\sigma_t(s',s)\) is \(1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) according as type \(t\) prefers \(s'\), prefers \(s\), or is indifferent or incomparable.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|T|\) agent types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Object types form a set \(S\), with \(m=|S|\), and have positive binary-rational supplies. Both total mass and total supply equal \(1\).
The input specifies acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq T\times S\), a strict partial order \(\succ_t\) on each type's acceptable objects, and a binary-rational threshold \(\kappa\geq0\). Objects have no preferences; incomparable objects receive a zero vote.
A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{ts}\geq0\), zero outside \(E\), satisfying \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}=\nu_s\). Assume at least one feasible assignment exists.
Define \(\sigma_t(s',s)=1\) if \(s'\succ_t s\), \(\sigma_t(s',s)=-1\) if \(s\succ_t s'\), and \(\sigma_t(s',s)=0\) otherwise.
The mass unpopularity margin is \(U(x)=\max_z\sum_{t,s,s'}\sigma_t(s',s)z_{tss'}\), where \(z_{tss'}\geq0\), \(\sum_{s'}z_{tss'}=x_{ts}\), and \(\sum_{t,s}z_{tss'}=\nu_{s'}\); require \(z_{tss'}=0\) unless both \((t,s)\) and \((t,s')\) belong to \(E\). Rivals reassign all mass and use all supplies.
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the explicit acceptability and preference relations; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding whether there exists a feasible assignment \(x\) with \(U(x)\leq\kappa\) solvable in polynomial time, or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite agents and indivisible objects, bounded unpopularity margin among perfect matchings is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete even with strict rankings, and \(\mathsf{W}_l[1]\)-hard parameterized by the integer margin bound with weak rankings ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10984)).
Popular mixed matchings with weak rankings can be computed in polynomial time, but their votes compare independently drawn assignments, rather than the maximizing couplings defining \(U\) here ([source](https://www.columbia.edu/~cs2035/courses/ieor8100.F18/popular.pdf)). No result settling this precise coupling-based decision problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made acceptability an explicit input relation and required both the target and rival objects in each coupling entry to be acceptable to that agent type.
  • Interpreted partial-order preferences as strict partial orders, with zero votes for identical or incomparable objects, as in the discrete definition.
  • Specified a nonnegative binary-rational threshold, replacing the discrete nonnegative integer margin bound.
  • Allowed arbitrary real assignment and coupling masses and required positive supplies on the listed object support.
  • Carried over the assumption that an assignment exists as a promise of feasible mass transportation.
  • Defined m as the object support size and L as the total binary input length; specified explicit encoding of acceptability and preference relations.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even with strict rankings and dyadic masses, supplies, and threshold.

The key fact is that the maximizing-coupling definition makes \(U\) concave in the incumbent assignment. Consequently, allowing divisible assignments does not improve the minimum beyond the vertices of the assignment polytope. For appropriately scaled integer margins, those vertices recover indivisible assignments.

1. Concavity and vertex minimizers

Write \(S_t=\{s:(t,s)\in E\}\), and let \(P\) be the feasible assignment polytope. By hypothesis, \(P\) is nonempty. It is bounded because every coordinate lies in \([0,1]\).

For \(x\in P\), let \(Z(x)\) denote its feasible coupling polytope. This polytope is nonempty: the diagonal coupling \(z_{tss}=x_{ts}\), with all other entries zero, is feasible. It is compact, since its coordinates are nonnegative and sum to \(1\). Thus the maximum defining \(U(x)\) is attained.

Lemma 1. The function \(U\) is concave on \(P\).

Proof. Fix \(x,y\in P\) and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). Choose optimal couplings \(z^x\in Z(x)\) and \(z^y\in Z(y)\). The coupling \(z=\lambda z^x+(1-\lambda)z^y\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{s'}z_{tss'}=\lambda x_{ts}+(1-\lambda)y_{ts}\)

and

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,s}z_{tss'}=\lambda\nu_{s'}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{s'}=\nu_{s'}.\)

It also respects all acceptability restrictions. Therefore it is feasible for \(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\). Linearity of the coupling objective gives

\(\displaystyle U(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\geq\lambda U(x)+(1-\lambda)U(y).\)

This proves concavity. \(\square\)

Corollary 2. Some vertex of \(P\) minimizes \(U\).

Proof. Every \(x\in P\) is a convex combination of vertices: \(x=\sum_i\lambda_i v^i\). Repeated application of Lemma 1 gives

\(\displaystyle U(x)\geq\sum_i\lambda_iU(v^i)\geq\min_iU(v^i).\)

Since \(P\) has finitely many vertices, its smallest vertex value is attained and is no larger than the value at any feasible assignment. \(\square\)

In particular, if any feasible assignment satisfies the threshold, then a vertex does too.

2. Transportation vertices and integrality

We need a basic property of transportation polytopes, including those with forbidden edges.

Lemma 3. Consider nonnegative edge variables on a bipartite graph, with prescribed sums at every vertex. At any vertex of the resulting transportation polytope:

  • The graph of positive coordinates is a forest.
  • Every positive coordinate is an integer linear combination of the prescribed margins.
  • If all margins are integers, all coordinates are integers.

Proof. If the positive support contains a cycle, alternately add and subtract a sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\) around that cycle. Both resulting vectors remain nonnegative and preserve all margins. The original vector is their midpoint, contradicting its being a vertex. Hence the positive support is a forest.

Let \(e=(a,b)\) be a positive edge, with \(a\) on the left. Delete \(e\) from its support tree, and let \(C\) be the component containing \(a\). Denote the left margins by \(r_a\) and the right margins by \(c_b\). Summing left margins in \(C\) and subtracting right margins in \(C\) cancels every internal edge. The only remaining positive edge crossing the cut is \(e\), so

\(\displaystyle x_e=\sum_{a'\in C\cap A}r_{a'}-\sum_{b'\in C\cap B}c_{b'}.\)

This proves the remaining statements. \(\square\)

Consequently, a nonempty bounded transportation polytope with integer margins has an integral optimizer for every linear objective: a linear objective attains an optimum at a vertex, and Lemma 3 applies there.

3. A polynomial reduction from indivisible assignments

We use the following established result. Given a bipartite graph with equally many agents and objects, strict rankings over acceptable objects, and a nonnegative integer \(k\), deciding whether there is a perfect matching whose unpopularity margin against all perfect matchings is at most \(k\) is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete. This is [Theorem 1.3 of Kavitha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2110.10984v3#S1.Thm3).

Let such an instance have \(n\) agents and \(n\) objects. For a perfect matching \(M\), write its integer margin as

\(\displaystyle u(M)=\max_{M'}\sum_a \sigma_a(M'(a),M(a)),\)

where the maximum ranges over perfect matchings.

Feasibility and dyadic normalization

We may restrict to source graphs admitting a perfect matching. Indeed, existence of a perfect matching can be tested in polynomial time. A source graph without one is a no-instance and can be mapped to a fixed feasible no-instance of our problem.

For completeness, such a fixed instance has one type of mass \(1\), three acceptable objects ranked \(a\succ b\succ c\), supplies \((1/4,1/4,1/2)\), and threshold \(0\). Its incumbent assignment is uniquely determined. The coupling

\(\displaystyle z_{aa}=0,\qquad z_{ac}=z_{ba}=z_{cb}=z_{cc}=1/4,\)

with every other entry zero, satisfies all margins and has objective \(1/4>0\). Thus this feasible instance is a no-instance.

Now let \(N\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(N\geq n\). Pad the source instance to \(N\) agents and \(N\) objects by adding isolated agent–object pairs, each agent accepting only its paired object. Every perfect matching contains these forced pairs, and they contribute zero votes. Padding therefore preserves every original matching’s unpopularity margin.

Group agents having identical acceptable sets and identical rankings into types. For a type \(t\), let \(c_t\) be the number of agents in its group. Define

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=\frac{c_t}{N},\qquad \nu_s=\frac1N,\qquad \kappa=\frac{k}{N}.\)

Use the group’s acceptable set and strict ranking as its type data.

All masses and supplies are positive and sum to \(1\). They and the threshold are dyadic. The representation uses only the positive-mass types, with \(\tau\leq N\) and \(m=N\). Grouping, padding, and writing the explicit relations take polynomial time.

Recovering an indivisible incumbent

Let \(x\) be a vertex of the constructed assignment polytope, and put \(X=Nx\). Scaling preserves vertices. The margins of \(X\) are the integers \(c_t\) and \(1\), so Lemma 3 shows that \(X\) is integral.

Each column sums to \(1\); hence \(X_{ts}\in\{0,1\}\). Type \(t\) receives exactly \(c_t\) objects. Assign these objects bijectively to the \(c_t\) individual agents in that type. Because those agents have identical acceptability and rankings, this produces a perfect matching \(M\).

Recovering an indivisible optimal rival

For this incumbent, scale the coupling variables as \(Z=Nz\). Their margins become

\(\displaystyle \sum_{s'}Z_{tss'}=X_{ts},\qquad \sum_{t,s}Z_{tss'}=1.\)

This is a transportation polytope whose left vertices are the pairs \((t,s)\) and whose right vertices are the destination objects \(s'\). Its margins are integers, so its linear objective has an integral maximizer.

Every left vertex with positive margin has margin \(1\) and corresponds to exactly one individual agent under the recovered incumbent matching \(M\). An integral coupling sends this unit to one acceptable destination object. Every destination receives exactly one unit. Thus an integral optimal coupling defines a rival perfect matching \(M'\).

Conversely, every rival perfect matching defines such an integral coupling. Identical rankings within each type ensure that the coupling objective is exactly the matching vote total. Therefore

\(\displaystyle U(x)=\frac{u(M)}{N}.\)

The same equality holds for the aggregate assignment induced by any perfect matching: its scaled incumbent entries have the integer margins needed for the preceding rival argument.

Equivalence of the decision instances

If the source has a perfect matching \(M\) with \(u(M)\leq k\), its aggregate assignment satisfies \(U(x)=u(M)/N\leq\kappa\).

Conversely, suppose the constructed society has any feasible \(x\) satisfying \(U(x)\leq\kappa\). By Corollary 2, there is a vertex \(v\) with \(U(v)\leq U(x)\). Recover its perfect matching \(M\) as above. Then

\(\displaystyle u(M)=NU(v)\leq N\kappa=k.\)

Removing the forced padding pairs preserves this margin. Hence the source and target instances have the same answer. This proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness under all the restrictions claimed.

4. Membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

It remains to justify a polynomial-size witness despite allowing real assignments.

By Corollary 2, every yes-instance has a witnessing vertex \(x\). Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and supplies. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). By the cut formula in Lemma 3, every positive vertex coordinate is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). Since each coordinate lies in \([0,1]\), it has binary encoding length \(O(L)\). There are at most \(|E|\leq\tau m\) coordinates.

A verifier guesses this rational assignment and checks its feasibility exactly. It then computes \(U(x)\) by rational linear programming. With \(d_t=|S_t|\), that coupling program has

\(\displaystyle q=\sum_t d_t^2\leq\tau m^2\)

variables and \(|E|+m\leq\tau m+m\) equality constraints, together with nonnegativity. Its constraint coefficients are zero or one; its objective coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The rational right-hand sides have polynomial total encoding length.

Exact rational linear programming and comparison with \(\kappa\) therefore take \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bit operations. This gives an \(\mathsf{NP}\) verifier.

5. Running times and conclusion

For a polynomial-size rational candidate, evaluating \(U(x)\) and verifying the threshold take \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time, using at most \(\tau m^2\) coupling variables.

A deterministic exact algorithm can enumerate the positive supports of transportation vertices. There are at most \(2^{|E|}\) candidate supports. Forest supports determine at most one assignment through their margin equations; each candidate can be reconstructed, checked, and evaluated in polynomial time. This yields the bound

\(\displaystyle 2^{|E|}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\leq 2^{\tau m}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L).\)

The decision problem is therefore \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete. In particular, a polynomial-time decision algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}\).
