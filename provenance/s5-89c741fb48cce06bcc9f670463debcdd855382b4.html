1. The knapsack-cover relaxation

For a type $t$ and a facility set $X$ serving that type, we first prove
$$ \sum_{i\in X\setminus S}a_{it}^S\ge r_t^S \qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq F. $$

If $r_t^S=0$, this follows from nonnegativity. Otherwise, write $b=r_t^S>0$. Since $X$ serves $t$,
$$ \sum_{i\in X\setminus S}a_{it} \ge r_t-\sum_{i\in X\cap S}a_{it} \ge r_t-\sum_{i\in S}a_{it} =b. $$
Truncating every summand to $b$ preserves this lower bound. Indeed, if a summand is at least $b$, its truncated value alone supplies $b$; otherwise no summand changes.

Because $\mu_t>0$, the substitution
$$ y_t^S=\mu_tq_t^S $$
is a bijection between nonnegative variable vectors. Consequently, the optimal revenue equals
$$ \operatorname{OPT} = \max\left\{ \sum_{t,S}r_t^Sy_t^S: \sum_{t}\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}} a_{it}^Sy_t^S\le c_i \text{ for every }i,\quad y\ge0 \right\}. $$
In particular, its value is independent of the positive masses.

The dual of this finite, although exponentially represented, LP is
$$ \operatorname{OPT} = \min\left\{ \sum_{i\in F}c_ix_i: \sum_{i\in F\setminus S}a_{it}^Sx_i\ge r_t^S \text{ for every }t,S,\quad x\ge0 \right\}. $$

Here are the hypotheses justifying the equality and attainment. The maximizing LP is feasible at $y=0$. The inequality proved above, applied to $X=F$, shows that $x_i=1$ is feasible for the minimizing LP. Weak duality therefore bounds the maximizing objective by $\sum_i c_i$. Finite-dimensional LP strong duality gives equal, attained optimal values.

2. Proven: every feasible solution satisfies the mass-coalition core property

Let $q$ be feasible, and let
$$ p_t=\sum_{S\subseteq F}r_t^Sq_t^S. $$
Fix any coalition $z\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]$, allowing its coordinates to be arbitrary real numbers.

If $z=0$, the required inequality is immediate. Otherwise, let
$$ U=\{t:z_t>0\}, $$
and choose a minimum-cost facility set $X$ serving every type in $U$. Such a set exists because $F$ serves every type and there are finitely many facility subsets.

The inequality from Section 1 and nonnegativity give
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_t z_tp_t &=\sum_{t\in U}\sum_{S\subseteq F}z_tr_t^Sq_t^S\\ &\le \sum_{t\in U}\sum_{S\subseteq F} z_tq_t^S\sum_{i\in X\setminus S}a_{it}^S\\ &= \sum_{i\in X} \sum_{t\in U} \sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}} z_ta_{it}^Sq_t^S\\ &\le \sum_{i\in X} \sum_{t\in T} \sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{i\}} \mu_ta_{it}^Sq_t^S\\ &\le \sum_{i\in X}c_i\\ &=C(z). \end{aligned} $$

The rearrangement is legitimate because the sums are finite. The penultimate two inequalities use, respectively, $z_t\le\mu_t$ and the facility feasibility constraints.

This proves the core property for every feasible solution, without using optimality or budget balance. It directly extends the finite-society argument in [Aarts–Dentes–Wu–Shmoys, Theorem 5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16914v2).

3. Proven: the exact threshold problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$

$$ y_t^S\le \frac{c_i}{a_{it}^S}. $$
Thus the reduced feasible region is closed and bounded.

Introduce nonnegative slacks and write the constraints as
$$ Ay+s=c. $$
There are $m$ equations, and the matrix has row rank $m$ because it contains the slack identity matrix. Therefore an optimal basic feasible solution exists and has at most $m$ positive variables, counting both original variables and slacks.

Its encoding length is polynomial:

  • An index $(t,S)$ uses an $m$-bit subset indicator and a type index.
  • Residuals and truncated coefficients have polynomial bit length, since they are computed from input rationals by sums, subtraction, comparison, and truncation.
  • The basic variables solve an $m\times m$ nonsingular rational linear system.

For the last point, suppose the system entries have numerator and denominator lengths at most $h$. Clearing denominators separately in each row produces integer entries of bit length $O(mh)$. The determinant expansion has $m!$ terms, so its determinants have bit length
$$ O(m^2h+m\log m). $$
Cramer’s rule therefore gives polynomial-length rational basic variables.

A certificate lists the at most $m$ positive $y$-variables, their indices, and their rational values. A verifier computes their coefficients, checks nonnegativity and all $m$ facility constraints, and checks
$$ \sum_{t,S}r_t^Sy_t^S\ge B. $$
These operations take polynomial time in the input length. Hence the threshold problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

4.1. The auxiliary decision problem

Define $\mathsf{KC\text{-}VIOL}$ as follows.

The input consists of positive rational $r$, nonnegative rational contributions $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ satisfying $\sum_i a_i\ge r$, and a rational point

The question is whether there exists $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with
$$ b_S=r-\sum_{i\in S}a_i>0 $$
such that
$$ \sum_{i\notin S}\min\{a_i,b_S\}v_i<b_S. $$

4.2. A rational-point encoding lemma

Lemma. Given a rational vector $v\in[0,1]^n$, one can construct in polynomial time a system
$$ Ez=\mathbf 1,\qquad z\ge0, $$
with the following properties:

  • Every entry of $E$ is either $0$ or $1$.
  • Every row contains between one and three ones.
  • Every variable occurs in a row.
  • The system has a unique solution $z^*\in[0,1]^M$.
  • Its first $n$ coordinates equal $v$.
  • Its numbers of variables and rows are polynomial in the encoding length of $v$.

Proof. All equations below are sums of distinct variables equal to $1$.

Equality of two variables $u,u'$ can be enforced by introducing $h$ and imposing
$$ u+h=1,\qquad u'+h=1. $$
These equations imply $u=u'$.

Addition $w=u+v$ can be enforced by introducing $h$ and imposing
$$ w+h=1,\qquad u+v+h=1. $$
If the two inputs are the same variable, first introduce an equal copy using the preceding construction. Thus every row still has coefficients in $\{0,1\}$.

For a positive integer $k$, a binary arithmetic circuit constructs $ku$ using $O(\log(k+1))$ additions and copies. Starting with the leading binary digit, each subsequent digit doubles the current value and, when the digit is $1$, adds $u$. Every intermediate value is a positive integer multiple $\ell u$ with $\ell\le k$.

Now consider a desired coordinate
$$ v_i=\frac{p}{d},\qquad 0<p<d. $$
Introduce $u$, construct $du$ by such a circuit, and impose that its output equals $1$ using a singleton equation. This forces $u=1/d$. Construct $pu$ by another circuit, and equate its output to the designated variable $z_i$. Consequently,
$$ z_i=\frac pd. $$
All arithmetic intermediates lie in $[0,1]$, because their multipliers are at most $d$. All introduced complement variables also lie in $[0,1]$.

The singleton equation determines $u$ uniquely. The circuit equations then determine every arithmetic intermediate, copy, and complement uniquely.

For $v_i=1$, impose $z_i=1$. For $v_i=0$, introduce $e$ and impose
$$ e=1,\qquad z_i+e=1. $$

Perform these constructions independently for the $n$ coordinates. Every variable occurs in a defining equation. The construction uses a number of variables and rows linear in the total binary lengths of the numerators and denominators, up to a constant factor. This proves the lemma. $\square$

4.3. The facility instance

Apply the lemma to the prescribed point $v$. Let $E$ have $N$ rows and $M$ columns.

Create one facility for each coordinate of $z$, with opening cost
$$ c_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^N E_{\ell j}. $$
Every cost is a positive integer because every variable occurs in a row.

Create the following types:

  • One original type, with requirement $r$, contributions $a_i$ on the first $n$ facilities, and contribution zero on the additional facilities.
  • For each row $\ell$ of $E$, an auxiliary type with requirement $1$ and contribution $E_{\ell j}$ from facility $j$.

Assign every type mass $1/(N+1)$. These masses are positive rationals and sum to $1$. Every type can be served by all facilities: this follows from the input assumption for the original type and from each auxiliary row being nonempty.

For an auxiliary type, the full family of knapsack-cover inequalities is equivalent to
$$ \sum_j E_{\ell j}x_j\ge1. $$
Indeed, if $S$ contains a facility with contribution $1$, its residual is zero. Otherwise its residual is $1$, and precisely the row’s facilities have truncated contribution $1$.

For the original type, including additional facilities in $S$ does not change its residual or any positive coefficient. Therefore its inequalities are exactly the original single-type knapsack-cover inequalities on $x_1,\ldots,x_n$.

By Section 1, the revenue optimum equals the minimum of $c^\mathsf{T}x$ over this combined relaxation. For every feasible $x$,
$$ c^\mathsf{T}x = \sum_{\ell=1}^N (Ex)_\ell \ge N. $$
Moreover,
$$ c^\mathsf{T}x=N \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Ex=\mathbf 1. $$
The implication from equality holds because each of the $N$ row sums is at least $1$.

The encoding lemma now gives
$$ \operatorname{OPT}=N \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v\text{ satisfies every original knapsack-cover inequality}. $$

To justify both directions explicitly:

  • If $v$ satisfies those inequalities, the unique gadget solution $z^*$ satisfies the combined relaxation and has cost $N$.
  • If a feasible $x$ has cost $N$, then $Ex=\mathbf 1$, so $x=z^*$ and its first $n$ coordinates are $v$.

The minimum is attained by Section 1. Hence
$$ v\text{ violates an original inequality} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{OPT}>N. $$

4.4. An explicit gap converts strict inequality to a threshold

It remains to choose a polynomial-length rational $B$ that distinguishes $\operatorname{OPT}=N$ from $\operatorname{OPT}>N$.

Let $\lambda\ge1$ be the total binary length of the original numerical data $r,a_1,\ldots,a_n$. Set
$$ h=2\lambda+3. $$
Every coefficient and right-hand side in the combined minimizing LP has numerator and denominator of absolute value at most $2^h$.

To verify this bound, take the product $D$ of the denominators of $r,a_1,\ldots,a_n$. Then $D\le2^\lambda$. Every positive residual is at most $r\le2^\lambda$, and every truncated coefficient is at most that residual. Expressing either over denominator $D$ gives numerator at most $2^{2\lambda}$. Auxiliary coefficients and right-hand sides are $0$ or $1$.

The minimizing LP has an optimal extreme point. Indeed, all $c_j>0$, so every objective sublevel set in the nonnegative orthant is bounded. Its optimal face is therefore a nonempty compact polytope and has an extreme point; an extreme point of this optimal face is also an extreme point of the feasible polyhedron.

At such a point, choose $M$ linearly independent active constraints. They determine the point by a nonsingular rational system. Clearing denominators in each selected row, including its right-hand side, gives integer entries of absolute value at most
$$ 2^{(M+2)h}. $$
If $\Delta$ is the determinant of the resulting integer coefficient matrix, the determinant expansion gives
$$ 0<|\Delta| \le M!\,2^{M(M+2)h}. $$
Define
$$ K=M(M+2)h+M\left\lceil\log_2(M+1)\right\rceil+2. $$
Then $|\Delta|<2^K$.

By Cramer’s rule, every coordinate of this optimal point has denominator dividing $\Delta$. Since the costs and $N$ are integers, if $\operatorname{OPT}>N$, then
$$ \operatorname{OPT}-N \ge \frac1{|\Delta|} >2^{-K}. $$

Thus choose
$$ B=N+2^{-K}. $$
We obtain the exact equivalence
$$ v\text{ violates a knapsack-cover inequality} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{OPT}\ge B. $$

Finally, this construction is polynomial in the original input length. The encoding lemma makes $M$ and $N$ polynomially bounded; $\lambda$, $h$, and $K$ are also polynomially bounded. Writing the denominator $2^K$ takes $K+1$ bits. The facility contribution table, costs, and uniform masses all have polynomial encoding length.

This completes the many-one reduction.
