The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^C\) of approval types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), compute \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le k\), such that for every nonempty \(X\subseteq C\) and every \(\beta\in[k]\), \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal A:\,|A\cap X|\ge\beta,\,|A\cap W|<\beta}\mu_A < |X|/k\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\geq 1\) candidates and let \(k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^C\) of \(\tau\) approval types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Empty approval types are allowed.
A committee is an ordinary subset \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\leq k\). It satisfies mass FJR if, for every nonempty \(X\subseteq C\) and every integer \(\beta\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\),
\(\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A:\ |A\cap X|\geq\beta,\ |A\cap W|<\beta}}\mu_A<\frac{|X|}{k}.\)

Is computing a committee satisfying mass FJR solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is this search problem NP-hard?

For explicitly listed, equally weighted voters, an FJR committee can be computed in polynomial time ([Frank and Peters](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05397v1)); a strengthening, FJR+, can also be computed and verified in polynomial time ([Teh](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11500v1)).
No result settling the stated problem with binary-rational support masses was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question omitted the domain of k; impose integer 1 ≤ k ≤ m, as in the discrete model.
  • Define L as the total input bit length and measure polynomial time in m, τ, k, and L.
  • Allow empty approval types, as the discrete model permits arbitrary subsets of C.

Result

Computing a mass-FJR committee is solvable in deterministic polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

The residual-budget payment cap used below appears in [Frank and Peters, Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05397v1) and [Teh, Section 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11500v1). We give a direct proof for rational support masses, including a polynomial bound on the bit complexity. Approval types are processed directly; no expansion into individual voters is needed.

1. Algorithm on approval types

Fix orders on the candidates and approval types. Maintain a selected set \(W\), initially empty, and a remaining budget \(b_A\) for each type \(A\), initially \(b_A=\mu_A\). Write \(u_A(W)=|A\cap W|\).

Process levels \(\ell=k,k-1,\ldots,1\). At level \(\ell\), define the available payment for each \(c\notin W\) by

\( \displaystyle q_\ell(c)=\sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A:\ c\in A\\u_A(W)<\ell}}\frac{b_A}{\ell-u_A(W)}. \)

While some \(c\notin W\) satisfies \(q_\ell(c)\geq 1/k\), select the first such candidate and collect exactly \(1/k\) as follows. Define its type-specific payment caps by

\( \displaystyle a_A=\begin{cases}\dfrac{b_A}{\ell-u_A(W)},&c\in A\text{ and }u_A(W)<\ell,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \)

Starting with a remaining price \(r=1/k\), process types in their fixed order, assigning

\( \displaystyle d_A=\min\{a_A,r\},\qquad r\leftarrow r-d_A. \)

Because \(\sum_A a_A=q_\ell(c)\geq 1/k\), the remaining price becomes zero. Indeed, after any prefix of types, it equals the maximum of zero and \(1/k\) minus the sum of that prefix’s caps. Consequently,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}d_A=\frac1k. \)

Update \(b_A\leftarrow b_A-d_A\) for every type and \(W\leftarrow W\cup\{c\}\). Recompute scores and continue at the same level. When no candidate is affordable, decrease the level. Return the final set \(W^*\).

The budgets are total budgets of types, so their initial values already incorporate the masses. Empty types have zero payment caps throughout.

2. The algorithm selects at most \(k\) candidates

Every nonzero cap has a positive integer denominator, so \(0\leq d_A\leq a_A\leq b_A\). Thus all budgets remain nonnegative.

Every purchase decreases the total remaining budget by exactly \(1/k\). After \(j\) purchases,

\( \displaystyle \sum_{A\in\mathcal A}b_A=1-\frac{j}{k}. \)

Nonnegativity therefore implies \(j\leq k\). In particular, \(|W^*|\leq k\).

Every purchase adds a previously unselected candidate, and there are only \(k\) levels, so the algorithm terminates. After \(k\) purchases, all budgets are zero and no further candidate is affordable.

3. Correctness: the returned committee satisfies mass FJR

Suppose, for a contradiction, that \(W^*\) violates mass FJR. Then some nonempty \(X\subseteq C\) and some \(\beta\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) satisfy

\( \displaystyle \mathcal G=\{A\in\mathcal A:|A\cap X|\geq\beta,\ |A\cap W^*|<\beta\},\qquad M:=\sum_{A\in\mathcal G}\mu_A\geq\frac{|X|}{k}. \)

Since \(X\) is nonempty, \(M>0\), so \(\mathcal G\) is nonempty.

At every intermediate state, \(W\subseteq W^*\). Hence, for every \(A\in\mathcal G\),

\( \displaystyle u_A(W)<\beta. \)

Define

\( \displaystyle s_A=\beta-u_A(W),\qquad r_A=|A\cap(X\setminus W)|. \)

Here \(s_A\geq1\), and

\( \displaystyle r_A=|A\cap X|-|A\cap X\cap W|\geq\beta-u_A(W)=s_A. \)

In particular, \(X\setminus W\) is nonempty at every intermediate state.

Consider the potential

\( \displaystyle \Phi(W,b)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal G}r_A\frac{b_A}{s_A}. \)

We prove that, throughout the execution up to and including the end of level \(\beta\),

\[ \displaystyle \Phi(W,b)\geq\frac{|X\setminus W|}{k}. \tag{1} \]

Initial state

Initially \(W=\varnothing\), \(b_A=\mu_A\), and \(s_A=\beta\). Therefore,

\( \displaystyle \Phi(\varnothing,\mu)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal G}|A\cap X|\frac{\mu_A}{\beta}\geq\sum_{A\in\mathcal G}\mu_A=M\geq\frac{|X|}{k}. \)

Thus (1) holds initially.

Effect of a purchase at a level \(\ell\geq\beta\)

Let \(c\) be the purchased candidate, and use unprimed quantities for the state before its purchase.

If \(A\in\mathcal G\) does not approve \(c\), its budget, utility, and potential contribution are unchanged.

If \(A\in\mathcal G\) approves \(c\), then its utility increases by one. Since its final utility is still below \(\beta\), we have \(s_A\geq2\). Also, \(u_A(W)<\beta\leq\ell\), so its payment satisfies

\[ \displaystyle 0\leq d_A\leq\frac{b_A}{\ell-u_A(W)}\leq\frac{b_A}{s_A}. \tag{2} \]

Its new budget is \(b_A-d_A\), and its new gap is \(s_A-1\).

Case \(c\notin X\). The value \(r_A\) is unchanged. By (2),

\( \displaystyle \frac{b_A-d_A}{s_A-1}-\frac{b_A}{s_A}=\frac{b_A-s_A d_A}{s_A(s_A-1)}\geq0. \)

Therefore every affected contribution is nondecreasing. The potential does not decrease, and \(X\setminus W\) is unchanged. Thus (1) is preserved.

Case \(c\in X\). For an approving type, \(r_A\) decreases by one. Its old contribution minus its new contribution equals

\( \displaystyle \frac{r_A b_A}{s_A}-\frac{(r_A-1)(b_A-d_A)}{s_A-1} =d_A+\frac{r_A-s_A}{s_A-1}\left(d_A-\frac{b_A}{s_A}\right)\leq d_A. \)

The last inequality follows from \(r_A\geq s_A\) and (2). Summing over types in \(\mathcal G\), the potential decreases by at most

\( \displaystyle \sum_{A\in\mathcal G}d_A\leq\sum_{A\in\mathcal A}d_A=\frac1k. \)

Meanwhile, \(|X\setminus W|\) decreases by exactly one. Thus (1) is again preserved.

All purchases before the end of level \(\beta\) occur at levels at least \(\beta\). Changes of level do not change the state. This proves (1) at the end of level \(\beta\).

Contradiction at the end of level \(\beta\)

At that point, every unselected candidate satisfies \(q_\beta(c)<1/k\), because the while-loop has terminated.

On the other hand, exchanging the order of summation gives

\( \displaystyle \Phi(W,b)=\sum_{c\in X\setminus W}\ \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal G\\c\in A}}\frac{b_A}{\beta-u_A(W)}\leq\sum_{c\in X\setminus W}q_\beta(c). \)

Every type in \(\mathcal G\) is eligible at level \(\beta\), which justifies the inequality. Since \(X\setminus W\) is nonempty,

\( \displaystyle \Phi(W,b)<\frac{|X\setminus W|}{k}, \)

contradicting (1). Therefore \(W^*\) satisfies mass FJR. This argument also excludes violations whose mass is exactly \(|X|/k\), as required by the strict inequality in the definition.

4. Polynomial bit complexity

The fixed-order payment allocation is essential to the following explicit arithmetic bound.

Write each input mass as \(\mu_A=p_A/t_A\), with positive integers encoded in binary. Set

\( \displaystyle Q=\prod_{A\in\mathcal A}t_A,\qquad H=k!,\qquad D_j=QkH^j. \)

The binary length of \(Q\) is \(O(L)\). We claim that after \(j\) purchases every budget can be represented with denominator \(D_j\).

Initially, each mass has denominator dividing \(Q\), hence also dividing \(D_0\). Suppose the claim holds after \(j\) purchases. Every positive gap \(\ell-u_A(W)\) belongs to \(\{1,\ldots,k\}\), and therefore divides \(H\). Consequently, all caps and candidate scores can be represented with the common denominator \(D_jH\).

The initial remaining price \(1/k\) also has this denominator. Fixed-order allocation uses only comparisons, minima, and subtractions, so every payment and intermediate remaining price has denominator \(D_jH\). Updated budgets therefore have denominator

\( \displaystyle D_jH=D_{j+1}. \)

This proves the claim by induction.

Budgets, caps, scores, payments, and remaining prices all lie in \([0,1]\). For scores, this follows from

\( \displaystyle q_\ell(c)\leq\sum_A b_A\leq1. \)

Thus their numerators are no larger than their respective common denominators. Because \(j\leq k\), every integer used in these calculations has

\( \displaystyle O\!\left(L+\log(k+1)+(k+1)\log(k!)\right)=O\!\left(L+k^2\log(k+1)\right) \)

bits.

There are at most \(k\) successful purchases and one terminating affordability check per level. Recomputing all candidate scores takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations per check; allocating payments and updating utilities takes \(O(\tau)\) operations per purchase. Hence the algorithm uses \(O(km\tau)\) arithmetic operations on integers of polynomial bit length, in addition to polynomial preprocessing.

The deterministic committee-valued function defined by the fixed orders is therefore computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\).
