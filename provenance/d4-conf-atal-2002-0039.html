The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) items and \(\tau\) supplier types, where type \(t\) has integer ask vector \(q_t\), rational price \(p_t\), and population mass \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\), along with per-supplier demand \(d\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), determine whether there are accepted masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) satisfying \(\sum_t q_{ti}x_t=d_i\) for every item \(i\); if feasible, find a minimum-cost choice minimizing \(\sum_t p_t x_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) supplier types with positive masses \(\mu_t\) encoded as binary rationals, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge 1\) items. Type \(t\) offers the bundle \(q_t\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\) at cost \(p_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) per unit of accepted supplier mass. Demand is \(d\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), measured per unit of total supplier mass. All numerical inputs use binary encoding; \(L\) is their total bit length.
Supplier mass is divisible: choose real accepted masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Each accepted supplier supplies its entire stated bundle. There is no free disposal, so feasibility requires \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}q_{ti}x_t=d_i\) for every \(i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\). If feasible, find a choice minimizing \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}p_tx_t\).

Is this feasibility and minimum-cost winner determination problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete combinatorial reverse auctions without free disposal, deciding feasibility is NP-complete, even with integer quantities and prices (Sandholm, Suri, Gilpin, and Levine).
That result concerns binary acceptance of asks and provides no complexity classification for divisible supplier masses.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted integer ask vectors to nonnegative coordinates and rational prices to nonnegative values, as required by the discrete reverse-auction definition.
  • Required strictly positive demand for every listed item, carrying over the discrete definition's positive required quantities.
  • Interpreted accepted masses as real and divisible, with whole bundles supplied by each accepted supplier and prices charged per unit of accepted mass.
  • Required at least one item and defined L as the total bit length of all numerical inputs.

Result

Both feasibility and minimum-cost winner determination are solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Divisible acceptance makes the stated problem a rational linear program.

Exact linear-programming formulation

Define \(Q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{m\times\tau}\) by \(Q_{it}=q_{ti}\), and let \(p=(p_t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\) and \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\). Consider the linear program

\[ \min_{x\in\mathbb R^\tau}p^\mathsf Tx \quad\text{subject to}\quad Qx=d,\qquad 0\le x\le\mu. \]

All vector inequalities are coordinatewise.

We verify that this formulation is exact:

  • For any admissible supplier selection, \(x_t\) is the accepted mass of type \(t\), so \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). Its supply of item \(i\) is \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}q_{ti}x_t\). Exact demand satisfaction therefore gives \(Qx=d\).
  • Conversely, any feasible vector \(x\) specifies an admissible selection: divisibility permits accepting exactly mass \(x_t\) of each type. Every accepted supplier still delivers its entire bundle, and \(Qx=d\) ensures exact demand satisfaction.
  • The selection’s cost is exactly \(p^\mathsf Tx\).

Thus the original problem and this linear program have identical feasible choices and costs. In particular, the equality constraints preserve the prohibition on free disposal.

Polynomial bit complexity and exact optimization

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: given an explicitly encoded rational linear program, one can decide feasibility and, when a finite optimum exists, compute an exact rational optimal solution in time polynomial in the program’s binary encoding length. This is a theorem about bit complexity, including exact solution recovery; see [Trevisan’s linear-programming notes, Sections 2.4–2.5](https://lucatrevisan.github.io/cs261/lecture05.pdf).

Here its hypotheses hold:

  • There are \(\tau\) real variables.
  • Replacing \(Qx=d\) by \(Qx\le d\) and \(-Qx\le-d\) gives \(2m+2\tau\) inequalities, including the bounds.
  • All coefficients are rational and explicitly given. The formulation only copies input coefficients, changes signs, and adds coefficients from \(\{-1,0,1\}\). Even a dense encoding therefore has length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and can be constructed in polynomial time.

If the feasible set is nonempty, it is closed and contained in the bounded box \(\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}[0,\mu_t]\), hence compact. The continuous objective consequently attains its minimum. Moreover, every feasible choice satisfies

\[ 0\le p^\mathsf Tx\le\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}p_t\mu_t, \]

so the optimum is finite. The rational linear-programming theorem therefore returns either an infeasibility decision or an exact optimal accepted-mass vector, in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Only the listed support types are used: the algorithm introduces no variables for unlisted possible types and does not expand rational masses into individual suppliers.

Why discrete hardness does not transfer

Binary acceptance would impose \(x_t\in\{0,\mu_t\}\). Those restrictions are absent here, and replacing them by intervals can change feasibility.

For example, take \(m=\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=1\), \(q_{11}=2\), \(p_1=1\), and \(d_1=1\). Divisible acceptance is feasible with \(x_1=\tfrac12\). Binary acceptance permits only \(x_1=0\) or \(x_1=1\), supplying respectively zero or two units, so neither satisfies demand exactly.

The cited discrete hardness result thus concerns a different feasible set. The linear-programming argument completely settles the stated divisible-mass problem.
