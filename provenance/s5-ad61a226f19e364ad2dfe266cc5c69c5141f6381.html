The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(r\) referenda, a target \(q\in\{0,1\}^r\), and a finite support of ballot-and-price types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), what is the infimum cost of influencing mass from each type so that \(q\) has a strict majority on every referendum, and is that infimum attained? The instance should also admit a feasible transfer within any requested rational \(\epsilon>0\) of the infimum.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m=r\geq1\) be the number of referenda and \(q\in\{0,1\}^m\) the target. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) types \(t=(b_t,p_t)\), with ballots \(b_t\in\{0,1\}^m\), nonnegative binary-rational prices \(p_t\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Mass is divisible: influencing \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) costs \(p_tx_t\) and permits changing its entire ballot at that single price. Use the target ballot \(q\) for influenced mass; uninfluenced mass retains its ballot.
A transfer is feasible when \(\sum_{t:b_{tj}=q_j}\mu_t+\sum_{t:b_{tj}\ne q_j}x_t>1/2\) for every \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\); ties fail. Write \(C(x)=\sum_t p_tx_t\) and \(C_* = \inf\{C(x):x\text{ is feasible}\}\).
For rational \(\epsilon>0\), interpret an approximation factor \(\rho\) as a feasible rational transfer satisfying \(C(x)\leq\rho C_*+\epsilon\), including when \(C_*=0\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including \(\epsilon\); state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Does this continuous lobbying problem admit a polynomial-time approximation with \(\rho=1+\ln(m\tau)\), or is achieving this guarantee NP-hard?
Can attainment of \(C_*\) by a feasible transfer be decided in polynomial time, or is that decision NP-hard?

For discrete weighted lobbying, a polynomial-time greedy algorithm achieves \(H_D=\sum_{i=1}^{D}1/i\leq1+\ln D\) when \(D\geq1\), where \(D\) is the sum of the minimum numbers of ballot-entry changes needed for the target majorities; its harmonic bound is asymptotically tight for that algorithm. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0703097).
No result settling this exact divisible-mass, strict-majority formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize total society mass to one and require a nonempty support and at least one referendum.
  • Interpret each price as a nonnegative rational cost per unit of influenced mass, charged once for access to the entire ballot; allow arbitrary subdivision of each type.
  • Represent influenced mass by changing its ballot to the target, which is permitted by the unrestricted discrete ballot-change model.
  • Use m=r for the number of referenda and L for total binary input length, including the requested tolerance.
  • Replace the integer deficit-dependent logarithmic scale by 1+ln(m tau), using the explicitly represented ballot support rather than an implicit number of individual voters.
  • Allow additive epsilon in the multiplicative guarantee to accommodate strict majorities, unattained infima, and zero infimum cost; algorithmic transfers have rational coordinates.

Result

Both tasks are polynomial-time solvable. In fact, for every rational \(\epsilon>0\), one can compute a feasible rational transfer satisfying \(C(x)\leq C_*+\epsilon\). This implies the requested guarantee with \(\rho=1+\ln(m\tau)\).

Attainment has an especially simple characterization: \(C_*\) is attained exactly when influencing all types with zero price already gives strict target majorities.

1. The infimum equals a linear-programming optimum

For each referendum \(j\), define its initial target support and its target support after a transfer by

\[ a_j=\sum_{t:b_{tj}=q_j}\mu_t, \qquad s_j(x)=a_j+\sum_{t:b_{tj}\ne q_j}x_t. \]

Consider the closed relaxation

\[ K=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^\tau: 0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\text{ for every }t,\quad s_j(x)\geq\tfrac12\text{ for every }j \right\}. \]

Let \(u_t=\mu_t\) for every type. Since all mass is then influenced toward the target, \(s_j(u)=1\) for every \(j\). Thus \(K\) is nonempty. It is also closed and bounded, hence compact. Consequently, the continuous objective \(C(x)\) attains a minimum on \(K\); write

\[ c_0=\min_{x\in K}C(x), \qquad \bar x\in K,\quad C(\bar x)=c_0. \]

Every strictly feasible transfer belongs to \(K\), so \(c_0\leq C_*\).

Conversely, for any \(0<\lambda\leq1\), set

\[ x^{(\lambda)}=(1-\lambda)\bar x+\lambda u. \]

This transfer satisfies the coordinate bounds by convexity. Moreover, the affine form of \(s_j\) gives

\[ s_j\bigl(x^{(\lambda)}\bigr) =(1-\lambda)s_j(\bar x)+\lambda s_j(u) \geq\frac{1-\lambda}{2}+\lambda =\frac12+\frac{\lambda}{2}>\frac12. \]

Therefore \(x^{(\lambda)}\) is strictly feasible. Its cost satisfies

\[ C\bigl(x^{(\lambda)}\bigr) =(1-\lambda)c_0+\lambda C(u) \longrightarrow c_0 \quad\text{as }\lambda\longrightarrow0. \]

Since \(C_*\leq C(x^{(\lambda)})\) for every positive \(\lambda\), taking this limit yields \(C_*\leq c_0\). Hence

\[ C_*=c_0. \]

This establishes equality of the values without assuming that the strict problem has a minimizer.

2. A polynomial-time additive approximation

Use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: an explicitly given rational LP that is feasible and has a finite optimum admits an exact rational optimal solution computable in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. Polynomial-time solvability and polynomial bounds on the encoding of optimal vertices are discussed in [Goemans’s linear-programming notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-854j-advanced-algorithms-fall-2008/908fe54d659623efadda2d033b895caf_notes_lp.pdf).

The LP defining \(c_0\) has \(\tau\) variables and \(m+2\tau\) inequalities. Its coefficients are rational, and the preceding section verifies feasibility and boundedness. Computing each \(a_j\) requires summing input rationals; the resulting encoding length is polynomial in \(L\). Thus an exact rational optimizer \(\bar x\) can be computed in polynomial time.

Write \(P=C(u)=\sum_t p_t\mu_t\), and choose the rational number

\[ \alpha=\min\left\{1,\frac{\epsilon}{P+1}\right\}. \]

Since \(\epsilon>0\) and \(P\geq0\), we have \(0<\alpha\leq1\). Output

\[ x=(1-\alpha)\bar x+\alpha u. \]

Section 1 proves strict feasibility, with every referendum having target support at least \(\frac12+\frac{\alpha}{2}\). Since prices are nonnegative, \(C_*\geq0\), and therefore

\[ C(x) =(1-\alpha)C_*+\alpha P \leq C_*+\alpha P \leq C_*+\epsilon. \]

The last inequality follows from \(\alpha\leq\epsilon/(P+1)\). This construction also covers \(P=0\) and \(C_*=0\).

All quantities used in the output have polynomial binary encoding length. The algorithm requires one rational LP solve and polynomial additional arithmetic, so its bit running time is \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). In particular, its dependence on \(\epsilon\) is through its binary encoding, rather than through \(1/\epsilon\).

Finally, \(m\tau\geq1\), so \(1+\ln(m\tau)\geq1\). Thus

\[ C(x)\leq C_*+\epsilon \leq \bigl(1+\ln(m\tau)\bigr)C_*+\epsilon, \]

which proves the requested approximation guarantee.

3. Exact characterization of attainment

First, no strictly feasible transfer of positive cost can minimize cost.

To prove this, let \(x\) be strictly feasible with \(C(x)>0\). For every referendum, define

\[ g_j=s_j(x)-\frac12>0, \qquad S_j=\sum_{t:b_{tj}\ne q_j}x_t. \]

The coordinate bounds and normalization of masses give \(0\leq S_j\leq1\). Because there are finitely many referenda, the number

\[ \delta=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac12\min_{1\leq j\leq m}g_j\right\} \]

is positive. Set \(y=(1-\delta)x\). This preserves all coordinate bounds, and for every \(j\),

\[ s_j(y)=s_j(x)-\delta S_j \geq\frac12+g_j-\delta \geq\frac12+\frac{g_j}{2}>\frac12. \]

Thus \(y\) remains strictly feasible, while

\[ C(y)=(1-\delta)C(x)<C(x). \]

Consequently, attainment of \(C_*\) can occur only at cost zero.

Now define the transfer using all mass with zero price:

\[ z_t= \begin{cases} \mu_t,&p_t=0,\\ 0,&p_t>0, \end{cases} \qquad F_j=s_j(z) =a_j+\sum_{\substack{t:b_{tj}\ne q_j\\p_t=0}}\mu_t. \]

We claim that

\[ C_*\text{ is attained} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_j>\frac12\text{ for every }j. \]

If every displayed inequality holds, \(z\) is strictly feasible and \(C(z)=0\). Nonnegative prices imply that zero is the minimum possible cost, so \(z\) attains \(C_*\).

Conversely, suppose a feasible transfer \(x\) attains \(C_*\). The scaling argument proves \(C(x)=0\). Each summand \(p_tx_t\) is nonnegative, so \(x_t=0\) whenever \(p_t>0\). For types with zero price, \(x_t\leq\mu_t=z_t\). Hence \(x_t\leq z_t\) for every type. All coefficients in \(s_j\) are nonnegative, giving

\[ F_j=s_j(z)\geq s_j(x)>\frac12 \]

for every \(j\). This proves the characterization.

The attainment algorithm simply computes these \(m\) sums and checks the strict inequalities. It uses \(O(m\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. Each sum involves a subset of the input masses and has \(O(L)\)-bit numerator and denominator, so elementary arithmetic gives a conservative bit-time bound of \(O(m\tau L^2)\).

4. Zero infimum versus attainment

The same quantities distinguish the possible boundary cases:

\[ C_*=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_j\geq\frac12\text{ for every }j. \]

Indeed, if these weak inequalities hold, then \(z\in K\) has cost zero, and Section 1 gives \(C_*=0\). Conversely, if \(C_*=0\), a closed-relaxation optimizer has cost zero. As above, its coordinates are bounded by those of \(z\), so every \(F_j\) is at least \(\frac12\).

Thus strict inequalities for all \(F_j\) mean attainment; weak inequalities with at least one equality mean a zero infimum that is not attained; and any \(F_j<\frac12\) means a positive infimum that is not attained. The approximation algorithm handles all three cases.
