The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite offer set \(X\) with rational probabilities \(p_x\), buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), total population mass \(M>0\), and per-search cost \(\kappa(M)>0\), compute an optimal policy and its expected net utility from an initial set of known offers. A state records each type’s best known offer \(r_t\in X\); stopping yields \(M\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t U_t(r_t)\), while continuing pays \(\kappa(M)\), draws an offer according to \(p\), and updates each type’s record if its utility improves. In B2C, each offer can serve all mass of every type.

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) buyer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Supply total mass \(M\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), the per-search cost \(c=\kappa(M)\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), and explicit utility tables \(U_t:X\to\mathbb{Q}\). Attributes are oriented so that \(x\leq y\) coordinatewise implies \(U_t(x)\leq U_t(y)\) for every type.
Initially a nonempty set \(K\subseteq X\) is known at no further cost, and each record \(r_t\) maximizes \(U_t\) over \(K\). Every known offer remains available and can serve all mass of every type.
At each stage, stop and obtain \(G(r)=M\sum_t\mu_tU_t(r_t)\), or pay \(c\), draw one offer independently from \(p\), and replace every record whose utility strictly improves. Retain the old record on utility ties; use a fixed ordering for initial ties. There is no outside option, discounting, or finite horizon.
A policy assigns stop or continue to each reachable record state. Its objective is \(\mathbb{E}[G(r_{\mathrm{stop}})-cH]\), where \(H\) counts additional searches; policies with infinite expected search count have value \(-\infty\). An optimal policy may be represented by an executable stopping rule rather than an exhaustive state table. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Result

Computing the exact optimal value is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even with two attributes, utilities in \(\{0,1\}\), dyadic probabilities and masses, and a singleton initially known set.

However, an optimal policy always has a polynomial-time executable stopping rule. Thus the difficulty lies in computing its exact expected payoff.

We prove both statements.

1. An optimal stopping rule

For a record state \(r\), define its expected one-search utility gain by

\(\displaystyle d(r)=M\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{x\in X}p_x\max\{U_t(x)-U_t(r_t),0\}.\)

Proposition. The following policy is optimal:

\(\displaystyle \text{continue if }d(r)>c,\qquad \text{stop if }d(r)\leq c.\)

Proof

Fix an infinite sequence of independent draws from \(p\). Let \(r_h\) denote the records after processing the first \(h\) draws, whether or not a particular policy would already have stopped. Put \(G_h=G(r_h)\), and let \(\mathcal F_h\) denote the information from those draws.

Every record utility is nondecreasing. Consequently, for every draw sequence,

\(\displaystyle d(r_{h+1})\leq d(r_h).\)

Indeed, increasing \(U_t(r_t)\) can only decrease each term \(\max\{U_t(x)-U_t(r_t),0\}\), and all its weights are nonnegative.

Define

\(\displaystyle S=\inf\{h\geq0:d(r_h)\leq c\}.\)

First, \(S\) has finite expectation. Let \(X_+=\{x\in X:p_x>0\}\), and let \(T_x\) be the first occurrence time of \(x\). After every offer in \(X_+\) has appeared, no subsequent draw can improve any record, so \(d=0\). Hence

\(\displaystyle S\leq\max_{x\in X_+}T_x,\qquad \mathbb E[S]\leq\sum_{x\in X_+}\mathbb E[T_x] =\sum_{x\in X_+}\frac1{p_x}<\infty.\)

Now consider any competing policy whose search count \(H\) has finite expectation. Its objective is \(J(H)=\mathbb E[G_H-cH]\). Conditional on \(\mathcal F_h\), the expected increment \(G_{h+1}-G_h\) is \(d(r_h)\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle J(H)=G_0+\sum_{h=0}^{\infty} \mathbb E\!\left[\mathbf1_{\{H>h\}}\bigl(d(r_h)-c\bigr)\right]. \)

To justify this identity, telescope the utility increments and use that \(\{H>h\}\) is \(\mathcal F_h\)-measurable. All record utilities, and thus all \(d(r_h)\), are bounded because the tables are finite. The series is absolutely integrable since \(\mathbb E[H]<\infty\).

The same identity holds for \(S\). For each \(h\), pointwise,

\(\displaystyle \bigl(\mathbf1_{\{S>h\}}-\mathbf1_{\{H>h\}}\bigr) \bigl(d(r_h)-c\bigr)\geq0. \)

There are two cases:

  • If \(h<S\), then \(d(r_h)>c\), and the first factor is either zero or one.
  • If \(h\geq S\), monotonicity gives \(d(r_h)\leq c\), and the first factor is either zero or negative one.

Taking expectations and summing yields \(J(S)\geq J(H)\). Policies with infinite expected search count have value \(-\infty\) by the problem’s convention, so they cannot improve on \(S\). This proves the proposition.

Computational consequence

Evaluating \(d(r)\) uses \(O(m\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations. Each term uses input rationals and record utilities taken directly from the input tables; exact summation and comparison have polynomial bit complexity in \(L\).

Thus an executable optimal policy can be constructed in polynomial time, and each stop/continue decision can be evaluated in polynomial time. This does not compute its exact expected payoff.

2. Reduction from Set Cover

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Set Cover problem. ([Karp, 1972](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9))

An instance consists of a nonempty universe \(E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_u\}\), sets \(S_1,\ldots,S_n\subseteq E\), and a budget \(b\), asking whether at most \(b\) sets cover \(E\). We restrict to \(n\geq1\) and \(1\leq b\leq n\). These restrictions preserve hardness: the excluded cases are immediately decidable and can be mapped to fixed yes or no instances satisfying the restrictions.

We construct a search instance and a rational threshold \(\theta\) such that

\(\displaystyle E\text{ has a cover using at most }b\text{ sets} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V^*\geq\theta, \)

where \(V^*\) is the optimal expected net utility.

Types, masses, and utilities

Let

\(\displaystyle q=2^{\lceil\log_2u\rceil}.\)

For each element \(e_t\), introduce a type with weight

\(\displaystyle w_t= \begin{cases} 1,&t<u,\\ q-u+1,&t=u, \end{cases} \qquad \mu_t=\frac{w_t}{q}. \)

Every mass is positive and dyadic, and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Set \(M=q\) and \(c=\frac12\). Consequently, type \(t\)’s contribution to total utility is \(w_tU_t(r_t)\).

Introduce the following offers:

  • An initially known offer \(x_0\), with \(U_t(x_0)=0\) for every type.
  • For each \(S_i\), an offer \(x_i\), with \(U_t(x_i)=1\) exactly when \(e_t\in S_i\).
  • A universal offer \(x_\star\), with \(U_t(x_\star)=1\) for every type.

Take \(K=\{x_0\}\). All utilities belong to \(\{0,1\}\). When every element is covered, total utility equals \(\sum_tw_t=q\).

Attributes and monotonicity

There are \(m=n+2\) offers. Index them by \(i=0,\ldots,n+1\), with \(x_\star=x_{n+1}\), and assign

\(\displaystyle x_i=(i,n+1-i)\in\mathbb Q^2.\)

Distinct offers are coordinatewise incomparable: their first coordinates increase while their second coordinates decrease. Thus the utility tables satisfy the required coordinatewise monotonicity.

They also extend to coordinatewise nondecreasing functions on all of \(\mathbb Q^2\), by defining

\(\displaystyle \widetilde U_t(z)= \max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{U_t(x):x\in X,\ x\leq z\}\bigr). \)

At an offer \(x_i\), the only offer coordinatewise below it is \(x_i\) itself, so this extension agrees with the table.

Draw probabilities

Define

\(\displaystyle \ell=\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil,\qquad B=2^{\,2+(b+1)\ell},\qquad \varepsilon=B^{-1}. \)

Set

\(\displaystyle p_{x_0}=0,\qquad p_{x_i}=\varepsilon\quad(1\leq i\leq n),\qquad p_{x_\star}=1-n\varepsilon. \)

These probabilities are dyadic and sum to one. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle B>4n^{b+1},\qquad n\varepsilon<\frac14,\qquad p_{x_\star}>\frac34.\)

3. The optimal policy in the constructed instance

A type’s record utility equals one precisely when its element has been covered by a drawn set offer, or the universal offer has appeared.

If some element remains uncovered, its type has weight at least one. Drawing \(x_\star\) improves that type’s contribution by at least one. Hence

\(\displaystyle d(r)\geq p_{x_\star}>\frac34>c.\)

If every element is covered, every record utility is already maximal, so \(d(r)=0\).

By the proposition, the optimal policy therefore continues until every element is covered, then stops. Let \(H^*\) be its search count. The universal offer guarantees

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[H^*]\leq\frac1{p_{x_\star}}<\frac43.\)

Its terminal utility is always \(q\), giving

\(\displaystyle V^*=q-\frac12\mathbb E[H^*].\)

Thus the hardness holds even though the optimal policy has bounded expected search count.

4. Extracting Set Cover from the expected search count

For each \(j\geq0\), let \(C_j\) count the ordered tuples

\(\displaystyle (i_1,\ldots,i_j)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^j\)

whose associated sets cover \(E\). Put \(A_j=n^j-C_j\). Because \(E\) is nonempty, \(C_0=0\) and \(A_0=1\).

The event \(H^*>j\) occurs exactly when the first \(j\) draws are set offers whose union fails to cover \(E\). Each specified tuple of set offers has probability \(\varepsilon^j\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \Pr(H^*>j)=A_j\varepsilon^j.\)

The tail-sum identity for a nonnegative integer-valued random variable gives

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[H^*] =\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}A_j\varepsilon^j. \)

Since \(n\varepsilon<1\), define

\(\displaystyle D=\frac1{1-n\varepsilon}-\mathbb E[H^*] =\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}C_j\varepsilon^j. \)

The series converge absolutely, because \(0\leq C_j\leq n^j\).

Yes instances

If a cover uses \(r\leq b\) sets, then \(r\geq1\). Order those sets and repeat one of them until obtaining a covering tuple of length \(b\). Thus \(C_b\geq1\), implying

\(\displaystyle D\geq\varepsilon^b.\)

No instances

If no cover uses at most \(b\) sets, then \(C_j=0\) for every \(j\leq b\). Indeed, a covering tuple of length \(j\) would give a cover consisting of at most \(j\) distinct sets.

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle D\leq \sum_{j=b+1}^{\infty}n^j\varepsilon^j =\frac{(n\varepsilon)^{b+1}}{1-n\varepsilon}. \)

Using \(n\varepsilon<\frac14\) and \(B>4n^{b+1}\), we obtain

\(\displaystyle D< \frac43\frac{n^{b+1}}{B^{b+1}} < \frac13\,B^{-b} = \frac13\,\varepsilon^b. \)

In particular, no instances satisfy \(D<\varepsilon^b\).

The separating threshold

Define the rational number

\(\displaystyle \theta= q-\frac1{2(1-n\varepsilon)}+\frac{\varepsilon^b}{2}. \)

Since

\(\displaystyle V^*= q-\frac1{2(1-n\varepsilon)}+\frac D2, \)

the two cases prove exactly

\(\displaystyle V^*\geq\theta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E\text{ has a cover using at most }b\text{ sets}. \)

5. Encoding size and conclusion

The construction has \(m=n+2\), \(\tau=u\), and \(k=2\). Its utility tables, coordinates, masses, and total mass have polynomial encoding length.

Also,

\(\displaystyle \log_2B=2+(b+1)\ell=O(b\log(n+1)).\)

Thus the probabilities have polynomial binary length. The threshold involves \(B^b\) and \(B-n\), so its binary length is \(O(b^2\log(n+1)+\log u)\), also polynomial because \(b\leq n\). The entire reduction is computable in polynomial time.

For completeness, the constructed optimal value is rational. If \(T(A)\) is the expected remaining search count when the covered elements are \(A\subseteq E\), then \(T(E)=0\), and for \(A\neq E\),

\(\displaystyle T(A)= \frac{ 1+\varepsilon\sum_{i:S_i\not\subseteq A}T(A\cup S_i) }{ 1-\varepsilon|\{i:S_i\subseteq A\}| }. \)

This follows by conditioning on the next draw and moving self-loop terms to the left. Every successor in the numerator strictly contains \(A\), and the denominator is at least \(1-n\varepsilon>0\). Backward induction over subsets therefore gives rational \(T(A)\), and hence rational \(V^*\).

We have proved that deciding whether \(V^*\) is at least a supplied rational threshold is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. A polynomial-time algorithm returning the exact value as a binary rational would solve this decision problem by rational comparison. Therefore exact value computation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, while constructing an optimal executable policy is polynomial-time solvable.
