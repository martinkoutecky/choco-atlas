The number of stable candidates is

$$ g_i\geq1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k}g_i=n-k. $$
There are $\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}$ positive compositions of this sum. There are $n$ choices of marked element, and every unmarked stable set has exactly $k$ markings. This proves the formula.

4. Reduction to normalized self-dual Boolean utilities

A Boolean utility is a function
$$ f:2^M\longrightarrow\{0,1\}. $$
Call it self-dual if
$$ f(M\setminus A)=1-f(A) $$
for every $A\subseteq M$.

Theorem 4: Boolean self-dual normal form

There is a polynomial-time search reduction from the stated problem to its restriction to monotone, self-dual Boolean utilities satisfying
$$ f_t(\varnothing)=0, \qquad f_t(M)=1. $$
The reduction preserves $M$, $N$, $\tau$, and the masses. Every output for the transformed instance is an output for the original instance.

Proof. Fix an item $a\in M$, possible because $m\geq1$. For each type, define
$$ D_t(A)=u_t(A)-u_t(M\setminus A). $$
If $A\subseteq B$, monotonicity gives
$$ u_t(A)\leq u_t(B), \qquad u_t(M\setminus A)\geq u_t(M\setminus B). $$
Subtracting yields
$$ D_t(A)\leq D_t(B). $$
Also,
$$ D_t(M\setminus A)=-D_t(A). $$

Now define
$$ f_t(A)= \begin{cases} 1,&D_t(A)>0,\\ 1,&D_t(A)=0\text{ and }a\in A,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$

We check every required property.

Monotonicity. Suppose $A\subseteq B$ and $f_t(A)=1$.

If $D_t(A)>0$, then $D_t(B)\geq D_t(A)>0$, so $f_t(B)=1$.

Otherwise $D_t(A)=0$ and $a\in A$. Then $D_t(B)\geq0$ and $a\in B$. Either clause assigning value $1$ therefore applies to $B$.

Self-duality. If $D_t(A)\neq0$, complementation reverses its sign, so exactly one of $A$ and its complement receives value $1$. If $D_t(A)=0$, exactly one contains $a$. Thus again
$$ f_t(M\setminus A)=1-f_t(A). $$

Normalization. Monotonicity of the original utility gives $D_t(\varnothing)\leq0$. If equality holds, the tie rule still assigns $f_t(\varnothing)=0$. Self-duality then gives $f_t(M)=1$.

Solution preservation. For a self-dual Boolean function,
$$ f_t(S)\geq f_t(M\setminus S) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_t(S)=1. $$
By construction, $f_t(S)=1$ implies $D_t(S)\geq0$. Consequently any transformed solution satisfies
$$ u_t(S)\geq u_t(M\setminus S) $$
for every original type. Its cardinality bound is unchanged.

Circuit construction. Evaluate the original circuit on $A$ and its complement. Compare the two rationals by cross multiplication, and implement the tie rule with the input bit for $a$. Elementary binary multiplication and comparison have polynomial-size Boolean circuits. Output denominator $1$. The construction therefore has polynomial size and takes polynomial time. $\square$

The reverse search reduction is simply inclusion: Boolean utilities with denominator $1$ are allowed original utilities. Hence the general problem is polynomial-time search equivalent to this restricted problem.

The restriction turns the objective into finding a small set satisfying
$$ f_1(S)=\cdots=f_\tau(S)=1. $$
It does not yield a polynomial-time algorithm by itself.

Why greedy deletion is insufficient

Even one monotone self-dual Boolean utility can have inclusion-minimal winning sets larger than the permitted bound.

For $M=\{1,2,3,4\}$, define
$$ f(A)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \bigl(1\in A\text{ and }A\cap\{2,3,4\}\neq\varnothing\bigr) \text{ or } \{2,3,4\}\subseteq A. $$

Both clauses are monotone. To check self-duality, first suppose $1\in A$. Then $f(A)=1$ exactly when $A$ contains another item, while $f(M\setminus A)=1$ exactly when it contains no other item. The case $1\notin A$ follows by exchanging $A$ with its complement.

The set $\{2,3,4\}$ is inclusion-minimal winning: it wins, and each proper subset loses. Nevertheless, when $N=\tau=1$, the bound is $2$, and $\{1,2\}$ is winning. Deleting item $1$ first from $M$ leaves the larger inclusion-minimal set. Thus inclusion-minimality alone does not establish the required cardinality.

5. An explicit reduction to Tucker search

The following gives a complexity upper placement, not a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm.

For signed vectors
$$ x\in\{-1,0,1\}^n, $$
write
$$ x^+=\{i:x_i=1\}, \qquad x^-=\{i:x_i=-1\}. $$
Write $x\preceq y$ when every nonzero coordinate of $x$ has the same value in $y$.

Octahedral Tucker search takes a circuit computing an antipodal labeling
$$ \lambda:\{-1,0,1\}^n\setminus\{0\} \longrightarrow \{\pm1,\ldots,\pm(n-1)\}, $$
where
$$ \lambda(-x)=-\lambda(x), $$
and asks for nonzero $x\preceq y$ with
$$ \lambda(x)=-\lambda(y). $$

The known theorem we use is that this search problem belongs to $\mathsf{PPA}$. It is the prime-$2$ case of the $\mathbb Z_p$-Tucker membership theorem in [Haviv, Definition 18 and Theorem 19](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol287-itcs2024/LIPIcs.ITCS.2024.60/LIPIcs.ITCS.2024.60.pdf). Here $\mathsf{PPA}$ denotes the class of total search problems reducible in polynomial time to finding another leaf in a succinctly represented undirected graph of maximum degree $2$, given one leaf.

5.1 From agreeable-set search to a coloring

Continue with $N<m$, and use
$$ n=2k+\tau-1 $$
and $X\subseteq M$ as in Section 2. Identify $X$ with $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.

For every $k$-subset $A\subseteq X$, define $c(A)$ to be its smallest offending type if $A$ is bad, and set $c(A)=1$ otherwise. This gives a polynomial-size coloring circuit
$$ c:\binom{X}{k}\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,\tau\}. $$

If disjoint $A,B$ have $c(A)=c(B)$, they cannot both be bad: otherwise the common color would offend on both, contradicting Lemma 1. Thus testing their complements produces a valid output.

5.2 From the coloring to an antipodal labeling

Fix the order on $X$. For any subset $B$ of size at least $k$, let $K(B)$ be its first $k$ elements.

For a nonzero signed vector $x$, construct $\lambda(x)$ as follows.

Small case. If
$$ |x^+|\leq k-1 \quad\text{and}\quad |x^-|\leq k-1, $$
give the label magnitude
$$ |\lambda(x)|=|x^+|+|x^-|. $$
Its sign is the sign of the first nonzero coordinate of $x$.

This magnitude lies between $1$ and $2k-2$. When $k=1$, this case never occurs.

Large case. Otherwise, at least one side has size at least $k$. For each such side, compute
$$ c(K(x^+)) \quad\text{or}\quad c(K(x^-)). $$
Choose the side with smaller color. If both colors exist and are equal, choose the side whose sign is the sign of the first nonzero coordinate of $x$.

If the selected color is $j$, set
$$ |\lambda(x)|=2k-2+j, $$
with the sign of the selected side.

The largest possible magnitude is
$$ 2k-2+\tau=n-1. $$
Thus all labels lie in the required range. All operations are polynomial-time computable and can be compiled into a polynomial-size circuit.

Negation exchanges the two sides. In the small case it reverses the first nonzero sign. In the large case it exchanges the colors’ sides, and the tie rule also reverses. Therefore
$$ \lambda(-x)=-\lambda(x). $$
This verifies the antipodality hypothesis.

5.3 Decoding every Tucker solution

Suppose Tucker search returns nonzero $x\preceq y$ with opposite labels.

They cannot both be in the small case. Equal label magnitudes would imply equal support cardinalities. Since $x\preceq y$, their supports and signs would then coincide, giving $x=y$ and hence identical labels.

They cannot belong to different cases, because small magnitudes are at most $2k-2$, while large magnitudes are at least $2k-1$.

Thus both are in the large case. Their opposite labels select opposite sides with the same color $j$.

If the selected sides are $x^+$ and $y^-$, then
$$ x^+\subseteq y^+, \qquad y^+\cap y^-=\varnothing. $$
Therefore $K(x^+)$ and $K(y^-)$ are disjoint and have color $j$. If the selected sides are $x^-$ and $y^+$, the same argument uses $x^-\subseteq y^-$.

We have therefore decoded a monochromatic disjoint pair. Section 5.1 turns it into a valid agreeable set.

All construction and decoding times are polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. No step expands the binary-encoded population scale $N$.

5.4 Handling the monotonicity promise

Strictly speaking, the original problem is a promise search problem. The reduction above maps every promised instance to a valid Tucker instance, and every Tucker solution decodes to an original solution.

There is also a total, polynomially verifiable extension in $\mathsf{PPA}$. Allow these outputs:

  • A set satisfying the required cardinality and locally evaluated utility comparisons.
  • A monotonicity violation

$$ A\subseteq B,\qquad u_t(A)>u_t(B). $$

  • A zero-denominator witness, or a polynomially detectable malformed-input certificate.

For completeness, the decoder can produce these outputs even without monotonicity. Replace any zero denominator by $1$ when constructing the coloring circuit, so the Tucker instance is always defined. When decoding its pair, evaluate all endpoint utilities. If any denominator is zero, return that witness.

Otherwise suppose both disjoint endpoints $A,B$ are bad with the same offending type $t$. If
$$ u_t(B)>u_t(M\setminus A), $$
then $B\subseteq M\setminus A$ is a monotonicity violation. If this inequality fails, then
$$ u_t(A)>u_t(M\setminus A)\geq u_t(B)>u_t(M\setminus B), $$
so $A\subseteq M\setminus B$ is a monotonicity violation.

If at least one endpoint is not bad, its complement is a valid set.

For $N\geq m$, evaluating $M$ and $\varnothing$ similarly gives either the set $M$, a zero-denominator witness, or a monotonicity violation. Other malformed structural and mass conditions can be checked directly in polynomial time.

All output certificates are polynomially verifiable. On promised inputs, only the requested agreeable sets are available. This proves the stated total-extension membership.

6. What the complexity results do and do not establish

Randomized parameterized running time

The coloring in Section 5.1 has exactly the permitted palette size
$$ \tau=n-2k+1. $$
Haviv’s randomized Kneser algorithm applies for $n\geq2k$ and finds a monochromatic disjoint pair in time $n^{O(1)}k^{O(k)}$ with high probability. These hypotheses were checked above. See [Haviv, Theorem 1.1 and its Kneser consequence](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.09009v3#S1.SS1).

Accounting for exact circuit evaluations gives
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\,k^{O(k)}. $$
Verify the decoded set and restart on failure. This yields an always-correct algorithm with that expected running-time bound. Its dependence on $N$ is through its encoding and $k$, rather than the numerical value of $N$.

The half-size hardness theorem has a different bound

For even $m$ and $N=\tau=2$, this task permits cardinality
$$ \frac m2+1. $$
The cited two-agent hardness concerns cardinality $m/2$. See [Manurangsi and Suksompong](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/0048.pdf).

The distinction occurs already with four items and additive weights
$$ (1,1,1,3), \qquad (5,5,5,3). $$
A two-item set avoiding item four has first utility $2$, versus complement utility $4$. A two-item set containing item four has second utility $8$, versus complement utility $10$. Thus no two-item set is agreeable.

But the first three items have utilities $3$ and $15$, versus complement utilities $3$ and $3$. They satisfy this task’s bound. Hence the smaller-bound hardness theorem does not by itself establish the requested hardness.

A conditional obstruction to promise-preserving hardness

Suppose a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm decides $\mathsf{SAT}$ using an oracle for this problem, and satisfies both conditions:

  • Every query satisfies the promises after every sequence of legal previous answers.
  • Its decision is correct for every sequence of legal answers.

Then
$$ \mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}. $$

Proof. For an unsatisfiable formula, certify a rejecting oracle transcript. Every query has a solution by Proposition 2, so such a transcript exists. Its length is polynomial.

A verifier simulates the algorithm and checks each proposed oracle answer by cardinality and exact rational comparisons. Promise preservation applies inductively along this transcript. If a fixed oracle must answer repeated queries consistently, the verifier checks that too.

For a satisfiable formula, no verified transcript can reject, because all its answers are legal and the assumed algorithm must accept for every legal answer sequence.

Thus $\mathsf{UNSAT}\in\mathsf{NP}$, implying $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$. $\square$

This is a conditional barrier for the specified reduction notion, not an unconditional non-hardness theorem.

The unrestricted deterministic polynomial-time question remains unsettled by this write-up. The Boolean normal form and $\mathsf{PPA}$ placement narrow its formulation; the disjoint-block algorithm settles an additional parameter regime.
