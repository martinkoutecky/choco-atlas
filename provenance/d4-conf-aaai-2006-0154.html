The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\) and a finite support of strict rankings \(t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and with no pair tied in the population, compute a ranking \(\succ\) minimizing the number of unordered candidate pairs that \(\succ\) orders opposite to their population pairwise-majority direction.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a finite nonempty candidate set, with \(m=|C|\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings \(t\) of \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For distinct candidates, define \(a\to b\) when \(\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t>1/2\). Promise that no pair has mass exactly \(1/2\) in either direction.
For a strict ranking \(\succ\) of \(C\), its disagreement count is \(D(\succ)=|\{(a,b)\in C^2:a\succ b\text{ and }b\to a\}|\); each unordered pair contributes at most one. Compute a ranking minimizing \(D\), allowing any minimizer when several exist.

Is computing an optimal ranking for this society possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of voters with strict rankings, computing a Slater ranking is NP-hard even without pairwise ties.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required the candidate set to be nonempty, as naturally implicit in the ranking problem.
  • Specified that any optimal ranking may be returned; no tie-breaking among minimizers was prescribed.
  • Defined L as the total bit length of the support-encoded input.

Conclusion

Computing an optimal ranking is \(\text{NP-hard}\). This holds even when every mass is a dyadic rational, meaning a rational with denominator a power of two. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

Starting hardness result

We use the supplied premise: computing a Slater ranking from an explicitly listed finite electorate of strict rankings is \(\text{NP-hard}\), even without pairwise ties. This is also established in [Conitzer’s paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-098.pdf).

Let such an instance have candidates \(C\) and voters with rankings \(v_1,\ldots,v_N\), where \(N\geq 1\). For distinct candidates \(a,b\), put
\[ p_{ab}=\frac{|\{i:a\succ_{v_i}b\}|}{N}. \]
The absence of ties implies
\[ \left|p_{ab}-\frac12\right| =\frac{\left|2|\{i:a\succ_{v_i}b\}|-N\right|}{2N} \geq\frac{1}{2N}, \]
because the numerator is a nonzero integer.

We construct a support-list society with exactly the same pairwise majority relation.

Construction with dyadic masses

Choose the smallest power of two \(Q\) satisfying \(Q>2N^2\). Then \(Q\leq 4N^2\). Write
\[ Q=Nq+r,\qquad 0\leq r<N. \]
Assign voter \(i\) the temporary mass
\[ w_i= \begin{cases} (q+1)/Q,&1\leq i\leq r,\\ q/Q,&r<i\leq N. \end{cases} \]
Since \(Q>N\), we have \(q\geq1\), so every mass is positive. Moreover,
\[ \sum_{i=1}^Nw_i =\frac{r(q+1)+(N-r)q}{Q}=1. \]
Each mass approximates the original uniform mass:
\[ \left|w_i-\frac1N\right|\leq\frac1Q. \]
Indeed, for the two possible values of \(w_i\), the differences are respectively \((N-r)/(NQ)\) and \(r/(NQ)\).

Now merge identical rankings. For each distinct ranking \(t\) appearing among the voters, define
\[ \mu_t=\sum_{\{i:v_i=t\}}w_i. \]
These masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. The resulting support has \(\tau\leq N\) distinct rankings.

Preservation of every majority edge

For distinct candidates \(a,b\), the constructed society’s mass preferring \(a\) to \(b\) is
\[ p'_{ab} =\sum_{\{t:a\succ_tb\}}\mu_t =\sum_{\{i:a\succ_{v_i}b\}}w_i. \]
Consequently,
\[ |p'_{ab}-p_{ab}| \leq\sum_{\{i:a\succ_{v_i}b\}}\left|w_i-\frac1N\right| \leq\frac NQ <\frac1{2N}. \]
The original probability \(p_{ab}\) lies at distance at least \(1/(2N)\) from \(1/2\). Thus this perturbation cannot reach or cross \(1/2\). It follows that
\[ p'_{ab}>\frac12 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{ab}>\frac12. \]
The constructed society therefore satisfies the no-ties promise and has exactly the original majority tournament.

For every output ranking \(\succ\), its disagreement count consequently equals its Slater disagreement count in the original electorate. Hence every minimizer for the constructed society is a Slater ranking for the original instance.

Polynomial size and hardness transfer

The electorate is explicitly listed, so \(N\) is bounded by its input length. Grouping identical rankings and calculating their masses take polynomial time.

Every merged mass has the form \(A_t/Q\), with \(1\leq A_t\leq Q\). Since \(Q\leq4N^2\), its numerator and denominator require \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits. There are at most \(N\) support entries, each containing one original ranking. Thus the constructed input length \(L\), candidate count \(m\), and support size \(\tau\) are all polynomially bounded in the original input length.

A hypothetical algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), applied to this construction, would therefore compute a Slater ranking in polynomial time for every instance of the supplied hard problem. This proves the claimed \(\text{NP-hardness}\).
