The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite two-player game \(G=(A_1,A_2,u_1,u_2)\) with rational payoffs, a target set \(D\subseteq A_1\times A_2\), and \(k\ge 0\), consider a unit mass of independent encounters, each retaining the original atomic two-player game. The population has role types \(1\) and \(2\), each of mass \(1/2\). Does there exist a pair of nonnegative bonus tables \(v_i:A_1\times A_2\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) such that \(\varnothing\ne ND_1(v)\times ND_2(v)\subseteq D\) and every rational outcome distribution \(\rho\) supported on \(ND_1(v)\times ND_2(v)\) has \(\int (v_1+v_2)\,d\rho\le k\)?

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here the types are roles \(1,2\), each of mass \(1/2\), so \(\tau=2\).

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even when \(\tau=2\) and \(k=0\). The encounter-mass cost constraint is exactly the discrete worst-case cost constraint.

1. The undominated product is always nonempty

Fix finite nonnegative bonus tables \(v_1,v_2\). For player \(i\), write \(w_i=u_i+v_i\), and choose an action \(a_i\) maximizing
\(\sum_{b\in A_{-i}}w_i(a_i,b)\).

Such a maximizer exists because the action set is finite and nonempty. If another action weakly dominated \(a_i\), its payoff would be at least as large at every opponent action and strictly larger at one. Its sum would therefore be strictly larger, contradicting maximality.

Consequently, \(ND_i(v)\ne\varnothing\) for both players. In particular, the nonemptiness requirement in the task does not introduce an additional restriction.

2. Encounter-mass cost equals worst-case cost

Let \(S(v)=ND_1(v)\times ND_2(v)\), and define \(c_v(a,b)=v_1(a,b)+v_2(a,b)\). Since \(S(v)\) is finite and nonempty, the number
\(C(v)=\max_{(a,b)\in S(v)}c_v(a,b)\)
exists.

For every permitted rational probability distribution \(\rho\),
\[ \text{ } \]
\(\displaystyle \int(v_1+v_2)\,d\rho =\sum_{(a,b)\in S(v)}\rho(a,b)c_v(a,b) \le C(v)\sum_{(a,b)\in S(v)}\rho(a,b) =C(v).\)

Conversely, choose a pair \((a^\ast,b^\ast)\) attaining this maximum. Its point-mass distribution has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), hence rational entries, and is permitted by the task. Its cost is exactly \(C(v)\).

Thus, for every fixed pair of bonus tables,
\(\displaystyle \left[\text{every permitted }\rho\text{ has cost at most }k\right] \iff \left[\max_{(a,b)\in S(v)}(v_1(a,b)+v_2(a,b))\le k\right]. \)

This equivalence holds for arbitrary real bonuses; it does not require rational bonuses or an approximation argument.

The role masses \(1/2,1/2\) introduce no additional factor: the task explicitly defines cost per unit encounter mass as the integral of the total payment \(v_1+v_2\).

3. The hardness theorem and its hypotheses

The external result used is [Chen et al., Theorem 3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00699): two-player Game Implementation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard at zero budget, with cost measured by the maximum total payment over undominated profiles. Their domination rule compares pure actions against every original opponent action, requiring a weak inequality everywhere and a strict inequality somewhere. Their reduction has payoffs in \(\{0,1,2\}\) and a nonempty rectangular desired set.

The intended payment domain is nonnegative bonuses, as defined in the [original model, Section 2](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs/project/jair/pub/volume21/monderer04a.pdf). We use the corrected hardness result, not the original hardness proof.

There is a finite-payment detail in the corrected proof: some forward witnesses are written using \(\infty\). These can be replaced by \(3\). The actions eliminated by those comparisons receive zero bonuses, so their modified payoffs are at most \(2\); a bonus of \(3\) preserves every required strict comparison. Bonuses vanish on the product of the chosen action sets, so eliminating the other actions still gives zero cost. Thus the hardness construction admits finite nonnegative witnesses.

4. Polynomial reduction

Take a zero-budget instance from that hardness construction. Retain its two action sets, payoff tables, and desired set, setting \(D\) equal to that desired set and \(k=0\). Supply the two supported role types, each with mass \(1/2\). Hence \(\tau=2\).

All input requirements hold:

  • The game is explicitly tabulated, finite, and has rational payoffs.
  • The desired set is nonempty. If supplied through its two rectangular factors, explicitly list their Cartesian product.
  • The budget \(0\) has constant binary encoding length.
  • The society has exactly two supported types with rational masses.

For any finite nonnegative bonus tables, the domination tests in the source and target problems coincide. By Section 2, their cost tests also coincide. Therefore those tables implement the source desired set at zero worst-case cost if and only if they are feasible for the encounter-mass instance. Taking the existential quantifier over bonus tables preserves this equivalence.

The reduction copies the payoff tables and lists at most \(|A_1||A_2|\le m^2/4\) desired pairs. Including binary action identifiers, its running time is \(O(L+m^2\log(m+1)+\tau)\), where \(L\) is the total explicit encoding length; here \(\tau=2\). In particular, it is polynomial.

Consequently, an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) for the stated problem would solve an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard problem in polynomial time. Such an algorithm cannot exist unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). This settles the requested hardness classification; no claim of membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) is needed.
