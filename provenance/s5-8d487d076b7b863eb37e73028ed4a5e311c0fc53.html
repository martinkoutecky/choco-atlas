The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a horizon \(T\), common cost functions satisfying the paper’s assumptions, and \(\tau\) retailer types with rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\) and rational per-unit-mass demand profiles \(d^r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^T\), compute cost-share rates \(a_r\). For each fractional subpopulation \(\nu\) with \(0\le \nu_r\le\mu_r\), let \(D_t(\nu)=\sum_r \nu_r d_t^r\) and let \(F(\nu)\) be the minimum ELS cost of meeting \(D(\nu)\). Require \(\sum_r\mu_r a_r=F(\mu)\) and \(\sum_r\nu_r a_r\le F(\nu)\) for every such \(\nu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(m:=T\ge1\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) retailer types, each with a positive binary rational mass \(\mu_r\), where \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\), and a per-unit-mass demand profile \(d^r\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{T}\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
All types share nonnegative rational holding and backlogging rates \(h_t^+,h_t^-\). Ordering costs are explicitly encoded by \(c_t(0)=0\) and \(c_t(q)=\min_{1\le j\le k_t}(K_{tj}+s_{tj}q)\) for \(q>0\), with \(k_t\ge1\) and nonnegative rational coefficients; these costs are nondecreasing and concave and may include setup charges. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
For every real fractional subpopulation \(0\le\nu_r\le\mu_r\), set \(D_t(\nu)=\sum_r\nu_rd_t^r\). Define \(F(\nu)\) as the minimum of \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}[c_t(z_t)+h_t^+I_t^++h_t^-I_t^-]\), over nonnegative real orders and inventories satisfying \(z_t+I_{t-1}^+-I_{t-1}^-=D_t(\nu)+I_t^+-I_t^-\) and \(I_0^+=I_0^-=I_T^+=I_T^-=0\). Orders are uncapacitated, lead time is zero, and backlogging within the horizon is allowed.
The computational problem is to find rates \(a\in\mathbb Q^\tau\), with either sign permitted, satisfying \(\sum_r\mu_ra_r=F(\mu)\) and \(\sum_r\nu_ra_r\le F(\nu)\) for every such \(\nu\), or determine that none exist.

Is this computational problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m=T\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite retailers with common nondecreasing concave ordering costs and linear holding and backlogging costs, the core is nonempty and a core allocation is computable in polynomial time ([Chen–Zhang](https://publish.illinois.edu/xinchen/files/2016/04/elsgame_pom_final.pdf)).

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Polynomial-time solvability

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m=T\), \(\tau\), and \(L\). Moreover, a feasible rate vector always exists.

The key is that the optimized lot-sizing cost is concave as a function of aggregate demand. Consequently, the constraints for coalitions containing whole supported types imply all fractional-subpopulation constraints. This reduces the problem to the finite-retailer theorem stated in the question.

1. Concavity of the optimized demand cost

Let \(C(D)\) denote the lot-sizing cost for a demand vector \(D\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\), under the costs and boundary conditions in the question. Thus \(F(\nu)=C(D(\nu))\).

Define the cost of carrying one unit from its ordering period \(s\) to its demand period \(t\) by

\(H_{st}=\begin{cases} \sum_{u=s}^{t-1}h_u^+,&s<t,\\ 0,&s=t,\\ \sum_{u=t}^{s-1}h_u^-,&s>t. \end{cases}\)

We first establish a transportation representation.

Lemma 1: Transportation representation

For every nonnegative demand vector \(D\),

\(C(D)=\min_{\substack{x_{st}\ge0\\\sum_s x_{st}=D_t\ \text{for every }t}} \left\{\sum_s c_s\!\left(\sum_t x_{st}\right)+\sum_{s,t}H_{st}x_{st}\right\}.\)

Here \(x_{st}\) is demand in period \(t\) supplied by an order in period \(s\).

Proof. Given a feasible transportation matrix, set \(z_s=\sum_t x_{st}\). At a boundary \(u\), define the forward and backward crossings

\(A_u=\sum_{s\le u<t}x_{st},\qquad B_u=\sum_{t\le u<s}x_{st}.\)

The cumulative order surplus is

\(J_u=\sum_{s\le u}z_s-\sum_{t\le u}D_t=A_u-B_u.\)

Choose inventories \(I_u^+=\max\{J_u,0\}\) and \(I_u^-=\max\{-J_u,0\}\). These satisfy the inventory equations and the initial and terminal conditions. Moreover, \(I_u^+\le A_u\) and \(I_u^-\le B_u\). Nonnegative holding and backlogging rates therefore imply that this inventory solution costs at most the transportation solution.

Conversely, take any feasible inventory solution. Summing its balance equations gives

\(\sum_s z_s=\sum_tD_t=:W.\)

Its net inventory at boundary \(u\) is necessarily \(J_u\). Replacing its inventories by the positive and negative parts of \(J_u\) preserves feasibility and weakly decreases cost.

To construct transportation attaining this reduced cost, let

\(Z_u=\sum_{s\le u}z_s,\qquad P_u=\sum_{t\le u}D_t,\qquad Z_0=P_0=0,\)

and set

\(x_{st}=\operatorname{length}\bigl([Z_{s-1},Z_s)\cap[P_{t-1},P_t)\bigr).\)

The two families of intervals partition \([0,W)\), so this matrix has row sums \(z_s\) and column sums \(D_t\). Its forward crossing at boundary \(u\) is

\(\operatorname{length}\bigl([0,Z_u)\cap[P_u,W)\bigr)=\max\{Z_u-P_u,0\},\)

and its backward crossing is \(\max\{P_u-Z_u,0\}\). Hence its transportation charges equal the reduced inventory and backlogging charges.

The two constructions prove equality of the optimal values. Attainment also follows from the finite representation proved next. \(\square\)

Lemma 2: Concavity in aggregate demand

The function \(C\) is concave on \(\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\).

Proof. For a map \(\phi:\{1,\ldots,T\}\to\{1,\ldots,T\}\), define

\(G_\phi(D)= \sum_{s=1}^{T}c_s\!\left(\sum_{t:\phi(t)=s}D_t\right) +\sum_{t=1}^{T}H_{\phi(t),t}D_t.\)

This is the transportation cost when all demand in each period \(t\) is supplied from the single period \(\phi(t)\). We claim that

\(C(D)=\min_\phi G_\phi(D).\)

For fixed \(D\), the transportation feasible set is a product of simplices, one for each demand period. Its vertices assign each positive-demand column entirely to one ordering period. Every feasible matrix is a convex combination of these vertices.

The transportation objective is concave in its matrix variables: each ordering term is a concave function of a linear row sum, and the transportation charges are linear. Thus its value at any convex combination of vertices is at least the corresponding weighted average of vertex values, and hence at least the smallest vertex value. A minimizing vertex therefore exists, proving the claimed representation. Zero-demand columns are fixed at zero and cause no difficulty.

For each fixed \(\phi\), the function \(G_\phi\) is concave in \(D\). For \(D,E\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\) and \(0\le\lambda\le1\), it follows that

\(\begin{aligned} C(\lambda D+(1-\lambda)E) &=\min_\phi G_\phi(\lambda D+(1-\lambda)E)\\ &\ge\min_\phi\bigl[\lambda G_\phi(D)+(1-\lambda)G_\phi(E)\bigr]\\ &\ge\lambda C(D)+(1-\lambda)C(E). \end{aligned}\)

This argument uses concavity at zero as well as at positive quantities; continuity at zero is unnecessary. Thus setup charges are covered. \(\square\)

Since \(D(\nu)\) is linear in \(\nu\), Lemma 2 also proves that \(F\) is concave on the subpopulation box.

2. Exact reduction to whole-type coalitions

Let \(N=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Define a finite retailer game with retailer \(r\) having demand vector

\(b^r=\mu_rd^r.\)

For \(S\subseteq N\), define the box vertex

\(\nu_r^S=\begin{cases}\mu_r,&r\in S,\\0,&r\notin S,\end{cases}\)

and the finite coalition cost

\(v(S)=C\!\left(\sum_{r\in S}b^r\right)=F(\nu^S).\)

Suppose \(x\in\mathbb Q^\tau\) is a finite-game core allocation, meaning

\(\sum_{r\in N}x_r=v(N),\qquad \sum_{r\in S}x_r\le v(S)\quad\text{for every }S\subseteq N.\)

Set \(a_r=x_r/\mu_r\), which is well defined because every supported mass is positive.

Proposition: These rates satisfy every fractional constraint

Proof. Efficiency follows immediately:

\(\sum_r\mu_ra_r=\sum_rx_r=v(N)=F(\mu).\)

Now fix any real subpopulation \(\nu\) with \(0\le\nu_r\le\mu_r\). Put \(\theta_r=\nu_r/\mu_r\) and define

\(w_S=\prod_{r\in S}\theta_r\prod_{r\notin S}(1-\theta_r).\)

These weights are nonnegative and satisfy

\(\sum_{S\subseteq N}w_S=1,\qquad \sum_{S\ni r}w_S=\theta_r,\qquad \nu=\sum_{S\subseteq N}w_S\nu^S.\)

Concavity of \(F\), followed by the finite core inequalities, gives

\(\begin{aligned} F(\nu) &\ge\sum_{S\subseteq N}w_SF(\nu^S)\\ &=\sum_{S\subseteq N}w_Sv(S)\\ &\ge\sum_{S\subseteq N}w_S\sum_{r\in S}x_r\\ &=\sum_r\theta_rx_r =\sum_r\nu_ra_r. \end{aligned}\)

This applies to real \(\nu\), without any rationality restriction. \(\square\)

Conversely, any feasible fractional rate vector yields a finite core allocation \(x_r=\mu_ra_r\), simply by applying its inequalities to the vertices \(\nu^S\).

Thus the two allocation problems correspond exactly under this rescaling. The exponentially many weights above are used only in the proof; the algorithm never enumerates them.

3. Polynomial-time computation and binary encoding

We apply the following known result.

Chen–Zhang finite-retailer theorem. For finite retailers with common nondecreasing concave ordering costs satisfying \(c_t(0)=0\), and common linear holding and backlogging costs, the economic lot-sizing game has a nonempty core, and a core allocation can be computed in polynomial time. Their construction uses a polynomial-size LP whose coefficients involve ordering-cost evaluations at interval-demand sums. See [Theorem 2 and LP (19) in the working-paper version](https://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/26279/2/06-01.pdf).

All structural hypotheses hold for the finite game above: there are exactly \(\tau\) retailers, their demands are \(b^r\), and they share precisely the costs specified in the question.

To address rational demands explicitly, choose a positive integer \(Q\) divisible by all denominators of the coordinates of the vectors \(b^r\). The product of these denominators suffices and has polynomial binary length. Replace the data by

\(\widehat b^r=Qb^r,\qquad \widehat c_t(q)=c_t(q/Q),\qquad \widehat h_t^\pm=h_t^\pm/Q.\)

The demands are now integers. Scaling orders and inventories by \(Q\) gives a cost-preserving bijection of feasible solutions, so every finite coalition cost remains unchanged. The transformed ordering costs retain the explicit representation

\(\widehat c_t(q)=\min_j\left(K_{tj}+\frac{s_{tj}}Qq\right)\quad(q>0),\qquad \widehat c_t(0)=0.\)

This transformation has polynomial encoding length.

Each required cost evaluation can be performed exactly by evaluating the encoded affine pieces and taking their minimum. Interval-demand sums and the resulting costs have polynomial binary length. Consequently, the Chen–Zhang construction is a rational LP of polynomial size and coefficient length for this input. Polynomial-time rational linear programming therefore produces a rational core allocation \(x\) of polynomial encoding length.

The algorithm is:

  • Form the finite retailer demands \(b^r=\mu_rd^r\).
  • Compute a finite core allocation \(x\) using Chen–Zhang’s construction.
  • Return \(a_r=x_r/\mu_r\) for every supported type.

The final divisions also take polynomial time and preserve polynomial encoding length. Section 2 proves that the returned rates satisfy every required fractional-subpopulation inequality.

4. Conclusion

A feasible rational rate vector always exists and is computable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\). In particular, the number of supported types determines the number of finite retailers; no expansion according to mass denominators is needed.
