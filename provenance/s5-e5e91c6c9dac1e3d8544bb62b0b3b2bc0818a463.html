Result

Yes. There is a deterministic algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon)\) time that returns a rational feasible allocation with the requested approximation guarantee. Thus the success probability can be taken to be one.

The proof applies directly to the given mass recurrence. It uses diminishing returns, a polynomial-size discretization, and a polynomial-time numerical oracle obtained through convex optimization. In fact, the algorithm works for finite rates even without the nonsaturation promise.

1. Fixed-point formulation and diminishing returns

Write \(Bz\) for the vector whose coordinate indexed by \(u\) is \((Bz)_u=\sum_t\beta_{tu}z_t\), and define

\(\displaystyle F_x(z)_u=\mu_u-(\mu_u-x_u)\exp(-(Bz)_u).\)

All vector inequalities below are coordinatewise.

1.1. The recurrence iterates this map

The original recurrence gives \(y^r\ge0\) and \(0\le a^r\le\mu\). Consequently, \(a^r\) increases coordinatewise and has a limit.

Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \mu_u-a_u^{r+1}=(\mu_u-a_u^r)\exp(-(By^r)_u).\)

Multiplying these identities through round \(r\), and using \(\sum_{j=0}^r y^j=a^r\), gives

\(\displaystyle \mu_u-a_u^{r+1}=(\mu_u-x_u)\exp(-(Ba^r)_u).\)

Thus

\[\displaystyle a^0=x,\qquad a^{r+1}=F_x(a^r).\tag{1}\]

The map \(F_x\) is continuous, maps the box \([0,\mu]\) into itself, and is increasing in both \(x\) and \(z\). Therefore the terminal vector, denoted below by \(a(x)\), satisfies \(a(x)=F_x(a(x))\).

It is the least fixed point in this box: every fixed point \(z\) satisfies \(z=F_x(z)\ge x\), so induction in (1) gives \(a^r(x)\le z\), and hence \(a(x)\le z\).

1.2. Diminishing returns survives the infinite limit

We use the following definition. A function \(f\) on \([0,\mu]\) has diminishing returns if, whenever \(x\le z\) and both increments remain in the box,

\[\displaystyle f(x+h e_i)-f(x)\ge f(z+h e_i)-f(z),\qquad h\ge0.\tag{2}\]

For a twice continuously differentiable function, nonpositive entries of its Hessian imply (2), by integrating its coordinate derivative.

Regard \(F_x(z)_u\) as a function of the joint variables \((x,z)\). Its first partial derivatives are nonnegative. Its nonzero second partial derivatives are

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial^2F_x(z)_u}{\partial z_t\partial z_s} =-(\mu_u-x_u)\beta_{tu}\beta_{su}\exp(-(Bz)_u)\le0,\)

and

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial^2F_x(z)_u}{\partial x_v\partial z_t} =-\mathbf 1_{\{v=u\}}\beta_{tu}\exp(-(Bz)_u)\le0.\)

The second derivatives involving two seed coordinates are zero.

Starting with \(a^0(x)=x\), the chain rule now proves inductively that every coordinate of \(a^r(x)\) has nonnegative first derivatives and nonpositive second derivatives. Indeed, for a composition \(f(H(x))\),

\(\displaystyle \partial_{ij}(f\circ H) =\sum_{p,q}f_{pq}(H)\,\partial_iH_p\,\partial_jH_q +\sum_p f_p(H)\,\partial_{ij}H_p.\)

Both sums are nonpositive under the stated sign conditions.

Define the finite-round utility and welfare by

\(\displaystyle u_c^r(x)=\frac{\sum_{t:g(t)=c}a_t^r(x)}{\mu_c}, \qquad W^r(x)=\sum_c\mu_c(u_c^r(x))^\alpha.\)

On the interior of the seed box, all community utilities are positive. The Hessian entries contributed by a community are

\(\displaystyle \mu_c\left[ \alpha(u_c^r)^{\alpha-1}\partial_{ij}u_c^r +\alpha(\alpha-1)(u_c^r)^{\alpha-2} \partial_i u_c^r\,\partial_j u_c^r \right]\le0.\)

Thus \(W^r\) is increasing and has diminishing returns. Its continuity extends (2) to the boundary of the box.

Finally, \(W^r(x)\) converges pointwise to \(W_\alpha(x)\). Taking limits in each diminishing-returns inequality proves:

Lemma 1. The exact terminal welfare \(W_\alpha\) is nonnegative, increasing, and has diminishing returns on \([0,\mu]\).

This conclusion does not require continuity or differentiability of the terminal welfare at boundary allocations.

1.3. Scaling loses at most the scaling factor

Fix \(x,z\in[0,\mu]\). For each coordinate, put \(q=(Bz)_u\). As a function of \(\lambda\in[0,1]\),

\(\displaystyle F_{\lambda x}(\lambda z)_u =\mu_u-(\mu_u-\lambda x_u)e^{-\lambda q}\)

has second derivative

\(\displaystyle -\left[2x_uq+(\mu_u-\lambda x_u)q^2\right]e^{-\lambda q}\le0.\)

Its value at zero is zero. Concavity therefore gives

\(\displaystyle F_{\lambda x}(\lambda z)\ge\lambda F_x(z).\)

Using monotonicity and induction in (1), we obtain

\(\displaystyle a^r(\lambda x)\ge\lambda a^r(x),\)

and hence the same inequality for the terminal vectors. Consequently,

\[\displaystyle W_\alpha(\lambda x)\ge\lambda^\alpha W_\alpha(x) \ge\lambda W_\alpha(x),\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1.\tag{3}\]

2. A polynomial-time welfare oracle at positive allocations

The numerical issue is substantive: direct iteration of (1) need not converge in polynomially many rounds. We instead evaluate positive allocations through convex optimization.

Lemma 2. Given a rational allocation \(x\) with \(0<x_t\le\mu_t\) for every type and a rational accuracy \(0<\nu<1\), one can compute a rational value \(\widehat W(x)\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |\widehat W(x)-W_\alpha(x)|\le\nu\)

in time polynomial in the input bit length, the bit length of \(x\), and \(\log(1/\nu)\).

We prove the lemma, including its numerical conditioning.

2.1. Positive allocations have a unique fixed point

For fixed \(x>0\), each coordinate of \(F_x\) is concave in \(z\), and \(F_x(0)=x>0\).

Suppose \(p,q\) are distinct fixed points. Both are strictly positive. After interchanging them if necessary, some coordinate of \(p\) is smaller than the corresponding coordinate of \(q\). Set

\(\displaystyle \theta=\min_i p_i/q_i\in(0,1).\)

Then \(p\ge\theta q\), with equality in some coordinate. Concavity and monotonicity imply

\(\displaystyle p=F_x(p)\ge F_x(\theta q) \ge\theta F_x(q)+(1-\theta)F_x(0) =\theta q+(1-\theta)x>\theta q,\)

contradicting that equality. The terminal vector is therefore the unique fixed point.

Consider the compact convex set

\[\displaystyle C_x=\{z\in[0,\mu]:z\le F_x(z)\}.\tag{4}\]

Convexity follows because each constraint \(z_u-F_x(z)_u\le0\) is convex.

Every point \(z\in C_x\) satisfies \(z\le a(x)\). To see this, iterate \(F_x\) starting from \(z\). The iterates increase, remain in the box, and converge to a fixed point; uniqueness identifies that limit with \(a(x)\). Thus \(a(x)\) is the greatest point of \(C_x\).

In particular, maximizing \(\sum_tz_t\) over \(C_x\) determines the terminal vector.

2.2. An explicit interior ball

Put

\(\displaystyle s=\min_t x_t>0,\qquad c=x/2,\qquad r=s/4.\)

The Euclidean ball of radius \(r\) about \(c\) lies in \([0,x]\). For every \(z\in[0,x]\), we have \(F_x(z)\ge x\ge z\), so

\[\displaystyle \operatorname{Ball}(c,r)\subseteq C_x.\tag{5}\]

Also, \(C_x\) lies in the Euclidean unit ball, because every nonnegative vector in \([0,\mu]\) has coordinate sum at most one.

Both bounding radii have polynomial-size binary descriptions. Their construction uses only the positive seed masses.

2.3. A weak separation oracle with certified rational cuts

Let

\(\displaystyle h_u(z)=z_u-F_x(z)_u.\)

Given a rational query \(z\) and a rational tolerance \(0<\omega<1\), first separate exactly if \(z\) lies outside \([0,\mu]\). Otherwise set

\(\displaystyle \xi=\omega s/24.\)

Compute each \(h_u(z)\) within additive error \(\xi\).

If an approximation exceeds \(2\xi\), its true value exceeds \(\xi\). Compute a rational approximation \(\widetilde g\) to \(\nabla h_u(z)\) with coordinatewise error at most \(\xi/(2\tau)\). For every \(w\in C_x\), convexity gives

\(\displaystyle \nabla h_u(z)\cdot(w-z)\le-h_u(z)<-\xi.\)

Since \(w,z\in[0,\mu]\subseteq[0,1]^\tau\), replacing the gradient by \(\widetilde g\) changes this dot product by at most \(\xi/2\). Hence the rational halfspace

\(\displaystyle \widetilde g\cdot(w-z)\le0\)

contains \(C_x\). Its normal is nonzero, since the strict inequality holds at the feasible point \(c\).

If no constraint triggers such a cut, all true residuals satisfy \(h_u(z)\le3\xi\). At the known interior point,

\(\displaystyle h_u(c)\le-x_u/2\le-s/2.\)

Set \(t=6\xi/s=\omega/4\). Convexity then gives

\(\displaystyle h_u((1-t)z+tc) \le(1-t)3\xi-t\,s/2\le0.\)

Thus \((1-t)z+tc\in C_x\). Because \(\|z-c\|_2\le2\),

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{dist}(z,C_x)\le2t=\omega/2.\)

This is a valid weak separation oracle: it returns either a certified rational separating halfspace or a certificate of the requested distance bound.

Its computations take polynomial bit time. Explicitly,

\(\displaystyle \partial_{z_t}h_u(z) =\mathbf1_{\{t=u\}} -(\mu_u-x_u)\beta_{tu}\exp(-(Bz)_u).\)

At queried points inside the box, all exponential arguments are nonpositive. To approximate \(e^{-q}\) to \(p\) binary digits, with rational \(q\ge0\), one may return zero when \(q\ge p+1\); otherwise range reduction and a Taylor expansion use polynomially many bit operations. Multiplication by the binary-encoded rates requires only polynomially many additional precision bits. No positive saturation gap enters these computations.

2.4. Weak optimization gives coordinate accuracy

We use the standard bit-model weak-separation-to-weak-optimization theorem for convex bodies. Given known inner and outer radii and a polynomial-time weak separation oracle, it returns a rational point within distance \(\omega\) of the body whose linear objective is within \(\omega\) of every point having an \(\omega\)-ball inside the body. Its running time is polynomial in the dimension, the relevant encoding lengths, and \(\log(1/\omega)\). This is a standard consequence of the ellipsoid method of [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf).

Here all hypotheses have been established: compactness and convexity follow from (4), the radii from (5), and weak separation from the preceding construction.

For desired coordinate accuracy \(0<d<1\), use

\(\displaystyle \omega=\frac{ds}{100\tau}\)

and maximize the normalized linear objective \(\ell(z)=\sum_tz_t/\tau\).

Write \(a=a(x)\). The point

\(\displaystyle y=(1-\theta)a+\theta c,\qquad \theta=\omega/r,\)

has an \(\omega\)-ball inside \(C_x\): this follows by taking the same convex combination of \(a\) with the ball in (5). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle \sum_ty_t\ge\sum_ta_t-\theta,\)

because both coordinate sums lie between zero and one.

The weak optimizer returns a rational \(z\) such that

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{dist}(z,C_x)\le\omega,\qquad \sum_tz_t\ge\sum_ta_t-\theta-\tau\omega.\)

Choose \(k\in C_x\) with \(\|z-k\|_2\le\omega\). Since \(k\le a\),

\(\displaystyle \|a-k\|_1 =\sum_ta_t-\sum_tk_t \le\theta+2\tau\omega.\)

It follows that

\[\displaystyle \|a-z\|_1 \le\theta+3\tau\omega =\frac{4\omega}{s}+3\tau\omega<d.\tag{6}\]

Thus terminal activation can be approximated to absolute accuracy \(d\) in polynomial time in \(\log(1/d)\).

2.5. Fractional powers require no inverse dependence on the exponent

Clip each coordinate of \(z\) to \([x_t,\mu_t]\), obtaining \(v\). Since \(a_t\) belongs to that interval, clipping does not increase the error in (6).

Every community utility computed from \(v\), or from \(a\), is at least \(s\): every community contains a type, and \(\mu_c\le1\). On this range,

\(\displaystyle \alpha q^{\alpha-1}\le1/s,\qquad s\le q\le1.\)

Consequently,

\[\displaystyle \left| W_\alpha(x) -\sum_c\mu_c\left(\frac{\sum_{t:g(t)=c}v_t}{\mu_c}\right)^\alpha \right| \le\frac{\|a-v\|_1}{s}.\tag{7}\]

Take \(d=\nu s/2\). The right side is at most \(\nu/2\).

The remaining rational powers can be evaluated with total additive error at most \(\nu/2\), using \(q^\alpha=\exp(\alpha\log q)\). Since \(q\ge s\), the necessary computation is polynomial in the encoding lengths and \(\log(1/\nu)\). In particular, \(|\log q|\le\log(1/s)\), so no running-time factor \(1/\alpha\) is needed.

This proves Lemma 2.

3. A polynomial-size lattice captures nearly all optimal welfare

Put

\(\displaystyle \eta=\epsilon/4,\qquad \delta=\frac{\eta^2\kappa}{\tau}.\)

Define a positive rational baseline allocation by

\(\displaystyle b_t= \begin{cases} \mu_t,&\mu_t<\delta/\eta,\\ \delta,&\mu_t\ge\delta/\eta. \end{cases}\)

Every coordinate satisfies \(b_t\le\delta/\eta\). Therefore, writing \(B_0=\sum_tb_t\),

\[\displaystyle B_0\le\tau\delta/\eta=\eta\kappa.\tag{8}\]

Set

\(\displaystyle K=\left\lfloor\frac{\kappa-B_0}{\delta}\right\rfloor,\qquad D_t=\min\left\{K,\left\lfloor\frac{\mu_t-b_t}{\delta}\right\rfloor\right\}.\)

Here \(K\ge1\), and

\[\displaystyle K\le\frac{\tau}{\eta^2}=\frac{16\tau}{\epsilon^2}.\tag{9}\]

For each type, create \(D_t\) distinct elements, each representing an additional seed mass \(\delta\) in that type. There are at most \(\tau K\) elements.

For a set \(S\) of these elements, let \(n_t(S)\) count its elements of type \(t\), and define

\(\displaystyle f(S)=W_\alpha(b+\delta n(S)).\)

This function is defined on all subsets: the capacities ensure that every resulting vector stays in \([0,\mu]\). Lemma 1 implies that \(f\) is increasing and submodular. Every set with at most \(K\) elements produces a feasible allocation.

We next compare its best feasible value, denoted \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{lat}}\), with the original supremum \(\mathrm{OPT}\).

Fix any original feasible allocation \(x'\), and put \(\lambda=1-\eta\). Define a lattice allocation \(q\) as follows:

  • If \(\mu_t<\delta/\eta\), set \(q_t=b_t=\mu_t\).
  • Otherwise, set \(q_t=\delta\max\{1,\lceil\lambda x'_t/\delta\rceil\}\).

In the second case,

\(\displaystyle q_t\le\lambda\mu_t+\delta\le\mu_t,\)

because \(\delta\le\eta\mu_t\). In both cases \(q_t\ge\lambda x'_t\).

For a type in the second case, its additional mass satisfies

\(\displaystyle q_t-b_t =\delta\left(\max\{1,\lceil\lambda x'_t/\delta\rceil\}-1\right) \le\lambda x'_t.\)

Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_t(q_t-b_t)\le\lambda\kappa\le\kappa-B_0,\)

where the final inequality follows from (8). The additional masses are integer multiples of \(\delta\), so their total number is at most \(K\). This also proves that the individual truncated capacities \(D_t\) suffice.

Monotonicity and (3) now give

\(\displaystyle W_\alpha(q)\ge W_\alpha(\lambda x') \ge(1-\eta)W_\alpha(x').\)

Taking the supremum over all \(x'\) proves

\[\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{lat}}\ge(1-\eta)\mathrm{OPT}.\tag{10}\]

This argument does not assume that the original supremum is attained.

4. Greedy optimization with numerical errors

Run cardinality-constrained greedy on the constructed ground set, starting from the empty set. Evaluate every queried welfare using Lemma 2 with accuracy

\[\displaystyle \nu=\frac{\epsilon\kappa}{16K}.\tag{11}\]

If fewer than \(K\) elements exist, select all of them; monotonicity makes this optimal on the lattice. Otherwise perform \(K\) greedy selections.

At each step, select an available element with the largest estimated marginal gain. Each estimated marginal has error at most \(2\nu\), so the selected element has true marginal at least the largest true marginal minus \(4\nu\).

Let \(S_j\) be the greedy set after \(j\) steps, and let \(O\) be an optimal lattice set. Submodularity gives

\(\displaystyle f(O)-f(S_j) \le\sum_{e\in O\setminus S_j} \bigl(f(S_j\cup\{e\})-f(S_j)\bigr).\)

There are at most \(K\) terms. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle f(S_{j+1})-f(S_j) \ge\frac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{lat}}-f(S_j)}{K}-4\nu.\)

Iterating this inequality, using \(f(\varnothing)\ge0\) and \((1-1/K)^K\le e^{-1}\), yields

\[\displaystyle f(S_K)\ge(1-1/e)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{lat}}-4K\nu.\tag{12}\]

For a lower bound on the original optimum, consider the feasible allocation \(x_t=\kappa\mu_t\). Since terminal activation is at least seed mass, every community has utility at least \(\kappa\). Therefore

\[\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}\ge\kappa^\alpha\ge\kappa.\tag{13}\]

Combining (10)–(13), the returned allocation \(x=b+\delta n(S_K)\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle W_\alpha(x) \ge(1-1/e)(1-\eta)\mathrm{OPT} -\frac{\epsilon\kappa}{4} \ge(1-1/e-\epsilon/2)\mathrm{OPT} \ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Its feasibility follows directly from the type capacities and \(B_0+K\delta\le\kappa\).

5. Bit complexity and the nonsaturation promise

The algorithm makes polynomially many oracle calls: the ground set has at most \(\tau K\) elements, and \(K=O(\tau/\epsilon^2)\). Identical copies of a type can also be handled together.

All queried allocations are rational and have polynomial encoding length. Every queried coordinate is at least its positive baseline, and

\(\displaystyle \min_tb_t\ge\min\{\min_t\mu_t,\delta\}.\)

Thus the logarithm of the reciprocal of the smallest queried seed mass is polynomially bounded by \(L+\log\tau+\log(1/\epsilon)\). Equation (11) likewise gives a polynomial bound on \(\log(1/\nu)\). Lemma 2 therefore makes every oracle call polynomial-time in the standard bit model. The same holds for all allocation arithmetic and the final rational output.

For completeness, the given qualitative promise has a simple characterization for finite rates:

\(\displaystyle \sup_{x\text{ feasible}}\max_cu_c(x)<1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \kappa<\min_c\mu_c.\)

Necessity follows because a community of mass at most \(\kappa\) can be seeded entirely. For sufficiency, let \(M=\max_u\sum_t\beta_{tu}\mu_t<\infty\). The terminal fixed-point identity gives, for every feasible \(x\),

\(\displaystyle 1-u_c(x) =\frac{\sum_{u:g(u)=c}(\mu_u-x_u)e^{-(Ba(x))_u}}{\mu_c} \ge e^{-M}\frac{\mu_c-\kappa}{\mu_c}>0.\)

This is a uniform positive gap, but its magnitude need not be useful computationally. The algorithm above instead uses its explicitly constructed positive baseline to control numerical accuracy.

The cited [FIMM theorem](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/d242dafdb2c5407ae420bc54c9325fdf-Paper-Conference.pdf) concerns finite independent-cascade networks. The argument here establishes the guarantee directly for the specified mass recurrence, with running time depending only on the support and input parameters.
