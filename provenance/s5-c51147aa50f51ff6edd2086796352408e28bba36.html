The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite worker and employer type sets with rational masses, strict rankings, and schedule quotas, can one find a worker-optimal stable allocation? Represent each type’s population by a distribution over feasible integral schedules; require matching contract masses to balance and rule out any contract category that a positive mass of both endpoint types would accept through schedule changes under their choice maps.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(W,F,D\) be finite worker-type, employer-type, and day sets, and let \(X\subseteq W\times F\times D\) be the explicitly listed contract categories, with \(m=|X|\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau=|W|+|F|\) types with positive mass \(a_t\), each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Masses need not sum to one, and the two sides may have different total masses.
For each type \(t\), input an acceptable set \(Y_t\) of incident categories, a strict ranking of \(Y_t\), a nonnegative integer total quota \(q_t\), and pairwise disjoint groups \(G_{t,j}\subseteq Y_t\) with nonnegative integer quotas \(q_{t,j}\), all explicitly encoded. An integral schedule is a subset \(S\subseteq Y_t\) satisfying \(|S|\leq q_t\) and \(|S\cap G_{t,j}|\leq q_{t,j}\); each category occurs at most once. Define \(C_t(A)\) by scanning \(A\cap Y_t\) in ranking order and retaining each category precisely when doing so preserves all quotas.
An allocation gives each type a probability distribution \(p_t\) over its feasible integral schedules. For each \(x=(w,f,d)\), require \(a_w\sum_{S\ni x}p_w(S)=a_f\sum_{T\ni x}p_f(T)\). Stability means that no category \(x=(w,f,d)\) satisfies both \(\sum_{S:\,x\notin S,\,x\in C_w(S\cup\{x\})}p_w(S)>0\) and \(\sum_{T:\,x\notin T,\,x\in C_f(T\cup\{x\})}p_f(T)>0\); adding a category may discard existing contracts through the choice map.
For worker schedules define \(S\succeq_w T\) by \(C_w(S\cup T)=S\). A distribution \(p_w\) dominates \(p'_w\) if they admit a coupling supported on pairs \((S,T)\) with \(S\succeq_w T\). A stable allocation is worker-optimal if its distribution dominates that of every stable allocation for every worker type.
Let \(L\) be the total input bit length and \(r\) the number of quota groups. Allocations are represented by lists of schedules with nonzero masses.
Does every instance admit a worker-optimal stable allocation? Is finding an exact rational worker-optimal stable allocation, or determining that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,r,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?
The discrete existence theorem guarantees a stable contract set for revealing choice maps; its finite construction finds a worker-optimal set, and the disjoint-group quota maps above are revealing ([Komornik, Komornik, and Viauroux](https://economics.umbc.edu/files/2014/09/wp_10_120.pdf)).
Continuous quantities assigned to individual pairs admit stable and side-optimal schedules under continuous revealing choice functions ([Alkan and Gale](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/1117/1/3011800000834.pdf)). Ex ante stable fractional allocations also exist under matroid-responsive preferences ([Imamura and Kawase](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18574v1)); these results use different allocation and stability definitions, and do not settle the distribution-over-schedules problem stated here.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Undefined schedule quotas were instantiated as a nonnegative integer total quota plus pairwise disjoint quota groups, using the construction that guarantees the anchor's revealing-choice hypothesis.
  • Undefined contract categories were taken to be explicitly listed worker-type–employer-type–day triples; integral schedules are subsets, so repeated copies of a category are excluded.
  • Masses were taken as arbitrary positive binary rationals without normalization or equality of the two sides' total masses; each type's schedule distribution is normalized to one.
  • Acceptance through schedule changes was defined by adding an absent category and applying the choice map, including any resulting cancellation of existing contracts.
  • Undefined worker optimality was extended from the choice-based schedule order to distributions through dominating couplings, requiring domination of every stable allocation rather than mere maximality.
  • The input size parameters and allocation representation were fixed explicitly, and the computational question asks for an exact rational allocation or a determination of nonexistence.

Worker-optimal stable allocations do not always exist. I give a complete counterexample below.

The new computational result is an exact polynomial-time criterion for single-worker star markets with an arbitrary total quota and no quota groups. The proof also identifies a structural obstruction: in these markets, a worker-optimal distribution must have pairwise comparable schedules in its support.

The polynomial-time versus NP-hardness question for general markets remains open in this write-up.

1. A strict utility consequence of dominance

Let a type’s acceptable categories, in ranking order, be $x_1,\ldots,x_n$. For a feasible schedule $S$, define
$$ U(S)=\sum_{i:x_i\in S}2^{n-i}. $$

Lemma 1. For the quota choice maps in the question, if $C(S\cup T)=S$, then $U(S)\geq U(T)$, with equality only when $S=T$.

Proof. Suppose $S\neq T$, and let $x_k$ be the highest-ranked category in their symmetric difference. All higher-ranked categories offered in $S\cup T$ belong to both schedules. Their set $H$ is feasible, and the greedy scan retains all of them.

If $x_k\in T\setminus S$, then $H\cup\{x_k\}$ is a subset of the feasible schedule $T$. It is therefore feasible. The scan would retain $x_k$, contradicting $C(S\cup T)=S$.

Thus $x_k\in S\setminus T$. Consequently,
$$ U(S)-U(T) \geq 2^{n-k}-\sum_{i=k+1}^n2^{n-i} =1. $$
This proves the claim. $\square$

Corollary 2. If a distribution $p$ dominates a distribution $p'$, then
$$ \mathbb E_p[U]\geq\mathbb E_{p'}[U]. $$
If equality holds, then $p=p'$.

Proof. In a witnessing coupling, every utility difference is nonnegative, and it is strictly positive on every unequal pair. Equality of expectations therefore forces the coupling to assign probability one to identical pairs. $\square$

2. Counterexample to universal existence

Take one worker type $w$, four employer types $f_1,\ldots,f_4$, and one day. Let $x_i$ be the category joining $w$ to $f_i$.

The worker has mass $1$, accepts all four categories, ranks them
$$ x_1\succ x_2\succ x_3\succ x_4, $$
and has quota $2$. Each employer has mass $\tfrac12$, accepts only its own category, and has quota $1$. There are no quota groups.

Consider two allocations whose employer distributions always assign their sole category:

| Allocation | Worker distribution |
|---|---|
| $P$ | $\{x_1,x_2\}$ and $\{x_3,x_4\}$, each with probability $\tfrac12$ |
| $Q$ | $\{x_1,x_3\}$ and $\{x_2,x_4\}$, each with probability $\tfrac12$ |

Every category has worker inclusion probability $\tfrac12$, so both allocations satisfy balance. Both are stable: every employer always holds its category, and hence its willingness probability is zero.

For any feasible allocation $R$, write $z_i=\Pr_R(x_i\in S_w)$. Balance gives
$$ z_i=\frac12\Pr_R(x_i\in S_{f_i})\leq\frac12. $$
Therefore,
$$ \mathbb E_R[U] = 8z_1+4z_2+2z_3+z_4 \leq\frac{15}{2}. $$
Both $P_w$ and $Q_w$ attain this bound.

If $R_w$ dominated $P_w$, Corollary 2 would force $R_w=P_w$. If it also dominated $Q_w$, the same argument would force $R_w=Q_w$. These distributions are different.

Thus no feasible allocation dominates both stable allocations. In particular, this instance has no worker-optimal stable allocation.

3. An exact criterion for star markets

Consider the following subclass:

  • There is one worker type, of mass $A>0$.
  • There are $n$ distinct employer types, with masses $b_1,\ldots,b_n>0$.
  • Category $x_i$ joins the worker to employer $i$.
  • Employer $i$ accepts only $x_i$ and has quota $1$.
  • The worker accepts all categories, ranks them $x_1\succ\cdots\succ x_n$, and has total quota $q$.
  • There are no quota groups.

Define
$$ c_i=\min\left\{1,\frac{b_i}{A}\right\}. $$
For a worker distribution $p$, let
$$ z_i=\Pr_p(x_i\in S). $$

Balance uniquely determines each employer’s distribution:
$$ \Pr(x_i\in T_i)=\frac{A z_i}{b_i}. $$
Consequently, a worker distribution extends to a feasible allocation exactly when
$$ z_i\leq c_i \qquad\text{for every }i. $$
Denote the set of these feasible worker distributions by $\mathcal K$.

Compute a vector $z^*$ by filling the worker’s quota in ranking order:
$$ z_i^* = \min\left\{ c_i,\, \max\left\{0,q-\sum_{j<i}z_j^*\right\} \right\}. $$
Define
$$ H=\{x_i:z_i^*=1\}, \qquad I=\{x_i:0<z_i^*<1\}, \qquad h=|I|, \qquad Q=q-|H|. $$
When $h\geq2$, list the fractional categories in ranking order as
$$ f_1\succ f_2\succ\cdots\succ f_h. $$

Theorem 3. A star market admits a worker-optimal stable allocation if and only if one of the following holds:

  • $h\leq1$.
  • $h\geq2$, $Q=1$, and every category in $H$ is ranked above $f_2$.

When $H$ is empty, the ranking condition in the second case is vacuous.

In the affirmative cases, an exact worker-optimal distribution is:

  • If $h=0$, assign probability $1$ to $H$.
  • Otherwise, assign

$$ p(H\cup\{x_i\})=z_i^* \qquad(x_i\in I), $$
and
$$ p(H)=1-\sum_{x_i\in I}z_i^*. $$

Zero-probability schedules are omitted. Employer distributions are then obtained from balance.

The remaining sections prove the theorem.

4. Stability can be eliminated from the star comparison problem

For this section, $C(B)$ selects the highest-ranked $\min\{q,|B|\}$ categories of $B$.

4.1 The schedule order and distribution order are transitive

The uniform-quota choice map satisfies
$$ C(B\cup E)=C(C(B)\cup E). $$
Indeed, an element discarded from $B$ already has at least $q$ higher-ranked elements in $B$. Adding $E$ cannot make that discarded element enter the highest-ranked $q$ elements.

It follows that the schedule relation is transitive. If $S\succeq T$ and $T\succeq R$, then
$$ C(S\cup T\cup R) = C(S\cup C(T\cup R)) = C(S\cup T) = S, $$
while also
$$ C(S\cup T\cup R) = C(C(S\cup T)\cup R) = C(S\cup R). $$
Hence $S\succeq R$. Antisymmetry follows from Lemma 1.

Distribution dominance is transitive as well. To verify this explicitly, suppose couplings $\gamma(S,T)$ and $\eta(T,R)$ witness two successive dominances, and their common intermediate marginal is $p'(T)$. The formula
$$ \kappa(S,R) = \sum_{T:p'(T)>0} \frac{\gamma(S,T)\eta(T,R)}{p'(T)} $$
defines a coupling of the outer distributions. Every positive term has $S\succeq T\succeq R$, so $\kappa$ witnesses the desired dominance.

4.2 Every feasible distribution has a stable dominator

Lemma 4. For every $p_0\in\mathcal K$, there is a stable allocation whose worker distribution dominates $p_0$.

Proof. There are finitely many feasible schedules. The set of distributions in $\mathcal K$ dominating $p_0$ is nonempty, since it contains $p_0$.

This set is compact: dominance can be expressed by nonnegative coupling variables with fixed second marginal, and all relevant probability variables lie in finite-dimensional bounded simplexes. Maximize $\mathbb E[U]$ over this compact set, and call a maximizer $p$.

Suppose its allocation were unstable at $x_i$. Employer willingness is positive exactly when
$$ z_i<\frac{b_i}{A}. $$
Positive worker willingness supplies a positive-probability schedule $S$ omitting $x_i$ for which
$$ R=C(S\cup\{x_i\}) $$
contains $x_i$.

Because $S$ omits $x_i$ with positive probability, $z_i<1$. Together with employer willingness, this gives $z_i<c_i$.

Move a sufficiently small probability $\varepsilon>0$ from $S$ to $R$. The inclusion probability of $x_i$ increases by $\varepsilon$; every other inclusion probability either stays unchanged or decreases. Choosing
$$ \varepsilon\leq\min\{p(S),c_i-z_i\} $$
preserves feasibility.

Moreover, $R\succeq S$ and $R\neq S$. Thus the modified distribution dominates $p$, and by transitivity it dominates $p_0$. Lemma 1 shows that its expected utility is strictly larger, contradicting maximality.

The allocation associated with $p$ is therefore stable. $\square$

Proposition 5. In a star market, a worker-optimal stable distribution exists exactly when $\mathcal K$ has a greatest element under distribution dominance.

Proof. If $p$ is worker-optimal and $p_0\in\mathcal K$, Lemma 4 gives a stable distribution $r$ dominating $p_0$. Worker-optimality gives $p\succeq r$, and transitivity gives $p\succeq p_0$.

Conversely, suppose $p$ is greatest in $\mathcal K$. If it were unstable, the modification in Lemma 4 would produce a feasible distribution $r$ with strictly larger expected utility. Greatestness would imply that $p$ dominates $r$, contradicting Corollary 2. Thus $p$ is stable and dominates every stable distribution. $\square$

4.3 Incomparable support schedules prevent greatestness

Lemma 6. A greatest element of $\mathcal K$ must have pairwise comparable schedules in its positive-probability support.

Proof. Suppose $p(S)>0$ and $p(T)>0$, but $S$ and $T$ are incomparable. Put
$$ J=C(S\cup T). $$
The choice identity above shows that $J\succeq S$ and $J\succeq T$. Incomparability implies $J\neq S,T$.

For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, form a new distribution by removing probability $\varepsilon$ from each of $S,T$ and adding probability $\varepsilon$ to each of $J,\varnothing$. This preserves total probability.

For every category, its new marginal is at most its old marginal, because $J\subseteq S\cup T$. Hence the new distribution belongs to $\mathcal K$.

Consider the upward set
$$ \mathcal U_J=\{B:B\succeq J\}. $$
Neither $S$ nor $T$ belongs to this set: otherwise antisymmetry would identify it with $J$. The empty schedule also does not belong, while $J$ does. The modification therefore increases $\Pr(\mathcal U_J)$ by $\varepsilon$.

Dominance cannot decrease the probability of an upward set. Indeed, in a dominance coupling, if the second schedule dominates $J$, transitivity says the first schedule also dominates $J$.

Thus $p$ cannot dominate the modified distribution, contradicting greatestness. $\square$

5. The unique maximizing marginal vector

Lemma 7. The possible marginal vectors of distributions over uniform-quota schedules are exactly
$$ \left\{ z\in[0,1]^n:\sum_i z_i\leq q \right\}. $$

Proof. Necessity follows by taking expectations of schedule feasibility.

For sufficiency, take a vector in the displayed polytope. If it has at least two fractional coordinates, perturb one upward and another downward, preserving their sum, until a coordinate reaches $0$ or $1$. Perturbing in both directions expresses the original vector as a convex combination of two vectors with fewer fractional coordinates.

If exactly one fractional coordinate remains, let $d$ be the number of coordinates equal to $1$. Feasibility gives $d+z_i\leq q$. Since $q,d$ are integers and $z_i>0$, we have $d+1\leq q$. The remaining coordinate can therefore be expressed as a mixture of $0$ and $1$, preserving feasibility.

Induction expresses the vector as a convex combination of feasible incidence vectors. $\square$

Therefore the marginal optimization problem over $\mathcal K$ is
$$ \max\left\{ \sum_i2^{n-i}z_i: 0\leq z_i\leq c_i,\ \sum_i z_i\leq q \right\}. $$

Lemma 8. This problem has the unique solution $z^*$ defined in Theorem 3.

Proof. Let $z\neq z^*$ be feasible, and take the first index $i$ at which they differ. Because the previous coordinates agree, the individual bound and total quota imply $z_i<z_i^*$.

If $\sum_jz_j<q$, increasing $z_i$ slightly improves the objective. If $\sum_jz_j=q$, the smaller prefix at $i$ implies that some later coordinate $z_j$ exceeds $z_j^*$. Moving a sufficiently small amount from that coordinate to coordinate $i$ preserves feasibility and strictly improves the objective, since its weight is smaller.

Thus every vector other than $z^*$ fails to maximize. Lemma 7 establishes that $z^*$ is realizable by a distribution. $\square$

By Proposition 5 and Corollary 2, any worker-optimal distribution must have marginals $z^*$. Furthermore:

Consequence. If two distinct distributions realize $z^*$, no worker-optimal distribution exists.

Indeed, both have maximum feasible expected utility. A greatest distribution would have to dominate both with equal expected utility, and Corollary 2 would identify it with both.

6. Necessity of the star criterion

Any distribution with marginals $z^*$ always includes $H$, never includes a zero-marginal category, and chooses a subset of $I$ of size at most $Q$.

Write $v_i=z_i^*$ for the fractional coordinates. Each satisfies $0<v_i<1$.

6.1 At least two fractional categories with $Q\geq2$

First suppose
$$ \sum_{i\in I}v_i<Q. $$
Then $v$ lies in the interior of
$$ P_Q=\left\{u\in[0,1]^h:\sum_i u_i\leq Q\right\}. $$

There is a representation of $v$ assigning positive probability to every feasible incidence vector. To see this, let $b$ be their average. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$,
$$ y=\frac{v-\varepsilon b}{1-\varepsilon} $$
still belongs to $P_Q$. Lemma 7 decomposes $y$ into feasible incidence vectors, and
$$ v=\varepsilon b+(1-\varepsilon)y $$
gives the required positive representation.

Because $h,Q\geq2$, the feasible incidence vectors include
$$ \varnothing,\quad\{a\},\quad\{b\},\quad\{a,b\} $$
for two distinct fractional categories $a,b$. Their incidence vectors satisfy
$$ \mathbf 1_\varnothing+\mathbf 1_{\{a,b\}} = \mathbf 1_{\{a\}}+\mathbf 1_{\{b\}}. $$
A small probability transfer along this identity produces two distinct distributions with the same marginals. The preceding consequence rules out worker-optimality.

Now suppose $\sum_i v_i=Q$. Every supporting fractional subset must then have size exactly $Q$.

If
$$ 2\leq Q\leq h-2, $$
choose four distinct categories $a,b,c,d$ and a disjoint set $R$ of size $Q-2$. The four feasible bases satisfy
$$ \mathbf 1_{R\cup\{a,b\}}+\mathbf 1_{R\cup\{c,d\}} = \mathbf 1_{R\cup\{a,c\}}+\mathbf 1_{R\cup\{b,d\}}. $$
The same positive-representation argument applies in the affine hyperplane $\sum_i u_i=Q$, because every coordinate of $v$ lies strictly between $0$ and $1$. Perturbing the four probabilities yields distinct distributions with identical marginals. Again, worker-optimality is impossible.

The remaining case is $Q=h-1$. Here $h\geq3$, and the only possible schedules are
$$ S_i=H\cup(I\setminus\{f_i\}). $$
Each has forced probability $1-v_i>0$.

Let $\ell$ be the lowest-ranked category in $H\cup I$. If $\ell\in H$, any two distinct $S_i,S_j$ are incomparable: their union is $H\cup I$, and choice discards $\ell$, giving neither schedule.

If $\ell\in I$, choose distinct $i,j$ whose omitted categories differ from $\ell$, possible since $h\geq3$. Their union again has choice
$$ (H\cup I)\setminus\{\ell\}, $$
which differs from both schedules. They are incomparable.

Lemma 6 rules out worker-optimality. Thus, whenever $h\geq2$, existence requires $Q=1$.

6.2 The ranking requirement when $Q=1$

When $Q=1$, the distribution with marginals $z^*$ is unique:
$$ p(H\cup\{f_i\})=v_i, \qquad p(H)=1-\sum_i v_i. $$

Suppose some category in $H$ is ranked below $f_2$. The union of
$$ H\cup\{f_1\} \quad\text{and}\quad H\cup\{f_2\} $$
has $q+1$ categories. Its lowest-ranked category belongs to $H$, so choice discards that category and returns neither schedule.

Both schedules have positive probability and are incomparable. Lemma 6 therefore rules out worker-optimality.

This proves necessity of both conditions in Theorem 3.

7. Sufficiency of the star criterion

We prove that the specified distribution dominates every element of $\mathcal K$. Proposition 5 then gives stability and worker-optimality.

7.1 At most one fractional category

If $h=0$, the set $H$ is the highest-ranked $\min\{q,n\}$ categories. This follows from the greedy construction and the positivity of every $c_i$. Thus $H$ dominates every feasible schedule, and its deterministic distribution is greatest.

If $h=1$, write $I=\{f\}$ and $v=z_f^*$. The greedy vector cannot have total $q$: its total is the noninteger number $|H|+v$. Hence it leaves quota slack, so every category has positive marginal and
$$ Y=H\cup\{f\}, \qquad v=c_f. $$
The proposed distribution gives $Y$ probability $v$ and $H$ probability $1-v$.

For any feasible competing distribution, the probability of schedules containing $f$ is at most $c_f=v$. Couple those schedules to copies of $Y$. All remaining schedules are subsets of $H$ and can be coupled to the remaining copies of $Y$ and $H$. Inclusion implies schedule dominance, so this is a dominance coupling.

7.2 Several fractional categories and one remaining slot

Assume $h\geq2$, $Q=1$, and every category in $H$ is ranked above $f_2$. Put
$$ S_i=H\cup\{f_i\}. $$
The schedules form a dominance chain:
$$ S_1\succeq S_2\succeq\cdots\succeq S_h\succeq H. $$
Indeed, for $i<j$, the lowest-ranked category in $H\cup\{f_i,f_j\}$ is $f_j$, so its choice is $S_i$. Also, each $S_i$ contains $H$.

The positive coordinates of $z^*$ form an initial segment of the ranking. The ranking condition therefore implies that $S_1$ consists of the highest-ranked $q$ categories. It dominates every feasible schedule.

For $1\leq j<h$, the lowest-ranked category of $S_{j+1}$ is $f_{j+1}$. Consequently, for any feasible schedule $T$,
$$ S_{j+1}\not\succeq T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\cap\{f_1,\ldots,f_j\}\neq\varnothing. $$
All fractional coordinates before the last one are filled to their caps, so
$$ v_i=c_{f_i} \qquad(i<h). $$
For any competing distribution $\mu\in\mathcal K$, the union bound gives
$$ \Pr_\mu(S_{j+1}\not\succeq T) \leq \sum_{i=1}^j\Pr_\mu(f_i\in T) \leq \sum_{i=1}^j c_{f_i} = \sum_{i=1}^j p(S_i). $$

If $p(H)>0$, then $\sum_i v_i<1$, so the greedy vector has quota slack. Hence $Y=H\cup I$ and $v_i=c_{f_i}$ for every $i$. Since an underfilled schedule $H$ dominates exactly its subsets,
$$ \Pr_\mu(H\not\succeq T) = \Pr_\mu(T\not\subseteq H) \leq \sum_i c_{f_i} = 1-p(H). $$

These inequalities imply a dominance coupling. Here is the precise transportation argument.

Make the proposed support the left side of a bipartite graph and the support of $\mu$ the right side. Connect $S$ to $T$ exactly when $S\succeq T$. Because the left schedules form a descending chain, each right schedule has a nonempty initial segment of left neighbors.

For any collection $B$ of right schedules, its combined neighbor set is an initial segment. If that segment ends immediately before a left schedule $S$, every member of $B$ fails to be dominated by $S$. The inequalities above bound $\mu(B)$ by the probability of that initial segment. If the neighbor set is the whole left support, the bound is simply $\mu(B)\leq1$.

Thus every right subset satisfies the weighted Hall condition
$$ \mu(B)\leq p(N(B)). $$
This condition gives a transportation flow of value $1$: use left capacities $p(S)$, right capacities $\mu(T)$, and capacity $2$ on allowed edges. A cut of capacity below $1$ cannot cross an allowed edge and would yield a right subset violating the displayed inequality. The maximum-flow minimum-cut theorem therefore supplies the required coupling.

The proposed distribution dominates every feasible distribution. This completes the proof of Theorem 3. $\square$

8. Algorithmic consequence and remaining open question

For the star subclass, the algorithm is exact:

  • Compute $c_i$ and the greedy vector $z^*$.
  • Identify $H$, $I$, and $Q$.
  • Check the criterion in Theorem 3.
  • Return the displayed rational allocation, or report nonexistence.

The worker output uses at most $n+1$ schedules; each employer uses at most two. The algorithm performs polynomially many rational arithmetic operations, and all intermediate and output numbers have polynomial bit length. Thus it runs in polynomial time in the explicitly encoded input size.

This resolves a subclass with arbitrary rational masses, unequal side totals, and arbitrary worker total quota. It does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm or NP-hardness for general markets with multiple worker types and disjoint quota groups.
