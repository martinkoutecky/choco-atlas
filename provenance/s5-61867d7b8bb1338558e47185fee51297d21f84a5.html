Proposition 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite object set \(O\), rational per-capita capacities \(q_a\), and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies a strict ranking \(P_t\) and positive weight \(w_t\), determine whether a mass-popular assignment exists and return one if it does. An assignment is \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\), including \(a=\emptyset\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}\le q_a\) for each object. It is mass-popular if no feasible reassignment makes the total weight of the mass strictly preferring its new assignment greater than the total weight of the mass strictly preferring its old assignment.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(O\) be an object set with \(m=|O|\ge 3\), and put \(A=O\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Capacities \(q_a\ge 0\) are binary rationals; the outside option \(\emptyset\) has unlimited capacity.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type has a strict ranking \(P_t\) over \(A\) and a positive binary-rational weight \(w_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Mass is nonatomic and divisible. A feasible assignment has real entries \(x_{t,a}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,a}\le q_a\) for every \(a\in O\). Objects below \(\emptyset\) may be assigned, as feasibility imposes no acceptability restriction.
A reassignment is a nonnegative real array \(z_{t,a,b}\), recording mass moving from \(a\) to \(b\), with \(\sum_b z_{t,a,b}=x_{t,a}\) and \(\sum_{t,a}z_{t,a,b}\le q_b\) for every \(b\in O\). All such movements, including movements within a type and to or from \(\emptyset\), are allowed.
The assignment is mass-popular if every reassignment satisfies \(\sum_t w_t\sum_{a,b:\,bP_ta}z_{t,a,b}\le\sum_t w_t\sum_{a,b:\,aP_tb}z_{t,a,b}\). Equal outcomes contribute nothing; equal vote totals do not defeat an assignment.

Is deciding whether a mass-popular assignment exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can an assignment also be computed in polynomial time whenever one exists?

For finite agents, weighted popular matchings exist for every preference and capacity instance exactly when weights, sorted decreasingly, satisfy \(w_i\ge\sum_{j>i}w_j\); otherwise some instance has none. [Afacan–Bó](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.01004).
Discrete weighted capacitated house allocation with strict preferences has a polynomial-time existence and construction algorithm. [Sng–Manlove](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25731/1/25731.pdf).
Popular random assignments exist and can be computed in polynomial time under a comparison using products of lottery probabilities, which differs from the unrestricted reassignment comparison above. No result settling the precise mass problem above was located. [Aziz–Brandt–Stojanovic](https://pub.dss.in.tum.de/brandt-research/assign.pdf).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified positive binary-rational weights and nonnegative binary-rational capacities to give a finite computational input.
  • Made rankings strict over objects together with the outside option, whose capacity is unlimited.
  • Interpreted masses as nonatomic populations receiving individual objects; specified arbitrary old-to-new mass movements because assignment marginals alone do not identify which individuals improve.
  • Allowed real assignment and reassignment masses without an indivisibility restriction.
  • Retained the discrete feasibility definition's allowance of unacceptable assignments; imposed no additional acceptability constraint.
  • Used the published condition of at least three objects. The finite-agent lower bound has no additional restriction on support size in a nonatomic society.

Result

Existence is decidable in \(\mathrm{P}\), and a mass-popular assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) whenever one exists.

The proof below gives a construction using a flow network with polynomially many vertices and edges. It does not expand rational masses into individual agents. A finite-agent characterization is used only to prove necessary structural conditions.

1. Preprocessing and an exact dual certificate

Write \(o=\emptyset\), and define the weighted vote for a movement by

\(c_t(a,b)=w_t\) if \(bP_ta\), \(c_t(a,b)=-w_t\) if \(aP_tb\), and \(c_t(a,a)=0\).

An assignment is therefore mass-popular exactly when every feasible reassignment has total score at most zero.

We may first replace each capacity by \(\min(q_a,1)\): assignments and reassignments both have total mass one, so this changes neither feasible set. Objects of capacity zero can be deleted.

Every mass-popular assignment assigns zero mass below the outside option. Otherwise, moving positive mass from such an object to \(o\) gives a strictly positive score. We can consequently truncate each preference list immediately after \(o\). This preserves popularity: for an assignment supported on the truncated lists, replacing any reassignment destination below \(o\) by \(o\) preserves feasibility and weakly increases its score.

Henceforth all real objects have positive capacity, and only objects preferred to \(o\) appear on a type’s truncated list.

Lemma 1: dual certificate. A feasible assignment \(x\) is mass-popular if and only if there are prices \(p_a\ge 0\) for real objects, with \(p_o=0\), satisfying:

  • If \(x_{t,a}>0\), then, for every outcome \(b\) on the truncated list,

\(p_b-p_a\ge c_t(a,b)\).

  • If \(p_a>0\), then \(\sum_t x_{t,a}=q_a\).

Proof. For fixed \(x\), maximize \(\sum_{t,a,b}c_t(a,b)z_{t,a,b}\) over feasible reassignments. Its dual has unrestricted variables \(\alpha_{t,a}\), nonnegative variables \(p_b\) for real objects, and \(p_o=0\). Its constraints and objective are

\(\alpha_{t,a}+p_b\ge c_t(a,b)\),

\(\displaystyle \min\left(\sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}\alpha_{t,a}+\sum_{b\in O}q_bp_b\right).\)

The identity reassignment is feasible and has score zero. The primal is bounded, so strong linear-programming duality applies.

The diagonal dual constraints give \(\alpha_{t,a}+p_a\ge 0\). Moreover, the dual objective equals

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}(\alpha_{t,a}+p_a)+\sum_{a\in O}p_a\left(q_a-\sum_t x_{t,a}\right).\)

Every summand is nonnegative. If \(x\) is popular, the optimal objective is zero. Thus \(x_{t,a}>0\) implies \(\alpha_{t,a}=-p_a\), yielding condition 1, and \(p_a>0\) implies condition 2.

Conversely, suppose these prices exist. For any feasible reassignment \(z\), condition 1 gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,a,b}c_t(a,b)z_{t,a,b}\le \sum_{t,a,b}(p_b-p_a)z_{t,a,b}\)

\(\displaystyle =\sum_{b\in O}p_b\left(\sum_{t,a}z_{t,a,b}-\sum_t x_{t,b}\right)\le 0.\)

The final inequality uses destination capacities and condition 2. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

In particular, if a real object \(a\) is assigned to type \(t\), comparison with the outside option gives

\(p_a\le w_t.\)

This certificate concerns exactly the unrestricted reassignment model in the question.

2. First and second choices, and necessary structure

Let the distinct weights be

\(v_1>v_2>\cdots>v_k>0,\)

and let \(r(t)\) satisfy \(w_t=v_{r(t)}\).

We define first choices in increasing order of weight-group index. For a real object \(a\), let

\(\displaystyle F_i(a)=\sum_{\substack{t:r(t)=i\\ f_t=a}}\mu_t,\qquad H_i(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{i}F_j(a),\qquad H_0(a)=0.\)

For every type in group \(i\), its first choice \(f_t\) is the most preferred outcome satisfying either \(a=o\), or \(H_{i-1}(a)<q_a\). All first choices in the same group are defined using the same preceding-group totals.

Put \(F(a)=H_k(a)\). If \(f_t\ne o\), define \(s_t\) as the most preferred outcome different from \(f_t\) satisfying either \(a=o\), or \(H_{r(t)}(a)<q_a\). The outside option ensures that \(s_t\) exists. Set \(s_t\) undefined when \(f_t=o\).

Every defined \(s_t\) is worse than \(f_t\): any real object preferred to \(f_t\) already satisfies \(H_{r(t)-1}(a)\ge q_a\).

For \(F(a)\ge q_a\), define

\(\displaystyle \ell(a)=\max\{i:F_i(a)>0\}.\)

Once \(H_i(a)\ge q_a\), no later group chooses \(a\) as its first choice. Consequently, if \(F(a)>q_a\), then

\(H_{\ell(a)-1}(a)<q_a<F(a)=H_{\ell(a)}(a).\)

We call an assignment well formed when it is feasible and satisfies the following conditions:

  • Type \(t\) receives only \(f_t\) and, when defined, \(s_t\).
  • Type \(t\) receives all its mass at \(f_t\) if \(f_t=o\), or if \(F(f_t)\le q_{f_t}\), or if \(F(f_t)>q_{f_t}\) and \(r(t)<\ell(f_t)\).
  • Every object with \(F(a)\ge q_a\) is full and receives mass only from types whose first choice is \(a\).

Lemma 2. Every mass-popular assignment is well formed.

The finite result needed here is Theorem 7 of [Sng–Manlove, *Popular Matchings in the Weighted Capacitated House Allocation Problem*](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25731/1/25731.pdf). With agent counts replacing masses in the definitions above, it says that every popular matching assigns agents to their first or second choices. An object whose first-choice count is at most its capacity receives all its first-choice agents. An oversubscribed object is full, receives only first-choice agents, and receives every group preceding its marginal group. These are necessary conditions; they are not asserted to be sufficient.

We must justify applying this finite result to potentially irrational assignments.

Proof of Lemma 2. Let \(x\) be mass-popular and let \(p\) be a certificate from Lemma 1. There exists a rational assignment \(x'\) that:

  • has exactly the same positive entries as \(x\);
  • has exactly the same tight object-capacity constraints as \(x\);
  • is also certified by \(p\).

Indeed, choose a positive rational \(\varepsilon\) smaller than every positive entry of \(x\) and every positive capacity slack. Impose the original rational feasibility constraints, together with:

  • zero entries wherever \(x\) is zero;
  • entries at least \(\varepsilon\) wherever \(x\) is positive;
  • equality at every capacity tight in \(x\);
  • load at most \(q_a-\varepsilon\) at every other capacity.

This is a nonempty bounded rational polyhedron, because it contains \(x\). It has a rational point \(x'\). Its support and tight capacities agree with those of \(x\), so \(p\) still satisfies both certificate conditions.

Choose an integer \(N\) clearing all denominators of \(\mu_t,q_a,x'_{t,a}\). Create \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each of weight \(w_t\), and give object \(a\) integer capacity \(Nq_a\). Give each agent its own private last-resort house representing \(o\). Construct a matching assigning exactly \(Nx'_{t,a}\) agents of type \(t\) to each real object \(a\), with the remaining agents assigned to their private last resorts.

This matching is popular. Any competing matching can first be completed using private last resorts without decreasing its vote score. Its movements, aggregated by type and initial and final outcomes and divided by \(N\), then constitute a reassignment of \(x'\). A positive finite vote score would contradict popularity of \(x'\).

The finite theorem’s hypotheses hold: preferences are strict, weights are positive, capacities are positive integers, and every agent has a private last resort. Our capacity preprocessing ensures \(Nq_a\le N\). Objects acceptable to no agent can simply be omitted from the finite instance.

The first- and second-choice definitions for real objects scale exactly by \(N\); private last resorts represent the outside option. The finite theorem therefore implies that \(x'\) is well formed.

Finally, well-formedness transfers to \(x\). Support restrictions transfer because their supports agree. A type wholly assigned to its first choice in \(x'\) has no other positive entry, so the same holds in \(x\). Full objects remain full because their tight-capacity patterns agree. \(\square\)

Cloning in this proof is purely existential. The algorithm below never constructs these agents.

3. Prices forced by oversubscription

Lemma 3. Let \(x\) be mass-popular, with certificate \(p\). Then:

  • If \(F(a)>q_a\), then \(p_a=v_{\ell(a)}\).
  • Every used second choice has price zero.
  • If \(F(a)<q_a\), then \(p_a=0\).

Proof. By Lemma 2, \(x\) is well formed.

Suppose \(F(a)>q_a\), and write \(v=v_{\ell(a)}\). All preceding-group first-choice mass is assigned to \(a\). Thus the marginal group receives exactly

\(q_a-H_{\ell(a)-1}(a)>0\)

mass at \(a\), and has exactly

\(F(a)-q_a>0\)

mass assigned elsewhere.

A marginal-group type assigned positive mass at \(a\) gives \(p_a\le v\), by comparison with \(o\). A marginal-group type assigned positive mass elsewhere must receive its second choice \(s_t\). Since \(a=f_tP_ts_t\), its certificate gives

\(p_a-p_{s_t}\ge v.\)

Nonnegative prices imply \(p_a\ge v\). Therefore \(p_a=v\), and every such used second choice has price zero.

Any type receiving its second choice must be in the marginal group of an oversubscribed first-choice object; all other types are forced to their first choices by well-formedness. Hence every used second choice has price zero.

Now suppose \(F(a)<q_a\). Its first-choice types are wholly assigned there, contributing exactly \(F(a)\). If no other type receives \(a\), it has slack capacity, so \(p_a=0\). Otherwise, another type receives \(a\) as a second choice, which also gives \(p_a=0\). \(\square\)

The only prices not fixed by this lemma are those of objects with \(F(a)=q_a\).

4. Computing canonical prices

We compute a price vector \(\pi\) entirely from the input.

First set

\(\pi_o=0,\qquad \pi_a=0\quad\text{when }F(a)<q_a.\)

Process the objects with \(F(a)\ge q_a\) in increasing order of \(\ell(a)\).

If \(F(a)>q_a\), set

\(\pi_a=v_{\ell(a)}.\)

If \(F(a)=q_a\), set

\(\displaystyle \pi_a=\min\left(\{w_t:f_t=a\}\ \cup\ \{\pi_b-w_t:f_t=a,\ bP_ta\}\right).\)

In the second set, \(b\) ranges over real objects on the truncated list. The first set is nonempty because \(F(a)=q_a>0\). If the computed value is negative, report nonexistence.

This recursion is well defined. If \(bP_tf_t\), then

\(H_{r(t)-1}(b)\ge q_b.\)

Thus \(F(b)\ge q_b\), and no group after \(r(t)-1\) chooses \(b\) first. Consequently,

\(\ell(b)\le r(t)-1<\ell(f_t).\)

Every price referenced by the recursion has therefore already been computed.

Lemma 4: domination. If a mass-popular assignment exists, this computation never produces a negative price. Moreover, every certificate \(p\) of every mass-popular assignment satisfies \(p_a\le\pi_a\) for all outcomes.

Proof. Lemma 3 gives equality for \(o\), for objects with \(F(a)<q_a\), and for objects with \(F(a)>q_a\).

Consider an object with \(F(a)=q_a\). Every type with \(f_t=a\) is wholly assigned to \(a\), so its certificate requires

\(p_a\le w_t,\qquad p_a\le p_b-w_t\quad\text{for every }bP_ta.\)

Inductively, all such \(b\) satisfy \(p_b\le\pi_b\). Taking the minimum of these bounds gives \(p_a\le\pi_a\). Since \(p_a\ge0\), the computed price cannot be negative. \(\square\)

Domination alone would not preserve a certificate if a source price increased. The next lemma addresses that issue.

Lemma 5: preservation of used outcomes. If \(x\) is mass-popular, then every pair with \(x_{t,a}>0\) satisfies

\(\pi_b-\pi_a\ge c_t(a,b)\)

for every outcome \(b\) on the truncated list.

Proof. Choose a certificate \(p\) for \(x\).

First suppose \(F(a)=q_a\). Well-formedness implies \(a=f_t\). The definition of \(\pi_a\) gives

\(\pi_a\le w_t,\qquad \pi_b-\pi_a\ge w_t\quad\text{whenever }bP_ta.\)

If \(aP_tb\), nonnegative prices give

\(\pi_b-\pi_a\ge-\pi_a\ge-w_t.\)

The diagonal inequality is immediate.

For every other used outcome \(a\), Lemma 3 gives \(\pi_a=p_a\): this covers the outside option, objects with \(F(a)<q_a\), and objects with \(F(a)>q_a\). Lemma 4 then yields

\(\pi_b-\pi_a\ge p_b-p_a\ge c_t(a,b).\)

This proves preservation. \(\square\)

5. The final flow problem

If the price computation succeeds, call a pair \((t,a)\) eligible when:

  • \(a\) is \(f_t\) or its defined second choice \(s_t\); and
  • \(\pi_b-\pi_a\ge c_t(a,b)\) for every outcome \(b\) on the truncated list.

There are at most \(2\tau\) eligible pairs.

Decide whether a well-formed assignment exists using only eligible pairs. This is a flow-feasibility problem:

  • Delete any eligible pair \((t,a)\) with real \(a\), \(F(a)\ge q_a\), and \(f_t\ne a\).
  • For every type forced to its first choice by well-formedness, delete its other pair.
  • Add an arc from a source to type \(t\), with lower and upper bounds both \(\mu_t\).
  • Add an arc from type \(t\) to each remaining eligible outcome \(a\), with bounds zero and \(\mu_t\).
  • Add an arc from each real object \(a\) to a sink:
  • bounds \(q_a,q_a\) if \(F(a)\ge q_a\);
  • bounds zero and \(q_a\) otherwise.
  • Add an outside-option arc to the sink with bounds zero and one, and a return arc from sink to source with bounds one and one.

Flow conservation makes the type-to-outcome flows exactly the desired assignment entries.

Soundness. Any feasible flow produces a feasible, well-formed assignment \(x\). Eligibility supplies the price inequalities of Lemma 1 with prices \(\pi\). Every positive-price object satisfies \(F(a)\ge q_a\), and the flow makes every such object full. Hence Lemma 1 certifies \(x\) as mass-popular.

Completeness. Suppose a mass-popular assignment \(x\) exists. Lemma 4 shows that price computation does not reject. Lemma 2 makes \(x\) well formed, and Lemma 5 makes every used pair eligible. Therefore \(x\) supplies a feasible flow in the constructed network.

Thus the algorithm reports existence exactly when a mass-popular assignment exists, and every assignment it returns is mass-popular.

6. Polynomial running time and output size

The first- and second-choice calculations use rational mass sums and comparisons over polynomially many types, objects, and weight groups.

Canonical-price computation also uses polynomially many exact arithmetic operations. Every computed price is an input weight minus a sum of input weights along a chain of strictly decreasing group indices. Such a chain has length at most \(k\le\tau\), so all price encodings have polynomial length in \(L\) and \(\tau\). Eligibility requires at most \(2\tau(m+1)\) price comparisons.

The flow network has \(O(\tau+m)\) vertices and edges. All its bounds are input masses, preprocessed capacities, zero, or one. Let \(D\) be the product of their denominators. Its binary encoding has length \(O(L)\). Multiplying bounds by \(D\) gives an integer network without creating additional vertices or edges.

The standard lower-bound feasibility reduction, followed by a polynomial-time max-flow algorithm whose number of iterations is independent of capacity magnitudes, therefore runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Dividing the resulting type-to-outcome flows by \(D\) gives a rational mass-popular assignment of polynomial encoding length.

This establishes both polynomial-time decision and polynomial-time construction.

7. Existence is not automatic

For a simple instance with no popular assignment, take one type of mass one and weight one, ranking

\(aP_tbP_tcP_to,\)

with each real-object capacity equal to \(1/4\).

Every feasible assignment leaves at least \(1/4\) at \(o\). If a real object has slack capacity, moving outside mass there defeats the assignment. Thus a popular assignment would have to assign \(1/4\) to each of the four outcomes.

But moving \(1/4\) along each movement

\(o\to c,\qquad c\to b,\qquad b\to a,\qquad a\to o\)

is feasible and gives improving vote mass \(3/4\) against worsening vote mass \(1/4\). Hence no mass-popular assignment exists in this instance.

6. Lemmas 4 and 5: canonical prices

The price recursion has no circular dependencies. If an object is preferred to a type’s first choice, its capacity was reached by preceding groups. Its final contributing group therefore precedes the group of that type, and hence precedes the final contributing group of the current first-choice object.

At an exactly filled first-choice object, all first-choice types are assigned there. Their certificate inequalities give precisely the upper bounds used in the recursion. Induction therefore proves \(p_a\le\pi_a\). Nonnegativity of \(p_a\) justifies rejection whenever a canonical price becomes negative.

Lemma 5 correctly handles the limitation of this domination statement:

  • At exactly filled first-choice objects, the canonical recursion directly supplies the inequalities toward preferred destinations. Its upper bound by the type’s weight, together with nonnegative destination prices, supplies the inequalities toward worse destinations.
  • At every other used outcome, the source price is unchanged. Increasing destination prices therefore preserves all certificate inequalities.

The outside option and diagonal comparisons are included.
