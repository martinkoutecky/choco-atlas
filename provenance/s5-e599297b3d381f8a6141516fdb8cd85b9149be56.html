The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many binary agent types \(a\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\), house categories \(h\) with rational per-capita supplies \(\nu_h\), binary utilities \(u(a,h)\), and a rational threshold \(\Gamma\), does there exist an envy-free assignment of mass \(x_{a,h}\) and nonnegative cell subsidies \(p_{a,h}\) with total cost \(\sum_{a,h}x_{a,h}p_{a,h}\leq\Gamma\)? Require \(\sum_h x_{a,h}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{a,h}\leq\nu_h\), and \(u(a,h)+p_{a,h}\geq u(a,h')+p_{a',h'}\) for every pair of used cells; each person still receives one indivisible house.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\geq1\) distinct binary utility types \(a\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_a\), where \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) house categories, with positive binary-rational per-capita supplies \(\nu_h\) satisfying \(\sum_h\nu_h\geq1\), utilities \(u(a,h)\in\{0,1\}\), and a positive binary-rational budget \(\Gamma\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The population is atomless. An assignment specifies real masses \(x_{a,h}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_hx_{a,h}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_ax_{a,h}\leq\nu_h\). Each person receives one indivisible house; types may be split among categories, and houses may remain unassigned.
Every person in a used cell \((a,h)\), meaning \(x_{a,h}>0\), receives the same real subsidy \(p_{a,h}\geq0\). Envy-freeness requires \(u(a,h)+p_{a,h}\geq u(a,h')+p_{a',h'}\) for every ordered pair of used cells \((a,h),(a',h')\), including cells of the same type. Unassigned houses are not compared.
Decide whether such an envy-free outcome exists with \(\sum_{a,h}x_{a,h}p_{a,h}\leq\Gamma\).

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely listed agents and individually listed houses, the binary-utility decision problem with a positive integer subsidy budget is NP-complete. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22216v1)
No prior result settling exactly the mass-assignment problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total population mass to one, so supplies and total subsidy are measured per capita.
  • Specified an atomless population and real assignment masses: splitting a type partitions its population while each person receives one indivisible house.
  • Required total supply to be at least total population mass, carrying over the discrete assumption that there are at least as many houses as agents; omitted zero-supply categories.
  • Required a positive rational budget, carrying over the anchor's positive-budget restriction while retaining the continuous question's rational encoding.
  • Specified real nonnegative subsidies, weak envy inequalities for all ordered pairs of positive-mass cells including same-type pairs, and no comparison with unassigned houses.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, hence \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The hardness holds even when all masses, supplies, and the budget are dyadic rationals.

The proof transfers the finite-agent hardness result through two lemmas. The essential point is that, after fixing subsidies, deleting used cells preserves envy-freeness.

1. Subsidies can be normalized to zero or one

Lemma 1. For any feasible envy-free assignment with binary utilities, there are subsidies in \(\{0,1\}\) on the same used cells that preserve envy-freeness and do not increase any subsidy.

Proof. Let \(V\) be the finite set of used cells. For \(v=(a,h)\) and \(w=(b,g)\), define
\(d_{vw}=u(a,g)-u(a,h)\in\{-1,0,1\}\).
Envy-freeness is precisely
\(p_v\ge p_w+d_{vw}\).

In particular, \(p_v\ge p_w-1\) for every ordered pair. Thus, writing \(c=\min_{v\in V}p_v\), we have
\(0\le p_v-c\le1\).
Define
\(q_v=\lfloor p_v-c\rfloor\in\{0,1\}\).

Because \(d_{vw}\) is an integer, the original envy inequality gives
\[ \lfloor p_v-c\rfloor \ge \lfloor p_w-c+d_{vw}\rfloor =\lfloor p_w-c\rfloor+d_{vw}. \]
Consequently, \(q_v\ge q_w+d_{vw}\), so the new subsidies are envy-free. Finally, \(0\le q_v\le p_v\), proving the cost assertion. \(\square\)

The same proof applies to a finite-agent allocation, with vertices representing agents and their assigned houses.

2. Integral rounding with fixed support

Lemma 2. Let \(A\) and \(H\) be finite sets, and let \(E\subseteq A\times H\) be a set of allowed cells. Suppose the demands \(k_a\) and capacities \(b_h\) are nonnegative integers. If real numbers \(y_{a,h}\ge0\), supported on \(E\), satisfy
\[ \sum_h y_{a,h}=k_a, \qquad \sum_a y_{a,h}\le b_h, \]
then, for any fixed linear cost coefficients, there is an integral feasible assignment supported on \(E\) with no larger cost.

Proof. Introduce a slack row indexed by \(\ast\), with
\(z_{\ast,h}=b_h-\sum_a y_{a,h}\), and write \(z_{a,h}=y_{a,h}\) for original rows. Every column now sums to \(b_h\), every original row sums to \(k_a\), and the slack row sums to the integer
\(\sum_h b_h-\sum_a k_a\).
Give slack entries cost zero.

Consider the bipartite graph whose edges are precisely the nonintegral entries of \(z\). Any vertex incident with such an edge has degree at least two: its incident entries sum to an integer, so exactly one nonintegral entry is impossible. Therefore, if any nonintegral entry exists, this graph contains an even cycle.

Perturb entries around this cycle by alternating additions and subtractions of a common amount. Either orientation preserves all row and column sums; choose an orientation whose linear cost change is nonpositive. Increase the perturbation until the first changed entry reaches its floor or ceiling. All entries remain nonnegative, at least one nonintegral entry becomes integral, and previously integral entries are untouched.

Repeating this procedure terminates because the number of nonintegral entries strictly decreases. Removing the slack row gives the required assignment. Cells outside \(E\) remain zero throughout. \(\square\)

3. Reduction from the finite-agent problem

We use the finite-agent result supplied in the question: with explicitly listed agents, individually listed houses, binary utilities, and a positive integer budget \(\gamma\), bounded-subsidy envy-free allocation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Its model assigns one distinct house to every agent, permits unused houses, and compares assigned houses only. [Dai–Li–Wu–Zhang, Theorem 3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22216v1#S3.SS2).

Let a source instance have \(n\ge1\) agents and \(M\ge n\) houses. Group agents with identical utility vectors into a set \(T\) of distinct types, and let \(k_a\) denote the number of agents of type \(a\). Thus \(\sum_{a\in T}k_a=n\).

Choose the least power of two \(N\) strictly exceeding \(n\), and put \(r=N-n\). Then \(1\le r\le n\) and \(N\le2n\).

Construction

Retain each source house as a separate category and add one category \(h_\ast\). Retain the types in \(T\) and add a new type \(b\).

Set the masses and supplies to
\[ \mu_a=\frac{k_a}{N}\quad(a\in T), \qquad \mu_b=\frac rN, \qquad \nu_h=\frac1N\quad(h\text{ a source house}), \qquad \nu_{h_\ast}=\frac rN. \]

Original types retain their source utilities on source houses and have \(u(a,h_\ast)=0\). The new type has \(u(b,h)=0\) on every source house and \(u(b,h_\ast)=1\). Set \(\Gamma=\gamma/N\).

All masses and supplies are positive, the masses sum to one, and total supply is
\((M+r)/N\ge(n+r)/N=1\).
The added type is distinct from every original type because only it values \(h_\ast\).

The construction has \(\tau=|T|+1\le n+1\) types and \(m=M+1\) categories. All numerical data are dyadic and have polynomial encoding length. Hence the reduction is polynomial-time.

Forward implication

Suppose the source instance has an envy-free allocation of total subsidy at most \(\gamma\). By Lemma 1, assume its subsidies belong to \(\{0,1\}\).

Replace each assigned source agent by mass \(1/N\) in the corresponding type–house cell, with that agent’s subsidy. Assign all mass \(r/N\) of type \(b\) to \(h_\ast\), with subsidy zero.

Comparisons among original cells are unchanged. An original person values the added bundle at zero, so cannot envy it. Type \(b\) receives utility one and values every original bundle at its subsidy, which is at most one. Thus all additional comparisons hold.

The total subsidy is the source total divided by \(N\), so it is at most \(\Gamma\).

Reverse implication: original types cannot use the added category

Suppose the constructed mass instance has an envy-free outcome within budget. Normalize its subsidies by Lemma 1.

We first prove that \(x_{a,h_\ast}=0\) for every original type \(a\).

Suppose instead that some original cell \((a,h_\ast)\) is used. If a cell \((b,g)\) on a source house were also used, the two envy inequalities would imply
\[ p_{a,h_\ast}\ge u(a,g)+p_{b,g}, \qquad p_{b,g}\ge1+p_{a,h_\ast}. \]
Combining them gives \(0\ge u(a,g)+1\), a contradiction.

Hence type \(b\) could receive no source house. Its entire mass \(r/N\) would then occupy \(h_\ast\), exhausting that category’s supply. This contradicts its positive use by the original type \(a\).

Therefore all original mass is assigned exclusively to source-house categories.

Reverse implication: rounding recovers a source allocation

For original types and source houses, define \(y_{a,h}=Nx_{a,h}\). These masses satisfy
\[ \sum_h y_{a,h}=k_a, \qquad \sum_a y_{a,h}\le1. \]

Let \(E\) be their used support, and let \(q_{a,h}\in\{0,1\}\) be the normalized subsidies. Their scaled cost satisfies
\[ \sum_{(a,h)\in E}y_{a,h}q_{a,h} \le N\Gamma=\gamma, \]
because this sum omits only nonnegative costs incurred by type \(b\).

Apply Lemma 2 with demands \(k_a\), unit house capacities, allowed cells \(E\), and costs \(q_{a,h}\). It gives an integral assignment \(\widehat y\) with cost at most \(\gamma\). Unit capacities imply \(\widehat y_{a,h}\in\{0,1\}\).

Assign the \(k_a\) source agents of type \(a\) bijectively to the houses selected in row \(a\), giving each the corresponding subsidy \(q_{a,h}\). Every agent receives one house, and no house is assigned twice.

Every comparison in this finite allocation is between cells belonging to \(E\), so it already held in the mass outcome. This includes comparisons between agents of the same type. The recovered source outcome is therefore envy-free and costs at most \(\gamma\).

Both implications are proved, establishing \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

It remains to show that real masses and subsidies do not require certificates of unbounded precision.

Suppose an arbitrary input admits a feasible outcome within budget. By Lemma 1, replace its subsidies by \(q_{a,h}\in\{0,1\}\) on its used support \(E\).

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and supplies, in their binary rational encoding. Its bit length is polynomial in \(L\). Scaling by \(Q\) gives integer demands \(Q\mu_a\) and integer capacities \(Q\nu_h\).

Apply Lemma 2 to the scaled assignment, restricted to \(E\), with costs \(q_{a,h}\). Divide the resulting integral assignment by \(Q\). This produces rational masses \(\widehat x_{a,h}\) with:

  • the required type totals and supply bounds;
  • support contained in \(E\), hence preserved envy-freeness;
  • total subsidy at most \(\Gamma\).

Each scaled entry is a nonnegative integer bounded by \(Q\nu_h\), so every mass has polynomial bit length. A certificate can therefore list all masses and their zero-or-one subsidies. Exact rational arithmetic verifies feasibility, the budget, and every ordered pair of used-cell envy inequalities in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with the reduction, this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
