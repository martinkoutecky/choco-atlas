The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a public-budget-regular value distribution \(\mu\), common public budget \(B\), and supply share \(0<\rho<1\), consider \(N\)-agent markets with unit-demand advertisers and \(K_N\) identical slots, where \(K_N/N\to\rho\). Determine whether the multi-unit public-budget clinching auction’s normalized interim allocations and payments have a limit independent of integer rounding, and whether its limiting per-capita welfare is at least \(1/e\) of the optimal BIC per-capita welfare for \((\mu,B,\rho)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support: \(\tau\) distinct nonnegative rational values \(v_j\), each with positive binary rational mass \(\mu_j\), where \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\) and \(h=\max_jv_j>0\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. The common budget \(B>0\) and supply share \(0<\rho<1\) are binary rationals.
For this finite-support formulation, public-budget regularity means that the piecewise-linear interpolation through \((0,\mu(\{0\}))\) and \((v_j,\sum_{v_k\leq v_j}\mu_k)\) is concave on \([0,h]\).
An advertiser receiving service probability \(x\in[0,1]\) and paying \(p\) has utility \(vx-p\) when \(p\leq B\), and \(-\infty\) otherwise; payments are nonnegative and may be charged regardless of realized service.
For each \(N\), values are drawn independently from \(\mu\); there are \(K_N\) identical slots, with \(1\leq K_N<N\) and \(K_N/N\to\rho\). Feasible service probabilities satisfy \(\sum_i x_i\leq K_N\).
The public-budget clinching auction starts with zero allocations and payments and an ascending price clock \(c\). An active advertiser has residual demand \(d_i=\min\{1-x_i,(B-p_i)/c\}\); at zero price use \(d_i=1-x_i\). With residual supply \(s\), advertiser \(i\) clinches \(\min\{d_i,\max\{0,s-\sum_{\ell\ne i}d_\ell\}\}\), paying \(c\) per unit; update supply, allocations, and budgets continuously.
Advertisers exit at their values. Process simultaneous exits in a uniformly random order, performing clinching at that value after each exit; freeze exiting allocations. Fractional allocations are implemented as lotteries serving at most \(K_N\) advertisers, with payments independent of lottery realization.
Let \(x_N(v_j)\) and \(p_N(v_j)\) be a tagged advertiser’s interim service probability and expected payment, conditional on value \(v_j\). These are already per-advertiser quantities; normalized aggregate type allocations and payments are \(\mu_jx_N(v_j)\) and \(\mu_jp_N(v_j)\).
Define the optimal continuum BIC welfare \(W^*(\mu,B,\rho)\) as the maximum of \(\sum_j\mu_jv_ja_j\) over \(0\leq a_j\leq1\), \(0\leq t_j\leq B\), \(\sum_j\mu_ja_j\leq\rho\), interim IR \(v_ja_j-t_j\geq0\), and BIC inequalities \(v_ja_j-t_j\geq v_ja_k-t_k\) for every \(j,k\).
There is one slot type, \(m=1\). State any running times in \(m,\tau,L\), and relevant parameters such as \(N,K_N\), where \(L\) is the total binary input length.

Do the interim allocation and payment vectors converge for every such society to limits independent of the integer sequence \(K_N\) satisfying \(K_N/N\to\rho\)? If they do, is their limiting per-capita welfare \(\sum_j\mu_jv_jx_\infty(v_j)\) always at least \(W^*(\mu,B,\rho)/e\)?

For finitely many i.i.d. advertisers with a common public budget and a concave value CDF, single-item clinching achieves at least \(1/e\) of optimal BIC welfare. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/1804.01977v2)
For position environments with common budgets, clinching achieves at least \(1/2\) of optimal envy-free welfare, a different benchmark. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1212.5766) Neither statement establishes the specified continuum limit or its BIC welfare guarantee; no result settling that precise question was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The required finite-support representation conflicts with literal concavity of a nontrivial value CDF. Adopted concavity of its interpolation through cumulative support masses as an explicit discrete regularity convention.
  • Normalized total society mass to one; required nonnegative rational values with positive maximum, a positive rational budget, and a rational supply share strictly between zero and one.
  • Retained independent value sampling from the finite-agent model. Integer-rounding independence concerns the supply sequence, rather than deterministic rounding of type populations.
  • Used fractional service probabilities implemented by feasible lotteries, with payments charged independently of realized service; capped residual demand at the remaining unit-demand capacity.
  • Specified uniformly random sequential processing of tied exits, with clinching between exits.
  • Interpreted normalized interim outcomes as per-advertiser quantities and explicitly distinguished them from normalized aggregate type outcomes.
  • Defined the continuum BIC benchmark using mass feasibility, incentive constraints on supported types, interim individual rationality, nonnegative payments, and a pointwise public-budget cap.
  • Set m to one homogeneous slot type and defined L as total binary input length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

1. Conclusion

No. The interim payment vector can fail to converge, even with strictly concave interpolated CDF and a budget strictly below the highest value. The counterexample below has convergent allocations but two different subsequential payment limits.

Thus the universal convergence premise in the second question is false. The example’s limiting welfare is optimal; the failure concerns payments.

2. Society and supply sequence

Take \(m=1\), \(\tau=2\), and
\[ v_1=1,\qquad v_2=3,\qquad \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad B=2,\qquad \rho=\frac12. \]

The interpolated CDF passes through \((0,0)\), \((1,\tfrac12)\), and \((3,1)\). Its successive slopes are \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac14\), so it is strictly concave at its interior knot. All input numbers are binary rationals.

Let \(\Delta_N=\lfloor N^{3/4}\rfloor\). For \(N\geq256\), set
\[ K_N=\left\lfloor\frac N2\right\rfloor+(-1)^N\Delta_N. \]
For smaller \(N\geq2\), take \(K_N=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\). These supplies satisfy \(1\leq K_N<N\) and \(K_N/N\to\tfrac12\).

Even \(N\) therefore approach the high-type mass from above, while odd \(N\) approach it from below.

3. Exact auction outcomes for relevant realized profiles

Fix a realized profile with \(n\) advertisers of value \(3\), and write \(K=K_N\).

When \(n\leq K\)

Before price \(1\), every active advertiser has demand \(1\), and there are more than \(K\) active advertisers. Consequently nobody clinches.

During the exits at price \(1\), the active count eventually reaches \(K\). At that point every remaining advertiser clinches
\[ \min\{1,K-(K-1)\}=1, \]
paying \(1\). This exhausts supply.

All value-\(3\) advertisers remain active until then, so each receives allocation \(1\) and pays \(1\). The remaining \(K-n\) recipients are chosen uniformly among the \(N-n\) value-\(1\) advertisers.

When \(n>K\) and \(c_0=2(n-1)/K<3\)

No value-\(1\) advertiser receives anything: throughout their exits, the active count remains at least \(K+1\), with demand \(1\).

After those exits, the \(n\) active advertisers are identical. Their first continuous clinching occurs at
\[ c_0=\frac{2(n-1)}K, \]
where \(K=(n-1)B/c_0\).

Here is the continuous evolution, including a verification of the demand cap. For \(c_0\leq c<3\), put \(r=c_0/c\). The residual supply \(s\), common residual budget \(b\), and common allocation \(x\) are
\[ s(c)=Kr^n,\qquad b(c)=2r^{n-1},\qquad x(c)=\frac Kn(1-r^n). \]

To derive these formulas, continuous clinching maintains the boundary \(s=(n-1)b/c\). Otherwise either no advertiser can clinch, or a positive clinch is immediately available. Using \(s'=-nx'\) and \(b'=-cx'\), differentiation gives
\[ -nx'=-(n-1)x'-\frac{(n-1)b}{c^2}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad x'=\frac{s}{c}. \]
Solving with initial conditions \(s(c_0)=K\), \(b(c_0)=2\), and \(x(c_0)=0\) yields the stated formulas.

The budget demand is
\[ d(c)=\frac{b(c)}c=\frac K{n-1}r^n. \]
It respects the unit cap because
\[ 1-x(c)-d(c) =1-\frac Kn-\frac{K}{n(n-1)}r^n \geq 1-\frac K{n-1} \geq0. \]
Thus the formulas remain valid until the exits at price \(3\).

Immediately before those exits, write \(b_*=b(3)\), \(d_*=b_*/3\), and \(s_*=s(3)\). Since \(s_*=(n-1)d_*\), after the first advertiser exits, every remaining advertiser clinches exactly
\[ \min\{d_*,s_*-(n-2)d_*\}=d_*. \]
This exhausts supply and spends each survivor’s remaining budget. The first exiting advertiser pays \(2-b_*\); all other high advertisers pay \(2\).

Uniform random exit ordering therefore gives each high advertiser conditional expected allocation and payment
\[ \frac Kn \quad\text{and}\quad 2-\frac{b_*}{n} = 2\left[1-\frac1n\left(\frac{c_0}{3}\right)^{n-1}\right], \]
respectively. In particular, the conditional expected payment lies between \(2(1-1/n)\) and \(2\).

4. Interim limits

Conditional on the tagged advertiser having value \(3\), the number of high advertisers is
\[ H_N=1+\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,\tfrac12). \]
Define the event
\[ E_N=\left\{\left|H_N-\frac N2\right|\leq\frac{\Delta_N}{2}\right\}. \]
Its complement has probability tending to zero. Explicitly, the mean differs from \(N/2\) by \(1/2\), so Chebyshev’s inequality gives
\[ \Pr(E_N^c)\leq\frac{N-1}{(\Delta_N-1)^2} =O(N^{-1/2}). \]

For even \(N\), on \(E_N\) we have \(H_N<K_N\). The first profile calculation therefore gives allocation \(1\) and payment \(1\). Since allocations and payments are bounded by \(1\) and \(2\),
\[ x_N(3)\longrightarrow1, \qquad p_N(3)\longrightarrow1 \qquad\text{along even }N. \]

For odd \(N\), on \(E_N\) we have \(H_N>K_N\). Uniformly over this event,
\[ \frac{H_N}{K_N}\longrightarrow1, \qquad \frac{2(H_N-1)}{K_N}\longrightarrow2<3. \]
Thus the second profile calculation applies for all sufficiently large odd \(N\). Its expected allocation tends to \(1\), while its expected payment is between \(2(1-1/H_N)\) and \(2\). Boundedness controls the exceptional event, giving
\[ x_N(3)\longrightarrow1, \qquad p_N(3)\longrightarrow2 \qquad\text{along odd }N. \]

For completeness, conditional on the tagged advertiser having value \(1\), let \(H_N=\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,\tfrac12)\). The first profile calculation and uniform ordering give the exact formulas
\[ x_N(1)=\mathbb E\left[\frac{(K_N-H_N)_+}{N-H_N}\right], \qquad p_N(1)=x_N(1). \]
On the corresponding event \(E_N\), the ratio is zero for odd \(N\), and for even \(N\) it is bounded by
\[ \frac{3\Delta_N/2+1}{N/2-\Delta_N/2}\longrightarrow0. \]
The exceptional event again has vanishing probability. Hence \(x_N(1)\to0\) and \(p_N(1)\to0\).

We have therefore proved
\[ (x_N(1),x_N(3))\longrightarrow(0,1), \]
but
\[ (p_N(1),p_N(3)) \longrightarrow \begin{cases} (0,1),&N\text{ even},\\ (0,2),&N\text{ odd}. \end{cases} \]

These are the requested per-advertiser quantities. In particular, the payment vector does not converge along this single admissible supply sequence. Taking the positive or negative supply perturbation for every \(N\) also produces two admissible sequences with different payment limits.

5. Welfare in the counterexample

The allocation limit has welfare \(3/2\). This equals the continuum BIC optimum.

Indeed, choose \(a_1=0\), \(a_2=1\), \(t_1=0\), and \(t_2=1\). Supply usage is \(1/2\), both payments respect \(B=2\), and both types satisfy IR. The low type obtains utility \(0\) under either report; the high type obtains utility \(2\) truthfully and \(0\) from the low report. Thus the outcome is BIC.

Conversely, every feasible outcome has welfare at most
\[ 3\sum_j\mu_j a_j\leq3\rho=\frac32. \]
Therefore \(W^*=3/2\).

The welfare inequality holds in this example. Nevertheless, the universal convergence assertion asked in the question is false, so its conditional welfare question has no universal limiting allocation-and-payment pair to which it can refer.

Verdict

The counterexample correctly disproves universal convergence of the interim allocation and payment vectors. Its allocation vector converges, while its high-type expected payment has different limits along even and odd population sizes.

The argument is sound in substance. There is one minor terminology issue: the interpolated CDF is concave with strictly decreasing slopes at its interior knot, but it is not strictly concave on its entire domain.

The example also correctly establishes that its limiting welfare equals the continuum BIC optimum. It does not establish or refute a general welfare guarantee for allocation limits considered separately; the task’s second question is conditional on the universal convergence assertion that this example disproves.

Step-by-step verification

1. Society and regularity

The two supported values are distinct and nonnegative, both masses are positive, and their sum is \(1\). The budget and supply share satisfy the required restrictions, and \(h=3>0\). All parameters are binary rationals.

The CDF interpolation has successive slopes \(1/2\) and \(1/4\), so it satisfies the task’s concavity condition.

Minor issue: The opening claim “even with strictly concave interpolated CDF” is literally false under the usual definition of strict concavity. The interpolation is affine on each interval between knots. The later qualification “strictly concave at its interior knot” describes the intended strict decrease in slopes adequately.

Minimum repair: Replace the opening phrase with “even with a concave interpolated CDF whose slopes strictly decrease at its interior knot.”

2. Supply sequence

The sequence is admissible. For \(N\geq256\), the perturbation satisfies \(\Delta_N\leq N/4\), so both signs give \(1\leq K_N<N\). The separately specified smaller population sizes also satisfy these bounds.

Moreover, \(\Delta_N/N\to0\), hence \(K_N/N\to1/2\). The positive perturbation on even \(N\) and negative perturbation on odd \(N\) dominate the fluctuations of the number of high types used later.

No issue arises from the floor functions or parity.

3. Profiles with \(n\leq K\)

Before price \(1\), every advertiser has unit demand: budgets cannot bind at these prices, and no allocation has occurred. With at least \(K+1\) active advertisers, every clinch is zero.

During low-type exits, the active count reaches \(K\). At that point each remaining advertiser can receive one unit, and these \(K\) units exhaust supply. All high types are among the remaining advertisers.

Uniform exit ordering makes the remaining \(K-n\) low advertisers a uniform subset of the \(N-n\) low advertisers. Consequently, the claimed high-type allocation and payment, both equal to \(1\), and the low-type selection probabilities are correct.

4. Profiles with \(n>K\) and \(c_0<3\)

Low advertisers receive nothing during their exits: the active count never falls below \(K+1\), and demand at price \(1\) remains \(1\).

Since \(n>K\), one has \(n-1\geq K\) and therefore \(c_0\geq2\). The claimed first clinching price is consistent with the budget demand and the boundary \(K=(n-1)B/c_0\).

The continuous calculation is internally consistent. Differentiating the stated boundary, using \(s'=-nx'\) and \(b'=-cx'\), gives exactly the displayed equation and \(x'=s/c\). The proposed formulas satisfy this equation and all three initial conditions.

The demand-cap calculation is also correct:

\[ 1-x(c)-d(c) = 1-\frac Kn-\frac{K}{n(n-1)}r^n \geq 1-\frac K{n-1} \geq0. \]

Here \(0<r\leq1\) and \(K\leq n-1\). Thus the use of budget demand remains valid throughout the stated interval.

At price \(3\), after the first exit, there are \(n-1\) survivors and residual supply \((n-1)d_*\). Each survivor’s clinch is \(d_*\), respecting its remaining allocation cap. These clinches exhaust supply and each survivor’s residual budget.

The first exiting high advertiser pays \(2-b_*\); every other high advertiser pays \(2\). Uniform exit ordering therefore gives expected payment \(2-b_*/n\). The stated formula and bounds follow correctly. Total allocation is \(K\), and symmetry gives expected high-type allocation \(K/n\).

5. High-type interim limits

Conditional on a high-type tag, the distribution \(H_N=1+\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\) is correct.

The Chebyshev estimate correctly accounts for the mean displacement of \(1/2\):

\[ \Pr(E_N^c)\leq\frac{N-1}{(\Delta_N-1)^2}=O(N^{-1/2}). \]

On even \(N\), the event \(E_N\) implies \(H_N<K_N\). Hence allocation and payment both equal \(1\) on an event whose probability tends to \(1\).

On odd \(N\), it implies \(H_N>K_N\), while \(H_N/K_N\to1\) uniformly on that event. Thus \(c_0\to2<3\), making the second profile calculation applicable eventually. Its allocation tends to \(1\), and its payment bounds both tend to \(2\).

The boundedness arguments for the exceptional events are valid. Therefore the two claimed high-type payment limits are established.

6. Low-type interim limits

Conditional on a low-type tag, \(H_N=\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,1/2)\) is correct, and \(N-H_N\geq1\), so the displayed denominator never vanishes.

The exact allocation formula follows from uniform low-type selection. Every selected low advertiser pays \(1\), giving \(p_N(1)=x_N(1)\).

The stated bound on the even subsequence tends to zero, and the ratio vanishes on the corresponding odd-subsequence event. The exceptional event again has vanishing probability. Both low-type interim quantities therefore converge to zero.

Together, these calculations establish payment nonconvergence along a single admissible supply sequence. That suffices to refute the universal convergence assertion.

7. Welfare benchmark

The proposed continuum outcome satisfies supply, payment, IR, and both nontrivial BIC inequalities. Its welfare is \(3/2\).

The upper bound \(3\sum_j\mu_ja_j\leq3\rho=3/2\) applies to every feasible outcome. Thus the asserted optimum and equality with limiting auction welfare are correct.

No running time is claimed, so there is no running-time assertion to audit.
