The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(k\ge2\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational locations, draw an anchor \(p\) from \(\mu\), then choose \(k-1\) distinct support locations \(S\) excluding \(p\) with probability proportional to \(\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y\), where \(\Delta_p(S)\) is the product of consecutive gaps among \(p\) and the locations in \(S\). If the support has fewer than \(k\) locations, open at every support location. Does this mass-weighted RAV lottery always satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)]\le 4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its nonempty support \(X=\{x_1<\cdots<x_\tau\}\subset\mathbb Q\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_x\) satisfying \(\sum_{x\in X}\mu_x=1\). Types are locations; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length and \(k\ge2\) an integer. Any running times are measured in \(\tau,L,k\); there is no separate \(m\) parameter.
For a nonempty facility set \(F\subset\mathbb R\), define \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)=\sum_{x\in X}\mu_x\min_{f\in F}|x-f|\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)=\min_{F\subset\mathbb R,\,1\le|F|\le k}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)\).
Define the mass-weighted Random-Anchor Volume lottery as follows. If \(\tau<k\), output \(F=X\). Otherwise draw \(p\in X\) with probability \(\mu_p\). For each unordered \(S\subseteq X\setminus\{p\}\) of size \(k-1\), sort \(\{p\}\cup S\) as \(z_0<\cdots<z_{k-1}\) and put \(\Delta_p(S)=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(z_j-z_{j-1})\).
Conditional on \(p\), draw \(S\) with probability \(\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y\big/\sum_{T\subseteq X\setminus\{p\},\,|T|=k-1}\Delta_p(T)\prod_{y\in T}\mu_y\), and output \(F=\{p\}\cup S\). The denominator is positive whenever \(\tau\ge k\).
Does this lottery satisfy \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)]\le4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\) for every such society and every \(k\ge2\)?
For finite lists of agents, uniformly choosing an anchor identity and weighting additional identity sets by their consecutive-gap product gives the \(4(k-1)\) social-cost guarantee, including repeated locations ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16550)). No separate result for this mass-weighted formulation was located.

Result

Yes. The mass-weighted lottery is exactly the distribution on facility locations obtained by applying the finite-agent lottery to a list with suitable multiplicities. Rationality of the masses makes this an exact reduction.

If \(\tau<k\), the lottery outputs \(X\), so both its social cost and \(\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\) are zero. We henceforth assume \(\tau\ge k\).

Finite-agent theorem used

For a finite nonempty set \(A\) of agent identities, let \(\ell(a)\in\mathbb R\) denote the location of identity \(a\), allowing repeated locations. Define

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{SC}_A(F)=\sum_{a\in A}\min_{f\in F}|\ell(a)-f|,\qquad \mathrm{OPT}_{k,A}=\min_{1\le |F|\le k}\mathrm{SC}_A(F).\)

The finite-agent Random-Anchor Volume lottery chooses an anchor identity uniformly and assigns each additional unordered identity set of size \(k-1\) weight equal to its consecutive-gap product together with the anchor. Its guarantee is

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}_A(F)]\le 4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_{k,A}.\)

This is [Theorem 2 of Aziz, Mackenzie, and Suzuki](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16550v1#Thmtheorem2). Their definition in Section 3.1 permits repeated locations: a candidate has positive weight exactly when all its selected locations, including the anchor, are distinct.

Exact equality of the lotteries

Because every mass is rational, choose a positive integer \(N\) such that

\(\displaystyle n_x:=N\mu_x\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\quad\text{for every }x\in X.\)

Then \(\sum_{x\in X}n_x=N\). Construct the identity set

\(\displaystyle A=\{(x,j):x\in X,\ 1\le j\le n_x\},\)

with \(\ell(x,j)=x\). Thus \(A\) contains exactly \(n_x\) identities at location \(x\).

A uniformly selected anchor identity has location \(p\) with probability

\(\displaystyle \frac{n_p}{N}=\mu_p,\)

as required by the mass-weighted lottery.

Now fix a particular anchor identity \(a\) at location \(p\). Consider an additional unordered identity set \(B\subseteq A\setminus\{a\}\) of size \(k-1\).

If two identities in \(B\) have the same location, or an identity in \(B\) has location \(p\), the sorted list of \(k\) selected locations has a zero consecutive gap. Consequently, \(B\) has zero weight.

Every positive-weight candidate therefore selects exactly one identity at each location of some set

\(\displaystyle S\subseteq X\setminus\{p\},\qquad |S|=k-1.\)

For a fixed such \(S\), the number of identity sets realizing it is exactly

\(\displaystyle \prod_{y\in S}n_y.\)

Indeed, one independently chooses one of the \(n_y\) identities at each distinct location \(y\in S\); these choices determine an unordered identity set uniquely. Each resulting identity set has weight \(\Delta_p(S)\). Hence their aggregate weight is

\(\displaystyle \Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}n_y =N^{k-1}\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y.\)

Writing

\(\displaystyle Z_p=\sum_{\substack{T\subseteq X\setminus\{p\}\\|T|=k-1}}\Delta_p(T)\prod_{y\in T}\mu_y,\)

the total identity-level weight conditional on \(a\) is \(N^{k-1}Z_p\). This is positive: since \(\tau\ge k\), at least one admissible \(T\) exists, and all its gaps and masses are positive.

The conditional probability of selecting additional locations \(S\) is therefore

\(\displaystyle \frac{N^{k-1}\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y}{N^{k-1}Z_p} =\frac{\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y}{Z_p}.\)

This probability depends only on the anchor location \(p\), so conditioning on that location instead of its identity gives the same expression. Together with the anchor probability \(\mu_p\), this proves that the two lotteries have exactly the same distribution on facility sets.

Transfer of social cost and optimum

For every admissible facility set \(F\),

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{SC}_A(F) =\sum_{x\in X}n_x\min_{f\in F}|x-f| =N\,\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F).\)

The feasible facility sets are identical in the two optimization problems. Taking their minima therefore gives

\(\displaystyle \mathrm{OPT}_{k,A}=N\,\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu).\)

All hypotheses of the finite-agent theorem hold: \(A\) is finite and nonempty, its locations are real, repeated locations are allowed, and \(k\ge2\). Applying that theorem and the equality of output distributions yields

\(\displaystyle N\,\mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)] =\mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}_A(F)] \le4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_{k,A} =4(k-1)N\,\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu).\)

Dividing by \(N>0\) proves the requested inequality.

The replicated list is solely a mathematical construction. Its size may be large relative to \(L\); the proof makes no running-time claim and introduces no additional input parameter.
