The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type and good-class sets \(T\) and \(G\), rational utilities \(u_{tg}\in\{a_t,b_t\}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\ge0\) with \(\sum_gq_g=1\), compute \(p\) and \(z\) such that \(p_g\ge0\), \(\min_{g\in G}p_g=0\), \(z_{tg}\ge0\), \(\sum_g z_{tg}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{tg}=q_g\), \(\sum_gp_gz_{tg}\le1\), and \(z_t\in\arg\max\{\sum_gu_{tg}y_g:\ y_g\ge0,\ \sum_gy_g=1,\ \sum_gp_gy_g\le1\}\) for every \(t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=|G|\ge1\) good classes with positive binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), where \(\sum_gq_g=1\). Utilities satisfy \(u_{tg}\in\{a_t,b_t\}\), with binary-rational \(0\le a_t<b_t\); a type need not use both values. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each individual has budget \(1\) and consumes a bundle of total size \(1\). For prices \(p\), define \(F(p)=\{y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m}:\sum_g y_g=1,\ \sum_g p_gy_g\le1\}\).
An equilibrium consists of prices \(p_g\ge0\), normalized by \(\min_g p_g=0\), and per-individual bundles \(z_t\in F(p)\), with \(\sum_t\mu_tz_{tg}=q_g\) for every good class.
For every type, \(z_t\) maximizes \(\sum_g u_{tg}y_g\) over \(F(p)\) and minimizes \(\sum_g p_gy_g\) among those maximizers; remaining ties are unrestricted. Budgets need not be exhausted.

Is computing an exact equilibrium for this mass-based bivalued market possible in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely listed agents and equally many unit-supply goods, exact bivalued HZ equilibrium computation admits a strongly polynomial algorithm and a rational equilibrium ([Vazirani–Yannakakis](https://ics.uci.edu/~vazirani/VY.pdf)).
No result settling the formulation with binary-rational masses and supplies was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted utilities to rational 0 ≤ a_t < b_t, as in the discrete bivalued definition; neither value must occur.
  • Retained only positive-mass types, following the required support convention.
  • Required positive supplies for listed good classes, reflecting the discrete model's positive supply of every available good.
  • Restored minimum-expenditure tie-breaking among utility maximizers, as required by the full version; remaining ties are unrestricted.
  • Specified exact computation and total binary input length L.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact equilibrium computation is polynomial-time solvable

Theorem. For the stated market, an exact equilibrium with rational prices and rational bundles can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The construction uses capacities equal to the type masses and good supplies, without expanding the population. It extends the tight-set strategy in [Vazirani–Yannakakis, Section 4](https://ics.uci.edu/~vazirani/VY.pdf). The weighted construction and its complexity are proved below.

1. Reduction to dichotomous utilities

For each type \(t\), define its set of high-valued goods by
\[ H_t=\{g\in G:u_{tg}=b_t\}. \]
For every size-one bundle \(y\),
\[ \sum_g u_{tg}y_g = a_t+(b_t-a_t)\sum_{g\in H_t}y_g. \]
Since \(b_t-a_t>0\), maximizing the original utility is equivalent to maximizing the amount of high-valued goods. The sets of utility maximizers coincide, so the minimum-cost tie rule is also preserved.

Thus we solve the dichotomous instance whose preference graph has an edge \(tg\) exactly when \(g\in H_t\). Empty and full sets \(H_t\) are allowed.

Write
\[ \mu(A)=\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t, \qquad q(B)=\sum_{g\in B}q_g. \]

We construct aggregate allocations \(x_{tg}\). The final per-individual bundles will be \(z_{tg}=x_{tg}/\mu_t\).

2. A capacitated transportation lemma

We use the following consequence of the max-flow/min-cut theorem.

Lemma. Let \(I,J\) be the two sides of a bipartite graph. Give vertices \(i\in I\) nonnegative capacities \(c_i\), and vertices \(j\in J\) nonnegative demands \(d_j\). There exist nonnegative edge quantities \(f_{ji}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_i f_{ji}=d_j \quad(j\in J), \qquad \sum_j f_{ji}\le c_i \quad(i\in I) \]
if and only if every \(S\subseteq J\) satisfies
\[ \sum_{j\in S}d_j\le \sum_{i\in N(S)}c_i. \]

Proof. Necessity follows by summing the demands of \(S\): all their flow enters \(N(S)\).

For sufficiency, build a network with source-to-\(j\) capacities \(d_j\), preference-edge capacities
\[ M=1+\sum_jd_j+\sum_ic_i, \]
and \(i\)-to-sink capacities \(c_i\).

A minimum cut cannot cross a preference edge, because the cut consisting only of the source has capacity \(\sum_jd_j<M\). If its source side contains goods \(S\), it must contain \(N(S)\). Its capacity is therefore at least
\[ \sum_{j\notin S}d_j+\sum_{i\in N(S)}c_i \ge \sum_jd_j. \]
Consequently, a maximum flow saturates every source-to-\(j\) arc. This supplies the required quantities. \(\square\)

The same lemma applies with the two sides interchanged.

3. Separating zero-priced goods from the remaining goods

First compute a maximum flow in the preference graph with type capacities \(\mu_t\) and good capacities \(q_g\).

If its value is \(1\), every type receives aggregate amount \(\mu_t\) entirely in high-valued goods, and every supply is exhausted. Set all prices to zero. Each type then receives maximum possible dichotomous utility at minimum possible cost. This is an equilibrium.

Henceforth suppose the maximum-flow value is less than \(1\).

Consider the network
\[ s\longrightarrow T\longrightarrow G\longrightarrow r \]
with capacities \(\mu_t\), \(2\), and \(q_g\), respectively. A minimum cut has capacity at most \(1\), so no preference edge of capacity \(2\) crosses it.

Let \(A_1\) be the types on its source side and \(A_2=T\setminus A_1\). Let \(B_1\) be the goods on its source side and \(B_2=G\setminus B_1\). Then:

  • \(A_2\cup B_1\) is a minimum-weight vertex cover, where type weights are \(\mu_t\) and good weights are \(q_g\);
  • no preference edge joins \(A_1\) to \(B_2\);
  • its weight satisfies

\[ W=\mu(A_2)+q(B_1)<1. \]

Minimality of this cover gives two useful inequalities.

For every \(U\subseteq A_2\), replacing \(U\) in the cover by \(N(U)\cap B_2\) produces another cover. Therefore
\[ \mu(U)\le q(N(U)\cap B_2). \tag{1} \]
By the transportation lemma, allocate every type in \(A_2\) its full aggregate amount \(\mu_t\), using only high-valued goods in \(B_2\), without exceeding their supplies.

Similarly, for every \(S\subseteq B_1\), replacing \(S\) by \(N(S)\cap A_1\) gives
\[ q(S)\le \mu(N(S)\cap A_1). \tag{2} \]

Set the prices of all goods in \(B_2\) to zero. Notice that
\[ q(B_2)-\mu(A_2)=1-W>0. \tag{3} \]
In particular, \(B_2\) is nonempty and has positive supply remaining after the allocation to \(A_2\).

4. Pricing the remaining goods in at most \(m\) rounds

Initialize the remaining type and good sets as
\[ C=A_1,\qquad D=B_1. \]
For \(S\subseteq D\), let \(N_C(S)=N(S)\cap C\).

At a round with \(D\ne\varnothing\), define
\[ \lambda=\min_{\varnothing\ne S\subseteq D} \frac{\mu(N_C(S))}{q(S)}. \tag{4} \]
We will show both that this value is computable in polynomial time and that every round has \(\lambda\ge1\).

Choose a nonempty set \(S\subseteq D\) attaining (4), and put \(R=N_C(S)\). Thus
\[ \mu(R)=\lambda q(S). \tag{5} \]
Give every good in \(S\) price \(\lambda\). Allocate its full supply to types in \(R\), along preference edges, so that each such type receives aggregate high-good amount \(\mu_t/\lambda\). The existence and computation of this allocation are established below.

Then remove the entire sets:
\[ C\gets C\setminus R,\qquad D\gets D\setminus S. \tag{6} \]

Every round removes at least one good, so there are at most \(m\) rounds.

4.1. Computing the exact round price

Solve the rational linear program
\[ \text{maximize }\lambda \]
subject to
\[ \lambda\ge0,\qquad f_{gt}\ge0, \]
\[ \sum_{t:tg\text{ is an edge},\,t\in C}f_{gt} =\lambda q_g \quad(g\in D), \tag{7} \]
\[ \sum_{g:tg\text{ is an edge},\,g\in D}f_{gt} \le\mu_t \quad(t\in C). \tag{8} \]

By the transportation lemma, a value of \(\lambda\) is feasible exactly when
\[ \lambda q(S)\le\mu(N_C(S)) \quad\text{for every }S\subseteq D. \tag{9} \]
Hence the optimum is precisely (4).

The feasible region is nonempty, since zero flow is feasible. It is bounded: summing (7) and (8) gives
\[ \lambda q(D)\le\mu(C). \]
All flow variables are also bounded by \(\mu(C)\). Thus an optimum is attained.

The LP has polynomially many variables and constraints, with rational coefficients taken from the input. Standard exact rational linear programming computes its optimum and an optimal flow in polynomial time.

4.2. Finding a nonempty tight set

It remains to extract a set satisfying (5), without enumerating subsets.

At the computed optimum, build a network with source-to-good capacities \(\lambda q_g\), preference-edge capacities \(2\), and type-to-sink capacities \(\mu_t\). Its maximum-flow value is
\[ V=\lambda q(D)\le\mu(C)\le1. \]

For each \(g\in D\), temporarily add an additional source-to-\(g\) arc of capacity \(2\), and compute a minimum cut. Stop when its value equals \(V\).

Such a good exists: a minimizing set \(S\) from (4) gives the original cut with source side \(\{s\}\cup S\cup N_C(S)\), of capacity
\[ \lambda q(D\setminus S)+\mu(N_C(S))=V. \]
If \(g\in S\), the added arc does not cross this cut. Since adding an arc cannot decrease the minimum-cut value, the modified minimum-cut value is exactly \(V\).

Conversely, a cut of value \(V\le1\) cannot cross any capacity-\(2\) arc. Its source-side goods therefore form a nonempty set \(S\), containing the forced good \(g\), and its source-side types contain \(N_C(S)\). There are no additional source-side types: deleting one would strictly reduce the cut capacity, because every \(\mu_t\) is positive. Thus its capacity is
\[ \lambda q(D\setminus S)+\mu(N_C(S))=V, \]
which proves (5).

This procedure requires at most \(|D|\) minimum-cut computations per round.

4.3. Allocating a tight set

Use an optimal LP flow \(f\) from (7)–(8). All flow leaving goods in \(S\) enters \(R=N_C(S)\). Its total is
\[ \lambda q(S)=\mu(R). \]
This equals the sum of all receiving capacities in \(R\). Consequently, every type \(t\in R\) receives exactly \(\mu_t\) units of flow from \(S\).

Set the aggregate allocation on these edges to
\[ x_{tg}=\frac{f_{gt}}{\lambda} \quad(t\in R,\ g\in S). \tag{10} \]
Then
\[ \sum_{t\in R}x_{tg}=q_g \quad(g\in S), \qquad \sum_{g\in S}x_{tg}=\frac{\mu_t}{\lambda} \quad(t\in R). \tag{11} \]
Thus the round clears these goods and gives every removed type per-individual high-good amount \(1/\lambda\), costing exactly \(1\).

4.4. Round prices are nondecreasing and at least one

Initially, (2) shows that every ratio in (4) is at least \(1\).

Suppose a round removes the tight set \(S\) at price \(\lambda\). For any \(U\subseteq D\setminus S\), inequality (9), applied to \(S\cup U\), gives
\[ \mu(N_C(S\cup U))\ge\lambda\bigl(q(S)+q(U)\bigr). \]
The neighborhood decomposes disjointly as
\[ N_C(S\cup U) = N_C(S)\ \cup\ N_{C\setminus N_C(S)}(U). \]
Subtracting the tight equality (5) yields
\[ \mu(N_{C\setminus N_C(S)}(U))\ge\lambda q(U). \tag{12} \]
Therefore the next round price is at least \(\lambda\). Inductively, all round prices are at least \(1\), and every type receives at most its aggregate size requirement in (11).

5. Completing the size-one bundles

After all rounds:

  • every good in \(B_1\) has been fully allocated;
  • every type in \(A_2\) has already received aggregate size \(\mu_t\);
  • a type in \(A_1\) removed at price \(\lambda\) has received aggregate size \(\mu_t/\lambda\);
  • any type left in \(C\) has no high-valued goods at all.

For the last assertion, a remaining type cannot like any good in \(B_1\): it would have been removed when that good’s round occurred. It also cannot like a good in \(B_2\), by the vertex-cover property.

For \(t\in A_1\), let \(d_t\) denote its remaining aggregate size requirement. For \(g\in B_2\), let \(r_g\) denote its remaining supply. These quantities are nonnegative, and
\[ \sum_{t\in A_1}d_t = \mu(A_1)-q(B_1) = q(B_2)-\mu(A_2) = \sum_{g\in B_2}r_g = 1-W. \tag{13} \]
The common value is strictly positive.

Complete the allocation by adding
\[ x^{\mathrm{fill}}_{tg} = \frac{d_t r_g}{1-W} \quad(t\in A_1,\ g\in B_2). \tag{14} \]
Its row sums are \(d_t\), and its column sums are \(r_g\). These filler goods have price zero and are low-valued for every type in \(A_1\).

Finally set
\[ z_{tg}=\frac{x_{tg}}{\mu_t}. \]
All bundles have size one, and all markets clear.

6. Utility optimality and the minimum-cost tie rule

There are three cases.

Types in \(A_2\). Their bundles consist entirely of high-valued goods priced zero. Their dichotomous utility is \(1\), the largest possible value for a size-one bundle, and their cost is zero, the smallest possible cost.

Types in \(A_1\) with no high-valued goods. Their utility is constant over size-one bundles. They receive only zero-priced goods, so they satisfy the minimum-cost tie rule.

Types removed in a round at price \(\lambda\). Such a type has no edge to any earlier round’s goods; otherwise it would have been removed earlier. It has no edge to \(B_2\), and subsequent round prices are at least \(\lambda\). Therefore every high-valued good available to it has price at least \(\lambda\).

For any feasible bundle \(y\), put
\[ h=\sum_{g\in H_t}y_g. \]
Nonnegative prices and the budget constraint imply
\[ 1\ge\sum_g p_gy_g\ge\lambda h. \]
Hence \(h\le1/\lambda\). The constructed bundle attains this bound, so it maximizes utility.

Moreover, every utility-maximizing bundle has high-good amount \(1/\lambda\), and therefore costs at least
\[ \lambda\cdot\frac1\lambda=1. \]
The constructed bundle costs exactly \(1\), so it also minimizes cost among utility maximizers.

Thus all equilibrium conditions hold. Price normalization holds because \(B_2\ne\varnothing\) and every good in \(B_2\) has price zero. The initial full-flow case also has minimum price zero.

7. Exactness and polynomial bit complexity

The algorithm uses:

  • an initial maximum flow and minimum cut;
  • one transportation flow for \(A_2\);
  • at most \(m\) rational LPs, each with at most \(m\tau+1\) variables and polynomially many constraints;
  • at most \(m^2\) additional minimum-cut computations;
  • rational arithmetic for (10), (14), and the final normalization.

Each round’s LP uses the original masses and supplies as coefficients; only the remaining vertex sets change. Therefore its encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Exact rational LP algorithms return optimal solutions of polynomial bit length. In particular, each round price is a ratio of two subset sums,
\[ \lambda=\frac{\mu(N_C(S))}{q(S)}, \]
and hence has polynomial bit length. The subsequent flow capacities and allocation calculations also have polynomial bit length. There are only polynomially many such quantities and arithmetic operations.

Consequently, the entire computation, including writing the exact rational equilibrium, takes time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

8. A small example with two positive price levels

Take three types with masses
\[ (\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3)=\left(\frac14,\frac12,\frac14\right), \]
three goods with supplies
\[ (q_A,q_B,q_C)=\left(\frac18,\frac18,\frac34\right), \]
and high-valued sets
\[ H_1=\{A,B\},\qquad H_2=\{B\},\qquad H_3=\{C\}. \]

A minimum cover is \(A_2=\{3\}\), \(B_1=\{A,B\}\). The first tight set is \(\{A\}\), at price \(2\); after removing type \(1\), good \(B\) is priced at \(4\).

The resulting prices and bundles are
\[ p=(2,4,0), \]
\[ z_1=\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right),\qquad z_2=\left(0,\frac14,\frac34\right),\qquad z_3=(0,0,1). \]
Their aggregate consumption is exactly
\[ \frac14z_1+\frac12z_2+\frac14z_3 = \left(\frac18,\frac18,\frac34\right), \]
and their costs are \(1,1,0\), respectively. This illustrates both multiple price rounds and unexhausted budgets.
