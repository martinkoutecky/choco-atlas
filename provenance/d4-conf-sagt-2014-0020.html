The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a bipartite market with finite job and machine type sets \(J\) and \(M\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\), common strict rankings within each type, per-capita quotas \(q_t\), pool quotas \(Q_t=\mu_tq_t\), pool-pair capacities \(K_{jm}\), and a feasible initial flow \(x^0\), compute a finite sequence of type-pool better-response reallocations ending at a stable allocation. Does every such instance admit such a sequence, and what running time suffices in the support size \(\tau=|J|+|M|\), the compatible-pair set \(E_T\), and the input bit length?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: disjoint finite job and machine type sets \(J,M\), with each type's positive mass \(\mu_t\) encoded as a binary rational. Masses need not sum to one. Let \(\tau=|J|+|M|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types, and let \(m=|M|\).
The input includes compatible pairs \(E_T\subseteq J\times M\), nonnegative rational per-capita quotas \(q_t\), pool quotas \(Q_t=\mu_tq_t\), nonnegative rational pair capacities \(K_{jm}\), and a rational feasible initial allocation \(x^0\). Each type strictly ranks its compatible opposite types; all members share that ranking. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
An allocation assigns divisible quantities \(x_{jm}\geq0\), with \(x_{jm}\leq K_{jm}\) and \(x(t):=\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\leq Q_t\). These are pool constraints; no individual-level realization is required.
A pair \(jm\) blocks \(x\) when \(x_{jm}<K_{jm}\) and each endpoint \(t\in\{j,m\}\) either has \(x(t)<Q_t\) or ranks \(jm\) above some incident pair carrying positive allocation. Stability means absence of blocking pairs.
A type-pool better-response move chooses a blocking pair \(jm\), increases its allocation by a positive rational amount, and may decrease allocations only on incident pairs that the rejecting endpoint ranks below \(jm\). All other coordinates remain unchanged and the resulting allocation must be feasible. Refusals may exceed what is necessary to restore feasibility; jobs initiate moves.
Does every instance admit a finite better-response sequence from \(x^0\) to stability, and is constructing such a sequence polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,|E_T|,L\), or NP-hard?
Known results: finite-vertex stable allocation admits a better-response path from any feasible allocation in \(O(|V|^2|E|)\) arithmetic time, even with real quotas and capacities. The [later full version](https://real.mtak.hu/83554/1/MTDP1820.pdf) explicitly establishes polynomial-length paths of individual better-response moves.
Those results already encompass the pool allocation model defined above; a separate open continuous complexity target is therefore not established.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Unspecified mass normalization: allow arbitrary positive rational masses, since the allocation definitions impose no normalization.
  • Unspecified numerical domains: quotas and pair capacities are nonnegative binary rationals; the initial allocation and move amounts are rational.
  • Unspecified pool semantics: feasibility and blocking are evaluated at type pools, without additional individual-level realization constraints.
  • Undefined better-response reallocations: use single blocking-pair increases and optional refusals on worse incident pairs, preserving feasibility; permit refusals beyond those needed to restore feasibility.
  • Unspecified active side: jobs initiate better-response moves.
  • Unspecified size notation: set m to the number of machine types and L to the total binary input length.

Result

Every instance admits a finite rational better-response sequence from $x^0$ to stability, and such a sequence can be constructed in polynomial binary time in $m,\tau,|E_T|,L$.

The obstruction identified in the verifier report is real: the proposed cycle expansion need not reach the accelerated endpoint. We repair it by restoring the temporary refusal after completing the cycle. This restoration is itself a legal better-response move.

1. Reduction to finite stable allocation

Construct the bipartite graph
$$ G=(J\cup M,E_T), $$
with vertex quotas $Q_t=\mu_tq_t$, edge capacities $K_{jm}$, and the given strict preferences.

Its feasible allocations are exactly the allocations in the question. The blocking definitions also coincide: when a vertex has positive allocation, an edge is preferred to some positively allocated incident edge if and only if it is preferred to the worst such edge. When there is no positive incident allocation, that preference alternative is false in both definitions.

Consequently, one vertex per supported type gives an exact representation. No expansion according to the masses is necessary. The hypotheses of the supplied stable-allocation theorem hold: the graph is finite and bipartite, quotas and capacities are nonnegative, preferences are strict, and $x^0$ is feasible.

If $E_T$ is empty, the empty sequence suffices. Henceforth suppose it is nonempty.

2. The known accelerated construction used

We use the accelerated construction underlying the supplied theorem. With $n$ vertices and $h$ edges, it performs $O(nh)$ simultaneous augmentations along simple alternating paths or cycles. The first proposal blocks; every proposal is preferred at its active endpoint to its paired refusal, which is that endpoint’s worst positive edge. Proposals gain a common positive amount and refusals lose it. Paths can additionally reject worse edges at their starting passive vertex. Numerical operations are sums, differences, minima and maxima.

Its second phase adds a dummy job $d$, with
$$ Q_d=\sum_{m\in M}Q_m,\qquad K_{dm}=\max_{m'\in M}Q_{m'},\qquad x_{dm}=Q_m-x(m), $$
ranks $d$ last at each machine, and swaps the active and passive sides. The final projection onto the original graph is stable. These are properties of the accelerated construction; we do not use its proposed individual cycle expansion. See Algorithm 2, Sections 5.1–5.2, and Theorem 8 of [Cseh–Skutella, *Paths to stable allocations*](https://real.mtak.hu/83554/1/MTDP1820.pdf).

We now prove that every simultaneous augmentation can be realized exactly by a short sequence of legal moves.

3. A criterion for legality

Lemma. Suppose $x$ and $z$ are feasible allocations, and their differences consist of:

  • a positive increase $\delta=z_e-x_e$ on one edge $e$;
  • decreases only on other edges incident to an endpoint of $e$, ranked below $e$ by that endpoint.

Then $e$ blocks $x$, and the transition is a legal better-response move.

Proof. Since
$$ x_e<z_e\leq K_e, $$
the edge is unsaturated before the transition.

Fix an endpoint $t$ of $e$. If $x(t)<Q_t$, its blocking condition holds through unused quota. Otherwise $x(t)=Q_t$. Feasibility of $z$ implies
$$ Q_t+\delta- \sum_{\substack{f\ni t\\f\neq e}}(x_f-z_f) =z(t)\leq Q_t. $$
Thus the sum of decreases incident to $t$ is at least $\delta>0$. Some such edge $f$ decreases strictly. Since $z_f\geq0$, it previously had $x_f>0$; by hypothesis, $t$ prefers $e$ to $f$.

Both endpoints therefore satisfy the blocking condition. All other requirements of a move hold by hypothesis. $\square$

This proves the useful legality argument from the notes without relying on the disputed endpoint-constrained linear program.

4. Exact expansion of a path augmentation

For this section, write the bipartition as $U\cup V$, allowing either side to be designated active. Consider a simple alternating path
$$ v_1,u_1,v_2,u_2,\ldots,v_s,u_s,v_{s+1}. $$
Set
$$ p_i=u_iv_i,\qquad r_i=u_iv_{i+1}. $$
Suppose $p_1$ blocks the initial allocation $x$, and each $u_i$ prefers $p_i$ to $r_i$.

Let the simultaneous augmentation amount be $a>0$, satisfying
$$ a\leq x_{r_i}, \qquad a\leq K_{p_i}-x_{p_i} \quad\text{for every }i. $$
Every $p_i$ gains $a$ and every $r_i$ loses $a$. At $v_1$, the augmentation additionally rejects amounts $b_f$ from edges ranked below $p_1$, where
$$ 0\leq b_f\leq x_f,\qquad \sum_f b_f= \max\{0,a-(Q_{v_1}-x(v_1))\}. $$
These rejection edges lie outside the path: its only edge incident to $v_1$ is $p_1$.

Perform the following $s$ transitions:

  • Increase $p_1$ by $a$, decrease $r_1$ by $a$, and perform all the additional rejections $b_f$.
  • For $i=2,\ldots,s$, increase $p_i$ by $a$ and decrease $r_i$ by $a$.

The first transition is feasible. At $u_1$, the increase and decrease cancel; at $v_1$, the additional rejections prevent quota violation. All remaining changes are decreases.

Before transition $i\geq2$, vertex $v_i$ has lost $a$ on $r_{i-1}$ and has received no increase. It therefore has at least $a$ unused quota. At $u_i$, the increase and decrease cancel, while any earlier additional rejection could only have lowered its total. Each $r_i$ still has its original allocation, because no additional rejection edge belongs to the path. Thus all transitions preserve nonnegativity, capacities and quotas.

Every decrease is authorized by an endpoint ranking the increased edge above the decreased edge. The lemma in Section 3 proves legality of every transition.

The resulting allocation is exactly the simultaneous path endpoint.

5. Exact expansion of a cycle augmentation

Consider a simple alternating cycle
$$ v_1,u_1,v_2,u_2,\ldots,v_s,u_s,v_1, $$
where $s\geq2$. Define
$$ p_i=u_iv_i,\qquad r_i=u_iv_{i+1}, \qquad v_{s+1}=v_1. $$
Again, $p_1$ blocks $x$, each $u_i$ prefers $p_i$ to $r_i$, and
$$ 0<a\leq x_{r_i},\qquad a\leq K_{p_i}-x_{p_i}. $$

Let $y$ be the simultaneous endpoint:
$$ y_{p_i}=x_{p_i}+a,\qquad y_{r_i}=x_{r_i}-a, $$
with all other coordinates unchanged. This allocation is feasible: every cycle vertex receives and loses the same amount.

5.1 Initialization

Put
$$ s_1=Q_{v_1}-x(v_1). $$

If $s_1>0$, choose
$$ \varepsilon=\frac12\min\{a,s_1\}, $$
and make no temporary rejection at $v_1$.

If $s_1=0$, blocking of $p_1$ guarantees an incident edge $f$ with $x_f>0$ that $v_1$ ranks below $p_1$. Choose one and put
$$ \varepsilon=\frac12\min\{a,x_f\}. $$
The only cycle edges incident to $v_1$ are $p_1$ and $r_s$. Consequently, either $f=r_s$ or $f$ is outside the cycle.

In both cases,
$$ 0<\varepsilon<a. $$

Make the first transition:

  • increase $p_1$ by $\varepsilon$;
  • decrease $r_1$ by $a$;
  • when $s_1=0$, decrease $f$ by $\varepsilon$.

At $u_1$, the total decreases by $a-\varepsilon$. At $v_1$, either unused quota accommodates the increase or the decrease on $f$ offsets it. All other affected totals decrease. Coordinate bounds hold by the choice of $\varepsilon$.

The transition is therefore feasible, and every decrease is permitted. Section 3 proves its legality.

5.2 Propagate around the cycle

For $i=2,\ldots,s$, increase $p_i$ by $a$ and decrease $r_i$ by $a$, with one exception: if the initialization decreased $f=r_s$, the last transition decreases $r_s$ by only $a-\varepsilon$.

Before transition $i$, vertex $v_i$ has lost $a$ on $r_{i-1}$, so it has at least $a$ free quota. Each proposal has its original allocation before its increase, hence has enough residual capacity.

Normally the changes at $u_i$ cancel. If initialization decreased an external edge incident to $u_i$, that only gives additional slack. In the exceptional case $f=r_s$, vertex $u_s$ previously lost $\varepsilon$, and the last transition restores its total by adding $a$ and subtracting $a-\varepsilon$.

Each refusal has sufficient allocation: it is unchanged before its transition, except that $r_s$ may already have lost $\varepsilon$, leaving at least $a-\varepsilon$.

Thus every transition is feasible. All decreases are on the corresponding $r_i$, which its active endpoint ranks below $p_i$. Section 3 again proves legality.

5.3 Complete the first proposal

Increase $p_1$ by $a-\varepsilon$, without any decrease.

Vertex $u_1$ has at least this much unused quota from initialization. At $v_1$, its current total is:

$$ x(v_1)+\varepsilon-a \quad\text{if initialization used unused quota}, $$

$$ x(v_1)-(a-\varepsilon) \quad\text{if initialization decreased }r_s, $$

or

$$ x(v_1)-a \quad\text{if initialization decreased an external }f. $$

In every case, $v_1$ has at least $a-\varepsilon$ free quota. The final value on $p_1$ is $x_{p_1}+a\leq K_{p_1}$. Hence this transition is feasible and legal.

5.4 Restore an external temporary refusal

If initialization used unused quota or decreased $r_s$, the allocation now equals $y$.

Otherwise it equals $y$ except that the external edge $f$ has allocation $y_f-\varepsilon$. Restore $f$ by increasing it by $\varepsilon$, with no decreases.

Both endpoints have totals exactly $\varepsilon$ below their totals in the feasible allocation $y$. They therefore each have at least $\varepsilon$ unused quota. The restoration also respects the capacity of $f$. It is feasible and, by Section 3, a legal better-response move.

We have reached exactly $y$ in at most $s+2$ individual moves.

5.5 Check against the verifier’s example

In that example, choose $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$ and temporarily reject $\tfrac12$ from $j_3m_1$. The moves are:

  • Increase $j_1m_1$ by $\tfrac12$, decrease $j_1m_2$ by $1$, and decrease $j_3m_1$ by $\tfrac12$.
  • Increase $j_2m_2$ by $1$ and decrease $j_2m_1$ by $1$.
  • Increase $j_1m_1$ by $\tfrac12$.
  • Restore $j_3m_1$ by $\tfrac12$.

Before the last move, both $j_3$ and $m_1$ have unused quota $\tfrac12$. The restoration therefore blocks and reaches the accelerated stable endpoint exactly.

The verifier correctly rejected the three-move order; the additional restoration repairs it.

6. Applying the expansions in both phases

In the first phase, Sections 4–5 apply directly.

For the auxiliary second phase, the dummy initialization is feasible:
$$ 0\leq Q_m-x(m)\leq Q_m\leq K_{dm}, $$
and
$$ \sum_m x_{dm}\leq\sum_m Q_m=Q_d. $$
It changes no original coordinate.

After the sides are swapped, machines are the active vertices. A proposal must be preferred by its active machine to its worst positively allocated edge. Since the dummy edge is ranked last, no dummy edge can be a proposal.

It follows that:

  • every proposal increased by our expansions is an original edge;
  • the starting passive vertex is an original job;
  • cycles contain no dummy vertex;
  • an external edge restored by the cycle expansion is incident to an original job and therefore is also an original edge.

Dummy edges may decrease during a path expansion. Project each transition onto the original edges. Projection preserves feasibility, and every remaining decrease is permitted by the unchanged original preferences. Each projected transition still increases an original edge positively. Section 3 therefore proves that it is a legal move in the original instance.

The definition of a move is symmetric in its two endpoints apart from initiation. For every projected transition, designate the original job endpoint as the initiator.

Each expanded augmentation reaches its accelerated endpoint exactly. Consequently, the expansions concatenate without changing the subsequent accelerated computation. Its final projection is stable by the supplied constructive theorem.

This establishes a finite better-response path. We next establish polynomial binary complexity.

7. Rational encoding and binary running time

7.1 Common denominator and magnitude bounds

Computing $Q_t=\mu_tq_t$ preserves polynomial encoding length: multiplication adds the numerator and denominator bit lengths.

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators of all transformed quotas, capacities and initial allocation coordinates. Then
$$ \log_2 D=O(L), $$
and all these quantities belong to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

Set
$$ W=\max\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{Q_t:t\in J\cup M\} \cup\{K_e:e\in E_T\}\bigr). $$
Its binary magnitude satisfies $\log_2 W=O(L)$. Auxiliary edge capacities are at most $W$, and the dummy quota is at most $mW$.

The accelerated numerical operations preserve $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. The additional path rejections can be computed by successively taking the minimum of the remaining rejection amount and the allocation on a permitted edge. These operations also preserve that lattice.

All allocation coordinates are bounded by their capacities. Sums used in quota and augmentation calculations are bounded by a polynomial in $\tau$ times $W$. Thus their integer numerators over denominator $D$ have
$$ O\bigl(L+\log(\tau+1)\bigr) $$
bits.

7.2 The cycle repair causes no denominator accumulation

Each cycle initialization chooses
$$ \varepsilon=\frac12\min\{a,s_1\} \quad\text{or}\quad \varepsilon=\frac12\min\{a,x_f\}. $$
Thus its intermediate allocations and move quantities belong to $(2D)^{-1}\mathbb Z$.

Crucially, the repair reaches the exact accelerated endpoint, which belongs to $D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. The next augmentation starts on that original lattice. Successive cycle repairs therefore do not accumulate additional powers of two in their denominators.

Every output quantity consequently has polynomial binary encoding length.

7.3 Polynomial number of moves

The auxiliary graph has at most
$$ n'=\tau+1,\qquad h'=|E_T|+m $$
vertices and edges, respectively. The known construction uses $O(n'h')$ augmentations. Each expansion uses $O(n')$ individual moves. Hence the total number of moves is
$$ O\!\left((\tau+1)^2(|E_T|+m)\right). $$

The accelerated computation and the expansions require polynomially many arithmetic operations and comparisons on numbers of polynomial bit length. They therefore run in polynomial binary time. Outputting each increased edge, its increment, and the permitted decreases also takes polynomial time.

This proves polynomial-time construction of the requested rational sequence. The guarantee concerns a constructible path; it does not assert convergence under every possible choice of moves.
