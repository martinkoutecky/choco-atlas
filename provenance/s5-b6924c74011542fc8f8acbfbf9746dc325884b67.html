The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an obstacle \(o\), traversal factor \(k\in[0,1)\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over locations in \([0,o)\cup(o,1]\), determine the smallest \(\rho\) for which a deterministic rule \(F\) maps every such society to a feasible pathway \((a,b)\), is mass-GSP, and satisfies \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(F(\mu))\le \rho\min_{(a,b)}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(a,b)\). Mass-GSP means no positive-mass coalition can change its reports within its members’ regions so that every member strictly lowers its true-location cost.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix public binary rationals \(o\in(0,1)\) and \(k\in[0,1)\). Facilities are fixed at \(0\) and \(1\); feasible pathways have real endpoints \((a,b)\in[0,o)\times(o,1]\).
A society is given by its support: \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\delta_{x_i}\), with distinct rational locations \(x_i\in[0,o)\cup(o,1]\), positive binary rational masses \(w_i\), and \(\sum_iw_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Either region may have zero mass. State running times in \(\tau\) and total input bit length \(L\), including the encodings of \(o,k\); the number of facilities is fixed at two.
Define \(c(x;a,b)=\min\{x,|x-a|+k(b-a)+1-b\}\) for \(x<o\), and \(c(x;a,b)=\min\{1-x,|x-b|+k(b-a)+a\}\) for \(x>o\). Let \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(a,b)=\sum_iw_i c(x_i;a,b)\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{(a,b)\in[0,o)\times(o,1]}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(a,b)\).
A deterministic rule \(F\) maps every such society to a feasible pathway and observes only reported locations and masses. Region membership and mass are public and cannot be changed.
A coalition may select rational submasses \(s_i\in[0,w_i]\), with \(\sum_i s_i>0\), and redistribute each selected submass among finitely many rational reports in its original region, using rational amounts. Unselected mass retains its report. The rule is mass-GSP if no such deviation to \(\mu'\) satisfies \(c(x_i;F(\mu'))<c(x_i;F(\mu))\) for every \(i\) with \(s_i>0\).

What is the optimal deterministic mass-GSP social-cost approximation factor \(\rho^*(o,k)=\inf\{\rho\geq1:\exists F\text{ mass-GSP},\ \forall\mu,\ \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(F(\mu))\leq\rho\,\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\}\), allowing \(\rho^*(o,k)=+\infty\)? If finite, is the infimum attained?

For discrete societies of \(n\) agents, TwoExtreme is GSP and guarantees social-cost factor \(\frac{n}{1+k(n-1)}\), uniformly over obstacles and profiles; this factor is tight for that mechanism. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08488v1#S4.SS2).
No result settling the exact mass-GSP problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one; support size is unrestricted.
  • Required obstacle, traversal factor, support locations, and masses to have binary rational encodings; feasible output endpoints remain real.
  • Made coalition deviations precise by allowing rational partial masses and finite rational splitting among same-region reports, with mass conserved.
  • Preserved the discrete model's public region membership and allowed either region to be empty.
  • Interpreted the given obstacle and traversal factor as fixed public parameters, so the optimal factor may depend on both.
  • Replaced the potentially nonexistent smallest factor by an infimum and explicitly asked whether it is attained; the approximation inequality also applies when the optimum is zero.

1. Proven conclusions

The exact positive-$k$ optimum and its attainment remain unresolved. The following results give a sharper upper bound and a lower bound that diverges as $k$ tends to zero.

Define
$$ D=\max\{o,1-o\},\qquad q=\frac{k}{1+k}, $$
and, for $0<k<1$, define
$$ H=k+(1-k)D,\qquad P=(1+k)^2o(1-o), $$
$$ V(o,k)= \max\left\{ \frac{H}{k(2-H)}, 1, \frac{P-k^2}{k(1-P)} \right\}. $$

We prove
$$ \rho^*(o,0)=+\infty, $$
and, for $0<k<1$,
$$ 1<\rho^*(o,k)\leq V(o,k)<\frac1k, $$
together with the quantitative lower bound
$$ \rho^*(o,k)\geq \left(\frac{D}{120k}\right)^{1/3}. $$

The upper bound is achieved by an explicit deterministic mass-GSP rule. Its exact worst-case approximation factor is $V(o,k)$. In particular, at the central obstacle,
$$ V\left(\frac12,k\right)=\frac{1+3k}{k(3+k)}. $$

This identifies the exact factor of the constructed rule, not the optimum over all mass-GSP rules.

2. Cost geometry

For a region of length $d$, measure a location by its distance $t\in[0,d)$ from its original facility. For any feasible pathway, the cost in that region has the form
$$ c(t)=\min\{t,|t-z|+h\}, \qquad z\in[0,d),\qquad h\geq k(1-z). $$

On the left, $z=a$ and
$$ h=k(b-a)+1-b =k(1-a)+(1-k)(1-b). $$
On the right, reflection gives $z=1-b$ and
$$ h=k(b-a)+a =k(1-z)+(1-k)a. $$

Individual minimum costs

The function $|t-z|+k(1-z)$ has slope $-(1+k)$ for $z<t$ and slope $1-k$ for $z>t$. Its minimum is therefore $k(1-t)$, attained at $z=t$ with the opposite endpoint at its facility. Consequently,
$$ \min_{(a,b)}c(t;a,b)=m(t):=\min\{t,k(1-t)\}. $$

Thus:

  • If $t\leq q$, every feasible pathway gives cost $t$.
  • If $t>q$, the individual minimum is $k(1-t)<t$.

Call a location active when $t>q$. An inactive type cannot belong to a strictly improving coalition.

For fixed $z,h$, the improvement over the direct route is
$$ t-|t-z|-h= \begin{cases} 2t-z-h,&t\leq z,\\ z-h,&t\geq z. \end{cases} $$
This is nondecreasing in $t$. Hence, within one region, strict benefit at a location implies strict benefit at every larger distance.

A pathway cannot strictly benefit both regions

Strict benefit at a left location $x$ implies
$$ x>|x-a|+k(b-a)+1-b \geq x+1-(1+k)a-(1-k)b, $$
so
$$ (1+k)a+(1-k)b>1. $$

Strict benefit at a right location $y$ implies
$$ 1-y>|y-b|+k(b-a)+a \geq 1-y+(1-k)a+(1+k)b-1, $$
so
$$ (1-k)a+(1+k)b<1. $$

The second left-hand side exceeds the first by $2k(b-a)\geq0$. The two strict inequalities are incompatible.

In particular, every strictly improving coalition lies in one region: its members’ new costs must all be strictly below their direct-route costs.

The social-cost optimum exists

If a pathway strictly benefits some left type, every right type has its direct-route cost. Replacing $b$ by $1$ decreases every left pathway-route cost by $(1-k)(1-b)$, while right actual costs cannot increase. The reflected replacement $a=0$ applies when a right type benefits. If nobody benefits, every actual cost is already its direct-route cost.

Therefore an optimum can be sought among pathways $(a,1)$ and $(0,b)$.

Let $r$ be the largest left support location, or $0$ if that region is empty. For $a>r$, each left pathway-route cost is
$$ a-x+k(1-a), $$
which increases with $a$. Thus the first family can be restricted to $a\in[0,r]$. Similarly, if $\ell$ is the smallest right support location, or $1$ if empty, the second family can be restricted to $b\in[\ell,1]$.

These intervals are compact and contained in the feasible domain. Social cost is continuous on them, so its minimum is attained.

A Lipschitz consequence of mass-GSP

Fix positive rational masses $\lambda,\theta$ summing to $1$ and a rational location $r$ in one region. Put
$$ \mu_s=\lambda\delta_s+\theta\delta_r, \qquad g(s)=c(s;F(\mu_s)). $$

Moving the selected mass $\lambda$ from $s$ to $t$ is allowed. Mass-GSP implies
$$ g(s)\leq c(s;F(\mu_t)). $$
For a fixed pathway, both route costs are $1$-Lipschitz within a region; their minimum is also $1$-Lipschitz. Hence
$$ g(s)\leq g(t)+|s-t|. $$
Reversing the deviation proves
$$ |g(s)-g(t)|\leq |s-t|. $$

This also holds when $s=r$ or $t=r$: the merged atom permits selection of the submass $\lambda$. Thus $g$, initially defined at rational locations, extends continuously to any closed interval inside the region.

3. A sharper mass-GSP rule

Suppose $0<k<1$. Write
$$ d=o,\qquad e=1-o, $$
and define
$$ \gamma_L=\max\{0,(1+k)d-k\}, \qquad \gamma_R=\max\{0,(1+k)e-k\}. $$

Let $A_L,A_R$ be the reported active masses in the two regions:
$$ A_L=\sum_{\substack{x_i<o\\x_i>q}}w_i, \qquad A_R=\sum_{\substack{x_i>o\\1-x_i>q}}w_i. $$

Define the rule $F_{\mathrm{act}}$ as follows.

  • If both scores $\gamma_LA_L$ and $\gamma_RA_R$ vanish, output $(0,1)$.
  • Otherwise, choose the region with the larger score, breaking ties toward the left.
  • In the chosen region, place its endpoint at its largest active reported distance from its facility. Place the opposite endpoint at its facility.

Thus a left choice outputs $(r,1)$, where $r$ is the largest active left report. A right choice outputs $(0,1-r)$, where $r$ is the largest active distance from $1$.

Positive active mass in a region implies its length exceeds $q$, and therefore its coefficient $\gamma$ is positive. Every endpoint selected by the rule is feasible.

Proof of mass-GSP

Suppose a deviation strictly improves every selected type.

Every selected true type must be active. Moreover, every selected type must belong to the region served by the new outcome: all types in the other region receive their direct-route costs, which cannot be strictly below their old costs.

Let this improving coalition lie in region $j$. Its deviation cannot increase that region’s active mass. Indeed, all selected true mass was already active, while its redistributed reports can place at most that same mass at active locations. The other region’s active mass is unchanged.

If region $j$ was previously unserved, lowering or preserving its score cannot make it win against the unchanged score of the previously served region. The fixed tie-breaking rule preserves this conclusion. Thus an improving coalition must remain in the previously served region.

Let $r,r'$ be its largest active reported distances before and after the deviation. Since the opposite endpoint is fixed at its facility, a true distance $t$ has pathway-route cost
$$ |t-r|+k(1-r). $$

If $r'\geq r$, every true coalition location satisfies $t\leq r$, and its pathway-route cost changes by
$$ (r'-t)+k(1-r')-\bigl((r-t)+k(1-r)\bigr) =(1-k)(r'-r)\geq0. $$
Its actual cost therefore cannot strictly decrease.

If $r'<r$, all mass originally at $r$ must have been selected; otherwise some positive unselected amount would preserve the extreme report $r$. The selected true type at $r$ has its pathway-route cost increased by
$$ (r-r')+k(1-r')-k(1-r) =(1+k)(r-r')>0. $$
It cannot strictly improve either.

If the deviation removes all active reports from the chosen region, that region becomes unserved or the rule outputs $(0,1)$; its selected types then receive their direct-route costs and cannot strictly improve.

This proves mass-GSP under arbitrary allowed submass selection and splitting.

4. Exact approximation factor of the constructed rule

Serving the same region as an optimum

For $d>q$, define
$$ H_d=k+(1-k)d, \qquad M(d)=\frac{H_d}{k(2-H_d)}. $$

If the rule serves a region of length $d$ with extreme report $r<d$, then, for every reported distance $t\leq r$,
$$ c(t;F_{\mathrm{act}}) =\min\{t,r-t+k(1-r)\} \leq\min\{t,H_d-t\}. $$

For $t\leq q$, its cost equals $m(t)=t$. For $t>q$, division by the individual minimum gives
$$ \frac{c(t;F_{\mathrm{act}})}{m(t)} \leq \frac{\min\{t,H_d-t\}}{k(1-t)}. $$

The breakpoint satisfies
$$ q<\frac{H_d}{2}<d. $$
Indeed,
$$ \frac{H_d}{2}-q=\frac{1-k}{2}(d-q), \qquad d-\frac{H_d}{2}=\frac{1+k}{2}(d-q). $$

The ratio increases before $H_d/2$. After that point,
$$ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{H_d-t}{k(1-t)} =\frac{H_d-1}{k(1-t)^2}<0. $$
Its maximum is therefore $M(d)$.

If an optimum strictly benefits the region chosen by the rule, the other region has direct-route costs under both outcomes. Summing the preceding pointwise bound yields a social-cost ratio at most $M(d)\leq M(D)$.

If the optimum benefits nobody, the rule’s social cost is at most the optimum.

Serving the opposite region from an optimum

Suppose the rule chooses a region of length $d$, with active mass $A$, and leaves a region of length $e=1-d$ unserved, with active mass $B>0$.

Assume its selection condition guarantees
$$ A\geq hB $$
for some $h>0$. Let $T$ be the direct-route social cost of the opposite region’s active mass. Let $Z$ be the direct-route cost of the chosen region’s active mass plus the cost of all inactive mass. Then
$$ T\leq eB,\qquad Z\geq qA\geq hqB. $$

If the optimum benefits the opposite region, the chosen region has direct-route costs under that optimum. Individual minimum costs in the opposite region give
$$ \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)\geq Z+k(B-T). $$
Since the rule’s costs never exceed direct-route costs,
$$ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(F_{\mathrm{act}}(\mu))\leq Z+T. $$

Also $T>qB$, because all mass counted in $T$ is active. Therefore
$$ \frac{Z+T}{Z+k(B-T)} $$
decreases with $Z$ and increases with $T$. Substituting the preceding bounds proves
$$ \frac{\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F_{\mathrm{act}}(\mu))} {\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)} \leq X(e,h):=\frac{hq+e}{hq+k(1-e)}. $$

Balancing the two regional penalties

When both region lengths exceed $q$, put
$$ \eta=\frac{e-kd}{d-ke}>0. $$
The rule chooses left exactly when $A_L\geq\eta A_R$. Thus the two opposite-region penalties are
$$ X(e,\eta),\qquad X(d,1/\eta). $$

Direct substitution, using $d+e=1$ and $q=k/(1+k)$, gives
$$ X(e,\eta)=X(d,1/\eta) =\frac{(1+k)^2de-k^2}{k\bigl(1-(1+k)^2de\bigr)} =\frac{P-k^2}{k(1-P)}. $$

If one region has length at most $q$, every type there is inactive. The rule serves the other region whenever active mass exists, so only the $M(D)$ bound is needed.

These cases prove the stated guarantee $V(o,k)$.

Sharpness for this rule

First consider the region of length $D$. Put mass tending to $1$ at
$$ t=\frac{H}{2} $$
and positive mass tending to $0$ at rational distances $r$ tending to $D$. Use dyadic masses, and put no mass in the other region.

The rule selects endpoint $r$. Its social cost tends to $t$. The individual-minimum lower bound and the feasible pathway with endpoint $t$ show that the optimum tends to $k(1-t)$. Consequently the approximation ratio tends to
$$ \frac{t}{k(1-t)}=M(D). $$

When both regions are active, choose dyadic masses $A_m,B_m$ summing to $1$ such that
$$ \frac{A_m}{B_m}\longrightarrow\eta, \qquad A_m\geq\eta B_m. $$
Place the left mass at rational distances $s_m\downarrow q$ and the right mass at rational distances $u_m\uparrow e$. The rule chooses left, and its cost is
$$ A_m k(1-s_m)+B_m u_m. $$
Serving the right atom instead gives social cost
$$ A_m s_m+B_m k(1-u_m). $$
Thus the ratio to the optimum is at least the ratio of these two expressions, which tends to $X(e,\eta)$.

Both constructions use legal societies. They establish that the exact worst-case factor of $F_{\mathrm{act}}$ is $V(o,k)$.

More generally, within the family that chooses left when $A_L\geq\eta A_R$ and then uses the chosen region’s extreme, the exact factor is
$$ \max\{M(D),X(e,\eta),X(d,1/\eta)\}. $$
For $e>q$, $X(e,\eta)$ strictly decreases with $\eta$; for $d>q$, $X(d,1/\eta)$ strictly increases. Their equality at the displayed $\eta$ therefore minimizes the factor within this family.

Comparison with the previous upper bound

The previous long-region extreme bound was
$$ U(o,k)= \max\left\{ M(D),1,\frac{1-D}{kD} \right\}. $$

If the shorter region is inactive, $V=U=M(D)$. Otherwise, writing $d=D$ and $e=1-D$,
$$ X(e,\eta)<\frac{e}{kd}. $$
Hence $V\leq U$, with strict improvement whenever the previous bound is dominated by $e/(kd)$ rather than $M(D)$.

Also $H<1$, so $M(D)<1/k$. Finally,
$$ 2P\leq\frac{(1+k)^2}{2}<1+k^2, $$
which implies
$$ \frac{P-k^2}{k(1-P)}<\frac1k. $$
Thus $V(o,k)<1/k$ for every fixed obstacle, including $o=1/2$.

At $o=1/2$,
$$ M(D)=\frac{1+k}{k(3-k)}, \qquad \frac{P-k^2}{k(1-P)}=\frac{1+3k}{k(3+k)}. $$
The second quantity exceeds the first by
$$ \frac{4(1-k)}{9-k^2}>0, $$
proving the central-obstacle formula stated above.

5. A quantitative impossibility argument

We now prove both the zero-$k$ impossibility and the cube-root lower bound.

Work entirely in a region of length $D$, reflecting the problem if necessary. Suppose a mass-GSP rule has a finite approximation factor $\rho\geq1$.

Fee bound when every type benefits

Suppose every support location strictly benefits from an output represented by
$$ c(t)=|t-z|+h<t. $$
Then
$$ h\leq \rho k(1-z)\leq\rho k. $$

To prove this, strict benefit implies $z>h\geq0$, so $z\in(0,D)$. If instead
$$ h>\rho k(1-z), $$
choose a rational $u\in(0,D)$ sufficiently close to $z$ that
$$ |u-z|+\rho k(1-u)<h. $$

The homogeneous society $\delta_u$ has optimum at most $k(1-u)$, hence
$$ c(u;F(\delta_u))\leq\rho k(1-u). $$
By the fixed-pathway Lipschitz property, every original true location satisfies
$$ \begin{aligned} c(t;F(\delta_u)) &\leq |t-u|+\rho k(1-u)\\ &\leq |t-z|+|z-u|+\rho k(1-u)\\ &<|t-z|+h. \end{aligned} $$
The whole society can report $u$, strictly improving every type. This contradicts mass-GSP and proves the fee bound.

Construction of the contradiction

Fix rational $r\in(0,D)$. Assume
$$ \rho^3k<\frac r{120}. $$

Choose a dyadic rational $\theta$ such that
$$ \frac1{3\rho+1}<\theta<\frac1{2\rho+1}. $$
Set
$$ \lambda=1-\theta,\qquad p=\frac{\lambda}{\theta}. $$
Then
$$ 2\rho<p<3\rho, \qquad \lambda-\rho\theta=\frac{p-\rho}{p+1}>\frac13. $$

Choose rational $\varepsilon$ satisfying
$$ \frac r{4\rho p}<\varepsilon<\frac r{3\rho p}. $$
The choices imply
$$ \varepsilon<\frac r6, \qquad \varepsilon>\frac r{12\rho^2}, \qquad \rho k<\frac{\varepsilon}{10}. $$

Consider
$$ \mu_s=\lambda\delta_s+\theta\delta_r, \qquad g(s)=c(s;F(\mu_s)). $$

At $\mu_\varepsilon$, the pathway with regional endpoint $r$ and opposite endpoint at its facility has social cost at most
$$ \lambda\varepsilon+\theta k(1-r). $$
Therefore
$$ c(r;F(\mu_\varepsilon)) \leq \rho\bigl(p\varepsilon+k(1-r)\bigr) <\frac r3+\frac{\varepsilon}{10} <\frac r2. $$

Writing that output’s regional parameters as $z,h$, this forces $z>r/2>2\varepsilon$. Thus the type at $\varepsilon$ uses its direct route:
$$ g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon. $$

At the merged homogeneous society $\mu_r=\delta_r$,
$$ g(r)\leq\rho k(1-r)<\frac{\varepsilon}{10}. $$
The Lipschitz extension of $g$, the intermediate value theorem, and rational density give a rational $s\in(\varepsilon,r)$ with
$$ \frac{9\varepsilon}{10}<g(s)<\frac{19\varepsilon}{20}. $$

In particular, the type at $s$ strictly benefits. Monotonicity of regional benefit implies that the type at $r$ also strictly benefits. The fee bound therefore applies to $F(\mu_s)$:
$$ h\leq\rho k<\frac{\varepsilon}{10}. $$
Put
$$ E=|s-z|=g(s)-h. $$
Then
$$ E>\frac{4\varepsilon}{5}, \qquad E<s, \qquad z>h. $$

Choose positive rational $\delta$ sufficiently small that
$$ \delta<\frac{z-h}{3}, \qquad \delta<\frac E2, \qquad (2\lambda+\rho\theta)\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{15}. $$
Choose rational $u\in(0,D)$ with $|u-z|<\delta$. These inequalities ensure
$$ u>h+\delta,\qquad u\neq s. $$

Now consider
$$ \nu=\lambda\delta_s+\theta\delta_u. $$
The mass at $u$ can report $r$, recreating $\mu_s$. Mass-GSP implies
$$ c(u;F(\nu)) \leq c(u;F(\mu_s)) \leq |u-z|+h<h+\delta<u. $$

Thus the type at $u$ uses the pathway under $F(\nu)$. If that output has parameters $z',h'$, then
$$ |u-z'|+h'<h+\delta, $$
and consequently
$$ |z'-z|<h+2\delta. $$
Since $h'\geq0$,
$$ c(s;F(\nu)) \geq \min\{s,E-h-2\delta\} =E-h-2\delta. $$
Hence
$$ \mathrm{SC}_\nu(F(\nu)) \geq\lambda(E-h-2\delta). $$

The pathway with regional endpoint $s$ and opposite endpoint at its facility gives
$$ \mathrm{OPT}(\nu) \leq k(1-s)+\theta|u-s| \leq k+\theta(E+\delta). $$

Finally,
$$ \begin{aligned} &\lambda(E-h-2\delta)-\rho\bigl(k+\theta(E+\delta)\bigr)\\ &\quad=(\lambda-\rho\theta)E-\lambda h-\rho k -(2\lambda+\rho\theta)\delta\\ &\quad>\frac{4\varepsilon}{15} -\frac{\varepsilon}{5} -\frac{\varepsilon}{15}\\ &\quad=0. \end{aligned} $$
This contradicts the approximation guarantee.

Therefore every finite factor satisfies
$$ \rho^3k\geq\frac r{120} $$
for every rational $r<D$. Letting $r$ increase to $D$ proves
$$ \rho\geq\left(\frac{D}{120k}\right)^{1/3} $$
when $k>0$.

When $k=0$, the contradiction applies to every finite $\rho$. Thus
$$ \rho^*(o,0)=+\infty. $$

6. An explicit lower bound strictly above one

For completeness, the strict positive-$k$ lower bound from the notes can also be proved self-containedly.

Define
$$ \theta=\frac{1-k}{4},\qquad \lambda=1-\theta, $$
$$ \alpha=\frac{(1+k)\theta(D-q)}{5+k}, \qquad s_* =q+\theta(D-q)+\alpha, $$
and
$$ B(o,k)= 1+\frac{(1+k)\alpha} {k(1-s_*)+\theta(D-s_*)}. $$
We prove $\rho^*(o,k)\geq B(o,k)>1$.

Choose rational $r\in(q,D)$ and consider
$$ \mu_s=\lambda\delta_s+\theta\delta_r, \qquad q<s<r. $$
Two feasible pathway choices give
$$ \mathrm{OPT}(\mu_s)\leq\min\{S(s),R(s)\}, $$
where
$$ S(s)=k(1-s)+\theta(r-s), \qquad R(s)=\lambda s+\theta k(1-r). $$
The endpoint $s$ gives exactly $S(s)$, because both types strictly benefit. The endpoint $r$ gives cost at most $R(s)$.

Let $g(s)=c(s;F(\mu_s))$.

Conditional social-cost bound

If the type at $s$ strictly benefits, so does the type at $r$. Write
$$ h=k(1-z)+v,\qquad v\geq0. $$
The actual social cost is then
$$ \lambda|s-z|+\theta|r-z|+k(1-z)+v. $$

The following calculations compare it with $S(s)$:

| Endpoint position | $\mathrm{SC}-S(s)$ | $g(s)-k(1-s)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $z\leq s$ | $(1+k)(s-z)+v$ | $(1+k)(s-z)+v$ |
| $s\leq z\leq r$ | $\frac{1-k}{2}(z-s)+v$ | $(1-k)(z-s)+v$ |
| $z\geq r$ | $(1-k)(z-s)-2\theta(r-s)+v$ | $(1-k)(z-s)+v$ |

The first two rows show directly that
$$ \mathrm{SC}\geq S(s)+\frac12\bigl(g(s)-k(1-s)\bigr). $$
For the third row, subtracting half the individual excess gives
$$ \frac{1-k}{2}(z-r)+\frac v2\geq0, $$
because $2\theta=(1-k)/2$. Thus the same inequality holds in every case.

Two nearby profiles

Put
$$ s_0=q+\theta(r-q),\qquad G=(1+k)\theta(r-q),\qquad \beta=\frac{G}{5+k}, $$
$$ s_-=s_0-\beta,\qquad s_+=s_0+\beta,\qquad N=(1+k)\beta. $$
Since
$$ \frac{\beta}{\theta(r-q)}=\frac{1+k}{5+k}<\frac13, \qquad \theta<\frac14, $$
we have
$$ q<s_-<s_+<r. $$

Direct calculation gives
$$ S(s)-R(s)=(1+k)(s_0-s), $$
so
$$ S(s_-)=R(s_-)+N, \qquad R(s_+)=S(s_+)+N. $$
Moreover,
$$ S(s_+)-R(s_-) =\bigl(\lambda-k-\theta\bigr)\beta =\frac{1-k}{2}\beta>0. $$

Suppose the rule guarantees
$$ \rho<1+\frac{N}{S(s_+)}. $$
Then
$$ \rho R(s_-)<S(s_-). $$
If the type at $s_-$ strictly benefited, the conditional bound would give social cost at least $S(s_-)$, contradicting the guarantee. Hence
$$ g(s_-)=s_-. $$

At $s_+$, non-benefit would give
$$ \mathrm{SC}\geq\lambda s_++\theta k(1-r) =R(s_+) >S(s_+)\rho. $$
Thus this type strictly benefits, and the conditional bound yields
$$ g(s_+) \leq k(1-s_+)+2(\rho-1)S(s_+) <k(1-s_+)+2N. $$

Lipschitz continuity, however, gives
$$ g(s_+)\geq g(s_-)-(s_+-s_-)=s_0-3\beta. $$
The definitions imply
$$ \begin{aligned} s_0-3\beta-k(1-s_+) &=G-(3-k)\beta\\ &=2(1+k)\beta\\ &=2N, \end{aligned} $$
contradicting the preceding strict upper bound.

Therefore
