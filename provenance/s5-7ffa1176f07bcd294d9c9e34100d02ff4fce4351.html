The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a nondegenerate, explicitly listed domain \(D\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\) and a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\), determine whether there are rational quotas \(q_j\in(0,1]\) such that, for every \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\), the judgment \(A_q(\nu)\) belongs to \(D\), where \(p_j(\nu)=\sum_{t\in D:t_j=1}\nu_t\) and \(A_q(\nu)_j=1\) exactly when \(p_j(\nu)\ge q_j\); if so, output such quotas and \(A_q(\mu)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given an explicitly listed domain \(D\subseteq\{0,1\}^m\), with \(m\ge1\) and \(d=|D|\), assume nondegeneracy: \(\{t_j:t\in D\}=\{0,1\}\) for every issue \(j\).
A society \(\mu\) is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types in \(D\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For any real probability distribution \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\), put \(p_j(\nu)=\sum_{t\in D:t_j=1}\nu_t\). For rational quotas \(q\in(0,1]^m\), define \(A_q(\nu)_j=1\) exactly when \(p_j(\nu)\ge q_j\); equality therefore yields acceptance.
The problem is to decide whether some such \(q\) satisfies \(A_q(\nu)\in D\) for every \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\), and, when it does, produce \(q\) and \(A_q(\mu)\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? In the polynomial-time case, can the requested quotas and judgment also be produced in time polynomial in \(m,d,\tau,L\)?

For finite electorates, recognition of domains admitting a unanimous issuewise aggregator with no coordinate function equal to any voter projection, for some arity at least \(2\), takes \(O(d^2m^2)\) time and yields a defining formula with \(O(dm)\) clauses. Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). Whenever admissible quotas exist, rational quotas \(q\) and the judgment \(A_q(\mu)\) can be produced in time polynomial in \(m,d,\tau,L\).

The proof constructs a polynomial-size description of \(D\) by minimal clauses. Each such clause imposes one linear inequality on the quotas. The acceptance-at-equality convention determines which inequalities must be strict.

1. A polynomial-size description by minimal clauses

Let \(x_1,\ldots,x_m\) be Boolean variables. A clause is a disjunction of literals on distinct coordinates. It is valid on \(D\) if every \(t\in D\) satisfies it, and minimal valid if deleting any literal makes it invalid on \(D\).

We first construct a conjunctive formula defining exactly \(D\).

Build the binary prefix trie of the listed vectors in \(D\). For every occurring prefix \(a_1\cdots a_{k-1}\) whose extension \(a_1\cdots a_k\) does not occur, introduce the clause

\(\displaystyle C_a=\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\ell_i,\qquad \ell_i=x_i\ \text{if }a_i=0,\quad \ell_i=\neg x_i\ \text{if }a_i=1.\)

This clause forbids precisely the assignments extending that missing prefix.

Every vector in \(D\) satisfies every constructed clause. Conversely, any vector outside \(D\) has a first missing prefix and therefore violates its associated clause. Thus the conjunction defines exactly \(D\).

The trie has at most \(1+dm\) nodes. Each node has at most two missing children, so there are \(O(dm)\) clauses, each containing at most \(m\) literals.

Now minimize each clause by deleting a literal whenever the remaining clause is still valid on \(D\). Validity can be tested by scanning the listed domain.

One pass over the literals suffices. Indeed, if deleting a literal fails a validity test, some \(t\in D\) falsifies all the other literals then present. That same \(t\) continues to falsify any subset of those other literals after further deletions. Consequently, that literal remains indispensable.

Replacing a clause by its minimized version preserves the domain described by the entire formula:

  • Every vector in \(D\) satisfies the minimized clause.
  • The minimized clause implies the original clause.

We have therefore constructed a family \(\mathcal C\) of \(O(dm)\) minimal valid clauses such that

\(\displaystyle D=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{m}:x\text{ satisfies every }C\in\mathcal C\}.\)

A straightforward implementation takes \(O(d^2m^3)\) time: there are \(O(dm)\) clauses, at most \(m\) deletion tests per clause, and each test takes \(O(dm)\) time.

2. Exact quota condition for a minimal clause

Fix a minimal valid clause

\(\displaystyle C=\bigvee_{j\in P}x_j\ \vee\ \bigvee_{j\in N}\neg x_j,\)

where \(P\cap N=\varnothing\). Define

\(\displaystyle S_C(q)=\sum_{j\in P}q_j+\sum_{j\in N}(1-q_j).\)

Lemma. For \(q\in(0,1]^m\), the judgment \(A_q(\nu)\) satisfies \(C\) for every real distribution \(\nu\in\Delta(D)\) if and only if

\(\displaystyle S_C(q)\le 1\quad\text{when }P\ne\varnothing,\)

and

\(\displaystyle S_C(q)<1\quad\text{when }P=\varnothing.\)

Sufficiency

For a distribution \(\nu\), let the probability that a literal is true be

\(\displaystyle r_j=p_j(\nu)\quad(j\in P),\qquad r_j=1-p_j(\nu)\quad(j\in N).\)

Every type in \(D\) satisfies at least one literal of \(C\). Taking the expected number of true literals gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j\in P\cup N}r_j\ge 1.\)

Suppose that \(A_q(\nu)\) violates \(C\). For a positive literal, violation means

\(\displaystyle p_j(\nu)<q_j.\)

For a negative literal, violation means that the coordinate is accepted, hence

\(\displaystyle p_j(\nu)\ge q_j,\qquad 1-p_j(\nu)\le 1-q_j.\)

Thus, when \(P\ne\varnothing\),

\(\displaystyle 1\le\sum_{j\in P\cup N}r_j<S_C(q).\)

This contradicts \(S_C(q)\le1\).

When \(P=\varnothing\), instead we obtain

\(\displaystyle 1\le\sum_{j\in N}r_j\le S_C(q),\)

contradicting \(S_C(q)<1\).

Necessity

Minimality supplies a witness \(t^{(i)}\in D\) for each literal \(i\): it falsifies every other literal of \(C\). Because \(C\) is valid on \(D\), it satisfies literal \(i\). Its vector of literal truth values on the coordinates of \(C\) is therefore the unit vector \(e_i\).

Consequently, for any nonnegative weights \(\alpha_i\) summing to \(1\), the distribution assigning weight \(\alpha_i\) to \(t^{(i)}\) has literal truth probabilities exactly \(\alpha_i\).

Write

\(\displaystyle c_i=q_i\quad(i\in P),\qquad c_i=1-q_i\quad(i\in N),\)

so that \(\sum_i c_i=S_C(q)\).

If \(S_C(q)>1\), choose

\(\displaystyle \alpha_i=\frac{c_i}{S_C(q)}.\)

For every positive literal, \(c_i=q_i>0\), so \(\alpha_i<q_i\). For every negative literal, \(\alpha_i\le1-q_i\). The resulting distribution therefore makes the quota judgment falsify every literal of \(C\).

There remains the boundary case \(P=\varnothing\) and \(S_C(q)=1\). Choose \(\alpha_i=c_i\). Then, on every coordinate of the clause,

\(\displaystyle 1-p_i(\nu)=1-q_i,\qquad p_i(\nu)=q_i.\)

Equality yields acceptance, so every negative literal is false. The judgment again violates \(C\). This proves necessity. \(\square\)

As a boundary check, the domain \(D=\{01,10\}\) is defined by the minimal clauses \(x_1\vee x_2\) and \(\neg x_1\vee\neg x_2\). Their conditions are respectively \(q_1+q_2\le1\) and \(q_1+q_2>1\), so this domain admits no quotas under the specified equality convention.

3. A polynomial-size linear program

Because \(\mathcal C\) defines exactly \(D\), the lemma gives the equivalence

\(\displaystyle A_q(\nu)\in D\text{ for every }\nu\in\Delta(D) \iff q\text{ satisfies the clause conditions for every }C\in\mathcal C. \)

Introduce a common slack variable \(\varepsilon\). Consider the linear program maximizing \(\varepsilon\), subject to:

\(\displaystyle 0\le\varepsilon\le1,\qquad \varepsilon\le q_j\le1\quad(j=1,\ldots,m),\)

\(\displaystyle S_C(q)\le1 \quad(C\in\mathcal C,\ P_C\ne\varnothing),\)

\(\displaystyle S_C(q)+\varepsilon\le1 \quad(C\in\mathcal C,\ P_C=\varnothing).\)

Admissible quotas exist if and only if this program is feasible and its optimum is positive.

To prove the forward implication, suppose admissible quotas exist. The lemma gives \(q_j>0\) for every coordinate and \(1-S_C(q)>0\) for every all-negative clause. There are finitely many coordinates and clauses, so

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon= \min\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{q_j:1\le j\le m\} \cup\{1-S_C(q):C\in\mathcal C,\ P_C=\varnothing\}\bigr) \)

is positive and satisfies all program constraints.

Conversely, any feasible solution with \(\varepsilon>0\) has \(q_j>0\) and satisfies every required strict clause inequality. The lemma then proves collective rationality for every real distribution.

The program has \(m+1\) variables and \(O(dm+m)\) constraints. After expanding \(S_C(q)\), its coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and its integer right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(m\). Its encoding length is therefore polynomial in \(m,d\).

Rational linear programming admits polynomial-time feasibility and optimization algorithms in the bit model; this is the sole external algorithmic theorem used here. Its hypotheses apply because our program is explicitly given with integer coefficients. See [MIT’s treatment of polynomial-time linear programming](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-251j-introduction-to-mathematical-programming-fall-2009/e13f5e6650f7be1e21101a2e7bd0d5b8_MIT6_251JF09_lec18.pdf).

Thus we can decide feasibility, compute the exact optimum, and test whether it is positive in polynomial time.

4. Rational quotas of polynomial bit length

The feasible region is bounded: all \(q_j\) and \(\varepsilon\) lie in \([0,1]\). If it is nonempty, an optimal extreme point exists.

Let \(n=m+1\). At an extreme point, \(n\) linearly independent tight constraints determine the solution. Their coefficient matrix is an integer matrix with entries of absolute value at most \(1\); their right-hand sides have absolute value at most \(m\).

Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound show that each coordinate is a ratio of integers whose absolute values are at most

\(\displaystyle m\,n^{n/2}.\)

Indeed, every coefficient column has Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt n\), while a replacement right-hand-side column has norm at most \(m\sqrt n\).

Every coordinate consequently has \(O(m\log(m+1))\) bits. A positive optimal extreme point therefore supplies admissible rational quotas with polynomial output length.

An optimal extreme point can also be obtained in polynomial time: after finding the optimal objective value, successively minimize each coordinate over the current optimal face and fix it to its minimum. After at most \(m+1\) additional rational linear programs, the resulting face is a singleton, hence an extreme point.

This also establishes that allowing real quotas would not enlarge the class of admissible domains.

5. Producing the society’s judgment

Let the supplied support types be \(t^{(1)},\ldots,t^{(\tau)}\), with rational masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_\tau\). Once \(q\) has been constructed, compute

\(\displaystyle p_j(\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\,t^{(i)}_j,\)

and output

\(\displaystyle A_q(\mu)_j=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_j(\mu)\ge q_j.\)

These comparisons are exact rational comparisons.

To check bit complexity, write each supplied mass with denominator \(b_i\). The common denominator \(\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}b_i\) has bit length at most the sum of the input denominator lengths, hence \(O(L)\). Since every marginal lies in \([0,1]\), its numerator over that denominator also has \(O(L)\) bits. Computing all marginals takes \(O(m\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations of polynomial bit complexity. Comparing them with the constructed quotas is likewise polynomial-time.

The clause conditions already prove that the resulting judgment belongs to \(D\). Hence both requested outputs are computable in time polynomial in \(m,d,\tau,L\).
