The general polynomial-time-versus-NP-hard classification remains unresolved here. I prove an exact algorithm with running time
$$ \bigl(2m+K+2NK+NK^2\bigr)^m \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,K,\beta), \qquad N=\prod_{j=1}^m(b_j+1), $$
where $\beta$ is the binary encoding length of $b$.

Because the valuations are complete tables, this is
$$ (L+1)^{O(m)} = 2^{O((\log(L+1))^2)}. $$

The proof avoids face enumeration altogether, repairing the verifier’s algorithmic concern. It also establishes a new upper bound: the requested exact computation belongs to $\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}$. 1. Input size and bounded price space

Let
$$ B=\prod_{j=1}^m\{0,\ldots,b_j\}, \qquad N=|B|, \qquad \beta=\sum_{j=1}^m\left\lceil\log_2(b_j+1)\right\rceil. $$

Each complete valuation table has $N$ entries. Consequently,
$$ \tau N=O(L). $$
Since every $b_j$ is positive,
$$ N\geq 2^m, \qquad m=O(\log(L+1)). $$
Expanding $\prod_j(1+b_j)$ also gives
$$ \sum_j b_j\leq N-1. $$

Define
$$ V=\max_{t,x\in B}v_t(x), \qquad H_j=\max_t q_{tj}^{-1}(V+1). $$
Every inverse exists: each payment function is continuous, strictly increasing, starts at zero, and has a final unbounded segment with positive slope. Its inverse at a rational argument is rational. Computing the affine intercepts and performing the inversion uses polynomially many rational arithmetic operations, with polynomial encoding length.

If $p_j\geq H_j$ and $x_j>0$, then
$$ v_t(x)-v_t(x-e_j)\leq V<q_{tj}(p_j). $$
Thus removing one unit of good $j$ strictly increases utility. Every demanded bundle has zero quantity in that coordinate.

Replacing $p_j>H_j$ by $H_j$ therefore preserves the entire demand set: at both prices, maximization can be restricted to bundles with $x_j=0$, whose utilities are unchanged. Applying this argument successively yields
$$ D_t(p)=D_t(p\wedge H). $$
In particular, every equilibrium price can be truncated into the box $[0,H]$.

2. Existence of the componentwise least equilibrium price

I use the following finite-market result: with at least two buyers, integer supply $s$, common domain $[0,s]_{\mathbb Z}$, normalized monotone $M^\natural$-concave valuations, and continuous strictly increasing piecewise-linear payments satisfying the stated reachability condition, a componentwise least equilibrium price exists. This is the structural consequence of [Oki–Sato, Theorems 3.1–3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10563v3). Our payments are unbounded, so they satisfy reachability.

The passage to rational masses requires the following argument.

2.1 Saturating valuations preserves the exchange axiom

Lemma. If $s\geq b$ is integral, then
$$ \widetilde v(x)=v(x\wedge b), \qquad x\in[0,s]_{\mathbb Z}, $$
is normalized, monotone, and $M^\natural$-concave.

Proof. Normalization and monotonicity follow immediately. Take $x,y\in[0,s]_{\mathbb Z}$ with $x_j>y_j$.

If $x_j>b_j$, removing one unit from $x_j$ leaves its clipped bundle unchanged, while adding one unit to $y_j$ weakly increases its clipped bundle. Thus the exchange with index $0$ satisfies the required inequality. Both resulting bundles are feasible because $x_j>y_j$.

Otherwise, put $u=x\wedge b$ and $w=y\wedge b$. Then $u_j>w_j$, so the exchange axiom for $v$ supplies an index $k$.

For $k=0$, clipping commutes with the exchanges in coordinate $j$, and the original inequality applies.

For $k\neq0$, the relation $u_k<w_k$ implies
$$ x_k=u_k<b_k, \qquad x_k<y_k. $$
Hence the exchange is feasible in the larger box. Moreover,
$$ (x-e_j+e_k)\wedge b=u-e_j+e_k, $$
and
$$ (y+e_j-e_k)\wedge b\geq w+e_j-e_k. $$
The latter inequality allows for $y_k>b_k$. Monotonicity and the exchange inequality for $v$ prove the result. $\square$

At nonnegative prices, clipping preserves valuation and weakly reduces payment. Therefore the original and extended maximization problems have the same maximum utility. Every original demanded bundle remains demanded in the extension, and clipping an extended demanded bundle gives an original demanded bundle.

2.2 Recovering a mass equilibrium from a finite market

Let
$$ Z=\{j:\sigma_j=0\}. $$
Choose an integer $A\geq2$ such that $A\mu_t$ and $A\sigma_j$ are integral, and
$$ A\sigma_j\geq b_j \quad\text{whenever }\sigma_j>0. $$
This is possible by clearing denominators and multiplying further.

Create $A\mu_t$ buyers of each type. Give the finite market supply
$$ s_j= \begin{cases} A\sigma_j,&j\notin Z,\\ b_j,&j\in Z, \end{cases} $$
and use the saturated valuations from the lemma.

If $Z$ is nonempty, add one dummy buyer with identity payments and additive valuation
$$ v_{\mathrm{dum}}(x)=C\sum_{j\in Z}x_j, \qquad C=1+\max_jH_j. $$
This valuation satisfies all required valuation hypotheses. The finite theorem therefore supplies its least equilibrium price $r$.

I claim that $r\leq H$. For $j\notin Z$, if $r_j>H_j$, every original buyer demands zero units of $j$. The dummy also demands zero there, since its valuation for that coordinate is zero and the price is positive. This contradicts positive supply.

For $j\in Z$ with $r_j>H_j$, the original buyers demand zero, so the dummy must receive all $b_j$ units. Lowering that price to $H_j<C$ preserves original demand and preserves the dummy’s allocated bundle as a demanded bundle. Performing these replacements gives an equilibrium at $r\wedge H$. Leastness forces $r\leq r\wedge H$, establishing the claim.

Now clip each original buyer’s allocation to $b$. The clipped allocations remain demanded. The dummy consumes all supply in coordinates belonging to $Z$.

In any other coordinate with $r_j>0$, an original buyer cannot demand more than $b_j$ units: removing a unit above the cap preserves valuation and strictly reduces payment. The dummy consumes none of that coordinate. Thus clipping produces no deficit at a positive price.

Any remaining deficit occurs at $r_j=0$. Increase original buyers’ quantities within their capacities until that deficit is filled. This is possible because aggregate original capacity is $Ab_j\geq A\sigma_j$. Each increase preserves demand: its payment is zero, and monotonicity prevents utility from decreasing below its already maximal value.

Dividing original-buyer allocation counts by $A$ gives the required equilibrium masses. Thus a mass equilibrium exists.

2.3 Compactness and downward directedness

Let $E_H$ be the equilibrium-price set in $[0,H]$.

It is closed. Indeed, along convergent equilibrium prices, the mass vectors have a convergent subsequence because
$$ \lambda_{t,x}\geq0, \qquad \sum_x\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t $$
describe a compact set. A positive limiting mass is eventually positive, and its demand inequalities pass to the limit by continuity. The mass and clearing equalities also pass to the limit. Hence $E_H$ is nonempty and compact.

Take any finite collection
$$ p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(h)}\in E_H. $$
At each price, equilibrium masses can be chosen rational: fixing the demand sets gives a nonempty bounded polyhedron with rational coefficients and right-hand sides, which has a rational vertex.

Choose a common replication factor $A$ clearing all these mass vectors and satisfying the preceding construction. Every $p^{(i)}$ is an equilibrium of that finite market. Assign original buyers according to the replicated masses, and give the dummy all zero-supply goods. The dummy’s bundle is demanded because $p^{(i)}\leq H<C$ on its valued coordinates.

The finite market’s least price $r$ satisfies
$$ r\leq p^{(i)} \quad\text{for every }i. $$
In particular, $r\leq H$, and the recovery argument produces a mass equilibrium at $r$. Thus $E_H$ is downward directed.

For each coordinate $j$, compactness supplies a price $p^{(j)}$ attaining its coordinate minimum $a_j$. Downward directedness supplies $u\in E_H$ below all these prices. Therefore
$$ a_j\leq u_j\leq p^{(j)}_j=a_j $$
for every $j$. Hence $u=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)$ is the componentwise least price in $E_H$.

Finally, truncation shows that this price, denoted $p^\star$, is below every unrestricted equilibrium price:
$$ p^\star\leq p\wedge H\leq p. $$

3. A local characterization of demand

For a bundle $x$, its feasible local neighbors are bundles obtained by an addition, deletion, or swap:
$$ x+e_j,\qquad x-e_j,\qquad x-e_j+e_k, $$
where the resulting bundle belongs to $B$ and $j\neq k$ for a swap.

Lemma. If $f:B\to\mathbb R$ is $M^\natural$-concave, then $x$ maximizes $f$ on $B$ if and only if
$$ f(x)\geq f(z) $$
for every feasible local neighbor $z$.

Proof. Global optimality implies all local inequalities.

Conversely, suppose the local inequalities hold and $x$ is not globally optimal. Choose a global maximizer $y$ minimizing $\|y-x\|_1$.

If some $x_j>y_j$, apply the exchange axiom to $x,y$. It gives
$$ f(x)+f(y) \leq f(x-e_j+e_k)+f(y+e_j-e_k) $$
for an allowed $k$. Local optimality bounds the first term on the right by $f(x)$; global optimality bounds the second by $f(y)$. Consequently, both bounds are equalities.

Thus $y+e_j-e_k$ is another global maximizer. Its distance to $x$ is smaller: coordinate $j$ moves toward $x$, and, when $k\neq0$, the condition $x_k<y_k$ makes coordinate $k$ move toward $x$ as well. This contradicts the choice of $y$.

Otherwise, $x\leq y$ and $x\neq y$. Apply the exchange axiom to $y,x$ at a coordinate where $y_j>x_j$. There is no coordinate with $y_k<x_k$, so the exchange index must be $0$:
$$ f(y)+f(x)\leq f(y-e_j)+f(x+e_j). $$
The same local and global bounds imply that $y-e_j$ is a closer global maximizer, again a contradiction. $\square$

Subtracting a linear function preserves the exchange axiom. Therefore the lemma applies to
$$ f(x)=v_t(x)-\sum_jq_{tj}(p_j)x_j. $$

In particular, every demand-membership test is determined by comparisons involving at most two price coordinates.

4. Enumerating candidate vertices instead of faces

Let $k_{tj}$ be the number of segments of $q_{tj}$, so
$$ \sum_{t,j}k_{tj}=K. $$

Construct a finite collection $\mathcal G$ of affine hyperplanes in price space:

  • Include the box boundaries $p_j=0$ and $p_j=H_j$.
  • Include every payment-breakpoint hyperplane intersecting $[0,H]$.
  • For every type, bundle, and feasible local neighbor, include the utility-equality hyperplane for every choice of payment segments in the coordinates changed by that move.

For example, a swap changes only coordinates $j,k$, and its equality is
$$ v_t(x)-v_t(x-e_j+e_k) = q_{tj}(p_j)-q_{tk}(p_k). $$
Choosing one affine segment for each of the two payments makes this an affine hyperplane.

We may include these hyperplanes outside the regions where their chosen segments apply. Such additional cuts only refine the arrangement.

The number $h=|\mathcal G|$ satisfies
$$ h\leq 2m+K+2NK+NK^2. $$
Indeed, additions and deletions contribute at most $2N\sum_{t,j}k_{tj}$. Swaps contribute at most
$$ N\sum_t\sum_{j\neq k}k_{tj}k_{tk}\leq NK^2. $$
All coefficients are rational and have polynomial encoding length.

4.1 Demand is constant on each relative-open face

A relative-open arrangement face fixes the sign of every hyperplane expression, including zero signs.

Breakpoint signs fix the applicable payment segments. If a coordinate equals a breakpoint, either adjacent segment gives the same payment value there. Within the face, every local utility comparison consequently has a fixed sign.

By the local characterization in Section 3, each complete demand set $D_t(p)$ is constant on the face.

4.2 The least price is a vertex

Let $F$ be the relative-open face containing $p^\star$, and let
$$ P=\overline F. $$
Replacing the strict sign constraints defining $F$ by weak inequalities gives exactly $P$. To see the reverse inclusion, take any point satisfying the weak constraints and form convex combinations with a point in $F$. Every strict constraint becomes strict in those combinations, which converge to the chosen point.

Thus $P$ is a bounded rational polytope whose defining hyperplanes belong to $\mathcal G$.

Choose equilibrium masses at $p^\star$. Demand is constant on $F$, so those masses establish equilibrium throughout $F$. Continuity of the finitely many demand inequalities establishes equilibrium throughout $P$.

Every point of $P$ is therefore componentwise at least $p^\star$. If
$$ p^\star=\alpha u+(1-\alpha)w, \qquad u,w\in P, \qquad 0<\alpha<1, $$
then the nonnegative coordinate differences $u-p^\star$ and $w-p^\star$ must both vanish. Hence $u=w=p^\star$.

This proves that $p^\star$ is a vertex of $P$.

At a vertex, active constraint normals span $\mathbb R^m$. Otherwise, a nonzero vector orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions while preserving every constraint, contradicting extremality.

Consequently, $p^\star$ is the unique intersection of some $m$ hyperplanes in $\mathcal G$ with linearly independent normals.

4.3 Exact algorithm

Enumerate all subsets of $m$ hyperplanes from $\mathcal G$. For each subset:

  • Discard it if its normals are linearly dependent.
  • Otherwise solve its linear equations exactly.
  • Discard the resulting price if it lies outside $[0,H]$.
  • Compute all demand sets, retaining all ties.
  • Test the rational linear feasibility system

$$ \lambda_{t,x}\geq0, \qquad \sum_{x\in D_t(p)}\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_t\sum_{x\in D_t(p)}\lambda_{t,x}x=\sigma. $$

Among the feasible candidates, return one minimizing $\sum_jp_j$, together with feasible masses.

The candidate list contains $p^\star$. Every feasible candidate is an equilibrium price and therefore lies componentwise above $p^\star$. Any distinct candidate has strictly larger coordinate sum. Thus the algorithm returns exactly the requested price.

This argument covers degeneracies, breakpoint prices, and equilibria requiring demand ties. It requires no enumeration of arrangement faces.

4.4 Running time and encoding length

There are at most $h^m$ candidate subsets. Construction, exact linear-system solving, demand evaluation, and each mass-feasibility problem take polynomial time in the stated parameters. Therefore
$$ T\leq \bigl(2m+K+2NK+NK^2\bigr)^m \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,K,\beta). $$
Since $N,K=O(L)$ and $m=O(\log(L+1))$, this is quasipolynomial in $L$ and polynomial for fixed $m$.

The price has polynomial encoding length. Explicitly, clear denominators separately in each hyperplane equation. Let $S$ bound the binary length of every resulting integer coefficient and right-hand side. This bound is computable and polynomial in the input length.

Cramer’s rule bounds each price-coordinate numerator and denominator by
$$ m!\,2^{mS}. $$
Thus a polynomial bound on their bit lengths is
$$ d=mS+\left\lceil\log_2(m!)\right\rceil+2. $$

At the returned price, the mass-feasibility polytope has $\tau+m$ equality rows. A basic feasible solution has at most $\tau+m$ positive masses. Its constraint matrix consists of integer bundle coordinates and type indicators, and its right-hand side is rational input data. Determinant bounds therefore give polynomial encoding length for those masses.

5. A polynomial number of NP-oracle queries suffices

Consider the coordinate-threshold question, for rational $c$:
$$ \text{Does an equilibrium price }p\text{ exist with }p_j\leq c? $$
By leastness, its answer is yes exactly when
$$ p^\star_j\leq c. $$

This question has a polynomial-size certificate whenever its answer is yes: use $p^\star$ and a basic feasible equilibrium mass vector. Section 4 bounds their encoding lengths polynomially.

A verifier checks nonnegativity, the type-mass equalities, exact clearing, the price inequality, and every demand inequality for positively assigned bundles. All checks use polynomially many exact rational operations on the complete tables and listed payment functions. Thus the threshold question belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

To obtain an exact computation, let $Q=2^d$, using the bound from Section 4. Every coordinate of $p^\star$ has numerator and denominator at most $Q$, and lies in $[0,Q]$.

For each coordinate, binary search with threshold queries until its containing interval has width less than $Q^{-2}$. This takes $O(d)$ queries. Two distinct rationals with denominators at most $Q$ differ by at least
$$ \left|\frac ab-\frac ce\right| = \frac{|ae-bc|}{be} \geq Q^{-2}. $$
Hence each resulting interval contains exactly one possible price coordinate satisfying the encoding bound.

Finally, recover a certificate satisfying all these intervals using polynomially many NP prefix queries: fix its encoded bits one at a time, asking whether the current prefix has a valid completion. A polynomial certificate-length bound follows from Section 4. A completion exists because $p^\star$ and its equilibrium masses supply one.

Every recovered price coordinate must equal the unique bounded-denominator rational in its interval, namely $p^\star_j$. The certificate also supplies associated masses.

Therefore the requested computation belongs to
$$ \mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}. $$

This oracle bound does not establish polynomial-time solvability.

6. Polynomial algorithm for separable valuations

Suppose each supported valuation is coordinatewise separable:
$$ v_t(x)=\sum_{j=1}^m f_{tj}(x_j), \qquad f_{tj}(0)=0. $$
The functions can be obtained from the axis entries:
$$ f_{tj}(k)=v_t(ke_j). $$
Separability itself can be checked against the complete table in polynomial time.

Define marginal values
$$ a_{tj,k}=f_{tj}(k)-f_{tj}(k-1), \qquad 1\leq k\leq b_j. $$
Monotonicity makes them nonnegative. Applying the exchange axiom to $(k+1)e_j$ and $(k-1)e_j$ gives
$$ f_{tj}(k+1)+f_{tj}(k-1)\leq2f_{tj}(k), $$
so they are nonincreasing.

Set
$$ \theta_{tj,k}=q_{tj}^{-1}(a_{tj,k}). $$
For a scalar price $r$, define
$$ \ell_{tj}(r)=|\{k:\theta_{tj,k}>r\}|, \qquad u_{tj}(r)=|\{k:\theta_{tj,k}\geq r\}|. $$
Writing utility as the sum of successive marginal utilities shows that demanded quantities in coordinate $j$ are precisely the integers between these endpoints. Separability then gives
$$ D_t(p)= \prod_{j=1}^m \{\ell_{tj}(p_j),\ldots,u_{tj}(p_j)\}. $$

Define
$$ W_{j,>}(r)=\sum_t\mu_t\ell_{tj}(r), \qquad W_{j,\geq}(r)=\sum_t\mu_tu_{tj}(r). $$
An equilibrium must satisfy
$$ W_{j,>}(p_j)\leq\sigma_j\leq W_{j,\geq}(p_j) $$
for every coordinate.

These inequalities are also sufficient. Each type’s expected quantity can be chosen anywhere between its demand endpoints, and the coordinate distributions can be combined by taking their product. Every resulting bundle is demanded.

For each good, the least feasible scalar price is
$$ p^\star_j= \min\{r\geq0:W_{j,>}(r)\leq\sigma_j\}. $$
This minimum exists because $W_{j,>}$ eventually vanishes.

If it is zero, the upper inequality holds because $W_{j,\geq}(0)=b_j$. If it is positive, it is one of the finitely many thresholds. Immediately below that threshold, $W_{j,>}$ exceeds supply, and its left limit equals $W_{j,\geq}(p^\star_j)$. Thus the upper inequality holds there too.

Sorting thresholds separately for each good computes these prices exactly.

For masses, start with expected quantity $\mu_t\ell_{tj}(p^\star_j)$ for each type. Distribute the remaining supply among types using increments
$$ 0\leq\delta_{tj} \leq \mu_t\bigl(u_{tj}(p^\star_j)-\ell_{tj}(p^\star_j)\bigr). $$
The equilibrium inequalities guarantee sufficient increment capacity. When the endpoints differ, give the upper endpoint probability
$$ \alpha_{tj} = \frac{\delta_{tj}} {\mu_t\bigl(u_{tj}(p^\star_j)-\ell_{tj}(p^\star_j)\bigr)} $$
and the lower endpoint probability $1-\alpha_{tj}$. When they coincide, use that quantity deterministically.

Taking the product of these coordinate distributions and multiplying by $\mu_t$ produces exact equilibrium masses. There are at most $2^m\leq N$ supported bundles per type.

Let
$$ A_{\mathrm{marg}}=\tau\sum_j b_j\leq\tau(N-1). $$
Preprocessing payment breakpoints, computing and sorting thresholds, checking separability, and constructing the masses take time bounded by
$$ O\!\left( K+\tau Nm+ A_{\mathrm{marg}}\log(A_{\mathrm{marg}}+K+2) \right)\operatorname{poly}(L+\beta). $$
Thus this case is polynomial-time solvable for arbitrary numbers of goods and supported types, with arbitrary permitted type-good payment functions.

7. What remains unresolved

For the general model, the candidate-vertex algorithm still enumerates up to $h^m$ intersections. Complete valuation tables imply $m=O(\log L)$, which makes this quasipolynomial; that implication does not make it polynomial.

Replication supplies structural existence and leastness, but may require exponentially many buyers. For example, with one good, capacity one, masses $2^{-r}$ and $1-2^{-r}$, and supply $2^{-(r+1)}$, integral replicated supply requires a replication factor divisible by $2^{r+1}$.

The remaining task is therefore to find a polynomial method for selecting the least feasible candidate, or to give a hardness reduction respecting the complete-table representation and all valuation and payment restrictions.

The quasipolynomial upper bound also constrains potential hardness: a polynomial-time reduction from SAT to this exact computation would yield a quasipolynomial-time algorithm for SAT. This observation supplies no unconditional exclusion of NP-hardness.
