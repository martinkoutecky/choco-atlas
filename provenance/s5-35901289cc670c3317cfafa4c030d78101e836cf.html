The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(E\) of complete cohort or contract types, rational supplies \(\mu_e\), the two preference preorders, the partition \(E=E_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}E_2\), and two endpoint-supply-respecting polymatroids \(P_D,P_H\) arising from clone-symmetric matroid constraints, decide whether there is an \(x\in P_D\cap P_H\) with \(0\le x_e\le\mu_e\) such that no type \(e\) with \(x_e<\mu_e\) weakly blocks on both sides and, for \(e\in E_2\), strongly blocks on at least one side. A weak block on side \(S\) is a feasible positive-mass addition \(x+\epsilon\mathbf 1_e\in P_S\), or exchange \(x+\epsilon(\mathbf 1_e-\mathbf 1_f)\in P_S\) for some \(f\ne e\) with \(x_f\ge\epsilon\) and \(e\succeq_S f\); a strong block uses \(e\succ_S f\) for an exchange. Output such an \(x\) when one exists.

1. Notation and elementary blocking facts

For a vector $x$ and a subset $A$, write
$$ x(A)=\sum_{e\in A}x_e. $$
A polymatroid rank function is normalized, nonnegative, monotone, and submodular. For auxiliary ranks arising below, singleton ranks may be zero.

Fix one side with rank function $r$, and let $x\in P(r)$. A set $A$ is tight if
$$ x(A)=r(A). $$

Tight sets are closed under union and intersection. Indeed, if $A,B$ are tight, then
$$ \begin{aligned} r(A)+r(B) &=x(A)+x(B)\\ &=x(A\cup B)+x(A\cap B)\\ &\le r(A\cup B)+r(A\cap B)\\ &\le r(A)+r(B). \end{aligned} $$
Equality throughout proves the assertion.

For an unsaturated coordinate $e$, addition is impossible precisely when a tight set contains $e$. To see this, addition increases exactly those subset sums indexed by sets containing $e$. If none of these finitely many constraints is tight, their minimum slack is positive, permitting a sufficiently small addition.

When addition is impossible, let $T(e)$ be the intersection of all tight sets containing $e$. It is tight and contains $e$. For $f\ne e$ with $x_f>0$,
$$ \text{exchange from }f\text{ to }e\text{ is possible} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\in T(e). $$
If $f\notin T(e)$, some tight set contains $e$ and excludes $f$, preventing exchange. If $f\in T(e)$, every constraint increased by the exchange has positive slack. Finiteness permits a positive increment respecting those slacks, $x_f$, and $\mu_e-x_e$.

Consequently, absence of a strong block at an unsaturated $e$ is equivalent to the existence of a tight set $A\ni e$ satisfying
$$ x_f=0 \qquad \text{for every }f\in A\setminus\{e\}\text{ with }e\succ f. $$
Necessity follows by taking $A=T(e)$. For sufficiency, tightness prevents addition and exchanges from outside $A$, while eligible donors inside $A$ have zero allocation. The corresponding statement with $e\succeq f$ characterizes absence of a weak block.

These facts retain the required exclusion of exchanges within one type.

2. An exact test for the highest common preference class

Consider an instance on a ground set $V$ whose two preorders coincide. Let $B$ be its highest indifference class. Thus, on both sides,
$$ e\sim_S f\quad(e,f\in B), \qquad e\succ_S f\quad(e\in B,\ f\in V\setminus B). $$
Write its ranks as $t_D,t_H$, with
$$ 0\le t_S(\{e\})\le\mu_e. $$
Define
$$ c_e=\min\{t_D(\{e\}),t_H(\{e\})\}, \qquad e\in B. $$
Every common feasible allocation satisfies $x_e\le c_e$.

2.1. Coordinates at their caps are protected

If $x_e=c_e$, choose a side attaining this minimum. Its singleton constraint is tight, so every addition or exchange increasing $e$ is impossible on that side. Hence $e$ cannot violate either stability condition.

This includes $c_e=0$.

2.2. Coordinates below their caps weakly block on both sides

Suppose $e\in B$ and $x_e<c_e$. Fix a side $S$. If addition is possible, $e$ weakly blocks.

Otherwise, $T_S(e)$ exists, and monotonicity gives
$$ x(T_S(e)) =t_S(T_S(e)) \ge t_S(\{e\}) >x_e. $$
Thus some $f\in T_S(e)\setminus\{e\}$ has $x_f>0$. Exchange from $f$ to $e$ is possible. Since $e$ belongs to the highest class, $e\succeq_S f$. This is a weak block.

Therefore every stable allocation must satisfy
$$ x_e=c_e \qquad(e\in E_1\cap B). $$
For $e\in E_2\cap B$ below its cap, stability requires absence of a strong block on both sides.

2.3. Strong-block exclusion depends only on the allocation within $B$

Let $y=x|_B$, and suppose $e\in B$ lies below its cap.

If $e$ has no strong block on side $S$, Section 1 supplies a tight set $A\ni e$ whose coordinates in $A\setminus B$ are all zero: every such type is strictly worse than $e$. Hence
$$ x(A)=y(A\cap B). $$
Feasibility and monotonicity imply
$$ y(A\cap B) \le t_S(A\cap B) \le t_S(A) =x(A) =y(A\cap B). $$
Thus $A\cap B$ is tight in the restricted polymatroid on $B$. Addition at $e$ is impossible there.

Conversely, suppose addition at $e$ is impossible in the restriction to $B$. There is a tight set $A\subseteq B$ containing $e$. It remains tight in every feasible completion of $y$. It prevents addition and every exchange from a donor outside $B$. Donors inside $B$ are tied with $e$, so none qualifies for a strong exchange.

We have proved:

A common feasible allocation has no stability violation from $B$ precisely when its restriction $y$ satisfies:

- $y_e=c_e$ for every $e\in E_1\cap B$;
- for every $e\in E_2\cap B$ with $y_e<c_e$, addition is impossible in both restricted polymatroids.

These conditions protect $B$ in every common feasible completion.

3. Expressing the test as linear feasibility

For each side define
$$ R_S^B= \left\{ y\in P(t_S|_B): 0\le y_e\le c_e\text{ for every }e\in B \right\}, $$
and
$$ b_S=\max_{y\in R_S^B}y(B). $$
These maxima exist because the sets are nonempty and compact.

We need the following maximality fact.

Lemma. For $y\in R_S^B$, no coordinate admits a positive addition within $R_S^B$ if and only if $y(B)=b_S$. Moreover,
$$ b_S= \min_{A\subseteq B} \left(t_S(A)+c(B\setminus A)\right). $$

Proof. A maximum-total allocation admits no addition. Conversely, suppose no addition is possible. Every coordinate below its cap has a tight set containing it in $P(t_S|_B)$; otherwise a sufficiently small addition would satisfy both the rank constraints and its cap.

Let $T$ be the union of these tight sets, taking $T=\varnothing$ if none are needed. Then $T$ is tight, and every coordinate outside $T$ attains its cap. Consequently,
$$ y(B)=t_S(T)+c(B\setminus T). $$
Every $z\in R_S^B$ satisfies
$$ z(B)\le t_S(T)+c(B\setminus T)=y(B), $$
so $y$ is maximum-total.

For every $A\subseteq B$, the expression $t_S(A)+c(B\setminus A)$ bounds every feasible total from above. A maximum-total point supplies a set $T$ attaining equality, proving the formula. $\square$

Below a coordinate’s cap, addition in $R_S^B$ is possible exactly when addition in $P(t_S|_B)$ is possible, because an increment can be reduced to respect the cap.

It follows that the allocations protecting the highest class are exactly
$$ \begin{aligned} K_B=\bigl\{y:\;& y\in P(t_D|_B)\cap P(t_H|_B),\\ &y_e=c_e\quad(e\in E_1\cap B),\\ &y(B)=b_D=b_H \bigr\}. \end{aligned} $$
In particular, unequal values $b_D,b_H$ imply nonexistence.

4. Why the choice within the highest class needs no backtracking

The following two polymatroid facts make the reduction exact.

4.1. Adding coordinate caps

For a rank function $t$ and nonnegative caps $d$, define
$$ \rho(A)= \min_{T\subseteq A} \left(t(T)+d(A\setminus T)\right). $$
Then $\rho$ is a polymatroid rank function and
$$ P(\rho)=P(t)\cap\{x:x_e\le d_e\text{ for every }e\}. $$

For the polytope identity, a vector satisfying the right-hand side obeys, for every $T\subseteq A$,
$$ x(A)\le t(T)+d(A\setminus T). $$
The reverse inclusion follows from $\rho(A)\le t(A)$ and $\rho(\{e\})\le d_e$.

Normalization and nonnegativity are immediate. For monotonicity, take a minimizing set for $A\cup\{e\}$. If it contains $e$, remove $e$ and use monotonicity of $t$; otherwise discard the nonnegative cap contribution $d_e$.

For submodularity, let $U\subseteq A$ and $W\subseteq C$ attain the respective minima. The modular identity
$$ \begin{aligned} d(A\setminus U)+d(C\setminus W) ={}&d((A\cup C)\setminus(U\cup W))\\ &+d((A\cap C)\setminus(U\cap W)) \end{aligned} $$
and submodularity of $t$ give
$$ \rho(A)+\rho(C)\ge\rho(A\cup C)+\rho(A\cap C). $$

4.2. Fixing a base gives a choice-independent remaining polymatroid

Let $\rho$ be a rank function on $V$, and suppose
$$ y\in P(\rho|_B), \qquad y(B)=\rho(B). $$
Define, for $A\subseteq V\setminus B$,
$$ (\rho/B)(A)=\rho(A\cup B)-\rho(B). $$
Then
$$ (y,z)\in P(\rho) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\in P(\rho/B). $$

Necessity follows from the constraints indexed by $B\cup A$. For sufficiency, let $U\subseteq B$ and $A\subseteq V\setminus B$. Submodularity gives
$$ \rho(U)+\rho(B\cup A)-\rho(B)\le\rho(U\cup A), $$
so
$$ y(U)+z(A) \le\rho(U)+\rho(B\cup A)-\rho(B) \le\rho(U\cup A). $$
These are all required rank constraints. The function $\rho/B$ is normalized, monotone, and submodular directly from the corresponding properties of $\rho$.

Crucially, the remaining feasible set depends only on $\rho$ and $B$, not on the particular base $y$.

4.3. Applying these facts to stability

Set
$$ d_e= \begin{cases} c_e,&e\in B,\\ \mu_e,&e\in V\setminus B, \end{cases} $$
and cap each side’s rank as in Section 4.1, obtaining $\rho_D,\rho_H$.

All common feasible allocations already satisfy these caps: the caps within $B$ are common singleton bounds, and the remaining mass bounds are implied by singleton ranks. Thus capping does not change common feasibility.

If $y\in K_B$, then
$$ y(B)=b_S=\rho_S(B) \qquad(S\in\{D,H\}). $$
Section 4.2 therefore shows that, after fixing $y$, the remaining feasible set on side $S$ is
$$ P(\rho_S/B), $$
independently of the choice of $y\in K_B$.

Moreover, a remaining type cannot exchange with a donor in $B$: that donor is strictly preferred on both sides. All eligible blocking operations preserve $y$, and their feasibility is exactly their feasibility in the remaining polymatroids.

Consequently,
$$ \begin{aligned} &\text{the instance on }V\text{ has a stable allocation}\\ &\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K_B\ne\varnothing \text{ and the instance on }V\setminus B\\ &\hspace{115pt}\text{with ranks }\rho_D/B,\rho_H/B \text{ has a stable allocation}. \end{aligned} $$
Every $y\in K_B$ may be chosen. Section 2 protects $B$ in every feasible completion, and the remaining instance is independent of that choice.

This proves the reduction without a backtracking assumption.

5. The algorithm and direct residual-rank formulas

Let
$$ B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_k $$
be the common preference classes, from best to worst. Process them in this order.

Suppose $H$ is the union of processed classes, and $u$ is their fixed common feasible allocation. For $A\subseteq E\setminus H$, define
$$ t_S^u(A)= \min_{T\subseteq H} \left(r_S(A\cup T)-u(T)\right). $$
Then
$$ (u,z)\in P_S \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\in P(t_S^u). $$
Indeed, the original constraints are exactly
$$ u(T)+z(A)\le r_S(T\cup A) \qquad(T\subseteq H,\ A\subseteq E\setminus H). $$

The residual function is a polymatroid rank function. Nonnegativity follows from feasibility of $u$, normalization follows by taking $T=\varnothing$, and monotonicity follows from monotonicity of $r_S$. For submodularity, choose minimizing sets $T,U\subseteq H$ for $A,C$ and apply submodularity to $A\cup T,C\cup U$, together with
$$ u(T)+u(U)=u(T\cup U)+u(T\cap U). $$
Also,
$$ t_S^u(\{e\})\le r_S(\{e\})\le\mu_e. $$

At the next class $B$, compute
$$ c_e=\min\{t_D^u(\{e\}),t_H^u(\{e\})\}, $$
then compute $b_D,b_H$ and test the system $K_B$ from Section 3.

If it is empty, report nonexistence. Otherwise choose a vertex $y$ of $K_B$, append it to $u$, and continue. At termination, return $u$.

Section 4 proves that any accepted choice preserves the existence of a stable completion. It also proves that previously processed classes remain protected. Induction over the classes establishes both the rejection and construction claims.

Importantly, the algorithm need not implement a recursively nested rank oracle. For example,
$$ b_S = c(B)+ \min_{W\subseteq H\cup B} \left( r_S(W)-u(W\cap H)-c(W\cap B) \right). $$
This is one submodular minimization using the original rank oracle.

Likewise, separation of a proposed $y$ from the conditional common feasible set uses
$$ \min_{W\subseteq H\cup B} \left( r_S(W)-u(W\cap H)-y(W\cap B) \right). $$
A negative minimum supplies the violated inequality
$$ y(W\cap B)\le r_S(W)-u(W\cap H). $$
Thus the number of processed classes does not increase the depth of oracle composition.

6. Oracle and bit complexity

I use two standard algorithmic results.

First, exact submodular minimization on an $n$-element ground set, with rational value-oracle answers of encoding length at most $B$, uses polynomially many oracle calls and bit operations in $n,B$. The strongly polynomial real-valued algorithm of Iwata–Fleischer–Fujishige, Theorem 4.2, provides the minimization procedure; its exact rational implementation has polynomial encoding growth. Oracle evaluation time is added separately. [Iwata–Fleischer–Fujishige](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0004089)

Second, for rational polyhedra with a known polynomial bound on inequality encoding lengths, a strong separation oracle yields exact emptiness testing and feasible-point construction in oracle-polynomial time, including lower-dimensional polyhedra. Strong linear optimization is also available. These are the strong nonemptiness theorem, Theorem 6.4.1, and the optimization–separation results in Section 6.4 of Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver. [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf)

All minimizations above satisfy the first theorem’s submodularity hypotheses: they restrict an original rank function, optionally fix included elements, and subtract a modular function. It remains to establish polynomial bounds on the numbers supplied to these procedures.

6.1. Polynomial encoding of the constructed prefixes

For $H\subseteq E$, let
$$ Q_H=P(r_D|_H)\cap P(r_H|_H). $$
Every vertex of $Q_H$ has polynomial encoding length. If $h=|H|>0$, select $h$ linearly independent active constraints. Their matrix has entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$, and their right-hand sides are zero or original rank values.

The product of their denominators has $O(hL)$ bits. Hadamard’s bound gives
$$ |\det A|\le h^{h/2}. $$
After clearing denominators, Cramer’s rule therefore bounds each coordinate’s encoding length by
$$ O\!\left(hL+h\log(h+1)\right). $$

The algorithm maintains $u$ as a vertex of $Q_H$. To prove this, suppose it holds before processing $B$. The conditional feasible set on $B$ is
$$ F_B=P(t_D^u|_B)\cap P(t_H^u|_B). $$
Every point of $F_B$ satisfies $y_e\le c_e$ and $y(B)\le b_S$. Hence the equalities defining $K_B$ make valid inequalities tight, so a nonempty $K_B$ is a face of $F_B$. A vertex $y$ of $K_B$ is therefore a vertex of $F_B$.

Now suppose
$$ (u,y)=\tfrac12(v+w), \qquad v,w\in Q_{H\cup B}. $$
Their restrictions to $H$ belong to $Q_H$ and average to its vertex $u$, so both restrictions equal $u$. Their restrictions to $B$ then belong to $F_B$ and average to its vertex $y$, so both equal $y$. Thus $(u,y)$ is a vertex of $Q_{H\cup B}$.

The empty prefix starts this induction. Consequently, every constructed prefix has the uniform polynomial encoding bound above.

6.2. Polynomial implementation of each stage

Residual-rank answers are an original rank value minus a sum of coordinates of $u$. The preceding bound makes their encoding lengths polynomial in $m,L$. The same holds for $c_e$ and $b_S$.

The inequalities defining $K_B$ have coefficients in $\{-1,0,1\}$ and right-hand sides of polynomial encoding length. The box
$$ 0\le y_e\le c_e\le\mu_e $$
bounds the feasible set. The separation procedure in Section 5 is exact and polynomial.

The strong nonemptiness theorem therefore tests $K_B$ and constructs a point. A vertex can be obtained by successively maximizing the coordinates over the current optimal face. After all coordinates are fixed, the resulting singleton is a vertex. This requires at most $|B|$ linear optimizations.

There are at most $m$ classes, and
$$ \sum_{i=1}^k |B_i|=m. $$
All stages consequently use polynomially many bit operations and original rank-oracle calls in $m,L$, and hence in $m,\tau,L$. This completes the theorem’s complexity proof.

7. Arbitrary preorders when one side is modular

Suppose, for example,
$$ r_D(A)=\sum_{e\in A}a_e, \qquad 0<a_e\le\mu_e. $$
Then $P_D$ imposes exactly the separate constraints $x_e\le a_e$.

For an unsaturated $e$:

  • if $x_e<a_e$, a sufficiently small addition is feasible, so $e$ both weakly and strongly blocks on side $D$;
  • if $x_e=a_e$, every operation increasing $e$ violates its singleton constraint, so neither kind of block is possible.

Thus both blocking predicates on side $D$ are independent of its preorder. Replacing $\succeq_D$ by $\succeq_H$ leaves the stability problem unchanged. The theorem then decides existence and constructs an allocation in polynomial time. The argument is symmetric if side $H$ is modular.

Modularity itself can be recognized with $m+1$ rank queries. For a normalized submodular rank function, it is equivalent to
$$ r(E)=\sum_{e\in E}r(\{e\}). $$
The forward implication is immediate. Conversely, setting $a_e=r(\{e\})$, submodularity gives, for every $A$,
$$ r(E)\le r(A)+r(E\setminus A) \le a(A)+a(E\setminus A)=r(E). $$
Both upper bounds must be attained, yielding $r(A)=a(A)$.

8. What remains unresolved

The proof uses the existence of a highest class that is strictly preferred to every remaining type on both sides. This ensures that remaining types cannot use processed types as eligible exchange donors.

For differing preorders, that inference can fail. A processed type may be an eligible donor on the other side, so fixing its allocation need not preserve the remaining blocking problem. The argument above therefore supplies neither a general polynomial algorithm nor a hardness reduction.
