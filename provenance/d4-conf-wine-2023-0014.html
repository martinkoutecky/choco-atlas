The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and type-specific values \(v_t\), budgets \(B_t\), and integral caps \(K_t\), together with a rational monotone submodular rank oracle \(F_\mu:2^T\to\mathbb{Q}_+\) whose capacities scale with population mass, compute the terminal mass table \(\pi_{t,k,p}\) produced by an ascending-price clinching auction. Here \(\pi_{t,k,p}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) people receiving \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K_t\}\) indivisible goods and paying \(p\); cohort splits preserve the aggregate polymatroid constraints. Is this outcome computable in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(\sum_t K_t\), the rational input bit length, and the number of value-oracle calls?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: an ordered list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge2\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size. Each type has positive rational per-unit value \(v_t\), positive rational per-person budget \(B_t\), and integral per-person cap \(K_t\ge1\). Put \(m=\sum_tK_t\).
A value oracle supplies a normalized monotone submodular rank \(F_\mu:2^T\to\mathbb Q_+\), with \(F_\mu(T)>0\), \(F_\mu(\{t\})\le\mu_tK_t\), and \(F_\mu(T)=F_\mu(T\setminus\{t\})\) for every type. Capacities measure goods mass and scale linearly under common rescaling of population mass. Let \(L\) include the rational input encoding and a bound on oracle-answer bit lengths.
Use the following cohort interpretation of the ascending-price auction. A cohort \(C=(t,a,k,p,d)\) consists of mass \(a>0\) whose people each have \(k\) goods, have paid \(p\), and have integral demand \(d\ge0\). Initially each type forms one cohort \((t,\mu_t,0,0,K_t+1)\), and the price is \(c=0\); a cohort is active when \(d>0\).
For the current cohorts, write \(x_t=\sum_{C:t(C)=t}a_Ck_C\). Feasible additional aggregate transactions form \(R=\{y\ge0:\sum_{C:t(C)\in S}y_C\le F_\mu(S)-\sum_{t\in S}x_t\ (S\subseteq T),\ y_C\le a_C\min(d_C,K_{t(C)}-k_C)\}\).
A clinching sweep visits the cohorts present at its start in lexicographic order of \((t,k,p,d)\). At each visit recompute \(R\) and choose the largest \(r\ge0\) for which \(\{y_{-C}:y\in R,\ y_C=r\}=\{y_{-C}:y\in R,\ y_C=0\}\).
With \(q=\lfloor r/a_C\rfloor\) and \(\theta=r/a_C-q\), replace the visited cohort by masses \(a_C(1-\theta)\) and \(a_C\theta\) receiving respectively \(q\) and \(q+1\) additional goods; receiving \(j\) goods changes its state to \((k+j,p+cj,d-j)\). Discard zero masses, visit no newly created cohort in that sweep, and merge identical states after the sweep.
While active cohorts remain, advance the price to \(\min_{C:d_C>0}\min\{v_{t(C)},(B_{t(C)}-p_C)/d_C\}\). At that price, first process active valuation-equality cohorts by setting their demand to zero, then process active budget-equality cohorts by decreasing their demand by one; select ties lexicographically and execute a clinching sweep after every individual update, reevaluating pending equalities.
The terminal table \(\pi_{t,k,p}\) records the mass of people of type \(t\) receiving \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K_t\}\) goods and paying total amount \(p\), summing over terminal demands.

Is exact computation of this terminal mass table polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), with polynomially many value-oracle calls, or is it NP-hard? For hardness, restrict to ranks supplied by polynomial-size descriptions with polynomial-time evaluation.

The finite-buyer integer-polymatroid clinching auction is computable in time polynomial in the numbers of buyers and goods; with the goods count encoded in binary, that bound is pseudo-polynomial. [Hirai–Sato](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.00231v4)
No complexity classification for the exact cohort model specified here was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized population mass to one and interpreted capacities as goods mass, scaling linearly under common population rescaling.
  • Required positive rational values and budgets, positive integral caps, normalized rank, positive total capacity, and at least two supported types.
  • Interpreted the initial-competition condition at the type level as deletion of any whole type leaving total rank unchanged; this is stronger than deletion of an individual in an atomless society.
  • Made the supplied caps explicit per-person feasibility constraints, required singleton capacities not to exceed aggregate caps, and initialized demand at cap plus one to retain the extra initial demand unit.
  • Defined the otherwise unspecified mass clinching operation at cohort level through equality of residual-polytope projections, protecting the other cohorts.
  • Specified a fractional cohort clinch by splitting into adjacent integral allotments, charging each recipient for its actual goods and decreasing its demand accordingly.
  • Fixed deterministic event and sweep orders, retained valuation updates before budget updates, and required a clinching sweep after every individual cohort demand update.
  • Interpreted payment indices as total per-person payments and the terminal table as the explicit list of its nonzero rational entries.
  • Defined the size parameter as the sum of per-person caps, bounded oracle-answer bit lengths, and restricted hardness claims to efficiently evaluable, finitely described ranks.

1. Result and computational convention

For explicitly listed terminal tables, the problem is NP-hard under polynomial-time metric reductions. More substantively, even the following scalar problem is NP-hard under ordinary polynomial-time many-one reductions:

Given an auction instance and a rational threshold $\rho$, decide whether its terminal revenue is less than $\rho$.

This holds for ranks with polynomial-size descriptions and polynomial-time evaluation. On the constructed instances, terminal revenue is a rational number of polynomial bit length.

I use the explicit-output interpretation of the table: all nonzero entries are listed. Because such tables can be exponentially long, the reduction convention matters. A polynomial-time metric reduction from a function $f$ to a function $g$ consists of polynomial-time functions $A,D$ satisfying
$$ f(z)=D(z,g(A(z))). $$
The decoding function is polynomial-time in the combined length of its arguments, including the returned table. This is the function-reduction convention used below; it does not assert that an exponentially long table can be scanned in time polynomial in the original instance. See, for example, the [definition of metric reductions](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol365-icdt2026/html/LIPIcs.ICDT.2026.12/LIPIcs.ICDT.2026.12.html).

The scalar revenue hardness is independent of this output-length distinction. I prove it first, then derive the table consequence and reprove the exponential output-size obstruction.

2. An admissible auction family

Fix an integer $n\ge 2$. Let the ordered types be $0,1,\ldots,n$, all with cap $n$. Thus
$$ \tau=n+1,\qquad m=n(n+1). $$

Define
$$ \alpha=\frac{n2^n}{2n2^n-1}, \qquad D_0=\alpha n, \qquad D_i=\frac{\alpha(2n-2i+1)}{2^i} \quad(1\le i\le n), $$
and set
$$ \mu_0=\alpha,\qquad \mu_i=\frac{D_{i-1}-D_i}{n} \quad(1\le i\le n). $$

These masses are positive, since
$$ D_0-D_1=\frac{\alpha}{2} $$
and, for $i\ge 2$,
$$ D_{i-1}-D_i =\frac{\alpha(2n-2i+5)}{2^i}>0. $$
Also $D_n=\alpha/2^n$, so
$$ \sum_{t=0}^n\mu_t =\alpha+\frac{D_0-D_n}{n} =\alpha\left(2-\frac{1}{n2^n}\right) =1. $$

Put
$$ G=n(1-\alpha),\qquad F_\mu(S)=\min\left\{G,\ n\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\right\}. $$

Choose strictly increasing positive integer prices
$$ c_1<c_2<\cdots<c_n<V. $$
Assign
$$ v_0=V,\qquad v_i=c_i\quad(1\le i\le n), $$
and give every type budget
$$ B=(2n+2)V. $$

Type $0$ will generate histories; the other types will exit at the specified prices.

Verification of the rank hypotheses

Normalization and monotonicity are immediate. For $e\notin S$, the marginal increment is
$$ F_\mu(S\cup\{e\})-F_\mu(S) = \min\left\{ n\mu_e,\ \max\{0,G-n\mu(S)\} \right\}, $$
where $\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t$. This expression decreases as $S$ increases, proving submodularity.

The singleton bounds follow from the definition:
$$ F_\mu(\{t\})\le n\mu_t=\mu_tK_t. $$

Every type satisfies $n\mu_t\le D_0$: this is equality for type $0$, and for $i\ge 1$ follows from
$$ n\mu_i=D_{i-1}-D_i\le D_{i-1}\le D_0. $$
Consequently,
$$ n\sum_{s\ne t}\mu_s =n-n\mu_t \ge n-D_0 =G. $$
Thus
$$ F_\mu(T\setminus\{t\})=F_\mu(T)=G>0. $$

Finally,
$$ F_\mu(S) = n\min\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n\mu_i,\ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t \right\}. $$
This formula scales linearly under common rescaling of all population masses.

3. Exact clinching dynamics

For a current cohort $C$, write
$$ b_C=a_C\min(d_C,n-k_C). $$
Let
$$ X=\sum_Ca_Ck_C,\qquad U=\sum_Cb_C,\qquad s=G-X,\qquad D=U-s. $$

Lemma 1: the feasible region and clinching amount

For this rank, the transaction region is
$$ R=\left\{ y\ge 0: y_C\le b_C\text{ for all }C,\quad \sum_Cy_C\le s \right\}. $$
The specified clinching amount is
$$ r_C=\min\{b_C,\max\{0,b_C-D\}\}. $$
In particular, when $D\ge 0$,
$$ r_C=\max\{0,b_C-D\}. $$

Proof. The rank constraints require both the total-supply bound and the bounds
$$ x(S)+\sum_{C:t(C)\in S}y_C\le n\mu(S). $$
The latter follow from the cohort bounds, because for each type $t$,
$$ x_t+\sum_{C:t(C)=t}b_C \le \sum_{C:t(C)=t}a_Cn =n\mu_t. $$
Conversely, the original constraint for $T$ gives the total-supply bound. This proves the displayed description of $R$.

When $y_C=0$, the other coordinates can attain every total between zero and
$$ \min\{s,U-b_C\}. $$
Fixing $y_C=r$ preserves their entire feasible projection precisely when
$$ 0\le r\le b_C,\qquad s-r\ge\min\{s,U-b_C\}. $$
Solving these inequalities gives the claimed formula. $\square$

Lemma 2: a clinch preserves the deficit

Every clinch preserves $D=U-s$. When $D\ge 0$, the children of a visited cohort have combined transaction bound
$$ \min\{b_C,D\}. $$

Proof. A child receiving $j$ goods satisfies
$$ \min(d_C-j,n-k_C-j) =\min(d_C,n-k_C)-j. $$
The permitted increments stay within both demand and remaining cap. Weighting this identity by the child masses shows that their combined bound is $b_C-r_C$. Both $U$ and $s$ therefore decrease by $r_C$, preserving $D$.

For $D\ge 0$, Lemma 1 gives
$$ b_C-r_C=\min\{b_C,D\}. $$
$\square$

No budget updates occur

Every event price is at most $V$, because every valuation is at most $V$. Hence every person pays at most $nV$, and every demand is at most $n+1$. For every active cohort,
$$ \frac{B-p_C}{d_C} \ge \frac{(2n+2)V-nV}{n+1} = \frac{n+2}{n+1}V >V. $$
Thus budget equality never occurs. The trigger exits occur at $c_1,\ldots,c_n$, followed by type-zero valuation updates at $V$.

Until those final updates, every type-zero cohort satisfies
$$ d_C=n+1-k_C, $$
so it remains active, even when at cap, and
$$ b_C=a_C(n-k_C). $$

The deficit after trigger exit $i$ is exactly $D_i$

Initially $U=n$ and $s=G$, so the deficit is $D_0$.

An unallocated trigger type $j$ has bound
$$ A_j=n\mu_j=D_{j-1}-D_j. $$
At the sweep following trigger exit $i$, every future trigger $j>i$ satisfies
$$ A_j\le D_{j-1}\le D_i. $$
It therefore clinches zero by Lemma 1. Inductively, all triggers remain unallocated until their valuation exits.

Exit $i$ removes exactly $D_{i-1}-D_i$ from $U$, leaving $s$ unchanged. Subsequent clinches preserve the deficit. Therefore the sweep at $c_i$ has deficit exactly $D_i$, regardless of any merging among type-zero cohorts.

4. Histories, merging, and extra early allocation

Suppose no type-zero cohorts have merged before stage $i$.

After stage $i-1$, each type-zero cohort has mass
$$ a_{i-1}=\frac{\alpha}{2^{i-1}} $$
and goods count either $i-2$ or $i-1$.

At the first stage,
$$ \frac{r}{\alpha} =n-\frac{D_1}{\alpha} =\frac12. $$
At stage $i\ge 2$,
$$ \frac{D_i}{a_{i-1}}=n-i+\frac12. $$
Consequently, the per-person clinch is $3/2$ for a parent with $k=i-2$, and $1/2$ for a parent with $k=i-1$.

Every parent therefore splits into equal halves with goods counts $i-1$ and $i$. The rule prohibiting visits to new children during the same sweep is essential here.

Label a history by bits $z_1,\ldots,z_i$, where
$$ k_i=i-1+z_i. $$
Its purchase increments are $z_1$ at the first stage and
$$ 1+z_h-z_{h-1} $$
at stage $h\ge 2$. Its payment is therefore
$$ p_i = \sum_{h=2}^i c_h +c_i z_i -\sum_{h=1}^{i-1}(c_{h+1}-c_h)z_h. $$

For a fixed $z_i$, two histories have identical payment precisely when their earlier bits give equal subset sums of the price gaps
$$ c_2-c_1,\ldots,c_i-c_{i-1}. $$
They then also have identical goods count and demand, so they merge. Histories with different $z_i$ have different goods counts.

The following bound handles the auction after merging, without assuming that the original history formula continues to describe it.

Lemma 3: an earlier merge increases allocation before the final price

Let $P$ be the number of type-zero cohorts present at the start of the sweep at $c_n$, and put
$$ a=\frac{\alpha}{2^n}=D_n. $$
Immediately after that sweep,
$$ U_0\le Pa, $$
where $U_0$ is the combined type-zero transaction bound.

If any merge occurred by the end of stage $n-1$, then
$$ P\le 2^{n-1}-1, $$
and the type-zero allocation $X_{\mathrm{pre}}$ before price $V$ satisfies
$$ X_{\mathrm{pre}} \ge \alpha\left(n-\frac12\right)+a. $$

Proof. By Lemma 2, the children of each parent visited at stage $n$ have combined bound at most $D_n=a$. Summing over the $P$ parents gives $U_0\le Pa$. Merging after the sweep preserves this sum.

Every parent produces at most two children. A merge by stage $n-1$ makes the cohort count strictly smaller than the maximum obtainable by repeated doubling; subsequent stages cannot recover that maximum. Thus $P\le 2^{n-1}-1$.

All triggers have exited without receiving goods. For type zero, demand still exceeds remaining cap by one, so
$$ U_0=\alpha n-X_{\mathrm{pre}}. $$
It follows that
$$ X_{\mathrm{pre}} \ge \alpha n-(2^{n-1}-1)a = \alpha\left(n-\frac12\right)+a. $$
$\square$

5. NP-hardness of terminal revenue

The source problem is Equal-Subset-Sum: given positive integers $w_1,\ldots,w_N$, decide whether two disjoint nonempty subsets have equal sums. This problem is NP-complete; see [Woeginger–Yu](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/002001909290226L) and the positive-integer formulation in [Mucha–Nederlof–Pawlewicz–Węgrzycki](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.02424v2).

For positive integers, this is equivalent to asking whether two distinct subsets have equal sums. Indeed, canceling their intersection gives disjoint equal-sum subsets; neither remaining subset can be empty, because all weights are positive.

Let
$$ W=\sum_{h=1}^Nw_h,\qquad n=N+2. $$
Use the auction family above, with prices determined by
$$ c_1=1, $$
$$ c_{i+1}-c_i=w_i \quad(1\le i\le N), $$
and
$$ c_{N+2}-c_{N+1}=W+1. $$
Thus $c_n=2W+2$. Choose
$$ V=(n2^n+2)c_n. $$

Define the rational threshold
$$ \rho = \alpha\left(\frac{c_1}{2}+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\right) + V\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}-a\right), \qquad a=\frac{\alpha}{2^n}. $$

Let terminal revenue be
$$ \operatorname{Rev}(\pi)=\sum_{t,k,p}p\,\pi_{t,k,p}. $$
All triggers pay zero, so this is also type zero’s terminal revenue.

I prove the exact equivalence
$$ \text{Equal-Subset-Sum is a yes-instance} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Rev}(\pi)<\rho. $$

No-instance: terminal revenue equals the threshold

If the original weights have distinct subset sums, adding the gap $W+1$ preserves that property. A sum containing $W+1$ is greater than every sum of the original weights; sums on the same side reduce to original subset sums.

Every prefix of the price-gap list therefore has distinct subset sums. Section 4 proves inductively that no merge occurs and that stage $i$ produces $2^i$ equal-mass cohorts.

The mean goods count after stage $i$ is $i-1/2$. Hence allocation and revenue before $V$ are
$$ X_{\mathrm{pre}}=\alpha\left(n-\frac12\right) $$
and
$$ R_{\mathrm{pre}} = \alpha\left(\frac{c_1}{2}+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\right). $$

At that time, half the type-zero population has $n-1$ goods and half has $n$ goods. Each cohort has mass $a$.

At price $V$, lexicographic valuation processing first selects a cohort with $n-1$ goods. Its exit removes bound $a=D_n$, making the deficit zero. The ensuing sweep gives every other uncapped cohort its last good. Later updates remove only zero transaction bounds.

The terminal allocation is consequently
$$ X_{\mathrm{term}}=\alpha n-a. $$
The definition of $\alpha$ gives
$$ n=\alpha(2n-2^{-n}), $$
so
$$ G=n(1-\alpha)=\alpha n-a. $$
Thus purchases at $V$ total
$$ X_{\mathrm{term}}-X_{\mathrm{pre}} =\frac{\alpha}{2}-a. $$
Therefore
$$ \operatorname{Rev}(\pi) = R_{\mathrm{pre}} + V\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}-a\right) =\rho. $$

Yes-instance: terminal revenue is strictly below the threshold

Suppose the original weights have a subset-sum collision. A type-zero merge must occur by stage $N+1=n-1$.

To see this, if a merge has already occurred there is nothing to prove. Otherwise all $2^{N+1}$ histories exist at stage $N+1$. Choose equal-sum subsets of $w_1,\ldots,w_N$ and give the corresponding histories the same last bit. The payment formula in Section 4 makes their states identical, so they merge.

Lemma 3 therefore gives
$$ X_{\mathrm{pre}} \ge \alpha\left(n-\frac12\right)+a. $$

Before $V$, each type-zero person buys at most $n$ goods, each at price at most $c_n$. Thus
$$ R_{\mathrm{pre}}\le \alpha n c_n. $$
Feasibility always gives $X_{\mathrm{term}}\le G$. All subsequent purchases occur at $V$, so
$$ \operatorname{Rev}(\pi) = R_{\mathrm{pre}}+V(X_{\mathrm{term}}-X_{\mathrm{pre}}) \le \alpha n c_n + V\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}-2a\right). $$

Our choice of $V$ ensures
$$ Va = (n2^n+2)c_n\frac{\alpha}{2^n} = \alpha n c_n+2ac_n > \alpha n c_n. $$
Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{Rev}(\pi) < V\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}-a\right) \le \rho. $$

This completes the many-one reduction to the terminal-revenue decision problem.

6. Encoding bounds and the full-table reduction

Put
$$ H=2n2^n-1. $$
The masses have the explicit representations
$$ \mu_0=\frac{n2^n}{H},\qquad \mu_1=\frac{2^{n-1}}{H}, $$
and, for $i\ge 2$,
$$ \mu_i=\frac{2^{n-i}(2n-2i+5)}{H}. $$
Their numerators and denominators have $O(n+\log n)$ bits.

The prices, valuations and budgets have $O(n+\log n+\log W)$ bits. The threshold $\rho$ has polynomial bit length. Therefore the reduction constructs an instance with
$$ m=n(n+1),\qquad \tau=n+1, $$
and $L$ polynomial in the source encoding length.

The rank description is simply
$$ F_\mu(S)=\min\{G,n\mu(S)\}. $$
It is evaluated by rational summation and comparison in polynomial time. Every answer has denominator dividing $H$ and numerator at most $nH$, so its bit length is polynomial as required.

Terminal revenue itself has polynomial bit length

Initially every cohort mass is an integer multiple of $1/H$. This property persists.

Indeed, when it holds, $b_C$, $U$, $s$, $D$ and $r_C$ are integer multiples of $1/H$. The two child masses are
$$ r_C-q a_C \quad\text{and}\quad a_C-(r_C-q a_C), $$
so they have the same property. Merging also preserves it.

All event prices are integers, so all per-person payments are integers, bounded by $nV$. Hence terminal revenue has denominator dividing $H$ and numerator at most $nVH$, a polynomial-bit quantity.

The execution also terminates: each valuation update removes at least $1/H$ of active population mass, and sweeps never reactivate inactive people.

Thus exact terminal-revenue computation is NP-hard even when its output is polynomially short.

Reduction to the explicit terminal-table function

Let $A$ be the auction-instance map above. Given the original weights and the explicitly returned table $Y$, let the decoder:

  • compute $\operatorname{Rev}(Y)$ by summing $p\,\pi_{t,k,p}$;
  • compute $\rho$;
  • return whether $\operatorname{Rev}(Y)<\rho$.

The decoder is polynomial-time in the combined encoding length of the weights and table. Exact rational summation has polynomial bit complexity in that length. Section 5 proves its correctness.

This is a polynomial-time metric reduction from the characteristic function of Equal-Subset-Sum to the explicit terminal-table function. Since Equal-Subset-Sum is NP-complete, the latter function is NP-hard under the stated convention.

7. Explicit output can also be exponentially large

For completeness, the output-size obstruction from the notes follows from the same family and can be proved independently of the hardness reduction.

Take
$$ c_i=2^i-1,\qquad V=2^n. $$
The price gaps are $2,4,\ldots,2^{n-1}$, whose subset sums are distinct. Thus stage $n$ produces exactly $2^n$ type-zero cohorts of mass
$$ a=\frac{\alpha}{2^n}. $$

Write
$$ A=\sum_{h=2}^n c_h,\qquad S(z)=\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}2^h z_h. $$
The pre-final payment formula is
$$ p_n=A+c_nz_n-S(z). $$

As proved in the no-instance analysis, one cohort with $z_n=0$ exits first at $V$ and keeps $n-1$ goods. Every other such cohort buys one last good; cohorts with $z_n=1$ are already capped.

The terminal payments of capped cohorts are therefore
$$ A+V-S(z) $$
for the former family, and
$$ A+V-1-S(z) $$
for the latter.

Within each family, payments are distinct by uniqueness of binary expansion. Between the families, payments have opposite parity because every $S(z)$ is even. The first exiting cohort has a different goods count from every capped cohort.

Hence type zero contributes exactly $2^n$ nonzero terminal-table entries. In this family,
$$ m=n(n+1),\qquad \tau=n+1,\qquad L=O(n^2). $$
Explicitly writing the table therefore cannot take time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, regardless of the efficiency of oracle access.

8. Scope of the classification

The explicit terminal-table function is NP-hard under the stated polynomial-time metric reductions, and its output can be exponentially large. The terminal-revenue decision problem has conventional many-one NP-hardness even though the relevant revenue is a single polynomial-bit rational.

The proof does not classify access models that return only one specified table entry, or arbitrary succinct representations of the table. It also does not establish a separate lower bound on the number of value-oracle calls.
