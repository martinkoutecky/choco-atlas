The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of disutility types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(r\) chore classes with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j>0\), and type costs \(d_{tj}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_j q_jd_{tj}=1\), let \(\delta_t=\max_j d_{tj}\) and \(W(\delta)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N V_N(\delta/N)\), where \(V_N\) is the guarantee in Theorem 2 for \(N\) agents. For integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\), assign mass \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) so that \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=q_j\) for every type and chore class. Minimize \(\rho=\sup_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0}(\sum_jd_{tj}b_j)/W(\delta_t)\); in particular, determine whether an allocation with \(\rho\le1\) can be constructed in time polynomial in the compressed input size, including for fixed \(r\).

The last assertion has a direct constructive proof. It does not require expanding the rational masses into explicitly listed agents or chores.

Throughout, rationals are encoded by binary numerators and denominators.

1. A polynomial construction at \(R=1\)

We first prove a statement about one cost vector.

Lemma. Let \(q_j>0\), \(d_j\ge0\), and \(\sum_jq_jd_j=1\). Put \(\delta=\max_jd_j\) and \(k=\lfloor1/\delta\rfloor\). In polynomial time, one can construct a rational probability distribution \(y_b\) on at most \(m+1\) integral bundles satisfying

\(\sum_by_b=1,\qquad \sum_bb_jy_b=q_j\quad\text{for every }j,\)

and

\(\sum_jd_jb_j\le\max\{(k+1)\delta,(k+2)/(k+1)\}=W(\delta)\)

for every bundle of positive probability.

1.1. Batched filling algorithm

Represent a distribution by groups \((c,b)\), where \(c>0\) is the probability mass receiving bundle \(b\). Initially there is one group \((1,0)\).

Process all positive-cost classes in nonincreasing order of \(d_j\). For the current class \(j\), write \(p=d_j>0\), and let \(Q=q_j\) be its remaining supply.

A group is active when its current cost \(\ell=d\cdot b\) satisfies \(\ell\le1\). For an active group \((c,b)\), define

\(h=\left\lfloor(1-\ell)/p\right\rfloor+1.\)

Thus

\(\ell+(h-1)p\le1<\ell+hp.\)

Process active groups until \(Q=0\):

  • If \(Q\ge hc\), give every member of the group \(h\) chores of class \(j\), and replace \(Q\) by \(Q-hc\). The resulting group has cost greater than \(1\), so it receives no later positive-cost chores.
  • If \(Q<hc\), put \(a=\lfloor Q/c\rfloor\) and \(z=Q-ac\). Replace the group by mass \(c-z\) receiving \(a\) chores and mass \(z\) receiving \(a+1\) chores, omitting any zero-mass group. This allocates exactly \(Q\) chores, because

\((c-z)a+z(a+1)=ac+z=Q.\)
Set \(Q=0\).

Previous bundle coordinates are retained in every replacement.

Why sufficient active mass always exists. The average cost after completing the current class would be the total cost of all classes processed so far. This is at most \(\sum_jq_jd_j=1\).

Suppose instead that positive remaining supply persisted after every active group had been filled with its \(h\) chores. Every group would then have cost strictly greater than \(1\): previously inactive groups already had that property, and all active groups would have acquired it. Consequently, the current average cost would exceed \(1\).

But the current average cost is at most the average after allocating the entire current class, which is at most \(1\). This contradiction proves that the procedure completes every positive-cost class.

1.2. Proof of the Hill bound

Consider a final bundle with at least one positive-cost chore. Let \(C\) be its cost, let \(s\) be its number of positive-cost chores, and let \(p\) be the cost of its last positive-cost chore.

The batching rule ensures that its cost immediately before that last chore was at most \(1\). Indeed, every group receives at most its corresponding \(h\) chores in a batch. Therefore,

\(C-p\le1.\)

Because classes were processed in nonincreasing cost order, every positive-cost chore in the bundle costs at least \(p\). Thus

\(sp\le C.\)

There are two cases.

If \(s\le k+1\), every chore costs at most \(\delta\), so

\(C\le s\delta\le(k+1)\delta.\)

If \(s\ge k+2\), then \(s\ge2\), and

\(C\le1+p\le1+C/s.\)

Rearranging gives

\(C\le s/(s-1)\le(k+2)/(k+1).\)

Thus every resulting bundle satisfies \(C\le W(\delta)\). Bundles with no positive-cost chores have cost zero.

1.3. Zero-cost classes

For a class with \(d_j=0\), choose any group \((c,b)\). Put

\(a=\lfloor q_j/c\rfloor,\qquad z=q_j-ac.\)

Split this group into masses \(c-z\) and \(z\), receiving respectively \(a\) and \(a+1\) chores of class \(j\). This supplies exactly \(q_j\), and changes no bundle cost.

Apply this separately to every zero-cost class.

1.4. Support size and bit complexity

A full-group filling replaces one group by one group. Each class creates at most one partial split, increasing the group count by at most one. Starting with one group, the final support therefore has size at most \(m+1\). Each positive-cost class scans at most \(m+1\) groups, so there are \(O(m^2)\) group operations.

For the bit bound, let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of the supplies \(q_j\). Its binary length is at most their total input length. All group masses remain integer multiples of \(1/D\), because the algorithm changes masses only by operations of the form

\(Q\leftarrow Q-hc,\qquad z=Q-ac.\)

In particular, every positive group mass is at least \(1/D\).

For a positive-cost class,

\(h\le\lfloor1/d_j\rfloor+1,\)

so all its bundle counts have polynomial binary length. For a zero-cost class, the count is at most \(Dq_j+1\), which also has polynomial binary length. Costs, floor divisions, supplies, and masses can consequently all be handled by polynomial-time exact rational arithmetic.

This proves the lemma.

1.5. Combining the types

For each type \(t\), apply the lemma to its cost vector \(d_t\) and the same supply vector \(q\). The hypothesis holds by the input normalization. Let the resulting distribution be \(y_{t,b}\), and set

\(x_{t,b}=\mu_t y_{t,b}.\)

Then

\(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t,\)

and, for every class \(j\),

\(\sum_{t,b}b_jx_{t,b}=\sum_t\mu_tq_j=q_j.\)

Every used bundle satisfies \(d_t\cdot b\le W(\delta_t)\), so \(\rho(x)\le1\).

There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) listed type–bundle pairs, all with polynomial encoding length. Running the preceding algorithm for every type takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus the requested construction exists for every valid instance, without a feasibility promise.

2. A bounded configuration formulation for arbitrary \(R\)

Set

\(C_t=R\,W(\delta_t).\)

Normalization implies \(\delta_t>0\). Moreover, \(C_t\) can be computed exactly in polynomial time: computing \(\lfloor1/\delta_t\rfloor\) requires binary integer division, not iteration over that integer.

For every type and class, define the integer bound

\(B_{tj}=\lfloor C_t/d_{tj}\rfloor\quad\text{if }d_{tj}>0,\)

and

\(B_{tj}=\lceil q_j/\mu_t\rceil\quad\text{if }d_{tj}=0.\)

Let

\(\mathcal B_t=\{b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m:d_t\cdot b\le C_t,\ b_j\le B_{tj}\text{ for every }j\}.\)

Each \(\mathcal B_t\) is finite, and all its coordinate bounds have polynomial binary length.

2.1. Why the zero-cost bounds preserve feasibility

Positive-cost coordinates automatically satisfy their stated bounds.

For a zero-cost coordinate of type \(t\), let its conditional mean under a feasible allocation be

\(z_{tj}=\mu_t^{-1}\sum_bb_jx_{t,b}.\)

Nonnegativity and the global supply equation imply \(z_{tj}\le q_j/\mu_t\).

Replace that coordinate by a rational mixture of \(\lfloor z_{tj}\rfloor\) and \(\lceil z_{tj}\rceil\) having mean \(z_{tj}\). Perform these replacements independently for all zero-cost coordinates, retaining the distribution of positive-cost coordinates.

The resulting distribution is finitely supported and rational. It preserves every coordinate mean and every bundle cost, while satisfying all bounds \(B_{tj}\).

Hence an allocation is feasible exactly when one exists using the finite sets \(\mathcal B_t\).

2.2. Convex-hull equivalence

Let \(e_t\) denote the \(t\)-th unit vector in \(\mathbb R^\tau\), and define

\(P_R=\operatorname{conv}\{(e_t,b):t\in T,\ b\in\mathcal B_t\}\subseteq\mathbb R^{\tau+m}.\)

Writing \(v=(\mu,q)\), feasibility is equivalent to

\(v\in P_R.\)

Indeed, allocation masses give the convex-combination coefficients, since their total is \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Conversely, the type coordinates of a convex combination enforce the type masses, and its bundle coordinates enforce the supplies.

A real convex combination here can always be replaced by a rational one, as justified next. Thus this equivalence respects the required rational allocation masses.

3. Membership in NP

Put \(N=\tau+m\). Every generating point of \(P_R\) is integral, has polynomial coordinate length, and lies in the affine hyperplane

\(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}u_t=1.\)

By Carathéodory’s theorem, a feasible rational vector \(v\) has a representation using at most \(N\) affinely independent generating points.

These points are also linearly independent. Any linear dependence among them has coefficients summing to zero, by their type coordinates, and would therefore be an affine dependence.

Choose an invertible square minor of their column matrix. Their coefficients are obtained from this minor and the corresponding coordinates of \(v\). Cramer’s rule shows that the coefficients are rational. Hadamard’s determinant bound shows that their numerators and denominators have polynomial binary length, because the matrix dimension and all entry lengths are polynomially bounded.

A certificate therefore lists at most \(\tau+m\) type–bundle pairs and their masses. Exact rational arithmetic verifies nonnegativity, type masses, supply equations, and all cost bounds in polynomial time.

The unrestricted decision problem belongs to NP.

4. NP-hardness with one type

We give a polynomial reduction from PARTITION with positive integer inputs \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and even total

\(A=\sum_{i=1}^na_i.\)

This restriction remains NP-hard: doubling every number in an arbitrary PARTITION instance preserves its answer and gives even total. We may assume \(n\ge2\).

Define

\(\beta=n+1,\qquad H=nA+1,\qquad u_i=H\beta^{i-1},\qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^nu_i,\)

and

\(M=(n+1)(S+A+1),\qquad C=nM+S+A/2.\)

Introduce \(2n\) chore classes, indexed by \((i,0)\) and \((i,1)\), with integer weights

\(w_{i,0}=M+u_i,\qquad w_{i,1}=M+u_i+a_i.\)

There is one type of mass \(1\). Set

\(q_{i,0}=q_{i,1}=1/2,\qquad d_{i,\sigma}=w_{i,\sigma}/C.\)

All costs and supplies are positive, and

\(\sum_{i,\sigma}q_{i,\sigma}d_{i,\sigma}=(nM+S+A/2)/C=1.\)

Compute \(\delta=\max_{i,\sigma}d_{i,\sigma}\), and choose

\(R=1/W(\delta).\)

The allowed bundle cost is exactly \(R\,W(\delta)=1\). All these numbers have polynomial encoding length: in particular, \(\beta^{n-1}\) has \(O(n\log n)\) bits.

4.1. Exact-weight bundles encode partitions

Suppose an integral bundle satisfies

\(\sum_{i,\sigma}w_{i,\sigma}b_{i,\sigma}=C.\)

Let \(h=\sum_{i,\sigma}b_{i,\sigma}\).

Since every weight is at least \(M\), and \(C<(n+1)M\), we have \(h\le n\).

If \(h\le n-1\), every weight is at most \(M+S+A\), so

\(\sum_{i,\sigma}w_{i,\sigma}b_{i,\sigma}\le(n-1)(M+S+A)<nM<C.\)

The strict middle inequality follows from \(M>(n-1)(S+A)\). Thus \(h=n\).

Subtracting \(nM\) from the exact-weight equation gives

\(\sum_i u_i(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1})+\sum_i a_ib_{i,1}=S+A/2.\)

The first sum and \(S\) are multiples of \(H\). Also,

\(0\le\sum_i a_ib_{i,1}\le nA<H,\qquad 0\le A/2<H.\)

Reduction modulo \(H\) therefore yields the equality

\(\sum_i a_ib_{i,1}=A/2.\)

After subtracting this equality and dividing by \(H\), we obtain

\(\sum_i\beta^{i-1}(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1})=\sum_i\beta^{i-1}.\)

Every coefficient \(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1}\) lies between zero and \(n=\beta-1\). Uniqueness of base-\(\beta\) expansion consequently implies

\(b_{i,0}+b_{i,1}=1\quad\text{for every }i.\)

Hence the indices with \(b_{i,1}=1\) form a partition of weight \(A/2\).

4.2. Equivalence with allocation feasibility

If a feasible allocation exists, every used bundle has normalized cost at most \(1\). Its average normalized cost is

\(\sum_bd\cdot b\,x_b=\sum_{i,\sigma}d_{i,\sigma}q_{i,\sigma}=1.\)

Since the total mass is \(1\), every positive-mass bundle must have cost exactly \(1\). Any such bundle satisfies the exact-weight equation above and yields a partition.

Conversely, suppose a partition exists. Construct a bundle choosing class \((i,1)\) for indices in the partition and class \((i,0)\) for the others. Construct a second bundle making the opposite choices.

Each bundle has weight \(C\), because both sides of the partition have weight \(A/2\). Give each bundle mass \(1/2\). Every class then has supply \(1/2\), and every used bundle has normalized cost \(1\).

This proves the reduction.

Together with Section 3, the unrestricted problem is NP-complete, even with one type and strictly positive costs. The reduction uses \(R<1\), because \(W(\delta)>1\).

5. Polynomial-time decision and construction for fixed \(r\)

Fix \(m=r\). We use two standard algorithmic results:

  • Integer linear programming with a fixed number of integer variables is polynomial-time solvable. Bounded linear optimization follows by clearing objective denominators and binary-searching its integer objective value. [Lenstra, *Integer Programming with a Fixed Number of Variables*](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rrw/presentations/Lenstra81.pdf)
  • For a rational polytope with polynomially bounded description complexity, strong optimization gives polynomial-time strong separation. It also permits polynomial-time decomposition of a rational point into affinely independent vertices with rational coefficients. These are Theorems 6.4.9 and 6.5.11 of [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, *Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization*](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf).

We verify the hypotheses for \(P_R\).

5.1. Strong optimization oracle

For any rational objective \((a,p)\in\mathbb Q^\tau\times\mathbb Q^m\),

\(\max_{(u,z)\in P_R}(a\cdot u+p\cdot z)=\max_t\left(a_t+\max_{b\in\mathcal B_t}p\cdot b\right).\)

For each type, the inner problem is the bounded integer linear program

\(\max p\cdot b,\qquad d_t\cdot b\le C_t,\qquad 0\le b_j\le B_{tj},\qquad b\in\mathbb Z^m.\)

It has exactly \(m\) integer variables. Clearing rational denominators preserves polynomial encoding length. The coordinate bounds give an objective range with polynomial binary length, so Lenstra’s algorithm and binary search produce an exact maximizer in polynomial time for fixed \(m\).

Solving these problems for all \(\tau\) types gives a polynomial-time strong optimization oracle for \(P_R\).

5.2. Description complexity and recovery

The polytope is bounded and nonempty: every \(\mathcal B_t\) contains the empty bundle.

Its vertices are among the integral generating points, whose coordinate lengths are polynomially bounded by Section 2. Standard determinant bounds therefore give polynomial bounds on its affine-hull and facet encoding lengths. Thus \(P_R\) is a well-described rational polytope. The oracle results apply also when it has lower affine dimension.

Strong separation decides whether \(v=(\mu,q)\) belongs to \(P_R\). If it does, the constructive decomposition theorem returns rational coefficients and affinely independent vertices. Each vertex has the form \((e_t,b)\) with \(b\in\mathcal B_t\), so the decomposition coefficients are the desired allocation masses.

Because \(\dim P_R\le\tau+m-1\), at most \(\tau+m\) type–bundle pairs are needed.

For every fixed \(r\), both decision and construction are polynomial-time solvable. The dependence on the fixed dimension need not be polynomial when \(r\) varies; the separate construction in Section 1 supplies that stronger bound specifically at \(R=1\).
