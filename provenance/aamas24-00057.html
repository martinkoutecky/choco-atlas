The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a planar graph \(G=(V,E)\), candidates \(C\), target \(b\in C\), approval map \(a:V\to C\), integer \(k\), and rational masses \(\mu_v>0\) with \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\), does there exist a partition \(V=D_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\dot\cup}D_k\) into nonempty connected districts such that for some \(D_i\), \(\sum_{v\in D_i:a(v)=b}\mu_v>\sum_{v\in D_i:a(v)=c}\mu_v\) for every \(c\in C\setminus\{b\}\)?

An instance consists of a planar graph \(G=(V,E)\), candidates \(C\), a target \(b\in C\), an approval map \(a:V o C\), an integer \(k\), and rational masses \(\mu_v>0\) with \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\). A district \(D\subseteq V\) is connected when \(G[D]\) is connected; \(b\) wins \(D\) when \(\sum_{v\in D:a(v)=b}\mu_v>\sum_{v\in D:a(v)=c}\mu_v\) for every \(c\in C\setminus\{b\}\).

Target. Is deciding whether \(V\) admits a partition into \(k\) nonempty connected districts with at least one district won by \(b\) solvable in polynomial time, or NP-hard?

Nearest known results. For unweighted planar graphs with an unrestricted number of candidates, deciding whether a partition with one winning district exists is NP-hard. The rational-mass formulation above permits arbitrary positive rational vertex masses summing to \(1\).

Complexity Classification

Theorem

Under the stated known NP-hardness result, the rational-mass problem is \( \mathrm{NP} \)-complete. In particular, it is \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hard even when every vertex has the same mass.

Proof of NP-hardness

Consider the known unweighted problem from the prompt. An instance consists of a planar graph \(G=(V,E)\), candidates \(C\), a target candidate \(b\), an approval map \(a:V\to C\), and an integer \(k\). Candidate \(b\) wins a district \(D\) precisely when

\[ \left|\{v\in D:a(v)=b\}\right| > \left|\{v\in D:a(v)=c\}\right| \]

for every \(c\in C\setminus\{b\}\). By hypothesis, deciding whether there is a partition into \(k\) nonempty connected districts having at least one such district is NP-hard, even when \(G\) is planar.

Let \(n=|V|\). The existence of positive masses summing to \(1\) implies that relevant instances have \(n\geq 1\). Construct an instance of the rational-mass problem by retaining \(G,C,b,a,k\) and assigning

\[ \mu_v=\frac{1}{n} \]

to every \(v\in V\). These masses are positive and satisfy

\[ \sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=n\cdot\frac1n=1. \]

For any district \(D\subseteq V\) and candidate \(c\in C\),

\[ \sum_{v\in D:a(v)=c}\mu_v = \frac{\left|\{v\in D:a(v)=c\}\right|}{n}. \]

Because multiplication by the positive number \(n\) preserves strict inequalities, for every \(c\neq b\),

\[ \sum_{v\in D:a(v)=b}\mu_v > \sum_{v\in D:a(v)=c}\mu_v \]

holds if and only if

\[ \left|\{v\in D:a(v)=b\}\right| > \left|\{v\in D:a(v)=c\}\right|. \]

Thus \(b\) wins exactly the same subsets \(D\) in the unweighted instance and in the rational-mass instance. Connectivity, nonemptiness, the graph, and the required number \(k\) of districts are unchanged. Consequently, the original instance is a yes-instance if and only if the constructed rational-mass instance is a yes-instance.

Each mass \(1/n\) has binary encoding length \(O(\log n)\), so the construction has polynomial size and is computable in polynomial time. It also preserves planarity. This proves NP-hardness.

Membership in \( \mathrm{NP} \)

A certificate assigns each vertex one of the \(k\) district labels.

It can be verified in polynomial time that:

  • every district label is used;
  • vertices with each label induce a connected subgraph, using graph search;
  • for at least one district, the rational mass of \(b\)'s supporters is strictly greater than that of every other candidate.

Sums and comparisons of binary-encoded rational numbers can be performed using integer arithmetic whose bit complexity is polynomial in the input length. Hence the problem belongs to \( \mathrm{NP} \).

Combining membership in \( \mathrm{NP} \) with the reduction above proves \( \mathrm{NP} \)-completeness. Therefore it is not polynomial-time solvable unless \( \mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP} \).

Overall assessment

The prover correctly reduces the stated unweighted planar problem to the rational-mass problem by assigning every vertex mass \(1/n\). The reduction preserves exactly which districts are won by \(b\), as well as connectivity, nonemptiness, the number of districts, and planarity.

The membership-in-\(\mathrm{NP}\) argument is also correct. Therefore, accepting the nearest known NP-hardness result supplied in the task, the prover establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness and hence fully answers the requested polynomial-time-versus-\(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard classification.

Step-by-step verification

  • Source problem. The prover uses precisely the unweighted planar problem identified as NP-hard in the prompt, with the same graph, candidates, target, approval map, and district requirement.
  • Mass construction. Setting \(\mu_v=1/n\) is valid for \(n=|V|\geq 1\). Every mass is positive and their sum is \(1\).
  • Preservation of winning districts. For every district \(D\) and candidate \(c\), the rational mass of \(c\)'s supporters is their count divided by \(n\). Since \(n>0\), the strict rational-mass comparison is equivalent to the corresponding strict count comparison. This verifies the claimed equivalence for every candidate \(c\neq b\).
  • Preservation of feasible partitions. The construction does not change the graph, vertex set, or \(k\). Thus nonemptiness, induced connectivity, and the requirement of exactly \(k\) districts are preserved.
  • Polynomial reduction size. The rational number \(1/n\) has \(O(\log n)\) binary encoding length. Listing it for all \(n\) vertices produces polynomial output size, and planarity is unchanged.
  • Certificate verification. A labeling of the vertices identifies a proposed partition. Checking that all \(k\) labels occur and that every labeled vertex set induces a connected subgraph is polynomial-time. If \(k>n\), the instance can immediately be rejected.
  • Rational arithmetic. Exact sums and strict comparisons of binary-encoded rationals have polynomial bit complexity relative to the total input length. Iterating over the explicitly represented candidates is also polynomial.
  • Conclusion. The reduction proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, and the certificate argument proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\). The claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness therefore follows.
