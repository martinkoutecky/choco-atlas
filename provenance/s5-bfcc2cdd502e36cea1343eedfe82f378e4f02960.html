The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of schools \(C\), a finite support of student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\), each type’s school preferences and priority attributes, and finite-stage monotone, non-excessive-reduction choice protocols encoded by rational piecewise-affine rules, determine whether there is an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|C|\), \(|T|\), input bit length \(L\), and protocol size that computes the unique student-optimal procedurally stable mass assignment. Does the induced mechanism remain group-strategyproof against positive-mass coalitions?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) student types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) schools; each type strictly ranks schools and the unmatched option \(\varnothing\), and cares only about its school, not its admission stage.
School \(c\) has \(K_c\) stages, each with a fixed strict ranking \(\succ_{c,s}\) of priority-attribute classes and an eligibility set. Students within one class have equal priority. Its stage capacities are \(q_{c,1}\in[0,1]\) and \(q_{c,s}:[0,1]^{s-1}\to[0,1]\), encoded by explicit rational polyhedral partitions and affine formulas agreeing on shared boundaries; total protocol encoding size is \(S\).
The rational physical capacity is \(\kappa_c\in[0,1]\), with \(\sum_s q_{c,s}(0)=\kappa_c\). For every recursively feasible vacancy history, require \(\sum_s(q_{c,s}(r_{<s})-r_{c,s})\le\kappa_c\). Zero capacities and overlapping eligibility sets are allowed.
Protocols are promised monotone: \(r\le\widetilde r\) implies \(q_{c,s}(r)\le q_{c,s}(\widetilde r)\); non-excessive reduction means \(\sum_{s=1}^{j}[q_{c,s}(\widetilde r_{<s})-q_{c,s}(r_{<s})]\le\sum_{s<j}(\widetilde r_s-r_s)\) for every \(j\) and coordinatewise ordered histories.
A school's choice from applicant masses processes stages in order, admitting eligible remaining mass by priority up to the stage capacity, splitting a partially admitted priority class proportionally among its applicant types; admitted mass is removed before subsequent stages, and vacancies determine subsequent capacities.
An assignment consists of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,c,s}\) and \(x_{t,\varnothing}\) summing to \(\mu_t\) for each type, with vacancies \(r_{c,s}=q_{c,s}(r_{c,<s})-\sum_t x_{t,c,s}\ge0\) and school totals at most \(\kappa_c\). Assigned mass must be mutually acceptable.
It is procedurally stable if each school's stage decomposition equals its protocol's choice from its assigned aggregate mass, and there are no \(t,c,d,s\) with positive type-\(t\) mass at \(d\), \(c\) preferred to \(d\), and \(t\) eligible at \((c,s)\), such that \(r_{c,s}>0\) or stage \(s\) admits positive mass of a strictly lower-priority class; here \(d\) may be \(\varnothing\).
Compare type assignments by first-order stochastic dominance under their school rankings. A student-optimal assignment dominates every procedurally stable assignment for every type; uniqueness includes the stage decomposition.
A coalition may split any positive amount of each type into finitely many report classes and change only school rankings, retaining masses, eligibility and priorities. Group-strategyproofness means no such deviation gives every participating report class a strictly stochastically better normalized assignment under its true ranking; truthful participation has its original type's normalized assignment.

Does every instance admit a unique student-optimal procedurally stable mass assignment, and is its exact computation in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,S\), or NP-hard, where \(L\) is total input bit length?
Does the resulting mechanism remain group-strategyproof against positive-mass coalitions as defined above?

For finite students, monotone protocols admit a unique student-optimal procedurally stable matching, computed by student-proposing deferred acceptance; non-excessive reduction additionally gives group-strategyproofness. [Westkamp](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/74644/1/74743543X.pdf).
For continuum students with ordinary school capacities, stable matching has a supply-and-demand characterization; this does not address these stage-dependent protocols and finite-support rationing conventions. [Azevedo–Leshno](https://eduardomazevedo.github.io/papers/Azevedo-Leshno-Supply-and-Demand-Matching.pdf).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Positive-mass types contain indistinguishable students, so priority ties within an immutable attribute class are resolved by proportional mass rationing; preference reports cannot alter priority.
  • Student preferences are strict and include an unmatched option; school eligibility is stage-specific, eligibility sets may overlap, and students are indifferent between stages at the same school.
  • Student optimality and strict improvement of mass assignments use first-order stochastic dominance of normalized school distributions.
  • Procedural stability retains both the prohibition of vacancy or lower-priority blocking and the requirement to allocate assigned mass through the school's complete stage protocol, including earlier-stage admission.
  • Stage-capacity functions have domain and range normalized to the unit society, use explicit continuous rational piecewise-affine encodings, and satisfy the two protocol restrictions as promises.
  • The supplied physical capacities bound actual total admissions for every feasible vacancy history; target stage capacities sum to the physical capacity.
  • Coalitions can split types into finitely many preference-report classes with arbitrary positive masses, retaining immutable attributes; every participating class must strictly improve, matching the anchor's all-members-strictly-benefit convention.
  • Existence and uniqueness are included as questions because the discrete theorem does not establish them for the chosen mass model.

Conclusion

No. A student-optimal procedurally stable assignment need not exist under the stated definition. This prevents both the proposed computation task and the resulting student-optimal mechanism from being defined on every instance.

The obstruction already occurs with tied types at a single school. The construction below also includes positive stage capacities, active vacancy transfers, overlapping eligibility, and proportional splitting of a partially admitted class.

1. A rational instance satisfying every protocol promise

There are two schools, \(A\) and \(B\), and three distinct types:

| Type | Mass | School ranking | Priority class |
|---|---:|---|---|
| \(a\) | \(\frac14\) | \(A\succ B\succ\varnothing\) | \(H\) |
| \(u\) | \(\frac38\) | \(A\succ B\succ\varnothing\) | \(M\) |
| \(v\) | \(\frac38\) | \(A\succ\varnothing\succ B\) | \(M\) |

The masses are positive binary rationals and sum to \(1\). Types \(u\) and \(v\) are distinct because their school rankings differ.

Every stage ranks the two priority classes strictly as \(H\succ M\).

School \(A\) has physical capacity \(\kappa_A=\frac12\) and three stages:

  • Stage \(1\) admits only type \(a\), with \(q_{A,1}=\frac38\).
  • Stages \(2\) and \(3\) admit all three types, with

\(q_{A,2}(r_1)=\frac1{16}+\frac12r_1\) and
\(q_{A,3}(r_1,r_2)=\min\{\frac1{16}+\frac12r_1+r_2,1\}\).

School \(B\) has one stage, admitting all types, with \(q_{B,1}=\kappa_B=\frac14\).

Encoding and capacity bounds

All initial stage capacities are positive, and

\(q_{A,1}+q_{A,2}(0)+q_{A,3}(0,0)=\frac38+\frac1{16}+\frac1{16}=\frac12=\kappa_A.\)

The function \(q_{A,2}\) takes values in \([\frac1{16},\frac9{16}]\). The function \(q_{A,3}\) takes values in \([\frac1{16},1]\), with an explicit rational partition along \(r_1/2+r_2=15/16\). Its affine formulas agree on that boundary. Thus the protocol has constant encoding size.

For every recursively feasible history, all vacancies are nonnegative, and

\(\sum_{s=1}^3(q_{A,s}(r_{<s})-r_s) \le \frac38+\left(\frac1{16}+\frac12r_1\right)+\left(\frac1{16}+\frac12r_1+r_2\right)-r_1-r_2-r_3 =\frac12-r_3\le\kappa_A.\)

School \(B\) satisfies its physical-capacity requirement immediately.

Monotonicity and non-excessive reduction

Both variable capacities are coordinatewise nondecreasing.

For ordered histories, write \(\Delta_i=\widetilde r_i-r_i\ge0\). Then

\(q_{A,2}(\widetilde r_1)-q_{A,2}(r_1)=\frac12\Delta_1.\)

Because \(z\mapsto\min\{z,1\}\) is nondecreasing and its increase is at most the increase in its argument,

\(0\le q_{A,3}(\widetilde r_1,\widetilde r_2)-q_{A,3}(r_1,r_2)\le\frac12\Delta_1+\Delta_2.\)

Consequently, the cumulative capacity increases through stages \(2\) and \(3\) are respectively at most \(\Delta_1\) and \(\Delta_1+\Delta_2\). The condition for stage \(1\) is immediate because its capacity is constant. Hence non-excessive reduction holds. It also holds for the constant protocol at \(B\).

2. A family of procedurally stable assignments

Fix any \(\theta\in[0,\frac14]\), and define

\(b(\theta)=\min\{\frac38-\theta,\frac14\}.\)

Consider the following aggregate assignment:

| Type | At \(A\) | At \(B\) | Unmatched |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(a\) | \(\frac14\) | \(0\) | \(0\) |
| \(u\) | \(\theta\) | \(b(\theta)\) | \(\frac38-\theta-b(\theta)\) |
| \(v\) | \(\frac14-\theta\) | \(0\) | \(\frac18+\theta\) |

These masses are nonnegative and sum to each type’s mass. All assigned schools are mutually acceptable.

At \(A\), use this stage decomposition:

  • Stage \(1\) admits \(\frac14\) of type \(a\).
  • Stage \(2\) admits \(\frac{\theta}{2}\) of type \(u\) and \(\frac12(\frac14-\theta)\) of type \(v\).
  • Stage \(3\) admits the same masses as stage \(2\).

The resulting vacancies are \(r_{A,1}=\frac18\) and \(r_{A,2}=r_{A,3}=0\), since

\(q_{A,2}(\frac18)=q_{A,3}(\frac18,0)=\frac18.\)

In particular, the first-stage vacancy actively increases both subsequent capacities above their initial values.

The stage decomposition equals protocol choice

Apply \(A\)’s protocol to its assigned aggregate mass.

Stage \(1\) admits all assigned type-\(a\) mass. The remaining applicants are types \(u\) and \(v\), in the same priority class, with total mass \(\frac14\).

Stage \(2\), of capacity \(\frac18\), therefore admits exactly half of each applicant type’s remaining mass. This gives the stated proportional split. Stage \(3\) admits all remaining mass, totaling \(\frac18\).

School \(B\)’s assigned aggregate consists solely of \(b(\theta)\) units of type \(u\), which its protocol admits in full.

Thus both schools satisfy the required choice consistency.

No blocking tuple exists

At \(A\):

  • Type \(a\) is entirely at its favorite school.
  • Types \(u\) and \(v\) are ineligible at stage \(1\).
  • Stages \(2\) and \(3\) have no vacancies and admit only their own priority class \(M\). Neither type has strictly higher priority than an admitted class.

Hence no type blocks \(A\).

At \(B\), only unmatched type-\(u\) mass could want admission: type \(a\) is at its favorite school, and type \(v\) prefers being unmatched to \(B\). If type \(u\) has positive unmatched mass, then \(\frac38-\theta>\frac14\), so \(b(\theta)=\frac14\). School \(B\) is therefore full and admits only type \(u\)’s own class.

Hence no type blocks \(B\). Every assignment in this family is procedurally stable.

3. These are all the stable assignments

This characterization makes the obstruction explicit.

In any procedurally stable assignment, stage \(1\) of \(A\) admits at most \(\mu_a=\frac14\), so its vacancy is at least \(\frac18\). If positive type-\(a\) mass were assigned below \(A\), it would block that stage. Therefore all type-\(a\) mass must be assigned to \(A\).

Choice consistency then places that entire mass in stage \(1\), giving \(r_{A,1}=\frac18\).

Let \(z\) be the total type-\(u\) and type-\(v\) mass assigned to \(A\). Its physical capacity implies \(z\le\frac14\). Also, \(r_{A,2}\le\frac18\), so the third-stage formula is unclipped and equals \(\frac18+r_{A,2}\). The vacancy equations give

\(z=(\frac18-r_{A,2})+(\frac18+r_{A,2}-r_{A,3})=\frac14-r_{A,3}.\)

If \(z<\frac14\), then stage \(3\) has a positive vacancy. Some positive mass of \(u\) or \(v\) lies below \(A\), because their combined mass is \(\frac34>z\). Both types prefer \(A\) and are eligible at stage \(3\), producing a blocking tuple. Therefore \(z=\frac14\).

Writing \(\theta=x_{u,A}\) gives \(x_{v,A}=\frac14-\theta\). Choice consistency forces the proportional stage decomposition described above.

Finally, only type \(u\) can be assigned to \(B\). If its residual mass exceeds \(B\)’s capacity, stability requires \(B\) to fill; otherwise, leaving any of that residual mass unmatched would create a vacancy block. Thus \(x_{u,B}=b(\theta)\).

This proves the complete characterization.

4. No student-optimal assignment exists

For a type whose favorite school is \(A\), stochastic dominance requires at least as much normalized mass at \(A\). Since the type’s mass is fixed, this requires at least as much unnormalized mass there.

The stable assignment with \(\theta=\frac14\) assigns \(\frac14\) of type \(u\) to \(A\). The stable assignment with \(\theta=0\) assigns \(\frac14\) of type \(v\) to \(A\). Every assignment in the family assigns \(\frac14\) of type \(a\) to \(A\).

Therefore any assignment dominating both endpoint assignments would require

\(x_{a,A}\ge\frac14,\qquad x_{u,A}\ge\frac14,\qquad x_{v,A}\ge\frac14.\)

Its total mass at \(A\) would be at least \(\frac34\), exceeding \(\kappa_A=\frac12\).

No feasible assignment dominates both stable endpoints. In particular, there is no student-optimal procedurally stable assignment.

Proportional rationing does not remove this obstruction: it constrains the decomposition of already assigned aggregate mass, while the blocking condition permits different allocations between tied types.

5. Uniqueness, computation, and incentives

Conditional uniqueness does hold. If two student-optimal assignments existed, each would stochastically dominate the other for every type. Under a strict finite ranking, equality of every cumulative probability implies equality of each school’s assigned mass. The deterministic protocol choice would then give identical stage decompositions.

Existence, however, fails in the instance above.

Accordingly, exact computation of the specified student-optimal assignment is not a total search task on the promised domain: even this constant-size rational instance has no valid output. This establishes nonexistence, rather than an NP-hardness classification. Computing an arbitrary stable assignment, or deciding whether a student-optimal assignment exists, would be different computational questions.

Likewise, there is no resulting student-optimal mechanism on the stated domain, so it cannot have the requested universal group-strategyproofness guarantee. This does not establish that every alternative stable-selection mechanism is manipulable.

The cited results do not supply the missing existence claim. Westkamp’s finite model uses strict stage priorities over individual students. Azevedo–Leshno’s continuum model assumes that each school’s indifference classes have zero population mass. The positive-mass tied class in this construction falls outside those respective assumptions. See [Westkamp, Section 4.1](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/74644/1/74743543X.pdf) and [Azevedo–Leshno, Assumption 1](https://eduardomazevedo.github.io/papers/Azevedo-Leshno-Supply-and-Demand-Matching.pdf).
