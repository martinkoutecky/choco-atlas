The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(K\) types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), initial colours \(c_t\in\{r,b,u\}\), a rational contact kernel \(q_{tu}\ge 0\) with each row sum at most \(1\), a finite integer horizon \(H\), and a seeding budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), choose seeding fractions \(z_t\in[0,1]\) to maximize blue mass after \(H\) rounds. Require \(z_t=0\) for initially blue types and \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t\le\beta\). Initially set \(x_{t,b}(0)=1\) for blue types; for red types set \((x_{t,b},x_{t,r},x_{t,u})(0)=(z_t,1-z_t,0)\); and for uncoloured types set it to \((z_t,0,1-z_t)\). For \(s=0,\ldots,H-1\), let \(p_{t,c}(s)=\sum_u q_{tu}x_{u,c}(s)\) and update \(x_{t,b}(s+1)=x_{t,b}(s)(1-p_{t,r}(s))+(1-x_{t,b}(s))p_{t,b}(s)\), \(x_{t,r}(s+1)=x_{t,r}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s))+(1-x_{t,r}(s))p_{t,r}(s)\), and \(x_{t,u}(s+1)=x_{t,u}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s)-p_{t,r}(s))\). What are the exact and approximation complexities, in \(K\), input bit length, and \(H\), of maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,b}(H)\)?

Partial results and a stronger obstruction to transferring discrete hardness

For every fixed $\eta>0$, the general problem admits a deterministic polynomial-time $(1-1/e-\eta)$-approximation.

The new progress is more specific:

  • For an all-uncoloured bipartite society consisting of stubborn sources and receivers that contact only those sources, a $(1-\eta)$-approximation is achievable in polynomial time for every fixed $\eta>0$.
  • On the direct mass analogue of the cited maximum-coverage reduction, uniformly splitting the budget among candidate sources gives a $(1-e^{-kd})$-approximation at that construction’s horizon. Here $k$ is its cardinality-budget parameter and $d$ its number of copies per object.

Thus the direct discrete reduction does not establish the proposed hardness threshold for fractional mass seeding. These results do not settle whether general instances admit a fixed improvement over $1-1/e$, or whether every such improvement has the stated complexity consequence.

1. Representation of the general objective

Let
$$ I=\{t:c_t\ne b\}, \qquad d_t=\sum_vq_{tv}, \qquad D=2^H. $$

We regard $F_H$ as a function of the coordinates indexed by $I$.

1.1 Independent-copy trees

Combine two colours by adopting the contact colour when it is red or blue, and retaining the current colour when the contact colour is uncoloured. Treat missing contact probability as an uncoloured contact.

If current and contact colours are independent, the resulting blue probability is
$$ p_{t,b}+x_{t,b}(1-d_t+p_{t,u}) =x_{t,b}(1-p_{t,r})+(1-x_{t,b})p_{t,b}. $$
The same calculation gives the specified red and uncoloured updates. In particular, the dynamics preserve probability distributions.

Construct a random tree for type $t$ at time $s+1$ as follows:

  • Draw no contact with probability $1-d_t$, or contact type $v$ with probability $q_{tv}$.
  • For no contact, use a fresh tree for $(t,s)$.
  • For contact with $v$, use independent fresh trees for $(t,s)$ and $(v,s)$, and combine their root colours by the rule above.

At time zero, independently draw each leaf’s initial colour according to the seeding fractions. Induction proves that the root distribution is exactly $x_t(s)$. There are at most $2^s$ leaves.

Independence is imposed between copies even when their types agree. This represents the given deterministic equations; it does not identify them with correlated finite-agent dynamics.

1.2 Conditional coverage polynomials

Order leaves recursively with the contact subtree before the current-state subtree. The root colour is the first non-uncoloured leaf colour, or uncoloured if no such leaf exists.

Fix the tree and inspect the leaves’ colours before seeding.

  • If the first initially coloured leaf is blue, the root is always blue.
  • If it is red, the root becomes blue precisely when an earlier uncoloured leaf is seeded or that red leaf is seeded.
  • If every leaf is initially uncoloured, the root becomes blue precisely when some leaf is seeded.

Consequently, conditional blue probability is either $1$ or
$$ G_a(z)=1-\prod_{i\in I}(1-z_i)^{a_i}, \qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}, \qquad \sum_i a_i\le D. $$
Repeated occurrences of a type give repeated independent initial draws, which explains the exponents.

Averaging over trees and root types expresses $F_H$ as a convex combination of these polynomials and the constant $1$.

1.3 Diminishing returns and quantitative bounds

For positive exponents,
$$ \frac{\partial G_a}{\partial z_i} =a_i(1-z_i)^{a_i-1}\prod_{j\ne i}(1-z_j)^{a_j}\ge0. $$
For distinct $i,j$,
$$ \frac{\partial^2G_a}{\partial z_i\partial z_j} =-a_i a_j(1-z_i)^{a_i-1}(1-z_j)^{a_j-1} \prod_{\ell\ne i,j}(1-z_\ell)^{a_\ell}\le0. $$
The diagonal second derivative is also nonpositive, with coefficient $-a_i(a_i-1)$. Terms with zero coefficients are omitted; boundary statements follow by polynomial continuity.

Therefore $F_H$ is monotone and satisfies coordinatewise diminishing returns:
$$ z\le w \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial F_H}{\partial z_i}(z) \ge \frac{\partial F_H}{\partial z_i}(w). $$
Also,
$$ 0\le\frac{\partial F_H}{\partial z_i}\le D, $$
and hence
$$ |F_H(z)-F_H(w)| \le D\sum_{i\in I}|z_i-w_i|. $$

We will also use
$$ \mathrm{OPT}\ge\beta. $$
To prove this, seed every seedable type by fraction $\beta$. Its cost is at most $\beta$, and all initial blue fractions are at least $\beta$. Rewriting the blue update gives
$$ x_{t,b}(s+1) =(1-d_t)x_{t,b}(s) +\sum_vq_{tv}\bigl(x_{v,b}(s)+x_{t,b}(s)x_{v,u}(s)\bigr). $$
If every blue fraction at time $s$ is at least $\beta$, the right-hand side is at least
$$ (1-d_t)\beta+d_t\beta=\beta. $$
Induction proves the bound.

2. Polynomial-time evaluation to prescribed additive accuracy

Exact polynomial expansion is unnecessary.

Suppose two arrays of valid distributions differ by at most $E$ in each coordinate. Their contact probabilities differ by at most $E$, because each contact row sums to at most one.

Write the blue update as
$$ b'=b(1-p_b-p_r)+p_b. $$
Its absolute sensitivity to each of $b,p_b,p_r$ is at most one on the probability domain. Thus the blue output error is at most $3E$. The same bound holds for red and uncoloured outputs.

Choose a dyadic mesh size $h$. Initially and after every update, round blue and red coordinates downward to multiples of $h$, and define the uncoloured coordinate as one minus their sum. This preserves valid distributions and introduces coordinate error at most $2h$.

Thus
$$ E_0\le2h, \qquad E_{s+1}\le3E_s+2h, \qquad E_H\le h(3^{H+1}-1). $$
Taking
$$ h\le\frac{\delta}{3^{H+1}} $$
gives objective error at most $\delta$.

The stored precision is $O(H+\log(1/\delta))$ bits. Each round uses $O(m+\tau)$ rational operations. Combining the stored dyadic values with input rationals creates only polynomial-length intermediate representations; rounding prevents recursive precision growth.

Therefore, for rational $z$, there is a deterministic additive evaluation oracle running in polynomial time in
$$ m,\tau,L,H,\operatorname{length}(z),\log(1/\delta). $$

3. General $(1-1/e-\eta)$-approximation

This section gives a self-contained proof of the general guarantee.

Fix rational
$$ 0<\eta<1-1/e, \qquad \alpha=\eta/4. $$
If $\beta=0$, the feasible seeding vector is unique. If $I$ is empty, every type is initially blue. Assume otherwise.

3.1 Discretize seeded mass

Let $\mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t$. Choose a positive dyadic number $\Delta$ satisfying
$$ \Delta\le\frac{\alpha\beta\mu_{\min}}{D\tau}. $$
Its binary encoding length is polynomial in $L,H,\log\tau,\log(1/\alpha)$.

Define
$$ B=\left\lfloor\frac{\beta}{\Delta}\right\rfloor, \qquad C_i=\left\lfloor\frac{\mu_i}{\Delta}\right\rfloor, $$
and, throughout the integer coordinate box, define
$$ f(n)=F_H\left(\left(\frac{\Delta n_i}{\mu_i}\right)_{i\in I}\right). $$
The grid-feasible vectors satisfy
$$ 0\le n_i\le C_i, \qquad \sum_i n_i\le B. $$
They give feasible original allocations.

Integrating the continuous diminishing-returns inequality over one coordinate increment proves discrete diminishing returns:
$$ f(n+e_i)-f(n)\ge f(n'+e_i)-f(n') $$
whenever $n\le n'$ and both increments stay within the coordinate box.

Let $Q$ be the grid optimum. A continuous optimum exists because the feasible set is compact and $F_H$ is continuous. Round each of its seeded masses downward to a multiple of $\Delta$. The resulting allocation is grid-feasible and loses at most
$$ D\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\Delta}{\mu_i} \le\frac{D\tau\Delta}{\mu_{\min}} \le\alpha\beta. $$
Hence
$$ Q\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\alpha\beta. $$

3.2 Threshold batches

Use thresholds
$$ \theta_j=(1-\alpha)^j, \qquad j=0,\ldots,J, $$
where $J$ is the first index satisfying
$$ \theta_J\le\frac{\alpha\beta}{B}. $$
Their number is
$$ O\left(1+\alpha^{-1}\log\frac{B}{\alpha\beta}\right). $$

Let
$$ M=|I|(J+1), \qquad \delta=\frac{\alpha\beta}{4(B+M+1)}. $$
Use objective evaluations with additive error at most $\delta$.

Start at $n=0$. At each threshold, process every coordinate $i$. Put
$$ T=\min\left\{C_i-n_i,\ B-\sum_\ell n_\ell\right\}. $$
A positive integer $k\le T$ is accepted when the estimated gain is at least $k\theta_j$; declare $0$ accepted without evaluation.

If $T$ is accepted, choose it. Otherwise binary-search between the accepted lower endpoint $0$ and rejected upper endpoint $T$, maintaining those endpoint properties until they are adjacent, and choose the lower endpoint. Add the chosen batch to $n$.

Estimated gains have error at most $2\delta$. Therefore every positive chosen batch satisfies
$$ f(n+ke_i)-f(n)\ge k\theta_j-2\delta. $$

If $k<T$, the test at $k+1$ was rejected. Discrete coordinatewise concavity gives
$$ \begin{aligned} f(n+(k+1)e_i)-f(n+ke_i) &\le \frac{f(n+(k+1)e_i)-f(n)}{k+1}\\ &\le\theta_j+2\delta. \end{aligned} $$
This remains valid even if numerical errors make acceptance nonmonotone: only the final adjacent accepted and rejected endpoints are used.

After a complete pass with budget remaining, every coordinate is capped or has unit marginal at most $\theta_j+2\delta$. Subsequent additions preserve this bound by diminishing returns.

3.3 Approximation analysis

Let $d_{\max}(n)$ be the largest unit marginal available under the coordinate caps, disregarding the budget, or zero if all coordinates are capped. Then
$$ Q-f(n)\le B\,d_{\max}(n). $$
Indeed, take a grid-optimal vector $n^*$ and add units from $n$ to its coordinatewise maximum with $n^*$. At most $B$ units are needed. Every added marginal is at most $d_{\max}(n)$, while the final value is at least $Q$. The comparison stays within the coordinate box, where $f$ is defined.

Before a batch at threshold $\theta_j$, all available marginals are at most
$$ \frac{\theta_j}{1-\alpha}+2\delta. $$
For the first threshold, this follows from $0\le f\le1$. For later thresholds, it follows from the preceding completed pass.

Thus
$$ \theta_j\ge (1-\alpha)\left(\frac{Q-f(n)}{B}-2\delta\right). $$
Combining this with the accepted-batch bound gives
$$ f(n+ke_i)-f(n) \ge \frac{(1-\alpha)k}{B}(Q-f(n)) -2(1-\alpha)k\delta-2\delta. $$

There are at most $M$ positive batches and their total size is at most $B$, so the sum of these error terms is at most
$$ 2\delta(B+M)\le\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}. $$

If the budget fills, the residual multipliers are
$$ 1-\frac{(1-\alpha)k}{B}\in[0,1], $$
and their product is at most
$$ \exp\left(-\frac{1-\alpha}{B}\sum k\right) =e^{-(1-\alpha)}. $$
Since $f(0)\ge0$, iteration yields
$$ f(n)\ge \bigl(1-e^{-(1-\alpha)}\bigr)Q-\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}. $$
For $0\le\alpha\le1$,
$$ 1-e^{-(1-\alpha)}\ge1-1/e-\alpha. $$
Using $Q\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\alpha\beta$ and $\beta\le\mathrm{OPT}$ therefore gives
$$ f(n)\ge(1-1/e-3\alpha)\mathrm{OPT}. $$

If budget remains after the final pass, instead
$$ Q-f(n)\le B(\theta_J+2\delta)\le\frac{3\alpha\beta}{2}, $$
and consequently
$$ f(n)\ge\mathrm{OPT}-\frac{5\alpha\beta}{2} \ge(1-5\alpha/2)\mathrm{OPT}. $$
This also implies the claimed guarantee because $3\alpha<\eta$.

3.4 Running time

Although $B$ and the caps may be exponentially large, their binary lengths are polynomial. Each batch uses $O(\log(B+1))$ oracle calls, and there are at most $M$ batches.

Threshold encodings and $\log(1/\delta)$ have polynomial length. Section 2 therefore supplies every evaluation in polynomial time. The output fractions also have polynomial encoding length.

This proves a deterministic polynomial-time $(1-1/e-\eta)$-approximation for every fixed $\eta>0$.

4. New tractable class: stubborn sources and receivers

Assume every type is initially uncoloured. Partition the types into nonempty stubborn sources $S$ and receivers $R$, with
$$ q_{sv}=0\quad(s\in S), \qquad q_{tv}=0\quad(t\in R,\ v\notin S). $$
Receivers may have missing contact probability.

Write
$$ W=\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s, \qquad a_t(z)=\sum_{s\in S}q_{ts}z_s. $$
Assume $H\ge1$; Section 6 handles $H=0$.

4.1 Exact objective and source dominance

There is no red mass, so $b_t=1-u_t$. Sources have constant blue fractions $z_s$. Therefore
$$ u_t(s+1)=u_t(s)(1-a_t), $$
and
$$ F_H(z)= \sum_{s\in S}\mu_s z_s+ \sum_{t\in R}\mu_t \left[1-(1-z_t)(1-a_t(z))^H\right]. $$

The marginal gain per unit of seeded mass at receiver $t$ is
$$ \frac1{\mu_t}\frac{\partial F_H}{\partial z_t} =(1-a_t)^H\le1. $$
For source $s$, it is
$$ \frac1{\mu_s}\frac{\partial F_H}{\partial z_s} = 1+\frac1{\mu_s}\sum_{t\in R} \mu_t(1-z_t)Hq_{ts}(1-a_t)^{H-1} \ge1. $$

Suppose a receiver has positive seeding and a source is not fully seeded. Transfer seeded mass $h$ from that receiver to that source:
$$ z_t\longmapsto z_t-\frac h{\mu_t}, \qquad z_s\longmapsto z_s+\frac h{\mu_s}, $$
until the receiver becomes unseeded or the source becomes fully seeded. Along the whole segment, the objective derivative with respect to $h$ is nonnegative by the two inequalities above. Thus the transfer preserves cost and cannot decrease the objective.

Repeated transfers require at most $|S|+|R|$ steps, since each step permanently empties a receiver or fills a source.

If $\beta\le W$, a feasible allocation cannot have all sources full and a positively seeded receiver. Hence an optimum exists with
$$ z_t=0\quad(t\in R). $$

If $\beta>W$, an optimum exists with every source full. After the transfers, either this already holds, or all receivers are unseeded and the total cost is less than $W<\beta$; increasing source seeding then improves the objective until all sources are full.

4.2 Large budgets are exactly solvable

When $\beta\ge W$, set all source fractions to one. Let
$$ d_t=\sum_{s\in S}q_{ts}. $$
The remaining receiver objective is linear:
$$ F_H= W+\sum_{t\in R}\mu_t \left[1-(1-d_t)^H+z_t(1-d_t)^H\right]. $$
Allocate the remaining budget by fractional knapsack, sorting receivers by $(1-d_t)^H$.

All required powers and comparisons have polynomial rational bit complexity because $H$ is unary.

4.3 Smaller budgets reduce to concave maximization

When $0<\beta\le W$, source-only seeding gives
$$ \Phi(z)= \sum_{s\in S}\mu_s z_s+ \sum_{t\in R}\mu_t \left[1-(1-a_t(z))^H\right] $$
over
$$ P=\left\{z\in[0,1]^S:\sum_{s\in S}\mu_s z_s\le\beta\right\}. $$

For $H\ge2$, the scalar function $g(a)=1-(1-a)^H$ satisfies
$$ g''(a)=-H(H-1)(1-a)^{H-2}\le0. $$
For $H=1$, it is linear. Each $a_t$ is affine, so $\Phi$ is concave on $P$. Source dominance proves that its maximum equals the unrestricted optimum.

For completeness, we check polynomial bit complexity rather than merely invoking concavity.

Let $n_S=|S|$ and form the convex hypograph
$$ K=\{(z,y):z\in P,\ -1\le y\le\Phi(z)\}. $$
It contains the Euclidean ball centred at
$$ \left((\beta/2)_{s\in S},-1/2\right) $$
with radius
$$ r=\frac{\beta}{8(n_S+1)}. $$
Indeed, within this ball all coordinates remain in $[0,1]$, seeded cost is at most $\beta/2+r<\beta$, and $-1<y<0\le\Phi(z)$. The body is contained in a ball of radius $2(n_S+1)$ about the same centre.

There is an exact rational separation oracle. Check the linear constraints first. If those hold but $y>\Phi(z)$, concavity supplies the valid separating inequality
$$ y'\le\Phi(z)+\nabla\Phi(z)\mathbin{\cdot}(z'-z). $$
Evaluating $\Phi$ and its gradient at rational arguments takes polynomial time and polynomial bit length: the powers have exponent at most $H$.

The standard weak-optimization-from-separation theorem gives, in polynomial time, an approximately optimal rational point within prescribed distance $\varepsilon$ of $K$. Its running time is polynomial in the dimension and binary lengths of the radii and accuracy. These are precisely the required hypotheses here. [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1981a.pdf)

To ensure exact feasibility, let the returned point be $(v,\bar y)$, with
$$ \operatorname{dist}((v,\bar y),K)\le\varepsilon, \qquad \bar y\ge\max_P\Phi-\varepsilon. $$
There is $(z^*,y^*)\in K$ within distance $\varepsilon$, so
$$ \Phi(z^*)\ge\max_P\Phi-2\varepsilon. $$

Clip $v$ coordinatewise into $[0,1]$, obtaining $w$. Clipping gives
$$ |w_s-z_s^*|\le\varepsilon, \qquad \sum_s\mu_s w_s\le\beta+\varepsilon. $$
If necessary, scale $w$ down to cost $\beta$, obtaining $\widehat z$. Then
$$ |\widehat z_s-z_s^*| \le\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}. $$
Every partial derivative of $\Phi$ is at most $H+1$. Hence
$$ \Phi(\widehat z)\ge \max_P\Phi- \left[2+(H+1)n_S(1+1/\beta)\right]\varepsilon. $$
Choosing
$$ \varepsilon= \frac{\delta}{2+(H+1)n_S(1+1/\beta)} $$
produces an exactly feasible allocation with additive loss at most $\delta$, in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L,H,\log(1/\delta)$.

Finally, $\mathrm{OPT}\ge\beta$: source seeding of total mass $\beta$ contributes that much blue mass from sources alone. Taking $\delta=\eta\beta$ proves a polynomial-time $(1-\eta)$-approximation for every fixed $\eta>0$ in this class.

5. New obstruction: uniform fractional seeding nearly solves the direct coverage construction

The cited discrete reduction uses stubborn candidate nodes, object nodes contacting their incident candidates, and $d$ copies per object contacting that object; all nodes are initially uncoloured. Its horizon is $\ell d+1$, where $\ell$ is the number of candidates. [Manohara–Zehmakan, Section 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04564v2#S4.SS1)

Consider its direct mass analogue. Let there be:

  • $\ell$ stubborn candidate types;
  • $M$ object types, each with a normalized contact row supported on a nonempty subset of candidates;
  • $d$ copy types per object, each contacting that object with probability one.

Give every type mass $1/n$, where
$$ n=\ell+(d+1)M, $$
and set
$$ \beta=\frac{k}{n}, \qquad 1\le k\le\ell. $$

5.1 Explicit fractional allocation

Seed every candidate by
$$ z_s=\theta=\frac{k}{\ell}, $$
and leave every other type unseeded. Its cost is exactly
$$ \ell\frac1n\frac{k}{\ell}=\beta. $$

Every candidate has constant blue fraction $\theta$. Because each object’s contact row sums to one, every object sees blue contact probability $\theta$, independently of its incidence set. Thus
$$ u_{\mathrm{object}}(s)=(1-\theta)^s. $$

For a copy,
$$ u_{\mathrm{copy}}(s+1) =u_{\mathrm{copy}}(s)u_{\mathrm{object}}(s). $$
Starting from $u_{\mathrm{copy}}(0)=1$ gives
$$ u_{\mathrm{copy}}(H) =\prod_{s=0}^{H-1}(1-\theta)^s =(1-\theta)^{H(H-1)/2}. $$
Consequently, the allocation has value
$$ F_H= \frac{ k+M\left[1-(1-\theta)^H\right] +dM\left[1-(1-\theta)^{H(H-1)/2}\right] }{n}. $$

5.2 Approximation against arbitrary fractional allocations

For every feasible allocation, stubborn candidates contribute at most $\beta=k/n$, while all other types together contribute at most $(d+1)M/n$. Therefore
$$ \mathrm{OPT}\le\frac{k+(d+1)M}{n}. $$

For $H\ge2$, both $H$ and $H(H-1)/2$ are at least $H-1$. Put
$$ \rho=(1-k/\ell)^{H-1}. $$
The explicit allocation satisfies
$$ \begin{aligned} F_H &\ge\frac{k+(d+1)M(1-\rho)}{n}\\ &\ge(1-\rho)\frac{k+(d+1)M}{n}\\ &\ge(1-\rho)\mathrm{OPT}. \end{aligned} $$

At the cited construction’s horizon $H=\ell d+1$,
$$ \rho=(1-k/\ell)^{\ell d}\le e^{-kd}, $$
so uniformly splitting the budget gives a
$$ (1-e^{-kd}) $$
approximation against the full fractional optimum, including allocations that seed object or copy types.

As $d$ grows, this guarantee approaches one, regardless of the underlying coverage instance. This is a quantitative obstruction to transferring that particular reduction.

There is also concavity under source-only seeding. If
$$ a_j(z)=\sum_s q_{js}z_s, $$
then object $j$ contributes $1-(1-a_j)^H$, and each of its copies contributes
$$ 1-(1-a_j)^{H(H-1)/2}. $$
These are concave functions of the source fractions. This statement concerns the source-only restriction; it does not prove unrestricted tractability for arbitrary two-layer networks.

6. Other exactly solvable cases

If there are no initially uncoloured types, uncoloured mass remains zero. Let $Q_{\mathrm{contact}}=(q_{tv})$. The blue dynamics reduce to
$$ b(s+1)=A b(s), \qquad A=I-\operatorname{diag}(d)+Q_{\mathrm{contact}}. $$
The matrix $A$ is nonnegative and row-stochastic. Therefore
$$ F_H(z)=\mu^{\mathsf T}A^H b(0). $$
Initially blue coordinates are fixed, and initially red coordinates equal their seeding fractions. This is fractional knapsack: compute
$$ w^{\mathsf T}=\mu^{\mathsf T}A^H, $$
sort red types by $w_t/\mu_t$, and allocate in that order.

Exact rational computation is polynomial-time. A common denominator for $A$ has polynomial bit length, and a common denominator for $A^H$ has at most $H$ times that bit length.

For $H=0$, writing
$$ B_0=\sum_{t:c_t=b}\mu_t, $$
the exact optimum is
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=B_0+\min\{\beta,1-B_0\}. $$
This follows because each unit of seeded mass contributes exactly one unit to the objective.
