Theorem 3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

For \(0<\Delta<1/2\), let \(\mu\) be an unknown distribution on \(\rho_a=(a\succ b)\) and \(\rho_b=(b\succ a)\), with masses \(1/2+\Delta\) and \(1/2-\Delta\) in either order. Given independent samples from \(\mu\), determine the least worst-case expected number of samples and the workspace needed to identify the winner with error at most \(\epsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is represented by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(m=\tau=2\), the types are \(\rho_a=(a\succ b)\) and \(\rho_b=(b\succ a)\), and total mass is \(1\).
Known binary rationals \(0<\Delta<1/2\) and \(0<\epsilon<1/2\) specify two possible societies: \(\mu_+(\rho_a)=1/2+\Delta\) and \(\mu_-(\rho_a)=1/2-\Delta\). The winner is the candidate with greater mass.
The actual labeled mass list is hidden: a classical randomized algorithm obtains information about the society only through independent samples from it. The support types and the two possible mass assignments are known.
The algorithm may stop adaptively, must halt almost surely, and must have error at most \(\epsilon\) under each society. Sample cost is the worst-case expected number of samples; workspace is the worst-case expected peak number of mutable bits, including counters and retained samples, excluding read-only parameters. There is no uncounted clock or mutable external storage.
Let \(L\) be the total binary encoding length of the parameters and possible support descriptions; express resource bounds in \(m,\tau,L,\Delta,\epsilon\).

What are the least worst-case expected sample cost and expected workspace for identifying the winner under these conditions?

The discrete sampling-only result states expected-time \(\Omega(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\operatorname{MoV}^2)\) and expected-space \(\Omega(\log(n^2(1/2-\epsilon)^2/\operatorname{MoV}^2))\) lower bounds. Its two-candidate family already has sampling probabilities \(1/2\pm\operatorname{MoV}/n\), exactly the distribution family specified here. [Verified source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v216/liu23a/liu23a.pdf)
No separate open continuous complexity question was verified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the support representation to a hidden instance accessed through samples; revealing its labeled masses would reveal the winner and contradict the sampling-only discrete model.
  • Made Delta and epsilon known binary rational parameters and restricted epsilon to (0,1/2), excluding the zero-sample random-guess endpoint.
  • Allowed adaptive stopping and internal classical randomization, with almost-sure termination and a separate error guarantee under each possible society.
  • Defined expected workspace as expected peak mutable bits, excluding read-only parameters but counting counters, retained samples, and other mutable state.

Results and scope

The least worst-case expected sample count can be determined exactly. For workspace, I prove stronger bounds than those in the notes: they match in their dependence on $\Delta$ and $\epsilon$, up to an additive $O(\log L)$ term for parameter processing. That remaining term prevents a complete characterization in every regime.

Throughout, $m=\tau=2$. Define
$$ p=\frac12+\Delta,\qquad q=\frac12-\Delta,\qquad r=\frac pq,\qquad \theta=\ln r, $$
and
$$ \delta=1-2\epsilon,\qquad t=\ln\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}. $$
Thus $r>1$, $\theta>0$, $0<\delta<1$, and
$$ \delta=\tanh(t/2). $$

Let $N_*$ be the minimum worst-case expected sample count. Let $W_*$ be the infimum of worst-case expected peak workspace over admissible algorithms, minimizing workspace independently of sample count.

The results proved below are:

  • An exact formula for $N_*$, and consequently

$$ N_*= \Theta\left( \frac{\delta}{\Delta} \max\left\{1,\frac{t}{\theta}\right\} \right). $$

  • Universal constants $c,C>0$ such that, with

$$ F=1+\delta\log_2\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right), $$
$$ cF\leq W_*+1 \leq C\bigl(F+\log_2(L+1)\bigr). $$

  • A uniform algorithm with expected sample count $O(N_*)$ and deterministic peak workspace

$$ O\left( \log(L+1)+\log\left(1+\frac{t}{\theta}\right) \right). $$

The workspace upper bounds use ordinary explicit binary encodings of rational numerators and denominators, or explicit finite binary expansions.

For the workspace lower bound, I count a complete changing configuration: control location, input addresses, counters, and retained samples are included. This implements the stipulated absence of an uncounted clock or mutable external storage. Allowing arbitrarily many uncharged changing control states would permit those states to act as storage.

1. Exact optimum for expected samples

Encode a sampled $\rho_a$ by $X=1$ and a sampled $\rho_b$ by $X=-1$. Under $\mu_+$,
$$ \Pr(X=1)=p,\qquad \mathbb E X=2\Delta, $$
and under $\mu_-$ these probabilities are exchanged.

For every integer $j\geq0$, put
$$ d_j=\frac{r^j-1}{r^j+1} =\tanh(j\theta/2). $$
Then $d_0=0$, and $d_j$ increases strictly to $1$.

Define
$$ k=\left\lceil\frac{t}{\theta}\right\rceil,\qquad \lambda=\frac{\delta-d_{k-1}}{d_k-d_{k-1}}. $$
Because $\delta=\tanh(t/2)$,
$$ d_{k-1}\leq\delta\leq d_k, $$
so $0\leq\lambda\leq1$.

Proven claim.
$$ \boxed{ N_*= \frac{(1-\lambda)(k-1)d_{k-1} +\lambda k d_k}{2\Delta}. } $$

1.1 Threshold tests and achievability

For $j\geq1$, maintain
$$ S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i $$
and stop at
$$ T_j=\inf\{n:|S_n|=j\}. $$
Announce $a$ at $S_{T_j}=j$ and $b$ at $S_{T_j}=-j$.

Under $\mu_+$, the probability $h(s)$ of reaching $j$ before $-j$ satisfies
$$ h(s)=p\,h(s+1)+q\,h(s-1), \qquad h(-j)=0,\quad h(j)=1. $$
Writing $z=q/p$, its solution is
$$ h(s)=\frac{1-z^{s+j}}{1-z^{2j}}. $$
In particular,
$$ \Pr_+(S_{T_j}=j)=\frac{r^j}{1+r^j}, \qquad \Pr_+(S_{T_j}=-j)=\frac{1}{1+r^j}. $$
Sign exchange gives the corresponding probabilities under $\mu_-$. The error under each society is therefore
$$ \frac{1-d_j}{2}. $$

This stopping time has finite expectation. From any interior state, $2j$ consecutive positive samples force absorption. Under either society, that event has probability at least $q^{2j}>0$. Consequently,
$$ \Pr(T_j>2j\ell)\leq(1-q^{2j})^\ell. $$

Since $\{T_j\geq i\}$ is determined before sample $i$, independence and absolute summability give
$$ \mathbb E_+S_{T_j} =\sum_{i\geq1}\mathbb E_+[X_i\mathbf 1_{\{T_j\geq i\}}] =2\Delta\,\mathbb E_+T_j. $$
The terminal probabilities imply $\mathbb E_+S_{T_j}=jd_j$. Hence
$$ \mathbb E_+T_j=\mathbb E_-T_j=\frac{jd_j}{2\Delta}. $$

For $j=0$, take no samples and announce a fair random candidate. Choose threshold $k$ with probability $\lambda$, and threshold $k-1$ otherwise. Its error under each society is exactly $\epsilon$, since
$$ (1-\lambda)d_{k-1}+\lambda d_k=\delta. $$
Its expected sample count is the displayed formula for $N_*$.

Exact threshold computation

The ceiling defining $k$ can be computed without numerical logarithms.

Write
$$ r=\frac AB,\qquad \frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}=\frac CD $$
with positive integers $A>B$ and $C>D$. Starting at $j=1$, compute successive integer powers until
$$ A^jD\geq B^jC. $$
The first such $j$ is exactly $k$. The procedure terminates because $(A/B)^j$ tends to infinity, and the non-strict comparison handles equality correctly.

The numbers $d_j$ and $\lambda$ are rational. To generate an exact coin with rational probability $u/v$, draw a uniformly random integer using $\lceil\log_2v\rceil$ fair bits, reject values at least $v$, and accept precisely values below $u$. Each trial succeeds with probability greater than $1/2$, so this randomization halts almost surely and uses no society samples.

This establishes exact sample-optimal achievability; it does not assert that this implementation minimizes workspace.

1.2 Lower bound for arbitrary adaptive algorithms

Consider any admissible algorithm with finite expected sample count under both societies. Put an equal prior on the two societies, writing $H=1$ for $\mu_+$ and $H=-1$ for $\mu_-$. Let $D\in\{1,-1\}$ be the announced winner, $T$ the sample count, and
$$ S_T=\sum_{i=1}^T X_i. $$

For a complete stopped transcript, including private randomness, the likelihood ratio is $r^{S_T}$. Sampling contributes this factor; the algorithm's randomization and stopping rules are identical under the two hypotheses and cancel. Therefore
$$ \mathbb E[H\mid\text{transcript}] =\tanh(\theta S_T/2). $$

The two error guarantees imply
$$ \mathbb E[HD]\geq\delta. $$
Conditioning on the transcript yields
$$ \delta\leq\mathbb E[d_{|S_T|}]. $$

There is also an exact cost identity:
$$ 2\Delta\,\mathbb ET =\mathbb E[HS_T] =\mathbb E[|S_T|d_{|S_T|}]. $$
For the first equality, expand the stopped sum. Conditional on $H$ and the past, $HX_i$ has mean $2\Delta$, and $\{T\geq i\}$ is determined before sample $i$. Interchanging sum and expectation is justified by $\mathbb ET<\infty$. The second equality follows from the posterior formula and $|S_T|\leq T$.

Let $g$ be the piecewise linear interpolant through
$$ (d_j,jd_j),\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots. $$
These points lie on
$$ f(x)=\frac{2}{\theta}x\,\operatorname{artanh}(x). $$
Direct differentiation gives
$$ f''(x)=\frac{4}{\theta(1-x^2)^2}>0 \qquad(0\leq x<1). $$
Thus successive secant slopes increase, so $g$ is convex. Its ordinates increase, so it is also increasing.

Jensen's inequality now gives
$$ 2\Delta\,\mathbb ET =\mathbb E[g(d_{|S_T|})] \geq g(\mathbb E[d_{|S_T|}]) \geq g(\delta). $$
Here the required expectations are finite by the cost identity. The prior-average expected sample count is at most the larger society-specific expectation. Consequently every admissible algorithm has worst-case expected sample count at least
$$ \frac{g(\delta)}{2\Delta}, $$
which is precisely the value attained in Section 1.1.

2. Uniform consequences for sample complexity

At each interpolation point, $jd_j\geq d_j$, so $g(x)\geq x$. Convexity of $f$ also gives $g(x)\geq f(x)$. Therefore
$$ N_*\geq \frac{\delta}{2\Delta} \max\left\{1,\frac{t}{\theta}\right\}. $$

For an upper bound, run the threshold-$k$ test with probability $\delta/d_k$, and otherwise announce a random candidate. Its error is exactly $\epsilon$, and its expected sample count is
$$ \frac{\delta k}{2\Delta} \leq\frac{\delta}{2\Delta}\left(1+\frac{t}{\theta}\right). $$
Hence
$$ \boxed{ N_*= \Theta\left( \frac{1-2\epsilon}{\Delta} \max\left\{ 1, \frac{\ln((1-\epsilon)/\epsilon)} {\ln((1/2+\Delta)/(1/2-\Delta))} \right\} \right). } $$

For $0<\Delta\leq1/4$,
$$ \frac{d\theta}{d\Delta}=\frac{4}{1-4\Delta^2}, $$
and integration gives
$$ 4\Delta\leq\theta\leq\frac{16}{3}\Delta. $$
Thus
$$ N_*= \Theta\left( \frac{\delta}{\Delta} +\frac{\delta t}{\Delta^2} \right). $$

In particular:

  • If $\epsilon$ is bounded above by a fixed constant below $1/2$, then

$$ N_*=\Theta\left(\Delta^{-2}\ln\frac1\epsilon\right). $$

  • If $\epsilon$ tends to $1/2$, then $t=\Theta(\delta)$ and

$$ N_*= \Theta\left( \frac{\delta}{\Delta} +\frac{\delta^2}{\Delta^2} \right). $$

  • If $\epsilon\geq q$, equivalently $\delta\leq2\Delta$, then $k=1$ and

$$ N_*=\frac{\delta}{2\Delta}. $$

3. A finite-state lemma for expected peak workspace

Let $B$ denote an execution's peak number of mutable bits. Under the equal prior, write
$$ \overline W=\mathbb EB. $$
Then
$$ \overline W\leq \max\{\mathbb E_+B,\mathbb E_-B\}. $$

A complete configuration using at most $b$ bits has at most
$$ K_b\leq2^{b+1} $$
possible encodings. Fixed additive encoding overhead changes only constants below.

The following lemma controls executions with an exact peak workspace, rather than merely executions below a memory cap.

Proven lemma. For every integer $b\geq0$ and announcement $d\in\{1,-1\}$, put
$$ u_{b,d}=\Pr_+(B=b,D=d),\qquad v_{b,d}=\Pr_-(B=b,D=d). $$
Then either both probabilities vanish, or
$$ e^{-16\cdot2^b\theta} \leq\frac{u_{b,d}}{v_{b,d}} \leq e^{16\cdot2^b\theta}. $$
Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ \delta\leq \mathbb E\left[\tanh(8\cdot2^B\theta)\right]. } $$

3.1 Proof of the probability-ratio bound

Truncate the algorithm immediately before its workspace would exceed $b$. Augment its configuration with one flag recording whether workspace exactly $b$ has already been reached.

There are at most $2K_b$ transient states and five terminal outcomes:

  • announcement $a$, with peak below $b$;
  • announcement $b$, with peak below $b$;
  • announcement $a$, with peak exactly $b$;
  • announcement $b$, with peak exactly $b$;
  • overflow.

This truncated process terminates almost surely: an execution either overflows or follows an almost-surely terminating original execution.

Use elementary transitions consuming at most one society sample. Under the two societies, every nonzero transition probability has ratio in $[r^{-1},r]$. Deterministic operations and private random bits have ratio one. Merging transitions preserves these bounds. Because $p,q>0$, the reachable transition graph is the same under both societies.

Reset each terminal state to the initial state, and remove unreachable states. The resulting finite chain is irreducible: every reachable state has a path to a terminal state, terminals return to the initial state, and the initial state reaches every retained state.

Its number of states satisfies
$$ n_b\leq2K_b+5\leq2^{b+2}+5. $$

For a finite irreducible Markov chain, the Markov chain tree formula expresses stationary mass at a state as its total rooted spanning-tree weight divided by the sum of these weights. Each tree weight is the product of its transition probabilities. These hypotheses are exactly those checked above. [Aldous and Fill, Corollary 9.11](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/RWG/Book_Ralph/Ch9.S2.html)

Every tree has $n_b-1$ edges. Its weight ratio between the two societies therefore lies in
$$ [r^{-(n_b-1)},r^{n_b-1}], $$
as does the ratio of a sum of weights over the same trees.

The probability of a terminal outcome in one execution equals its stationary mass divided by the total stationary mass of terminal outcomes. To justify this, divide the reset chain into successive executions: each cycle contains exactly one terminal visit. The mean cycle length is finite, because finite-state almost-sure absorption supplies a uniformly positive chance of absorption within some fixed number of steps, hence a geometric tail.

The terminal-outcome probability ratio is consequently bounded by
$$ r^{2(n_b-1)} \leq \exp\bigl((2^{b+3}+8)\theta\bigr) \leq \exp(16\cdot2^b\theta). $$
The terminal outcome with peak exactly $b$ and announcement $d$ has probability $\Pr(B=b,D=d)$, proving the assertion.

3.2 Converting the lemma into an information bound

If $u/v$ and $v/u$ are at most $e^z$, then
$$ |u-v|\leq\tanh(z/2)(u+v). $$
Indeed, after exchanging $u,v$ if necessary, the ratio $(u-v)/(u+v)$ increases with $u/v$ and is at most $(e^z-1)/(e^z+1)$.

Put
$$ \eta_b=\tanh(8\cdot2^b\theta), \qquad \pi_b=\Pr(B=b) $$
under the equal prior. Applying the preceding inequality separately to the two announcements gives
$$ \mathbb E[HD\,\mathbf 1_{\{B=b\}}] \leq\eta_b\pi_b. $$
Summing over $b$, using boundedness to justify the sum, yields
$$ \delta\leq\mathbb E[HD] \leq\sum_b\eta_b\pi_b =\mathbb E[\eta_B]. $$

4. Stronger workspace lower bounds

4.1 Dependence on small $\Delta$, even for weak advantage

First suppose $\theta\leq1/128$, and set
$$ A=\log_2\frac1{8\theta}\geq4. $$
For every integer $b\geq0$,
$$ \eta_b\leq\min\{1,2^{b-A}\} \leq\frac{2(b+1)}{A}. $$
For the last inequality:

  • If $b+1\geq A/2$, its right side is at least one.
  • Otherwise $2^{b-A}\leq2^{-A/2}\leq1/A$, where $2^{A/2}\geq A$ for $A\geq4$.

The lemma therefore implies
$$ \delta\leq\frac{2(\overline W+1)}A. $$
Also,
$$ \log_2(1+1/\theta)\leq A+4\leq2A. $$
Hence
$$ \overline W+1 \geq\frac{\delta}{4}\log_2(1+1/\theta). $$

If $\theta>1/128$, then $\log_2(1+1/\theta)<8$, and the trivial inequality $\overline W+1\geq1$ handles this range. Combining the ranges gives
$$ \boxed{ W_*+1= \Omega\left( 1+\delta\log_2(1+1/\theta) \right). } $$

This improves the notes' lower bound when $\delta$ is small: the logarithm contains $1/\theta$, rather than only $\delta/\theta$.

4.2 Dependence on high confidence

The lemma and monotonicity of $\eta_b$ imply
$$ \delta\leq \eta_b\Pr(B\leq b)+\Pr(B>b). $$
Thus
$$ \Pr(B>b)\geq \frac{\delta-\eta_b}{1-\eta_b} $$
whenever the numerator is positive.

Choose $b$ satisfying
$$ 8\cdot2^b\theta\leq t/4. $$
Put $z=\tanh(t/4)$. Since $\delta=\tanh(t/2)$,
$$ \delta=\frac{2z}{1+z^2}. $$
Because $\eta_b\leq z$ and $(\delta-x)/(1-x)$ decreases with $x<1$,
$$ \Pr(B>b) \geq\frac{\delta-z}{1-z} =\frac{z(1+z)}{1+z^2} =\frac{\delta(1+z)}2 \geq\frac{\delta}{2}. $$

If $t/\theta\geq2^{12}$, take
$$ b=\left\lfloor\log_2(t/\theta)\right\rfloor-5. $$
Then $2^b\leq t/(32\theta)$ and
$$ b\geq\frac12\log_2(t/\theta). $$
Consequently,
$$ \overline W\geq b\Pr(B>b) \geq\frac{\delta}{4}\log_2(t/\theta). $$
For smaller $t/\theta$, the additive one absorbs the bounded logarithm. Therefore
$$ W_*+1= \Omega\left( 1+\delta\log_2(1+t/\theta) \right). $$

Finally,
$$ 1+\frac{1+t}{\theta} \leq (1+1/\theta)(1+t/\theta). $$
Combining the two lower bounds proves
$$ \boxed{ W_*+1= \Omega\left( 1+\delta\log_2\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right) \right). } $$

5. Parameter processing in $O(\log L)$ workspace

The upper bounds require a uniform implementation that does not retain $L$-bit rational operands.

Arithmetic fact. For a rational expression formed from the input rationals using a fixed number of additions, subtractions, and multiplications, one can compute signs, compare values, and determine the leading binary exponent of a positive value using $O(\log(L+1))$ mutable bits.

Here is an explicit justification.

Clear denominators symbolically. All resulting integers have $O(L)$ bits. A requested output bit can be recomputed from the input:

  • Addition and subtraction scan columns from least significant to most significant, keeping a bounded carry or borrow.
  • Multiplication scans the same columns, adding at most $O(L)$ bit products per column. The carry is $O(L)$ and needs $O(\log L)$ bits.
  • A fixed expression depth requires only a fixed number of nested loop counters and carries.
  • Input addresses and column indices need $O(\log L)$ bits.

Repeated scans find the highest nonzero bit and compare integers, without retaining the integers themselves.

For a positive rational $A/D$, let $a,d$ be the bit lengths of $A,D$. Its binary exponent is either $a-d-1$ or $a-d$. Comparing $A/D$ with $2^{a-d}$ decides which, by shifted integer comparison. Equality is handled exactly.

All scans are finite. Their time may be large, but they consume no society samples.

5.1 Compact approximations to $\theta$ and $t$

We can compute compact dyadic or small-integer values $\theta_0,u$ satisfying
$$ \theta_0\leq\theta\leq4\theta_0, \qquad t\leq u\leq4t. $$

For $\theta_0$:

  • If $\Delta\leq1/4$, let $h=-\lfloor\log_2\Delta\rfloor$ and $\alpha=2^{-h}$. Then $\alpha\leq\Delta<2\alpha$. Set

$$ \theta_0=4\alpha. $$
Section 2 gives $\theta_0\leq\theta\leq4\theta_0$.

  • If $\Delta>1/4$, let $h=-\lfloor\log_2q\rfloor\geq3$ and set

$$ \theta_0=h/4. $$
Since $2^{-h}\leq q<2^{1-h}$ and $p>3/4$,
$$ \theta\geq(h-1)\ln2-\ln(4/3) \geq\frac{2h}{3}-1 \geq\frac h4, $$
while $\theta\leq h\ln2\leq h$.
The elementary inequalities used here are
$$ \ln x\geq\frac{2(x-1)}{x+1}\quad(x\geq1), \qquad \ln x\leq x-1. $$
The first follows by differentiating the difference, whose derivative is $(x-1)^2/[x(x+1)^2]$.

For $u$:

  • If $\delta\leq1/2$, let $f=-\lfloor\log_2\delta\rfloor$ and $\beta=2^{-f}$. Set $u=8\beta$. Since

$$ 2\delta\leq t =\int_0^\delta\frac{2\,dx}{1-x^2} \leq\frac83\delta, $$
and $\beta\leq\delta<2\beta$, we obtain $t\leq u\leq4t$.

  • If $\delta>1/2$, then $\epsilon<1/4$. Let $h=-\lfloor\log_2\epsilon\rfloor$ and set $u=h$. The same calculation used for $q$ gives

$$ h/4\leq t\leq h. $$

Every exponent or small coefficient here has magnitude $O(L)$, so its compact representation needs $O(\log(L+1))$ bits.

6. Uniform upper bounds

6.1 Constant-factor optimal samples with small workspace

First suppose $\delta\leq2\Delta$. Put
$$ a_0=\frac{\delta}{2\Delta}\leq1 $$
and choose the smallest power of two $a_1$ satisfying $a_1\geq a_0$. Then
$$ a_0\leq a_1<2a_0. $$
The exponent of $a_1$ is computable by the arithmetic procedure above, including equality cases.

With probability $a_1$, take one sample and announce its top candidate. Otherwise announce a fair random candidate. Its error under either society is
$$ \frac12-a_1\Delta\leq\epsilon. $$
Its sample count is less than $2N_*$.

A coin of probability $2^{-h}$ is generated by requiring $h$ consecutive fair heads, stopping at the first tail. Its counter needs $O(\log(L+1))$ bits because $h=O(L)$.

Now suppose $\delta>2\Delta$. Compute $\theta_0,u$ as above and run the signed-sum test with
$$ J=\left\lceil\frac{u}{\theta_0}\right\rceil. $$
Then
$$ J\theta\geq t,\qquad J\leq1+\frac{16t}{\theta}, $$
so its error is at most $\epsilon$.

Moreover,
$$ \delta>2\Delta=\tanh(\theta/2) $$
implies $t>\theta$. Hence $J\theta\leq17t$. Concavity of $\tanh$ on the nonnegative reals, together with $\tanh(0)=0$, gives
$$ d_J=\tanh(J\theta/2)\leq17\tanh(t/2)=17\delta. $$
Its expected sample count is therefore
$$ \frac{Jd_J}{2\Delta} \leq \frac{17\delta}{2\Delta} \left(1+\frac{16t}{\theta}\right) =O(N_*). $$

The compact representations of $u,\theta_0$ allow $J$ to be formed using
$$ O(\log(L+1)+\log(J+1)) $$
bits. Explicitly, their ratio has the form $A2^e/C$, with $A,C=O(L)$. If the ratio is below one, $J=1$. Otherwise its numerator and denominator can be materialized using $O(\log(J+1)+\log(L+1))$ bits and divided by ordinary integer arithmetic.

The signed sum remains between $-J$ and $J$; no sample-count counter is needed. Thus this algorithm has deterministic peak workspace
$$ \boxed{ O\left( \log(L+1)+\log\left(1+\frac{t}{\theta}\right) \right). } $$

6.2 Smaller expected workspace when $\delta$ is small

For minimizing workspace, a different algorithm improves the dependence on $\delta$.

Suppose $\delta\leq1/4$. Choose the smallest power of two $a_1$ satisfying
$$ a_1\geq2\delta. $$
Then
$$ 2\delta\leq a_1<4\delta,\qquad a_1\leq1. $$
Compute and retain only its exponent and the compact representation of $\theta_0$. This costs $O(\log(L+1))$ bits.

With probability $a_1$, construct
$$ J=\left\lceil\frac2{\theta_0}\right\rceil $$
and run the threshold-$J$ test. Otherwise announce a fair random candidate.

Because $J\theta\geq2$,
$$ d_J\geq\tanh(1)>\frac12. $$
The algorithm's advantage under either society is therefore
$$ a_1d_J\geq\delta, $$
which proves its error guarantee.

Also,
$$ J\leq1+\frac8\theta. $$
The additional $O(\log(J+1))$ bits are allocated only on the branch of probability $a_1$. Its worst-case expected peak workspace is consequently
$$ O\left( \log(L+1)+ \delta\log(1+1/\theta) \right). $$
It halts almost surely and has finite expected sample count. This branch need not be sample-optimal.

For $\delta>1/4$, use Section 6.1. Since $\delta$ is then bounded below,
$$ \log(1+t/\theta) =O\left( \delta\log\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right) \right). $$

Together, these constructions prove
$$ \boxed{ W_*+1= O\left( 1+\log(L+1)+ \delta\log_2\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right) \right). } $$

7. Resolved workspace regimes and the cited bound

The lower and upper bounds match whenever
$$ \log(L+1)= O\left( 1+\delta\log_2\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right) \right). $$
In that regime,
$$ W_*+1= \Theta\left( 1+\delta\log_2\left(1+\frac{1+t}{\theta}\right) \right). $$

Two concrete cases are fully covered.

One sample suffices. If $\epsilon\geq q$, simply announce the top candidate of one sample. This uses bounded workspace, so
$$ W_*+1=\Theta(1). $$
Its sample count need not equal the independently minimized $N_*$.

Fixed $\Delta$ and increasingly small error. Fix a rational $\Delta\in(0,1/2)$ and let $\epsilon=2^{-h}$, with ordinary explicit encoding and no padding. Then $L=\Theta(h)$, $\theta$ is constant, $t=\Theta(h)$, and $\delta$ tends to one. Therefore
$$ W_*=\Theta(\log h) =\Theta\left(\log\log\frac1\epsilon\right), $$
while
$$ N_*=\Theta\left(\log\frac1\epsilon\right). $$

The cited discrete theorem states sample and space lower bounds involving $\operatorname{MoV}/n$. Substituting $\Delta=\operatorname{MoV}/n$ into its sample bound yields $\Omega(\delta^2/\Delta^2)$, consistent with Section 2. [Liu et al., Theorem 3](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v216/liu23a/liu23a.pdf)

Its displayed space expression cannot be used as a uniform lower bound in the present expected-peak model when $\epsilon$ varies. The following admissible family demonstrates this directly.

For integers $h\geq2$, take
$$ \Delta_h=2^{-2^h},\qquad \delta_h=2^{-h},\qquad \epsilon_h=\frac{1-2^{-h}}2. $$
Their explicit encoding length is
