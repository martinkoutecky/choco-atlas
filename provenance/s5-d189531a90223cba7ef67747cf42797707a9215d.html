The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), an undirected compatibility graph, and a ranking with ties of compatible types for each type, does a strongly stable mass matching exist? Choose \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge 0\) for compatible unordered pairs, where \(x_{tt}\) is within-type pair mass; let \(\ell_{tu}=x_{tu}\) for \(t\ne u\), \(\ell_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_u\ell_{tu}\ge0\). Define \(S_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:u\succ_t v}\ell_{tv}\) and \(W_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v:u\succeq_t v}\ell_{tv}\). Ask whether there is such an \(x\) for which \(S_{t,u}W_{u,t}=S_{u,t}W_{t,u}=0\) for every compatible pair \(t,u\), or report that none exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Compatibility is an undirected graph with optional loops; a loop permits pairing distinct individuals of the same type. Let \(m\) count compatible unordered pairs, including loops, and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has a weak order over exactly its compatible types; all compatible partners are strictly preferred to being unmatched, and ties are retained without breaking them. Empty compatibility lists are allowed.
A mass matching has real variables \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\geq0\) on compatible pairs, with \(x_{tt}\) denoting within-type pair mass; variables on incompatible pairs are zero. Define \(\ell_{tu}=x_{tu}\) for \(t\neq u\), \(\ell_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and require \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_{v\in T}\ell_{tv}\geq0\).
For each compatible pair, define \(S_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v\in T:\,u\succ_t v}\ell_{tv}\) and \(W_{t,u}=r_t+\sum_{v\in T:\,u\succeq_t v}\ell_{tv}\), restricting these sums to compatible \(v\). Call the matching strongly stable when \(S_{t,u}W_{u,t}=S_{u,t}W_{t,u}=0\) for every compatible pair, including loops.

Is deciding whether a strongly stable mass matching exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete strongly stable roommates with incomplete lists and ties admits an \(O(nm)\) existence-and-construction algorithm, where \(n\) counts individuals and \(m\) counts edges ([Kunysz, 2016](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESA.2016.60)).

Polynomial-time solvability

The decision problem is in $\mathrm{P}$. A strongly stable mass matching, when one exists, can also be constructed in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

The proof below replaces the unverified capacitated-assignment theorem in the notes with a self-contained bipartite algorithm. Populations remain binary encoded; no algorithm constructs individual clones.

1. Strong stability forces a single partner rank

For each type $t$, number its indifference classes from best to worst as $1,\ldots,d_t$. Write $a_t(u)$ for the rank of compatible type $u$, and give unmatched status rank $d_t+1$.

Lemma 1. In a strongly stable mass matching, each type is either entirely unmatched or fully matched within one indifference class.

Proof. If $x_{tu}>0$ with $t\neq u$, then
$$ W_{t,u}\geq x_{tu}>0, \qquad W_{u,t}\geq x_{tu}>0. $$
The two stability equations imply $S_{t,u}=S_{u,t}=0$. In particular, both residual masses vanish.

If $x_{tt}>0$, then $W_{t,t}\geq 2x_{tt}>0$, so the loop equation implies $S_{t,t}=0$, again forcing zero residual mass.

Finally, suppose a type has positive allocations in two different classes. Its strict quantity for a partner in the better class includes the positive incidence allocated to the worse class. This contradicts the vanishing strict quantity established above. A type with no positive allocation is entirely unmatched. $\square$

Let $q_t$ be its selected partner rank, or $d_t+1$ when entirely unmatched. Since $\mu_t>0$,
$$ S_{t,u}=\mu_t\mathbf{1}[q_t>a_t(u)], \qquad W_{t,u}=\mu_t\mathbf{1}[q_t\geq a_t(u)]. $$

Thus a feasible allocation having the structure in Lemma 1 is strongly stable exactly when every compatible pair satisfies
$$ \neg\bigl(q_t>a_t(u)\ \text{and}\ q_u\geq a_u(t)\bigr), $$
$$ \neg\bigl(q_u>a_u(t)\ \text{and}\ q_t\geq a_t(u)\bigr). $$
These conditions also apply to loops.

We will use the analogous bipartite problem. Its vertices have positive capacities $b_v$, its edges have nonnegative allocations $y_{vw}$, and its residual capacities and comparison quantities are defined exactly as above, with ordinary edge incidence $y_{vw}$. There are no binding edge capacities. The same proof establishes Lemma 1 and the rank characterization for this problem.

2. A polynomial algorithm for the bipartite problem

We first prove the algorithmic ingredient needed later.

Lemma 2. For a bipartite graph with positive binary-encoded integer vertex capacities, incomplete weak preference lists, and no binding edge capacities, existence and construction of an assignment satisfying the strict/weak product equations are polynomial-time solvable.

2.1 The algorithm

Write the bipartition as $A\cup B$. Maintain:

  • a surviving compatibility graph $K$, initially the input graph;
  • an engagement graph $G\subseteq K$, initially empty;
  • a set $Q\subseteq B$ of right vertices that have received a proposal, initially empty.

Deleting a pair removes it from both $K$ and $G$.

Repeat the following operations.

Proposal phase. While some left vertex $i$ has no incident engagement but has a nonempty surviving list, let it propose to every right vertex $j$ in its best surviving class. For each proposal:

  • Add $ij$ to $G$ and add $j$ to $Q$.
  • Delete every surviving pair $kj$ for which $j$ strictly prefers $i$ to $k$.

Critical-set phase. For $X\subseteq A$, define
$$ \delta_G(X)=b(X)-b(N_G(X)), \qquad b(U)=\sum_{v\in U}b_v. $$
Find the inclusion-minimal set $X$ maximizing $\delta_G(X)$, and put $Y=N_G(X)$.

If $Y\neq\varnothing$, delete, for every $j\in Y$, its entire worst surviving indifference class, and return to the proposal phase.

If $Y=\varnothing$, compute a maximum allocation in $G$. Accept precisely when this allocation saturates every vertex in $Q$, and return it on acceptance.

We prove that all operations, including the critical-set computation, have polynomial implementations and that this acceptance criterion is exact.

2.2 Engagement invariants

At every completed proposal or deletion operation:

  • If a left vertex has engagements, its engaged neighbors are exactly its best surviving class.
  • If a right vertex has engagements, all its engaged neighbors form part of its worst surviving class, and have the same rank.

For the first assertion, proposals include an entire best class. Subsequent operations only delete pairs, and every removed engagement is also deleted from the surviving lists.

For the second assertion, a proposal deletes all strictly worse entries from the recipient’s list. A later surviving proposal cannot be worse than a retained engagement, because the earlier proposal would already have deleted it. A better proposal removes all worse engagements; a tied proposal preserves the common rank. A critical-set deletion removes the entire worst surviving class, including every engagement at that right vertex.

Consequently, after the proposal phase, a left vertex has a nonempty surviving list if and only if it has an engagement.

2.3 Computing the critical set by flow

Put $C=b(A)$. Construct the network
$$ s\longrightarrow i\longrightarrow j\longrightarrow z $$
with capacities $b_i$ on $si$, $C+1$ on each engagement edge $ij$, and $b_j$ on $jz$.

A minimum cut has capacity at most $C$, using the cut immediately after $s$. It therefore cuts no engagement arc of capacity $C+1$. A minimum cut whose left vertices on the source side are $X$ places exactly $N_G(X)$ on the source side on the right: additional right vertices would increase its capacity, since all capacities are positive. Its capacity is
$$ C-b(X)+b(N_G(X))=C-\delta_G(X). $$
Thus minimum cuts correspond to maximizers of $\delta_G$.

After a maximum flow, the vertices reachable from $s$ through positive residual-capacity arcs form the inclusion-minimal source side of a minimum cut. Indeed, no minimum cut has a positive residual arc leaving its source side, so it must contain every such reachable vertex. Hence the reachable left set is the required inclusion-minimal maximizer $X$.

This uses a network of the original graph’s size and capacities of polynomial bit length.

2.4 Every deletion preserves all strongly stable assignments

Fix any strongly stable assignment $N$ in the original instance. We prove inductively that every positive edge of $N$ survives.

For a proposal from $i$ to $j$, all positive partners of $i$ in $N$ still survive by induction. Therefore $i$ weakly prefers $j$ to its selected rank in $N$, including the possibility that $i$ is unmatched.

Suppose a deleted pair $kj$ had positive allocation in $N$. Then $j$ would have selected the rank of $k$, which is strictly worse than $i$. Thus $j$ strictly prefers $i$, while $i$ weakly prefers $j$, contradicting stability of $N$. Proposal deletions are therefore safe.

Now consider a critical-set phase with $Y\neq\varnothing$. For each engaged left vertex $i$, let $h_i$ be its best surviving rank. For each $j\in Y$, let $w_j$ be its worst surviving rank; by the engagement invariants, every engagement at $j$ has rank $w_j$.

Each $j\in Y$ must be fully matched in $N$. Otherwise Lemma 1 makes it entirely unmatched, and an engagement neighbor weakly willing to pair with $j$ gives a blocking pair. Moreover,
$$ q_j^N\leq w_j. $$

Define
$$ Y_0=\{j\in Y:q_j^N=w_j\}, $$
and let $X_0$ consist of the engaged vertices $i\in X$ with $q_i^N=h_i$.

Every positive allocation of $N$ at a vertex in $X_0$ uses its surviving best class, hence an engagement edge. Its right endpoint belongs to $Y$, and its selected right rank equals the engagement rank. Therefore all the capacity $b(X_0)$ is allocated into $Y_0$, giving
$$ b(X_0)\leq b(Y_0). $$

There is no engagement edge between $X\setminus X_0$ and $Y_0$. Such an edge would have a left endpoint strictly preferring its engagement class to its assignment in $N$, and a right endpoint indifferent between that edge and its assignment. It would block $N$. Hence
$$ N_G(X\setminus X_0)\subseteq Y\setminus Y_0. $$
It follows that
$$ \begin{aligned} \delta_G(X\setminus X_0) &\geq b(X)-b(X_0)-b(Y)+b(Y_0)\\ &=\delta_G(X)+b(Y_0)-b(X_0)\\ &\geq\delta_G(X). \end{aligned} $$

If $Y_0$ were nonempty, some vertex of $X$ would be engaged to a vertex of $Y_0$. Stability forces that left vertex into $X_0$, so $X_0$ would be nonempty. The displayed inequality would then make the proper subset $X\setminus X_0$ another maximizer, contradicting the inclusion-minimality of $X$.

Thus $Y_0=\varnothing$: every vertex in $Y$ receives a strictly better rank in $N$ than its worst surviving class. No edge deleted in the critical-set phase has positive allocation in $N$. This completes the induction.

2.5 The final test is exact

At termination, $Y=\varnothing$. Let $P$ be the left vertices with nonempty surviving lists.

Every maximum flow saturates every vertex of $P$. If some such vertex had unused capacity, it would be reachable directly from $s$ in the residual network, and its engagement neighbors would belong to $Y$, contradicting $Y=\varnothing$. Thus the returned maximum allocation $M$ has total mass
$$ |M|=b(P). $$
Here $|M|$ denotes the sum of its edge allocations.

Suppose an original strongly stable assignment $N$ exists. The safe-deletion result implies that its positive edges survive, so
$$ |N|\leq b(P). $$

Every vertex of $Q$ is fully matched in $N$. To see this, consider a proposal it received. At that moment the proposing left vertex weakly preferred the proposal to its assignment in $N$, because every positive edge of $N$ survived. If the recipient were unmatched in $N$, that proposal pair would block. Lemma 1 therefore makes the recipient fully matched. Consequently,
$$ |N|\geq b(Q). $$

All edges of $M$ end in $Q$. Combining these facts gives
$$ b(P)=|M|\leq b(Q)\leq |N|\leq b(P). $$
All inequalities are equalities, so every vertex in $Q$ is saturated by $M$. The algorithm accepts whenever a strongly stable assignment exists.

Conversely, suppose the algorithm accepts. Its allocation saturates all vertices of $P$ and $Q$ and leaves all other vertices unmatched. The engagement invariants give one partner class at every saturated vertex.

A surviving edge below its left endpoint’s best surviving class is strictly dominated there and cannot block. A surviving edge in that best class is engaged, so both endpoints are indifferent between it and their allocated partners; neither endpoint strictly improves.

Finally, every deleted edge is strictly dominated at its right endpoint by every eventual surviving partner. A proposal deletion removed strict successors; a critical-set deletion removed the entire worst surviving class. Subsequent operations only delete more entries. Its right endpoint belongs to $Q$ and is saturated on acceptance, so such an edge cannot block either.

Thus $M$ satisfies the original product equations.

2.6 Polynomial running time

Each pair is proposed along at most once: whenever an engagement disappears, the pair is deleted. Every critical phase with $Y\neq\varnothing$ deletes at least one engagement edge. Therefore there are at most a polynomial number of proposals and at most $|E|+1$ maximum-flow computations.

Integer maximum flows provide integer output allocations. The networks retain the original vertices and edges, and their capacities have polynomial bit length. This proves Lemma 2. $\square$

3. Integer realization, cloning, and closure of rank profiles

We next establish the structural properties used to impose symmetry.

Lemma 3. Every feasible strongly stable bipartite rank profile with integer vertex capacities has an integer realization.

Proof. Retain only edges whose ranks agree with the selected rank at both endpoints. Require degree $b_v$ at each selected matched vertex and zero at each selected unmatched vertex.

The given assignment witnesses feasibility. These requirements form a bipartite transportation problem with integer demands. A feasible integer flow therefore exists. Positive capacities ensure that every selected matched vertex receives a positive allocation in its selected class. The profile is unchanged, so the rank characterization preserves stability. $\square$

For proofs only, replace each vertex $v$ by $b_v$ individual clones. Replace every original edge by the complete bipartite graph between its clone groups. All clones inherit the original rankings, with clones of tied partner types tied.

An integer assignment can be realized as an individual matching by distributing its allocated units among unused endpoint clones.

Both stability directions hold:

  • A realization of a strongly stable integer assignment is strongly stable. By Lemma 1, every clone in a group has the same partner rank, or all are unmatched. A blocking clone pair would therefore give a forbidden strict/weak comparison between the original vertices.
  • Aggregating a strongly stable clone matching gives a strongly stable assignment. If a product were positive, choose a strictly willing clone at one endpoint and a weakly willing clone at the other. Their edge exists, and they cannot already be paired because one strictly wants a change. They would block.

In particular, every strongly stable clone matching is homogeneous within each group, by Lemma 1 applied to its aggregate.

Lemma 4. In a fixed bipartite instance:

  • Every strongly stable assignment saturates the same set of vertices.
  • Feasible rank profiles are closed under choosing the better rank on the left and the worse rank on the right, and under the opposite operation.

Proof. First consider individual strongly stable bipartite matchings $M,N$.

If an individual is unmatched in $M$ but matched in $N$, follow its component in $M\triangle N$. Its first $N$-edge strictly improves its assignment relative to $M$. Stability of $M$ forces the other endpoint to have an $M$-partner it strictly prefers. Stability of $N$ then forces the next endpoint to have an $N$-partner it strictly prefers. This propagates along the alternating path and prevents termination. Since the component is a finite path, this is impossible. Interchanging $M,N$ proves equality of their matched sets.

Their nontrivial symmetric-difference components are therefore alternating cycles. On a cycle, if a left individual strictly prefers its $M$-partner, stability of $N$ forces the adjacent right individual strictly to prefer its $N$-partner. Stability of $M$ forces the next left individual strictly to prefer its $M$-partner. Continuing around the cycle gives a uniform orientation. The reverse preference gives the reverse orientation. If no left individual has a strict preference, a strict preference on the right would force one on the left by the same argument; hence everyone on that cycle is indifferent.

Choose one entire matching on each cycle to give every left individual its better partner and every right individual its worse partner. This is a matching $R$. If an edge blocked $R$, choose the input matching agreeing with $R$ at its right endpoint. The right comparison is unchanged, and the left endpoint’s weak or strict willingness transfers because its partner in $R$ is weakly better than its partner in that input matching. The edge would block that input matching, a contradiction. The opposite operation is stable by the symmetric argument.

Now use Lemma 3 to realize any two assignment profiles as clone matchings. Equality of matched clone sets implies equality of saturated original vertices. Homogeneity makes the two cycle-selection operations exactly the asserted coordinatewise operations on original ranks. $\square$

4. A polynomial oracle for arbitrary rank bounds

Fix a bipartite instance $H$ having a strongly stable assignment. Let $C$ be its invariant saturated set, determined by one call to Lemma 2.

All ranks below refer to the original lists, including after truncation. Consider bounds
$$ \lambda_v\leq q_v\leq\upsilon_v, \qquad 1\leq\lambda_v,\upsilon_v\leq d_v+1. $$

Initialize $U_v=\upsilon_v$. For each oriented original edge $vw$ with $a_v(w)<\lambda_v$, tighten
$$ U_w\leftarrow\min\{U_w,a_w(v)-1\}. $$

Reject if any $U_v<\lambda_v$, or if any vertex outside $C$ has $U_v<d_v+1$. Otherwise delete every edge $vw$ with
$$ a_v(w)>U_v \quad\text{or}\quad a_w(v)>U_w. $$
Run Lemma 2 on the truncated instance. Accept precisely when it returns an assignment with saturated set $C$.

Lemma 5. This procedure accepts exactly the feasible rank bounds.

Proof. Suppose a strongly stable original assignment satisfies the bounds. If $a_v(w)<\lambda_v$, then $q_v>a_v(w)$, so stability requires $q_w<a_w(v)$. It therefore satisfies every tightened upper bound. Its positive edges survive, and it remains stable after deletion. By Lemma 4 in the truncated instance, whichever assignment the algorithm returns has the same saturated set as this witness. Thus the procedure accepts.

Conversely, suppose the procedure accepts. At every saturated vertex, surviving partners give $q_v\leq U_v$. At every unmatched vertex, the preliminary check gives $q_v=d_v+1\leq U_v$.

A saturated vertex cannot have $q_v<\lambda_v$. A positive edge $vw$ at that rank would have imposed $U_w<a_w(v)$ and thus been deleted. Unmatched vertices automatically satisfy their lower bounds.

Every deleted edge is strictly dominated at an endpoint responsible for its deletion. For example, $a_v(w)>U_v$ implies that $v$ is saturated and
$$ q_v\leq U_v<a_v(w). $$
That endpoint has zero weak willingness, excluding both blocking orientations. Surviving edges are stable by Lemma 2. Hence the returned assignment is stable in the original instance and satisfies all bounds. $\square$

5. An exact Boolean representation of feasible profiles

Choose a positive integer common denominator $D$ of the masses and put
$$ b_t=D\mu_t. $$
The product of the input denominators is a suitable choice, with binary length $O(L)$. Each $b_t$ is a positive integer and $\sum_t b_t=D$.

Construct a bipartite graph $H$ with copies $t^{\mathrm L},t^{\mathrm R}$, each of capacity $b_t$. For every compatible nonloop pair $\{t,u\}$, include
$$ t^{\mathrm L}u^{\mathrm R}, \qquad u^{\mathrm L}t^{\mathrm R}. $$
For a compatible loop, include $t^{\mathrm L}t^{\mathrm R}$. Preferences are inherited. This graph has $2\tau$ vertices and at most $2m$ edges.

Run Lemma 2. If no strongly stable assignment exists, reject. Otherwise introduce, for each $t$ and $2\leq h\leq d_t+1$, Boolean variables
$$ A_{t,h}=\mathbf{1}[q_{t^{\mathrm L}}\geq h], \qquad B_{t,h}=\mathbf{1}[q_{t^{\mathrm R}}<h]. $$

Let $F$ be the family of sets of true variables arising from feasible strongly stable profiles in $H$. Lemma 4 implies that $F$ is closed under intersection and union: better left ranks and worse right ranks give intersection; the opposite operation gives union.

The number of variables is
$$ p=2\sum_t d_t\leq 4m. $$
Any prescribed truth values translate into rank bounds. Lemma 5 therefore tests one-variable and two-variable prescriptions in polynomial time.

Construct a formula $\Phi$ containing:

  • $\neg i$ if no feasible set contains variable $i$;
  • $i$ if every feasible set contains $i$;
  • $\neg i\lor j$ if no feasible set contains $i$ while omitting $j$.

This requires $O((p+1)^2)$ bound queries.

Lemma 6. The satisfying assignments of $\Phi$ are exactly $F$.

Proof. Every feasible set satisfies the clauses by construction.

Conversely, let $X$ satisfy $\Phi$. Since $F$ is finite and nonempty, its intersection
$$ F_0=\bigcap_{Y\in F}Y $$
belongs to $F$. The positive unit clauses give $F_0\subseteq X$.

For each $i\in X$, the negative unit clauses ensure that some feasible set contains $i$. Define
$$ F_i=\bigcap\{Y\in F:i\in Y\}. $$
Then $F_i\in F$ and $i\in F_i$. If $j\in F_i$, every feasible set containing $i$ contains $j$, so $\Phi$ contains $i\Rightarrow j$. Thus $F_i\subseteq X$.

Consequently,
$$ X=F_0\cup\bigcup_{i\in X}F_i. $$
Closure under finite unions gives $X\in F$, including when $X$ is empty. $\square$

6. Enforcing symmetry gives a $2$-SAT instance

The required symmetry is
$$ q_{t^{\mathrm L}}=q_{t^{\mathrm R}} \qquad\text{for every }t. $$
At each threshold, this is equivalent to $A_{t,h}=\neg B_{t,h}$. Add
$$ A_{t,h}\lor B_{t,h}, \qquad \neg A_{t,h}\lor\neg B_{t,h} $$
to $\Phi$, obtaining a $2$-CNF formula $\Psi$.

Lemma 7. The mass instance is solvable if and only if $H$ has a strongly stable assignment with equal ranks at the two copies of every type.

Proof. Given a strongly stable mass matching, define
$$ y_{t^{\mathrm L}u^{\mathrm R}}=Dx_{tu} \quad(t\neq u), \qquad y_{t^{\mathrm L}t^{\mathrm R}}=2Dx_{tt}. $$
Both copies of $t$ have residual $Dr_t$ and comparison quantities equal to $D$ times the original ones. Thus the assignment is stable and has equal copy ranks.

Conversely, let $y$ have common copy rank $q_t$. Abbreviate its allocation on $t^{\mathrm L}u^{\mathrm R}$ by $y_{tu}$, assigning zero to nonexistent edges. Set
$$ x_{tu}=\frac{y_{tu}+y_{ut}}{2D} \quad(t\neq u), \qquad x_{tt}=\frac{y_{tt}}{2D}. $$

Equal ranks imply equal saturation status at the two copies. Their row and column sums are both $b_t$, or both zero. Therefore
$$ \sum_{u\neq t}x_{tu}+2x_{tt} = \frac{\sum_u y_{tu}+\sum_u y_{ut}}{2D} $$
equals $\mu_t$ or zero, establishing feasibility.

Every positive allocation incident to $t$ lies in rank $q_t$: both copy profiles have that rank. Stability of $t^{\mathrm L}u^{\mathrm R}$ gives the two forbidden-comparison conditions for the original pair $\{t,u\}$. Lemma 1’s rank characterization therefore establishes strong stability. The same argument uses $t^{\mathrm L}t^{\mathrm R}$ for loops, with the displayed factor ensuring the correct incidence $2x_{tt}$. $\square$

By Lemma 6, satisfiability of $\Psi$ is exactly existence of the symmetric profile in Lemma 7. Polynomial-time $2$-SAT therefore decides the mass problem.

For construction, recover the profile from a satisfying assignment. Retain edges agreeing with its selected rank at both endpoints, and require degree $b_t$ at each matched copy and zero at each unmatched copy. Lemma 6 guarantees feasibility; an integer flow realizes the profile. Applying Lemma 7 constructs the mass matching.

7. Bit complexity and conclusion

The algorithm uses $O((m+1)^2)$ rank-bound queries. Each query invokes the bipartite algorithm on at most $2\tau$ vertices and $2m$ edges, using at most $O(m+1)$ maximum-flow computations. The flow capacities are bounded by $D+1$, with $O(L)$ bits.

The Boolean formula has $O(m)$ variables and $O((m+1)^2)$ clauses. Reconstruction requires one further integer flow. Its output satisfies
$$ 2Dx_{tu}\in\mathbb Z $$
for every compatible pair, including loops, so the output encoding also has polynomial length.

Empty lists, isolated types, and loops are all included in the arguments. Hence the exact strict/weak mass-stability problem stated in the question is polynomial-time decidable and constructible.
