Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational interaction matrix \(a_{tu}\), and fixed rational \(0<\alpha<1\), let \(d_t=\sum_u a_{tu}\mu_u>0\). Find an initial seed vector \(x\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) of minimum total mass \(\sum_t x_t\) such that the irreversible cascade \(q_t^{(0)}=x_t\), \(q_t^{(k+1)}=q_t^{(k)}+(\mu_t-q_t^{(k)})\mathbf{1}[\sum_u a_{tu}q_u^{(k)}\ge\alpha d_t]\) reaches \(q_t^{(K)}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\) at some finite round \(K\).

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(\tau\ge2\), and let \(a\) be a symmetric nonnegative binary-rational interaction matrix. Require the graph with edges \(\{t,u\}\) for distinct types satisfying \(a_{tu}>0\) to be connected; diagonal entries may represent interactions within a type. Fix a rational \(0<\alpha<1\), and set \(d_t=\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}\mu_u>0\).
An initial seed is a real vector \(x\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\), costing \(\sum_t x_t\). Its synchronous irreversible cascade is \(q_t^{(0)}=x_t\) and \(q_t^{(k+1)}=q_t^{(k)}+(\mu_t-q_t^{(k)})\mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_{u\in T}a_{tu}q_u^{(k)}\ge\alpha d_t\right]\). Equality triggers activation.
A seed succeeds if \(q_t^{(K)}=\mu_t\) for every type at some finite integer \(K\ge0\). Given a binary-rational budget \(B\in[0,1]\), decide whether a successful seed of cost at most \(B\) exists. Let \(m\) count the positive off-diagonal unordered interaction pairs and \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

For each fixed rational \(0<\alpha<1\), is this minimum-seed-mass decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For connected simple discrete graphs, the minimum number of irreversible seeds satisfies \(1\le\operatorname{OPT}\le n(\alpha\delta+1)/(\delta+1)\), where \(n\) is the vertex count and \(\delta\) the minimum degree; these bounds are tight up to an additive constant. This is a size bound, not an exact optimization algorithm. [Zehmakan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.05917)
No result settling the exact continuous decision problem above was identified in the literature search.

  • Retained nonnegative diagonal interactions as interactions between members of the same type; a type represents a mass of individuals.

1. Result

For every fixed rational \(0<\alpha<1\), the minimum-seed-mass decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This holds with a connected interaction graph, dyadic rational input data, and \(m=O(\tau)\).

An exact algorithm runs in
\(\tau!\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\)
bit operations. Thus, for fixed support size \(\tau\), the problem is polynomial-time solvable.

The hardness proof below explicitly accounts for arbitrary real seeds, including seeds placed on auxiliary types.

2. Normalized coordinates

Define the initial seeded fraction and the current active fraction by
\(y_t=x_t/\mu_t\) and \(z_t=q_t/\mu_t\).
Define symmetric weights
\(w_{tu}=\mu_ta_{tu}\mu_u\).
Multiplying the activation inequality for type \(t\) by \(\mu_t\) gives the equivalent condition
\[ \]
\( \displaystyle \sum_u w_{tu}z_u\ge \alpha\sum_u w_{tu}. \)

The seed cost is \(\sum_t\mu_ty_t\). Until a type becomes fully active, its fraction remains exactly \(y_t\); afterward its fraction is \(1\).

We construct symmetric weights first and then obtain the required interaction matrix from them.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and an exact algorithm

Fix a permutation \(v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\) of the types. Consider the following rational linear inequalities in the original seed variables:
\( \displaystyle 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_t\le B, \)
and, for every \(i\),
\[ \displaystyle \sum_{j<i}a_{v_iv_j}\mu_{v_j} +\sum_{j\ge i}a_{v_iv_j}x_{v_j} \ge \alpha d_{v_i}. \tag{1} \]

These inequalities are feasible for some permutation if and only if a successful seed of cost at most \(B\) exists.

To prove sufficiency, use induction on \(i\). By round \(i-1\), the preceding types can be assumed fully active. Every other type has active mass at least its seed. Nonnegativity of the interactions and inequality (1) therefore imply that \(v_i\) is fully active by round \(i\).

For necessity, take a successful seed. Order the types that were not initially full by their activation times, breaking ties arbitrarily. Place initially full types last. For a type that activates during the cascade, the expression in (1) is at least its actual input immediately before activation: placing types activated in the same round earlier in the permutation can only increase that expression. For an initially full type placed last, the expression is exactly \(d_{v_i}\), because all remaining seed variables equal their masses. Thus every inequality holds.

For a fixed permutation, this is a rational linear program with \(\tau\) variables, \(3\tau+1\) inequalities, and coefficient bit length polynomial in \(L\). Rational linear-program feasibility is decidable in polynomial time, so guessing a permutation establishes membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Equivalently, any nonempty feasible polytope has a rational vertex of polynomial encoding length, by clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule to a set of tight independent constraints. Thus allowing real seeds does not obstruct polynomial-size certificates.

Enumerating permutations gives the claimed running time
\(\tau!\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\).
The same formulation also shows that the minimum seed mass is attained and is rational: the successful seeds form a finite union of closed bounded rational polytopes.

4. Source problem and numerical parameters

We reduce from Vertex Cover. Its decision version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete: given a simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether some subset of at most \(k\) vertices meets every edge. This is Karp’s “Node Cover” problem. [Karp, *Reducibility among Combinatorial Problems*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

Write \(n=|V|\) and \(r=|E|\). We may restrict to \(n\ge2\), \(r\ge1\), and \(0\le k\le n\). Requiring at least one edge preserves hardness, since adding a disjoint edge and increasing \(k\) by one preserves the answer.

Choose a fixed dyadic power
\(\beta=2^{-b}\)
satisfying
\(0<\beta\le\min\{\alpha/4,1/8\}\).
Its value depends only on the fixed threshold \(\alpha\).

Set
\( \displaystyle H=\frac{16(n+1)^2}{\beta},\qquad G_0=2\beta H=32(n+1)^2, \)
\( \displaystyle J=\max\left\{8nG_0+8,\left\lceil\frac2\alpha\right\rceil,4\right\}, \qquad H_g=\left\lceil\frac{2rJ}{\alpha}\right\rceil. \)

All four quantities are positive integers. Define
\[ \displaystyle \eta=\frac{\beta}{4(n+1)},\qquad \epsilon=\min\left\{ \frac1{16(n+1)},\ \frac1{16J},\ \frac{J}{8(J+H_g)} \right\}. \tag{2} \]

The construction has one type for each vertex, one for each edge, an anchor \(h\), a gate \(g\), and a ballast type \(f\).

5. Interactions and residual thresholds

All unspecified weights are zero. For every vertex \(v\), put
\(w_{vv}=H\) and \(w_{vg}=G_0\).
For every edge \(e=\{u,v\}\), put
\(w_{ue}=w_{ve}=1\) and \(w_{eg}=J\).
Put \(w_{gg}=H_g\).

Connect every vertex type, edge type, and the gate to the anchor. Their respective weights are
\( \displaystyle P_v=\left\lceil \frac{\alpha(H+G_0+\deg_G(v))-\beta H}{1-\alpha} \right\rceil, \)
\( \displaystyle P_e=\frac18\left\lfloor 8\,\frac{\alpha(2+J)-1/2}{1-\alpha} \right\rfloor, \)
\( \displaystyle P_g=\left\lfloor \frac{\alpha(H_g+rJ+nG_0)-(r-1/2)J}{1-\alpha} \right\rfloor. \)

Thus \(w_{vh}=P_v\), \(w_{eh}=P_e\), and \(w_{gh}=P_g\), with symmetric counterparts.

All these weights are strictly positive:

  • For \(P_v\), the numerator is positive because \(\beta\le\alpha/4\).
  • For \(P_e\), \(\alpha J\ge2\), so the expression before rounding is larger than \(3/2\).
  • For \(P_g\), \(\alpha H_g\ge2rJ\), so its numerator is at least \((r+1/2)J\).

When the anchor is fully active, subtract its contribution from each activation inequality. The resulting residual thresholds are
\( \displaystyle R_v=\alpha(H+G_0+\deg_G(v))-(1-\alpha)P_v, \)
\( \displaystyle R_e=\alpha(2+J)-(1-\alpha)P_e, \)
\( \displaystyle R_g=\alpha(H_g+rJ+nG_0)-(1-\alpha)P_g. \)

The rounding choices give
\[ \displaystyle \beta H-1<R_v\le\beta H, \tag{3} \]
\[ \displaystyle \frac12\le R_e<\frac58, \tag{4} \]
\[ \displaystyle (r-\tfrac12)J\le R_g<(r-\tfrac12)J+1. \tag{5} \]

For example, writing
\(A_v=[\alpha(H+G_0+\deg_G(v))-\beta H]/(1-\alpha)\)
gives
\(R_v=\beta H-(1-\alpha)(\lceil A_v\rceil-A_v)\),
which proves (3). The other two bounds follow identically from rounding downward, with mesh \(1/8\) and \(1\), respectively.

If the anchor is not fully active, these residual inequalities remain necessary for activation: replacing its active fraction by \(1\) only increases the available input. Consequently, the soundness proof can allow the anchor its maximum possible contribution.

6. Masses, budget, and binary encoding

Choose a dyadic power \(c\) with
\(0<c\le1/[4(r+1)]\).
Give every edge type and the gate mass \(c\).

Choose a dyadic power \(s>0\) satisfying
\[ \displaystyle ns\le\frac14,\qquad \frac{(n+1)s\beta}{c}\le\epsilon. \tag{6} \]
Give every vertex type mass \(s\). Give the anchor mass
\(\mu_h=s\beta/4\), which is also a dyadic power.

Set the budget to
\[ \displaystyle B=ks\beta+\mu_h=(k+\tfrac14)s\beta. \tag{7} \]

Give the ballast the remaining mass
\( \displaystyle \mu_f=1-ns-(r+1)c-\mu_h. \)
This is positive: the first two subtracted terms total at most \(1/2\), and \(\mu_h<1/4\).

For the interactions specified above, define
\(a_{tu}=w_{tu}/(\mu_t\mu_u)\).
All involved masses are dyadic powers, and all specified weights are integers or integer multiples of \(1/8\). Hence these interaction entries are dyadic rationals.

Finally, connect the ballast only to the anchor, with
\(a_{fh}=a_{hf}=1\).
This avoids dividing by the ballast mass when specifying an interaction.

The resulting matrix is symmetric and nonnegative. Every type is connected to the anchor, so its off-diagonal interaction graph is connected. Every type therefore has \(d_t>0\). The masses sum to \(1\), and \(B\in[0,1]\).

7. Completeness: a vertex cover produces a successful seed

Suppose \(C\subseteq V\) is a vertex cover with \(|C|\le k\). Seed the anchor fully, seed fraction \(\beta\) of each vertex type in \(C\), and seed nothing else.

Its cost is
\(\mu_h+|C|s\beta\le B\).

The cascade succeeds as follows.

  • First round: Each selected vertex receives self-input \(H\beta\), which meets its residual threshold by (3). Thus every selected vertex becomes full. The ballast also becomes full because its only neighbor, the anchor, is full and \(\alpha<1\).
  • Second round: Each edge has a selected endpoint. That endpoint contributes \(1\), exceeding \(R_e<5/8\). Hence every edge type becomes full.
  • Third round: The edge types contribute \(rJ\) to the gate. By (5) and \(J\ge4\),

\(rJ>R_g\).
Thus the gate becomes full.

  • Fourth round: Each remaining vertex receives gate input

\(G_0=2\beta H\ge R_v\),
so it becomes full.

Thus a vertex cover of size at most \(k\) gives a successful seed within budget.

8. Soundness: every successful real seed yields a vertex cover

Now let an arbitrary real seed have cost at most \(B\).

Every edge type and the gate has initial fraction at most
\[ \displaystyle \frac{B}{c} \le\frac{(n+1)s\beta}{c} \le\epsilon. \tag{8} \]
In particular, none is initially full.

Define
\( \displaystyle S=\{v\in V:y_v\ge\beta-\eta\}. \)

We prove that \(S\) is a vertex cover and that \(|S|\le k\).

The budget bounds the size of \(S\). If \(|S|\ge k+1\), the vertex seed cost alone is at least
\((k+1)s(\beta-\eta)\).
But
\( \displaystyle (k+1)s(\beta-\eta)-B =s\left(\frac{3\beta}{4}-(k+1)\eta\right) \ge\frac{s\beta}{2}>0, \)
where \(k+1\le n+1\) and the definition of \(\eta\) were used. This contradicts the budget. Hence \(|S|\le k\).

Vertices outside \(S\) cannot activate before the gate. Before its first activation, the gate has fraction \(y_g\le\epsilon\). If \(v\notin S\) has not yet activated, its non-anchor input is at most
\(Hy_v+n+G_0\epsilon\):
there are at most \(n\) incident edge types, each contributing at most \(1\).

From the definitions,
\(H\eta=4(n+1)\), and
\(G_0\epsilon\le2(n+1)\).
Therefore
\( \displaystyle n+G_0\epsilon+1 \le3(n+1)<H\eta. \)

Since \(y_v<\beta-\eta\), it follows that
\( \displaystyle Hy_v+n+G_0\epsilon <\beta H-1<R_v. \)
Thus \(v\) cannot activate while the gate remains unactivated. This conclusion holds even if every edge type is already full and the anchor contributes its maximum possible input.

An uncovered edge cannot activate before the gate. Suppose \(e=\{u,v\}\) has neither endpoint in \(S\). Before the gate activates, both endpoints remain at their initial fractions, each smaller than \(\beta\). The edge’s non-anchor input is therefore at most
\( \displaystyle 2\beta+J\epsilon \le\frac14+\frac1{16} =\frac5{16}<\frac12\le R_e. \)
So this edge cannot activate before the gate.

The gate requires every edge to be full already. Consider a round immediately before the gate’s first activation. If any edge is not full, that edge still has its initial fraction, at most \(\epsilon\). Consequently, the gate’s non-anchor input is at most
\( \displaystyle (r-1)J+(J+H_g)\epsilon+nG_0. \)

By (2) and the choice of \(J\),
\( \displaystyle (J+H_g)\epsilon\le\frac J8,\qquad nG_0<\frac J8. \)
Thus this input is smaller than
\( \displaystyle (r-1)J+\frac J4 <(r-\tfrac12)J\le R_g. \)
Activation is impossible. Therefore all edge types must be full strictly before the gate’s first activation.

If \(S\) failed to cover some edge, that edge could not activate before the gate, whereas the gate could not activate before that edge. A successful cascade necessarily activates the gate, so this is a contradiction.

Hence \(S\) is a vertex cover with \(|S|\le k\). Soundness applies to every real seed, without any restriction on which types receive seed mass.

9. Size and complexity of the reduction

The construction has
\(\tau=n+r+3\)
types and exactly
\(m=4r+2n+2=O(\tau)\)
positive off-diagonal unordered pairs.

For fixed \(\alpha\),
\(H,G_0=O_\alpha(n^2)\),
\(J=O_\alpha(n^3)\), and
\(H_g=O_\alpha(rn^3)\).
Also,
\(\epsilon^{-1}=O_\alpha(n^3+r)\).
Choosing the largest dyadic powers satisfying the mass inequalities makes all mass denominators polynomially bounded in \(n+r\). The anchor weights and interaction entries consequently have \(O_\alpha(\log(n+r))\) bits.

The dense output has
\(L=O_\alpha(\tau^2\log\tau)\),
and can be constructed in
\(O_\alpha(\tau^2\operatorname{poly}(\log\tau))\)
bit operations. Thus this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

Completeness and soundness establish
\( \displaystyle G\text{ has a vertex cover of size at most }k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed society has a successful seed of cost at most }B. \)

Together with membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for every fixed rational \(0<\alpha<1\). A polynomial-time algorithm for unrestricted support size would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
