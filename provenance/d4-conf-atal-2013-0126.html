The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings over candidates \(C\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), define \(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\). Include an edge \(a\to b\) of strength \(M_\mu(a,b)\) whenever this margin is positive. Given a target \(c^\star\), determine whether it is top-ranked in at least one outcome of neutral Ranked Pairs over all permitted tie-breakings.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set with \(m=|C|\), and let \(c^\star\in C\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For distinct candidates define \(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\).
Neutral Ranked Pairs processes every unordered candidate pair in decreasing order of \(|M_\mu(a,b)|\), allowing every ordering within equal-strength groups. Start with an empty directed relation. For a nonzero margin, try adding the majority-preferred orientation; if this creates a directed cycle, add the opposite orientation instead. For a zero margin, either orientation may be tried first, again adding its opposite if necessary. After every pair has been processed, the relation is a strict complete ranking. All rankings obtainable through these choices are outcomes.
Is deciding whether \(c^\star\) is top-ranked in at least one outcome solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?
Discrete winner membership is NP-complete for every fixed even number of voters at least eight (Brandt, Harrenstein, Kardel, and Seedig, 2013). [Brill and Fischer](https://webspace.maths.qmul.ac.uk/felix.fischer/publications/bf_ranked.pdf) also establish unrestricted discrete NP-completeness and polynomial-time computation when the tie-breaking rule is fixed.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(\tau\leq 8\) and every mass belongs to \(\{1/8,2/8,\ldots,8/8\}\). Consequently, it admits an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof uses the discrete hardness theorem stated in the question and a polynomial-time reduction that preserves every obtainable ranking.

1. The discrete theorem applies to this rule

The known theorem states that deciding whether a designated candidate is a neutral Ranked Pairs winner is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for profiles consisting of exactly eight voters with strict complete rankings. This is the eight-voter case of Theorem 3 of [Brandt, Harrenstein, Kardel, and Seedig](https://pub.dss.in.tum.de/brandt-research/fewvoters.pdf).

The relevant rule agrees with the formulation in the question: process unordered pairs by decreasing absolute majority margin; try the majority orientation, or a freely chosen orientation when the margin is zero; and add the opposite orientation if the attempted orientation creates a cycle. Winners are the top candidates obtainable under some tie-breaking rule. See Section 3 of [Brill and Fischer](https://webspace.maths.qmul.ac.uk/felix.fischer/publications/bf_ranked.pdf).

For completeness, unrestricted tie-breaking in that formulation permits precisely the choices allowed here. Its tie-breaking rule is a total order on ordered candidate pairs. Given any desired processing order and any desired first orientations for zero-margin pairs, place the two orientations of each unordered pair consecutively in that processing order, placing the desired first orientation first. This defines a compatible total order; diagonal pairs may be placed arbitrarily. Conversely, any such total order induces an allowed processing order and zero-margin choices. Thus the possible outcomes coincide.

2. Reduction to a support-based society

Take an instance of the eight-voter problem, with rankings \(R_1,\ldots,R_8\), candidate set \(C\), and designated candidate \(c^\star\).

Let \(T\) contain exactly the distinct rankings appearing in the profile. For each \(t\in T\), define its multiplicity and mass by

\(k_t=|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,8\}:R_i=t\}|\), and \(\mu_t=k_t/8\).

This produces a valid society:

  • Each listed ranking is strict and complete, and the listed rankings are distinct.
  • Each mass is a positive binary rational.
  • Their sum is \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=(\sum_{t\in T}k_t)/8=1\).
  • The support size satisfies \(\tau\leq 8\).

Let the discrete majority margin be

\(D_R(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{8}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_{R_i}b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_{R_i}a]\bigr)\).

Grouping identical rankings gives, for every distinct \(a,b\),

\(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\frac{k_t}{8}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr)=\frac{1}{8}D_R(a,b)\).

Multiplication by the same positive constant preserves all margin signs, all zero margins, and all comparisons and equalities between absolute strengths. Therefore the two instances admit exactly the same processing orders and attempted orientations.

Fix any such choices. Initially both procedures have the empty relation. If their relations agree before processing a pair, their attempted orientations agree, and the cycle tests have the same answer. Hence their added orientations agree. Induction over all pairs proves that their final rankings coincide. Because the admissible choices also coincide, their entire outcome sets are equal.

In particular, \(c^\star\) is a discrete Ranked Pairs winner if and only if it is top-ranked in some outcome of the constructed society.

The transformation is polynomial-time: compare the eight rankings, group identical ones, and attach their multiplicities divided by eight. It outputs at most eight rankings and masses of constant encoding length. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under the asserted restrictions.

3. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate specifies a permutation of the \(\binom{m}{2}\) unordered pairs, together with a first-orientation bit for every zero-margin pair. Its length is \(O(m^2\log(m+1))\).

First, all margins and strength comparisons can be computed exactly in polynomial time. Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), with binary-encoded integers and \(q_t>0\). Set \(Q=\prod_{t\in T}q_t\). The bit length of \(Q\) is \(O(L)\). For each pair compute the integer

\(W(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}p_t(Q/q_t)\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr)\).

Then \(M_\mu(a,b)=W(a,b)/Q\). Consequently, signs and absolute-strength comparisons reduce to exact integer comparisons. All integers involved have polynomial bit length, and there are \(O(\tau m^2)\) summands to process.

The verifier checks that the certificate lists every unordered pair exactly once and that strengths are nonincreasing. It then simulates the stated procedure. In an acyclic directed graph \(G\), adding \(a\to b\) creates a cycle exactly when \(G\) already contains a directed path from \(b\) to \(a\); reachability is polynomial-time computable.

The fallback orientation preserves acyclicity. Indeed, if \(G\) contains a path from \(b\) to \(a\), it cannot also contain a path from \(a\) to \(b\), since those paths would form a directed cycle. Thus adding \(b\to a\) is safe.

After all pairs have been processed, the graph is an acyclic tournament. It is transitive: if \(a\to b\) and \(b\to d\), the alternative orientation \(d\to a\) would form a directed triangle. Hence the graph represents a strict complete ranking.

Finally, the verifier accepts exactly when \(c^\star\) has an outgoing edge to every other candidate. Every accepting certificate therefore witnesses a valid outcome with \(c^\star\) on top, and every such outcome supplies an accepting certificate. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the proof.
