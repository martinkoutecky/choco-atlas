The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a nonempty \(k\)-uniform hypergraph \(H=(V,E)\) and nonnegative rational weights \(w_e\), form a unit-mass population with one type \(e\) of mass \(1/|E|\) and valuation \(v_e(S)=w_e\) if \(e\subseteq S\), and \(0\) otherwise; give each item category \(v\) supply \(1/|E|\). Choose masses \(x_{e,S}\ge 0\) of type \(e\) receiving each integral bundle \(S\subseteq V\), with \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}x_{e,S}=1/|E|\) and \(\sum_{e\in E,\,S\ni v}x_{e,S}=1/|E|\) for every \(v\in V\), to maximize \(\sum_{e\in E,\,S\subseteq V}x_{e,S}v_e(S)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(H=(V,E)\) be an explicitly listed hypergraph with \(E\ne\varnothing\), \(m=|V|\), and every edge of size \(k\), where \(1\le k\le m\). Each edge \(e\) defines a type of mass \(1/\tau\), where \(\tau=|E|\), with weight \(w_e\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) and valuation \(v_e(S)=w_e\) when \(e\subseteq S\), and \(v_e(S)=0\) otherwise.
The population has total mass \(1\), and each item category \(v\in V\) has supply \(1/\tau\). A feasible allocation consists of real masses \(x_{e,S}\ge0\), indexed by \(e\in E\) and integral bundles \(S\subseteq V\), satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq V}x_{e,S}=1/\tau\) for every \(e\) and \(\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{S\ni v}x_{e,S}=1/\tau\) for every \(v\).
Its welfare is \(\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{S\subseteq V}x_{e,S}v_e(S)\). The input also includes a threshold \(B\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\); let \(L\) be the total binary input length.

Is deciding whether a feasible allocation has welfare at least \(B\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For the discrete counterpart, with one player per edge and one indivisible item per vertex, maximum weighted \(k\)-set packing admits an approximation-preserving reduction to welfare maximization with these valuations; consequently, exact welfare maximization is NP-hard for every fixed \(k\ge3\). This discrete result does not classify the mass-allocation problem above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified 1 ≤ k ≤ m, excluding empty edges so that every valuation vanishes on the empty bundle, as required by the discrete definition.
  • Introduced a nonnegative binary-rational welfare threshold B to state the exact optimization question as a decision problem.
  • Specified real allocation masses and defined L as the total binary input length, including the explicit hypergraph, masses, weights, and threshold.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable. Its optimum is exactly \(1/\tau\) times the optimum of a fractional weighted set-packing linear program. The exact supply equalities do not obstruct this reduction: unused supply can be added to existing bundles without decreasing welfare.

1. The linear program

Consider the rational linear program
\[ \max \left\{\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{e\in E}w_e z_e: 0\le z_e\le1\ \text{for every }e\in E,\quad \sum_{e\ni v}z_e\le1\ \text{for every }v\in V \right\}. \]

Here \(z_e\) represents the fraction of type \(e\) receiving a bundle containing \(e\). We prove that this program and the allocation problem have equal optimal values.

2. Every allocation gives a feasible linear-program solution

Given a feasible allocation, define
\[ z_e=\tau\sum_{S\supseteq e}x_{e,S}. \]
The type-mass equality implies \(0\le z_e\le1\). For each vertex \(v\),
\[ \sum_{e\ni v}z_e =\tau\sum_{e\ni v}\sum_{S\supseteq e}x_{e,S} \le\tau\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{S\ni v}x_{e,S} =1. \]
The inequality holds because every bundle containing an edge \(e\ni v\) also contains \(v\), and all masses are nonnegative.

Thus \(z\) is feasible for the linear program. Moreover, the allocation’s welfare is exactly
\[ \sum_{e\in E}w_e\sum_{S\supseteq e}x_{e,S} =\frac1\tau\sum_{e\in E}w_ez_e. \]
Consequently, the linear-program optimum is an upper bound on achievable welfare.

3. Every feasible linear-program solution yields an allocation

Fix any feasible \(z\). We first use masses scaled by \(\tau\), so each type has mass \(1\), each vertex has supply \(1\), and total population mass is \(\tau\).

Enumerate the edges as \(e_1,\ldots,e_\tau\). Represent type \(e_i\) by the interval \(I_i=[i-1,i)\), with ordinary length as its scaled mass. Initially give bundle \(e_i\) to the first portion of \(I_i\) of length \(z_{e_i}\), and give the empty bundle to its remaining portion.

The initial consumption of vertex \(v\) is therefore
\[ a_v=\sum_{e\ni v}z_e\le1. \]
This allocation already contributes scaled welfare \(\sum_e w_ez_e\), but may leave supply unused.

Process the vertices one at a time. When processing \(v\), add \(v\) to bundles of consumers who do not yet hold it, choosing consumers of total scaled mass \(1-a_v\). Such a choice exists: their available mass is \(\tau-a_v\), and
\[ \tau-a_v\ge1-a_v \]
because \(\tau\ge1\).

Processing other vertices does not change whether a bundle contains \(v\). Hence, immediately before its own processing, the consumption of \(v\) is still \(a_v\). Afterward it is exactly \(1\), and subsequent steps preserve that equality.

This construction can be made finite explicitly. Maintain a partition into intervals on which both the type and bundle are constant. To fill vertex \(v\), traverse intervals whose bundles omit \(v\), adding \(v\) to whole intervals until the required mass is reached, and split the final interval if necessary. Each vertex introduces at most one new interval boundary. Starting with at most \(2\tau\) intervals, the construction ends with at most \(2\tau+m\) intervals.

Divide all interval lengths by \(\tau\), and aggregate intervals having the same type and bundle to obtain \(x_{e,S}\). Each type then has mass \(1/\tau\), and each vertex is supplied in mass exactly \(1/\tau\).

All initially successful consumers remain successful because bundles only grow. Since every \(w_e\ge0\), adding items cannot decrease welfare. The resulting allocation therefore has welfare at least
\[ \frac1\tau\sum_{e\in E}w_ez_e. \]

Together with the upper bound in Section 2, this proves equality of the two optimal values. A linear-program optimum exists because its feasible region is nonempty and compact.

4. Polynomial-time decision

The answer to the decision problem is therefore “yes” exactly when the following rational linear system is feasible:
\[ 0\le z_e\le1\quad(e\in E),\qquad \sum_{e\ni v}z_e\le1\quad(v\in V),\qquad \sum_{e\in E}w_ez_e\ge\tau B. \]

It has \(\tau\) variables and \(m+2\tau+1\) inequalities. Its incidence coefficients are determined directly by the explicitly listed edges, with \(k\tau\) nonzero incidence entries. Its other coefficients are the given rational weights and \(\tau B\), whose binary encoding length is polynomial in the input length.

The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear-program feasibility therefore applies and gives running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

The discrete hardness result does not transfer: here each type’s mass can be divided, and the exact supply constraints can be satisfied by enlarging bundles.
