The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) of types \(t=(P_t,\rho_1(t),\ldots,\rho_m(t))\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), and a threshold \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), compute a feasible assignment \(x_{t,s}\) (including the outside option) by a mass version of \(k\)-TA. Each type’s mass proposes in preference order; each school processes proposals by priority score and may replace held mass only when the proposer’s score exceeds the held mass’s score by more than \(\kappa\), splitting mass as needed and using a fixed rule for ties. Does the resulting assignment guarantee that no positive mass assigned below a preferred school faces either an unfilled seat there or assigned mass whose priority score is lower by more than \(\kappa\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\geq3\) schools \(S\), with positive binary-rational capacities \(q_s\) summing to \(1\).
Each type \(t=(P_t,\rho_1(t),\ldots,\rho_m(t))\) has strict preferences \(P_t\) over \(S\cup\{\emptyset\}\) and binary-rational priority scores \(\rho_s(t)\in[0,1]\), with larger scores meaning higher priority. The outside option \(\emptyset\) has unlimited capacity; only schools preferred to it are acceptable. The input threshold is binary-rational \(\kappa\in[0,1]\).
Mass is divisible. A feasible assignment has \(x_{t,a}\geq0\), \(\sum_{a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\leq q_s\); unacceptable schools receive no mass. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, and express running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Define mass Threshold Acceptance in synchronous rounds, starting with all mass unmatched. Each unmatched portion proposes to its favorite acceptable school not previously proposed to by that portion. Split portions inherit proposal histories; rejected or displaced mass can propose again only in the next round. Exhausted portions take \(\emptyset\).
In each round, each school first fills vacant capacity from proposals in decreasing score order. Break equal-score ties by support-list order, including when selecting lowest-priority held mass.
Once full, compare the highest-score remaining proposer \(t\) with the lowest-score held type \(u\). If \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(u)>\kappa\), transfer the minimum of their available masses, rejecting the displaced portion, and repeat. Otherwise reject all remaining proposals to that school. Stop when no proposals remain, and finalize held mass.
An assignment is free of very justified envy if there are no \(t,a,s\) with \(x_{t,a}>0\) and \(sP_t a\) such that either \(\sum_u x_{u,s}<q_s\), or some \(u\) satisfies \(x_{u,s}>0\) and \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(u)>\kappa\).

Does mass Threshold Acceptance always terminate with a feasible assignment free of very justified envy? Is exact computation of its terminal assignment polynomial-time in \(m,\tau,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite agents with strict priorities, Threshold Acceptance eliminates very justified envy for every integer threshold \(k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\), using upward-increasing priority ranks and the strict displacement comparison. [Hamdan](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07682v2).
No result settling this exact finite-support divisible-mass procedure was located in the literature search.

Result

Yes. The specified procedure always terminates with a feasible assignment free of very justified envy. Its terminal assignment can be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The polynomial-time algorithm need not simulate every round. It skips stretches during which schools’ acceptance rules remain fixed, using exact counting in a finite directed graph.

1. Scaling and proposal histories

For each type \(t\), write its acceptable schools in preference order as \(s_{t,1},\ldots,s_{t,\ell_t}\), where \(\ell_t\leq m\).

Every portion of type \(t\) proposes along a prefix of this list. Consequently:

  • An unmatched portion preparing its next proposal is described by a state \((t,j)\), meaning that it will propose to \(s_{t,j}\).
  • Every portion of type \(t\) held at \(s_{t,j}\) has the same proposal history. If displaced, it next proposes to \(s_{t,j+1}\), or takes the outside option if \(j=\ell_t\).

Thus histories can be aggregated into at most \(N:=m\tau\) proposal states. Let \(b_{t,j}\) denote the mass preparing proposal \((t,j)\), and let \(h_{t,s}\) denote held mass.

Choose a common denominator \(D\) for all masses and capacities, for example the product of their encoded denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Scale masses by \(D\), defining \(M_t:=D\mu_t\) and \(Q_s:=Dq_s\). These are positive integers, and both totals equal \(D\).

All subsequent scaled masses remain integers: the procedure uses only additions, subtractions, and transfers of the minimum of two available masses. We use scaled masses until converting the final assignment back by division by \(D\).

2. Termination and feasibility

For the termination proof only, regard the initial mass as \(D\) labeled atoms, each of unscaled mass \(1/D\). Every integer transfer can be implemented by transferring the corresponding number of atoms. Atoms of the same type have identical preferences and scores, so their labels do not affect aggregated outcomes.

Each atom proposes to each acceptable school at most once. The total number of atom-proposals is therefore at most \(mD\).

Every nonempty round contains at least one atom-proposal. It follows that there are at most \(mD\) nonempty rounds. This establishes termination, although this bound alone is not polynomial in the input length.

Each individual round also finishes after finitely many operations. There are at most \(\tau\) proposing types at a school. Each successful displacement exhausts either the current proposer mass or the selected held mass. Proposals are processed in decreasing score order, and each displacement replaces held mass by strictly higher-score mass because \(\kappa\geq0\). Thus mass accepted earlier in that round cannot be displaced by a later, lower-score proposer. Only finitely many proposer and incumbent masses can be exhausted.

Feasibility follows from the following invariants:

  • Every atom is held at one school, unmatched, or assigned outside.
  • Vacancy filling never exceeds capacity.
  • A displacement exchanges equal masses and preserves school occupancy.
  • Only acceptable schools receive proposals.

At termination, all mass is held or outside. These invariants give precisely the required assignment constraints.

3. Freedom from very justified envy

Once a school becomes full, it remains full: its subsequent changes consist solely of equal-mass displacements.

For a full school \(s\), let its minimum held score be \(c_s:=\min\{\rho_s(u):h_{u,s}>0\}\). This minimum never decreases. Indeed, a displacement removes mass having the minimum score and replaces it by mass whose score exceeds that minimum by more than \(\kappa\). A partial removal leaves the minimum unchanged; a complete removal can only increase it.

Consider any portion of type \(t\) rejected from \(s\).

Rejection of a proposal. Such rejection occurs when the school is full and its highest-score remaining proposer fails the displacement comparison. If the minimum held score at that moment is \(c\), every rejected proposer \(t\) satisfies \(\rho_s(t)\leq c+\kappa\). Because the minimum held score never subsequently decreases, every terminally held type \(u\) satisfies \(\rho_s(u)\geq c\). Hence \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(u)\leq\kappa\).

Displacement of held mass. The displaced type \(t\) has the minimum held score at the moment of displacement. All mass retained at the school has score at least \(\rho_s(t)\), and the replacing mass has strictly higher score. Monotonicity therefore gives \(\rho_s(u)\geq\rho_s(t)\) for every terminally held type \(u\). Again, \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(u)\leq\kappa\).

Now suppose terminal mass satisfies \(x_{t,a}>0\) and \(sP_ta\). Feasibility implies that \(a\) is acceptable or is the outside option, so \(s\) is acceptable. Any atom contributing to \(x_{t,a}\) must previously have proposed to \(s\) and subsequently been rejected or displaced there.

The school was full at that rejection and remains full terminally. The preceding argument also bounds the atom’s score advantage over every terminally held type by \(\kappa\). Neither condition for very justified envy can therefore hold.

This proves freedom from very justified envy. The monotonic-minimum argument parallels [Hamdan’s finite-agent proof, Proposition 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.07682v2); the argument above explicitly covers partial displacement.

4. Only polynomially many changes of acceptance rules occur

At a round boundary, classify each school as either unfilled or full. For a full school \(s\), let \(u_s\) be the type selected as its lowest-priority held type under the specified tie rule.

Call a round an event round if some school becomes full, or if the selected lowest held type at an already full school changes during that round.

Each school becomes full at most once. Afterward, its selected lowest held type remains unchanged until all held mass of that type is removed:

  • New accepted mass has strictly higher score than the current minimum.
  • Thus it cannot change the selected minimum while that incumbent type remains.
  • The fixed tie rule also cannot change its selection while that type remains.

Moreover, a type removed completely when it is the selected minimum can never reenter that school. If its score is \(r\), every later minimum score is at least \(r\); accepting that type would require \(r-c_s>\kappa\), which is impossible because \(c_s\geq r\) and \(\kappa\geq0\).

There are at most \(\tau-1\) changes of the selected minimum after a school first fills. Including its filling event, each school contributes at most \(\tau\) events. Thus there are at most \(m\tau=N\) event rounds.

It remains to compute stretches between event rounds efficiently.

5. Fixed acceptance rules give deterministic mass routing

Fix a round boundary and its school classifications. For each school, define a positive resource \(v_s\):

  • If \(s\) is unfilled, set \(v_s:=Q_s-\sum_t h_{t,s}\), its vacancy.
  • If \(s\) is full, set \(v_s:=h_{u_s,s}\), its held mass of the selected minimum type.

Temporarily freeze these classifications and selected minimum types. Under the frozen rules, a proposal state \((t,j)\), with \(s=s_{t,j}\), behaves as follows.

| School and comparison | Held-mass update | Next-round proposal |
|---|---|---|
| \(s\) unfilled | Add the proposal mass to \(h_{t,s}\) | None |
| \(s\) full and \(\rho_s(t)-\rho_s(u_s)>\kappa\) | Add the proposal mass to \(h_{t,s}\), remove equal mass from \(h_{u_s,s}\) | The displaced type \(u_s\)’s next acceptable school |
| \(s\) full and the comparison fails | No held-mass change | Type \(t\)’s next acceptable school |

Whenever the indicated next school does not exist, the corresponding mass goes outside.

Each proposal state therefore sends its mass to at most one next-round proposal state. This defines a directed graph with outdegree at most one, with a sink representing disappearance from the proposal process. In particular, within these frozen rules there is no branching.

A proposal consumes school \(s\)’s resource exactly when it is accepted there: every proposal to a frozen unfilled school consumes vacancy, and every threshold-eligible proposal to a frozen full school consumes mass of \(u_s\).

Let \(f\) be the graph’s successor function, with \(f(\bot)=\bot\). Starting with proposal vector \(b\), define the total visits to state \(p\) over \(K\) frozen rounds by

\(W_p(K):=\sum_v b_v\,\#\{i:0\leq i<K,\ f^i(v)=p\}\).

Let \(\mathcal A_s\) be the states whose proposals consume resource at \(s\). The cumulative hypothetical resource consumption is

\(C_s(K):=\sum_{p\in\mathcal A_s}W_p(K)\).

Each \(C_s(K)\) is nondecreasing in \(K\).

Safe skipping lemma

Claim. If \(C_s(K)<v_s\) for every school \(s\), the first \(K\) frozen rounds coincide exactly with the specified procedure.

Proof. Use induction over the rounds.

At an unfilled school, cumulative consumption below its vacancy means that the school remains unfilled throughout every round considered. Every proposal is accepted.

At a full school, cumulative consumption below \(h_{u_s,s}\) means that the selected minimum type remains present throughout every round considered. Its mass cannot be replenished, because its own score fails the displacement comparison against itself. Therefore the selected minimum remains \(u_s\).

Proposers exceeding its score by more than \(\kappa\) precede all failing proposers in decreasing score order. The school consequently accepts exactly the eligible proposals and rejects exactly the others, as specified by the frozen rules.

The held-mass updates and next-round proposals agree at every school. Synchronous timing is preserved because every graph edge represents a proposal in the next round. This completes the induction. \(\square\)

The resulting state can be reconstructed from the visit counts: add the held-mass and outside-mass updates in the table, multiplied by \(W_p(K)\), and route each initial \(b_v\) to \(f^K(v)\) if it has not reached the sink.

6. Computing visit counts and finding the next event

Exact counting along graph paths

From any state \(v\), its successor sequence reaches either the sink or a repeated state after at most \(N\) distinct proposal states.

For a path reaching the sink, each state is visited once or not at all during the first \(K\) rounds.

Otherwise, write its distinct states as \(p_0,\ldots,p_{a+c-1}\), where \(a\) is the transient length and \(c\) is the cycle length. For a transient index \(i<a\), the visit count is \(1\) if \(i<K\), and \(0\) otherwise.

For a cycle index \(a\leq i<a+c\), the visit count is zero when \(K\leq i\); otherwise it is

\(1+\left\lfloor (K-1-i)/c\right\rfloor\).

These formulas also determine the state after \(K\) transitions. Enumerating the path from each possible starting state computes all \(W_p(K)\), all \(C_s(K)\), and the next proposal vector using \(O(N^2)\) integer operations. Crucially, this cost depends polynomially on the bit length of \(K\), rather than its value.

Locating and processing events

Set \(H:=mD+1\). Its binary length is \(O(L+\log m)\).

At the current round boundary, construct the frozen routing graph and evaluate all \(C_s(H)\).

If \(C_s(H)<v_s\) for every school, the safe skipping lemma permits advancing \(H\) rounds exactly. The resulting proposal vector must be zero. Otherwise, because zero proposals cannot generate new proposals, all these \(H\) rounds would have been nonempty, contradicting the bound of \(mD\) nonempty rounds. Reconstruct the terminal assignment and stop.

Otherwise, binary search for the smallest positive integer \(r\leq H\) satisfying \(C_s(r)\geq v_s\) for at least one school. The predicate is monotone.

Minimality gives \(C_s(r-1)<v_s\) for every school. Skip those \(r-1\) rounds exactly, then execute one ordinary round of the specified procedure.

That ordinary round is an event round. To verify this:

  • For an unfilled school witnessing the inequality, round-\(r\) proposals suffice to fill its remaining vacancy.
  • For a full school witnessing it, round-\(r\) eligible proposals suffice to remove its remaining mass of \(u_s\). Until that removal occurs, \(u_s\) remains the minimum and those eligible proposals pass the comparison. Thus the selected minimum changes.

Rebuild the graph and repeat. Every iteration either terminates or processes an event round. Section 4 bounds the latter iterations by \(N\).

This proves that the algorithm computes exactly the terminal assignment of the specified synchronous procedure.

7. Bit complexity

Let \(B:=L+\lceil\log_2(N+2)\rceil\).

All actual scaled masses are at most \(D\). During a hypothetical evaluation with \(K\leq H\), total proposal mass at any step is at most \(D\), since each state sends mass to at most one successor. Hence total weighted visits are at most \(HD\). All visit counts, resource consumptions, and reconstruction intermediates consequently have \(O(B)\) bits.

Using elementary integer arithmetic:

  • One frozen-graph evaluation takes \(O(N^2B^2)\) bit operations.
  • Binary search uses \(O(B)\) evaluations.
  • There are at most \(N\) event iterations and one final terminating iteration.
  • Constructing graphs, comparing rational scores, and executing ordinary event rounds also fit within this bound.

A conservative overall running-time bound is therefore

\(O\!\left((m\tau)^3\bigl(L+\log_2(m\tau+2)\bigr)^3\right)\)

bit operations. The final integer masses are divided by \(D\); their exact rational encodings have polynomial length.

Why round-by-round simulation is insufficient

Exponential round counts can occur even with three schools and three types.

Let the schools be \(A,B,C\), with capacities \(1/4,1/4,1/2\). For an integer \(b\geq3\), put \(\varepsilon:=2^{-b}\). Let the types have:

| Type | Mass | Acceptable schools, in preference order |
|---|---:|---|
| \(u\) | \(1/4+\varepsilon\) | \(A,B\) |
| \(v\) | \(1/4\) | \(B,A\) |
| \(w\) | \(1/2-\varepsilon\) | \(C\) |

Take \(\kappa=1/2\). At \(A\), give \(v\) score \(1\) and \(u\) score \(0\); at \(B\), give \(u\) score \(1\) and \(v\) score \(0\). Other scores are arbitrary.

Round one leaves \(\varepsilon\) of \(u\) unmatched. In round two it displaces \(\varepsilon\) of \(v\) at \(B\); in round three that displaced mass displaces \(\varepsilon\) of \(u\) at \(A\). This alternation continues.

Writing \(M:=(1/4)/\varepsilon=2^{b-2}\), the last original \(v\)-mass leaves \(B\) in round \(2M\), and the last original \(u\)-mass leaves \(A\) in round \(2M+1\). The resulting \(\varepsilon\) of \(u\) is rejected from \(B\) in round \(2M+2\), because \(B\) now holds only \(u\).

Thus literal simulation takes \(2^{b-1}+2\) nonempty rounds on inputs of length \(O(b)\). The graph-counting algorithm skips this alternating stretch exactly.
