The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite applicant cohorts \(\mathcal H\) and programs \(\mathcal D\), rational cohort masses \(\mu_h\) and program capacities \(\kappa_d\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and an input bound \(q\), suppose each cohort has an unknown strict ranking \(\succ_h\) of programs. In each query, an attacker chooses a strict priority order over cohorts for every program; a mass deferred-acceptance procedure returns the aggregate assignment matrix \(x\), with each cohort retaining its highest-ranked offers up to mass \(\mu_h\) and rejected mass continuing to its next proposal. Decide whether an adaptive strategy can identify every \(\succ_h\) exactly for every hidden preference profile within \(q\) queries, and determine the computational complexity in \(|\mathcal H|\), \(|\mathcal D|\), and the bit length of the rational inputs.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal H\) be labeled applicant cohorts and \(\mathcal D\) labeled programs, with \(\tau=|\mathcal H|\geq1\) and \(m=|\mathcal D|\geq1\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Masses \(\mu_h>0\) and capacities \(\kappa_d>0\) satisfy \(\sum_h\mu_h=\sum_d\kappa_d=1\). Each cohort has one unknown complete strict ranking \(\succ_h\) of all programs, fixed across queries; all pairs are acceptable.
Each query specifies a complete strict priority order over cohorts for every program. Programs propose divisible mass. Starting with holdings \(x=0\), repeatedly select the first program in a fixed public order with free capacity and an eligible cohort. It offers \(\min\{\kappa_d-\sum_g x_{gd},\mu_h-x_{hd}\}\) to its highest-priority eligible cohort \(h\). A cohort retains its highest-ranked program offers, including previous holdings, up to \(\mu_h\), splitting an offer if necessary and rejecting the rest. A pair is eligible exactly when it has never suffered rejection and \(x_{hd}<\mu_h\). Rejected mass becomes free program capacity; continue until no offer is possible.
Queries restart from zero holdings. The attacker observes only the exact final aggregate matrix \(x\), and may choose subsequent priorities from previous queries and matrices. An identifying strategy must terminate with exactly one hidden ranking profile consistent with its transcript, for every possible hidden profile. The input is the masses, capacities, and a nonnegative integer budget \(q\) encoded in binary; \(L\) is their total bit length.

Is deciding whether such an adaptive strategy exists using at most \(q\) queries in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), including any dependence on \(q\).

For discrete balanced markets with \(n\) agents per side, strict complete rankings, and the attacker’s side proposing, an adaptive attack recovers all honest rankings within \(n^2\) matching queries ([discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13015v1)); this does not determine the complexity of deciding sufficiency of an arbitrary query budget.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already with two programs and a fixed budget of two queries. This holds even when every mass and capacity is dyadic, meaning its denominator is a power of two.

The proof below handles adaptive strategies and uses only the final aggregate matrices.

1. Basic properties of the query mechanism

Termination and balance

Let \(B\) be the product of the denominators of all masses and capacities. Scale every quantity by \(B\). All allocations and offers are then integers.

For a fixed hidden profile, assign weight \(m\) to a cohort’s favorite program, weight \(m-1\) to its second favorite, and so forth. Define the potential

\(\displaystyle \Phi=\sum_{h,d}w_{hd}(Bx_{hd}).\)

It satisfies \(0\le\Phi\le mB\).

Consider an offer. Let \(\alpha\) be the increase in the receiving cohort’s holdings of the offering program, in scaled units.

  • If \(\alpha>0\), the retained units either fill previously unfilled cohort mass or replace units from strictly worse programs. If \(r\le\alpha\) units are replaced, the potential increases by at least \((\alpha-r)+r=\alpha\ge1\).
  • If \(\alpha=0\), the allocation remains unchanged and the offered mass is entirely rejected. The offered pair was eligible, so this creates a previously absent rejection flag.

Thus there are at most \(mB\) allocation-changing offers and at most \(m\tau\) other offers. The mechanism terminates after at most \(mB+m\tau\) offers.

Once a cohort holds its full mass, it remains full. A rejection occurs only when the receiving cohort becomes full. Consequently, a cohort deficient at termination has never rejected any pair.

If total assigned mass were less than one, some program would have free capacity and some cohort would be deficient. Their pair would be eligible, contradicting termination. Therefore every final matrix satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_d x_{hd}=\mu_h,\qquad \sum_h x_{hd}=\kappa_d.\)

In particular, \(m=1\) is always a yes-instance, whereas \(\tau=1\) and \(m\ge2\) is always a no-instance: the sole observed row is the capacity vector, independently of its ranking.

Common priorities with two programs

Write the programs as \(a,b\). Suppose both use the same cohort priority order.

A cohort never rejects its favorite program: an offer from that program cannot increase its holdings there above \(\mu_h\). Its holdings of its favorite therefore never decrease.

Consider cohort \(h\), and let

\(\displaystyle r_d=\kappa_d-\sum_{g\text{ preceding }h}x_{gd}\)

be the capacity of program \(d\) remaining after preceding cohorts’ final allocations.

If \(d\) is \(h\)’s favorite and \(x_{hd}<\mu_h\), then \(h\) was eligible for \(d\) throughout the query. Program \(d\) could therefore never offer to a later cohort. Column balance gives \(r_d=x_{hd}\). If instead \(x_{hd}=\mu_h\), feasibility gives \(r_d\ge\mu_h\). Hence

\(\displaystyle x_{hd}=\min\{\mu_h,r_d\}\)

for the favorite program. Row balance determines the other entry. Thus common priorities implement allocation in cohort priority order, with each cohort choosing its preferred remaining capacity first.

Suppose both residual capacities are positive and

\(\displaystyle 0<\mu_h<r_a+r_b.\)

The two possible rankings give respectively

\(\displaystyle x_{ha}=\min\{\mu_h,r_a\}\quad\text{and}\quad x_{ha}=\max\{0,\mu_h-r_b\}.\)

These values differ. If \(\mu_h\le r_b\), the second is zero and the first is positive. Otherwise, both \(\mu_h\) and \(r_a\) strictly exceed \(\mu_h-r_b\).

Accordingly, under these hypotheses the final matrix identifies \(h\)’s ranking.

2. A necessary condition for adaptive identification

Let \(a\) have capacity \(c\), and consider the hidden profile \(P_0\) in which every cohort prefers \(a\) to \(b\).

Every offer from \(a\) is fully retained. Its holdings never decrease. If its priority order is \(h_1,\ldots,h_\tau\), its final allocation is therefore

\(\displaystyle x_{h_i a}=\min\!\left\{\mu_{h_i},\max\!\left\{0,c-\sum_{\ell<i}\mu_{h_\ell}\right\}\right\}.\)

The cohorts receiving positive mass from \(a\) form a priority prefix. The total mass preceding the last cohort of this prefix is strictly less than \(c\).

Now follow any adaptive strategy on \(P_0\). Every cohort must receive positive mass from \(a\) in at least one query along this path.

To prove this, suppose \(h\) never does. Under \(P_0\), it never receives an offer from \(a\), since such an offer would be fully retained. Change only \(h\)’s ranking to \(b\succ_h a\). In each query on the original path, \(h\) receives offers only from \(b\), so its retention behavior is unchanged. Induction over offer steps gives the same final matrix. Induction over queries then gives the same adaptive choices and the same complete transcript.

The strategy cannot distinguish these two profiles, contradicting identification.

3. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the classical \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete PARTITION problem on positive binary integers. Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is stated, for example, in [Theorem 6.4 of the hardness textbook draft](https://hardness.mit.edu/drafts/2022-09-21.pdf).

Let the integers be \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), with

\(\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i.\)

We may assume \(A\) is even and \(A\ge2\): doubling every integer preserves the answer and ensures these conditions.

Choose a power of two \(T\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle 4A\le T<8A.\)

Create ordinary cohorts \(h_1,\ldots,h_k\) and two additional cohorts \(u,v\), with masses

\(\displaystyle \mu_{h_i}=\frac{a_i}{T},\qquad \mu_u=\mu_v=\beta:=\frac{T-A}{2T}.\)

Create two programs with capacities

\(\displaystyle \kappa_a=c:=\frac{A+1}{2T},\qquad \kappa_b=1-c,\)

and set \(q=2\).

All masses and capacities are positive dyadic rationals, and both totals equal one. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle c<\frac12,\qquad \beta>c,\)

because \(T\ge4A>2A+1\).

The support size is exactly \(\tau=k+2\). Every rational uses \(O(\log A)\) bits, so the output length is \(O(k\log A)\), and the construction takes polynomial time.

A successful strategy implies a partition

Suppose an identifying strategy uses at most two queries. Follow its path on \(P_0\).

By Section 2, both \(u\) and \(v\) must receive positive mass from \(a\) somewhere on this path. A single query cannot give positive mass from \(a\) to both: the first such cohort encountered has mass \(\beta>c\), so it absorbs all remaining capacity of \(a\).

The path must therefore contain two queries, each covering a different additional cohort. In each query, that cohort is last in the positive-\(a\) prefix. All other cohorts in that prefix are ordinary cohorts, whose total mass is strictly less than \(c\).

Every ordinary cohort belongs to at least one of these prefixes. Assign each ordinary cohort to one query covering it, obtaining a partition \(I_1,I_2\) of the ordinary indices. For each part,

\(\displaystyle \frac1T\sum_{i\in I_j}a_i<c=\frac{A+1}{2T}.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in I_j}a_i<\frac{A+1}{2}=\frac A2+\frac12.\)

The sum is an integer, so it is at most \(A/2\). The two sums total \(A\); hence both equal \(A/2\). This solves the PARTITION instance.

This argument applies to arbitrary priorities and fully adaptive strategies.

A partition gives two identifying queries

Conversely, suppose \(I_1,I_2\) partition the ordinary indices and each integer sum equals \(A/2\).

For the first query, give both programs the common priority order consisting of:

  • the cohorts indexed by \(I_1\);
  • cohort \(u\);
  • all remaining cohorts.

For the second query, use the analogous order with \(I_2\) followed by \(v\).

Consider any cohort in the designated initial group of either query. The total mass \(s\) preceding it satisfies

\(\displaystyle s\le\frac{A}{2T}<c.\)

Therefore its residual capacities satisfy

\(\displaystyle r_a\ge c-s>0,\qquad r_b\ge1-c-s>0.\)

The other additional cohort appears later and has positive mass \(\beta\). Thus

\(\displaystyle \mu_h<1-s=r_a+r_b.\)

The common-priority result from Section 1 identifies the ranking of every cohort in the designated group, using only the final matrix.

The first query identifies all cohorts in \(I_1\) and \(u\); the second identifies all cohorts in \(I_2\) and \(v\). Together they identify the entire hidden profile, for every possible profile. These queries are nonadaptive.

We have proved the equivalence

\(\displaystyle \text{PARTITION is yes}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{identification within two queries is possible}.\)

Hence the requested decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

4. Exact decision algorithm and running time

For completeness, an exhaustive exact algorithm gives an explicit upper bound.

Let

\(\displaystyle N=(m!)^\tau,\qquad Q=(\tau!)^m\)

be the numbers of hidden profiles and possible queries. Enumerate both, and precompute every final matrix.

The termination bound above gives a simulation time of

\(\displaystyle (mB+m\tau)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L),\)

where \(B\le2^L\). Integer scaling permits exact arithmetic with \(O(L)\)-bit allocation entries.

For a candidate set \(S\) of profiles, let \(D(S)\) be the minimum worst-case number of additional queries required to identify its member, or \(\infty\) if identification is impossible. Set \(D(S)=0\) when \(|S|\le1\). For larger sets, each query \(p\) partitions \(S\) into cells \(\mathcal P_p(S)\) having identical observed matrices. Then

\(\displaystyle D(S)=1+\min_{p:\,|\mathcal P_p(S)|\ge2}\ \max_{C\in\mathcal P_p(S)}D(C),\)

with value \(\infty\) if no finite choice exists.

Every cell of a splitting query is a proper subset. Evaluating states by increasing cardinality therefore computes this recurrence exactly. The recurrence expresses adaptive identification: the first query is chosen at the current candidate set, and the continuation depends on its observed cell.

A conservative bit-operation bound, including subset indexing, is

\(\displaystyle \left[QN(m2^L+m\tau)+2^NQN^2\right]\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)+O(\log(q+2)).\)

Finally, compare the value for the full profile set with \(q\). No iteration proportional to the binary-encoded budget is required. Whenever identification is possible, its optimal depth is at most \(\min\{N-1,Q\}\): informative queries strictly shrink the candidate set, and asking every possible query also suffices.

The hardness with fixed \(m=2\) and \(q=2\) rules out a running time \(f(m,q)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
