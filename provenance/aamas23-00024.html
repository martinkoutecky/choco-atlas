The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many valuation types \(\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{<0}^{2}\), masses \(\mu\), and rational per-agent supplies \((\bar a,\bar b)\), find a finite-support mass allocation \(\lambda_{\theta,a,b}\) over integral bundles \((a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{2}\) satisfying \(\sum_{a,b}\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\mu_{\theta}\), \(\sum_{\theta,a,b}a\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\bar a\), and \(\sum_{\theta,a,b}b\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\bar b\), such that every positive-mass cohort is EF1 and the allocation is \(fPO\) against all supply-preserving fractional reallocations.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(\Theta\subseteq\mathbb Q_{<0}^{2}\) of \(\tau\) valuation types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), where \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=2\) chore types; utility is additive, \(u_\theta(a,b)=\theta_Aa+\theta_Bb\), and per-agent supplies \((\bar a,\bar b)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{2}\) are encoded in binary.
An allocation has finitely many nonzero binary-rational masses \(\lambda_{\theta,a,b}\ge0\), indexed by integral bundles \((a,b)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{2}\), satisfying \(\sum_{a,b}\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_{\theta,a,b}a\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\bar a\), and \(\sum_{\theta,a,b}b\lambda_{\theta,a,b}=\bar b\). A positive-mass cohort consists of the agents of type \(\theta\) receiving bundle \((a,b)\).
EF1 is required between every ordered pair of positive-mass cohorts, including cohorts of the same type: an empty own bundle satisfies it automatically; otherwise, deleting one chore from the envious cohort's own bundle must make its utility at least its utility for the other cohort's bundle.
Fractional Pareto optimality (fPO) excludes any supply-preserving reassignment of nonnegative real bundles to the agents that weakly improves utility almost everywhere and strictly improves utility on a set of positive mass; cohorts may be subdivided in such a reassignment.
Is finding such an EF1 and fPO allocation, or determining that none exists, solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m=2\), \(\tau\), and the total binary input length \(L\).
For finitely listed agents and explicitly listed chores of two types, an EF1 and fPO allocation always exists and is computable in polynomial time ([Aziz et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.00879v2)); this extends to weighted EF1 and fPO for finite agents ([Garg et al.](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0310.pdf)).
No result settling exactly the mass-allocation problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, consistent with a distribution over types and per-agent supplies; required nonempty support.
  • Allowed nonnegative rational supplies, including zero, because the discrete setting permits at most two chore types.
  • Required EF1 between all positive-mass cohorts, including those sharing a valuation type, with deletion from the cohort's own bundle and automatic satisfaction for empty own bundles.
  • Defined continuous Pareto improvement agentwise almost everywhere, with strict improvement on positive mass, and allowed fractional reassignment to subdivide cohorts.
  • Required rational output masses and binary encoding of bundle counts to make the computational representation explicit.
  • Used m for the number of chore types, fixed at two; there is no finite total item count in this mass model.
  • Included determining nonexistence because the continuous question does not supply an existence guarantee.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable for \(m=2\), and every instance admits an EF1 and fPO allocation.

Here, “binary-rational” means a rational encoded by binary numerator and denominator. The algorithm below outputs at most \(2\tau+2\) positive-mass cohorts, with total encoding length \(O(\tau L)\). Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, its running time is

\(O\!\left((\tau+L)\log(\tau+1)L^2\right).\)

The proof is direct and does not expand the society into individual agents.

1. A family of allocations indexed by a population cut

Write \(a=\bar a\), \(b=\bar b\), and replace negative utilities by positive disutilities:

\(d_{iA}=-\theta_{iA}>0,\qquad d_{iB}=-\theta_{iB}>0,\qquad r_i=d_{iA}/d_{iB}.\)

Sort the supported types so that \(r_1\leq\cdots\leq r_\tau\), breaking ties arbitrarily. Let

\(s_0=0,\qquad s_i=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mu_j.\)

First suppose \(a,b>0\). For a cut \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), put

\(\mu_i^A=\min\{\mu_i,\max\{0,\alpha-s_{i-1}\}\},\qquad \mu_i^B=\mu_i-\mu_i^A.\)

Thus the first \(\alpha\) units of population mass receive only A chores, and the remaining \(1-\alpha\) receive only B chores. At most one type is split between these parts.

Define

\(q_A=a/\alpha,\qquad q_B=b/(1-\alpha),\)

\(k_A=\lfloor q_A\rfloor,\qquad k_B=\lfloor q_B\rfloor,\)

\(\delta_A=q_A-k_A,\qquad \delta_B=q_B-k_B.\)

For each type \(i\), assign the following masses:

| Bundle | Mass |
|---|---|
| \((k_A,0)\) | \(\mu_i^A(1-\delta_A)\) |
| \((k_A+1,0)\) | \(\mu_i^A\delta_A\) |
| \((0,k_B)\) | \(\mu_i^B(1-\delta_B)\) |
| \((0,k_B+1)\) | \(\mu_i^B\delta_B\) |

Discard zero masses and combine identical bundles.

Every type receives its prescribed mass. Moreover, the total A supply is

\(\sum_i\mu_i^A(k_A+\delta_A)=\alpha q_A=a,\)

and similarly the total B supply is \(b\). Therefore this is a feasible allocation, denoted \(X(\alpha)\).

2. Every cut allocation is fPO

Let

\(r_A(\alpha)=\max\{r_i:\mu_i^A>0\},\qquad r_B(\alpha)=\min\{r_i:\mu_i^B>0\}.\)

Sorting implies \(r_A(\alpha)\leq r_B(\alpha)\). Choose any \(r\) in this interval and define the positive linear functional

\(p(x,y)=rx+y.\)

For type \(i\), put

\(\kappa_i=\min\{d_{iA}/r,d_{iB}\}>0.\)

Every nonnegative real bundle \(z\) satisfies

\(d_i(z)\geq\kappa_i p(z).\)

Every bundle allocated to type \(i\) in \(X(\alpha)\) satisfies equality:

  • If it contains only A chores, then \(r_i\leq r_A(\alpha)\leq r\), so \(\kappa_i=d_{iA}/r\).
  • If it contains only B chores, then \(r_i\geq r_B(\alpha)\geq r\), so \(\kappa_i=d_{iB}\).
  • The equality also holds for an empty bundle.

Suppose a supply-preserving real reassignment gives each agent a bundle \(z\), weakly decreasing disutility almost everywhere. If its original bundle was \(x\), then

\(\kappa_i p(z)\leq d_i(z)\leq d_i(x)=\kappa_i p(x),\)

hence \(p(z)\leq p(x)\). Wherever utility strictly improves, the last inequality is strict.

Consequently, strict improvement on a set of positive mass implies a strict decrease in the integral of \(p\). Indeed, \(p(x)-p(z)\) is nonnegative almost everywhere and positive on a set of positive mass. But supply preservation fixes that integral at \(ra+b\), a contradiction.

Thus \(X(\alpha)\) is fPO. This argument allows arbitrary cohort subdivision.

3. An exact EF1 test

Define

\(c_A=\lceil q_A\rceil-1,\qquad c_B=\lceil q_B\rceil-1.\)

Since \(a,b>0\), both \(c_A,c_B\) are nonnegative. Also,

\(c_A\leq k_A,\qquad c_B\leq k_B.\)

Within the A-only part, bundle sizes differ by at most one. Deleting one A chore from any nonempty own bundle leaves at most \(k_A\) chores, while every other A-only bundle contains at least \(k_A\). Therefore every ordered comparison within this part satisfies EF1. The same holds within the B-only part.

For comparisons across the parts, the largest remaining A-only bundle after deletion contains exactly \(c_A\) chores, while the smallest B-only bundle contains \(k_B\). Every type with positive A mass receives the same bundle-size support, so the worst A-to-B comparison occurs at ratio \(r_A(\alpha)\). Thus A-to-B EF1 holds exactly when

\(r_A(\alpha)c_A\leq k_B.\)

Similarly, B-to-A EF1 holds exactly when

\(c_B\leq r_B(\alpha)k_A.\)

These tests include comparisons between cohorts of the same type.

Call the cut A-bad if the first inequality fails, B-bad if the second fails, and fair if neither fails. A cut cannot be both A-bad and B-bad: otherwise,

\(r_Ak_A\geq r_Ac_A>k_B\geq c_B>r_Bk_A,\)

contradicting \(r_A\leq r_B\).

Hence every cut has exactly one of these three statuses.

4. A transition from A-bad to B-bad contains a fair cut

The quantities in the EF1 test are locally constant except at cuts of the forms

\(s_i,\qquad a/k,\qquad 1-b/\ell,\)

where \(k,\ell\) are positive integers.

We now check the status at such a breakpoint \(z\in(0,1)\). Superscripts \({}^-\) and \({}^+\) denote values immediately to its left and right.

Because \(q_A=a/\alpha\) decreases with \(\alpha\),

\(c_A(z)=c_A^+,\qquad k_A(z)=k_A^-.\)

Because \(q_B=b/(1-\alpha)\) increases with \(\alpha\),

\(k_B(z)=k_B^+,\qquad c_B(z)=c_B^-.\)

These identities also hold when the corresponding quantity has no jump. Sorting the types gives

\(r_A(z)\leq r_A^+,\qquad r_B(z)\geq r_B^-.\)

Therefore:

  • If the right side is not A-bad, then \(z\) is not A-bad, since

\(r_A(z)c_A(z)\leq r_A^+c_A^+\leq k_B^+=k_B(z)\).

  • If the left side is not B-bad, then \(z\) is not B-bad, since

\(c_B(z)=c_B^-\leq r_B^-k_A^-\leq r_B(z)k_A(z)\).

In particular, if the left side is A-bad and the right side is B-bad, neither side has the other bad status, by Section 3. Both implications apply, so the exact breakpoint \(z\) is fair.

We will also use the contrapositives: an A-bad breakpoint has an A-bad right side, and a B-bad breakpoint has a B-bad left side.

5. Finding a fair cut in polynomial time

Initial bracket

Let \(r_{\min}=r_1\), \(r_{\max}=r_\tau\), and choose

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\min\left\{\frac14,\frac{ar_{\min}}{2(2b+r_{\min}+1)},\frac{b}{2(2r_{\max}a+1)}\right\}.\)

This is a positive rational of \(O(L)\) bits.

At \(\alpha=\varepsilon\), we have \(k_B\leq 2b\), whereas

\(\displaystyle r_Ac_A\geq r_{\min}\left(\frac a\varepsilon-1\right)\geq 4b+r_{\min}+2>2b.\)

Thus this cut is A-bad.

At \(\alpha=1-\varepsilon\), we have \(k_A\leq 2a\), whereas

\(\displaystyle c_B\geq\frac b\varepsilon-1\geq4r_{\max}a+1>2r_{\max}a\geq r_Bk_A.\)

Thus this cut is B-bad.

Start with the bracket \([u,v]=[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\).

Bisection

Evaluate the status of the midpoint:

  • If fair, return it.
  • If A-bad, replace \(u\) by the midpoint.
  • If B-bad, replace \(v\) by the midpoint.

The endpoints remain A-bad and B-bad, respectively. No global monotonicity assumption is needed.

To bound the number of iterations, let \(d_a,d_b,d_i\) be the input denominators of \(a,b,\mu_i\), and set

\(\displaystyle U=1+\left\lceil\frac{\max\{a,b\}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil,\qquad D=U\,d_a d_b\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}d_i.\)

Every breakpoint in \([\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\) has reduced denominator at most \(D\):

  • A cumulative mass \(s_i\) has denominator dividing \(\prod_jd_j\).
  • At \(a/k\) in this interval, \(k\leq a/\varepsilon<U\), so its denominator is at most \(d_aU\).
  • The analogous denominator bound for \(1-b/\ell\) is \(d_bU\).

Distinct rationals with denominators at most \(D\) differ by at least \(1/D^2\). Furthermore, \(\log D=O(L)\). Consequently, after \(O(L)\) bisections, either a fair midpoint has been found or

\(v-u<1/D^2.\)

In the latter case, the closed bracket contains at most one distinct breakpoint. It contains at least one, because the status is constant on intervals without breakpoints and the endpoint statuses differ.

That breakpoint cannot equal \(u\): an A-bad breakpoint has an A-bad right side, which would force \(v\) to be A-bad. Likewise, it cannot equal \(v\). Thus there is exactly one breakpoint \(z\in(u,v)\); its left side is A-bad and its right side is B-bad. Section 4 proves that \(z\) is fair.

Exact extraction of the breakpoint

Extraction uses only rational arithmetic:

  • Scan the cumulative masses \(s_i\) for membership in \([u,v]\).
  • A breakpoint \(a/k\) lies in the bracket exactly when

\(\lceil a/v\rceil\leq k\leq\lfloor a/u\rfloor\).

  • A breakpoint \(1-b/\ell\) lies in the bracket exactly when

\(\lceil b/(1-u)\rceil\leq\ell\leq\lfloor b/(1-v)\rfloor\).

Each integer range contains at most one integer; two would give two distinct breakpoints in the bracket. Deduplicating the candidates yields \(z\).

The algorithm therefore finds an exact rational fair cut.

6. Zero supplies and encoding bounds

If \(b=0\), assign every type only A bundles of sizes \(\lfloor a\rfloor\) and \(\lceil a\rceil\), using the same proportions for every type to obtain mean \(a\). Bundle sizes differ by at most one, so the allocation is EF1.

For fPO, any supply-preserving reassignment has zero B quantity almost everywhere. Since all A disutilities are positive, weak improvement requires each agent’s A quantity to weakly decrease; strict improvement on positive mass would strictly decrease total A supply. This is impossible. The case \(a=0\) is symmetric; if both supplies vanish, assign everyone the empty bundle.

For positive supplies, every cut examined lies in \([\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\), so all output bundle coordinates are bounded by \(U\) and have \(O(L)\) bits. Bisection endpoints can be stored on successive dyadic refinements of the initial rational bracket; after \(O(L)\) iterations they still have \(O(L)\) bits. The extracted breakpoint and all output masses likewise have \(O(L)\) bits.

Each unsplit type receives at most two bundles, and at most one split type receives four. Thus the output has at most \(2\tau+2\) positive cohorts and total length \(O(\tau L)\).

Sorting takes \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) rational comparisons. Each of the \(O(L)\) bisections locates the cut among cumulative masses using \(O(\log(\tau+1))\) comparisons, then performs a constant number of rational operations. Breakpoint extraction and output construction use \(O(\tau)\) further operations. All operands have \(O(L)\) bits. With schoolbook arithmetic, this proves the stated bound

\(O\!\left((\tau+L)\log(\tau+1)L^2\right).\)

Thus the search problem belongs to polynomial time for \(m=2\), with unrestricted support size \(\tau\) and binary-encoded supplies. Under the stated assumptions, there are no nonexistence instances.
