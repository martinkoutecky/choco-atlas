Partial solution: exact computation with few additional constraints

The unrestricted complexity question remains unresolved here. I prove a new tractable case that permits arbitrary positive binary-rational masses, including exponentially small ones.

For each type, normalize its costs by its cheapest axes-cutting alternative. Let $k$ be the number of alternatives whose normalized costs sum to less than $1$. Then an exact rational equilibrium can be computed in time
$$ \binom{\tau+k}{k}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
Thus the requested search problem is polynomial-time solvable whenever $k$ is fixed, independently of the numerical value of the smallest mass. When $k\le 1$, an explicit formula avoids vertex enumeration entirely.

The covering-LP approach is consistent with the formulation in [Cookson–Ebadian–Peters–Shah, Section 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26053v1#S6). All claims used below are proved directly for the rational-mass formulation.

1. Normalization and the parameter $k$

Call an alternative *pure for type $t$* if it has positive cost for $t$ and zero cost for every other type. Axes-cutting guarantees at least one such alternative for each type.

Choose a cheapest pure alternative $a_t$ for each type, and define
$$ \alpha_t=c_t(a_t)>0, \qquad d_t(a)=\frac{c_t(a)}{\alpha_t}. $$
Then
$$ d_t(a_t)=1, \qquad d_{t'}(a_t)=0\quad(t'\ne t). $$
The chosen alternatives are distinct: an alternative cannot be pure for two different types.

Consider the covering polyhedron
$$ Q= \left\{ q\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}: \sum_t d_t(a)q_t\ge1 \text{ for every }a\in A \right\}. $$
The constraints corresponding to the chosen pure alternatives imply
$$ q_t\ge1\qquad(t\in T). $$

Define
$$ R= \left\{ a\in A:\sum_t d_t(a)<1 \right\}, \qquad k=|R|. $$
If $a\notin R$, then every vector satisfying $q_t\ge1$ also satisfies
$$ \sum_t d_t(a)q_t \ge \sum_t d_t(a) \ge1. $$
Consequently,
$$ Q= \left\{ q\in\mathbb R^T: q_t\ge1\text{ for all }t,\quad \sum_t d_t(a)q_t\ge1\text{ for all }a\in R \right\}. $$

No pure alternative belongs to $R$. Indeed, if $a$ is pure for $t$, minimality of $\alpha_t$ gives
$$ d_t(a)=\frac{c_t(a)}{\alpha_t}\ge1. $$
In particular,
$$ k\le m-\tau. $$

The normalization and $R$ can be computed using polynomially many exact rational operations, with polynomial encoding length.

2. A polynomial-time test that extracts an exact equilibrium

For a rational vector $q\in Q$, define the LP value
$$ J(q)= \min_{v\in Q} \sum_t\frac{\mu_t}{q_t}v_t. $$
Since $q$ itself is feasible and the masses sum to $1$,
$$ J(q)\le \sum_t\frac{\mu_t}{q_t}q_t =1. $$

Lemma. If $q\in Q$ is rational and $J(q)=1$, an exact rational equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time from $q$ and the input. If $q$ has polynomial encoding length, so does the resulting equilibrium.

Proof. Set
$$ w_t=\frac{\mu_t}{q_t}>0. $$
Using the original description of $Q$, the primal and dual LPs are
$$ \min_{v\ge0} \sum_t w_tv_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t d_t(a)v_t\ge1 \quad(a\in A), $$
and
$$ \max_{z\ge0} \sum_a z(a) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_a d_t(a)z(a)\le w_t \quad(t\in T). $$

The primal is feasible. Its objective sublevel set through $q$ is bounded, because all $w_t$ are positive and $v\ge0$. Thus it has a finite attained optimum. The dual is feasible at zero, so LP strong duality applies.

If $J(q)=1$, then $q$ is an optimal primal solution. Choose a rational optimal dual vertex $z$. Strong duality gives
$$ \sum_a z(a)=1. $$
Thus $x=z$ is a rational lottery.

For completeness, the primal-dual gap has the decomposition
$$ \sum_t w_tq_t-\sum_a z(a) = \sum_t q_t\left(w_t-\sum_a d_t(a)z(a)\right) + \sum_a z(a)\left(\sum_t d_t(a)q_t-1\right). $$
Every summand on the right is nonnegative, and the gap is zero. Since $q_t\ge1$, it follows that
$$ d_t(x)=\frac{\mu_t}{q_t} \qquad(t\in T). $$
It also follows that
$$ x(a)>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_t d_t(a)q_t=1. $$

Define
$$ \pi_t(a)=\frac{q_t}{\mu_t}d_t(a) = \frac{q_t}{\mu_t\alpha_t}c_t(a). $$
These prices are nonnegative and respect zero costs. Their spending is exactly
$$ \pi_t\cdot x = \frac{q_t}{\mu_t}d_t(x) =1. $$
Their aggregate satisfies
$$ \sum_t\mu_t\pi_t(a) = \sum_t q_td_t(a) \ge1, $$
with equality whenever $x(a)>0$.

Finally, let
$$ \lambda_t=\frac{q_t}{\mu_t\alpha_t}>0. $$
For every nonnegative comparison vector $y$,
$$ \pi_t\cdot y\ge\pi_t\cdot x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_tc_t(y)\ge\lambda_tc_t(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_t(y)\ge c_t(x). $$
This proves the required comparison condition for vectors that need not be lotteries.

To justify the dual-vertex selection, its feasible region is bounded. For each alternative $a$, choose a type $t$ with $d_t(a)>0$; then every feasible $z$ satisfies
$$ z(a)\le\frac{w_t}{d_t(a)}. $$
Hence a rational optimal dual vertex exists. Rational LP optimization can return such a vertex in polynomial time, and standard determinant bounds give polynomial encoding length when the LP data have polynomial encoding length. The formulas for the prices preserve that bound. $\square$

This formulation explicitly requires rational input $q$ and a rational dual optimizer; it avoids the rationality qualifications identified in the verifier report.

3. Existence of a passing vertex

The enumeration algorithm below needs a vertex $q$ with $J(q)=1$. I prove its existence rather than assume it from the notes.

Theorem. There is a vertex $q^\star$ of $Q$ satisfying $J(q^\star)=1$. It is rational with polynomial encoding length. Consequently, every valid instance has an exact rational equilibrium of polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Define
$$ \delta= \min\{d_t(a):d_t(a)>0\}, \qquad H=\frac1\delta. $$
Every alternative has some positive cost, so $\delta>0$. Because a chosen pure alternative has normalized cost $1$, we also have $\delta\le1$ and $H\ge1$.

The vector $H\mathbf1$ belongs to $Q$: each alternative has a positive normalized cost of at least $\delta$, and its corresponding contribution is at least $\delta H=1$.

For $q\in Q$, cap each coordinate at $H$:
$$ \bar q_t=\min\{q_t,H\}. $$
The capped vector remains feasible. To see this, fix an alternative $a$. If no coordinate with $d_t(a)>0$ was capped, its covering sum is unchanged. Otherwise, one capped positive-cost coordinate alone contributes
$$ d_t(a)H\ge\delta H=1. $$
Also, $\bar q_t\ge1$.

Consider
$$ F(q)=-\sum_t\mu_t\log q_t. $$
Capping cannot decrease $F$, because $F$ decreases in each coordinate. Therefore maximizing $F$ over $Q$ is equivalent to maximizing it over the nonempty compact set
$$ Q\cap[1,H]^T. $$
Continuity gives a global maximizer $q^\star$ on $Q$.

The Hessian of $F$ is diagonal with entries
$$ \frac{\mu_t}{q_t^2}>0. $$
Hence $F$ is strictly convex. If $q^\star$ were not a vertex, it could be written as
$$ q^\star=\theta u+(1-\theta)v $$
for distinct $u,v\in Q$ and $0<\theta<1$. Strict convexity would give
$$ F(q^\star) < \theta F(u)+(1-\theta)F(v) \le F(q^\star), $$
a contradiction. Thus $q^\star$ is a vertex of $Q$.

For any $v\in Q$, the segment
$$ q^\star+h(v-q^\star), \qquad 0\le h\le1, $$
is feasible. Global maximality implies that the right derivative of $F$ at $h=0$ is nonpositive:
$$ -\sum_t\frac{\mu_t}{q_t^\star}(v_t-q_t^\star)\le0. $$
Therefore
$$ \sum_t\frac{\mu_t}{q_t^\star}v_t \ge \sum_t\mu_t =1. $$
Since equality holds at $v=q^\star$, this proves
$$ J(q^\star)=1. $$

A vertex of a rational polyhedron in $\tau$ dimensions is determined by $\tau$ linearly independent tight constraints. Here their coefficients are normalized input rationals of polynomial encoding length. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule therefore gives polynomial encoding length for $q^\star$: determinants of integer matrices with polynomial-bit entries have polynomially many bits.

The extraction lemma now supplies the exact rational equilibrium and its encoding bound. $\square$

This argument establishes existence of a suitable vertex. It does not give a polynomial-time method for maximizing the convex function $F$.

4. Exact computation when $k$ is fixed

The reduced description of $Q$ makes its vertices easier to enumerate.

Lemma. At any vertex $q$ of $Q$, at most $k$ coordinates exceed $1$.

More precisely, let
$$ I=\{t:q_t>1\}, \qquad j=|I|. $$
There are $j$ tight alternatives $B\subseteq R$ such that the square matrix
$$ \bigl(d_t(a)\bigr)_{a\in B,\ t\in I} $$
is nonsingular.

Proof. Consider all tight constraints from $R$, restricted to the coordinates in $I$. Suppose their coefficient vectors had rank less than $j$. There would then be a nonzero vector $h$, supported on $I$, such that
$$ \sum_t d_t(a)h_t=0 $$
for every tight alternative $a\in R$.

For sufficiently small $\rho>0$, both $q+\rho h$ and $q-\rho h$ remain feasible. Indeed:

  • Coordinates outside $I$ remain equal to $1$.
  • Coordinates in $I$ remain greater than $1$, by choosing $\rho$ sufficiently small.
  • Tight alternative constraints are preserved by the equations above.
  • Every other alternative constraint has positive slack; finitely many such inequalities remain satisfied for sufficiently small $\rho$.

Thus $q$ would be the midpoint of two distinct feasible vectors, contradicting its being a vertex.

The restricted tight vectors therefore have rank $j$. Selecting $j$ independent ones gives $B$, and necessarily $j\le k$. $\square$

Theorem. An exact equilibrium can be computed in time
$$ \binom{\tau+k}{k}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

Proof. For every
$$ 0\le j\le\min\{\tau,k\}, $$
enumerate a set $I\subseteq T$ of size $j$ and a set $B\subseteq R$ of size $j$.

Set $q_t=1$ for $t\notin I$, and solve the equations
$$ \sum_{t\in I}d_t(a)q_t = 1-\sum_{t\notin I}d_t(a) \qquad(a\in B). $$
Skip singular systems. Discard solutions that do not belong to $Q$. For $j=0$, the candidate is $q=\mathbf1$.

The preceding lemma guarantees that every vertex of $Q$ occurs among these candidates.

For each feasible candidate, compute $J(q)$ by exact rational LP optimization. Since $J(q)\le1$, an exact comparison determines whether $J(q)=1$. Upon finding equality, apply the extraction lemma.

The existence theorem guarantees that some candidate passes.

The number of systems considered is
$$ \sum_{j=0}^{\min\{\tau,k\}} \binom{\tau}{j}\binom{k}{j} = \binom{\tau+k}{k}, $$
by Vandermonde’s identity. Each candidate is obtained from a rational linear system with input coefficients of polynomial encoding length, so it has polynomial encoding length. Feasibility checks, LP optimization, exact comparisons, and equilibrium extraction therefore take polynomial time per candidate. $\square$

For fixed $k$, this is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. It outputs spending exactly equal to $1$, so it works for every permitted $\varepsilon$ without any dependence on $1/\varepsilon$.

In particular, fixing $m-\tau$ suffices because $k\le m-\tau$. The parameter $k$ can be smaller than $m-\tau$, since alternatives with normalized cost sum at least $1$ contribute no additional constraint.

5. Explicit equilibria for $k=0$ and $k=1$

5.1 No additional constraint

If $k=0$, take
$$ x(a_t)=\mu_t $$
and give every other alternative probability zero. Define
$$ \pi_t(a)=\frac{d_t(a)}{\mu_t}. $$
Then $d_t(x)=\mu_t$, so every type spends exactly $1$. Aggregate prices are
$$ \sum_t\mu_t\pi_t(a)=\sum_t d_t(a)\ge1, $$
with equality at each chosen pure alternative. Positive proportionality to costs proves the comparison condition.

Thus this is an exact equilibrium.

5.2 One additional constraint

Suppose $R=\{b\}$. Write
$$ d_t=d_t(b), \qquad D=1-\sum_t d_t>0. $$
Some $d_t$ is positive because every alternative has a positive cost for some type.

Choose
$$ i\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{t:d_t>0}\frac{\mu_t}{d_t}. $$
Define
$$ q_i=1+\frac{D}{d_i}, \qquad q_t=1\quad(t\ne i), \qquad p=\frac{\mu_i}{d_i+D}. $$
Set
$$ x(b)=p, \qquad x(a_i)=0, \qquad x(a_t)=\mu_t-d_tp\quad(t\ne i), $$
and assign zero probability to all remaining alternatives.

These probabilities are nonnegative. If $t\ne i$ and $d_t>0$, the choice of $i$ gives
$$ p=\frac{\mu_i}{d_i+D} < \frac{\mu_i}{d_i} \le \frac{\mu_t}{d_t}, $$
so $\mu_t-d_tp>0$. If $d_t=0$, the probability is $\mu_t>0$.

Their sum is
$$ \begin{aligned} p+\sum_{t\ne i}(\mu_t-d_tp) &= 1-\mu_i+ \left(1-\sum_{t\ne i}d_t\right)p\\ &= 1-\mu_i+(D+d_i)p\\ &=1. \end{aligned} $$
Hence $x$ is a lottery.

Define prices by
$$ \pi_t(a)=\frac{q_t}{\mu_t}d_t(a). $$
For $t\ne i$,
$$ d_t(x)=x(a_t)+d_tp=\mu_t. $$
For $i$,
$$ q_i d_i(x) = \left(1+\frac{D}{d_i}\right)d_ip = (d_i+D)p = \mu_i. $$
Therefore every type spends exactly $1$.

The additional constraint is tight:
$$ \sum_t d_tq_t = \sum_t d_t+D =1. $$
Every remaining alternative has normalized cost sum at least $1$, so $q\ge\mathbf1$ implies aggregate price at least $1$. At a positively used pure alternative $a_t$, we have $t\ne i$ and aggregate price $q_t=1$. At $b$, aggregate price is also $1$.

Zero-respecting prices and cost minimization follow from positive proportionality to costs. This proves an exact equilibrium, using only polynomially many rational operations.

6. A qualification concerning NP-hardness

There is also a precise limitation on one natural meaning of NP-hardness for this total search problem.

Suppose a polynomial-time map sends every Boolean formula $\varphi$ to a valid instance, and a polynomial-time decoder determines whether $\varphi$ is satisfiable from any valid equilibrium output for that instance. Then
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

To prove this, use the polynomial-size exact equilibrium established above as a certificate for unsatisfiability. A verifier guesses rational $q,x$ and checks the finite conditions
$$ q\in Q,\qquad x\ge0,\qquad \sum_a x(a)=1, $$
$$ q_td_t(x)=\mu_t\quad(t\in T), $$
and
$$ x(a)>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_t d_t(a)q_t=1. $$
These conditions are polynomial-time checkable and, with
$$ \pi_t(a)=\frac{q_t}{\mu_t}d_t(a), $$
certify an exact equilibrium by the calculations in Section 2.

The verifier then runs the decoder and accepts precisely when it declares $\varphi$ unsatisfiable. Every unsatisfiable formula has an accepting certificate, while no satisfiable formula does, by the decoder’s assumed correctness for every valid equilibrium. Thus unsatisfiability belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, implying $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

This is a conditional statement about the specified reduction notion; it does not settle the search problem’s complexity.
