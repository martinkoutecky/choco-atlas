The continuous mirror question, as graded

For fixed \(k\ge3\), given a finite set of network types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a feasible rational supply \(\kappa\) of channels per unit of network mass, and type-level bid tables for every channel pattern, choose \(x_{p,S}\ge0\) to maximize total revenue. Here \(p\in T\) is the primary type, \(S\) is a multiset of at most \(k\) secondary types, and each pattern earns the primary bid plus the bids of its secondary slots. Require \(\sum_{p,S}x_{p,S}=\kappa\) and \(\sum_{p,S}a_t(p,S)x_{p,S}\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\), where \(a_t(p,S)\) counts the slots of type \(t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types.
Fix \(k\ge3\). Channels are identical; a pattern \((p,S)\) consists of one primary type \(p\in T\) and a multiset \(S\) of at most \(k\) secondary types, including the empty multiset. Repeated types represent distinct networks; each network can occupy only one slot across all channels.
For every pattern, explicit tables give nonnegative binary rational bids \(b^P(p,S)\) and \(b^S(t;p,S)\) for each secondary type \(t\) occurring in \(S\). Bids depend only on the pattern, and its revenue is \(r(p,S)=b^P(p,S)+\sum_{t\in T}\operatorname{mult}_S(t)b^S(t;p,S)\).
Let \(a_t(p,S)=\mathbf{1}_{p=t}+\operatorname{mult}_S(t)\). Given binary rational channel supply \(0<\kappa\le1\), choose real channel masses \(x_{p,S}\ge0\) maximizing \(\sum_{p,S}r(p,S)x_{p,S}\), subject to \(\sum_{p,S}x_{p,S}=\kappa\) and \(\sum_{p,S}a_t(p,S)x_{p,S}\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\). Unused network mass is allowed.
Let \(m\) be the number of patterns and \(L\) the total binary input length, including an additional nonnegative rational revenue threshold \(D\); state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(k\) fixed.

Is deciding whether the maximum revenue is at least \(D\) solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard, for each fixed \(k\ge3\)?

The discrete decision problem with distinct bidders, one slot per bidder, and at most \(k\) secondary networks per channel is NP-complete, even with at most two secondary networks per channel ([Kasbekar–Sarkar](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~swati/auction_ton32_singlecolumn.pdf)). No result for this precise mass formulation was identified in the search.

Result

For every fixed \(k\ge 3\), the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable, in \((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) bit operations.

The proof uses the real channel masses exactly as specified in the question.

1. Number of patterns

A secondary multiset \(S\) is determined by its multiplicities \(s_t\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\), satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}s_t\le k\). Introducing a slack coordinate converts this inequality into an equation with \(\tau+1\) nonnegative integer coordinates summing to \(k\). Stars and bars therefore gives \(\binom{\tau+k}{k}\) secondary multisets, including the empty multiset.

There are \(\tau\) choices of primary type, independently of the secondary multiset. Hence

\(m=\tau\binom{\tau+k}{k}=O_k(\tau^{k+1})\).

Here \(\tau\) counts only the supported types. Thus, for fixed \(k\), enumerating every pattern takes polynomial time in the support size.

2. Feasibility and attainment

Let \(\mathcal P\) denote the set of patterns. Define \(A\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{\tau\times m}\) by \(A_{t,(p,S)}=a_t(p,S)\), and let \(r\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}^m\) contain the pattern revenues.

The feasible set is

\(F=\{x\in\mathbb R^m:x\ge 0,\ Ax\le\mu,\ \mathbf 1^\top x=\kappa\}\).

It is nonempty. Indeed, set \(x_{p,\varnothing}=\kappa\mu_p\) for every \(p\in T\), and set all other coordinates to zero. Then

\(\sum_{p\in T}x_{p,\varnothing}=\kappa\sum_{p\in T}\mu_p=\kappa\),

and type \(t\) is used in mass \(\kappa\mu_t\le\mu_t\), because \(\kappa\le 1\).

Moreover, every \(x\in F\) satisfies \(0\le x_j\le\kappa\) for every coordinate \(j\), by nonnegativity and the channel equality. Consequently, \(F\) is closed and bounded, hence compact. The continuous linear objective \(r^\top x\) therefore attains a finite maximum on \(F\).

3. Exact threshold test

Construct the following system of rational linear inequalities:

  • \( -x_j\le 0\) for every pattern coordinate \(j\);
  • \( \sum_{j\in\mathcal P}A_{tj}x_j\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\);
  • \( \sum_{j\in\mathcal P}x_j\le\kappa\);
  • \( -\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}x_j\le-\kappa\);
  • \( -\sum_{j\in\mathcal P}r_jx_j\le-D\).

This system has \(m\) variables and \(m+\tau+3\) inequalities.

If it is feasible, its feasible vector belongs to \(F\) and has revenue at least \(D\), so the maximum revenue is at least \(D\). Conversely, if the maximum revenue is at least \(D\), an attaining maximizer from Section 2 satisfies every displayed inequality. Thus feasibility of this system is equivalent to the requested decision.

We now apply the standard exact rational linear-feasibility theorem: feasibility of an explicitly represented finite system of linear inequalities with binary rational coefficients can be decided in time polynomial in its dimensions and total binary encoding length. This is the polynomial-time linear-programming theorem of [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). It applies to systems containing equalities represented by opposite inequalities; no full-dimensionality assumption is required.

4. Encoding length and running time

All hypotheses of that theorem hold:

  • The variables are real, as required.
  • The system is explicitly constructed.
  • Each entry of \(A\) is an integer between \(0\) and \(k+1\).
  • The masses, supply, and threshold are binary rationals from the input.
  • Each revenue is a sum of at most \(k+1\) input bids, counting repetitions.

For the last point, write those bids as \(u_i/v_i\), with \(v_i>0\). A common denominator is \(\prod_i v_i\), whose binary length is at most the sum of the denominator lengths. The corresponding numerator is a sum of at most \(k+1\) integer products, adding at most \(O(\log(k+1))\) bits beyond the lengths of those products. Hence each revenue has \(O_k(L)\) bits and can be computed using polynomially many bit operations.

Even a dense representation of the entire inequality system consequently has encoding length

\(B=O_k\bigl(m(m+\tau+L)\bigr)\).

Its construction takes polynomial time, and exact rational feasibility takes \(\operatorname{poly}(m,m+\tau,B)\) bit operations. Therefore the complete decision algorithm runs in

\((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\).

Since \(m=O_k(\tau^{k+1})\), this also gives a bound of \((\tau^{k+1}+L)^{O(1)}\) for each fixed \(k\). The bound includes the numerical input length; it does not assert strong polynomiality.

5. Relation to the discrete hardness result

The [Kasbekar–Sarkar problem](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~swati/auction_ton32_singlecolumn.pdf) allocates whole channels to distinct bidders, each usable on at most one channel. Its discrete allocation decisions impose integrality.

In the stated mass problem, pattern quantities are arbitrary nonnegative real numbers. Multiplicities are accounted for by the coefficients \(a_t(p,S)\), and the prescribed network-use restrictions are precisely the linear capacity inequalities. There is no additional integrality constraint. Thus the discrete NP-completeness result does not establish hardness of this formulation.

The exact linear-feasibility algorithm above settles the mass problem for every fixed \(k\ge 3\).
