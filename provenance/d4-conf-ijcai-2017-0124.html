The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite doctor types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), hospitals \(H\), type-specific contract menus and preferences, positive wages \(w_x\), additive hospital utility densities \(u_h(x)\), and per-capita budgets \(b_h\), compute a mass allocation \(y_{\theta,x}\) minimizing the budget inflation \(\delta\) required for continuous stability. Each individual receives one integral contract, while type mass may split across contracts; a blocking coalition is positive mass assigned to strictly preferred contracts that gives some hospital strictly higher additive utility within budget \(b_h+\delta\). Determine whether fractional density-greedy generalized deferred acceptance computes an optimum in polynomial time, in particular whether it achieves \(\delta=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) doctor types \(\Theta\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), normalized by \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type represents an atomless population.
There are \(m\) hospitals \(H\). Each type has an explicitly listed finite menu \(X_\theta\) of contracts \(x=(\theta,h,w_x)\) and a strict preference order over \(X_\theta\cup\{\varnothing\}\), where \(\varnothing\) means unmatched. Hospital budgets \(b_h\) and wages are binary rationals satisfying \(0<w_x\le b_h\); hospital utility densities \(u_h(x)\) are nonnegative binary rationals. Every offered contract is acceptable to its hospital.
An allocation consists of real masses \(y_{\theta,a}\ge0\), with \(\sum_{a\in X_\theta\cup\{\varnothing\}}y_{\theta,a}=\mu_\theta\). Each individual receives one contract or remains unmatched; type mass may split. Require \(y_{\theta,x}=0\) whenever \(\varnothing\succ_\theta x\). Define \(W_h(y)=\sum_{\theta,x:x_H=h}w_xy_{\theta,x}\) and \(U_h(y)=\sum_{\theta,x:x_H=h}u_h(x)y_{\theta,x}\).
For a common inflation \(\delta\ge0\), feasibility means \(W_h(y)\le b_h+\delta\) for every hospital. A blocking alternative for hospital \(h\) selects disjoint submasses from the current allocation and assigns each selected individual a contract with \(h\): an existing contract may be retained, but every changed contract must be strictly preferred to that individual's current assignment. The hospital may discard incumbents. The alternative must have positive mass, total wage at most \(b_h+\delta\), and utility strictly greater than \(U_h(y)\). Stability means feasibility and absence of such an alternative.
Let \(N=\sum_\theta|X_\theta|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including any rational threshold \(q\ge0\). Running times are measured in \(m,\tau,N,L\).

Is computing the minimum inflation \(\delta\) admitting a stable allocation solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard, with the threshold question asking whether such an allocation exists for some \(0\le\delta\le q\)? Is \(\delta=0\) always attainable?

For finite doctors, density-greedy generalized deferred acceptance computes a stable matching in \(O(|X|\log|X|)\) time for hospital-specific budgets satisfying \(B_h\le B'_h<B_h+\max_{x:x_H=h}w_x\); this does not assert minimum inflation ([Kawase–Iwasaki](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07643v5)). No result settling exactly the atomless problem defined above was identified in the search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, so the stated per-capita budgets are the total budgets in the normalized market.
  • Specified atomless populations and real allocation masses; all numerical input data are binary rationals.
  • Carried over strict doctor preferences, an unmatched option, nonnegative additive hospital utilities, positive budgets, and the restriction that each wage does not exceed its hospital's original budget.
  • Required doctor individual rationality, consistent with the intended matching model and the doctor choice rule.
  • Restored the blocking alternative's ability to retain existing contracts without strict improvement and to discard incumbents; only individuals changing contracts must strictly prefer their new assignment.
  • Interpreted inflation as a common nonnegative additive amount applied to both allocation feasibility and blocking budgets.
  • Introduced a rational threshold and explicit menu size to make the complexity question and input representation precise.

Result for the stated problem

The minimum inflation is always \(\delta_{\min}=0\). It is polynomial-time computable, and a stable allocation witnessing this value can also be constructed in polynomial time. Every threshold instance with \(q\ge 0\) has answer YES.

The normalization makes the stated budgets nonbinding. Below I prove this directly, then address the intended binding-budget case: even there, atomless divisibility implies existence at zero inflation.

1. A polynomial-time construction

For each type \(\theta\), let \(a_\theta\) be its most-preferred element of \(X_\theta\cup\{\varnothing\}\). Define the allocation by giving its entire mass \(\mu_\theta\) to \(a_\theta\), with all other coordinates zero.

The menu is finite and preferences are strict, so \(a_\theta\) exists and is unique. If it is a contract, it is preferred to being unmatched. Thus the allocation satisfies individual acceptability and all type-mass constraints.

Budget feasibility

In fact, every allocation satisfying the type-mass constraints is feasible at zero inflation. For each hospital \(h\),

\(\displaystyle W_h(y)=\sum_{\theta,x:x_H=h}w_xy_{\theta,x}\le b_h\sum_{\theta,x:x_H=h}y_{\theta,x}\le b_h\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=b_h.\)

The first inequality uses \(w_x\le b_h\). The second uses nonnegativity and the fact that each type’s total allocated mass, including unmatched mass, is \(\mu_\theta\). As usual, budgets are nonnegative.

Absence of blocking alternatives

Every individual receives their most-preferred available assignment. Consequently, no individual can make a strictly preferred change.

An admissible alternative for hospital \(h\) can therefore only retain some of its current incumbents. Since all utility densities are nonnegative, retaining a submass and discarding other incumbents gives utility at most \(U_h(y)\). Such an alternative cannot block.

The constructed allocation is therefore stable at \(\delta=0\).

Computational conclusion

Since inflation must be nonnegative and zero is attainable, \(\delta_{\min}=0\).

Scanning the explicitly listed preference orders identifies each \(a_\theta\). The output coordinates are simply input masses or zero, so the construction has polynomial running time and output length in \(m,\tau,N,L\). For every permitted threshold \(q\ge0\), the same allocation witnesses existence with \(0\le\delta\le q\).

2. The binding-budget case also admits zero inflation

To address the substantive question beyond the normalization observation, consider the same allocation and blocking definitions, but allow arbitrary positive rational type masses and positive wage densities. In particular, drop both \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\) and \(w_x\le b_h\). Assume hospital budgets are strictly positive; hospitals with no contracts can be omitted.

Proven claim. This more general atomless model also admits a stable allocation at zero inflation.

The proof uses the finite-doctor theorem supplied in the question, with a separate argument establishing that strict blockers survive discretization.

Finite theorem used

For finite doctors with strict contract preferences and additive hospital utilities, Kawase–Iwasaki’s Theorem 5 gives a matching stable under budgets satisfying \(B_h\le B'_h<B_h+\max_{x:x_H=h}w_x\), provided individual wages satisfy \(0<w_x\le B_h\). Their blocking definition permits retaining incumbents and requires strictly preferred changed contracts, as here. [Kawase–Iwasaki, Definition 1 and Theorem 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07643v5).

Construction of finite approximations

Let \(M=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta\), and choose a positive integer \(D\) such that every \(D\mu_\theta\) is integral.

For each sufficiently large positive multiple \(n\) of \(D\), replace type \(\theta\) by \(n\mu_\theta\) distinct finite doctors. Each represents mass \(1/n\), inherits the type’s preferences, and receives a copy of each contract \(x\) with wage \(w_x/n\).

Give that copied contract hospital utility \((u_h(x)+1/n)/n\). The perturbation makes all contract utilities strictly positive and will vanish in the limit.

Keep the original budgets \(b_h\). Define \(\bar w_h=\max_{x:x_H=h}w_x\). For sufficiently large \(n\), every copied wage is at most its hospital’s budget. The remaining hypotheses also hold: menus are finite, doctor preferences are strict, utilities are additive, and contracts are hospital-acceptable. Hence the finite theorem supplies a matching stable under budgets

\(\displaystyle b_h\le b_h^{(n)}<b_h+\frac{\bar w_h}{n}.\)

Let \(y_{\theta,a}^{(n)}\) be the number of type-\(\theta\) clones assigned to \(a\), divided by \(n\). These coordinates satisfy the original type-mass constraints and individual acceptability. Moreover,

\(\displaystyle W_h(y^{(n)})\le b_h^{(n)}<b_h+\frac{\bar w_h}{n}.\)

There are finitely many coordinates, each between zero and its type mass. Thus some subsequence converges to an allocation \(y\). Type-mass constraints and individual acceptability pass to the limit, and continuity of the wage sums gives \(W_h(y)\le b_h\).

It remains to prove stability.

A strict atomless blocker would yield a finite blocker

Suppose, for contradiction, that hospital \(h\) has a blocking alternative against \(y\).

Represent its selected submasses by \(z_{\theta,a,x}\), where the current assignment is \(a\) and the proposed contract is \(x\in X_\theta\) with \(x_H=h\). A coordinate is permitted only when either \(x=a\), retaining an incumbent, or \(x\succ_\theta a\). Disjointness means

\(\displaystyle z_{\theta,a,x}\ge0,\qquad \sum_xz_{\theta,a,x}\le y_{\theta,a}.\)

Let its wage and utility be

\(\displaystyle C=\sum_{\theta,a,x}w_xz_{\theta,a,x}\le b_h,\qquad V=\sum_{\theta,a,x}u_h(x)z_{\theta,a,x}>U_h(y).\)

Since \(U_h(y)\ge0\), we have \(V>0\). Choose \(t\) satisfying \(U_h(y)/V<t<1\). Scaling the alternative by \(t\) preserves a strict utility improvement and creates budget slack:

\(\displaystyle tV>U_h(y),\qquad tC\le tb_h<b_h.\)

For each permitted coordinate, select

\(\displaystyle k_{\theta,a,x}^{(n)}=\left\lfloor ntz_{\theta,a,x}\right\rfloor\)

clones currently assigned to \(a\), and assign them to the copied contract \(x\).

These selections are possible for all sufficiently large \(n\) along the convergent subsequence. Indeed, if \(y_{\theta,a}>0\), convergence and \(t<1\) imply \(y_{\theta,a}^{(n)}\ge ty_{\theta,a}\) eventually. Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \frac1n\sum_x k_{\theta,a,x}^{(n)}\le t\sum_xz_{\theta,a,x}\le ty_{\theta,a}\le y_{\theta,a}^{(n)}.\)

If \(y_{\theta,a}=0\), every corresponding selection coordinate is zero. Thus distinct clones can be selected for all proposed assignments. Their changes satisfy the inherited strict preferences.

The finite alternative’s wage is at most \(tC<b_h\le b_h^{(n)}\). Its utility before the perturbation converges to \(tV\), because there are finitely many coordinates and \(k_{\theta,a,x}^{(n)}/n\to tz_{\theta,a,x}\).

The current finite matching’s unperturbed utility is \(U_h(y^{(n)})\to U_h(y)\). Its total utility perturbation is at most \(M/n\): there are \(nM\) clones and each selected contract contributes an additional \(1/n^2\).

Since \(tV>U_h(y)\), the proposed finite alternative has strictly greater perturbed utility than the current finite matching for all sufficiently large \(n\). It also has positive mass, since its limiting utility is positive. It therefore blocks that matching, contradicting finite stability.

This proves the stronger existence claim.

It does not provide a polynomial-time construction of a stable allocation for that broader variant: expanding rational masses into clones can require arbitrarily many doctors.
