The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(i\in I\) with rational masses \(\alpha_i\), weak rankings, and per-unit assignment costs \(w_{ij}\), and position types \(j\in J\) with rational capacities \(\beta_j\), find a minimum-cost popular assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), or report that none exists. Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\), with each type’s last-resort option having capacity \(\alpha_i\). An assignment is popular if, for every feasible challenger flow \(z_{ijk}\ge0\) with \(\sum_k z_{ijk}=x_{ij}\) and \(\sum_{i,j}z_{ijk}\le\beta_k\), \(\sum_{i,j,k}z_{ijk}(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_i j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_i k])\le0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list \(I\) of \(\tau\ge1\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\alpha_i\), normalized by \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\). Here \(\tau=|I|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Let \(J\) contain \(m\) position types with positive binary-rational capacities \(\beta_j\). Each applicant type \(i\) weakly ranks a nonempty acceptable set \(A_i\subseteq J\); positions have no preferences or votes. Its private last resort \(\ell_i\in A_i\) is strictly worst, acceptable only to \(i\), and has capacity \(\beta_{\ell_i}=\alpha_i\).
For each acceptable pair, the per-unit cost \(w_{ij}\in\mathbb Q\) is binary encoded and may have either sign. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible assignment has \(x_{ij}\ge0\), with \(x_{ij}=0\) for \(j\notin A_i\), \(\sum_jx_{ij}=\alpha_i\), and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\).
A challenger is any nonnegative flow \(z_{ijk}\), zero unless \(j,k\in A_i\), satisfying \(\sum_kz_{ijk}=x_{ij}\) and \(\sum_{i,j}z_{ijk}\le\beta_k\). The assignment is popular precisely when every challenger satisfies \(\sum_{i,j,k}z_{ijk}(\mathbf1[k\succ_i j]-\mathbf1[j\succ_i k])\le0\); ties contribute zero.
The optimization problem is to minimize \(\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}x_{ij}\) over popular assignments, or report that none exists.

Is this continuous minimum-cost popular-assignment problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). In fact, its popular assignments form a rational polytope with a polynomial-size description.

The proof uses the discrete first-choice characterization, but the algorithm never constructs the potentially enormous discrete instance. A separate argument extends the characterization from rational assignments to arbitrary real assignments.

1. First-choice flows and the proposed polytope

If \(A_i=\{\ell_i\}\), every assignment and challenger must leave all of type \(i\)'s mass at \(\ell_i\). This contributes zero votes and the fixed cost \(\alpha_iw_{i\ell_i}\). Remove these types and their private positions, adding their fixed costs back at the end. If no types remain, the problem is solved.

Thus assume henceforth that every remaining type has at least two acceptable positions. Its private last resort is consequently not a first choice. The remaining masses need not sum to one; none of the arguments requires that normalization.

Let

\( E=\{(i,j):j\in A_i\},\qquad F_i=\{j\in A_i:j\text{ is most preferred by }i\}, \)

and let \(E_1=\{(i,j):j\in F_i\}\).

Define the first-choice flow polytope by

\( \mathcal Y= \left\{ y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{E_1}: \sum_{j\in F_i}y_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\ \text{for every }i,\quad \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E_1}y_{ij}\leq\beta_j\ \text{for every }j \right\}. \)

Let its maximum total flow be

\( K=\max_{y\in\mathcal Y}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_1}y_{ij}. \)

For every position \(j\), compute

\( r_j= \min\left\{ \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E_1}y_{ij}: y\in\mathcal Y,\ \sum_{(i,k)\in E_1}y_{ik}=K \right\}. \)

Define

\( U=\{j:r_j=\beta_j\}. \)

Thus \(U\) consists exactly of the positions saturated by every maximum first-choice flow.

For each type \(i\), let \(S_i\) be its most-preferred positions in \(A_i\setminus U\), and put

\( E_2=\{(i,j):j\in F_i\cup S_i\}. \)

These sets are well defined: the private last resort has no incident first-choice edge, so \(r_{\ell_i}=0<\beta_{\ell_i}\), and hence \(\ell_i\notin U\).

Let \(\mathcal X\) denote the feasible-assignment polytope from the question. Consider

\( \mathcal C= \left\{ x\in\mathcal X: x_{ij}=0\ \text{for }(i,j)\in E\setminus E_2,\quad \sum_{(i,j)\in E_1}x_{ij}=K \right\}. \)

We will prove that \(\mathcal C\) is exactly the set of popular assignments, including assignments with irrational coordinates.

2. The discrete theorem used

We use the following discrete characterization, stated as Theorem 1 in [Matsui–Hamaguchi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.03458v2).

For a finite applicant–post instance with weak preferences and a private, strictly worst last resort for each applicant, let \(P_1\) be the posts matched in every maximum matching of the first-choice graph. For each applicant \(a\), let \(f(a)\) be its first choices and \(s(a)\) its best posts outside \(P_1\). An applicant-complete matching \(M\) is popular exactly when:

  • its first-choice edges form a maximum matching of the first-choice graph; and
  • every edge of \(M\) assigns an applicant \(a\) to \(f(a)\cup s(a)\).

We next verify that this theorem applies to a discrete expansion of our model and that the expansion preserves precisely the challenger comparisons in the question.

3. Exact correspondence for rational assignments

Fix a feasible assignment \(x\) with rational coordinates. Choose a positive integer \(D\) such that all of

\( N_i=D\alpha_i,\qquad B_j=D\beta_j,\qquad n_{ij}=Dx_{ij} \)

are integers.

Construct a finite discrete instance as follows.

  • Replace type \(i\) by \(N_i\) individual applicants.
  • Replace every non-last-resort position \(j\) by \(B_j\) unit-capacity slots.
  • Each applicant of type \(i\) ranks all slots of an acceptable position \(j\) at that position's original rank.
  • Give each individual applicant its own private, strictly worst last resort.

There are \(N_i=B_{\ell_i}\) private last resorts for type \(i\), so their aggregate capacity equals the original last-resort capacity.

Realize \(x\) by an applicant-complete matching \(M\): assign exactly \(n_{ij}\) individuals of type \(i\) to slots of \(j\), and assign those counted at \(\ell_i\) to their own private last resorts. Integer counts and the capacity inequalities guarantee such a realization.

3.1. Popularity is preserved exactly

Claim. The continuous assignment \(x\) is popular if and only if \(M\) is popular.

First, any applicant-complete discrete challenger \(M'\) induces

\( z_{ijk}= \frac{1}{D} \left| \left\{ a\text{ of type }i: M(a)\text{ has original position }j,\ M'(a)\text{ has original position }k \right\} \right|. \)

Here every private last resort of a type-\(i\) individual is interpreted as \(\ell_i\). This is a feasible challenger flow, and its vote margin is the discrete margin divided by \(D\).

Conversely, suppose a continuous challenger has positive margin. Scale its optimization problem by \(D\), using variables \(h_{ijk}=Dz_{ijk}\). Its constraints are

\( \sum_{k\in A_i}h_{ijk}=n_{ij},\qquad \sum_{i,j}h_{ijk}\leq B_k,\qquad h_{ijk}\geq0. \)

This is a transportation problem from source nodes \((i,j)\), with integer supplies \(n_{ij}\), to destination nodes \(k\), with integer capacities \(B_k\). The allowed arcs are exactly those with \(j,k\in A_i\).

The integral-flow theorem applies because all supplies and capacities are integers. The feasible region is nonempty and bounded, so the maximum vote margin has an integral optimum. Since a positive-margin flow exists, an integral positive-margin optimum exists.

Partition the individuals currently assigned to \(j\) into groups of sizes \(h_{ijk}\). Send each group to destination \(k\), using distinct public slots when \(k\) is public and each individual's own private last resort when \(k=\ell_i\). The capacity constraints make this an applicant-complete matching with the same positive margin.

Finally, allowing incomplete discrete challengers does not change the conclusion: complete each unmatched applicant at its unused private last resort. This cannot decrease its vote relative to the applicant-complete \(M\).

This proves the claim.

3.2. First-choice structure is preserved

The expanded first-choice graph has maximum matching size \(DK\).

Indeed, aggregating a first-choice matching and dividing its counts by \(D\) gives a member of \(\mathcal Y\). Conversely, the scaled first-choice flow polytope has integer capacities \(N_i,B_j\). Network-flow integrality therefore gives an integral maximum flow, which can be realized as a first-choice matching.

We also identify its universally matched posts.

For a public position \(j\):

  • If \(j\in U\), every maximum first-choice matching occupies all \(B_j\) slots.
  • If \(j\notin U\), minimize occupancy of \(j\) over the scaled maximum-flow face. That face is a face of an integral flow polytope, so this minimum has an integral optimizer. Its occupancy is \(Dr_j<B_j\), leaving at least one slot unmatched. Permuting the slots of \(j\) preserves the graph, so any specified slot can be left unmatched by some maximum matching.

Thus all slots of \(j\) are universally matched exactly when \(j\in U\); otherwise none are universally matched. Private last resorts have no first-choice edges and are never universally matched.

Consequently, each expanded applicant's best posts outside the universally matched set correspond exactly to \(S_i\).

The discrete theorem now applies, with all its hypotheses satisfied. Its two conditions translate respectively to

\( \sum_{(i,j)\in E_1}x_{ij}=K \)

and

\( x_{ij}=0\qquad((i,j)\in E\setminus E_2). \)

We have proved

\( x\text{ rational and feasible} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigl(x\text{ popular}\iff x\in\mathcal C\bigr). \)

4. Extension to arbitrary real assignments

Rational equivalence alone does not rule out additional irrational popular assignments. The following argument closes that gap.

For \(j,k\in A_i\), define

\( c_{ijk}=\mathbf1[k\succ_i j]-\mathbf1[j\succ_i k]. \)

Let \(V(x)\) be the maximum challenger margin against a feasible \(x\). Its primal linear program is feasible: the diagonal flow \(z_{ijj}=x_{ij}\) is a challenger with margin zero. It is also bounded, since its total flow is the fixed total applicant mass. Hence

\( V(x)\geq0,\qquad x\text{ popular}\iff V(x)=0. \)

The challenger LP has dual

\( V(x)= \min_{(u,v)\in\mathcal Q} \left( \sum_{(i,j)\in E}u_{ij}x_{ij} +\sum_{k\in J}\beta_kv_k \right), \)

where

\( \mathcal Q= \left\{ (u,v): v_k\geq0,\quad u_{ij}+v_k\geq c_{ijk} \ \text{for every }i,\ j,k\in A_i \right\}, \)

and the variables \(u_{ij}\) are unrestricted in sign.

The polyhedron \(\mathcal Q\) is nonempty, since \(u_{ij}=1,\ v_k=0\) is feasible. It contains no line: a line direction must have zero \(v\)-coordinates because \(v\geq0\); the inequalities then force every \(u\)-direction to be zero.

A nonempty polyhedron containing no line has finitely many vertices, and every attained finite linear-programming minimum occurs at a vertex. Strong duality supplies such a minimum here. Moreover, every vertex of \(\mathcal Q\) is rational because its defining coefficients are rational.

Write its vertices as \((u^h,v^h)\), for \(h=1,\ldots,H\), and define

\( g_h(x)= \sum_{(i,j)\in E}u^h_{ij}x_{ij} +\sum_k\beta_kv^h_k. \)

It follows that

\( V(x)=\min_{1\leq h\leq H}g_h(x). \)

Each \(g_h\) has rational coefficients. Weak duality and the diagonal challenger give \(g_h(x)\geq0\) on \(\mathcal X\). Therefore the popular set is

\( \mathcal P= \bigcup_{h=1}^{H} \{x\in\mathcal X:g_h(x)=0\}. \)

Each component is a rational polytope: \(\mathcal X\) is rational and bounded, and \(g_h(x)=0\) is a rational equality. Thus:

  • \(\mathcal P\) is closed, being a finite union of closed sets.
  • Rational points are dense in \(\mathcal P\). Indeed, each component is the convex hull of its rational vertices, and its convex-combination coefficients can be approximated by rational coefficients.

The polytope \(\mathcal C\) is likewise closed and has dense rational points. Section 3 proves that \(\mathcal P\) and \(\mathcal C\) have exactly the same rational points. Taking closures yields

\( \mathcal P =\overline{\mathcal P\cap\mathbb Q^E} =\overline{\mathcal C\cap\mathbb Q^E} =\mathcal C. \)

This establishes the characterization for all real assignments.

5. Polynomial-time optimization

The algorithm is now explicit:

  • Solve the first-choice flow LP to obtain \(K\).
  • For each position \(j\), solve the minimum-occupancy LP defining \(r_j\).
  • Compute \(U\), the sets \(S_i\), and \(E_2\).
  • Minimize \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\) over \(\mathcal C\).

If the final LP is infeasible, no popular assignment exists. Otherwise its optimum is a minimum-cost popular assignment.

There are at most \(m+2\) LP solves. Each LP has at most \(\tau m\) variables and polynomially many constraints. The input data are rational, and exact rational LP algorithms compute \(K,r_j\) and the final optimum in polynomial time. Their rational output lengths are polynomially bounded by the LP encoding lengths, so testing \(r_j=\beta_j\) also takes polynomial time.

Signed costs cause no difficulty: \(\mathcal C\) is bounded, so every feasible cost-minimization instance has an attained finite optimum.

The integer \(D\) and the expanded applicant–post instance occur only in the proof. Neither is constructed by the algorithm. Hence the running time is polynomial in the original \(m,\tau,L\).
