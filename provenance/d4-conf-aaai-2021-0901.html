1. Payoff reduction and equilibrium certificates

Define the effective weights
$$ w_t=\sum_u\mu_u a_u H_{t,u}. $$
They satisfy $w_t\geq 0$ and
$$ \sum_t w_t =\sum_u\mu_u a_u\sum_tH_{t,u} =1. $$
Interchanging finite sums gives
$$ U_k(b)=\sum_t w_t\frac{s_{t,k}+b_{t,k}}{D_t(b)}, \qquad D_t(b)=1+\sum_\ell b_{t,\ell}. $$
Because $\sum_k s_{t,k}=1$,
$$ \sum_k U_k(b)=1. $$

Let
$$ X_k=\left\{v\in\mathbb R^\tau: v_t\geq 0,\ \sum_t\mu_t v_t\leq\beta_k\right\}, \qquad X=\prod_kX_k. $$
Every $X_k$ is nonempty, compact, and convex: in particular, $v_t\leq\beta_k/\mu_t$.

Write
$$ g_{t,k}(b)=\frac{\partial U_k}{\partial b_{t,k}}(b) =\frac{w_t(D_t-s_{t,k}-b_{t,k})}{D_t^2}. $$
For fixed opposing spends, put $c_{t,k}=1+\sum_{\ell\neq k}b_{t,\ell}$. The derivatives of the type contribution with respect to the advertiser’s own spend $x$ are
$$ \frac{w_t(c_{t,k}-s_{t,k})}{(c_{t,k}+x)^2}, \qquad -\frac{2w_t(c_{t,k}-s_{t,k})}{(c_{t,k}+x)^3}. $$
Since $c_{t,k}\geq 1\geq s_{t,k}$, the payoff is differentiable and concave in the advertiser’s entire spend vector.

Consequently, a feasible profile $b$ is an equilibrium exactly when, for every advertiser $k$,
$$ \sum_t g_{t,k}(b)(v_t-b_{t,k})\leq 0 \qquad\text{for every }v\in X_k. $$
Necessity follows by differentiating the payoff along each feasible segment from $b_{\cdot,k}$ to $v$. Sufficiency follows from the concave-function inequality
$$ U_k(v,b_{-k})-U_k(b) \leq \sum_tg_{t,k}(b)(v_t-b_{t,k}). $$
Thus these conditions address arbitrary feasible vector deviations.

A useful sufficient certificate is budget exhaustion together with multipliers $\lambda_k\geq 0$ satisfying
$$ g_{t,k}(b)=\mu_t\lambda_k \quad\text{if }b_{t,k}>0, \qquad g_{t,k}(b)\leq\mu_t\lambda_k \quad\text{if }b_{t,k}=0. $$
Indeed, for every feasible $v$,
$$ \begin{aligned} U_k(v,b_{-k})-U_k(b) &\leq\sum_tg_{t,k}(b)(v_t-b_{t,k})\\ &\leq\lambda_k\sum_t\mu_t(v_t-b_{t,k})\\ &\leq 0. \end{aligned} $$
The second inequality uses $v_t\geq 0$ at coordinates where $b_{t,k}=0$.

2. Existence and uniqueness for all nontrivial instances

For $m=1$, the payoff is identically $1$, so the zero profile is a rational equilibrium. Henceforth assume $m\geq 2$.

2.1. Existence

Let $g(b)$ denote the vector of all the partial derivatives above. Consider
$$ \Phi(b)=P_X(b+g(b)), $$
where $P_X$ is Euclidean projection onto $X$.

Projection exists and is unique because $X$ is compact and convex and squared Euclidean distance is strictly convex. Its characterization is
$$ p=P_X(u) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (u-p)\cdot(y-p)\leq 0 \quad\text{for every }y\in X. $$
This follows by differentiating squared distance along segments in $X$; the converse follows by expanding $\|u-y\|^2$.

Applying this characterization to $p=P_X(u)$ and $q=P_X(v)$ gives
$$ \|p-q\|^2\leq(p-q)\cdot(u-v)\leq\|p-q\|\|u-v\|. $$
Thus projection is continuous. The vector $g$ is continuous because all denominators satisfy $D_t\geq 1$. Therefore $\Phi$ is a continuous self-map of the nonempty compact convex set $X$.

Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem gives $b=\Phi(b)$. The projection characterization then yields
$$ g(b)\cdot(y-b)\leq 0 \qquad\text{for every }y\in X. $$
Taking $y$ to differ from $b$ in only one advertiser’s vector and applying Section 1 proves that $b$ is an equilibrium.

2.2. Budgets and positive multipliers

Let
$$ T_+=\{t:w_t>0\}. $$
This set is nonempty.

At an equilibrium, each advertiser $k$ has a strictly positive marginal at some type in $T_+$. Suppose instead that all those marginals were zero. Their nonnegative numerators would imply
$$ s_{t,k}=1, \qquad b_{t,j}=0\quad\text{for every }j\neq k, \qquad t\in T_+. $$
For any other advertiser $j$, the simplex condition would give $s_{t,j}=0$ on $T_+$, and hence $U_j(b)=0$. Advertiser $j$ could obtain positive payoff by spending its positive budget on any one type in $T_+$. This contradicts equilibrium.

The existence of a positive marginal has two consequences.

First, every advertiser exhausts its budget: otherwise it could increase spend slightly at a type with positive marginal.

Second, no advertiser spends at a coordinate with zero marginal. If it did, transferring a sufficiently small positive expenditure from that coordinate to one with positive marginal would have positive directional derivative and would improve its payoff. In particular,
$$ b_{t,k}=0\qquad\text{when }w_t=0. $$

Since $\beta_k>0$ and the budget is exhausted, advertiser $k$ has an active coordinate. At any two active coordinates, feasible transfers in both directions show that their normalized marginals $g_{t,k}/\mu_t$ are equal. Transfers from an active coordinate to an inactive coordinate show that the inactive normalized marginal is no larger.

Thus every equilibrium satisfies the certificate in Section 1 with
$$ \lambda_k>0. $$

2.3. A monotonicity identity

Fix a type $t$, suppress its index, and write
$$ x_k=s_{t,k}+b_{t,k}, \qquad D=\sum_kx_k, \qquad p_k=\frac{x_k}{D}. $$
Then $p_k\geq 0$ and $\sum_kp_k=1$.

Let $J$ be the Jacobian, with respect to the spends at this type, of the vector whose entries are $g_k=w(D-x_k)/D^2$. Direct differentiation gives
$$ J_{kk}=-\frac{2w(D-x_k)}{D^3}, \qquad J_{kj}=\frac{w(2x_k-D)}{D^3} \quad(j\neq k). $$

For any $d\in\mathbb R^m$, put $S=\sum_kd_k$. Then
$$ \begin{aligned} d^{\mathsf T}Jd &=\frac{w}{D^3} \left(2(x\cdot d)S-DS^2-D\sum_kd_k^2\right)\\ &=-\frac{w}{D^2} \sum_i p_i \left( \sum_{j\neq i}d_j^2+ \left(\sum_{j\neq i}d_j\right)^2 \right) \leq 0. \end{aligned} $$
The second equality uses
$$ \sum_{j\neq i}d_j^2+ \left(\sum_{j\neq i}d_j\right)^2 =\sum_jd_j^2+S^2-2Sd_i. $$

For $w>0$, equality holds precisely when, for every $i$ with $p_i>0$, all coordinates $d_j$ with $j\neq i$ vanish.

Since each type’s marginals depend only on that type’s spends, integrating this identity along a segment gives
$$ (g(b)-g(c))\cdot(b-c)\leq 0 \qquad\text{for all }b,c\in X. $$

2.4. Uniqueness

Suppose $b$ and $c$ are equilibria, and put $d=b-c$. Their first-order conditions imply
$$ g(b)\cdot d\geq 0, \qquad g(c)\cdot d\leq 0. $$
Together with monotonicity, this gives
$$ (g(b)-g(c))\cdot d=0. $$

Along the segment $c+\theta d$, the expression in Section 2.3 is a sum of continuous nonpositive terms, one for each type. Its integral is zero. Therefore each term is zero at every point of the segment.

Fix $t\in T_+$ and examine the midpoint. If its vector $p$ has at least two positive coordinates, the equality characterization forces $d_{t,\cdot}=0$.

Otherwise $p_i=1$ for some $i$. Nonnegativity of beliefs and spends then implies
$$ s_{t,i}=1, \qquad s_{t,j}=b_{t,j}=c_{t,j}=0 \quad(j\neq i). $$
Advertiser $i$ has zero marginal at this type at both endpoints. Section 2.2 therefore gives
$$ b_{t,i}=c_{t,i}=0. $$
Again $d_{t,\cdot}=0$.

For $t\notin T_+$, both equilibria spend zero by Section 2.2. Hence $b=c$.

Every instance with $m\geq 2$ has exactly one pure Nash equilibrium.

3. A reusable family with an explicit equilibrium

Choose positive rational numbers $q_1,\ldots,q_n$ and positive rational weights $\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_n$ satisfying
$$ \sum_i\rho_i=1, \qquad 1\leq r_i:=\sqrt{q_i}<\frac{17}{16}. $$
Define
$$ A=\sum_i\rho_iq_i, \qquad \bar r=\sum_i\rho_i r_i, \qquad C=\frac54. $$

There are two advertisers and two types $(i,+),(i,-)$ for each index. Set
$$ \mu_{i,+}=\mu_{i,-}=\frac{\rho_i}{2}, \qquad a_{i,+}=a_{i,-}=\frac{q_i}{A}, \qquad \beta_1=\beta_2=\frac18, $$
and
$$ (s_{i,+,1},s_{i,+,2})=\left(\frac14,\frac34\right), \qquad (s_{i,-,1},s_{i,-,2})=\left(\frac34,\frac14\right). $$
Take $H=I$. The Friedkin–Johnsen promise holds with $Q=0$, and salience normalization holds because
$$ \sum_t\mu_ta_t=\sum_i\rho_i\frac{q_i}{A}=1. $$

Define
$$ x_i=C\frac{r_i}{\bar r}-1 $$
and the profile
$$ b_{i,+,1}=x_i,\quad b_{i,+,2}=0, \qquad b_{i,-,1}=0,\quad b_{i,-,2}=x_i. $$
Since $1\leq r_i,\bar r<17/16$,
$$ \frac3{17}<x_i<\frac{21}{64}<\frac12. $$
Each advertiser’s expenditure is
$$ \frac12\sum_i\rho_i x_i =\frac12(C-1) =\frac18. $$

Here $w_t=\mu_ta_t$. Put
$$ \lambda=\frac{3\bar r^2}{4AC^2}>0. $$
At an active coordinate, the advertiser’s initial belief is $1/4$ and the opposing spend is zero, so
$$ \frac{g_{t,k}}{\mu_t} =\frac{q_i}{A}\frac{3/4}{(1+x_i)^2} =\lambda. $$
At an inactive coordinate, its initial belief is $3/4$ and its opponent spends $x_i$, so
$$ \frac{g_{t,k}}{\mu_t} =\frac{q_i}{A}\frac{1/4+x_i}{(1+x_i)^2} =\lambda\frac{1/4+x_i}{3/4} <\lambda. $$
The certificate in Section 1 applies. Section 2 proves uniqueness.

Thus the displayed profile is the unique equilibrium of this rational family.

4. Superpolynomial output length

4.1. Rational instances of polynomial input length

For each $n\geq 2$, choose distinct primes
$$ 2^{i+8}<p_i<2^{i+9}, \qquad 1\leq i\leq n. $$
Bertrand’s postulate guarantees these choices: for every integer $N\geq 2$, there is a prime strictly between $N$ and $2N$. Apply it with $N=2^{i+8}$; the intervals are disjoint. [Soundararajan, *Bertrand’s Postulate and the Existence of Finite Fields*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01389)

Put
$$ d_i=\lfloor\sqrt{p_i}\rfloor, \qquad q_i=\frac{p_i}{d_i^2}, \qquad r_i=\frac{\sqrt{p_i}}{d_i}, \qquad \rho_i=\frac1n. $$
Since the primes are nonsquares and $d_i\geq 16$,
$$ 1<r_i<1+\frac1{d_i}\leq\frac{17}{16}. $$
Section 3 therefore applies.

Each $p_i,d_i,q_i$ has $O(i)$ bits. The rational number
$$ A=\frac1n\sum_iq_i $$
can be written with numerator and denominator of $O(n^2)$ bits by using the product of the denominators as a common denominator. Each salience $q_i/A$ consequently has $O(n^2)$ bits.

Including the explicit identity matrix,
$$ m=2,\qquad \tau=2n,\qquad L=O(n^3). $$
This is an existence argument for a worst-case family; efficient selection of these primes is not needed.

4.2. Independent square-root sign changes

For completeness, define
$$ F_j=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_j}). $$
I prove inductively that $F_j$ has basis
$$ e_S=\prod_{i\in S}\sqrt{p_i}, \qquad S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,j\}, $$
and admits all independent sign changes of the displayed square roots as automorphisms.

For $j=0$, the field is $\mathbb Q$, its basis is $\{1\}$, and its sole sign-change automorphism is the identity.

Assume the assertion for $j$. If some $z\in F_j$ satisfied $z^2=p_{j+1}$, every sign-change automorphism $\sigma$ would satisfy $\sigma(z)=z$ or $\sigma(z)=-z$.

Expand $z$ in the asserted basis. Distinct basis elements have distinct sign characters: changing the sign at an index in the symmetric difference of two subsets distinguishes them. Therefore two different basis elements cannot both have nonzero coefficients in an element sent to either itself or its negative by every sign change. Hence
$$ z=ce_S \qquad\text{for some }c\in\mathbb Q. $$
But then
$$ p_{j+1}=c^2\prod_{i\in S}p_i, $$
which contradicts prime-exponent parity: the exponent of the new prime on the left is odd and its exponent on the right is even.

Thus adjoining $\sqrt{p_{j+1}}$ doubles the degree and produces the claimed enlarged basis. Each existing automorphism extends in both ways, by sending
$$ a+b\sqrt{p_{j+1}} \quad\text{to}\quad \sigma(a)\pm\sigma(b)\sqrt{p_{j+1}}. $$
These maps preserve multiplication because $\sigma$ fixes $p_{j+1}$. This completes the induction.

In particular, the $r_i$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$, and all independent sign changes of them are automorphisms of $F_n$.

4.3. Exponentially many real conjugates

The equilibrium coordinate
$$ x_1=\frac{Cn r_1}{\sum_i r_i}-1 $$
has, under a sign vector $\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^n$, the image
$$ x_1^{(\varepsilon)} =\frac{Cn\varepsilon_1r_1}{\sum_i\varepsilon_i r_i}-1. $$
Every denominator is nonzero by linear independence.

Two images are equal exactly when
$$ \varepsilon_1\sum_i\delta_i r_i =\delta_1\sum_i\varepsilon_i r_i. $$
Linear independence makes this equivalent to
$$ \varepsilon_1\delta_i=\delta_1\varepsilon_i \qquad\text{for every }i. $$
Thus equality occurs exactly when $\delta=\varepsilon$ or $\delta=-\varepsilon$. There are $2^{n-1}$ distinct images.

All are real, and none is zero. Indeed, a zero image would imply
$$ Cn\varepsilon_1r_1=\sum_i\varepsilon_i r_i, $$
contradicting linear independence because $n\geq 2$.

If a nonzero integer polynomial $P$ satisfies $P(x_1)=0$, applying every sign-change automorphism shows that all these images are roots of $P$. Therefore every defining integer polynomial has at least $2^{n-1}$ distinct nonzero real roots.

4.4. Sparse encoding does not avoid the obstruction

A nonzero real polynomial with $h$ nonzero monomials has at most $h-1$ distinct positive roots and at most $h-1$ distinct negative roots.

For positive roots, prove this by induction on $h$. A monomial has none. Otherwise factor out the smallest power of the variable; this preserves positive roots and leaves a polynomial with a nonzero constant term. Its derivative has exactly $h-1$ nonzero monomials, so by induction it has at most $h-2$ distinct positive roots. Rolle’s theorem bounds the original polynomial’s distinct positive roots by $h-1$. Apply the same argument to $P(-X)$ for negative roots.

Consequently, any defining polynomial for $x_1$ has
$$ 2^{n-1}\leq 2(h-1), \qquad h\geq 2^{n-2}+1. $$
An explicit sparse list therefore requires exponentially many entries. A dense list is no shorter. An isolating interval does not remove the need to supply the polynomial.

Since the equilibrium is unique, an algorithm cannot choose a different equilibrium with shorter coordinates. The required output length is $\Omega(2^n)$ while $L=O(n^3)$.

Under explicit dense or sparse coefficient-list encoding, no algorithm can compute an exact equilibrium in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

5. Square-root-sum reduction for equilibrium-coordinate comparison

This section concerns a decision problem, so its output has constant length.

Define equilibrium-coordinate comparison as follows: given a valid instance with $m\geq 2$, a coordinate $(t,k)$, and a rational threshold $h$, decide whether its unique equilibrium satisfies
$$ b^*_{t,k}\geq h. $$

The standard integer-threshold square-root-sum problem asks, for positive binary integers $N_1,\ldots,N_n,B$, whether
$$ S:=\sum_{i=1}^n\sqrt{N_i}\leq B. $$
This formulation appears in [Etessami and Yannakakis, *On the Complexity of Nash Equilibria and Other Fixed Points*](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/kousha/nash_focs07_full_j_spec_issue_sub.pdf).

5.1. Polynomial-time construction

For $1\leq i\leq n$, set
$$ M_i=256N_i, \qquad d_i=\lfloor\sqrt{M_i}\rfloor, \qquad q_i=\frac{M_i}{d_i^2}. $$
Add a reference index $0$ with
$$ M_0=256,\qquad d_0=16,\qquad q_0=1. $$
Define
$$ E=\sum_{i=0}^n d_i, \qquad \rho_i=\frac{d_i}{E}. $$

Every $d_i\geq 16$, and
$$ 1\leq r_i:=\sqrt{q_i}<1+\frac1{d_i}\leq\frac{17}{16}. $$
Thus these quantities define an instance of Section 3 with $2(n+1)$ types.

All supplied quantities are rational. Integer square roots can be computed by binary search in polynomial time. The integers $d_i$ have bit lengths bounded by the source input length. A common denominator formed from the product of the $d_i^2$ also has polynomial bit length. Hence the normalized saliences, masses, identity matrix, and the entire instance can be constructed in polynomial time and have polynomial total bit length.

5.2. The designated coordinate answers the source instance

The weighted mean of the square roots is
$$ \bar r =\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{d_i}{E}\frac{\sqrt{M_i}}{d_i} =\frac{16(1+S)}{E}. $$
Since $r_0=1$, the unique equilibrium has reference coordinate
$$ b^*_{0,+,1} =C\frac{r_0}{\bar r}-1 =\frac{CE}{16(1+S)}-1. $$
Supply the rational threshold
$$ h=\frac{CE}{16(1+B)}-1. $$
Its bit length is polynomial in the source input length.

Because both denominators are positive and reciprocal is strictly decreasing,
$$ b^*_{0,+,1}\geq h \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\leq B. $$
Equality is preserved.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from square-root-sum comparison to equilibrium-coordinate comparison. It already holds with two advertisers, identity influence, positive saliences, and strictly positive beliefs.

This reduction does not prove $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness. In particular, it does not supply an $\mathrm{NP}$-hard source problem or a polynomial-time recovery procedure for such a source problem from an equilibrium output.

6. A polynomial-size exact equilibrium description

The preceding comparison problem also has a direct formulation in the existential theory of the reals.

Introduce real variables $b_{t,k}$ and $\lambda_k$, and abbreviate
$$ D_t=1+\sum_jb_{t,j}, \qquad R_{t,k} =\mu_t\lambda_kD_t^2-w_t(D_t-s_{t,k}-b_{t,k}). $$
Consider the constraints
$$ \begin{gathered} b_{t,k}\geq 0,\qquad \lambda_k\geq 0,\\ \sum_t\mu_t b_{t,k}=\beta_k,\\ R_{t,k}\geq 0,\qquad b_{t,k}R_{t,k}=0. \end{gathered} $$

Every equilibrium satisfies them by Section 2.2. Conversely, dividing by $D_t^2>0$ gives exactly the certificate of Section 1, so every solution is an equilibrium.

The polynomials have degree at most $4$. Their coefficients are rational and have polynomial bit length; computing the $w_t$ and clearing denominators preserves polynomial description length. Expanding each $D_t^2$ also produces only polynomially many monomials.

Adding the inequality $b_{t,k}\geq h$ therefore gives a polynomial-size existential real formula equivalent to equilibrium-coordinate comparison.

This description is compact even when an individual coordinate’s explicit univariate defining polynomial is exponentially long. It does not provide a polynomial-time algorithm for the requested output encoding.

7. What remains open

The output-length result completely rules out polynomial-time production of the stipulated explicit coefficient-list output. The convex-programming argument in the supplied paper does not establish that stronger output guarantee. [Christia et al., Section 3](https://jpdickerson.com/pubs/christia21scalable.pdf)

The new uniqueness theorem and square-root-sum reduction give structural and decision-complexity information beyond that obstruction. However, neither proves $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness of the exact equilibrium search task.

The unresolved part is a hardness classification under an explicitly specified polynomial-time reduction notion for the requested search output. Output length alone does not establish such hardness, and the square-root-sum reduction concerns a coordinate-comparison decision problem.
