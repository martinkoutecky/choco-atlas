Theorem 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(A\), job types \(J\), strict rankings over the opposite type set, and positive rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, define \(\mathcal S\) as the set of flows \(x_{aj}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_a\), column sums \(\nu_j\), and, for each \(a,j\), at least one of \(\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_a j}x_{aj'}=\mu_a\) or \(\sum_{a':\,a'\succeq_j a}x_{a'j}=\nu_j\). Compute the number \(b_0(\mathcal S)\) of connected components; the extremal version asks for its maximum over instances with \(p\) applicant types, \(q\) job types, and mass encoding length at most \(L\).

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass encoded as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types. The masses satisfy \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_j\nu_j=1\); let \(L\) be their total encoding length.
Write \(\succeq\) for a strict ranking extended by equality. Define \(\mathcal S\subseteq\mathbb R^{p\times q}\) to consist of all real flows satisfying \(x_{aj}\geq0\), \(\sum_jx_{aj}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_ax_{aj}=\nu_j\), and, for every \((a,j)\),

Let \(b_0(\mathcal S)\) count connected components in the Euclidean topology, and let \(B(p,q,L)\) be its maximum over all such instances with mass encoding length at most \(L\), whenever this instance class is nonempty.

Result

For every instance in the question, \(\mathcal S\) is nonempty and path connected. Consequently,

\(b_0(\mathcal S)=1\), and \(B(p,q,L)=1\)

whenever the instance class defining \(B(p,q,L)\) is nonempty.

Thus exact component counting is polynomial-time solvable. The essential structural result is the rotation parametrization in Dean–Munshi’s paper, beyond its algorithm for finding one allocation.

1. Matching the stated stability condition to stable allocation

For a feasible flow, define the masses assigned to strictly worse partners by

\(R_{aj}(x)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_a j'}x_{aj'}\), and \(C_{aj}(x)=\sum_{a':\,a\succ_j a'}x_{a'j}\).

The prescribed row and column sums imply that the stability condition in the question is exactly

\(R_{aj}(x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad C_{aj}(x)=0\)

for every pair \((a,j)\).

Set an edge capacity \(u_{aj}=\min\{\mu_a,\nu_j\}\). Every feasible flow already satisfies \(x_{aj}\leq u_{aj}\).

In the capacitated stable-allocation model, a pair blocks precisely when \(x_{aj}<u_{aj}\) and both endpoints have positive allocation to strictly worse partners. This agrees with the condition above, including at saturated edges: if \(x_{aj}=u_{aj}\), then either \(x_{aj}=\mu_a\), giving \(R_{aj}(x)=0\), or \(x_{aj}=\nu_j\), giving \(C_{aj}(x)=0\). These are the blocking conditions used in [Dean–Munshi, Section 2](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf#page=3).

We next check the dummy-element convention in their model explicitly.

Add a dummy applicant \(a_*\) and a dummy job \(j_*\), each with quota \(1\). Retain the original quotas and rankings, append the opposite dummy last to every real type’s ranking, and let each dummy rank all real partners before the other dummy. Give every augmented edge capacity equal to the minimum of its endpoints’ quotas.

Let \(\mathcal T\) denote the stable allocations of this augmented instance, with all augmented quotas filled. For \(z\in\mathcal T\), the real and dummy quota equations give

\(\sum_{a\in A}z_{aj_*}=\sum_{j\in J}z_{a_*j}=d\).

If \(d>0\), choose \(a,j\) with \(z_{aj_*}>0\) and \(z_{a_*j}>0\). Then

\(z_{aj}<\mu_a\quad\text{and}\quad z_{aj}<\nu_j\),

so \(z_{aj}<u_{aj}\). Both real endpoints prefer each other to their dummy partners, producing a blocking pair. Therefore \(d=0\).

It follows that every allocation in \(\mathcal T\) fills all original row and column sums and has \(z_{a_*j_*}=1\). Conversely, every \(x\in\mathcal S\) extends to an allocation in \(\mathcal T\) by setting all real-to-dummy coordinates to zero and \(z_{a_*j_*}=1\). No edge involving a dummy blocks: its real endpoint, if present, ranks the dummy last; the dummy-to-dummy edge is saturated.

Thus restriction and extension are mutually inverse continuous maps between \(\mathcal T\) and \(\mathcal S\). In particular, they preserve connected components.

2. The structural theorem used

We use the following rotation parametrization.

For a finite bipartite stable-allocation instance with strict preferences, real quotas and capacities, and the dummy convention above, there exist a stable allocation \(z^0\), finitely many rotations indexed by a directed acyclic graph \(D\), positive rotation amounts \(r_i\), and fixed signed edge vectors \(v_i\), such that

\(F(y)=z^0+\sum_i y_i v_i\)

parametrizes all stable allocations bijectively as \(y\) ranges over

\(K=\{y:0\leq y_i\leq r_i,\ \ y_j>0\Longrightarrow y_i=r_i\text{ for every arc }i\to j\}\).

This is [Dean–Munshi, Section 4, Lemma 12](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf#page=13). The affine formula expresses the fixed signed flow change of each rotation.

All hypotheses hold for our augmented instance: its graph is finite and bipartite, rankings are strict, quotas are positive real numbers, and capacities satisfy the required endpoint bounds. The theorem explicitly allows nonintegral data. Moreover, \(0\in K\), so \(\mathcal T\), and therefore \(\mathcal S\), is nonempty.

3. Proof of path connectivity

We prove directly that the parameter set \(K\) is path connected.

Choose a topological ordering \(i_1,\ldots,i_h\) of \(D\), meaning that every arc points from an earlier index to a later index. Fix any \(y\in K\).

Starting at \(0\), set the coordinates to their target values one at a time in this order. During stage \(k\), with parameter \(t\in[0,1]\), use the vector \(w\) defined by:

  • \(w_{i_\ell}=y_{i_\ell}\) for \(\ell<k\);
  • \(w_{i_k}=t\,y_{i_k}\);
  • \(w_{i_\ell}=0\) for \(\ell>k\).

The coordinate bounds \(0\leq w_i\leq r_i\) hold immediately. To check the remaining condition, take an arc \(i\to j\) with \(w_j>0\). The coordinate \(j\) has already been reached in the ordering, and \(y_j>0\). Since \(y\in K\), we have \(y_i=r_i\). Since \(i\) precedes \(j\), its coordinate has already been set to its target value. Hence \(w_i=y_i=r_i\).

Therefore every stage lies entirely in \(K\). Concatenating the finitely many stages gives a continuous path from \(0\) to \(y\). If there are no rotations, \(K\) is simply a singleton.

Because \(F\) is affine, it is continuous. Its image is all of \(\mathcal T\), so these paths connect every allocation in \(\mathcal T\) to \(z^0\). The established correspondence then gives paths connecting every flow in \(\mathcal S\) to the restriction of \(z^0\).

This proves \(b_0(\mathcal S)=1\). Taking the maximum over any nonempty instance class proves \(B(p,q,L)=1\).

4. Running time and the discrete comparison

On the promised input class, the exact counting algorithm simply returns \(1\). Its running time and auxiliary space are \(O(1)\), independently of \(m,\tau,p,q,L\).

If the input is first read in full, the usual explicit ranking encoding gives a reading-and-counting bound of

\(O\!\left(L+pq\log(m+1)\right)\),

which is also \(O\!\left(L+m^2\log(m+1)\right)\) and \(O\!\left(L+\tau^2\log(\tau+1)\right)\). These bounds concern promised valid instances.

Discrete stable-matching counts do not count the components here. For example, take two applicants and two jobs, all of mass \(1/2\), with rankings

\(a_1:j_1\succ j_2,\quad a_2:j_2\succ j_1,\quad j_1:a_2\succ a_1,\quad j_2:a_1\succ a_2\).

Every feasible matrix has the form

\(x(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t&1/2-t\\1/2-t&t\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0\leq t\leq1/2\).

Each pair is the least-preferred partner of at least one endpoint, so every \(x(t)\) satisfies the stated stability condition. The two discrete stable matchings are the endpoints of one stable interval. The rotation argument proves the corresponding connectivity for arbitrary instances.
