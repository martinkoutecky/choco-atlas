The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), a population scale \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb{Z}\), additive valuation vectors \(v_t\) over \(r\) good categories, and \(q_g=N\sigma_g\) indivisible copies of each category \(g\), let \(d=\lceil3N/2\rceil\) and \(\theta_t=\max_{\mathcal P=(B_1,\ldots,B_d)}\min_{j\in[d]}v_t(B_j)\), where \(\mathcal P\) partitions the multiset of goods. Output a finite-support rational allocation law \(y_{t,b}\), for \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^r\), satisfying \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_g=\sigma_g\), and \(y_{t,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t(b)\ge\theta_t\), in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), \(\log N\), and the encoding length.

The input also contains a binary-encoded integer \(N\ge1\), with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\), and \(r\ge1\) good categories. Each category has supply \(\sigma_g\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(q_g=N\sigma_g\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\); let \(m=\sum_gq_g\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has nonnegative rational additive valuations \(v_{t,g}\). Let \(G\) contain \(q_g\) indistinguishable copies of category \(g\), set \(d=\lceil3N/2\rceil\), and define \(\theta_t=\max_{(B_1,\ldots,B_d)}\min_{1\le j\le d}v_t(B_j)\), where the maximum ranges over partitions of \(G\), allowing empty bundles. Thresholds are defined by the input, not supplied separately.
An allocation law is a finitely supported family \(y_{t,b}\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), indexed by \(t\in T\) and \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\), with \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_g=\sigma_g\), and \(y_{t,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t(b)\ge\theta_t\). Its positive entries and bundle vectors are output explicitly in binary; masses may split arbitrarily subject to these conditions.

A polynomial-time algorithm for the compressed allocation law

1. Result and proof strategy

The search problem is polynomial-time solvable in the requested parameters. In fact, the algorithm produces a law whose masses are integer multiples of \(1/N\), with only \(O(\tau(r+1)^2)\) positive entries.

The algorithm does not compute the thresholds \(\theta_t\). Instead, it computes auxiliary thresholds \(H_t\ge\theta_t\) using a homogeneous bag-filling procedure. It then constructs an allocation satisfying these stronger thresholds.

The main issue is compression: neither the \(N\) agents nor the \(m\) goods can be listed explicitly. We prove that the bag-filling procedure and the conversion back to category bundles can both be performed in polynomially many batches.

2. Preprocessing and the published lemma

Write \(n_t=N\mu_t\). By the input assumptions, every \(n_t\) is a positive integer and \(\sum_t n_t=N\).

For each type \(t\), multiply its valuations by a common positive integer denominator \(D_t\), obtaining nonnegative integers

\(a_{t,g}=D_t v_{t,g}\).

For example, the product of the denominators in that valuation row suffices. Its bit length, and the bit lengths of the resulting integers, are polynomial in \(L\). Let \(\Theta_t=D_t\theta_t\). Since every bundle has integer scaled value, \(\Theta_t\) is an integer.

For convenience, add a dummy category containing \(N\) goods valued at zero by every type. Let \(M=m+N\) be the augmented number of goods. This does not change any MMS threshold: removing dummy goods from a partition preserves every bundle value, and dummy goods can be added to any partition without changing its values. We discard dummy goods before producing the output.

We use the following published result.

Homogeneous bag-filling lemma. Take a finite list of goods with a nonnegative additive valuation, and sort the goods by decreasing value. Repeatedly start a bag with the largest remaining good and add smallest remaining goods until the bag reaches a specified threshold. When that threshold is the \(1\)-out-of-\(\lceil3N/2\rceil\) MMS, this procedure can complete \(N\) bags.

This is the guarantee proved in Lemma 4 of [Hosseini, Searns and Segal-Halevi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.01925), using their bidirectional bag-filling procedure. We apply it to each type’s augmented goods list. Its hypotheses hold because that list is finite and its scaled valuations are nonnegative and additive. A zero threshold is handled directly: the \(N\) dummy goods ensure that \(N\) bags can be started.

Everything concerning compression and the eventual allocation law is proved below.

3. Auxiliary thresholds without computing MMS

Fix a type \(t\). Let

\(x_{t,1}\ge x_{t,2}\ge\cdots\ge x_{t,M}\)

be its sorted list of scaled individual-good values, including the dummy goods.

For an integer \(h\ge0\), let \(P_t(h)\) mean that homogeneous bag filling can complete \(N\) bags at threshold \(h\). Define

\(H_t=\max\{h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:P_t(h)\}\).

This maximum exists. The predicate holds at zero, and every successful threshold is at most

\(U_t=\sum_g q_g a_{t,g}\).

The published lemma gives \(P_t(\Theta_t)\), so

\(H_t\ge\Theta_t\).

We next justify binary search for \(H_t\).

Lemma 1. The predicate \(P_t(h)\) is monotone: if it holds at \(h\), it holds at every integer \(h'\) with \(0\le h'\le h\).

Proof. Compare the two runs. Before their \(j\)-th bags, both have removed the first \(j-1\) goods as bag starters. All additional removals form a suffix of the sorted list.

Inductively, the lower-threshold run has removed no more suffix goods than the higher-threshold run. Thus its remaining right endpoint is at least as large.

Both start their \(j\)-th bags with good \(j\). If the lower-threshold run continues adding suffix goods down to the endpoint reached by the higher-threshold run, its bag contains every suffix good used in the higher-threshold bag, possibly together with additional goods. Nonnegativity implies that its value is at least the higher-threshold bag’s value, hence at least \(h'\). Therefore it stops at least as early.

This proves the induction and establishes monotonicity. \(\square\)

Consequently, binary search over the integers from zero to \(U_t\) computes \(H_t\), provided each homogeneous simulation can be performed in compressed form. Section 5 proves this.

4. The global procedure succeeds

For the moment, consider an expanded, conceptual instance.

For each rank \(j\), define an artificial good whose value to type \(t\) is \(x_{t,j}\). Thus all types agree on the decreasing order of artificial goods, although their values can differ.

Maintain \(n_t\) unallocated agents of type \(t\). Repeatedly:

  • Start a bag with the smallest-index remaining artificial good.
  • Add largest-index remaining goods until some remaining type \(t\) values the bag at least \(H_t\).
  • Allocate the bag to one agent of such a type and decrease its remaining count.

Fix a deterministic ordering of types to resolve ties.

Lemma 2. This procedure completes \(N\) bags.

Proof. For each type \(t\), consider its successful homogeneous simulation at threshold \(H_t\), with \(N\) clones.

After \(j\) bags, both the global procedure and any homogeneous simulation have removed the first \(j\) goods as starters, together with a suffix. We prove the following invariant:

For every type still present after \(j\) global bags, the global procedure’s remaining right endpoint is at least the endpoint after \(j\) bags in that type’s homogeneous simulation.

Initially this holds with equality. Suppose it holds before bag \(j+1\), and fix a currently remaining type \(t\). Both runs start their next bag with good \(j+1\). Starting from its possibly larger right endpoint, the global procedure would reach value at least \(H_t\) by the time it had added suffix goods down to the endpoint reached by \(t\)’s homogeneous simulation. Indeed, it would then contain all goods in that simulated bag, possibly with additional nonnegative-valued goods.

The global procedure stops as soon as any remaining type accepts. It therefore stops no later than this comparison point, preserving the invariant for every type that remains.

Each homogeneous simulation completes \(N\) bags. Hence a global bag can be completed whenever fewer than \(N\) agents have received one. \(\square\)

This argument establishes success, but does not yet establish the required running time. We now compress the procedure.

5. Bag filling takes polynomially many batches

5.1 Common valuation intervals

For each type, its sorted list has at most \(r+1\) constant blocks: one for each real category and one for the dummy category, with equal-valued adjacent blocks optionally merged.

Take the common refinement of all these block boundaries. This produces \(K=O(\tau(r+1))\) rank intervals. On each interval, the entire valuation vector

\((x_{t,j})_{t\in T}\)

is constant.

The intervals, their lengths and their valuation vectors can be constructed by sorting category values and merging binary-encoded cumulative counts. No individual goods need to be listed.

5.2 A regular batch

At any point, the remaining artificial goods occupy a rank interval \([\ell,R]\).

Let \(u_t=x_{t,\ell}\) be the starter value to type \(t\), and let \(w_t=x_{t,R}\) be the value of a good in the current rightmost common interval.

For each remaining type, calculate the number of goods from that rightmost interval needed after the starter:

\(k_t=0\) if \(u_t\ge H_t\);

\(k_t=\lceil(H_t-u_t)/w_t\rceil\) if \(u_t<H_t\) and \(w_t>0\);

\(k_t=+\infty\) otherwise.

Let \(k=\min_t k_t\), and choose the first minimizing type \(t\). If \(k\) suffix goods are available in that interval after taking the starter, this is exactly the next bag of the conceptual procedure.

While both endpoints remain in their current common intervals and type \(t\) remains present, the values \(u_t,w_t\), the minimum \(k\), and the chosen type stay unchanged. Therefore consecutive identical steps can be batched.

If the endpoint intervals are different, let their currently available lengths be \(A\) on the left and \(C\) on the right. The maximal batch size is

\(p=\min\{n_t^{\mathrm{rem}},A,\lfloor C/k\rfloor\}\)

when \(k>0\), and \(p=\min\{n_t^{\mathrm{rem}},A\}\) when \(k=0\).

If both endpoints lie in the same common interval, put \(C=R-\ell+1\). Then

\(p=\min\{n_t^{\mathrm{rem}},\lfloor C/(k+1)\rfloor\}\).

The batch allocates \(p\) bags to type \(t\), and updates

\(\ell\leftarrow\ell+p,\qquad R\leftarrow R-pk\).

All calculations use binary integers.

5.3 Bags crossing interval boundaries

If no type accepts using the starter and goods from the current rightmost interval alone, simulate one bag across common intervals.

For each interval, compute whether some remaining type would reach its threshold within the available copies there. This uses the same ceiling calculation, applied to the type’s current accumulated bag value. Either the bag finishes within that interval, or the entire available interval is added and the simulation proceeds to the next interval.

This requires polynomially many arithmetic operations and never iterates over individual copies.

Every such bag crosses a common-interval boundary, or exhausts the goods and terminates the procedure.

5.4 Bound on the number of batches

Each maximal regular batch does at least one of the following:

  • exhausts a type’s remaining agent count;
  • moves an endpoint past a common-interval boundary;
  • leaves too few goods in the rightmost interval for another regular bag.

In the last case, the next bag must cross an interval boundary or terminate. If both endpoints lie in the same interval, the analogous remainder is smaller than \(k+1\), so the next step terminates unless a type was exhausted.

There are at most \(\tau\) type-exhaustion events and at most \(2K\) endpoint-boundary events. Thus the number of regular batches and boundary-crossing bags is \(O(K+\tau)\).

The argument also applies to unsuccessful homogeneous simulations: exhaustion simply ends the simulation. Hence every binary-search predicate \(P_t(h)\) is computable in polynomial time.

Since each \(U_t\) has polynomial bit length, all thresholds \(H_t\), and then the successful global allocation, can be computed in the requested polynomial time.

6. Converting artificial goods to real categories

We must preserve individual bundle guarantees during this conversion; aggregating only total category consumption would not suffice.

6.1 Compressed records of artificial bundles

Each regular batch can be stored as a record \((t,p,a,R,k)\). It represents \(p\) agents, indexed by \(j\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\). Agent \(j\) receives:

  • the starter at rank \(a+j\);
  • the \(k\) suffix ranks from \(R-(j+1)k+1\) through \(R-jk\).

A boundary-crossing bag has the same representation with \(p=1\), because its suffix goods still form one contiguous rank interval.

Let \(B=O(K+\tau)\) be the number of records.

6.2 Picking real goods

Visit all allocated artificial ranks in increasing order. At a rank allocated to type \(t\), give its recipient a remaining real or dummy good of maximum value to \(t\). Ties between categories are resolved deterministically. Unallocated artificial ranks are skipped.

Lemma 3. At every visited rank \(i\), the selected good has scaled value at least \(x_{t,i}\) to its recipient’s type \(t\).

Proof. At most \(i-1\) goods have previously been selected. Among type \(t\)’s \(i\) highest-valued augmented goods, at least one therefore remains. Its value is at least \(x_{t,i}\). Choosing a maximum-valued remaining good gives at least that value. \(\square\)

Summing this inequality over each agent’s assigned ranks shows that its real bundle has scaled value at least \(H_t\), hence original value at least \(\theta_t\).

6.3 Executing the picking sequence without expansion

For each record, its starter ranks form one interval assigned to a single type. Its suffix ranks, when nonempty, also form one interval assigned to that type. Thus the allocated ranks can be processed as at most \(2B\) intervals of constant recipient type.

During one such interval, the type repeatedly selects its best remaining category. Process this by taking as many copies as possible from that category, then moving to its next-best available category. There are at most \(r+1\) category segments per interval, including dummy goods.

Therefore the complete picking sequence can be executed using polynomially many count operations.

6.4 Recovering individual bundle patterns

Consider one record with \(p\) agents.

Its starter picks are divided into at most \(r+1\) category segments. Consequently, the starter category changes at most \(r\) times as the agent index \(j\) varies.

Its suffix picks likewise have at most \(r+1\) category segments. The suffix chunk of each agent has exactly \(k\) consecutive ranks, and these chunks are disjoint. A boundary between two category segments can therefore lie inside at most one agent’s chunk.

For each suffix-category boundary:

  • if it lies between chunks, split the agent-index range there;
  • if it lies inside a chunk, isolate that agent as a singleton.

Also split at every starter-category change. These operations divide the \(p\) agents into \(O(r+1)\) groups, each having one identical category-count vector. For a singleton whose suffix crosses a category boundary, its vector is calculated by intersecting its chunk with the recorded category segments.

All endpoints, divisions and intersection lengths are calculated in binary. Dummy coordinates are discarded.

Across all records, this produces \(O(B(r+1))=O(\tau(r+1)^2)\) bundle groups.

Finally, give all unselected real goods to one already allocated agent. Nonnegativity preserves its guarantee. If that agent belonged to a group containing multiple agents, split off one agent and modify only its bundle vector. This adds at most one group.

7. Producing and verifying the allocation law

For every resulting group of \(c\) agents of type \(t\), each receiving category vector \(b\), output mass

\(y_{t,b}=c/N\).

Combine entries with identical pairs \((t,b)\).

For each type, exactly \(n_t\) agents were allocated, so

\(\sum_b y_{t,b}=n_t/N=\mu_t\).

Every real good is allocated exactly once, including the final leftovers. Therefore, for every category \(g\),

\(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_g=q_g/N=\sigma_g\).

Every positively weighted bundle satisfies

\(D_t v_t(b)\ge H_t\ge\Theta_t=D_t\theta_t\).

Since \(D_t>0\), this implies \(v_t(b)\ge\theta_t\).

The output therefore satisfies all requirements of the allocation law.

8. Bit complexity and conclusion

All rank endpoints and counts are bounded by \(m+N\), so their bit lengths are polynomial in \(\log N+\log(m+1)\). Scaled values and threshold-search bounds have bit lengths polynomial in \(L+\log(m+1)\).

There are:

  • \(O(\tau(r+1))\) common valuation intervals;
  • polynomially many binary-search simulations;
  • \(O(\tau(r+1))\) global bag records;
  • \(O(\tau(r+1)^2)\) output groups.

Each output mass has the form \(c/N\), and each bundle coordinate is at most \(q_g\). Thus both the computation and the explicit output size are polynomial in \(\tau,r,\log N,\log(m+1),L\).

This resolves the stated search problem: arbitrary splitting is allowed, but the construction already succeeds with masses that are integer multiples of \(1/N\).
