The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over user types, where each type \(t\) specifies a MOCO instance, its criterion values, an initial admissible OWA-weight polytope, and a pairwise-comparison oracle, can a protocol run RIGA for each type and return a policy \(t\mapsto x_t\) in polynomial total computation and queries, with candidate-set maximum regret at most \(\delta\) for every type? The policy’s expected candidate-set regret is \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{MR}_t(x_t,Q_t,\Omega_t)\).

Partial results for the prescribed fixed-order RIGA

1. Scope and conclusions

The general complexity question remains unresolved. The following results substantially narrow it:

  • Exact solving on a weight polytope of affine dimension at most one: the prescribed algorithm terminates with at most $\binom{S}{2}$ comparisons per generation, for every fixed tie order. This includes the exact two-criterion case and populations containing arbitrarily many distinct candidates.
  • Exact solving with three criteria: an admissible population of three candidates can cause nontermination, even when every initial candidate is the unique exact optimum at its associated strictly positive weight.
  • Approximate solving with two criteria: every positive solver tolerance permits a nonterminating example, even when the elicitation threshold is arbitrarily large relative to that tolerance.

These distinguish two quantifiers. A nonterminating admissible execution disproves a guarantee covering all permitted populations and executions. It does not disprove the existence of a specially chosen polynomial implementation.

A singleton candidate set gives a trivial existential implementation with zero candidate-set regret. The substantive discussion below concerns populations with multiple distinct candidates; the general existential question under that reading remains open.

2. Exact regret computation for a generated population

Fix one supported type and suppress its index. Write $v(x)$ for the criterion vector of $x$, sorted increasingly. Then

$$ \operatorname{OWA}(x,\omega)=v(x)^{\mathsf T}\omega. $$

An answer preferring $a$ to $b$ adds

$$ \bigl(v(a)-v(b)\bigr)^{\mathsf T}\omega\leq 0. $$

Consistency with the fixed unknown weight $\omega^*$ means that $\omega^*$ remains feasible after every update. Hence the current polytope $\Omega$ is nonempty. It is compact because it is a closed subset of the simplex.

For every ordered candidate pair,

$$ \operatorname{PMR}(x,z,\Omega) = \max_{\omega\in\Omega} \bigl(v(x)-v(z)\bigr)^{\mathsf T}\omega. $$

This is an explicit rational linear program with a nonempty feasible region and a bounded objective. The standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore computes its exact rational optimal value in time polynomial in the encoded constraints and objective.

For $n=|Q|$, at most $n^2$ such programs determine all maximum regrets, the minimax regret, and the candidates selected by the fixed tie order. Exact comparison with $\delta$, including $\delta=0$, is consequently possible.

Also,

$$ \operatorname{PMR}(x,x,\Omega)=0, $$

so every candidate’s maximum regret is nonnegative.

This proves polynomial computation in the current encoded population and polytope size. Bounding that size requires a comparison bound, supplied next for a nontrivial subclass.

3. A polynomial bound with exact solving and one-dimensional weights

3.1 Hypotheses and theorem

Assume for this type that:

  • $\varepsilon=0$;
  • $\Omega^0$ has affine dimension at most one;
  • every candidate is generated by the exact solver at a weight in $\Omega^0$.

The last condition can be maintained by choosing rational initial weights in $\Omega^0$, using convex-combination crossovers, and disabling mutation.

Theorem. Under these hypotheses, the prescribed fixed-order elicitation loop queries each unordered candidate pair at most once during a generation. It therefore terminates after at most $\binom{|Q|}{2}$ comparisons.

The proof rests on the following lemma.

3.2 A necessarily dominated candidate cannot be a positive-regret minimizer

Suppose first that $\Omega^0$ has affine dimension one. Because it is a nonempty compact convex set, it is a segment. Parameterize it affinely as

$$ \Omega^0=\{\omega(s):0\leq s\leq 1\}. $$

For a fixed population $Q$, define

$$ f_x(s)=v(x)^{\mathsf T}\omega(s), \qquad g(s)=\min_{z\in Q}f_z(s), \qquad h_x(s)=f_x(s)-g(s). $$

Each $f_x$ is affine. Moreover,

$$ h_x(s)=\max_{z\in Q}\bigl(f_x(s)-f_z(s)\bigr), $$

so $h_x$ is convex and nonnegative.

Every $x\in Q$ was generated by exact solving at some $\omega(s_x)\in\Omega^0$. Since $Q\subseteq X$ and $x\in Q$, exact optimality over $X$ implies

$$ h_x(s_x)=0. $$

The current nonempty polytope corresponds to a closed interval $J=[\alpha,\beta]\subseteq[0,1]$, possibly a singleton. Taking maxima over the product of the finite set $Q$ and the compact interval $J$ gives

$$ \operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega) = \max_{s\in J}h_x(s). $$

Now suppose $u$ is necessarily weakly preferred to $x$ on the current polytope:

$$ f_u(s)\leq f_x(s) \quad\text{for every }s\in J, $$

and suppose additionally that

$$ \operatorname{PMR}(x,u,\Omega)>0. $$

Set

$$ D(s)=f_x(s)-f_u(s). $$

Thus $D$ is affine, nonnegative on $J$, and positive somewhere on $J$. We will prove that whenever $\operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega)>0$,

$$ \operatorname{MR}(u,Q,\Omega) < \operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega). $$

If $D$ is positive everywhere on $J$, compactness gives $d=\min_{s\in J}D(s)>0$. Since $h_u=h_x-D$,

$$ \max_{s\in J}h_u(s) \leq \max_{s\in J}h_x(s)-d, $$

as required. This also covers a singleton $J$.

Otherwise, $J$ is nondegenerate. A nonzero affine function that is nonnegative on an interval can vanish only at an endpoint. Reverse the parameterization if necessary so that

$$ D(\alpha)=0, \qquad D(\beta)>0. $$

The slope of $D$ is then positive. Exact optimality of $x$ at $s_x$ implies

$$ D(s_x)=f_x(s_x)-f_u(s_x)\leq 0, $$

and hence $s_x\leq\alpha$.

We next show that

$$ h_x(\beta)>h_x(\alpha). $$

If $h_x(\alpha)=0$, this follows from positive maximum regret and the fact that a convex function on an interval attains its maximum at an endpoint.

If $h_x(\alpha)>0$, then $s_x<\alpha$, because $h_x(s_x)=0$. Convexity on $[s_x,\beta]$ yields

$$ h_x(\alpha) \leq \frac{\alpha-s_x}{\beta-s_x}h_x(\beta). $$

The coefficient lies strictly between zero and one, so again $h_x(\beta)>h_x(\alpha)$.

Consequently,

$$ \operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega)=h_x(\beta). $$

At the two endpoints,

$$ h_u(\alpha)=h_x(\alpha)<h_x(\beta), $$

and

$$ h_u(\beta)=h_x(\beta)-D(\beta)<h_x(\beta). $$

Convexity of $h_u$ now gives

$$ \operatorname{MR}(u,Q,\Omega) = \max\{h_u(\alpha),h_u(\beta)\} < \operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega). $$

This proves the lemma: a positive-regret minimax candidate cannot be necessarily weakly dominated by a candidate against which it has positive regret.

If $\Omega^0$ has affine dimension zero, it consists of one weight. All exact solver-generated candidates have the same optimal value at that weight, so their maximum regrets are zero. No elicitation is needed.

3.3 Why no candidate pair can be repeated

Suppose the loop selects minimax candidate $x$ and maximum-regret opponent $z$. Because the loop is active,

$$ \operatorname{PMR}(x,z,\Omega) = \operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega) > \delta\geq 0. $$

We must also have

$$ \operatorname{PMR}(z,x,\Omega)>0. $$

Indeed, if that reverse regret were nonpositive, then $z$ would be necessarily weakly preferred to $x$. The lemma would contradict minimax optimality of $x$.

After the comparison, the returned preference makes one direction’s pairwise maximum regret nonpositive. It remains nonpositive under every subsequent restriction of $\Omega$.

That direction cannot be queried again, because a queried direction has positive regret. The reverse direction cannot be queried either: it would select a candidate necessarily weakly dominated by its opponent, contrary to the lemma.

Thus each unordered pair is queried at most once. There are only $\binom{|Q|}{2}$ such pairs, proving the theorem. The argument does not change the fixed tie order.

3.4 This theorem covers populations with many distinct candidates

For any integer $N\geq 1$, consider the explicitly listed feasible alternatives $x_0,\ldots,x_N$ with criterion vectors

$$ y(x_i)=\bigl(i^2,(N-i)^2+N^2\bigr). $$

These vectors are increasingly sorted, because

$$ (N-i)^2+N^2-i^2=2N(N-i)\geq 0. $$

Take the full two-dimensional weight simplex and seed

$$ \omega^i=\left(1-\frac{i}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right). $$

For every $j$,

$$ \operatorname{OWA}(x_j,\omega^i) - \operatorname{OWA}(x_i,\omega^i) = (j-i)^2. $$

Hence $x_i$ is the unique exact optimum at $\omega^i$. Initializing with these pairs gives $S=N+1$ distinct candidates. Evaluating the explicitly listed alternatives is an exact polynomial-time known-weight solver.

Thus the theorem is not restricted to singleton or two-candidate populations.

4. Exact solving with three criteria can still fail to terminate

We now give a stronger counterexample than one based on initially dominated candidates.

Let there be one type, with mass $1$, feasible set $X=\{b,a,c\}$, and increasingly sorted criterion vectors

$$ v(b)=(1,4,6), \qquad v(a)=(1,3,7), \qquad v(c)=(0,4,7). $$

Let $\Omega^0$ be the full three-coordinate simplex. Set

$$ \varepsilon=0, \qquad \delta=0, \qquad S=3, \qquad M=1, \qquad K=1. $$

Use fixed candidate order $b,a,c$. The exact known-weight solver evaluates the three alternatives and returns a minimum.

4.1 Every initialized candidate is uniquely optimal at a positive weight

Initialize with

$$ (\omega^b,b),\qquad(\omega^a,a),\qquad(\omega^c,c), $$

where

$$ \omega^b=\frac18(1,1,6), \qquad \omega^a=\frac18(1,6,1), \qquad \omega^c=\frac18(6,1,1). $$

Every coordinate is strictly positive. Direct evaluation gives:

| Seed | Value of $b$ | Value of $a$ | Value of $c$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $\omega^b$ | $41/8$ | $46/8$ | $46/8$ |
| $\omega^a$ | $31/8$ | $26/8$ | $31/8$ |
| $\omega^c$ | $16/8$ | $16/8$ | $11/8$ |

The associated candidate is therefore uniquely optimal at each seed. The population is already full.

4.2 The first query is informative

Over the full simplex, maximizing a linear functional gives the largest coordinate of its coefficient vector. The three forward differences are

$$ v(b)-v(a)=(0,1,-1), $$

$$ v(b)-v(c)=(1,0,-1), $$

$$ v(a)-v(c)=(1,-1,0), $$

and the reverse differences are their negatives. Every off-diagonal pairwise maximum regret is consequently $1$. Thus

$$ \operatorname{MR}(b,Q,\Omega^0) = \operatorname{MR}(a,Q,\Omega^0) = \operatorname{MR}(c,Q,\Omega^0) =1. $$

The fixed order selects $b$, then opponent $a$.

Choose the unknown weight $\omega^*=\omega^a$. The oracle strictly prefers $a$ to $b$, by a difference of $5/8$. Its answer adds

$$ -\omega_2+\omega_3\leq 0. $$

This first update genuinely changes the polytope, because it excludes the third unit vector.

4.3 Every subsequent query repeats without progress

The updated region is

$$ \Omega^1 = \{\omega\in\Omega^0:\omega_3\leq\omega_2\} = \operatorname{conv} \left\{ e_1,e_2,\frac12(e_2+e_3) \right\}. $$

To verify the last equality, every point of $\Omega^1$ has the decomposition

$$ \omega = \omega_1e_1 + (\omega_2-\omega_3)e_2 + 2\omega_3\frac12(e_2+e_3), $$

whose coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one.

Evaluating the difference vectors at these three vertices gives:

| Candidate $x$ | $\operatorname{PMR}(x,b)$ | $\operatorname{PMR}(x,a)$ | $\operatorname{PMR}(x,c)$ | $\operatorname{MR}(x,Q,\Omega^1)$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $b$ | $0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $a$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $c$ | $1/2$ | $1$ | $0$ | $1$ |

All candidates remain minimax. The fixed order again selects $b$ and opponent $a$.

The oracle again strictly prefers $a$. Its inequality is already imposed, so the polytope remains $\Omega^1$. The same calculation and selection repeat indefinitely.

This example uses exact solving, distinct candidates, strictly positive seed weights, and unique initial optima. It shows that those additional safeguards do not establish a universal termination guarantee.

Combined with Section 3, it also shows that affine dimension two already permits failure, whereas affine dimension at most one prevents it under exact solving.

5. Positive solver tolerance permits failure with two criteria

The exactness assumption in Section 3 is essential. We prove this for every rational tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ and every rational threshold $\delta\geq 0$.

Define

$$ r=\delta+\varepsilon+1, \qquad T=\frac{r^2}{\varepsilon}. $$

Then $r>\delta$ and $r>\varepsilon$. Use three increasingly sorted vectors

$$ v(b)=(r,2r), \qquad v(a)=(r,r), \qquad v(c)=(0,2r+T), $$

and let $\Omega^0$ be the full two-coordinate simplex.

Supply the following uniform polynomial-time approximate solver: evaluate all three alternatives, compute their minimum value, and return the first alternative in order $b,a,c$ whose value is at most that minimum plus $\varepsilon$. Its approximation guarantee follows directly from its selection rule.

Writing $\omega=(1-p,p)$, the three values are

$$ f_b(p)=r+rp, \qquad f_a(p)=r, \qquad f_c(p)=(2r+T)p. $$

Set

$$ p_0=\frac{r}{T+2r}. $$

At $p_0$, both $a$ and $c$ have value $r$, whereas the excess value of $b$ is

$$ f_b(p_0)-r = \frac{r^2}{T+2r} = \frac{r\varepsilon}{r+2\varepsilon} < \varepsilon. $$

The supplied solver therefore returns $b$ at $(1-p_0,p_0)$. At $e_2$ it returns $a$, and at $e_1$ it returns $c$, because in each case $b$ exceeds the minimum by $r>\varepsilon$.

Initialize with these three pairs and take $S=3$, $M=1$, and $K=1$.

Over the full simplex,

$$ \operatorname{MR}(b,Q,\Omega^0)=r, \qquad \operatorname{MR}(a,Q,\Omega^0)=r, \qquad \operatorname{MR}(c,Q,\Omega^0)=r+T. $$

Indeed,

$$ v(b)-v(a)=(0,r), \qquad v(b)-v(c)=(r,-T), \qquad v(a)-v(c)=(r,-r-T), $$

which give these regrets by coordinatewise maximization.

The fixed order selects $b$. Both $a$ and $c$ attain its maximum regret $r$, so it selects opponent $a$.

Choose $\omega^*=e_2$. The oracle strictly prefers $a$, adding

$$ -r\omega_2\leq 0. $$

This is redundant. Therefore the same query repeats forever while minimax regret remains

$$ r>\delta. $$

All constructed numbers have encoding length polynomial in that of $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$. In particular, failure is possible even when $\delta$ is much larger than $\varepsilon$.

6. Polynomial computation and the distributional certificate in the proved subclass

Suppose every supported type satisfies the exact, affine-dimension-at-most-one hypotheses of Section 3.

The total comparison count is at most

$$ \tau M\binom{S}{2}. $$

Each generation needs at most one initial regret computation and one after each answer. Each computation uses at most $S^2$ linear programs. The total number of regret linear programs is therefore

$$ O(\tau M S^4). $$

Polynomial bit complexity can be ensured as follows. Obtain rational seed weights by rational linear programming and use midpoint crossovers with mutation disabled. Let $D$ be the product of all seed-coordinate denominators. Its binary length is bounded by their total encoding length.

After $H$ midpoint operations, every generated coordinate has denominator dividing

$$ D2^H. $$

This follows inductively: one averaging operation introduces at most one additional factor of two into a common denominator. Since coordinates remain in the simplex, numerator lengths are bounded as well. With polynomially many generations and population entries, $H$ is polynomial.

The uniform polynomial-time solver and criterion evaluator consequently produce polynomial-size solutions and rational criterion values. The comparison bound then ensures polynomial-size current polytopes. Retention can compare squared Euclidean distances using exact rational arithmetic.

Thus this subclass has polynomial total computation and comparison queries in the requested parameters.

More generally, for any terminating execution, including approximate solving, the final minimax choice satisfies

$$ \operatorname{MR}_t(x_t,Q_t,\Omega_t)\leq\delta. $$

Since the listed positive masses sum to one,

$$ \sum_t\mu_t\operatorname{MR}_t(x_t,Q_t,\Omega_t) \leq \sum_t\mu_t\delta = \delta. $$

No additive $\varepsilon_t$ enters this candidate-set certificate: approximation affects candidate generation, while regret is computed exactly over the generated candidates. This does not certify regret against every feasible alternative in $X_t$.

  • A polynomial implementation for the general case, maintaining the prescribed rule and substantively generating multiple candidates, has not been established here.
