The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite rational metric \((X,d)\), candidate stops \(C\subseteq X\), budget \(k\), and supported route types \(t=(a_t,b_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), under null transit cost, determine the complexity of finding \(Y\subseteq C\), \(|Y|\leq k\), minimizing \(\sum_{t}\mu_t\min\{d(a_t,b_t),d(a_t,Y)+d(b_t,Y)\}\), as a function of \(m=|C|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); take \(d(x,\emptyset)=+\infty\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite metric \((X,d)\) by its binary-rational distance matrix, candidate stops \(C\subseteq X\), and a positive integer budget \(k\), let \(m=|C|\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct route types \(t=(a_t,b_t)\in X^2\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; endpoints may coincide with each other or with candidate stops.
Transit between selected stops has zero cost. A feasible placement is a subset \(Y\subseteq C\) with \(|Y|\leq k\). Define \(d(x,Y)=\min_{y\in Y}d(x,y)\), with \(d(x,\emptyset)=+\infty\), and \(F(Y)=\sum_t\mu_t\min\{d(a_t,b_t),d(a_t,Y)+d(b_t,Y)\}\).
The decision input additionally contains a nonnegative binary-rational threshold \(B\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Is deciding whether some feasible placement satisfies \(F(Y)\leq B\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

For a finite list of agents, minimizing total travel cost in general metric spaces is NP-hard even with zero transit cost. [Aziz et al.](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/busstoppaper.pdf)
No separate complexity result for this rational-mass formulation was verified.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(C=X\), every distance between distinct points is \(1\), and all masses are dyadic rationals.

There are nevertheless exact algorithms polynomial for fixed \(k\) or fixed \(\tau\). Writing \(r=\min\{k,m\}\), their bit running times include:

  • Placement enumeration: \(\bigl(1+\binom mr\,\tau(r+1)\bigr)L^{O(1)}\).
  • An algorithm parameterized by support size: \(\bigl(1+m\tau5^\tau+\min\{k,m,2\tau\}10^\tau\bigr)L^{O(1)}\).

The following proofs apply directly to the support representation; they do not expand rational masses into lists of agents.

1. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a subset \(Y\subseteq C\) of size at most \(r\). For nonempty \(Y\), computing both nearest-stop distances for every route uses \(O(\tau r)\) distance comparisons. We then evaluate \(F(Y)\) exactly and compare it with \(B\). For \(Y=\emptyset\), we instead evaluate \(\sum_t\mu_t d(a_t,b_t)\).

These computations have polynomial bit complexity. To see this explicitly, let \(D\) be the product of all denominators appearing in the input. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Every product \(\mu_t d(x,z)\), and also \(B\), has denominator dividing \(D\). Thus all objective calculations can use integer multiples of \(1/D\), with integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Here the bound also uses \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), so weighted sums of endpoint distances are at most twice the largest input distance.

Verification therefore takes \(\bigl(1+\tau(r+1)\bigr)L^{O(1)}\) bit operations. Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. A direct hardness reduction

We reduce from CLIQUE: given a finite simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) and a positive integer \(s\), decide whether \(G\) contains a clique of size at least \(s\). Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness is a standard result of [Karp](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Write \(n=|V|\), \(q=|E|\), and \(K=\binom{s}{2}\).

The cases \(s=1\), \(s>n\), and \(q<K\) can be answered directly and mapped to fixed yes or no instances. Such fixed instances exist: take two points \(u,v\) at distance \(1\), candidates \(\{u,v\}\), the single route \((u,v)\) of mass \(1\), and threshold \(0\). Budget \(2\) gives a yes instance; budget \(1\) gives a no instance.

We may therefore assume \(2\leq s\leq n\) and \(q\geq K\geq1\).

Construction

Set \(X=C=V\), let \(k=s\), and use the discrete metric:

\[ \]

\(d(u,v)=0\) if \(u=v\), and \(d(u,v)=1\) otherwise.

This is a metric: if \(u\neq v\), then for any intermediate point \(z\), at least one of \(d(u,z)\) and \(d(z,v)\) equals \(1\).

Let \(Q=2^{\lceil\log_2 q\rceil}\). Fix an ordering of \(V\). For each edge \(\{u,v\}\), include exactly one route \((u,v)\), oriented according to that ordering, with mass \(1/Q\).

If \(q<Q\), choose any \(v_0\in V\) and add the route \((v_0,v_0)\) with mass \((Q-q)/Q\). If \(q=Q\), add no extra route.

All listed types are distinct, all masses are positive dyadic rationals, and their sum is \(1\). The extra route always costs \(0\), because its direct distance is \(0\).

Finally, set \(B=(q-K)/Q\), which is nonnegative.

Objective calculation

Let \(e_G(Y)\) denote the number of edges with both endpoints in \(Y\).

An edge route costs \(0\) exactly when both endpoints belong to \(Y\). Indeed, for nonempty \(Y\), each endpoint’s nearest-stop distance is \(0\) if selected and \(1\) otherwise. Thus the transit expression is \(0\) when both endpoints are selected and at least \(1\) otherwise; the direct distance is \(1\). For empty \(Y\), the route also costs \(1\).

Consequently, for every placement,

\(F(Y)=\dfrac{q-e_G(Y)}{Q}.\)

Therefore,

\(F(Y)\leq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e_G(Y)\geq K.\)

Correctness

If \(G\) has a clique of size at least \(s\), select any \(s\) of its vertices. The resulting placement has \(e_G(Y)=K\), so \(F(Y)=B\).

Conversely, suppose \(|Y|\leq s\) and \(F(Y)\leq B\). Then

\(K\leq e_G(Y)\leq\binom{|Y|}{2}\leq\binom{s}{2}=K.\)

All inequalities must be equalities. Since \(s\geq2\), this forces \(|Y|=s\). Moreover, \(e_G(Y)=\binom{s}{2}\), so every pair of vertices in \(Y\) is adjacent. Hence \(Y\) is a clique.

The output has \(m=n\), support size at most \(q+1\), and an explicitly written distance matrix with \(n^2\) entries. Masses and threshold have \(O(\log(q+1))\)-bit encodings. The construction is therefore polynomial in the CLIQUE input length.

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. Together with Section 1, it proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

3. Exact enumeration and fixed budgets

If \(m=0\), only the empty placement is feasible, so direct evaluation suffices.

Otherwise, adding stops cannot increase either nearest-stop distance and therefore cannot increase \(F\). Every feasible placement can consequently be extended to a placement of exactly \(r=\min\{k,m\}\) stops without increasing its cost.

Enumerating all \(\binom mr\) such placements gives the bit running time

\(\bigl(1+\binom mr\,\tau(r+1)\bigr)L^{O(1)}.\)

In particular, for fixed \(k\), the problem is polynomial-time solvable, with the bound

\(\bigl(1+m^k\tau(k+1)\bigr)L^{O(1)}.\)

For \(k=1\), triangle inequality gives \(d(a_t,b_t)\leq d(a_t,y)+d(b_t,y)\) for every candidate \(y\). Thus every feasible placement has the direct-walking cost, and this case requires only direct evaluation.

4. An exact algorithm for fixed support size

Assume \(m\geq1\). Index the support by \([\tau]=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), and write \(\delta_t=d(a_t,b_t)\).

For \(A\subseteq[\tau]\), define

\(H_A=\min_{\substack{Y\subseteq C\\|Y|\leq k}}\sum_{t\in A}\mu_t\bigl(d(a_t,Y)+d(b_t,Y)\bigr),\)

with \(H_\emptyset=0\). For nonempty \(A\), the empty placement has infinite cost in this expression.

The following identity is exact:

\(\min_{\substack{Y\subseteq C\\|Y|\leq k}}F(Y)=\min_{A\subseteq[\tau]}\left(\sum_{t\notin A}\mu_t\delta_t+H_A\right).\)

For any fixed \(Y\), independently choosing the direct or transit expression for each route gives precisely its minimum cost. These choices are indexed by \(A\). Minimizing over the finite choices of \(A\) and \(Y\) in either order proves the identity.

Computing \(H_A\) by subset dynamic programming

Fix nonempty \(A\), with \(a=|A|\). Create \(p=2a\) labeled endpoint occurrences, one for each endpoint of each route in \(A\). An occurrence from route \(t\) has weight \(\mu_t\). Labels remain distinct even when their locations coincide.

Let \(J\) be this occurrence set. For \(S\subseteq J\), define

\(g(S)=\min_{c\in C}\sum_{j\in S}w_jd(x_j,c),\qquad g(\emptyset)=0.\)

All values \(g(S)\) can be computed in \(O(mp2^p)\) arithmetic operations by scanning candidates and occurrences.

Let \(D_h(S)\) be the minimum sum of \(g\)-values over partitions of \(S\) into at most \(h\) nonempty groups. Initialize \(D_0(\emptyset)=0\) and \(D_0(S)=+\infty\) for nonempty \(S\). The recurrence is

\(D_h(S)=\min_{T\subseteq S}\bigl(D_{h-1}(S\setminus T)+g(T)\bigr).\)

An empty \(T\) uses no additional group; a nonempty \(T\) specifies one group. Removing one group from any partition proves the recurrence.

Set \(h_A=\min\{k,m,p\}\). Then \(H_A=D_{h_A}(J)\), as follows.

  • Given a placement \(Y\), assign every occurrence to a nearest selected stop. The nonempty assignment groups number at most \(h_A\). For each group, \(g(S)\) is no larger than its cost at the assigned stop. Hence \(D_{h_A}(J)\leq H_A\).
  • Given an optimal partition, select a candidate attaining \(g(S)\) for each group. Their union contains at most \(h_A\) stops. Each occurrence’s nearest-stop distance is no larger than its distance to its group’s chosen stop. Hence \(H_A\leq D_{h_A}(J)\).

This proves equality, including when different groups choose the same candidate.

For each dynamic-programming layer, the number of pairs \(T\subseteq S\subseteq J\) is \(3^p\): each occurrence lies outside \(S\), in \(S\setminus T\), or in \(T\). Thus computing \(H_A\) takes

\(O\bigl(mp2^p+h_A3^p\bigr)\)

arithmetic operations.

Total running time

Let \(h_*=\min\{k,m,2\tau\}\). Summing over all route subsets gives

\(\sum_{a=0}^{\tau}\binom{\tau}{a}4^a=5^\tau,\qquad \sum_{a=0}^{\tau}\binom{\tau}{a}9^a=10^\tau,\)

and

\(\sum_{a=0}^{\tau}\binom{\tau}{a}(2a)4^a=8\tau5^{\tau-1}.\)

Therefore the complete algorithm takes

\(\bigl(1+m\tau5^\tau+h_*10^\tau\bigr)L^{O(1)}\)

bit operations. The polynomial bit factor follows from the same common-denominator calculation used in Section 1.

Hence the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and polynomial-time solvable for every fixed support size, despite being \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete when the parameters vary with the input.
