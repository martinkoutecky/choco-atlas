The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite policy schema, complete case types \(T\), and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), represent a population interpretation by masses \(x_{t,S}\) assigned to local stable states \(S\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). Lift PAS's strata and conflict comparisons so a preference rule witnesses a comparison only on positive mass. Decide whether some PAS-preferred population interpretation gives \(G\) positive mass, and whether every such interpretation gives \(G\) mass one.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input consists of a finite propositional policy \(\Pi=(P,\Phi)\) over \(m\) atoms, a designated set \(E\) of exogenous atoms absent from rule heads of \(P\), and a query atom \(G\). \(P\) is a disjunctive logic program with default negation and constraints; exclusive disjunction means exactly one head atom when the body holds. Preference rules have form \(a_1>\cdots>a_k\leftarrow B\), with \(k>1\) and \(B\) a conjunction of atoms and default-negated atoms.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) complete types \(t\subseteq E\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length and \(r\) the number of policy rules; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,r\).
For each type, let \(\mathcal S_t=\operatorname{SM}(P\cup\{e\leftarrow:e\in t\})\); these local stable states are implicit, not supplied as a list. A population interpretation is a family of real masses \(x_{t,S}\geq0\), indexed by \(S\in\mathcal S_t\), satisfying \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\). Define \(g(x)=\sum_{t,S:G\in S}x_{t,S}\).
Split preference chains into adjacent binary rules and take their conditional transitive closure \(\Phi^*\): compose \(a>b\leftarrow B\) and \(b>c\leftarrow C\) into \(a>c\leftarrow B\land C\) when \(a\ne c\). Treat bodies as sets of signed literals.
To obtain strata, form a graph on policy atoms and a fresh \(\mathsf{true}\): preference rules give edges from every body atom to every head atom, of weight \(1\); generating rules give edges from every head or body atom to every head atom, of weight \(0\). Ignore negation signs and treat empty bodies as \(\mathsf{true}\). Collapse strongly connected components; a source has level \(0\), and every other component has the maximum predecessor level plus edge weight. A closure rule's stratum is the maximum level of its body atoms, with empty bodies at level \(0\).
For \(\rho:a>b\leftarrow B\), put \(R_{t,\rho}(x)=\sum_{S\models B\land(a\lor b)}x_{t,S}\). Opposite-head rules are conflicting. Interpret \(R_{t,\rho}(x)>0\) as relevance on positive mass.
Populations \(x,y\) are comparable at stratum \(0\). They remain comparable at stratum \(i+1\) precisely when they were comparable at \(i\) and there are no type \(t\) and conflicting rules \(\rho,\sigma\) in stratum \(i\) with \(R_{t,\rho}(x)>0=R_{t,\sigma}(x)\) and \(R_{t,\sigma}(y)>0=R_{t,\rho}(y)\).
A rule \(\rho:a>b\leftarrow B\) witnesses \(x\) over \(y\) if some \(t\) and \(S,U\in\mathcal S_t\) satisfy \(x_{t,S}>y_{t,S}\), \(y_{t,U}>x_{t,U}\), \(S,U\models B\), \(a\in S\setminus U\), and \(b\in U\setminus S\).
Define \(x\succeq y\) when a rule in some stratum \(i\) witnesses \(x\) over \(y\), the populations are comparable there, and no rule in any stratum \(j<i\) witnesses \(y\) over \(x\). Define \(x\succ y\) by \(x\succeq y\) and \(y\not\succeq x\), without taking a transitive closure. A population is PAS-preferred if no feasible population strictly dominates it.
Are each of the following decision problems in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard: does some PAS-preferred population \(x\) satisfy \(g(x)>0\), and does every PAS-preferred population \(x\) satisfy \(g(x)=1\)? Universal quantification over an empty preferred set is true.
Discrete PAS atom truth in some preferred stable model is \(\Sigma_3^p\)-complete, and truth in every preferred stable model is \(\Pi_3^p\)-complete, under data complexity; for disjunction-free generating programs the corresponding bounds are \(\Sigma_2^p\)- and \(\Pi_2^p\)-completeness. [Source](https://www.ed.aaai.org/Papers/JAIR/Vol30/JAIR-3013.pdf).
No result settling this exact population formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The undefined policy schema is an input propositional prioritized program; local stable states remain implicitly represented.
  • Complete case types specify all exogenous atoms, which cannot be derived by the generating program; each type supplies its true atoms as facts.
  • Positive type masses sum to one, making query mass one mean the entire population; interpretation masses range over nonnegative reals.
  • Positive-mass comparison witnesses use excess and deficit mass at local states of the same type, with the preference body satisfied in both states and the preferred and dispreferred atoms in their respective set differences.
  • Conflict relevance is measured separately for each type; the discrete exclusive-relevance condition becomes positive relevance for one rule and zero relevance for its opposite.
  • Strata retain empty levels and use the weighted component graph recurrence; the inconsistent stratum indexing in the examples does not override the formal definitions.
  • Strict preference uses the two-direction definition, and population preferences receive no transitive closure.
  • No existence promise is imposed on local stable states or preferred populations; the universal query uses ordinary vacuous truth.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Both decision problems are NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds already with \(\tau=1\), a disjunction-free generating program, and one unconditional binary preference rule. The reduction always has at least one preferred population, so the universal question does not rely on vacuous truth.

1. Construction from satisfiability

Let \(F=C_1\land\cdots\land C_c\) be a \(3\)-CNF formula over variables \(z_1,\ldots,z_n\). Introduce distinct policy atoms \(G,D,p_1,n_1,\ldots,p_n,n_n\). Here \(p_i,n_i\) are ordinary atoms representing the two possible truth values of \(z_i\).

Take \(E=\varnothing\), with the single supported type \(t=\varnothing\) and mass \(\mu_t=1\). The query is \(G\).

The generating program \(P_F\) contains the following rules:

  • \(G\leftarrow\operatorname{not}D\) and \(D\leftarrow\operatorname{not}G\).
  • For each \(i\), \(p_i\leftarrow G,\operatorname{not}n_i\) and \(n_i\leftarrow G,\operatorname{not}p_i\).
  • For each clause \(C_j=\ell_{j,1}\lor\cdots\lor\ell_{j,k_j}\), the constraint \(\leftarrow G,f(\ell_{j,1}),\ldots,f(\ell_{j,k_j})\), where \(f(z_i)=n_i\) and \(f(\neg z_i)=p_i\).

Thus the constraint for a clause forbids precisely its falsification in the \(G\) branch.

The preference set consists of the single rule \(\rho:G>D\leftarrow\), with empty body.

2. Exact characterization of the stable states

For an assignment \(A\), define

\(S_A=\{G\}\cup\{p_i:A(z_i)=1\}\cup\{n_i:A(z_i)=0\}\).

Also define \(S_D=\{D\}\).

Lemma 1. The local stable states are exactly

\(\mathcal S=\{S_D\}\cup\{S_A:A\models F\}\).

Proof. For a normal program, its reduct removes each rule whose default-negated body contains an atom in the candidate state, and then removes the remaining default negations. The positive generating rules have a least model. A candidate is stable precisely when it equals that least model and satisfies the reduct’s constraints: every model contains the least model, and a positive constraint violated by the least model is violated by every larger model.

Let \(S\) be a stable state.

First, \(S\) contains exactly one of \(G,D\):

  • If it contains neither, the reduct contains both facts \(G\leftarrow\) and \(D\leftarrow\), contradicting stability.
  • If it contains both, both rules generating these atoms disappear. No other rule has either atom in its head, so neither belongs to the reduct’s least model, again contradicting stability.

If \(D\in S\), then \(G\notin S\). Every rule generating an assignment atom requires \(G\), so the reduct’s least model is exactly \(\{D\}\). All constraints require \(G\), so \(S_D\) is indeed stable.

Suppose instead that \(G\in S\). For each \(i\), exactly one of \(p_i,n_i\) must belong to \(S\):

  • If neither belongs to \(S\), both rules survive the reduct and derive both atoms from \(G\).
  • If both belong to \(S\), both rules disappear, leaving neither atom derivable.

Consequently \(S=S_A\) for a unique assignment \(A\). For such a candidate, the reduct contains the fact \(G\) and derives exactly the selected assignment atoms. Its least model is therefore \(S_A\). A clause constraint is violated exactly when every literal in that clause is false under \(A\). Hence \(S_A\) is stable exactly when \(A\models F\). This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

In particular, feasibility is guaranteed even when \(F\) is unsatisfiable.

3. Exact characterization of population dominance

The preference closure contains only \(\rho\): there is no rule with which it can compose. Its empty body puts it in stratum \(0\). All populations are comparable at that stratum, and there are no earlier strata to check.

Suppress the singleton type index and write \(x_S=x_{t,S}\). Let \(q(x)=x_{S_D}\). By Lemma 1, every other stable state contains \(G\), while \(S_D\) does not. Thus

\(g(x)=1-q(x)\).

Lemma 2. For any feasible populations \(x,y\),

\(x\succeq y\ \Longleftrightarrow\ q(x)<q(y)\),

and consequently

\(x\succ y\ \Longleftrightarrow\ q(x)<q(y)\).

Proof. The sole rule can witness \(x\) over \(y\) only by using a satisfying-assignment state \(S_A\) as its \(G\) state and \(S_D\) as its \(D\) state. Its witness conditions are therefore precisely

\(x_{S_A}>y_{S_A}\) and \(y_{S_D}>x_{S_D}\)

for some \(A\models F\). A witness immediately implies \(q(x)<q(y)\).

Conversely, suppose \(q(x)<q(y)\). Since both populations have total mass \(1\),

\(\sum_{A\models F}(x_{S_A}-y_{S_A})=q(y)-q(x)>0\).

This is a finite sum, so some satisfying assignment \(A\) has \(x_{S_A}>y_{S_A}\). Together with \(y_{S_D}>x_{S_D}\), this supplies a witness. Comparability and the absence of earlier strata then give \(x\succeq y\).

Finally, \(q(x)<q(y)\) excludes \(q(y)<q(x)\), so the same characterization holds for strict dominance. \(\square\)

This argument applies to arbitrary real masses and uses exactly the specified dominance relation, without taking a transitive closure.

4. Preferred populations and both reductions

If \(F\) is unsatisfiable, Lemma 1 gives just \(S_D\). The unique feasible population assigns it mass \(1\). It is preferred and has \(g(x)=0\). Therefore both questions have answer no.

If \(F\) is satisfiable, fix a satisfying assignment \(A\). Any feasible population \(x\) with \(q(x)>0\) is strictly dominated: transfer all its mass on \(S_D\) to \(S_A\), obtaining a feasible population \(y\) with \(q(y)=0<q(x)\). Lemma 2 gives \(y\succ x\).

Conversely, a population with \(q(x)=0\) cannot be strictly dominated, because no feasible population has negative mass on \(S_D\). Such populations exist, for example the population assigning mass \(1\) to \(S_A\). Thus the preferred populations are exactly those with \(q(x)=0\), and every one has \(g(x)=1\). Therefore both questions have answer yes.

We have proved the two equivalences

\(F\text{ is satisfiable}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists x\text{ preferred with }g(x)>0\),

\(F\text{ is satisfiable}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \forall x\text{ preferred},\ g(x)=1\).

Since \(3\)-SAT is NP-hard, these are polynomial-time many-one reductions proving NP-hardness of both target problems.

5. Size and running time

The construction has

\(m=2n+2,\qquad \tau=1,\qquad |P_F|=2n+c+2,\qquad |\Phi_F|=1\).

Counting generating and preference rules together gives \(r=2n+c+3\). All bodies have constant length, and the sole society mass is the binary rational \(1\).

With binary atom identifiers, the output length satisfies \(L=O((m+r)\log(m+r+2))\). The construction requires \(O(m+r+\tau)\) rule and support operations; including encoding and reading the source formula, its bit running time is \(O(N+L)\), where \(N\) is the formula’s encoded length. This is polynomial in the source input size.

Thus an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,r\) for either question would yield a polynomial-time algorithm for \(3\)-SAT. No claim of membership in NP or of an exact higher complexity classification is needed for the requested NP-hardness conclusion.

1. Construction and permitted inputs

The construction produces a valid instance of the task:

  • The generating program is normal, hence disjunction-free, and uses only default negation and constraints.
  • The exogenous set \(E=\varnothing\) satisfies the requirement that exogenous atoms occur in no rule heads.
  • The single supported type has positive binary-rational mass \(1\), so normalization holds.
  • The preference rule has two distinct head atoms and an allowed empty body.

For each clause, the constraint body contains precisely the assignment atoms representing falsity of its literals. In an assignment state, the constraint therefore excludes exactly assignments falsifying that clause.

The reduction does not require finding a satisfying assignment during construction.

2. Stable-state characterization

Lemma 1 correctly applies the reduct characterization for finite normal programs with constraints. After removing default negation, the nonconstraint rules are positive Horn rules and have a least model. Positive constraints are preserved under taking subsets of a satisfying interpretation; equivalently, if the least model violates a constraint, every interpretation containing it violates that constraint. Thus the stated stability criterion is valid.

The cases exhaust all candidate stable states:

  • If neither \(G\) nor \(D\) is present, both become facts in the reduct, contradicting stability.
  • If both are present, their generating rules disappear, and neither atom is derivable.
  • If only \(D\) is present, no assignment atom is derivable because all their rules require \(G\). The least model is exactly \(S_D\), which satisfies every constraint.
  • If only \(G\) is present, each pair \(p_i,n_i\) must contain exactly one atom. Neither choice would cause both atoms to be derived; both choices would remove both generating rules.
  • For a candidate \(S_A\), the reduct derives exactly its atoms, and the constraints hold exactly when \(A\) satisfies \(F\).

Consequently,
\[ \mathcal S=\{S_D\}\cup\{S_A:A\models F\} \]
is established in both directions. In particular, every constructed instance has a feasible population.

3. Closure, strata, and comparability

The preference closure contains only the original rule: composing it with itself would require its second head atom \(D\) to equal its first head atom \(G\), which is impossible.

Its stratum is \(0\) because its body is empty. This follows directly from the task’s rule-stratum definition, regardless of the graph levels assigned to \(G\) and \(D\).

All populations are comparable at stratum \(0\), and there are no earlier strata. Therefore, in this construction, existence of a witness for the sole preference rule is exactly the condition for weak dominance.

4. Dominance characterization

Every stable state other than \(S_D\) contains \(G\), so normalization gives
\[ g(x)=1-q(x). \]

A witness must use a satisfying-assignment state for its \(G\) side and \(S_D\) for its \(D\) side. Thus a witness implies \(q(x)<q(y)\).

Conversely, the identity
\[ \sum_{A\models F}\bigl(x_{S_A}-y_{S_A}\bigr)=q(y)-q(x) \]
is correct. The state set is finite, so a positive sum implies that at least one summand is positive. This gives exactly the required witness.

If \(F\) is unsatisfiable, the assignment-state sum is empty and every feasible population has \(q=1\); the converse implication has an impossible premise. There is no missing edge case.

The resulting weak-dominance characterization also correctly implies
\[ x\succ y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q(x)<q(y), \]
because the reverse strict inequality cannot simultaneously hold. No transitive closure is used.

5. Preferred populations and decision answers

For unsatisfiable \(F\), there is exactly one feasible population. It cannot be strictly dominated and has \(g=0\). Both decision questions therefore answer no, including the universal question because its preferred set is nonempty.

For satisfiable \(F\), transferring the mass on \(S_D\) to any fixed satisfying-assignment state preserves feasibility and strictly lowers \(q\) whenever \(q>0\). Hence every such population is strictly dominated.

A population with \(q=0\) cannot be strictly dominated, since feasible populations have nonnegative coordinates. Such populations exist. Thus the preferred populations are exactly those with \(q=0\), all of which have \(g=1\).

Both stated equivalences with satisfiability follow. These are separate valid many-one reductions from \(3\)-SAT to the existential and universal decision problems.
