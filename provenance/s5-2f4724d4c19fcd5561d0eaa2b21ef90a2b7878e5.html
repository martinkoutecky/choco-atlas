The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\), agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), unit valuations \(p_t(v)=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_v\), maximize \(z\) by choosing masses \(x_{t,I}\ge 0\) of agents of type \(t\) receiving each independent set \(I\) of \(G\), subject to \(\sum_I x_{t,I}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,I:v\in I}x_{t,I}\le b_v\), and \(x_{t,I}=0\) whenever \(|I|<z\). In particular, on the graph formed from two copies of \(H\) joined completely, with one type of mass \(1\) and \(b_v=\tfrac12\) for every vertex, determine or approximate the maximum \(z\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite simple undirected conflict graph with \(m=|V|\ge2\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. All types have additive unit valuations \(p_t(v)=1\). Each item has a nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supply \(b_v\).
Let \(\mathcal I(G)\) contain all independent sets, including the empty set. An allocation chooses real masses \(x_{t,I}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{I\in\mathcal I(G)}x_{t,I}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,I:v\in I}x_{t,I}\le b_v\). Supplies may remain unused.
Its guaranteed satisfaction is the largest \(z\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\) such that \(x_{t,I}=0\) whenever \(|I|<z\); write \(z^*\) for the maximum feasible guarantee. A factor \(r\) approximation returns a feasible allocation with guarantee at least \(z^*/r\).
The specified restricted family takes a nonempty graph \(H\), two disjoint copies of it, and all edges between the copies, with one type of mass \(1\) and \(b_v=\tfrac12\) for every vertex. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any approximation parameter.

For each fixed \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), does this continuous problem admit a polynomial-time \(m^{1-\varepsilon}\)-factor approximation, or is achieving that factor NP-hard? What is the answer on the specified restricted family?

For discrete allocations to any fixed number \(k\ge1\) of agents, maximizing the minimum cardinality of pairwise disjoint independent bundles is NP-hard to approximate within \(m^{1-\varepsilon}\), even with unit valuations ([source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00453-022-01079-8)).
No result settling this exact continuous approximation problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, matching the distribution and per-capita interpretation.
  • Specified nonnegative rational supplies and allowed unused supply, matching partial allocations.
  • Included the empty independent set and allowed real allocation masses; restricted the guarantee to integer cardinalities.
  • Specified simple undirected graphs, at least two items, and a nonempty graph H for the restricted family.
  • Defined approximation through a feasible allocation and its guaranteed satisfaction, rather than expected utility or an unsupported value estimate.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

For every fixed \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), achieving an \(m^{1-\varepsilon}\)-factor approximation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already on the specified restricted family. Consequently, neither that family nor the general problem admits such a polynomial-time approximation unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof directly treats continuous allocations; it does not infer hardness merely from the discrete allocation result.

1. Aggregating types and representing allocations

Because every type has the same valuation, types can be aggregated. Given an allocation, define \(y_I=\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,I}\). Its constraints become

\(\sum_{I\in\mathcal I(G)}y_I=1,\qquad \sum_{I\ni v}y_I\le b_v\ \text{for every }v,\qquad y_I\ge0.\)

Conversely, any such distribution yields an allocation by setting \(x_{t,I}=\mu_t y_I\). Indeed, its type totals equal \(\mu_t\), and its aggregate item consumption equals \(\sum_{I\ni v}y_I\), since \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Both transformations preserve the guarantee.

We use the usual finite output representation: list positive allocation entries and omit zero entries. Rational allocations with polynomial-size support suffice whenever a guarantee is feasible. To verify this, fix a feasible guarantee \(z\), retain only independent sets of size at least \(z\), and introduce supply slacks \(s_v\ge0\). The resulting system is

\(\sum_I y_I=1,\qquad \sum_{I\ni v}y_I+s_v=b_v.\)

Its feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded: \(0\le y_I\le1\) and \(0\le s_v\le b_v\). A vertex therefore exists. There are \(m+1\) equality constraints, so a basic feasible solution has at most \(m+1\) positive variables, including slacks. Its coordinates have polynomial bit length: the constraint matrix has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), its basis determinants have absolute value at most \((m+1)^{(m+1)/2}\), and the rational right-hand side has encoding length bounded by \(O(L)\). Cramer’s rule gives the claimed bound.

Thus the distribution needs at most \(m+1\) positive entries, and converting it to types needs at most \(\tau(m+1)\) entries.

2. Exact optimum on the restricted family

Let \(H\) have \(n\ge1\) vertices, and let \(\alpha(H)\) denote its maximum independent-set cardinality. Construct \(G\) from two disjoint copies \(H_1,H_2\), adding every edge between the copies. Thus \(m=2n\). Use one type of mass \(1\) and supplies \(b_v=\tfrac12\).

Claim. This instance satisfies \(z^*=\alpha(H)\).

Upper bound. An independent set of \(G\) cannot meet both copies: any vertex in one copy is adjacent to every vertex in the other. Consequently, every independent set of \(G\) has cardinality at most \(\alpha(H)\). Since the allocation masses sum to \(1\), some bundle has positive mass, and no allocation can guarantee more than \(\alpha(H)\).

Lower bound. Choose a maximum independent set \(S\) of \(H\), and denote its copies by \(S_1,S_2\). Set

\(x_{1,S_1}=\tfrac12,\qquad x_{1,S_2}=\tfrac12,\)

with all other entries zero. The masses sum to \(1\). Every selected vertex consumes exactly \(\tfrac12\), and every other vertex consumes zero, so all supply constraints hold. Both supported bundles have size \(\alpha(H)\).

This proves the claim.

Moreover, any positive-mass bundle of an allocation with guarantee \(g\) maps to an independent set of \(H\) of size at least \(g\). This extraction is the essential link between continuous approximation and independent-set approximation.

3. Approximation hardness and the vertex-count factor

We use Zuckerman’s theorem: for every fixed \(\delta>0\), approximating maximum clique on an \(n\)-vertex graph within \(n^{1-\delta}\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Taking graph complements preserves vertex count and exchanges cliques with independent sets, so the same theorem holds for maximum independent set. [Zuckerman, Theorem 1.1](https://www.theoryofcomputing.org/articles/v003a006/v003a006.pdf).

Fix \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\). Suppose a polynomial-time algorithm achieves factor \(m^{1-\varepsilon}\) on the restricted family. Given \(H\), construct the preceding instance and run that algorithm. Its allocation has guarantee \(g\) satisfying

\(g\ge \dfrac{z^*}{m^{1-\varepsilon}}=\dfrac{\alpha(H)}{(2n)^{1-\varepsilon}}.\)

Since \(\alpha(H)\ge1\), the right-hand side is positive; because \(g\) is an integer, \(g\ge1\). Select any positive-mass bundle and map it back to an independent set \(S\) of \(H\). Then

\(|S|\ge \dfrac{\alpha(H)}{2^{1-\varepsilon}n^{1-\varepsilon}}.\)

To absorb the constant factor, set \(\delta=\varepsilon/2\). Whenever

\(n\ge 2^{\,2(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon},\)

we have \(2^{1-\varepsilon}\le n^{\varepsilon/2}\), and hence

\(|S|\ge \dfrac{\alpha(H)}{n^{1-\varepsilon/2}}=\dfrac{\alpha(H)}{n^{1-\delta}}.\)

For smaller \(n\), solve maximum independent set exactly by enumerating vertex subsets. The threshold depends only on the fixed \(\varepsilon\), so this costs a constant with respect to input size.

We have therefore obtained a polynomial-time \(n^{1-\delta}\)-factor approximation for maximum independent set. Zuckerman’s theorem implies \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). This proves the requested hardness on the restricted family, and therefore on the general problem.

4. Running times

Let \(T_\varepsilon(m,\tau,L)\) be the running time of the hypothesized approximation algorithm, including writing its output. Using an explicit graph encoding, the reduction produces

\(m=2n,\qquad \tau=1,\qquad L=O(n^2\log(n+1)).\)

Construction takes \(O(n^2\log(n+1)+L_H)\) bit operations, where \(L_H\) is the input length of \(H\). Extracting a positive-mass bundle takes time linear in the allocation output length, and therefore \(O(T_\varepsilon)\).

Writing \(N_\varepsilon=\left\lceil 2^{\,2(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}\right\rceil\), the resulting independent-set algorithm has running time bounded by

\(O\!\left(L_H+n^2\log(n+1)+T_\varepsilon(2n,1,O(n^2\log(n+1)))+2^{N_\varepsilon}N_\varepsilon^2\right).\)

For each fixed \(\varepsilon\), this is polynomial if \(T_\varepsilon\) is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

For comparison, an unconditional exact algorithm for the general continuous problem takes \(2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) time: enumerate all independent sets, test the aggregated rational linear program for each integer guarantee, and convert a feasible optimum distribution to the listed types. On the restricted family, enumerating independent sets of \(H\) and using the two-bundle construction gives an exact algorithm in \(O(2^{m/2}m^2+L)\) time.
