The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite connected role graph \(H=(R,E)\) in the paper’s augmented quasi-path or augmented-cycle classes, rational role masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), and initial product masses \(x^0_{r,p}\), determine whether a finite schedule of rational mass updates can end at a stable state whose white mass equals the supremum over all feasible schedules; if so, return such an SPMS schedule. Each member of role \(r\) sees the product masses in the adjacent roles and follows the paper’s majority rule and tie-breaking, while white recommendations have priority whenever eligible mass exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) role types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=3\) products \(P=\{\mathrm{black},\mathrm{gray},\mathrm{white}\}\).
The input is a finite simple connected undirected role graph \(H=(R,E)\), with \(\tau=|R|\), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) satisfying \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\), and rational initial allocations \(x^0_{r,p}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_p x^0_{r,p}=\mu_r\). The graph is an augmented quasi-path if acyclic, or an augmented cycle if it contains a cycle: in either class, every vertex of degree \(3\) or at least \(5\) has at most one neighbor whose degree exceeds \(1\).
At state \(x\), members of role \(r\) see scores \(s_{r,p}(x)=\sum_{u:\{r,u\}\in E}x_{u,p}\). A member currently holding \(p\) keeps it if \(p\) maximizes these scores; otherwise she chooses white if white is a maximizer, and otherwise chooses either maximizing nonwhite product. Both choices are allowed when black and gray tie.
A legal update selects one role \(r\), one current product \(p\), a different product \(q\) prescribed by this rule, and a rational amount \(0<\delta\leq x_{r,p}\); it transfers \(\delta\) from \(x_{r,p}\) to \(x_{r,q}\), leaving everything else fixed. Initial white holders may subsequently leave white; no further seeding occurs.
A state is stable if no positive mass can legally change product. A feasible schedule is a finite sequence of legal updates from \(x^0\) ending in a stable state, including the empty sequence when already stable. An SPMS schedule must update to white whenever any positive mass anywhere is eligible to change to white; otherwise any legal update is allowed.
Let \(W(x)=\sum_r x_{r,\mathrm{white}}\) and let \(M\) be the supremum of terminal white mass over all feasible schedules, without the SPMS restriction. The problem asks whether an SPMS schedule ends with \(W(x)=M\), and requests such a schedule when one exists; if no feasible schedule exists, the answer is no. Schedules are explicit lists of updates with binary rational amounts. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding whether an SPMS schedule attains \(M\) in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? Can an attaining schedule also be returned in polynomial time on yes-instances?

For discrete agents on these graph classes, an optimal SPMS evolution always exists, but computing one is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. [Source](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/pdf/doi/10.3233/FAIA200071)

$$ C_r<3s_{r,w}\leq C_r, $$

$$ s_{r,b}=s_{r,g}=s_{r,w}=C_r/3. $$

4. Complete solution for two roles

Theorem. For $m=3$ and $\tau=2$, every instance has an SPMS schedule attaining $M$. Such a schedule uses at most five updates and can be constructed in $O(L^3)$ bit operations. In particular, the decision and construction problems are polynomial-time solvable on $\tau\leq2$.

Let the two roles be $A,B$, joined by one edge. Their allocation vectors are denoted by $a,b$, with respective total masses $\mu_A,\mu_B$. Define
$$ \operatorname{Max}(a)=\{p:a_p=\max_{q\in P}a_q\}, \qquad \operatorname{Supp}(a)=\{p:a_p>0\}. $$

The scores at $A$ are exactly $b$, and the scores at $B$ are exactly $a$.

4.1. Classification of stable states

Stability is equivalent to
$$ \operatorname{Supp}(a)\subseteq\operatorname{Max}(b), \qquad \operatorname{Supp}(b)\subseteq\operatorname{Max}(a). $$
Because both masses are positive, maximizing coordinates are positive. Hence
$$ \operatorname{Supp}(a) \subseteq\operatorname{Max}(b) \subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(b) \subseteq\operatorname{Max}(a) \subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(a). $$
All these sets are therefore equal to a nonempty set $S\subseteq P$.

Both roles distribute their masses uniformly over $S$:
$$ a_p= \begin{cases} \mu_A/|S|,&p\in S,\\ 0,&p\notin S, \end{cases} \qquad b_p= \begin{cases} \mu_B/|S|,&p\in S,\\ 0,&p\notin S. \end{cases} $$
Conversely, every state of this form is stable.

Thus there are exactly seven stable states. Their white masses belong to
$$ \left\{0,\frac13,\frac12,1\right\}. $$

If the input is already stable, no first update is legal, and the empty schedule is optimal. Assume henceforth that it is unstable.

4.2. A common nonwhite maximum blocks positive terminal white

We first prove a lemma needed in the case analysis.

Lemma. Suppose a nonwhite product $c$ maximizes both allocation vectors, and
$$ a_c>a_w,\qquad b_c>b_w. $$
Then every reachable stable state has zero white mass.

Proof. Consider any legal schedule. Until $c$ first ceases to maximize one of the allocation vectors, its allocations cannot decrease: a holder of $c$ sees $c$ maximizing at the other role. White cannot receive transfers either, because the other role's $c$ allocation is strictly larger than its white allocation. Consequently, throughout this interval, both strict inequalities persist.

If $c$ remains a maximizer of both allocation vectors for the whole schedule, white is strictly below a maximizing score at each role. Terminal stability then forces both white allocations to be zero.

Otherwise, consider the first update after which $c$ no longer maximizes an allocation vector, say that of role $A$. The destination must be the other nonwhite product $d$. A destination of white is impossible, and a destination of $c$ cannot destroy its maximality. The $c$ allocation at $A$ cannot decrease, so after the update,
$$ a'_d>a'_c>a'_w. $$
Therefore
$$ a'_w<\mu_A/3. $$

For the transfer to $d$ to be legal, $d$ must maximize the allocation vector of $B$. Before this first loss of maximality, $c$ also maximizes that vector. Hence
$$ b_d=b_c>b_w, $$
which gives
$$ b_w<\mu_B/3. $$

Both roles now have strictly less than one-third white. Section 2.2 shows that these strict bounds persist and exclude any stable state with positive white. This proves the lemma. $\square$

4.3. Case analysis and constructions

The following cases exhaust all initial allocation vectors.

Case 1: White uniquely maximizes an allocation vector

Suppose white uniquely maximizes $a$. Transfer each positive nonwhite allocation at $B$ entirely to white. These transfers are legal because the scores at $B$ remain $a$.

Role $B$ is then entirely white. Transfer each positive nonwhite allocation at $A$ entirely to white.

This is an SPMS schedule with at most four updates, ending at white consensus. Thus $M=1$.

Case 2: White ties with exactly one nonwhite maximum

Suppose
$$ \operatorname{Max}(a)=\{w,c\}, $$
and let $d$ be the remaining nonwhite product. Thus
$$ a_w=a_c>a_d. $$

A positive allocation of $d$ at $B$ can legally be transferred entirely to white. After that transfer,
$$ b_d=0,\qquad b_w=\mu_B-b_c. $$

There are three subcases.

If $b_c<\mu_B/2$, this makes white the unique maximum at $B$. Case 1 then yields white consensus. Counting only the remaining positive source allocations, the complete schedule uses at most four updates.

If $b_c=\mu_B/2$, role $B$ becomes balanced between $c$ and white.

If $a_d>0$, transfer it to white. This is legal because its score is zero at $B$, whereas white has score $\mu_B/2$. White now uniquely maximizes $a$, so white consensus follows. The complete schedule again uses at most four updates.

If $a_d=0$, role $A$ was already balanced between $c$ and white. The constructed terminal state has white mass $1/2$ and is optimal. Indeed, initially,
$$ a_c=\mu_A/2,\qquad b_c=\mu_B/2. $$
The property that both $c$ allocations are at least half their role masses is invariant. Under that property, $c$ maximizes both allocation vectors and its holders cannot leave. Therefore every reachable state satisfies
$$ W\leq(\mu_A-a_c)+(\mu_B-b_c)\leq\frac12. $$

If $b_c>\mu_B/2$, every reachable terminal state has zero white. To prove this, observe that the following properties are invariant:
$$ b_c>\mu_B/2, \qquad c\in\operatorname{Max}(a). $$
At $A$, the unique maximizing score is $c$, so every update there increases $a_c$ and decreases another coordinate. At $B$, a holder of $c$ cannot leave because $c$ maximizes $a$. These observations prove the invariance by induction.

Terminal stability therefore forces $A$ entirely to $c$, since $c$ uniquely maximizes $b$. It then forces $B$ entirely to $c$.

An SPMS schedule with at most four updates is explicit: first transfer $b_d$ to white if positive; then transfer $a_w$ to $c$, followed by the remaining positive non-$c$ allocations to $c$. After the first transfer, no white update remains available; after the second, $c$ uniquely maximizes both allocation vectors. Thus the schedule respects SPMS and attains $M=0$.

Case 3: A role is uniform over all three products

This case remains only if Cases 1 and 2 do not apply. Suppose
$$ a_b=a_g=a_w=\mu_A/3. $$
The other role cannot have white as a maximizer: it would fall under Cases 1 or 2, or both roles would be uniform and already stable.

Initially, no update at $B$ is legal because all its scores are equal. Every legal first update is therefore at $A$ and has a maximizing nonwhite destination $c$ of $b$. Any positive such transfer makes $c$ the unique maximum of the allocation vector at $A$. At $B$, $c$ was already a maximum and strictly exceeded white.

The lemma in Section 4.2 now applies. Every reachable terminal state has zero white.

A short attaining SPMS schedule transfers $a_w$ to any such $c$, then all non-$c$ allocations at $B$ to $c$, and finally the remaining non-$c$ allocation at $A$ to $c$. It uses at most four updates. Throughout this construction, white is strictly below a maximizing score at both roles, so no white update is available.

Case 4: White maximizes neither vector, and there is a common nonwhite maximum

Choose
$$ c\in\operatorname{Max}(a)\cap\operatorname{Max}(b). $$
Both strict inequalities required by Section 4.2 hold, so $M=0$.

If some role has positive white, transfer its entire white allocation to $c$. This makes $c$ uniquely maximize that role's allocation vector. Transfer all non-$c$ allocations at the other role to $c$, and then the first role's remaining non-$c$ allocation to $c$. This uses at most four updates.

If both white allocations are zero, instability implies that $c$ uniquely maximizes at least one vector; otherwise both roles would be balanced between black and gray and stable. Transfer the other role's non-$c$ allocation to $c$, and then do the same at the first role. At most two updates suffice.

All these transfers preserve a common maximum $c$ strictly above white, so SPMS permits them.

Case 5: White maximizes neither vector, and the maximum sets are disjoint

The maximum sets must be opposite singletons. Relabeling the nonwhite products if necessary, suppose
$$ \operatorname{Max}(a)=\{b\}, \qquad \operatorname{Max}(b)=\{g\}. $$

If both white allocations are at most one-third of their role masses, Section 2.2 gives $M=0$.

A short attaining schedule drains each positive white allocation to a currently maximizing nonwhite product. These drains are legal: initially white is not maximizing at either role, and draining white at one role can only worsen white relative to nonwhite scores at the other. No white update is available by Section 2.2.

After at most two drains, only two products remain. If the resulting state is unstable, choose a legal update and transfer its entire source allocation. The updated role becomes monochromatic; the other role can then transfer its allocation of the other color to that color. Thus at most two additional updates produce a stable state.

Finally, suppose one role has more than one-third white. Choose that role as $A$, and relabel the opposite maxima as above. Then
$$ a_w>\mu_A/3. $$
Transfer
$$ \delta=\frac{a_b-a_g}{2} $$
from black to gray at $A$. This amount is positive and no larger than $a_b$. The transfer is legal because gray uniquely maximizes the scores at $A$.

After it,
$$ a'_b=a'_g=\frac{\mu_A-a_w}{2}<a_w. $$
White now uniquely maximizes the allocation vector at $A$, so Case 1 produces white consensus.

The initial transfer respects SPMS because white initially maximizes neither allocation vector; every subsequent transfer is to white. The schedule uses at most five updates and attains $M=1$.

4.4. Exhaustiveness and complexity

If white maximizes an allocation vector, its maximizing set is either a singleton, a two-element set, or all three products. Cases 1–3 cover these possibilities. Otherwise, the two nonempty maximum sets are subsets of $\{b,g\}$; they either intersect or are opposite singletons, giving Cases 4–5.

Every construction uses at most five updates. The case tests and update amounts require only a constant number of rational comparisons, additions, subtractions, and divisions by two. All resulting numbers have $O(L)$ bits. Exact arithmetic therefore gives the conservative bound $O(L^3)$ for $m=3,\tau=2$, or $O(m\tau^2L^3)$ on the stated subclass $\tau\leq2$.

This proves the theorem.

5. Consequences and remaining questions

The four-role counterexample establishes that the rational-mass decision problem has actual no-instances even on paths. It also shows why the discrete existence theorem cannot be imported directly: discrete agents and divisible role allocations have different attainable evolutions. The cited paper proves existence of an optimal SPMS evolution in its discrete model, and hardness of computing one. [Auletta, Ferraioli, and Greco, Theorems 2–3](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/pdf/doi/10.3233/FAIA200071)
