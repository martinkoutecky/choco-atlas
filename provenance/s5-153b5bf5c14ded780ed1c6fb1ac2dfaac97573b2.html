The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite metric support \(T=\{p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\}\), rational distances, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and \(2\le k\le\tau\), find rational assignments \(x_{ti}\ge0\) with \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(q_i=\sum_t x_{ti}>0\). Define \(D_i(t)=\sum_u x_{ui}d(p_t,p_u)/q_i\). Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in \((\tau,L)\) that returns an assignment and support centers \(z_i\in T\), with universal constants \(A,B\), such that \(D_i(t)\le A D_h(t)\) for every \(i\) with \(x_{ti}>0\) and every \(h\ne i\), while \(d(p_t,z_i)\le B\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\) whenever \(x_{ti}>0\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}_k\) is the support’s optimal \(k\)-center radius?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T=\{p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\}\) of distinct metric types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. The rational metric distance matrix and an integer \(2\le k\le\tau\) are input; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
An assignment consists of rational masses \(x_{ti}\ge0\), for \(1\le t\le\tau\) and \(1\le i\le k\), satisfying \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\) and \(q_i:=\sum_t x_{ti}>0\). Mass at a type may be split between clusters.
Define \(D_i(t):=q_i^{-1}\sum_{u=1}^{\tau}x_{ui}d(p_t,p_u)\). Individuals are infinitesimal, so evaluating their preferences removes no positive mass, including mass at their own type.
Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_k:=\min_{Z\subseteq T,\,|Z|=k}\max_{t}\min_{z\in Z}d(p_t,z)\), independent of the masses.

Does there exist an algorithm polynomial in \((\tau,k,L)\) and universal constants \(A,B\ge1\) that, on every input, returns such an assignment and centers \(z_1,\ldots,z_k\in T\), with \(D_i(t)\le A D_h(t)\) for every \(x_{ti}>0\) and every \(h\ne i\), and \(d(p_t,z_i)\le B\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\) whenever \(x_{ti}>0\)?

For finite lists of individuals, Aamand et al. give a polynomial-time clustering with constant IP-stability factor and constant approximation to the optimal \(k\)-center radius; discrete own-cluster averages exclude the individual, and singleton clusters are stable. [Source](https://www.proceedings.com/content/075/075280-1892open.pdf).
Mosenzon and Vakilian give a discrete \(O(\log n)\)-IP-stability algorithm in \(\widetilde O(nk)\) time. [Source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/mosenzon24a.html). No result settling the exact divisible-mass question above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined L as the total binary input bit length, including the rational distance matrix, masses, and k.
  • Specified that the optimal k-center radius uses exactly k distinct support centers and ignores masses.
  • Interpreted divisible masses as populations of infinitesimal individuals: removing an individual removes zero mass, rather than removing its entire type.
  • Specified that the universal approximation constants are at least one and independent of every input parameter.

1. Result

Yes. There is a deterministic algorithm polynomial in \((\tau,k,L)\) with universal constants \(A=232\) and \(B=4\).

The construction is a divisible-mass adaptation of the ball-carving method in Section 3 of [Aamand et al.](https://www.proceedings.com/content/075/075280-1892open.pdf). We prove the weighted guarantees directly, including the case where a type receives positive mass in several preliminary clusters.

Write \(d(t,u)=d(p_t,p_u)\). For \(S\subseteq T\), define \(\mu(S)=\sum_{p_t\in S}\mu_t\), and let \(B(a,s)=\{p_t\in T:d(a,p_t)\le s\}\).

2. Choosing centers and a scale

Choose distinct centers \(c_1,\ldots,c_k\in T\) by farthest-first traversal: choose \(c_1\) arbitrarily, and subsequently choose a type maximizing its distance to the previously chosen centers. Define their covering radius by \(R=\max_{p_t\in T}\min_i d(p_t,c_i)\).

Two properties will be used:

  • \(d(c_i,c_h)\ge R\) whenever \(i\ne h\).
  • \(R\le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\).

For the first property, the covering radius cannot increase when centers are added. Consequently, when \(c_h\) is selected, its distance to every earlier center is at least the final covering radius \(R\).

For the second property, suppose \(R>0\), and choose \(p_*\) attaining the covering radius. The \(k+1\) distinct types \(c_1,\ldots,c_k,p_*\) have pairwise distances at least \(R\). An optimal set of \(k\) centers covers them by \(k\) balls of radius \(\mathrm{OPT}_k\). Two of these types lie in the same ball, so their distance is at most \(2\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\), proving the claim.

If \(R=0\), every type is a selected center, because distinct types have positive distance. Thus \(k=\tau\); assigning each type entirely to its own cluster solves the problem.

Henceforth assume \(R>0\), and set \(r=R/15\).

3. Weighted ball carving

We construct preliminary clusters described by nonnegative rational masses \(v_{jt}\), with \(\sum_jv_{jt}=\mu_t\).

First choose carving centers \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\). Initially there are none. While some type has distance greater than \(6r\) from every previously chosen carving center, choose such a type maximizing \(\mu(B(b_j,r))\). Put \(s_j=\mu(B(b_j,r))>0\), and define the annulus \(A_j=B(b_j,3r)\setminus B(b_j,2r)\).

Initialize seed masses \(y_{jt}\) as follows.

  • Balanced case: If \(\mu(A_j)\ge s_j\), allocate all mass in \(B(b_j,r)\), together with exactly \(s_j\) mass from \(A_j\), to seed \(j\). The latter allocation may split a type.
  • Full-ball case: If \(\mu(A_j)<s_j\), allocate all mass in \(B(b_j,3r)\) to seed \(j\).

The carving centers have pairwise distances greater than \(6r\). Their closed radius-\(3r\) balls are therefore disjoint: an intersection would imply center distance at most \(6r\). Since every seed is supported inside its corresponding ball, these allocations never exceed the available mass at any type.

After all carving centers have been chosen, define the residual mass \(\rho_t=\mu_t-\sum_jy_{jt}\). Allocate all of \(\rho_t\) to the earliest carving center \(b_j\) satisfying \(d(p_t,b_j)\le7r\). Such a center exists: termination of center selection guarantees a carving center within distance \(6r\).

Let \(v_{jt}\) denote the resulting preliminary allocation. Define its mass and support by \(w_j=\sum_tv_{jt}\) and \(S_j=\{p_t:v_{jt}>0\}\). Then \(w_j\ge s_j>0\), and every point of \(S_j\) is within \(7r\) of \(b_j\). In particular, \(\operatorname{diam}(S_j)\le14r\).

The sets \(S_j\) need not be disjoint. The following lemma addresses that explicitly.

4. Separation lemma

Lemma. If \(v_{at}>0\) and \(j\ne a\), then \(\frac{1}{w_j}\sum_uv_{ju}d(t,u)\ge r/4\).

Proof. Fix \(x=p_t\) with \(v_{at}>0\), and suppose, for contradiction, that its average distance to preliminary cluster \(j\) is less than \(r/4\).

Let \(E=B(x,r/2)\), and write \(v_j(E)=\sum_{p_u\in E}v_{ju}\). Since points outside \(E\) have distance at least \(r/2\) from \(x\), the assumed average implies \(v_j(E)>w_j/2\).

We first record a consequence of \(v_j(E)>s_j\). Since \(v_{ju}\le\mu_u\), this inequality implies \(\mu(B(x,r))\ge\mu(E)>s_j\). Thus, when \(b_j\) was selected, \(x\) could not have been eligible: its radius-\(r\) ball had greater mass than the maximizing choice \(b_j\). There must consequently be an earlier carving center \(b_\ell\), with \(\ell<j\), satisfying \(d(x,b_\ell)\le6r\).

Every \(y\in E\) then satisfies \(d(y,b_\ell)\le6r+r/2<7r\). The rule for residual allocation assigns any residual mass at \(y\) to a center no later than \(b_\ell\). Therefore, whenever \(v_j(E)>s_j\), cluster \(j\) receives no residual mass in \(E\), and \(v_j(E)=y_j(E)\).

We now distinguish the two initialization cases.

Balanced case

Here \(w_j\ge2s_j\), so \(v_j(E)>w_j/2\ge s_j\). The preceding argument gives \(v_j(E)=y_j(E)\).

The seed consists of mass \(s_j\) in \(B(b_j,r)\) and mass \(s_j\) in \(A_j\). Every point of the former is at distance greater than \(r\) from every point of the latter: for \(u\in B(b_j,r)\) and \(z\in A_j\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(u,z)\ge d(b_j,z)-d(b_j,u)>2r-r=r\).

Any two points in \(E\) have distance at most \(r\). Hence \(E\) cannot meet both seed portions, and \(y_j(E)\le s_j\). This contradicts \(v_j(E)>s_j\).

Full-ball case

All mass at every type in \(B(b_j,3r)\) was allocated to seed \(j\). Because \(x\) has positive mass in another preliminary cluster, \(x\notin B(b_j,3r)\), so \(d(x,b_j)>3r\).

For any \(y\in E\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(y,b_j)\ge d(x,b_j)-d(x,y)>3r-r/2>2r\). Thus \(E\) is disjoint from \(B(b_j,r)\), and \(E\cap B(b_j,3r)\subseteq A_j\).

Cluster \(j\) contains the entire mass \(s_j\) of \(B(b_j,r)\), disjoint from its mass in \(E\). Consequently, \(w_j\ge s_j+v_j(E)>s_j+w_j/2\). It follows that \(w_j>2s_j\) and \(v_j(E)>s_j\).

Again, no residual mass in \(E\) enters cluster \(j\). Therefore \(v_j(E)=y_j(E)=\mu(E\cap B(b_j,3r))\le\mu(A_j)<s_j\), a contradiction.

Both cases contradict the assumed average. This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

5. Merging into exactly the required number of clusters

For each preliminary cluster \(j\), choose \(f(j)\) minimizing \(d(c_i,S_j)=\min_{p_t\in S_j}d(c_i,p_t)\) over \(i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), breaking ties arbitrarily.

Define the final assignment by \(x_{ti}=\sum_{j:f(j)=i}v_{jt}\), and return centers \(z_i=c_i\). The masses are rational and nonnegative, and \(\sum_ix_{ti}=\mu_t\).

Every final cluster has positive mass

Suppose \(c_i\in S_j\). For every \(h\ne i\) and every \(y\in S_j\), we have \(d(c_h,y)\ge d(c_h,c_i)-d(c_i,y)\ge R-14r=R/15>0\).

Thus \(d(c_i,S_j)=0<d(c_h,S_j)\), so \(f(j)=i\). Every preliminary cluster carrying mass at \(c_i\) is therefore merged into final cluster \(i\). Consequently, all mass at \(c_i\) is assigned there, giving \(q_i\ge\mu(c_i)>0\).

Radius guarantee

Fix \(j\) with \(f(j)=i\). Every type is within \(R\) of some selected center, so \(\min_h d(c_h,S_j)\le R\). Since \(i\) minimizes this distance, choose \(y\in S_j\) with \(d(c_i,y)\le R\).

For every \(p_t\in S_j\), we obtain \(d(p_t,c_i)\le d(p_t,y)+d(y,c_i)\le14r+R=29R/15\).

Therefore, whenever \(x_{ti}>0\), we have \(d(p_t,z_i)\le29R/15\le58\,\mathrm{OPT}_k/15\le4\,\mathrm{OPT}_k\).

Stability guarantee

Fix \(t,i,h\) with \(x_{ti}>0\) and \(h\ne i\). There is a preliminary cluster \(a\) with \(v_{at}>0\) and \(f(a)=i\).

Every preliminary cluster \(j\) merged into \(h\) differs from \(a\). By the separation lemma, its average distance from \(p_t\) is at least \(r/4\). The final average \(D_h(t)\) is a mass-weighted average of those preliminary averages, so \(D_h(t)\ge r/4=R/60\).

On the other hand, every type carrying mass in final cluster \(i\), including \(p_t\), is within \(29R/15\) of \(c_i\). Hence every such type is within \(58R/15\) of \(p_t\), and \(D_i(t)\le58R/15\).

Combining these bounds gives \(D_i(t)\le232\,D_h(t)\).

This uses precisely the infinitesimal averages in the question: no positive mass is removed when evaluating preferences.

6. Polynomial bit complexity

The algorithm works directly on the support.

Farthest-first traversal, computing ball masses, selecting carving centers, assigning residual masses, and merging preliminary clusters each require polynomially many arithmetic operations and comparisons. There are at most \(\tau\) carving centers, since their pairwise distances are positive.

To bound rational encoding lengths, write each input mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), and let \(H=\prod_tb_t\). The binary length of \(H\) is at most the sum of the denominator encoding lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Every input mass is an integer multiple of \(1/H\).

In a balanced annulus, allocate mass by traversing its types, taking whole masses until the required total \(s_j\) is reached and taking the necessary portion of the last type. Since \(s_j\) is itself an integer multiple of \(1/H\), every selected portion is also such a multiple.

All subsequent mass operations are sums and differences. Thus every intermediate mass and every output entry is a nonnegative integer multiple of \(1/H\), at most \(1\), and has \(O(L)\) encoding length. The scale \(r=R/15\) also has polynomial encoding length because \(R\) is an input distance. Exact comparisons therefore take polynomial bit time.

The algorithm returns a valid rational assignment and support centers in time polynomial in \((\tau,k,L)\), with the proved constants \(A=232\) and \(B=4\).
