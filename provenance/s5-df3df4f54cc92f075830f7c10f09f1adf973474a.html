Proposition 2 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational society μ over complete rankings of \(m ≥ 2S-1\) candidates, with \(S>1\), and fixed tie-breaking, decide whether there are a ranking \(b\) and rational λ>0 such that the original winner set \(W(μ,S)\) has size \(S\), its members occupy the top \(S\) positions of \(b\), and after adding mass λ of type \(b\), the new winner set intersects the original in exactly one candidate, excludes the candidates in the top \(S-1\) positions of \(b\), and includes every candidate in the bottom \(S-1\) positions of \(b\). Run weighted STV with quota equal to total mass divided by \(S+1\) and proportional surplus transfers.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

An input consists of integers \(S>1\), \(m\ge 2S-1\), and a society \(\mu\) over strict complete rankings of the candidate set \(C\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings. Require total mass \(\mu(C)=1\), where here \(\mu(C)\) denotes total society mass; let \(L\) be the input bit length.
For any profile of total mass \(M\), define weighted STV with fixed quota \(q=M/(S+1)\) throughout the count and exact rational arithmetic. Initially each type's mass belongs to its first-ranked candidate. Continuing candidates are those neither elected nor eliminated.
After initialization and every transfer, declare all continuing candidates with tally at least \(q\) elected; if they exceed the remaining vacancies, take the highest tallies first. Transfer pending elected candidates' surpluses one at a time, largest first: from tally \(v\), multiply every held parcel's current mass by \((v-q)/v\) and transfer it to its highest-ranked continuing candidate. Elected candidates receive no further transfers.
When no surplus remains pending, eliminate the continuing candidate with least tally and transfer all its current mass to the highest-ranked continuing candidate on each ranking. Whenever the continuing candidates equal the remaining vacancies, elect them all; stop once \(S\) seats are filled. A fixed candidate priority order resolves every tie, including surplus order and excess quota achievers. Write \(W(\nu,S)\) for the resulting winner set.
Define \(T_k(b)\) and \(B_k(b)\) as the sets in the top and bottom \(k\) positions of ranking \(b\). The decision problem asks whether a complete ranking \(b\) and \(\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) exist such that, with \(A=W(\mu,S)\) and \(D=W(\mu+\lambda\delta_b,S)\),
\( |A|=S,\quad T_S(b)=A,\quad |A\cap D|=1,\quad T_{S-1}(b)\cap D=\varnothing,\quad B_{S-1}(b)\subseteq D. \)
Here \(\delta_b\) is unit mass on ranking \(b\); the augmented society is not renormalized and uses quota \((1+\lambda)/(S+1)\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,S\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite electorates with the integer Droop quota, such reversals exist for every \(S>1\): constructions use four candidates for \(S=2\) and \(2S-1\) for \(S>2\). [McCune](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.20045v2)
Recognizing a group no-show paradox under single-winner STV is NP-complete with lexicographic tie-breaking; that problem removes existing voters who all prefer the resulting winner. [IJCAI 2024](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0328.pdf) No result settling the exact continuous problem above was found.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize the original society to total mass one; addition increases total mass without renormalization.
  • Treat S as part of the input and L as the total binary input length; rankings are strict and complete.
  • Specify simultaneous election of quota achievers and descending pending-surplus transfers, following the Scottish conventions; replace historical and random tie resolution with a fixed candidate priority order.
  • Use exact arithmetic without Scottish five-decimal truncation, as appropriate for the stipulated proportional mass transfers.
  • The continuous quota can permit more quota achievers than vacancies at equality; select the highest tallies, resolving ties by the fixed priority order.
  • Include election of all remaining candidates when their number equals the unfilled vacancies, and transfer current parcel weights rather than original masses.

1. Basic constraints on successful witnesses

Write $b(j)$ for the candidate in position $j$ of $b$. Let
$$ q_0=\frac1{S+1}, \qquad q_\lambda=\frac{1+\lambda}{S+1}, $$
and let $t_c$ be candidate $c$’s initial tally in $\mu$.

1.1. Every count fills exactly $S$ seats

Let $r$ be the number of vacancies and $k$ the number of continuing candidates.

Initially $k=m\geq S=r$. Election decreases $k$ and $r$ equally. Elimination is permitted only when $k>r$, so it preserves $k\geq r$. Equality causes election of all remaining continuing candidates.

Each candidate is elected or eliminated at most once, and each elected candidate has its surplus processed at most once. Thus the count terminates, and termination fills all $S$ seats. Complete rankings ensure that every transfer made before termination has a continuing recipient.

1.2. A necessary upper bound on $\lambda$

Suppose $(b,\lambda)$ is successful, and put $a=b(1)$. Because $S>1$ and
$$ T_{S-1}(b)\cap D=\varnothing, $$
we have $a\notin D$.

I claim that
$$ t_a+\lambda<q_\lambda. $$

If at most $S$ candidates initially reach augmented quota, every such candidate is elected. Hence $a$ cannot reach quota.

If more than $S$ candidates initially reach quota, their tallies sum to at most
$$ 1+\lambda=(S+1)q_\lambda. $$
Consequently, exactly $S+1$ candidates reach quota, each has tally exactly $q_\lambda$, and every other candidate has tally zero.

Suppose $a$ is among those achievers but is excluded by priority. The other $S$ achievers have unchanged baseline tallies $q_\lambda>q_0$. They are all initially elected in the baseline count: there cannot be $S+1$ baseline quota achievers when even one tally exceeds $q_0$. Thus they fill all baseline seats, contradicting $a\in A$.

Therefore
$$ t_a+\lambda<\frac{1+\lambda}{S+1}. $$
Rearranging gives
$$ 0<\lambda< \frac{1-(S+1)t_a}{S} \leq\frac1S. $$

In particular, a successful ballot must begin with a baseline winner whose initial tally is strictly below $q_0$.

1.3. The augmented winner set is prescribed by the ballot

The outcome conditions imply
$$ D=\{b(S)\}\cup B_{S-1}(b). $$

Indeed, $T_S(b)=A$, and none of the first $S-1$ candidates belongs to $D$. Since $|A\cap D|=1$, the shared winner is $b(S)$. The bottom $S-1$ candidates supply all remaining augmented winners.

2. The bottom block receives no added support before the outcome is forced

Let
$$ z=b(S), \qquad H=B_{S-1}(b). $$
For a successful witness, $D=\{z\}\cup H$.

Proposition. Every transfer of positive added-ballot mass to a candidate in $H$ occurs only when the continuing-candidates rule already forces all remaining winners.

Proof. Every candidate outside $H$ is ranked above every candidate in $H$. Thus, if the added parcel is transferred to a continuing candidate in $H$, there is no continuing candidate outside $H$.

Let $E$ be the candidates already elected at that point. Since the final winner set is $\{z\}\cup H$, no member of $H$ has been eliminated. Moreover, $z$ is no longer continuing and must already have been elected. Therefore
$$ E=\{z\}\cup(E\cap H), $$
and the continuing candidates are exactly $H\setminus E$. Their number is
$$ |H\setminus E| =(S-1)-(|E|-1) =S-|E|, $$
which equals the number of vacancies. The rule therefore elects them all. $\square$

This proposition gives a useful reduction of the ballot search.

Corollary. If a ballot is successful, arbitrarily permuting its bottom $S-1$ positions preserves success.

Proof. Until added mass first reaches the bottom block, the two ballots have identical transfer recipients. Hence their counts are identical. At the first possible difference, the proposition shows that the remaining winners are already forced. $\square$

We may consequently require the bottom block to be ordered canonically by candidate priority.

With $A$ fixed, the number of rankings satisfying $T_S(b)=A$ and this canonical-bottom restriction is
$$ S!\binom{m-S}{S-1}(m-2S+1)! =S(m-S)!. $$
Here the three factors choose the top order, the bottom set, and the middle order, respectively. This reduction is substantial, although still exponential in general.

3. A sharper exact decision algorithm

3.1. Enumerating actions instead of comparison outcomes

A counting history records:

  • each election batch;
  • each elimination;
  • each surplus transfer;
  • whether an elected candidate has zero or positive surplus.

It need not record every intermediate comparison outcome.

There are at most $m$ election-or-elimination events and at most $S-1$ surplus transfers before the count stops. Election batches and checkpoints can be represented by delimiters in a word of length $O(m)$ over an alphabet of size $O(m)$. Therefore the number of possible histories is bounded by
$$ 2^{O(m\log m)}. $$

For a prescribed history, its validity can be expressed using a conjunction of tally comparisons.

For example, suppose a checkpoint has $r$ vacancies and the history specifies an election batch $E$.

  • If $|E|<r$, every member of $E$ must reach quota, and every continuing candidate outside $E$ must be below quota.
  • If $|E|=r$, every member of $E$ must reach quota, and each selected candidate must precede every unselected continuing candidate when tallies are ordered from highest to lowest with the prescribed tie-breaking.
  • If the number of continuing candidates equals $r$, automatic election applies instead; this condition depends only on the discrete history.

A specified elimination is enforced by requiring its candidate to have minimum continuing tally, with strict or weak inequalities according to priority. A specified surplus transfer is enforced similarly among pending candidates. Since quota is fixed, ordering surpluses is equivalent to ordering the elected candidates’ tallies.

Zero or positive surplus is enforced by equality or strict inequality against quota. The discrete history also checks that a surplus is transferred only after all mandatory elections and that elimination occurs only when no surplus remains pending.

There are $O(m)$ checkpoints. Quota tests, minimum and maximum tests, and the final election-batch comparisons together require $O(m^2)$ comparisons.

3.2. Polynomial expressions along one history

Fix a ballot $b$ and a history. Introduce a variable $x$ for the added mass.

Choose a positive integer $K$ divisible by $S+1$ and every input mass denominator. We may use their product, so
$$ \operatorname{bits}(K)=O(L+\log(S+1)). $$
Scaling masses and quota by $K$ preserves every count decision. The scaled augmented quota is
$$ Q(x)=\frac{K}{S+1}(1+x). $$

For each original ranking type, and for the added ballot, retain its active parcel. The quota portion retained by an elected candidate is discarded from this representation.

There is at most one active parcel per type. An elimination moves that parcel intact. A surplus transfer retains part of it and sends the remaining part to one recipient; it does not create two active descendants.

Represent all active masses with a common denominator:
$$ p_i(x)=\frac{P_i(x)}{D(x)}. $$
Initially $D=1$, the original $P_i$ are integer constants, and the added numerator is $Kx$.

Elimination changes only parcel holders. If an elected candidate has tally $V/D$, where $V$ is the sum of its parcel numerators, its transfer factor is
$$ \frac{V-QD}{V}. $$
The following common-denominator update is exact:
$$ D_{\mathrm{new}}=DV, $$
$$ P_{i,\mathrm{new}}= \begin{cases} P_i(V-QD),&\text{if parcel }i\text{ is transferred},\\ P_iV,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
The transferred parcels change holders; their retained portions are removed.

Include $D>0$ and $V>0$ among the history conditions whenever needed. Clearing denominators then preserves signs.

After $r$ surplus transfers,
$$ \deg P_i\leq 2^r, \qquad \deg D\leq 2^r-1. $$
This follows by induction. If the bounds hold at $r$, then both $V$ and $QD$ have degree at most $2^r$. Each new numerator consequently has degree at most $2^{r+1}$, while $DV$ has degree at most $2^{r+1}-1$.

At most $S-1$ surplus transfers are processed: the count stops when the last seat is filled. Every comparison polynomial therefore has degree at most
$$ d=2^{S-1}. $$

For coefficient bounds, use the polynomial norm
$$ \|F\|_1=\sum_j |F_j|. $$
It satisfies
$$ \|FG\|_1\leq\|F\|_1\|G\|_1, \qquad \|F+G\|_1\leq\|F\|_1+\|G\|_1. $$
Let $N=\tau+1$, and let $h_r$ bound the logarithmic norms of the active numerators and denominator after $r$ transfers. Let $h_0$ additionally bound that of $Q$. A tally sums at most $N$ numerators. The update above gives
$$ h_{r+1}\leq 2h_r+h_0+\log_2 N+2. $$
Solving this recurrence bounds comparison-polynomial coefficient bit lengths by
$$ H=2^{O(S)} \bigl(L+\log(\tau+1)+\log(S+1)+1\bigr). $$

Thus a prescribed history gives $k=O(m^2)$ integer-polynomial sign conditions, with degree and coefficient bounds $d,H$ as above.

3.3. Rational feasibility, with endpoint details

Impose
$$ x>0, \qquad 1-Sx>0, $$
using Section 1.

First remove identically zero comparison polynomials. Their conditions are either automatic or contradictory. Form the product $F$ of the remaining polynomials, together with $x$ and $1-Sx$.

Its degree and coefficient bit length are polynomial in $k,d,H$.

I use the standard effective univariate root-isolation theorem: an integer polynomial of degree $D$ and coefficient bit length $H_F$ has its distinct real roots isolated with rational endpoints in time polynomial in $D,H_F$; multiplicities are permitted. Isolation and refinement to a prescribed additional polynomial number of bits also have polynomial bit complexity. These are the hypotheses needed here, and they hold for the explicitly constructed integer polynomial $F$. See [Emiris, Mourrain, and Tsigaridas, *Real Algebraic Numbers: Complexity Analysis and Experimentation*](https://who.paris.inria.fr/Elias.Tsigaridas/files/emt-lncs-2006.pdf).

The roots divide $(0,1/S)$ into open intervals and individual root points.

Open intervals. Refine the isolating intervals for the sorted distinct roots, including the known boundary roots $0$ and $1/S$, until adjacent isolating intervals are strictly separated. For an exactly represented rational root, either use its singleton representation or surround it by a sufficiently small rational isolating interval.

If consecutive root enclosures have right and left endpoints $u_i<\ell_{i+1}$, respectively, use
$$ \frac{u_i+\ell_{i+1}}2 $$
as the sample. It lies strictly between the roots, including for cells adjacent to the boundary roots.

Standard root-separation bounds guarantee that polynomially many bits of refinement suffice. Every comparison polynomial has constant sign on each open cell, so testing this rational sample decides the whole cell.

Individual roots. Let
$$ B=|\operatorname{lc}(F)|. $$
If an isolated root is a reduced rational $p/q$, the rational-root theorem gives
$$ q\mid B, \qquad q\leq B. $$

Refine its isolating interval to width less than $1/(4B^2)$, and let $r$ be its rational midpoint. If the root is $p/q$, then
$$ \left|r-\frac pq\right| <\frac1{8B^2} <\frac1{2q^2}. $$
By Legendre’s continued-fraction theorem, $p/q$ is a convergent of $r$. Enumerate the finitely many convergents of $r$, retain those in the isolating interval with denominator at most $B$, and test $F(p/q)=0$ exactly.

There can be at most one eligible rational in the interval, because distinct rationals with denominators at most $B$ differ by at least $1/B^2$. Thus this procedure determines whether the isolated root is rational. For a rational root, evaluate all history conditions exactly; discard irrational roots.

The required extra precision is $O(\log B)$. Continued fractions and rational polynomial evaluation have polynomial bit complexity in these bounds.

This also repairs the numerator issue identified by the verifier. For a root in the permitted range,
$$ 0<\frac pq<\frac1S<1, $$
so
$$ 0<p<q\leq B. $$
No numerator-divisibility argument using the zero constant coefficient of $F$ is needed.

3.4. Running time and fixed-seat certificates

Compute the baseline winner set exactly. The same arithmetic bounds give time
$$ 2^{O(S)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

Enumerate the canonically ordered ballots from Section 2 and all action histories. Reject histories whose prescribed winner sets violate the required outcome conditions. Test the remaining histories by Section 3.3.

Each history costs
$$ \operatorname{poly}(k,d,H) =2^{O(S)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
The ballot and history enumerations each have size at most $2^{O(m\log m)}$. Since $m\geq 2S-1$, the total bound is
$$ 2^{O(m\log m)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L). $$

The procedure is complete: every successful witness can be put into canonical bottom order and supplies one enumerated history. It is sound because an accepted rational parameter satisfies every condition enforcing that history.

Moreover, every yes-instance has a rational witness of bit length
$$ 2^{O(S)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
For an open cell this follows from the rational sample bounds. For an isolated rational root it follows from $0<p<q\leq B$.

For fixed $S$, this certificate length is polynomial. Given $(b,\lambda)$, exact simulation uses polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial bit length, by the same norm recurrence after incorporating $\lambda$’s denominator into $K$. Therefore the problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ for every fixed $S$.

For variable $S$, these arguments establish neither polynomial certificate length nor polynomial-time verification.

4. The two-seat cases

4.1. Three candidates: every instance is negative

Suppose a successful witness exists with $S=2,m=3$. Relabel so that
$$ A=\{a,z\}, \qquad b=a\succ z\succ d, \qquad D=\{z,d\}. $$
Let baseline initial tallies be $u,v,w$, respectively. Thus
$$ u+v+w=1. $$
Put $q_0=1/3$ and $q'=(1+\lambda)/3$.

Section 1 gives
$$ u+\lambda<q', \qquad u<q_0. $$
At least one augmented initial tally reaches quota, because the three tallies sum to $3q'$.

Candidate $d$ cannot initially reach augmented quota. Otherwise $w\geq q'>q_0$, forcing its initial baseline election, contrary to $d\notin A$. Hence $z$ initially reaches quota in both counts.

Also $w<q_0$: otherwise $z$ and $d$ would fill both baseline seats initially. Thus $z$ is the only initial quota achiever in either count.

Let $p\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of $z$’s initial mass whose highest-ranked continuing candidate after $z$’s election is $a$. The remaining fraction goes to $d$. The added ballot initially belongs to $a$, so it does not alter this fraction.

After the baseline surplus transfer,
$$ U_a=u+p(v-q_0), \qquad U_d=w+(1-p)(v-q_0). $$
Their sum is $2q_0$. For $a$ to win, necessarily $U_a\geq U_d$.

The augmented tallies are
$$ U'_a=u+\lambda+p(v-q'), \qquad U'_d=w+(1-p)(v-q'). $$
Therefore
$$ U'_a-U'_d =(U_a-U_d)+\frac{4-2p}{3}\lambda>0. $$
Their sum is $2q'$, so $U'_a>q'$. Candidate $a$ wins, a contradiction.

This proof includes tally ties.

4.2. Four candidates: an interval of successful masses

Take the following dyadic society:

| Mass | Ranking |
|---|---|
| $205/512$ | $z\succ d\succ c\succ a$ |
| $118/512$ | $a\succ z\succ d\succ c$ |
| $109/512$ | $c\succ a\succ z\succ d$ |
| $80/512$ | $d\succ c\succ a\succ z$ |

Use
$$ b=a\succ z\succ d\succ c. $$
Scale masses by $512$, and write $x=512\lambda$.

In the baseline count, quota is $512/3$. Candidate $z$ is elected and transfers surplus $103/3$ to $d$. The remaining tallies are
$$ a:118,\qquad c:109,\qquad d:\frac{343}{3}. $$
Candidate $c$ is uniquely lowest, and its mass transfers to $a$. Thus
$$ A=\{z,a\}. $$

Now take any rational
$$ 16<x<79. $$
The augmented quota is $(512+x)/3$. Only $z$ initially reaches quota: in particular,
$$ 118+x<\frac{512+x}{3} $$
is equivalent to $x<79$.

After $z$’s surplus transfer, the remaining tallies are
$$ a:118+x,\qquad c:109,\qquad d:\frac{343-x}{3}. $$
Since $x>16$, candidate $d$ is uniquely lowest. Its mass transfers to $c$, giving
$$ v_c=\frac{670-x}{3}> \frac{512+x}{3}, $$
where the strict inequality is equivalent to $x<79$. Hence
$$ D=\{z,c\}. $$

All required ranking conditions hold. For example, $x=18$ gives the dyadic witness
$$ \lambda=\frac9{256}. $$

Here the shared winner is initially elected in both counts. The reversal results from the smaller surplus changing the first elimination.

5. Minimal-candidate constructions for every larger seat count

I now construct yes-instances for every $S\geq 3$ with $m=2S-1$.

5.1. Definition of the society

Put
$$ n=S-1\geq 2, \qquad t=\frac{n+1}{2n}, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{100n^3}. $$
Use candidates
$$ \{z\}\cup X\cup Y, \qquad X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}, \qquad Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_n\}. $$

First specify an unnormalized society of mass $n+2=S+1$. Its baseline quota is therefore $1$.

Give $z$ mass $1+\varepsilon$ on a ranking beginning
$$ z\succ x_1\succ y_1. $$

Define
$$ u_1=t-\varepsilon,\qquad u_2=t+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad u_i=t\quad(3\leq i\leq n), $$
and
$$ v_1=t-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad v_j=t\quad(2\leq j\leq n). $$

For each $i,j$, place mass $u_i/n$ on a ranking beginning
$$ x_i\succ y_j, $$
and mass $v_j/n$ on a ranking beginning
$$ y_j\succ x_i. $$
Complete every specified prefix to a strict complete ranking arbitrarily.

The total mass is
$$ 1+\varepsilon+\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j =1+\varepsilon+2nt-\varepsilon =n+2. $$
There are $2n^2+1$ positive types.

Add mass
$$ \ell=\frac34(n+2)\varepsilon $$
on the ballot
$$ b=x_2\succ x_3\succ\cdots\succ x_n \succ x_1\succ z \succ y_1\succ\cdots\succ y_n. $$
The augmented quota is
$$ q'=1+\frac34\varepsilon. $$

After dividing the original society by $n+2$, the input has total mass $1$ and the witness mass is
$$ \lambda=\frac{\ell}{n+2}=\frac34\varepsilon. $$
Scaling invariance makes it sufficient to prove the outcomes in the unnormalized society.

5.2. Baseline outcome

Candidate $z$ is the only initial quota achiever. It transfers surplus $\varepsilon$ to $x_1$.

The resulting tallies are
$$ x_1:t,\qquad x_2:t+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad x_i:t\quad(i\geq3), $$
and
$$ y_1:t-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad y_j:t\quad(j\geq2). $$
Thus $y_1$ is uniquely lowest.

Suppose $k$ candidates from $Y$ have been eliminated, with $y_1$ among them, and no candidate from $X$ has yet been elected. Every eliminated $Y$ candidate transfers its mass equally among all $n$ candidates in $X$. Their tallies lie between
$$ t\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2n} $$
and
$$ t\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$

For $k\leq n-2$, the upper bound is at most
$$ t\left(1+\frac{n-2}{n}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2} =1-\frac1{n^2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<1. $$
So no member of $X$ has reached quota.

For $k\geq1$, the lower bound exceeds $t$, since
$$ \frac{t}{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2n}>0. $$
Every remaining $Y$ candidate has tally at most $t$. Consequently, another member of $Y$ is eliminated.

This inductively justifies the stated transfer calculation up to $k=n-1$. At that point, every member of $X$ has tally at least
$$ t\left(1+\frac{n-1}{n}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2n} = 1+\frac{n-1}{2n^2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2n} >1. $$
All $n$ members of $X$ are declared elected together, filling the remaining seats. Hence
$$ A=\{z\}\cup X. $$

Before this final declaration, the continuing-candidate count is $2n-k>n$, so automatic election does not intervene.

5.3. Augmented outcome

Initially, only $z$ reaches augmented quota. Indeed, the largest other possible initial tally is
$$ t+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\ell =t+\left(\frac{3n}{4}+2\right)\varepsilon \leq t+2n\varepsilon<1. $$
Here $n\geq2$, $t\leq3/4$, and $2n\varepsilon\leq1/200$.

Candidate $z$ transfers surplus $\varepsilon/4$ to $x_1$. Thus $x_1$ has tally
$$ t-\frac34\varepsilon, $$
strictly less than $y_1$’s tally $t-\varepsilon/2$. It is uniquely lowest and is eliminated first.

Its own mass $u_1$ is divided equally among $Y$. The inherited $z$ surplus goes to $y_1$, because the $z$ ranking begins $z\succ x_1\succ y_1$.

As long as an $X$ candidate remains continuing, the added ballot stays within $X$: all members of $X$ are ranked above $z$ and $Y$, and $z$ has already been elected.

Let $E_k\subseteq X$ be the $k$ eliminated candidates, including $x_1$. Until the final election batch, each $y_j$ has tally
$$ v_j+\frac1n\sum_{x_i\in E_k}u_i +\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mathbf1_{\{j=1\}}. $$
The initial $X$ masses satisfy
$$ kt-\varepsilon \leq\sum_{x_i\in E_k}u_i \leq kt+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$
Therefore every $Y$ tally lies between
$$ t\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right) -\left(\frac12+\frac1n\right)\varepsilon $$
and
$$ t\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right) +\frac{\varepsilon}{2n}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4} \leq t\left(1+\frac{k}{n}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$

For $k\leq n-2$, the upper bound is less than $1$, and hence below $q'$. No member of $Y$ is elected prematurely.

Each remaining $X$ candidate has tally at most
$$ t+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\ell \leq t+2n\varepsilon. $$
For $k\geq1$, every $Y$ tally is at least
$$ t+\frac tn-\varepsilon. $$
The difference between this lower bound and the $X$ upper bound is
$$ \frac tn-(2n+1)\varepsilon>0. $$
Indeed,
$$ \frac{t/n}{\varepsilon}=50n(n+1)>2n+1. $$
Thus another member of $X$ is eliminated at every nonfinal stage.

At $k=n-1$, every member of $Y$ has tally at least
$$ 1+\frac{n-1}{2n^2} -\left(\frac12+\frac1n\right)\varepsilon. $$
This exceeds $q'=1+3\varepsilon/4$, because
$$ \frac{(n-1)/(2n^2)}{\varepsilon} =50n(n-1) > \frac54+\frac1n. $$

All $n$ members of $Y$ are therefore declared elected together. The last continuing $X$ candidate loses. Consequently,
$$ D=\{z\}\cup Y. $$

The top $S$ positions of $b$ are precisely $\{z\}\cup X$, its first $S-1$ positions are $X$, and its bottom $S-1$ positions are $Y$. All required conditions hold.

5.4. Dyadic masses and polynomial-size descriptions
