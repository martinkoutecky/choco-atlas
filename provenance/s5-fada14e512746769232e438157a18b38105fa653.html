Partial progress on the unrestricted mass-allocation problem

I do not resolve the requested complexity classification. I prove two additional results for arbitrary support size and binary-encoded supplies:

  • There is a fully polynomial approximation scheme that preserves every supply equality exactly and returns rational masses. For any rational $0<\varepsilon<1$, its welfare is at least $e^{-\varepsilon}$ times optimal.
  • One can decide in polynomial time whether the allocation-independent Jensen upper bound is attainable. When it is, one can construct an exact maximizing allocation in polynomial time.

The proofs use a further structural reduction: bundles can be restricted to a constant number of one-dimensional families of valuation-count profiles.

1. Definitions and algorithmic prerequisite

Fix the constants $p>q\ge 3$, with $\gcd(p,q)=1$, and put
$$ a=\frac pq,\qquad B=\sum_{j=1}^m b_j. $$
For a bundle $z$, define its cardinality and its utility to type $t$ by
$$ n(z)=\sum_j z_j,\qquad u_t(z)=\sum_j v_{tj}z_j. $$
Thus every admissible bundle satisfies
$$ 1\le u_t(z)\le a\,n(z). $$

Write
$$ F(x)=\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log u_t(z). $$
Maximizing welfare is equivalent to maximizing $F$, because the exponential function is strictly increasing.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: feasibility and bounded optimization for an explicitly given rational linear program can be solved exactly in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. An optimal vertex of a nonempty bounded polytope can also be obtained in polynomial time, for example by successively optimizing coordinates on the optimal face. This is the rational linear-programming framework originating with [Khachiyan’s polynomial algorithm](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). All applications below have explicitly listed rational constraints; the optimization application also has rational objective coefficients.

2. Existence and a finite bundle bound

These facts are needed to justify approximating an actual optimum rather than merely a supremum.

Proposition 1: An optimum exists

First relax the supply equalities to inequalities:
$$ \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j\le b_j. $$
Retain the type-mass equalities and the requirement that bundles be nonempty.

The relaxed problem is feasible. Indeed, assigning
$$ x_{t,e_j}=\mu_t\frac{b_j}{B} $$
gives type mass $\mu_t$ and consumes $b_j/B\le b_j$ of cohort $j$.

Every relaxed feasible allocation satisfies
$$ \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}n(z)\le B. $$
Consequently, its logarithmic objective is finite and bounded above, since
$$ 0\le F(x)\le \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}a\,n(z)\le aB. $$

Take a maximizing sequence. The pairs $(t,z)$ form a countable set, and each mass lies in $[0,1]$. A diagonal subsequence therefore converges coordinatewise to some $x^*$.

For every $R>0$, the mass assigned to bundles with $n(z)>R$ is at most $B/R$, uniformly along this subsequence. There are finitely many bundles with $n(z)\le R$. Coordinatewise convergence on that finite set, followed by letting $R$ tend to infinity, proves
$$ \sum_zx^*_{t,z}=\mu_t $$
for each type. The supply inequalities pass to the limit by taking finite partial sums of their nonnegative terms.

The objective passes to the limit as well. Define
$$ \eta_R=\sup_{\substack{n\in\mathbb Z\\n>R}}\frac{\log(an)}n. $$
Then $\eta_R\to0$, and uniformly over relaxed feasible allocations,
$$ \sum_{t,z:\,n(z)>R}x_{t,z}\log u_t(z)\le B\eta_R. $$
The finite initial sums converge, and this bound makes their tails uniformly negligible. Hence $x^*$ attains the relaxed maximum.

Finally, a relaxed optimum cannot leave positive slack in any cohort. If cohort $j$ has slack, choose a positive-mass atom $(t,z)$ and move a sufficiently small positive portion of its mass to $(t,z+e_j)$. This preserves type mass, respects all supply inequalities, and strictly increases utility on that portion. Thus all supply inequalities are tight at an optimum. This proves existence for the original problem. $\square$

Proposition 2: Every optimal bundle has bounded cardinality

Let two positive-mass atoms of an optimum have utilities
$$ u=u_t(z),\qquad w=u_s(y). $$
I claim that
$$ u\le aw+a. $$

Otherwise, choose a cohort $j$ present in $z$, and write
$$ A=v_{tj},\qquad C=v_{sj}. $$
Transfer one unit of cohort $j$ from the donor bundle to the recipient bundle on equal sufficiently small portions of population mass. This preserves every supply and every type mass.

The donor remains nonempty because
$$ u-A>w\ge1. $$
The product of the two affected utilities changes by
$$ (u-A)(w+C)-uw =C\left(u-\frac ACw-A\right)>0, $$
using $A/C\le a$, $A\le a$, and $u>aw+a$. Therefore the logarithmic objective increases, a contradiction.

The mean utility of an optimum is at most $aB$, so some positive-mass atom has utility at most $aB$. Applying the preceding inequality with that atom as the recipient gives
$$ u_t(z)\le a^2B+a $$
for every positive-mass atom. Since $n(z)\le u_t(z)$, every such bundle satisfies
$$ n(z)\le K,\qquad K=\left\lfloor a^2B+a\right\rfloor. $$
The binary length of $K$ is polynomial in $L$.

In particular, an optimum has finite support: there are only finitely many bundles of cardinality at most $K$. $\square$

3. A reduction to one-dimensional profile families

For type $t$, define
$$ J_t^H=\{j:v_{tj}=a\},\qquad J_t^L=\{j:v_{tj}=1\}. $$
A bundle has profile $(h,l)$ if it contains $h$ high-valued goods and $l$ low-valued goods. Its utility is
$$ ah+l=\frac{ph+ql}{q}. $$

Lemma 3: Concentrating each valuation class

A finite allocation can be replaced, preserving every supply and its objective, by one in which each bundle uses at most one high-valued cohort and at most one low-valued cohort.

To prove this, consider an atom of mass $\lambda$ with bundle $z$ and profile $(h,l)$.

If both counts are positive, put
$$ \rho_j=\frac{z_j}{h}\quad(j\in J_t^H),\qquad \sigma_k=\frac{z_k}{l}\quad(k\in J_t^L). $$
Replace the atom by bundles
$$ h e_j+l e_k $$
of masses $\lambda\rho_j\sigma_k$. Their total mass is $\lambda$, and they all have the original utility. Their consumption of a high-valued cohort $j$ is
$$ \sum_k\lambda\rho_j\sigma_k h=\lambda z_j. $$
The low-valued calculation is identical.

If exactly one count is positive, use the corresponding one-class construction. These replacements preserve rationality whenever the original masses are rational. $\square$

Lemma 4: Restriction to two strips

Every nonempty profile $(h,l)$ can be replaced by a mixture of at most two profiles, preserving its utility and its average high and low counts, such that each replacement satisfies
$$ h<q\quad\text{or}\quad l<p. $$

Let
$$ k=ph+ql>0. $$
The nonnegative integer solutions to
$$ ph'+ql'=k $$
have high counts in an arithmetic progression of step $q$. Let $h_{\min}$ and $h_{\max}$ be its minimum and maximum high counts.

One has
$$ h_{\min}<q. $$
Indeed, otherwise replacing $(h_{\min},l_{\max})$ by
$$ (h_{\min}-q,l_{\max}+p) $$
would give another nonnegative solution with smaller high count.

Similarly, the low count corresponding to $h_{\max}$ satisfies
$$ l_{\min}<p, $$
because otherwise $(h_{\max}+q,l_{\min}-p)$ would be feasible.

If the endpoints differ, set
$$ \theta=\frac{h_{\max}-h}{h_{\max}-h_{\min}}. $$
Then $0\le\theta\le1$ and
$$ (h,l) =\theta(h_{\min},l_{\max}) +(1-\theta)(h_{\max},l_{\min}). $$
Both endpoints have utility $k/q$. If the endpoints coincide, no replacement is needed.

These profiles are nonempty because $k>0$. Also, if the original profile uses only one valuation class, it is already an endpoint, so the replacement does not require an unavailable valuation class. $\square$

Combining the two lemmas, an optimum can be represented using the families
$$ (h,l)=(r,n),\quad 0\le r<q, $$
and
$$ (h,l)=(n,r),\quad 0\le r<p, $$
with the empty profile excluded.

Moreover, all endpoint profiles obtained from an optimal bundle satisfy
$$ h+l\le\frac{k}{q}\le aK. $$
Thus their coordinates are at most
$$ R=\lceil aK\rceil. $$

This improves the profile enumeration from a two-dimensional grid to a constant number of one-dimensional families. Its direct enumeration is still not polynomial in binary input length, because $R$ can be numerically large.

4. A fully polynomial approximation scheme

Theorem 5

For every rational $0<\varepsilon<1$, one can construct a feasible allocation $\widehat x$ satisfying
$$ F(\widehat x)\ge F^*-\varepsilon, $$
where $F^*$ is the optimal logarithmic objective.

Equivalently,
$$ \operatorname{Welfare}(\widehat x) \ge e^{-\varepsilon}\operatorname{Welfare}^*. $$

The running time is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, the encoding length of $\varepsilon$, and $1/\varepsilon$. The output can have at most $\tau+m$ positive rational masses, with polynomial total binary length.

Step 1: Construct an integer mesh

Let
$$ s=\left\lceil\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right\rceil. $$
Construct a mesh $\mathcal G\subseteq\{0,\ldots,R\}$ containing $0$ and $1$. Starting from its largest positive entry $n<R$, append
$$ \min\left(R,n+\max\left(1,\left\lfloor\frac ns\right\rfloor\right)\right). $$

The mesh has
$$ O\bigl(s(1+\log R)\bigr) $$
entries. There are at most $s$ initial unit steps. Once $n\ge s$, every uncapped step increases $n$ by at least $n/(2s)$, because
$$ \left\lfloor\frac ns\right\rfloor\ge\frac{n}{2s}. $$
Geometric growth then gives the stated bound.

If an integer $n$ is not in the mesh and lies between consecutive entries $L<U$, then
$$ L\ge1,\qquad U-L\le\frac Ls. $$
Indeed, below $s$ the mesh takes unit steps, which leave no intervening integers. At or above $s$, the displayed step bound follows from the construction.

Step 2: Bound the loss from interpolation

Consider one of the profile families from Lemma 4, holding its small coordinate fixed and varying its other coordinate $n$.

Its logarithmic utility has the form
$$ f(n)=c_0+\log(c+n) $$
for constants $c\ge0$ and $c_0$. For example,
$$ \log(ah+n) $$
has this form directly, while
$$ \log(an+l)=\log a+\log(n+l/a). $$

For an integer $n\notin\mathcal G$, let $L<U$ be the neighboring mesh entries and put
$$ \theta=\frac{U-n}{U-L}. $$
Replacing the profile by its versions at $L$ and $U$, with weights $\theta$ and $1-\theta$, preserves the mean varying count.

On $[L,U]$,
$$ f''(x)=-\frac1{(c+x)^2}\ge-\frac1{L^2}. $$
The function $f(x)+x^2/(2L^2)$ is therefore convex. Its value at $n$ is at most its chord value, giving
$$ f(n)-\theta f(L)-(1-\theta)f(U) \le \frac{\theta(1-\theta)(U-L)^2}{2L^2} \le\frac1{8s^2}. $$
The loss is nonnegative by concavity of $f$.

If $n$ already belongs to the mesh, no replacement is needed. If $n>0$, both interpolating endpoints are positive; hence this operation never creates an empty profile.

Step 3: List the retained bundles

Retain the profiles
$$ (r,n),\qquad 0\le r<q,\quad n\in\mathcal G, $$
and
$$ (n,r),\qquad 0\le r<p,\quad n\in\mathcal G, $$
excluding $(0,0)$.

For each type, list the corresponding bundles using one high-valued cohort and one low-valued cohort when both counts are positive, or just the appropriate cohort when only one count is positive.

The total number of columns is
$$ O\bigl(\tau m^2(p+q)s(1+\log R)\bigr). $$
For fixed $p,q$, this is polynomial in the required approximation parameters and input size.

Step 4: The retained columns contain a near-optimum

Take an optimum, which is finite by Proposition 2. Apply Lemma 3, then Lemma 4, and finally the mesh interpolation.

Each operation preserves type masses and every cohort total. For the last operation, preserve the cohort identities used by the concentrated bundle and interpolate only its multiplicity. The total objective loss is at most
$$ \frac1{8s^2}\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z} =\frac1{8s^2} \le\frac{\varepsilon}{8}. $$

Consequently, the linear program over retained columns is nonempty, and its best true logarithmic objective is at least
$$ F^*-\frac{\varepsilon}{8}. $$

Step 5: Use rational objective coefficients

For every retained bundle, compute a rational approximation to its logarithmic utility with absolute error at most
$$ \eta=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}. $$

These approximations can be computed in polynomial time. Here is an explicit justification. For a positive rational utility $u\ge1$, write
$$ u=2^d y,\qquad 1\le y<2. $$
Then
$$ \log u=d\log2+\log y. $$
For $1\le y\le2$, put $t=(y-1)/(y+1)$, so $0\le t\le1/3$. The expansion
$$ \log y =2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2i+1}}{2i+1} $$
has tail after its first $N$ terms at most
$$ \frac{2t^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-t^2)}. $$
The same expansion computes $\log2$. Geometric decay permits the required accuracy with a number of terms polynomial in the binary length of $u$ and $\log(1/\eta)$. The rational partial sums also have polynomial binary length.

Now solve the retained-column linear program exactly with these rational objective coefficients. Let $\widetilde F$ denote its approximate objective. Since every feasible allocation has total mass one,
$$ |\widetilde F(x)-F(x)|\le\eta $$
for every feasible $x$.

Let $\widehat x$ maximize $\widetilde F$, and let $x^\circ$ be the near-optimum constructed in Step 4. Then
$$ F(\widehat x) \ge\widetilde F(\widehat x)-\eta \ge\widetilde F(x^\circ)-\eta \ge F(x^\circ)-2\eta \ge F^*-\frac{5\varepsilon}{8}. $$
This proves the asserted, slightly weaker, error bound $\varepsilon$.

All constraints are exact rational constraints, so every supply equality holds exactly in the returned allocation.

Step 6: Output length and sparsity

Choose an optimal vertex of the rational linear program. Its constraint column for a bundle of type $t$ is
$$ (e_t,z) $$
in dimension
$$ d=\tau+m. $$
The positive columns at a vertex are linearly independent: a dependence among them would permit sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions. Thus at most $d$ masses are positive.

Put
$$ C=\max(R,p,q), $$
and let $D$ be the product of the denominators of the input masses and supplies. Every constraint-matrix entry has absolute value at most $C$.

For the positive columns, select a nonsingular square row submatrix. Cramer’s rule gives a common mass denominator dividing
$$ D|\Delta|, $$
where
$$ |\Delta|\le d!C^d. $$
Therefore mass denominators have binary length at most
$$ \log_2D+O(d\log d+d\log C). $$
Every mass lies in $[0,1]$, so its numerator has the same bound. Bundle coordinates also have polynomial binary length.

Discarding zero entries gives the claimed finite rational output. $\square$

5. Exact feasibility for prescribed utilities

The next result gives an exact polynomial-time primitive, without approximating logarithms.

Theorem 6

Suppose each type $t$ is given a positive rational target utility $c_t$. One can decide in polynomial time whether there is an allocation in which every positive-mass bundle of type $t$ has utility exactly $c_t$.

If feasible, such an allocation can be constructed with polynomially many bundles and rational masses of polynomial binary length.

The result remains valid if each type is restricted to a specified set of allowed cohorts.

Proof

Every scaled utility is an integer. Thus feasibility requires
$$ k_t=qc_t\in\mathbb Z_{>0}. $$
Reject if this condition fails.

For a fixed type, its admissible profiles solve
$$ ph+ql=k_t,\qquad h,l\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}. $$
Compute the unique residue
$$ h_0\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\} $$
such that
$$ ph_0\equiv k_t\pmod q. $$
It exists uniquely because $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

A nonnegative solution exists exactly when
$$ h_0\le\left\lfloor\frac{k_t}{p}\right\rfloor. $$
If it exists, the endpoint high counts are
$$ h_{\min}=h_0, $$
and
$$ h_{\max} =h_0+q\left\lfloor \frac{\lfloor k_t/p\rfloor-h_0}{q} \right\rfloor. $$
Their low counts are obtained from
$$ l=\frac{k_t-ph}{q}. $$

For each type, retain these at most two endpoint profiles. List their concentrated bundles as in Lemma 3, using only allowed cohorts. These are nonempty because $k_t>0$.

There are $O(\tau m^2)$ columns. Impose the original type-mass and supply equalities on their masses.

If this rational linear program is feasible, every listed bundle of type $t$ has utility $c_t$, so it directly supplies the required allocation.

Conversely, any allocation with the prescribed utilities has finite support: its type-$t$ bundles have cardinality at most $k_t/q$. Apply Lemma 3 and then the endpoint replacement of Lemma 4 to every atom. These operations preserve cohort consumption and use the same allowed cohort identities. They produce a feasible solution of the listed linear program.

The endpoint coordinates have binary length polynomial in that of $c_t$. Rational linear programming therefore decides feasibility and returns a polynomial-length rational solution. $\square$

This construction explicitly excludes zero utility and the empty profile, repairing the domain issue identified in the previous write-up.

6. Recognizing an attainable Jensen upper bound

Define the maximum possible value of each cohort by
$$ V_j=\max_t v_{tj}, $$
and put
$$ M=\sum_j V_jb_j. $$
This is a rational, allocation-independent bound on mean utility.

Theorem 7

One can decide in polynomial time whether optimal welfare equals $M$. If it does, one can construct an exact maximizing allocation in polynomial time.

Proof

For any feasible allocation, let
$$ U=\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}u_t(z). $$
Using the supply equalities,
$$ U\le\sum_jV_jb_j=M. $$

The tangent inequality for the logarithm states that
$$ \log u\le\log M+\frac{u-M}{M}, $$
with equality exactly when $u=M$. Summing it over the allocation gives
$$ F(x)\le\log M+\frac{U-M}{M}\le\log M. $$
Hence welfare is at most $M$.

Equality holds precisely when both of the following conditions hold:

  • Every positive-mass bundle has utility $M$.
  • Every allocated good is assigned to a type valuing its cohort at $V_j$.

For the second condition, observe that
$$ M-U =\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\sum_j(V_j-v_{tj})z_j. $$
All terms are nonnegative. Thus $U=M$ exactly when every positive-mass bundle uses only cohorts satisfying $v_{tj}=V_j$.

Apply Theorem 6 with the common target
$$ c_t=M $$
and allowed cohort sets
$$ E_t=\{j:v_{tj}=V_j\}. $$
A feasible answer constructs an allocation satisfying both equality conditions, and therefore an exact optimum of welfare $M$.

An infeasible answer proves that no allocation reaches $M$. Since an optimum exists by Proposition 1, optimal welfare is then strictly less than $M$.

The numbers $V_j$, $M$, and the allowed sets have polynomial encoding length, so the entire test runs in polynomial time. $\square$
