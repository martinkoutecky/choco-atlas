Theorem B.3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), an explicitly listed family \(\mathcal G=\{G_1,\ldots,G_\gamma\}\) with \(G_j\subseteq T\), and an integer \(g\ge 1\), compute an optimal rational vector \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in G_j}x_t\ge \mu(G_j)/g\) for each \(j\), where \(\mu(G_j)=\sum_{t\in G_j}\mu_t\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a finite list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The remaining input is an explicitly listed family \(\mathcal G=\{G_1,\ldots,G_\gamma\}\), with \(G_j\subseteq T\), and a binary encoded integer \(g\ge1\). Groups may overlap or be empty; no rankings or alternatives are required.
Write \(\mu(G)=\sum_{t\in G}\mu_t\). A selection assigns rational mass \(x_t\) to each type, subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in G_j}x_t\ge\mu(G_j)/g\) for every \(j\). Its cost is \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\); the problem is to compute an exact minimum-cost selection.
Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including masses, group lists, and \(g\). State running times in \(\tau,\gamma,L\), and any dependence on \(g\); there is no alternative-count parameter \(m\).

Is this exact continuous optimization problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For a finite set \(N\) with explicitly listed groups, minimizing \(|K|\) over \(K\subseteq N\) subject to \(|K\cap G_j|\ge\lfloor |G_j|/g\rfloor\) is NP-hard. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09813v2#ThmtheoremB.3)

Conclusion

The exact continuous problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau,\gamma,L\). The dependence on the binary encoded parameter \(g\) is through its bit length, already included in \(L\).

This follows from an explicit linear programming formulation with polynomial encoding length. The argument below also verifies that an exact rational optimum has polynomial output length.

1. An equivalent linear program

Index the types by \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\). Write each input mass as \(\mu_t=p_t/q_t\), where \(p_t,q_t\) are positive integers. Define

\(Q=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t,\qquad r_t=p_t(Q/q_t),\qquad D=gQ.\)

These are integers, and the normalization of the masses gives

\(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}r_t=Q.\)

For each explicitly listed group, compute the integer

\(h_j=\sum_{t\in G_j}r_t.\)

Make the invertible change of variables \(z_t=Dx_t\). The selection constraints become

\(0\le z_t\le gr_t\quad(t\in T),\qquad \sum_{t\in G_j}z_t\ge h_j\quad(1\le j\le\gamma).\)

Indeed, \(D\mu_t=gr_t\) and \(D\mu(G_j)/g=h_j\). Moreover,

\(\sum_{t\in T}x_t=D^{-1}\sum_{t\in T}z_t.\)

Since \(D>0\), minimizing \(\sum_tz_t\) under these constraints is equivalent to the original optimization problem. The variables \(z_t\) remain continuous; no integrality requirement is imposed.

This linear program has \(\tau\) variables and \(\gamma+2\tau\) inequalities. Empty groups contribute the valid inequality \(0\ge0\); overlaps simply produce repeated occurrences of variables in different inequalities.

The vector \(z_t=r_t\), corresponding to \(x_t=\mu_t/g\), is feasible: its group sums equal \(h_j\), and \(r_t\le gr_t\) because \(g\ge1\). The feasible region is closed and bounded by the coordinate constraints. Consequently, a minimum exists.

2. Polynomial encoding length and running time

All logarithms in the encoding bounds are base two. Because the denominators and \(g\) are part of the input,

\(\log D=\log g+\sum_t\log q_t=O(L).\)

Also, positivity and \(\sum_tr_t=Q\) imply

\(0\le h_j\le Q\le D,\qquad 0<gr_t\le D.\)

Thus every right hand side has \(O(L)\) bits. After expressing all inequalities in one direction, every constraint coefficient belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and every objective coefficient equals one.

A dense encoding of the constructed linear program therefore has length

\(S=O\!\left(\tau(\gamma+2\tau)+(\gamma+2\tau)L+\tau\right) =O\!\left((\tau+\gamma)(\tau+L)\right).\)

Constructing it takes \(\operatorname{poly}(\tau,\gamma,L)\) bit operations: products, exact divisions, and group sums involve integers with \(O(L)\) bits, and there are at most \(\tau\gamma\) group memberships.

We use the standard exact rational linear programming theorem: a linear program with binary encoded rational data can be solved in time polynomial in its encoding length, returning an exact rational optimizer whenever a finite attained optimum exists. Exact polynomial algorithms based on the ellipsoid method are discussed explicitly in [Gleixner and Steffy, Section 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/1912.12820#S1.SS3).

Its hypotheses hold here: all data are integers, the feasible region is nonempty and bounded, and the objective is linear. Applying that theorem and returning \(x_t=z_t/D\) gives the running time

\(\operatorname{poly}(S)=\operatorname{poly}(\tau,\gamma,L).\)

Under the explicitly listed input representation, both \(\tau\) and \(\gamma\) are bounded by the input length, so this is also polynomial in \(L\). There is no dependence polynomial in the numerical value of \(g\); its contribution is through \(\log(g+1)\).

3. Why exact output has polynomial length

The fundamental theorem of linear programming states that a linear objective on a nonempty bounded polytope has an optimal vertex. At any vertex \(z^*\), the active constraint normals span the whole variable space: otherwise a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions, contradicting the definition of a vertex.

Choose \(\tau\) linearly independent active constraints and write their equations as

\(Bz^*=b.\)

Here \(B\) is a nonsingular integer matrix with entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and \(|b_i|\le D\). By Cramer’s rule,

\(z_t^*=\frac{\det B_t}{\det B},\)

where \(B_t\) replaces column \(t\) of \(B\) by \(b\).

Hadamard’s inequality, applied to columns, gives

\(1\le|\det B|\le\tau^{\tau/2},\qquad |\det B_t|\le D\tau^{\tau/2}.\)

The second bound uses column norms at most \(\sqrt{\tau}\), except for the replacement column, whose norm is at most \(D\sqrt{\tau}\). Therefore

\(x_t^*=\frac{\det B_t}{D\det B}\)

has numerator and denominator of bit length

\(O\!\left(L+\tau\log(\tau+1)\right).\)

The entire optimum selection, and its cost obtained by summing these fractions over their common denominator, have polynomial encoding length. This confirms that exactness creates no output-size obstruction.

4. Why the discrete hardness result does not transfer

The cited [Theorem B.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09813v2#ThmtheoremB.3) concerns selecting whole elements. The continuous formulation permits splitting a type’s mass, and this can strictly improve the optimum even when the discrete floor thresholds introduce no discrepancy.

For example, take four types \(a,b,c,d\), each of mass \(1/4\), let \(g=2\), and list the groups \(\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{c,a\}\). Each group requires selected mass \(1/4\).

Adding the three inequalities yields

\(2(x_a+x_b+x_c)\ge3/4.\)

Since \(x_d\ge0\), every selection costs at least \(3/8\). Equality is attained by

\(x_a=x_b=x_c=1/8,\qquad x_d=0.\)

If types must instead be selected whole, at least two of \(a,b,c\) are necessary, and any two suffice, giving cost \(1/2\). Thus the discrete restriction changes the optimum, and its hardness does not settle the continuous problem.
