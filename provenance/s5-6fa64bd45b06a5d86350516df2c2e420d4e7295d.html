The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an explicitly listed, non-degenerate domain \(X\subseteq D^m\), decide whether there are Borel maps \(g_j:\Delta(D_j)\to D_j\), where \(D_j=\operatorname{pr}_jX\), such that for every population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(X)\), the evaluation \(A(\mu)_j=g_j(\mu_j)\) belongs to \(X\), with \(\mu_j(d)=\sum_{x\in X:x_j=d}\mu_x\), \(g_j(\delta_d)=d\), and \(g_j(\nu)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Input: a finite alphabet \(D\), a positive integer \(m\), and an explicitly listed nonempty domain \(X\subseteq D^m\), with \(D_j=\operatorname{pr}_jX\) and \(|D_j|\geq2\) for every \(j\). Write \(N=|X|\) and \(d=|D|\).
For a finite set \(S\), \(\Delta(S)\) is its probability simplex, including distributions with zero coordinates and arbitrary real masses. Give it its Euclidean topology and give \(D_j\) the discrete topology.
A represented society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types in \(X\) with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, summing to one. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Size parameters are \(m,N,d,\tau\) and total binary encoding length \(L\); the decision input is only the domain, so it has no supplied society.
Call \(X\) admissible if there exist Borel maps \(g_j:\Delta(D_j)\to D_j\) such that \(g_j(\delta_a)=a\) for every \(a\in D_j\), and \(g_j(\nu)\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\) for every \(\nu\in\Delta(D_j)\).
These same maps must satisfy \(A(\mu)=(g_1(\mu_1),\ldots,g_m(\mu_m))\in X\) for every \(\mu\in\Delta(X)\), where \(\mu_j(a)=\sum_{x\in X:x_j=a}\mu_x\).

Is deciding whether \(X\) is admissible solvable in polynomial time in its binary input length, or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies, existence of a supportive, issue-by-issue aggregator of some arity \(n\geq2\) that is not projection onto one fixed voter is decidable in \(O(\operatorname{poly}(N,m,d)d^{md^3})\) time [Szegedy–Xu](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.01315).
A later result gives a polynomial-time algorithm for that discrete existence problem [Kirousis–Kolaitis–Livieratos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.01574). No result settling the Borel distribution problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the alphabet finite, required a positive number of issues and a nonempty domain, and expanded non-degeneracy as at least two positions on every issue.
  • Interpreted the simplices as containing all real probability distributions, including boundary points; rational support lists specify represented societies without restricting the universal quantifier.
  • Fixed the Borel structures using the Euclidean topology on simplices and the discrete topology on positions.
  • Distinguished the domain-only decision input from a represented society, whose support size and mass encoding are not additional decision inputs.
  • Retained dependence only on population distributions; voter identities and a separate finite-voter non-dictatorship condition are not added.

Admissibility is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard

In fact, deciding admissibility is $\mathsf{PSPACE}$-hard, even when there are exactly six issues. The alphabet size is allowed to vary.

The reduction below proves the hardness alternative in the question. It does not assert membership of the general problem in $\mathsf{PSPACE}$.

1. A known hard problem involving partial bijections

A partial bijection of a finite set $C$ is an injective map whose domain is a subset of $C$. Its inverse is another partial bijection.

Given two explicitly listed partial bijections $p_1,p_2$ of $C$, let
$$ M=\langle \operatorname{id}_C,p_1,p_2,p_1^{-1},p_2^{-1}\rangle $$
be the finite monoid of partial bijections generated under composition. Call $M$ aperiodic if some integer $K\geq 1$ satisfies
$$ q^K=q^{K+1}\qquad\text{for every }q\in M. $$

We use the following known hardness result:

Deciding whether $M$ is aperiodic is $\mathsf{PSPACE}$-hard.

Here is why the precise formulation above follows from the literature. Birget–Margolis prove that aperiodicity of finite inverse automata over a two-letter alphabet is $\mathsf{PSPACE}$-complete. The two letters act by partial bijections, and their formal inverse letters act by the inverse maps. See [Theorem 2.8](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~margolis/papers/Two%20letter%20group%20codes%20that%20preserve%20aperiodicity%20of%20finite%20inverse%20automata.pdf).

To check the passage from language aperiodicity to our transformation formulation, consider such an automaton, with initial state $s$ and its single final state $f$. Since every transition has its inverse, states in the same undirected component are mutually reachable.

If $s$ and $f$ belong to different components, the language is empty and is aperiodic; we can output two identity maps on a one-element set. Otherwise, retain their common component $C$. This preserves the language, and every retained state is both accessible from $s$ and able to reach $f$.

Two words induce the same partial transformation of $C$ exactly when they are equivalent under the syntactic congruence of the accepted language:

  • Equal partial transformations give identical acceptance behavior in every prefix and suffix context.
  • Suppose words $u,v$ induce different partial transformations. There is a state $x$ at which their values differ, with at least one value defined. Interchange $u,v$ if necessary so that $u$ sends $x$ to a state $y$. Choose a prefix sending $s$ to $x$, and a suffix sending $y$ to $f$. The transformation induced by this suffix is injective. Therefore, if $v$ sends $x$ to a different state, that state cannot be sent to $f$ by the same suffix; an undefined value also cannot accept. These contexts distinguish $u$ from $v$.

Thus the syntactic monoid is precisely the transition monoid, with the usual composition convention. Extracting its two generating partial bijections takes polynomial time. This proves the stated hardness result and checks its applicability.

2. Aperiodicity is equivalent to a supportive distribution choice

For a partial bijection $p$ and a distribution $\nu$ supported in $\operatorname{dom}p$, write $p_*\nu$ for its pushforward:
$$ (p_*\nu)(b)=\sum_{\substack{a\in\operatorname{dom}p\\p(a)=b}}\nu(a). $$

A map $h:\Delta(C)\to C$ is supportive if
$$ h(\nu)\in\operatorname{supp}\nu. $$
In particular, supportiveness forces $h(\delta_a)=a$.

Lemma. For the monoid $M$ above, the following are equivalent:

  • $M$ is aperiodic.
  • There exists a supportive map $h:\Delta(C)\to C$ satisfying

$$ h((p_t)_*\nu)=p_t(h(\nu)) $$
for $t\in\{1,2\}$ and every $\nu$ supported in $\operatorname{dom}p_t$.

  • Such a map exists which is Borel and depends only on the support of its argument.

Proof that condition 2 implies condition 1

First, covariance under a partial bijection also gives covariance under its inverse.

Indeed, let $p$ be one of the generators, let $\nu$ be supported in $\operatorname{im}p$, and put
$$ \lambda=(p^{-1})_*\nu. $$
Then $p_*\lambda=\nu$, so the assumed equation gives
$$ h(\nu)=p(h(\lambda)). $$
Supportiveness ensures that $h(\nu)\in\operatorname{im}p$. Applying $p^{-1}$ therefore gives
$$ h((p^{-1})_*\nu)=p^{-1}(h(\nu)). $$

Induction on a word in the generators and their inverses now proves
$$ h(q_*\nu)=q(h(\nu)) $$
for every $q\in M$ and every $\nu$ supported in $\operatorname{dom}q$. At each induction step, the intermediate distribution is supported in the domain of the next map, so the corresponding covariance equation applies.

We next record an elementary fact about partial bijections on a set of size $n=|C|$. If a partial bijection $q$ has no directed cycle of length greater than one, then
$$ q^n=q^{n+1}. $$
To see this, follow the trajectory of any state. Within $n$ steps, either the trajectory becomes undefined or it reaches a repeated state. A repeated state lies on a directed cycle, which by assumption is a fixed point. In either case, the two displayed powers agree.

Consequently, if $M$ is not aperiodic, some $q\in M$ has a cycle
$$ S=\{a_0,\ldots,a_{r-1}\},\qquad r\geq 2, $$
on which $q$ acts cyclically. Let
$$ \nu_S=\frac1r\sum_{a\in S}\delta_a. $$
Then $q_*\nu_S=\nu_S$. Covariance forces
$$ h(\nu_S)=q(h(\nu_S)). $$
But supportiveness puts $h(\nu_S)$ in $S$, and $q$ fixes no element of this cycle. This contradiction proves aperiodicity.

This direction does not use measurability.

Proof that condition 1 implies condition 3

Construct a finite directed graph whose vertices are the nonempty subsets of $C$. For each
$$ r\in\{p_1,p_2,p_1^{-1},p_2^{-1}\} $$
and each nonempty $S\subseteq\operatorname{dom}r$, include an edge
$$ S\longrightarrow r(S) $$
labeled by $r$.

Every edge has its reverse, labeled by the inverse map. In each connected component, choose a representative subset $S_0$ and an element $a_0\in S_0$.

For a vertex $S$ in this component, choose a path from $S_0$ to $S$. Its labels compose to a partial bijection $q\in M$, defined throughout $S_0$, with $q(S_0)=S$. Define
$$ c(S)=q(a_0). $$

We must prove that this definition is independent of the chosen path. Suppose another path induces $r\in M$ with $r(S_0)=S$. The partial bijection
$$ v=r^{-1}\circ q $$
belongs to $M$ and restricts to a permutation of $S_0$. Aperiodicity gives $v^K=v^{K+1}$. Restricting this equality to $S_0$ and cancelling the permutation $v^K|_{S_0}$ shows
$$ v|_{S_0}=\operatorname{id}_{S_0}. $$
Hence $r^{-1}(q(a_0))=a_0$, or equivalently $q(a_0)=r(a_0)$.

Thus $c$ is well-defined and selects an element of every nonempty subset. Appending a generator edge to a path also proves
$$ c(p_t(S))=p_t(c(S)) $$
whenever $S\subseteq\operatorname{dom}p_t$.

Define
$$ h(\nu)=c(\operatorname{supp}\nu). $$
This is supportive. Since pushforward by an injective map transports the support exactly, it satisfies the required covariance equations.

Finally, for each nonempty $S\subseteq C$, the set of distributions having support exactly $S$ is
$$ E_S= \{\nu:\nu(a)>0\text{ for }a\in S,\ \nu(a)=0\text{ for }a\notin S\}. $$
This is Borel in $\Delta(C)$. Each fiber of $h$ is a finite union of such sets, so $h$ is Borel.

Condition 3 plainly implies condition 2, completing the proof. $\square$

3. Encoding the partial bijections as a single domain

We now construct a domain $X$ from $C,p_1,p_2$. Assume $C$ is nonempty, as in the hard source problem.

Introduce a blank symbol $\bot$ and six block names, all distinct from the elements of $C$:
$$ Q=\{r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4,t_1,t_2\}. $$
Set
$$ D=C\sqcup\{\bot\}\sqcup Q. $$

There are five value issues, indexed by $0,1,2,3,4$, and one selector issue. Thus
$$ m=6. $$

Each block has a two-coordinate scope and a relation of permitted full pairs:

| Block | Scope | Full-pair relation |
|---|---|---|
| $r_i$, for $1\leq i\leq4$ | $(0,i)$ | $\{(a,a):a\in C\}$ |
| $t_1$ | $(1,2)$ | $\{(a,p_1(a)):a\in\operatorname{dom}p_1\}$ |
| $t_2$ | $(3,4)$ | $\{(a,p_2(a)):a\in\operatorname{dom}p_2\}$ |

For each block $q$, include the following tuples in $X$, all with selector value $q$:

  • The tuple blank at all five value issues.
  • For each coordinate in its scope and each $a\in C$, the tuple displaying $a$ at that coordinate and blank elsewhere.
  • For every permitted full pair, the tuple displaying that pair on the scope and blank elsewhere.

A transition block with empty full-pair relation still contains its blank and singleton tuples.

Every value-issue projection is $C\cup\{\bot\}$, and the selector projection is $Q$. Therefore every issue has at least two possible values, as required.

Distinct block scopes intersect in at most one coordinate. Also,
$$ |X| = 4(1+3|C|) +2(1+2|C|) +|\operatorname{dom}p_1| +|\operatorname{dom}p_2| \leq 6+18|C|. $$

4. Admissibility of the constructed domain forces aperiodicity

Suppose $X$ is admissible. Denote its value-issue maps by $g_0,\ldots,g_4$ and its selector map by $g_{\mathrm{sel}}$.

View $\Delta(C)$ as the face of $\Delta(C\cup\{\bot\})$ on which the blank has zero mass.

Fix $i\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ and $\nu\in\Delta(C)$. Form a society supported on the full equality tuples of block $r_i$, placing mass $\nu(a)$ on the tuple displaying $(a,a)$.

Its marginals at issues $0$ and $i$ are both $\nu$. Its selector marginal is $\delta_{r_i}$, and all other value marginals are $\delta_\bot$.

Supportiveness forces both aggregate scope values to lie in $C$. Since the aggregate must belong to $X$ and its selector is $r_i$, it must be a full equality tuple. Consequently,
$$ g_0(\nu)=g_i(\nu). $$
This holds for every $\nu$, so all five restrictions to $\Delta(C)$ coincide. Write their common restriction as
$$ h:\Delta(C)\to C. $$
It is supportive and Borel.

Now fix $t\in\{1,2\}$ and a distribution $\nu$ supported in $\operatorname{dom}p_t$. Put mass $\nu(a)$ on the full tuple of block $t_t$ displaying $(a,p_t(a))$.

The two scope marginals are $\nu$ and $(p_t)_*\nu$. Both aggregate values lie in $C$, so the aggregate must be a full graph tuple of this block. Therefore,
$$ h((p_t)_*\nu)=p_t(h(\nu)). $$

The lemma now implies that $M$ is aperiodic.

5. Aperiodicity supplies Borel admissibility maps

Conversely, suppose $M$ is aperiodic. By the lemma, choose a supportive Borel map $h:\Delta(C)\to C$ satisfying the two covariance equations and depending only on supports.

At every value issue, define
$$ g_i(\nu)= \begin{cases} \bot,&\nu(\bot)>0,\\ h(\nu|_C),&\nu(\bot)=0. \end{cases} $$
In the second case, $\nu|_C$ is a probability distribution on $C$.

At the selector issue, fix any total order on $Q$ and choose its first supported block:
$$ g_{\mathrm{sel}}(\eta)=\min\operatorname{supp}\eta. $$

These maps are supportive and fix point masses. They depend only on supports, so the support-stratum argument from the lemma proves that they are Borel.

We verify feasibility for an arbitrary $\mu\in\Delta(X)$, allowing arbitrary real masses. Let
$$ Y=\operatorname{supp}\mu, $$
and let $B\subseteq Q$ be the set of blocks represented by tuples in $Y$.

Case 1: At least two blocks are represented

If the aggregate at a value issue is nonblank, that issue's marginal assigns zero mass to $\bot$. Since every tuple in $Y$ has positive mass, every tuple in $Y$ must therefore be nonblank at that issue.

Hence every nonblank aggregate coordinate belongs to the scope of every block in $B$. Two distinct scopes intersect in at most one coordinate, so the aggregate has at most one nonblank value coordinate.

The selector chooses a block belonging to $B$. Any nonblank aggregate coordinate belongs to that selected block's scope. The corresponding blank or singleton tuple is explicitly included in that block. Thus the aggregate belongs to $X$.

Case 2: Exactly one block is represented

Let its name be $q$. The selector returns $q$, and every coordinate outside its scope returns $\bot$.

If at most one scope coordinate aggregates to a nonblank value, the result is an included blank or singleton tuple.

Suppose both scope outputs are nonblank. Both scope marginals then assign zero mass to $\bot$. Thus every tuple in $Y$ is nonblank at both scope coordinates and is necessarily a full-pair tuple.

If $q=r_i$, the two scope marginals coincide. Applying the common map $h$ produces an equality pair, which is included.

If $q=t_t$, the full-pair society has scope marginals $\nu$ and $(p_t)_*\nu$ for some distribution $\nu$ supported in $\operatorname{dom}p_t$. Its scope output is
$$ \bigl(h(\nu),h((p_t)_*\nu)\bigr) = \bigl(h(\nu),p_t(h(\nu))\bigr). $$
This is an included graph pair.

All cases give an aggregate in $X$. Therefore $X$ is admissible.

Combining the two directions proves
$$ X\text{ is admissible} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M\text{ is aperiodic}. $$

6. Complexity conclusion

The reduction explicitly lists every tuple of $X$. It uses six issues,
$$ d=|C|+7, \qquad N\leq 6+18|C|, $$
and can be performed in polynomial time in the source input's binary length. With states indexed by integers, the output encoding has length $O(|C|\log(|C|+2))$.

The source problem is $\mathsf{PSPACE}$-hard, and the equivalence above is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. Thus admissibility is $\mathsf{PSPACE}$-hard, already for six issues, and therefore is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard.

No represented society is part of this reduction's decision input. The positive construction handles every real society, while the negative implication applies even without the Borel requirement.

The passage to the partial-transformation formulation is valid:

  • Otherwise, retaining their common component preserves the language. Inverse transitions ensure every retained state is accessible and can reach the accepting state.
  • Equal partial transformations have identical acceptance behavior in all contexts.
  • For unequal transformations, the proposed prefix reaches a state where they differ. The proposed suffix accepts from one image. Its injectivity prevents acceptance from the other image, and an undefined image cannot accept.

Thus the contextual equivalence classes of words coincide with their induced partial transformations. The resulting monoid is isomorphic to the syntactic monoid, so aperiodicity is preserved. Component restriction and extraction of the two maps are polynomial-time operations.
