Theorem 3.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite proposer and arm type sets with rational masses, strict type-level preferences, reward means, and the APKP information pattern, run OCA-UCB for a nonatomic population, tracking the distribution of individual learner states and the induced matching flow \(x(t)\). Does there exist a bound \(T(\varepsilon,\eta,r,s,L,\Delta,\lambda)\) such that, for every \(t \geq T\), the probability that \(x(t)\) is more than \(\varepsilon\) in \(L_1\) distance from the set of stable mass matchings is at most \(\eta\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(r\geq1\) proposer types and \(s\geq1\) arm types, with masses \(a_i,b_k>0\), \(\sum_i a_i\leq\sum_k b_k=1\); set \(m=\max\{r,s\}\), \(\tau=r+s\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Any running time is measured in \(m,\tau,L\) and the other displayed parameters.
Each proposer type has rational reward means \(\mu_i^k\in\mathbb{Q}\), distinct across arm types, inducing its strict preferences; each arm type has a strict ranking of proposer types. Individuals of the same type are indifferent to one another, and every partner is preferred to being unmatched. Set \(\Delta=\min_{i,k\neq\ell}|\mu_i^k-\mu_i^\ell|>0\), with \(\Delta=1\) when \(s=1\). Arms know their rankings; proposers initially know neither their means nor arm rankings, receive private feedback, and observe the public matching flow after each round; there is no proposer communication.
A feasible mass matching \(x\geq0\) has row sums at most \(a_i\) and column sums at most \(b_k\). It is stable if no pair \((i,k)\) has both positive proposer mass unmatched or assigned to types worse than \(k\), and positive arm mass unmatched or assigned to types worse than \(i\). Let \(\mathcal S\) be this set and \(d_1(x,\mathcal S)=\inf_{y\in\mathcal S}\sum_{i,k}|x_{ik}-y_{ik}|\).
Define nonatomic OCA-UCB by an exact distribution of individual proposer states, with no pooling of samples. Each state stores its previous attempted arm type, successful-sample counts \(n_k\), sample means \(\widehat\mu_k\), and sets \(H_k\) of proposer types learned to outrank its own type at arm type \(k\); initially all counts are zero and all \(H_k\) are empty.
At round \(1\), each learner chooses an arm type uniformly. At each later integer round \(t\), independently with probability \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) it repeats its previous attempt; otherwise it maximizes \(\widehat\mu_k+\sqrt{3\ln(t)/(2n_k)}\), interpreted as \(+\infty\) when \(n_k=0\), over plausible types, breaking ties by smallest index. Type \(k\) is plausible if its previous column has positive unmatched mass or contains positive mass of a proposer type outside \(H_k\), including the learner's own type.
All proposals to arm type \(k\) form a pool: capacity \(b_k\) is filled in its strict proposer-type order, accepting every proposal if capacity permits. A partially accepted proposer type is thinned uniformly across its learner states. Accepted learners receive fresh independent \(\mathcal N(\mu_i^k,1)\) rewards and update only their own successful-sample statistics; rejected learners receive zero, which is not a reward sample.
Each rejected learner independently samples a winner type from the accepted mass at its attempted arm type, proportionally to that mass, and adds it to \(H_k\) if it differs from its own type; rejection within the same type creates no ranking comparison. Private random choices, acceptance thinning, feedback, and rewards act through their probability kernels on the state distributions. Thus \(x(t)\) is the resulting deterministic accepted-mass flow, and probabilities concerning it are interpreted as probabilities of deterministic events.
Does there exist a finite bound \(T(\varepsilon,\eta,r,s,L,\Delta,\lambda)\), uniform over these inputs, such that for every \(\varepsilon,\eta\in(0,1)\) and every integer \(t\geq T\), \(\Pr\{d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)>\varepsilon\}\leq\eta\), or can such a bound fail?
For finite markets, OCA-UCB is stated to inherit CA-UCB's convergence guarantee under private but known arm preferences ([Pokharel–Das](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06176v4)). With publicly known arm preferences, CA-UCB has a cumulative instability-probability bound of \(O((\log H)^2)\) over horizon \(H\), for fixed market parameters and \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) ([Liu et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume22/20-1429/20-1429.pdf)).

Conclusion

Such a bound can fail. There is a fixed instance with \(r=s=2\), \(\tau=4\), and \(\Delta=1\) for which, for every \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)\), the rounds satisfying \(d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)>\varepsilon\) have asymptotic density one.

The proof concerns the exact state-distribution dynamics specified in the question.

1. The counterexample

Take proposer masses and arm capacities

\(a_1=a_2=1/2,\qquad b_1=1/4,\qquad b_2=3/4.\)

Both proposer types have means

\(\mu_i^1=2,\qquad \mu_i^2=1,\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.\)

Thus both prefer arm type \(1\), and \(\Delta=1\). Both arm types rank proposer type \(1\) strictly above proposer type \(2\).

All masses and means are binary rationals, and the instance has fixed finite input length. Fix any \(\lambda\in(0,1)\).

For proposer type \(1\), let \(p_k(t)\) be the fraction of its mass attempting arm type \(k\) at round \(t\). Then

\(p_1(t)+p_2(t)=1.\)

Because type \(1\) has highest priority, its acceptance probabilities are

\(\alpha_1(t)=\min\{1,1/(2p_1(t))\}\ge 1/2,\qquad \alpha_2(t)=1.\)

These formulas apply whenever the corresponding proposal fraction is positive.

Initially \(p_1(1)=p_2(1)=1/2\). The repeat branch gives

\(p_k(t)\ge\lambda p_k(t-1)>0.\)

Consequently, each arm always accepts positive mass of proposer type \(1\). Both arms therefore remain plausible for every type-\(1\) learner, because the plausibility rule explicitly includes the learner’s own type.

Moreover, a rejected type-\(1\) learner can only observe another type-\(1\) winner: rejection is possible only at arm \(1\), when type-\(1\) proposals alone exceed its capacity. Hence its sets \(H_k\) remain empty.

The dynamics are also well-defined for type \(2\): arm \(2\) is always plausible. If that arm has unmatched capacity, this follows directly. If it is full, it must contain positive type-\(2\) mass because \(b_2=3/4>a_1\).

Finally, all type-\(1\) proposals to arm \(2\) are accepted, so

\(x_{12}(t)=\tfrac12 p_2(t).\)

2. The stable matching is unique

Let \(y\) be stable. If its total matched mass were less than \(1\), there would be both positive unmatched proposer mass and positive unmatched arm mass. Any pair of types having these two properties would block. Thus its total matched mass equals \(1\).

Since total proposer mass and total arm capacity both equal \(1\), every row and column must be full.

In particular,

\(y_{12}=1/2-y_{11}\ge 1/4,\)

because arm \(1\) has capacity \(1/4\). If \(y_{21}>0\), the pair \((1,1)\) would block: positive type-\(1\) mass is assigned to its worse arm, while arm \(1\) contains positive mass of its worse proposer type. Therefore \(y_{21}=0\).

Full rows and columns now force

\(y_{11}=1/4,\qquad y_{12}=1/4,\qquad y_{21}=0,\qquad y_{22}=1/2.\)

Denote this matching by \(y^\star\). It is stable: neither proposer type is unmatched or assigned below arm \(2\); at arm \(1\), type \(1\) encounters only its own type, and type \(2\) encounters the strictly higher-ranked type \(1\). Thus no pair blocks.

Consequently,

\(\mathcal S=\{y^\star\},\qquad d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)\ge |x_{12}(t)-1/4|.\)

We will prove that the time average of \(x_{12}(t)\) tends to zero.

3. Representing one learner’s private history

Sample a representative learner from proposer type \(1\), normalized to a probability distribution. Write \(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}\) for probabilities concerning this auxiliary learner.

Its history has exactly the law generated by the prescribed probability kernels. In particular,

\(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}\{A_t=k\}=p_k(t),\)

where \(A_t\) is its attempted arm at round \(t\).

Let \(J_k(t)\) be its number of attempts to arm \(k\) through round \(t\), and let \(n_k(t)\) be its number of successful samples from that arm. Thus

\(J_1(t)+J_2(t)=t.\)

We can generate its history using independent arrays:

  • uniforms \(U_{k,j}\), used on the \(j\)-th attempt to arm \(k\);
  • rewards \(Z_{k,n}\sim\mathcal N(\mu_1^k,1)\), revealed on the \(n\)-th successful sample from arm \(k\);
  • independent delay indicators \(D_t\), with \(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}(D_t=1)=\lambda\), where \(D_t=1\) means repeat.

Acceptance on an attempt to arm \(k\) is implemented by comparing its fresh uniform with \(\alpha_k(t)\). This construction reproduces uniform thinning and fresh independent rewards.

Define

\(\overline Z_k(n)=n^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^n Z_{k,\ell}.\)

By the strong law of large numbers,

\(\overline Z_1(n)\longrightarrow 2,\qquad \overline Z_2(n)\longrightarrow 1\)

almost surely under \(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}\).

Because \(\alpha_k(t)\ge1/2\), every attempt whose uniform satisfies \(U_{k,j}\le1/2\) succeeds. Therefore

\(n_k(t)\ge\sum_{j=1}^{J_k(t)}\mathbf 1\{U_{k,j}\le1/2\}.\)

The indicators on the right are independent Bernoulli variables with mean \(1/2\). Their strong law implies that, almost surely, finite random constants \(C_1,C_2\) exist such that, simultaneously for every \(t\),

\(J_k(t)\le4n_k(t)+C_k,\qquad k\in\{1,2\}.\)

Indeed, their partial sums eventually exceed one quarter of their length; a finite constant covers the remaining lengths. It follows that

\(n_1(t)+n_2(t)\ge\tfrac14(t-C_1-C_2).\)

4. The preferred arm is sampled infinitely often

We claim that \(n_1(t)\longrightarrow\infty\) almost surely.

Work on an outcome where the preceding strong laws hold and nonrepeat rounds occur infinitely often. The latter property holds almost surely because the delay indicators are independent and \(1-\lambda>0\).

Suppose instead that \(n_1(t)\) is bounded. Being integer-valued and nondecreasing, it is eventually constant. The preceding lower bound then implies that \(n_2(t)\) grows at least linearly. Thus arm \(2\)’s UCB index tends to \(1\): its empirical mean tends to \(1\), and its exploration bonus tends to zero.

Arm \(1\)’s index tends to positive infinity. If its eventual count is zero, its index is always positive infinity. Otherwise its empirical mean is fixed and its bonus grows as a positive constant times \(\sqrt{\ln t}\).

Hence every sufficiently late nonrepeat round chooses arm \(1\). There are infinitely many such rounds, so \(J_1(t)\) is unbounded. This contradicts

\(J_1(t)\le4n_1(t)+C_1.\)

The claim follows.

5. Attempts to the worse arm are sublinear

We prove the pathwise bound

\(J_2(H)=O((\ln H)^2)\)

almost surely; its implicit constant may depend on the learner’s history.

Nonrepeat choices

Because \(n_1(t)\longrightarrow\infty\), there is almost surely a finite time \(t_0\) after which arm \(1\)’s empirical mean is at least \(7/4\). Its UCB index is therefore at least \(7/4\).

Also, there is almost surely a finite integer \(N\) such that

\(\overline Z_2(n)\le5/4\quad\text{for every }n\ge N.\)

Suppose that at a nonrepeat round \(t\ge t_0\), the learner chooses arm \(2\), and that \(n_2(t-1)\ge N\). Maximizing UCB requires

\(\tfrac54+\sqrt{\frac{3\ln t}{2n_2(t-1)}}\ge\tfrac74.\)

Consequently,

\(n_2(t-1)\le6\ln t.\)

For a horizon \(H\), put

\(K_H=\lceil\max\{N,6\ln H\}\rceil.\)

Every nonrepeat choice of arm \(2\) between \(t_0\) and \(H\) therefore occurs when \(n_2(t-1)\le K_H\). At such a round,

\(J_2(t-1)\le4K_H+C_2.\)

The values of \(J_2(t-1)\) at distinct arm-\(2\) attempts are distinct nonnegative integers. Thus there are at most \(4K_H+C_2+1\) such nonrepeat choices. Adding the finitely many earlier rounds, their total number \(R_2(H)\) satisfies

\(R_2(H)=O(\ln H)\)

almost surely.

Repeat blocks

Let \(M_H\) be the longest consecutive run of repeat indicators among rounds \(2,\ldots,H\). For any positive integer \(h\), a union bound gives

\(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}\{M_H\ge h\}\le H\lambda^h.\)

Choose

\(h_H=\left\lceil\frac{3\ln H}{-\ln\lambda}\right\rceil.\)

Then

\(\mathbb P_{\mathrm{tag}}\{M_H\ge h_H\}\le H^{-2}.\)

These probabilities are summable over integer \(H\ge2\). The first Borel–Cantelli lemma therefore gives

\(M_H=O(\ln H)\)

almost surely.

Every arm-\(2\) attempt belongs to a block beginning either at round \(1\) or at a nonrepeat choice of arm \(2\), followed by consecutive repeats. Each block has length at most \(M_H+1\). Hence

\(J_2(H)\le(R_2(H)+1)(M_H+1)=O((\ln H)^2)\)

almost surely. In particular,

\(J_2(H)/H\longrightarrow0\)

almost surely.

6. Translating private histories into deterministic mass flows

Since \(0\le J_2(H)/H\le1\), dominated convergence gives

\(\frac1H\sum_{t=1}^H p_2(t)=\mathbb E_{\mathrm{tag}}\!\left[J_2(H)/H\right]\longrightarrow0.\)

Using \(x_{12}(t)=p_2(t)/2\), we obtain the deterministic statement

\(\frac1H\sum_{t=1}^H x_{12}(t)\longrightarrow0.\)

Fix \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)\). If \(d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)\le\varepsilon\), then the coordinate bound from Section 2 requires

\(x_{12}(t)\ge1/4-\varepsilon>0.\)

Therefore

\(\frac{\#\{1\le t\le H:d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)\le\varepsilon\}}{H} \le\frac{\sum_{t=1}^H x_{12}(t)}{H(1/4-\varepsilon)} \longrightarrow0.\)

Thus the unstable rounds have asymptotic density one. In particular, they occur arbitrarily late.

Take \(\varepsilon=1/8\) and \(\eta=1/2\). At each such unstable round, the matching flow is deterministic, so

\(\Pr\{d_1(x(t),\mathcal S)>1/8\}=1>\eta.\)

No finite eventual bound exists even for this single fixed input. A uniform bound of the requested form therefore cannot exist.

7. Relation to the cited finite-market results

The finite CA-UCB model gives arms strict preferences over individual players and awards a conflicting arm to its most preferred player; finite OCA-UCB learns comparisons between individual players. Those assumptions differ from the stipulated indifference within a type and the absence of comparisons from rejection within that type. See [Liu et al., model definition](https://jmlr.org/papers/volume22/20-1429/20-1429.pdf) and [Pokharel–Das, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06176v4).

Here, persistent own-type mass keeps both arms plausible. Learners consequently favor the higher-mean arm despite its insufficient capacity, whereas stability requires positive type-\(1\) mass at the worse arm. The proof above establishes this failure directly, without applying either finite-market theorem.
