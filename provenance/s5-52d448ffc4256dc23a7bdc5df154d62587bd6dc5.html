Theorem 3.1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite student types \(S\) with rational masses \(\mu_s\) summing to \(1\), and finite school types \(H\) with rational masses \(\nu_h\) and per-school capacities \(\kappa_h\), compute the student-optimal stable assignment flow \(x_{sh}\). Each complete student type records its public rating, full realized shortlist of school types, post-interview ranking, and utility vector; school types use the common public-score priority ordering from the school-choice setting. Require \(0\le x_{sh}\), \(\sum_h x_{sh}\le\mu_s\), \(\sum_s x_{sh}\le\nu_h\kappa_h\), and \(x_{sh}=0\) for schools outside type \(s\)'s shortlist; the flow must have no blocking type pair.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(S\) and \(H\) be nonempty finite sets of student and school types, with \(m=|H|\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|S|+|H|\) types with positive binary-rational masses; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Student masses satisfy \(\sum_s\mu_s=1\). School masses \(\nu_h>0\) are measured in the same population units, with positive integer per-school capacities \(\kappa_h\) and aggregate capacities \(q_h=\nu_h\kappa_h\); total capacity need not equal student mass.
Each student type supplies a rational public rating \(r_s\in[0,1)\), a nonempty realized shortlist \(A_s\subseteq H\), a strict post-interview ranking \(\succ_s\), and rational utilities \(u_{sh}\in[0,3)\) consistent with that ranking. Every listed school is preferred to being unmatched. Schools accept every student and share the priority order \(\succ_H\), given by decreasing public rating with an input common tie-breaking order. Let \(k=\max_s|A_s|\).
A feasible divisible assignment satisfies \(x_{sh}\geq0\), \(\sum_hx_{sh}\leq\mu_s\), \(\sum_sx_{sh}\leq q_h\), and \(x_{sh}=0\) whenever \(h\notin A_s\).
A listed pair \((s,h)\) blocks when both \(\mu_s-\sum_gx_{sg}+\sum_{g:\,h\succ_s g}x_{sg}>0\) and \(q_h-\sum_tx_{th}+\sum_{t:\,s\succ_H t}x_{th}>0\). Stability means having no blocking pair, including pairs already carrying positive flow.
A stable flow is student-optimal if, for every student type and every initial segment of its ranking, its assigned mass in that segment is at least as large as in every other stable flow.

Can the student-optimal stable assignment flow be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\), or is its computation \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite random markets, the specified interview-selection strategy yields an \(\epsilon\)-Nash equilibrium for students above the public-rating threshold, with \(\epsilon=O(\ln k/k)\), regardless of lower-rated students’ strategies; this is not a computational hardness result. [Cole–Jangir](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.19345v1)
Divisible stable allocation already has polynomial algorithms, including computation of the proposing side’s optimal allocation. The formulation above is a special case, so its complexity target is already settled on the polynomial side. [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • School masses use the student-population normalization; they need not sum to one, and aggregate school capacity may differ from total student mass.
  • Per-school capacities are positive integers, preserving their meaning as numbers of places; aggregate capacities are rational.
  • Realized shortlists are nonempty and supplied as input; their maximum length defines k. Interview-selection randomness and market-size-dependent cone parameters are not inputs to this conditional assignment computation.
  • Public ratings and utilities are binary rationals in their original ranges; post-interview rankings are strict, and every shortlisted school is acceptable even when its utility is zero.
  • Equal public ratings between distinct types are resolved by a supplied common tie-breaking order; school priorities remain identical.
  • Blocking permits filling vacancies and replacing worse assignments with positive mass, including increasing flow on an already used pair. The vacancy condition repairs the supplied finite definition’s omission.
  • Student optimality is interpreted through cumulative assigned mass along each type’s ranking.

Result

The student-optimal stable flow is exactly computable in polynomial time. In fact, the common strict school priority order implies that there is exactly one stable flow, obtained by processing student types in priority order and assigning each type greedily down its shortlist.

This agrees with the general polynomial solvability of divisible stable allocation established by [Dean–Munshi](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf). Below is a direct proof for the stated formulation, including its exact bit complexity.

1. Simplifying the blocking conditions

Write \(n=|S|\), and index student types so that \(s_1\succ_H s_2\succ_H\cdots\succ_H s_n\). This order is strict because the input specifies a common tie-breaking order.

For each student \(s_i\), write its listed schools in preference order as \(h_{i1}\succ_{s_i}\cdots\succ_{s_i}h_{i\ell_i}\), where \(\ell_i=|A_{s_i}|\).

For any feasible flow \(x\), define the remaining mass before the \(j\)-th choice and the capacity remaining after higher-priority students by

\(R_{ij}(x)=\mu_{s_i}-\sum_{a<j}x_{s_i h_{ia}}\),

\(B_{ij}(x)=q_{h_{ij}}-\sum_{t<i}x_{s_t h_{ij}}\).

The student-side quantity in the blocking definition equals

\(\mu_{s_i}-\sum_gx_{s_i g}+\sum_{g:\,h_{ij}\succ_{s_i}g}x_{s_i g}=R_{ij}(x)-x_{s_i h_{ij}}\).

Indeed, every positive entry in this student's row belongs to its shortlist, and the contributions from worse schools cancel.

Likewise, the school-side quantity equals

\(q_{h_{ij}}-\sum_tx_{s_t h_{ij}}+\sum_{t:\,s_i\succ_H s_t}x_{s_t h_{ij}}=B_{ij}(x)-x_{s_i h_{ij}}\),

because contributions from lower-priority students cancel.

Feasibility implies

\(0\leq x_{s_i h_{ij}}\leq\min\{R_{ij}(x),B_{ij}(x)\}\).

Consequently, the listed pair does not block precisely when at least one of the two differences above is zero. Thus a feasible flow is stable if and only if, for every listed pair,

\(x_{s_i h_{ij}}=\min\{R_{ij}(x),B_{ij}(x)\}.\) \((*)\)

This equivalence applies equally to pairs already carrying positive flow.

2. Algorithm and existence

Initialize every school residual capacity to \(C_h=q_h\), and initialize all flow entries to zero.

Process students \(s_1,\ldots,s_n\) in that order. For student \(s_i\), initialize \(R=\mu_{s_i}\). Then, for \(j=1,\ldots,\ell_i\), perform:

  • Set \(\delta=\min\{R,C_{h_{ij}}\}\).
  • Set \(x_{s_i h_{ij}}=\delta\).
  • Replace \(R\) by \(R-\delta\), and \(C_{h_{ij}}\) by \(C_{h_{ij}}-\delta\).

Leave any remaining student mass unmatched.

All residuals stay nonnegative. The algorithm assigns at most \(\mu_{s_i}\) to each student and at most \(q_h\) to each school, using only listed pairs. Therefore its output is feasible.

Immediately before processing \((s_i,h_{ij})\), the student residual is exactly \(R_{ij}(x)\). The school residual is exactly \(B_{ij}(x)\): only higher-priority students have previously received mass there, since \(s_i\) has not yet processed this school. Later steps change neither quantity.

Hence the output satisfies \((*)\) at every listed pair and is stable. This proves existence without requiring total capacity to equal total student mass.

3. Uniqueness and student optimality

Let \(y\) be any stable flow. We prove \(y=x\) by induction, processing students in priority order and schools within each student's preference order.

Suppose all rows preceding \(s_i\) agree, and the entries preceding \(h_{ij}\) in the current row agree. Then the defining sums give

\(R_{ij}(y)=R_{ij}(x)\) and \(B_{ij}(y)=B_{ij}(x)\).

Both stable flows satisfy \((*)\), so

\(y_{s_i h_{ij}}=\min\{R_{ij}(y),B_{ij}(y)\}=\min\{R_{ij}(x),B_{ij}(x)\}=x_{s_i h_{ij}}\).

This establishes the induction step. The initial step has empty defining sums, so the induction proves equality on every listed pair. Both flows are zero on unlisted pairs; therefore \(y=x\).

Thus the stable flow is unique. In particular, every student's assigned mass in every initial preference segment equals that in every other stable flow. The unique flow is therefore student-optimal under the definition in the question.

4. Exact polynomial bit complexity

Let \(E=\sum_s|A_s|\), so \(E\leq nk\).

After sorting students by public rating and the specified tie-breaking order, the algorithm uses \(O(E+m+n)\) arithmetic operations. Sorting uses \(O(n\log n)\) comparisons, each computable exactly from the input rational ratings.

To bound arithmetic sizes, write each student mass and school mass as an integer numerator divided by a positive integer denominator. Let \(D\) be the product of these denominators. Because their total encoding length is bounded by \(L\), the bit length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\).

Scale all masses and capacities by \(D\). Then \(D\mu_s\) and \(Dq_h=D\nu_h\kappa_h\) are integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Every algorithmic update is a minimum or subtraction of nonnegative integers. Student residuals never exceed their initial scaled masses, and school residuals never exceed their initial scaled capacities. Thus all intermediate quantities have polynomially bounded bit length.

Computing \(D\), forming the scaled inputs, sorting, and performing the allocation therefore take polynomial bit time. Dividing the resulting integer entries by \(D\) gives an exact rational flow of polynomial encoding length. Unlisted zero entries may be represented implicitly; explicitly outputting them also costs only polynomial time.

Hence the requested computation is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
