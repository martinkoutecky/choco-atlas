The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), entitlement densities \(\beta_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t=1\), and value-query access to monotone submodular valuations \(v_t:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\), where copies of each item kind \(j\) are perfect substitutes, find masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow v_t(S)\ge \frac13 A_t^\infty\), where \(A_t^\infty=\min_{\pi\ge0:\sum_jq_j\pi_j=1}\max_{S:\sum_{j\in S}\pi_j\le\beta_t}v_t(S)\). Determine the complexity of finding such an allocation or maximizing the guaranteed fraction.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) item kinds with positive binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and positive binary-rational entitlement densities \(\beta_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t=1\). Copies of a kind are perfect substitutes, so individual bundles are subsets \(S\subseteq[m]\).
Each type has a normalized, monotone, nonnegative submodular valuation \(v_t\), accessible through value queries returning binary rationals. Let \(L\) bound the total numerical input length and the bit length of each oracle answer.
Define \(A_t^\infty=\min_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m:\,\sum_jq_j\pi_j=1}\max_{S\subseteq[m]:\,\sum_{j\in S}\pi_j\le\beta_t}v_t(S)\).
An allocation consists of nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,S}\), represented by their nonzero entries, satisfying \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\). It guarantees fraction \(\alpha\) if \(x_{t,S}>0\Rightarrow v_t(S)\ge\alpha A_t^\infty\); this requirement applies to every assigned bundle, rather than expected value.

Can a \(\frac13\)-guaranteeing allocation always be found with running time and value-query count polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is attaining this guarantee NP-hard or subject to a superpolynomial value-query lower bound?

For finite agents with arbitrary positive entitlements, a polynomial-time value-query algorithm guarantees each agent \(\frac13\) of her APS; its numerical dependence is polynomial in \(\log K\), where \(K\) bounds the ratio of largest to smallest positive bundle value ([Ben Uziahu–Feige, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12444)).
No result settling this precise mass-allocation model was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the society to total mass one, consistent with a distribution over types and per-capita supplies.
  • Required positive entitlement densities, following the discrete requirement of positive entitlements; densities have no individual upper bound because they are not individual entitlement shares.
  • Required at least one item kind and positive supplies, removing absent kinds so the price normalization is well-defined.
  • Required normalized valuations with value zero on the empty set, as assumed throughout the discrete setting.
  • Replaced unrestricted real oracle answers by binary rationals with bounded answer length to make bit complexity meaningful.
  • Specified rational allocation masses represented by nonzero entries to avoid requiring an exponentially long table.
  • Selected the fixed one-third APS allocation guarantee from the anchor, rather than exact maximization of the guaranteed fraction.

Result

Yes. A deterministic algorithm can find a \(\frac13\)-guaranteeing allocation with running time and value-query count polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\).

In fact, the argument below gives a \(\frac25\) guarantee. It computes distributions over bundles directly, without expanding the society into finite agents.

1. A distribution characterization of the benchmark

Fix a type, and abbreviate its valuation, entitlement density, and benchmark by \(v,\beta,A\). Put \(b_j=\beta q_j\). For a bundle \(S\), let \(a_S\in\{0,1\}^m\) be its incidence vector.

For a finite family of bundles \(\mathcal F\), consider the feasibility system

\[\displaystyle \lambda_S\ge0,\qquad \sum_{S\in\mathcal F}\lambda_S=1,\qquad \sum_{S\in\mathcal F}\lambda_S a_S\le b.\tag{1}\]

All vector inequalities are coordinatewise.

Lemma 1. System (1) is feasible if and only if, for every \(p\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m\), there is an \(S\in\mathcal F\) satisfying \(p\cdot a_S\le p\cdot b\).

Proof. If (1) is feasible, the average bundle cost under its distribution is at most \(p\cdot b\); at least one supported bundle therefore has cost at most that amount.

Conversely, suppose (1) is infeasible. If \(\mathcal F\) is empty, the stated condition already fails. Otherwise, define the closed convex set

\(\displaystyle C=\operatorname{conv}\{a_S:S\in\mathcal F\}+\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m.\)

Its closedness follows because the convex hull is compact. Infeasibility means \(b\notin C\). Strict separation of a point from a closed convex set gives a nonzero vector \(p\) with

\(\displaystyle p\cdot b<\inf_{y\in C}p\cdot y.\)

Necessarily \(p\ge0\): a negative coordinate would make the infimum unbounded below along the corresponding nonnegative ray of \(C\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle p\cdot b<\min_{S\in\mathcal F}p\cdot a_S,\)

contradicting the stated condition. \(\square\)

For \(z\ge0\), define \(\mathcal F_z=\{S:v(S)\ge z\}\).

For every nonzero nonnegative \(p\), the vector \(\pi=p/(q\cdot p)\) is an admissible normalized price vector. Moreover,

\[\displaystyle \pi\cdot a_S\le\beta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\cdot a_S\le p\cdot b.\tag{2}\]

It follows that, whenever \(z\le A\), every nonnegative price vector admits a bundle in \(\mathcal F_z\) costing at most \(p\cdot b\). Lemma 1 therefore proves:

Corollary 2. There is a distribution supported entirely on bundles of value at least \(A\), whose item marginals are at most \(\beta q_j\).

Thus a full-benchmark allocation always exists in the mass model. The remaining question is how to compute a sufficiently good distribution using polynomially many queries.

2. The computational ingredients

Submodular knapsack approximation

We use the following known theorem: maximizing a nonnegative monotone submodular function under one knapsack constraint admits a deterministic \((1-1/\mathrm e)\)-approximation using \(O(m^5)\) value queries. This is [Sviridenko’s theorem](https://thibaut.horel.org/submodularity/papers/sviridenko2004.pdf).

Its hypotheses apply here. Rational costs and budgets can be scaled to integers with polynomial encoding length, and the algorithm’s iteration count does not depend on their magnitudes. Zero-cost items can first be included: if their set is \(Z\), apply the theorem to the normalized residual function \(T\mapsto v(Z\cup T)-v(Z)\). Adding back \(v(Z)\) preserves the approximation guarantee. A zero budget is handled directly by taking all zero-cost items.

Because \(1-1/\mathrm e>\frac35\), this gives a polynomial-time routine which, for every rational \(p\ge0\), returns a bundle \(S\) satisfying

\[\displaystyle p\cdot a_S\le p\cdot b,\qquad v(S)\ge\frac35\,\operatorname{OPT}(p),\tag{3}\]

where

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}(p)=\max\{v(T):p\cdot a_T\le p\cdot b\}.\)

By (2), \(\operatorname{OPT}(p)\ge A\) for every nonzero \(p\ge0\). This also holds for \(p=0\), when \(\operatorname{OPT}(0)=v([m])\).

Rational central-cut ellipsoid method

We also use the standard rational ellipsoid cutting-plane theorem. Starting with a rational outer ball of radius \(R\), polynomially many central cuts suffice to reduce its volume below that of a ball of prescribed rational radius \(r>0\). The iteration count and arithmetic complexity are polynomial in the dimension, coefficient encoding lengths, and \(\log(R/r)\). The maintained ellipsoid contains every point in the initial ball satisfying all retained halfspaces. Rational rounding preserves these properties with polynomial encoding lengths. See the ellipsoid analysis in [Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, Section 2](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf). In dimension one, interval halving gives the same statement.

The next lemma provides the radius needed to apply this theorem without relaxing any supply constraint.

3. A quantitative separation lemma

Choose a positive integer \(D\) such that every \(h_j=Db_j\) is an integer. Define

\(\displaystyle B=\max\{1,D,h_1,\ldots,h_m\},\qquad C=(m+1)!\,B^{m+1},\qquad \gamma=C^{-1}.\)

These quantities have polynomial encoding length: a common denominator can be formed by multiplying the denominators of the coordinates of \(b\).

For each bundle, put \(w_S=Da_S-h\). Every coordinate of \(w_S\) has absolute value at most \(B\). Finally, set

\(\displaystyle \delta=\frac{\gamma}{4},\qquad r=\frac{\gamma}{16mB}.\)

Lemma 3. If (1) is infeasible for a finite family \(\mathcal F\), then

\[\displaystyle K_{\mathcal F}=\{p\in[0,1]^m:w_S\cdot p\ge\delta\text{ for every }S\in\mathcal F\}\tag{4}\]

contains a Euclidean ball of radius \(r\).

Proof. For nonempty \(\mathcal F\), Lemma 1 gives a strict nonnegative separator. Normalize it so that its coordinates sum to one. Hence the following linear program has positive optimum:

\[\displaystyle \max d\quad\text{subject to}\quad p\ge0,\quad \sum_jp_j=1,\quad -B\le d\le B,\quad w_S\cdot p\ge d\quad(S\in\mathcal F).\tag{5}\]

For empty \(\mathcal F\), its optimum is \(B>0\), so the same conclusion holds.

The feasible region of (5) is nonempty and compact. An optimum occurs at a vertex. There are \(m+1\) variables, and all constraint coefficients are integers of absolute value at most \(B\). Each coordinate of a vertex has, by Cramer’s rule, denominator at most

\(\displaystyle (m+1)!\,B^{m+1}=C.\)

The positive optimal coordinate \(d\) is therefore at least \(\gamma\). Thus some \(p^*\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle p^*\ge0,\qquad \sum_jp_j^*=1,\qquad w_S\cdot p^*\ge\gamma\quad(S\in\mathcal F).\)

Let

\(\displaystyle u=(1/2,\ldots,1/2),\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\gamma}{4mB},\qquad \bar p=(1-\varepsilon)p^*+\varepsilon u.\)

Every coordinate of \(\bar p\) lies between \(\varepsilon/2\) and \(1-\varepsilon/2\). Also, since \(w_S\cdot u\ge-mB/2\) and \(\varepsilon\le1/4\),

\(\displaystyle w_S\cdot\bar p\ge(1-\varepsilon)\gamma-\varepsilon mB/2\ge5\gamma/8.\)

If \(\|y-\bar p\|_2\le r=\varepsilon/4\), then \(y\in[0,1]^m\). Moreover,

\(\displaystyle |w_S\cdot(y-\bar p)|\le\|w_S\|_2r\le mBr=\gamma/16.\)

Consequently,

\(\displaystyle w_S\cdot y\ge9\gamma/16>\delta.\)

The radius-\(r\) ball around \(\bar p\) is therefore contained in (4). \(\square\)

Crucially, this radius depends only on the encoded marginal bound \(b\) and the dimension, not on the number of bundles in \(\mathcal F\).

4. A polynomial threshold routine

We now construct a routine taking a positive rational threshold \(z\). It returns either:

  • a rational distribution with item marginals at most \(b\), supported entirely on bundles of value at least \(\frac35z\); or
  • a correct conclusion that \(z>A\).

Start the rational ellipsoid procedure with a ball centered at \((1/2,\ldots,1/2)\) of radius \(R=m\), which contains \([0,1]^m\). Maintain a family \(\mathcal F\), initially empty.

At each ellipsoid center \(p\):

  • If \(p\notin[0,1]^m\), use a violated box inequality to obtain a central cut preserving the box.
  • Otherwise, run the knapsack routine (3), obtaining \(S\).
  • If \(v(S)<\frac35z\), terminate with conclusion 2.
  • Otherwise, add \(S\) to \(\mathcal F\).

The conclusion in the third step is correct, because (3) gives

\(\displaystyle \frac35\operatorname{OPT}(p)\le v(S)<\frac35z,\)

so \(\operatorname{OPT}(p)<z\). Since \(\operatorname{OPT}(p)\ge A\), this implies \(z>A\).

For the last step, feasibility of the returned bundle gives

\(\displaystyle w_S\cdot p=D(p\cdot a_S-p\cdot b)\le0.\)

If \(w_S=0\), the distribution concentrated on \(S\) already has marginals exactly \(b\), so return it. Otherwise, make the central cut retaining

\[\displaystyle w_S\cdot(y-p)\ge0.\tag{6}\]

This cut preserves every point satisfying \(w_S\cdot y\ge\delta\), because \(\delta>w_S\cdot p\).

Continue for the polynomial number of iterations needed to make the ellipsoid’s volume smaller than that of a radius-\(r\) ball.

Why termination produces an exact allocation. Every point of the final \(K_{\mathcal F}\) lies in the initial box and satisfies every retained cut. Hence \(K_{\mathcal F}\) lies in the final ellipsoid. If (1) were infeasible for the collected bundles, Lemma 3 would put a radius-\(r\) ball inside \(K_{\mathcal F}\), contradicting the volume bound.

Therefore the explicit linear program (1), restricted to the polynomially many collected bundles, is feasible. Solve it exactly and return a rational feasible distribution. Every collected bundle has value at least \(\frac35z\).

This proves both outcomes of the routine. In particular, every threshold \(z\le A\) succeeds.

All computations are polynomially bounded. Indeed,

\(\displaystyle \log(R/r)=O\bigl(m\log B+m\log m\bigr),\)

all cut normals have polynomial encoding length, and each knapsack call uses polynomial time and queries. The final explicit rational linear program also has polynomial size.

A basic feasible solution has at most \(m+1\) positive bundle probabilities: adding the \(m\) supply slacks puts (1) into standard form with \(m+1\) equations.

5. Choosing thresholds without knowing the benchmark

Under the usual binary numerator-and-denominator encoding, every positive oracle value is at least \(2^{-L}\), and every oracle value is at most \(2^L\).

For each fixed type, the objective defining \(A\) always equals the value of some bundle. Its image is therefore finite and nonempty, so its minimum is attained and is itself a bundle value. Consequently,

\[\displaystyle A=0\quad\text{or}\quad A\ge2^{-L}.\tag{7}\]

Query \(V=v([m])\). If \(V=0\), assign the empty bundle to this type.

Otherwise run the threshold routine at

\(\displaystyle z_k=2^{-L}(3/2)^k,\qquad k=0,\ldots,K,\)

where \(K\) is the first index with \(z_K>V\). There are \(O(L)\) thresholds, each with polynomial encoding length. Keep the distribution associated with the largest successful threshold.

If \(A>0\), (7) and \(A\le V\) imply that some index satisfies

\(\displaystyle z_k\le A<z_{k+1}=\frac32z_k.\)

That threshold necessarily succeeds. The retained threshold is at least \(z_k\), so every bundle in the retained distribution has value at least

\[\displaystyle \frac35z_k>\frac35\cdot\frac23A=\frac25A.\tag{8}\]

If no threshold succeeds, then \(A=0\), and assigning the empty bundle satisfies the desired guarantee. If \(A=0\) and some threshold succeeds, its returned distribution also satisfies the guarantee.

Thus each type receives a polynomially computable rational distribution \(\lambda_{t,S}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=1,\qquad \sum_{S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}\le\beta_tq_j,\)

with every supported bundle worth at least \(\frac25A_t^\infty\).

6. Combining types and verifying the output

Set

\(\displaystyle x_{t,S}=\mu_t\lambda_{t,S}.\)

Then every type receives exactly its prescribed mass:

\(\displaystyle \sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t.\)

For each item kind,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\sum_t\mu_t\beta_tq_j=q_j,\)

where the last equality uses the given entitlement normalization \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta_t=1\).

Finally, every positive entry satisfies

\(\displaystyle v_t(S)\ge\frac25A_t^\infty\ge\frac13A_t^\infty.\)

The guarantee therefore applies to every assigned bundle, as required.

There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) nonzero entries. Their masses have polynomial encoding length. The threshold grid, ellipsoid iterations, knapsack calls, and explicit linear programs together take time and value queries polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\). This settles the stated algorithmic question.
