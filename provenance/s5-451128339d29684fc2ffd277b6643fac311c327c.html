Theorem 1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a strict school ranking \(P_t\) and school-specific strict partial priority orders \(\succ_s\) over types, and rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\), compute a mass assignment \(x_{t,a}\) for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Require \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\), and individual rationality. Require stability: no positive mass assigned to a less-preferred option can claim a school that has spare capacity or has positive mass assigned to a lower-priority type. Require constrained efficiency: no other stable assignment can be coupled to the given assignment within each type so every student weakly improves and positive mass strictly improves. State the complexity in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(S\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) schools and \(A=S\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Each type has a strict linear preference order \(P_t\) on \(A\), including the outside option \(\emptyset\). Each school has a positive binary-rational capacity \(q_s\) and an explicitly listed strict partial priority order \(\succ_s\) on \(T\). Priority comparisons apply uniformly between types; students within a type are incomparable. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An assignment consists of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,a}\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\). Individual rationality requires \(x_{t,s}=0\) whenever \(\emptyset P_t s\).
An assignment is stable if it is individually rational and, whenever \(x_{t,a}>0\) and \(sP_t a\), both \(\sum_u x_{u,s}=q_s\) and \(x_{u,s}=0\) for every \(u\) with \(t\succ_s u\). Incomparability creates no priority claim.
A stable assignment \(y\) dominates \(x\) if there are nonnegative real coupling masses \(c_{t,a,b}\) with \(\sum_b c_{t,a,b}=x_{t,a}\), \(\sum_a c_{t,a,b}=y_{t,b}\), and \(c_{t,a,b}=0\) unless \(b=a\) or \(bP_t a\), such that \(\sum_{t,a,b:\,bP_t a}c_{t,a,b}>0\). A stable assignment is constrained efficient if no stable real-valued assignment dominates it.

Is computing an exact rational constrained-efficient stable mass assignment polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite students with strict partial school priorities, EADA computes a constrained-efficient stable matching in polynomial time ([Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12543v1)).
For probabilistic assignments with weak-order priorities, the vigilant eating rule yields constrained-efficient ex ante stable assignments ([Aziz–Brandl](https://brandlf.com/docs/ver.pdf)); this does not classify the support-encoded mass problem with arbitrary strict partial priorities. No classification of this exact problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Completed each strict preference ranking by explicitly including the outside option; schools ranked below it are unacceptable.
  • Required positive rational capacities, matching the positive-capacity discrete domain; imposed no upper bound or total-capacity normalization.
  • Made priorities uniform between types and left students within each type incomparable, with no additional tie-breaking condition.
  • Specified nonnegative assignment masses and formalized within-type Pareto improvement through coupling marginals.
  • Required an exact rational output while allowing real-valued stable competitors and couplings in the efficiency definition.
  • Used a nonempty school set and imposed no minimum support size: the discrete minimum of three students does not imply three types in a nonatomic society.

Polynomial-time solvability

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof combines a compressed implementation of finite EADA with an integrality argument that rules out improvements by arbitrary real-valued stable assignments.

The finite population used below is only conceptual. The algorithm stores one count per type and computes each deferred-acceptance outcome through a polynomial-time stable-allocation algorithm.

1. Integer scaling and a conceptual finite population

Choose a positive integer \(D\) divisible by every denominator appearing in the masses and capacities, with \(D\geq 3\). For example, three times the product of those denominators suffices. Define

\(N_t=D\mu_t,\qquad Q_s=Dq_s.\)

These are positive integers, and \(\sum_t N_t=D\). Although \(D\) can be exponentially large, its binary encoding has length \(O(L)\).

Conceptually, replace type \(t\) by \(N_t\) distinct students, all having preference \(P_t\). At school \(s\), every student of type \(t\) has priority over every student of type \(u\) exactly when \(t\succ_s u\); students within a type remain incomparable.

These lifted priorities are strict partial orders: irreflexivity follows from the type relation, and two consecutive lifted comparisons imply the corresponding type comparison by transitivity.

For a finite matching \(M\), let \(X_{t,a}\) count its type-\(t\) students assigned to \(a\). Its mass projection is

\(x_{t,a}=X_{t,a}/D.\)

Stability correspondence. A finite matching is stable under the lifted partial priorities if and only if its mass projection is stable under the definition in the question.

Indeed, positive mass at \(a\) means that some corresponding student is assigned to \(a\). That student wanting \(s\) triggers exactly the requirements that \(s\) be full and contain no student of a lower-priority type. Individual rationality also agrees. This correspondence does not depend on which particular students of a type occupy its assigned schools.

2. Integral faces of the stable-assignment set

We first establish the integrality fact needed both for compression and for efficiency against real-valued improvements.

Consider integer type supplies \(d_t\) and integer school capacities \(Q_s\), with the same stability definition but without normalization. Let \(z\) be any stable real-valued assignment. Define its support and demanded schools by

\(E(z)=\{(t,a):z_{t,a}>0\},\)

\(H(z)=\{s:\text{some }(t,a)\in E(z)\text{ satisfies }sP_t a\}.\)

Let \(\mathcal F(z)\) consist of all assignments \(w\) satisfying:

  • the type supplies \(d_t\) and school capacity bounds \(Q_s\);
  • \(w_{t,a}=0\) whenever \((t,a)\notin E(z)\);
  • \(\sum_t w_{t,s}=Q_s\) for every \(s\in H(z)\).

Lemma 1

Every assignment in \(\mathcal F(z)\) is stable, and every vertex of \(\mathcal F(z)\) is integral.

Proof. Individual rationality follows because every allowed coordinate belongs to the support of the individually rational assignment \(z\).

Suppose \(w_{t,a}>0\) and \(sP_t a\). Then \((t,a)\in E(z)\), so \(s\in H(z)\) and \(w\) fills \(s\). Stability of \(z\) implies \(z_{u,s}=0\) whenever \(t\succ_s u\). Those coordinates are excluded from \(E(z)\), so \(w_{u,s}=0\) as well. Thus \(w\) is stable.

For integrality, \(\mathcal F(z)\) is a transportation-flow polytope with integer supplies and integer bounds. The outside option is an additional destination whose capacity can be set to \(\sum_t d_t\). Requiring a school to be full imposes equal integer lower and upper bounds on its outgoing flow.

Directed network incidence matrices are totally unimodular. Consequently, a flow polytope with integer supplies and bounds has integral vertices. This applies to \(\mathcal F(z)\). \(\square\)

Lemma 2: a real improvement implies a same-grid improvement

Suppose \(x_{t,a}\) is an integer multiple of \(1/D\), and \(y\) is a stable real-valued assignment dominating \(x\). Then some stable assignment \(y'\), also on the \(1/D\) grid, dominates \(x\).

Proof. Let \(c_{t,a,b}\) be a domination coupling from \(x\) to \(y\).

Construct a flow network with an origin node for each \((t,a)\), having integer supply \(Dx_{t,a}\), and a destination node for each \(b\in A\). Permit the arc from \((t,a)\) to \(b\) precisely when

\((t,b)\in E(y)\quad\text{and}\quad(b=a\text{ or }bP_t a).\)

School destination \(s\) has outgoing capacity \(Q_s\), and its outgoing flow is required to equal \(Q_s\) when \(s\in H(y)\). The outside destination has capacity \(D\).

The flows \(Dc_{t,a,b}\) make this network feasible. Give an origin-to-destination arc value \(1\) when \(bP_t a\), and value \(0\) otherwise. The given flow has strictly positive total value.

Maximizing this value over the network has an integral optimum, by network-flow integrality. Its value remains strictly positive. Write its integral arc flows as \(C_{t,a,b}\), and set

\(y'_{t,b}=D^{-1}\sum_a C_{t,a,b}.\)

The scaled assignment \(Dy'\) belongs to the support-and-fullness face determined by \(Dy\), so Lemma 1 makes \(y'\) stable. Moreover, \(C/D\) is a domination coupling from \(x\) to \(y'\), with positive improved mass. \(\square\)

This lemma addresses the quantifier over all stable real-valued dominators.

3. Computing deferred acceptance without expanding types

For each school, choose a strict linear extension of its priority order on types. In the conceptual finite population, order students in consecutive type blocks according to that extension.

Give students within each type a common fixed order, used at every school. All resulting school priorities are strict linear orders extending the original lifted partial priorities.

We use the following established stable-allocation result.

Stable-allocation theorem. In a bipartite allocation problem with strict preference lists and rational vertex capacities, a proposer-optimal stable allocation can be computed in polynomial time. Proposer-optimal means that each proposer’s allocation vector, listed from best to worst, is lexicographically maximal among all stable allocations. Unallocated quantities and unused capacities can be represented by dummy vertices. The algorithm and this optimality property are established in Section 2, Lemma 1, and Section 3, Lemma 5, of [Dean–Munshi, *Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

Here the proposers are types, with integer supplies, and the receiving vertices are schools, with capacities \(Q_s\). Schools strictly rank types by the selected extensions. Only currently acceptable type-school pairs are included. There are no effective edge restrictions beyond vertex capacities; edge bounds \(\min(d_t,Q_s)\), if required by the formulation, are redundant.

Lemma 3

The stable-allocation algorithm returns exactly the aggregate counts of student-proposing deferred acceptance in the conceptual finite population.

Proof. Fix a round’s active students and their preference lists. Students of a type share a list, and every school uses the same within-type student order.

Let \(F\) be the aggregate of the finite deferred-acceptance matching. Across different types, finite stability implies the stability conditions of the type-level stable-allocation problem. Thus \(F\) is a stable type-level allocation.

Now consider any integral stable type-level allocation \(Z\). Realize it as a finite matching by assigning, within each type, earlier students to better alternatives.

This realization is stable under the strict refined school priorities:

  • A blocking comparison between different types would contradict stability of \(Z\).
  • Within one type, an earlier student receives an alternative weakly preferred to that of every later student. Hence no earlier student wants a school occupied by a later student.
  • A desired vacancy would also contradict stability of \(Z\).

The student-proposing deferred-acceptance matching weakly improves every student relative to every stable finite matching under strict school priorities. Therefore, for each type, \(F\)’s allocation vector is lexicographically at least that of every integral stable type-level allocation.

It remains to compare \(F\) with real-valued stable allocations. Suppose a real stable allocation \(Z\) has a lexicographically better vector for some type. By Lemma 1, its support-and-fullness face \(\mathcal F(Z)\) is integral and consists entirely of stable allocations.

Lexicographically maximize that type’s vector over \(\mathcal F(Z)\). This can be done by successively maximizing coordinates and restricting to the resulting faces. The final face contains an integral vertex, whose vector is lexicographically at least that of \(Z\). This contradicts the preceding optimality of \(F\) among integral stable allocations.

Thus \(F\) is proposer-optimal among all real stable allocations. The stable-allocation theorem returns that same allocation. \(\square\)

The lemma also proves that every such computed outcome is integral. Only active type counts are needed: deleting students within a type preserves their common relative order, and replacing their labels by their ranks among the remaining students changes no aggregate outcome.

4. Compressed EADA

We use two finite EADA facts for strict partial school priorities:

  • EADA, using any strict linear extension of the priorities, produces a constrained-efficient stable matching.
  • Its intermediate matchings weakly improve every student from one round to the next.

These are Theorem 1 and Appendix Lemma 1 of [Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.12543v1).

Here is the compressed implementation. Maintain:

  • integer active counts \(d_t\), initially \(N_t\);
  • a remaining school set \(R\), initially \(S\);
  • current acceptable-school lists, initially those specified by \(P_t\);
  • fixed assignment counts \(X_{t,a}\), initially zero.

In each round, compute deferred-acceptance aggregate counts \(Y_{t,a}\) using Lemma 3.

A remaining school is underdemanded when no active student prefers it to their current assignment, using the current preference lists. Thus its membership in the set \(U\) is determined entirely from the positive coordinates of \(Y\).

Set

\(e_{t,a}=Y_{t,a}\quad\text{if }a\in U\cup\{\emptyset\},\qquad e_{t,a}=0\quad\text{otherwise}.\)

Fix these counts by adding \(e\) to \(X\), and update

\(d_t\leftarrow d_t-\sum_a e_{t,a},\qquad R\leftarrow R\setminus U.\)

For every remaining school \(s\), make \(s\) unacceptable to active type \(t\) whenever there exist \(u,a\) such that

\(e_{u,a}>0,\qquad u\succ_s t,\qquad sP_u a.\)

The comparison \(sP_u a\) uses the original preference order. Retain the original relative order of all still-acceptable schools. Repeat until no active students remain.

Exact correspondence with finite EADA

The algorithm is an exact simulation of EADA on the conceptual finite population.

To verify this inductively, suppose the current counts and lists describe its active students. Lemma 3 gives exactly its next deferred-acceptance aggregate. Underdemandedness depends only on whether a positive number of students prefer a school to their current assignment, so the set \(U\) also agrees.

Most importantly, finite EADA’s deletion condition is identical for all remaining students of a type. An eliminated student of type \(u\), assigned to \(a\), causes school \(s\) to be deleted for a remaining type-\(t\) student exactly when \(u\succ_s t\) and \(sP_u a\). Existence of such an eliminated student is precisely \(e_{u,a}>0\).

Thus no type needs to be split into different active preference classes. The count updates, school removals, and preference deletions all agree with finite EADA.

The hypotheses of the finite theorem hold: preferences are strict, capacities are positive integers, lifted priorities are strict partial orders, and the chosen strict school orders extend them.

Polynomially many rounds

All unmatched students are removed after the first round. By EADA’s intermediate monotonicity, every student active thereafter remains assigned to a school acceptable under their original preferences. Hence subsequent rounds remove no unmatched students.

In any finite deferred-acceptance run with active students, some student is either unmatched or assigned to an underdemanded school. Here is a direct verification. If no rejection occurs, every school is underdemanded. Otherwise, consider the last rejected student. After that rejection, the student either remains unmatched or is accepted by a school that never rejected anyone: a school that previously rejected remains full, so accepting a new student there would cause another rejection.

Consequently, each round after the first with active students removes at least one school. There are at most \(m+1\) rounds.

5. Constrained efficiency against real assignments

Let \(M\) be the conceptual finite EADA output. The compressed algorithm returns its aggregate \(X\), and we output

\(x_{t,a}=X_{t,a}/D.\)

The finite EADA theorem makes \(M\) stable and constrained efficient. By the stability correspondence, \(x\) is stable.

Suppose, for contradiction, that a stable real-valued assignment dominates \(x\). Lemma 2 gives a stable dominator \(y'\) on the same \(1/D\) grid, together with integral coupling counts \(C_{t,a,b}\).

For each type \(t\) and original assignment \(a\), partition the \(X_{t,a}\) students assigned to \(a\) in \(M\) into groups of sizes \(C_{t,a,b}\), and send each group to \(b\). The coupling’s origin-supply equations make these partitions possible.

The resulting finite matching \(M'\) has aggregate \(Dy'\). Every student receives either their original alternative or a strictly preferred one, and at least one student strictly improves. Since \(y'\) is stable, the stability correspondence makes \(M'\) stable under the original lifted partial priorities.

Thus \(M'\) Pareto dominates \(M\), contradicting finite EADA’s constrained efficiency. Therefore \(x\) is constrained efficient against every stable real-valued assignment.

6. Running time and exact output

The algorithm stores only type counts, school sets, preference-deletion flags, and assignment counts.

  • The scaling integer \(D\) and all scaled capacities have polynomial bit length.
  • Every deferred-acceptance computation uses a stable-allocation instance with \(O(\tau+m)\) vertices and \(O(\tau m)\) edges.
  • Underdemandedness and preference deletions require only polynomially many comparisons.
  • There are at most \(m+1\) rounds.
  • Every output numerator satisfies \(0\leq X_{t,a}\leq N_t\leq D\).

Hence the total computation and the exact rational output have size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The exponential conceptual population is never enumerated.
