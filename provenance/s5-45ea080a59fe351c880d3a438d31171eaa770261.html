The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational population distribution \(\mu\), and strict preferences over acceptable partner types, decide whether there exists a mass matching with blocking-agent mass at most \(b\). Let \(x_{tu}\) denote mass paired between distinct types \(t,u\), \(x_{tt}\) mass of same-type pairs, and \(x_{t\bot}\) unmatched mass, subject to \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}+x_{t\bot}=\mu_t\). A positive assigned cell is blocking-exposed when there is a positive-mass pair \(u\!-\!w\) with \(u\succ_t v\) and \(t\succ_u w\); minimize the total mass in exposed cells.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\) encoded as binary rationals, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types.
Each type \(t\) strictly ranks an acceptable set \(A_t\subseteq T\), possibly including \(t\), above being unmatched \(\bot\). Only mutually acceptable types may pair or block; every type has a mutually acceptable partner type. Same-type partnerships involve distinct individuals.
A mass matching has nonnegative real variables \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) for distinct types, \(x_{tt}\) for same-type pairs, and \(x_{t\bot}\) for unmatched mass, satisfying \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}+x_{t\bot}=\mu_t\); unacceptable pair variables are zero.
Define assigned-agent masses \(a_{tv}=x_{tv}\) for \(v\ne t\), \(a_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and \(a_{t\bot}=x_{t\bot}\).
A cell \((t,v)\) with \(a_{tv}>0\) is exposed exactly when there exist \(u\in T\) and \(w\in A_u\cup\{\bot\}\) such that \(t,u\) are mutually acceptable, \(a_{uw}>0\), \(u\succ_t v\), and \(t\succ_u w\). This includes witnesses who are unmatched and witnesses of the same type.
Let \(B(x)=\sum_{(t,v)\text{ exposed}}a_{tv}\), counting each assigned-agent cell once. The input includes a binary rational bound \(b\in[0,1]\); the decision problem asks whether some mass matching satisfies \(B(x)\le b\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m=\tau\) partner types, support size \(\tau\), and total input bit length \(L\), including the encoding of \(b\).

For finite agents, minimizing the number involved in blocking pairs is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by that number, even with strict preference lists of length at most five. [Chen et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol107-icalp2018/LIPIcs.ICALP.2018.35/LIPIcs.ICALP.2018.35.pdf)
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the population distribution to total mass one and took the bound b to be a binary rational in [0,1].
  • Required mutual acceptability for both partnerships and blocking, and retained the exclusion of isolated types.
  • Ranked every acceptable partner type above being unmatched and allowed unmatched positive mass to witness exposure, restoring a move included in the discrete blocking definition.
  • Allowed same-type acceptability and blocking between distinct individuals of that type, consistent with the graded question's same-type pair variables.
  • Specified nonnegative real matching variables with symmetric cross-type pair masses; same-type pair mass contributes twice its value to assigned-agent mass.
  • Counted exposed assigned-agent cells once, rather than counting witness pairs or charging both endpoints whenever only one endpoint's cell is exposed.
  • Did not impose parity on the number of types: the discrete even-agent convention has no counterpart for divisible population mass.
  • Set m equal to tau because partner alternatives here are the supported types; defined L to include the entire encoded input.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). In fact, every valid instance admits a mass matching with \(\ B(x)=0\), including instances with arbitrary positive rational masses and same-type partnerships.

This follows from Scarf’s lemma. The key is to apply it to assigned-agent masses, so that a same-type partnership consumes twice its pair mass.

1. An equivalent stability condition

Delete unilaterally acceptable partners from each preference list; their matching variables are necessarily zero, and they cannot block. This preserves all relevant comparisons.

For a mutually acceptable pair of types \(\ t,u\), define
\[ D_t(u)=\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}a_{tv}, \]
where the sum includes \(\ v=\bot\).

Thus \(\ D_t(u)>0\) exactly when some positive mass of type \(\ t\) is assigned to an outcome strictly worse than \(\ u\).

If a cell \(\ (t,v)\) is exposed with witness \(\ (u,w)\), then both \(\ D_t(u)>0\) and \(\ D_u(t)>0\). Conversely, if these two quantities are positive, positive cells witnessing both inequalities exist and are exposed. This also applies when \(\ t=u\).

Consequently, it suffices to construct a feasible matching satisfying, for every mutually acceptable pair,
\[ D_t(u)=0\quad\text{or}\quad D_u(t)=0. \]
For a same-type pair, this condition becomes \(\ D_t(t)=0\).

2. The existence theorem used

We use the following matrix form of Scarf’s lemma.

Let \(\ Q,C\in\mathbb R^{n\times q}\), with \(\ q>n\), and let \(\ d\in\mathbb R_{>0}^n\). Suppose:

  • the first \(\ n\) columns of \(\ Q\) form the identity matrix;
  • \(\ \{y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^q:Qy=d\}\) is bounded;
  • \(\ C_{ii}<C_{ik}<C_{ij}\) whenever \(\ i,j\le n\), \(\ i\ne j\), and \(\ k>n\).

Then there exists \(\ y\ge0\) with \(\ Qy=d\) such that, for every column \(\ e\), some row \(\ t\) satisfies
\[ C_{t,e}\le C_{t,f}\qquad\text{for every }f\text{ with }y_f>0. \]

No integrality assumption on \(\ d\) is required. This is Theorem 1.1 of [Aharoni and Fleiner, *On a lemma of Scarf*](https://egres.elte.hu/tr/egres-01-15.pdf).

3. Encoding the mass matching

Index rows by the \(\ \tau\) supported types. Introduce one column for each of the following activities:

  • \(\ s_t\): unmatched mass of type \(\ t\);
  • \(\ e_{tu}\): a mutually acceptable partnership between distinct types;
  • \(\ e_{tt}\): a same-type partnership, when acceptable.

Put the unmatched columns first. Write \(\ \mathbf e_t\) for the unit vector at type \(\ t\), and define
\[ Q_{\cdot,s_t}=\mathbf e_t,\qquad Q_{\cdot,e_{tu}}=\mathbf e_t+\mathbf e_u\ (t\ne u),\qquad Q_{\cdot,e_{tt}}=\mathbf e_t. \]

The corresponding activity variables are
\[ y_{s_t}=x_{t\bot},\qquad y_{e_{tu}}=x_{tu}\ (t\ne u),\qquad y_{e_{tt}}=2x_{tt}. \]

Therefore \(\ Qy=\mu\) is exactly the mass-conservation system in the question.

The singleton column for \(\ e_{tt}\) represents assigned-agent mass, not an individual partnering with itself. Its pair mass is \(\ y_{e_{tt}}/2\), consistently with partnerships between distinct individuals.

We now define \(\ C\). Let \(\ d_t\) be the number of mutually acceptable partner types of \(\ t\), including itself if acceptable. Assign numerical values \(\ r_t(v)\in\{1,\ldots,d_t\}\), increasing with preference, and set \(\ r_t(\bot)=0\). Thus better outcomes have larger values, and \(\ d_t\le m=\tau\).

For unmatched columns, set
\[ C_{t,s_t}=0,\qquad C_{t,s_u}=m+2\quad(u\ne t). \]

For partnership columns, set
\[ C_{t,e}= \begin{cases} r_t(v),&\text{if }e\text{ assigns type }t\text{ to partner type }v,\\ m+1,&\text{if }e\text{ does not involve }t. \end{cases} \]

All hypotheses of Scarf’s lemma hold:

  • The first \(\ \tau\) columns of \(\ Q\) are the identity.
  • The feasible set is nonempty: take \(\ y_{s_t}=\mu_t\) and all partnership variables zero.
  • Every column has a coefficient equal to \(\ 1\). Hence each coordinate satisfies \(\ 0\le y_e\le\mu_t\le1\) for some incident type, so the feasible set is bounded.
  • Every partnership entry of \(\ C\) lies between \(\ 1\) and \(\ m+1\), strictly between the own unmatched entry \(\ 0\) and any other unmatched entry \(\ m+2\).
  • There is at least one partnership column by the partner-existence assumption, so \(\ q>\tau\); all \(\ \mu_t\) are positive.

Apply the lemma with \(\ d=\mu\).

4. The resulting matching has no exposed cells

Let \(\ y\) be the solution supplied by Scarf’s lemma, and consider a partnership column \(\ e\). Its domination property supplies a row \(\ t\) with
\[ C_{t,e}\le C_{t,f}\qquad\text{whenever }y_f>0. \]

First, this row must be incident to \(\ e\). Indeed, \(\ (Qy)_t=\mu_t>0\) implies that some positive column \(\ f\) involves \(\ t\). For such a column, \(\ C_{t,f}\le m\). If \(\ e\) did not involve \(\ t\), then \(\ C_{t,e}=m+1\), contradicting the domination inequality.

Suppose \(\ e\) assigns \(\ t\) to partner type \(\ u\). For every positive activity involving \(\ t\), the same inequality says that its assigned outcome is at least as good as \(\ u\). In particular, unmatched mass is excluded, because its preference value is \(\ 0<r_t(u)\). Thus \(\ D_t(u)=0\).

Therefore every distinct-type partnership column has an endpoint satisfying \(\ D_t(u)=0\) or \(\ D_u(t)=0\). A same-type column has only one incident row, so it gives \(\ D_t(t)=0\).

Recover \(\ x\) from \(\ y\) using the formulas above. Section 1 now rules out every exposure witness, including unmatched witnesses and same-type witnesses. Hence \(\ B(x)=0\). Since \(\ B\) is nonnegative, its minimum is exactly zero.

5. Running time and the finite-agent comparison

Every valid bound satisfies \(\ b\ge0\). The decision algorithm therefore returns YES for every valid input.

Reading the complete input and returning YES takes \(\ O(L)\) bit operations, including reading the encoding of \(\ b\). This bound applies for arbitrary \(\ m=\tau\); equivalently it is \(\ O(L+m+\tau)=O(L)\). Under the valid-input promise, returning the answer without reading the input takes constant time.

The finite-agent hardness does not transfer. Divisible mass permits stable fractional configurations that have no integral counterpart. For example, with three equal-mass types and cyclic preferences, assigning \(\ x_{12}=x_{23}=x_{31}=1/6\) gives zero exposed mass: for each potential pair, one endpoint already receives no partner worse than the other endpoint. This is a substantive consequence of the continuous model, and the argument above establishes it for every valid instance.
