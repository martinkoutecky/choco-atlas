The anchor (named result in the paper)

Corollary 5 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete student preference rankings, partial school priorities over types, and rational per-capita school capacities \(q_s\), compute a feasible stable assignment of mass to schools and the outside option that is Pareto-undominated among stable assignments. Pareto improvement is evaluated by coupling assignments within each type so that no mass receives a less-preferred assignment and positive mass receives a strictly preferred one.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) schools \(S\), positive binary-rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\), and an unlimited outside option \(\varnothing\). Each type has a strict complete ranking \(P_t\) of \(S\cup\{\varnothing\}\), with the outside option allowed anywhere.
Each school has an explicitly listed strict partial order \(\succ_s\) on types. All members of a type share its ranking and priorities; members of the same type have no priority over one another. Incomparabilities impose no additional tie-breaking conditions.
An assignment specifies divisible masses \(x_{ta}\geq0\), with \(\sum_{a\in S\cup\{\varnothing\}}x_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq q_s\).
Write \(D_{ts}=\sum_{a:sP_ta}x_{ta}\). Stability means individual rationality \(x_{ta}=0\) whenever \(\varnothing P_ta\), non-wastefulness \(D_{ts}>0\Rightarrow\sum_u x_{us}=q_s\), and fairness: no \(t,u,s\) satisfy \(D_{ts}>0\), \(x_{us}>0\), and \(t\succ_su\).
An assignment \(y\) Pareto dominates \(x\) if, within each type, their assigned masses admit a coupling sending every unit to a weakly preferred option, with positive total mass sent to strictly preferred options. Undominated means undominated among all stable assignments.

Is computing an exact rational stable assignment that is Pareto-undominated among stable assignments solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard, where \(L\) is the total binary input length?

In the discrete model with strict partial-order priorities, the Pareto-undominated stable matchings are exactly the outputs of efficiency-adjusted deferred acceptance as total-order priority extensions vary. [Kitahara–Okumura](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.00641v2)
For weak-order priorities, fractional deferred acceptance and trading produces an ex-ante stable random assignment that is ordinally Pareto-undominated among ex-ante stable assignments; this addresses a restricted priority domain. [Kesten–Ünver](https://lies.mat.uc.cl/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/A-theory-of-theory-of-school-choice-lotteries.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one so that capacities have the stated per-capita interpretation; retained strictly positive rational capacities without imposing an aggregate capacity constraint.
  • Interpreted complete student rankings as strict rankings including the outside option, which may lie anywhere, and restored individual rationality.
  • Interpreted partial priorities as strict partial orders, matching the anchor's transitivity requirement; members of the same type are mutually incomparable.
  • Defined mass stability through positive mass desiring a school, full capacity whenever such mass exists, and exclusion of lower-priority assigned mass whenever higher-priority mass desires that school.
  • Used divisible masses and required an exact rational output; Pareto comparisons range over all stable mass assignments.
  • Used a nonempty finite school set and nonempty support. The discrete restriction of at least three students has no cardinality analogue for an atomless society and does not require three distinct types.

1. Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\).

The algorithm starts from a rational stable assignment and repeatedly solves a linear program. It terminates after at most \(m\tau+1\) such optimization rounds. The key fact is that every dominated stable assignment admits a stable improvement through a trading cycle whose recipients have no higher-priority type still desiring the receiving school.

The argument applies to arbitrary strict partial-order priorities and permits the outside option anywhere in each ranking.

2. Pareto dominance as cumulative inequalities

Write \(A=S\cup\{\varnothing\}\). For each type \(t\), list its options from best to worst as \(a_{t,1},\ldots,a_{t,m+1}\), and define

\(C_{t,k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{t,a_{t,i}}\), for \(k=1,\ldots,m\).

Write \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\) when

\(C_{t,k}(y)\geq C_{t,k}(x)\) for every \(t,k\).

This is equivalent to the existence, within every type, of a coupling that moves each unit to a weakly preferred option. Necessity follows because such a coupling cannot move mass out of any initial segment of the ranking.

For sufficiency, identify the mass of type \(t\) with the interval \((0,\mu_t]\). Under each assignment, assign consecutive intervals to options in preference order. The cumulative inequalities imply that the option assigned under \(y\) to each interval point is weakly preferred to its option under \(x\). This gives the required coupling.

Moreover, Pareto dominance as defined in the question is equivalent to \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\) and \(y\neq x\). Indeed, define the linear objective

\(U(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=1}^{m}C_{t,k}(x)\).

Moving a unit from rank \(i\) to a strictly better rank \(j\) increases this objective by \(i-j>0\). Equivalently, under cumulative dominance, \(U(y)>U(x)\) exactly when at least one cumulative inequality is strict.

If \(r_t(s)\) is the rank of school \(s\), then

\(D_{ts}(x)=\mu_t-C_{t,r_t(s)}(x)\),

where the cumulative sum at rank \(m+1\) is understood to equal \(\mu_t\). Consequently,

\(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad D_{ts}(y)\leq D_{ts}(x)\).

3. Occupancy is invariant under stable Pareto improvements

Let \(h_s(x)=\sum_t x_{ts}\).

Lemma 1. If \(x,y\) are stable and \(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), then \(h_s(y)=h_s(x)\) for every school \(s\), and \(y_{t\varnothing}=x_{t\varnothing}\) for every type \(t\).

*Proof.* First suppose \(y_{ts}>x_{ts}\). Define the mass strictly above \(s\) by

\(B_{ts}(x)=\sum_{a:\,aP_ts}x_{ta}\).

Cumulative dominance gives \(B_{ts}(y)\geq B_{ts}(x)\). Since the row sums equal \(\mu_t\),

\(D_{ts}(x)-D_{ts}(y)=B_{ts}(y)-B_{ts}(x)+y_{ts}-x_{ts}>0\).

Thus \(D_{ts}(x)>0\), and non-wastefulness of \(x\) implies \(h_s(x)=q_s\).

If \(h_s(y)>h_s(x)\), some type must satisfy \(y_{ts}>x_{ts}\). The preceding argument would give \(h_s(x)=q_s\), contradicting feasibility of \(y\). Hence

\(h_s(y)\leq h_s(x)\) for every \(s\).

Individual rationality makes all options below \(\varnothing\) have zero mass. The cumulative inequality immediately above \(\varnothing\) therefore gives

\(y_{t\varnothing}\leq x_{t\varnothing}\) for every \(t\).

This also holds when \(\varnothing\) is ranked first, because individual rationality then forces all mass outside. Summing yields

\(\sum_s h_s(y)=1-\sum_t y_{t\varnothing}\geq 1-\sum_t x_{t\varnothing}=\sum_s h_s(x)\).

Together with the school-by-school inequalities, this forces equality at every school. The outside masses have equal sums and weakly decrease individually, so they too are individually equal. \(\square\)

4. A polytope consisting entirely of stable improvements

For a stable assignment \(x\), define

\(\mathcal A(x)=\{(t,s)\in T\times S:\text{ there is no }v\succ_s t\text{ with }D_{vs}(x)>0\}\).

Fairness implies that \(x_{ts}>0\) only if \((t,s)\in\mathcal A(x)\).

Also, cumulative dominance makes these sets grow monotonically:

\(y\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal A(x)\subseteq\mathcal A(y)\).

Define \(\mathcal F(x)\) to be the set of assignments \(z\) satisfying:

  • feasibility and individual rationality;
  • \(z\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\);
  • \(h_s(z)=h_s(x)\) for every school \(s\);
  • \(z_{ts}=0\) whenever \((t,s)\notin\mathcal A(x)\).

These are linear constraints. The set is nonempty because it contains \(x\), and it is bounded because every coordinate lies between \(0\) and its type mass.

Lemma 2. Every \(z\in\mathcal F(x)\) is stable.

*Proof.* Individual rationality holds by definition. If \(D_{ts}(z)>0\), cumulative dominance implies \(D_{ts}(x)>0\). Hence non-wastefulness of \(x\), together with the fixed school totals, gives

\(h_s(z)=h_s(x)=q_s\).

For fairness, suppose \(D_{vs}(z)>0\), \(z_{ts}>0\), and \(v\succ_s t\). Then \(D_{vs}(x)>0\), while positive \(z_{ts}\) requires \((t,s)\in\mathcal A(x)\). These statements contradict the definition of \(\mathcal A(x)\). \(\square\)

A dominating stable assignment need not itself belong to \(\mathcal F(x)\). The next lemma supplies the crucial bridge.

5. Every dominated stable assignment has an admissible trading cycle

An admissible trading cycle at \(x\) consists of distinct schools \(s_1,\ldots,s_k\), with \(k\geq2\), and types \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\), which may repeat, such that, using cyclic indices,

\(x_{t_i s_i}>0,\qquad s_{i+1}P_{t_i}s_i,\qquad (t_i,s_{i+1})\in\mathcal A(x)\).

Lemma 3. If a stable assignment \(x\) is Pareto dominated by another stable assignment, then an admissible trading cycle exists at \(x\). Every such cycle produces an assignment in \(\mathcal F(x)\) with strictly larger \(U\).

*Proof.* Let the stable assignment \(y\) Pareto dominate \(x\). Define

\(G=\{s\in S:y_{rs}>x_{rs}\text{ for some }r\in T\}\).

By Lemma 1, every type’s outside mass is unchanged. Since \(y\neq x\) and row sums are fixed, some school coordinate increases. Thus \(G\neq\varnothing\).

Fix \(s\in G\), and choose \(r\) with \(y_{rs}>x_{rs}\). The calculation in Lemma 1 gives \(D_{rs}(x)>0\). Let

\(E_s=\{t:D_{ts}(x)>0\}\).

Choose a maximal element \(v\), under \(\succ_s\), of the nonempty set

\(H_s=\{r\}\cup\{t\in E_s:t\succ_s r\}\).

We claim that no member of \(E_s\) has priority above \(v\). If \(w\in E_s\) and \(w\succ_s v\), then either \(v=r\), or transitivity gives \(w\succ_s r\). In either case, \(w\in H_s\), contradicting maximality of \(v\). Therefore

\((v,s)\in\mathcal A(x)\).

We next show that \(v\) loses some mass at an option below \(s\).

If \(v=r\), the calculation in Lemma 1 gives \(D_{vs}(x)>D_{vs}(y)\). If \(v\succ_s r\), fairness of \(y\), together with \(y_{rs}>0\), gives \(D_{vs}(y)=0\), whereas \(v\in E_s\) gives \(D_{vs}(x)>0\). Thus in either case there is an option \(a\) satisfying

\(sP_va,\qquad x_{va}>y_{va}\).

Lemma 1 excludes \(a=\varnothing\). Hence \(a\) is a school. Its total occupancy is unchanged, so the decrease of \(v\)’s mass at \(a\) must be offset by another type’s increase. Therefore \(a\in G\).

For every \(s\in G\), we have consequently constructed a directed edge \(a\to s\), labelled by \(v\), satisfying

\(a\in G,\qquad x_{va}>0,\qquad sP_va,\qquad(v,s)\in\mathcal A(x)\).

Every vertex of this finite directed graph has an incoming edge. Following incoming predecessors must eventually repeat a vertex, producing a directed cycle. No edge is a loop, since its label strictly prefers its destination to its source. Taking a simple directed cycle gives an admissible trading cycle.

To execute it, choose

\(0<\delta\leq\min_i x_{t_i s_i}\),

and, for every \(i\), move mass \(\delta\) of type \(t_i\) from \(s_i\) to \(s_{i+1}\).

The distinct source schools ensure nonnegativity even when types repeat. Each type’s total mass is preserved, and every moved unit strictly improves. Each school loses and gains exactly \(\delta\), so its total occupancy is preserved. Individual rationality holds because each destination is preferred to an initially occupied, individually rational source option.

Every newly positive school coordinate belongs to \(\mathcal A(x)\), and every initially positive coordinate already belongs to \(\mathcal A(x)\). Thus the resulting assignment \(z\) belongs to \(\mathcal F(x)\). Finally,

\(U(z)-U(x)=\delta\sum_i\bigl(r_{t_i}(s_i)-r_{t_i}(s_{i+1})\bigr)>0\).

Lemma 2 establishes its stability. \(\square\)

Transitivity is used precisely when showing that the selected recipient \(v\) has no higher-priority desiring type.

6. Computing the initial stable assignment

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for bipartite stable allocation with strict preferences and rational capacities. In this model, vertices have quotas, edges have upper bounds, and an unsaturated edge blocks when both endpoints have either unused quota or positive allocation on a less-preferred edge. A stable allocation can be computed in strongly polynomial time by the inductive algorithm of Baïou and Balinski. See their [corrected paper, Section 2](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

Apply the theorem as follows:

  • create a type vertex with quota \(\mu_t\) and a school vertex with quota \(q_s\);
  • include an edge \((t,s)\) exactly when \(sP_t\varnothing\), with upper bound \(\mu_t\);
  • use the type’s given ranking on its incident edges;
  • topologically sort each school’s strict partial order to obtain a strict total-order extension, and use that extension on its incident edges.

All quotas are positive, all edge bounds are rational, and the graph is bipartite. The preference lists are strict. Thus the theorem’s hypotheses hold. The construction has polynomial size in \(m,\tau,L\).

Let \(f\) be the resulting stable allocation, and set

\(x_{ts}=f_{ts},\qquad x_{t\varnothing}=\mu_t-\sum_s f_{ts}\),

with zero mass on absent school edges.

This assignment is feasible and individually rational. To check its stability, consider a school with \(D_{ts}(x)>0\). Such a school must be acceptable to \(t\); otherwise individual rationality would imply \(D_{ts}(x)=0\). Moreover,

\(\sum_{a\in S:\,a=s\text{ or }aP_ts}f_{ta}<\mu_t\),

so the type endpoint desires more allocation on \((t,s)\), and \(f_{ts}<\mu_t\), its edge bound.

Stable-allocation stability therefore forces the school to be full and to allocate no positive mass to a type below \(t\) in the total-order extension. This gives non-wastefulness and fairness for that extension. Since the extension preserves every original priority comparison, fairness also holds for the original partial order.

We have therefore obtained an exact rational stable starting assignment in polynomial time.

7. The algorithm and its termination

Starting from the assignment just constructed, repeat:

  • Compute \(\mathcal A(x)\).
  • Solve the linear program maximizing \(U(z)\) over \(z\in\mathcal F(x)\), choosing an optimal vertex \(z\).
  • If \(\mathcal A(z)=\mathcal A(x)\), output \(z\). Otherwise replace \(x\) by \(z\) and repeat.

Each iterate is stable by Lemma 2 and weakly Pareto improves its predecessor.

Whenever the algorithm continues,

\(\mathcal A(x)\subsetneq\mathcal A(z)\).

There are only \(m\tau\) type-school pairs. Hence there are at most \(m\tau\) continuation rounds and at most \(m\tau+1\) linear-program optimization rounds.

It remains to prove that the returned assignment is undominated.

Suppose the algorithm returns \(z\), but some stable assignment Pareto dominates \(z\). Lemma 3 supplies an assignment \(w\in\mathcal F(z)\) with \(U(w)>U(z)\).

Because \(z\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\), we have \(w\succeq_{\mathrm{SD}}x\). Its school totals equal those of \(z\), hence those of \(x\). Its school support is contained in \(\mathcal A(z)=\mathcal A(x)\). Consequently \(w\in\mathcal F(x)\), contradicting the optimality of \(z\) in Step 2.

Thus the returned assignment is Pareto-undominated among all stable assignments, including assignments with real-valued masses.

8. Exact arithmetic and polynomial encoding length

Each linear program has \(d=\tau(m+1)\) variables and polynomially many constraints. Its constraint coefficients lie in \(\{-1,0,1\}\); the objective coefficients are integers between \(0\) and \(m\).

To verify that repeated optimization does not cause excessive denominator growth, let \(B_0\) be a common denominator for the input data and the initial assignment. Its binary length is polynomial because initialization is a polynomial-time exact rational computation.

Suppose the current assignment and input data have common denominator \(B\). Every right-hand side in the next linear program is then an integer multiple of \(1/B\). At a vertex, choose \(d\) linearly independent active constraints, with integer coefficient matrix \(M\). Cramer’s rule gives a common denominator dividing

\(B\,|\det M|\).

Hadamard’s inequality gives

\(1\leq|\det M|\leq d^{d/2}\).

Thus each optimization round increases the logarithm of a common denominator by at most \((d/2)\log d\). Over at most \(m\tau+1\) rounds, the increase is polynomial. All assignment coordinates lie in \([0,1]\), so their numerators also have polynomial binary length.

Exact rational linear programming, including finding an optimal vertex, therefore takes polynomial time at every round. Computing cumulative masses, testing zeros, and comparing the sets \(\mathcal A(x)\) likewise takes polynomial time.

This proves the claimed polynomial-time algorithm in the support representation.
