The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses μ_i and ν_j on male and female types, with each type specifying acceptable counterpart types, a strict order over them, and whether it is distinguished, find a mass matching x covering all distinguished female mass and minimizing \(\operatorname{BP}(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}u^M_{ij}(x)u^W_{ji}(x)\), where every female type has at most two acceptable male types and every male type has at most three acceptable female types; equivalently, decide whether \(\operatorname{BP}(x)\le B\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: male types \(M\) and female types \(W\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_i\) or \(\nu_j\). Let \(\tau=|M|+|W|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(m=|E|\) count mutually acceptable type pairs and \(L\) be the total input bit length. Masses need not sum to one or have equal totals on the two sides.
Each type strictly ranks its acceptable counterpart types, all above being unmatched. Acceptability is mutual; each female list has length at most two and each male list at most three. A subset \(W^\star\subseteq W\) is distinguished; there are no distinguished male types.
A mass matching has real entries \(x_{ij}\geq0\) on \(E\), with \(\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\) and \(\sum_{i:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\leq\nu_j\); feasibility requires equality in the latter constraint for every \(j\in W^\star\). Remaining mass is unmatched.
For \((i,j)\in E\), define \(u^M_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{k:(i,k)\in E,\ k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}\) and \(u^W_{ji}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{h:(h,j)\in E,\ h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}\), where \(\succeq\) includes equality. These are the masses unmatched or assigned to strictly worse types. Set \(\operatorname{BP}(x)=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}u^M_{ij}(x)u^W_{ji}(x)\).

Given a binary-rational threshold \(B\geq0\), is deciding whether a feasible mass matching \(x\) satisfies \(\operatorname{BP}(x)\leq B\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

The discrete problem of covering distinguished women while minimizing blocking pairs is NP-hard with female lists of length at most two and male lists of length at most three ([Mnich–Schlotter](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.08230v3)).
For fractional bipartite matchings with strict preferences, maximizing welfare or the number of fully matched agents subject to ordinal stability is polynomial-time solvable ([Chen–Roy–Sorge](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/0013.pdf)); those objectives differ from the product objective above. No result settling this exact mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Defined the previously unspecified willingness terms as unmatched mass plus mass assigned to strictly worse counterpart types, excluding mass already assigned to the proposed counterpart type.
  • Defined mass matchings using nonnegative real entries, marginal upper bounds, and full coverage of every distinguished female type; infeasible instances are rejected.
  • Used arbitrary positive rational masses without normalization or equal side totals, reflecting the discrete model's unrestricted population sizes.
  • Restricted distinguished types to women, as required by the one-sided anchor.
  • Made acceptability mutual and placed every acceptable counterpart above being unmatched.
  • Specified a nonnegative binary-rational decision threshold and defined m as the number of mutually acceptable type pairs.

1. An exact decomposition of the objective

Let $V=M\cup W$. Write $x_e=x_{ij}$ for an acceptable pair $e=(i,j)$, and define
$$ s_v(x)=\sum_{e\ni v}x_e. $$
For $e=(i,j)$, let
$$ A_e(x)=\sum_{h\succ_i j}x_{ih}, \qquad C_e(x)=\sum_{g\succ_j i}x_{gj}. $$
Thus $A_e$ and $C_e$ count assignments strictly preferred to $e$ at its male and female endpoints, respectively. The dissatisfaction quantities are
$$ u^M_e=\mu_i-x_e-A_e, \qquad u^W_e=\nu_j-x_e-C_e. $$

Expanding the objective gives
$$ \operatorname{BP}(x)=c+\ell(x)+Q(x), $$
where
$$ c=\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\mu_i\nu_j, $$
$$ \ell(x)=- \sum_{e=(i,j)\in E} \left[ \nu_j(x_e+A_e(x))+ \mu_i(x_e+C_e(x)) \right], $$
and the homogeneous quadratic part is
$$ Q(x)=\sum_{e\in E}(x_e+A_e(x))(x_e+C_e(x)). $$

Lemma 1. For arbitrary strict preference lists,
$$ Q(x)= \frac12\sum_{v\in V}s_v(x)^2+ \sum_{e\in E}A_e(x)C_e(x). $$

Proof. Expanding $Q$ yields
$$ Q(x)= \sum_e x_e^2+ \sum_e x_e(A_e+C_e)+ \sum_e A_eC_e. $$
At a fixed vertex, each unordered pair of distinct incident edges contributes exactly once to the middle sum: it contributes through the less-preferred edge, whose strict-better sum contains the more-preferred edge. Consequently,
$$ \sum_e x_e(A_e+C_e) = \sum_{v\in V} \sum_{\substack{\{e,f\}\subseteq E\\e,f\ni v,\ e\ne f}} x_ex_f. $$
Every edge has two endpoints. Therefore
$$ \frac12\sum_{v\in V}s_v^2 = \sum_e x_e^2+ \sum_{v\in V} \sum_{\substack{\{e,f\}\subseteq E\\e,f\ni v,\ e\ne f}} x_ex_f. $$
Substitution proves the identity. $\square$

These are polynomial identities, valid even for signed vectors $x$; feasibility is not needed.

2. A sum-of-squares formula under the stated list bounds

From now on, assume female lists have length at most $2$ and male lists have length at most $3$.

Define two sets of acceptable pairs:

  • $D$ consists of pairs ranked last at both endpoints.
  • $R$ consists of pairs whose male endpoint has a list of length exactly $3$ and ranks the pair second, and whose female endpoint has a list of length exactly $2$ and ranks the pair second.

For $e\in R$, let $a(e)$ be the first-choice edge at its male endpoint, and let $b(e)$ be the first-choice edge at its female endpoint.

Because every nonisolated vertex has a unique last-choice edge, distinct edges of $D$ have disjoint endpoints. Let $V(D)$ denote their endpoints.

Define
$$ Q_0(x)= \frac12\sum_{v\notin V(D)}s_v(x)^2 + \frac12\sum_{e=(i,j)\in D} \left[ (s_i(x)+s_j(x)-x_e)^2+x_e^2 \right]. $$

Theorem 2. Under the stated list bounds,
$$ \boxed{ \operatorname{BP}(x) = c+\ell(x)+Q_0(x)+ \sum_{e\in R}x_{a(e)}x_{b(e)}. } $$
In particular, $Q_0$ is positive semidefinite.

Proof. We first classify the products $A_eC_e$ in Lemma 1.

If an edge is ranked first at either endpoint, the corresponding strict-better sum is zero, so $A_eC_e=0$.

Suppose instead that both strict-better sums are nonzero. The female endpoint must have two acceptable men and rank $e$ second.

At the male endpoint there are exactly two possibilities:

  • The man ranks $e$ last. Then $e\in D$, and

$$ A_e=s_i-x_e, \qquad C_e=s_j-x_e. $$

  • The man has three acceptable women and ranks $e$ second. Then $e\in R$, and

$$ A_e=x_{a(e)}, \qquad C_e=x_{b(e)}. $$

This exhausts the possibilities. Edges in $D$ that are also first choices at an endpoint have a zero strict-better sum there, and the same formula remains valid. Hence
$$ \sum_e A_eC_e = \sum_{e=(i,j)\in D}(s_i-x_e)(s_j-x_e) + \sum_{e\in R}x_{a(e)}x_{b(e)}. $$

For every $e=(i,j)\in D$, direct expansion gives
$$ \frac12s_i^2+\frac12s_j^2+(s_i-x_e)(s_j-x_e) = \frac12(s_i+s_j-x_e)^2+\frac12x_e^2. $$
The edges in $D$ have disjoint endpoints, so these replacements use disjoint terms of $\frac12\sum_v s_v^2$. Applying them to Lemma 1 gives the claimed decomposition.

Finally, $Q_0$ is a sum of squares of linear functions, with nonnegative coefficients. Thus $Q_0(x)\geq0$ for every real vector $x$, which proves positive semidefiniteness. $\square$

3. A polynomial-time solvable case for every threshold

Corollary 3. If $R=\varnothing$, the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in $m,\tau,L$, for arbitrary positive rational masses, arbitrary $W^\star$, and every rational threshold $B\geq0$.

Every coordinate satisfies
$$ 0\leq x_{ij}\leq\min\{\mu_i,\nu_j\}, $$
so $P$ is bounded. An empty $P$ gives a no-instance.

The objective is convex because its quadratic part is positive semidefinite.

Compute the exact minimum and compare it with $B$. This handles equality at the threshold as well as strict inequality.

4. Quantifying the remaining nonconvexity

The decomposition also gives a bound on the number of negative eigenvalues.

Proposition 4. Let $k=|R|$, and let $H$ be the Hessian of $\operatorname{BP}$. Then $H$ has at most $k$ negative eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity.

Moreover, fixing the coordinates $x_{a(e)}$ for all $e\in R$ makes the objective convex in the remaining coordinates. For prescribed rational values of those coordinates, the resulting constrained decision problem is polynomial-time solvable.

Proof. Consider the linear subspace
$$ T=\{z\in\mathbb R^m:z_{a(e)}=0\text{ for every }e\in R\}. $$
Its codimension is at most $k$. On this subspace, every residual product vanishes, so Theorem 2 gives
$$ \frac12z^{\mathsf T}Hz=Q(z)=Q_0(z)\geq0. $$

If the negative eigenspace of $H$ had dimension greater than $k$, it would intersect $T$ in a nonzero vector. The quadratic form would be strictly negative on that vector, contradicting the displayed inequality.

After fixing $x_{a(e)}=\alpha_e$, every residual product becomes the linear term
$$ \alpha_e x_{b(e)}. $$
The remaining quadratic part is the restriction of the positive-semidefinite form $Q_0$. The feasible constraints remain rational and linear when the prescribed values are rational. Corollary 3’s quadratic programming argument therefore applies, with input length including the prescribed values. $\square$

This proposition does not provide a global algorithm: the optimal values of the fixed coordinates are still unknown.

Every woman’s dissatisfaction toward her second-choice man is zero. For the first-choice pairs involving $w_{ac},w_{bd},w_{cd},w_{da}$, the man ranks that woman last and is saturated, so his dissatisfaction is zero.

Only $(a,w_{ab})$ can contribute. Its two factors are
$$ u^M_{a,w_{ab}}=2-(p+q)-(1-p)=1-q, $$
$$ u^W_{w_{ab},a}=p. $$
Consequently,
$$ \operatorname{BP}(p,q)=p(1-q). $$

Along the feasible affine segment $p=q=t$, with $0\leq t\leq\frac12$,
$$ \operatorname{BP}(t,t)=t-t^2. $$
At its midpoint,
$$ \operatorname{BP}\left(\frac14,\frac14\right) =\frac{3}{16} > \frac18 = \frac12\left( \operatorname{BP}(0,0)+ \operatorname{BP}\left(\frac12,\frac12\right) \right). $$
Hence the objective is not convex on the feasible set.

This segment also supplies a direction of strictly negative curvature. Proposition 5. Every yes-instance has a rational feasible witness of polynomial encoding length.

Proof. Write
$$ q(x)=\operatorname{BP}(x) =\frac12x^{\mathsf T}Hx+h^{\mathsf T}x+c. $$
The coefficients have polynomial encoding length.

For a yes-instance, the compact feasible polytope $P$ is nonempty, so $q$ has a global minimizer $x^\ast$. Let $F$ be the smallest face of $P$ containing $x^\ast$. Then $x^\ast$ lies in the relative interior of $F$.

$$ \operatorname{aff}(F)=\{x:Cx=d\}. $$
Let the columns of $U$ form a rational basis of $\ker C$. Gaussian elimination produces such a basis with polynomial encoding length.

Every direction in $\ker C$ permits sufficiently small perturbations of both signs from $x^\ast$ within $F$. First-order optimality therefore gives
$$ U^{\mathsf T}(Hx^\ast+h)=0. $$
Define the rational polytope
$$ S=\{x\in P:Cx=d,\ U^{\mathsf T}(Hx+h)=0\}. $$
It is nonempty and bounded.

The objective is constant on $S$. Indeed, for $a,b\in S$, put $z=b-a$. Since $Cz=0$, stationarity at both points gives
$$ z^{\mathsf T}(Ha+h)=z^{\mathsf T}(Hb+h)=0. $$
Subtracting yields $z^{\mathsf T}Hz=0$. Thus
$$ q(b)-q(a) = z^{\mathsf T}(Ha+h)+\frac12z^{\mathsf T}Hz =0. $$
Every point of $S$ therefore has the global minimum value.

A nonempty bounded polytope has a vertex. At a vertex of $S$, a nonsingular subsystem of its active constraints determines all coordinates. Those constraints have rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule gives polynomial bit bounds for the coordinates: determinants of matrices of polynomial dimension and polynomial-bit entries themselves have polynomial bit length.

Hence $S$ contains a polynomial-size rational global minimizer. In a yes-instance, this point has objective at most $B$. Exact rational arithmetic verifies its feasibility and objective comparison in polynomial time. $\square$
