The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite simple graph \(H=(V,E)\), positive rational masses \(a_v\) for accepting-community types and \(b_e\) for rejecting-boundary types, with \(\sum_{v\in V}a_v+\sum_{e\in E}b_e=1\), and a budget \(k\le |V|\), choose exactly \(k\) communities to seed, one seed per chosen community, to maximize \(\Pi(S)=\sum_{v\in S}a_v-\sum_{e\in E:\,e\cap S\ne\varnothing}b_e\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(H=(V,E)\) be a finite simple undirected graph, with \(m=|V|\geq 1\). Each vertex represents an accepting community; each edge represents a rejecting boundary shared by exactly its two endpoint communities.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|V|+|E|\) types with positive masses, each encoded as a binary rational. Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Vertex masses \(a_v>0\) and edge masses \(b_e>0\) satisfy \(\sum_{v\in V}a_v+\sum_{e\in E}b_e=1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Given an integer budget \(0\leq k\leq m\), choose \(S\subseteq V\) with \(|S|=k\), placing one seed in each chosen community. Seeding reaches that community's entire accepting mass and every incident rejecting boundary; rejecting boundaries do not propagate, and each boundary's mass is charged once. Seeds and community selections are indivisible.
The objective is to maximize \(\Pi(S)=\sum_{v\in S}a_v-\sum_{e\in E:\,e\cap S\neq\varnothing}b_e\).

Is exact maximization of \(\Pi(S)\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(k\) part of the input, or is it NP-hard?

For the discrete version with unit vertex rewards and unit edge costs, maximizing payoff over exactly \(k\) selected communities is NP-hard, even on unweighted cluster graphs. The supplied result states no separate complexity classification for normalized rational-mass societies.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the budget an integer with \(0\leq k\leq |V|\); normalization and positive masses require a nonempty vertex set.
  • Made explicit that masses describe whole communities and boundaries, while seeds and community selections remain indivisible.
  • Defined \(m\) as the number of communities and \(L\) as the total binary input length.

Result

Exact maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Its rational-threshold decision version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The hardness holds even when every mass is a positive dyadic rational, so either interpretation of “binary rational” is covered.

The supplied claim about unit weights on cluster graphs is problematic under the stated objective. The proof below establishes hardness independently.

Reduction from CLIQUE

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of CLIQUE: given a finite simple graph and an integer \(k\), decide whether it contains \(k\) pairwise adjacent vertices. See [Karp’s original paper](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9). We may restrict to \(2\leq k\leq n\), where \(n\) is the number of vertices: excluded cases are directly decidable and can be mapped to fixed yes or no instances within this restriction.

Let \(G=(U,F)\) be such an instance, with \(n=|U|\) and \(q=|F|\).

Construction and normalization

Let \(M\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(M\geq n\). Construct \(H=(V,E)\) by adding \(M-n\) isolated vertices to \(G\). Thus \(m=M<2n\), and \(E=F\).

Let \(Q\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(Q\geq M+3q\), and define

\(\displaystyle c=\frac{Q-3q}{M}.\)

Since \(Q\geq M+3q\), we have \(c\geq1\). Writing \(d(v)\) for the degree of \(v\) in \(H\), assign masses

\(\displaystyle a_v=\frac{d(v)+c}{Q} =\frac{M d(v)+Q-3q}{MQ}, \qquad b_e=\frac1Q.\)

Every listed mass is strictly positive. Both denominators \(Q\) and \(MQ\) are powers of two, so every mass is dyadic.

The degree-sum identity gives \(\sum_{v\in V}d(v)=2q\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v\in V}a_v+\sum_{e\in E}b_e =\frac{2q+Mc+q}{Q} =\frac{3q+(Q-3q)}{Q} =1. \)

Thus the construction satisfies the society’s normalization exactly. Preserve the budget \(k\).

Payoff identity

For a set \(S\subseteq V\), define

\(\displaystyle E(S)=\{e\in E:e\subseteq S\},\qquad \delta(S)=\{e\in E:|e\cap S|=1\}.\)

An internal edge contributes twice to the sum of selected degrees; a crossing edge contributes once. Hence

\(\displaystyle \sum_{v\in S}d(v)=2|E(S)|+|\delta(S)|.\)

The edges charged by the objective are exactly \(E(S)\cup\delta(S)\), a disjoint union. Therefore, whenever \(|S|=k\),

\(\displaystyle \Pi(S) =\frac{\sum_{v\in S}d(v)+ck-|E(S)|-|\delta(S)|}{Q} =\frac{ck+|E(S)|}{Q}. \)

This identity uses precisely the requirement that each incident boundary is charged once.

Correctness

Set the decision threshold to

\(\displaystyle T=\frac{ck+\binom{k}{2}}{Q}.\)

A simple graph on \(k\) vertices has at most \(\binom{k}{2}\) edges, with equality exactly when those vertices form a clique. The payoff identity therefore implies

\(\displaystyle \Pi(S)\geq T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |E(S)|=\binom{k}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\text{ is a clique}. \)

Since \(k\geq2\), such a clique cannot contain an added isolated vertex. Thus

\(\displaystyle G\text{ contains a clique of size }k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \max_{|S|=k}\Pi(S)\geq T. \)

Encoding length

We have \(M<2n\), \(q\leq\binom n2\), and \(Q<2(M+3q)\). Thus all numerators and denominators above have \(O(\log n)\) binary bits, including those defining \(T\). The support contains exactly \(M+q\) types, and the construction has polynomial total encoding length.

An exact optimizer would therefore decide CLIQUE in polynomial time by computing its returned payoff and comparing it with \(T\). This proves the claimed \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

Decision-version membership

For a rational threshold \(T\), a certificate is a set \(S\) of exactly \(k\) vertices. Its size and incident edges can be checked directly, and \(\Pi(S)\) can be computed and compared with \(T\) using exact rational arithmetic.

To justify the bit complexity, take the product of all input denominators, including the threshold’s denominator, as a common denominator. Its binary length is at most the sum of their binary lengths. The resulting integer arithmetic therefore has polynomial bit complexity.

The decision version belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), and the reduction proves it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Clarification of the supplied cluster-graph claim

Normalization alone preserves any discrete instance’s optimizers: assigning \(a_v=b_e=1/\tau\) makes its payoff equal to the unit-weight payoff divided by \(\tau\).

However, the unit-weight problem on cluster graphs described here has a polynomial-time algorithm. A cluster graph is a disjoint union of cliques. In a clique of size \(t\), selecting exactly \(s\) vertices yields payoff

\(\displaystyle f_t(s)=s-\left[\binom t2-\binom{t-s}{2}\right],\qquad 0\leq s\leq t.\)

Indeed, the uncharged edges are exactly those between the \(t-s\) unselected vertices.

If the component sizes are \(t_1,\ldots,t_r\), let \(D_i(j)\) be the maximum payoff from selecting exactly \(j\) vertices in the first \(i\) components. Initialize \(D_0(0)=0\), with other states infeasible, and use

\(\displaystyle D_i(j)= \max_{0\leq s\leq\min(t_i,j)} \bigl[D_{i-1}(j-s)+f_{t_i}(s)\bigr], \)

omitting infeasible predecessor states. Every selection decomposes into component selections, and payoffs add across components, which proves the recurrence. There are polynomially many states and transitions because \(r,k,t_i\leq m\).

Consequently, the supplied cluster-graph hardness statement cannot hold for this exact model unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). This discrepancy does not affect the independent hardness proof for normalized rational-mass societies.
