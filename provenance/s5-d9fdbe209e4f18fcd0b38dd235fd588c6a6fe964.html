The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over advertiser types \(t=(v_t,B_t)\) and per-capita inventory \([0,\kappa]\) with a piecewise rational, strictly decreasing click-through profile \(\theta(q)\), determine whether there is a measurable allocation coupling advertiser mass to inventory, together with value bids, that implements the allocation under the mass-quantile version of BCSP(price). Require each inventory unit to be assigned, each type to supply at most \(\mu_t\) mass, assigned advertisers to afford their prices, and almost every assigned advertiser to weakly prefer their assignment to every affordable placement and to receiving none.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) types \(t=(v_t,B_t)\) with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Assume \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational \(v_t\geq0\), and positive rational budgets \(B_t\) distinct across types.
Inventory is Lebesgue mass on \([0,\kappa]\), with rational \(0<\kappa\leq1\). Its positive, strictly decreasing, right-continuous click-through profile \(\theta\) has \(m\) rational-function pieces, specified by rational breakpoints and densely encoded rational coefficients, with no poles on their pieces. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Represent type \(t\)'s advertisers by an interval of length \(\mu_t\), with fixed lexicographic labels. Each advertiser may submit a finite nonnegative measurable value bid; budget bids are fixed at true budgets. Sort all advertiser mass by decreasing value bid, breaking ties by labels; write \(t(r)\) and \(\beta(r)\) for type and bid at rank \(r\in[0,1]\). The mass-quantile next-bid price is \(p(r)=\lim_{s\downarrow r}\beta(s)\) for \(r<1\), and \(p(1)=0\).
An allocation is a measurable map \(Q:[0,1]\to[0,\kappa]\cup\{\bot\}\), where \(\bot\) means unassigned, inducing a coupling of types to inventory. Require \(Q\)'s assigned pushforward to equal Lebesgue inventory measure; thus each advertiser receives at most one placement and type \(t\) supplies at most \(\mu_t\) mass.
Define remaining inventory before rank \(r\) by \(R_r(A)=|A|-|\{s<r:Q(s)\in A\}|\) for measurable inventory sets \(A\). Mass-quantile BCSP(price) requires \(Q(r)\) to be the \(R_r\)-essential infimum of affordable remaining placements \(\{q:\theta(q)p(r)\leq B_{t(r)}\}\) whenever this set has positive \(R_r\)-mass, and requires \(Q(r)=\bot\) otherwise, for almost every \(r\).
Require a measurable nonnegative inventory price \(P\) satisfying \(P(Q(r))=p(r)\) and \(\theta(Q(r))p(r)\leq B_{t(r)}\) for almost every assigned rank. Utility is \(u(r)=\theta(Q(r))(v_{t(r)}-p(r))\) when assigned and \(u(r)=0\) otherwise.
Envy-freeness requires, for almost every advertiser, including unassigned ones, \(u(r)\geq\max\{0,\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{q:\theta(q)P(q)\leq B_{t(r)}}\theta(q)(v_{t(r)}-P(q))\}\), where the essential supremum uses Lebesgue inventory measure and is \(-\infty\) for an empty affordable set.

Is deciding whether such bids, allocation, and prices exist solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite advertisers with pairwise distinct budgets and distinct positive click-through rates, an envy-free assignment always exists and is realizable under BCSP(price), using truthful budget bids; this is an existence result without a polynomial running-time bound. [Díaz et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.6474v2)
No result settling the precisely defined mass-quantile decision problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized advertiser mass to one and restricted inventory mass to a positive rational at most one, carrying over the requirement that there are at least as many advertisers as slots.
  • Specified nonnegative rational values, positive rational budgets, and positive click-through rates; retained budget distinctness across support types, since distinct budgets for every individual are incompatible with positive masses of finitely many types.
  • Interpreted the unspecified piecewise rational profile as finitely many rational functions with rational breakpoints and dense coefficient encoding, without poles, and chose right-continuity at breakpoints.
  • Allowed measurable individual value bids within each type, without a no-overbidding restriction, and fixed budget bids to true budgets as in the anchor's realization.
  • Resolved bid ties using fixed advertiser labels and defined the atomless next-lower-bid price by the right limit of the decreasing bid quantile, with zero at the bottom endpoint.
  • Defined highest affordable remaining placement through the essential infimum with respect to residual inventory measure, including the rule that an advertiser remains unassigned when no positive affordable inventory mass remains.
  • Represented the allocation coupling by individually single-placement assignments on labelled atomless advertisers, permitting each type's mass to split across placements.
  • Restored envy-freeness for unassigned advertisers and interpreted comparisons over inventory essentially, so changes on null sets do not affect feasibility.

Proved progress and scope

The complexity classification for the full problem remains unresolved. I prove two additional results:

  • With the additional hypothesis that $\theta$ is continuous, the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in $m,\tau,L$, for arbitrary support size.
  • With a discontinuous profile, an envy-free allocation need not be realizable under the specified rank prices. I give an explicit two-type example where every envy-free allocation violates a necessary condition imposed by monotone rank prices.

The second result identifies a genuine obstruction to extending an algorithm for envy-free allocation feasibility to the full problem.

1. Common utilities and a finite payment representation

Let $\lambda$ denote Lebesgue inventory measure. Define
$$ a=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{q\in[0,\kappa]}\theta(q), \qquad b=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{q\in[0,\kappa]}\theta(q). $$
The hypotheses imply $0<b\leq a<\infty$.

Given an inventory price $P$, define
$$ U_t= \max\left\{ 0,\, \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{q:\,\theta(q)P(q)\leq B_t} \theta(q)(v_t-P(q)) \right\}. $$
Nonnegative prices imply $0\leq U_t\leq av_t$.

Lemma 1: common type utilities

In any envy-free outcome satisfying inventory clearing:

  • almost every advertiser of type $t$ has utility $U_t$;
  • if $U_t>0$, all mass $\mu_t$ is assigned;
  • on type $t$’s assigned mass, total payment equals

$$ \theta(Q(r))P(Q(r))=v_t\theta(Q(r))-U_t. $$

Proof. Define the assigned inventory submeasure
$$ \nu_t(A)= \left|\{r:t(r)=t,\ Q(r)\in A\}\right|. $$
Clearing gives $\sum_t\nu_t=\lambda$, hence $\nu_t\leq\lambda$. Therefore an inventory null set has a null preimage among assigned advertisers of every type.

By the essential-supremum definition, an affordable placement gives type $t$ utility at most $U_t$, except on an inventory null set. The domination $\nu_t\leq\lambda$ transfers this upper bound to almost every assigned advertiser. Envy-freeness supplies the reverse inequality.

An unassigned advertiser has utility zero, so a positive mass of unassigned advertisers of type $t$ implies $U_t=0$. The payment identity follows by rearranging the utility equality. $\square$

Write
$$ c_t(x)=v_tx-U_t. $$
These are total-payment curves, with $x$ denoting click-through rate.

Define the eligibility set
$$ E_t= \left\{ q: 0\leq c_t(\theta(q))\leq B_t,\ \text{and for every }s,\ c_t(\theta(q))>B_s \ \text{or}\ c_t(\theta(q))\geq c_s(\theta(q)) \right\}. $$
The second condition says that type $s$ either cannot afford the proposed payment or obtains utility at most $U_s$.

Lemma 1 and envy-freeness imply that $\nu_t$ is concentrated on $E_t$.

Lemma 2: eligible payment curves are unique

If $q\in E_t\cap E_s$, then
$$ c_t(\theta(q))=c_s(\theta(q)). $$

Proof. Put $x=\theta(q)$. If $c_t(x)<c_s(x)$, then
$$ c_t(x)<c_s(x)\leq B_s. $$
Thus the condition defining $E_t$ for type $s$ requires $c_t(x)\geq c_s(x)$, a contradiction. Interchanging $t$ and $s$ excludes the reverse strict inequality. $\square$

Every defining comparison for the sets $E_t$ is a sign comparison involving an affine function of $x$. The relevant equations are
$$ c_t(x)=0,\qquad c_t(x)=B_s,\qquad c_t(x)=c_s(x). $$
There are $O(\tau^2)$ nonidentically-zero affine expressions. Each has at most one root, and strict decrease of $\theta$ gives at most one inventory preimage of each root.

After adding the profile breakpoints, inventory therefore has a partition into $O(m+\tau^2)$ open intervals, apart from finitely many endpoints, on which all eligibility memberships are constant. Clearing and Lemma 2 imply that on each such interval the total-payment function
$$ D(q)=\theta(q)P(q) $$
agrees almost everywhere with one curve $c_t(\theta(q))$.

This conclusion applies without assuming continuity of $\theta$.

2. Additional structure for continuous profiles

For Sections 2–5, impose the additional hypothesis that $\theta$ is continuous on $[0,\kappa]$. Then
$$ a=\theta(0),\qquad b=\theta(\kappa), $$
and $\theta$ is a homeomorphism from $[0,\kappa]$ onto $[b,a]$.

Use click-through rate as the coordinate, and write
$$ C(x)=D(\theta^{-1}(x)). $$
By the preceding section, $C$ is almost everywhere a finite piecewise-affine function whose pieces are curves $c_t(x)$.

Lemma 3: continuity and convexity of total payments

The function $C$ has a continuous, convex representative on $[b,a]$. Its slopes are nonnegative and are values of assigned types.

Proof of continuity. Consider adjacent affine pieces meeting at $x_0$. Let their limiting payments be $A$ and $D$.

Suppose $A>D$, and let $t$ be a type whose curve supplies the piece with limit $A$. Its own budget feasibility gives $B_t\geq A$. Consequently, payments on the other piece sufficiently near $x_0$ are strictly below $B_t$, so type $t$ can afford them.

Envy-freeness there requires
$$ C(x)\geq c_t(x). $$
Taking the limit gives $D\geq A$, a contradiction. The case $D>A$ is excluded by interchanging the pieces.

Thus adjacent pieces meet continuously. Replacing values on the finitely many partition endpoints does not change any essential-supremum or almost-everywhere condition.

Proof of convexity. Suppose the lower-$x$ piece has type $s$ and the higher-$x$ piece has type $t$. Continuity gives
$$ c_s(x_0)=c_t(x_0)=C_0. $$
If $v_s>v_t$, then immediately below $x_0$,
$$ c_s(x)-c_t(x)=(v_s-v_t)(x-x_0)<0. $$
Also $v_s>0$, so $c_s(x)<C_0\leq B_t$. Type $t$ can therefore afford this lower-$x$ inventory, but would obtain utility strictly greater than $U_t$, contradicting envy-freeness.

Hence slopes are nondecreasing as $x$ increases. All slopes are values $v_t\geq0$, proving convexity and monotonicity of $C$. $\square$

On a piece belonging to type $t$,
$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{C(x)}x\right) = \frac{U_t}{x^2}\geq0. $$
Continuity at joins therefore shows that $C(x)/x$ is nondecreasing in $x$. Equivalently, inventory price is nonincreasing in $q$.

Let
$$ p_0=\frac{C(b)}b. $$
This is the price at the worst placement for the continuous representative.

Merge adjacent pieces with equal slope. Let the resulting slopes, in increasing $x$ order, be
$$ w_1<w_2<\cdots<w_G. $$
At a join $x_g$, continuity gives the utility recurrence
$$ U_{g+1}=U_g+(w_{g+1}-w_g)x_g. $$
At the bottom,
$$ U_1=b(w_1-p_0)\geq0. $$
It follows that:

  • $w_1\geq p_0$;
  • every group above the first has positive utility;
  • zero-utility assigned mass, if present, forms a bottom interval, has value $p_0$, and faces constant inventory price $p_0$.

Lemma 4: one scalar determines all positive-utility types

A type has positive utility exactly when
$$ v_t>p_0 \quad\text{and}\quad B_t>bp_0. $$

Proof. If both strict inequalities hold, continuity at $x=b$ gives a positive-length inventory interval near the worst placement on which
$$ C(x)<B_t,\qquad \frac{C(x)}x<v_t. $$
These affordable placements offer positive utility, so $U_t>0$.

Conversely, suppose $U_t>0$. Type $t$ is fully assigned. At its assigned placements,
$$ v_t-\frac{C(x)}x=\frac{U_t}{x}>0. $$
Since $C(x)/x\geq p_0$, we get $v_t>p_0$.

If $p_0=0$, the budget inequality follows from $B_t>0$. If $p_0>0$, every slope of $C$ is at least $p_0$, so
$$ C(x)>bp_0\qquad(x>b). $$
Almost every assigned placement has $x>b$, and budget feasibility gives $B_t>bp_0$. $\square$

Thus the unknown utility vector is largely determined by the single scalar $p_0$.

3. A budget-matching lemma

The following elementary matching fact will handle types with equal values.

Lemma 5: richest-first feasibility test

Consider an inventory interval $[l,h]$ of length $d=h-l$, with continuous nonincreasing total-payment function $D(q)$. Available types have capacities $\mu_i$ and budgets ordered as
$$ B_1>B_2>\cdots>B_k. $$
Put
$$ M_j=\sum_{i=1}^j\mu_i,\qquad M_0=0. $$

An affordable assignment clearing this interval exists if and only if:

  • $M_k\geq d$;
  • for every $j$ with $M_{j-1}<d$,

$$ D(l+M_{j-1})\leq B_j. $$

When these conditions hold, assign consecutive inventory blocks to types in decreasing budget order, using each capacity until inventory is exhausted.

Proof. The displayed inequalities make the proposed assignment affordable because $D$ is nonincreasing.

For necessity, suppose
$$ D(l+M_{j-1})>B_j. $$
Continuity gives an inventory prefix of length strictly greater than $M_{j-1}$ on which payment exceeds $B_j$. Only types with budgets strictly greater than $B_j$ can serve that prefix, but their total capacity is $M_{j-1}$. This contradicts clearing. The total-capacity condition is immediate. $\square$

If total available capacity equals $d$, every type is fully assigned. If it exceeds $d$, only a prefix of the budget order is needed, with at most one partially assigned type.

4. Exact feasibility test for a proposed bottom price

Fix a candidate $p\geq0$. Define
$$ A(p)=\{t:v_t>p,\ B_t>bp\}, \qquad Z(p)=\{t:v_t=p\}. $$
By Lemma 4, $A(p)$ must be precisely the positive-utility types.

Put
$$ M(p)=\sum_{t\in A(p)}\mu_t, \qquad \rho=\kappa-M(p). $$
If $\rho<0$, the candidate is infeasible.

If $\rho>0$, the remaining inventory must be supplied by zero-utility types in $Z(p)$. If $\rho=0$, no zero-utility assigned mass is needed.

Let
$$ h=\kappa-\rho=M(p). $$
The bottom interval $[h,\kappa]$, when nonempty, has price $p$, hence total payment
$$ D(q)=p\theta(q). $$

Group the types in $A(p)$ by their common value, and order the distinct group values as
$$ w_1<\cdots<w_G. $$
Write $M_g$ for group $g$’s total mass. Define
$$ q_0=h,\qquad q_g=h-\sum_{j=1}^gM_j,\qquad x_g=\theta(q_g). $$
Then $q_G=0$.

The group with value $w_g$ must occupy $[q_g,q_{g-1}]$. Starting with
$$ C_0=px_0, $$
define recursively
$$ C_g=C_{g-1}+w_g(x_g-x_{g-1}). $$
On that group’s click-through interval, set
$$ C(x)=C_{g-1}+w_g(x-x_{g-1}). $$
Its utility is
$$ U_g=w_gx_{g-1}-C_{g-1}. $$
The first utility is
$$ U_1=(w_1-p)x_0>0, $$
and subsequent utilities are positive by the recurrence from Section 2.

The candidate passes the test precisely when:

  • Lemma 5 succeeds on each positive-utility group’s inventory interval, using that group’s types and their full capacities;
  • if $\rho>0$, Lemma 5 succeeds on $[h,\kappa]$, using types in $Z(p)$ and their capacities.

Necessity

Sections 1–2 show that assigned value groups occur in this order, each positive-utility type is fully assigned, and zero-utility assignment can occur only at the bottom with value $p$.

The group masses therefore determine all interval boundaries. Continuity determines $C$ through the displayed recurrence. Budget matching within each group is then exactly the problem addressed by Lemma 5.

Sufficiency for envy-free allocation

Suppose the test succeeds. Use the richest-first assignments from Lemma 5 and the constructed inventory price
$$ P(q)=\frac{C(\theta(q))}{\theta(q)}. $$
On the bottom plateau this equals $p$.

The constructed function $C$ is continuous and convex, with slopes in increasing order. For every positive-utility group, its affine segment
$$ c_t(x)=v_tx-U_t $$
is a supporting line:
$$ C(x)\geq c_t(x)\qquad(b\leq x\leq a). $$
Indeed, to the right of its segment all subsequent slopes are at least $v_t$, and to the left all preceding slopes are at most $v_t$. Integrating these slope inequalities proves the displayed bound.

Consequently, every inventory placement gives type $t$ utility at most $U_t$, even before restricting to affordable placements. Its assigned placements give exactly $U_t$.

All other types have zero utility:

  • If $v_t\leq p$, then $P(q)\geq p$ makes every offered utility nonpositive.
  • If $v_t>p$ and $t\notin A(p)$, then $B_t\leq bp$. Here $p>0$, because budgets are positive. Since $C(x)>bp$ for every $x>b$, this type has no positive-measure affordable inventory.

Lemma 5 supplies budget feasibility, and the assignments clear inventory. Thus this constructs an envy-free allocation.

5. BCSP realization and polynomial running time

Tied bids on the bottom plateau

A remaining issue is that fixed labels, rather than budgets, order advertisers whose bid is $p$.

For this purpose, consider any inventory set with a common inventory price $p$. Every advertiser’s affordable subset is a suffix in quality order because affordability is
$$ p\theta(q)\leq B_t. $$

Greedy matching fact. If such inventory has an affordable clearing assignment under capacity upper bounds, processing advertisers in their fixed order and giving each the best affordable remaining placement also clears it.

Here is an exchange proof for one type block. Let the current type have capacity $\mu$. Greedy takes the earliest affordable inventory set $A$ of mass
$$ \delta=\min\{\mu,\text{remaining affordable inventory mass}\}. $$
In an existing deterministic clearing assignment, let this type receive a set $B$ of mass $s\leq\mu$. Since $B$ is affordable, $s\leq\delta$.

Every point of $B\setminus A$ lies after every point of $A\setminus B$, apart from null endpoints. Moreover,
$$ |B\setminus A|\leq |A\setminus B|. $$
Move an equal mass of advertisers displaced from $A\setminus B$ to $B\setminus A$, using a measure-preserving quantile matching. These advertisers receive worse placements, so their suffix affordability constraints remain satisfied. Any remaining displaced mass becomes unassigned, which is permitted by capacity upper bounds.

The current type now receives $A$, and the remaining types still admit a clearing assignment of the residual inventory. Applying this exchange successively proves the claim. Quantile allocation within each type interval gives a measurable continuum construction.

Realizing a successful candidate

Allocate positive-utility groups according to the preceding construction, and give an advertiser assigned to $q<h$ the bid
$$ \beta=P(q). $$
On this positive-utility region, $P$ is continuous and strictly decreasing in $q$: on every affine payment segment,
$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{C(x)}x\right)=\frac{U_t}{x^2}>0. $$
Thus these advertisers appear first, in inventory order. Their rank is $r=q$, and their next-bid price is $p(r)=P(r)$.

At each such rank, the placement $q=r$ is affordable. Because $\theta$ decreases and the current price is nonnegative, every remaining placement after $r$ is also affordable at that current price. Therefore its remaining-inventory essential infimum is $r$, as required by BCSP.

If $\rho>0$, give every advertiser in $Z(p)$ bid $p$, including those ultimately unassigned. They follow the positive-utility advertisers. Process their labels greedily on $[h,\kappa]$. The greedy matching fact proves that this clears the plateau, and its construction satisfies the BCSP essential-infimum rule. The next-bid price throughout this tied block is $p$.

Give all other advertisers bid zero. They occur after inventory has cleared. If $p=0$, all types outside $A(p)$ have value zero and belong to $Z(p)$, so the same tied-block construction covers them.

When $\rho=0$, give all advertisers outside $A(p)$ bid zero; inventory has already cleared before they are processed.

All exceptional block boundaries are null sets. This proves BCSP realization of every successful candidate.

Enumerating candidates exactly

Define
$$ \alpha_t=\min\left\{v_t,\frac{B_t}{b}\right\}. $$
Then
$$ A(p)=\{t:\alpha_t>p\}. $$

Partition the nonnegative $p$-axis using the candidate points
$$ \{0\}\cup\{\alpha_t:t\}\cup\{v_t:t\}. $$
There are $O(\tau)$ points and intervening open intervals.

At each candidate point, apply the exact test of Section 4.

Within an open interval:

  • $A(p)$ is constant;
  • $Z(p)$ is empty;
  • feasibility therefore requires

$$ \sum_{t\in A(p)}\mu_t=\kappa. $$

If this equality holds, all inventory boundaries are fixed rational numbers. The total payment at any budget-test boundary has the form
$$ bp+d, $$
where $d$ is rational and independent of $p$ within the interval. Hence every budget condition is a rational linear inequality
$$ bp+d\leq B_t. $$
Feasibility is exactly nonemptiness of the open candidate interval intersected with these finitely many closed upper bounds. This is decidable by rational comparisons.

The interval above all candidate points has $A(p)=Z(p)=\varnothing$ and cannot clear positive inventory.

Every inventory boundary used by the algorithm is a rational sum or difference of $\kappa$ and input masses. Evaluating a densely encoded rational function at such a point has polynomial bit complexity. The construction uses only polynomially many rational operations; in particular, it never needs to compute an inverse-profile algebraic root.

There are $O(\tau)$ candidate regions, with $O(\tau)$ budget checks and profile evaluations per region. Thus the running time is
$$ (m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}. $$
Continuity itself can also be checked in polynomial time by comparing rational one-sided values at the finitely many profile breakpoints.

This completes the polynomial-time result under the additional continuity hypothesis.

6. A necessary condition imposed by monotone rank prices

The following obstruction applies to the original problem, including discontinuous profiles.

Lemma 6: disjoint inventory sets cannot share overlapping nonatomic price distributions

Let $A$ and $B$ be disjoint measurable inventory sets. In any feasible outcome, if the price distributions
$$ P_\#(\lambda|_A) \quad\text{and}\quad P_\#(\lambda|_B) $$
are nonatomic, then they are mutually singular.

Here $P_\#(\lambda|_A)$ denotes the pushforward of inventory measure on $A$ under $P$.

Proof. Sorted bids imply that the next-bid price $p(r)$ is nonincreasing.

Let
$$ R_A=\{r:Q(r)\in A\},\qquad R_B=\{r:Q(r)\in B\}. $$
Choose disjoint Borel versions of these rank sets, changing them only on null sets. Clearing and price consistency give
$$ P_\#(\lambda|_A)=p_\#(\lambda|_{R_A}), \qquad P_\#(\lambda|_B)=p_\#(\lambda|_{R_B}). $$

Let $D$ be the set of price levels whose preimage under $p$ has positive rank measure. There are at most countably many such levels.

Outside $D$, each attained price has a unique rank preimage: if two distinct ranks had the same price, monotonicity would make $p$ constant on the interval between them.

Define the measurable generalized inverse
$$ \varrho(y)=\inf\{r:p(r)\leq y\}. $$
Whenever $p(r)=y\notin D$, we have $\varrho(y)=r$.

Consequently, outside $D$, the first pushforward is supported on
$$ S_A=\{y\notin D:\varrho(y)\in R_A\}, $$
and the second on the disjoint set
$$ S_B=\{y\notin D:\varrho(y)\in R_B\}. $$
Their assumed nonatomicity gives zero measure to the countable set $D$. Thus they are mutually singular. $\square$

In particular, two disjoint inventory regions cannot both have price distributions with strictly positive densities on the same open price interval.

7. A discontinuous two-type counterexample to relaxed realizability

Take
$$ \kappa=1,\qquad \mu_H=\mu_L=\frac12, $$
with types
$$ (v_H,B_H)=(2,6),\qquad (v_L,B_L)=(3,4). $$
Their budgets are distinct.

Use the two-piece profile
$$ \theta(q)= \begin{cases} 5-2q,&0\leq q<\frac12,\\ 3-2q,&\frac12\leq q\leq1. \end{cases} $$
It is positive, strictly decreasing, right-continuous, and has no poles. The better half has click-through rates in $(4,5]$, and the worse half has rates in $[1,2]$.

An envy-free allocation exists when bid order and BCSP are omitted

Assign $H$ to the better half and $L$ to the worse half. Set
$$ P(q)= \begin{cases} 2-\dfrac4{\theta(q)},&q<\frac12,\\ 3-\dfrac2{\theta(q)},&q\geq\frac12. \end{cases} $$
Assigned utilities are
$$ U_H=4,\qquad U_L=2. $$

On the better half, total payments are $2\theta(q)-4\in(4,6]$, so $H$ can afford them and $L$ cannot.

On the worse half, total payments are $3\theta(q)-2\in[1,4]$. Type $H$’s utility there is
$$ 2\theta(q)-(3\theta(q)-2)=2-\theta(q)\leq1<4. $$
Thus the allocation is envy-free and budget feasible.

Every envy-free allocation has a forced form

Consider any envy-free outcome, even before imposing bid order. Since inventory and advertiser mass both equal one, all advertisers are assigned almost everywhere.

Suppose $L$ supplies positive mass to the better half. At those placements, payment is at most $4$, so they are affordable to $H$, and give $H$ utility
$$ 2\theta(q)-\theta(q)P(q) \geq2\theta(q)-4>4. $$
Envy-freeness forces $U_H>4$.

Nonnegative prices on $H$’s assigned placements then imply
$$ 2\theta(q)\geq U_H>4. $$
Hence $H$ can receive only better-half inventory. Its full mass $\frac12$ exhausts that half, contradicting the assumed positive mass of $L$ there.

Therefore $H$ supplies the entire better half and $L$ the entire worse half.

Payment feasibility now implies
$$ 4\leq U_H\leq8,\qquad 2\leq U_L\leq3. $$
If $U_H>4$, better-half placements with click-through rates sufficiently close to $4$ have payment
$$ 2\theta(q)-U_H<4. $$
They become affordable to $L$ and offer utility
$$ 3\theta(q)-(2\theta(q)-U_H) =\theta(q)+U_H>8, $$
contradicting $U_L\leq3$. Consequently,
$$ U_H=4,\qquad 2\leq U_L\leq3. $$

Thus every envy-free outcome has prices
$$ P_H(q)=2-\frac4{\theta(q)}, \qquad P_L(q)=3-\frac{U_L}{\theta(q)} $$
on the respective halves.

Monotone rank prices are impossible

Let
$$ I=\left(1,\frac65\right). $$
The better-half price range contains $I$. For every $U_L\in[2,3]$, the worse-half price range also contains $I$, because its endpoints are
$$ 3-U_L\leq1, \qquad 3-\frac{U_L}{2}\geq\frac32>\frac65. $$

The price distributions on both halves are nonatomic and have positive densities on $I$. Explicitly, solving for inventory position gives
$$ q_H(y)=\frac12\left(5-\frac4{2-y}\right), \qquad q_L(y)=\frac12\left(3-\frac{U_L}{3-y}\right). $$
Their density magnitudes are
$$ |q_H'(y)|=\frac2{(2-y)^2}>0, \qquad |q_L'(y)|=\frac{U_L}{2(3-y)^2}>0 \qquad(y\in I). $$
These two price distributions therefore cannot be mutually singular. Lemma 6 gives a contradiction.

Hence this instance has an envy-free allocation in the relaxation, but no feasible outcome under the problem’s monotone rank prices, and therefore no feasible BCSP outcome.
