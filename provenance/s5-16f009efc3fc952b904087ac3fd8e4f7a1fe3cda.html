Proven conclusion and remaining question

Let \(\#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{dot}}\) and \(\#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{Euc}}\) denote the two stable-table counting problems in the question. I prove
\[ \#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{dot}}\equiv_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{BIS} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{Euc}}\equiv_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{BIS}. \]

Consequently, either stable-table problem admits the requested FPRAS if and only if \(\#\mathrm{BIS}\) admits an FPRAS. The proof handles binary-encoded \(q\) without expanding the population into individuals.

This is a complete conditional classification. It does not settle the unconditional existence of an FPRAS.

1. Lower bound: singleton types preserve the count

Take a labelled strict-preference instance with \(n\) men and \(n\) women in either geometric model. Give every individual its own type and set
\[ q=n,\qquad \mu_x=\nu_y=\frac1n. \]
The masses have binary rational encodings of polynomial length, and every row and column sum is one.

Thus feasible tables are permutation matrices, in bijection with labelled perfect matchings. For such a table, the two positive-sum conditions defining a blocking type pair say exactly that the corresponding individuals prefer one another to their assigned partners. Stability therefore agrees under this bijection.

Using the labelled-model AP reductions stipulated in the question gives
\[ \#\mathrm{BIS}\leq_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{dot}}, \qquad \#\mathrm{BIS}\leq_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{Euc}}. \]
The supplied results give these lower bounds already in dimensions three and two, respectively. Appending zero coordinates preserves all rankings. [Chebolu–Goldberg–Martin, Theorems 1 and 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1004.1836v4)

The reverse reduction requires handling multiplicities and their binary encoding.

2. Stable tables are integer stable allocations

Write
\[ A_x=q\mu_x,\qquad B_y=q\nu_y. \]
These are positive integers, with \(\sum_x A_x=\sum_y B_y=q\).

Construct the complete bipartite graph on \(X\cup Y\). Give vertex \(x\) quota \(A_x\), vertex \(y\) quota \(B_y\), and every edge capacity \(q+1\). Use the given strict type rankings as orders on incident edges.

An integer allocation assigns nonnegative integers to edges, respecting edge capacities and vertex quotas. In the ordinary stable-allocation model, an unsaturated edge blocks when both endpoints would accept an additional unit, either because their quotas are unfilled or because they hold a positive amount on a worse edge.

Every stable allocation fills every quota. Indeed, suppose its total allocation is \(T<q\). Since both sides have total quota \(q\), there are vertices \(x\) and \(y\) with unfilled quotas. Their edge has allocation at most \(T<q+1\), so both would accept another unit: it blocks. Hence a stable allocation has total \(q\), which forces every vertex quota to be filled.

For an allocation filling every quota, edge capacity never prevents an increase: every entry is at most \(q<q+1\). An endpoint would accept another unit on an edge precisely when it has a positive allocation on a worse incident edge. Therefore its blocking condition is exactly the table condition in the question.

We have consequently established an identity of counting objects:
\[ N=\#\{\text{integer stable allocations of this instance}\}. \]
No labelled-matching weights enter this identity.

The rankings can be computed in polynomial time. Dot products are rational; Euclidean rankings can be compared using rational squared distances, without extracting square roots.

3. A compact weighted-poset representation

I use the following known theorem, stated in its ordinary stable-allocation specialization.

Representation theorem. For integer stable allocations on a finite bipartite graph with integer capacities, integer quotas, and strict incident-edge orders, one can construct a weighted poset \((R,\prec,w)\) in time polynomial in the graph size and the binary numerical input length. It has
\[ |R|=O(|V||E|^2) \]
and positive integer weights \(w_r\leq b_{\max}\), where \(b_{\max}\) is the largest edge capacity. Integer stable allocations are in bijection with functions
\[ \xi:R\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_{\geq0},\qquad 0\leq\xi(r)\leq w_r, \]
satisfying
\[ r\prec s,\ \xi(s)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \xi(r)=w_r. \]
Such functions are called closed.

This follows from the linear-order specialization of Karzanov’s Theorems 4.3, 5.2 and 6.7. The additional gapless hypothesis used there holds for choices generated by strict linear orders. [Karzanov, *On one generalization of stable allocations in a two-sided market*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.19978)

Here all hypotheses hold: the graph is finite, simple and connected; the rankings are strict; and capacities and quotas are positive integers. The choice operation needed by the algorithm is explicit: scan incident edges in preference order and retain offered units until the quota is reached. It is computable in polynomial bit time.

For our instance,
\[ |V|=\tau,\qquad |E|=|X||Y|\leq m^2,\qquad b_{\max}=q+1. \]
Thus the weighted poset has polynomial size, and its weights have polynomial binary length.

It remains to eliminate these weights without making \(w_r\) copies.

4. Compressing a binary weight into a small poset

An ideal of a poset is a subset containing every predecessor of each of its elements. Write \(d(P)\) for its number of ideals.

Lemma. For every integer \(c\geq1\), a poset \(H_c\) with exactly \(c\) ideals can be constructed using at most \(2\lfloor\log_2 c\rfloor\) elements.

Proof. The empty poset gives \(H_1\). Two operations suffice:

  • Adding an isolated element doubles the ideal count.
  • Adding a new element above every existing element increases the ideal count by one: the old ideals remain, and the only ideal containing the new element is the whole enlarged poset.

Recursively construct \(H_{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}\), add an isolated element, and, when \(c\) is odd, add a new greatest element. The resulting ideal count is \(c\). Each recursion halves the argument and adds at most two elements, proving the size bound. \(\square\)

For every \(r\in R\), take a separate copy of \(H_{w_r+1}\). Construct a poset \(P\) by additionally placing every element of the gadget for \(r\) below every element of the gadget for \(s\) whenever \(r\prec s\).

An ideal \(J\) of \(P\) restricts to an ideal in each gadget. Its restriction to the gadget for \(s\) can be nonempty only if its restriction to every predecessor gadget is full.

Each gadget has exactly:

  • one empty ideal;
  • one full ideal;
  • \(w_r-1\) other ideals.

Choose any bijection between its ideals and \(\{0,\ldots,w_r\}\), sending the empty ideal to zero and the full ideal to \(w_r\). Under these bijections, the cross-gadget ideal condition becomes precisely the closed-function condition. Conversely, any closed function determines gadget ideals whose union is an ideal of \(P\).

Hence
\[ d(P)=N. \]
The bijections need not be computed; their existence proves the counting identity.

Moreover,
\[ |P|\leq 2\sum_{r\in R}\lfloor\log_2(w_r+1)\rfloor =O(|R|\log(q+2)). \]
Both the gadgets and all order relations can therefore be constructed in polynomial time in the original binary input length.

5. An explicit AP reduction from ideals to \(\#\mathrm{BIS}\)

For completeness, the required reduction from counting ideals to \(\#\mathrm{BIS}\) can be proved directly.

Let \(P\) have \(h\geq1\) elements, and let \(D=d(P)\). For each element \(i\), create a left block \(L_i\) and a right block \(R_i\), each containing \(t\) vertices. Add all edges between \(L_i\) and \(R_i\). Whenever \(i\prec j\), also add all edges between \(L_j\) and \(R_i\).

Put
\[ A=2^t-1. \]
In an independent set, each pair of blocks has one of three states: a nonempty subset of the left block, a nonempty subset of the right block, or neither. Each nonempty-block state has exactly \(A\) realizations.

Consider independent sets with no empty state. The indices whose left blocks are used form an ideal: using \(L_j\) and \(R_i\) is forbidden whenever \(i\prec j\). Conversely, every ideal determines a permissible choice of left or right state at every index. These independent sets contribute exactly
\[ D A^h. \]

There are at most \(3^h\) state patterns having an empty state. Each contributes at most \(A^{h-1}\). If \(Z\) is the total number of independent sets, then
\[ D A^h\leq Z\leq D A^h+3^h A^{h-1}. \]

Choose \(t\) so that
\[ A\geq\frac{4\cdot3^h}{\epsilon}. \]
This requires only \(t=O(h+\log(\epsilon^{-1}))\). Since \(D\geq1\),
\[ D\leq\frac{Z}{A^h}\leq D\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right). \]

Use a \(\#\mathrm{BIS}\) approximation oracle with relative tolerance \(\delta=\epsilon/4\), obtaining \(\widehat Z\), and return \(\widehat Z/A^h\). On the oracle’s success event, the returned value is at least \(D(1-\epsilon/4)\), and at most
\[ D\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\right)^2 \leq D(1+\epsilon), \]
where the final inequality uses \(0<\epsilon<1\).

The graph has \(2ht\) vertices and polynomially many edges. All scaling numbers have polynomial binary length. This is an AP reduction, establishing
\[ \#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{dot}}\leq_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{BIS}, \qquad \#\mathrm{ST}_{\mathrm{Euc}}\leq_{\mathrm{AP}}\#\mathrm{BIS}. \]

The unresolved point is the absolute existence of a \(\#\mathrm{BIS}\) FPRAS. The reductions prove neither its existence nor its nonexistence.
