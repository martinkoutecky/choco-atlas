Proposition C.3 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of complete applicant types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), type preferences and school priorities, and per-capita capacities \(q_c\) and reserve quotas \(q_c^R\), compute the MR and JSA mass assignments by type. For instances satisfying high competitiveness, \(\sum_{t\in T_m}x^{MR}_{t,c}\le q_c^R\) for every school \(c\), decide whether for every disadvantaged type and every preference cutoff the mass assigned to schools at least that preferred under JSA is at least the corresponding mass under MR.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). There are \(m\ge1\) schools; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type specifies disadvantaged or advantaged status and a strict ranking of its acceptable schools above being unassigned, \(\varnothing\); schools have arbitrary strict priority orders over types. Write \(T_m\) for disadvantaged types. Capacities and reserves are binary rationals satisfying \(0\le q_c^R\le q_c\le1\), and \(q_c^G=q_c-q_c^R\).
Mass is divisible; applicants of the same type are interchangeable, and preferences depend only on school, not seat category. For a nonnegative offered-mass vector \(a\), let \(H_c(a,b)\) greedily retain up to mass \(b\) in school \(c\)'s type-priority order, splitting the boundary type; let \(a_m\) restrict \(a\) to \(T_m\), and \(|a|=\sum_t a_t\).
MR's choice from \(a\) is \(r+H_c(a-r,q_c-|r|)\), where \(r=H_c(a_m,q_c^R)\).
JSA's choice from \(a\) is \(g+r+H_c(a-g-r,q_c-|g+r|)\), where \(g=H_c(a,q_c^G)\) and \(r=H_c((a-g)_m,q_c^R)\). Thus unused reserved capacity is released to all remaining applicants.
Define \(x^{MR}\) and \(x^{JSA}\) by synchronous applicant-proposing mass deferred acceptance: initially all mass proposes to its first acceptable school; each school applies its choice to held mass plus new proposals, and rejected portions propose to their next acceptable school. Use the limiting held masses if infinitely many rounds occur; exhausted portions remain unassigned.
The input promises high competitiveness: \(\sum_{t\in T_m}x^{MR}_{t,c}\le q_c^R\) for every school \(c\). Compute both assignments and decide whether \(\sum_{d\succeq_t c}x^{JSA}_{t,d}\ge\sum_{d\succeq_t c}x^{MR}_{t,d}\) for every \(t\in T_m\) and acceptable-school cutoff \(c\).
Is this promised dominance decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with exact MR and JSA assignments computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is the decision problem NP-hard?
Discrete JSA equals applicant-proposing deferred acceptance after splitting each school into general and reserved copies, prioritizing disadvantaged applicants at reserved copies and placing each school's general copy immediately before its reserved copy in applicant preferences; discrete JSA also dominates MR under high competitiveness. [Faenza, Gupta, and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00544v2).
A continuum model with random school priorities characterizes stable assignments under minority reserves, but does not settle this finite-support MR–JSA decision problem. [Escobar and Huerta](https://www.dii.uchile.cl/~jescobar/school-choice-segregation-2024-july.pdf). No exact continuous complexity classification was located.

Result. The problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\): both specified assignments are exactly computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). This computation works even without high competitiveness. Under the promise, every requested dominance inequality holds, so the promised decision answer is always yes.

The substantive computational step is to use a polynomial stable-allocation algorithm on a graph whose applicant vertices are the supported types. Expanding rational masses into individual applicants proves correctness but is unnecessary for computation.

1. Rational masses give an exact finite discrete correspondence

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, capacities, and reserves. Then

\(N_t=D\mu_t,\qquad Q_c=Dq_c,\qquad R_c=Dq_c^R,\qquad G_c=Q_c-R_c\)

are nonnegative integers, and \(\sum_tN_t=D\). Moreover, \(\log D=O(L)\).

For a correctness argument, replace each type \(t\) by \(N_t\) individual applicants. Every such applicant has type \(t\)'s status and acceptable-school ranking. At each school, replace every type in its priority order by its applicants in a fixed strict order. Use the same order within each type at every school.

We claim that synchronous discrete DA in this expanded instance, aggregated by type and divided by \(D\), reproduces the mass DA in the question, round by round.

Initially, proposal counts agree. Suppose held masses and new proposals agree at the beginning of a round. Greedy selection with an integer capacity selects whole individual applicants; its selected count from each type is exactly \(D\) times the corresponding mass selection. This applies successively to every selection stage defining MR or JSA. Consequently, held and rejected counts agree after the round. Rejected applicants have their type's next acceptable school, so the next proposals also agree. This proves the claim by induction.

Each individual applicant proposes to each acceptable school at most once. There are at most \(Dm\) individual proposals. Thus both mass processes terminate after finitely many rounds on these rational inputs: the limiting convention introduces no additional outcome here.

Write \(M^{MR}\) and \(M^{JSA}\) for the resulting discrete matchings. For either mechanism \(K\),

\(x^K_{t,c}=\dfrac{|\{s:\ s\text{ has type }t,\ M^K(s)=c\}|}{D}.\)

This correspondence is exact, although explicitly constructing the \(D\) applicants can take exponential time.

2. The school-copy representations

For each school \(c\), create two copies:

  • \(c^G\), with capacity \(G_c\) and the original type-priority order;
  • \(c^R\), with capacity \(R_c\), placing every disadvantaged type before every advantaged type and preserving the original order within each group.

Both copies accept all types that find \(c\) acceptable. In particular, \(c^R\) can admit advantaged applicants when disadvantaged demand is insufficient.

For a type whose school ranking is \(c_1\succ_t\cdots\succ_t c_k\), use these copy rankings:

\(c_1^R\succ_t c_1^G\succ_t c_2^R\succ_t c_2^G\succ_t\cdots\succ_t c_k^R\succ_t c_k^G\quad\text{for MR},\)

\(c_1^G\succ_t c_1^R\succ_t c_2^G\succ_t c_2^R\succ_t\cdots\succ_t c_k^G\succ_t c_k^R\quad\text{for JSA}.\)

The finite discrete correspondence is established in Propositions C.1 and C.3 of [Faenza, Gupta, and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.00544v2): applicant-proposing DA on these copy instances, projected back to schools, gives MR and JSA, respectively.

To check explicitly that these representations implement the release rule in this question, consider an integer offered vector \(A\). Here \(H_c\) denotes greedy selection in the original priority order.

For MR, let \(r=H_c(A_m,R_c)\). If disadvantaged offered mass is at least \(R_c\), the reserved copy selects precisely \(r\), and the general copy selects \(H_c(A-r,G_c)\). Their union is MR's choice because \(|r|=R_c\).

If disadvantaged offered mass is less than \(R_c\), the reserved copy selects all of it, then selects the highest-priority remaining advantaged applicants up to its unused capacity. The general copy continues in the same priority order. Together these two selections retain

\(r+H_c(A-r,(R_c-|r|)+G_c)=r+H_c(A-r,Q_c-|r|),\)

again exactly MR's choice.

For JSA, the general copy first selects \(g=H_c(A,G_c)\). If \(|g|<G_c\), then \(g=A\), so nothing remains. Otherwise \(|g|=G_c\), and the reserved copy selects

\(r=H_c((A-g)_m,R_c)\)

followed by \(H_c(A-g-r,R_c-|r|)\). Since \(R_c-|r|=Q_c-|g+r|\), this is precisely the specified JSA choice.

The discrete copy results apply to our expanded instance: preferences and priorities are strict, capacities are integral, acceptable lists may be incomplete, and applicants care only about schools before introducing the stated adjacent-copy refinements. Zero-capacity copies can simply be deleted.

3. The polynomial algorithm operates on types

We use the following established stable-allocation result.

For a finite bipartite graph with strict preference orders, rational vertex quotas, and rational edge capacities, the allocation produced by left-proposing mass DA can be computed in strongly polynomially many arithmetic operations. An implementation without dynamic trees uses \(O(en)\) operations, where \(e\) and \(n\) are the graph's edge and vertex counts. It uses bottleneck minima, additions, subtractions, and comparisons, and preserves integrality for integral data. Crucially, it computes the proposing side's DA outcome, rather than merely some stable allocation. See Lemmas 1 and 5 and Section 3 of [Dean and Munshi, *Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems*](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf).

For clarity, an allocation is feasible when its edge values are nonnegative, satisfy the edge capacities, and have incident sums at most the vertex quotas. An unsaturated edge blocks when each endpoint can increase its allocation on that edge using unused quota or by reducing allocation on a less-preferred edge. This is the ordinary responsive-choice mass model.

Construct one compressed copy instance for each mechanism:

  • The left vertices are the \(\tau\) supported types, with quotas \(N_t\).
  • The right vertices are the school copies, with quotas \(G_c\) and \(R_c\).
  • Connect \(t\) to both copies of every acceptable school.
  • Use the preferences and priorities from Section 2.
  • Give an edge from \(t\) to a copy \(j\) upper capacity \(\min(N_t,Q_j)\).

These edge bounds impose no additional restriction: the endpoint quotas already imply them.

We now justify why this compressed problem has the required outcome. Ordinary mass DA on its responsive school copies can be expanded into the individual applicants from Section 1. At each copy, greedy selection produces exactly the corresponding aggregate type counts. Conversely, a batch of individual proposals can be processed one applicant at a time, postponing further proposals by rejected applicants until the batch is complete. Responsive selection retains the highest-priority applicants among those received, so the resulting batch totals agree.

Thus the compressed copy DA is an aggregation of an execution of ordinary individual applicant-proposing DA. Proposal-order independence for ordinary DA identifies its terminal matching with the applicant-optimal matching of the expanded copy instance. Section 2 then identifies its school projection with \(M^{MR}\) or \(M^{JSA}\).

Therefore, if the stable-allocation algorithm returns integer copy allocations \(Y^K\), the required answer is

\(x^K_{t,c}=\dfrac{Y^K_{t,c^G}+Y^K_{t,c^R}}{D}.\)

This establishes correctness for the exact synchronous assignments specified in the question.

4. Graph size and bit complexity

Each compressed instance has at most

\(n=\tau+2m,\qquad e=2m\tau.\)

The stable-allocation algorithm consequently needs polynomially many arithmetic operations; the \(O(en)\) implementation gives \(O(m\tau(\tau+m))\), apart from routine preprocessing.

There is no requirement that total applicant quota equal total school quota. If an implementation requires equality, add a dummy applicant vertex with quota equal to total school-copy capacity and a dummy school vertex with quota \(D\). Connect each real vertex to the opposite dummy, ranking that dummy last, and connect the two dummies.

Indeed, if a real allocation has total value \(s\), allocate each real vertex's unused quota to its opposite dummy and allocate \(s\) between the dummies. This fills all quotas. These dummy edges cannot block: a real vertex ranks its dummy last, and each dummy ranks the opposite dummy last. Hence the construction represents unused capacity and unassigned mass faithfully. It adds only \(O(\tau+m)\) edges.

All initial integer quantities have \(O(L+\log m)\) bits. The total real school capacity is at most \(mD\), and the dummy construction has total quota \(O((m+1)D)\). Using explicit augmenting paths, all allocation and residual quantities stay within these bounds. Bottleneck minima and additions or subtractions therefore operate on integers of polynomial bit length. The polynomial arithmetic-operation bound is consequently also a polynomial bit-time bound.

Finally, every output entry is an integer divided by \(D\), with polynomial encoding length. For each disadvantaged type, traverse its school ranking and compare the two running prefix sums. This checks all requested inequalities using \(O(m\tau)\) additional arithmetic operations.

Thus exact assignment computation and dominance testing are polynomial-time solvable even without the promise.

5. Under high competitiveness, dominance always holds

The promise and Section 1 imply, at every school,

\(|M^{MR}(c)\cap S^m|=D\sum_{t\in T_m}x^{MR}_{t,c}\le Dq_c^R=R_c,\)

where \(S^m\) is the set of disadvantaged individual applicants in the expanded instance.

This is exactly the discrete high-competitiveness condition. Theorem 4.2 of [Faenza, Gupta, and Zhang](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.00544v2) therefore gives

\(M^{JSA}(s)\succeq_s M^{MR}(s)\)

for every disadvantaged individual applicant \(s\).

Fix a disadvantaged type \(t\) and an acceptable-school cutoff \(c\). Every applicant of that type has the same school ranking. Consequently, applicant-by-applicant dominance implies that the number assigned to a school at least as good as \(c\) under JSA is at least the corresponding number under MR. Dividing by \(D\) gives

\(\sum_{d\succeq_t c}x^{JSA}_{t,d}\ge\sum_{d\succeq_t c}x^{MR}_{t,d}.\)

This proves every inequality in the question and completes the classification.
