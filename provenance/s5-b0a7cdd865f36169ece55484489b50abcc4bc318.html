The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite candidates \(Q\cup S\), a candidate-arrival order, a current candidate, an addition budget \(k\), a chair order \(\sigma\), a distinguished candidate \(d\), and a rational mass distribution over partial ranking types of the already revealed candidates, determine whether the chair has a nonanticipating policy for adding at most \(k\) spoiler candidates such that, for every consistent future refinement of each partial mass into complete ranking types, the final plurality winner set contains some \(a\in Q\cup S\) with \(a\succeq_\sigma d\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given disjoint finite sets of qualified candidates \(Q\) and spoilers \(S\), let \(C=Q\cup S\) and \(m=|C|\). The input specifies an arrival permutation of \(C\), a current candidate \(c\), a binary-encoded budget \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\), a strict chair order \(\sigma\) on \(C\), and \(d\in C\).
The snapshot records the irrevocable inclusion decisions for all earlier spoilers; at most \(k\) spoilers may be included in total, counting these past decisions. Every qualified candidate must be included.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here each type is a strict linear ranking of all candidates revealed through \(c\), including rejected spoilers; its mass is positive and the masses sum to \(1\). These rankings are partial only because future candidates are absent. The quantity \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\).
At each subsequent arrival, an adversary may split each existing type's mass into arbitrary nonnegative real submasses over rankings obtained by inserting the arriving candidate, preserving the previous ranking and total mass. The chair observes the entire refined distribution before deciding whether to include that candidate if it is a spoiler; rejection is permanent. Rankings are revealed even for candidates that are rejected.
A deterministic nonanticipating policy depends only on the observed history. At the end, restrict rankings to included candidates and use plurality with scores measured by mass; every candidate tied for the maximum is a winner, and an empty candidate set has no winners. The chair succeeds if some winner \(a\) satisfies \(a\succeq_\sigma d\).

Is deciding whether such a policy guarantees success against every consistent sequence of mass refinements solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For finite, explicitly listed voters, the corresponding candidate-sequential constructive plurality control problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), with the same observation timing and nonunique-winner objective ([source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1202.6649v2)). No result settling exactly the mass-refinement problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted partial ranking types as strict linear rankings of the entire revealed prefix, including the current candidate and rejected spoilers, rather than arbitrary partial orders.
  • Normalized the rational input masses to sum to one, as specified by a distribution.
  • Allowed arbitrary real mass splitting at future arrivals, with each refinement preserving its parent ranking and mass.
  • Restored the snapshot's past spoiler decisions and treated k as the total addition budget, including past additions.
  • Specified that all qualified candidates enter automatically and that spoiler decisions occur after observing the new rankings.
  • Specified a deterministic policy observing the full distribution history, with rankings retained even for rejected spoilers.
  • Retained all plurality co-winners and defined an empty final candidate set to have no winners.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable: it belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

The finite-voter result appears as Theorem 4.2 in [Hemaspaandra, Hemaspaandra, and Rothe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.6649). The proof below establishes an exact criterion for the stated mass-refinement model, including arbitrary real refinements, budget accounting, and empty candidate sets.

1. Reduce to a state after deciding on the current candidate

Let \(H=\{a\in C:a\succeq_\sigma d\}\). Call candidates in \(H\) good, and all other candidates bad.

Let \(p\) be the number of previously included spoilers. If \(p>k\), there is no legal policy. Otherwise, consider each legal decision concerning the current candidate \(c\):

  • If \(c\in Q\), include it.
  • If \(c\in S\), consider rejection and, when \(p<k\), inclusion.

There are at most two cases. Fix one such decision, and let:

  • \(I\) be the set of included candidates revealed through \(c\);
  • \(\ell\) be the remaining spoiler budget;
  • \(F_Q\) and \(F_S\) be the qualified candidates and spoilers arriving strictly after \(c\);
  • \(r=|F_Q|\).

When \(I\ne\varnothing\), let \(s(a)\) be candidate \(a\)'s plurality score in the observed distribution restricted to \(I\). Thus
\(\sum_{a\in I}s(a)=1\).

Define
\[ \]
\(A=\max\bigl(\{s(a):a\in I\cap H\}\cup\{0\}\bigr)\),
\(B=\max\bigl(\{s(b):b\in I\setminus H\}\cup\{0\}\bigr)\), and
\(E=\sum_{a\in I\cap H}\max\{s(a)-B,0\}\).

We will prove the following complete characterization.

Post-decision criterion.

  • If \(F_Q\setminus H\ne\varnothing\), guaranteed success is impossible.
  • Otherwise, if \(I=\varnothing\), guaranteed success is possible exactly when

\(r>0\) or \(\ell\geq1\) and \(F_S\cap H\ne\varnothing\).

  • Otherwise, if \(r=0\), guaranteed success is possible exactly when \(A\geq B\).
  • Otherwise, if \(r\geq1\), guaranteed success is possible exactly when

\(E\geq rB\).

The original instance is positive exactly when at least one legal decision on \(c\) satisfies this criterion.

2. Consistency of the adversarial constructions

We will construct adversarial continuations by specifying a finite distribution over complete rankings.

Realization lemma. Any finite distribution over complete rankings whose restriction to the revealed candidates equals the snapshot distribution can be realized by legal sequential mass refinements.

Proof. At each arrival, group the complete rankings by their restrictions to the candidates revealed so far. Assign each restricted ranking the sum of the masses in its group.

When the next candidate arrives, each group splits into groups distinguished by that candidate's insertion position. These submasses are nonnegative and sum to the group's previous mass. Removing the arriving candidate restores the previous ranking. Hence every step is a permitted refinement. This construction also reveals rankings for rejected spoilers, as required. \(\square\)

Consequently, a fixed complete distribution that defeats every possible future inclusion decision suffices to defeat every deterministic nonanticipating policy.

3. Future bad qualified candidates and empty states

A future bad qualified candidate forces failure

Suppose \(b\in F_Q\setminus H\). On every initial type, insert \(b\) at the top when it arrives, and insert every other future candidate at the bottom when it arrives.

At the end, \(b\) ranks above every other candidate on all mass. It must be included because it is qualified. Its score is \(1\), and every other included candidate has score \(0\), regardless of the chair's spoiler decisions.

Thus \(b\) is the unique winner and is bad. This proves criterion 1.

Henceforth assume \(F_Q\subseteq H\).

An empty included set

Suppose \(I=\varnothing\).

If \(r>0\), the chair can reject every future spoiler. The final candidate set is then the nonempty set \(F_Q\), consisting entirely of good candidates, so success is certain.

If \(\ell\geq1\) and a good future spoiler exists, the chair can include the first such spoiler and reject all other future spoilers. Again, the final included set is nonempty and consists entirely of good candidates.

Conversely, if neither condition holds, no good candidate can legally enter the election: there are no future qualified candidates, and either no good future spoiler exists or the remaining budget is zero. Therefore the final set is empty or contains only bad candidates. Success is impossible.

This proves criterion 2.

4. Nonempty states with no future qualified candidates

Suppose \(I\ne\varnothing\) and \(r=0\).

If \(A\geq B\), rejecting every future spoiler preserves the election on \(I\). Since its scores sum to \(1\), this inequality ensures that a good candidate is among its winners.

If \(A<B\), the adversary inserts every future spoiler at the bottom of every ranking. Each such spoiler lies below every member of \(I\), so it receives score \(0\), even if included. All scores on \(I\) remain unchanged. A bad candidate with score \(B>0\) therefore strictly outscores every good candidate.

This defeats every future policy and proves criterion 3.

5. The exact mass-splitting threshold

Assume \(I\ne\varnothing\), \(r\geq1\), and \(F_Q\subseteq H\). Write
\(G=\sum_{a\in I\cap H}s(a)\).

Sufficiency of \(E\geq rB\)

Use the policy that rejects every future spoiler. The final included set is \(I\cup F_Q\).

Two facts hold under every consistent refinement.

First, each old candidate's score can only decrease. Its relative position against every other member of \(I\) remains fixed, while newly included candidates may take its votes.

Second, all mass initially voting for an old good candidate still votes for a good candidate at the end. On such mass, no old bad candidate can overtake the old good candidate. Every newly included candidate belongs to \(F_Q\) and is good. Hence the final total good score is at least \(G\).

If \(B=0\), every final bad score is zero. Since the final scores sum to \(1\), some good candidate wins.

Now suppose \(B>0\), and assume for contradiction that no good candidate wins. Some bad candidate then has score \(\beta\leq B\), strictly exceeding every good score.

For each old good candidate \(a\), its final score is at most \(s(a)\) and is strictly less than \(B\). In particular, it is at most \(\min\{s(a),B\}\). Each of the \(r\) new qualified candidates has score strictly less than \(B\).

Because \(r\geq1\), the final total good score is therefore strictly less than

\(\sum_{a\in I\cap H}\min\{s(a),B\}+rB=G-E+rB\).

If \(E\geq rB\), this quantity is at most \(G\), contradicting the lower bound \(G\) on the final total good score.

Thus the stated policy guarantees success.

Necessity: a refinement defeating every policy when \(E<rB\)

Suppose \(E<rB\). Necessarily \(B>0\). Choose an old bad candidate \(b\) with score \(B\).

Let \(h=|I\cap H|\), and define

\(\Delta=rB-E>0,\qquad \varepsilon=\dfrac{\Delta}{2(h+r)},\qquad t=B-\varepsilon.\)

Since \(\Delta\leq rB\), we have \(0<\varepsilon\leq B/2\), so \(0<t<B\).

For each old good candidate \(a\), define its retained mass and transferred mass by

\(x_a=\min\{s(a),t\},\qquad z_a=s(a)-x_a=\max\{s(a)-t,0\}.\)

Let \(Z=\sum_{a\in I\cap H}z_a\). For every real number \(u\),

\(\max\{u-(B-\varepsilon),0\} \leq\max\{u-B,0\}+\varepsilon.\)

Applying this inequality to the old good scores gives

\(Z\leq E+h\varepsilon.\)

Moreover,

\(E+h\varepsilon =r(B-\varepsilon)-\bigl(\Delta-(h+r)\varepsilon\bigr) =rt-\Delta/2<rt.\)

Consequently, \(Z/r<t<B\).

We now construct a complete ranking distribution with these scores:

  • Every old bad candidate keeps its entire current score.
  • Each old good candidate \(a\) ends with score \(x_a<B\).
  • Each of the \(r\) future qualified candidates ends with score \(Z/r<B\).
  • Every future spoiler has score zero, whether included or rejected.

Here is the construction at the level of the given types. For an initial type whose top candidate in \(I\) is an old good candidate \(a\), we have \(s(a)>0\). Split that type's mass into:

  • a proportion \(x_a/s(a)\), assigned to retain its old top candidate;
  • for each \(q\in F_Q\), a proportion \(z_a/(r\,s(a))\), assigned to vote for \(q\).

These proportions are nonnegative and sum to

\(\dfrac{x_a}{s(a)}+ r\dfrac{z_a}{r\,s(a)}=1.\)

For a portion assigned to \(q\), insert \(q\) at the top when it arrives and insert every other future candidate at the bottom. For retained portions, and for every initial type topped by an old bad candidate, insert all future candidates at the bottom.

Every resulting ranking extends its initial ranking. The realization lemma makes this a legal refinement sequence.

Since \(I\ne\varnothing\), every future spoiler lies below an included old candidate and receives zero score under every chair policy. The designated bad candidate \(b\) retains score \(B\). Every good candidate has score strictly below \(B\).

Thus no good candidate wins, regardless of future spoiler decisions. This proves necessity and completes criterion 4.

In particular, equality \(E=rB\) guarantees success. Strict inequality is required for the adversary to make every good score strictly smaller than a bad score.

6. Algorithm, running time, and conclusion

For each legal current decision, compute \(I\) and \(\ell\), check for a future bad qualified candidate, and apply the appropriate criterion. In a nonempty state, compute scores by scanning each of the \(\tau\) listed rankings until its highest included candidate is found. Each ranking has length at most \(m\).

All arithmetic can be exact. Write the input masses as \(p_i/q_i\), and use the common denominator
\(D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i\).
Its binary length is at most the sum of the input denominator lengths, hence is \(O(L)\).

Represent scores by their integer numerators over \(D\). If \(U_a=Ds(a)\) and \(V=DB\), the threshold test becomes

\(\sum_{a\in I\cap H}\max\{U_a-V,0\}\geq rV.\)

Thus no real-number computation or enumeration of future rankings is needed. Using elementary integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for the entire algorithm is

\(O\bigl((m\tau+m+\tau+1)L^2\bigr)\)

bit operations. There is no dependence on the numerical value of \(k\); its binary length, \(O(\log(k+1))\), is already included in \(L\).

Every affirmative case supplies a deterministic nonanticipating policy. Every negative case supplies a consistent refinement defeating all such policies. Therefore the characterization settles the stated decision problem.

Because the distribution through the current candidate is already observed, the chair can choose between these cases using exactly the information allowed by the task. Consequently, accepting the original instance when at least one legal post-decision case permits guaranteed success has the correct quantifier order.

  • Realization lemma and policy quantifiers

The realization lemma is valid. Grouping complete rankings by their restrictions gives precisely the observed mass on each currently revealed ranking. On the next arrival, the finer groups differ only in the insertion position of that candidate, and their masses sum to the preceding group’s mass.

Thus each step preserves previous rankings and total mass. Zero submasses are permitted, and arbitrary real submasses are explicitly allowed.

Rejected candidates remain in the revealed rankings throughout this construction. Their exclusion affects only the final election restriction.

The subsequent use of the lemma is also logically sound: a fixed legal continuation that defeats every possible future inclusion choice defeats every deterministic nonanticipating policy. The negative constructions establish this stronger property, so they do not require an additional argument about adaptive adversaries.

  • A future bad qualified candidate

The construction in Section 3 works regardless of the position of the designated bad qualified candidate in the remaining arrival order.

Other future candidates inserted earlier are below the previously revealed candidates; inserting the designated candidate at the top places it above all of them. Candidates inserted afterward go to the bottom. The designated candidate therefore ranks first on every unit of mass and is necessarily included.

Its score is \(1\), while every other included candidate has score \(0\). It is the unique bad winner. Criterion 1 is proved.

  • Empty included states

With no future bad qualified candidate, all future qualified candidates are good. If at least one exists, rejecting every future spoiler leaves a nonempty election containing only good candidates.

If no future qualified candidate exists but a good future spoiler is affordable, including that spoiler and rejecting the others also produces a nonempty election containing only good candidates.

Conversely, when neither condition holds, no good candidate can enter legally. An empty final set has no winners, and a final set containing only bad candidates cannot satisfy the objective.

This covers zero remaining budget, absence of qualified candidates, and the empty-election convention. Criterion 2 is proved.

  • Nonempty states with no future qualified candidates

Rejecting every future spoiler preserves all scores on \(I\).

The sentinel zeros in the definitions of \(A\) and \(B\) cause no false positive. Since \(I\) is nonempty and its scores sum to \(1\), if there is no included good candidate then \(A=0<B\). Thus \(A\geq B\) really does imply that some good candidate is a winner.

When \(A<B\), inserting all future spoilers at the bottom preserves the existing scores even if spoilers are included: each spoiler remains below an included old candidate on every ranking. A bad candidate with score \(B\) strictly exceeds every good score.

Criterion 3 is proved.

  • Sufficiency of the mass threshold

Both invariants used in Section 5 are valid under the policy rejecting all future spoilers:

  • An old candidate can only lose plurality mass, since the order among old included candidates is preserved.
  • Mass originally voting for an old good candidate can finish voting only for that candidate or for a newly included good qualified candidate. Hence the total final good score is at least \(G\).

The case \(B=0\) is handled correctly. All old bad scores remain zero, and there are no newly included bad candidates. Since the final included set is nonempty and total score is \(1\), a good candidate wins.

For \(B>0\), failure means that some maximum-scoring bad candidate has score \(\beta\leq B\), and every good candidate has score strictly below \(\beta\). Each old good score is consequently at most \(\min\{s(a),B\}\), and each new qualified score is strictly below \(B\).

Since \(r\geq1\), summing produces the strict upper bound stated in the proof. The identity \(G-E=\sum_{a\in I\cap H}\min\{s(a),B\}\) is correct. Under \(E\geq rB\), the resulting upper bound contradicts the lower bound \(G\).

The strictness is sufficient even when \(E=rB\). No unsupported limiting argument is used.

  • Necessity of the mass threshold

All parameter bounds and calculations check out:

  • From \(E<rB\) and \(E\geq0\), one obtains \(B>0\) and \(0<\Delta\leq rB\).

  • Therefore \(0<t<B\).
  • The positive-part inequality holds for every real \(u\).
  • The displayed calculation \(E+h\varepsilon=rt-\Delta/2<rt\) is exact.

Thus the assigned future qualified score \(Z/r\) is strictly below \(B\).

The type-level partition is well-defined: whenever an initial positive-mass type has old good candidate \(a\) as its highest included candidate, \(s(a)>0\). The proportions are nonnegative and sum to \(1\). No division by a zero score occurs.

In transferred portions, placing the assigned qualified candidate at the top and every other future candidate at the bottom produces the claimed final vote. In retained portions, bottom insertion preserves the old highest included candidate.

Previously rejected candidates may occur above that old included candidate, but remain excluded. Future spoilers are below an included old candidate on every portion, so including them cannot change any final score.

Consequently, the designated bad candidate retains score \(B\), while every included good candidate has score strictly below \(B\), for every future spoiler decision. Criterion 4 is proved.
