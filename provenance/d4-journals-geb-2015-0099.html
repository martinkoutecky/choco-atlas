The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a population scale \(N\), budget \(B\), and finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over verified-bit, linear-cost types \((b,v)\), choose a submeasure \(\xi\le\mu\) with \(\xi_{(b,v)}=r(v)\mu_{(b,v)}\) for a common selection rate \(r(v)\in[0,1]\). Maximize selected mass \(q=\|\xi\|\) subject to \(qv_q/(1-q)\le B\) and \(q\le 1-1/N\), where \(v_q\) is the cutoff in the marginal valuation distribution and partial selection at a tie is allowed. Return the threshold allocation and payment, with per-selected-person privacy level \(\epsilon=1/[N(1-q)]\) and payment \(v_q/[N(1-q)]\), and the estimate \(\hat{s}=\int b\,d\xi+(1-q)/2+\operatorname{Lap}(1-q)\) of the normalized statistic \(\int b\,d\mu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is a probability distribution \(\mu\) on types \((b,v)\), where \(b\in\{0,1\}\) is verified and \(v\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq0}\) gives privacy cost \(c(v,\epsilon)=v\epsilon\); using valuations incurs no privacy cost. Its input is the list of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types with binary rational masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; the bit-domain size is \(m=2\).
Additional inputs are a binary-encoded integer population scale \(N\geq2\) and a binary rational budget \(B\geq0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; masses need not be multiples of \(1/N\).
For \(q>0\), define \(v_q=\min\{t:\mu(v\leq t)\geq q\}\). A threshold allocation is \(\xi_{(b,v)}=r(v)\mu_{(b,v)}\), with \(r(v)=1\) below \(v_q\), \(r(v)=0\) above it, and a common fractional rate at \(v_q\) chosen so that \(\|\xi\|=q\), independently of \(b\).
Maximize \(q\) subject to \(0\leq q\leq1-1/N\) and \(qv_q/(1-q)\leq B\). At \(q=0\), set \(\xi=0\), \(v_0=0\), and expenditure to zero.
Return the optimal threshold allocation and its uniform per-selected-person privacy level \(\epsilon=1/[N(1-q)]\) and payment \(p=v_q/[N(1-q)]\); unselected mass receives zero privacy and payment. Total expenditure is \(Nqp\).
The associated normalized estimate has law \(\hat{s}=\int b\,d\xi+(1-q)/2+\operatorname{Lap}(1-q)\), estimating \(\int b\,d\mu\); \(\operatorname{Lap}\) denotes centered Laplace noise with the stated scale. Return its location and scale.

Is this exact continuous threshold-allocation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(N\) encoded in binary, or is it NP-hard?

For finite lists of agents, FairQuery is a polynomial-time, truthful, individually rational, budget-feasible algorithm attaining instancewise optimal accuracy among truthful, individually rational, envy-free mechanisms that purchase the same positive privacy level from every selected agent. [Ghosh and Roth](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.1375)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made the distribution a probability measure, specified verified binary bits and nonnegative binary rational linear-cost valuations, and retained the insensitive-value privacy model.
  • Specified N as a binary-encoded integer at least two and B as a nonnegative binary rational; imposed no integrality restriction on population masses.
  • Defined the cutoff by the lower marginal quantile, including at cumulative-mass endpoints, and required full selection below it with the same fractional rate for both bit types at the cutoff.
  • Defined the zero-selection allocation, cutoff, payments, and expenditure so that the problem includes instances where no positive selection is feasible.
  • Interpreted the randomized estimate output as its location and Laplace scale, avoiding an undefined requirement to output an exact real-valued random sample.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Theorem. The stated continuous threshold-allocation problem is exactly solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(N\) encoded in binary. All requested outputs are rational numbers with polynomial encoding length.

The proof below follows directly from the finite-support formulation. The [Ghosh–Roth paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.1375) supplies the finite-agent context; no extension of its mechanism-design theorem is needed here.

Proof: reduction to finitely many intervals. Write the distinct supported valuations in increasing order as \(a_1<\cdots<a_k\), where \(k\leq\tau\). Define their aggregate masses and cumulative masses by

\(w_j=\sum_{(b,v):\,v=a_j}\mu_{(b,v)},\qquad C_0=0,\qquad C_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}w_i.\)

Every \(w_j\) is positive, and \(C_k=1\). Consequently, for every \(q\in(0,1]\),

\(v_q=a_j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_{j-1}<q\leq C_j.\)

Indeed, throughout this interval the cumulative mass below \(a_j\) is insufficient to reach \(q\), whereas the cumulative mass through \(a_j\) reaches \(q\). In particular, \(q=C_j\) belongs to the interval with threshold \(a_j\), not the next interval.

Set \(h=1-1/N\). Since \(N\geq2\), we have \(0<h<1\). On the interval with threshold \(a_j\), the budget condition is

\(\frac{q a_j}{1-q}\leq B.\)

The denominator is positive for \(q\leq h\), so this condition is equivalent to

\(q(a_j+B)\leq B.\)

Define

\(\beta_j=\begin{cases}1,&a_j=0,\\[2pt]\dfrac{B}{B+a_j},&a_j>0.\end{cases}\)

If \(a_j=0\), expenditure is zero, so the budget imposes no additional restriction. If \(a_j>0\), the denominator \(B+a_j\) is strictly positive, and the budget condition is exactly \(q\leq\beta_j\). This includes \(B=0\), in which case \(\beta_j=0\).

Thus the feasible masses in the \(j\)-th interval are precisely

\((C_{j-1},t_j]\quad\text{if }t_j>C_{j-1},\qquad t_j=\min\{C_j,h,\beta_j\},\)

and there are no feasible masses in that interval if \(t_j\leq C_{j-1}\).

Exact algorithm and optimality. Sort and aggregate the input as above. Compute every \(t_j\), retaining it only when \(t_j>C_{j-1}\). Return

\(q^\star=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{t_j:1\leq j\leq k,\ t_j>C_{j-1}\}\bigr).\)

Each retained candidate is feasible: it lies in its stated valuation interval and satisfies both the population cap and the budget bound. Zero is feasible by the given convention.

Conversely, let \(q>0\) be any feasible mass. There is a unique \(j\) such that \(C_{j-1}<q\leq C_j\). Feasibility gives \(q\leq h\) and \(q\leq\beta_j\), hence \(q\leq t_j\). Since \(q>C_{j-1}\), this candidate was retained. Therefore \(q\leq q^\star\).

This proves that the returned mass is feasible and dominates every feasible mass. It also proves that a maximum exists despite the jumps in the threshold valuation.

For example, take masses \(1/2,1/2\) at valuations \(1,3\), with \(N=4\) and \(B=2\). The first interval contributes \(t_1=1/2\). The second has \(\beta_2=2/5< C_1\), so it contributes nothing. Thus \(q^\star=1/2\), with expenditure \(1<B\). This illustrates why an algorithm must allow an optimum at a cumulative-mass endpoint with unused budget.

Construction of every requested output. If \(q^\star=0\), return

\(\xi=0,\qquad \epsilon=\frac1N,\qquad p=0,\qquad \text{location}=\frac12,\qquad \text{scale}=1.\)

Here \(\epsilon\) is the requested formula; no mass is selected.

If \(q^\star>0\), find the unique index \(j^\star\) satisfying

\(C_{j^\star-1}<q^\star\leq C_{j^\star}.\)

Set

\(\alpha=\frac{q^\star-C_{j^\star-1}}{w_{j^\star}}.\)

The interval inequalities imply \(0<\alpha\leq1\). Define

\(r(v)=\begin{cases} 1,&v<a_{j^\star},\\ \alpha,&v=a_{j^\star},\\ 0,&v>a_{j^\star}, \end{cases} \qquad \xi_{(b,v)}=r(v)\mu_{(b,v)}.\)

This rate depends only on \(v\), so tied valuations receive the required common fractional rate independently of \(b\). Moreover,

\(\|\xi\|=C_{j^\star-1}+\alpha w_{j^\star}=q^\star.\)

The selected mass receives the uniform levels

\(\epsilon=\frac{1}{N(1-q^\star)},\qquad p=\frac{a_{j^\star}}{N(1-q^\star)}.\)

Unselected mass receives zero privacy and payment. Total expenditure is

\(Nq^\star p=\frac{q^\star a_{j^\star}}{1-q^\star}\leq B,\)

where the inequality follows from the established feasibility of \(q^\star\).

Finally, return the location and scale

\(\ell=\sum_{(b,v)}b\,\xi_{(b,v)}+\frac{1-q^\star}{2}, \qquad s=1-q^\star.\)

Then the specified estimate has exactly the law \(\ell+\operatorname{Lap}(s)\). Since \(q^\star\leq1-1/N\), its scale satisfies \(s\geq1/N>0\).

Bit complexity. All computations can use exact integer and rational arithmetic. The following bounds establish polynomial time in the binary input length, without expanding the population into \(N\) agents.

Write each input mass as \(n_i/d_i\), with positive denominator, and let

\(D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}d_i.\)

The binary length of \(D\) is \(O(L)\), because the sum of the input denominator lengths is at most \(L\). Each mass can therefore be written as \(A_i/D\). Positivity and normalization give \(0<A_i\leq D\) and \(\sum_i A_i=D\). Group masses and cumulative masses consequently have denominator \(D\) and numerators of \(O(L)\) bits.

If \(B=x/y\) and \(a_j=u/z>0\), then

\(\beta_j=\frac{xz}{xz+yu}.\)

Its numerator and denominator have \(O(L)\) bits. The cap \(h=(N-1)/N\) also has \(O(L)\) bits. Every candidate \(t_j\), and hence \(q^\star\), is one of these bounds or a cumulative mass. Write \(q^\star=Q/R\), with numerator and denominator of \(O(L)\) bits.

To check the allocation sizes explicitly, suppose \(q^\star>0\), and write

\(C_{j^\star-1}=M/D,\qquad w_{j^\star}=W/D.\)

Then

\(\alpha=\frac{QD-MR}{RW}.\)

For a type at the threshold, its allocated mass is

\(\xi_i=\frac{A_i(QD-MR)}{DRW}.\)

Every integer in this expression has \(O(L)\) bits. Allocations below and above the threshold likewise have \(O(L)\)-bit representations. All allocations admit the common denominator \(DRW\), so summing the selected bit-one mass does not cause uncontrolled denominator growth. The location, scale, privacy level, and payment also have \(O(L)\)-bit rational representations.

Sorting uses \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) rational comparisons. Aggregation, candidate generation, maximization, and output construction use polynomially many arithmetic operations on integers of polynomial bit length. Integer addition, multiplication, division, and exact comparison all have polynomial bit complexity. Therefore the complete algorithm runs in polynomial time in \(\tau,L\), and hence in \(m,\tau,L\) for the stipulated \(m=2\).

The use of binary-encoded \(N\) requires only arithmetic on its binary representation; there is no iteration proportional to \(N\). This completes the proof.
