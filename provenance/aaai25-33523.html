The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many complete agent types with masses summing to 2 and finitely many complete room types with masses summing to 1, binary type-to-type compatibility and type-to-room valuations, compute or approximate the maximum utilitarian welfare achievable by assigning room mass to unordered pairs of agent types subject to all mass-balance constraints.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given finite resident types \(T\) and room types \(R\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) and supplies \(\lambda_r\ge0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_{r\in R}\lambda_r=\tfrac12\), let \(v_t(t')\in\{0,1\}\) be symmetric and let \(\widehat v_t(r)\in\{0,1\}\). Set \(u_t(t',r)=\min\{v_t(t'),\widehat v_t(r)\}\).

An allocation has variables \(x_{\{t,t'\},r}\ge0\). It satisfies \(\sum_r\left(2x_{\{t,t\},r}+\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{\{t,t'\},r}\right)=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{\{t,t'\}}x_{\{t,t'\},r}=\lambda_r\) for every \(r\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{r,t}2x_{\{t,t\},r}u_t(t,r)+\sum_{r,\{t,t'\}:t\ne t'}x_{\{t,t'\},r}\bigl(u_t(t',r)+u_{t'}(t,r)\bigr)\).

Is deciding whether an allocation of welfare at least \(K\) exists, equivalently maximizing this welfare, in \(\mathrm{P}\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite individual agents, maximum-welfare roommate-and-room matching under binary symmetric Leontief utilities is APX-hard (and hence NP-hard).

Complexity of the Mass-Allocation Problem

Theorem

Under the standard binary encoding of the rational numbers \(\mu_t\), \(\lambda_r\), and \(K\), the welfare-threshold problem is polynomial-time solvable. Consequently, maximum welfare can also be computed in polynomial time by rational linear programming.

Proof

Let \(n=|T|\) and \(m=|R|\). For every unordered pair \(\{t,t'\}\), including the case \(t=t'\), and every \(r\in R\), introduce the variable \(x_{\{t,t'\},r}\).

There are exactly

\[ m\binom{n+1}{2} \]

variables, which is polynomial in the explicit input size.

For each variable, define its welfare coefficient by

\[ c_{\{t,t\},r}=2u_t(t,r) \]

and, when \(t\neq t'\), by

\[ c_{\{t,t'\},r} = u_t(t',r)+u_{t'}(t,r). \]

Because

\[ u_t(t',r)=\min\{v_t(t'),\widehat v_t(r)\}, \]

every coefficient \(c_{\{t,t'\},r}\) belongs to \(\{0,1,2\}\) and is computable directly from the input.

The existence of an allocation of welfare at least \(K\) is therefore exactly the feasibility of the following rational linear system:

\[ x_{\{t,t'\},r}\geq 0 \]

for all \(\{t,t'\}\) and \(r\),

\[ \sum_{r\in R} \left( 2x_{\{t,t\},r} + \sum_{t'\neq t}x_{\{t,t'\},r} \right) = \mu_t \]

for every \(t\in T\),

\[ \sum_{\{t,t'\}}x_{\{t,t'\},r} = \lambda_r \]

for every \(r\in R\), and

\[ \sum_{r\in R}\sum_{\{t,t'\}}c_{\{t,t'\},r}x_{\{t,t'\},r} \geq K. \]

This system has \(n+m\) equality constraints, one welfare inequality, and \(m\binom{n+1}{2}\) nonnegativity constraints. Its coefficients belong to \(\{0,1,2\}\), apart from the rational right-hand sides supplied in the input. Thus its encoding length is polynomial in the encoding length of the original instance.

Feasibility of a rational linear program is decidable in polynomial time in the Turing bit model. Hence the welfare-threshold problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

Similarly, maximum welfare is the rational linear program

\[ \max \sum_{r\in R}\sum_{\{t,t'\}} c_{\{t,t'\},r}x_{\{t,t'\},r} \]

subject to the resident-mass and room-supply constraints above. Rational linear programming computes an optimal solution and its exact rational objective value in polynomial time.

Feasibility and attainment

The feasible region is always nonempty under the stated mass-balance assumptions. Indeed, set

\[ x_{\{t,t\},r}=\mu_t\lambda_r \]

and set all cross-type variables to zero. Then, for every \(t\),

\[ \sum_{r\in R}2x_{\{t,t\},r} = 2\mu_t\sum_{r\in R}\lambda_r = \mu_t, \]

while, for every \(r\),

\[ \sum_{t\in T}x_{\{t,t\},r} = \lambda_r\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t = \lambda_r. \]

Moreover, every variable satisfies \(0\leq x_{\{t,t'\},r}\leq\lambda_r\). Thus the feasible region is compact, and the linear welfare objective attains its maximum.

Why the finite-agent hardness does not transfer

The finite-agent problem imposes indivisible matching decisions. In the mass formulation, every resident type can be divided among arbitrarily many roommate-type and room-type combinations. The variables are genuinely continuous, and no integrality or support restriction is imposed.

Accordingly, the displayed formulation is a fractional relaxation of the corresponding finite matching problem. APX-hardness of the indivisible problem does not imply hardness of this continuous linear program.

Therefore the stated mass-allocation problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). It could be NP-hard under polynomial-time reductions only if \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
