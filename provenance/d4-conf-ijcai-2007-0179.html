The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(|C|=2^k\), a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings over \(C\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with a strict majority for one candidate in every pair, and a target \(A\), decide whether there is a balanced binary voting tree with one leaf per candidate in which \(A\) wins under the majority relation induced by \(\mu\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a candidate set with \(m=|C|=2^k\), where \(k\) is a nonnegative integer, and let \(A\in C\).
A society is given by its support \(S\): the list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau=|S|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
For distinct candidates, define \(x\succ_\mu y\) iff \(\sum_{t\in S:\,x\succ_t y}\mu_t>1/2\). Promise that exactly one direction holds for every pair.
A voting tree has exactly two children at each internal node and exactly one leaf per candidate. It is balanced when its maximum and minimum leaf depths differ by at most \(1\). Each internal node returns the majority winner between its children's returned candidates; the root returns the overall winner.

Is deciding whether some balanced voting tree returns \(A\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The discrete weighted-majority-graph formulation was asserted to be \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete by [Lang et al.](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/07/Papers/221.pdf).
For deterministic tournament-graph input with a power-of-two number of players, balanced tournament fixing is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete ([Aziz et al.](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/ko_journal.pdf)); this result uses graph input rather than a rational-support society.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete rankings as strict total orders, matching the discrete preference domain.
  • Specified balance by the difference between maximum and minimum leaf depths, and specified recursive majority evaluation.
  • Allowed nonnegative integer k, including the single-candidate case, because the discrete definitions include order zero.
  • Defined L as total encoded input bit length and required A to belong to C.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when every mass is a dyadic rational and the support size satisfies \(\tau\leq m(m-1)+2\).

The essential step is a polynomial-size construction representing any tournament as the strict majority relation of such a society.

1. Balanced trees with a power-of-two number of leaves

We first verify that the question’s definition of balance yields precisely the usual complete knockout bracket.

Let \(d\) be the minimum leaf depth, with the root at depth \(0\). Every leaf has depth \(d\) or \(d+1\). Because every internal node has exactly two children and there are no leaves before depth \(d\), there are exactly \(2^d\) nodes at depth \(d\).

Let \(a\) of those nodes be leaves. By the definition of minimum depth, \(1\leq a\leq 2^d\). Every remaining node has two leaf children: a deeper descendant would violate balance. Consequently,

\(m=a+2(2^d-a)=2^{d+1}-a\),

and therefore \(2^d\leq m<2^{d+1}\).

Since \(m=2^k\), these inequalities force \(d=k\). Substituting gives \(a=2^k\), so every leaf has depth \(k\).

Thus every admissible balanced tree is a perfect binary tree, and choosing such a tree amounts to assigning the candidates bijectively to the leaves of a fixed perfect bracket. Ordering its two children at any node does not affect its winner.

For \(m=1\), the answer is trivially yes. Hardness concerns the unbounded family of larger instances.

2. A polynomial-size society representing any tournament

Let \(T\) be an arbitrary tournament on \(C\), where \(m\geq 2\). Write \(u\to v\) when \(u\) beats \(v\) in \(T\).

We construct a multiset of rankings first, then merge duplicates to obtain the required distinct support.

For each edge \(u\to v\), list the other candidates in any order as \(r_1,\ldots,r_{m-2}\), and introduce these two rankings:

\(P_{uv}: u\succ v\succ r_1\succ\cdots\succ r_{m-2}\),

\(Q_{uv}: r_{m-2}\succ\cdots\succ r_1\succ u\succ v\).

The contribution of a ranking multiset to the margin of \(x\) over \(y\) means the number of rankings placing \(x\) above \(y\), minus the number placing \(y\) above \(x\).

This pair of rankings contributes margin \(2\) for \(u\) over \(v\), and margin \(0\) for every other unordered pair. Indeed:

  • Both rankings place \(u\) above \(v\).
  • For each remaining candidate \(r_i\), the two rankings disagree on its comparison with \(u\), and also on its comparison with \(v\).
  • For two remaining candidates, their order is reversed between the rankings.

There are \(\binom{m}{2}\) edges, so the resulting multiset contains \(N=2\binom{m}{2}=m(m-1)\) rankings. Each edge’s own gadget contributes margin \(2\) in its prescribed direction; every other gadget contributes zero to that pair. Thus the multiset realizes every edge of \(T\) with margin \(2\).

Dyadic normalization and distinct support

To ensure dyadic masses, let \(D\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(D\geq N\). Then \(N\leq D<2N\).

Both \(D\) and \(N\) are even, so \(h=(D-N)/2\) is a nonnegative integer. Choose any ranking \(\rho\), and add \(h\) copies of \(\rho\) and \(h\) copies of its complete reversal. These additions cancel on every pairwise margin. There are now exactly \(D\) rankings.

For each distinct ranking \(t\) appearing in this multiset, let \(b_t\) be its multiplicity and set \(\mu_t=b_t/D\). This produces a society with distinct support, positive dyadic masses, and total mass \(1\).

For every edge \(u\to v\), aggregation preserves the margin, giving

\(\sum_{t:\,u\succ_t v}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,v\succ_t u}\mu_t=2/D\).

Because every ranking is strict and complete, these two sums add to \(1\). Hence

\(\sum_{t:\,u\succ_t v}\mu_t=1/2+1/D>1/2\).

Therefore the society satisfies the no-ties promise and has majority tournament exactly \(T\).

Before aggregation there are at most \(N+2\) distinct rankings, so \(\tau\leq m(m-1)+2\). Each ranking requires \(O(m\log m)\) bits, and each mass requires \(O(\log m)\) bits, since \(b_t\leq D<2m(m-1)\). Thus the total output length is \(O(m^3\log m)\). Constructing the rankings, padding them, and merging duplicates all take polynomial time.

3. Hardness transfers to society input

We use the following established result: given a complete deterministic tournament on a power-of-two number of players and a distinguished player, deciding whether some assignment to a perfect knockout bracket makes that player win is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is Theorem 1 of [Aziz et al., *Fixing Balanced Knockout and Double Elimination Tournaments*](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/ko_journal.pdf). Their model explicitly assumes a power-of-two player count, and their hardness construction preserves that condition.

Take an instance \((T,A)\) of this problem. Keep its candidate set and distinguished candidate, and construct the society from Section 2.

All relevant hypotheses are preserved:

  • The candidate count remains a power of two.
  • The rankings are strict and complete.
  • The support consists of distinct rankings with positive dyadic masses summing to \(1\).
  • Every pair has a strict majority winner, matching its edge in \(T\).

For any fixed leaf assignment, evaluation in the society and evaluation in \(T\) return the same candidate at every node. This follows by induction from the leaves: if the two child winners agree between evaluations, their pairwise winner also agrees because the majority tournament is exactly \(T\).

By Section 1, the admissible balanced voting trees are precisely the brackets used in the source problem. Consequently,

\(A\text{ wins some bracket in }T\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\text{ wins some balanced voting tree in the constructed society}.\)

The construction is polynomial, so this proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness for rational-support society input. It does not require relying on the earlier weighted-majority-graph assertion.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate is a permutation of the candidates specifying their positions in a fixed perfect bracket. Its length is \(O(m\log m)\).

From the input society, compute each pairwise majority by summing the corresponding rational masses exactly and comparing the sum with \(1/2\). Exact rational addition and comparison take polynomial time in the total input bit length: even using products of input denominators, their bit lengths are bounded by the sum of the denominator bit lengths, which is at most \(L\).

Then evaluate the bracket using its \(m-1\) internal comparisons and accept exactly when its root returns \(A\). Verification is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Together with the reduction, this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
