The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many positive-mass role types \(T\) with population distribution \(\mu\), a directed role graph \(H=(T,F)\), common \(0/1\) utilities over a finite resource catalogue \(R\), and per-capita supplies \(\rho_r\), find a mass allocation \(\lambda_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq R\) such that each type receives mass \(\mu_t\), every resource supply is met, and for every role edge \(t\to t'\), every supported bundle of type \(t\) is at least as valuable to type \(t\) as every supported bundle of type \(t'\); otherwise report infeasibility.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\geq1\) role types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also contains a loopless directed role graph \(H=(T,F)\), a resource catalogue \(R\) with \(m=|R|\), a common additive utility specified by \(u(r)\in\{0,1\}\), and binary-rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_r\in[0,1]\).
An allocation assigns nonnegative real mass \(\lambda_{t,B}\) to each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(B\subseteq R\); empty bundles are allowed, and each bundle contains at most one unit of each catalogue resource.
Completeness requires \(\sum_{B\subseteq R}\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{B\ni r}\lambda_{t,B}=\rho_r\) for every \(r\).
Graph envy-freeness requires \(u(B)\geq u(C)\) whenever \((t,t')\in F\), \(\lambda_{t,B}>0\), and \(\lambda_{t',C}>0\); thus comparisons concern every pair of supported bundles.
Is feasibility of a complete graph-envy-free mass allocation solvable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
For finite agents and indivisible resources, complete graph-envy-free allocation with identical binary utilities is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with maximum outdegree two; strongly connected comparison graphs admit a linear-time algorithm. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.11596)
No complexity result for exactly this support-wise mass-allocation problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the population distribution to total mass one and defined supplies in the same per-capita units.
  • Specified supplies as binary rationals in [0,1], since integral bundles permit at most one unit of each catalogue resource per individual.
  • Interpreted common binary utilities as one identical additive utility function for all roles.
  • Interpreted meeting every supply as exact equality, carrying over completeness.
  • Allowed empty bundles and nonnegative real allocation masses.
  • Used a loopless role graph, carrying over comparisons between distinct agents; no within-role comparison is imposed without an edge.
  • Defined L as the total input bit length and targeted the feasibility decision problem associated with the allocation search question.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Hardness holds even when \(m=1\), the single resource has utility one, all masses and supplies are dyadic rationals, and the role graph is a disjoint union of directed cycles of length two. In particular, every vertex has indegree and outdegree one.

The supplied [finite-agent paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2011.11596) also uses equality of utilities within strongly connected components. The proof below establishes the necessary rigidity directly for supported mass allocations and gives a reduction for this precise model.

1. Mutual comparisons force a common utility

Lemma. Suppose two positive-mass types \(p,q\) satisfy \((p,q),(q,p)\in F\). Then every supported bundle of either type has the same utility.

Proof. By positive masses and type completeness, each type has at least one supported bundle. If \(B\) is supported by \(p\) and \(C\) by \(q\), the two arcs require both \(u(B)\geq u(C)\) and \(u(C)\geq u(B)\). Hence \(u(B)=u(C)\).

Fixing one supported bundle of \(q\) shows that all supported bundles of \(p\) have that same utility; fixing one of \(p\) gives the corresponding conclusion for \(q\). \(\square\)

For a catalogue consisting of one resource \(r\), with \(u(r)=1\), the only bundles are \(\varnothing\) and \(\{r\}\), with distinct utilities. Consequently, a mutually comparing pair must allocate its entire combined mass either to \(\varnothing\) or to \(\{r\}\). Mixing these bundles is impossible.

2. Polynomial-time reduction from Subset Sum

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete Subset Sum problem: given positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and a positive integer \(b\), decide whether some \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in I}a_i=b\). We may restrict to \(1\leq b\leq S\), where \(S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\), since targets outside this range are immediately decidable.

Choose a power of two \(D\) such that \(D>S+b\), and introduce the additional integer

\(a_0=D-S.\)

Thus \(a_0>b\), and \(\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_i=D\).

Construction

Use one resource \(r\), with

\(R=\{r\},\qquad u(r)=1,\qquad \rho_r=b/D.\)

For every \(i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\), introduce two distinct types \(p_i,q_i\), with masses

\(\mu_{p_i}=\mu_{q_i}=a_i/(2D).\)

The graph has exactly the arcs \((p_i,q_i)\) and \((q_i,p_i)\) for each \(i\).

All masses are positive, and their sum is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=0}^{n}\bigl(\mu_{p_i}+\mu_{q_i}\bigr)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_i}{D}=1.\)

Also \(0<\rho_r<1\). Since \(D\) is a power of two, every mass and the supply are dyadic rationals.

The graph is loopless and consists of disjoint directed cycles of length two. Its support size is \(\tau=2(n+1)\).

Forward implication

Suppose \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{i\in I}a_i=b\).

For each \(i\in I\), allocate the entire mass of both \(p_i\) and \(q_i\) to \(\{r\}\). Allocate every other type entirely to \(\varnothing\).

Each type receives its prescribed mass. The total mass receiving \(r\) is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in I}\bigl(\mu_{p_i}+\mu_{q_i}\bigr)=\frac{\sum_{i\in I}a_i}{D}=\frac bD=\rho_r.\)

Within each comparison pair, both types receive bundles of equal utility. There are no other comparisons. The allocation is therefore complete and graph-envy-free.

Reverse implication

Suppose the constructed instance admits a complete graph-envy-free mass allocation.

By the lemma, each pair \(p_i,q_i\) allocates all its mass to a common bundle. Let \(x_i=1\) if that bundle is \(\{r\}\), and \(x_i=0\) if it is \(\varnothing\). Resource completeness gives

\(\displaystyle \frac bD=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{a_i}{D}x_i,\)

and hence

\(\displaystyle b=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_i x_i.\)

Because \(a_0>b\) and all summands are nonnegative, necessarily \(x_0=0\). Therefore the indices \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) with \(x_i=1\) form a subset summing to \(b\).

Encoding size

Take \(D\) to be the smallest power of two strictly exceeding \(S+b\). Then \(D\leq 2(S+b)\), so its binary length is polynomial in the Subset Sum input length.

There are \(2(n+1)\) types and arcs, and every constructed rational has binary encoding length \(O(\log D)\). Thus the construction is a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under all the restrictions stated above.

3. Polynomial-size certificates

We next prove membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), despite the exponentially many possible bundles and the permission to use real allocation masses.

Suppose a feasible allocation exists. Let \(J\) be its set of supported pairs \((t,B)\). Retain only these pairs as permitted variables and impose the completeness equations. Write this system as

\(Ax=c,\qquad x\geq 0,\)

where \(A\) has \(N=\tau+m\) rows. A column for \((t,B)\) has a one in the row for type \(t\), and a one in each resource row corresponding to a resource in \(B\). All other entries are zero.

Every feasible solution using only these columns remains graph-envy-free: its support is a subset of the original feasible support.

A sparse feasible solution exists

Choose a feasible solution with the smallest number \(k\) of positive coordinates. Its corresponding columns must be linearly independent.

Indeed, otherwise there is a nonzero vector \(z\) on those coordinates with \(Az=0\). Summing the type rows gives \(\sum_j z_j=0\), because every column has exactly one nonzero type entry. Thus \(z\) has both positive and negative coordinates.

Set

\(\displaystyle \theta=\min_{j:z_j>0}\frac{x_j}{z_j}>0.\)

Then \(x-\theta z\) remains nonnegative, satisfies the same equations, and has fewer positive coordinates, a contradiction.

Consequently \(k\leq N=\tau+m\).

The positive masses have polynomial bit length

Select \(k\) rows on which these independent columns form a nonsingular square matrix \(M\). Let \(c'\) be the corresponding right-hand side.

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses and supplies. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and \(Qc'\) is integral. Every entry of \(Qc'\) lies between zero and \(Q\).

Cramer’s rule expresses each positive coordinate as

\(\displaystyle x_j=\frac{\det M_j(Qc')}{Q\det M},\)

where \(M_j(Qc')\) replaces column \(j\) of \(M\) by \(Qc'\).

Since \(M\) has entries in \(\{0,1\}\), the determinant expansion gives \(|\det M|\leq k!\). The same expansion gives \(|\det M_j(Qc')|\leq Qk!\). Therefore each mass has a rational representation of bit length \(O(L+k\log k)\).

A certificate can thus list at most \(\tau+m\) supported pairs, encoding each bundle by \(m\) bits and each positive mass by polynomially many bits.

The verifier checks all completeness equations using exact rational arithmetic. It then checks the utility inequality for every pair of listed bundles whose types are connected by an arc. These checks take polynomial time.

Hence the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Conclusion

Feasibility is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The decisive obstruction is that mutual support-wise comparisons prohibit mixing utility values, allowing binary-encoded component masses to encode Subset Sum. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
