The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two complete agent types \(T=\{1,2\}\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), \(k\) indivisible chore kinds with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and rational additive disutilities \(d_t(j)\), find a finite-support rational allocation law \(z_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{k}\) such that \(\sum_B z_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}B_j=q_j\), and \(d_t^{-1}(B)\le d_t(B')\) for every pair of supported classes \((t,B)\) and \((h,B')\), where \(d_t^{-1}(B)=0\) for \(B=0\) and otherwise \(d_t^{-1}(B)=\min_{j:B_j>0}d_t(B-e_j)\). The allocation must also admit no type-symmetric fractional reassignment \(u_{t,B,j}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}u_{t,B,j}=q_j\), \(\sum_jd_t(j)u_{t,B,j}\le d_t(B)\) for every supported class, and strict inequality for one class.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given \(m=k\ge1\) indivisible chore kinds, let \(T=\{1,2\}\) be two agent types with complete additive disutility vectors \(d_t(j)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) and supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau=2\), \(\mu_t>0\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An allocation law is a finite list of bundles \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m}\) and positive rational masses \(z_{t,B}\), with unlisted masses zero, satisfying \(\sum_Bz_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}B_j=q_j\). Bundle coordinates and masses are encoded in binary.
Write \(d_t(B)=\sum_jd_t(j)B_j\), and let \(e_j\) be the \(j\)-th unit vector. Define \(d_t^{-1}(0)=0\) and otherwise \(d_t^{-1}(B)=\min_{j:B_j>0}d_t(B-e_j)\).
Require EF1 across every pair of supported classes: \(d_t^{-1}(B)\le d_t(B')\) whenever \(z_{t,B}>0\) and \(z_{h,B'}>0\).
Require fractional Pareto optimality in the following classwise sense: there is no reassignment \(u_{t,B,j}\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) satisfying \(\sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}u_{t,B,j}=q_j\) for every kind and \(\sum_jd_t(j)u_{t,B,j}\le d_t(B)\) for every supported class, with strict inequality for at least one supported class. Each \(u_{t,B,j}\) is a per-agent fractional quantity for that class.

Can a law satisfying both conditions be computed, or its nonexistence reported, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computational problem NP-hard?

For finite societies with at most two additive disutility functions, an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal integral allocation always exists and is computable in strongly polynomial time ([Garg, Murhekar, and Qin](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.02440)).
No result settling this exact mass-law problem was located in the literature search.

Polynomial-time solvability for two supported types

Every instance in the question admits an EF1 and fractionally Pareto-optimal law. Such a law can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,L\), with at most \(4m+2\) supported classes.

The construction compresses the two-type transfer approach underlying the [finite-society result of Garg, Murhekar, and Qin](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.02440). The essential additional ingredients are exact formulas for round-robin allocations with binary multiplicities and a monotone stopping condition permitting binary search. We prove these ingredients and the resulting algorithm below.

1. Clearing denominators without expanding the society

Let \(N\) be the product of the denominators occurring in the masses and supplies. Define

\[ n_t=N\mu_t,\qquad Q_j=Nq_j. \]

Here and below, these displayed expressions are understood as \(\displaystyle n_t=N\mu_t,\quad Q_j=Nq_j\).

The quantities \(n_1,n_2,Q_j\) are integers, \(n_t\ge 1\), and \(n_1+n_2=N\). Their binary lengths are \(O(L)\).

We work conceptually with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), each having mass \(1/N\), and \(Q_j\) copies of kind \(j\). The algorithm never explicitly constructs these agents or copies.

Let

\(\displaystyle J=\{j:Q_j>0,\ d_1(j)>0,\ d_2(j)>0\}.\)

Every supplied kind outside \(J\) has zero disutility for some type. Assign all copies of such a kind to one type having zero disutility for it. We call these the zero-cost assignments. Their distribution among agents of that type will be specified later.

First construct an allocation of the kinds in \(J\).

2. A certificate implying both required properties

For a nonnegative payment vector \(p\), write

\(\displaystyle p(B)=\sum_jp_jB_j,\qquad p^{-1}(B)= \begin{cases} 0,&B=0,\\ p(B)-\max_{j:B_j>0}p_j,&B\ne0. \end{cases}\)

Call an allocation payment EF1 if \(p^{-1}(B)\le p(B')\) for every pair of supported classes.

Lemma 1. Suppose positive weights \(w_1,w_2\) and nonnegative payments satisfy

\(\displaystyle p_j\le w_td_t(j)\quad\text{for every }t,j,\)

with equality whenever a supported bundle of type \(t\) contains kind \(j\). If the law is payment EF1, then it satisfies EF1 and the question’s classwise fractional Pareto optimality.

Proof. For a supported bundle \(B\) of type \(t\), equality on every contained kind gives

\(\displaystyle w_td_t(B)=p(B),\qquad w_td_t^{-1}(B)=p^{-1}(B).\)

These identities also hold for empty bundles and bundles whose contained kinds all have zero payment. For any comparison bundle \(B'\),

\(\displaystyle w_td_t^{-1}(B)=p^{-1}(B)\le p(B')\le w_td_t(B').\)

Division by \(w_t>0\) proves EF1.

For fractional Pareto optimality, the law’s weighted total disutility is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}w_td_t(B)=\sum_jp_jq_j.\)

Any feasible fractional reassignment satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}w_t\sum_jd_t(j)u_{t,B,j} \ge \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}\sum_jp_ju_{t,B,j} = \sum_jp_jq_j. \)

If every supported class weakly improved and at least one strictly improved, the left-hand side would be strictly smaller than the law’s weighted total disutility: every coefficient \(z_{t,B}w_t\) is positive. This is a contradiction. \(\square\)

Our construction uses a positive threshold \(a\) and the certificate

\(\displaystyle w_1=a,\qquad w_2=1,\qquad p_j=\min\{a\,d_1(j),d_2(j)\}.\)

Thus kinds with \(d_2(j)/d_1(j)\ge a\) may be assigned to type \(1\), kinds with the reverse inequality may be assigned to type \(2\), and kinds at equality may be split.

3. Exact round-robin statistics

Consider a multiset \(S\) of \(s\) nonnegative payments, sorted as

\(\displaystyle c_1\ge c_2\ge\cdots\ge c_s.\)

Allocate them cyclically among \(n\ge1\) agents in this decreasing order. Agent \(h\), for \(1\le h\le n\), receives positions \(h,h+n,h+2n,\ldots\).

Define

\(\displaystyle \ell_n(S)=\sum_{\substack{k\ge1\\kn\le s}}c_{kn}, \qquad \beta_n(S)=\sum_{\substack{k\ge1\\kn+1\le s}}c_{kn+1}. \)

Lemma 2. In this cyclic allocation, the minimum total payment is \(\ell_n(S)\), and the maximum payment after deleting a highest-payment chore is \(\beta_n(S)\). In particular,

\(\displaystyle \beta_n(S)\le\ell_n(S).\)

Proof. Agent \(n\) receives \(c_n,c_{2n},\ldots\). Every other agent has at least as many terms, with each corresponding term weakly larger. Thus agent \(n\) attains the minimum.

For agent \(h\), deleting a highest-payment chore leaves the payments at positions \(h+n,h+2n,\ldots\). These are termwise bounded above by the corresponding payments at positions \(1+n,1+2n,\ldots\), and there are no more terms. Hence agent \(1\) attains the maximum remaining payment.

Finally, \(c_{kn+1}\le c_{kn}\), and the sum defining \(\beta_n(S)\) has no more terms than the sum defining \(\ell_n(S)\). \(\square\)

We need two further properties.

Lemma 3. Adding a nonnegative-payment chore cannot decrease either \(\ell_n\) or \(\beta_n\). Moreover, for any added chore \(c\),

\(\displaystyle \beta_n(S\cup\{c\})\le\ell_n(S).\)

Equivalently, for any deleted chore,

\(\displaystyle \ell_n(S\setminus\{c\})\ge\beta_n(S).\)

Proof. Write \(c'_1\ge\cdots\ge c'_{s+1}\) for the payments after insertion. Sorted order statistics satisfy

\(\displaystyle c'_i\ge c_i\ge c'_{i+1}\quad(1\le i\le s).\)

The first inequality, together with nonnegativity of any new terms, proves both monotonicity statements. The second gives

\(\displaystyle \beta_n(S\cup\{c\}) = \sum_{\substack{k\ge1\\kn\le s}}c'_{kn+1} \le \sum_{\substack{k\ge1\\kn\le s}}c_{kn} = \ell_n(S). \)

Applying this insertion inequality to \(S\setminus\{c\}\) proves the deletion inequality. \(\square\)

Computing these statistics from binary multiplicities

Suppose payment values \(v_1\ge\cdots\ge v_r\) have multiplicities \(A_1,\ldots,A_r\). Put \(s_0=0\), \(s_i=\sum_{h=1}^iA_h\), and define

\(\displaystyle g_n(s)=\max\!\left\{0,\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{n}\right\rfloor\right\}.\)

Counting the relevant ranks in each block gives

\(\displaystyle \ell_n(S)= \sum_{i=1}^r v_i \left( \left\lfloor\frac{s_i}{n}\right\rfloor- \left\lfloor\frac{s_{i-1}}{n}\right\rfloor \right), \)

and

\(\displaystyle \beta_n(S)= \sum_{i=1}^r v_i\bigl(g_n(s_i)-g_n(s_{i-1})\bigr). \)

Each statistic therefore requires \(O(r)\) arithmetic operations, regardless of the total multiplicity.

4. The threshold-transfer algorithm

Assume \(J\ne\varnothing\). Sort its kinds by nondecreasing ratios

\(\displaystyle r_j=\frac{d_2(j)}{d_1(j)},\)

breaking ties deterministically.

Initially assign all copies of kinds in \(J\) to type \(1\), and none to type \(2\). Set \(a=\min_{j\in J}r_j\) and \(p_j=\min\{a\,d_1(j),d_2(j)\}\).

Within each type, distribute assigned copies cyclically in decreasing payment order. Let \(S_t\) be that type’s multiset of assigned payments, and abbreviate

\(\displaystyle \ell_t=\ell_{n_t}(S_t),\qquad \beta_t=\beta_{n_t}(S_t).\)

By Lemma 2, the allocation is payment EF1 precisely when both cross-type inequalities hold:

\(\displaystyle \beta_1\le\ell_2,\qquad \beta_2\le\ell_1.\)

Initially \(S_2\) is empty, so the second inequality holds. If the first also holds, terminate.

Otherwise process the kinds in ratio order. At the stage for kind \(j\), set \(a=r_j\). All earlier kinds have been completely transferred to type \(2\), all later kinds remain with type \(1\), and kind \(j\) is available for transfer.

The certificate of Section 2 holds throughout this stage. In particular, a copy of the current kind has the same payment on either side:

\(\displaystyle p_j=a\,d_1(j)=d_2(j).\)

Suppose there are \(M\) copies of the current kind to transfer. For \(0\le x\le M\), let

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x)= \beta_{n_1}(S_1\text{ after transferring }x\text{ copies}) - \ell_{n_2}(S_2\text{ after transferring }x\text{ copies}). \)

Lemma 3 makes \(\Phi\) nonincreasing.

  • If \(\Phi(M)>0\), transfer all \(M\) copies and proceed to the next kind.
  • Otherwise, use binary search to find the smallest \(x\) with \(\Phi(x)\le0\), transfer those copies, and terminate.

Every evaluation uses the multiplicity formulas of Section 3.

Why increasing the threshold preserves the outstanding violation

At a transition from threshold \(a\) to threshold \(a'\ge a\), every remaining type-\(1\) kind has ratio at least \(a'\). Its payment therefore increases from \(a\,d_1(j)\) to \(a'd_1(j)\). All type-\(1\) payments scale by \(a'/a\).

Every kind already assigned to type \(2\) has ratio at most \(a\), so its payment remains \(d_2(j)\).

Consequently, if \(\beta_1>\ell_2\) before the threshold change, this inequality still holds afterward. Thus each binary search starts with \(\Phi(0)>0\).

Why the first crossing yields payment EF1

Consider the single-copy transfer from \(x-1\) to the selected \(x\). Immediately before it,

\(\displaystyle \beta_1^{\mathrm{old}}>\ell_2^{\mathrm{old}}.\)

By the deletion and insertion inequalities in Lemma 3,

\(\displaystyle \ell_1^{\mathrm{new}} \ge \beta_1^{\mathrm{old}} > \ell_2^{\mathrm{old}} \ge \beta_2^{\mathrm{new}}. \)

Therefore the reverse cross-type inequality holds after the selected transfer. The forward inequality holds by the choice \(\Phi(x)\le0\). Lemma 2 supplies the within-type inequalities. The resulting allocation is payment EF1.

This also explains why we find the *first* crossing rather than transferring the whole current kind whenever its endpoint crosses.

Termination

If the algorithm reaches the last kind, transferring all remaining copies leaves \(S_1\) empty. At that endpoint,

\(\displaystyle \beta_1=0\le\ell_2.\)

Thus a crossing must occur no later than the last stage. The algorithm always terminates with a payment-EF1 allocation and the positive-weight certificate of Section 2.

5. Zero disutilities

Append the zero-cost assignments from Section 1. Every such copy is assigned to a type having zero disutility for it, and its payment

\(\displaystyle p_j=\min\{a\,d_1(j),d_2(j)\}\)

is zero.

Distribute these copies cyclically after the positive-payment copies within their receiving type. Adding zero-payment chores changes neither an agent’s total payment nor its payment after deleting a highest-payment chore. Payment EF1 is therefore preserved. Equality \(p_j=w_td_t(j)\) also holds for every newly assigned copy.

If \(J=\varnothing\), simply choose \(a=1\) and perform these zero-cost assignments. Every agent has total payment zero, so payment EF1 holds directly.

Lemma 1 now proves EF1 and classwise fractional Pareto optimality for the complete allocation.

6. Constructing a short allocation law

It remains to encode the cyclic allocations without listing virtual agents.

Fix a type with \(n\) agents, indexed by \(h\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\). Order the kinds in decreasing payment order, putting zero-payment kinds last. Let \(A_j\) be the number of copies of kind \(j\) assigned to this type. Write

\(\displaystyle A_j=nb_j+\rho_j,\qquad 0\le\rho_j<n.\)

Let \(o_j\) be the total multiplicity of preceding kinds, reduced modulo \(n\). In the cyclic allocation, agent \(h\) receives

\(\displaystyle B_j(h)=b_j+ \mathbf 1\{(h-o_j)\bmod n<\rho_j\}. \)

Thus each coordinate is constant except at the endpoints of one cyclic interval. Partition \(\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\) using the endpoints \(0,n\) and, for each \(\rho_j>0\),

\(\displaystyle o_j,\qquad (o_j+\rho_j)\bmod n.\)

There are at most \(2m+2\) distinct endpoints and hence at most \(2m+1\) nonempty intervals. All agents in any one interval receive the same bundle.

For an interval \([u,v)\), output that bundle with mass

\(\displaystyle z=\frac{v-u}{N}.\)

Merge identical bundles within a type by adding their masses. This produces at most \(2m+1\) supported classes per type, and at most \(4m+2\) overall.

The law has the required masses and supplies because it groups exactly the conceptual agents and copies:

\(\displaystyle \sum_Bz_{t,B}=\frac{n_t}{N}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B}z_{t,B}B_j=\frac{Q_j}{N}=q_j. \)

Grouping preserves payment EF1 and the equality conditions of Lemma 1. Hence the output law satisfies both requested properties.

7. Polynomial running time and encoding length

There are at most \(m\) transfer stages. A binary search uses \(O(1+\log(Q_j+1))=O(L)\) statistic evaluations. Each evaluation requires \(O(m)\) arithmetic operations.

The order of assigned payments within each type is fixed by its disutility ordering: payments are \(a\,d_1(j)\) for type \(1\) and \(d_2(j)\) for type \(2\). Thus only a constant number of kind lists need sorting.

All arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity:

  • \(N,n_t,Q_j\) have \(O(L)\) bits.
  • Each threshold is a ratio of two input disutilities.
  • Statistics are rational sums of input disutilities multiplied by integer counts, optionally multiplied by the current threshold.
  • Every output coordinate is at most \(Q_j\), and every output mass is an integer divided by \(N\).

The transfer computation uses \(O(m^2L+m\log m)\) arithmetic operations on numbers of polynomial binary length. The interval construction and the complete output also have polynomial size.

Therefore the stated problem is polynomial-time solvable for exactly two supported types, and nonexistence never occurs under the given hypotheses.

\[ w_td_t(B)=p(B). \]
It also gives
\[ w_td_t^{-1}(B)=p^{-1}(B), \]
because minimizing the remaining additive disutility is equivalent to deleting a contained chore with maximum disutility, and multiplication by a positive weight preserves that ordering. The stipulated empty-bundle convention agrees on both sides.

At a fixed threshold, transferring additional copies cannot increase \(\beta_1\) and cannot decrease \(\ell_2\), by Lemma 3. Hence \(\Phi\) is nonincreasing, and binary search is justified.

The central stopping argument is valid. Immediately before the selected single-copy transfer,
\[ \beta_1^{\mathrm{old}}>\ell_2^{\mathrm{old}}. \]
The deletion and insertion inequalities give exactly
\[ \ell_1^{\mathrm{new}} \ge\beta_1^{\mathrm{old}} >\ell_2^{\mathrm{old}} \ge\beta_2^{\mathrm{new}}. \]

Notably, this argument does not require the reverse cross-type inequality to hold at every intermediate stage.
