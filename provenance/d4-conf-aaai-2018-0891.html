The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), valuation schedules \(v_t(j)\) for integral bundle sizes \(j\in\{0,\ldots,B\}\), and rational per-capita supply \(s\), choose a common item price \(p\ge0\) and mass shares \(q_{t,j}\ge0\). Require \(\sum_j q_{t,j}=1\), \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j j q_{t,j}\le s\), \(q_{t,j}>0\Rightarrow v_t(j)-pj\ge0\), and \(v_t(j)-pj\ge v_t(k)-pk\) whenever \(q_{t,j}>0\) and \(q_{u,k}>0\). Maximize \(p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j j q_{t,j}\). What is the complexity, in \(B,\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), of finding an optimum; in particular, is approximation within \(O((\log\tau)^\epsilon)\) R3SAT-hard for some \(\epsilon>0\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). The parameter \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Input also includes an integer maximum bundle size \(B\ge1\), explicitly tabulated binary-rational valuations \(v_t(j)\ge0\) for \(j\in\{0,\ldots,B\}\), with \(v_t(0)=0\), and positive binary-rational per-capita supply \(s\). Valuations need not be monotone. Write \(m:=B\) and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; running times are measured in \(B,\tau,L\).
Choose a common real item price \(p\ge0\) and real conditional mass shares \(q_{t,j}\ge0\), satisfying \(\sum_{j=0}^{B}q_{t,j}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j=0}^{B}j q_{t,j}\le s\). Each individual receives an integral bundle; shares split a type's population between bundle sizes.
Require individual rationality \(q_{t,j}>0\Rightarrow v_t(j)-pj\ge0\) and pair envy-freeness \(q_{t,j}>0\land q_{u,k}>0\Rightarrow v_t(j)-pj\ge v_t(k)-pk\), for all types and sizes, including \(t=u\) and size \(0\). Ties may be resolved by the seller. Revenue is \(R(p,q)=p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j=0}^{B}j q_{t,j}\); let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be its maximum over feasible outcomes.
Here R3SAT-hardness means that a polynomial-time approximation would contradict the following random-formula hypothesis: for every fixed \(\delta>0\) and sufficiently large constant \(\Delta\), no randomized polynomial-time algorithm labels at least half of random formulas on \(N\) variables with \(\Delta N\) clauses as typical, while labeling every formula with at least \((1-\delta)\Delta N\) simultaneously satisfiable clauses as exceptional with exponentially small error. Random formulas independently sample each literal uniformly. [Hardness convention](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~pbriest/papers/uniformBudgets.pdf).

Is approximating \(\mathrm{OPT}\) within \(O((\log(2+\tau))^\epsilon)\) R3SAT-hard for some \(\epsilon>0\), or can algorithms polynomial in \(B,\tau,L\) attain this ratio for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\)?

For finite societies with \(n\) buyers, general valuations and common item pricing, approximation within \(O((\log n)^\epsilon)\) is R3SAT-hard for some \(\epsilon>0\), even with unlimited supply. [Discrete hardness](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/11438/11297).
The discrete problem admits a polynomial-time \(O(\log n)\)-approximation. [Discrete algorithm](https://ricerca.univaq.it/retrieve/e2188bfa-63a3-f28d-e053-d805fe0ac2bb/envyfree-944-revised.pdf). No published result settling the approximation question for exactly the mass-allocation model above was found.

1. Result

There exists a constant \(\epsilon_0>0\) such that approximating the stated mass-allocation problem within \(O((\log(2+\tau))^{\epsilon_0})\) is R3SAT-hard. This holds already when \(s=B\), so supply never binds.

The key fact is that, with nonbinding supply, population splitting cannot improve optimal revenue over deterministic allocation to each type.

2. Removing population splitting

Lemma. Suppose \(s\ge B\). Every feasible outcome \((p,q)\) can be replaced, at the same price, by a feasible outcome assigning one bundle size to each type and earning at least as much revenue.

Proof. For each type \(t\), define its nonempty allocation support and largest assigned size by

\(\displaystyle A_t=\{j:q_{t,j}>0\},\qquad x_t=\max A_t.\)

Assign size \(x_t\) to the entire population of type \(t\).

Individual rationality follows because \(x_t\in A_t\). For every pair of types \(t,u\), the original pair envy-freeness constraint applied to \(x_t\in A_t\) and \(x_u\in A_u\) gives

\(\displaystyle v_t(x_t)-px_t\ge v_t(x_u)-px_u.\)

Thus the new outcome is pair envy-free. Its expected quantity satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\mu_t x_t\le B\sum_t\mu_t=B\le s,\)

so it also satisfies supply. Finally, since \(p\ge0\) and every size in \(A_t\) is at most \(x_t\),

\(\displaystyle p\sum_t\mu_t x_t\ge p\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j=0}^{B}j q_{t,j}.\)

This proves the lemma. In particular, the argument applies at ties without requiring any perturbation. ∎

3. An exact reduction with dyadic masses and distinct support types

Consider a discrete instance with \(n\) buyers, valuations \(v_i(0),\ldots,v_i(B)\), and nonbinding supply. Write \(\operatorname{OPT}_{D}\) for its maximum pair envy-free revenue.

Let

\(\displaystyle Q=2^{\lceil\log_2 n\rceil}.\)

Add \(Q-n\) dummy buyers whose valuation is zero at every size. Group the resulting buyers by their complete valuation vectors. For each distinct vector \(t\), let \(c_t\) be its multiplicity and define

\(\displaystyle \mu_t=\frac{c_t}{Q}.\)

The mass instance uses these distinct vectors as its support, the same maximum bundle size \(B\), and supply \(s=B\). Consequently:

  • Every mass is positive and dyadic, and the masses sum to \(1\).
  • Identical valuation vectors are merged.
  • The support size satisfies \(\tau\le n+1\).
  • Each mass requires \(O(\log n)\) bits.

I claim that this reduction satisfies

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_{M}=\frac{\operatorname{OPT}_{D}}{Q}.\)

From discrete outcomes to mass outcomes. Start with any feasible discrete outcome. Give every dummy buyer size \(0\). This preserves pair envy-freeness: an original buyer weakly prefers its allocation to size \(0\) by individual rationality, while a dummy buyer values another buyer’s size \(j\) at utility \(-pj\le0\).

Within each valuation group, define \(q_{t,j}\) as the fraction of its augmented buyers assigned size \(j\). Every positive share therefore corresponds to an actual assigned bundle. The discrete pair envy-freeness inequalities imply all required mass inequalities, including comparisons between two sizes assigned within the same type. For an identical size compared with itself, the inequality is equality.

Supply is automatic because \(s=B\). The resulting mass revenue is exactly the original discrete revenue divided by \(Q\). Hence

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}_{M}\ge\frac{\operatorname{OPT}_{D}}{Q}.\)

From mass outcomes to discrete outcomes. Take any feasible mass outcome with revenue \(R\). Apply the lemma, obtaining sizes \(x_t\) and revenue \(R'\ge R\).

If \(p>0\), individual rationality forces any identically zero valuation type to receive size \(0\), since \(-px_t\ge0\). Assign each original buyer the size chosen for its valuation type. These assignments are individually rational and pair envy-free by the lemma. Dummy buyers contribute zero, so the discrete revenue is

\(\displaystyle p\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{t(i)} =p\sum_t c_t x_t =Q R' \ge Q R.\)

If \(p=0\), then \(R=0\), and assigning every original buyer size \(0\) supplies the required discrete outcome.

Thus every feasible mass revenue yields a discrete revenue at least \(Q\) times larger, proving the reverse inequality and the claimed equality.

Both transformations take polynomial time. They also show that any approximation factor for the mass instance transfers unchanged to the discrete instance.

4. Checking the published hardness theorem’s encoding

The invoked discrete result is Theorem 11 of [*On Social Envy-Freeness in Multi-Unit Markets*](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/11438/11438-13-14966-1-2-20201228.pdf): for some \(\epsilon_0>0\), approximation within \(O((\log n)^{\epsilon_0})\) is R3SAT-hard for general valuations and common item pricing under pair envy-freeness, even with unlimited supply.

Its hard instances meet the present explicit-table requirement. The construction starts from \(M\) sets over a universe \(U\), uses \(\kappa=O(\log M)\) classes, and creates at most \(M2^\kappa\) buyers. Its nonzero valuations are integers, and every positively valued size is at most \(2^\kappa|U|\). Thus the entire valuation table through

\(\displaystyle B=2^\kappa|U|\)

has polynomial size. Larger sizes have zero valuation: at positive prices, individual rationality excludes them, and at price zero they earn no revenue. Restricting to this \(B\) therefore preserves the optimum.

The hardness convention agrees with the question’s randomized, exponentially small error convention; see Hypothesis 1 and Definition 3 in [Briest’s paper](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~pbriest/papers/uniformBudgets.pdf).

5. Transferring the approximation barrier

Suppose an algorithm polynomial in \(B,\tau,L\) always produces revenue at least

\(\displaystyle \frac{\operatorname{OPT}_{M}}{C(\log(2+\tau))^{\epsilon_0}}\)

for some constant \(C\).

Apply it to the mass instance constructed above, then convert its output to a discrete outcome. The resulting discrete revenue is at least

\(\displaystyle \frac{Q\operatorname{OPT}_{M}}{C(\log(2+\tau))^{\epsilon_0}} = \frac{\operatorname{OPT}_{D}}{C(\log(2+\tau))^{\epsilon_0}}. \)

Because \(\tau\le n+1\),

\(\displaystyle \log(2+\tau)\le\log(n+3)=O(\log n).\)

The resulting algorithm would therefore be a polynomial-time \(O((\log n)^{\epsilon_0})\)-approximation for the discrete hard instances, contradicting the stated random-formula hypothesis.

This settles the requested alternative: the hardness transfers to support size despite allowing population splitting.
