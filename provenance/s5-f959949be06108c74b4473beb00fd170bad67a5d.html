The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), target \(c^\star\), \(k\), a rational budget \(\beta\), and types \(i \in [\tau]\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\), initial rankings \(\sigma_i\), and nonnegative rational arrays \(a^i,b^i\), decide whether there are masses \(x_{i,\rho} \ge 0\) over complete rankings \(\rho\) such that \(\sum_\rho x_{i,\rho}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_{i,\rho}\pi_i(\rho)x_{i,\rho}\le\beta\), and \(\sum_{i,\rho}x_{i,\rho}(I_{c^\star,\rho}-I_{c,\rho})\ge 0\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\). Here \(I_{c,\rho}\) indicates whether \(c\) is in \(\rho\)'s top \(k\), and \(\pi_i(\rho)\) sums \(a^i_j+b^i_{j'}\) over pairs whose order is reversed from \(\sigma_i\), where \(j<j'\) are their original positions.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, with target \(c^\star\in C\) and input integer \(1\le k<m\).
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge 1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; total mass need not equal \(1\).
Each type has an initial strict complete ranking \(\sigma_i\) and arrays \(a^i,b^i\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\). The budget is \(\beta\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\); let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Write \(\sigma_i(j)\) for the candidate originally in position \(j\). For a complete ranking \(\rho\), define \(\pi_i(\rho)=\sum_{j<j':\,\sigma_i(j')\text{ precedes }\sigma_i(j)\text{ in }\rho}(a^i_j+b^i_{j'})\); positions always refer to the initial ranking.
Let \(\mathcal R\) be all strict complete rankings and \(I_{c,\rho}\) indicate membership in the top \(k\). Mass may be split among rankings, but cannot be deleted or made inactive.
The decision problem asks whether real masses \(x_{i,\rho}\ge0\) exist satisfying \(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R}x_{i,\rho}=\mu_i\), \(\sum_{i,\rho}\pi_i(\rho)x_{i,\rho}\le\beta\), and \(\sum_{i,\rho}x_{i,\rho}(I_{c^\star,\rho}-I_{c,\rho})\ge0\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\). Ties count as wins.

Is this continuous decision problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discretely, constructive \(k\)-Approval Swap Bribery with additively separable costs is NP-complete for every fixed \(k\ge2\), even with \(a^i_j\in\{0,1\}\) and \(b^i_j\in\{1,2\}\).
The continuous problem with these costs is already polynomial-time solvable, even when \(k\) varies and arbitrary rational target score margins are specified.

  • Mass normalization was unspecified; allow arbitrary positive rational masses without requiring their sum to be 1, as in the supplied society definition.
  • The budget's sign was unspecified; require a nonnegative rational budget.

Result

The continuous decision problem is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) for arbitrary nonnegative rational arrays \(a^i,b^i\).

The proof constructs a polynomial-size rational linear program whose feasible flows correspond exactly to admissible mass distributions.

1. A cheapest ranking for each approval set

Fix a type \(i\), and identify candidates with their positions in its initial ranking. Let \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(|S|=k\), be the positions of the candidates to approve.

Define \(\rho_i(S)\) by listing the candidates in \(S\) in their original order, followed by the candidates outside \(S\) in their original order.

Every ranking with approval set \(S\) must reverse every pair of original positions \(p<q\) for which \(p\notin S\) and \(q\in S\): the candidate at \(q\) must precede the candidate at \(p\). These mandatory inversions have total cost

\[ F_i(S)=\sum_{\substack{1\le p<q\le m\\p\notin S,\ q\in S}}(a^i_p+b^i_q). \]

The ranking \(\rho_i(S)\) has exactly these inversions. Any other ranking with approval set \(S\) can have additional inversions within the approved or unapproved block. Since all inversion costs are nonnegative, its cost is at least \(F_i(S)\).

Consequently, replacing any ranking by \(\rho_i(S)\) preserves every approval indicator and weakly decreases its cost. It therefore suffices to distribute each type’s mass among these canonical rankings.

2. Computing the cost along a path

For each position \(j\), let

\[ t_j=\bigl|S\cap\{1,\ldots,j-1\}\bigr|. \]

Split each summand of \(F_i(S)\) into its \(a\)-part and \(b\)-part.

If \(j\notin S\), then exactly \(k-t_j\) selected positions occur after \(j\). Thus the terms whose earlier endpoint is \(j\) contribute \(a^i_j(k-t_j)\).

If \(j\in S\), then exactly \(j-1-t_j\) unselected positions occur before \(j\). Thus the terms whose later endpoint is \(j\) contribute \(b^i_j(j-1-t_j)\).

Every mandatory inversion contributes once through each of its two parts. Hence

\[ F_i(S)= \sum_{j\notin S}a^i_j(k-t_j) + \sum_{j\in S}b^i_j(j-1-t_j). \]

This identity means that scanning the original ranking requires only the number of positions selected so far.

For each type \(i\), construct a directed layered graph with vertex set

\[ V=\{(j,t):0\le j\le m,\ \max(0,k-m+j)\le t\le\min(j,k)\}. \]

Here \(j\) positions have been processed and \(t\) selected. The bounds ensure that selecting exactly \(k\) positions remains possible. The source is \(s=(0,0)\), and the sink is \(z=(m,k)\).

For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), include the following arcs whenever both endpoints belong to \(V\):

  • A skip arc \((j-1,t)\to(j,t)\), with cost \(a^i_j(k-t)\).
  • A select arc \((j-1,t)\to(j,t+1)\), with cost \(b^i_j(j-1-t)\).

Both costs are nonnegative because every tail vertex satisfies \(t\le k\) and \(t\le j-1\).

Every source–sink path selects exactly \(k\) positions, and every set \(S\) of size \(k\) determines one such path. By the identity above, that path has cost exactly \(F_i(S)\).

3. The polynomial-size flow LP

For every type \(i\) and arc \(e\), introduce a nonnegative variable \(f^i_e\). Write \(d^i_e\) for its arc cost.

Require flow conservation:

\[ \sum_{e\in\delta^+(v)}f^i_e-\sum_{e\in\delta^-(v)}f^i_e = \begin{cases} \mu_i,&v=s,\\ -\mu_i,&v=z,\\ 0,&v\notin\{s,z\}. \end{cases} \]

Thus each type sends precisely its entire mass from source to sink.

For a candidate \(c\), define its score by

\[ A_c(f)= \sum_{i=1}^{\tau} \sum_{\substack{e\text{ is a select arc}\\e\text{ processes position }j\\\sigma_i(j)=c}} f^i_e. \]

A path contributes to this sum exactly when it selects \(c\). Impose the budget and winning constraints:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\sum_e d^i_e f^i_e\le\beta, \qquad A_{c^\star}(f)-A_c(f)\ge0 \quad(c\ne c^\star). \]

These are linear constraints over rational coefficients.

4. Exact equivalence

An admissible mass distribution yields a feasible flow

Suppose the original instance has feasible masses \(x_{i,\rho}\).

For each ranking \(\rho\), let \(S\) be its approval set in original-position coordinates. Replace \(\rho\) by \(\rho_i(S)\), carrying the same mass. Section 1 proves that this preserves every candidate’s score and does not increase expenditure.

Route that mass along the path associated with \(S\). Summing the path flows gives a flow of value \(\mu_i\) for each type. Its expenditure equals the expenditure of the canonical rankings, and its scores equal the original scores. All LP constraints therefore hold.

A feasible flow yields an admissible mass distribution

Conversely, suppose the flow LP is feasible.

Each type’s graph is finite and acyclic, so its nonnegative flow decomposes into finitely many source–sink paths with nonnegative weights. This also holds for real flows: whenever positive source flow remains, follow positive-flow arcs until reaching the sink, subtract the minimum flow on that path, and repeat. Conservation guarantees an outgoing positive-flow arc at every intermediate vertex reached. Each subtraction eliminates at least one positive-flow arc, so the process terminates.

When source flow becomes zero, all remaining arcs have zero flow, by induction over the layers. Thus the path weights sum to \(\mu_i\), and their weighted incidence vectors recover the entire flow.

Assign each path’s weight to its canonical ranking \(\rho_i(S)\). Section 2 shows that its ranking cost equals its path cost. Its approval indicators equal the select-arc indicators. Consequently, the resulting masses satisfy:

  • total mass \(\mu_i\) for every type;
  • total expenditure at most \(\beta\);
  • target score at least every rival’s score.

This is a feasible solution of the original problem.

5. Polynomial complexity

Each type’s graph has at most \((m+1)(k+1)\) vertices and at most \(2m(k+1)\) arcs. Hence the LP has \(O(\tau mk)\) variables and \(O(\tau mk+m)\) constraints.

Each arc cost is an input rational multiplied by an integer between \(0\) and \(m\), increasing its binary encoding length by at most \(O(\log m)\). All other coefficients are input rationals or integers from \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The LP’s total encoding length is therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

Polynomial-time feasibility algorithms for rational linear programming apply directly. By the proved equivalence, they decide the original continuous problem exactly.

Thus the stated tractability for restricted costs extends to all nonnegative rational additively separable costs. Replacing the winning inequalities’ right-hand sides by arbitrary rational margins leaves the same polynomial-size formulation.
