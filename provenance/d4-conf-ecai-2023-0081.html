The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and ordered types \(i\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) with approval sets \(A_i\subseteq C\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), where each candidate’s approving types form an interval, choose a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\) and mass assignments \(x_{i,c}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{c\in W}x_{i,c}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{i,c}=1/k\) for every \(c\in W\), minimizing \(\sum_i\sum_{c\in W}x_{i,c}\mathbf{1}[c\notin A_i]\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\geq 1\) candidates and \(1\leq k\leq m\) an integer committee size. A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\geq 1\) types with positive masses, each encoded as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible approval sets.
The support is supplied in an order \(1,\ldots,\tau\); type \(i\) has approval set \(A_i\subseteq C\) and mass \(\mu_i>0\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). For every candidate \(c\), the set \(\{i:c\in A_i\}\) is an interval in this order, possibly empty. Empty approval sets are allowed.
A feasible outcome consists of a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), and divisible assignments \(x_{i,c}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}\) satisfying \(\sum_{c\in W}x_{i,c}=\mu_i\) for each type and \(\sum_i x_{i,c}=1/k\) for each committee member. Its cost is \(\sum_i\sum_{c\in W}x_{i,c}\mathbf{1}[c\notin A_i]\).
Given also a binary rational threshold \(b\in[0,1]\), decide whether a feasible outcome has cost at most \(b\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

Is this continuous approval Monroe decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For explicitly listed discrete voters with the same interval condition, approval Monroe winner determination is polynomial-time solvable for both total and maximum misrepresentation, with each selected candidate representing between \(\lfloor n/k\rfloor\) and \(\lceil n/k\rceil\) voters ([Chen, Hatschka, and Simola](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.16864)). No result settling the exact divisible-mass problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The committee-size domain was unspecified; require an integer k with 1 ≤ k ≤ m and nonempty support.
  • To give the optimization question a precise complexity target, use its decision version with a binary rational cost threshold b in [0,1], and define L as total binary input length.
  • Empty approval sets and empty candidate approval intervals are allowed, consistent with the discrete preference domain.
  • Assignments range over nonnegative real masses and may split a type among representatives; the committee itself remains a set of exactly k candidates.

Partial results for the divisible-mass problem

I do not settle the general polynomial-time versus $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness question. I establish two additional polynomial-time cases for arbitrary binary rational thresholds:

  • The nonempty candidate intervals admit an ordering in which both left and right endpoints are nondecreasing.
  • All nonempty candidate intervals contain a common type.

The first case permits crossing intervals. The second permits arbitrary nesting and crossing around the common type. The arguments below are self-contained, except for a precisely stated scheduling theorem used in the second case.

1. Definitions and integer scaling

Write
$$ q=\frac1k,\qquad I_c=\{i:c\in A_i\}. $$
Each $I_c$ is an interval or is empty. For $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\tau\}$, define
$$ \mu(J)=\sum_{i\in J}\mu_i. $$

Write $\mu_i=p_i/d_i$ and $b=u/v$, with integer numerators and positive integer denominators. Define
$$ D=v\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}d_i, \qquad N=kD, \qquad T_i=N\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mu_j, \qquad T_0=0. $$
Then $T_\tau=N$, every $T_i$ is an integer, and
$$ Nq=D,\qquad Nb\in\mathbb Z. $$

Let
$$ B=L+\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil+\lceil\log_2(k+1)\rceil+1. $$
All these integers have $O(B)$ bits: the sum of the binary lengths of the encoded denominators is at most $L$.

For a nonempty interval $I_c=[l_c,r_c]$, associate the integer time window
$$ [\alpha_c,\omega_c) = [T_{l_c-1},T_{r_c}). $$
Type $i$ corresponds to the block $[T_{i-1},T_i)$.

2. Approved flow, deficiency, and membership in $\mathrm{NP}$

For $Q\subseteq C$, with $|Q|\leq k$, let $F(Q)$ be the maximum approved mass in a partial assignment satisfying
$$ z_{i,c}\geq0,\qquad z_{i,c}=0\text{ if }i\notin I_c, $$
$$ \sum_{c\in Q}z_{i,c}\leq\mu_i, \qquad \sum_i z_{i,c}\leq q. $$

This is a maximum-flow problem: use source-to-candidate capacities $q$, candidate-to-approved-type capacities $2$, and type-to-sink capacities $\mu_i$.

Proposition 1: fixed-committee cost

For every committee $W$ of size $k$,
$$ \operatorname{cost}(W)=1-F(W). $$

Proof. The approved part of any complete assignment is feasible for the partial-flow problem. Hence every complete assignment has cost at least $1-F(W)$.

Conversely, take a maximum approved partial assignment. Its residual type supplies and residual candidate quotas both total $1-F(W)$. Because assignments between every type and every selected candidate are permitted, these residual quantities can be matched. The resulting complete assignment has cost at most $1-F(W)$. Together with the lower bound, this proves equality. $\square$

After scaling by $N$, all capacities are integers. Integral maximum flow therefore gives an optimal approved assignment on the $1/N$ grid. Residual supplies and quotas are also integral after scaling, so completion can likewise be performed on that grid.

Consequently, a committee is a polynomial-size certificate. Its optimum can be verified by maximum flow, establishing membership in $\mathrm{NP}$. For example, Edmonds–Karp gives the conservative bit-time bound
$$ O\!\left( L B^2+ (\tau+k+2)(k\tau+k+\tau)^2B^2 \right). $$

Proposition 2: deficiency formula

Define
$$ \delta(Q)=q|Q|-F(Q). $$
Then
$$ \delta(Q)= \max_{S\subseteq Q} \left( q|S|-\mu\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right) \right). $$

Proof. The source-only cut has capacity $q|Q|\leq1$, so a minimum cut cannot cross an arc of capacity $2$.

If $S$ is the set of source-side candidates, every type in their approval union must also lie on the source side. Including any further type increases the cut capacity, since its mass is positive. Thus
$$ F(Q)= \min_{S\subseteq Q} \left( q(|Q|-|S|) + \mu\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right) \right). $$
Subtracting from $q|Q|$ proves the formula. The choice $S=\varnothing$ also shows $\delta(Q)\geq0$. $\square$

If a committee contains $e$ empty-interval candidates and its nonempty candidates form $Q$, then
$$ \operatorname{cost}(W)=eq+\delta(Q). $$

Interpretation as partial scheduling

A scaled integral approved assignment for $Q$ can be represented by assigning integer time units to its candidates. Each candidate receives at most $D$ units, all inside its window, and no time unit is assigned twice.

Indeed, within each type block, partition time units according to the integral approved-flow amounts; unused units remain unassigned. Conversely, such a schedule determines approved-flow amounts by counting candidate time units in each type block.

Thus $NF(Q)$ is the maximum total number of executed units when candidates in $Q$ may each execute between zero and $D$ units inside their windows.

3. Polynomial-time case: endpoints ordered together

Assume the nonempty candidates can be ordered as $c_1,\ldots,c_s$ so that
$$ \alpha_1\leq\alpha_2\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_s, \qquad \omega_1\leq\omega_2\leq\cdots\leq\omega_s. $$

This condition is checkable by sorting on left endpoints, breaking ties by increasing right endpoint, and checking that the right endpoints are nondecreasing. In particular, it holds if no nonempty candidate interval properly contains another.

Lemma 3: a greedy schedule evaluates every fixed selection

Fix any subsequence of this candidate ordering. Starting with cursor $t=0$, process its selected candidates in order.

For a selected candidate with window $[\alpha,\omega)$:

  • If $t\geq\omega$, execute no units and leave $t$ unchanged.
  • Otherwise, put

$$ a=\max(t,\alpha),\qquad p=\min(D,\omega-a), $$
execute the candidate throughout $[a,a+p)$, and set $t=a+p$.

This greedy schedule maximizes the total approved units for that fixed selection.

Proof. Start with an optimal integral partial schedule. We transform it successively to agree with the greedy schedule.

Suppose previously processed candidates already agree with the greedy schedule, whose current cursor is $t$. All remaining execution is at times at least $t$.

Consider the next candidate, with window $[\alpha,\omega)$. Every later candidate has release time at least $\alpha$ and deadline at least $\omega$.

If $\omega\leq\max(t,\alpha)$, then $\omega\leq t$, because $\alpha<\omega$. The current candidate has no execution in the remaining schedule. The required greedy step already holds.

Otherwise, define
$$ a=\max(t,\alpha),\qquad p=\min(D,\omega-a),\qquad P=[a,a+p). $$
Let:

  • $z$ be the current candidate’s total executed units;
  • $h$ be its executed units inside $P$;
  • $y$ be the units inside $P$ executed by later candidates.

Since the current candidate executes only inside $[a,\omega)$,
$$ z\leq p. $$
Since time units are disjoint,
$$ h+y\leq p. $$

Assign every unit of $P$ to the current candidate, and remove its old execution outside $P$. This displaces $y$ units of later candidates.

Reassign $\min(y,z-h)$ displaced units to distinct former current-candidate units outside $P$. Every such reassignment is valid: the destination is later than the displaced unit and earlier than $\omega$; the receiving candidate’s release is no later than its displaced execution time, and its deadline is at least $\omega$. Discard any remaining displaced units.

No later candidate’s executed amount increases. If $V$ and $V'$ denote total execution before and after this transformation, then
$$ V'-V = p-z-y+\min(y,z-h) = p-z-\max(0,y-z+h). $$
Both
$$ p-z\geq0 \quad\text{and}\quad y-z+h\leq p-z $$
hold, so $V'\geq V$.

The current candidate now executes exactly the greedy prefix $P$, and all later execution occurs at times at least $a+p$. This proves the induction step.

After all selected candidates have been processed, the resulting schedule is the greedy schedule. It is feasible and has value at least the original optimum, so it is optimal. $\square$

Polynomially many cursor coordinates

Define
$$ \mathcal T= \{T_i+hD: 0\leq i\leq\tau,\ 0\leq h\leq k,\ T_i+hD\leq N\}. $$
Its cardinality $R$ satisfies
$$ R\leq(\tau+1)(k+1). $$

Every greedy cursor belongs to $\mathcal T$. Initially the cursor is $T_0$. A positive-execution step ends either at a deadline $\omega$, which is some $T_i$, or at $\max(t,\alpha)+D$. The latter is either $\alpha+D$ or $t+D$, and belongs to $\mathcal T$ whenever it is at most $N$. An expired candidate leaves the cursor unchanged.

The numerical size of $N$ therefore does not determine the number of states.

Exact dynamic program

For candidate $c_j$, let $g_j(t)$ be the number of units added by its greedy step and $f_j(t)$ its resulting cursor.

Let
$$ G_j(r,t) $$
be the maximum greedy execution among selections of exactly $r$ candidates from $c_1,\ldots,c_j$ whose final cursor is $t$.

Initialize
$$ G_0(0,0)=0 $$
and all other entries to $-\infty$.

For each layer, initialize its entries to $-\infty$ and apply the following updates to every finite preceding entry:
$$ G_{j+1}(r,t) \leftarrow \max\{G_{j+1}(r,t),G_j(r,t)\}, $$
and, if $r<k$,
$$ G_{j+1}(r+1,f_{j+1}(t)) \leftarrow \max\{ G_{j+1}(r+1,f_{j+1}(t)), G_j(r,t)+g_{j+1}(t) \}. $$

These are respectively the choices to omit or select the next candidate. Future greedy steps depend on the preceding selection only through its cursor, so retaining the largest value at each state is valid. Induction on $j$ proves the table’s stated meaning.

Let $e$ be the number of empty-interval candidates. The optimum approved units for a committee of size $k$ are
$$ G^*= \max_{\substack{0\leq r\leq k\\k-r\leq e}} \ \max_{t\in\mathcal T}G_s(r,t). $$
The other $k-r$ selections are empty candidates. Lemma 3 and Proposition 1 give
$$ \operatorname{OPT}=1-\frac{G^*}{N}. $$
Thus the decision answer is affirmative exactly when
$$ G^*\geq N(1-b). $$

Running time

Sort the coordinates and precompute all cursor transitions using binary search. This requires
$$ O(mR\log(R+1)) $$
arithmetic operations; the dynamic program requires $O(mkR)$.

All finite table entries and coordinates have $O(B)$ bits. A conservative total bit-time bound is
$$ O\!\left( \left[ L+m\log(m+1) +m\tau k\bigl(k+\log(\tau k+2)\bigr) \right]B^2 \right). $$
This is polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L$, independently of denominator magnitudes.

4. Polynomial-time case: a common approved type

Assume there is a type $h$ satisfying
$$ h\in I_c $$
for every nonempty candidate interval.

This additional hypothesis allows endpoints to occur in arbitrary orders.

Removing unnecessary empty selections

Let $s$ be the number of nonempty candidates, and put
$$ t=\min(k,s),\qquad e=k-t. $$

There is an optimal committee with exactly $t$ nonempty candidates. To see this, whenever a committee contains an empty candidate and omits a nonempty candidate, replace the former by the latter. Its existing approved partial assignment remains feasible, so approved mass cannot decrease. Repetition yields the asserted composition.

If $s=0$, every outcome has cost $1$, and the decision is immediate. Assume henceforth $t\geq1$.

Set
$$ \beta=b-\frac ek. $$
If $\beta<0$, reject: the empty candidates alone cause cost at least $e/k$.

Lemma 4: adding mass at the common type absorbs the deficiency budget

For $\beta\geq0$, define augmented supplies
$$ \mu'_h=\mu_h+\beta,\qquad \mu'_i=\mu_i\quad(i\neq h). $$
Keep each candidate’s capacity equal to $q$.

For every set $Q$ of nonempty candidates,
$$ \delta_\mu(Q)\leq\beta $$
if and only if all candidates in $Q$ can receive their full quotas using approved augmented supply.

Proof. Every nonempty $S\subseteq Q$ has an approval union containing $h$. Therefore
$$ \mu'\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right) = \mu\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right)+\beta. $$

The minimum-cut calculation from Proposition 2 remains valid with augmented supplies: source-only cut capacity is still at most $1$, so capacity-$2$ arcs cannot cross a minimum cut.

Full approved quotas are feasible exactly when
$$ q|S|\leq \mu'\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right) $$
for every $S\subseteq Q$. For nonempty $S$, these inequalities are exactly
$$ q|S|-\mu\!\left(\bigcup_{c\in S}I_c\right)\leq\beta. $$
The empty-subset inequality is automatic. Proposition 2 now proves the equivalence. $\square$

For a committee with nonempty part $Q$ of size $t$, its original cost is
$$ \frac ek+\delta_\mu(Q). $$
Hence it meets threshold $b$ exactly when its nonempty candidates can all receive full approved quotas under the augmented supplies.

Reduction to equal-length preemptive scheduling

Define augmented integer cumulative coordinates
$$ U_i= N\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mu'_j = T_i+N\beta\,\mathbf 1[i\geq h]. $$
These are integers because
$$ N\beta=Nb-eD\in\mathbb Z. $$

For each nonempty candidate, create a job with:

  • release time $U_{l_c-1}$;
  • deadline $U_{r_c}$;
  • processing time $D$;
  • weight $1$.

There is a full approved assignment for some $t$ nonempty candidates exactly when at least $t$ jobs can be completed.

For the forward direction, scale an approved full-quota flow by $N$, take an integral flow, and partition augmented type blocks into job time units. Every selected job receives exactly $D$ units inside its window.

For the reverse direction, choose any $t$ completed jobs and count their execution in each augmented type block. These counts yield an approved assignment respecting augmented supplies and giving every chosen candidate $D/N=q$. Unused time units correspond to unused augmented supply.

Scheduling theorem and hypothesis checks

Baptiste, Chrobak, Dürr, Jawor, and Vakhania prove that maximum weighted throughput for preemptive single-machine jobs with a common positive integer processing time, integer releases and deadlines, and nonnegative integer weights is computable in $O(s^4)$ arithmetic operations, where $s$ is the number of jobs. [Theorem 2.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0209033)

Here processing time is the positive integer $D$, all releases and deadlines are integers, and every weight is $1$.

Discard jobs whose windows have length less than $D$. If fewer than $t$ jobs remain, reject immediately. This explicitly handles an empty retained job list. Otherwise, shift releases and deadlines by the minimum retained release time and apply the theorem. Accept exactly when maximum throughput is at least $t$.

The theorem’s tables are indexed by jobs and job counts. Their time calculations involve input coordinates and at most $O(m)$ multiples of $D$, so their integers have $O(B)$ bits. A conservative total bit-time bound is
$$ O\!\left((L+m\tau+m^4)B^2\right). $$

This proves polynomial-time decision for arbitrary rational $b$ under the common-type hypothesis.

5. Why the first algorithm does not extend directly

The endpoint-order hypothesis in Lemma 3 is essential to its exchange argument.

Take two candidates $a,b$, committee size $k=2$, and three types with
$$ A_1=\{a\},\qquad A_2=\{a,b\},\qquad A_3=\{a\}, $$
$$ \mu_1=\frac15,\qquad \mu_2=\frac15,\qquad \mu_3=\frac35. $$
In unscaled cumulative coordinates, the windows are
$$ a:[0,1),\qquad b:\left[\frac15,\frac25\right). $$

Processing $a$ greedily first assigns it $[0,1/2)$ and leaves no approved mass for $b$. This obtains approved mass $1/2$.

The optimum approved mass is instead $7/10$: give $b$ the entire middle type and give $a$ mass $1/2$ from the first and third types. This is optimal because $a$ contributes at most $1/2$ and $b$ at most $1/5$.

The exchange proof fails here because the later candidate’s deadline is earlier. Displaced execution cannot necessarily be moved to the first candidate’s later execution times.

This example refutes the unrestricted greedy extension; it supplies no hardness proof. It satisfies the common-type hypothesis and is covered by Section 4.
