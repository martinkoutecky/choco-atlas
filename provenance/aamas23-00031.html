The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finitely many complete two-agent team types \(q\) with rational masses \(\rho_q\), rational item-type supplies \(\nu_o\), category capacities, and additive utilities \(u_{q,i}(o)\), choose masses \(z_{q,P}\) over whole pair configurations \(P=(B_1,B_2)\) such that \(\sum_P z_{q,P}=\rho_q\) and \(\sum_{q,P}z_{q,P}(n_o(B_1)+n_o(B_2))=\nu_o\) for every item type \(o\). Require every used configuration to be \(\mathrm{EF[1,1]}\), or EF1 on same-sign instances, and output positive weights \(\lambda_{q,i}\) certifying that \(z\) maximizes total weighted utility over all feasible configuration-mass assignments, or report that no such assignment exists.

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) ordered two-agent team types \(q\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\rho_q\), normalized by \(\sum_q\rho_q=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; each type specifies both agents’ additive rational utilities \(u_{q,i}(o)\), which may be positive, negative, or zero.
There are \(m\geq1\) item types with positive binary-rational supplies \(\nu_o\), partitioned into \(k\) nonempty categories \(C_c\). Each category has a positive binary-encoded integer capacity \(s_c\), common to all agents, with \(\sum_{o\in C_c}\nu_o\leq2s_c\). Supplies are measured per unit mass of teams.
A configuration \(P=(B_1,B_2)\) consists of two finite multisets of whole item copies, allowing repeated item types, with \(\sum_{o\in C_c}n_o(B_i)\leq s_c\) for each \(i,c\). Counts are encoded in binary; configurations are implicit, and no inventory is prescribed separately for a team.
For type \(q\), a configuration is \(\mathrm{EF[1,1]}\) if, for each ordered pair \(i\neq j\), there are submultisets \(T\subseteq B_i\), \(G\subseteq B_j\), each of cardinality at most one, satisfying \(u_{q,i}(B_i\setminus T)\geq u_{q,i}(B_j\setminus G)\); if both are nonempty, their copies must belong to the same category. Empty removals are permitted.
An instance is same-sign if, for every \(q,i,c\), all \(u_{q,i}(o)\) in \(C_c\) are nonnegative or all are nonpositive. On these instances require mixed-goods-and-chores EF1 instead.

Partial resolution

The general problem with freely chosen positive weights remains unclassified. I prove a new polynomial-time case:

If $\tau=1$ and every supply $\nu_o$ is an integer, the requested pair always exists and can be found in $O(m^4)\operatorname{poly}(L)$ time, even with signed utilities and binary-encoded capacities.

The algorithm returns a single configuration. Its running time depends on the number of item types, rather than the possibly enormous number of item copies.

I also establish polynomial-size certificates and an exact exponential algorithm for the general problem, repairing the rational-point qualification identified in the verifier’s report.

1. Definitions and direct fairness tests

Write
$$ n_{i,o}(P)=n_o(B_i), \qquad a_o(P)=n_{1,o}(P)+n_{2,o}(P). $$

An assignment is feasible if it satisfies the prescribed type masses and supplies and uses capacity-feasible configurations. It is fair if every positively used configuration satisfies the fairness condition required by the instance.

Fix a type $q$, a configuration $P$, and an ordered pair $i\neq j$. Define
$$ D_{q,i}(P)=u_{q,i}(B_i)-u_{q,i}(B_j). $$
For each category $c$, let
$$ A_{q,i,c}(P) = \max\left( \{0\}\cup \{-u_{q,i}(o):o\in C_c,\ n_{i,o}(P)>0\} \right), $$
and
$$ G_{q,i,c}(P) = \max\left( \{0\}\cup \{u_{q,i}(o):o\in C_c,\ n_{j,o}(P)>0\} \right). $$

Removing an own item changes the utility difference by the negative of its utility; removing an item from the other bundle changes it by its utility. The included zero corresponds to an empty removal. Thus $\mathrm{EF[1,1]}$ for this ordered pair is equivalent to
$$ D_{q,i}(P) + \max_c\bigl(A_{q,i,c}(P)+G_{q,i,c}(P)\bigr) \geq 0. $$

Mixed-goods-and-chores EF1 is equivalent to
$$ D_{q,i}(P) + \max\left\{ \max_c A_{q,i,c}(P), \max_c G_{q,i,c}(P) \right\} \geq 0. $$

On a same-sign instance, each category has $A_{q,i,c}(P)=0$ or $G_{q,i,c}(P)=0$: nonnegative utilities preclude a beneficial own removal, and nonpositive utilities preclude a beneficial other-bundle removal. Consequently, the two displayed tests coincide.

These formulas give polynomial-time fairness tests with binary counts.

2. Unrestricted optimization and a necessary local property

2.1 A compact linear program

For fixed positive rational weights $\lambda$, define expected counts
$$ x_{q,i,o}=\sum_P z_{q,P}n_{i,o}(P). $$

Consider the linear program
$$ \max\ \sum_{q,i,o}\lambda_{q,i}u_{q,i}(o)x_{q,i,o} $$
subject to
$$ \sum_{q,i}x_{q,i,o}=\nu_o \quad\text{for every }o, $$
$$ \sum_{o\in C_c}x_{q,i,o}\leq \rho_qs_c \quad\text{for every }q,i,c, $$
and
$$ x_{q,i,o}\geq 0. $$

Proposition 1. The rational feasible points of this linear program are exactly the expected-count vectors of feasible rational configuration assignments.

Proof. An assignment gives a feasible vector by summing its supply and capacity equations.

Conversely, let $x$ be a rational feasible vector. For each $q$, put
$$ v_{q,i,o}=\frac{x_{q,i,o}}{\rho_q}. $$
For a fixed agent-category block, its vector satisfies
$$ v_o\geq 0, \qquad \sum_{o\in C_c}v_o\leq s_c. $$

Represent this vector by a distribution that chooses $s_ce_o$ with probability $v_o/s_c$ for each $o\in C_c$, and chooses the zero vector with the remaining probability. Every chosen vector has integer counts and respects capacity.

Couple the $2k$ block distributions using a common parameter in $[0,1]$: place each block’s choices on consecutive intervals having the prescribed probabilities. The union of all interval endpoints partitions $[0,1]$ into intervals on which every block choice is constant. Each interval therefore specifies one configuration.

There are at most $2m$ internal endpoints, hence at most $2m+1$ configurations. Their probabilities and counts are rationally representable in polynomial space. Multiplying their probabilities by $\rho_q$ gives the desired configuration masses. $\square$

The qualification “rational feasible points” is essential: irrational linear-programming points cannot be represented by finitely many rational masses.

The linear program is nonempty because
$$ x_{q,i,o}=\frac{\rho_q\nu_o}{2} $$
is feasible. It is bounded because
$$ x_{q,i,o}\leq \rho_qs_{c(o)}. $$

Standard rational linear programming therefore supplies a rational optimum in
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L+\ell_\lambda) $$
time, where $\ell_\lambda$ is the encoding length of the weights. Proposition 1 shows that its value is exactly the unrestricted assignment optimum.

2.2 Every used configuration is locally optimal

Proposition 2. Suppose $z$ is globally optimal for positive weights $\lambda$. Every positively used configuration of type $q$ maximizes
$$ \lambda_{q,1}u_{q,1}(B_1)+\lambda_{q,2}u_{q,2}(B_2) $$
among capacity-feasible divisions of its own inventory.

In particular,
$$ \lambda_{q,1}D_{q,1}(P) + \lambda_{q,2}D_{q,2}(P) \geq 0 $$
for every positively used configuration $P$.

Proof. Replacing a positive-mass configuration by another division of the same inventory preserves its type mass and every supply. An improvement of its weighted utility would therefore improve the global objective.

Swapping its two bundles is capacity-feasible because capacities are common to the agents. The difference between the current weighted utility and that of the swapped configuration is exactly the displayed expression. Local optimality makes it nonnegative. $\square$

Thus both agents cannot strictly envy each other in a used configuration. Society-wide optimality additionally permits inventories to move between configurations, so this local property alone does not settle the task.

3. A polynomial algorithm for one type and integer supplies

Assume throughout this section that $\tau=1$ and every $\nu_o$ is a positive integer. Since type masses sum to one, $\rho_1=1$. Suppress the type index.

The finite-inventory theorem guarantees fairness together with optimality for some positive weights; it does not guarantee fairness for arbitrary prescribed weights. This is the quantifier in [Shoshan, Hazon, and Segal-Halevi, Theorem 4.14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.07779). The following argument proves that, in the present integer-supply case, its weight-sweep approach can be implemented without expanding binary multiplicities.

3.1 Completing each category with dummy copies

For category $c$, define
$$ N_c=\sum_{o\in C_c}\nu_o. $$
Add one dummy type with zero utility for both agents and multiplicity
$$ 2s_c-N_c. $$
This is a nonnegative integer by the input hypothesis.

The augmented inventory now contains exactly $2s_c$ copies in category $c$. Give each agent exactly $s_c$ augmented copies from that category. Deleting dummy copies produces an original capacity-feasible allocation.

Dummy copies do not affect utilities or either fairness test: removing a dummy has the same utility effect as an empty removal.

There are at most
$$ M=m+k\leq 2m $$
augmented types. All multiplicities remain binary encoded.

3.2 Weighted optimization by sorting

Use unnormalized positive weights $(r,1)$, where $r>0$. For an augmented type $o$, define
$$ \delta_o(r)=r\,u_1(o)-u_2(o). $$

For a fixed category, the weighted utility equals a constant plus
$$ \sum_{o\in C_c}\delta_o(r)n_{1,o}. $$
Hence an optimal augmented allocation gives agent 1 the $s_c$ copies with largest $\delta_o(r)$ and gives agent 2 the remaining copies. This allocation is found by sorting types and consuming their binary multiplicities in order.

This greedy allocation is optimal even among fractional divisions of the fixed inventory. To verify this, choose a cutoff value $\alpha$ so that the greedy count vector $n_1$ fills every type with score greater than $\alpha$, uses no type with score less than $\alpha$, and possibly divides types with score $\alpha$. For every fractional feasible vector $y_1$,
$$ \sum_o n_{1,o}=\sum_o y_{1,o}=s_c. $$
Therefore
$$ \sum_o\delta_o(r)(n_{1,o}-y_{1,o}) = \sum_o(\delta_o(r)-\alpha)(n_{1,o}-y_{1,o}) \geq 0. $$
Each summand is nonnegative: above the cutoff, $n_{1,o}$ is the full multiplicity; below it, $n_{1,o}=0$; at the cutoff, the coefficient vanishes.

This also certifies optimality against assignments whose configurations have different inventories. Indeed, any feasible assignment has rational expected counts $x_{i,o}$ satisfying
$$ x_{1,o}+x_{2,o}=\nu_o, \qquad \sum_{o\in C_c}x_{i,o}\leq s_c. $$
Complete its expected counts with
$$ x_{i,\mathrm{dummy}(c)} = s_c-\sum_{o\in C_c}x_{i,o}. $$
These dummy counts are nonnegative and sum to $2s_c-N_c$. Thus the expected vector is a fractional division of the augmented fixed inventory. The greedy bound applies to it.

Consequently, every augmented greedy optimum, and every tied optimum constructed below, is globally optimal for the original configuration-mass problem.

3.3 Only polynomially many weight events occur

Two score lines in the same category can change order only at
$$ r= \frac{u_2(a)-u_2(b)}{u_1(a)-u_1(b)}, $$
when the denominator is nonzero and the ratio is positive.

There are at most $O(M^2)$ distinct positive crossing values. They have polynomial encoding length and can be sorted exactly.

Use a fixed order of type identifiers to break persistent ties.

For $r$ above every positive crossing, the ordering first prioritizes larger $u_1$. Thus agent 1 receives the top $s_c$ copies according to its own utility in every category. Pairing these top copies with the remaining copies shows
$$ u_1(B_1)\geq u_1(B_2). $$
The initial allocation is envy-free for agent 1.

For $r$ below every positive crossing, agent 1 receives the bottom $s_c$ copies according to $u_2$. Agent 2 therefore receives its top copies and is envy-free.

If there are no positive crossings, the greedy allocation is constant for all $r>0$ and has both endpoint properties.

3.4 Connecting consecutive greedy allocations

Consider a crossing value $r^\ast$. Let $n_1^+$ be the canonical greedy count vector immediately above it, and let $n_1^-$ be the vector immediately below it.

In each category, changes occur only within the score group that is tied at the selection cutoff at $r^\ast$. All higher groups remain fully selected, and all lower groups remain unselected. The number selected from the cutoff group is unchanged.

Therefore the transition can be performed by repeatedly choosing, in one category,

  • a type $a$ whose current agent-1 count exceeds its target count; and
  • a type $b$ whose current agent-1 count is below its target count,

and exchanging a copy of $a$ from agent 1 with a copy of $b$ from agent 2.

These types satisfy
$$ \delta_a(r^\ast)=\delta_b(r^\ast). $$
Every intermediate allocation remains optimal at weights $(r^\ast,1)$.

Moreover, put
$$ \Delta_1=u_1(a)-u_1(b), \qquad \Delta_2=u_2(a)-u_2(b). $$
Above $r^\ast$, the greedy ordering puts $a$ no later than $b$: some $a$ is selected while some $b$ remains unselected. Hence
$$ \Delta_1\geq 0. $$
The tie equality gives
$$ \Delta_2=r^\ast\Delta_1\geq 0. $$

Such an exchange changes the utility differences by
$$ D_1^{\mathrm{new}}=D_1^{\mathrm{old}}-2\Delta_1, \qquad D_2^{\mathrm{new}}=D_2^{\mathrm{old}}+2\Delta_2. $$

3.5 The first allocation fair for agent 2 is fair for both

For an augmented allocation $A$, abbreviate its $\mathrm{EF[1,1]}$ correction by
$$ R_i(A)=\max_c\bigl(A_{i,c}(A)+G_{i,c}(A)\bigr). $$

Lemma 3. Consider one tied exchange at positive weights $(r^\ast,1)$, with $\Delta_2=r^\ast\Delta_1$ as above. If the old allocation is not fair for agent 2, then the new allocation is fair for agent 1.

Proof. In the old allocation, agent 2 owns a copy of $b$ and the other agent owns a copy of $a$, in the same category. Therefore
$$ R_2(A^{\mathrm{old}}) \geq \max\{0,-u_2(b)\}+\max\{0,u_2(a)\} \geq \Delta_2. $$
Failure of fairness for agent 2 implies
$$ D_2^{\mathrm{old}}<-\Delta_2. $$

Suppose, for contradiction, that the new allocation fails fairness for agent 1. In that allocation, agent 1 owns $b$ and agent 2 owns $a$. Hence
$$ R_1(A^{\mathrm{new}}) \geq \max\{0,-u_1(b)\}+\max\{0,u_1(a)\} \geq \Delta_1, $$
and thus
$$ D_1^{\mathrm{new}}<-\Delta_1. $$
Using the exchange equation gives
$$ D_1^{\mathrm{old}} = D_1^{\mathrm{new}}+2\Delta_1 < \Delta_1. $$
It follows that
$$ r^\ast D_1^{\mathrm{old}}+D_2^{\mathrm{old}} < r^\ast\Delta_1-\Delta_2 = 0. $$
This contradicts weighted optimality of the old allocation: swapping its complete bundles is feasible, so Proposition 2 makes this expression nonnegative. $\square$

Start with the initial greedy allocation. If agent 2 is already fair, both agents are fair because agent 1 is envy-free.

Otherwise, follow the tied exchanges while decreasing $r$. The final greedy allocation is envy-free for agent 2, so there is a first exchange after which agent 2 is fair. Immediately before that exchange, agent 2 is unfair. Lemma 3 makes the resulting allocation fair for agent 1 as well.

On same-sign instances, Section 1 shows that the resulting $\mathrm{EF[1,1]}$ allocation is EF1.

3.6 Processing huge multiplicities in batches

It remains to avoid performing exponentially many single-copy exchanges.

For a chosen surplus type $a$ and deficit type $b$, exchange up to
$$ t= \min\left\{ n_{1,a}-n_{1,a}^{-}, \ n_{1,b}^{-}-n_{1,b} \right\} $$
copies. This positive integer is binary encoded.

After $h$ exchanges,
$$ D_2(h)=D_2(0)+2h\Delta_2. $$

For every integer
$$ 1\leq h\leq t-1, $$
the set of types present in each bundle is constant. The increasing counts are positive throughout this range, and the decreasing counts cannot reach zero before its endpoint. Consequently, $R_2(h)$ is a constant $R_2^\circ$ on this range.

For $t\geq 2$, compute $R_2^\circ$ at $h=1$. If $\Delta_2>0$, the least interior integer satisfying fairness is
$$ h= \max\left\{ 1,\ \left\lceil \frac{-R_2^\circ-D_2(0)}{2\Delta_2} \right\rceil \right\}, $$
provided it does not exceed $t-1$. If $\Delta_2=0$, either all interior integers satisfy fairness or none do. Check the endpoint $h=t$ separately, because a type may disappear there.

Thus the first fair allocation in a batch is found using a constant number of fairness evaluations and exact rational calculations. If no allocation in the batch is fair for agent 2, perform the whole batch and continue.

Each completed batch brings at least one type to its target count. Counts already at their targets are never changed again during that transition. There are therefore at most $O(M)$ batches per crossing value.

There are $O(M^2)$ crossing values, and a fairness evaluation takes $O(M)$ rational arithmetic operations. Including sorting and construction of the consecutive greedy vectors, the algorithm uses
$$ O(M^4)\operatorname{poly}(L) = O(m^4)\operatorname{poly}(L) $$
bit operations. Counts, crossing ratios, and intermediate utility sums all have polynomial encoding length.

Delete dummy copies from the returned allocation $P$. Output
$$ z_{1,P}=1, \qquad \lambda_{1,1}=\frac{r}{1+r}, \qquad \lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{1+r}, $$
where $r$ is the supporting ratio at which the algorithm stops.

This proves the announced polynomial-time result.

4. Polynomial certificates for the general problem

4.1 A compact optimality certificate

The dual of the compact linear program uses unrestricted item prices $p_o$ and nonnegative variables $t_{q,i,c}$, with constraints
$$ p_o+t_{q,i,c(o)} \geq \lambda_{q,i}u_{q,i}(o). $$
Its objective is
$$ \sum_o p_o\nu_o + \sum_{q,i,c}\rho_qs_ct_{q,i,c}. $$

For a capacity-feasible configuration, define its gap
$$ g_{q,P} = \sum_{i,c}s_ct_{q,i,c} - \sum_{i,o} \bigl(\lambda_{q,i}u_{q,i}(o)-p_o\bigr)n_{i,o}(P). $$
Equivalently,
$$ g_{q,P} = \sum_{i,c} t_{q,i,c} \left(s_c-\sum_{o\in C_c}n_{i,o}(P)\right) + \sum_{i,o} \bigl(p_o+t_{q,i,c(o)}-\lambda_{q,i}u_{q,i}(o)\bigr) n_{i,o}(P). $$
Every term is nonnegative, so $g_{q,P}\geq 0$.

For any feasible assignment,
$$ \text{dual objective}-\text{weighted utility} = \sum_{q,P}z_{q,P}g_{q,P}. $$
Thus dual feasibility and zero gaps on all positively used configurations certify unrestricted optimality.

Conversely, the compact primal is nonempty and bounded. Rational linear-programming strong duality supplies a rational optimal dual. Equality of objectives then forces every positive-mass gap to vanish.

4.2 Small supports and small weights

Proposition 4. Every yes-instance has a requested pair using at most $m+\tau$ configurations and having polynomial total encoding length.

Proof. Start with a requested pair and an optimal dual certificate. Restrict to its positively used configurations. They are fair and have zero gaps.

Their mass equations form
$$ Az=b, \qquad z\geq 0, $$
with $m+\tau$ rows. This polytope is nonempty and bounded, since every mass is bounded by its type mass. A basic feasible solution has at most
$$ \operatorname{rank}(A)\leq m+\tau $$
positive entries. Keeping these columns preserves fairness and optimality.

Fix the retained support $S$. To obtain small weights and dual variables, maximize $\epsilon$ subject to
$$ \epsilon\geq 0, \qquad \lambda_{q,i}\geq\epsilon, \qquad \sum_{q,i}\lambda_{q,i}=1, $$
the dual inequalities, and
$$ g_{q,P}=0 \quad\text{for every }(q,P)\in S. $$

The original positive weights make this system feasible with positive $\epsilon$. Normalization bounds its objective by $1/(2\tau)$. Its coefficients have polynomial encoding length: each configuration count is at most its binary-encoded category capacity.

The standard rational linear-programming theorem states that a feasible rational linear program with finite optimal value has a polynomial-size rational optimum and can be solved in polynomial time. Applying it here gives positive normalized weights and a polynomial-size dual certificate. The bounded mass system likewise has a polynomial-size rational basic solution. $\square$

Therefore the decision version of the requested free-weight problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. A verifier checks capacities, fairness, masses, supplies, positive normalized weights, dual feasibility, and zero gaps.

5. An exact algorithm for arbitrary instances

Put $d_c=|C_c|$. The number of capacity-feasible configurations is
$$ N= \prod_{c=1}^k \binom{s_c+d_c}{d_c}^{\,2}. $$
For one agent and category, the binomial coefficient counts nonnegative integer vectors whose sum is at most $s_c$; the square accounts for both agents.

Enumerate the fair labeled configurations $(q,P)$. For every support $S$ of size at most $m+\tau$, solve:

  • Its rational mass-and-supply feasibility system.
  • The weight-and-dual linear program from Proposition 4.

Accept the support exactly when the first system is feasible and the second has positive optimum $\epsilon$. Zero masses may be discarded. Zero gaps certify optimality for any feasible masses on the retained columns.

Proposition 4 proves completeness of this enumeration. Its running time is at most
$$ (\tau N+1)^{m+\tau} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$
Since
$$ N\leq \prod_c(s_c+1)^{2d_c}, $$
this is
$$ 2^{O\left((m+\tau) \left(\sum_c d_c\log(s_c+1)+\log(\tau+1)\right)\right)} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L), $$
and in particular
$$ 2^{O((m+\tau)(mL+\log(\tau+1)))} \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L). $$

The algorithm produces a requested pair or correctly reports nonexistence.

6. A polynomial class with arbitrary rational supplies

A second tractable class allows arbitrary $\tau$ and rational supplies.

Proposition 5. Suppose that for every type $q$ there is a positive rational number $\alpha_q$ such that
$$ u_{q,2}(o)=\alpha_q u_{q,1}(o) \quad\text{for every }o. $$
Then the search problem is solvable in
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,k,L) $$
time.

Proof. Choose weights proportional to
$$ \lambda_{q,1}=\alpha_q, \qquad \lambda_{q,2}=1, $$
then normalize all weights together. The two agents of each type now have identical weighted utility coefficients.

Solve the compact unrestricted linear program. Symmetrize its rational optimal solution by setting
$$ \bar x_{q,1,o} = \bar x_{q,2,o} = \frac{x_{q,1,o}+x_{q,2,o}}{2}. $$
Supplies are preserved. Each capacity inequality is preserved by averaging its two feasible left-hand sides. The objective is preserved because the corresponding weighted coefficients are equal.

For each $q$, decompose the rational common bundle vector
$$ v_{q,o}=\frac{\bar x_{q,1,o}}{\rho_q} $$
using the block construction in Proposition 1, now with only $k$ blocks. Use each resulting integer bundle twice, as $P=(B,B)$.

This realizes the optimal expected counts. Identical bundles are exactly envy-free for both agents, including with signed utilities. There are at most $m+1$ configurations per type.

The proportionality condition can be checked in polynomial time by taking a ratio at a nonzero utility coordinate and verifying all coordinates; if both utility vectors are zero, take $\alpha_q=1$. All subsequent steps use polynomial-time rational arithmetic and linear programming. $\square$

7. What remains open

The new integer-supply algorithm uses a fixed integral augmented inventory. Its greedy fractional optimum has integral counts, and tied exchanges preserve that inventory. For nonintegral supplies, this argument does not provide a supply-realizing distribution whose every configuration is fair.

With multiple team types, a society-wide optimum can also move inventory between types. The one-dimensional weight sweep above does not control those transfers.

Accordingly, the remaining target is to prove either a polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary rational supplies and arbitrary support size, or hardness for that exact problem with freely chosen positive weights. Hardness with prescribed weights does not establish this claim.
