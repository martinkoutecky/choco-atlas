Theorem 1.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational signal mass \(q>1/2\), an error threshold \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), and precision \(\xi>0\), consider a homogeneous population with mass \(q\) holding private signal \(1\) and mass \(1-q\) holding signal \(0\). Starting from an infinitesimal signal-seeded announcement that selects opinion \(1\), agents update asynchronously at rate one, follow the majority of announced mass, and break ties using their private signal. Compute, within \(\xi\), the least scaled time \(s\) at which the mass announcing \(1\) reaches \(1-\varepsilon\) and stays above that level.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) counts supported types, not possible types. Here total mass is \(1\), the \(m=2\) opinions are \(0,1\), and the \(\tau=2\) private-signal types have masses \(q\) and \(1-q\), with rational \(q\in(1/2,1)\).
Inputs also include rational \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\) and \(\xi>0\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Every agent observes the global announced masses; unannounced mass is ignored when comparing opinions. Agents may revise announcements, and ties follow their fixed private signal.
Define rate-one asynchronous mass evolution as follows. For \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), initially a fraction \(\alpha\) of each signal type announces its signal, so \(z_1^\alpha(0)=\alpha q\), \(z_0^\alpha(0)=\alpha(1-q)\), and the remaining mass is unannounced.
Let \(b(z)=1\) when \(z_1>z_0\), \(b(z)=0\) when \(z_1<z_0\), and \(b(z)=q\) at equality. The announced masses satisfy \(\frac{d z_1^\alpha}{ds}=b(z^\alpha)-z_1^\alpha\) and \(\frac{d z_0^\alpha}{ds}=1-b(z^\alpha)-z_0^\alpha\); unannounced mass is \(1-z_1^\alpha-z_0^\alpha\). Time \(s\) measures updates per unit population mass.
The infinitesimal signal seed means the pointwise limit \(z_j(s)=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow0}z_j^\alpha(s)\). Define \(s_* = \inf\{s\geq0: z_1(r)\geq1-\varepsilon\text{ for every }r\geq s\}\), with \(\inf\varnothing=+\infty\).

Is computing \(s_*\) to additive error \(\xi\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this approximation problem NP-hard?

For discrete asynchronous majority dynamics with initially unannounced agents and fixed signal bias \(\delta\in(0,1/10]\), on \(G(n,p)\) with \(p=o(1)\) and \(np/\log n\to\infty\), correct consensus occurs with probability tending to one exactly when every agent has first been selected, in \((1+o(1))n\log n\) rounds. Result

The stated approximation problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time. In fact,
\[ s_*=\log\frac1{\varepsilon}, \]
where \(\log\) is the natural logarithm. The value is independent of \(q\), provided \(q\in(1/2,1)\).

This conclusion concerns precisely the globally observed, infinitesimal-seed mass evolution specified in the question.

1. The positive seed preserves a strict majority

Fix \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and define
\[ d^\alpha(s)=z_1^\alpha(s)-z_0^\alpha(s). \]
Initially,
\[ d^\alpha(0)=\alpha(2q-1)>0. \]
Whenever \(d^\alpha(s)>0\), the update rule gives \(b(z^\alpha(s))=1\). On such an interval, the equations become
\[ \frac{dz_1^\alpha}{ds}=1-z_1^\alpha, \qquad \frac{dz_0^\alpha}{ds}=-z_0^\alpha. \]
Solving these linear equations with the given initial conditions yields
\[ z_1^\alpha(s)=1-(1-\alpha q)e^{-s}, \qquad z_0^\alpha(s)=\alpha(1-q)e^{-s}. \]
Consequently,
\[ d^\alpha(s)=1-\bigl(1-\alpha(2q-1)\bigr)e^{-s}>0 \]
for every \(s\geq0\).

To justify that these formulas describe the entire evolution, suppose a continuous solution first reached equality at a finite time \(T\). Before \(T\), its difference is positive, so the linear equations force the displayed formulas. Continuity then gives
\[ d^\alpha(T)=1-\bigl(1-\alpha(2q-1)\bigr)e^{-T}>0, \]
contradicting equality. Thus equality is never reached. The displayed formulas also directly satisfy the original equations and initial conditions, establishing existence and uniqueness.

Their sum is
\[ z_1^\alpha(s)+z_0^\alpha(s)=1-(1-\alpha)e^{-s}, \]
so the unannounced mass is \((1-\alpha)e^{-s}\), as required.

2. The infinitesimal-seed limit and its hitting time

Taking the prescribed pointwise limit gives, for every \(s\geq0\),
\[ z_1(s)=1-e^{-s}, \qquad z_0(s)=0. \]

There is a boundary subtlety: at \(s=0\), the limiting masses are equal, but the limiting curve’s right derivative is \((1,0)\), whereas evaluating the vector field at equality gives \((q,1-q)\). This does not invalidate the calculation: the task defines the evolution by the limit of positive-seed trajectories, rather than by imposing the differential equation classically at the zero-seed initial state.

The function \(z_1(s)=1-e^{-s}\) is strictly increasing. Therefore, for any \(s\geq0\), the condition \(z_1(r)\geq1-\varepsilon\) for every \(r\geq s\) is equivalent to the same inequality at \(r=s\). That inequality is equivalent to
\[ e^{-s}\leq\varepsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\geq\log\frac1\varepsilon. \]
Equality satisfies the threshold condition. Hence
\[ s_*=\log\frac1\varepsilon. \]

3. A polynomial-time approximation algorithm

An explicit algorithm establishes polynomial bit complexity, without treating logarithm evaluation as a unit-cost operation.

Write \(\varepsilon=a/b\), where \(a,b\) are positive binary-encoded integers and \(a<b\). Put \(x=b/a\). Find the integer \(k\geq0\) satisfying
\[ 2^k\leq x<2^{k+1}. \]
This requires only integer comparisons. Since the input has total bit length \(L\), we have \(k\leq L\).

Define
\[ y=\frac{x}{2^k}\in[1,2), \qquad u=\frac{y-1}{y+1}\in[0,1/3). \]
Then
\[ s_*=k\log2+\log y. \]

For \(0\leq v\leq1/3\), define the rational partial sum
\[ S_N(v)=2\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{v^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. \]
The geometric series for \(1/(1-t^2)\) converges uniformly on \([0,v]\). Integrating it term by term gives
\[ \log\frac{1+v}{1-v} = 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{v^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. \]
The omitted tail satisfies
\[ 0\leq \log\frac{1+v}{1-v}-S_N(v) \leq 2\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}v^{2j+1} = \frac{2v^{2N+1}}{1-v^2} \leq \frac34\,9^{-N} \leq9^{-N}. \]

Since \((1+u)/(1-u)=y\), and \((1+1/3)/(1-1/3)=2\), the exact rational number
\[ R=kS_N(1/3)+S_N(u) \]
satisfies
\[ 0\leq s_*-R\leq(k+1)9^{-N}. \]

Choose \(N=2L+2\). Every positive input rational, including \(\xi\), is at least \(2^{-L}\), because its positive integer numerator is at least \(1\) and its denominator has at most \(L\) bits. Using \(k+1\leq L+1\leq2^{L+1}\), we obtain
\[ (k+1)9^{-N} \leq 2^{L+1}2^{-(2L+2)} = 2^{-(L+1)} \leq\frac{\xi}{2}. \]

Finally, output the dyadic rational
\[ \widehat{s} = 2^{-(L+1)} \left\lfloor2^{L+1}R\right\rfloor. \]
Its rounding error is less than \(2^{-(L+1)}\leq\xi/2\). Thus
\[ 0\leq s_*-\widehat{s} = (s_*-R)+(R-\widehat{s}) \leq\xi. \]

All these computations take polynomial time:

  • The numerator and denominator of \(u=(b-a2^k)/(b+a2^k)\) have \(O(L)\) bits.
  • There are \(N=O(L)\) terms. Each term has numerator and denominator of \(O(NL+\log N)\) bits.
  • Even summing without fraction reduction produces intermediate integers of at most \(O(N^2L+N\log N)=O(L^3)\) bits.
  • Integer arithmetic and the final division therefore require polynomially many bit operations.

This proves polynomial time in \(L\), and hence in \(m,\tau,L\) for the stipulated \(m=\tau=2\).
