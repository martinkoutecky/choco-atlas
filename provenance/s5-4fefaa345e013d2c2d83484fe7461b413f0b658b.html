The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite bipartite graph of types with positive rational masses summing to \(1\), strict rankings of compatible counterpart types, and rational costs per type pair, represent agents of each type by an atomless cohort. Find a minimum-cost measurable one-to-one matching \(M\) such that for every alternative measurable matching \(N\) on the same population, the mass of agents who strictly prefer their partner type in \(M\) to their partner type in \(N\) is at least the mass who strictly prefer \(N\). Treat being unmatched as last; compare types rather than identities within a type.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=(A\cup B,E)\) be a finite bipartite graph of compatible types, with \(m=|E|\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=|A\cup B|\) positive-mass types, each with binary-rational mass \(s_t>0\), where \(\sum_t s_t=1\). Support size means the number of listed types, not the number of possible types; the two sides need not have equal total mass.
Each type strictly ranks its compatible counterpart types. All members of a type share this ranking, are indifferent between identities of the same counterpart type, and rank being unmatched strictly last. Costs \(c_{ab}\in\mathbb Q\) may have either sign.
Represent type \(t\) by a disjoint interval \(I_t=[0,s_t)\) with Lebesgue measure; write \(\mu\) for the resulting population measure. A matching is a measurable, measure-preserving bijection between measurable subsets of the two sides, with measurable inverse, using only compatible type pairs; agents outside these subsets are unmatched. Arbitrary measurable subdivisions of cohorts are allowed.
For matchings \(M,N\) on this same population, let \(\phi(M,N)\) be the \(\mu\)-mass of agents strictly preferring their partner type in \(M\) to their partner type in \(N\). Call \(M\) popular if \(\phi(M,N)\ge\phi(N,M)\) for every matching \(N\). Let \(x_{ab}(M)\) be the mass paired between types \(a,b\), and define \(C(M)=\sum_{(a,b)\in E}c_{ab}x_{ab}(M)\), counting each pair once and charging nothing for unmatched agents.
The minimum-cost problem has rational-threshold decision version: given \(K\in\mathbb Q\), does a popular matching \(M\) with \(C(M)\le K\) exist? Let \(L\) be the total binary input length. Let \(p\) count connected components containing at least four types in the graph retaining exactly the edges carrying positive mass in some popular matching.

Is this continuous minimum-cost popular matching problem, in its rational-threshold decision version, in \(\mathrm P\) or \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, is it FPT parameterized by \(p\), with running time \(f(p)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\)?

Discrete minimum-cost popular matching is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with costs in \(\{0,1\}\). [Faenza et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.11473)
On \(n\) discrete agents, an exact algorithm takes \(O^*(2^{n/4})\) time, more precisely \(2^{p_d}\operatorname{poly}(n,L)\), where \(p_d\) counts components of at least four vertices in the graph of edges belonging to some popular matching. [Kavitha](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol182-fsttcs2020/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.25/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.25.pdf)
A related unit-capacity fractional model with adversarial pairings admits polynomial-time maximum-size optimization. [Csáji](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.16173) No result settling the exact atomless minimum-cost problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made atomless cohorts concrete as disjoint Lebesgue intervals and required measurable pairings to preserve mass, so both endpoints of a paired cohort have equal mass.
  • Defined total cost as cost per type pair times paired mass, counting pairs once and assigning zero cost to unmatched agents; retained arbitrary signed rational costs.
  • Used the rational-threshold decision version to give the exact minimum-cost question a finite-input complexity target without assuming that a popular matching exists.
  • Extended the discrete structural parameter by retaining type pairs used with positive mass by some popular matching and counting components containing at least four support types.

Partial classification and additional structure

The rational-threshold decision problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete, even with equal dyadic type masses and costs in $\{0,1\}$. For equal masses, the requested running time $2^p\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$ follows from the discrete algorithm.

For arbitrary rational masses, I do not establish or refute fixed-parameter tractability in $p$. I prove two additional results that bear on that question:

  • Status rigidity: if a type has two positive statuses in one popular matching, those are its only possible statuses across all popular matchings, including unmatched status.
  • Uniqueness when $p=0$: there is at most one popular type-pair mass vector. Given the exact popular-edge union graph, this vector and its popularity can be checked in polynomial time.

The proofs below include the finite formulation and complexity arguments needed to make these results self-contained.

1. A finite formulation of measurable popularity

Write $\bot$ for unmatched status. For compatible partner types or unmatched status, define
$$ v_t(j,i)= \begin{cases} 1,&j\succ_t i,\\ 0,&j=i,\\ -1,&i\succ_t j. \end{cases} $$

Thus $v_t(j,i)$ is an agent’s vote when its status changes from $i$ to $j$.

Fix a measurable matching $M$. Let $q_{t,i}$ be the mass of type $t$ having status $i$ in $M$. In particular,
$$ q_{a,b}=q_{b,a}=x_{ab}(M), \qquad q_{t,\bot}=s_t-\sum_{e\ni t}x_e(M). $$
Let
$$ R_t(M)=\{i:q_{t,i}>0\}. $$

A challenger can match mass $z_{a,i;b,j}$ between old cohorts $(a,i)$ and $(b,j)$, provided $(a,b)\in E$. Let $h_{t,i}$ be the old cohort’s mass left unmatched by the challenger. Its feasibility constraints are
$$ \sum_{b:(a,b)\in E}\sum_{j\in R_b(M)} z_{a,i;b,j}+h_{a,i}=q_{a,i}, $$
$$ \sum_{a:(a,b)\in E}\sum_{i\in R_a(M)} z_{a,i;b,j}+h_{b,j}=q_{b,j}, $$
with all variables nonnegative.

Its voting advantage over $M$ is
$$ \sum_{(a,b)\in E} \sum_{\substack{i\in R_a(M)\\j\in R_b(M)}} \bigl(v_a(b,i)+v_b(a,j)\bigr)z_{a,i;b,j} + \sum_t\sum_{i\in R_t(M)}v_t(\bot,i)h_{t,i}. $$

Lemma 1. This linear program describes exactly the attainable measurable challenger scores.

Proof. Every measurable challenger induces the displayed variables and constraints.

Conversely, subdivide each old cohort into measurable pieces with the prescribed masses. Such subdivisions exist because Lebesgue measure is atomless. For each positive pair variable, match its two pieces by a measure-preserving bijection, leaving the pieces prescribed by the $h$ variables unmatched.

The measure-theoretic fact used here is that two measurable subsets of intervals with the same positive Lebesgue measure are measure-isomorphic after removing null subsets. One construction uses the cumulative-measure functions
$$ F_S(u)=\mu(S\cap[0,u]). $$
After replacing $S$ by a Borel representative and deleting a null set, this map and its generalized inverse identify $S$ with an interval of length $\mu(S)$. Flat intervals of $F_S$ meet $S$ in measure zero; there are only countably many nontrivial flat intervals. Composing the resulting identifications for the two pieces gives the required bijection with measurable inverse. Removed null sets may remain unmatched.

There are finitely many pieces, so their bijections combine into a feasible measurable matching. Its score is the stated objective. $\square$

The baseline $M$ is feasible in this program and has score zero. Consequently,
$$ M\text{ is popular} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the maximum challenger score is zero}. $$

In particular, popularity depends only on the type-pair mass vector: any two realizations of the same vector have identical cohort masses and identical challenger programs.

2. Integral cohort witnesses

The dual program has a variable $\alpha_{t,i}$ for every positive old cohort. It minimizes
$$ \sum_t\sum_{i\in R_t(M)}q_{t,i}\alpha_{t,i} $$
subject to
$$ \alpha_{a,i}+\alpha_{b,j} \ge v_a(b,i)+v_b(a,j) $$
for every compatible edge and every pair of positive old cohorts at its endpoints, together with
$$ \alpha_{t,i}\ge v_t(\bot,i). $$

Lemma 2. A matching $M$ is popular if and only if there are labels
$$ \alpha_{t,i}\in\{-1,0,1\} $$
satisfying these inequalities and
$$ \alpha_{a,b}+\alpha_{b,a}=0 \quad\text{whenever }x_{ab}(M)>0, $$
$$ \alpha_{t,\bot}=0 \quad\text{whenever }q_{t,\bot}>0. $$

Proof. Suppose first that $M$ is popular. The primal program is feasible and bounded, and its optimum is zero. Linear-programming duality therefore gives an optimal dual solution of objective zero.

The baseline uses positive pair variables between cohorts $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ whenever $x_{ab}(M)>0$. Its corresponding edge score is zero. It also uses its positive unmatched variables, whose scores are zero. Complementary slackness therefore gives the two displayed equalities.

The dual constraint matrix is totally unimodular. Indeed, before transposition, every pair-variable column is the incidence vector of an edge in the bipartite graph of old cohorts, and every unmatched-variable column is a singleton column. Signing the rows on one side converts the pair columns into directed incidence columns. Transposition and adjoining singleton rows preserve total unimodularity. All right-hand sides are integers.

There is an optimal vertex even if the cohort masses are irrational. Every retained $q_{t,i}$ is positive, and every dual variable is at least $-1$. On the optimal face,
$$ \sum_{t,i}q_{t,i}\alpha_{t,i}=0. $$
Thus each variable is also bounded above, since
$$ q_{t,i}\alpha_{t,i} \le \sum_{(u,j)\ne(t,i)}q_{u,j}. $$
The optimal face is nonempty and compact, and hence has an extreme point. An extreme point of this optimal face is also an extreme point of the dual feasible region. Total unimodularity makes it integral.

Every matched-cohort label is at least $-1$. Its baseline counterpart also has label at least $-1$, and their labels sum to zero. Hence each matched-cohort label is at most $1$. Unmatched-cohort labels are zero. This proves the asserted label range.

Conversely, suppose the labels exist. Their dual objective is
$$ \sum_{(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}(M) \bigl(\alpha_{a,b}+\alpha_{b,a}\bigr) + \sum_tq_{t,\bot}\alpha_{t,\bot} =0. $$
Weak duality bounds every challenger’s score by zero. Thus $M$ is popular. $\square$

3. Polynomial certificates and exact finite optimization

A witness pattern specifies:

  • For each edge, either absence or an endpoint label from $\{-1,0,1\}$, with the opposite label at the other endpoint.
  • For each type, whether unmatched status is permitted, with label zero.

The selected statuses must satisfy all pair and singleton witness inequalities. There are polynomially many such inequalities: an edge needs at most $(\tau+1)^2$ cohort-pair checks.

For a valid pattern, consider the rational constraints
$$ x_e\ge0,\qquad x_e=0\text{ on absent edges}, $$
$$ u_t=s_t-\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\ge0, $$
$$ u_t=0\text{ when unmatched status is not permitted}, $$
and, for the decision problem,
$$ \sum_ec_ex_e\le K. $$

Every feasible vector is popular. Selected statuses with zero mass can simply be deleted; the remaining labels still satisfy Lemma 2. Conversely, every popular matching of cost at most $K$ supplies a feasible pattern.

Each pattern defines a bounded rational polytope. If nonempty, it has a rational vertex of polynomial encoding length. To see the bit bound, clear denominators in a vertex-defining subsystem and apply Cramer’s rule. Its dimension and coefficient bit lengths are polynomial in the input length, and the determinant bound gives polynomial bit lengths for the resulting coordinates.

A rational feasible mass vector and its witness therefore constitute a polynomial-size, polynomially checkable certificate. Hence the decision problem belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$.

There are at most
$$ 4^m2^\tau $$
patterns. Enumerating them and solving their linear programs gives an exact algorithm with running time
$$ 4^m2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$

Without the threshold constraint, these are compact polytopes. Their finite union is exactly the set of popular mass vectors. Thus, whenever popular matchings exist, minimum cost is attained by a rational mass vector. This holds for costs of either sign.

4. Equal masses: exact transfer to the discrete problem

Suppose every type has mass $1/T$, where $T=\tau$. Interpret each type as one discrete agent with the same strict preferences.

Lemma 3. If $M$ is continuously popular, then $T x(M)$ is a convex combination of incidence vectors of discrete popular matchings.

Proof. Put $y=T x(M)$. Then
$$ y_e\ge0,\qquad \sum_{e\ni t}y_e\le1. $$
The bipartite matching polytope is integral: its constraint matrix is totally unimodular and its capacities are integers. Therefore
$$ y=\sum_k\lambda_k\chi^{D_k}, \qquad \lambda_k>0,\qquad \sum_k\lambda_k=1, $$
for discrete matchings $D_k$.

Every edge of a constituent $D_k$ has positive mass in $M$. If $D_k$ leaves $t$ unmatched, then $M$ has positive unmatched mass at $t$. Otherwise $y$ would have degree $1$ at $t$, whereas averaging a constituent of degree zero with constituents of degree at most $1$ would give degree strictly below $1$.

Choose a witness for $M$ from Lemma 2. For a constituent $D_k$, let $i_k(t)$ be its partner or unmatched status, and set
$$ \beta_t=\alpha_{t,i_k(t)}. $$
Every chosen status is a positive status of $M$. Opposite labels on the edges of $D_k$ and zero labels on its unmatched vertices give
$$ \sum_t\beta_t=0. $$

For every compatible edge,
$$ \beta_a+\beta_b \ge v_a(b,i_k(a))+v_b(a,i_k(b)), $$
and
$$ \beta_t\ge v_t(\bot,i_k(t)). $$
Summing the relevant inequalities over any discrete challenger counts each vertex once. Its advantage over $D_k$ is at most $\sum_t\beta_t=0$. Thus $D_k$ is popular. $\square$

Lemma 4. Every discrete popular matching lifts to a continuously popular matching by matching entire type intervals along its edges.

Proof. In this baseline, each type has one status. A continuous challenger’s score is therefore a linear function of its normalized pair masses and unmatched masses.

Its normalized pair vector lies in the bipartite matching polytope and decomposes into discrete matchings. Each constituent has nonpositive score against the discrete popular baseline. Their weighted average, multiplied by $1/T$, is the continuous challenger’s score and is also nonpositive. $\square$

Consequently,
$$ \min_{M\text{ continuously popular}} C(M) = \frac1T \min_{D\text{ discretely popular}}\sum_{e\in D}c_e. $$

Faenza et al. establish $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness of deciding whether a strict-preference bipartite instance has a popular matching avoiding two specified edges. Assigning cost $1$ to those edges and cost $0$ elsewhere makes this precisely the cost-zero decision problem. See Section 5.2 and Corollary 2 of [their paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.11473).

Pad such an instance with isolated vertices until its number $T$ of vertices is a power of two. Padding adds fewer than the original number of vertices and preserves popularity and costs: isolated vertices are unmatched in every matching and cast no comparative votes. Give every type mass $1/T$ and use threshold zero. The masses are dyadic, and the construction has polynomial binary length.

Lemmas 3 and 4 prove both directions of this reduction. Together with Section 3, they establish $\mathrm{NP}$-completeness under the stated restrictions.

The same lemmas identify the discrete and continuous popular-edge union graphs in the equal-mass case. Every positive continuous edge occurs in a discrete popular constituent, and every discrete popular edge occurs in its continuous lift. Thus $p=p_d$.

Kavitha’s algorithm applies to bipartite instances with strict preferences and arbitrary edge costs, and runs in $2^{p_d}\operatorname{poly}(T,L)$ time. Its parameter counts exactly the components of at least four vertices in the discrete popular-edge union graph. These hypotheses hold here. Scaling the threshold to $TK$ therefore gives the equal-mass bound
$$ 2^p\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
See [Kavitha’s theorem and algorithm](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol182-fsttcs2020/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.25/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.25.pdf).

5. Common tightness between popular matchings

Assume at least one popular matching exists. Define
$$ H=(A\cup B,E_{\mathrm{pop}}), $$
where $E_{\mathrm{pop}}$ consists of edges carrying positive mass in some popular matching. Also define the globally possible status set
$$ S_t=\bigcup_{N\text{ popular}}R_t(N). $$
This includes $\bot$ exactly when some popular matching leaves positive mass of type $t$ unmatched.

Lemma 5. Fix a popular matching $M$ and any witness $\alpha$ from Lemma 2.

For every $(a,b)\in E_{\mathrm{pop}}$ and every $i\in R_a(M)$, $j\in R_b(M)$,
$$ \alpha_{a,i}+\alpha_{b,j} = v_a(b,i)+v_b(a,j). $$
If $\bot\in S_t$, then for every $i\in R_t(M)$,
$$ \alpha_{t,i}=v_t(\bot,i). $$

Proof. Choose a popular mass vector $x(N)$ with $x_{ab}(N)>0$. By Lemma 1, every realization of that vector is popular.

Fix the two old cohorts $(a,i)$ and $(b,j)$. Choose
$$ 0<\varepsilon< \min\{q_{a,i},q_{b,j},x_{ab}(N)\}. $$
There is a realization $\widehat N$ of $x(N)$ matching mass $\varepsilon$ between these old cohorts. After reserving this piece, distribute all remaining target partner-status masses among the remaining old cohorts. The required totals agree separately at every type, so atomless subdivisions and pairwise measure isomorphisms realize the remainder.

Since both $M$ and $\widehat N$ are popular, their voting difference is zero:
$$ \phi(\widehat N,M)-\phi(M,\widehat N)=0. $$

For the challenger variables induced by $\widehat N$, the difference between the dual objective and the primal objective is
$$ \sum_{a,i;b,j}z_{a,i;b,j} \bigl( \alpha_{a,i}+\alpha_{b,j} -v_a(b,i)-v_b(a,j) \bigr) + \sum_{t,i}h_{t,i} \bigl(\alpha_{t,i}-v_t(\bot,i)\bigr). $$
Every summand is nonnegative. Both objectives are zero, so every summand having positive coefficient has zero slack. The reserved pair variable is positive, proving the first equality.

If some popular vector has positive unmatched mass at $t$, similarly realize a positive portion of that unmatched mass inside any prescribed old cohort $(t,i)$. Its singleton slack must vanish, proving the second equality. $\square$

An immediate consequence is that, for every $j\in S_t$,
$$ \alpha_{t,i}-v_t(j,i) $$
is independent of $i\in R_t(M)$. For a matched status $j$, fix any positive old cohort at type $j$ in the first equality of Lemma 5. For unmatched status, use its second equality.

6. Status rigidity

Theorem 6. Every type has at most two positive statuses in a popular matching. Moreover, if
$$ R_t(M)=\{h,l\}, \qquad h\succ_t l, $$
for some popular matching $M$, then
$$ S_t=\{h,l\}. $$

Proof. First suppose there were three positive statuses
$$ i_1\succ_t i_2\succ_t i_3. $$
Using the independence established after Lemma 5 with $j=i_1$ gives
$$ \alpha_{t,i_2}=\alpha_{t,i_1}+1, \qquad \alpha_{t,i_3}=\alpha_{t,i_1}+1. $$
Using it with $j=i_2$ gives
$$ \alpha_{t,i_3}=\alpha_{t,i_2}+1, $$
a contradiction. This includes the case $i_3=\bot$.

Now suppose $R_t(M)=\{h,l\}$ with $h\succ_t l$. Taking $j=h$ gives
$$ \alpha_{t,l}-\alpha_{t,h}=1. $$
For any globally possible status $j\in S_t$, independence gives
$$ v_t(j,l)-v_t(j,h)=1. $$

Strict rankings allow us to compute this difference explicitly:
$$ v_t(j,l)-v_t(j,h)= \begin{cases} 0,&j\succ_t h\text{ or }l\succ_t j,\\ 1,&j=h\text{ or }j=l,\\ 2,&h\succ_t j\succ_t l. \end{cases} $$
Therefore $j$ must equal $h$ or $l$. Both already occur in $M$, so $S_t=\{h,l\}$. $\square$

Several consequences concern the union graph, rather than merely one matching:

  • A type split between two partner types in one popular matching has degree exactly two in $H$ and is fully matched in measure in every popular matching.
  • A type having both a partner and unmatched status in one popular matching has degree exactly one in $H$.
  • If $|S_t|\ge3$, every popular matching assigns all mass of $t$ to one status.

These conclusions do not require equal masses.

7. Complete classification on a star

Consider a compatibility graph that is a star, with center $b$ of mass $S$ and leaves $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ of masses $s_1,\ldots,s_r$. The center ranks them in this order:
$$ a_1\succ_b a_2\succ_b\cdots\succ_b a_r. $$
Each leaf accepts only the center.

Define the greedy vector
$$ x_i^{\mathrm{gr}} = \min\left\{ s_i,\, \max\left\{ S-\sum_{j<i}s_j,\, 0 \right\} \right\}. $$
Let
$$ u_b^{\mathrm{gr}} = \max\left\{S-\sum_i s_i,0\right\}, $$
and let
$$ k=\bigl|\{i:x_i^{\mathrm{gr}}>0\}\bigr|. $$

Theorem 7. A popular matching on the star exists exactly when
$$ k+\mathbf 1_{\{u_b^{\mathrm{gr}}>0\}}\le2. $$
Whenever one exists, its type-pair mass vector is uniquely $x^{\mathrm{gr}}$.

Proof: necessity and uniqueness. A popular matching cannot leave positive mass unmatched at both the center and a leaf. Matching a small amount of these cohorts improves both and harms nobody. Thus its total matched mass is
$$ \min\left\{S,\sum_i s_i\right\}. $$

Suppose $i<j$, leaf $a_i$ has positive unmatched mass, and $x_j>0$. Take a small amount $\varepsilon$ of center agents currently paired with $a_j$. Match them instead to unmatched agents of $a_i$, leaving their former $a_j$ partners unmatched.

The center agents and the new leaf agents each gain mass $\varepsilon$ of votes; the former partners lose mass $\varepsilon$. The challenger’s net advantage is $\varepsilon>0$. Hence
$$ x_j>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_i=s_i\text{ for every }i<j. $$
Together with the total matched mass, this forces $x=x^{\mathrm{gr}}$.

The center has $k$ positive partner statuses and an additional unmatched status exactly when $u_b^{\mathrm{gr}}>0$. Theorem 6 proves the necessary bound.

Proof: sufficiency. It remains to construct witnesses whenever the bound holds.

If the star has no edges, unmatched labels zero suffice.

If the center has positive unmatched mass, the bound permits only one leaf. That leaf is fully matched. Give the center’s matched cohort label $-1$, its unmatched cohort label zero, and the leaf label $1$. The pair inequalities are equalities with right-hand sides zero or $1$, and the singleton inequalities hold.

Suppose the center is fully matched and has one partner, necessarily $a_1$. Give the center label $1$, the matched leaf cohort label $-1$, and every unmatched leaf cohort label zero. On the used edge, the pair score is zero against a matched leaf cohort and $1$ against an unmatched leaf cohort. The corresponding label sums are zero and $1$. On any unused edge, the center loses a vote and the unmatched leaf gains one, so the score is zero and the label sum is $1$. All singleton inequalities hold.

Finally, suppose the center is fully matched with two partners, $a_1$ and $a_2$. The first leaf is fully matched. Give the center labels zero and $1$ on its $a_1$ and $a_2$ cohorts, respectively. Give the matched leaf cohorts labels zero at $a_1$ and $-1$ at $a_2$, and all unmatched leaf cohorts label zero.

For the edge to $a_1$, the scores and label sums are respectively zero and $1$ for the two center cohorts. For the edge to $a_2$, against its matched cohort they are respectively $-1$ and zero; against its unmatched cohort, when present, they are zero and $1$. Every unused leaf is ranked below both current partners, so its pair score is zero and its label sum is nonnegative. The singleton inequalities also hold.

In every case, used edges have opposite labels and unmatched cohorts have label zero. Lemma 2 proves popularity. $\square$

Therefore, when every compatibility component is a star, arbitrary-mass existence and minimum cost are polynomial-time solvable: compute the unique greedy vector in each component and apply Theorem 7. Popularity decomposes over compatibility components because challenger scores add, and a challenger in one component can be extended by the baseline elsewhere.

The nonexistence example from the notes is an immediate instance. A center of mass $1/2$ with three leaves of masses $1/4,1/8,1/8$ must use all three partner statuses in the greedy vector, so no popular matching exists. The argument concerns positive masses; it never requires matching every agent in a null set.

8. What follows when $p=0$

Theorem 8. If $p=0$, there is at most one popular type-pair mass vector.

Proof. Every component of $H$ has at most three vertices. Since $H$ is bipartite, its components are isolated vertices, single edges, or two-edge stars.

Any popular matching uses only edges of $H$. Its restriction to a component must be popular against challengers using that component’s edges: otherwise extend a winning component challenger by the original matching elsewhere.

Theorem 7 gives at most one popular mass vector on each such component. Hence all globally popular matchings have the same type-pair vector. $\square$

The vector is explicit when $H$ is known. On a single-edge component $(a,b)$,
$$ x_{ab}=\min\{s_a,s_b\}. $$
On a three-vertex component with center $b$ ranking leaf $h$ above leaf $l$, both edges belong to $H$. The star theorem therefore forces
$$ s_h<s_b\le s_h+s_l, $$
and
$$ x_{hb}=s_h, \qquad x_{lb}=s_b-s_h. $$
Isolated types have no matched mass.

Thus, given the exact graph $H$ with $p=0$, one can reconstruct the only possible vector, check its popularity against the original compatibility graph using Section 1, and compare its cost with $K$, all in polynomial time. The final popularity check accounts for challengers using edges outside $H$.

This is not an algorithm for discovering $H$ from the original input. The uniqueness statement alone does not supply such an algorithm.

9. Remaining parameter question

The unparameterized decision problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete, and the equal-mass case has the requested parameterized algorithm.

For arbitrary rational masses, the new rigidity theorem controls every type that splits in a popular matching. It also shows that components of at most three types contribute no ambiguity to the popular mass vector once the union graph is known.

What remains unproved is an algorithm with running time
$$ f(p)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
for the original arbitrary-mass input. In particular, neither polynomial-time recovery of its popular-edge union graph nor suitable optimization within its large components has been established here. The finite witness enumeration depends exponentially on $m$ and $\tau$, which can be arbitrarily large even when $p=1$.
