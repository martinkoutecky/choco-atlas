The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a rational row-stochastic confidence matrix \(Q\), true opinions \(\hat x_t\in[0,1]\), positive costs \(c_t\), a threshold \(x^*\), and a specified recurrent class \(K\), find the minimum per-capita intervention cost and an attaining intervention \((\delta_t)_{t\in K}\), subject to \(0\le\delta_t\le1-\hat x_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in K}\pi_t^{(K)}(\hat x_t+\delta_t)\ge x^*\), where \(\pi^{(K)}\) is the normalized stationary distribution of \(Q\) on \(K\).

Result

The exact minimum and an attaining intervention are computable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The budget decision problem therefore belongs to \(\mathrm P\).

The mass-weighted formulation admits a direct proof: the relevant cost per unit of consensus increase is \(\mu_t c_t/\pi_t^{(K)}\). The given paper’s Theorem 1 uses the corresponding ordering for individual agents; the proof below establishes the result for the stated mass-weighted inputs independently. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.08484)

1. Computing the stationary weights

Write \(k=|K|\), \(P=Q_{K,K}\), and \(\pi_t=\pi_t^{(K)}\). Because \(K\) is closed and irreducible, \(P\) is a row-stochastic irreducible matrix.

We first justify that its normalized stationary distribution is uniquely determined and strictly positive.

For existence, take any probability row vector \(v\) and define

\( w_N=\frac1N\sum_{r=0}^{N-1}vP^r. \)

Each \(w_N\) lies in the probability simplex, which is compact. Also,

\( w_NP-w_N=\frac{vP^N-v}{N}\longrightarrow0. \)

Consequently, a convergent subsequence has a probability-vector limit \(\pi\) satisfying \(\pi P=\pi\).

Some coordinate \(\pi_i\) is positive because the coordinates sum to \(1\). For any \(j\), irreducibility supplies an integer \(r\) with \((P^r)_{ij}>0\). Stationarity then gives

\( \pi_j=(\pi P^r)_j\ge\pi_i(P^r)_{ij}>0. \)

For uniqueness, suppose a real column vector \(z\) satisfies \(Pz=z\). At an index attaining the maximum coordinate of \(z\), this equality expresses that maximum as a convex combination of coordinates no larger than it. Every successor with positive transition probability must therefore attain the same maximum. Irreducibility propagates this equality to every index, so \(z\) is constant. Thus

\( \operatorname{rank}(P-I)=k-1. \)

The left nullspace consequently has dimension one, and normalization uniquely determines \(\pi\).

For exact computation, solve

\( (P^{\mathsf T}-I)\pi^{\mathsf T}=0,\qquad \sum_{t\in K}\pi_t=1. \)

One may delete any one stationary equation and retain the normalization equation. Indeed, the stationary-equation rows sum to zero and have rank \(k-1\), so deleting any row leaves \(k-1\) independent rows. Their span annihilates \(\pi^{\mathsf T}\), whereas the normalization row has scalar product \(1\) with it. The resulting square system is therefore nonsingular.

2. Reduction to a continuous allocation problem

Define, for each \(t\in K\),

\( a_t=\mu_tc_t,\qquad u_t=1-\hat x_t,\qquad b_t=\pi_tu_t,\qquad \rho_t=\frac{a_t}{\pi_t}. \)

Here \(a_t>0\), \(\pi_t>0\), and \(u_t,b_t\ge0\). Let the initial consensus and required increase be

\( h=\sum_{t\in K}\pi_t\hat x_t,\qquad d=\max\{0,x^*-h\}. \)

If \(d=0\), the zero intervention is feasible and optimal: every allowed intervention has nonnegative cost. Hence its minimum cost is \(0\).

Assume henceforth that \(d>0\). Feasibility is automatic under the stated threshold bound:

\( \sum_{t\in K}b_t =\sum_{t\in K}\pi_t(1-\hat x_t) =1-h \ge x^*-h =d. \)

Introduce \(y_t=\pi_t\delta_t\), the consensus increase contributed by type \(t\). Since \(\pi_t>0\), this substitution is bijective between allowed interventions and allocations satisfying \(0\le y_t\le b_t\). Moreover,

\( C(\delta)=\sum_{t\in K}\rho_ty_t. \)

The optimization problem is therefore exactly

\( \min\left\{ \sum_{t\in K}\rho_ty_t: 0\le y_t\le b_t,\ \sum_{t\in K}y_t\ge d \right\}. \)

3. Exact formula and attaining intervention

Discard types with \(b_t=0\); their intervention must be zero. Order the remaining types \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\) so that

\( \rho_{t_1}\le\rho_{t_2}\le\cdots\le\rho_{t_n}, \)

breaking ties arbitrarily. Define

\( S_0=0,\qquad S_j=\sum_{i=1}^{j}b_{t_i}. \)

Because \(S_n\ge d>0\), there is a smallest index \(j\) with \(S_j\ge d\). It satisfies

\( 0<d-S_{j-1}\le b_{t_j}. \)

An attaining intervention is:

  • For \(i<j\), set \(\delta_{t_i}^*=u_{t_i}\).
  • Set \(\delta_{t_j}^*=(d-S_{j-1})/\pi_{t_j}\).
  • For \(i>j\), and for discarded types, set \(\delta_{t_i}^*=0\).

The cutoff inequality proves that every intervention bound is respected. Its consensus increase is exactly \(d\), so it is feasible.

The exact minimum is

\( C_{\min} = \sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_{t_i}u_{t_i} + \frac{a_{t_j}}{\pi_{t_j}} \left(d-S_{j-1}\right). \)

Thus an optimum requires at most one type to receive a strictly intermediate increase.

Proof of optimality

Let \(y^*_t=\pi_t\delta_t^*\) and \(\lambda=\rho_{t_j}>0\). For any feasible allocation \(y\), using \(\sum_t y_t^*=d\), we have

\( \sum_t\rho_ty_t-\sum_t\rho_ty_t^* = \sum_t(\rho_t-\lambda)(y_t-y_t^*) + \lambda\left(\sum_t y_t-d\right). \)

Every contribution on the right is nonnegative:

  • Before the cutoff, \(\rho_t-\lambda\le0\) and \(y_t-y_t^*=y_t-b_t\le0\).
  • At the cutoff, \(\rho_t-\lambda=0\).
  • After the cutoff, \(\rho_t-\lambda\ge0\) and \(y_t-y_t^*=y_t\ge0\).
  • Discarded types have \(y_t=y_t^*=0\).
  • Feasibility gives \(\sum_t y_t-d\ge0\).

Therefore \(C(\delta)\ge C(\delta^*)\) for every feasible intervention. This proves the formula and attainment, including cases with tied ratios.

4. Polynomial bit complexity

It remains to show that these exact rational calculations take polynomial time, rather than merely polynomially many arithmetic operations.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators appearing in \(P\). Since their encodings are part of the input,

\( \log_2D\le L. \)

Multiply the nonsingular stationary system from Section 1 by \(D\). Its coefficient matrix and right-hand side are integers of absolute value at most \(D\): entries of \(P\) lie in \([0,1]\), and the normalization coefficients are \(1\).

By the determinant expansion, the coefficient determinant and every Cramer numerator have absolute value at most \(k!D^k\). Therefore every stationary weight has numerator and denominator of bit length

\( O\!\left(kL+k\log k\right). \)

The system can be solved in polynomial bit time by fraction-free Gaussian elimination with pivoting. Its intermediate integer entries are controlled by minors of the integer augmented matrix; those minors satisfy the same polynomial bit-length bound. The algorithm uses polynomially many arithmetic operations on integers of polynomial bit length.

All subsequent quantities are formed using rational additions, multiplications, divisions, and comparisons. Each prefix sum contains at most \(k\) terms, so even using products of denominators as common denominators yields polynomial bit length. Sorting uses \(O(k\log k)\) comparisons, each performed exactly by cross multiplication of positive-denominator rationals. The resulting intervention and minimum consequently have polynomial-size rational encodings and are computable in polynomial time.

Since \(k\le\tau\), this establishes polynomial time in \(\tau,L\), and hence in \(m,\tau,L\). No expansion of the rational masses into individual agents is needed.

For the decision problem, compute \(C_{\min}\) and compare it exactly with \(B\), including the encoding of \(B\) in the decision input length. Thus the decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm P\); NP-hardness under polynomial-time many-one reductions would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
