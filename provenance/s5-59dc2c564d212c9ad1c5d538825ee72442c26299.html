Theorem 3.1 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(p\in[-\infty,1]\), \(\lambda>0\), and a nonnegative finite-mean law \(\mathcal D\), let \(N\) statistically identical agents arrive sequentially and let \(M_N=\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor\) indivisible items have independent values from \(\mathcal D\); determine the limiting optimal online and offline ex-ante welfare \(W_p(u)\), their competitive ratio, and an attaining causal policy as \(N\to\infty\), where \(u(s)\) is expected utility at normalized arrival position \(s\in[0,1]\) and \(W_{-\infty}(u)=\inf_s u(s)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix \(p\in[-\infty,1]\), \(\lambda>0\), and a known law \(\mathcal D\) on \(\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) with finite, strictly positive mean; all remain fixed as \(N\to\infty\).
A society is specified by its support: its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their binary-rational masses; \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types. Here there is one statistically identical type of mass \(1\). Any running-time claims must specify dependence on \(m,\tau,L,N,p,\lambda\) and the representation of \(\mathcal D\), where \(L\) is input bit length.
For each \(N\ge2\), there are \(m=M_N=\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor\) indivisible items and independent agent–item values \(X_{ij}\sim\mathcal D\). Utilities are additive, and agents have no bundle-size constraint.
At arrival \(i\), a causal randomized policy observes that agent's values for remaining items and immediately allocates an irrevocable bundle; each item is allocated at most once and may remain unallocated. An offline policy observes the entire valuation matrix. Randomization is allowed, including at ties.
For a policy \(\pi\), let \(u_N^\pi(s)=\mathbb E[\sum_{j\in A_i}X_{ij}]\) on \([(i-1)/N,i/N)\), with the final interval including \(1\).
Let \(W_p\) be the generalized \(p\)-mean under uniform Lebesgue mass on \([0,1]\), including the geometric mean at \(p=0\) and \(W_{-\infty}(u)=\inf_{s\in[0,1]}u(s)\); for these step profiles, any zero coordinate gives welfare zero when \(p\le0\).
Define \(A_N=\sup_{\pi\text{ causal}}W_p(u_N^\pi)\), \(O_N=\sup_{\pi\text{ offline}}W_p(u_N^\pi)\), and \(R_N=A_N/O_N\) for sufficiently large \(N\). Evaluate welfare before taking limits; no convergence of utility profiles is assumed.
For each fixed \((p,\lambda,\mathcal D)\), does \(R_N\) converge, and what is its sharp limiting competitive ratio (or sharp lower and upper limits if it does not)? Which causal policy sequences attain the corresponding asymptotic guarantees?
Known results: the discrete worst-case egalitarian ratio is at least \(\Gamma\approx0.70591334\) for every horizon and converges to \(\Gamma\); this takes the infimum over laws before the horizon limit. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25424v1).
For every finite horizon and item count, optimal many-item ex-ante welfares equal the item count times their single-item counterparts, so their ratios coincide exactly. This identity also applies along \(M_N=\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor\), but does not characterize the fixed-law limits asked above. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25424v1).

Partial progress: an exact asymptotic reduction to scalar integrals

The new result below applies to every fixed law in the question, without a regularity assumption on its tail. It replaces the finite-horizon optimization recurrences by nonrecursive, one-dimensional integral formulas that are asymptotically exact.

These formulas give sharp lower and upper limits in terms of explicit functions of the law. What remains unresolved here is their analytical evaluation for arbitrary oscillating tails, including the exact lower limit of the nonconvergent example constructed below. I therefore classify this write-up as partial.

1. Definitions and the integral formulas

Write
$$ \mu=\mathbb E[X]\in(0,\infty), \qquad F(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x), $$
and define the upper-quantile integral
$$ T(a)=\int_{1-a}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\,du, \qquad 0\leq a\leq1. $$
Thus $T(a)$ is the largest expected value obtainable by accepting a fraction $a$ of observations, with randomization at atoms.

The function $T$ is continuous, nondecreasing and concave, with
$$ T(0)=0,\qquad T(1)=\mu,\qquad T(a)\geq \mu a. $$
For real $t\geq1$, put
$$ B(t)=\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-F(x)^t\bigr)\,dx. $$
At integer arguments,
$$ B(N)=\mathbb E\!\left[\max_{i\leq N}X_i\right]. $$
This integral is finite: $B(t)\leq B(\lceil t\rceil)\leq\lceil t\rceil\mu$.

Here are the law-dependent scalar functions used in the reduction.

Egalitarian welfare

For $0<x<\mu$, let $A(x)$ be the unique solution of
$$ T(A(x))=x. $$
Uniqueness holds because $T$ is strictly increasing before reaching $\mu$. Define
$$ I(x)=\int_x^{\mu/2}\frac{du}{uA(u)}, \qquad 0<x\leq\mu/2, $$
and let $C(t)$ be its inverse:
$$ I(C(t))=t,\qquad t\geq0. $$
This inverse exists because $I$ is continuous and strictly decreasing, with $I(\mu/2)=0$ and $I(x)\to\infty$ as $x\downarrow0$. Indeed, $T(a)\geq\mu a$ implies $A(x)\leq x/\mu$.

Set
$$ Z_{-\infty}(t)=tC(t). $$

Positive finite parameters

For $0<p<1$, define
$$ f_p(y)=\max_{0\leq a\leq1}\bigl\{T(a)^p-pay\bigr\}, \qquad y\geq\mu^p. $$
For $p=1$ and an unbounded law, define
$$ f_1(y)=\mathbb E[(X-y)_+] =\int_y^\infty(1-F(x))\,dx, \qquad y\geq\mu. $$
For these parameters, put
$$ J_p(y)=\int_{\mu^p}^{y}\frac{du}{f_p(u)}, \qquad Y_p(t)=J_p^{-1}(t), $$
and
$$ Z_p(t)=t^{\,1-1/p}Y_p(t)^{1/p}. $$
The functions $f_p$ are positive on their stated domains. For $0<p<1$, this follows from $T(a)\geq\mu a$ and the fact that $a^p$ dominates $a$ near zero. For $p=1$, unboundedness gives a positive stop-loss expectation at every finite threshold.

Negative finite parameters

For $p<0$, write $r=-p>0$ and define
$$ g_r(y)=\min_{0<a\leq1} \bigl\{T(a)^{-r}+ray\bigr\}, \qquad y\geq\mu^{-r}. $$
Then put
$$ J_p(y)=\int_{\mu^p}^{y}\frac{du}{g_{-p}(u)}, \qquad Y_p(t)=J_p^{-1}(t), $$
and again
$$ Z_p(t)=t^{\,1-1/p}Y_p(t)^{1/p}. $$
The minimum defining $g_r$ exists: the objective is continuous away from zero and tends to infinity as $a\downarrow0$.

Geometric welfare

Define
$$ G(t)=\max_{0\leq a\leq1}T(a)e^{-ta}, \qquad t\geq1, $$
and
$$ Z_0(t) = t\exp\!\left(\frac1t\int_1^t\log G(s)\,ds\right). $$
Here $G(t)>0$. In particular,
$$ \frac{\mu}{et}\leq G(t)\leq\mu. $$

Proven asymptotic reduction

For every fixed unbounded law satisfying the question’s assumptions, and every fixed $p\in[-\infty,1]$,
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle R_N\sim\frac{Z_p(N)}{B(N)}.} $$
Consequently, the sharp endpoints satisfy
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle \liminf_{N\to\infty}R_N = \liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_p(t)}{B(t)}, \qquad \limsup_{N\to\infty}R_N = \limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z_p(t)}{B(t)}.} $$
Every quantity on the right is defined directly from the fixed law, using scalar optimization, integration and inversion; no finite-horizon policy recurrence occurs there.

For bounded laws, the sharper conclusion is
$$ R_N\longrightarrow1. $$

The rest of the document proves these assertions, specifies attaining policies, and proves that convergence can fail for a single fixed law.

2. Exact reduction to one item and optimal policies

For a single-item causal policy, let
$$ v_i=\mathbb E[X_i\mathbf1_{\{\text{item allocated to }i\}}], $$
and denote its optimal $p$-mean by $a_{p,N}$.

Offline welfare and the many-item reduction

Every offline single-item policy satisfies
$$ W_p(v_1,\ldots,v_N) \leq \frac1N\sum_i v_i \leq \frac{B(N)}N. $$
Allocating to a maximum-value agent, with uniform tie-breaking, gives every agent expected utility $B(N)/N$. Hence the offline optimum is exactly $B(N)/N$ for every specified $p$.

For $m\geq1$, any many-item causal policy induces a single-item causal policy as follows. Choose an item uniformly, use its actual observations, and simulate the other items independently while running the original policy. Independence makes this simulation causal. Its expected-utility vector is the original vector divided by $m$.

Conversely, applying a single-item policy independently to each item multiplies its expected-utility vector by $m$. There is no bundle-size constraint to obstruct this implementation. Positive homogeneity therefore gives
$$ A_N=ma_{p,N}, \qquad O_N=\frac{mB(N)}N, \qquad R_N=\frac{Na_{p,N}}{B(N)}. $$
Thus $\lambda$ cancels exactly whenever $m\geq1$, which holds for all sufficiently large $N$.

Define the single-item egalitarian and utilitarian optima
$$ c_N=\sup_{\pi\text{ causal}}\min_i v_i, \qquad V_N=\sup_{\pi\text{ causal}}\sum_i v_i. $$
The power-mean inequality yields
$$ \frac{Nc_N}{B(N)} \leq R_N \leq\frac{V_N}{B(N)} \leq1. $$

The supplied theorem also gives
$$ \Gamma\leq R_N\leq1. $$
Its hypotheses are exactly satisfied: the observations are independent and identically distributed, nonnegative, and have finite positive mean. The egalitarian guarantee extends to every $p\leq1$ by monotonicity of generalized means. [Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25424v1)

Threshold domination, including endpoints

At a reachable agent, average a policy’s acceptance decision over the previous history and its randomness. This gives a measurable function $h(x)\in[0,1]$ with acceptance probability $a$.

If $0<a<1$, accepting the upper $a$ quantile maximizes expected reward. To verify this, let $h^\star$ be that rule, randomized at its threshold value $z$. Since the acceptance probabilities agree,
$$ \mathbb E[X(h-h^\star)] = \mathbb E[(X-z)(h-h^\star)] \leq0. $$
The inequality holds pointwise: above $z$, $h-h^\star\leq0$; below $z$, $h-h^\star\geq0$.

At $a=0$, both rules accept with probability zero and have reward zero. At $a=1$, $h=1$ almost surely, and both rewards equal $\mu$. For an unreachable agent, choose any threshold; its utility is zero.

Replacing each decision by its upper-quantile rule preserves conditional acceptance probabilities and therefore reach probabilities. It weakly increases every coordinate. Allocating at the final agent whenever the item remains further weakly improves the vector.

Atoms are handled by generating
$$ U=F(X^-)+T_0(F(X)-F(X^-)), $$
where $T_0$ is independent uniform randomness. Then $U$ is uniform and $X=F^{-1}(U)$ almost surely.

A threshold policy with conditional acceptance probabilities $a_1,\ldots,a_{N-1}$ has coordinates
$$ v_i=\left(\prod_{j<i}(1-a_j)\right)T(a_i), $$
with $a_N=1$.

Finite-horizon recurrences and attainment

For egalitarian welfare, $c_1=\mu$, and
$$ c_{n+1}=T(a_n)=(1-a_n)c_n, $$
where $a_n\in(0,1)$ is the unique crossing of the two displayed expressions. Continuity and their opposite ordering at the endpoints prove existence; monotonicity gives uniqueness. The policy obtained recursively equalizes all expected-utility coordinates.

For finite $p\ne0$, let
$$ H_{p,n}=n\,a_{p,n}^{\,p}. $$
Then $H_{p,1}=\mu^p$, and
$$ H_{p,n+1} = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \max_{0\leq a\leq1} \{T(a)^p+(1-a)^pH_{p,n}\}, &0<p\leq1,\\[6pt] \displaystyle \min_{0<a<1} \{T(a)^p+(1-a)^pH_{p,n}\}, &p<0. \end{cases} $$
For positive $p$, continuity on the compact interval gives attainment. For negative $p$, the objective tends to infinity at both endpoints, while it is finite in the interior; hence a minimum is attained there.

For the geometric mean, let $P_n$ be the maximum product of utilities. Then
$$ P_1=\mu, \qquad P_n=P_{n-1}\phi_n, $$
where
$$ \phi_n=\max_{0\leq a\leq1}T(a)(1-a)^{n-1}. $$
For $n\geq2$, this continuous objective vanishes at both endpoints and is positive in the interior, so an interior maximum exists.

Finally,
$$ V_1=\mu, \qquad V_{n+1}=V_n+\mathbb E[(X-V_n)_+]. $$
This follows by comparing the current reward with the optimal expected continuation reward.

Choosing the scalar optimizers recursively specifies exact optimal single-item policies. Their independent application to all items attains $A_N$ at every horizon.

3. Proof of the integral reduction

3.1 Egalitarian welfare

First,
$$ c_n\longrightarrow0. $$
Indeed, for every $K>0$,
$$ \frac{B(n)}n \leq \frac Kn+\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>K\}}], $$
because the sample maximum is at most $K$ plus the sum of sampled values exceeding $K$. Integrability gives $B(n)/n\to0$, and $c_n\leq B(n)/n$.

The recurrence can be written exactly as
$$ a_n=A(c_{n+1}), \qquad c_n=\frac{c_{n+1}}{1-a_n}. $$
In particular, $a_n\to0$.

Choose $b>0$ with $\mathbb P(X\geq b)>0$. For sufficiently small acceptance probabilities, all selected upper quantiles have value at least $b$. Hence, for sufficiently small $x<y$,
$$ A(y)-A(x)\leq\frac{y-x}{b}. $$
Applying this with $x=c_{n+1}$ and $y=c_n$ gives
$$ 1 \leq \frac{A(c_n)}{A(c_{n+1})} \leq 1+\frac{c_n}{b} \longrightarrow1. $$

For all sufficiently large $n$, monotonicity of $A$ yields
$$ \frac{\log(c_n/c_{n+1})}{A(c_n)} \leq I(c_{n+1})-I(c_n) \leq \frac{\log(c_n/c_{n+1})}{A(c_{n+1})}. $$
Since
$$ \log(c_n/c_{n+1})=-\log(1-a_n)\sim a_n, $$
both bounds tend to $1$. Summation therefore gives
$$ I(c_n)\sim n. $$

It remains to justify inversion. Because $T$ is concave, its inverse $A$ is convex and $A(0)=0$. Thus
$$ A(u/z)\leq A(u)/z,\qquad z\geq1. $$
Changing variables in the integral and retaining the portion up to $\mu/2$ gives
$$ I(x/z)\geq zI(x). $$
Consequently,
$$ \frac{C(t)}z\leq C(zt)\leq C(t), \qquad z\geq1. $$
This proves that a relative error tending to zero in the argument of $C$ produces a relative error tending to zero in its value. Since $I(c_n)\sim n$,
$$ \boxed{c_n\sim C(n).} $$
Therefore
$$ Na_{-\infty,N}=Nc_N\sim Z_{-\infty}(N). $$

3.2 Parameters $0<p<1$

Write $H_n=H_{p,n}$ and $f=f_p$. The finite-horizon recurrence gives
$$ H_{n+1}-H_n = \max_a \{T(a)^p-[1-(1-a)^p]H_n\}. $$
A policy that chooses the recipient uniformly in advance has all utilities $\mu/n$. Therefore
$$ H_n\geq\mu^p n^{1-p}\longrightarrow\infty. $$

Let $a_y$ maximize
$$ f(y)=T(a_y)^p-pa_yy. $$
Since $f(y)>0$,
$$ a_y\leq\frac{\mu^p}{py}\longrightarrow0. $$
A stronger inequality follows from concavity. For $0<s<1$, optimality and $T(sa_y)\geq sT(a_y)$ imply
$$ pa_yy(1-s) \leq (1-s^p)T(a_y)^p. $$
Letting $s\uparrow1$ gives
$$ a_yy\leq T(a_y)^p, \qquad f(y)\geq(1-p)T(a_y)^p. $$

For small $a$,
$$ 1-(1-a)^p=pa+O(a^2). $$
Also, concavity of $x^p$ gives $1-(1-a)^p\geq pa$. Thus the actual increment is at most $f(H_n)$. Testing its objective at $a_{H_n}$ shows that its loss relative to $f(H_n)$ is at most
$$ O(a_{H_n}^2H_n) = O(a_{H_n}T(a_{H_n})^p) = o(f(H_n)). $$
Hence
$$ H_{n+1}-H_n\sim f(H_n). $$

The function $f$ is positive and nonincreasing. If $d_n=H_{n+1}-H_n$, testing the maximizer at $H_n$ in the definition at $H_{n+1}$ gives
$$ f(H_{n+1}) \geq f(H_n)-pa_{H_n}d_n. $$
Therefore $f(H_{n+1})/f(H_n)\to1$. Monotonicity now yields
$$ \frac{d_n}{f(H_n)} \leq J_p(H_{n+1})-J_p(H_n) \leq \frac{d_n}{f(H_{n+1})}, $$
so each increment tends to $1$. Consequently,
$$ J_p(H_n)\sim n. $$

The inversion argument is supplied in Section 3.5. It gives
$$ \boxed{H_{p,n}\sim Y_p(n).} $$

3.3 Parameters $p<0$

Write $r=-p$, $H_n=H_{p,n}$, and $g=g_r$. Since every single-item coordinate is at most $\mu$, every positive-utility vector satisfies
$$ H_n\geq n\mu^{-r}\longrightarrow\infty. $$
The recurrence gives
$$ H_{n+1}-H_n = \min_{0<a<1} \{T(a)^{-r}+[(1-a)^{-r}-1]H_n\}. $$

For any fixed $a>0$,
$$ \limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{g(y)}y\leq ra. $$
Letting $a\downarrow0$ proves
$$ g(y)=o(y). $$
If $a_y$ minimizes $g(y)$, then
$$ a_y\leq\frac{g(y)}{ry}\longrightarrow0. $$
Because
$$ (1-a)^{-r}-1\geq ra, $$
the actual increment is at least $g(H_n)$. Testing $a_{H_n}$ and using
$$ (1-a)^{-r}-1=ra+O(a^2) $$
gives the reverse asymptotic bound:
$$ H_{n+1}-H_n\sim g(H_n). $$
In particular, $(H_{n+1}-H_n)/H_n\to0$.

The function $g$ is nondecreasing. Moreover, for $z\geq1$,
$$ g(zy)\leq zg(y), $$
by testing the same acceptance probability. Hence
$$ \frac{g(H_{n+1})}{g(H_n)}\longrightarrow1. $$
The corresponding integral bounds imply
$$ J_p(H_{n+1})-J_p(H_n)\longrightarrow1, $$
and therefore
$$ J_p(H_n)\sim n. $$

3.4 Utilitarian welfare

Assume the law is unbounded. The sequence $V_n$ tends to infinity. Otherwise it would converge to a finite $v$, while its increments would converge to
$$ \mathbb E[(X-v)_+]>0, $$
a contradiction. Continuity of the stop-loss expectation follows from its Lipschitz dependence on the threshold.

Put $f=f_1$. For $d=f(y)$,
$$ 0\leq f(y)-f(y+d)\leq d\,\mathbb P(X>y). $$
Thus
$$ \frac{f(y+f(y))}{f(y)}\longrightarrow1. $$
The exact recurrence $V_{n+1}-V_n=f(V_n)$ now gives
$$ J_1(V_{n+1})-J_1(V_n)\longrightarrow1, \qquad J_1(V_n)\sim n. $$

3.5 Stability of the increasing inverses

For the positive parameters, including $p=1$, the denominator $f_p$ is nonincreasing. Hence, for $z\geq1$,
$$ J_p(zy)\geq zJ_p(y). $$

For negative $p=-r$, concavity of $T$ gives a stronger scaling estimate than the one used above. Test
$$ a'=az^{-1/(r+1)} $$
in the definition of $g_r(zy)$. Since
$$ T(a')\geq z^{-1/(r+1)}T(a), $$
we obtain
$$ g_r(zy)\leq z^{r/(r+1)}g_r(y). $$
Changing variables in the integral therefore gives
$$ J_p(zy)\geq z^{1/(r+1)}J_p(y). $$

These inequalities show that the inverses $Y_p$ preserve asymptotic equivalence of their arguments. More explicitly, if
$$ J_p(h_n)/n\longrightarrow1, $$
then the displayed scaling inequalities bound $h_n/Y_p(n)$ between quantities tending to $1$. Thus, in all finite nonzero cases considered above,
$$ H_{p,n}\sim Y_p(n). $$
The integrals defining $J_p$ diverge at infinity: for positive $p$, $f_p$ is bounded above; for negative $p$, $g_{-p}(y)=o(y)$. Hence all these inverses have the required domain.

Finally,
$$ Na_{p,N} = N^{1-1/p}H_{p,N}^{1/p} \sim Z_p(N). $$

3.6 Geometric welfare

Recall
$$ \phi_n=\max_aT(a)(1-a)^{n-1}. $$
For $n\geq2$, a maximizer can be chosen with $a\leq1/n$. Indeed, $T(a)/a$ is nonincreasing, while $a(1-a)^{n-1}$ strictly decreases after $1/n$.

Likewise, a maximizer of $G(n)$ has $a\leq1/n$. If $a>1/n$, concavity gives
$$ \frac{T(1/n)e^{-1}}{T(a)e^{-na}} \geq \frac{e^{na-1}}{na}>1. $$

Uniformly for $0\leq a\leq1/n$,
$$ (n-1)\log(1-a)+na=O(1/n). $$
Comparing the two maximization problems therefore gives
$$ \log\phi_n=\log G(n)+O(1/n). $$
Since
$$ \log a_{0,N} = \frac1N\left(\log\mu+\sum_{n=2}^N\log\phi_n\right), $$
we obtain
$$ \log a_{0,N} = \frac1N\sum_{n=2}^N\log G(n)+o(1). $$

The function $\log G(t)$ is nonincreasing and lies between
$$ \log\mu-1-\log t \quad\text{and}\quad \log\mu. $$
The sum–integral discrepancy is therefore $O(1+\log N)$. It follows that
$$ a_{0,N} \sim \exp\!\left(\frac1N\int_1^N\log G(t)\,dt\right), $$
and hence
$$ Na_{0,N}\sim Z_0(N). $$

3.7 Sharp endpoints and real arguments

The preceding subsections establish
$$ R_N\sim Z_p(N)/B(N). $$

Passing from integer to real arguments does not change either endpoint. For unbounded laws, $B(N)\to\infty$, and
$$ 0\leq B(N+1)-B(N)\leq\mu. $$
Thus $B(t)/B(N)\to1$ uniformly for $N\leq t\leq N+1$.

The inverse scaling inequalities similarly imply
$$ Z_p(t)/Z_p(N)\longrightarrow1 $$
uniformly on these intervals for $p\ne0$. For $p=0$, the bounds on $\log G$ show that the change of $\log Z_0(t)$ across such an interval is $O((1+\log N)/N)$.

This proves the endpoint formulas in Section 1.

The exact optimal policy sequence from Section 2 attains these endpoints. Conversely, every causal policy has horizon-$N$ ratio at most $R_N$. Consequently, no causal sequence can have a larger lower limit or a larger upper limit than the respective optimal endpoint.

4. Two classes with evaluated limits

4.1 Every bounded law has limit one

Let
$$ B=\operatorname{ess\,sup}X<\infty. $$
Since $\mu>0$, we have $B>0$.

Put
$$ b_N=N^{-1/2}, \qquad h_N=\frac{T(b_N)}{b_N}, \qquad \delta_N=\frac{b_N}{1+(N-1)b_N}. $$
Consider the following single-item policy. At agent $i$, if the value is in the upper $b_N$ quantile, accept with additional independent probability
$$ \frac{\delta_N}{b_Nr_i}, \qquad r_i=1-(i-1)\delta_N. $$
This probability is at most one: at the final agent, $r_N=1/[1+(N-1)b_N]$, and the probability equals one.

Induction shows that every agent has unconditional acceptance probability $\delta_N$, and hence utility
$$ \delta_Nh_N. $$
All coordinates are equal, so this is its welfare for every specified $p$.

For any $x<B$, eventually the upper $b_N$ quantile consists of values at least $x$. Thus $h_N\to B$. Also,
$$ N\delta_N\longrightarrow1, \qquad B(N)\longrightarrow B. $$
The last convergence follows from almost-sure convergence of the sample maximum and bounded convergence. Therefore this policy has ratio tending to one. Since $R_N\leq1$,
$$ \boxed{R_N\longrightarrow1.} $$
Along $m=\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor$,
$$ A_N\longrightarrow\lambda B, \qquad O_N\longrightarrow\lambda B. $$

4.2 Eventually exact Pareto tails

Suppose that, for some $\alpha>1$, $c>0$ and $x_0>0$,
$$ \mathbb P(X>x)=cx^{-\alpha}, \qquad x\geq x_0. $$
Put
$$ \beta=1-\frac1\alpha\in(0,1). $$
Then
$$ \boxed{\displaystyle R_N\longrightarrow \frac{\beta^{\beta-1}}{\Gamma_{\mathrm E}(\beta)},} $$
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm E}$ denotes the Euler gamma function. The equalizing egalitarian policy attains this limit for every $p$.

For completeness, the calculations are as follows.

With $a_N=(cN)^{1/\alpha}$, the normalized sample maximum has distribution converging to
$$ \mathbb P(M\leq t)=e^{-t^{-\alpha}}. $$
Its survival functions are eventually bounded by $\min\{1,t^{-\alpha}\}$, an integrable function. Tail integration and dominated convergence give
$$ B(N)\sim c^{1/\alpha}\Gamma_{\mathrm E}(\beta)N^{1/\alpha}. $$
Indeed, substituting $y=t^{-\alpha}$ and integrating by parts yields
$$ \int_0^\infty(1-e^{-t^{-\alpha}})\,dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-y}y^{-1/\alpha}\,dy = \Gamma_{\mathrm E}(\beta). $$
The boundary terms vanish because $0<1/\alpha<1$.

For sufficiently small $a$,
$$ T(a)=Ka^\beta, \qquad K=\frac{c^{1/\alpha}}{\beta}. $$
Hence
$$ A(x)=(x/K)^{1/\beta}, \qquad I(x)\sim\beta K^{1/\beta}x^{-1/\beta}. $$
The egalitarian integral reduction gives
$$ c_N\sim K\beta^\beta N^{-\beta} = c^{1/\alpha}\beta^{\beta-1}N^{-\beta}. $$

For sufficiently large $y$,
$$ f_1(y)=\frac{c}{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha}, $$
so
$$ J_1(y)\sim\frac{\beta}{c}y^\alpha, \qquad V_N\sim c^{1/\alpha}\beta^{-1/\alpha}N^{1/\alpha}. $$
Since $\beta^{-1/\alpha}=\beta^{\beta-1}$, both
$$ \frac{Nc_N}{B(N)} \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{V_N}{B(N)} $$
converge to the stated constant. The finite-horizon squeeze proves the assertion for every $p$.

In particular, at $\alpha=2$ the limit is
$$ C=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}. $$

5. One fixed law for which convergence fails for every parameter

The following construction proves that a universal fixed-law convergence theorem is false.

Construction

Let
$$ \varepsilon_k=2^{-k-3}, $$
and let $Z$ have the Pareto law
$$ \mathbb P(Z>z)=z^{-2}, \qquad z\geq1. $$
Initialize $b_0=0$ and $\ell_0=1$.

At stage $k$, choose a scale $a_k>b_{k-1}$. Choose $w_k>0$ sufficiently small that
$$ w_k\leq2^{-k-2}, \qquad 2a_kw_k\leq2^{-k}, $$
and, for every $h<k$,
$$ 2a_kw_k\leq2^{-(k-h)}\eta_h. $$
These are finitely many positive upper bounds, so such a weight exists.

Let $Y^{(k)}$ equal $a_kZ$ with probability $w_k$ and zero otherwise. It has an exact Pareto tail above $a_k$. Choose an integer $n_k>\ell_{k-1}$ sufficiently large that, with
$$ d_k=B_{Y^{(k)}}(n_k), $$
we have
$$ \left|\frac{n_kc_{n_k}(Y^{(k)})}{d_k}-C\right|\leq\varepsilon_k, \qquad \left|\frac{V_{n_k}(Y^{(k)})}{d_k}-C\right|\leq\varepsilon_k, $$
and
$$ b_{k-1}\leq\varepsilon_kd_k. $$
Such a choice exists by Section 4.2 and $d_k\to\infty$ as the auxiliary horizon increases.

Choose a cap $H_k>1$ sufficiently large that
$$ \frac{n_kw_ka_k}{H_k}\leq\varepsilon_kd_k. $$
Set
$$ b_k=a_kH_k, \qquad r_k=\frac{w_k}{H_k^2}. $$
Choose an integer $\ell_k>n_k$ with
$$ \ell_kr_k\geq1/\varepsilon_k, $$
and define
$$ \eta_k = \varepsilon_k \min\left\{\frac{d_k}{n_k},\frac{b_k}{\ell_k}\right\}. $$

Now define the fixed law $\mathcal D$: with probability $w_k$, draw
$$ a_k\min\{Z,H_k\}, $$
and put the remaining probability at zero. Its component supports are disjoint and ordered. Moreover,
$$ 0<\mathbb E[X] \leq\sum_k2a_kw_k \leq\sum_k2^{-k}<\infty. $$

Let $M_{>k}$ be the mean contribution from later components. The inductive constraints imply
$$ M_{>k} \leq \sum_{j>k}2a_jw_j \leq \eta_k\sum_{j>k}2^{-(j-k)} = \eta_k. $$

The Pareto subsequence

Couple $X\sim\mathcal D$ and $Y\sim Y^{(k)}$ by using the same Pareto sample on component $k$ and setting $Y=0$ on every other component. The expected truncation loss is
$$ \delta_k=\mathbb E[(Y-X)_+] = w_ka_k\mathbb E[(Z-H_k)_+] = \frac{w_ka_k}{H_k}. $$
At horizon $n=n_k$, sample maxima satisfy
$$ d_k-n\delta_k \leq B_{\mathcal D}(n) \leq d_k+b_{k-1}+nM_{>k}. $$
For the lower bound, truncation can decrease the maximum by at most the sum of truncation losses. For the upper bound, earlier components contribute at most $b_{k-1}$, and later components contribute at most their sampled sum.

The auxiliary equalizing policy can be simulated causally from actual observations. Disjoint supports identify component $k$. Below its cap use $Y=X$; at its cap sample the conditional untruncated Pareto value; outside it use $Y=0$. The resulting auxiliary observations are independent and have the correct law. Each utility coordinate loses at most $\delta_k$, so
$$ c_n(\mathcal D)\geq c_n(Y^{(k)})-\delta_k. $$

Conversely, simulate actual observations conditionally from the auxiliary observations and run any actual causal policy. This produces a causal auxiliary stopping rule. Pathwise, the actual selected reward is at most the auxiliary selected reward plus $b_{k-1}$ plus the sum of all later-component values. Hence
$$ V_n(\mathcal D) \leq V_n(Y^{(k)})+b_{k-1}+nM_{>k}. $$

All three error terms satisfy
$$ n\delta_k\leq\varepsilon_kd_k, \qquad b_{k-1}\leq\varepsilon_kd_k, \qquad nM_{>k}\leq\varepsilon_kd_k. $$
The finite-horizon squeeze therefore gives, simultaneously for every $p$,
$$ \frac{C-2\varepsilon_k}{1+2\varepsilon_k} \leq R_{n_k}(p) \leq \frac{C+3\varepsilon_k}{1-\varepsilon_k}. $$
Thus
$$ R_{n_k}(p)\longrightarrow C. $$

The plateau subsequence

At horizon $\ell=\ell_k$, components through $k$ have values at most $b_k$. Therefore
$$ B_{\mathcal D}(\ell) \leq b_k+\ell M_{>k} \leq(1+\varepsilon_k)b_k. $$

The event $X=b_k$ has probability exactly $r_k$. Accept only this event, using the equalizing thinning policy from Section 4.1 with
$$ \delta=\frac{r_k}{1+(\ell-1)r_k}. $$
Every coordinate equals $b_k\delta$, and
$$ \ell\delta = \frac1{1+(1-r_k)/(\ell r_k)} \geq1-\varepsilon_k. $$
Consequently, for every $p$,
$$ \frac{1-\varepsilon_k}{1+\varepsilon_k} \leq R_{\ell_k}(p)\leq1, $$
so
$$ R_{\ell_k}(p)\longrightarrow1. $$

Thus one fixed finite-mean law satisfies, simultaneously for all specified parameters,
$$ R_{n_k}(p)\longrightarrow\sqrt{\frac2\pi}, \qquad R_{\ell_k}(p)\longrightarrow1. $$
In particular, convergence fails, and its sharp upper limit is
$$ \limsup_{N\to\infty}R_N(p)=1. $$
The presently evaluated bounds on its lower limit remain
$$ \Gamma \leq \liminf_{N\to\infty}R_N(p) \leq \sqrt{\frac2\pi}. $$
The integral formulas in Section 1 apply to this law, but I have not evaluated their lower limit explicitly.
