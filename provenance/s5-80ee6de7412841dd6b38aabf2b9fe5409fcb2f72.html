The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supported type sets on two sides, rational masses of total \(1\) per side, an acceptable type-pair graph with strict rankings, a stable allocation \(\sigma\), and rational thresholds \(\kappa,q,t\), decide whether there is an allocation \(x\) with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_A(a)\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\mu_B(b)\), \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\ge|\sigma|+t\), \(\sum_{a,b}|x_{ab}-\sigma_{ab}|\le q\), and \(\mathcal B(x)\le\kappa\). For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(R_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) assigned below \(b\), including unmatched mass, and define \(S_{ab}(x)\) symmetrically; set \(\mathcal B(x)=\sum_{(a,b)}R_{ab}(x)S_{ab}(x)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Here the two supports are \(A,B\), each has total mass \(1\), and \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
An acceptable-pair graph \(E\subseteq A\times B\) has \(m=|E|\) edges. Each type strictly ranks its acceptable neighbors and prefers every neighbor to being unmatched.
An allocation is \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{E}\) satisfying \(\sum_{b:ab\in E}x_{ab}\le\mu_A(a)\) and \(\sum_{a:ab\in E}x_{ab}\le\mu_B(b)\); write \(|x|=\sum_{ab\in E}x_{ab}\).
For \(ab\in E\), define \(R_{ab}(x)=\mu_A(a)-\sum_{c:ac\in E,\ c\succeq_a b}x_{ac}\) and \(S_{ab}(x)=\mu_B(b)-\sum_{c:cb\in E,\ c\succeq_b a}x_{cb}\), where \(\succeq\) includes equality. These are masses assigned strictly worse partners, including unmatched mass.
Set \(\mathcal B(x)=\sum_{ab\in E}R_{ab}(x)S_{ab}(x)\). The input includes a rational allocation \(\sigma\) promised stable, meaning \(\mathcal B(\sigma)=0\), and nonnegative binary rational thresholds \(\kappa,q,t\).
Decide whether an allocation satisfies \(|x|\ge|\sigma|+t\), \(\sum_{ab\in E}|x_{ab}-\sigma_{ab}|\le q\), and \(\mathcal B(x)\le\kappa\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length and \(p=\lceil\kappa\rceil+\lceil q\rceil+\lceil t\rceil\).

Is this continuous decision problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, can it nevertheless be solved in \(f(p)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) time?

With indivisible agents, blocking-edge counts, and symmetric-difference distance, the corresponding problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard parameterized by \(k+q+t\), but FPT parameterized by \(q+d\), where \(d\) is maximum preference-list length ([Gupta et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol182-fsttcs2020/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.24/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2020.24.pdf)).
No result settling this exact continuous decision problem was found in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted allocations to nonnegative real masses on acceptable pairs; the given stable allocation has binary rational entries.
  • Defined stability of the supplied allocation by zero blocking mass under the stated product measure.
  • Required nonnegative rational thresholds, following the discrete problem's nonnegative integer thresholds; zero remains allowed.
  • Made every acceptable neighbor strictly preferable to being unmatched and excluded mass assigned to the pair itself from its strictly worse partner masses.
  • Defined m as the number of acceptable type pairs, tau as the combined support size, and L as total input bit length.
  • The rational thresholds leave the FPT parameter undefined; used the sum of their ceilings as the integer analogue of the discrete numerical parameter.

Result

The continuous decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already on instances with \(p=4\). Consequently, it has no algorithm running in \(f(p)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) time unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The reduction below handles arbitrary real allocations. It uses equal positive dyadic masses and supplies a stable rational allocation.

Bounds from normalization

For every feasible allocation, \(0\le R_{ab}(x)\le\mu_A(a)\) and \(0\le S_{ab}(x)\le\mu_B(b)\). Therefore,

\[ \mathcal B(x) \le \sum_{ab\in E}\mu_A(a)\mu_B(b) \le \left(\sum_{a\in A}\mu_A(a)\right) \left(\sum_{b\in B}\mu_B(b)\right) =1. \]

Also, \(|x|\le1\), and nonnegativity gives

\[ \sum_{ab\in E}|x_{ab}-\sigma_{ab}| \le |x|+|\sigma| \le2. \]

Thus thresholds \(\kappa>1\) and \(q>2\) can be replaced by \(1\) and \(2\), respectively. Instances with \(|\sigma|+t>1\) are immediately infeasible. After this preprocessing, \(p\le4\).

This already shows that the proposed parameter cannot yield a tractability distinction: an FPT algorithm in \(p\) would give a polynomial-time algorithm for the whole problem.

Reduction from MAX-CUT

We use the following known result: given a simple undirected graph and an integer threshold, deciding whether some cut contains at least that many edges is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. This is the Simple Max Cut theorem of [Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975%2876%2990059-1).

Let the source instance be \(G=(V,F)\), with cut threshold \(K\). We may restrict to \(|F|\ge1\) and \(1\le K\le|F|\), because the excluded cases are decidable immediately.

Pad \(G\) with isolated vertices until \(n=|V|\) is a power of two, with \(n\ge2\). This increases its size by less than a factor of two and does not change its cuts. Label the vertices \(1,\ldots,n\), and define

\[ r=n,\qquad h=2r,\qquad \delta=\frac1{nh}=\frac1{2n^2},\qquad C=|F|-K. \]

Because \(n\) is a power of two, \(\delta\) is dyadic.

Types and acceptable pairs

For each graph vertex \(i\), create a block containing types

\[ A_i=\{a_{i,s}:0\le s<h\}, \qquad B_i=\{b_{i,s}:0\le s<h\}. \]

Every type has mass \(\delta\). Each side contains \(nh\) types, so each side has total mass \(1\).

Within block \(i\), add the edges

\[ a_{i,s}b_{i,s}, \qquad a_{i,s}b_{i,s+1}, \]

where indices are taken modulo \(h\). Call these the diagonal and cyclic edges, respectively.

For every source edge \(\{i,j\}\in F\), with \(i<j\), add all edges in the following two sets:

\[ \{a_{i,s}b_{j,u}:r\le s<h,\ 0\le u<r\}, \]

\[ \{a_{i,s}b_{j,u}:0\le s<r,\ r\le u<h\}. \]

Each set has \(r^2\) edges. These cross edges always go from the \(A\)-side of an earlier block to the \(B\)-side of a later block.

Strict preferences

At every type, put one internal neighbor first, all cross neighbors next in any fixed strict order, and the other internal neighbor last:

| Type | Index range | First neighbor | Last neighbor |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(a_{i,s}\) | \(0\le s<r\) | \(b_{i,s}\) | \(b_{i,s+1}\) |
| \(a_{i,s}\) | \(r\le s<h\) | \(b_{i,s+1}\) | \(b_{i,s}\) |
| \(b_{i,s}\) | \(0\le s<r\) | \(a_{i,s-1}\) | \(a_{i,s}\) |
| \(b_{i,s}\) | \(r\le s<h\) | \(a_{i,s}\) | \(a_{i,s-1}\) |

Every acceptable neighbor is preferred to being unmatched.

The supplied stable allocation and thresholds

Run ordinary deferred acceptance on this finite graph to obtain a stable matching \(M_0\). Strict incomplete lists satisfy its usual hypotheses; the algorithm makes at most one proposal per acceptable edge.

Set \(\sigma_{ab}=\delta\) on matching edges and \(\sigma_{ab}=0\) elsewhere.

For this allocation, \(R_{ab}(\sigma)\) equals \(0\) if \(a\) is matched to \(b\) or to someone preferred to \(b\), and equals \(\delta\) otherwise. The analogous statement holds for \(S_{ab}(\sigma)\). Hence \(R_{ab}(\sigma)S_{ab}(\sigma)>0\) precisely when \(ab\) blocks \(M_0\). Since \(M_0\) is stable, \(\mathcal B(\sigma)=0\).

Choose

\[ t=1-|\sigma|, \qquad q=2, \qquad \kappa=\delta^2(r^2C+n). \]

These are nonnegative dyadic rationals. The size constraint becomes \(|x|\ge1\), and therefore forces \(|x|=1\). The distance constraint is automatically satisfied by the normalization bound.

Structure of every allocation of mass \(1\)

Let \(x\) be any feasible allocation with \(|x|=1\). Every type must be saturated: the nonnegative deficits on either side sum to \(1-|x|=0\).

Cross edges carry no mass

Fix a prefix consisting of blocks \(1,\ldots,k\). Its \(B\)-types have total mass \(kh\delta\).

Every neighbor of these \(B\)-types belongs to an \(A\)-block in the same prefix: internal edges stay within blocks, and cross edges go only from earlier \(A\)-blocks to later \(B\)-blocks.

Consequently, saturation of the prefix’s \(B\)-types consumes all \(kh\delta\) capacity of its \(A\)-types. No mass can leave the prefix on a cross edge. Taking \(k=i\) shows that every cross edge from block \(i\) to a later block has allocation zero.

One real variable per block

Write \(u_{i,s}=x_{a_{i,s}b_{i,s}}\). Saturation of \(a_{i,s}\), together with zero cross flow, gives

\[ x_{a_{i,s}b_{i,s+1}}=\delta-u_{i,s}. \]

Saturation of \(b_{i,s}\) then gives

\[ u_{i,s}+(\delta-u_{i,s-1})=\delta, \]

so \(u_{i,s}=u_{i,s-1}\). Thus there is a common \(z_i\in[0,1]\) such that, throughout block \(i\),

\[ x_{a_{i,s}b_{i,s}}=\delta z_i, \qquad x_{a_{i,s}b_{i,s+1}}=\delta(1-z_i). \]

Conversely, every \(z\in[0,1]^n\) defines a feasible allocation of mass \(1\) by these formulas and zero cross flow.

Exact blocking formula

We now compute \(\mathcal B(x)\) for every allocation of mass \(1\).

Internal contributions

An internal edge that is the last neighbor of either endpoint contributes zero: that endpoint’s cumulative assignment through the edge equals its entire mass \(\delta\).

No diagonal edge is first at both endpoints. For \(s<r\), it is last at \(b_{i,s}\); for \(s\ge r\), it is last at \(a_{i,s}\).

A cyclic edge \(a_{i,s}b_{i,s+1}\) is first at both endpoints exactly when \(s=h-1\). Indeed, the \(A\)-endpoint ranks it first precisely for \(s\ge r\), while the \(B\)-endpoint ranks it first precisely when \(s+1\), modulo \(h\), lies below \(r\). Their unique intersection is \(s=h-1\).

This edge receives mass \(\delta(1-z_i)\), so both residuals equal \(\delta z_i\). Therefore block \(i\)’s internal contribution is exactly \(\delta^2z_i^2\).

Cross contributions

A cross neighbor lies between the two internal neighbors. Since all cross allocations are zero, the residual at a cross edge equals the mass assigned to the last internal neighbor.

For \(\{i,j\}\in F\) with \(i<j\):

  • Each edge from the second half of \(A_i\) to the first half of \(B_j\) has residuals \(\delta z_i\) and \(\delta z_j\).
  • Each edge from the first half of \(A_i\) to the second half of \(B_j\) has residuals \(\delta(1-z_i)\) and \(\delta(1-z_j)\).

There are \(r^2\) edges of each kind. Combining all contributions gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal B(x)= \delta^2\left( \sum_{i=1}^n z_i^2 + r^2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in F} \bigl[z_i z_j+(1-z_i)(1-z_j)\bigr] \right). } \]

This identity holds for arbitrary real \(z_i\in[0,1]\).

Correctness of the reduction

A qualifying cut gives a feasible allocation

Suppose \(G\) has a cut with at least \(K\) crossing edges. Encode its sides by \(z_i\in\{0,1\}\).

For each source edge, \(z_i z_j+(1-z_i)(1-z_j)\) equals \(1\) exactly when that edge does not cross the cut. There are at most \(C=|F|-K\) such edges, and \(\sum_i z_i^2\le n\). Hence

\[ \mathcal B(x)\le\delta^2(n+r^2C)=\kappa. \]

The corresponding allocation has mass \(1=|\sigma|+t\), and its distance from \(\sigma\) is at most \(2=q\). Thus the constructed instance is feasible.

No qualifying cut excludes every real allocation

Suppose every cut has fewer than \(K\) crossing edges. Because cut sizes are integers, every cut leaves at least \(C+1\) edges uncut.

For any \(z\in[0,1]^n\), independently choose random bits \(Z_i\) with \(\Pr(Z_i=1)=z_i\). For each edge,

\[ \Pr(Z_i=Z_j)=z_i z_j+(1-z_i)(1-z_j). \]

Every realized bit vector defines a cut with at least \(C+1\) uncut edges. Taking expectations therefore yields

\[ \sum_{\{i,j\}\in F} \bigl[z_i z_j+(1-z_i)(1-z_j)\bigr] \ge C+1. \]

Using the exact blocking formula and \(\sum_i z_i^2\ge0\),

\[ \mathcal B(x)\ge\delta^2r^2(C+1). \]

Since \(r=n\ge2\), we have \(r^2>n\), and consequently

\[ \delta^2r^2(C+1) > \delta^2(r^2C+n) =\kappa. \]

Thus every allocation satisfying the size constraint violates the blocking constraint. This proves soundness without assuming integrality or rounding a feasible allocation.

Encoding size and the parameter

The construction has

\[ \tau=4nr=4n^2, \qquad m=4nr+2r^2|F|=O(n^4). \]

All preference lists are explicit. The masses, \(\sigma\), and thresholds have polynomial binary encoding length. Deferred acceptance and the entire construction take polynomial time.

Moreover,

\[ 0<\kappa =\frac{n^2C+n}{4n^4} \le\frac{n-1}{8n}+\frac1{4n^3} <1. \]

We also have \(t>0\). Otherwise \(\sigma\) would have mass \(1\), so the blocking formula would apply to it. Its stability would force every \(z_i=0\), because each term \(z_i^2\) is nonnegative. But then every source edge contributes \(r^2\delta^2>0\), contradicting \(|F|\ge1\) and \(\mathcal B(\sigma)=0\).

Thus \(0<t\le1\), while \(q=2\), giving

\[ p=\lceil\kappa\rceil+\lceil q\rceil+\lceil t\rceil =1+2+1=4. \]

The problem is therefore \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard at the fixed parameter value \(p=4\). An algorithm with running time \(f(p)(m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) would solve these reduced instances in polynomial time and hence imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
