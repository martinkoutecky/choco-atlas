The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a prior \(\mu \in (0,1)\) and response level \(\lambda > 0\), let \(\nu\) be a mass distribution over posterior types \(p \in [0,1]\) with \(\int p\,d\nu(p)=\mu\); by the paper’s reduction, restrict to support in \(\{0,p,1\}\) for some \(p\). A type \(p\) reports \(1\) with probability \(\psi_\lambda(p)\), so the exact conditional report shares are \(r_1(\nu)=\frac{1}{\mu}\int p\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p)\) and \(r_0(\nu)=\frac{1}{1-\mu}\int(1-p)\psi_\lambda(p)\,d\nu(p)\). The decision maker observes the share \(r\) and chooses a policy \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(f(r)\) is the probability of choosing state \(1\). Characterize or compute a policy minimizing worst-case regret \(\rho^*=\min_f\sup_\nu[V_\nu-U_\nu(f)]\), where \(U_\nu(f)=\mu(2f(r_1(\nu))-1)+(1-\mu)(1-2f(r_0(\nu)))\) and \(V_\nu=\max_g U_\nu(g)\).

A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) posterior types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Posterior types are binary rationals in \([0,1]\).

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm P\). In fact, \(\rho^*=0\) for every valid input, so every permitted threshold \(b\) gives a YES instance.

Here \(\mathcal N_\mu\) retains the stipulated rationality of both posterior types and masses. The key is that exact shares from opposite states cannot coincide across societies, except at the uninformative society.

1. Parametrizing societies and shares

Write any \(\nu\in\mathcal N_\mu\) as
\[ \]
\( \nu=a_0\delta_0+t\delta_p+a_1\delta_1 \),
where \(p\in(0,1)\) is rational and \(a_0,t,a_1\) are nonnegative rationals. Zero masses are omitted from the actual support. For an endpoint-only society, choose \(p=\mu\) and \(t=0\).

Normalization and the mean constraint give
\(a_1=\mu-pt\) and \(a_0=1-\mu-(1-p)t\).

Set \(A=\psi_\lambda(0)\) and \(C=\psi_\lambda(1)\). Substitution into the share definitions yields
\(r_1(\nu)=(1-\alpha)C+\alpha\psi_\lambda(p)\), where \(\alpha=pt/\mu\), and
\(r_0(\nu)=(1-\beta)A+\beta\psi_\lambda(p)\), where \(\beta=(1-p)t/(1-\mu)\).

Both coefficients are rational and belong to \([0,1]\): these bounds follow respectively from \(a_1\geq0\) and \(a_0\geq0\).

2. Opposite-state collision lemma

Lemma. If \(\nu,\eta\in\mathcal N_\mu\) satisfy \(r_1(\nu)=r_0(\eta)\), then \(\nu=\eta=\delta_\mu\).

Proof. Let the interior types and masses of \(\nu,\eta\) be \(p,t\) and \(q,s\), respectively. Define
\(\alpha=pt/\mu\) and \(\beta=(1-q)s/(1-\mu)\).

The hypothesized equality is
\((1-\alpha)C+\alpha\psi_\lambda(p)=(1-\beta)A+\beta\psi_\lambda(q)\).
Here \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\).

Choose a positive integer \(D\) such that
\(n_p=D(1-2p)\) and \(n_q=D(1-2q)\) are integers. Since both types are interior, \(|n_p|<D\) and \(|n_q|<D\). Put \(z=e^{2\lambda/D}\).

We use Hermite’s theorem that \(e\) is transcendental; see [his original memoir](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/27940137). It implies that \(z\) is transcendental. Indeed, write \(2\lambda/D=a/d\) with positive integers \(a,d\). If \(z\) were algebraic, then \(z^d=e^a\) would be algebraic. A nonzero polynomial \(P\in\mathbb Q[X]\) annihilating \(e^a\) would make \(P(X^a)\) a nonzero rational polynomial annihilating \(e\), a contradiction.

For each integer \(n\), define the rational function
\(H_n(X)=1/(1+X^n)\) when \(n\geq0\), and
\(H_n(X)=X^{-n}/(1+X^{-n})\) when \(n<0\).
In particular, \(H_0(X)=1/2\).

Then
\(A=1/(1+z^D)\),
\(C=z^D/(1+z^D)\),
\(\psi_\lambda(p)=H_{n_p}(z)\), and
\(\psi_\lambda(q)=H_{n_q}(z)\).

Consequently, the rational function
\(F(X)=(1-\alpha)X^D/(1+X^D)+\alpha H_{n_p}(X)-(1-\beta)/(1+X^D)-\beta H_{n_q}(X)\)
vanishes at \(z\). Its coefficients are rational and its denominators are nonzero at the positive number \(z\). Clearing denominators gives a rational polynomial vanishing at a transcendental number. That polynomial must be identically zero, so \(F\) is identically zero.

Now set \(\zeta=e^{\pi i/D}\), so \(\zeta^D=-1\). For every integer \(k\) with \(0<k<D\), the argument \(\pi k/D\) lies strictly between \(0\) and \(\pi\), hence \(\zeta^k\neq-1\). Thus both \(H_{n_p}\) and \(H_{n_q}\) are finite at \(\zeta\); the case \(n=0\) is constant.

Multiply the identity \(F(X)=0\) by \(1+X^D\) and evaluate at \(\zeta\). The interior-type terms vanish, leaving
\(-(1-\alpha)-(1-\beta)=0\), or \(\alpha+\beta=2\).

Because both coefficients lie in \([0,1]\), this forces \(\alpha=\beta=1\). The original equality therefore becomes \(\psi_\lambda(p)=\psi_\lambda(q)\). The function is strictly increasing, since
\(\psi_\lambda'(u)=4\lambda\psi_\lambda(u)(1-\psi_\lambda(u))>0\).
Hence \(p=q\).

Finally, \(\alpha=1\) gives \(pt=\mu\). Since \(t\leq1\), it follows that \(p\geq\mu\). Similarly, \(\beta=1\) gives \((1-q)s=1-\mu\), and \(s\leq1\) implies \(q\leq\mu\). Together with \(p=q\), these inequalities give \(p=q=\mu\), and then \(t=s=1\). Both societies are \(\delta_\mu\). This proves the lemma.

3. One policy attains every benchmark

Define the sets of attainable state-specific shares by
\(R_1=\{r_1(\nu):\nu\in\mathcal N_\mu\}\) and
\(R_0=\{r_0(\nu):\nu\in\mathcal N_\mu\}\).
Let \(x_*=\psi_\lambda(\mu)\).

The lemma, together with the admissible society \(\delta_\mu\), proves
\(R_1\cap R_0=\{x_*\}\).
It also proves that either state’s share can equal \(x_*\) only for \(\delta_\mu\), by comparison with the opposite-state share of \(\delta_\mu\).

Choose \(h=1\) when \(\mu\geq1/2\), and \(h=0\) otherwise. Define a single policy by
\(f(x)=1\) for \(x\in R_1\setminus\{x_*\}\),
\(f(x_*)=h\), and
\(f(x)=0\) everywhere else.
This definition depends only on \(\mu,\lambda\) and the specified adversarial class, as permitted.

For every \(\nu\neq\delta_\mu\), the collision lemma implies
\(f(r_1(\nu))=1\) and \(f(r_0(\nu))=0\).
Therefore \(U_\nu(f)=1\). Since no policy can obtain utility greater than \(1\), we also have \(V_\nu=1\).

For \(\nu=\delta_\mu\), both shares equal \(x_*\). A policy choosing state \(1\) with probability \(y\) there obtains
\(U_{\delta_\mu}=(2\mu-1)(2y-1)\).
Its maximum is \(|2\mu-1|\), attained by the chosen value \(h\). Thus \(U_{\delta_\mu}(f)=V_{\delta_\mu}\) as well.

The constructed policy consequently has zero regret for every society. Conversely, regret is nonnegative because \(V_\nu\) maximizes utility over a class containing \(f\). Hence \(\rho^*=0\), with the minimum attained.

4. Binary Turing complexity

On every promised input, \(b\in[0,2]\), so \(\rho^*=0\leq b\). The decision algorithm reads the input and returns YES, taking \(O(L)\) time, including input reading. It need not evaluate an exponential or construct the policy.

The conclusion uses the problem’s rational societies, exact observations, and unrestricted policies. Existence of the policy suffices for deciding the minimax value; efficient policy evaluation from approximate observations is a separate question.
