Theorem 2 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of man types \(A\) and woman types \(B\), rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), a strict partial order over counterpart types for each type, and a rational threshold \(r\), decide whether the maximum entropy score over stable mass couplings is at least \(r\). A coupling \(\pi\) has marginals \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); it is stable if, for every \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\), \(\left(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}\pi_{ab'}\right)\left(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}\pi_{a'b}\right)=0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be nonempty finite supports of man and woman types, with strictly positive binary rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), satisfying \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\). Mass is divisible; all counterpart types are acceptable, and nobody remains unmatched.
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types; put \(m=\max\{|A|,|B|\}\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each type has an explicitly given strict partial order over counterpart types; \(b\succ_a b'\) means that \(a\) strictly prefers \(b\) to \(b'\). Incomparability does not constitute strict preference, and no tie-breaking is imposed.
A coupling is a real matrix \(\pi\geq0\) with \(\sum_b\pi_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a\pi_{ab}=\nu_b\). It is stable precisely when, for every \((a,b)\in A\times B\), \(\bigl(\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}\pi_{ab'}\bigr)\bigl(\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}\pi_{a'b}\bigr)=0\).
Define its entropy score by \(H(\pi)=-\sum_{a,b}\pi_{ab}\log_2\pi_{ab}\), with \(0\log_2 0=0\). The input includes \(r\in\mathbb{Q}\); the decision asks whether \(\max_{\pi\text{ stable}}H(\pi)\geq r\), with exact comparison, including equality.

Is this entropy-threshold decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite balanced markets with arbitrary partial orders and \(n\geq2\), an exact inclusion–exclusion expression counts stable perfect matchings ([Drgas-Burchardt, 2015](https://ord.pwr.edu.pl/assets/papers_archive/1131%20-%20published.pdf)); this is not a polynomial-time complexity result.
Counting stable perfect matchings is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-complete even for strict total orders ([Irving and Leather, 1986](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/0215048)). No result settling the exact continuous entropy-threshold problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The entropy score was undefined; chose Shannon entropy of the joint coupling, using base-two logarithms and the usual zero convention.
  • Normalized each side to total mass one, with nonempty supports and strictly positive masses, preserving the balanced-market requirement.
  • Carried over universal acceptability and compulsory matching; incomparable alternatives do not block through strict preference, and no tie-breaking is imposed.
  • Specified real, divisible coupling entries and exact threshold comparison including equality, without an approximation promise.
  • Specified explicit preference-relation input, total input bit length L, support size tau, and m as the maximum counterpart support size.

Result

The entropy-threshold decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This holds even when all masses are equal dyadic rationals and every preference order has no chain of three distinct types.

The reduction handles divisible mass directly and uses a rational threshold separated from the optimum by an inverse-polynomial gap.

1. Source problem and construction

We reduce from INDEPENDENT SET: given a finite simple graph \(G\) and an integer \(k\), decide whether its independence number \(\alpha(G)\) satisfies \(\alpha(G)\geq k\). Its \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness follows from the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness of CLIQUE by taking graph complements. [Karp, 1972](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

We may assume the number \(n\) of vertices is a power of two and \(n\geq4\): pad any graph with isolated vertices to the next such size and increase \(k\) by the number added. This preserves the answer and increases the graph size by at most a constant factor. We also restrict to \(1\leq k\leq n\), since other thresholds are trivial.

Label the vertices \(1,\ldots,n\). Create types

\(A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_n\},\qquad B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\},\)

with masses

\(\mu_{a_i}=\nu_{b_i}=1/n.\)

These masses are positive dyadic rationals.

For each edge \(\{i,j\}\) with \(i<j\), specify exactly the comparisons

\(b_j\succ_{a_i}b_i,\qquad a_i\succ_{b_j}a_j.\)

There are no other strict comparisons. All counterpart types remain acceptable.

These are strict partial orders. For each type, all comparisons have the same lower element, and none of their upper elements is that lower element. Hence there are no chains of two successive comparisons, so transitivity imposes no additional comparisons.

Write \(\pi_{ij}=\pi_{a_i b_j}\). For an edge oriented as \(i<j\), the two lower sets at \((a_i,b_j)\) are exactly \(\{b_i\}\) and \(\{a_j\}\). Its stability condition is therefore

\(\pi_{ii}\pi_{jj}=0.\)

At every other pair, at least one lower set is empty. Consequently,

\(\pi\text{ is stable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \pi_{ii}\pi_{jj}=0\text{ for every }\{i,j\}\in E(G).\)

Thus the positive diagonal entries of a stable coupling form an independent set.

2. Entropy with a prescribed set of allowed diagonals

For \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\), let \(\mathcal C_S\) be the set of nonnegative matrices with every row and column sum equal to \(1/n\), subject to

\(\pi_{ii}=0\quad\text{for }i\notin S.\)

All off-diagonal entries are unrestricted. We now compute the maximum entropy over this entire continuous polytope.

Its optimum depends only on \(\ell=|S|\); denote it by \(F_n(\ell)\).

For \(\ell=0\), the maximizing matrix is uniform on the off-diagonal cells, giving

\(F_n(0)=\log_2[n(n-1)].\)

For \(\ell=n\), the maximizing matrix is uniform on all cells, giving

\(F_n(n)=2\log_2 n.\)

For \(1\leq\ell\leq n-1\), put \(s=n-\ell\). Define \(t\) to be the unique root in \([1/2,1]\) of

\(P(t)=\ell t^2+(s-\ell)t-(s-1)=0.\)

This root exists and is unique: \(P(1)=1\), while

\(P(1/2)=1-s/2-\ell/4\leq0,\)

and, throughout this interval,

\(P'(t)=2\ell t+s-\ell\geq s>0.\)

Set

\(D=\ell t+s-1,\qquad c=\frac1{nD},\qquad w_i=t\text{ for }i\in S,\quad w_i=1\text{ for }i\notin S.\)

Define \(Q^S\) by setting its forbidden diagonal entries to zero and every other entry to

\(Q^S_{ij}=c\,w_iw_j.\)

For \(i\notin S\), its row sum is \(cD=1/n\). For \(i\in S\), its row sum is

\(c(\ell t^2+st)=c(\ell t+s-1)=1/n,\)

where the middle equality follows from \(P(t)=0\). The matrix is symmetric, so its column sums also equal \(1/n\). Thus \(Q^S\in\mathcal C_S\), and it is positive on every allowed cell.

To prove optimality, let \(\pi\in\mathcal C_S\). On its support,

\(\log_2 Q^S_{ij}=\log_2 c+\log_2 w_i+\log_2 w_j.\)

Because \(\pi\) and \(Q^S\) have identical marginals,

\(\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\log_2 Q^S_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}Q^S_{ij}\log_2 Q^S_{ij}.\)

Therefore their relative entropy satisfies

\(D_2(\pi\Vert Q^S)=\sum_{\pi_{ij}>0}\pi_{ij}\log_2\frac{\pi_{ij}}{Q^S_{ij}}=H(Q^S)-H(\pi)\geq0.\)

For completeness, nonnegativity follows from \(-\ln x\geq1-x\): summing this inequality with \(x=Q^S_{ij}/\pi_{ij}\) gives

\((\ln2)D_2(\pi\Vert Q^S)\geq1-\sum_{\pi_{ij}>0}Q^S_{ij}\geq0.\)

Hence \(Q^S\) maximizes entropy. The same argument proves the two boundary cases above.

Using its marginals to evaluate its entropy gives the explicit formula

\(F_n(\ell)=\log_2[n(\ell t+s-1)]-\frac{2\ell}{n}\log_2 t\qquad(1\leq\ell\leq n-1).\)

3. A quantitative gap between consecutive optima

We prove that, for every \(0\leq\ell<n\),

\(F_n(\ell+1)-F_n(\ell)\geq\frac1{4n^2}.\)

Choose sets \(S\subset T\) with \(|S|=\ell\), \(T=S\cup\{j\}\), and write \(p=Q^S\), \(q=Q^T\). Let \(z=q_{jj}\).

When \(|T|<n\), the preceding construction gives

\(z=ct^2\geq\frac1{4n(n-1)}\geq\frac1{4n^2},\)

because \(t\geq1/2\) and \(D\leq n-1\). When \(|T|=n\), we instead have \(z=1/n^2\), so the same lower bound holds.

The relative-entropy identity from the previous section applies to \(p\) and \(q\):

\(F_n(\ell+1)-F_n(\ell)=D_2(p\Vert q).\)

Since \(p_{jj}=0\), normalize the restriction of \(q\) to the remaining cells, obtaining a probability distribution \(q'\). Then

\(D_2(p\Vert q)=D_2(p\Vert q')-\log_2(1-z)\geq-\log_2(1-z).\)

Finally, \(-\ln(1-z)\geq z\) and \(\ln2<1\), yielding

\(F_n(\ell+1)-F_n(\ell)\geq\frac z{\ln2}\geq\frac1{4n^2}.\)

In particular, \(F_n\) is strictly increasing.

4. The stable optimum encodes the independence number

Let \(\pi\) be any stable coupling, and define

\(S(\pi)=\{i:\pi_{ii}>0\}.\)

Section 1 shows that \(S(\pi)\) is independent. Since \(\pi\in\mathcal C_{S(\pi)}\), Sections 2–3 imply

\(H(\pi)\leq F_n(|S(\pi)|)\leq F_n(\alpha(G)).\)

Conversely, choose a maximum independent set \(I\). The matrix \(Q^I\) is a coupling whose positive diagonal entries occur exactly at vertices of \(I\). Section 1 therefore shows that it is stable, and its entropy is \(F_n(\alpha(G))\).

Thus we have the exact identity

\(\max_{\pi\text{ stable}}H(\pi)=F_n(\alpha(G)).\)

This conclusion covers all real-valued couplings, without an integrality assumption.

5. Constructing a rational threshold in polynomial time

We must output a rational threshold, rather than the generally irrational value \(F_n(k)\).

Set

\(g=\frac1{4n^2},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac g8=\frac1{32n^2}.\)

Compute a dyadic rational \(\widehat F\) satisfying

\(|\widehat F-F_n(k-1)|\leq\varepsilon,\)

and output

\(r=\widehat F+\frac g2.\)

The entropy gap proves

\(F_n(k-1)+\frac{3g}{8}\leq r\leq F_n(k)-\frac{3g}{8}.\)

Consequently,

\(\max_{\pi\text{ stable}}H(\pi)\geq r\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(G)\geq k.\)

Both cases have a separation of at least \(3/(32n^2)\). Thus the reduction is valid for exact comparison, including the problem’s equality convention.

Here are details establishing polynomial-time computability of \(\widehat F\).

For the boundary value \(F_n(0)\), only a logarithm of an integer is needed. For an intermediate value, bisect the rational polynomial \(P\) on \([1/2,1]\). Approximating \(t\) within \(\varepsilon/(40n)\) requires \(O(\log n)\) iterations.

To control propagation of this error, define

\(f_\ell(x)=\log_2[n(\ell x+s-1)]-\frac{2\ell}{n}\log_2 x.\)

For \(x\in[1/2,1]\), the denominator \(\ell x+s-1\) is at least \(1\), and

\(|f_\ell'(x)|\leq\frac1{\ln2}\left(\frac{\ell}{\ell x+s-1}+\frac{2\ell}{nx}\right)\leq5n.\)

Hence the root approximation contributes at most \(\varepsilon/8\) error. Approximating each logarithm within \(\varepsilon/8\) contributes at most \(3\varepsilon/8\), since \(2\ell/n\leq2\). Rounding the result to a dyadic rational within \(\varepsilon/4\) leaves total error below \(\varepsilon\).

These logarithms can be computed using rational arithmetic. Normalize a positive rational \(x\) as \(x=2^e y\), with \(1\leq y<2\), and use

\(\ln y=2\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2h+1}}{2h+1},\qquad z=\frac{y-1}{y+1}.\)

Here \(0\leq z\leq1/3\), and the tail after \(T\) terms is at most \((3/4)9^{-T}\). The same series computes \(\ln2\), which is at least \(1/2\); dividing gives \(\log_2 y\). Thus \(O(\log n)\) terms suffice, with polynomially many bits throughout.

The resulting threshold \(r\) has \(O(\log n)\) bits.

6. Size, running time, and conclusion

The constructed society has

\(m=n,\qquad\tau=2n.\)

There are exactly \(2|E(G)|\) strict comparisons. With explicit lists of comparable pairs, its total bit length is

\(L=O((\tau+|E(G)|)\log m)=O(\tau m\log m).\)

A conservative implementation using ordinary rational arithmetic constructs the threshold in \(O((\log m)^8)\) bit operations. Including graph processing and output, the reduction runs in

\(O\!\left(L+\tau m\log m+(\log m)^8\right)\)

bit operations. If partial orders are represented by full relation tables, emitting those tables is covered by the \(O(L)\) term.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction from INDEPENDENT SET. Therefore the stated entropy-threshold problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even under the restrictions proved above. It can belong to \(\mathrm P\) only if \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
