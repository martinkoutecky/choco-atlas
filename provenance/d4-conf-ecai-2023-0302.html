The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support of complete Borda-ranking schedules with rational masses \(\mu_s\) summing to \(1\), revealed round by round, choose one candidate at each round by lexicographically maximizing the mass-weighted distribution of accumulated per-type utilities. For population scales \(N\) with \(N\mu_s\) integral, determine the computation required and whether the rule gives each type the paper's \(1\)-additive proportionality guarantee with benchmark \(T(m-1)/N\), including the limiting behavior as \(N\to\infty\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a fixed set of \(m\geq2\) candidates and \(T\geq1\) rounds. A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq1\) complete ranking schedules \(s\), with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_s\) satisfying \(\sum_s\mu_s=1\); \(\tau\) counts supported schedules, not possible schedules. Each schedule specifies a strict ranking in each round, converted to standard Borda utilities \(v_s^t\).
Masses and type identities are known initially; rankings are revealed only in their respective rounds. Choose one common candidate \(o^t\in C\) irrevocably each round, with \(U_s^0=0\) and \(U_s^t=\sum_{r=1}^t v_s^r(o^r)\).
For candidate \(c\) in round \(t\), define \(h_c(k)=\sum_{s:U_s^{t-1}+v_s^t(c)=k}\mu_s\), for integer \(0\leq k\leq t(m-1)\). Prefer \(c\) to \(d\) when \(h_c(k)<h_d(k)\) at the least \(k\) where their histograms differ. Choose a maximal candidate, breaking identical-histogram ties by a fixed candidate order.
An admissible population scale is an integer \(N\geq1\) with \(N\mu_s\in\mathbb Z\) for every supported type. Its per-individual proportional benchmark at round \(t\) is \(t(m-1)/N\), and its final additive deficit is \(D_N=\max_s\max\{0,T(m-1)/N-U_s^T\}\). Let \(L\) be the total binary encoding length of masses and ranking schedules; encode \(N\) in binary.

Does this rule always satisfy \(U_s^t+1\geq t(m-1)/N\) for every supported type, every round, and every admissible \(N\), or can this additive guarantee fail?
Is computing its choices polynomial-time or NP-hard, with running time measured in \(m,\tau,T,L,\log N\)?
For fixed masses and ranking schedules, what are the limiting behavior of its choices and \(D_N\) as admissible \(N\to\infty\)?

For finitely many individuals with Borda valuations, online leximin guarantees a \(1\)-additive approximation to proportionality and is efficiently computable; no mechanism uniformly guarantees an additive constant below \(1\). [Kahana–Hazon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18024)
The literature search located no result explicitly treating this support-encoded mass-distribution rule and its population-scale limit.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made mass-weighted lexicographic comparison precise by comparing utility histograms from the lowest utility upward, preferring less mass at the first differing level.
  • Resolved outcome ties using a fixed candidate order independent of the population scale.
  • Specified that each round selects one common, irrevocable candidate and utilities remain unnormalized individual Borda utilities.
  • Specified that masses and persistent type identities are initially known, while future rankings remain unavailable.
  • Excluded the one-candidate case to preserve the positive-valuation condition; required positive support size and horizon.
  • Interpreted the proportional benchmark per individual represented by a type, rather than assigning a mass-dependent entitlement to the type.
  • Interpreted the scale limit with the society, candidate set, horizon, and schedules fixed, and measured approximation by the nonnegative worst-type deficit.

Result

The additive guarantee always holds. The choices are computable in polynomial time from the supported schedules and rational masses. For fixed masses and schedules, the choices are independent of \(N\), and \(D_N\to0\) along admissible scales.

The proof uses the finite-population online leximin theorem stated in the question; the required correspondence is exact.

1. Admissible scales and finite-population equivalence

Write each mass in lowest terms as \(\mu_s=a_s/b_s\), and define \(N_0=\operatorname{lcm}_s b_s\). Since \(\gcd(a_s,b_s)=1\), the condition \(N\mu_s\in\mathbb Z\) is equivalent to \(b_s\mid N\). Thus the admissible scales are exactly the positive multiples of \(N_0\).

Fix an admissible \(N\). Replace each supported type \(s\) by \(n_s=N\mu_s\) individuals with that schedule. Positivity and admissibility imply \(n_s\geq1\), and \(\sum_s n_s=N\).

Suppose the replicated population has followed the same choices through round \(t-1\). Its number of individuals receiving accumulated utility \(k\) after choosing candidate \(c\) is

\(H_c(k)=\sum_{s:U_s^{t-1}+v_s^t(c)=k}n_s=Nh_c(k)\).

We now verify the ordering correspondence. For two different histograms, let \(k_0\) be their least differing utility level. If \(H_c(k_0)<H_d(k_0)\), their sorted individual-utility vectors have identical entries below \(k_0\). After the common entries equal to \(k_0\), the vector for \(d\) still has an entry equal to \(k_0\), while the next entry for \(c\) is larger. Hence the sorted vector for \(c\) is lexicographically larger.

Consequently, comparing mass histograms exactly implements finite-population leximin. Equal histograms give equal sorted vectors, so the specified candidate tie order also agrees.

Starting from zero utilities, induction over rounds proves that the two rules make identical choices.

2. The additive guarantee

The finite-population result we use is: with standard Borda utilities, online leximin on \(n\) individuals guarantees, for every individual \(i\) and every prefix \(t\),

\(u_i^t\geq\left\lfloor t(m-1)/n\right\rfloor\).

This stronger integer bound appears in the proof of Theorem 14 of [Kahana–Hazon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18024). Its online rule maximizes the sorted accumulated-utility vector over the current candidates.

All hypotheses hold for the replicated population: rankings are strict, utilities are standard Borda scores, accumulated utilities start at zero, and each decision uses only rankings revealed so far. Repeated schedules are allowed, and every histogram maximizer is a leximin maximizer. If \(N=1\), the sole supported type instead gives the conclusion directly: its top candidate is chosen every round.

Applying the theorem with \(n=N\), and using the equivalence proved above, gives

\(U_s^t\geq\left\lfloor t(m-1)/N\right\rfloor\)

for every supported type, round, and admissible scale. Because \(\lfloor x\rfloor+1>x\), this implies the requested guarantee, even strictly:

\(U_s^t+1>t(m-1)/N\).

Thus the additive guarantee cannot fail under the stated assumptions.

3. Polynomial-time computation in the support encoding

Replication is a proof device; the algorithm need not construct the individuals.

Let \(Q=\prod_s b_s\) and \(w_s=Q\mu_s=a_s(Q/b_s)\). These are integers, and \(\log Q\leq L\). Because the masses sum to one, \(\sum_s w_s=Q\). Define integer histograms

\(G_c(k)=\sum_{s:U_s^{t-1}+v_s^t(c)=k}w_s=Qh_c(k)\).

Multiplication by the common positive factor \(Q\) preserves every comparison.

At round \(t\), compute a candidate’s histogram by sorting the \(\tau\) pairs \((U_s^{t-1}+v_s^t(c),w_s)\) by utility and combining equal keys. The resulting list has at most \(\tau\) entries. Compare two lists by merging their utility keys, treating missing entries as zero, and stopping at the least differing key. A scan through the candidates maintains the best candidate, respecting the fixed order for equal histograms.

After selecting it, update all \(\tau\) accumulated utilities. This uses only the current round’s rankings.

Over all rounds, sorting and comparison require

\(O\!\left(Tm\tau\log(\tau+1)\right)\)

integer operations, in addition to polynomial-time preprocessing. Histogram weights have \(O(L)\) bits, since every histogram total is at most \(Q\). Utility keys have \(O(\log(Tm+1))\) bits. Thus exact arithmetic also has polynomial bit complexity.

The choice algorithm does not use \(N\). If admissibility must be checked, testing \(b_s\mid N\) for every type adds only polynomial time in \(L+\log N\).

Therefore, computing the choices is polynomial-time solvable in the parameters specified in the question.

4. Choices and deficits as the population scale grows

The rule’s inputs and tie order are fixed, and its definition contains no \(N\). Therefore its choices—and consequently all \(U_s^T\)—are identical at every admissible scale.

Put \(A=T(m-1)\) and \(u_*=\min_s U_s^T\). The deficit has the exact expression

\(D_N=\max\{0,A/N-u_*\}\).

Indeed, among supported types, the largest shortfall occurs at the smallest accumulated utility. Since \(u_*\geq0\),

\(0\leq D_N\leq A/N\),

so \(D_N\to0\) along admissible \(N\to\infty\). More precisely:

  • If \(u_*=0\), then \(D_N=A/N>0\) at every finite admissible scale.
  • If \(u_*>0\), then \(D_N=0\) whenever \(N\geq A/u_*\).

The first case can occur. Take two candidates, one round, and two equally weighted types with opposite rankings. Both candidates have the same histogram, so the fixed tie order selects one. The final utilities are \(1\) and \(0\); admissible scales are the positive even integers, and \(D_N=1/N\).

Thus vanishing deficit need not mean that every type eventually receives positive utility: the fixed utility profile is compared with a benchmark that tends to zero.
