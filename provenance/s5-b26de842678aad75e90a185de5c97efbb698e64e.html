The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) criteria and a society of \(\tau\) evaluator types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type reports positive rational ratios \(a^t_{ij}\) on a set \(E_t\) of criterion pairs, find \(y\in\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(y_n=0\) minimizing \(F_\mu(y)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}\bigl(\log a^t_{ij}-(y_i-y_j)\bigr)^2\), to additive objective error at most \(\epsilon\). When is the minimizing priority vector \(w_i=\exp(y_i)/\sum_j\exp(y_j)\) unique, and what is the algorithm’s bit complexity in \(n,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\epsilon\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(n\ge 2\) be the number of criteria. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge 1\) evaluator types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; write \(m=n\) when using the generic society-size notation.
Type \(t\) specifies \(E_t\subseteq\{(i,j):1\le i<j\le n\}\) and positive binary-rational ratios \(a^t_{ij}\). Reverse comparisons are implicitly reciprocal, and diagonal comparisons equal one; each unordered pair is counted once. Empty comparison sets and disconnected comparison graphs are allowed.
With natural logarithms, define \(F_\mu(y)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}(\log a^t_{ij}-(y_i-y_j))^2\) on \(\{y\in\mathbb R^n:y_n=0\}\). For a binary-rational tolerance \(0<\epsilon<1\), the computational problem is to find a rational \(\widehat y\) in this domain satisfying \(F_\mu(\widehat y)\le\inf_{y:y_n=0}F_\mu(y)+\epsilon\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including the tolerance; logarithms are not supplied as exact real-number inputs.
The priority vector associated with an exact minimizer is \(w_i=\exp(y_i)/\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(y_j)\).

Is this additive-accuracy optimization problem solvable in time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\), or is it NP-hard?
When does an exact minimizer exist and yield a unique normalized priority vector, and what bit-complexity bounds hold in these parameters?

For a single incomplete reciprocal comparison matrix, the normalized logarithmic least-squares priority vector is unique exactly when its comparison graph is connected ([Bozóki–Fülöp–Rónyai](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mcm.2010.02.047)).
For complete reciprocal matrices from multiple evaluators with positive weights summing to one, weighted group logarithmic least squares has an explicit unique normalized solution ([Andrecut](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1976)); this covers the complete-comparison special case, without establishing the stated general bit-complexity target.

Result

The additive-accuracy optimization problem is solvable in deterministic polynomial time in \(n,\tau,L,\log(1/\epsilon)\).

An exact real minimizer always exists. Its normalized priority vector is unique if and only if the union comparison graph is connected. Disconnected individual comparison graphs do not prevent uniqueness when their union is connected.

The proof below includes explicit bounds on logarithm precision, rational output length, and exact symbolic representations. Throughout, binary-rational inputs are understood in the usual explicit binary encoding of numerators and denominators.

1. The quadratic formulation and its nullspace

Index the reported comparisons by
\(\mathcal O=\{(t,i,j):(i,j)\in E_t\}\), and put
\(q=|\mathcal O|\le \tau n(n-1)/2\).

For an observation \(o=(t,i,j)\), define
\(b_o=\log a^t_{ij}\). Let \(B\) be the \(q\times n\) matrix whose corresponding row is \(e_i^{\mathsf T}-e_j^{\mathsf T}\), and let \(W\) be diagonal with \(W_{oo}=\mu_t\). Then

\[ F_\mu(y)=(b-By)^{\mathsf T}W(b-By). \]

Define the union comparison graph
\(G=(\{1,\ldots,n\},\bigcup_t E_t)\), and let its connected components be \(C_1,\ldots,C_c\). Because every reported observation has positive weight,

\[ x^{\mathsf T}B^{\mathsf T}WBx =\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}(x_i-x_j)^2. \]

Consequently, this expression vanishes exactly when \(x\) is constant on each connected component of \(G\). This also characterizes \(\ker B\).

Choose one root in each component, choosing vertex \(n\) in its component, and temporarily impose zero coordinates at all roots. There are \(r=n-c\) remaining variables. Let \(R\) insert these variables into the full vector, and set

\[ \bar B=BR,\qquad H=\bar B^{\mathsf T}W\bar B. \]

If \(r>0\), then \(H\) is positive definite: zero quadratic form would imply that \(Rx\) is constant on every component, and the zero root coordinates force all those constants to vanish.

Thus the uniquely rooted minimizer is

\[ x^*=H^{-1}\bar B^{\mathsf T}Wb,\qquad y^*=Rx^*. \]

If \(r=0\), the graph has no edges, and \(F_\mu\) is identically zero.

Every vector can be shifted separately on its components to satisfy the root conditions, without changing any reported difference. Therefore the rooted minimum is also the minimum on the original domain \(y_n=0\).

Hence an exact real minimizer always exists, including when comparisons are empty or disconnected. All minimizers in the original domain are obtained from \(y^*\) by adding componentwise constants, with zero constant on the component containing \(n\). Their affine dimension is \(c-1\).

2. Exactly when the priority vector is unique

The normalization map is injective on the domain \(y_n=0\), since

\[ \frac{w_i}{w_n}=\exp(y_i),\qquad y_i=\log\frac{w_i}{w_n}. \]

It follows that the normalized priority vector is unique exactly when \(c=1\), namely when \(G\) is connected.

More explicitly, in the disconnected case, each component has fixed internal proportions

\[ v_i^{(C)} =\frac{\exp(y_i^*)}{\sum_{j\in C}\exp(y_j^*)}, \qquad i\in C. \]

The minimizing priority vectors are exactly

\[ w_i=P_Cv_i^{(C)},\qquad P_C>0,\qquad \sum_C P_C=1. \]

To verify the converse, given such component masses, set \(y_i=\log(w_i/w_n)\). Within each component, its coordinate differences equal those of \(y^*\); therefore every reported residual is unchanged.

For one evaluator this specializes to the connectedness characterization in [Bozóki–Fülöp–Rónyai](https://real.mtak.hu/65496/1/BozokiFulopRonyai2010Manuscript.pdf). For complete comparisons, the normal equations also give the weighted row-logarithm formula appearing in [Andrecut](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.1976):

\[ z_i=\frac1n\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j=1}^n\log a^t_{ij}, \qquad y_i^*=z_i-z_n. \]

Here reverse and diagonal entries are interpreted as in the question.

3. A perturbation bound for additive accuracy

The crucial estimate avoids any dependence on the inverse of the smallest positive eigenvalue.

Suppose rational approximations \(\widetilde b_o\) satisfy
\(|\widetilde b_o-b_o|\le\delta\) for every observation. Solve the rational system exactly:

\[ H\widehat x=\bar B^{\mathsf T}W\widetilde b, \qquad \widehat y=R\widehat x. \]

Write \(d=\widetilde b-b\) and \(e=\widehat x-x^*\). Subtracting the normal equations gives

\[ He=\bar B^{\mathsf T}Wd. \]

Use the weighted norm \(\|u\|_W^2=u^{\mathsf T}Wu\). Multiplying by \(e^{\mathsf T}\) and applying Cauchy–Schwarz yields

\[ \|\bar Be\|_W^2 =(\bar Be)^{\mathsf T}Wd \le \|\bar Be\|_W\|d\|_W. \]

If the first norm vanishes, the desired bound is immediate; otherwise divide by it. In either case,

\[ \|\bar Be\|_W^2\le\|d\|_W^2. \]

The normal equations for \(x^*\) make the cross term in the quadratic expansion vanish. Hence

\[ F_\mu(\widehat y)-F_\mu(y^*) =e^{\mathsf T}He \le \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E_t}d_{tij}^2. \]

In particular,

\[ F_\mu(\widehat y)-\min F_\mu \le S\delta^2, \qquad S:=\sum_t\mu_t|E_t| \le\frac{n(n-1)}2. \]

Choose the least nonnegative integer \(p\) for which
\(n^2\,2^{-2p}\le\epsilon\), and take \(\delta=2^{-p}\). This condition can be checked using exact rational arithmetic, and

\[ p=O\!\left(\log n+\log(1/\epsilon)\right). \]

The resulting rational solution satisfies the required additive guarantee. Tiny evaluator masses do not require additional logarithm precision.

4. Computing the logarithms in polynomial bit complexity

Under the stated encoding, every positive input ratio satisfies
\(2^{-L}\le a\le2^L\), so \(|\log a|\le L\).

Write \(a=2^k u\), where \(1\le u<2\). The integer \(k\), with \(|k|\le L\), can be found by binary shifts and comparisons. Put

\[ z=\frac{u-1}{u+1},\qquad 0\le z<\frac13. \]

The convergent identity

\[ \log u=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1} \]

has the following explicit truncation bound:

\[ \left|\log u- 2\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}\right| \le\frac{2z^{2K+1}}{1-z^2} \le 9^{-K}. \]

The same formula computes \(\log2\), using \(z=1/3\). Combining these approximations in
\(\log a=k\log2+\log u\) gives error at most
\((|k|+1)9^{-K}\).

Choose \(K\) so this is at most \(2^{-(p+2)}\), and round the resulting rational to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-p}\). Rounding contributes at most \(2^{-(p+1)}\), so the final error is strictly less than \(2^{-p}\).

The required number of terms is

\[ K=O\!\left(p+\log(L+1)\right). \]

All truncated sums can be evaluated exactly with polynomially many bits. Indeed, if \(z=P/Q\), a common denominator for the first \(K\) terms is

\[ Q^{2K-1}\prod_{j=0}^{K-1}(2j+1), \]

whose bit length is \(O(KL+K\log K)\). Exact rational arithmetic and the final rounding therefore take polynomial time.

Thus each logarithm approximation has the form
\(\widetilde b_o=k_o/2^p\), with integer \(k_o\) satisfying
\(|k_o|\le2^p(L+1)\).

5. Rational solving and output-length bounds

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=u_t/v_t\), and define

\[ D=\prod_t v_t,\qquad d_t=D\mu_t. \]

Then \(D\) and every \(d_t\) are integers,
\(\log_2D\le L\), and \(\sum_t d_t=D\).

Clear denominators in the normal equations by setting

\[ A=DH=\bar B^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{diag}(d_t)\bar B, \qquad C=\bar B^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{diag}(d_t)k. \]

Here the diagonal entries are indexed by observations, repeating \(d_t\) for each comparison of type \(t\). The rational system is

\[ A\widehat x=\frac{C}{2^p}. \]

For any unordered pair, its aggregated integer weight is at most \(D\). Consequently,

\[ |A_{ij}|\le nD,\qquad |C_i|\le nD\,2^p(L+1). \]

When \(r>0\), \(A\) is nonsingular. Hadamard’s determinant bound gives

\[ |\det A|\le(\sqrt n\,nD)^r. \]

If \(A_i(C)\) replaces column \(i\) by \(C\), the same column-norm bound gives

\[ |\det A_i(C)| \le(\sqrt n\,nD)^r\,2^p(L+1). \]

Cramer’s rule therefore shows that

\[ \widehat x_i=\frac{\det A_i(C)}{2^p\det A} \]

has numerator and denominator bit lengths

\[ O\!\left(n(L+\log n)+p\right). \]

Reduction to lowest terms cannot increase these lengths. Thus the full output can be encoded using

\[ O\!\left(n^2(L+\log n)+n\log(1/\epsilon)\right) \]

bits.

These bounds also yield a polynomial-time exact solve. For completeness, positive definiteness ensures that every leading principal block is nonsingular. During Gaussian elimination, each remaining matrix entry is a bordered minor divided by a leading principal determinant; the same identity holds for the augmented column \(C\), by the block determinant formula. Hadamard’s bound therefore controls the intermediate numerator and denominator lengths by a polynomial in \(n,L,p\). Back substitution uses polynomially many further rational operations.

Together with the logarithm computation, this proves the claimed deterministic polynomial bit complexity. Only the listed support is processed; no enumeration of possible evaluator types occurs.

Bounds on the magnitude of minimizers

There is also a bound independent of the smallest mass. Let

\[ M=\max_{o\in\mathcal O}|b_o|, \]

taking \(M=0\) when there are no observations. In a connected component with \(s\) vertices, sort the minimizing coordinates as
\(y_{(1)}\le\cdots\le y_{(s)}\).

For a consecutive gap \(g=y_{(k+1)}-y_{(k)}\), sum the normal equations over the upper \(s-k\) vertices. Internal edges cancel. Orient every crossing observation from the upper side to the lower side, writing its signed logarithm as \(\beta_o\). Then

\[ \sum_{\text{crossing }o}\mu_{t(o)}(y_i-y_j) =\sum_{\text{crossing }o}\mu_{t(o)}\beta_o. \]

Every left-hand difference is at least \(g\), and every \(\beta_o\le M\). Connectedness ensures positive total crossing weight. Division by that weight gives \(g\le M\). Summing the consecutive gaps proves

\[ \max_{i\in C}y_i-\min_{i\in C}y_i\le(s-1)M. \]

For the rooted minimizer, therefore,

\[ |y_i^*|\le(n-1)L. \]

The rational solution obeys the analogous bound
\(|\widehat y_i|\le(n-1)(L+1)\), since it exactly minimizes the quadratic with targets \(\widetilde b\).

6. Exact representations and priority approximation

The rational matrix \(A\) also provides a polynomial-length symbolic exact representation:

\[ y_i^*=\sum_{o\in\mathcal O}\lambda_{io}\log a_o, \qquad \lambda_{io}\in\mathbb Q. \]

Indeed, these coefficients are entries of
\(A^{-1}\bar B^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{diag}(d_t)\), followed by root insertion. Cofactor bounds give each coefficient bit length
\(O(n(L+\log n))\). There are at most \(nq\) coefficients.

Consequently,

\[ \exp(y_i^*)=\prod_o a_o^{\lambda_{io}} \]

is a positive algebraic number, and normalization gives a symbolic exact priority vector of polynomial length.

This does not imply polynomial length in a dense minimal-polynomial representation. Consider two criteria and two evaluator types, both comparing the single pair, with

\[ \mu_1=2^{-k},\quad \mu_2=1-2^{-k}, \qquad a^1_{12}=2,\quad a^2_{12}=1. \]

The unique minimizer and first priority coordinate are

\[ y_1^*=2^{-k}\log2,\qquad w_1=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}, \qquad \alpha=2^{1/2^k}. \]

The polynomial \(X^{2^k}-2\) is irreducible by Eisenstein’s criterion at \(2\): every nonleading coefficient is divisible by \(2\), and its constant coefficient is not divisible by \(4\). Therefore \(\alpha\) has degree \(2^k\). Since
\(\alpha=w_1/(1-w_1)\), the fields generated by \(\alpha\) and \(w_1\) coincide, so \(w_1\) has the same degree. The input length is \(O(k)\), while a dense minimal-polynomial representation requires exponentially many coefficient positions.

Arbitrary-precision numerical priorities

Rational approximations to the rooted minimizer and its priority vector can nevertheless be computed in polynomial time at any requested binary precision.

To see the coordinate bound, choose a spanning tree in each component. For any rooted vector \(x\), the path from a root to a vertex has at most \(n-1\) edges. Cauchy–Schwarz along these paths, followed by summation over vertices, gives

\[ \|x\|_2^2 \le n^2\sum_{\text{forest edges}}(x_i-x_j)^2. \]

Every observed edge has aggregated weight at least \(2^{-L}\). Hence

\[ x^{\mathsf T}Hx\ge\frac{2^{-L}}{n^2}\|x\|_2^2. \]

Combining this with Section 3 yields

\[ \|\widehat y-y^*\|_\infty \le n^2\,2^{L/2}\,2^{-p}. \]

Thus coordinate error at most \(2^{-h}\) requires only
\(p=O(L+\log n+h)\).

For the normalization map, direct differentiation gives

\[ \frac{\partial w_i}{\partial y_j} =w_i(\mathbf1_{i=j}-w_j), \qquad \sum_j\left|\frac{\partial w_i}{\partial y_j}\right| =2w_i(1-w_i)\le\frac12. \]

Integration along a line segment therefore proves

\[ \|w(\widehat y)-w(y^*)\|_\infty \le\frac12\|\widehat y-y^*\|_\infty. \]

Finally, exponentiation also has polynomial bit complexity here. Shift \(\widehat y\) by its largest coordinate, so the shifted coordinates lie in \([-R,0]\), with \(R=O(n(L+1))\). For \(z\in[0,R]\), approximate \(\exp(z)\) by its rational Taylor polynomial \(T_{N-1}(z)\). The remainder satisfies

\[ 0\le\exp(z)-T_{N-1}(z) \le e^R\frac{R^N}{N!} \le e^R\left(\frac{eR}{N}\right)^N. \]

Taking an integer \(N\ge8R+u+1\) bounds this by \(2^{-u}\). Reciprocation then approximates \(\exp(-z)\) with no greater absolute error, because both denominators are at least one.

One shifted exponent is exactly one, so both normalization denominators are at least one. If each exponent approximation has error at most \(\rho\), exact rational normalization has coordinate error at most \((n+1)\rho\). Taking
\(\rho\le2^{-h}/(2(n+1))\), together with the preceding coordinate precision, gives priority error at most \(2^{-h}\). The number of terms and all rational bit lengths are polynomial in \(n,\tau,L,h\).
