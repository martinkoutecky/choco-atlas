The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite complete agent types \(\Theta\) with masses \(\mu_\theta\), bad types \(O\) with per-capita supplies \(\rho_o\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_o\rho_o\le1\), and positive additive disutilities \(d_{\theta o}\), compute an assignment \(x_{\theta o}\ge0\) minimizing \(\sum_{\theta,o}x_{\theta o}\log d_{\theta o}\), subject to \(\sum_o x_{\theta o}\le\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta o}=\rho_o\), and return an atomless allocation realizing \(x\) that is \(m\)NDW\(^{-}_\infty\), Pareto efficient, and XEF.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Normalize \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\), and represent each type by an atomless interval of length \(\mu_\theta\).
There are \(m\ge1\) bad types \(O\), binary rational supplies \(\rho_o\ge0\) with \(\sum_o\rho_o\le1\), and binary rational disutilities \(d_{\theta o}>0\) for every pair. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Individual bads remain indivisible: an allocation assigns each person a finite multiset \(B\) of bad types, measurably, with aggregate count \(\rho_o\) for each type. Disutility is additive, \(D_\theta(B)=\sum_{o\in B}d_{\theta o}\), counting multiplicities.
An allocation is \(\mathrm{mNDW}^{-}_\infty\) if it first maximizes the measure of people with nonempty bundles and, among those allocations, minimizes \(\int_{B(a)\ne\emptyset}\log D_{\theta(a)}(B(a))\,da\).
XEF means that, for almost every pair of people \(a,b\), removing any one bad from a nonempty \(B(a)\) leaves \(D_{\theta(a)}(B(a)\setminus\{o\})\le D_{\theta(a)}(B(b))\). Pareto efficiency excludes a feasible measurable allocation weakly reducing disutility almost everywhere and strictly reducing it on a set of positive measure; competing allocations may assign multiple bads per person.
The computational problem is to find an exact minimizer \(x_{\theta o}\ge0\) of \(\sum_{\theta,o}x_{\theta o}\log d_{\theta o}\), subject to \(\sum_o x_{\theta o}\le\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_{\theta o}=\rho_o\), together with a finite rational partition realizing these masses by assigning at most one bad per person, which is \(\mathrm{mNDW}^{-}_\infty\), Pareto efficient, and XEF. Any optimal tie is allowed.

Is this exact computational problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) in the binary Turing model, or is it NP-hard?

For finite societies with \(m\le n\) indivisible bads and additive valuations, an XEF, Pareto-efficient allocation minimizing Nash diswelfare over a largest set of negatively affected agents is reported computable in \(O(n^3)\) time. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00422v2).
No result settling this exact atomless computational problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the unspecified total society mass to one, matching the per-capita supply bound.
  • Specified supplies and positive disutilities as binary rationals, and used exact optimization in the binary Turing model.
  • Interpreted bad types as repeated indivisible units assigned to individual people, rather than divisible quantities consumed by each person.
  • Defined mNDW-minus-infinity by first maximizing recipient mass and then minimizing integrated log disutility over recipients; empty bundles are excluded from the logarithm.
  • Defined atomless XEF almost everywhere and Pareto improvements by strict improvement on positive measure; Pareto comparisons allow arbitrary finite multiset bundles.
  • Specified a finite rational partition as the representation of the returned atomless allocation and allowed arbitrary optimal ties.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Exact polynomial-time solvability

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) in the binary Turing model.

The key is to approximate the logarithmic coefficients sufficiently accurately to preserve the sign of every nonzero simple-cycle cost. An exact optimum for these rational approximations is then an exact optimum for the original objective. Pareto efficiency follows from positive weights obtained from the original residual graph.

1. Maximizing recipient mass forces singleton bundles

Write \(R=\sum_{o\in O}\rho_o\), so \(0\le R\le1\). For a feasible allocation \(A\), let \(C_A(a)=|B_A(a)|\), counting multiplicities. Feasibility gives

\(\displaystyle \int C_A(a)\,da=\sum_{o\in O}\rho_o=R.\)

Since \(\mathbf 1_{\{C_A(a)>0\}}\le C_A(a)\), the measure of recipients is at most \(R\).

This bound is attainable. For example, the rational masses \(x_{\theta o}=\mu_\theta\rho_o\) satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_o x_{\theta o}=\mu_\theta R\le\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_\theta x_{\theta o}=\rho_o.\)

Partitioning each type interval accordingly gives an allocation with at most one bad per person and recipient mass \(R\).

Moreover, any allocation attaining recipient mass \(R\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \int\bigl(C_A(a)-\mathbf 1_{\{C_A(a)>0\}}\bigr)\,da=0.\)

The integrand is nonnegative, so it vanishes almost everywhere. Thus every allocation maximizing recipient mass assigns at most one bad per person almost everywhere.

Consequently, among allocations maximizing recipient mass, the logarithmic objective is exactly

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{o\in O}x_{\theta o}\log d_{\theta o}.\)

Therefore, an exact minimizer of the stated linear program, realized by singleton bundles, is \(\mathrm{mNDW}^{-}_\infty\).

2. Transportation formulation and cycle optimality

Introduce a dummy column \(0\), representing an empty bundle, with

\(\displaystyle \rho_0=1-R,\qquad d_{\theta0}=1,\qquad c_{\theta0}=0.\)

For real bads, put \(c_{\theta o}=\log d_{\theta o}\). The original program is equivalent to the transportation problem

\(\displaystyle \sum_{o\in O\cup\{0\}}x_{\theta o}=\mu_\theta,\qquad \sum_\theta x_{\theta o}=\rho_o,\qquad x_{\theta o}\ge0.\)

Indeed, the dummy entries are uniquely determined by the original row slacks.

Let \(N=\tau+m+1\), the number of row and column vertices. Represent each variable by an arc from its row vertex to its column vertex. Give every arc capacity \(2\); this is redundant because the total transported mass is \(1\).

For a feasible \(x\), its residual graph contains:

  • every forward arc \(\theta\to o\), with cost \(c_{\theta o}\), since \(x_{\theta o}\le1<2\);
  • the reverse arc \(o\to\theta\), with cost \(-c_{\theta o}\), whenever \(x_{\theta o}>0\).

Cycle optimality criterion. A feasible \(x\) is optimal if and only if its residual graph has no negative-cost directed simple cycle.

To prove necessity, augment a sufficiently small positive amount around a negative residual cycle. Row and column sums remain unchanged, nonnegativity is preserved, and the objective strictly decreases.

For sufficiency, take any other feasible \(y\). Orient each positive difference \(y_{\theta o}-x_{\theta o}\) forward, and each negative difference backward, with its absolute value as flow. Equality of the row and column sums makes this a nonnegative circulation. Every arc carrying this circulation belongs to the residual graph of \(x\).

A finite nonnegative circulation decomposes into directed simple cycles with positive coefficients: follow positive-flow arcs until a vertex repeats, subtract the minimum flow on the resulting cycle, and repeat. Each subtraction eliminates at least one positive-flow arc. Hence

\(\displaystyle c\cdot(y-x)=\sum_j\lambda_j c(C_j),\qquad \lambda_j>0.\)

If all residual simple cycles have nonnegative cost, then \(c\cdot y\ge c\cdot x\). This proves the criterion for arbitrary real costs.

3. Polynomial separation of nonzero cycle costs

Write each input disutility as \(d_{\theta o}=a_{\theta o}/b_{\theta o}\), with positive binary integers. Let \(B\ge1\) bound the bit lengths of all these numerators and denominators. We may take \(B\le L\).

A directed simple cycle has at most \(N\) arcs. Its cost has the form

\(\displaystyle c(C)=\log(P/Q),\)

where \(P,Q\) are positive integers obtained by multiplying numerators and denominators, reversing their roles for reverse arcs. Dummy arcs contribute the factor \(1\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle P<2^{NB},\qquad Q<2^{NB}.\)

If \(P>Q\), then

\(\displaystyle \log(P/Q)=\int_Q^P\frac{dt}{t} \ge\frac{P-Q}{P}\ge\frac1P>2^{-NB}.\)

The same argument with \(P,Q\) interchanged handles \(P<Q\). Thus, setting \(\delta=2^{-NB}\), every simple-cycle cost satisfies

\(\displaystyle c(C)=0\quad\text{or}\quad |c(C)|>\delta.\)

Crucially, this bound concerns cycle costs, which do not involve the amounts of transported mass.

Choose

\(\displaystyle H=NB+\lceil\log_2N\rceil+3.\)

Compute dyadic rational costs \(\widehat c_{\theta o}=k_{\theta o}/2^H\), with integer \(k_{\theta o}\), such that

\(\displaystyle |\widehat c_{\theta o}-c_{\theta o}|\le2^{-H},\)

and set dummy costs exactly to zero. Reverse costs are the negatives of the corresponding forward costs.

For every simple cycle,

\(\displaystyle |\widehat c(C)-c(C)|\le N2^{-H}\le\delta/8.\)

Hence every originally negative simple cycle remains strictly negative after rounding.

Now let \(x^*\) be any exact optimum for the rounded costs. Its residual graph has no negative rounded-cost cycle. It therefore has no negative original-cost cycle. By Section 2, \(x^*\) is an exact optimum for the original logarithmic objective.

An originally zero-cost cycle may acquire either sign after rounding. This only selects among original optima; the preceding argument applies to every rounded optimum.

4. Computing the rounded costs and optimum in polynomial time

Computing logarithms with certified precision

For completeness, the required logarithmic approximations can be computed using rational arithmetic.

Given \(r=a/b>0\), find an integer \(s\) such that \(r=2^s z\) and \(1\le z<2\). Binary comparisons give such an \(s\) with \(|s|\le B\).

For \(1\le z\le2\), put \(t=(z-1)/(z+1)\), so \(0\le t\le1/3\). Integrating the geometric series for \(1/(1-t^2)\) gives

\(\displaystyle \log z=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2j+1}}{2j+1}.\)

Let \(S_J(t)\) denote the first \(J\) terms. Its remainder satisfies

\(\displaystyle 0\le\log z-S_J(t) \le\frac{2t^{2J+1}}{1-t^2} \le3^{-2J}.\)

The same formula computes \(\log2\) using \(t=1/3\). Thus

\(\displaystyle sS_J(1/3)+S_J(t)\)

approximates \(\log r\) with error at most \((B+1)3^{-2J}\). Choosing \(J=O(H+\log(B+1))\) makes this error at most \(2^{-(H+2)}\).

All terms are rational. There are polynomially many terms, and their numerators and denominators have polynomial bit length in \(B,H\). Exact rational summation is therefore polynomial-time. Rounding the resulting approximation to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-H}\) gives error at most

\(\displaystyle 2^{-(H+2)}+2^{-(H+1)}<2^{-H},\)

as required.

Also, \(|\log r|<B\), so each integer cost \(k_{\theta o}\) has bit length \(O(H+\log(B+1))\).

Solving the integer transportation instance

Let \(Q\) be the product of the denominators of the original masses and supplies. Then \(\log_2Q=O(L)\), and all numbers

\(\displaystyle Q\mu_\theta,\qquad Q\rho_o,\qquad Q\rho_0\)

are integers.

Scale the transportation variables by \(Q\), and multiply the rounded objective by \(2^H\). The resulting instance has:

  • \(N\) vertices and \(\tau(m+1)\) transportation arcs;
  • integer supplies and demands of bit length \(O(L)\);
  • redundant integer arc capacities \(2Q\);
  • integer costs \(k_{\theta o}\) of polynomial bit length.

A feasible integral starting flow is obtained greedily by matching remaining row supply with remaining column demand.

We use the standard polynomial-time integer minimum-cost flow algorithm. More explicitly, minimum-mean cycle cancellation terminates after \(O(n e\log(nC))\) cancellations for integer costs of maximum absolute value \(C\), with each minimum-mean cycle obtainable in \(O(ne)\) arithmetic operations. This is the integer-cost result of [Goldberg and Tarjan, *Finding Minimum-Cost Circulations by Canceling Negative Cycles*, Theorem 3.7](https://sirius.cs.put.poznan.pl/~inf84789/acm/p873-goldberg.pdf).

Here the network size and \(\log(C+1)\) are polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Flows remain integral and bounded by \(2Q\); the integer cost calculations also have polynomial bit length. Thus the algorithm runs in polynomial time in the binary Turing model.

Dividing the resulting integral flow by \(Q\) produces a rational \(x^*\). By Section 3, it exactly minimizes the original logarithmic objective.

5. Rational partition and XEF

Order the supported types and represent them by consecutive intervals of lengths \(\mu_\theta\). Within each type interval, create consecutive subintervals of lengths

\(\displaystyle x^*_{\theta1},\ldots,x^*_{\theta m},x^*_{\theta0}.\)

Assign bad \(o\) to each person in the corresponding real-bad subinterval, and assign the empty bundle on the dummy subinterval.

Every endpoint is a multiple of \(1/Q\), because both the type masses and the computed entries are multiples of \(1/Q\). There are at most \(\tau(m+1)\) subintervals, and all endpoints have polynomial bit length.

This partition realizes the exact supplies and assigns at most one bad per person. Section 1 proves that it is \(\mathrm{mNDW}^{-}_\infty\).

It is also XEF: removing the sole bad from a nonempty bundle leaves disutility zero, whereas every bundle has nonnegative disutility. The required inequality therefore holds pointwise.

6. Pareto efficiency against arbitrary finite bundles

It remains to address competitors that may assign several bads per person. We prove that every singleton realization of an optimal transportation solution is Pareto efficient under the question’s full definition.

Consider the residual graph of \(x^*\) with the original logarithmic costs. By Section 3, it has no negative cycle.

Add an auxiliary source with zero-cost arcs to every vertex. Let \(h(v)\) be the shortest-path distance from this source to \(v\). These distances are finite: every vertex is reachable, and any repeated-vertex portion of a walk is a nonnegative-cost cycle that can be removed. Thus a minimum is attained among finitely many simple paths.

For every residual arc \(u\to v\),

\(\displaystyle h(v)\le h(u)+c(u,v).\)

Every forward arc is residual, so

\(\displaystyle \log d_{\theta o}\ge h(o)-h(\theta).\)

If \(x^*_{\theta o}>0\), the reverse arc is also residual and gives the opposite inequality. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \log d_{\theta o}=h(o)-h(\theta) \quad\text{whenever }x^*_{\theta o}>0.\)

Define strictly positive weights and prices by

\(\displaystyle w_\theta=e^{h(\theta)},\qquad p_o=e^{h(o)}.\)

Exponentiating yields

\(\displaystyle w_\theta d_{\theta o}\ge p_o,\)

with equality on every positive entry of \(x^*\).

For any finite multiset \(B\), additivity, including multiplicities, now gives

\(\displaystyle w_\theta D_\theta(B) =\sum_{o\in B}w_\theta d_{\theta o} \ge\sum_{o\in B}p_o.\)

Therefore every feasible measurable allocation \(A'\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle \int w_{\theta(a)}D_{\theta(a)}(B_{A'}(a))\,da \ge\sum_{o\in O}p_o\rho_o.\)

For the constructed allocation \(A^*\), equality holds because every assigned singleton corresponds to a positive entry:

\(\displaystyle \int w_{\theta(a)}D_{\theta(a)}(B_{A^*}(a))\,da =\sum_{o\in O}p_o\rho_o.\)

These integrals are finite. Indeed, there are finitely many types and bad types, all weights and disutilities are finite, and the integral of the total bundle cardinality is \(R\).

Suppose a feasible \(A'\) weakly reduced disutility almost everywhere and strictly reduced it on a set of positive measure. The disutility reduction would be a nonnegative integrable function positive on a set of positive measure. Since the finitely many weights have a strictly positive minimum, its weighted integral would be strictly positive. Hence

\(\displaystyle \int w_{\theta(a)}D_{\theta(a)}(B_{A'}(a))\,da < \int w_{\theta(a)}D_{\theta(a)}(B_{A^*}(a))\,da,\)

contradicting the preceding lower bound and equality.

Thus \(A^*\) is Pareto efficient even against feasible measurable reallocations assigning arbitrary finite multisets.

7. Conclusion

The algorithm returns an exact rational minimizer and a finite rational partition in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). The realized allocation is \(\mathrm{mNDW}^{-}_\infty\), Pareto efficient under the specified unrestricted comparison class, and XEF. The proof covers zero supplies and all optimal ties.
