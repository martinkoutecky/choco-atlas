The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), baseline seed-membership masses, campaign-specific rational contact intensities \(\lambda^a_{st}\), and per-capita budget \(\kappa\), choose additional seed masses \(x_{t,10},x_{t,01},x_{t,11}\) subject to the available-mass constraints and \(\sum_t(x_{t,10}+x_{t,01}+2x_{t,11})\leq\kappa\). Return an allocation whose limiting balanced mass \(B(x)=\sum_t(z_{t,00}+z_{t,11})\) is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\), where \(z\) is induced by the independent mean-field cascade with \(q^r_{t,j}=1-\exp(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}a^r_{s,j})\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=2\) campaigns. A society is supplied by its support: \(\tau\) types of positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Each type includes its baseline seed-membership vector \(b_t\in\{0,1\}^2\); split mixed baseline memberships into separate types with inherited contact intensities.
Inputs also include finite nonnegative rational intensities \(\lambda^j_{st}\), a rational per-capita budget \(0\leq\kappa\leq2\), and rational accuracy \(0<\epsilon<1\). Let \(L\) be the total binary input length.
Choose nonnegative additional seed masses \(x_{t,d}\), for \(d\in\{10,01,11\}\), satisfying \(\sum_d x_{t,d}\leq\mu_t\), \(x_{t,d}=0\) whenever \(d\) includes a campaign already in \(b_t\), and \(\sum_t(x_{t,10}+x_{t,01}+2x_{t,11})\leq\kappa\). The selected portions are disjoint within each type; a portion selected with vector \(d\) starts exposed to \(b_t\lor d\), and the remainder starts exposed to \(b_t\).
Let \(z^r_{t,v}\) be the mass exposed to exactly the campaigns in \(v\in\{0,1\}^2\) after round \(r\). Initially, \(a^0_{t,j}\) is the seed mass exposed to campaign \(j\). In round \(r+1\), each currently missing campaign \(j\) is acquired independently with probability \(q^r_{t,j}=1-\exp(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}a^r_{s,j})\); apply these transition probabilities to each state mass to obtain \(z^{r+1}\).
Exposure persists, and \(a^{r+1}_{t,j}\) is only the mass newly acquiring campaign \(j\) in that round: every exposed portion transmits once per campaign. Define \(z_{t,v}=\lim_{r\to\infty}z^r_{t,v}\), \(B(x)=\sum_t(z_{t,00}+z_{t,11})\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sup_{x\text{ feasible}}B(x)\).

Can an algorithm return a feasible rational allocation satisfying \(B(x)\geq\frac{1-1/e}{2}\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), or is achieving this approximation guarantee NP-hard?

For finite networks, maximizing the expected number exposed to both campaigns or neither is NP-hard; a greedy algorithm compared with the empty allocation achieves factor \((1-1/e)/2\) for independent and fully correlated cascades, with additive sampling error. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.01491)
No result settling this approximation guarantee for the specified mass-based mean-field dynamics was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total society mass to one, making the budget per capita and the objective a balanced fraction; restricted the budget to the meaningful interval from zero to two.
  • Required finite nonnegative contact intensities and rational accuracy strictly between zero and one.
  • Represented joint baseline seed memberships explicitly by refining mixed types into positive-mass membership subtypes with inherited intensities.
  • Allowed additional seeds among baseline seeds of the opposing campaign. Excluded redundant seeding of an already seeded campaign, which cannot improve the objective under the discrete union definition.
  • Interpreted the three additional seed masses as disjoint portions, with common additional seeds costing two units per unit mass.
  • Specified synchronous independent transitions, persistent exposure, and newly activated rather than cumulative transmitting masses, so each portion transmits once per campaign.
  • Used a supremum over feasible real allocations and required a rational returned allocation, without assuming attainment of the limiting objective.
  • Used the anchor's multiplicative approximation factor with additive numerical tolerance instead of the graded question's additive near-optimality requirement; measured running time polynomially in the reciprocal tolerance.

Result

The requested approximation is achievable in deterministic polynomial time for the specified mass-based dynamics.

More precisely, an algorithm can return a feasible rational allocation satisfying
\( B(x)\ge \frac{1-1/e}{2}\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon \)
in time polynomial in \(\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). Since \(m=2\), this also meets the stated dependence on \(m\).

The proof requires two ingredients beyond the finite-network argument: a polynomial-time procedure for evaluating the physical mean-field fixed point, and a regularization that gives polynomial derivative bounds even when the budget is extremely small.

1. Marginal allocations and final-size equations

Write \(h_{t,j}\) for the additional mass seeded for campaign \(j\) in type \(t\). Thus, when \(b_t=00\),
\( h_{t,1}=x_{t,10}+x_{t,11}, \qquad h_{t,2}=x_{t,01}+x_{t,11}. \)
For types with an existing baseline membership, the corresponding additional marginal is zero.

The feasible marginal allocations are exactly those satisfying
\( h_{t,j}=0\ \text{if }b_{t,j}=1, \qquad 0\le h_{t,j}\le\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,j}h_{t,j}\le\kappa. \)

Indeed, the forward implication follows from the allocation constraints. Conversely, for a type with baseline \(00\), set
\( x_{t,11}=\min\{h_{t,1},h_{t,2}\}, \quad x_{t,10}=h_{t,1}-x_{t,11}, \quad x_{t,01}=h_{t,2}-x_{t,11}. \)
The selected mass is \(\max\{h_{t,1},h_{t,2}\}\le\mu_t\), and its cost is \(h_{t,1}+h_{t,2}\). For baseline \(10\), use \(x_{t,01}=h_{t,2}\); for baseline \(01\), use \(x_{t,10}=h_{t,1}\). This construction preserves rationality.

Let
\( i_{t,j}=\mu_t b_{t,j}+h_{t,j} \)
be the initial campaign marginal. Let \(w^r_{t,j}\) denote cumulative exposure through round \(r\), including seeds, and let \(w_{t,j}\) be its limit.

Because each newly exposed portion transmits exactly once, the mass still missing campaign \(j\) after round \(r+1\) is
\( (\mu_t-i_{t,j}) \exp\!\left(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}w^r_{s,j}\right). \)
Consequently,
\( w^0_{t,j}=i_{t,j}, \qquad w^{r+1}_{t,j} = \mu_t-(\mu_t-i_{t,j}) \exp\!\left(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}w^r_{s,j}\right). \)

This iteration is increasing and bounded above by \(\mu_t\). Its limit is therefore the least fixed point above the initial seed vector.

Define
\( H_{t,j}=\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}w_{s,j}, \qquad u_t(x)=z^0_{t,00}. \)
Independence of the two acquisition probabilities in every round gives
\( z_{t,00}=u_t(x)e^{-H_{t,1}-H_{t,2}}. \)
Since the two final exposed marginals are \(w_{t,1},w_{t,2}\),
\( B(x) = \sum_t(w_{t,1}+w_{t,2})-1 + 2\sum_tu_t(x)e^{-H_{t,1}-H_{t,2}}. \)
In particular, the final campaign marginals depend only on \(h\).

2. A branching-tree representation and diminishing returns

Fix one campaign and suppress its campaign index.

Construct a random rooted tree whose root has type \(t\). A node of type \(v\) has, independently for each type \(s\), a Poisson number of children of type \(s\), with mean \(\lambda_{sv}\mu_s\). Different nodes reproduce independently.

A node of type \(s\) is marked independently with probability
\( p_s=i_s/\mu_s. \)
A baseline-seeded type is therefore marked deterministically.

For a tree truncated at depth \(r\), the probability that some node is marked equals \(w^r_t/\mu_t\). To verify this, let \(P_s\) be the probability that a child subtree of type \(s\) contains a mark. Poisson thinning gives the probability of no marked child subtree as
\( \exp\!\left(-\sum_s\lambda_{sv}\mu_sP_s\right). \)
Including the root mark reproduces exactly the recurrence in Section 1. Taking increasing limits establishes the representation for final exposure.

For a fixed finite tree containing no baseline-marked node, let \(N_i\) count its nodes of eligible type \(i\). Its probability of containing a mark is
\( 1-\prod_i(1-p_i)^{N_i}. \)
Every first partial derivative is nonnegative. Every second partial derivative, including diagonal derivatives, is nonpositive.

It follows, after averaging over trees, that every finite-round marginal has diminishing returns: for coordinatewise \(a\le b\), and any feasible coordinate increment \(d e_i\),
\( w_t(a+d e_i)-w_t(a) \ge w_t(b+d e_i)-w_t(b). \)
The inequality persists under pointwise limits. Thus every final marginal is monotone and has diminishing returns.

The same finite-tree calculation shows concavity along every nonnegative direction. This also persists under limits, including at zero seeds. In particular, for \(0\le s\le1\),
\( w_t(sh)\ge s\,w_t(h)+(1-s)w_t(0). \)

These statements allow discontinuities at zero seeding; continuity there is not being assumed.

3. A balance surrogate

Let
\( \alpha_{t,j}=w_{t,j}(0)/\mu_t \)
be the baseline probability of exposure to campaign \(j\). Define
\( \Omega(h) = \sum_t \left[ \alpha_{t,1}w_{t,2}(h) + \alpha_{t,2}w_{t,1}(h) - \mu_t\alpha_{t,1}\alpha_{t,2} \right]. \)
Also define the baseline mass exposed to neither campaign:
\( U_0=\sum_t\mu_t(1-\alpha_{t,1})(1-\alpha_{t,2}). \)

We prove that, for every feasible allocation \(x\) with marginals \(h\),
\( 0\le\Omega(h)\le B(x)\le\Omega(h)+U_0. \)

For a root of type \(t\), use independent branching trees for the two campaigns. Couple baseline and augmented marking on each tree so that baseline exposure implies augmented exposure. At the common root, use the categorical initial membership prescribed by \(x\); off the root, sample markings independently.

This construction reproduces the prescribed final distribution: it has the correct campaign marginals, and its probability of missing both campaigns is
\( \frac{u_t(x)}{\mu_t}e^{-H_{t,1}-H_{t,2}}, \)
which, together with the marginals, determines all four state probabilities.

Let \(E_j^0\) and \(E_j^h\) denote baseline and augmented exposure events. Baseline membership is deterministic within a type. Therefore \(E_1^0\) is independent of \(E_2^h\), and \(E_2^0\) is independent of \(E_1^h\). Moreover, \(E_j^0\subseteq E_j^h\).

The event that the root is exposed to both campaigns and was exposed to at least one campaign under the baseline is
\( (E_1^0\cap E_2^h)\cup(E_2^0\cap E_1^h). \)
Its probability is
\( \alpha_{t,1}\frac{w_{t,2}(h)}{\mu_t} + \alpha_{t,2}\frac{w_{t,1}(h)}{\mu_t} - \alpha_{t,1}\alpha_{t,2}. \)
Thus \(\Omega\) counts precisely this part of balanced exposure. Every other balanced root belongs to the event of baseline exposure to neither campaign, whose total mass is \(U_0\). This proves the claimed inequalities.

Since \(\Omega\) is a nonnegative weighted sum of campaign marginals plus a constant, Section 2 also proves that \(\Omega\) is monotone, has diminishing returns, and is concave along nonnegative directions. In particular,
\( \Omega(sh)\ge s\Omega(h), \qquad 0\le s\le1. \)

Writing
\( \Omega^*=\sup_{h\text{ feasible}}\Omega(h), \)
we obtain
\( \mathrm{OPT}\le\Omega^*+U_0, \qquad B(0)\ge U_0. \)

4. Polynomial-time evaluation of the physical fixed point

The following lemma is essential for the running-time claim.

Evaluation lemma. Given rational masses, intensities and seed marginals for one campaign, and rational \(0<\gamma<1\), one can compute a rational vector \(\widehat w\) satisfying
\( \|\widehat w-w\|_1\le\gamma \)
in time polynomial in the data’s binary length, \(\tau\), and \(\log(1/\gamma)\).

4.1 Restricting to the physically reachable types

Direct an edge from \(s\) to \(t\) whenever \(\lambda_{st}>0\). Let \(R\) consist of types reachable from a type with positive initial seed mass.

Types outside \(R\) have exposure zero in every round. Types with \(i_t=\mu_t\) have exposure \(\mu_t\). Remove both classes, retaining their known values.

Let \(T\) be the remaining types. Their final-size map is
\( F_t(v) = \mu_t-(\mu_t-i_t) \exp\!\left(-C_t-\sum_{s\in T}\lambda_{st}v_s\right), \)
where \(C_t\) is the rational incoming intensity from fully seeded types.

Every component of \(F\) is increasing and concave. Every type in \(T\) is reachable, within \(T\), from a type satisfying \(F_t(0)>0\). Hence, for some integer \(k\le\tau\),
\( F^k(0)>0 \)
componentwise.

The map \(F^k\) is also increasing and componentwise concave: composition preserves these properties when the outer map is increasing. Therefore, for \(0<a<1\),
\( F^k(av) \ge aF^k(v)+(1-a)F^k(0) > aF^k(v). \)

This proves uniqueness of the fixed point on \(T\). Indeed, if \(u,v\) were distinct fixed points, both would be strictly positive. After interchanging them if necessary, let
\( a=\min_t u_t/v_t<1. \)
Then \(u\ge av\), with equality in some coordinate, whereas
\( u=F^k(u)\ge F^k(av)>aF^k(v)=av, \)
a contradiction.

Furthermore, every subsolution \(v\le F(v)\) is componentwise bounded above by this unique fixed point: iterating from \(v\) increases to a fixed point.

4.2 Convex optimization of subsolutions

Choose \(0<\delta\le1/2\). Define
\( K_\delta= \left\{ v: 0\le v_t\le(1-\delta)\mu_t,\quad g_t(v)\ge0\ \text{for every }t\in T \right\}, \)
where
\( g_t(v) = \log(\mu_t-v_t)-\log(\mu_t-i_t) + C_t+\sum_{s\in T}\lambda_{st}v_s. \)

Each \(g_t\) is concave, so \(K_\delta\) is convex. Its nonlinear constraints are exactly \(v\le F(v)\). Consequently every point of \(K_\delta\) is componentwise at most the physical fixed point \(w\).

Concavity of \(F\), together with \(F(0)\ge0\), gives
\( F((1-\delta)w)\ge(1-\delta)F(w)=(1-\delta)w. \)
Thus \((1-\delta)w\in K_\delta\), and
\( \max_{v\in K_\delta}\sum_t v_t \ge (1-\delta)\sum_t w_t. \)

A feasible solution within \(\gamma/2\) of this linear optimum, with \(\delta=\gamma/2\), therefore satisfies
\( \|w-v\|_1 = \sum_t(w_t-v_t) \le \delta+\gamma/2 = \gamma. \)

It remains to justify polynomial-time optimization of \(K_\delta\).

4.3 Explicit interior bounds and a weak separation oracle

Call a type a source if \(i_t>0\) or \(C_t>0\). Let \(\ell_t\) be its shortest distance from a source within \(T\).

Let \(a\) be the minimum of \(1\) and the positive quantities among
\( i_t/\mu_t,\qquad C_t,\qquad \lambda_{st}\mu_s \)
for the retained types and edges. Set
\( \theta=a/8, \qquad \bar v_t=\mu_t\theta^{\ell_t+1}. \)

The inequality
\( 1-e^{-z}\ge\min\{z/2,1/2\}, \qquad z\ge0, \)
follows from concavity on the interval from zero to one, and monotonicity thereafter.

For a source, it gives \(F_t(\bar v)\ge4\bar v_t\). For a nonsource, choose a predecessor at distance \(\ell_t-1\); its contribution to the incoming intensity is at least \(a\theta^{\ell_t}\), giving the same inequality. Hence
\( g_t(\bar v) = \log\frac{\mu_t-\bar v_t}{\mu_t-F_t(\bar v)} \ge \frac{F_t(\bar v)-\bar v_t}{\mu_t} \ge 3\theta^{\ell_t+1}. \)

Put
\( \beta=\theta^{\tau+1}, \quad \mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t, \quad \lambda_{\max}=\max_{s,t}\lambda_{st}, \quad G=1+\tau\lambda_{\max}+\frac1{\delta\mu_{\min}}. \)
On the truncated box, each \(g_t\) has Euclidean gradient norm at most \(G\).

Therefore the Euclidean ball centered at \(\bar v\) with radius
\( r= \min\left\{ \frac{\mu_{\min}\theta^{\tau+1}}2,\, \frac{\mu_{\min}}8,\, \frac{\beta}{2G} \right\} \)
lies in \(K_\delta\). The first two terms keep the ball inside the box; the last term preserves all nonlinear inequalities. Both \(\bar v\) and \(r\) are rational and have polynomial binary length. Also \(K_\delta\) lies in the unit Euclidean ball.

Here is a weak separation oracle with tolerance \(\zeta>0\). Outside the rational box, return a separating box inequality. Inside it, evaluate every \(g_t\) with error at most
\( e=\zeta\beta/8. \)
The gradient is rational and computed exactly.

If an approximate value is below \(-e\), the true value is negative. Concavity then supplies a valid separating hyperplane using its gradient. Otherwise every true value is at least \(-2e\). By mixing the query point with \(\bar v\), with mixing weight
\( \frac{2e}{\beta+2e}, \)
one obtains a point in \(K_\delta\). Its distance from the query is at most \(4e/\beta\le\zeta\). Thus the oracle is valid.

Logarithms of positive rational arguments can be evaluated to the required precision in polynomial time. Here the arguments, precision requirements, and rational gradients all have polynomial binary length.

We use the standard weak-separation-to-weak-optimization theorem: linear optimization over a bounded convex body reduces in polynomial time to weak separation, with a rational approximate optimizer in the outer parallel body and comparison against the inner parallel body. A precise statement is [Brieden et al., Proposition 1.8](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/77925048B503F5FBBBD51625EC693E17/S0025579300014364a.pdf/deterministic_and_randomized_polynomialtime_approximation_of_radii.pdf).

For completeness, weak feasibility can be converted to exact feasibility here. If the theorem returns \(z\) at distance at most \(\xi<r/4\) from \(K_\delta\), then
\( v=(1-2\xi/r)z+(2\xi/r)\bar v \)
belongs to \(K_\delta\). To see this, choose \(u\in K_\delta\) within distance \(\xi\) of \(z\). The corresponding mixture of \(u\) and \(\bar v\) contains a ball of radius \(2\xi\) in \(K_\delta\), and \(v\) is within distance \(\xi\) of that mixture.

Similarly, mixing any optimizer toward \(\bar v\) with weight \(\xi/r\) puts it in the inner parallel body of radius \(\xi\). The resulting objective losses are bounded by \(8\tau\xi/r\). Taking \(\xi\) sufficiently small therefore gives the desired exactly feasible objective approximation. Its required precision has polynomial binary length because \(\log(1/r)\) does.

This proves the evaluation lemma.

4.4 Evaluating the objectives

The lemma evaluates \(\alpha\) and \(\Omega(h)\) to any additive accuracy \(\nu\) in time polynomial in the input length and \(\log(1/\nu)\). Division by \(\mu_t\) only requires an additional number of precision bits polynomial in \(L\).

It also evaluates \(B(x)\). If each campaign marginal has error at most \(\gamma\) in total mass, each pressure has error at most \(\lambda_{\max}\gamma\). The function \(e^{-z}\) is Lipschitz with constant one for \(z\ge0\), so Section 1’s formula bounds the resulting error in \(B\) by
\( 2\gamma+4\lambda_{\max}\gamma, \)
apart from the separately controlled exponential-evaluation error.

Thus sufficiently small \(\gamma\) has only polynomially many additional precision bits. Large exponential arguments cause no difficulty: once the argument exceeds a logarithmic accuracy threshold, returning zero already meets the additive tolerance.

5. Regularization with polynomial derivative bounds

If \(\kappa=0\), return the empty allocation. If there are no eligible additional campaign memberships, do the same. These cases satisfy the guarantee immediately.

Otherwise enumerate the eligible type-campaign coordinates by \(i\), and let their number be \(n\le2\tau\). Define
\( c_i=\min\{\mu_{t(i)},\kappa\}, \qquad P=\left\{ y\in[0,1]^n:\sum_i c_i y_i\le\kappa \right\}. \)
Every feasible marginal allocation has the form \(h_i=c_i y_i\) for some \(y\in P\).

Set
\( \eta=\epsilon/64, \qquad f_i=\eta c_i/n, \qquad h_i(y)=f_i+(1-2\eta)c_i y_i, \qquad g(y)=\Omega(h(y)). \)

For every \(y\in P\),
\( \sum_i h_i(y) \le \eta\kappa+(1-2\eta)\kappa \le\kappa, \)
and
\( h_i(y)\le(1-\eta)c_i\le\mu_{t(i)}. \)
Thus these allocations are feasible.

For every original feasible allocation \(h\), the point \(y_i=h_i/c_i\) lies in \(P\), and monotonicity and ray concavity give
\( g(y) \ge \Omega((1-2\eta)h) \ge (1-2\eta)\Omega(h). \)
Consequently,
\( \max_{y\in P}g(y)\ge(1-2\eta)\Omega^*. \)

We next establish a derivative bound independent of the numerical sizes of \(\kappa\) and the intensities.

Every nonbaseline marking probability is now positive. On an infinite branching tree, the probability of avoiding every mark is zero: all marking probabilities are bounded below by a positive number for the fixed instance.

For a finite, baseline-free tree, the failure probability is
\( \prod_i(1-p_i(y))^{N_i}, \qquad p_i(y)=h_i(y)/\mu_{t(i)}. \)
Its coordinate slope satisfies
\( p'_i=\frac{(1-2\eta)c_i}{\mu_{t(i)}} \le \frac n\eta\,p_i. \)

For every nonnegative integer \(N\) and probability \(p\),
\( Np(1-p)^{N-1}\le1, \qquad N(N-1)p^2(1-p)^{N-2}\le2, \)
with zero terms interpreted naturally. These bounds follow from the probabilities of exactly one and exactly two successes in a binomial distribution.

The finite-tree derivative formulas therefore bound every mixed second derivative in absolute value by \((n/\eta)^2\), and every diagonal second derivative by \(2(n/\eta)^2\). They also bound first derivatives by \(n/\eta\).

Averaging over finite trees preserves these bounds. Infinite trees contribute zero to the failure probability throughout this positive-seeding region. The uniform bounds justify differentiation under the expectation by dominated convergence. Thus \(g\) is twice continuously differentiable, has nonnegative gradient, and satisfies
\( -M\le \frac{\partial^2g}{\partial y_i\partial y_j} \le0, \qquad M=\frac{2n^2}{\eta^2}. \)
Here the weighted sum over root types introduces no larger factor, because its weights are at most \(\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Derivatives between coordinates of different campaigns are zero.

6. A rational continuous-greedy algorithm

Set
\( N=\left\lceil\frac{16Mn^2}{\epsilon}\right\rceil, \qquad q=\frac{\epsilon}{64n}, \qquad \rho=\min\{1/N,q/(2M)\}. \)

Start from \(y^0=0\). At iteration \(k\), obtain a rational gradient estimate \(\widehat\nabla g(y^k)\) with coordinatewise error at most \(q\). This can be done with forward differences of step \(\rho\), evaluating \(g\) with additive error at most \(q\rho/8\).

Indeed, the second-derivative bound gives finite-difference bias at most \(M\rho/2\le q/4\), and the two value errors contribute at most \(q/4\). The needed evaluations remain inside the box: inductively \(y_i^k\le k/N\), and \(k<N\).

Choose a rational vector \(v^k\) maximizing
\( \widehat\nabla g(y^k)\cdot v \)
over \(v\in P\), and update
\( y^{k+1}=y^k+\frac1N v^k. \)
The linear optimization is fractional knapsack with rational data. It is solvable exactly in polynomial time.

Each \(y^k\) lies in \((k/N)P\), because it is \(k/N\) times an average of vectors in \(P\). In particular, \(y^N\in P\).

Let \(y^*\) maximize \(g\) over \(P\), which exists by continuity and compactness. Monotonicity and concavity along nonnegative directions imply
\( g(y^*)-g(y) \le g(y\vee y^*)-g(y) \le \nabla g(y)\cdot(y\vee y^*-y) \le \nabla g(y)\cdot y^*. \)
All these function evaluations lie in the full box, where \(g\) is defined.

The gradient approximation and exact linear optimization give
\( \nabla g(y^k)\cdot v^k \ge g(y^*)-g(y^k)-2nq. \)
Taylor’s theorem, together with \(\|v^k\|_1\le n\), gives
\( g(y^{k+1}) \ge g(y^k) + \frac{g(y^*)-g(y^k)-2nq}{N} - \frac{Mn^2}{2N^2}. \)

Solving this scalar recurrence, using \(g(0)\ge0\), yields
\( g(y^N) \ge (1-1/e)g(y^*) - 2nq - \frac{Mn^2}{2N}. \)
Combining this with Section 5 and \(\Omega^*\le1\),
\( \Omega(h(y^N)) \ge (1-1/e)\Omega^* - 2\eta - 2nq - \frac{Mn^2}{2N}. \)
Each of the three error terms is at most \(\epsilon/32\). Therefore
\( \Omega(h(y^N)) \ge (1-1/e)\Omega^*-\epsilon/8. \)

Convert \(h(y^N)\) into a feasible rational allocation \(x^{\mathrm{gr}}\) using Section 1.

7. Final comparison and running time

Put \(c=1-1/e\). The established inequalities give
\( B(x^{\mathrm{gr}})\ge c\Omega^*-\epsilon/8, \qquad B(0)\ge U_0, \qquad \mathrm{OPT}\le\Omega^*+U_0. \)
Consequently,
\( c\,\mathrm{OPT} \le B(x^{\mathrm{gr}})+B(0)+\epsilon/8. \)
Hence
\( \max\{B(x^{\mathrm{gr}}),B(0)\} \ge \frac c2\,\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon/16. \)

Evaluate both candidate objectives to additive error \(\epsilon/16\), and return the candidate with the larger estimate. Its true objective is within \(\epsilon/8\) of the better candidate, so the returned allocation satisfies
\( B(x) \ge \frac{1-1/e}{2}\mathrm{OPT}-3\epsilon/16 \ge \frac{1-1/e}{2}\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon. \)

Finally:

  • The number of greedy iterations is \(O(n^4/\epsilon^3)\), with \(O(n)\) value calls per iteration.
  • Each value call uses the polynomial-time evaluation lemma.
  • All linear optimizations, updates, and conversions use rational arithmetic with polynomial binary length.
  • The convex evaluator depends logarithmically on its interior radius and requested numerical accuracy. Thus exponentially small budgets or masses, and exponentially large intensities, cause only polynomial increases in bit complexity.

No attainment of either supremum was assumed. The inequalities hold for every feasible allocation and hence pass directly to the suprema.
