The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of valuation types \(T\u005c), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\u005c) summing to \(1\u005c), positive rational valuations \(v_t=(1,r_t)\u005c), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_1,q_2\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\u005c), does there always exist, and can one compute in time polynomial in \(\tau\u005c) and input bit length \(L\u005c), a finite-support allocation \(\lambda_{t,a,b}\ge 0\u005c) of type-\(t\u005c) mass to integral bundles \((a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^2\u005c), with type masses and aggregate supplies matching the input, that is EFX across every pair of positive-mass type-and-bundle cohorts and Pareto optimal? For Pareto optimality, compare feasible allocations through type-by-type couplings of old and new bundles: no such coupling may make almost every agent weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) valuation types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m=2\) good types, positive binary rational per-capita supplies \(q_1,q_2\), and positive binary rational valuations \(v_t=(1,r_t)\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Write \(B=\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2\) and \(u_t(a,b)=a+r_tb\). A feasible allocation assigns masses \(\lambda_{t,x}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{x\in B}\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,x}\lambda_{t,x}x=(q_1,q_2)\). The requested allocation has finite support and binary rational masses; bundle coordinates are encoded in binary.
EFX holds across every ordered pair of positive-mass cohorts \((t,x),(s,y)\): \(u_t(x)\ge u_t(y-e_c)\) for each \(c\in\{1,2\}\) with \(y_c>0\), where \(e_c\) is the corresponding unit vector. An empty envied bundle imposes no condition.
An allocation is PO if there is no feasible allocation \(\lambda'\) and type-preserving coupling \(\gamma_{t,x,y}\ge0\) with marginals \(\lambda_{t,x}\) and \(\lambda'_{t,y}\), supported on \(u_t(y)\ge u_t(x)\), such that \(\sum_{t,x,y:\,u_t(y)>u_t(x)}\gamma_{t,x,y}>0\). Competitors and couplings may have real masses and countable support; competitors need not satisfy EFX.

Does every such instance admit a finite-support rational EFX+PO allocation?
Is deciding existence in \(\mathrm P\) or NP-hard, and can an allocation, whenever one exists, be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For finite societies with positive additive valuations and two good types, EFX+PO allocations always exist; the algorithm uses \(O(\log n+\log M)\) arithmetic operations after preprocessing, with a compressed output, and \(O(n\log n+\log M)\) including preprocessing, where \(n\) counts agents and \(M\) counts goods. [Source](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/38723/42685).
No result settling the exact mass model above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted both supplies to be strictly positive, carrying over the discrete setting's assumption that both good types are present.
  • Required rational masses and binary-encoded bundle coordinates in the finite output to make exact computation well-defined.
  • Allowed Pareto competitors and couplings to have real masses and countable support, with the same exact supplies; competitors are not required to be EFX.
  • Retained arbitrary positive total per-capita supply: the discrete treatment handles fewer goods than agents separately, so its preprocessing restriction does not restrict the intended allocation problem.
  • Used m for the fixed number of good types and M for the discrete total number of goods, avoiding a population-dependent goods count in the mass model.

1. Result and interpretation

Every instance admits a finite-support rational EFX+PO allocation. For \(m=2\), such an allocation can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Consequently, existence is in \(\mathrm P\): every valid instance is a yes-instance.

Here, “binary rational” means a rational number represented by binary-encoded numerator and denominator. The construction can return at most \(\tau+2\) positive-mass cohorts.

We use the finite-society existence theorem supplied in the question. Its application alone does not establish the result: a Pareto improvement involving fractions of cohorts need not be implementable in a particular finite population. The proof below supplies the required transfer argument.

2. A uniform bound on EFX bundles

Fix a type \(t_0\), and write \(r_0=r_{t_0}\), \(\mu_0=\mu_{t_0}\). Define

\(\displaystyle C=\frac{q_1+r_0q_2}{\mu_0}+\max\{1,r_0\},\qquad H=\left\lceil\max\{1,C,C/r_0\}\right\rceil.\)

Lemma 1. Every positive-mass bundle in any feasible EFX allocation has both coordinates at most \(H\).

Proof. Let \(y\) be a positive-mass bundle. For every positive-mass cohort \((t_0,x)\), EFX implies

\(\displaystyle u_{t_0}(x)\ge u_{t_0}(y)-\max\{1,r_0\}.\)

For nonempty \(y\), this follows by removing an available good and bounding its value by \(\max\{1,r_0\}\). For empty \(y\), the inequality follows from nonnegative utility.

Multiplying by \(\lambda_{t_0,x}\) and summing gives

\(\displaystyle \mu_0\bigl(u_{t_0}(y)-\max\{1,r_0\}\bigr)\le \sum_x\lambda_{t_0,x}u_{t_0}(x)\le q_1+r_0q_2.\)

The last inequality holds because the sum over type \(t_0\) is part of the total value of all allocated goods, evaluated using \(u_{t_0}\), and every omitted term is nonnegative.

Thus \(y_1+r_0y_2\le C\), implying \(y_1\le H\) and \(y_2\le H\). \(\square\)

The integer \(H\) has bit length polynomial in \(L\). This bound also applies to a finite EFX allocation after dividing agent counts and total supplies by its population size.

3. Bounded integer witnesses for Pareto improvements

Define

\(\displaystyle J=\left\lceil 1+H\max_{t\in T}\{1+r_t,\,1+r_t^{-1}\}\right\rceil,\qquad K=18J^2.\)

Both integers have bit length polynomial in \(L\), and \(J\ge H+1\).

A *transition* is a triple \((t,x,y)\), meaning that a cohort of type \(t\) changes from bundle \(x\) to bundle \(y\). It is admissible when \(u_t(y)\ge u_t(x)\), and strict when this inequality is strict.

Lemma 2. Suppose an allocation has finite support, all its source bundles lie in \(\{0,\ldots,H\}^2\), and it fails PO under the definition in the question. Then there exist at most three admissible transitions \((t_i,x_i,y_i)\), sourced in its positive support, and positive integers \(h_i\), such that:

  • Each destination lies in \(\{0,\ldots,J\}^2\).
  • \(\sum_i h_i(y_i-x_i)=(0,0)\).
  • At least one transition is strict.
  • \(h_i\le 6J^2\), and therefore \(\sum_i h_i\le K\).

Proof.

Reduction of countable competitors to bounded transitions

Take a witnessing competitor and coupling \(\gamma\). For every positive coupling entry \((t,x,y)\), set \(U=u_t(x)\).

If the transition is weakly improving with equality, choose a coordinatewise minimal integer bundle \(z\le y\) satisfying \(u_t(z)\ge U\). If it is strict, choose such a minimal bundle satisfying \(u_t(z)>U\). These choices exist because the integer box below \(y\) is finite and contains a qualifying bundle.

Whenever \(z_c>0\), minimality gives

\(\displaystyle u_t(z-e_c)\le U.\)

For weak transitions the inequality is actually strict; the displayed weaker form suffices. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle z_1\le U+1\le H(1+r_t)+1\le J,\)

and

\(\displaystyle z_2\le U/r_t+1\le H(1+r_t^{-1})+1\le J.\)

The reduced transition remains admissible, and every originally strict transition remains strict.

Aggregate the coupling entries by their source and reduced destination. There are now only finitely many transition kinds. If \(d=z-x\), their weighted sum is coordinatewise nonpositive: reducing destinations only saves goods.

Let \(w_c\ge0\) be the saved amount of good \(c\). Select any positive-mass source cohort \((t_*,x_*)\), and add the transition \(x_*\longrightarrow x_*+e_c\) with coefficient \(w_c\). These added transitions are strict because valuations are positive, and their destinations are bounded by \(J\).

The resulting finite nonnegative combination of transitions has total resource change zero and positive total coefficient on strict transitions. All sums used here are well-defined: the original source and destination first moments are finite by feasibility.

Reduction to three transition kinds

For each transition, form the integer column

\(\displaystyle a_i=(d_{i,1},d_{i,2},s_i)^{\mathsf T},\)

where \(s_i=1\) for a strict transition and \(s_i=0\) otherwise. After normalizing the positive total strict coefficient, the preceding construction gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_i\alpha_i a_i=(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T},\qquad \alpha_i\ge0.\)

This representation can be reduced to linearly independent positive columns. Indeed, if its positive columns are dependent, choose a nonzero dependence \(\sum_i\beta_i a_i=0\), oriented so that some \(\beta_i>0\). Subtract

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon\beta_i,\qquad \varepsilon=\min_{\beta_i>0}\alpha_i/\beta_i,\)

from their coefficients. Nonnegativity and the represented vector are preserved, and at least one coefficient becomes zero. Repeating leaves at most three independent columns.

Suppose \(r\le3\) columns remain. Choose \(r\) rows on which they form an invertible square integer matrix. Cramer’s rule expresses their positive coefficients using a common nonzero integer determinant. Multiplication by its absolute value gives positive integers \(h_i\).

Each numerator determinant contains the right-hand-side column, whose entries are zero or one. The other entries have absolute value at most \(J\). Hence the Leibniz determinant formula bounds each numerator by

\(\displaystyle r!J^{r-1}\le6J^2.\)

Thus \(h_i\le6J^2\). The first two equations give resource balance; the third guarantees a strict transition. There are at most three transitions, so their total multiplicity is at most \(18J^2=K\). \(\square\)

Corollary 3. Let \(X\) be a finite PO allocation with source bundles bounded by \(H\). Let \(R\) consist of cohorts containing at least \(K\) agents in \(X\). Every mass allocation whose positive support is contained in \(R\) is PO.

Proof. Otherwise Lemma 2 supplies an integer improving reallocation using at most \(K\) agents in total, with every source in \(R\). Each source cohort contains enough distinct agents to implement its transitions. Resource balance preserves the finite society’s supplies, every affected agent weakly improves, and at least one strictly improves. This contradicts finite PO. \(\square\)

This corollary allows the masses on retained cohorts to change arbitrarily.

4. Removing small cohorts while preserving exact feasibility

Let \(Q_0\) be the product of the denominators of all \(\mu_t,q_1,q_2\). Thus their products with \(Q_0\) are integers. Set

\(\displaystyle d=\tau+2,\qquad k_{\max}=\tau(H+1)^2,\qquad \Delta=Q_0d!H^d,\)

and choose

\(\displaystyle N=Q_0\bigl(k_{\max}K\Delta+1\bigr).\)

In particular,

\(\displaystyle N>k_{\max}K\Delta.\)

Construct a finite society with \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_c\) goods of type \(c\). Its populations and supplies are positive integers, its total population is \(N\), and its valuations are positive and additive. Thus the supplied finite-society theorem applies.

Take a finite EFX+PO allocation \(X\). Let \(S\) be its set of distinct cohorts, and let \(n_{t,x}\) be their positive integer counts. The normalized masses

\(\displaystyle z^0_{t,x}=n_{t,x}/N\)

satisfy the original feasibility equations. Lemma 1 bounds every bundle by \(H\), so \(|S|\le k_{\max}\).

Partition \(S\) into retained and discarded cohorts:

\(\displaystyle R=\{(t,x)\in S:n_{t,x}\ge K\},\qquad D=S\setminus R.\)

Lemma 4. There is a rational feasible allocation supported entirely on \(R\).

Proof. For each cohort \((t,x)\in S\), form the column

\(\displaystyle A_{t,x}=(e_t,x_1,x_2)^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb Z^d,\)

where \(e_t\) is the type’s unit vector in \(\mathbb R^\tau\). Write

\(\displaystyle b=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau,q_1,q_2)^{\mathsf T}.\)

Consider the nonempty compact polytope

\(\displaystyle P=\{z\ge0:Az=b\},\)

and minimize \(\sum_{j\in D}z_j\) over \(P\). Compactness follows from the type equations, which imply \(\sum_jz_j=1\).

At any vertex of \(P\), its positive columns are linearly independent. Otherwise a dependence would permit sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality. There are therefore at most \(d\) positive coordinates. Select an invertible square submatrix on those columns and independent rows. Its entries have absolute value at most \(H\), so its determinant has absolute value at most \(d!H^d\).

Because \(Q_0b\) is integral, Cramer’s rule shows that the positive coordinates of this vertex have a common denominator of magnitude at most \(\Delta\). Every positive coordinate is consequently at least \(1/\Delta\).

A linear objective on a nonempty compact polytope has an optimal vertex. Therefore, if the minimum discarded mass were positive, it would be at least \(1/\Delta\).

But the original normalized allocation satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j\in D}z^0_j<\frac{|D|K}{N}\le\frac{k_{\max}K}{N}<\frac1\Delta.\)

The minimum must thus be zero. An optimal vertex is rational, feasible, and supported on \(R\). \(\square\)

Let \(z^*\) be such a vertex. It is EFX because its positive support is a subset of the EFX support of \(X\); all relevant pairwise inequalities are inherited. It is PO by Corollary 3. Its support has at most \(d=\tau+2\) cohorts.

This proves existence, including PO against real-mass competitors with countable support.

5. Polynomial-time construction

The preceding scaling has polynomial bit length:

\(\displaystyle \log Q_0=O(L),\qquad \log H=O(L),\qquad \log J=O(L),\)

and

\(\displaystyle \log\Delta=O\bigl(L+d\log d+d\log H\bigr).\)

Hence \(\log N\), and the bit lengths of \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nq_c\), are polynomial in \(\tau,L\). We must nevertheless avoid explicitly constructing \(N\) agents.

The supplied finite algorithm permits this. After sorting agents, it uses \(O(\log n+\log M)\) arithmetic operations and outputs bundles on a constant number of intervals of agent ranks, allowing an exceptional split agent. The interval representation follows from its equitable, split, and reallocation constructions. See Definitions 4, 5, 8, Claim 1, and Theorem 5 in the [finite-society paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/38723/42685).

We simulate that algorithm as follows:

  • Sort the \(\tau\) types by valuation ratio and store prefix sums of their integer populations.
  • Answer a valuation query at any virtual agent rank by binary search in the prefix sums. Preference grouping, normalization, and any interchange of good types can likewise be performed on type blocks.
  • Run the finite algorithm on this virtual population.
  • Intersect its constant number of bundle intervals with the type blocks, producing \(O(\tau)\) cohorts and their exact integer counts.
  • Retain precisely the cohorts with counts at least \(K\).
  • Solve \(A_Rz=b,\ z\ge0\) by polynomial-time rational linear programming, returning a basic feasible solution.

The finite algorithm uses binary searches and explicit arithmetic formulas with integer floors and ceilings. Its intermediate numbers are counts, search indices, or products and quotients involving counts and input valuations. Their bit lengths are polynomial in \(\log N+\log(Nq_1+Nq_2)+L\). Thus the virtual simulation has polynomial bit complexity.

Lemma 4 guarantees feasibility of the final linear program. It has \(O(\tau)\) variables, \(\tau+2\) equations, and polynomial-size rational data. Standard rational linear programming and basic feasible solution extraction therefore take polynomial time.

The returned allocation has at most \(\tau+2\) positive masses. Their common denominator is bounded by \(\Delta\), as in Lemma 4, and its bundle coordinates are bounded by \(H\). Its entire output consequently has polynomial bit length.
