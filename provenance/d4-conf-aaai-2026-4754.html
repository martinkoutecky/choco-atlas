1. Marginal representation

For each seller and buyer type, define

$$ a_q(w)=w(m-q+1)-w(m-q), \qquad b_q(v)=v(q)-v(q-1), \qquad 1\le q\le m. $$

Strict increase and submodularity imply

$$ 0<a_1(w)\le\cdots\le a_m(w), \qquad b_1(v)\ge\cdots\ge b_m(v)>0. $$

Conversely, any positive vectors satisfying these orders define admissible schedules through

$$ w(j)=\sum_{q=m-j+1}^{m}a_q, \qquad v(j)=\sum_{q=1}^{j}b_q. $$

Indeed, the increments of $w$ are $a_m,\ldots,a_1$, and those of $v$ are $b_1,\ldots,b_m$. Both increment sequences are positive and nonincreasing.

Let $A_q=a_q(w)$ and $B_q=b_q(v)$ under the independent draws. Define

$$ h_p(a,b)=a+(b-a)\mathbf1_{\{a\le p\le b\}}. $$

Lemma 1. Posted-price and efficient welfare satisfy

$$ W(p)=\sum_{q=1}^{m}\mathbb E h_p(A_q,B_q), \qquad O=\sum_{q=1}^{m}\mathbb E\max\{A_q,B_q\}. $$

Proof. If $a_q\le p\le b_q$, then for every $r<q$,

$$ a_r\le a_q\le p\le b_q\le b_r. $$

Thus acceptance of unit $q$ implies acceptance of every preceding unit. Its trade indicator is therefore exactly $\mathbf1_{\{a_q\le p\le b_q\}}$. Initial welfare is $\sum_q a_q$, and trade $q$ changes welfare by $b_q-a_q$, proving the first identity.

After $j$ trades, welfare is

$$ \sum_{q=1}^{m}a_q+\sum_{q=1}^{j}(b_q-a_q). $$

The differences $b_q-a_q$ are nonincreasing, so their positive terms form a prefix. Taking that prefix maximizes welfare, giving

$$ \sum_{q=1}^{m}a_q+\sum_{q=1}^{m}(b_q-a_q)_+ = \sum_{q=1}^{m}\max\{a_q,b_q\}. $$

Taking expectations proves the second identity. $\square$

In particular, only the coordinate marginal distributions matter. Dependence between coordinates on the same side does not affect either expression; independence between the two sides remains essential.

Write

$$ S=\sup_{p\ge0}W(p), \qquad R=\frac SO. $$

Pointwise, posted-price welfare is an available allocation’s welfare, so $0<S\le O$ and $0<R\le1$.

2. Exact evaluation of a supplied society

This section also repairs the bit-complexity imprecision identified in the verifier report.

Let $\tau$ be the total number of listed types. The set of marginal events is

$$ \mathcal E= \{a_q(w):w\text{ listed},\ 1\le q\le m\} \cup \{b_q(v):v\text{ listed},\ 1\le q\le m\}. $$

It has at most $m\tau$ distinct values.

Every positive marginal gain is active on a closed interval $[a_q(w),b_q(v)]$. If $p$ lies strictly between consecutive events $x<y$, moving to $x$ preserves every positive-gain trade active at $p$. Outside the event range, no positive gain occurs. Hence

$$ S=\max_{p\in\mathcal E}W(p). $$

For an exact sweep, maintain

$$ F_q(p)=\Pr(A_q\le p), \qquad C_q(p)=\mathbb E[A_q\mathbf1_{\{A_q\le p\}}], $$

$$ H_q(p)=\Pr(B_q\ge p), \qquad T_q(p)=\mathbb E[B_q\mathbf1_{\{B_q\ge p\}}]. $$

Independence gives expected marginal gain

$$ G_q(p)=F_q(p)T_q(p)-H_q(p)C_q(p). $$

Before the first event, initialize $F_q=C_q=0$, $H_q=1$, and $T_q=\mathbb E B_q$. At each event $x$:

  • Add seller atoms at $x$. A seller atom of mass $\alpha$ changes $G_q$ by

$$ \alpha(T_q-xH_q). $$

  • Evaluate welfare, with buyer atoms at $x$ still present.
  • Remove buyer atoms at $x$. A buyer atom of mass $\beta$ changes $G_q$ by

$$ -\beta(xF_q-C_q). $$

Maintaining $\sum_qG_q$ requires constant arithmetic work per atom.

Efficient welfare is accumulated simultaneously. When a seller atom $A_q=x$ is processed, the maintained buyer tail includes precisely $B_q\ge x$, and

$$ \mathbb E\max\{x,B_q\}=x(1-H_q)+T_q. $$

Multiplying by the seller atom’s mass and summing yields $O$.

Thus exact evaluation takes

$$ O\!\left(m\tau\log(m\tau)\right) $$

rational arithmetic operations.

For total input bit length $L$, the sweep quantities and welfare totals have $O(L)$ bits. Their expanded expressions involve input masses, at most one marginal value per term, and sums; a common denominator divides the product of input denominators. Their magnitudes also have logarithmic size $O(L)$.

The final ratio needs a separate argument: dividing two fractions of $O(L)$ bit length produces another fraction of $O(L)$ bit length, even though its denominator need not divide that common denominator. Reduced-fraction arithmetic therefore gives the conservative bit running time

$$ O\!\left(m\tau\log(m\tau)L^3\right). $$

3. Normalization and control of large prices

Multiplying all marginal values and prices by the same positive number preserves every trade pattern and scales $S$ and $O$ equally. Normalize the instance so that

$$ O=1. $$

Lemma 1 then implies

$$ \sum_{q=1}^{m}\mathbb E A_q\le1, \qquad \sum_{q=1}^{m}\mathbb E B_q\le1. $$

Consequently,

$$ \sum_{q=1}^{m}(\mathbb E A_q)(\mathbb E B_q) \le \left(\sum_q\mathbb E A_q\right) \left(\sum_q\mathbb E B_q\right) \le1. $$

The argument below also applies to independent real-valued ordered marginal vectors with finite expected total values.

Lemma 2. For every $M>0$,

$$ S\le\sup_{0\le p\le M}W(p)+\frac1M. $$

Proof. For $p>M$, if $a\le M$, every trade occurring at $p$ also occurs at $M$, with the same welfare. Thus $h_p(a,b)\le h_M(a,b)$.

If $a>M$, then $h_M(a,b)=a$, and

$$ h_p(a,b)-h_M(a,b)\le b. $$

Combining the cases,

$$ h_p(a,b)\le h_M(a,b)+b\mathbf1_{\{a>M\}}. $$

Independence and Markov’s inequality give

$$ \mathbb E[B_q\mathbf1_{\{A_q>M\}}] = (\mathbb E B_q)\Pr(A_q>M) \le \frac{(\mathbb E A_q)(\mathbb E B_q)}M. $$

Summing over coordinates bounds the extra welfare by $1/M$. Taking the supremum proves the claim. $\square$

This bound controls large prices using first moments, without bounding individual marginal values.

4. Quantile averaging with uniform support size

For a positive integrable random variable $X$, let

$$ F_X(x)=\Pr(X\le x), \qquad Q_X(t)=\inf\{x:F_X(x)\ge t\}, \qquad 0<t<1. $$

Fix an integer $N\ge1$. Partition $(0,1)$ into intervals

$$ I_i=\left(\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right), \qquad 1\le i\le N, $$

and define

$$ x_i=N\int_{I_i}Q_X(t)\,dt. $$

Let $\widehat X$ take the values $x_1,\ldots,x_N$, each with probability $1/N$.

4.1 Means, positivity, and distribution functions

Quantile integration gives

$$ \mathbb E\widehat X = \frac1N\sum_i x_i = \int_0^1Q_X(t)\,dt = \mathbb EX. $$

These identities follow by representing $X$ in distribution as $Q_X(U)$ for a uniform random variable $U$.

Every $x_i$ is finite by integrability. It is positive because $X>0$ almost surely: right continuity of $F_X$ at zero implies $Q_X(t)>0$ for every $t>0$.

The approximation satisfies

$$ \sup_x|F_{\widehat X}(x)-F_X(x)|\le\frac1N. $$

To verify this, fix $x$ and put $f=F_X(x)$. Up to endpoints of measure zero, $Q_X(t)\le x$ exactly for $t\le f$. Every interval entirely below $f$ has mean at most $x$, and every interval entirely above $f$ has mean greater than $x$. At most one interval straddles $f$. Hence the number of averages at most $x$, divided by $N$, differs from $f$ by at most $1/N$.

Taking left limits also gives

$$ \sup_p \left| \Pr(\widehat X\ge p)-\Pr(X\ge p) \right| \le\frac1N. $$

4.2 Ordered vector types

Apply this construction to every coordinate, using the same intervals within each side:

$$ \widehat a_{q,i} = N\int_{I_i}Q_{A_q}(t)\,dt, \qquad \widehat b_{q,i} = N\int_{I_i}Q_{B_q}(t)\,dt. $$

Almost-sure order $A_q\le A_{q+1}$ implies

$$ F_{A_q}(x)\ge F_{A_{q+1}}(x), $$

and therefore

$$ Q_{A_q}(t)\le Q_{A_{q+1}}(t). $$

Integrating proves

$$ \widehat a_{1,i}\le\cdots\le\widehat a_{m,i}. $$

The buyer coordinates satisfy the reverse order by the same argument.

Thus the $N$ seller vectors and $N$ buyer vectors are admissible types. Select their indices independently and uniformly. If $N$ is a power of two, their masses are dyadic; duplicate vectors can be combined.

Normalization and preservation of means give the useful bound

$$ 0<\widehat a_{q,i}\le N\mathbb E A_q\le N, \qquad 0<\widehat b_{q,i}\le N\mathbb E B_q\le N. $$

5. Welfare errors under quantile averaging

Denote the averaged instance’s quantities by $\widehat W$, $\widehat S$, and $\widehat O$.

5.1 Posted-price welfare

For one coordinate, define

$$ F(p)=\Pr(A\le p), \qquad H(p)=\Pr(B\ge p), $$

$$ K(p)=\mathbb E(p-A)_+, \qquad L(p)=\mathbb E(B-p)_+. $$

Splitting $B-A=(B-p)+(p-A)$ on the trading event, and using independence, gives

$$ G(p)=\mathbb E[(B-A)\mathbf1_{\{A\le p\le B\}}] = F(p)L(p)+H(p)K(p). $$

For a nonnegative random variable $X$,

$$ \mathbb E(p-X)_+=\int_0^pF_X(t)\,dt. $$

Therefore the uniform distribution-function error implies

$$ |\widehat K(p)-K(p)|\le\frac pN. $$

Also,

$$ \mathbb E(B-p)_+ = \mathbb EB-p+\mathbb E(p-B)_+. $$

Since quantile averaging preserves $\mathbb EB$,

$$ |\widehat L(p)-L(p)|\le\frac pN. $$

Using $L(p)\le\mathbb EB$, $K(p)\le p$, and probabilities at most one,

$$ \begin{aligned} |\widehat G(p)-G(p)| &\le |\widehat F-F|L +\widehat F|\widehat L-L|\\ &\quad+ |\widehat H-H|K +\widehat H|\widehat K-K|\\ &\le \frac{\mathbb EB+3p}{N}. \end{aligned} $$

The seller baseline is preserved exactly. Summing over coordinates yields, for $0\le p\le M$,

$$ |\widehat W(p)-W(p)| \le \frac{1+3mM}{N}. $$

The averaged instance preserves both sides’ first moments, so Lemma 2 applies to it with the same bound. Consequently,

$$ \widehat S \le S+\frac{1+3mM}{N}+\frac1M. $$

5.2 Efficient welfare

First,

$$ \widehat O\le O=1. $$

Indeed, represent the original coordinate pair as $Q_{A_q}(U),Q_{B_q}(V)$ for independent uniform $U,V$. Conditional on $U\in I_i$ and $V\in I_j$, the expected maximum is at least each conditional mean, hence at least

$$ \max\{\widehat a_{q,i},\widehat b_{q,j}\}. $$

Averaging over $i,j$ and summing over coordinates proves the inequality.

For the opposite bound, use

$$ \max\{A,B\}=A+B-\min\{A,B\}. $$

Means are preserved, so

$$ O-\widehat O = \sum_q \left( \mathbb E\min\{\widehat A_q,\widehat B_q\} - \mathbb E\min\{A_q,B_q\} \right). $$

Independence and integration of nonnegative tails give

$$ \mathbb E\min\{A,B\} = \int_0^\infty\Pr(A>t)\Pr(B>t)\,dt. $$

On $[0,M]$, the product of the two survival functions changes by at most $2/N$. On $(M,\infty)$, the averaged tail satisfies

$$ \begin{aligned} \int_M^\infty \Pr(\widehat A_q>t)\Pr(\widehat B_q>t)\,dt &\le \Pr(\widehat A_q>M) \int_M^\infty\Pr(\widehat B_q>t)\,dt\\ &\le \frac{(\mathbb E A_q)(\mathbb E B_q)}M. \end{aligned} $$

Dropping the nonnegative original tail and summing proves

$$ 0\le1-\widehat O \le \frac{2mM}{N}+\frac1M. $$

6. Rationalization on a universal grid

The averaged instance has at most $2mN$ distinct marginal values. List them as

$$ 0<x_1<\cdots<x_J\le N, \qquad J\le2mN. $$

Choose a positive integer $D$, and put

$$ Q=2mN+1. $$

Replace every occurrence of $x_j$ by

$$ z_j=\frac{\lfloor Dx_j\rfloor}{D}+\frac{j}{DQ}. $$

The floor terms are nondecreasing and the rank terms strictly increase. Thus

$$ 0<z_1<\cdots<z_J, \qquad |z_j-x_j|<\frac1D. $$

Every replacement belongs to the universal grid

$$ \mathcal G= \left\{ \frac{\ell}{DQ}:1\le\ell\le H \right\}, \qquad H=Q(DN+1). $$

Strict preservation of the global event order preserves every price-induced trade pattern: an event price corresponds to its replacement event, and an intervening interval corresponds to the intervening interval with the same index. Equality cases are preserved as well.

For corresponding prices, each marginal welfare term selects the seller value in both instances or the buyer value in both. Therefore, writing $S_{\mathrm{rat}}$ and $O_{\mathrm{rat}}$ for the rationalized instance,

$$ |S_{\mathrm{rat}}-\widehat S|\le\frac mD, \qquad |O_{\mathrm{rat}}-\widehat O|\le\frac mD. $$

The efficient-welfare bound follows because changing both arguments by less than $1/D$ changes their maximum by less than $1/D$.

Reconstructing schedules from these positive ordered marginals produces admissible rational types.

7. Explicit approximation parameters

Fix rational $0<\epsilon\le1$. Set

$$ M=\frac{16}{\epsilon}, $$

let $N$ be the smallest power of two satisfying

$$ N\ge\frac{256m}{\epsilon^2}, $$

and choose

$$ D=\left\lceil\frac{16m}{\epsilon}\right\rceil. $$

Then

$$ \frac{mM}{N}\le\frac{\epsilon}{16}, \qquad \frac1N\le\frac{\epsilon}{256}, \qquad \frac1M=\frac{\epsilon}{16}, \qquad \frac mD\le\frac{\epsilon}{16}. $$

The posted-price bounds give

$$ S_{\mathrm{rat}} \le S+\frac{65\epsilon}{256}+\frac{\epsilon}{16} = S+\frac{81\epsilon}{256}. $$

The efficient-welfare bounds give

$$ O_{\mathrm{rat}} \ge 1-\frac{\epsilon}{8}-\frac{\epsilon}{16}-\frac{\epsilon}{16} = 1-\frac{\epsilon}{4}. $$

Since the normalized original ratio is $R=S\le1$,

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{S_{\mathrm{rat}}}{O_{\mathrm{rat}}}-R &\le \frac{R+81\epsilon/256}{1-\epsilon/4}-R\\ &= \frac{81\epsilon/256+R\epsilon/4}{1-\epsilon/4}\\ &\le \frac{145\epsilon/256}{3/4}\\ &= \frac{145}{192}\epsilon < \epsilon. \end{aligned} $$

This proves the following uniform statement.

Theorem 3. Every admissible society with ratio $R$ has an admissible rational approximation with ratio at most $R+\epsilon$, at most $N$ types per side, dyadic masses, and all marginal values in the explicit grid $\mathcal G$ above.

The proof also applies to independent integrable real-valued admissible priors.

8. Certified finite search and its complexity

Let $u_{m,\epsilon}$ be the minimum ratio among societies obtained by selecting $N$ seller vectors and $N$ buyer vectors from $\mathcal G^m$, with the required coordinate orders, and assigning probability $1/N$ to every selected vector. Repeated vectors are combined.

This is a finite nonempty collection of valid societies, so

$$ \rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}}\le u_{m,\epsilon}. $$

Theorem 3 applies to every society in the definition of $\rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}}$. Taking the infimum of its original ratio gives

$$ u_{m,\epsilon}\le\rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}}+\epsilon. $$

Thus the exact finite-search output certifies

$$ \boxed{ u_{m,\epsilon}-\epsilon \le \rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}} \le u_{m,\epsilon}. } $$

No attainment of the unrestricted infimum is assumed.

The parameters satisfy

$$ N=O\!\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon^2}\right), \qquad D=O\!\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon}\right), \qquad H=O\!\left(\frac{m^4}{\epsilon^5}\right). $$

Each candidate has $\tau\le2N$. Its schedules have denominator $DQ$ and numerators at most $mH$, while its masses have denominator $N$. Its total bit length is therefore bounded by

$$ L_* = O\!\left(mN\log(mND)\right) = O\!\left( \frac{m^2}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{m}{\epsilon} \right). $$

There are at most $H^{2mN}$ candidate arrays. Using the exact evaluator from Section 2, exhaustive search takes at most

$$ H^{2mN}\, O\!\left(mN\log(mN)L_*^3\right) $$

bit operations, apart from polynomial work in the encoding length of $\epsilon$ to form the parameters. Equivalently, the search contribution is bounded by

$$ \exp\!\left( O\!\left( \frac{m^2}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{m}{\epsilon} \right) \right). $$

This proves computability with a certified error bound; the running time is not practical.

There is also an immediate extension of the finite-prior equivalence in the notes. Let $r_m^{\mathrm{int}}$ be the infimum over independent integrable real-valued admissible priors. Subclass inclusion gives $r_m^{\mathrm{int}}\le\rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}}$. Applying Theorem 3 to every such prior gives the reverse inequality up to arbitrary $\epsilon>0$. Hence

$$ r_m^{\mathrm{int}}=\rho_m^{\mathrm{pop}}. $$
