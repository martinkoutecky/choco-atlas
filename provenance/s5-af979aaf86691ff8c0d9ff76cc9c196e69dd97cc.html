The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite left and right type sets \(I\) and \(J\), rational type masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and strict rankings of the opposite-side types with being unmatched last, find a rational matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\nu_j\). For each pair \((i,j)\), define \(w^L_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\) and \(w^R_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_{i':\,i'\succeq_j i}x_{i'j}\); require \(\min\{w^L_{ij}(x),w^R_{ij}(x)\}=0\). Among such stable matrices, find one \(x\) such that for every stable matrix \(y\), type \(i\), and cutoff \(j\), \(\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}\ge\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}y_{ij'}\).

1. The known result being used

Consider stable allocation with positive row supplies \(s_i\), positive column demands \(d_j\), strict preferences, and entry capacities \(c_{ij}\). Feasibility includes \(0\leq x_{ij}\leq c_{ij}\); stability requires

\(x_{ij}<c_{ij}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}=s_i\ \text{or}\ \sum_{h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}=d_j.\)

The corrected Baïou–Balinski paper supplies an inductive algorithm for a stable allocation and a subsequent row-optimal algorithm. Together they use polynomially many arithmetic and comparison operations in the support dimensions. Their numerical updates use addition, subtraction, and minimum selection. The row-optimal output \(z\) satisfies, for every real stable allocation \(y\),

\(z_{ik}<y_{ik}\ \Longrightarrow\ z_{i\ell}=0\quad\text{for every }\ell\prec_i k.\)

These statements hold for arbitrary positive real supplies and demands, without a nondegeneracy assumption. See Theorem 2 and Corollary 1, the preference definition in Section 3, and Theorem 10 and Corollary 4 in the [corrected paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.27.4.662.302).

2. Exact correspondence of stability

Set \(s_i=\mu_i\), \(d_j=\nu_j\), and \(c_{ij}=2\) for every pair.

Every feasible matrix in the question satisfies \(x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\leq1<2\). Thus the added entry capacities impose no restriction, and the antecedent \(x_{ij}<c_{ij}\) always holds.

Moreover, feasibility makes both \(w^L_{ij}(x)\) and \(w^R_{ij}(x)\) nonnegative. Consequently,

\(\min\{w^L_{ij}(x),w^R_{ij}(x)\}=0\)

is equivalent to

\(\sum_{k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}=\mu_i\ \text{or}\ \sum_{h\succeq_j i}x_{hj}=\nu_j.\)

Hence the two models have exactly the same feasible and stable matrices, including their real stable matrices. All hypotheses of the cited result hold: the sets are finite, supplies and demands are positive, preferences are strict, and capacities are nonnegative.

3. Row optimality implies the required prefix inequalities

First, every stable matrix saturates every row and column.

Indeed, let \(T=\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\). If \(T<1\), the equal total masses imply that some row \(i\) and some column \(j\) are unsaturated. Then

\(w^L_{ij}(x)\geq\mu_i-\sum_kx_{ik}>0,\qquad w^R_{ij}(x)\geq\nu_j-\sum_hx_{hj}>0,\)

contradicting stability. Therefore \(T=1\). Since the nonnegative row deficits sum to \(1-T=0\), every row is saturated; the same argument applies to columns.

Now let \(z\) be the cited row-optimal output, let \(y\) be any real stable matrix, and fix \(i,j\). Write \(P=\{k:k\succeq_i j\}\).

If \(z_{ik}\geq y_{ik}\) for every \(k\in P\), summation gives the desired inequality.

Otherwise, choose \(k\in P\) with \(z_{ik}<y_{ik}\). The cited preference property gives \(z_{i\ell}=0\) for every \(\ell\prec_i k\). Because preferences are strict and \(k\succeq_i j\), every \(\ell\notin P\) satisfies \(\ell\prec_i k\). Thus \(z\) assigns no mass outside \(P\). Row saturation and feasibility of \(y\) yield

\(\sum_{k\in P}z_{ik}=\mu_i\geq\sum_{k\in P}y_{ik}.\)

This proves every required prefix inequality, with the required quantification over real stable matrices.

4. Rational output and polynomial bit complexity

Write the input masses as rational numbers with positive integer denominators, and let \(D\) be the product of those denominators. Their total encoding length is at most \(L\), so \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

Scale supplies, demands, and capacities by \(D\). The resulting instance has positive integer supplies and demands, total supply and demand \(D\), and capacities \(2D\). Scaling gives a bijection between real stable matrices of the two instances and preserves all preference comparisons.

Run the two cited algorithms on this integer instance. Their numerical operations preserve integrality. The number of operations is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), independently of \(D\).

For completeness, integer sizes also remain polynomially bounded: starting with \(O(L)\)-bit integers, each addition or subtraction increases the maximum operand bit length by at most one; comparison and minimum selection do not increase it. Thus polynomially many such operations produce only polynomial-bit integers. Exact execution therefore takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\).

Finally, divide each output entry by \(D\). This produces a rational stable matrix with the left optimality proved above. There are \(m\) output entries, each of polynomial encoding length.
