The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), a target \(p\in C\), rational \(\beta\in[0,1]\), and approval types \(A_i\subseteq C\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), is there a mass transfer of total cost at most \(\beta\) that makes \(p\) a winner? Each unit of mass may be bribed at unit cost and assigned any approval ballot.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite candidate set \(C\) with \(m=|C|\geq 1\), a target \(p\in C\), and a binary rational budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), consider approval voting with divisible voter mass.
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) distinct approval types \(A_i\subseteq C\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_i\) satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(2^m\) of possible ballots; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
An action may split each type's mass into arbitrary nonnegative real portions, retain any portion at its original ballot for free, and reassign the remaining portions to any approval ballots \(B\subseteq C\), including ballots absent from the support. Empty and full approval ballots are permitted.
Reassigning mass \(x\) costs \(x\), independently of how many approvals change; total mass is conserved. Approval scores are computed using mass, and all candidates tied for maximum score are winners.

Is deciding whether an action of cost at most \(\beta\) makes \(p\) a winner solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete voters, unit-cost replacement of entire approval ballots is NP-complete, while approval manipulation, including its weighted version, is in P ([Faliszewski–Hemaspaandra–Hemaspaandra](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0608081)).
Continuous priced bribery under ranking-based scoring protocols is in P ([Koutecký–Melissinos–Vu–Sabater](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25444v1)); that result concerns rankings rather than unrestricted approval ballots. No result settling the exact formulation above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted winner as co-winner, preserving the discrete problem's nonunique-winner convention.
  • Made divisibility explicit: portions may have arbitrary nonnegative real masses and may be sent to multiple destination ballots.
  • Specified that retained mass is free, reassignment conserves mass, and destination ballots need not occur in the input support.
  • Allowed every approval subset, including the empty and full ballots, consistently with unrestricted approval vectors.
  • Defined L as total input bit length and required a nonempty candidate set because the target belongs to it.

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). A successful action exists exactly when a rational linear program with at most \(\tau\) variables and \(2\tau+m\) inequalities is feasible.

The argument uses the unrestricted approval ballots and the stated cost per unit of reassigned mass.

1. A canonical form for successful actions

Lemma. If an action makes \(p\) a winner, there is an action of no greater cost making \(p\) a winner in which every reassigned portion receives ballot \(\{p\}\).

Proof. Consider any reassigned portion of mass \(z\) whose destination ballot is \(B\). Changing its destination to \(\{p\}\) changes its contribution to \(p\)’s score from either \(0\) or \(z\) to \(z\). For each rival \(c\ne p\), its contribution changes from either \(0\) or \(z\) to \(0\).

Consequently, this replacement weakly increases \(p\)’s score and weakly decreases every rival’s score. Its cost remains \(z\), because the reassignment cost is independent of the destination ballot.

Perform this replacement for every reassigned portion. If any such portion originally had ballot \(\{p\}\), retain it for free instead. This preserves the resulting scores and weakly decreases the cost. Since every inequality asserting that \(p\)’s score is at least a rival’s score is preserved, \(p\) remains a winner. \(\square\)

Thus it suffices to choose how much mass to take from each original type; no enumeration of destination ballots is necessary.

2. The linear program

For each type \(i\) and candidate \(c\), define the approval indicator

\[ a_{ic}= \begin{cases} 1,&c\in A_i,\\ 0,&c\notin A_i. \end{cases} \]

The original score of \(c\) is \(s_c=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i a_{ic}\).

Let \(I=\{i:A_i\ne\{p\}\}\). For each \(i\in I\), introduce a real variable \(x_i\), representing the mass reassigned from \(A_i\) to \(\{p\}\). Require

\[ 0\le x_i\le\mu_i\qquad(i\in I) \]

and

\[ \sum_{i\in I}x_i\le\beta. \]

After these reassignments, the scores are

\[ s'_p=s_p+\sum_{i\in I}(1-a_{ip})x_i, \]

and, for every \(c\ne p\),

\[ s'_c=s_c-\sum_{i\in I}a_{ic}x_i. \]

Indeed, reassignment adds approval of \(p\) exactly when the source ballot did not approve \(p\), and removes approval of \(c\) exactly when the source ballot approved \(c\).

Subtracting the two score expressions shows that \(s'_p\ge s'_c\) is equivalent to

\[ \sum_{i\in I}(1-a_{ip}+a_{ic})x_i\ge s_c-s_p. \]

Impose this inequality for every \(c\in C\setminus\{p\}\). Each coefficient \(1-a_{ip}+a_{ic}\) belongs to \(\{0,1,2\}\). These non-strict inequalities express precisely the requested winner condition, including ties.

3. Proof of equivalence

From an action to a feasible point. Suppose an action of cost at most \(\beta\) makes \(p\) a winner. Apply the lemma. Let \(x_i\) be the mass reassigned from each type \(i\in I\). Availability gives \(0\le x_i\le\mu_i\), and the action’s cost is \(\sum_{i\in I}x_i\le\beta\). Its winning score inequalities give all the remaining linear constraints by the calculation above.

From a feasible point to an action. Conversely, suppose the linear constraints have a feasible solution. For each \(i\in I\), retain mass \(\mu_i-x_i\) at \(A_i\) and reassign mass \(x_i\) to \(\{p\}\). Retain every original \(\{p\}\) type entirely.

This action is permitted by divisibility. It conserves mass because each original mass \(\mu_i\) is partitioned into portions summing to \(\mu_i\). Its cost is \(\sum_{i\in I}x_i\le\beta\). The calculated score formulas and the final constraints imply \(s'_p\ge s'_c\) for every rival. Hence \(p\) is a winner.

This proves the equivalence in both directions.

4. Encoding size and running time

Write \(n=|I|\le\tau\). The formulation has \(n\) variables, \(2n\) bound inequalities, one budget inequality, and \(m-1\) winner inequalities: altogether \(2n+m\) inequalities.

Its rational data have polynomial encoding length. To verify the only potentially nontrivial point, write \(\mu_i=u_i/v_i\) with positive integer denominators and set \(D=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}v_i\). The binary length of \(D\) is at most one plus the sum of the denominator bit lengths, hence is \(O(L)\). Each score can be written as \(s_c=N_c/D\), where \(0\le N_c\le D\), since the total mass is \(1\). Thus every difference \(s_c-s_p\) also has an \(O(L)\)-bit rational representation. These quantities can be computed by polynomial-time integer arithmetic.

We now apply the standard rational linear-programming theorem: feasibility over real variables of finitely many non-strict linear inequalities with binary rational coefficients is decidable in time polynomial in the system’s encoding length. This is the polynomial-time linear-programming result of [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319). All its hypotheses hold here, so the resulting algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

For \(m=1\), there are no winner inequalities, and retaining all mass already succeeds. The same formulation correctly accepts.
