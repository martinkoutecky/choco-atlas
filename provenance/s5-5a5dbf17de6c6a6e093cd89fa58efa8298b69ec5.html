The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite supports of pair types \(P\) and altruist types \(A_0\), rational masses \(\mu_u\) summing to \(1\), type-determined compatibility, and length limits \(l_p,l_c\), compute the maximum pair mass that can be covered by feasible typed chains and cycles. Equivalently, for a target share \(\rho\), decide whether at least \(\rho\sum_{u\in P}\mu_u\) pair mass can be covered, with each type’s available mass respected.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: disjoint sets of pair types \(P\) and altruist types \(A_0\), with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_u\) summing to \(1\). Write \(\tau=|P|+|A_0|\); this is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Compatibility is an explicitly given relation \(E\subseteq(P\cup A_0)\times P\), with \(m=|E|\). Compatibility concerns distinct participants; diagonal entries allow donations between distinct participants of the same type, never self-donation.
The input also contains binary-encoded nonnegative integers \(l_p,l_c\) and a binary-rational share \(\rho\in[0,1]\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A typed chain is \((a,u_1,\ldots,u_k)\), where \(a\in A_0\), all \(u_i\in P\), consecutive transitions belong to \(E\), and \(1\le k\le l_p\). Its length is \(k\); its final donor need not donate.
A typed cycle is \((u_1,\ldots,u_k)\), with all \(u_i\in P\), all consecutive and closing transitions in \(E\), and \(2\le k\le l_c\). Types may repeat in chains and cycles, representing distinct participants.
Let \(\mathcal S\) contain these structures, and let \(n_u(s)\) count occurrences of type \(u\) in \(s\), counting the initial altruist once and counting a cycle's closing occurrence only once. A mass allocation assigns \(x_s\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) with \(\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}n_u(s)x_s\le\mu_u\) for every type. Its covered pair mass is \(\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}x_s\sum_{u\in P}n_u(s)\).

Is deciding whether the covered pair mass can reach \(\rho\sum_{u\in P}\mu_u\) in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard? If NP-hard, is it FPT parameterized by \(\tau\)? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,l_p,l_c\).

Discrete kidney exchange admits a deterministic \(O^*(6.855^t)\) algorithm, where \(t\) is the number of recipients to cover; it is NP-hard even without altruists and with \(l_c=3\), by the [APX-completeness result for bounded cycle packing](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25732/1/25732.pdf).
The discrete problem is FPT in the number of neighborhood types and is \(\mathrm{W}[1]\)-hard in treewidth, by [Parameterized Complexity of Kidney Exchange Revisited](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0009.pdf).
No result settling this exact mass formulation was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified that length limits are nonnegative binary-encoded integers counting edges, and that the target share is a binary rational in the closed unit interval.
  • Allowed repeated type occurrences to represent distinct participants, charging available mass once per occurrence rather than imposing simplicity on type labels.
  • Interpreted diagonal type compatibility as compatibility between distinct participants of that type; excluded individual self-donation and cycles of length one.
  • Required exactly one initial altruist per chain, prohibited transitions into altruist types, and allowed the final chain donor to remain unused.
  • Made divisibility explicit through nonnegative real structure masses and occurrence-count capacity constraints.
  • Defined m as the number of compatibility transitions and L as the total input bit length.
  • Used support size tau for the conditional FPT question, since normalization leaves no integer recipient-count parameter corresponding to discrete t.

Result

The decision problem is in $\mathrm P$, even when $l_p$ and $l_c$ are binary encoded. Consequently, it is also FPT parameterized by $\tau$.

Define
$$ h=1+\left\lceil\log_2(l_p+1)\right\rceil +\left\lceil\log_2(l_c+1)\right\rceil. $$

We construct an equivalent rational linear program with
$$ O(\tau+\tau^2h) \quad\text{variables and}\quad O(\tau+m+\tau^3h) \quad\text{constraints}. $$

Its total encoding length is polynomial in $L+m+\tau^3h$. Thus a deterministic exact rational linear programming algorithm gives a bit running time
$$ \operatorname{poly}(L+m+\tau^3h). $$

Since the length bounds occur in the input in binary, $h=O(L)$, so this is polynomial in the total input length.

The proof uses exactly the stipulated interpretation: types may repeat within a structure, and allocation variables are real and nonnegative.

1. The allocation LP and its dual

Write
$$ b_s=\sum_{u\in P}n_u(s) $$
for the number of pair occurrences in a structure $s$. The maximum covered pair mass is the optimum of
$$ \begin{aligned} \max\quad &\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}b_sx_s\\ \text{subject to}\quad &\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}n_u(s)x_s\le\mu_u &&\text{for every }u\in P\cup A_0,\\ &x_s\ge0 &&\text{for every }s\in\mathcal S. \end{aligned} $$

The set $\mathcal S$ is finite because both length bounds are finite. This LP is feasible, by taking every allocation to be zero. Each variable is bounded: choosing any type occurring in $s$ gives
$$ x_s\le\frac{\mu_u}{n_u(s)}. $$
Its maximum therefore exists. Moreover, summing the pair capacity constraints bounds its objective by $\sum_{u\in P}\mu_u$.

The dual is
$$ \begin{aligned} \min\quad &\sum_{u\in P\cup A_0}\mu_uy_u\\ \text{subject to}\quad &\sum_{u\in P\cup A_0}n_u(s)y_u\ge b_s &&\text{for every }s\in\mathcal S,\\ &y_u\ge0 &&\text{for every }u\in P\cup A_0. \end{aligned} $$

It is feasible: take $y_u=1$ for pair types and $y_a=0$ for altruist types. Finite-dimensional LP strong duality therefore identifies its optimum with the maximum covered pair mass.

We may additionally impose
$$ 0\le y_u\le K, \qquad K=\max\{1,l_p,l_c\}, $$
without changing the optimum. Indeed, replace any feasible $y_u$ by $\min\{y_u,K\}$. For a structure containing a clipped coordinate, that coordinate alone contributes at least $K$, whereas $b_s\le K$. A structure containing no clipped coordinate retains its original constraint value. The objective cannot increase because all masses are positive.

If $P$ is empty, the target is zero and the answer is immediately yes. Henceforth assume $|P|\ge1$.

2. Dual constraints are additive walk constraints

For each pair type $v$, define
$$ w_v=1-y_v. $$

For a chain $s=(a,u_1,\ldots,u_k)$, its dual constraint is equivalent to
$$ -y_a+\sum_{i=1}^kw_{u_i}\le0. $$

For a cycle $s=(u_1,\ldots,u_k)$, its dual constraint is equivalent to
$$ \sum_{i=1}^kw_{u_i}\le0. $$

Thus dual feasibility requires that every admissible chain and cycle have nonpositive score.

Crucially, these are scores of walks on the type graph: there is no restriction against revisiting a type.

Let $n=|P|$. Define an $n$ by $n$ matrix
$$ M_{uv}= \begin{cases} w_v,&(u,v)\in E,\\ -\infty,&(u,v)\notin E. \end{cases} $$

Use max-plus operations
$$ (X\oplus Y)_{ij}=\max\{X_{ij},Y_{ij}\}, \qquad (X\otimes Y)_{ij}=\max_t\{X_{it}+Y_{tj}\}. $$
Here $-\infty$ denotes an unavailable transition, with $-\infty+z=-\infty$.

The max-plus identity $I$ has diagonal entries zero and all other entries $-\infty$.

By induction on $r$, $(M^r)_{uv}$ is the maximum score of a pair-type walk with exactly $r$ transitions from $u$ to $v$, counting the destination of each transition. For the induction step, every walk of length $r+1$ consists of a length-$r$ walk to some intermediate type $t$, followed by the transition $(t,v)$; maximizing over $t$ gives matrix multiplication.

This convention handles cycles correctly. For the closed walk
$$ u_1,u_2,\ldots,u_k,u_1, $$
the transition destinations are $u_2,\ldots,u_k,u_1$, so its score is precisely $\sum_{i=1}^kw_{u_i}$. The closing occurrence is counted once.

3. Binary length bounds require only logarithmically many matrix operations

For an integer $q\ge0$, put
$$ R_q=M^q, \qquad H_q=I\oplus M\oplus\cdots\oplus M^{q-1}. $$
For $q=0$, define $H_0$ to have every entry $-\infty$.

The base values are
$$ R_0=I, \qquad H_0=(-\infty)_{ij}. $$

For every $q\ge0$, the following identities hold:
$$ \begin{aligned} R_{2q}&=R_q\otimes R_q,\\ H_{2q}&=H_q\oplus(R_q\otimes H_q),\\ R_{2q+1}&=R_{2q}\otimes M,\\ H_{2q+1}&=H_{2q}\oplus R_{2q}. \end{aligned} $$

To verify the second identity, the first summand represents exponents from zero through $q-1$, and the second represents exponents from $q$ through $2q-1$. The last identity adds exponent $2q$. The power identities follow from concatenation.

Recursing on the binary expansion of $q$, while reusing intermediate matrices, therefore builds $R_q,H_q$ with
$$ O(1+\log_2(q+1)) $$
matrix operations.

Chain score

For $v\in P$, define
$$ r_v=\max_{\substack{a\in A_0\\(a,v)\in E}} \{w_v-y_a\}, $$
with value $-\infty$ if there is no such altruist transition.

If $l_p\ge1$, the maximum admissible chain score is
$$ V_p=\max_{u,v\in P} \{r_u+(H_{l_p})_{uv}\}. $$

Indeed, the initial altruist transition supplies one pair occurrence, and $H_{l_p}$ supplies between zero and $l_p-1$ further transitions. All terminal types $v$ are permitted, matching the premise that the final donor need not donate.

If $l_p=0$, there are no chain constraints.

Cycle score

If $l_c\ge2$, the maximum admissible cycle score is
$$ V_c=\max_{u\in P} (M^2\otimes H_{l_c-1})_{uu}. $$

The matrix inside this expression equals
$$ M^2\oplus M^3\oplus\cdots\oplus M^{l_c}, $$
so it includes exactly the allowed closed-walk lengths.

In particular, a diagonal compatibility entry does not introduce a forbidden length-one cycle. It can introduce a length-two cycle using two distinct participants of the same type, as required by the task.

If $l_c<2$, there are no cycle constraints.

Consequently, the dual structure constraints are equivalent to requiring $V_p\le0$ and $V_c\le0$ for whichever families are present.

4. Converting the computations into a compact LP

The computations above use maxima and additions, starting from affine expressions in $y$. We now represent them by linear inequalities.

An entry equal to $-\infty$ is omitted. Which entries are omitted depends only on compatibility and the length bounds, because every available transition has a finite score for every real $y$.

For each finite computed entry, introduce a real variable representing an upper bound on its value.

For a matrix product $Z=X\otimes Y$, impose
$$ z_{ij}\ge x_{it}+y_{tj} $$
for every $t$ for which both summands are finite. Here $x_{it}$ and $y_{tj}$ denote the corresponding predecessor variables or affine leaf expressions.

For a matrix maximum $Z=X\oplus Y$, impose
$$ z_{ij}\ge x_{ij}, \qquad z_{ij}\ge y_{ij} $$
whenever the respective predecessor entry is finite.

Likewise, represent each finite initial chain value $r_v$ by a variable satisfying
$$ r_v\ge1-y_v-y_a $$
for every $(a,v)\in E$ with $a\in A_0$.

At the chain output impose
$$ r_u+(H_{l_p})_{uv}\le0 $$
for every finite candidate. At the cycle output impose
$$ (M^2\otimes H_{l_c-1})_{uu}\le0 $$
for every finite diagonal entry. In these inequalities, computed entries mean their introduced variables.

All these constraints are linear.

Equivalence proof. Fix a vector $y$.

  • If $y$ satisfies the original dual constraints, assign every auxiliary variable its actual computed value. Every intermediate inequality holds, and every finite chain or cycle output is nonpositive. Thus an auxiliary extension exists.
  • Conversely, suppose an auxiliary extension exists. Induct through the computation. Affine leaves have their exact values. A product-entry variable is at least every sum of predecessor upper bounds, hence at least the actual maximum of those sums. A maximum-entry variable is at least each predecessor upper bound, hence at least the actual maximum. Therefore every auxiliary variable upper-bounds its actual computed value. The terminal inequalities force every actual chain and cycle score to be nonpositive. Thus $y$ satisfies every original dual constraint.

This proves that projecting the compact LP onto its $y$ coordinates gives exactly the bounded dual feasible region.

The argument remains valid for negative scores: addition and maximum are monotone in every operand, regardless of sign.

5. Size, exact solution, and decision

The two binary-doubling computations use $O(h)$ matrix operations. Each introduces at most $n^2$ entry variables and at most $n^3$ inequalities. Initial altruist scores use at most $n$ variables and $m$ inequalities; terminal chain comparisons use at most $n^2$ inequalities.

Including the $\tau$ dual coordinates and their bounds gives
$$ N=O(\tau+\tau^2h), \qquad C=O(\tau+m+\tau^3h). $$

Each circuit inequality contains only a constant number of terms. Its coefficients and constants are bounded integers; the upper bound $K$ has $O(h)$ bits. The objective coefficients are the input masses. Thus the complete rational LP has encoding length polynomial in $L+m+\tau^3h$.

We use the standard exact rational LP theorem: a linear program with explicitly given rational data can be optimized, or classified as infeasible or unbounded, in deterministic time polynomial in its total binary encoding length. See Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, [*Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization*, Chapter 6](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/pubnew/paper/groetschellovaszschrijver1988.pdf). Its hypotheses hold here: the LP is explicit, rational, feasible, and its objective is bounded below by zero.

Minimize $\sum_u\mu_uy_u$ in this compact LP and let the exact optimum be $z^\star$. By the projection equivalence and strong duality, $z^\star$ is the maximum achievable covered pair mass.

Compute the rational target
$$ T=\rho\sum_{u\in P}\mu_u. $$
The decision answer is yes exactly when
$$ z^\star\ge T. $$
This is an exact rational comparison, so equality at the threshold causes no issue.

The resulting bit running time is
$$ \operatorname{poly}\!\left( L+m+\tau^3\bigl(1+\log_2(l_p+1)+\log_2(l_c+1)\bigr) \right). $$
The polynomial has a universal exponent, independent of $\tau$ and the length bounds. Hence the problem is in $\mathrm P$ and, in particular, FPT parameterized by $\tau$.

6. Why the discrete hardness does not transfer

The cited hardness result concerns integral, vertex-disjoint cycle packing. The cited parameterized results likewise concern selecting vertex-disjoint paths and cycles in a finite participant graph. See [Biró–Manlove–Rizzi](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25732/1/25732.pdf) and [*Parameterized Complexity of Kidney Exchange Revisited*](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2024/0009.pdf). Their statements do not establish hardness of this fractional allocation problem.

A concrete difference already occurs with $l_c=3$. Take five pair types indexed cyclically, no altruists, masses $\mu_i=1/5$, and compatibility in both directions between consecutive types. There are no loops or triangles.

Allocate mass $1/10$ to each of the five adjacent length-two cycles. Each type occurs in two such cycles and therefore consumes exactly $1/5$ mass. The allocation covers all pair mass.

With one discrete participant of each type, every allowed cycle has length two: a length-three cycle would require a triangle. At most two disjoint length-two cycles fit, covering only four of the five participants.

The compact LP above resolves the mass formulation directly, including its repeated-type and binary-length features.
