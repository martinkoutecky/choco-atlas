1. Admissible populations and the benchmark

Let $D$ be the least common multiple of the denominators of the masses, written in lowest terms, and put

$$ \ell_0=\left\lceil\frac{2}{D}\right\rceil. $$

The admissible population sizes are precisely

$$ N=D\ell,\qquad \ell\in\mathbb Z,\qquad \ell\ge\ell_0. $$

Indeed, the integrality conditions are equivalent to $D\mid N$. For such a population,

$$ \sum_i\alpha_i = \sum_\theta N\mu_\theta\frac{a_\theta}{N} =1. $$

Every agent has a positive share, and there are at least two agents. Consequently each share is strictly less than $1$, so the individual share constraint imposes no further restriction.

For a finite-support distribution $F$, define

$$ g_F(x) = \max_{\substack{0\le\rho(v)\le1\\ \mathbb E[\rho(V)]\le x}} \mathbb E[V\rho(V)], \qquad 0\le x\le1. $$

Thus

$$ v^*_{\theta,N}=g_{F_\theta}(a_\theta/N). $$

Remove zero-probability atoms and merge repeated values. Write the remaining atoms in decreasing order as

$$ v_1>v_2>\cdots>v_s\ge0, \qquad \Pr(V=v_j)=p_j>0, $$

where $s\le m$. Define

$$ q_0=s_0=0,\qquad q_j=\sum_{r=1}^j p_r,\qquad s_j=\sum_{r=1}^j p_rv_r. $$

Here $s_j$ denotes accumulated utility, whereas $s$ denotes the number of atoms.

A maximizing selector first selects the largest values. To justify this, write the selected probability mass at atom $j$ as $z_j=p_j\rho(v_j)$. If $v_j>v_k$, $z_j<p_j$, and $z_k>0$, transferring a positive amount of selected mass from $k$ to $j$ strictly increases utility without changing the total selected mass. Because values are nonnegative, the constraint can be saturated, including by selecting zero values when necessary.

It follows that, for $q_{j-1}\le x\le q_j$,

$$ g_F(x) = s_{j-1}+v_j(x-q_{j-1}). $$

In particular, $g_F$ is increasing, concave, and piecewise affine. On each affine piece it has the form

$$ g_F(x)=d+cx,\qquad c,d\ge0, $$

because

$$ c=v_j,\qquad d=\sum_{r<j}p_r(v_r-v_j). $$

Let

$$ M=v_1,\qquad p=p_1. $$

Positive mean implies $M>0$. For $0<x\le p$,

$$ g_F(x)=Mx. $$

2. A uniform finite-horizon policy lemma

The next lemma repairs both specification issues identified in the verifier report.

Lemma. Fix a protected agent with share $0<\alpha<1$, horizon $T$, and initial budget $B_0=\alpha T$. Suppose a valuation selector has activation probability and conditional active value

$$ q=\mathbb E[\rho(V)]>0, \qquad w=\frac{\mathbb E[V\rho(V)]}{q}. $$

Set

$$ b=1-\alpha+\frac{\alpha}{q}, \qquad \delta=\frac{1}{1+B_0}. $$

The policy is:

  • If the remaining budget is below $b+\delta$, bid zero.
  • Otherwise activate with probability $\rho(V)$; upon activation bid $b+\delta U$, with a fresh independent uniform sample $U$.
  • If not activated, bid zero.

Against all feasible opponent strategies and all tie rules,

$$ \mathbb E\left[\sum_{t=1}^T V[t]W[t]\right] \ge w\left( \frac{B_0}{b}-2-2\sqrt{\frac{B_0}{b}+1} \right). $$

Proof. Since $q\le1$, we have $b\ge1$. The budget test makes every bid feasible. Conditional on the history, activation, and the opponents’ current bids, the protected positive bid has a continuous distribution independent of those bids. Positive-bid ties therefore have probability zero.

Let $S$ count protected wins with positive bids and put

$$ K=\frac{B_0}{b}. $$

We first specify an unconditional coupling used below. On the full probability space, take an infinite independent sequence

$$ X_1,X_2,\ldots\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(q). $$

An eligible round is one whose protected budget is at least $b+\delta$. Call an eligible round low if the maximum opponent bid is below $b$. Whether a round is eligible and low is determined before observing the protected current value or fresh samples. Supply successive eligible low rounds with successive $X_r$ as their activation indicators, and sample the current value and selector randomness conditionally on that indicator. In other rounds use separate fresh randomness.

This construction preserves the required joint law of the current value and activation. Unused $X_r$ remain defined. In particular, neither this sequence nor the random variable defined from it below is conditioned on eventual budget exhaustion.

Set

$$ h_0=T-\frac{(1-\alpha)T}{b}, \qquad n_0=\lceil h_0\rceil, $$

and define unconditionally

$$ Z= \max_{0\le j\le n_0} \left|\sum_{r=1}^j(X_r-q)\right|. $$

There are two budget cases.

Case 1: the protected budget falls below $b+\delta$. This includes initial insufficiency and a threshold crossing after the last round. Only positive-bid wins reduce the protected budget, and each costs at most $b+\delta$. Hence

$$ S\ge\frac{B_0}{b+\delta}-1. $$

Because $B_0\delta<1$ and $b\ge1$,

$$ \frac{B_0}{b+\delta} = K-\frac{B_0\delta}{b(b+\delta)} \ge K-1. $$

Thus $S\ge K-2$ in this case.

Case 2: the protected budget never falls below $b+\delta$. All rounds are eligible. Let $H$ count low rounds, and let $S_h$ count protected wins in the remaining, high rounds.

Every high round not won by the protected agent requires an opponent payment of at least $b$. The opponents’ aggregate initial budget is $(1-\alpha)T$, so

$$ T-H-S_h\le\frac{(1-\alpha)T}{b}, $$

and therefore

$$ H\ge h_0-S_h. $$

The definition of $b$ gives

$$ qh_0 = \frac{qT(b-1+\alpha)}{b} = \frac{\alpha T}{b} =K. $$

Activation guarantees a win in a low round. Consequently,

$$ S\ge \sum_{r=1}^{\min(H,n_0)}X_r+S_h. $$

Since $n_0\ge h_0$ and $H\ge h_0-S_h$,

$$ \min(H,n_0)\ge h_0-S_h. $$

Using the definition of $Z$,

$$ S \ge q\min(H,n_0)-Z+S_h \ge K+(1-q)S_h-Z \ge K-Z. $$

Combining the two cases gives, on the full probability space,

$$ S\ge K-2-Z. $$

The centered Bernoulli partial sums form a square-integrable martingale. Doob’s maximal inequality and Cauchy–Schwarz give

$$ \mathbb E[Z] \le 2\sqrt{n_0q(1-q)} \le 2\sqrt{K+1}, $$

where the last inequality follows from $n_0q\le qh_0+q\le K+1$. Hence

$$ \mathbb E[S]\ge K-2-2\sqrt{K+1}. $$

Finally, conditional on activation, the current value has mean $w$. Eligibility depends on the past, and the positive bid uses no further information about the active value. The opponents cannot observe that current value before bidding. Thus every counted win contributes expected utility $w$ conditional on the variables determining its winning event. Zero-bid wins contribute nonnegative utility. Therefore total expected utility is at least $w\mathbb E[S]$. $\square$

3. Optimizing the valuation selector

For fixed $F$ and $\alpha$, use a selector attaining $g_F(q)$ with activation probability exactly $q$. The leading factor from the lemma is

$$ R_F(\alpha,q) = \frac{\alpha g_F(q)} {g_F(\alpha)\bigl(\alpha+(1-\alpha)q\bigr)}. $$

Define

$$ R_F(\alpha)=\max_{0<q\le1}R_F(\alpha,q). $$

This optimization has a finite exact solution.

Proposition. An optimal $q$ belongs to the set of upper-tail probabilities $\{q_1,\ldots,q_s\}$. Consequently,

$$ R_F(\alpha) = \max_{1\le j\le s} \frac{\alpha s_j} {g_F(\alpha)\bigl(q_j+(1-q_j)\alpha\bigr)}. $$

Proof. The factors independent of $q$ can be omitted when maximizing. On a piece where $g_F(q)=d+cq$,

$$ \frac{d}{dq} \left( \frac{d+cq}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)q} \right) = \frac{c\alpha-(1-\alpha)d} {\bigl(\alpha+(1-\alpha)q\bigr)^2}. $$

The derivative has constant sign on that piece. Its maximum therefore occurs at an endpoint, unless the function is constant, when either endpoint suffices. The endpoint $q=0$ has value zero, whereas $q_1>0$ has positive value. The remaining endpoints are precisely $q_1,\ldots,q_s$. $\square$

The corresponding policy activates whenever the value is one of the top $j$ atoms. No cutoff randomization is needed.

There is also a uniform error bound. Write

$$ C_F=\frac{M}{\mathbb E[V]}. $$

Since the selector $\rho(v)=\alpha$ is feasible,

$$ g_F(\alpha)\ge\alpha\mathbb E[V]. $$

Moreover $w\le M$ and $K\le\alpha T$. Applying the lemma to a maximizing cutoff gives

$$ \frac{\mathbb E[\sum_t V[t]W[t]]}{Tg_F(\alpha)} \ge R_F(\alpha) - C_F\frac{2+2\sqrt{\alpha T+1}}{\alpha T}. $$

For each type,

$$ \alpha_{\theta,N}T=a_\theta T/N. $$

Thus the error tends to zero along every sequence specified in the question, uniformly over protected agents, opponent strategies, and tie rules.

4. Computing the worst population without enumerating populations

Define the sufficient factor

$$ G_{\mathrm{tail}} = \min_\theta \inf_{\ell\ge\ell_0} R_{F_\theta}\left(\frac{a_\theta}{D\ell}\right), $$

where $\ell$ ranges over integers.

Although the population sizes are unbounded, this number can be computed from polynomially many rational candidates.

Fix one type, suppress its subscript, and define

$$ f_j(\alpha) = \frac{s_j}{q_j+(1-q_j)\alpha}. $$

The maximizing cutoff in $R_F(\alpha)$ is a maximizer of $f_j(\alpha)$. Two nonidentical functions $f_j,f_k$ can intersect at most once, because their equality is equivalent to the linear equation

$$ s_j\bigl(q_k+(1-q_k)\alpha\bigr) = s_k\bigl(q_j+(1-q_j)\alpha\bigr). $$

Make a breakpoint set consisting of:

  • all $q_j$;
  • all pairwise intersections lying in $(0,1)$;
  • the endpoint $1$.

It has size $O(m^2)$, and all its entries are rational.

Between consecutive breakpoints, $g_F(\alpha)=d+c\alpha$ with $c,d\ge0$, and a single index $j$ can be chosen as a maximizer throughout the interval. There,

$$ R_F(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha s_j} {\bigl(q_j+(1-q_j)\alpha\bigr)(d+c\alpha)}. $$

The sign of its derivative is the sign of

$$ q_jd-(1-q_j)c\alpha^2. $$

As $\alpha$ increases, this expression can change sign only from positive to negative. Therefore the function has no strict interior minimum: it is monotone, constant, or increases and then decreases.

It follows that the minimum over the discrete points $\alpha=a/(D\ell)$ in any such interval occurs at an extreme discrete point of that interval. For a positive breakpoint $r$, these extreme points are obtained from integers adjacent to

$$ \frac{a}{Dr}. $$

Accordingly, form the candidate set

$$ \mathcal C= \{\ell_0\} \cup \bigcup_r \left\{ \max\left\{\ell_0,\left\lfloor\frac{a}{Dr}\right\rfloor\right\}, \max\left\{\ell_0,\left\lceil\frac{a}{Dr}\right\rceil\right\} \right\}. $$

The unbounded final interval also has the limiting endpoint $\alpha=0$. Its limit is

$$ \lim_{\alpha\downarrow0}R_F(\alpha)=1. $$

To verify this, the maximum-atom cutoff alone gives

$$ R_F(\alpha)\ge \frac{p}{p+(1-p)\alpha} \qquad \text{when }\alpha\le p. $$

Conversely, $R_F(\alpha,q)\le1$ for every $q$. If $q\le\alpha$, use $g_F(q)\le g_F(\alpha)$; if $q\ge\alpha$, concavity and $g_F(0)=0$ give

$$ \frac{g_F(q)}{q}\le\frac{g_F(\alpha)}{\alpha}. $$

Substitution proves the claimed upper bound in both cases.

We have therefore established the exact finite formula

$$ \inf_{\ell\ge\ell_0} R_F\left(\frac{a}{D\ell}\right) = \min\left( \{1\}\cup \left\{ R_F\left(\frac{a}{D\ell}\right):\ell\in\mathcal C \right\} \right). $$

This computes $G_{\mathrm{tail}}$ exactly without enumerating the populations.

5. Synthesis running time and the supplied baseline

Proved synthesis result. Every requested factor

$$ \beta\le G_{\mathrm{tail}} $$

is attainable by a compact type-indexed policy.

For each type, the program stores its sorted support and the prefix data $(q_j,s_j)$. Given $N$, it computes $\alpha=a_\theta/N$, chooses a maximizing cutoff in Section 3, and applies the policy of Section 2.

There are $O(m^2)$ candidate populations per type. Evaluating $g_F(\alpha)$ and all cutoff expressions takes $O(m)$ rational operations per candidate. All preprocessing numbers have bit lengths $O(L)$: this includes $D$, prefix sums, pairwise intersections, and candidate population sizes.

Using elementary integer arithmetic, a conservative preprocessing and synthesis bound is

$$ O\bigl((\tau m^3+1)L^3\bigr) $$

bit operations. The descriptions have polynomial length and contain formulas and support tables, rather than expanded agents or a horizon table. A round uses $O(m)$ exact arithmetic and comparison operations and finitely many uniform samples.

The supplied baseline can also be retained. [Theorem 1 and the RRB specification](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13824v2) give the limiting factor $c_0=2-\sqrt2$ for each fixed positive share. The policy activates using an ideal selector and, while its budget is at least $1+\sqrt2$, bids uniformly on $[0,1+\sqrt2]$. Its assumptions—independent private values, first-price payments, arbitrary opponents, and arbitrary ties—match the present fixed-population setting.

To obtain the stronger population-sequence quantifier, set

$$ H= \left\lceil \max\left\{2,\ 4\max_\theta\frac{a_\theta}{p_\theta}\right\} \right\rceil. $$

Use RRB for $N<H$ and the maximum-atom policy for $N\ge H$. In the latter branch, $\alpha\le p_\theta/4$, and its leading factor is

$$ \frac{1}{1+\alpha(1/p_\theta-1)} \ge\frac45. $$

Its uniform error vanishes when $T/N\to\infty$. The former branch contains finitely many populations, so their fixed-population convergence thresholds can be made simultaneous.

Thus the strengthened sufficient interval is

$$ \boxed{ \beta\le \max\{2-\sqrt2,\ G_{\mathrm{tail}}\}. } $$

The same polynomial synthesis bound applies. This is a sufficient-condition algorithm, not an algorithm deciding unrestricted attainability.

6. A strict improvement over the previous cutoff rule

Consider one type with

$$ \mu=a=1 $$

and distribution

$$ \Pr(V=10)=\frac1{10},\qquad \Pr(V=9)=\frac25,\qquad \Pr(V=1)=\frac12. $$

The admissible populations are all $N\ge2$, with $\alpha=1/N$.

For the top-two-atoms cutoff,

$$ q=\frac12,\qquad g_F(q)=\frac{23}{5}. $$

For $0<\alpha\le1/10$,

$$ g_F(\alpha)=10\alpha, $$

and its leading factor is

$$ R_F(\alpha,1/2) = \frac{23}{25(1+\alpha)} \ge\frac{46}{55}>\frac23. $$

For $1/10\le\alpha\le1/2$,

$$ g_F(\alpha)=9\alpha+\frac1{10}. $$

The inequality $R_F(\alpha,1/2)\ge2/3$ is equivalent to

$$ -9\alpha^2+\frac{47}{10}\alpha-\frac1{10}\ge0. $$

This polynomial is concave. Its values at $1/10$ and $1/2$ are respectively $7/25$ and zero, so it is nonnegative throughout that interval.

Consequently the top-two-atoms policy guarantees at least $2/3$ for every admissible population.

At $N=2$, the three possible cutoff probabilities give leading factors

$$ R_F(1/2,1/10)=\frac{50}{253},\qquad R_F(1/2,1/2)=\frac23,\qquad R_F(1/2,1)=\frac{51}{92}. $$

Hence

$$ G_{\mathrm{tail}}=\frac23. $$

For comparison, the previous rule—maximum-atom activation when $\alpha\le1/10$, and ideal-selector activation otherwise—has worst leading factor $10/19$, attained at $N=10$. Even after including the supplied baseline, its sufficient factor is only $2-\sqrt2<2/3$.

This proves that optimizing all upper-tail cutoffs strictly enlarges the previously certified interval.

7. A necessary bound from weighted subsets

The following bound applies to every allowed policy, including arbitrary history-dependent policies.

Fix an admissible population $N$. Choose any subset $S$ of its agents and nonnegative weights $\lambda_i$, not all zero on $S$. If $\beta$ is attainable, then

$$ \boxed{ \beta \sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i v^*_{\operatorname{type}(i),N} \le \mathbb E\left[\max_{i\in S}\lambda_i V_i\right]. } $$

Proof. Run all agents’ assigned programs simultaneously, with a fixed permissible tie rule. For each protected agent, the other programs constitute an admissible opponent strategy: they do not observe its current private value or fresh randomization.

The requested robustness condition applies to a constant-population sequence. Therefore, for every $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently large $T$ satisfy

$$ \mathbb E\left[\sum_{t=1}^T V_i[t]W_i[t]\right] \ge (\beta-\varepsilon)T v^*_{\operatorname{type}(i),N} $$

for every agent. Since the population is finite, one horizon threshold works for all agents in $S$.

In each round, at most one agent receives the item, so

$$ \sum_{i\in S}\lambda_iV_i[t]W_i[t] \le \max_{i\in S}\lambda_iV_i[t]. $$

Multiply the robustness inequalities by their weights, sum, and compare with this roundwise bound. Division by $T$ and passage to $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ prove the result. $\square$

For type-symmetric subsets containing $k_\theta$ agents of type $\theta$, with weight $\lambda_\theta$, this becomes

$$ \beta \sum_\theta k_\theta\lambda_\theta g_{F_\theta}(a_\theta/N) \le \mathbb E\left[ \max_{\theta,\ 1\le r\le k_\theta} \lambda_\theta V_{\theta,r} \right]. $$

Taking every agent and every weight equal to $1$ recovers the earlier total-welfare bound. Subsets can give strictly stronger obstructions.

For example, take two types with

$$ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad a_1=a_2=1, $$

where

$$ F_1=\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/4), \qquad F_2\text{ is deterministic at }100. $$

At $N=4$, there are two agents of each type, each with share $1/4$. The subset of the two Bernoulli agents has total ideal utility $1/2$, whereas

$$ \mathbb E[\max(V_1,V_2)] = 1-\left(\frac34\right)^2 = \frac7{16}. $$

Thus every attainable factor satisfies

$$ \beta\le\frac78. $$

The total-welfare bound instead gives

$$ \beta\le \frac{100}{2(1/4)+2(25)} = \frac{200}{101}>1, $$

which excludes no requested factor.

8. Complete special cases

8.1 Deterministic values

Suppose every type has a single positive value $M_\theta$. Use $q=1$ in the policy lemma. Then $b=1$, activation is deterministic, and the Bernoulli deviations vanish.

The two budget cases in its proof give the pathwise bound

$$ S\ge\alpha T-2. $$

Since $g_F(\alpha)=\alpha M_\theta$,

$$ \frac{\mathbb E[\sum_tV[t]W[t]]}{Tg_F(\alpha)} \ge 1-\frac{2}{\alpha T}. $$

The error vanishes whenever $T/N\to\infty$. Therefore every permitted $\beta\in[0,1]$ is attainable.

In particular, the valid-input restriction $m=1$ is polynomial-time solvable, with compact-policy synthesis.

8.2 The endpoint $\beta=1$ when there is one type

Proposition. For $\tau=1$, the factor $\beta=1$ is attainable if and only if the value distribution is deterministic.

Proof. The input normalizations force $\mu=a=1$. Deterministic distributions were handled above.

Suppose instead that $F$ is nonconstant. Let $M$ be its largest value, $p$ the probability of that value, and $M'<M$ the largest remaining value. Then $p<1$.

Choose an integer

$$ N\ge\max\left\{2,\left\lceil\frac1p\right\rceil\right\}. $$

This population is admissible, and

$$ v^*_N=g_F(1/N)=\frac{M}{N}. $$

Thus the population’s total ideal utility is $M$. Applying the necessary bound to all $N$ agents gives

$$ \beta M\le\mathbb E[\max_{1\le i\le N}V_i]. $$

With probability $(1-p)^N>0$, no agent realizes $M$; on that event the maximum is at most $M'$. Hence

$$ \mathbb E[\max_iV_i] \le M-(M-M')(1-p)^N<M. $$

It follows that $\beta<1$, excluding $\beta=1$. $\square$

This endpoint decision requires only checking whether the distribution has one distinct positive-probability value. It is polynomial-time solvable, and the affirmative case has the explicit policy above.

9. Reduction to finitely many populations for $\beta<1$

The endpoint $\beta=0$ is immediate from nonnegative utility. Fix $0<\beta<1$.

For each type, let $M_\theta$ be its maximum value and $p_\theta$ its probability. Define

$$ H_\beta= \left\lceil \max\left\{ 2,\ \max_\theta\frac{a_\theta}{p_\theta},\ \max_\theta \frac{a_\theta\beta(1-p_\theta)} {p_\theta(1-\beta)} \right\} \right\rceil. $$

When $N\ge H_\beta$, the maximum-atom policy has $\alpha\le p_\theta$ and leading factor

$$ \frac{1}{1+\alpha(1/p_\theta-1)} \ge\beta. $$

Its error from Section 2 vanishes uniformly under $T/N\to\infty$.

Therefore the original instance is attainable if and only if each admissible population $N<H_\beta$ admits finite type-indexed programs satisfying the fixed-$N$, $T\to\infty$ robustness condition, including its worst-case opponent and tie quantifiers.

Necessity follows by taking constant-population sequences. For sufficiency, combine the assumed programs for the finitely many exceptional populations by branching on $N$, and use the maximum-atom policy for larger populations. The finite branch has simultaneous convergence thresholds because it contains finitely many populations; the large branch has the uniform error bound.

This is an existential equivalence. It does not decide whether the exceptional programs exist.

Moreover, the number of exceptional populations can be exponential in $L$. For one type with

$$ \mu=a=1,\qquad F=\operatorname{Bernoulli}(2^{-b}),\qquad \beta=\frac34, $$

the threshold is

$$ H_\beta=3(2^b-1) $$

for $b\ge2$. Thus the reduction alone does not provide a polynomial-time decision algorithm.

10. Remaining gap

Even a particularly small fixed-population case is not settled by these results. For one type with

$$ \mu=a=1,\qquad F=\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2), $$
