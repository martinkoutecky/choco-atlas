Interpretation and conclusion. The incompatibility is correct if the finite list specifies the cost distribution exactly: each listed mass is the probability of its associated cost. A finite list of realized costs sampled from a continuous distribution would have a different meaning.

Under the exact distribution interpretation, the admissible family is empty. This settles the consistency issue; it does not establish a complexity classification for a revised model.

Proof of incompatibility. Let \(C=c_{\max}>0\), let \(\tau\geq 1\) be finite, and suppose the distribution is specified by costs \(c_i\in[0,C]\) and masses \(p_i\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), with \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i=1\). Its probability measure and CDF are
\( \mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\delta_{c_i} \)
and
\( F(x)=\sum_{i:c_i\leq x}p_i \).

Here \(\delta_c\) denotes the probability measure concentrated at \(c\). Repeated costs cause no difficulty: their masses add.

First, this distribution has no density with respect to Lebesgue measure. Indeed, if an integrable density \(f\) existed, then for every Borel set \(A\) we would have \(\mu(A)=\int_A f(x)\,dx\). Taking \(A=\{c_i\}\) gives
\( \mu(\{c_i\})=\sum_{j:c_j=c_i}p_j>0 \),
whereas \( \int_{\{c_i\}}f(x)\,dx=0 \), because a singleton has Lebesgue measure zero. This is a contradiction. In particular, a twice-differentiable density cannot exist.

Second, the CDF cannot be strictly concave on \([0,C]\). Strict concavity would require, for distinct \(x,y\in[0,C]\) and every \(t\in(0,1)\),
\( F(tx+(1-t)y)>tF(x)+(1-t)F(y) \).
Because the support is finite and \(C>0\), there exists a nonempty open interval inside \((0,C)\) containing no support point. Choose distinct \(x,y\) in that interval. The function \(F\) is constant between them, so
\( F(tx+(1-t)y)=F(x)=F(y)=tF(x)+(1-t)F(y) \).
This contradicts strict concavity. The argument also covers distributions whose atoms occur only at the endpoints.

Thus either smoothness requirement alone rules out the specified finite support distribution. Rational encoding and positivity beyond the existence of an atom are unnecessary for the density contradiction.

What the cited paper establishes. Section 2.2 introduces a cost distribution with a PDF and bounded support, and Assumption 2.1 requires its CDF to be strictly concave. Section 3 treats the distribution as known to the requester. Section 3.2 introduces approximations to the optimization objective, and Lemma 3.5 states concavity for PA under conditions (i) and (ii). Accordingly, that lemma concerns the relaxed objective under its stated assumptions; it does not supply a concavity theorem for a finite support replacement. [Published paper, Sections 2.2 and 3.2](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/16/Papers/532.pdf)

The smooth assumptions themselves are consistent. For example, on \([0,C]\), define
\( f(c)=\frac{3}{2C}-\frac{c}{C^2} \)
and
\( F(c)=\frac{3c}{2C}-\frac{c^2}{2C^2} \).

The density is positive, integrates to one, and is twice differentiable. Moreover,
\( F''(c)=-\frac{1}{C^2}<0 \),
so \(F\) is strictly concave. Also,
\( f''(c)=0 \)
and
\( \frac{d^2}{dc^2}\bigl(cF(c)\bigr)=\frac{3(C-c)}{C^2}\geq 0 \),
so \(cF(c)\) is convex.

If \(C\) is rational, these formulas even have a finite rational description. Consequently, finite *description* is compatible with the smooth assumptions; finite *support* is the obstruction.

A meaningful complexity question therefore requires a revised specification: either permit an encoded continuous distribution, or replace the smooth assumptions with conditions appropriate to atomic distributions. No complexity classification for either revision follows from this incompatibility proof.
