The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(K\ge2\) outcomes, \(\tau\) trader types with rational masses \(\nu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational belief parameters \(\theta_t\in\mathbb{Q}^K\), positive rational risk aversions \(a_t\), positive rational per-capita liquidity \(\beta\), a cost family \(C\) chosen from LMSR or IND, and rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a rational price vector \(\hat p\) within Euclidean distance \(\varepsilon\) of the unique minimizer \(p^\star\) of \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t}{a_t}\bigl(T^*(p)-\theta_t^\top p\bigr)+\beta C^*(p)\) over \(p\in\Delta_K\), where \(T(\theta)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\theta_k}\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=K\ge2\) outcomes. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has unrestricted natural belief parameters \(\theta_t\in\mathbb Q^K\) and risk aversion \(a_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\). The inputs also include per-capita liquidity \(\beta\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), accuracy \(\varepsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), and a choice of LMSR or IND. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including accuracy.
Use natural logarithms and \(0\log0=0\). For \(T(\theta)=\log\sum_{k=1}^K e^{\theta_k}\), its convex conjugate on \(\Delta_K\) is \(T^*(p)=\sum_k p_k\log p_k\).
On \(\Delta_K\), define \(C^*_{\mathrm{LMSR}}(p)=\sum_k p_k\log p_k\) and \(C^*_{\mathrm{IND}}(p)=\sum_k[p_k\log p_k+(1-p_k)\log(1-p_k)]\).
Let \(p^\star\) be the unique minimizer over \(\Delta_K\) of \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t}{a_t}(T^*(p)-\theta_t^\top p)+\beta C^*(p)\). The computational problem is to output \(\hat p\in\mathbb Q^K\cap\Delta_K\) with \(\|\hat p-p^\star\|_2\le\varepsilon\).

Can this problem be solved in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\) for both cost families, or is it NP-hard for at least one family?

For finitely many exponential-utility traders, equilibrium exists, prices are unique, and they minimize the corresponding sum of trader conjugates plus liquidity times the cost conjugate; local linear convergence of trading dynamics is also established. Both cost families are polynomial-time solvable

Theorem. For both LMSR and IND, there is a deterministic algorithm that outputs \(\hat p\in\mathbb Q^K\cap\Delta_K\) satisfying \(\|\hat p-p^\star\|_2\le\varepsilon\), in time polynomial in \(K,\tau,L\).

The proof below gives a direct bit-complexity bound. It does not require the trading-dynamics convergence analysis in [Dudík et al.](https://www.jennwv.com/papers/decomposition.pdf).

Exact aggregation of the types

Define the rational quantities

\(\displaystyle A=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t}{a_t},\qquad b_k=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t\theta_{t,k}}{a_t},\qquad s=A+\beta.\)

The positivity assumptions imply \(A>0\) and \(s>0\). Set \(h_k=b_k/s\), and set

\(\displaystyle r=0\quad\text{for LMSR},\qquad r=\frac{\beta}{A+\beta}\quad\text{for IND}.\)

Thus \(0\le r<1\). Dividing the objective by \(s\), which preserves its minimizer, gives the common form

\(\displaystyle f_r(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl[\phi_r(p_k)-h_kp_k\bigr],\qquad \phi_r(x)=x\log x+r(1-x)\log(1-x).\)

Endpoint values use the stipulated continuous extension.

All these coefficients can be computed exactly in polynomial time and have polynomial binary length. Indeed, they are sums of explicitly listed products and quotients of input rationals. A common denominator can be formed by multiplying the relevant denominators; its bit length is bounded by the sum of their bit lengths. The subsequent rational normalizations preserve a polynomial bound. Only the \(\tau\) listed types are processed.

The minimizer is interior and has a scalar characterization

The function \(f_r\) is continuous on the compact simplex, so a minimizer exists. The function \(x\log x\) is strictly convex on \([0,1]\), and \((1-x)\log(1-x)\) is convex there. Consequently, \(f_r\) is strictly convex and its minimizer is unique.

We next show that every coordinate of the minimizer is positive. Suppose \(p_i=0\), and choose \(j\) with \(p_j>0\). For small \(u>0\), transfer mass \(u\) from coordinate \(j\) to coordinate \(i\).

The new coordinate contributes

\(\displaystyle \frac{\phi_r(u)-\phi_r(0)}{u}=\log u-r+o(1).\)

If \(0<p_j<1\), the donor coordinate contributes a finite limit, because \(\phi_r\) is differentiable at \(p_j\). If \(p_j=1\), its contribution is

\(\displaystyle \frac{\phi_r(1-u)-\phi_r(1)}{u}=r\log u-1+o(1).\)

The linear part contributes the finite quantity \(h_j-h_i\). In either case, the total difference quotient tends to negative infinity. Hence the transfer decreases the objective for sufficiently small \(u\), contradicting minimality.

Therefore \(p^\star_k>0\) for every \(k\). Since \(K\ge2\), we also have \(p^\star_k<1\).

On \((0,1)\), define

\(\displaystyle g_r(x)=\log x-r\log(1-x).\)

Since \(\phi_r'(x)=g_r(x)+1-r\), stationarity in every direction \(e_i-e_j\), where \(e_i\) is the \(i\)-th coordinate unit vector, implies that there is a real scalar \(\lambda^\star\) such that

\(\displaystyle g_r(p^\star_k)=h_k-\lambda^\star\qquad\text{for every }k.\)

For \(r>0\), the function \(g_r\) is strictly increasing from negative infinity to positive infinity. Define its inverse by \(q_r(z)=g_r^{-1}(z)\).

For \(r=0\), define the continuous extension

\(\displaystyle q_0(z)=\begin{cases}e^z,&z<0,\\1,&z\ge0.\end{cases}\)

Because \(p^\star_k<1\), the stationarity equations give, in both families,

\(\displaystyle p^\star_k=q_r(h_k-\lambda^\star).\)

Thus it remains to solve the scalar equation

\(\displaystyle S(\lambda)=1,\qquad S(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_r(h_k-\lambda).\)

A rational bracket and a stable error certificate

Each \(q_r\) is continuous and nondecreasing. It is also globally Lipschitz with constant \(1\).

For \(r>0\), this follows from

\(\displaystyle g_r'(x)=\frac1x+\frac{r}{1-x}\ge1\)

and the mean value theorem applied between two inverse images. For \(r=0\), the derivative of \(e^z\) is at most \(1\) on \(z<0\), and \(q_0\) is constant on \(z>0\). Across the joining point, \(1-e^z\le-z\) for \(z\le0\), which proves the same bound.

Let \(h_{\min}=\min_k h_k\) and \(h_{\max}=\max_k h_k\). A rational bracket for the root is

\(\displaystyle \ell=h_{\min},\qquad u=h_{\max}+K.\)

To verify the lower endpoint, observe that

\(\displaystyle g_r(1/2)=-(1-r)\log2\le0.\)

Therefore \(q_r(0)\ge1/2\), including the separately defined case \(r=0\). Since \(h_k-\ell\ge0\),

\(\displaystyle S(\ell)\ge K/2\ge1.\)

For the upper endpoint,

\(\displaystyle g_r(1/K)=-\log K-r\log(1-1/K)\ge-\log K>-K.\)

Here \(\log K=\int_1^K t^{-1}\,dt\le K-1<K\). Since \(h_k-u\le-K\), it follows that \(q_r(h_k-u)<1/K\), and hence \(S(u)<1\).

Continuity gives a root in this bracket. For \(r>0\), \(S\) is strictly decreasing, so the root is unique. For \(r=0\), any solution of \(S(\lambda)=1\) must have every summand strictly below \(1\): one summand equal to \(1\), together with the other positive summands, would make the sum exceed \(1\). Therefore any root satisfies \(\lambda>h_{\max}\), where \(S\) is strictly decreasing. The root is again unique.

The following identity is the essential error certificate. Write

\(\displaystyle q(\lambda)=\bigl(q_r(h_1-\lambda),\ldots,q_r(h_K-\lambda)\bigr).\)

Then

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\ \|q(\lambda)-p^\star\|_1=|S(\lambda)-1|\ }.\)

Indeed, if \(\lambda\le\lambda^\star\), every coordinate of \(q(\lambda)\) is at least the corresponding coordinate of \(p^\star\). If \(\lambda\ge\lambda^\star\), every coordinate is at most its counterpart. In either case, summing the absolute differences gives exactly the displayed identity.

This permits termination based on a sum residual even when recovering the multiplier itself would be numerically ill-conditioned.

Certified evaluation of the scalar inverse

We now give a rational-arithmetic subroutine that, for rational \(r,z\) and \(0<\delta<1\), returns a dyadic rational \(\tilde q\in[0,1]\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |\tilde q-q_r(z)|\le\delta,\)

in time polynomial in the input bit lengths and \(\log(1/\delta)\).

If \(r=0\) and \(z\ge0\), return \(1\). Otherwise, \(q_r(z)\) is the unique solution of \(g_r(x)=z\) in \((0,1)\).

Bisect the interval \([0,1]\). At each midpoint \(x\), compute a rational approximation \(\tilde g\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |\tilde g-g_r(x)|\le\delta/4.\)

Use the following certified comparisons:

  • If \(\tilde g<z-\delta/4\), then \(g_r(x)<z\), so replace the lower endpoint by \(x\).
  • If \(\tilde g>z+\delta/4\), then \(g_r(x)>z\), so replace the upper endpoint by \(x\).
  • Otherwise, \(|g_r(x)-z|\le\delta/2\). Since \(g_r'\ge1\), the mean value theorem gives \(|x-q_r(z)|\le\delta/2\); return \(x\).

If no earlier return occurs, stop once the interval width is at most \(\delta\), and return its midpoint. All updates preserve the root bracket, so this return also has error at most \(\delta\).

Let \(M\) be the smallest integer such that \(2^{-M}\le\delta\). There are at most \(M\) bisections, and all trial points and returned values have dyadic denominator at most \(2^{M+1}\).

It remains to justify the logarithm evaluations used in this subroutine.

Polynomial-time logarithms of the trial points

For a positive dyadic \(x<1\) with denominator at most \(2^D\), write

\(\displaystyle x=2^e v,\qquad 1\le v<2,\qquad -D\le e\le0.\)

The integer \(e\) can be found from the binary representation. Then \(\log x=e\log2+\log v\).

For any rational \(v\in[1,2]\), put \(w=(v-1)/(v+1)\), so \(0\le w\le1/3\). Integrating the uniformly convergent geometric series for \(2/(1-w^2)\) gives

\(\displaystyle \log v=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1}.\)

Let \(R_n(v)\) denote the sum of the first \(n\) terms. Its tail satisfies

\(\displaystyle 0\le\log v-R_n(v)\le2\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}(1/3)^{2j+1}=\frac34\,9^{-n}.\)

Thus \(eR_n(2)+R_n(v)\) approximates \(\log x\) with absolute error at most \((D+1)9^{-n}\). To obtain error at most \(\eta\), it suffices to choose

\(\displaystyle n=O\bigl(\log(D+1)+\log(1/\eta)\bigr).\)

These partial sums are exact rationals computable in polynomial time: their powers and the products of their denominators have bit lengths polynomial in \(D,n\).

Apply this construction to both \(x\) and \(1-x\), with error at most \(\delta/8\) for each logarithm. Because \(0\le r<1\), their combination approximates \(g_r(x)\) with error at most \(\delta/4\), as required.

Consequently, scalar inverse evaluation is certified and polynomial-time. It never needs to represent an exponentially tiny inverse value accurately in relative error.

The outer algorithm and its accuracy

Set

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon_0=\min\{\varepsilon,1\},\qquad \delta=\frac{\varepsilon_0}{8K}.\)

Start with the rational bracket \([\ell,u]\) above. At each midpoint \(\lambda\), use the scalar subroutine to obtain dyadic values \(\tilde q_k\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle |\tilde q_k-q_r(h_k-\lambda)|\le\delta.\)

Compute the rational sum \(\tilde S=\sum_k\tilde q_k\) exactly. Since \(|\tilde S-S(\lambda)|\le K\delta\), use these comparisons:

  • If \(\tilde S>1+K\delta\), then \(S(\lambda)>1\), hence \(\lambda<\lambda^\star\). Replace \(\ell\) by \(\lambda\).
  • If \(\tilde S<1-K\delta\), then \(S(\lambda)<1\), hence \(\lambda>\lambda^\star\). Replace \(u\) by \(\lambda\).
  • Otherwise, return \(\hat p_k=\tilde q_k/\tilde S\).

If the outer interval reaches width at most \(\delta\), evaluate once more at its midpoint and return the same normalized vector.

We verify both termination cases.

Termination by the sum test

Here \(|\tilde S-1|\le K\delta\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle |S(\lambda)-1|\le2K\delta.\)

The stability identity gives

\(\displaystyle \|\tilde q-p^\star\|_1\le\|\tilde q-q(\lambda)\|_1+\|q(\lambda)-p^\star\|_1\le3K\delta.\)

Moreover, \(\tilde S\ge1-K\delta>0\). Because the coordinates of \(\tilde q\) are nonnegative,

\(\displaystyle \left\|\frac{\tilde q}{\tilde S}-\tilde q\right\|_1=|1-\tilde S|\le K\delta.\)

Thus

\(\displaystyle \|\hat p-p^\star\|_1\le4K\delta.\)

Termination by interval width

The maintained interval contains \(\lambda^\star\). At its midpoint, \(|\lambda-\lambda^\star|\le\delta\). The Lipschitz bound for each coordinate gives

\(\displaystyle \|q(\lambda)-p^\star\|_1\le K\delta.\)

Including the scalar evaluation errors yields \(\|\tilde q-p^\star\|_1\le2K\delta\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle |\tilde S-1|\le2K\delta,\qquad \tilde S\ge1-2K\delta>0.\)

Normalization changes the vector by at most another \(2K\delta\) in the same norm, so again

\(\displaystyle \|\hat p-p^\star\|_1\le4K\delta.\)

In both cases, normalization produces a rational vector in the simplex, and

\(\displaystyle \|\hat p-p^\star\|_2\le\|\hat p-p^\star\|_1\le4K\delta=\varepsilon_0/2\le\varepsilon.\)

Bit complexity

The initial interval width is the rational number

\(\displaystyle W=h_{\max}-h_{\min}+K,\)

whose binary length is polynomial in the input length. Let \(N\) be the smallest integer such that \(2^{-N}W\le\delta\). This integer can be found using exact rational comparisons, and

\(\displaystyle N=O\bigl(\operatorname{bitlength}(W)+\log(1/\delta)\bigr).\)

After at most \(N\) outer bisections, the interval-width termination condition holds. Outer midpoints have polynomial bit length because each bisection adds at most one binary digit to their common-denominator representation.

Each outer step invokes \(K\) scalar inverse evaluations. Each scalar evaluation uses at most

\(\displaystyle M=O\bigl(\log K+\log(1/\varepsilon_0)\bigr)\)

inner bisections, with polynomial-time rational logarithm approximations. The rational encoding of the accuracy implies \(\log(1/\varepsilon_0)=O(L)\). Hence the number and bit complexity of all operations are polynomial in \(K,\tau,L\).

Finally, every returned \(\tilde q_k\) has a common dyadic denominator dividing \(2^{M+1}\). After normalization, each output coordinate is a ratio of integers with bit length \(O(M+\log K)\). The output itself therefore has polynomial length.

This proves the theorem for both cost families, with unrestricted rational belief parameters and without any lower bound on the optimal coordinates.
