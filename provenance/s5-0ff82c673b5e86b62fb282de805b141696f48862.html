The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite task set \(\mathcal J\), service set \(\mathcal S\), robot types \(\mathcal R\subseteq 2^{\mathcal S}\), rational type masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one, and rational service requirements \(q_{js}>0\), compute a feasible allocation or report that none exists. An allocation has masses \(x_{rjs}\ge 0\) assigned from type \(r\) to task \(j\) for service \(s\), and unassigned masses \(x_{r\emptyset}\), with \(\sum_{j,s}x_{rjs}+x_{r\emptyset}=\mu_r\), \(x_{rjs}=0\) when \(s\notin r\), and \(z_{js}=\sum_r x_{rjs}\le q_{js}\). Each assigned unit’s reward is \(\rho_{js}(x)=\exp(1-z_{js}/q_{js})\), and the unassigned role has reward zero. Stability means there is no feasible deviation in which a positive mass of one type changes roles, or equal positive masses of two types exchange roles, and every deviating unit strictly improves its reward after the change.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(\mathcal J\) and \(\mathcal S\) be nonempty finite task and service sets, with \(m=|\mathcal J|\) and \(p=|\mathcal S|\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) distinct nonempty types \(r\subseteq\mathcal S\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_r\), where \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Requirements \(q_{js}>0\) are binary rationals; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of nonnegative masses \(x_{rjs}\) and \(x_{r\emptyset}\), satisfying \(\sum_{j,s}x_{rjs}+x_{r\emptyset}=\mu_r\), \(x_{rjs}=0\) if \(s\notin r\), and \(z_{js}=\sum_r x_{rjs}\le q_{js}\). A role is a task-service pair or the unassigned role \(\emptyset\).
The reward in role \((j,s)\) is \(\rho_{js}(x)=\exp(1-z_{js}/q_{js})\); the unassigned reward is zero. All units have these same role rewards.
A deviation moves a batch of real mass \(\delta>0\) of one type from one role to another, or simultaneously moves two disjoint batches, each of mass \(\delta\), from their respective roles to arbitrary new roles. The two batches may have the same type; destinations need not exchange the source roles. All other units retain their roles, and the resulting allocation must satisfy every feasibility constraint.
An allocation is stable if no such deviation gives every deviating unit a strictly higher reward at its destination after the entire deviation than at its source before it. Equal rewards do not constitute improvement.
The computational problem is to output an exact stable allocation encoded by binary-rational masses, or report that none exists.

Is this stable-allocation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,p,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite robot populations, Pair-GRAPE-S is proved to produce a pairwise stable allocation under the same normalized exponential reward; its stated computation bound is \(O(n^2pm d_G)\), where \(n\) is the robot count and \(d_G\) the communication-network diameter ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12480)).

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • The graded question requested only feasibility despite defining stability; the output is required to be both feasible and stable, matching the anchor.
  • Exchange-only deviations omit the discrete algorithm's replacement-and-relocation move. Two equal-mass batches may therefore move simultaneously to arbitrary new roles.
  • Two discrete deviators need not have different capabilities; two disjoint batches of the same type are allowed.
  • Deviation sizes range over positive real masses, with rewards compared before and after the complete simultaneous move.
  • Tasks, services, and robot capability sets are taken to be nonempty; the positive requirements and unit-total mass normalization are retained.
  • Exact output is encoded by binary-rational allocation masses to specify the computational representation.
  • No promise that all requirements can be met, and no equality between total requirements and total mass, is imposed: those restrictions belong to the experimental instances rather than the anchor's theorem.

Result

The problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,p,\tau,L\). Under the usual encoding of rationals by binary numerators and denominators, an exact rational stable allocation always exists.

The key fact is that, in this continuous model, stability against single-batch deviations already implies stability against the permitted two-batch deviations.

1. Characterizing single-batch stability

Write \(\mathcal A=\mathcal J\times\mathcal S\) for the assigned roles. For \(a=(j,s)\), set \(q_a=q_{js}\), \(z_a=z_{js}\), and

\(h_a=z_a/q_a\).

Feasibility gives \(0\le h_a\le 1\). Let \(\mathcal A_r=\{(j,s):s\in r\}\) be the roles accessible to type \(r\). Write \(x_{ra}\) for its assigned masses and \(u_r=x_{r\emptyset}\) for its unassigned mass.

Because the exponential is strictly increasing, moving from an assigned role \(a\) to an assigned role \(b\) strictly improves reward precisely when the destination’s load after the deviation is strictly less than \(h_a\).

Consider these conditions:

  • Assigned-role condition: If \(x_{ra}>0\), then \(h_a\le h_b\) for every \(b\in\mathcal A_r\).
  • Unassigned-role condition: If \(u_r>0\), then \(h_b=1\) for every \(b\in\mathcal A_r\).

Lemma 1. A feasible allocation admits no strictly improving single-batch deviation if and only if these two conditions hold.

Proof. Suppose the assigned-role condition fails, so \(x_{ra}>0\) and \(h_b<h_a\) for an accessible role \(b\). Necessarily \(b\ne a\). Choose

\(0<\delta<\min\{x_{ra},\,q_b(h_a-h_b)\}\).

Moving this mass from \(a\) to \(b\) gives destination load \(h_b+\delta/q_b<h_a\le 1\). The move is feasible and strictly improves every deviating unit’s reward.

If the unassigned-role condition fails, there are \(u_r>0\) and an accessible role \(b\) with \(z_b<q_b\). Moving any mass satisfying

\(0<\delta<\min\{u_r,\,q_b-z_b\}\)

from \(\emptyset\) to \(b\) is feasible and improves reward from zero to a positive number.

Conversely, assume both conditions. A single move from an assigned role \(a\) to a distinct assigned role \(b\) produces destination load

\(h'_b=h_b+\delta/q_b\ge h_b\ge h_a\),

so it cannot strictly improve reward. A move from an assigned role to \(\emptyset\) cannot improve reward either. Finally, an unassigned batch cannot enter any accessible assigned role because all such roles are full. This exhausts the possible single-batch improvements. ∎

2. These conditions also exclude two-batch improvements

Lemma 2. Every feasible allocation satisfying the two conditions of Lemma 1 is stable under the full deviation rule.

Proof. Single-batch deviations are already excluded. Suppose, for contradiction, that a feasible two-batch deviation strictly improves both batches. Both destinations must be assigned roles, because reward at \(\emptyset\) is zero.

For each assigned role \(b\), let \(I_b\) be the number of batches whose destination is \(b\), and \(O_b\) the number whose source is \(b\). Since both batches have mass \(\delta\),

\(z'_b-z_b=\delta(I_b-O_b)\).

There are three cases.

Case 1: Both source roles are assigned. For each batch, denote its source by \(a\) and destination by \(b\). The assigned-role condition gives \(h_a\le h_b\). Strict improvement therefore requires

\(h'_b<h_a\le h_b\),

hence \(z'_b<z_b\) and \(O_b>I_b\).

If the two destinations coincide at \(b\), then \(I_b=2\) and \(O_b\le 2\), contradicting \(O_b>I_b\).

If the destinations are distinct, each has \(I_b=1\), so each would require \(O_b\ge 2\). That would require at least four source batches, whereas there are only two.

Case 2: Exactly one source is unassigned. Let \(b\) be the destination of the unassigned batch. The unassigned-role condition gives \(z_b=q_b\). Feasibility therefore requires \(I_b\le O_b\).

Here \(I_b\ge 1\), while \(O_b\le 1\), because only the other batch has an assigned source. Consequently \(I_b=O_b=1\): the other batch must leave \(b\), and its destination must be a different assigned role \(c\).

For that other batch’s type, the assigned-role condition gives

\(h_c\ge h_b=1\).

Thus \(c\) is also full. Neither source is \(c\), so the deviation increases its mass by \(\delta\), violating its capacity.

Case 3: Both sources are unassigned. Each destination is initially full by the unassigned-role condition for the entering batch’s type. No assigned role loses any mass. Hence entering either destination violates its capacity.

Every case is impossible. The argument applies equally when the two batches have the same type. ∎

Thus full stability is exactly characterized by the two conditions in Lemma 1.

3. A convex quadratic program produces these conditions

Over the original feasible allocation polytope, minimize

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\frac{z_a^2}{2q_a}+\sum_r u_r.\)

This polytope is nonempty: assigning every type’s entire mass to \(\emptyset\) is feasible. It is compact because every variable is nonnegative and bounded by its type’s mass. The continuous objective therefore attains a minimum.

Lemma 3. Every minimizer of \(\Phi\) satisfies both conditions of Lemma 1.

Proof. If the assigned-role condition fails at \(x_{ra}>0\), with \(h_b<h_a\), move a sufficiently small mass \(t>0\) from \(a\) to \(b\). The move is feasible, and direct expansion gives

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x(t))-\Phi(x)=t(h_b-h_a)+\frac{t^2}{2}\left(\frac1{q_a}+\frac1{q_b}\right).\)

The coefficient of \(t\) is negative. Thus the difference is negative for sufficiently small positive \(t\), contradicting minimality.

If the unassigned-role condition fails at \(u_r>0\), with \(h_b<1\), move sufficiently small mass \(t>0\) from \(\emptyset\) to \(b\). Now

\(\displaystyle \Phi(x(t))-\Phi(x)=t(h_b-1)+\frac{t^2}{2q_b}.\)

Again, feasibility holds for sufficiently small \(t\), and the difference is negative, contradicting minimality. ∎

By Lemma 2, every minimizer is stable. The linear term \(\sum_r u_r\) is an auxiliary optimization cost; the unassigned reward remains zero.

For later use, the converse also holds. Define \(c_{ra}=h_a\) for assigned roles and \(c_{r\emptyset}=1\). Under the two conditions, every role receiving positive mass from type \(r\) minimizes \(c_{r\cdot}\) among that type’s accessible roles and \(\emptyset\). Denote this minimum by \(\kappa_r\).

For any feasible allocation \(y\), with role totals \(w_a\), expansion yields

\(\displaystyle \Phi(y)-\Phi(x)=\sum_r\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_r\cup\{\emptyset\}}c_{ra}(y_{ra}-x_{ra})+\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\frac{(w_a-z_a)^2}{2q_a}.\)

The first term equals

\(\displaystyle \sum_r\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_r\cup\{\emptyset\}}(c_{ra}-\kappa_r)y_{ra}\ge 0,\)

using type-mass conservation and the fact that positive entries of \(x\) have cost \(\kappa_r\). The squared term is also nonnegative. Hence \(x\) minimizes \(\Phi\).

Consequently, stable allocations are exactly the minimizers of \(\Phi\). The same identity shows that all stable allocations have identical totals \(z_a\): if \(x\) and \(y\) both minimize \(\Phi\), every squared term must vanish.

4. Exact polynomial-time computation

The quadratic program is a continuous minimum-cost flow problem with separable convex quadratic costs.

Construct a directed network with one node for each supported type, one node for each assigned role, and a sink \(t\). Type node \(r\) supplies \(\mu_r\); role nodes have zero balance; the sink demands \(1\).

Use these arcs:

| Arc | Capacity | Cost for flow \(f\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(r\to a\), for \(a\in\mathcal A_r\) | \(1\) | \(0\) |
| \(r\to t\) | \(1\) | \(f\) |
| \(a\to t\) | \(q_a\) | \(f^2/(2q_a)\) |

Flow conservation at a type node gives precisely its mass-conservation constraint. Conservation at a role node makes its outgoing flow equal to \(z_a\); its outgoing capacity enforces \(z_a\le q_a\). Thus feasible flows correspond exactly to feasible allocations, and their objective is \(\Phi\).

The network has

\(\displaystyle N=\tau+mp+1,\qquad E=m\sum_r|r|+\tau+mp.\)

In particular, \(E\le \tau mp+\tau+mp\). Only the \(\tau\) explicitly supported types are represented.

The algorithmic theorem used here is Végh’s Theorem 20: continuous minimum-cost flows with separable convex quadratic costs, including linear costs and finite capacities, can be solved exactly in strongly polynomially many arithmetic operations. For rational input, the proof also establishes that all computed numbers are rational with polynomially bounded encoding length. [Végh, *A Strongly Polynomial Algorithm for a Class of Minimum-Cost Flow Problems with Separable Convex Objectives*, Theorem 20 and the following bit-length analysis](https://www.laszlovegh.eu/papers/vegh-quadratic.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here:

  • Each arc cost is \(cf^2+df\) with rational \(c\ge 0\) and rational \(d\).
  • Supplies and capacities are rational.
  • The instance is feasible.
  • The feasible flow set is bounded.
  • Forming the coefficients \(1/(2q_a)\) increases encoding length only polynomially.

Therefore the algorithm returns an exact rational minimizer, in polynomial bit time in \(m,p,\tau,L\). Reading its flows gives the required stable allocation by Lemmas 2 and 3. No exponential evaluations or expansion into individual robots are needed.

5. If “binary-rational” means dyadic fractions

If the phrase instead requires finite binary expansions, the polynomial-time conclusion still holds, but existence is no longer automatic.

For example, with one task, two services, a single type supporting both, mass \(1\), and requirements \(q_1=1\), \(q_2=2\), stability forces no unassigned mass and equal normalized loads. Hence the unique role totals are \(1/3\) and \(2/3\), which are not dyadic.

Under this alternative interpretation, compute a rational optimum as above. A dyadic stable allocation exists if and only if every \(\mu_r\) and every optimal role total \(z_a\) is dyadic.

Necessity follows because each is a sum of output masses, and all stable allocations have the same role totals.

For sufficiency, use a transportation network with type supplies \(\mu_r\), assigned-role demands \(z_a\), and one unassigned-role demand \(1-\sum_a z_a\). Include precisely the accessible type-to-role arcs and all type-to-unassigned arcs. It is feasible because the computed optimum supplies such a flow. Scale all supplies and demands by a common power of two. They become integers, so integral-flow existence gives an integral feasible flow. Scaling back yields dyadic allocation masses. Its totals are optimal, hence its value of \(\Phi\) is optimal, so it is stable.

Testing reduced denominators for being powers of two and performing this flow computation take polynomial time; the necessary scaling exponent is polynomially bounded by the computed rationals’ encoding lengths.
