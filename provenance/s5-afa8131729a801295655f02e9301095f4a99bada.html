The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite type sets \(H,S,C\), rational masses \(\nu,\mu^S,\mu^C\), complete strict preferences for single and couple types, type-symmetric responsive hospital priorities with capacities, and fixed processing orders, run a mass-batched sequential-couples algorithm: deferred acceptance on single mass, followed by preferred-pair proposals by couple mass with capacity-respecting transfers of displaced mass and reprocessing of singles. Decide whether it succeeds without an aggregate collision; on success output the mass matching and certify that no positive-mass single/hospital or couple/pair block exists, while a collision is reported only as algorithmic failure.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Definitions. An instance has finite hospital, single, and couple type sets \(H,S,C\), rational hospital capacities \(\nu_h\), and rational masses \(\mu^S_s,\mu^C_c\). Preferences are strict over hospitals or ordered hospital pairs (including the outside option); each hospital has a strict, type-symmetric responsive priority order. It retains offered mass up to capacity in priority order, splitting mass only at the cutoff.

Run deferred acceptance on single mass. Then process couple types in the given order: unassigned couple mass proposes to its best untried hospital pair; accepted mass displaces lower-priority single mass, and rejected residual continues. Afterwards, reprocess displaced single mass in the given order. An aggregate collision occurs when a couple type proposes to a hospital previously proposed to by a different couple type; this is algorithmic failure. A successful output is a capacity-feasible mass matching with no positive-mass single--hospital or couple--hospital-pair block, where every proposed hospital can profitably accept the incoming mass by vacancy or displacement.

Target.

Is deciding whether this mass-batched sequential-couples algorithm succeeds, and producing its successful mass matching, solvable in \(P\) or \(NP\)-hard?

Known results. For finite individual agents, success of the sequential couples algorithm implies that its matching is stable. Separately, deciding whether a stable matching exists in the finite intern-assignment problem with couples is \(NP\)-complete. Neither result analyzes divisible type masses or the mass-batched algorithm above.

  • The initial singles-only deferred-acceptance outcome is computable in polynomial time.
  • Given that outcome, the entire couple-processing phase, including collision detection, is computable in polynomial time.

1. Encoding and conventions

Assume preference and priority lists are explicitly represented, and all capacities and masses are nonnegative rationals encoded in binary. Let \(L\) be the total input length. The outside option consumes no hospital capacity.

Distinguish the two member roles of each couple type when their hospital priorities differ. A *priority category* means a single type or a couple-member category appearing in a hospital’s strict priority order.

For the couple-processing phase, I interpret “accepted mass displaces lower-priority single mass” literally: a proposal may remove single mass, but must preserve previously accepted couple mass. Acceptance must agree with the hospital’s responsive choice from the offered mass.

For stability verification, I use the following withdrawal convention: deviating couple mass leaves its previous pair before its proposed pair is tested. Hospitals then apply responsive choice to remaining incumbents and incoming members. Replacing incumbent mass by incoming mass of the same priority category does not constitute a profitable displacement.

These conventions matter when a couple revisits a hospital or both members propose to the same hospital.

2. The initial singles outcome is computable in polynomial time

Proposition 1. The aggregate outcome of the initial singles-only deferred-acceptance phase is computable in time polynomial in \(L\).

Proof. Construct a bipartite stable-allocation instance:

  • Each single type \(s\) is a proposing vertex with quota \(\mu^S_s\).
  • Each hospital \(h\) is a receiving vertex with quota \(\nu_h\).
  • Each mutually acceptable single–hospital pair is an edge.
  • The edge capacity is \(\min\{\mu^S_s,\nu_h\}\).
  • Vertex preferences are the given single preferences and hospital priorities.

The edge capacities impose no additional restriction: every feasible allocation already satisfies those bounds. Unallocated mass represents the outside option; equivalently, dummy vertices can represent it.

The stable-allocation theorem needed here states that, with strict vertex preferences, batched proposing-side deferred acceptance has a unique proposing-side-optimal endpoint, independently of proposal order, and that endpoint is computable by a strongly polynomial algorithm. Dean and Munshi establish these properties and also explain why literal batched deferred acceptance need not itself have a polynomial number of iterations. [Faster Algorithms for Stable Allocation Problems](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~bcdean/stable_alloc.pdf)

The constructed instance is bipartite, has strict preferences, and has nonnegative rational vertex and edge capacities, so the theorem applies. Its responsive receiving rule is exactly the hospital rule in the singles-only phase. Thus its proposing-side-optimal allocation is the required aggregate outcome.

For binary complexity, multiply the input quantities by the product \(D\) of their denominators. The binary length of \(D\) is at most the sum of those denominator lengths. The stable-allocation algorithm uses polynomially many arithmetic operations on quantities with polynomial encoding length; scaling back gives the required rational outcome. \(\square\)

This proposition concerns computing the endpoint. It does not justify counting every type–hospital pair as a single literal proposal event.

3. Couple processing and collision detection are polynomial

Write \(P_c\) for the explicitly listed pairs of couple type \(c\), and let \(K=\sum_{c\in C}|P_c|\).

Proposition 2. Given the initial singles allocation, the couple-processing phase can be computed, or its first aggregate collision detected, in polynomial time.

Proof.

Number of pair proposals

A couple type proposes only to an untried pair. Consequently, there are at most \(K\) pair proposals.

Maintain, for each hospital, the identity of the first couple type that proposed there. A proposal involving a hospital marked by another type immediately detects a collision. Repeated proposals by the same type do not cause a collision.

Computing one acceptance quantity

Fix a proposal with residual couple mass \(r\). For a hospital \(h\) and priority category \(t\), let:

  • \(B_{h,\succeq t}\) be current incumbent mass at priorities at least as high as \(t\);
  • \(k_{h,\succeq t}\) be the number of members of the proposed pair assigned to \(h\) whose priorities are at least as high as \(t\).

Thus \(k_{h,\succeq t}\in\{0,1,2\}\).

Consider accepting couple mass \(a\). All offered mass of category \(t\) is retained precisely when

\(B_{h,\succeq t}+k_{h,\succeq t}a\leq \nu_h.\)

Indeed, responsive choice processes the entire priority prefix through \(t\) before considering any lower category.

Impose this inequality for:

  • every category of an incoming member at \(h\); and
  • every category with positive incumbent couple-member mass at \(h\).

The first group ensures that both incoming members are retained. The second ensures that previously accepted couple mass is retained. Hence any rejected incumbent mass consists only of singles, as required.

Together with \(0\leq a\leq r\), these conditions characterize admissible acceptance quantities. The maximum is the minimum of \(r\) and the applicable bounds

\(a\leq (\nu_h-B_{h,\succeq t})/k_{h,\succeq t}\)

with positive denominator. Zero-denominator constraints already hold because the current assignment is feasible.

This calculation also handles a pair assigning both members to the same hospital. Once \(a\) is known, a priority scan computes retained and displaced single masses.

Bit complexity

Let \(D\) be a common denominator for the initial allocation and input quantities, of polynomial binary length. Each proposal uses additions, subtractions, comparisons, and division by at most two. After at most \(K\) proposals, every quantity has a denominator dividing \(D2^K\).

All quantities are bounded by sums of input masses and capacities. Their numerators therefore also have polynomial binary length. There are polynomially many scans and arithmetic operations on polynomial-length numbers. \(\square\)

In particular, whether an aggregate collision occurs during couple processing is decidable in polynomial time. This does not yet decide overall success, which additionally requires the final matching and its stability.

4. Stability of a supplied matching is polynomially checkable

Let a proposed matching be represented by rational quantities \(x_{s,h}\) and \(y_{c,q}\), including outside assignments. Capacity feasibility and mass conservation are checked by rational summation.

Proposition 3. Under the withdrawal convention in Section 1, positive-mass single and couple blocks can be detected in polynomial time in the input and matching encoding lengths.

Single blocks

For an acceptable single–hospital pair \((s,h)\), define \(W_{s,h}\) to be the mass of type \(s\) assigned to options strictly worse than \(h\).

Let \(v_h\) be the vacancy at \(h\), and let \(B_{h,\prec s}\) be its incumbent mass strictly below \(s\) in priority.

There is a positive-mass block exactly when

\(W_{s,h}>0\quad\text{and}\quad v_h+B_{h,\prec s}>0.\)

Necessity follows from the single’s strict preference and the hospital’s admission rule. For sufficiency, choose a positive amount no greater than both displayed available quantities; the hospital accepts it using vacancy and then lower-priority displacement.

Couple blocks

Fix a couple type \(c\) and a proposed destination pair \(p\). For each current pair \(q\) with \(p\succ_c q\), introduce a variable \(z_q\), representing mass moving from \(q\) to \(p\). Require

\(0\leq z_q\leq y_{c,q},\qquad \delta=\sum_{q:\,p\succ_c q}z_q.\)

For hospital \(h\) and category \(t\), let \(b_{h,t}\) be its current incumbent mass. Let \(m_{h,t}(q)\) count members of a type-\(c\) couple who occupy category \(t\) at \(h\) under pair \(q\).

After withdrawing the deviators and offering them at \(p\), the offered mass is

\(o_{h,t}=b_{h,t}-\sum_{q:\,p\succ_c q}m_{h,t}(q)z_q+m_{h,t}(p)\delta.\)

For every category \(t\) of an incoming member at \(h\), impose

\(\sum_{u\succeq_h t}o_{h,u}\leq \nu_h.\)

Maximize \(\delta\) subject to these constraints.

This is a rational linear program of polynomial size.

Necessity. Any block supplies feasible withdrawal quantities \(z_q\). Every incoming category must fit together with the offered categories above it and existing mass of its own category; otherwise accepting all incoming mass would require rejecting higher-priority mass or replacing equal-priority mass. Thus every prefix inequality holds.

Sufficiency. A feasible solution with \(\delta>0\) selects positive couple mass that strictly prefers \(p\). Every relevant priority prefix fits, so responsive choice retains all incoming members. Any additional rejected incumbents lie strictly below an incoming category whose admission requires their displacement. The selected deviation is therefore a block.

If a block is initially described using several source assignments, the variables already capture it. Conversely, each feasible vector selects legitimate submass from those assignments because \(z_q\leq y_{c,q}\).

The optimum is positive exactly when this destination pair blocks. Polynomial-time rational linear programming, applied to every listed destination pair and couple type, completes the test. \(\square\)

This verifies a supplied matching; it does not certify that the specified algorithm produces that matching.

5. A resolved subclass and the remaining gap

The preceding propositions completely solve the subclass of runs in which couple processing displaces no single mass:

  • Compute the initial singles allocation.
  • Execute the couple phase and reject on collision.
  • Since no singles require reprocessing, check the resulting matching for feasibility and stability.
  • Return it precisely when those checks pass.

This subclass permits arbitrary couple preferences, partial acceptance, repeated hospitals within a pair, and repeated hospital proposals by the same couple type.

For the general case, the missing step is a polynomial method that computes the prescribed singles-reprocessing outcome, or determines its failure, while preserving the treatment of already accepted couple mass. The singles-only theorem cannot simply be applied without establishing that equivalence. In particular, if a reprocessed single outranks an incumbent couple member, the handling of that couple’s two linked assignments must be accounted for.

The finite-agent results in the question do not close this gap. Success implying stability supplies neither an efficient mass simulation nor a converse. Hardness of stable-matching existence supplies no reduction to success of this particular ordered algorithm.
