The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses for supported patient-donor types and altruist-donor types, together with compatibility data and an exchange-length limit of three, choose feasible exchange masses to maximize total transplanted mass; among those maximizing allocations, maximize the minimum patient-type transplant rate.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(P\neq\varnothing\) be the supported incompatible patient-donor types and \(N\) the supported altruist-donor types; write \(m=|P|\). A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau=|P|+|N|\) types with positive mass \(a_i\), each a binary rational, with \(\sum_i a_i=1\). Thus \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Compatibility is an explicitly given Boolean relation from donor types to patient types, uniform within each type, with no compatibility from a patient-donor type to itself.
An exchange is either a directed cycle containing two or three patient-donor occurrences, or a directed chain containing one altruist followed by one or two patient-donor occurrences. Every successive donation must be compatible, including the closing donation of a cycle. The terminal chain donor leaves the current allocation.
For each exchange \(c\), choose a real mass \(x_c\geq0\). If \(n_i(c)\) counts its occurrences of type \(i\), feasibility means \(\sum_c n_i(c)x_c\leq a_i\) for every supported type; mass may be divided among exchanges.
For \(i\in P\), define transplanted mass \(y_i=\sum_c n_i(c)x_c\) and transplant rate \(r_i=y_i/a_i\). Lexicographically maximize \(\bigl(\sum_{i\in P}y_i,\min_{i\in P}r_i\bigr)\), including patient types with zero attainable rate.

Is this exact lexicographic optimization problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite societies, retaining one optimal exchange plan for each distinct set of transplanted patients preserves the optimal maxmin and mean-deviation fairness values; maxmin is computable by an LP once these plans are listed. [Farnadi et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/17369)
Recent work also studies fairness through lotteries over finite exchange packings. No result settling the mass-allocation problem defined here was located. [Chang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.20070v1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize the combined patient-donor and altruist masses to one, since normalization was unspecified.
  • Define compatibility as Boolean and uniform within each supported type, excluding own-type compatibility to retain incompatible patient-donor pairs.
  • Count chain length by participating vertices, including the altruist, following the explicit example: a length-three chain contains two patients.
  • Count only transplanted patients in the primary objective, resolving the notation that otherwise appears to count altruists.
  • Define feasible exchange masses by occurrence-counted capacities, with divisible nonnegative real allocations and no reuse of terminal chain donors in the current allocation.
  • Define fairness as transplanted mass divided by positive patient-type mass, over every supported patient type, including types with zero attainable rate; require a nonempty patient support.
  • Define m as the patient-type support size and L as the total input bit length; retain the fixed exchange-length limit.

Exact polynomial-time solvability

The problem is solvable exactly in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Both stages of the lexicographic objective are linear programs with polynomially many variables and constraints. The proof also establishes that an optimal allocation has a rational representation of polynomial length.

1. Enumerating all permitted exchanges

Write \(h=|N|=\tau-m\), and let \(K(u,v)\) indicate compatibility from the donor of type \(u\) to the patient of type \(v\).

Enumerate the following ordered type tuples, retaining precisely those that pass the indicated compatibility tests:

| Exchange | Candidate tuples | Compatibility tests |
|---|---|---|
| Two-patient cycle | \((i,j)\in P^2\) | \(K(i,j)\) and \(K(j,i)\) |
| Three-patient cycle | \((i,j,k)\in P^3\) | \(K(i,j)\), \(K(j,k)\), and \(K(k,i)\) |
| One-patient chain | \((n,i)\in N\times P\) | \(K(n,i)\) |
| Two-patient chain | \((n,i,j)\in N\times P^2\) | \(K(n,i)\) and \(K(i,j)\) |

There is no closing compatibility test for chains: their terminal donors leave the allocation, as stipulated.

These tuples describe every permitted exchange because compatibility depends only on types. The absence of self-compatibility automatically excludes repeated patient types in these short exchanges: any repetition would require a donation from a patient-donor type to itself.

Cycle rotations may be identified by keeping a canonical representative. This does not change feasibility or either objective, since rotations have identical occurrence counts and their assigned masses can be summed.

Let \(C\) be the resulting exchange list and \(d=|C|\). Then

\[ \]
\(d\le m^2+m^3+hm+hm^2=O(\tau m^2).\)

Each candidate requires at most three compatibility tests, so this list can be constructed in polynomial time. If \(d=0\), the empty allocation is optimal and both objective values are zero. Henceforth assume \(d>0\).

2. A linear program for maximum transplanted mass

Define the occurrence matrix and objective coefficients by

\(A_{ic}=n_i(c)\quad(i\in P\cup N,\ c\in C),\qquad q_c=\sum_{i\in P}n_i(c).\)

Thus \(q_c\in\{1,2,3\}\), and the total transplanted patient mass is

\(T(x)=q^{\mathsf T}x=\sum_{i\in P}y_i.\)

By the problem’s definition, the feasible allocations are exactly

\(F=\{x\in\mathbb R^d:Ax\le a,\ x\ge0\}.\)

In particular, every altruist capacity constraint is included. The first-stage optimum is therefore the linear-programming value

\(\displaystyle T^\star=\max\{q^{\mathsf T}x:Ax\le a,\ x\ge0\}.\)

This maximum exists. Indeed, \(F\) contains the zero allocation and is closed. For every exchange \(c\), choose any type \(i\) occurring in it. Nonnegativity and the corresponding capacity constraint give

\(n_i(c)x_c\le\sum_{c'\in C}n_i(c')x_{c'}\le a_i,\)

so \(0\le x_c\le a_i/n_i(c)\le1\). Consequently, \(F\) is compact.

3. A linear program for maxmin fairness on the optimal face

Every supported patient type has \(a_i>0\). Therefore, for any real \(t\),

\(\displaystyle t\le r_i(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{c\in C}n_i(c)x_c\ge a_i t.\)

After computing \(T^\star\) exactly, solve the second linear program:

\(\displaystyle \max t\)

subject to

\(Ax\le a,\qquad x\ge0,\qquad q^{\mathsf T}x=T^\star,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{c\in C}n_i(c)x_c\ge a_i t\quad\text{for every }i\in P,\)

\(0\le t\le1.\)

The bounds on \(t\) lose no relevant solution because every feasible transplant rate lies in \([0,1]\). This LP is feasible: any first-stage optimal allocation together with \(t=0\) satisfies its constraints. Its feasible set is closed and contained in the compact set \(F\times[0,1]\), so an optimum exists.

For any fixed first-stage optimal allocation \(x\), its largest feasible value of \(t\) is exactly

\(\displaystyle \min_{i\in P}r_i(x).\)

Thus, if \((x^\star,t^\star)\) solves the second LP, then

\(\displaystyle t^\star=\max_{\substack{x\in F\\T(x)=T^\star}}\min_{i\in P}r_i(x).\)

This proves lexicographic optimality: no feasible allocation has a larger first coordinate, and among allocations with first coordinate \(T^\star\), none has a larger second coordinate.

Every patient type remains in the fairness constraints. In particular, if a type has zero attainable transplanted mass, its constraint forces \(t=0\); such types are not discarded.

4. Exactness and polynomial bit complexity

We use the classical rational linear-programming theorem: a linear program with explicitly given rational coefficients can be solved exactly in time polynomial in its binary encoding length, returning a rational optimum when the optimum is finite and attained. Polynomial-time solvability in the rational bit model is recalled in [Adler and Beling](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf).

The first LP has \(d\) variables and \(\tau\) capacity constraints, besides nonnegativity. Its matrix and objective entries are bounded integers, and its right-hand side consists of the input masses. Its encoding length is therefore polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

For completeness, the exact value \(T^\star\) also has polynomial bit length, so inserting it into the second LP preserves polynomial size. Here is a direct bound.

A linear objective on the nonempty compact polytope \(F\) has an optimal vertex. At such a vertex, choose \(d\) linearly independent active constraints. They give

\(Bx=b,\)

where \(B\) is an invertible integer matrix with entries of absolute value at most \(3\), and each entry of \(b\) is either an input mass or zero.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses. Its bit length is \(O(L)\), and \(Db\) is an integer vector whose entries have absolute value at most \(D\). Cramer’s rule gives

\(\displaystyle x_j=\frac{\det B_j}{D\det B},\)

where \(B_j\) replaces column \(j\) of \(B\) by \(Db\). Hadamard’s determinant inequality gives

\(\displaystyle |\det B|\le(3\sqrt d)^d,\qquad |\det B_j|\le D\sqrt d\,(3\sqrt d)^{d-1}.\)

Hence these coordinates have a common denominator and numerators of bit length \(O(L+d\log(d+1))\). Since \(q_j\le3\), the same polynomial bound, with an additional \(O(\log(d+1))\) term, holds for \(T^\star=q^{\mathsf T}x\).

The second LP has \(d+1\) variables, \(\tau\) capacity constraints, \(m\) fairness constraints, one equality, and the bounds on \(t\). Its only additional rational coefficients are the input masses and \(T^\star\). It consequently has polynomial encoding length and can also be solved exactly in polynomial time.

5. Why finite-packing enumeration is unnecessary

The cited work concerns lotteries over finite exchange packings: [Farnadi et al.](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/17369) enumerate optimal plans, while [Chang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.20070v1) optimize over marginals of acceptable packings.

The present problem specifies feasibility directly through divisible exchange masses and type capacities. Its LP variables therefore represent individual permitted exchanges, whose number is polynomial because their lengths are bounded by constants. No enumeration of complete exchange plans is needed.

The two LPs above settle the stated mass-allocation problem, returning both objective values and an optimal allocation exactly.
