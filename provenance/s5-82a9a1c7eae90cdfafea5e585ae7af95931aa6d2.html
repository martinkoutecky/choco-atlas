The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite rational distribution \(\rho\) on shares \(x_i\in[0,1]\), with \(\pi=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[X]\in(0,1)\), and target interim probabilities \((Q(a),Q(b))\), does there exist an anonymous rule \(q_i\in[0,1]\) at each support point such that \(Q(a)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[Xq(X)]/\pi\) and \(Q(b)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[(1-X)q(X)]/(1-\pi)\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m=2\) alternatives, \(a,b\), and voters strictly prefer one to the other. A composition type \(x\in[0,1]\) specifies the share preferring \(a\).
A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) distinct composition types \(x_i\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) with positive masses \(\rho_i\), each mass a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; \(\sum_i\rho_i=1\).
Assume \(\pi:=\sum_i\rho_i x_i\in(0,1)\). The input also contains binary rational targets \(Q(a),Q(b)\in[0,1]\); both denote probabilities of choosing \(a\), conditional on the voter's preferred alternative.
An anonymous randomized rule assigns a real probability \(q_i\in[0,1]\) of choosing \(a\) at each composition type. The targets are implementable when \(\sum_i\rho_i x_iq_i=\pi Q(a)\) and \(\sum_i\rho_i(1-x_i)q_i=(1-\pi)Q(b)\).
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is deciding implementability for this support-encoded society in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For finite electorates with \(n\ge2\) voters and an exchangeable prior, anonymous reduced-form implementability is characterized by \(2(n+1)\) necessary and sufficient linear inequalities. [Lang and Mishra](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5400).
No separate complexity classification for the arbitrary rational-share, support-encoded formulation was located in the literature search.

  • Specified that rule probabilities may be real, as in the discrete randomized-rule definition.
  • Interpreted the support types as aggregate composition shares; the two individual preference types remain strict rankings of the two alternatives.

Result

Implementability is in \(\mathrm{P}\). For the stated case \(m=2\), an exact algorithm uses \(O(\tau\log(2\tau))\) rational arithmetic operations and \(O(\tau\log(2\tau)L^2)\) bit operations.

The proof works directly with the supplied support, including arbitrary rational composition types.

1. Reduction to two weighted sums

Define
\(t:=\pi Q(a)\) and \(s:=\pi Q(a)+(1-\pi)Q(b)\).

Because both targets belong to \([0,1]\), we have \(s\in[0,1]\). Set \(w_i:=\rho_iq_i\). Since every \(\rho_i\) is positive, the rule constraints \(q_i\in[0,1]\) are equivalent to \(0\leq w_i\leq\rho_i\).

Adding the two implementation equations, and retaining the first, shows that implementation is equivalent to
\[ \sum_i w_i=s,\qquad \sum_i x_iw_i=t,\qquad 0\leq w_i\leq\rho_i. \]

Conversely, these conditions imply the second implementation equation because
\(\sum_i(1-x_i)w_i=s-t=(1-\pi)Q(b)\).

Thus it suffices to determine the attainable values of \(\sum_i x_iw_i\) for a fixed total weight \(s\).

2. Exact lower and upper bounds

Sort the support so that \(x_1<\cdots<x_\tau\). Let \(C_0:=0\) and \(C_k:=\sum_{i=1}^k\rho_i\).

For any \(u\in[0,1]\), define
\[ w_i^-(u):=\min\{\rho_i,\max\{0,u-C_{i-1}\}\}, \qquad f(u):=\sum_i x_iw_i^-(u). \]

This fills support types in ascending order. Choose \(k\) with \(C_{k-1}\leq u\leq C_k\), which exists because \(C_\tau=1\). Then
\[ w_i^-(u)= \begin{cases} \rho_i,&i<k,\\ u-C_{k-1},&i=k,\\ 0,&i>k. \end{cases} \]
In particular, these weights satisfy their bounds and sum to \(u\).

Minimum claim. Every feasible weight vector \(w\) with \(\sum_iw_i=u\) satisfies \(\sum_i x_iw_i\geq f(u)\).

Indeed, since the two weight vectors have the same sum,
\[ \sum_i x_iw_i-f(u) = \sum_i(x_i-x_k)\bigl(w_i-w_i^-(u)\bigr). \]
For \(i<k\), both factors are nonpositive: \(x_i-x_k<0\) and \(w_i-\rho_i\leq0\). For \(i>k\), both factors are nonnegative. The term for \(i=k\) is zero. Hence the sum is nonnegative, proving the claim.

To obtain the maximum at total weight \(s\), observe that complementation \(w_i\mapsto\rho_i-w_i\) transforms total weight \(s\) into total weight \(1-s\), and transforms the objective into
\(\pi-\sum_i x_iw_i\).

Therefore the maximum is
\[ g(s):=\pi-f(1-s), \]
attained by \(w_i^+(s):=\rho_i-w_i^-(1-s)\).

3. Sufficiency and an implementing rule

The exact implementation criterion is
\[ \boxed{f(s)\leq t\leq \pi-f(1-s).} \]

Necessity follows from the minimum and maximum just proved.

For sufficiency, suppose these inequalities hold. If \(g(s)=f(s)\), they force \(t=f(s)\), so use \(w=w^-(s)\).

Otherwise, define
\(\lambda:=(t-f(s))/(g(s)-f(s))\).
The assumed bounds give \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). Set
\[ w_i:=(1-\lambda)w_i^-(s)+\lambda w_i^+(s). \]
Convexity preserves \(0\leq w_i\leq\rho_i\) and \(\sum_iw_i=s\). Moreover,
\[ \sum_i x_iw_i =(1-\lambda)f(s)+\lambda g(s)=t. \]
Consequently \(q_i:=w_i/\rho_i\) is an implementing rule. All quantities in this construction are rational.

The criterion also covers \(\tau=1\) and the endpoint cases \(s=0\) and \(s=1\).

4. A characterization by \(2\tau\) inequalities

For completeness, the criterion can be written as \(2\tau\) linear inequalities in the targets. For every \(k\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), require
\[ x_ks-\sum_{i<k}\rho_i(x_k-x_i) \ \leq\ t\ \leq\ x_ks+\sum_{i>k}\rho_i(x_i-x_k). \]

To verify necessity, write
\(t-x_ks=\sum_i(x_i-x_k)w_i\).
For a lower bound, use \(w_i\leq\rho_i\) on the negative coefficients and \(w_i\geq0\) on the positive coefficients. For an upper bound, use the opposite choices.

For sufficiency, the ascending filling rule attains equality in the lower inequality at its partially filled type. Thus the maximum of these lower bounds is \(f(s)\). The descending filling rule similarly attains equality in an upper inequality, so the minimum of the upper bounds is \(g(s)\). The preceding construction therefore proves sufficiency.

5. Exact bit complexity

The decision algorithm sorts the support, computes \(\pi,s,t\), evaluates \(f(s)\) and \(f(1-s)\), and checks the boxed inequalities.

To justify polynomial bit complexity, let \(D\) be the product of all denominators appearing in the input. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), since the sum of the denominator lengths is at most \(L\). Every input rational can be represented with denominator \(D\).

Under this representation:

  • \(\pi\) can be represented with denominator \(D^2\).
  • \(s\) and \(t\) can be represented with denominator \(D^3\).
  • The filling weights can be represented with denominator \(D^3\).
  • The values \(f(s)\) and \(f(1-s)\) can be represented with denominator \(D^4\).

All corresponding numerators have \(O(L)\) bits: the quantities are bounded, and the denominators are fixed powers of \(D\). Thus the entire calculation can use exact integers of \(O(L)\) bits, without accumulating uncontrolled rational denominators.

Constructing \(D\), converting the inputs, and evaluating the bounds require \(O(\tau)\) integer arithmetic operations. Sorting requires \(O(\tau\log(2\tau))\) comparisons. Using schoolbook integer arithmetic gives the conservative bound
\(O(\tau\log(2\tau)L^2)\) bit operations for \(m=2\).

The [Lang–Mishra paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5400) treats anonymous rules under exchangeable finite-electorate priors. The proof here establishes the complexity classification directly for the arbitrary rational-share, support-encoded formulation; no electorate-size expansion is needed.
