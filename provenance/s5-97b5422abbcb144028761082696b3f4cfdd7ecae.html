The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) schools and \(\tau\) student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-level school preferences and priority tiers, a master-list ordering of the tiers, and a rational downward-closed feasible occupancy polytope \(\mathcal F\), compute the exact aggregate assignment \(x_{tc}\) produced by staged GDA. At each stage, a supplied polynomial-time rule chooses a rational mass limit \(\delta_k\) such that the feasible residual occupancy vectors within that limit form a polymatroid; determine the runtime in the input bit length, \(\tau\), \(m\), the polytope description, and the number \(K\) of stages.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Assume \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), divisible mass, and \(m\ge1\). Each type strictly ranks its available schools and being unmatched; only mutually acceptable contracts may receive mass.
The input supplies school priority tiers, a strict master-list order of types refining its supplied tier ordering, and distinct positive rational contract weights \(w_{tc}\) respecting school priorities; these weights resolve ties and comparisons across schools.
Feasible occupancies form a nonempty bounded downward-closed polytope \(\mathcal F=\{z\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^m:Az\le b\}\), given by explicit rational inequalities, with \(0\in\mathcal F\).
Initially the finalized occupancy is \(a=0\). At stage \(k\), a supplied deterministic rule selects \(0<\delta_k\le\) remaining mass, with the promise that \(P_k=\{z\ge0:a+z\in\mathcal F,\ \sum_c z_c\le\delta_k\}\) is a polymatroid. Select exactly the first \(\delta_k\) remaining mass in master-list order, splitting a type at the boundary.
For offered amounts \(q_{tc}\), the joint school choice retains the amounts \(y_{tc}\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,c}w_{tc}y_{tc}\), subject to \(0\le y_{tc}\le q_{tc}\) and \((\sum_t y_{tc})_c\in P_k\); any residual optimization tie is resolved lexicographically in decreasing weight order.
Stage GDA starts with all selected mass offering its favorite acceptable school. In synchronous rounds, schools choose from retained offers plus new offers; each rejected portion advances to its next acceptable school in the following round, and portions exhausting their lists remain unmatched. Rejections are irrevocable for that portion and contract.
Use the terminal retained amounts, or their limit if infinitely many rounds occur. Finalize these amounts, add their occupancy to \(a\), and remove all selected mass, including unmatched mass. Reset proposal histories for the next stage; the rule is promised to exhaust the society in \(K\) stages. The output \(x_{tc}\) sums finalized assignments across stages.

Is exact computation of \(x_{tc}\) polynomial-time solvable, or is it NP-hard? State the runtime in \(m,\tau,L,h,K\) and the stage-selection cost \(T_{\mathrm{sel}}\), where \(h\) counts the inequalities describing \(\mathcal F\), \(L\) is the total input bit length, and \(T_{\mathrm{sel}}\) bounds one rule invocation on its rational state.

Discrete MS-GDA runs in polynomial time when feasibility evaluation and stage-size selection are polynomial-time computable. [Kimura et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10458-025-09713-9)
For real-valued stable allocation in two totally ordered polymatroids, a side-optimal allocation can be computed in \(O(N^3\gamma)\), where \(N\) is the ground-set size and \(\gamma\) is the saturation/exchange-capacity computation time; this result does not explicitly treat the staged output specified above. [Iwata–Yokoi](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one because the continuous society is a distribution; all occupancy bounds and stage limits use the same mass units.
  • Specified strict student preferences including unmatched, available contracts, and mutual acceptability, following the discrete domain.
  • Refined master-list tier ties by a supplied strict type order and allowed a stage boundary to split a type.
  • Added distinct positive rational weights on type-school contracts respecting school priorities, because priorities within schools do not determine the joint choice under shared constraints.
  • Specified explicit rational inequalities and bounded, nonempty feasible occupancies containing zero; boundedness is explicit in the journal version.
  • Defined fractional joint school choice by weighted optimization with a deterministic residual tie-break, and specified synchronous proposal, retention, rejection, and limit conventions.
  • Made each positive stage limit also determine the selected mass; all selected mass exits after that stage, including unmatched portions, as in discrete MS-GDA.
  • Promised exhaustion in K stages rather than assuming that positive rational stage sizes imply finite termination, and exposed the stage-selection invocation cost in the runtime parameters.

Result and computational interpretation

Exact computation is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L,h,K,T_{\mathrm{sel}}\). The algorithm computes precisely the specified staged output, including the outcome of synchronous GDA.

I use the computational interpretation that each rule invocation returns a binary rational \(\delta_k\), with its output-writing cost included in \(T_{\mathrm{sel}}\). Without an effective representation of the selected stage sizes, an exact bit-complexity question would need further specification.

Put \(n=m\tau\). One conservative bound, using ordinary quadratic-cost rational arithmetic, is
\[ O\!\left( L^3+KT_{\mathrm{sel}} +Kn^3(hm+n)B^2 \right), \]
where
\[ B=O\!\left( (1+Kn^3)\bigl(L+\log(n+h+2)\bigr) +KT_{\mathrm{sel}} \right). \]
Thus the bound is polynomial in all the requested parameters. Polynomial dependence on \(K\) and \(T_{\mathrm{sel}}\) is explicit; they need not themselves be polynomial in \(L\).

The essential point is to prove that the side-optimal polymatroid allocation algorithm reproduces each synchronous stage.

1. A stage is an intersection of two ordered polymatroids

Fix a stage, its finalized occupancy \(a\), its size \(\delta\), and the selected amounts \(M_t\), so that
\[ \sum_t M_t=\delta. \]
Let \(E\) contain the mutually acceptable contracts of selected types with \(M_t>0\). Write \(N=|E|\le m\tau\), and let \(E_t\) denote the contracts of type \(t\).

The type-side feasible polytope is
\[ H=\left\{ y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{E}: \sum_{e\in E_t}y_e\le M_t\quad\text{for every }t \right\}. \]
It is the polymatroid with rank function
\[ g(U)=\sum_{t:U\cap E_t\ne\varnothing}M_t. \]
Choose any total order \(\succ_H\) on \(E\) extending every type’s school preferences.

By the stage promise, \(P_k\) is a polymatroid. Its canonical rank function is
\[ r(S)=\max_{z\in P_k}\sum_{c\in S}z_c. \]
The standard canonical-rank characterization of a polymatroid gives
\[ P_k=\left\{ z\ge0:\sum_{c\in S}z_c\le r(S) \quad\text{for every }S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\} \right\}. \]
In particular, \(r\) is normalized, monotone, and submodular. Every \(r(S)\) is rational because it is the optimal value of a bounded rational linear program.

For \(U\subseteq E\), let \(C(U)\) be its set of schools, and define
\[ f(U)=r(C(U)). \]
This is also normalized, monotone, and submodular: school sets preserve unions, while
\[ C(U\cap V)\subseteq C(U)\cap C(V). \]
Submodularity and monotonicity of \(r\) therefore imply submodularity of \(f\).

Moreover,
\[ F=P(f) =\left\{ y\ge0: \left(\sum_t y_{tc}\right)_c\in P_k \right\}. \]
Indeed, feasible occupancies imply every contract-subset inequality. Conversely, applying those inequalities to all contracts at any chosen set of schools implies the corresponding occupancy inequality.

Order \(E\) by decreasing contract weight, denoted \(\succ_F\). Distinct weights make this a total order.

We have therefore obtained two ordered polymatroids on the same ground set of at most \(m\tau\) contracts.

2. Three ordered-polymatroid lemmas

These lemmas establish the connection to the specified proposal process.

Let \(P(p)\) be a polymatroid on a finite ordered ground set. For an element \(e\), write
\[ U_e=\{u:u\succeq e\}. \]
A set \(S\) is tight at \(x\) if \(x(S)=p(S)\). Define the blocking condition
\[ \mathsf B_p(x,e) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{some tight set }S\text{ satisfies }e\in S\subseteq U_e. \]
Unions and intersections of tight sets are tight. This follows by combining submodularity with
\[ x(S)+x(T)=x(S\cup T)+x(S\cap T). \]

The condition \(\mathsf B_p(x,e)\) is the usual ordered-polymatroid condition that \(e\) is saturated and its minimal tight set contains no element below \(e\).

Lemma 1: Greedy choice and rejected coordinates

Given upper bounds \(q\ge0\), process coordinates in order, increasing each as far as feasibility and its bound \(q_e\) permit. Call the resulting vector \(C(q)\).

For \(y=C(q)\),
\[ y_e<q_e\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathsf B_p(y,e). \]

To prove this, consider a coordinate stopped by feasibility. Some rank inequality containing that coordinate is tight. At that moment all lower coordinates are zero. Intersecting the tight set with \(U_e\) leaves its vector sum unchanged; monotonicity of \(p\) and feasibility force the intersection to be tight as well. Later steps preserve it because it contains only coordinates already processed.

This greedy vector uniquely maximizes any strictly decreasing positive weighted sum. Here is an explicit justification. For a feasible \(v\le q\) and any order prefix \(U\), take the union \(T\) of blocking tight sets for coordinates \(e\in U\) with \(y_e<v_e\). Then \(T\subseteq U\),
\[ y(T)=p(T)\ge v(T), \]
and \(y_e\ge v_e\) on \(U\setminus T\). Hence
\[ y(U)\ge v(U). \]
Writing the weighted objective as a positive linear combination of prefix sums proves optimality. If \(v\ne y\), at least one prefix inequality is strict, proving uniqueness.

Consequently, this greedy choice is exactly the school optimization specified in the task. Its residual lexicographic tie-breaking is unnecessary.

Lemma 2: Blocking persists when retained offers are supplemented

Suppose \(x\in P(p)\), \(q\ge x\), and \(y=C(q)\). Then
\[ \mathsf B_p(x,e)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathsf B_p(y,e). \]

Let \(S\subseteq U_e\) be tight at \(x\) and contain \(e\). For every \(u\in U_e\) with \(y_u<x_u\), Lemma 1 supplies a tight set at \(y\) contained in \(U_u\). Let \(T\) be their union; an empty union is allowed.

Thus \(T\subseteq U_e\) is tight at \(y\), and \(y\ge x\) on \(S\setminus T\). Therefore
\[ \begin{aligned} y(S\cup T) &=p(T)+y(S\setminus T)\\ &\ge p(T)+x(S\setminus T)\\ &=p(T)+p(S)-x(S\cap T)\\ &\ge p(T)+p(S)-p(S\cap T)\\ &\ge p(S\cup T). \end{aligned} \]
Feasibility forces equality. Thus \(S\cup T\) witnesses \(\mathsf B_p(y,e)\).

Lemma 3: A comparison principle

Suppose \(x,z\in P(p)\) and
\[ \forall u,\qquad x_u\ge z_u \quad\text{or}\quad \mathsf B_p(x,u). \]
Then
\[ \mathsf B_p(z,e)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_e\ge x_e. \]

Choose a tight set \(S\subseteq U_e\) at \(z\) containing \(e\). For each \(u\in S\setminus\{e\}\) with \(x_u<z_u\), choose a blocking tight set at \(x\), and let \(T\) be their union.

Every such \(u\) precedes \(e\), so \(e\notin T\). Also \(T\) is tight at \(x\). Submodularity gives
\[ \begin{aligned} z(S\setminus T) &=p(S)-z(S\cap T)\\ &\ge p(S)-p(S\cap T)\\ &\ge p(S\cup T)-p(T)\\ &\ge x(S\cup T)-x(T)\\ &=x(S\setminus T). \end{aligned} \]
On \(D=S\setminus(T\cup\{e\})\), the construction gives \(x(D)\ge z(D)\). Subtracting these coordinates yields \(z_e\ge x_e\).

3. The synchronous stage terminates for rational data

All school-side ranks \(f(U)\) and selected amounts \(M_t\) are rational. Choose a positive integer \(D\) making all of them integral after multiplication by \(D\).

By Lemma 1’s greedy construction, offers on the grid
\[ D^{-1}\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{E} \]
produce retained amounts on the same grid: each increment is the minimum of an offered residual and finitely many rank slacks. Thus every round’s offers, retained amounts, and rejected amounts remain on this grid.

Every round having a positive rejection rejects at least \(1/D\) mass. Each portion can be rejected at no more than \(m\) contracts, because it advances strictly down its list. Total rejected mass, counted with multiplicity over contracts, is therefore at most \(m\delta\).

There are consequently at most \(Dm\delta\) rounds with positive rejection. A round without rejection produces no subsequent proposals. The stage terminates after finitely many rounds.

This proves existence of its terminal output. It does not give a polynomial round bound, and the efficient algorithm below does not enumerate those rounds.

4. The synchronous output is the unique side-optimal stable allocation

For the type-side polymatroid,
\[ \mathsf B_g(y,e) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{\substack{u\in E_t\\u\succeq_t e}}y_u=M_t, \qquad e\in E_t. \]
To see this, any tight set containing \(e\) must saturate the selected amount of its type. Its contracts of that type must all precede \(e\). Conversely, a saturated type preference prefix is itself a suitable tight set.

Call \(z\in H\cap F\) stable when, for every contract \(e\),
\[ \mathsf B_g(z,e)\quad\text{or}\quad\mathsf B_f(z,e). \]
This is precisely stability for the two ordered polymatroids.

Rejection invariant

Fix any stable allocation \(z\). We prove that, after every round,
\[ e\text{ has ever received a positive rejection} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_e\ge z_e, \tag{1} \]
where \(y\) is the currently retained vector.

Suppose the invariant holds before a round. Let \(q\) consist of previous retained offers plus new offers, and let \(y=C_F(q)\). Since those offers represent distinct portions of selected mass,
\[ q\in H. \]

If \(q_e>z_e\), every school that its type prefers to \(e\) has received a positive rejection before this round: some portion could reach \(e\) only by passing all those schools. The induction hypothesis therefore gives
\[ q_u\ge y^{\mathrm{previous}}_u\ge z_u \]
at every such better contract \(u\).

If \(\mathsf B_g(z,e)\) held, these inequalities together with \(q_e>z_e\) would make \(q\)’s corresponding type prefix exceed \(M_t\), contradicting \(q\in H\). Stability of \(z\) consequently implies
\[ q_e>z_e\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathsf B_f(z,e). \]
Because \(y\le q\), we obtain
\[ \forall e,\qquad z_e\ge y_e \quad\text{or}\quad \mathsf B_f(z,e). \]
Apply Lemma 3 with \(x=z\). At every newly rejected contract, Lemma 1 gives \(\mathsf B_f(y,e)\), hence \(y_e\ge z_e\).

For a previously rejected contract that is not newly rejected,
\[ y_e=q_e\ge y^{\mathrm{previous}}_e\ge z_e. \]
This proves (1).

Stability of the terminal vector

Let \(y^*\) be the terminal vector. If \(\mathsf B_g(y^*,e)\) fails, positive mass of its type is assigned below \(e\) or remains unmatched. That mass must have passed through and been rejected at \(e\). Thus \(e\) has received a positive rejection.

At its rejection round, Lemma 1 established school-side blocking. Every later round supplements the retained vector with nonnegative new offers, so Lemma 2 preserves that blocking. Hence
\[ \mathsf B_f(y^*,e). \]
Therefore \(y^*\) is stable.

Side-optimality and uniqueness

Fix a type preference prefix \(V\). If \(y^*_e\ge z_e\) throughout \(V\), then \(y^*(V)\ge z(V)\).

Otherwise choose \(e\in V\) with \(y^*_e<z_e\). By (1), \(e\) was never rejected. No portion of its type can therefore finish below \(e\) or unmatched. Consequently,
\[ y^*(V)=M_t\ge z(V). \]

Thus \(y^*\) dominates every stable allocation on every type preference prefix. Every prefix of \(\succ_H\) is a disjoint union of such type prefixes, so \(y^*\) is \(\succ_H\)-preferable to every stable allocation.

There can be only one allocation with this property: if two allocations dominate each other on every total-order prefix, all their prefix sums agree, and successive differences give equality coordinatewise.

The specified synchronous stage output is therefore the unique \(\succ_H\)-optimal stable allocation.

5. Computing that allocation efficiently

Iwata–Yokoi’s Theorems 6.6 and 7.7 compute the \(\succ_H\)-optimal stable allocation of two totally ordered polymatroids on \(N\) elements in \(O(N^3\gamma)\) time, where \(\gamma\) is the saturation/exchange-capacity computation time. The result applies to real-valued polymatroids. [Iwata–Yokoi](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf)

All hypotheses were verified in Section 1; Section 4 proves that its output is exactly the required stage output.

The needed capacities can be computed directly from the explicit inequalities, without enumerating rank subsets.

Write
\[ \widehat A= \begin{pmatrix} A\\ \mathbf 1^\top \end{pmatrix}, \qquad d= \begin{pmatrix} b-Aa\\ \delta \end{pmatrix}. \]
For a school-feasible contract vector \(y\), let \(s_c=\sum_t y_{tc}\) and
\[ \sigma_i=d_i-\widehat A_i s. \]

For \(e=(t,c)\), its school-side saturation capacity is
\[ \widehat c_F(y,e) = \min_{i:\widehat A_{ic}>0} \frac{\sigma_i}{\widehat A_{ic}}. \]
For distinct contracts \(e=(t,c)\) and \(v=(u,d')\), its exchange capacity is
\[ \overline c_F(y,e,v) = \min\!\left\{ y_v,\; \min_{i:\widehat A_{ic}-\widehat A_{id'}>0} \frac{\sigma_i}{\widehat A_{ic}-\widehat A_{id'}} \right\}. \]
An empty inner minimum is interpreted as \(+\infty\). These formulas follow by substituting the proposed one-coordinate increase or two-coordinate exchange into every explicit inequality. The term \(y_v\) enforces nonnegativity. They remain valid when the two contracts share a school.

On the type side,
\[ \widehat c_H(y,e)=M_t-y(E_t), \]
and, for distinct contracts,
\[ \overline c_H(y,e,v)= \begin{cases} y_v,&e,v\in E_t,\\ \min\{y_v,M_t-y(E_t)\},&e\in E_t,\ v\notin E_t. \end{cases} \]

Recomputing occupancies, type totals, and row slacks gives
\[ \gamma=O(hm+N) \]
rational arithmetic operations. Thus a stage requires
\[ O\!\left(N^3(hm+N)\right) \]
such operations.

If \(E\) is empty, all selected mass is unmatched and the stage is handled directly.

6. Staging and exact bit complexity

At each stage, invoke the supplied selection rule, take the specified master-list prefix, compute its unique side-optimal allocation, finalize it, and remove the entire selected mass.

Induction over stages proves correctness: the computed finalized assignments equal those of synchronous GDA, so the next occupancy, remaining masses, and rational state are identical. The next rule invocation therefore selects the same stage.

It remains to bound rational lengths.

Clear the denominators in each original inequality using a positive multiplier. The resulting integer coefficients and right-hand sides have \(O(L)\) bits. Every school-capacity computation then divides a rational slack by an integer coefficient or coefficient difference of \(O(L)\) bits.

The Iwata–Yokoi algorithm updates allocations by adding or subtracting a minimum of computed capacities; its numerical updates introduce no further variable denominators. This follows from its augment and cycle update formulas. [Iwata–Yokoi, Section 3](https://www.keisu.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/data/2017/METR17-02.pdf)

Maintain a common denominator for stored numerical state. Initially its logarithm is \(O(L)\). Each rule output can add at most \(O(T_{\mathrm{sel}})\) bits to that logarithm. Each capacity query can enlarge it by at most \(O(L)\) bits. There are \(O(Kn^3)\) queries altogether.

Selected, retained, finalized, and remaining masses are bounded by one. Row slacks introduce only the sizes of the integer input coefficients and sums over schools. Hence all relevant rational numerators and denominators have at most
\[ B=O\!\left( (1+Kn^3)\bigl(L+\log(n+h+2)\bigr) +KT_{\mathrm{sel}} \right) \]
bits.

Using \(O(B^2)\) elementary bit operations per rational arithmetic operation or comparison gives the stated bound
\[ O\!\left( L^3+KT_{\mathrm{sel}} +Kn^3(hm+n)B^2 \right). \]

This settles exact computation of the specified output with polynomial dependence on support size, stages, and selection cost.

  • Rejection invariant

The invariant is initially vacuous.

  • Terminal stability and side-optimality

The terminal vector is therefore stable.

For side-optimality, if a coordinate in a type prefix is smaller than its value in a stable competitor, the rejection invariant shows that this coordinate was never rejected. Hence no mass of that type can finish below it or unmatched, and the terminal vector saturates that type prefix.

Summing type-prefix comparisons establishes dominance on every prefix of the total type-side order. Mutual prefix dominance implies coordinatewise equality, so the uniqueness argument is valid.
