The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite goods set \(V\), a complete compatibility graph, a nonnegative rational additive utility \(u\), a rational threshold \(k\), and population mass \(1\) with per-capita supply \(\sigma_v=\frac12\) for every \(v\in V\), decide whether variables \(x_B\ge0\), for \(B\subseteq V\), exist such that \(\sum_{B\subseteq V}x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni v}x_B=\frac12\) for every \(v\in V\), and \(x_B>0\Rightarrow u(B)=\sum_{v\in B}u(v)\ge k\). Each positive \(x_B\) is mass receiving the integral bundle \(B\), with at most one copy of each good type per bundle.

Every subset \(B\subseteq V\), including the empty set, is an admissible integral bundle, containing at most one copy of each good; its utility is \(u(B)=\sum_{v\in B}u(v)\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau=1\), with mass \(1\); \(\tau\) denotes support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total binary input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each good has per-capita supply \(\sigma_v=\frac12\). Feasibility means that real masses \(x_B\geq0\), indexed implicitly by all \(B\subseteq V\), satisfy \(\sum_{B\subseteq V}x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni v}x_B=\frac12\) for every \(v\in V\), and \(x_B>0\Rightarrow u(B)\geq k\). Is this feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

The discrete problem of partitioning all goods into two bundles each worth at least \(k\) is NP-complete, even with a complete compatibility graph and nonnegative integer utilities ([source](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.03038v2)). Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, already with \(\tau=1\) and nonnegative integer utilities. Thus a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

In fact, continuous feasibility is equivalent to partitioning the goods into two bundles each worth at least \(k\).

Exact equivalence

Write \(U=u(V)=\sum_{v\in V}u(v)\).

Proposition. A feasible mass distribution exists if and only if there is a subset \(B\subseteq V\) such that \(u(B)\geq k\) and \(u(V\setminus B)\geq k\).

Proof. Suppose first that \((x_B)_{B\subseteq V}\) is feasible. Additivity and the supply equalities give

\(\displaystyle \sum_{B\subseteq V}x_Bu(B)=\sum_{v\in V}u(v)\sum_{B\ni v}x_B=\frac12\sum_{v\in V}u(v)=\frac U2.\)

Every bundle with positive mass has utility at least \(k\), and the masses sum to \(1\). Consequently \(U/2\geq k\).

The support is nonempty and finite because there are only \(2^m\) bundles. There must therefore be a supported bundle \(B_0\) with \(u(B_0)\leq U/2\): otherwise the weighted average above would exceed \(U/2\). Since \(B_0\) is supported,

\(\displaystyle k\leq u(B_0)\leq \frac U2.\)

Its complement satisfies

\(\displaystyle u(V\setminus B_0)=U-u(B_0)\geq \frac U2\geq k.\)

Thus \(B_0\) and its complement constitute the required partition.

Conversely, suppose \(B\) and \(V\setminus B\) both have utility at least \(k\). Assign mass \(1/2\) to each and zero to every other bundle. These two bundles are distinct because \(V\neq\varnothing\). The masses sum to \(1\), and every good occurs in exactly one of them, so its assigned mass is exactly \(1/2\). Both bundles meet the threshold and are admissible. This distribution is feasible. \(\square\)

In particular, whenever a feasible distribution exists, one exists using exactly two complementary bundles with rational masses \(1/2\). Allowing arbitrary real masses introduces no additional feasible instances.

Hardness

Reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given a nonempty list of positive binary integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), determine whether some subset sums to half their total. This is also the source problem used in [Proposition 3.1 of the supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1905.03038v2).

Construct one good \(v_i\) for each integer, and set

\(\displaystyle u(v_i)=a_i,\qquad S=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_i,\qquad k=\frac S2.\)

Use a complete compatibility graph, one utility type of mass \(1\), and supply \(1/2\) for every good.

By the proposition, this instance is feasible exactly when some \(B\subseteq V\) satisfies

\(\displaystyle u(B)\geq \frac S2,\qquad S-u(B)\geq \frac S2.\)

Together these inequalities are equivalent to \(u(B)=S/2\), precisely the PARTITION condition. This also handles odd \(S\): no integer-valued subset can attain \(S/2\).

The transformation has polynomial output length. Using ordinary binary arithmetic, it can be implemented in \(O(mL^2+m^2)\) bit operations, where \(L\) is the constructed instance’s total bit length; the \(m^2\) term accommodates an explicitly written adjacency matrix. Throughout the reduction, \(\tau=1\). Hence the problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under the stated restrictions.

Membership and running times

By the proposition, a certificate is just the \(m\)-bit incidence vector of a subset \(B\). The verifier computes \(u(B)\) and \(u(V\setminus B)\) exactly and compares both with \(k\).

For binary rational inputs of total length \(L\), these calculations admit \(O(L)\)-bit intermediate numerators and denominators: products of input denominators have bit length bounded by their total encoded length, and summing at most \(m\) terms adds only \(O(\log m)\) bits. Schoolbook arithmetic therefore gives verification time \(O(mL^2)\), polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) with \(\tau=1\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness proof. Enumerating all subsets also gives an exact deterministic algorithm taking \(O(2^m mL^2)\) bit operations.
