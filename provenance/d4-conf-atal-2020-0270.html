The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite support \(G\) of game types with masses \(\mu_g\), action sets \(A_g\), horizon \(T\), and \(N\) dyads distributed across types, but unknown reward laws \(P_{g,a}\), characterize the minimax population-average individual-rational regret. Each round, allocate fractions \(x_{g,a,t}\) of type \(g\) to joint actions \(a\), with \(\sum_{a\in A_g}x_{g,a,t}=\mu_g\); pooled feedback consists of the corresponding finite-\(N\) dyad observations. Determine the regret rate in \(N,T,\mu\), and the action-set sizes, and give a matching algorithm and lower bound.

Type \(g\) has joint-action set \(A_g=A_g^1\times A_g^2\), with \(|A_g^i|\ge2\); write \(K_g=|A_g|\) and \(m=\max_gK_g\). There are \(N\) persistent dyads, exactly \(n_g=N\mu_g\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\) of each type, and a known integer horizon \(T\ge m\).
Playing \(a\) in type \(g\) produces a reward pair from an unknown fixed law \(P_{g,a}\) on \([0,1]^2\), with mean \(r_g(a)\); draws are independent across dyads and rounds conditional on their actions, while the two rewards within a pair may be dependent. No relationship between laws of different types is assumed.
Let \(s_g^i\) be player \(i\)'s maximin value in the mean-payoff game. The benchmark \(v_g\) is the egalitarian bargaining value: among \(v\in\operatorname{conv}\{r_g(a):a\in A_g\}\), lexicographically maximize \((\min_i(v^i-s_g^i),\max_i(v^i-s_g^i))\).

2. Geometry and stability of the benchmark

Fix a type and suppress its subscript. Let
$$ C=\operatorname{conv}\{r(a):a\in A\}, \qquad w(p)=\sum_a p_a r(a), \qquad p\in\Delta_A, $$
where $\Delta_A$ is the simplex of distributions over joint actions. Write
$$ z=\max_{p\in\Delta_A}\min_i\{w^i(p)-s^i\}. $$

The benchmark exists: first maximize the continuous minimum-advantage objective over the compact simplex, and then maximize the continuous maximum-advantage objective over the resulting compact set.

A geometric property

Suppose the benchmark has unequal advantages, with
$$ v^1-s^1=z<v^2-s^2. $$
Then every $u\in C$ satisfies $u^1\le v^1$.

Indeed, if $u^1>v^1$, then for sufficiently small $\lambda>0$, the payoff
$$ (1-\lambda)v+\lambda u $$
has both advantages strictly greater than $z$. Its first advantage increases strictly, and its second remains above $z$ by continuity. This contradicts the definition of $z$.

This property also implies uniqueness of the benchmark payoff. In the unequal-advantage case, its lower-advantage coordinate is fixed at the maximum feasible value, and its other coordinate is fixed by secondary optimality. In the equal-advantage case, both coordinates are fixed by $s$ and $z$.

Stability lemma with relaxed constraints

Suppose estimated means satisfy
$$ \max_{a,i}|\widehat r^i(a)-r^i(a)|\le\eta. $$
Let $\widehat s^i$ be the maximin values of the estimated game, and put
$$ \widehat w(p)=\sum_a p_a\widehat r(a), \qquad \widehat z= \max_{p\in\Delta_A} \min_i\{\widehat w^i(p)-\widehat s^i\}. $$
Choose $\widehat p$ to maximize $\widehat w^1(p)+\widehat w^2(p)$ subject to
$$ \widehat w^i(p)-\widehat s^i\ge\widehat z-4\eta, \qquad i=1,2. $$

Then
$$ w^i(\widehat p)\ge v^i-8\eta, \qquad i=1,2. $$

Proof. Uniform perturbation by at most $\eta$ changes every mixed-strategy payoff by at most $\eta$. Taking minima and maxima preserves this bound, so
$$ |\widehat s^i-s^i|\le\eta. $$
Consequently, every policy’s estimated advantage differs from its true advantage by at most $2\eta$, and
$$ |\widehat z-z|\le2\eta. $$

A policy attaining $v$ is feasible for the relaxed estimated problem: its estimated advantages are at least $z-2\eta\ge\widehat z-4\eta$. Hence the relaxed feasible set is nonempty.

Feasibility of $\widehat p$ gives
$$ w^i(\widehat p)-s^i\ge z-8\eta. $$
Its total-payoff optimality, compared with a policy attaining $v$, gives
$$ w^1(\widehat p)+w^2(\widehat p) \ge v^1+v^2-4\eta. $$

If both benchmark advantages equal $z$, the coordinatewise conclusion follows from the first inequality. Otherwise, exchange the players if necessary so that $v^1-s^1=z<v^2-s^2$. The geometric property gives $w^1(\widehat p)\le v^1$, and therefore the total-payoff inequality implies
$$ w^2(\widehat p)\ge v^2-4\eta. $$
The first coordinate is at least $v^1-8\eta$. This proves the lemma. $\square$

3. A feasible population algorithm and its upper bound

Run the following procedure independently for each supported type. Fix a type with $n$ dyads and $K$ joint actions. Define
$$ \ell=\left\lceil\log_2(4KT)\right\rceil, \qquad q=\left\lceil \left(\frac{n^2T^2\ell}{K^2}\right)^{1/3} \right\rceil, \qquad h=\left\lceil\frac{Kq}{n}\right\rceil. $$

If $h>T$, play any fixed action throughout. Otherwise:

  • During the first $h$ rounds, use a predetermined assignment of actions that designates exactly $q$ samples of each joint action. Fill unused dyad-round slots arbitrarily.
  • After round $h$, compute empirical means from the designated samples.
  • Solve the relaxed bargaining problem from Section 2.
  • In every remaining round, independently sample $\widehat p$ for every dyad.

The exploration schedule exists because $nh\ge Kq$. It uses no observations from the current round. During exploitation, the conditional expected count of action $a$ is $n\widehat p_a$, so the required allocation is
$$ x_{g,a,t}=\mu_g\widehat p_a. $$
Thus the algorithm respects both the batching and allocation requirements.

Estimation

For finite computation, quantize each reward downward to mesh $2^{-b}$, where
$$ b=\left\lceil\log_2(16T)\right\rceil, \qquad \rho=2^{-b}\le\frac1{16T}. $$
This changes each sample by at most $\rho$.

Coordinatewise Hoeffding inequalities and a union bound over the $2K$ coordinates give
$$ \Pr\left( \max_{a,i}|\widehat r^i(a)-r^i(a)| > \sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2q}}+\rho \right) \le4K e^{-\ell}\le\frac1T. $$
Here $\ell\ge\ln(4KT)$. Dependence between the two rewards within a pair does not affect the coordinatewise inequalities.

Choose a rational tolerance satisfying
$$ \sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2q}}+\rho \le\eta \le2\left(\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2q}}+\rho\right). $$
A dyadic approximation supplies such a tolerance.

Individual fluctuations: corrected variance argument

Condition on the exploration history, which fixes $\widehat p$. Write $S=T-h$ and, for one dyad, let $X_t=(X_t^1,X_t^2)$ denote its exploitation reward pair. Define
$$ U_i=\sum_{t=h+1}^T\bigl(w^i(\widehat p)-X_t^i\bigr). $$
The exploitation reward pairs are independent across rounds, and $\mathbb E U_i=0$ under this conditioning.

The relevant variance concerns the coordinate difference. Since
$$ X_t^1-X_t^2\in[-1,1], $$
we have
$$ \operatorname{Var}(X_t^1-X_t^2) \le \mathbb E(X_t^1-X_t^2)^2\le1, $$
regardless of dependence within the reward pair. Independence across rounds therefore gives
$$ \mathbb E(U_1-U_2)^2\le S. $$
Using the identity
$$ \max(U_1,U_2)=\frac{U_1+U_2+|U_1-U_2|}{2} $$
and Cauchy–Schwarz yields
$$ \mathbb E\max(U_1,U_2)\le\frac{\sqrt S}{2}. $$

Regret accounting

On the estimation event, the stability lemma bounds both exploitation mean deficits by $8\eta$. Exploration contributes at most $h$ to either player’s deficit, because rewards and benchmark coordinates belong to $[0,1]$.

On the exceptional event, regret is pathwise at most $T$. Its probability is at most $1/T$. Consequently, the expected average regret of this type satisfies
$$ \mathbb E R_{\mathrm{type}} \le h+8T\eta+\frac{\sqrt T}{2}+1. $$

Put
$$ B=\left(\frac{KT^2\ell}{n}\right)^{1/3}. $$
The unrounded quantity defining $q$ is at least $1$, because $T\ge K$ and $n\ge1$. Hence
$$ h\le2B+1, \qquad T\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{2q}}\le\frac B{\sqrt2}. $$
The quantization contribution satisfies $T\rho\le1/16$. Thus
$$ \mathbb E R_{\mathrm{type}}\le C(\sqrt T+B). $$

If $h>T$, then $T<2B+1$. Since $K\ge4$ and $T\ge K$, this implies $B>3T/8$, so the fallback policy also satisfies this bound.

Every policy has pathwise regret at most $T$. Enlarging the universal constant if necessary gives
$$ \mathbb E R_{\mathrm{type}} \le C\min\left\{ T,\, \sqrt T+\left(\frac{KT^2\ell}{n}\right)^{1/3} \right\}. $$
Finally,
$$ R_{N,T}=\sum_g\mu_gR_g, $$
where $R_g$ is the average regret within type $g$. Taking expectations and summing proves the population upper bound.

4. Expected regret is nonnegative

This fact is needed because regret is defined without positive parts.

Fix an environment, a controller, and a dyad. Conditional mean rewards equal the means of its assigned actions. Therefore its expected time-average payoff is some $u\in C$.

The payoff $u$ cannot strictly exceed $v$ in both coordinates: that would strictly increase the minimum advantage above $z$. Hence
$$ \max_i(v^i-u^i)\ge0. $$
Jensen’s inequality for the convex maximum function gives
$$ \mathbb E\max_i\left\{Tv^i-\sum_{t=1}^T X_t^i\right\} \ge T\max_i(v^i-u^i)\ge0. $$

Thus expected regret is nonnegative for each dyad, each type, and every fixed environment. No pathwise nonnegativity is asserted.

5. Learning lower bound for one type

Fix an arbitrary permitted rectangular action set with $K$ joint actions and $n$ dyads. Let
$$ M=nT, \qquad H=K-1, \qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{16}\left(\frac HM\right)^{1/3}. $$
Because $M\ge K$, we have $0<\varepsilon\le1/16$.

Choose one distinguished action $a_0$. In the null environment, set
$$ r_0(a_0)=(1/2,1), \qquad r_0(a)=(1/2,1/2)\quad(a\ne a_0). $$
For every $j\ne a_0$, define an alternative differing only at $j$, with
$$ r_j(j)=(1/2+\varepsilon,1/2+\varepsilon). $$
Use independent Bernoulli coordinates with these means; a coordinate of mean $1$ is deterministic.

Benchmarks

In the null environment, both maximin values are $1/2$. For player $1$, this follows because its payoff is constantly $1/2$. For player $2$, all payoffs are at least $1/2$, and an opposing row different from the row of $a_0$ caps its guarantee at $1/2$. Such a row exists by the action-set hypotheses.

All null payoffs have first coordinate $1/2$, so secondary optimality gives
$$ v_0=(1/2,1). $$

In alternative $j$, player $1$ still has maximin value $1/2$: all payoffs are at least $1/2$, while an opposing column different from the column of $j$ makes every first-player payoff exactly $1/2$.

Write player $2$’s maximin value as $1/2+d$. All its payoffs are at least $1/2$, and a row different from the row of $a_0$ contains only payoffs at most $1/2+\varepsilon$. Therefore
$$ 0\le d\le\varepsilon. $$
The policy assigning mass $2d$ to $a_0$ and $1-2d$ to $j$ has both advantages equal to
$$ (1-2d)\varepsilon. $$
Thus the alternative’s optimal minimum advantage is at least $(1-2\varepsilon)\varepsilon$, giving
$$ v_j^1\ge\frac12+\frac34\varepsilon. $$

Information comparison, including batched feedback

Let $Q_j$ be the total number of plays of action $j$, and put
$$ D_0=\mathbb E_0\sum_{j\ne a_0}Q_j. $$
Under the null, the expected type-average regret is at least the second-player deficit:
$$ \mathbb E_0R_{\mathrm{type}}\ge\frac{D_0}{2n}. $$

Let $\mathbb P_0,\mathbb P_j$ be the complete transcript laws. The conditional assignment kernel is the same under both environments. Conditional on assignments, reward laws factor across dyads within each round. The relative-entropy chain rule therefore applies to these batches, and only rewards from action $j$ contribute.

For one reward pair at $j$, the divergence from null to alternative is
$$ -\ln(1-4\varepsilon^2)\le8\varepsilon^2. $$
Consequently,
$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb P_0\Vert\mathbb P_j) \le8\varepsilon^2\mathbb E_0Q_j. $$

Pinsker’s inequality, together with $0\le Q_j\le M$, yields
$$ \mathbb E_jQ_j \le\mathbb E_0Q_j+ 2M\varepsilon\sqrt{\mathbb E_0Q_j}. $$
Averaging over alternatives and using concavity of the square root gives
$$ \frac1H\sum_j\mathbb E_jQ_j \le \frac{D_0}{H} +2M\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{D_0}{H}}. $$

A fixed prior

Give the null probability $1/2$ and every alternative probability $1/(2H)$.

If $D_0\ge\varepsilon M$, the null contribution to Bayes regret is at least $\varepsilon M/(4n)$. The alternative contributions are nonnegative by Section 4.

Otherwise, $D_0<\varepsilon M$. Since $\varepsilon\le1/16$ and $H\ge1$,
$$ \frac{D_0}{H}\le\frac M{16}. $$
Also,
$$ 2\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{D_0}{H}} \le2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^3M}{H}} =\frac1{32}. $$
Hence
$$ \frac1H\sum_j\mathbb E_jQ_j\le\frac{3M}{32}. $$

In alternative $j$, the first-player deficit gives
$$ \mathbb E_jR_{\mathrm{type}} \ge \frac{\varepsilon}{n} \left(\frac{3M}{4}-\mathbb E_jQ_j\right). $$
The alternatives’ combined prior contribution is therefore at least
$$ \frac{\varepsilon M}{2n} \left(\frac34-\frac3{32}\right) >\frac{\varepsilon M}{4n}. $$

Both cases give Bayes regret at least $\varepsilon M/(4n)$. Since $H=K-1\ge3K/4$,
$$ \inf_\Lambda\sup_P\mathbb E R_{\mathrm{type}} \ge c\left(\frac{KT^2}{n}\right)^{1/3}. $$

6. Direct sum and individual-noise lower bounds

Direct sum over supported types

Take the product of the priors from Section 5, one for every supported type.

For a given type, condition on fixed environments for all other types. In the null-versus-alternative comparison for the chosen type, the other reward kernels are identical. Although their observations can influence subsequent assignments, they contribute zero relative entropy. The same conditional assignment kernels and the same batch chain-rule calculation remain valid.

The one-type Bayes lower bound therefore holds conditionally on every choice of the other environments. Integrating and summing the type contributions gives
$$ \mathcal R^* \ge c\sum_g\mu_g \left(\frac{K_gT^2}{n_g}\right)^{1/3}. $$
This argument permits arbitrary coordination between types and dyads.

Unavoidable individual noise

For every type and every action, use the action-independent law
$$ (X^1,X^2)=(B,1-B), \qquad B\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2). $$
Every mean payoff and benchmark is $(1/2,1/2)$. A dyad’s regret is exactly
$$ \left|\sum_{t=1}^T(B_t-1/2)\right|. $$

Let $Z=\sum_{t=1}^T(2B_t-1)$. Independence gives
$$ \mathbb EZ^2=T, \qquad \mathbb EZ^4=T+6\binom T2=3T^2-2T\le3T^2. $$
Paley–Zygmund applied to $Z^2$ implies
$$ \Pr(Z^2\ge T/2)\ge\frac1{12}. $$
Therefore
$$ \mathbb E|Z|/2\ge\frac{\sqrt T}{24\sqrt2}. $$

Population averaging preserves this expectation, so
$$ \mathcal R^*\ge c\sqrt T. $$
Combining the two lower bounds using $\max\{a,b\}\ge(a+b)/2$ proves $\mathcal R^*\ge cW$.

7. New result: fixed exploration followed by commitment requires a logarithm

This section identifies a limitation of the upper-bound algorithm’s architecture.

Restricted controller class

Consider one type with $n$ dyads. Before receiving any rewards, the controller selects its exploration length and complete exploration assignment schedule, possibly using randomness independent of the rewards. After exploration, subsequent assignments may depend on the exploration observations and fresh randomness, but must ignore all subsequent rewards.

Call this class $\mathsf{ETC}$. The algorithm in Section 3 belongs to it. The unrestricted controller class is larger.

An implicit lower bound

For any controller in $\mathsf{ETC}$, write
$$ r=\sup_P\mathbb E R_{\mathrm{type}}. $$
If $r\le T/1024$, then
$$ r^3\ge \frac{(K-1)T^2}{8192n} \ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right). $$

Proof. Section 6 ensures $r>0$. Use the family from Section 5, now with
$$ \varepsilon=\frac{8r}{T}\le\frac1{128}. $$
All benchmark calculations remain valid.

Let $Q_j$ again count all plays of alternative action $j$. The null regret bound gives
$$ D_0=\mathbb E_0\sum_{j\ne a_0}Q_j\le2nr. $$
For every alternative,
$$ r\ge \frac{\varepsilon}{n} \left(\frac{3nT}{4}-\mathbb E_jQ_j\right). $$
Since $nr/\varepsilon=nT/8$, this implies
$$ \mathbb E_jQ_j\ge\frac{5nT}{8}. $$

Set $M=nT$ and define the event
$$ E_j=\{Q_j\ge M/2\}. $$
Because $Q_j\le M$,
$$ \mathbb E_jQ_j \le\frac M2+\frac M2\Pr_j(E_j). $$
Thus
$$ \Pr_j(E_j)\ge\frac14. $$
Under the null, Markov’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr_0(E_j) \le\frac{2\mathbb E_0Q_j}{M} \le\frac{4r}{T}. $$

Now retain only the controller’s initial random choices, its exploration observations, and its complete subsequent action sequence. Subsequent reward observations are excluded. Because the controller ignores those rewards, the post-exploration action sequence is produced by a common stochastic kernel from the retained exploration information.

Let $e_j$ be the expected number of exploration plays of $j$. The exploration design is independent of rewards, so $e_j$ is the same under the null and every alternative.

For a Bernoulli coordinate,
$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left( \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2+\varepsilon) \Vert \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2) \right) \le4\varepsilon^2. $$
Indeed, relative entropy is at most the chi-squared divergence, which here equals $4\varepsilon^2$. The two independent coordinates give a pair divergence at most $8\varepsilon^2$.

The chain rule for exploration observations and data processing for subsequent actions therefore bound the retained transcript divergence, in the alternative-to-null direction, by
$$ 8\varepsilon^2e_j. $$

The event $E_j$ is measurable from that transcript. Write
$$ p_j=\Pr_j(E_j), \qquad \alpha_j=\Pr_0(E_j). $$
Data processing to this binary event gives
$$ 8\varepsilon^2e_j \ge d(p_j\Vert\alpha_j), $$
where $d$ denotes Bernoulli relative entropy. The elementary entropy bound
$$ d(p\Vert\alpha)\ge p\ln(1/\alpha)-\ln2 $$
implies
$$ 8\varepsilon^2e_j \ge \frac14\ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right)-\ln2. $$
Since $r\le T/1024$, the logarithm is at least $8\ln2$, and hence
$$ e_j\ge \frac1{64\varepsilon^2} \ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right). $$

Summing over the $H=K-1$ alternatives, and observing that exploration plays are included among all null non-$a_0$ plays, yields
$$ 2nr\ge D_0\ge\sum_j e_j \ge \frac{H}{64\varepsilon^2} \ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right). $$
Substituting $\varepsilon=8r/T$ proves
$$ r^3\ge \frac{HT^2}{8192n} \ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right). $$
$\square$

Matching restricted rate for a single $2\times2$ game

Let $n=1$ and $K=4$. For every $\mathsf{ETC}$ controller, the preceding inequality implies, for all sufficiently large $T$,
$$ r\ge cT^{2/3}(\ln T)^{1/3}. $$

To check this implication, split into two cases. If $r\ge T^{3/4}$, the asserted bound follows because
$$ \frac{T^{3/4}}{T^{2/3}(\ln T)^{1/3}} =\frac{T^{1/12}}{(\ln T)^{1/3}} \longrightarrow\infty. $$
If $r<T^{3/4}$, then for sufficiently large $T$, we have $r\le T/1024$ and
$$ \ln\left(\frac{T}{4r}\right) \ge\frac14\ln T-\ln4 \ge\frac18\ln T. $$
The implicit lower bound then gives the asserted rate.

Section 3 supplies an $\mathsf{ETC}$ upper bound of the same order when $n=1$ and $K=4$. Therefore
$$ \inf_{\Lambda\in\mathsf{ETC}}\sup_P \mathbb E R_{1,T} = \Theta\!\left(T^{2/3}(\ln T)^{1/3}\right). $$

The divergence argument uses the fact that exploitation rewards are ignored. For a fully adaptive controller, those rewards may affect subsequent action counts; the retained-transcript argument above no longer applies. Consequently, this theorem does not close the unrestricted minimax gap.

8. Computation and an exactly resolved population regime

Running time

For each supported type, the upper-bound algorithm solves four rational linear programs:

  • two programs computing the players’ maximin values;
  • one computing $\widehat z$;
  • one maximizing total payoff under the relaxed constraints.

Each has $O(m)$ variables and constraints. Quantized empirical means have bit length $O(\log(NT))$. Exact rational linear-programming solutions and the subsequent coefficients have encoding lengths polynomial in $m$ and $L+\log(NT)$.

Processing observations and generating assignments requires $O(NT)$ operations, apart from arithmetic and sampling. Rational categorical sampling can be implemented with expected polynomial bit cost. A conservative expected bit-operation bound is
$$ (NT+\tau)\operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,L+\log(NT)\right) +NT\,C_{\mathrm{quant}}(b), \qquad b=\left\lceil\log_2(16T)\right\rceil, $$
where $C_{\mathrm{quant}}(b)$ is the cost of obtaining one $b$-bit quantization of a reward pair.

If the observation interface returns that quantization in $O(b)$ bit operations, the last term is absorbed into the displayed polynomial bound. If rewards arrive as explicitly encoded rationals, their reading and quantization costs must instead be included. For arbitrary real observations with no specified access model, an unconditional bit-running-time statement is not defined; the mathematical regret bound does not require such a representation assumption.

A regime with matching unrestricted bounds

If every supported type satisfies
$$ n_g\ge K_g\sqrt T\,\ell_g, $$
then
$$ \left(\frac{K_gT^2\ell_g}{n_g}\right)^{1/3} \le\sqrt T. $$
The population upper bound is therefore $O(\sqrt T)$, while Section 6 gives the matching lower bound. Hence, in this regime,
$$ \mathcal R^*=\Theta(\sqrt T). $$

9. Remaining open point

For general populations, the proved unrestricted bounds still differ by a logarithmic factor. The new restricted-class theorem shows that simply sharpening the analysis of fixed exploration followed by commitment cannot remove that factor, even with four joint actions and one dyad.
