The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per capita capacities \(\kappa_s\) with \(\sum_s\kappa_s<1\), and list limits \(k,\ell\), decide whether \(M_{GS_k}>0\) and \(M_{BM_\ell}=0\). Each type records a student’s known priority tier, strict school ranking, and cardinal utilities; all schools share the tier order, with a common uniformly random tie break within each tier. In the population limit, evaluate each type’s truthful list and best permitted unilateral report by expected utility over its unknown tie break position, holding the aggregate society fixed; \(M_A\) is the total mass of types with a strictly profitable report under mechanism \(A\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) schools with positive binary-rational capacities \(\kappa_s\), where \(\sum_s\kappa_s<1\), and integer list limits \(1<k<m\), \(1\leq\ell\leq m\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type specifies a priority tier in a common ordered tier system, a strict ranking of all schools above the outside option \(\varnothing\), and binary-rational utilities strictly consistent with that ranking, normalized by \(u_t(\varnothing)=0\).
The nonatomic population is \(T\times[0,1]\), with measure \(\mu_t\,dx\) on type \(t\). All schools prioritize better tiers, then smaller lottery coordinates \(x\); the same coordinate is used at every school and in every round. Students know the society and their tier, but not their coordinate.
For limit \(d\), truthful reporting lists the top \(d\) schools; permitted reports are any ordered \(d\) distinct schools, chosen before observing \(x\). All other students report truthfully.
The constrained student-proposing deferred acceptance mechanism \(GS_k\), under these common priorities, is continuum serial dictatorship: process tiers in priority order and coordinates increasingly, assigning each type's flowing mass to its first listed school with remaining capacity, or to \(\varnothing\).
The constrained Boston mechanism \(BM_\ell\) uses immediate acceptance: in round \(r\leq\ell\), unassigned students apply to their report's \(r\)-th school; each school permanently admits the highest-priority applicant mass up to its remaining capacity. Exhausted lists yield \(\varnothing\).
A unilateral report changes only a tagged student of zero mass: aggregate capacity trajectories and round cutoffs stay fixed. Evaluate its assignment at each coordinate using those trajectories or cutoffs; reject at exact cutoff equality, which affects only a null set.
Let \(V_A(t,R)=\int_0^1u_t(A(t,x;R))\,dx\), and let \(M_A\) be the sum of \(\mu_t\) over types admitting a permitted report \(R\) with \(V_A(t,R)>V_A(t,R_t^{\mathrm{truth}})\).

Result

The decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in the stated model, including when \(k\) and \(\ell\) are part of the input.

The key distinction is between the two best-response problems:

  • For \(GS_k\), an optimal report can be found by a dynamic program over increasing availability cutoffs and decreasing utilities.
  • For \(BM_\ell\), each school has a fractional acceptance cutoff in at most one round. This permits a dynamic program that respects distinct-school reports without storing subsets of schools.

All computations below use exact rational arithmetic.

1. Acceptance cutoffs and report values

Fix a type \(t\), and write \(u_s=u_t(s)\). Because all schools rank above the outside option, \(u_s>0\).

For either mechanism, the tagged student's acceptance opportunity at a school is, up to endpoints of measure zero, an initial interval of lottery coordinates. Denote its endpoint by \(a\in[0,1]\): admission is possible precisely when \(x<a\).

Consider a report \((s_1,\ldots,s_d)\), and let \(a_j\) be its acceptance cutoff at position \(j\). For \(GS_k\), this cutoff depends only on the school; for \(BM_\ell\), it also depends on the round.

Define

\( b_0=0,\qquad b_j=\max\{b_{j-1},a_j\}. \)

After the first \(j-1\) applications or listed choices, the coordinates still unassigned are \([b_{j-1},1]\), up to null sets. At position \(j\), the newly assigned coordinates therefore have length \(b_j-b_{j-1}\). Consequently,

\( V_t(s_1,\ldots,s_d)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{s_j}(b_j-b_{j-1}). \)

This formula uses the conditional lottery distribution of type \(t\), so it contains no factor \(\mu_t\).

Serial-dictatorship cutoffs

A school either never exhausts its capacity or exhausts it at a particular tier and coordinate. Suppose its exhaustion occurs in tier \(p_s\), at coordinate \(c_s\). For a tagged type in tier \(p(t)\), its availability cutoff is

\( a_s= \begin{cases} 1,&p(t)<p_s,\\ c_s,&p(t)=p_s,\\ 0,&p(t)>p_s. \end{cases} \)

Here smaller tier indices mean better priority. A school that never exhausts has cutoff \(a_s=1\).

Indeed, capacity only decreases during serial dictatorship. Within a tier, the school has positive remaining capacity exactly before its exhaustion coordinate.

Boston cutoffs

Let \(h_s\) be the round in which school \(s\) fills, with \(h_s=\ell+1\) if it never fills during the mechanism.

If \(h_s\leq\ell\), record its admission boundary in that round as a tier \(p_s\) and coordinate \(c_s\). Define the tagged type's cutoff in the filling round by

\( \alpha_s= \begin{cases} 1,&p(t)<p_s,\\ c_s,&p(t)=p_s,\\ 0,&p(t)>p_s. \end{cases} \)

Its round-dependent cutoff is then

\( a_{s,r}= \begin{cases} 1,&r<h_s,\\ \alpha_s,&r=h_s\leq\ell,\\ 0,&r>h_s. \end{cases} \)

To justify this structure, before the filling round the school admits all truthful applicant mass and retains positive capacity. A zero-mass tagged applicant therefore obtains admission regardless of priority. In the filling round, admission is determined by the priority boundary. In every subsequent round, remaining capacity is zero.

In particular, a school can have a cutoff strictly between zero and one in at most one round.

2. Computing the truthful trajectories

Both mechanisms can be simulated exactly with polynomially many operations.

Serial dictatorship

Process the nonempty tiers in priority order. At a coordinate within a tier, each type flows to its highest-ranked school among its truthful top \(k\) that still has capacity.

Between capacity-exhaustion events, these choices remain constant. The flow rate into school \(s\) is therefore

\( w_s=\sum_{\substack{t\text{ in the current tier}\\ t\text{ currently chooses }s}}\mu_t. \)

If its remaining capacity is \(C_s\) and \(w_s>0\), its prospective time to exhaustion is \(C_s/w_s\). Advance to the earliest such event or the end of the tier, update capacities, and recompute choices.

Each school exhausts at most once. Thus there are at most \(m\) exhaustion events and at most \(\tau\) tier-ending events.

Reexamining each current type's top \(k\) at every event, and scanning all schools to update capacities, gives the arithmetic-operation bound

\( O\!\left(m\tau k+m(m+\tau)+\tau\log(\tau+1)\right). \)

The simulation records every school's exhaustion boundary.

Boston

Maintain, for each type \(t\), a coordinate \(b_t\) such that its truthful unassigned population is \([b_t,1]\), up to null sets. Initially \(b_t=0\).

This representation is preserved because every round's acceptance opportunity is an initial interval. If a type faces cutoff \(a\), its new unassigned boundary becomes \(\max\{b_t,a\}\).

In round \(r\), school \(s\)'s applicants are precisely the truthful types whose \(r\)-th school is \(s\), restricted to their current unassigned intervals. Their total mass is

\( D_s=\sum_{\substack{t:\,s\text{ is }t\text{'s}\\ r\text{-th truthful school}}}\mu_t(1-b_t). \)

If \(D_s<C_s\), all applicants are admitted and positive capacity remains. If \(D_s\geq C_s>0\), find the first tier whose cumulative applicant mass reaches \(C_s\). Within that tier, the applicant mass up to coordinate \(x\) is

\( \sum_{t\text{ applying in that tier}}\mu_t\max\{x-b_t,0\}. \)

This function is piecewise affine, with breakpoints among the \(b_t\). Sorting those breakpoints identifies the relevant segment and gives the cutoff by solving one affine equation. Its slope is positive: a positive remaining amount of capacity is being filled by positive applicant density.

If equality occurs at the end of a tier, use that tier with coordinate cutoff one. Later tiers are rejected, as required.

Grouping applicants by school and tier and sorting their boundaries costs

\( O\!\left(\ell\bigl(m+\tau\log(\tau+1)\bigr)\right) \)

arithmetic and comparison operations. The simulation records each school's filling round and admission boundary.

3. An exact best response for \(GS_k\)

Fix a type and its school cutoffs \(a_s\).

For any chosen set of schools, ordering them by decreasing utility weakly improves the outcome at every coordinate: the report then selects the highest-utility available school in that set. Therefore some optimal report follows the type's true ranking among its listed schools.

Read such a report in decreasing utility order. A school contributes positive assignment probability only if its cutoff strictly exceeds every earlier cutoff. Removing the other schools leaves a sequence \((s_1,\ldots,s_j)\) satisfying

\( u_{s_1}>\cdots>u_{s_j}, \qquad 0<a_{s_1}<\cdots<a_{s_j}, \qquad j\leq k. \)

Its value is

\( u_{s_1}a_{s_1} +\sum_{i=2}^{j}u_{s_i}(a_{s_i}-a_{s_{i-1}}). \)

Call such a sequence a productive chain.

Conversely, any productive chain of length at most \(k\) can be extended to a permitted report of exactly \(k\) distinct schools. Add unused schools and order the resulting set by decreasing utility. Adding schools cannot lower the highest available utility at any coordinate.

These two directions show that the maximum over permitted reports equals the maximum value of a productive chain of length at most \(k\).

Define \(F_j(s)\) as the largest value of a productive chain of exactly \(j\) schools ending at \(s\), using \(-\infty\) for an impossible state. Then

\( F_1(s)=u_sa_s \)

for \(a_s>0\), and, for \(j\geq2\),

\( F_j(s)= \max_{\substack{q:\,u_q>u_s\\a_q<a_s}} \left\{F_{j-1}(q)+u_s(a_s-a_q)\right\}. \)

The strict inequalities ensure that the appended school is distinct and that the resulting sequence is productive. Every productive chain of length \(j\) arises from exactly this kind of predecessor.

Thus the best-response value is

\( W^{GS}_t= \max\left(\{0\}\cup \{F_j(s):1\leq j\leq k,\ s\text{ a school}\}\right). \)

There are \(km\) states and at most \(m\) predecessors per state. The running time is \(O(km^2)\) arithmetic operations per type.

4. An exact best response for \(BM_\ell\)

Here reports cannot generally be sorted by utility. We instead use the filling-round structure.

Fix a type and write \(a_{s,r}\) for its Boston cutoffs. A partial report is nonterminal if it leaves a positive interval of coordinates unassigned, equivalently if its running maximum cutoff \(b\) satisfies \(b<1\).

The distinct-school lemma

For a nonterminal partial report of length \(r-1\), the following statements hold at round \(r\).

First, every previously listed school has current cutoff zero.
Suppose school \(s\) was listed at an earlier position \(j<r\). Its cutoff at that position could not have been one, since that would have assigned every remaining coordinate and made the report terminal. Hence \(a_{s,j}<1\). By the cutoff structure, \(h_s\leq j<r\), so \(a_{s,r}=0\).

Second, every school with current cutoff strictly between zero and one is unused.
Such a school fills in round \(r\). In every earlier round its cutoff was one, so listing it earlier would have made the report terminal.

Third, every school with current cutoff one is unused.
Its cutoff was also one in every earlier round, giving the same conclusion.

Let

\( Z_r=\left|\{s:a_{s,r}=0\}\right|. \)

The \(r-1\) previously listed schools are all in this zero-cutoff set. Therefore an unused zero-cutoff school exists if and only if

\( Z_r\geq r. \)

This condition depends only on the round, not on the identities of the previously listed schools. Positive-cutoff schools are automatically unused.

Dynamic program

Let

\( \mathcal B=\{0\}\cup\{\alpha_s:0<\alpha_s<1\}. \)

Its size is at most \(m+1\). Every nonterminal running maximum belongs to \(\mathcal B\).

Define \(D_r(b)\) to be the largest accumulated expected utility of a nonterminal partial report of length \(r\) with running maximum \(b\). Initialize

\( D_0(0)=0, \)

and set all other states to \(-\infty\).

At round \(r\), process each finite state \(D_{r-1}(b)=v\).

  • Choose a zero-cutoff school. If \(Z_r\geq r\), update

\( D_r(b)\gets\max\{D_r(b),v\}. \)

  • Choose a fractional-cutoff school. For each school with \(0<a_{s,r}<1\), set

\( b'=\max\{b,a_{s,r}\} \)
and update
\( D_r(b')\gets \max\{D_r(b'),\,v+u_s(b'-b)\}. \)

  • Choose a cutoff-one school. For each school with \(a_{s,r}=1\), record the terminal value

\( v+u_s(1-b). \)

A terminal partial report can always be completed to length \(\ell\) using unused schools: if it terminates at position \(r\), there are \(m-r\geq\ell-r\) unused schools. These later entries do not change any assignment, apart from null sets.

After round \(\ell\), the best-response value \(W^{BM}_t\) is the maximum of all recorded terminal values and all finite values \(D_\ell(b)\). In the latter case, coordinates above \(b\) receive the outside option and contribute zero utility.

Correctness

Induct on the report length.

Every extension of a nonterminal partial report falls into exactly one of the three cutoff categories. The distinct-school lemma gives the exact feasibility condition for the zero-cutoff category and proves that every positive-cutoff option is unused. The payoff increments follow from the formula in Section 1.

Conversely, each dynamic-program transition extends a valid partial report by an unused school. For a fixed state \((r-1,b)\), the available transition categories and their increments depend only on \(r\) and \(b\), not on the particular history realizing the state. Keeping only its largest accumulated value therefore loses no optimal continuation.

This proves that the program computes the maximum over all permitted distinct-school reports.

There are at most \(m+1\) states per round and at most \(m\) school transitions per state. Its running time is \(O(\ell m^2)\) arithmetic operations per type.

5. Exact arithmetic and input bit length

A polynomial number of rational operations alone would not establish polynomial bit complexity. We therefore bound all intermediate bit lengths.

Let \(D\) be the product of the denominators of all input masses, capacities, and utilities. Then

\( \log_2D=O(L). \)

Write \(\mu_t=M_t/D\), with positive integers \(M_t\). Since the masses sum to one,

\( \sum_tM_t=D. \)

For either truthful simulation, maintain conceptually a common denominator \(Q\) for all generated coordinate boundaries. Initially \(Q=1\).

Scaled remaining capacities, obtained by multiplying capacities by \(D\), have denominators dividing \(Q\):

  • Subtracting admitted mass introduces only input integer weights \(M_t\) and already generated coordinate boundaries.
  • Generating a new exhaustion or admission boundary requires division by an active flow rate. In scaled units that rate is an integer

\( A=\sum_{t\in S}M_t, \)
satisfying \(1\leq A\leq D\).

  • Thus a newly generated boundary has denominator dividing \(QA\), and we may replace \(Q\) by \(QA\).

There are at most \(m\) capacity-filling events in either mechanism. Consequently,

\( \log_2Q=O(mL). \)

Prospective event times considered during serial-dictatorship simulation involve one additional division by such a rate, so they obey the same asymptotic bit bound.

Expected utilities are sums of input utilities multiplied by differences of generated boundaries. Their denominators divide \(DQ\). Their magnitudes are at most the largest input school utility, whose bit length is bounded by \(L\). The dynamic-program values are likewise accumulated utilities of valid partial reports.

Therefore all stored rational numbers, and the temporary products used in elementary operations and comparisons, have bit length

\( B=O((m+1)L). \)

Using ordinary binary integer arithmetic, a reduced rational operation can conservatively be implemented in \(O(B^3)\) bit operations: multiplication and division have quadratic elementary bounds, and the Euclidean algorithm uses at most linearly many remainder steps. This suffices for an explicit polynomial bound.

6. The decision algorithm and running time

For each supported type, evaluate its truthful report using Section 1 and compute both optimal report values.

Because every supported type has positive mass,

\( M_{GS_k}>0 \iff \exists t:\ W^{GS}_t>V_{GS_k}(t,R_t^{\mathrm{truth}}). \)

Similarly,

\( M_{BM_\ell}=0 \iff \forall t:\ W^{BM}_t=V_{BM_\ell}(t,R_t^{\mathrm{truth}}). \)

The equality in the second statement is valid because the truthful report is permitted, so the optimal value cannot be smaller than its truthful value.

The total number of arithmetic and comparison operations is

\( O\!\left( m\tau k+m(m+\tau)+\tau\log(\tau+1) +\ell\bigl(m+\tau\log(\tau+1)\bigr) +\tau(k+\ell)m^2 \right). \)

Using the stated parameter restrictions, this simplifies to

\( O\!\left( \tau\bigl((k+\ell)m^2+\ell\log(\tau+1)\bigr) \right). \)

Including input reading and exact rational arithmetic, one conservative bit-operation bound is

\( O\!\left( L+ \tau\bigl((k+\ell)m^2+\ell\log(\tau+1)\bigr) ((m+1)L)^3 \right). \)

All strict profitability comparisons are exact. The algorithm operates directly on the \(\tau\) explicitly supported types and does not enumerate possible types or reports.
