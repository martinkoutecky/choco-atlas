4. Polynomial-size exact rational solutions

Existence alone does not establish that the requested exact output has manageable length. The next proposition supplies that guarantee.

Proposition 4. Every promised instance has an exact rational equilibrium whose total encoding length is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$.

Proof. Fix a subset $E\subseteq F$. Introduce prices $p_j$, aggregate expenditure variables $z_{tj}$, and a variable $\eta$. Maximize $\eta$ subject to
$$ \eta\ge0,\qquad p_j\ge\eta,\qquad \sum_jp_j=1, $$
$$ z_{tj}\ge0,\qquad z_{tj}=0\quad((t,j)\notin E), $$
$$ \sum_jz_{tj}=\mu_t\sum_jw_{tj}p_j\quad\text{for every }t, $$
$$ \sum_tz_{tj}=s_jp_j\quad\text{for every }j, $$
and
$$ d_{tj}p_\ell\le d_{t\ell}p_j \quad\text{whenever }(t,j)\in E,\ (t,\ell)\in F. $$

This is a rational linear program with polynomially many variables and constraints.

Take a real equilibrium $(p^*,x^*)$, whose existence was proved in Section 2, and choose
$$ E=\{(t,j):x_{tj}^*>0\}. $$
Then
$$ z_{tj}^*=\mu_tx_{tj}^*p_j^*, \qquad \eta^*=\min_jp_j^*>0 $$
satisfy every constraint. In particular, the comparison inequalities follow by cross-multiplying the equilibrium ratios with positive denominators. The optimal value is therefore positive.

The feasible region is closed and bounded:
$$ 0\le p_j\le1,\qquad 0\le\eta\le1,\qquad 0\le z_{tj}\le s_j. $$
The last bound follows from the column equation and nonnegativity. Thus the linear program has an optimal vertex.

Let $v$ be its number of variables. At a vertex, the active constraint normals span the full variable space. Otherwise, a nonzero direction orthogonal to all active normals would permit sufficiently small moves in both directions while preserving every constraint, contradicting extremality. Hence $v$ independent active constraints determine the vertex, including when equality constraints reduce the affine dimension.

All rational coefficients have polynomial bit length: they are input entries, products of input entries, or sums of polynomially many such products. Clearing denominators in each determining equation gives integer coefficients, including right-hand sides, with bit length bounded by some polynomial $H$.

By Cramer’s rule, each coordinate is a ratio of integer determinants. Every determinant in question has absolute value at most
$$ v!\,2^{vH}, $$
using the expansion into $v!$ products. Its bit length is at most
$$ vH+\log_2(v!)+1, $$
which is polynomial.

Choose an optimal vertex $(p,z,\eta)$. Its positive optimal value gives $p_j>0$. Define
$$ x_{tj}=\frac{z_{tj}}{\mu_tp_j}. $$
These are rational coordinates of polynomial encoding length.

Dividing a row equation by $\mu_t$ proves the type budget:
$$ \sum_jx_{tj}p_j=\sum_jw_{tj}p_j. $$
Dividing a column equation by $p_j$ proves weighted clearing:
$$ \sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}=s_j. $$
A positive allocation coordinate belongs to $E$, so the comparison constraints prove optimality against every allowed alternative. Forbidden alternatives have infinite effective disutility. $\square$

A proposed rational equilibrium can be checked in polynomial time in the combined input and output encoding lengths, using exact arithmetic. Restricting certificates to the polynomial bound just proved gives polynomial verification in the instance length.

There is also an exact finite algorithm: enumerate $E\subseteq F$, solve each linear program, skip infeasible programs and those with optimal value $0$, and recover an equilibrium from any positive optimum. Its running time is

Existence guarantees that some support succeeds.

5. A polynomial-time subclass: proportional disutilities within components

This section gives an additional result beyond the hardness and representation arguments.

Assume that, for each component $T_k\cup B_k$, there are positive rational numbers $\alpha_t,\beta_j$ satisfying
$$ d_{tj}=\alpha_t\beta_j \quad(t\in T_k,\ j\in B_k). $$

This condition is polynomial-time checkable. Choose $t_0\in T_k$ and $j_0\in B_k$, set
$$ \beta_j=d_{t_0j}, \qquad \alpha_t=\frac{d_{tj_0}}{d_{t_0j_0}}, $$
and check every displayed factorization.

Proposition 5. Under this additional hypothesis, an exact rational equilibrium can be computed in polynomial time. Its normalized price vector is unique.

5.1 Necessary price proportions

At any equilibrium, positive supply and clearing imply that every chore $j\in B_k$ is assigned positively to some type in $T_k$.

For that type, optimality gives
$$ \frac{\beta_j}{p_j}\le\frac{\beta_\ell}{p_\ell} \quad\text{for every }\ell\in B_k, $$
after cancelling $\alpha_t>0$.

Applying this argument to every chore gives equality of all these ratios. Consequently,
$$ p_j=q_k\beta_j\quad(j\in B_k) $$
for some $q_k>0$.

Define
$$ b_k=\sum_{j\in B_k}s_j\beta_j>0 $$
and
$$ A_{kh} =\sum_{t\in T_k}\sum_{j\in B_h}\mu_tw_{tj}\beta_j. $$

Summing the budgets of types in $T_k$ and using clearing gives
$$ b_kq_k=\sum_hA_{kh}q_h. $$
Moreover,
$$ \sum_kA_{kh}=b_h. $$

Set
$$ r_k=b_kq_k, \qquad M_{kh}=\frac{A_{kh}}{b_h}. $$
The price equations become
$$ r=Mr. $$
The matrix $M$ is nonnegative and column stochastic:
$$ \sum_kM_{kh}=1. $$

If the task’s component graph has $k\to h$, then $A_{kh}>0$. Thus the directed graph with arcs $h\to k$ whenever $M_{kh}>0$ contains the reverse of a strongly connected graph. It is itself strongly connected.

5.2 Existence and uniqueness of the stationary vector

Here is an elementary proof sufficient for the algorithm.

Take any probability vector $u$ and form
$$ v_N=\frac1N\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}M^\ell u. $$
Every $v_N$ belongs to the probability simplex, and
$$ Mv_N-v_N=\frac{M^Nu-u}{N}\longrightarrow0. $$
Compactness gives a convergent subsequence with limit $r$. Continuity gives
$$ Mr=r,\qquad r\ge0,\qquad \sum_kr_k=1. $$

This stationary vector is strictly positive. If $r_h>0$ and $M_{kh}>0$, then
$$ r_k=\sum_\ell M_{k\ell}r_\ell\ge M_{kh}r_h>0. $$
Some coordinate is positive, and strong connectivity propagates positivity to every coordinate.

To prove uniqueness, let $r,r'$ be stationary probability vectors and define
$$ c=\max_k\frac{r'_k}{r_k}, \qquad z=cr-r'. $$
Then $z\ge0$, $Mz=z$, and at least one coordinate of $z$ is zero. If $z\ne0$, the same positivity-propagation argument makes every coordinate positive, a contradiction. Hence $r'=cr$; normalization gives $c=1$.

The linear system
$$ (I-M)r=0,\qquad \sum_kr_k=1 $$
also has a unique solution among unrestricted real vectors. Otherwise, a second real solution differs from the positive solution by a nonzero stationary vector of coordinate sum zero. A sufficiently small nonzero multiple of that difference could be added to the positive solution while retaining positivity, contradicting uniqueness among probability vectors.

Therefore exact rational Gaussian elimination computes $r$ in polynomial time, with polynomial bit length by the determinant bound used in Section 4.

5.3 Recovering prices and bundles

Define
$$ \widetilde p_j=\frac{r_k}{b_k}\beta_j \quad(j\in B_k), \qquad p_j=\frac{\widetilde p_j}{\sum_\ell\widetilde p_\ell}. $$
These are positive normalized rational prices. They satisfy all component budget-balance equations.

Let
$$ e_t=\sum_jw_{tj}p_j, \qquad R_k=\sum_{j\in B_k}s_jp_j>0. $$
Component balance gives
$$ \sum_{t\in T_k}\mu_te_t=R_k. $$

For $t\in T_k$, assign
$$ x_{tj}=\frac{e_t}{R_k}s_j\quad(j\in B_k), \qquad x_{tj}=0\quad(j\notin B_k). $$

The type budget holds because
$$ \sum_jx_{tj}p_j =\frac{e_t}{R_k}\sum_{j\in B_k}s_jp_j =e_t. $$
For $j\in B_k$, clearing holds because
$$ \sum_t\mu_tx_{tj} =\frac{s_j}{R_k}\sum_{t\in T_k}\mu_te_t =s_j. $$

Finally, all allowed chores have the same disutility-to-price ratio for each type:
$$ \frac{d_{tj}}{p_j} =\frac{\alpha_t\beta_j}{q_k\beta_j} =\frac{\alpha_t}{q_k}. $$
Thus the allocation is optimal, including when $e_t=0$.

All computations use polynomially many rational arithmetic operations with polynomial bit lengths. Necessity of the price proportions and uniqueness of $r$ prove uniqueness of normalized prices. $\square$

This subclass includes every promised instance in which each component contains only one type, or each component contains only one chore.

6. What remains open about the requested dichotomy

The general problem is $\mathsf{PPAD}$-hard, so a polynomial-time algorithm would yield polynomial-time algorithms for every $\mathsf{PPAD}$ search problem. That implication does not establish $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness.

There is also a precise obstruction to one standard interpretation of $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness.

Suppose there were polynomial-time functions $f$ and $D$ such that:

  • For every Boolean formula $\varphi$, $f(\varphi)$ is a promised market.
  • For every valid exact equilibrium $z$ of $f(\varphi)$, $D(\varphi,z)$ correctly decides whether $\varphi$ is satisfiable.

Then $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$.

Indeed, an unsatisfiable formula has a polynomial-size certificate consisting of an equilibrium $z$ of $f(\varphi)$. Its existence and length follow from Sections 2 and 4. A polynomial-time verifier checks the equilibrium and checks that $D(\varphi,z)$ returns “unsatisfiable.” For a satisfiable formula, the decoder hypothesis excludes every accepting certificate. Thus unsatisfiability belongs to $\mathsf{NP}$, implying the stated equality.

Equivalently, the promises are polynomial-time checkable, and the polynomially bounded equilibrium relation can be extended to all inputs by permitting a distinguished output for invalid instances. This yields a total polynomial search formulation.

These observations do not exclude every possible oracle-hardness notion. They show why the reduction notion matters, and why the established $\mathsf{PPAD}$-hardness cannot be relabeled as $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness.

The unresolved general step is to obtain either a polynomial-time equilibrium algorithm beyond the proportional-disutility subclass, or an $\mathsf{NP}$-hardness reduction under an explicitly specified notion.
