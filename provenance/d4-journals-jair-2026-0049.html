The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given ‎\(\tau\) types with rational masses ‎\(\mu_t>0\) summing to ‎\(1\), a rational type-to-type contact-rate kernel ‎\(K\), and a per-capita budget ‎\(\kappa\), choose type-level seed probabilities ‎\(d_t\in[0,1]\) with ‎\(\sum_t\mu_t d_t\le\kappa\) to maximize limiting active mass ‎\(\Phi_K(d)=\sum_t\mu_t a_t^{(\infty)}\), where ‎\(a_t^{(0)}=d_t\) and ‎\(a_t^{(r+1)}=1-(1-d_t)\exp(-\sum_s\mu_sK_{st}a_s^{(r)})\). Does there exist an algorithm, polynomial in ‎\(\tau\), input bit length ‎\(L\), and ‎\(1/\epsilon\), that returns a feasible rational ‎\(d\) with value at least ‎\((1-1/e-\epsilon)\) times optimum?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also contains a directed contact-rate matrix \(K\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau\times\tau}\), a binary-rational per-capita budget \(0<\kappa\le1\), and a binary-rational accuracy parameter \(0<\epsilon<1-1/e\). Entries of \(K\), including diagonal entries, are unrestricted nonnegative rates; \(K_{st}\) describes influence from type \(s\) to type \(t\).
An allocation is \(d\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t d_t\le\kappa\). Define \(a_t^{(0)}=d_t\) and \(a_t^{(r+1)}=1-(1-d_t)\exp(-\sum_s\mu_sK_{st}a_s^{(r)})\) for \(r\ge0\).
Use the componentwise limit of this iteration, \(a^{(\infty)}\), to define \(\Phi_K(d)=\sum_t\mu_t a_t^{(\infty)}\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sup\{\Phi_K(d):d\in[0,1]^\tau,\ \sum_t\mu_td_t\le\kappa\}\).
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length. Computation uses the ordinary bit model with no value or gradient oracle; there is no separate parameter \(m\) in this model.

Does there exist an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\) that returns a feasible rational allocation \(d\) satisfying \(\Phi_K(d)\ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\)?

For finite independent-cascade networks with independent initial activation probabilities and a unit-cost incentive budget, MLE-Greedy achieves a \((1-1/e)\) approximation using a discrete influence subroutine ([Umrawal et al.](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.20410)).
Finite-network independent-cascade seed selection also admits a \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\) approximation ([Borgs et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.0884)). No result settling the stated mass-based iteration problem was identified in the search.

  • The budget's domain was unspecified: use a positive binary-rational per-capita budget at most 1, corresponding to the total normalized population mass.
  • The accuracy parameter's domain was unspecified: use binary-rational epsilon strictly between 0 and 1-1/e.

Result

Yes. There is a deterministic algorithm with the requested running time and approximation guarantee. The proof uses an explicit convex program, together with certified finite-precision computations.

Write \(c=1-1/e\), and define the nonnegative matrix \(B\) by \(B_{ts}=\mu_sK_{st}\). Thus \((Ba)_t=\sum_s\mu_sK_{st}a_s\).

1. Basic properties of the iteration

For an allocation \(d\), let

\[ M_d(a)_t=1-(1-d_t)\exp(-(Ba)_t). \]

The map \(M_d\) is componentwise nondecreasing in both \(a\) and \(d\). Moreover, \(M_d(d)\ge d\), and \(M_d\) maps \([0,1]^\tau\) into itself. Consequently, the prescribed iteration increases componentwise and has a limit. Continuity of \(M_d\) implies that this limit is a fixed point.

Every fixed point \(x\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfies \(x=M_d(x)\ge d\). Induction therefore shows that the prescribed limit is the least fixed point in this cube. Comparing iterations also proves that \(\Phi_K\) is componentwise nondecreasing.

We will use the bounds

\[ \kappa\le\mathrm{OPT}\le1. \]

The lower bound follows from the feasible uniform allocation \(d_t=\kappa\), whose limiting activation is at least its initial activation. The upper bound follows from \(a_t^{(\infty)}\le1\).

2. An exponential change of variables

For \(h\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^\tau\), define

\[ q(h)_t=1-\exp(-h_t),\qquad T_h(a)_t=1-\exp(-h_t-(Ba)_t). \]

Let \(b^*(h)\) be the limit of the iteration starting at \(q(h)\), and put \(F(h)=\sum_t\mu_tb_t^*(h)\). By definition,

\[ F(h)=\Phi_K(q(h)). \]

Concavity

Each coordinate of \(T_h(a)\) is jointly concave in \((h,a)\) and nondecreasing in \(a\). The initial vector \(q(h)\) is componentwise concave in \(h\).

Inductively, every finite iterate is componentwise concave in \(h\): first use monotonicity in \(a\) to insert the concavity inequality for the preceding iterate, and then use joint concavity of \(T\). Passing to the componentwise limit preserves these inequalities.

Thus \(F\) is concave and nondecreasing on \(\mathbb R_{\ge0}^\tau\), and \(F(0)=0\). In particular,

\[ F(\alpha h)\ge\alpha F(h) \quad\text{for }0\le\alpha\le1. \]

The approximation comparison

For every \(d\in[0,1]^\tau\),

\[ F(d)\ge c\,\Phi_K(d). \]

Here \(d\) on the left is the argument of \(F\), so the corresponding initial activation is \(q(d)\).

To prove this, fix \(x\in[0,1]\) and \(z\ge0\), and define

\[ H_x(z)=1-\exp(-x-cz)-c\bigl(1-(1-x)\exp(-z)\bigr). \]

Its derivative satisfies

\[ H_x'(z) =c\bigl(\exp(-x-cz)-(1-x)\exp(-z)\bigr)\ge0, \]

because \(\exp(-x)\ge1-x\) and \(\exp(-cz)\ge\exp(-z)\). Concavity of \(x\mapsto1-\exp(-x)\), applied to its endpoints on \([0,1]\), gives

\[ H_x(0)=1-\exp(-x)-cx\ge0. \]

Therefore

\[ 1-\exp(-x-cz) \ge c\bigl(1-(1-x)\exp(-z)\bigr). \]

Let \(a^{(r)}\) be the original iteration for \(d\), and let \(b^{(r)}\) be the iteration for \(q(d)\). Initially, \(b^{(0)}\ge ca^{(0)}\). If \(b^{(r)}\ge ca^{(r)}\), nonnegativity of \(B\) and the scalar inequality above give

\[ b^{(r+1)}=T_d(b^{(r)})\ge T_d(ca^{(r)}) \ge ca^{(r+1)}. \]

Taking limits and then weighted sums proves the comparison.

3. Positive seeding makes subsolutions valid lower bounds

Suppose every coordinate of \(h\) is positive. We claim that every \(u\in[0,1]^\tau\) satisfying \(u\le T_h(u)\) also satisfies \(u\le b^*(h)\).

First, \(b^*(h)\ge q(h)>0\). Suppose, toward a contradiction, that

\[ r=\max_t\frac{u_t}{b_t^*(h)}>1. \]

The map \(T_h\) is concave on the entire nonnegative orthant. Applying concavity to

\[ b^*(h)=\frac1r\bigl(rb^*(h)\bigr) +\left(1-\frac1r\right)0 \]

gives

\[ T_h(rb^*(h)) \le rT_h(b^*(h))-(r-1)T_h(0) <rb^*(h) \]

componentwise. The strict inequality uses \(T_h(0)=q(h)>0\).

Choose a coordinate \(j\) attaining the maximum ratio. Since \(u\le rb^*(h)\), monotonicity implies

\[ u_j\le T_h(u)_j \le T_h(rb^*(h))_j <rb_j^*(h)=u_j, \]

a contradiction.

This proves the claim. It also proves uniqueness of the fixed point when \(h>0\).

Positivity matters: with one type, contact rate \(2\), and zero seeding, the prescribed iteration stays at zero, although \(a=1-\exp(-2a)\) has a positive fixed point. We will enforce positive seeding explicitly.

4. The convex program and its approximation bound

Set

\[ \sigma=\frac{\epsilon}{16},\qquad \delta=\sigma\kappa,\qquad b=(1-2\sigma)\kappa. \]

Since \(\epsilon<1\), we have \(0<\delta\le\sigma<1/16\).

For \(a_t<1\), define

\[ g_t(a)=-\log(1-a_t)-(Ba)_t. \]

Each \(g_t\) is convex, and \(g_t(0)=0\).

Consider the compact convex set \(C\) of pairs \((a,h)\) satisfying

\[ 0\le a_t\le1-\delta,\qquad \delta\le h_t\le1,\qquad \sum_t\mu_th_t\le b,\qquad g_t(a)\le h_t \]

for every type \(t\). Let \(V\) be the maximum of the linear objective \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t\) on \(C\).

The last inequalities are equivalent to \(a\le T_h(a)\). Because \(h_t\ge\delta>0\), Section 3 implies that every feasible pair satisfies

\[ \sum_t\mu_ta_t\le F(h). \]

We next prove

\[ V\ge(c-4\sigma)\mathrm{OPT}. \]

Take any feasible allocation \(d\) for the original problem and set

\[ h_d=(1-3\sigma)d+\delta\mathbf1. \]

Its coordinates lie in \([\delta,1]\), and

\[ \sum_t\mu_t(h_d)_t \le(1-3\sigma)\kappa+\delta=b. \]

By monotonicity, concavity, and Section 2,

\[ F(h_d)\ge F((1-3\sigma)d) \ge(1-3\sigma)F(d) \ge(1-3\sigma)c\,\Phi_K(d). \]

Write \(x=b^*(h_d)\). Every coordinate of \(x\) is strictly below \(1\), because \(h_d\) and \(B\) are finite and \(x=T_{h_d}(x)\). Its fixed-point equation gives \(g_t(x)=(h_d)_t\).

Convexity and \(g_t(0)=0\) imply

\[ g_t((1-\delta)x) \le(1-\delta)g_t(x) \le(h_d)_t. \]

Consequently, \(((1-\delta)x,h_d)\in C\), and hence

\[ V\ge(1-\delta)(1-3\sigma)c\,\Phi_K(d). \]

This holds for every feasible \(d\), whether or not the original supremum is attained. Taking the supremum and using \(\delta\le\sigma\) yields

\[ V\ge(1-4\sigma)c\,\mathrm{OPT} \ge(c-4\sigma)\mathrm{OPT}. \]

5. Polynomial bit complexity

We now establish that this convex program can be approximately optimized in the ordinary bit model.

Explicit inner and outer balls

Define

\[ G=\delta^{-1}+1+\max_t\sum_sB_{ts}. \]

For \(f_t(a,h)=g_t(a)-h_t\), the gradient on the bounding box satisfies

\[ \|\nabla f_t(a,h)\|_1 \le\delta^{-1}+\sum_sB_{ts}+1\le G. \]

Thus each \(f_t\) is \(G\)-Lipschitz in the maximum norm on that box.

Take the rational center

\[ z_0=(a_0,h_0),\qquad a_0=\frac{\delta}{4}\mathbf1,\qquad h_0=2\delta\mathbf1. \]

Using \(-\log(1-u)\le u/(1-u)\) gives

\[ f_t(z_0) \le-\log(1-\delta/4)-2\delta \le-\frac{3\delta}{2}. \]

The budget slack at \(z_0\) is

\[ b-\sum_t\mu_t(h_0)_t=(1-4\sigma)\kappa>\kappa/2. \]

Set

\[ r=\frac{\delta}{16(G+1)},\qquad R=2\tau. \]

The Euclidean ball of radius \(r\) about \(z_0\) lies in \(C\). Indeed:

  • Each box constraint has slack greater than \(r\).
  • A perturbation of Euclidean norm at most \(r\) changes the budget by at most \(r\), since \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\).
  • Each nonlinear constraint changes by at most \(Gr<\delta/16\), less than its displayed slack.

Also, \(C\) lies in the Euclidean ball of radius \(R\) about \(z_0\), because both \(C\) and \(z_0\) lie in \([0,1]^{2\tau}\).

These radii and the center have polynomial encoding length. Large contact rates enter through the binary encoding of \(G\), rather than through a dependence polynomial in their numerical magnitude.

An implemented weak separation routine

Given a rational query point \(y=(a,h)\) and rational tolerance \(t>0\), first check all linear constraints exactly. A violated linear constraint supplies a rational separating hyperplane.

Otherwise, \(y\) is in the bounding box and satisfies the budget. Put

\[ u=\frac{t\delta}{4R}. \]

Approximate each \(f_t(y)\) to absolute error at most \(u/4\).

If an approximation exceeds \(u/2\), the corresponding true value is positive. Its gradient is rational:

\[ \nabla f_t(a,h) =\left(\frac{e_t}{1-a_t}-B_{t\bullet},-e_t\right). \]

For every \(z\in C\), convexity gives

\[ \nabla f_t(y)\cdot(z-y) \le f_t(z)-f_t(y)<0. \]

Thus this gradient supplies a genuine rational separating hyperplane.

If no approximation exceeds \(u/2\), all true constraint values are at most \(u\). Define

\[ \lambda=\frac{u}{u+\delta},\qquad z=(1-\lambda)y+\lambda z_0. \]

The linear constraints remain satisfied. Since \(f_t(y)\le u\) and \(f_t(z_0)\le-\delta\), convexity gives \(f_t(z)\le0\), so \(z\in C\). Moreover,

\[ \|z-y\|_2 \le\lambda R\le\frac{Ru}{\delta}=\frac t4. \]

The routine therefore correctly certifies proximity to \(C\).

Only logarithms require transcendental computation in this routine. They can be computed with certified error in polynomial time. Explicitly, scale a positive rational argument as \(2^{-k}v\), where \(1\le v<2\), and use

\[ \log v =2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^{2j+1}}{2j+1}, \qquad w=\frac{v-1}{v+1}\in[0,1/3]. \]

The tail decreases geometrically; the same series computes \(\log2\). Rational partial sums and tail bounds give the required certified precision with polynomially many bit operations.

Applying weak convex optimization

We use the standard weak separation-to-optimization theorem: for a compact convex body with explicitly supplied inner and outer balls, polynomial-time weak separation permits polynomial-time linear optimization with both additive objective error and distance-to-feasibility error bounded by a prescribed rational tolerance. This is the bit-model theorem of [Grötschel, Lovász, and Schrijver, Sections 1–3](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/10046/10046D.pdf).

All its hypotheses have just been verified. Apply it with objective \((\mu,0)\), whose Euclidean norm is at most \(1\), and tolerance

\[ \theta=\frac{\delta}{32(G+1)}=\frac r2. \]

It returns a rational point \((\widetilde a,\widetilde h)\) satisfying

\[ \operatorname{dist}((\widetilde a,\widetilde h),C)\le\theta, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t\widetilde a_t\ge V-\theta. \]

The routine and the resulting optimization algorithm have polynomial bit complexity. In particular, the encoding lengths of \(\delta,G,r,R,\theta\) are polynomial in the input length.

6. Rational output and the final guarantee

Clip \(\widetilde a_t\) to \([0,1-\delta]\) and \(\widetilde h_t\) to \([\delta,1]\), obtaining rational vectors \(\bar a,\bar h\).

Choose a closest point of \(C\). Coordinatewise clipping cannot increase distance to that point. Each clipped coordinate is therefore within \(\theta\) of its corresponding coordinate in \(C\). It follows that

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\bar a_t\ge V-2\theta, \qquad \sum_t\mu_t\bar h_t\le b+\theta, \qquad g_t(\bar a)-\bar h_t\le G\theta. \]

Define \(h^+=\bar h+G\theta\mathbf1\). Then \(h^+>0\) and \(\bar a\le T_{h^+}(\bar a)\). Section 3 gives

\[ F(h^+)\ge\sum_t\mu_t\bar a_t. \]

Set \(\zeta=\delta/32\). Compute rational coordinates \(d_t\in[0,1]\) satisfying

\[ 1-\exp(-h_t^+) \le d_t \le1-\exp(-h_t^+)+\zeta. \]

This is polynomial-time certified upward rounding. Indeed, \(h_t^+<2\); Taylor expansion of the bounded-argument exponential has factorial remainder bounds, and a sufficiently fine dyadic rounding adds at most the prescribed error.

Because \(1-\exp(-x)\le x\) for \(x\ge0\),

\[ \sum_t\mu_td_t \le b+(G+1)\theta+\zeta =b+\frac{\delta}{16}<\kappa. \]

Thus the rational output is feasible.

Monotonicity of \(\Phi_K\), followed by the preceding bounds, gives

\[ \Phi_K(d) \ge F(h^+) \ge V-2\theta \ge(c-4\sigma)\mathrm{OPT}-2\theta. \]

Finally, \(2\theta\le\delta=\sigma\kappa\le\sigma\mathrm{OPT}\), so

\[ \Phi_K(d) \ge(c-5\sigma)\mathrm{OPT} \ge(1-1/e-\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}. \]

The construction uses polynomially many bit operations in \(\tau\) and \(L\), and therefore satisfies the requested polynomial bound in \(\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). It computes neither values nor gradients of the original limiting objective.
