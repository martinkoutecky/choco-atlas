The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\) indivisible goods, a known bundle distribution \(\mathcal D\) over \(2^{[n]}\), and a society with support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and one gross-substitutes valuation \(f_t\) per type, how well can a learner using value queries to the \(f_t\) approximate them? Find the smallest achievable \(\alpha\) such that, with confidence at least \(1-\delta\), its polynomially evaluable hypotheses satisfy \(\Pr_{t\sim\mu,\,S\sim\mathcal D}[\hat f_t(S)\le f_t(S)\le\alpha\hat f_t(S)]\ge1-\epsilon\), with running time and query complexity measured in \(n\), \(|T|\), the input bit lengths, \(1/\epsilon\), and \(1/\delta\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(n=m\ge2\) indivisible goods. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible valuations.
Each type has an unknown monotone gross-substitutes valuation \(f_t:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with zeros allowed and no normalization imposed. An adaptive value query specifies \((t,S)\) and returns \(f_t(S)\) exactly.
The known bundle distribution \(\mathcal D\) is arbitrary and accessed through independent-sampling and exact point-probability oracles. Types and bundles are drawn independently. Let \(L\) bound the total bit length of the explicit input and the bit length of every rational oracle response; charge each oracle call and all processing of its response.
For binary-rational \(0<\epsilon,\delta<1/4\), a randomized learner must output nonnegative hypotheses \(\hat f_t\), evaluable without further access to the valuation oracles, such that \(\Pr_{\mathrm{learner}}[\Pr_{t\sim\mu,\,S\sim\mathcal D}[\hat f_t(S)\le f_t(S)\le\alpha\hat f_t(S)]\ge1-\epsilon]\ge1-\delta\). Hypotheses need not themselves be gross substitutes.
Running time, oracle-query complexity, output size, and hypothesis evaluation time must be polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon,1/\delta\).

What is the optimal worst-case asymptotic approximation factor \(\alpha(m,\tau)\) achievable under these requirements, uniformly over all admissible societies, valuations, and bundle distributions?

For a single valuation, factors \(o(m^{1/3}/\log m)\) are impossible with polynomially many observations, even with a known distribution and adaptive value queries ([Balcan–Constantin–Iwata–Wang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.5669v2)). Polynomial-time PMAC learning achieves \(O(\sqrt m)\) for the larger class of monotone nonnegative submodular valuations ([Balcan–Harvey](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ninamf/papers/learning-submodular-sicomp.pdf)).
No result settling the optimal factor for the stated mass-weighted model was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, as required by sampling a type from the society.
  • Specified access to the otherwise undefined known bundle distribution through sampling and exact point-probability oracles, without requiring an explicit list of all bundles.
  • Used nonnegative rational valuation values with bounded response bit lengths to make the requested bit complexity meaningful; retained monotonicity, permitted zeros, and imposed no empty-bundle normalization.
  • Allowed adaptive queries to any specified type and bundle, and required hypotheses to be evaluable without subsequent valuation queries.
  • Allowed improper nonnegative hypotheses, consistent with the anchor's learning model.
  • Specified confidence over all learner randomness, independent type and bundle draws, and polynomial bounds for output size and hypothesis evaluation.
  • Identified the goods parameter n with m in the required running-time convention and interpreted the smallest achievable factor asymptotically.

Partial solution: stronger bounds depending on the support size

I do not determine the optimal factor. The gap remains unresolved already for $\tau=1$. However, I prove two improvements over the bounds in the notes:

  • A learner using features indexed by small subsets achieves a factor of order $\sqrt{m/k}$, rather than $m/k$, when those features fit within the polynomial budget.
  • The lower-bound construction extends to some superpolynomial support sizes. If $\log(m+\tau)=o(m^{1/3})$, factors $o(m^{1/3}/\log(m+\tau))$ are impossible.

All algorithms below satisfy the stated bit-complexity, output-size, and evaluation-time requirements. Throughout, $\log$ denotes the binary logarithm, and $\ln$ denotes the natural logarithm.

1. Statements of the proved bounds

Fix a positive integer $d$, independent of the input. Define
$$ B_d=(m+\tau)^d, \qquad k_d=\max\left\{k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}: \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom mj\le B_d\right\}, $$
and
$$ D_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom mj. $$
The maximum defining $k_d$ exists because $m+1\le(m+\tau)^d$.

Proved upper bound. There is a uniform polynomial-time learner achieving
$$ \alpha_d(m,\tau) = \min\left\{ \frac{m}{k_d}, \left(1+\frac1m\right)D_{k_d}^{1/(2k_d)} \right\}. $$
In particular,
$$ \alpha_d(m,\tau) \le \min\left\{ \frac{m}{k_d}, \left(1+\frac1m\right)\sqrt{\frac{em}{k_d}} \right\}. $$
For $k_d=1$, the second expression is $O(\sqrt m)$.

Proved lower bound. Let $\tau=\tau(m)$ be any prescribed sequence of positive integers satisfying
$$ \log(m+\tau(m))=o(m^{1/3}). $$
No learner meeting the required uniform polynomial resource bounds can achieve
$$ \alpha(m,\tau(m)) = o\left(\frac{m^{1/3}}{\log(m+\tau(m))}\right) $$
uniformly over admissible inputs.

Resolved regime. If
$$ 2^m\le(m+\tau)^d $$
for some fixed $d$, exact learning is achievable. Consequently, for every fixed $c>0$, the optimal factor is $1$ throughout the regime $\tau\ge2^{cm}$, using a suitable fixed polynomial exponent.

The remaining sections prove these statements.

2. Mass-weighted error and normalization

For a hypothesis for type $t$, define its conditional error by
$$ e_t= \Pr_{S\sim\mathcal D} \left[ \hat f_t(S)\le f_t(S)\le\alpha\hat f_t(S) \text{ fails} \right]. $$
Independence of types and bundles gives joint error exactly
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t. $$

Suppose a single-type learner has conditional error at most $\epsilon$ with probability at least $1-\delta/\tau$. Applying it to every type and taking a union bound gives, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t \le \epsilon\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t = \epsilon. $$
This incurs polynomial overhead in $\tau$, with no dependence on the reciprocal of the smallest mass.

For one type, query
$$ c=f(\varnothing), \qquad g(S)=f(S)-c. $$
Then $c\ge0$, and $g$ is normalized, monotone, and nonnegative. Subtracting $c$ shifts every utility by the same constant, so it preserves demand correspondences and gross substitutes.

I use the established implication that gross-substitutes valuations are submodular, and the established fact that matroid rank functions are gross substitutes. Both are stated in [Balcan–Constantin–Iwata–Wang, Lemmas 1 and 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.5669v2). Thus $g$ satisfies the hypotheses used below.

3. A structural approximation for powers of a submodular valuation

The following lemma supplies the improved upper bound.

Lemma. Let $g:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ be normalized, monotone, and submodular. For every integer $1\le k\le m$, there are nonnegative real coefficients $w_A$, indexed by nonempty sets $A$ with $|A|\le k$, such that, for every $S\subseteq[m]$,
$$ \left(\sum_{\substack{\varnothing\ne A\subseteq S\\|A|\le k}}w_A\right)^{1/(2k)} \le g(S) \le D_k^{1/(2k)} \left(\sum_{\substack{\varnothing\ne A\subseteq S\\|A|\le k}}w_A\right)^{1/(2k)}. $$

3.1. Representation as a maximum of additive functions

For a permutation $\pi$ of $[m]$, let
$$ P_j=\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(j)\}, \qquad a_{\pi,\pi(j)}=g(P_j)-g(P_{j-1}). $$
Monotonicity gives $a_{\pi,i}\ge0$.

For any $S$, diminishing returns gives, whenever $\pi(j)\in S$,
$$ a_{\pi,\pi(j)} \le g\bigl((P_{j-1}\cap S)\cup\{\pi(j)\}\bigr) - g(P_{j-1}\cap S). $$
Summing over the elements of $S$ telescopes on the right, yielding
$$ \sum_{i\in S}a_{\pi,i}\le g(S). $$
If $\pi$ places all elements of $S$ first, equality holds. Therefore
$$ g(S)=\max_\pi\sum_{i\in S}a_{\pi,i}. $$

For each $\pi$, expand the $k$th power and group ordered tuples by their set of distinct elements. This gives nonnegative coefficients $b_{\pi,A}$ satisfying
$$ \left(\sum_{i\in S}a_{\pi,i}\right)^k = \sum_{\substack{\varnothing\ne A\subseteq S\\|A|\le k}}b_{\pi,A}. $$
Explicitly,
$$ b_{\pi,A} = \sum_{\substack{(i_1,\ldots,i_k)\in[m]^k\\ \{i_1,\ldots,i_k\}=A}} \prod_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{\pi,i_\ell}. $$
Since all additive values are nonnegative,
$$ g(S)^k = \max_\pi \sum_{\substack{\varnothing\ne A\subseteq S\\|A|\le k}}b_{\pi,A}. $$

These representations are used only to prove existence; the learner will not construct them.

3.2. An ellipsoidal bound for nonnegative linear forms

Here is the geometric fact needed for the lemma.

Let $\mathcal B$ be a finite collection of vectors in $\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{D}$, and define
$$ F(z)=\max_{b\in\mathcal B}\sum_{j=1}^{D}b_j|z_j|. $$
Remove coordinates that vanish in every vector of $\mathcal B$. If none remain, then $F=0$, and the desired bounds hold with zero coefficients.

Otherwise, let $q\le D$ be the number of remaining coordinates. On $\mathbb R^q$, $F$ is a norm: homogeneity and the triangle inequality follow from the displayed formula, and every nonzero coordinate is detected by some vector in $\mathcal B$.

Consequently,
$$ K=\{z:F(z)\le1\} $$
is compact, convex, centrally symmetric, and has nonempty interior.

The symmetric form of John’s theorem states that the unique maximum-volume ellipsoid $E$ contained in such a body satisfies
$$ E\subseteq K\subseteq\sqrt q\,E. $$
Existence, uniqueness, and this containment are recorded in [Güler–Gürtuna, Theorems 3.3 and 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0709.0707).

The body $K$ is invariant under every coordinate sign change. Uniqueness therefore makes $E$ invariant under these sign changes as well. Its center is zero, and its defining positive-definite quadratic form is diagonal: flipping one coordinate negates each off-diagonal entry involving that coordinate, so invariance forces that entry to vanish.

Write
$$ E=\left\{z:\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_jz_j^2\le1\right\}, \qquad \lambda_j>0. $$
Taking gauges in the two containments gives
$$ \frac1{\sqrt q} \left(\sum_j\lambda_jz_j^2\right)^{1/2} \le F(z) \le \left(\sum_j\lambda_jz_j^2\right)^{1/2}. $$
Set $w_j=\lambda_j/D$ on the retained coordinates and set the other coefficients to zero. Since $q\le D$,
$$ \left(\sum_jw_jz_j^2\right)^{1/2} \le F(z) \le \sqrt D\left(\sum_jw_jz_j^2\right)^{1/2}. $$

3.3. Application to subset features

Index coordinates by
$$ \mathcal A_k=\{A\subseteq[m]:1\le|A|\le k\}, $$
and define
$$ \phi_A(S)=\mathbf1[A\subseteq S]. $$
Thus the number of coordinates is $D_k$.

Apply the preceding geometric argument to the vectors $(b_{\pi,A})_{A\in\mathcal A_k}$. The power representation gives
$$ F(\phi(S))=g(S)^k. $$
Since $\phi_A(S)^2=\phi_A(S)$,
$$ \left(\sum_Aw_A\phi_A(S)\right)^{1/2} \le g(S)^k \le \sqrt{D_k} \left(\sum_Aw_A\phi_A(S)\right)^{1/2}. $$
Taking $k$th roots proves the lemma.

4. Learning the structural approximation with rational hypotheses

Fix $k$, and abbreviate $D=D_k$.

4.1. A feasible rational linear program

Draw $N$ independent bundles $S_1,\ldots,S_N$ and obtain
$$ y_j=g(S_j). $$
Solve the rational linear feasibility problem
$$ v_A\ge0, \qquad y_j^{2k} \le \sum_Av_A\phi_A(S_j) \le D\,y_j^{2k} \quad (1\le j\le N). $$

The structural lemma makes this feasible: take $v_A=Dw_A$.

Each $y_j$ has bit length $O(L)$, and $y_j^{2k}$ has bit length $O(kL)$. Hence the constraint encoding has polynomial size in $N,D,k,L$. Polynomial-time rational linear programming returns a feasible rational vector of polynomial encoding length.

When $y_j=0$, the constraints force the corresponding coordinate sum to vanish. No positive-value assumption is required.

4.2. Distribution-free generalization

For a nonnegative vector $v$, define
$$ E_v= \left\{ S: \sum_Av_A\phi_A(S)<g(S)^{2k} \ \text{or}\ \sum_Av_A\phi_A(S)>Dg(S)^{2k} \right\}. $$
The returned vector has $S_j\notin E_v$ for every training bundle.

Under the fixed feature map
$$ S\longmapsto\bigl(\phi(S),g(S)^{2k}\bigr)\in\mathbb R^{D+1}, $$
each $E_v$ is a union of two strict halfspaces. This feature map is needed only for analysis.

Affine halfspaces in $\mathbb R^{D+1}$ have VC dimension at most $r=D+2$. On $q\ge r$ points, their growth function is at most $(eq/r)^r$. Unions of two halfspaces consequently induce at most $(eq/r)^{2r}$ classifications. For $q=16r$,
$$ (16e)^{2r}<2^{16r}. $$
Thus the VC dimension of the error-range family is less than $16(D+2)$.

The VC epsilon-net theorem states that, for a range family of VC dimension at most $a$, an independent sample of size
$$ \frac{C}{\epsilon} \left(a\ln\frac1\epsilon+\ln\frac1\rho\right) $$
meets every range of probability greater than $\epsilon$, with probability at least $1-\rho$, for a universal sufficiently large $C$.

Its hypotheses hold here: the bundle domain is finite, the distribution is arbitrary, and the range family has the dimension bound just proved. Therefore it suffices to take
$$ N= \left\lceil \frac{C}{\epsilon} \left( (D+2)\ln\frac1\epsilon+\ln\frac{\tau}{\delta} \right) \right\rceil. $$
With probability at least $1-\delta/\tau$, every sample-consistent vector, including the selected vector, satisfies
$$ \mathcal D(E_v)\le\epsilon. $$

Outside $E_v$, the ideal hypothesis
$$ H_v(S)= \left(\frac1D\sum_Av_A\phi_A(S)\right)^{1/(2k)} $$
satisfies
$$ H_v(S)\le g(S)\le D^{1/(2k)}H_v(S). $$

4.3. Rational root evaluation

For rational $x\ge0$, construct a rational $R_{k,m}(x)$ satisfying
$$ \frac{x^{1/(2k)}}{1+1/m} \le R_{k,m}(x)\le x^{1/(2k)}. $$

For $x=0$, return zero. Otherwise find the integer $j$ satisfying
$$ 2^{2kj}\le x<2^{2k(j+1)}. $$
Its magnitude is bounded by a constant multiple of the encoding length of $x$.

Bisect the dyadic interval $[2^j,2^{j+1}]$, comparing the $2k$th power of each midpoint with $x$. After $O(\log m)$ steps, its width is at most $2^j/(2m)$. Return its lower endpoint $R$. Then
$$ R\le x^{1/(2k)}, \qquad R\ge \left(1-\frac1{2m}\right)x^{1/(2k)} \ge \frac{x^{1/(2k)}}{1+1/m}. $$
The last inequality follows from $m\ge1$.

All endpoints have polynomial bit length, and the comparisons use rational arithmetic on numbers of polynomial bit length in $k,m$ and the encoding length of $x$.

Output
$$ \hat f(S) = c+ R_{k,m}\left(\frac1D\sum_Av_A\phi_A(S)\right). $$
Let
$$ \gamma=\left(1+\frac1m\right)D^{1/(2k)}. $$
Outside $E_v$,
$$ \hat f(S)\le f(S), $$
and
$$ f(S)=c+g(S) \le c+\gamma R_{k,m}\left(\frac1D\sum_Av_A\phi_A(S)\right) \le\gamma\hat f(S). $$
Here $c\ge0$ and $\gamma\ge1$. If $g(S)=0$, the consistency inequalities also force the coordinate sum to be zero, so this argument covers zeros.

4.4. Resource accounting across types

Use the same $N$ independent bundles for all types, querying each type on each sampled bundle and on the empty bundle. The single-type analysis applies separately to every fixed target. A union bound gives the society guarantee from Section 2.

There are $\tau(N+1)$ valuation queries and $N$ sampling calls. The output stores the rational coefficients and empty-set values. Evaluating it requires containment tests, rational summation, and the root procedure above, without valuation-oracle access.

If $k=k_d$, then
$$ D+1\le(m+\tau)^d, \qquad k\le m. $$
Thus query complexity, processing time, output size, and evaluation time are polynomial in the parameters required by the task. Exact point probabilities are unnecessary.

5. Combining the power bound with the table bound

Query every bundle of size at most $k$, including the empty bundle, and define
$$ h(S)=\max\{f(A):A\subseteq S,\ |A|\le k\}. $$
Monotonicity gives $h(S)\le f(S)$.

For $|S|=s>k$, choose an additive vector $a_\pi$ from Section 3.1 with
$$ \sum_{i\in S}a_{\pi,i}=g(S). $$
Let $A$ contain the $k$ largest coordinates of this vector within $S$. Because the coordinates are nonnegative,
$$ \sum_{i\in A}a_{\pi,i} \ge \frac{k}{s}\sum_{i\in S}a_{\pi,i} = \frac{k}{s}g(S). $$
The additive lower-bound property gives
$$ f(A) \ge c+\sum_{i\in A}a_{\pi,i} \ge c+\frac{k}{s}g(S) \ge\frac{k}{s}f(S). $$
Consequently,
$$ h(S)\le f(S)\le\frac{m}{k}h(S). $$
For $|S|\le k$, the table hypothesis is exact.

For $k=k_d$, the table has at most $B_d$ entries per type. Its construction, storage, and evaluation by scanning entries therefore meet all resource requirements. Choosing whichever of the table and power learners has the smaller guaranteed factor proves
$$ \alpha_d(m,\tau) = \min\left\{ \frac{m}{k_d}, \left(1+\frac1m\right)D_{k_d}^{1/(2k_d)} \right\}. $$
This comparison is computable by raising positive quantities to the integer power $2k_d$.

To obtain the simpler bound, set $z=k/m\le1$. Since $z^j\ge z^k$ for $j\le k$,
$$ \sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom mj \le z^{-k}(1+z)^m \le \left(\frac{em}{k}\right)^k. $$
Therefore
$$ D_k^{1/(2k)}\le\sqrt{\frac{em}{k}}. $$

For example, if $\tau=\lceil2^{m^\beta}\rceil$ for fixed $0<\beta<1$, taking $d=1$ gives
$$ k_d=\Omega\left(\frac{m^\beta}{\log m}\right). $$
Indeed, for sufficiently large $m$, the integer
$$ k=\left\lfloor\frac{m^\beta}{4\log m}\right\rfloor $$
satisfies the preceding binomial bound and hence the budget constraint. Thus an achievable factor is
$$ O\left(m^{(1-\beta)/2}\sqrt{\log m}\right). $$
This is an upper bound, not a claim of optimality.

If $2^m\le B_d$, then $k_d=m$ and the table contains every bundle. Exact learning follows. For fixed $c>0$, choosing $d\ge1/c$ covers $\tau\ge2^{cm}$.

Factor $1$ is optimal in that regime: for a society of distinct positive constant valuations, the two required inequalities cannot hold anywhere when $\alpha<1$.

6. A lower bound for some superpolynomial support sizes

I give the probability quantifiers explicitly, including randomized adaptive queries.

6.1. The external matroid construction

The construction theorem used is the following consequence of [Balcan–Harvey, Theorem 1](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ninamf/papers/learning-submodular-sicomp.pdf).

For perfect-cube ground-set sizes $p=r^3$ and integers $K=2^u\ge8$ with $u=o(r)$, there exist, for sufficiently large instances, a family $\mathcal A$ of $K$ bundles and matroids
$$ \{M_{\mathcal B}:\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A\} $$
such that every $A\in\mathcal A$ has size $r$ and
$$ \operatorname{rank}_{M_{\mathcal B}}(A) = \begin{cases} 8u,&A\in\mathcal B,\\ r,&A\notin\mathcal B. \end{cases} $$
Their rank functions are admissible gross-substitutes valuations by the fact cited in Section 2.

6.2. A quantitative observation bound

Use the uniform distribution on $\mathcal A$, and choose $\mathcal B$ uniformly among its subsets. Thus the low/high choices on the $K$ support bundles are independent fair bits.

Suppose a learner makes at most $q$ queries to the unknown rank function on a ground set of size at most $m$. Assume
$$ \alpha<\frac{r}{8u}. $$
For any fixed hypothesis value $h(A)$, the admissible intervals for the two possible ranks are
$$ [8u/\alpha,8u] \quad\text{and}\quad [r/\alpha,r]. $$
They are disjoint. Hence a fixed hypothesis succeeds on each support bundle with probability at most $1/2$ over the random choice of $\mathcal B$.

The success indicators are independent because the hypothesis is fixed and the target labels are independent. Hoeffding’s inequality gives
$$ \Pr_{\mathcal B} \left[ \text{a fixed hypothesis succeeds on at least }3K/4 \text{ support bundles} \right] \le e^{-K/8}. $$

Fix all learner randomness, including its sampling randomness. Distribution-oracle replies are then fixed functions of the calls and do not depend on $\mathcal B$. Adaptive valuation queries form a decision tree with at most $m+1$ branches at each query, because each rank response is an integer in $\{0,\ldots,m\}$.

Padding shorter paths with dummy queries if necessary, the learner has at most $(m+1)^q$ possible output hypotheses. A union bound therefore gives
$$ \Pr_{\mathcal B} \left[ \text{the actual output succeeds on at least }3K/4 \text{ support bundles} \right] \le \exp\left(q\ln(m+1)-K/8\right). $$
This bound holds for every fixed random seed, so it also holds after averaging over learner randomness.

In particular, if
$$ K\ge16q\ln(m+1)+32, $$
the right-hand side is at most $e^{-4}$. Averaging over targets then shows that some fixed target has conditional error greater than $1/4$ with probability at least $1-e^{-4}$ over learner randomness.

This argument permits arbitrary adaptive queries and full knowledge of the distribution.

6.3. Embedding the target as a heavy type

For $\tau=1$, give the unknown target mass $1$.

For $\tau\ge2$, give it mass $\mu_1=1/2$. Put
$$ a=\lceil\log(\tau-1)\rceil, $$
and choose the remaining masses as
$$ \mu_t=2^{-(a+1)} \quad(2\le t\le\tau-1), \qquad \mu_\tau=\frac12-(\tau-2)2^{-(a+1)}. $$
These are positive dyadic rationals summing to $1$: positivity of the last mass follows from $\tau-2<2^a$.

For each padding type use the known constant valuation
$$ f_t(S)=t. $$
A constant valuation has the same demand correspondences as the zero valuation, so it is gross substitutes. The constants are distinct, and their positive empty-set values distinguish them from the normalized rank-function target.

If the society learner succeeds with $\epsilon=1/16$, its heavy-type conditional error satisfies
$$ e_1\le1/8 $$
when $\tau\ge2$, and $e_1\le1/16$ when $\tau=1$. Either conclusion is incompatible with conditional error greater than $1/4$.

6.4. Choosing parameters and checking bit lengths

Suppose, toward contradiction, that a uniform polynomial-resource learner achieves
$$ \alpha(m,\tau(m)) = o\left(\frac{m^{1/3}}{\log(m+\tau(m))}\right) $$
along a sequence satisfying
$$ \log(m+\tau(m))=o(m^{1/3}). $$
Fix $\epsilon=\delta=1/16$, and write $x=m+\tau$.

We may choose the input bit-length bound to be $L=x^3$. The masses and type identifiers have total encoding length $O(\tau\log(\tau+1))$, which fits within this bound for sufficiently large instances.

The learner’s polynomial resource guarantee then bounds its unknown-target query count by
$$ q\le Cx^b $$
for fixed constants $C,b$. Choose
$$ u= \left\lceil \log\bigl(16q\ln(m+1)+32\bigr) \right\rceil, \qquad K=2^u. $$
Then
$$ u=O(\log x)=o(m^{1/3}). $$

Set $r=\lfloor m^{1/3}\rfloor$ and $p=r^3$. Apply the construction on $p$ goods, and add $m-p$ matroid loops. This preserves all designated ranks and produces a matroid rank function on exactly $m$ goods. Since $r\sim m^{1/3}$,
$$ u=o(r), $$
so the construction’s hypothesis is satisfied.

The assumed factor bound implies, for sufficiently large instances,
$$ \alpha(m,\tau)<\frac{r}{8u}. $$
Also, the choice of $K$ satisfies the observation bound in Section 6.2.

The distribution is uniform on $K=2^u$ bundles. Its point probabilities are zero or $2^{-u}$ and have $O(u)$ bits. Samples require $m$ bits. Rank responses have $O(\log m)$ bits, and padding responses have $O(\log\tau)$ bits. Thus all oracle responses fit within $L=x^3$; no explicit listing of the distribution’s support is required by the model.

Section 6.2 now supplies an admissible target for which the probability of heavy-type accuracy on even three quarters of the support is at most $e^{-4}$. Society success would require accuracy on at least seven eighths of that support. Its probability is therefore at most $e^{-4}$, contradicting the required success probability $15/16$.

This proves the stated lower bound.

For polynomially bounded $\tau$, it recovers the obstruction to
$$ o(m^{1/3}/\log m). $$
For $\tau=\lceil2^{m^\beta}\rceil$ with fixed $0<\beta<1/3$, it rules out
$$ o(m^{1/3-\beta}). $$

7. What remains open

The mass-weighted requirement introduces no approximation loss in the upper bound, and a heavy type retains the information-theoretic obstruction.

Nevertheless, these bounds do not match. Already at $\tau=1$, they leave the obstruction to $o(m^{1/3}/\log m)$ versus an achievable $O(\sqrt m)$ factor. The power construction improves the upper bound when larger feature families fit within the budget, and the counting argument extends the lower bound beyond polynomial support sizes, but neither determines the optimal interpolation.

Thus the general target remains unresolved; the exact-learning exponential-support regime is resolved.
