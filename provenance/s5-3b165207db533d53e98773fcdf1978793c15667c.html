The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a rational type distribution μ over households with positive additive valuations \(u_{tj}\), and rational per-household supplies \(q_j\) of indivisible goods, set \(h_t=∑_j q_j u_{tj}\). A type-\(t\) bundle is a vector \(b∈ℤ_{≥0}^{J}\) with \(u_t·b≥ h_t\) such that removing any item makes its value fall below \(h_t\). Find assignment masses \(x_{t,b}≥0\) satisfying \(∑_b x_{t,b}≤μ_t\) and \(∑_{t,b} b_jx_{t,b}≤q_j\) for every good \(j\), that first maximize \(∑_{t,b}x_{t,b}\) and then maximize the worst preference-rank score among bundles assigned positive mass, with higher scores denoting more preferred bundles.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: \(\tau\) household types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\geq1\) good categories, with interchangeable indivisible copies, positive binary-rational per-household supplies \(q_j\), and \(\sum_jq_j\geq1\). Each type has positive binary-rational additive values \(u_{tj}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Set \(h_t=\sum_jq_ju_{tj}\). Define \(B_t\) as all \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^m\) satisfying \(u_t\cdot b\geq h_t\) and \(u_t\cdot(b-e_j)<h_t\) whenever \(b_j>0\), where \(e_j\) is the unit vector.
Preferences compare bundles by additive value, allowing ties. Set \(r_t(b)=1+|\{c\in B_t:u_t\cdot c>u_t\cdot b\}|\) and score \(s_t(b)=-r_t(b)\). Bundle sets and ranks are implicit in the input.
An assignment consists of masses \(x_{t,b}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_{b\in B_t}x_{t,b}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{b\in B_t}b_jx_{t,b}\leq q_j\). Each served household receives one minimal bundle; unserved households receive nothing, and supplies may remain unused.
Find an assignment lexicographically maximizing \((M(x),S(x))\), where \(M(x)=\sum_t\sum_{b\in B_t}x_{t,b}\) and \(S(x)=\min\{s_t(b):x_{t,b}>0\}\).

Is this exact lexicographic mass-allocation problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

Discrete: fallback allocation maximizes the number of households receiving minimal bundles and then optimizes their worst rank; its computational complexity is explicitly left unstudied. [Brams, Kilgour, and Klamler](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/56587/1/MPRA_paper_56585.pdf).
Continuous: no result settling exactly the problem defined above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the type distribution to total mass one, so supplies and proportional thresholds are measured per household.
  • Required positive supplies and total per-household item supply at least one, carrying over the discrete assumption that there are at least as many items as households; absent categories are omitted.
  • Interpreted coordinates as categories of interchangeable copies, permitting one category even though the discrete setting has at least two individual items; the atomless society replaces the discrete household-count restriction.
  • Derived bundle preferences from additive valuations, with equal values tied, rather than introducing separately supplied rankings.
  • Used competition ranks, counting strictly preferred bundles as specified by the fallback rule, and negated them to obtain scores with higher values preferred.
  • Kept all minimal integer bundles implicit, without bounding a household's bundle coordinates by per-household aggregate supplies.
  • Restricted each served household to one minimal bundle, left unserved households empty, and allowed unused supplies.

Result

The exact lexicographic allocation problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, already with one household type, positive integer utilities, and every category supply equal to \(\tfrac12\). In fact, deciding whether the maximum served mass equals \(1\) is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

We prove this by a polynomial-time reduction from PARTITION. The secondary rank objective does not need to be computed.

1. Full service forces exact-threshold bundles

Consider one type with mass \(\mu=1\), utility vector \(u>0\), supply vector \(q>0\), and threshold \(h=u\cdot q\). Write \(B\) for its minimal bundles.

First, \(B\) is finite. If \(b\in B\) and \(b_j>0\), minimality implies

\[ (b_j-1)u_j\le u\cdot(b-e_j)<h, \]

so \(b_j\le\lceil h/u_j\rceil\). Thus every coordinate has a finite bound. Consequently, the feasible allocation set is a closed, bounded subset of a finite-dimensional space, and its maximum mass \(M^*\) is attained.

Suppose an allocation serves mass \(1\). Its resource constraints give

\[ h=h\sum_{b\in B}x_b \le \sum_{b\in B}(u\cdot b)x_b =\sum_j u_j\sum_{b\in B}b_jx_b \le\sum_j u_jq_j=h. \]

Every inequality is therefore an equality. In particular,

\[ \sum_{b\in B}(u\cdot b-h)x_b=0. \]

Every summand is nonnegative. Hence every bundle receiving positive mass satisfies \(u\cdot b=h\).

Conversely, every nonnegative integer bundle of value exactly \(h\) belongs to \(B\): removing any present good reduces its value strictly below \(h\), because all utilities are positive.

These observations will connect full service to an integer equation.

2. Constructing the allocation instance

An instance of PARTITION consists of positive binary-encoded integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), with \(n\ge1\). Put \(W=\sum_i a_i\). The question is whether some subset \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies \(2\sum_{i\in I}a_i=W\). This problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; see [Karp’s original paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Define

\[ R=n+1,\qquad D=2nW+1,\qquad C=DR^{n-1}+2W,\qquad A=(n+1)C. \]

Construct one household type of mass \(1\), and \(2n\) categories indexed by \((i,+)\) and \((i,-)\). Their supplies and utilities are

\[ q_{i,+}=q_{i,-}=\tfrac12, \]

\[ u_{i,+}=A+DR^{i-1}+2a_i,\qquad u_{i,-}=A+DR^{i-1}. \]

All utilities are positive integers, and all supplies are positive binary rationals. Also, \(\sum_jq_j=n\ge1\). The threshold prescribed by the task is exactly

\[ h=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n(u_{i,+}+u_{i,-}) =nA+D\sum_{i=1}^nR^{i-1}+W. \]

The construction has polynomial bit length. Indeed, each utility has bit length bounded by \(O(n\log(n+1)+\log W)\), and there are \(2n\) utilities.

3. Exact-threshold bundles encode partitions

We prove that every nonnegative integer bundle \(b\) with \(u\cdot b=h\) chooses exactly one category from each pair and encodes a partition.

Exactly \(n\) copies

Let \(k=\sum_i(b_{i,+}+b_{i,-})\). Every utility lies between \(A\) and \(A+C\). Moreover,

\[ nA<h\le nA+nC<(n+1)A, \]

where the last inequality follows from \(A=(n+1)C\).

If \(k\ge n+1\), then \(u\cdot b\ge(n+1)A>h\), a contradiction.

If \(k\le n-1\), then

\[ u\cdot b\le(n-1)(A+C)<nA<h, \]

because \((n-1)C<A\). This is also a contradiction. Therefore \(k=n\).

The partition equation

Set \(d_i=b_{i,+}+b_{i,-}\). Subtracting \(nA\) from \(u\cdot b=h\) gives

\[ D\sum_i d_iR^{i-1}+2\sum_i a_i b_{i,+} =D\sum_iR^{i-1}+W. \]

Since the bundle contains \(n\) copies,

\[ 0\le2\sum_i a_i b_{i,+}\le2nW<D, \]

and \(0<W<D\). Reducing the equation modulo \(D\), both residues lie in the same complete residue range, so they must be equal:

\[ 2\sum_i a_i b_{i,+}=W. \]

Subtracting these equal terms and dividing by \(D\) yields

\[ \sum_i d_iR^{i-1}=\sum_iR^{i-1}. \]

Here \(0\le d_i\le n=R-1\). Thus both sides are base-\(R\) representations with valid digits, and uniqueness of that representation gives \(d_i=1\) for every \(i\). Explicitly, reduction modulo \(R\) gives \(d_1=1\); subtraction and division by \(R\), repeated, gives the remaining equalities.

Consequently, \(b_{i,+}\in\{0,1\}\) and \(b_{i,-}=1-b_{i,+}\). The subset \(I=\{i:b_{i,+}=1\}\) satisfies \(2\sum_{i\in I}a_i=W\), as required.

4. Partitions give full-service allocations

Suppose \(I\) is a partition. Define \(b\) by selecting \((i,+)\) when \(i\in I\), and \((i,-)\) otherwise. Thus \(b\) contains exactly one copy from each pair. Its value is

\[ u\cdot b=nA+D\sum_iR^{i-1}+2\sum_{i\in I}a_i=h. \]

Let \(\bar b=\mathbf1-b\) be its coordinatewise complement. Since \(\sum_j u_j=2h\), we also have \(u\cdot\bar b=h\).

Both bundles are minimal by Section 1. Assign mass \(\tfrac12\) to each. The served mass is \(1\), and every resource constraint holds with equality:

\[ \tfrac12b_j+\tfrac12\bar b_j=\tfrac12=q_j. \]

Thus a partition implies \(M^*=1\).

Conversely, if \(M^*=1\), an attaining allocation exists by Section 1. Every bundle in its positive support has value exactly \(h\), and its support is nonempty. Section 3 converts any such bundle into a partition.

We have therefore proved

\[ M^*=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (a_1,\ldots,a_n)\text{ is a yes-instance of PARTITION}. \]

5. Consequence for the lexicographic objective

The construction is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the decision problem asking whether full mass can be served. Hence exact mass maximization is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

Every lexicographically optimal allocation must maximize mass first. Therefore an exact polynomial-time algorithm returning such an allocation in the usual explicit rational-support representation would decide PARTITION by summing its allocated masses and testing whether the result equals \(1\).

The rank objective cannot alter this conclusion. On every constructed instance admitting full service, all bundles used by any full-service allocation have value \(h\), and hence have the same rank under the task’s tie convention.

Thus the exact lexicographic problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard and has no polynomial-time algorithm unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\). The reduction establishes ordinary hardness; no claim of strong hardness or membership of the full rank-sensitive problem in \(\mathrm{NP}\) is needed.
