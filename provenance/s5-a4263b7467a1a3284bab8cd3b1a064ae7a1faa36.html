The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(C\), an integer \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(b\), decide whether there are a committee \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=k\) and allocations \(x_{t,c}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for each \(c\in S\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}\operatorname{rank}_t(c)\le b\). Classify this problem, including its complexity when parameterized by \(p=\lceil b\rceil\), with input size measured using \(|C|\), \(|T|\), and the rational bit length.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a candidate set with \(m\ge 1\), let \(1\le k\le m\) be an integer, and let \(b\ge 0\) be rational.
A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
Define \(\operatorname{rank}_t(c)\) as the number of candidates preceding \(c\) in ranking \(t\); thus the first candidate has rank \(0\).
The decision problem asks whether there exist a committee \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|=k\), and divisible allocations \(x_{t,c}\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\) satisfying
\(\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,c}=1/k\) for every \(c\in S\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{c\in S}x_{t,c}\operatorname{rank}_t(c)\le b\).
Let \(L\) be the total binary input length and \(p=\lceil b\rceil\); state running times in \(m,\tau,L,k,p\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathsf P\) or \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard? If it is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard, is it fixed-parameter tractable with respect to \(p\)?

Discrete Monroe with Borda misrepresentation is NP-hard, W[2]-hard parameterized by committee size, and in XP for the total misrepresentation bound ([Betzler–Slinko–Uhlmann](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.0580)); it is W[1]-hard for that bound alone ([Chen–Roy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12006)).
Fractional Monroe assignments were considered by [Potthoff–Brams](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0951692898010002001); no complexity classification for this exact rational-support decision problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Resolved the unspecified rank convention using zero-based ranks, as in the discrete definition.
  • Restricted the candidate and committee domains to m >= 1 and 1 <= k <= m, ensuring a nonempty committee and defined quotas.
  • Restricted the rational budget to b >= 0, consistent with nonnegative misrepresentation and a nonnegative parameter.
  • Interpreted complete rankings as strict linear orders and support masses as strictly positive.
  • Made allocations nonnegative real quantities, allowing each type's mass to split among committee members.
  • Defined L as the total binary input length, including rankings, masses, committee size, and budget.

Result

The problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete even on instances with \(0<b<1\), hence with \(p=1\).

Consequently, parameterization by \(p=\lceil b\rceil\) is not fixed-parameter tractable, and is not even in \(\mathsf{XP}\), unless \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\). The distinction from the discrete total-misrepresentation parameter is essential: here \(b\) bounds a normalized average.

1. Fixed committees and membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

For a fixed committee \(S\) of size \(k\), the allocation constraints and budget inequality constitute a rational linear-programming feasibility problem with \(\tau k\) variables. Its coefficients have polynomial encoding length: the ranks are integers between \(0\) and \(m-1\), and the remaining coefficients are input rationals or \(1/k\).

Polynomial-time solvability of rational linear programming therefore gives an exact feasibility test in time

\(\displaystyle (m\tau+\tau k+L)^{O(1)}.\)

A certificate can consist solely of the committee: the verifier performs this polynomial-time test. Thus the decision problem belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\).

Enumerating committees also gives an unconditional algorithm with running time

\(\displaystyle \binom{m}{k}(m\tau+\tau k+L)^{O(1)}.\)

In particular, fixed \(k\) gives polynomial time.

2. Integrality when the quotas are integers

We first establish why divisible allocations preserve a suitable discrete hardness result.

Lemma. Let \(a_1,\ldots,a_s\) and \(Q\) be positive integers satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_i=kQ\). Fix \(k\) selected candidates and integer assignment costs \(r_{i,c}\). Among matrices satisfying

\(\displaystyle y_{i,c}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_c y_{i,c}=a_i,\qquad \sum_i y_{i,c}=Q,\)

there is an integral minimum-cost matrix.

Proof. The feasible set is nonempty: assigning \(a_i/k\) to every selected candidate in row \(i\) satisfies the constraints. It is compact, since every entry lies between \(0\) and \(a_i\). A linear objective therefore attains its minimum at an extreme point.

Construct the bipartite graph whose vertices are rows and columns, with an edge for each positive entry of an extreme point. This graph cannot contain a cycle. Otherwise, adding and subtracting a sufficiently small positive quantity alternately around that cycle produces two distinct feasible matrices whose midpoint is the original matrix, contradicting extremality.

The positive-entry graph is consequently a forest. At a leaf, its unique incident entry equals that vertex’s prescribed margin, which is an integer. Remove the leaf and its edge and subtract this integer from the neighboring margin. The remaining margins are still integers. Repeating this procedure proves that every positive entry is integral. \(\square\)

If row \(i\) represents \(a_i\) identical discrete voters, an integral matrix can be realized by assigning exactly \(y_{i,c}\) of those voters to candidate \(c\). Thus, with integer quotas, allowing voters to split does not decrease the optimal discrete cost.

3. A discrete hardness source with exact quotas

We use discrete Monroe with strict rankings and Borda costs, restricted to instances in which the number \(N\) of voters is divisible by the committee size \(k_0\).

This restriction remains \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard. Specifically, the polynomial reduction from Hitting Set in the proof of Theorem 2 of [Betzler–Slinko–Uhlmann](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.0580) produces \(h k_0\) voters, where \(h\) is the number of input sets, and requires \(k_0\) winners. Every winner therefore represents exactly \(h\) voters. Its total-cost bound is positive.

Write an instance of this restricted problem as follows:

  • \(A\) is its candidate set, with \(|A|=M\).
  • There are \(N\) explicitly listed voters.
  • The committee size is \(k_0\), and \(Q=N/k_0\) is a positive integer.
  • The integer total-cost bound is \(R>0\).

We may assume \(R\le N(M-1)\). Indeed, every assignment has cost at most \(N(M-1)\), and a balanced assignment exists for every size-\(k_0\) committee. Replacing a larger bound by \(N(M-1)\) therefore preserves the answer.

Aggregate identical original rankings into a support \(T_0\), and let \(a_t\) denote each ranking’s positive integer multiplicity. Thus \(\sum_{t\in T_0}a_t=N\). Aggregation changes neither discrete feasibility nor optimal cost.

4. Reduction to instances with \(p=1\)

Set

\(\displaystyle q=\left\lfloor \frac{R}{Q}\right\rfloor+1,\qquad K=k_0+q,\qquad N'=N+qQ=KQ.\)

Introduce \(q\) new candidates \(D=\{d_1,\ldots,d_q\}\). Construct the target instance as follows.

  • Every original ranking retains its order on \(A\), followed by all candidates in \(D\). Give this ranking mass \(a_t/N'\).
  • For each \(i\), introduce one ranking that places \(d_i\) first, then the other candidates in \(D\), and then all candidates in \(A\). Give it mass \(Q/N'\).
  • Set the committee size to \(K\) and the budget to \(b=R/N'\).

All orders within unspecified blocks are fixed arbitrarily. All rankings are strict and complete. Original rankings remain distinct, and the new rankings are mutually distinct and distinct from every original ranking because their first candidates belong to \(D\).

The masses are positive and sum to

\(\displaystyle \frac{N+qQ}{N'}=1.\)

Furthermore, \(qQ>R>0\), so

\(\displaystyle 0<b=\frac{R}{N'}<1,\qquad p=1.\)

For the correctness proof, multiply all target allocations by \(N'\). Original rows then have supplies \(a_t\), new rows have supply \(Q\), every selected candidate has demand \(Q\), and the allowed total cost is \(R\).

Forward implication

Suppose the discrete instance has a committee \(S_0\subseteq A\) of size \(k_0\) and a balanced assignment of cost at most \(R\).

Select \(S_0\cup D\). Retain the original assignment and assign all \(Q\) units from new row \(i\) to \(d_i\). Each selected candidate receives exactly \(Q\) units, and every new row incurs zero cost.

Dividing these allocations by \(N'\) yields a feasible target allocation of cost at most \(R/N'=b\).

Reverse implication: all new candidates must be selected

Suppose the target instance has a feasible committee and scaled allocation of cost at most \(R\).

Let \(r\) be the number of candidates from \(D\) omitted by the committee. Because the committee has size \(k_0+q\), it selects exactly \(k_0+r\) candidates from \(A\). These original candidates require total supply

\(\displaystyle (k_0+r)Q=N+rQ.\)

The original rows supply only \(N\). Hence at least \(rQ\) units from new rows must be assigned to original candidates.

Every new ranking places all \(q\) new candidates before every original candidate. Each such unit therefore incurs cost at least \(q\). If \(r\ge 1\), the total cost is at least

\(\displaystyle qrQ\ge qQ>R,\)

a contradiction.

Thus every candidate in \(D\) is selected, and exactly \(k_0\) original candidates are selected. Denote their set by \(S_0\).

Reverse implication: removing transfers between the two populations

The allocation might still send original voters to new candidates and new voters to original candidates. We now remove these transfers without increasing cost.

For each original row \(t\), let \(u_t\) be its total allocation to \(D\). For each \(c\in S_0\), let \(v_c\) be its total allocation received from new rows. Conservation of supply and demand gives

\(\displaystyle \sum_{t\in T_0}u_t=\sum_{c\in S_0}v_c=\alpha.\)

If \(\alpha>0\), retain every existing original-to-original allocation and add

\(\displaystyle z_{t,c}=\frac{u_t v_c}{\alpha}\)

to each pair \((t,c)\). These additions have row sums \(u_t\) and column sums \(v_c\), so they restore every original row’s supply and every original selected candidate’s demand. If \(\alpha=0\), no additions are needed.

Delete all assignments involving new candidates or new rows, and assign each new row entirely to its own first candidate.

The resulting allocation is feasible. To compare costs, observe:

  • Every deleted original-to-new unit had rank at least \(M\).
  • Every added original-to-original unit has rank at most \(M-1\).
  • The new rows now incur zero cost.

Writing \(J\) for the previous total cost and \(I'\) for the modified total cost, we obtain

\(\displaystyle I'\le J-M\alpha+(M-1)\alpha=J-\alpha\le R.\)

Thus the original rows alone admit a divisible assignment to \(S_0\) with supplies \(a_t\), demands \(Q\), and total cost at most \(R\). By the integrality lemma, they admit an integral assignment of no greater cost. Distributing each integral row allocation among its identical voters gives a feasible discrete Monroe assignment.

This proves both directions of the reduction.

5. Polynomial size of the reduction

The construction uses the support directly; it need not list the new voters individually.

Since \(R\le N(M-1)\),

\(\displaystyle q\le k_0(M-1)+1,\qquad N'\le N(M+1).\)

The output parameters are

\(\displaystyle m=M+q,\qquad \tau=|T_0|+q,\qquad k=K=k_0+q,\qquad p=1.\)

Using candidate labels of logarithmic length, the output encoding satisfies

\(\displaystyle L=O\!\left(\tau m\log(2+m)+(\tau+1)\log(2+N(M+1))\right).\)

The masses and budget are rational numbers with polynomial binary encoding length, and all rankings can be constructed in polynomial time. Hence this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

Combined with membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\), it proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness even when \(p=1\).

6. Parameterized consequence and the zero-budget boundary

An algorithm running in

\(\displaystyle f(p)(m+\tau+L+k)^{O(1)}\)

would, on the hard instances above, run in polynomial time because \(p=1\). It would therefore imply \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\). The same argument excludes an \(\mathsf{XP}\) algorithm parameterized by \(p\), unless \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\).

For contrast, \(p=0\) means \(b=0\) and is polynomial-time solvable. Let \(B\) be the set of candidates ranked first by at least one support ranking, and define

\(\displaystyle w_c=\sum_{\{t:\operatorname{rank}_t(c)=0\}}\mu_t.\)

Every nonnegative cost term must vanish in a zero-cost allocation. Consequently, each ranking must allocate all its mass to its unique first candidate. Feasibility is therefore equivalent to

\(\displaystyle |B|=k\quad\text{and}\quad w_c=1/k\ \text{for every }c\in B.\)

These conditions can be checked exactly in \((m+\tau+L)^{O(1)}\) time.

Step-by-step verification

1. Fixed-committee feasibility and membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\)

For a specified committee \(S\), the allocation constraints and budget inequality form a rational linear-programming feasibility problem with \(\tau k\) variables. The ranks have polynomial encoding length, and the masses, budget, and quota \(1/k\) are rational numbers of polynomial encoding length.

The invoked theorem—polynomial-time feasibility testing for rational linear programs in their binary encoding length—applies directly. It provides an exact decision procedure, including when the optimum equals the budget.

A committee has polynomial certificate length. Checking its size and then running that feasibility procedure is a valid deterministic polynomial-time verifier. Consequently, membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\) is established without requiring a certificate containing real allocations.

The stated running times
\[ (m\tau+\tau k+L)^{O(1)} \]
and
\[ \binom{m}{k}(m\tau+\tau k+L)^{O(1)} \]
are valid. Polynomial time for each fixed \(k\) follows.

2. Integrality lemma

The lemma and its proof are correct.

  • The proposed matrix \(y_{i,c}=a_i/k\) has the required row sums and column sums because \(\sum_i a_i=kQ\).
  • The feasible set is nonempty and compact.
  • A linear objective on this polytope has an extreme-point minimizer.
  • A cycle in the positive-entry graph permits alternating positive and negative perturbations. Bipartiteness ensures an even cycle, and sufficiently small perturbations preserve nonnegativity and all margins.
  • Thus the positive-entry graph of an extreme point is a forest.
  • Leaf elimination proves integrality: each removed edge equals the current integer margin of its leaf, and the neighboring margin remains integral after subtraction.

Isolated vertices have no positive entries to determine and do not obstruct the argument. Integer row allocations can be distributed among the corresponding identical discrete voters.

The lemma is subsequently applied to precisely these integer supplies and demands. It does not assert integrality for arbitrary rational quotas.

3. Discrete hardness source

The source restriction is supported by the cited proof. In Theorem 2, the Borda Monroe construction has \(h\) set voters and \(h(k_0-1)\) additional voters, hence \(hk_0\) voters and quota \(h\). Its total-cost threshold is a positive integer. The proof supplies a polynomial-size reduction from Hitting Set, supporting classical \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness of the required restriction. Strict complete rankings and zero-based Borda costs agree with the target model. [Betzler–Slinko–Uhlmann, Theorem 2, pp. 491–492](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.0580)

Clamping the bound to \(N(M-1)\) preserves the answer: all assignment costs are at most that quantity, and integer quotas permit a balanced assignment for every valid committee.

Aggregation of identical rankings preserves assignment costs and discrete feasibility through integer multiplicities.

4. Target construction and normalization

The definitions
\[ q=\left\lfloor R/Q\right\rfloor+1,\qquad K=k_0+q,\qquad N'=KQ \]
are consistent, with \(qQ>R>0\).

The output is a valid rational-support instance:

  • All rankings are strict and complete.
  • Original support rankings remain distinct.
  • Each new ranking has a different new candidate first, so the new rankings are mutually distinct.
  • New rankings differ from original rankings, whose first candidates are original candidates.
  • Every mass is positive, and their sum is one.
  • The committee size satisfies \(1\le K\le M+q\).
  • The budget satisfies \(0<R/N'<1\), giving \(p=1\).

Scaling allocations by \(N'\) converts each selected candidate’s quota into
\[ N'/K=Q \]
and converts the budget into \(R\), exactly as claimed.

5. Forward implication

The original balanced assignment supplies \(Q\) units to each selected original candidate. Assigning each new row’s \(Q\) units to its first candidate satisfies every additional quota at zero cost.

Appending new candidates does not change ranks of original candidates in original rankings. Thus the total scaled cost remains at most \(R\), and normalization gives the required target allocation.

6. Reverse implication: forcing the new candidates

If \(r\) new candidates are omitted, the committee contains \(k_0+r\) original candidates. Their demand is
\[ (k_0+r)Q=N+rQ. \]

Original rows supply only \(N\), so at least \(rQ\) units must reach these candidates from new rows. Every such unit costs at least \(q\), since all \(q\) new candidates precede every original candidate in every new ranking.

For \(r\ge1\), the cost is therefore at least
\[ qrQ\ge qQ>R. \]

This contradiction is valid for divisible allocations; no integrality assumption is used here. Every new candidate must consequently be selected.

7. Reverse implication: removing transfers

Once all new candidates are selected, original rows and selected original candidates both have total mass \(N\). Therefore the two directions of cross-population transfer have the same total \(\alpha\).

For \(\alpha>0\), the proposed additions
\[ z_{t,c}=\frac{u_tv_c}{\alpha} \]
are nonnegative and have exactly the stated row and column sums. The \(\alpha=0\) case is explicitly covered.

Retaining original-to-original allocations, adding these amounts, and assigning each new row entirely to its own candidate satisfies every supply and demand.

The cost comparison is correct. Deleted original-to-new allocations have total mass \(\alpha\) and rank at least \(M\); added allocations have total mass \(\alpha\) and rank at most \(M-1\). All other deleted costs are nonnegative. Hence
\[ I'\le J-M\alpha+(M-1)\alpha=J-\alpha\le R. \]

The integrality lemma then applies to the resulting original allocation and produces a discrete balanced assignment of no greater cost. Both directions of the reduction are established.

8. Polynomial size

The bounds
\[ q\le k_0(M-1)+1,\qquad N'\le N(M+1) \]
are correct. The first follows from \(R\le N(M-1)\) and \(N=k_0Q\); the second follows from \(qQ\le R+Q\) and \(Q\le N\).

The source explicitly lists its voters, so polynomial dependence on \(N\) is legitimate. The output support size, candidate count, rational encoding lengths, and stated bound on \(L\) are polynomial in the source input length.

The construction is therefore a polynomial-time many-one reduction.

9. Parameterized consequence and boundary case

Hardness on the fixed slice \(p=1\) rules out both an FPT algorithm and an XP algorithm unless \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\): either algorithm would run in polynomial time on that slice.

For \(p=0\), nonnegativity of \(b\) implies \(b=0\). Every positive allocation must go to its ranking’s unique first candidate. Positivity of all support masses forces every represented first candidate into the committee, while positive committee quotas prohibit additional candidates. The stated conditions \(|B|=k\) and \(w_c=1/k\) are therefore necessary and sufficient, and exact rational checking takes polynomial time.
