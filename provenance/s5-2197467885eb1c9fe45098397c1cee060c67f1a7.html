The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given jobs \(J\), vertical categories, HR traits, and a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rankings, eligibility, traits, and merit tiers, let each job have rational per-capita capacities and reservation quotas. Find a type-mass assignment \(x\) that is stable under fractional 2SMH choices: HR-position mass is matched to eligible trait mass, selected in merit order with mass split at tied score tiers, remaining capacity is filled by merit, and open-category choices precede VR-category choices. Require \(x\) to Pareto-dominate every other stable assignment, comparing each type’s assigned mass across preference prefixes; determine whether such an assignment can be computed in time polynomial in the input dimensions and bit length.

There are \(m=|J|\) jobs, reserved categories \(R\), open category \(o\), and HR traits \(H\).
Each type has a strict ranking of \(J\cup\{\varnothing\}\), membership in exactly one category of \(R\cup\{g\}\), any subset of \(H\), and a merit tier at each job. Everyone is eligible for open positions; only members of \(c\) are eligible for category \(c\). Preferences ignore position categories.
For \(v\in V=R\cup\{o\}\), capacities \(r_j^v\) and HR quotas \(r_j^{v,h}\) are nonnegative binary rationals, with \(\sum_h r_j^{v,h}\le r_j^v\); total job capacity is \(q_j=\sum_v r_j^v\). Unused HR quota becomes ordinary capacity within its category; unused VR capacity is not transferred.

Thus each unit of applicant mass counts toward at most one HR quota.
First maximize \(\sum_t b_t\), then lexicographically maximize the totals of \(b\) in successive merit tiers, highest first; among the remaining possibilities choose the unique \(b\) minimizing \(\sum_t b_t^2/\mu_t\). Partial result: a greatest stable assignment always exists

The complexity classification remains unresolved. I prove a new structural result for the exact choice rule in the question:

Every instance admits a greatest stable assignment. Moreover, that assignment is unique, including its position-category allocation.

The main step is to prove persistence of the specified choice rule, including both quadratic tie breaks and the sequential open-then-reserved stages. This permits an application of the Alkan–Gale existence and optimality theorem, after verifying that its stability notion matches the one in the question.

I also prove polynomial-time solvability when all types have the same ranking of jobs and the outside option. This special case permits arbitrary merit tiers, overlapping HR traits, and reserved categories.

1. Definitions and the properties to be proved

All vector inequalities below are coordinatewise. Write $x\wedge y$ and $x\vee y$ for coordinatewise minimum and maximum.

A choice function $D$, with $0\le D(a)\le a$, is:

  • consistent if $D(a)\le a'\le a$ implies $D(a')=D(a)$;
  • persistent if $a\le a'$ implies

$$ D(a)\ge D(a')\wedge a; $$

  • size-monotone if $a\le a'$ implies

$$ \sum_t D(a)_t\le\sum_t D(a')_t. $$

Persistence says, in particular, that a coordinate already partially rejected cannot receive more selected mass when offers increase.

Fix a job and one category, restricting coordinates to its eligible types. Denote its capacity by $r$, its HR quotas by $r^h$, its HR-selected vector by $b(a)$, and its complete single-category selection by $D(a)$.

Throughout, the positive weights in the quadratic objectives are the given masses $\mu_t$.

2. The HR feasibility region and an exchange lemma

2.1. A polymatroid description

For a set $S$ of eligible types, let $N(S)$ be the set of traits possessed by at least one type in $S$, and define
$$ \rho(S)=\sum_{h\in N(S)}r^h. $$

This function is normalized, nonnegative, monotone, and submodular. To check submodularity, each trait contributes its nonnegative quota multiplied by the indicator that $S$ intersects the types possessing that trait. Each such indicator is submodular, and their nonnegative weighted sum is submodular.

Define
$$ P(\rho)= \left\{ b\ge0: \sum_{t\in S}b_t\le\rho(S) \text{ for every }S \right\}. $$

Lemma 1. A vector $b$ admits the required fractional trait matching if and only if $b\in P(\rho)$.

Proof. Necessity follows by summing the matching constraints over $S$:
$$ \sum_{t\in S}b_t = \sum_{t\in S}\sum_h f_{t,h} \le \sum_{h\in N(S)}r^h. $$

For sufficiency, construct a flow network with source-to-type capacities $b_t$, type-to-trait arcs whenever the type possesses the trait, and trait-to-sink capacities $r^h$. Give every type-to-trait arc capacity greater than $\sum_t b_t$.

A minimum cut has capacity at most $\sum_t b_t$, so it crosses none of those large-capacity arcs. If $S$ is its source-side set of types, all traits in $N(S)$ must also be on the source side. Consequently its capacity is at least
$$ \sum_{t\notin S}b_t+\rho(S) \ge \sum_t b_t. $$
The max-flow min-cut theorem therefore supplies a flow saturating every source-to-type arc. Its type-to-trait flows give the required matching. $\square$

Thus the feasible HR-selected vectors from offers $a$ are precisely
$$ P(\rho)\cap\{b: b\le a\}. $$

2.2. Exchange using tight sets

A set $S$ is tight for $x\in P(\rho)$ if $x(S)=\rho(S)$, where $x(S)=\sum_{t\in S}x_t$.

Tight sets for the same vector are closed under union and intersection. Indeed, if $S,T$ are tight, then
$$ \begin{aligned} \rho(S)+\rho(T) &=x(S)+x(T)\\ &=x(S\cup T)+x(S\cap T)\\ &\le\rho(S\cup T)+\rho(S\cap T)\\ &\le\rho(S)+\rho(T). \end{aligned} $$
Equality throughout makes both $S\cup T$ and $S\cap T$ tight.

Lemma 2. Suppose $x,y\in P(\rho)$ and $x_t>y_t$. Either:

  • for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both $y+\varepsilon e_t$ and $x-\varepsilon e_t$ belong to $P(\rho)$; or
  • there is an $s$ with $y_s>x_s$ such that, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both

$$ y+\varepsilon e_t-\varepsilon e_s \quad\text{and}\quad x-\varepsilon e_t+\varepsilon e_s $$
belong to $P(\rho)$.

Proof. If no tight set for $y$ contains $t$, every inequality affected by increasing $y_t$ has positive slack. There are finitely many inequalities, so a sufficiently small increase is feasible. Decreasing $x_t$ is feasible by downward closure and $x_t>0$. This proves the first alternative.

Otherwise, let $Y$ be the intersection of all tight sets for $y$ containing $t$. It is tight and contains $t$.

Let $X$ be the union of all tight sets for $x$ not containing $t$. The empty set is tight, so this union is defined; it is tight and does not contain $t$. Submodularity gives
$$ \begin{aligned} y(Y\setminus X) &=\rho(Y)-y(Y\cap X)\\ &\ge\rho(Y)-\rho(Y\cap X)\\ &\ge\rho(Y\cup X)-\rho(X)\\ &\ge x(Y\cup X)-x(X)\\ &=x(Y\setminus X). \end{aligned} $$

Since $t\in Y\setminus X$ and $x_t>y_t$, some $s\in Y\setminus X$ satisfies $y_s>x_s$.

Every tight set for $y$ containing $t$ contains $s$, so the direction $e_t-e_s$ does not increase any tight-set sum. Every tight set for $x$ containing $s$ contains $t$, because $s\notin X$. Hence the direction $e_s-e_t$ likewise does not increase any tight-set sum for $x$.

All remaining inequalities have positive slack. Nonnegativity also permits a sufficiently small step because $y_s>x_s\ge0$ and $x_t>y_t\ge0$. The second alternative follows. $\square$

3. Persistence of the exact HR tie rule

Proposition 3. The HR-selected vector is persistent:
$$ a\le a' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b(a)\ge b(a')\wedge a. $$

Proof. Write $b=b(a)$ and $c=b(a')$. Suppose persistence fails at coordinate $t$, so
$$ b_t<\min\{c_t,a_t\}. $$

Apply Lemma 2 with $x=c$ and $y=b$.

Its first alternative is impossible: a sufficiently small increase of $b_t$ respects $b\le a$ and increases the total HR-selected mass, contradicting its maximization.

In the second alternative, choose the step small enough also to respect the offer bounds. This is possible because
$$ b_t<a_t, \qquad c_s<b_s\le a_s\le a'_s. $$

If $t$ has higher merit than $s$, the feasible exchange
$$ b+\varepsilon e_t-\varepsilon e_s $$
preserves total HR-selected mass and improves the first affected merit-tier total. That contradicts the lexicographic HR objectives at $a$.

If $s$ has higher merit than $t$, the reverse exchange improves the lexicographic HR objectives at $a'$.

Therefore $t$ and $s$ lie in the same merit tier. Both exchanges then preserve every HR objective preceding the quadratic tie break. First-order optimality for the quadratic minimizations gives
$$ \frac{b_t}{\mu_t}\ge\frac{b_s}{\mu_s}, \qquad \frac{c_s}{\mu_s}\ge\frac{c_t}{\mu_t}. $$
But $b_s>c_s$ and $c_t>b_t$, so
$$ \frac{b_t}{\mu_t} \ge \frac{b_s}{\mu_s} > \frac{c_s}{\mu_s} \ge \frac{c_t}{\mu_t} > \frac{b_t}{\mu_t}, $$
a contradiction. $\square$

Two consequences are useful.

First, residual offers are monotone:
$$ a\le a' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a-b(a)\le a'-b(a'). $$
Indeed, if $b_t(a)<a_t$, persistence forces $b_t(a')\le b_t(a)$. If $b_t(a)=a_t$, the old residual is zero.

Second, the total HR-selected mass
$$ B(a)=\sum_t b_t(a) $$
is nondecreasing in $a$, because its maximization is over nested feasible regions.

4. Persistence of the ordinary-capacity stage

Let $Z_K(u)$ be the additional selection from residual offers $u$ and additional capacity $K\ge0$.

Let $E_1,\ldots,E_s$ be the merit tiers, highest first. The amount available for tier $E_i$, after serving earlier tiers, is
$$ K_i= \left( K-\sum_{k<i}u(E_k) \right)_+. $$
The selected total in that tier is
$$ \kappa_i=\min\{K_i,u(E_i)\}. $$

The quadratic objective separates across tiers once these totals are fixed. Its unique minimizer in tier $E_i$ has the form
$$ Z_K(u)_t=\min\{u_t,\lambda_i\mu_t\}, \qquad t\in E_i, $$
where $\lambda_i\ge0$ is chosen to give total $\kappa_i$. This follows from the quadratic optimality conditions: uncapped positive coordinates have equal ratios $z_t/\mu_t$, and capped coordinates have ratios no greater than that common value. If the whole tier is selected, the vector is simply $u$ on that tier.

Lemma 4. If $u\le u'$ and $K'\le K$, then
$$ Z_K(u)\ge Z_{K'}(u')\wedge u. $$

Proof. It suffices to consider a coordinate $t$ with $Z_K(u)_t<u_t$.

Its tier is not selected in full, so $\kappa_i=K_i<u(E_i)$ and its water level $\lambda_i$ is finite. The new remaining capacity satisfies
$$ K'_i= \left( K'-\sum_{k<i}u'(E_k) \right)_+ \le K_i. $$
At the old water level,
$$ \sum_{t\in E_i}\min\{u'_t,\lambda_i\mu_t\} \ge \sum_{t\in E_i}\min\{u_t,\lambda_i\mu_t\} = K_i \ge K'_i. $$

Also, $u'(E_i)\ge u(E_i)>K_i\ge K'_i$, so the new tier is not selected in full. Its water level can therefore be chosen no larger than $\lambda_i$. Consequently
$$ Z_{K'}(u')_t \le \lambda_i\mu_t = Z_K(u)_t. $$
This proves persistence. $\square$

For the single-category rule,
$$ D(a)=b(a)+Z_{r-B(a)}(a-b(a)). $$
The additional capacity is nonnegative because
$$ B(a)\le\sum_h r^h\le r. $$

Proposition 5. The single-category choice $D$ is persistent and size-monotone.

Proof. For $a\le a'$, the preceding results give
$$ a-b(a)\le a'-b(a'), \qquad r-B(a')\le r-B(a). $$

If $D(a)_t<a_t$, then $b(a)_t<a_t$, so Proposition 3 gives
$$ b(a')_t\le b(a)_t. $$
Moreover, the additional selection partially rejects coordinate $t$ of the old residual. Lemma 4 therefore gives
$$ Z_{r-B(a')}(a'-b(a'))_t \le Z_{r-B(a)}(a-b(a))_t. $$
Thus $D(a')_t\le D(a)_t$ whenever $D(a)_t<a_t$, which is exactly persistence.

For size-monotonicity, the additional stage fills as much remaining capacity as the remaining offers permit. Hence
$$ \sum_t D(a)_t = B(a)+ \min\left\{ r-B(a),\sum_t a_t-B(a) \right\} = \min\left\{r,\sum_t a_t\right\}. $$
This is nondecreasing in $a$. $\square$

5. Consistency, continuity, and sequential reserved categories

5.1. Single-category consistency

Lemma 6. If $D(a)\le a'\le a$, then $D(a')=D(a)$.

Proof. Write $D(a)=b+z$. Since $b\le a'$, the original HR vector and a matching witnessing its feasibility remain feasible.

The HR feasible region has shrunk while retaining that vector. Therefore the original maximum total remains optimal. Inductively, each original merit-tier optimum remains optimal. The original quadratic minimizer remains feasible on the restricted optimal face and, by uniqueness of its selected vector, remains the selected $b$.

For the additional stage,
$$ z\le a'-b\le a-b. $$
The same restriction argument preserves its merit-tier optima and its unique quadratic minimizer $z$. $\square$

5.2. Persistence and size-monotonicity imply continuity

The following elementary bound avoids any separate parametric-optimization continuity assumption.

Lemma 7. Any persistent, size-monotone choice $F$ satisfies
$$ \|F(a)-F(a')\|_1\le2\|a-a'\|_1. $$

Proof. First suppose $a\le a'$. If $F(a)_t<a_t$, persistence gives $F(a')_t\le F(a)_t$. Otherwise,
$$ F(a')_t-F(a)_t\le a'_t-a_t. $$
Thus the sum $P$ of positive coordinate changes is at most $\|a'-a\|_1$.

If $N$ is the sum of absolute negative changes, size-monotonicity gives $P-N\ge0$. Hence
$$ \|F(a')-F(a)\|_1=P+N\le2P\le2\|a'-a\|_1. $$

For arbitrary $a,a'$, apply this bound through $a\vee a'$ and use the triangle inequality. $\square$

In particular, every single-category choice is continuous.

5.3. The complete job choice

Fix a job. Write its open selection as $c^o(a)$ and define the residual
$$ u(a)=a-c^o(a). $$
For a reserved category $v$, let $E_v$ restrict coordinates to its members. Its selection is
$$ c^v(a)=D_v(E_vu(a)). $$

Because every type belongs to at most one reserved category, these selections together satisfy
$$ \widehat C(a)=c^o(a)+\sum_{v\in R}c^v(a)\le a. $$

Proposition 8. The aggregate job choice $\widehat C$ is persistent, size-monotone, consistent, and continuous. Consistency also preserves every position-category selection.

Proof.

For persistence, suppose $a\le a'$. Open persistence implies $u(a)\le u(a')$.

Consider a coordinate partially rejected by the aggregate choice:
$$ \widehat C(a)_t<a_t. $$
Its open selection partially rejects it, so $c^o(a')_t\le c^o(a)_t$.

If $t$ is a reserved-category member, its reserved selection also partially rejects its old residual:
$$ c^v(a)_t<u(a)_t. $$
Reserved persistence then gives $c^v(a')_t\le c^v(a)_t$. A general-category type has no reserved selection. Thus aggregate selection cannot increase at a partially rejected coordinate.

For size-monotonicity, the open-selected total is nondecreasing. The residual offers are coordinatewise nondecreasing, so each reserved-selected total is also nondecreasing.

For consistency, let
$$ c=\widehat C(a), \qquad c\le a'\le a. $$
Since $c^o(a)\le a'$, Lemma 6 preserves the open selection. For every reserved category, the reduced eligible residual offers lie between its old selection and its old residual offers:
$$ c^v(a)\le E_v(a'-c^o(a))\le E_v(a-c^o(a)). $$
Lemma 6 preserves that category’s selection as well.

Continuity follows from Lemma 7 for the aggregate. The category-specific selections are also continuous, since they are compositions of continuous single-category choices with the continuous open residual. $\square$

In particular, the category-preserving idempotence identity holds:
$$ C_j^v(\widehat C_j(a))=C_j^v(a) \qquad\text{for every }j,v. $$

6. Existence and uniqueness of a greatest stable assignment

6.1. Applicant-side choice

Treat each supported type as one divisible applicant with available mass $\mu_t$.

Given offers $w=(w_j)_{j\in J}$, define $A_t(w)$ by accepting acceptable jobs in its strict preference order until its mass $\mu_t$ is exhausted.

If the acceptable jobs, in order, are $j_1,\ldots,j_k$, then
$$ A_t(w)_{j_i} = \min\left\{ w_{j_i}, \left(\mu_t-\sum_{\ell<i}w_{j_\ell}\right)_+ \right\}. $$
Unacceptable coordinates are always zero.

This formula proves continuity. It also proves persistence: if a coordinate is partially rejected, increasing offers only decreases the remaining mass available at that coordinate. The selected total is
$$ \min\left\{\mu_t,\sum_{j\text{ acceptable}}w_j\right\}, $$
so applicant choice is size-monotone.

Consistency follows by retaining the selected vector while reducing offers: its lexicographically optimal selected totals in the strict ranking remain feasible and optimal.

6.2. The theorem being applied

For a choice function $F$, a self-chosen vector $x=F(x)$ is satiated at coordinate $i$ if
$$ F(w)_i\le x_i \qquad\text{for every admissible }w\ge x. $$

The Alkan–Gale existence and optimality theorem states that, in a finite bipartite allocation problem with continuous, consistent, persistent choice functions on bounded product domains, there exists a stable allocation $y^*$ such that, for every other stable allocation $y$, applicant-side revealed preference satisfies
$$ A_t(y_t^*\vee y_t)=y_t^* \qquad\text{for every applicant }t. $$
Here stability means self-choice on both sides and that every pair is satiated on at least one side. This is the existence theorem together with Theorem 2 of [Alkan and Gale, *Stable schedule matching under revealed preference*](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/266/1/3011800000835.pdf).

Apply the theorem on the product box
$$ 0\le y_{t,j}\le\mu_t. $$
The applicant choices and the aggregate job choices map their respective boxes into themselves. Sections 3–5 and Section 6.1 verify continuity, consistency, and persistence. Thus all its choice-function hypotheses hold.

It remains essential to check stability and dominance for the present task.

6.3. Equivalence of the stability definitions

Suppose $y$ is feasible, acceptable, and self-chosen on both sides.

For an acceptable job $j$, type $t$ is nonsatiated at $j$ exactly when
$$ d_{t,j}>0. $$
Indeed, positive worse-outcome mass allows a sufficiently small increase at $j$ under greedy applicant choice. If $d_{t,j}=0$, all mass is already assigned to $j$ or better jobs, and increasing offers cannot increase its selection at $j$. An unacceptable job is always satiated.

If a blocking amount from the task exists, both sides of that pair are nonsatiated.

Conversely, suppose $d_{t,j}>0$ and the job is nonsatiated at $t$. There is an offered vector $w\ge y_j$ with
$$ \widehat C_j(w)_t>y_{t,j}. $$
Choose
$$ 0<\delta\le \min\left\{ d_{t,j}, \widehat C_j(w)_t-y_{t,j} \right\}. $$
Since choice is bounded by offers,
$$ y_j+\delta e_t\le w. $$
Persistence therefore gives
$$ \widehat C_j(y_j+\delta e_t)_t \ge \min\{\widehat C_j(w)_t,y_{t,j}+\delta\} = y_{t,j}+\delta. $$
This is a block under the task’s definition.

Thus aggregate stability is exactly Alkan–Gale stability.

To recover position categories from an aggregate stable allocation, define
$$ x_{\cdot,j,v}=C_j^v(y_j). $$
Aggregate self-choice ensures that these category vectors sum to $y_j$. They satisfy eligibility and category capacities by construction, and their aggregate applicant masses satisfy the type bounds.

Conversely, a stable assignment in the task aggregates to a self-chosen allocation of this kind. Its category vectors are necessarily the displayed canonical vectors.

6.4. Revealed preference implies every required prefix inequality

Let $y^*$ be the applicant-optimal allocation supplied by the theorem, and let $y$ be any other stable allocation.

For a prefix $P$ consisting of acceptable jobs, greedy applicant choice gives
$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j\in P}y^*_{t,j} &= \sum_{j\in P}A_t(y_t^*\vee y_t)_j\\ &= \min\left\{ \mu_t, \sum_{j\in P}\max\{y^*_{t,j},y_{t,j}\} \right\}\\ &\ge \sum_{j\in P}y_{t,j}. \end{aligned} $$
The last inequality uses $\sum_{j\in P}y_{t,j}\le\mu_t$.

Any prefix containing the outside option has mass $\mu_t$ in every acceptable assignment: all outcomes below the outside option are unacceptable jobs and receive zero mass. Hence its required comparison also holds.

The category lift of $y^*$ is therefore greatest in the precise prefix-dominance order of the task.

Theorem 9. Every instance admits a greatest stable assignment.

Uniqueness. If two assignments are greatest, their prefix masses are equal for every type. Differences of successive prefix masses determine each job’s aggregate mass and the unmatched mass. Their aggregates therefore coincide. Category-specific self-choice then determines both assignments by the same vectors $C_j^v(y_j)$, so the assignments coincide. $\square$

This removes the possible nonexistence obstruction. It does not provide a polynomial-time bound for computing the greatest assignment.

7. A polynomial-time case: all types share one ranking

Assume every type has the same complete ranking. Write the acceptable jobs, in that common order, as
$$ j_1\succ j_2\succ\cdots\succ j_k\succ\varnothing. $$

Set $U^1=\mu$. For $i=1,\ldots,k$, compute
$$ x_{\cdot,j_i,v}=C_{j_i}^v(U^i), \qquad a_{j_i}=\widehat C_{j_i}(U^i), \qquad U^{i+1}=U^i-a_{j_i}. $$
Assign zero mass to unacceptable jobs.

7.1. Stability

Category-preserving idempotence proves self-choice.

At job $j_i$, the mass of type $t$ at worse outcomes is exactly
$$ d_{t,j_i}=U^i_t-a_{j_i,t}. $$
For every admissible blocking amount,
$$ a_{j_i} \le a_{j_i}+\delta e_t \le U^i. $$
Category-preserving consistency, applied to the selection from $U^i$, gives
$$ C_{j_i}^v(a_{j_i}+\delta e_t)=C_{j_i}^v(U^i). $$
No such amount blocks. Therefore the constructed assignment is stable.

7.2. Uniqueness of every stable assignment

Consider another stable assignment. Inductively suppose its allocations at jobs before $j_i$ agree with the construction. Its remaining type masses at this stage are therefore $U^i$.

Let $y$ be its aggregate allocation at $j_i$. Then $y\le U^i$ and $\widehat C_{j_i}(y)=y$.

If $\widehat C_{j_i}(U^i)_t>y_t$, necessarily $U^i_t-y_t>0$. Persistence, applied to
$$ y+(U^i_t-y_t)e_t\le U^i, $$
would give
$$ \widehat C_{j_i}\bigl(y+(U^i_t-y_t)e_t\bigr)_t \ge \widehat C_{j_i}(U^i)_t > y_t. $$
Because the common ranking makes $U^i_t-y_t$ exactly its worse-outcome mass at this job, this is a block.

Consequently

7.3. Exact computation and bit complexity

A single-category evaluation uses:

  • a linear program maximizing total HR-selected mass;
  • successive linear programs fixing the merit-tier optima;
  • a convex quadratic program minimizing $\sum_t b_t^2/\mu_t$;
  • successive linear programs for additional merit-tier totals;
  • a convex quadratic program minimizing $\sum_t z_t^2/\mu_t$.

The HR matching polytope is nonempty and compact. The additional-selection polytope is nonempty and compact because $B\le r$. Both quadratic matrices are rational and positive semidefinite. The selected $b$ and $z$ are unique by strict convexity in those coordinates.

Exact rational convex quadratic programming is polynomial-time solvable under these hypotheses; the relevant theorem is [Kozlov–Tarasov–Khachiyan, *The polynomial solvability of convex quadratic programming*](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/zvmmf5189).

For completeness, repeated choice evaluations do not create an uncontrolled denominator growth. Here is a uniform bound.

$$ Qw+c+A^{\mathsf T}\lambda+E^{\mathsf T}\nu=0, \qquad \lambda\ge0, $$
together with complementary slackness. These conditions are necessary and sufficient over polyhedral feasible regions: necessity follows from the polyhedral normal-cone description, and sufficiency follows from convexity.

Choose, for each complementary-slackness pair, either zero multiplier or zero slack. Each resulting branch is a linear system with polynomially many variables and constraints. Its coefficient matrix depends only on the original instance; the offers enter its right-hand side affinely. Reserved-stage residual offers are incorporated as affine expressions in the open-stage variables.

$$ D F, $$
where $\log F$ is bounded by a polynomial in the original instance size, uniformly over branches. Every feasible optimality branch produces the specified unique category selections, so this bound applies to the actual output.

Initially the masses have a common denominator of polynomial encoding length. Each of the at most $m$ evaluations increases its logarithm by at most that uniform polynomial bound. Subtraction to form the next residual preserves the current common denominator. All residual and selected coordinates lie in $[0,1]$, so their numerators have polynomial encoding length too.

Thus the sequential construction uses polynomially many exact operations on numbers of polynomial encoding length.
