The general question remains unresolved. I prove three results:

  • The stated problem is $\mathrm{PPAD}$-hard for inverse-polynomial accuracy.
  • Polynomial-size rational solutions always exist and can be checked in polynomial time. Consequently, $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness under solution-preserving search reductions would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

The third result includes every instance with $m=2$, since the prescribed utility normalization then forces dichotomous utilities.

1. Definitions and the weight condition

Write
$$ \Delta(G)=\left\{y\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{G}:\sum_g y_g=1\right\}, $$
and, whenever an affordable lottery exists, define
$$ V_a(p)=\max\left\{u_a\cdot y:y\in\Delta(G),\ p\cdot y\leq1\right\}. $$
The maximum exists because the feasible set is nonempty and compact. At any proposed solution, $q_a=x_a/\mu_a$ itself witnesses nonemptiness.

The task permits all weights to be zero. This makes every feasible flow maximize the weighted objective. Thus, under the stated reading, the general search problem is equivalent to finding prices and a clearing flow satisfying
$$ p\cdot q_a\leq1, \qquad u_a\cdot q_a\geq V_a(p)-\delta. $$
The substantive equilibrium search remains. Moreover, the dichotomous construction below uses strictly positive weights.

2. An exact algorithm for arbitrary dichotomous supports

Theorem. Suppose $u_{ag}\in\{0,1\}$ for every $a,g$, with the masses and normalization specified in the question. An exact solution can be computed in time $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$, with $\lambda_a=1$ for every $a$.

Call $g$ liked by $a$ if $u_{ag}=1$. For subsets $R\subseteq A$, $H\subseteq G$, and $S\subseteq R$, define
$$ N_H(S)=\{g\in H:\text{some }a\in S\text{ likes }g\}. $$
Also write
$$ \mu(S)=\sum_{a\in S}\mu_a, \qquad \nu(B)=\sum_{g\in B}\nu_g. $$

2.1. A flow criterion

For $0\leq t\leq1$, there is a flow on liked pairs between $R$ and $H$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{g\in H}f_{ag}=t\mu_a \quad(a\in R), \qquad \sum_{a\in R}f_{ag}\leq\nu_g \quad(g\in H) $$
if and only if
$$ t\mu(S)\leq\nu(N_H(S)) \quad\text{for every }S\subseteq R. $$

To prove this, construct a network with capacities $t\mu_a$ from the source to each agent, capacity $2$ on each liked agent-good edge, and capacity $\nu_g$ from each good to the sink.

The cut separating the source alone has capacity $t\mu(R)\leq1$. Hence a minimum cut cannot cross an edge of capacity $2$. If its source-side agent set is $S$, all goods in $N_H(S)$ must also lie on the source side. A minimum cut contains no additional source-side goods: moving any such good to the sink side strictly decreases the cut capacity, because its mass is positive.

Consequently, the minimum-cut value is
$$ \min_{S\subseteq R} \left(t\mu(R\setminus S)+\nu(N_H(S))\right). $$
By the max-flow/min-cut theorem, all source-agent edges can be saturated exactly when every expression in this minimum is at least $t\mu(R)$. This is precisely the stated inequality.

2.2. Removing successive scarce groups

Start with $R=A$ and $H=G$. At a stage with $R\neq\varnothing$, define
$$ r=\min_{\varnothing\neq S\subseteq R} \frac{\nu(N_H(S))}{\mu(S)}. $$

If $r\geq1$, stop. The flow criterion supplies a liked-pair flow giving every remaining agent its full row mass $\mu_a$.

If $r<1$, choose a minimizing set $T$ and put
$$ B=N_H(T). $$
Allocate to agents in $T$, using only liked goods in $B$, a flow with row masses $r\mu_a$.

Such a flow exists. For every $S\subseteq T$,
$$ r\mu(S)\leq\nu(N_H(S)), $$
and $N_H(S)\subseteq B$, so the flow criterion applies. Furthermore,
$$ \nu(B)=r\mu(T). $$
Thus the total flow equals the total capacity of $B$, and every good in $B$ is exhausted.

Record $(T,B,r)$, remove $T$ from $R$ and $B$ from $H$, and repeat. Denote the recorded groups by
$$ (T_1,B_1,r_1),\ldots,(T_k,B_k,r_k). $$

Every recorded ratio is positive, and
$$ 0<r_1\leq r_2\leq\cdots\leq r_k<1. $$
Here is the required justification.

Initially, every nonempty agent subset has a nonempty liked neighborhood, because every utility row has maximum one. Positive good masses therefore make the initial minimum ratio positive.

Suppose $(T,B,r)$ has just been removed. For every nonempty $S\subseteq R\setminus T$, minimality of $r$ gives
$$ r\mu(T\cup S)\leq\nu(N_H(T\cup S)). $$
Since $B=N_H(T)$,
$$ N_H(T\cup S)=B\cup N_{H\setminus B}(S), $$
with disjoint sets on the right. Substituting $\nu(B)=r\mu(T)$ yields
$$ r\mu(S)\leq\nu(N_{H\setminus B}(S)). $$
Thus the next minimum ratio is at least $r$. This also proves that it cannot be zero.

Every removal deletes at least one agent type. Hence there are at most $\tau$ removal stages.

2.3. Computing a minimum ratio in polynomial time

The minimum over subsets need not be enumerated.

Choose a common denominator $K$ for all original masses. For example, the product of their denominators suffices and has bit length at most $L$. Then
$$ \mu_a=\frac{M_a}{K}, \qquad \nu_g=\frac{N_g}{K}, $$
for positive integers $M_a,N_g$, with each family summing to $K$.

Every subset ratio is consequently a fraction whose numerator and denominator are integers at most $K$. Any two distinct such ratios differ by at least
$$ \frac1{K^2}. $$

First test the flow criterion at $t=1$. If it succeeds, stop. Otherwise the minimum ratio $r$ is less than one. Binary search using the flow test maintains a feasible lower endpoint $\ell$ and an infeasible upper endpoint $h$, so
$$ \ell\leq r<h. $$
After $O(\log K)$ tests, arrange that
$$ h-\ell<\frac1{K^2}. $$

Take a minimum cut at the infeasible value $h$. Its nonempty source-side agent set $T$ satisfies
$$ \frac{\nu(N_H(T))}{\mu(T)}<h. $$
Let this ratio be $\rho$. Since $r$ is the minimum,
$$ r\leq\rho<h, \qquad \rho-r<h-\ell<\frac1{K^2}. $$
The separation bound forces $\rho=r$. Thus the cut supplies an exact minimizing set.

All capacities used in this procedure have polynomial bit length. At a recorded ratio, the capacities $r\mu_a$ also have polynomial bit length. Rational maximum flow can be computed by clearing denominators and applying an integer maximum-flow algorithm; the resulting integer capacities have polynomial bit length.

There are at most $\tau$ stages and $O(\log K)$ flow tests per stage. The entire procedure therefore takes time $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)$.

2.4. Completing the allocation

Let $R,H$ be the sets remaining when the procedure stops. If $R$ is empty, take its liked-pair flow to be zero. Otherwise, use the full-row liked-pair flow supplied by the stopping condition.

For $g\in H$, let $b_g\geq0$ be its unused mass after this final flow. The total unused mass is
$$ D=\sum_{g\in H}b_g =\nu(H)-\mu(R) =\sum_{i=1}^k(1-r_i)\mu(T_i). $$

If $k=0$, then $D=0$ and the final flow already clears the market.

If $k>0$, then $D>0$. For each $a\in T_i$, define its unfilled row mass
$$ d_a=(1-r_i)\mu_a. $$
Fill its row using the remaining goods by setting
$$ x^{\mathrm{fill}}_{ag}=\frac{d_a b_g}{D} \quad(g\in H). $$
These additions have row sum $d_a$ and column sum $b_g$, because
$$ \sum_{a\notin R}d_a=D. $$
Together with the liked-pair flows already constructed, they give exact row and column masses.

An agent removed in $T_i$ likes no good in final $H$. Indeed, all its then-remaining liked goods were included in $B_i$ and removed at that stage. Hence its filling flow has utility zero.

All these quantities are rational with polynomial encoding length: they are obtained from polynomially many rational flow entries and arithmetic operations whose denominators have polynomially many bits.

2.5. Prices and individual optimality

Set
$$ p_g= \begin{cases} 1/r_i,&g\in B_i,\\ 0,&g\in H. \end{cases} $$

For $a\in T_i$, the conditional allocation contains a liked fraction $r_i$ at price $1/r_i$, followed by a fraction $1-r_i$ of zero-price, disliked goods. Therefore,
$$ p\cdot q_a=1, \qquad u_a\cdot q_a=r_i. $$

Every good liked by $a\in T_i$ belongs to some $B_j$ with $j\leq i$. By monotonicity of the recorded ratios,
$$ p_g=\frac1{r_j}\geq\frac1{r_i} $$
on every liked good. Consequently, for any affordable lottery $y$,
$$ 1\geq p\cdot y \geq\frac1{r_i}\sum_{g:u_{ag}=1}y_g =\frac1{r_i}u_a\cdot y. $$
Thus $V_a(p)\leq r_i$, and the allocated lottery attains this bound.

Every agent in final $R$ receives only liked, zero-price goods. Its cost is zero and utility is one, which is the largest possible utility.

This proves exact affordability and exact utility optimality for every agent.

2.6. Weighted-flow optimality with all weights equal to one

Let
$$ B=\bigcup_{i=1}^k B_i. $$
The constructed allocation has total utility
$$ \sum_{a,g}u_{ag}x_{ag}=\nu(B)+\mu(R). $$
Indeed, every good in $B$ is allocated on a liked pair, every remaining agent receives a full liked allocation, and all filling allocations are disliked.

For any feasible flow $z$, its utility contribution on $B$ is at most $\nu(B)$.

On $H$, only agents in $R$ can receive positive utility, because removed agents like no good in $H$. Hence the utility contribution on $H$ is at most $\mu(R)$.

Therefore,
$$ \sum_{a,g}u_{ag}z_{ag}\leq\nu(B)+\mu(R) =\sum_{a,g}u_{ag}x_{ag}. $$
Taking $\lambda_a=1$ proves the required weighted-flow optimality and completes the theorem.

3. The general $\mathrm{PPAD}$ lower bound

3.1. Exact budgets permit normalization

Lemma. Every solution can be converted to one with $\min_g p_g=0$, preserving its allocation and utility error.

Market clearing and affordability give
$$ \sum_g\nu_gp_g =\sum_a\mu_a(p\cdot q_a)\leq1. $$
Let $c=\min_g p_g$. Positive masses imply $c\leq1$.

If $c<1$, set
$$ \widehat p_g=\frac{p_g-c}{1-c}. $$
For every lottery $y$,
$$ \widehat p\cdot y=\frac{p\cdot y-c}{1-c}. $$
Thus $\widehat p\cdot y\leq1$ if and only if $p\cdot y\leq1$. Every affordable set and optimum value is preserved.

If $c=1$, all prices are at least one, and their positive-mass average is at most one. Hence all prices equal one. Every lottery is affordable, and replacing the prices by zero preserves this fact.

The conversion uses rational arithmetic of polynomial bit complexity. Moreover, every normalized solution satisfies
$$ 0\leq p_g\leq\frac1{\nu_g}. $$

3.2. The external hardness theorem

Chen, Chen, Peng, and Yannakakis prove that computing a $1/n^5$-approximate HZ equilibrium is $\mathrm{PPAD}$-hard. Their approximation requires exact unit row and column sums, normalized prices, expenditure at most $1+1/n^5$, and utility loss at most $1/n^5$ relative to budget-one optimization. Cheaper-lottery tie-breaking is omitted. Their construction has maximum utility one in every row and universally zero-utility dummy goods. These are the hypotheses needed here. [Theorem 3.1, Definition 2.3, and Section 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.05746)

3.3. Compressing and expanding supports

Take a market from that construction, with utilities $v_{ij}$ for $i,j\in[n]$.

Partition agents into classes $I_a$ of identical utility rows and goods into classes $J_g$ of identical utility columns. Put
$$ k_a=|I_a|, \qquad \ell_g=|J_g|, $$
and define the support instance by
$$ \mu_a=\frac{k_a}{n}, \qquad \nu_g=\frac{\ell_g}{n}, \qquad u_{ag}=v_{ij}\quad(i\in I_a,\ j\in J_g). $$
The utility entry is independent of both representative choices.

The resulting masses are positive rationals summing to one. Grouping preserves each row’s minimum zero and maximum one; therefore $m\geq2$. Also,
$$ \tau\leq n, \qquad m\leq n. $$
The compression has polynomial encoding length and can be computed in polynomial time. Set $\delta=1/n^5$.

Given any returned solution, normalize its prices and define
$$ X_{ij}=\frac{q_{ag}}{\ell_g}, \qquad P_j=\widehat p_g \quad(i\in I_a,\ j\in J_g). $$
For every original agent,
$$ \sum_jX_{ij}=\sum_gq_{ag}=1. $$
For every original good $j\in J_g$,
$$ \sum_iX_{ij} =\frac1{\ell_g}\sum_a k_aq_{ag} =\frac n{\ell_g}\sum_a x_{ag} =1. $$

Utilities and expenditures are preserved:
$$ \sum_jv_{ij}X_{ij}=\sum_gu_{ag}q_{ag}, \qquad \sum_jP_jX_{ij}=\sum_g\widehat p_gq_{ag}\leq1. $$

Finally, aggregating any original-goods lottery by
$$ y_g=\sum_{j\in J_g}Y_j $$
preserves utility and expenditure. Conversely, any support lottery can be distributed among the goods in each class, preserving both. Thus the comparison optimum values are equal.

The expanded solution satisfies the cited approximate-HZ conditions, including the stronger expenditure bound of one. Consequently, a $\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,1/\delta)$ algorithm for the stated problem would solve every $\mathrm{PPAD}$ search problem in polynomial time.

This proves $\mathrm{PPAD}$-hardness, not $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

4. Polynomial-size rational solutions and the obstruction to $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness

4.1. Exact real solutions exist

Choose a common denominator $N$ and write
$$ \mu_a=\frac{N_a}{N}, \qquad \nu_g=\frac{M_g}{N}. $$
Replicate type $a$ into $N_a$ unit-demand agents and type $g$ into $M_g$ unit-supply goods. There are $N$ agents and $N$ goods.

The HZ existence theorem applies to this finite market and supplies an exact real equilibrium. This replication is used only for existence; it may have exponentially many copies. [Theorem 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.05746)

For each good type, use the minimum price among its copies. The individual optimum in the resulting support market equals the optimum in the replicated market:

  • Aggregating an original lottery preserves utility and weakly decreases expenditure.
  • A support lottery can be implemented using cheapest copies, preserving utility and expenditure.

Individual lottery optimization imposes no supply constraints, so the latter implementation is legitimate.

Aggregate the equilibrium flows over copies and divide by $N$. The resulting flow has masses $\mu,\nu$ and remains affordable at the minimum-copy prices. Identical agents have the same optimum value, so averaging their optimal lotteries preserves optimal utility.

This gives an exact real support equilibrium. Normalize it using Section 3.1, and denote it by $(p,x)$.

4.2. Rational prices with controlled regret

Set $\eta=\delta/2$ and round prices downward:
$$ p'_g=\eta\left\lfloor\frac{p_g}{\eta}\right\rfloor. $$
Then
$$ p'_g\leq p_g<p'_g+\eta. $$
A zero-price good $h$ remains zero.

If $p'\cdot y\leq1$, then $p\cdot y\leq1+\eta$. The lottery
$$ \widetilde y=\frac{y+\eta e_h}{1+\eta} $$
is affordable at $p$. Nonnegative utilities imply
$$ u_a\cdot\widetilde y\geq\frac{u_a\cdot y}{1+\eta}. $$
Taking maxima and using $V_a(p)\leq1$ gives
$$ V_a(p')\leq(1+\eta)V_a(p)\leq V_a(p)+\eta. $$

The original flow remains affordable after downward rounding and satisfies
$$ u_a\cdot q_a=V_a(p)\geq V_a(p')-\eta. $$

The normalized price bound gives
$$ \left\lfloor\frac{p_g}{\eta}\right\rfloor \leq\frac2{\delta\nu_g}. $$
Thus every rounded price has polynomial bit length: both $\log(1/\delta)$ and $\log(1/\nu_g)$ are bounded by the input encoding length.

4.3. A short rational clearing flow

Fix these rational prices and consider the linear system
$$ z_{ag}\geq0, \qquad \sum_gz_{ag}=\mu_a, \qquad \sum_az_{ag}=\nu_g, $$
$$ \sum_gp'_gz_{ag}\leq\mu_a, \qquad \sum_gu_{ag}z_{ag}\geq \mu_a\bigl(V_a(p')-\delta\bigr). $$
The exact real flow from above satisfies this system. Its feasible region is bounded, because
$$ 0\leq z_{ag}\leq\mu_a. $$

Each $V_a(p')$ has polynomial rational encoding length. Indeed, its defining lottery polytope is nonempty, bounded, and given by rational coefficients of polynomial bit length; an optimal vertex exists.

For completeness, the standard vertex encoding bound follows directly from Cramer’s rule. In dimension $d$, select $d$ independent active constraints at a vertex. Clearing denominators produces an integer system whose entries have polynomial bit length. Each determinant is a sum of at most $d!$ products of $d$ entries, so its bit length is polynomial in $d$ and the coefficient encoding length. Cramer’s rule therefore gives polynomial-size rational coordinates. This applies both to an optimal lottery vertex and to a vertex of the displayed flow system.

Consequently, there is a rational flow $z$ of polynomial encoding length. Together with $p'$ and zero weights, it is a valid solution.

This proves short-solution existence, without supplying an efficient way to find the prices.

4.4. Polynomial verification

A candidate rational solution can be checked in polynomial time in its encoding length.

Nonnegativity, margins, and affordability are rational arithmetic checks. For each agent, rational linear programming computes $V_a(p)$ exactly: its feasible region is nonempty, as witnessed by $q_a$, and bounded.

Weighted-flow optimality is checked by solving the transportation linear program with coefficients $\lambda_a u_{ag}$ and comparing its optimum with the candidate objective value. Its feasible region is nonempty and bounded.

Thus, after imposing the polynomial solution-length bound established above, the problem has a total, polynomially balanced, polynomial-time recognizable search relation. This is a $\mathrm{TFNP}$ formulation. Invalid input encodings can be handled by a trivial distinguished output, since the input hypotheses are themselves polynomial-time checkable.

4.5. Why standard $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness would imply a collapse

Here, a solution-preserving reduction from satisfiability search means polynomial-time procedures $F,D$ such that:

  • $F(\varphi)$ is a valid instance of our problem for every Boolean formula $\varphi$.
  • Whenever $\varphi$ is satisfiable, every valid solution $s$ of $F(\varphi)$ is decoded by $D(\varphi,s)$ into a satisfying assignment.

Suppose such a reduction existed. An unsatisfiable formula would have the following polynomial-size certificate: a valid short solution $s$ of $F(\varphi)$ for which $D(\varphi,s)$ fails to satisfy $\varphi$.

The verifier checks validity of $s$ and then checks the decoded assignment.

If $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable, a short valid $s$ exists by Sections 4.1–4.3, and no decoded assignment can satisfy $\varphi$. Thus a certificate exists.

If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, the reduction guarantees that every valid $s$ decodes to a satisfying assignment. Thus no such certificate exists.

Unsatisfiability would therefore belong to $\mathrm{NP}$, implying
$$ \mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}. $$

This is the total-search obstruction underlying the classical $\mathrm{TFNP}$ framework; the argument above verifies it directly for this problem. [Megiddo and Papadimitriou](https://theory.stanford.edu/~megiddo/pdf/papadimX.pdf)

Accordingly, $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness in this standard solution-preserving sense would establish a major complexity-class collapse. The $\mathrm{PPAD}$ lower bound does not establish that collapse.

5. A further exact boundary case

For $\tau=1$, arbitrary normalized rational utilities admit an exact polynomial-time solution.

Here $\mu_1=1$ and the only feasible flow is
$$ x_{1g}=\nu_g. $$
Define
$$ U=\sum_gu_{1g}\nu_g, \qquad p_g=\frac{u_{1g}}U, \qquad \lambda_1=1. $$
Since a utility-one good has positive mass, $U>0$.

The allocated lottery costs one. Every affordable lottery satisfies
$$ u_1\cdot y=U(p\cdot y)\leq U, $$
so the allocation is exactly optimal. The unique feasible flow also satisfies weighted-flow optimality. All computations have polynomial bit complexity.

6. What remains open

The general problem has a proved $\mathrm{PPAD}$ lower bound and a total polynomially verifiable formulation with short rational solutions. Neither establishes a general polynomial-time algorithm.

The exact dichotomous algorithm handles arbitrary support masses directly, without replication. Its proof uses binary utilities essentially: utility is exactly the probability assigned to liked goods. It does not extend as given to intermediate utility values.

I have not established $\mathrm{PPAD}$ membership for arbitrary binary-encoded masses. Exponentially large replication cannot justify that upper bound. I have also not established $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness; under the reduction notion defined above, that would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.
