The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n\), a support of \(\tau\) rankings \(\pi_t\in S_n\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and fixed positive rational item weights \(w(e)\), find a ranking \(\hat{x}\in S_n\) satisfying \(F_\mu(\hat{x})\le (1.9677+\varepsilon)\min_{x\in S_n}F_\mu(x)\), where \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t d_U^w(x,\pi_t)\) and \(d_U^w\) is the element-weighted Ulam distance, in time polynomial in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length, for each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq 1\) items, and rankings are strict permutations in \(S_m\). A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq 1\) distinct rankings \(\pi_t\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible types.
Each item has a positive binary rational weight \(w(e)\), supplied as input and common to all rankings. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length. Define the element-weighted Ulam distance by \(d_U^w(x,y)=\sum_{e=1}^m w(e)-\max_C\sum_{e\in C}w(e)\), where \(C\) ranges over common subsequences of \(x\) and \(y\); equivalently, moving an item costs its weight.
Set \(F_\mu(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t d_U^w(x,\pi_t)\). The output is a single permutation; any minimizing permutation is admissible when the optimum is nonunique.

For every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), does a randomized algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time return \(\hat{x}\in S_m\) with \(F_\mu(\hat{x})\leq(1.9677+\varepsilon)\min_{x\in S_m}F_\mu(x)\) with probability at least \(2/3\)?

2. Metric properties and a computable anchor

Abbreviate \(d_U^w\) by \(d\), and write \(F=F_\mu\).

First, \(d\) is a metric. For the triangle inequality, let \(K_{xy}\) and \(K_{yz}\) be the item sets of maximum-weight common subsequences for \(x,y\) and \(y,z\). Their intersection is a common subsequence of \(x,z\): its order in both agrees with its order in \(y\). Consequently,

\[ \]
\(d(x,z)\leq w([m]\setminus(K_{xy}\cap K_{yz}))\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).\)

Symmetry is immediate. Because all item weights are positive, distance zero requires a common subsequence containing every item, and hence identical permutations.

Let \(x^*\) minimize \(F\), and put \(\mathrm{OPT}=F(x^*)\). Compute

\(a\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{t\in[\tau]}F(\pi_t),\qquad A=F(a).\)

The triangle inequality gives

\(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_tF(\pi_t)=\sum_{t,u}\mu_t\mu_u d(\pi_t,\pi_u)\leq 2F(x^*).\)

Therefore,

\[\mathrm{OPT}\leq A\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}.\tag{1}\]

If \(A=0\), then every support ranking equals \(a\), since every mass is positive. Returning \(a\) is optimal. Henceforth assume \(A>0\).

A further consequence used below is that, for every permutation \(x\),

\[d(x,a)\leq \sum_t\mu_t\bigl(d(x,\pi_t)+d(\pi_t,a)\bigr)=F(x)+A.\tag{2}\]

All these quantities are computable exactly in polynomial time by maximum-weight common-subsequence dynamic programming.

3. Concentrating objective differences

Let \(P_1,\ldots,P_N\) be independent rankings sampled according to the masses, and define their empirical objective, retaining multiplicities, by

\(G(x)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x,P_i).\)

Fix a permutation \(x\), and set \(r_x=d(x,a)\). The reverse triangle inequality implies

\(\lvert d(x,P_i)-d(a,P_i)\rvert\leq r_x.\)

If \(r_x>0\), Hoeffding’s inequality for independent variables in \([-r_x,r_x]\) gives

\(\Pr\!\left[\left|(G(x)-G(a))-(F(x)-A)\right|>\eta r_x\right]\leq 2\exp(-N\eta^2/2).\)

When \(r_x=0\), the difference is identically zero. Taking a union bound over the \(m!\) possible outputs shows that the event

\[\mathcal E:\quad \left|(G(x)-G(a))-(F(x)-A)\right|\leq\eta d(x,a)\quad\text{for every }x\in S_m\tag{3}\]

has probability at least \(1-\beta\), provided

\[N\geq \frac{2}{\eta^2}\left(\log(m!)+\log\frac{2}{\beta}\right).\tag{4}\]

Thus a polynomial sample suffices to control all objective differences, independently of the numerical range of the weights.

The anchor cost itself requires only a one-sided estimate. Since \(G(a)\) is nonnegative and has expectation \(A\), Markov’s inequality gives

\[\Pr[G(a)\leq(1+\eta)A]\geq \frac{\eta}{1+\eta}=:p_0.\tag{5}\]

This probability need not be large; it depends only on the fixed accuracy parameter.

4. Encoding a sampled multiset as distinct rankings

We next justify applying the uniform theorem even when sampled rankings repeat.

Introduce \(N\) new items \(b_1,\ldots,b_N\), each of weight \(\eta A/N\). Their total weight is

\(D=\eta A.\)

Let \(T_1,\ldots,T_N\) be the distinct cyclic shifts of \((b_1,\ldots,b_N)\). Extend each sampled ranking by concatenation:

\(\widetilde P_i=P_iT_i.\)

These extended rankings are pairwise distinct, regardless of repetitions among the \(P_i\). Define their uniform objective by

\(H(z)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde d(z,\widetilde P_i),\)

where \(\widetilde d\) uses the extended item weights. Let \(\operatorname{pr}(z)\) delete all new items.

For every extended permutation \(z\),

\[d(\operatorname{pr}(z),P_i)\leq\widetilde d(z,\widetilde P_i).\tag{6}\]

Indeed, an extended common subsequence has original-item weight at most the maximum common-subsequence weight of \(\operatorname{pr}(z),P_i\), and new-item weight at most \(D\). Subtracting from the extended total weight proves (6).

Conversely, extend any original permutation \(x\) by appending the new items in a fixed order, obtaining \(\widetilde x\). Retaining a maximum-weight original common subsequence gives

\[\widetilde d(\widetilde x,\widetilde P_i)\leq d(x,P_i)+D.\tag{7}\]

Suppose the uniform algorithm returns \(z\) with approximation factor \(\alpha\). For \(x=\operatorname{pr}(z)\), equations (6)–(7) imply

\[G(x)\leq H(z)\leq\alpha\min_y H(y)\leq\alpha\bigl(G(x^*)+D\bigr).\tag{8}\]

Thus the theorem for distinct rankings supplies an approximate solution for the sampled multiset, with a controlled additive error.

Its success probability can be amplified to \(1-\beta\): run it independently several times and retain the output with minimum exact \(H\)-cost. For each fixed sample, the conditional failure probability is at most \(\beta\).

5. Transferring the approximation to the original objective

Assume simultaneously that:

  • event \(\mathcal E\) holds;
  • \(G(a)\leq(1+\eta)A\);
  • the uniform algorithm succeeds, giving (8).

Applying (3) first to \(x\) and then to \(x^*\), we obtain

\(F(x)\leq G(x)-G(a)+A+\eta d(x,a)\)

and

\(G(x^*)\leq \mathrm{OPT}+G(a)-A+\eta d(x^*,a).\)

Substitution into (8) yields

\[F(x)\leq\alpha\mathrm{OPT}+(\alpha-1)(G(a)-A)+\eta d(x,a)+\alpha\eta d(x^*,a)+\alpha D.\tag{9}\]

We will choose \(1<\alpha<2\). Using (1), (2), the anchor event, and \(D=\eta A\), we have

\((\alpha-1)(G(a)-A)\leq 2(\alpha-1)\eta\,\mathrm{OPT},\)

\(d(x,a)\leq F(x)+2\mathrm{OPT},\qquad d(x^*,a)\leq3\mathrm{OPT},\)

and

\(\alpha D\leq2\alpha\eta\,\mathrm{OPT}.\)

Consequently, (9) becomes

\((1-\eta)F(x)\leq\alpha(1+7\eta)\mathrm{OPT},\)

so that

\[F(x)\leq\frac{\alpha(1+7\eta)}{1-\eta}\,\mathrm{OPT}.\tag{10}\]

This proves the transfer without requiring multiplicative concentration of the empirical objective.

Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose a fixed positive rational number \(s\leq\min\{\varepsilon,1/100\}\), and set

\(\eta=s/100,\qquad \alpha=c+s/4.\)

\[\frac{\alpha(1+7\eta)}{1-\eta}=\alpha+\frac{8\alpha\eta}{1-\eta}<\alpha+17\eta=c+\frac{42s}{100}<c+\varepsilon.\tag{11}\]

\(\beta=p_0/4,\qquad p_0=\eta/(1+\eta),\)

and choose \(N\) satisfying (4). The probability that all three events in Section 5 hold is at least

\[p_0-\beta-\beta=p_0/2.\tag{12}\]

Here the uniform algorithm’s failure probability is bounded conditionally on every sample; no independence between the three events is needed.
