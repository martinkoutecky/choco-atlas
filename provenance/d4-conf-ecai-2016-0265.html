The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a candidate set \(M\), a tie-break order \(\triangleright\), and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings, is there a rational allocation \(q_{t,a}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{a\in M}q_{t,a}=\mu_t\), such that no positive-mass coalition whose members all prefer some \(y\) to the current winner \(w\) can reassign its veto ballots to make \(y\) the tie-broken minimum-veto winner? If so, construct such an allocation.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(M\) contain \(m\ge 2\) candidates, with a fixed strict tie-break order \(\triangleright\). A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) strict ranking types \(\succ_t\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings; \(L\) is the total input bit length.
An allocation consists of rational masses \(q_{t,a}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_a q_{t,a}=\mu_t\). Each individual casts one veto; these masses describe a divisible population's deterministic ballots. Write \(V_a(q)=\sum_t q_{t,a}\); the winner \(w(q)\) is the first candidate in \(\triangleright\) among those minimizing \(V_a(q)\).
A coalition selects real masses \(0\le c_{t,a}\le q_{t,a}\) with \(\sum_{t,a}c_{t,a}>0\), and replaces them by real masses \(r_{t,a}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_a r_{t,a}=\sum_a c_{t,a}\) for every \(t\). Its resulting allocation is \(q'=q-c+r\); all outside ballots remain fixed. It improves if \(y=w(q')\) satisfies \(y\succ_t w(q)\) for every type with \(\sum_a c_{t,a}>0\). Call \(q\) stable if no such coalition improves.

Is deciding whether a rational stable allocation exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? Can such an allocation be constructed whenever one exists in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

For finitely many voters with strict preferences and fixed priority tie-breaking, veto always admits a strong equilibrium, constructible in polynomial time in the explicit input size. [Discrete result](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/6021/3ee1b7993e4326437fa344eb7c69fe076f86.pdf)
No published result settling this exact divisible-population problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one and required at least two candidates.
  • Interpreted each type's mass as divisible individuals casting deterministic veto ballots, rather than as a single player using a mixed strategy.
  • Made coalitions arbitrary positive submasses of the current type-by-ballot cells, with type-preserving reassignment and fixed outside ballots.
  • Allowed real coalition and reassignment masses while retaining the requested rational equilibrium allocation.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Every valid instance admits a rational stable allocation. Such an allocation can be constructed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Thus the decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\).

The construction compresses the feasible-elimination approach underlying the discrete result in [Proposition 3 of Gourvès, Lesca, and Wilczynski](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/6021/3ee1b7993e4326437fa344eb7c69fe076f86.pdf). Below, we prove the elimination procedure directly and establish stability against arbitrary real-valued coalition deviations.

1. Universal veto thresholds

Let \(p\) denote the first candidate in \(\triangleright\).

For any allocation of total mass \(1\), a winning candidate has veto mass at most \(1/m\), because the minimum of the \(m\) veto masses is at most their average.

Moreover, a candidate \(y\ne p\) can win only if its veto mass is strictly less than \(1/m\). Indeed, if \(y\) won with veto mass \(1/m\), every candidate would have veto mass at least \(1/m\). Since the masses sum to \(1\), all would equal \(1/m\), and tie-breaking would elect \(p\).

Consequently:

  • Veto mass strictly greater than \(1/m\) prevents \(p\) from winning.
  • Veto mass at least \(1/m\) prevents every \(y\ne p\) from winning.

These thresholds apply equally to rational and real allocations.

2. Integer units for the construction

Choose a positive integer \(D\) such that \(D\mu_t\) is integral for every \(t\). For example, take the product of the denominators in the input.

Set \(N=mD\), and represent type \(t\)'s mass by the integer \(n_t=N\mu_t\). Then \(\sum_t n_t=N\). These units are only a device for constructing the allocation; deviations will remain unrestricted real masses.

Define integer elimination thresholds by

\(\beta_p=D+1,\qquad \beta_a=D\text{ for }a\ne p.\)

Thus \(\sum_{a\in M}\beta_a=N+1\).

We will assign exactly \(\beta_a\) veto units to every eliminated candidate \(a\), using only units whose type ranks \(a\) last among the candidates still present.

3. The elimination algorithm always progresses

Initially, let \(S=M\), and let the remaining count of each type be \(n_t\). Maintain the invariant

\(\sum_{a\in S}\beta_a=n+1,\)

where \(n\) is the total remaining count.

Suppose \(|S|\ge2\). Each remaining type has a unique least-preferred candidate in \(S\), because preferences are strict. Let \(b_a\) be the total remaining count whose least-preferred candidate in \(S\) is \(a\). Then

\(\sum_{a\in S}b_a=n.\)

There must be some \(a\in S\) with \(b_a\ge\beta_a\). Otherwise, integrality would give \(b_a\le\beta_a-1\) for every \(a\in S\), and hence

\(n=\sum_{a\in S}b_a\le\sum_{a\in S}(\beta_a-1)=n+1-|S|\le n-1,\)

a contradiction.

Choose such an \(a\). Extract exactly \(\beta_a\) units from the types currently ranking \(a\) last in \(S\), assign those units to veto \(a\), and remove them from the remaining counts. Remove \(a\) from \(S\).

Extraction is possible because \(b_a\ge\beta_a\): traverse the eligible types, taking the smaller of their remaining count and the still-needed amount. All counts remain integers. Both sides of the invariant decrease by \(\beta_a\), so the invariant persists.

Repeat until only one candidate \(w\) remains. At that point, the invariant gives a remaining count of \(\beta_w-1\). Assign all these remaining units to veto \(w\).

Finally, divide every assigned count by \(N\), obtaining \(q_{t,a}\). Every initial unit has been assigned exactly once, so

\(q_{t,a}\ge0,\qquad \sum_a q_{t,a}=\mu_t.\)

Thus \(q\) is a rational allocation.

4. The surviving candidate wins

Every eliminated candidate \(a\ne w\) receives veto mass \(\beta_a/N\), while \(w\) receives \((\beta_w-1)/N\).

There are two cases.

If \(w=p\): every candidate receives veto mass \(D/N=1/m\). Tie-breaking elects \(p=w\).

If \(w\ne p\): the veto masses are

\(V_w(q)=\frac{D-1}{N}=\frac1m-\frac1N,\qquad V_p(q)=\frac{D+1}{N}=\frac1m+\frac1N,\)

and

\(V_a(q)=\frac DN=\frac1m\quad\text{for }a\notin\{w,p\}.\)

Therefore \(w\) has strictly the smallest veto mass and wins.

In both cases, \(w(q)=w\).

5. Stability against arbitrary real coalitions

The construction has the following protection property:

For every losing candidate \(y\ne w\), if \(q_{t,y}>0\), then \(w\succ_t y\).

To prove this, observe that every unit assigned to veto \(y\) was extracted when \(y\) was least preferred among the then-remaining candidates. The eventual survivor \(w\) was still present. Strict preferences therefore imply \(w\succ_t y\).

Now suppose a coalition improves from \(w\) to some \(y\ne w\), using real masses \(c_{t,a}\) and \(r_{t,a}\).

Any participating type must satisfy \(y\succ_t w\). By the protection property, such a type has \(q_{t,y}=0\), and hence \(c_{t,y}=0\). Every nonparticipating type also has \(c_{t,y}=0\). Consequently, the coalition withdraws no veto mass from \(y\), and

\(V_y(q')=V_y(q)+\sum_t r_{t,y}\ge V_y(q).\)

If \(y=p\), then \(w\ne p\), so \(V_y(q')\ge 1/m+1/N>1/m\). Section 1 shows that \(p\) cannot win.

If \(y\ne p\), then \(V_y(q')\ge1/m\). Again by Section 1, \(y\) cannot win.

Both cases contradict the proposed improvement. Therefore \(q\) is stable. This argument imposes no integrality or rationality restriction on the coalition.

6. Polynomial running time and output size

The product \(D\) of the input denominators has bit length \(O(L)\), even though its numerical value may be large. Hence \(N=mD\) has bit length \(O(L+\log m)\).

All remaining counts, assigned counts, and thresholds are integers bounded by \(N\). There are exactly \(m-1\) elimination steps. Scanning each ranking at every step to find its least-preferred remaining candidate uses \(O(\tau m^2)\) elementary operations; aggregation and extraction also take polynomial time. All arithmetic uses integers of polynomial bit length.

The output contains \(\tau m\) rational entries, each expressible with denominator \(N\) and numerator bounded by \(N\). Its total encoding length is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus the algorithm constructs a rational stable allocation for every valid input in polynomial time, without expanding the society into an explicit electorate.
